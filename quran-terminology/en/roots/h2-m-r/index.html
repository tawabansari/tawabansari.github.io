Root H M R — Pouring down and abundant flow

Qur’anic and lexical forms

مُنْهَمِرٍ
(munhamirin)
Pouring copiously; active participle

From heavy rain and streaming water or tears to speech, generosity, and movement

The Arabic triliteral root ه م ر concerns pouring, descending, and flowing copiously. It particularly describes water, rain, and tears visibly streaming rather than merely releasing a few drops.

The Qur’an uses it once as مُنْهَمِرٍ, describing water pouring from the sky. In Noah’s story, the opening of heaven’s gates presents a scene of intense, extensive rainfall.

Hamr is not simply a name for rain: it describes pouring and flow. Arabic accordingly applies related forms to tears, running water, and even an outpouring of speech.

1. The root in early Arabic

Classical Arabic records هَمَرَ both for pouring liquid and for liquid flowing or falling.

هَمَرَ الْمَاءَ can mean “he poured the water”; هَمَرَ الْمَاءُ, “the water flowed or poured down.”

هَمَرَ الدَّمْعُ describes tears streaming from the eyes.

These expressions emphasize movement and pouring, not merely the presence of liquid. Rain and tear descriptions commonly involve abundance or intensity.

A pre-Qur’anic poetic witness

A rain description in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

«سَاعَةً ثُمَّ انْتَحَاهَا وَابِلٌ
سَاقِطُ الْأَكْنَافِ وَاهٍ مُنْهَمِرْ»

Sāʿatan thumma ntaḥāhā wābilun / sāqiṭu l-aknāfi wāhin munhamir.

“After a while, a heavy downpour swept toward it, spreading its hanging sides, pouring freely and copiously.”

The poem describes rainfall spreading over land and increasing when a heavier downpour arrives.

وَابِل denotes heavy rain; مُنْهَمِر describes its pouring descent.

The words contribute differently: wābil characterizes the rain’s intensity; munhamir emphasizes its flow. Together they depict heavy, copiously pouring rain.

The next line describes winds affecting the rain and intensifying its flow. Munhamir here characterizes actual rainfall, not a technical term for punishment or a supernatural event.

The poem survives in Imruʾ al-Qays’s attributed collection and older anthologies. Its attribution has literary support, though the line’s exact date is not independently established beyond later transmission.

The same form مُنْهَمِر used in the Qur’an thus appears in early poetic rain descriptions.

Its abundant-water sense has independent Arabic support and is not derived solely from Noah’s story.

Streaming tears

A poem attributed to Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah al-Hudhalī reads:

«وَجَاءَ خَلِيلَاهُ إِلَيْهَا كِلَاهُمَا
يَفِيضُ دُمُوعًا لَا يَرِيثُ هُمُورُهَا»

Wa-jāʾa khalīlāhu ilayhā kilāhumā / yafīḍu dumūʿan lā yarīthu humūruhā.

“His two companions came to her, both shedding tears whose streaming did not delay.”

هُمُور concerns the flow of tears.

The tears are not merely pooled in the eyes: they flow. لَا يَرِيثُ indicates that their streaming does not linger or delay.

Hamr is therefore not restricted to rainfall; it can describe other visibly abundant liquid flow.

Sāʿidah lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. The line’s date is unknown, so it cannot confidently be assigned to pre-Islamic times; it nevertheless witnesses usage near the revelation period.

Other Arabic uses

هَمْرَة is recorded for an occasion or burst of rain; هَمَّار can describe a rain-laden cloud or something releasing abundant water.

Liquid flow remains central in these branches.

The root’s use nevertheless extends beyond liquids.

هَمَرَ فِي كَلَامِهِ describes speaking profusely; مِهْمَار can describe a very talkative person.

Speech is pictured as repeatedly pouring from the mouth. This explains a semantic association without dating its historical development from the physical sense.

هَمَرَ لَهُ مِنْ مَالِهِ is recorded for giving someone property; copious generosity can evoke pouring forth.

هَمَرَ الْفَرَسُ الْأَرْضَ describes a horse pounding the ground with its hooves; اِهْتَمَرَ الْفَرَسُ can describe rapid movement.

These concern forceful motion, but cannot all be historically derived from rainfall or flooding without evidence.

Some sources record additional descriptions, such as a large-built person, and proper names. Their existence does not prove a single simple semantic origin.

The directly supported Qur’anic branch concerns pouring and flowing water.

2. Forms and derivatives

The Qur’an uses the root once as مُنْهَمِرٍ.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • هَمَرَ: poured or, intransitively, flowed.
  • هَمْر: pouring or streaming, especially liquid.
  • اِنْهَمَرَ: poured down or began flowing.
  • هَامِر: a basic-form active participle, pouring or flowing.
  • مُنْهَمِر: a Form VII active participle, pouring or streaming down.
  • هَمْرَة: an occasion or burst of rain.
  • هَمَّار: in some usages, a cloud or phenomenon releasing abundant water.
  • مِهْمَار: a very talkative person.

Arabic also records اِهْتَمَرَ and يُهَامِرُ. Among the listed forms, only مُنْهَمِرٍ occurs in the Qur’an.

The structure of مُنْهَمِر

It is the Form VII active participle of اِنْهَمَرَ.

The sequence is:

اِنْهَمَرَ ← يَنْهَمِرُ ← مُنْهَمِر

Form VII here concerns the liquid itself pouring or flowing.

هَمَرَ الْمَاءَ can describe an agent pouring water; in اِنْهَمَرَ الْمَاءُ, the water is the subject and its flowing is described.

This does not make every basic-form usage transitive or every Form VII event externally uncaused. Lexicons also record intransitive basic-form usage.

The Qur’anic sentence supplies the rainfall’s agency and setting. Form VII does not establish absence of will or external causation.

مُنْهَمِر is morphologically active, not passive: the water is what pours down.

3. Qur’anic usage — Abundant water pouring from the sky

Al-Qamar 54:11

«فَفَتَحْنَا أَبْوَابَ السَّمَاءِ بِمَاءٍ مُّنْهَمِرٍ»

Fa-fataḥnā abwāba s-samāʾi bi-māʾin munhamir.

“So We opened the gates of heaven with copiously pouring water.”

The verse belongs to Noah’s story.

Al-Qamar 54:9 describes Noah’s rejection and accusations of madness; in al-Qamar 54:10 he calls upon his Lord, declaring himself overcome and seeking help.

Al-Qamar 54:11 then describes heaven’s gates opening and water pouring down.

فَفَتَحْنَا — Opening after the request for help

Initial fāʾ connects the event with what precedes.

Noah has sought help; the opening is presented in response.

فَتَحْنَا belongs to ف ت ح, denoting opening.

Opening belongs to فَتَحْنَا; pouring water is expressed by بِمَاءٍ مُنْهَمِرٍ.

The concepts should remain distinct in analysis.

أَبْوَابَ السَّمَاءِ — Heaven’s opening gates

The Qur’an speaks of opening “the gates of heaven.”

Opening gates can picture an opened passage or removed obstruction. With abundant water it presents extensive, intense pouring from above.

The expression alone does not establish the gates’ physical shape, number, or construction.

Nor does understanding munhamir require assuming a precisely located reservoir.

The verse describes heaven opening and water descending in a major extraordinary event. Its intelligible imagery does not make the physical details of heaven or rainfall part of hamr’s lexical meaning.

بِمَاءٍ — The substance pouring

مَاء means water; بِمَاءٍ connects it with heaven’s opening gates.

Water is the substance involved in that event.

Māʾ alone specifies neither quantity nor intensity.

The following description, مُنْهَمِرٍ, clarifies these qualities.

مُنْهَمِرٍ — The water’s pouring condition

It modifies مَاءٍ and shares its genitive case.

It describes water pouring freely and copiously.

Applied to rain, it pictures a heavy, streaming downpour.

Compare light, spaced drops with rain so intense that it falls in connected streams or streaks.

The latter more closely illustrates munhamir.

Yet continuous-looking flow differs from the duration of rainfall.

Munhamir can convey abundant, intense flow without specifying hours, days, or another duration.

Duration and geographical extent require other evidence.

The word alone does not establish uninterrupted rain for a fixed period or a specified depth covering the entire earth.

Its core is copious pouring and flowing water.

4. Water from heaven and earth in Noah’s story

Al-Qamar 54:12

«وَفَجَّرْنَا الْأَرْضَ عُيُونًا فَالْتَقَى الْمَاءُ عَلَىٰ أَمْرٍ قَدْ قُدِرَ»

Wa-fajjarnā l-arḍa ʿuyūnan fa-ltaqā l-māʾu ʿalā amrin qad qudir.

“And We caused the earth to burst forth in springs; then the waters met for a matter already ordained.”

The next verse extends the scene with different actions and subjects.

Al-Qamar 54:11 described water pouring from heaven; al-Qamar 54:12 brings the earth’s springs into the scene.

فَجَّرْنَا belongs to ف ج ر, here concerning splitting open and releasing water.

عُيُونًا denotes springs.

فَالْتَقَى الْمَاءُ then describes the waters meeting.

Inhimār and tafjīr

Inhimār in al-Qamar 54:11 concerns copious pouring flow.

Tafjīr in al-Qamar 54:12 concerns opening outlets for water from the earth.

They are different actions.

Inhimār suits water pouring intensely from above.

Water springing from the earth or emerging through openings can be described through ف ج ر.

The narrative combines water pouring from heaven with water gushing from earth.

The two flows then meet.

Munhamir alone does not describe the entire event; the next verse’s verbs contribute independently.

Does hamr mean a flood or storm?

Hamr means pouring or abundant flow, not every kind of storm or flood.

Such events may involve rain, floodwater, wind, or other phenomena; munhamir in al-Qamar 54:11 specifically describes pouring water.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14 uses الطُّوفَانُ in Noah’s story. It belongs to a different root.

Heavy rain may form part of the flood, but munhamir is not the flood’s name.

The combined heavenly and earthly waters also do not, without further evidence, determine geography, depth, rainfall measurements, or geological mechanisms.

Those questions exceed the root’s lexical meaning.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

ه م ر and ص ب ب — Pouring down and pouring liquid

Ṣabb denotes pouring something, especially liquid.

ʿAbasa 80:25 says:

«أَنَّا صَبَبْنَا الْمَاءَ صَبًّا»

Annā ṣababnā l-māʾa ṣabbā.

“That We poured down the water in an outpouring.”

صَبَبْنَا expresses pouring; the verbal noun صَبًّا emphasizes it.

Hamr also concerns pouring, but مَاءٍ مُنْهَمِرٍ highlights copious flowing descent.

Ṣabb is a broad pouring term; rain-related munhamir depicts free and abundant flow.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary. Ṣabb can describe abundant rainfall, and hamr can name the act of pouring itself.

ه م ر and ث ج ج — Abundant flow and intense pouring

Al-Nabaʾ 78:14 describes descending water as ثَجَّاجًا.

From ث ج ج, it concerns copious, intense liquid outpouring.

Thajjāj and munhamir therefore have nearby semantic ranges.

Both can describe heavy rain.

Thajj clearly emphasizes copious outpouring; inhimār highlights freely streaming descent in its usage.

This does not establish that thajjāj is always stronger or munhamir always longer-lasting.

Such rankings require independent evidence.

ه م ر and و ب ل — Heavy rain and its flow

وَابِل denotes heavy rain.

Al-Baqarah 2:264 and al-Baqarah 2:265 use it in rain-related examples.

Wābil characterizes the rainfall; munhamir describes its pouring flow.

The Imruʾ al-Qays witness places both together: heavy rain pouring down.

They can describe different aspects of one phenomenon without being identical.

ه م ر and و د ق — Rain and how it descends

Al-Nūr 24:43 mentions الْوَدْقَ emerging through clouds.

Wadq here means rain.

Hamr describes pouring and flowing liquid, rather than merely naming rain.

It can accordingly describe tears through هَمَرَ الدَّمْعُ, while rain-related wadq names the rainfall itself.

One thus principally identifies the phenomenon; the other its manner of flow.

ه م ر and س ك ب — Streaming and being poured

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 says:

«وَمَاءٍ مَسْكُوبٍ»

Wa-māʾin maskūb.

“And water poured forth.”

مَسْكُوب is a passive participle from س ك ب.

That root also concerns pouring or causing liquid to flow.

Maskūb and munhamir have different morphology.

Maskūb describes what has been poured or made to flow; munhamir is active from inhamara and describes the liquid’s pouring condition.

Both may describe a water flow, but their grammatical and contextual differences remain.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 describes blessings; al-Qamar 54:11 places pouring water within the response to Noah’s rejection.

The event’s value follows from context, not from hamr inherently meaning punishment and sakb inherently meaning blessing.

6. Lexical meaning and evaluation of events

The family can describe abundant water, streaming tears, copious speech, and giving property.

It has no inherent positive or negative moral value.

Heavy rain can benefit dry land and water supplies, or under different conditions cause flooding and destruction.

Tears can express sorrow, longing, joy, or pain; hamr does not identify their cause.

Profuse speech can be useful in one situation and burdensome in another.

Abundance or intense pouring alone does not establish an event’s goodness or badness.

Al-Qamar 54:11 connects the rain with Noah’s people and his appeal for help; its narrative role follows from the whole passage.

Its place within aid and the consequences of rejection does not redefine munhamir as “water of punishment.”

Nor does the verse establish that every present-day heavy rain or flood punishes a particular group.

Such a judgment requires independent evidence; the root describes pouring water.

7. Suggested translations for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for مُنْهَمِرٍ in al-Qamar 54:11 include “pouring down,” “streaming down,” and contextually “copiously pouring.”

“Flowing” conveys part of the meaning but may leave intensity and abundance unclear.

“Pouring” is concise; “copiously pouring water” more fully conveys the Qur’anic usage.

For the phrase:

«بِمَاءٍ مُّنْهَمِرٍ»

Bi-māʾin munhamir.

the suggested translation is:

“With copiously pouring water”

For the complete verse:

“So We opened the gates of heaven with copiously pouring water.”

Opening comes from فَتَحْنَا, water from مَاء, and its pouring condition from مُنْهَمِر.

A freer rendering could say:

“So We opened the gates of heaven, and abundant water poured down.”

This conveys the general image but restructures the Arabic. Forqan’s base translation preferably retains the connection between بِمَاءٍ مُنْهَمِرٍ and فَتَحْنَا.

A lexical explanation can read:

“Munhamir: water pouring freely and copiously; here, heavy rainfall from the sky.”

Other usages require equivalents suited to their subjects.

اِنْهَمَرَ الْمَطَرُ means “the rain poured down heavily.”

اِنْهَمَرَ الدَّمْعُ means “tears streamed down.”

هَمَرَ فِي كَلَامِهِ means “he spoke profusely,” which may be explained as “words poured from him.”

Duration, cause, and moral value should not be added without evidence.

8. Conclusion

ه م ر concerns pouring and copious flow, especially water, rain, and tears.

The Imruʾ al-Qays witness illustrates munhamir for heavy rain in early poetic tradition; Sāʿidah’s poem illustrates tears, though its date is uncertain.

Classical Arabic also records profuse speech, giving property, and rapid horse movement. Some semantic associations are intelligible, but their historical origins are not all established.

The sole Qur’anic form is مُنْهَمِرٍ, a Form VII active participle describing water pouring when heaven’s gates open in al-Qamar 54:11.

The next verse adds earthly springs and meeting waters. Different roots describe these events; hamr does not encompass the entire story.

Forqan definition: The principal branch of ه م ر denotes pouring or streaming liquid, especially freely and copiously. Qur’anic مُنْهَمِر describes water pouring intensely and abundantly, without independently fixing duration, geography, quantity, or mechanism.

Its distinctions from ص ب ب, ث ج ج, و ب ل, و د ق, and س ك ب remain important: related rain and water descriptions can emphasize phenomenon, intensity, flow, or the act of pouring differently.

Al-Qamar 54:11 presents copiously pouring water within Noah’s great flood event. Its lexical meaning remains pouring and streaming, not the flood or punishment itself.
