Pomegranate: The name of the fruit and its tree, and the question of its derivation

Brief root meaning: Rummān names the pomegranate fruit and, in general usage, its tree. Rummānah denotes one pomegranate. Early lexicographers disagreed over whether the nūn is radical or added. In the Qur’an, this family occurs only as the name of the pomegranate.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

Rummān is the Arabic name of the familiar fruit called anār in Persian and pomegranate in English. It has a relatively firm rind containing many seeds with juicy edible coverings.

The tree is also known by this name in Arabic. Depending on context, rummān can therefore denote the fruit or the kind of tree.

Unlike many Qur’anic verbal roots, this word has no extensive family of verbs and semantic derivatives. Most recorded forms concern the fruit, tree, individual fruit, and figurative uses of its shape.

Rummān occurs three times in the Qur’an: twice in Al-Anʿām alongside olives and other vegetation, and once in Al-Raḥmān describing the blessings of two gardens in the Hereafter.

All three occurrences concern the familiar fruit. None expresses a separate meaning such as gathering, decay, repair, or accumulation.

This matters methodologically because some early analyses discuss a connection between rummān and R M M. Disagreement over the word’s structure must not alter its clear Qur’anic meaning.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

The Qur’anic morphological index records R M N three times in one basic lexical form:

  • وَالرُّمَّانَ: Pomegranates, with the conjunction wa- and definite article al-; Al-Anʿām 6:99.
  • وَالرُّمَّانَ: The same form; Al-Anʿām 6:141.
  • وَرُمَّانٌ: Pomegranates, indefinite and nominative; Al-Raḥmān 55:68.

Other forms and uses are also known in Arabic:

  • رُمَّان: The pomegranate fruit and tree.
  • رُمَّانَة: One whole pomegranate, not one seed inside it.
  • رُمَّانَات: The counting plural of rummānah.
  • رُمَّانِيّ: Related to pomegranate, such as a color or something resembling it.
  • مَرْمَنَة: A place with abundant pomegranate trees.
  • رُمَيْمِينَة: The diminutive of rummānah.
  • رُمَّانَة الفَرَس: A part within a horse’s abdomen containing its ingested fodder, rather than a separate feeding vessel.
  • رَمَّان: With a fatḥah on rāʾ, a place name in some records.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an, nor are their derivational histories and morphological structures equally clear.

The name rummān itself remains the focus for understanding the Qur’anic uses.

2. Word structure and disagreement about the root

Does rummān belong to R M N or R M M?

The main lexical question is whether the nūn in rummān is radical.

An Arabic word may consist of three root letters with other letters added to its structure.

Yet the original letters cannot always be identified from its outward form.

Two early analyses exist for rummān.

In the first, nūn is added and the word belongs to R M M.

In the second, nūn is radical and the root is R M N.

This disagreement is not merely a modern theoretical debate; it is recorded in early lexical and morphological discussions.

The analysis treating nūn as added

An early morphological discussion asks whether rummān, when used as a personal name, takes the ordinary declensional endings.

Under one transmitted analysis, it is treated as a diptote when used as a proper name: it does not take nunation, and its ordinary genitive ending differs from a fully declinable noun.

The rationale is to favor the more likely pattern when the word’s derivation is unclear.

In this analysis, the final alif and nūn are treated as added, relating the form to R M M. This is a morphological classification, not by itself proof of the word’s historical development.

The view relies on a morphological principle, not on demonstrating a meaning derived directly from the fruit.

In other words, this approach uses structure to distinguish radical and added letters; it does not equate pomegranate with decay, repair, or restoration.

Even if this morphological analysis is accepted, the other meanings of R M M cannot be transferred to the fruit’s name.

The analysis treating nūn as radical

A contrasting early analysis treats the nūn of rummān as radical.

On this account, the word follows the fuʿʿāl pattern, with R M N as its root letters.

This view points to similarly structured plant names in Arabic, such as حُمَّاض and others.

A separate early lexical entry for R M N states:

الرَّاءُ وَالْمِيمُ وَالنُّونُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ، وَهِيَ الرُّمَّانُ

al-rāʾu wa-al-mīmu wa-al-nūnu kalimatun wāḥidatun wa-hiya al-rummānu

Translation: “Rāʾ, mīm, and nūn have one word: rummān.”

This statement is significant.

Unlike entries for many other roots, this account does not assign R M N an abstract core such as movement, gathering, or transformation.

It simply records the name of the pomegranate.

An extensive semantic family unsupported by Arabic evidence should therefore not be constructed from this entry.

مَرْمَنَة: Evidence concerning radical nūn

Marmanah is recorded for a place with many pomegranate trees.

The form matters to the structural analysis because it retains nūn.

If R M N is treated as the root, the formation of marmanah is intelligible within that family.

If nūn is treated as added in rummān, its presence in a further derivative calls for additional explanation.

Nevertheless, retaining a letter in derivatives formed from an established noun does not by itself settle the noun’s historical origin.

A language can form new derivatives from an established word, treating its letters as radicals even if its earlier historical structure differed.

Marmanah is thus useful evidence, but does not independently resolve the entire disagreement.

Connections with Semitic languages

The name of the pomegranate is also known in some early Semitic languages in forms resembling rummān.

Ancient Hebrew uses rimmōn, with related forms recorded in Aramaic and Syriac.

This resemblance indicates a long history for this fruit name in the region’s languages.

However, sharing a word across languages does not alone establish which language Arabic borrowed it from, or the chronological sequence of consonant and vowel changes.

Nor does this evidence make the Arabic name necessarily a derivative of ramma meaning repair or decay.

For Qur’anic research, the clear conclusion is that rummān was a familiar name for the pomegranate, which the Qur’an uses.

Disagreement about the status of nūn does not alter the verses’ meaning.

Was the pomegranate named for its gathered seeds?

The fruit has a clear physical feature: many seeds are enclosed within one rind.

One might infer that its name originated in the seeds gathering together.

Such an image is intelligible but insufficient for historical etymology.

Arabic roots such as J M ʿ, R K M, and R K B concern gathering and placing things together.

A conceptual resemblance to clustered seeds does not prove that rummān derives from them.

Likewise, the resemblance between R M N and R M M does not establish that the fruit’s name derives from decay or restoration.

Such claims require independent historical and linguistic evidence.

A root study should therefore avoid inventing an unsupported underlying meaning.

The clear, attested Qur’anic meaning of R M N is the name of the pomegranate.

3. Early Arabic usage and poetic witnesses

Rummān as a collective genus noun

In Arabic, rummān can denote the fruit as a kind or a collection of pomegranates.

Al-rummān may mean the pomegranate species rather than a particular fruit.

Rummānah denotes an individual unit.

This structure is familiar among Arabic plant names, as with تَمْر and تَمْرَة.

Qur’anic rummān therefore need not refer to one particular pomegranate.

In Al-Anʿām 6:99 and Al-Anʿām 6:141, it appears alongside kinds of plants and trees.

In Al-Raḥmān 55:68, it is introduced as one of the blessings of the two gardens.

The generic sense fits all three instances.

The fruit and its tree

In Arabic, some fruit names can denote both the fruit and its tree.

Rummān has this use as well.

A sentence about eating pomegranates refers to the fruit.

Another may name pomegranates alongside palms and olives, referring to the kind of plant or tree.

This does not require two roots or two wholly independent names.

Likewise, Persian anār names the fruit and, with the word “tree,” identifies the plant.

Qur’anic translation need not therefore render rummān as “pomegranate tree” every time.

In a passage about gardens and growing plants, “pomegranate” can carry the appropriate contextual sense.

Pomegranate in al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

يُخَطِّطْنَ بِالْعِيدَانِ فِي كُلِّ مَقْعَدٍ
وَيَخْبَأْنَ رُمَّانَ الثُّدِيِّ النَّوَاهِدِ

yukhaṭṭiṭna bi-al-ʿīdāni fī kulli maqʿadin / wa-yakhbaʾna rummāna al-thudiyyi al-nawāhidi

Translation: “Wherever they sit, they trace lines with little sticks and conceal their rounded, youthful breasts, likened to pomegranates.”

Rummān appears here in a comparison.

The poet uses the fruit’s familiar shape to describe roundness and prominence.

The witness matters because the name occurs in pre-Islamic poetry and its shape was familiar to the audience.

This image does not make roundness, prominence, or a human body part the word’s lexical meaning.

The fruit has a recognizable shape, whose resemblance the poet uses in the comparison.

Distinguishing an object’s name from the feature selected for a comparison is important in lexical research.

This witness also shows that rummān was not merely a religious or Qur’anic term; it belonged to pre-Islamic Arabic language and poetry.

Figurative applications of the fruit’s shape

The expression رُمَّان الصُّدُور is recorded in figurative usage.

It likewise concerns the pomegranate’s round, prominent shape.

In another use, رُمَّانَة البَطْن names the navel and the surrounding area.

رُمَّانَة الفَرَس is also recorded for a part inside a horse containing its ingested fodder; it should not be presented as a separate vessel for feeding the animal.

These usages show that the fruit’s name also appears in comparisons and names for other things.

Such naming does not change rummān’s basic meaning.

When a body part or another object is likened to a pomegranate, the resemblance may concern shape or prominence.

In the Qur’an, however, al-rummān denotes the fruit or plant itself, not a body part or some other object.

Rummānatān as a place name

Arabic geographical and lexical records identify al-Rummānatān as two elevations in the territory of the tribe of ʿAbs.

The Umayyad poet al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī says:

عَلَى الدَّارِ بِالرُّمَّانَتَيْنِ تَعُوجُ
صُدُورُ مَهَارَى سَيْرُهُنَّ وَسِيجُ

ʿalā al-dāri bi-al-rummānatayni taʿūju / ṣudūru mahārā sayruhunna wasīju

Translation: “Toward the dwelling at al-Rummānatayn turn the chests of swift camels whose gait is brisk.”

Al-Rummānatayn here is a place name.

This name is also recorded under the lexical entry R M N.

Its existence does not establish that the two elevations were named for the fruit’s shape or for pomegranate trees growing there.

Geographical names may have independent histories.

A place name should not be turned without evidence into a botanical feature or a historical meaning of the root.

This witness belongs to the Umayyad period rather than pre-Islamic times, but illustrates early Arabic usage of the name.

4. Pomegranates in Al-Anʿām: Growth and variety

Al-Anʿām 6:99: Pomegranates among the signs of growth

Verse text:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ نَبَاتَ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ فَأَخْرَجْنَا مِنْهُ خَضِرًا نُخْرِجُ مِنْهُ حَبًّا مُتَرَاكِبًا وَمِنَ النَّخْلِ مِنْ طَلْعِهَا قِنْوَانٌ دَانِيَةٌ وَجَنَّاتٍ مِنْ أَعْنَابٍ وَالزَّيْتُونَ وَالرُّمَّانَ مُشْتَبِهًا وَغَيْرَ مُتَشَابِهٍ ۗ انْظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكُمْ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يُؤْمِنُونَ

wa-huwa alladhī anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhrajnā bihi nabāta kulli shayʾin fa-akhrajnā minhu khaḍiran nukhriju minhu ḥabban mutarākiban wa-mina al-nakhli min ṭalʿihā qinwānun dāniyatun wa-jannātin min aʿnābin wa-al-zaytūna wa-al-rummāna mushtabihan wa-ghayra mutashābihin unẓurū ilā thamarihi idhā athmara wa-yanʿihi inna fī dhālikum la-āyātin li-qawmin yuʾminūna

Al-Anʿām 6:99

Translation:

“He is the One who sent down water from the sky. With it We brought forth the growth of every thing, then brought forth from it greenery from which We produce clustered grain; and from the palms, from their spathes, clusters hanging near, and gardens of grapes, olives, and pomegranates, similar and dissimilar. Look at their fruit when they bear fruit and at its ripening. Surely in these are signs for people who believe.”

The meaning of wa-al-rummān

وَالرُّمَّانَ combines the conjunction wa- with the definite noun al-rummān.

It is accusative and appears alongside olives and other plants.

Its meaning in this context is pomegranates.

The verse concerns vegetation growing after water descends, grains and fruits emerging, and the variety of natural produce.

Rummān thus names one kind of fruit-bearing plant here.

The word alone does not identify a particular variety with a specified color or taste.

Pomegranates can vary in size, rind color, seed color, and flavor.

Those differences are not inherent in the name rummān, which identifies the familiar kind of fruit.

مُشْتَبِهًا وَغَيْرَ مُتَشَابِهٍ

After olives and pomegranates, the verse says:

مُشْتَبِهًا وَغَيْرَ مُتَشَابِهٍ

mushtabihan wa-ghayra mutashābihin

Al-Anʿām 6:99

The phrase concerns resemblance and difference: “similar and dissimilar.”

Early interpretations offer different explanations of what the two descriptions concern.

One explains that olives and pomegranates, or their varieties, may resemble one another in leaves or appearance yet differ in fruit, color, or taste.

Another explains that one kind of fruit can resemble another in some features while differing in others.

For example, two pomegranates may look similar in shape and rind color, while one tastes sweet and the other sour.

These examples help illustrate natural variety.

However, the Qur’anic phrase does not explicitly name sweetness, sourness, or rind color.

They are observable examples, not lexical meanings of rummān or details all expressly stated in the verse.

The difference between mushtabih and mutashābih

The verse contains two forms of SH B H.

Mushtabih belongs to Form VIII, while mutashābih belongs to Form VI.

They share a semantic field of resemblance.

In some uses, ishtibāh describes such close resemblance that distinguishing things becomes difficult.

Tashābuh likewise concerns resemblance between things.

The morphological difference alone does not justify an absolute rule making one always complete resemblance and the other always partial resemblance.

Both forms occur in Al-Anʿām 6:99, whose general subject includes similarities and differences among vegetation.

In Al-Anʿām 6:141, both parts use the form mutashābih.

This morphological difference matters to a study of SH B H but does not alter the name of the pomegranate.

انْظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ

The verse continues with a call to observe the fruit and its ripening.

Thamar belongs to TH M R and concerns fruit or a plant’s yield.

Yanʿ concerns fruit ripening and reaching maturity.

These words relate contextually to pomegranate but do not belong to R M N.

Like other fruit-bearing plants, the pomegranate tree progresses from fruit formation and growth to ripening.

During this process, the fruit’s color, size, texture, and flavor can change.

These changes are observable natural examples that help explain the verse’s call to look.

The word rummān itself, however, does not mean ripening, changing color, or reaching maturity.

The Qur’an speaks of bearing and ripening fruit through separate words.

The root’s meaning and the fruit’s growth and ripening must therefore not be merged.

Pomegranate as an example of creation’s variety

This verse places pomegranates alongside palms, grapes, olives, and grains.

It does not mean that all these plants share the same form and structure.

Rather, it draws attention to growth, variety, resemblance, and difference in natural produce.

Trees may all draw on water and soil while producing different fruits.

Pomegranates and olives differ in fruit type, taste, and use.

This variety is among the signs offered for reflection.

It does not follow that every plant’s name contains a hidden symbolic or philosophical meaning.

Here, pomegranate is a real, familiar fruit, and that natural reality belongs to the verse’s expression.

5. Al-Anʿām 6:141: Pomegranates, enjoying produce, and respecting others’ rights

Verse text

Al-Anʿām 6:141

وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنْشَأَ جَنَّاتٍ مَعْرُوشَاتٍ وَغَيْرَ مَعْرُوشَاتٍ وَالنَّخْلَ وَالزَّرْعَ مُخْتَلِفًا أُكُلُهُ وَالزَّيْتُونَ وَالرُّمَّانَ مُتَشَابِهًا وَغَيْرَ مُتَشَابِهٍ ۚ كُلُوا مِنْ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَآتُوا حَقَّهُ يَوْمَ حَصَادِهِ ۖ وَلَا تُسْرِفُوا ۚ إِنَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُسْرِفِينَ

wa-huwa alladhī anshaʾa jannātin maʿrūshātin wa-ghayra maʿrūshātin wa-al-nakhla wa-al-zarʿa mukhtalifan ukuluhu wa-al-zaytūna wa-al-rummāna mutashābihan wa-ghayra mutashābihin kulū min thamarihi idhā athmara wa-ātū ḥaqqahu yawma ḥaṣādihi wa-lā tusrifū innahu lā yuḥibbu al-musrifīna

Al-Anʿām 6:141

Translation:

“He is the One who brought into being gardens, trellised and untrellised, palms and crops with differing produce, and olives and pomegranates, similar and dissimilar. Eat of their fruit when they bear fruit, and give its due on the day of harvest. Do not be excessive; He does not love those who are excessive.”

The meaning of wa-al-rummān here

وَالرُّمَّانَ again denotes pomegranates.

The word appears alongside olives, palms, crops, and various gardens.

There is no new lexical meaning compared with Al-Anʿām 6:99.

The contexts nevertheless differ.

Al-Anʿām 6:99 focuses more on vegetation growing, bearing fruit, and ripening.

Al-Anʿām 6:141 adds eating the fruit, giving its due at harvest, and avoiding excess to the creation and variety of produce.

The fruit’s name thus appears within a context of human enjoyment and responsibility.

Rummān itself does not mean the produce’s due, charitable spending, or avoiding excess.

Those ideas are expressed by the verse’s separate commands.

كُلُوا مِنْ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ

This phrase calls for enjoying the fruit.

It presents the named fruits as blessings available for human use.

Pomegranates are one clear example of that enjoyment.

The call to eat does not mean that every person must eat every kind of fruit named in the Qur’an.

Nor does liking or disliking a fruit’s flavor determine its permissibility.

Someone may like pomegranates or dislike them, finding them sweet, sour, or unsuitable to personal taste.

These personal and cultural differences are separate from the lexical meaning of rummān.

وَآتُوا حَقَّهُ يَوْمَ حَصَادِهِ

After calling for eating the fruit, the verse speaks of giving its due on harvest day.

This phrase clarifies the connection between enjoying produce and human responsibility.

Agricultural produce is not presented solely as enjoyment for its owner or harvester; the Qur’an mentions a due to be given at harvest.

The exact amount, manner of payment, and implementation details cannot be derived from the fruit’s name.

Nor does rummān itself establish a special rule for pomegranates absent from other produce.

The verse names gardens, crops, and trees before stating the command concerning their yield.

Determining the rule’s precise scope belongs to a full analysis of the sentence and related verses.

For this root study, the relevant point is that pomegranates appear among the produce, while the verse discusses enjoyment and respecting its due.

وَلَا تُسْرِفُوا: Avoiding excess

The passage about produce ends with a prohibition of excess.

Isrāf belongs to S R F and concerns exceeding bounds.

The prohibition stands alongside eating fruit and giving its due.

People thus bear responsibility for their enjoyment of blessings and treatment of produce.

The pomegranate itself is not an intrinsic sign of excess or moderation.

Someone may be excessive in consuming or harvesting any crop.

The ethical assessment concerns human behavior, not the fruit’s name.

Wider context: Criticism of unsupported prohibitions

In Al-Anʿām 6:136, Al-Anʿām 6:137, Al-Anʿām 6:138, Al-Anʿām 6:139, and Al-Anʿām 6:140, the Qur’an criticizes unsupported attributions to The God concerning crops, livestock, assigned shares, and food rules.

Al-Anʿām 6:140 also condemns those who prohibit what The God has provided by falsely attributing their claims to Him.

Al-Anʿām 6:141 then speaks of creating gardens, produce, and trees, and calls for eating their fruit and giving its due.

This continuity matters.

Natural produce such as olives and pomegranates is presented as created blessings available for enjoyment.

Nearby verses, by contrast, criticize prohibitions without divine grounds.

Nevertheless, this root study should not become a comprehensive investigation of lawful and unlawful foods.

The lexical point is clear: the pomegranate’s name contains no meaning from which a religious prohibition or particular legal privilege can be extracted.

Practical rulings are understood from relevant passages, not from imagined meanings of the fruit’s name.

6. Pomegranates in Al-Raḥmān 55:68 and related vocabulary

Al-Raḥmān 55:68

Verse text:

فِيهِمَا فَاكِهَةٌ وَنَخْلٌ وَرُمَّانٌ

fīhimā fākihatun wa-nakhlun wa-rummānun

Al-Raḥmān 55:68

Translation:

“In them both are fruit, palms, and pomegranates.”

The meaning of wa-rummān

وَرُمَّانٌ combines the conjunction wa- and the noun rummān.

Unlike the two occurrences in Al-Anʿām, it has no definite article here and is nominative.

Rummān is indefinite in this construction and denotes pomegranate as a kind of fruit.

Differences in case or definiteness do not change the fruit name’s basic meaning.

All three Qur’anic occurrences mean pomegranate.

Here, however, it appears among the blessings of two gardens in the Hereafter.

Fīhimā: The two gardens

Al-Raḥmān 55:62 states:

وَمِنْ دُونِهِمَا جَنَّتَانِ

wa-min dūnihimā jannatāni

Al-Raḥmān 55:62

Meaning: “And besides those two are two other gardens.”

Subsequent verses describe these two gardens.

Al-Raḥmān 55:64 describes them as dark green.

Al-Raḥmān 55:66 speaks of two gushing springs within them.

Al-Raḥmān 55:68 then names fruit, palms, and pomegranates.

Fīhimā refers back to those two gardens.

Pomegranate forms part of their picture of blessing and enjoyment.

Its name alone does not specify the number of trees, fruit size, colors, or precise manner of eating.

Are palms and pomegranates excluded from fruit?

One lexical question arises from the conjunction wa-.

The verse says:

فَاكِهَةٌ وَنَخْلٌ وَرُمَّانٌ

fākihatun wa-nakhlun wa-rummānun

Al-Raḥmān 55:68

This means “fruit, palms, and pomegranates.” At first glance, separate mention after fākihah might suggest that palms and pomegranates fall outside fruit in the Qur’an.

Arabic grammar does not require that conclusion.

Arabic may first state a general category and then name particular instances separately for emphasis.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:238 first mentions the prayers, then separately names الصَّلَاةِ الْوُسْطَىٰ, the middle prayer.

Its mention after the prayers does not place it outside their category.

Likewise, Al-Baqarah 2:98 names Gabriel and Michael after the angels, although they belong to that group.

Early lexical discussion also applies this grammatical point to فاكهة ونخل ورمان.

It rejects the claim that merely coordinating palms and pomegranates with fākihah proves their exclusion from fruit.

Thus, separate mention after fākihah does not by itself establish that pomegranate is not fruit.

The construction may highlight particular familiar blessings.

Does naming pomegranate establish absolute superiority?

Pomegranates are named separately here alongside palms.

This gives them a specific place in the image of the garden’s blessings.

It does not establish that they surpass every other fruit nutritionally, therapeutically, or spiritually.

The verse offers no such ranking.

Nor does naming a fruit in the gardens of the Hereafter make eating it in this world a distinct act of worship.

Pomegranate is one of the blessings introduced.

Its name supplies no further details about size, quality, or extraordinary properties.

Are otherworldly pomegranates exactly like earthly ones?

Al-Raḥmān 55:68 uses a name familiar to its audience.

Rummān evokes the pomegranate fruit for the reader.

This concrete linguistic image should not be removed.

Yet a shared name does not establish that every physical property in the Hereafter exactly matches earthly pomegranates.

Conversely, there is no warrant for claiming that the fruit here is only a spiritual symbol without any reality as fruit.

The Qur’an names pomegranates alongside the two gardens’ other blessings.

The basic translation should therefore remain “pomegranates,” without asserting details of their otherworldly reality beyond the verse.

R M N (rummān) and R M M (ramm)

These families resemble one another in their letters.

One early analysis proposes a morphological connection between rummān and R M M.

R M M has established uses such as decaying, deteriorating, and repairing.

Under R M N, the clear Qur’anic meaning is pomegranate.

Even accepting a possible historical connection does not permit translating pomegranate as something decayed or restored.

Nor does the fruit’s cluster of seeds establish derivation from the verb ramma.

This boundary should remain clear in root research.

R M N (rummān) and TH M R (thamar)

Thamar concerns fruit and a plant’s yield in the Qur’an.

Rummān names a particular kind of fruit.

Al-Anʿām 6:99 and Al-Anʿām 6:141 speak of fruit after naming plants and produce such as pomegranates.

Pomegranate can therefore be an instance of thamar, but not every thamar is pomegranate.

Its name does not itself mean bearing fruit, ripening, or harvesting.

Those actions and concepts are expressed by separate words.

R M N (rummān) and F K H (fākihah)

Fākihah names fruit; the broader F K H family also concerns pleasant enjoyment.

Rummān names a particular fruit.

The two words appear together in Al-Raḥmān 55:68.

This illustrates that a general category and a particular instance can be coordinated in one sentence.

Their semantic scope is nevertheless different.

Fākihah is broader; rummān identifies a particular kind of fruit.

Their coexistence should not be treated as a contradiction or evidence excluding pomegranate from fruit.

R M N (rummān) and Z Y T (zaytūn)

Olives and pomegranates appear together in Al-Anʿām 6:99 and Al-Anʿām 6:141.

They name familiar trees and fruits.

They differ, however, in fruit type and natural uses.

Olives are fruits from which oil is also obtained.

Pomegranates contain many seeds within a relatively firm rind.

These features identify the referents without transferring one word’s meaning to the other.

Their juxtaposition in the Qur’an likewise does not imply identical nutritional or therapeutic properties.

R M N (rummān) and N KH L (nakhl)

Nakhl denotes date palms.

Palms and pomegranates appear together in Al-Raḥmān 55:68.

They differ in tree form, fruit type, and natural features.

Both nevertheless belong to the passage’s plant blessings.

Al-Anʿām also names palms and pomegranates alongside other produce.

Their juxtaposition illustrates natural variety, but neither name alone establishes a ruling or the other’s absolute superiority.

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan and conclusion

Suggested translations of the Qur’anic forms

وَالرُّمَّانَ — Al-Anʿām 6:99

Suitable equivalent: “and pomegranates.”

“And olives and pomegranates” is a natural and precise rendering of وَالزَّيْتُونَ وَالرُّمَّانَ.

The fruit’s name should be preserved without unnecessarily adding a tree, color, or particular variety.

وَالرُّمَّانَ — Al-Anʿām 6:141

Suitable equivalent: “and pomegranates.”

The same meaning applies here as in the first occurrence.

The second verse’s context of the produce’s due and avoiding excess does not alter the fruit’s name.

وَرُمَّانٌ — Al-Raḥmān 55:68

Suitable equivalent: “and pomegranates.”

“In them both are fruit, palms, and pomegranates” preserves the meaning of فِيهِمَا فَاكِهَةٌ وَنَخْلٌ وَرُمَّانٌ.

The basic translation likewise needs no added descriptions of the otherworldly fruit’s size or quality.

Conclusion and final definition

Rummān names the pomegranate fruit and, in general usage, its tree.

Rummānah denotes one fruit, while rummān can denote the kind or a collection of pomegranates.

Recorded forms include marmanah, rummānah, and figurative uses of the fruit’s name.

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī’s poetry also shows that the fruit and its shape were known in pre-Qur’anic Arabic.

The main derivational question nevertheless concerns the word’s radical letters.

One early analysis treats nūn as added and connects the structure with R M M.

Another treats nūn as radical and assigns the root R M N.

R M N is also recorded as a separate lexical entry without an abstract meaning beyond the fruit’s name.

This disagreement should be preserved in scholarly research.

Neither analysis authorizes translating pomegranate as decay, repair, gathering seeds, or another feature.

Rummān occurs three times in the Qur’an.

Al-Anʿām 6:99 names it among natural produce and signs of plant growth and variety.

Al-Anʿām 6:141 places it within enjoying produce, giving its due at harvest, and avoiding excess.

Al-Raḥmān 55:68 describes it among the blessings of two gardens in the Hereafter.

The fruit name’s meaning remains constant in all three; differences come from their contexts.

Final definition of R M N:

In Qur’anic classification, R M N is a limited family whose clear meaning is the pomegranate fruit and tree.

Rummān is a genus noun; rummānah denotes one fruit.

Early lexicographers disagreed about whether nūn is radical or added. No unproven historical underlying meaning should therefore be invented.

In the Qur’an, rummān does not itself name ethical behavior, convey a symbol, or prescribe a special rule for eating or using fruit.

It names a familiar fruit appearing in natural signs, in the produce’s due and human responsibility, and in blessings of the Hereafter.

The most suitable rendering in all three Qur’anic occurrences is simply “pomegranates.”

Forqan’s research should distinguish the fruit’s name, disagreement over word structure, the fruit’s natural features, and the verses’ contextual messages.

This preserves the Qur’an’s clear meaning without constructing unsupported etymologies or interpretations from the fruit’s shape, seed count, similar letters, or figurative uses.
