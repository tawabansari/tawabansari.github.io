In early Arabic, Q S R denotes overpowering by force, defeating another, and making someone act against their wishes. The family also describes powerful beings and people capable of overcoming others.

Historical and literary witness

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

إِذَا مَا هَتَفْنَا هَتْفَةً فِي نَدِيِّنَا / أَتَانَا الرِّجَالُ الصَّائِدُونَ الْقَسَاوِرُ

Whenever we raise a call in our assembly, the strong hunting men come to us.

Al-qasāwir here describes strong men. Different versions transmit words other than al-ṣāʾidūn, but qasāwir retains the sense of strength and the ability to confront others.

The date of composition is unknown, so the verse cannot establish a securely pre-Islamic usage. Its transmission under Labīd’s name and classical Arabic evidence nevertheless illuminate the root’s semantic field.

Forms examined: قَسَرَ (qasara), يَقْسِرُ (yaqsiru), قَسْر (qasr), قَاسِر (qāsir), مَقْسُور (maqsūr), اِقْتَسَرَ (iqtasara), اِقْتِسَار (iqtisār), قَسْوَر (qaswar), قَسْوَرَة (qaswarah), and قَسَاوِر (qasāwir).

Qur’anic form: قَسْوَرَة (qaswarah), occurring once, in Al-Muddaththir 74:51. The other verses quoted below explain its context; they are not additional occurrences of Q S R.

1. The root’s core meaning

The Arabic expression qasarahu ʿalā l-amr means making someone do something against their wishes. Iqtasarahu similarly means overpowering and defeating someone by force.

When someone uses superior strength to force another to act against their wishes, the action falls within the sense of qasr.

Qāsir denotes the person exercising such force; maqsūr denotes the person subjected to it.

Contemporary Arabic and Persian expressions for forced movement, migration, and displacement likewise describe motion or change imposed on a person or body by an external force.

Qaswarah belongs to the branch concerning strength and dominance. Arabic uses it for a lion, powerful men, and a group of hunters or archers.

These uses share the idea of a force capable of overcoming another creature or provoking a response.

Lexicons also record the beginning of the night and a kind of plant among the senses of qaswarah or qaswar. Their historical relationship to dominance is unclear, and they do not determine the Qur’anic usage.

2. Qaswarah: a lion or a band of hunters?

Qaswarah is one Arabic name for a lion, consistent with its strength and ability to overpower other animals.

The word is not confined to lions. Early Arabic sources also use it for a group of hunters, especially archers hunting wild animals.

The word and its plural qasāwir also describe strong and courageous men.

Some early explanations associate qaswarah with human voices and the noise that frightens wild animals away.

Morphologically, qaswarah is analyzed on the pattern faʿwalah and associated with Q S R. This analysis fits the sense of strength and dominance.

The root’s lexical meaning should therefore not be restricted to one animal’s name. The lion is one referent described through this word family.

3. Qaswarah in the Qur’an: fleeing from the reminder

The root’s only Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Muddaththir 74:51. Its context begins with a question about turning away from the reminder:

فَما لَهُم عَنِ التَّذكِرَةِ مُعرِضينَ

Fa-mā lahum ʿani l-tadhkirati muʿriḍīn.

What is the matter with them, turning away from the reminder?

Al-Muddaththir 74:49

كَأَنَّهُم حُمُرٌ مُستَنفِرَةٌ

Kaʾannahum ḥumurun mustanfirah.

As though they were startled wild asses,

Al-Muddaththir 74:50

فَرَّت مِن قَسوَرَةٍ

Farrat min qaswarah.

that have fled from a lion.

Al-Muddaththir 74:51

A separate reading of Al-Muddaththir 74:49

Al-Muddaththir 74:49 first identifies the conduct being criticized: turning away from the reminder. Muʿriḍīn is a circumstantial expression describing their state, and the question expresses reproach and astonishment rather than requesting an unknown fact. The following comparison illustrates the intensity of their aversion; it does not mean that the divine reminder is itself a destructive threat.

The image contains three distinct elements: being startled, fleeing, and the threatening agent.

Mustanfirah, from N F R, describes the animals’ alarm or flight. Farrat, from F R R, describes their fleeing movement. Qaswarah denotes the powerful being or group from which they flee.

Qur’anic readings include mustanfirah, with kasrah on the fāʾ, meaning fleeing or startled, and mustanfarah, with fatḥah, meaning frightened away. Both preserve the image of animals fleeing a threatening agent.

If qaswarah is translated as lion, the image is wild asses fleeing a predator. If translated as a band of hunters, it depicts animals fleeing people who pursue them.

Both interpretations have lexical support and fit the context.

Why does a person flee from a reminder?

A wild ass fleeing a lion is escaping a real threat to its life. Such flight is a natural response of self-preservation.

The people described here, however, flee from a reminder: speech inviting them to recognize truth and reconsider their conduct.

This is the difference between the situation being described and the image used to describe it. The divine reminder does not come to destroy people, yet some turn away as though facing a mortal threat.

The Qur’an continues:

بَل يُريدُ كُلُّ امرِئٍ مِنهُم أَن يُؤتىٰ صُحُفًا مُنَشَّرَةً

Bal yurīdu kullu mriʾin minhum an yuʾtā ṣuḥufan munashsharah.

Rather, each one of them wants to be given unfolded writings.

Al-Muddaththir 74:52

كَلّا بَل لا يَخافونَ الـٔاخِرَةَ

Kallā bal lā yakhāfūna l-ākhirah.

No indeed! Rather, they do not fear the Hereafter.

Al-Muddaththir 74:53

These verses show that the issue is not simply failure to receive the reminder. Their response to it and disregard for the consequences of their actions are also at issue.

Similar conduct appears in Noah’s account. Nuh 71:6 describes his call increasing his people’s flight, and Nuh 71:7 describes them putting their fingers in their ears and persisting in their stance.

Instead of examining whether a statement is true, a person may try not to hear it, because accepting it could require reconsidering beliefs, attachments, and a way of life.

Despite this aversion, the Qur’an continues to make the reminder available:

كَلّا إِنَّهُ تَذكِرَةٌ

Kallā innahu tadhkirah.

No indeed! It is a reminder.

Al-Muddaththir 74:54

فَمَن شاءَ ذَكَرَهُ

Fa-man shāʾa dhakarah.

So whoever wills may take heed of it.

Al-Muddaththir 74:55

4. Comparison with related roots

Q S R (qasr) and Q H R (qahr)

Qahr and qasr are close in Arabic: both concern dominance and overpowering.

Qasr, especially in qasarahu ʿalā l-amr, emphasizes applying force to make another act against their wishes.

Qahr has a wider field of dominance, sovereignty, and overcoming. The Qur’an describes The God as al-Qāhir and al-Qahhār, but uses no derivative of Q S R as a divine description.

Q S R (qasr) and K R H (ikrāh)

K R H concerns disliking, finding something unwelcome, and being made to act against one’s wishes.

Ikrāh and qasr overlap in compulsion. Qasr emphasizes the force exerted to overpower, while ikrāh emphasizes making someone act against their will. This is a useful comparison of emphasis, not a rigid boundary in every usage.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 and Yunus 10:99 use K R H to discuss compulsion in religion and faith.

Q S R (qasr) and GH L B (ghalabah)

Ghalabah denotes prevailing or gaining the upper hand. This may occur in battle, competition, argument, or other settings.

Qasr, in its principal branch, is more specifically associated with dominance through force and pressure.

A sound argument may overcome another argument without forcing its advocate to accept the conclusion.

Q S R (qasr) and Q S W (qaswah)

Despite their similar appearance, these families have different roots and meanings.

Qaswah concerns hardness, inflexibility, and resistance to an influence; qasr concerns overpowering and applying force to another.

Hardness of heart in the Qur’an concerns a heart resistant to truth and reminders. Qaswarah in Al-Muddaththir is the threatening agent from which animals flee.

Their resemblance does not make qaswarah mean hardness of heart or cruelty.

5. Translation guidance for Forqan

In general Arabic usage, render qasr as overpowering by force, compulsion, or making another act against their wishes.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:51, lion or predatory lion conveys a supported reading in natural language. Preserve the alternative, a band of hunters or archers, in the word’s explanation.

Brief root meaning: Overpowering by force, defeating, and compelling another against their wishes; in the nominal branch, a powerful being or group capable of overcoming and hunting.

Concluding synthesis

Q S R concerns strength, dominance, force, and compelling another.

Qaswarah has been used for a lion, hunters, or powerful men. The Qur’an employs it in an image of wild asses fleeing a threatening agent.

The criticism is identified first in Al-Muddaththir 74:49: turning away from the reminder. Al-Muddaththir 74:50 and Al-Muddaththir 74:51 illustrate the intensity of that aversion through animal flight. The comparison concerns the manner of turning away, not a claim that the reminder is harmful.

An animal flees a threat to its life; the people addressed flee a reminder that can help them correct their lives. The subsequent verses return attention to their response, accountability in the Hereafter, and the continuing availability of the reminder.
