Wabar: animal hair and fleece, especially camel fleece, and its use in clothing and household goods

1. Meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic root و ب ر concerns the hairy covering and fleece of certain animals. وَبَر names natural hairs and fibers growing on animals such as camels, rabbits, and others, forming a covering for their bodies.

In familiar Arabic usage, وَبَر especially denotes camel fleece: the covering that grows on the animal's skin and can be used to make yarn, fabric, clothing, and other goods.

Arabic has different words for animal coverings. صُوف is especially associated with sheep's wool, وَبَر with camel fleece, and شَعْر with hair, particularly goat hair.

These names reflect familiar features of the animals' coverings and their uses in people's lives.

The division is not absolute. Arabic sources also record وَبَر for the hair of rabbits, foxes, and other animals. The meaning is therefore not restricted to camels, although camels were a prominent example in Arab life.

Consider sheep's wool, typically a dense mass of crimped fibers. A camel's coat can contain coarser hairs and softer undercoat. Goat hair has traditionally been used for fabrics, ropes, and tent coverings.

Arabic had familiar names for these coverings without always drawing the precise boundaries of modern biology.

In its animal-covering branch, وَبَر primarily means fleece or a hairy coat: it does not mean the skin, every kind of wool, or the product later made from those fibers.

A witness from poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī contains these lines:

الوَاهِبُ المِائَةَ الأَبْكَارَ زَيَّنَهَا

al-wāhibu al-miʾata al-abkāra zayyanahā

سَعْدَانُ تُوضِحَ فِي أَوْبَارِهَا اللِّبَدِ

saʿdānu tūḍiḥa fī awbārihā al-libadi

“The giver of a hundred young camels, adorned by the saʿdān of Tūḍiḥ in their matted coats.”

The poet praises someone who gives valuable young camels to others.

Saʿdān is a plant eaten by camels, and Tūḍiḥ is a place where it grows.

The image presents camels nourished by good grazing and describes their coats as part of their appearance.

أَوْبَارِهَا is the plural of وَبَر with a feminine pronoun, referring to these camels' coats.

اللِّبَد concerns matted, compressed fleece. Some early explanations also mention saʿdān burrs adhering to the coats.

This witness uses أَوْبَار for camels' actual hair covering, not their skin, meat, or a product later obtained from them.

The verse is recorded in the famous poem attributed to al-Nābighah, with differences among transmitted versions. Examples include المائة الجرجور instead of المائة الأبكار, and في الأوبار ذي اللبد instead of the closing phrase.

These variants do not materially change the meaning of أَوْبَار.

Al-Nābighah was a pre-Islamic poet, and the verse has an established place in the tradition attributed to him. As with other poetry transmitted from that period, its exact composition date and earliest wording are not independently certain.

What the historical witness establishes

The poem uses أَوْبَار for camels' coats. The same sense occurs in Al-Naḥl 16:80.

Quranic usage therefore continues an established early Arabic semantic field.

This meaning does not require treating the word as a hidden concept or a new metaphor.

وَبَر is a tangible natural material growing on certain animals and useful to humans.

2. Forms and semantic branches

Important recorded Arabic forms include:

  • وَبَر: fleece or the hairy covering of certain animals.
  • وَبَرَة: a single hair or unit of fleece.
  • أَوْبَار: the plural of wabar; fleeces or hairy coverings.
  • وَبِرَ: became hairy or had abundant fleece.
  • وَبِر: an animal with abundant fleece.
  • أَوْبَر: thickly covered with hair or fleece.
  • وَبِرَة and وَبْرَاء: feminine forms for an animal with abundant fleece.
  • وَبَّرَ: in certain uses, concealed or erased its tracks; elsewhere, stayed in a place for a time.
  • تَوْبِير: concealing or erasing tracks, in uses concerning certain animals.
  • وَبْر: a small furry animal inhabiting rocky or arid terrain.
  • بَنَاتُ أَوْبَر: a kind of small desert fungus recorded in dictionaries.

These forms do not all occur in the Quran. It uses only أَوْبَار with a conjunction and attached pronoun.

The semantic core

In the main branch, وَبَر names the hairy covering of certain animals.

It consists of strands growing on the skin, which may be dense or sparse, soft or relatively coarse.

Three features matter for understanding this use.

First, wabar belongs to the animal's natural body covering.

Second, it differs from the skin. Skin is a bodily layer, while wabar consists of hairs and fibers growing on it.

Third, wabar can be separated from the animal and used to make goods; those manufactured products are not its primary meaning.

If someone weaves camel fleece into cloth, the cloth is a product made from wabar. The word originally names the fibers, not the fabric or garment.

This matters in Al-Naḥl 16:80: the Quran first names the natural material, then the goods made from it.

أَهْلُ الْوَبَر: a social expression

Arabic used أَهْلُ الْوَبَر for desert dwellers whose lives involved livestock, migration, and tents made from animal fibers.

أَهْلُ الْمَدَر, by contrast, generally referred to settled people living in houses built from materials such as clay and mud brick.

The distinction arose from ways of life and shelter-building materials.

A family moving between pastures with its livestock needs shelter that can be dismantled, carried, and erected again.

Someone living for a long period in a town or village may instead build a permanent house of brick and stone.

أَهْلُ الْوَبَر is understandable through this connection between animal hair, tents, and nomadic life.

It does not establish that all desert dwellers made tents exclusively from camel fleece or depended on one material for everything.

It is a social designation, not a complete definition of all desert lifestyles.

Tawbīr in descriptions of animals

Early Arabic sources use وَبَّرَتِ الأَرْنَبُ of a rabbit moving while fleeing in a manner that makes its footprints difficult to see.

The usage is also reported for other animals.

It is explained as hiding tracks by moving over hard or rocky ground.

Some lexicographers connected it with the soft hair on the animal's feet and its manner of movement.

This suggests a possible connection with wabar as hair covering, but does not establish the verb's historical origin.

Concealing tracks must not become the meaning of Quranic أَوْبَار.

وَبْر: an animal name

وَبْر, with a vowelless ب, also names a small furry animal living in rocky regions.

In modern zoological usage, the name generally refers to hyraxes.

Early Arabic sources likewise describe it as a small animal with a hairy coat.

Its name's similarity to وَبَر may relate to that covering, but the historical reason for the name requires independent evidence.

In any case, أَوْبَارِهَا in Al-Naḥl 16:80 is the plural of wabar meaning fleece, not the plural of this animal's name.

بَنَاتُ أَوْبَر: small fungi

In early Arabic, بَنَاتُ أَوْبَر named a type of small desert fungus.

Lexical descriptions characterize them as small and downy-looking.

Their name may relate to that appearance, but does not make fungi or plants the original meaning of every use of و ب ر.

This is a specialized designation absent from the Quran.

3. Quranic use: Al-Naḥl 16:80

The root occurs once in the Quran.

Al-Naḥl 16:80

وَاللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ بُيُوتِكُمْ سَكَنًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ جُلُودِ الْأَنْعَامِ بُيُوتًا تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا يَوْمَ ظَعْنِكُمْ وَيَوْمَ إِقَامَتِكُمْ ۙ وَمِنْ أَصْوَافِهَا وَأَوْبَارِهَا وَأَشْعَارِهَا أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

wa-allāhu jaʿala lakum min buyūtikum sakanan wa-jaʿala lakum min julūdi al-anʿāmi buyūtan tastakhiffūnahā yawma ẓaʿnikum wa-yawma iqāmatikum wa-min aṣwāfihā wa-awbārihā wa-ashʿārihā athāthan wa-matāʿan ilā ḥīn

Al-Naḥl 16:80

“And The God made your houses places of rest for you, and made for you houses from the skins of livestock, which you find light on the day of your departure and the day of your stay; and from their wool, fleece, and hair, furnishings and enjoyment for a time.”

The verse concerns provisions in nature made available by The God for human life.

It begins by presenting houses as places of dwelling and rest.

It then mentions shelters made from livestock skins for use during travel and residence.

Three animal coverings follow:

أَصْوَافِهَا, أَوْبَارِهَا, and أَشْعَارِهَا.

These materials are used to make various goods for human life.

The meaning of وَأَوْبَارِهَا

وَأَوْبَارِهَا has three components.

وَ is a conjunction.

أَوْبَارِ is the plural of وَبَر, fleece or animal hair covering.

هَا is an attached feminine singular pronoun referring here to الْأَنْعَامِ.

Grammatically, أَوْبَارِهَا is coordinated with أَصْوَافِهَا in مِنْ أَصْوَافِهَا وَأَوْبَارِهَا وَأَشْعَارِهَا.

مِنْ expresses the relationship between the raw material and what is made from it.

The expression thus concerns goods and equipment made available for human use from livestock wool, fleece, and hair.

أَوْبَار itself does not mean furniture, clothing, or tents. These are products that can be made from animal fleece.

The referent of هَا

In أَصْوَافِهَا, أَوْبَارِهَا, and أَشْعَارِهَا, هَا refers to الْأَنْعَامِ.

الْأَنْعَام denotes livestock in the Quran. Al-Anʿām 6:143 and Al-Anʿām 6:144 discuss sheep, goats, camels, and cattle within that group.

The pronoun in Al-Naḥl 16:80 therefore refers collectively to livestock, not necessarily to one particular animal.

The verse does not say that every animal in this group must have all three coverings in equal measure.

Sheep's wool, camel fleece, and goat hair are familiar examples of the three names.

The expression brings several sources of animal fibers together.

أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا: products made from fleece

After wool, fleece, and hair, the Quran mentions أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا.

أَثَاث comes from أ ث ث and here concerns household furnishings and equipment.

مَتَاع comes from م ت ع and concerns things people use or enjoy.

In pastoral and nomadic life, animal fibers can make mats, coverings, garments, ropes, blankets, and fabric.

Camel fleece, for example, can be cleaned and spun after collection, then made into fabric or coverings.

This illustrates natural material becoming useful goods through human labor and skill.

The stages of spinning, weaving, and manufacture are not all contained in the lexical meaning of wabar.

Wabar is the raw material.

أَثَاث and مَتَاع concern the goods and benefits derived from it.

إِلَىٰ حِينٍ: enjoyment for a time

The verse ends with إِلَىٰ حِينٍ.

حِين denotes a time or period whose exact length the word does not specify.

Here, people benefit for a time from what livestock provide.

A garment may wear out after years or an object need replacement. Human life and worldly enjoyment are also not everlasting.

These examples do not require restricting the direct translation of إِلَىٰ حِينٍ to “until death.”

The verse specifies no exact time.

Connections with surrounding passages

Al-Naḥl 16:5 mentions livestock's creation and their various benefits.

Al-Naḥl 16:6 describes their beauty when brought home and taken out to pasture.

Al-Naḥl 16:7 mentions carrying loads and assisting human travel.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 then discusses skins and hair coverings used for shelter and household goods.

Al-Naḥl 16:81 mentions shade, shelters, and clothing protecting people from heat and harm.

These verses show that livestock's benefits extend beyond meat and milk.

They also contribute to transport, clothing, manufactured goods, and the provisions of daily life.

Awbār is one natural resource people can use through their labor and skill.

4. Wabar, wool, hair, and skin

و ب ر and ص و ف: fleece and wool

صُوف means wool, especially the covering of sheep.

وَبَر is more often used for camel fleece and the coats of certain other animals.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 distinguishes them by naming أَصْوَاف and أَوْبَار separately.

In Persian, the ordinary word for wool can broadly cover the coats of different animals; “camel wool” is one example.

That is understandable in Persian, but translating both Arabic terms simply as “wool” would obscure their distinction.

The proposed rendering therefore distinguishes wool from fleece in this verse.

Their prevalent Arabic uses should not become the absolute claim that wabar never applies beyond camels or ṣūf beyond sheep.

و ب ر and ش ع ر: fleece and hair

شَعْر is a broad Arabic term for hair.

It applies to both human and animal hair.

In Al-Naḥl 16:80, أَشْعَار stands beside أَصْوَاف and أَوْبَار; goat hair is a prominent example in familiar livestock usage.

Wabar is also an animal's hair covering, but within this classification it more specifically names the fleece of certain animals.

The relationship can be described as follows:

Shaʿr is a more general name for hair.

Ṣūf denotes the wool of animals such as sheep.

Wabar is especially used for the fleece of animals such as camels.

They are not wholly unrelated substances: all come from animals' hair coverings, and their usages sometimes overlap.

Keeping three distinct names in Forqan's translation helps readers see the distinction.

و ب ر and ج ل د: fleece and skin

جِلْد concerns bodily skin.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 first mentions livestock skins, then separately names wool, fleece, and hair.

The material distinction is clear.

Skin belongs to the animal's bodily structure.

Wabar consists of the hairs and fibers growing on it.

An animal's skin can be made into leather, while its fleece can be collected for spinning and weaving without using the skin itself.

Both are useful, but they are lexically distinct.

The verse associates جُلُود with shelters and أَصْوَاف, أَوْبَار, and أَشْعَار with furnishings and useful goods.

This indicates prominent uses; it does not rule out using fleece for tents or skins for other goods.

و ب ر and غ ز ل: raw material and spinning

غَزْل concerns spinning fibers into yarn.

Al-Naḥl 16:92 describes a woman undoing her firmly spun thread.

غَزْلَهَا there concerns spinning and its product.

Wabar names material that can be spun.

Fabric production illustrates the difference.

First, fleece or wool is obtained from an animal.

The fibers are then cleaned and spun.

The yarn can subsequently become fabric, a blanket, or another product.

Wabar and ghazl thus denote different stages and concepts: natural material, and the work or product of spinning it.

أَوْبَار in Al-Naḥl 16:80 should not therefore be translated as “woven threads.”

و ب ر and ل ب د: fleece and matting

Arabic uses ل ب د for the matting and compression of materials such as hair and wool.

In the poem attributed to al-Nābighah, أَوْبَارِهَا اللِّبَدِ describes the camels' matted coats.

أَوْبَار names the material; اللِّبَد describes its condition.

An animal's fleece can be loose and soft or become compacted by moisture, pressure, and movement.

Wabar does not itself mean felted or compressed fleece.

That quality comes from its accompanying description.

5. Questions of interpretation and translation

Does wabar mean only camel fleece?

The camel is a prominent traditional example, but the word is not confined to it.

Early sources also apply wabar to rabbits, foxes, and other animals.

The primary definition should therefore be “fleece and the hairy covering of certain animals,” followed by an explanation that camels are a major familiar example in the Quranic context.

“Camel fleece” supplies contextual explanation; the direct equivalent of أَوْبَارِهَا is “their fleece.”

Is wabar the same as wool?

Ordinary Persian may use the terms similarly, but Al-Naḥl 16:80 places two independent names together.

Rendering both only as wool would conceal the Arabic distinction.

The distinction does not mean the materials have no similarities.

Both are natural animal fibers and may serve similar purposes in manufacturing.

Translation preserves the lexical distinction, while analysis explains their proximity and overlap.

Does awbār mean clothing or tents?

No. Wabar is material used to make clothing or tents, not the products' name.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 mentions natural materials followed by furnishings and useful goods.

The construction distinguishes raw material from what is made of it.

Wood and a wooden table illustrate the distinction. Similarly, wabar is raw material and fabric or a blanket can be its product.

Translating أَوْبَارِهَا as “their tents” or “their clothes” is therefore inaccurate.

Does using wabar require killing the animal?

Wabar means the animal's hair covering; the word itself entails no necessity to kill it.

Some animals' fleece can be obtained by shearing or collecting shed fibers.

Skin is a different material, and obtaining it can involve different methods.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 concerns useful natural resources, not precise instructions for obtaining each material.

Details of fiber collection or related rulings should not be derived from this verse alone.

Does naming wool, fleece, and hair exclude other materials?

No. The verse gives examples of natural provisions familiar in its audience's lives.

Naming them does not prevent using cotton, linen, plant fibers, or other materials for household goods.

Just as mentioning camels and livestock does not exclude other means of transport, mentioning wool and fleece does not limit every possible material for clothing and daily life to these examples.

The verse concerns recognizing the blessings and provisions available in creation.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for familiar forms include:

وَبَر: fleece; the hairy covering of certain animals.

وَبَرَة: a single hair or unit of fleece.

أَوْبَار: fleeces or hairy coverings.

وَبِرَ: became hairy or had abundant fleece.

وَبِر: thickly covered with fleece.

أَوْبَر: having abundant fleece.

وَبْر: in zoological use, a hyrax; a small furry animal.

تَوْبِير: in its specialized use, concealing or erasing tracks.

وَبَّرَ: concealed tracks in that context; in another use, stayed for a time.

For the sole Quranic form, وَأَوْبَارِهَا, the proposed equivalent is “and their fleece.”

The proposed rendering within Al-Naḥl 16:80 is:

“And from their wool, fleece, and hair, furnishings and enjoyment for a time.”

This rendering preserves the Quran's three independent names.

“Wool” for أَصْوَافِهَا, “fleece” for أَوْبَارِهَا, and “hair” for أَشْعَارِهَا convey the proposed distinction; the corresponding Persian terms likewise remain separate.

Using “camel wool” instead of fleece explains a familiar example but narrows the word's scope.

The Persian term for fur may also suggest skin with its hair attached or a manufactured product. The selected Persian equivalent for fleece is therefore more precise here.

7. Conclusion

The root's main branch concerns fleece and the hairy covering of certain animals.

Camels were a prominent early Arabic example. أَوْبَار in the poem attributed to al-Nābighah illustrates this in its description of camel coats.

Dictionaries record other forms for heavily furred animals, concealing tracks, remaining in a place, and certain animal and fungus names. These subsidiary uses should not be reduced to a definite single origin without historical evidence.

Only أَوْبَار occurs in the Quran.

In Al-Naḥl 16:80, it stands alongside أَصْوَاف and أَشْعَار as sources of animal fibers used for furnishings and household goods.

Wabar is the raw material, while furnishings and enjoyment concern the products and benefits derived from it.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its main branch, و ب ر denotes fleece and the hairy covering of certain animals, especially camels. These natural fibers grow on the skin and can be used for clothing, fabric, and household goods.

In its sole Quranic occurrence, أَوْبَارِهَا means “their fleece,” naming alongside wool and hair a useful natural resource for human life.
