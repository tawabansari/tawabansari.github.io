Finding fault, reproaching, and holding in contempt; being reproached and despised

Arabic DH ʾ M concerns finding fault with someone, reproaching, despising, or diminishing that person’s standing and worth. Some recorded uses also connect the family with expelling, disgracing, and driving someone away.

The Qur’an uses this root only once, as madhʾūman in Al-Aʿrāf, where The God responds to Iblīs’s defiance by commanding him to leave and describing him as madhʾūm and madḥūr.

Understanding the word requires distinguishing related concepts: fault and reproach, contempt, and driving away or exclusion. These meanings are connected in Arabic but not always identical. In this verse, madhʾūman and madḥūran come from different roots and convey different aspects of Iblīs’s condition.

1. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Dhaʾm: finding fault and despising

The family’s semantic core has been explained through dislike and fault. Arabic records dhaʾama for finding fault with, reproaching, or holding someone in contempt.

One early explanation understands dhaʾamtuhu as holding someone in contempt.

Arabic uses the following expressions:

Dhaʾamahu yadhʾamuhu dhaʾman: he found fault with, reproached, or despised him.

Huwa madhʾūm: he is reproached and held in contempt.

Al-dhaʾm likewise concerns fault, reproach, and contempt.

These uses go beyond simply identifying an undesirable characteristic: the verb can also express treating someone reproachfully.

Finding fault and actually having a fault are different concepts. Someone may genuinely have acted wrongly and deserve reproach, but one person’s reproach of another does not by itself establish that the judgment is correct.

The Qur’anic use differs because the reproach comes from The God within the account of Iblīs’s conduct. Its justice follows from the speaker and context, rather than from dhaʾm invariably denoting correct, just reproach in every Arabic use.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar says in an ode beginning “تَنَكَّرْتِ مِنَّا بَعْدَ مَعْرِفَةٍ لِمِي”:

فَإِنْ كُنْتَ لَا تَدْعُو إِلَى غَيْرِ نَافِعٍ
فَدَعْنِي وَأَكْرِمْ مَنْ بَدَا لَكَ وَاذْأَمِ

fa-in kunta lā tadʿū ilā ghayri nāfiʿin / fa-daʿnī wa-akrim man badā laka wa-dhʾami

Translation: If you call to nothing but what is beneficial, leave me alone; honor whomever you wish and reproach whomever you wish. Lā tadʿū ilā ghayri nāfiʿin denies calling to something unbeneficial; it must not be translated as calling only to something useless.

Wa-dhʾami is an imperative of this root, placed opposite akrim.

Akrim concerns honoring and respecting; wa-dhʾami contrasts with it through reproach and contempt.

This contrast is important for understanding the root. The poet asks to be left alone and tells the addressee to honor or reproach whomever he wishes. The verse alone does not establish that the poet avoids every difficult or unrewarding struggle: explanations of ghayri nāfiʿin also discuss a great undertaking whose harm is feared.

The next verse describes the poet’s readiness to respond to good and evil. The setting is therefore human relationships, praise, reproach, and social conflict.

The verse is preserved in early transmissions of Arabic poetry. Two variants are recorded: fa-dharnī instead of fa-daʿnī, and mā badā laka instead of man badā laka.

Neither changes the meaning of wa-dhʾami in this witness.

The verse demonstrates the root’s use in pre-Qur’anic poetic language. Here dhaʾm is neither a punishment’s name, an afterlife location, nor a ritual command, but a familiar verb of reproach and contempt within human relationships.

Other Arabic uses

Besides fault-finding and contempt, recorded Arabic uses also give dhaʾamahu the sense of driving away or expelling.

Ḥaqqarahu wa-ṭaradahu is another explanation: he despised him and drove him away.

Some explanations also connect dhaʾm with disgracing and humiliating.

Adhʾamtanī ʿalā kadhā is also attested in connection with aversion and compelling someone to do something unwelcome.

The lexical field is thus not confined to verbal reproach; it can extend toward contempt or exclusion.

This does not mean every dhaʾm necessarily entails physical expulsion. Exclusion and expulsion may accompany or follow reproach without being required components in every sentence.

The rare expression mā samiʿtu lahu dhaʾmatan is also recorded for not hearing even a word from someone. Without further evidence, this limited use should not simply be reduced to the core of reproach and contempt.

For the Qur’anic use, the most relevant strand remains finding fault, reproaching, and despising.

2. Madhʾūman in the Qur’an: reproaching Iblīs

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is Al-Aʿrāf 7:18:

قَالَ اخْرُجْ مِنْهَا مَذْءُومًا مَدْحُورًا لَمَنْ تَبِعَكَ مِنْهُمْ لَأَمْلَأَنَّ جَهَنَّمَ مِنْكُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

qāla ukhruj minhā madhʾūman madḥūran la-man tabiʿaka minhum la-amlaʾanna jahannama minkum ajmaʿīn

Translation: He said: “Get out of it, reproached and banished. Whoever among them follows you, I will surely fill Hell with you all.”

Madhʾūman is a passive participle of dhaʾama. It is accusative and functions as a circumstantial qualifier of the subject of the imperative ukhruj, “get out.”

Iblīs is thus commanded to leave in a state of being reproached.

The following madḥūran comes from D Ḥ R and concerns being driven away. It can be treated as another circumstantial qualifier of the same addressee. Other analyses make it an adjective qualifying madhʾūman or a circumstantial qualifier of its implicit pronoun. These syntactic differences do not change the core sense of expulsion.

The verse places two descriptions together: being reproached and being driven away.

Why is Iblīs called madhʾūm?

His preceding statements provide the context for this description.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:12, when The God asks why he did not prostrate, Iblīs replies that he is better than Adam, regarding himself as created from fire and Adam from clay.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:13, this arrogance leads to a command to descend and leave. The verse ends with al-ṣāghirīn, those humbled and brought low.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:14 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:15, Iblīs asks for a respite and receives one.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:16 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:17, however, he announces that he will lie in wait for human beings on The God’s straight path and approach them from different directions. He then says that most of them will not be found grateful.

Madhʾūman in Al-Aʿrāf 7:18 thus follows specific actions and statements: disobedience, a claim of superiority to Adam, arrogance, and the declared intention of misleading human beings.

The reproach is not presented without cause or independently of his behavior.

Those actions should nevertheless not all be placed within the lexical meaning of madhʾūm. The word concerns being reproached and despised; the preceding verses supply the reason.

The relationship between madhʾūman and madḥūran

Some Arabic uses of dhaʾm include expulsion, so explanations of madhʾūm and madḥūr may overlap.

The verse’s construction nevertheless allows a clear distinction to be preserved.

Madhʾūman emphasizes reproach and contempt: a condition in which someone has been subjected to fault-finding and reproach.

Madḥūran emphasizes being driven away and excluded.

The verse thus describes both Iblīs’s reproached condition and his expulsion.

This is not an absolute rule denying that dhaʾm ever means expulsion. Such a claim would conflict with attested Arabic usage.

More precisely, reproach and contempt suit madhʾūm here, while being driven away suits madḥūr. Their juxtaposition allows each description’s contribution to remain clear.

Does madhʾūm mean condemned to Hell?

The remainder of the verse speaks of filling Hell with Iblīs and those who follow him.

Madhʾūman by itself, however, means neither “an inhabitant of Hell” nor “condemned to eternal punishment.”

It conveys reproach and contempt. Hell and Iblīs’s followers are addressed in another part of the sentence.

The afterlife consequence should therefore not be inserted into the word’s lexical meaning.

La-man tabiʿaka minhum concerns following Iblīs. His reproach and his followers’ responsibility each require attention to their respective words and actions.

This distinction prevents a description of Iblīs from being indiscriminately extended to all human beings without considering their circumstances.

3. Distinctions from related roots

DH ʾ M (dhaʾama) and DH M M (dhamma): reproach and blame

Dhaʾm and dhamm are very close in meaning. Early lexical explanations sometimes use one to explain the other.

For instance, ʿābahu, “found fault with him,” and dhammahu, “blamed him,” occur together in explanations of dhaʾm. Some accounts also give dhaʾm a stronger degree of fault-finding than dhamm.

This difference in intensity should not be made an unvarying rule for every use of the two roots.

Morphologically, madhʾūm belongs to DH ʾ M, whereas madhmūm comes from DH M M.

Despite their close meanings, they are different words.

An important example occurs in Al-Isrāʾ 17:18, which has madhmūman madḥūran.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:18 has madhʾūman madḥūran; Al-Isrāʾ 17:18 has madhmūman madḥūran.

This parallel combination reveals considerable overlap between madhʾūm and madhmūm in reproach and contempt. Their different roots must nevertheless remain distinct in lexical study.

Arabic also records unhamzated forms such as dhām and dhīm. These phonetic and lexical variations do not make DH ʾ M and DH M M one morphological root.

DH ʾ M (dhaʾama) and D Ḥ R (daḥara): reproach and expulsion

Study of D Ḥ R

Daḥr concerns driving away, exclusion, and repulsion.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:18, this is directly related to ukhruj, “get out”: Iblīs receives the command to leave and is called madḥūr.

The main use of dhaʾm emphasizes finding fault, reproaching, and despising.

Someone may be reproached without being expelled. Someone may also be removed from a place without the act of removal itself necessarily judging that person’s moral worth.

Here both conditions occur together: Iblīs is reproached and driven away.

This matters in translation. Rendering both words simply as “expelled” would obscure one aspect of the verse’s wording.

DH ʾ M (dhaʾama) and Ṣ GH R (ṣaghura): reproach and humiliation

Study of Ṣ GH R

Al-Aʿrāf 7:13 places Iblīs among al-ṣāghirīn.

Ṣaghār in this use concerns lowliness and humiliation, whereas dhaʾm also includes reproach and fault-finding.

The concepts can coincide, as in the account of Iblīs.

They are nevertheless not fully synonymous. Humiliation may result from a judgment or treatment, while the main use of dhaʾm concerns reproach and finding fault.

Humiliation can therefore be mentioned when explaining madhʾūm, but “reproached” remains a direct and suitable equivalent.

4. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence, Persian nakūhīdah, “reproached,” is clear, natural, and faithful to the principal meaning.

Forqan’s current English translation of Al-Aʿrāf 7:18 reads:

He said, “Get out of it, despised and banished. Whoever among them follows you—I will surely fill Hell with you all.”

Installed translation of Al-Aʿrāf 7:18

The Persian translation preserves the distinction through “reproached” for madhʾūman and “driven away” for madḥūran; the installed English expresses the distinction through “despised” and “banished.”

“Held in contempt” or “reproached and despised” can also show part of madhʾūm’s semantic field. Multiple equivalents need not all be inserted for a single word in the direct translation.

There is therefore no need to change the existing translation.

The study can explain the connection between Iblīs’s reproached condition and his defiance and arrogance. “Arrogant,” “liar,” “one who misleads,” and “condemned to Hell,” however, should not replace madhʾūm itself: each concerns another concept or part of the context.

5. Conclusion

Arabic DH ʾ M concerns finding fault, reproaching, and despising. Some attested uses extend to humiliation, disgrace, and expulsion.

The poetry attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar shows dhaʾm used in pre-Qur’anic language for reproach in contrast to honoring. Reproach and contempt also matter in the Qur’anic use.

The root’s sole Qur’anic form is madhʾūman in Al-Aʿrāf 7:18. It describes Iblīs after disobedience, arrogance, and his declared hostility toward human beings.

Madḥūran in the same verse belongs to another root and conveys expulsion. Although the descriptions are related, preserving reproach and expulsion as distinct aspects better conveys the sentence.

Final definition: In its main use, DH ʾ M concerns finding fault with, reproaching, or despising someone. Madhʾūm is someone subjected to such reproach and contempt. Some Arabic uses also connect this meaning with disgrace and driving away, but expulsion is not necessary in every use.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:18, “reproached” is a precise, sufficient equivalent for madhʾūm. Its grounds come from the account of Iblīs, and its consequences from other parts of the verse. Neither should be inserted into the word’s lexical meaning without evidence.
