Qur’anic Root B Ṭ R

Exultation, arrogance, and ingratitude in responding to blessings and ability

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Qur’anic B Ṭ R describes an individual’s or community’s state of exultation and arrogance in response to blessings, prosperity, power, or resources. Instead of being accompanied by appreciation and responsibility, enjoyment of a blessing becomes an occasion for pride, excess, and misconduct.

Baṭar is more than happiness or a comfortable life. Someone may enjoy wealth, security, success, or ability without displaying it. The distinction lies in the response to what one possesses: being carried away by privilege, disregarding a blessing’s value and obligations, and behaving beyond proper bounds.

Arabic lexicons also record cutting or splitting for this root. Ibn Fāris treats that as the family’s principle and attempts to connect exultation and excess with it. The historical connection is not clear enough, however, to make splitting the direct meaning of every derivative without qualification.

The root occurs twice in two forms: بَطَرًا in Al-Anfāl 8:47 and بَطِرَتْ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58. The first is an accusative verbal noun; the second is a perfect verb. No derivative concerning splitting occurs in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two occurrences in two forms

1. The root’s meaning in Arabic

Lexicons use بَطِرَ for someone whose privilege or pride carries him beyond measured conduct. بَطَر names that condition.

Several lexical explanations clarify its semantic field.

First are الْأَشَر and شِدَّةُ الْمَرَح: intense, excessive exuberance. The person is not merely happy; pleasure and a sense of ability develop into arrogance and excessive behavior.

Second is الطُّغْيَانُ عِنْدَ النِّعْمَةِ, transgression when enjoying a blessing. The blessing itself is not condemned; the response to it is at issue.

Third is قِلَّةُ احْتِمَالِ النِّعْمَةِ. This is not a physical inability to bear something, but failure to respond properly to resources and their attendant responsibilities. Someone receives a blessing but cannot order his conduct appropriately.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī describes baṭar as a kind of دَهَش arising from mishandling a blessing, failing to fulfill its due, and directing it to an improper purpose. Here dahash is not ordinary surprise or bewilderment, but a loss of sound judgment through privilege.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, Mufradāt: defining baṭar

Lisān al-ʿArab records other uses, including liveliness, swaggering, bewilderment, confusion, and disliking something undeserving of dislike. These should not all be assembled as fixed components of the Qur’anic meaning. Some describe neighboring states; others are branches of the word’s wider usage.

The ethical branch can be summarized as exultation and arrogance arising from privilege or a sense of superiority, accompanied by ingratitude, excess, or ill-considered conduct.

This distinguishes baṭar from ordinary happiness without restricting every instance to wealth or one particular wrongdoing.

2. Splitting and the question of a physical image

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for B Ṭ R: الشَّقّ, splitting.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records بَطَرَ الشَّيْءَ, to split something. In this branch the action affects a body or something that can be cut or split.

To illustrate the early splitting sense, Ibn Fāris quotes a verse attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī:

شَكَّ الْفَرِيصَةَ بِالْمِدْرَى فَأَنْفَذَهَا
شَكَّ الْمُبَيْطِرِ إِذْ يَشْفِي مِنَ الْعَضَدِ

shakka al-farīṣata bi-al-midrā fa-anfadhahā / shakka al-mubayṭiri idh yashfī mina al-ʿaḍadi

Translation: “With his horn he pierced the flesh below its shoulder, driving it through, like an animal healer’s incision when treating a disease of the upper arm.”

Ibn Fāris and Lisān al-ʿArab: farīṣah and the upper-arm disease

The bull’s horn striking a dog’s body is compared with a healer cutting into a diseased place to treat it.

Versions of the verse differ. Some sources give طَعْنَ الْمُبَيْطِرِ instead of شَكَّ الْمُبَيْطِرِ. The wording used must therefore be identified when citing it.

The verse attests مُبَيْطِر in poetry attributed to a pre-Islamic poet. It does not itself use the verb بَطَرَ for splitting. Moreover, the origin of بَيْطَار has received different etymological explanations, including possible borrowing from another language.

Greek Horse Medicine in Arabic: the origin of bayṭār

The mere presence of مُبَيْطِر in poetry cannot therefore conclusively prove that every B Ṭ R derivative developed from splitting.

Ibn Fāris also connects ethical baṭar with splitting, explaining it as exceeding bounds in exuberance. The image is intelligible: conduct seems to break through its proper limit. But this is a proposed explanation of semantic relationships, rather than the word’s direct Qur’anic meaning.

Two points must remain distinct: lexicons record splitting, while Qur’anic usage emphasizes exultation, ingratitude, and excess. The historical relationship must not be presented more confidently than the evidence permits.

3. Important forms

بَطِرَ is a perfect verb meaning to become affected by baṭar: to adopt exultant, ill-considered conduct through privilege or a sense of superiority. With blessings, this may involve ingratitude and disregard for their due.

بَطَر is the verbal noun, denoting the condition of exultation and excess. بَطِر is an adjective describing someone in that state.

Causative أَبْطَرَ means to make someone affected by baṭar or carry him beyond moderation. Arabic can describe wealth and privilege making someone behave this way. This concerns resources contributing to the condition; it does not make possessing wealth inherently baṭar.

In the other lexical branch, بَطَرَ means to split something. The construction and object identify the physical meaning.

Lexicons also explain بَطَرَ الْحَقَّ as refusing truth or arrogantly disregarding it. The family can thus extend beyond responding to blessings to responding to truth. This expression does not occur in the Qur’an, however, and its particular sense must not be transferred without evidence to both Qur’anic occurrences.

Some lexical expressions concern bewilderment and confusion. These may relate to disordered judgment, but the Qur’anic contexts do not support translating baṭar simply as becoming bewildered.

The idiom ذَهَبَ دَمُهُ بِطْرًا is also recorded for blood being shed without redress. Such idioms illustrate special meanings not easily recovered from one short definition of the family.

For Qur’anic study, بَطَرًا and بَطِرَتْ remain central; other uses illuminate the word’s wider range and semantic history.

4. The first Qur’anic use: social conduct

Al-Anfāl 8:47

Qur’anic text:

وَلَا تَكُونُوا كَالَّذِينَ خَرَجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ بَطَرًا وَرِئَاءَ النَّاسِ وَيَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ۚ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا يَعْمَلُونَ مُحِيطٌ

wa-lā takūnū ka-alladhīna kharajū min diyārihim baṭaran wa-riʾāʾa al-nāsi wa-yaṣuddūna ʿan sabīli Allāhi wa-Allāhu bi-mā yaʿmalūna muḥīṭun

Translation:

Do not be like those who left their homes in exultation and arrogance, showing off before people and barring others from The God’s way. The God encompasses what they do.

بَطَرًا describes a group leaving their homes. The verse characterizes their conduct through baṭar, showing off before people, and obstructing The God’s way.

The immediate context concerns collective confrontation. Al-Anfāl 8:45 tells believers to stand firm when meeting an opposing force and remember The God often. Al-Anfāl 8:46 commands obedience, avoidance of disputes, and endurance. Al-Anfāl 8:47 then tells the same audience not to imitate those who departed in baṭar and ostentation.

Baṭar here need not arise specifically from wealth or economic comfort. The setting concerns a sense of power, social standing, pride, and conduct during confrontation.

A person or group may trust its strength and act confidently without displaying baṭar. Baṭar enters when that confidence becomes exultation, arrogance, and ill-considered conduct.

Baṭaran and riʾāʾ al-nās distinguished

The juxtaposition of بَطَرًا and رِئَاءَ النَّاسِ matters. They are close but do not express the same thing.

Baṭar describes exultation, pride, and an excessive response to ability or status. Riʾāʾ concerns performance before others: acting to be seen or win public admiration.

Someone can display baṭar privately, because an audience is not essential to its meaning. Conversely, someone may show off without visibly displaying every characteristic associated with baṭar.

Both features occur in this group: they move in proud exultation while seeking to display power and standing before people.

Grammatically, بَطَرًا is an accusative verbal noun. It may express the motive for departure, as in leaving out of exultation and ostentation. Alternatively, it may function circumstantially: departing while exultant and ostentatious.

Both analyses fit the basic sense. One emphasizes motivation; the other, the accompanying condition. Natural Persian can preserve their shared meaning without requiring the reader to enter the grammatical dispute.

Baṭar and obstructing The God’s way

وَيَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ depicts a further action: obstructing The God’s way.

This must not become part of baṭar’s lexical definition. Ṣ D D is a separate root describing the group’s action in this sentence.

The combination nevertheless matters. Their exultation and arrogance remain more than an inward state: they accompany public ostentation and obstruction of others from The God’s way.

The verse thus describes conduct manifest in social relations, rather than merely inward pride.

This helps explain Qur’anic baṭar, but does not prove that every instance must involve obstructing The God’s way. The second verse uses the same root in another setting.

5. The second Qur’anic use: livelihood and privilege

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58

Qur’anic text:

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ بَطِرَتْ مَعِيشَتَهَا ۖ فَتِلْكَ مَسَاكِنُهُمْ لَمْ تُسْكَنْ مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا ۖ وَكُنَّا نَحْنُ الْوَارِثِينَ

wa-kam ahlaknā min qaryatin baṭirat maʿīshatahā fa-tilka masākinuhum lam tuskan min baʿdihim illā qalīlan wa-kunnā naḥnu al-wārithīna

Translation:

How many settlements We destroyed that had become exultant and ungrateful in their livelihood! There are their dwellings, scarcely inhabited after them; We Ourselves were the inheritors.

Here B Ṭ R appears as the perfect verb بَطِرَتْ. Its subject is a settlement or community affected by baṭar in its response to its livelihood.

قَرْيَة denotes the settlement and its inhabitants. Assigning conduct to a settlement represents the inhabitants’ behavior: buildings and land do not themselves become morally arrogant or ungrateful.

Unlike Al-Anfāl, this use includes مَعِيشَتَهَا with the verb. The community’s way of life and resources for living are at issue.

Does baṭar mean being wealthy?

Livelihood concerns life and the means of sustaining it, potentially including food, income, property, and other resources.

Livelihood does not itself necessarily mean abundant wealth. بَطِرَتْ مَعِيشَتَهَا must not therefore be reduced to “the settlement was wealthy.”

The verse describes an ethical condition in responding to livelihood, involving exultation and ingratitude.

Abundant resources may create an occasion for such conduct, but abundance itself is not the basis of condemnation. A community can enjoy security, flourishing conditions, and prosperity while appreciating its blessings and acting responsibly.

Conversely, a community may use its resources to assert superiority, disregard rights or truth, or behave oppressively. That approaches baṭar’s semantic field.

Baṭar is neither wealth nor prosperity; it concerns a response to privilege.

The construction baṭirat maʿīshatahā

The sentence’s grammatical construction has been debated.

One recognized analysis takes the underlying expression as بَطِرَتْ فِي مَعِيشَتِهَا, “became affected by baṭar in its livelihood.” The preposition فِي is omitted and مَعِيشَتَهَا becomes accusative.

In another analysis, Al-Farrāʾ takes مَعِيشَتَهَا as an accusative of explanation or specification, tamyīz. Basran grammarians object that its definiteness prevents this analysis. This disagreement helps explain the construction’s multiple grammatical interpretations.

Abū Ḥayyān, Al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ: grammatical analyses of maʿīshatahā

The verb can also be explained through ingratitude and mishandling livelihood, producing a more direct semantic relationship with livelihood.

These differences should not obscure the basic statement: under each interpretation, the settlement has adopted a condemned response to its life and resources.

For translation, “became exultant and ungrateful in its livelihood” naturally conveys the central sense without imposing one grammatical analysis as certain and exclusive.

The settlement’s fate and the limits of inference

The continuation says their homes were scarcely inhabited after the community’s destruction, and The God was their inheritor.

This presents the community’s fate, but should not become an unqualified rule that every prosperous community or proud individual will be destroyed at a set time.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:59 connects the destruction of settlements with sending a messenger and their inhabitants’ wrongdoing. Responsibility, awareness, and injustice therefore matter in understanding their fate.

Baṭar in verse 58 cannot be isolated from the community’s other conduct and ethical circumstances as the sole independent cause of destruction.

Nor does the verse warrant declaring a contemporary community’s destruction or suffering divine punishment without specific evidence. It concerns communities whose condition and fate the Qur’an describes.

For root study, the key is that baṭar can characterize communal conduct as well as an individual state. Mishandling livelihood and resources acquires social consequences when established in a community’s public behavior.

6. Baṭar, blessings, and ingratitude

Baṭar in Al-Qaṣaṣ is closely connected with ingratitude for blessings, but the two words are not fully synonymous.

When concerning a blessing, K F R can involve rejecting, concealing, or being ungrateful for it. Baṭar emphasizes exultation and excessive conduct in responding to a blessing.

Study of K F R

Ingratitude can occur in many ways without overt exultation or ostentation. Baṭar commonly describes someone carried beyond measured conduct by privilege or a sense of superiority.

Al-Naḥl 16:112 offers a useful comparison: a settlement enjoys security, tranquility, and abundant provision but rejects The God’s blessings.

A clear thematic connection links that verse with Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58: both examine a community’s relationship with the means of life and the consequences of its conduct. Still, فَكَفَرَتْ بِأَنْعُمِ اللَّهِ is not a direct translation of بَطِرَتْ مَعِيشَتَهَا without regard to the roots’ differences.

One verse uses K F R to describe ingratitude for blessings; the other uses B Ṭ R for exultation and unsound conduct in responding to livelihood.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:6 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:7 also link human transgression to considering oneself self-sufficient. They are thematically close, while Ṭ GH Y retains its own semantic features.

Ingratitude, baṭar, and transgression may coexist in one person or community, but that overlap must not erase their lexical distinctions.

7. Baṭar and happiness distinguished

One major translation challenge is distinguishing baṭar from happiness.

Persian sometimes renders it as intense joy or exuberance. This works only if the sense of being carried away and exceeding moderation remains present.

The Qur’an does not condemn every joy. Yūnus 10:58 positively presents rejoicing in The God’s favor and mercy. In Qarun’s story, however, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76 says The God does not love the fariḥīn.

Joy and faraḥ must therefore be understood through their object and context. Happiness alone does not always signal baṭar.

Baṭar foregrounds more than pleasure: it concerns changed conduct under the influence of privilege or felt power.

Enjoying lawful earnings, a family’s health, or personal success is not baṭar merely because of that pleasure. Even great joy alone is insufficient for the judgment.

But using privilege to humiliate others, show off, disregard rights or truth, or misuse resources approaches baṭar’s meaning.

The distinction matters for both Qur’anic occurrences. In Al-Anfāl, baṭar accompanies ostentation and obstruction of The God’s way; in Al-Qaṣaṣ it accompanies ingratitude in responding to livelihood and resources.

Baṭar cannot be reduced to being happy; neither should every happiness or privilege be condemned as baṭar.

8. Related roots compared

B Ṭ R and M R Ḥ

Study of M R Ḥ

Maraḥ concerns lively exuberance, lightness of conduct, and in some uses swaggering and exultation. Al-Isrāʾ 17:37 forbids walking with maraḥ.

Baṭar and maraḥ overlap in exultation and excess. Qur’anic baṭar, however, particularly foregrounds privilege, felt power, or livelihood.

Maraḥ can describe behavior and the outward manifestation of exultation; baṭar also attends to mishandling a blessing or position.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary. In Arabic, both families can converge in describing exultation and arrogance.

B Ṭ R and F R Ḥ

Study of F R Ḥ

Faraḥ primarily concerns happiness and pleasure. Its Qur’anic value depends on the object of joy and context.

Unlike ordinary happiness, baṭar is condemnatory in both Qur’anic occurrences: a person or community has departed from sound conduct.

Baṭar may therefore be considered a particular condemned exultation, but not every faraḥ is baṭar.

B Ṭ R and K B R

Study of K B R

K B R concerns greatness and considering something great; certain ethical uses develop into arrogance and self-importance.

Baṭar can accompany arrogance, but its center is more than regarding oneself as great. Responding to blessings, ability, or privilege is also significant.

Someone may be arrogant without the discussion focusing on blessings. Equally, baṭar may appear chiefly through exultation, extravagance, or unsound conduct.

The words overlap but are not fully interchangeable.

B Ṭ R and Ṭ GH Y

Study of Ṭ GH Y

Ṭughyān concerns rebellion and exceeding bounds. Baṭar also exceeds moderation, but against a background of exultation and mishandling blessings or ability.

Ṭughyān can cover a wider range of rebellious conduct. Baṭar is one condition that may accompany or lead to such transgression.

Replacing Qur’anic baṭar with ṭughyān can consequently obscure its significant relationship with blessings and exultation.

B Ṭ R and ʾ SH R

Study of ʾ SH R

Arabic أَشَر concerns intense exultation and conceited conduct. Al-Qamar 54:25 uses it descriptively.

Lexicons treat ashar as one of baṭar’s closest neighboring terms. Both can describe excessive exuberance and pride.

Closeness does not mean every feature of one is present in the other. Baṭar’s two particular Qur’anic contexts determine its interpretation.

B Ṭ R and B Ṭ SH

Study of B Ṭ SH

The roots share their first two letters but differ in the third; they must not be confused.

B Ṭ SH concerns seizing or attacking with force. Qur’anic B Ṭ R concerns exultation, pride, and unsound conduct.

Baṭar may lead someone to violence, but this possible connection does not make the roots synonymous.

The same caution applies to the separate B Ṭ L. Similar letters do not authorize transferring the meaning of falsehood or invalidity to B Ṭ R.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should retain three central elements: exultation, arrogance, and an ungrateful or unsound response to blessings and ability. It need not insert separate words for every element into every verse.

For بَطَر, “arrogant exultation,” “exultant pride,” or “exultation accompanied by arrogance” is suitable. “Arrogant ingratitude” can express part of the sense in context, but baṭar must not always be confined to verbal ingratitude.

For بَطِرَ, possible renderings include “became exultant and arrogant,” “became exultant and ungrateful in responding to blessings,” or, where appropriate, “transgressed through privilege.”

For بَطَرًا in Al-Anfāl 8:47, the proposed rendering is:

“In exultation and arrogance.”

This fits the departure, ostentation, and display of power. Adding “ingratitude for blessings” at this point is unnecessary: the phrase describes the group’s departure without spelling out its complete relationship with blessings.

For بَطِرَتْ مَعِيشَتَهَا in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58, the proposed rendering is:

“Had become exultant and ungrateful in its livelihood.”

This preserves the verb’s direct relationship with livelihood without turning livelihood into unproven abundant wealth or economic prosperity.

A concise explanation may say: “Baṭar is exultation and arrogance in responding to blessings or ability, taking someone away from measured, grateful conduct.”

Translation must not confine baṭar to splitting, ordinary happiness, wealth, hypocrisy, or disbelief. Those concepts either belong to another lexical branch or require independent contextual evidence.

10. Conclusion

Qur’anic B Ṭ R concerns exultation and arrogance: a condition that can affect individuals or communities enjoying blessings, prosperity, power, or resources, carrying them away from measured and grateful conduct.

Al-Anfāl 8:47 depicts a group leaving home in pride and ostentation and obstructing The God’s way. Felt power and public display are prominent.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58 concerns a community’s response to livelihood and life’s resources. It enjoys blessings but responds with exultation and ingratitude.

Neither verse condemns the blessing or ability itself. Human conduct toward it is at issue. Power is not inherently baṭar, any more than wealth, security, prosperity, or happiness is baṭar by itself.

Lexicons record splitting for this family, and Ibn Fāris treats it as the root’s principle. Yet the historical connection of every branch with that image is uncertain and must not be imposed on the clear Qur’anic senses.

Final definition in Qur’anic use: Baṭar is exultation and arrogance in responding to blessings, ability, or privilege, accompanied by ingratitude, excess, or ill-considered conduct.

This preserves the relationship with blessings without limiting baṭar to wealth or happiness. The distinction from ordinary joy is that individuals or communities turn their privilege into an occasion for pride and departure from proper conduct.
