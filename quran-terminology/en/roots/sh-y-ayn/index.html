Core meanings: companionship, following, and support; groups joined by a shared affiliation or orientation; and, in another branch, the spread and circulation of something among people or within an environment.

The Arabic root SH Y ʿ has two important semantic fields.

The first concerns companionship, following, support, and shared direction. Shāyaʿa means to accompany or support someone; shīʿah denotes people linked by allegiance, belief, purpose, or affiliation.

In the second, shāʿa means to spread, circulate, or become widespread, as when news moves beyond a few people and becomes known more widely.

Both fields are recorded in Arabic lexicons. A semantic connection can be seen between a movement spreading and people joining it, but this alone does not establish which meaning developed historically from the other.

The root occurs twelve times in eleven Quranic verses, in the forms shīʿah, shiyaʿ, ashyāʿ, and the verb tashīʿa.

Shīʿah does not always have a negative meaning in the Quran. In the contextual reading adopted here, Abraham is described as one of Noah’s followers in As-Saffat 37:83, whereas other passages use this family for oppressive group divisions or division in religion.

A group’s value therefore follows from its allegiance, beliefs, and conduct, rather than from the designation shīʿah itself.

1. The root in early Arabic

Companionship, following, and support

In early Arabic, shāyaʿa fulānun fulānan described accompanying or supporting another person.

Such companionship could concern movement, a decision, an idea, or a task.

For example, two people may travel together: one knows the route and the other accompanies him.

Their companionship involves coordination toward a shared destination, beyond simply occupying the same place.

The same relationship appears in thought and action. Supporting someone’s decision and helping advance it can be described as mushāyaʿah.

Lexicons record shāyaʿahu ʿalā raʾyihi for supporting someone in his opinion or judgment.

Shayyaʿahu also describes accompanying someone who is leaving for part of the journey.

Thus, one clear sense is accompanying another and strengthening him along a particular course or in a task.

A witness from poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period

The Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddad contains:

ذُلُلٌ رِكَابِي حَيْثُ شِئْتُ مُشَايِعِي
لُبِّي وَأَحْفِزُهُ بِأَمْرٍ مُبْرَمِ

dhululun rikābī ḥaythu shiʾtu mushāyiʿī / lubbī wa-aḥfizuhu bi-amrin mubram

“My mounts are tractable wherever I wish; my understanding accompanies me, and I urge it onward with a firm resolve.”

Here mushāyiʿī expresses companionship and assistance.

The poet describes obedient mounts and an understanding that accompanies him in deciding and moving.

Mushāyiʿ here conveys coordination in will and action, without denoting adherence to a religious school or membership in a religious group.

Transmitted variants include ḥaythu shiʾtu and ḥaythu kuntu, and bi-amrin mubram and bi-raʾyin mubram. They do not alter the relevant meaning of mushāyiʿī.

The line belongs to the Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah. As with other early poetic witnesses, attributing a poem and independently dating an individual line are distinct matters.

The lexical evidence is clear: this family also expressed companionship and support in early poetry.

Shīʿah: a group with a shared connection

Shīʿah denotes people linked by allegiance, purpose, opinion, or affiliation.

People who support someone and agree with him on a matter can be called that person’s shīʿah.

The word alone does not say whether the person followed is upright or oppressive.

One group may support a righteous person and another an oppressor.

Following and shared direction exist in both cases, but their moral value differs.

Lexicons use shīʿah for a body of companions and when identifying an individual as belonging to such a body.

Accordingly, min shīʿatihi can describe someone belonging to a person’s companions or followers.

This matters in Al-Qasas 28:15 and As-Saffat 37:83.

Shāʿa: spreading and circulating

The other branch concerns dissemination and circulation.

Shāʿa al-khabaru means that news spread.

Ashāʿa al-khabara describes someone spreading news.

News may initially be known to one person, who tells several others; they pass it on until it circulates among many people.

Such news can be described with shāʿa.

Lexicons also record shāʿa al-shayʾu for something appearing, dispersing, or spreading.

This usage extends beyond news.

For example, shāʿa al-shaybu fī al-raʾs describes white hair spreading across the head.

In property terminology, sahmun shāʾiʿun is an undivided share in jointly held property, not yet allocated as a separate physical portion.

Two people may each own half of a house without particular rooms being assigned exclusively to either one.

Each share then remains distributed throughout the common property and physically undivided.

Shuyūʿ here concerns the share extending throughout the jointly held property.

Classical Arabic sources record these meanings. Quranic use confirms that the spreading branch was known in the language of revelation, but dating every usage to the pre-Islamic period requires independent evidence.

The relationship between companionship and dissemination

A connection can be envisaged between people accompanying one another and a report or movement spreading.

When an idea spreads, people may follow and support it.

Both the idea’s circulation and the formation of a group then occur.

Yet these are distinct events.

News may reach thousands without their following a particular person or forming a common group.

Conversely, a small group may remain closely associated without its activities or ideas spreading widely.

Dissemination and collective allegiance are therefore distinct branches and cannot be reduced to a single meaning in every usage.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

Shāʿa: spread, circulated, or became prevalent.

Yashīʿu: spreads or circulates.

Shuyūʿ: dissemination and prevalence.

Shāʾiʿ: widespread, current, or prevalent; in ownership, an undivided share in common property.

Ashāʿa: disseminated something or caused it to spread.

Ishāʿah: disseminating news or a matter.

Shayyaʿa: accompanied someone, saw him off, or supported him in a task.

Tashyīʿ: accompanying and seeing off; in some uses, strengthening or supporting.

Shāyaʿa: accompanied another and supported him in opinion or action.

Mushāyaʿah: companionship, following, and support.

Mushāyiʿ: a companion or supporter.

Tashayyaʿa: adopted allegiance to a person or group; in later technical usage, became an adherent of Shia Islam.

Shīʿah: a group linked by allegiance, affiliation, or shared orientation.

Shiyaʿ: the plural of shīʿah; groups or parties.

Ashyāʿ: associated or following groups; in some contexts, counterparts sharing a way or conduct.

Mushayyaʿ: accompanied or supported; in some lexical uses, courageous and strong.

Of these forms, the Quran uses shīʿah, shiyaʿ, ashyāʿ, and tashīʿa.

The verbs shāyaʿa, shayyaʿa, and ashāʿa do not occur in these forms in the Quran.

Tashīʿa in An-Nur 24:19 comes from the basic verb shāʿa, rather than tashayyaʿa.

Tashīʿa means “should spread,” whereas tashayyaʿa can concern following or affiliation with a group.

Their visual resemblance must not obscure this distinction.

3. Shīʿah: Quranic companionship and following

As-Saffat 37:83: Abraham among Noah’s followers in the contextual reading

وَإِنَّ مِنْ شِيعَتِهِ لَإِبْرَاهِيمَ

wa-inna min shīʿatihi la-ibrāhīm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:83

“And indeed Abraham was among his followers.”

As-Saffat, 37:83

This is an important witness for the Quranic meaning of shīʿah.

The preceding verses discuss Noah and his deliverance. On the contextual reading adopted here, the pronoun in shīʿatihi refers to Noah.

On this reading, Abraham is described as one of Noah’s followers.

Shīʿah here neither carries blame nor names a particular later religious denomination.

Abraham lived after Noah, so his companionship with Noah does not mean physically traveling beside him.

The connection concerns their way, faith, and orientation.

As-Saffat 37:84 then speaks of Abraham coming to his Lord with a sound heart.

In As-Saffat 37:85, Abraham questions the worship of his father and people.

The context places Abraham’s connection with Noah in faith and the worship of God.

Shīʿah can therefore describe a righteous person following the course of an earlier Prophet.

This prevents defining the word invariably as “a misguided sect.”

Al-Qasas 28:15: a man from Moses’ group

وَدَخَلَ الْمَدِينَةَ عَلَىٰ حِينِ غَفْلَةٍ مِنْ أَهْلِهَا فَوَجَدَ فِيهَا رَجُلَيْنِ يَقْتَتِلَانِ هَٰذَا مِنْ شِيعَتِهِ وَهَٰذَا مِنْ عَدُوِّهِ ۖ فَاسْتَغَاثَهُ الَّذِي مِنْ شِيعَتِهِ عَلَى الَّذِي مِنْ عَدُوِّهِ فَوَكَزَهُ مُوسَىٰ فَقَضَىٰ عَلَيْهِ ۖ قَالَ هَٰذَا مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ ۖ إِنَّهُ عَدُوٌّ مُضِلٌّ مُبِينٌ

wa-dakhala al-madīnata ʿalā ḥīni ghaflatin min ahlihā fa-wajada fīhā rajulayni yaqtatilāni hādhā min shīʿatihi wa-hādhā min ʿaduwwihi fa-staghāthahu alladhī min shīʿatihi ʿalā alladhī min ʿaduwwihi fa-wakazahu mūsā fa-qaḍā ʿalayhi qāla hādhā min ʿamali al-shayṭāni innahu ʿaduwwun muḍillun mubīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15

“He entered the city at a time when its inhabitants were unaware and found two men fighting: one from his group and the other from his enemies. The man from his group sought his help against the one from his enemies. Moses struck him and killed him. He said: This is of Satan’s work; he is clearly a misleading enemy.”

Al-Qasas, 28:15

Shīʿatihi occurs twice in this verse.

The first occurrence identifies one of the combatants; the second identifies the person asking Moses for help.

Here shīʿah identifies someone belonging to Moses’ group.

The other man is described as min ʿaduwwihi, “from his enemies.”

The verse thus distinguishes affiliation with Moses’ group from affiliation with his enemies.

Shīʿah can express social or collective affiliation without necessarily referring to an organized denomination with a codified creed.

Does belonging to a shīʿah guarantee upright conduct?

No.

The man from Moses’ group asks for help; Moses strikes the other man, who dies.

Moses then describes what happened as Satan’s work.

In Al-Qasas 28:18, the man who sought help the previous day asks again, and Moses calls him clearly misguided.

Membership in Moses’ group therefore does not establish the correctness of all the man’s conduct.

Nor does an individual’s fault prove that everyone sharing his group affiliation is wrong.

These verses distinguish group affiliation from the rightness or wrongness of individual conduct.

On the contextual reading of As-Saffat 37:83, Abraham follows Noah, and their connection lies in faith.

In Al-Qasas 28:15, someone belongs to Moses’ group, but the designation does not guarantee proper conduct.

Lexically, shīʿah identifies a relationship of following or affiliation; its value depends on the object of allegiance and people’s conduct.

4. Shiyaʿ and ashyāʿ: groups and counterparts

Al-Hijr 15:10: communities of earlier peoples

وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا مِنْ قَبْلِكَ فِي شِيَعِ الْأَوَّلِينَ

wa-la-qad arsalnā min qablika fī shiyaʿi al-awwalīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:10

“We certainly sent [Messengers] before you among the communities of earlier peoples.”

Al-Hijr, 15:10

Shiyaʿ is the plural of shīʿah here.

It denotes earlier communities among whom divine Messengers were sent.

Al-Hijr 15:11 then describes their audiences mocking the Messengers.

Shiyaʿ itself in verse 10 means groups or communities.

The word alone does not establish that each community was an organized religious sect with a defined creed before the Messengers came.

“Communities of earlier peoples” is therefore preferable to labeling them all “misguided sects” from the outset.

Maryam 19:69: groups in the scene of judgment

ثُمَّ لَنَنْزِعَنَّ مِنْ كُلِّ شِيعَةٍ أَيُّهُمْ أَشَدُّ عَلَى الرَّحْمَٰنِ عِتِيًّا

thumma la-nanziʿanna min kulli shīʿatin ayyuhum ashaddu ʿalā al-raḥmāni ʿitiyyā

Maryam 19:69

“Then We shall certainly draw out from every group those most defiant toward the Entirely Merciful.”

Maryam, 19:69

Shīʿatin here denotes a group.

Who, then, is being condemned?

The verse identifies those most defiant toward the Entirely Merciful as being drawn from each group.

ʿItiyyan expresses defiance and rebellion; shīʿah itself does not.

The verse also distinguishes the group from its most extreme members by selecting people with a particular quality.

Shīʿah must therefore not be defined simply as “a rebellious group.” Rebellion comes from a different word and construction.

Al-Qamar 54:51: earlier counterparts

وَلَقَدْ أَهْلَكْنَا أَشْيَاعَكُمْ فَهَلْ مِنْ مُدَّكِرٍ

wa-la-qad ahlaknā ashyāʿakum fa-hal min muddakir

Al-Qamar 54:51

“We certainly destroyed your earlier counterparts; is there anyone who will take heed?”

Al-Qamar, 54:51

Ashyāʿakum refers to groups resembling the audience in conduct and fate.

The surah discusses earlier peoples who denied divine warnings and faced the consequences.

Here ashyāʿakum does not mean the audience’s direct followers, since the peoples lived before them.

It denotes their earlier counterparts in conduct.

Two societies in separate periods may behave similarly, denying warnings and persisting in oppression.

They need not share an organization or leader to resemble one another in conduct.

Ashyāʿ here extends beyond direct companionship to similarity of course and behavior.

Saba 34:54: earlier counterparts

وَحِيلَ بَيْنَهُمْ وَبَيْنَ مَا يَشْتَهُونَ كَمَا فُعِلَ بِأَشْيَاعِهِمْ مِنْ قَبْلُ ۚ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا فِي شَكٍّ مُرِيبٍ

wa-ḥīla baynahum wa-bayna mā yashtahūna ka-mā fuʿila bi-ashyāʿihim min qablu innahum kānū fī shakkin murīb

Sabaʾ 34:54

“A barrier was placed between them and what they desired, as had been done to their counterparts before; they had been in unsettling doubt.”

Saba, 34:54

Ashyāʿihim again denotes earlier comparable groups.

Min qablu matters because it explicitly places those groups earlier.

“Their followers” could mislead here, since the groups lived before those under discussion.

“Their earlier counterparts” is more precise.

These two uses show that similarity of course, conduct, or fate can underlie ashyāʿ without direct, simultaneous companionship.

5. Shiyaʿ: group divisions, fragmentation, and power

Al-Qasas 28:4: Pharaoh’s division of the population

إِنَّ فِرْعَوْنَ عَلَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَجَعَلَ أَهْلَهَا شِيَعًا يَسْتَضْعِفُ طَائِفَةً مِنْهُمْ يُذَبِّحُ أَبْنَاءَهُمْ وَيَسْتَحْيِي نِسَاءَهُمْ ۚ إِنَّهُ كَانَ مِنَ الْمُفْسِدِينَ

inna firʿawna ʿalā fī al-arḍi wa-jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan yastaḍʿifu ṭāʾifatan minhum yudhabbiḥu abnāʾahum wa-yastaḥyī nisāʾahum innahu kāna mina al-mufsidīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:4

“Pharaoh exalted himself in the land and divided its people into groups, weakening a section of them, slaughtering their sons and keeping their women alive. He was among those who spread corruption.”

Al-Qasas, 28:4

Shiyaʿan here denotes separate groups.

The agent of this division is specified: Pharaoh.

Jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan indicates his division of the population into different groups.

The verse then describes weakening part of the population and killing their sons.

The grouping forms part of Pharaoh’s domination and corruption.

For example, a ruler might divide people by ethnicity, family, or social standing and assign different rights and resources to each group.

If used for supremacy, discrimination, and repression, such divisions can preserve oppressive power.

Condemnation comes from Pharaoh’s conduct, domination, oppression, and killing, rather than from shiyaʿ itself.

The distinction matters because social grouping is not inherently oppressive.

People may form groups around residence, occupation, or shared responsibilities without one being established to repress another.

Al-Anam 6:65: groups set against one another

قُلْ هُوَ الْقَادِرُ عَلَىٰ أَنْ يَبْعَثَ عَلَيْكُمْ عَذَابًا مِنْ فَوْقِكُمْ أَوْ مِنْ تَحْتِ أَرْجُلِكُمْ أَوْ يَلْبِسَكُمْ شِيَعًا وَيُذِيقَ بَعْضَكُمْ بَأْسَ بَعْضٍ ۗ انْظُرْ كَيْفَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَفْقَهُونَ

qul huwa al-qādiru ʿalā an yabʿatha ʿalaykum ʿadhāban min fawqikum aw min taḥti arjulikum aw yalbisakum shiyaʿan wa-yudhīqa baʿḍakum baʾsa baʿḍin unẓur kayfa nuṣarrifu al-āyāti laʿallahum yafqahūn

Al-Anʿām 6:65

“Say: He is able to send punishment upon you from above you or from beneath your feet, or to embroil you in factions and make some of you taste the violence of others. See how We vary the signs so that they may understand.”

Al-Anam, 6:65

Shiyaʿan occurs with yalbisakum.

Labs in this construction concerns intermingling and becoming embroiled in disorder.

The continuation speaks of making some people taste the baʾs of others.

The subject is therefore grouping accompanied by confusion, conflict, and mutual harm, beyond the mere existence of different groups.

A society can contain different groups living peacefully despite their differences.

The situation changes when they become enemies and seek each other’s destruction.

The verse mentions this danger alongside other forms of punishment and harm.

Shiyaʿ alone does not mean fighting; violence is understood from the sentence and “making some of you taste the violence of others.”

Al-Anam 6:159: fragmenting religion

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ فَرَّقُوا دِينَهُمْ وَكَانُوا شِيَعًا لَسْتَ مِنْهُمْ فِي شَيْءٍ ۚ إِنَّمَا أَمْرُهُمْ إِلَى اللَّهِ ثُمَّ يُنَبِّئُهُمْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَفْعَلُونَ

inna alladhīna farraqū dīnahum wa-kānū shiyaʿan lasta minhum fī shayʾin innamā amruhum ilā allāhi thumma yunabbiʾuhum bi-mā kānū yafʿalūn

Al-Anʿām 6:159

“Those who divided their religion and became separate groups—you have no part in their affair. Their affair rests with The God alone; then He will inform them of what they used to do.”

Al-Anam, 6:159

Shiyaʿan stands alongside farraqū dīnahum.

Farraqū belongs to F R Q and expresses separation and division.

Shiyaʿan identifies the groups arising from that division.

The verse discusses people who have fragmented their religion into separate groups, beyond simply observing a plurality of groups.

This distinction is fundamental.

People can interpret a matter differently; interpretive disagreement alone does not prove that they have fragmented religion.

For example, when partisan attachment replaces commitment to religious truth and each group places its own authority above truth, this can constitute religious division.

The verse’s condemnation arises from the combination of fragmenting religion and becoming separate groups.

Its continuation assigns their affair to God.

Alongside its explicit criticism of division, the verse leaves final judgment of their conduct to God.

Ar-Rum 30:32: each party rejoicing in what it possesses

مِنَ الَّذِينَ فَرَّقُوا دِينَهُمْ وَكَانُوا شِيَعًا ۖ كُلُّ حِزْبٍ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ

mina alladhīna farraqū dīnahum wa-kānū shiyaʿan kullu ḥizbin bi-mā ladayhim fariḥūn

Al-Rūm 30:32

“Among those who divided their religion and became separate groups, each party rejoicing in what it has.”

Ar-Rum, 30:32

Shiyaʿan again accompanies religious fragmentation.

A further statement follows:

كُلُّ حِزْبٍ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ

kullu ḥizbin bi-mā ladayhim fariḥūn

Al-Rūm 30:32

“Each party rejoices in what it has.”

Ḥizb belongs to a different root, Ḥ Z B, and denotes a party or group with a common connection.

The verse depicts each group rejoicing in what it possesses.

In this divisive context, such rejoicing concerns group attachment and satisfaction with one’s own position.

A group might accept an idea solely because it inherited it from its leaders or predecessors and reject opposing views without examining their evidence.

Another group might act identically in the opposite direction.

Group loyalty then gradually replaces the search for truth as the standard of judgment.

This is a contextual implication, rather than the lexical meaning of shiyaʿ.

The verse connects with Ar-Rum 30:31, which speaks of turning to God, reverence, and avoiding association of partners with Him.

Religious division here concerns people’s religious orientation; differing names or ordinary disagreements cannot automatically be equated with it.

Are all uses of shīʿah condemnatory?

Comparison of the passages shows that they are not.

On the contextual reading of As-Saffat 37:83, Abraham is one of Noah’s followers.

In Al-Qasas 28:15, one of the combatants belongs to Moses’ group.

Al-Anam 6:159 and Ar-Rum 30:32, however, discuss people who fragmented religion into parties.

The difference concerns the direction and consequences of allegiance and grouping.

When allegiance follows truth, its value derives from that commitment.

If group attachment produces religious division, fanaticism, and hostility, it can form part of a condemned condition.

A condemned group does not make the whole root invariably negative, just as Abraham’s positive example does not validate every group bearing the name shīʿah.

6. Shuyūʿ: spreading and circulation

An-Nur 24:19: indecency spreading among believers

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يُحِبُّونَ أَنْ تَشِيعَ الْفَاحِشَةُ فِي الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ ۚ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنْتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

inna alladhīna yuḥibbūna an tashīʿa al-fāḥishatu fī alladhīna āmanū lahum ʿadhābun alīmun fī al-dunyā wa-l-ākhirati wa-allāhu yaʿlamu wa-antum lā taʿlamūn

Al-Nūr 24:19

“Those who love that indecency should spread among those who believe will have a painful punishment in this world and the Hereafter. The God knows, and you do not know.”

An-Nur, 24:19

Tashīʿa comes from the basic verb shāʿa.

It expresses spreading and circulation.

Al-fāḥishatu is the subject: what spreads is indecency.

Fī alladhīna āmanū identifies the community within which this spreading is envisaged.

Grammatically, tashīʿa is a subjunctive imperfect following an. Its feminine form agrees with al-fāḥishatu.

Distinguishing dissemination from indecency

The verse contains two independent concepts.

Fāḥishah concerns a gravely shameful act or matter.

Tashīʿa expresses its spreading.

The root SH Y ʿ itself therefore does not mean indecency, sin, or corruption.

For example, the same family could express good news spreading among people.

When harmful speech spreads, dissemination can produce harmful consequences.

The meaning of dissemination remains in both cases; its moral value depends on what spreads and how people act.

The verse and people’s reputations

The preceding verses of An-Nur discuss accusations and wrongful claims about people’s chastity.

An-Nur 24:15 mentions passing speech from tongue to tongue and speaking without knowledge.

An-Nur 24:16 addresses refraining from repeating such claims.

An-Nur 24:19 thus stands in a context centrally concerned with spreading wrongful accusations and damaging reputations.

Someone might hear an allegation about another’s private life without evidence and circulate it among dozens of people.

A claim initially known to few then becomes widespread and can harm the person’s reputation, family, and social relationships.

This illustrates a clear consequence of circulating wrongful speech.

Nevertheless, tashīʿa al-fāḥishatu should not be lexically restricted to “spreading a false rumor.”

Shuyūʿ retains the meaning of spreading, and fāḥishah retains its independent meaning.

An-Nur particularly addresses wrongful accusations and speech without knowledge, but this example does not narrow the verb’s lexical definition.

The verse also identifies people who love such spreading. This desire comes from yuḥibbūna, rather than tashīʿa itself.

Dissemination, its subject matter, and people’s intentions must therefore be distinguished.

7. Related roots and later terminology

SH Y ʿ (shīʿah) and T B ʿ (tabiʿa)

Tabiʿa concerns following another.

Shāyaʿa can also express following or companionship, with support and shared direction prominent in its recorded uses.

Shīʿah usually identifies a collective relationship or a body of followers and companions.

A person may follow another on a journey without belonging to his ideological or social group.

General following and membership in an associated group therefore differ in specificity.

SH Y ʿ (shiyaʿ) and F R Q (furqah)

Faraqa concerns separating things and creating distance between them.

Shiyaʿ identifies groups whose members share some internal connection.

Al-Anam 6:159 and Ar-Rum 30:32 place these concepts together.

People have divided their religion and become separate groups.

Division highlights separation and broken connection; shiyaʿ identifies the resulting or existing groups.

Shīʿah consequently cannot always mean “a sect separated from religion.”

SH Y ʿ (shiyaʿ) and Ḥ Z B (ḥizb)

Ḥizb also denotes people linked by a shared purpose or orientation.

Ar-Rum 30:32 uses shiyaʿ and ḥizb together.

Ḥizb is not inherently negative either: Al-Mujadilah 58:22 speaks of ḥizb Allāh, “the party of The God.”

Calling a body ḥizb or shīʿah does not alone establish whether it is right or wrong.

Its direction and conduct supply the criteria.

SH Y ʿ (shuyūʿ) and DH Y ʿ (dhuyūʿ)

Dhāʿa and its derivatives concern news spreading and becoming known.

Shāʿa likewise expresses circulation.

Their meanings can closely overlap in some sentences.

They remain independent roots; semantic resemblance does not make all their derivatives equivalent.

Shīʿah from SH Y ʿ concerns groups and followers, a meaning not supplied by DH Y ʿ itself.

SH Y ʿ (ashyāʿ) and SH Y ʾ (ashyāʾ)

These words resemble one another in writing but belong to different roots.

Ashyāʿ from SH Y ʿ denotes associated or comparable groups.

Ashyāʾ is the plural of shayʾ, “thing,” from SH Y ʾ.

Ashyāʿ therefore does not mean “things,” and ashyāʾ is not the plural of shīʿah.

This distinction matters for accurate identification of Quranic roots.

Quranic shīʿah and the name Shia Islam

In subsequent Islamic history, shīʿah became associated with support for ʿAli ibn Abi Talib and with particular religious movements and denominations.

This historical designation draws on the general sense of following and companionship but does not exhaust the Quranic uses.

Describing Abraham as Noah’s follower does not import the later denominational meaning into the verse.

Likewise, condemnation of religious division into shiyaʿ does not, merely through a shared name, make a verse exclusively about one later denomination.

Conversely, Abraham’s being Noah’s follower cannot establish the correctness of every belief or practice of later groups called Shia.

Three matters must be distinguished: the lexical meaning, the varied Quranic uses, and later religious naming.

Following truth and avoiding religious fragmentation provide Quranic criteria independent of group names.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Shīʿatihi — As-Saffat 37:83

Brief root meaning: Companionship, following, and support; people linked by a common course.

Meaning in the verse: Abraham’s connection with Noah’s way and followers, on the contextual reading adopted here.

Suggested translation: “Among his followers.”

Shīʿatihi — Al-Qasas 28:15

Brief root meaning: Companionship and collective affiliation; following a person or shared direction.

Meaning in the verse: A man from Moses’ group or associates.

Suggested translation: “From his group.”

The form occurs twice, expressing the same affiliation both times.

Shiyaʿan — Al-Anam 6:65

Brief root meaning: Companionship, following, and shared affiliation; hence groups joined by a common direction.

Meaning in the verse: Groups embroiled in conflict.

Suggested translation: “Separate groups.”

The sentence must also independently preserve the contribution of yalbisakum.

Shiyaʿan — Al-Anam 6:159

Brief root meaning: Companionship, following, and shared affiliation; hence groups with a common connection.

Meaning in the verse: Groups separated through fragmenting religion.

Suggested translation: “Separate groups.”

Shiyaʿi — Al-Hijr 15:10

Brief root meaning: Companionship and shared affiliation; hence groups and communities.

Meaning in the verse: Communities of earlier peoples.

Suggested translation: “Communities of earlier peoples.”

Shīʿatin — Maryam 19:69

Brief root meaning: Following and shared affiliation; hence a group with a common orientation.

Meaning in the verse: Each group discussed in the scene of judgment.

Suggested translation: “Group.”

Shiyaʿan — Al-Qasas 28:4

Brief root meaning: Companionship and shared affiliation; hence groups whose members are connected.

Meaning in the verse: Sections of the population divided into separate groups by Pharaoh.

Suggested translation: “Into groups.”

Shiyaʿan — Ar-Rum 30:32

Brief root meaning: Following and common orientation; hence associated groups.

Meaning in the verse: Groups separated through the fragmentation of religion.

Suggested translation: “Separate groups.”

Ashyāʿakum — Al-Qamar 54:51

Brief root meaning: Companionship and shared direction; more broadly, similarity of course and conduct.

Meaning in the verse: Earlier peoples resembling the audience in conduct and fate.

Suggested translation: “Your earlier counterparts.”

Bi-ashyāʿihim — Saba 34:54

Brief root meaning: Companionship and shared course; similarity of orientation and conduct.

Meaning in the verse: Similar groups who lived earlier.

Suggested translation: “To their earlier counterparts.”

Tashīʿa — An-Nur 24:19

Brief root meaning: Spreading and circulating among people or within an environment.

Meaning in the verse: Indecency spreading among believers.

Suggested translation: “Should spread.”

This is a basic-stem subjunctive imperfect and must be distinguished from tashayyaʿa, following or affiliating with a group.

9. Conclusion

SH Y ʿ has two main Arabic semantic fields.

The first is companionship, following, and support, encompassing shīʿah, shiyaʿ, and ashyāʿ.

The second is dissemination and circulation, encompassing shāʿa, shuyūʿ, and ishāʿah.

Quranic shīʿah and shiyaʿ identify followers and groups whose value is determined contextually.

On the contextual reading, Abraham follows Noah; the same word identifies a member of Moses’ group. Elsewhere, the Quran criticizes Pharaoh’s oppressive divisions and the fragmentation of religion into partisan groups.

Ashyāʿ can denote earlier counterparts in conduct, without requiring companionship at the same time or place.

In the dissemination branch, tashīʿa in An-Nur 24:19 concerns indecency spreading. Its negative value follows from what spreads and the context, rather than from shāʿa itself.

Three principal conclusions follow:

First, shīʿah lexically denotes followers or companions and is not inherently commendatory or condemnatory.

Second, shiyaʿ in divisive passages identifies groups whose separation and partisanship emerge from context; the word itself does not mean error or hostility.

Third, dissemination is a distinct branch and must not be equated with religious allegiance or group affiliation.

Final definition of SH Y ʿ:

“In the companionship branch: following, support, and association around a shared orientation. Shīʿah denotes followers or companions, shiyaʿ denotes groups, and ashyāʿ denotes associates or counterparts in conduct. In the dissemination branch: something spreading among people or within an environment. Positive or negative value follows from the subject matter, allegiance, and context, rather than from the root alone.”
