Watchful waiting, observation, ambush, and preparation for an expected event

Arabic R Ṣ D concerns watchfulness and attentive waiting for something to arrive, pass, or occur. A person or group may take up a position, keep something under observation, or prepare for an event.

This appears in watching a road, ambushing an enemy, guarding something, awaiting someone’s arrival, and keeping equipment or a place ready for a particular purpose.

Raṣd goes beyond seeing and observing. Many uses involve waiting with readiness: someone in an ambush position watches the surroundings while preparing for a particular person or event.

Raṣd need not be hostile. Someone may lie in wait to harm another, stand guard to protect something, or await an opportunity to repay kindness. Context establishes the purpose and moral value rather than the root alone.

Qur’anic uses include wartime observation and ambush, preparing a place for collaboration with opponents, guarding against eavesdropping, protecting the communication of divine messages, and describing Hell and divine vigilance concerning accountability and deeds’ outcomes.

Watchfulness and waiting are shared, while the watcher, object, purpose, and result differ between verses.

The root occurs six times in six Qur’anic verses, in four main forms.

Qur’anic forms: مَرْصَدٍ (marṣadin), Al-Tawbah 9:5; إِرْصَادًا (irṣādan), Al-Tawbah 9:107; رَصَدًا (raṣadan), Al-Jinn 72:9 and Al-Jinn 72:27; مِرْصَادًا (mirṣādan), Al-Nabaʾ 78:21; الْمِرْصَادِ (al-mirṣādi), Al-Fajr 89:14.

Arabic also records رَصَدَ (raṣada), يَرْصُدُ (yarṣudu), رَصْد (raṣd), رَصَد (raṣad), رَاصِد (rāṣid), رَصِيد (raṣīd), رَصُود (raṣūd), أَرْصَدَ (arṣada), مُرْصِد (murṣid), مَرْصُود (marṣūd), تَرَصَّدَ (taraṣṣada), تَرَصُّد (taraṣṣud), اِرْتَصَدَ (irtaṣada), اِرْتِصَاد (irtiṣād), أَرْصَاد (arṣād), and مَرَاصِد (marāṣid). These do not occur in precisely these forms in the Qur’an; their lexical uses should be distinguished from actual Qur’anic derivatives.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic uses

Waiting with watchfulness and readiness

Lexical explanation defines the core as follows:

التَّهَيُّؤُ لِرِقْبَةِ شَيْءٍ عَلَى مَسْلَكِهِ

al-tahayyuʾu li-riqbati shayʾin ʿalā maslakihi

“Preparing to watch something along its route.”

Three elements appear together: readiness, watchfulness, and attention to the route or situation where the expected thing appears.

Raṣadtuhu arṣuduhu means watching or waiting for someone.

Taraṣṣadahu likewise describes watching another and awaiting an opportunity or event concerning them.

The waiting may be short or long.

The root itself does not specify its duration.

Nor does raṣd necessarily involve uninterrupted seeing: someone may wait in an ambush position before the expected subject appears.

Direct observation is therefore one possible condition; attentive waiting and readiness also matter.

Marṣad: a place of watching or ambush

Marṣad names a place where a watcher waits for something to arrive or pass.

Early usage can apply it to an ambush position or a road where someone sits watching passersby.

Mirṣād is also recorded for a position of observation or ambush.

A lexical explanation of its spatial use states:

وَالْمَرْصَدُ وَالْمِرْصَادُ عِنْدَ الْعَرَبِ الطَّرِيقُ

wa-al-marṣadu wa-al-mirṣādu ʿinda al-ʿarabi al-ṭarīqu

“Marṣad and mirṣād among the Arabs denote a road.”

This does not make every road a marṣad regardless of watching or waiting.

The use under discussion concerns a route or passage used for observation and waiting.

A road as a general location therefore differs from a marṣad as a watching position.

Traffic may make a passage suitable for observation, but passing along a road alone does not create the meaning of raṣd.

Watching an enemy or awaiting an opportunity for kindness

Lexical explanation of waiting to repay kindness includes:

أَنَا لَكَ مُرْصِدٌ بِإِحْسَانِكَ حَتَّى أُكَافِئَكَ بِهِ

anā laka murṣidun bi-iḥsānika ḥattā ukāfiʾaka bihi

“I await an opportunity to repay your kindness and respond to it.”

This matters because the family extends beyond threats and hostility.

The person is ready to repay another’s kindness and waits for a suitable occasion.

A hunter or enemy, by contrast, may wait in order to harm or catch another.

Raṣd should therefore not be lexically restricted to hostility, conspiracy, or punishment.

The root concerns watching and waiting; purpose and context determine whether the intended action is good or bad.

Raṣada and arṣada

These verbs are close in use, but lexical accounts also distinguish them.

One explanation states:

رَصَدْتُهُ أَرْصُدُهُ: تَرَقَّبْتُهُ، وَأَرْصَدْتُ لَهُ: أَعْدَدْتُ

raṣadtuhu arṣuduhu: taraqqabtuhu wa-arṣadtu lahu: aʿdadtu

“Raṣadtuhu means I awaited and watched him; arṣadtu lahu means I prepared something for him.”

On this account, raṣada emphasizes watching and waiting, while arṣada emphasizes preparing something for a particular purpose.

Someone may await a guest’s arrival; preparing a place to receive the guest additionally involves preparation.

Someone may likewise observe an enemy or keep equipment and a position ready for the enemy’s arrival.

The distinction should not become an absolute rule.

Some lexical accounts also give arṣada the sense of waiting, and the two verbs overlap in other uses.

Qur’anic analysis must therefore consider each form’s construction and the object of watching or preparation.

2. Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period: death waiting and watchfulness at a passage

Wa-inna al-manāyā li-al-rijāli bi-marṣadi

A poem by the pre-Islamic poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd al-ʿIbādī states:

أَعَاذِلُ إِنَّ الْجَهْلَ مِنْ ذِلَّةِ الْفَتَى
وَإِنَّ الْمَنَايَا لِلرِّجَالِ بِمَرْصَدِ

a-ʿādhilu inna al-jahla min dhillati al-fatā / wa-inna al-manāyā li-al-rijāli bi-marṣadi

“Reproacher, ignorance is part of a person’s abasement, and deaths lie in wait for people.”

Bi-marṣadi expresses being at a place of ambush.

The poet imagines death as waiting for human beings.

Marṣad thus appears beyond an actual military ambush site in literary imagery attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Death is pictured as a watcher whose arrival no person can escape forever.

This does not make death a physical being literally sitting beside a road and pursuing people.

The ambush image conveys death’s possible nearness and life’s instability.

The first hemistich has variants, including dhillah and ladhdhah. These do not change bi-marṣadi in the second.

The verse occurs in poetry attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd and early literary collections; its precise composition date is not independently established here.

Marāṣid in Hudhalī poetry

A verse attributed to Maʿqil ibn Khuwaylid al-Hudhalī states:

أَبَا مَعْقَلٍ لَا تُوطِئَنَّكَ بَغَاضَتِي
رُؤُوسَ الْأَفَاعِي فِي مَرَاصِدِهَا الْعُرْمِ

abā maʿqalin lā tūṭiʾannaka baghāḍatī / ruʾūsa al-afāʿī fī marāṣidihā al-ʿurmi

“Abū Maʿqil, do not let hostility between us lead you to tread upon the heads of speckled vipers in their hiding places.” Baghāḍatī concerns hostility involving the speaker; translation should not restrict it without explanation to hostility felt by the speaker alone.

Marāṣidihā concerns places where vipers hide and await prey or passersby.

Marāṣid is the plural of marṣad.

The poet warns against hostility through the image of entering dangerous snakes’ ambush sites.

A marṣad can therefore concern an animal’s ambush position too.

The root is not restricted to human or military observation.

Transmissions vary in some words and prepositions. The quoted form occurs in lexical accounts attributed to Maʿqil ibn Khuwaylid, but its precise date is not independently established here.

What the early witnesses show

The witnesses illustrate related but different uses.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd uses an ambush site as an image of death waiting.

The Hudhalī verse concerns a dangerous animal’s hiding and waiting place.

Both involve readiness and waiting at a suitable position.

Neither makes raṣd invariably secret or harmful.

The attested use for awaiting an opportunity to repay kindness also permits a positive purpose.

3. Additional uses: guards, hunting, and expected rain

Raṣad: watchers and guards

Raṣad can name a group of watchers in addition to the act of watching.

A lexical explanation of raṣad as watchers states:

الرَّصَدُ هُمُ الْقَوْمُ الَّذِينَ يَرْصُدُونَ كَالْحَرَسِ

al-raṣadu humu al-qawmu alladhīna yarṣudūna ka-al-ḥarasi

“Raṣad are people who watch, like guards.”

Raṣad can therefore be a collective noun.

Some uses apply the same form to one watcher.

Its apparently singular form therefore does not invariably mean only one guard.

The Qur’an uses the form in two settings: describing a shihāb, and naming what is stationed for guarding and protection.

Their difference should be retained in interpreting the verses.

Raṣīd and raṣūd: animals waiting

Raṣīd is recorded for an animal awaiting prey.

Laythun raṣīdun describes a lion waiting to leap upon its prey.

Ḥayyatun raṣīdun likewise describes a snake hiding along passersby’s route.

Raṣūd is also recorded for a camel that waits for the other camels to drink before approaching the water itself.

Raṣd therefore need not involve attack or hostility.

An animal may await prey in one setting and its drinking turn in another.

Attentive waiting or readiness for an opportunity is consequently broader than attack and violence.

Raṣdah: rain and hope of further rain

Al-raṣd and al-raṣad are also recorded for initial rain, or rain after which further rain is expected.

Raṣdah can denote one occurrence of rain.

Arḍun murṣidatun is recorded both for land with sparse herbage and land that has received rain and is expected to grow plants. These explanations should not be reduced to the amount of rainfall alone.

The rain use is also explained through expecting further rain after the first.

The semantic connection with expectation is understandable.

It does not make rain or plant growth the root’s basic meaning.

These belong to Arabic’s wider vocabulary; none of the six Qur’anic occurrences means rain or sparse herbage.

4. Kulla marṣadin: an ambush position in Al-Tawbah

The first Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Tawbah 9:5:

فَإِذَا انْسَلَخَ الْأَشْهُرُ الْحُرُمُ فَاقْتُلُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ حَيْثُ وَجَدْتُمُوهُمْ وَخُذُوهُمْ وَاحْصُرُوهُمْ وَاقْعُدُوا لَهُمْ كُلَّ مَرْصَدٍ فَإِنْ تَابُوا وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَوُا الزَّكَاةَ فَخَلُّوا سَبِيلَهُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

fa-idhā insalakha al-ashhuru al-ḥurumu fa-uqtulū al-mushrikīna ḥaythu wajadtumūhum wa-khudhūhum wa-uḥṣurūhum wa-uqʿudū lahum kulla marṣadin fa-in tābū wa-aqāmū al-ṣalāta wa-ātaw al-zakāta fa-khallū sabīlahum inna allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Al-Tawbah 9:5

“When the sacred months have passed, kill the associators wherever you find them, seize them, besiege them, and sit in every ambush position for them. If they repent, establish ṣalāh, and give zakāh, release their way. The God is indeed forgiving and merciful.”

The structure of marṣad

Marṣad is a noun of place on the pattern mafʿal, from R Ṣ D.

In kulla marṣadin it means a watching or ambush position.

Kulla expresses comprehensiveness of the relevant positions, and marṣadin is its genitive complement.

The expression concerns watching routes and passages through which the people addressed may pass.

This fits the preceding verb:

وَاقْعُدُوا لَهُمْ

wa-uqʿudū lahum

Al-Tawbah 9:5

“And sit for them.”

Sitting here means taking a watching and waiting position, rather than merely resting.

Marṣad is therefore rendered as an ambush position in this setting.

An ambush position and a public road

Lexical accounts sometimes explain marṣad as a road.

Here it is more than the road’s name: wa-uqʿudū lahum and the confrontation setting indicate watching and waiting for people to pass.

Sit on every road preserves part of the spatial meaning but makes watching and ambush less explicit.

Sit in every ambush position for them is more suitable.

Marṣad itself does not mean killing or capturing.

Separate verbs, fa-uqtulū, khudhūhum, and uḥṣurūhum, express those acts.

Their meanings should not all be incorporated into the definition of R Ṣ D.

Treaties and confrontation in context

The verse occurs at the beginning of Al-Tawbah among passages about treaties and relations with groups of associators.

Al-Tawbah 9:4 discusses those who have neither broken their treaty nor supported enemies against the Muslims:

فَأَتِمُّوا إِلَيْهِمْ عَهْدَهُمْ إِلَىٰ مُدَّتِهِمْ

fa-atimmū ilayhim ʿahdahum ilā muddatihim

Al-Tawbah 9:4

“Fulfill their treaty with them until its term.”

Al-Tawbah 9:6 also says of an associator requesting protection:

فَأَجِرْهُ حَتَّىٰ يَسْمَعَ كَلَامَ اللَّهِ ثُمَّ أَبْلِغْهُ مَأْمَنَهُ

fa-ajirhu ḥattā yasmaʿa kalāma allāhi thumma ablighhu maʾmanahu

Al-Tawbah 9:6

“Grant him protection until he hears the word of The God, then convey him to his place of safety.”

Kulla marṣadin should be read within this setting of confrontation, treaties, and protection.

It does not yield an unrestricted command to ambush every person with a different belief.

Marṣad’s lexical meaning alone authorizes no violence either.

The ruling’s scope belongs to a separate study of fighting and treaties. The lexical result is clear: marṣad here means an ambush or watching position along a passage.

5. Irṣādan: preparation for someone who previously fought

The second occurrence is in Al-Tawbah 9:107:

وَالَّذِينَ اتَّخَذُوا مَسْجِدًا ضِرَارًا وَكُفْرًا وَتَفْرِيقًا بَيْنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَإِرْصَادًا لِمَنْ حَارَبَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَلَيَحْلِفُنَّ إِنْ أَرَدْنَا إِلَّا الْحُسْنَىٰ وَاللَّهُ يَشْهَدُ إِنَّهُمْ لَكَاذِبُونَ

wa-alladhīna ittakhadhū masjidan ḍirāran wa-kufran wa-tafrīqan bayna al-muʾminīna wa-irṣādan li-man ḥāraba allāha wa-rasūlahu min qablu wa-la-yaḥlifunna in aradnā illā al-ḥusnā wa-allāhu yashhadu innahum la-kādhibūna

Al-Tawbah 9:107

“Those who took a mosque for causing harm, disbelief, division among believers, and preparation for one who had previously fought The God and His messenger; they will swear that they intended only good, but The God testifies that they are liars.”

The morphology of irṣād

Irṣād is the Form IV verbal noun of arṣada.

Its familiar Arabic use concerns preparing or keeping something ready for a person or purpose.

Irṣādan is accusative here, alongside ḍirāran, kufran, and tafrīqan.

These words state the purposes or orientations of those who built or adopted that mosque.

The four expressions are nevertheless distinct.

Ḍirāran concerns causing harm.

Kufran concerns disbelief.

Tafrīqan concerns dividing believers.

Irṣādan concerns preparation and waiting for the intended person or purpose.

The words should therefore not all be assigned one meaning.

Li-man ḥāraba allāha wa-rasūlahu

Li-man identifies the person for whom the preparation was made.

He is described as follows:

حَارَبَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ مِنْ قَبْلُ

ḥāraba allāha wa-rasūlahu min qablu

Al-Tawbah 9:107

“Had previously fought The God and His messenger.”

The preparation therefore concerns someone with a history of hostility and fighting against the messenger.

Historical and exegetical accounts commonly identify him as Abū ʿĀmir the monk and discuss awaiting his return or his joining the opponents.

The Qur’an itself does not name Abū ʿĀmir or his residence here.

Such information requires historical study rather than becoming part of irṣād’s lexical meaning.

Preparation or making an ambush site?

Irṣād can here concern making and keeping a place ready for the person intended.

Lexical accounts give two explanations: waiting and preparing.

These are compatible.

Someone may prepare a place and then await another’s arrival.

The word itself does not establish that the place was necessarily built for direct armed attacks upon passersby.

Making an ambush site emphasizes hostility, while irṣād has a broader capacity to express preparation and waiting.

Preparation for a particular purpose is therefore more suitable as the study’s basic meaning; context and historical evidence determine how the place was used.

In aradnā illā al-ḥusnā: a claim of good intent

Al-Tawbah 9:107 continues:

وَلَيَحْلِفُنَّ إِنْ أَرَدْنَا إِلَّا الْحُسْنَىٰ

wa-la-yaḥlifunna in aradnā illā al-ḥusnā

Al-Tawbah 9:107

“They will certainly swear that they intended only good.”

The same verse then says:

وَاللَّهُ يَشْهَدُ إِنَّهُمْ لَكَاذِبُونَ

wa-allāhu yashhadu innahum la-kādhibūna

Al-Tawbah 9:107

“The God testifies that they are liars.”

A building’s appearance or claim of good intent does not itself establish its actual purpose.

Al-Tawbah 9:108 also includes:

لَا تَقُمْ فِيهِ أَبَدًا

lā taqum fīhi abadan

Al-Tawbah 9:108

“Never stand in it.”

Preparing the place thus accompanies purposes the Qur’an condemns.

This does not make every irṣād intrinsically blameworthy.

Arabic also uses the family for preparing something to repay kindness.

Moral value follows purpose and circumstances, rather than the root alone.

6. Shihāban raṣadan: watchfulness and readiness in Al-Jinn

Al-Jinn 72:9 states:

وَأَنَّا كُنَّا نَقْعُدُ مِنْهَا مَقَاعِدَ لِلسَّمْعِ فَمَنْ يَسْتَمِعِ الْآنَ يَجِدْ لَهُ شِهَابًا رَصَدًا

wa-annā kunnā naqʿudu minhā maqāʿida li-al-samʿi fa-man yastamiʿi al-āna yajid lahu shihāban raṣadan

Al-Jinn 72:9

“We used to sit in positions there to listen; whoever listens now finds a shooting flame ready and lying in wait for him.”

The verse occurs in the speech of a group of jinn.

In Al-Jinn 72:8 they say:

وَأَنَّا لَمَسْنَا السَّمَاءَ فَوَجَدْنَاهَا مُلِئَتْ حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا وَشُهُبًا

wa-annā lamasnā al-samāʾa fa-wajadnāhā muliʾat ḥarasan shadīdan wa-shuhuban

Al-Jinn 72:8

“We searched the heaven and found it filled with stern guards and shooting flames.”

They then describe their previous listening positions.

What role does raṣadan have here?

Raṣadan is accusative and relates to shihāban.

It can be analyzed as an adjective or a circumstantial description: a shihāb prepared to intercept a secret listener.

An early explanation describes a shihāb made ready and waiting for that person.

Raṣadan should not here be a separate group of guards independent of the shihāb.

The expression is shihāban raṣadan, linking the watching description to that shihāb.

A shooting flame ready in wait can convey the contextual meaning.

Does the shihāb have human awareness and will?

The expression establishes no such conclusion.

Arabic can describe something as ready and waiting without giving it independent human awareness and intention.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd’s poem, for example, describes death waiting for people.

That does not necessarily make death a being with human bodily behavior.

Raṣadan here expresses readiness and the listener being targeted.

R Ṣ D alone does not explain the shihāb’s physical nature or relationship with jinn.

Related verses about the heaven

Al-Ḥijr 15:17 and Al-Ḥijr 15:18 state:

وَحَفِظْنَاهَا مِنْ كُلِّ شَيْطَانٍ رَجِيمٍ ۝ إِلَّا مَنِ اسْتَرَقَ السَّمْعَ فَأَتْبَعَهُ شِهَابٌ مُبِينٌ

wa-ḥafiẓnāhā min kulli shayṭānin rajīmin / illā mani istaraqa al-samʿa fa-atbaʿahu shihābun mubīnun

Al-Ḥijr 15:17; Al-Ḥijr 15:18

“We guarded it from every outcast devil, except one who steals a hearing, whom a manifest shooting flame then pursues.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:6, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:7, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:8, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9, and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10 likewise discuss guarding the heaven and a piercing shooting flame.

They relate to shihāban raṣadan.

Combining them does not establish a certain physical structure, trajectory, nature of jinn, or precise interception mechanism.

The clear lexical point is watchfulness, readiness, and preventing the access described in context.

7. Min bayni yadayhi wa-min khalfihi raṣadan: guards of the message

The other raṣadan occurrence is in Al-Jinn 72:27:

إِلَّا مَنِ ارْتَضَىٰ مِنْ رَسُولٍ فَإِنَّهُ يَسْلُكُ مِنْ بَيْنِ يَدَيْهِ وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِ رَصَدًا

illā mani irtaḍā min rasūlin fa-innahu yasluku min bayni yadayhi wa-min khalfihi raṣadan

Al-Jinn 72:27

“Except a messenger whom He has chosen; then He stations guards before him and behind him.”

Al-Jinn 72:26 should also be read:

عَالِمُ الْغَيْبِ فَلَا يُظْهِرُ عَلَىٰ غَيْبِهِ أَحَدًا

ʿālimu al-ghaybi fa-lā yuẓhiru ʿalā ghaybihi aḥadan

Al-Jinn 72:26

“He knows the unseen and discloses His unseen to no one.”

The exception concerns a chosen messenger, with raṣad stationed before and behind him.

Raṣadan: a guard or guards

Arabic raṣad can be collective, denoting watchers or guards.

Guards or watchers is therefore suitable here.

This differs from raṣadan in Al-Jinn 72:9.

There it concerns a shihāb ready to intercept.

Here raṣadan occupies the position of something stationed before and behind the messenger.

The collective sense of guards is consequently clearer in this setting.

Yasluku: placing along a path

Yasluku belongs to S L K and here concerns inserting or placing something along a path or at a position.

Min bayni yadayhi wa-min khalfihi identifies the guards’ positions.

The verse thus describes guards stationed in relation to the chosen messenger and his communication of the divine message.

Raṣad itself does not lexically mean before or behind.

Spatial phrases establish position; raṣad supplies the guarding role.

The following verse and the purpose of guarding

Al-Jinn 72:28 states:

لِيَعْلَمَ أَنْ قَدْ أَبْلَغُوا رِسَالَاتِ رَبِّهِمْ وَأَحَاطَ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ وَأَحْصَىٰ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ عَدَدًا

li-yaʿlama an qad ablaghū risālāti rabbihim wa-aḥāṭa bi-mā ladayhim wa-aḥṣā kulla shayʾin ʿadadan

Al-Jinn 72:28

“So that he may know that they have conveyed their Lord’s messages; He encompasses what is with them and has counted everything.” Interpretations differ over the subject of li-yaʿlama; this question is separate from raṣad’s own meaning.

The verse connects guarding with conveying divine messages.

Early interpretations commonly identify the raṣad as angels protecting the message’s reception and delivery.

This explanation fits the context.

Raṣadan alone does not specify their number, names, manner of presence, or mode of operation.

Nor does the verse establish that anyone claiming a special message necessarily has such guards.

It concerns a messenger chosen by The God.

Raṣad therefore concerns guarding in divine communication here, without supplying details beyond the text.

The two raṣadan uses in Al-Jinn

The same form occurs twice in one surah but has different roles.

Al-Jinn 72:9 applies it to a shihāb ready to intercept secret listening.

Al-Jinn 72:27 applies it to guards stationed in connection with the chosen messenger.

Both involve watchfulness and readiness.

One describes a shihāb; the other a group of guards.

A word form should therefore not receive one mechanical equivalent in every verse.

8. Inna jahannama kānat mirṣādan: Hell waiting

Al-Nabaʾ 78:21 states:

إِنَّ جَهَنَّمَ كَانَتْ مِرْصَادًا

inna jahannama kānat mirṣādan

Al-Nabaʾ 78:21

“Hell is indeed a prepared ambush.”

Al-Nabaʾ 78:22 continues:

لِلطَّاغِينَ مَآبًا

li-al-ṭāghīna maʾāban

Al-Nabaʾ 78:22

“A place of return for the transgressors.”

Mirṣādan is the predicate of kānat, referring to Hell.

Mirṣād: a watching and waiting position

Mirṣād follows the mifʿāl pattern.

Lexical accounts record it for watching, waiting, and ambush positions.

Some uses apply it to something ready and waiting.

Al-Nabaʾ describes Hell through this expression.

Interpretations explain it either as an ambush site or a place ready to receive its inhabitants.

Both relate to waiting and readiness.

The mifʿāl pattern alone does not necessarily mean unlimited intensity or endless repetition.

Morphological patterns have capacities, but context determines precise quality and extent.

For whom is Hell described this way?

Al-Nabaʾ 78:22 answers:

لِلطَّاغِينَ مَآبًا

li-al-ṭāghīna maʾāban

Al-Nabaʾ 78:22

“A place of return for the transgressors.”

Ṭāghīn belongs to Ṭ GH Y and concerns transgression and exceeding limits.

Mirṣādan therefore relates to a particular group’s fate.

Mirṣād itself does not mean transgression.

That describes the people named in the following verse.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:27 and Al-Nabaʾ 78:28 also discuss people who did not expect an accounting and denied the divine signs.

Hell as a mirṣād thus appears within accountability and deeds’ outcomes.

Is Hell waiting like a hunter?

Arabic can describe a place or event through ambush imagery without giving it independent human will.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd describes death waiting for people.

Mirṣād likewise pictures readiness and waiting for those whose fate concerns Hell.

It does not establish that Hell necessarily sits along a road like a human being and personally decides sinners’ fates.

Accounts of particular passages and bridges around Hell should not become part of mirṣād’s meaning without Qur’anic evidence either.

The verse makes Hell’s readiness for the transgressors clear; the root alone does not explain the ambush site’s precise nature.

9. Inna rabbaka la-bi-al-mirṣādi: divine vigilance and oppressors’ fate

Al-Fajr 89:14 states:

إِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَبِالْمِرْصَادِ

inna rabbaka la-bi-al-mirṣādi

Al-Fajr 89:14

“Your Lord is indeed ever watchful.”

This use is particularly important because mirṣād concerns The God.

The preceding verses help explain it.

ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh in context

After mentioning ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh, Al-Fajr 89:11 and Al-Fajr 89:12 say:

الَّذِينَ طَغَوْا فِي الْبِلَادِ ۝ فَأَكْثَرُوا فِيهَا الْفَسَادَ

alladhīna ṭaghaw fī al-bilādi / fa-aktharū fīhā al-fasāda

Al-Fajr 89:11; Al-Fajr 89:12

“Those who transgressed in the lands and greatly increased corruption there.”

Al-Fajr 89:13 then says:

فَصَبَّ عَلَيْهِمْ رَبُّكَ سَوْطَ عَذَابٍ

fa-ṣabba ʿalayhim rabbuka sawṭa ʿadhābin

Al-Fajr 89:13

“So your Lord poured upon them a scourge of punishment.”

Al-Fajr 89:14 follows:

إِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَبِالْمِرْصَادِ

inna rabbaka la-bi-al-mirṣādi

Al-Fajr 89:14

“Your Lord is indeed ever watchful.”

Divine watchfulness here relates to transgression, corruption, and deeds’ consequences.

It is therefore more than neutral observation without consequence.

The God does not overlook oppressors’ deeds; their fate remains subject to His oversight and judgment.

Mirṣād’s meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from punishment occurring.

Mirṣād belongs to watching and waiting, whereas the preceding verse expresses punishment separately.

The root itself therefore does not mean punishing, although this context connects it with accountability and punishment.

Does bi-al-mirṣādi give The God a bodily location?

In human use, bi-al-mirṣādi can picture occupying a watching or ambush position.

Applied to The God, however, it should not be made into a bodily location or human manner of observation.

The verse does not portray a guard who must sit beside a road to see passersby.

Contextually, human transgression and conduct remain within divine awareness and judgment.

This agrees with other Qur’anic verses.

For example, Al-Nisāʾ 4:1 includes:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلَيْكُمْ رَقِيبًا

inna allāha kāna ʿalaykum raqīban

Al-Nisāʾ 4:1

“The God is indeed watchful over you.”

Al-Baqarah 2:265 also includes:

وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

wa-allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīrun

Al-Baqarah 2:265

“The God sees what you do.”

These concern divine awareness and watchfulness, but R Q B and baṣīr are lexically distinct from R Ṣ D.

Al-Fajr’s mirṣād image emphasizes that no one’s deeds and consequences lie outside divine judgment.

Does divine vigilance concern only disbelievers?

The immediate context presents ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh as examples of transgression and corruption.

The warning thus addresses those behaving similarly.

It does not restrict divine awareness to these three peoples or to disbelievers alone.

Many other verses express divine knowledge of everyone’s actions.

Not all those meanings should be included in mirṣād’s lexical definition.

The root concerns watching and waiting; the wider verses establish the scope of divine knowledge and judgment.

Mirṣād in Al-Nabaʾ and Al-Fajr

Al-Nabaʾ 78:21 presents Hell as mirṣādan.

Al-Fajr 89:14 uses bi-al-mirṣādi concerning the Lord.

Both contain watchfulness and waiting.

Their referents and contextual meanings differ.

Al-Nabaʾ presents Hell as an ambush ready for the transgressors’ fate.

Al-Fajr emphasizes divine watchfulness and oppressors’ actions remaining subject to judgment.

The verses should not be reduced to one wholly bodily image.

Mirṣād here is not itself a divine proper name, but part of an expression describing divine vigilance and judgment.

10. Comparison with related roots

R Ṣ D (raṣd) and R Q B (raqb)

Arabic raqaba concerns watching, waiting, and observation.

Raṣada likewise concerns watching and waiting.

Readiness for something to arrive or pass is prominent in R Ṣ D’s familiar core.

R Q B in various uses can concern sustained watching and observation.

This does not make an absolute rule that R Q B always means only observation and R Ṣ D always ambush.

They overlap in some uses.

Bi-al-mirṣādi in Al-Fajr 89:14, for example, concerns divine vigilance but is not structurally or wholly identical to raqīban in Al-Nisāʾ 4:1.

Study of R Q B

R Ṣ D (raṣd) and N Ẓ R (naẓar)

Arabic naẓar concerns looking and, in some constructions, waiting.

Raṣd can also express waiting and watchfulness.

Naẓar does not invariably mean preparing for a particular event.

Someone may look at something without awaiting its arrival or passage.

Raṣd more often involves something kept under observation or an arrival prepared for.

Raṣad should therefore not always be translated as looking.

Marṣad in Al-Tawbah is an ambush site; raṣad in Al-Jinn concerns guarding and readiness.

Study of N Ẓ R

R Ṣ D (raṣd) and Ḥ R S (ḥaras)

Ḥaras concerns guards and protection.

Al-Jinn 72:8 includes:

مُلِئَتْ حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا وَشُهُبًا

muliʾat ḥarasan shadīdan wa-shuhuban

Al-Jinn 72:8

“It was filled with stern guards and shooting flames.”

Raṣadan in Al-Jinn 72:27 likewise concerns guards of the message.

Both share watching and protecting.

Ḥaras clearly concerns guards and protection; the R Ṣ D family includes forms for watching, ambush positions, groups of watchers, and preparation. The whole family’s range should not be treated as the meaning of the single form raṣad.

The R Ṣ D family thus extends beyond guards alone in these uses.

Study of Ḥ R S

R Ṣ D (raṣd) and ʿ D D (iʿdād)

Aʿadda is Form IV from ʿ D D and means preparing something for a purpose; its initial hamzah is not a root radical.

Arṣada can likewise express preparation.

Irṣād involves expectation or readiness for a person or event.

Al-Tawbah 9:107, for example, concerns preparing for someone who had previously fought The God and His messenger.

Irṣād is close in meaning to iʿdād here.

Not every preparation is necessarily raṣd.

Someone can prepare food or make a tool without watching or waiting.

They therefore overlap in some uses without being identical.

Study of ʿ D D

R Ṣ D (raṣd) and K M N (kumūn)

Kamana concerns hiding and lying in ambush.

Raṣada can also occur in an ambush setting.

Hiding and watching are nevertheless distinct.

Someone can hide without awaiting a particular person.

A watcher can also occupy a visible lookout position.

Secrecy is consequently not an invariable condition of R Ṣ D.

Confrontation in Al-Tawbah 9:5 emphasizes ambush, while Al-Jinn 72:27 concerns guarding and protection rather than necessarily hidden ambush.

R Ṣ D (raṣd) and Ḥ Ṣ Y (iḥṣāʾ)

Al-Jinn 72:28 includes:

وَأَحْصَىٰ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ عَدَدًا

wa-aḥṣā kulla shayʾin ʿadadan

Al-Jinn 72:28

“He has counted everything.”

Aḥṣā belongs to Ḥ Ṣ Y and concerns counting and comprehending quantity precisely.

Raṣad in the previous verse concerns guards.

They share a context without having the same meaning.

Watching may accompany counting and recording information, but raṣd itself does not mean counting.

Modern uses for monitoring statistics or information should therefore not turn every Qur’anic derivative into recording and counting data.

Study of Ḥ Ṣ Y

11. Modern uses and their limits for Qur’anic Arabic

Modern Persian and Arabic use raṣd in many fields.

Observing stars, monitoring weather, markets, social activity, and data are familiar examples.

They share watching and following something’s condition over time.

The Persian raṣadkhānah names a place constructed for observing and studying celestial or other scientific phenomena.

These uses relate to watching and waiting.

They do not make marṣad in Al-Tawbah 9:5 a scientific observatory or raṣadan in Al-Jinn necessarily a data-recording instrument.

Financial use of the family also includes setting aside money for a purpose, such as repaying a debt. In such cases, allocated funds or money set aside is clearer in Persian than an ambiguous expression literally meaning monitored funds.

This financial sense relates to irṣād as making something ready for a purpose, but Al-Tawbah 9:107 concerns preparation for a particular person.

Modern financial and administrative usage should therefore not replace the verse’s meaning.

Translation should distinguish watching, an ambush position, preparation, and descriptions of guards.

Al-Tawbah 9:5

“Your Lord is indeed ever watchful.”

Watchful conveys the contextual sense naturally.

13. Conclusion

Arabic R Ṣ D concerns watchfulness, attentive waiting, and readiness for something’s arrival, passage, or occurrence.

This appears in ambush sites, lookouts, guarding, preparing something for a purpose, and awaiting a suitable opportunity.

Early evidence shows varied purposes.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd depicts death waiting for people.

The Hudhalī verse uses marāṣid for vipers’ hiding places.

The lexical use concerning repaying kindness likewise describes awaiting an opportunity to respond to another’s good deed.

Raṣd is consequently neither invariably hostile nor invariably protective; context establishes its purpose and value.

The Qur’an uses the root six times in four main forms.

Marṣad in Al-Tawbah 9:5 is an ambush or watching position along a passage. It occurs in confrontation and treaty provisions and should not become an unrestricted command to harm all opponents.

Irṣād in Al-Tawbah 9:107 concerns preparing and waiting for someone who previously fought The God and His messenger. The mosque builders’ harmful aims accompany it, but the root itself means neither corruption nor warfare.

Raṣadan in Al-Jinn 72:9 describes a shihāb ready to intercept secret listening.

The same form in Al-Jinn 72:27 names guards stationed in connection with divine communication.

Mirṣād in Al-Nabaʾ 78:21 describes Hell as an ambush ready for transgressors’ fate.

Bi-al-mirṣādi in Al-Fajr 89:14 describes divine vigilance amid earlier peoples’ corruption and transgression, emphasizing that their conduct and fate do not go unnoticed.

These six uses show one family functioning in very different settings while retaining a recognizable core.

One setting’s particular features should not be transferred to every other use.

Military ambush, preparing a place, guarding a message, a shihāb ready to intercept, and divine vigilance all relate to raṣd, while differing in agent, subject, and realization.

Watching and waiting do not themselves mean knowing everything, recording all data, punishing, or attacking. Other forms and context must establish such features.

Final definition: R Ṣ D concerns watching and waiting with readiness for something’s arrival or occurrence. Raṣd names the act of watching; raṣad can name a watcher or group of watchers. Marṣad and mirṣād concern positions of ambush and waiting; irṣād emphasizes preparing or keeping something ready for a person or purpose. Qur’anic uses extend from ambush and guarding to ultimate outcomes and divine vigilance. The purpose’s goodness or badness, the watcher, the manner of waiting, and the outcome require word structure and context rather than the root alone.
