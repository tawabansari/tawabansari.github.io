Severe calamity and grievous events: from suffering a dreadful misfortune to astuteness, resourcefulness, and finding a way forward

The Arabic family D H Y has two familiar semantic fields: a grievous event or great calamity that befalls someone, and astuteness, quick understanding, and the ability to assess matters and find a suitable way to achieve a purpose.

Its forms include دَاهِيَة (dāhiyah), a great calamity or dreadful event; دَوَاهِي (dawāhī), great calamities; دَهَاء (dahāʾ), astuteness and resourcefulness; and دَاهٍ (dāhin) or دَاهِيَة (dāhiyah), describing someone exceptionally astute and capable.

In some uses, astuteness accompanies scheming, deception, or concealed maneuvering. Dahāʾ should nevertheless not be restricted to deceit: lexical sources also record quick understanding and sound assessment of affairs.

The family has both wāw and yāʾ forms in Arabic. Early sources therefore sometimes discuss it under D H W or D H W/Y. Qur’anic morphological indexing places its actual Qur’anic occurrence under D H Y.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as أَدْهَىٰ (adhā) in Al-Qamar 54:46. There it means “more severe, more dreadful, or more calamitous,” rather than more astute or deceitful.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one occurrence under D H Y

The central point of this study is that great calamity and exceptional astuteness are established uses of one family, but one cannot replace the other in every sentence. The word’s formation, subject, and context determine its precise meaning.

1. The semantic core in Arabic

Dahāhu: a grievous event befell him

Ibn Fāris writes of this family in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

يَدُلُّ عَلَى إِصَابَةِ الشَّيْءِ بِالشَّيْءِ بِمَا لَا يَسُرُّ

yadullu ʿalā iṣābati al-shayʾi bi-al-shayʾi bi-mā lā yasurru

It denotes one thing striking another in an unwelcome manner.

He illustrates this principle with مَا دَهَاهُ (mā dahāhu): what befell him, or what grievous event happened to him?

Ibn Fāris emphasizes that the usage concerns something causing distress or harm.

This branch therefore concerns more than an event merely occurring: its distressing character and severity for the person affected also matter.

For example, a sudden event that transforms someone’s life and causes great loss may be called a دَاهِيَة (dāhiyah).

An ordinary occurrence or small change is not necessarily a dāhiyah simply because it is new.

This usage emphasizes the calamity’s magnitude and harmful effect.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: an unwelcome occurrence and the proposed connection with judgment

Must a dāhiyah be sudden?

Some lexical explanations describe a dāhiyah as an event that strikes someone from an unexpected direction.

Lisān al-ʿArab states that a grievous occurrence reaching someone from a place of supposed safety can be described through this family.

This usage emphasizes surprise and unforeseen harm.

It does not establish that every dāhiyah must occur instantaneously without prior warning.

Someone may know of a danger and still experience a very severe calamity when it occurs.

Suddenness may therefore accompany a dāhiyah, but it is not essential in every use.

Dāhiyah: a great calamity

Mukhtār al-Ṣiḥāḥ states:

الدَّاهِيَةُ: الْأَمْرُ الْعَظِيمُ

al-dāhiyatu: al-amru al-ʿaẓīmu

Dāhiyah means a great event or affair.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains this through the description مُنْكَر (munkar): a severe, unwelcome, and extraordinary event.

The plural of dāhiyah is دَوَاهِي (dawāhī).

Arabic uses the expression:

دَوَاهِي الدَّهْرِ

dawāhī al-dahri

It means the calamities and grievous events of the passing ages.

Here time supplies the setting for life’s varied events.

The expression does not establish that time itself has an independent will that creates calamities.

Language can attribute an occurrence to the passing ages without deciding its ultimate cause.

Dāhiyah likewise does not mean every natural event. Such an event may be beneficial or harmful; this branch of dāhiyah emphasizes a grievous and dreadful outcome.

Mukhtār al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Al-Muḥkam: great calamity, sound judgment, and wāw/yāʾ forms

2. Dahāʾ: astuteness and sound assessment

Quick understanding and capability

Another branch of this family is دَهَاء (dahāʾ).

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الدَّهْوُ وَالدَّهَاءُ: الْعَقْلُ

al-dahwu wa-al-dahāʾu: al-ʿaqlu

Dahāʾ thus concerns reason and intelligence in one of its established uses.

Ibn Sīdah likewise relates al-dahy and al-dahāʾ to capability and discernment in affairs.

The Arabic Language Academy in Cairo explains دَهِيَ (dahiya) as becoming perceptive and experienced in affairs and developing sound judgment about them.

A dāhin may therefore be someone who examines problems well, recognizes different approaches, and makes a suitable decision after assessing the circumstances.

This is close to the Persian use of dāhī for an astute, capable person.

Astuteness itself, however, does not mean moral uprightness or wrongdoing.

People may use their intellectual ability to solve problems, prevent harm, or help others.

They may also use the same ability to deceive, exploit, or entrap others.

Intelligence and astuteness are therefore abilities; their moral value depends on their purpose and manner of use.

Dāhiyah as an exceptionally astute person

Arabic uses رَجُلٌ دَاهِيَةٌ (rajulun dāhiyatun) for someone highly skilled in understanding and managing affairs.

Dāhiyah here is a description of a very astute person, rather than the name of a calamity.

The final ة does not mean that the person is female. In this use, the ending can intensify the description.

Rajulun dāhiyatun is therefore a valid Arabic expression.

When dāhiyah refers to a grievous event, however, it should not be translated as an astute person.

The subject and sentence structure clarify this difference.

Astuteness and bringing trouble upon another

Ibn Fāris attempts to explain a connection between the calamity and astuteness branches.

In his analysis, an astute person can reach an intended target through judgment and resourcefulness, just as a grievous event strikes someone.

Reaching a target or affecting another person thus connects the two uses in this account.

The analysis deserves consideration, but should not be treated as conclusive proof of the historical course of derivation.

The two uses may have become associated over a long period.

What the available evidence clearly establishes is the presence of both calamity and astuteness in Arabic usage.

Is dahāʾ identical to scheming and deceit?

Lexical sources explain دَهَاء (dahāʾ) through reason and resourcefulness, but also through مَكْر (makr) and خَتْل (khatl).

Khatl concerns deceiving or entrapping another by concealed means.

Dahāʾ can therefore express deceptive cleverness in a suitable context.

That negative meaning does not encompass every use of the word.

In one situation, dahāʾ may mean sound discernment and a carefully considered decision.

In another, it may concern cunning and using knowledge to deceive someone.

A fixed translation of “deceitfulness” for every form in the family is therefore inaccurate.

Nor should every intelligent, farsighted person be considered deceitful merely because they are astute.

Lisān al-ʿArab: reason, astuteness, deception, and the intensive ending in dāhiyah

3. Early Arabic witnesses

Dahyāʾ: severe calamity in poetry

Al-Azharī’s Tahdhīb al-Lughah transmits this verse:

أَخُو مُحَافَظَةٍ إِذَا نَزَلَتْ بِهِ
دَهْيَاءُ دَاهِيَةٌ مِنَ الْأَزْمِ

akhū muḥāfaẓatin idhā nazalat bihi / dahyāʾu dāhiyatun mina al-azmi

Translation: “He is steadfast in safeguarding what he must, when a dreadful calamity from life’s hardships befalls him.”

The verse contains two forms of the family: دَهْيَاء (dahyāʾ) and دَاهِيَة (dāhiyah).

Both concern a severe and grievous event.

Their combination emphasizes the calamity’s severity.

Al-azm likewise concerns life’s hardships and constraints.

The sense of great calamity is therefore clear here.

The sources transmitting this verse do not, however, reliably identify its poet or exact date of composition.

It is evidence of usage recorded in early lexicons, but cannot be presented as certainly pre-Islamic poetry without independent evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab, quoting Al-Tahdhīb: the dahyāʾ witness without certain attribution

Dahāʾ and cunning in Al-ʿAjjāj’s poetry

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tahdhīb al-Lughah transmit this poetic expression from Al-ʿAjjāj:

وَبِالدَّهَاءِ يُخْتَلُ الْمُدْهِي

wa-bi-al-dahāʾi yukhtalu al-mudhī

It means: “Through astuteness and resourcefulness, even an astute person may be entrapped.”

Here al-dahāʾ accompanies يُخْتَلُ (yukhtalu), a verb concerning deception and entrapment.

Dahāʾ in this context therefore concerns cleverness used to deceive.

The deception is also conveyed by yukhtalu itself, however.

The entire meaning of dahāʾ should therefore not be restricted to deceit on the basis of this single poetic use.

Al-ʿAjjāj was an Islamic-era poet. This witness should not be treated as poetry composed before the Qur’an’s revelation.

What the witnesses establish

The early sources confirm two principal uses: dāhiyah as dreadful calamity, and dahāʾ as astuteness and resourcefulness, sometimes accompanied by cunning.

The examined evidence does not suffice to supply a poetic witness whose attribution and dating to the pre-Islamic period are certain.

It is better to retain these reliably transmitted witnesses than to attribute an uncertain verse to a famous poet merely to fill a section on pre-Islamic poetry.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-ʿAjjāj’s rajaz witness and D H W/Y variants

4. Qur’anic usage: Al-Qamar 54:46

بَلِ السَّاعَةُ مَوْعِدُهُمْ وَالسَّاعَةُ أَدْهَىٰ وَأَمَرُّ

bali al-sāʿatu mawʿiduhum wa-al-sāʿatu adhā wa-amarru

Translation: Rather, the Hour is their appointed time; and the Hour is more dreadful and more bitter.

Here أَدْهَىٰ (adhā) is an elative belonging to D H Y.

It follows the pattern أَفْعَل (afʿal) and is written with alif maqṣūrah in Qur’anic orthography.

In this context, adhā means more severe, calamitous, or dreadful.

It belongs to the branch of dāhiyah meaning a great calamity, rather than dahāʾ as astuteness and resourcefulness.

Why does adhā not mean more astute here?

Arabic elatives can be formed for various qualities.

Because this family concerns both astuteness and calamity, adhā too can have different meanings according to context.

When comparing people, for example, adhā might describe someone more astute or resourceful than another.

In Al-Qamar 54:46, however, the sentence concerns السَّاعَة (al-sāʿah), the Hour of resurrection.

أَمَرُّ (amarru) follows it, meaning more bitter in this context.

The two descriptions together express the severity and bitterness of a dreadful event.

“The Hour is more astute and more bitter” therefore does not fit the subject or construction.

The appropriate meaning is that the Hour is more severe and calamitous.

The role of amarru

أَمَرُّ (amarru) belongs to M R R, rather than D H Y.

Here it means more bitter.

Bitterness can literally describe taste. Arabic and Persian also use it figuratively for painful, unwelcome experience.

Adhā thus emphasizes the event’s severity and dreadfulness, while amarru emphasizes its bitterness and how hard it is to endure.

The descriptions are close but are not complete synonyms.

Both should therefore be retained in translation: “more dreadful and more bitter.”

Amarru here does not mean more commanding, either. Its resemblance to forms associated with أَمْر (amr) should not change the verse’s meaning.

Compared with what event?

Understanding the elative requires identifying what the Hour is said to be more dreadful than.

Al-Qamar 54:41 and Al-Qamar 54:42 describe warnings that reached Pharaoh’s people, their denial, and their subsequent punishment.

In Al-Qamar 54:43, the addressees are asked whether they are better than earlier peoples or have immunity recorded in the scriptures.

Al-Qamar 54:44 concerns a claim of collective ability to prevail.

Al-Qamar 54:45 foretells that group’s defeat and retreat.

Al-Qamar 54:46 then introduces a greater appointed time: the Hour.

The opening bal therefore redirects attention from defeat and worldly consequences to a more dreadful appointment.

The verse does not make worldly defeat unimportant; it says that their final appointment is more severe.

Ibn ʿĀshūr likewise explains this transition as the difference between warning of worldly punishment and warning of punishment in the Hereafter.

Ibn ʿĀshūr: the transition from a worldly warning to the Hereafter

Is the comparison only with the battle of Badr?

Al-Ṭabarī and other commentators connect the defeat in Al-Qamar 54:45 with the battle of Badr.

On this reading, the Hour is more severe for that group than its worldly defeat.

This reading fits the historical reports in the commentaries.

Verse 45 itself, however, does not explicitly name Badr.

Lexical study should therefore distinguish the Qur’anic wording from identifying its historical referent.

Even if the defeat is identified with Badr, adhā retains its meaning: the Hour is more dreadful than the grievous worldly event.

The same surah’s recollection of earlier peoples’ fate also broadens the field of comparison.

The root’s meaning should therefore not be restricted to a particular battle.

Al-Ṭabarī: comparison with worldly defeat at Badr

Is the time of the Hour itself a calamity?

The verse’s subject is al-sāʿah.

This does not mean that passing time is itself an independent cause of calamity.

Language can call a day or appointed time severe and dreadful while referring to the events occurring within it.

For example, “It was a hard day” usually refers to that day’s experiences and events, rather than attributing a physical quality of pain to time itself.

The dreadfulness of the Hour in Al-Qamar 54:46 likewise concerns the events and outcome described there.

Al-Qamar 54:47 and Al-Qamar 54:48 describe the offenders’ condition and punishment.

The context thus establishes that adhā concerns the severity of the outcome and punishment.

The word alone should not, however, yield the punishment’s precise nature, duration, or every detail of the Hereafter.

Is the Hour equally dreadful for everyone?

Verse 46 warns deniers and those resisting the signs and warnings.

The remainder of the surah distinguishes the offenders’ fate from that of the mindful.

Al-Qamar 54:54 and Al-Qamar 54:55 describe the mindful people’s good station.

Adhā in verse 46 should therefore not be read without regard for its addressees and context as meaning that resurrection will be exactly the same calamitous experience for everyone.

The verse warns the group under discussion.

This distinction comes from the network of verses; adhā itself expresses only severity and dreadfulness in that comparison.

5. Morphological and lexical forms

Dahiya and dahā

Arabic sources record دَهِيَ (dahiya) for becoming astute and capable, or possessing sound judgment.

دَهَى (dahā) also occurs for a calamity befalling someone or bringing a grievous event upon another.

Some recorded forms of these verbs also overlap in the field of astuteness and resourcefulness.

These differences must be examined through the verb’s formation and actual usage.

Dahāʾ

دَهَاء (dahāʾ) is a familiar verbal noun in the branch concerning astuteness and resourcefulness.

Some uses concern intelligence and the ability to assess matters soundly.

Other uses accompany cunning and deception.

It cannot therefore be considered invariably positive or negative.

Dāhin and dāhiyah

دَاهٍ (dāhin) may denote an astute, capable person.

In one branch, دَاهِيَة (dāhiyah) names a great calamity.

As a description, however, it can also designate a very astute person.

In رَجُلٌ دَاهِيَةٌ (rajulun dāhiyatun), the final ending can intensify the description rather than indicate a female person.

Dawāhī

دَوَاهِي (dawāhī) is the plural of دَاهِيَة (dāhiyah).

In expressions concerning the passing ages, it denotes calamities and grievous events.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Dahyāʾ and dahwāʾ

These forms accompany dāhiyah to emphasize the calamity’s magnitude and severity.

For example, دَاهِيَةٌ دَهْيَاءُ (dāhiyatun dahyāʾu) or دَاهِيَةٌ دَهْوَاءُ (dāhiyatun dahwāʾu) denotes a very severe calamity.

These forms show that wāw and yāʾ variants existed within the family.

Dahhā and tadahhā

دَهَّى (dahhā) is Form II and may denote attributing astuteness to someone.

تَدَهَّى (tadahhā) describes someone adopting the behavior of astute, resourceful people.

These formations do not establish that the person necessarily deceived another.

The context identifies the nature of the conduct.

Adhā

أَدْهَىٰ (adhā) is an elative.

In some Arabic uses it may mean more astute.

In Al-Qamar 54:46 it means more dreadful or calamitous.

This is the family’s only Qur’anic form.

D H W and D H Y: variants or separate families?

Early lexicons record wāw and yāʾ forms of this family.

Ibn Manẓūr reports from lexicographers that al-dahw and al-dahy are two linguistic forms within the field of dahāʾ.

The Arabic Language Academy in Cairo likewise presents these uses together when examining final-weak verbs with wāw and yāʾ.

Completely separating the two families without considering morphological and lexical evidence would therefore be inaccurate.

Counting Qur’anic roots, however, requires following a specified form and classification.

Qur’anic morphological indexing places adhā under D H Y.

This classification does not deny the wāw forms recorded in Arabic.

6. Comparison with related roots

D H Y and B L W

بَلَاء (balāʾ) in Arabic concerns testing and encountering circumstances that reveal a person’s character and response.

Such circumstances may be difficult or easy.

The calamity branch of dāhiyah, however, emphasizes a great and grievous event.

A dāhiyah can therefore be a test for a person, but not every test is necessarily a dreadful calamity.

Nor does adhā itself in Al-Qamar 54:46 mean testing; it expresses the appointment’s severity and dreadfulness.

Study of B L W

D H Y and Ṣ W B

أَصَابَ (aṣāba) in Arabic concerns something reaching or striking its target.

Ibn Fāris uses this image of striking to explain dahāhu too.

Striking or reaching can concern something beneficial or harmful, however.

Dahāhu in the calamity branch instead concerns an unwelcome event.

The expressions are thus close in something reaching a person, but differ in the event’s character and value.

Study of Ṣ W B

D H Y and M K R

مَكْر (makr) in Arabic concerns concealed planning and arranging a course of action to achieve a result.

Dahāʾ too accompanies maneuvering and cunning in some uses.

It can also denote astuteness and sound judgment without deception.

All dahāʾ should therefore not be equated with makr.

Nor does adhā in Al-Qamar 54:46 mean more scheming.

D H Y and K Y D

كَيْد (kayd) can denote planning and employing a strategy to achieve a goal, often accompanying confrontation or harm.

Dahāʾ may likewise include the ability to plan and identify ways of achieving a purpose.

The families are nevertheless distinct.

Kayd can concern the plan or action itself. Dahāʾ in its astuteness branch concerns more particularly a person’s ability and skill in assessing matters.

D H Y and Ḥ K M

حِكْمَة (ḥikmah) in Qur’anic usage concerns sound judgment, firm decisions, and considered conduct.

One branch of dahāʾ likewise approaches capability and sound judgment.

Astuteness and wisdom should nevertheless not be considered identical.

Someone may be very astute and use that ability to deceive others.

Wisdom in its commended Qur’anic uses, by contrast, accompanies sound judgment and action.

The ability to achieve a purpose therefore does not itself establish that purpose’s rightness.

Study of Ḥ K M

D H Y and N K R

نُكْر (nukr) and related forms can concern something unknown, objectionable, or severe in Arabic.

Lexical definitions of dāhiyah also use the description مُنْكَر (munkar).

This proximity does not make the families complete synonyms.

Dāhiyah in the calamity branch emphasizes a great, grievous event. N K R has a different range concerning recognition, denial, and objectionable conduct.

Study of N K R

D H Y and D H R

Despite their similar letters, these are separate roots.

D H R concerns time and the extent of the passing ages.

D H Y concerns great calamity and, in another branch, astuteness and resourcefulness.

Both families appear together in دَوَاهِي الدَّهْرِ (dawāhī al-dahri): the calamities of the passing ages.

Their combination does not make dāhiyah mean time, or dahr mean calamity.

Study of D H R

D H Y and D Ḥ W

D Ḥ W is a separate family whose Qur’anic use in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30 concerns spreading out and preparing the earth.

دَحَاهَا (daḥāhā) there belongs to D Ḥ W.

أَدْهَىٰ (adhā) in Al-Qamar 54:46 belongs to D H Y.

The written resemblance of some forms does not justify connecting spreading the earth with calamity or astuteness.

7. Challenges in understanding and translation

Does dāhiyah mean every great event?

Not without qualification.

In the principal calamity branch, dāhiyah is a great and grievous event.

An event’s magnitude alone is therefore insufficient.

A great event might be welcome and beneficial, whereas dāhiyah in its familiar usage concerns harm or hardship.

Does dahāʾ always indicate wisdom?

No.

Dahāʾ may be the ability to identify a suitable course and achieve a goal.

The same ability can be used to pursue a wrongful purpose.

Astuteness alone therefore does not guarantee sound moral judgment.

Someone may also have good intentions but lack sufficient skill or knowledge to solve a problem.

Intention, intelligence, and wisdom should therefore be distinguished.

Does Qur’anic adhā mean more deceitful?

No.

Al-Qamar 54:46 concerns the Hour and the severity of the outcome for the group being warned.

Adhā’s combination with amarru reinforces the meaning of severity and bitterness.

“More deceitful” or “more intelligent” is therefore unsuitable.

Does adhā identify the type of punishment?

No.

The word expresses comparative severity and dreadfulness here.

The elative alone does not determine the punishment’s type or nature.

Subsequent verses describe the offenders’ condition. Those descriptions must be examined separately rather than incorporated into adhā’s lexical definition.

Does mentioning the Hour mean it is calamitous for everyone?

Al-Qamar 54:46 warns particular addressees.

The end of the same surah describes a different outcome for the mindful.

The value and outcome of resurrection should therefore not be considered identical for everyone on the basis of one warning sentence without its complete context.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Al-Qamar 54:46 — وَالسَّاعَةُ أَدْهَىٰ وَأَمَرُّ (wa-al-sāʿatu adhā wa-amarru)

Preferred rendering:

“And the Hour is more dreadful and more bitter.”

“More severe and more bitter” is also defensible. “More dreadful,” however, better preserves the connection with dāhiyah as a great, calamitous event.

“More calamitous” explains the meaning precisely, but may make the verse’s translation less fluent.

“More intelligent,” “more astute,” or “more deceitful,” by contrast, does not fit the context.

The following equivalents can be used for non-Qur’anic forms:

  • دَهَاهُ أَمْرٌ (dahāhu amrun): a grievous event befell him.
  • دَاهِيَة (dāhiyah): a great calamity or dreadful event.
  • دَوَاهِي الدَّهْر (dawāhī al-dahr): the great calamities of the passing ages.
  • دَهَاء (dahāʾ): astuteness, capability, and resourcefulness; in suitable contexts, cunning.
  • رَجُلٌ دَاهٍ (rajulun dāhin): an astute, capable man.
  • رَجُلٌ دَاهِيَة (rajulun dāhiyah): a very astute man.
  • تَدَهَّى (tadahhā): adopted the behavior of astute, resourceful people.
  • دَهْيَاء / دَهْوَاء (dahyāʾ / dahwāʾ): with dāhiyah, descriptions intensifying the calamity.

Choose these equivalents according to the sentence rather than assigning one fixed translation to every derivative.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic family D H Y has two important semantic fields: grievous events and dreadful calamity, and astuteness, assessment of affairs, and finding a suitable course of action.

In the first field, dāhiyah is a great calamity befalling someone.

In the second, dahāʾ concerns quick understanding and capability, sometimes accompanying scheming and deception.

Ibn Fāris attempts to connect the fields through striking and reaching a target. That analysis should not become conclusive proof of a single historical origin.

Arabic sources record both wāw and yāʾ forms of the family. Qur’anic morphological indexing places its sole Qur’anic occurrence under D H Y.

That occurrence is adhā in Al-Qamar 54:46.

There the Hour is described as more dreadful and bitter than worldly defeat and hardship.

Brief root definition: D H Y concerns, in one branch, a great and grievous calamity befalling someone; in another, astuteness, capability, and achieving a purpose through resourcefulness or cunning.

The Qur’anic adhā belongs to the first branch and means “more dreadful or calamitous.”

The family’s essential distinction is that astuteness, deception, and severe calamity are not identical concepts, although recorded within one lexical family. Each form’s meaning must be established from lexical evidence, morphology, and its particular context.
