Root H Z M — Breaking resistance and defeat

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَهَزَمُوهُمْ
(fa-hazamūhum)
So they defeated them; active perfect

سَيُهْزَمُ
(sa-yuhzamu)
Will be defeated; passive imperfect

مَهْزُومٌ
(mahzūmun)
Defeated; passive participle

From indentation and structural breakage to the defeat of forces in confrontation

The Arabic triliteral root ه ز م concerns pressing inward, indentation, breaking, and disruption. Militarily, هَزَمَ means defeating the opposing force: overcoming it so that its capacity to stand and resist in that confrontation is broken.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs only for defeat in confrontation. It describes defeating an army, announces a group’s future defeat, or characterizes a group as مَهْزُوم.

Hazm is not identical to killing, destroying, or fleeing. Defeat may accompany those events, but its basic battlefield meaning is breaking the opposing force’s resistance.

1. The root in early Arabic

Classical Arabic also records هَزْم for pressing something so that part of it sinks inward. Pressure on soft fruit that creates a dent in its surface, for example, can be described through this family.

هَزْمَة denotes a depression or hollow, including low-lying ground. تَهَزَّمَ can describe cracking and structural breakage in something such as a dried leather waterskin.

These usages present two related images: indentation under pressure and the breaking of something formerly cohesive.

In war, هَزَمَ الجَيْشَ means defeating an army. An army standing in organized resistance is defeated when the opposing force breaks that resistance. Thus هَزِيمَة means military defeat, and اِنْهَزَمَ means to be defeated.

Yet the early Arabic family was not confined to defeat and indentation.

A witness from pre-Qur’anic poetry

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays describes an energetic horse:

«عَلَى الذَّبْلِ جَيَّاشٍ كَأَنَّ اهْتِزَامَهُ
إِذَا جَاشَ فِيهِ حَمْيُهُ غَلْيُ مِرْجَلِ»

ʿalā al-dhubli jayyāshin ka-anna ihtizāmahu / idhā jāsha fīhi ḥamyuhu ghalyu mirjali

“Though lean and slender, he surges with vigor; when his ardor boils within him, his resounding noise is like a boiling cauldron.”

اِهْتِزَام here refers to the powerful, reverberating sound associated with the horse’s movement and energy; explanations also describe it as the resonance of its neighing in its chest. The poet likens it to a cauldron’s boiling.

The verse is transmitted in early versions of Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah, with variants in some words. Attribution to a pre-Qur’anic poet matters for early usage, but does not by itself establish the date of this particular verse.

The sound-related branch also appears in هَزِيمُ الرَّعْد, the rolling roar of thunder. Lexicons describe the sound of a bow, a running horse, and a vigorously boiling pot with derivatives of this family.

The evidence shows that at least the sound-related branch was known in the early poetic tradition. Classical lexicons also record indentation and military defeat; the Qur’an itself directly attests the military use. This evidence does not establish which usage arose first in the language’s history.

An image of breaking structure may help explain the connection: breaking an object, breaking an army’s resistance, and a broken, reverberating sound. Such resemblance alone does not prove that every branch developed from one original material image.

The Qur’anic military use is clear in its own right. Understanding it does not require inserting indentation or thunder into verse translations.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

ه ز م occurs three times in the Qur’an, in two principal morphological categories:

  • فَهَزَمُوهُمْ: the basic verb’s active perfect, third-person masculine plural, “so they defeated them,” in Al-Baqarah 2:251.
  • سَيُهْزَمُ: the passive imperfect with a future marker, “will be defeated,” in Al-Qamar 54:45.
  • مَهْزُومٌ: the passive participle, “defeated” or “to be defeated” according to time and context, in Ṣād 38:11.

Forms such as هَزِيمَة, اِنْهَزَمَ, تَهَزَّمَ, اِهْتَزَمَ, هَزْمَة, and هَزِيم are known in Arabic but do not occur in those forms in the Qur’an.

All three Qur’anic occurrences concern the defeat of forces in confrontation. They do not employ the sound-related or material-indentation branches.

3. Qur’anic uses

Defeating Goliath’s army — Al-Baqarah 2:251

«فَهَزَمُوهُم بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ وَقَتَلَ دَاوُودُ جَالُوتَ وَآتَاهُ اللَّهُ الْمُلْكَ وَالْحِكْمَةَ وَعَلَّمَهُ مِمَّا يَشَاءُ وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُم بِبَعْضٍ لَّفَسَدَتِ الْأَرْضُ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ ذُو فَضْلٍ عَلَى الْعَالَمِينَ»

fa-hazamūhum bi-idhni Allāhi wa-qatala dāwūdu jālūta wa-ātāhu Allāhu al-mulka wa-l-ḥikmata wa-ʿallamahu mimmā yashāʾu wa-lawlā dafʿu Allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin la-fasadati al-arḍu wa-lākinna Allāha dhū faḍlin ʿalā al-ʿālamīn

“So they defeated them by the permission of The God, and David killed Goliath. The God gave David kingship and wisdom and taught him of what He willed. Had The God not repelled some people through others, the earth would have fallen into corruption; but The God is gracious toward all peoples.”

فَهَزَمُوهُمْ is formed from هَزَمَ. Initial fāʾ connects this outcome with the preceding confrontation; wāw marks the plural subject, and هُمْ refers to the opposing army.

The preceding verses describe a small group of Saul’s companions facing Goliath’s army. In Al-Baqarah 2:250 they ask their Lord for patience, firm footing, and support. The present verse states the outcome: they defeated the opposing army.

Lexically, فَهَزَمُوهُمْ means “they defeated them.” It does not alone say that every opposing soldier was killed or captured, or that all their equipment and possessions were destroyed.

The distinction between defeat and killing appears within the verse itself:

«فَهَزَمُوهُم بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ وَقَتَلَ دَاوُودُ جَالُوتَ»

fa-hazamūhum bi-idhni Allāhi wa-qatala dāwūdu jālūt

Defeating the army is attributed to a group of fighters, while killing Goliath is separately attributed to David.

If hazm meant killing, this clear distinction between an army’s collective defeat and its commander’s death would not be properly reflected in translation.

بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ is likewise not part of hazm’s own meaning. It qualifies victory in relation to divine permission. While attributing victory to The God’s permission, the verse preserves the human action of the group that fought.

The verse’s closing portion introduces دَفْع: restraining or repelling some people through others. Related to hazm but broader, repelling need not require an opponent’s complete defeat; stopping an advance or restraining a force can also constitute dafʿ.

Here hazm is thus the specific result of a confrontation in which Goliath’s army loses its resistance and the opposing army prevails.

A defeated army — Ṣād 38:11

«جُنْدٌ مَا هُنَالِكَ مَهْزُومٌ مِنَ الْأَحْزَابِ»

jundun mā hunālika mahzūmun mina al-aḥzāb

“An army of those factions that will be defeated there.”

مَهْزُومٌ is the passive participle of هَزَمَ. It describes someone or a group subjected to defeat, rather than one defeating others.

مَهْزُومٌ characterizes جُنْدٌ. Jund denotes an army or a group with collective force, while mahzūm describes its defeat.

مَا following جُنْدٌ is indefinite in force. Grammatical accounts assign it functions such as leaving the group unspecified or belittling it. It should not automatically be rendered as a definite number or quantity of troops.

The chapter’s context matters. Earlier verses describe arrogant resistance to divine reminder and claims of power. مَهْزُوم announces the outcome of that collective resistance to truth.

The passive participle alone does not establish past time. In a threatening, warning context, it can describe an outcome not yet realized. A translation should not assume that defeat has already occurred merely because the form is a passive participle.

Ṣād 38:12, Ṣād 38:13, and Ṣād 38:14 mention communities that rejected the Messengers. This connection shows that confidence in collective force and possession of an army do not guarantee ultimate victory.

Lexically, however, مَهْزُوم does not mean “false” or “disbelieving.” It describes defeat; who is defeated and why comes from context.

Defeat of a group confident of victory — Al-Qamar 54:45

«سَيُهْزَمُ الْجَمْعُ وَيُوَلُّونَ الدُّبُرَ»

sa-yuhzamu al-jamʿu wa-yuwallūna al-dubur

“The gathering will be defeated, and they will turn their backs.”

سَيُهْزَمُ is a passive imperfect. سَـ marks the future, while the passive expresses the gathering’s defeat without naming the agent within the verb.

الْجَمْعُ is grammatically singular although it denotes a group of people; سَيُهْزَمُ is correspondingly singular. وَيُوَلُّونَ then appears in the plural, referring to the members of that same group.

This grammatical shift clarifies the meaning: the gathering is defeated as one force, and its members turn their backs.

Al-Qamar 54:44 reports the opponents’ claim:

«أَمْ يَقُولُونَ نَحْنُ جَمِيعٌ مُّنْتَصِرٌ»

am yaqūlūna naḥnu jamīʿun muntaṣir

“Or do they say: We are a victorious gathering?”

The following verse answers this assertion of power. They rely on numbers, unity, or collective capacity, but the Qur’an announces their defeat.

وَيُوَلُّونَ الدُّبُرَ also matters. Turning one’s back here suggests retreat and turning away from the confrontation.

Defeat and turning one’s back are not identical. Hazm is the opposing force’s defeat; turning away can be its members’ visible behavior afterward.

An army may be defeated while some flee, others are captured, and others continue resisting. Flight should therefore not be made a necessary component of the root’s meaning.

Nor can سَيُهْزَمُ alone identify a particular war, people, or precise historical date. Identifying the historical referent requires separate evidence.

Al-Qamar 54:46 mentions their appointed Hour. The defeat in Al-Qamar 54:45 thus belongs to a broader warning, but that connection should not replace its direct meaning of defeat in confrontation with destruction in the hereafter.

4. Core meaning and morphological distinctions

The Qur’anic core of ه ز م can be stated as:

Breaking the opposing force’s resistance in a confrontation so that it is defeated.

This definition comes from the three Qur’anic occurrences; it should not be generalized to every lexical branch, such as thunder or a depression in the ground.

In فَهَزَمُوهُمْ, the agent of defeat is specified: a group performs the action, and the object pronoun identifies the defeated group.

سَيُهْزَمُ is passive and emphasizes a future defeat, without specifying its agent in the verb itself.

مَهْزُومٌ is a passive participle expressing a condition or description of defeat.

The forms differ in grammatical role, attribution of the action, and time; they do not create three entirely different kinds of defeat.

Breaking resistance does not mean that all physical capacity disappears or every member of the group perishes. A defeated force may reorganize later. Hazm itself does not pronounce on whether that return is possible.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

ه ز م and غ ل ب: defeating and overcoming

غ ل ب denotes overcoming and gaining the upper hand. This may occur in war, competition, or other confrontations.

In Al-Baqarah 2:249, a small group among Saul’s companions says:

«كَم مِّن فِئَةٍ قَلِيلَةٍ غَلَبَتْ فِئَةً كَثِيرَةً بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ»

kam min fiʾatin qalīlatin ghalabat fiʾatan kathīratan bi-idhni Allāh

“How many a small group has overcome a large group by the permission of The God.”

Al-Baqarah 2:251 expresses the confrontation’s outcome with فَهَزَمُوهُمْ.

Both words occur in one story. Ghalabah presents prevailing and gaining the upper hand; hazm presents the opposing force’s defeat.

In warfare both can refer to the same victory, however, so their distinction should not be made absolute.

ه ز م and ن ص ر: defeating and supporting

نَصْر fundamentally concerns providing help and support. In confrontation, that help may lead to victory.

In Al-Baqarah 2:250, Saul’s companions pray:

«وَانْصُرْنَا عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْكَافِرِينَ»

wa-nṣurnā ʿalā al-qawmi al-kāfirīn

“And help us against the disbelieving people.”

The following verse states the defeat of Goliath’s army.

Naṣr and hazm are related aspects of confrontation, not exact synonyms. Naṣr can be help and support before the outcome is settled; hazm concerns the opposing force’s defeat.

ه ز م and ف ر ر: defeat and flight

ف ر ر denotes fleeing and distancing oneself from something. Fear, danger, or pressure may prompt flight.

Hazm is defeat in confrontation. Defeat may lead to flight, but not every flight follows military defeat, nor must every defeat involve everyone fleeing.

Al-Qamar 54:45 displays this distinction by placing سَيُهْزَمُ الْجَمْعُ alongside وَيُوَلُّونَ الدُّبُرَ.

ه ز م and ك س ر: breaking structure and breaking resistance

In its familiar meaning, ك س ر denotes breaking an object or destroying its cohesion.

Arabic lexicons sometimes explain an army’s defeat through kasr, “breaking.” This connection shows the usefulness of that image for understanding military defeat.

But hazm should not always be translated as the breaking of a physical body. An army’s defeat does not mean the bones or bodies of all its members are broken; it concerns the breaking of their resistance in confrontation.

6. Challenges and suggested renderings

The main potential translation error is equating defeat with complete destruction.

“Defeat” and “be defeated,” like their natural Persian equivalents, clearly render the Qur’anic uses. “Break resistance” helps explain the core meaning but need not replace the simpler equivalent everywhere.

The following renderings suit the Qur’anic occurrences:

  • فَهَزَمُوهُمْ — Al-Baqarah 2:251: “So they defeated them.”
  • مَهْزُومٌ — Ṣād 38:11: “Defeated,” or in a future context, “to be defeated.”
  • سَيُهْزَمُ — Al-Qamar 54:45: “Will be defeated.”

In بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ, divine permission is a separate qualifier and should not be incorporated into hazm’s lexical meaning.

In Al-Qamar 54:45, وَيُوَلُّونَ should also be translated separately, rather than making flight replace the meaning of سَيُهْزَمُ.

In Ṣād 38:11, مَهْزُوم alone does not prove that defeat has already occurred. Its time must be determined from context.

For non-Qur’anic uses such as هَزِيمُ الرَّعْد, “the roar of thunder” is appropriate. Material indentation or structural breakage likewise requires its particular equivalent. These branches belong to the word’s history but should not be imposed on the three Qur’anic occurrences.

7. Conclusion

ه ز م has Arabic uses concerning indentation, breakage, disruption, and reverberating sounds. Classical sources record pressing and breaking to explain certain branches, while the verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays attests sound-related اِهْتِزَام in the early poetic tradition.

The Qur’an uses the root only for the defeat of forces in confrontation. فَهَزَمُوهُمْ expresses defeating, سَيُهْزَمُ a future defeat, and مَهْزُومٌ the description of being defeated.

Hazm is semantically related to ghalabah and naṣr without being identical to them. Killing, flight, capture, and total destruction are possible consequences of defeat, not necessary components of the root.

Final definition for Forqan: Qur’anic ه ز م means defeating or being defeated in confrontation, with the opposing force’s resistance broken. Killing, flight, or total destruction is not entailed by this root and requires independent evidence.

In the three verses examined, hazm is the outcome of forces confronting one another. Victory or defeat alone does not prove a group morally right or wrong; that judgment depends on the parties, the cause of confrontation, their conduct, and the verses’ context.
