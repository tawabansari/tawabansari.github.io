Harm, injury, and loss: from life's adversities to standing firm against threats

In early Arabic, ض ي ر concerns harm, injury, and adverse consequences affecting someone or something. ضَيْر means harm or loss, while ضَارَ means to harm or cause loss.

The Arabic expression لَا ضَيْرَ means “There is no harm” or “There is no objection.” It can be used when someone faces an unwelcome event but does not regard it as a decisive obstacle.

The meaning is not confined to bodily injury. Harm can affect property, ability, position, or interests. The root itself does not specify the kind, severity, or duration of the harm.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as ضَيْرَ in Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:50. It forms part of the response of those who believed after witnessing Moses' sign and whom Pharaoh threatened with amputation and crucifixion.

In response to this threat, they said:

لَا ضَيْرَ

lā ḍayra

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:50

Meaning: “There is no harm.”

The significance of their words is that they do not deny the real danger. Faced with a threat that could take their lives, they speak of returning to their Lord.

1. The root in early Arabic

Early Arabic dictionaries explain ضَيْر through ضَرَر and مَضَرَّة: harm or injury affecting someone or something.

The related expression ضَارَهُ يَضِيرُهُ is recorded with the meaning “to harm him.”

For example, لَا يَضِيرُكَ هَذَا means “This does not harm you.”

This usage shows that ḍayr can express the adverse result of an action or event. Something may be unwelcome without causing harm; in such a situation, one can say لَا ضَيْرَ.

Unpleasantness and harm are not identical. Someone may dislike a statement without suffering actual harm from it. Conversely, a danger can cause harm even when the person is unaware of it.

The meaning of ḍayr should therefore not be reduced to unpleasant feelings or fear.

A poetic witness attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī

A verse attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī reads:

فَقِيلَ تَحَمَّلْ فَوْقَ طَوْقِكَ إِنَّهَا
مُطَبَّعَةٌ مَنْ يَأْتِهَا لَا يَضِيرُهَا

fa-qīla taḥammal fawqa ṭawqika innahā / muṭabbaʿatun man yaʾtihā lā yaḍīruhā

Translation: “It was said: shoulder a load beyond your capacity; it is so full that whoever comes to it does it no harm.”

The traditional explanation understands the place as a village plentiful in food. Its abundance is such that those who come to obtain provisions do not diminish its supplies appreciably.

لَا يَضِيرُهَا means “does not harm it.”

This usage matters for understanding the root: its subject is not fear, death, or hostility, but harm that might arise from consuming resources or reducing supplies.

Here, the absence of harm is connected with abundance. Thus, ḍayr can describe a loss or injury affecting a thing or a community.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the transition from pre-Islamic to Islamic times. Early linguistic sources quote this verse, but its precise date has not been independently established. The poet's lifetime alone does not make it a securely pre-Quranic witness.

A witness distinguishing fear of harm from actual harm

A poem attributed to Ḍābiʾ ibn al-Ḥārith al-Burjumī states:

وَرُبَّ أُمُورٍ لَا تَضِيرُكَ ضَيْرَةً
وَلِلْقَلْبِ مِنْ مَخْشَاتِهِنَّ وَجِيبُ

wa-rubba umūrin lā taḍīruka ḍayratan / wa-li-l-qalbi min makhshātihinna wajību

Translation: “Many are the matters that do you no harm, yet the heart throbs with fear of them.”

In this verse, لَا تَضِيرُكَ ضَيْرَةً means that they cause you no harm.

وَجِيب refers to the heart's throbbing.

The poet places two different things side by side: what a person fears and what actually harms them.

A person may experience intense fear while awaiting an event that ultimately causes no injury.

This distinction shows that ḍayr is not fear itself, but the harm that an event may cause.

Ḍābiʾ ibn al-Ḥārith also lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This line is transmitted as part of a poem associated with his imprisonment under ʿUthmān. On that attribution, it belongs to the Islamic period and should not be presented as a securely pre-Quranic witness.

Its value lies in illustrating the continued use of ḍayr and its distinction from the feeling of fear.

Limits of the historical evidence

Early dictionaries clearly record harm and injury for ض ي ر. The Quranic occurrence also shows that this meaning was understood in the language of revelation.

The poetic witnesses examined here do not independently establish that these particular forms predate the Quran.

The root's meaning in early Arabic can therefore be explained with confidence without asserting a definite date of origin or historical priority over related roots.

2. Core meaning and forms

Important recorded forms include:

ضَيْر: harm, injury, or loss.

ضَارَ: he harmed or caused injury.

يَضِيرُ: he or it harms or causes loss.

ضَيْرَة: harm or injury; a form attested particularly in poetry.

ضَارَنِي: he or it harmed me.

لَا يَضِيرُ: it does no harm.

Dictionaries also record ضَارَنِي يَضُورُنِي ضَوْرًا alongside ضَارَنِي يَضِيرُنِي ضَيْرًا.

ضَوْر, associated with ض و ر, overlaps with ضَيْر in the field of harm. This overlap is clear in usage, but shared meaning alone does not establish the forms' historical relationship.

Similar words should not all be merged into one root without morphological and historical investigation.

Core meaning

In its established uses, ض ي ر can be defined as:

Harm or injury affecting someone or something adversely in condition, ability, or interests.

This definition does not predetermine the kind of harm.

For example, harm may be bodily, financial, or related to someone's position. These instances are identified from the sentence's subject matter; they are not separate meanings all contained in the word itself.

Nor does ḍayr inherently mean complete destruction, death, or irreversible injury.

Harm may be minor or severe, temporary or lasting. Its extent and outcome depend on context.

Conversely, the root does not imply that every injury can be repaired.

This family expresses harm itself, without specifying its magnitude, duration, or ultimate outcome.

3. Quranic usage: لَا ضَيْرَ

Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:50

قَالُوا لَا ضَيْرَ ۖ إِنَّا إِلَىٰ رَبِّنَا مُنْقَلِبُونَ

qālū lā ḍayra innā ilā rabbinā munqalibūn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:50

Translation: “They said: There is no harm; indeed, we are returning to our Lord.”

This is the root's only Quranic occurrence.

Understanding the expression requires attention to the preceding events.

In Ash-Shuʿarāʾ, Pharaoh gathers the magicians to confront Moses. They enter the contest trusting in Pharaoh's power and position, but after witnessing Moses' sign they fall in prostration and declare their belief in the Lord of all worlds, the Lord of Moses and Aaron.

Pharaoh refuses to accept their decision.

In Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:49, he accuses them of believing before receiving his permission. He then threatens to cut off their hands and feet on opposite sides and crucify them all.

They answer this threat:

لَا ضَيْرَ

lā ḍayra

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:50

They immediately give the reason for their stance:

إِنَّا إِلَىٰ رَبِّنَا مُنْقَلِبُونَ

innā ilā rabbinā munqalibūn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:50

The construction لَا ضَيْرَ

Here, لَا is the particle that categorically negates an entire class.

ضَيْرَ is its noun, built on fatḥah and occupying the accusative position. Describing it merely as an inflected accusative noun is not sufficiently precise.

The predicate is omitted and understood from context: there is no harm upon us, or we see no harm in this matter.

Arabic uses this construction to negate something categorically.

However, the grammatical negation of harm must be distinguished from the speakers' intention in this particular situation.

Without further context, لَا ضَيْرَ naturally means “There is no harm” or “There is no objection.”

But when spoken in response to threatened amputation and crucifixion, the issue plainly goes beyond a minor inconvenience.

Their following statement is therefore essential to understanding the sentence.

Are they denying bodily injury?

Pharaoh has threatened amputation and crucifixion. If carried out, these acts plainly entail bodily injury and potentially death.

Consequently, لَا ضَيْرَ cannot reasonably mean here that amputation or crucifixion causes no physical harm.

That reading would be incompatible with Pharaoh's threat.

Nor should their words be reduced to a mere pretence of fearlessness.

They state a clear reason for standing firm: their return to their Lord.

In their view, Pharaoh's power over their bodies and worldly lives cannot erase the truth they have recognized or prevent their ultimate return to God.

In this context, لَا ضَيْرَ thus denies the threat decisive weight against faith and return to God.

This conclusion comes from the expression's connection with إِنَّا إِلَىٰ رَبِّنَا مُنْقَلِبُونَ, rather than from ضَيْر alone.

They do not say that Pharaoh cannot injure their bodies; their words show that his threat cannot turn them away from the truth they have recognized.

Why does returning to their Lord matter?

مُنْقَلِبُونَ belongs to ق ل ب and denotes returning in this sentence.

Their return to their Lord is set against Pharaoh's power.

Pharaoh can threaten them and resolve to punish them in this life, but their ultimate destiny is not his to control.

This distinction clarifies their response.

After recognizing the truth, they do not make Pharaoh's power the final criterion for their decision.

His threat may have real bodily consequences, but it no longer determines their choice of faith.

This stance does not imply that life is worthless or that death is desirable.

Its significance lies in their refusal to knowingly deny the truth they have recognized merely to preserve life or advantage. Their particular choice should not be converted into a blanket rule for every situation of coercion.

4. Connections within the story of Moses

Returning to their Lord in Al-Aʿrāf 7:125

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:125, the same group answers Pharaoh's threat by saying that they are returning to their Lord.

That verse does not contain لَا ضَيْرَ, but it conveys the same fundamental response about returning to their Lord.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:126, they explain that Pharaoh resents them for believing in their Lord's signs.

They then ask God for patience and steadfastness.

This request matters: their resistance does not mean they have no need of help or endurance in suffering.

A person may refuse to yield to a threat while still needing help to endure hardship and remain firm.

Thus, لَا ضَيْرَ should not be understood as absence of pain, danger, or need for patience.

The limits of Pharaoh's power in Ṭā Hā 20:72

In Ṭā Hā 20:72, they tell Pharaoh that they will not prefer him over the clear signs they have received and the One who created them.

They then tell him to decree what he will, for his decree concerns this worldly life alone.

This verse helps clarify the context of لَا ضَيْرَ in Ash-Shuʿarāʾ.

They regard Pharaoh's political power, however extensive and violent, as limited to worldly life.

The human relationship with God and responsibility toward truth extend beyond that sphere.

They therefore refuse to deny what they have recognized in order to escape Pharaoh's threat.

Their stance illustrates resistance to coercion in belief; it does not make all worldly harm and suffering insignificant.

Hope of forgiveness in Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:51

In Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:51, they hope that their Lord will forgive their faults because they were the first to believe. This priority belongs to the narrative context; it does not mean that they were the first believers in all human history.

In Ṭā Hā 20:73, they also mention their past faults and the sorcery Pharaoh compelled them to practise.

This connection indicates that their change of position was more than a momentary response to losing a contest.

Having recognized the truth, they reassess their past and hope for divine forgiveness.

لَا ضَيْرَ thus accompanies acceptance of truth, departure from a wrongful command, and hope of forgiveness.

Nevertheless, ضَيْر itself does not mean repentance, faith, or forgiveness.

Those themes come from other verses in the narrative and help explain the setting of their response.

5. Comparison with related roots

ض ي ر (ḍayr) and ض ر ر (ḍarar)

The closest semantic family to ض ي ر is ض ر ر.

Early dictionaries frequently explain ضَيْر and ضَرَر through one another.

Both can denote harm or injury.

This similarity does not establish an identical derivational history.

ض ر ر has broader Quranic usage, addressing injury, illness, affliction, causing harm, and situations of necessity.

For example, Yūnus 10:107 speaks of harm affecting someone and its removal.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 addresses avoiding harm in documenting debt. The construction involving the scribe and witness has been understood both as forbidding harm to them and as forbidding them to cause harm.

Such uses illustrate harm in bodily, financial, or social matters.

By contrast, ض ي ر occurs in the Quran only in لَا ضَيْرَ.

That single occurrence cannot establish that ḍayr means only spiritual or minor harm while ḍarar is restricted to physical or severe injury.

Such a division lacks sufficient support.

The secure distinction here concerns Quranic distribution: ض ر ر appears in varied constructions, while ض ي ر occurs only in the response to Pharaoh's threat.

Nor does semantic proximity show that ض ي ر derives from ض ر ر. Morphologically, they are classified as separate roots; their historical relationship requires independent investigation.

ض ي ر (ḍayr) and أ ذ ي (adhā)

أَذًى concerns hurt or distress and can describe words or conduct that injure or upset someone.

Al-Baqarah 2:262 praises those who give without following their giving with reminders of generosity or hurtful treatment.

Here, hurt can involve humiliation or distress even without a separate financial loss.

Ḍayr can likewise denote harm, but the root alone does not identify its kind or how it is caused.

An act can be both hurtful and genuinely harmful.

Conversely, someone may dislike hearing a truth without that displeasure alone establishing that the speaker has wrongfully harmed them.

Hurt, displeasure, and harm must therefore be examined in context rather than collapsed into one concept.

ض ي ر (ḍayr) and خ و ف (khawf)

خَوْف concerns fear or apprehension about danger.

Ḍayr concerns harm or injury.

Someone may fear something that never harms them. Harm may also occur without prior awareness or fear.

In the account of Pharaoh's magicians, the threat is real, but they do not let fear govern their decision.

لَا ضَيْرَ therefore does not literally mean “We are not afraid,” although in context it can express disregard for the threat.

This distinction agrees with the poetic witness describing matters that provoke intense fear yet cause no harm.

ض ي ر (ḍayr) and ب أ س (baʾs)

In the Quran, بَأْس concerns severity, hardship, power, and, in some contexts, warfare or punishment.

Ḍayr denotes harm or injury.

A severe event may cause injury, but its severity and its harmful result are distinct concepts.

Pharaoh's threatened amputation and crucifixion are violent acts. Their potential harm is understood from the nature of those acts.

The word ضَيْر alone does not specify the kind or intensity of violence.

ض ي ر (ḍayr) and ض و ر (ḍawr)

Dictionaries also record ضَوْر with the meaning of harm alongside ضَيْر.

For example, لَا يَضُورُنِي is reported with the meaning “It does not harm me.”

This overlap shows that forms containing wāw and yāʾ could have similar meanings in certain Arabic constructions.

However, ض و ر also has other uses. تَضَوُّر is recorded for writhing or groaning in pain.

Semantic resemblance does not establish that ḍayr originally meant groaning, bodily twisting, or physical pain.

The Quran has ضَيْرَ with yāʾ, and this form should guide its root classification here.

6. Real injury and the meaning of “There is no harm”

The use of لَا ضَيْرَ in Moses' story raises a question about bodily harm and human values.

Human bodies are vulnerable. Amputation, torture, and death can inflict real injury.

The Quran repeatedly condemns injustice, corruption, killing, and harming people.

The magicians' resistance to Pharaoh should therefore not be turned into a claim that physical suffering is unimportant or that people should seek torture and death.

In this account, they have not created suffering for themselves. Pharaoh threatens violence to force them to abandon their new choice.

Responsibility for the threat and violence lies with Pharaoh.

They distinguish between two matters: the injury political power can inflict on their bodies and the truth in which they have come to believe.

They refuse to deny the latter in order to prevent the former.

This distinction is also intelligible in ordinary human life.

For example, a powerful person might threaten someone with losing employment and income if they testify truthfully. Losing work is real harm, but the threatened person may understand the consequences and still choose not to conceal the truth.

That resolve does not deny economic injury. The person has chosen responsibility toward truthful testimony over preserving personal advantage.

This illustration does not equate all situations or impose an obligation to accept every danger.

Elsewhere, the Quran addresses preservation of life, migration, defence against injustice, and human capacity. A group's choice in one situation must not become a general command to seek suffering or disregard safety.

The central point is that worldly harm does not necessarily make a choice wrong when it is made with awareness of truth and responsibility.

Nor does the ability to injure others give a ruler the right to dictate their faith, thought, or decisions through threats and torture.

Pharaoh attempts to make their believing dependent on his permission. Their response illustrates that recognizing truth and choosing faith cannot simply be made subordinate to a ruler's command.

7. Translation guidance for Forqan

In its basic usage, ضَيْر can be translated as “harm,” “injury,” or, in some contexts, “loss.”

“Harm” provides a broad, natural equivalent covering bodily injury, financial loss, and other adverse consequences.

“Injury” is also appropriate, although it may suggest physical or psychological damage more strongly.

“Loss” or “damage” may be preferable where property or an advantage is reduced.

For the Quranic expression:

لَا ضَيْرَ — “There is no harm.”

This concise rendering preserves the Arabic construction.

For Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:50, the proposed Persian rendering retains this meaning; its English counterpart is:

“They said: There is no harm; indeed, we are returning to our Lord.”

There is no need to add “Pharaoh cannot harm us” or “We are not afraid of death” to the base translation, since these are not the verse's actual words.

“No pain or suffering will befall us” is also inaccurate, given Pharaoh's threat of bodily violence.

An idiom such as “We have no fear” may convey the speakers' attitude, but it shifts the direct meaning of ḍayr toward fearlessness. That point belongs in contextual explanation rather than replacing the word's primary meaning.

Other forms can be rendered as follows: ضَارَهُ, “he harmed him”; يَضِيرُهُ, “he or it harms him”; and ضَيْرَة, “harm” or “injury.”

In each case, the kind and severity of harm must be understood from the verb's object and context.

8. Summary

In early Arabic, ض ي ر denotes harm and injury. Its verbs concern causing harm, while ضَيْر names the harm or adverse consequence itself.

Lexical and poetic evidence shows that this harm may involve diminished supplies, lost advantage, or injury to a person.

Early dictionaries often treat ḍayr and ḍarar as close or synonymous. They do not justify an absolute distinction in severity, kind, or duration without further historical evidence.

In the Quran, ض ي ر occurs only as ضَيْرَ in Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:50.

It forms part of the response of those whom Pharaoh threatens with amputation and crucifixion after they believe.

They say لَا ضَيْرَ and then speak of returning to their Lord.

This does not deny bodily injury. They stand against a real threat and refuse to let Pharaoh's power determine their acceptance of truth.

The word's lexical meaning and the statement's contextual significance must therefore remain distinct.

The final definition is:

ض ي ر: harm or injury adversely affecting someone's or something's condition, ability, or interests, without the root itself determining its kind, severity, or duration.

In Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:50, لَا ضَيْرَ negates harm. Within Pharaoh's threat, it expresses the stance of people who do not give possible worldly injury decisive weight against returning to their Lord and remaining faithful to truth.

The narrative thus supports a distinction between the power to injure and authority over another person's belief: Pharaoh can threaten them, but his threats cannot invalidate the truth they have recognized. This is a contextual conclusion, not an additional lexical meaning of ضَيْر.
