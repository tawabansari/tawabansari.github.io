The Qur’anic Root KH R B

Loss of habitation and upkeep: from disuse to structural ruin

The root خ ر ب occurs only twice in the Qur’an: خَرَاب in Al-Baqarah 2:114 and يُخْرِبُونَ in Al-Hashr 59:2. Both concern places: mosques in one passage and homes in the other. In the reading of Ḥafṣ, these are respectively a noun and a Form IV verb.

In Arabic, kharāb stands opposite ʿumrān, the inhabited, maintained, or flourishing condition of a place. A place becoming ruined therefore need not mean that all its walls collapse. It may fall into disuse, become deserted, suffer damage, or be physically destroyed: these are different ways of losing its maintained and inhabited condition. A brief interruption of use does not by itself make a building a ruin.

Lexical sources also record branches involving holes and breaches. Some lexicographers connect these with structural ruin through the image of an opening or break in something previously intact. This is a useful comparison, but it does not establish the historical origin of every use of the root.

An initial definition for the Qur’anic uses is:

KH R B: the loss or destruction of the inhabited, maintained, and usable condition of a place, leaving it deserted, neglected, damaged, or ruined. Physical destruction is a clear instance of kharāb, but does not exhaust its range.

Early Arabic: ruin contrasted with habitation and upkeep

The contrast between ruin and a formerly flourishing place appears in poetry transmitted under the name of al-Aʿshā, a poet of the pre-Islamic period who lived into the time of the revelation. His Dīwān and Yāqūt’s Muʿjam al-buldān, under Raymān, preserve the verse quoted below. It is an early poetic witness transmitted through later sources; it does not by itself establish the precise date of composition. Classical dictionaries explicitly place kharāb opposite ʿimārah.

يا من يرى ريمان أمسى خاويا خربا كعابه

“O you who see Raymān, now deserted, its buildings ruined.”

Khariban describes the condition of Raymān’s buildings. Al-Bakrī explains kiʿāb as the plural of kaʿbah. The verse witnesses ruin and deserted habitation; the word ʿumrān itself does not occur in this line.

This contrast distinguishes kharāb from a particular act such as knocking down a wall. Demolition can cause ruin, while kharāb can name the condition in which a place has ceased to be maintained or inhabited.

Lexicons also record خُرْبة for a hole or opening, including the loop of a waterskin. Such names for physical objects help clarify the lexical field, but their preservation in later dictionaries does not independently date every use to pre-Qur’anic Arabic. The connection between a breach and a ruined building is a lexical explanation, not a proven sequence of historical development.

Other branches are listed under the same root, including camel theft. These do not occur in the Qur’an and should not be forced into an artificial definition of its Qur’anic uses.

Al-Baqarah 2:114: the ruin of a mosque is not confined to demolition

وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنْ مَنَعَ مَسَاجِدَ اللَّهِ أَنْ يُذْكَرَ فِيهَا اسْمُهُ وَسَعَىٰ فِي خَرَابِهَا ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ مَا كَانَ لَهُمْ أَنْ يَدْخُلُوهَا إِلَّا خَائِفِينَ ۚ لَهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا خِزْيٌ وَلَهُمْ فِي الْآخِرَةِ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

“Who is more unjust than one who prevents the name of The God from being remembered in His mosques and strives for their ruin? Such people should not enter them except in fear. Disgrace awaits them in this world, and a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.”

Al-Baqarah 2:114

The order of the sentence matters. It first speaks of preventing remembrance of the name of The God in mosques, then of striving for their ruin. Their conjunction alone does not establish that the two expressions are synonymous or that the second merely explains the first.

Reading kharāb against ʿimārah allows a mosque’s ruin to include depriving it of worship and access, as well as damaging its structure. This interpretation is also transmitted from Ibn Zayd in al-Ṭabarī’s commentary; al-Ṭabarī himself prefers a different account of the event intended by the verse. Functional desolation is thus a contextual reading supported within the interpretive tradition, not a conclusion made compulsory by the conjunction alone.

This does not exclude physical destruction. Breaking and demolishing a building are clear instances of bringing it to ruin. The point is that the word need not be restricted to that single instance.

In this reading, the ruin of a mosque can include both damage to its fabric and the loss of its maintained life and use as a mosque.

KH R B and ʿ M R: two sides of the condition of a place

The lexical contrast between ruin and habitation also finds a place in the Qur’an’s vocabulary. Al-Tawbah says of maintaining mosques:

إِنَّمَا يَعْمُرُ مَسَاجِدَ اللَّهِ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَأَقَامَ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَى الزَّكَاةَ وَلَمْ يَخْشَ إِلَّا اللَّهَ ۖ فَعَسَىٰ أُولَٰئِكَ أَنْ يَكُونُوا مِنَ الْمُهْتَدِينَ

“Only those who believe in The God and the Last Day, establish Salat, give Zakat, and fear none but The God maintain the mosques of The God. It may be that they will be among the rightly guided.”

Al-Tawbah 9:18

The verse identifies those who maintain mosques through faith, Salat, Zakat, and reverence for The God. It therefore connects maintaining a mosque with the religious life of its community. These are qualities of the agents in the sentence; they should not all be made literal dictionary meanings of يَعْمُرُ.

This helps illuminate Al-Baqarah 2:114. On one side is a mosque where the name of The God is remembered and which is sustained by attendance and worship. On the other is someone who obstructs this and strives for its ruin.

In the context of a mosque, then, ʿumrān is connected with its active religious use, and kharāb with the loss of that maintained life.

The particular function should not be extended indiscriminately to every building. The setting determines what kind of use is lost. A mosque involves worship and remembrance of The God; a home may involve habitation and the soundness of its structure.

Al-Hashr 59:2: houses ruined by human hands

هُوَ الَّذِي أَخْرَجَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ لِأَوَّلِ الْحَشْرِ ۚ مَا ظَنَنْتُمْ أَنْ يَخْرُجُوا ۖ وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُمْ مَانِعَتُهُمْ حُصُونُهُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ فَأَتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَمْ يَحْتَسِبُوا ۖ وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ ۚ يُخْرِبُونَ بُيُوتَهُمْ بِأَيْدِيهِمْ وَأَيْدِي الْمُؤْمِنِينَ فَاعْتَبِرُوا يَا أُولِي الْأَبْصَارِ

“He is the One who expelled those among the People of the Book who disbelieved from their homes at the first gathering. You did not think they would leave, and they thought their fortresses would protect them against The God. But The God came upon them from where they had not reckoned, and cast terror into their hearts. They were destroying their homes with their own hands and the hands of the believers. So take heed, you who have insight.”

Al-Hashr 59:2

The physical aspect of ruin is clearer here. The verb takes houses as its object, and بأيديهم وأيدي المؤمنين, “with their own hands and the hands of the believers,” brings human, physical action into view.

The verse first mentions terror being cast into their hearts, then describes the destruction of their houses. That sequence matters within this event, but it cannot become a lexical rule that every instance of ruin begins with an inward collapse and then spreads to a building.

The root خ ر ب does not inherently contain such a psychological process. What the context supports is that fear and defeat accompanied the destruction of their houses.

Expressions such as “the inward death of a house before the death of its structure” may function as literary reflections, but they do not define the root.

Variant readings: يُخْرِبُونَ and يُخَرِّبُونَ

Al-Hashr 59:2 has been transmitted in two readings. يُخْرِبُونَ, the Form IV reading used by Ḥafṣ and most of the seven readers, has an ungeminated rā. Abū ʿAmr reads يُخَرِّبُونَ, with a geminated rā, as Form II. These are two readings of one occurrence, not additional occurrences of the root.

Lexical tradition offers different accounts of their relationship. Some distinguish ikhrāb, leaving a place deserted or in ruin, from takhrīb, directly dismantling or demolishing its structure. Al-Ṭabarī reports this distinction from Abū ʿAmr as an explanation of his preference for the geminated reading in the context of destruction by hand.

The distinction was not universally accepted. Other authorities treat the two forms here as synonymous or very close in meaning; gemination has also been explained in terms of intensity or extent of the action.

The readings are therefore relevant to the expressive range of Arabic, but they should not become an absolute rule separating the two forms. In either reading, the houses lose their inhabited and maintained condition.

KH R B and H D M: ruin and demolition are not exact synonyms

The Qur’an also uses the root ه د م of buildings:

الَّذِينَ أُخْرِجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ إِلَّا أَنْ يَقُولُوا رَبُّنَا اللَّهُ ۗ وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُمْ بِبَعْضٍ لَهُدِّمَتْ صَوَامِعُ وَبِيَعٌ وَصَلَوَاتٌ وَمَسَاجِدُ يُذْكَرُ فِيهَا اسْمُ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا ۗ وَلَيَنْصُرَنَّ اللَّهُ مَنْ يَنْصُرُهُ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَقَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ

“Those who were driven from their homes without right, only for saying, ‘Our Lord is The God.’ Had The God not checked some people by means of others, monasteries, churches, places of prayer, and mosques in which the name of The God is remembered much would certainly have been demolished. The God will surely help those who help Him. Truly, The God is Strong, Mighty.”

Al-Hajj 22:40

Hadm directly foregrounds breaking down or demolishing a building. Kharāb concerns the wider condition of losing habitation and upkeep.

Demolition can therefore be one way of producing ruin, but ruin does not always entail complete demolition. A house may become deserted, suffer partial damage, or fall out of use and become kharib, without its entire structure collapsing.

The tendency may be expressed as follows: hadm foregrounds the act of bringing a structure down; kharāb foregrounds ruin and the loss of its inhabited or maintained condition.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary governing every Arabic use.

Does kharāb mean “loss of purpose”?

In Al-Baqarah 2:114, depriving a mosque of its use is an important aspect of the contextual reading discussed above. A mosque where people are prevented from remembering the name of The God has been deprived of one of its essential functions.

The verse can express this without the philosophical language of a “death of purpose.”

The root خ ر ب does not itself say that every object possesses a philosophical final purpose, whose loss constitutes ruin. The linguistic and Qur’anic evidence supports a more limited description: a place loses its habitation, upkeep, or use.

This preserves the contextual reading of Al-Baqarah without making an external philosophical system part of the word’s definition.

Ruin and corruption are not identical

Both Qur’anic uses of kharāb directly concern places, whereas fasād has a much wider range. Corruption can concern conduct, society, the earth, a covenant, or a wider order.

An instance of ruin may also count as corruption in a particular context, but خ ر ب and ف س د are not synonymous.

Nor does the root automatically determine the moral judgment of the action. That depends on the agent, intention, and context. In the spatial uses examined here, the word describes the state of a place or an action that brings it out of an inhabited and maintained condition.

Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Baqarah 2:114, “and strives for their ruin” preserves the range well. “And seeks to destroy them” is also possible, but may suggest physical demolition more strongly to a modern English reader, narrowing the contextual possibilities for a mosque.

In Al-Hashr 59:2, “they were destroying their homes” or “they were laying their homes in ruins” are natural. Since the verse mentions action by hand, a rendering that foregrounds physical destruction is less restrictive here.

A single mechanical equivalent need not be imposed on both verses. The shared sense can be retained while allowing natural English suited to each context.

Conclusion

The root خ ر ب occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times of places: the ruin of mosques in Al-Baqarah 2:114 and the destruction of homes in Al-Hashr 59:2.

Classical Arabic places kharāb opposite ʿumrān. This contrast provides a useful approach to its Qur’anic meaning: a maintained, inhabited, usable place can lose that condition and become ruined.

The ruin of a mosque in Al-Baqarah 2:114 can be read more broadly than the breaking of walls. Obstructing remembrance and striving for ruin appear together, and one transmitted interpretation includes preventing the mosque’s use. This is a supported contextual reading, rather than an identity established by the conjunction alone.

Al-Tawbah 9:18 illuminates the other side. Maintaining mosques is connected with faith, Salat, and the life of their community. In this setting, habitation and ruin are also related to whether the place remains in active use or is deprived of that use.

Al-Hashr 59:2 makes the physical aspect clearer: houses are destroyed by hand. The preceding mention of terror belongs to the narrative of that event; it does not establish that ruin is lexically a psychological process later transferred to buildings.

The readings يُخْرِبُونَ and يُخَرِّبُونَ show that both Form IV and Form II occur in the transmitted reading tradition at this location. Some authorities distinguish leaving a place ruined from directly demolishing it; others regard the forms as very close in meaning.

Comparison with ه د م further clarifies the range. Hadm foregrounds the breaking down of a structure, while kharāb is wider and can include the loss of habitation, upkeep, and use.

KH R B: the loss or destruction of the inhabited, maintained, and usable condition of a place, leaving it deserted, neglected, damaged, or ruined. Physical demolition is a clear instance, but does not exhaust the Qur’anic field. The central contrast is with the inhabited and maintained condition expressed by ʿumrān.
