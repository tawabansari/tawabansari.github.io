Palatability, food and drink passing easily down the throat, and passage without difficulty or obstruction

The Arabic root S W Gh concerns food and drink going easily down the throat. In its concrete sense, something eaten or drunk passes without obstruction or difficulty. Related uses concern palatability, pleasantness, acceptability, and a task proceeding easily.

The root occurs three times in the Quran: twice as sāʾigh, describing milk and pleasant drinking water, and once as yusīghuhu, concerning a punishing drink that the person can scarcely swallow.

These uses differ significantly. Sāʾigh does not simply mean tasty or sweet: it primarily describes a drink that is easy and pleasant to swallow. Yusīghuhu in Sūrat Ibrāhīm concerns the person’s swallowing of a drink, but in circumstances of extreme difficulty.

The family’s central meaning can therefore be described as something passing smoothly and easily down the throat without obstruction or painful resistance. A drink’s palatability, and the acceptability of speech or conduct in extended uses, relate to this field. Their details should not all be combined into one physical or psychological definition.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Sāgha al-sharābu: a drink goes down easily

Early Arabic says sāgha al-sharābu when a drink passes easily down the throat.

Sāgha al-ṭaʿāmu likewise describes food that is easy to swallow.

Lexicons record sawgh and sawāgh for something entering and passing down the throat easily.

By contrast, ghuṣṣah concerns something sticking in the throat or becoming difficult to swallow.

This contrast clarifies the root’s concrete meaning.

In the first condition, food or drink goes down smoothly. In the second, swallowing encounters an obstruction, or the person feels something lodged in the throat.

Sāʾigh can therefore describe a drink that is easy and pleasant to swallow.

Palatability is not solely a matter of taste. A drink may taste pleasant yet be difficult for someone to swallow because of their physical condition. Conversely, a liquid may go down easily without being considered especially sweet or tasty.

It is useful to distinguish the drink’s qualities, ease of swallowing, and the drinker’s pleasant experience. These may coincide, but are not completely synonymous.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Yazīd ibn al-Ṣaʿiq states:

فَسَاغَ لِيَ الشَّرَابُ وَكُنْتُ قَبْلًا
أَكَادُ أَغُصُّ بِالْمَاءِ الْحَمِيمِ

fa-sāgha liya al-sharābu wa-kuntu qablan / akādu aghuṣṣu bi-al-māʾi al-ḥamīmi

“Now the drink goes down pleasantly for me; before, I could scarcely avoid choking even on water.” In one established explanation of this line, al-ḥamīm means cool water; another transmitted wording has al-furāt. This poetic explanation should not be imposed on every use of ḥamīm.

The line contrasts two conditions.

First, grief and anxiety are so intense that the poet finds no comfort even in drinking water.

Second, once the cause of his distress is removed, the drink again goes down easily and pleasantly.

The reported setting concerns conflict with another group and the poet’s effort to redress an injury. After attaining his aim, he finds relief and expresses the change through the drink becoming palatable.

Sāgha here does not describe a change in the drink’s chemical composition. It could be the same liquid, while the person’s condition has changed enough for him to drink comfortably.

This illustrates how the concrete ease of swallowing could express relief from distress in early language.

Literary sources transmit both fa-sāgha and wa-sāgha, and differ over the line’s attribution. Familiar grammatical commentaries attribute it to Yazīd ibn al-Ṣaʿiq and relate a historical setting.

The verse is attributed to a pre-Islamic poet and is well established in the literary tradition. Its precise composition date cannot, however, be determined independently of the transmitted accounts.

The relevant sense is clear: sāgha al-sharābu means that the drink went down easily and pleasantly; ghaṣṣa bi-al-māʾ expresses the opposite condition of obstruction and difficulty swallowing.

Siwāgh: something that eases an obstruction in swallowing

Arabic uses siwāgh, or siwāgh al-ghuṣaṣ, for something that helps food lodged in the throat go down.

In the old lexical example, water is described in this role.

The expression al-māʾu siwāghu al-ghuṣaṣ likewise presents water as helping a lodged mouthful pass down. Here it is cited as a lexical example.

The Umayyad poet al-Kumayt ibn Zayd says in a line of verse:

وَكَانَتْ سِوَاغًا أَنْ جَئِزْتُ بِغُصَّةٍ
يَضِيقُ بِهَا ذَرْعًا سِوَاهُمْ طَبِيبُهَا

wa-kānat siwāghan an jaʾiztu bi-ghuṣṣatin / yaḍīqu bihā dharʿan siwāhum ṭabībuhā

“They were a means of relief for me when I choked on a grief whose treatment would exhaust any healer other than them.”

Siwāgh extends here from its concrete meaning to an image of relieving grief and difficulty.

Just as the old expression pictures water easing a lodged mouthful, a person’s or group’s support can resolve a difficulty that constrains someone.

Siwāgh is no longer the name of a particular drink here, but an image of something that removes obstruction and difficulty.

Al-Kumayt lived in the Islamic period. The line is therefore not pre-Islamic evidence, but shows that the connection between ease of swallowing and relief from difficulty was familiar in Arabic of that period.

Palatability and acceptability

Sāgha lahu mā faʿala can describe an action regarded as permissible or acceptable for someone.

Sawwagha lahu al-amra is likewise recorded for allowing or permitting an action.

This use has an intelligible relationship with the root’s concrete sense.

When a drink passes easily, no obstruction prevents swallowing. In extended usage, an action judged permissible or acceptable similarly passes the obstacle in question and becomes possible.

This explanation does not establish the certain historical origin of the legal or social use.

Nor does acceptance by a person or society itself establish moral or divine legitimacy.

An action may be permitted within a legal system yet conflict with justice or truth. Conversely, someone may reject a right action through prejudice or ignorance.

Taswīgh as permitting or justifying is thus an extension within the family, but should not be transferred directly to the Quranic passages about drinks.

Other lexical uses

Arabic sources also use sawgh for a person born immediately after another child, with no intervening birth.

Huwa sawghuhu or hiya sawghatuhu can express such a relationship.

Various explanations connect this with smooth passage or succession, including the idea of one child following directly after another.

This usage alone does not establish birth order as the root’s earliest meaning.

Some lexical reports also give sāghat bihi al-arḍu for someone sinking into the ground.

These uses show the family’s breadth. In the Quran, however, only the branch concerning palatability and the ease or difficulty of swallowing a drink appears.

2. Quranic forms and word structure

S W Gh occurs three times in the Quran.

Two occurrences are the active participle sāʾigh; the third is a form IV imperfect verb.

The Quranic forms are:

  • Sāʾighan — Al-Naḥl 16:66
  • Sāʾighun — Fāṭir 35:12
  • Yusīghuhu — Ibrāhīm 14:17

These forms are related but do not have identical grammatical roles.

Sāgha and sāʾigh

The simple verb sāgha yasūghu primarily concerns food or drink going down easily.

Sāʾigh is its active participle.

S W Gh is a hollow weak root: its middle radical is wāw. In the active participle, that wāw becomes hamzah through a regular morphological change, producing sāʾigh.

In Al-Naḥl 16:66, sāʾighan is accusative and qualifies labanan, milk.

In Fāṭir 35:12, sāʾighun is nominative and is syntactically connected with sharābuhu.

Both uses describe a drink’s quality, rather than express drinking as a separate finite verb.

Yusīghuhu: a form IV verb

Yusīghuhu is the imperfect of form IV asāgha.

Yusīghu is the verb, while -hu is its object pronoun.

In Ibrāhīm 14:17, the pronoun refers to the drink introduced in the preceding verse.

Asāgha al-sharāba means swallowing a drink or enabling its passage down the throat.

Here the verb concerns someone’s effort to swallow a liquid that is extremely difficult to drink.

Form IV is transitive in this use, but the pattern alone does not specify the liquid’s exact movement, the number of attempts, or every cause of the difficulty.

Those details depend on the whole sentence and its context.

Forms not occurring independently in the Quran

Arabic has other forms in this family as well.

Sawgh and sawāgh: ease of passage down the throat.

Siwāgh: something that eases an obstruction in swallowing.

Asāgha: swallowed or made swallowing easier; its imperfect yusīghuhu does occur in the Quran.

Isāghah: the verbal noun of form IV.

Sawwagha: made easier, allowed, or permitted.

Taswīgh: permitting or justifying.

Istasāgha: found palatable, and by extension accepted something.

Masāgh: a passage or possibility of passing; figuratively, room or opportunity for accepting something.

Mustasāgh: something palatable or acceptable.

These exact forms are known in Arabic but do not occur as independent Quranic word forms. Asāgha, however, is the dictionary form of the verb yusīghuhu in Ibrāhīm 14:17.

3. Labanan khāliṣan sāʾighan: pure, palatable milk

Al-Naḥl 16:66 states:

وَإِنَّ لَكُمْ فِي الْأَنْعَامِ لَعِبْرَةً نُسْقِيكُمْ مِمَّا فِي بُطُونِهِ مِنْ بَيْنِ فَرْثٍ وَدَمٍ لَبَنًا خَالِصًا سَائِغًا لِلشَّارِبِينَ

wa-inna lakum fī al-anʿāmi la-ʿibratan nusqīkum mimmā fī buṭūnihi min bayni farthin wa-damin labanan khāliṣan sāʾighan li-al-shāribīn

Al-Naḥl 16:66

“And indeed, in livestock there is a lesson for you: We give you to drink from what is in their bellies, from between intestinal contents and blood, pure milk, pleasant for those who drink.”

Sāʾighan here describes milk.

It follows khāliṣan, from Kh L Ṣ, concerning purity and separation from admixture.

The Quran therefore presents milk with two different descriptions: pure and palatable.

Purity and palatability are not identical

Khāliṣ describes the milk in relation to what it emerges from.

The verse mentions farth and dam while describing the milk to be drunk as pure.

Sāʾigh, by contrast, concerns the milk’s relation to the drinker: milk that is easy and pleasant to drink.

Two distinct qualities are therefore expressed.

A liquid may be pure in composition yet unpleasant to drink because of its taste or condition.

Conversely, a drink may be tasty and easy to swallow while consisting of several ingredients.

Purity alone does not define palatability, nor does palatability mean purity.

In Al-Naḥl 16:66, the two qualities together disclose different aspects of this provision.

The role of li-al-shāribīn

Sāʾighan is followed by li-al-shāribīn.

Shāribīn belongs to Sh R B and means those who drink.

The phrase makes clear that the milk’s palatability is expressed in relation to its drinkers.

Sāʾigh here does not describe its color, the vessel’s shape, or its quantity. It concerns the quality of drinking it.

In the same sentence, nusqīkum belongs to S Q Y and expresses giving something to drink.

Three families thus appear in one scene: S Q Y for giving drink, Sh R B for drinking, and S W Gh for the drink’s easy, pleasant passage down the throat.

This distinction matters for understanding the word precisely.

Palatability is not identical with sweetness

Sāʾigh can describe a sweet and pleasant drink, but does not necessarily mean sweet.

Milk and water can both be called palatable even though they taste different.

Rendering sāʾigh in Al-Naḥl 16:66 simply as “sweet” would therefore lose part of its principal meaning.

The Persian “pleasant to drink” or “palatable” is a better equivalent because it encompasses both pleasantness and ease of swallowing.

The connection with natural provisions

The verse speaks of milk arising within livestock and draws attention to something people enjoy in daily life.

Nearby, Al-Naḥl 16:65 mentions rainwater and the earth, Al-Naḥl 16:67 fruits, and Al-Naḥl 16:69 the drink coming from bees.

The sequence offers examples of food and drink for reflection on creation and human provision.

The meaning of sāʾigh should not, however, be expanded into the whole biological process of milk production.

Livestock anatomy, milk production, nutrition, and the circulation of substances are separate subjects.

S W Gh concerns the quality of drinking, not the scientific mechanism producing milk.

4. Sāʾighun sharābuhu: pleasant drinking water contrasted with salt water

Fāṭir 35:12 states:

وَمَا يَسْتَوِي الْبَحْرَانِ هَٰذَا عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ سَائِغٌ شَرَابُهُ وَهَٰذَا مِلْحٌ أُجَاجٌ وَمِنْ كُلٍّ تَأْكُلُونَ لَحْمًا طَرِيًّا وَتَسْتَخْرِجُونَ حِلْيَةً تَلْبَسُونَهَا وَتَرَى الْفُلْكَ فِيهِ مَوَاخِرَ لِتَبْتَغُوا مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-mā yastawī al-baḥrāni hādhā ʿadhbun furātun sāʾighun sharābuhu wa-hādhā milḥun ujājun wa-min kullin taʾkulūna laḥman ṭariyyan wa-tastakhrijūna ḥilyatan talbasūnahā wa-tarā al-fulka fīhi mawākhira li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi wa-laʿallakum tashkurūn

Fāṭir 35:12

“And the two seas are not alike: one is fresh and sweet, pleasant to drink, and the other salty and bitter. From each you eat fresh flesh and extract ornaments that you wear. And you see ships plowing through it, so that you may seek His bounty and perhaps give thanks.”

Sāʾighun here describes fresh water.

It accompanies two further descriptions: ʿadhbun and furātun.

ʿAdhb, furāt, and sāʾigh

In this context, ʿadhb describes water contrasted with salt water, naturally fresh and pleasant.

Furāt belongs to F R T and, in its familiar usage, emphasizes water’s sweetness and pleasantness to drink.

Sāʾigh belongs to a different root and particularly concerns ease of swallowing and pleasant drinking.

These descriptions are related, but their semantic contributions are not completely identical.

For example, ʿadhb and furāt highlight the water’s quality against saltiness and bitterness, while sāʾighun sharābuhu concerns the drink in relation to the drinker.

Translation should therefore not flatten all three words into indistinguishable repetition.

One could say: “This is fresh and sweet, and drinking it is easy and pleasant.”

Unnecessary repetition should also be avoided in Persian. The aim is to communicate the real distinctions, not simply place several near-synonyms together without explanation.

What does sāʾighun sharābuhu describe?

In the sentence, sāʾigh is connected with sharābuhu.

Sharābuhu means “its drink,” what is drunk from that water. Here it is the grammatical subject governed by the active participle sāʾigh, giving the sense “its drink is palatable.”

The expression therefore describes the pleasantness of drinking that water.

Its grammar differs from sāʾighan li-al-shāribīn in Al-Naḥl 16:66.

In Al-Naḥl, accusative sāʾighan describes milk, while li-al-shāribīn relates it to drinkers.

In Fāṭir, sāʾigh is nominative and syntactically linked with sharābuhu.

The central sense remains close in both: a drink that goes down easily and pleasantly.

Does sāʾigh simply mean fresh water?

Fresh water is described as sāʾigh in Fāṭir 35:12.

Their association does not establish sweetness as the meaning of S W Gh itself.

The same word describes milk in Al-Naḥl 16:66.

In Ibrāhīm 14:17, the root’s verb concerns a drink that is difficult to swallow.

The semantic field is therefore broader than a specific kind of drink or taste.

Sāʾigh concerns how readily a drink goes down, not a substance’s name or sweetness as its only quality.

The difference between fresh and salt water

Fāṭir 35:12 compares two kinds of water.

One is ʿadhb, furāt, and sāʾigh; the other is salty and ujāj.

Ujāj is used for intensely salty or bitter, unpleasant water.

The contrast shows that waters differ in their suitability for pleasant drinking.

After stating the difference, however, the verse describes shared benefits: fresh food, ornaments, and ships traveling through them.

A different quality for drinking does not make salt water absolutely useless.

Each can benefit people in different ways.

That observation comes from the whole verse, not the lexical definition of sāʾigh.

5. Wa-lā yakādu yusīghuhu: difficulty swallowing a punishing drink

Ibrāhīm 14:16 states:

مِنْ وَرَائِهِ جَهَنَّمُ وَيُسْقَىٰ مِنْ مَاءٍ صَدِيدٍ

min warāʾihi jahannamu wa-yusqā min māʾin ṣadīd

Ibrāhīm 14:16

“Ahead of him is Hell, and he is given putrid water to drink.”

Ibrāhīm 14:17 continues:

يَتَجَرَّعُهُ وَلَا يَكَادُ يُسِيغُهُ وَيَأْتِيهِ الْمَوْتُ مِنْ كُلِّ مَكَانٍ وَمَا هُوَ بِمَيِّتٍ وَمِنْ وَرَائِهِ عَذَابٌ غَلِيظٌ

yatajarraʿuhu wa-lā yakādu yusīghuhu wa-yaʾtīhi al-mawtu min kulli makānin wa-mā huwa bi-mayyitin wa-min warāʾihi ʿadhābun ghalīẓ

Ibrāhīm 14:17

“He drinks it in gulps and can scarcely swallow it. Death comes to him from every direction, yet he is not dead; and ahead of him is severe punishment.”

This differs from sāʾigh in Al-Naḥl and Fāṭir.

In those two verses, palatability is a favorable quality of milk and water.

Here, the person faces a drink that is extremely difficult to swallow.

The relationship between yatajarraʿuhu and yusīghuhu

Yatajarraʿuhu belongs to J R ʿ and concerns drinking in gulps and swallowing liquid.

Yusīghuhu belongs to S W Gh and denotes swallowing it or getting it down the throat.

The verbs are close but make different semantic contributions.

Tajarruʿ here describes the manner of drinking: the person takes the drink gulp by gulp.

Isāghah concerns swallowing; its extreme difficulty in this sentence emerges through lā yakādu and the context.

The sentence thus expresses two aspects of one painful condition: drinking in gulps and severe difficulty swallowing.

Translating both verbs simply as “drinking” would erase the distinction.

Yatajarraʿuhu prevents reducing the scene to someone not approaching the drink at all. Yet whether any of it is successfully swallowed must be considered alongside the differing interpretations of lā yakādu.

The role of lā yakādu

Yakādu is a verb of approximation, indicating that an action is near fulfillment.

When negated, it can express how remote or difficult that fulfillment is.

In wa-lā yakādu yusīghuhu, swallowing is portrayed as so difficult that the person can scarcely get the drink down.

Grammatical explanations differ on whether the construction denies swallowing altogether or implies it occurs only after great suffering.

Both readings agree on the extreme difficulty.

It is therefore inappropriate to write, without explanation, either “he never swallows it” or “he swallows it easily.”

The clear sense is difficulty approaching an inability to swallow.

Where does the difficulty come from?

The preceding verse introduces the drink as māʾin ṣadīdin.

Ṣadīd is indexed under Ṣ D D and here concerns a putrid, unpleasant fluid.

The difficulty is thus connected with the kind of drink and the state of punishment.

Yusīghuhu alone does not specify all of the fluid’s properties.

For example, the root does not determine its chemical composition, temperature in degrees, or the bodily mechanism making it difficult to swallow.

Nor does the word establish punishment as purely psychological with no external reality.

The passage describes being given a drink, taking it in gulps, difficulty swallowing, and severe punishment. These are explicit elements of the Quranic portrayal, but their precise manner exceeds one verb’s lexical meaning.

Why is ease of swallowing denied here?

In Al-Naḥl 16:66, pure and palatable milk is a provision for drinkers.

In Fāṭir 35:12, fresh, sweet water is pleasant to drink.

In Ibrāhīm 14:17, however, the person is made to confront a drink that is very difficult to swallow.

The contrast illustrates the family’s range: a quality expressed as provision and comfort in one place becomes part of a portrayal of punishment through its denial in another.

The root itself means neither blessing nor punishment.

Sāʾigh describes pleasant drinking; lā yakādu yusīghuhu expresses difficulty swallowing.

Provision and punishment follow from the subject and context.

Ibrāhīm 14:13, Ibrāhīm 14:14, Ibrāhīm 14:15, Ibrāhīm 14:16, and Ibrāhīm 14:17 describe people threatening to expel their messengers from their land and defiantly resisting their call, followed by the outcome of obstinate tyrants.

This portrayal cannot be detached from its context and applied to anyone who experiences difficulty swallowing a drink.

In ordinary life, an obstruction in swallowing is a physical condition. Here, the difficulty forms part of the punishment described for a particular group.

6. Extended meaning: from swallowing a drink to accepting something

Palatability and acceptability

Istasāgha al-shayʾa can mean finding something palatable or accepting it.

In more recent usage, someone can say they accepted, or could not accept, a statement, behavior, or decision using istasāgha and its forms.

This usually does not mean physically swallowing liquid.

For instance, lā astasīghu hādhā al-qawla may mean “I cannot accept this statement” or “I find this statement unacceptable.”

The expression has an intelligible connection with the image of the throat.

Just as some foods go down readily while others stick, some statements and actions are acceptable to someone while others are hard to accept.

That image does not itself establish whether a statement is true or false.

A statement may be true while someone refuses to accept it through prejudice or ignorance.

Conversely, something false may seem pleasant and acceptable to its listener.

Mental palatability is therefore not identical with a claim’s truth or validity.

Taswīgh and permitting an action

Sawwagha lahu is also recorded for allowing or permitting something.

Persian uses taswīgh and musawwigh in legal, ethical, and philosophical discussions.

A musawwigh can be a reason or factor offered to make an action acceptable.

Having a reason must nevertheless be distinguished from having a valid reason.

Not every statement offered to justify an act makes that act right or legitimate.

In the Quran, establishing a claim’s truth or a ruling’s legitimacy involves evidence and proof, not merely making it seem pleasant or acceptable in one’s mind.

The later sense of taswīgh as justification should therefore not be presented as the primary meaning of sāʾigh in Al-Naḥl or Fāṭir.

Those passages concern actual drinks.

Passage and finding a way through

Lexicons also record masāgh for a place or route of passage.

Sugh fī al-arḍ is likewise recorded for entering or finding a way into the land.

This relates to passing readily and finding an opening for passage.

Not every kind of passage in Arabic, however, is called sawgh.

Other roots cover passage, entrance, movement, and passing by, including D Kh L, N F Dh, ʿ B R, and M R R.

S W Gh should not be expanded into “every kind of movement or passage” so broadly that its distinctive ease and palatability disappear.

7. Comparison with related roots

S W Gh (sawgh) and Sh R B (sharb): the quality of swallowing and drinking itself

In the Quran, Sh R B concerns drinking, drink, a share of water, and a place or means of access to it.

S W Gh chiefly concerns easy, pleasant passage down the throat; difficulty in the relevant verse arises from the negative construction and context.

The two families stand together in sāʾighan li-al-shāribīn in Al-Naḥl 16:66.

Shāribīn are the drinkers; sāʾighan describes the milk’s palatability.

Drinking and being pleasant to drink are therefore not identical.

The forms of Sh R B should be studied separately in their own contexts, so that the act of drinking is not confused with the quality of swallowing.

S W Gh (sawgh) and J R ʿ (jarʿ): drinking in gulps and ease of swallowing

J R ʿ concerns swallowing a drink and its gulps.

S W Gh concerns swallowing and easy passage; the negative construction can indicate difficulty.

Yatajarraʿuhu and yusīghuhu occur together in Ibrāhīm 14:17.

The first expresses drinking gulp by gulp; the second concerns swallowing, whose difficulty is made clear by lā yakādu.

The two verbs describe the same event but do not make identical semantic contributions.

Studying J R ʿ separately helps distinguish drinking in gulps from whether each gulp passes down easily or with difficulty.

S W Gh (sawgh) and B L ʿ (balʿ): swallowing and palatability

Balʿ concerns swallowing something.

In Hūd 11:44, the earth is commanded to swallow its water.

This illustrates a broader sense of taking something in or absorbing it.

The main sense of sawgh is closer to food or drink passing easily down the throat.

Swallowing and palatability are therefore not identical.

Something may be swallowed while going down with considerable difficulty or unpleasantness.

S W Gh (sawgh) and Gh Ṣ Ṣ (ghaṣaṣ): smooth swallowing and obstruction

Ghaṣṣa and ghuṣṣah concern something lodging in the throat and difficulty swallowing.

In its concrete sense, sawgh expresses the opposite: something passing smoothly without obstruction.

The poem attributed to Yazīd ibn al-Ṣaʿiq sets both meanings against each other.

Siwāgh al-ghuṣaṣ likewise names something understood to ease that obstruction.

Figurative ghuṣṣah can concern grief and inner constriction, while sawgh can extend to relief and acceptability.

These figurative uses should remain distinguishable from the bodily meanings.

S W Gh (sawgh) and ʿ Dh B (ʿadhb): easy passage and fresh water

ʿAdhb in Fāṭir 35:12 describes fresh, pleasant water.

Sāʾigh in the same verse concerns pleasantness and ease in swallowing the drink.

They overlap in the context of drinking water but differ in root and semantic contribution.

A drink may be ʿadhb in taste and sāʾigh in how readily it goes down.

Their occurrence together does not make them complete synonyms.

S W Gh (sawgh) and S Q Y (saqy): giving drink and palatability

Saqy concerns supplying or giving a drink to another.

Sawgh describes the quality of its easy passage down the throat.

In Al-Naḥl 16:66, nusqīkum expresses giving drink, while sāʾighan describes the milk.

Yusqā in Ibrāhīm 14:16 likewise concerns being given a liquid whose swallowing is described as difficult in Ibrāhīm 14:17.

The contrast shows that giving drink does not itself establish the drink’s palatability or benefit.

A drink can provide comfort or form part of punishment; other sentence elements specify its kind and quality.

S W Gh (sawgh) and S W Q (sawq): easy passage and driving onward

Sawq concerns driving or moving something in a particular direction.

The primary Quranic sense of sawgh concerns ease of swallowing.

Some movement or passage may be present in both, but their objects and manner differ.

Sawq can concern moving a being or group toward a destination, whereas sawgh highlights food and drink passing smoothly down the throat.

Similar letters and the presence of movement do not establish a shared historical derivation.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Sāʾighan — Al-Naḥl 16:66

The Quranic phrase:

لَبَنًا خَالِصًا سَائِغًا لِلشَّارِبِينَ

labanan khāliṣan sāʾighan li-al-shāribīn

Al-Naḥl 16:66

Suggested translation:

“Pure milk, pleasant for those who drink.”

This preserves the distinction between khāliṣ and sāʾigh and relates the description to the drinkers.

“Pure milk that goes down easily for drinkers” follows the lexical image closely, but the Persian equivalent of “palatable” is more natural.

“Pure and sweet milk” is inadequate because sāʾigh is not limited to sweetness.

“Pure and lawful milk” also introduces a meaning not conveyed by sāʾigh itself.

Sāʾighun — Fāṭir 35:12

The Quranic phrase:

هَٰذَا عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ سَائِغٌ شَرَابُهُ

hādhā ʿadhbun furātun sāʾighun sharābuhu

Fāṭir 35:12

Suggested translation:

“One is fresh and sweet, pleasant to drink.”

This agrees with the existing Forqan translation.

The word explanation should clarify that sāʾigh particularly concerns the drink’s easy and pleasant passage down the throat.

An alternative can also avoid repetition in Persian:

“This is fresh, pleasant water that is easy and agreeable to drink.”

Direct translation should not assume that ʿadhb, furāt, and sāʾigh have identical meanings merely because they appear together.

Yusīghuhu — Ibrāhīm 14:17

The Quranic phrase:

يَتَجَرَّعُهُ وَلَا يَكَادُ يُسِيغُهُ

yatajarraʿuhu wa-lā yakādu yusīghuhu

Ibrāhīm 14:17

Suggested translation:

“He drinks it in gulps and can scarcely swallow it.”

This preserves the distinction between tajarruʿ and isāghah.

“It goes down his throat with difficulty” is intelligible, but makes the person’s agency in yusīghuhu less explicit.

“He does not drink at all” is unsuitable because the verse explicitly describes taking it in gulps.

“He drinks willingly” likewise contradicts the context of difficulty and punishment.

“Can scarcely swallow it” is therefore a natural, accurate rendering of the phrase.

Brief root meaning and meaning in each verse

Sāʾighan — Al-Naḥl 16:66

Brief root meaning: food or drink passing easily and smoothly down the throat; palatability.

Meaning in this verse: palatable for those who drink; describing milk that is easy and pleasant to drink.

Sāʾighun — Fāṭir 35:12

Brief root meaning: a drink going down easily and pleasantly.

Meaning in this verse: pleasant to drink; describing the fresh water contrasted with salty, bitter water.

Yusīghuhu — Ibrāhīm 14:17

Brief root meaning: swallowing food or drink and passing it down the throat.

Meaning in this verse: he can scarcely swallow it, concerning the unpleasant drink he takes in gulps.

Conclusion

S W Gh concerns food and drink passing easily and smoothly down the throat.

Concretely, sāgha al-sharābu means that a drink went down without obstruction or difficulty; sharābun sāʾighun is a palatable drink that goes down readily.

The poem attributed to Yazīd ibn al-Ṣaʿiq clearly contrasts a drink going down easily with water catching in the throat. Its palatability is also connected with relief from grief and the person regaining calm.

Other Arabic uses extend to tasks proceeding easily, speech becoming acceptable, allowing, justifying, and relieving difficulty.

These uses should not be imposed on the Quranic passages’ direct meaning.

The root occurs three times in the Quran.

In Al-Naḥl 16:66, milk is both pure and sāʾigh. Purity concerns its condition, while palatability concerns the quality of drinking it.

In Fāṭir 35:12, sāʾigh describes fresh water alongside ʿadhb and furāt. It is not completely synonymous with them, emphasizing easy and pleasant swallowing.

In Ibrāhīm 14:17, yusīghuhu appears within a negative construction and a context of extreme difficulty. The person takes the punishing drink in gulps but can scarcely swallow it.

The three occurrences retain the family’s core in both palatability and the denial of easy swallowing.

The root can be defined as follows:

S W Gh denotes food or drink passing easily and smoothly down the throat without obstruction. Sāʾigh describes something easy and pleasant to swallow; asāgha can express swallowing or facilitating it. Related extended uses include becoming acceptable, permitting, and a matter proceeding readily.

In Quranic usage, palatability describes the drink’s quality and its relation to the drinker. It is neither a particular substance’s name, nor a synonym of purity or sweetness, nor a moral or religious ruling on permission.

The striking distinction is that drinking and palatability are not identical. Someone may receive a liquid and even take it gulp by gulp while finding it extremely difficult to swallow.

Conversely, milk and pleasant water in the creation passages exemplify people’s enjoyment of drinks that go down easily and agreeably.

The principal contrast between the Quranic uses thus concerns not whether a drink exists, but how it is swallowed: ease and pleasantness on one side, difficulty and obstruction on the other.
