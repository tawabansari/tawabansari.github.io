1. Root Meaning and Word Structure

Arabic qamṭarīr describes something extremely hard, severe, and dreadful, especially a day whose intense hardships place people under distress. It occurs only once in the Qur’an, describing a day whose consequences the righteous fear.

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī writes: “يَوْمٌ قُمَاطِرٌ وَيَوْمٌ قَمْطَرِيرٌ، أَيْ شَدِيدٌ”: a qumāṭir or qamṭarīr day is a severe, difficult day.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: severe day and iqmaṭarra

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records severity and intensity for qamṭarīr and nearby forms. It connects the word with distress so intense that it contracts a person’s face and draws together the area between the eyes.

That explanation distinguishes the situation’s severity from the human reaction it produces.

In one use, qamṭarīr can describe a day of extremely difficult events. In another, it describes someone whose face is deeply contracted by anger, pressure, or distress.

The relationship is understandable. Under intense pressure, people may draw their faces together, bring their eyebrows closer, and look grim or frowning.

The word should nevertheless not be restricted to that facial movement: early sources also use it for a day’s severity and the intensity of evil.

Overwhelming severity and intense pressure therefore clearly characterize qamṭarīr. Facial contraction is one related tangible image or manifestation; not every form in the family means severity.

Qur’anic qamṭarīran describes yawman: an extremely severe and dreadful day.

Its meaning here consequently concerns that day’s severity and difficulty, rather than only an individual’s facial expression.

Quadriliteral Structure

The Qur’anic morphological index records Q M Ṭ R as a quadriliteral root.

Qur’anic qamṭarīr is an adjective conveying a situation’s severity and weight.

Arabic also has the verb iqmaṭarra and verbal noun iqmiṭrār. Iqmaṭarra al-yawmu means that the day became severe and difficult.

The historical structure of qamṭarīr is nevertheless disputed.

Ibn Fāris connects it with triliteral Q M Ṭ, saying that rāʾ was added and repeated to intensify the meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Ibn Fāris’s added and repeated rāʾ

Other grammarians and lexicographers analyze the word through quadriliteral Q M Ṭ R forms.

The disagreement matters: simply observing qamṭarīr’s letters does not establish that they all belonged to its historical root from the beginning.

Conversely, the presence of a derivational analysis in a dictionary does not settle the word’s historical formation.

Qamṭarīr’s severity and intensity are documented; its precise historical formation requires separate investigation.

2. A Possible Connection with Q M Ṭ: Binding, Gathering, and Contraction

Ibn Fāris’s Analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris connects Q M Ṭ with collecting and gathering.

He writes:

القَافُ وَالْمِيمُ وَالطَّاءُ أُصَيْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى جَمْعٍ وَتَجَمُّعٍ

al-qāfu wa-l-mīmu wa-l-ṭāʾu uṣaylun yadullu ʿalā jamʿin wa-tajammuʿin

That is, Q M Ṭ indicates collecting and gathering together.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Q M Ṭ

He then gives examples of its use.

Qamṭ al-ṣabiyy concerns binding a child in swaddling cloth, which surrounds the body and holds its limbs together.

Qamaṭa al-asīra likewise describes binding a captive’s hands and feet together with rope.

What these examples share is drawing parts together and restricting their movement through binding.

Ibn Fāris then connects qamṭarīr with this family, saying that rāʾ was added and repeated to intensify the meaning.

Under that analysis, contraction, drawing together, and intense pressure can be semantically connected.

Just as rope draws limbs together and limits their movement, extreme hardship may place a person under pressure and constraint.

Although understandable, that explanation alone does not prove that qamṭarīr necessarily originated from binding a child or captive.

Ibn Fāris offers an analysis of the relationship among the words.

Establishing the historical sequence of their formation requires independent linguistic evidence.

Qamṭara and Collecting

Arabic records qamṭara for collecting and gathering things.

Lisān al-ʿArab states: “كُلُّ شَيْءٍ جَمَعْتَهُ فَقَدْ قَمْطَرْتَهُ”: anything you have gathered, you have qamṭartahu.

Qamṭara al-qirbata also describes tying a waterskin’s mouth with a cord.

Al-Liḥyānī reports another use: qamṭara al-qirbata means filling the waterskin.

Gathering contents, closing the mouth, and confining the space inside a container can be related in these examples.

Filling and tying are nevertheless different actions.

Their association does not make every use of qamṭara mean filling something to bursting point or exerting intense pressure.

In waterskin usage, the sentence’s subject matter determines the intended action.

Drawing the Body Together

Lisān al-ʿArab records iqmaṭarrat al-ʿaqrabu when a scorpion bends its tail and draws itself together.

Parts of the animal’s body are then contracted and gathered.

Iqmaṭarrat al-nāqatu likewise describes a she-camel raising her tail, drawing her body together, and holding her nose in a particular position.

Lisān al-ʿArab: waterskin and the contracted animal postures

These usages document gathering and contraction within the qmṭr family.

They do not make Qur’anic qamṭarīr mean the posture of a scorpion or camel.

In the Qur’anic verse, the word concerns a day.

A possible connection between contraction and pressure can illuminate the lexical image without importing an animal posture into the verse’s translation.

Is the Basic Meaning Gathering or Severity?

Two levels must be distinguished.

In Ibn Fāris’s derivational analysis, gathering and compression may help explain severity.

In recorded qamṭarīr usage, however, a severe day and intense evil are clearly attested.

Qur’anic translation should therefore not reduce qamṭarīr to “gathered,” “collected,” or “bound.”

“A gathered day,” for example, would not convey the verse’s established meaning.

“An extremely severe day” agrees with lexical usage and Qur’anic context.

Gathering and contraction may explain a semantic relationship within the family; extreme severity remains the preferred meaning of the Qur’anic form.

3. Linguistic Forms and Early Arabic Evidence

Principal Forms

Arabic sources record several forms in this family.

قَمْطَرِير — qamṭarīr: extremely severe, overwhelming, and dreadful, especially a day of intense difficulty. This form occurs in the Qur’an.

قُمَاطِر — qumāṭir: describing a severe, unpleasant day; also recorded in Arabic poetry.

مُقْمَطِرّ — muqmaṭirr: severe, grim, or contracted; used for days and other situations.

اِقْمَطَرَّ — iqmaṭarra: became severe or intense; in some uses, drew together or contracted.

اِقْمِطْرَار — iqmiṭrār: the verbal noun of iqmaṭarra.

قِمَطْر — qimaṭr: in some uses, a short, stout person or a strong, large camel.

قِمَطْرَة — qimaṭrah: a chest or container for such things as books.

قَمَاطِر — qamāṭir: the plural of qimaṭrah in container usage.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān, and Al-Miṣbāḥ: lexical forms and qimaṭrah

The forms are recorded in Arabic but do not all mean the same thing.

Qimaṭrah, for example, can name a book container, whereas qamṭarīr describes severity and intensity.

Gathering objects in a container may suggest a relationship with contraction, but does not establish every form’s historical derivation.

Likewise, qimaṭr for a short person or a stout camel can suggest a compact or bulky body. That is not a necessary meaning of qamṭarīr.

Only qamṭarīran occurs in the Qur’an.

A Poetic Witness to a Severe Day

Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Farrāʾ, and several commentaries transmit this verse:

بَنِي عَمِّنَا هَلْ تَذْكُرُونَ بَلَاءَنَا
عَلَيْكُمْ إِذَا مَا كَانَ يَوْمٌ قُمَاطِرُ

banī ʿamminā hal tadhkurūna balāʾanā / ʿalaykum idhā mā kāna yawmun qumāṭiru

“Our cousins, do you remember the ordeal we inflicted upon you when the day was severe?” The context suggests the hardship of conflict.

Qumāṭir describes yawm, “day,” in the verse.

The subject is a day of confrontation and conflict between groups.

This documents the family’s use for an extremely severe, distressing day.

The word is not confined to an individual’s grim face; it describes the situation’s intensity and weight.

The poetic verse is consequently relevant to Qur’anic qamṭarīr.

The examined sources nevertheless do not independently establish the poet’s identity or the precise date of composition.

It should not be assigned to a particular poet without evidence or necessarily called pre-Islamic.

What is more securely established is its recorded use in Arabic lexical and literary tradition.

Lisān al-ʿArab: banī ʿamminā and qumāṭir

Qumāṭir in Describing War

Another poetic witness, cited by Al-Akhfash and Qur’anic commentaries, reads:

فَفِرُّوا إِذَا مَا الْحَرْبُ ثَارَ غُبَارُهَا
وَلَجَّ بِهَا الْيَوْمُ الْعَبُوسُ الْقُمَاطِرُ

fa-firrū idhā mā al-ḥarbu thāra ghubāruhā / wa-lajja bihā al-yawmu al-ʿabūsu al-qumāṭiru

“Flee, then, when war’s dust rises and its grim, severe day presses relentlessly on.”

Al-ʿabūs and al-qumāṭir stand beside one another here.

That association closely resembles Al-Insān 76:10, which likewise places ʿabūsan and qamṭarīran together.

The poem concerns a day of intensified warfare and rising battlefield dust.

Qumāṭir conveys the situation’s severity and dread.

The witness nevertheless requires care.

Its structural resemblance to the Qur’anic wording does not establish that it predates the Qur’an.

The combination may have existed in earlier Arabic, but this particular verse’s historical independence requires separate evidence about its composition and transmission.

It helps identify recorded qumāṭir usage without constituting a securely pre-Qur’anic witness on its own.

Al-Qurṭubī: war witness, Al-Akhfash, and Al-Kisāʾī

Facial Contraction

Lexical and exegetical sources also record rajulun qamṭarīrun. Al-Qurṭubī transmits this usage from Abū ʿUbaydah.

Al-Qurṭubī: rajulun qamṭarīrun from Abū ʿUbaydah

It describes someone whose face is deeply contracted, particularly the area between the eyebrows and eyes.

Al-Zajjāj, as quoted by Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, likewise explains qamṭarīr through contraction between the eyes.

Al-Rāzī: Al-Zajjāj on contraction between the eyes

The image agrees with ordinary experience of anger, fear, or intense pressure.

Facial contraction nevertheless represents only one use of the family.

A day may, for example, be extremely severe without the speaker describing any particular face at that moment.

In yawman qamṭarīran, the principal subject is therefore the day and its severity, although a contracted face is a related lexical image.

Does Iqmaṭarra Also Mean Spreading?

Lisān al-ʿArab records two different uses of iqmaṭarra al-shayʾu.

One concerns gathering and contraction.

The other concerns spreading or extending.

They initially appear opposite.

Recording them under one form does not mean that every occurrence simultaneously conveys both.

Differences in subject matter, dialect, or sentence structure may have produced distinct uses.

Qur’anic study should not transfer spreading into qamṭarīr without evidence.

Al-Insān 76:10 describes a severe day, with no direct indication that the word means merely “spread out” or “dispersed.”

Lisān al-ʿArab: spreading and contraction

4. Qur’anic Usage in Al-Insān 76:10

Q M Ṭ R occurs only once in the Qur’an.

Al-Insān 76:10

إِنَّا نَخَافُ مِنْ رَبِّنَا يَوْمًا عَبُوسًا قَمْطَرِيرًا

innā nakhāfu min rabbinā yawman ʿabūsan qamṭarīran

Forqan’s existing Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“We fear, from our Lord, a grim and severe day.”

The Grammatical Position of Qamṭarīran

Qamṭarīran is singular, masculine, indefinite, and accusative here.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole adjective in Al-Insān 76:10

It follows yawman ʿabūsan.

Grammatically, ʿabūsan and qamṭarīran are two adjectives describing yawman.

The Qur’an therefore describes a day with two qualities.

First, ʿabūsan, connected with frowning, grimness, and unpleasantness.

Second, qamṭarīran, emphasizing the day’s intensity, severity, and dread.

They belong to different word families.

They should not be treated as one word with two pronunciations or two derivatives of one root.

Nor should both be translated simply as “severe” without explanation, losing part of their different images.

Distinguishing ʿAbūsan and Qamṭarīran

For a person, ʿabūs describes a drawn face that has lost its openness and cheerfulness.

Al-Insān 76:10 attributes that quality to a day.

A day has no literal face, forehead, or eyebrows with which to frown.

Its grimness is therefore a linguistic image of its events’ unpleasantness and dread.

Qamṭarīr likewise conveys the situation’s severity and intensity.

Some lexicographers explain the hardship as so severe that it contracts a person’s face.

The two descriptions consequently approach a shared semantic field while differing in emphasis.

ʿAbūs highlights grimness and unpleasantness; qamṭarīr highlights that situation’s overwhelming severity and pressure.

Lexical evidence and context support the distinction without making it an absolute boundary.

Some Arabic uses explain qamṭarīr itself through facial contraction.

The descriptions therefore overlap as well as differ.

Together they can intensify the day’s severity and dread.

Must a Qamṭarīr Day Be Extremely Long?

Some lexical and exegetical reports explain qamṭarīr as a day exceedingly prolonged in hardship.

Al-Akhfash calls it among the severest days and emphasizes prolonged affliction.

That is understandable: people in painful, difficult circumstances may experience time as slow and burdensome.

Intensity of hardship should nevertheless be distinguished from temporal duration.

Familiar lexical definitions explain qamṭarīr as severe and intense.

Prolonged affliction can characterize a severe day, but the word yields no specified number of hours, years, or periods.

“An extremely long day,” without mentioning severity, would therefore not fully convey its principal meaning.

Nor does this verse alone establish precisely how many thousand years, or multiples of earthly days, the day lasts.

That issue requires an independent study of verses concerning time and resurrection.

In Al-Insān 76:10, extreme severity and dread have clearer and broader lexical support.

Al-Ṭabarī: severity, facial contraction, and prolonged hardship

Who Fears That Day?

Understanding the verse requires considering its preceding verses.

Al-Insān 76:7 describes people who fulfill vows and fear a day whose evil is widespread.

Al-Insān 76:8 describes their feeding the needy, the orphan, and the captive.

In Al-Insān 76:9, they explain that they feed for The God’s sake and seek neither repayment nor thanks from the recipients.

In Al-Insān 76:10, the same speakers express fear of a grim, qamṭarīr day.

Al-Insān 76:10 thus belongs to the context of righteous conduct and its motivation.

These people do not arrange their actions solely around receiving reward or praise from others.

They fear their Lord and a severe day.

Their fear is connected with ethical responsibility and action.

Qamṭarīr should nevertheless not be defined as vows, feeding others, sincerity, or fear of The God.

Those concepts belong to the context; the word directly describes the day’s severity and intensity.

Does Nakhāfu Mean Fear of Divine Injustice?

The verse says that they fear, from their Lord, a grim and severe day.

That does not itself attribute injustice to The God.

It expresses fear before a day of grave and dreadful consequences.

Later, Al-Insān 76:11 describes the righteous being saved from that day’s evil.

Fear of the severe day and hope for divine protection therefore belong to one context.

The Qur’an also describes the righteous people’s actions before mentioning this fear.

Fear here does not mean abandoning good action or becoming helpless before responsibility.

Rather, good conduct, sincerity, and fear of the day’s consequences appear together.

Qamṭarīr itself does not judge fear’s ethical source; it describes the day’s intensity and severity.

Al-Insān 76:11: Deliverance from the Same Day’s Evil

The following Al-Insān 76:11 describes The God protecting the righteous from that day’s evil and granting them radiance and joy.

The connection is particularly significant.

Al-Insān 76:10 describes the day as ʿabūs and qamṭarīr.

Al-Insān 76:11 describes protection from the same day’s evil.

Its severity therefore does not mean that everyone suffers its consequences equally.

The same context reports people who are protected from that day’s evil.

The radiance and joy in Al-Insān 76:11 also contrast with the grim, dreadful atmosphere in Al-Insān 76:10.

The semantic contrast is clear.

It nevertheless does not make qamṭarīr the exact lexical opposite of surūr.

Qamṭarīr describes severity; surūr is a state of joy.

They contrast in context but belong to separate linguistic families.

Connection with Yawman Thaqīlan in Al-Insān 76:27

Elsewhere in the chapter, Al-Insān 76:27 describes people loving immediate life and leaving behind a weighty day.

The word there is thaqīlan.

Thaqīl belongs to TH Q L and tangibly means heavy. Nonphysical uses can convey burdensome responsibility, pressure, or difficulty.

It agrees in direction with qamṭarīr in Al-Insān 76:10: both emphasize that day’s difficulty and weight.

They are nevertheless not exact synonyms.

Thaqīl emphasizes weight, whereas lexical qamṭarīr concerns intensity, severity, and sometimes contraction or grimness.

Their presence in the same chapter cannot establish an etymological derivation of one from the other.

5. Distinguishing Qamṭarīr from Related Words

Qamṭarīr and ʿAbūs

ʿAbūs concerns a frowning face and unpleasantness.

Qamṭarīr is known for extreme severity and pressure.

Both describe a day in Al-Insān 76:10.

Its severity may make faces contract, but that relationship should not erase the words’ difference.

“A grim and severe day” is a natural, defensible translation.

Qamṭarīr and ʿAsīr

ʿAsīr belongs to ʿ S R and concerns difficulty and lack of ease.

The Qur’an uses this family for severe days and difficult situations.

Al-Muddaththir 74:9 and Al-Qamar 54:8, for example, describe a difficult day.

Qur’anic qamṭarīr is semantically close to ʿasīr.

Its lexical witnesses nevertheless also include intensity and the image of facial contraction.

Not every ʿasīr use can therefore be equated with qamṭarīr without examination.

Study of ʿ S R

Qamṭarīr and Thaqīl

Thaqīl conveys weight and can extend from physical heaviness to nonphysical pressure and difficulty.

Al-Insān 76:27 applies it to a weighty day.

Qamṭarīr can also describe such a day through the intensity of its dread and severity.

Weight and intensity, though close, are not identical meanings.

A task can be burdensome and lengthy without being as overwhelming or distressing as a dreadful event.

The difference in emphasis should therefore be preserved.

Study of TH Q L

Qamṭarīr and Baʾs

Baʾs concerns intensity, strength, and severity in confrontation.

Some Qur’anic uses concern warfare, great force, or severe affliction.

Poetic witnesses likewise use qamṭarīr’s family for severe days of war.

Baʾs and qamṭarīr nevertheless differ in root, structure, and usage range.

Qamṭarīr principally describes a situation’s severity, while baʾs can denote the power or severity of confrontation itself.

Study of B ʾ S

Qamṭarīr and Karb

Arabic karb concerns distress and pressure placing someone in a strait.

The Qur’an also uses its family for severe afflictions.

That can be compared with the qmṭr family’s image of compression and pressure.

Karb principally concerns experienced distress and constraint, whereas qamṭarīr can describe the day or situation producing that distress.

Karb consequently cannot directly replace qamṭarīr in every use.

Qamṭarīr and Zamharīr

Zamharīr occurs in Al-Insān 76:13, familiarly meaning intensely biting cold.

Phonetically, qamṭarīr and zamharīr both end in rīr.

A lexical report from Al-Kisāʾī likewise places verbs from these families together.

Al-Qurṭubī: Al-Kisāʾī on iqmaṭarra and izmaharra

That resemblance does not make their roots or referents identical.

Qamṭarīr describes a severe and dreadful day in Al-Insān 76:10.

In Al-Insān 76:13, zamharīr concerns conditions in the Garden and means intense cold.

Their shared ending therefore does not make qamṭarīr mean extreme cold.

Nor does occurrence in the same chapter independently establish a derivational relationship.

Study of Z M H2 R

Qamṭarīr and Qimaṭrah

Qimaṭrah names a chest or container for such things as books.

It has been connected with the qmṭr family through its consonantal structure.

A container gathering things within itself can be related conceptually to gathering.

Recorded qimaṭrah nevertheless does not mean a severe day.

Nor does the existence of a closed container make qamṭarīr necessarily mean a prison, chest, or enclosed space.

Al-Insān 76:10 mentions no physical container.

The severe day meaning comes from the adjective’s own usage and the verse’s context.

6. Conclusions and Translation Proposal

Arabic Q M Ṭ R records meanings involving gathering, contraction, compression, and intensity.

Among its forms, qamṭarīr particularly describes something extremely severe and overwhelming.

Early sources use yawmun qamṭarīrun and yawmun qumāṭirun for a difficult and dreadful day.

Muqmaṭirr can likewise describe a severe or contracted state.

Ibn Fāris connects qamṭarīr with Q M Ṭ, gathering and binding, saying that rāʾ was added and repeated to intensify the meaning.

The analysis is semantically understandable without establishing the word’s entire structural history.

Other family usages include tying a waterskin, a scorpion drawing itself together, a camel’s stoutness, and storing things in a container.

Those referents should not all be imported into qamṭarīr’s direct definition.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as qamṭarīran in Al-Insān 76:10.

There, the righteous fear a day described as ʿabūs and qamṭarīr.

Grammatically, qamṭarīran is an accusative adjective describing yawman; alongside ʿabūsan it emphasizes the day’s severity and dread.

Al-Insān 76:11 describes their protection from that day’s evil.

The day’s severity therefore does not conflict with the deliverance of those The God protects.

Lexical sources also mention prolonged affliction as a feature of a qamṭarīr day, but its word alone supplies no specified duration.

Brief root meaning: Gathering, contraction, and compression in some family usages; in qamṭarīr, overwhelming severity and intensity, especially of a dreadful, difficult day.

Meaning of Qur’anic qamṭarīran: extremely severe and dreadful; describing a day whose intensity and dread the righteous fear.

Proposed word translation: “extremely severe” or “overwhelming and difficult.”

Al-Insān 76:10, rendering the existing Persian wording in Forqan:

“We fear, from our Lord, a grim and severe day.”

It preserves the word’s principal meaning and, alongside “grim,” naturally conveys the day’s intensity and unpleasantness.

Lexical explanation can emphasize qamṭarīr’s intensity as “extremely severe and overwhelming” without requiring a change to the approved translation.

The principal finding is that Qur’anic qamṭarīr describes an extremely severe and dreadful day: it does not name a particular punishment, specify a duration, or merely describe a person’s grim face.

Facial contraction and compression belong to its related lexical field and can make that day’s severity tangible.

Its Qur’anic meaning nevertheless requires the sentence’s structure.

ʿAbūs highlights the day’s grimness and unpleasantness; qamṭarīr highlights its overwhelming severity.

Across Al-Insān 76:7, Al-Insān 76:8, Al-Insān 76:9, Al-Insān 76:10, Al-Insān 76:11, and Al-Insān 76:12, that dreadful day stands in relation to righteous conduct and divine protection.

Studying the root thus clarifies the Qur’anic description’s intensity without adding unestablished details about duration, punishment’s nature, or everyone’s bodily reaction.
