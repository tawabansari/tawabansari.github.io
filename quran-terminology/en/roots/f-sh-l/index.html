The root f-sh-l — Weakening resolve, loss of courage, and faltering in action

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(tafshala)

The three-consonant Arabic root f-sh-l concerns weakening resolve, diminished courage, weakness before difficulty, and losing the ability to carry an action forward. فَشِلَ describes a person or group losing firmness and readiness before danger, pressure, or a demanding responsibility.

In some modern uses, fashal is understood as failure or the unsuccessful outcome of an undertaking. Qur’anic usage does not necessarily mean final defeat. A person may experience fashal before being defeated: their resolve and courage weaken. Someone may also fail in an undertaking despite perseverance and sound effort.

The distinction matters: fashal primarily concerns resolve and readiness to act, whereas victory and defeat describe an action’s outcome.

F-sh-l occurs four times in four Qur’anic verses: Āl ʿImrān 3:122, Āl ʿImrān 3:152, al-Anfāl 8:43, and al-Anfāl 8:46. All four derive from the basic verb فَشِلَ.

These verses concern faltering in confrontation with an enemy, alongside disagreement, disobedience, patience, and trust in God. They distinguish approaching a state of weakness, experiencing it, and its possible consequences.

1. The root in early Arabic

Weakness, faltering, and loss of courage

In established Arabic usage, فَشِلَ الرَّجُلُ means that a man faltered, weakened, or lost courage.

Early lexicons explain the root through several words:

ضَعُفَ — became weak.

جَبُنَ — became afraid or lost courage.

تَرَاخَى — slackened, losing earnestness or firmness.

كَسِلَ — became lazy or unmotivated to act.

These meanings are related but not identical.

Someone may be physically strong yet lose courage before great danger.

Another may be physically weak yet remain firm in decision and conduct.

Laziness may also prevent action without any danger or fear being involved.

These words cannot therefore replace fashal indiscriminately without regard to context.

Qur’anic usage particularly foregrounds the weakening of heart and resolve before difficulty, especially when perseverance and action are needed.

Evidence from al-Aʿshā’s poetry

A rajaz poem attributed to the well-known Arab poet al-Aʿshā says:

لَا فَشَلٌ فِيَّ وَلَا سِقَاطُ

La fashalun fiyya wa-la siqatu

لَيْسَ أَوَانَ يُكْرَهُ الْخِلَاطُ

Laysa awana yukrahu al-khilatu

Translation: “There is neither weakness and cowardice in me nor faltering and baseness; this is no time to shrink from confrontation.”

The speaker denies fashal of himself.

The lines concern confrontation and the ability to stand firm. The poet presents himself as someone who does not weaken before difficulty or opposition.

Siqāṭ appears alongside fashal and concerns stumbling, falling short, or deficiency in conduct.

Their pairing shows that fashal here does not mean an unsuccessful outcome. It is weakness attributed to a person.

Al-Aʿshā was a prominent poet of the late pre-Islamic period who also lived into the beginning of Islam. These lines appear in his attributed collected poetry and literary collections, but their exact date of composition is not established.

They are therefore useful evidence of early usage, without establishing conclusively that this particular poem predates Qur’anic revelation.

Despite that historical limitation, the poem’s meaning agrees with Qur’anic usage and early lexical accounts.

A poetic example with variant readings

Lexicons also transmit another verse:

وَقَدْ أَدْرَكَتْنِي وَالْحَوَادِثُ جَمَّةٌ
أَسِنَّةُ قَوْمٍ لَا ضِعَافٍ وَلَا فُشْلِ

Wa-qad adrakatni wa-al-hawadithu jammatun / asinnatu qawmin la di'afin wa-la fushli

Translation: “Amid many harsh events, the spearheads of people reached me who were neither weak nor fainthearted.”

In this reading, فُشْل is a plural describing weak or timid people.

The wording of this example is disputed, however.

Several early sources end the verse with وَلَا عُزْلِ, “nor unarmed.”

A reading وَلَا فُسْلِ is also recorded.

Accounts also differ concerning the poet and the circumstances of composition.

ولا فشل therefore cannot serve as conclusive, independent pre-Qur’anic evidence. Its value is chiefly as one transmitted lexical reading.

A verse’s presence in a dictionary does not itself establish that every word appeared in that form in the poet’s original composition.

Secondary uses of the root

Lexicons record other uses under f-sh-l.

For example, الفِشْل or المِفْشَل named an item associated with a howdah, a compartment or seat carried on a camel.

Some sources also record تَفَشَّلَ الْمَاءُ as meaning that water flowed.

These senses are distant from weakening resolve.

Without sufficient historical evidence, we cannot conclude that howdah equipment, flowing water, and loss of courage all developed from one original material image.

The branch concerning weakness and diminished courage is directly relevant to the Qur’an. The other uses show the lexical family’s recorded range.

2. Linguistic forms and semantic core

The principal recorded forms include:

  • فَشِلَ: faltered, lost courage, or showed weakness before difficulty.
  • يَفْشَلُ: falters or loses courage.
  • فَشَل: weakness, weakened resolve, or loss of courage.
  • فَشِل: a weak or timid person.
  • فَشِيل: a fainthearted or timid person.
  • أَفْشَال: a plural for people characterized by weakness or timidity.
  • فُشْل: in some lexical uses, a plural adjective for fainthearted people.
  • تَفَشَّلَ: independently recorded for flowing water.
  • فِشْل and مِفْشَل: in another branch, equipment or a covering associated with a howdah.

In modern Arabic, فَشِلَ and فَشَل are widely used for the failure of a person, program, or activity.

For example, one might say that a project failed or an examination was unsuccessful.

This current usage should not be transferred to every Qur’anic verse without examination.

The Qur’an contains these four forms:

تَفْشَلَا — Āl ʿImrān 3:122.

فَشِلْتُمْ — Āl ʿImrān 3:152.

لَفَشِلْتُمْ — al-Anfāl 8:43.

فَتَفْشَلُوا — al-Anfāl 8:46.

All belong to the basic three-consonant verb, Form I.

The Qur’an does not use the verbal noun فَشَل, an active participle, or augmented verbal forms of this root.

The Qur’anic semantic core can be expressed as:

Weakening resolve, diminished courage, or loss of the firmness needed to continue acting under difficulty and pressure.

This relates to fear without being confined to it. It can lead to defeat, but the word itself does not necessarily mean final defeat.

3. أَنْ تَفْشَلَا: two groups approaching loss of resolve

Āl ʿImrān 3:122

إِذْ هَمَّتْ طَائِفَتَانِ مِنْكُمْ أَنْ تَفْشَلَا وَاللَّهُ وَلِيُّهُمَا ۗ وَعَلَى اللَّهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ

Idh hammat ta'ifatani minkum an tafshala wa-Allahu waliyyuhuma wa-'ala Allahi fal-yatawakkali al-mu'minun

Translation: “When two groups among you were on the verge of faltering, while The God was their protector; and upon The God let the believers rely.”

تَفْشَلَا here concerns two groups of believers.

The verse does not say that they necessarily suffered defeat or abandoned their duty.

Its wording is:

«هَمَّتْ طَائِفَتَانِ مِنْكُمْ أَنْ تَفْشَلَا»

Hammat ta'ifatani minkum an tafshala

That is, two groups came close to faltering.

هَمَّتْ expresses an inclination or movement toward an action here. “Being on the verge of faltering” therefore renders the whole construction without establishing that the faltering was fully realized.

The subject is a difficult situation in which loss of courage and firmness had become a possibility.

Approaching weakness versus actually succumbing to it

This distinction matters for understanding the verse.

Facing danger, someone may briefly feel unable to continue.

They may doubt their ability or fear the scale of the responsibility.

This does not necessarily mean abandoning their duty.

For example, rescue workers confronting a major disaster may hesitate because of the danger and difficulty. After assessing conditions and encouraging one another, they may continue their work.

Hesitation and approaching weakness are not identical to actually abandoning responsibility.

Āl ʿImrān 3:122 likewise describes two groups on the verge of faltering, without saying they necessarily carried all its consequences into action.

وَاللَّهُ وَلِيُّهُمَا

Immediately after describing their situation, the verse says:

«وَاللَّهُ وَلِيُّهُمَا»

Wa-Allahu waliyyuhuma

That is, God was the protector of both groups.

Their approaching weakness did not mean they had definitively lost divine protection or all the value of their faith.

The verse ends by commanding trust in God.

The connection matters.

Trust does not mean never encountering fear or doubt. It means maintaining reliance on God in difficulty and continuing one’s responsibility with confidence and effort.

Trust should not replace planning and practical action.

The preceding verse, Āl ʿImrān 3:121, describes Muhammad setting out and assigning believers their positions for confrontation.

Practical preparation and trust thus appear together in the same context.

A believer may be psychologically vulnerable to weakness, but this alone does not end their faith or responsibility.

4. فَشِلْتُمْ: faltering alongside dispute and disobedience

Āl ʿImrān 3:152

وَلَقَدْ صَدَقَكُمُ اللَّهُ وَعْدَهُ إِذْ تَحُسُّونَهُمْ بِإِذْنِهِ ۖ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فَشِلْتُمْ وَتَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ وَعَصَيْتُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا أَرَاكُمْ مَا تُحِبُّونَ ۚ مِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرِيدُ الدُّنْيَا وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرِيدُ الْآخِرَةَ ۚ ثُمَّ صَرَفَكُمْ عَنْهُمْ لِيَبْتَلِيَكُمْ ۖ وَلَقَدْ عَفَا عَنْكُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ ذُو فَضْلٍ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

Wa-la-qad sadaqakumu Allahu wa'dahu idh tahussunahum bi-idhnihi hatta idha fashiltum wa-tanaza'tum fi al-amri wa-'asaytum min ba'di ma arakum ma tuhibbuna minkum man yuridu al-dunya wa-minkum man yuridu al-akhirata thumma sarafakum 'anhum li-yabtaliyakum wa-la-qad 'afa 'ankum wa-Allahu dhu fadlin 'ala al-mu'minin

Translation: “The God indeed fulfilled His promise to you when you were striking them down by His permission, until you faltered, disputed about the matter, and disobeyed after He had shown you what you loved. Among you were those seeking this world and those seeking the hereafter. Then He turned you away from them to test you; and He has indeed pardoned you. The God is gracious to the believers.”

Believers initially enjoyed advantage and success, before a change occurred in their collective conduct.

The Qur’an places three actions together:

فَشِلْتُمْ — you faltered.

تَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ — you disputed and struggled over the matter.

عَصَيْتُمْ — you disobeyed.

These verbs are related but not synonymous.

Faltering is not the same as dispute

فشلتم concerns weakening resolve and firmness.

تنازعتم comes from n-z-ʿ and here concerns a dispute arising among people over the matter at hand.

A group may begin with a shared decision and firm resolve, but its members may disagree over the way forward when a new opportunity or danger appears.

This conflict can reduce the group’s focus and coordination.

F-sh-l itself does not mean disagreement.

The two separate verbs distinguish faltering from dispute.

Faltering is not the same as disobedience

عَصَيْتُمْ comes from ʿ-ṣ-y and means disobedience.

Someone may experience fear and weakened resolve before danger yet continue following a rightful instruction.

Conversely, someone may confidently and fearlessly refuse their responsibility on purpose.

Not every instance of faltering is disobedience, nor does all disobedience arise from fear.

Āl ʿImrān 3:152 places these behaviors together in a particular situation.

One should not be translated as though it represented them all.

Different desires

The verse continues:

«مِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرِيدُ الدُّنْيَا وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرِيدُ الْآخِرَةَ»

Minkum man yuridu al-dunya wa-minkum man yuridu al-akhirata

Some among you sought this world, while others sought the hereafter.

Not everyone in the group shared one motivation.

In a joint activity, some may remain committed to the primary responsibility while others prefer immediate benefits.

If this difference leads to conflict and abandoned commitments at a critical moment, collective ability suffers.

The verse does not justify accusing all the believers present of worldly ambition: it explicitly distinguishes two groups.

Pardon after wrongdoing

The verse’s ending is especially significant:

«وَلَقَدْ عَفَا عَنْكُمْ»

Wa-la-qad 'afa 'ankum

God has pardoned you.

The verse does not end with weakness and disobedience alone.

The Qur’an does not conceal the error, but neither does it close the way to pardon and return.

Faltering or stumbling at one stage does not necessarily mean permanent condemnation.

Individuals and communities can recognize mistakes, examine their causes, and work to improve subsequent conduct.

Pardon does not make the mistake insignificant, however. The verse takes the consequences of weakness and conflict seriously.

5. لَفَشِلْتُمْ: faltering that could have occurred

Al-Anfāl 8:43

إِذْ يُرِيكَهُمُ اللَّهُ فِي مَنَامِكَ قَلِيلًا ۖ وَلَوْ أَرَاكَهُمْ كَثِيرًا لَفَشِلْتُمْ وَلَتَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ سَلَّمَ ۗ إِنَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ

Idh yurikahumu Allahu fi manamika qalilan wa-law arakahum kathiran la-fashiltum wa-la-tanaza'tum fi al-amri wa-lakinna Allaha sallama innahu 'alimun bi-dhati al-sudur

Translation: “When The God showed them to you as few in your dream; had He shown them to you as many, you would have faltered and disputed about the matter. But The God kept you safe. He indeed knows what lies within the breasts.”

The verse concerns confrontation between two groups and believers’ readiness in difficult circumstances.

God first shows Muhammad the opposing group as few in a dream.

It then describes a hypothetical situation:

«وَلَوْ أَرَاكَهُمْ كَثِيرًا لَفَشِلْتُمْ»

Wa-law arakahum kathiran la-fashiltum

Had He shown them as many, you would have faltered.

Fashal in a conditional sentence

لَفَشِلْتُمْ is a perfect verb in the response to لَوْ.

The construction concerns a result that could have occurred had the condition been fulfilled.

It does not state that the believers actually faltered in that situation.

It explains a danger from which they were protected.

The distinction from Āl ʿImrān 3:152 is clear.

Āl ʿImrān describes faltering that occurred.

Al-Anfāl 8:43 describes possible faltering prevented through divine care.

Perception and courage

How people perceive the scale of danger can affect their resolve and decisions.

For example, a group facing a difficult task may imagine, before starting, that it is far beyond their ability.

This perception can cause lost confidence, hesitation, and disagreement.

A more accurate understanding of conditions can sometimes help people act without excessive fear.

This does not establish that deceiving people or intentionally concealing the truth is always permissible to maintain a group’s courage.

The verse concerns Muhammad’s dream and divine action in a particular situation.

That differs from people giving false information to control others’ decisions.

قَلِيلًا also supplies no exact enemy count. The sentence provides no specific number.

One person’s dream and a group’s response

The verse begins with:

«فِي مَنَامِكَ»

Fi manamika

In your dream.

The address is singular and refers to Muhammad.

It later says:

«لَفَشِلْتُمْ وَلَتَنَازَعْتُمْ»

La-fashiltum wa-la-tanaza'tum

You would have faltered and disputed.

Both verbs are plural.

The verse thus describes a dream shown to one person and a possible consequence concerning the group.

The construction alone does not establish every detail of how the dream was communicated or how others responded.

What is clear is the connection between the group’s psychological condition, potential disagreement, and divine protection from that danger.

وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ سَلَّمَ

The main clause ends by saying that God kept them safe.

Faltering and conflict were genuine dangers to the group’s continued fulfillment of its responsibility.

Again, fashal does not mean final defeat. It describes weakness that, had it arisen, could have affected decisions and coordination.

6. فَتَفْشَلُوا: conflict that undermines collective strength

Al-Anfāl 8:46

وَأَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَلَا تَنَازَعُوا فَتَفْشَلُوا وَتَذْهَبَ رِيحُكُمْ ۖ وَاصْبِرُوا ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

Wa-ati'u Allaha wa-rasulahu wa-la tanaza'u fa-tafshalu wa-tadhhaba rihukum wa-isbiru inna Allaha ma'a al-sabirin

Translation: “Obey The God and His Messenger, and do not dispute with one another, lest you falter and your strength depart. Be patient; indeed, The God is with the patient.”

This is the clearest Qur’anic use of f-sh-l in connection with the consequences of group conflict.

Unlike Āl ʿImrān 3:152, which reports a past event, it commands believers to avoid damaging conflict.

The relationship between dispute and faltering

The verse says:

«وَلَا تَنَازَعُوا فَتَفْشَلُوا»

Wa-la tanaza'u fa-tafshalu

Do not dispute with one another, lest you falter.

Tanāzuʿ here is conflict capable of undermining a group’s coordination and firmness.

When members spend their energy fighting each other instead of fulfilling shared responsibilities, their ability to decide and act declines.

For example, disagreement over allocating rescue duties can be natural and even necessary.

But when it turns into hostility, rejection of considered decisions, and refusal to cooperate, people needing assistance may be endangered.

The problem is not merely different views, but loss of the coordination needed to fulfill responsibility.

Does the Qur’an forbid every disagreement?

No. وَلَا تَنَازَعُوا should not be detached from context and made into a ban on all questions, criticism, or differences of opinion.

Al-Shūrā 42:38 describes consultation as a quality of believers.

Āl ʿImrān 3:159 also commands Muhammad to consult believers.

Genuine consultation requires people to be able to express different views.

Constructive discussion must therefore be distinguished from exhausting conflict.

Members may disagree about the best approach yet reach a considered decision by hearing one another’s reasons.

Alternatively, disagreement may turn into personal rivalry, humiliation, hostility, or abandoned responsibility.

Al-Anfāl 8:46 warns of faltering and loss of collective strength.

It should not become a means of silencing every criticism or imposing a leader’s personal commands.

Obedience to God and the Messenger concerns truth and divine responsibility, not unconditional obedience to anyone who holds power.

وَتَذْهَبَ رِيحُكُمْ

After فتفشلوا comes:

«وَتَذْهَبَ رِيحُكُمْ»

Wa-tadhhaba rihukum

Rīḥ literally means wind.

In this Arabic expression, losing one’s rīḥ can refer to loss of strength, authority, presence, and collective effectiveness.

The image is understandable.

Just as wind can move something, a group’s strength and authority enable movement and influence.

When this strength disappears, the group can no longer act as before.

Rīḥukum here does not describe actual wind or weather.

In this confrontation context, it concerns lost collective strength.

Faltering and losing power

The verse places two consequences together:

فَتَفْشَلُوا — you falter.

وَتَذْهَبَ رِيحُكُمْ — your strength and authority depart.

Faltering can begin within the group as individuals lose resolve, courage, and readiness.

Loss of power and authority is a broader consequence expressed in practical ability and effectiveness.

The Qur’an therefore describes more than an inward feeling: exhausting conflict can lead to actual inability to fulfill responsibility.

وَاصْبِرُوا

The command concludes:

«وَاصْبِرُوا»

Wa-isbiru

Be patient.

This connects with the preceding warning.

In difficulty, people may need patience to preserve coordination, withstand pressure, hear other views, and remain committed to responsibility.

Patience here does not mean silence before wrongdoing or acceptance of injustice.

It concerns perseverance and self-restraint.

For example, someone who resists a hasty response during disagreement and seeks a reasoned solution may show greater steadiness in fulfilling responsibility.

Someone who permits clear wrongdoing to continue in the name of patience has not necessarily fulfilled the Qur’anic command.

Patience must be assessed in relation to truth, justice, and responsibility.

7. How the four Qur’anic uses relate

The four verses present a connected but varied picture of weakened resolve.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:122, two groups approach faltering, while God remains their protector and trust in Him is commanded.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:152, faltering occurs alongside dispute and disobedience, with visible consequences for the group; divine pardon is also mentioned.

In al-Anfāl 8:43, faltering is a possible consequence of perceiving a large enemy and disagreeing over decisions; God keeps the believers safe from it.

Al-Anfāl 8:46 directly forbids conflict leading to weakness and lost collective authority.

Fashal may thus appear at different stages of an undertaking.

Sometimes it arises before action, during preparation.

Sometimes it occurs after initial success amid disagreement about proceeding.

Sometimes internal conflict reduces collective ability.

None of these passages defines fashal as necessarily meaning final defeat.

Is fashal always caused by fear?

Fear is one possible cause, but not the only one.

In al-Anfāl 8:43, perceiving a large enemy could have prompted weakness.

Āl ʿImrān 3:152 places disagreement and disobedience alongside faltering.

In al-Anfāl 8:46, internal conflict can lead to fashal.

People may therefore falter through overwhelming danger, or lose resolve and ability through conflict and poor coordination.

They may also become disorganized and less earnest after an initial success.

Fashal should therefore not be translated solely as “fearing the enemy.”

Does every defeat result from fashal?

No.

A group may act courageously, plan carefully, and work properly against a much greater force yet fail to achieve its aim.

Defeat is then the action’s outcome; it does not establish that the people involved were fainthearted.

Āl ʿImrān 3:146 describes groups who did not lose heart, weaken, or surrender despite hardship in God’s cause.

People can therefore encounter difficulty, suffering, and harm while remaining steadfast.

Fashal is not identified solely by external outcomes: resolve, conduct, and response also matter.

8. Comparison with related roots

F-sh-l (فَشِلَ) and w-h-n (وَهَنَ)

Wahn concerns weakness and frailty.

This may be bodily or inward.

Āl ʿImrān 3:139 tells believers not to weaken or grieve.

Āl ʿImrān 3:146 places wahn alongside weakness and submission.

Qur’anic fashal particularly concerns diminished resolve and courage when action or confrontation is required.

The roots are close, but wahn has a broader range of weakness.

The body may weaken while resolve to fulfill responsibility remains firm.

Not every wahn should therefore be called fashal.

F-sh-l (فَشِلَ) and ḍ-ʿ-f (ضَعُفَ)

Ḍaʿf concerns insufficient ability or strength.

This may be physical, financial, social, or psychological.

Qur’anic fashal concerns diminished courage and firmness for action.

Someone with few resources may nevertheless fulfill their responsibility well.

Conversely, someone with abundant resources may lose resolve in difficulty.

Limited resources and weak resolve are not one concept.

F-sh-l (فَشِلَ) and kh-w-f (خَافَ)

Khawf is fear of danger or an unwelcome consequence.

Fashal concerns weakening courage and resolve.

People may fear a danger yet fulfill their duty despite it.

For example, someone entering a dangerous place to rescue another may understand and fear the danger without allowing fear to prevent action.

Fear itself is therefore not fashal.

Fashal arises when firmness and readiness to act diminish.

F-sh-l (فَشِلَ) and f-z-ʿ (فَزِعَ)

Fazaʿ concerns alarm and agitation, particularly before something sudden.

Fashal concerns weakness of courage and resolve.

Someone may become alarmed by sudden danger but regain composure after understanding the situation and take the necessary action.

Fazaʿ has occurred without necessarily producing fashal.

Conversely, someone may gradually lose confidence and motivation without sudden alarm.

These roots should therefore be examined independently.

F-sh-l (فَشِلَ) and ʿ-j-z (عَجَزَ)

ʿAjz concerns inability to perform an action.

It may result from real limitations of ability and resources.

Fashal chiefly concerns weakened resolve and readiness.

Someone may lack the bodily strength to lift a heavy load; that limitation cannot be called fashal without reason.

Conversely, someone capable of a task may lose courage and resolve under pressure.

Actual inability and loss of firmness are distinct concepts.

F-sh-l (فَشِلَ) and h-z-m (هَزَمَ)

Qur’anic h-z-m concerns defeating and overcoming.

Al-Baqarah 2:251 describes defeating the opposing group by God’s permission.

Qur’anic fashal concerns resolve and courage, not simply the occurrence of defeat.

Faltering may lead to defeat, but not every defeat results from faltering.

Someone may also experience fashal yet avoid defeat through assistance and renewed firmness.

This distinction is essential in translation.

F-sh-l (فَشِلَ) and n-z-ʿ (تَنَازَعَ)

Tanāzuʿ occurs alongside fashal in al-Anfāl 8:46 and Āl ʿImrān 3:152.

It concerns struggle and disagreement among people.

Fashal concerns weakening resolve and ability to act.

Conflict can cause fashal, but the words are not identical.

A disagreement may be resolved through dialogue without any weakening.

Conversely, someone may falter before difficulty without disagreeing with anyone.

Both concepts must be retained in studying these verses.

F-sh-l (فَشِلَ) and ṣ-b-r (صَبَرَ)

Ṣabr concerns perseverance, self-restraint, and endurance of difficulty.

Al-Anfāl 8:46 commands patience after warning about dispute and faltering.

This association shows that patience can help preserve firmness.

Patience is not merely fearlessness.

Someone may recognize danger and even feel fear yet persevere in fulfilling responsibility.

Ṣabr and fashal therefore cannot be reduced to “unafraid” and “afraid.”

9. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first error equates fashal with final defeat.

Some current uses understand fashal as an undertaking’s failure.

Qur’anic usage foregrounds weakened resolve and diminished courage.

Rendering لَفَشِلْتُمْ in al-Anfāl 8:43 solely as “you would have been defeated” loses the important stage of faltering before the outcome.

A second error identifies fashal with every kind of fear.

Fear can be a natural response to danger.

Someone who recognizes danger and acts to prevent harm has not necessarily experienced fashal.

A third error categorically condemns those on the verge of faltering.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:122, God remains the two groups’ protector.

Approaching weakness should therefore not be equated with lost faith or definite disobedience.

A fourth error identifies fashal, dispute, and disobedience.

Āl ʿImrān 3:152 uses three different verbs.

Weakness, disagreement, and disobedience may reinforce one another, but each has its own meaning.

A fifth error turns the warning about conflict into a ban on all criticism and differences of opinion.

Other Qur’anic verses command consultation.

Preventing fashal should not be used to stop people speaking truth or questioning mistaken decisions.

Al-Anfāl 8:46 warns about conflict leading to weakness and lost collective ability.

A sixth error supposes that trust replaces effort.

Trust in Āl ʿImrān 3:122 appears amid preparation and confrontation.

Reliance on God is therefore compatible with responsibility, preparation, and considered decision-making.

A seventh error permanently condemns someone who has faltered once.

Āl ʿImrān 3:152 mentions divine pardon after reporting weakness and disobedience.

The mistake should be recognized and corrected, without becoming an immutable judgment on someone’s entire future.

10. Suggested translations for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for Qur’anic f-sh-l include:

“To falter.”

“To become discouraged and fainthearted.”

“To lose courage.”

“To weaken in resolve.”

“To lose firmness.”

“To lose morale” may also be a natural, close equivalent in some contexts.

“To be defeated” is not an accurate default for every Qur’anic form, since it substitutes the final result for the state of resolve and courage.

Āl ʿImrān 3:122 — هَمَّتْ … أَنْ تَفْشَلَا

“They were on the verge of faltering.”

This retains the distinction between approaching weakness and its full occurrence.

Āl ʿImrān 3:152 — فَشِلْتُمْ

“You faltered.”

Faltering is reported here as part of an actual event.

Al-Anfāl 8:43 — لَفَشِلْتُمْ

“You would certainly have faltered.”

This preserves the conditional construction: faltering that could have occurred had the condition been fulfilled.

Al-Anfāl 8:46 — فَتَفْشَلُوا

“Lest you falter.”

The warning presents the verb as a possible consequence of dispute and conflict.

Brief root meaning and meaning in each verse

A short explanation can distinguish the general root meaning from each contextual meaning as follows:

Brief root meaning: weakening resolve and courage; losing firmness before difficulty.

Meaning in Āl ʿImrān 3:122: two groups approaching faltering during confrontation.

Meaning in Āl ʿImrān 3:152: believers faltering in a situation accompanied by disagreement and disobedience.

Meaning in al-Anfāl 8:43: potential faltering if the enemy were seen as numerous and disagreement arose.

Meaning in al-Anfāl 8:46: reduced firmness and collective ability to act through conflict and dispute.

Each brief explanation foregrounds its particular usage; historical discussion and ethical reflection remain separate.

11. Conclusion

Early Arabic f-sh-l concerns weakness, faltering, diminished courage, and lost firmness. In poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā, the speaker denies fashal of himself to assert his ability and readiness for confrontation.

Early lexicons likewise explain it through weakness, timidity, and faltering in difficulty.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an, all as the basic verb.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:122, two groups approach faltering, while God remains their protector and trust is commanded.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:152, faltering occurs alongside dispute and disobedience, yet divine pardon is also mentioned.

In al-Anfāl 8:43, faltering is a potential danger associated with perceiving enemy numbers and disagreeing over decisions.

In al-Anfāl 8:46, conflict can undermine collective firmness and authority.

The semantic core of f-sh-l is weakening courage and resolve before difficulty, and losing the readiness needed to continue acting.

This relates to fear, weakness, and defeat without being identical to any of them.

People may fear without faltering, or act courageously without reaching their desired outcome. They may also begin strong and successful yet falter through conflict, disobedience, or lost focus.

Qur’anic usage shows that firmness depends on more than individual courage: considered decisions, responsibility, avoiding harmful conflict, patience, and trust also matter.

Moreover, approaching weakness, or even experiencing it, is not necessarily the end of the road. The Qur’an warns about fashal while also speaking of divine protection and pardon.

Translation and research must therefore distinguish natural fear, weakened resolve, disagreement, disobedience, and actual defeat. Preserving these distinctions allows the verses to be understood without imposing the modern sense of “failure” on Qur’anic language.
