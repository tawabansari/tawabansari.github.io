From estimation to conjecture and unsupported assertion: when guessing takes the place of knowledge

The Arabic root خ ر ص concerns estimating, assessing, and speaking on the basis of something not yet properly known. Its derivatives may be used when a farmer estimates a tree’s crop before harvest or when someone offers an opinion without sufficient information.

In early Arabic, خَرْص was familiar as the estimation of date-palm and grape crops. By observing fruit still on the tree, a person assessed the yield without harvesting and weighing it all. Such an assessment could be close to reality or far from it.

This semantic field also extends to statements and assertions, particularly when someone speaks without sufficient knowledge and presents a guess as truth. In this usage, the word approaches fabrication and unsupported attribution.

Estimation and lying must nevertheless be distinguished. Someone who offers a probable conclusion after examining indications and explicitly acknowledges its uncertainty is not necessarily lying. Conversely, someone claiming knowledge and certainty about something they do not know still lacks sufficient grounds, even if their statement happens to prove true.

The Qur’an uses this root in five verses. Four contain يَخْرُصُونَ or تَخْرُصُونَ, while one contains the intensive description الْخَرَّاصُونَ.

Every Qur’anic occurrence concerns conjecture and speaking without sufficient knowledge. Some passages associate it with following ظَنّ; others describe people appealing to divine will to justify associating partners or imposing invented prohibitions.

The Qur’anic criticism of خَرْص should therefore not become a ban on every scientific estimate or practical assessment. The problem arises when guesses replace knowledge and truth without sufficient evidence, especially in claims about The God, guidance, or divine rulings.

1. Root Forms and Semantic Core

The root خ ر ص occurs five times in the Qur’an, in two morphological groups.

The verbal forms are يَخْرُصُونَ in Al-Anʿām 6:116, Yūnus 10:66, and Al-Zukhruf 43:20, and تَخْرُصُونَ in Al-Anʿām 6:148.

The nominal form الْخَرَّاصُونَ occurs in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:10.

The first four occurrences belong to the unaugmented triliteral خَرَصَ ـ يَخْرُصُ. The fifth is the sound masculine plural of خَرَّاص, on the pattern فَعَّال.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four verbs and one noun in five verses

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • خَرَصَ ـ يَخْرُصُ: estimated a quantity; in speech, guessed or made an unsupported statement without sufficient knowledge.
  • خَرْص: estimation and assessment; also conjecture or falsehood in another usage.
  • خِرْص: in some usages, the estimated amount or estimation itself; in a separate lexical branch, an ornamental ring.
  • خَارِص: someone who estimates a quantity.
  • خَرَّاص: someone who frequently guesses or makes unsupported statements; in a censured usage, a fabricator of falsehoods.
  • تَخَرَّصَ: constructed a statement from conjecture or made an unsupported assertion.
  • اِخْتَرَصَ: invented a statement and attributed it to something.
  • خُرْص and خِرْص: in another branch, a small gold or silver ring; in other usages, a branch, spearhead, or wooden implement.
  • خَرِيص: in some lexical usages, a short spear; in another branch, cold water or a place where water collects.
  • خَرِص: someone affected by hunger together with cold.

These forms occur in Arabic lexical sources. Apart from the verb خَرَصَ and the description خَرَّاص, the other derivatives do not occur in the Qur’an.

Do All These Meanings Share One Core?

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies several different underlying principles for خ ر ص.

The first is estimating a quantity. He discusses خَرْصِ النَّخْلِ as estimating the date-palm crop and relates خَرَّاص, a fabricator of falsehoods, to this field because such a person speaks about what they do not know.

Ibn Fāris also records separate branches: a gold ring, something branching off from something else, and hunger accompanied by cold.

This classification matters. Without independent evidence, we cannot claim that earrings, spears, branches, and hunger all originally developed from estimating.

Some forms may have been historically related, but sharing three root letters alone does not establish that relationship.

To understand the Qur’anic usage, we should therefore examine estimation and unsupported speech closely while acknowledging the separate lexical branches.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the distinct branches of kharṣ

From Estimation to Conjecture

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

أَصْلُ الْخَرْصِ التَّظَنِّي فِيمَا لَا تَسْتَيْقِنُهُ

aṣlu al-kharṣi al-taẓannī fīmā lā tastayqinuhu

That is, the basic sense of خَرْص is conjecturing about something of which you are not certain.

The same source identifies estimating date-palm and grape crops as examples.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī likewise connects estimation and falsehood, explaining that someone may make a statement without knowledge or sufficient evidence that nevertheless happens to match reality.

This is essential to understanding the root.

An accidentally correct result alone does not show that someone reached it through knowledge and a sound method.

For example, if someone who knows nothing about the weather predicts rain tomorrow and rain happens to fall, that does not prove they possessed meteorological knowledge when speaking.

Conversely, a forecast of a high probability of rain based on reliable data is an evidence-based assessment, even if it does not rain.

We must therefore distinguish estimation based on indications and knowledge, conjecture without sufficient support, and deliberate fabrication.

These may sometimes be expressed with related Arabic words, but their methods of knowing and ethical responsibilities are not identical.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: knowledge and guessing, even when a guess happens to be true

2. The Root’s Usages in Early Arabic

Estimating Date and Grape Crops

A clear agricultural use of خَرْص was estimating a crop before harvest.

A date palm may carry many clusters, while the exact harvested yield remains unknown until harvesting and measurement.

An experienced person would examine the trees and estimate the amount of dates on them.

Lexical sources call this خَرْصُ النَّخْلِ.

The expression also applies to grapes. An estimator could assess the grapes present and the yield that would become raisins after drying.

This differs from directly measuring the entire crop: observation and experience support an approximate assessment, presented as an estimate.

Such work could be useful in agriculture and trade.

For example, before harvesting a farmer may wish to know whether the likely crop will cover household needs and sales. Comparing the present crop with earlier years can provide an approximate assessment.

Using that result is not inherently wrong if it is identified as an estimate.

But presenting the same quantity as certain without measurement or sufficient evidence improperly crosses the boundary between assessment and a claim to knowledge.

Islamic-period legal sources also use خَرْص technically for estimating crops in certain financial and agricultural calculations. This historical usage shows that the word was not always negative.

Later legal rules for crop calculations are nevertheless not part of the root’s lexical meaning and cannot supply a new ruling for the Qur’anic passages about خَرْص.

Lisān al-ʿArab: date and grape assessment and the forms of kharṣ

Is Every Estimate Censured Kharṣ?

No.

Arabic خَرَصَ النَّخْلَ can describe an ordinary practical assessment.

In the Qur’an, however, the root occurs in contexts of uninformed claims, following conjecture, and unsupported attribution.

This distinction matters.

Human knowledge does not always involve direct certainty about every detail. Observation, measurement, comparison, probability, and the examination of evidence are used in many fields.

Such methods can yield conclusions with differing degrees of confidence.

Problems begin when someone conceals their actual degree of confidence or presents a mere possibility as established truth.

In religious study, someone may also propose several possible meanings of a verse and offer evidence for each. Simply considering a possibility is not fabricating a statement about The God.

Turning that possibility into a definite divine ruling without sufficient support is a different matter.

The Qur’anic occurrences of يَخْرُصُونَ clarify this distinction further.

An Ornamental Ring: A Separate Branch from Estimation

Lexical sources record خُرْص and خِرْص for a small gold or silver ring, especially one worn as an earring.

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ records this usage.

It could be an item of women’s jewelry, but the word concerns its ring form rather than the ethical or legal value of wearing ornaments.

The earring sense should therefore not be equated with guessing or falsehood.

These words look similar in early sources but belong to different fields of use.

This branch does not occur in the Qur’an; it helps show the breadth of the lexical family.

A Branch and a Spearhead

Ibn Fāris identifies branching off as another underlying principle of this entry.

In this branch, خُرْص can denote a twig or branch, and in another usage a spearhead or spear.

A verse attributed to Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm, a pre-Islamic poet of Aws, reads:

تَرَى قِصَدَ الْمُرَّانِ تُلْقَى كَأَنَّهَا
تَذَرُّعُ خِرْصَانٍ بِأَيْدِي الشَّوَاطِبِ

tarā qiṣada al-murrāni tulqā ka-annahā / tadharruʿu khirṣānin bi-aydī al-shawāṭibi

Approximate translation:

“You see fragments of wooden spears falling, like palm rods that women making mats lay along their forearms, splitting and preparing them.”

Here خِرْصَان appears within an image of branches and rods.

الشَّوَاطِب refers to women preparing palm leaves and branches for mat-making. Al-Aṣmaʿī explains تَذَرَّعَ الْجَرِيدَ as laying palm material along the forearm and splitting it; the image should therefore not be restricted to measuring the rods’ length.

Early explanations differ over whether خِرْصَان here concerns branches or spears and their parts.

This variation matters because it shows a connection between branch and spear usages in Arabic.

The witness, however, has no direct connection with guessing or fabricating falsehoods.

The appearance of خِرْصَان in pre-Islamic poetry therefore does not mean that the poet is discussing unsupported conjecture or false speech.

Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm was a pre-Islamic poet, and lexical sources including Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab attribute this verse to him. Some versions differ slightly in wording, but the branch-and-spear usage is clear.

Lisān al-ʿArab, dh-r-ʿ: Qays’s witness and Al-Aṣmaʿī’s explanation of mat-making

Hunger Accompanied by Cold

In another Arabic branch, خَرِصَ describes someone who is both hungry and suffering from cold.

Lisān al-ʿArab explicitly relates it to hunger and cold together.

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah reads:

فَأَصْبَحَ طَاوِيًا خَرِصًا خَمِيصًا
كَنَصْلِ السَّيْفِ حُودِثَ بِالصِّقَالِ

fa-aṣbaḥa ṭāwiyan khariṣan khamīṣan / ka-naṣli al-sayfi ḥūditha bi-al-ṣiqāli

Translation:

“He became hungry, chilled, and lean, like a sword blade freshly polished.”

خَرِصًا here describes someone affected by hunger and cold.

This usage has no clear semantic connection with crop estimation or fabrication.

Labīd lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The date of this particular verse is not independently established, so it should not be presented without qualification as a securely pre-Islamic witness.

Lisān al-ʿArab: hunger with cold and the witness attributed to Labīd

This branch again shows why words sharing root letters cannot all be reduced to one hypothetical meaning.

3. Al-Anʿām 6:116: Following Conjecture and Making Unsupported Claims

وَإِنْ تُطِعْ أَكْثَرَ مَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ يُضِلُّوكَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ إِنْ يَتَّبِعُونَ إِلَّا الظَّنَّ وَإِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَخْرُصُونَ

wa-in tuṭiʿ akthara man fī al-arḍi yuḍillūka ʿan sabīli allāhi in yattabiʿūna illā al-ẓanna wa-in hum illā yakhruṣūna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“If you obey most of those on earth, they will lead you astray from the way of The God. They follow nothing but conjecture and do nothing but guess.”

Al-Anʿām 6:116

This verse places يَخْرُصُونَ and الظَّنَّ together in one sentence.

Their combination is fundamental to understanding the root’s Qur’anic meaning.

The verse first states the consequence of following most of those on earth. It then explains that the people concerned follow conjecture and engage in خَرْص.

يَتَّبِعُونَ الظَّنَّ and يَخْرُصُونَ: A Distinction

يَتَّبِعُونَ الظَّنَّ concerns following conjecture.

يَخْرُصُونَ describes judging or speaking on the basis of estimation and guesswork.

These two clauses should not be made completely synonymous.

The first identifies what is followed; the second describes their manner of judging and speaking.

The construction إِنْ ... إِلَّا also expresses restriction: they follow nothing but conjecture and engage in nothing but guessing.

This is a negative assessment of people’s grounds of understanding and behavior.

But the verse does not independently specify whether each person knowingly lies or mistakenly follows an unsupported guess.

Their knowledge and intention must be established through further evidence.

Does the Verse Reject Following Every Majority?

The verse mentions أَكْثَرَ مَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ, then explains the group’s qualities: following conjecture and making unsupported assertions.

This criticism should not become an absolute rule that a claim is false because it has many supporters.

Just as numerous followers do not prove a claim true, few followers are not in themselves evidence of truth either.

The criteria are knowledge, evidence, and truth.

The verse should therefore not automatically dismiss majority opinion or validate every minority view.

It concerns following people who, in this context, rely on conjecture and estimation in place of knowledge and guidance.

Forqan’s published study of abrogation in the Qur’an also explains this distinction: popularity and majority support do not replace evidence.

Is Every Kind of Ẓann Censured in the Qur’an?

In Arabic and Qur’anic usage, ظَنّ has a broader semantic field, sometimes involving expectation, conjecture, or even strong confidence.

Al-Anʿām 6:116 therefore does not establish that every Qur’anic occurrence of ظَنّ or every human probabilistic assessment is wrong.

Here conjecture stands against the way of The God, and its followers are described with يَخْرُصُونَ.

The criticism concerns a way of following and judging that lacks sufficient grounds for its claims.

In scientific inquiry, a probability or hypothesis can begin an investigation. A hypothesis acquires scientific value through assessment against evidence and the possibility of revision or rejection.

This differs from turning unsupported guesswork into a definite ruling.

4. Al-Anʿām 6:148 and Al-Zukhruf 43:20: Attributing Conjecture to Divine Will

Al-Anʿām 6:148: Do You Have Knowledge Supporting Your Claim?

سَيَقُولُ الَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا لَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ مَا أَشْرَكْنَا وَلَا آبَاؤُنَا وَلَا حَرَّمْنَا مِنْ شَيْءٍ كَذَٰلِكَ كَذَّبَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ ذَاقُوا بَأْسَنَا قُلْ هَلْ عِنْدَكُمْ مِنْ عِلْمٍ فَتُخْرِجُوهُ لَنَا إِنْ تَتَّبِعُونَ إِلَّا الظَّنَّ وَإِنْ أَنْتُمْ إِلَّا تَخْرُصُونَ

sa-yaqūlu alladhīna ashrakū law shāʾa allāhu mā ashraknā wa-lā ābāʾunā wa-lā ḥarramnā min shayʾin kadhālika kadhdhaba alladhīna min qablihim ḥattā dhāqū baʾsanā qul hal ʿindakum min ʿilmin fa-tukhrijūhu lanā in tattabiʿūna illā al-ẓanna wa-in antum illā takhruṣūna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Those who associate partners will say: Had The God willed, neither we nor our fathers would have associated partners, nor would we have prohibited anything. Those before them likewise rejected the truth until they tasted Our might. Say: Do you have any knowledge that you can bring forth for us? You follow nothing but conjecture and do nothing but guess.”

Al-Anʿām 6:148

تَخْرُصُونَ is second-person plural here.

Unlike Al-Anʿām 6:116, which describes a group in the third person, this verse directs that the claimants be asked for evidence themselves.

The central point appears in the following expression:

هَلْ عِنْدَكُمْ مِنْ عِلْمٍ فَتُخْرِجُوهُ لَنَا

Do you have knowledge that you can present?

This question marks the boundary between assertion and evidence.

A group says that had The God willed, neither they nor their fathers would have associated partners or prohibited anything.

In response, the Qur’an asks them to present the knowledge supporting that assertion.

It then says that they follow conjecture and make unsupported claims.

The Central Issue: Claiming Knowledge of Divine Will

This is more than an ordinary miscalculation or mistaken prediction.

The claimants discuss their behavior’s relationship to divine will, using it to justify associating partners and imposing prohibitions.

The verse asks what knowledge supports that claim.

تَخْرُصُونَ here thus concerns speaking without sufficient grounds about something attributed to The God.

This distinction matters when examining what is permitted and prohibited.

A person may avoid a food for health reasons, on the basis of experience, caution, or medical knowledge.

Claiming that The God has prohibited that food for all people is a different assertion, requiring divine evidence.

Forqan’s study “The Permitted and the Prohibited in the Qur’an” also examines this distinction.

Forqan study of the permitted and prohibited in the Qur’an

Al-Anʿām 6:148 is particularly important because it places prohibiting things alongside a claim about divine will, then demands knowledge.

A religious claim therefore cannot be established solely through family tradition, ancestral practice, or a personal conception of what The God wills.

Does the Verse Also Address Determinism and Free Will?

The claimants argue that, had The God not willed their associating partners, they would not have done so.

The response criticizes their lack of knowledge and their following conjecture.

This answer does not settle the entire philosophical and theological question of determinism and free will in one sentence.

Understanding divine will, human choice, guidance, and responsibility requires a wider network of verses.

What is clear here is that these people may not attribute their behavior and prohibitions to divine will without knowledge and use that attribution to justify their claim.

This conclusion follows from the context and does not require every philosophical dispute about divine will to be settled beforehand.

Al-Ṭabarī: the demand for knowledge and divine will versus alleged approval

Al-Zukhruf 43:20: Repeating the Claim about Worshiping Others

وَقَالُوا لَوْ شَاءَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ مَا عَبَدْنَاهُمْ مَا لَهُمْ بِذَٰلِكَ مِنْ عِلْمٍ إِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَخْرُصُونَ

wa-qālū law shāʾa al-raḥmānu mā ʿabadnāhum mā lahum bi-dhālika min ʿilmin in hum illā yakhruṣūna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“They said: Had the Entirely Merciful willed, we would not have worshiped them. They have no knowledge of that; they do nothing but guess.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:20

Its construction and argument resemble Al-Anʿām 6:148.

Both describe people invoking divine will to justify worshiping others besides The God.

Both emphasize the absence of knowledge.

Al-Zukhruf 43:20 expresses this concisely:

مَا لَهُمْ بِذَٰلِكَ مِنْ عِلْمٍ

They have no knowledge of that.

It then states:

إِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَخْرُصُونَ

They do nothing but guess.

This combination clarifies يَخْرُصُونَ.

Here خَرْص is speaking without sufficient knowledge about the matter asserted.

The verse does not say that anyone asking a religious question or examining different possibilities is a خَرَّاص.

The group concerned asserts something about divine will and uses it to justify worshiping others besides Him.

The criticism therefore concerns the assertion and its lack of a basis in knowledge.

Does يَخْرُصُونَ Necessarily Mean Deliberate Lying?

Lexical sources also explain this usage through كَذِب, falsehood.

Yet a false result, an unsupported method of speaking, and a conscious intention to deceive must be distinguished.

Someone may know a statement is false and deliberately attribute it to The God.

Another may consider it true through imitation, ignorance, or error and state it confidently without sufficient evidence.

Their intentions and ethical responsibilities are not identical.

يَخْرُصُونَ concerns speaking through unsupported conjecture and, in a censured context, can take the meaning of fabricating falsehoods.

Establishing every speaker’s conscious intention requires additional evidence.

A false claim alone therefore does not justify a definite judgment about every speaker’s inward knowledge and intention to deceive.

5. Yūnus 10:66: Asserting Partners on the Basis of Conjecture

أَلَا إِنَّ لِلَّهِ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَمَا يَتَّبِعُ الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ شُرَكَاءَ إِنْ يَتَّبِعُونَ إِلَّا الظَّنَّ وَإِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَخْرُصُونَ

alā inna li-allāhi man fī al-samāwāti wa-man fī al-arḍi wa-mā yattabiʿu alladhīna yadʿūna min dūni allāhi shurakāʾa in yattabiʿūna illā al-ẓanna wa-in hum illā yakhruṣūna

Proposed translation using the interrogative reading of mā, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Be aware: whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on earth belongs to The God. What do those who invoke partners besides The God follow? They follow nothing but conjecture and do nothing but guess.”

Yūnus 10:66

يَخْرُصُونَ again accompanies following conjecture.

Its subject matter, however, differs from Al-Anʿām 6:116.

Yūnus 10:66 concerns people invoking partners besides The God.

The verse first states His ownership and authority over those in the heavens and earth.

It then raises the question of following alleged partners.

يَخْرُصُونَ thus concerns unsupported statements and judgments about partners attributed to The God.

Syntactic Differences over وَمَا يَتَّبِعُ

Several analyses have been proposed for مَا in وَمَا يَتَّبِعُ الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ شُرَكَاءَ.

In one reading, مَا negates: people invoking others besides The God are not actually following real partners.

In another, مَا is interrogative: what are they actually following?

Other analyses treat مَا as a relative pronoun.

These differences affect the earlier construction, but yield a shared conclusion for the ending: their following rests on conjecture and their statements are described as خَرْص.

Understanding يَخْرُصُونَ therefore does not require declaring one possible analysis of the preceding clause certain without decisive evidence.

Al-Darwīsh’s Iʿrāb al-Qurʾān wa-Bayānuh: negative, interrogative, and relative mā

Kharṣ and Asserting Partners

In this context, people assign beings or authorities besides The God a place in worship and allegiance.

The Qur’an sets this claim against His ownership and authority and criticizes following conjecture.

خَرْص here therefore concerns a religious assertion and the justification of associating partners.

The root خ ر ص itself, however, does not mean associating partners.

People can also make unsupported statements on other subjects, or estimate a quantity or outcome in a nonreligious matter.

What defines this occurrence is the assertion’s subject and its lack of sufficient evidence.

This distinction matters in Qur’an-centered research. A belief’s popularity or place in a longstanding tradition cannot alone establish that its attribution to The God is true.

Criticizing a claim should nevertheless not produce one uniform judgment about all its followers’ intentions and awareness.

Someone knowingly inventing an unsupported assertion differs from someone following it through trust, upbringing, or lack of knowledge.

This distinction follows from examining people’s responsibility and knowledge, rather than خَرْص independently specifying every psychological state.

6. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:10: Who Are الْخَرَّاصُونَ?

قُتِلَ الْخَرَّاصُونَ

qutila al-kharrāṣūna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Perish the makers of unsupported conjectures.”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:10

This short verse is the only Qur’anic occurrence of the noun خَرَّاص.

It follows the intensive pattern فَعَّال, with الْخَرَّاصُونَ as its sound masculine plural.

This formation can convey the intensity or prominence of the characteristic.

Lexical sources explain خَرَّاص through كَذَّاب: a fabricator of falsehoods.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī nevertheless explains خَرْص as speaking through conjecture and estimation without knowledge or reliable support.

Two related aspects are therefore present: conjecture and fabrication.

To establish the verse’s precise sense, we must examine the context of Al-Dhāriyāt.

قُتِلَ: Imprecation and Strong Censure

قُتِلَ belongs to ق ت ل.

Besides reporting killing, this Arabic construction can express an imprecation or strong censure.

That reading fits Al-Dhāriyāt 51:10.

Accordingly, “Perish the conjecturers” or “May the conjecturers perish” is defensible.

This phrase alone should not be taken as a command to kill people who speculate about something.

It condemns a group with a particular characteristic and issues no independent command for violent action against them.

Study of Q T L

الَّذِينَ هُمْ فِي غَمْرَةٍ سَاهُونَ

The next verse, Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11, describes them as immersed in heedlessness and distraction.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:12 then mentions their question about when the Day of Recompense will come.

These verses show that the subject is more than estimating a crop or predicting an ordinary event.

The context concerns the divine promise, the Day of Recompense, and people’s response to it.

الْخَرَّاصُونَ here thus describes people making uninformed assertions about accounting and human destiny while remaining heedless.

Their question “When is the Day of Recompense?” does not alone establish that everyone asking about the Resurrection’s timing belongs to this group.

Seeking knowledge differs from a mocking question or an unsupported assertion.

Al-Dhāriyāt describes a group characterized by conjecture and heedlessness.

الْخَرَّاصُونَ therefore does not simply mean “questioners.”

Al-Ṭabarī: censure of conjecturers, heedlessness, and the Day of Recompense

Does a Kharrāṣ Necessarily Always Lie Deliberately?

In Arabic, خَرَّاص can describe someone repeatedly making unsupported statements.

The intentional nature of every false statement nevertheless requires care.

Someone may consciously fabricate a statement contrary to reality.

Another may mistake a guess for truth without sufficient knowledge and announce it confidently.

Both ways of speaking can depart from truth and sound grounds, but their intentions and degrees of responsibility differ.

Thus “fabricators of falsehoods” has lexical support, while “makers of unsupported conjectures” makes the connection with estimation and lack of knowledge clearer.

An explanation can add that this conjecture leads to or overlaps with fabrication in the verse’s context.

7. Distinctions from Related Roots

خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ, Kharṣ) and ظ ن ن (Ẓ N N, Ẓann): Conjecture and Speaking from It

These roots appear together in Al-Anʿām 6:116, Al-Anʿām 6:148, and Yūnus 10:66.

Qur’anic ظَنّ concerns conjecture, expectation, and different degrees of confidence.

In its Qur’anic branch, خَرْص concerns assessing and speaking through guesses and estimation.

In the censured usage, someone speaks without sufficient knowledge and presents the result as truth.

ظَنّ can therefore concern a cognitive state or the basis of following, whereas خَرْص in these verses concerns unsupported judgment and assertion.

Not every ظَنّ is خَرْص, however, and not every probabilistic assessment is wrong.

The main problem in these contexts is replacing knowledge and truth with unsupported conjecture.

Study of Ẓ N N

خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ, Kharṣ) and ك ذ ب (K DH B, Kadhib): Unsupported Guessing and Falsehood

Many Arabic dictionaries explain خَرْص in speech through كَذِب.

There is nevertheless a distinction.

كَذِب can describe a statement contrary to reality, sometimes accompanied by an intention to deceive.

خَرْص in its estimation branch concerns speaking without sufficient knowledge, whether the estimate matches reality or not.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī draws attention to precisely this distinction.

If someone guesses and happens to be right, the correct result does not necessarily show that they possessed knowledge.

Calling someone a conscious liar, however, requires distinguishing a false statement from an intention to deceive.

The Qur’an’s censured usages overlap with falsehood, but these roots should not be treated as completely identical in every usage.

Study of K DH B

خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ, Kharṣ) and ح ز ر (Ḥ Z R, Ḥazr): Estimating a Quantity

Arabic حَزْر concerns estimating a quantity.

Lexicographers repeatedly explain خَرْصُ النَّخْلِ through حَزْرُ الثَّمَرِ, estimating fruit.

Their proximity is clear in crop assessment.

Qur’anic خَرْص extends to speaking without sufficient knowledge, while حَزْر in lexical explanations of this family chiefly provides the sense of quantity estimation.

Analysis of the Qur’anic usage therefore cannot stop at agriculture.

Estimating a crop and inventing an unsupported religious claim also differ in subject and ethical value.

خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ, Kharṣ) and ح د س (Ḥ D S, Ḥads): Guessing and Conjecture

Arabic حَدْس concerns apprehending or estimating something without complete measurement.

Modern Persian “guessing” is likewise a natural equivalent for part of خَرْص’s meaning.

Qur’anic يَخْرُصُونَ, however, occurs in contexts of unsupported assertion and presenting a statement as truth.

Translating it merely as “they guess” may therefore leave its critical force unclear.

The explanation should clarify that the problem is more than lacking certainty: it is asserting something without sufficient knowledge and evidence.

خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ, Kharṣ) and ف ر ي (F R Y, Iftirāʾ): Conjecture and Fabricated Speech

Qur’anic افْتِرَاء concerns inventing and attributing a false statement, especially fabricating something about The God.

خَرْص can also concern an unsupported statement about Him.

افْتِرَاء highlights invention and attribution, whereas خَرْص in the branch discussed begins with unsupported assessment and estimation.

Both may come together in one assertion.

For example, attributing a specific ruling to The God without divine evidence can concern خَرْص through lack of knowledge and افْتِرَاء through attributing a fabricated claim to Him.

Intention and degree of awareness must still be examined separately.

خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ, Kharṣ) and ع ل م (ʿ L M, ʿIlm): Assertion and Its Basis in Knowledge

Al-Anʿām 6:148 and Al-Zukhruf 43:20 directly contrast knowledge with خَرْص.

The first asks claimants to present their knowledge.

The second explicitly states their lack of knowledge about the claim.

This contrast shows that the main problem is the absence of grounds for what is said.

It does not establish that people may speak only after reaching absolute certainty about every question.

Hypotheses, possibilities, and assessments can have a place in inquiry and practical decisions, provided their limits, confidence levels, and evidence are clear.

A claim of a definite divine ruling or an attribution to revelation, however, requires grounds appropriate to that claim.

8. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for the Qur’anic verb include “guess,” “conjecture,” and “make unsupported statements.”

“Lie” also has lexical support, but in some passages it may obscure the difference between lack of knowledge and a conscious intention to deceive.

The basic translation should therefore preserve the sense of guessing and conjecture, with its critical force clarified by construction and context.

يَخْرُصُونَ — Al-Anʿām 6:116

Root: خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ).

Grammar: Form I active imperfect, third-person masculine plural.

Brief root meaning: Estimating and assessing; in speech, presenting unsupported guesses and conjectures.

Meaning here: Those following conjecture speculate without sufficient knowledge.

Proposed translation: “They do nothing but guess.”

تَخْرُصُونَ — Al-Anʿām 6:148

Root: خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ).

Grammar: Form I active imperfect, second-person masculine plural.

Brief root meaning: Assessing and guessing; in a censured usage, making an unsupported assertion.

Meaning here: Without presenting knowledge, the addressees speculate about divine will to justify associating partners and imposing prohibitions.

Proposed translation: “You do nothing but guess.”

يَخْرُصُونَ — Yūnus 10:66

Root: خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ).

Grammar: Form I active imperfect, third-person masculine plural.

Brief root meaning: Estimation and conjecture without sufficient knowledge.

Meaning here: They speak on the basis of conjecture about alleged partners of The God.

Proposed translation: “They do nothing but guess.”

يَخْرُصُونَ — Al-Zukhruf 43:20

Root: خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ).

Grammar: Form I active imperfect, third-person masculine plural.

Brief root meaning: Assessing and guessing; in speech, presenting unsupported assertions.

Meaning here: They claim without knowledge that the Entirely Merciful willed their worship of others.

Proposed translation: “They do nothing but guess.”

الْخَرَّاصُونَ — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:10

Root: خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ).

Grammar: An intensive noun on فَعَّال, sound masculine plural, definite and nominative; the passive subject of قُتِلَ.

Brief root meaning: An intensive conjecturer; someone characterized by unsupported speech and fabrication.

Meaning here: People engaging in conjecture and unsupported assertions about the Day of Recompense and the truth of the divine promise.

Proposed translation: “Perish the makers of unsupported conjectures.”

This is more precise than “Perish those who guess,” which could wrongly include anyone making an estimate in a scientific or practical matter.

The intensive pattern of خَرَّاص also does not establish a specific number of lies or guesses. It expresses the intensity or prominence of the characteristic.

9. Conclusion

In the main branch relevant to Qur’anic usage, خ ر ص denotes assessment, estimation, and guessing. Early Arabic knew this sense in estimating date and grape yields.

The same family also concerns speech, particularly when someone speaks without sufficient knowledge or evidence and presents conjecture as truth.

Lexical sources also explain خَرْص through falsehood. Estimation, a false statement, and deliberate lying nevertheless differ. An unsupported estimate may happen to be right, just as an evidence-based estimate may produce an incorrect result despite a sound method.

Ibn Fāris records separate branches, including ornamental rings, branches, spears, and hunger accompanied by cold. These must be recognized, but cannot all be derived from estimation or falsehood without evidence.

The Qur’an uses خ ر ص five times. Four verbal occurrences concern following conjecture and making uninformed assertions. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:10 strongly condemns الْخَرَّاصُونَ.

Al-Anʿām 6:148 and Al-Zukhruf 43:20 explicitly state the lack of knowledge supporting claims about divine will. These verses show that behavior, worship, and invented prohibitions cannot be attributed to The God without evidence.

The central error is therefore more than having a conjecture: it is putting unsupported conjecture in the place of knowledge and truth.

This distinction also matters in Qur’an-centered research. People may examine possible meanings of a word or verse and keep their conclusion tentative where evidence is insufficient. Turning that possibility into a definite, binding divine ruling is a new claim requiring evidence.

Final definition: In its Qur’anic branch, خ ر ص concerns assessing and estimating without certain knowledge, extending in speech to conjecture and unsupported assertion. Qur’anic يَخْرُصُونَ and تَخْرُصُونَ describe people speaking without sufficient knowledge and replacing truth with conjecture; الْخَرَّاصُونَ intensively describes such conjecturers. This may overlap with fabrication, but a conscious intention to deceive should not be attributed to every speaker without evidence. Brief root meaning: Assessment and estimation; in censured speech, conjecture and unsupported assertion.
