1. Basic meaning and linguistic history

The Arabic root Q N Ṭ denotes despair and losing hope of receiving good, having a difficulty removed, or obtaining expected mercy.

Classical Arabic sources explain qunūṭ through yaʾs, despair. Some lexicographers specify despair of good; others describe it as an especially intense form of despair.

This study did not establish sufficient independent, datable evidence of pre-Qur’anic usage to determine the word’s precise earlier history. The Qur’an itself clearly attests its meaning of despair at the time of revelation, in contexts ranging from expecting a child to rainfall and forgiveness of sins.

Qur’anic forms examined: The root occurs six times in six verses: Al-Ḥijr 15:55, Al-Ḥijr 15:56, Al-Rūm 30:36, Al-Zumar 39:53, Fuṣṣilat 41:49, and Al-Shūrā 42:28. Four additional complete verses in this study provide context or comparative evidence.

  • قَنَطُوا (qanaṭū): they despaired.
  • يَقْنَطُ (yaqnaṭu) and يَقْنَطُونَ (yaqnaṭūna): he despairs and they despair.
  • لَا تَقْنَطُوا (lā taqnaṭū): do not despair; a second-person plural imperfect verb in a prohibition, not an imperative verb.
  • الْقَانِطِينَ (al-qāniṭīna): the despairing.
  • قَنُوط (qanūṭ): deeply despairing.

Other forms are known in Arabic, including قُنُوط (qunūṭ), the verbal noun meaning despair, and تَقْنِيط (taqnīṭ), making someone else despair. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Distinguish قُنُوط (qunūṭ), with ḍammah on the qāf, the verbal noun naming the state of despair, from قَنُوط (qanūṭ), with fatḥah on the qāf, a description of a despairing person.

2. Semantic core and Arabic usage

The Arabic expression قَنَطَ مِنَ الْخَيْرِ (qanaṭa mina al-khayr) means losing hope of receiving good; قَنَطَ مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ (qanaṭa mina al-raḥmah) means losing hope of receiving mercy.

Ibn Fāris identifies the root’s basic meaning as despair of something. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī more specifically defines qunūṭ as despair of good. Some lexicons emphasize the intensity of this despair.

Qunūṭ names an inward state related to a person’s expectations of the future. Explaining this meaning does not require assuming a physical origin such as cutting a rope or closing a gate; this study has not established such an origin.

Qunūṭ is not the difficulty itself. Difficulty is the situation a person faces, whereas qunūṭ concerns that person’s response to the possibility of its changing.

3. Qur’anic uses of the root

Despair in the face of divine good news

In Abraham’s story, when the divine messengers give him good news of a knowledgeable son, his old age leads him to express surprise:

قالَ أَبَشَّرتُمونى عَلىٰ أَن مَسَّنِىَ الكِبَرُ فَبِمَ تُبَشِّرونَ

Qāla a-bashshartumūnī ʿalā an massaniya al-kibaru fa-bi-ma tubashshirūn.

“He said: Do you give me good news when old age has overtaken me? Of what, then, do you give me good news?”

Al-Hijr 15:54

The messengers answer:

قالوا بَشَّرنٰكَ بِالحَقِّ فَلا تَكُن مِنَ القٰنِطينَ

Qālū bashsharnāka bi-l-ḥaqqi fa-lā takun mina al-qāniṭīn.

“They said: We have given you good news in truth, so do not be among the despairing.”

Al-Hijr 15:55

الْقَانِطِينَ (al-qāniṭīna) is the sound masculine plural of قَانِط (qāniṭ), the active participle of the Form I verb.

Abraham replies:

قالَ وَمَن يَقنَطُ مِن رَحمَةِ رَبِّهِ إِلَّا الضّالّونَ

Qāla wa-man yaqnaṭu min raḥmati rabbihi illā al-ḍāllūn.

“He said: Who despairs of his Lord’s mercy except those who are astray?”

Al-Hijr 15:56

This reply indicates that Abraham’s surprise at the news of a child did not amount to despair of his Lord’s mercy.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:40, Zechariah also mentions his old age and his wife’s barrenness after receiving good news of a child. His question follows the prayer he himself had addressed to his Lord for a child.

Asking how good news will be fulfilled therefore does not, by itself, constitute qunūṭ.

Despair when facing difficulty

The Qur’an describes one form of human instability in responding to ease and difficulty:

وَإِذا أَذَقنَا النّاسَ رَحمَةً فَرِحوا بِها وَإِن تُصِبهُم سَيِّئَةٌ بِما قَدَّمَت أَيديهِم إِذا هُم يَقنَطونَ

Wa-idhā adhaqnā al-nāsa raḥmatan fariḥū bi-hā wa-in tuṣibhum sayyiʾatun bi-mā qaddamat aydīhim idhā hum yaqnaṭūn.

“When We let people taste mercy, they rejoice in it; but if an evil befalls them because of what their hands have sent ahead, suddenly they despair.”

Ar-Rum 30:36

يَقْنَطُونَ (yaqnaṭūna) is a masculine plural imperfect verb. The sudden change of state is conveyed by إِذَا هُمْ (idhā hum), not by the root Q N Ṭ itself.

Here the difficulty under discussion is connected with people’s previous deeds. بِمَا قَدَّمَتْ أَيْدِيهِمْ (bi-mā qaddamat aydīhim) recalls their responsibility for bringing about this situation.

A person may face difficulty because of poor decisions, lack of foresight, or harmful conduct. If, even after recognizing the error, that person loses hope of improvement, despair may obstruct efforts to repair what was done.

The verse does not say that every difficulty or calamity necessarily results from an individual’s own wrongdoing. It identifies that relationship in the particular situation it connects with people’s previous deeds.

Despair of forgiveness of sins

One of the clearest uses of this root occurs in Al-Zumar:

قُل يٰعِبادِىَ الَّذينَ أَسرَفوا عَلىٰ أَنفُسِهِم لا تَقنَطوا مِن رَحمَةِ اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَغفِرُ الذُّنوبَ جَميعًا إِنَّهُ هُوَ الغَفورُ الرَّحيمُ

Qul yā ʿibādiya alladhīna asrafū ʿalā anfusihim lā taqnaṭū min raḥmati Allāhi inna Allāha yaghfiru al-dhunūba jamīʿan innahu huwa al-ghafūru al-raḥīm.

“Say: My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of The God. Indeed, The God forgives all sins. Indeed, He is the Forgiving, the Merciful.”

Az-Zumar 39:53

لَا تَقْنَطُوا (lā taqnaṭū) is a prohibition, warning those addressed against despair. مِنْ رَحْمَةِ اللَّهِ (min raḥmati Allāh) specifies what they must not despair of.

Those addressed have transgressed against themselves. Despite their wrongdoing, the Qur’an still calls them “My servants” and warns them against despair.

Someone who considers past sins so great that no return remains possible may resign themselves to continuing the same conduct. The Qur’an challenges this belief and presents a path of return.

The next verse says:

وَأَنيبوا إِلىٰ رَبِّكُم وَأَسلِموا لَهُ مِن قَبلِ أَن يَأتِيَكُمُ العَذابُ ثُمَّ لا تُنصَرونَ

Wa-anībū ilā rabbikum wa-aslimū lahu min qabli an yaʾtiyakumu al-ʿadhābu thumma lā tunṣarūn.

“Return to your Lord and submit to Him before the punishment comes upon you; then you will not be helped.”

Az-Zumar 39:54

The connection between the two verses is clear: people are first warned against despair, then called to return and submit.

Hope of forgiveness does not mean treating sin as unimportant or knowingly continuing wrongful conduct. Hope provides a basis for returning, reforming, and making amends.

Yaʾūs qanūṭ: despair and its intensity

The Qur’an says in Fuṣṣilat:

لا يَسـَٔمُ الإِنسٰنُ مِن دُعاءِ الخَيرِ وَإِن مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ فَيَـٔوسٌ قَنوطٌ

Lā yasʾamu al-insānu min duʿāʾi al-khayri wa-in massahu al-sharru fa-yaʾūsun qanūṭ.

“The human being does not tire of asking for good; but if evil touches him, he is despairing, deeply hopeless.”

Fussilat 41:49

This verse uses three different roots to describe human conduct: S ʾ M, Y ʾ S, and Q N Ṭ.

Saʾam concerns weariness and becoming tired of something; yaʾs concerns loss of hope; qunūṭ concerns despair of good and relief.

Both يَئُوس (yaʾūs) and قَنُوط (qanūṭ) follow the faʿūl pattern, which can express the intensity and prominence of a state in this context.

The pairing of yaʾūs and qanūṭ emphasizes intense despair. Some dictionaries’ description of qunūṭ as severe despair does not establish a fixed hierarchy between the two words. Nor does the verse set out successive psychological stages or particular bodily symptoms.

The verse highlights the contrast between unending requests for good and profound despair when evil arrives.

Despair before rainfall

وَهُوَ الَّذى يُنَزِّلُ الغَيثَ مِن بَعدِ ما قَنَطوا وَيَنشُرُ رَحمَتَهُ وَهُوَ الوَلِىُّ الحَميدُ

Wa-huwa alladhī yunazzilu al-ghaytha min baʿdi mā qanaṭū wa-yanshuru raḥmatahu wa-huwa al-waliyyu al-ḥamīd.

“He is the One who sends down relieving rain after they have despaired and spreads His mercy. He is the Guardian, the Praiseworthy.”

Ash-Shura 42:28

قَنَطُوا (qanaṭū) is a masculine plural perfect verb. مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا (min baʿdi mā) expresses the temporal relationship between people’s despair and the coming of rain.

A farmer who has waited a long time for rain while crops face destruction may lose hope that rain will come and save them.

Yet losing hope of rain does not mean that rain has become impossible. The limits of human expectation do not determine the limits of possibility in creation.

This usage also shows that Qur’anic qunūṭ is not restricted to despair of forgiveness or salvation in the Hereafter. The same root describes loss of hope of good and relief in earthly life.

4. Comparison with related roots

Q N Ṭ (qunūṭ) and Y ʾ S (yaʾs): despair and loss of hope

Qunūṭ and yaʾs are closely related Arabic concepts. Lexicographers repeatedly explain one through the other.

Yaʾs denotes losing hope or expectation that something will occur. Qunūṭ belongs to the same semantic field, with despair of good, mercy, and relief prominent in its Qur’anic uses.

Jacob tells his sons:

يٰبَنِىَّ اذهَبوا فَتَحَسَّسوا مِن يوسُفَ وَأَخيهِ وَلا تَا۟يـَٔسوا مِن رَوحِ اللَّهِ إِنَّهُ لا يَا۟يـَٔسُ مِن رَوحِ اللَّهِ إِلَّا القَومُ الكٰفِرونَ

Yā baniyya idhhabū fa-taḥassasū min Yūsufa wa-akhīhi wa-lā tayʾasū min rawḥi Allāhi innahu lā yayʾasu min rawḥi Allāhi illā al-qawmu al-kāfirūn.

“My sons, go and seek news of Joseph and his brother, and do not despair of relief from The God. Indeed, none despairs of relief from The God except the disbelieving people.”

Yusuf 12:87

This verse closely parallels Al-Ḥijr 15:56 in subject: Jacob’s words use Y ʾ S concerning divine relief, while Abraham’s words use Q N Ṭ concerning his Lord’s mercy.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:23, yaʾs itself is directly connected with divine mercy. Yaʾs therefore cannot be restricted to worldly matters, nor qunūṭ to divine mercy.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 speaks of the despair of those opposed to the believers’ religion. In Al-Ṭalāq 65:4, yaʾs concerns the ending of the expectation of menstruation.

Q N Ṭ (qunūṭ) and B L S (iblās): despair and helplessness

Lexical sources explain إِبْلَاس (iblās) through despair and deep distress; in some uses it also denotes being unable to answer or falling silent. These senses overlap with qunūṭ, but they should not all be imported into every occurrence at once.

The Qur’an describes people’s condition before rain:

وَإِن كانوا مِن قَبلِ أَن يُنَزَّلَ عَلَيهِم مِن قَبلِهِ لَمُبلِسينَ

Wa-in kānū min qabli an yunazzala ʿalayhim min qablihi la-mublisīn.

“Although before it was sent down upon them—before that—they had been deeply despairing.”

Ar-Rum 30:49

Al-Rūm 30:49 and Al-Shūrā 42:28 both describe despair before rain, one using mublisīn and the other qanaṭū. In this context, mublisīn alone does not establish that people were literally silent or that every remedy had permanently disappeared.

Al-Rūm 30:12 uses iblās in the setting of the Hour’s arrival and the condition of the guilty. The severity and consequences of that condition must be understood from its context, rather than made the fixed meaning of iblās in every verse.

Qunūṭ thus emphasizes losing hope; iblās can also express this despair, accompanied, according to context, by helplessness or inability to answer. The rainfall passage shows that iblās need not be permanent or irreversible.

Q N Ṭ (qunūṭ) and Ḥ Z N (ḥuzn): despair and grief

Ḥuzn concerns inward sorrow and suffering, whereas qunūṭ concerns loss of hope of good and relief.

A person may grieve over losing loved ones, illness, or hardship while still retaining hope for the future.

In Yūsuf 12:86, Jacob brings his suffering and grief before The God; immediately afterward, in Yūsuf 12:87, he warns his sons against despair.

Being sorrowful should therefore not, by itself, be treated as evidence of qunūṭ or weak faith.

Q N Ṭ (qunūṭ) and R J W (rajāʾ): despair and hope

Rajāʾ belongs to the semantic field of hope and expectation and stands in contrast to qunūṭ.

Hope of relief does not mean certainty that every wish will be fulfilled in exactly the form or at the time a person expects.

Someone may lose hope of a particular wish being fulfilled through a certain means while still hoping for good and relief through another.

Q N Ṭ must also be distinguished from Q N T, which concerns obedience and submission, and the four-letter root Q N Ṭ R, under which qinṭār is studied.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

For قَنَطَ (qanaṭa) and its verbal forms, “despair” or “lose hope” are suitable equivalents.

الْقَانِطِينَ (al-qāniṭīna) can be rendered “the despairing”; قَنُوط (qanūṭ) in Fuṣṣilat 41:49 can be rendered “deeply despairing” or “deeply hopeless.”

For لَا تَقْنَطُوا مِنْ رَحْمَةِ اللَّهِ (lā taqnaṭū min raḥmati Allāh), “do not despair of the mercy of The God” is a natural and accurate rendering.

Both descriptions in يَئُوسٌ قَنُوطٌ (yaʾūsun qanūṭ) should be preserved, for example, “despairing, deeply hopeless.” Reducing them to a single word weakens the emphasis of the construction.

Qunūṭ should not be translated as identical with depression, grief, fatigue, or ceasing to try. These may accompany despair, but none is, by itself, its lexical equivalent.

6. Conclusion

Q N Ṭ denotes despair and loss of hope of good and relief. Lexicons also emphasize intense despair, although a fixed and definitive difference in degree between qunūṭ and yaʾs has not been established for every usage.

The Qur’an uses the root in connection with good news of a child, difficulties in life, forgiveness of sins, and waiting for rain.

Proposed definition for Forqan: Despair and loss of hope of receiving good, having difficulty removed, or obtaining mercy and relief; in some contexts, intense despair.
