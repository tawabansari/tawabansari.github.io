Pulling, uprooting, and shaking: from forceful displacement to lifting a mountain

1. Introducing the root and its semantic core

The Arabic root N T Q concerns pulling, shaking, uprooting, and displacing something with perceptible force. In familiar uses, something is moved, shaken, or detached from its location, or its contents or attachments are dislodged through shaking and pulling.

This family includes several related meanings: pulling something, shaking it, uprooting it, lifting it, and shaking a container to empty its contents.

These meanings are related without being identical. Each involves movement or displacement through force. Yet every use does not necessarily include a sequence of shaking, uprooting, lifting, and finally throwing.

Pulling a bucket from a well does not necessarily mean shaking it; shaking a container to remove its contents does not necessarily mean uprooting the container itself.

A suitable semantic core is therefore pulling and displacing something from its previous state or location, often with shaking or perceptible force. The precise meaning depends on the verb, object, and context.

Qur’anic forms

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Aʿrāf 7:171:

نَتَقْنَا: “We uprooted”; in this verse, uprooting the mountain and raising it above them.

This is a Form I perfect verb in the first-person plural. Nā is its subject suffix, referring here to The God.

No other verb or noun of this root occurs in the Qur’an. Arabic also attests نَتْق, نُتُوق, نَاتِق, مِنْتَاق, and نَتِيقَة, but these are not Qur’anic forms.

2. Early Arabic usage

Pulling and uprooting

In material uses, نَتَقَ الشَّيْءَ means pulling something and moving or uprooting it from its place.

One attested expression is:

نَتَقْتُ الْغَرْبَ مِنَ الْبِئْرِ

nataqtu al-gharba mina al-biʾri

Meaning: “I pulled the large bucket up from the well.”

Gharb names a large bucket. Pulling and lifting are prominent here. The bucket need not be broken or detached from something; the point is pulling it from its previous location.

Uprooting is therefore an important meaning, but should not be presented as the root’s only possible sense.

Shaking a container to remove its contents

Another use concerns shaking a waterskin, bag, or container to remove something from within it.

نَتَقَ السِّقَاءَ and نَتَقَ الْجِرَابَ describe shaking a waterskin or bag in a way that dislodges or releases its contents.

A waterskin may be shaken to detach some of the material inside, or a bag moved so that its contents fall out.

The container itself need not be uprooted; shaking it is a means of removing something else.

The root should therefore not be confined to uprooting something from the earth. Uprooting is one instance of movement and pulling, rather than the entire semantic range.

An animal jolting its rider or load

Arabic also uses this root when a mount’s vigorous movement jolts its rider or load.

فَرَسٌ نَاتِقٌ, for example, describes a horse whose movements jolt its rider.

The root also describes a camel whose bodily movements pull or loosen the ropes securing its load.

Perceptible movement and jolting are central here. Emptying a container or uprooting something from the ground is not necessary to these uses.

A definition should therefore encompass pulling, shaking, and uprooting without becoming so broad that every simple movement is called natq.

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry: nātiq and prolific childbearing

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī uses نَاتِق in connection with prolific childbearing:

لَمْ يُحْرَمُوا حُسْنَ الْغِذَاءِ وَأُمُّهُمْ
طَفَحَتْ عَلَيْكَ بِنَاتِقٍ مِذْكَارِ

lam yuḥramū ḥusna al-ghidhāʾi wa-ummuhum / ṭafaḥat ʿalayka bi-nātiqin midhkāri

Translation:

“They were not deprived of good nourishment, and their mother surpassed you with a prolific womb that bore sons.”

The poet praises a group of men and their family. In this construction, nātiq is understood as a prolific womb, consistent with the translation above; in broader Arabic usage, it also describes a woman who bears many children. Midhkār concerns bearing sons frequently. The word’s referent in the verse should be distinguished from its general use.

A lexical explanation relates this use to bringing or releasing something from within.

This does not imply that Arabic always imagines childbirth as forceful uprooting. The use concerns prolific childbearing; violent movement or difficult delivery is not an essential part of its meaning.

The verse is attributed to Al-Nābighah. Some versions have دَحَقَتْ instead of طَفَحَتْ. The variant does not fundamentally alter nātiq’s relationship to prolific childbearing, but the chosen wording should remain clear. Attribution alone should not be treated as establishing the exact date of composition.

Another poetic witness: jolting the rider

Al-ʿAjjāj, a poet of the early Islamic period, describes the movement of mounts:

يَنْتُقْنَ بِالْقَوْمِ مِنَ التَّزَعُّلِ
مَيْسَ عُمَانَ وَرِحَالَ الْإِسْحِلِ

yantuqna bi-al-qawmi mina al-tazaʿʿuli / maysa ʿumāna wa-riḥāla al-isḥili

Sense-based translation:

“In their lively movement, the mounts jolt the riders and the saddles made of Oman’s mays wood and isḥil wood.”

يَنْتُقْنَ concerns jolting the riders and saddles rather than uprooting something from the ground. Mays and isḥil are tree names whose wood was used for saddles; Oman specifies the mays in the verse. The second line therefore names the material of the equipment, rather than merely repeating a general reference to a load.

This helps establish Arabic usage, but Al-ʿAjjāj belongs to the Islamic period; his verse should not be presented as certain evidence of pre-Islamic poetry.

Together with the bucket, waterskin, and bag examples, these witnesses connect N T Q with pulling, shaking, and displacement. Uprooting is a prominent instance, but not its entire meaning.

3. Qur’anic usage: Al-Aʿrāf 7:171

وَإِذْ نَتَقْنَا الْجَبَلَ فَوْقَهُمْ كَأَنَّهُ ظُلَّةٌ وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُ وَاقِعٌ بِهِمْ خُذُوا مَا آتَيْنَاكُمْ بِقُوَّةٍ وَاذْكُرُوا مَا فِيهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ

wa-idh nataqnā al-jabala fawqahum ka-annahu ẓullatun wa-ẓannū annahu wāqiʿun bihim khudhū mā ātaynākum bi-quwwatin wa-udhkurū mā fīhi laʿallakum tattaqūna

Translation:

“When We uprooted the mountain above them as though it were a canopy, and they believed it would fall upon them: Hold firmly to what We have given you, and remember what it contains, so that you may be mindful.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:171

This is the only verse containing a verb from N T Q. Unlike everyday uses concerning containers, loads, or mounts, its object is الْجَبَلَ, the mountain.

The meaning of نَتَقْنَا الْجَبَلَ

The action in نَتَقْنَا الْجَبَلَ is attributed to The God, with the mountain as its direct object.

The root’s semantic field points to moving and uprooting the mountain. To establish the result of that movement, however, the continuation matters:

فَوْقَهُمْ

fawqahum

Meaning: “above them.”

This locative expression describes the mountain’s position. The complete construction therefore concerns uprooting or displacing it so that it is above the group.

“We shook the mountain” alone is insufficient here, since it does not convey its position above them.

“We raised the mountain” clarifies the result, but conveys less of the uprooting and forceful displacement associated with N T Q.

“We uprooted the mountain” is closer to the root’s semantic range, yet should not become a pretext for adding unstated details about its detachment from the ground.

The verse does not specify the depth from which it was detached, whether some earth moved with it, or which natural or physical force was involved.

It reports moving the mountain and its position above the group, rather than a mechanical explanation.

كَأَنَّهُ ظُلَّةٌ: like a canopy above them

After describing its position, the verse says:

كَأَنَّهُ ظُلَّةٌ

ka-annahu ẓullatun

Meaning: “as though it were a canopy.”

Ẓullah belongs to Ẓ L L and names something standing above another thing and casting shade over it.

The mountain is compared to a canopy, clarifying its position above them.

This does not mean it actually became a canopy, cloud, or lightweight structure. Kaʾannahu expresses resemblance rather than a change into something of identical nature.

The canopy here does not necessarily suggest comfort or protection, since the next clause shows their fear that the mountain would fall.

Ẓullah concerns its overhead position. Kindness and threat are not independent lexical meanings of that word.

وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُ وَاقِعٌ بِهِمْ: expecting the mountain to fall

The verse continues:

وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُ وَاقِعٌ بِهِمْ

wa-ẓannū annahu wāqiʿun bihim

Meaning: “They believed it would fall upon them.”

Ẓannū reports their perception or belief. Arabic ẓann can involve different degrees of confidence and does not always mean a weak guess.

Here they viewed the mountain as about to fall upon them. Ẓann can be understood as strong conviction or certainty about its falling, rather than merely a weak possibility.

The verse nevertheless does not say that it actually fell upon them. Its overhead position should be distinguished from their belief about its falling.

Nor does this phrase establish that its fall had been irrevocably decreed from the outset. The verse reports their perception and then the command addressed to them.

Fear of its falling belongs to the event’s context; frightening or threatening is not the lexical meaning of N T Q.

This prevents نَتَقْنَا from being translated directly as “We threatened them.”

4. Connections with other Qur’anic accounts

Other verses concerning raising the Mount above the Children of Israel help clarify the event.

Al-Baqarah 2:63: raising the Mount and taking the covenant

وَإِذْ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَكُمْ وَرَفَعْنَا فَوْقَكُمُ الطُّورَ خُذُوا مَا آتَيْنَاكُمْ بِقُوَّةٍ وَاذْكُرُوا مَا فِيهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ

wa-idh akhadhnā mīthāqakum wa-rafaʿnā fawqakumu al-ṭūra khudhū mā ātaynākum bi-quwwatin wa-udhkurū mā fīhi laʿallakum tattaqūna

Translation:

“When We took your covenant and raised the Mount above you: Hold firmly to what We have given you, and remember what it contains, so that you may be mindful.”

Al-Baqarah 2:63

This verse uses رَفَعْنَا rather than نَتَقْنَا.

R F ʿ concerns raising, while N T Q, in its lexical uses, also conveys movement involving pulling, shaking, or uprooting.

Al-Baqarah states the result: the mountain being above them. Al-Aʿrāf’s different wording gives greater prominence to moving and uprooting it.

This does not establish two separate stages, as though the mountain first shook and was later raised in another stage.

The two verses may describe one event from different linguistic perspectives.

The covenant is also important. Al-Baqarah 2:63 explicitly mentions taking it, helping clarify the setting in Al-Aʿrāf.

Both verses contain خُذُوا مَا آتَيْنَاكُمْ بِقُوَّةٍ وَاذْكُرُوا مَا فِيهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ. The shared command points to a common setting of receiving the Book and responsibility toward it.

Al-Baqarah 2:93: the covenant and disobedience

وَإِذْ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَكُمْ وَرَفَعْنَا فَوْقَكُمُ الطُّورَ خُذُوا مَا آتَيْنَاكُمْ بِقُوَّةٍ وَاسْمَعُوا ۖ قَالُوا سَمِعْنَا وَعَصَيْنَا

wa-idh akhadhnā mīthāqakum wa-rafaʿnā fawqakumu al-ṭūra khudhū mā ātaynākum bi-quwwatin wa-ismaʿū qālū samiʿnā wa-ʿaṣaynā

Translation:

“When We took your covenant and raised the Mount above you: Hold firmly to what We have given you, and listen. They said: We have heard and disobeyed.”

Al-Baqarah 2:93, opening part of the verse

This recalls the covenant and raising the mountain, while emphasizing the response of disobedience.

An important point follows: confronting a great sign, even with fear of its consequences, does not necessarily ensure lasting commitment to a covenant.

That conclusion comes from the verses together, rather than belonging within the definition of N T Q.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:154: the mountain’s explicit connection with the covenant

وَرَفَعْنَا فَوْقَهُمُ الطُّورَ بِمِيثَاقِهِمْ

wa-rafaʿnā fawqahumu al-ṭūra bi-mīthāqihim

Translation:

“We raised the Mount above them on account of their covenant.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:154, opening part of the verse

This explicitly links raising the Mount with the covenant.

Read with the two Al-Baqarah verses, it identifies Al-Aʿrāf 7:171 as an event concerning the Children of Israel and their covenant.

The connection can be established within the Qur’an, without requiring outside accounts to identify the basic subject.

Are nataqnā and rafaʿnā identical?

Both verbs concern raising the mountain in these accounts, but their semantic fields are not identical.

Rafaʿa can describe lifting something, elevating a position, or raising a rank.

Nataqa is more closely associated in attested usage with pulling, shaking, and uprooting.

Rafaʿnā in the parallel verses helps explain the result of the movement in Al-Aʿrāf 7:171 without reducing N T Q to simple raising.

Conversely, imagining the mountain’s movement should not add meanings such as splitting the earth’s crust, causing an earthquake, or hurling a rocky mass. None is necessary to the verb’s meaning here.

5. Distinguishing the Qur’anic account from interpretive explanations

Was the mountain moved, or is this only a symbolic image?

Al-Aʿrāf 7:171 attributes the verb to The God, makes the mountain its object, locates it above the group, and reports their belief that it would fall.

The parallel verses also say وَرَفَعْنَا فَوْقَكُمُ الطُّورَ.

Together these expressions clearly support a direct reading involving the mountain’s actual displacement.

Turning the account into an entirely symbolic image—for example, taking the mountain as the burden of commandments and its raising as psychological pressure—requires independent evidence. The extraordinary nature of the event alone is not such evidence.

Accepting the direct meaning likewise does not justify inventing physical or historical details absent from the Qur’an.

The verse alone cannot establish the displaced portion’s size, its height, how long it remained above the people, or how it returned to its previous location.

Nor does the verse say that angels lifted it or that a particular group prostrated beneath it in a specified manner. Such details occur in some interpretive accounts, but should not be equated with the verse itself.

The appropriate approach is to preserve the direct image without claiming certainty about its unstated manner.

Does N T Q mean coercing people into faith?

The mountain’s overhead position and their fear of its falling introduce pressure and fear into the scene.

The verb itself does not lexically mean compelling, believing, or entering a covenant.

The event’s connection with the covenant and divine command emerges from Al-Baqarah, Al-Nisāʾ, and Al-Aʿrāf together.

Three matters should therefore remain distinct: moving the mountain, the people’s response, and the command to hold to what they had received.

خُذُوا مَا آتَيْنَاكُمْ بِقُوَّةٍ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:171 concerns taking what they received seriously and holding firmly to it. This strength belongs to a separate command, rather than defining nataqa.

The wider relationship between fear, covenant, faith, and human responsibility can be studied independently. A root article should not change the word’s meaning to settle that question or reach a predetermined theological conclusion.

Distinguishing the mountain event in Al-Aʿrāf 7:143

Al-Aʿrāf 7:143 also mentions a mountain:

فَلَمَّا تَجَلَّىٰ رَبُّهُ لِلْجَبَلِ جَعَلَهُ دَكًّا وَخَرَّ مُوسَىٰ صَعِقًا

fa-lammā tajallā rabbuhu li-al-jabali jaʿalahu dakkan wa-kharra mūsā ṣaʿiqan

Translation:

“When his Lord manifested Himself to the mountain, He reduced it to rubble, and Moses fell unconscious.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:143, part of the verse

This is a different event from Al-Aʿrāf 7:171.

Verse 143 concerns divine manifestation and Moses’ request; verse 171 places the mountain above a group in connection with the covenant and the command to receive the Book.

Nataqnā should therefore not be replaced with جَعَلَهُ دَكًّا, nor the two events merged merely because both involve a mountain.

Study of D K K

6. Comparison with related roots

N T Q (نَتَقَ) and R F ʿ (رَفَعَ): uprooting and raising

R F ʿ provides the closest Qur’anic comparison.

It concerns raising or lifting in both physical and nonphysical settings.

Familiar uses of N T Q emphasize movement involving pulling, shaking, and uprooting.

In the mountain account, both roots relate to the same result: its position above the group.

Their difference of emphasis is that N T Q evokes the manner of displacement more strongly, while R F ʿ emphasizes rising or raising.

This is not an absolute rule that every raising is effortless and every natq violent.

Study of R F ʿ

N T Q (نَتَقَ) and Q L ʿ (قَلَعَ): uprooting and detaching

Arabic Q L ʿ concerns uprooting, detaching, or removing something from its place of attachment or settlement.

This overlaps with one use of N T Q.

N T Q nevertheless extends beyond detachment to shaking a container, drawing a bucket, and a mount’s vigorous movement.

In the Qur’an, its object is the mountain, with the resulting position specified by فَوْقَهُمْ.

“Uprooting” is therefore defensible here, but Q L ʿ cannot replace every Arabic use of N T Q.

N T Q (نَتَقَ) and H Z Z (هَزَّ): shaking and moving

H Z Z concerns moving and shaking.

The Qur’an addresses Mary:

وَهُزِّي إِلَيْكِ بِجِذْعِ النَّخْلَةِ

wa-huzzī ilayki bi-jidhʿi al-nakhlati

Translation:

“Shake the trunk of the palm tree toward yourself.”

Maryam 19:25, part of the verse

Moving and shaking are prominent here.

N T Q can also convey shaking, but its lexical uses include pulling and uprooting.

Hazz and natq overlap in movement and shaking without being identical.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:171, “We shook the mountain” does not convey the complete construction, since the movement results in its being above the group.

N T Q (نَتَقَ) and N T F (نَتَفَ): uprooting and plucking small parts

These roots resemble one another in writing and some meanings, but differ in use.

N T F concerns plucking hair, feathers, or other parts from their attachment.

The examined uses of N T Q concern pulling, shaking, and sometimes uprooting an object.

Plucking hair can be called natf; shaking a bag to empty it is a use of natq.

Their overlap in detachment does not establish that one derives from the other, or justify ignoring their different uses.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The root’s general Arabic meanings should be distinguished from its particular Qur’anic use.

According to object and context, nataqa can be rendered “pulled,” “shook,” “detached,” or “uprooted.”

نَتَقَ السِّقَاءَ can be explained as “he shook the waterskin to bring out its contents.”

For نَتَقَ الْغَرْبَ مِنَ الْبِئْرِ, a natural equivalent is “he pulled the large bucket up from the well.”

For nātiq describing a mount, “jolting” or “a mount that jolts its rider” is appropriate. For a prolific woman, “prolific” or “bearing many children” suits the context; ordinary bodily movement does not adequately express that use.

Three principal options arise in Al-Aʿrāf 7:171:

RenderingAssessment
We shook the mountainConveys part of the semantic field, but does not adequately express the resulting position
We raised the mountainAgrees with the parallel verses, but conveys less of the uprooting
We uprooted the mountainClose to the lexical meaning and consistent with its position in the sentence

For نَتَقْنَا الْجَبَلَ فَوْقَهُمْ, “We uprooted the mountain above them” is a suitable rendering.

“Uprooted” preserves displacement and detachment, while “above them” states its resulting position.

It should not be expanded into “We detached it from the earth’s roots,” “We hurled it into the air through an earthquake,” or “We brought it crashing down upon them.” Such details are not explicit in the Arabic.

Their fear that it would fall should likewise remain part of the contextual explanation, rather than the verb’s meaning.

8. Conclusion

N T Q concerns pulling, shaking, uprooting, and displacing something. The movement can involve shaking, pulling, or detachment; its exact meaning depends on the particular use.

Arabic attests varied settings: drawing a bucket from a well, shaking waterskins and bags, a mount’s vigorous movement, and describing prolific childbearing.

The verse attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī illustrates nātiq in connection with childbearing. Poetry from the Islamic period also confirms its use for jolting riders and equipment; the two witnesses should remain distinct in their attribution and dating.

The sole Qur’anic form is نَتَقْنَا in Al-Aʿrāf 7:171, concerning a mountain above the Children of Israel which they believed would fall upon them.

Al-Baqarah 2:63, Al-Baqarah 2:93, and Al-Nisāʾ 4:154 use رَفَعْنَا for the same subject and clarify its relationship to their covenant.

The comparison shows N T Q expressing uprooting and raising the mountain here. Its physical mechanism, size, and other unstated details cannot be extracted from the root.

Nor should its lexical meaning be equated with frightening, compelling, or taking a covenant. These belong to the account’s context rather than the root’s independent definition.

Proposed final definition for Forqan:

N T Q denotes pulling, shaking, and displacing something from its previous state or location, sometimes conveying detachment and uprooting. The movement can involve perceptible force or shaking, but its manner depends on use and context.

In the Qur’an, نَتَقْنَا الْجَبَلَ فَوْقَهُمْ means “We uprooted the mountain above them.” This agrees with the parallel verses about raising the Mount, without adding unstated details of how it happened.
