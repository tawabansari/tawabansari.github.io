The Arabic root «ح ن ج ر» (Ḥ N J R) concerns the region of the throat associated with breathing and producing sound. «حَنْجَرَة» (ḥanjarah) names a part of the throat; «حُنْجُور» (ḥunjūr) denotes the throat in early usage; and «حَنَاجِر» (ḥanājir) is the plural of ḥanjarah.

Early dictionaries explain ḥanjarah through «حُلْقُوم» (ḥulqūm) and «غَلْصَمَة» (ghalṣamah). Some identify it with an upper or prominent part of the airway; others describe an inner part of that passage.

These explanations show that, before modern anatomical terminology developed, ḥanjarah already named a recognizable part of the throat in Arabic.

Within the same lexical family, «حَنْجَرَهُ» (ḥanjarahu) also means cutting someone’s throat or slaughtering. Another expression, «حَنْجَرَتْ عَيْنُهُ» (ḥanjarat ʿaynuhu), describes an eye becoming sunken; «حُنْجُورَة» (ḥunjūrah) is also recorded for a glass vessel. The historical connections among all these usages are not securely established.

The root Ḥ N J R occurs twice in the Qur’an, both times as the plural «الْحَنَاجِر» (al-ḥanājir):

  • Al-Aḥzāb 33:10, portraying intense fear when confronting attacking groups.
  • Ghāfir 40:18, describing the approaching Day and people’s dread of the outcome of their deeds.

In both verses, ḥanājir appears alongside «قُلُوب» (qulūb), hearts.

In Al-Aḥzāb, the hearts reach the throats.

In Ghāfir, the hearts are at the throats.

Both expressions convey intense fear and inner distress through an image drawn from the human body.

This image need not mean that the anatomical heart necessarily leaves its normal position in the chest and enters the larynx. Ordinary language can describe extreme fear, anxiety, and distress through the image of the heart rising into the throat.

A distinction is therefore needed between the bodily meaning of ḥanjarah and its rhetorical use in portraying extreme dread.

1. Morphological structure and derivatives

Ḥ N J R is a quadriliteral root consisting of ḥāʾ, nūn, jīm, and rāʾ.

Unlike triliteral Arabic roots, this family has four root consonants. The Qur’anic morphological index also records it as a quadriliteral root.

Its principal recorded forms include:

  • حَنْجَرَة (ḥanjarah): the laryngeal region, a part of the throat associated with breathing and voice.
  • حَنَاجِر (ḥanājir): the plural of ḥanjarah; throats or laryngeal regions.
  • حَنْجَر (ḥanjar): another plural of ḥanjarah; also, in a separate use, a place name.
  • حُنْجُور (ḥunjūr): the throat.
  • حُنْجُورَة (ḥunjūrah): in early lexical usage, a glass vessel for aromatic substances.
  • حَنْجَرَ ـ يُحَنْجِرُ (ḥanjara–yuḥanjiru): transitively, to cut someone’s or an animal’s throat; to slaughter.
  • حَنْجَرَتْ عَيْنُهُ (ḥanjarat ʿaynuhu): his eye became sunken.
  • مُحَنْجِر (muḥanjir): an early name for an abdominal ailment or pain; also a description of someone suffering from it.
  • حَنْجَرَةُ الصَّوْت (ḥanjarat al-ṣawt): in modern anatomical and linguistic usage, the organ associated with voice production.
  • حَنْجَرِيّ (ḥanjarī): laryngeal; related to the larynx, including modern terminology concerning voice and phonetics.

Some of these forms remain in current use; others are recorded only in early dictionaries.

For example, in modern linguistics, ḥanjarī can describe sounds produced in the laryngeal region.

This modern technical use should not be treated as the original meaning of every derivative.

Only «الْحَنَاجِرَ» (al-ḥanājira) and «الْحَنَاجِرِ» (al-ḥanājiri) occur in the Qur’an.

Morphologically, these are the same noun. Their difference concerns syntactic function and case ending.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:10, al-ḥanājira is accusative.

In Ghāfir 40:18, al-ḥanājiri is genitive.

The two Qur’anic forms are therefore not independent derivatives: both are plurals of ḥanjarah.

No independent verb from Ḥ N J R occurs in the Qur’an.

2. The meaning of ḥanjarah in early Arabic

Ḥanjarah: a part of the throat

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْحَنْجَرَةُ طَبَقَانِ مِنْ أَطْبَاقِ الْحُلْقُومِ مِمَّا يَلِي الْغَلْصَمَةَ

al-ḥanjaratu ṭabaqāni min aṭbāqi al-ḥulqūmi mimmā yalī al-ghalṣamata

That is: “The ḥanjarah comprises two layers among the layers of the ḥulqūm adjoining the ghalṣamah.”

It then transmits another explanation identifying the ḥanjarah with the upper part of the ghalṣamah.

A third explanation identifies it as the inner part of the ḥulqūm.

These differences show that early lexicographers did not offer a completely uniform account of the word’s precise anatomical boundaries.

They nevertheless share a central point: the ḥanjarah belongs to the throat and the passage associated with breathing.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Ḥ N J R: definitions and lexical forms

In modern usage, the larynx is an organ in the neck, situated along the airway and containing the vocal cords.

It contributes to voice production, airway protection during swallowing, and the passage of air.

National Cancer Institute dictionary: modern anatomical definition

The details of modern anatomy should not all be presented as the established meaning of early Arabic.

Early lexicographers described parts of the throat through observation and the terminology available in their own periods.

For Qur’anic lexical study, “the laryngeal region and throat” is therefore an appropriate explanation. More precise medical identification is a separate matter.

Ḥunjūr: the throat

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْحُنْجُورُ: الْحَلْقُ

al-ḥunjūru: al-ḥalqu

That is: “Ḥunjūr means the throat.”

This definition shows that ḥunjūr could have a broader meaning in early Arabic than a narrowly delimited anatomical structure.

«حَلْق» (ḥalq) likewise concerns the throat region.

Ḥunjūr and ḥanjarah are therefore close in bodily location.

They should not, however, always be imagined as technical terms with precisely identical anatomical boundaries.

For example, ḥalq may refer to the throat as a whole, whereas a more specific account treats ḥanjarah as a part of that region.

This matters in Qur’anic translation: ḥanājir can be rendered “throats” without turning the translation into the name of a narrowly defined medical structure.

The laryngeal region, breathing, and voice

Lexical sources associate the ḥanjarah with the passage of breath.

Those sources distinguish ḥunjūr and ḥulqūm from the passage for food and drink.

Al-Azharī explains that the passage for food and drink is the «مَرِيء» (marīʾ), rather than the airway.

This distinction is also intelligible anatomically.

In modern anatomy, the routes for air and food meet in the pharyngeal region but continue along different passages.

The larynx lies above the trachea and is directly associated with the vocal cords and voice production.

Modern language accordingly speaks of laryngeal voice production, hoarseness, and inflammation of the larynx.

These usages should not be imposed on every Qur’anic occurrence.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 and Ghāfir 40:18 do not discuss voice production or laryngeal disease.

The throat forms part of their portrayal of intense fear.

Ḥanjarahu: cutting the throat

Lexical sources record «حَنْجَرَ الرَّجُلَ» (ḥanjara al-rajula) for cutting a man’s throat or slaughtering him.

This usage relates to the bodily location of the ḥanjarah.

Just as «حَلْقَمَهُ» (ḥalqamahu) concerns cutting the ḥulqūm, ḥanjarahu describes an action at the throat.

The two verbs nevertheless do not have the same root.

Ḥanjara belongs to Ḥ N J R.

Ḥalqama is studied under Ḥ L Q M, although there is disagreement about whether its mīm is original or additional.

Neither verb occurs in the Qur’an.

This lexical usage alone also establishes no religious ruling about slaughter, lawful food, or an authorized method of killing an animal.

Those questions require independent study of the relevant verses.

Ḥanjarat ʿaynuhu: an eye becoming sunken

Lisān al-ʿArab records ḥanjarat ʿaynuhu for an eye becoming sunken.

At first sight, this usage appears distant from the meaning of ḥanjarah.

Its subject is no longer the throat or airway, but a change in the eye’s condition.

Shared consonants alone cannot establish that this meaning necessarily developed from the shape of the larynx.

The available sources provide no clear historical account securely establishing its origin.

It should therefore be recognized as a recorded use within the lexical family without inventing an etymological story for it.

This meaning does not occur in the Qur’an.

Although eyes appear alongside hearts and throats in Al-Aḥzāb 33:10, the verb concerning the eyes is «زَاغَتْ» (zāghat), not «حَنْجَرَتْ» (ḥanjarat).

The meaning of sunken eyes should therefore not be transferred to zāghat or to ḥanājir itself.

Ḥunjūrah: a glass vessel

In a report from Ibn al-Aʿrābī, ḥunjūrah denotes a glass vessel used for aromatic substances.

Another explanation describes a tall, narrow vessel.

A resemblance in shape between a narrow vessel and the throat passage is conceivable.

That resemblance alone, however, does not establish the word’s historical origin.

The glass-vessel usage should therefore be recorded separately.

In the Qur’an, ḥanājir does not mean vessels or glass containers.

In both verses, the presence of qulūb, hearts, makes the reference to the human body clear.

3. Evidence from early Arabic poetry

A witness concerning the throat and eating

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits this verse:

مُنِعَتْ تَمِيمٌ وَاللَّهَازِمُ كُلُّهَا
تَمْرَ الْعِرَاقِ وَمَا يَلَذُّ الْحَنْجَرُ

muniʿat tamīmun wa-al-lahāzimu kulluhā / tamra al-ʿirāqi wa-mā yaladhdhu al-ḥanjaru

That is: “Tamīm and all the Lahāzim were denied the dates of Iraq and what pleases the throat.”

Here, al-ḥanjar refers to the throat.

«يَلَذُّ» (yaladhdhu) concerns pleasure or pleasantness.

The expression therefore relates to food and its enjoyment.

The witness shows that ḥanjar could refer to the region associated with eating or its experience, even though more precise anatomical explanations distinguish the airway from the food passage.

Literary transmission preserves different versions of this verse.

Khizānat al-Adab gives a related wording:

مَنَعَتْ حَنِيفَةُ وَاللَّهَازِمُ مِنْكُمُ
قِشْرَ الْعِرَاقِ وَمَا يَلَذُّ الْحَنْجَرُ

manaʿat ḥanīfatu wa-al-lahāzimu minkumu / qishra al-ʿirāqi wa-mā yaladhdhu al-ḥanjaru

That is: “Ḥanīfah and the Lahāzim withheld from you the qishr of Iraq and what pleases the throat.” The transmitted qishr wording differs from the explicit dates in the other version.

The difference shows variation in the names of the groups and in some of the verse’s wording.

The verse should therefore not be attributed to a particular poet without acknowledging the variation, nor confidently presented as a securely dated pre-Qur’anic witness.

Khizānat al-Adab: alternative transmission of the poetic witness

In both versions, however, al-ḥanjar clearly denotes the throat.

A witness attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

Lisān al-ʿArab attributes a verse to Al-Nābighah:

مِنَ الْوَارِدَاتِ الْمَاءَ بِالْقَاعِ تَسْتَقِي
بِأَعْجَازِهَا قَبْلَ اسْتِقَاءِ الْحَنَاجِرِ

mina al-wāridāti al-māʾa bi-al-qāʿi tastaqī / bi-aʿjāzihā qabla istiqāʾi al-ḥanājiri

That is: “Among those palms that reach water in the low ground, drawing it through their lower parts and roots before their upper parts receive it.”

In Ibn Manẓūr’s explanation, ḥanājir is applied to palm trees by analogy.

The poet likens parts of a palm to the body of a living creature.

Just as water passes through a creature’s throat, the tree takes water through its lower parts and conveys it elsewhere.

The witness matters because ḥanājir can move from reference to human or animal anatomy into figurative description of a plant.

One important qualification is necessary.

Some editions of the collected poetry attributed to Al-Nābighah end the line with «الْخَنَاجِرِ» (al-khanājir), with khāʾ, rather than «الْحَنَاجِرِ» (al-ḥanājir), with ḥāʾ.

In the two texts examined, the clear difference relevant here is al-ḥanājir with ḥāʾ in Lisān al-ʿArab versus al-khanājir with khāʾ in the Dīwān. A claim about a further variant in the first part of the line requires independent manuscript evidence.

The lexical tradition thus treats the verse as evidence for ḥanājir, but its wording is not uniform across poetic editions.

It cannot therefore be used without qualification as independent, conclusive pre-Qur’anic evidence for this exact word form.

Dīwān of Al-Nābighah: the al-khanājir reading with khāʾ

Despite this limitation, the dictionaries’ figurative application to a plant is clear: literary comparison can liken parts of a plant to the throat of a living creature.

The boundary between poetic use and Qur’anic meaning

Early Arabic poetry can show the situations in which a word was used.

A single poetic verse cannot, however, determine every Qur’anic use.

The verse attributed to Al-Nābighah places ḥanājir within an image concerning palms.

In Al-Aḥzāb and Ghāfir, ḥanājir appears alongside qulūb.

The reference to the human throat is consequently clearer in the Qur’an.

The sentence structure also shows that this bodily region participates in a description of intense fear.

Thus, the lexical meaning “throats” remains, while the complete image is understood through its relation to the hearts and the people’s circumstances.

4. Al-ḥanājira in Al-Aḥzāb 33:10

Intense dread during the siege

Verse text:

إِذْ جَاءُوكُمْ مِنْ فَوْقِكُمْ وَمِنْ أَسْفَلَ مِنْكُمْ وَإِذْ زَاغَتِ الْأَبْصَارُ وَبَلَغَتِ الْقُلُوبُ الْحَنَاجِرَ وَتَظُنُّونَ بِاللَّهِ الظُّنُونَا

idh jāʾūkum min fawqikum wa-min asfala minkum wa-idh zāghati al-abṣāru wa-balaghat al-qulūbu al-ḥanājira wa-taẓunnūna bi-Allāhi al-ẓunūnā

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“When they came upon you from above you and from below you; when eyes became fixed in alarm and hearts reached the throats, and you entertained thoughts about The God.”

Al-ḥanājira is accusative here.

«بَلَغَتْ» (balaghat), from B L GH, means reaching.

«الْقُلُوبُ» (al-qulūbu), the hearts, is the verb’s subject.

Al-ḥanājira names the destination or place reached by the hearts within the verse’s image.

The phrase is therefore structured as follows:

وَبَلَغَتِ الْقُلُوبُ الْحَنَاجِرَ

wa-balaghati al-qulūbu al-ḥanājira

That is: “And the hearts reached the throats.”

The sentence must nevertheless be read in its full context.

The verse describes opposing groups approaching from above and below.

It then mentions eyes departing from their ordinary condition.

The hearts subsequently reach the throats, and people entertain various thoughts about The God.

Together, these details portray intense fear and distress in a dangerous situation.

Have the hearts actually left the chest?

Early commentaries explain the expression “the hearts reached the throats” in relation to intense fear.

Al-Ṭabarī speaks of hearts rising from their places and reaching the throats through fear. The wording “as if” and the preference for a rhetorical reading in this article are this study’s analysis; they should not be presented as Al-Ṭabarī’s exact words or explicit position.

Al-Ṭabarī on Al-Aḥzāb 33:10: the commentator’s account

In human experience, extreme fear can accompany a racing heart, rapid breathing, throat tightness, and difficulty speaking.

NHS: bodily symptoms of panic

Ordinary language can likewise say: “My heart rose into my throat.”

That expression should not be turned into the anatomical conclusion that the heart has actually left the chest and entered the airway.

The verse does not explain such a medical process.

The bodily image selected should not be removed either.

The Qur’an itself uses the heart and throat to make the severity of dread tangible.

“The hearts reached the throats” is therefore suitable in direct translation; the explanation of extreme fear belongs in the verse’s reflection.

The relation between zāghat al-abṣāru and balaghat al-qulūbu al-ḥanājira

The verse places two bodily descriptions alongside one another.

First:

زَاغَتِ الْأَبْصَارُ

zāghati al-abṣāru

That is: “The eyes deviated from their ordinary condition.”

Second:

بَلَغَتِ الْقُلُوبُ الْحَنَاجِرَ

balaghati al-qulūbu al-ḥanājira

That is: “The hearts reached the throats.”

Zāghat comes from Z Y GH. In this context, it concerns a change in the gaze and the eyes departing from their ordinary condition.

Balaghat comes from B L GH and expresses reaching.

Al-ḥanājir simply names the throats.

Fear, a fixed gaze, palpitations, and rapid breathing should therefore not all be included in the definition of ḥanjarah itself.

These notions arise from the relationship among several words and the dangerous situation described.

Wa-taẓunnūna bi-Allāhi al-ẓunūnā

After describing eyes and hearts, the verse mentions people’s thoughts about The God.

This sequence matters.

Intense distress and fear can affect a person’s judgment and expectations about the future.

The sentence does not, however, identify all those people’s thoughts as identical.

The following verses distinguish believers’ responses from the hypocrites’ words.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:12, a group calls the promise of The God and the Messenger a deception.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:22, believers seeing the allied forces affirm the truth of the promise of The God and the Messenger.

The fear described should therefore not lead to the conclusion that everyone present necessarily held the same belief or judgment about The God.

Throughout, ḥanājir continues to mean throats.

The image of hearts reaching the throats expresses dread; people’s responses in faith and conduct are understood from the continuing context.

Does the verse describe a condition exclusive to war?

The immediate setting of Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 is confrontation with attacking groups.

Its image of fear therefore concerns a military danger.

Linguistically, however, “hearts reaching the throats” is also intelligible as a description of other situations of extreme fear.

The root’s second occurrence, in Ghāfir 40:18, makes this clearer: there the image concerns the Day of Reckoning and people’s outcome.

The linguistic image is not confined to one historical battle, although Al-Aḥzāb applies it to a particular military situation.

5. Al-ḥanājiri in Ghāfir 40:18

Hearts at the throats

Verse text:

وَأَنْذِرْهُمْ يَوْمَ الْآزِفَةِ إِذِ الْقُلُوبُ لَدَى الْحَنَاجِرِ كَاظِمِينَ مَا لِلظَّالِمِينَ مِنْ حَمِيمٍ وَلَا شَفِيعٍ يُطَاعُ

wa-andhirhum yawma al-āzifati idhi al-qulūbu ladā al-ḥanājiri kāẓimīna mā li-al-ẓālimīna min ḥamīmin wa-lā shafīʿin yuṭāʿu

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Warn them of the approaching Day, when hearts are at the throats and they suppress their anguish. The wrongdoers have neither an intimate friend nor an intercessor whose plea is accepted.”

Al-ḥanājiri is genitive here because it is the genitive complement of the adverb «لَدَى» (ladā).

Ladā is an adverb in a construct phrase here, expressing nearness or presence beside something.

The phrase is therefore structured as follows:

إِذِ الْقُلُوبُ لَدَى الْحَنَاجِرِ

idhi al-qulūbu ladā al-ḥanājiri

That is: “When the hearts are at the throats.”

Its structure differs from Al-Aḥzāb 33:10.

Al-Aḥzāb uses the verb balaghat.

Ghāfir uses ladā to express location or proximity.

Both nevertheless share an image: hearts are described in relation to throats.

The setting: the approaching Day

The verse opens with «وَأَنْذِرْهُمْ» (wa-andhirhum), the command to warn them.

The warning concerns «يَوْمَ الْآزِفَةِ» (yawma al-āzifati).

Al-āzifah comes from ʾ Z F, concerning drawing near.

In the context of Ghāfir, this Day concerns reckoning, judgment, and the outcome of human deeds.

The image of hearts at the throats is therefore set within dread of the Day of Reckoning and the consequences of conduct.

Resurrection, reckoning, and punishment cannot, however, be derived from ḥanājir itself.

They are understood from other words in the verse and the surah’s context.

The role of kāẓimīn

«كَاظِمِينَ» (kāẓimīn) follows the phrase al-qulūbu ladā al-ḥanājiri.

It comes from K Ẓ M and concerns suppressing or keeping something within.

In Arabic, «كَظَمَ غَيْظَهُ» (kaẓama ghayẓahu) means restraining one’s anger.

Here, kāẓimīn concerns people’s inner distress and anguish.

The resulting image is of hearts seemingly rising to the throats through dread while people suppress their distress and anguish.

Kāẓimīn and al-ḥanājiri do not, however, share a root.

Ḥanājir identifies the throats.

Kāẓimīn describes the people’s condition.

Translation and analysis should preserve this difference.

Why is kāẓimīn a masculine plural?

Grammatically, al-qulūb is a non-rational plural.

Kāẓimīn, however, has the sound masculine plural ending.

Early grammatical explanations discuss this difference.

One established analysis treats kāẓimīn as a circumstantial expression describing the owners of the hearts, rather than a direct description of the hearts themselves.

In other words, the hearts are at the throats while their owners suppress anguish and distress.

Al-Zamakhsharī presents this analysis in Al-Kashshāf.

He also gives an alternative in which kāẓimīn refers to the hearts, through a rhetorical attribution to them of an action associated with rational beings.

The first analysis, referring to the hearts’ owners, nevertheless makes the connection clearer.

Al-Kashshāf: grammatical analyses of kāẓimīn

This grammatical discussion helps explain the sentence but should not be added to the brief meaning of Ḥ N J R.

Does suppressing anguish mean complete silence?

Some early commentaries explain kāẓimīn through silence or inability to speak.

This relates to the image of severe inner distress.

The root K Ẓ M nevertheless extends beyond silence: it can concern restraining anger, anguish, or inner pressure.

Kāẓimīn alone should therefore not establish that no person in that situation speaks in any way.

Other Qur’anic verses also address speech on the Day of Resurrection.

A full understanding of that subject requires considering the relevant verses together.

For the study of Ḥ N J R, the main point is that suppressing anguish accompanies the image of hearts at the throats.

Mā li-al-ẓālimīna min ḥamīmin wa-lā shafīʿin yuṭāʿu

The verse ends by denying the wrongdoers an intimate friend and an intercessor whose plea is accepted.

This sentence relates to the dread and helplessness portrayed at the beginning.

Ḥanājir does not thereby acquire the meaning of intercession.

The throats participate in the image of fear and distress.

The absence of a friend and an accepted intercessor is the separate subject of the following clause.

Intercession must therefore be examined through «شَفِيعٍ يُطَاعُ» (shafīʿin yuṭāʿu), the context of Ghāfir, and other relevant verses.

Ḥ N J R alone establishes no general ruling about the possibility or manner of intercession.

Does the image in Ghāfir describe the heart’s bodily displacement?

Al-Ṭabarī explains the expression by speaking of hearts rising from the chest and remaining at the throats. The wording “as if” and treatment of this as a rhetorical image are this study’s interpretive choice, rather than Al-Ṭabarī’s exact formulation.

Some transmitted explanations describe the hearts as neither returning to their places nor coming out.

Al-Ṭabarī on Ghāfir 40:18: hearts and throats

These explanations should not be turned without argument into conclusive anatomical reports.

Anatomically, the human heart is in the chest, whereas the larynx is in the neck.

Extreme fear can involve throat tightness and changes in heartbeat; this does not mean that the heart actually moves into the larynx.

This study reads the bodily image as a portrayal of intense dread and helplessness.

It favors a rhetorical reading while preserving the bodily meaning of ḥanjarah itself. Nevertheless, the anatomy of earthly bodies alone does not exclude every interpretation of an afterlife scene; this root’s meaning also cannot determine the details of unseen events.

6. Comparison with related roots and words

Ḥ N J R and Ḥ L Q M: ḥanjarah and ḥulqūm

Study of Ḥ L Q M

In Arabic, ḥulqūm concerns the throat and airway.

Ḥanjarah likewise names a part of the throat associated with breathing and voice.

Early sources explain the two words in closely related terms.

Their root structures nevertheless differ.

Ḥ N J R is quadriliteral.

The analysis of ḥulqūm as having a triliteral or quadriliteral root is disputed.

Their Qur’anic uses also differ.

«الْحُلْقُومَ» (al-ḥulqūma) appears in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 within a scene of approaching death.

Al-ḥanājir appears in Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 and Ghāfir 40:18 within portrayals of intense fear.

Their bodily proximity should therefore not lead to equating all their meanings and images.

Ḥ N J R and Ḥ L Q: laryngeal region and throat

Study of Ḥ L Q

Ḥalq is an Arabic name for the throat region.

Ḥanjarah is a part of that region or a structure associated with it.

Lexical explanations define ḥunjūr through ḥalq.

Ḥ L Q is nevertheless an independent family with other uses, including shaving hair.

The proximity of the larynx and throat does not mean that Qur’anic ḥanājir denotes shaving or cutting hair.

Ḥ N J R and Q L B: throat and heart

Study of Q L B

Qulūb accompanies both Qur’anic occurrences of ḥanājir.

«قَلْب» (qalb) comes from Q L B. Beyond the bodily organ, Qur’anic usage concerns understanding, belief, fear, and people’s inner states.

Ḥanājir comes from Ḥ N J R and denotes throats.

In “the hearts reached the throats,” the two words form a shared image.

Lexically, however, the heart and laryngeal region are not the same organ.

Intense fear is likewise understood from the whole image and its context, rather than from ḥanjarah itself.

Ḥ N J R and B L GH: the throat and reaching

Study of B L GH

Balaghat in Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 comes from B L GH.

The verb expresses reaching.

Al-ḥanājira identifies the place reached by the hearts within the verse’s image.

Rendering ḥanājir as “reaching the utmost fear” would therefore mix the verb’s meaning and contextual interpretation into the noun’s meaning.

An accurate translation should give balaghat and al-ḥanājira their separate roles.

Ḥ N J R and K Ẓ M: the throat and suppression

Kāẓimīn in Ghāfir 40:18 comes from K Ẓ M.

It concerns suppressing something and keeping it within.

Ḥanājir names the throats.

Both participate in the image of distress and anguish, but their roots and meanings differ.

Ḥanjarah should therefore not be translated as “suppressed anguish.”

Ḥ N J R and Z Y GH: the throat and altered gaze

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:10, zāghat al-abṣāru precedes balaghat al-qulūbu al-ḥanājira.

Zāghat concerns deviation or alteration in the gaze.

Ḥanājir denotes throats.

Both contribute to the portrayal of extreme fear, but they are distinct images.

Lexical sources also record ḥanjarat ʿaynuhu for an eye becoming sunken; this non-Qur’anic use should not be equated with zāghat.

Ḥ N J R and T R Q W: throat and collarbones

«التَّرَاقِيَ» (al-tarāqiya) appears in Al-Qiyāmah 75:26.

It is the plural of «تَرْقُوَة» (tarquwah), referring to the bones near the neck and upper chest.

In that verse, reaching the collarbones forms part of the image of approaching death.

The collarbone and larynx are nevertheless anatomically distinct.

The subject of Al-Qiyāmah 75:26 also differs from Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 and Ghāfir 40:18.

Although all these expressions concern structures near the neck, they should not be treated as complete synonyms.

Ḥ N J R and W R D: laryngeal region and neck vein

Qāf 50:16 contains «حَبْلِ الْوَرِيدِ» (ḥabli al-warīdi).

The expression concerns a vein in the neck.

The larynx is also in the neck, but is associated with breathing and voice.

The two structures are therefore not identical.

Qāf 50:16 concerns divine knowledge of and nearness to human beings, whereas the two ḥanājir verses describe intense fear.

Bodily structures’ proximity does not establish that the verses have the same meaning.

7. Questions of interpretation and semantic development

Is ḥanājir a name for fear in the Qur’an?

No.

Ḥanājir is the plural of ḥanjarah and denotes throats.

In both verses, however, it participates in an image of intense fear.

Fear is the image’s contextual significance, rather than the lexical meaning of ḥanjarah itself.

Direct translation should preferably retain “throats.”

The account of intense fear can appear in the verse’s reflection.

Does the heart actually move into the larynx?

In ordinary fear experienced by a living body, the heart does not actually rise out of the chest and enter the larynx. This anatomical point alone should not arbitrate every interpretation concerning the afterlife.

“The hearts reached the throats” should therefore not be presented as a medical account of the heart’s bodily movement.

The image itself nevertheless expresses an intelligible human experience.

Extreme fear may involve palpitations, throat tightness, breathlessness, and difficulty speaking.

By bringing hearts and throats together, the Qur’an portrays the severity of that condition.

None of these bodily symptoms, however, should be made a mandatory condition for the verse’s meaning to apply.

Is ḥanājir used only in the context of death?

No.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 concerns military confrontation.

Ghāfir 40:18 concerns the approaching Day and reckoning.

Both involve intense fear, but the word itself does not directly denote death.

The difference from ḥulqūm in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 matters: there, the throat belongs to an image of approaching death.

A bodily region can thus participate in different rhetorical situations.

Does ḥanjarah mean sound or shouting in the Qur’an?

The larynx is bodily associated with voice production.

Al-Aḥzāb and Ghāfir nevertheless do not describe shouting through the larynx.

In Ghāfir 40:18, kāẓimīn instead concerns suppression of anguish and distress.

Ḥanājir should therefore not be translated as “cries” or “voices” in these verses.

That would replace the bodily region’s name with one of its functions without the verses themselves making that substitution.

Does ḥanājir in Ghāfir mean actual suffocation?

Some translations explain “hearts at the throats” through an image of choking or arrested breath.

Such imagery can convey intense anxiety.

The verse itself, however, does not describe breathing stopping completely, an airway becoming blocked, or death by suffocation.

“Suffocation” should therefore not replace the direct meaning of ḥanājir without explanation.

“The hearts are at the throats” is closer to the Arabic structure.

Do the two verses describe exactly the same condition?

As linguistic images, both point to intense fear.

Their subjects nevertheless differ.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 concerns military threat and siege.

Ghāfir 40:18 warns of the Day of Reckoning and the outcome of deeds.

The two phrases are also structured differently.

In Al-Aḥzāb, hearts reach the throats.

In Ghāfir, hearts are at the throats.

The shared word ḥanājir should therefore not lead to equating every detail of the two scenes.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For al-ḥanājira in Al-Aḥzāb 33:10, the most suitable brief Persian equivalent is “throats”.

It clearly conveys the bodily region intended.

For a fuller rendering, the following phrase is suitable:

“And the hearts reached the throats.”

It preserves the Qur’anic image and conveys intense fear without replacing the wording with an interpretive explanation.

A contextual note can explain that the image portrays the dread of a siege.

“Throats” is also appropriate for al-ḥanājiri in Ghāfir 40:18.

The fuller phrase can be rendered:

“When the hearts are at the throats.”

The Persian equivalent “galūhā” is also intelligible and conveys the word’s general meaning.

For lexical study, “galūgāhhā” is more precise because it more clearly identifies the bodily region intended.

In this verse, suppressing anguish comes from kāẓimīn and should not be merged into the meaning of ḥanājir itself.

For the non-Qur’anic forms, the following equivalents are appropriate:

  • «حَنْجَرَة» (ḥanjarah): laryngeal region and throat.
  • «حُنْجُور» (ḥunjūr): throat.
  • «حَنْجَرَ» (ḥanjara): to cut someone’s or an animal’s throat.
  • «حَنْجَرَتْ عَيْنُهُ» (ḥanjarat ʿaynuhu): his eye became sunken.
  • «حُنْجُورَة» (ḥunjūrah): in early usage, a glass vessel for aromatic substances.

A Forqan word box should retain two separate fields.

Brief root meaning: “The laryngeal region and throat; the area associated with breathing and voice.”

Meaning in Al-Aḥzāb 33:10: “Throats; within the image of hearts reaching the throats through intense dread in a military situation.”

Meaning in Ghāfir 40:18: “Throats; within the image of hearts at the throats on the Day of Reckoning, accompanied by suppressed anguish and distress.”

Direct translation should preferably preserve the bodily meaning, leaving the rhetorical account of intense fear for a separate reflection on the verse.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ḥ N J R concerns the laryngeal region and throat.

Ḥanjarah names a part of the throat explained in early sources through ḥulqūm and ghalṣamah.

Ḥunjūr also denotes the throat.

The same family records cutting the throat, an eye becoming sunken, and a glass vessel’s name; the historical connections among all these meanings remain uncertain.

Poetic witnesses likewise connect ḥanjar and ḥanājir with the throat and figurative applications. Some verses’ wording and attribution, especially the one attributed to Al-Nābighah, vary; conclusions should remain proportionate to their evidential strength.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an, both times as the plural al-ḥanājir.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:10, the hearts reach the throats.

In Ghāfir 40:18, hearts are at the throats while people suppress their anguish and distress.

Both images concern intense fear.

One, however, belongs to military confrontation; the other warns of the Day of Reckoning.

Thus, ḥanājir directly means throats, whereas hearts reaching or being at them forms an image of intense dread and inner distress.

This distinction matters.

Translating ḥanājir directly as “fear” would remove the Qur’an’s bodily image.

Treating the image as an anatomical report about the ordinary human body goes beyond lexical analysis. This study’s preference for a rhetorical reading should likewise remain distinct from its reporting of other exegetical positions.

“Throats” is therefore the most suitable equivalent for Forqan’s translation.

The different structures should be retained: “hearts reached the throats” in Al-Aḥzāb, and “hearts are at the throats” in Ghāfir.

This approach preserves clear Arabic meaning while allowing the context of each verse to explain intense fear, the limits of human ability, and people’s responses to danger or reckoning.
