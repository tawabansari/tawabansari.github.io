Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root S W Y

Straightness, balance, proportioning, and uprightness without crookedness

Qur’anic forms of this root

The semantic geometry of س و ي (S W Y) rests on straightness, balance, evenness, moderation, and freedom from crookedness. This root must be kept separate from س و أ (S W ʾ): S W Y relates to proportioning, istiwā, sawā, balance, straightness, and harmony, while S W ʾ relates to evil, wrong, ugliness, and harm.

The central meaning of the root

The central meaning of س و ي (S W Y) can be stated this way: bringing something to a state of balance, straightness, proportion, freedom from crookedness, and readiness for its proper function.

In this meaning, سَوَّىٰ (sawwā) means to arrange something and bring it into proportion. اسْتَوَىٰ (istawā) means that something reaches firmness, settling, or balance. سَوَاء (sawāʾ) means that two sides or two states stand on one level, or that a path is in the middle and in balance. سَوِيّ (sawiyy) means something sound, upright, free of crookedness, and proportioned.

So this root is not only about numerical equality. Sometimes it speaks of the geometry of creation; sometimes of justice in judgment; sometimes of the straight path; sometimes of maturity and firmness; and sometimes of divine istiwā, which must be understood with full transcendence.

The sensory layer: making straight and free of crookedness

To understand this root, the image of smoothing and straightening is very important. Something crooked, unfinished, or uneven, when it undergoes taswiyah, reaches a state of balance and reliability. A crooked piece of wood, an uneven road, land with rises and dips, or an unfinished structure all need taswiyah in order to function correctly.

Arabic lexical explanations mention straightening a spear, piece of wood, or arrow and removing crookedness from it. This image shows that taswiyah is not merely surface smoothing; it is the removal of structural crookedness and knots so that the thing can perform its proper function.

In the Qur’an, this image comes close to the creation of the human being and the world. Creation is not only bringing something into existence. After creation, measure, proportion, firmness, and balance are also mentioned. This is why the Qur’an places خَلَقَ (khalaqa) together with فَسَوَّىٰ (fa-sawwā). Creation shows the original coming-into-being; taswiyah shows ordering, proportion, and readiness for role and capacity.

Sawwā: proportioning and bringing to harmony

سَوَّىٰ (sawwā) is one of the most important forms of this root. It means to arrange, proportion, straighten, measure, and bring something to a more complete state.

In Al-Aʿla 87:2, it says: الَّذِي خَلَقَ فَسَوَّىٰ (alladhī khalaqa fa-sawwā). God created, then proportioned. This combination shows that creation in the Qur’an is joined with order, measure, and harmony. The world and the human being are not left formless or without measure.

In Al-Infitar 82:7, it says: الَّذِي خَلَقَكَ فَسَوَّاكَ فَعَدَلَكَ. This verse links creation, taswiyah, and balance in the structure of the human being. The human being is created, then arranged, then placed in balanced composition.

In the verses about Adam, it is said: فَإِذَا سَوَّيْتُهُ وَنَفَخْتُ فِيهِ مِنْ رُوحِي. Here taswiyah shows the preparation of the human structure for a greater stage. The human being must reach a measure and firmness that can carry responsibility, awareness, and the trust of existence.

Therefore sawwā does not merely mean “created.” Taswiyah is a stage of ordering, proportion, firmness, and preparation. Translating it only as “created” loses part of the meaning.

Nusawwiya: precise and detailed restoration

In Al-Qiyamah 75:4, it says: بَلَىٰ قَادِرِينَ عَلَىٰ أَنْ نُسَوِّيَ بَنَانَهُ. God says that He is able even to proportion the fingertips of the human being.

This verse is very precise. It is not only speaking of recreating the human being in general; it points to detailed restoration. Banān, the fingers or fingertips, are among the most delicate structural signs of the human body. Taswiyah here means restoring exact proportion, form, and arrangement.

So S W Y here is connected with God’s power over measured and precise restoration, not only general resurrection.

Tusawwā: being leveled and made one with the earth

In An-Nisa 4:42, those who rejected and disobeyed the Messenger will wish on that Day: لَوْ تُسَوَّىٰ بِهِمُ الْأَرْضُ.

This means: if only the earth were leveled with them, or if only they became one with the earth. Here taswiyah carries a heavy meaning: the wish to disappear, be hidden, and become level with the soil before a truth that can no longer be denied.

The wish to be leveled with the earth is the height of fear before the exposure of inner crookedness. The one who did not set himself upon sawiyy in the world and built imbalance in being and action wishes, in the presence of the truth of Resurrection, that he could disappear into the undifferentiated evenness of the soil so that his imbalance would not be exposed.

This usage shows that taswiyah is not always positive. Sometimes being leveled means losing visible distinction and falling into a condition in which the human being wishes not to be seen.

Sāwā: making equal and balancing two sides

سَاوَىٰ (sāwā) appears in Al-Kahf 18:96, where Dhul-Qarnayn balances between two sides or mountain walls: حَتَّىٰ إِذَا سَاوَىٰ بَيْنَ الصَّدَفَيْنِ.

Here sāwā means to make equal, level, and establish balance between two sides. This use shows the concrete and structural side of the root. Something is placed between two sides and must become aligned and level with them so that the structure becomes complete.

This meaning fits the root’s central concept completely: from disharmony and gap toward balance, strength, and proportion.

Istawā: firmness, settling, maturity, and equality

اسْتَوَىٰ (istawā) is one of the broadest forms of this root. Depending on context, it may mean becoming equal, settling, becoming firm, reaching maturity, standing upright, and, in references to God, it must be understood with transcendence and without bodily comparison.

In Al-Qasas 28:14, regarding Moses, it says: وَلَمَّا بَلَغَ أَشُدَّهُ وَاسْتَوَىٰ. Here istawā means reaching firmness, maturity, and the stability of growth.

In Al-Fath 48:29, regarding a plant, it says: فَاسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِ. After growth and strengthening, the plant stands upright on its stalks. This image is very clear for understanding istiwā: something passes beyond weakness and dependence and stands upon its own structure.

In Hud 11:44, regarding Noah’s ark, it says: وَاسْتَوَتْ عَلَى الْجُودِيِّ. Here istiwā means the ark came to rest and settled upon Mount Judi. Thus istawā does not always mean rising upward; sometimes it means settling and becoming stable.

Istawā ilā al-samāʾ: turning to the ordering of heaven

In Al-Baqarah 2:29 and Fussilat 41:11, the expression اسْتَوَىٰ إِلَى السَّمَاءِ (istawā ilā al-samāʾ) appears. This expression concerns the creation of the heavens and shows that after a stage of creation, divine attention and governance relate to the ordering of the heavens.

Here istawā must not be understood as bodily movement. God does not move from place to place like created beings. The more fitting meaning in this context is a governing turning, attending to the ordering of heaven, and setting it within a measured system. The verse itself continues with فَسَوَّاهُنَّ سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ; that is, istiwā toward heaven is linked to taswiyah and ordering the heavens.

This use of the root shows the geometry of creation: turning toward a domain of creation and arranging it in measured forms.

Istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh: divine istiwā and the need for transcendence

The expression اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ (istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh) is one of the most sensitive uses of this root. Several times, after speaking of the creation of the heavens and earth, the Qur’an speaks of God’s istiwā over the Throne.

This expression must not be understood bodily. God is not a body, is not contained in place, does not sit upon something like created beings, and the quality of His essence and action is not measured by the quality of created beings. These verses must be understood with transcendence.

Within the Qur’anic frame, istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh may be understood as the establishment of sovereignty, encompassing governance, and divine lordship over the order of existence, without imagining its quality like the action of a creature. In the Qur’an, the Throne is connected with dominion, command, governance, and divine sovereignty. Divine istiwā over the Throne means that creation is not abandoned or unmanaged; everything is under God’s knowledge, judgment, power, and lordship.

Two errors must be avoided here: tashbīh, imagining bodily sitting or placement; and emptying the verse of meaning by ignoring its indication of lordship and governance. The sound path is transcendence together with Qur’anic understanding.

Yastawī / tastawī: being equal or not equal

The forms يَسْتَوِي (yastawī) and تَسْتَوِي (tastawī) appear many times in the Qur’an for comparing two matters. The Qur’an asks: Are the blind and the seeing equal? Are darknesses and light equal? Are the impure and the good equal? Are those who know and those who do not know equal? Are the people of the Fire and the people of Paradise equal?

This usage shows that S W Y is not only about outward equality; it is also about ethical and epistemic judgment. The Qur’an does not allow everything to be flattened into sameness. Qur’anic justice means that each thing is measured in its place, not that the difference between truth and falsehood, knowledge and ignorance, faith and corruption, good and impure, or light and darkness is ignored.

Therefore lā yastawī in the Qur’an is a foundational rule of judgment: unmeasured equalization is not justice. Sometimes justice means distinguishing between things that are truly not equal.

Sawāʾ: equality, middle, and the straight way

سَوَاء (sawāʾ) is one of the most frequent forms of this root. Depending on context, it can mean equal, the same, middle, straight way, or a state of indifference between two matters.

In expressions like سَوَاءٌ عَلَيْهِمْ أَأَنْذَرْتَهُمْ أَمْ لَمْ تُنْذِرْهُمْ, sawāʾ means that it is the same for them; warning and not warning make no outward difference because their hearts have been sealed.

In expressions such as سَوَاءَ السَّبِيلِ or سَوَاءِ الصِّرَاطِ, sawāʾ means the middle, straight, and non-deviating way. Here sawāʾ is not merely equality; it is the correct center of the road, the balanced path, and a direction without crookedness.

In the expression فِي سَوَاءِ الْجَحِيمِ, sawāʾ means the middle or center: in the very midst of Hell. Therefore sawāʾ must be translated carefully: sometimes “same,” sometimes “equal,” sometimes “middle,” sometimes “straight way,” and sometimes “balanced point.”

Laysū sawāʾan: avoiding unjust equalization

In Al-Imran 3:113, it says: لَيْسُوا سَوَاءً (laysū sawāʾan). This verse concerns the People of the Book and is very important. The Qur’an does not treat all of them as the same. Therefore no religious, ethnic, or historical group should be judged by one sweeping and unjust ruling.

This verse is one of the precise examples of Qur’anic justice in language. If some among the People of the Book have erred, that does not permit condemning all of them with one ruling. The Qur’an distinguishes; and distinguishing in the right place is part of justice.

Kalimatin sawāʾ: a balanced word among the people of faith

In Al-Imran 3:64, it says: تَعَالَوْا إِلَىٰ كَلِمَةٍ سَوَاءٍ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَكُمْ.

This expression is often translated as “a common word,” and that translation carries part of the meaning. But kalimatin sawāʾ is not only verbal commonality. This word must be balanced, just, and free of domination: a word that does not turn any group into the lord of another group.

The verse itself explains what this word is: that we worship none but God, associate nothing with Him, and do not take one another as lords besides God. So kalimatin sawāʾ is a word that levels human beings before God. No people, cleric, prophet, scholar, school, or power has the right to occupy the place of God’s lordship.

This is one of the most beautiful uses of S W Y in religious dialogue. The call to tawhid must be built upon a balanced word, not upon domination, humiliation, or making human beings into lords.

Suwā: a balanced and even place

سُوًى (suwā) appears in Ta-Ha 20:58: مَكَانًا سُوًى (makānan suwā). Here it means a suitable, even, balanced place where both sides can be present; a place where neither side has unfair spatial advantage.

This word shows that S W Y carries the meaning of justice and balance even in relation to place. Makānan suwā is a place suitable for encounter or appointment: not hidden, not uneven, and not to the advantage of one side.

Sawiyy: soundness, uprightness, and freedom from crookedness

سَوِيّ (sawiyy) is an adjective pointing to soundness, uprightness, freedom from crookedness, and proportion.

In Maryam 19:17, the Spirit appears to Mary as بَشَرًا سَوِيًّا (basharan sawiyyan), a sound and proportioned human form. In Maryam 19:10, Zechariah is prevented from speaking to people while being sawiyyan, meaning in ordinary health and without bodily injury.

In the expression صِرَاطًا سَوِيًّا (ṣirāṭan sawiyyan), the meaning is a straight, sound, and non-crooked path. Sawiyy here is not merely “normal” or “ordinary”; it is a path without deviation, crookedness, or unevenness.

In Al-Mulk 67:22, the one who walks sawiyyan on a straight path is contrasted with one who moves fallen upon his face. This image shows that being sawiyy is not only bodily health; it is a symbol of right direction, upright posture, and conscious walking upon guidance.

Taswiyah and justice in creation

The root S W Y in creation verses shows that creation is joined with measure and proportion. God does not only bring into being; He orders, gives proportion, and prepares everything for its function.

In the creation of the human being, sawwā points to the balance of existential structure. In the creation of the heavens, taswiyah relates to the ordering of the cosmic system. In the creation of the self, وَنَفْسٍ وَمَا سَوَّاهَا shows that the human self also has measure, firmness, and capacity.

This view takes the human being out of meaninglessness. If the human being has been proportioned, then he is not purposeless or shapeless. A relation of measure, capacity, and responsibility has been placed within him.

Istiwā and human maturity

When the Qur’an says about Moses, وَلَمَّا بَلَغَ أَشُدَّهُ وَاسْتَوَىٰ, it shows that human growth is not only reaching bodily strength. Istiwā is the stage of firmness and maturity, where the human being’s powers reach balance and he becomes ready to receive judgment and knowledge.

This verse is important for understanding Qur’anic maturity. Maturity is not only age or bodily force. The human being must reach a firmness that can carry responsibility, judgment, and knowledge. Therefore istawā here is a word for reaching balance and stability in human existence.

Suggested rendering in Forqan

For the root S W Y, one fixed translation is not enough. The form of the word and the context of the verse must be considered.

  • تُسَوَّىٰ (tusawwā): to be leveled, to be made one with the earth.
  • سَوَّىٰ (sawwā): proportioned, arranged, measured, brought to firmness.
  • نُسَوِّيَ (nusawwiya): to arrange, restore, and proportion precisely.
  • سَاوَىٰ (sāwā): made equal, leveled, established balance.
  • اسْتَوَىٰ (istawā) in humans or plants: became firm, upright, mature.
  • اسْتَوَىٰ (istawā) in divine context: encompassed in governance and lordship, without bodily comparison.
  • يَسْتَوِي / تَسْتَوِي (yastawī / tastawī): is equal / is not equal, is on the same level / is not on the same level.
  • سَوَاء (sawāʾ): same, equal, middle, balanced way, straight way.
  • سُوًى (suwā): even, balanced, suitable, equal.
  • سَوِيّ (sawiyy): sound, upright, straight, free of crookedness, proportioned.

Summary

The root س و ي (S W Y) in the Qur’an is the root of straightness, balance, proportioning, harmony, settling, and freedom from crookedness.

In the creation of the human being and the world, it speaks of order and measure. In ethical and epistemic comparisons, it shows that not everything is equal. In expressions of path, it speaks of a straight and non-deviating road. In religious dialogue, kalimatin sawāʾ becomes the balanced and just foundation for calling to tawhid. And in istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh, it calls the human being to a transcendent understanding of God’s governance and lordship.

So S W Y is not merely the root of equality. It is the root of measure, firmness, straightness, and balance. Through this root, the Qur’an shows that creation, path, judgment, dialogue, and lordship must all be understood free from crookedness, injustice, and imbalance.
