Qur’anic Root H M Z (ه م ز)

Hamz: pricking, applying pressure, faultfinding, and incitement to wrongdoing

(humazah)

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root ه م ز (h m z) concerns applying pressure, pricking, thrusting, and driving something forward. In physical usage, هَمَزَ (hamaza) denotes squeezing or pricking: for example, pressing something with the hand or goading an animal to make it move. In human interaction, the same family denotes faultfinding, taunting, belittling, and harming another person’s reputation.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an, in three forms. Two occurrences concern harmful human behaviour; the third concerns the hamazāt of devils.

The Qur’anic forms are:

  • هَمَّاز (hammāz): someone who habitually finds fault with and taunts others; in Al-Qalam 68:11.
  • هُمَزَة (humazah): a habitual faultfinder and taunter; in Al-Humazah 104:1.
  • هَمَزَات (hamazāt): the plural of هَمْزَة (hamzah), pricks, pressures, or incitements; in Al-Muʾminūn 23:97.

Arabic also records هَمَزَ (hamaza), “he pressed or pricked”; هَمْز (hamz), the action of pricking; هَامِز (hāmiz), a faultfinder; and مِهْمَاز (mihmāz), an implement for pricking and driving an animal. These particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

The distinction between هَمْزَة (hamzah) and هُمَزَة (humazah) matters. Hamzah, with sukūn on mīm (no following vowel), can denote a single prick or application of pressure; its plural is hamazāt. Humazah, with ḍammah on hāʾ and fatḥah on mīm, describes a person known for faultfinding and taunting in the Qur’anic construction. Their similar spelling should not obscure the difference. The plural hamazāt indicates multiplicity, but does not by itself establish uninterrupted duration or a fixed intensity of these incitements.

2. The semantic centre in early Arabic

Pressure, pricking, and striking

In physical Arabic usage, hamz denotes pressing something with the hand, pricking, striking, and causing movement.

هَمَزَ الشَّيْءَ فِي كَفِّهِ (hamaza al-shayʾa fī kaffihi) means squeezing something in one’s palm; هَمَزَ الدَّابَّةَ (hamaza al-dābbah) means pricking or urging an animal to make it move.

مِهْمَاز (mihmāz) belongs to this family. It denotes an implement with which a rider pricks an animal to make it move or increase its speed. Related names for such implements are also recorded in Arabic. A tool’s name being recorded in lexicons does not itself establish when it originated or whether it circulated before the Qur’an.

The common element in these usages is pressure or a blow applied to something or someone. It can serve to break or reshape an object, make an animal move, or drive something away or aside.

Not every use of hamz implies a severe blow. A brief prick or limited pressure can also be expressed through this family. “Applying pressure or a prick” therefore gives a better semantic centre than invariably “pounding and breaking.”

Witnesses from earlier Arabic

A verse attributed to Al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār, an Arab poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

أَقَامَ الثِّقَافُ وَالطَّرِيدَةُ دَرْأَهَا
كَمَا قَوَّمَتْ ضِغْنَ الشَّمُوسِ الْمَهَامِزُ

“The straightening implements corrected its crookedness, just as goads correct the resistance of an unruly horse.”

The poet describes tools for correcting a bow’s crookedness and compares their action with an implement that brings a resistant horse under control. الْمَهَامِز (al-mahāmiz) here denotes physical implements for pricking an animal, rather than words or reproaches. Two explanations of the form are reported: omission of the yāʾ of الْمَهَامِيز (al-mahāmīz) for poetic necessity, or a plural of مِهْمَز (mihmaz).

Al-Shammākh lived in both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The precise date of this particular verse is nevertheless unclear; attribution to him alone does not establish it as certainly predating the Qur’an.

A rajaz fragment attributed to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj reads:

وَمَنْ هَمَزْنَا رَأْسَهُ تَهَشَّمَا

“Whoever’s head we pressed and struck was shattered.”

This witness belongs to a poet of the Islamic period. It illustrates pressure and striking in Arabic, rather than serving as a pre-Islamic witness. Nor does the fragment’s image of shattering by itself establish that a corresponding event actually occurred.

Alongside these witnesses, recorded expressions for squeezing a walnut, goading an animal, and applying pressure to wood or a spear shaft clarify the root’s physical field.

These witnesses matter for its ethical usage because they show that faultfinding and taunting were not the family’s only meanings. They do not, however, allow us to date precisely a transition from physical pressure to verbal injury.

From a physical prick to taunting and faultfinding

In human interaction, hamaza can denote finding fault with someone, taunting, belittling, or speaking ill of them.

Pricking and faultfinding have a recognizable experiential similarity: just as a prick injures the body, contemptuous speech or gestures can injure a person’s reputation and dignity.

Arabic usage does not restrict the behaviour to explicit speech. Eye gestures, movements of the head or mouth, mocking facial expressions, and speaking ill of people behind their backs also appear in explanations of hamz.

Hamz in human behaviour can therefore take place through speech or gesture. Faultfinding and demeaning another person connect these usages.

The image of pricking does not make every criticism or disclosure of a real fault an instance of hamz. In its condemnatory usage, the behaviour concerns injurious faultfinding, belittling, or taunting. Examining an error to correct it, stating a truth when needed, or making a reasoned objection does not become hamz merely because the words are unwelcome.

This distinction emerges from linguistic usage together with the Qur’an’s ethical context, rather than from a claim that the root alone identifies a speaker’s intention.

3. Humazah: faultfinding and belittling others

Al-Humazah 104:1

وَيْلٌ لِكُلِّ هُمَزَةٍ لُمَزَةٍ

“Woe to every faultfinder and taunter.”

This is the root’s best-known Qur’anic occurrence. The chapter opens with a warning concerning a person known for finding fault with and belittling others.

The morphology of humazah

هُمَزَة (humazah) follows the pattern فُعَلَة (fuʿalah). Arabic can use this pattern to describe someone in whom a behaviour is frequent and for which that person is known.

Humazah therefore does not merely mean someone who once said something unwelcome or criticized another person. It highlights a characteristic trait or habitual behaviour.

Lexical explanations apply humazah to both a man and a woman. Its final tāʾ should therefore not be restricted to indicating the person’s feminine gender. In this pattern it can contribute to an intensive description.

The morphology alone does not establish that someone finds fault a particular number of times each day, does so as a formal profession, or has crossed a numerical threshold. It marks the prominence of the trait, rather than a countable criterion.

Humazah and lumazah: one meaning or two behaviours?

The verse places humazah beside لُمَزَة (lumazah). This matters lexically: two related roots condemn human behaviour together.

Some older explanations distinguish hamz as faultfinding behind someone’s back and lamz as faultfinding in their presence. Others reverse the distinction. Another explanation associates hamz more with gestures and bodily movements, and lamz with verbal faultfinding.

These differences prevent us from drawing an absolute, permanent boundary based only on presence versus absence, or speech versus gesture.

Semantic overlap does not require treating the words as identical without examination. Both concern faultfinding and belittling, but can encompass different methods and manifestations of that behaviour.

Together, humazah and lumazah broaden the condemnation. The person makes others targets of taunting, faultfinding, and contempt, whether through words or gestures, in their presence or behind their backs.

That contextual interpretation should not become a lexical law requiring every hamz to be hidden and every lamz to be public.

Faultfinding and the accumulation of wealth

Al-Humazah 104:2

الَّذِي جَمَعَ مَالًا وَعَدَّدَهُ

“The one who has gathered wealth and counted it repeatedly.”

Al-Humazah 104:3

يَحْسَبُ أَنَّ مَالَهُ أَخْلَدَهُ

“He supposes that his wealth has made him immortal.”

These verses sharpen the portrait of the condemned person. Besides being known for faultfinding and belittling, he gathers and counts wealth and imagines that his property has made him immortal.

In this context, faultfinding accompanies an image of self-importance and reliance on property. Someone who treats wealth as the measure of superiority and immortality may look down on others.

Two points should nevertheless remain distinct.

First, accumulating wealth is not part of the lexical meaning of humazah. A person can be a faultfinder who belittles others without being wealthy.

Second, the chapter describes the faultfinder alongside other behaviours; it does not call every property owner, or everyone who counts their assets, a humazah simply for doing so.

The portrait brings together contempt for others, attachment to wealth, and a mistaken conception of immortality. Each feature nevertheless retains its own meaning.

Humazah should therefore not be translated “an arrogant rich person.” Its basic equivalent should remain a faultfinder and taunter.

Does the verse concern only one historical person?

Historical and exegetical reports propose several people as examples of this verse. The verse itself, however, begins لِكُلِّ هُمَزَةٍ لُمَزَةٍ (li-kulli humazatin lumazatin), “to every faultfinder and taunter.”

كُلّ (kull), “every,” broadens the warning. The verse’s general meaning cannot therefore be restricted to one historical individual without evidence.

Even if a particular person’s conduct provided the immediate setting for the condemnation, the sentence’s general construction still encompasses everyone with the described trait.

The root study’s main concern is identifying hamz as behaviour, rather than deciding conclusively whose conduct occasioned the chapter.

4. Hammāz: faultfinding as a recognized habit

Al-Qalam 68:11

هَمَّازٍ مَشَّاءٍ بِنَمِيمٍ

“A habitual faultfinder and taunter, constantly going about with malicious gossip.”

Two descriptions stand together here: هَمَّاز (hammāz) and مَشَّاءٍ بِنَمِيمٍ (mashshāʾin bi-namīmin).

The morphology of hammāz

هَمَّاز (hammāz) follows فَعَّال (faʿʿāl), a pattern often expressing frequency, repetition, or the prominence of a behaviour in Arabic.

Hammāz consequently describes someone who repeatedly finds fault and taunts others, becoming known for that behaviour.

The pattern alone does not establish that the person earns money from faultfinding or does it as a formal occupation. The point is the behaviour’s prominence.

Nor should the difference between hammāz and humazah be exaggerated. Both describe a habitual faultfinder. Their morphological difference is real, but it does not establish that one necessarily denotes an earlier stage and the other the final or more severe stage of the same behaviour.

In Al-Qalam, hammāz appears within a series of condemnatory descriptions. The preceding verse speaks of someone who frequently swears oaths and is contemptible; subsequent verses add other traits. Hammāz identifies one recognizable behaviour within that portrait.

Distinguishing hamz from malicious gossip

مَشَّاءٍ بِنَمِيمٍ (mashshāʾin bi-namīmin) is semantically connected with hammāz, but is not identical with it.

Hamz here concerns faultfinding and taunting. نَمِيم (namīm) concerns carrying words between people, especially in a way that creates hostility and corruption.

Someone who speaks ill of another person may be both a faultfinder and a malicious gossip. The two behaviours do not, however, always occur together.

For example, someone who belittles another person before a gathering through movements of the eyes or mouth can engage in hamz without carrying information from one person to another.

Conversely, someone may relay another person’s words to create hostility between people, even when those words do not directly attribute a fault to anyone.

Placing hammāz and mashshāʾin bi-namīmin together condemns faultfinding and malicious gossip within one person’s conduct. One expression cannot simply replace the other in translation.

“A malicious gossip” is therefore an incomplete translation of hammāz itself. That equivalent belongs more directly to the verse’s second expression.

Faultfinding through words and gestures

In Arabic, hamz is not confined to direct speech. Contemptuous gestures, mocking imitation of someone’s movements, or facial expressions used to demean them can also belong to its behavioural field.

Translating hammāz solely as “a backbiter” therefore loses some manifestations of faultfinding.

“A faultfinder and taunter” offers a more suitable range, encompassing both verbal conduct and other means of belittling.

The verse nevertheless does not specify exactly which gestures or words the person used. Such details should not be invented as its certain meaning.

5. Hamazāt al-shayāṭīn: the pricks and incitements of devils

Al-Muʾminūn 23:97

وَقُلْ رَبِّ أَعُوذُ بِكَ مِنْ هَمَزَاتِ الشَّيَاطِينِ

“And say: My Lord, I seek refuge in You from the pricks and incitements of devils.”

This is a different use of the root. In the two occurrences examined earlier, hamz concerned human conduct toward other people; here the subject is the hamazāt of devils.

The meaning of hamazāt

هَمَزَات (hamazāt) is the plural of هَمْزَة (hamzah). It can denote individual pricks, applications of pressure, or incitements.

Goading an animal with an implement such as a mihmāz is one clear physical use of this family. The prick makes the animal move or influences its movement.

In هَمَزَاتِ الشَّيَاطِينِ (hamazāt al-shayāṭīn), this meaning of pressure and incitement provides a suitable background for understanding the word’s nonphysical application.

The verse attributes the hamazāt to devils and calls for taking refuge in The God from them. In this context, they can be understood as incitements, pressing temptations, or impulses driving a person toward wrongdoing.

The image of a physical prick should not be extended into a definitive theory of how devils physically affect the human body. The verse mentions their hamazāt without explaining the mechanism in detail.

Nor should hamazāt be restricted to an audible voice or a thought entering the mind. The lexical field of pricking and incitement is broader than such a restriction.

Hamazāt in the surrounding verses

Al-Muʾminūn 23:96

ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ السَّيِّئَةَ ۚ نَحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَصِفُونَ

“Repel evil with what is better; We know best what they describe.”

Here Muhammad is commanded to respond to evil in a better way. The command to seek refuge from the hamazāt of devils follows immediately.

This connection matters. Faced with mistreatment, accusations, or injury, someone may be drawn into anger, retaliation, or a wrongful response. In that setting, seeking refuge from devilish incitements has a clear relationship with the command to repel evil through what is better.

Hamazāt should not, however, be defined lexically as nothing but anger or revenge. These can be instances of incitement in this context without exhausting the word’s meaning.

The following verse continues:

Al-Muʾminūn 23:98

وَأَعُوذُ بِكَ رَبِّ أَنْ يَحْضُرُونِ

“And I seek refuge in You, my Lord, from their being present with me.”

Seeking refuge from the hamazāt is followed by seeking refuge from the devils’ presence.

The two sentences are connected but not identical. The first concerns their hamazāt and the second their presence. Presence should therefore not be made part of the lexical meaning of hamz or hamazāt.

وَقُلْ (wa-qul), “and say,” also directly addresses Muhammad. The Qur’an commands him to seek refuge in his Lord from devilish incitements. Without contextual support, this should not be turned into a merely performative statement whose only actual addressees are other people.

More broadly, the connection between taking refuge in The God and resisting devilish incitement also appears in Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 and Fuṣṣilat 41:36. This Qur’anic network supports understanding hamazāt as misleading incitements.

Hamazāt and waswasah

Hamazāt and وَسْوَسَة (waswasah), whispering or prompting, can approach each other in contextual meaning, but belong to different roots.

Waswasah is usually connected with concealed suggestion and the repetition of speech or thoughts. Hamz, in its physical sense, concerns pricking and pressure; in reference to devils it can highlight inciting and setting someone in motion.

“The whisperings of devils” is therefore an intelligible rendering of the verse’s contextual sense, while “the incitements of devils” retains a clearer connection with the lexical family of hamz.

This difference should not become an absolute rule requiring waswasah always to be gentle and hamz always severe or sudden. The limited Qur’anic occurrences do not establish such a precise boundary.

Nor does the verse itself provide evidence for restricting all hamazāt to one particular kind of human thought, feeling, or illness.

6. Hamz, lamz, ghamz, and related behaviours

H M Z (hamz) and L M Z (lamz): faultfinding and taunting

These are among the most closely related terms in this discussion.

In Al-Humazah 104:1, both appear as intensive descriptions. Their combination demonstrates shared meaning in the field of social conduct and belittling others.

The Qur’an also uses the root ل م ز (l m z) elsewhere.

Al-Tawbah 9:58

وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَلْمِزُكَ فِي الصَّدَقَاتِ فَإِنْ أُعْطُوا مِنْهَا رَضُوا وَإِنْ لَمْ يُعْطَوْا مِنْهَا إِذَا هُمْ يَسْخَطُونَ

“Among them are those who find fault with you concerning the charitable contributions. If they are given some of them, they are pleased; but if they are given none, they suddenly become angry.”

Lamz here concerns finding fault with Muhammad regarding charitable contributions. The context connects faultfinding with the response to receiving or being denied property.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:11, the Qur’an also prohibits lamz among members of the community. These usages place it within faultfinding, demeaning, and taunting others.

Arabic lexical tradition proposes several distinctions between hamz and lamz. One is taken as faultfinding behind someone’s back and the other as faultfinding face to face; other reports reverse that division. Hamz is also sometimes explained more through gestures and bodily movements, and lamz through speech.

These differences prevent establishing an absolute boundary.

A more secure distinction is that hamz has a recognized connection with pressure and pricking within its lexical family and concerns injurious faultfinding in social usage. Lamz likewise concerns disparaging and belittling people in its Qur’anic usage.

A translation can use “faultfinding and taunting” to convey the combined expression, while independent root studies should examine each root’s witnesses separately.

H M Z (hamz) and GH M Z (ghamz): pressure and gesture

غَمْز (ghamz) concerns applying pressure, squeezing, and making a gesture in Arabic. Some usages also involve eye gestures or bodily movements used to belittle and mock.

Ghamz and hamz therefore overlap. Both can concern physical pressure or a contemptuous action.

Semantic similarity does not make them the same root. Each has its own history of usage and word forms.

In the Qur’an, ghamz occurs as يَتَغَامَزُونَ (yataghāmazūna) in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30, in a context describing wrongdoers exchanging gestures and mocking those who have faith.

This is close to hamz in the field of belittling and hurtful gestures. It does not establish that every hamz must involve the eyes or every ghamz is verbal faultfinding.

H M Z (hamz) and N M M (namīm): faultfinding and malicious gossip

Al-Qalam 68:11 places hammāz beside mashshāʾin bi-namīmin.

Hamz concerns faultfinding and taunting, while namīm concerns carrying words between people in a destructive way.

Faultfinding can lead to malicious gossip, but does not necessarily do so in every instance.

Hammāz should therefore not be translated simply “a malicious gossip.” That would erase the distinction between the verse’s two descriptions.

H M Z (hamz) and S KH R (sukhriyyah): taunting and mocking

سُخْرِيَّة (sukhriyyah), in social usage, concerns mocking and demeaning others.

Hamz can accompany mockery, especially when someone belittles another person through gestures, facial expressions, or cutting remarks.

Not all mockery is necessarily called hamz. Hamz can also take the form of speaking ill of someone or finding fault without laughter or mocking imitation.

In some contexts, demeaning another person is their common element; their behaviours are not identical in every respect.

H M Z (hamz) and N Z GH (nazgh): pricking and incitement

In verses concerning devils, نَزْغ (nazgh) is among the terms close to hamazāt.

In Qur’anic usage, nazgh can denote inciting, provoking, or creating discord. Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 and Fuṣṣilat 41:36 connect devilish incitement with the command to seek refuge in The God.

This proximity helps illuminate Al-Muʾminūn 23:97. In both cases a person needs divine refuge against a devilish influence or incitement.

The proximity does not make hamazāt and nazgh completely synonymous in every usage. Hamz belongs to a family involving pricking and pressure, while nazgh has a separate root and field of usage.

7. Hamz in language and the name of the letter hamzah

Arabic also uses هَمْز (hamz) in discussions of the pronunciation of letters.

هَمْزَة (hamzah) names an Arabic speech sound produced by closing and reopening the passage of air at the larynx. Older lexical explanations also connect its name with pressure during articulation. The name, with fatḥah on hāʾ and sukūn on mīm, differs in pronunciation from the Qur’anic description هُمَزَة (humazah), a faultfinder.

This connection matters for the history of linguistic terminology, but should not be confused with the Qur’anic usages of hammāz, humazah, and hamazāt.

Not every Arabic word written with a hamzah belongs to the root h m z. The presence of the letter at the beginning or within a word is not evidence of derivation from this root.

Likewise, hamazāt al-shayāṭīn in Al-Muʾminūn 23:97 does not mean hamzah letters or written signs. The expression concerns pricks and incitements attributed to devils.

Root study must distinguish a letter’s name, a pronunciation term, and the root’s semantic derivatives.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For هُمَزَة (humazah) in Al-Humazah 104:1, “a faultfinder and taunter” is suitable. Since lumazah also appears in that verse, a fluent rendering of the whole can be “Woe to every faultfinder and taunter.” The lexical explanation should examine each root’s contribution separately.

For هَمَّاز (hammāz) in Al-Qalam 68:11, “a habitual faultfinder,” “a faultfinder and taunter,” or “someone who continually taunts others” is proposed. The complete verse should render mashshāʾin bi-namīmin separately, keeping malicious gossip distinct from the meaning of hammāz itself.

For هَمَزَاتِ الشَّيَاطِينِ (hamazāt al-shayāṭīn) in Al-Muʾminūn 23:97, “the pricks and incitements of devils” is suitable in a research article. “The incitements of devils” is proposed for a shorter, more natural verse translation. “The whisperings of devils” also conveys the contextual meaning, but retains less of this family’s particular image of pricking and pressure.

In the word-by-word boxes, the main translation should remain brief, with the fuller explanation placed under contextual meaning:

  • هُمَزَة (humazah): a faultfinder and taunter; someone known for belittling and finding fault with others.
  • هَمَّاز (hammāz): a habitual faultfinder; someone who continually taunts others.
  • هَمَزَات (hamazāt): pricks and incitements; in this verse, the harmful incitements of devils.

None of these usages should restrict hamz absolutely to lying. Someone can use a true statement contemptuously and harmfully. Faultfinding can also occur through gestures and behaviour, rather than words alone.

Conversely, not every criticism, disagreement, or disclosure of an error should be called hamz. The condemnatory Qur’anic usages concern contemptuous and injurious behaviour, rather than the mere expression of disagreement or truth.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root h m z has a recognized physical field: applying pressure, pricking, thrusting, and causing movement. The same family also denotes faultfinding, taunting, speaking ill of others, and belittling them in human interaction.

In the Qur’an, humazah and hammāz describe someone in whose conduct faultfinding and taunting are prominent. This can occur through words, gestures, or other means of belittling. Their morphological patterns alone do not establish an absolute boundary concerning the intensity or kind of faultfinding.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:97, hamazāt concerns incitements and pricks attributed to devils. The image of pressure and pricking can help explain incitement to wrongdoing, but the nature and details of that influence do not follow from the word alone.

Hamz is semantically connected with lamz, ghamz, namīm, and nazgh, but none should replace another without examination. In particular, the familiar division of hamz into faultfinding behind someone’s back and lamz into faultfinding face to face is not an absolute linguistic law, given the differing older reports.

The family’s final definition should preserve its two main fields: In physical usage, hamz concerns applying pressure, a prick, or a blow; in human behaviour it denotes injurious faultfinding and taunting; and in “the hamazāt of devils” it concerns incitements and pressures from which a person seeks refuge in The God.

In understanding this root in the Qur’an, the condemned conduct is not merely speaking about another person’s error. It is faultfinding and contemptuous behaviour that targets human dignity. The distinction between sound criticism and hurtfully pricking others is among the most important boundaries to preserve in the family’s ethical usage.
