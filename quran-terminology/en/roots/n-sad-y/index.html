The front of the head and its forelock: grasping the forelock, expressing power, and human responsibility for lying and wrongdoing

1. Introducing the root and its semantic branches

The Qur’anic morphological classification identifies N Ṣ Y through nāṣiyah and its plural nawāṣī. Arabic nāṣiyah names the front of the head, especially the hairline above the forehead and the hair growing there.

In established Arabic usage it is more than an anatomical name: expressions such as grasping the forelock can picture control, restraint, authority, or overcoming someone’s resistance.

Firmly grasping a person’s or animal’s forelock can make them move or prevent movement. This tangible experience underlies an Arabic expression for someone or something being within another’s power and control.

Another figurative use identifies a group’s leading figures, much as Persian “heads of a people” means prominent leaders rather than merely their physical heads.

These senses nevertheless require distinction.

Nāṣiyah’s basic meaning is the front of the head and its hairline. Control, restraint, humiliation, and leadership arise in particular combinations with verbs and contexts.

Grasping the forelock must also be distinguished from the forelock itself. Nāṣiyah alone does not mean captivity, compulsion, or rule.

The Qur’an uses this family in three settings: The God’s power over moving creatures, seizing wrongdoers at resurrection, and warning someone who prevents another from prayer.

The three share an image, but their messages and situations differ.

An important point about root classification

Although the Qur’anic morphological classification places nāṣiyah and nawāṣī under N Ṣ Y, many early Arabic lexicons examine this family under N Ṣ W or the heading naṣā.

One early lexical analysis explicitly notes that the third radical is wāw in many verbal forms.

For example, naṣawtuhu, “I grasped his forelock,” belongs to this family.

Nominal forms such as nāṣiyah, naṣiyyah, and other derivatives, by contrast, are written with yāʾ.

N Ṣ Y in the Qur’anic classification and N Ṣ W in early lexicons should therefore not be treated as completely unrelated families.

The difference partly reflects ways of classifying final-weak roots and forms containing a weak radical.

This article retains N Ṣ Y as the selected study heading while also examining evidence recorded under naṣā in early lexicons.

Qur’anic forms

The family occurs four times, in four verses of three surahs.

VerseQur’anic formDirect meaning
Hūd 11:56بِنَاصِيَتِهَاIts forelock
Al-Raḥmān 55:41بِالنَّوَاصِيThe forelocks; fronts of heads
Al-ʿAlaq 96:15بِالنَّاصِيَةِThe forelock; front of the head
Al-ʿAlaq 96:16نَاصِيَةٍA forelock

Three instances use the singular nāṣiyah; one uses its broken plural nawāṣī.

No verb of this family, such as naṣā, yanṣū, nāṣā, tanāṣā, or intaṣā, occurs in the Qur’an.

Arabic also records naṣiyy, naṣiyyah, munāṣāh, and muntaṣā, but these are not Qur’anic derivatives of this root.

2. Nāṣiyah in early Arabic

Nāṣiyah: the front hairline

A lexical explanation states:

النَّاصِيَةُ وَاحِدَةُ النَّوَاصِي

al-nāṣiyatu wāḥidatu al-nawāṣī

Meaning:

“Nāṣiyah is the singular of nawāṣī.”

One early definition explains it as قُصَاصُ الشَّعَرِ فِي مُقَدَّمِ الرَّأْسِ: the boundary or hairline at the front of the head.

A more precise explanation distinguishes the hairline from the hair itself.

On this account, the basic Arabic use identifies where the hair grows at the front of the head; the hair there then receives the same name.

That distinction matters in translation.

The Persian “forehead” commonly identifies the front surface below the hairline and above the eyes.

“Forelock” or “front hair” identifies the hair at the front of the head.

Arabic nāṣiyah can relate to both areas, but the front hairline and the hair growing there provide the most precise lexical explanation.

“Forehead” is accessible and natural, but does not convey every lexical detail.

“Forelock” conveys the hair-related sense, but in some contexts, especially discussion of wrongdoers, may restrict attention too narrowly to the hair alone.

The lexical explanation should retain both aspects.

The nāṣiyah of humans and animals

Arabic does not restrict nāṣiyah to humans.

Nāṣiyatu al-farasi also identifies a horse’s forelock and front of the head.

Grasping the forelock can therefore concern a human or an animal.

That matters in Hūd 11:56, which says dābbah rather than only “human.”

The image does not mean every moving creature has precisely the same forehead hair as a human.

Arabic can use a familiar name in a general expression of power and control.

Some animals’ physical features should therefore be distinguished from the image’s rhetorical scope.

Nāṣāh: an attested Ṭayyiʾ dialect form

Some speakers of the Ṭayyiʾ tribe used nāṣāh in place of nāṣiyah.

A verse attributed to Ḥurayth ibn ʿAttāb al-Ṭāʾī says:

لَقَدْ آذَنَتْ أَهْلَ الْيَمَامَةِ طَيِّئٌ
بِحَرْبٍ كَنَاصَاةِ الْحِصَانِ الْمُشَهَّرِ

la-qad ādhanat ahla al-yamāmati ṭayyiʾun / bi-ḥarbin ka-nāṣāti al-ḥiṣāni al-mushahhari

A rendering close to the wording: “Ṭayyiʾ has indeed warned the people of Al-Yamāmah of a war like the forelock of a conspicuous stallion.”

Nāṣāh is the Ṭayyiʾ dialect form of nāṣiyah here.

The poet invokes a horse’s forelock within an image concerning war.

The example especially illustrates dialect and sound differences: some forms written with yāʾ in one dialect appear with alif in another.

The second hemistich varies between transmissions: some have al-ḥiṣān al-mushahhar, others al-agharr al-mushahhar.

The precise composition date and details of attribution also require caution.

The main value is evidence for nāṣāh and for applying the forelock term to a horse, rather than establishing a certain historical picture of that war.

3. Grasping the forelock: from a bodily action to power and restraint

Naṣāhu: he grasped his forelock

Arabic records naṣawtuhu as “I grasped his forelock.”

Early lexical explanations record two related senses:

Grasping the forelock.

And pulling it.

They may occur together, since grasping front hair may serve to pull or restrain someone.

Grasping and pulling nevertheless differ.

Someone can grasp a forelock without pulling it forcefully.

Pulling it, meanwhile, can involve pain, compulsion, or humiliation.

The verb and context therefore determine the action’s intensity and outcome.

Munāṣāh: grasping one another’s forelocks

Nāṣaytuhu can describe contending with someone, each grasping the other’s forelock.

Munāṣāh consequently also relates to conflict and confrontation.

The semantic extension is intelligible: grasping front hair can occur during a physical struggle, and the name can extend to contention or rivalry.

Not every disagreement should nevertheless be called munāṣāh.

Nor does this verbal form make every Qur’anic nāṣiyah concern two equally powerful opponents.

Hūd 11:56 concerns The God’s power over all moving creatures.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 warns a defiant person.

The relationships between the parties differ.

Grasping the forelock in expressions of power

In Arabic language and culture, grasping someone’s forelock can express restraining them and bringing them under another’s control.

Holding another’s forelock can limit their movement or pull them in a particular direction.

The image supports “someone’s forelock is in someone else’s hand” as an expression of power and control.

An early explanation of Hūd 11:56 records Arabic use of such expressions for obedience and being under another’s authority.

Accounts of customs at that time describe sometimes cutting a captive’s forelock upon release, so the captor could later invoke the victory when boasting.

Such social accounts are not the lexical meaning of nāṣiyah.

The word names part of the head; power and humiliation arise from grasping, pulling, or the accompanying treatment.

4. Figurative uses and other linguistic branches

Nawāṣī al-qawm: a group’s leading figures

Nawāṣī al-qawmi can identify prominent or leading members of a group.

This relates to the image of the head and its front.

As the head is above the body, prominent figures can figuratively be called the group’s heads and leaders.

A poem attributed to Umm Qubays al-Ḍabbiyyah says:

وَمَشْهَدٍ قَدْ كَفَيْتُ الْغَائِبِينَ بِهِ
فِي مَجْمَعٍ مِنْ نَوَاصِي النَّاسِ مَشْهُودِ

wa-mashhadin qad kafaytu al-ghāʾibīna bihi / fī majmaʿin min nawāṣī al-nāsi mashhūdi

Approximate sense:

“And many a gathering where I fulfilled the task on behalf of those absent, in an assembly attended by the people’s leading figures.”

Nawāṣī al-nāsi does not identify the attendees’ bodily forelocks here.

It identifies the prominent people attending the assembly.

The example illustrates a figurative social-standing use.

It does not make nāṣiyah mean a group’s leaders in Al-Raḥmān 55:41 as well.

There, nawāṣī occurs alongside aqdām, foregrounding the image of seizing body parts.

A figurative meaning in one setting should not be imposed on every context.

Naṣiyyah: the selected members of a group

Arabic also records naṣiyyatu al-qawmi for a group’s selected or prominent members.

Intaṣaytu al-shayʾa can mean “I selected something.”

One lexical analysis connects the family with prominence, selection, and rising above other things.

This is intelligible beside the image of a forehead or a group’s heads: a part or person stands out and is selected.

It should not become a certain historical origin for nāṣiyah.

A forehead, height, leadership, and selection can share imagery without establishing the historical sequence of every use.

Naṣiyy: a plant name

Lexicons also record naṣiyy for a plant considered good pasture.

Some explanations relate the name to a growth stage, with the same plant receiving another name after drying or turning white.

These uses occur in sources examining the naṣā family.

The visible similarity between naṣiyy and nāṣiyah does not make the plant name derive directly from the human forehead.

The plant name itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

It belongs in the lexical survey without changing the four Qur’anic instances’ meaning.

Height and adjoining areas of ground

Some Arabic uses of muntaṣā identify a high position or prominent part of an area.

Tunāṣī al-arḍu al-arḍa can describe two areas of land adjoining one another.

These concern rising, approaching, or adjoining areas.

They should not all be derived simply from grasping a forehead.

They are established linguistic branches whose precise historical relationship with nāṣiyah requires independent evidence.

5. Hūd 11:56: mā min dābbatin illā huwa ākhidhun bi-nāṣiyatihā

إِنِّي تَوَكَّلْتُ عَلَى اللَّهِ رَبِّي وَرَبِّكُمْ مَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ إِلَّا هُوَ آخِذٌ بِنَاصِيَتِهَا إِنَّ رَبِّي عَلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

innī tawakkaltu ʿalā allāhi rabbī wa-rabbikum mā min dābbatin illā huwa ākhidhun bi-nāṣiyatihā inna rabbī ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīmin

Translation:

“I have placed my trust in The God, my Lord and your Lord. There is no moving creature but He holds its forelock. My Lord is on a straight path.”

Hūd 11:56

This is the Qur’an’s first occurrence of nāṣiyah in textual order.

Hūd is the speaker.

After rejecting the deities his people associate with The God, he declares his trust in The God before them.

A. Mā min dābbah: the subject’s breadth

Dābbah belongs to D B B and generally describes a creature moving on the earth.

Mā min dābbatin illā expresses comprehensiveness here.

No moving creature falls outside the stated condition.

That does not make all moving creatures identical in bodily form, hair, skull structure, or nervous system.

If grasping the forelock expresses power and control, it can apply to all moving creatures even when their anatomy differs from humans or horses.

The verse alone therefore does not establish that every living creature has forehead hair.

B. Ākhidhun bi-nāṣiyatihā: power and control

Ākhidhun is an active participle from ʾ KH DH, concerning taking or grasping.

Bi-nāṣiyatihā identifies what is grasped in the sentence’s image.

A close rendering places the creature’s forelock within The God’s control.

Grasping the forelock is an established Arabic expression of power over another person or creature.

The expression can therefore be understood as placing all moving creatures within The God’s power.

This reading fits Hūd’s declaration of trust at the verse’s beginning.

He does not fear his people because he regards no moving creature as beyond his Lord’s power.

The expression does not establish a human-like physical divine hand materially holding every animal’s forehead hair.

The verse uses a familiar image to express power and authority.

C. Does grasping the forelock deny human choice?

Ākhidhun bi-nāṣiyatihā can express The God’s power over creation.

Does the expression alone say that every human choice and action occurs without any choice or responsibility?

No.

Such a conclusion would require examining the verses about will, choice, guidance, error, and human responsibility together.

In Hūd’s story itself, his people receive a call and warning and are held responsible for their conduct.

Hūd 11:57 says that if they turn away, the message has been delivered, and The God can replace them with another people.

Divine power and human responsibility therefore appear within the same context.

Neither should be interpreted so as to remove the other without examination.

D. Inna rabbī ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm

The verse concludes:

إِنَّ رَبِّي عَلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

inna rabbī ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīmin

“My Lord is on a straight path.”

This follows the declaration of divine power.

The context therefore does not separate The God’s power from His path’s rightness and uprightness.

The connection suggests that divine authority should not be equated with arbitrary, oppressive human behavior.

Justice and rightness are nevertheless not nāṣiyah’s lexical meaning.

Nāṣiyah names part of the head; power comes from ākhidhun bi-nāṣiyatihā, and the straight path from the concluding sentence.

6. Al-Raḥmān 55:41: seizing wrongdoers by forelocks and feet

يُعْرَفُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ بِسِيمَاهُمْ فَيُؤْخَذُ بِالنَّوَاصِي وَالْأَقْدَامِ

yuʿrafu al-mujrimūna bi-sīmāhum fa-yuʾkhadhu bi-al-nawāṣī wa-al-aqdāmi

Translation:

“Wrongdoers are recognized by their marks, then seized by their forelocks and feet.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:41

The plural al-nawāṣī appears here.

It is the plural of nāṣiyah and identifies the fronts of heads.

A. Nāṣiyah and nawāṣī

Nāṣiyah is singular.

Nawāṣī is its broken plural.

The difference concerns number, rather than a different body part.

Arabic nawāṣī can figuratively identify group leaders, but Al-Raḥmān 55:41 provides an important contextual clue:

وَالْأَقْدَامِ

wa-al-aqdāmi

“And the feet.”

Nawāṣī and aqdām in the same construction support the bodily reading.

“A people’s heads and leaders” is therefore unsuitable here.

B. Yuʿrafu al-mujrimūna bi-sīmāhum

The verse begins by saying wrongdoers are recognized by their marks.

Sīmā concerns a sign or mark.

It does not belong to N Ṣ Y.

Some early explanations identify the marks through changes in facial color or other visible features.

The verse’s own expression does not specify the exact kind of mark.

Commentarial details should not therefore be inserted into the basic translation without an explicit textual basis.

Nāṣiyah does not mean “a mark of guilt” here; it is a body part by which the recognized wrongdoers are described as being seized.

C. Fa-yuʾkhadhu bi-al-nawāṣī wa-al-aqdām

Yuʾkhadhu is passive.

Nawāṣī and aqdām identify the body parts involved in the seizing.

One early explanation describes seizing the wrongdoers and taking them toward punishment.

Some commentarial accounts describe bringing together or binding a wrongdoer’s forelock and feet, and carrying the person in that position.

The verse’s own wording does not explicitly say the forelock is tied to the feet.

Its explicit image is seizing by the forelocks and feet.

The direct text should therefore be distinguished from commentators’ detailed pictures.

D. Is the verse only a symbol of humiliation?

Seizing someone by the forelock can express restraint and humiliation in Arabic culture.

At resurrection, that image relates to wrongdoers’ situation and their conduct’s consequences.

A figurative dimension should nevertheless not erase the literal image altogether.

The verse names forelocks and feet as body parts.

Explaining power and humiliation can aid understanding, but “the wrongdoers are humiliated” alone does not translate the whole expression.

7. Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:16: a lying, wrongdoing forelock

كَلَّا لَئِنْ لَمْ يَنْتَهِ لَنَسْفَعًا بِالنَّاصِيَةِ

kallā la-in lam yantahi la-nasfaʿan bi-al-nāṣiyati

Translation:

“No indeed. If he does not desist, We will surely seize and drag him by the forelock.”

Al-ʿAlaq 96:15

نَاصِيَةٍ كَاذِبَةٍ خَاطِئَةٍ

nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭiʾatin

Translation:

“A lying, wrongdoing forelock.”

Al-ʿAlaq 96:16

These are especially important N Ṣ Y instances: the forelock is both the object of seizing and pulling and qualified by two ethical descriptions.

A. The context: preventing another person from prayer

Al-ʿAlaq 96:9 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:10 say:

أَرَأَيْتَ الَّذِي يَنْهَىٰ

a-raʾayta alladhī yanhā

“Have you seen the one who prevents?”

عَبْدًا إِذَا صَلَّىٰ

ʿabdan idhā ṣallā

“A servant when he prays?”

Al-ʿAlaq 96:11 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:12 ask about being on guidance and enjoining mindfulness.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:13 then says:

أَرَأَيْتَ إِنْ كَذَّبَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ

a-raʾayta in kadhdhaba wa-tawallā

“Have you seen if he denies and turns away?”

And Al-ʿAlaq 96:14:

أَلَمْ يَعْلَمْ بِأَنَّ اللَّهَ يَرَىٰ

a-lam yaʿlam bi-anna allāha yarā

“Does he not know that The God sees?”

The warning about the forelock therefore concerns someone preventing another from prayer, in a setting of denial and turning away.

The commentarial tradition commonly identifies that person as Abū Jahl.

The verses themselves do not name him.

Understanding the root and the warning’s general message therefore does not require inserting a particular historical name into the translation.

B. La-nasfaʿan bi-al-nāṣiyah

La-nasfaʿan belongs to S F ʿ, rather than N Ṣ Y.

That verb has established senses concerning forcible grasping and pulling, and in some uses marking or discoloring.

One early explanation foregrounds seizing the front of the head and humiliating the person.

Another proposes blackening the face.

The verb and noun nevertheless remain distinct.

La-nasfaʿan expresses the seizing or pulling.

Bi-al-nāṣiyah identifies the part of the head involved.

Nāṣiyah itself therefore does not mean violent pulling.

Nor does the verb alone determine every physical detail of how the threat is carried out.

C. Nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭiʾah

Al-ʿAlaq 96:16 qualifies the forelock with two descriptions:

كَاذِبَةٍ

kādhibatin

And:

خَاطِئَةٍ

khāṭiʾatin

Kādhibah belongs to K DH B and concerns lying.

Khāṭiʾah belongs to KH Ṭ ʾ and here concerns wrongdoing or misconduct.

The front hairline is not an independent agent that speaks, invents lies, or bears moral responsibility like a person.

The relationship between these descriptions and the forelock’s owner therefore requires examination.

An established Arabic rhetorical method attributes a person’s quality to a body part.

A “tyrannical hand” or “treacherous eye,” for example, can express conduct for which the person is morally responsible.

The established rhetorical reading here likewise relates the lying and wrongdoing forelock to its owner.

An early rhetorical explanation explicitly states that lying and wrongdoing are attributed to the forelock while its owner is meant.

D. Why the forelock in place of the person?

Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 introduces grasping the forelock.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:16 then qualifies that same forelock with the two descriptions.

Repeating it directly connects the threat with its owner’s behavior.

Someone who prevents another and denies is described through a forelock called lying and wrongdoing.

The image can foreground the humiliation of someone previously asserting power and preventing another’s action.

Humiliation is not an independent N Ṣ Y meaning; it comes from grasping the forelock, the person’s conduct, and the threatening context together.

E. Does the verse locate lying in the brain?

Some contemporary writings infer from nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭiʾah that the Qur’an locates lying and moral decision-making in the brain’s front region.

Several questions must be distinguished when examining that claim.

First, early Arabic nāṣiyah names the front hairline and front of the head.

Second, the verse attributes lying and wrongdoing to that part, but early commentary explains the attribution as concerning its owner.

Third, modern neuroscience studies the roles of brain regions in planning, behavioral control, decisions, and cognitive processes.

The spatial correspondence with the front of the head does not establish that the verse presents a specialist theory about the prefrontal cortex.

The verse does not name the brain, its regions, neural networks, or the mechanisms of lying.

Nor should lying and misconduct be assigned to just one brain region without precise scientific evidence.

Thus the rhetorical and ethical reading has clear linguistic support, while a categorical claim that the verse discovers the brain’s lying location goes beyond its wording and context.

This does not deny the brain’s scientific importance. It distinguishes Qur’anic language from scientific theories requiring their own methods of investigation.

8. Comparison with related words

Nāṣiyah and jabhah

Jabhah concerns the forehead and front of the face.

The precise lexical explanation of nāṣiyah concerns especially the front hairline and the hair there.

The areas are adjacent and can overlap in some uses.

They are nevertheless not complete synonyms.

“Forehead” can communicate the general sense in translation, while the lexical study should explain its particular extent.

Study of J B H

Nāṣiyah and jabīn

Jabīn concerns the side or sides of the forehead.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:103 says:

وَتَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ

wa-tallahu li-al-jabīni

“And he laid him down upon the side of his forehead.”

This word belongs to J B N and differs from nāṣiyah.

Both concerning the head does not justify translating them identically without distinction.

Study of J B N

Nāṣiyah and raʾs

Raʾs names the head and has a broader semantic field.

Nāṣiyah identifies only part of the head or its front hair.

Both can figuratively express leadership and power in Arabic.

That figurative use does not erase their bodily distinction.

Every nāṣiyah relates to the head, but the whole head is not the nāṣiyah.

Study of R ʾ S

Nāṣiyah and wajh

Wajh concerns the face and, in other uses, direction or turning toward something.

Nāṣiyah lies above the face at the front of the head.

Some explanations of Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 propose blackening the face, but this should not make nāṣiyah translate directly as “face.”

Attributing lying to the forelock and mentioning the face within a rhetorical explanation also do not make the words identical.

Study of W J H

Nāṣiyah and quṣṣah

Arabic quṣṣah can identify forehead hair or a lock of hair at the front of the head.

It approaches one use of nāṣiyah in that respect.

Nāṣiyah can also identify the hairline, whereas quṣṣah more particularly concerns the front hair itself.

The distinction helps lexical explanation, even if ordinary translation may render both as “forelock.”

Study of Q Ṣ Ṣ

Nāṣiyah and N Z ʿ

N Z ʿ concerns pulling out or separating.

Grasping a forelock may involve pulling, but nāṣiyah does not belong to N Z ʿ.

Ākhidh in Hūd 11:56 belongs to ʾ KH DH.

La-nasfaʿan in Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 belongs to S F ʿ.

The body part grasped must therefore be distinguished from the action performed on it.

This prevents directly loading every meaning of grasping, pulling, humiliating, and restraining into N Ṣ Y’s definition.

Study of N Z ʿ; Study of ʾ KH DH

9. Translation questions and proposals for Forqan

Is “forehead” suitable?

The Persian “forehead” is natural and accessible.

Precisely, however, nāṣiyah concerns the front hairline and the hair growing there.

The root article should therefore retain the precise definition:

Nāṣiyah: the front of the head, especially the front hairline and the hair growing there.

Verse translation can use nāṣiyah or “forehead” according to the sentence’s fluency.

Where grasping and pulling are involved, retaining nāṣiyah can avoid ambiguity about what is grasped.

Hūd 11:56

مَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ إِلَّا هُوَ آخِذٌ بِنَاصِيَتِهَا

mā min dābbatin illā huwa ākhidhun bi-nāṣiyatihā

A rendering close to the wording:

“There is no moving creature but He holds its forelock.”

Explanation:

Grasping the forelock expresses The God’s power over creation.

“All beings are completely compelled” is unsuitable: that is a judgment about choice, not a meaning supplied by nāṣiyah alone.

Al-Raḥmān 55:41

فَيُؤْخَذُ بِالنَّوَاصِي وَالْأَقْدَامِ

fa-yuʾkhadhu bi-al-nawāṣī wa-al-aqdāmi

Proposal:

“Then they are seized by their forelocks and feet.”

The reflection can examine the wrongdoers’ seizing and humiliation.

Details such as tying hair to feet or the exact manner of moving people should not enter the basic translation without explicit wording.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:15

لَنَسْفَعًا بِالنَّاصِيَةِ

la-nasfaʿan bi-al-nāṣiyati

Proposal:

“We will surely seize and drag him by the forelock.”

The grasping and pulling come from la-nasfaʿan.

Nāṣiyah names the front of the head.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:16

نَاصِيَةٍ كَاذِبَةٍ خَاطِئَةٍ

nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭiʾatin

Proposal:

“A lying, wrongdoing forelock.”

This preserves the verse’s rhetorical construction.

The reflection can clarify that lying and wrongdoing concern its owner.

“A lying, wrongdoing person” in the direct translation removes nāṣiyah and loses the distinctive image.

Nor should the basic text replace it with “prefrontal cortex” or “decision-making center,” terms absent from the verse’s Arabic.

10. Conclusion

The Qur’anic morphological classification identifies N Ṣ Y through nāṣiyah and its plural nawāṣī.

Early lexicons commonly examine the family under naṣā or N Ṣ W and record verbal forms with wāw.

Nāṣiyah basically identifies the front hairline and the hair growing there.

The bodily use supports grasping the forelock as an expression of restraint, control, and, in some settings, humiliation.

Other Arabic uses figuratively identify a group’s prominent figures and leaders.

Lexicons also record naṣiyyah for a group’s selected members and naṣiyy for a plant.

These uses should not be reduced to one certain historical origin without sufficient evidence.

The family occurs four times in the Qur’an.

In Hūd 11:56, Hūd’s image of grasping the forelock expresses The God’s power over all moving creatures.

Al-Raḥmān 55:41 places forelocks alongside feet in the image of seizing wrongdoers at resurrection.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 describes seizing and pulling the forelock of someone preventing another from prayer.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:16 qualifies that same forelock as lying and wrongdoing; in the established rhetorical reading, these descriptions concern its owner.

Nāṣiyah itself consequently means neither power nor lying and sin.

Power comes from grasping the forelock and the context.

Lying and wrongdoing come from the descriptions attributed to it and its owner’s behavior.

Nor does nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭiʾah establish, without independent evidence, a specialist Qur’anic theory locating lying in the frontal cortex.

That would go beyond the lexical meaning and context.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

Arabic nāṣiyah basically means the front of the head, especially the hairline above the forehead and the hair growing there. Its plural is nawāṣī.

Grasping it can express control, restraint, or humiliation, but these meanings arise from the verb and context, not from the anatomical name alone.

Qur’anic nāṣiyah and nawāṣī identify this part of the head, with differing functions in each verse.

Hūd 11:56 expresses divine power; Al-Raḥmān 55:41 depicts seizing wrongdoers; Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:16 connect a threat with attributing lying and wrongdoing to the forelock’s owner.

Thus the front of the head and the front hairline are the family’s shared, direct Qur’anic meaning. Power, humiliation, and moral responsibility must be identified separately from each sentence and context.
