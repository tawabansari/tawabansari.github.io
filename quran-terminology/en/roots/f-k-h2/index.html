Qur’anic root ف ك ه (fakiha)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَاكِهَة، فَوَاكِه
(fākihah, fawākih)
Fruit and fruits

فَاكِهُونَ، فَاكِهِينَ
(fākihūn, fākihīn)
Joyful and enjoying blessings

فَكِهِينَ، تَفَكَّهُونَ
(fakihīn, tafakkahūn)
Merriment; astonishment and regret

Fruit, pleasure, enjoyment, and good humor; from the gifts of creation to gladness and taking pleasure in speech and conduct

The root in early Arabic

The three-letter root ف ك ه has two prominent branches in Arabic: فَاكِهَة, “fruit,” and فَكِهَ and related forms concerning happiness, good humor, jesting, pleasure, and enjoyment of something.

In classical Arabic, فَكِهَ الرَّجُلُ describes someone who is cheerful, laughing, or fond of joking. فَاكِهٌ can also describe someone living in comfort and enjoyment. فَاكِهَة denotes edible fruit, and فَوَاكِه is its plural.

The family also occurs in accounts of ordinary conversation. مُفَاكَهَة denotes joking and pleasant conversation, while تَفَكَّهَ بِالشَّيْءِ can mean enjoying something.

A verse attributed to Ṣakhr ibn ʿAmr al-Sulamī, a pre-Islamic poet and brother of al-Khansāʾ, reads:

فَكِهُ العَشِيِّ إِذَا تَأَوَّبَ رَحْلَهُ

Fakihu al-ʿashiyyi idhā taʾawwaba raḥlahu

رَكْبُ الشِّتَاءِ مُسَامِحٌ بِالْمَيْسِرِ

Rakbu al-shitāʾi musāmiḥun bi-al-maysiri.

“Cheerful in the evening, when winter travelers arrive at his dwelling; generous in maysir.” Here maysir refers to the customary distribution of meat by gaming arrows, a setting in which generosity could be praised.

In this verse, فَكِه describes a person’s manner when receiving others. The usage concerns cheerfulness and good humor.

Later sources attribute the verse to Ṣakhr. The final word is transmitted differently: the reading بِالْمَيْسِرِ is used here, while some copies have بِالْمَسِيرِ. The difference does not affect فَكِه itself, but the translation must follow the chosen wording rather than combine the two readings.

Classical lexicons also record فَكِه for someone who makes others laugh through speech or is good-humored in company. Some uses describe taking pleasure in talking about other people, which may be commendable or blameworthy.

The recognized classical Arabic meaning of فَاكِهَة is edible fruit. This study does not present independent, datable evidence establishing every branch of this noun before the Qur’an; its precise date of emergence should therefore not be asserted with certainty.

Some lexicographers explain a semantic connection between fruit and pleasure: fruit is something people enjoy eating, while pleasant conversation is also a form of enjoyment and diversion. This relationship helps explain the word family, but does not establish that either meaning necessarily developed historically from the other.

A shared idea that may explain much of this family is the pleasantness and enjoyment of something that brings pleasure or gladness. A precise Qur’anic definition must nevertheless keep the noun فاكِهة distinct from descriptions of a person’s state, since one equivalent cannot translate all forms in every verse.

Qur’anic forms and semantic branches

The root occurs in the Qur’an in the following forms:

فَاكِهَة, the noun “fruit”; فَوَاكِه, “fruits”; فَاكِهُونَ and فَاكِهِينَ, describing those enjoying pleasure and comfort; فَكِهِينَ, describing those who are merry or amused; and تَفَكَّهُونَ, an imperfect Form V verb.

These forms occur 19 times in 19 verses: eleven instances of فاكِهة, three of فواكه, three plural forms of فاكه, one فَكِهِينَ, and one تَفَكَّهُونَ.

The nouns فُكَاهَة, “humor,” and مُفَاكَهَة, “joking or pleasant conversation,” and the designation فَاكِهَانِيّ, “fruit seller,” are known in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

Two semantic fields are especially clear in Qur’anic usage:

Fruit available for eating and enjoyment; and a human state of pleasure, comfort, delight, or merriment.

The branches are not unrelated: fruit can be a source of enjoyment. Yet فَاكِهِينَ does not mean “eating fruit” in every verse, nor should فَاكِهَة be translated as any kind of pleasure or entertainment.

Fākihah and fawākih: fruit in earthly life and the Hereafter

Fruit as a product of creation

فَأَنْشَأْنَا لَكُمْ بِهِ جَنَّاتٍ مِنْ نَخِيلٍ وَأَعْنَابٍ لَكُمْ فِيهَا فَوَاكِهُ كَثِيرَةٌ وَمِنْهَا تَأْكُلُونَ

Fa-anshaʾnā lakum bihi jannātin min nakhīlin wa-aʿnābin lakum fīhā fawākihu kathīratun wa-minhā taʾkulūn.

“Then through it We brought forth for you gardens of date palms and grapes; in them you have abundant fruits, and from them you eat.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:19

The verse brings together rain, the emergence of gardens, dates, grapes, abundant fruits, and eating the garden’s produce.

فَوَاكِه refers to fruits, كَثِيرَة expresses their abundance, and تَأْكُلُونَ makes human nourishment from them explicit.

This usage shows that فاكِهة is not merely an abstract idea or symbol of pleasure. In the Qur’an it also names actual food obtained from growing plants.

ʿAbasa 80:31 says:

وَفَاكِهَةً وَأَبًّا

Wa-fākihatan wa-abbā.

“And fruit and pasture.”

ʿAbasa 80:31

This follows a description of plants and the earth’s produce growing. ʿAbasa 80:32 makes clear that the whole is provided for the benefit of human beings and their livestock.

Fruit therefore appears both among earthly foods and in descriptions of the Hereafter’s blessings.

Fruit in the Garden

لَهُمْ فِيهَا فَاكِهَةٌ وَلَهُمْ مَا يَدَّعُونَ

Lahum fīhā fākihatun wa-lahum mā yaddaʿūn.

“There they will have fruit, and they will have whatever they ask for.”

Yā Sīn 36:57

Fruit is one of the blessings of the Garden’s inhabitants. The phrase وَلَهُمْ مَا يَدَّعُونَ extends the scope of their enjoyment beyond fruit.

In al-Ṣāffāt 37:42, فَوَاكِه accompanies the honoring of the Garden’s inhabitants. Ṣād 38:51 mentions plentiful fruit together with drink. Al-Zukhruf 43:73 speaks of abundant fruit from which they eat.

Al-Dukhān 44:55 associates requesting every kind of fruit with security. In al-Ṭūr 52:22, fruit and meat are among the provisions given to the Garden’s inhabitants. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:20 speaks of fruit that they choose, while al-Wāqiʿah 56:32 describes its abundance. Al-Mursalāt 77:42 connects fruits with the desires of those enjoying them.

These uses show that فاكهة names a food blessing in the Qur’an. Describing fruit in the Hereafter does not, however, reveal all its biological properties, manner of growth, or material structure.

The verse’s image is preserved, without transferring details from earthly fruit to the Hereafter when the text does not state them.

Are dates and pomegranates excluded from fākihah?

فِيهِمَا فَاكِهَةٌ وَنَخْلٌ وَرُمَّانٌ

Fīhimā fākihatun wa-nakhlun wa-rummān.

“In both are fruit, date palms, and pomegranates.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:68

Here فاكِهة occurs alongside نخل and رمان.

Some lexical and legal interpretations have inferred from this coordination that dates and pomegranates fall outside the category of fākihah. But mentioning a member separately from a general category does not by itself establish its exclusion.

Arabic can mention a general category first, then name one or more examples separately to emphasize them.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:19 itself speaks of gardens of date palms and grapes containing abundant fruits. This does not establish an exclusive boundary between dates, grapes, and other fruits.

Al-Raḥmān 55:11 also says:

فِيهَا فَاكِهَةٌ وَالنَّخْلُ ذَاتُ الْأَكْمَامِ

Fīhā fākihatun wa-al-nakhlu dhātu al-akmām.

“In it are fruit and date palms with sheathed clusters.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:11

The date palm is named with a description of its structure. Mentioning it independently may therefore draw attention to the tree and its features, rather than deny that dates are fruit.

In al-Raḥmān 55:52, مِنْ كُلِّ فَاكِهَةٍ زَوْجَانِ speaks of two kinds of every fruit. زَوْجَانِ here should not be restricted without evidence to a biological system of sex applying to all fruits.

Fākihah names fruit; dates, grapes, and pomegranates should not be excluded merely because they are mentioned separately.

Fākihūn and fākihīn: pleasure and enjoyment

In the root’s other branch, the subject is not directly fruit, but the human state of enjoyment and pleasure.

إِنَّ أَصْحَابَ الْجَنَّةِ الْيَوْمَ فِي شُغُلٍ فَاكِهُونَ

Inna aṣḥāba al-jannati al-yawma fī shughulin fākihūn.

“Indeed, the inhabitants of the Garden that day will be occupied and joyful.”

Yā Sīn 36:55

فَاكِهُونَ describes the Garden’s inhabitants as living in pleasure and enjoyment.

فِي شُغُلٍ expresses being occupied, while فَاكِهُونَ describes their state. The description itself does not say that they are necessarily eating fruit.

Yā Sīn 36:57 mentions fruit separately two verses later. Equating فَاكِهُونَ with “fruit eaters” therefore restricts the description’s broader meaning.

Al-Ṭūr 52:18 also says:

فَاكِهِينَ بِمَا آتَاهُمْ رَبُّهُمْ وَوَقَاهُمْ رَبُّهُمْ عَذَابَ الْجَحِيمِ

Fākihīna bi-mā ātāhum rabbuhum wa-waqāhum rabbuhum ʿadhāba al-jaḥīm.

“Delighting in what their Lord has given them, and their Lord has protected them from the punishment of the blazing Fire.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:18

فَاكِهِينَ is accompanied by بِمَا آتَاهُمْ رَبُّهُمْ. What their Lord has given them is the object of their delight and enjoyment.

This gift should not be restricted to fruit. The context describes the blessings and comfort of the Garden’s inhabitants, and the continuation names various blessings.

Here فاكِهين can be rendered “delighting,” “enjoying blessings,” or “in pleasure and comfort.”

The same description in earthly life

وَنَعْمَةٍ كَانُوا فِيهَا فَاكِهِينَ

Wa-naʿmatin kānū fīhā fākihīn.

“And comfort in which they used to delight.”

Al-Dukhān 44:27

The verse concerns blessings that Pharaoh’s people enjoyed during their lives and then lost.

The same word family used in al-Ṭūr 52:18 for the delight of the Garden’s inhabitants appears here for people on the other side of the narrative.

The difference in moral value does not come from فَاكِهِينَ itself, but from the conduct and history of those enjoying the blessings.

People can enjoy blessings gratefully, or live in prosperity while turning to oppression and rebellion. The root ف ك ه alone does not determine whether their enjoyment is commendable or blameworthy.

فَاكِه should therefore not always be taken to mean objectionable indulgence or prideful exultation.

Fakihīn and tafakkahūn: merriment, mockery, and regret

Fakihīn in al-Muṭaffifīn: taking pleasure in humiliating others

وَإِذَا انْقَلَبُوا إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِمُ انْقَلَبُوا فَكِهِينَ

Wa-idhā inqalabū ilā ahlihimu inqalabū fakihīn.

“And when they returned to their families, they returned merry.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:31

In the reading used here, فَكِهِينَ has no long vowel after the first consonant. Its pattern differs from فَاكِهِينَ in al-Ṭūr 52:18 and al-Dukhān 44:27; this distinction concerns the reading, not simply whether a full alif is written in the Qur’anic orthography.

In some Arabic uses, فَكِه is more closely associated with good humor, jesting, and merriment, whereas فَاكِه can refer to comfort and enjoyment. The two descriptions nevertheless overlap, and variant forms are also recorded in different readings. No absolute, permanent boundary should therefore be imposed between them.

The context of al-Muṭaffifīn is decisive.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:29 describes offenders laughing at believers. Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30 describes their exchanging winks when passing them. Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:31 then says that they return to their families merry.

In this sequence, their pleasure is linked to mockery of others. They enjoy their humiliating behavior and return to their families in that state.

The direct meaning of فَكِهِينَ is merriment or good humor, but in this context it accompanies mockery of believers.

This distinction shows that joking, laughter, and pleasure are not intrinsically sinful; their subject, direction, and consequences determine whether the conduct is commendable or harmful.

Jesting intended to foster affection and closeness differs from laughter aimed at humiliating and hurting another person. The root can encompass both kinds of merriment, while conduct and context determine their moral value.

Tafakkahūn in al-Wāqiʿah: astonishment and regret after a crop’s destruction

لَوْ نَشَاءُ لَجَعَلْنَاهُ حُطَامًا فَظَلْتُمْ تَفَكَّهُونَ

Law nashāʾu la-jaʿalnāhu ḥuṭāman fa-ẓaltum tafakkahūn.

“If We willed, We could turn it into broken stubble; then you would remain astonished.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:65

The verse follows a discussion of cultivation and the growth of crops. People sow seed, but causing it to grow and reach harvest is not wholly within their control.

The Qur’an depicts a crop turning into stubble before it can be enjoyed. In that situation, تَفَكَّهُونَ cannot simply mean ordinary happiness and enjoyment.

Early lexical reports offer several explanations of this usage: astonishment at what has happened, regret and remorse, and mutual reproach.

The following verses, al-Wāqiʿah 56:66 and al-Wāqiʿah 56:67, express the words of people who see themselves as suffering loss and deprivation. The state described therefore relates to sorrow over the lost crop.

In Arabic, تَفَكَّهَ can concern diversion through conversation, wondering, or enjoying something. Here, talking about the destroyed crop and expressing astonishment and regret fit the context better.

Some lexical explanations understand it as speech mixed with regret: what should have brought enjoyment has become a subject of bitter, regretful conversation.

Another lexical explanation relates تَفَكَّهُونَ to the dialectal form تَفَكَّنُونَ and regret. Reporting that relationship does not prove that the Qur’anic form belongs to another root or that every derivative of ف ك ه must be reinterpreted on that basis.

“You would remain astonished” is one defensible basic translation. The sorrow and regret arising from the crop’s destruction belong in the contextual explanation.

This use shows why a single fixed translation such as “being happy” cannot cover every form of ف ك ه.

Related roots and translation proposals

ف ك ه (fākihah) and ث م ر (thamar)

In its recognized sense, ثَمَر concerns the fruit or produce of trees and other plants. The family also has broader Qur’anic uses concerning yield and possessions.

فَاكِهَة names fruit that people eat and enjoy.

Many examples belong to both categories: a fruit can be both thamar and fākihah. An absolute distinction that makes thamar exclusively economic produce and fākihah exclusively recreational food would therefore be unwarranted.

The pleasantness of fruit does not mean that fākihah must be unnecessary food or a dessert eaten after meals. Dates and other fruits can form an important part of a community’s daily diet.

The main difference is a tendency in usage: thamar highlights the plant’s yield, while fākihah presents fruit as food and something enjoyed. For further detail, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root ث م ر.

ف ك ه (fākihah) and أ ك ل (akala)

أ ك ل concerns eating and consuming, whereas fākihah names something that can be eaten.

In al-Muʾminūn 23:19, فَوَاكِهُ names the fruits and تَأْكُلُونَ describes eating from them. Their semantic roles are therefore distinct.

Being fruit does not mean that something serves only pleasure and has no nutritional value.

ف ك ه (fakiha) and ف ر ح (fariḥa)

ف ر ح concerns joy and happiness. The human-description branch of ف ك ه can express a similar state, while also connecting with good humor, diversion, jesting, and enjoyment.

The roots overlap but are not complete synonyms. Someone may rejoice at good news without joking or engaging in diversion.

Someone may also be merry through mocking another person, as the context of al-Muṭaffifīn 83:31 shows.

ف ك ه (fākih) and ن ع م (naʿima)

ن ع م belongs to the field of blessings, comfort, and enjoyment. Fākih can describe the pleasure of someone enjoying a blessing.

In al-Dukhān 44:27, نَعْمَة and فَاكِهِينَ occur together. Blessing and comfort describe the favorable condition, while فَاكِهِينَ describes the people’s pleasure within it.

Their association does not make the roots identical in meaning.

ف ك ه (fakiha) and س خ ر (sakhira)

The mockery branch of س خ ر denotes ridiculing and belittling others.

فَكِه can express good humor and merriment without humiliation being intrinsic to its meaning. In al-Muṭaffifīn 83:31, however, this state occurs in a context of mocking conduct.

فَكِهِينَ is thus connected with mockery in that verse, but ف ك ه does not itself mean “to mock.”

Proposed equivalents for Forqan

For the fruit forms, “fruit” and “fruits” are clear, natural equivalents. The singular فاكِهة can name fruit generically, while فَوَاكِه is its plural.

Depending on context, فَاكِهُونَ and فَاكِهِينَ can be rendered “joyful,” “merry,” “in pleasure and comfort,” or “enjoying blessings.”

In Yā Sīn 36:55, “occupied in enjoyment” or “occupied and joyful” conveys the meaning.

In al-Ṭūr 52:18, “delighting in what their Lord has given them” is appropriate because the verse itself states the object of delight.

In al-Dukhān 44:27, “in which they used to delight” fits the prosperous life of the people described.

For فَكِهِينَ in al-Muṭaffifīn 83:31, “merry” or “cheerful and amused” is appropriate. Mockery of others should be explained contextually, rather than defining the word as “mocking” in every use.

For تَفَكَّهُونَ in al-Wāqiʿah 56:65, “you would remain astonished” is defensible. “You would lament” is also consistent with lexical explanations and context, but the two readings should not be combined without explanation. The basic translation selects one meaning; the commentary explains the surrounding regret and deprivation.

Conclusion

ف ك ه has two prominent branches in Arabic and the Qur’an: edible fruit, and human pleasure, good humor, and enjoyment.

فَاكِهَة and فَوَاكِه refer to fruits, both in earthly life and in descriptions of the Hereafter’s blessings. They should not be turned into an abstract notion of pleasure, nor should arbitrary boundaries exclude dates or pomegranates.

فَاكِهُونَ and فَاكِهِينَ describe pleasure and enjoyment. This may concern the Garden’s inhabitants or people who enjoyed earthly blessings. Conduct and context determine the moral value.

فَكِهِينَ in al-Muṭaffifīn 83:31 describes the merriment of a group shown in the surrounding verses mocking believers. Their pleasure is blameworthy in that context, but this judgment is not part of the root’s permanent meaning.

تَفَكَّهُونَ in al-Wāqiʿah 56:65 shows that the family can also express astonishment and regret after the destruction of a crop, a sense different from ordinary joy.

Final definition for Forqan:

ف ك ه: a word family with two principal branches: فاكِهة, edible fruit; and فَكِه and فاكِه, concerning cheerfulness, merriment, jesting, comfort, and enjoyment. Depending on usage, تَفَكَّهَ also expresses enjoyment, diversion through conversation, astonishment, or regret.

A connection through pleasantness can be explained, but a single historical origin for all these branches has not been conclusively established. Each derivative must be understood through its form and the context of its verse.
