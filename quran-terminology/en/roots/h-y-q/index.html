An adverse consequence befalling and encompassing someone: when people are caught by the outcome of their own conduct

The Arabic root Ḥ Y Q concerns something befalling and encompassing someone, who becomes caught in its consequences. When harm, trouble, or the adverse outcome of conduct reaches and besets a person, the verb ḥāqa–yaḥīqu is used.

In recognized uses, more than an event simply arriving is involved: it falls upon someone and catches that person in its consequences. Lexicographers therefore explain it through nazala bihi, “it befell him”; aḥāṭa bihi, “it encompassed him”; and lazimahu, “it clung to him.”

The Qur’an uses this root to describe the outcome for people who mocked messengers and their warnings, acted arrogantly toward clear divine signs, or devised schemes to harm others.

In many of these verses, the consequence they denied or mocked eventually befalls them. Fāṭir 35:43 expresses this through a principle concerning evil scheming: wa-lā yaḥīqu al-makru al-sayyiʾu illā bi-ahlihi.

Three matters must be distinguished: ḥāqa itself, expressing befalling and encompassing; the adverse consequence affecting a person; and its relationship to that person’s previous conduct. The Qur’an repeatedly brings these together, but they are not all independent components of the verb’s meaning.

1. Forms and the semantic core

Ḥ Y Q occurs ten times in ten Qur’anic verses. Every occurrence belongs to the Form I verb ḥāqa–yaḥīqu.

The written forms are fa-ḥāqa, wa-ḥāqa, and yaḥīqu.

Fa-ḥāqa occurs twice, in Al-Anʿām 6:10 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:41. Wa-ḥāqa occurs seven times: Hūd 11:8, Al-Naḥl 16:34, Al-Zumar 39:48, Ghāfir 40:45, Ghāfir 40:83, Al-Jāthiyah 45:33, and Al-Aḥqāf 46:26. Yaḥīqu occurs once, in Fāṭir 35:43.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: ten verbal occurrences in ten verses

Initial fa- and wa- in the perfect forms are not root letters. The Qur’an thus has three written forms, but one verb in two morphological forms: perfect and imperfect.

Arabic records other members of the root family:

  • حَاقَ ـ يَحِيقُ (ḥāqa–yaḥīqu): to befall, encompass, or beset someone.
  • حَيْق (ḥayq): befalling and encompassing; also an adverse consequence that besets someone.
  • حُيُوق and حَيَقَان (ḥuyūq and ḥayaqān): other recorded verbal nouns.
  • حَائِق (ḥāʾiq): something encompassing or besetting.
  • أَحَاقَ (aḥāqa): to cause something to befall or encompass someone.
  • مُحَايَقَة (muḥāyaqah): in a limited use, envying or harboring ill will toward another.
  • مَحِيق and مَحْيُوق (maḥīq and maḥyūq): in certain lexical uses, something that has been rubbed.

These forms are recorded in Arabic lexicons, but none occurs in the Qur’an apart from ḥāqa in its perfect and imperfect forms. Nor should all these limited branches be forced into one semantic image without historical examination.

The basic meaning

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris defines the root as follows:

الْحَاءُ وَالْيَاءُ وَالْقَافُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ، وَهُوَ نُزُولُ الشَّيْءِ بِالشَّيْءِ

al-ḥāʾu wa-al-yāʾu wa-al-qāfu kalimatun wāḥidatun wa-huwa nuzūlu al-shayʾi bi-al-shayʾi

That is, Ḥ Y Q denotes something befalling something else. (Al-ḥāʾu wa-al-yāʾu wa-al-qāfu kalimatun wāḥidatun, wa-huwa nuzūlu al-shayʾi bi-al-shayʾi.)

He then gives this example:

حَاقَ بِهِ السُّوءُ يَحِيقُ

ḥāqa bihi al-sūʾu yaḥīqu

That is, evil or trouble befell and beset him. (Ḥāqa bihi al-sūʾu yaḥīqu.)

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: befalling and ḥāqa bihi al-sūʾu

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī explains the same verb through aḥāṭa bihi, “it encompassed him,” combining befalling and encompassing in discussing punishment.

These explanations are not inherently incompatible. Nuzūl emphasizes arrival and occurrence; encompassing emphasizes surrounding or besetting the person. Both dimensions may be present in ḥāqa bihi.

This does not mean that anything befalling someone must lexically be called ḥāqa. Its recognized connotations, particularly in Qur’anic use, concern adversity and something unwelcome.

Nor must encompassing here mean a physical siege from four sides. Trouble can beset a person without a material wall or barrier surrounding them.

Taken together, the evidence supports this semantic core: something befalling and encompassing someone, especially an adverse consequence in which the person becomes caught.

The outcome of conduct returning upon its author is an important use, but the source of that relationship must still be identified in each sentence.

2. Early Arabic usage

Trouble befalling someone

Ibn Durayd’s Jamharat al-Lughah records ḥāqa bihimu al-sharru: evil or trouble befell them.

This use concerns a consequence affecting a person or group.

Al-sharru is what befalls, while the pronoun in bihim identifies those affected.

The verb itself therefore does not specify punishment or the particular kind of trouble. That follows from the subject and context.

Arabic also uses ḥāqa bihi al-balāʾu for affliction arriving and a person becoming caught in it.

These examples approach the Qur’anic uses, but their recording in post-revelation lexicons does not by itself establish exactly when each expression circulated before Islam.

Ḥayq: a consequence that besets a person

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits the following explanation of al-ḥayq from Al-Layth:

مَا حَاقَ بِالْإِنْسَانِ مِنْ مَكْرٍ أَوْ سُوءِ عَمَلٍ يَعْمَلُهُ فَيَنْزِلُ ذَلِكَ بِهِ

mā ḥāqa bi-al-insāni min makrin aw sūʾi ʿamalin yaʿmaluhu fa-yanzilu dhālika bihi

That is, what returns upon someone from their own scheming or evil conduct and befalls them. (Mā ḥāqa bi-al-insāni min makrin aw sūʾi ʿamalin yaʿmaluhu fa-yanzilu dhālika bihi.)

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Layth on ḥayq and consequences

This explanation matters for Qur’anic usage because it connects a person’s act with the consequence in which they are later caught.

For example, people may devise a plan to harm another yet, through that very conduct, become caught in unexpected harm themselves.

An adverse consequence has then befallen the author of the conduct.

Two levels must nevertheless be distinguished: an unwelcome event befalling and encompassing someone, and that event being the outcome of the person’s previous conduct.

The first is closer to the verb’s general meaning. The second emerges in uses such as ḥāqa bihi makruhu or ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna.

The relationship between recompense and conduct should therefore not be imposed on every possible use without attending to the sentence’s construction.

A consequence clinging to someone

Lexicons also explain ḥāqa bihi al-amru through lazimahu wa-wajaba ʿalayhi: an affair became binding upon or beset someone.

This emphasizes that the person can no longer regard the affair as detached from themselves.

It is connected with encompassing and befalling, but not every legal obligation or contractual commitment should therefore be called ḥayq.

Being caught in something unwelcome is prominent in recognized usage. Being bound to it is one explanation of the force of that predicament, rather than the root always meaning that a religious ruling has become obligatory.

Does Ḥ Y Q have an independent physical image?

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Azharī considers a possible connection between ḥāqa and ḥūq. One lexical use of ḥūq concerns a rounded, encircling form.

He examines the possibility of wāw and yāʾ alternating, including ḥūq itself deriving from ḥāqa–yaḥīqu: yāʾ would have become wāw because of the preceding ḍammah.

This may help illustrate a connection between encompassing and surrounding, but it is not a certain etymology.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ḥūq and possible yāʾ–wāw alternation

Ḥ W Q and Ḥ Y Q are different entries in the root index. Sound resemblance and an associated image do not by themselves establish that one arose from the other.

A ring or physical enclosure therefore need not be declared the root’s historical origin to understand ḥāqa bihim.

The evidence for befalling, encompassing, and clinging is sufficient to explain the verb’s semantic field.

Limits of pre-Qur’anic evidence

The witnesses in this article do not include an independent poetic verse for ḥāqa–yaḥīqu with a securely established pre-Islamic text, attribution, and date.

This does not mean that the word was absent from pre-Qur’anic Arabic. It means that completing an article does not justify assigning an uncertain verse or proverb to a pre-Islamic poet.

What is securely available is the Qur’an’s explicit use and its explanation in early lexicons such as Jamharat al-Lughah, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Maqāyīs al-Lughah.

Al-Zamakhsharī’s Asās al-Balāghah also records ḥāqa bihi al-makru al-sayyiʾu and describes a scheme besetting its author. This illustrates recognized rhetorical usage, but the work was written after revelation and cannot be an independent pre-Islamic witness.

3. Ḥāqa and the outcome for those who mocked warnings

Most Qur’anic uses of ḥāqa concern mocking messengers or the consequences about which they warned.

Mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna accompanies the verb in eight verses.

This repetition shows the Qur’an describing an outcome that people had previously dismissed or ridiculed.

Al-Anʿām 6:10: what they mocked beset them

وَلَقَدِ اسْتُهْزِئَ بِرُسُلٍ مِنْ قَبْلِكَ فَحَاقَ بِالَّذِينَ سَخِرُوا مِنْهُمْ مَا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ

wa-laqadi istuhziʾa bi-rusulin min qablika fa-ḥāqa bi-alladhīna sakhirū minhum mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“Messengers before you were indeed mocked; then what they used to mock encompassed those who ridiculed them.”

Al-Anʿām 6:10

First, the verse mentions earlier messengers being mocked. It then states the outcome for those who mocked them.

Fa-ḥāqa expresses befalling and encompassing.

Its subject is mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna: what they used to mock.

The central point is that what was previously the object of ridicule now befalls the mockers themselves.

The messengers’ warnings and the deniers’ outcome establish an adverse consequence whose arrival they had not taken seriously.

Yet a direct translation should not silently replace mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna with divine punishment. The verse uses the broader expression “what they used to mock.”

Punishment or the outcome of warnings is a contextual explanation, rather than the lexical meaning of mā itself.

Nor does ḥāqa mean mocking. Ridicule is their previous act; ḥāqa concerns the consequence that subsequently besets them.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:41 repeats the same sentence concerning earlier messengers and their mockers. This places the issue beyond one isolated event, within the messengers’ histories and their opponents’ responses.

Hūd 11:8: delaying a consequence does not negate it

وَلَئِنْ أَخَّرْنَا عَنْهُمُ الْعَذَابَ إِلَىٰ أُمَّةٍ مَعْدُودَةٍ لَيَقُولُنَّ مَا يَحْبِسُهُ أَلَا يَوْمَ يَأْتِيهِمْ لَيْسَ مَصْرُوفًا عَنْهُمْ وَحَاقَ بِهِمْ مَا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ

wa-laʾin akhkharnā ʿanhumu al-ʿadhāba ilā ummatin maʿdūdatin la-yaqūlunna mā yaḥbisuhu alā yawma yaʾtīhim laysa maṣrūfan ʿanhum wa-ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“And if We delay the punishment from them until a numbered term, they will surely say: What is holding it back? Be aware: on the day it comes to them, it will not be turned away from them, and what they used to mock will encompass them.”

Hūd 11:8

This differs significantly from Al-Anʿām 6:10.

Here, punishment is delayed. Those dismissing the warning turn the delay into an occasion for ridicule, asking what prevents its arrival.

The verse then explains that when that day arrives, the punishment will not be turned away.

Its ending contains wa-ḥāqa bihim.

Although morphologically perfect, this verb occurs in a sentence concerning a future day. Its form alone therefore does not establish that the event occurred before the time of speaking.

Qur’anic Arabic sometimes uses the perfect form for a future event presented as certain to occur.

Al-Ṭabarī: the future day and the consequence befalling them

The central point is the difference between postponement and the consequence being ruled out.

Here, what was mocked becomes more than speech or warning: it becomes a reality in which the person is caught.

Laysa maṣrūfan ʿanhum also expresses the punishment not being turned away. That follows from the sentence and should not be made an independent component of Ḥ Y Q.

Al-Naḥl 16:34: conduct and its consequence explicitly connected

فَأَصَابَهُمْ سَيِّئَاتُ مَا عَمِلُوا وَحَاقَ بِهِمْ مَا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ

fa-aṣābahum sayyiʾātu mā ʿamilū wa-ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“Then the evils of what they had done reached them, and what they used to mock encompassed them.”

Al-Naḥl 16:34

Two expressions stand together in this verse.

First, fa-aṣābahum sayyiʾātu mā ʿamilū concerns the evils of their conduct reaching them.

Then wa-ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna describes their being encompassed by what they previously mocked.

This juxtaposition helps clarify ḥāqa.

Aṣāba concerns something reaching or striking another. Here, ḥāqa emphasizes people becoming caught in an adverse consequence.

Their juxtaposition does not make aṣāba always a superficial impact or ḥāqa always a complete physical encirclement. No absolute boundary of that kind follows from the lexicons.

The two related expressions describe the outcome from two angles: the consequence of conduct reaching people, and their being encompassed by what they mocked.

Ghāfir 40:83: attachment to their own knowledge and ridicule of warnings

فَلَمَّا جَاءَتْهُمْ رُسُلُهُمْ بِالْبَيِّنَاتِ فَرِحُوا بِمَا عِنْدَهُمْ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ وَحَاقَ بِهِمْ مَا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ

fa-lammā jāʾathum rusuluhum bi-al-bayyināti fariḥū bi-mā ʿindahum mina al-ʿilmi wa-ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“Then, when their messengers came to them with clear signs, they delighted in the knowledge they possessed, and what they used to mock encompassed them.”

Ghāfir 40:83

These people delighted in their own knowledge despite the messengers coming with clear signs.

The familiar expression about their being encompassed by the outcome of their ridicule then follows.

This does not condemn knowledge itself. It concerns people relying on what they possessed in the face of clear signs and treating warnings with ridicule.

Nor does ḥāqa itself mean intellectual arrogance or denial of truth. Those features are understood from the preceding clauses.

Here too, an adverse consequence befalls a group that previously dismissed it.

Al-Zumar 39:48 and Al-Jāthiyah 45:33: deeds disclosed and consequences encompassing people

Al-Zumar 39:48 first describes the evils of what they acquired becoming apparent, followed by ḥāqa bihim.

In Al-Jāthiyah 45:33, the evils of their conduct likewise become apparent, and what they mocked encompasses them.

These verses are closely related in construction and message.

Deeds being disclosed and their consequences encompassing people are connected matters. Past conduct is no longer hidden or forgotten; its result confronts the person.

Badā lahum means becoming apparent and belongs to B D W. Ḥāqa itself does not mean disclosure.

The distinction must be maintained in translation and explanation.

4. The outcome of scheming and punishment befalling people

Fāṭir 35:43: evil scheming and its author’s outcome

اسْتِكْبَارًا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَمَكْرَ السَّيِّئِ وَلَا يَحِيقُ الْمَكْرُ السَّيِّئُ إِلَّا بِأَهْلِهِ فَهَلْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا سُنَّتَ الْأَوَّلِينَ فَلَنْ تَجِدَ لِسُنَّتِ اللَّهِ تَبْدِيلًا وَلَنْ تَجِدَ لِسُنَّتِ اللَّهِ تَحْوِيلًا

istikbāran fī al-arḍi wa-makra al-sayyiʾi wa-lā yaḥīqu al-makru al-sayyiʾu illā bi-ahlihi fa-hal yanẓurūna illā sunnata al-awwalīna fa-lan tajida li-sunnati allāhi tabdīlan wa-lan tajida li-sunnati allāhi taḥwīlan

Proposed translation for this study:

“Out of arrogance in the land and evil scheming; and evil scheming besets none but its own authors. Do they then await anything but the way of earlier peoples? You will never find any substitution in The God’s established way, nor will you find any diversion of it.”

Fāṭir 35:43

This is a prominent use of Ḥ Y Q: unlike many other verses, it uses the imperfect yaḥīqu.

Its opening, istikbāran fī al-arḍi and makra al-sayyiʾi, continues the preceding verse. Fāṭir 35:42 describes people swearing emphatic oaths, yet becoming more averse when a warner came.

Fāṭir 35:43 then mentions arrogance and evil scheming, and says:

«وَلَا يَحِيقُ الْمَكْرُ السَّيِّئُ إِلَّا بِأَهْلِهِ» (Wa-lā yaḥīqu al-makru al-sayyiʾu illā bi-ahlihi.)

Al-makru al-sayyiʾu is the subject of yaḥīqu.

Evil scheming is thus what besets the person or group.

Bi-ahlihi identifies those upon whom the consequence returns: its authors and perpetrators.

Unlike the verses about ridicule, the relationship between wrongful conduct and its author is stated directly.

Illā bi-ahlihi emphasizes that relationship through an exclusive construction.

The sentence shows an outcome of evil scheming from which its author cannot dissociate their responsibility.

Does this mean that evil schemes never harm the innocent?

That conclusion does not follow from the Qur’an as a whole.

Evil schemes can harm others in worldly life. Innocent people may fall victim to deceit, corruption, aggression, or unfair decisions.

Reading wa-lā yaḥīqu al-makru al-sayyiʾu illā bi-ahlihi as saying that no scheme ever harms its victims would conflict with the reality of wrongdoing and numerous verses about harming others.

The main distinction is between an act’s direct harm to its victim and its moral outcome and reckoning for the perpetrator.

The verse concerns the ḥayq of evil scheming: the consequence that ultimately besets its authors.

It can thus be understood that even a schemer who achieves an immediate aim remains subject to the act’s consequences and responsibility.

This also fits the following reference to sunnata al-awwalīna and the unchanging character of The God’s established way.

Yet the verse does not establish that every schemer must be punished in this life with exactly the same kind and degree of harm inflicted. The consequence’s timing and form are separate from the meaning of yaḥīqu.

The verse therefore neither licenses indifference to victims nor provides a formula for dating every oppressor’s downfall.

Al-Ṭabarī: the outcome of evil scheming for its authors

Ghāfir 40:45: their scheme was averted, and punishment beset them

فَوَقَاهُ اللَّهُ سَيِّئَاتِ مَا مَكَرُوا وَحَاقَ بِآلِ فِرْعَوْنَ سُوءُ الْعَذَابِ

fa-waqāhu allāhu sayyiʾāti mā makarū wa-ḥāqa bi-āli firʿawna sūʾu al-ʿadhābi

Proposed translation for this study:

“Then The God protected him from the evils of what they plotted, and grievous punishment encompassed Pharaoh’s household and followers.”

Ghāfir 40:45

Two events are contrasted here.

First, The God protects the person concerned from the evils of the enemies’ scheme.

Second, an adverse punishment befalls Pharaoh’s people.

Unlike mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna, the subject of ḥāqa is explicitly named:

«سُوءُ الْعَذَابِ» (Sūʾu al-ʿadhābi.)

That is, grievous punishment.

Ḥāqa itself therefore does not mean scheming. Scheming appears in the first clause; punishment in the second is what besets Pharaoh’s people.

Nor should ḥāqa be restricted to an act returning upon its author: here it directly expresses punishment befalling and encompassing people.

Its relation to Pharaoh’s people’s previous conduct follows from context, while the verb concerns befalling and besetting.

The next verse, Ghāfir 40:46, continues their outcome through exposure to the Fire and the day the Hour arises. The consequence should therefore not be reduced to a single worldly event without contextual examination.

Al-Ṭabarī: protection of the believer and punishment encompassing Pharaoh’s people

Al-Aḥqāf 46:26: power and resources did not avert the outcome

وَلَقَدْ مَكَّنَّاهُمْ فِيمَا إِنْ مَكَّنَّاكُمْ فِيهِ وَجَعَلْنَا لَهُمْ سَمْعًا وَأَبْصَارًا وَأَفْئِدَةً فَمَا أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُمْ سَمْعُهُمْ وَلَا أَبْصَارُهُمْ وَلَا أَفْئِدَتُهُمْ مِنْ شَيْءٍ إِذْ كَانُوا يَجْحَدُونَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَحَاقَ بِهِمْ مَا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ

wa-laqad makkannāhum fī-mā in makkannākum fīhi wa-jaʿalnā lahum samʿan wa-abṣāran wa-afʾidatan fa-mā aghnā ʿanhum samʿuhum wa-lā abṣāruhum wa-lā afʾidatuhum min shayʾin idh kānū yajḥadūna bi-āyāti allāhi wa-ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“We had indeed established them in things in which We have not established you, and gave them hearing, eyes, and hearts. Yet their hearing, eyes, and hearts availed them nothing, because they denied The God’s signs; and what they used to mock encompassed them.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:26

Human resources and capacities appear alongside the outcome of denial and ridicule.

The group possessed power, hearing, sight, and hearts, but these could not avert the consequence they faced.

This does not make hearing, sight, or knowledge inherently worthless. The verse concerns people who denied The God’s signs despite possessing these capacities.

Nor does ḥāqa mean their hearing being destroyed or bodily organs ceasing to function. It concerns their being encompassed by what they mocked.

The preceding verses concern ʿĀd and the warnings they received. The outcome belongs within that account.

This use shows that power and capacity do not themselves guarantee immunity from the consequences of conduct.

5. Distinguishing ḥāqa from related words

Ḥ Y Q (ḥayq) and Ḥ W Ṭ (ḥawṭ): adversity befalling people and encompassing

The lexicons sometimes explain ḥāqa through aḥāṭa.

The two roots do not have identical semantic fields, however.

Encompassing can concern surrounding, being around something, comprehensive awareness, or control.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:19 uses it for the condition of a group of disbelievers, while Al-Baqarah 2:255 discusses divine knowledge through a construction concerning encompassing and awareness.

Qur’anic ḥāqa, however, concerns an adverse consequence befalling and encompassing people.

Every Qur’anic ḥayq thus involves a kind of encompassing, but not every encompassing is ḥayq.

Divine knowledge encompassing something does not mean a person becoming caught in punishment or a scheme.

Nor does the lexical explanation aḥāṭa bihi require punishment physically enclosing someone from four directions in every use.

Study of Ḥ W Ṭ

Ḥ Y Q (ḥayq) and N Z L (nuzūl): descending and becoming caught

Qur’anic nuzūl concerns diverse things, from rain and provision to scripture and punishment.

Qur’anic ḥāqa, however, concerns adverse consequences.

In Ghāfir 40:45, for example, punishment befalls and encompasses Pharaoh’s people.

Ḥāqa thus approaches nuzūl, but cannot replace every use of N Z L.

Beneficial rain falling or scripture being sent down is not, of itself, called ḥayq.

Nuzūl is therefore one element explaining ḥāqa, rather than its complete, invariable equivalent.

Study of N Z L

Ḥ Y Q (ḥayq) and Ṣ W B (iṣābah): a consequence reaching people and encompassing them

Qur’anic aṣāba can concern benefit, harm, calamity, or other events reaching someone.

Al-Naḥl 16:34 places fa-aṣābahum beside wa-ḥāqa bihim.

Aṣāba expresses the consequence reaching them; ḥāqa emphasizes their becoming caught in it.

This does not mean that aṣāba is never comprehensive or that ḥāqa must always be a later chronological stage.

The verbs may describe one outcome from two related perspectives.

What is clear is that Qur’anic ḥāqa here concerns something adverse, whereas aṣāba has a broader range.

Study of Ṣ W B

Ḥ Y Q (ḥayq) and Ḥ Q Q (ḥaqq): becoming caught and right being established

Ḥ Y Q and Ḥ Q Q resemble each other in sound and writing, but are independent roots.

Qur’anic ḥaqq can concern truth, being established, entitlement, and what is right.

Ḥāqa concerns befalling and encompassing.

A consequence may sometimes follow from an established right or judgment, but that contextual relationship does not make the roots synonymous.

For example, ḥāqa bihimu al-ʿadhābu concerns punishment encompassing people; ḥaqqa ʿalayhimu al-qawlu can concern a word or judgment becoming established and binding upon them.

Ḥāqa should therefore not be rendered merely “became right” or “became true.”

Study of Ḥ Q Q

Ḥ Y Q (ḥayq) and M K R (makr): a scheme and its consequence

Fāṭir 35:43 places makr and ḥayq in one sentence.

Makr concerns planning toward an aim. Here, al-sayyiʾ explicitly marks it as evil.

Ḥayq, however, concerns a consequence befalling someone.

Devising an evil scheme and becoming caught in its outcome are therefore distinct.

Planning harm to another belongs to makr; when the plan’s consequence besets its author, ḥayq is at issue.

This is explicit in Fāṭir 35:43, but not every ḥayq concerns scheming: in Ghāfir 40:45, the subject is sūʾu al-ʿadhābi.

Ḥ Y Q (ḥayq) and ʿ W D (ʿawd): returning and a consequence befalling someone

Al-Farrāʾ explains ḥāqa bihim as what they mocked returning upon them in Arabic usage.

This clarifies the verb’s connection with the outcome of conduct.

Yet ʿawd broadly means returning and need not involve adversity or harm.

People may return home or to an activity, or something may return to a previous condition.

Qur’anic ḥāqa, by contrast, concerns an adverse consequence encompassing someone.

Returning therefore fits when explaining the outcome of conduct returning upon its author. It does not make every ḥāqa a spatial return.

6. Morphology and translation proposals

The verb ḥāqa–yaḥīqu

Ḥ Y Q is hollow, with yāʾ as the middle radical.

Its perfect is ḥāqa, and its imperfect is yaḥīqu.

Qur’anic fa-ḥāqa and wa-ḥāqa are third-person masculine singular perfect verbs. Yaḥīqu in Fāṭir 35:43 is third-person masculine singular imperfect.

Initial fa- or wa- connects the sentence with what precedes.

Qur’anic uses commonly combine the verb with bi- and a pronoun or noun identifying those affected:

«حَاقَ بِهِمْ»، «حَاقَ بِالَّذِينَ سَخِرُوا» and «حَاقَ بِآلِ فِرْعَوْنَ» (ḥāqa bihim, ḥāqa bi-alladhīna sakhirū, and ḥāqa bi-āli firʿawna).

The preposition connects the verb with those caught in the consequence.

In wa-ḥāqa bi-āli firʿawna sūʾu al-ʿadhābi, the subject is sūʾu al-ʿadhābi; bi-āli firʿawna identifies those upon whom punishment befalls.

In Fāṭir 35:43, al-makru al-sayyiʾu is the subject of yaḥīqu, while bi-ahlihi identifies those whom the evil scheme besets.

The subject must not be confused with the affected person. Those affected may also have committed the earlier act, but that relationship follows from the whole sentence.

Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable renderings include encompassed, beset, and befell.

“Encompassed” approaches the lexical explanation through surrounding and fits most verses clearly and naturally.

“Beset” is particularly natural for the consequence of someone’s conduct or scheme, conveying their becoming caught in it. Persian also uses the idiom of something taking someone by the collar.

In some sentences, however, “befell them” approaches the sense of descending and occurring more closely, as in wa-ḥāqa bi-āli firʿawna sūʾu al-ʿadhābi.

Different translations should not be generated automatically for every verse unless a real contextual distinction warrants them.

The following equivalents can be proposed:

فَحَاقَ (fa-ḥāqa) — Al-Anʿām 6:10 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:41

  • Brief root meaning: an adverse consequence befalling and encompassing someone.
  • Meaning in these verses: what they mocked encompassed the mockers themselves.

وَحَاقَ (wa-ḥāqa) — Hūd 11:8, Al-Naḥl 16:34, Al-Zumar 39:48, Ghāfir 40:83, Al-Jāthiyah 45:33, and Al-Aḥqāf 46:26

  • Brief root meaning: encompassing and besetting.
  • Meaning in these verses: what was previously mocked befell them and caught them in its consequences.

In Hūd 11:8, a natural rendering can be “will encompass,” given its future context, although the Arabic verb is morphologically perfect. In Al-Zumar 39:48 and Al-Jāthiyah 45:33, the scene of final reckoning must likewise inform the translation’s temporal reference.

وَحَاقَ (wa-ḥāqa) — Ghāfir 40:45

  • Brief root meaning: an adverse consequence befalling and encompassing someone.
  • Meaning in this verse: grievous punishment encompassed Pharaoh’s people.

يَحِيقُ (yaḥīqu) — Fāṭir 35:43

  • Brief root meaning: besetting and encompassing.
  • Meaning in this verse: evil scheming besets its own authors.

This is a suitable equivalent because it naturally expresses the relationship between a scheme and its author without a lengthy addition.

Renderings that restrict the meaning

Always rendering ḥāqa as “was punished” merges the verb’s befalling and encompassing with its judicial or moral outcome.

Always rendering it “besieged” may create an unnecessary image of physical, spatial enclosure.

Always rendering it “returned upon its author” would require further explanation in cases such as Ghāfir 40:45, where punishment is explicitly the subject.

The sentence’s construction must therefore guide the choice.

“Encompassing” is the most suitable general equivalent, while “besetting” can convey a person’s relation to the consequence more naturally when that is prominent.

7. Conclusion

Arabic Ḥ Y Q denotes something befalling and encompassing someone, especially an adverse consequence in which the person becomes caught.

Lexical witnesses explain it through descending, encompassing, and clinging. Each illuminates part of the semantic field, without individually capturing its whole use.

The Qur’an uses the root ten times, only in ḥāqa–yaḥīqu. All concern adverse consequences. In most, those who mocked messengers or divine warnings eventually become caught in what they dismissed.

Fāṭir 35:43 makes the relation between conduct and outcome clearer through wa-lā yaḥīqu al-makru al-sayyiʾu illā bi-ahlihi. Evil scheming may harm others, but its author remains subject to responsibility and the outcome of the act.

Ḥāqa itself nevertheless does not mean ridicule, scheming, denial, or even punishment. These are things associated with the verb in context.

Final definition: Ḥ Y Q denotes something befalling and encompassing someone, especially an adverse consequence that besets the person. Qur’anic uses frequently concern the outcome of evil conduct or the realization of warnings previously dismissed. A consequence returning upon the perpetrator is a prominent relationship, but must be identified from the construction and context, rather than made a fixed component of every form’s meaning. Brief root meaning: Something befalling and encompassing someone, especially an adverse consequence in which the person becomes caught.
