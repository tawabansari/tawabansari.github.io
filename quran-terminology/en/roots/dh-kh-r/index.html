Storing, keeping something for the future, and preserving it for a time of need

Arabic DH KH R concerns keeping something for future use: a person preserves what is currently available instead of consuming or losing it.

Attested forms include dhakhara, iddakhara, dhukhr, dhakhīrah, and madhākhir. They belong to the field of keeping, storing, and preserving things considered valuable or necessary.

Storage may concern food, property, provisions, tools, or anything else someone intends to use later. Extended Arabic uses can also describe knowledge, good deeds, or valuable friendships as reserves.

Storing, concealing, and accumulating are nevertheless distinct. Something may be hidden without having been stored for later use; something kept for the future may also be stored in a clearly visible place.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as taddakhirūna in Āl ʿImrān 3:49. Jesus describes his ability to tell the Children of Israel what they eat and what they store in their homes.

This retains the familiar meaning of storing. The verse concerns not only keeping food, but also reporting matters known to the addressees, enabling them to assess the report’s accuracy.

1. The semantic core and root forms

One early lexical explanation describes the family’s core as follows:

إِحْرَازُ شَيْءٍ يُحْفَظُ

iḥrāzu shayʾin yuḥfaẓ

Meaning: safeguarding and keeping secure something that is preserved.

This emphasizes preservation: something is kept so that it is not lost and remains available for appropriate use. The expression alone does not establish that the verb always denotes two separate events, first acquiring and then keeping something.

Arabic uses the following expressions:

Dhakhara al-shayʾ: he stored or preserved something.

Iddakharahu: he stored it for himself or for later use.

Al-dhukhr: something kept for the future.

Al-dhakhīrah: a reserve or stored item; its plural is dhakhāʾir.

Ikhtārahu and ittakhadhahu are also attested explanations of dhakhara: selecting or taking something for oneself. These are connected with selecting something to preserve, but “selecting” should not replace “storing” in every use.

Dhakhara al-shayʾ and iddakharahu overlap considerably in ordinary usage. Their different verb forms alone do not establish that Form I always means short-term keeping and Form VIII always long-term storage.

The formation of taddakhirūna

The root’s sole Qur’anic form, taddakhirūna, is a second-person plural imperfect verb of Form VIII.

Its corresponding past form is iddakhara.

Early morphological analysis derives it from idhtakhara. Bringing the root’s dhāl together with the Form VIII tāʾ produces sound changes and assimilation, yielding the usual iddakhara.

Similarly, the morphological analysis of taddakhirūna goes back to tadhtakhirūna.

Morphological explanations discuss this change and also record tadhdhakhirūna, with doubled dhāl, in some Arabic usage.

The doubled dāl must therefore not lead to assigning the verb to D KH R. Its root is DH KH R; the consonant change follows from word formation and pronunciation.

Only this Form VIII verb from the family occurs in the Qur’an. Dhakhr, dhakhīrah, dhākhir, and madhākhir are known in Arabic but do not occur as separate Qur’anic words.

2. Early Arabic usage: reserves, food, and property

Keeping something for a time of need

One clear use is storing food and provisions.

People may consume part of their food immediately and keep the rest for later days. In times of abundant harvest, they may also preserve some for periods when obtaining food becomes harder.

Arabic uses dhakhara al-shayʾ for such keeping. The explanation khabaʾahu li-waqti ḥājatihi also occurs: he concealed or preserved it for a time of need.

Iddikhār may therefore accompany concealment, but its purpose is more than removing something from view. Its central concern is preservation for use at another time.

Nor must the stored item have high monetary value. Simple food, water, travel provisions, or anything kept for a future need can constitute a reserve.

Madhākhir: places holding food within the body

Madhākhir is attested for internal parts of the body, particularly the belly and intestines.

Descriptions of the lower belly use malaʾa al-baʿīru madhākhirahu for an animal’s interior filling with food.

The internal organs are described here as places into which food and water enter and remain.

This image is related to storage, but should not be converted into a medical theory of nutrient storage. It concerns Arabic naming and description.

A poetic witness with disputed attribution

One early lexical transmission attributes the following verse to Manẓūr al-Asadī:

فَلَمَّا سَقَيْنَاهَا الْعَكِيسَ تَمَلَّأَتْ
مَذَاخِرُهَا وَازْدَادَ رَشْحًا وَرِيدُهَا

fa-lammā saqaynāhā al-ʿakīsa tamallaʾat / madhākhiruhā wa-zdāda rashḥan warīduhā

Translation: When we gave her al-ʿakīs to drink, her inner parts filled, and perspiration increased around the vein of her neck.

Lexical explanations describe al-ʿakīs as a food or drink made by mixing milk with meat broth or similar ingredients.

Madhākhir here denotes the internal parts filled by eating or drinking.

Two qualifications matter for this witness.

First, the verb has transmitted variants: tamallaʾat in some accounts, tamadhdhaḥat or related forms in others. These describe filling or swelling and do not change madhākhir’s meaning in the witness.

Second, the attribution is uncertain. Alongside attribution to Manẓūr al-Asadī, some transmissions and commentaries assign the verse to al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī.

Biographical accounts identify Manẓūr al-Asadī as an Islamic-era poet, as was al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī. Without further evidence, this verse should not be presented as a securely pre-Qur’anic witness.

Its transmission in early lexical accounts nevertheless documents madhākhir for internal body parts.

A reserve as something valuable

Dhukhr and dhakhīrah are not restricted to food or material property.

A well-known verse transmitted in lexical accounts reads:

لَعَمْرُكَ مَا مَالُ الْفَتَى بِذَخِيرَةٍ
وَلَكِنَّ إِخْوَانَ الصَّفَاءِ الذَّخَائِرُ

la-ʿamruka mā mālu al-fatā bi-dhakhīratin / wa-lākinna ikhwāna al-ṣafāʾi al-dhakhāʾir

Translation: By your life, a person’s wealth is no true reserve; sincere friends are the valuable reserves.

The material meaning of reserve extends here into a social and evaluative sense. Faithful friendships are treated as resources worth preserving.

The lexical transmissions examined do not clearly identify the poet. Neither the date of composition nor a pre-Qur’anic origin can therefore be asserted with certainty.

Despite uncertain authorship, the witness distinguishes the basic meaning from an extended use. In both, something is preserved for its future value or benefit: material possessions or food in one case, a human relationship in the other.

Other Arabic uses describe good deeds as valuable reserves. This extension alone does not make every act of storage inherently morally positive.

Does idhkhir belong to the same root?

Arabic idhkhir names a fragrant plant known in Arabia and used for various everyday purposes.

Because of its consonants, some lexical works discuss it alongside DH KH R.

Some lexical analyses explicitly distinguish this plant name from the semantic family of storing.

Similar spelling alone does not establish that the plant’s name derives from keeping reserves.

Idhkhir names a plant. Any historical connection with dhakhr requires independent evidence. The plant name does not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Taddakhirūna in Āl ʿImrān: reporting what is stored at home

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is Āl ʿImrān 3:49:

وَرَسُولًا إِلَىٰ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ أَنِّي قَدْ جِئْتُكُمْ بِآيَةٍ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ ۖ أَنِّي أَخْلُقُ لَكُمْ مِنَ الطِّينِ كَهَيْئَةِ الطَّيْرِ فَأَنْفُخُ فِيهِ فَيَكُونُ طَيْرًا بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ ۖ وَأُبْرِئُ الْأَكْمَهَ وَالْأَبْرَصَ وَأُحْيِي الْمَوْتَىٰ بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ ۖ وَأُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا تَأْكُلُونَ وَمَا تَدَّخِرُونَ فِي بُيُوتِكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ مُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-rasūlan ilā banī isrāʾīla annī qad jiʾtukum bi-āyatin min rabbikum annī akhluqu lakum mina al-ṭīni ka-hayʾati al-ṭayri fa-anfukhu fīhi fa-yakūnu ṭayran bi-idhni allāhi wa-ubriʾu al-akmaha wa-al-abraṣa wa-uḥyī al-mawtā bi-idhni allāhi wa-unabbiʾukum bi-mā taʾkulūna wa-mā taddakhirūna fī buyūtikum inna fī dhālika la-āyatan lakum in kuntum muʾminīn

Translation: And a messenger to the Children of Israel: “I have brought you a sign from your Lord. I fashion for you from clay the form of a bird, then breathe into it, and it becomes a bird by The God’s permission. I heal the congenitally blind and the person with vitiligo, and bring the dead to life by The God’s permission. I tell you what you eat and what you store in your homes. In this is a sign for you, if you are believers.”

Jesus presents several signs to the Children of Israel: fashioning a form from clay and its becoming a bird by The God’s permission, healing the sick, bringing the dead to life by His permission, and reporting food and household reserves.

Understanding taddakhirūna requires examining the sentence’s construction.

Taʾkulūna and taddakhirūna: eating and storing

Bi-mā taʾkulūna wa-mā taddakhirūna juxtaposes two verbs from different roots.

Taʾkulūna belongs to ʾ K L and concerns eating.

Study of ʾ K L

Taddakhirūna belongs to DH KH R and concerns storing or keeping for the future.

The contrast is clear: some of what is available is consumed, while some remains for later use.

The verse does not require the stored items to be leftovers from the very food someone has eaten. The expressions are joined by wa, “and,” and may concern two categories within the addressees’ lives.

Both verbs are imperfect forms, naturally rendered “what you eat” and “what you store.” Restricting the first to yesterday’s food or the second to one particular day’s reserves requires evidence beyond the verb forms themselves.

Fī buyūtikum: the place of keeping

Fī buyūtikum means “in your homes.”

This specifies where the storing takes place.

The verse concerns things kept in people’s homes, potentially food, provisions, or other possessions intended for use.

Food is a natural, prominent instance because taddakhirūna accompanies taʾkulūna. The wording mā taddakhirūna, however, does not name a particular kind of food.

Being stored at home does not itself mean concealment from others. Household reserves may be visible or hidden.

“What you store in your homes” is therefore more precise than “what you secretly hide from other people,” which adds a motive and manner not specified in the verse.

What constitutes the sign?

In the same verse, Jesus says:

وَأُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا تَأْكُلُونَ وَمَا تَدَّخِرُونَ فِي بُيُوتِكُمْ

wa-unabbiʾukum bi-mā taʾkulūna wa-mā taddakhirūna fī buyūtikum

Meaning: I tell you what you eat and what you store in your homes.

Unabbiʾukum comes from N B ʾ and concerns informing or reporting.

Study of N B ʾ

The main subject is not storing itself, but Jesus reporting matters within the addressees’ lives.

Knowing what people keep in their homes can be a verifiable sign for them, since they know what they actually possess.

The verse does not establish that Jesus independently and without limit knew every hidden human matter at all times.

The Qur’an places this reporting among signs he brought from his Lord. The same verse twice uses bi-idhni allāh, “by The God’s permission,” regarding extraordinary events.

It presents a sign in Jesus’s mission, rather than proving intrinsic, unlimited knowledge of everything unseen.

Al-Māʾidah 5:110 also connects Jesus’s abilities with divine permission and support when recalling his signs.

The Qur’anic report about his ability must therefore be distinguished from wider theories about the manner and limits of his knowledge.

Is storing condemned in this verse?

Āl ʿImrān 3:49 contains no direct prohibition or condemnation of storing.

Jesus does not tell people that keeping food or property is objectionable. He says he can report what they have stored.

The occurrence of taddakhirūna alone therefore does not establish that keeping food reserves is prohibited or objectionable.

Some early interpretations connect the expression with the Table and keeping its food. Āl ʿImrān 3:49 itself does not explicitly make that connection.

The account of the Table likewise requires distinguishing the Qur’an’s statements from details supplied in later reports.

These differences do not alter iddikhār’s meaning: keeping something for future use. Whether a particular instance is right, wrong, allowed, or forbidden depends on that situation’s circumstances and ruling, rather than a fixed ethical value within the root.

4. Distinctions from related roots

DH KH R (dhakhara) and KH B ʾ (khabaʾa): storing and concealing

Study of KH B ʾ

Khabaʾa concerns hiding or keeping something concealed.

Al-Naml 27:25 uses al-khabʾ for what is hidden in the heavens and earth.

Not everything hidden is necessarily a reserve. Something may be out of sight without anyone having set it aside for future use.

Conversely, a reserve can be kept in a visible storehouse or location.

Dhakhr emphasizes preservation for later use, whereas khabʾ highlights concealment or being hidden.

The two may occur in the same action but are not fully synonymous.

DH KH R (dhakhara) and KH Z N (khazana): reserves and storehouses

Study of KH Z N

Khazn also concerns keeping and preserving things. Its Qur’anic forms include khazāʾin.

Yūsuf 12:55, for example, contains khazāʾini al-arḍ.

Khazāʾin concerns places or resources for keeping and administering possessions, whereas iddikhār more particularly expresses setting something aside and preserving it for the future.

The families nevertheless overlap: something may be stored in a treasury and constitute part of its holdings.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute distinction governing every form.

DH KH R (dhakhara) and K N Z (kanaza): storing and accumulating

Arabic kanz concerns gathering and accumulating wealth, especially valuable property.

Al-Tawbah 9:34 describes those who accumulate gold and silver without spending in The God’s way.

The issue is more than possessing reserves: it includes conduct toward wealth and responsibility for spending.

Iddikhār has a wider range, encompassing food, provisions, tools, and nonfinancial matters.

Not every reserve is therefore kanz in the condemned sense of Al-Tawbah 9:34. Its condemnation requires the conduct described in the complete verse.

DH KH R (dhakhara) and D KH R (dakhara): independent roots

Study of D KH R

Distinguishing this family from D KH R is particularly important.

Dakhara, with dāl, concerns humiliation, submission, and being brought low or subdued.

Dākhirūna in Al-Naḥl 16:48 and dākhirīna in Ghāfir 40:60 belong to that root.

Taddakhirūna in Āl ʿImrān 3:49 belongs instead to DH KH R.

Its doubled dāl results from the sound changes of Form VIII. Similar written forms must not lead to confusing the two families.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

“You store” is a precise, natural rendering of the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

The expression:

وَمَا تَدَّخِرُونَ فِي بُيُوتِكُمْ

wa-mā taddakhirūna fī buyūtikum

Translation: “And what you store in your homes.”

This conveys keeping and, in its ordinary sense, the prospect of later use.

“You lay aside” is also close. The Persian alternative andūkhtah mīkonīd may suggest money or accumulating wealth more readily; “you store” corresponds to zakhīrah mīkonīd, naturally covering food, provisions, and other things needed.

“You hide” is not a complete equivalent, since a reserve need not be concealed.

Nor is “you engage in wrongful hoarding” appropriate. Such hoarding is an economic and legal concept with specific conditions, not something established by DH KH R itself.

Forqan’s current translation of Āl ʿImrān 3:49 also uses “store,” corresponding to Persian zakhīrah mīkonīd. No change is needed to that lexical choice.

6. Conclusion

Arabic DH KH R concerns preserving something for future use. Its main sense involves setting aside what is available for an appropriate time or a future need.

The family concerns food, property, and provisions, with extended uses for valuable nonmaterial reserves.

Madhākhir illustrates naming internal body parts through the image of holding food. It neither changes every form’s meaning nor establishes a biological theory or an unsupported history of derivation.

Only taddakhirūna in Āl ʿImrān 3:49 occurs in the Qur’an. It concerns what people keep in their homes for later use.

Storing is not made the subject of an independent command or prohibition. Jesus’s report of matters within his audience’s lives appears among the signs of his mission.

Final definition: DH KH R concerns keeping and safeguarding something for later use or benefit. Iddikhār is this act of preservation; a dhakhīrah is what has been preserved for that purpose.

Concealment, monetary value, quantity, length of keeping, and ethical rightness or wrongness are not necessary components of the root. They must be determined from the particular situation and context.
