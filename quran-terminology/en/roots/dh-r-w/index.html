The root DH R W (dharā)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(tadhrūhu)

Scattering and dispersing; height and elevation; seeking shelter under shade or cover

The Arabic root DH R W has several semantic branches. In one familiar use, wind lifts and scatters soil, dust, chaff or light plant remains. The same field includes words for a scattering wind, winnowing and separating grain from chaff, and particles dispersed by the wind.

Another branch concerns height and the uppermost part of something. Dhurwah denotes the top of a mountain, a hump or other things. Dharā also denotes shelter or cover that protects a person or animal from wind and cold.

These meanings cannot be reduced to one concept without evidence. Two semantic axes can be distinguished: height and cover, and things falling apart in scattered pieces.

Only the scattering branch occurs in the Qur’an. In Al-Kahf 18:45, winds scatter dry plant fragments; in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:1, an oath is sworn by “those that scatter.”

These uses connect scattering with displacement, separation and dispersal. They should not be restricted or expanded to complete destruction, creating beings or every kind of movement.

The Qur’anic forms are tadhrūhu in Al-Kahf 18:45, and al-dhāriyāti and dharwan in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:1. The root thus occurs three times in two verses, in three morphological forms.

1. Semantic branches and usage in early Arabic

Wind lifts and scatters something

Arabic expressions include:

Dharati al-rīḥu al-turāba tadhrūhu dharwan: the wind scattered the soil and carried it away.

Dharati al-rīḥu al-shayʾa: the wind displaced and scattered something.

Rīḥun dhāriyatun: a wind that scatters soil or light things.

An existing thing is displaced by wind, and its parts move in different directions.

The verb is explained by expressions such as aṭārathu, safathu and adhhabathu: the wind sent something flying, scattered it or carried it away from its former place.

Some explanations emphasize stirring up and lifting soil; others emphasize scattering particles and casting them down.

This does not make them two entirely unrelated verbs. Wind blowing across soil or light plant remains can lift them from the ground, carry them away and eventually cast them down elsewhere.

Dharā alone does not express every stage of this movement. The common element is separation and dispersal through movement.

Nor does scattering necessarily destroy the material. Soil or chaff may move away from its original place while remaining as particles or pieces elsewhere.

Winnowing and cleaning grain

In agriculture, dharw and tadhriyah denote a familiar operation: separating grain from chaff and lighter material with wind.

Threshed grain was exposed to moving air. Wind could carry off chaff and light particles, separating them from the heavier grain.

Arabic expressions include:

Dharawtu al-ḥinṭata: I winnowed and cleaned the wheat.

Dharraytu al-ḥabba: I separated grain from chaff by tossing it into the wind.

Al-midhrā or al-midhrāh: a tool for lifting and tossing grain during winnowing.

Al-dhurāwah: what separates and falls away during winnowing.

Scattering in Arabic is therefore not restricted to natural winds. People can also use wind to toss material and separate its parts.

Dharw in its basic sense does not necessarily denote cleaning wheat or producing an agricultural crop. Winnowing is a practical use of scattering and separation.

Winnowing grain must also be distinguished from sowing seed. Both can involve scattering, but one distributes seed in soil for cultivation, while the other exposes grain material to wind for separation. Their purposes and results differ.

DH R W and DH R Y: related Arabic forms

Both tadhrūhu and tadhrīhi are recorded for scattering by wind.

The following expression is recorded for the two forms:

ذَرَتِ الرِّيحُ التُّرَابَ تَذْرُوهُ وَتَذْرِيهِ

dharati al-rīḥu al-turāba tadhrūhu wa-tadhrīhi

That is: the wind scatters the soil, with both verbal forms used in this sense.

The forms occupy one semantic field. Some lexical explanations regard the wāw form as preferable or more common.

This variation concerns Arabic weak verbs, whose final root consonant is wāw or yāʾ and whose forms can approach one another in usage.

The existence of tadhrīhi therefore does not mean that every wāw and yāʾ use necessarily belongs to an entirely independent historical family.

Conversely, this proximity does not permit every word containing DH R to be treated as one root. DH R ʾ, with hamzah, is a different family whose Qur’anic uses chiefly concern creation and bringing into being.

In the Ḥafṣ reading, tadhrūhu in Al-Kahf 18:45 has wāw. A yāʾ form, tadhrīhi, is also transmitted, while some accounts give tudhrīhi, with initial tu-, from Form IV. These transmissions show semantic proximity, but the morphological differences must remain clear. The reading used in the text should not be changed without explanation.

Dhurwah: height and elevation

Another branch appears in dhurwah.

Dhurwatu al-jabali denotes a mountain’s highest part.

Dhurwatu al-sanāmi likewise denotes the top of a camel’s hump.

A recognized plural is dhuran.

By extension, dhurwah also denotes a high rank or prominent standing of a person or group. The move from physical height to elevated status is familiar in Arabic.

Height and scattering nevertheless remain different meanings. Their treatment under DH R W does not establish that one developed directly from the other.

Dharā: shelter and cover against wind

Dharā denotes shelter protecting a person or animal from wind and cold.

For example:

Istadhrā bi-al-shajarati: he took shelter under or behind a tree.

Tadharrā bi-al-ḥāʾiṭi: he sheltered behind a wall from wind and cold.

Fulānun fī dharā fulānin: someone is under another person’s shelter and protection.

In pastoral life, people could gather branches or use trees and walls to shelter livestock from cold wind.

This relates to the height-and-cover branch: something stands against the wind, leaving a protected space behind it for people or animals.

Seeking shelter does not occur among this root’s Qur’anic derivatives. Al-dhāriyāt at the opening of the surah therefore cannot be translated as “those providing shelter.”

2. Witnesses from early poetry

Aws ibn Ḥajar: the loss of a tooth’s sharpness

Aws ibn Ḥajar, a pre-Islamic poet, describes the succession of leaders among his people:

إِذَا مُقْرَمٌ مِنَّا ذَرَا حَدُّ نَابِهِ
تَخَمَّطَ فِينَا نَابُ آخَرَ مُقْرَمِ

idhā muqramun minnā dharā ḥaddu nābihi / takhammaṭa fīnā nābu ākhara muqram

Translation: Whenever one of our great leaders loses the sharpness of his tooth, another rises among us with strength and vigor.

Muqram describes a great leader likened to a powerful breeding camel.

Dharā ḥaddu nābihi concerns losing the sharp edge of a tooth. Through this image, the poet describes one leader’s declining strength and contrasts it with the emergence of a strong successor.

Dharā here has been explained as becoming blunt, breaking at the edge or falling away. It should not be translated as a tooth being scattered in the wind.

This illustrates a use concerning the loss or separation of part of something. It does not by itself establish that scattering by wind developed from this use.

Transmissions vary between idhā muqramun and wa-in muqramun, and in some words of the second hemistich. The main theme and dharā ḥaddu nābihi are recognized in the transmitted lexical witnesses.

Ibn Aḥmar: wind like a sieve

ʿAmr ibn Aḥmar al-Bāhilī, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, describes wind:

لَهَا مُنْخُلٌ تُذْرِي إِذَا عَصَفَتْ بِهِ
أَهَابِيَ سَفْسَافٍ مِنَ التُّرْبِ تَوْأَمِ

lahā munkhulun tudhrī idhā ʿaṣafat bihi / ahābiya safsāfin mina al-turbi tawʾam

Translation: The wind seems to have a sieve with which, when it blows fiercely, it scatters and casts down fine particles of soil.

The wind is likened to someone using a sieve to separate fine particles from coarser material.

Munkhul denotes a sieve or separating tool; safsāf here concerns fine, loose soil.

Tudhrī in this vocalization, with initial tu-, is from adhrā yudhrī, Form IV. In the poetic image it concerns casting down or shedding. Its morphology should not be equated with tadhrīhi, with initial ta-, from the yāʾ-final Form I verb.

In the explanation of the image, the sieve passes fine material through and retains coarser pieces. Casting down and shedding are therefore prominent here. These are close to scattering, but every detail of sieving should not be made an invariant meaning of each root derivative.

The witness is particularly relevant to Qur’anic tadhrūhu al-riyāḥu, since both concern dispersal by wind.

The poem’s precise date remains unknown. Ibn Aḥmar’s having lived across both periods does not establish that this verse predates the Qur’an.

Its value lies in a clear, examinable use of the family in Arabic poetry close to the period of revelation.

3. Al-Kahf 18:45: winds scattering dry plant fragments

Al-Kahf 18:45 states:

وَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ مَثَلَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا كَمَاءٍ أَنْزَلْنَاهُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَاخْتَلَطَ بِهِ نَبَاتُ الْأَرْضِ فَأَصْبَحَ هَشِيمًا تَذْرُوهُ الرِّيَاحُ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُقْتَدِرًا

wa-iḍrib lahum mathala al-ḥayāti al-dunyā ka-māʾin anzalnāhu mina al-samāʾi fa-ikhtalaṭa bihi nabātu al-arḍi fa-aṣbaḥa hashīman tadhrūhu al-riyāḥu wa-kāna allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin muqtadirā

Translation: Set forth for them the example of worldly life: like water We sent down from the sky, with which the earth’s vegetation mingled; then it became dry plant fragments that the winds scatter. The God is powerful over everything.

This is one of the clearest Qur’anic witnesses for DH R W because it names both the action and its agent.

Tadhrūhu is an imperfect verb from dharā yadhrū. The pronoun hu refers to vegetation that has become dry, brittle fragments.

Al-riyāḥu is the subject: the winds perform the scattering.

Scattering differs from drying and crumbling

The sequence of events matters for understanding the verb.

Water first descends from the sky. The earth’s plants mingle with it and grow. The vegetation then becomes hashīman: dry, crumbled plant material.

The winds subsequently scatter those fragments.

Tadhrūhu itself therefore does not mean drying or crumbling. Those features are expressed by hashīman and the stage preceding the verb.

Dharw here acts upon material ready to be dispersed. The winds carry light, dry parts away from their place.

This distinction matters for translation. Rendering tadhrūhu merely as “destroys it” obscures the difference between dispersal and the material’s ceasing to exist.

In the verse’s image, plant remains are dispersed by wind. It does not require every part to be physically annihilated.

Dharw in the example of worldly life

The verse opens with wa-iḍrib lahum mathala al-ḥayāti al-dunyā: the image is given as an example concerning worldly life.

Al-Kahf 18:32, Al-Kahf 18:34, Al-Kahf 18:35, Al-Kahf 18:36, Al-Kahf 18:39 and Al-Kahf 18:42 connect the two-garden story with wealth and children, confidence in lasting prosperity and reminders of The God’s power.

The following verse, Al-Kahf 18:46, calls wealth and children the adornment of worldly life and sets enduring good deeds alongside them.

The vegetation-and-wind image thus shows that what appears green, prosperous and secure in worldly life may not endure.

The example’s conclusion should not be imported into the root’s meaning. Dharw alone does not mean the destruction of wealth, worldly life’s worthlessness or the decline of human power.

The verb expresses scattering. The message about the transience of worldly advantages comes from the whole image and its context.

Nor does the verse describe worldly life as inherently worthless or forbidden. It emphasizes the impermanence and limits of advantages that people might regard as their ultimate support.

Is wind a sign of punishment?

Wind scatters the plant fragments in this verse.

Elsewhere the Qur’an mentions winds in different settings: with rain, ships’ movement and mercy, and with harm and destruction.

Dharw itself therefore does not establish that wind is an instrument of punishment in every occurrence.

In Al-Kahf 18:45, wind belongs to a natural image illustrating worldly life’s transience. This does not make every strong wind or dispersal of soil and plants a divine penalty upon humans.

4. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:1: an oath by those that scatter

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:1 states:

وَالذَّارِيَاتِ ذَرْوًا

wa-al-dhāriyāti dharwā

Translation: By those that scatter, scattering.

This contains the root’s other two Qur’anic derivatives.

Al-dhāriyāti is a feminine plural active participle from the family of dharā, denoting those who or that perform scattering.

Dharwan is the family’s accusative verbal noun, functioning here as a cognate accusative emphasizing the act of scattering.

Structurally, the expression thus swears by those that perform scattering.

Who or what are those that scatter?

Unlike Al-Kahf 18:45, this verse does not explicitly contain al-riyāḥ, “the winds.”

Al-dhāriyāt describes agents of scattering; the description alone does not explicitly name the agent.

Nevertheless, rīḥun dhāriyatun and related expressions are recognized for a scattering wind. Al-Kahf 18:45 also explicitly identifies winds as the agents of dharw.

Accordingly, winds are a clear and strongly defensible referent for al-dhāriyāt on linguistic and Qur’anic grounds.

Early explanations likewise predominantly interpret al-dhāriyāt as winds.

Two levels should still be distinguished: the wording al-dhāriyāt means “those that scatter,” while identifying them as winds is supported by linguistic and Qur’anic evidence.

This allows the main translation to remain close to the wording and the referent to be explained in its proper place.

Connection with the subsequent oaths

After Al-Dhāriyāt 51:1 come three further expressions in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:2, Al-Dhāriyāt 51:3 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:4:

فَالْحَامِلَاتِ وِقْرًا

fa-al-ḥāmilāti wiqrā

Translation: Then by those that carry a load.

فَالْجَارِيَاتِ يُسْرًا

fa-al-jāriyāti yusrā

Translation: Then by those that move with ease.

فَالْمُقَسِّمَاتِ أَمْرًا

fa-al-muqassimāti amrā

Translation: Then by those that apportion a matter.

The expressions form a sequence of oaths. The familiar explanation identifies al-dhāriyāt as winds, al-ḥāmilāt as rain-bearing clouds, al-jāriyāt as ships and al-muqassimāt as angels.

Another reading relates the entire sequence to winds and their effects on clouds, movement and the distribution of rain.

Linguistically, each expression describes performing an action: scattering, carrying, moving and apportioning.

Their adjacency alone therefore does not prove that all name a single entity or necessarily refer to four entirely separate groups.

For DH R W, the primary subject remains the first description. Identifying every subsequent oath requires an independent study of Ḥ M L, J R Y and Q S M and the verses’ connections.

Swearing by scattering and affirming the promise

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:5 states:

إِنَّمَا تُوعَدُونَ لَصَادِقٌ

innamā tūʿadūna la-ṣādiq

Translation: What you are promised is surely true.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:6 also states:

وَإِنَّ الدِّينَ لَوَاقِعٌ

wa-inna al-dīna la-wāqiʿ

Translation: Recompense will certainly occur.

These verses clarify the response to the opening oaths: affirming the promise’s truth and the occurrence of recompense.

Dispersal by wind belongs to the familiar natural order. People observe its effects on soil, dust, plants and light materials.

That image does not warrant a theory that scattered particles necessarily directly depict how the dead are resurrected.

The opening oath concerns scattering, followed by the promise and recompense. The rhetorical connection matters, while dharw itself continues to mean scattering and dispersing.

5. Comparison with related roots

DH R W (dharā) and DH R ʾ (dharaʾa): scattering and bringing into being

Study of DH R ʾ

The roots are very similar in spelling and sound but clearly differ in their Qur’anic uses.

DH R W, with wāw, concerns scattering. In Al-Kahf 18:45, winds carry away parts of dry vegetation.

DH R ʾ, with hamzah, concerns bringing creatures into being and creation in its Qur’anic uses.

In Al-Naḥl 16:13, dharaʾa concerns what The God has brought into being on earth.

In their principal Qur’anic uses, one thus focuses on bringing creatures into being, the other on scattering something already present.

This should not become an absolute law for every historical Arabic use, since agricultural expressions can connect scattering seed with plants’ emergence.

That overlap does not permit treating the roots as identical or changing the clear meaning of tadhrūhu al-riyāḥu.

DH R W (dharā) and DH R R (dharra): scattering and a tiny particle

Study of DH R R

Qur’anic dharrah belongs to DH R R and denotes a very small amount or portion.

Dharw, by comparison, denotes the act of scattering.

What wind scatters may already consist of small particles. This connection does not mean dharw always denotes becoming particles.

In Al-Kahf 18:45, hashīm expresses the vegetation’s dry, crumbled state and tadhrūhu its dispersal.

Crumbling and dispersal are related but distinct concepts.

Dharrah should not simply be equated, through its modern name, with scientific atomic theory or the smallest particle of present-day physics. Its Qur’anic meaning must be established from language and context.

DH R W (dharā) and N S F (nasafa): scattering and uprooting

Study of N S F

Nasafa concerns uprooting, disrupting and dispersing something.

Ṭā Hā 20:105 concerns the nasf of mountains; Ṭā Hā 20:97 uses the verb for dispersing remains into the sea.

Dharw can likewise concern scattering light, detached parts.

Qur’anic nasf appears in scenes involving uprooting and disrupting a thing’s structure, whereas dharw in Al-Kahf 18:45 acts upon dry fragments ready to disperse.

These limited examples do not establish that nasf is always violent or dharw always gentle. Intensity, agency and precise results must be determined from each sentence’s context.

DH R W (dharā) and B TH TH (baththa): scattering and spreading

Study of B TH TH

Baththa is also used for dispersing and spreading creatures in the Qur’an.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:1, baththa concerns the spread of men and women from a pair.

Qur’anic dharw concerns dispersal through movement, particularly by wind.

The roots are close in dispersal, but their subjects and images differ in the Qur’an.

Nor does dharw in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:1 alone establish that those scattering necessarily distribute humans or human generations across the earth. Such an identification requires separate evidence.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Scatter” and “disperse” are the principal natural equivalents for this family’s Qur’anic uses.

In Al-Kahf 18:45, the phrase:

تَذْرُوهُ الرِّيَاحُ

tadhrūhu al-riyāḥ

is appropriately rendered “the winds scatter it.”

This expresses the winds’ action without introducing destruction, burning or crumbling into the verb.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:1, the phrase:

وَالذَّارِيَاتِ ذَرْوًا

wa-al-dhāriyāti dharwā

can be rendered “By those that scatter, scattering.”

This retains the active participle and verbal noun without determining the referent before contextual explanation.

“By the scattering winds” is also a defensible reading with strong linguistic and Qur’anic support, though it introduces winds as the intended referent into the translation.

Forqan’s current Persian wording, meaning “By those that scatter, scattering,” more clearly preserves the boundary between wording and identifying the referent. It does not appear to require change.

Neither Qur’anic occurrence uses these derivatives to mean height, summit, shade or shelter.

7. Conclusion

DH R W has several Arabic branches. One concerns scattering and dispersing things, particularly by wind; others include words for height, elevation and shelter under cover or shade.

Agricultural uses such as winnowing wheat and separating chaff from grain show that dharw can apply to natural phenomena and human activities alike.

Only the scattering branch occurs in the Qur’an.

In Al-Kahf 18:45, winds scatter dry plant remains. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:1 swears by those that scatter. Arabic usage and the Al-Kahf verse make winds a clear and highly defensible referent.

Dharwan in Al-Dhāriyāt is the family’s verbal noun and emphasizes the act of scattering.

Final definition: In Qur’anic usage, DH R W denotes scattering and dispersing something: parts or movable things leave their former place and move in different directions. Wind can perform this action, which does not in itself mean annihilating material or creating something new.

Other Arabic branches concern height and shelter, but those meanings should not be imposed upon the Qur’anic scattering uses.

Translation and interpretation should therefore distinguish the act of scattering, its agent, the material dispersed and the sentence’s figurative or theological message.
