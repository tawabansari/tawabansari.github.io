The Qur’anic Root Ṣ Y R

Reaching an end: maṣīr as the outcome to which affairs lead

The root ص ي ر occurs 29 times in the Qur’an, in a small range of forms: once as the verb تَصِيرُ and 28 times as the noun مَصِير, including الْمَصِير، مَصِيرًا and مَصِيرَكُمْ. The Qur’an does not use this root in the Arabic construction صار كذا, “became such-and-such” or “entered a certain state.” Its sole verb occurs with the directional preposition إلى: إِلَى اللَّهِ تَصِيرُ الْأُمُورُ.

In Qur’anic usage, this family directs attention to what an affair comes to. Sometimes إلى identifies the destination; elsewhere, maṣīr itself names the outcome or destination. Both sentence structure and context matter in identifying the meaning.

An initial definition is therefore:

Ṣ Y R: coming or leading to a destination, state, or end. Maṣīr is the destination or outcome that a person, thing, or affair ultimately reaches. The root itself does not determine whether that outcome is good or bad, spatial or nonspatial, or reached freely or under compulsion.

Qur’anic forms

تَصِيرُ
Imperfect verb; 1 occurrence

مَصِير
Noun; 28 occurrences

Pre-Qur’anic Arabic: awaiting the outcome of a relationship

An important pre-Qur’anic example occurs in the poetry of Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā:

وقد كنت من سلمى سنين ثمانيا
على صير أمر ما يمر وما يحلو

“For eight years, in my dealings with Salmā, I had remained on the brink of an outcome that turned neither bitter nor sweet.”

This verse belongs to Zuhayr’s poem beginning صحا القلب عن سلمى. In the following verse, a visit ends, yet the next day’s desire and expectation remain. In this context, على صير أمر can be understood as “on the brink of an outcome”: the relationship has become neither bitter enough to extinguish hope nor sweet enough to bring union.

Zuhayr’s verse presents a human situation unrelated to the afterlife. Its point is waiting for the relationship’s outcome to become clear, rather than asserting that the relationship has already reached a definitive end. The expression must therefore be read together with ما يمر وما يحلو.

No independent pre-Qur’anic evidence was found that establishes a particular spatial meaning as this root’s earliest physical image. The Qur’anic إلى النار, “to the Fire,” identifies a destination, but does not by itself establish the historical origin of the meaning.

These examples support an association with reaching an outcome or standing on its brink. Zuhayr’s verse does not establish that the outcome has already been completed.

The Qur’an’s only verb: إِلَى اللَّهِ تَصِيرُ الْأُمُورُ

The Qur’an uses a verb from this root only once:

صِرَاطِ اللَّهِ الَّذِي لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ أَلَا إِلَى اللَّهِ تَصِيرُ الْأُمُورُ

“The path of TheGod, to whom belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on earth. Be aware: to TheGod all affairs lead.”

Al-Shūrā 42:53

In تَصِيرُ الْأُمُورُ, إلى specifies the direction in which affairs lead. “Affairs return” can be a contextual rendering; “affairs lead” keeps attention more directly on their ultimate destination.

This matters for the root’s definition. Ṣ Y R need not describe a physical body moving between two points. الأمور, affairs or matters, lead to TheGod. Reaching can therefore concern an outcome, judgment, or the culmination of an affair.

In this use, the root highlights the destination of the movement, rather than describing the route.

Maṣīr: destination, arrival, or outcome?

The other 28 Qur’anic occurrences are all مَصِير. Morphologically, the form can be examined as a mīm-prefixed verbal noun; in use, it can also convey destination or outcome. Its function in a particular verse depends on the sentence in which it occurs.

The Qur’an’s own uses suggest that maṣīr should not be imagined as a physical “place” in every verse.

Sometimes a destination or state is explicitly identified:

فَإِنَّ مَصِيرَكُمْ إِلَى النَّارِ

“For your ultimate destination is the Fire.”

Ibrāhīm 14:30

Concerning the Garden:

قُلْ أَذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ أَمْ جَنَّةُ الْخُلْدِ الَّتِي وُعِدَ الْمُتَّقُونَ كَانَتْ لَهُمْ جَزَاءً وَمَصِيرًا

“Say: Is that better, or the everlasting Garden promised to the mindful? It is their reward and destination.”

Al-Furqān 25:15

Elsewhere, the sentence says:

وَإِلَى اللَّهِ الْمَصِيرُ

Al-Nūr 24:42

This construction does not identify a geographical destination; it relates the culmination of human lives and affairs to TheGod. The surrounding verses supply the sense of judgment and accountability.

Depending on context, maṣīr can therefore be translated as ultimate destination, outcome, end, or sometimes “return.” None should become an automatic equivalent regardless of the verse.

Maṣīr does not inherently mean Hell

Many Qur’anic occurrences of maṣīr concern punishment. Expressions such as:

وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

For example: Al-Baqarah 2:126

and:

وَسَاءَتْ مَصِيرًا

Al-Nisāʾ 4:97 and 4:115; Al-Fatḥ 48:6

occur repeatedly in references to Hell and the Fire.

That frequency can make maṣīr seem inherently negative. The Qur’an’s uses, however, do not support that restriction.

In Al-Furqān 25:15, the Garden is described as:

جَزَاءً وَمَصِيرًا

“a reward and destination.”

In other verses, including Āl ʿImrān 3:28, Al-Nūr 24:42, and Qāf 50:43, the ultimate destination is toward TheGod. These uses likewise do not restrict the word’s meaning to the Fire.

The negative judgment in بِئْسَ الْمَصِير comes from بئس and the destination identified in context, rather than from the root itself.

In ساءت مصيرا, the sentence likewise explicitly describes the destination as bad. The clearest evidence that maṣīr does not inherently mean Hell or a bad outcome is its use for the Garden in Al-Furqān 25:15.

Thus:

Maṣīr conveys an outcome or destination.
Badness is not inherent in that meaning.

The destination and context determine its value.

إِلَى اللَّهِ الْمَصِيرُ: does maṣīr mean “return”?

A recurring Qur’anic construction is:

وَإِلَى اللَّهِ الْمَصِيرُ

Āl ʿImrān 3:28

or:

وَإِلَيْهِ الْمَصِيرُ

Al-Māʾidah 5:18

and:

وَإِلَيْنَا الْمَصِيرُ

Qāf 50:43

This construction occurs in several verses.

It is often translated as “to TheGod is the return.” That is understandable within the Qur’anic context, but the meaning conveyed by the sentence should be distinguished from the root’s own semantic emphasis.

In Ṣ Y R, reaching a state in which a person has previously been is not a necessary part of the meaning.

A person can يصير إلى شيء, ultimately arrive at something, without having previously occupied that state.

Accordingly, in:

إِلَى اللَّهِ الْمَصِيرُ

a rendering such as:

“The ultimate destination is toward TheGod”

or:

“To Him is the final destination”

makes the direction of arrival explicit without asserting prior residence at that destination.

Nevertheless, these expressions genuinely overlap in verses concerning the Hereafter. Their lexical distinction should not be turned into a claim about previous bodily presence with TheGod.

Ṣāra ilā and ṣāra kadhā: different sentence structures

In a grammatical description of Arabic, the following construction expresses a change of state:

صار الشيء كذا

“The thing became such-and-such.”

The Qur’an does not use this construction for Ṣ Y R. Its only verb, تَصِيرُ الْأُمُورُ, occurs in a sentence with إلى. That syntactic distinction is decisive for translation.

The relationship between the two constructions is clear:

When something صار إلى كذا, it has come to a destination.

When something صار كذا, it has entered a state it did not previously occupy.

In a general description of the language, the destination can be a place or a state. Reaching a new state also need not mean that the state is permanent or unchangeable.

The absence of صار كذا from the Qur’an does not establish that the construction arose after the Qur’an. The narrower, firmer point is that Al-Shūrā 42:53 uses صار إلى and should be translated accordingly.

Maṣīr and the choice of “fate” or “destiny”

In English, “fate” or “destiny” can refer to the eventual course or outcome of a life and may sometimes render maṣīr appropriately. But if the reader understands an inevitable, predetermined future, the translation introduces an additional idea.

The issue is therefore not that “fate” or “destiny” is always wrong. What matters is the meaning it conveys in the sentence. “Outcome” or “ultimate destination” generally identifies the end without specifying how it was determined.

The Qur’anic uses of Ṣ Y R do not, on their own, establish that the future is already written.

The word denotes an outcome. How that outcome comes about must be understood from the verse and its relationship to other verses.

Thus, when the Qur’an says:

وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

the more direct meaning is:

“And what an evil destination!”

rather than:

“And what an evil fate has been written for him!”

The root concerns what an affair comes to, rather than a theory of compulsion or predetermination governing its course.

Translation should distinguish describing an outcome from adding an explanation of how it was determined.

Maṣīr and human responsibility

The root alone cannot establish a theory of free will or compulsion. Yet the verses show that maṣīr often follows a description of human conduct and choices.

For example:

وَمَنْ يُشَاقِقِ الرَّسُولَ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُ الْهُدَىٰ وَيَتَّبِعْ غَيْرَ سَبِيلِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ نُوَلِّهِ مَا تَوَلَّىٰ وَنُصْلِهِ جَهَنَّمَ وَسَاءَتْ مَصِيرًا

“Whoever opposes the Messenger after guidance has become clear to him and follows a path other than that of the believers, We shall leave him to what he has turned toward and consign him to the fire of Hell. What an evil destination!”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:115

The verse speaks of guidance becoming clear, opposition to the Messenger, and following a path, then states the outcome of that conduct. The connection between action and consequence is visible in the sequence of the sentence.

This does not mean that choice, action, and desert are themselves components of the lexical meaning of maṣīr. Those elements come from the context.

The verse nevertheless provides a reason not to translate maṣīr automatically as “a fate already written.”

Maṣīr: the destination or the act of arriving?

Analysis of this form should distinguish its verbal-noun sense from its use to denote a destination.

On a verbal-noun reading, maṣīr refers to coming or leading to an end.

On a destination reading, attention falls on what a person or affair reaches. The reference to the Garden in Al-Furqān 25:15 clarifies this perspective.

Qur’anic usage permits attention to both perspectives, without every occurrence having an identical construction.

In:

إِلَيْهِ الْمَصِيرُ

Ghāfir 40:3

one can understand coming to Him and the culmination with Him. The expression itself does not describe the separate stages of the journey.

In:

جَنَّةُ الْخُلْدِ ... كَانَتْ لَهُمْ جَزَاءً وَمَصِيرًا

the Garden itself is the destination reached.

In:

فَإِنَّ مَصِيرَكُمْ إِلَى النَّارِ

إلى النار identifies the destination, while مصيركم expresses the addressees’ coming to it or their outcome.

Consequently, one morphological and semantic analysis should not be imposed on all 28 occurrences without examining each sentence. A form’s status as a verbal noun does not prevent it from denoting a destination or the result of arriving in actual use.

Ṣ Y R compared with R J ʿ

Both families are often translated using “return,” but their emphasis is not identical.

In many uses, ر ج ع highlights returning. For example, فَرَجَعْنَاكَ إِلَىٰ أُمِّكَ in Ṭā Hā 20:40 describes returning Moses to his mother. Still, this family’s entire range cannot be reduced to repeating a bodily movement.

ص ي ر emphasizes the end or result of movement: where an affair eventually leads, or what state it reaches.

Conceptually, returning can also involve reaching a destination. Reaching a destination, however, does not always mean returning to a previous state.

This is a difference of emphasis, not an absolute boundary between the families. In verses about the Hereafter, expressions of return and final destination converge, and translation must also consider context.

Ṣ Y R and maʾāl

مآل, maʾāl, can serve as a gloss for maṣīr: what an affair ultimately comes to. This is a semantic explanation, not independent evidence of the root’s Qur’anic or pre-Qur’anic use.

The two words are close in meaning: both concern what an affair ultimately reaches.

Maʾāl belongs to a different root family with its own history and uses. Their proximity can aid explanation, but does not make every derivative of the two roots fully synonymous.

For an English reader, the Arabic gloss maʾāl still requires explanation. “Outcome” or “ultimate destination” usually communicates the intended meaning more directly.

Translation proposals

For the Qur’an’s sole verb:

أَلَا إِلَى اللَّهِ تَصِيرُ الْأُمُورُ

A proposed rendering is:

“Be aware: to TheGod all affairs lead.”

Or:

“Be aware: the ultimate destination of all affairs is toward TheGod.”

The first retains the verbal construction more directly.

For:

وَإِلَيْكَ الْمَصِيرُ

Al-Baqarah 2:285; Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:4

A basic rendering is:

“And to You is the ultimate destination.”

“And to You is the return” is possible within the Qur’anic context, but the root does not itself require a return to an earlier state.

For:

وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

“And what an evil destination!”

For:

وَسَاءَتْ مَصِيرًا

“What an evil destination!”

Or, depending on the surrounding syntax:

“And it is an evil destination.”

For:

فَإِنَّ مَصِيرَكُمْ إِلَى النَّارِ

“For your ultimate destination is the Fire.”

For the Garden in Al-Furqān 25:15:

كَانَتْ لَهُمْ جَزَاءً وَمَصِيرًا

“It is their reward and destination.”

“Destination” is natural in some contexts, while “outcome” or “end” may suit others. Maṣīr should not always be made to describe a physical place.

“Fate” or “destiny” can work when it means the eventual outcome. If it suggests an already determined future, “outcome” or “ultimate destination” will be more precise.

Conclusion

Ṣ Y R concerns what an affair comes to.

In pre-Qur’anic poetry, Zuhayr speaks of remaining on the brink of a relationship’s outcome: it becomes neither bitter nor sweet. This demonstrates a use unrelated to the afterlife, but establishes neither a physical origin for the root nor that the relationship has already ended.

The Qur’an uses only two forms from this family: تَصِيرُ once, and مَصِير 28 times.

Its sole verbal occurrence says:

إِلَى اللَّهِ تَصِيرُ الْأُمُورُ

All affairs lead to TheGod.

Maṣīr is not inherently negative in the Qur’an. Hell can be بئس المصير, while the Garden is جزاء ومصيرا. The word expresses the end or destination; whether it is good or bad comes from context.

“Return” can be a contextual rendering, but Ṣ Y R does not necessarily express a return to a previous state. “Fate” or “destiny” may also work if it means the outcome; predetermination should not be inferred from the word alone.

Ṣ Y R: coming or leading to a destination, state, or end. Maṣīr is the ultimate destination or outcome reached by a person or affair. The root itself requires neither a return to a previous state nor a good or bad outcome, and establishes no theory of how that outcome was predetermined.
