Root w-ṭ-n — Residence, settlement, and the scene of an event

Qur’anic and lexical forms

مَوَاطِنَ
(mawāṭina)
Settings; Qur’anic plural

مَوْطِن
(mawṭin)
Place of presence; lexical singular

وَطَن
(waṭan)
Residence; non-Qur’anic noun

أَوْطَنَ
(awṭana)
He settled; non-Qur’anic verb

The Arabic root w-ṭ-n relates to residing, settling, and being present in a place. Waṭan denotes where someone lives or makes a home. Mawṭin can denote a residence, but also has a broader meaning: a place where someone is present or takes a stand for a particular purpose.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as مَوَاطِنَ. In al-Tawbah 9:25, The God recalls helping the believers on many fields of encounter, then names the day of Ḥunayn specifically.

Here mawāṭin concerns fields and situations of confrontation, rather than a homeland understood as a country or native land.

This distinction matters because in modern Persian and Arabic, waṭan often evokes a land to which someone belongs, alongside nationality, citizenship, and national affiliation. These modern meanings should not automatically be imposed on Qur’anic mawāṭin.

1. The root in early and classical Arabic

Mawṭin: a place of presence or taking a stand

A clear attestation appears in the Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd:

عَلَىٰ مَوْطِنٍ يَخْشَى الْفَتَىٰ عِنْدَهُ الرَّدَىٰ

ʿalā mawṭinin yakhshā al-fatā ʿindahu al-radā

مَتَىٰ تَعْتَرِكْ فِيهِ الْفَرَائِصُ تُرْعَدِ

matā taʿtarik fīhi al-farāʾiṣu turʿadi

“On a field where a young man fears death, where the flesh near the shoulders quivers in the turmoil of fear.”

In the preceding verse, the poet describes holding himself steady during confrontation and danger.

He then calls the scene of that encounter a mawṭin.

This is not his permanent home. It is a place where he stands in battle, facing the danger of death.

The attestation shows that in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period, mawṭin could denote the scene of an event, particularly a battlefield.

The central idea here is taking a stand in a particular setting, rather than ownership of land or ancestral belonging.

The verse is recorded in familiar transmissions of Ṭarafah’s Muʿallaqah, and early commentaries explain mawṭin as the place of battle. Some wording varies between transmissions, but its application to an encounter remains clear.

Waṭan: a place of residence and living

In classical Arabic, وَطَن denotes a dwelling or place where someone resides.

This may be a home, a settlement, or land chosen as a place to live.

Lexicons record وَطَنَ بِالْمَكَانِ and أَوْطَنَ in the sense of residing.

أَوْطَنَ الْأَرْضَ likewise means that someone made a land their place of residence.

A poem by Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj, of the Umayyad period, states:

كَيْمَا تَرَىٰ أَهْلُ الْعِرَاقِ أَنَّنِي

kaymā tarā ahlu al-ʿIrāqi annanī

أَوْطَنْتُ أَرْضًا لَمْ تَكُنْ مِنْ وَطَنِي

awṭantu arḍan lam takun min waṭanī

“So that the people of Iraq may see that I have settled in a land that was not previously my home.”

Here أَوْطَنْتُ means choosing a place in which to reside.

The poet distinguishes his former home from the land where he has now settled.

This matters because waṭan in Arabic is not necessarily confined to a birthplace.

Someone may be born in one land and later choose another place to live.

Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj belongs to the Umayyad period; this verse is therefore not independent pre-Islamic evidence. The quoted wording is recorded in classical lexicons.

Its meaning of settling and making a home is clear and agrees with recorded classical usage.

Ṭarafah’s pre-Islamic attestation supports mawṭin as a battlefield. Independently dating every detail of waṭan as a residence to before Islam is more difficult. Not all meanings recorded in dictionaries should be assumed to have the same securely established antiquity.

Animals’ awṭān

In Arabic, أَوْطَانُ الْغَنَمِ وَالْبَقَرِ denotes places to which sheep and cattle return and where they rest.

A verse by al-Akhṭal, of the Umayyad period, states:

كُرُّوا إِلَىٰ حَرَّتَيْكُمْ تَعْمُرُونَهُمَا

kurrū ilā ḥarrataykum taʿmurūnahumā

كَمَا تَكُرُّ إِلَىٰ أَوْطَانِهَا الْبَقَرُ

kamā takurru ilā awṭānihā al-baqaru

“Return to your two stony tracts and inhabit them, as cattle return to their familiar places.”

Here awṭān refers to animals returning to a known place.

Waṭan is therefore not restricted to a human-built home. A place to which an animal is accustomed and returns for rest can also receive this name.

This verse also postdates the revelation of the Qur’an and should not be presented as pre-Islamic evidence.

The semantic implication is clear: the living creature’s relationship to a place of residence or return matters in this branch.

Does waṭan always imply permanent residence?

Residence and settlement are prominent in waṭan. Mawṭin, however, can have a broader scope.

Someone may live in a house and regard it as their waṭan.

Yet a field or setting where they appear for a purpose may also be called a mawṭin in that situation, without any intention of living there permanently.

Arabic sources also record mawṭin for any place where someone stands or is present for a particular undertaking.

The two uses can therefore be distinguished:

Waṭan is chiefly associated with a place of residence and living.

Mawṭin may be a residence or the scene of participation in a particular event.

This distinction is decisive for understanding the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

2. Forms and semantic core

Important forms in this Arabic family include:

وَطَن: a residence, home, or land where someone lives.

أَوْطَان: plural of waṭan; places of residence and lands where people live.

مَوْطِن: a place of settlement, presence, or taking a stand; in a particular context, a field of confrontation or battle.

مَوَاطِن: plural of mawṭin; places and settings of presence.

وَطَنَ: to reside or settle in a place.

أَوْطَنَ: to choose a place to reside and make it one’s home.

إِيطَان: making a home and choosing a place to live.

وَطَّنَ: to settle someone or something in a place; also, to prepare oneself for an undertaking.

تَوْطِين: settling or preparing for residence; in tawṭīn al-nafs, preparing and habituating oneself to accept something.

تَوَطَّنَ: to settle somewhere or become accustomed to a situation.

اسْتَوْطَنَ: to choose a place for residence and living.

اسْتِيطَان: settling and making a place one’s residence.

مِيطَان: in an early usage, the starting place from which horses are released in a race.

وَاطَنَ: in some Arabic usages, to form a shared intention with someone to undertake something.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but they do not all occur in the Qur’an.

Modern وَطَنِيَّة, مُواطِن meaning citizen, and مُواطَنَة meaning citizenship have established social and political uses. These concepts must be distinguished from Qur’anic مَوَاطِن.

The Qur’anic form

Only one form of this family occurs in the Qur’an:

مَوَاطِنَ — al-Tawbah 9:25

It is the broken plural of مَوْطِن.

Morphologically, mawṭin is a noun of place: a location of presence or settlement.

Its plural follows the pattern mafāʿil.

In al-Tawbah 9:25, مَوَاطِنَ follows the preposition فِي and is grammatically genitive.

As a diptote plural without the definite article or a following genitive complement, it takes fatḥah rather than kasrah as its genitive marker; hence مَوَاطِنَ.

The adjective كَثِيرَةٍ modifies that noun and has kasrah.

Both words are genitive, but their declension differs: mawāṭin is a diptote, whereas kathīrah declines fully. Their different endings do not change their syntactic relationship.

Mawāṭin and muwāṭin are different forms

In modern Arabic, مُواطِن means a citizen.

The verse instead has مَوَاطِنَ, the plural of مَوْطِن.

The former, in its modern use, names a person.

The Qur’anic form names multiple places or settings.

Thus فِي مَوَاطِنَ كَثِيرَةٍ does not mean “among many citizens.”

This illustrates the importance of vowels and morphological structure in identifying meaning.

Semantic core

A defensible core for this family is:

Settling or residing in a place, or being present and taking a stand somewhere for living or a particular undertaking.

In waṭan, the place of residence is prominent.

In mawṭin, the setting of presence or settlement may be prominent even when the presence is temporary.

Tawṭīn al-nafs applies the idea to preparing and steadying oneself to accept a situation.

These meanings are related, but should not all be confined to permanent residence or national belonging.

3. Al-Tawbah 9:25 — The God’s help on different fields of encounter

لَقَدْ نَصَرَكُمُ اللَّهُ فِي مَوَاطِنَ كَثِيرَةٍ وَيَوْمَ حُنَيْنٍ ۙ إِذْ أَعْجَبَتْكُمْ كَثْرَتُكُمْ فَلَمْ تُغْنِ عَنْكُمْ شَيْئًا وَضَاقَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَرْضُ بِمَا رَحُبَتْ ثُمَّ وَلَّيْتُمْ مُدْبِرِينَ

la-qad naṣarakumu Allāhu fī mawāṭina kathīratin wa-yawma Ḥunaynin idh aʿjabatkum kathratukum fa-lam tughni ʿankum shayʾan wa-ḍāqat ʿalaykumu al-arḍu bi-mā raḥubat thumma wallaytum mudbirīn

Al-Tawbah 9:25

“The God has indeed helped you on many fields, and on the day of Ḥunayn, when your great number impressed you, yet availed you nothing, and the earth, despite its vastness, became narrow for you; then you turned away in retreat.”

Here مَوَاطِنَ refers to settings where the believers faced difficult encounters and experienced The God’s help.

The accompanying verb and clauses are essential to identifying its meaning.

نَصَرَكُمُ belongs to n-ṣ-r and relates to help and victory.

فِي مَوَاطِنَ كَثِيرَةٍ identifies the settings where that help occurred.

وَيَوْمَ حُنَيْنٍ then names a particular event.

The following clauses mention the people’s great number, its failure to benefit them, the earth becoming narrow for them, and their retreat.

Together, these features show that mawāṭin here concerns fields of confrontation and battle.

Mawṭin itself, however, does not mean fighting or winning.

Those ideas emerge from naṣr, the event of Ḥunayn, and the other expressions in the verse.

Why is mawāṭin plural?

مَوَاطِنَ كَثِيرَةٍ refers to multiple places or settings.

Before mentioning Ḥunayn, the Qur’an recalls The God’s help in many settings, rather than naming only one field.

The multiplicity here concerns the fields or occasions of help.

It should not be equated with كَثْرَتُكُمْ later in the verse.

The first expression concerns many settings.

The second concerns the large number of the people addressed.

The two expressions concern different subjects, and recognizing this helps clarify the verse’s connections.

Mawāṭin kathīrah and yawm Ḥunayn

The opening contains a spatial expression:

فِي مَوَاطِنَ كَثِيرَةٍ

fī mawāṭina kathīratin

Al-Tawbah 9:25

That is, “on many fields.”

A temporal expression follows:

وَيَوْمَ حُنَيْنٍ

wa-yawma Ḥunaynin

Al-Tawbah 9:25

That is, “and on the day of Ḥunayn.”

Mawāṭin is the plural of a noun of place.

Yawm is a noun denoting time.

These introduce an event’s setting in two ways: through place and through time.

Mentioning the day of Ḥunayn after the many fields moves the reader’s attention from multiple earlier experiences to a specific event.

Yawm Ḥunayn is therefore not another derivative of w-ṭ-n. It is lexically independent, although it belongs to the same discussion of help and confrontation.

Large numbers and a sense of strength

The verse continues:

إِذْ أَعْجَبَتْكُمْ كَثْرَتُكُمْ

idh aʿjabatkum kathratukum

Al-Tawbah 9:25

That is, when your great number impressed you.

It immediately states that this numerical strength availed them nothing.

These two clauses matter.

A group may feel confident because its forces are numerous.

It may imagine that having more people guarantees victory.

The verse shows that numerical strength alone provides no such guarantee.

While the people were impressed by their numbers, the earth, despite its vastness, became narrow for them and they retreated.

In context, mawāṭin evokes experiences in which people encountered the limits of their own power, beyond simply naming a location.

That conclusion comes from the whole verse, however, rather than the lexical meaning of w-ṭ-n.

Is having numerous forces inherently objectionable?

The verse does not say that large forces or preparation for confrontation are wrong.

Its concern is the confidence the people placed in their numbers, even though those numbers could not rescue them from difficulty.

Possessing resources should therefore be distinguished from relying on them absolutely.

People may have sufficient forces, appropriate preparation, and necessary capabilities; these do not make them immune to error or defeat.

The event of Ḥunayn itself demonstrates this.

Connection with al-Tawbah 9:26

In al-Tawbah 9:26, The God speaks of sending down tranquility upon His Messenger and the believers, and of helping them.

Verse 25 should therefore be read alongside the following verse.

Verse 25 describes difficulty, the ineffectiveness of large numbers, and retreat.

Verse 26 speaks of tranquility and The God’s help.

Recalling Ḥunayn therefore does more than describe defeat or retreat: it brings out the distinction between reliance on visible numbers and The God’s help during the same event.

Mawṭin itself means neither defeat nor victory.

It denotes the setting of an event; what happens there is identified through the verbs and context.

4. Does waṭan mean country and nationality in the Qur’an?

In modern Persian, waṭan commonly denotes a native land or a country to which someone feels they belong.

Modern political language also associates it with nationality, citizenship, national borders, and citizens’ rights and responsibilities.

These uses should not be imposed on the direct meaning of mawāṭin in al-Tawbah 9:25.

This verse does not discuss citizenship, nationality, or national belonging.

It concerns the fields and settings where The God’s help occurred.

Does this absence of the modern sense deny the value of one’s homeland?

No.

A word’s meaning in a particular verse differs from a concept’s value across the Qur’an.

Various verses discuss homes, lands, expulsion from one’s dwelling places, security where people live, and migration.

For example, al-Ḥashr 59:8 mentions people driven from their homes and possessions.

In al-Baqarah 2:246, a group of the Children of Israel speaks of being driven from their homes and children.

These verses show that homes, places of residence, and people’s connections to land and family are not insignificant in the Qur’an.

These concepts nevertheless need to be understood through their own vocabulary.

A shared subject does not make diyār, arḍ, balad, and waṭan identical in every sense.

Nor does the absence of waṭan in its modern political sense imply that every attachment to a place of residence is objectionable.

That question requires a separate examination of verses about homes, land, human rights, and migration.

Birthplace or place of residence?

In Arabic usage, waṭan can be where someone was born or has lived for a long time.

As Ruʾbah’s poem shows, however, someone may also choose a new land in which to settle.

Waṭan is therefore not absolutely confined to a birthplace.

A person may be born in one land, then live for years in another and regard it as home.

Lexically, this fits the idea of making a place one’s residence.

This explanation does not determine political or citizenship rights through etymology. Nationality and citizenship have their own legal frameworks.

5. Comparison with related roots

W-ṭ-n (mawṭin) and w-ṭ-ʾ (mawṭiʾ)

These words are close in form and sound, but derive from different roots.

مَوْطِن belongs to w-ṭ-n.

مَوْطِئ belongs to w-ṭ-ʾ.

Mawṭiʾ denotes a place of stepping: where a foot is set.

Mawṭin concerns a place of presence, settlement, or taking a stand for an undertaking.

Al-Tawbah 9:120 has مَوْطِئًا, a place where someone sets foot.

Al-Tawbah 9:25 has مَوَاطِنَ, fields or settings of presence and confrontation.

The referents can overlap: someone may be present on a field and set foot on its ground.

Yet setting foot somewhere and being present in a situation remain distinguishable ideas.

Mawāṭin in al-Tawbah 9:25 should therefore not be treated as the plural of mawṭiʾ, or assigned the meaning of foot pressure or trampling.

Study of w-ṭ-ʾ

W-ṭ-n (waṭan) and s-k-n (sakan)

In the Qur’an, s-k-n relates to stillness, coming to rest, and dwelling.

Waṭan in its residential use also relates to where someone lives.

The two nevertheless do not share all their meanings.

Sakan can concern becoming calm or ceasing movement without permanent residence being involved.

Waṭan in its familiar use chiefly concerns the place where someone lives.

Mawṭin, by contrast, may denote temporary presence at an event.

Not every Qur’anic occurrence of s-k-n can therefore be translated as homeland or native land.

W-ṭ-n (mawṭin) and q-w-m (maqām)

Maqām belongs to q-w-m and can denote a place of standing, a station, or someone’s position.

In this respect it is close to mawṭin.

In Arabic and Qur’anic usage, however, maqām has a wider range and can concern a place, rank, or state of presence.

In its Qur’anic occurrence, mawṭin refers to particular fields of confrontation.

They may overlap as places of presence, but every meaning of maqām should not be transferred to mawṭin.

W-ṭ-n (waṭan) and b-l-d (balad)

The Qur’an uses balad for a land or a place of collective habitation.

Waṭan in Arabic relates a person’s residence to a place.

One balad may be the waṭan of many people.

Nevertheless, balad and waṭan are not identical.

Balad names a land or place.

Residential waṭan identifies that place in terms of someone’s living or settling there.

Al-Tawbah 9:25 likewise says mawāṭin kathīrah, rather than buldān kathīrah.

Its subject is multiple fields and settings of confrontation, rather than multiple territories understood as countries or settlements.

W-ṭ-n (waṭan) and d-w-r (dār)

In Arabic and the Qur’an, dār denotes a house or dwelling and, in other uses, an abode or realm.

It overlaps with waṭan.

Dār may denote the house or residential enclosure itself, whereas waṭan can cover the wider land or environment where someone resides.

Waṭan therefore need not be a particular building.

Nor is every dār someone’s enduring home; a house may serve as a temporary residence.

W-ṭ-n (wāṭana) and w-ṭ-ʾ (wāṭaʾa)

Some Arabic usages record وَاطَنَ for forming a shared intention to undertake something.

وَاطَأَ, from w-ṭ-ʾ, is used for agreeing or bringing into correspondence.

The forms approach each other in meaning, but have different roots.

In al-Tawbah 9:37, لِيُوَاطِئُوا belongs to w-ṭ-ʾ and concerns matching the number of months.

It should not be assigned to w-ṭ-n.

Nor does residential waṭan inherently mean political agreement, a covenant, or citizenship.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Several Persian equivalents can express مَوَاطِنَ in al-Tawbah 9:25; their senses are:

“Many places.”

“Many fields.”

“Fields of confrontation.”

Of these, “many fields” is natural in translation and fits the context.

The proposed rendering of the main phrase is therefore:

“The God has indeed helped you on many fields, and on the day of Ḥunayn…”

“Battlefields” is also contextually defensible, since the verse discusses help, the number of forces, retreat, and Ḥunayn.

“Many fields,” however, preserves the word’s meaning with little added explanation.

“Many places” is lexically acceptable but may not make the type of setting sufficiently clear to a general reader.

An explanation can identify these as fields and situations of confrontation.

“Many homelands” is unsuitable, since it evokes multiple countries or native lands in modern usage.

“Many citizens” is also wrong: the Qur’anic form is the plural of mawṭin, not the noun citizen.

Other equivalents within the family

Depending on context, وَطَن can be rendered “place of residence,” “land where one lives,” or “homeland.”

أَوْطَان: “homelands,” “places of residence,” or “lands where people live.”

مَوْطِن: “place of presence,” “place of settlement,” or, in a military context, “battlefield.”

أَوْطَنَ الْأَرْضَ: “He made that land his place of residence.”

اسْتَوْطَنَ: “He settled somewhere” or “made it his home.”

وَطَّنَ نَفْسَهُ عَلَى الْأَمْرِ: “He prepared himself for the undertaking” or “habituated himself to accepting it.”

Modern muwāṭanah can be translated “citizenship,” but that legal concept should not be transferred to Qur’anic mawāṭin.

7. Conclusion

In Arabic, w-ṭ-n relates to residence, settlement, and presence in a place.

Waṭan denotes a place where someone lives.

Mawṭin can denote a residence, but more broadly names a place where someone is present for an undertaking.

The poem attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd attests mawṭin as the scene of battle in pre-Islamic language.

Umayyad poetry also illustrates settling and choosing a new land as home.

The root occurs in the Qur’an only as مَوَاطِنَ in al-Tawbah 9:25.

There The God recalls His help on many fields, then specifically names the day of Ḥunayn.

The usage concerns fields and situations of confrontation, rather than homeland as country, native land, or political nationality.

The same verse distinguishes the many fields of help from the people’s numerical strength. Their numbers at Ḥunayn had made them feel powerful, yet did not prevent retreat.

Al-Tawbah 9:26 describes tranquility and The God’s help as the same event continues.

This connection shows that mawṭin is the setting; the verbs and context identify what takes place there.

The final definition is: being settled or residing in a place; and, as mawṭin, a setting of presence or taking a stand for a particular undertaking, whether a residence or the scene of a temporary event such as battle.

This definition keeps the two main uses distinct:

Waṭan: a place of residence and living.

Mawṭin: a setting of presence, settlement, or confrontation in a particular situation.

In translation, mawṭin should also be distinguished from mawṭiʾ, from w-ṭ-ʾ. Mawṭiʾ is a place of stepping; mawṭin is a place where someone is present or stationed for a purpose.

The modern meaning of homeland, with its emotional, social, and political associations, must not replace the historical meaning of Qur’anic mawāṭin.

For Forqan, “on many fields” in al-Tawbah 9:25 is natural and appropriate. The root explanation clarifies that these fields exemplify the broader meaning of mawṭin: settings where people are present and encounter an event.

The core is settling and being present. Permanent residence, land ownership, national belonging, warfare, and victory must be established through context and independent evidence, rather than inferred from the root alone.
