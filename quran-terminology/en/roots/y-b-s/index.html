Qur'anic Root Y B S

Becoming dry or being dry; standing opposite moisture and wetness.

1. Short Introduction to the Root

The root ي ب س in the Qur'an denotes being dry or becoming dry: a state in which water, moisture, or wetness is absent, or has departed from something.

The proposed short meaning of the root is: becoming dry or being dry; being in a state opposite to moisture and wetness.

This root appears in the Qur'an four times and in three forms: يَبَسًا, once describing a path in the sea; يَابِسٍ, once in contrast with رَطْب; and يَابِسَاتٍ, twice describing dry ears of grain.

Qur'anic usage shows that dryness does not carry one fixed value by itself. In the story of Moses, the dry path through the sea is a means of rescue. In the king's dream, the dry ears are connected with difficult years. In Al-An'am 6:59, wet and dry are two sides of an encompassing contrast, both within the knowledge of The God.

Therefore dryness in the Qur'an is neither inherently punishment nor inherently mercy. Its value and function are known from context.

2. Important Forms and Derivatives of the Root

يَبِسَ / يَيْبَسُ

It became dry and lost its moisture. This verb does not occur in the Qur'an, but the Qur'anic descriptive forms belong to this semantic field.

يَابِس

Dry; something that lacks moisture or has lost its moisture. In the Qur'an it appears once in the phrase وَلَا رَطْبٍ وَلَا يَابِسٍ.

يَابِسَات

The feminine plural of يَابِسَة; feminine things that are dry. In the Qur'an it appears twice, both times for dry ears of grain.

يَبَس

Dryness or something dry. This form may be a verbal noun and may also function descriptively. In the Qur'an it appears once in the phrase طَرِيقًا فِي الْبَحْرِ يَبَسًا.

يُبْس / يَبْس / يَبَس

Nominal or verbal-noun forms related to dryness. Lexical sources record different vocalizations and uses for these forms. Their existence alone does not prove that each one expresses a separate degree or an entirely different kind of dryness.

يَبِيس

Something that has dried, especially dry vegetation. This form does not occur in the Qur'an.

4. The Semantic Core of the Root

The defensible semantic core of the root is: absence or loss of moisture, so that something is dry or becomes dry.

This core is preserved in all three Qur'anic forms: the sea path is يَبَس because water does not cover it; يَابِس stands opposite رَطْب; and the ears of grain are يَابِسَات because they have lost their greenness and moisture.

The following meanings should not be inserted into the base definition without contextual evidence: hardness, firmness, evenness, brittleness, death, fruitlessness, famine, or destruction.

These conditions may exist in some dry things, but they do not necessarily follow from the root alone. For example, the dryness of the sea-bed likely fits the possibility of crossing, but 'being passable' is known from the whole event. Likewise, the dry ears in the dream are connected with difficult years, but 'year of famine' is not the lexical meaning of يَابِسَات.

5. The Material Image of the Root

The material image of this root is something in which water or moisture is absent, or from which it has gone: ground from whose surface water has withdrawn; a plant that has lost freshness and wetness; an ear of grain that is no longer green and moist; a path in the middle of the sea that water does not cover.

The clearest material criterion of the root is seen in the Qur'anic contrast between رَطْب and يَابِس: رَطْب means wet and possessing moisture; يَابِس means dry and lacking moisture.

This contrast shows that the main axis of the root is moisture and its absence, not hardness, color, usefulness, or moral value.

6. Use of the Root in Arabic

In Arabic one says: يَبِسَ الشَّيْءُ, meaning that thing became dry; and هُوَ يَابِسٌ, meaning it is dry.

Lexical sources define يُبْس as the opposite of moisture. يَبَس is also used for a dry thing or for dryness.

Some sources say about يَبَس that dryness seems to be in its nature or state. But this explanation should not be made into an absolute rule. Arabic itself uses يَبَس for land, firewood, plants, and things that may previously have had moisture.

On the other hand, يَابِس is the active participle of يَبِسَ and can describe something that has dried or is now in a state of dryness. Still, an active participle does not always give a definite judgment about freshness, temporariness, or the beginning time of the state.

A cautious distinction is therefore: يَبَس can be dryness itself or a compressed description for something dry; يَابِس is an explicit description of something that is dry. From this morphological difference alone one cannot conclude, without context, that يَبَس always means essential and permanent dryness; that يَابِس always means recent, accidental, temporary dryness; or that one is necessarily more intense than the other.

7. Connection with Other Semitic Languages

The root ي ب س belongs to an ancient family in the Semitic languages. In Classical Hebrew, the root י־ב־ש also denotes becoming dry and being dry. Forms such as יָבֵשׁ, meaning dry or dried, and יַבָּשָׁה, meaning dry land or land as opposed to water, are made from it.

This similarity confirms the antiquity and stability of the semantic field of dryness in the Semitic languages.

The overlap between the 'dry path in the sea' in the Qur'an and the use of 'dry land' in Hebrew texts is noteworthy comparatively, but it is only linguistic evidence for a shared field of meaning. It should not be used to conclude that the Qur'an borrowed its phrase directly from a Hebrew text; that all details of the two accounts are the same; or that the Arabic form يَبَس corresponds exactly in morphology to the Hebrew form.

Comparison with Semitic languages should clarify the meaning of the word, not replace analysis of the Qur'an itself.

8. Challenges in Understanding and Rendering

Equating dryness with hardness

Dryness and hardness may accompany one another in one instance, but they are not one meaning. A dry leaf may be brittle; dry soil may be firm; dry cloth may still be soft; stone can be hard without 'becoming dry' being its main description.

In Ta-Ha 20:77, the dry sea-bed had to be suitable for crossing. But translating يَبَس as 'hard' or 'firm' is not translating the word itself; it states one possible contextual consequence. The base translation should remain dry or without water.

Claiming essential dryness in يَبَس

The pattern and form يَبَس may create a more compressed descriptive state than يَابِس, but the claim that يَبَس necessarily denotes original, essential, or never-before-wet dryness goes beyond the evidence.

The sea path had been under water before it opened. If يَبَس is defined as intrinsic dryness with no previous moisture, the context of the verse itself will not fit such an absolute definition.

Claiming recent dryness in يَابِس

يَابِس is morphologically an active participle, but the active participle in Arabic does not always denote a recent and temporary event. In وَلَا رَطْبٍ وَلَا يَابِسٍ, the meaning is all wet and dry things, not only things that have just dried. Therefore 'dried' may suit some contexts, but 'dry' is the more general and safer translation.

Inserting death into the root meaning

A dry plant may be dead, withered, or at the end of its growth cycle, but يابس by itself does not mean dead. The dry path in the sea is not in the domain of living and dead at all.

Equating dryness with fruitlessness

A dry ear of grain may still contain grain. Dryness alone does not prove that the ear is empty, destroyed, or completely fruitless. The connection of the dry ears with difficult years is known from the interpretation of the dream, not from the lexical meaning of يَابِسَات alone.

Turning the contrast of رطب and يابس into a philosophical system

In later philosophy and medicine, moisture and dryness, together with heat and cold, became part of the system of four qualities. But Al-An'am 6:59 first uses the natural and understandable contrast of wet and dry. Introducing the system of elements or humors into this verse without textual evidence imposes a later framework on the Qur'an.

The history of these terms can be studied separately, but it should not be made part of the Qur'anic meaning of رطب and يابس.

Moral valuation of dryness

Dryness is not inherently negative in the Qur'an: the dry path is a means of rescue; the dry ear in the dream is a sign of difficult years; and the dry thing in Al-An'am is simply one of the things known to The God. The value of each use comes from the context.

9. Methodological Note

In examining each verse, one should ask: what is described as dry? What state or thing is dryness set against? Does the verse itself speak of a process of drying, or only describe the dry state? Which outcome comes from the root itself and which comes from context? Is a translation such as hard, firm, dead, or fruitless the meaning of the word, or a possible result? Does the morphological difference really create a provable semantic difference in that verse?

These levels must be kept distinct: direct meaning: dry and lacking moisture; morphological and syntactic role: verbal noun, description, active participle, or descriptive plural; contextual condition: path for crossing, ear set against greenness, or one side of the wet/dry contrast; outcome: rescue, reduced yield, firmness, brittleness, or the end of freshness. The outcome must not replace the direct meaning.

10. Qur'anic Uses of the Root

A dry path through the sea

وَلَقَدْ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ أَنْ أَسْرِ بِعِبَادِي فَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ طَرِيقًا فِي الْبَحْرِ يَبَسًا لَا تَخَافُ دَرَكًا وَلَا تَخْشَىٰ

And We revealed to Moses: Travel by night with My servants, and open for them a dry path in the sea; do not fear being overtaken, and do not be afraid.

Ta-Ha 20:77

يَبَسًا states the condition of the path. A path that should have been under water is now dry and prepared for crossing. The direct meaning is dry and without water. The contextual outcome is being passable.

The verse does not state how the sea-bed dried, how hard or firm it became, how long it remained dry, or what its geological material was. Therefore 'a hard path' or 'a firm path' is not a direct translation of يَبَس. Dryness may have gone along with stability for crossing, but that relation is understood from the event, not from the base meaning of the word.

Suggested translation: open for them a dry path in the heart of the sea. Or, to highlight the contrast between water and path: open for them a waterless path in the heart of the sea. 'A dry path' is more natural and closer to the text; 'a waterless path' makes the visual contrast clearer.

The syntactic position of يَبَسًا

Several analyses of يَبَسًا have been proposed. It may be read descriptively as an adjective of طَرِيقًا: a dry path in the sea. A verbal noun can function descriptively in Arabic, compressing the description as if the thing were dryness itself.

It may also be understood as a state or as a description interpreted as يَابِسًا or ذَا يَبَسٍ: a path in a state of dryness, or a path possessing dryness. Some analyses allow the structure to be understood as producing a path in a dry state. In translation, the main difference does not change: يَبَسًا describes the dry state of the path.

For the Forqan text it is better to say: يَبَسًا is a verbal noun or descriptive form that expresses the dry state of the path. This avoids forcing one disputed syntactic analysis.

The dry path and security

The continuation says: لَا تَخَافُ دَرَكًا وَلَا تَخْشَىٰ. The dryness of the path makes crossing possible, but complete safety does not come only from the condition of the ground. The enemy not overtaking them and divine protection are also present in the structure of the verse. So يَبَس should not be translated as 'safe path'; safety is the result of the whole event, not the meaning of the word itself.

Wet and dry in the knowledge of The God

وَعِنْدَهُ مَفَاتِحُ الْغَيْبِ لَا يَعْلَمُهَا إِلَّا هُوَ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا فِي الْبَرِّ وَالْبَحْرِ وَمَا تَسْقُطُ مِنْ وَرَقَةٍ إِلَّا يَعْلَمُهَا وَلَا حَبَّةٍ فِي ظُلُمَاتِ الْأَرْضِ وَلَا رَطْبٍ وَلَا يَابِسٍ إِلَّا فِي كِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ

The keys of the unseen are with Him; none knows them except Him. He knows what is in the land and the sea. No leaf falls except that He knows it, and no grain in the darknesses of the earth, and no wet thing and no dry thing, except that it is in a clear book.

Al-Anam 6:59

رَطْب and يَابِس are two sides of a contrast: wet and dry. Their placement in a negative structure expresses comprehensiveness: no wet thing, no dry thing, none is outside the knowledge of The God.

This contrast can express the scope of inclusion, but it should not be turned without evidence into other pairs such as living and dead, good and evil, body and spirit, hot and cold, or the four qualities of traditional medicine. The direct meaning is wet and dry; comprehensiveness comes from the sentence structure.

Is يَابِس in this verse “dried”?

Morphologically, يَابِس is an active participle, but in this verse it functions as a general description. If it is limited only to something that dried after being wet, many things known in a dry state are needlessly excluded. The suitable translation is: no wet thing and no dry thing, not: no wet thing and no thing that has just dried.

Seven green ears and seven dry ears

وَقَالَ الْمَلِكُ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ يَا أَيُّهَا الْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِي فِي رُؤْيَايَ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ لِلرُّؤْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ

The king said: I see seven fat cows that seven lean ones are eating, and seven green ears and others dry. O chiefs, give me an opinion about my dream, if you interpret dreams.

Yusuf 12:43

The contrast is between سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ and أُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ. Greenness in this image is joined with freshness and moisture. Dryness stands opposite it, showing the loss of that state.

But in the dream report itself, it has not yet been said that the dry ears are without grain, dead, symbols of famine, or seven specific years. That layer becomes clear in Yusuf's interpretation.

يُوسُفُ أَيُّهَا الصِّدِّيقُ أَفْتِنَا فِي سَبْعِ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعِ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ لَعَلِّي أَرْجِعُ إِلَى النَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

Joseph, O truthful one, give us an opinion about seven fat cows that seven lean ones are eating, and seven green ears and others dry, so that I may return to the people and perhaps they may know.

Yusuf 12:46

The dream is carried to Yusuf with the same contrast between green and dry. Yusuf then connects it to periods of abundance and hardship. Three levels should remain distinct: يَابِسَات means dry ears; the dream image is the contrast between green and dry ears; and the interpretation is the difficult years that come after the years of cultivation.

The difference between خُضْر and يَابِسَات

خُضْر is a broken plural and يَابِسَات is a sound feminine plural. Morphologically, خُضْر is the plural form of the description green, and يَابِسَات is the plural of يَابِسَة; both relate semantically to سُنْبُلَات.

A reader may sense a different rhetorical rhythm from the different plural patterns, but from the type of plural alone one should not conclude that the green ears are necessarily interwoven and abundant, that the dry ears are necessarily scattered or worthless, that a broken plural always denotes life and abundance, or that a sound plural always denotes weakness and smallness. Broken and sound plurals are part of Arabic morphology, and their selection often depends on the established structure of each adjective. Any further rhetorical reflection must be supported by context, order of the verse, or other uses of the same forms.

11. Examination of the Qur'anic Forms

يَبَسًا

It appears once in Ta-Ha 20:77. It may be a verbal noun used descriptively, or understood as يَابِسًا or ذَا يَبَسٍ. In all cases, it states the condition of the path: a dry path in the sea. This form alone does not prove that the path was intrinsically dry, permanently dry, never moist from the beginning, or drier than يَابِس.

يَابِسٍ

It appears once in Al-An'am 6:59. It is an active participle or masculine singular adjective in the genitive, standing in contrast with رَطْب. In this context it has a general meaning: every dry thing.

يَابِسَاتٍ

It appears twice, in Yusuf 12:43 and 12:46. It is the sound feminine plural of يَابِسَة and describes dry ears of grain. This form agrees in number and grammatical gender, but by itself does not indicate intensity, worthlessness, lifelessness, or scattering.

12. Grammatical and Structural Notes

طَرِيقًا فِي الْبَحْرِ يَبَسًا

طَرِيقًا is the object of اضْرِبْ or part of the structure that expresses bringing forth a path. فِي الْبَحْرِ specifies the location of the path. يَبَسًا states its dry condition and may be understood as a verbal noun functioning adjectivally, as a description interpreted as يَابِسًا, or as part of a resultative structure. For Forqan, insisting on one disputed analysis is unnecessary; what matters is that يَبَسًا introduces the path in a dry state.

وَلَا رَطْبٍ وَلَا يَابِسٍ

رَطْب and يَابِس occur in a parallel structure. This parallelism clarifies the semantic contrast, conveys comprehensiveness, and places both states under the knowledge of The God. Neither word has an inherent ethical or philosophical role.

أُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ

أُخَرَ points to other ears, and يَابِسَات describes them. The feminine plural of يَابِسَات agrees with سُنْبُلَات.

خُضْرٍ and يَابِسَاتٍ

The difference in plural form is a morphological fact. Any rhetorical conclusion about abundance, scattering, life, or death must be proven independently from clear indicators, not from morphology alone.

13. ي ب س Compared with Near Roots

ي ب س compared with ر ط ب

رطب is the direct opposite of يابس in Al-An'am 6:59. رطب means having wetness and moisture; يابس means lacking wetness and moisture. This is the clearest Qur'anic contrast for recognizing ي ب س. But رطب is not always living and يابس is not always dead; both first describe the material state of moisture.

ي ب س compared with ج ف ف

جفاف is also related to drying and may highlight the process of losing moisture. يابس more often describes the dry state. The Qur'an does not use the root ج ف ف, so no direct Qur'anic boundary between them is available.

ي ب س compared with ج ر ز

جرز is used for land whose vegetation has been removed or in which nothing remains. يابس describes the state of dryness. Land may be dry while dry vegetation still remains on it; it may also be جرز because its cover has been stripped away.

ي ب س compared with ذ ب ل

ذبول relates to wilting and loss of freshness. Wilting may happen before complete dryness. يبس emphasizes the state of dryness. The root ذ ب ل does not occur in the Qur'an, so this is a lexical comparison.

ي ب س compared with خ ض ر

In the king's dream, خضر means green ones and يابسات means dry ones. Greenness in this context is connected with freshness and growth, while dryness stands opposite it. Still, this contrast in a dream should not be turned into an ethical rule about every green and dry thing.

ي ب س compared with م و ت

Death is the end of life. Dryness is a condition related to moisture. The dryness of a plant may accompany its death, but the two are not synonyms. The dry path in the sea is not a living being at all.

ي ب س compared with ص ل ب

صلابت concerns hardness and firmness. يبس concerns dryness and lack of moisture. The sea-bed may have become firm after drying, but يبس and صلابت are separate concepts.

14. Dryness as the Possibility of Rescue

In Ta-Ha 20:77, dryness is not deprivation; it creates the possibility of crossing through the sea. This use shows that a material state does not have one fixed value: dryness in farmland may be harmful, while dryness of a path in the sea is rescuing.

In this verse, the withdrawal of water and the appearance of a dry path are part of opening and rescue. Therefore water cannot always be made the symbol of mercy and dryness always the symbol of punishment. The Qur'an shows the function of each phenomenon in its own context.

15. Dryness and the End of a Period of Greenness

In the king's dream, the dry ears stand opposite the green ears. This image can show the loss of freshness and the end of a period of growth. Yusuf connects it with difficult years that come after years of cultivation and abundance.

But the boundaries must be kept: the meaning of the word is dry; the dream image is dry ears opposite green ears; and Yusuf's interpretation is a difficult period after abundance. يَابِسَات should not be translated directly as years of famine.

16. Wet and Dry as a Statement of Comprehensiveness

Al-An'am 6:59 places wet and dry together to show the breadth of the knowledge of The God. No leaf, grain, wet thing, or dry thing is outside His knowledge.

At the level of reflection, one may say that the changing states of things also do not remove them from this encompassing knowledge: when they are moist and fresh, when they have dried, and during the transition between these states. But the verse itself names only رطب and يابس. Theories of elements, humors, or philosophical pairs should not be attributed to it.

17. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

يَبَسًا

Base translation: dry; without water. Main suggestion for Ta-Ha 20:77: open for them a dry path in the heart of the sea. Alternative for highlighting the contrast: open for them a waterless path in the heart of the sea. Adding 'even', 'firm', or 'hard' is possible only as contextual explanation, not as direct translation of the word.

يَابِسٍ

Dry; dry thing. Suggested rendering for Al-An'am 6:59: and no wet thing and no dry thing exists except that it is in a clear book.

يَابِسَاتٍ

Dry; dry ears. Suggested rendering for Yusuf 12:43: and seven green ears and others dry.

Short Definition for Forqan

Becoming dry or being dry; being in a state opposite to moisture and wetness.

Supplementary Explanation

Dryness in the Qur'an does not carry a fixed positive or negative value. It can be a path of rescue, a description of ears in a period of hardship, or one side of the comprehensive contrast between wet and dry.

18. Final Definitions of the Qur'anic Forms

يَبَس

Dryness or something dry; in the Qur'an it appears descriptively for the dry path in the sea.

يَابِس

Something dry and lacking moisture.

يَابِسَات

The feminine plural of يابسة; in the Qur'an it describes dry ears of grain.

19. Conclusion

The root ي ب س in the Qur'an denotes becoming dry or being dry. It appears four times and in three forms: يَبَسًا describing the dry path in the sea; يَابِسٍ in contrast with رطب; and يَابِسَاتٍ describing dry ears of grain.

The examination of the verses shows that the main axis of the root is absence or loss of moisture; dryness is not synonymous with hardness, firmness, death, or fruitlessness; يَبَس may be a verbal noun used descriptively; يَابِس is an active participle, but it does not always denote recent and temporary dryness; the difference between يَبَس and يَابِس does not definitely prove a different degree or duration of dryness; the difference between خُضْر and يَابِسَات in plural type does not by itself carry a rhetorical judgment about life, abundance, or worthlessness; the connection with Hebrew confirms the antiquity of the Semitic meaning of dry and dry land; the philosophical system of moisture and dryness should not be imposed on Al-An'am 6:59; the positive or negative value of dryness comes from context; the dry path in the sea is a means of rescue; the dry ears in the dream are connected with difficult years; and wet and dry in Al-An'am are two sides of an encompassing contrast.

The final definition of the root is: ي ب س denotes becoming dry or being in a dry state; that is, the absence or loss of water and moisture. The type, cause, duration, intensity, value, and consequence of this dryness are known from the structure and context of each verse, not from the root alone.
