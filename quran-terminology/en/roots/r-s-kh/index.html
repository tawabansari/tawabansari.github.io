Firmness, establishment, and being grounded in knowledge

In Arabic, R S KH denotes becoming firmly established, remaining steady, and being securely set in place. It describes stable objects, mountains, settled deposits, and an impression that has penetrated something and endured.

From this field, رُسُوخ (rusūkh) also describes knowledge established within someone as deep and firm understanding. رَاسِخٌ فِي الْعِلْمِ (rāsikhun fī al-ʿilmi) describes someone whose knowledge is not superficial or fleeting, but marked by proficiency and firmness.

Firmness in knowledge should not be equated with claiming to know everything, infallibility, or clinging to every prior belief. A person can persist in a false belief; persistence alone does not prove firm grounding in knowledge. The description concerns firmness in knowledge and understanding themselves.

R S KH occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times in الرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ (al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi): in Āl ʿImrān, concerning clear and ambiguous verses, and in Al-Nisāʾ, describing people among the People of the Book who believe in revealed scripture.

The Qur’an thus connects rusūkh with knowledge. Both occurrences also discuss belief and human responses to revelation.

Qur’anic form: الرَّاسِخُونَ (al-rāsikhūna), in Āl ʿImrān 3:7 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:162.

Arabic also has رَسَخَ (rasakha), يَرْسَخُ (yarsakhu), رُسُوخ (rusūkh), رَاسِخ (rāsikh), رَاسِخَة (rāsikhah), أَرْسَخَ (arsakha), إِرْسَاخ (irsākh), رَسَّخَ (rassakha), تَرْسِيخ (tarsīkh), and تَرَسَّخَ (tarassakha). They do not occur in precisely these forms in the Qur’an.

The family requires distinguishing material firmness, its application to knowledge, and ethical or epistemic conclusions drawn from the verses’ contexts.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Rusūkh: becoming firmly established in place

Lexical explanations identify firmness as this root’s semantic core:

الرَّاءُ وَالسِّينُ وَالْخَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الثَّبَاتِ

al-rāʾu wa-al-sīnu wa-al-khāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-thabāti

Meaning: “Rāʾ, sīn, and khāʾ have one core denoting firmness.”

The same explanation continues:

رَسَخَ: ثَبَتَ، وَكُلُّ رَاسِخٍ ثَابِتٌ

rasakha thabata wa-kullu rāsikhin thābitun

“Rasakha means became firm, and everything rāsikh is firm.”

This clarifies the family’s central meaning.

رَسَخَ الشَّيْءُ فِي مَوْضِعِهِ (rasakha al-shayʾu fī mawḍiʿihi) means that something became securely established in its place and is not easily detached.

جَبَلٌ رَاسِخٌ (jabalun rāsikhun) describes a firm, steadfast mountain.

Firmness here does not imply absolute immobility or impossibility of change. Something stable under ordinary conditions may be moved by another force.

Rāsikh therefore describes something established firmly in place, rather than necessarily something that can never change under any circumstances.

Arsakha: making something firm

أَرْسَخَ (arsakha) is Form IV of this root.

In established Arabic usage, أَرْسَخَهُ (arsakhahu) means fixing or establishing something firmly in its place.

The morphological distinction matters.

Rasakha can describe becoming or being firmly established.

Transitive arsakha concerns making something stable and firm.

Form IV alone does not identify the force, method, or duration of stabilization.

Something may become firm by being embedded in the ground, securely fastened, dried, or established in another way.

Irsākh alone does not supply those details.

Penetrating and becoming established within something

Lexical usage concerning ink includes:

كَالْحِبْرِ يَرْسَخُ فِي الصَّحِيفَةِ

ka-al-ḥibri yarsakhu fī al-ṣaḥīfati

“Like ink that becomes established in a page and leaves a lasting impression.”

This is not simply something lying on a surface: the ink’s impression remains in the page.

Rusūkh can thus accompany penetration and becoming established.

That does not establish that the root necessarily began with penetrating deeply.

Some uses emphasize stability and firmness; others emphasize penetration into something.

A rāsikh mountain is securely established, whereas ink can penetrate a page and leave a lasting impression.

The uses occupy a related field without sharing every detail.

Taking root deeply can help illustrate firmly grounded knowledge, but should not be asserted as the definite historical origin of every use without evidence.

Rainwater penetrating the ground

رَسَخَ الْمَطَرُ (rasakha al-maṭaru) describes rainwater entering the ground, its moisture reaching inner layers of soil.

The explanation describes water penetrating until moisture in different parts of the soil meets.

رَسَخَ الْغَدِيرُ (rasakha al-ghadīru) also describes a pool whose water has receded or dried up.

Both uses concern water and its condition relative to the ground.

Water entering soil can be connected with visible surface water disappearing, but not every pool necessarily dries because its water seeps into the ground.

Evaporation and other factors can reduce water; rasakha alone does not establish the precise cause.

These lexical uses should therefore be understood in their recorded senses.

Nor does a pool’s receding water make destruction or drying the root’s basic meaning. Its established core remains firmness and establishment, while some water uses describe penetration or the disappearance of visible water.

2. An early poetic witness: deposits firmly settled in place

A poem attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah includes:

رَاسِخِ الدِّمْنِ عَلَىٰ أَعْضَادِهِ
ثَلَمَتْهُ كُلُّ رِيحٍ وَسَبَلْ

rāsikhi al-dimni ʿalā aʿḍādihi / thalamathu kullu rīḥin wa-sabal

“Old deposits lay firmly settled along the trough’s sides; wind and rain had breached the trough.”

The verse describes an old watering trough.

الدِّمْن (al-dimn) here concerns material such as mud, earth, and animal dung accumulated and compacted over time on the ground or around the trough.

أَعْضَاد (aʿḍād) concerns its supporting sides.

سَبَل (sabal) here means rain.

The trough has weathered through time and exposure to wind and rain, but some accumulated material remains settled in place.

Rāsikh here concerns the material’s firmness and settlement.

This helps explain the root’s material core: rusūkh describes something that has become established and persistent in its location.

Labīd was a mukhḍaram poet, living across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The verse is attributed to him in lexical transmissions, but its precise date of composition has not been established in this study.

It is valid evidence for early Arabic usage, but should not be asserted to predate the Qur’an without definite evidence.

Must rusūkh mean penetrating deep into the earth?

The poetic witness, together with water and ink usage, shows that firmness and establishment can accompany penetration and attachment.

It does not make every rāsikh something embedded in the ground.

The Qur’an, for instance, uses al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi to describe knowledge.

Knowledge here is not a physical substance whose penetration can be measured like water or ink.

Firm grounding in knowledge concerns knowledge becoming established within a person.

Material imagery can help explain the word without replacing its core meaning and Qur’anic context.

3. Al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi: meaning and construction

Al-rāsikhūn occurs in the Qur’an only with fī al-ʿilm.

That accompaniment matters in both verses.

The morphology of al-rāsikhūn

Al-rāsikhūn is the sound masculine plural of the active participle rāsikh.

Rāsikh follows the fāʿil pattern from the Form I verb rasakha.

The plural is nominative in both verses.

The word carries firmness and stability; its accompanying phrase identifies the kind and field of firmness.

A rāsikh mountain concerns stability in place.

Rāsikhun fī al-ʿilm concerns firmness in knowledge.

Qur’anic al-rāsikhūn therefore describes people established and proficient in knowledge.

Lexically, the title does not mean a religious officeholder, political leader, authority to be imitated, or infallible person.

Applying such titles to someone requires separate evidence.

The role of fī al-ʿilm

Fī is a preposition and al-ʿilmi a genitive noun.

Together they identify the field of firmness.

Arabic fī does not always mean physical containment inside a vessel.

In rāsikhun fī al-ʿilm, it identifies the domain in which the person’s quality is realized.

The person is not physically inside something called knowledge.

Fī al-ʿilm indicates an established position and firmness in knowledge.

Fī alone, however, does not establish that the person has reached every depth of knowledge or has no remaining unknowns.

That conclusion exceeds the phrase’s grammatical capacity.

Firm knowledge and repeated study

Lexical explanation describes rāsikhun fī al-ʿilm as someone with a firmly established position in knowledge.

Another explanation connects the firmly grounded with those who study and examine knowledge.

Another gives الْحُفَّاظُ وَالْمُذَاكِرُونَ (al-ḥuffāẓu wa-al-mudhākirūna): those who retain knowledge and review and discuss it.

These explanations connect firm grounding with learning, retention, review, and proficiency.

It should not be restricted simply to memorizing texts.

Someone may memorize many statements without understanding their relationships or applying knowledge in new situations.

Firm knowledge can instead accompany understanding, critical examination, distinguishing differences, and applying what has been learned.

These qualities fit firm grounding in knowledge, while each requires its own evidence and uses.

Rāsikh itself denotes firmness and establishment; its lexical definition does not contain every educational and research method.

4. Wa-al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi in Āl ʿImrān 3:7

The root’s first Qur’anic occurrence discusses clear and ambiguous verses.

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 states:

هُوَ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ عَلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ مِنْهُ آيَاتٌ مُحْكَمَاتٌ هُنَّ أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ وَأُخَرُ مُتَشَابِهَاتٌ فَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ زَيْغٌ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ مَا تَشَابَهَ مِنْهُ ابْتِغَاءَ الْفِتْنَةِ وَابْتِغَاءَ تَأْوِيلِهِ وَمَا يَعْلَمُ تَأْوِيلَهُ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَالرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ يَقُولُونَ آمَنَّا بِهِ كُلٌّ مِنْ عِنْدِ رَبِّنَا وَمَا يَذَّكَّرُ إِلَّا أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

huwa alladhī anzala ʿalayka al-kitāba minhu āyātun muḥkamātun hunna ummu al-kitābi wa-ukharu mutashābihātun fa-ammā alladhīna fī qulūbihim zayghun fa-yattabiʿūna mā tashābaha minhu ibtighāʾa al-fitnati wa-ibtighāʾa taʾwīlihi wa-mā yaʿlamu taʾwīlahu illā allāhu wa-al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi yaqūlūna āmannā bihi kullun min ʿindi rabbinā wa-mā yadhdhakkaru illā ulū al-albābi

The verse identifies two kinds of passages in the Book: مُحْكَمَات (muḥkamāt), called أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ (umm al-kitāb), and مُتَشَابِهَات (mutashābihāt).

It then describes people with زَيْغ (zaygh) in their hearts who pursue ambiguous passages seeking discord and their interpretation.

Al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilm are subsequently introduced.

Firm knowledge contrasted with deviation of the heart

The verse presents two different conditions.

The first group has فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ زَيْغٌ (fī qulūbihim zayghun): deviation or crookedness in their hearts.

They pursue ambiguous passages for the purpose stated in the verse itself.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:7, the second group, al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilm, says of the Book; an excerpt:

آمَنَّا بِهِ كُلٌّ مِنْ عِنْدِ رَبِّنَا

āmannā bihi kullun min ʿindi rabbinā

“We believe in it; all is from our Lord.”

Firm grounding here is therefore not merely having abundant information.

The verse also concerns people’s conduct and position toward the Book.

The lexical meaning and the contextual conclusion must nevertheless remain distinct.

Rāsikh lexically means firm and established.

The statement that these people believe in the whole Book and recognize its origin in their Lord comes from yaqūlūna āmannā bihi.

Rāsikh should therefore not be lexically translated directly as believer in the whole Book.

That quality accompanies firm knowledge in this verse without being the root’s definition.

Umm al-kitāb and the place of clear verses

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 says of clear verses; an excerpt:

هُنَّ أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ

hunna ummu al-kitābi

“They are the foundation of the Book.”

This highlights the clear verses’ place in the Book’s structure and understanding.

A reading that ignores clear, foundational verses and relies solely on passages admitting multiple possibilities therefore conflicts with the classification and order stated in the verse.

A complete method for understanding clear and ambiguous verses nevertheless requires independent study of both concepts and their Qur’anic uses.

A study of R S KH should not construct a comprehensive theory of every kind of ambiguity or interpretation without that research.

For this root, the point is that firm knowledge is presented in the context of the Book’s structure and people’s responses to its verses.

Yaqūlūna āmannā bihi: belief and claims to knowledge

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 describes those grounded in knowledge through this statement; an excerpt:

آمَنَّا بِهِ كُلٌّ مِنْ عِنْدِ رَبِّنَا

āmannā bihi kullun min ʿindi rabbinā

The phrase acknowledges the Book’s single source.

It does not establish that the firmly grounded never examine or study ambiguous passages.

Nor does their belief make every interpretation they offer certainly correct.

Belief in the Book and correctness of every human understanding of it are different matters.

A person may recognize revelation as true yet err about a phrase’s meaning or a verse’s referent.

The verse therefore does not make the title firmly grounded in knowledge a position of interpretive infallibility.

5. Do those firmly grounded in knowledge know the interpretation of ambiguous passages?

A major grammatical question concerns wa-al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilm in Āl ʿImrān 3:7.

Part of Āl ʿImrān 3:7 reads:

وَمَا يَعْلَمُ تَأْوِيلَهُ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَالرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ يَقُولُونَ آمَنَّا بِهِ

wa-mā yaʿlamu taʾwīlahu illā allāhu wa-al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi yaqūlūna āmannā bihi

Two established analyses in grammar and exegesis discuss how wa-al-rāsikhūn connects with the preceding phrase.

First analysis: stopping at Allāh

The first analysis ends the clause at illā Allāhu; an excerpt from Āl ʿImrān 3:7:

وَمَا يَعْلَمُ تَأْوِيلَهُ إِلَّا اللَّهُ

wa-mā yaʿlamu taʾwīlahu illā allāhu

“And none knows its interpretation except The God.”

A new clause then begins; an excerpt from Āl ʿImrān 3:7:

وَالرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ يَقُولُونَ آمَنَّا بِهِ

wa-al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi yaqūlūna āmannā bihi

“And those firmly grounded in knowledge say: We believe in it.”

Here wa-al-rāsikhūn is the subject of a nominal clause and yaqūlūna its predicate.

The wāw introduces a new clause.

The interpretation intended here is, on this analysis, known only to The God. The firmly grounded are described through belief and acknowledgment of the Book’s divine source.

This analysis had recognized supporters among early grammarians and commentators.

Accepting it does not necessarily mean people understand nothing in ambiguous passages or that no interpretation is humanly possible.

That conclusion would first require establishing precisely what taʾwīl means here.

Second analysis: coordinating al-rāsikhūn with Allāh

The other analysis coordinates wa-al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilm with Allāhu.

The passage is then understood as:

“And none knows its interpretation except The God and those firmly grounded in knowledge.”

The next clause in Āl ʿImrān 3:7 reads; an excerpt:

يَقُولُونَ آمَنَّا بِهِ

yaqūlūna āmannā bihi

In this analysis, it can be a circumstantial clause: knowing the interpretation, they say that they believe in it.

The firmly grounded thus also possess knowledge concerning interpretation.

This analysis likewise appears in early grammatical and exegetical sources and can be considered within Arabic sentence structure.

It does not establish that everyone described as firmly grounded knows every interpretation of the Book or every reality of the unseen.

The coordination itself does not support that generalization.

A different stopping point does not change the root’s meaning

The analyses have significantly different interpretive consequences.

One restricts knowledge of the interpretation intended in the verse to The God.

The other includes those firmly grounded among those knowing it.

In both, al-rāsikhūn retains the same meaning: people firmly established and proficient in knowledge.

The disagreement concerns sentence structure and the scope of knowledge of interpretation, rather than the basic meaning of R S KH.

Stopping signs in printed Qur’ans belong to a system guiding recitation. Different stopping points should not be mistaken for two different Qur’anic texts.

Both analyses discuss the connections among words in the same passage.

Taʾwīl also requires independent examination

The Qur’an uses taʾwīl in different contexts.

Yūsuf 12:100 states; an excerpt:

هَٰذَا تَأْوِيلُ رُؤْيَايَ مِنْ قَبْلُ

hādhā taʾwīlu ruʾyāya min qablu

“This is the fulfillment of my earlier dream.”

Al-Kahf 18:78 also states; an excerpt:

سَأُنَبِّئُكَ بِتَأْوِيلِ مَا لَمْ تَسْتَطِعْ عَلَيْهِ صَبْرًا

sa-unabbiʾuka bi-taʾwīli mā lam tastaṭiʿ ʿalayhi ṣabran

“I shall inform you of the interpretation of what you could not bear patiently.”

Here taʾwīl concerns clarifying the reality or purpose of something previously unclear to the addressee.

These two examples do not establish precisely one application for taʾwīl in every Qur’anic verse.

The disagreement concerning Āl ʿImrān 3:7 cannot therefore be settled through R S KH’s lexical definition alone.

Taʾwīl and its relationship to clear passages, ambiguous passages, and the realization of events require separate study.

For this study, the clear conclusion is that firm grounding in knowledge proves neither absolute knowledge of interpretation nor absolute inability to understand interpretation by itself.

The question requires the verse’s construction and the meaning of taʾwīl, rather than rāsikh alone.

6. Firm knowledge and the possibility of human error

After the verse concerning the firmly grounded, Āl ʿImrān 3:8 gives a prayer:

رَبَّنَا لَا تُزِغْ قُلُوبَنَا بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا وَهَبْ لَنَا مِنْ لَدُنْكَ رَحْمَةً إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْوَهَّابُ

rabbanā lā tuzigh qulūbanā baʿda idh hadaytanā wa-hab lanā min ladunka raḥmatan innaka anta al-wahhābu

“Our Lord, do not let our hearts deviate after You have guided us, and grant us mercy from Yourself. You are the Bestower.”

This prayer connects with the preceding verse’s context.

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 discusses deviation of the heart and those firmly grounded in knowledge.

Āl ʿImrān 3:8 requests preservation of guidance and protection from deviation of the heart.

Having guidance and knowledge should therefore not be equated with needing no care against error.

That contextual conclusion must be distinguished from the root’s lexical meaning.

Rusūkh lexically concerns firmness.

Avoiding deviation and seeking The God’s help to remain guided come from the verses’ construction and message.

Firm knowledge or persistence in belief?

Persian sometimes uses firm conviction for a belief to which someone is strongly committed.

Strong commitment alone does not establish that the belief is true.

Someone may persist in error through fear of changing their mind, losing standing, or encountering opposition.

That is simply persistence in a personal position.

Al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilm, by contrast, concerns firmness in knowledge.

Knowledge should not be equated with every firmly held belief.

Firm grounding in knowledge in Qur’anic usage therefore does not mean dogmatic attachment to a prior conclusion.

When reliable evidence reveals a mistaken interpretation, defending it merely from habit or social position is not automatically a sign of firm knowledge.

Conversely, changing a view on better evidence does not necessarily mean intellectual instability.

Firm knowledge can coexist with careful attention to evidence, reconsideration, and correcting error.

This follows from distinguishing knowledge from insistence on belief, rather than from a lexical meaning of correcting error in rasakha.

7. Lākini al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi minhum: the root in Al-Nisāʾ

The root’s second Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Nisāʾ 4:162:

لَٰكِنِ الرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ مِنْهُمْ وَالْمُؤْمِنُونَ يُؤْمِنُونَ بِمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكَ وَمَا أُنْزِلَ مِنْ قَبْلِكَ وَالْمُقِيمِينَ الصَّلَاةَ وَالْمُؤْتُونَ الزَّكَاةَ وَالْمُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ أُولَٰئِكَ سَنُؤْتِيهِمْ أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

lākini al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi minhum wa-al-muʾminūna yuʾminūna bi-mā unzila ilayka wa-mā unzila min qablika wa-al-muqīmīna al-ṣalāta wa-al-muʾtūna al-zakāta wa-al-muʾminūna bi-allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri ulāʾika sa-nuʾtīhim ajran ʿaẓīman

“But those firmly grounded in knowledge among them, and the believers, believe in what has been sent down to you and what was sent down before you. Those establishing prayer, giving zakāt, and believing in The God and the Last Day—we shall give them a great reward.”

The firmly grounded are described as a group among those whose members’ wrongdoing was discussed in preceding verses.

The role of lākin

The verse begins with لَٰكِنِ (lākini).

This word introduces a qualification or contrast with the preceding statement.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:160 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:161 discuss wrongdoing, obstructing The God’s path, taking usury, and consuming people’s property unjustly.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:162 then introduces a different group with lākin.

The Qur’an thus does not place every individual in that community under one identical judgment.

Among the same people whose members’ conduct was discussed, some are firmly grounded in knowledge and believe in revelation.

This follows from lākin and minhum, rather than from R S KH itself.

Minhum: the firmly grounded within that community

مِنْهُمْ (minhum) identifies the firmly grounded as coming from the group discussed in the preceding context.

Exegetical explanations connect them with believing scholars among the People of the Book.

The text clearly distinguishes individuals within that community.

Some have been described through wrongdoing, whereas the firmly grounded among them occupy a different position.

A people’s or community’s name therefore cannot support the same judgment about all its members.

Nor does firmly grounded in knowledge become an ethnic or genealogical title here.

The Qur’an describes their knowledge and belief; mere membership in a people does not establish firm grounding or its absence.

Yuʾminūna bi-mā unzila ilayka wa-mā unzila min qablika

Al-Nisāʾ 4:162 states; an excerpt:

يُؤْمِنُونَ بِمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكَ وَمَا أُنْزِلَ مِنْ قَبْلِكَ

yuʾminūna bi-mā unzila ilayka wa-mā unzila min qablika

“They believe in what has been sent down to you and what was sent down before you.”

This helps explain the firmly grounded people’s position.

They are described through belief in the revelation sent to the Prophet and in earlier revelation.

Here firm knowledge is therefore not associated with rejecting truth simply because it appears among another group.

The semantic boundary nevertheless remains.

Rāsikh lexically means firm or established; fī al-ʿilm identifies knowledge as the domain of that firmness.

Believing in earlier revelation and revelation now sent down is attributed to them by the accompanying clause.

The word itself should therefore not be redefined as accepting every heavenly book.

That quality occurs in context but is not identical with the root’s definition.

Firm knowledge and conduct

The same verse mentions establishing prayer, giving zakāt, and believing in The God and the Last Day alongside belief in revelation.

It does not concern people’s scholarly title alone. Belief and conduct also characterize the groups praised.

Their association does not make R S KH lexically mean establishing prayer or paying zakāt.

They are independent concepts within one context.

The established grammatical disagreement about وَالْمُقِيمِينَ الصَّلَاةَ (wa-al-muqīmīna al-ṣalāta) likewise requires separate examination; it does not change al-rāsikhūn’s meaning.

Does the title identify a closed, exclusive group?

These two Qur’anic occurrences provide no permanent list naming everyone firmly grounded in knowledge.

Āl ʿImrān introduces them through their position toward the Book.

Al-Nisāʾ discusses them among the People of the Book and the believers.

The word alone therefore cannot establish an exclusive title for a family, sect, religious class, or particular group while denying others access to firm knowledge.

Conversely, everyone calling themselves a scholar, commentator, or firmly grounded person does not necessarily possess that quality.

A title or claim differs from actual knowledge.

Firm grounding should be recognized through real knowledge and relevant evidence, rather than titles, fame, or social acceptance alone.

8. R S KH and related roots

R S KH (rusūkh) and TH B T (thabāt)

The roots are close in the idea of firmness.

Arabic thabata concerns remaining stable or steadfast and has wide Qur’anic application.

For example, Ibrāhīm 14:27 states; an excerpt:

يُثَبِّتُ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا بِالْقَوْلِ الثَّابِتِ

yuthabbitu allāhu alladhīna āmanū bi-al-qawli al-thābiti

“The God makes those who believe steadfast through the firm word.”

Stability here is connected with speech and belief.

Rusūkh likewise denotes firmness, while established uses can emphasize something’s secure settlement and establishment.

Al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilm applies that firmness to knowledge.

There is no absolute boundary making thabāt always superficial and rusūkh always deep.

Thabāt can also be secure and persistent, while rusūkh varies with what it describes.

Their distinction is chiefly a tendency in application and imagery, rather than a fixed division for all cases.

Study of TH B T

R S KH (rusūkh) and R S W (rusuww)

R S W also concerns settlement and stability.

Al-Naḥl 16:15 uses رَوَاسِي (rawāsiya) for mountains; an excerpt:

وَأَلْقَىٰ فِي الْأَرْضِ رَوَاسِيَ أَنْ تَمِيدَ بِكُمْ

wa-alqā fī al-arḍi rawāsiya an tamīda bikum

“And He placed firm mountains in the earth lest it sway with you.”

Rawāsī belongs to R S W, rather than R S KH.

Both roots concern establishment and stability within their semantic fields.

Two shared initial letters and similar meanings do not establish that they were originally one root.

Rawāsī should not be registered as a Qur’anic form of R S KH.

The two morphological families must remain distinct.

Independent study of R S W can clarify its applications to mountains and stability without imposing its results on R S KH beforehand.

R S KH (rusūkh) and M K N (tamakkun)

Makkana and related Qur’anic forms concern establishment, empowerment, or providing means and capability.

For example, Al-Kahf 18:84 states; an excerpt:

إِنَّا مَكَّنَّا لَهُ فِي الْأَرْضِ

innā makkan-nā lahu fī al-arḍi

“We gave him power and standing in the land.”

Tamkīn here concerns Dhū al-Qarnayn’s position and resources.

Rusūkh can also describe an established position within a field.

Capability and resources, however, do not always amount to firm grounding.

Someone may possess substantial power and equipment without proficiency or firmness in knowledge.

Someone else may have firmly grounded knowledge without political power or extensive resources.

Al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilm should therefore not be translated as holders of scholarly and political power or people who dominate others.

The verse concerns firmness in knowledge.

R S KH (rusūkh) and ʿ L M (ʿilm)

ʿIlm concerns knowing and understanding.

Rusūkh concerns the quality of something’s establishment and firmness.

Al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilm is therefore not simply another designation for everyone who knows something.

Al-rāsikhūn indicates that the knowledge concerned has become firmly established in them.

The phrase specifies neither how many things they know, their specialization, memory capacity, nor official rank.

Nor does the title alone establish complete knowledge of every subject.

Firm grounding in knowledge thus describes knowledge’s quality and stability; it is neither an exact synonym of possessing knowledge nor a claim to unlimited knowledge.

Study of ʿ L M

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Persian equivalents should distinguish material firmness from firm knowledge.

رَسَخَ (rasakha): became firm; became fixed in place; settled and remained steady.

يَرْسَخُ (yarsakhu): becomes firm; remains stable in place.

رُسُوخ (rusūkh): firmness; stability; secure establishment.

رَاسِخ (rāsikh): firm; steadfast. In knowledge usage, possessing firm, established knowledge.

رَاسِخَة (rāsikhah): firm; steadfast; the feminine of rāsikh.

أَرْسَخَ (arsakha): made firm; established securely.

إِرْسَاخ (irsākh): making firm; establishing.

رَسَّخَ (rassakha): made firm or established.

تَرْسِيخ (tarsīkh): consolidation; establishing; making something settled.

تَرَسَّخَ (tarassakha): became firmly established; in knowledge and thought, acquired a lasting place in the mind or understanding.

الرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ (al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi): those firmly grounded in knowledge; people established and proficient in knowledge.

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 — excerpt

وَالرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ يَقُولُونَ آمَنَّا بِهِ

wa-al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi yaqūlūna āmannā bihi

“And those firmly grounded in knowledge say: We believe in it.”

This rendering fits the analysis that begins a new clause after illā Allāhu.

Coordinating al-rāsikhūn with Allāhu changes the translation’s sentence connection, while al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilm still means those firmly grounded in knowledge.

The grammatical disagreement belongs in the verse’s explanation, rather than in a changed lexical meaning of rāsikh.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:162 — excerpt

لَٰكِنِ الرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ مِنْهُمْ

lākini al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi minhum

“But those firmly grounded in knowledge among them.”

Minhum must be retained because the Qur’an does not describe every member of the group identically.

Those firmly grounded in knowledge is a concise, natural equivalent close to the Arabic construction.

Rooted scholars and deeply discerning scholars can convey aspects of the meaning, but rooted in Persian may suggest family or historical longevity, while discerning places greater emphasis on outlook and manner of thinking than the word itself.

For a basic translation, those firmly grounded in knowledge is suitable; the explanation can add: people who have become proficient and firmly established in knowledge.

The basic translation should not replace al-rāsikhūn with the infallible, possessors of unseen knowledge, religious authorities, or those who know every interpretation.

The root itself does not contain such meanings.

10. Conclusion

Arabic R S KH denotes becoming firmly established, settling securely, and remaining steady.

Material applications include stable mountains, settled deposits, ink penetrating a page, and water entering soil.

Labīd’s poetic witness also applies rāsikh to deposits and accumulated material in an old watering trough.

From this field, rusūkh describes knowledge securely established within a person.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both in al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilm.

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 introduces the firmly grounded amid discussion of clear and ambiguous verses, distinguishing them from people with deviation in their hearts.

They are described through āmannā bihi kullun min ʿindi rabbinā, but that contextual quality should not replace rāsikh’s lexical meaning.

Two established grammatical analyses connect wa-al-rāsikhūn with wa-mā yaʿlamu taʾwīlahu illā Allāhu. One restricts knowledge of the intended interpretation to The God; the other includes those firmly grounded among those knowing it.

The disagreement does not change the root’s basic meaning. Both understand rāsikh as someone firm in knowledge.

Determining the scope of knowledge of interpretation also requires independent study of taʾwīl and its Qur’anic uses.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:162 introduces the firmly grounded from a group whose members’ conduct was discussed previously. It distinguishes people within that community instead of describing them through one uniform judgment.

Firm knowledge, belief in current and earlier revelation, and conduct appear together, but belief, establishing prayer, and zakāt should not be made lexical meanings of R S KH.

Neither Qur’anic occurrence independently establishes al-rāsikhūn as an exclusive title for a sect, family, or religious class.

Firm knowledge should not, conversely, be confused with dogmatic attachment to prior beliefs, persistence in error, or claims to complete knowledge.

Intellectual firmness concerns the quality of knowledge, rather than the force of someone’s insistence on what they consider true.

Final definition: R S KH denotes becoming firmly established and remaining steady in a place or field. In knowledge usage, rāsikhun fī al-ʿilm describes someone in whom knowledge has become established and who has acquired proficiency in it. The Qur’an describes such people as responding to revelation with belief and a position differing from those with deviating hearts who pursue discord. The scope of knowledge, correctness of an interpretation, understanding of taʾwīl, and individuals’ ethical or religious standing do not follow from the root alone; they require the verses’ construction and context.
