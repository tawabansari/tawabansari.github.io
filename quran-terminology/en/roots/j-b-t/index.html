The Qur’anic Root J B T

Jibt: false belief and authority in opposition to guidance

The Qur’an uses جِبْت (jibt) alongside ṭāghūt. In early Arabic lexical sources, this name is applied to an idol, sorcerer, soothsayer, and that which is wrongfully worshipped or believed in. Its uses are not confined to one referent, and there is no agreement over its linguistic origin or earliest meaning.

The Qur’an uses jibt only once, in Al-Nisāʾ 4:51. The verse speaks of people given a share of the Scripture who nevertheless believe in jibt and ṭāghūt and judge those who disbelieve better guided than the believers.

The main question is therefore not merely whether jibt named an idol, sorcerer, or belief. We must examine what shared semantic field allows the name to cover such different referents, and what the verse itself discloses about it.

1. Qur’anic form and semantic field

J B T occurs in the Qur’an only as بِالْجِبْتِ (bi-al-jibti): the noun jibt with the preposition bi- and definite article al-. J B T serves here as the word’s lexical filing label; particularly given its possible borrowed origin, this label does not establish derivation from a triliteral Arabic verb.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole occurrence in Al-Nisāʾ 4:51

The word is a noun; the Qur’an contains no verb, verbal noun, or other derivative from it. Unlike many Arabic roots, the early dictionaries do not record an extensive family of independent verbs and derivatives for it. Moreover, a meaning’s inclusion in an Islamic-period dictionary is not independent evidence that it was used before the Qur’an; this study does not claim such antiquity for every reported sense.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies only one word under this entry and explains jibt as a sorcerer or soothsayer. Kitāb al-ʿAyn likewise gives these two meanings.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: sorcerer and soothsayer

Other sources record a wider field: an idol or an object of worship other than The God, a sorcerer, soothsayer, sorcery, and sometimes Satan or a person wrongfully accorded religious and doctrinal authority.

These senses are not identical. A sorcerer and a soothsayer are people; sorcery is an activity or phenomenon; an idol may be an object of worship; and Satan denotes a being or a misleading role.

A possible common thread is belief and credibility wrongfully invested in something or someone, especially when claims to unseen power, sanctity, or authority lead people to accept falsehood.

This description synthesizes recorded uses; it is not a certain etymological meaning. We cannot claim that jibt originally had precisely this abstract definition.

2. Jibt in early Arabic and the question of origin

Sorcerer, soothsayer, and false object of worship

Sorcerer and soothsayer are among the familiar explanations of jibt in the older dictionaries.

In early Arabic culture, a kāhin commonly claimed knowledge of hidden matters, foretelling the future, or access to knowledge beyond ordinary human ability. A sorcerer could likewise use particular practices, performances, or claims to induce belief in a special power possessed by them.

This does not mean jibt always designated only a person practising sorcery or divination. Its application to idols and false objects of worship shows a range extending beyond people.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records the broad explanation “everything worshipped besides The God,” followed by uses for an idol, soothsayer, and sorcerer.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab: recorded uses of jibt

Jibt’s recorded uses can thus be connected with forms of false belief and veneration. Sometimes belief concerns an object being worshipped, sometimes a person whose words are accepted without valid evidence, and sometimes a practice invested with an unreal or illegitimate power.

This lexical breadth does not make all these instances identical in every setting. The referent depends on the sentence and situation.

Is jibt identical with jibs?

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī places جِبْت (jibt) alongside جِبْس (jibs), explaining both through فَسْل (fasl): a base, ignoble person, deficient in character and conduct.

He also reports a view that the tāʾ in jibt replaces the sīn in jibs, emphasizing baseness still further.

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: jibs and the reported replacement of sīn by tāʾ

This matters for understanding some lexicographers’ interpretations, but two points must remain distinct.

First, dictionaries do record jibs for a base, ignoble, or cowardly person. Second, historical change from jibs to jibt is a separate claim, not established by resemblance alone.

The proposed semantic relationship can therefore be reported without asserting that jibt certainly originated as jibs or originally meant only “base person.”

Even if their historical relationship were established, jibt in Al-Nisāʾ could not simply be translated “base person,” since it appears there as an object of belief.

Is jibt a loanword?

The early sources also debate whether the word itself is Arabic in origin.

Al-Jawharī considered it not purely Arabic. His precise argument concerns the combination of jīm and tāʾ in one word without a letter of the class he calls dhawlaqī; it is not a judgment against every occurrence of jīm and tāʾ together in Arabic.

Al-Jawharī, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: the precise argument concerning origin

Such an argument expresses a traditional linguistic judgment, but does not by itself establish a non-Arabic origin. Identifying a borrowing also involves a corresponding form and meaning in the proposed source language, historical evidence, and an account of sound and semantic development.

Some early exegetical reports attribute jibt to the language of Abyssinia, explaining it as “sorcerer” or a name for Satan.

Al-Suyūṭī, Muʿtarak al-Aqrān: reports of Abyssinian attribution

These reports suggest a possible connection with languages of the Abyssinian region. Until an independently attested form and a route into Arabic are identified, however, the attribution should not be presented as certain linguistic history.

Two questions should be kept distinct in these reports: the word’s established use in Arabic and its historical origin. Proposed origins include a possible connection with jibs and borrowing from another language; established Arabic use alone neither proves nor disproves either proposal.

These lexical and exegetical reports alone do not settle the earliest origin. This study can establish more clearly the range of recorded uses and the word’s place in its sole Qur’anic occurrence.

3. Jibt in Sūrat Al-Nisāʾ

Al-Nisāʾ 4:51

أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا نَصِيبًا مِنَ الْكِتَابِ يُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْجِبْتِ وَالطَّاغُوتِ وَيَقُولُونَ لِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا هَٰؤُلَاءِ أَهْدَىٰ مِنَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا سَبِيلًا

a-lam tara ilā alladhīna ūtū naṣīban mina al-kitābi yuʾminūna bi-al-jibti wa-al-ṭāghūti wa-yaqūlūna li-alladhīna kafarū hāʾulāʾi ahdā mina alladhīna āmanū sabīlan

“Have you not considered those given a share of the Scripture? They believe in jibt and ṭāghūt and say concerning those who disbelieve: ‘These are better guided in their way than those who believe.’”

The verse identifies a group given نَصِيبًا مِنَ الْكِتَابِ (naṣīban mina al-kitābi), “a share of the Scripture.” Their conduct nevertheless conflicts with what might be expected from knowing and accepting divine guidance.

The Qur’an places two behaviours together.

First, they believe in jibt and ṭāghūt. Second, they describe those who disbelieve as better guided than the believers.

The two behaviours are joined in the verse’s construction. The issue is not merely a private belief about an unknown phenomenon, but a belief affecting judgments about right and wrong paths.

The meaning of yuʾminūna bi-al-jibti

يُؤْمِنُونَ (yuʾminūna) belongs to ʾ M N and is linked to jibt and ṭāghūt through the preposition bi-.

Study of ʾ M N

Believing, assenting, trusting, and worshipping should be distinguished here. They may accompany one another in certain situations, but yuʾminūna does not necessarily denote performing worship.

The phrase يُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْجِبْتِ (yuʾminūna bi-al-jibti) alone therefore does not establish that everyone criticized in the verse prostrated before an idol or directly worshipped a sorcerer.

The construction makes acceptance and belief in jibt clear. What form that acceptance took in practice depends on identifying the historical referent and reading the surrounding passage.

This especially matters when considering reports explaining jibt as a soothsayer or sorcerer. Someone may regard a soothsayer’s statements about the unseen and guidance as authoritative without directly worshipping them. Such a relationship may fit belief and assent in the verse, but cannot be declared its certain referent without independent evidence.

Jibt and ṭāghūt: two expressions in one sentence

The Qur’an places jibt alongside ṭāghūt: بِالْجِبْتِ وَالطَّاغُوتِ (bi-al-jibti wa-al-ṭāghūti).

Coordinating the two titles shows that both contribute to the Qur’anic criticism of this conduct. Merely placing two names together does not establish entirely separate beings or exclude semantic overlap.

Early reports also vary in assigning their referents. One explains jibt as a sorcerer and ṭāghūt as a soothsayer; another explains jibt as sorcery and ṭāghūt as Satan; others connect both with idols or particular people.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing referents of jibt and ṭāghūt

These differences caution against assigning either term an exclusive referent on the strength of a single report.

The linguistic evidence for the two titles nevertheless differs in an important respect.

Ṭāghūt belongs to the Ṭ GH Y family, whose connection with transgression and exceeding a limit can be examined through Qur’anic and lexical usage. Jibt has no comparably clear and extensive derivational family in the Qur’an.

The meaning “transgression” should therefore not be made jibt’s own lexical definition without evidence.

At the level of contextual analysis, jibt may concern accepting a false object or authority, while ṭāghūt concerns authority and transgression beyond rightful limits. They may reinforce one another in practice. This remains a conceptual synthesis, not an established exclusive lexical distinction.

Declaring the disbelievers better guided

The verse’s final part says:

«وَيَقُولُونَ لِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا هَٰؤُلَاءِ أَهْدَىٰ مِنَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا سَبِيلًا».

wa-yaqūlūna li-alladhīna kafarū hāʾulāʾi ahdā mina alladhīna āmanū sabīlan

Those criticized judge the way of a group of disbelievers superior to the believers’ way.

This matters for understanding the role of jibt and ṭāghūt: the belief under criticism is connected with assessing guidance.

The verse does not establish that every mistaken statement or assessment of a person is an instance of jibt. It does show acceptance of jibt and ṭāghūt accompanied here by a judgment the Qur’an condemns.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:50 speaks of inventing lies against The God; Al-Nisāʾ 4:52 continues the criticism of the same group. This continuity places Al-Nisāʾ 4:51 within a critique of religious conduct and mistaken judgments about guidance.

Nor does الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا نَصِيبًا مِنَ الْكِتَابِ (alladhīna ūtū naṣīban mina al-kitābi) itself permit generalizing this conduct to everyone given Scripture or to every follower of a religious tradition. The verse concerns a group identified by particular characteristics and conduct.

4. Jibt and ṭāghūt among Qur’anic concepts

Although jibt appears only once in the Qur’an, ṭāghūt occurs in other verses as well.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 places rejecting ṭāghūt alongside believing in The God. Al-Naḥl 16:36 presents worshipping The God and avoiding ṭāghūt as a shared message of the messengers.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:60 also speaks of people wishing to seek judgment from ṭāghūt despite having been commanded to reject it.

These uses show that ṭāghūt is not limited to a physical object of worship, but can also concern accepting false authority and adjudication.

Jibt, however, is paired with ṭāghūt only in Al-Nisāʾ 4:51. We cannot transfer ṭāghūt’s entire semantic range to jibt or assume that every occurrence of ṭāghūt simultaneously counts as an occurrence of jibt.

The clearer relationship is that both titles in Al-Nisāʾ 4:51 concern false belief and authority: people may regard something as religiously or epistemically authoritative without that authority having been established by The God.

In uses such as sorcerer and soothsayer, this can concern accepting claims about the unseen and destiny. With idols and false objects of worship, it may concern worship or veneration of something other than The God.

Jibt’s range should therefore not be restricted to one activity, such as fortune-telling, magic, or making idols. Equally, not every mistaken belief or unsupported statement automatically becomes jibt. Applying the title requires evidence connecting it with the word’s established semantic field.

5. Distinguishing J B T from nearby roots

J B T (jibt) and J B B (jabab)

Study of J B B

In J B B, جَبَّ (jabba) is an established verb for cutting and separating. جُبّ (jubb) also means a well in Joseph’s story.

No comparable verbal family is recorded for J B T; its familiar Qur’anic meaning concerns jibt.

Sharing the first two letters does not establish a common root or derivation of one from the other. Jibt cannot therefore be defined through jabba as something cut off or separated.

J B T (jibt) and J B S (jibs)

Lexical sources use جِبْس (jibs) of a base, ignoble, or cowardly person.

Al-Rāghib reports the possibility of sīn changing to tāʾ and a connection between the words. This possibility does not establish their historical identity.

Nor should jibt in Al-Nisāʾ be translated merely “base person”: it is an object of belief there, rather than simply an ethical description of someone.

J B T (jibt) and S Ḥ R (siḥr)

Study of S Ḥ R

Siḥr occurs repeatedly in the Qur’an and concerns practices, claims, and perceptions requiring examination in their respective contexts.

Jibt occurs only once; early sources sometimes apply it to a sorcerer or sorcery itself.

Sorcery is therefore one reported referent of jibt, rather than the two words being identical in every usage. Explaining jibt through sorcery does not establish that everything called siḥr in the Qur’an must also be called jibt.

J B T (jibt) and Ṭ GH Y (ṭughyān)

Study of Ṭ GH Y

Ṭ GH Y is associated with exceeding a limit and transgression. Ṭāghūt belongs within this semantic field.

Jibt and ṭāghūt appear together in Al-Nisāʾ 4:51, but co-occurrence establishes neither a common root nor complete identity of meaning.

A defensible distinction is that ṭāghūt’s derivational connection with transgression is clearer, whereas jibt requires greater reliance on recorded uses and the verse’s context.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Because jibt occurs only once and lexical sources report multiple referents, choosing one fully specific equivalent is difficult.

“Sorcery” and “magic” convey only some reported uses, leaving out idols and false objects of worship.

“Idol” is clear and simple, but restricts uses concerning soothsayers, sorcerers, and other false authorities.

“Superstition” has a broad range and may convey baseless beliefs, but does not necessarily convey the role of a person or an object of worship. Alone, it may also suggest that the verse concerns only popular beliefs or imaginary matters.

“False object of worship” fits one important lexical use, but does not fully convey possible application to a soothsayer or sorcerer accepted without direct worship.

For a basic translation, retaining jibt is therefore a cautious and defensible choice, especially if the word box or root study provides the necessary explanation.

Proposed translation for Al-Nisāʾ 4:51:

“They believe in jibt and ṭāghūt.”

This follows the Arabic construction and leaves the meaning to a separate explanation without inserting a certain referent.

Proposed brief explanation:

“Jibt: a name for an idol, sorcerer, soothsayer, or other false object or authority wrongfully invested with belief and veneration.”

A fuller explanation can note that this range comes from lexical uses and early reports; the verse itself does not explicitly identify a particular referent for jibt.

Conclusion

Jibt occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Nisāʾ 4:51. Early Arabic sources apply it to an idol, sorcerer, soothsayer, sorcery, and other objects of false worship or belief.

Its etymological origin is uncertain. Both the proposed connection with jibs and the attribution to the language of Abyssinia are reported in lexical and exegetical history, but require independent evidence for final establishment.

Jibt’s defensible semantic field concerns belief and credibility wrongfully accorded to an object, person, practice, or authority. This synthesis must not become a claim to have discovered a certain historical root meaning.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:51, the Qur’an criticizes people who, despite having a share of Scripture, believe in jibt and ṭāghūt and judge the disbelievers’ way superior to that of the believers.

Jibt cannot therefore confidently be reduced to “magic,” “idol,” or one historical individual. The word belongs to the field of false belief and authority; the Qur’an does not specify its exclusive referent in that verse.

Retaining jibt in the basic translation, together with a clear explanation of its lexical range, avoids adding restrictions not imposed by the Qur’anic text itself.
