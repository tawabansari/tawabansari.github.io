Core meaning: covering something and keeping it from direct sight, access, or awareness by placing a covering or barrier between it and a potential observer. This may be material, as with a curtain or shelter, or extend to concealment from awareness and perception. Qur’anic uses concern a concealed barrier, shelter from the sun, and concealing oneself in relation to testimony.

1. Introducing the root and its early Arabic meaning

The Arabic root S T R concerns covering, hiding, and being behind a covering.

Satara al-shayʾa means covering or concealing something.

Satarahu ʿani al-nāsi means hiding it from people’s sight.

Al-sitr denotes a covering or curtain behind which something is placed.

Al-sutrah likewise denotes something used to cover or protect oneself.

The central point is concealment, not the object’s disappearance from existence.

A cloth placed over an object may hide it while it remains in the same place.

A wall may prevent a person standing behind it from being seen.

In both cases, something intervenes between the observer and the object of sight.

A covering need not be manufactured cloth: a wall, darkness, natural barrier, or another obscuring thing may serve this function in an appropriate usage.

A pre-Islamic witness: a barrier against wrongdoing in Zuhayr

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says in the poem beginning Li-mani al-diyāru bi-qunnati al-ḥijri:

وَالسِّتْرُ دُونَ الْفَاحِشَاتِ وَمَا
يَلْقَاكَ دُونَ الْخَيْرِ مِنْ سِتْرِ

wa-al-sitru dūna al-fāḥishāti wa-mā / yalqāka dūna al-khayri min sitri

Translation:

“A curtain stands between you and shameful acts, but no curtain meets you on the way to good.”

Sitr appears twice.

The first covering stands between the person and shameful acts.

The second occurrence denies a barrier between him and good action.

The image praises someone kept away from wrongdoing and ready to do good.

It uses the image of a curtain or barrier to describe an ethical relationship, rather than an actual material covering.

This matters historically.

In poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, sitr was not confined to material curtains: the covering image could describe distance from objectionable conduct.

The verse does not make chastity or beneficence the lexical meaning of sitr.

Chastity and avoidance of indecency are contextual implications.

Sitr continues to express a covering or barrier.

Here the covering bars wrongdoing, rather than necessarily hiding wrongdoing already committed.

Preventing approach to an action and concealing an action already performed are different situations.

The poem is recorded in recognized transmissions of Zuhayr’s poetry. Its attribution supports ancient usage, while later transmission of pre-Islamic poetry and the lack of an independent date for this verse should be kept in view.

Material uses in Arabic

Lexical records use sitr for a covering behind which something is hidden.

سَتَرْتُ الشَّيْءَ

satartu al-shayʾa

“I covered something.”

فَاسْتَتَرَ

fa-istatara

“Then it became concealed.”

The constructions differ clearly.

The first attributes covering an object to an agent.

The second attributes becoming concealed to the subject itself.

Sitār and sitārah are also recorded for curtains and coverings.

Sutrah may denote something behind which a person shelters or remains protected from sight and direct exposure.

The covering need not consist of any particular material.

It may be cloth, a wall, or something else acting as a barrier.

Extension to modesty and respectable conduct

Satīr and satīrah are also recorded for a chaste, modest person.

Here the covering sense relates to conduct and manner of life.

Someone may avoid exposing private matters or observe boundaries in social relations.

This does not make every concealment chaste or everything visible immodest.

These adjectives do not occur in the Qur’an, and their senses should not be transferred to its forms without examination.

A historically grounded definition

The poetic witness attributed to Zuhayr and recorded Arabic uses support the following definition:

S T R denotes covering or concealing something so that it lies behind a covering or barrier, away from direct sight or access.

The covering may be material or figuratively separate someone from a behavior, awareness, or situation.

Zuhayr’s witness illustrates figurative covering and separation in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Material uses are also clearly recorded in classical Arabic, but later dictionary entries alone cannot date every derivative.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Satara yasturu

The basic verb of this family.

It means covering, concealing, or keeping something out of sight.

سَتَرَ الشَّيْءَ

satara al-shayʾa

“He covered something.”

The act is attributed to an agent.

Satr and satar

Recorded verbal nouns of the basic verb, denoting covering and concealing.

The verbal noun naming the act must be distinguished from the noun naming the covering itself.

Sitr

A curtain, covering, or something preventing an object from being manifest.

This nominal form occurs in Al-Kahf 18:90.

It relates there to the sun and people exposed to its rays.

Sitār and sitārah

A curtain or covering used to conceal something.

Arabic sitārah here means a curtain; it should not be confused with Persian setāreh, meaning a star. Similar spelling does not establish identical meaning or origin.

Sutrah

Something used to cover, conceal, or protect oneself.

It may denote a material barrier or covering.

Sātir

The active participle of the basic verb.

Someone or something that covers or conceals.

حِجَابٌ سَاتِرٌ

ḥijābun sātirun

“A concealing barrier.”

The act of covering is attributed to the barrier itself.

Mastūr

The passive participle of the basic verb.

It means covered or concealed.

شَيْءٌ مَسْتُورٌ

shayʾun mastūrun

“Something covered or hidden.”

The form occurs in Al-Isrāʾ 17:45.

Sātir and mastūr must be distinguished.

Sātir is what covers.

Mastūr is what has been covered.

A passive participle may in some expressions approach an active meaning, but that possibility should not erase its basic morphology without contextual examination.

Istatara yastatiru

A Form VIII verb.

It means covering oneself, hiding, or keeping oneself from another’s sight or awareness.

Concealment is attributed to the agent itself.

Tastatirūna in Fuṣṣilat 41:22 belongs to this form.

Istitār

The verbal noun of istatara: hiding or keeping oneself concealed.

It is recognized in Arabic but does not occur in this form in the Qur’an.

Tasattara

A Form V verb denoting covering oneself or hiding.

تَسَتَّرَ بِالثَّوْبِ

tasattara bi-l-thawbi

“He covered himself with the garment.”

Its morphology differs from istatara, although their meanings often overlap.

Forms actually occurring in the Qur’an

S T R occurs three times in three forms:

  • Mastūran in Al-Isrāʾ 17:45: passive participle.
  • Sitran in Al-Kahf 18:90: noun denoting a covering.
  • Tastatirūna in Fuṣṣilat 41:22: Form VIII imperfect verb.

Other forms, including sātir, sitārah, sutrah, tasattara, and istitār, are recognized in Arabic but do not occur in those forms in the Qur’an.

3. Ḥijāban mastūran: a concealed barrier in Al-Isrāʾ 17:45

وَإِذَا قَرَأْتَ الْقُرْآنَ جَعَلْنَا بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَ الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْآخِرَةِ حِجَابًا مَسْتُورًا

wa-idhā qaraʾta al-qurʾāna jaʿalnā baynaka wa-bayna alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-l-ākhirati ḥijāban mastūrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45

Translation:

“When you recite the Qur’an, We place between you and those who do not believe in the Hereafter a concealed barrier.”

Mastūran is a passive participle from S T R.

It modifies ḥijāban.

Ḥijāb belongs to Ḥ J B and denotes a veil or barrier.

Mastūr conveys being covered.

The phrase combines a separating barrier with a description of its concealment.

Is the barrier concealed, or does it conceal others?

Two linguistic readings are prominent among the interpretations of this phrase.

In the first, mastūr retains the ordinary passive-participle meaning.

It denotes a concealed barrier, itself hidden from view.

In the second, mastūr is understood closer to sātir.

It then denotes a concealing barrier, obstructing contact between two sides.

Both readings occur in early lexical and grammatical discussions.

Morphologically, mastūr is a passive participle whose direct sense is covered or concealed.

An active rendering requires contextual grounds.

Since ḥijāb already means a barrier, “a concealed barrier” can preserve both components without equating them.

Ḥijāb names the barrier and mastūr describes its concealment.

Is this a material curtain?

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:45, the barrier stands between Muhammad and those who do not believe in the Hereafter.

The clause concerns reciting the Qur’an.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:46 mentions coverings over their hearts and heaviness in their ears in relation to not understanding it.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:47 also mentions listening to Muhammad and the opponents’ private conversations.

This connection does not justify limiting the barrier to cloth or a physical wall.

The audience may hear the Qur’an’s sound while rejecting its message or turning away from understanding and accepting it.

A barrier then separates hearing words from receiving their truth.

A perceptual or spiritual reading is therefore consistent with the context.

Mastūr itself does not establish an invisible physical field or an unknown scientific mechanism.

Nor should everyone with different beliefs be assigned this condition without examining their knowledge and conduct.

The verse describes a particular group; neighboring verses explain their response to the Qur’an.

Can a hidden barrier be real?

Something may remain unseen while its effects become apparent.

For example, one may not see an obstruction’s cause but experience its effect in failing to reach a goal.

The barrier itself and its effect on the relationship between messenger and audience may likewise be distinguished.

This only explains the semantic possibility of a concealed barrier.

It does not establish an invisible physical object with specified properties.

The context supports a barrier in the audience’s relationship to the Qur’anic message.

Distinguishing ḥijāb and mastūr

Ḥijāb names the barrier between two sides.

Mastūr describes it.

Giving both words an identical rendering can lose the adjective’s grammatical and semantic contribution.

The phrase should not be reduced to a repetitive “barrier-like barrier” without explaining the chosen reading. The transmitted active interpretation must likewise be assessed contextually.

“A concealed barrier” more visibly preserves the noun–adjective distinction.

A contextual explanation can describe a barrier obstructing reception of the Qur’anic message.

That follows from this and neighboring verses, not from mastūr in isolation.

4. Sitran: shelter from the sun in Al-Kahf 18:90

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغَ مَطْلِعَ الشَّمْسِ وَجَدَهَا تَطْلُعُ عَلَىٰ قَوْمٍ لَمْ نَجْعَلْ لَهُمْ مِنْ دُونِهَا سِتْرًا

ḥattā idhā balagha maṭliʿa al-shamsi wajadahā taṭluʿu ʿalā qawmin lam najʿal lahum min dūnihā sitrā

Al-Kahf 18:90

Translation:

“Until, when he reached the place of sunrise, he found it rising upon a people for whom We had provided no covering against it.”

Sitran is a noun denoting a covering or barrier between people and the sun.

The verse belongs to the account of Dhū al-Qarnayn’s journey.

Al-Kahf 18:89 says he followed a way; the next verse describes reaching the place of sunrise.

There he encounters people without a covering against the sun.

The meaning of sitr here

This use concerns covering and protection from a natural phenomenon.

A roof, canopy, wall, mountain, or other suitable barrier may intervene between someone and direct sunlight.

It may block direct light or some effect of exposure.

Arabic sitr can denote such a covering.

The word does not specify its type or material.

Sitran alone does not identify a house, clothing, mountain, tree, or particular structure.

The context states the absence of a covering against the sun for those people.

Did the people have no clothes or houses?

The verse alone does not establish that conclusion.

Lam najʿal lahum min dūnihā sitran concerns covering against the sun.

It need not mean the absence of every garment, shelter, resource, or social structure.

People might live without adequate shelter against direct sunlight.

They might possess structures or coverings ineffective against exposure in the situation described.

Natural conditions might likewise leave no effective covering between them and the sun.

Identifying the actual historical or geographical situation requires independent evidence.

One word cannot justify categorical claims about the entire society’s clothing, civilization, technology, or lifestyle.

Maṭliʿ al-shams and the observer’s position

Dhū al-Qarnayn reaches maṭliʿ al-shams and finds the sun rising upon a group.

The description occurs within his travel and observation.

Wajadahā taṭluʿu should not without evidence be converted into a physical model of the sun’s actual point of emergence in the universe.

Nor does sitr specify the people’s latitude or exact terrestrial location.

The root’s particular subject is shelter against the sun.

Difference from Al-Isrāʾ 17:45

Mastūr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 describes a barrier between the messenger and his audience.

Sitr in Al-Kahf 18:90 is a covering against the sun.

Both involve covering and separation.

Their subjects differ.

The first concerns reception of the Qur’anic message.

The second describes people’s exposure to sunlight.

The first verse’s perceptual or spiritual sense should not be imposed on the second.

Nor should the second’s material covering govern every use of the root.

Covering and separation are shared; context identifies the kind of covering.

5. Tastatirūna: concealment and bodily testimony in Fuṣṣilat 41:22

وَمَا كُنْتُمْ تَسْتَتِرُونَ أَنْ يَشْهَدَ عَلَيْكُمْ سَمْعُكُمْ وَلَا أَبْصَارُكُمْ وَلَا جُلُودُكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ ظَنَنْتُمْ أَنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَعْلَمُ كَثِيرًا مِمَّا تَعْمَلُونَ

wa-mā kuntum tastatirūna an yashhada ʿalaykum samʿukum wa-lā abṣārukum wa-lā julūdukum wa-lākin ẓanantum anna Allāha lā yaʿlamu kathīran mimmā taʿmalūn

Fuṣṣilat 41:22

Translation:

“You did not conceal yourselves against your hearing, eyes, and skins testifying against you; rather, you thought The God did not know much of what you did.”

Tastatirūna is a Form VIII imperfect verb.

It occurs within mā kuntum tastatirūna.

Mā negates the clause, while kuntum relates it to the audience’s past condition.

Unlike Al-Isrāʾ 17:45, this is a verbal expression concerning hiding or keeping oneself concealed.

The context of bodily testimony

Fuṣṣilat 41:20 describes hearing, eyes, and skins testifying to people’s deeds.

Fuṣṣilat 41:21 recounts their asking their skins why they testified against them.

The answer attributes speech to The God, who gives speech to everything.

Fuṣṣilat 41:22 then raises concealment.

Tastatirūna thus belongs to a context of testimony and accountability.

The subject is not sheltering from sunlight or standing behind a material curtain.

It concerns actions and testimony by bodily faculties present in performing or perceiving them.

From whom does someone hide?

Ordinarily, people can conceal actions from others.

They may do something in private that no other person knows about.

They may speak beyond others’ hearing.

Or act where no human witness is present.

Fuṣṣilat 41:22, however, concerns testimony by their own bodily faculties.

An agent cannot separate an action from every one of their own faculties as readily as conceal it from other people.

The accountability context shows that the audience had not reckoned with testimony by their hearing, eyes, and skins.

They supposed The God unaware of much of their conduct.

The issue includes a mistaken view of divine knowledge, not merely the lack of a material curtain.

Does tastatirūna mean concealing truth?

Istitār basically concerns concealment. In explaining this clause, readings also include failing to guard against such testimony or refrain from wrongdoing out of fear of it. These are contextual explanations, not a requirement to hide one’s body from its own parts.

The verb is not simply equivalent to kitmān.

Kitmān can concern withholding information or truth one knows.

Istitār can concern concealing the person from another’s sight or awareness.

Here concealment is discussed in relation to bodily testimony; the precise relation of an yashhada to tastatirūna requires grammatical and contextual analysis, not the root alone.

Concealing an act from others does not place it outside divine knowledge.

Tastatirūna itself does not mean denying divine knowledge.

That mistaken supposition is explicitly stated afterward:

وَلَٰكِنْ ظَنَنْتُمْ أَنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَعْلَمُ كَثِيرًا مِمَّا تَعْمَلُونَ

wa-lākin ẓanantum anna Allāha lā yaʿlamu kathīran mimmā taʿmalūn

Fuṣṣilat 41:22

Rather, you thought The God did not know much of what you did.

Concealment and the supposition about divine knowledge must be distinguished.

Is bodily testimony a known biological process?

These verses report bodily testimony and speech enabled by The God.

S T R does not disclose its biological or physical mechanism.

The verse alone does not establish that every action is stored in skin cells like recorded pictures or sound.

Nor can testimony at judgment be defined solely through present knowledge of memory, nerves, or tissues.

The verse states the testimony and the mistaken supposition limiting divine knowledge.

How that testimony occurs is a separate question.

Is every concealment blameworthy?

The verse does not make concealing anything from others automatically sinful.

Privacy, bodily covering, legitimate confidentiality, and protection of personal matters can have valid purposes.

Sitr in Al-Kahf 18:90 also concerns protection against the sun.

S T R therefore has no inherent negative moral meaning.

Fuṣṣilat 41:22 censures a particular group’s conduct and mistaken supposition about divine knowledge.

That distinction must be retained.

6. Distinctions from neighboring roots

S T R and Ḥ J B

Ḥ J B concerns intervening and preventing access, sight, or direct contact.

S T R concerns covering and concealing.

The semantic fields are close.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 combines the families.

Ḥijāb names the veil or barrier.

Mastūr describes its concealment in the reading adopted here.

Two roots can thus describe different aspects of a shared situation.

No absolute boundary should be imposed between them.

A curtain covering something may be both sātir and a ḥijāb.

The precise distinction follows from construction and usage.

S T R and K T M

Kitmān concerns withholding something known or possessed.

S T R has a broader field of covering and concealing.

Covering an object with cloth is satr but not necessarily withholding truth.

Deliberately withholding information one ought to disclose raises the issue of kitmān.

Al-Baqarah 2:159 addresses concealing clear signs and guidance within the responsibility to disclose truth.

Sitr in Al-Kahf 18:90, however, is shelter from the sun.

Kitmān’s ethical and epistemic associations should not be imposed on every satr usage.

S T R and KH F Y

KH F Y concerns hiddenness and lack of visibility.

S T R may name the act or covering that produces concealment.

The families overlap in this respect.

Not everything hidden is behind a material curtain.

Distance, limited perception, or lack of knowledge may also cause hiddenness.

S T R can denote a particular barrier restricting sight or direct access.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule for every usage.

S T R and K N N

K N N concerns covering, protection, and being within a covering.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:46 uses akinnah for coverings over hearts.

Its context is closely related to ḥijāban mastūran in Al-Isrāʾ 17:45.

Akinnah and mastūr nevertheless have different roots.

The heart coverings appear alongside heaviness in the ears and failure to understand the message.

These clarify the barrier’s context without all becoming meanings of mastūr itself.

S T R and GH F R

GH F R concerns covering and protection in Arabic and also divine forgiveness in Qur’anic usage.

S T R likewise concerns covering.

A shared image does not make their entire ranges identical.

People may conceal conduct without eliminating its responsibility or consequences.

Divine forgiveness should likewise not be reduced to hiding a fault from others.

Qur’anic forgiveness is connected with repentance, mercy, responsibility, and divine judgment.

S T R and GH F R should not be exchanged in translation without contextual grounds.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Important meanings in the family

Satara: covered; concealed.

Satr: covering and concealing.

Sitr: a covering, curtain, or barrier to sight and direct exposure.

Sutrah: a covering or barrier used for protection or concealment.

Sitār and sitārah: curtain and covering.

Sātir: covering; concealing.

Mastūr: covered; concealed.

Istatara: covered oneself; hid.

Istitār: hiding or keeping oneself concealed.

Tasattara: covered or concealed oneself.

Satīr and satīrah: in human usage, chaste and modest.

Proposed renderings of Qur’anic forms

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:45:

حِجَابًا مَسْتُورًا

ḥijāban mastūran

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45

“A concealed barrier.”

This preserves the passive-participle construction.

“A concealing barrier” is an attested reading worth examining, but approaches an active meaning and requires grounds for preference.

In Al-Kahf 18:90:

لَمْ نَجْعَلْ لَهُمْ مِنْ دُونِهَا سِتْرًا

lam najʿal lahum min dūnihā sitran

Al-Kahf 18:90

“We had provided no covering for them against it.”

Covering is natural here.

The explanation may identify shelter against the sun.

No particular shelter type should be inserted into the base translation without evidence.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:22:

وَمَا كُنْتُمْ تَسْتَتِرُونَ أَنْ يَشْهَدَ عَلَيْكُمْ سَمْعُكُمْ وَلَا أَبْصَارُكُمْ وَلَا جُلُودُكُمْ

wa-mā kuntum tastatirūna an yashhada ʿalaykum samʿukum wa-lā abṣārukum wa-lā julūdukum

Fuṣṣilat 41:22

“You did not conceal yourselves against your hearing, eyes, and skins testifying against you.”

The rendering follows the concealment sense. Explaining the relation to testimony requires noting the contextual reading of failing to guard against it or refrain from wrongdoing for fear of it.

Bodily testimony comes from the following construction, not the root itself.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meaning

For the brief root box:

“Covering and concealing; being behind a covering or barrier.”

For Al-Isrāʾ 17:45:

“The concealment of the barrier placed between Muhammad and those who do not believe in the Hereafter.”

For Al-Kahf 18:90:

“Covering or shelter against the sun, absent for the people described.”

For Fuṣṣilat 41:22:

“Concealing oneself in relation to testimony by hearing, eyes, and skins concerning human deeds.”

Preserve the exact forms mastūran, sitran, and tastatirūna in the word boxes.

Mastūr is a passive participle.

Sitr names a covering.

Tastatirūna is a Form VIII imperfect verb addressing a plural audience.

Keep these grammatical distinctions clear in verse-specific explanations. These proposals do not replace existing authored word-box text.

8. Conclusion

S T R concerns covering, concealing, and being behind a covering.

Poetry attributed to Zuhayr uses sitr for a barrier against wrongdoing while the way to good remains open.

Subject to the transmission limits noted above, this witness supports early figurative use of covering to describe ethical relationships.

Lexical records attest curtains, coverings, hiding, covering others, and some uses concerning modesty and respectable conduct.

These should not be reduced to one fixed moral meaning without examination.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an.

Mastūr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 describes a barrier between Muhammad and a particular audience.

Its direct passive sense is concealed, though the active reading concealing is also discussed grammatically.

The context points to a barrier in receiving the message.

Sitr in Al-Kahf 18:90 denotes shelter against the sun.

This illustrates material, protective usage without specifying the shelter type or the people’s way of life.

Tastatirūna in Fuṣṣilat 41:22 concerns concealment, with contextual explanations involving failing to guard against testimony.

It appears in the setting of hearing, eyes, and skins testifying to human deeds.

The false supposition about divine knowledge comes from another clause, not the verb’s lexical meaning.

These uses encompass material covering and concealment relating to perception and awareness.

Something hidden need not be destroyed, nonexistent, or ineffective.

Covering itself has no fixed moral value.

It may protect.

It may preserve legitimate privacy.

Or it may relate to wrongful secrecy in another context.

The subject and context determine the value of each use.

S T R denotes covering or concealing something by a covering or barrier to sight or direct access. It can function in material, perceptual, and behavioral settings without every use sharing one referent or moral value.

For Forqan, “concealed” for mastūr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:45, “covering” for sitr in Al-Kahf 18:90, and “you were concealing yourselves” for tastatirūna in Fuṣṣilat 41:22 are suitable lexical equivalents; the last must retain the sentence’s negation.

The family emphasizes covering or concealment; restricting access, knowingly withholding truth, hiddenness from perception, and divine forgiveness each require examination through their own words and contexts.
