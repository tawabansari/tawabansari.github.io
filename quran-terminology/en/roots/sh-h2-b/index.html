Visible whiteness, a bright flame and a celestial streak of light: early Arabic usage, luminous phenomena and the guarding of heaven in the Quran

The Arabic root SH H B has two important semantic fields: visible whiteness, especially when mixed with darkness or another color, and a bright flame or luminous phenomenon appearing in the sky.

Shuhbah describes a color in which whiteness is apparent without necessarily being pure or uniform. White hairs among dark hairs, or a covering combining light and dark, may be described with this family of words.

Shihāb denotes a clear, bright flame. It may burn on a piece of wood and provide light or warmth. In celestial usage it also denotes a brilliant phenomenon that appears swiftly against the dark sky as a streak of light or a passing flame.

These uses share brightness and the appearance of light against a dark background. That common feature does not prove that every meaning developed historically from a single original image.

The Quran uses shihāb in two settings: in Moses’ story for a flame that can be taken from a fire and brought to his family, and elsewhere for celestial shuhub associated with eavesdropping and its prevention.

The distinction is fundamental. Not every Quranic shihāb is a celestial phenomenon, and the word alone does not determine its physical properties, origin or mechanism of movement.

1. The root in early Arabic

Whiteness mixed with darkness

Early Arabic records shuhbah for a color in which white is visible together with black or another color.

Lexical explanations sometimes describe white interrupted by darkness, and sometimes white predominating over a dark color.

Ashhab describes something of that color; shahbāʾ is its feminine form.

For hair, ashhab can denote white mixed with dark. It is also used of animals whose coats combine light and dark hairs.

For example, a horse with white hairs among dark ones may be called ashhab. However, this description has not been applied to every equine coat color uniformly or without disagreement.

Some early sources differ over precisely which horse color shuhbah describes. This cautions against imposing one very narrow color definition on every use of the root.

An early poetic witness

A line attributed to Maqqās al-ʿĀʾidhī, described in some sources as a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

فِدًى لِبَنِي ذُهْلِ بْنِ شَيْبَانَ نَاقَتِي

fidan li-banī dhuhli bni shaybāna nāqatī

إِذَا كَانَ يَوْمٌ ذُو كَوَاكِبَ أَشْهَبُ

idhā kāna yawmun dhū kawākiba ashhabu

Translation:

“May my she-camel be a ransom for Banū Dhuhl ibn Shaybān when a harsh, dusky-white day, as though filled with stars, arrives.”

The poet describes a difficult, tumultuous day. Ashhab qualifies the day; dhū kawākib evokes its severity, as though darkness had made stars visible by day. It is not necessarily a report of actual stars in the sky.

Early explanations connect the image with battle and hardship. The gleam of weapons amid dusty darkness is one proposed explanation, not the inevitable meaning of every word in the line.

Ashhab here does not mean the sun, a star or a flame itself; it describes a mingling of light and darkness.

The verse is transmitted in early Arabic works. Its poet’s classification and the independent date of this particular line do not establish a certain pre-Quranic date. It helps explain early ashhab without dating the development of every branch of the root.

Ashāhib: whiteness and descriptions of people

A line attributed to al-Aʿshā, the pre-Islamic poet, reads:

وَبَنِي الْمُنْذِرِ الْأَشَاهِبِ بِالْحِيـ

wa-banī al-mundhiri al-ashāhibi bi-l-ḥī-

رَةِ يَمْشُونَ غُدْوَةً كَالسُّيُوفِ

rati yamshūna ghudwatan ka-l-suyūfi

Translation:

“And the sons of al-Mundhir, the fair ones of al-Ḥīrah, walking in the morning like swords.”

Ashāhib describes the sons of al-Mundhir here.

The lexical explanation connects this description with their beauty. Fairness and brightness may inform the image, but dignity and high status should not be added as certain lexical meanings of ashāhib.

The witness shows that this family could describe people. It does not make the Quranic shihāb mean a beautiful person, warrior or person of rank.

Here ashāhib describes people; in the Quran shihāb names a flame or luminous phenomenon. A common root does not make every derivative identical in meaning.

A bright flame

Arabic records al-shihāb as a clear and bright flame of fire.

Such a flame can be taken from a larger fire on a piece of wood or other combustible material.

In this use, shihāb does not simply name the wood; it concerns the bright, burning fire carried with it.

For example, someone beside a large fire on a cold night may light a stick and carry its flame away, carrying a shihāb.

This use directly concerns nighttime activity, travel, lighting and warmth.

Some lexical explanations define shihāb as wood bearing a bright fire; others make the brilliant flame central.

The explanations need not conflict. One emphasizes the flame, the other the material carrying it.

Moses’ story in Al-Naml 27:7 clearly exemplifies this use.

The celestial shihāb in early language

Classical Arabic also uses shihāb for a bright phenomenon seen in the night sky, moving swiftly or appearing as a passing light.

Older language might call it a kawkab munqaḍḍ, a “falling star.” That describes its appearance to an observer on Earth, rather than necessarily providing a precise classification of celestial bodies.

Three questions must be distinguished: the phenomenon’s existence in nature, its observation and naming in early language, and the scientific explanation of how it occurs.

Some poems attributed to the pre-Islamic period describe the falling or swift movement of bright celestial objects. Yet the historical attribution of verses later cited for shihāb is not uniformly reliable.

For example, a line containing shihāb al-qadhf and attributed to al-Afwah al-Awdī was challenged as inauthentic in early literary criticism. It cannot therefore be treated as certain evidence of that expression before Islam.

What can be established is that “bright flame” was understood in the Arabic of revelation; the Quran itself uses shihāb for both portable fire and a luminous celestial phenomenon.

Exactly when the celestial use developed, and from which semantic branch, requires further historical evidence.

2. Quranic forms and core meaning

The root occurs five times in five Quranic verses, in two nominal forms:

Shihāb in Al-Ḥijr 15:18, Al-Naml 27:7, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10 and Al-Jinn 72:9; and its plural shuhub in Al-Jinn 72:8.

Shihāb is a singular noun on the fiʿāl pattern. Shuhub is its broken plural.

Shihābun, bi-shihābin and shihāban are case forms of the same noun, not independent derivatives.

Verbs and descriptions such as shahuba, shahiba, ashhab, shahbāʾ and ish-hābba are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Quran.

The two main Quranic uses suggest the following core meaning:

A flame or bright luminous phenomenon whose light is conspicuous, whether fire or a brilliant appearance in the sky.

This emphasizes perceptible brightness but does not itself specify motion, speed, pursuit, guarding or the burning of another being.

Where the Quran mentions such features, they come from the verbs, descriptions and context.

The color term shuhbah must also remain distinct from the noun shihāb. Whiteness in the root’s broader semantic field does not prove that every Quranic shihāb is necessarily white.

3. Shihāb in Moses’ story: a flame for light and warmth

Al-Naml 27:7 states:

إِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِأَهْلِهِ إِنِّي آنَسْتُ نَارًا سَآتِيكُمْ مِنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ آتِيكُمْ بِشِهَابٍ قَبَسٍ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ

idh qāla mūsā li-ahlihi innī ānastu nāran sa-ātīkum minhā bi-khabarin aw ātīkum bi-shihābin qabasin laʿallakum taṣṭalūn

Al-Naml 27:7

Translation:

“When Moses said to his family: I have perceived a fire. I will bring you news from it, or bring you a flame taken from the fire, so that you may warm yourselves.”

In the story Moses sees a fire while traveling and tells his family he will bring news or a flame from it for warmth. The verse places the hope expressed by laʿalla in “so that you may warm yourselves,” rather than making the promise to bring something explicitly tentative.

Shihāb here concerns a bright, burning portion of fire that can be taken and brought to others.

The subject is not a passing celestial phenomenon. Moses speaks of a fire he believes he can approach and bring something from.

Shihāb and qabas

The phrase bi-shihābin qabasin places two words together.

Shihāb refers to the bright flame.

Qabas belongs to Q B S and concerns fire taken from another fire, such as a flame carried to kindle a new fire or provide warmth.

Qabas therefore clarifies that the flame is taken from a fire.

In the installed Ḥafṣ reading, both words are indefinite and genitive: bi-shihābin qabasin. Another recognized reading uses the construct phrase bi-shihābi qabasin.

The structural difference does not change the basic purpose: Moses speaks of bringing a burning flame taken from the fire he has seen.

The two words do not imply that he intends to bring two separate things, one shihāb and the other qabas.

Comparison with jadhwah in Moses’ story

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:29 describes the same event with another expression:

لَعَلِّي آتِيكُمْ مِنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ جَذْوَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ

laʿallī ātīkum minhā bi-khabarin aw jadhwatin mina al-nāri laʿallakum taṣṭalūn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:29

Translation:

“Perhaps I may bring you news from it, or a burning piece from the fire, so that you may warm yourselves.”

Here jadhwatin mina al-nār replaces shihāb qabas.

Jadhwah concerns a piece of wood or other material bearing fire and may describe glowing embers or fire remaining in wood.

Shihāb gives greater prominence to the visible, bright flame.

Both expressions occur in a shared setting and serve the same purpose: bringing fire with which Moses’ family can warm themselves.

The difference does not prove that Moses necessarily envisaged a tall flame in one account and a flameless coal in the other. The phrases alone do not establish those details.

The main distinction is the descriptive emphasis: shihāb foregrounds brightness and flame; jadhwah is closer to a burning piece of material.

Why is shihāb not celestial here?

Because other verses associate shihāb with the sky, one might try to restrict every occurrence to a single kind of celestial phenomenon.

That restriction is incompatible with Al-Naml 27:7.

Moses speaks of a fire he has seen and intends to bring a flame from. Laʿallakum taṣṭalūn gives the purpose of warming themselves.

The direct meaning here is therefore a bright, portable flame.

This illustrates why one occurrence must not be chosen and its meaning imposed on all derivatives and contexts of a root.

4. Celestial shihāb: brightness, pursuit and prevention

Elsewhere shihāb appears in a celestial setting connected with preventing eavesdropping.

Al-Ḥijr 15:18 states:

إِلَّا مَنِ اسْتَرَقَ السَّمْعَ فَأَتْبَعَهُ شِهَابٌ مُبِينٌ

illā mani istaraqa al-samʿa fa-atbaʿahu shihābun mubīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:18

Translation:

“Except one who listens stealthily, whom a visibly bright shihāb then pursues.”

Shihāb is qualified here by mubīn.

Mubīn belongs to B Y N and here concerns being visible and manifest.

Shihāb mubīn is thus a shihāb whose brightness and appearance are clear.

The description alone specifies neither size nor material nor speed.

The meaning of fa-atbaʿahu

Fa-atbaʿahu belongs to T B ʿ and concerns following, pursuing or bringing something after another.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:18, shihāb is the grammatical subject, and -hu refers to the one who listened stealthily.

The sentence therefore says that a shihāb pursues that listener.

Pursuit is conveyed by the verb, not by the noun shihāb itself.

Shihāb alone does not mean “pursuer.” The same word names the flame to be brought in Moses’ story, not something following another person.

Nor does “following” alone specify every detail of impact, contact or the outcome of pursuit. Those details require other evidence.

Forqan’s study of T B ʿ is relevant to the meanings of following and pursuit.

Study of T B ʿ

Shihāb thāqib in Al-Ṣāffāt

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10 states:

إِلَّا مَنْ خَطِفَ الْخَطْفَةَ فَأَتْبَعَهُ شِهَابٌ ثَاقِبٌ

illā man khaṭifa al-khaṭfata fa-atbaʿahu shihābun thāqib

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10

Translation:

“Except one who snatches something away, whom a shihāb of piercing brightness then pursues.”

Here shihāb is qualified by thāqib.

Thāqib belongs to TH Q B, whose material uses concern piercing, perforating and penetrating.

When describing a luminous object or phenomenon, it can evoke penetrating brightness: light manifest in darkness, as though piercing it.

Al-Ṭāriq 86:3 also uses al-najmu al-thāqib. The shared description matters, but does not by itself prove identical physical natures for everything described as najmun thāqib and shihābun thāqib.

In Al-Ṣāffāt, thāqib describes a quality of the shihāb; the noun still names the brilliant phenomenon.

Shihāb mubīn and shihāb thāqib

The distinction between these phrases should be retained.

Shihāb mubīn foregrounds visibility and clarity.

Shihāb thāqib foregrounds penetrating brightness. This need not be converted into a definite physical perforation of a body.

The two descriptions can coexist in one luminous phenomenon without being exact synonyms.

Mubīn does not mean thāqib, nor thāqib mubīn. Each comes from a separate root and supplies a different descriptive element.

5. Shuhub and the guarding of heaven in Al-Jinn

Al-Jinn 72:8 states:

وَأَنَّا لَمَسْنَا السَّمَاءَ فَوَجَدْنَاهَا مُلِئَتْ حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا وَشُهُبًا

wa-annā lamasnā al-samāʾa fa-wajadnāhā muliʾat ḥarasan shadīdan wa-shuhubā

Al-Jinn 72:8

Translation:

“We sought access to heaven and found it filled with stern guards and shuhub.”

This belongs to the speech of a group of jinn after hearing the Quran.

Shuhuban is the plural of shihāb.

The verse places two expressions together: ḥarasan shadīdan and shuhuban.

Ḥaras concerns guarding and guards; shuhub denotes the luminous phenomena under discussion.

Their conjunction names guards and shuhub separately, but conjunction alone does not determine their entire relationship in nature or function.

Several elements may cooperate in guarding without all being identical in nature.

The verse does not therefore establish that a shihāb is necessarily an intelligent being or identical to a guard. Nor should it be declared, without evidence, a purely mechanical instrument unrelated to guarding.

The text describes both as present in this situation; their precise relationship requires separate examination.

Shihāban raṣadan

Al-Jinn 72:9 states:

وَأَنَّا كُنَّا نَقْعُدُ مِنْهَا مَقَاعِدَ لِلسَّمْعِ فَمَنْ يَسْتَمِعِ الْآنَ يَجِدْ لَهُ شِهَابًا رَصَدًا

wa-annā kunnā naqʿudu minhā maqāʿida li-l-samʿi fa-man yastamiʿi al-āna yajid lahu shihāban raṣadā

Al-Jinn 72:9

Translation:

“We used to sit at places there to listen, but whoever listens now finds a shihāb lying in wait for him.”

Here shihāb occurs with raṣadan.

Raṣad belongs to R Ṣ D and concerns watching, guarding, waiting and readiness to encounter something.

The shihāb is described as lying in wait for someone intending to listen.

Grammatically, raṣadan can function as a description meaning watchful or lying in wait; it is explained as a verbal noun used descriptively.

That feature comes from raṣad, not from SH H B itself.

The phrase therefore presents a shihāb in a role of waiting or prevention.

It does not by itself give the shihāb independent will or intelligence. Nor does it specify the material mechanism by which it occupies that position; this limit on physical description does not deny the divine agency presented in the wider Quranic context.

The contrast between kunnā and al-āna

The speakers distinguish the past from the present.

They say they formerly sat in places to listen, whereas someone listening now encounters a shihāb lying in wait.

The contrast concerns a change in access and listening.

It does not establish that shuhub had never existed in nature before that moment.

The verse concerns listening and its prevention, rather than the first appearance of every luminous celestial phenomenon.

In Al-Jinn 72:10, the speakers explicitly say they do not know whether harm is intended for Earth’s inhabitants or their Lord intends right guidance for them.

Their account thus distinguishes observing a change from knowing all its causes and purposes.

Without independent evidence, these verses cannot supply a complete account of heaven’s structure or access to unseen knowledge.

6. Celestial shihāb and modern scientific knowledge

In modern Persian, shihāb usually names the bright streak that appears briefly in the night sky and disappears.

In astronomy, a meteor is the luminous phenomenon produced when a meteoroid enters an atmosphere at high speed and interacts with its gases. An observer may see a bright streak or a short-lived flash.

This differs from an actual star.

A star is a celestial body seen from a great distance. A meteor observed in Earth’s atmosphere is not a star torn from its position and falling to Earth.

If some of the incoming material survives its passage through the atmosphere and reaches Earth’s surface, it is called a meteorite in modern scientific terminology.

The distinction helps avoid confusing different phenomena.

However, the full modern physical definition of a meteor should not be imported automatically into every Quranic use of shihāb.

In Al-Naml 27:7, shihāb is the flame Moses intends to take from a fire and bring back. The celestial passages present a different setting.

The shared name alone does not prove identical physical composition in every use.

Astronomical observations and theories about natural meteors do not by themselves explain every detail of the Quran’s account of jinn, eavesdropping and the guarding of heaven.

Conversely, uncertainty about those matters does not authorize inventing mechanisms, instruments or specific physical properties for the shihāb.

One can understand the word’s clear meaning and study natural phenomena while distinguishing that knowledge from the unseen subject of the verses.

Shihāb and stars in the Quran

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:6 describes the nearest heaven as adorned with kawākib. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:7, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:8, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10 then address protection against rebellious devils and the shihāb thāqib.

Kawkab and shihāb are different words.

Kawkab denotes a bright celestial object; shihāb in the passage discussed focuses on a brilliant, flame-like phenomenon.

Al-Mulk 67:5 also mentions heavenly lamps and their role in driving away devils.

These passages are thematically related to the shuhub in Al-Ḥijr, Al-Ṣāffāt and Al-Jinn. Determining the exact relation between celestial objects, bright phenomena and the means of repelling devils requires examining the complete constructions.

Shihāb alone does not establish that great stars leave their orbits and are hurled toward Earth or other beings.

Likewise, rajm in another verse does not physically restrict all shuhub to a single kind of rocky body.

The word’s meaning, its described motion and its stated purpose are distinct levels of analysis.

7. Distinctions from related roots

SH H B and N W R

Nūr concerns light and has varied sensory and spiritual uses in the Quran.

The Quranic shihāb denotes a particular bright flame or luminous phenomenon.

A shihāb gives light, but not every light is a shihāb.

Sunlight, moonlight, lamplight and the light of guidance cannot all be called shihāb merely because they involve light.

Nūr therefore has a broader semantic field, while shihāb names a particular bright flame or phenomenon in these Quranic uses.

SH H B and SH ʿ L

SH ʿ L concerns kindling and blazing.

In Maryam 19:4, wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shayban portrays the spread of white hair through the verb of blazing.

A shihāb may be a flame, but the noun is not equivalent in every context to the act of becoming aflame.

Ishtiʿāl can foreground kindling or the spread of flame; shihāb names the bright flame or luminous phenomenon.

A fiery shihāb thus belongs within the field of flame, without every use of ishtiʿāl referring to shihāb.

SH H B and Q B S

Q B S concerns taking fire from another source.

Shihāb concerns the bright flame.

In Al-Naml 27:7 they occur together to describe a flame taken from a fire.

Their combination shows a close relation without making the words interchangeable in every sentence.

Shihāb describes the flame and its brightness; qabas foregrounds its being taken from a source.

SH H B and J DH W

Jadhwah concerns a piece of burning material, such as wood or a glowing ember.

In the fire context, shihāb more strongly evokes a bright flame.

The two accounts of Moses’ story use these words for what he intends to bring from the fire.

They differ in descriptive emphasis, rather than necessarily naming entirely separate kinds of fire.

SH H B and TH Q B

TH Q B concerns piercing and penetration.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10, thāqib qualifies the shihāb and can describe its piercing brightness; it does not necessarily assert literal perforation of a body.

In Al-Ṭāriq 86:3, thāqib likewise qualifies najm.

Shihāb names the phenomenon; thāqib describes a quality.

The pairing does not make “piercer” the independent meaning of shihāb.

SH H B and R Ṣ D

R Ṣ D concerns watching, guarding and waiting to encounter something.

In Al-Jinn 72:9, shihāban raṣadan describes a shihāb lying in wait.

Shihāb names the luminous phenomenon; raṣad describes its role in that setting.

Lying in wait is therefore not an essential part of shihāb’s lexical definition.

SH H B and B R Q

B R Q concerns sudden, passing brightness and is familiar in natural usage as lightning.

A shihāb may also appear as a passing light in the sky.

That visual resemblance does not make the phenomena identical.

In modern physical understanding, lightning is an atmospheric electrical phenomenon, whereas meteors arise when small incoming objects interact with the atmosphere.

Similar brightness and sudden appearance therefore do not establish a shared root or identical physical nature.

SH H B and SH Y B

SH Y B concerns whitening hair and old age.

Shuhbah in the color branch concerns white mixed with dark and can also describe hair.

The families overlap in the field of hair color but remain separate roots.

Shayb is especially associated with white hair and aging; shuhbah may also describe animal coats, objects’ coverings or mixtures of light and dark.

Shayb occurs in the Quran, while shuhbah does not occur there as a color noun.

Although the image of spreading brightness in Maryam 19:4 may seem close to the field of shihāb, shayb belongs to SH Y B and must not be confused with SH H B.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

In the fire context, “bright flame” or “burning flame” is suitable for shihāb.

In Al-Naml 27:7, where qabas accompanies it, a rendering is:

“Or I will bring you a flame taken from the fire, so that you may warm yourselves.”

Shihāb supplies the flame and brightness; qabas supplies its being taken from a fire.

For celestial uses, the familiar Persian word shihāb is natural and appropriate. It need not be replaced in the basic Persian translation with “falling star,” “space rock” or “fireball.”

Those alternatives are either more specialized or add details of material and motion not necessarily conveyed by the Quranic word itself.

The proposed renderings follow. They do not change the installed verse translations:

Al-Ḥijr 15:18

“Except one who listens stealthily, whom a visibly bright shihāb then pursues.”

Al-Naml 27:7

“…Or I will bring you a flame taken from the fire, so that you may warm yourselves.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10

“Except one who snatches something away, whom a shihāb of piercing brightness then pursues.”

Al-Jinn 72:8

“We sought access to heaven and found it filled with stern guards and shuhub.”

Al-Jinn 72:9

“We used to sit at places there to listen, but whoever listens now finds a shihāb lying in wait for him.”

Mubīn, thāqib and raṣad remain distinct descriptions rather than being absorbed into the meaning of shihāb itself.

In the color branch, ashhab may be rendered “white mixed with dark,” “grayish” or “light-colored,” depending on context. None should replace the Quranic noun shihāb.

Concluding synthesis

SH H B concerns visible whiteness, particularly white mixed with dark, as well as a bright and brilliant flame.

Ashhab and shahbāʾ describe color, and early poetic witnesses illustrate that field. In the Arabic of revelation, shihāb names a bright flame and a luminous appearance in the sky.

These uses should not be reduced to an unproven historical image. The color meaning does not make every shihāb white, nor does “flame” establish that every Quranic shihāb has the physical nature of an ordinary fire.

The Quran uses the two forms shihāb and shuhub.

In Al-Naml 27:7, shihāb is the flame Moses intends to take from a fire and bring to his family to provide warmth.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:18, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10, Al-Jinn 72:8 and Al-Jinn 72:9, it appears in a celestial setting associated with eavesdropping, pursuit and guarding.

The descriptions differ: mubīn highlights visibility, thāqib penetrating brightness, and raṣad the position of watching or lying in wait.

None of these features should replace the independent meaning of SH H B.

The passages about guarding heaven do not alone determine the shihāb’s physical composition, how it is directed or every mechanism connecting beings with the unseen. What the Quran says should be retained without filling those gaps with outside stories or unestablished scientific theories.

The final definition in Quranic usage is therefore:

In its color branch, SH H B concerns visible whiteness, sometimes mixed with darkness. The Quranic shihāb names a flame or luminous phenomenon with conspicuous brightness. It can be a flame taken from a fire or a brilliant celestial appearance; its origin, motion and role are understood from each verse’s context rather than from the noun alone.

This preserves both Quranic uses without forcing one use’s physical, celestial or protective features onto the other.
