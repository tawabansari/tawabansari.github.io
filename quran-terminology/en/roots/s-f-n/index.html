The root S F N: scraping, abrasion, and ships

Qur’anic and lexical forms

The Arabic root S F N concerns scraping, rubbing, and removing a layer from a surface. Safana al-shayʾ means scraping or peeling something, while al-safan names an implement used to scrape and smooth wood or other materials.

Safīnah, the name for a ship, also belongs to this family: a vessel used to move people and goods over water.

Lexical accounts offer several connections between the name and scraping. Some connect it with cutting through or pushing aside water; others with tools used to shape timber for construction. These explanations are semantically intelligible, but none should be treated as the proven origin without independent historical evidence.

The Qur’an uses this root only through safīnah. Its occurrences belong to two narratives: the vessel of needy people working at sea in the account of Moses and his knowledgeable companion, and Noah’s vessel, which carried him and his companions to safety from the flood.

In both accounts, the ship is a real means of transport and preserving life. The first also concerns ownership, work, and encroachment on others’ property; the second emphasizes rescue and the event’s significance as a sign.

1. The root in early Arabic

Safn: scraping and removing an outer layer

Classical Arabic explains al-safn through al-qashr: scraping or removing a covering or outer layer.

Safana al-ʿūd means scraping wood and taking layers from its surface.

An implement or friction removes material from an object. The purpose may be smoothing a surface, shaping timber, or removing rough and excess material.

A lexical definition identifies the family’s core as removing something from the surface of something else.

It begins with a concrete action: an object has a surface, and abrasion, scraping, or contact removes part of that surface.

Safanat al-rīḥ al-turāb also describes wind removing soil from the ground or reducing and dispersing it through its action.

Here wind acts on the earth’s surface, rather than a person using a woodworking tool.

The shared feature is contact and motion over a surface, removing or shifting some of its material.

This image should not be generalized to every kind of movement. Motion alone is not safn; the established verb concerns scraping, abrasion, or removing material from a surface.

A poetic witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays contains:

فَجَاءَ خَفِيًّا يَسْفِنُ الْأَرْضَ بَطْنُهُ
تَرَى التُّرْبَ مِنْهُ لَاصِقًا كُلَّ مَلْصَقِ

fa-jāʾa khafiyyan yasfinu al-arḍa baṭnuhu / tarā al-turba minhu lāṣiqan kulla malṣaqi

“He came stealthily, his belly scraping the ground, with earth visibly clinging to him everywhere.”

Yasfinu al-arḍa baṭnuhu depicts the belly contacting and rubbing against the ground.

The moving figure stays close to the earth, his belly touching the soil as he advances.

Yasfinu here therefore means scraping and frictional contact with the ground, rather than travelling or floating.

The line is transmitted among the poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and in early lexical accounts. His pre-Islamic lifetime does not by itself establish the precise date of every line or its transmitted wording.

Within that limitation, the witness illuminates an early use of safn for scraping and abrasion.

Safan and misfan: scraping implements

Arabic records al-safan and al-misfan for implements used to scrape wood or other materials.

Such implements could be made of iron, stone, or rough animal skin.

Safan is also recorded for an adze or similar tool with which a carpenter cuts a wooden surface.

Rough skins from certain fish and other animals were also used to rub and smooth wood, arrows, sword handles, and other objects.

In these uses, safan names the implement, while the verb safana expresses its action.

A half-line attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā depicts cutting wood with al-safan:

ضَرْبًا كَنَحْتِ جُذُوعِ الْأَثْلِ بِالسَّفَنِ

ḍarban ka-naḥti judhūʿi al-athli bi-al-safani

“Blows like hewing tamarisk trunks with a cutting implement.”

Al-safan here names the tool, not a ship.

These usages connect the family with woodworking and finishing surfaces in everyday Arabic life.

The tool sense alone does not prove that the name for a ship derives from that implement; the derivation requires independent evidence.

Safīn: ships in pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī reads:

وَهُنَّ كَذَاكَ حِينَ قَطَعْنَ فَلْجًا
كَأَنَّ حُمُولَهُنَّ عَلَى سَفِينِ

wa-hunna kadhāka ḥīna qaṭaʿna faljan / ka-anna ḥumūlahunna ʿalā safīni

يُشَبَّهْنَ السَّفِينَ وَهُنَّ بُخْتٌ
عُرَاضَاتُ الْأَبَاهِرِ وَالشُّؤُونِ

yushabbahna al-safīna wa-hunna bukhtun / ʿurāḍātu al-abāhiri wa-al-shuʾūni

“As they crossed Falj, it was as though their loads were borne on ships. Those broad-backed Bactrian camels resembled ships.”

The poet compares the camels’ movement and the loads they bear with ships.

Safīn here is a collective plural meaning ships.

The image draws on the resemblance between large laden camels and vessels carrying cargo.

The witness matters because the word already names a familiar means of transport. The poet can liken camels to ships because the audience recognizes their appearance and function.

This usage is independent of Qur’anic interpretation.

The poem survives in the early poetic tradition attributed to al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī. He is counted among pre-Islamic poets; his lifetime and the exact dating of each transmitted line nevertheless remain separate questions.

Both ḥumūlahunna and ḥudūjahunna are transmitted in versions of the poem. The variant does not alter safīn’s basic meaning as ships.

The witness shows that the ship word and its plural were familiar names for real waterborne transport in early Arabic, rather than originating as a religious technical term.

Why is a ship called safīnah?

Lexical accounts record several explanations of the name.

The first connects safīnah with a vessel moving over water as though scraping or pushing aside its surface.

This analysis takes the pattern faʿīlah in an active sense: something performing safn.

A second explanation connects the name with worked timber. Ships were built from wood shaped with scraping tools, so the name is associated with their construction.

Here faʿīlah can be understood passively: something on which the work of scraping has been performed.

A third explanation concerns contact with sand or a shallow bed. In shallow water, a vessel’s bottom may scrape sand or ground.

All three explanations relate to attested scraping and rubbing senses of safn.

A conceivable explanation is nevertheless different from a demonstrated historical derivation.

A ship normally floats on water; “scraping the water” is a linguistic image for its motion, not a claim that water is shaved like wood.

Nor does construction from shaped timber by itself prove that the name derives from a carpenter’s implement.

Safīnah’s established lexical meaning is a waterborne vessel. Its connection with scraping is discussed in lexical accounts, but the precise history of the name should not be asserted as certain.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

S F N occurs four times in three Qur’anic verses.

Every occurrence belongs to the noun safīnah.

The attested forms are:

السَّفِينَةِ: the ship, definite and genitive; in Al-Kahf 18:71 and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15.

السَّفِينَةُ: the ship, definite and nominative; in Al-Kahf 18:79.

سَفِينَةٍ: a ship, indefinite and genitive; also in Al-Kahf 18:79.

These are three case or definiteness forms, rather than three semantically independent derivatives.

Safīnah is a feminine singular noun. Its recorded plurals include sufun, safāʾin, and safīn.

These plurals do not occur in the Qur’an in those forms.

The verb safana, verbal noun safn, implement noun misfan, and saffān for a mariner or shipbuilder are also recorded in Arabic, but not in those forms in the Qur’an.

In Qur’anic usage safīnah names a vessel that people board and travel in over water.

The word itself does not determine its size, hull material, passenger numbers, propulsion, or destination.

Such features require separate descriptions.

3. Safīnah in the account of Moses and his knowledgeable companion

Boarding and making a hole in the ship

Al-Kahf 18:71 states:

فَانْطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا رَكِبَا فِي السَّفِينَةِ خَرَقَهَا قَالَ أَخَرَقْتَهَا لِتُغْرِقَ أَهْلَهَا لَقَدْ جِئْتَ شَيْئًا إِمْرًا

fa-nṭalaqā ḥattā idhā rakibā fī al-safīnati kharaqahā qāla a-kharaqtahā li-tughriqa ahlahā la-qad jiʾta shayʾan imrā

Al-Kahf 18:71

“They set out, until, when they boarded the ship, he made a hole in it. He said: Did you make a hole in it to drown its people? You have indeed done something grievous.”

Al-safīnah is the vessel the two companions board.

Rakibā expresses their boarding, while fī al-safīnah identifies the vessel and the place they occupy.

Kharaqahā then states that he made a hole in it.

Kharaqa belongs to KH R Q and concerns making a breach or hole.

Perforating is therefore not part of safīnah’s meaning: it is an action performed on the ship.

The distinction matters because the ship begins as a means of transport and is then damaged through one companion’s action.

Concern that the occupants might drown

Seeing the hole made, Moses asks:

«أَخَرَقْتَهَا لِتُغْرِقَ أَهْلَهَا»

a-kharaqtahā li-tughriqa ahlahā

Al-Kahf 18:71

That is: Did you make a hole in it to drown its people?

His objection shows that he perceives the damage as a serious danger to those aboard.

This concern is distinct from drowning actually occurring.

The verse does not say the occupants drowned. Moses’ question alone also does not establish that drowning them was his companion’s real intention.

The text reports the damage and Moses’ objection to the dangerous consequence he understands from it.

It does not establish that every hole in a vessel necessarily causes sinking. The hole’s size and location and the ship’s condition are not specified.

Moses’ concern is nevertheless understandable: a vessel depends on the integrity of its hull to stay afloat and carry its occupants safely, and a breach can threaten that safety.

Safīnah remains the vessel’s name; the danger of drowning follows from the action performed upon it.

A vessel belonging to needy people who worked

Al-Kahf 18:79 states:

أَمَّا السَّفِينَةُ فَكَانَتْ لِمَسَاكِينَ يَعْمَلُونَ فِي الْبَحْرِ فَأَرَدْتُ أَنْ أَعِيبَهَا وَكَانَ وَرَاءَهُمْ مَلِكٌ يَأْخُذُ كُلَّ سَفِينَةٍ غَصْبًا

ammā al-safīnatu fa-kānat li-masākīna yaʿmalūna fī al-baḥri fa-aradtu an aʿībahā wa-kāna warāʾahum malikun yaʾkhudhu kulla safīnatin ghaṣbā

Al-Kahf 18:79

“As for the ship, it belonged to needy people working at sea. I wished to damage it, for beyond them was a king who seized every ship by force.”

Safīnah occurs twice here.

The definite al-safīnah identifies the particular ship previously damaged.

The indefinite safīnatin appears in kulli safīnatin and concerns the vessels seized by the king.

The distinction between definite and indefinite is significant.

Al-safīnah is the known vessel in the account; kulli safīnatin is a general expression describing the ships subject to his seizure.

Neediness and owning a means of work

Three expressions occur together:

Al-safīnah, li-masākīn, and yaʿmalūna fī al-baḥr.

The ship belongs to needy people who work at sea.

These people therefore need not lack every possession or means of work.

They have something with which they work, yet are still described as masākīn.

Possessing a working tool or asset does not by itself rule out poverty or need.

Someone may own a vessel, land, woodworking tools, or another means of earning a living while having insufficient income for their needs.

Owning a ship alone does not make its owner wealthy.

This follows from the combination of li-masākīn and yaʿmalūna fī al-baḥr, rather than the lexical meaning of safīnah.

The ship is both transport and the property and livelihood resource of a group of people.

Its seizure or destruction can therefore affect their ability to work and maintain their livelihood.

Seizing the ship and violating others’ property

The end of the verse describes the king’s conduct:

«يَأْخُذُ كُلَّ سَفِينَةٍ غَصْبًا».

yaʾkhudhu kulla safīnatin ghaṣbā

Al-Kahf 18:79

Yaʾkhudhu expresses taking, while ghaṣban specifies how: forcibly and without right.

The issue is forcible appropriation of others’ property, rather than a mere transfer between owners.

Safīnah does not itself mean stolen property. A vessel may be its owner’s legitimate possession and serve work, travel, or cargo transport.

Ghaṣban identifies what makes the king’s conduct objectionable.

The distinction matters: an object may sustain its owner’s livelihood, while a powerful person’s wrongful seizure violates their ownership and ability to work.

Damaging the vessel to prevent its confiscation

Moses’ companion explains his action:

«فَأَرَدْتُ أَنْ أَعِيبَهَا».

fa-aradtu an aʿībahā

Al-Kahf 18:79

That is: I wished to damage it.

Aʿībahā concerns introducing a defect, rather than completely destroying the ship.

His declared purpose was thus neither destroying it nor drowning its occupants.

In context, the damage is connected with preventing the king from seizing the vessel.

The Qur’an does not give the hole’s dimensions, how water was kept out, the repair method, or the vessel’s precise fate after the danger passed.

Such details should not be presented as part of the verse merely because they occur in familiar exegetical narratives.

Nor does the story establish a general permission to damage others’ property on the claim that it will benefit them later.

At the end of his explanation in Al-Kahf 18:82, the companion expressly says he did not act on his own initiative.

His particular action is thus situated within divine knowledge and command. The same authority cannot be assigned to everyone without justification.

For S F N, the main finding is clear: the ship is transport, a working asset, and valuable property belonging to needy people. Damaging and forcibly seizing it are separate actions expressed through other words and verbs.

4. Safīnah in Noah’s story

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15 states:

فَأَنْجَيْنَاهُ وَأَصْحَابَ السَّفِينَةِ وَجَعَلْنَاهَا آيَةً لِلْعَالَمِينَ

fa-anjaynāhu wa-aṣḥāba al-safīnati wa-jaʿalnāhā āyatan li-al-ʿālamīn

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15

“We saved him and the companions of the ship, and made it a sign for the worlds.”

The verse follows the account of Noah’s mission and the flood that overtook his wrongdoing people.

Al-safīnah names the vessel associated with Noah and his companions.

The significant expression is aṣḥāb al-safīnah.

Aṣḥāb al-safīnah: the ship’s companions

Aṣḥāb comes from Ṣ Ḥ B and concerns companionship and association.

Here it identifies those accompanying the ship and associated with it in the rescue account.

It should not automatically be translated “owners of the ship.”

Depending on context, ṣāḥib can mean an owner, companion, associate, or someone closely connected with a person or thing.

Here the ship’s companions are saved alongside Noah. “The companions of the ship” is consequently a more fitting rendering.

This differs from li-masākīn in Al-Kahf 18:79.

In Moses’ account, lām expresses the vessel’s ownership by or belonging to the needy people.

In Noah’s account, aṣḥāb al-safīnah emphasizes companionship and association with the vessel.

The two constructions should not be reduced to a single legal meaning.

Noah’s ship in other verses

Elsewhere the Qur’an calls Noah’s means of rescue a fulk.

Hūd 11:37 contains the command to build it.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:27 likewise describes its construction under divine command and revelation.

Hūd 11:41 mentions the vessel’s course and anchoring.

Al-Qamar 54:13 describes Noah’s means of transport as dhāti alwāḥin wa-dusur: something with planks and fastenings.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:11 uses al-jāriyah for the vessel in which people were carried.

These expressions present a floating vessel from different perspectives.

Safīnah is the familiar name for a ship.

Fulk also denotes a ship or floating vessel.

Jāriyah in this context foregrounds running or moving over water.

Dhāti alwāḥin wa-dusur describes structural components.

No single expression gives every technical feature of Noah’s vessel.

They do not independently determine its exact dimensions, number of decks, geometry, timber species, capacity, or final resting place.

The ship as a sign

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15 ends:

«وَجَعَلْنَاهَا آيَةً لِلْعَالَمِينَ».

wa-jaʿalnāhā āyatan li-al-ʿālamīn

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15

The feminine pronoun hā can refer to safīnah, the nearest explicit feminine noun.

On this reading, the ship is made a sign for the worlds.

The rescue of Noah and his companions can also be part of that significance, since the ship is the means of rescue in the narrative.

Āyah does not necessarily mean an archaeological object physically surviving and visible in every age.

The sign can also lie in an event, its story, and its message.

The phrase alone therefore does not establish that the ship’s hull survives today or that people can identify its location.

What is clear is Noah’s rescue with the ship’s companions and its being made a sign for the worlds.

In the flood context the vessel serves to preserve life as well as transport people.

That role follows from the rescue event; safīnah does not lexically mean salvation or mercy.

5. Comparison with related roots

S F N and F L K: ships and floating vessels

Fulk is a major Qur’anic word for ships.

Fulk may denote one ship or a group of vessels. Other words in the family, such as falak, concern circular courses; that does not make circular motion the direct meaning of fulk as a ship.

Safīnah occurs only in two particular narratives, whereas fulk appears more broadly in passages about the sea, travel, and rescue.

Both words describe Noah’s vessel.

They therefore need not designate entirely separate kinds of watercraft.

The different roots also do not prove that a safīnah must be small and a fulk large, or that one carries people and the other only goods.

Those properties are not established by the words alone.

Both can denote floating waterborne transport. Differences in size, construction, and function require independent evidence.

S F N and J R Y: the ship and its movement

J R Y concerns flowing and movement along a course.

The Qur’an uses this family for water and rivers as well as ships moving over water.

Jāriyah in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:11 denotes a moving vessel within Noah’s flood narrative.

Safīnah names the vessel itself, while jāriyah foregrounds its movement.

A ship can be moving or stationary and still be called a safīnah.

Continuous movement is not a condition of the name.

An object’s name and the description of its movement are thus distinct.

S F N and R K B: the ship and boarding

R K B concerns mounting or taking a position on a means of travel.

Rakibā in Al-Kahf 18:71 describes Moses and his companion boarding; al-safīnah is what they board.

Rukūb expresses the human action, and safīnah the vessel.

People may mount an animal, board a ship, or use another conveyance.

Safīnah therefore does not mean boarding, just as R K B does not itself name a ship.

S F N and KH R Q: scraping and perforating

Kharaqa concerns making a hole or breach.

Moses’ companion makes a hole in the ship in Al-Kahf 18:71.

The verbal branch of S F N, however, concerns scraping or removing a surface layer.

The actions may overlap in working materials but are not identical.

Scraping a wooden surface need not make a hole. Perforating, by contrast, can breach an object’s continuity or covering at a particular point.

Safīnah’s possible connection with scraping does not give it the basic meaning of a hole or breach.

S F N and Ṣ F N: two different families

S F N begins with sīn and concerns scraping and the ship word.

Ṣ F N begins with ṣād; its Qur’anic al-ṣāfināt refers to a posture of standing horses.

Ṣād 38:31 uses al-ṣāfinātu al-jiyād for fine horses.

Safīnah and ṣāfināt therefore do not share a root.

Similar-looking letters do not establish derivational kinship or identical meaning.

S F N and N J W: transport and escape from danger

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15 places the ship within Noah’s rescue story.

Rescue belongs to a different root.

A ship may be used for rescue, trade, travel, or cargo transport.

Safīnah itself does not specifically mean a rescue vessel.

In Noah’s story that role follows from the flood and fa-anjaynāhu.

In Moses’ story the vessel supports needy people’s work and livelihood.

One object with an unchanged lexical name can thus perform different functions in different circumstances.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The clear, natural Persian equivalent of safīnah is the ordinary word for a ship; “ship” likewise works in English.

There is no need to retain the Arabic noun in the translation, since the ordinary equivalent conveys the familiar vessel.

السَّفِينَةِ — Al-Kahf 18:71: “the ship.”

Rakibā fī al-safīnah can be rendered:

“They boarded the ship.”

السَّفِينَةُ — Al-Kahf 18:79: “the ship.”

Ammā al-safīnah is naturally “as for the ship.”

سَفِينَةٍ — Al-Kahf 18:79: “a ship.”

Kulli safīnatin ghaṣban is naturally rendered within the sentence as “every ship by force.”

السَّفِينَةِ — Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15: “the ship.”

Aṣḥāb al-safīnah is better rendered “the companions of the ship,” since aṣḥāb here identifies companions and those associated with it, not necessarily its owners.

Safīnah should not be restricted to a small boat, warship, merchant ship, or rescue vessel unless the context supports that specification.

Al-Kahf 18:79 identifies it as a working vessel belonging to needy people, but does not specify their exact maritime activity.

They may have worked in transport or other sea-related activities; the text does not name a more specific occupation.

Further lexical forms may be rendered as follows; safīnah, the Qur’anic noun, is included for comparison:

  • سَفَنَ الْعُودَ: scraped or shaved the wood.
  • سَفَنَ الشَّيْءَ: rubbed something or removed a layer from it.
  • سَفْن: scraping and abrasion; سَفَن is a scraping implement.
  • مِسْفَن: an implement for scraping or finishing a surface.
  • سَفَنَتِ الرِّيحُ التُّرَابَ: the wind removed or dispersed surface soil.
  • سَفِينَة: a ship.
  • سُفُن / سَفَائِن / سَفِين: ships.
  • سَفَّان: a mariner or shipbuilder, according to usage.

The translated name of the vessel should remain distinct from its proposed derivation.

Even if a connection with pushing aside water is accepted, replacing “ship” with “water-scraping implement” is neither natural nor accurate.

The lexical image belongs in a root explanation; the basic translation should convey the word’s established meaning.

Conclusion

S F N concerns scraping, abrasion, and removing a layer from a surface.

Early uses concern wood, soil, and bodily contact with the ground. Scraping and finishing implements also bear names from this family.

Safīnah names a ship. Lexical explanations connect it variously with pushing aside water in motion and with the tools used to shape its timber.

These explanations can be examined semantically, but the precise history of the name is not established with certainty.

The poetry attributed to al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī shows safīn as ships in early Arabic, even serving as an image for laden camels.

The Qur’an uses S F N four times, exclusively through safīnah.

In Al-Kahf 18:71, Moses and his companion board a vessel that is subsequently damaged.

Al-Kahf 18:79 explains that it belonged to needy people working at sea, while a king forcibly confiscated ships.

Owning a working asset or valuable possession therefore does not by itself remove economic need. The king’s conduct violates the owners’ rights, since the seizure is described as ghaṣb.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15, safīnah appears in Noah’s story, associated with the rescue of him and his companions and its significance as a sign.

These usages show a vessel serving work, transport, property protection, and preservation of life. No single function exhausts the noun’s lexical meaning.

The family may therefore be defined as follows:

In its verbal sense, S F N concerns scraping, rubbing, and removing material from a surface. Safīnah is the established name for a ship: a vessel for movement and carrying people or goods over water. Its precise historical derivation from scraping has not been conclusively established.

“Ship” is the appropriate basic equivalent for safīnah in Forqan’s translations.

The Qur’anic usages show that an object’s name does not determine its value or role. A vessel may sustain needy people, be targeted by powerful usurpers, or save lives in danger.

These differences follow from human actions and the circumstances of each event, while safīnah retains its meaning throughout the verses.
