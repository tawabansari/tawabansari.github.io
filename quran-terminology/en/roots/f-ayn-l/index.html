Linguistic Note

The root ف ع ل (F ʿ L) is one of the broadest roots in the Qur’an. By itself, it does not carry a positive or negative moral value. It points to the basic occurrence or performance of an act. A good deed, a bad deed, The God’s act, a human act, a legal decision, a historical event, or a gap between speech and conduct may all be expressed with this root.

The core idea is the issuing and realization of an act. Something that was only a capacity, intention, statement, possibility, or will enters the field of reality through fiʿl. For that reason, F ʿ L focuses more on “doing” or “bringing about” than on the moral quality, kind, or final result of the act. Those are determined by the verse’s context.

When the Qur’an says افْعَلُوا الْخَيْرَ (ifʿalū al-khayr), the goodness comes from al-khayr. When it says مُّنكَرٍ فَعَلُوهُ (munkarin faʿalūhu), the wrongness comes from munkar and from the social context. F ʿ L is the general container of doing.

Central Meaning

The central meaning of F ʿ L in the Qur’an is doing and bringing an act into reality: something leaves possibility, intention, will, or speech and takes place in the real world.

This is why the root is simpler and broader than words such as ʿamal, ṣunʿ, khalq, jaʿl, and kasb. ʿAmal often carries a stronger sense of effort, intention, continuity, and moral responsibility. Ṣunʿ is closer to careful making or crafting. Khalq concerns creation and foundational measuring-out. Jaʿl means placing, appointing, or making something into a certain state. Kasb looks toward what a person acquires or bears as the result of action. Fiʿl, however, names the basic occurrence or performance of an act.

Material Image

The simple image behind this root is a movement or deed that leaves an effect outside itself. Something is still, hidden, unsaid, or undone; then a movement takes place, a decision is carried out, a hand does something, or a force changes the present condition. That is where fiʿl appears.

In this image, fiʿl stands opposite mere stillness or claim. A person may want something, say something, or promise something, but until it is done, fiʿl has not yet occurred. Fiʿl is the passage from possibility to realization, from inner intention to outward effect, from speech to conduct.

Past Forms: An Act That Happened

Forms such as فَعَلَ (faʿala), فَعَلْتَ (faʿalta), and فَعَلُوا (faʿalū) point to an act that has occurred and can now be reported, evaluated, remembered, or blamed. The act has moved beyond possibility and become a reality that can be attributed to a doer.

Imperfect Forms: Doing, Habit, and Ongoing Pattern

Forms such as يَفْعَلُ (yafʿalu), تَفْعَلُونَ (tafʿalūn), and يَفْعَلُونَ (yafʿalūn) may show present action, future action, habitual conduct, or an ongoing pattern. In verses such as لِمَ تَقُولُونَ مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ (lima taqūlūna mā lā tafʿalūn), the imperfect form exposes the living gap between speech and conduct.

Imperative: A Command to Bring Something About

In the imperative, such as افْعَلُوا (ifʿalū), the matter is not only knowing or accepting; the addressee is called to bring something into action. In 22:77, وَافْعَلُوا الْخَيْرَ (wa-ifʿalū al-khayr) means: make good real in your conduct.

The God’s Action and Realized Will

One important Qur’anic use of F ʿ L is attributing action to The God. In these contexts, fiʿl is not limited human doing. It points to the realization of divine will: The God brings about what He wills within His knowledge, wisdom, and power.

In 3:40, Zechariah sees the matter through ordinary natural limits: his old age and his wife’s barrenness. The answer is: كَذَٰلِكَ اللَّهُ يَفْعَلُ مَا يَشَاءُ (kadhālika Allāhu yafʿalu mā yashāʾ). If The God’s will is attached to bringing something about, ordinary human limitations do not confine it.

In 14:27, وَيَفْعَلُ اللَّهُ مَا يَشَاءُ (wa-yafʿalu Allāhu mā yashāʾ) appears beside faith, firmness, and wrongdoing. It should not be understood as arbitrary will separated from wisdom. Across the Qur’an, The God’s action is joined with His knowledge, justice, wisdom, and mercy.

Faʿʿālun Limā Yurīd

In Surah Al-Buruj, after mentioning the severity of the Lord’s seizing, His beginning and returning, His forgiveness and love, and the majesty of the Throne, the verse says: فَعَّالٌ لِّمَا يُرِيدُ (faʿʿālun limā yurīd) in 85:16.

فَعَّال (faʿʿāl) is an intensive form. It conveys power, continuity, and the absence of inability in action. The God is not merely one who wills; His will has the power to be realized. Yet the same context calls Him الْغَفُورُ الْوَدُودُ (al-ghafūr al-wadūd). So faʿʿālun limā yurīd should not be translated in a way that detaches The God’s action from mercy and wisdom. It speaks of unhindered power, not irrationality.

The God Is Not Questioned About What He Does

In 21:23, the Qur’an says: لَا يُسْأَلُ عَمَّا يَفْعَلُ وَهُمْ يُسْأَلُونَ (lā yusʾalu ʿammā yafʿalu wa-hum yusʾalūn). This verse concerns the station of The God and His creatures. The God is not under a higher authority that can interrogate Him; He is Creator, Owner, Knower, and Wise. Human beings are questioned because they stand within responsibility, limitation, and choice.

Therefore, the verse does not mean that The God’s action is without order or wisdom. It means The God is not subject to the judgment of creatures, while creatures are responsible before Him.

The God’s Action and the Question of Punishment

In 4:147, the Qur’an asks a piercing question: مَّا يَفْعَلُ اللَّهُ بِعَذَابِكُمْ إِن شَكَرْتُمْ وَآمَنتُمْ (mā yafʿalu Allāhu bi-ʿadhābikum in shakartum wa-āmantum). The verse breaks a false image, as though punishment were an independent goal for The God.

The question asks: what would The God do with punishing you if there is gratitude and faith? Divine action in this context is connected with the human condition. Punishment in the Qur’an is not a display of power for its own sake; it is the truthful response to kufr, wrongdoing, corruption, and denial. When the human direction changes, the ground of punishment changes as well.

Human Action: The Gap Between Speech and Conduct

One of the clearest uses of F ʿ L for human beings is the gap between saying and doing: لِمَ تَقُولُونَ مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ (lima taqūlūna mā lā tafʿalūn) and كَبُرَ مَقْتًا عِندَ اللَّهِ أَن تَقُولُوا مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ (kabura maqtan ʿinda Allāhi an taqūlū mā lā tafʿalūn) in 61:2-3.

Here fiʿl is the outward test of speech. A person says something, but does not make it real in conduct. The force of the verse lies in this simple contrast: saying what one does not do. For translation, “do” or “carry out” should remain simple.

Doing Good

The Qur’an also uses F ʿ L for good. In 22:77, it says: وَافْعَلُوا الْخَيْرَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ (wa-ifʿalū al-khayra laʿallakum tufliḥūn). This expression leaves the field of good open; it does not restrict the command to one specific deed. Any act directed toward goodness, truth, reform, and real benefit may fall under it.

In 2:215, the verse says: وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ بِهِ عَلِيمٌ (wa-mā tafʿalū min khayrin fa-inna Allāha bihi ʿalīm). The good that is done is under The God’s knowledge. No good that you bring into action is hidden from Him.

Action in Private Counsel and Reform

In 4:114, action is joined with charity, what is recognized as right, and reform: وَمَن يَفْعَلْ ذَٰلِكَ ابْتِغَاءَ مَرْضَاتِ اللَّهِ (wa-man yafʿal dhālika ibtighāʾa marḍāti Allāh). The verb returns to dhālika: commanding ṣadaqah, maʿrūf, or reconciliation between people.

The important point is that fiʿl here refers to a whole behavioral field, not merely a small movement. The phrase “seeking The God’s pleasure” also gives the act its direction. A deed may look like a simple act outwardly, but its value is also shaped by aim and intention.

Bad Action and Social Habit

In 5:79, the verse says: كَانُوا لَا يَتَنَاهَوْنَ عَن مُّنكَرٍ فَعَلُوهُ لَبِئْسَ مَا كَانُوا يَفْعَلُونَ (kānū lā yatanāhawna ʿan munkarin faʿalūhu labiʾsa mā kānū yafʿalūn). The wrongness does not come from F ʿ L itself. It comes from munkar and from the failure to restrain one another.

Fiʿl here points to an act that has happened and become socially repeated. When munkar is done and the community remains silent toward it, bad action moves from individual behavior into collective habit.

Action, Personal Decision, and Right

In some verses, F ʿ L appears in legal and social contexts, pointing to a decision a person carries out concerning their own condition. In 2:234, regarding women after the waiting period, the Qur’an says: فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِيمَا فَعَلْنَ فِي أَنفُسِهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ (fa-lā junāḥa ʿalaykum fīmā faʿalna fī anfusihinna bil-maʿrūf).

This is not only ordinary “doing.” It refers to what the women do concerning themselves within maʿrūf. The structure recognizes their decision and agency regarding their own situation, while separating others from improper interference.

A similar expression appears in 2:240: فِي مَا فَعَلْنَ فِي أَنفُسِهِنَّ مِن مَّعْرُوفٍ (fī mā faʿalna fī anfusihinna min maʿrūf). In both verses, the action is neither lawless nor under the control of others; it belongs to the women themselves and is framed by what is recognized as right.

Faʿlataka: Attributing an Act to a Person

In the story of Moses, Pharaoh throws Moses’s past back at him: وَفَعَلْتَ فَعْلَتَكَ الَّتِي فَعَلْتَ (wa-faʿalta faʿlataka allatī faʿalta) in 26:19.

The repetition intensifies the tone of blame and reminder: “you did that deed of yours which you did.” Pharaoh attributes Moses’s past act to him in accusatory language. But the continuation of the story shows that attributing an act to a person is not enough for final judgment. Context, awareness, intention, circumstance, and later guidance all matter.

The God’s Action in History

Surah Al-Fil opens with a short question: أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِأَصْحَابِ الْفِيلِ (a-lam tara kayfa faʿala rabbuka bi-aṣḥābi al-fīl) in 105:1.

Here kayfa faʿala rabbuka draws attention to how The God acted in history. The verse does not merely say that The God did something; it asks how He did it. The best translation should stay simple: “what your Lord did with the Companions of the Elephant.” That simplicity fits the root.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

In يَفْعَلُ مَا يَشَاءُ (yafʿalu mā yashāʾ), the object of the verb is mā yashāʾ: what He wills. The structure shows the relation between will and realization: divine will becomes realized in action.

In فَعَّالٌ لِّمَا يُرِيدُ (faʿʿālun limā yurīd), the intensive form is stronger than yafʿal and conveys power, intensity, and continuity. In لَا يُسْأَلُ عَمَّا يَفْعَلُ (lā yusʾalu ʿammā yafʿal), the structure is set against وَهُمْ يُسْأَلُونَ (wa-hum yusʾalūn), showing the difference between The God and creatures in accountability.

In فِيمَا فَعَلْنَ فِي أَنفُسِهِنَّ (fīmā faʿalna fī anfusihinna), the action is attributed to the women and directed toward their own situation. The addition of بِالْمَعْرُوفِ (bil-maʿrūf) or مِن مَّعْرُوفٍ (min maʿrūf) shows that the action is understood within recognized moral and social rightness.

Difference From Nearby Words

F ʿ L is close to ʿamal, but they are not identical. ʿAmal is usually more specific and often carries stronger moral, intentional, and accountable weight. In simple terms, every ʿamal can be a fiʿl, but not every fiʿl is necessarily ʿamal in the fuller Qur’anic sense.

F ʿ L also differs from ṣunʿ. Ṣunʿ points more toward careful making or skilled crafting. Every ṣunʿ is a kind of fiʿl, but not every fiʿl is ṣunʿ. F ʿ L also differs from khalq; khalq concerns creation and foundational measuring-out, while fiʿl may name any act or realization.

F ʿ L differs from jaʿl, kasb, and arād as well. Jaʿl concerns placing, appointing, or making something into a state. Kasb looks toward what a person acquires or bears as the result of action. Arād is willing or intending; fiʿl is that intention realized in action. In phrases such as يَفْعَلُ مَا يَشَاءُ (yafʿalu mā yashāʾ) and فَعَّالٌ لِّمَا يُرِيدُ (faʿʿālun limā yurīd), will and action stand side by side.

Forqan Translation Suggestions

  • yafʿalu mā yashāʾ: He does what He wills; or He brings into reality what He wills.
  • yafʿalu Allāhu mā yashāʾ: The God does what He wills.
  • faʿʿālun limā yurīd: the One who brings about what He wills; or the Doer of what He wills.
  • lā yusʾalu ʿammā yafʿal: He is not questioned about what He does.
  • lima taqūlūna mā lā tafʿalūn: why do you say what you do not do?
  • ifʿalū al-khayr: do good; or do what is good.
  • wa-mā tafʿalū min khayr: whatever good you do.
  • wa-man yafʿal dhālika ibtighāʾa marḍāti Allāh: whoever does that seeking The God’s pleasure.
  • munkarin faʿalūhu: a wrong they had done.
  • labiʾsa mā kānū yafʿalūn: evil was what they used to do.
  • fīmā faʿalna fī anfusihinna bil-maʿrūf: in what they do concerning themselves according to maʿrūf; or in the recognized decision they make and carry out concerning themselves.
  • wa-faʿalta faʿlataka allatī faʿalta: and you did that deed of yours which you did.
  • kayfa faʿala rabbuka: what your Lord did; or how your Lord dealt with the Companions of the Elephant.

Summary

The root F ʿ L in the Qur’an points to doing and bringing an act into reality. At the material level, fiʿl is an act that leaves the state of possibility, intention, will, or speech and has an effect in reality. The root is used both for The God’s action and for human action, but the precise meaning is determined by context.

When attributed to The God, F ʿ L points to the realization of divine will, a will never separated from knowledge, wisdom, and power. When attributed to human beings, it names the act itself, whether in good, in wrong, in the gap between speech and conduct, or in personal and social decision.

In short: F ʿ L in the Qur’an means doing and making an act real; a matter moves from intention, will, possibility, or speech into the field of reality.
