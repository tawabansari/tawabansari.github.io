Root ه ي ج — Arousal and the changing state of plants

Qur’anic and lexical forms

يَهِيجُ
(yahīju)
Withers; describing vegetation

From stirring into activity to vegetation drying and yellowing after growth

Arabic ه ي ج concerns stirring, movement, and intensified states. It describes rising wind and dust, anger, longing, conflict, and changes in vegetation.

For plants, hāja al-nabtu describes the loss of freshness and greenery, with drying and yellowing.

The Qur’an uses the root twice, always as yahīju, describing plants that dry and yellow after growth and ultimately become broken remains.

This differs from modern Persian hayajān, chiefly psychological excitement. Both Qur’anic verses describe a particular stage of vegetation’s development.

The broad Arabic arousal branch and the specific botanical sense must be distinguished.

1. Early Arabic meaning

Classical hāja yahīju describes leaving a quieter or previous condition for movement or intensity.

Hāja al-ghubāru means dust rose.

Hāja al-sharru means trouble or conflict was stirred up.

Hāja al-ghaḍabu means anger intensified.

Hāja al-shawqu describes awakened longing.

These uses share a shift toward a more active, agitated condition.

That does not define every branch: botanical hāja also means drying and lost freshness. Drying should not be interpreted as greater excitement or movement without evidence.

An example from pre-Qur’anic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

«إِذَا تَغَنَّى الْحَمَامُ الْوُرْقُ هَيَّجَنِي
وَلَوْ تَغَرَّبْتُ عَنْهَا أُمَّ عَمَّارِ»

Idhā taghannā al-ḥamāmu al-wurqu hayyajanī / wa-law tagharrabtu ʿanhā umma ʿAmmāri.

“When gray doves sing, they awaken my longing and memory of Umm ʿAmmār, even though I have gone far from her.”

Hayyajanī awakens an inner state here.

The doves’ song revives memory of the beloved and longing.

In this verse, the stirring recalls an existing attachment. This contextual reading does not establish that tahyīj can never produce a new feeling.

Sībawayh’s Book quotes the verse, which is attributed to al-Nābighah. Versions differ in the second hemistich, but hayyajanī and its sense are clear.

The pre-Qur’anic attribution has recognized literary support, although the exact date of this verse is not independently established.

It illustrates the stirring of feeling and longing in early Arabic poetic tradition.

Hayjāʾ: conflict and war

Arabic al-hayjāʾ denotes war or the battlefield.

War brings forces into motion, intensifies anger and confrontation, and ends peace between groups.

Yawmu al-hiyāji means a day of battle; tahayyaja al-farīqāni describes two groups engaging in confrontation.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says in an elegy for Arbad:

«وَأَرْبَدُ فَارِسُ الْهَيْجَا إِذَا مَا
تَقَعَّرَتِ الْمَشَاجِرُ بِالْفِئَامِ»

Wa-Arbadu fārisu al-hayjā idhā mā / taqaʿʿarati al-mashājiru bi-al-fiʾāmi.

“Arbad was the horseman of battle when the litters, with their coverings, were uprooted and overturned.”

Al-hayjā here denotes battle.

The elegy follows Arbad’s death during Labīd’s Islamic lifetime, so it is not independent pre-Qur’anic dating evidence. It shows continued usage near the revelation period.

It must not be merged with Qur’anic yahīju: the verses in al-Zumar and al-Ḥadīd concern neither war nor conflict.

Wind, dust, and animals

Hayj is also recorded for strong wind.

Wind raises dust and moves what was still.

Hayyaja al-ghubāra means stirring up dust.

Hāja al-faḥlu describes a male animal’s sexual arousal.

Hājat al-ibilu can describe camels becoming thirsty; hāja al-ibila can mean driving them toward water or grazing.

These branches involve movement or aroused states in animals.

They should not be generalized into “every change of state,” a definition too broad to distinguish this root.

The arousal branch emphasizes rising, stimulation, or intensification after relative quiet.

The botanical branch more specifically denotes drying and loss of freshness.

2. Vegetation: drying and yellowing

Classical hāja al-nabtu means vegetation dried or began drying.

Hāja al-baqlu describes vegetation losing freshness, changing color, and reaching dryness.

Arḍun hāʾijah describes land whose plants have dried or yellowed.

This particularly matters for seasonal vegetation.

Plants are green and fresh during a growth stage. Seasonal change, completed growth, or less water may alter their color, dry their tissues, and eventually cause breakage.

Early descriptions apply hayj from the first signs of changing color and dryness through extensive yellowing.

The Islamic-era botanist Abū Ḥanīfah al-Dīnawarī connects its beginning with the first color changes, as greenery gradually disappears.

It can thus describe a transition from freshness to dryness rather than one brief instant.

Is hayj simply yellowing?

Drying and yellowing are closely connected in plants, and some lexicons explain hāja al-nabātu with both descriptions.

They are nevertheless distinct.

Yellowing is a color change.

Drying changes moisture and tissue condition.

Many plants yellow while drying, but yellowing need not mean destruction. Some crops become yellow or golden when ripe and still yield valuable produce.

The Qur’an follows yahīju with fa-tarāhu muṣfarran.

The process links drying with visible yellowing, yet the latter has its own word.

Yahīju is best understood through drying and lost freshness, with yellowing explained as the visible contextual sign.

Does hayj mean breaking into fragments?

No. Breaking into plant debris is another stage.

Both Qur’anic verses follow drying and yellowing with ḥuṭām.

Ḥuṭām belongs to ح ط م and denotes broken, crushed material.

Vegetation loses freshness, dries and yellows, then may become broken remains.

The stages can overlap naturally without becoming lexically identical.

3. Qur’anic forms

The root occurs twice, in one form:

  • يَهِيجُ — al-Zumar 39:21: a crop drying after growth.
  • يَهِيجُ — al-Ḥadīd 57:20: vegetation drying and yellowing after its impressive growth.

Yahīju is an active third-person masculine singular imperfect of Form I.

Its conjugation is:

هَاجَ — يَهِيجُ

Both verses refer the verb to vegetation.

In al-Zumar 39:21, its antecedent is zarʿan.

In al-Ḥadīd 57:20, it is nabātuhu.

The imperfect presents a vivid observable process. It does not alone establish identical seasonal timing for every species.

Other Arabic derivatives

Lexicons also record:

  • هَاجَ: stirred, became active, or, botanically, dried.
  • هَيْج and هِيَاج: arousal, disturbance, movement, and in particular contexts vegetation’s drying.
  • هَيَجَان: intensified movement, condition, or feeling.
  • هَائِج: stirred up, or vegetation going dry.
  • هَيَّجَ: aroused, stimulated, or set in motion.
  • تَهْيِيج: arousing or stimulating.
  • اِهْتَاجَ: became stirred or intensified.
  • تَهَيَّجَ: became stimulated or more intense.
  • تَهَايَجَ: mutual engagement, as with confronting groups.
  • هَيْجَاء: war or a battlefield.

Only yahīju occurs in the Qur’an.

Form I yahīju must be distinguished from Form II yuhayyiju, written يُهَيِّجُ.

Yahīju can describe the subject’s own arousal or change.

Yuhay yiju usually describes stimulating something else.

The Qur’anic form is the first: it must not be translated “stimulates,” since the vegetation itself changes.

4. Al-Zumar 39:21

«أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَسَلَكَهُ يَنَابِيعَ فِي الْأَرْضِ ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُ بِهِ زَرْعًا مُخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهُ ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ حُطَامًا إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَذِكْرَىٰ لِأُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ»

A-lam tara anna Allāha anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-salakahu yanābīʿa fī al-arḍi thumma yukhriju bihi zarʿan mukhtalifan alwānuhu thumma yahīju fa-tarāhu muṣfarran thumma yajʿaluhu ḥuṭāman inna fī dhālika la-dhikrā li-ulī al-albāb.

“Have you not seen that The God sent down water from the sky and channeled it as springs in the earth, then brings forth through it crops of different colors? Then they dry and you see them yellow; then He makes them broken debris. In that is a reminder for people of understanding.”

The verse depicts water’s circulation and stages of vegetation.

Water descends and moves through the earth; plants of different colors and characteristics emerge.

They do not remain in their first condition.

After green growth comes drying and changing color.

Thumma yahīju: the crop changes

Yahīju refers to zarʿan.

Zarʿ denotes a crop, grammatically masculine singular though encompassing many plants.

The verb accordingly appears masculine singular.

Here it describes lost freshness and the onset of drying.

A wheat field illustrates this: initially green, it develops stems and ears, then becomes yellow or golden and drier as the crop matures.

This is a familiar example of botanical hāja.

It does not make every yellowing plant useless. Yellowing often accompanies ripeness and harvest readiness.

The verse describes change, not every yellow stage as crop destruction or corruption.

Fa-tarāhu muṣfarran: visible yellowing

This follows yahīju.

Muṣfarran, from ص ف ر, expresses yellow coloration.

It is independent of yahīju.

Earlier colors give way to a stage visibly different from the crop’s previous condition.

Growth, color, and drying are linked in the verse but should not be merged into one word’s definition.

Yahīju describes drying; muṣfarran its visible color.

Thumma yajʿaluhu ḥuṭāman: becoming broken remains

Yajʿaluhu introduces the next stage.

Ḥuṭāman denotes broken debris.

Previously green, standing plants may break under pressure, wind, animals, or other agents after drying.

Here making them debris is attributed to God.

Three concepts stand together: drying, visible yellowing, and becoming broken remains.

They concern one natural process but use separate words.

The reminder

The verse ends: إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَذِكْرَىٰ لِأُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ.

It invites observation and reflection on nature’s changes.

What is green and growing today need not remain so. Each stage has conditions and an outcome.

Other verses describe earth revived after death, including al-Rūm 30:50 and Fuṣṣilat 41:39.

They portray renewed growth after dryness when water arrives.

Yahīju in al-Zumar 39:21 therefore does not establish permanent destruction of the earth or the end of all possibilities of life.

It depicts the end of a growth cycle and invites reflection on changing material conditions.

5. Al-Ḥadīd 57:20

«اعْلَمُوا أَنَّمَا الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا لَعِبٌ وَلَهْوٌ وَزِينَةٌ وَتَفَاخُرٌ بَيْنَكُمْ وَتَكَاثُرٌ فِي الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْأَوْلَادِ كَمَثَلِ غَيْثٍ أَعْجَبَ الْكُفَّارَ نَبَاتُهُ ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا ثُمَّ يَكُونُ حُطَامًا وَفِي الْآخِرَةِ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانٌ وَمَا الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا إِلَّا مَتَاعُ الْغُرُورِ»

Iʿlamū annamā al-ḥayātu al-dunyā laʿibun wa-lahwun wa-zīnatun wa-tafākhurun baynakum wa-takāthurun fī al-amwāli wa-al-awlādi ka-mathali ghaythin aʿjaba al-kuffāra nabātuhu thumma yahīju fa-tarāhu muṣfarran thumma yakūnu ḥuṭāman wa-fī al-ākhirati ʿadhābun shadīdun wa-maghfiratun mina Allāhi wa-riḍwānun wa-mā al-ḥayātu al-dunyā illā matāʿu al-ghurūr.

“Know that worldly life is play, diversion, adornment, mutual boasting, and rivalry in wealth and children—like rain whose vegetation delights the cultivators, then dries and you see it yellow, then becomes debris. In the Hereafter are severe punishment, and forgiveness from The God and approval. Worldly life is only deceptive enjoyment.”

Yahīju again concerns vegetation, but the wider subject differs from al-Zumar 39:21.

Al-Zumar emphasizes water and plant changes as signs for reflection.

Al-Ḥadīd compares that image with worldly life and attachment to its passing attractions.

Aʿjaba al-kuffāra nabātuhu: delight in growth

Al-kuffār can here mean cultivators.

Arabic kāfir can denote a farmer covering seed with soil, from ك ف ر’s covering branch.

“Cultivators” is natural and lexically supported in the rain-and-growth context.

Another traditional reading understands disbelievers, highlighting their attachment to worldly splendor. That fits the verse’s wider subject but does not justify dismissing the agricultural sense.

In the agricultural image, successful growth delights the farmer.

After sowing and working for growth, the farmer sees striking greenery.

It does not last unchanged.

Thumma yahīju: passing beyond flourishing

The verb refers to the vegetation produced by rain.

After growth and admiration, it enters a drying stage.

Its meaning matches al-Zumar 39:21.

The main difference is the comparison’s subject and conclusion.

Al-Ḥadīd illustrates the transience of worldly attractions.

People may attach themselves to wealth, children, standing, or achievements as though these would last forever.

Human circumstances change just as a striking green field changes.

This reflection follows the analogy. Yahīju itself does not mean human death, lost wealth, or social defeat.

Yajʿaluhu ḥuṭāman and yakūnu ḥuṭāman

Al-Zumar 39:21 says:

«ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ حُطَامًا»

Thumma yajʿaluhu ḥuṭāmā.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:20 says:

«ثُمَّ يَكُونُ حُطَامًا»

Thumma yakūnu ḥuṭāmā.

In al-Zumar, yajʿaluhu attributes making the plants debris to God.

In al-Ḥadīd, yakūnu describes their resulting state: they become debris.

The grammatical difference should remain.

Both describe vegetation drying and breaking after growth.

The different verbs do not alone establish two entirely different natural mechanisms.

Does the verse deny every worldly blessing’s value?

No. The image emphasizes transience.

Wealth, children, beauty, and blessings are not condemned intrinsically in every circumstance.

The concern here is boasting, competitive accumulation, and attachment to things that cannot last unchanged.

Elsewhere the Qur’an can present property and children as blessings and tests. Their moral value involves use, responsibility, and consequences.

A plant may yield useful produce as it yellows. Drying ends a stage without proving its entire growth worthless.

Yahīju should not make every kind of growth, economic effort, or worldly success seem futile.

Outward flourishing cannot guarantee permanence; life’s entire value should not depend on changing possessions and circumstances.

6. Neighboring roots

ه ي ج and ث و ر: stirring and rising

ث و ر concerns rising and setting in motion.

In al-Rūm 30:48, winds stir and move clouds.

Hāja and thāra overlap in wind, dust, and conflict contexts.

Qur’anic ه ي ج concerns drying vegetation, which ث و ر does not necessarily mean.

Their shared arousal branch does not justify “rebels” or “starts moving” for yahīju in these verses.

ه ي ج and ي ب س: becoming dry and dryness

ي ب س concerns dryness and lost moisture.

Botanical hāja can likewise mean drying.

Hayj in plant-stage descriptions commonly emphasizes transition from freshness.

Yabas has a broader range of dry objects and drying.

Botanical hāja can emphasize declining freshness; yabas can also describe the dry state itself.

The distinction is a tendency: lexicons sometimes directly explain hāja al-nabātu as yabisa.

ه ي ج and ص ف ر: drying and yellowing

Both families occur together in each Qur’anic instance.

Yahīju describes vegetation drying.

Muṣfarran describes its yellow color.

They may occur in one process without being identical.

Not every species dries to bright yellow, and yellowing can accompany natural ripening.

Both words need their independent contribution in translation.

ه ي ج and ح ط م: drying and breaking

ح ط م concerns breaking and crushing.

Ḥuṭām follows yahīju in al-Zumar 39:21 and al-Ḥadīd 57:20.

The sequence distinguishes conditions.

First vegetation loses freshness and dries.

Then its structure may break into debris.

Yahīju should not itself be translated “becomes debris,” since that stage has its own word.

ه ي ج and ه ش م: lost freshness and broken structure

ه ش م appears as hashīm in the Qur’an.

In al-Kahf 18:45, plants become hashīm and winds scatter their remains.

Hashīm denotes dry, broken vegetation.

Qur’anic hayj describes drying and change.

Vegetation may become hashīm after hayj; the words are not complete synonyms.

Wind scattering comes from tadhrūhu, written تَذْرُوهُ, in al-Kahf 18:45, not from ه ي ج.

ه ي ج and ه م د: drying plants and dormant ground

Ḥajj 22:5 uses ه م د for ground without visible growth.

Hāmidah describes land whose growth has subsided.

Yahīju describes vegetation drying after growth.

The natural conditions can relate, but from different perspectives.

Hāmidah describes the ground; yahīju the plant’s change.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 proceeds from water to renewed movement and growth, while the two yahīju verses describe subsequent drying.

ه ي ج and ب ه ج: different roots

Their sounds are close, but the roots differ.

Bahīj in al-Ḥajj 22:5 describes beautiful, pleasing plants.

Yahīju in al-Zumar 39:21 and al-Ḥadīd 57:20 concerns drying plants.

Bahīj belongs to ب ه ج, not ه ي ج.

Sound similarity does not place beauty and freshness in yahīju’s direct meaning.

7. Translation challenges

Modern Persian hayajān readily suggests excitement, motion, and strong feelings.

“Then it becomes excited” would obscure the Qur’anic usage.

“Then it starts moving” is unsuitable here too: the context concerns drying and yellowing, not increasing growth.

“Then it is destroyed” is overly broad and loses the particular botanical stage.

“Begins to dry” or “dries” suits both occurrences.

Al-Zumar 39:21

For:

«ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا»

Thumma yahīju fa-tarāhu muṣfarrā.

A suggested translation is:

“Then it dries, and you see it yellow.”

This preserves change and assigns yellowing to the separate muṣfarran.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:20

For the same phrase:

«ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا»

Thumma yahīju fa-tarāhu muṣfarrā.

The same rendering is suitable:

“Then it dries, and you see it yellow.”

The shared meaning does not require artificial variation.

The verses differ in context, comparison, and the final-stage verb, not yahīju’s meaning.

Non-Qur’anic uses

Other derivatives require context-sensitive equivalents.

Hāja al-ghubāru: “dust rose.”

Hāja al-ghaḍabu: “anger arose” or “intensified.”

Hayyaja al-shawqa: “awakened longing.”

Hājat al-ḥarbu: “war flared up” or “fighting intensified.”

Hāja al-nabtu: “vegetation began drying” or “dried.”

One family does not require translating everything as either excitement or drying.

8. Conclusion

ه ي ج expresses arousal, movement, and intensity in wind, dust, anger, longing, animals, and conflict.

The poem attributed to al-Nābighah illustrates emotional stirring in early poetic tradition. Hayjāʾ for war is also recorded in early and later poetry.

The botanical branch is more specific.

It describes vegetation losing freshness and changing color as it dries after growth.

The Qur’an uses the root twice, always as yahīju.

In al-Zumar 39:21, plants grow, dry and yellow, then become broken remains.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:20 uses the same process to illustrate worldly transience.

Final definition for Forqan: The general arousal branch of ه ي ج denotes stirring, rising, or intensified states and movement. Its botanical and Qur’anic branch describes transition from fresh greenery toward dryness and yellowing. This is distinct from psychological excitement and from final fragmentation.

Yahīju marks drying, muṣfarran visible yellowing, and ḥuṭāman the broken final state.

The image can teach material impermanence, but that moral and philosophical conclusion is not the root’s lexical definition.

Plants may dry after greenery and still yield useful produce. The Qur’an uses natural change to recall a wider truth: today’s flourishing beauty need not remain unchanged.

Distinguishing growth, ripening, drying, and fragmentation clarifies yahīju and preserves both verses’ images without unsupported additions.
