From deserted houses and empty bellies to collapsed structures, fallen palms, and ruined settlements

Arabic KH W Y has two closely related meanings: becoming empty and falling or collapsing. They may coincide, as when a house is abandoned and subsequently collapses. They are not invariably identical: a house may be empty yet remain sound, or collapse while people remain among and around its ruins.

Beyond houses and settlements, early Arabic uses the root for an empty belly, land deprived of rain and habitation, stars whose expected season brings no rain, and the posture of human or animal bodies.

Some uses emphasize an empty interval between two parts. The same family describes a kneeling camel, or a person prostrating, holding the belly away from the ground or thighs and leaving a space between them.

The root occurs five times in the Qur’an, always as خَاوِيَة. Three occurrences describe a settlement or garden fallen upon its structures, one deserted or ruined houses, and one compares the dead of ʿĀd to palm trunks.

Emptiness and falling are central, but what the word describes and each verse’s context determine the prominent aspect.

1. Forms and Semantic Field

KH W Y is a triliteral root with a weak final yāʾ, which changes in some inflected forms.

Its principal recorded Arabic forms include:

  • خَوَى ـ يَخْوِي: To become empty of inhabitants or contents; in another use, to collapse or fall.
  • خَوِيَ ـ يَخْوَى: To become empty; in some uses, concerning the belly or a place.
  • خَيّ, خَوًى, خَوَاء, خُوِيّ, and خَوَايَة: Verbal nouns and nouns concerning emptiness.
  • خَاوٍ and خَاوِيَة: Empty, deserted, or collapsed, depending on what is described.
  • أَخْوَى: To become empty or deprived; of stars and clouds, to bring no rain.
  • خَوَّى ـ يُخَوِّي: In some uses, to empty or cause emptiness; also to leave space between limbs.
  • تَخْوِيَة: Emptiness or leaving an interval; in another use, stars falling or tending toward setting.
  • اِخْتَوَى: In some sources, to snatch something or take it quickly; in another use, to become mentally disordered.
  • خَوَاء: Empty space or an interval, including beneath an animal’s belly and between its limbs.
  • خَوِيّ: Soft or low-lying ground; in some uses, an open, extensive place.
  • خَوِيَّة: In one particular use, food prepared for a woman after childbirth; in another, a space between parts of an animal’s body.
  • خَوَاة: A word for sound mentioned by Ibn Fāris at the end of the entry, which he does not regard as regularly derivable from the main core. It is distinct from خَوَايَة in uses concerning the hollow space of an implement.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an, and the historical connections among all their subsidiary meanings are not equally clear.

The Qur’an uses only خَاوِيَة, a feminine active participle from the family of خَوَى.

خَاوِيَةٌ occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:259, Al-Kahf 18:42, and Al-Ḥajj 22:45; خَاوِيَةً in Al-Naml 27:52; and خَاوِيَةٍ in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7.

These endings reflect grammatical case and function, rather than three separate derivatives with distinct meanings.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five occurrences of the active participle khāwiyah

Ibn Fāris on the Semantic Core

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْخَاءُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالْيَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الْخُلُوِّ وَالسُّقُوطِ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-yāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-khuluwwi wa-al-suqūṭi

Translation:

“Khāʾ, wāw, and yāʾ have one fundamental principle, indicating emptiness and falling.”

This definition places two aspects alongside one another.

First, something formerly present within a place or whole is absent: a house loses its inhabitants or a belly its food.

Second, something falls from its former position: a building collapses or a star disappears from sight.

The two may be connected in material experience, but neither should become a necessary meaning of the other.

A house whose inhabitants have disappeared can remain standing. A building can also collapse without becoming entirely empty of objects.

The two branches, emptiness and collapse, should therefore be retained, while recognizing that some uses combine them in one image.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: emptiness and falling

2. Emptiness in Houses, Bodies, and Nature

A House That Has Lost Its Inhabitants

Arabic has the expression:

خَوَتِ الدَّارُ

khawati al-dāru

Meaning: “The house became empty,” or “The house fell into ruin.”

Lisān al-ʿArab gives two explanations: the house became empty of inhabitants, or its structure collapsed.

Al-Aṣmaʿī explains خَوَى الْبَيْتُ as a house becoming empty of its people.

Another account describes a house still standing after its inhabitants have died or departed.

The distinction matters.

Someone may pass a house whose walls, door, and roof remain intact, although nobody has lived there for years.

Structurally it stands, but it is empty of human presence and life.

In another situation, the roof and walls may also have collapsed.

Early sources record both under KH W Y.

Translating دار خاوية therefore requires determining whether context emphasizes absence of people or collapse of the building.

If both aspects are present, an equivalent such as “deserted and ruined” may be appropriate.

An Empty Belly and Hunger

Arabic خَوَى الْبَطْنُ مِنَ الطَّعَامِ means the belly became empty of food.

أَصَابَهُ الْخَوَى describes someone experiencing hunger.

Here the root concerns an empty bodily interior.

Someone may not have eaten for a time and have an empty belly. In that circumstance, the root is associated with a lack of food.

Yet an empty belly should be distinguished from more severe circumstances such as famine or dire necessity.

Becoming empty some time after eating is natural, while prolonged or severe hunger can cause weakness and bodily harm.

The root itself does not necessarily specify the severity of danger.

KH W Y and مَخْمَصَة

KH M Ṣ emphasizes a belly’s indentation or reduced prominence. Its derivative مَخْمَصَة occurs in the Qur’an for hunger and shortage of food.

KH W Y, however, foregrounds the belly’s empty interior.

These conditions can coincide: lack of food empties the belly, whose appearance may also become more sunken.

Emptiness and indentation nevertheless differ lexically.

Something may have an empty interior without being indented on the outside.

The roots therefore overlap in hunger without having completely identical semantic cores.

KH W Y and a Woman after Childbirth

Lexical sources record خَوِيَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ for a woman who has given birth, leaving her womb empty of its pregnancy.

The family is also used for a woman who has not eaten during or after childbirth.

Here emptiness concerns bodily condition.

Two matters should be distinguished: the womb’s becoming empty after delivery and an empty stomach from lack of food.

Both may occur together, but they are different.

Some sources call food prepared for a woman after childbirth خَوِيَّة.

This name shows the family’s use in childbirth-related circumstances, but the food’s name does not change the root’s core meaning.

Clouds and Stars That Bring No Rain

Early Arabic uses خَوَى النَّجْمُ when a star’s expected rising or setting season passes without the anticipated rain.

أَخْوَى النَّجْمُ is recorded with the same meaning.

Traditional seasonal observation associated particular stars and their rising or setting with periods of rainfall.

When the period passed without rain, this lexical family was used.

خَوَى السَّحَابُ likewise describes a cloud empty of rain.

These uses emphasize absence of what was expected.

They do not establish that stars themselves physically cause rainfall.

Early language may describe rainfall seasons and expectations through stellar positions without such naming constituting a valid scientific account of rainfall’s causes.

3. Spaces between Limbs and Other Uses

خَوَّى: Leaving Space When Kneeling or Prostrating

Arabic خَوَّى الْبَعِيرُ describes a camel kneeling with its belly above the ground, leaving a space underneath.

خَوَّى الرَّجُلُ فِي سُجُودِهِ describes someone prostrating with the belly away from the thighs and upper arms away from the sides.

Nothing is destroyed or abandoned here. An empty interval remains between body parts.

Ibn Fāris connects this with the root’s core because space between two body parts is left empty.

The root therefore concerns more than destruction and loss. An empty interval between things that could touch is another use.

The family also describes a bird spreading its wings and extending its legs when landing.

Spacing and openness between limbs are prominent here.

This use should not become a Qur’anic ruling about an obligatory prostration posture: that derivative does not occur in the Qur’anic text.

خَوَاء: Empty Space

Arabic خَوَاء denotes the empty space between two things.

For a quadruped, it may name the space beneath the belly, between its forelegs and hind legs.

The word also describes open space between earth and sky, or extensive empty land.

Emptiness here does not mean the absence of all matter or living beings throughout the space.

A space may be empty of people, buildings, or major obstacles while containing air, soil, or other things.

خَوَاء therefore does not invariably mean an absolute physical vacuum.

Restricted Subsidiary Uses

Arabic dictionaries record other subsidiary uses, including خَوَايَة for the open part of certain implements or an open space, and خَوِيّ for soft ground or a low, extensive place. Ibn Fāris also records الخَوَاة for sound, but does not regard it as regularly derivable from the main core; this form should not be equated with خَوَايَة.

Some constructions with خَوَى are also reported for snatching or quickly taking something.

Some dictionaries record اِخْتَوَى for mental disorder.

Such uses reveal the family’s breadth, but do not all equally support a definite common historical origin.

A single story should not therefore be invented in which every meaning necessarily developed from ruined houses or empty bellies.

In the Qur’anic uses, emptiness and falling have direct, clear support.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ, Tāj al-ʿArūs, and Asās: material and subsidiary uses

4. Early Arabic Witnesses and Textual Variants

A Collapsing Support: Verse Attributed to Al-Khansāʾ

Kitāb al-ʿAyn, Lisān al-ʿArab, and other early sources attribute this verse to Al-Khansāʾ:

كَانَ أَبُو حَسَّانَ عَرْشًا خَوَى
مِمَّا بَنَاهُ الدَّهْرُ دَانٍ ظَلِيلُ

kāna abū ḥassāna ʿarshan khawā / mimmā banāhu al-dahru dānin ẓalīlu

Translation of the sense:

“Abū Ḥassān was a sheltering support that collapsed: a near, shade-giving structure that time had built.”

In this transmission, خَوَى means falling and collapsing.

Al-Khansāʾ describes someone who provided a firm shelter for family and those around him. His death removed that refuge.

The image fits the root’s sense of collapse.

There is, however, an important variant.

One text of Al-Khansāʾ’s collection records:

إِنَّ أَبَا حَسَّانَ عَرْشٌ هَوَى
مِمَّا بَنَى اللَّهُ بِكِنٍّ ظَلِيلِ

inna abā ḥassāna ʿarshun hawā / mimmā banā allāhu bi-kinnin ẓalīli

Translation of the second transmission’s sense: “Abū Ḥassān was a sheltering support that fell, among what God built, in a shaded shelter.”

Here هَوَى belongs to H W Y, rather than KH W Y.

Although خَوَى occurs in early dictionaries’ transmission, it is not an undisputed poetic witness for the root under study.

The variant also matters semantically: هَوَى concerns falling, whereas خَوَى can express either emptiness or collapse.

The verbs converge in the image of collapse without being the same root.

The witness is therefore useful for studying the history of KH W Y, provided the variant is retained alongside it.

Al-Khansāʾ’s Dīwān: hawā against the lexical transmission khawā

Rainless Stars: An Unattributed Witness

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab transmit:

وَأَخْوَتْ نُجُومُ الْأَخْذِ إِلَّا أَنِضَّةً
أَنِضَّةَ مَحْلٍ لَيْسَ قَاطِرُهَا يُثْرِي

wa-akhwat nujūmu al-akhdhi illā aniḍḍatan / aniḍḍata maḥlin laysa qāṭiruhā yuthrī

Translation of the sense:

“The expected stellar rain seasons passed rainless, except for scant drops in drought whose falling did not moisten the ground.”

أَخْوَتْ concerns disappointed expectations of rain.

Lexical commentary relates نُجُومُ الْأَخْذِ to the stars and seasons through which rainfall was assessed.

أَنِضَّة means a small, scant amount of water or drops of rain.

The poet describes expected rainy periods passing without useful rainfall.

أَخْوَتْ here means neither a building’s collapse nor merely a star’s apparent descent below the horizon. The subject is rainlessness and the expected season yielding nothing.

The examined sources do not securely attribute the verse to a particular poet. It is an Arabic witness recorded in early sources, rather than securely dated pre-Islamic poetry.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the unattributed rainless-season witness

Empty Space in an Animal Description

Al-Zamakhsharī’s Asās al-Balāghah quotes this half-line from Abū al-Najm, describing a male ostrich:

هَاوٍ تَضِلُّ الرِّيحُ فِي خَوَائِهِ

hāwin taḍillu al-rīḥu fī khawāʾihi

Translation of the sense:

“Tall and long-limbed, so that the wind loses itself in the empty space beneath his body.”

Lexical commentary relates خَوَائِهِ to the space between the animal’s limbs.

The image illustrates material خَوَاء: an open interval between body parts.

This witness belongs to the Islamic period and should not be presented as pre-Qur’anic verse.

Together, the witnesses attest emptiness, falling, and empty spacing in early Arabic, while requiring care over some poetic variants and precise dates.

Asās al-Balāghah: Abū al-Najm’s witness describing a male ostrich

5. خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا: Three Qur’anic Uses

خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:259, Al-Kahf 18:42, and Al-Ḥajj 22:45.

In all three, خَاوِيَة concerns something formerly inhabited, built, or productive that now has a different condition.

The subjects differ: a settlement in Al-Baqarah, a garden on which its owner relied in Al-Kahf, and settlements destroyed because of their inhabitants’ wrongdoing in Al-Ḥajj.

A Desolate Settlement and the Question of Revival: Al-Baqarah 2:259

أَوْ كَالَّذِي مَرَّ عَلَىٰ قَرْيَةٍ وَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا قَالَ أَنَّىٰ يُحْيِي هَٰذِهِ اللَّهُ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا

aw ka-alladhī marra ʿalā qaryatin wa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā qāla annā yuḥyī hādhihi allāhu baʿda mawtihā

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Or like the one who passed a settlement fallen upon its structures and said: ‘How will The God bring this to life after its death?’”

Al-Baqarah 2:259

خَاوِيَة describes the settlement’s condition.

Al-Ṭabarī relates the word to the settlement’s being empty of people. Other accounts explain خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا as buildings fallen upon their roofs.

Both meanings have lexical support.

عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا is nevertheless an important contextual indication.

عُرُوش is the plural of عرش and here can refer to buildings, roofs, and erected structures.

Under the collapse reading, the settlement’s structure has broken down, with parts fallen upon its roofs or structures.

A report from Al-Suddī expresses this as سَاقِطَةٌ عَلَىٰ سُقُوفِهَا.

Another reading emphasizes absence of inhabitants while structures or their traces remain visible.

Must the Whole Settlement Have Collapsed?

The phrase describes a ruined, empty settlement but does not specify surviving buildings, damage to every wall, or the precise condition of all roofs.

خَاوِيَة alone therefore cannot establish that not even one intact wall or house remained.

Conversely, interpreting it as empty of people must not make عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا insignificant.

Overall, a settlement depleted of former life and habitation, with visible signs of ruin, fits the expression.

The person then speaks of its being brought to life after death.

This relates the settlement’s condition, in the observer’s eyes, to death and the loss of former life.

خَاوِيَة itself does not mean human death. The settlement’s being dead is a broader contextual description.

The verse continues with the person’s death and raising, a hundred years passing, and the demonstration of bones being raised and clothed with flesh.

This develops the subject of God’s power to revive, rather than adding all those events to خَاوِيَة’s lexical definition.

Al-Ṭabarī: empty of people and the report of collapse upon roofs

A Collapsed Garden and Lost Produce: Al-Kahf 18:42

وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِ فَأَصْبَحَ يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ عَلَىٰ مَا أَنْفَقَ فِيهَا وَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا

wa-uḥīṭa bi-thamarihi fa-aṣbaḥa yuqallibu kaffayhi ʿalā mā anfaqa fīhā wa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“His produce was destroyed, and he began turning his hands over what he had spent on it, while the garden lay fallen upon its structures.”

Al-Kahf 18:42

This verse belongs to the story of the two garden owners.

Earlier, one owner speaks of his gardens, wealth, and resources, treating their continuance as almost certain.

Verse forty-two describes lost produce and his regret.

خَاوِيَة concerns a garden formerly containing trees, cultivation, and productive facilities.

What Does عُرُوش Mean in a Garden?

Arabic عرش is not limited to a house’s roof.

عَرْشُ الْكَرْمِ denotes the trellis or structure supporting vine branches.

عَرِيش can also mean a canopy or structure providing cover and support.

The gardens in Al-Kahf contain grapes, palms, and crops. Trellises and garden structures are therefore appropriate possibilities alongside the general senses of buildings and roofs.

Al-Ṭabarī discusses destroyed produce and the garden’s becoming empty upon its vegetation and structures.

Other explanations understand the expression as garden structures and trellises collapsing.

“Collapsed upon its trellises” is therefore defensible in a garden context, without necessarily restricting every عرش to a vine trellis.

خَاوِيَة and Lost Wealth

Here خَاوِيَة describes a situation in which the owner has lost investment and produce.

The word itself does not mean poverty, remorse, or associationism.

Lost produce is expressed by أُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِ.

The owner’s regret emerges from يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ and his subsequent words.

خَاوِيَة depicts a garden fallen from, or emptied of, its former productivity and stability.

The entire story’s message should not be placed inside one adjective’s meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: produce and garden structures

Settlements, Abandoned Wells, and Lofty Palaces: Al-Ḥajj 22:45

فَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ أَهْلَكْنَاهَا وَهِيَ ظَالِمَةٌ فَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا وَبِئْرٍ مُعَطَّلَةٍ وَقَصْرٍ مَشِيدٍ

fa-ka-ayyin min qaryatin ahlaknāhā wa-hiya ẓālimatun fa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā wa-biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin wa-qaṣrin mashīdin

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“How many a settlement We destroyed while it was wrongdoing! It lies fallen upon its structures; and how many an abandoned well and firmly built palace.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:45

خَاوِيَة follows the report of settlements’ destruction.

An abandoned well and a lofty or firmly built palace follow.

Their juxtaposition strengthens the image of a settlement no longer functioning and empty of life.

A well may remain structurally sound, and even contain water, while nobody uses it.

A palace may likewise remain standing after losing its former inhabitants.

Thus ruin includes more than fallen stone and walls: lost life and use of a place also enter the image.

This comes from the settlement, well, and palace together, rather than خَاوِيَة alone.

Al-Ṭabarī brings both aspects together: loss of inhabitants and collapse of the settlement’s buildings.

This reading fits the root’s semantic field.

Does Every Abandoned Settlement Result from Punishment?

Al-Ḥajj 22:45 concerns wrongdoing settlements whose destruction is described in that context.

Yet خَاوِيَة itself does not mean “punished.”

A house may become empty through migration, a watercourse changing, natural deterioration, or another event.

The word alone therefore cannot establish that every ruined house or abandoned settlement was destroyed because of its inhabitants’ sins.

The connection between wrongdoing and destruction is explicit in Al-Ḥajj, but comes from the full verse rather than KH W Y’s lexical core.

Al-Ṭabarī: settlement, abandoned well, and palace

6. Empty Houses and Hollow Palms: Two Further Uses

Thamūd’s Houses: Al-Naml 27:52

فَتِلْكَ بُيُوتُهُمْ خَاوِيَةً بِمَا ظَلَمُوا إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِقَوْمٍ يَعْلَمُونَ

fa-tilka buyūtuhum khāwiyatan bi-mā ẓalamū inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yaʿlamūna

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Those are their houses, empty and ruined because of their wrongdoing. In this is a sign for people who know.”

Al-Naml 27:52

This follows Thamūd’s story and a group’s plot to harm Ṣāliḥ and his family.

The preceding verses describe their plotting and its outcome.

Their houses are then described as خَاوِيَة.

In the common reading, خَاوِيَةً is accusative; a familiar grammatical analysis treats it as a circumstantial expression.

Unlike the preceding three verses, عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا is absent.

The difference matters.

بُيُوتُهُمْ خَاوِيَةً can depict houses whose inhabitants have disappeared, leaving them deserted and lifeless.

The houses may also have been damaged or collapsed, but the phrase does not conclusively determine the damage’s degree or form.

Al-Ṭabarī explains خَاوِيَة here as the houses’ being empty of people.

This fits the context of the people’s destruction.

Is خَاوِيَة in Al-Naml Identical to خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا?

The word’s core is the same, but the complete constructions differ.

Al-Naml 27:52 describes the houses’ condition.

In Al-Baqarah 2:259, Al-Kahf 18:42, and Al-Ḥajj 22:45, خَاوِيَة is accompanied by عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا.

Structures and their possible collapse are therefore more prominent in those three verses.

In Al-Naml 27:52, empty, deserted houses make clear sense without assuming a particular pattern of collapse.

One derivative cannot therefore receive an identical detailed translation everywhere without regard to construction.

Al-Ṭabarī: houses empty of inhabitants

أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ خَاوِيَةٍ: Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7

سَخَّرَهَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَبْعَ لَيَالٍ وَثَمَانِيَةَ أَيَّامٍ حُسُومًا فَتَرَى الْقَوْمَ فِيهَا صَرْعَىٰ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ خَاوِيَةٍ

sakhkharahā ʿalayhim sabʿa layālin wa-thamāniyata ayyāmin ḥusūman fa-tarā al-qawma fīhā ṣarʿā ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin khāwiyatin

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“He subjected it to them for seven nights and eight successive days. You see the people lying fallen within it, as though they were trunks of hollow, fallen palms.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7

خَاوِيَةٍ describes نَخْلٍ.

Unlike earlier uses, the subject is palms rather than a house or settlement.

أَعْجَاز is the plural of عَجُز and here refers to trunks or lower portions of palms.

The Qur’an compares the dead of ʿĀd to palm trunks.

The image evokes palms that have lost their former living, stable condition.

Does خَاوِيَة Mean Hollow Palm Trunks?

Lexical and exegetical sources discuss this interpretation.

Drying, wear, or decay can remove parts of a trunk’s interior and leave it hollow.

خَاوِيَة can then describe its empty interior.

Another reading concerns palms fallen or uprooted, no longer standing firmly.

This foregrounds the root’s branch of falling.

A similar verse, Al-Qamar 54:20, matters for examining that possibility:

تَنْزِعُ النَّاسَ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ مُنْقَعِرٍ

tanziʿu al-nāsa ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin munqaʿirin

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“It uproots people as though they were trunks of uprooted palms.”

مُنْقَعِرٍ belongs to another root and concerns removal from a position or root.

The images are close, but خَاوِيَة and مُنْقَعِر are not necessarily complete synonyms.

مُنْقَعِر emphasizes uprooting, while خَاوِيَة can express emptiness, loss of stability, or falling.

“Hollow and fallen palms” in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 therefore preserves both lexical aspects of the image.

This does not establish that the bodies of ʿĀd literally became hollow like palm trunks. A simile need not make every bodily feature identical.

The Grammar of نَخْلٍ خَاوِيَةٍ

خَاوِيَةٍ is grammatically feminine singular and genitive.

نَخْلٍ is a collective noun and can take a feminine singular adjective in Arabic.

The adjective’s feminine form therefore does not restrict the verse to female palms.

It also grammatically describes the palms, rather than directly modifying الْقَوْم.

Within the simile, however, the palms’ condition depicts the fallen people.

This distinction between grammatical construction and the comparison’s purpose matters.

Al-Ṭabarī: khāwiyah palms and the comparison with the people

7. Comparison with Related Roots

KH W Y (خَوَى) and KH L W (خَلَا)

KH L W also concerns emptiness and something’s absence from a place.

The roots overlap in uses such as houses empty of inhabitants.

KH W Y, however, also has an established branch concerning falling and collapse.

خَوَتِ الدَّارُ can therefore concern a building’s ruin and collapse, whereas an ordinary house’s being empty with خَلَا need not mean it has collapsed.

This does not mean خَلَا is never used concerning ruin. The difference concerns the roots’ recorded ranges of use.

Study of KH L W

KH W Y (خَوَى) and H W Y (هَوَى)

One branch of H W Y concerns falling.

The خَوَى / هَوَى variant in verse attributed to Al-Khansāʾ shows how close the verbs can be within a collapse image.

Their roots nevertheless differ.

In its falling use, هَوَى emphasizes falling itself.

Besides falling, خَوَى can describe something becoming or remaining empty.

Collapse is therefore defensible in خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا, without eliminating emptiness because of its proximity to هَوَى.

Study of H W Y

KH W Y (خَوَى) and S Q Ṭ (سَقَطَ)

سقط concerns something falling from its former position.

This is close to KH W Y’s falling branch.

Yet KH W Y can describe a house empty of inhabitants but still standing.

Not every empty house has therefore fallen.

Conversely, not everything that falls becomes empty or deserted.

Qur’anic عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا can clarify the collapsing aspect, but the construction’s meaning extends beyond خَاوِيَة alone.

KH W Y (خَوَى) and KH R B (خَرَاب)

Arabic خراب concerns ruin and the loss of habitation or flourishing.

خَاوِيَة can likewise describe a ruined house or settlement.

Ruin may result from collapse, damage, or a building’s becoming unusable.

Some uses of KH W Y, however, concern absence of inhabitants or contents even while the structure stands.

خَاوِيَة and خراب therefore overlap for ruined settlements without losing all their differences.

Study of KH R B

KH W Y (خَوَى) and Q ʿ R (اِنْقِعَار)

مُنْقَعِر describes palm trunks in Al-Qamar 54:20.

It belongs to Q ʿ R and concerns uprooting from a position in that context.

خَاوِيَة in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7, besides falling, can depict hollowness and loss of the palms’ living condition.

Both verses concern the same people and a wind-related event, but choose different descriptions.

They let the reader see different aspects of an image without making the roots completely synonymous.

KH W Y (خَوَى) and KH M Ṣ (خَمْص)

KH M Ṣ concerns indentation and reduced prominence; Qur’anic مَخْمَصَة concerns hunger.

Early Arabic also uses KH W Y for a belly empty of food.

Their images nevertheless differ.

KH M Ṣ foregrounds shape and indentation.

KH W Y emphasizes emptiness within.

Although they can share an instance of hunger, their core meanings should remain distinct.

Study of KH M Ṣ

KH W Y (خَوَى) and ʿ R SH (عَرْش)

Arabic عرش can also denote a building, roof, canopy, or erected structure.

In خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا, it names the place or structure in relation to which the settlement or garden’s condition is described.

عرش itself does not mean ruin.

Nor does every use of KH W Y require a roof or structure: the family also describes empty bellies or rainless clouds.

خَاوِيَة and عُرُوشِهَا are separate components whose relationship produces the complete image.

8. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish the root’s two main branches.

For houses and places, “empty,” “vacant,” or “deserted” may fit; if context describes structural collapse, “collapsed” or “ruined” is more precise.

In خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا, collapse has clear support from the accompanying phrase, while absence of life and inhabitants may also be present.

For خَاوِيَةً in Al-Naml 27:52, without عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا and with the inhabitants’ destruction in context, “empty and deserted” is a clearer basic equivalent; “ruined” can also fit the overall narrative situation.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 concerns palms, so “hollow and fallen” approaches the image.

“Uprooted” should not become a necessary meaning of خَاوِيَة everywhere: the parallel in Al-Qamar expresses that feature through the separate word مُنْقَعِر.

Brief Root Meaning

Becoming empty of what was formerly present; in another branch, falling and collapsing from a former state.

Meaning in Qur’anic Uses

خَاوِيَةٌ — Al-Baqarah 2:259: An empty, ruined settlement; with عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا, fallen upon its structures.

خَاوِيَةٌ — Al-Kahf 18:42: A ruined garden emptied of its flourishing, fallen upon trellises or structures.

خَاوِيَةٌ — Al-Ḥajj 22:45: A deserted, collapsed settlement after its inhabitants’ destruction.

خَاوِيَةً — Al-Naml 27:52: Houses empty of inhabitants and ruined.

خَاوِيَةٍ — Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7: Hollow, fallen palms whose trunks provide the comparison for the dead of ʿĀd.

These equivalents distinguish the brief root meaning from each verse’s particular sense.

Divine punishment, human death, lost wealth, and revival should not become parts of خَاوِيَة’s lexical definition. They come from the verses’ construction and context.

Conclusion

Arabic KH W Y concerns two principal fields: becoming empty and falling or collapsing.

A house may lose its inhabitants or its structure may collapse. A belly can lack food, while an empty interval may remain between limbs. In natural uses, clouds and stellar seasons are described through this family when expected rain fails.

All five Qur’anic occurrences use خَاوِيَة, but describe different subjects.

Three combine it with عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا to depict a ruined settlement or garden. Al-Naml 27:52 describes Thamūd’s houses as empty and deserted after their inhabitants’ destruction. Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 compares the dead of ʿĀd to trunks of hollow, fallen palms.

Lexical witnesses prevent reducing every خَاوِيَة to either “empty” alone or “collapsed” alone: both branches exist in Arabic.

This breadth should not become a vague definition covering every weakness, ruin, loss, or change. The core is more specific: loss of former contents or presence; in a related branch, something falling from its structure or position.

The Qur’anic context and the adjective’s subject determine which aspect is prominent.

This matters especially in خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا. One image can convey both lost inhabitants and flourishing, and collapsed structures, without both meanings necessarily operating together in every use of the root. Brief root meaning: Becoming empty of what was formerly present; in another branch, falling and collapsing from a former state.
