Principal senses: wounding; in animal contexts, hamstringing and bringing down an animal; in the reproductive branch, infertility and not bearing children. A further branch concerns the base or central part of a place. Disabling function is a possible comparison between some senses, not a definition that replaces them all.

1. The root in early Arabic

The three-consonant root «ع ق ر» has several semantic branches: wounding and disabling movement, infertility and not bearing children, and the base or central part of a place.

In early Arabic, «عَقَرَ» means to wound or injure. It is particularly used of horses and camels whose legs are struck or injured so that they lose the ability to stand or move.

«عَقَرَ الْبَعِيرَ» means to hamstring a camel. «عَقِير» and «مَعْقُور» can describe an animal injured in this way.

This might be done to bring the animal down or prepare to kill it. In a practice recorded in early lexicons, the camel's leg was first injured so that it fell, and it was then slaughtered by naḥr.

Consequently, «عَقَرَ» also occurs with the meaning of killing an animal.

Hamstringing must nevertheless be distinguished from killing. An animal may be hamstrung and remain alive, or it may die during the injury or be killed afterward.

In this animal-related branch, ʿaqr denotes injury, especially hamstringing. Where killing is the outcome, it can also name the whole operation; loss of movement is not a necessary condition of every use meaning injury.

In another branch, «عاقر» describes a woman or man who does not produce children. Applied to a woman, it concerns infertility and not becoming pregnant.

Some lexicographers explain a connection between the branches by comparing infertility to loss of reproductive capacity, as a hamstrung animal loses its usual mobility.

This is an intelligible semantic comparison, but it is not an established history of the word's origin.

Nor does it imply that an infertile person necessarily has a particular cut or bodily injury. The word «عاقر» does not identify a medical cause.

A further branch uses «عُقْر» for the base or central location of something, as in «عُقْرُ الدَّارِ», the main or central part of a dwelling.

Lexicons discuss this branch separately from wounding.

The family should therefore not be reduced to an invented single image. Two senses matter especially in the Quran: hamstringing and bringing down an animal, and infertility or not bearing children.

2. Evidence from early Arabic poetry

Hamstringing and killing an animal

A clear early example occurs in the Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays:

وَيَوْمَ عَقَرْتُ لِلْعَذَارَى مَطِيَّتِي
فَيَا عَجَبًا مِنْ كَوْرِهَا الْمُتَحَمَّلِ

wa-yawma ʿaqartu li-al-ʿadhārā maṭiyyatī / fa-yā ʿajaban min kawrihā al-mutaḥammali

“And the day I hamstrung and brought down my riding camel for the maidens—how remarkable its saddle and equipment, carried away by others!”

Here «عَقَرْتُ» concerns the poet's riding camel.

The following line mentions the meat and fat of the same animal, enjoyed by the maidens.

In this account, then, the act resulted in the animal's death and consumption of its meat.

The example shows that early ʿaqr could cover more than a limited wound: the entire act of bringing down and killing a camel.

It does not establish exact lexical identity with dhabḥ or naḥr.

Early explanations frequently associate ʿaqr with injury to the legs and disabling movement, while naḥr is a distinct method of slaughtering a camel.

The line belongs to the famous Muʿallaqah of Imruʾ al-Qays and is important evidence attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic. Like other poetry transmitted from that period, its wording reaches us through later recensions; its use of «عَقَرْتُ» for a camel is clear.

Injury without immediate death

Another line in the same poem says:

تَقُولُ وَقَدْ مَالَ الْغَبِيطُ بِنَا مَعًا
عَقَرْتَ بَعِيرِي يَا امْرَأَ الْقَيْسِ فَانْزِلِ

taqūlu wa-qad māla al-ghabīṭu binā maʿan / ʿaqarta baʿīrī yā imraʾa al-qaysi fa-nzili

“As the howdah tilted with both of us, she said: You have injured my camel, Imruʾ al-Qays—get down!”

Here «عَقَرْتَ بَعِيرِي» concerns injury associated with the riders' weight and the saddle or howdah. Lexicons cite it for injury to the back.

The scene does not require the animal to have been killed at that moment.

It illustrates the broader sense of injury, not necessarily hamstringing. Poetic exaggeration is also possible; the line alone does not establish the actual extent of injury.

Together, the two lines show that early ʿaqr could concern killing a camel or injuring it, without every injury having to be a severed leg tendon or complete loss of movement.

The final outcome of one occurrence should not be generalized to every use.

ʿĀqir: infertility in early usage

Another important witness is attributed to ʿĀmir ibn al-Ṭufayl, an Arab poet and warrior who lived near the emergence of Islam:

لَبِئْسَ الْفَتَى إِنْ كُنْتُ أَعْوَرَ عَاقِرًا
جَبَانًا فَمَا عُذْرِي لَدَى كُلِّ مَحْضَرِ

la-biʾsa al-fatā in kuntu aʿwara ʿāqiran / jabānan fa-mā ʿudhrī ladā kulli maḥḍari

“What a poor fellow I would be if I were one-eyed, infertile, and cowardly—what excuse would I have at every gathering?”

Here «عَاقِرًا» describes a man.

This matters because Quranic «عاقر» describes only Zechariah's wife, while Arabic usage does not restrict it to women.

A man who does not father children can also be called «عاقر».

The line also reflects an association between producing children and male social standing in some Arab environments.

That social valuation is not part of the word's lexical meaning.

Having no children does not make someone less worthy as a human being or in moral or social terms.

The poet uses standards of his environment for praise or reproach; those standards are not necessarily endorsed by the Quran.

ʿĀmir ibn al-Ṭufayl lived near the emergence of Islam. Early sources attribute this line to him, and it attests masculine «عاقر». Its exact date of composition has not been independently established in this study.

Both animal injury and infertility are thus attested in early Arabic. These examples alone cannot establish which came first or the exact path of semantic development.

3. Lexical and Quranic forms

Important forms include «عَقَرَ», «يَعْقِرُ», «عَقْر», «عَقِير», «مَعْقُور», «عَقُور», «عَاقِر», «عُقْر», «عَقَار», «عَاقَرَ», and «تَعَاقَرَ».

These forms belong to several semantic branches.

«عَقَرَ»: to wound, especially to hamstring and bring down an animal.

«عَقْر»: a wound or injury; in animal contexts, hamstringing.

«عَقِير» and «مَعْقُور»: an injured or hamstrung animal or person.

«عَقُور»: an animal that bites or injures, as in «كَلْبٌ عَقُورٌ».

«عَاقِر»: a woman or man who does not produce children.

«عُقْر»: in the spatial branch, the base, center, or main location of something.

«عَقَار»: in the property branch, a house, land, or immovable property.

«عَاقَرَ» and «تَعَاقَرَ»: in some uses, competing in hamstringing or slaughtering camels, including displays of generosity and rivalry.

In some early social settings, two people might compete in killing camels to demonstrate generosity or standing. The act belongs to the root; rivalry and competition are identified through the verbal pattern and context.

That social practice is not the meaning of every derivative.

The base and center of a place

«عُقْرُ الدَّارِ» denotes the main or central part of a dwelling.

The same branch includes «عَقَار» for fixed property, particularly land and real estate.

A person may own a house, land, or an orchard that stays in place, unlike movable goods.

Such property can be called «عقار» in Arabic.

This differs from animal injury and infertility.

Some lexical explanations propose connections between remaining in a fixed place and other branches. The evidence examined here does not establish a single historical origin.

The branch should therefore be recognized separately, without forcing it to derive from cutting an animal's legs or not bearing children.

Quranic forms

The root occurs eight times in eight Quranic verses: five verbs and three instances of the adjective «عاقر».

First, the basic verb «عَقَرَ» occurs in the account of Salih and Thamud's she-camel:

«فَعَقَرُوا», «فَعَقَرُوهَا», and «فَعَقَرَ».

Second, «عَاقِر» describes Zechariah's wife:

«عَاقِرٌ» and «عَاقِرًا».

All the verbal occurrences use the basic stem.

«فَعَقَرُوا» is a third-person plural past verb.

«فَعَقَرُوهَا» is the same plural verb with a feminine object pronoun.

«فَعَقَرَ» is a third-person masculine singular past verb.

«عَاقِر» has the pattern «فَاعِل» and describes infertility in its Quranic occurrences.

«عُقْر», «عَقَار», «عَقِير», «مَعْقُور», and «عَقُور» do not occur in the Quran.

They help explain the wider lexical family, but Quranic analysis centers on hamstringing and infertility.

4. Hamstringing Salih's she-camel

The first verbal occurrence in the order of the Quran is in Al-Aʿrāf:

فَعَقَرُوا النَّاقَةَ وَعَتَوْا عَنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّهِمْ وَقَالُوا يَا صَالِحُ ائْتِنَا بِمَا تَعِدُنَا إِنْ كُنْتَ مِنَ الْمُرْسَلِينَ

fa-ʿaqarū al-nāqata wa-ʿataw ʿan amri rabbihim wa-qālū yā ṣāliḥu iʾtinā bi-mā taʿidunā in kunta mina al-mursalīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:77

Al-Aʿrāf 7:77

“Then they hamstrung the she-camel, rebelled against their Lord's command, and said: Salih, bring us what you promise us, if you are one of the Messengers.”

«فَعَقَرُوا» is from the basic verb «عَقَرَ».

Its object is «النَّاقَةَ», the she-camel.

The act is hamstringing and bringing down the she-camel.

In the earlier presentation of the sign, Al-Aʿrāf 7:73, Salih commands his people to let the she-camel feed in The God's land and not harm her.

Hamstringing her in Al-Aʿrāf 7:77 violates that command.

Why «عَقَرُوا» rather than «ذَبَحُوا»?

Dhabḥ and naḥr describe particular ways of killing an animal.

ʿAqr concerns wounding and disabling an animal, especially by injuring its legs.

In some Arabic uses, hamstringing precedes slaughter.

The Quran's «فَعَقَرُوا النَّاقَةَ» therefore names an act lexically associated with hamstringing and bringing down the she-camel.

The verse does not specify which leg was injured, the instrument used, or the precise manner of death.

Details from external narratives should not be inserted into the verb's meaning.

Equally, ʿaqr here should not be reduced to an insignificant surface wound.

Across the account, it is a destructive attack on a sign The God had provided for the people.

The appropriate contextual sense is “hamstringing and bringing down the she-camel.”

Injury and rebellion

The same verse says:

وَعَتَوْا عَنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّهِمْ

wa-ʿataw ʿan amri rabbihim

Al-Aʿrāf 7:77

This phrase is from ʿ T W and concerns rebellion against a command.

The Quran thus places two matters together:

First, hamstringing the she-camel.

Second, rebelling against the Lord's command.

They are connected, but one meaning should not replace the other.

ʿAqr does not itself mean disbelief or rebellion. Arabic can use it of killing an animal for meat.

In Salih's account, however, the act opposes an explicit command.

Its wrongdoing follows from the context and prior command; every lexical use of ʿaqr does not inherently denote Thamud's sin.

5. The share of water and the attack on the she-camel

Before mentioning the attack, Al-Shuʿarāʾ records Salih's words about access to water:

قَالَ هَٰذِهِ نَاقَةٌ لَهَا شِرْبٌ وَلَكُمْ شِرْبُ يَوْمٍ مَعْلُومٍ

qāla hādhihi nāqatun lahā shirbun wa-lakum shirbu yawmin maʿlūm

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:155

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:155

“He said: This is a she-camel; she has a share of water, and you have a share on a known day.”

The verse assigns the she-camel and the people a defined share of water.

It concerns more than the animal's presence: a specific entitlement to water must be respected.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:156 then commands them not to harm her.

It continues:

فَعَقَرُوهَا فَأَصْبَحُوا نَادِمِينَ

fa-ʿaqarūhā fa-aṣbaḥū nādimīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157

“Then they hamstrung her and became regretful.”

The sequence shows that despite the clear command about the she-camel and her entitlement, they injured and brought her down.

Their regret is then stated.

But «نَادِمِينَ» belongs to N D M, not ʿ Q R.

Regret is their subsequent state in the account, not the meaning of «فَعَقَرُوهَا» itself.

Protecting a sign

The she-camel is identified as «نَاقَةُ اللَّهِ» and as a sign for the people.

That attribution explains her importance in this account.

Neither the noun for she-camel nor ʿaqr implies that every she-camel always has the same special standing.

A specific command applied here, and the people disobeyed by harming her.

The verses thus go beyond the lexical act to human responsibility toward a divine sign and command.

The account can also prompt reflection on rights and limits in using resources, since turns at the water are explicitly mentioned.

Such reflection is not part of the lexical definition of ʿaqr.

Here ʿaqr is the injury and bringing down of the animal. Water rights, her status as a sign, and the people's rebellion come from other parts of the account.

6. One person's act and the group's responsibility

A significant feature is the distinction between singular and plural forms.

Most occurrences say:

«فَعَقَرُوا» or «فَعَقَرُوهَا».

These are plural.

Al-Qamar, however, says:

فَنَادَوْا صَاحِبَهُمْ فَتَعَاطَىٰ فَعَقَرَ

fa-nādaw ṣāḥibahum fa-taʿāṭā fa-ʿaqar

Al-Qamar 54:29

Al-Qamar 54:29

“Then they called their companion; he undertook the act and hamstrung her.”

A group first calls its companion.

«فَتَعَاطَىٰ» then appears in the singular.

«فَعَقَرَ» is singular too.

The verse thus identifies a person who directly carries out the act.

This relates also to Al-Shams 91:12:

إِذِ انْبَعَثَ أَشْقَاهَا

idhi inbaʿatha ashqāhā

Al-Shams 91:12

Al-Shams 91:12

“When the most wretched of them rose up.”

Yet Al-Shams 91:14 uses the plural:

فَكَذَّبُوهُ فَعَقَرُوهَا فَدَمْدَمَ عَلَيْهِمْ رَبُّهُمْ بِذَنْبِهِمْ فَسَوَّاهَا

fa-kadhdhabūhu fa-ʿaqarūhā fa-damdama ʿalayhim rabbuhum bi-dhanbihim fa-sawwāhā

Al-Shams 91:14

Al-Shams 91:14

“They denied him and hamstrung her; then their Lord brought destruction down upon them for their sin and leveled it.”

«فَعَقَرُوهَا» attributes the act to the group.

Did one person or a group hamstring her?

The comparison distinguishes direct performance from attribution to a group.

Al-Qamar 54:29 identifies a particular person as the direct agent.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:77, Hūd 11:65, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157, and Al-Shams 91:14 attribute the act to the people in the plural.

The statements need not conflict.

One person may perform the final act while others contribute to the decision, encouragement, preparation, acceptance, or support.

For example, a group might decide to destroy property and one member carry out the destruction.

Direct execution can then belong to one person while group responsibility remains relevant.

This illustration does not prove that every member of Thamud participated equally or personally struck the animal.

The verses distinguish the direct agent from the group credited with the action, but do not detail each individual's contribution.

Lexically, «فَعَقَرَ» and «فَعَقَرُوهَا» are forms of the same verb; their subjects differ in number, not grammatical person, and the latter includes an object pronoun.

«فَتَعَاطَىٰ» and «فَعَقَرَ»

Two verbs occur in sequence in Al-Qamar 54:29.

«فَتَعَاطَىٰ» belongs to ʿ Ṭ W and here concerns undertaking an action.

«فَعَقَرَ» belongs to ʿ Q R and names the hamstringing.

They should not be translated as identical actions.

The Quran first mentions the person's undertaking, then what he did.

Neither verb specifies the instrument or full procedure.

7. ʿĀqir: infertility and not bearing children

The second Quranic branch is the adjective «عَاقِر».

All three occurrences describe Zechariah's wife.

Arabic applies it to a woman or man who does not produce children.

For a woman, it means infertility and not becoming pregnant.

The description does not itself identify a cause.

The word names neither a particular disease nor a specific medical diagnosis; the person's circumstances require separate knowledge.

Nor does it mean a woman who deliberately chooses not to have children.

This matters morphologically.

Although «عَاقِر» has the pattern «فَاعِل», it describes a condition here, not necessarily its voluntary agent.

Without a feminine ending

The Quran says:

وَامْرَأَتِي عَاقِرٌ

wa-imraʾatī ʿāqir

Āl ʿImrān 3:40

And elsewhere:

وَكَانَتِ امْرَأَتِي عَاقِرًا

wa-kānati imraʾatī ʿāqirā

Maryam 19:5

Both use «عاقر» without the feminine tāʾ.

Arabic permits this form for a woman. Some bodily descriptions also lack the ending, but that does not make every such adjective identical in usage or gender distribution.

Absence of the ending does not make the referent masculine.

The word also describes men, as the verse of ʿĀmir ibn al-Ṭufayl showed.

Its meaning is therefore not restricted to women, although all three Quranic occurrences concern one woman.

8. ʿĀqir in Zechariah's prayer

Maryam says:

وَإِنِّي خِفْتُ الْمَوَالِيَ مِنْ وَرَائِي وَكَانَتِ امْرَأَتِي عَاقِرًا فَهَبْ لِي مِنْ لَدُنْكَ وَلِيًّا

wa-innī khiftu al-mawāliya min warāʾī wa-kānati imraʾatī ʿāqiran fa-hab lī min ladunka waliyyā

Maryam 19:5

Maryam 19:5

“I fear my kinsfolk after me, and my wife has been infertile; so grant me, from Yourself, a guardian.”

Zechariah describes his wife's condition with «عَاقِرًا».

This follows his account of his own old age and bodily frailty.

In ordinary circumstances, the situation would seem to offer little hope of a child.

Yet he asks The God for a walī.

In Maryam 19:6, the request is explained through inheritance and the successor being pleasing; «عاقر» alone does not specify all those dimensions.

The description conveys difficulty in having children, not blame or a charge of wrongdoing against Zechariah's wife.

Infertility and old age are distinct

Āl ʿImrān says:

قَالَ رَبِّ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لِي غُلَامٌ وَقَدْ بَلَغَنِيَ الْكِبَرُ وَامْرَأَتِي عَاقِرٌ ۖ قَالَ كَذَٰلِكَ اللَّهُ يَفْعَلُ مَا يَشَاءُ

qāla rabbi annā yakūnu lī ghulāmun wa-qad balaghaniya al-kibaru wa-imraʾatī ʿāqirun qāla kadhālika Allāhu yafʿalu mā yashāʾ

Āl ʿImrān 3:40

Āl ʿImrān 3:40

“He said: My Lord, how shall I have a boy when old age has reached me and my wife is infertile? He said: Thus; The God does whatever He wills.”

Zechariah mentions two things separately.

First, his own old age.

Second, his wife's infertility.

This distinction shows that «عاقر» does not itself mean elderly.

Someone can be elderly without being described as «عاقر», while infertility can concern a younger person.

They are lexically different conditions, even when they occur together.

Maryam 19:8 also says:

قَالَ رَبِّ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لِي غُلَامٌ وَكَانَتِ امْرَأَتِي عَاقِرًا وَقَدْ بَلَغْتُ مِنَ الْكِبَرِ عِتِيًّا

qāla rabbi annā yakūnu lī ghulāmun wa-kānati imraʾatī ʿāqiran wa-qad balaghtu mina al-kibari ʿitiyyā

Maryam 19:8

Maryam 19:8

“He said: My Lord, how shall I have a boy when my wife has been infertile and I have reached advanced frailty through old age?”

Again, his wife's infertility and his advanced age are separate factors.

«عِتِيًّا» belongs to ʿ T W and concerns the frailty and difficulty of old age here.

That frailty should not be inserted into «عاقر» itself.

Does it mean permanent infertility?

Ordinarily, «عاقر» describes a woman who does not bear children.

The adjective alone is not a philosophical or medical judgment that childbearing is forever impossible.

A description of long-standing childlessness does not by itself identify its cause or establish whether the situation can change.

In Zechariah's account, the Quran describes something unexpected in his known circumstances.

It then announces the birth of John.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:90 says that The God answered him, granted him John, and set his wife right for him.

The earlier condition did not prevent the divine gift.

The statement does not explain a bodily change or medical mechanism.

No specific disease should be inferred from «عاقر», nor a particular change to a named organ or biological process from «أَصْلَحْنَا لَهُ زَوْجَهُ».

The lexical meaning remains infertility; childbearing contrary to ordinary expectation follows from the wider account.

9. ʿAqr and ʿāqir: related forms, different uses

A semantic relationship between hamstringing and infertility can be considered.

Hamstringing causes injury that impairs normal movement.

In infertility, the expected reproductive outcome does not occur.

One can compare them in terms of an absent or impaired expected function.

That comparison does not make infertility necessarily a wound or cut in lexical meaning.

Nor does «عاقر» mean someone unable to live, work, or participate in society.

In the reproductive branch, it concerns not producing children.

An infertile woman may have good general health and substantial abilities in work, learning, and other areas.

Infertility should not be generalized into overall human incapacity.

In Salih's account, ʿaqr concerns an attack on an animal; in Zechariah's, ʿāqir describes a bodily condition.

In the first, an agent injures the animal.

In the second, the word identifies no human agent who caused harm.

A shared root does not make the agents, objects, or moral implications of every derivative identical.

10. Comparison with related roots

ʿ Q R and ʿ Q M

ʿ Q M concerns infertility and unfruitfulness in the Quran.

In Al-Shūrā 42:50, «عَقِيمًا» describes someone The God leaves without children.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29, Abraham's wife calls herself «عَجُوزٌ عَقِيمٌ».

«عَقِيم» also describes wind.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41 uses «الرِّيحَ الْعَقِيمَ».

Its range thus extends beyond human childbearing to something that does not produce the expected beneficial fruit or outcome.

Quranic «عاقر», by contrast, describes only Zechariah's wife.

Lexicons treat the two roots as close, sometimes synonymous, in infertility.

One cannot categorically reserve ʿāqir for acquired infertility and ʿaqīm for congenital infertility.

Arabic usage does not establish that absolute division.

The defensible Quranic distinction is distribution: ʿāqir describes Zechariah's wife, while ʿaqīm has a wider range, including wind.

ʿ Q R and N Ḥ R

Naḥr concerns a particular method of killing an animal, especially a camel.

ʿAqr concerns injury and bringing an animal down, especially by harming its legs.

Hamstringing sometimes preceded naḥr, allowing ʿaqr to name the whole act of slaughter.

This overlap does not make the roots interchangeable everywhere.

In Salih's account, ʿaqr preserves the character of the attack and the bringing down of the she-camel.

“Hamstrung” retains that lexical image better than substituting “slaughtered by naḥr.”

ʿ Q R and Dh B Ḥ

Dhabḥ concerns killing by cutting a particular part of the neck.

ʿAqr may lead to death, but here its direct sense is closer to injury that disables an animal's movement.

An animal may be slaughtered without previously being hamstrung.

It may also be hamstrung but remain alive.

Both verbs can feature in killing an animal, but their forms and meanings differ.

ʿ Q R and Q T L

Q T L concerns killing and ending a living being's life.

Animal-related ʿaqr describes an injury and bringing down that can result in killing.

In Salih's account, it belongs to the destruction of the she-camel.

Qatl foregrounds the ending of life, while ʿaqr here foregrounds a particular injurious act.

Translating every verb of death and injury with one word loses part of the Quran's imagery.

ʿ Q R and ʿ J Z

ʿ J Z concerns inability to do something.

Maryam 19:8 describes Zechariah's advanced age using «عِتِيًّا», from ʿ T W, not ʿ J Z. The comparison here is conceptual, not a claim that ʿ J Z occurs in that verse.

«عاقر», meanwhile, concerns his wife's infertility.

General incapacity and inability to produce children are not identical.

Aging may reduce some capacities, but ʿāqir names neither age nor overall bodily ability.

It should therefore not be translated “old and incapable.”

ʿ Q R and J R Ḥ

Jarḥ concerns wounding and bodily injury.

In one main branch, ʿaqr is a kind of injury.

Its familiar use for camels and horses has a special association with injury to the legs and impaired movement.

In this field, ʿaqr is injury, but not every wound must be called ʿaqr.

A small surface scratch need not be described that way; an injury bringing an animal down is closer to the familiar hamstringing sense.

The distinction depends on usage and context, not an absolute rule covering all Arabic.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

The root has two main Quranic senses that must remain distinct in translation.

In Salih's account, “hamstringing and bringing down” fits.

In Zechariah's account, ʿāqir means “infertile.”

Brief meanings for Forqan word boxes

A short definition can be:

“Wounding and bringing down; in the reproductive branch, infertility and not producing children.”

Each derivative's contextual meaning should be recorded separately.

«عَقَرَ» in Salih's account concerns hamstringing.

«عَاقِر» in Zechariah's account describes infertility.

One should neither translate ʿāqir as “wounded” nor «فَعَقَرُوا» as “they made infertile.”

Conclusion

The Arabic root has several semantic branches.

The first concerns wounding, especially hamstringing camels and horses and disabling movement. It may lead to death, but is not identical in every use to dhabḥ, naḥr, or qatl.

The second describes a woman or man who does not produce children. It does not itself identify a medical cause, voluntary choice, or permanent condition.

Another branch concerns the base or central place, as in «عُقْرُ الدَّارِ» and «عقار». It should not be reduced to injury or infertility without adequate evidence.

The Quran uses the root in two settings.

In Salih's account, Thamud hamstring the she-camel. Al-Qamar 54:29 attributes direct execution to one person, while other verses attribute it to the group. Direct action and collective responsibility are distinguishable.

In Zechariah's account, ʿāqir describes his wife. He mentions her infertility alongside his own age and asks The God for a child. The Quran reports the gift but does not detail its bodily mechanism.

No single narrow meaning should be imposed on every derivative. The Quranic branches of hamstringing and infertility must be kept distinct.

“Hamstrung” for Salih's account and “infertile” for Zechariah's wife are clear equivalents. They preserve each usage without replacing its direct meaning with an event's outcome or a hypothetical history of the root.
