The Qur’anic Root AYN D W (ʿ D W)

Swift movement, passing beyond a limit, crossing a boundary, transgression, and entering a position of enmity.

Scope of This Entry

The root ع د و (ʿ d w) originally points to swift movement, passing beyond a limit, crossing a boundary, transgressing, and taking a position of enmity. The meaning of enmity in this root does not begin as a simple feeling; it begins with crossing the boundary of a healthy relationship. An عَدُوّ (ʿaduww) is someone who has passed beyond the safe and proper limit and has entered a position of harm, attack, or hostility.

Important Forms from This Root

Important Qur’anic forms from this root include عَدُوّ (ʿaduww), أَعْدَاء (aʿdāʾ), عَدَاوَة (ʿadāwah), عُدْوَان (ʿudwān), اعْتَدَى (iʿtadā), يَتَعَدَّى (yataʿaddā), تَعْتَدُوا (taʿtadū), مُعْتَدِين (muʿtadīn), عُدْوَة (ʿudwah), and عَادِيَات (ʿādiyāt). عَدُوّ (ʿaduww) refers to an enemy or a clear enemy. عَدَاوَة (ʿadāwah) is the state of enmity and hostility. عُدْوَان (ʿudwān) is transgression and overreaching beyond the limit. اعْتَدَى (iʿtadā) and يَتَعَدَّى (yataʿaddā) point to crossing a boundary and committing transgression. عُدْوَة (ʿudwah) refers to an edge, side, or bank of land. عَادِيَات (ʿādiyāt) is related to those that rush or charge forward.

Central Meaning

The linguistic core of ع د و (ʿ d w) is movement beyond a limit. Sometimes this movement is bodily and physical, such as running or charging. Sometimes it is geographical, such as being on an edge or side. Sometimes it is legal and ethical, meaning that someone crosses the limit of right and justice. Sometimes it is relational, meaning that a relationship leaves safety and soundness and becomes enmity.

Material Image of the Root

The material image of this root is an agent moving swiftly from his place and crossing a boundary; or a horse charging forward; or someone passing from an edge and entering another person’s protected space. The same image works in ethics and law: transgression means moving from a permitted place into a territory that is not one’s right.

Classical Arabic Note

In classical Arabic usage, ع د و (ʿ d w) is connected with running, charging, crossing, transgressing, and enmity. This connection matters, because an enemy in this root is not merely someone who carries resentment in the heart; rather, it is someone who has taken the relationship beyond the limit of soundness and has moved toward aggression or harm. In the final version, if a reliable witness from pre-Islamic poetry or old Arabic usage is found, it can be added.

Transgressing the Limits of The God

In the Qur’an, one of the important uses of the root is crossing the limits of The God:

تِلْكَ حُدُودُ اللَّهِ فَلَا تَعْتَدُوهَا

(tilka ḥudūdu Allāhi fa-lā taʿtadūhā)

These are the limits of The God, so do not transgress them. Al-Baqarah 2:229

In this ayah, transgression means crossing boundaries that The God has placed. A limit is a line of discernment; transgression means stepping beyond that line. This use is not personal enmity, but crossing the boundary of right.

The same meaning appears in another ayah:

وَمَن يَتَعَدَّ حُدُودَ اللَّهِ فَقَدْ ظَلَمَ نَفْسَهُ

(wa-man yataʿadda ḥudūda Allāhi fa-qad ẓalama nafsah)

And whoever transgresses the limits of The God has wronged himself. At-Talaq 65:1

Here, transgression is tied to wronging the self. The one who crosses a divine limit does not merely break an external law; he also moves himself out of his proper place.

A subtle distinction should also be kept between يَتَعَدَّى (yataʿaddā) and اعْتَدَى (iʿtadā). In the context of limits, يَتَعَدَّى (yataʿaddā) more often shows a conscious boundary-crossing from a ruling or appointed measure, as though the actor steps beyond the line. In many contexts, اعْتَدَى (iʿtadā) carries a more active color of violation, aggression, or response to aggression. This distinction is not absolute or mechanical, but it is worth noticing in Qur’anic usage.

Transgression in War and Defense

The second field is عُدْوَان (ʿudwān) and transgression in war and defense:

وَقَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُعْتَدِينَ

(wa-qātilū fī sabīli Allāhi alladhīna yuqātilūnakum wa-lā taʿtadū inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu al-muʿtadīn)

And fight in the path of The God those who fight you, and do not transgress; indeed, The God does not love the transgressors. Al-Baqarah 2:190

This ayah is very important for understanding the ethics of war in the Qur’an. Even against those who fight, transgression is forbidden. Fighting in the path of The God is not a license to be released from limits. لَا تَعْتَدُوا (lā taʿtadū) means that the enmity of the other side must not pull you into transgressing the boundary of justice.

In another ayah, a proportionate response to aggression appears:

فَمَنِ اعْتَدَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ فَاعْتَدُوا عَلَيْهِ بِمِثْلِ مَا اعْتَدَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ

(fa-mani iʿtadā ʿalaykum fa-iʿtadū ʿalayhi bi-mithli mā iʿtadā ʿalaykum)

So whoever transgresses against you, respond against him in the like of what he transgressed against you. Al-Baqarah 2:194

In this ayah, the word فَاعْتَدُوا (fa-iʿtadū) appears as a response to transgression, but it is bound and limited by بِمِثْلِ (bi-mithl), “in the like of.” Therefore, the meaning is not that the Qur’an releases transgression without restraint; rather, the response to transgression must have measure, boundary, and proportion. Anger must not exceed the original violation.

Hatred and the Prohibition of Transgression

The third field is the prohibition of transgression because of hatred:

وَلَا يَجْرِمَنَّكُمْ شَنَآنُ قَوْمٍ أَن صَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ أَن تَعْتَدُوا

(wa-lā yajrimannakum shanaʾānu qawmin an ṣaddūkum ʿani al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi an taʿtadū)

And let not the hatred of a people, because they barred you from the Sacred Mosque, drive you to transgress. Al-Ma’idah 5:2

This ayah shows that the enmity of the other side must not remove a human being from justice. Even if a people previously blocked and harmed, resentment must not become an excuse for transgression. Here, the root ع د و (ʿ d w) marks the boundary between justice and blind revenge.

Satan as Enemy

The fourth field is the enmity of Satan:

وَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا خُطُوَاتِ الشَّيْطَانِ إِنَّهُ لَكُمْ عَدُوٌّ مُّبِينٌ

(wa-lā tattabiʿū khuṭuwāti al-shayṭān innahu lakum ʿaduwwun mubīn)

And do not follow the footsteps of Satan; indeed, he is a clear enemy to you. Al-Baqarah 2:168

Satan is an عَدُوّ (ʿaduww) because he takes the human being out of the path of truth. His enmity is not only hatred; his work is to draw the human being into steps that cross the limit of The God. This point clarifies the link between ع د و (ʿ d w) and SH Ṭ N: Satan is an enemy because he pulls the human being toward transgression from the path of truth.

In Surah Fatir, it says:

إِنَّ الشَّيْطَانَ لَكُمْ عَدُوٌّ فَاتَّخِذُوهُ عَدُوًّا

(inna al-shayṭāna lakum ʿaduwwun fa-ittakhidhūhu ʿaduwwā)

Indeed, Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy. Fatir 35:6

This means that the human being should not take Satan as an adviser, protector, or inner guide. An enemy must be recognized as an enemy, not treated as a friend.

Social Enmity

The fifth field is social enmity:

إِنَّمَا يُرِيدُ الشَّيْطَانُ أَن يُوقِعَ بَيْنَكُمُ الْعَدَاوَةَ وَالْبَغْضَاءَ

(innamā yurīdu al-shayṭānu an yūqiʿa baynakumu al-ʿadāwata wa-al-baghḍāʾ)

Satan only wants to cast enmity and hatred among you. Al-Ma’idah 5:91

عَدَاوَة (ʿadāwah) in this ayah is a relational state: distance, hostility, and resentment are created among people. Through things such as intoxicants and gambling, Satan takes human relationships out of soundness and draws them into enmity and hatred.

Against this state, the Qur’an shows the path by which enmity can be transformed:

ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ فَإِذَا الَّذِي بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُ عَدَاوَةٌ كَأَنَّهُ وَلِيٌّ حَمِيمٌ

(idfaʿ bi-allatī hiya aḥsan fa-idhā alladhī baynaka wa-baynahu ʿadāwatun ka-annahu waliyyun ḥamīm)

Repel with what is better; then suddenly the one between you and whom there was enmity becomes as though he were a close and warm friend. Fussilat 41:34

This ayah shows that enmity is not always a final and sealed condition. Better conduct can return a relationship that has left soundness back toward closeness and warm friendship.

ʿUdwa as Edge or Side

The sixth field is عُدْوَة (ʿudwah), meaning an edge or side:

إِذْ أَنتُم بِالْعُدْوَةِ الدُّنْيَا وَهُم بِالْعُدْوَةِ الْقُصْوَىٰ

(idh antum bi-al-ʿudwati al-dunyā wa-hum bi-al-ʿudwati al-quṣwā)

When you were on the nearer edge and they were on the farther edge. Al-Anfal 8:42

Here عُدْوَة (ʿudwah) does not mean enmity; it refers to the edge, side, or bank of the place of settlement. This use shows that the root ع د و (ʿ d w) is also connected with boundary, edge, and spatial direction. Enmity can be understood from this same direction: two sides stand in opposing positions, and distance and confrontation arise between them.

ʿĀdiyāt and Swift Movement

The seventh field is عَادِيَات (ʿādiyāt):

وَالْعَادِيَاتِ ضَبْحًا

(wa-al-ʿādiyāti ḍabḥā)

By those that charge, panting. Al-Adiyat 100:1

In this ayah, the kinetic field of the root is clear: those that charge, with panting and speed. This use helps us understand that ع د و (ʿ d w) is not only a legal boundary; at its core it also has swift, advancing, boundary-crossing movement. When this movement enters the right of The God or the right of other people, it becomes transgression and aggression.

Distinguishing the Uses

The uses of ع د و (ʿ d w) must be distinguished. Transgressing the limits of The God means crossing a divine boundary. عُدْوَان (ʿudwān) means active and harmful aggression. عَدُوّ (ʿaduww) means an enemy who stands in a position of hostility or aggression. عَدَاوَة (ʿadāwah) is the state of enmity between persons or groups. عُدْوَة (ʿudwah) is an edge or side. عَادِيَات (ʿādiyāt) shows swift, forward movement. All of these are close to the field of movement, boundary, and crossing the limit, but they take meaning in different contexts.

Structural Note

Structurally, the preposition and object are important in this root. لَا تَعْتَدُوهَا (lā taʿtadūhā) means “do not cross them,” meaning the limits. اعْتَدَى عَلَيْكُمْ (iʿtadā ʿalaykum) means “he transgressed against you.” بِمِثْلِ مَا اعْتَدَى عَلَيْكُمْ (bi-mithli mā iʿtadā ʿalaykum) restricts the response to the measure of that same transgression. عَدُوّ لَكُمْ (ʿaduwwun lakum) means an enemy in relation to you. بَيْنَكُمُ الْعَدَاوَةَ (baynakumu al-ʿadāwah) shows that enmity is a state that occurs between people, not only inside one person.

Difference from Nearby Roots

ع د و (ʿ d w) differs from several nearby roots. Ẓ L M points to darkening a right, diminishing a right, and placing a thing somewhere other than its proper place; transgression can be one form of wronging, but ع د و (ʿ d w) highlights crossing a limit more strongly. B GH Y is related to wrongful seeking, rebellion, and wanting beyond one’s right; ع د و (ʿ d w) shows the act of violation and crossing the boundary itself. Ṭ GH Y points to overflowing beyond the limit with a color of rebellion and power-seeking; ع د و (ʿ d w) may occur even in one specific act. KH Ṣ M is related to dispute and adversarial argument between sides, while enmity is a broader state of hostility and standing in a position of harm. Q T L points to killing and ending life; enmity can lead to killing, but it is not itself killing. F S D points to the corruption of order; aggression is one of the ways corruption is produced.

Forqan Translation Suggestions

For translation in Forqan, ع د و (ʿ d w) must be translated according to context. عَدُوّ (ʿaduww) is usually “enemy,” but the explanation should remember that an enemy in this root is someone who has crossed the boundary of a healthy relationship. عَدَاوَة (ʿadāwah) is “enmity” or “a state of hostility.” عُدْوَان (ʿudwān) is “aggression,” “transgression,” or “overreaching beyond the limit.” اعْتَدَى (iʿtadā) and يَتَعَدَّى (yataʿaddā) can be translated as “transgressed,” “passed beyond the limit,” or “crossed the boundary.” عُدْوَة (ʿudwah) should not be translated as “enmity”; in the context of Al-Anfal 8:42, “edge,” “side,” or “bank” is better. عَادِيَات (ʿādiyāt) is “those that charge” or “those that rush forward.”

Linguistic Summary

Linguistically, the root ع د و (ʿ d w) points to swift movement, crossing a limit, passing beyond a boundary, transgression, and enmity. Its material image is charging forward and crossing an edge. In the Qur’an, this root extends from transgressing the limits of The God to aggression in war, from the enmity of Satan to social enmity, from the edges of the field of Badr to the charging ones in Surah Al-Adiyat. Its difference from wronging is that wronging focuses on displacing right and darkening the proper place, while ع د و (ʿ d w) focuses on movement beyond the limit and transgression across the boundary.
