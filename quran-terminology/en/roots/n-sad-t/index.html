Becoming silent to listen: from ordinary silence to attentive hearing of speech and the Qur’an

1. Introducing the root and its central meaning

Arabic N Ṣ T concerns becoming silent, refraining from speech, and listening to speech or a sound one intends to hear.

The family prominently connects silence and listening.

Anṣata lahu describes someone becoming silent to hear another person’s speech and attending to it.

This differs from ordinary silence. Someone may be silent through sleep, fear, having nothing to say, or indifference. In inṣāt, silence ordinarily relates to attending to something to be heard.

Conversely, inṣāt cannot mean merely receiving sound. Sound may reach a person’s ears while they are speaking or attending to something else.

In the established use of inṣāt, a person sets aside their own speech to make room for hearing another’s.

This does not mean that every instance necessarily leads to complete understanding or acceptance. A person can listen attentively and then judge whether the speech is true or false.

Four matters should therefore remain distinct: being silent, hearing sound, listening attentively, and accepting what is said.

They can coexist, but are not identical.

Silence accompanied by listening is the established central meaning of Qur’anic N Ṣ T. Understanding, reflection, and acceptance can be desired outcomes, but are not all independent components of the root’s meaning.

Qur’anic forms

N Ṣ T occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in two verses:

  • Wa-anṣitū — Al-Aʿrāf 7:204.
  • Anṣitū — Al-Aḥqāf 46:29.

Both are masculine plural imperatives of Form IV:

أَنْصَتَ — يُنْصِتُ — إِنْصَاتًا

anṣata — yunṣitu — inṣātan

“He became silent to listen; he becomes silent to listen; silence accompanied by listening.”

In Al-Aʿrāf, the verb follows the conjunction wa and comes after a command to listen.

In Al-Aḥqāf, the same verb is spoken by a group of jinn calling one another to silence and listening.

No other form such as naṣata, yanṣitu, naṣt, inṣāt, intaṣata, or mustanṣit occurs in the Qur’an.

The two Qur’anic occurrences both concern being silent to listen.

2. Early Arabic uses and semantic branches

Naṣata and anṣata: becoming silent to listen

A lexical explanation gives this central meaning:

النُّونُ وَالصَّادُ وَالتَّاءُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ تَدُلُّ عَلَى السُّكُوتِ

al-nūnu wa-al-ṣādu wa-al-tāʾu kalimatun wāḥidatun tadullu ʿalā al-sukūti

Translation:

“This root denotes silence.”

The explanation then connects silence with attending to speech.

It illuminates an important part of the family, but the established use anṣata li-al-ḥadīth connects silence with hearing.

An early explanation of the family’s forms states:

نَصَتَ الرَّجُلُ يَنْصِتُ نَصْتًا، وَأَنْصَتَ، وَانْتَصَتَ: سَكَتَ

naṣata al-rajulu yanṣitu naṣtan wa-anṣata wa-intaṣata: sakata

Meaning:

“The person became silent; naṣata, anṣata, and intaṣata are used for becoming silent.”

The explanation of inṣāt continues:

الْإِنْصَاتُ هُوَ السُّكُوتُ وَالِاسْتِمَاعُ لِلْحَدِيثِ

al-inṣātu huwa al-sukūtu wa-al-istimāʿu li-al-ḥadīthi

Translation:

“Inṣāt is silence and attentive listening to speech.”

This fits the Qur’anic uses closely.

It does not make silence and listening identical in every Arabic sentence.

Silence can occur without any object of hearing. In its central use, inṣāt accompanies attention to speech or sound to be heard.

A poetic example from al-Ṭirimmāḥ

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ ibn Ḥakīm, an Umayyad poet, says:

يُخَافِتْنَ بَعْضَ الْمَضْغِ مِنْ خَشْيَةِ الرَّدَى
وَيُنْصِتْنَ لِلسَّمْعِ انْتِصَاتَ الْقَنَاقِنِ

yukhāfitna baʿḍa al-maḍghi min khashyati al-radā / wa-yunṣitna li-al-samʿi intiṣāta al-qanāqini

A translation close to the sense:

“For fear of death they quiet even their chewing, and fall silent to hear, like experts in locating water.”

The verse is an important lexical example for N Ṣ T.

Yunṣitna li-al-samʿi means becoming silent in order to hear.

The poet connects reducing chewing sounds with attending to surrounding sounds.

Al-qanāqin is explained through expertise in locating underground water or assessing its depth or distance in a well. One explanation concerns listening to judge whether well water is near or far; this does not establish a certain ability to hear hidden flowing water beneath any ground.

The comparison thus emphasizes attentive hearing.

Inṣāt is not merely keeping the mouth closed: silence can provide a means of hearing more precisely.

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ lived after the Qur’an’s revelation. His verse is evidence for classical Arabic, rather than direct pre-Islamic poetry.

Nor does this example make every instance of inṣāt involve fear or danger. Fear belongs to the scene described, rather than the root’s meaning.

Anṣata lahu: listening to another person

Arabic can say:

أَنْصَتَ لَهُ

anṣata lahu

Meaning:

“He became silent to hear him.”

A construction without the preposition is also recorded:

أَنْصَتَهُ

anṣatahu

In some applications, this means listening to him.

The syntactic variation matters: the absence of li does not invariably change the verb’s meaning.

Some Arabic uses relate the verb to the person heard either directly or through a preposition.

The familiar Ḥadhām example

Arabic poetry and proverbs transmit:

إِذَا قَالَتْ حَذَامِ فَأَنْصِتُوهَا
فَإِنَّ الْقَوْلَ مَا قَالَتْ حَذَامِ

idhā qālat ḥadhāmi fa-anṣitūhā / fa-inna al-qawla mā qālat ḥadhāmi

Translation:

“When Ḥadhām speaks, listen to her in silence, for what Ḥadhām says is the word to heed.”

Fa-anṣitūhā here has no preposition.

It means listening to Ḥadhām’s speech.

Several qualifications concerning the wording and attribution should remain clear.

Another familiar version has fa-ṣaddiqūhā instead of fa-anṣitūhā: believe what she says.

The verse has also been attributed to different poets, including Lujaym ibn Ṣaʿb and a person from the family of Ṭāriq.

The version with fa-anṣitūhā therefore cannot be presented without qualification as the sole certain form of pre-Qur’anic poetry.

Its value here is the attested use of anṣata for listening.

The difference between the versions also matters semantically:

Fa-anṣitūhā concerns listening.

Fa-ṣaddiqūhā concerns accepting speech as true.

These are different actions.

Someone may listen and, after examination, judge the speech false.

Affirming its truth should therefore not become part of the fixed meaning of inṣāt.

Anṣatahu: silencing someone else

Arabic anṣata is not used only intransitively.

In some applications it can be transitive, meaning to silence another person.

Lexical explanations distinguish two constructions:

أَنْصَتُّ لَهُ

anṣattu lahu

Meaning:

“I became silent to hear him.”

And:

أَنْصَتُّهُ

anṣattuhu

Which can mean “I silenced him.”

Early explanations record and distinguish both listening and silencing another person.

A verse with uncertain attribution reads:

أَبُوكَ الَّذِي أَجْدَى عَلَيَّ بِنَصْرِهِ
فَأَنْصَتَ عَنِّي بَعْدَهُ كُلَّ قَائِلِ

abūka alladhī ajdā ʿalayya bi-naṣrihi / fa-anṣata ʿannī baʿdahu kulla qāʾili

A translation close to the sense:

“Your father was the one who benefited me with his support, and through that support he silenced everyone speaking against me.”

Anṣata here means silencing another person, with kulla as its accusative object. Simply translating “everyone fell silent” would obscure the transitive construction; the verse is not about their attending to particular speech either.

The family thus has a transitive application as well.

In the two Qur’anic verses, however, anṣitū commands the addressees themselves to become silent and listen.

“Silence other people” is therefore unsuitable for those Qur’anic forms.

Intaṣata and istanṣata: two further forms

Intaṣata is recorded for becoming silent and attending to hearing.

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ’s verse used the verbal noun intiṣāt in that sense.

Istanṣatahu also means asking someone to become silent and listen.

The morphological distinction is intelligible:

Inṣāt describes becoming silent to listen oneself.

Istinṣāt requests that behavior from someone else.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an; their existence does not justify adding a new meaning or grammatical construction to anṣitū.

A less common branch: inclining toward something

A further, uncommon use is recorded in early Arabic:

أَنْصَتَ لِلَّهْوِ

anṣata li-al-lahwi

With the meaning of inclining or turning toward amusement.

It is lexically attested but has no Qur’anic occurrence.

It does not make the two Qur’anic instances mean inclining toward amusement.

Both verses concern hearing the Qur’an; silence with listening has clearer, direct contextual support.

3. Al-Aʿrāf 7:204: listening and silence during Qur’anic recitation

وَإِذَا قُرِئَ الْقُرْآنُ فَاسْتَمِعُوا لَهُ وَأَنْصِتُوا لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ

wa-idhā quriʾa al-qurʾānu fa-istamiʿū lahu wa-anṣitū laʿallakum turḥamūna

Translation:

“When the Qur’an is recited, listen to it and remain silent, so that you may receive mercy.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:204

This is the root’s first Qur’anic occurrence in canonical order.

Two commands stand alongside each other:

فَاسْتَمِعُوا لَهُ

fa-istamiʿū lahu

“Then listen to it.”

And:

وَأَنْصِتُوا

wa-anṣitū

“And remain silent.”

Their distinction is essential to understanding the root.

A. Fa-istamiʿū lahu: listen to it

Istamiʿū belongs to S M ʿ and Form VIII.

Its familiar use concerns hearing with attention and an intention to listen.

This differs from involuntary hearing or sound merely reaching the ears.

Someone may hear Qur’anic recitation while their mind and attention are elsewhere.

Sound reaching the ears alone does not fulfill the whole sense of listening attentively.

“Listen” is therefore an appropriate rendering of istamiʿū.

Listening still does not necessarily mean complete understanding.

Understanding content depends on linguistic knowledge, attention, cognitive capacity, and other conditions.

B. Wa-anṣitū: become silent to listen

After the listening command comes:

وَأَنْصِتُوا

wa-anṣitū

Lexically, inṣāt concerns becoming silent while attending to hearing.

The two commands occur in one situation, each foregrounding an aspect of the addressee’s conduct.

Istimāʿ emphasizes attending to the recited speech.

Inṣāt emphasizes becoming silent and setting aside speech or sound that obstructs hearing.

The commands’ juxtaposition therefore need not be pointless repetition.

Someone may be silent without listening.

Someone may also wish to listen but obstruct their own or others’ attention through simultaneous speech.

The verse presents both aspects through its two commands.

The distinction should not become a mechanical rule that attentive listening is always possible without silence, or that inṣāt always takes place without attention.

In actual usage, the behaviors can overlap.

C. The role of idhā quriʾa al-qurʾānu

The verse opens with a conditional or temporal construction:

وَإِذَا قُرِئَ الْقُرْآنُ

wa-idhā quriʾa al-qurʾānu

“When the Qur’an is recited.”

Quriʾa is a passive perfect verb from Q R ʾ.

The subject is therefore the Qur’an being recited.

The phrase itself specifies no particular place or form of recitation.

It does not state that the conduct occurs only at home, in a mosque, or at a particular gathering.

That general wording should not yield unlimited legal detail without examination—for instance, that everyone hearing distant Qur’anic recitation in any situation must stop every activity.

The command’s general meaning differs from determining its precise practical scope.

The bounds of obligation, hearing conditions, the addressee’s situation, and other duties require independent argument.

D. Does the verse concern only prayer?

Exegetical tradition transmits differing views about the command’s setting.

Some relate it to listening during recitation in prayer.

Others also discuss sermons or gatherings at which the Qur’an was recited.

Some interpreters consider the command general in principle, then discuss conditions for applying it.

One early exegetical reading favors applying it during prayer and the sermon.

Two matters should remain distinct.

First, anṣitū itself means becoming silent to listen.

Second, identifying the situations in which the command entails a particular legal obligation.

Prayer and sermon interpretations concern the command’s referent or practical scope, rather than different meanings of N Ṣ T.

A historical report about prayer recitation alone also does not conclusively establish that the wording’s meaning is confined to that setting.

Conversely, the general wording cannot yield every practical detail without examination.

The root study should retain the clear lexical meaning and leave the command’s limits to an independent study of the verse.

E. Does inṣāt mean acceptance without examination?

No.

The verse commands listening and silence; it does not say that every interpretation or explanation one hears about the Qur’an must be accepted without examination.

Its direct subject is Qur’anic recitation, rather than necessarily every person claiming to interpret or represent the Qur’an.

Al-Zumar 39:18 includes:

الَّذِينَ يَسْتَمِعُونَ الْقَوْلَ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ أَحْسَنَهُ

alladhīna yastamiʿūna al-qawla fa-yattabiʿūna aḥsanahu

Translation:

“Those who listen to speech and follow the best of it.”

Listening and following what is best are distinct stages here.

Hearing and assessing need not be opposed.

Inṣāt in Al-Aʿrāf 7:204 provides an opportunity to hear. Understanding, reflection, and response should be examined through the Qur’an’s wider evidence.

F. Laʿallakum turḥamūna: hope of mercy

The verse ends:

لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ

laʿallakum turḥamūna

“So that you may receive mercy.”

This expresses the command’s purpose and desired result.

Mercy is not the lexical meaning of inṣāt.

The construction does not establish that silence alone, without hearing or attention, automatically brings about every desired effect of the Qur’an.

An exegetical explanation relates receiving mercy to hearing with understanding, taking admonition, and acting on what the Qur’an explains.

This fits the relationship between listening, silence, and mercy, but understanding and action are not independent meanings of anṣitū.

4. Al-Aḥqāf 46:29: the jinn becoming silent to hear the Qur’an

وَإِذْ صَرَفْنَا إِلَيْكَ نَفَرًا مِنَ الْجِنِّ يَسْتَمِعُونَ الْقُرْآنَ فَلَمَّا حَضَرُوهُ قَالُوا أَنْصِتُوا فَلَمَّا قُضِيَ وَلَّوْا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِمْ مُنْذِرِينَ

wa-idh ṣarafnā ilayka nafaran mina al-jinni yastamiʿūna al-qurʾāna fa-lammā ḥaḍarūhu qālū anṣitū fa-lammā quḍiya wallaw ilā qawmihim mundhirīna

Translation:

“When We directed toward you a group of jinn listening to the Qur’an, then when they attended it they said, ‘Be silent and listen.’ When its recitation ended, they returned to their people as warners.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:29

This is the second and final Qur’anic occurrence of N Ṣ T in canonical order.

Unlike Al-Aʿrāf 7:204, this verse quotes anṣitū as speech by a group of jinn.

A. Who gives the command?

The opening describes jinn coming toward the Prophet to hear the Qur’an.

It then says:

فَلَمَّا حَضَرُوهُ قَالُوا أَنْصِتُوا

fa-lammā ḥaḍarūhu qālū anṣitū

Meaning:

“When they attended it, they said: be silent.”

The construction indicates that some group members called the others to inṣāt.

Anṣitū is therefore quoted speech by the jinn, rather than a command directly addressed in that construction to every reader of the Qur’an.

Their behavior is nevertheless presented within the context of hearing the Qur’an and responding positively.

B. Why anṣitū after yastamiʿūna?

The verse is particularly important for comparing listening and inṣāt.

The opening says:

يَسْتَمِعُونَ الْقُرْآنَ

yastamiʿūna al-qurʾāna

“They were listening to the Qur’an.”

Then, when they attended it, they said:

أَنْصِتُوا

anṣitū

“Be silent.”

The sequence shows that, despite their related meanings, listening and inṣāt can foreground distinct aspects of conduct.

The group had turned toward hearing the Qur’an; upon attending its recitation, becoming silent also came into view.

Inṣāt may have prevented simultaneous conversation, provided room for hearing, or supported concentration.

The verse does not say that they must previously have been talking loudly or arguing.

Details such as a dispute, a disorderly gathering, or loud noise should therefore not be added without evidence.

C. Fa-lammā quḍiya: the recitation ending

The verse continues:

فَلَمَّا قُضِيَ

fa-lammā quḍiya

Meaning:

“Then, when it was completed.”

Context indicates the completion of Qur’anic recitation in that gathering.

It then says:

وَلَّوْا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِمْ مُنْذِرِينَ

wallaw ilā qawmihim mundhirīna

“They returned to their people as warners.”

The sequence shows inṣāt as behavior appropriate to the time of hearing.

After recitation ended, the group departed and conveyed its message to others.

Inṣāt therefore does not mean permanent silence or abandoning speech in every situation.

Becoming silent to hear can prepare for considered speech at the appropriate time.

That conclusion follows from the group’s whole conduct, rather than the word inṣāt alone.

D. Understanding and conveying the message

The next verse says:

قَالُوا يَا قَوْمَنَا إِنَّا سَمِعْنَا كِتَابًا أُنْزِلَ مِنْ بَعْدِ مُوسَىٰ مُصَدِّقًا لِمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ يَهْدِي إِلَى الْحَقِّ وَإِلَىٰ طَرِيقٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

qālū yā qawmanā innā samiʿnā kitāban unzila min baʿdi mūsā muṣaddiqan li-mā bayna yadayhi yahdī ilā al-ḥaqqi wa-ilā ṭarīqin mustaqīmin

Translation:

“They said: our people, we have heard a book sent down after Moses, confirming what came before it and guiding to truth and a straight path.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:30

Then in Al-Aḥqāf 46:31 they call their people to respond to the divine summons.

Their hearing was therefore more than receiving sound: they discuss the message and convey it to others.

Not all these stages should become parts of inṣāt’s lexical definition.

Inṣāt is conduct during hearing.

Recognizing the message, believing it, and warning others are understood from the continuing narrative.

E. The connection with Al-Jinn

Al-Jinn 72:1 and Al-Jinn 72:2 state:

قُلْ أُوحِيَ إِلَيَّ أَنَّهُ اسْتَمَعَ نَفَرٌ مِنَ الْجِنِّ فَقَالُوا إِنَّا سَمِعْنَا قُرْآنًا عَجَبًا

qul ūḥiya ilayya annahu istamaʿa nafarun mina al-jinni fa-qālū innā samiʿnā qurʾānan ʿajaban

“Say: it has been revealed to me that a group of jinn listened and said, ‘We have heard a wondrous Qur’an.’”

Then:

يَهْدِي إِلَى الرُّشْدِ فَآمَنَّا بِهِ

yahdī ilā al-rushdi fa-āmannā bihi

“It guides to right judgment, so we have believed in it.”

Al-Jinn 72:1 and Al-Jinn 72:2 are thematically close to Al-Aḥqāf 46:29, Al-Aḥqāf 46:30, Al-Aḥqāf 46:31, and Al-Aḥqāf 46:32.

Al-Jinn does not use N Ṣ T, however; it speaks of attentive listening and hearing.

Occurrences of S M ʿ should therefore not be added to the Qur’anic occurrence count for N Ṣ T.

5. Distinguishing related roots

N Ṣ T and S M ʿ: silence and hearing

S M ʿ concerns hearing and receiving sound.

Form VIII istamaʿa often describes listening with attention and an intention to hear.

In its established use, inṣāt foregrounds becoming silent to hear.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:204 includes both.

Listening and inṣāt also occur together in Al-Aḥqāf 46:29.

The Qur’an thus connects attending to speech with becoming silent to hear it.

That distinction does not make attentive hearing invariably involve simultaneous speech, or make inṣāt alone guarantee complete understanding.

Study of S M ʿ

N Ṣ T and Ṣ M T: silence and a listener’s silence

Ṣamata in Arabic concerns being silent and not speaking.

The Qur’an includes:

سَوَاءٌ عَلَيْكُمْ أَدَعَوْتُمُوهُمْ أَمْ أَنْتُمْ صَامِتُونَ

sawāʾun ʿalaykum a-daʿawtumūhum am antum ṣāmitūna

Translation:

“It is the same for you whether you call them or remain silent.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:193, closing excerpt

Ṣāmitūna here means being silent.

Qur’anic inṣāt, however, concerns becoming silent while hearing the Qur’an.

Ṣamt can occur without a speaker or something to hear, whereas inṣāt usually relates to attending to speech or sound.

Their Arabic semantic fields overlap; this should not become an absolute distinction without exceptions.

Study of Ṣ M T

N Ṣ T and S K T: becoming silent

S K T concerns becoming silent and cessation of sound or speech.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:154 includes:

وَلَمَّا سَكَتَ عَنْ مُوسَى الْغَضَبُ أَخَذَ الْأَلْوَاحَ

wa-lammā sakata ʿan mūsā al-ghaḍabu akhadha al-alwāḥa

Translation:

“When Moses’ anger subsided, he took up the tablets.”

Silence here appears in an expression describing anger subsiding.

Inṣāt in the verses under examination concerns becoming silent to hear.

Sakata can thus describe sound ending or a condition becoming calm without necessarily making listening the sentence’s subject.

N Ṣ T and Ṣ GH W: inclining to hear

Ṣ GH W concerns inclining and turning toward something.

Aṣghā ilayhi can mean inclining one’s ear toward someone and listening attentively.

That use is close to listening and inṣāt.

Qur’anic ṣaghw, however, also concerns the heart’s inclination.

Not every iṣghāʾ is inṣāt, and inṣāt need not involve bodily movement of the head or ear.

“Listen” naturally renders istimāʿ, and sometimes inṣāt, but the two verbs should remain distinct in translating Al-Aʿrāf 7:204.

Study of Ṣ GH W

N Ṣ T and L GH W: silence and idle speech

L GH W concerns idle speech and conduct departing from a proper purpose.

Fuṣṣilat 41:26 states:

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَا تَسْمَعُوا لِهَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ وَالْغَوْا فِيهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَغْلِبُونَ

wa-qāla alladhīna kafarū lā tasmaʿū li-hādhā al-qurʾāni wa-alghaw fīhi laʿallakum taghlibūna

Translation:

“Those who disbelieved said: do not listen to this Qur’an; make idle noise and disturbance during it, so that you may prevail.”

Thematically, this contrasts with the conduct in Al-Aʿrāf 7:204.

Al-Aʿrāf calls for listening and silence during recitation.

Fuṣṣilat describes a group urging others not to listen and to disrupt the recitation.

Laghw should not therefore be defined directly as the absence of inṣāt.

Laghw takes different forms; not every act of speaking is laghw.

The contextual contrast is between enabling hearing and trying to obstruct it.

N Ṣ T and D B R: inṣāt and reflection

Form V tadabbur from D B R concerns considering the message, its meaning, and its implications.

Muḥammad 47:24 states:

أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ الْقُرْآنَ أَمْ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبٍ أَقْفَالُهَا

a-fa-lā yatadabbarūna al-qurʾāna am ʿalā qulūbin aqfāluhā

Translation:

“Do they not reflect upon the Qur’an, or do hearts have their locks upon them?”

Tadabbur is not identical with inṣāt.

A person can remain silent during recitation without considering its meaning.

Someone may also reflect and investigate later, after hearing or reading the Qur’an.

Inṣāt can provide an opportunity to hear, but does not itself mean reflection.

This distinction prevents reducing a person’s relationship with the Qur’an to hearing sound.

Study of D B R

6. Questions of understanding and translation for Forqan

Is inṣāt merely outward silence?

Established Arabic inṣāt relates to listening.

Silence with no attention to the speech heard does not convey its full semantic range.

Conversely, inṣāt cannot mean complete understanding, coming to faith, or acting on the message.

These are conceptually distinct stages.

The definition should retain silence and hearing together without adding further meaning.

Does inṣāt mean silencing other people?

Arabic records transitive anṣatahu for making someone else silent.

In both Qur’anic verses, however, anṣitū commands the addressees themselves to become silent and listen.

“Silence other people” is therefore inappropriate for those verses.

The jinn ask one another to become silent through this command, but the verb directs the addressees’ own conduct.

Does inṣāt mean unquestioning acceptance?

No.

Arabic inṣāt concerns listening accompanied by silence.

Judging speech, affirming it as true, and following it are other actions.

The versions fa-anṣitūhā and fa-ṣaddiqūhā in the Ḥadhām verse illustrate that distinction.

Anṣitū should therefore not be translated as “obey without question.”

Nor does listening to the Qur’an mean surrendering understanding and judgment to everyone who recites or interprets it.

Does Al-Aʿrāf 7:204 apply only to one kind of gathering?

The Qur’anic wording itself names no specific place or time.

Exegetical tradition discusses prayer, sermons, and other settings.

Determining a precise legal ruling for all such situations requires a broader study.

The root article should explain that anṣitū means becoming silent to hear across those readings.

Disagreement about the command’s setting and limits is distinct from its root’s lexical meaning.

For Al-Aʿrāf 7:204, the proposed rendering is:

“When the Qur’an is recited, listen to it and remain silent, so that you may receive mercy.”

This preserves the distinction between the verbs.

For Al-Aḥqāf 46:29:

“When they attended it, they said: be silent and listen.”

Since the opening already mentions listening, “be silent” can also convey the command’s meaning in context.

For the lexical explanation, however, “becoming silent to listen” is clearer and fuller.

7. Conclusion

Arabic N Ṣ T concerns silence and becoming silent. In its established inṣāt use, that silence accompanies attending to speech or sound.

Naṣata, anṣata, and intaṣata are recorded for becoming silent; istanṣata concerns requesting silence from another.

Some transitive uses of anṣata also mean silencing someone else.

Poetic examples, especially al-Ṭirimmāḥ’s verse, illuminate silence and attentive hearing. The versions fa-anṣitūhā and fa-ṣaddiqūhā in the Ḥadhām verse also show that listening and affirming truth are distinct actions.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:204, inṣāt accompanies attentive listening. The commands concern attention to the Qur’an and becoming silent during its recitation.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:29, jinn attending Qur’anic recitation call one another to silence. Afterward, they return to their people and convey the message.

In both verses, inṣāt relates to hearing the Qur’an.

The word itself does not mean complete understanding, coming to faith, affirming truth, or acting.

These actions may follow hearing and matter within the Qur’anic context, but remain distinct from inṣāt’s lexical meaning.

Inṣāt also differs from ordinary silence: a silent person need not be listening, while established inṣāt has hearing as its purpose.

Refraining from speech does not entail surrendering judgment or accepting every statement without examination.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N Ṣ T centrally concerns becoming silent and refraining from speech. Anṣata particularly concerns becoming silent to attend to another person’s speech or another sound.

Arabic uses the family for becoming silent, listening, and, in some transitive constructions, silencing someone else.

Qur’anic anṣitū and wa-anṣitū are Form IV imperatives meaning “be silent and listen.”

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:204, the behavior accompanies a command to listen to the Qur’an.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:29, the jinn call one another to the same behavior when hearing the Qur’an.

Thus, silence accompanied by attentive listening is an appropriate shared definition of Qur’anic N Ṣ T. Understanding, reflection, affirmation, and action are related but independent matters, rather than components of the root’s direct meaning.
