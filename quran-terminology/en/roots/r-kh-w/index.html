Softness, slackness, ease, and gentle movement

The Arabic root R KH W denotes softness, slackness, and the absence of hardness or tension. This appears in soft objects, loosened ties and ropes, fluid movement, gentle wind, and living conditions free of constraint and hardship.

Softness in this family does not necessarily mean weakness or slowness. Something can be soft and flexible without ceasing to move. Wind can blow gently yet move effectively. Conversely, slackness and lack of firmness can carry negative meanings in some uses.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as rukhāʾan. In Ṣād it describes the wind placed at Solomon’s disposal by The God, moving at his command.

Qur’anic form: rukhāʾan (Ṣād 38:36).

Other recorded forms include rikhw, rakhw, rakhāwah, rakhiyy, rakhuwa, rakhiya, rakhā, arkhā, rākhā, tarākhā, istarkhā, irkhāʾ, and mirkhāʾ. These do not occur as such in the Qur’an, but help establish the root’s semantic range.

Rukhāʾ, with ḍammah on the initial rāʾ, must be distinguished from rakhāʾ, with fatḥah. The first commonly describes soft, gentle wind; the second generally concerns ease of life and freedom from material hardship. They belong to one semantic family but should not be confused in translation.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Softness and absence of hardness

Rikhw and rakhw describe something soft, slack, or lacking great hardness or firmness.

The root’s core has been explained through līn, softness, with related uses also recorded for weakness of judgment or understanding.

Both rikhw and rakhw are recorded, although their antiquity and preferred usage are not agreed upon.

Rakhiya and rakhuwa can express becoming soft. They may concern an object lacking hardness and tension, or one that has become loose after being tight and firm.

Softness here is a general quality. The word alone does not identify the material, degree of flexibility, resistance, or cause of softening.

A loosened rope differs physically from an inherently soft object, yet both can fall within the semantic field of rakhāwah.

No single material image, such as hanging cloth or a slack rope, should therefore be treated as the certain origin of every use of the root.

Arkhā: loosening and letting down

Arkhā is a clear use within this family.

Arkhā al-ribāṭa means loosening a tie; arkhā al-sitra means letting down or hanging a curtain.

Arkhā lahu al-ḥabla can mean giving more rope or slack and providing room for movement.

Something previously taut, bound, or restrained is released from that tight condition.

Loosening a tie, undoing it entirely, and cutting a rope nevertheless differ.

Arkhā need not mean cutting or removing the restraint; it may merely reduce pressure or tension.

This also matters in social and figurative uses: easing conditions does not always remove every restriction.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness: loosening a knot

A verse attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā states:

وَمُدَفَّعٍ ذَاقَ الْهَوَانَ مُلَعَّنٍ
رَاخَيْتُ عُقْدَةَ حَبْلِهِ فَانْحَلَّتِ

wa-mudaffaʿin dhāqa al-hawāna mulaʿʿanin / rākhaytu ʿuqdata ḥablihi fa-inḥallati

“Many a rejected, humiliated man had I relieved by loosening the knot of his rope until it came undone.”

Rākhaytu belongs to this family and concerns loosening the knot and reducing its tension.

The image expresses the relief or release the poet provided for someone distressed and humiliated.

Some transmissions read kablihi, his fetter, instead of ḥablihi, his rope. The variant preserves the basic image of loosening a bond and relieving distress.

The witness connects physically loosening something with the broader idea of release from pressure in early language.

It does not mean that every R KH W usage denotes freeing a prisoner, ending oppression, or relieving distress. Those meanings emerge from the complete poetic image, rather than the root alone.

Istirkhāʾ: easing of hardship

Istarkhā can mean that something became soft or slack.

Expressions such as istarkhat bihi ḥāluhu are also used for someone whose living conditions improved after difficulty.

Here softness is no longer merely physical: pressure and hardship in life diminish.

Poetry attributed to Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī includes:

فَأَبَّلَ وَاسْتَرْخَى بِهِ الشَّأْنُ بَعْدَمَا
أَسَافَ وَلَوْلَا سَعْيُنَا لَمْ يُؤَبِّلْ

fa-abbala wa-istarkhā bihi al-shaʾnu baʿdamā / asāfa wa-lawlā saʿyunā lam yuʾabbil

“He gained camels and his circumstances eased after his wealth had dwindled; without our effort, he would not have gained camels again.”

The poet describes improved circumstances after poverty and loss of camels, followed by renewed possessions.

Istarkhā bihi al-shaʾnu conveys an easing of difficulty and improvement in his condition.

Lexical transmissions also read al-khaṭbu instead of al-shaʾnu. Both concern the person’s circumstances and troubles here.

This witness shows ease of life expressed in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

It does not establish wealth or economic prosperity as the entire root’s basic meaning.

The broader meaning is reduced pressure and hardship; financial means provide one setting in which it is used.

Rakhāʾ: ease and comfortable living

Al-rakhāʾ denotes ample means and an easy condition of life free of hardship.

ʿAyshun rakhiyyun describes comfortable living; rakhiyyu al-bāli describes a person at ease in mind and circumstances.

Absence of pressure and hardship here extends to livelihood and living conditions.

Rakhāʾ does not necessarily mean unlimited possessions or great wealth. Someone can be relatively comfortable without owning much.

Rakhiyyu al-bāli concerns peace of mind and should not always be restricted to financial comfort.

Softness and reduced pressure are not invariably positive, however.

Tarākhā ʿani al-amri can express slackness, delay, or neglect in carrying out a task.

Reduced firmness and tension can then become insufficient effort or negligence.

R KH W therefore has no fixed moral value. Softness and ease can be desirable, while slackness and instability can be undesirable in other circumstances.

Irkhāʾ in a horse’s movement

Irkhāʾ also has an important use describing a horse’s movement.

Farasun mirkhāʾ describes a horse moving or running fluidly and freely.

Irkhāʾ is also explained as a kind of running in which the horse has scope to move and continues without being driven to exhaustion.

Lexical accounts differ over its precise speed. Some describe it as faster than ordinary movement, others as slower or gentler than other kinds of running.

These differences do not establish a fixed speed for every use of irkhāʾ.

Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī uses the following expression for a horse running fluidly and swiftly:

فَهِيَ رِخْوٌ تَمْزَعُ

fa-hiya rikhwun tamzaʿu

“She runs softly and fluidly, swiftly onward.”

Some words in the complete verse vary in transmission, but fa-hiya rikhwun tamzaʿu is recorded to explain this usage.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods; the precise composition date of this verse is not independently established here. It witnesses early Arabic but should not be called certainly pre-Qur’anic without firm evidence.

This matters for Qur’anic rukhāʾ: softness and fluidity of movement do not necessarily mean slowness or immobility.

2. Rukhāʾ: soft and gentle wind in Arabic

Rīḥun rukhāʾun describes wind blowing softly and gently.

One early explanation describes it as soft yet swift, without uprooting or disturbing things like a destructive wind.

Rukhāʾ with initial ḍammah is also directly explained as soft wind.

These explanations concern the manner and effect of its blowing.

A rukhāʾ wind need not be motionless or extremely slow. If it did not move at all, blowing and flowing would no longer be at issue.

Softness accompanies movement in this usage.

Nor does rukhāʾ mean the absence of all force. Gentle wind can move, affect objects, and provide motive force under suitable conditions.

Three qualities should therefore be distinguished:

First, softness and gentleness of blowing, close to rukhāʾ’s main meaning.

Second, speed of movement, which may vary; the word alone supplies no fixed value.

Third, destructive or disruptive effect, dependent on the intensity and conditions of blowing, and not identical with every movement of wind.

Qur’anic rukhāʾ describes wind and should be understood within that field.

3. Tajrī bi-amrihi rukhāʾan: the Qur’anic occurrence

The root’s only Qur’anic occurrence is in Ṣād 38:36:

فَسَخَّرْنَا لَهُ الرِّيحَ تَجْرِي بِأَمْرِهِ رُخَاءً حَيْثُ أَصَابَ

fa-sakhkharnā lahu al-rīḥa tajrī bi-amrihi rukhāʾan ḥaythu aṣāba

“We subjected the wind to him, blowing gently at his command wherever he intended.”

The verse concerns Solomon and follows his request to The God in Ṣād 38:35:

قَالَ رَبِّ اغْفِرْ لِي وَهَبْ لِي مُلْكًا لَا يَنْبَغِي لِأَحَدٍ مِنْ بَعْدِي إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْوَهَّابُ

qāla rabbi ighfir lī wa-hab lī mulkan lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī innaka anta al-wahhābu

“He said: My Lord, forgive me and grant me a kingdom fitting for no one after me. You are the Ever-Giving.”

The Qur’an then speaks of the wind being subjected to him.

Its movement is thus part of the blessing The God bestowed on Solomon.

Understanding rukhāʾan precisely requires identifying each word’s contribution.

Fa-sakhkharnā lahu al-rīḥa

Sakhkharnā comes from S KH R and here means subjecting the wind and placing it at someone’s disposal.

Al-rīḥa is the verb’s object: wind is what The God subjected to Solomon.

This quality comes from sakhkharnā, rather than rukhāʾ.

Rukhāʾ does not itself mean obedient or under human control. Wind may blow softly without being under anyone’s command.

The wind’s subjection and gentleness are related but distinct qualities.

Tajrī bi-amrihi

Tajrī is an imperfect verb from J R Y, conveying the wind’s movement or flow here.

Bi-amrihi connects this movement with Solomon’s command.

The wind thus moves, and its movement depends on his command.

Rukhāʾ is neither movement itself nor the act of commanding.

It describes the manner of the wind’s movement.

Tajrī and rukhāʾan should not be turned into two wholly separate movements. The verse describes one flowing wind characterized by softness and gentleness.

Rukhāʾan: the wind’s condition

Grammatically, rukhāʾan is accusative and functions as a circumstantial expression concerning al-rīḥa.

It expresses the wind’s condition while flowing.

Rukhāʾ here means soft, gentle wind or blowing with softness.

The verse concerns wind exhibiting this quality while in motion.

This rules out two mistaken readings.

The first treats rukhāʾ as complete stillness, which does not fit tajrī.

The second makes it mean extreme speed or destructive power, neither of which belongs to its independent meaning.

Softness and gentleness come from the word itself; that softness can accompany effective and even swift movement.

Rukhāʾ here has initial ḍammah. It should not be translated directly as economic prosperity or Solomon’s comfortable life.

Although al-rakhāʾ, ease of life, belongs to the same family, the present subject is wind and how it blows.

Ḥaythu aṣāba: wherever he intended

Ṣād 38:36 ends:

حَيْثُ أَصَابَ

ḥaythu aṣāba

“Wherever he intended.”

Ḥaythu concerns place and direction; aṣāba here means intending or heading toward a destination.

This usage is also established in lexical and exegetical explanations.

Ḥaythu is grammatically an adverb of place, connected with the wind’s movement.

The wind moves at Solomon’s command toward whichever direction he intends.

Aṣāba here should not become “he always made the right decision” or “he never made a mistake.”

The phrase does not establish the correctness of his decisions; it concerns the intended direction and destination.

Determining the destination is not rukhāʾ’s meaning either. The destination comes from ḥaythu aṣāba, compliance with command from bi-amrihi, and gentleness from rukhāʾan.

Each part contributes its own meaning.

4. Rukhāʾ and ʿāṣifah: two descriptions of Solomon’s blessing

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:81 speaks of the same Solomon:

وَلِسُلَيْمَانَ الرِّيحَ عَاصِفَةً تَجْرِي بِأَمْرِهِ إِلَى الْأَرْضِ الَّتِي بَارَكْنَا فِيهَا وَكُنَّا بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَالِمِينَ

wa-li-sulaymāna al-rīḥa ʿāṣifatan tajrī bi-amrihi ilā al-arḍi allatī bāraknā fīhā wa-kunnā bi-kulli shayʾin ʿālimīna

“For Solomon We subjected the fiercely blowing wind, moving at his command toward the land We had blessed. We knew all things.”

The wind is described here as ʿāṣifatan.

ʿĀṣifah comes from ʿ Ṣ F and concerns fierce, intense blowing.

Ṣād 38:36 describes the wind subjected to Solomon as rukhāʾan.

At first the descriptions may seem opposed: fierce and intense wind, versus soft and gentle wind.

Their relationship requires examining the complete verses.

Are ʿāṣifah and rukhāʾ contradictory?

ʿĀṣifah highlights intense blowing; rukhāʾ concerns gentleness and softness.

Their lexical meanings differ, and that difference should be preserved.

Both verses nevertheless say that the wind moved at Solomon’s command.

Describing different conditions of the subjected wind is therefore consistent with the text.

It may have moved intensely in one situation and gently in another. This explanation fits both the different descriptions and its compliance with command.

Lexical accounts also show that soft blowing need not be slow. Speed should therefore not automatically be equated with violence or disruption in analyzing these verses.

The verses do not conclusively establish that Solomon made the wind fierce or gentle at particular times for particular purposes.

Accounts in which fierce wind lifted his throne or belongings and gentle wind carried them along a particular route contain details absent from these two verses.

Such explanations may be transmitted in commentaries, but should not be presented as the lexical meaning of rukhāʾ or an explicit Qur’anic report.

These verses describe wind subjected to Solomon, moving at his command, and characterized by two different descriptions. Exactly when or under what circumstances they apply is a separate question.

Does rukhāʾ wind always blow slowly?

No.

Early sources sometimes describe it as soft yet swift, without disturbing things like a destructive wind.

A particular speed is therefore not its defining criterion.

“Slow wind” may restrict the meaning unnecessarily.

“Gentle wind,” “softly blowing wind,” or “wind that blew gently” are more suitable.

They preserve softness without excluding movement or speed.

Ghuduwwuhā shahrun wa-rawāḥuhā shahrun

Part of Sabaʾ 34:12 also states:

وَلِسُلَيْمَانَ الرِّيحَ غُدُوُّهَا شَهْرٌ وَرَوَاحُهَا شَهْرٌ

wa-li-sulaymāna al-rīḥa ghuduwwuhā shahrun wa-rawāḥuhā shahrun

“For Solomon We subjected the wind: its morning course was a month, and its evening course a month.”

The verse connects the wind’s movement with ghuduww, rawāḥ, and the measure of a month.

A familiar reading understands a distance ordinarily requiring a longer time to cover.

This explanation concerns the statement about morning and evening movement, rather than R KH W itself.

Combining it with rukhāʾ does not independently allow calculation of precise wind speed, geographical distance, transport, or Solomon’s method of movement.

The Qur’an does not specify those details.

Ṣād 38:36, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:81, and Sabaʾ 34:12 share the gift of wind placed at Solomon’s disposal. Movement at his command is explicit in Ṣād 38:36 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:81; the quoted part of Sabaʾ 34:12 describes the measure of its morning and evening course.

Ṣād highlights gentleness, Al-Anbiyāʾ intensity, and Sabaʾ movement described on the scale of a month.

These descriptions can be studied together without substituting one for another or asserting unstated details as certain.

5. Related roots and concepts

R KH W (rukhāʾ) and L Y N (līn)

Līn means softness and gentleness, close to the core of R KH W.

Part of Āl ʿImrān 3:159 states:

فَبِمَا رَحْمَةٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ لِنْتَ لَهُمْ

fa-bi-mā raḥmatin mina allāhi linta lahum

“Through mercy from The God, you were gentle with them.”

Here gentleness concerns human behavior and interaction.

R KH W can likewise extend from physical softness to ease of life or reduced pressure.

Not all uses of the two roots should be equated, however.

Qur’anic līn can directly express gentle treatment, while rukhāʾan in Ṣād 38:36 describes wind.

Some R KH W derivatives concern slackness, reduced tension, or delay; not every softness in this family means kindness or good treatment.

R KH W (rukhāʾ) and ʿ Ṣ F (ʿaṣf)

ʿAṣf describing wind concerns intense, fierce blowing.

Rukhāʾ concerns soft and gentle blowing.

The two occur in different verses concerning Solomon.

Their contrast distinguishes the descriptions, without making every fast wind necessarily ʿāṣifah and every rukhāʾ wind necessarily slow.

Intensity, speed, and destructive effect are related but distinct concepts in actual usage.

Wind can move effectively without having destructive effects on its surroundings.

The distinction should therefore concern the manner of blowing and its context, rather than two fixed numerical speeds.

R KH W (rakhāʾ) and W S ʿ (saʿah)

Saʿah concerns breadth, capacity, and extent.

Rakhāʾ concerning life may denote ease and comfortable means.

The concepts can be close without being identical.

Wusʿ can also concern capacity and ability, as in part of Al-Baqarah 2:286:

لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا

lā yukallifu allāhu nafsan illā wusʿahā

“The God burdens no soul beyond its capacity.”

Wusʿ here concerns capacity, rather than merely comfortable living.

Rakhāʾ in its livelihood sense instead concerns freedom from pressure and hardship.

Their connection does not make every breadth prosperity, or every ability rakhāʾ.

Study of W S ʿ

R KH W (rukhāʾ) and S KH R (taskhīr)

The roots appear together in Ṣād 38:36. The following abbreviates the expression; the ellipsis marks omitted words:

فَسَخَّرْنَا لَهُ الرِّيحَ ... رُخَاءً

fa-sakhkharnā lahu al-rīḥa … rukhāʾan

“We subjected the wind to him … blowing gently.”

Taskhīr here concerns subjecting something and placing it at someone’s disposal.

Rukhāʾ describes the wind’s softness and gentleness.

The concepts should remain distinct.

Something can be soft without being subjected or obedient. Something can also be under control while acting forcefully or powerfully.

The wind’s compliance comes from sakhkharnā and bi-amrihi; its gentleness comes from rukhāʾan.

Study of S KH R

Softness and slackness: different outcomes

This family shows how the absence of hardness and tension can have different outcomes.

Softness may make movement fluid and a task easier, as with gentle wind or a horse moving without excessive pressure.

Slackness can also lead to instability, delay, or neglect of responsibility, as in tarākhā ʿani al-amri.

R KH W does not establish a timeless moral rule that softness is always good and hardness always bad.

Value depends on circumstances, effects, and context.

In Solomon’s verse, rukhāʾ accompanies divine giving and the subjection of wind. Its beneficial quality emerges from the complete verse, rather than every use of the root being inherently commendatory.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translations must distinguish physical softness, loosening, ease of life, and gentle wind.

Rikhw / rakhw: soft, slack, or lacking great hardness and tension.

Rakhāwah: softness, slackness, looseness.

Rakhuwa / rakhiya: became soft or slack; acquired that condition.

Arkhā: loosened, released, let down, or reduced tension or pressure.

Rākhā: loosened, increased distance, or eased constraint and pressure.

Istarkhā: became loose or soft; was relieved of pressure; concerning life, conditions became easier.

Tarākhā: slackened, delayed, or neglected a task; the precise meaning depends on context.

Irkhāʾ: loosening or allowing freedom; concerning a horse, a kind of fluid, free movement.

Mirkhāʾ: a mount that moves fluidly and freely.

Rakhiyy: soft or comfortable; rakhiyyu al-bāli means at ease in mind.

Rakhāʾ: ease of life, comfortable means, freedom from material pressure.

Rukhāʾ: soft, gentle wind; gentle blowing without violence.

The only Qur’anic occurrence, in Ṣād 38:36, reads:

فَسَخَّرْنَا لَهُ الرِّيحَ تَجْرِي بِأَمْرِهِ رُخَاءً حَيْثُ أَصَابَ

fa-sakhkharnā lahu al-rīḥa tajrī bi-amrihi rukhāʾan ḥaythu aṣāba

The existing Persian Forqan translation is:

“We subjected the wind to him, blowing gently at his command wherever he intended.”

This rendering fits the root’s lexical meaning and the verse’s construction.

“Blowing gently” conveys rukhāʾan; “at his command” corresponds to bi-amrihi; and “wherever he intended” conveys ḥaythu aṣāba.

There is no need to replace it with “extremely slow wind,” “motionless wind,” “always swift wind,” or “completely obedient wind” to express rukhāʾ.

Compliance is stated separately in the verse, while rukhāʾan alone does not specify speed.

“Comfort” or “prosperity” is unsuitable here because the context concerns how wind moves, rather than livelihood.

7. Conclusion

R KH W concerns softness, slackness, and absence of hardness or tension.

It appears physically in loosening ties, letting down curtains, soft objects, and fluid movement.

The family also extends to improved living conditions, reduced pressure, and comfortable means. Conversely, derivatives such as tarākhā can express slackness, delay, and neglect.

Softness and looseness do not inherently mean prosperity, kindness, weakness, or laziness. Each branch requires its own usage evidence.

The root occurs only in Ṣād 38:36, as rukhāʾan, describing the wind The God subjected to Solomon.

It concerns softness and gentleness of blowing, rather than complete stillness or necessarily very low speed.

Early lexical explanations can even describe it as soft and swift; speed should not be equated with violent blowing.

Comparing Ṣād 38:36 with Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:81 shows the subjected wind described as rukhāʾ and ʿāṣifah. One highlights gentleness, the other intensity. Their difference fits compliance with command, but the Qur’an does not state precisely how its condition changes.

Sabaʾ 34:12 describes morning and evening movement through the measure of a month. This belongs to Solomon’s blessing and should not become part of R KH W’s independent meaning.

Final definition: R KH W denotes softness, slackness, and reduced hardness or tension. Its uses may concern loosening, fluid movement, ease of life, or slackness and delay. Qur’anic rukhāʾan describes wind blowing softly and gently, without implying immobility or necessarily slow movement. Compliance with command, direction, and other features come from the verse’s other words and context, rather than from this root alone.
