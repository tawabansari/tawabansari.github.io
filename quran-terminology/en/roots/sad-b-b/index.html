Pouring, letting flow and pouring down: from flowing water to the infliction of punishment

Arabic Ṣ B B concerns pouring and flowing down. Its most familiar use is pouring water or another liquid, but early Arabic also used it for movement down a slope, something descending upon another, and events befalling someone.

Ṣabba is a doubled verb: its second and third root consonants are identical. Its Qur’anic forms are ṣababnā, ṣabban, yuṣabbu, ṣubbū and fa-ṣabba, occurring in four verses.

The Qur’an uses this root in two very different settings: pouring water that helps produce food and vegetation, and pouring punishment upon those subjected to it.

This difference shows that ṣabb intrinsically names neither a blessing nor a punishment; it expresses an action or movement. Other sentence elements and context identify what is poured, its direction and its result.

1. The semantic core and physical image

Pouring and causing to flow

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies the central meaning as:

إِرَاقَةُ الشَّيْءِ

irāqatu al-shayʾi

That is, pouring something out.

He illustrates this with ṣababtu al-māʾa aṣubbuhu ṣabban: I poured the water, I pour it, a pouring.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains ṣababtu al-māʾa through sakabtuhu: I poured the water.

The clear, direct sense is transferring something by pouring, particularly when the substance is a liquid capable of flowing.

Water may be poured from a vessel, fall from a height or flow toward another place.

Ṣabba alone does not specify the height, speed or quantity involved.

These require attention to the object, prepositions and other indications in the sentence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: pouring, slopes and groups of animals

Does ṣabb always mean pouring from above?

Many uses involve downward movement, since liquids ordinarily flow downward under their weight.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib defines ṣabbu al-māʾ as pouring from above, so downward movement has lexical support in the word’s usual image. The verb alone nevertheless specifies neither exact height, angle nor destination.

Ṣabba al-māʾa fī al-qadaḥi, for example, means pouring water into a cup. Fī al-qadaḥi identifies where it enters.

Ṣabba ʿalayhi, by contrast, highlights pouring onto something or someone.

Downward flow is thus a familiar image of the root, without restricting every use to vertical movement from a particular height without further evidence.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: pouring from above in the usual image

A slope and downward movement

Kitāb al-ʿAyn records:

الصَّبَبُ: تَصَوُّبُ نَهْرٍ أَوْ طَرِيقٍ يَكُونُ فِي حُدُورٍ

al-ṣababu: taṣawwubu nahrin aw ṭarīqin yakūnu fī ḥudūrin

That is, ṣabab denotes the downward slope of a river or road.

Here ṣabab names a feature of sloping land or a path, rather than the act of pouring liquid.

Water flows down such a route; people and animals likewise travel downhill along it.

The family therefore also concerns downward movement and descent.

Ibn Fāris explains this through an image of something apparently pouring down as it slopes.

This clarifies a semantic connection without proving that the slope noun historically developed after the pouring verb.

Al-ʿAyn: slopes, remainder and extract

2. Early Arabic uses

Pouring a drink into a vessel

A verse by Al-Aʿshā, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, reads:

بِكَأْسٍ وَإِبْرِيقٍ كَأَنَّ شَرَابَهُ
إِذَا صُبَّ فِي الْمِصْحَاةِ خَالَطَ بَقَّمَا

bi-kaʾsin wa-ibrīqin ka-anna sharābahu / idhā ṣubba fī al-miṣḥāti khālaṭa baqqamā

Translation: “With a cup and a pitcher whose drink, when poured into the vessel, seems mixed with the red dye of baqqam.”

Baqqam names plant material used as a red dye.

Ṣubba is passive here and concerns a drink being poured into a vessel.

The poet depicts the drink’s appearance as it is poured.

This witnesses the verb’s use for actual liquid pouring in early Arabic.

The verb itself indicates neither plentiful drink nor its goodness or badness. Such qualities come from the poem’s other expressions and imagery.

Lisān al-ʿArab, ṣ-ḥ-w: Al-Aʿshā’s witness and miṣḥāh

Blood and flowing liquids

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim reads:

وَحَالَفْتُمْ قَوْمًا هَرَاقُوا دِمَاءَكُمْ
لَوِشْكَانَ هَذَا وَالدِّمَاءُ تَصَبَّبُ

wa-ḥālaftum qawman harāqū dimāʾakum / la-wishkāna hādhā wa-al-dimāʾu taṣabbabu

Translation: “You allied yourselves with people who had shed your blood; how quickly you did so, while the blood still flows!” The ending emphasizes the alliance’s concurrence with flowing blood, rather than merely two events close together in the past.

Taṣabbabu concerns blood in this witness.

It is the imperfect of taṣabbaba, expressing blood flowing or pouring down. The underlying tataṣabbabu has lost one of its two initial tāʾs.

The root is therefore not confined to water. Another liquid, such as blood, may also be poured or flow.

Bloodshed here involves violence, but violence is not an independent component of taṣabbaba. The verb expresses flowing blood; the bloodshed is established by other wording.

Asās al-Balāghah: Bishr’s witness and flowing blood

A group moving together

Arabic ṣubbah is recorded for a group of horses, camels or sheep.

Ibn Fāris explains it through horses apparently pouring together toward their target in a raid.

The point is collective movement and a mass, rather than liquid.

Ṣubbah in some uses merely names a group of animals, without implying an actual attack.

“Attacking” should consequently not be made a fixed component of every form.

Ṣubābah: what remains in a vessel

Ṣubābah, with ḍammah on the first consonant, denotes a small remainder of drink at the bottom of a vessel.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn records it for drink remaining at a vessel’s base.

Arabic taṣābabtu al-māʾa means I drank the remaining water.

Its relation to pouring is intelligible: a little may remain after most of the contents have been poured out or consumed.

Ṣubābah here names the remainder, rather than the action of pouring.

Each derivative needs its own sense; a shared root does not justify translating every occurrence as “pour down.”

Ṣabābah: longing and attachment

Ṣabābah, with fatḥah on the first consonant, concerns longing, attachment and the tenderness or intensity of affection.

Rajulun ṣabbun describes a loving, yearning person.

Lexical sources also record ṣabba ilayhi for inclining toward or becoming attached to someone.

The image of the heart and attention flowing toward a beloved can explain its relation to physical pouring.

This is a semantic explanation, not proof of a historical origin.

The longing sense shows the family’s extension into emotion, but it is not central to any of the four Qur’anic verses containing this root.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Asās al-Balāghah: ṣubābah and ṣabābah

3. The Qur’anic uses

ʿAbasa 80:25: ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabban

أَنَّا صَبَبْنَا الْمَاءَ صَبًّا

annā ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabban

Translation: That We poured down the water, a pouring.

This follows the invitation to consider one’s food.

ʿAbasa 80:24 calls people to look at their food. Verse 25 then mentions pouring water; ʿAbasa 80:26 describes splitting the earth.

The following verses mention growing grain, grapes, greens, olives, date palms, thickly planted gardens and fruit.

Ṣababnā al-māʾa thus concerns water helping establish the conditions for plant growth and food production.

Rainfall is the clear referent, although al-māʾ means water and the expression does not independently name rain.

The role of ṣabban after the verb

Ṣabban is the verbal noun of ṣababnā and functions as a cognate accusative, mafʿūl muṭlaq.

Arabic uses this construction to emphasize an action or describe its manner.

The cognate noun foregrounds the pouring, but alone determines neither the precise amount of water nor the number or intensity of showers. Renderings such as “poured abundantly” may convey emphasis and contextually understood abundance; that differs from specifying a numerical quantity or destructive rainfall.

Ṣabban should therefore not necessarily become “a destructive, torrential downpour.”

The central meaning is pouring water; its part in vegetation follows from the connected verses.

The verb alone does not yield the mechanism of cloud formation, rainfall measurements or a detailed water cycle.

The passage draws attention to an important stage in supplying food.

Al-Ḥajj 22:19: yuṣabbu min fawqi ruʾūsihimu al-ḥamīmu

هَٰذَانِ خَصْمَانِ اخْتَصَمُوا فِي رَبِّهِمْ فَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا قُطِّعَتْ لَهُمْ ثِيَابٌ مِنْ نَارٍ يُصَبُّ مِنْ فَوْقِ رُءُوسِهِمُ الْحَمِيمُ

hādhāni khaṣmāni ikhtaṣamū fī rabbihim fa-alladhīna kafarū quṭṭiʿat lahum thiyābun min nārin yuṣabbu min fawqi ruʾūsihimu al-ḥamīmu

Translation: These are two opposing groups who have disputed concerning their Lord. For those who disbelieved, garments of fire have been cut, and scalding liquid is poured from above their heads.

Yuṣabbu is a passive imperfect verb from Ṣ B B.

Unlike ṣababnā in ʿAbasa 80:25, whose subject is expressed in the verb, the performer is not named in this passive construction.

Al-ḥamīmu is what is poured: extremely hot, scalding liquid or water.

Min fawqi ruʾūsihimu specifies its direction and location: from above their heads.

This phrase expressly identifies pouring from above their heads. Even if downward movement belongs to ṣabb’s ordinary image, the particular location above the heads is supplied by this separate expression.

Al-Ḥajj 22:20 describes the liquid’s effect on what is inside their bellies and on their skins, establishing its burning effect.

Ṣ B B alone does not specify the substance’s exact nature or temperature, or the physical details of punishment in the hereafter.

The verb expresses pouring; al-ḥamīmu identifies the substance, and the next verse its effects.

Al-Dukhān 44:48: thumma ṣubbū fawqa raʾsihi

ثُمَّ صُبُّوا فَوْقَ رَأْسِهِ مِنْ عَذَابِ الْحَمِيمِ

thumma ṣubbū fawqa raʾsihi min ʿadhābi al-ḥamīmi

Translation: Then pour above his head some of the punishment of scalding water.

Ṣubbū is an imperative addressed to a plural audience.

It should not be confused with a past passive verb. Al-Dukhān 44:47 commands seizing and dragging the person into the midst of the blazing Fire; verse 48 continues the address with the command to pour.

Ṣubbū expresses the instruction to perform the action.

Fawqa raʾsihi specifies its location.

Both this verse and Al-Ḥajj 22:19 concern pouring scalding liquid onto a head, but their grammar differs.

Al-Ḥajj uses a passive verb, focusing on the event. Al-Dukhān uses an imperative, expressing a command.

Min ʿadhābi al-ḥamīmi also explicitly connects the wording with punishment.

Ṣubbū cannot therefore be reduced to generic “punish,” discarding the pouring image. Conversely, it does not warrant physical details or methods beyond what the Qur’an states.

Al-Fajr 89:13: fa-ṣabba ʿalayhim rabbuka sawṭa ʿadhābin

فَصَبَّ عَلَيْهِمْ رَبُّكَ سَوْطَ عَذَابٍ

fa-ṣabba ʿalayhim rabbuka sawṭa ʿadhābin

Translation: So your Lord poured upon them a scourge of punishment.

This follows the mention of ʿĀd, Thamūd and Pharaoh.

Al-Fajr 89:11 describes their transgression in the lands; Al-Fajr 89:12 their multiplication of corruption.

Verse 13 then describes punishment befalling them.

Fa-ṣabba is an active past verb; rabbuka is its subject.

Sawṭa ʿadhābin is the object: what was poured upon them.

The object here is punishment, rather than a liquid such as water.

Why use a pouring verb for punishment?

Arabic ṣabba ʿalayhi al-ʿadhāba expresses inflicting punishment upon someone.

The image familiar from pouring liquid describes punishment descending upon a person.

This does not establish that these peoples’ punishment necessarily took the form of liquid or rain from the sky.

Other Qur’anic passages describe different ways peoples were destroyed, involving wind, a blast, an earthquake or another event.

Ṣabba in Al-Fajr 89:13 thus conveys inflicted punishment through a pouring image without specifying its physical mechanism.

The role of sawṭa ʿadhābin

Sawṭ ordinarily means a whip or scourge.

It brings an image of striking and punishment to this construction.

The verse does not state that the Lord struck these peoples with a physical whip. Arabic sawṭa ʿadhābin expresses severe punishment inflicted upon someone.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the expression’s extension from whipping and bodily punishment to different severe punishments.

A direct translation can therefore retain “a scourge of punishment,” while contextual explanation clarifies that punishment is being inflicted, without necessarily a blow from a physical implement.

This is a clear figurative use of ṣabba, where pouring describes punishment descending. Figurative wording does not itself mean that the punishment was unreal or immaterial.

Al-Ṭabarī: a scourge of punishment and figurative use

4. Morphology and semantic extensions

Every Qur’anic form belongs to Form I ṣabba or its verbal noun.

Ṣababnā: first-person plural active past verb, “We poured down.”

Ṣabban: accusative verbal noun emphasizing the verb in ʿAbasa 80:25.

Yuṣabbu: masculine singular passive imperfect, “is poured.”

Ṣubbū: second-person plural imperative, “pour.”

Fa-ṣabba: active past verb preceded by fa-, “so he poured.”

Active, passive, imperative and verbal-noun constructions occur in these five instances, with the basic pouring sense still recognizable.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four Form I verbs and one verbal noun

Ṣabb, inṣibāb and taṣabbub

Non-Qur’anic Arabic inṣabba and taṣabbaba also belong to this family.

Inṣabba al-māʾu describes water flowing down, and taṣabbaba al-ʿaraqu describes sweat flowing.

Attention can shift from someone pouring a liquid to the liquid and its own flow.

The morphological and syntactic distinction matters: ṣabba can be transitive, whereas inṣabba, as in water flowing down, expresses the flowing event itself.

Neither of these augmented forms occurs in the Qur’an.

Ṣabīb and ṣubbah

Arabic ṣabīb has several uses, including flowing material, plant extracts and coloring substances.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn records it for henna extract and certain dyes. Other sources also apply ṣabīb to blood and sweat.

The substance’s name may relate to its flowing or extracted condition, without confining every ṣabīb to one material.

Besides groups of animals, ṣubbah can denote a quantity or collection of things gathered together.

The family thus extends beyond the pouring verb, although the Qur’an contains the verb and verbal-noun branch.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

Ṣ B B (ṣabb) and S K B (sakb)

Arabic sakb also concerns pouring liquid.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 reads:

وَمَاءٍ مَسْكُوبٍ

wa-māʾin maskūbin

That is, “and water poured out or flowing.”

Some physical uses of ṣabb and sakb are very close; lexicographers explain one through the other.

Qur’anic maskūb describes water, whereas ṣababnā in ʿAbasa 80:25 expresses the action of pouring.

This difference follows more from form and sentence structure than from an absolute separation between the roots.

Without broader evidence, ṣabb cannot be declared invariably more forceful and sakb invariably gentler.

Ṣ B B (ṣabb) and N Z L (nuzūl)

N Z L concerns descending or coming down.

The Qur’an uses descent of water, scripture, angels and other things.

Ṣabb particularly conveys pouring and causing to flow down.

Water is poured in ʿAbasa 80:25. Other verses use anzalnā al-māʾa in rainfall contexts.

Both may concern one natural phenomenon, but nuzūl does not necessarily mean pouring liquid, nor does ṣabb always mean merely coming down.

Al-Fajr 89:13 uses ṣabba of punishment, with descent present in the context.

The relationship is partial overlap, rather than complete equivalence.

Study of N Z L

Ṣ B B (ṣabb) and F R GH (faragha)

Certain F R GH constructions concern emptying a vessel or pouring out its contents.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:126 reads:

رَبَّنَا أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا

rabbanā afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabran

That is, “Our Lord, pour patience upon us.”

Afrigh requests patience here, rather than pouring an actual liquid.

This illustrates Qur’anic imagery: verbs originally concerning pouring and emptying can also describe nonmaterial things.

Physical afragha may highlight emptying contents from a vessel, while ṣabba focuses on pouring.

The two may nevertheless overlap in many actual constructions.

Ṣ B B (ṣabb) and Ṣ W B (ṣawb)

Ṣ W B is a separate root.

Qur’anic ṣayyib belongs to Ṣ W B in Al-Baqarah 2:19, where it concerns rain.

Aṣāba can express reaching, striking or something befalling someone in different uses.

Pouring water, falling rain and reaching a destination have semantic connections, but these do not prove that Ṣ B B and Ṣ W B historically form one root.

Ṣabb directly expresses pouring; Ṣ W B has a different range of descent, arrival and impact.

They require separate research, without merging their meanings merely on the basis of similar sounds.

6. Challenges of understanding and translation

The main point is to distinguish pouring from the substance poured and its effects.

ʿAbasa 80:25 pours water, contextually contributing to vegetation and food.

Al-Ḥajj 22:19 pours scalding liquid onto heads; the next verse describes its effects.

Al-Dukhān 44:48 commands pouring some of the scalding punishment onto a person’s head.

Al-Fajr 89:13 uses pouring for inflicted punishment.

The semantic core remains recognizable while objects and consequences differ.

Does ṣabb always imply intensity and abundance?

Pouring may be forceful or plentiful, especially when a liquid flows continuously and extensively.

Intensity and abundance are not necessary features of every use.

Someone can pour a little water into a cup and still use ṣabba.

In ʿAbasa 80:25, ṣabban emphasizes the action without specifying a quantity.

The severity of punishment in Al-Fajr 89:13 follows from the entire construction and context, rather than the verb always meaning “punish severely.”

“Pour down forcefully” is therefore not an accurate fixed rendering for every occurrence.

Can ṣabb mean inflicting punishment?

Yes, in certain constructions, as a contextual and figurative use.

Ṣabba ʿalayhi al-ʿadhāba means to inflict punishment upon someone.

Punishment is not, however, an independent part of ṣabba’s meaning.

The same verb can concern water, blood or another substance.

The root should not be divided into two completely unrelated meanings, pouring water and punishing.

Pouring supplies the shared image; the object determines the particular action.

Does pouring always require a physical body and liquid?

No.

Al-Fajr 89:13 shows Arabic using pouring imagery to describe punishment descending.

Arabic also uses pouring verbs of trouble, calamity or hardship befalling someone.

These figurative uses do not establish that every punishment or nonmaterial thing mentioned in a verse physically exists as a liquid.

Conversely, when a verse itself mentions scalding liquid, its wording should not be reduced to an abstract concept alone.

Understanding requires preserving the word’s sense and distinguishing its imagery from unspecified physical details.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Pour” and “pour down” are appropriate general equivalents for ṣabba.

“Let flow down” also suits contexts emphasizing flow or downward movement.

Punishment passages can retain pouring imagery, with its significance explained in the analysis.

ʿAbasa 80:25: ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabban

“We poured down the water, a pouring.”

Natural Persian can convey the cognate noun’s emphasis through repetition or a suitable emphatic construction, without adding a specified quantity or destructive rain without sufficient evidence.

Al-Ḥajj 22:19: yuṣabbu min fawqi ruʾūsihimu al-ḥamīmu

“Scalding liquid is poured from above their heads.”

This preserves the passive verb, the direction and the liquid’s scalding quality.

Al-Dukhān 44:48: thumma ṣubbū fawqa raʾsihi min ʿadhābi al-ḥamīmi

“Then pour above his head some of the punishment of scalding water.”

This is an imperative and should not become a passive statement.

Al-Fajr 89:13: fa-ṣabba ʿalayhim rabbuka sawṭa ʿadhābin

“So your Lord poured upon them a scourge of punishment.”

The rendering preserves the imagery without restricting the punishment to one physical implement or mechanism.

8. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ B B centrally denotes pouring or letting something flow down, especially liquids. Its family also concerns slopes, flowing blood or sweat, inflicted punishment and certain emotional expressions.

Early Arabic ṣabab denotes a slope, ṣubābah a remainder of drink, and ṣabābah longing or attachment. These branches have intelligible semantic connections without proving a single historical derivational path.

The Qur’an has five occurrences in four verses: pouring water in ʿAbasa 80:25; pouring scalding liquid in punishment in Al-Ḥajj 22:19 and Al-Dukhān 44:48; and a scourge of punishment descending in Al-Fajr 89:13.

Short definition: Ṣ B B denotes pouring and letting something flow down; figuratively it can present something’s descending or befalling another through a pouring image. Brief root meaning: Pouring and letting flow down; figuratively, causing something to descend upon or befall another.

The basic sense is pouring, rather than abundance, punishment or intensity. Quantity, direction, substance, force and consequences follow from the complete construction and context.

This distinction helps preserve the verb’s clear meaning in Qur’anic translation without replacing it unnecessarily with explanations of rain, plant growth or punishment.
