Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root SH ʿ R (Sh Ayn R)

Perception, subtle awareness, situational sensitivity, sign-recognition, and unawareness of a near reality

Qur’anic forms of this root

The root ش ع ر (SH Ayn R) in the Qur’an points to subtle perception, sensitive awareness, attention to signs, recognition through marks, and becoming aware of something that may be very near while the human being remains unaware of it. Shuʿūr is not merely mental knowledge; it is a living awareness that awakens a person to the truth of a situation, the effect of an action, and the meaning of a sign.

The central meaning of the root

ش ع ر (SH Ayn R) in the Qur’an is the root of subtle, sign-aware perception: an awareness that makes the human being sensitive to the truth of a matter. This is not only verbal or mental knowledge. Shuʿūr means that a person catches the sign, senses the presence of truth, and understands the situation he is in.

In the Qur’an, SH Ayn R does not point only to knowing information. It points to a more living kind of awareness, one that arises through contact with a sign, a sense of presence, careful attention, and subtle reception. A person may see, hear, or live inside something, yet still lack shuʿūr toward it; he does not sense its truth, does not catch its sign, and does not understand his relation to it.

In Arabic, shaʿr also means hair. This sensory connection helps clarify the field of the root. Hair, in contact with skin, responds to subtle touch, wind, fear, cold, heat, or sudden bodily reaction. In this way, the root SH Ayn R is connected to sensitivity, delicate reception, and response to a near effect.

In brief: SH Ayn R in the Qur’an means perceiving truth through sign, attention, and sensitive wakefulness.

The difference between shuʿūr and ʿilm

ʿIlm in the Qur’an has a broad field: knowing, recognizing, being aware, God’s knowledge, human knowledge, knowledge of the Book, knowledge of truth, and knowledge of reality. But shuʿūr is more subtle and situational. It is closer to the wakefulness a person develops when facing signs and events.

A person may know something but still lack shuʿūr toward it. He may speak about death but not take death seriously in his own life. He may hear the signs but fail to sense the danger of corruption, hypocrisy, or denial within himself. Such a person has information, but not Qur’anic shuʿūr toward the truth.

So shuʿūr is living awareness: knowledge that awakens the human being to his own situation.

Lā yashʿurūn: unawareness of a near reality

One of the most important uses of this root in the Qur’an is the expression lā yashʿurūn. The Qur’an uses it for people who are in a situation of danger, corruption, deception, or heedlessness, yet do not perceive it.

At the beginning of Sūrat al-Baqarah, regarding the hypocrites, it says:

يُخَادِعُونَ اللَّهَ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَمَا يَخْدَعُونَ إِلَّا أَنفُسَهُمْ وَمَا يَشْعُرُونَ

They seek to deceive God and those who believe, but they deceive none except themselves, and they do not perceive. Al-Baqarah 2:9

The problem here is not merely lack of information. They are doing something whose effect returns upon themselves, but they lack shuʿūr. They do not perceive that their deception first damages them. They build the structure of deception but do not sense its return upon themselves.

In another verse, about those who cause corruption while imagining themselves to be reformers, it says:

أَلَا إِنَّهُمْ هُمُ الْمُفْسِدُونَ وَلَٰكِن لَّا يَشْعُرُونَ

Be aware: they themselves are the corrupters, but they do not perceive. Al-Baqarah 2:12

This context is one of the clearest examples of damaged shuʿūr. A person may be causing corruption while his self-image is that of reform. His problem is not only a mistaken theory; his sense of discernment has been damaged. He corrupts but does not perceive the corruption of what he is doing.

Shuʿūr and hypocrisy

Hypocrisy is one of the main fields where shuʿūr is absent. The hypocrite speaks outwardly, makes claims, and displays agreement, but does not understand his relation to truth. He imagines that appearance can bypass reality. The Qur’an shows that such deception returns upon him.

Wa mā yashʿurūn means that they do not perceive the depth of what they are doing. They do not perceive that deceiving religion, believers, or society first ruins their own inner structure. When a person makes falsehood into a tool of survival, he loses sensitivity toward truth.

So hypocrisy is not only a problem of outward honesty. It is also a disease of human perception. The hypocrite sees the sign but does not receive its message. Truth is near, but his inward sensitivity toward it has become numb.

Shuʿūr and corruption

The Qur’an says of some who cause corruption that they themselves are the corrupters, but they do not perceive. This shows that corruption is not always accompanied by an open intention to corrupt. Sometimes a person acts in the name of reform, religion, politics, tribe, security, or tradition while the real effect of his action is corruption.

When shuʿūr is lost, a person does not see the real effect of his action. He sees only the claimed intention or outward form. The Qur’an returns the standard to the true effect: if an action damages the earth, society, truth, justice, fitrah, or the human being’s relation with God, it is corruption, even if the actor calls himself a reformer.

Here shuʿūr means the ability to perceive the real effect of action. A person without shuʿūr may build ruin and name it reform.

Shuʿūr and hidden life

The Qur’an says about those who are killed in the way of God:

وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَن يُقْتَلُ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ أَمْوَاتٌ بَلْ أَحْيَاءٌ وَلَٰكِن لَّا تَشْعُرُونَ

Do not say of those who are killed in the way of God, “dead.” Rather, they are alive, but you do not perceive. Al-Baqarah 2:154

In this verse, lā tashʿurūn shows that the human being does not have ordinary sensory access to every layer of reality. Something may appear as death outwardly, while with God it has another truth. The human being does not perceive it through surface-level vision.

Here shuʿūr means perception of a hidden reality, a reality not measured by ordinary appearance. The Qur’an does not say that you have no report at all; it says you do not perceive it. There is a reality beyond the level of ordinary human reception.

Shaʿāʾir: signs that should awaken perception

One important branch of this root is shaʿāʾir. The shaʿāʾir of God are signs that should lead the human being to remembrance of God, sanctity, servanthood, and taqwā. The Qur’an says:

ذَٰلِكَ وَمَن يُعَظِّمْ شَعَائِرَ اللَّهِ فَإِنَّهَا مِن تَقْوَى الْقُلُوبِ

And whoever magnifies the signs of God, that is from the taqwā of hearts. Al-Hajj 22:32

Shaʿāʾir are not merely outward markers. If they become empty forms, they lose their primary function. A shaʿīrah should awaken shuʿūr: it should take the human being from the visible sign to the truth of God, taqwā, sanctity, and responsibility.

For this reason, magnifying the shaʿāʾir is not merely enlarging the outward form of rites. True magnification means not wasting the meaning of the sign. If a divine sign becomes an instrument of pride, religious commerce, tribalism, or power, then the sign has been separated from shuʿūr.

Poetry and the poet: sensory perception and effective speech

From this same root come poetry and poet in the Qur’an. Poetry is connected to sensation, imagination, image, rhythm, and verbal effect. The poet can receive with heightened sensitivity something others merely see, then transmit it through effective language.

This connection fits the root. Just as shaʿr, hair, is affected by the slightest contacts, poetry is speech that affects the subtle layers of sensation and imagination. The poet works through image, rhythm, comparison, intensification, and linguistic movement.

But this ability becomes dangerous if separated from truth. Poetic speech can awaken the human being, but it can also carry him away from reality. It can clarify a truth, but it can also place imagination where truth should stand. For this reason, the Qur’an does not treat poetry as absolutely evil, but it does separate revelation from poetry.

Why the Qur’an is not poetry

The opponents of the Messenger sometimes called him a poet. The Qur’an rejects this attribution:

وَمَا عَلَّمْنَاهُ الشِّعْرَ وَمَا يَنبَغِي لَهُ

We did not teach him poetry, nor is it fitting for him. Yā Sīn 36:69

This negation is not a total rejection of the literary value of poetry. The Qur’an does not say that every poem is false. Rather, it separates the nature of revelation from poetry. Poetry arises from taste, sensation, imagination, rhythm, emotion, and human experience. Revelation, however, is from God; it is reminder and clear Qur’an, not poetic speech.

The first difference is source. Poetry rises from within the human being: from sensation, imagination, grief, joy, anger, love, or personal experience. The Qur’an is the speech of God. Its source is not the Messenger’s emotion or human imagination.

The second difference is relation to truth. The poet may enlarge, shrink, shift, or re-create reality through imaginative images. Revelation comes to clarify reality, not dissolve it into imagination.

The third difference is purpose. Poetry can overpower feeling and carry the human being on the wave of language. The Qur’an also shakes the heart, but its purpose is to awaken responsibility: not to numb sensation, not to play with imagination, and not to escape accountability.

The fourth difference is stability. The Qur’an says about misguided poets:

أَنَّهُمْ فِي كُلِّ وَادٍ يَهِيمُونَ

They wander in every valley. Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225

And it also says:

وَأَنَّهُمْ يَقُولُونَ مَا لَا يَفْعَلُونَ

And they say what they do not do. Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:226

This critique is directed at poetry separated from truth: speech moving through valleys of imagination and claim without commitment to action and sincerity. The Qur’an immediately gives the exception and does not close the door on believing and righteous poets:

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ

Except those who believe and do righteous deeds. Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:227

So the issue is not poetic ability itself. The issue is that revelation must not be reduced to poetry. The opponents wanted to explain the Qur’an’s deep effect on hearts as poetic enchantment, so they could escape the weight of its truth and responsibility. By denying this attribution, the Qur’an declares that this speech is not human taste or poetic imagination. It is reminder, warning, furqān, and clear speech from God.

Methodological note

In translating this root, one should not always use only “know.” In many verses, “do not perceive,” “are not aware,” “do not sense,” “do not notice,” or “are unaware of the truth of the matter” may be more accurate.

Lā yashʿurūn should not always be translated as though it were the same as lā yaʿlamūn. The difference matters. Lā yaʿlamūn is more directly connected to not knowing, while lā yashʿurūn points to lacking a living and sensitive perception of a near reality. A person who is lā yashʿurūn may be inside the event but fail to receive its meaning.

In translating shaʿāʾir, one must also avoid reducing it to “rituals” alone. Shaʿāʾir are meaningful signs of God: markers that, if understood properly, awaken perception, sanctity, and taqwā.

In translating poetry and poet, the boundary of revelation must also be preserved. A poet should not always be reduced to a harmless artist, and poetry should not be treated as absolutely false. The Qur’an criticizes poetry that is baseless, imagination-wandering, and disconnected from action; but the central boundary in these verses is the separation of revelation from poetic speech.

Suggested rendering in Forqan

  • Yashʿur / yashʿurūn: perceives, they perceive, notices, they notice, senses the truth of the matter.
  • Lā yashʿurūn: they do not perceive, they are unaware, they do not sense the truth of the situation, they are unaware of the near meaning of the matter.
  • Lā tashʿurūn: you do not perceive, you are unaware, you are not aware of its hidden reality.
  • Shaʿāʾir Allāh: the signs of God, the shaʿāʾir of God, markers of sanctity and servanthood, signs that bring the human being to remembrance of God and taqwā.
  • Shāʿir: poet, poetic speaker; in critical contexts, one whose speech moves through imagination and sensory effect.
  • Shiʿr: poetry, poetic speech, language arising from taste and imagination; not revelation.
  • Wa mā ʿallamnāhu al-shiʿr: We did not teach him poetry; meaning this Qur’an is not of the category of poetic speech, human taste, or imaginative composition.

Summary

ش ع ر (SH Ayn R) in the Qur’an is the root of subtle perception and wakefulness toward signs. Shuʿūr means that the human being does not deal only with the outward surface of things, but perceives the near reality, the effect of action, and the meaning of the sign.

For this reason, the Qur’an says lā yashʿurūn about hypocrites and corrupters. They are not absolutely uninformed; rather, they are numb and unperceiving toward the truth of what they are doing. They deceive and do not perceive that they deceive themselves. They corrupt and do not perceive that they are corrupters.

Shaʿāʾir are also signs that should awaken this shuʿūr within the human being. If the sign remains but shuʿūr disappears, religion becomes outward form. If shuʿūr becomes alive, the sign returns the human being to taqwā, sanctity, and truth.

Poetry and the poet also belong to this field of sensitivity and subtle reception; but revelation is not poetry. The Qur’an is separate from poetic imagination, emotional fluctuation, and speech without commitment. The Qur’an is reminder and clear speech: a word that brings the human being out of perceptive numbness and awakens him before truth, responsibility, and God.
