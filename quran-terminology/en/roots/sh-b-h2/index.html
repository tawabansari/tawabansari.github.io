Linguistic Note

The core of ش ب ه (SH B H) is nearness in form or sign, such that the mind sees a relationship of likeness between two things. This likeness can be sound and natural, or it can become a cause of confusion.

Not every tashābuh is bad. Sometimes it means harmony, kinship, and structural nearness. Sometimes it means ambiguity and becoming hard to distinguish. Context decides.

If two fruits look alike, resemblance is in form. If cows become ambiguous to people, resemblance causes difficulty in identifying the case. If verses are mutashābihāt, the issue concerns layered meaning, the need to return them to muḥkamāt, and the danger of following ambiguity for fitnah. If the Qur’an says about Jesus شُبِّهَ لَهُمْ (shubbiha lahum), it speaks of the matter being made ambiguous to them, not of proving specific details the Qur’an does not state.

Central Meaning

SH B H points to resemblance, likeness in form or sign, becoming ambiguous, and a state in which two things, because of outward or meaningful nearness, are not clearly separated for the one who sees or understands.

Material Image

The material image is seeing two things that are outwardly close: two faces, two fruits, two animals, two paths, or two signs. The eye or mind perceives likeness between them. If the distinguishing sign is enough, one identifies them; if the signs become too close, the matter becomes ambiguous.

In Arabic usage, shabah can refer to things outwardly close to something else, such as yellow metal that resembles gold and may mislead an untrained eye. This image helps explain the root: outward similarity can be real without making the two things identical in truth.

In this image, SH B H is not only about physical sight. The mind can also perceive resemblance between meanings. For this reason, tashābuh in the Qur’an appears in the sensory world, in understanding the text, and in judging a reported event.

Classical Language Note

In classical Arabic, SH B H concerns likeness, resemblance, and becoming ambiguous. Shabah, shubhah, and tashābuh all come from the field of nearness and likeness. When something resembles another thing, the mind can see them as close; if the distinguishing signs are not clear, confusion arises.

Older usage can include likeness of face, likeness of a child to parents, likeness of paths, metals, or signs. For the Forqan text, however, what matters most is what the Qur’an itself shows: tashābuh can be natural and beautiful, and it can also become a field of ambiguity, fitnah, or misreading.

Resemblance in Outward Identification

In the story of the cow of Banī Isrā’īl, SH B H appears in the sense of ambiguity in identification. In 2:70, they say: إِنَّ الْبَقَرَ تَشَابَهَ عَلَيْنَا (inna al-baqara tashābaha ʿalaynā).

Tashābaha ʿalaynā means the cows have become similar and ambiguous upon us in signs and identification. The issue is not that all cows are one and the same. Rather, the signs are not sufficiently separating for them, or they have complicated the matter for themselves through repeated questioning. This use shows that SH B H can indicate ambiguity in identifying a specific case.

Mutashābih in Blessing and Fruit

In the description of paradise, the Qur’an says in 2:25: وَأُتُوا بِهِ مُتَشَابِهًا (wa-utū bihi mutashābihā).

Mutashābihā here points to likeness in form or sign. The blessing is new, yet it has a familiar and similar appearance. This verse shows that tashābuh is not always negative ambiguity. Sometimes it points to the link between the familiar and the new.

Similarity and Difference in Natural Things

In verses about plants, resemblance and non-resemblance appear together. In 6:99, the Qur’an says: وَالزَّيْتُونَ وَالرُّمَّانَ مُشْتَبِهًا وَغَيْرَ مُتَشَابِهٍ (wa-al-zaytūna wa-al-rummāna mushtabihā wa-ghayra mutashābih).

Mushtabihā wa-ghayra mutashābih shows that things can be similar in one respect and different in another. Olives and pomegranates, or kinds of fruit, may share likeness in leaf, shape, growth, color, or outward appearance, while differing in reality, taste, fruit, or other qualities.

Translation should not turn resemblance into sameness of truth. The Qur’an precisely places outward likeness and real difference together.

Mutashābihāt of the Qur’an Against Muḥkamāt

The most important epistemic use of this root appears in 3:7: مِنْهُ آيَاتٌ مُحْكَمَاتٌ هُنَّ أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ وَأُخَرُ مُتَشَابِهَاتٌ (minhu āyātun muḥkamāt hunna ummu al-kitāb wa-ukharu mutashābihāt).

In this verse, mutashābihāt stand opposite muḥkamāt. The muḥkamāt are the foundation and mother of the Book: clear, firm verses that must be the measure of understanding. The mutashābihāt are verses whose indication, layer, image, or ta’wīl can carry a capacity for confusion or misuse.

The Qur’an does not say that the mutashābihāt are false or should not be read. The problem is crooked-hearted following of the mutashābihāt: pursuing verses with ambiguity apart from the muḥkamāt, seeking fitnah and a desired ta’wīl. In the Forqan method, mutashābih must be returned to muḥkam. A layered or ambiguous verse must not rule over clear verses.

A Mutashābih Book: Internal Harmony

In Sūrat al-Zumar, the Qur’an calls itself kitāban mutashābihā. In 39:23, it says: اللَّهُ نَزَّلَ أَحْسَنَ الْحَدِيثِ كِتَابًا مُتَشَابِهًا مَّثَانِيَ (Allāhu nazzala aḥsana al-ḥadīthi kitāban mutashābihan mathānī).

Here mutashābihā is not negative. It means the parts of the Book are harmonious, of the same kind, mutually resonant, and internally coherent. Its verses do not estrange or contradict one another. Through clarifying repetitions and meaningful returns, they illuminate each other.

So a distinction must be made between mutashābihāt in 3:7, which can become a field of fitnah for crooked hearts, and kitāban mutashābihā in 39:23, which points to the harmony and resonance of the Book.

Ambiguity in the Matter of Jesus

One of the most sensitive uses of this root in the Qur’an concerns the claim of killing Jesus. In 4:157, the Qur’an says: وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ وَمَا صَلَبُوهُ وَلَٰكِن شُبِّهَ لَهُمْ (wa-mā qatalūhu wa-mā ṣalabūhu wa-lākin shubbiha lahum).

Shubbiha lahum must be translated with caution. The Qur’an denies the claim of killing and crucifixion and says the matter was made ambiguous to them. But the verse does not explain exactly how it became ambiguous. Therefore, the verse itself should not be made to prove a detailed story that someone else was exactly made to look like Jesus, nor should external historical details be imposed on it.

The verb shubbiha is passive. This passivity matters: the verse does not identify the agent or mechanism of the resemblance. It turns attention instead to the collapse of the claimants’ certainty. The verse emphasizes that they did not kill him, did not crucify him, the matter was made ambiguous to them, those who differed are in doubt, they have no knowledge, and they follow assumption.

Distinguishing Qur’anic Uses

In 2:70, tashābuh creates difficulty in identifying the specific cow. In 2:25, the mutashābih nature of the fruits points to likeness in form and familiarity of blessing. In 6:99, mushtabih and ghayr mutashābih appear together to show likeness and difference in natural things.

In 3:7, mutashābihāt stand opposite muḥkamāt and the danger of fitnah-seeking pursuit of ambiguity is named. In 39:23, a mutashābih book means a harmonious and internally coherent Book. In 4:157, shubbiha lahum refers to the matter of Jesus being made ambiguous to them.

The core meaning in all of these is resemblance or likeness in form or sign, but its result is not always the same. Sometimes resemblance is beauty and harmony; sometimes it causes ambiguity; sometimes it becomes a field of misuse and fitnah; and sometimes it breaks a historical claim of certainty.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

In تَشَابَهَ عَلَيْنَا (tashābaha ʿalaynā), ʿalā shows that the matter became ambiguous upon them, as a burden of confusion on their understanding. In أُتُوا بِهِ مُتَشَابِهًا (utū bihi mutashābihā), mutashābihā is a circumstantial state: what is given to them is presented in a state of likeness.

In مُشْتَبِهًا وَغَيْرَ مُتَشَابِهٍ (mushtabihā wa-ghayra mutashābih), two descriptions appear together, showing that one phenomenon can be similar in one respect and different in another. In أُخَرُ مُتَشَابِهَاتٌ (ukharu mutashābihāt), mutashābihāt describes a group of verses, not the entire Book.

But in كِتَابًا مُتَشَابِهًا (kitāban mutashābihā), the whole Book is described, and the meaning is internal harmony. These two uses must not be confused. In شُبِّهَ لَهُمْ (shubbiha lahum), the verb is passive. The verse does not specify the agent or exact mechanism, and that passivity requires interpretive restraint.

Difference from Nearby Roots

SH B H is close to مثل (M TH L), but they are not the same. Mathal more often points to equivalence, example, comparison, or likeness in value and attribute. Shabah points more to resemblance in form or sign, and sometimes to ambiguity arising from resemblance. Two things may be shabīh without being mithl.

SH B H differs from نظير (naẓīr). Naẓīr is closer to counterpart or peer. Shabah may be only outward or partial likeness, without the two things being true counterparts.

SH B H is close to لبس (L B S), but not identical. Labs means mixing or covering one matter with another so that truth and falsehood, or two things, become obscured together. Tashābuh can become a ground for labs, but by itself it is likeness or becoming ambiguous. Tashābuh is a ground of mistake; labs is mixture and covering of boundaries.

SH B H also differs from rayb and shakk. Rayb is an unsettled and troubling doubt; shakk is mental hesitation between two sides. Tashābuh can cause rayb or shakk, but the root itself focuses on likeness and becoming ambiguous.

SH B H is close to iltibās. Iltibās means entanglement and unclear boundaries. Tashābuh can lead to iltibās, but it can also be positive harmony and likeness.

Forqan Translation Method

In Forqan translation, SH B H should not always be translated as “ambiguity” or always as “resemblance.” In some verses, “similar” or “alike” is enough. In others, “became ambiguous” or “was made ambiguous” is more accurate. In 3:7, mutashābihāt should be preserved and explained as verses that must be returned to the muḥkamāt.

In 39:23, mutashābih means harmonious and of one kind, not obscure and fitnah-producing. In 4:157, “the matter was made ambiguous to them” is more cautious and closer to the text than “someone was made to resemble him.”

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

  • For إِنَّ الْبَقَرَ تَشَابَهَ عَلَيْنَا (inna al-baqara tashābaha ʿalaynā): the cows have become ambiguous to us; or: the cows appear similar and hard to distinguish to us.
  • For وَأُتُوا بِهِ مُتَشَابِهًا (wa-utū bihi mutashābihā): and it was given to them in a similar and familiar form; or: it was given to them as something recognizable and alike.
  • For مُشْتَبِهًا وَغَيْرَ مُتَشَابِهٍ (mushtabihā wa-ghayra mutashābih): outwardly similar, yet truly different; or: similar in one respect and different in another.
  • For آيَاتٌ مُحْكَمَاتٌ ... وَأُخَرُ مُتَشَابِهَاتٌ (āyātun muḥkamāt ... wa-ukharu mutashābihāt): firm verses ... and other mutashābih verses; or in explanation: clear foundational verses, and verses with likeness and capacity for ambiguity that must be returned to the muḥkamāt.
  • For فَيَتَّبِعُونَ مَا تَشَابَهَ مِنْهُ (fa-yattabiʿūna mā tashābaha minhu): they follow what is mutashābih from it; or: they pursue its ambiguous part.
  • For كِتَابًا مُتَشَابِهًا مَّثَانِيَ (kitāban mutashābihan mathānī): a mutashābih and mathānī Book; or in explanation: a Book internally harmonious, of one kind, and mutually resonant.
  • For وَلَٰكِن شُبِّهَ لَهُمْ (wa-lākin shubbiha lahum): rather, the matter was made ambiguous to them; or: rather, the event became ambiguous to them.

Summary

SH B H in the Qur’an points to resemblance, likeness in form or sign, and sometimes a matter becoming ambiguous. At the material level, two things come close in outward form or sign. At the level of understanding, that nearness may create ambiguity, doubt, or need for clarification. But tashābuh is not always negative; sometimes it marks harmony, familiarity, and shared kind.

In the Qur’an, this root appears in identifying the cow, fruits of paradise, natural phenomena, muḥkam and mutashābih verses, the harmony of the Book, and the matter of Jesus being made ambiguous. Each use must be understood in its own context.

The main difference between SH B H and mathal is that mathal more often means example, comparison, or value-like equivalence, while shabah is closer to resemblance in form or sign and the possibility of confusion. Its difference from labs is that labs is mixing and covering the boundary between truth and falsehood, while tashābuh may be only likeness. Its difference from shakk is that shakk is the mental state of doubt, while tashābuh may be its cause or ground.

In summary: SH B H in the Qur’an points to likeness, resemblance, and becoming ambiguous; sometimes natural and beautiful resemblance, sometimes internal harmony, sometimes ambiguity that must be returned to muḥkamāt, and sometimes a historical matter about which humans do not possess certain knowledge.
