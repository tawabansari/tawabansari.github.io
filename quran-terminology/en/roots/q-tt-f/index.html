Qur’anic Root Q Ṭ F

Picking and harvesting; fruits and clusters available for picking

Qur’anic forms

قُطُوفُهَا

Quṭūfuhā

Its fruits available for picking; 69:23 and 76:14

1. Basic meaning and linguistic background

The Arabic root Q Ṭ F denotes picking, cutting, and separating something from its point of attachment, especially picking fruit from a tree or a cluster from a vine.

Poetry attributed to al-Ḥuṭayʾah and Ḥassān ibn Thābit, both of whom lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, attests agricultural uses of this family. A use concerning the gait of animals also appears in verse attributed to Zuhayr. Attribution does not establish an exact date of composition: the evidence examined here does not independently prove that the two agricultural witnesses predate the Qur’an.

Qur’anic form examined: quṭūfuhā, “its fruits or clusters available for picking.”

Other Arabic forms include qaṭafa, “to pick”; qaṭf, “picking”; qiṭf, a fruit for picking or a cluster; qiṭāf, harvest time; and miqṭaf, a harvesting implement. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. Material imagery and early Arabic evidence

When a gardener separates a bunch of grapes from a vine or picks fruit from a branch, the action can be expressed by qaṭafa.

Cutting here serves to detach and harvest produce. Agricultural qaṭf therefore concerns obtaining a crop as well as cutting something.

In verse attributed to al-Ḥuṭayʾah, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, a group is praised for generosity:

وَنُبِّئْتُ أَنَّ الْجُودَ مِنْهُمْ خَلِيقَةٌ / يَجُودُونَ فِي يَبْسِ الزَّبِيبِ وَفِي الْقَطْفِ

“I have been told that generosity is their nature: they give both in the season of dried raisins and at harvest time.”

Qaṭf here concerns harvesting and contrasts with the time of dried raisins. The contrast emphasizes generosity at both times.

Verse attributed to Ḥassān ibn Thābit describes prosperous life in Yathrib:

وَفِيمَا اشْتَهَوْا مِنْ عَصِيرِ الْقِطَافِ / وَعَيْشٍ رَخِيٍّ عَلَى غَيْرِ هَمٍّ

“And whatever they desired of the juice of harvested fruit, and a comfortable life without care.”

ʿAṣīr al-qiṭāf concerns harvested produce and the drink obtained from it. A different wording of the second hemistich is also transmitted. The exact composition dates of these two witnesses have not been established here; they attest agricultural usage but do not conclusively establish its priority to the Qur’an.

Classical Arabic also uses qaṭf for certain kinds of cuts and scratches. Qaṭūf describes an animal that moves slowly with short, closely spaced steps. This usage is also recorded in verse attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā.

Qaṭūf, a slow-stepping animal, must not be confused with quṭūf, fruits or clusters for picking. The different vowel on the qāf accompanies a difference in meaning.

3. The Qur’anic uses of quṭūf

Quṭūf is the broken plural of qiṭf. Lexical explanations record two closely related uses of qiṭf: fruit that has been picked, and a cluster or fruit that is the object of picking. The second does not itself say that picking has already occurred. The sense of harvested produce should therefore not be imposed on every context.

Both Qur’anic occurrences describe the blessings of the Garden.

Fruits near at hand

قُطوفُها دانِيَةٌ

Quṭūfuhā dāniyah.

“Its fruits for picking are near.”

Al-Haqqah 69:23

The preceding verses describe someone who receives their record of deeds in their right hand and enjoys a pleasing life in an elevated Garden.

The attached pronoun hā in quṭūfuhā refers to the Garden mentioned in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:22.

Grammatically, quṭūfuhā is the subject and dāniyah is its predicate. Dāniyah comes from D N W, meaning to be near.

Quṭūf names the Garden’s fruits for picking; dāniyah expresses their nearness. Being low or near is thus not part of Q Ṭ F’s independent meaning, but is conveyed by the accompanying description.

The following verse states:

كُلوا وَاشرَبوا هَنيـًٔا بِما أَسلَفتُم فِى الأَيّامِ الخالِيَةِ

Kulū wa-shrabū hanīʾan bi-mā aslaftum fī l-ayyāmi l-khāliyah.

“Eat and drink with enjoyment for what you sent ahead in the days gone by.”

Al-Haqqah 69:24

In this context, the nearness of the fruits forms part of the depiction of easy, pleasant enjoyment of the reward for past deeds.

Fruit made easy to reach

وَدانِيَةً عَلَيهِم ظِلٰلُها وَذُلِّلَت قُطوفُها تَذليلًا

Wa-dāniyatan ʿalayhim ẓilāluhā wa-dhullilat quṭūfuhā tadhlīlā.

“And its shades are near over them, and its fruits for picking have been made fully accessible.”

Al-Insan 76:14

Alongside the nearness of the Garden’s shades, this verse describes easy access to its fruit.

Dhullilat is a passive perfect verb of Form II from DH L L. Quṭūfuhā is its passive subject, while tadhlīlan is a cognate accusative emphasizing that the fruits have been made easy to reach.

Tadhlīl in this context means removing difficulty and making enjoyment easy, rather than humiliating.

In earthly life, fruit growing on a tree is not necessarily easy to reach. Picking may require climbing, a ladder, or a harvesting implement. The Qur’anic image of the Garden presents enjoyment of fruit without such difficulty.

The verse does not specify the precise arrangement of the branches or how the fruit becomes accessible. It clearly expresses ease of enjoyment.

4. Comparison with related roots

Q Ṭ F (qaṭf) and Q Ṭ ʿ (qaṭʿ): picking and cutting

Qaṭʿ has a broad semantic range: cutting, separating, and severing a connection or continuity.

Qaṭf likewise concerns separating something from its point of attachment, but its prominent agricultural use is picking and harvesting produce.

Cutting a branch can be expressed by qaṭʿ, while picking a grape cluster from a vine is a clear instance of qaṭf. Neither root’s Arabic uses are limited to these two examples.

Q Ṭ F (qaṭf) and J N Y (jany): picking and harvesting

These roots overlap considerably in the domain of picking fruit and harvesting produce.

Al-Raḥmān states:

مُتَّكِـٔينَ عَلىٰ فُرُشٍ بَطائِنُها مِن إِستَبرَقٍ وَجَنَى الجَنَّتَينِ دانٍ

Muttakiʾīna ʿalā furushin baṭāʾinuhā min istabraqin wa-janā l-jannatayni dān.

“Reclining on couches whose linings are of thick brocade, with the fruit for picking of the two Gardens near at hand.”

Ar-Rahman 55:54

The verses in Al-Raḥmān and Al-Ḥāqqah describe the nearness of fruit for picking through two different expressions.

Qaṭf highlights detaching and picking fruit; jany also concerns harvesting and obtaining the yield. Both families, however, can denote the act of picking and its product.

Through words from two roots, these verses present a shared image of the nearness of the Garden’s produce and the opportunity to enjoy it.

Q Ṭ F (qaṭf) and TH M R (thamar): picking and bearing fruit

Thamar denotes the fruit or yield of plant growth, whether still on a branch or already harvested.

Qiṭf describes that produce in relation to picking and harvesting.

Grapes on a vine are its thamar, and a cluster ready to be picked may also be called qiṭf. The difference chiefly concerns the perspective from which the produce is named, not necessarily its species or stage of growth.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

For quṭūf in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:23 and Al-Insān 76:14, “fruits for picking” is proposed.

“Fruit clusters” is also defensible, although it may suggest only fruits growing in bunches, such as grapes.

“Fruits” is fluent and natural but makes the word’s connection with picking and harvesting less apparent.

“Picked fruits” is not required in these verses: the noun can designate produce for picking, while the contexts emphasize nearness and ease of access.

6. Conclusion

Q Ṭ F denotes cutting and separating something from its point of attachment, especially picking fruit and harvesting produce.

The Qur’an uses the noun quṭūf for the Garden’s fruits for picking. Al-Ḥāqqah emphasizes their nearness, and Al-Insān the ease of access and harvesting.

Proposed Forqan definition: Picking and separating, especially harvesting fruit from a branch. In Arabic, qiṭf names both harvested produce and a cluster or fruit that is the object of picking. Its plural quṭūf is translated here in its two Qur’anic occurrences as “fruits for picking,” without claiming that they have already been detached.
