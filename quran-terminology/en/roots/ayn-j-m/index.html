Main senses: obscurity or difficulty in expressing or understanding speech; in another branch, biting and testing something's hardness; and, materially, a fruit's hard stone or seed. In the Quran, this root describes non-Arabic language and non-Arabic speakers.

1. The root in early Arabic

The three-consonant root ع ج م has several semantic branches in early Arabic. Its principal linguistic branch concerns speech that is unclear to its listener or a speaker's inability to express something clearly.

عُجْمَة describes a condition in which speech is not clearly expressed or understood. In one established use, أَعْجَم describes someone unable to speak clearly and fluently.

This may arise from an inability to speak, impaired articulation, unfamiliarity with the listener's language, or a difference between their languages.

Arabic also uses عَجْمَاء of animals that do not speak as humans do.

In this semantic field, عَجَم designates peoples whose languages were unfamiliar to Arabic speakers. This need not mean that those peoples lack speech or clear expression: their languages differ from Arabic and may be unintelligible to an Arabic-speaking listener without learning or translation.

Three situations must therefore be distinguished: inability to speak, unclear expression, and speaking a language the listener does not know.

They may produce a similar result for a listener, but they are linguistically different situations.

Early lexicons also record other branches: hardness and firmness, biting to test something, and the hard stones or seeds of fruit.

Shared consonants alone do not justify reducing these meanings to one definition. Early analyses themselves distinguish silence and unclear expression from hardness, firmness, and biting.

First poetic witness: أَعْجَم in pre-Islamic poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād contains:

أَعْيَاكَ رَسْمُ الدَّارِ لَمْ يَتَكَلَّمِ

aʿyāka rasmu al-dāri lam yatakallami

حَتَّى تَكَلَّمَ كَالْأَصَمِّ الْأَعْجَمِ

ḥattā takallama ka-l-aṣammi al-aʿjami

Translation: The traces of the dwelling baffled your recognition; they did not speak, until they spoke like someone deaf and unable to express himself clearly.

The poet stands before the remains of a dwelling abandoned by its inhabitants. He tries to recognize it, but its worn, obscure traces seem unable to answer him.

In this image, الْأَصَمّ refers to someone who cannot hear, and الْأَعْجَم to someone unable to articulate clearly.

The poet uses these descriptions to portray the dwelling's inability to respond.

This transmitted witness associates أَعْجَم with unclear expression and an inability to convey intelligible speech in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Here the word is not the name of a particular people or nationality. It describes a condition of expression.

The verse is transmitted in the Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAntarah with الْأَصَمِّ الْأَعْجَمِ. This study has not independently verified the supplied claim that الْأَعْمَى replaces الْأَعْجَم in another version, so that claim is not treated as established evidence.

The transmission of a poetic witness must be distinguished from independently establishing its exact date; the meaning of أَعْجَم in the version quoted here is clear.

Second poetic witness: biting and testing hardness

A poem attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī contains:

فَظَلَّ يَعْجُمُ أَعْلَى الرَّوْقِ مُنْقَبِضًا

fa-ẓalla yaʿjumu aʿlā al-rawqi munqabiḍan

فِي حَالِكِ اللَّوْنِ صَدْقٍ غَيْرِ ذِي أَوَدِ

fī ḥāliki al-lawni ṣidqin ghayri dhī awadi

Translation: It kept biting the upper part of the horn, drawn together upon a deeply black, strong horn without a bend.

الرَّوْق here means a horn.

يَعْجُمُ is a form of عَجَمَ meaning to bite or press with the teeth.

Lexicons record عَجَمَ الْعُودَ for biting a piece of wood to test its hardness or softness.

The same action can involve other objects, such as biting a bone or pressing the teeth against something hard.

Al-Nābighah's verse belongs to his well-known poem beginning يَا دَارَ مَيَّةَ بِالْعَلْيَاءِ فَالسَّنَدِ and is cited in early lexicons to explain this verb.

The verse directly illustrates biting. Testing hardness is another recorded use of this branch, but the quoted verse alone does not establish that testing is the animal's purpose.

This sense differs from أَعْجَم as a description of unintelligible speech.

Biting or testing wood must not become the meaning of Quranic أَعْجَمِيّ; equally, its linguistic use does not make every occurrence of the root refer to speech.

What the historical witnesses show

The verses attributed to ʿAntarah and Al-Nābighah illustrate two different branches of this family:

First, unclear or impaired expression; second, biting, a branch that also includes testing hardness in other expressions.

These witnesses come from poems attributed to pre-Islamic poets and matter for the root's earlier usage, although this study has not independently established the precise composition date of either verse.

Lexicons also record hard fruit stones and the distinction between Arabic speakers and non-Arabic speakers.

Semantic resemblance or inclusion in later dictionaries alone cannot establish when each branch arose or the order in which the senses developed.

2. Lexical forms and semantic branches

Important forms include عَجَمَ, عَجُمَ, عَجْم, عُجْمَة, عَجَم, عُجْم, عَجَمِيّ, أَعْجَم, أَعْجَمِيّ, عَجْمَاء, أَعْجَمَ, إِعْجَام, اسْتَعْجَمَ, مُعْجَم, عَاجِم, and عَجَمَة.

عُجْمَة: obscurity of speech and expression

In linguistic usage, عُجْمَة concerns unclear speech or inability to express oneself clearly.

Someone may not articulate words properly, or their speech may be unclear to a listener.

This can result from difficulty with pronunciation or expression.

Across languages, however, someone may speak perfectly clearly in their own language while remaining unintelligible to a listener who does not know it.

The causes of these two situations differ.

For example, fluent, precise Persian may be unintelligible to someone who knows only Arabic. That does not mean the Persian speaker lacks clear expression.

Thus speech being unintelligible to a particular listener is not the same as its being inherently unclear.

This distinction is especially important for the Quranic uses.

أَعْجَم: someone whose expression is unclear

In one established Arabic use, أَعْجَم describes someone unable to speak clearly and fluently.

It can also describe an Arabic speaker.

Lexicons explicitly allow that someone may be Arab by ancestry yet be called أَعْجَم because of impaired or unclear speech.

In this use, it describes expression rather than ethnic ancestry.

More broadly, it may describe someone who does not know the audience's language and whose speech they cannot understand.

Such a person may nevertheless speak their own language very well.

عَجَمِيّ: affiliation with non-Arab peoples

In established usage, عَجَمِيّ describes someone belonging to the عَجَم: someone not regarded as Arab in ethnic or linguistic affiliation.

Here the word may concern origin or affiliation rather than speaking ability.

A person from a non-Arab family may speak Arabic very clearly and fluently.

Such a person can be عَجَمِيّ in affiliation without necessarily being أَعْجَم in Arabic expression.

In some historical contexts, عَجَم refers particularly to Iranians; its broader older scope also includes other non-Arab peoples.

It should therefore not always be translated as “Iranians.”

أَعْجَمِيّ: non-Arabic language or speech

In linguistic contexts, أَعْجَمِيّ describes non-Arabic speech or language, or speech that is not clear to Arabic speakers.

Lexicons offer different explanations of the relationship between أَعْجَم and أَعْجَمِيّ.

Some treat أَعْجَمِيّ as a relational form derived from أَعْجَم; others recognize usage with essentially the same meaning.

By contrast, عَجَمِيّ can more particularly express affiliation with non-Arab peoples.

This distinction is useful but should not be made an absolute rule excluding all overlap.

The accompanying words in the Quran clearly place أَعْجَمِيّ in the field of language and speech.

عَجْمَاء: a creature without human speech

عَجْمَاء is the feminine form of أَعْجَم.

Arabic uses it of animals because they do not express themselves through human speech and sentences.

This designation does not mean that animals have no sounds, communicative behavior, or means of receiving and transmitting information.

Many animals communicate through sounds, movement, scent, and other behavior.

The Arabic designation concerns the difference from human speech; it does not settle every question about living creatures' cognitive capacities.

This is also consistent with Al-Naml 27:16 and Al-Naml 27:18, which mention communication and the reception of messages involving animals.

The word عَجْمَاء itself, however, does not occur in the Quran.

عَجَمَ: biting and testing

In the material branch, عَجَمَ الشَّيْءَ means to bite or press something with the teeth.

In عَجَمَ الْعُودَ, a person presses wood with the teeth to learn whether it is hard or soft.

The lexicons relate this action to the extended sense of testing and discovering a thing's quality; this semantic explanation does not by itself establish a precise historical sequence.

عَجَمْتُ فُلَانًا can mean “I tested him and came to know him.”

عَجَمَتْهُ الْأُمُورُ describes someone tested and seasoned by life's experiences and difficulties.

Actual biting is no longer meant: the expression concerns testing someone and revealing their qualities.

The expression صُلْبُ الْمَعْجَم is recorded for someone who proves firm and resilient when tested.

This branch differs from unclear speech and should not be transferred to Quranic أَعْجَمِيّ without evidence.

عَجَم: a fruit's hard stone or seed

عَجَم, with fatḥah on both the first and second consonants, is recorded for stones or seeds of fruits such as dates, grapes, and pomegranates.

Its unit noun is عَجَمَة, with fatḥah on the jīm, as in قَصَب and قَصَبَة; it should not be confused with عُجْمَة in the linguistic sense.

Here the word denotes a hard part within fruit.

Some lexicographers connect the designation with its hardness and the difficulty of breaking or chewing it.

That physical relationship is understandable, but it does not establish “fruit stone” as the root's historically first meaning.

عَجَم meaning fruit stones and عَجَم meaning non-Arab peoples are different uses despite their shared written form.

The fruit-stone branch does not occur in the Quran.

أَعْجَمَ and إِعْجَام: obscuring or clarifying writing

In one use, أَعْجَمَ concerns making speech obscure or unclear.

In writing terminology, إِعْجَام also denotes adding distinguishing dots to letters and removing confusion between similar shapes.

In Arabic script, for example, dots distinguish ب, ت, and ث, or ج, ح, and خ.

Accordingly, حُرُوفُ الْمُعْجَم and الْكِتَابُ الْمُعْجَم occur in discussions of writing and language.

Here dotting clarifies the letter rather than making it obscure.

Lexicographers differ over the designation: some emphasize removing ambiguity through dots, while others discuss the letters themselves and their naming.

The word's form alone therefore does not prove that إِعْجَام always meant making something unintelligible.

مُعْجَم meaning a dictionary is a specialized use in lexicographical history and does not occur in the Quran.

اسْتَعْجَمَ: becoming unintelligible or falling silent

اسْتَعْجَمَ describes speech or something else becoming obscure or unintelligible.

اسْتَعْجَمَ عَلَيْهِ الْكَلَامُ means “the speech became unintelligible to him.”

In another use, اسْتَعْجَمَتِ الدَّارُ describes an abandoned dwelling with nobody left to answer an inquiry.

This image is related to ʿAntarah's portrayal of silent dwelling traces.

Nevertheless, actual silence, inability to speak, and failure to understand another language should be distinguished.

Their effect on a listener may be similar, but their causes are not identical.

3. Quranic occurrences

This root occurs four times in three verses.

Three occurrences use أَعْجَمِيّ and one uses the plural الْأَعْجَمِينَ.

They are in Al-Naḥl 16:103, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:198, and Fuṣṣilat 41:44. The last verse contains two occurrences.

None concerns biting, testing hardness, or fruit stones.

All belong to language and expression.

A. أَعْجَمِيّ: the language of the alleged human teacher

وَلَقَدْ نَعْلَمُ أَنَّهُمْ يَقُولُونَ إِنَّمَا يُعَلِّمُهُ بَشَرٌ ۗ لِسَانُ الَّذِي يُلْحِدُونَ إِلَيْهِ أَعْجَمِيٌّ وَهَٰذَا لِسَانٌ عَرَبِيٌّ مُبِينٌ

wa-laqad naʿlamu annahum yaqūlūna innamā yuʿallimuhu basharun lisānu alladhī yulḥidūna ilayhi aʿjamiyyun wa-hādhā lisānun ʿarabiyyun mubīn

Al-Naḥl 16:103

And We certainly know that they say, “Only a human being teaches him.” The language of the one to whom they allude is non-Arabic, while this is a clear Arabic tongue.

Al-Naḥl 16:103

أَعْجَمِيٌّ is grammatically the predicate of لِسَانُ الَّذِي, describing its language as non-Arabic; it is not a dependent adjective modifying that definite phrase.

The immediate subject is the language of the person from whom the opponents claimed the Quran had been learned.

لِسَان here concerns language and manner of speech.

The verse does not judge that person's ethnicity, human worth, or entire range of abilities: it compares his language with the Quran's language.

The alleged teacher's language and the Quran's language

The opening reports the claim إِنَّمَا يُعَلِّمُهُ بَشَرٌ.

The opponents said that a human being was teaching Muḥammad.

The Quran then refers to the language spoken by the alleged source.

لِسَانُ الَّذِي يُلْحِدُونَ إِلَيْهِ أَعْجَمِيٌّ means that the language of the person to whom they allude is non-Arabic.

In contrast, the verse says وَهَٰذَا لِسَانٌ عَرَبِيٌّ مُبِينٌ.

That is, the language of this message is Arabic and clear.

The argument rests on the difference between the alleged source's language and the Quran's clear language.

This difference challenges their allegation about its source of instruction.

The verse does not imply that a non-Arab can never learn Arabic or teach someone. People can learn other languages and transmit knowledge.

Its subject is a particular allegation about the Quran's origin and the difference between that person's language and the revealed message's language.

أَعْجَمِيّ and مُبِين

At the end of the verse, أَعْجَمِيّ contrasts with عَرَبِيٌّ مُبِينٌ.

عَرَبِيّ identifies Arabic.

مُبِين comes from ب ي ن and concerns clarity or manifestness.

These words do not have the same meaning.

Speech may be Arabic yet difficult or obscure in its construction or explanation. Speech in another language may be perfectly clear to those who know it.

Being non-Arabic therefore does not make a language incapable of expressing truth or conveying knowledge.

Here the Quran refers to its clear Arabic language in contrast to the alleged teacher's non-Arabic language.

This linguistic contrast should not become a claim that one people is inherently superior to another.

B. الْأَعْجَمِينَ: one of the non-Arabic speakers

وَلَوْ نَزَّلْنَاهُ عَلَىٰ بَعْضِ الْأَعْجَمِينَ

wa-law nazzalnāhu ʿalā baʿḍi al-aʿjamīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:198

And had We sent it down upon one of the non-Arabic speakers,

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:198

الْأَعْجَمِينَ here refers to non-Arabic-speaking people.

Morphological explanations connect this plural with أَعْجَم and the relational form أَعْجَمِيّ.

One explanation involves omission of the relational yāʾ.

The contextual sense is clear: a person belonging to the non-Arabic-speaking group.

This verse does not complete the conditional sentence by itself; its consequence follows in the next verse.

فَقَرَأَهُ عَلَيْهِمْ مَا كَانُوا بِهِ مُؤْمِنِينَ

fa-qaraʾahu ʿalayhim mā kānū bihi muʾminīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:199

And he had recited it to them, they would not have believed in it.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:199

Together, the verses mean that had the Quran been sent down upon a non-Arabic speaker who recited it to the audience, they would still not have believed.

The role of language in this hypothetical case

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:195 identifies the Quran as a message in clear Arabic.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:198 then imagines this message being sent down upon one of the non-Arabic speakers.

The message's language must therefore be distinguished from the person receiving it.

The immediate hypothetical change concerns its recipient; it does not necessarily change the Quranic text into another language.

This matters especially in comparison with Fuṣṣilat 41:44, where the Quran itself is hypothetically non-Arabic.

الْأَعْجَمِينَ and human worth

Here being non-Arabic-speaking describes a group of people.

The word itself does not imply that such people cannot recognize truth, receive revelation, or understand a divine message.

The Quran presents a hypothetical situation concerning the message's bearer and the audience's response.

The continuation concerns that audience's failure to believe, not a universal judgment on all non-Arab peoples.

الْأَعْجَمِينَ should therefore not be translated as “ignorant people” or “people incapable of understanding.”

Those renderings confuse ethnicity or language with judgments about knowledge and intellect.

Being a non-Arabic speaker does not mean being unable to think or recognize truth.

C. أَعْجَمِيًّا: a hypothetically non-Arabic Quran

وَلَوْ جَعَلْنَاهُ قُرْآنًا أَعْجَمِيًّا لَقَالُوا لَوْلَا فُصِّلَتْ آيَاتُهُ ۖ أَأَعْجَمِيٌّ وَعَرَبِيٌّ ۗ قُلْ هُوَ لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا هُدًى وَشِفَاءٌ ۖ وَالَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ فِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرٌ وَهُوَ عَلَيْهِمْ عَمًى ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ يُنَادَوْنَ مِنْ مَكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ

wa-law jaʿalnāhu qurʾānan aʿjamiyyan la-qālū lawlā fuṣṣilat āyātuhu a-aʿjamiyyun wa-ʿarabiyyun qul huwa li-lladhīna āmanū hudan wa-shifāʾun wa-lladhīna lā yuʾminūna fī ādhānihim waqrun wa-huwa ʿalayhim ʿaman ulāʾika yunādawna min makānin baʿīd

Fuṣṣilat 41:44

And had We made it a non-Arabic Quran, they would have said, “Why have its verses not been clearly set out? Non-Arabic and Arabic?” Say, “For those who believe, it is guidance and healing.” As for those who do not believe, there is heaviness in their ears, and it is blindness to them. Those are called from a distant place.

Fuṣṣilat 41:44

The root occurs twice in this verse.

The first occurrence, أَعْجَمِيًّا, modifies قُرْآنًا.

The second, أَأَعْجَمِيٌّ, occurs in the question أَأَعْجَمِيٌّ وَعَرَبِيٌّ.

قُرْآنًا أَعْجَمِيًّا: a non-Arabic text

The verse begins with The God presenting a hypothetical Quran in a non-Arabic language.

أَعْجَمِيًّا modifies قُرْآنًا and concerns the message's language.

The text itself is therefore hypothetically non-Arabic.

This differs from Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:198.

There the message is hypothetically revealed to a non-Arabic speaker.

In Fuṣṣilat, the Quran itself is hypothetically in a non-Arabic language.

One hypothesis concerns the recipient, the other the message's language.

This distinction shows why the derived forms and what they describe must be examined separately.

لَوْلَا فُصِّلَتْ آيَاتُهُ: a demand for clear verses

The continuation says that if the Quran were non-Arabic, the opponents would ask why its verses had not been clearly set out.

فُصِّلَتْ comes from ف ص ل and concerns distinguishing parts and setting something out clearly or in detail.

In this context, the hypothetical objection concerns expression and intelligibility.

فُصِّلَتْ does not lexically mean “made Arabic”; here, however, clarification can contextually imply expression in Arabic so that the audience can understand it.

A text can be clearly explained in any language, just as familiar language does not guarantee sufficient explanation.

Thus the language of the message and the clarity and detail of its exposition are distinguishable, though connected in this objection.

أَأَعْجَمِيٌّ وَعَرَبِيٌّ: message and audience

This is a brief question contrasting two descriptions.

أَعْجَمِيٌّ indicates non-Arabic language.

عَرَبِيٌّ is the contrasting description.

The nouns to which the descriptions apply are not explicitly repeated.

Different grammatical explanations have consequently been offered.

One interpretation understands a non-Arabic Quran in contrast with an Arabic-speaking Prophet or audience.

The question then concerns a mismatch between the message's language and those to whom it is recited.

Another explanation emphasizes the objection to non-Arabic speech where an Arabic message is expected.

These explanations share a contrast between the message's non-Arabic language and the Arabic-speaking audience or bearer.

A basic translation can therefore retain the brief question:

“Non-Arabic and Arabic?”

An explanation can then identify the difference between the message's language and that of its audience or bearer.

Words such as “Prophet” or “audience” should not be presented as directly expressed Arabic nouns without marking their explanatory role.

The interrogative hamzah and the word's hamzah

أَأَعْجَمِيٌّ begins with two hamzahs in its grammatical analysis.

One is interrogative.

The other belongs to the derived word أَعْجَمِيّ.

This matters morphologically: neither hamzah belongs to the three root consonants ع ج م; one marks interrogation and the other belongs to the derived formation.

Recitations differ in how the two hamzahs are pronounced; the interrogative construction and contrast remain clear in the common reading followed here.

In all cases, أَعْجَمِيّ belongs to ع ج م; the interrogative hamzah must not be added to the root consonants.

Does non-Arabic language prevent guidance?

Fuṣṣilat 41:44 continues by describing the Quran as guidance and healing for believers.

In contrast, unbelievers are described through heaviness in their ears and blindness toward the message.

This matters because reception is not reduced to language alone.

A person may hear words in their own language and understand them, yet turn away from accepting the truth expressed.

Another may not speak the language initially, yet understand and consider the message after learning it or receiving a clear translation.

Thus understanding words, recognizing a message, and accepting it are distinct.

Intelligible language makes reception possible but does not by itself guarantee belief or acceptance of truth.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:198 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:199 also show this distinction: changing the hypothetical recipient is not said to make the opponents believe.

The Quran's Arabic language and its audience

Ibrāhīm 14:4 says that each Messenger was sent in the language of his people to explain to them.

The verse connects the message's language with the audience's opportunity to understand.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:195 describes the Quran's language as عَرَبِيٍّ مُبِينٍ.

Fuṣṣilat 41:3 describes an Arabic Quran whose verses are clearly set out.

Together, these verses connect its Arabic language with communicating to its initial audience.

This does not mean that only Arabic speakers can understand its truth or that other peoples cannot receive divine guidance.

Language conveys meaning.

Someone unfamiliar with Arabic needs language learning or translation to understand an Arabic text, just as an Arabic speaker needs knowledge of another language to understand a text in it.

Linguistic differences must therefore not be confused with inherent ethnic or racial superiority.

4. Comparison with related roots

ع ج م and ع ر ب: unfamiliar speech and Arabic

Quranic عَرَبِيّ identifies Arabic.

أَعْجَمِيّ in the passages discussed identifies non-Arabic language.

They stand in contrast in these Quranic contexts.

That contrast does not mean all Arabic speech is clear to everyone or all non-Arabic speech inherently unintelligible.

Arabic can be intelligible to an Arabic speaker yet unintelligible to someone unfamiliar with it.

Conversely, Persian is intelligible to Persian speakers, although not necessarily to listeners unfamiliar with it.

The name of a language must therefore be distinguished from a listener's ability to understand it.

ع ر ب also has other lexical branches that require independent study; they should not all be derived from its contrast with ع ج م.

ع ج م and ب ي ن: obscurity and making meaning clear

ب ي ن concerns separation, becoming evident, and clarification.

بَيَان can mean clarifying meaning and removing ambiguity.

The linguistic branch of عُجْمَة, by contrast, concerns obscurity and difficulty understanding speech.

Al-Naḥl 16:103 contrasts أَعْجَمِيٌّ with عَرَبِيٌّ مُبِينٌ.

بَيَان, however, is not simply the name of a language.

Someone can speak Arabic while remaining obscure in organization or explanation.

Someone speaking another language can explain something very clearly to an audience familiar with it.

Thus obscurity and clarification contrast in expression, but being Arabic and being clear are distinct qualities.

ع ج م and ف ص ح: unclear expression and fluency

ف ص ح concerns clear, fluent expression.

فَصِيح describes someone who expresses speech clearly and fluently.

In one established sense, أَعْجَم describes someone whose expression lacks that clarity and fluency.

This distinction particularly concerns speaking ability.

A person of Arab ancestry may have difficulty expressing themselves in Arabic.

A non-Arab may learn Arabic well enough to speak clearly and fluently.

Fluency is therefore not identical with ethnicity.

عَجَمِيّ and “inarticulate” should not be treated as synonyms in every context.

ع ج م and ن ط ق: unintelligible expression and speech

ن ط ق concerns speaking and verbal expression.

أَعْجَم can sometimes refer to someone who does not speak or cannot express themselves clearly.

Speaking and speaking clearly are different matters.

Someone may speak without the listener understanding their intention.

Conversely, someone physically unable to speak may communicate clearly through writing or other signs.

Inability to speak orally must not be equated with inability to think or to communicate by any means.

Quranic أَعْجَمِيّ concerns language and expression, not a judgment about a person's entire cognitive capacity.

ع ج م and ع ض ض: biting and testing

عَضَّ means to bite.

The material branch of عَجَمَ also concerns biting or pressing with the teeth.

In the established expression عَجَمَ الْعُودَ, however, testing wood's hardness or softness is also involved.

Biting is then a means of discovering its quality.

This usage can extend to testing and experience.

Not every bite is a test, and not every test is performed with the teeth.

The testing sense must not be transferred to the Quranic references to non-Arabic language.

ع ج م and خ ب ر: testing and knowledge

One branch of خ ب ر concerns knowledge and experience gained through examination.

خَبَرَ الشَّيْءَ can mean testing something and coming to know it.

عَجَمَ in testing expressions, such as testing wood with the teeth, likewise concerns discovering quality.

The physical image of biting to assess hardness is established for عَجَمَ, whereas examination and experience have a wider scope.

عَجَمْتُ الْعُودَ can therefore be rendered “I tested the wood with my teeth.”

Quranic أَعْجَمِيّ does not belong to this branch of testing and knowledge.

5. Morphological and semantic points

عَجَمِيّ and أَعْجَمِيّ

Arabic lexicons describe a recognized distinction between these forms.

عَجَمِيّ more particularly concerns affiliation with non-Arab peoples.

أَعْجَمِيّ can particularly describe language, speech, or a person whose speech is unclear to Arabic speakers.

Nevertheless, their actual uses have not been wholly separate.

عَجَمِيّ without the initial hamzah does not occur in the Quran.

The Quranic form أَعْجَمِيّ occurs three times in linguistic contexts.

The actual form should therefore be preserved when examining the Quranic text.

الْأَعْجَمِينَ in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:198

الْأَعْجَمِينَ is genitive as the second member of the construction with بَعْضِ, marked by yāʾ in the manner of the sound masculine plural. Morphological accounts explain it through أَعْجَمِيّ with the relational yāʾ omitted, or as a non-regular plural of أَعْجَم; it should not be presented as an unproblematic regular formation from أَعْجَم.

Here it identifies the group from which the hypothetical non-Arabic-speaking recipient comes.

Its plural form does not mean the Quran is hypothetically revealed to that entire group.

بَعْضِ limits the scope of the hypothesis.

“Upon one of the non-Arabic speakers” fits the construction and context.

الْأَعْجَمِينَ should not be restricted to people without speech: the passage hypothetically concerns revelation to a non-Arabic speaker.

Language and cognitive ability

A possible misreading turns linguistic difference into a judgment about intellectual capacity.

Quranic أَعْجَمِيّ describes language or linguistic affiliation.

It does not inherently mean ignorant, unintelligent, uncultured, or incapable of recognizing truth.

Nor does عَرَبِيّ inherently mean more knowledgeable, righteous, or morally superior.

The passages about the Quran's language show the importance of intelligibility to an audience.

The response after understanding, however, is a separate question.

One person may understand and accept a statement; another may understand the same statement and reject it.

Fuṣṣilat 41:44 describes differing responses through guidance and healing on one side, and heaviness of hearing and blindness on the other.

Thus unfamiliarity with a message's language is not the same as knowingly refusing it.

Later technical terms and Quranic meaning

مُعْجَم, إِعْجَام, and حُرُوفُ الْمُعْجَم became established terms in lexicography and writing.

These terms do not occur in the Quran.

Letter-dotting belongs lexically to this family but cannot determine the meaning of أَعْجَمِيّ in these verses.

The Quranic discussion concerns language, expression, and the relation between message and audience.

Later technical senses involving dotting or dictionary compilation should therefore not be imposed on these passages.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For this root, distinguish language and expression, testing, and hard fruit stones or seeds.

عُجْمَة: unclear expression; difficulty expressing or understanding speech.

أَعْجَم: someone unable to speak clearly; in some contexts, someone whose language is unintelligible to the audience.

أَعْجَمِيّ: non-Arabic; in Quranic usage, describing a non-Arabic language or speaker.

عَجَمِيّ: affiliated with non-Arab peoples.

عَجَم and عُجْم: non-Arab peoples; in particular contexts, Iranians.

عَجْمَاء: an animal without human speech, or a creature without clear verbal expression.

عَجَمَ الشَّيْءَ: bit it; tested its hardness or softness with the teeth.

عَجَمَ فُلَانًا: tested someone and discovered their qualities.

عَجَم: in another material branch, fruit stones or seeds.

أَعْجَمَ الْكِتَابَ: in writing, removed ambiguity by adding distinguishing dots.

اسْتَعْجَمَ: became obscure or unintelligible; in another use, fell silent.

Al-Naḥl 16:103

For أَعْجَمِيٌّ:

“Is non-Arabic.”

In the complete phrase:

“The language of the one to whom they allude is non-Arabic, while this is a clear Arabic tongue.”

Here the word describes language, making “non-Arabic” more precise than simply “unintelligible.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:198

For الْأَعْجَمِينَ:

“Non-Arabic speakers.”

Verse translation:

“And had We sent it down upon one of the non-Arabic speakers,”

This preserves the linguistic sense without restricting the word to a particular people or nationality.

Fuṣṣilat 41:44

For قُرْآنًا أَعْجَمِيًّا:

“A non-Arabic Quran.”

For أَأَعْجَمِيٌّ وَعَرَبِيٌّ:

“Non-Arabic and Arabic?”

The explanation identifies the contrast between the message's non-Arabic language and its Arabic-speaking audience or bearer.

Meaning here: “Non-Arabic?” in the question contrasting the message's language with its Arabic-speaking audience or bearer.

7. Conclusion

This Arabic root has several semantic branches, particularly unclear speech or difficulty understanding language, biting and testing hardness, and hard fruit stones or seeds.

In the poetry attributed to ʿAntarah, أَعْجَم describes someone unable to express himself clearly. In this transmitted pre-Islamic poetic usage, it describes expression rather than merely naming an ethnic group.

In the verse attributed to Al-Nābighah, يَعْجُمُ means biting or pressing with the teeth. Arabic also records testing wood's hardness and, figuratively, discovering a person's qualities.

The family should not be reduced to one narrow sense. Language and expression differ from biting and fruit stones; the historical priority of these branches is not established by the evidence considered here.

Linguistically, عَجَمِيّ more particularly concerns affiliation with non-Arab peoples, whereas أَعْجَمِيّ concerns unclear expression or non-Arabic language. Lexicons recognize this distinction, though the usages sometimes overlap.

The root occurs four times in the Quran, always concerning language and expression.

Al-Naḥl 16:103 contrasts the non-Arabic language of the alleged human teacher with the Quran's clear Arabic language.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:198 imagines revelation to a non-Arabic speaker; Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:199 says the audience would still not believe.

Fuṣṣilat 41:44 imagines the Quran itself as non-Arabic, alongside an objection about clear exposition and a question contrasting its language with its Arabic-speaking audience or bearer.

These three verses distinguish the message's language, the language of its recipient or bearer, and the audience's response.

A non-Arabic language is not unintelligible to everyone. Any language can be clear to those who know it.

Nor does the Quran's Arabic language ensure that every Arabic speaker accepts its truth. The text distinguishes opportunity to understand from human response.

Quranic أَعْجَمِيّ should therefore not be rendered as ignorance, intellectual incapacity, or ethnic inferiority. These verses concern language and expression rather than inherent human worth.

Final definition: In its linguistic branch, ع ج م concerns obscurity or difficulty in expressing and understanding speech. In cross-language contexts, it describes language or speech that is non-Arabic and unfamiliar to an Arabic-speaking audience. Other branches concern biting, testing hardness, and hard fruit stones or seeds. Quranic أَعْجَمِيّ and الْأَعْجَمِينَ concern non-Arabic language and speakers, without making linguistic difference a judgment about human worth, knowledge, or cognitive ability.
