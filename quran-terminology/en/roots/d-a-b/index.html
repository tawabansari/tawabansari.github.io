Continuity and persistence in action, diligence, established habits, and recurring conduct and outcomes among peoples in the Qur’an

In Arabic, D ʾ B concerns continuing an action, adhering to it, and persisting in conduct or movement. Two major uses belong to this field: sustained effort and activity, and a habit or practice that repetition makes characteristic of a person or group.

Someone who works persistently can be called dāʾib. Likewise, when a behavior recurs and becomes someone’s usual practice, Arabic can call it that person’s daʾb.

The family’s semantic core is explained through adherence and continuity:

الدَّالُ وَالْهَمْزَةُ وَالْبَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى مُلَازَمَةٍ وَدَوَامٍ

al-dālu wa-al-hamzatu wa-al-bāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā mulāzamatin wa-dawāmin

“Dāl, hamzah, and bāʾ form one root indicating adherence and continuity.”

Habit and customary practice also belong to this family. Daʾaba describes someone who works earnestly and diligently.

In the Qur’an, the root concerns sustained cultivation, the continuing activity of the sun and moon, and comparisons with earlier peoples’ conduct and fate.

Daʾb is not inherently good or bad. People can persist in beneficial work or continue wrongful conduct. Evaluation follows the action and context, rather than the root alone.

1. Qur’anic count and forms

D ʾ B occurs six times in six verses across five surahs.

VerseFormApproximate contextual meaning
Āl ʿImrān 3:11كَدَأْبِLike a practice and precedent
Al-Anfāl 8:52كَدَأْبِLike a pattern of conduct
Al-Anfāl 8:54كَدَأْبِLike a practice and its outcome
Yūsuf 12:47دَأَبًاSteadily and persistently
Ibrāhīm 14:33دَائِبَيْنِBoth continuing in movement or activity
Ghāfir 40:31دَأْبِPractice and fate

The morphological count includes four occurrences of the noun daʾb, one daʾaban with reported differences in the hamzah’s vowel, and one dual active participle, dāʾibayn.

The basic verb daʾaba, the verbal noun duʾūb, and forms such as daʾūb and idʾāb do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. The basic meaning in early Arabic

Adherence and continuity: the semantic core

Placing adherence and continuity at the center of the root highlights two features.

First, an activity is not confined to one occasion; a person or being continues it.

Second, continuity through repetition can become a customary practice and a recognizable characteristic.

A farmer who continues working during the growing season persists in that activity. If it becomes the farmer’s customary way of life, it can also be described as that person’s daʾb.

The two meanings are not identical. Sustained effort focuses on the activity itself; habit emphasizes repeated behavior becoming established over time.

One lexical explanation expresses the relationship as follows:

الدَّأْبُ، أَصْلُهُ مِنْ دَأَبْتُ، إِلَّا أَنَّ الْعَرَبَ حَوَّلَتْ مَعْنَاهُ إِلَى الشَّأْنِ

al-daʾbu aṣluhu min daʾabtu illā anna al-ʿaraba ḥawwalat maʿnāhu ilā al-shaʾni

“Daʾb originally comes from daʾabtu, but the Arabs extended its meaning to someone’s characteristic condition or affair.”

Al-daʾb is also explained through al-ʿādah, habit, and al-mulāzamah, adherence.

Must daʾb involve hardship and fatigue?

Early Arabic explains daʾaba fī ʿamalihi through jadda wa-taʿiba: he worked earnestly and underwent toil.

Effort and enduring difficulty are therefore genuine uses of the root.

Fatigue is not, however, essential to every derivative of D ʾ B.

When the Qur’an describes the sun and moon as dāʾibayn, it concerns continuing activity, rather than their becoming tired like humans.

Daʾb as habit can also concern conduct that requires no strenuous physical work.

The central meaning is adherence and continuity. Intense effort and fatigue can arise from the circumstances of an activity or its sentence’s context.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Daʾaba yadʾabu: working persistently

Daʾaba is a basic triliteral verb.

Daʾaba fī ʿamalihi describes someone showing diligence and persistence in work.

Daʾabati al-dābbatu fī sayrihā describes an animal continuing its movement.

The verbal nouns daʾb, daʾab, and duʾūb are attested from this verb.

Daʾaba al-amru is also attested for an affair becoming prolonged or continuing.

Continuity can therefore concern an event, rather than only voluntary human activity.

Daʾb and daʾab: habit and customary practice

Daʾb with a vowelless hamzah and daʾab with an a vowel are attested for habit, practice, and characteristic condition.

Hādhā daʾbuka means “This is your customary way” or “This is what you continually do.”

Daʾb is not simply a mental characteristic or inward belief here. It concerns a pattern recognized in someone’s conduct or condition.

In some contexts, the noun also acquires the broader sense of condition or fate.

This extension matters in verses about earlier peoples.

Duʾūb and daʾūb: persistence and sustained effort

Duʾūb is a verbal noun denoting persistence and effort in work.

Daʾūb describes someone diligent and persistent in an activity.

The latter is also known in Persian as doʾūb, commonly expressing sustained effort and pursuit.

It must not always be restricted to commendable hard work. Someone can also persist in a wrongful activity.

The object of the work establishes its value.

Dāʾib: someone or something continuing steadily

Dāʾib is the active participle of the basic triliteral verb.

It describes someone persisting in work or movement.

Dāʾibayn is its accusative or genitive dual form, used in the Qur’an for the sun and moon.

The dual indicates two beings sharing the description. Their continuity comes from the word’s meaning and context, rather than duality itself.

Adʾaba and idʾāb: making another persist in work

Adʾaba is Form IV.

In adʾaba al-rajulu dābbatahu, a person makes an animal continue or intensify its movement and activity.

Unlike daʾabati al-dābbatu, another agent brings about the continued activity.

In some uses, the resulting pressure tires the animal.

The verbal noun is idʾāb.

The morphological distinction matters: daʾaba can describe the subject’s own persistence, whereas adʾaba can describe causing another’s activity to continue or intensify.

Al-dāʾibān: night and day

Early Arabic also uses al-dāʾibān for night and day.

The basis is their continuing succession: night and day keep following one another.

Night and day do not literally work like humans. Their recurring succession provides the basis for dāʾib.

Al-dāʾibān is used in precisely this sense for the succession of night and day.

4. Early Arabic witnesses and poetry

Daʾb as habit in Imruʾ al-Qays’ Muʿallaqah

An important pre-Islamic witness occurs in Imruʾ al-Qays’ Muʿallaqah:

كَدَأْبِكَ مِنْ أُمِّ الْحُوَيْرِثِ قَبْلَهَا
وَجَارَتِهَا أُمِّ الرَّبَابِ بِمَأْسَلِ

ka-daʾbika min ummi al-ḥuwayrithi qablahā / wa-jāratihā ummi al-rabābi bi-maʾsali

A rendering close to the sense:

“As was your earlier habit concerning Umm al-Ḥuwayrith and her neighbor Umm al-Rabāb at Maʾsal.”

Ka-daʾbika refers to the addressee’s recognizable and recurring conduct.

The expression is explained through habitual attachment, sorrow, and tears over separation from a beloved.

The poet is not describing strenuous physical work. Daʾb concerns the person’s usual condition and conduct.

The verse thus attests habit and customary practice in pre-Islamic Arabic poetry.

Some transmissions of the Muʿallaqah have ka-dīnika rather than ka-daʾbika. Both forms have been explained as habit in this context.

The variant must be acknowledged. The familiar ka-daʾbika transmission and its early explanations nevertheless provide valid evidence of the root’s use.

Dāʾib in an early rajaz verse

Lexical transmissions preserve this rajaz verse:

رَاحَتْ كَمَا رَاحَ أَبُو رِئَالِ
قَاهِي الْفُؤَادِ دَائِبُ الْإِجْفَالِ

rāḥat kamā rāḥa abū riʾāli / qāhī al-fuʾādi dāʾibu al-ijfāli

“It went as Abū Riʾāl went, keen of heart and ceaseless in swift flight.”

Dāʾibu al-ijfāl describes someone or something continually moving away swiftly.

Its significance is persistence in movement.

A significant textual difference also exists: daʾibu al-ijfāl is transmitted too, whereas the witness above reads dāʾib.

Since the poet and date are not identified in the transmission examined here, it should not confidently be presented as pre-Islamic poetry.

It helps establish dāʾib in the lexical tradition, but does not have the same value for dating the word as the clear Muʿallaqah witness.

Driving an animal forcefully and causing fatigue

A poetic fragment with dhī daʾabin shirwāṭi is also transmitted among lexical witnesses.

Daʾb in this use is explained as forceful driving and pursuit.

Another transmission of the same fragment has dhī zajalin instead of dhī daʾabin.

This difference prevents assigning forceful driving as the independent, certain meaning of every use of the root on this fragment alone.

The sense is better considered within the familiar field of sustained effort and movement.

What the witnesses establish

Imruʾ al-Qays’ poetry is an important witness for habit and recurring conduct. Rajaz verses in the lexical tradition also illustrate continuing movement and activity.

The latter witnesses have textual or attribution differences and should not be given greater authority than the evidence supports.

The relationship between sustained action and its becoming habitual is clear. The root’s uses must nevertheless not be confined to exhausting labor.

5. Daʾaban in Yūsuf 12:47: sustained cultivation

Yūsuf 12:47

قَالَ تَزْرَعُونَ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ دَأَبًا فَمَا حَصَدْتُمْ فَذَرُوهُ فِي سُنْبُلِهِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِمَّا تَأْكُلُونَ

qāla tazraʿūna sabʿa sinīna daʾaban fa-mā ḥaṣadtum fa-dharūhu fī sunbulihi illā qalīlan mimmā taʾkulūna

Forqan’s installed translation:

He said, “You will sow for seven years steadily. Leave what you harvest in its ear, except a little of what you eat.”

The translation fits the semantic field. Steadily appropriately renders daʾaban here.

Morphology and syntax

Daʾaban is an accusative verbal noun from D ʾ B.

In Ḥafṣ’s reading from ʿĀṣim, the hamzah has an a vowel: daʾaban.

Many other readings have a vowelless hamzah: daʾban.

A reduced dāban without hamzah is also reported in some readings.

These differences do not fundamentally change the meaning of continuity and persistence.

Grammatical explanations propose several analyses of its accusative role.

One takes it as a verbal noun related to the meaning of tazraʿūna: you cultivate steadily and persist in cultivation.

Another explains daʾaban as a circumstantial expression: while working steadily and diligently.

Understanding it as according to your customary agricultural practice is also proposed.

These are differences in sentence analysis, all related to continuing activity.

Do seven continuous years mean working without rest?

No.

Daʾaban describes continuity of agricultural activity through the seven-year period.

It does not necessarily mean working every moment of the day and night or every day of the year without rest.

Agriculture involves seasons, cultivation, tending, and harvesting. People can persist in it over several years without spending every moment on physical work.

The word itself also does not specify the number of plantings per year or farmers’ daily schedules.

Daʾaban and Joseph’s storage plan

The verse must be read within the king’s dream and Joseph’s interpretation.

Responding to the question about seven fat cows and seven green ears of grain, Joseph speaks of seven years of cultivation.

He then instructs them to leave the harvested crop in its ears, except for a small amount consumed as food.

Yūsuf 12:48 describes seven difficult years consuming what was stored beforehand.

Sustained cultivation is therefore connected here with preparing for the future and preserving produce.

Daʾb alone does not mean economic planning, saving, or crisis management.

Those concepts emerge from Joseph’s combined instructions and the relationship between the agricultural years and the difficult years.

Nor should his speech be reduced to a passive prediction. Joseph provides practical guidance for responding to the future he describes.

Tazraʿūna is an imperfect verb, while the following fa-dharūhu explicitly commands preserving the crop.

The context combines awareness of the future with responsible action while resources are available.

6. Dāʾibayn in Ibrāhīm 14:33: the sun and moon’s continuing activity

Ibrāhīm 14:33

وَسَخَّرَ لَكُمُ الشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ دَائِبَيْنِ وَسَخَّرَ لَكُمُ اللَّيْلَ وَالنَّهَارَ

wa-sakhkhara lakumu al-shamsa wa-al-qamara dāʾibayni wa-sakhkhara lakumu al-layla wa-al-nahāra

A rendering close to the wording:

“He subjected the sun and moon to you, both continuing in movement, and subjected night and day to you.”

Morphology

Dāʾibayn is the accusative dual active participle of dāʾib.

It is circumstantial in the sentence, describing the condition of both the sun and moon.

Dāʾib here denotes continuity of movement or activity.

Idāmat al-sayr, continuing movement, appropriately explains this use.

Do the sun and moon become tired?

The verse does not convey that meaning.

Dāʾibayn denotes continuing movement or activity, rather than physical fatigue.

Fatigue can form part of a description of a person working persistently, but that human feature is not essential when describing heavenly bodies.

Sakhkhara lakum also describes the sun and moon within an arrangement beneficial to human life.

Dāʾibayn itself does not mean beneficial. That aspect arises from sakhkhara and the context of creation’s blessings.

Continuing movement and astronomy

Dāʾibayn does not specify a detailed model of orbits, their centers, or the solar system’s physical structure.

The word alone neither establishes a geocentric model nor should be forced into an account of every modern astronomical finding.

The sun’s apparent movement across the sky is a human observation; scientific explanations of the movements of earth, sun, and moon belong to astronomy.

The lexical meaning remains continuity of movement or activity.

Relationship to Yā Sīn 36:38, Yā Sīn 36:39, and Yā Sīn 36:40

Yā Sīn 36:38 reads:

وَالشَّمْسُ تَجْرِي لِمُسْتَقَرٍّ لَهَا

wa-al-shamsu tajrī li-mustaqarrin lahā

“The sun runs toward a resting place of its own.”

Yā Sīn 36:40 also reads:

وَكُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ

wa-kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūna

“Each swims in an orbit.”

These verses can be read alongside Ibrāhīm 14:33 in studying the Qur’anic vocabulary of heavenly movement.

Jarayān, sibāḥah, falak, and daʾb are four independent lexical concepts.

Daʾb emphasizes continuity here; the nature of movement and its course follow from other words and their context.

7. Ka-daʾbi āli firʿawn: recurring practice or fate?

Three verses use ka-daʾbi āli firʿawn:

  • Āl ʿImrān 3:11
  • Al-Anfāl 8:52
  • Al-Anfāl 8:54

Daʾb occurs within a comparison in all three.

Ka is the comparative particle, while daʾbi is a genitive noun forming a possessive construction.

Āli firʿawn follows as its genitive complement.

The construction consequently means like the daʾb of Pharaoh’s people.

The rest of each verse clarifies the precise comparison.

Āl ʿImrān 3:11

كَدَأْبِ آلِ فِرْعَوْنَ وَالَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا فَأَخَذَهُمُ اللَّهُ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ وَاللَّهُ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

ka-daʾbi āli firʿawna wa-alladhīna min qablihim kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā fa-akhadhahumu allāhu bi-dhunūbihim wa-allāhu shadīdu al-ʿiqābi

“Like the practice and precedent of Pharaoh’s people and those before them: they denied Our signs, so The God seized them for their sins. The God is severe in punishment.”

Two elements are connected: denying the signs and experiencing the consequences of sins.

Daʾb can concern earlier peoples’ pattern of conduct while the complete comparison also includes its outcome.

Explanations of daʾb here use sunnah, customary way, or ʿamal, action.

Another reading emphasizes peoples’ persistence and exertion in rejection and opposition.

Al-Anfāl 8:52

كَدَأْبِ آلِ فِرْعَوْنَ وَالَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ كَفَرُوا بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ فَأَخَذَهُمُ اللَّهُ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ قَوِيٌّ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

ka-daʾbi āli firʿawna wa-alladhīna min qablihim kafarū bi-āyāti allāhi fa-akhadhahumu allāhu bi-dhunūbihim inna allāha qawiyyun shadīdu al-ʿiqābi

“Like the way of Pharaoh’s people and those before them: they disbelieved in the signs of The God, so The God seized them for their sins. The God is Strong, severe in punishment.”

The construction closely resembles Āl ʿImrān 3:11.

Here, however, kafarū bi-āyāti allāhi appears, while Āl ʿImrān 3:11 has kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā.

This difference must be preserved.

Rejection and treating something as false can be related in different contexts, but must not be made completely synonymous without separate study.

Daʾb in both verses concerns recurring conduct and established practice, rather than itself meaning disbelief or denial.

The expression recalls earlier peoples’ conduct and practices toward the messengers.

Al-Anfāl 8:54

كَدَأْبِ آلِ فِرْعَوْنَ وَالَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِ رَبِّهِمْ فَأَهْلَكْنَاهُمْ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ وَأَغْرَقْنَا آلَ فِرْعَوْنَ وَكُلٌّ كَانُوا ظَالِمِينَ

ka-daʾbi āli firʿawna wa-alladhīna min qablihim kadhdhabū bi-āyāti rabbihim fa-ahlaknāhum bi-dhunūbihim wa-aghraqnā āla firʿawna wa-kullun kānū ẓālimīna

“Like the way and fate of Pharaoh’s people and those before them: they denied their Lord’s signs, so We destroyed them for their sins and drowned Pharaoh’s people. All were unjust.”

The peoples’ outcome is more prominent here.

After describing denial, the verse mentions their destruction and the drowning of Pharaoh’s people.

Rendering daʾb as fate or precedent can therefore be contextually defensible.

Fate is nevertheless broader than the semantic core, encompassing conduct together with its result.

Practice or customary way is closer to the root in a more direct translation. The reflection can explain where that practice led.

Why does the expression occur twice in Al-Anfāl?

Between Al-Anfāl 8:52 and Al-Anfāl 8:54 stands Al-Anfāl 8:53:

ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّ اللَّهَ لَمْ يَكُ مُغَيِّرًا نِعْمَةً أَنْعَمَهَا عَلَىٰ قَوْمٍ حَتَّىٰ يُغَيِّرُوا مَا بِأَنْفُسِهِمْ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

dhālika bi-anna allāha lam yaku mughayyiran niʿmatan anʿamahā ʿalā qawmin ḥattā yughayyirū mā bi-anfusihim wa-anna allāha samīʿun ʿalīmun

“That is because The God does not change a blessing He has bestowed on a people until they change what is within themselves. The God is Hearing, Knowing.”

The verse highlights a relationship between human conduct and changing circumstances.

Al-Anfāl 8:52 recalls earlier peoples’ conduct and its consequence.

Al-Anfāl 8:53 states a principle concerning changed blessings and a people’s inner change.

Al-Anfāl 8:54 repeats the pattern with emphasis on denial, destruction, and injustice.

This continuity matters for understanding the verses.

Ka-daʾbi āli firʿawn does more than repeat a historical name. It places human conduct and its outcome within a relationship to be examined.

Daʾb alone does not mean a law of recompense or divine punishment. The relationship between conduct and consequences arises from all three verses together.

Nor do these verses establish that every hardship or calamity affecting a society necessarily punishes that society’s sin. Such a generalization requires independent evidence and consideration of the wider verses on trials, hardship, and human responsibility.

8. Daʾbi qawmi nūḥ in Ghāfir 40:31

Ghāfir 40:31

مِثْلَ دَأْبِ قَوْمِ نُوحٍ وَعَادٍ وَثَمُودَ وَالَّذِينَ مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ وَمَا اللَّهُ يُرِيدُ ظُلْمًا لِلْعِبَادِ

mithla daʾbi qawmi nūḥin wa-ʿādin wa-thamūda wa-alladhīna min baʿdihim wa-mā allāhu yurīdu ẓulman li-al-ʿibādi

Forqan’s existing translation:

“Like the fate of the people of Noah, Ad, Thamud, and those after them. The God does not desire injustice for His servants.”

Daʾb occurs in the speech of a believing man from Pharaoh’s people.

In Ghāfir 40:30 he warns his people that he fears a fate for them like the day of earlier groups.

He then recalls Noah’s people, Ad, Thamud, and those who followed.

Daʾb can concern their practice and condition, but the warning also brings their conduct’s outcome into the comparison.

Two early explanations are proposed:

Mithla ḥāli: like their condition.

Mithla mā aṣābahum: like what befell them.

The difference shows how the expression can concern both peoples’ condition and conduct and their fate within a warning.

A people’s daʾb and divine sunnah

Some interpretations also explain daʾbi qawmi nūḥ through The God’s way of dealing with those people.

That interpretation attends to their conduct’s result, but grammatically daʾb is attached to Noah’s people.

Directly translating it as The God’s way of punishing is therefore an additional interpretive step.

The literal practice and condition of the people should first be preserved; the reflection can then explain the conduct’s consequence.

The verse’s ending is explicit:

وَمَا اللَّهُ يُرِيدُ ظُلْمًا لِلْعِبَادِ — Wa-mā allāhu yurīdu ẓulman li-al-ʿibād: “The God does not desire injustice for His servants.”

It must remain part of the analysis, framing the warning about earlier peoples within the denial of divine injustice.

9. Distinctions from related roots

D ʾ B and D W M: persistence and continuity

Dāma belongs to D W M and concerns continuing or remaining established.

Daʾb is explained through continuity and adherence, but the roots are distinct.

D ʾ B emphasizes persistence in action and its becoming customary.

D W M can concern a condition remaining or an affair continuing without necessarily involving effort or activity.

This distinction is not absolute; the families overlap in some uses.

Study of D W M

D ʾ B and J H D: persistence and effort

Juhd and jahd concern ability, effort, and enduring difficulty.

Some uses of daʾb also involve intense effort.

Someone can exert great effort on a single occasion, however, while persistence can concern a quiet activity without strenuous physical pressure.

Effort and persistence are related but distinct concepts.

Study of J H D

D ʾ B and ʿ W D: habit and return

ʿĀda belongs to ʿ W D and concerns returning and recurrence.

ʿĀdah also denotes repeated conduct in Arabic.

Daʾb can mean habit, while also emphasizing adherence and sustained pursuit.

Hādhā daʾbuka concerns someone’s established practice; the verb ʿāda can describe a single return.

D ʾ B and S N N: practice and sunnah

Sunnah concerns a way or practice and can describe familiar human conduct or a divine pattern.

Daʾb in interpretations of the Pharaoh verses is sometimes explained through sunnah.

The two words are not equivalent in every use.

Daʾb also describes continuing tangible activity, as with dāʾibayn concerning the sun and moon.

Sunnah in an early explanation does not, by itself, mean the collections of reports or codified legal rules developed in later periods.

D ʾ B and D B B: sustained activity and creatures’ movement

The written distinction is especially significant.

D ʾ B includes hamzah and concerns continuity and adherence.

D B B contains one dāl and two bāʾ consonants, with no hamzah. It concerns moving and walking, particularly creatures moving on the earth.

Dābbah belongs to D B B.

Dāʾib, by contrast, is an active participle of D ʾ B.

Their visible resemblance does not give them the same root.

Dāʾibayn in Ibrāhīm 14:33 must therefore not be counted under the same root as dābbah or earthbound creatures.

Study of D B B

D ʾ B and D B R: persistence and directing affairs

Tadbīr belongs to D B R and concerns arranging and administering affairs in the Qur’an.

Someone may act both persistently and with planning, but those concepts differ.

Daʾb concerns continuity and adherence; tadbīr concerns organizing and administering work.

Joseph’s plan for preserving produce can be studied in the verse’s context, but daʾb alone should not become management and planning in translation.

Study of D B R

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

The brief root meaning and the meaning in the verse should be kept distinct for D ʾ B.

For Yūsuf 12:47, steadily is appropriate. Forqan’s existing translation preserves that meaning and needs no change.

For Ibrāhīm 14:33, both continuing in movement or activity is appropriate. Tirelessly can be a literary explanation, but continuously is more cautious in a precise translation.

For earlier peoples, practice and customary way are closer to the lexical meaning. Fate or precedent can also be defensible when the context combines a people’s conduct with its outcome.

The brief word-box meaning should nevertheless remain close to adherence and continuity; habit and customary practice, allowing readers to distinguish the lexical sense from the whole verse’s message.

11. Conclusion

In early Arabic, D ʾ B concerns adherence and continuity. Uses involving effort, movement, and habit can be examined within this semantic family.

Daʾaba describes continuing an action and exerting effort in it; dāʾib describes someone or something continuing in movement or activity; daʾb denotes habit and recognizable practice.

The established Muʿallaqah witness attests habitual and recurring conduct in pre-Islamic Arabic. Other witnesses also confirm continuing movement and activity.

The root occurs six times in the Qur’an.

Yūsuf 12:47 concerns persistence in cultivation over seven years.

Ibrāhīm 14:33 describes the sun and moon’s continuing movement or activity.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:11, Al-Anfāl 8:52, Al-Anfāl 8:54, and Ghāfir 40:31, daʾb compares earlier peoples’ conduct and fate.

Their recurring rejection and denial are connected with consequences, but the root itself does not mean disbelief, denial, injustice, or punishment.

Nor does effort in some uses mean daʾb always entails hardship and fatigue. Continuity can concern heavenly movement or familiar human conduct.

Proposed final definition for Forqan:

D ʾ B denotes adhering to an action and continuing it. Its uses include sustained effort, continuing movement, and habitual or recurring practice. Daʾaban in Yūsuf 12:47 concerns sustained cultivation; dāʾibayn in Ibrāhīm 14:33 concerns the sun and moon’s continuing movement; daʾb in verses about earlier peoples concerns their recognizable practice and condition, whose outcome is clarified by the context.
