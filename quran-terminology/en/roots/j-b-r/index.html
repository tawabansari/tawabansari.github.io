The Qur’anic Root J B R

Mending, firmness, power, and dominance: from repair and restoration to exercising power over others

The Arabic root J B R concerns repairing fractures and deficiencies, bringing separated parts together, firmness, power, and dominance. Its family includes jabr, repair or restoration; ijbār, compulsion; and jabbār, someone possessing power and dominance.

Restoration can be beneficial in everyday life: stabilizing a broken bone so it can reunite, or meeting the needs of someone suffering deprivation. Yet the power enabling repair and order can also be used to compel and dominate others.

In the Qur’an, this family occurs ten times, exclusively through jabbār. The description mostly concerns people whose power is accompanied by rebelliousness, imposing their will, or disregard for right. Once, the Qur’an calls The God al-Jabbār.

Understanding this difference requires first examining the root’s Arabic semantic field, then identifying whom jabbār describes in each verse, the situation, and its accompanying words.

1. Root forms and semantic core

Recognized forms include جَبَرَ (jabara), جَبْر (jabr), جَبَّرَ (jabbara), أَجْبَرَ (ajbara), إِجْبَار (ijbār), اِنْجَبَرَ (injabara), اِجْتَبَرَ (ijtabara), تَجَبَّرَ (tajabbara), جَابِر (jābir), جَبِيرَة (jabīrah), جَبَرُوت (jabarūt), and جَبَّار (jabbār).

These forms do not have identical meanings. Some emphasize repair and restoration, others firmness and power, and others compulsion and imposing one person’s will on another. Brief root meaning: repairing fractures and remedying deficiencies; firmness, power, dominance, and compulsion.

The following forms occur in the Qur’an:

  • جَبَّارًا (jabbāran): Maryam 19:14, Maryam 19:32, and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:19.
  • جَبَّارِينَ (jabbārīna): Al-Māʾidah 5:22 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130.
  • جَبَّارٍ (jabbārin) and بِجَبَّارٍ (bi-jabbārin): Hūd 11:59, Ibrāhīm 14:15, Ghāfir 40:35, and Qāf 50:45.
  • الْجَبَّارُ (al-Jabbāru): Al-Ḥashr 59:23.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: ten occurrences of jabbār

These are singular or plural forms of one description, differing in case and grammatical construction. The verb jabara, verbal noun jabr, and noun jābir do not occur in the Qur’anic text. Nor should the proper name جِبْرِيل (Jibrīl) be treated as a derivative of Arabic jabara merely because of shared letters; this study covers the ten occurrences of jabbār.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the root through greatness, elevation, and uprightness. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī begins from another angle in Al-Mufradāt: repairing something through a degree of force or overcoming.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: greatness, elevation, and uprightness

The explanations illuminate two recognized aspects of the family: firmness and power, and the act of repair together with the force used in it.

Neither alone completely defines every derivative. Meeting a poor person’s needs is not the same act as compelling someone against their wishes, although both belong to this family.

2. Physical imagery and early Arabic usage

Mending a broken bone

Mending a broken bone is one clear use of the root. Arabic says:

«جَبَرَ الْعَظْمَ»

jabara al-ʿaẓma

That is, he repaired or set the broken bone.

When the bone itself heals, forms such as جَبَرَ الْعَظْمُ (jabara al-ʿaẓmu) and injabara are used. The distinction is between performing a repair and the broken thing recovering.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: transitive and intransitive jabara

Jabīrah also names something used to stabilize a broken limb and help it reunite, such as wood or a firm device holding the fracture in place.

This image helps explain one important branch. A broken bone must be positioned appropriately to heal; an external force may hold it there. Such force serves repair rather than necessarily causing harm.

Its use for bones does not establish that every form originally referred to a broken bone. It is one clear application, not the necessary definition of every derivative.

Remedying deficiency and meeting need

Another application concerns remedying human deprivation.

جَبَرَ الْفَقِيرَ (jabara al-faqīra) describes meeting a poor person’s needs. جَبَرَ الْيَتِيمَ (jabara al-yatīma) is also recorded for caring for an orphan and relieving their need.

No fractured bone is involved here: another person’s help remedies a deficiency or injury in someone’s circumstances.

Dictionaries also record جَبَرَ اللَّهُ مُصِيبَتَهُ (jabara Allāhu muṣībatahu) for making good a misfortune: a loss is compensated for or replaced in some way.

These uses show that jabr can refer to repair, support, and meeting need without implying tyranny.

Compensation does not always restore the very thing lost. Sometimes something is repaired; sometimes a replacement or recompense is provided for loss or damage.

Evidence from early poetry

A fragment attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm reads:

«مَنْ عَالَ مِنَّا بَعْدَهَا فَلَا اجْتَبَرْ
وَلَا سَقَى مَاءً وَلَا رَاءَ الشَّجَرْ»

man ʿāla minnā baʿdahā fa-lā ijtabar / wa-lā saqā māʾan wa-lā rāʾa al-shajar

On the vocalization saqā in this version: “Whoever among us acts unjustly after that—may his injury never be repaired; may he give no water or see a tree.”

Lexical sources: giving water distinguished from drinking

Fa-lā ijtabar is an imprecation concerning recovery or restoration to well-being. In the second part, saqā means giving water, rather than drinking; the fragment varies across sources, and a translation based on one reading should not silently be imposed on another.

Tāj al-ʿArūs and Lisān al-ʿArab: the ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm witness

The fragment shows that restoration was not limited to treating physical fractures. Nevertheless, attributing it to a pre-Islamic poet does not, by itself, establish this particular fragment’s date.

A rajaz line attributed to Al-ʿAjjāj likewise reads:

«قَدْ جَبَرَ الدِّينَ الْإِلَهُ فَجَبَرْ»

qad jabara al-dīna al-ilāhu fa-jabar

On the reading that takes the second verb as intransitive: “The God put religion in order, and it became sound.”

Quoted in dictionaries, this illustrates repair and restoration applied to something nonphysical. Al-ʿAjjāj belongs to the Islamic period, so this line is not independent evidence of pre-Qur’anic language. Alongside the familiar intransitive analysis, Al-Rāghib reports another: the second verb repeats the transitive action and marks completion of the repair. The intransitive reading is therefore not the only transmitted analysis.

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: two analyses of fa-jabar

Height, firmness, and power

Another branch describes things that are large, tall, or powerful.

نَخْلَةٌ جَبَّارَةٌ (nakhlatun jabbāratun) describes a palm so tall that a person’s hand cannot reach it. The tree compels nobody; its height and firmness make its top difficult to reach.

Lexical sources also record فَرَسٌ جَبَّارٌ (farasun jabbārun) for a large, powerful horse.

Maqāyīs: jabbār applied to a palm and horse

These examples matter because they show that jabbār is not restricted to morally oppressive behaviour in every recorded usage.

Height, ability, and inaccessibility can be descriptive properties. When the power concerns human conduct towards others, context determines whether the point is ability alone or rebelliousness and imposing one’s will.

Compelling and exercising power

Arabic uses جَبَرَ فُلَانًا عَلَى الْأَمْرِ (jabara fulānan ʿalā al-amri) and أَجْبَرَهُ عَلَيْهِ (ajbarahu ʿalayhi) for compelling someone to do something.

In this construction, someone exercises power to draw another into an action that may be contrary to their wishes.

Tajabbara also describes someone displaying their greatness and power or dominating others with rebellious force.

Two uses must therefore be distinguished: force serving repair and restoration, and force imposing personal wishes on others.

This distinction is fundamental to understanding the Qur’anic uses of jabbār.

3. Jabbār describing people in the Qur’an

Powerful people whom the Israelites feared

قَالُوا يَا مُوسَىٰ إِنَّ فِيهَا قَوْمًا جَبَّارِينَ وَإِنَّا لَن نَّدْخُلَهَا حَتَّىٰ يَخْرُجُوا مِنْهَا فَإِن يَخْرُجُوا مِنْهَا فَإِنَّا دَاخِلُونَ

qālū yā Mūsā inna fīhā qawman jabbārīna wa-innā lan nadkhulahā ḥattā yakhrujū minhā fa-in yakhrujū minhā fa-innā dākhilūna

“They said: Moses, there are powerful, dominant people there. We will never enter until they leave it. If they leave, then we will enter.” (Al-Māʾidah 5:22)

Jabbārīn here describes people the Israelites fear confronting.

The statement is spoken by that group; it is not an independent detailed Qur’anic account of the inhabitants’ physical size, military strength, or moral behaviour.

In this context, jabbārīn concerns strength, dominance, and capacity to resist. The Israelites see them as a formidable obstacle to entering.

Exegetical reports describe extraordinary height and physical strength. The verse itself gives no measurement of their stature. Jabbār cannot therefore be restricted to “giant,” nor does it establish people of abnormal, legendary dimensions.

Nor does this use necessarily carry the same emphasis on oppression found in some other verses. The speakers principally stress the strength and dominance of those they fear.

Power accompanied by violence

وَإِذَا بَطَشْتُم بَطَشْتُمْ جَبَّارِينَ

wa-idhā baṭashtum baṭashtum jabbārīna

“And when you strike with force, you act as overbearing tyrants.” (Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130)

This forms part of Hūd’s address to the people of ʿĀd.

Baṭashtum belongs to B Ṭ SH and here denotes laying hands on others with power and violence. Jabbārīna is a circumstantial accusative describing the agents’ manner of acting.

Study of B Ṭ SH

Being jabbār here is not merely having physical strength: the verse itself pairs it with baṭsh. The criticism concerns how power is exercised.

A person may possess power for defence, management, or implementing justice. Turning it into violence against others without respecting right and limit makes its exercise oppressive.

The negative aspect emerges from the description’s association with violent conduct in this verse, rather than every use of jabr or every powerful person being inherently unjust.

Jabbār contrasted with a reformer

فَلَمَّا أَنْ أَرَادَ أَن يَبْطِشَ بِالَّذِي هُوَ عَدُوٌّ لَّهُمَا قَالَ يَا مُوسَىٰ أَتُرِيدُ أَن تَقْتُلَنِي كَمَا قَتَلْتَ نَفْسًا بِالْأَمْسِ إِن تُرِيدُ إِلَّا أَن تَكُونَ جَبَّارًا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَمَا تُرِيدُ أَن تَكُونَ مِنَ الْمُصْلِحِينَ

fa-lammā an arāda an yabṭisha bi-alladhī huwa ʿaduwwun lahumā qāla yā Mūsā a-turīdu an taqtulanī ka-mā qatalta nafsan bi-al-amsi in turīdu illā an takūna jabbāran fī al-arḍi wa-mā turīdu an takūna mina al-muṣliḥīna

“When he intended to seize the man who was an enemy to them both, he said: Moses, do you want to kill me as you killed someone yesterday? You want only to be a tyrant in the land; you do not want to be among those who put things right.” (Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:19)

Jabbār is contrasted here with muṣliḥīn, those who put things right.

Study of Ṣ L Ḥ

The speaker accuses Moses of seeking dominance through force and violence instead of reform. The contrast matters: repair belongs to one lexical branch of jabr, yet jabbār here describes power directed against reform.

These are a character’s words in the story. Their inclusion in the Qur’an does not by itself make them The God’s definitive judgment of Moses’ character.

The linguistic point is clear: the speaker connects jabbār with a desire to dominate and use force, contrasting it with being a reformer.

Thus the root’s connection with repair does not make jabbār positive in every situation.

Jabbār, rebelliousness, and disobedience

وَبَرًّا بِوَالِدَيْهِ وَلَمْ يَكُن جَبَّارًا عَصِيًّا

wa-barran bi-wālidayhi wa-lam yakun jabbāran ʿaṣiyyan

“And dutiful to his parents; he was neither overbearing nor disobedient.” (Maryam 19:14)

This verse concerns John.

Jabbāran accompanies ʿaṣiyyan, both within a negation; dutifulness to parents precedes them.

The verse thus describes someone dutiful to his parents who does not behave rebelliously or disobediently.

ʿAṣiyyan highlights disobedience in the context, but its entire meaning should not be transferred to jabbār. Jabbār concerns overbearing self-exaltation, while ʿaṣiyy concerns disobedience.

The same sūrah also presents Jesus as saying:

وَبَرًّا بِوَالِدَتِي وَلَمْ يَجْعَلْنِي جَبَّارًا شَقِيًّا

wa-barran bi-wālidatī wa-lam yajʿalnī jabbāran shaqiyyan

“And He made me dutiful to my mother; He did not make me overbearing and wretched.” (Maryam 19:32)

Again, jabbār is contrasted with dutifulness to a mother. Its companion here is shaqiyyan, however, rather than ʿaṣiyyan.

The repeated construction for John and Jesus, despite the different final description, shows that overbearing self-exaltation conflicts with the dutifulness and humility praised by the Qur’an.

ʿAṣiyy and shaqiyy nevertheless remain distinct words; neither should be treated as merely repeating jabbār’s meaning.

Jabbār ʿanīd: power joined to resistance against truth

وَتِلْكَ عَادٌ جَحَدُوا بِآيَاتِ رَبِّهِمْ وَعَصَوْا رُسُلَهُ وَاتَّبَعُوا أَمْرَ كُلِّ جَبَّارٍ عَنِيدٍ

wa-tilka ʿĀdun jaḥadū bi-āyāti rabbihim wa-ʿaṣaw rusulahu wa-ittabaʿū amra kulli jabbārin ʿanīdin

“That was ʿĀd: they denied their Lord’s signs, disobeyed His messengers, and followed the command of every obstinate tyrant.” (Hūd 11:59)

Jabbār accompanies ʿanīd here. From ʿ N D, ʿanīd concerns obstinacy and stubborn resistance.

Study of ʿ N D

The verse concerns not only the powerful acting oppressively, but people following their commands.

Oppressive power can therefore operate through commanding and securing obedience. A jabbār need not perform every act personally: orders and influence can induce others to follow.

Ibrāhīm 14:15 likewise uses jabbārin ʿanīdin and speaks of such a person’s failure.

Ghāfir 40:35 uses mutakabbirin jabbārin. It criticizes people disputing The God’s signs without proof and describes their arrogance and drive to dominate.

Across these verses, jabbār is associated with disobedience, arrogance, obstinate resistance to truth, and following the commands of powerful people.

The Prophet is not a compeller over people

نَحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَقُولُونَ وَمَا أَنتَ عَلَيْهِم بِجَبَّارٍ فَذَكِّرْ بِالْقُرْآنِ مَن يَخَافُ وَعِيدِ

naḥnu aʿlamu bi-mā yaqūlūna wa-mā anta ʿalayhim bi-jabbārin fa-dhakkir bi-al-Qurʾāni man yakhāfu waʿīdi

“We know best what they say, and you are not an overbearing compeller over them. So remind, through the Qur’an, whoever fears My warning.” (Qāf 50:45)

The verse denies that the Prophet is jabbār.

The construction ʿalayhim bi-jabbārin matters. ʿAlā foregrounds power and domination over the addressees; bāʾ in the negative sentence reinforces the negation.

In contrast to being a compeller, the verse sets out reminding through the Qur’an. The Prophet is charged with conveying and reminding, rather than imposing belief through domination.

This accords with Al-Baqarah 2:256 on the absence of compulsion in religion and Al-Ghāshiyah 88:22 on the Prophet not dominating his addressees, though each verse retains its own construction and setting.

Jabbār here therefore does not concern mending fractures or remedying need. It concerns imposing one’s will, denied in the Prophet’s relation to his audience’s faith.

4. Al-Jabbār describing The God

هُوَ اللَّهُ الَّذِي لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الْمَلِكُ الْقُدُّوسُ السَّلَامُ الْمُؤْمِنُ الْمُهَيْمِنُ الْعَزِيزُ الْجَبَّارُ الْمُتَكَبِّرُ سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ عَمَّا يُشْرِكُونَ

huwa Allāhu alladhī lā ilāha illā huwa al-maliku al-quddūsu al-salāmu al-muʾminu al-muhayminu al-ʿazīzu al-jabbāru al-mutakabbiru subḥāna Allāhi ʿammā yushrikūna

“He is The God, besides whom there is no deity: the Sovereign, the Holy, the Sound and Giver of Security, the Guardian, the Invincible, the Possessor of Overmastering Power, and the Possessor of Greatness. Exalted is The God above what they associate with Him.” (Al-Ḥashr 59:23)

Al-Jabbār stands among divine descriptions, preceded by al-ʿAzīz and followed by al-Mutakabbir.

This association brings power, might, greatness, and dominance into view.

How can a description condemned in most human uses be praiseworthy when applied to The God?

The answer must be sought in the bearer’s different standing and conduct.

Human power and divine power

Human beings are limited. A person making themselves jabbār may exceed their real standing, disregard others’ rights, and impose their will.

The God’s power in the Qur’an is not that of a limited being concealing deficiency or weakness through tyranny. The surrounding Qur’anic account also presents Him as possessing knowledge, mercy, justice, and sovereignty.

Condemned features of a human tyrant—oppression, rebelliousness, and disregard for right—cannot therefore be transferred to the divine description.

In Al-Ḥashr 59:23, al-Jabbār can convey supreme power and dominance, with nothing outside The God’s ability and sovereignty.

This reading does not make the human and divine words wholly unrelated. Both concern power and superiority; limited human power may become aggression and injustice, whereas the divine description concerns incomparable power.

Does al-Jabbār mean the mender of brokenness?

Some lexical and exegetical explanations interpret al-Jabbār as the one who remedies servants’ deficiencies, meets their needs, and repairs their injuries.

This relies on a valid branch: jabara al-faqīra and jabara al-kasra are established usages.

In Al-Ḥashr, however, jabbār stands between ʿazīz and mutakabbir, both associated with power, might, and greatness. “Mender of brokenness” cannot therefore be declared the sole certain meaning here.

Equally, this semantic branch should not be rejected without reason. Restorative ability is consistent with divine power and can be one aspect of explaining the description.

Al-Rāghib, Lisān, and Tāj: explanations of the divine description

A possible lexical meaning is not necessarily the principal meaning selected for a particular verse’s translation.

Does divine jabbār imply compelling every human action?

In theological debates after the Qur’an, jabr became a significant term for discussing human choice, decree and destiny, and the relation of divine will to human actions.

That theological term must be distinguished from the Qur’anic meaning of al-Jabbār.

Calling The God jabbār in Al-Ḥashr 59:23 does not by itself establish that every voluntary human action is directly compelled by Him, leaving people without choice or responsibility.

Such a conclusion requires an independent argument from the verses concerning will, choice, and responsibility.

Al-Kahf 18:29 speaks of choosing belief or disbelief; Al-Insān 76:3 of being shown the way and the possibility of gratitude or ingratitude; Al-Zalzalah 99:7 and Al-Zalzalah 99:8 of seeing the result of one’s actions.

These verses matter for discussing choice and responsibility, although they do not alone settle every philosophical question about human will and divine volition.

Conversely, Qāf 50:45 denies the same description of the Prophet in relation to imposing on his audience.

Al-Jabbār applied to The God should therefore not become a doctrine of absolute human determinism without examining the other verses.

Jabbār in relation to ʿazīz and mutakabbir

Al-Ḥashr 59:23 places al-ʿAzīz, al-Jabbār, and al-Mutakabbir consecutively.

All three concern greatness and power, but remain different words.

ʿAzīz, from ʿ Z Z, concerns might, strength, and invincibility. Jabbār here foregrounds dominant, effective power. Mutakabbir, from K B R, concerns greatness and majesty.

Study of ʿ Z Z; Study of K B R

For people, arrogance and tyrannical dominance are usually blameworthy: a person claims superiority and authority that may exceed their rightful limits.

For The God, greatness and power are attributed to the one the Qur’an identifies as the source of truth and just judgment.

Their order or morphological patterns nevertheless cannot establish a definitive hierarchy of divine power. Semantic distinctions can be examined, but co-occurrence alone does not prove such a system.

5. Distinguishing J B R from nearby roots

J B R (jabr) and Q H R (qahr)

Study of Q H R

Qahr concerns overcoming and dominance. Al-Qahhār is also a Qur’anic divine description.

Some uses of jabr overlap with qahr, especially compulsion and exercising power. Jabr’s family additionally includes established uses for mending fractures and remedying deficiencies.

Qahr and jabr are therefore not completely synonymous in every situation.

J B R (jabr) and K R H (kurh)

Study of K R H

K R H concerns unpleasantness, unwillingness, and coercion. Ikrāh denotes making someone act against their wishes.

Ijbār can convey this in its established use too, but jabr has a wider semantic family.

Mending a fracture or helping a poor person cannot simply be rendered as coercion. When someone makes another act against their will, however, the fields approach one another.

J B R (jabr) and B Ṭ SH (baṭsh)

Study of B Ṭ SH

Baṭsh concerns laying hands on something with power, and in some settings exercising violence.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130 joins jabbār with baṭsh, showing the people addressed exercising power oppressively.

The words are not identical. Baṭsh chiefly concerns the act and manner of seizing or striking; jabbār describes how those wielding power behave in that sentence.

J B R (jabr) and K B R (kibr)

Study of K B R

K B R concerns greatness and, in certain derivatives, self-exaltation.

Ghāfir 40:35 places mutakabbir and jabbār together. Arrogance can describe a person’s view of their standing; jabbār in the same setting concerns exercising power and dominance.

They can coexist: someone regarding themselves as superior to truth and other people may use power to impose their will.

Their association nevertheless does not make all uses of K B R and J B R identical in Arabic.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

J B R appears in the Qur’an only through jabbār. Its translation must consider whom it describes and the sentence’s context.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:22, “powerful and dominant” is suitable: the speakers fear the people’s strength.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:19, “tyrant” or “overbearing oppressor” fits the violence and imposition of power.

In Maryam 19:14 and Maryam 19:32, “overbearing” is concise and natural. ʿAṣiyy and shaqiyy must be translated separately rather than transferring their entire meanings into jabbār.

In Hūd 11:59 and Ibrāhīm 14:15, “obstinate tyrant” can convey jabbārin ʿanīdin.

For mutakabbirin jabbārin in Ghāfir 40:35, “arrogant tyrant” is possible.

In Qāf 50:45, “overbearing compeller” conveys the concern with domination and forcing the addressees.

In Al-Ḥashr 59:23, however, Persian zūrgū, “tyrant,” is unsuitable for the divine description because it implies injustice and violating others’ rights. “Possessor of overmastering power” or “dominant and mighty” can convey power and superiority without importing condemned human features.

“Restorer” is also a valid lexical explanation of divine jabbār, but is better discussed separately than selected without justification as the verse’s only meaning.

One Persian word need not cover the entire family. Preserving each usage’s actual meaning matters more than imposing one fixed equivalent on every verse.

Conclusion

Arabic J B R concerns repairing fractures, remedying deficiencies, firmness, power, and dominance. Jabr may denote repair and restoration; ijbār, compulsion; and jabbār, someone possessing dominant power.

Mending bones and meeting the poor’s needs show that the root is not inherently negative in every derivative. Uses involving compulsion and rebelliousness show power serving imposed wishes and violations of others’ rights.

The Qur’an usually applies jabbār to people marked by oppression, violence, arrogance, or obstinate resistance to truth. Al-Māʾidah 5:22 nevertheless shows it stressing a people’s strength and dominance in reported speech without that word alone determining their entire moral character.

Al-Jabbār is a divine description in Al-Ḥashr 59:23. Divine power and dominance are foregrounded and must not be interpreted as human tyranny or oppression. Repair and restoration remain valid lexical branches, but cannot be declared the description’s sole meaning without contextual evidence.

A defensible shared semantic core concerns firmness, ability, and effective power; repair, restoration, overcoming, and compulsion nevertheless remain distinct uses whose precise meanings depend on form and context. This synthesizes usages; it does not establish one historical route by which every derivative developed.

Thus human jabbār can describe power exceeding rightful limits, while divine al-Jabbār concerns supreme power and dominance. Neither usage alone justifies denying human choice and responsibility or legitimizing tyrants’ power as divine will.
