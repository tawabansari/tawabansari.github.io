Core meaning: depth and distance extending through a space, from the depth of a well to a distant route

In Arabic, ʿ M Q concerns depth, a faraway bottom, and extended distance. When a well or pit is described as ʿamīq, its bottom is far below the surface; when the adjective describes a route, it conveys distance and extent.

This distinction matters for the Quranic usage: ʿamīq does not always mean extending downward into the earth. A route may be ʿamīq because it is distant and lengthy, rather than sunken.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as عَمِيقٍ in Al-Ḥajj 22:27.

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic lexicons record ʿumq and ʿamq for the distance to the bottom or to a remote point within something.

A clear example is biʾrun ʿamīqah: a well with a bottom far below.

Here the central idea is the great distance between the well's opening and its bottom, beyond merely being a hollow. A well may have a broad opening without being deep, or a narrow opening with its bottom far beneath the surface.

When applied to a route, the same lexical family concerns distance. Thus fajjun ʿamīq means a distant route.

The direction of the distance depends on what is described: toward the bottom in a well, and toward distant places along a route.

A poetic witness near the period of revelation

A poem attributed to al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār al-Dhubyānī says:

نَظَرْتُ وَسَهْبٌ مِنْ بُوَانَةَ بَيْنَنَا
وَأَفْيَحُ مِنْ رَوْضِ الرُّبَابِ عَمِيقُ

naẓartu wa-sahbun min buwānata baynanā / wa-afyaḥu min rawḍi al-rubābi ʿamīq

“I looked, with an open plain of Buwānah between us, and a broad, far-reaching expanse of Rawḍ al-Rubāb.”

Here ʿamīq describes a distant expanse rather than a deep-bottomed well.

The poet describes the space separating him from what he sees. The accompanying sahb and afyaḥ reinforce the image of spaciousness and distance.

Al-Shammākh lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. The exact date of this verse has not been independently established in this study, so it cannot confidently be presented as evidence predating the Quran. It nevertheless attests spatial use of ʿamīq for distance in language close to that period.

Early lexicons also record the distinction between the depth of a well and the distance of a route. Both uses are therefore identifiable in classical Arabic, though their precise emergence and development require further independent evidence.

2. Forms and derivatives

Several forms belong to this Arabic family:

عَمُقَ: became deep, or developed greater depth.

عُمْق and عَمْق: depth, distance to the bottom, and spatial distance extending through a place.

عَمِيق: deep; when describing a route, distant or far-reaching.

أَعْمَقَ and عَمَّقَ: to deepen something, such as a well.

تَعَمَّقَ: to go deeply into something or pursue an inquiry. Classical lexicons also record excessive elaboration or affectation in speech; its negative sense should not be assigned to a later period without evidence.

أَعْمَاق: a plural associated with depth and the remote parts or outer reaches of a place; it is not restricted to downward depth.

Only عَمِيقٍ occurs in the Quran. The other forms illuminate the lexical field, but their technical or figurative senses should not be imported into the verse.

Some lexical accounts also record maʿīq alongside ʿamīq. Al-Farrāʾ associates the two forms with usage in the Hijaz and among Banū Tamīm. This reversal of consonant order concerns the word's forms and does not change the meaning of ʿamīq in the verse.

3. Quranic usage: a route extending from afar

Al-Ḥajj 22:27

وَأَذِّنْ فِي النَّاسِ بِالْحَجِّ يَأْتُوكَ رِجَالًا وَعَلَىٰ كُلِّ ضَامِرٍ يَأْتِينَ مِنْ كُلِّ فَجٍّ عَمِيقٍ

wa-adhdhin fī al-nāsi bi-al-ḥajji yaʾtūka rijālan wa-ʿalā kulli ḍāmirin yaʾtīna min kulli fajjin ʿamīq

Al-Ḥajj 22:27

“Proclaim the pilgrimage among the people: they will come to you on foot and on every lean mount, coming from every distant route.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:27

This verse follows the account of Abraham and the location of the House. Al-Ḥajj 22:26 mentions assigning the House's location to Abraham, avoiding association with The God, and purifying the House. The command to proclaim the pilgrimage then follows.

Two words matter in the verse's final phrase:

فَجٍّ belongs to F J J, and عَمِيقٍ to ʿ M Q.

In Arabic, fajj denotes an open, broad way, especially a passage between mountains; more broadly, it can mean a route or passage.

ʿAmīq qualifies fajj and expresses the route's distance here.

Min kulli fajjin ʿamīq therefore means “from every distant route.”

Why does ʿamīq mean “distant” here?

In contemporary Persian, hearing the borrowed word ʿamīq commonly brings to mind a deep well, sea, or pit.

Imposing that restricted sense without considering context might produce “from every deep valley,” as though the verse referred only to routes extending downward into the ground.

The verse concerns people coming to the pilgrimage by different routes. They travel on foot or on mounts and approach their destination from afar.

The central issue here is the extent of the route and spatial distance, rather than the ground's depth.

For example, someone may set out from a faraway city and cross plains, mountains, and deserts. The route is long and distant even if much of it lies on level ground.

This distinction should guide the translation of fajjin ʿamīq.

Grammatically, عَمِيقٍ is a masculine singular adjective in the genitive, agreeing with فَجٍّ. It directly describes the route, rather than the traveler, mount, or pilgrimage destination.

Min kulli expresses the breadth of the routes of arrival. The verse depicts people coming along various distant routes rather than restricting the picture to one particular route or geographical region.

ʿAmīq, travel, and pilgrimage

Here rijālan means people on foot, rather than men as opposed to women. Ḍāmir describes a lean mount, especially a camel. The word itself does not establish the cause of its leanness.

Alongside these images, fajjin ʿamīq expresses the distance of the route.

Together, the expressions depict an actual human journey toward the House, potentially beginning far away and following different routes.

ʿAmīq itself concerns distance and extent here. The traveler's exertion and the mount's fatigue may be understood from the overall image, but hardship and suffering are not independent meanings of this root.

Nor does the expression imply that greater distance automatically gives the traveler greater spiritual merit or reward. The verse makes no such comparison.

4. Distinctions from related roots

ʿ M Q and B ʿ D

These families overlap in expressing distance, but their ranges differ.

Buʿd is a broad word for remoteness, whether spatial, temporal, or abstract.

In material usage, ʿumq concerns depth and distance to the bottom; when applied to a route or expanse, it conveys extent and remoteness.

Something deep has a dimension of distance or extent, but not every distant interval can be called depth in every context.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:27, ʿamīq and baʿīd are close in spatial sense, though ʿamīq retains its particular linguistic image.

ʿ M Q and GH W R

Ghawr also concerns downward movement and depth.

In Al-Mulk 67:30, water is described as ghawran: water that has sunk away beyond ordinary reach.

Its downward recession and loss of accessibility matter in this usage.

ʿUmq can describe such spatial depth, but is not limited to a downward direction, as the distant route in Al-Ḥajj illustrates.

Their shared connection with depth does not make the two roots fully synonymous.

ʿ M Q and S Ḥ Q

One branch of S Ḥ Q denotes great distance.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:31, saḥīq describes a distant place.

That verse differs in subject and imagery from Al-Ḥajj 22:27, but comparing the adjectives is useful.

ʿAmīq in Al-Ḥajj 22:27 describes a distant route; saḥīq in Al-Ḥajj 22:31 expresses the remoteness of a place.

Both concern distance in these contexts, without making every derivative of their roots synonymous.

ʿ M Q and F J J

This comparison matters because the words occur together in one Quranic phrase.

Fajj names the route or passage; ʿamīq describes it.

A passage between two mountains may be short, or such a passage may lead onward into a long route reaching far away.

In the latter case, ʿamīq can express that distance and extent.

The meaning of a route comes from fajj, while “distant” comes from the use of ʿamīq to describe it.

5. Extended uses in language

Other uses related to material depth have developed or been recorded in Arabic lexicons.

For example, taʿammaqa fī al-kalām can mean going deeply into the details of speech or indulging in excessive elaboration and affectation.

Contemporary language also speaks of deep thought, in-depth research, and profound understanding.

Depth here pictures advancing from a topic's outward surface into its inner layers, rather than measuring the physical distance to a well's bottom.

These uses should not be transferred to Quranic عَمِيقٍ.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:27, the adjective accompanies an actual route, so spatial distance and extent take precedence over mental or other figurative meanings.

Taʿammuq does not invariably carry positive connotations. In some contexts it concerns careful, sustained inquiry; in others, affectation and excess.

That evaluation comes from usage and context, not the root alone.

6. Translation proposal for Forqan

“Distant” is the proposed equivalent for the Quranic use of ʿ M Q in Al-Ḥajj 22:27.

Quranic word: عَمِيقٍ

Root: ʿ M Q

Word type: adjective on the faʿīl pattern, masculine singular and genitive.

Brief root meaning: depth and distance to the bottom; remoteness and extensive distance when describing a route.

Contextual meaning: distant, involving a long route.

Suggested phrase: “from every distant route.”

“From every deep valley” can misdirect the reader toward a depression in the ground, whereas the context concerns distant routes along which people approach the pilgrimage.

“From every faraway road” is also natural. The Persian proposal favors a word conveying remoteness and the route's extent.

7. Conclusion

ʿ M Q concerns depth and considerable distance in Arabic. With wells, that distance is measured from the surface to the bottom; with routes, it concerns remoteness and extent.

Its Quranic occurrence in Al-Ḥajj 22:27 shows that ʿamīq should not always be restricted to downward depth or a depression in the ground.

In fajjin ʿamīq, fajj names the route or passage, and ʿamīq expresses its remoteness.

“A distant route” is therefore a natural rendering of this phrase.

Final definition of ʿ M Q:

“Depth and considerable distance extending through a space: in a well or pit, a bottom far below the surface; in a route or passage, remoteness and length. The precise meaning depends on what is described.”
