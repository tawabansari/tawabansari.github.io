Returning, turning back to an earlier condition or course, and restoring something: from shifting shadows to abandoning aggression and property coming into others’ control

1. The root in early Arabic

The triliteral Arabic root F Y ʾ concerns returning, turning from one condition to another, and restoring something.

فَاءَ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ means returning toward something. فَاءَ عَنْ غَضَبِهِ means turning back from anger and becoming calm. أَفَاءَ الشَّيْءَ concerns restoring something or bringing it into another’s control.

Classical lexical sources explain this family through الرُّجُوع: returning.

The sense applies to reversing decisions or conduct, shade returning as the sun changes position, restoring property or benefit, and turning away from states such as anger or hostility.

All involve some reversal or return, but what returns differs.

Someone may reverse a wrong decision. A tree’s shadow may extend in another direction as the sun’s position changes. Property may pass from one group’s control to another’s.

“Return” is linguistically recognizable, but the root alone does not identify what returns, to whom it returns, or whether the return is morally right or wrong.

Those features emerge from sentence structure and context.

Evidence from early Arabic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imru’ al-Qays reads:

تَيَمَّمَتِ الْعَيْنَ الَّتِي عِنْدَ ضَارِجٍ
يَفِيءُ عَلَيْهَا الظِّلُّ عَرْمَضُهَا طَامِي

Tayammamati l-ʿayna llatī ʿinda Ḍārijin / yafīʾu ʿalayhā ẓ-ẓillu ʿarmaḍuhā ṭāmī

“She headed for the spring near Darij, over which shade returns, its water plants abundant and rising.”

ضَارِج names a place, and الْعَيْن a spring.

يَفِيءُ عَلَيْهَا الظِّلُّ describes the movement and return of shade over the spring.

عَرْمَض concerns aquatic growth on the water’s surface; طَامِي describes its abundance or rising extent.

يَفِيءُ here concerns returning shade, not human repentance, restoring property, or a religious technical term.

Classical lexical and literary sources transmit this verse as attributed to Imru’ al-Qays. Its wording varies: some versions have الطَّلْحُ instead of الظِّلُّ.

This matters: in the first reading the shade itself is the subject of يَفِيءُ; in the second, the shade-casting tree appears in the sentence.

The attribution is familiar in the literary tradition, but its recording in later compilations does not independently establish the verse’s date of composition.

With that qualification, the example helps explain the old association between F Y ʾ and returning shade.

How was returning shade understood?

In classical Arabic, فَيْء is a name for shade, especially shade that extends again or turns in another direction after the sun passes its midday position.

Some lexicographers distinguish ظِلّ from فَيْء.

In this account, ظِلّ can be the general name for shade, while فَيْء more particularly denotes shade reappearing or extending after sunlight as the light’s direction changes.

A familiar usage distinguishes morning shade from shade after midday, calling the latter فَيْء.

Poetry attributed to Humayd ibn Thawr al-Hilali also contrasts morning shade and evening fayʾ. He lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods; the verse is not securely dated before Islam. It helps illuminate language near the revelatory period rather than conclusively dating the distinction’s origin.

The lexical explanation is clear: as the sun apparently moves across the sky, a shadow’s position and extent change. A shadow that had shortened or withdrawn from an area extends again or turns elsewhere.

Return and displacement are consequently prominent in the image of فَيْء.

This is not an absolute rule making every ظِلّ morning shade and every فَيْء afternoon shade. ظِلّ has a wider Arabic range, including diverse Qur’anic uses.

Nor does the image make shade an independently willing being. Its apparent movement results from changes in the relationship between light, object, and the surface receiving the shadow.

2. Forms and the Qur’anic semantic core

Important Arabic forms include:

فَاءَ: he returned.

يَفِيءُ: he returns.

فَيْء: return; in a particular branch, returning or extending shade.

فَيْئَة: a single return or a state of return.

أَفَاءَ: restored or brought something into another’s control.

إِفَاءَة: the act of restoring.

تَفَيَّأَ: shifted, turned to another side, or took shade.

تَفَيُّؤ: shifting and returning, especially of shade.

أَفْيَاء and فُيُوء: plurals of فَيْء in the shade sense.

Lexical sources also record forms such as اِسْتَفَاءَ, in some uses concerning taking something as fayʾ.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

F Y ʾ occurs seven times in six Qur’anic verses, in three verbal forms.

Form I has three occurrences:

  • فَاءُوا in Al-Baqarah 2:226.
  • تَفِيءَ in Al-Hujurat 49:9.
  • فَاءَتْ in Al-Hujurat 49:9.

Form IV أَفَاءَ occurs three times:

  • Al-Ahzab 33:50.
  • Al-Hashr 59:6.
  • Al-Hashr 59:7.

Form V occurs once:

  • يَتَفَيَّأُ in Al-Nahl 16:48.

The nouns فَيْء, أَفْيَاء, and فُيُوء do not occur in these forms, although the financial sense of fayʾ is understood from أَفَاءَ in Al-Hashr.

The common core is returning or restoring.

In Form I, the subject itself returns.

In Form IV, an agent causes something to return or reach another.

In Form V, shifting and returning within a situation, as with moving shadows, are involved.

These distinctions fit the Qur’anic uses, but morphology alone does not establish every detail of movement, intention, or result.

3. Al-Baqarah 2:226: returning from sworn withdrawal

لِلَّذِينَ يُؤْلُونَ مِنْ نِسَائِهِمْ تَرَبُّصُ أَرْبَعَةِ أَشْهُرٍ فَإِنْ فَاءُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

Li-lladhīna yuʾlūna min nisāʾihim tarabbuṣu arbaʿati ashhurin fa-in fāʾū fa-inna llāha ghafūrun raḥīm.

“For those who swear to withdraw from their wives, there is a waiting period of four months. If they return, then indeed The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

Al-Baqarah 2:226

فَاءُوا concerns the return of men who have sworn to withdraw from their wives.

It is a masculine plural perfect verb following conditional إِنْ.

The conditional construction with فَاءُوا means “if they return.”

What do they return to?

The context supplies the answer.

The verse concerns sworn withdrawal from wives. Returning therefore means leaving that withdrawal and relinquishing the decision suspending the marital relationship.

It does not mean returning from a journey or to a location here.

A man may remain physically in the same house while withdrawing from marital relations.

Conduct and relationship are involved, not necessarily spatial movement.

Returning and family responsibility

The verse assigns four months to the situation arising from the oath.

The next verse, Al-Baqarah 2:227, presents resolving on divorce as the other course.

Together they indicate that unresolved withdrawal cannot continue indefinitely.

Returning in Al-Baqarah 2:226 is accompanied by divine forgiveness and mercy. Yet فَاءُوا alone does not specify every legal and bodily detail of how return occurs.

Later jurisprudence developed extensive discussions of its realization, marital relations, and rulings on the oath.

Those discussions should not replace the word’s initial Qur’anic meaning.

The direct sense is return; its object emerges from the context of the oath and withdrawal.

The same distinction holds generally: someone may reverse a decision without فَاءَ itself denoting a particular physical or legal act.

4. Al-Hujurat 49:9: returning from aggression to the divine command

وَإِنْ طَائِفَتَانِ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ اقْتَتَلُوا فَأَصْلِحُوا بَيْنَهُمَا فَإِنْ بَغَتْ إِحْدَاهُمَا عَلَى الْأُخْرَىٰ فَقَاتِلُوا الَّتِي تَبْغِي حَتَّىٰ تَفِيءَ إِلَىٰ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ فَإِنْ فَاءَتْ فَأَصْلِحُوا بَيْنَهُمَا بِالْعَدْلِ وَأَقْسِطُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُقْسِطِينَ

Wa-in ṭāʾifatāni mina l-muʾminīna qtatalū fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā fa-in baghat iḥdāhumā ʿalā l-ukhrā fa-qātilū llatī tabghī ḥattā tafīʾa ilā amri llāhi fa-in fāʾat fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā bi-l-ʿadli wa-aqsiṭū inna llāha yuḥibbu l-muqsiṭīn.

“If two groups of believers fight, reconcile them. If one transgresses against the other, fight the one that transgresses until it returns to the command of The God. If it returns, reconcile them justly and act equitably. Indeed, The God loves those who act equitably.”

Al-Hujurat 49:9

The root occurs twice here:

تَفِيءَ and فَاءَتْ.

Both concern a group returning from aggression to the divine command.

تَفِيءَ is a subjunctive imperfect after حَتَّىٰ, expressing the stated action’s endpoint.

فَاءَتْ is a feminine singular perfect referring to that same group.

Its referent is the transgressing group, hence the feminine singular form.

Returning from what?

The verse addresses conflict and fighting between two groups of believers.

It first commands reconciliation.

If one group transgresses against the other, confrontation with the aggressing group continues until it returns to the divine command.

Here تَفِيءَ therefore means abandoning aggression and yielding to the divine command in this dispute.

It does not mean returning to a place or joining another religious group, since the verse initially identifies both sides as believers.

A group may therefore be called believers yet transgress in a confrontation and need to abandon that conduct.

فَاءَ expresses a change of behavioral course here.

Returning and establishing justice are distinct stages

The continuation of Al-Hujurat 49:9 links return with a further command:

The community must reconcile the two sides justly and act equitably.

If the aggressing group returns, reconciliation must therefore be established between them with justice.

The sequence matters.

Returning from aggression opens the way for further reconciliation, but فَاءَتْ does not itself mean redressing every injury or automatically achieving justice.

Justice is expressed through the separate commands فَأَصْلِحُوا and أَقْسِطُوا.

The root does not imply that every return necessarily removes all earlier responsibilities.

Someone may abandon wrongdoing while its consequences still require just treatment.

The command of confrontation is also tied to this particular situation of aggression between two groups. The root itself creates no general authorization for war or coercion.

Both occurrences mean returning; their subject is ending aggression and returning to the divine command in a specific dispute.

5. Al-Nahl 16:48: returning and shifting shadows

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَىٰ مَا خَلَقَ اللَّهُ مِنْ شَيْءٍ يَتَفَيَّأُ ظِلَالُهُ عَنِ الْيَمِينِ وَالشَّمَائِلِ سُجَّدًا لِلَّهِ وَهُمْ دَاخِرُونَ

A-wa-lam yaraw ilā mā khalaqa llāhu min shayʾin yatafayyaʾu ẓilāluhu ʿani l-yamīni wa-sh-shamāʾili sujjadan li-llāhi wa-hum dākhirūn.

“Have they not looked at anything The God has created, whose shadows turn from the right and left sides, prostrating to The God while they are humbled?”

Al-Nahl 16:48

The root appears here in Form V:

يَتَفَيَّأُ.

Its subject is ظِلَالُهُ: the shadows of what The God created.

الْيَمِينِ and الشَّمَائِلِ identify the directions in which the shadows move and shift.

This concerns a natural phenomenon, not a person reversing a decision or behavior.

How do shadows shift?

When sunlight falls on an object, a shadow forms on its other side.

As the sun’s apparent position changes, the shadow’s direction and size change.

A shadow may extend in one direction early in the day, shorten, and later extend in another direction.

Arabic describes such shifting and return with F Y ʾ.

The verse does more than acknowledge shade: it draws attention to movement and changing direction.

The distinction between ظِلّ and تَفَيُّؤ matters.

ظِلّ names the shade itself.

تَفَيُّؤ expresses its movement, shifting, or return.

Rendering يَتَفَيَّأُ simply as “has shade” would omit the movement and change introduced by the verb.

Does shade possess a will?

The verse continues by describing prostration and humility before The God.

The Qur’an places the movement of shadows among the signs of prostration and humility before Him.

Without independent evidence, this does not establish that shadows possess human-like will and awareness.

Yet the Qur’anic image should not be removed as though the verse were only a physical account of light’s path.

It describes an actual created phenomenon and relates it to prostration and humility before The God.

Al-Nahl 16:49 continues with the prostration of what is in the heavens and earth, and of the angels.

Moving shadows thus belong to the wider presentation of creation’s submission.

يَتَفَيَّأُ itself, however, does not mean prostrating; the separate word سُجَّدًا supplies that meaning.

Does fayʾ mean only afternoon shade?

Classical lexical accounts often associate fayʾ with shade after the sun passes midday.

Al-Nahl 16:48 nevertheless speaks of shadows turning in different directions without specifying an exact time of day.

The sense should remain shifting and returning shadows, without deriving a precise time or detailed astronomical rule from the verb alone.

For shade and its Qur’anic forms, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root Ẓ L L.

6. أَفَاءَ: restoring and bringing into another’s control

The other three Qur’anic occurrences are Form IV:

أَفَاءَ.

The agent causes something to reach another or come into their control.

The God is the agent in all three occurrences.

These differ from returning from an oath or aggression: something passes from one group into another’s control.

Al-Hashr 59:6: property acquired without urging horses or camels forward

وَمَا أَفَاءَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِ مِنْهُمْ فَمَا أَوْجَفْتُمْ عَلَيْهِ مِنْ خَيْلٍ وَلَا رِكَابٍ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ يُسَلِّطُ رُسُلَهُ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَاللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

Wa-mā afāʾa llāhu ʿalā rasūlihi minhum fa-mā awjaftum ʿalayhi min khaylin wa-lā rikābin wa-lākinna llāha yusalliṭu rusulahu ʿalā man yashāʾu wa-llāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr.

“Whatever The God restored from them to His Messenger, you urged neither horses nor riding camels against it. But The God gives His Messengers power over whom He wills, and The God has power over everything.”

Al-Hashr 59:6

أَفَاءَ concerns what reaches the Messenger from the group under discussion.

مِنْهُمْ identifies its source.

رَسُولِهِ identifies the person into whose control the property comes.

Lexically, أَفَاءَ concerns restoring or bringing back; this construction foregrounds bringing property into the recipient’s control.

Crucially, the verb alone does not establish that the property was previously owned personally by the Messenger or the believers.

Earlier ownership requires independent evidence. Restoring must not become an unproven account of each asset’s ownership history.

The verse then explains an important feature:

It describes acquisition for which the audience had urged forward neither horses nor riding camels.

They had not ridden horses or camels to obtain the property.

This differs from a situation in which fighters advance to acquire spoils.

The distinction comes from the verse’s explanation, not from F Y ʾ invariably meaning “property acquired without fighting” in every Arabic use.

Financial fayʾ and later terminology

In Arabic and later legal terminology, فَيْء has denoted property reaching Muslims under specified circumstances without direct battle.

غَنِيمَة, in familiar usage, concerns gain and acquisition, and in warfare property obtained through victory over an enemy.

This distinction became important in discussions of distribution.

Lexical meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from a legal category.

F Y ʾ generally concerns returning and restoring.

The financial branch of Gh N M concerns gain and acquisition.

The text and conditions of the ruling determine how the property was obtained and how it is distributed.

Not everything restored to someone is automatically wartime fayʾ. Nor does every financial acquisition fall under Al-Hashr 59:6 without examining its context.

See Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root Gh N M for the related family.

Al-Hashr 59:7: restoring property and preventing its monopoly

مَا أَفَاءَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْقُرَىٰ فَلِلَّهِ وَلِلرَّسُولِ وَلِذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ كَيْ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً بَيْنَ الْأَغْنِيَاءِ مِنْكُمْ وَمَا آتَاكُمُ الرَّسُولُ فَخُذُوهُ وَمَا نَهَاكُمْ عَنْهُ فَانْتَهُوا وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

Mā afāʾa llāhu ʿalā rasūlihi min ahli l-qurā fa-li-llāhi wa-li-r-rasūli wa-li-dhī l-qurbā wa-l-yatāmā wa-l-masākīni wa-bni s-sabīli kay lā yakūna dūlatan bayna l-aghniyāʾi minkum wa-mā ātākumu r-rasūlu fa-khudhūhu wa-mā nahākum ʿanhu fa-ntahū wa-ttaqū llāha inna llāha shadīdu l-ʿiqāb.

“Whatever The God restored to His Messenger from the people of the towns belongs to The God, the Messenger, relatives, orphans, the needy, and the stranded traveler, so that it does not circulate solely among the wealthy among you. Take what the Messenger gives you, and refrain from what he forbids you. Be mindful of The God; indeed, The God is severe in punishment.”

Al-Hashr 59:7

أَفَاءَ has the same construction as in the preceding verse, but the property’s source is identified as the people of the towns.

The verse then addresses its allocation and distribution.

Orphans, the needy, and stranded travelers are among those named.

The verse states an important purpose: the property must not circulate solely among the wealthy.

Transfer alone is not the whole issue; circulation and access to its benefits also matter.

Fair distribution, however, should not be inserted into the lexical definition of أَفَاءَ.

The verb denotes restoring or bringing something into another’s control.

The clause about circulation among the wealthy identifies a purpose and limit governing this property.

Both ideas are connected in one verse but remain distinct.

Al-Hashr 59:8 also mentions poor migrants expelled from their homes and possessions. This nearby context further clarifies distribution and the situation of those deprived of property.

The financial use in Al-Hashr thus accompanies responsibility for management and distribution as well as transfer.

The financial arrangement’s details emerge from the verses together, not from the root alone.

Al-Ahzab 33:50: what The God brought to the Prophet

The third Form IV occurrence is in Al-Ahzab 33:50.

This verse addresses categories of women and situations concerning the Prophet within its specific ruling.

Among these, it discusses those whom his right hand possesses, from what The God has brought to him.

أَفَاءَ again concerns something coming into the Prophet’s control.

Its subject is not entirely identical with Al-Hashr 59:6 and Al-Hashr 59:7.

Al-Hashr primarily concerns property and distribution. Al-Ahzab places the expression among specific rulings concerning the Prophet and women’s circumstances.

The same verb does not justify transferring every detail of Al-Hashr’s financial ruling to Al-Ahzab 33:50.

Nor should أَفَاءَ be defined here as a particular kind of marriage or family relationship. Other constructions in the verse supply those subjects.

The shared verbal meaning is bringing or placing at someone’s disposal; its referent and associated ruling emerge independently from each context.

7. Comparison with related words

F Y ʾ and R J ʿ: returning

R J ʿ has a broad range of uses for returning in the Qur’an.

The return expressed by F Y ʾ can also be explained with «رَجَعَ».

This does not make their uses identical.

R J ʿ can describe a person's return, returning an answer, returning to The God, and other situations.

«فَاءَ» occurs in contexts of decisions, conduct, returning to a command, and shade.

These are tendencies in usage, not an absolute division between moral and spatial return.

The direction and subject of the return remain important.

For comparison, see the Qur’anic root R J ʿ.

F Y ʾ and T W B: return and repentance

Repentance also involves returning.

In Qur’anic usage, repentance is associated with error, reform, and turning to The God.

«فَاءَ» has a wider range of subjects.

Shade can return without repentance being involved.

In Al-Baqarah 2:226, the issue is withdrawing from a decision to abstain.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9, it is returning from aggression.

These returns have moral consequences, but that does not make every use of the root a technical term for repentance.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9, returning from aggression and restoring justice are stated separately.

For comparison, see the Qur’anic root T W B.

F Y ʾ and Ẓ L L: returning shade and shade

«ظِلّ» names shade.

«فَيْء» highlights shade that returns.

The same phenomenon can thus be named from different perspectives.

One name foregrounds shade itself; the other foregrounds its return.

Both roots occur in An-Naḥl 16:48.

«ظِلَال» names the shadows, while «يَتَفَيَّأُ» describes their movement.

This provides a clear example of the distinction between naming something and describing its movement.

«أَفَاءَ» and G N M: restoration and gain

Gh N M is associated with obtaining a gain or benefit.

Form IV of F Y ʾ concerns restoring something or bringing it under someone's control.

Both can be used of property.

One foregrounds acquisition; the other, restoration or transfer into another's possession.

Al-Anfāl 8:41 discusses the allocation of gains.

Al-Ḥashr 59:6 and Al-Ḥashr 59:7 discuss property The God brings to His Messenger, its acquisition, and its allocation.

These contexts require separate examination.

Later legal definitions should not be imposed on every form of either root.

F Y ʾ and ʾ W B: two families of return

ʾ W B also concerns returning.

«أَوَّاب» describes one who repeatedly turns back to The God.

The two families are related in meaning, but their uses are not identical.

F Y ʾ includes shade and property, while ʾ W B has its own network of usages.

The distinction should not be reduced to a hierarchy of physical and spiritual return.

F Y ʾ and F ʾ Y: similar letters, distinct entries

The second and third letters exchange positions in these two entries.

F Y ʾ has established Qur’anic uses concerning return and shade.

F ʾ Y is the dictionary entry associated with «فِئَة», a group.

Etymological explanations of that noun include a separated part and a group whose members return to one another for support.

A possible historical connection should not be treated as a demonstrated identity between the two families.

In actual usage, «فِئَة» names a group, not the act of returning.

For comparison, see the Qur’anic root F ʾ Y.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

«فَاءَ»: returned, or reversed a prior decision.

«فَاءَ إِلَى»: returned toward something.

«فَاءَ عَنْ»: turned back from something, or desisted.

«فَيْء»: returning shade.

«تَفَيَّأَ»: shifted or turned.

«أَفَاءَ»: restored, or placed something at another's disposal.

The translation should select the meaning appropriate to the context, rather than accumulate every possible equivalent.

The seven Qur’anic occurrences

«فَاءُوا» — Al-Baqarah 2:226

“They returned.”

The context concerns withdrawing from an oath of abstention and resuming the marital relationship.

«تَفِيءَ» — Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9

“It returns.”

The direction of return is toward The God's command concerning the dispute.

«فَاءَتْ» — Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9

“It returned.”

The conditional clause concerns the group's return from aggression.

«يَتَفَيَّأُ» — An-Naḥl 16:48

“Turn” or “shift.”

The subjects are shadows, and their movement is described toward the right and left sides.

«أَفَاءَ» — Al-Aḥzāb 33:50

“Brought back” or “placed at your disposal.”

The context concerns what The God has placed at the Prophet's disposal.

«أَفَاءَ» — Al-Ḥashr 59:6

“Restored” or “brought under the Messenger's control.”

The following clause explains the circumstances in which that property was obtained.

«أَفَاءَ» — Al-Ḥashr 59:7

“Restored.”

The context concerns allocating property from the people of the towns.

The translation should preserve the idea of return without inventing a claim of previous ownership that the verb alone does not establish.

9. Summary

F Y ʾ concerns returning and restoring.

It applies both to human conduct and to natural phenomena such as shade.

The root occurs seven times in six Qur’anic verses.

In Al-Baqarah 2:226, it concerns returning from an oath of abstention.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9, it concerns returning from aggression, followed by reconciliation with justice.

In An-Naḥl 16:48, the movement of shadows is presented as a sign.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:50, «أَفَاءَ» concerns what The God has placed at the Prophet's disposal; in Al-Ḥashr 59:6 and Al-Ḥashr 59:7, the same verb concerns property He has brought to His Messenger.

The verses in Al-Ḥashr explain the circumstances and distribution of that property, so that wealth does not circulate only among the rich.

This social purpose belongs to the context; it is not the lexical definition of the root.

R J ʿ, T W B, and ʾ W B concern related forms of return; Ẓ L L concerns shade, and Gh N M concerns gain.

Each family should be examined in its own contexts, without replacing its meaning with later technical definitions.

Final definition for Forqan:

F Y ʾ expresses return from a state, direction, or decision and, in its causative form, restoring something.

«فَيْء» names returning shade, and «تَفَيُّؤ» describes its movement.

In human conduct, return may concern reversing a decision or withdrawing from aggression.

«أَفَاءَ» concerns restoring something or bringing it under another's control, with financial responsibilities explained by the context.

Return is the common thread, but the subject, direction, moral value, and consequences must be established from each passage.
