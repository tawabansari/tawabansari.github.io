The Root ف ر ت — Fresh, Pleasant, and Agreeable Water

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فُرَاتٌ
(furātun)
Water adjective; two nominative occurrences

فُرَاتًا
(furātan)
Accusative water adjective; one occurrence

In its principal established Arabic usage, the three-letter root ف ر ت concerns the freshness and pleasant taste of water. فُرَات describes water free from saltiness and disagreeable taste, whose freshness makes it pleasant to drink.

Lexicons describe furāt either as fresh water or as water of especially pleasant freshness.

This root occurs three times in the Qur’an: al-Furqān 25:53, Fāṭir 35:12, and al-Mursalāt 77:27. In all three, فُرَات describes water; none uses it as the proper name of the Euphrates River.

In two verses, the Qur’an places عَذْب and فُرَات together. We must therefore clarify each word’s contribution and why, in the third verse, furāt alone suffices to describe pleasant water.

1. The Root’s Meaning in Early Arabic

Fresh Water of Especially Pleasant Taste

In established Arabic usage, فَرُتَ الْمَاءُ means that water became fresh and pleasant.

The verbal forms فَرُتَ يَفْرُتُ فُرُوتَةً are recorded for water acquiring freshness and pleasant taste.

مَاءٌ فُرَاتٌ denotes fresh, agreeable water. Lexicons explain it through أَشَدُّ الْمَاءِ عُذُوبَةً: water of especially pleasant freshness.

In Persian, “sweet water” contrasts with salt water. This does not mean that it must taste like sugar or honey, but that it lacks disagreeable salinity and is pleasant to drink.

For example, water from a clean freshwater spring may be described as furāt. Intensely salty, acrid water belongs to a different semantic field.

Several features should be distinguished when explaining furāt:

First, the water’s freshness and pleasant taste: the word’s primary, attested meaning.

Second, the pleasure of drinking it, which is connected with its freshness.

Third, quenching thirst, which may result from drinking such water, but should not be presented without evidence as the root’s independent and exclusive meaning.

Evidence from Pre-Qur’anic Arabic

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur al-Nahshalī includes:

نَزَلُوا بِأَنْقِرَةٍ يَسِيلُ عَلَيْهِمْ
مَاءُ الْفُرَاتِ يَجِيءُ مِنْ أَطْوَادِ

Nazalū bi-Anqiratin yasīlu ʿalayhim / māʾu al-Furāti yajīʾu min aṭwādi.

Translation: “They settled at Anqirah, while the water of the Euphrates flowed toward them, coming from mountains.”

The line appears in his poetry concerning known lands and settlements.

A distinction must be preserved: māʾ al-Furāt here refers to the water of the familiar Euphrates River. The line thus attests its name in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, but does not by itself prove that the poet used furāt as a general adjective for any fresh water.

The Euphrates’ name also has a long history in ancient Mesopotamian languages and writings. Forms of this name were known in the region before Islam. Its exact origin and historical relationship to the Arabic adjective furāt, however, have been explained in different ways.

The river’s freshwater character alone does not prove that its Arabic name necessarily arose from the adjective furāt. Nor can the reverse—that the general adjective developed from the river’s name—be asserted without independent evidence.

The Qur’anic usage itself clearly attests that furāt was a general description of water in Arabic at the time of revelation. More securely dated evidence would be needed to establish its independent adjectival use in pre-Qur’anic poetry, apart from the river name.

Early lexicons record the meaning of fresh, pleasant water clearly and consistently. Its lexical definition is therefore well grounded, even though the precise origin and development of every use remain unclear.

2. Linguistic Forms and the Semantic Core

Arabic sources record several forms in this family:

  • فَرُتَ: to become fresh and especially pleasant, particularly of water.
  • يَفْرُتُ: the imperfect form of the verb.
  • فُرُوتَة: the freshness and pleasant taste of water.
  • فُرَات: fresh, especially pleasant water, or an adjective describing it.
  • فِرْتَان: a recorded plural form for fresh waters, though of limited use.

Furāt can be used with both singular and plural water nouns, as in māʾ furāt and miyāh furāt. Its form therefore need not always change with the noun’s number.

Some lexical accounts also record فَرِتَ الرَّجُلُ for a person’s intellect weakening after previously being sound. Other reports place usages concerning objectionable human behavior under the same letters.

These limited reports should not be merged with the freshness of water without clear historical and semantic evidence. They do not appear in the root’s Qur’anic uses, and there is no need to construct one shared image for every form.

Only فُرَاتٌ and فُرَاتًا appear in the Qur’an. They are two case forms of one adjective, not derivatives with separate meanings.

The semantic core relevant to the Qur’an is the pronounced freshness and pleasant taste of water, making it agreeable to drink.

3. Qur’anic Uses of the Root

A. Al-Furqān 25:53 — Fresh Water and Salt Water

Al-Furqān 25:53

وَهُوَ الَّذِي مَرَجَ الْبَحْرَيْنِ هَٰذَا عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ وَهَٰذَا مِلْحٌ أُجَاجٌ وَجَعَلَ بَيْنَهُمَا بَرْزَخًا وَحِجْرًا مَحْجُورًا

Wa-huwa alladhī maraja al-baḥrayni hādhā ʿadhbun furātun wa-hādhā milḥun ujājun wa-jaʿala baynahumā barzakhan wa-ḥijran maḥjūran.

Translation: “He is the One who released the two bodies of water: this one fresh and pleasant, that one salty and acrid; and He placed between them a barrier and a restraining boundary.”

Two kinds of water are contrasted here.

One is عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ; the other is مِلْحٌ أُجَاجٌ.

This contrast is the central Qur’anic evidence for understanding furāt’s semantic field.

ʿAdhb expresses the water’s freshness and pleasantness in contrast to saltiness. Furāt emphasizes its pronounced freshness and agreeable taste.

On the other side, milḥ expresses salinity, while ujāj adds acridity and disagreeable intensity.

The verse therefore concerns more than waters of different colors or locations; it describes a real difference in their qualities.

The fresh water described in the verse has a desirable taste for drinking, unlike intensely salty, acrid water. Freshness of taste alone, however, does not establish freedom from contamination.

Why Do ʿAdhb and Furāt Occur Together?

The combination may be considered from two perspectives.

First, ʿadhb expresses the basic quality of freshness, while furāt conveys its intensity or prominence.

Second, their combination gives a fuller picture of desirable drinking water: fresh and pleasant rather than salty and disagreeable.

An artificial boundary should not be imposed between them—for example, claiming that ʿadhb concerns only chemical composition and furāt only the speed at which thirst is quenched.

Lexicons record an overlapping field of freshness and pleasant taste for both words. Their difference lies more in emphasis and prominence than in two entirely independent properties.

Nor does ʿadhb furāt imply measurable sweetness like that of a sugar solution. These words relate to the familiar human experience of fresh water in contrast to salt water.

Does This Mean a River and a Sea?

Al-baḥrayn denotes two bodies or expanses of water in this verse.

One is fresh and the other salty. This description may relate to river water and seawater.

Furāt itself, however, names no particular river here. Any water possessing this quality can exemplify the description.

Barzakh and ḥijran maḥjūran are other components of the verse and must be studied separately from ف ر ت.

Furāt alone does not establish how the waters flow, how much they mix, whether an invisible wall exists, or the scientific mechanism of a freshwater–saltwater boundary.

The verse describes two differing kinds of water and a restraining boundary between them. Establishing its exact nature requires separate study of the other words and the phenomenon concerned.

The Verse in Context

Al-Furqān 25:48 and al-Furqān 25:49 speak of sending water, reviving land, and providing creatures with water to drink.

Al-Furqān 25:53 extends the discussion to differences between waters.

This connection shows that water is not simply a uniform, undifferentiated substance in human life. Its kind and qualities also matter.

Furāt itself does not mean reviving the earth or providing sustenance; those consequences emerge from the wider network of verses about water and life.

B. Fāṭir 35:12 — Pleasant Water and Differing Natural Resources

Fāṭir 35:12

وَمَا يَسْتَوِي الْبَحْرَانِ هَٰذَا عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ سَائِغٌ شَرَابُهُ وَهَٰذَا مِلْحٌ أُجَاجٌ ۖ وَمِنْ كُلٍّ تَأْكُلُونَ لَحْمًا طَرِيًّا وَتَسْتَخْرِجُونَ حِلْيَةً تَلْبَسُونَهَا ۖ وَتَرَى الْفُلْكَ فِيهِ مَوَاخِرَ لِتَبْتَغُوا مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

Wa-mā yastawī al-baḥrāni hādhā ʿadhbun furātun sāʾighun sharābuhu wa-hādhā milḥun ujājun. Wa-min kullin taʾkulūna laḥman ṭariyyan wa-tastakhrijūna ḥilyatan talbasūnahā. Wa-tarā al-fulka fīhi mawākhira li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi wa-laʿallakum tashkurūna.

Translation: “The two bodies of water are not alike: this one is fresh and pleasant, agreeable to drink, and that one salty and acrid. From each you eat fresh flesh and extract ornaments that you wear; and you see the ships plowing through it, so that you may seek His bounty and perhaps give thanks.”

This verse repeats the contrast between ʿadhb furāt and milḥ ujāj, but adds a description of the fresh water:

«سَائِغٌ شَرَابُهُ»

Sāʾighun sharābuhu.

Its drinking is easy and pleasant.

This addition matters for a precise study of the root.

The Difference between Furāt and Sāʾigh

Furāt belongs to ف ر ت and expresses water’s pronounced freshness and pleasant taste.

Sāʾigh belongs to س و غ and concerns drink passing easily down the throat and being agreeable to consume.

The concepts are close, but not identical.

For example, water may have an acceptable degree of salinity but be unpleasant to drink for other reasons.

A drink may also go down easily without being fresh water.

Thus, sāʾighun sharābuhu describes the experience of drinking, and its entire meaning should not be assigned to furāt.

This does not sever the connection between the two descriptions. Water’s freshness is usually linked to agreeable drinking, but this verse expresses each feature through its own wording.

Difference Does Not Make One Water Useless

The verse begins وَمَا يَسْتَوِي الْبَحْرَانِ: these two bodies of water are not alike.

After stating their difference, however, the Qur’an says:

«وَمِنْ كُلٍّ تَأْكُلُونَ لَحْمًا طَرِيًّا»

Wa-min kullin taʾkulūna laḥman ṭariyyan.

From each, you obtain fresh food.

It also mentions ornaments and the movement of ships.

The difference between fresh and salty water therefore does not mean that one is valuable and the other useless.

Each can play a role in human life.

This distinction matters for understanding the context. Fresh water has a desirable quality for drinking, while salt water too can have an important place in nature and human benefit.

Here furāt describes water’s quality, rather than pronouncing all freshwater resources superior in every respect.

Natural Meaning and Broader Reflection

In some verses, differences between natural things can provide a basis for reflection on truth and falsehood, knowledge and ignorance, or guidance and error.

But in Fāṭir 35:12, the direct meaning must be preserved before any figurative extension: two kinds of water have different qualities, and people benefit from both.

ʿAdhb furāt alone does not establish fresh water as a definite symbol of believers or salt water as a definite symbol of disbelievers.

If such an interpretation is proposed, it requires separate supporting evidence and compatibility with the verse’s context.

C. Al-Mursalāt 77:27 — Fresh Water as a Drinking Blessing

Al-Mursalāt 77:27

وَجَعَلْنَا فِيهَا رَوَاسِيَ شَامِخَاتٍ وَأَسْقَيْنَاكُمْ مَاءً فُرَاتًا

Wa-jaʿalnā fīhā rawāsiya shāmikhātin wa-asqaynākum māʾan furātan.

Translation: “We placed in it lofty, firmly set mountains and provided you with fresh, pleasant water to drink.”

This is the root’s third and final Qur’anic occurrence.

Here furāt appears without ʿadhb:

«مَاءً فُرَاتًا»

Māʾan furātan.

This usage shows that furāt alone is sufficient to describe fresh, pleasant water.

In the two earlier verses, ʿadhb reinforced the description. In al-Mursalāt, furāt itself conveys the familiar quality.

The Role of أَسْقَيْنَاكُمْ

أَسْقَيْنَاكُمْ belongs to س ق ي and here concerns giving people water to drink or providing water for them.

The act of supplying water or giving drink therefore comes from the sentence’s verb.

Māʾ names what is given to people.

Furātan describes that water’s quality.

This distinction matters: translating furāt by itself as “thirst-quenching” may conflate the function of asqaynākum with the adjective’s meaning.

The verse is not merely about water’s existence in nature; people receive it and drink it.

Water’s Connection with the Earth and Mountains

Al-Mursalāt 77:25 and al-Mursalāt 77:26 speak of the earth as a place for the living and the dead.

Al-Mursalāt 77:27 then mentions lofty mountains and fresh water.

Together these verses direct attention to the actual conditions of earthly life: the earth, firmly set heights, and drinking water.

Fresh water is a basic necessity for humans and many other living beings.

The verse does not, however, explain exactly how every freshwater source originated, which geological layer it passed through, or its particular geological relationship to each mountain.

These may be questions for scientific research, but they do not change furāt’s lexical meaning.

In the context of al-Mursalāt, fresh water is a blessing people should contemplate and appreciate.

4. Morphological and Syntactic Notes

فُرَات follows the pattern فُعَال.

The Qur’an uses the word in two grammatical cases.

In al-Furqān 25:53 and Fāṭir 35:12:

فُرَاتٌ

And in al-Mursalāt 77:27:

فُرَاتًا

In the first two verses it appears in the nominal construction هَٰذَا عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ, describing the kind of water.

In al-Mursalāt 77:27, فُرَاتًا is an accusative adjective modifying مَاءً.

The difference between nominative and accusative endings does not change its basic meaning.

Another point is that every Qur’anic occurrence of furāt concerns water. No other verb or verbal noun from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

Recorded lexical forms should therefore not be presented as additional Qur’anic derivatives.

Nor does the pattern فُعَال alone establish extreme intensity or any particular property. The prominence of freshness follows from actual usage and lexical explanations, not morphology alone.

In ʿadhb furāt and milḥ ujāj, related descriptions stand together. Their combination may heighten the contrast, but does not require each word to identify a separate scientific property.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

ف ر ت (فُرَات) and ع ذ ب (عَذْب)

These roots appear together in al-Furqān 25:53 and Fāṭir 35:12.

When applied to water, ʿadhb expresses freshness and pleasantness in contrast to saltiness.

Furāt also describes fresh water, with lexicons emphasizing its pronounced freshness.

ʿAdhb furāt may therefore be translated “fresh and especially pleasant.”

Their difference should not be exaggerated as though ʿadhb meant only salt-free water while furāt expressed something entirely separate, such as microbial purity or therapeutic power.

The words themselves do not establish such distinctions.

ف ر ت (فُرَات) and م ل ح (مِلْح)

Milḥ in these verses indicates water’s saltiness.

Furāt water and milḥ water stand on opposite sides of a contrast: fresh water against salt water.

Salinity also varies in degree. These verses add ujāj to describe the saline side more intensely.

Milḥ itself should therefore not be equated with every characteristic of intensely salty, acrid water.

ف ر ت (فُرَات) and أ ج ج (أُجَاج)

Ujāj describes salty, acrid, disagreeable water.

Opposite ʿadhb furāt, the combination milḥ ujāj depicts intensely salty and unpleasant water.

The Qur’an sets the descriptions in clear contrast.

Nevertheless, ujāj does not mean every kind of dangerous or contaminated water, and furāt is not in itself a scientific guarantee that any fresh water is safe to drink.

Water may taste fresh yet be harmful because of unseen contamination.

This does not alter the verse’s lexical meaning; it shows that freshness, salinity, and sanitary safety are different matters.

ف ر ت (فُرَات) and س و غ (سَاغَ)

س و غ concerns easy passage and agreeable swallowing or drinking.

In Fāṭir 35:12, سَائِغٌ شَرَابُهُ follows ʿadhb furāt.

In al-Naḥl 16:66, سَائِغًا لِلشَّارِبِينَ describes milk.

This shows that sāʾigh is not restricted to fresh water; another beverage may also be agreeable and pleasant to drink.

Furāt therefore describes water’s quality, whereas sāʾigh emphasizes the ease and pleasure of drinking.

ف ر ت (فُرَات) and ر و ي (رَوِيَ)

ر و ي in some Arabic uses concerns becoming satisfied with drink and having one’s thirst relieved.

Furāt describes water; having one’s thirst quenched describes the drinker’s condition.

Fresh, pleasant water can relieve thirst, but that result should not replace the adjective’s primary meaning.

In al-Mursalāt 77:27, أَسْقَيْنَاكُمْ concerns providing drink, while فُرَاتًا identifies the water’s quality.

ف ر ت (فُرَات) and ف ر ث (فَرْث)

These roots may be confused because of their similar letters, but they are semantically distinct.

ف ر ث occurs as فَرْثٍ in al-Naḥl 16:66 and concerns material within an animal’s digestive system.

ف ر ت, however, appears in the Qur’an only for fresh, pleasant water.

The difference between the final letters ت and ث changes the root and its meaning. No relationship should be invented between the families without lexical support.

6. The Euphrates’ Name and the General Adjective

Al-Furāt is also the Arabic name of the great, familiar Euphrates River.

The name has an ancient history in Mesopotamian geography and is recognized in Arabic as the river’s proper name.

None of the Qur’an’s three occurrences of furāt names that river.

Al-Furqān 25:53 discusses two kinds of water.

Fāṭir 35:12 describes the difference between two bodies of water.

Al-Mursalāt 77:27 concerns fresh, pleasant water provided for people to drink.

None of these verses identifies the Euphrates River by name.

Translating māʾan furātan as “water of the Euphrates” would therefore restrict a general description to a particular river without justification.

Conversely, the river name’s presence in ancient regional languages does not permit the Arabic adjective’s entire meaning to be derived from it without historical research.

A proper name and a general adjective may share one linguistic form, while their respective histories and meanings require separate examination.

7. Challenges in Understanding and Translation

One possible error is translating furāt as “very sweet water” in a way that suggests a high sugar content.

In Arabic, water’s ʿudhūbah contrasts with salinity and disagreeable taste. Persian “sweet” must therefore be understood as fresh water, not a sweetened drink.

A second challenge is equating furāt with chemically pure water.

These verses do not specify dissolved substances, salt percentages, or laboratory standards. The word expresses a familiar, perceptible water quality.

A third challenge is assigning the whole meaning of sāʾighun sharābuhu to furāt.

Although pleasant drinking is related to freshness, Fāṭir 35:12 uses a separate expression for it. That contribution should be preserved.

A fourth challenge is restricting furāt to the Euphrates’ name. All its Qur’anic occurrences are general descriptions of water.

A fifth challenge is turning the word into a fixed philosophical or spiritual symbol.

In broader reflection, fresh water may exemplify divine blessing and mercy. But furāt does not lexically mean guidance, truth, moral purity, or faith.

If such ideas enter a reflection, they must be supported by the Qur’an’s network of verses and the context, rather than presented as the root’s direct meaning.

8. Suggested Renderings for Forqan

Natural Persian equivalents of فُرَات correspond to:

“Fresh,” “especially fresh,” “pleasant,” and “especially pleasant.”

When translating water descriptions, “fresh and pleasant” is generally more natural than “very sweet water,” since it avoids confusion with sugary sweetness.

For هَٰذَا عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ in al-Furqān 25:53, a suitable rendering is:

“This one is fresh and pleasant.”

In Fāṭir 35:12, the presence of سَائِغٌ شَرَابُهُ makes it preferable to preserve each expression’s contribution:

“This one is fresh and pleasant, agreeable to drink.”

For مَاءً فُرَاتًا in al-Mursalāt 77:27, a suitable rendering is:

“Fresh, pleasant water.”

This conveys the water’s freshness and fits natural Persian expression.

Brief word-box information may be proposed as follows:

Brief root meaning: The pronounced freshness and pleasant taste of water.

Meaning in this verse: Fresh, pleasant water in contrast to salt water; or a description of water provided for human drinking, according to each verse’s context.

The full distinction between ʿadhb, furāt, and sāʾigh need not be repeated in the word box. The root article is the place for detailed discussion.

9. Conclusion

In its principal established Arabic usage, ف ر ت concerns the pronounced freshness and pleasant taste of water. Furāt describes fresh, agreeable drinking water.

Lexicons clearly record this meaning. Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period also attests the Euphrates’ name, but a proper name’s occurrence does not by itself establish the general adjective’s origin.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an, only as furāt.

Al-Furqān 25:53 contrasts fresh, pleasant water with salty, acrid water.

Fāṭir 35:12 repeats the contrast and separately describes agreeable drinking through sāʾighun sharābuhu.

In al-Mursalāt 77:27, furāt alone describes water The God provides people to drink.

A defensible semantic core is water’s pronounced freshness and pleasant taste, a quality that makes it agreeable in familiar human experience.

This meaning should not be restricted to sugary sweetness, chemical purity, a particular river name, or a fixed spiritual symbol. Other features, such as easy drinking, relief of thirst, and water’s role in life, should be examined separately through the accompanying words and verse contexts.
