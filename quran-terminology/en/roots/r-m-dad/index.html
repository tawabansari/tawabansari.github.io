Scorching heat, the effects of intense heat, and the month name Ramadan

Brief root meaning: Intense, scorching heat, especially the heating of ground, pebbles, and stones under the sun; burning discomfort caused by heat. Extended usages also express pain and distress. Ramaḍān names a particular lunar-calendar month and is lexically associated with this family.

1. Introducing the root and its Quranic form

The three-consonant Arabic root R M Ḍ concerns intense heat, burning sensations, and the effects of heat.

In familiar Arabic usage, ramaḍ denotes scorching heat, especially that of ground, pebbles, and stones exposed to strong sunlight.

Ramḍāʾ also denotes heated ground or a surface whose heat can hurt human or animal feet and make walking difficult.

The related verb ramiḍa can describe a person affected by intense heat or a day that has become very hot.

Extended uses also express pain, discomfort, and inward anguish.

In the Quran, however, the family appears in only one form: Ramaḍāna, the name of a particular lunar month.

This distinction matters. The verse names a month; it does not directly describe hot weather, heated ground, or bodily burning.

The root's lexical meaning can illuminate the name's background, but the name's actual reference and role in the verse must be considered separately.

The form examined and its Quranic occurrence

رَمَضَانَ — Al-Baqarah 2:185

This is the proper name Ramadan in the expression shahru Ramaḍāna, the month of Ramadan.

Other forms are also known in Arabic:

  • رَمَض — ramaḍ: Intense heat and its scorching effect, especially the heat of ground and stones.
  • رَمْضاء — ramḍāʾ: Ground, pebbles, or a surface heated by strong sunlight.
  • رَمِضَ يَرْمَضُ — ramiḍa yarmaḍu: Suffered burning discomfort from heat; of a day, became very hot.
  • رَمِضَتْ قَدَمُهُ — ramiḍat qadamuhu: His foot suffered burning discomfort from the ground's heat.
  • أَرْمَضَ — armaḍa: Caused burning discomfort or exposed something or someone to painful heat.
  • إِرْمَاض — irmāḍ: Causing burning discomfort or distress; extended to causing pain or worry.
  • ارْتَمَضَ — irtamaḍa: Became deeply distressed or worried over something.
  • تَرَمَّضَ — tarammaḍa: Moved in intense heat; in hunting, pursued an animal in daytime heat.
  • رَمَضِيّ — ramaḍiyy: Describing certain rains or clouds near the end of the hot season.
  • رَمِيض — ramīḍ: In another branch, a sharpened, prepared blade or implement.
  • مَرْمُوض — marmūḍ: Something cooked with heated stones.
  • رَمَضَان — Ramaḍān: The familiar lunar month name.

These forms establish the Arabic semantic field, but only Ramaḍāna occurs in the Quran in the forms listed here.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Intense heat: the family's principal sense

An early lexical analysis gives the following foundation for this root:

الرَّاءُ وَالْمِيمُ وَالضَّادُ أَصْلٌ مُطَّرِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى حِدَّةٍ فِي شَيْءٍ مِنْ حَرٍّ وَغَيْرِهِ

al-rāʾu wa-al-mīmu wa-al-ḍādu aṣlun muṭṭaridun yadullu ʿalā ḥiddatin fī shayʾin min ḥarrin wa-ghayrihi

Translation: “Rāʾ, mīm, and ḍād form a regular foundation indicating sharpness or intensity in something, whether heat or otherwise.”

The analysis then explains ramaḍ as the heat of stones under intense sunlight.

The central field involves more than ordinary warmth: heat's intensity and painful effect matter.

Sunlight can warm a place without making the ground painful to walk on.

When stones and pebbles become so hot that contact hurts the feet, the image of ramaḍ and ramḍāʾ is especially clear.

This does not mean that every use necessarily entails an actual skin burn.

Heat can be intense without a specific injury, and the word can also figuratively describe pain and distress.

The principal core is intense heat and its scorching or painful effect, rather than bodily injury as an inevitable outcome.

Ramḍāʾ: ground heated by sunlight

Early lexical definitions connect ramaḍ and ramḍāʾ with intense heat.

Ramaḍ is also explained as the heat of stones and pebbles exposed to the sun.

The surface matters in this usage.

Ground, stones, and pebbles can become extremely hot in strong sunlight. Someone walking barefoot may be unable to remain comfortably on them.

Arabic accordingly says:

رَمِضَتْ قَدَمُهُ مِنَ الرَّمْضَاءِ

ramiḍat qadamuhu mina al-ramḍāʾi

Translation: “His foot suffered burning discomfort from the scorching ground.”

The expression relates the hot surface to its bodily effect.

Ramḍāʾ names the heated ground or surface; ramiḍat qadamuhu describes the effect of contact.

The meanings are connected, but their roles differ.

Ramiḍa yawmunā: our day became very hot

Lexical records attest:

رَمِضَ يَوْمُنَا يَرْمَضُ رَمَضًا

ramiḍa yawmunā yarmaḍu ramaḍan

Meaning: “Our day became intensely hot.”

The day is described by its intense heat.

This is an ordinary expression, comparable to “today is very hot” or “it was a scorching day.”

It does not make time itself a heated material object.

Describing a day as hot refers to its environmental conditions.

The expression cannot establish a theory about the physical nature of time.

Nor is this sense restricted to one season; a day of intense heat can be described this way whenever it occurs.

Armaḍa: causing burning discomfort or distress

Armaḍa al-ḥarru al-qawma means that the heat became harsh and painful for a group.

Heat is the cause of their discomfort.

The family also includes armaḍanī hādhā al-amru.

This means that a matter has distressed, troubled, or pained someone.

The expression extends physical heat to inward distress.

News, conduct, or an event may trouble someone so deeply that they describe the feeling through an image of burning.

That extension does not make every worry or sorrow lexically ramaḍ.

Nor does it establish that Arabic speakers held a single fixed bodily theory of emotions.

It illustrates the use of heat and burning to describe intense distress.

Irtamaḍa: distress and inward strain

Lexical records use irtamaḍa of someone deeply troubled or worried by a matter.

Irtamaḍat kabiduhu can also express inward pain or injury.

These expressions extend the family from tangible heat to intense distress.

They need not report a literal rise in organ temperature in every situation.

An expression can depict anguish rather than provide a medical account of a particular organ.

Physical pain and figurative distress should therefore be distinguished when examining irtamaḍa.

Ramaḍiyy: late-hot-season rain and clouds

Arabic sources use ramaḍiyy for certain rains and clouds at the end of the hot season or near the beginning of autumn.

One explanation is that rain falls on ground still hot from the preceding heat.

The family therefore extends beyond sunlight or dry ground: rain can receive this description through the surroundings' thermal conditions.

Ramaḍiyy in this use is not the month name Ramadan.

Its use for rain does not require Ramadan always to be rainy, summery, or near autumn.

These are separate usages requiring separate consideration.

Ramīḍ: a sharpened blade

Another branch uses ramaḍa al-naṣla for placing a blade or point between stones and hammering it thin and sharp.

This usage is recorded among early explanations of ramḍ.

Sikkīnun ramīḍ denotes a sharp, cutting knife.

It concerns sharpness and intensity but should not simply be equated with solar heat.

Hammering and thinning the blade are central to the method described. The word itself does not establish that the blade was heated in fire.

The lexical idea of ḥiddah, sharpness or intensity, can explain a conceptual connection, but the historical direction between heat and sharpness remains uncertain.

Marmūḍ: food cooked with hot stones

Arabic marmūḍ can describe food cooked using heated stones.

Lexical accounts describe heating stones in fire and using their heat to cook meat.

The connection with intense heat is clear.

The Quran does not use this root to mean cooking or heating food.

This cooking method is one attested lexical use, not a meaning to transfer to the month name or the command of siyam.

Other restricted usages

Lexical records also give tarmīḍ for briefly waiting when visiting someone who is not there.

This usage concerns staying and waiting.

Its exact historical relation to intense heat is unclear and should not be supplied through an invented image.

In hunting, tarammuḍ describes pursuing an animal in intense daytime heat until heat and exhaustion make it easier to catch.

The usage belongs to life and hunting practices in hot surroundings.

This particular method is not the general meaning of ramaḍ.

The family extends from direct heat into practical and social situations, but not every branch occurs in the Quran.

3. Early language and poetry

Hot pebbles in al-Quṭāmī's poetry

A poem by al-Quṭāmī al-Taghlibī states:

يَمْشِينَ رَهْوًا فَلَا الأَعْجَازُ خَاذِلَةٌ
وَلَا الصُّدُورُ عَلَى الأَعْجَازِ تَتَّكِلُ

yamshīna rahwan fa-lā al-aʿjāzu khādhilatun / wa-lā al-ṣudūru ʿalā al-aʿjāzi tattakilu

فَهُنَّ مُعْتَرِضَاتٌ وَالْحَصَى رَمِضٌ
وَالرِّيحُ سَاكِنَةٌ وَالظِّلُّ مُعْتَدِلُ

fa-hunna muʿtariḍātun wa-al-ḥaṣā ramiḍun / wa-al-rīḥu sākinatun wa-al-ẓillu muʿtadilu

Approximate translation: “They walk smoothly; their hindquarters do not fail them, nor do their forequarters rely on their hindquarters. They move across the way while the pebbles are scorching, the wind still, and the shadow even.” Here the shadow's description concerns its position or shape, not moderate air temperature.

Al-ḥaṣā ramiḍ describes pebbles heated by the sun.

It clearly attests ramiḍ as a description of heat at the ground's surface.

The poet combines heated pebbles, still air, and the animals' movement into a tangible scene.

Ramiḍ here does not mean fire, ashes, or inevitable bodily burning. It describes the pebbles' painful heat.

Al-Quṭāmī is an early Islamic poet; this is evidence from after Quranic revelation, not securely pre-Islamic poetry.

The proverb of seeking refuge from scorching ground in fire

Arabic language and literature preserve the expression:

كَالْمُسْتَجِيرِ مِنَ الرَّمْضَاءِ بِالنَّارِ

ka-al-mustajīri mina al-ramḍāʾi bi-al-nāri

Translation: “Like someone seeking refuge from scorching ground in fire.”

It describes escaping a difficulty through a choice that leads to something worse.

Ramḍāʾ is scorching ground; nār is fire.

The image relies on differing intensities: fleeing heated ground into fire moves from a bad situation to a worse one.

Ramḍāʾ itself does not mean misfortune or a bad decision.

The mistaken choice emerges from the relation between the two situations.

Literary accounts connect the saying with different stories from early Arab history. The attribution of the familiar verse and its earliest use are disputed.

It is evidence of a familiar literary use, but no particular origin story should be treated as certain without separate investigation.

Ramadan in a poem attributed to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj

The following verse is attributed to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj; some early works transmit it without naming its poet:

لَقَدْ أَتَى فِي رَمَضَانَ الْمَاضِي
جَارِيَةٌ فِي دِرْعِهَا الْفَضْفَاضِ

la-qad atā fī ramaḍāna al-māḍī / jāriyatun fī dirʿihā al-faḍfāḍi

تُقَطِّعُ الْحَدِيثَ بِالإِيمَاضِ

tuqaṭṭiʿu al-ḥadītha bi-al-īmāḍi

Approximate translation: “Last Ramadan there came a young woman in a loose gown, interrupting conversation with the flash of her smile.” Explanations understand either her own pauses to smile or others breaking off their talk to look at her. Al-īmāḍ here concerns the gleam of a smile, not winking.

Ramadan is used as a month name.

The poet does not say “in the previous scorching heat”; he names a particular past month.

This attests Ramadan as a calendrical name in Arabic.

Ruʾbah lived during the Umayyad and early Abbasid periods. On that attribution, the poem postdates Quranic revelation.

The verse alone does not establish when the name arose or why it was first given.

It does illustrate the distinction between a month name and the root's general meaning.

4. Ramadan as a month name and its possible origin

The connection with intense heat

Early lexical sources associate Ramadan with R M Ḍ.

One early account says that when Arabs replaced older month names with the later familiar names, they linked each to the season or conditions at the time of naming.

On this account, Ramadan coincided with intense heat when it was named.

Another early lexical treatment transmits this with dhakara qawmun, “some people said.”

Even the lexical report presents it as a transmitted explanation, not independently proven historical detail.

The semantic relationship with ramaḍ is intelligible: the name belongs to the intense-heat family.

The word itself does not identify the precise naming date, the people who named it, or the calendar then in use.

Is Ramadan always a hot month?

Ramadan is a lunar month.

In the un-intercalated Islamic lunar calendar, months follow the Moon and move through the solar seasons over successive years.

Ramadan may coincide with summer heat during one period and winter or milder weather during another.

This does not conflict with a possible historical naming connected to heat.

A month name can arise in particular weather conditions yet remain its calendrical name in every season.

The two Arabic months named Rabīʿ provide a parallel: their names do not require them always to coincide with solar spring.

A possible historical naming sense is distinct from the month's actual place in the lunar calendar.

Al-Baqarah 2:185 names a recognized month, not any period of very hot weather.

Was Ramadan named after a fasting person's thirst?

Some lexical accounts transmit another explanation: a fasting person's intense thirst produces an inward feeling of heat or burning.

Ramiḍa al-ṣāʾimu is intelligible as a description of burning discomfort from thirst.

Two questions must nevertheless remain separate.

First, an attested lexical expression does not prove the historical reason for naming a month.

Second, Ramadan is a recognized month name; independent evidence would be needed to claim it was originally coined to describe difficulty in Quranic siyam.

Thirst and heat are also not necessary features of every day of siyam.

People may observe siyam in cold weather or experience different levels of thirst and difficulty.

Ramadan should therefore not be translated as “the month of enduring intense heat and thirst.”

Does Ramadan mean burning away sins?

Some religious writings say that Ramadan burns away or removes sins.

This can function as a spiritual image of forgiveness and moral reform.

It should not be treated as the certain lexical meaning of the month name.

Al-Baqarah 2:185 does not say that the month was named for burning away sins.

Nor does the verse use a verb ramaḍa meaning forgiveness, erasing sin, or moral purification.

Forgiveness and reform require examination through their own relevant verses.

The spiritual image cannot substitute for the historical or lexical meaning of the name.

This distinction allows ethical reflection while preventing it from becoming an unsupported etymological claim.

Ramadan as a proper name

In shahru Ramaḍāna, Ramadan is a proper name.

A person's or place's name may originate in a meaningful word without requiring that word's original sense to replace the name whenever it is mentioned.

If a place name is associated with greenness, brightness, or elevation, it cannot simply be translated as that quality in every sentence.

Ramadan works similarly.

The Quran names a particular month, and the name should be retained.

“The month of scorching heat” replaces a calendrical name with a possible explanation of its origin and is therefore inappropriate.

5. Quranic usage: shahru Ramaḍāna in Al-Baqarah 2:185

The verse

Al-Baqarah 2:185

شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ الَّذِي أُنْزِلَ فِيهِ الْقُرْآنُ هُدًى لِلنَّاسِ وَبَيِّنَاتٍ مِنَ الْهُدَىٰ وَالْفُرْقَانِ ۚ فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ ۖ وَمَنْ كَانَ مَرِيضًا أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ فَعِدَّةٌ مِنْ أَيَّامٍ أُخَرَ ۗ يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ وَلِتُكْمِلُوا الْعِدَّةَ وَلِتُكَبِّرُوا اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَاكُمْ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

shahru ramaḍāna alladhī unzila fīhi al-qurʾānu hudan li-al-nāsi wa-bayyinātin mina al-hudā wa-al-furqāni fa-man shahida minkumu al-shahra fa-l-yaṣumhu wa-man kāna marīḍan aw ʿalā safarin fa-ʿiddatun min ayyāmin ukhara yurīdu allāhu bikumu al-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu al-ʿusra wa-li-tukmilū al-ʿiddata wa-li-tukabbirū allāha ʿalā mā hadākum wa-laʿallakum tashkurūna

Al-Baqarah 2:185

Translation:

“The month of Ramadan, in which the Quran was sent down: guidance for people and clear proofs of guidance and discernment. Whoever among you witnesses the month shall observe siyam in it; whoever is ill or traveling, then a number of other days. The God intends ease for you and does not intend hardship for you; that you complete the prescribed number and magnify The God for having guided you, and that you may give thanks.”

The construction shahru Ramaḍāna

Shahru at the opening means a month.

Ramaḍāna is the proper name of that month.

Grammatically it is the second term of a possessive construction and is genitive.

As a proper name ending in an additional alif and nūn, it is a diptote; here it takes fatḥah in place of kasrah to mark the genitive.

Hence the Quranic form Ramaḍāna.

The fatḥah does not make it accusative in this sentence; it marks the genitive for this noun type.

Nor is Ramadan a verbal noun expressing an act of burning here.

It is a month's proper name, and shahr identifies its category.

Alladhī unzila fīhi al-Qurʾān

The verse introduces Ramadan through a clear characteristic:

الَّذِي أُنْزِلَ فِيهِ الْقُرْآنُ

alladhī unzila fīhi al-qurʾānu

Al-Baqarah 2:185

Meaning: “In which the Quran was sent down.”

This statement is central to Ramadan's role in the verse.

The Quran identifies the month by its revelation, rather than the weather or the name's possible origin.

Ramadan's Quranic significance should not be derived from burning imagery or intense heat.

The explicitly stated characteristic is that the Quran was sent down in this month.

Al-Qadr 97:1 also speaks of its being sent down on the Night of Decree.

That verse matters for revelation's connection with a particular night; the word Ramadan itself does not explain every stage of revelation.

For this root study, the clear point is that Ramadan names the month to which the Quran attributes its revelation.

Hudan li-al-nās: guidance

The Quran is then described as hudan li-al-nās, guidance for people.

The verse also mentions bayyinātin mina al-hudā wa-al-furqān.

These descriptions concern the Quran itself.

Hudā concerns guidance, bayyināt clear proofs or signs, and furqān distinguishing truth from falsehood.

The month is thus identified through the revelation of a book for human guidance, beyond merely supplying a calendar date.

These features should not become the lexical meaning of Ramadan.

Ramadan does not lexically mean guidance, clear proofs, or discernment.

The verse expresses those concepts through separate words.

Fa-man shahida minkumu al-shahra fa-l-yaṣumhu

The verse continues:

فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ

fa-man shahida minkumu al-shahra fa-l-yaṣumhu

Al-Baqarah 2:185

Al-shahr refers back to Ramadan.

The pronoun -hu in fa-l-yaṣumhu also refers to the month.

The command concerns the month named at the beginning.

This matters in context: Al-Baqarah 2:184 mentions numbered days, followed by Ramadan in Al-Baqarah 2:185.

The temporal scope is established by the sentences, rather than the lexical meaning of R M Ḍ.

Ramadan itself does not mean thirty days, twenty-nine days, or a particular duration of heat.

Its days are counted through the beginning and end of the lunar month.

Al-Baqarah 2:189 also describes lunar crescents as time markers for people.

Ramadan's identification is therefore tied to its calendrical role as a lunar month.

Ramadan and ease in observing siyam

After addressing illness and travel, the verse states:

يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ

yurīdu allāhu bikumu al-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu al-ʿusra

Al-Baqarah 2:185

Meaning: “The God intends ease for you and does not intend hardship for you.”

This guards against a mistaken inference.

Someone might derive from Ramadan's lexical connection with intense heat that thirst, suffering, and bodily hardship are the principal purpose of siyam.

The Quran does not state that conclusion.

Al-Baqarah 2:183 connects siyam with attaining taqwā. Al-Baqarah 2:184 discusses illness, travel, and human capacity. Al-Baqarah 2:185 expressly states the intention of ease rather than hardship.

The name's possible origin must not become a rule conflicting with the verse's explicit statement of ease.

This does not deny that worship can involve natural difficulty.

Difficulty is not an end to derive from the name and then increase through newly invented requirements.

The separate study “Siyam in the Quran: Taqwa, Human Capacity, and the Place of Fidyah” examines siyam, human capacity, fidyah, and the scope of the ruling in detail.

Study of siyam and fidyah

The essential distinction here is this: the month name is lexically associated with intense heat, but siyam's rulings come from the Quran's commands and explanations, not the name's etymology.

Must Ramadan be hot everywhere?

Ramadan is identified by the lunar month.

In the same year, one region may be hot while another is cold.

As its seasonal position changes, daylight duration and weather conditions vary across locations.

Heat is therefore not a condition for the month to be Ramadan.

Cold weather does not invalidate the month, nor does a very hot period become Ramadan simply because it is hot.

The name has a particular calendar reference.

This also matters practically, since people's actual conditions differ across seasons and regions.

Any ruling concerning those conditions requires examination of the siyam verses and human capacity, rather than the month name's earlier sense.

6. Related roots and translation questions

R M Ḍ (ramaḍ) and Ḥ R R (ḥarr)

Both families overlap in the field of heat.

Arabic ḥarr has a broad heat sense.

Al-Naḥl 16:81 uses it in connection with protection from heat.

The familiar use of ramaḍ emphasizes intense, scorching heat, especially sun-heated ground or stones.

In this thermal branch, ramaḍ describes intense heat, but not every ordinary warmth warrants that word. Other branches, such as sharpening a blade, require separate explanation.

The distinction does not establish an absolute rule that ḥarr can never mean intense heat.

Ḥarr can also be very intense in context.

R M Ḍ (ramaḍ) and Ḥ R Q (ḥarq)

Ḥarq concerns burning and setting alight.

Some uses of ramaḍ involve burning discomfort or heat injury.

The roots are nevertheless distinct.

Very hot ground can burn someone's foot, connecting ground heat with bodily injury.

Ramaḍ does not always mean complete combustion in fire.

Ramadan is a month name in the Quran and should not be rendered “the month of burning” because of its semantic proximity to heat.

R M Ḍ (ramaḍ) and R M D (ramād)

The roots differ only in the final consonant, but their principal senses differ.

Quranic R M D concerns ashes.

Ibrāhīm 14:18 uses ka-ramādin for ashes scattered by a violent wind.

R M Ḍ instead concerns intense heat and burning discomfort.

Ashes may remain after combustion, in which heat plays a role.

Ashes are nevertheless not heat itself.

Ramād and ramaḍ should not be conflated because of similar sounds or their connection with heat.

R M Ḍ (ramaḍ) and Ṣ W M (ṣawm)

Ṣawm belongs to Ṣ W M and concerns abstention and restraint in Quranic usage.

Al-Baqarah 2:183 and Al-Baqarah 2:185 place siyam within a command of worship.

Ramadan names a month.

The families are lexically distinct.

Siyam denotes abstention whose form is understood through the Quran and the ruling's context; Ramadan names the particular period connected with that command.

Ramadan does not originally mean siyam, and siyam does not mean intense heat.

Their association in one verse does not make their roots or meanings identical.

R M Ḍ (ramaḍ) and Sh H R (shahr)

Shahr names a month as a unit of time.

Ramadan names one such month.

They occur together as shahru Ramaḍāna in Al-Baqarah 2:185.

Shahr gives the kind of temporal unit; Ramadan identifies it.

Ramadan should not become the generic word for month, nor should shahr be translated as heat.

The construction does not require shahr to precede every Arabic mention of Ramadan.

Arabic can use the month's name alone, although this Quranic occurrence uses the full expression.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan and conclusions

Translating the Quranic form

Word: رَمَضَانَ — Al-Baqarah 2:185

Direct equivalent:

“Ramadan.”

In the full phrase:

شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ — “The month of Ramadan.”

This rendering is precise and natural.

As a proper name, it should not be replaced by “the month of scorching heat,” “the month of thirst,” or “the month of burning away sins.”

Its lexical connection with intense heat belongs in the root study.

The supplied rendering, “the month of Ramadan, in which the Quran was sent down,” preserves the direct sense and does not call for an etymological replacement of the name.

A proposed Forqan word box

Word: رَمَضَانَ

Root: R M Ḍ

Brief root meaning: Intense, scorching heat, especially sun-heated ground and stones; heat-induced burning discomfort and distress.

Meaning in this verse: Ramadan, the particular lunar month to which the Quran attributes its revelation and connects the command of siyam.

The Quranic form Ramadan should remain a proper name in the box. These proposals do not replace existing authored verse-box explanations.

A possible naming history must not replace the word's contextual meaning.

Conclusions and final definition

Arabic R M Ḍ is best known for intense heat, burning discomfort, and heat's painful effect.

Ramaḍ particularly concerns the heat of ground and stones exposed to sunlight.

Ramḍāʾ names a scorching surface that can hurt human or animal feet.

Extended uses express pain, distress, and inward anguish.

Other attested branches include ramīḍ for a sharpened blade, marmūḍ for food cooked with hot stones, and ramaḍiyy for certain late-hot-season rains and clouds.

These senses have conceptual and practical connections, but should not all be derived from one another without historical evidence.

Lexical sources also associate Ramadan with this family.

One early explanation relates its naming to intense heat when the month names were established.

Another associates it with a fasting person's thirst.

Their historical standing is not identical, and neither permits replacing the Quranic name with heat, thirst, or burning away sins.

The family occurs only once in the Quran: Ramaḍāna in Al-Baqarah 2:185.

It is the proper name of a lunar month.

The Quran identifies the month through revelation, links siyam to it, and then emphasizes ease rather than intended hardship.

Intense heat comes from the root's lexical field; Ramadan's Quranic role and associated rulings come from the verses themselves.

Final definition of R M Ḍ:

Its principal field is intense scorching heat, especially sun-heated ground and stones, and the burning discomfort or distress caused by that heat.

Extended usages express intense pain or distress; some other derivatives concern sharpening blades and cooking with heated stones.

Ramadan names a particular month lexically associated with this family, but functions as a proper name in the Quran.

Forqan's study should therefore distinguish three subjects:

The root's lexical meaning, the possible historical origin of the month name, and Ramadan's Quranic role and rulings.

The root concerns intense heat; the exact naming history requires historical inquiry; the Quranic role concerns revelation, guidance, siyam, and explicit divine commands.

This prevents a month name from generating new religious rulings or making heat and bodily difficulty the purpose of siyam.

For Al-Baqarah 2:185, “the month of Ramadan” is the appropriate sufficient rendering. The root article explains its wider connection with heat and burning discomfort.
