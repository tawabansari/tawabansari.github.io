Turning away, inclining to one side, and two facing sides: from aversion to the signs to mountain faces

The Arabic root Ṣ D F has several semantic branches. The main ones concern turning or inclining away from something; the side of a body or place; two sides facing each other; and the hard shell of a marine animal.

The verb صَدَفَ عَنْ means to turn away or withdraw from something. The noun الصَّدَف, by contrast, can designate a mountain side or an elevated face opposite another side. صَادَفَ is also used in Arabic for encountering, meeting, or finding someone or something.

The root occurs five times in three Qur’anic verses. Its four verbal occurrences are in Al-Anʿām 6:46 and Al-Anʿām 6:157; its only nominal occurrence is in Al-Kahf 18:96.

The Qur’anic forms are يَصْدِفُونَ, occurring three times; وَصَدَفَ, once; and الصَّدَفَيْنِ, once.

The crucial distinction is that Al-Anʿām describes people turning away from the signs, whereas الصَّدَفَيْنِ in Al-Kahf denotes two facing rock sides or mountain faces.

The root should therefore neither be restricted to aversion nor have every derivative explained through seashells or accidental collision.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five occurrences in three verses

1. Semantic cores of the root

Turning away and departing from a direction

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris proposes two semantic principles for this family: inclination or deviation, and the seashell.

Of the first branch he writes:

صَدَفَ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ، إِذَا مَالَ عَنْهُ وَوَلَّى ذَاهِبًا

ṣadafa ʿani al-shayʾi idhā māla ʿanhu wa-wallā dhāhiban

That is, someone turns away from something, inclines away from it, and goes off in another direction.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains الصُّدُوف as inclining away from something.

This may concern physical movement, such as turning one’s face or path away from someone. It may also concern attention, decision, or conduct, such as withdrawing from a statement or sign presented to a person.

The central image in this branch is of someone facing something or following a direction, then moving aside and turning away.

The verb صَدَفَ alone, however, does not fully identify the person’s intention. Someone may turn away deliberately, withdraw through lack of interest, or choose another direction. The reason and value of the conduct depend on context.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: two principles and aversion

Physical deviation: an animal’s crooked legs

In Arabic, الصَّدَف also designates a deviation or inclination of the feet or hooves.

Ibn Fāris explains it through a camel’s hoof inclining to one side.

Lexicons also record أَصْدَف for a horse whose legs or hooves are inclined or crooked.

Some definitions describe the knees approaching one another while the hooves stand apart.

This use relates semantically to inclination and deviation. Its subject, however, is an animal’s outward bodily conformation, rather than a human decision or moral aversion.

It shows that inclination and deviation in this family extend beyond human responses to statements or beliefs.

Side and flank

صَدَف and صَدَفَة are also recorded in Arabic for a side or flank.

With reference to a mountain, صَدَف can denote its projecting side or rock face.

Some lexical accounts extend it to any large, elevated formation, such as a substantial wall or high rock mass.

Ibn Fāris connects this use with a mountain side inclining in a particular direction.

This provides an intelligible semantic relationship: one mountain side has a direction and extent relative to another surface or side.

It does not prove, however, that every mountain ṣadaf has a steep slope or inclines at a fixed angle.

What matters in the Qur’anic use is the presence of two facing rock sides, rather than a measurement of their slopes.

Seashell

Another branch is الصَّدَف, the hard shell of a marine animal.

In Tahdhīb al-Lughah, an account attributed to Al-Layth describes a marine covering whose two halves enclose an animal’s soft body. A pearl may form within such a shell.

In this use, صَدَفَة denotes one shell or a half of it, and أَصْدَاف is its familiar plural.

This meaning is also familiar in Persian through the word ṣadaf.

Ibn Fāris nevertheless separates the seashell branch from inclination and aversion. His distinction shows that even early lexical analysis did not take a common origin for all the meanings for granted.

The two halves of a shell and two facing mountain sides can be compared visually. That resemblance alone does not establish which name, if either, arose from the other.

Nor does الصَّدَفَيْنِ in Al-Kahf 18:96 refer to two marine shells.

2. Uses in early Arabic

Turning aside in Al-Aʿshā’s poetry

A verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā, the pre-Islamic poet, reads:

وَلَقَدْ سَاءَهَا الْبَيَاضُ فَلَطَّتْ
بِحِجَابٍ مِنْ بَيْنِنَا مَصْدُوفِ

wa-laqad sāʾahā al-bayāḍu fa-laṭṭat / bi-ḥijābin min bayninā maṣdūfi

An approximate rendering: “The whiteness [of my hair] displeased her, so she drew a curtain between us and concealed herself from my sight.”

In one transmitted reading, مَصْدُوف is explained as concealed or screened from view.

The wording is not uniform across sources: some transmit مَسْدُوف instead.

The verse thus supplies an early poetic image of withdrawal and screening. The variant wording, however, prevents it from being decisive evidence for every morphological feature of maṣdūf.

Moreover, the sense of turning away in صَدَفَ عَنْ has independent lexical support; it does not depend on choosing one of these poetic variants.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the masdūf variant in Al-Aʿshā’s witness

Ṣadūf: someone who turns away

Arabic sources record امرأة صَدُوف for a woman who turns away from someone or pays that person no attention.

Some accounts apply the description to a woman who withdraws from her husband.

Here صَدُوف describes the conduct of turning away.

This does not make Ṣ D F inherently a root about women, marriage, or lack of affection. These are examples of its use.

Nor does the description alone establish a person’s reasons or intentions.

Ṣawādif: camels standing aside

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab record الصَّوَادِف for camels standing beside a watering place, waiting for other camels to drink and move away.

The following rajaz fragment is cited in their description:

النَّاظِرَاتُ الْعُقَبَ الصَّوَادِفُ

al-nāẓirātu al-ʿuqaba al-ṣawādifu

That is, “The camels standing aside and waiting for their turn.”

Ṣawādif here refers to animals beside the main route or place of drinking.

It describes their position and conduct, without implying aversion to truth or hostility toward others.

The example shows how standing aside, moving out of a route, and being at the side can be related uses.

The fragment’s precise date and poet are not identified in the account examined. It should therefore not be assigned to the pre-Islamic period without independent evidence.

Maqāyīs and Lisān: the ṣawādif fragment with the reading al-ʿuqab

Ṣādafa: encountering and finding

In Arabic, صَادَفَ فُلَانًا means to encounter or meet someone.

For example:

صَادَفْتُ فُلَانًا بِمَوْضِعِ كَذَا

ṣādaftu fulānan bi-mawḍiʿi kadhā

That is, “I met him at such-and-such a place.”

Lexicons also record مُصَادَفَة for correspondence, meeting, or encounter.

The direction involved differs from صَدَفَ عَنْ.

With صَدَفَ عَنْ, someone turns away from something.

With صَادَفَ, two people or things meet or encounter each other.

Lexicographers also explain a connection with صَدَفَا الْجَبَلِ, since two mountain sides may face each other.

That relationship can be examined without restricting every use of ṣādafa to an accidental, causeless encounter.

In present-day Persian, taṣādof may mean an unintended collision, while “accidental” may describe an unexpected event. These modern uses should not automatically be imported into Qur’anic Ṣ D F.

3. Turning away from the signs in the Qur’an

Al-Anʿām 6:46: ثُمَّ هُمْ يَصْدِفُونَ

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِنْ أَخَذَ اللَّهُ سَمْعَكُمْ وَأَبْصَارَكُمْ وَخَتَمَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِكُمْ مَنْ إِلَٰهٌ غَيْرُ اللَّهِ يَأْتِيكُمْ بِهِ ۗ انْظُرْ كَيْفَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ ثُمَّ هُمْ يَصْدِفُونَ

qul a-raʾaytum in akhadha allāhu samʿakum wa-abṣārakum wa-khatama ʿalā qulūbikum man ilāhun ghayru allāhi yaʾtīkum bihi unẓur kayfa nuṣarrifu al-āyāti thumma hum yaṣdifūna

Translation: Say: “Have you considered: if The God took away your hearing and sight and sealed your hearts, what deity other than Him could restore it to you? See how We present the signs in varied ways; then they turn away.”

يَصْدِفُونَ is the third-person masculine plural imperfect of the Form I verb صَدَفَ.

In ثُمَّ هُمْ يَصْدِفُونَ, the person or thing turned away from is not repeated with the preposition ʿan. The preceding sentence nevertheless makes clear that the subject is the divine signs.

“They turn away” therefore fits the verse’s construction.

Distinguishing نُصَرِّفُ from يَصْدِفُونَ

The earlier part says:

انْظُرْ كَيْفَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ

unẓur kayfa nuṣarrifu al-āyāti

نُصَرِّفُ belongs to Ṣ R F and here concerns presenting the signs in varied forms and ways.

يَصْدِفُونَ belongs to Ṣ D F and expresses turning away.

Despite their visual and phonetic resemblance, the words belong to different families.

The verse presents a clear contrast: the signs are offered in varied ways, yet a group continues to turn away from them.

The criticism thus concerns more than initial lack of knowledge. It concerns how people respond when signs are presented to them and they withdraw their attention.

The verb يَصْدِفُونَ alone, however, does not specify how much each person inwardly knows or why that person turns away.

In this verse, the aversion follows the presentation of the signs. The context indicates responsibility in responding to them, but the root’s meaning alone cannot establish everyone’s intention.

Does يَصْدِفُونَ mean preventing others?

Some commentators, alongside turning away, also mention preventing others from following the truth when explaining this verse.

The direct construction ثُمَّ هُمْ يَصْدِفُونَ, however, contains no object identifying other people being prevented.

Lexicons also clearly attest صَدَفَ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ for a person’s own turning away.

“They turn away” is therefore the more precise direct translation.

Someone who turns away may encourage others to do so, but that second conduct requires independent contextual evidence.

Ibn Kathīr: aversion and the interpretation of preventing others

Al-Anʿām 6:157: denial and aversion

أَوْ تَقُولُوا لَوْ أَنَّا أُنْزِلَ عَلَيْنَا الْكِتَابُ لَكُنَّا أَهْدَىٰ مِنْهُمْ ۚ فَقَدْ جَاءَكُمْ بَيِّنَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ وَهُدًى وَرَحْمَةٌ ۚ فَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنْ كَذَّبَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَصَدَفَ عَنْهَا ۗ سَنَجْزِي الَّذِينَ يَصْدِفُونَ عَنْ آيَاتِنَا سُوءَ الْعَذَابِ بِمَا كَانُوا يَصْدِفُونَ

aw taqūlū law annā unzila ʿalaynā al-kitābu la-kunnā ahdā minhum fa-qad jāʾakum bayyinatun min rabbikum wa-hudan wa-raḥmatun fa-man aẓlamu mimman kadhdhaba bi-āyāti allāhi wa-ṣadafa ʿanhā sa-najzī alladhīna yaṣdifūna ʿan āyātinā sūʾa al-ʿadhābi bi-mā kānū yaṣdifūna

Translation: Or lest you say: “Had the Scripture been sent down to us, we would have been better guided than they.” Clear evidence from your Lord, guidance, and mercy have now come to you. Who, then, is more unjust than someone who denies the signs of The God and turns away from them? We shall repay those who turn away from Our signs with severe punishment for their persistent turning away.

Ṣ D F occurs three times in this verse:

وَصَدَفَ عَنْهَا

wa-ṣadafa ʿanhā

يَصْدِفُونَ عَنْ آيَاتِنَا

yaṣdifūna ʿan āyātinā

كَانُوا يَصْدِفُونَ

kānū yaṣdifūna

This repetition places the conduct of turning away at the center of attention.

كَذَّبَ and صَدَفَ: two distinct verbs

The earlier part says:

كَذَّبَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَصَدَفَ عَنْهَا

kadhdhaba bi-āyāti allāhi wa-ṣadafa ʿanhā

كَذَّبَ belongs to K DH B and here means to deny or treat the signs as false.

صَدَفَ belongs to Ṣ D F and expresses turning away.

The two verbs occur together but do not have identical meanings.

Denial concerns someone’s position on the truth of the signs. Aversion describes withdrawing from them and declining to attend to or follow them.

Someone may consider something false and turn away from it; both actions are attributed to one person here.

Their co-occurrence does not make explicit denial part of every Arabic use of صَدَفَ.

In Al-Anʿām 6:46, the concluding phrase does not itself contain a word for denial, even though the surah’s wider context concerns deniers’ conduct.

Denial and aversion are therefore related but distinct actions.

Study of K DH B

عَنْهَا: turning away from what?

In وَصَدَفَ عَنْهَا, the pronoun hā refers back to آيَاتِ اللَّهِ.

The preposition عَنْ marks the movement away or withdrawal.

The combination thus means turning away from the signs.

This need not be a physical movement away from a text or person, since its object is the divine signs.

The verb here concerns intellectual, religious, and practical conduct.

That nonphysical use does not mean the root’s core sense has become wholly unrelated to changing direction. The relationship between physically turning away and withdrawing from a statement or truth is recognizable in Arabic.

كَانُوا يَصْدِفُونَ: recurrent conduct

The verse ends with:

بِمَا كَانُوا يَصْدِفُونَ

bi-mā kānū yaṣdifūna

In this context, kānū with the imperfect emphasizes conduct in the past and its continuation or repetition.

The verse thus presents turning away as something they used to do, rather than describing only a passing encounter.

The continued conduct comes from كَانُوا يَصْدِفُونَ as a construction; Ṣ D F does not inherently mean perpetual repetition.

Does every turning away entail equal responsibility?

The verb صَدَفَ itself does not measure each person’s knowledge, motivation, or capacity to understand.

Al-Anʿām 6:157 nevertheless provides important context: clear evidence, guidance, and mercy have come, after which denial and aversion are described.

The issue is therefore how people respond to the signs and evidence made available to them, rather than mere failure to receive a message.

Determining that a particular person fully recognized the truth and knowingly denied it still requires further evidence.

A distinction must therefore be maintained between the lexical act of turning away, the blameworthy conduct described in the verse, and judgments about people’s intentions.

Al-Ṭabarī: denial, aversion, and its recompense

4. الصَّدَفَيْنِ: two facing mountain sides

Al-Kahf 18:96

آتُونِي زُبَرَ الْحَدِيدِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا سَاوَىٰ بَيْنَ الصَّدَفَيْنِ قَالَ انْفُخُوا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَعَلَهُ نَارًا قَالَ آتُونِي أُفْرِغْ عَلَيْهِ قِطْرًا

ātūnī zubara al-ḥadīdi ḥattā idhā sāwā bayna al-ṣadafayni qāla unfukhū ḥattā idhā jaʿalahu nāran qāla ātūnī ufrigh ʿalayhi qiṭran

Translation: “Bring me pieces of iron.” Then, when he had leveled [the structure] between the two mountain sides, he said: “Blow.” Then, when he had made it like fire, he said: “Bring me [molten copper] to pour over it.”

The verse belongs to the account of Dhū Al-Qarnayn and the barrier he builds.

In Al-Kahf 18:93 he reaches the two barriers or mountains; in Al-Kahf 18:94 the people request a barrier.

In Al-Kahf 18:95, he asks them to assist him with strength and labor.

Al-Kahf 18:96 then describes bringing pieces of iron and building the structure.

الصَّدَفَيْنِ here denotes the two facing natural rock sides.

The meaning of صَدَف in this verse

Lexicons record:

الصَّدَفُ جَانِبُ الْجَبَلِ

al-ṣadafu jānibu al-jabali

That is, “Ṣadaf is the side of a mountain.”

Some definitions also describe the two sides of a valley or mountain passage.

In explaining the verse, Al-Ṭabarī likewise treats the word as the sides or flanks of the mountains.

“Two mountain sides” or “two facing rock faces” therefore fits the context.

Does الصَّدَفَيْنِ mean two entire mountains?

Some explanations paraphrase the term as “two mountains.”

The paraphrase is understandable in context, since the two facing sides belong to mountainous formations.

More precisely, however, ṣadaf denotes the side or face of a mountain, rather than necessarily the whole mass from base to summit.

Some transmitted explanations also use رُؤُوسُ الْجَبَلَيْنِ, meaning the upper parts of the two mountains.

This variation mainly concerns the extent of the rock formation intended and does not change the verse’s overall meaning.

In direct translation, “two mountain sides” conveys the word’s particular feature more clearly than “two mountains.”

Were the two sides necessarily touching?

Some lexical explanations say the sides are called ṣadafān because they face each other.

Facing each other does not mean touching.

In Dhū Al-Qarnayn’s account, there is a space between the two sides that a barrier is to close.

The context thus indicates opposing sides with an open space between them.

الصَّدَفَيْنِ should therefore not be understood as two fully adjoining walls or two mountains with no gap.

The role of سَاوَىٰ بَيْنَ الصَّدَفَيْنِ

The verb سَاوَىٰ belongs to S W Y.

It refers to leveling or making equal.

بَيْنَ الصَّدَفَيْنِ identifies where that action occurs.

In the barrier-building context, Dhū Al-Qarnayn places iron pieces in the gap between the rock sides until the structure reaches the level expressed by sāwā.

الصَّدَفَيْنِ itself does not specify the barrier’s exact height, breadth, length, or geometry.

Nor does the word name the mountains, their geographical location, or the complete engineering method.

Al-Kahf 18:97 describes Gog and Magog’s inability to climb or pierce the barrier. These are consequences of the construction and should not be added to the lexical definition of ṣadaf.

Variant readings of الصَّدَفَيْنِ

Three vocalizations occur among recognized readings:

الصَّدَفَيْنِ, with fatḥah on both ṣād and dāl;

الصُّدُفَيْنِ, with ḍammah on both ṣād and dāl;

الصُّدْفَيْنِ, with ḍammah on ṣād and sukūn on dāl.

Al-Ṭabarī treats all three as established Arabic forms with the same meaning here.

The variants show diversity in pronunciation; they do not yield three meanings or three different kinds of mountain.

All three refer to two facing rock sides.

Al-Ṭabarī: the two mountain sides and three vocalizations

Is a mountain ṣadaf related to a seashell?

The two halves of a marine shell can face each other and enclose something between them.

The two mountain sides likewise face each other with a space in between.

That resemblance can help explain a visual relationship between the uses.

Resemblance in shape alone, however, does not establish a word’s historical origin.

Nor should Qur’anic الصَّدَفَيْنِ be translated “two seashells.” The mountains, iron pieces, and barrier construction establish the geographical rock sense.

5. Important forms and extensions

صَدَفَ, يَصْدِفُ

In behavioral uses, the Form I verb صَدَفَ refers to turning or inclining away from something.

Its past form occurs with ʿan in Al-Anʿām 6:157.

Its imperfect appears in Al-Anʿām 6:46 without a repeated complement, which is nevertheless clear from context.

Arabic records the verbal nouns صَدْف and صُدُوف for this branch.

أَصْدَفَ

Arabic records أَصْدَفَهُ عَنْهُ for making someone turn or withdraw from something.

The subject here affects another person’s direction or conduct.

This differs from صَدَفَ عَنْ: in one construction someone turns away personally, while in the other an agent makes someone else turn away.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

صَادَفَ and مُصَادَفَة

صَادَفَ refers to encountering, meeting, or finding something.

Arabic also records مُصَادَفَة for encounter, meeting, and correspondence.

In modern Persian, moṣādafeh and taṣādof may convey an accidental encounter or unexpected event.

Those senses should not be assigned to all early uses without evidence.

For the two mountain sides, the relevant feature is their facing each other, rather than any accidental quality.

صَدُوف and أَصْدَف

In behavioral use, صَدُوف describes someone who turns away.

In an animal’s description, أَصْدَف concerns crooked or inclined legs.

The forms and their subjects differ and should not receive one identical Persian equivalent.

صَدَف, صَدَفَة, and أَصْدَاف

In the marine branch, صَدَف names a marine animal’s hard shell.

صَدَفَة denotes one shell, and أَصْدَاف denotes several.

In the mountain-related use, however, this family can express a rock side or flank.

Formal resemblance alone is therefore insufficient: the noun must be examined in its actual construction.

Ṣadif: names of peoples and individuals

Arabic sources also record الصَّدِف as the name of a group of Arab tribes in Yemen.

This is an ethnic proper name. Its naming should not be derived from aversion or seashells without independent historical evidence.

The name’s presence in Arabic texts does not mean it occurs as a separate Qur’anic derivative.

Arabic lexicons: derived forms and the tribal name Al-Ṣadif

6. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ D F (صَدَفَ) and Ṣ D D (صَدَّ)

These roots overlap in uses concerning turning away.

صَدَّ عَنْ can express someone’s own aversion; صَدَّهُ عَنْ is also used for preventing someone else from following a path or doing something.

صَدَفَ عَنْ likewise expresses turning or withdrawing from something.

Qur’anic verbal Ṣ D F emphasizes the person’s own aversion, whereas Ṣ D D frequently describes preventing others from following the way of The God or accessing the Sacred Mosque.

This difference in Qur’anic use should not become an absolute distinction throughout Arabic: lexicons also attest transitive Ṣ D F forms.

In its three Qur’anic verses, Ṣ D F thus concerns turning away or mountain sides, while Ṣ D D appears across a broader network of aversion and obstruction.

Study of Ṣ D D

Ṣ D F (صَدَفَ) and ʿ R Ḍ (أَعْرَضَ)

أَعْرَضَ عَنْ means to turn or withdraw from something.

Commentaries commonly explain يَصْدِفُونَ through يُعْرِضُونَ.

The semantic proximity is clear in Al-Anʿām 6:46 and Al-Anʿām 6:157.

Ṣ D F also has branches concerning mountain sides, seashells, and encounter, which differ from the usual sense of iʿrāḍ.

Synonymy in one context does not make all forms of these roots interchangeable in every sentence.

Study of ʿ R Ḍ

Ṣ D F (صَدَفَ) and M Y L (مَالَ)

M Y L concerns inclination, changing direction, and leaning toward something.

Ibn Fāris likewise explains the main behavioral branch of Ṣ D F through inclination.

The relationship is particularly close in the use concerning crooked legs.

M Y L can concern emotional or bodily inclination or a change of direction. صَدَفَ عَنْ, however, emphasizes moving or turning away from something.

In that construction, every ṣadafa involves a change of direction, but every inclination is not necessarily an aversion.

Ṣ D F (صَدَفَ) and L Q Y (لَقِيَ)

لَقِيَ concerns meeting and encountering in Arabic.

Some uses of صَادَفَ also mean meeting or encountering.

The two families nevertheless have different ranges.

لَقِيَ occurs in varied human and nonhuman contexts in the Qur’an, whereas صَادَفَ as a verb of meeting does not occur there.

The sense of meeting therefore cannot be imposed on يَصْدِفُونَ in Al-Anʿām.

Study of L Q Y

Ṣ D F (صَدَفَ) and S D D (سَدَّ)

سَدَّ concerns closing a gap or establishing a barrier.

Al-Kahf 18:93 contains السَّدَّيْنِ, and Al-Kahf 18:94 mentions building a barrier.

الصَّدَفَيْنِ in Al-Kahf 18:96, however, refers to the two mountain sides.

The distinction matters: the mountain sides are the site of the construction; ṣadaf itself does not name the constructed barrier.

In the same account, Dhū Al-Qarnayn uses iron pieces to close the gap between the sides.

الصَّدَفَيْنِ thus names the two natural sides, whereas sadd or radm concerns a barrier built to prevent passage.

The phonetic resemblance of Ṣ D F and S D D does not establish shared derivation or identical meanings.

7. Challenges in understanding and translation

One difficulty is that ṣadaf in modern Persian mainly suggests a marine shell.

In Al-Kahf 18:96, however, its meaning is quite different.

Translating الصَّدَفَيْنِ as “two shells” without explanation would suggest two marine shells, whereas the verse describes two rock sides in mountainous terrain.

Another difficulty is equating ṣadafa with ṣadda.

In Al-Anʿām, صَدَفَ means turning away; it should not be changed into preventing other people’s movement without contextual evidence.

Nor does the verb’s negative use in two verses mean the root always denotes sin, unbelief, or denial.

Turning away is an action. Its value depends on what someone turns away from and on the sentence’s other contextual indicators.

In Al-Anʿām 6:157, aversion accompanies denial of the signs and is condemned. That condemnation should not be generalized to the very concept of changing direction.

الصَّدَفَيْنِ likewise does not specify exact height, geographical location, or the faces’ mineral composition.

Facing each other does not establish that the sides were equal in size, perfectly parallel, or equally inclined.

The account clearly describes building a barrier, but its unstated details should not be filled with geographical or engineering conjectures.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

In Al-Anʿām 6:46:

ثُمَّ هُمْ يَصْدِفُونَ

thumma hum yaṣdifūna

Preferred rendering:

“Then they turn away.”

This preserves the verb’s meaning; what they turn away from is clear from the preceding context.

In Al-Anʿām 6:157:

وَصَدَفَ عَنْهَا

wa-ṣadafa ʿanhā

Preferred rendering:

“And turned away from them.”

In the same verse:

سَنَجْزِي الَّذِينَ يَصْدِفُونَ عَنْ آيَاتِنَا

sa-najzī alladhīna yaṣdifūna ʿan āyātinā

Preferred rendering:

“We shall repay those who turn away from Our signs.”

And for:

بِمَا كَانُوا يَصْدِفُونَ

bi-mā kānū yaṣdifūna

Preferred rendering:

“For their turning away.”

The continued or repeated conduct here is conveyed by كَانُوا يَصْدِفُونَ as a construction.

In Al-Kahf 18:96:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا سَاوَىٰ بَيْنَ الصَّدَفَيْنِ

ḥattā idhā sāwā bayna al-ṣadafayni

Preferred rendering:

“Then, when he had leveled [the structure] between the two mountain sides.”

“Two facing rock faces” is also suitable as a lexical explanation.

“Two seashells,” “two perfectly parallel mountains,” and “two barriers” are unsuitable equivalents for this combination.

For non-Qur’anic uses, صَادَفَ can be rendered “met” or “encountered”; in a marine context, صَدَفَة can be translated “shell” or “seashell.”

9. Conclusion

Ṣ D F is a family with several branches in Arabic.

In the verbal branch, صَدَفَ عَنْ means to turn or incline away from something. Physical uses in the same family concern crooked feet or a change of direction.

In the spatial branch, صَدَف can mean the side of a mountain or elevated surface; الصَّدَفَانِ can denote two facing sides.

Elsewhere, صَادَفَ concerns encounter and meeting, while صَدَفَة denotes a marine animal’s hard shell. Some semantic relationships can be explained, but a single definite historical origin cannot be assumed for every branch.

The Qur’an uses this root in only two domains: turning away from the divine signs in Al-Anʿām, and the two facing rock sides in Dhū Al-Qarnayn’s account.

Short definition: In its verbal branch, Ṣ D F expresses inclining or turning away from something; in its spatial branch, a side or flank, especially two facing mountain sides; in other branches, encounter and meeting or a marine shell. Brief root meaning: Inclining and turning away; sides, especially facing mountain sides; also encounter and seashell.

In the Qur’anic uses, يَصْدِفُونَ should be translated “they turn away,” and الصَّدَفَيْنِ “the two mountain sides.”

Sharing a root does not necessarily make every derivative mean the same thing. Each word’s precise meaning comes from its form, sentence construction, and context.
