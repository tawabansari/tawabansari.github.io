The Arabic root S Q F concerns the upper covering of a building or sheltered space. Saqf names the part above that covers the space beneath. Saqafa al-bayta means “made a roof for the house,” while musaqqaf means “roofed.”

Saqīfah also denotes a canopy or covered space beneath which a person can stand. Forms relating to height and curvature are attested within the same lexical family.

The Quran uses saqf for both buildings and the sky. In Al-Naḥl 16:26, a building's roof collapses; Al-Zukhruf 43:33 describes silver roofs for houses; Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 calls the sky saqfan maḥfūẓan; and Al-Ṭūr 52:5 mentions al-saqf al-marfūʿ.

These occurrences share a feature: a roof is above and relates to the space beneath. Its material, geometry, means of support, and protective function are not identical in every use.

The study must therefore distinguish a house's roof, the word's application to the sky, and qualities such as being preserved or raised. None should become an invariant meaning of the root without evidence.

1. The root in early Arabic

Saqf: a house's upper covering

Classical Arabic defines al-saqf as the upper covering of a house, opposite its floor or lower space.

In saqf al-bayt, the roof is part of a building and is situated above its interior.

This definition has two clear features: being above the space below and covering that space.

A roof can rest on walls, columns, or other supports. It may be made of wood, stone, branches, earth, or other materials.

Material is not an invariant part of the word's meaning. Wooden and stone roofs remain roofs despite their different materials.

Nor must a roof be a perfectly flat plane. It can be level, sloping, domed, or another shape.

Saqf therefore cannot be restricted to “a completely flat surface without curvature.”

Conversely, not everything overhead is necessarily a roof. A bird passing above someone is not called a roof merely because it is higher.

The distinguishing feature is a covering over a space.

Lexical definition, height, and curvature

One lexical account connects this family with irtifāʿ, iṭlāl, and inḥināʾ: height, overlooking from above, and curvature.

A house's roof illustrates its elevated position and relation to the space beneath.

This explanation clarifies the connection with height.

Curvature, however, is not a necessary condition in every use of saqf.

Arabic allows a level or extended covering to be called a roof. Curvature in some branches does not prove that every roof must be curved or domed.

Saqifa al-rajulu is also attested for a tall, bent person, and asqaf can describe such a person.

This concerns posture and differs from a building's covering.

A semantic relationship between height and curvature in the two settings is conceivable, but it does not establish a single historical origin for every form.

For Quranic usage, the direct meaning is clearer and more relevant: the upper covering of a building or space.

A poetic witness attributed to Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī reads:

وَإِنِّي وَإِنْ طَالَ الثَّوَاءُ لَمَيِّتٌ
وَيَضْطَمُّنِي مَاوِيَّ بَيْتٌ مُسَقَّفُ

wa-innī wa-in ṭāla al-thawāʾu la-mayyitun / wa-yaḍṭammunī māwiyya baytun musaqqafu

“Though my stay be long, I shall surely die; and, Māwiyyah, a roofed house will enclose me.”

Musaqqaf describes the house in this verse.

The poet speaks of his death, picturing the body's final resting place as a roofed house.

The roof is part of what encloses and covers a person.

The witness thus illustrates early use of musaqqaf for a place with an upper covering.

The verse is transmitted with variations in the verb preceding baytun musaqqaf. The reading used here is wa-yaḍṭammunī, “will enclose me”; variations do not change the meaning of musaqqaf.

Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī is a recognized pre-Islamic poet whose death is reported before the prophetic mission. Attribution and the precise date of recording are nevertheless distinct matters. This provides evidence of a usage attributed to a pre-Quranic poet, not the date of the word's first appearance.

A roof as an image of lost support in life

A lament for her husband Kulayb attributed to Jalīlah bint Murrah reads:

يَا قَتِيلًا قَوَّضَ الدَّهْرُ بِهِ
سَقْفَ بَيْتَيَّ جَمِيعًا مِنْ عَلِ

yā qatīlan qawwaḍa al-dahru bihi / saqfa baytayya jamīʿan min ʿali

“O slain one, through whom time brought down the roof of both my homes from above.”

The poet is not reporting the actual collapse of two buildings.

Her husband's death is pictured as the destruction of her married home and her family home: in the transmitted account, her brother is her husband's killer. Both homes represent support in her life.

This illustrates how a roof could become a literary image of support, security, and stability beyond its architectural sense.

The figurative meaning emerges from the roof's role and the lament's context; saqf does not directly mean husband, protector, or security in every use.

The poem is attributed to Jalīlah bint Murrah in the early literary tradition, with minor textual variants. Its story concerns the pre-Islamic period, but the date when the present wording became fixed is not independently established.

Together, these witnesses show a familiar upper-covering sense and its use in images of death, loss, and the collapse of life's support.

Saqīfah: a canopy or covered space

Saqīfah denotes a canopy, portico, or covered area with a covering above it.

Unlike a complete house, it may be open on one or more sides, principally providing covering and shade beneath.

Lexical usage also applies the word to wooden or stone coverings.

Saqāʾif is its plural and can denote coverings, boards, and components associated with roofed structures.

This shows that saqf is not restricted to an enclosed, four-walled building.

What matters is a covering over the space beneath.

Saqāʾif is also attested for ship planks and certain skeletal parts. Comparing extended or covering components to a roof can illuminate the semantic relationship.

None of these subsidiary uses permits translating Quranic saqf as any arbitrary board, bone, or covering.

2. Quranic forms and derivatives

S Q F occurs four times in four Quranic verses.

All occurrences are nominal forms of saqf; no independent verb from the root occurs in the Quran.

Al-saqfu — Al-Naḥl 16:26: definite singular noun in the nominative; the roof.

Saqfan — Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32: indefinite singular noun in the accusative; a roof.

Suqufan — Al-Zukhruf 43:33: accusative plural; roofs.

Wa-al-saqfi — Al-Ṭūr 52:5: definite singular noun in the genitive, following the oath particle wāw; by the roof.

In the reading of Ḥafṣ, Al-Zukhruf 43:33 has plural suqufan. Other recognized readings have singular saqfan, referring generically or distributively to the houses' roofs.

The difference does not change the basic meaning: both readings concern roofs of houses.

Other forms are also known in Arabic.

Saqafa al-bayta: made a roof for the house.

Saqqafa: roofed or built a roof.

Tasqīf: roofing.

Musaqqaf: roofed or provided with a roof.

Masqūf: something for which a roof has been made.

Suqūf, asquf, and suquf: attested plurals of saqf.

Saqīfah: a canopy or covered space.

Saqīf: a form mentioned in some lexical accounts of plural suquf, with a sense close to a roof or overhead covering; a broad roofing board is also called saqīfah.

Saqifa and asqaf: in the branch concerning posture, being tall and bent.

Usquf also occurs as a title for a Christian religious leader. It is described as a loanword, with a Syriac connection; spelling alone does not establish derivation from the Arabic verb for roofing.

The listed verbs and subsidiary forms do not occur in these exact formations in the Quran. Suquf, already identified as a Quranic plural, is an exception within the list of plurals.

The Quran thus uses a noun in two principal settings: buildings' coverings and descriptions of the sky.

3. A building's roof: covering, support, and collapse

The roof collapsing upon the building's occupants

Al-Naḥl 16:26 states:

قَدْ مَكَرَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ فَأَتَى اللَّهُ بُنْيَانَهُمْ مِنَ الْقَوَاعِدِ فَخَرَّ عَلَيْهِمُ السَّقْفُ مِنْ فَوْقِهِمْ وَأَتَاهُمُ الْعَذَابُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

qad makara alladhīna min qablihim fa-atā Allāhu bunyānahum mina al-qawāʿidi fa-kharra ʿalayhimu al-saqfu min fawqihim wa-atāhumu al-ʿadhābu min ḥaythu lā yashʿurūn

Al-Naḥl 16:26

“Those before them also schemed. Then The God struck their building at its foundations, and the roof fell upon them from above; punishment came to them from where they did not perceive.”

Al-saqfu appears alongside bunyān and al-qawāʿid.

Bunyān concerns a building or erected structure.

Qawāʿid are foundations, the parts upon which a building rests.

Saqf is that building's upper covering.

The sentence therefore presents three elements: the building, its foundations, and the roof above it.

It describes damage reaching the foundations, followed by the roof's collapse.

The relationship between roof and foundations

In ordinary construction, a roof needs support to remain in place.

If the foundations or supporting components are destroyed, the roof may collapse.

This relationship is prominent in fa-kharra ʿalayhimu al-saqfu min fawqihim.

Kharra belongs to KH R R and concerns falling down.

Collapse is therefore the verb's meaning, not that of saqf itself.

A roof may remain stable for a long time and still be a roof. In this verse it is accompanied by a verb of falling.

Min fawqihim also specifies the direction of the fall.

What was above falls upon those beneath it.

Does the verse describe a literal building collapse?

The verse begins with people who schemed before:

«قَدْ مَكَرَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ».

qad makara alladhīna min qablihim

Al-Naḥl 16:26

It then describes the destruction of their structure and the roof collapsing upon them.

This sequence can depict scheming's outcome and the collapse of the structure upon which the schemers relied.

On this reading, foundations and roof illustrate the connection between an undertaking's basis and its outcome: when the basis collapses, what rests upon it falls as well.

The verse alone does not establish that a particular building fell upon all the schemers in one identifiable historical incident.

Nor should the architectural senses of bunyān, qawāʿid, and saqf be denied and reduced to purely abstract terms.

The image's force comes from the familiar, concrete sense of a building.

The lexical sense is clear; a historical or figurative reading requires separate contextual analysis.

In either reading, the roof is above and rests upon supports beneath it.

4. Silver roofs: buildings and the value of worldly possessions

Al-Zukhruf 43:33 states:

وَلَوْلَا أَنْ يَكُونَ النَّاسُ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً لَجَعَلْنَا لِمَنْ يَكْفُرُ بِالرَّحْمَٰنِ لِبُيُوتِهِمْ سُقُفًا مِنْ فِضَّةٍ وَمَعَارِجَ عَلَيْهَا يَظْهَرُونَ

wa-lawlā an yakūna al-nāsu ummatan wāḥidatan la-jaʿalnā li-man yakfuru bi-al-raḥmāni li-buyūtihim suqufan min fiḍḍatin wa-maʿārija ʿalayhā yaẓharūn

Al-Zukhruf 43:33

“Were it not that people would become one community, We would have made, for those who disbelieve in the Entirely Merciful, silver roofs for their houses and stairways upon which they ascend.”

Suqufan is the plural of saqf here.

Min fiḍḍatin specifies the material: silver.

Unlike the collapsing roof in Al-Naḥl 16:26, these roofs are described through their precious material.

Saqf itself names a building's upper covering; its being silver comes from min fiḍḍatin.

Silver, expense, and luxury should therefore not be built into the word's lexical meaning.

Earthen and silver roofs are both roofs; their material and economic value differ.

Roofs, stairways, and other parts of a house

The verse continues with maʿārij, stairways or means of ascent.

ʿAlayhā yaẓharūn describes ascending upon them.

A roof and a stairway are distinct building components.

The roof covers the space beneath, while stairs allow movement to a higher level.

Al-Zukhruf 43:34 also mentions doors and couches.

Together these form an image of houses with varied components and abundant luxury.

Saqf here should not be translated as the entire building or all its amenities: it identifies one particular component.

Does a silver roof indicate superiority before The God?

The verse envisages silver roofs for those who disbelieve in the Entirely Merciful.

Its construction is conditional; it does not say such roofs were actually built for all of them.

Al-Zukhruf 43:35 calls these amenities enjoyment of worldly life, while the Hereafter with the Lord is for the God-conscious.

The image thus shows that a building's monetary value and luxury do not alone establish its owner's truthfulness, faith, or divine approval.

Someone may possess considerable wealth and an extravagant home without that determining their moral standing.

Conversely, inexpensive building materials do not prove an owner's inferiority or lack of human dignity.

This conclusion comes from the passage, not the lexical meaning of saqf.

For the root study, the verse's point is narrower: a roof covers a house from above and may be made from a valuable material such as silver.

5. The sky as a roof: preservation and elevation

Wa-jaʿalnā al-samāʾa saqfan maḥfūẓan

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 states:

وَجَعَلْنَا السَّمَاءَ سَقْفًا مَحْفُوظًا وَهُمْ عَنْ آيَاتِهَا مُعْرِضُونَ

wa-jaʿalnā al-samāʾa saqfan maḥfūẓan wa-hum ʿan āyātihā muʿriḍūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32

“We made the sky a preserved roof, yet they turn away from its signs.”

Al-samāʾ is the subject matter, while saqfan expresses the description or role assigned to it.

The sky is compared to, or designated as, a roof: something above the earth and human life.

This relates to the familiar sense of a house's roof.

Just as a roof is above and covers the space beneath, the sky is above the earth in human experience and description.

This linguistic relationship should be distinguished from a complete physical account of the sky's shape.

Saqf alone does not prove that the sky is a flat plane, a solid shell, or a dome made of a particular material.

Nor should it be restricted to one specific layer of the earth's atmosphere.

Samāʾ has a broader Quranic range and must be understood contextually in each verse.

Here saqf highlights the sky's relation to what is beneath it; the word alone does not supply the details of cosmic structure.

Maḥfūẓ: preserved or protecting?

Maḥfūẓan is an important element of this verse.

It belongs to Ḥ F Ẓ and is a passive participle.

Maḥfūẓ means preserved or protected; ḥāfiẓ means a keeper or protector.

This grammatical distinction should remain visible.

Saqfan maḥfūẓan describes the sky as a roof that is preserved or maintained.

The expression does not directly state that the roof itself protects everything beneath it, although such a role could be investigated through other evidence.

Al-Ḥijr 15:17 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:7 discuss guarding the sky in particular contexts. They matter for related uses of preservation, but should not automatically restrict maḥfūẓ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 to that single instance.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 does not fully specify the protection: it does not state precisely what the sky is preserved from or by what mechanism.

“A preserved roof” therefore follows the word formation more closely than “a protecting roof.”

Possible protective roles should be explained separately from the basic translation, with appropriate evidence.

The roof and the sky's signs

The verse ends:

«وَهُمْ عَنْ آيَاتِهَا مُعْرِضُونَ».

wa-hum ʿan āyātihā muʿriḍūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32

“Yet they turn away from its signs.”

This places the sky within an invitation to observe and attend to signs in creation.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:31 mentions mountains and earthly paths; Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:33 presents night, day, the sun, the moon, and their movement in a falak.

The sky is therefore not presented separately from nature and observable phenomena in this passage.

But saqf should not be expanded to mean the movements of the sun and moon, celestial orbits, or every biological function of the atmosphere. Other verses express these matters through distinct words and constructions.

Saqf retains the image of an upper covering; maḥfūẓ adds a further quality.

Wa-al-saqfi al-marfūʿ

Al-Ṭūr 52:5 states:

وَالسَّقْفِ الْمَرْفُوعِ

wa-al-saqfi al-marfūʿ

Al-Ṭūr 52:5

“And by the raised roof.”

Al-saqf is definite and accompanied by al-marfūʿ.

Marfūʿ belongs to R F ʿ and denotes something raised or elevated.

Saqf names what is overhead; marfūʿ emphasizes its elevation.

The surrounding passage mentions the Mount, a written book, and the frequented house before this phrase, followed by the sea described as masjūr.

Al-Ṭūr 52:9 also describes the sky's powerful movement on a coming day.

The clear identification of the sky as a roof in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 gives strong Quranic support to reading al-saqf al-marfūʿ as the raised sky.

Al-Ṭūr 52:5 does not itself repeat samāʾ explicitly. Identifying the referent therefore involves context and related verses; saqf does not independently mean sky in every lexical use.

Do a preserved roof and a raised roof express the same quality?

Maḥfūẓ and marfūʿ are distinct adjectives.

Maḥfūẓ concerns being kept or protected.

Marfūʿ concerns being raised or elevated.

Something can be raised and also preserved, but these qualities are not identical.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 emphasizes preservation; Al-Ṭūr 52:5 emphasizes elevation.

They should not both be reduced to “the high sky” or “the protecting sky.”

Neither adjective independently specifies the sky's material, full shape, or means of support.

These questions require study through the network of verses and independent evidence.

6. Comparison with related roots

S Q F and B N Y: roof and building

Bināʾ concerns building and an erected structure.

Al-Naḥl 16:26 contains both bunyānahum and al-saqfu.

Bunyān is the building or connected structure as a whole; the roof is one part of it.

Al-Baqarah 2:22 also describes the sky as bināʾan.

This relates to Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32: both describe the sky using architectural vocabulary.

Bināʾ and saqf, however, are not exact synonyms.

A building may have walls, foundations, a roof, and other parts, while saqf concerns its upper covering.

Describing the sky as a building and roof does not establish that every component of human architecture exists there in the same shape and material.

S Q F and S M W: an upper covering and the sky

Samāʾ concerns height and what is above in Arabic.

Saqf primarily denotes an upper covering over a space.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 connects the concepts by designating the sky as a roof.

This does not make every Quranic occurrence of samāʾ a reference to a building's roof.

Nor is every house roof ordinarily called the sky, although some Arabic usage can describe the upper part of a space as its samāʾ.

Saqf highlights covering and an overhead position; samāʾ has a broader semantic range.

S Q F and Q ʿ D: roof and foundation

In construction, qāʿidah denotes a foundation or base.

Al-Naḥl 16:26 places foundations and roof in a structural relationship.

Foundations support the building; the roof occupies its upper part.

If the foundations disappear, the roof may lose its stability.

This relationship does not make saqf itself mean “dependent structure” or “the result of scheming.”

Roof and foundation are distinct but connected components.

S Q F and Ẓ L L: covering and shade

A roof can create shade, but saqf and ẓill differ in meaning.

Ẓill concerns shade, where direct light is absent or an obstacle intervenes between the source of light and a place.

A roof, by contrast, is a covering or building component.

A roof may cast shade, but someone can also shelter in the shade of a tree or mountain without a structural roof.

A saqīfah can likewise be a canopy built to provide shade.

Creating shade is a possible function, not the full definition of a roof.

S Q F and R F ʿ: an upper covering and raising

Rafʿ concerns lifting and raising.

Al-Ṭūr 52:5 combines al-saqf with al-marfūʿ.

The construction distinguishes the roof itself from its being raised.

Someone can raise an object that is not a roof.

Likewise, a roof's name alone does not tell us how or when it was raised into position.

Saqf names the upper covering; rafʿ expresses raising or elevation.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For Quranic S Q F, the basic Persian equivalent is saqf, and the English equivalent is “roof.”

The familiar Persian word preserves the principal sense of its Arabic counterpart.

Al-saqfu — Al-Naḥl 16:26

“The roof.”

In the full phrase:

“Then the roof fell upon them from above.”

Saqfan maḥfūẓan — Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32

“A preserved roof” or “a roof kept safe.”

“A preserved roof” is suitable for the basic translation.

“A protecting roof” requires separate explanation, since maḥfūẓ is a passive participle, not an active one.

Suqufan min fiḍḍatin — Al-Zukhruf 43:33

“Roofs of silver.”

This preserves the plural and the specified material.

For readings with singular saqfan, “a roof of silver” follows that reading.

Al-saqfi al-marfūʿ — Al-Ṭūr 52:5

“The raised roof.”

This preserves both the noun and its adjective separately.

An explanation can identify the referent as the elevated sky, but the direct translation need not remove “roof” and replace it with “sky.”

Other lexical equivalents follow; the Quranic noun saqf is included for comparison:

  • Saqafa al-bayta: made a roof for the house.
  • Saqqafa al-bayta: roofed the house.
  • Saqf: the upper covering of a building or space.
  • Suqūf: roofs.
  • Saqīfah: canopy, portico, or covered space.
  • Musaqqaf: roofed.
  • Masqūf: provided with a roof.
  • Tasqīf: roofing.
  • Asqaf, as an Arabic adjective: tall and bent.

Modern Persian also uses saqf in expressions for spending, income, or demands, where it denotes an upper limit or maximum allowed amount, much like English “ceiling.”

The image of an upper limit makes this understandable, but modern terminology should not be projected into Quranic usage without independent evidence.

The Quran uses saqf for the upper covering of buildings and for the sky, not to set a numerical cap.

Conclusion

S Q F concerns the upper covering of a building or space.

A roof is above and covers what is beneath. Its material, size, and geometry can differ. Flatness, curvature, wood, stone, and construction technique are not invariant parts of the word's meaning.

Early poetic witnesses show both the architectural sense and literary images of covering, stability, and the collapse of life's support.

The root occurs four times in the Quran.

In Al-Naḥl 16:26, the roof collapses after the foundations are struck. Within the schemers' fate, the image highlights the relationship between foundation and upper structure.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:33, silver roofs form part of luxurious houses. Context shows that luxury and wealth alone do not measure human worth or truthfulness.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 calls the sky saqfan maḥfūẓan. Saqf expresses the overhead covering; maḥfūẓ expresses its preservation.

Al-Ṭūr 52:5 presents al-saqf al-marfūʿ, the raised roof. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 and the surrounding passage strongly support the sky as its referent.

Yet the roof image does not independently specify material, full geometry, or a particular physical structure. Maḥfūẓ should also not automatically become “protecting.”

The central meaning can therefore be stated as follows:

S Q F primarily denotes the upper covering of a building or space, situated above what lies beneath. The Quran uses it both for buildings' roofs and to describe the sky. Preservation, elevation, material, and collapse are expressed by accompanying words and verbs.

For Forqan, “roof” is the basic, precise equivalent.

The word's Quranic significance concerns structure and position as well as covering. A roof may rest on foundations, consist of valuable material, be preserved, or occupy an elevated position.

None of these differing features should replace the word's own meaning. Study begins with the upper covering, then turns to each verse's context to identify the feature and message emphasized.
