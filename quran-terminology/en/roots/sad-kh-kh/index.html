Pounding sound, forceful impact, and its effect on hearing: from clashing hard objects to a deafening blast

The Arabic root Ṣ KH KH concerns the sound of forceful impact, striking hard objects, and sound’s powerful effect on the ear. Ṣakhkh and ṣakhīkh describe clashing sounds; ṣākhkhah describes an intense blast that pounds or deafens the ears.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as al-ṣākhkhah in ʿAbasa 80:33. Within the context of resurrection, it introduces an event characterized by an overwhelming, pounding blast.

Ṣākhkhah should not simply be equated with “a loud sound.” Lexical witnesses emphasize forceful impact, a pounding quality, and the effect on hearing, alongside loudness.

Al-ṣākhkhah is the family’s only Qur’anic form. Lexicons record ṣakhkha, ṣakhkh, ṣakhkhah, ṣakhīkh, and aṣakhkha, but these do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic core

The sound of forceful impact

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris writes:

الصَّادُ وَالْخَاءُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى صَوْتٍ مِنَ الْأَصْوَاتِ

al-ṣādu wa-al-khāʾu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā ṣawtin mina al-aṣwāti

That is, this family denotes a kind of sound.

He gives the examples of stone striking stone and a blast that deafens the ears.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: clashing sound and ṣākhkhah

Lisān al-ʿArab also records ṣakhkh for striking iron against iron or a hard stick against a solid object.

Here sound arises from hard objects colliding. Such an impact can produce a sharp, pounding, sudden sound.

Arabic records the expression:

ضَرَبْتُ الصَّخْرَةَ بِحَجَرٍ فَسَمِعْتُ لَهَا صَخَّةً

ḍarabtu al-ṣakhrata bi-ḥajarin fa-samiʿtu lahā ṣakhkhatan

That is, “I struck the rock with a stone and heard its clash.”

Ṣakhkhah names the sound produced by the blow, rather than the stone or the striking tool.

The example connects this family with a particular sound quality arising from collision or striking.

It does not establish that every loud sound or every shout was necessarily called ṣakhkh.

Striking and pounding

In some lexical witnesses, ṣakhkha describes the act of striking as well as the sound of impact.

For example, ṣakhkha al-ghurābu bi-minqārihi is recorded for a raven pecking or thrusting its beak into an animal’s body.

The blow or thrust is prominent here; a loud sound is not necessarily the subject.

Ṣakhkhahu is also recorded for a blow that can injure the ear or destroy hearing.

The family therefore has two closely related aspects: forceful impact, and the pounding sound arising from it.

Ordinary collisions between hard objects help explain the relationship. They do not establish whether the striking meaning historically preceded the sound meaning or vice versa.

The linguistic evidence establishes that both aspects occur in the family.

Lisān al-ʿArab: striking hard objects and a raven’s peck

2. The effect on the ears and hearing

An important use describes sound intense enough to affect the ears.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn explains al-ṣākhkhah as:

صَيْحَةٌ تَصُخُّ الْآذَانَ فَتُصِمُّهَا

ṣayḥatun taṣukhkhu al-ādhāna fa-tuṣimmuhā

That is, a blast that strikes the ears so forcefully that it deafens them.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah transmits the same explanation.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: a deafening blast and a great calamity

Ṣakhkha al-ṣawtu al-udhuna describes sound striking the ear forcefully and disrupting hearing.

Hearing a loud sound and experiencing ṣakhkh are therefore distinct descriptions.

A person may hear a loud sound without hearing damage or disruption. In ṣakhkha al-udhuna, the sound’s forceful effect on the ear is prominent.

Literary language can also call a sound “deafening” to describe its overwhelming intensity without necessarily asserting permanent, measurable deafness.

Ṣākhkhah is lexically connected with deafening intensity. The word alone does not establish that everyone hearing a sound loses their hearing forever.

A blow pounding the ear

Ibn Sīdah compares the ṣākhkhah sound to a thrust or blow delivered to the ear.

The expression ka-anna fī udhunihi ṣākhkhatan is also recorded: as though a thrust or blow had entered his ear.

Intense sound can thus be described as striking the ear, rather than merely being heard.

This matters for the Qur’anic word: al-ṣākhkhah conveys the sound’s powerful, pounding effect as well as its loudness.

3. Uses in early Arabic

Ṣakhkh and ṣakhīkh

Arabic lexicons record ṣakhkh and ṣakhīkh for stone striking stone, or another hard-object collision.

These uses concern sounds arising from collision and pounding.

Ṣakhkhah can describe an individual clash or particular sound event.

The subject here is sound, while the act of striking also belongs to the lexical family.

Early lexicons preserve these expressions, but an independently dated pre-Qur’anic poetic witness is not available for each one. They cannot all be attributed to pre-Islamic poets as documented usages without further historical evidence.

Ṣākhkhah as a great calamity

Alongside a deafening blast, Kitāb al-ʿAyn records al-ṣākhkhah for an immense, dreadful event.

One recorded expression is:

رَمَاهُ اللَّهُ بِصَاخَّةٍ

ramāhu allāhu bi-ṣākhkhatin

That is, “God struck him with a tremendous calamity.” The sentence also explicitly attributes the event to God.

Ṣākhkhah need not name an actual sound here; it can refer to a great, shocking calamity.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains al-ṣākhkhah through al-dāhiyah, a severe calamity.

This semantic extension is understandable. Just as a pounding sound can affect someone suddenly, a major event can transform their life.

That explains a relationship between meanings; it does not prove that the calamity meaning arose from the deafening-sound meaning at a particular historical moment.

Nor should ṣākhkhah always be restricted to calamity: its auditory meaning is explicit in the lexical sources.

Arabic lexicons: ṣākhkhah as calamity

4. The Qur’anic use: ʿAbasa 80:33

فَإِذَا جَاءَتِ الصَّاخَّةُ

fa-idhā jāʾati al-ṣākhkhatu

Translation: Then, when the deafening blast comes.

The verse follows reminders of human creation, the provision of food, and life’s blessings.

In ʿAbasa 80:24, people are called to consider their food. The following verses describe water pouring down, the earth splitting, and plants and fruits growing.

In ʿAbasa 80:32, these provisions are described as enjoyment for people and their livestock.

Verse 33 then begins with fa-idhā, shifting attention from present life to a great future event:

فَإِذَا جَاءَتِ الصَّاخَّةُ

fa-idhā jāʾati al-ṣākhkhatu

The following verses describe a day when someone flees from their brother, mother, father, spouse, and children.

ʿAbasa 80:37 explains the situation: each person is so occupied with their own concern and fate that they cannot attend to others.

ʿAbasa 80:38, ʿAbasa 80:39, ʿAbasa 80:40, ʿAbasa 80:41, and ʿAbasa 80:42 then describe people’s contrasting faces and conditions that day.

In this context, al-ṣākhkhah concerns an event introducing or accompanying resurrection and humanity’s encounter with the outcome of its deeds.

Why is this event called al-ṣākhkhah?

Directly, ṣākhkhah denotes an intense blast that pounds the ears.

Arabic also uses the word for an immense calamity.

The meanings are compatible here: an event introduced through a pounding blast, so great that it transforms ordinary human circumstances.

Two levels nevertheless require distinction.

First, the lexical meaning: a pounding, deafening blast.

Second, the Qur’anic referent: an event associated with resurrection, whose effects and human circumstances the following verses describe.

Resurrection is the referent established by context; the root Ṣ KH KH does not simply mean “resurrection.”

Merging these levels loses the word’s distinctive pounding auditory effect in translation.

Is al-ṣākhkhah a proper name for resurrection?

Early commentaries describe al-ṣākhkhah as one of the names of resurrection.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits this use and reports it as a recognized designation for resurrection.

Al-Ṭabarī: resurrection designation and tentative listening connection

The designation fits the context, which concerns the resurrection scene.

Lexically, however, ṣākhkhah is descriptive, conveying the intensity of a sound’s effect.

It can therefore be called a Qur’anic designation for the resurrection event. Its descriptive meaning should still be retained instead of translating it solely as “the Day of Resurrection.”

That rendering identifies the referent but loses the particular linguistic quality selected in the verse.

Is al-ṣākhkhah the second trumpet blast?

Some commentaries connect al-ṣākhkhah with the blast after which resurrection occurs.

Al-Zumar 39:68 describes the trumpet being blown, then blown again, and people rising.

Qāf 50:42 likewise describes a cry that people hear, on the day identified as the day of emergence.

These verses matter for understanding the connected events of resurrection.

ʿAbasa 80:33 itself, however, does not name the trumpet, a blowing, or the number of blasts.

Connecting al-ṣākhkhah with the resurrection blast is a contextual reading to examine. The word itself does not identify it definitively as the first or second blast.

Nor does it specify the sound’s physical properties, producing instrument, measurable intensity, or propagation.

Al-ṣākhkhah emphasizes the event’s forceful, pounding effect without describing its scientific or physical details.

5. Morphology and syntax of al-ṣākhkhah

In the usual morphological analysis, ṣākhkhah is the feminine active participle of the doubled verb ṣakhkha. Some early sources also propose a verbal-noun use.

The masculine form is ṣākhkh and the feminine ṣākhkhah.

The repeated khāʾ radical appears as consonant doubling, marked by shaddah.

The Qur’anic word has the definite article al-:

الصَّاخَّةُ

al-ṣākhkhatu

This definite form is the subject of jāʾat in fa-idhā jāʾati al-ṣākhkhatu.

Jāʾat is also feminine, agreeing with the feminine noun al-ṣākhkhah.

Does the word itself mean shouting?

Morphologically, ṣākhkhah is a feminine active participle, rather than an imperative or past-tense verb.

In use, however, it can function as the name of an event or tremendous blast.

Simply translating it as “one who shouts” is unsuitable, since that may suggest a person or creature doing the shouting.

“Deafening blast” or “pounding sound” more naturally conveys its actual use.

Some early lexical analyses discuss whether this form is an active participle or has a verbal-noun use. The difference primarily concerns the construction and naming of the event, without removing the core sense of intense sound.

Lisān al-ʿArab: active-participle and verbal-noun analyses

The role of fa-idhā jāʾat

The verse begins with fa-idhā.

Idhā introduces a future event, whose scene and consequences the following sentences describe.

Some grammarians consider its consequent clause omitted and understood from the following descriptions; others discuss its connection to the subsequent clauses.

In either analysis, the clear sense is that the event’s arrival places people in extraordinary, changed circumstances.

Jāʾat expresses the event’s arrival. Al-ṣākhkhah itself does not mean coming or arriving.

“Comes” therefore renders jāʾat, and “the deafening blast” renders al-ṣākhkhah.

6. Distinction from aṣākha, to listen attentively

Some commentaries propose another possibility for understanding al-ṣākhkhah.

Using the tentative aḥsabuhā, Al-Ṭabarī suggests a connection with ṣākha fulānun li-ṣawti fulānin: someone listened to another’s voice. He then observes that the listening person is described as muṣīkh, and suggests that the sound itself may be called ṣākhkh.

On this account, the resurrection blast could be understood as drawing everyone’s attention.

This derivational analysis nevertheless requires caution.

Lexicons record aṣākha yuṣīkhu in a separate family, usually under Ṣ W KH or Ṣ Y KH.

Ibn Fāris likewise records aṣākha yuṣīkhu under Ṣ Y KH, explaining it as listening attentively.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: aṣākha under Ṣ Y KH

Similarity between ṣakhkha and aṣākha cannot by itself establish that they historically belong to one root.

Al-Ṭabarī himself advances the possibility cautiously rather than presenting it as certain.

“Deafening blast” has direct lexical support for al-ṣākhkhah and need not be derived from listening attentively.

“A sound to which everyone listens” can therefore be a possible explanation of the event’s effect. The pounding, deafening-blast meaning remains better grounded as a basic definition.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the separate listening family

7. Comparison with related roots and words

Ṣ KH KH (ṣakhkh) and Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣayḥah)

Ṣ Y Ḥ concerns calling, shouting, and cries.

Ṣayḥah occurs repeatedly in the Qur’an for a sound or powerful cry.

In Qāf 50:42, al-ṣayḥah appears in the resurrection scene; the day it is heard is identified as the day of emergence.

Al-ṣākhkhah also concerns an intense blast, with its pounding effect on the ear particularly prominent in lexical witnesses.

Ṣayḥah and ṣākhkhah can overlap in describing a great sound event, but their semantic ranges are not identical.

“Cry” or “shout” can suit ṣayḥah. For al-ṣākhkhah, “deafening” better conveys the word’s distinctive quality.

Ṣ KH KH (ṣakhkh) and Ṣ R KH (ṣurākh)

Ṣ R KH concerns shouting, raising the voice, and, in some uses, calling for help.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, muṣrikh occurs in a statement about the ability or inability to provide help.

In its auditory branch, ṣakhkh concerns clashing sound and its powerful effect on the ear.

Shouting and pounding sound can be close in some circumstances, but every shout is not necessarily a ṣākhkhah.

A request for help or a human voice is not an essential component of ṣākhkhah’s meaning.

Ṣ KH KH (ṣakhkh) and Ṣ KH B (ṣakhab)

Ṣakhab denotes loud sound, uproar, or clamor.

Ibn Fāris explains its root through ṣawtin ʿālin: a loud sound.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: ṣakhab and loud sound

Ṣakhkh also concerns sound, with forceful impact and a pounding effect prominent.

Ṣakhab can describe a crowd’s noise or a clamorous environment; familiar ṣakhkh witnesses concern hard-object collisions and sound pounding the ears.

This distinction should not be turned into an absolute boundary between every use of the roots.

Ṣ KH KH (ṣakhkh) and Ṣ M M (ṣamam)

In its hearing-related use, Ṣ M M concerns deafness or an inability to hear.

In ṣakhkha al-ṣawtu al-udhuna, intense sound affects the ear and can cause deafness.

The relationship is therefore one of an action and a possible consequence.

Ṣakhkh does not name deafness itself. A sound can be called deafening without every use asserting permanent deafness as its result.

Deafness can also have causes and circumstances unrelated to intense sound.

The roots are therefore not synonymous, despite their relationship in some expressions.

Study of Ṣ M M

Ṣ KH KH (ṣakhkh) and Q R ʿ (qarʿ)

Qarʿ concerns striking or knocking against something.

Al-Qāriʿah occurs in Al-Qāriʿah 101:1.

It also appears in a resurrection context and carries an image of striking impact.

Ṣākhkhah and qāriʿah are close insofar as both can describe a forceful, shattering event.

Lexical ṣākhkhah witnesses particularly concern the auditory effect on the ear. Qāriʿah belongs to the striking family and is not necessarily restricted to sound.

They are therefore not interchangeable names without a distinction: each foregrounds a particular linguistic quality.

Ṣ KH KH (ṣakhkh) and Ṭ M M (ṭāmmah)

Al-ṭāmmatu al-kubrā appears in the resurrection scene of Al-Nāziʿāt 79:34.

It belongs to Ṭ M M, concerning covering or overwhelming. Some uses describe an event prevailing over everything else.

Al-ṣākhkhah likewise occurs in a resurrection context, with a pounding, deafening sound as its principal quality.

The expressions can refer to a great event or related resurrection scenes without having an identical lexical meaning.

Different designations emphasize different aspects of the event’s magnitude and effect.

8. Challenges in understanding and translation

Translating al-ṣākhkhah involves choosing among “blast,” “shout,” “pounding sound,” “deafening,” and “dreadful calamity.”

Each conveys part of the word’s semantic range.

A bare word for a cry or blast conveys audible sound without necessarily expressing its exceptional intensity.

“Shout” can convey intensity but often suggests a human or living creature’s voice. Al-ṣākhkhah is not necessarily a human voice.

“Pounding sound” emphasizes forceful impact and effect.

“Deafening blast” conveys both an auditory event and its intense effect on hearing.

“Dreadful calamity” reflects another recorded use of ṣākhkhah, but may weaken the verse’s auditory image.

For ʿAbasa 80:33, “the deafening blast” is therefore a more precise, natural equivalent.

Does deafening mean literal, universal deafness?

Lexicons describe ṣākhkhah as a blast that deafens the ears.

Such a description does not necessarily provide biological information about everyone’s hearing at resurrection.

Arabic and Persian can call an extremely intense sound deafening to express its overwhelming force.

The word therefore does not establish that everyone becomes permanently deaf in that event.

Conversely, its deafening quality should not be removed entirely by reducing it to fear or terror.

The root’s auditory meaning has clear support and should be retained in translation.

Does al-ṣākhkhah mean only the Day of Resurrection?

In ʿAbasa 80:33, it concerns the resurrection event.

Its lexical meaning is not simply the name of a date in the afterlife.

It attributes a particular quality to that event, which should remain recognizable in translation.

“When resurrection comes” conveys the verse’s general message but loses the distinction between al-ṣākhkhah and other resurrection designations.

For Forqan’s study text, the word’s meaning should be retained, with resurrection identified through contextual explanation.

9. Translation proposal for Forqan

For the expression:

فَإِذَا جَاءَتِ الصَّاخَّةُ

fa-idhā jāʾati al-ṣākhkhatu

The preferred rendering is:

“Then, when the deafening blast comes.”

“Then, when the pounding sound comes” is also defensible. “Deafening” more explicitly conveys the important quality found in early explanations of al-ṣākhkhah.

“Then, when the dreadful shout comes” also approaches the context, but “shout” may be understood as a human voice, while dreadfulness comes more from the resurrection setting than directly from the root.

“Then, when resurrection comes” is an interpretive rendering that identifies the event without preserving the word’s linguistic quality.

“The deafening blast” therefore better suits a direct translation. Reflection can explain its resurrection context.

10. Conclusion

Ṣ KH KH concerns the sound of forceful impact and pounding. Lexical witnesses use the family for hard-object collisions, the sound of a blow, sound’s powerful effect on the ear, and sometimes the act of striking itself.

Al-ṣākhkhah belongs to this family and primarily describes an intense, deafening blast. It is also recorded for a tremendous calamity.

The Qur’an uses only al-ṣākhkhah, in ʿAbasa 80:33. Context connects the blast with resurrection, when people face extraordinary circumstances and the outcome of their deeds.

The word alone does not specify the number of trumpet blasts, the event’s exact stage, the sound’s physical properties, or its definitive effect on human hearing.

Short definition: Ṣ KH KH denotes pounding sound from forceful impact and its powerful effect on the ear; in some uses it also expresses a blow or great, shocking calamity. Brief root meaning: Pounding impact sound and its powerful effect on the ear; also a blow or tremendous calamity.

In ʿAbasa 80:33, al-ṣākhkhah is best rendered “the deafening blast.” This retains the word’s main quality while leaving identification and details of the resurrection event to the surrounding verses.
