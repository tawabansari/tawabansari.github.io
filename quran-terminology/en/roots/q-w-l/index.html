Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root Q W L

Saying, expression, claim, command, attribution, and the responsibility of speech

Qur’anic forms of this root

The root ق و ل (Q W L) in the Qur’an is not limited to producing sound with the tongue. Qawl may be speech, statement, opinion, judgment, claim, answer, promise, accusation, command, or attribution. What is hidden inside a person becomes exposed through qawl; so qawl means bringing meaning, position, or claim into the field of responsibility.

The central meaning of the root

ق و ل (Q W L) in the Qur’an is the root of responsible speech: speech that brings meaning from inwardness into the open, reveals a person’s position, and makes him responsible for what he says.

Qawl in the Qur’an is not mere pronunciation. A person may say something and, by that saying, reveal faith, make a covenant, fabricate a lie, revive a right, spread falsehood, comfort someone, incite a people, or attribute a statement to God.

For this reason, qawl in the Qur’an stands between tongue, heart, action, society, and truth. Speech does not come only from the mouth; it also reveals intention, understanding, faith, fear, desire, partisanship, or sincerity.

In brief: Q W L in the Qur’an means making meaning visible through speech, with responsibility toward truth, the listener, and consequence.

Qāla and qālū: speech, response, and position

Qāla and qālū occur very often in the Qur’an. Their repetition shows that human history in the Qur’an is not only a history of actions; it is also a history of speech. Through speech, people believe, deny, make excuses, pray, repent, threaten, promise, and set truth or falsehood in motion within society.

Qāla may be the speech of prophets: invitation, warning, counsel, and clarification of the path. It may be the speech of believers: faith, trust, prayer, and surrender. It may be the speech of deniers: rejection, mockery, excuse, or denial of resurrection. It may also be the speech of angels, Iblīs, rulers, peoples, or social groups.

So qāla is not merely historical reporting. By reporting speech, the Qur’an exposes inward positions. Every qāla and qālū shows where the speaker stands before truth.

Yaqūl and yaqūlūn: ongoing speech and repeated claim

Yaqūl and yaqūlūn show that speech is not always a completed event. Sometimes speech is a living current repeated in society and shaping people’s minds.

The Qur’an speaks of those who say, “We believe,” while they do not truly believe. It speaks of people who say groundless things about God, the Messenger, the Hereafter, or the believers. It speaks of people who, whenever they face a sign, invent new speech to escape accepting truth.

Here yaqūlūn is not merely a sentence on the tongue; it is the sign of a stable inward and social condition. A person or society that repeatedly produces false speech is not making a verbal slip only; it is covering reality with repeated statements.

Qul: the command to deliver

Qul is one of the most important forms of this root in the Qur’an. Outwardly, it means “Say,” but in Qur’anic context it is not merely an order to pronounce words. Qul is the command to deliver: a truth must be made clear, a boundary must be drawn, a divine answer must stand before question or denial, and the message of revelation must enter the field of address.

When the Qur’an says qul, the Prophet does not own the speech. He does not produce it from himself. He is commanded to deliver. This places an important boundary between revelation and the personal speech of the Prophet. Qul shows that speech must be brought from the divine source into the field of people without the Messenger adding to it or reducing it according to personal desire.

In the final sūrahs of the Qur’an, beginning with qul is deeply meaningful: Qul huwa Allāhu aḥad, Qul aʿūdhu bi-rabbi al-falaq, Qul aʿūdhu bi-rabbi al-nās, and Qul yā ayyuhā al-kāfirūn. These are not only recited sounds. They are declarations of tawḥīd, conscious seeking of refuge, separation from false worship, and placing truth into language and society.

This human effect must not be confused with God’s creative command. The human being, through qul, does not become the absolute creator of reality. But his speech in the human field is not ineffective. Human qawl can establish a covenant, separate a line, reveal seeking refuge, reject falsehood, and make truth present in life.

Qawl: speech, statement, and responsibility

Qawl carries serious weight in the Qur’an. Human speech is not light or consequence-free. A person can speak truth, fabricate a lie, reform, destroy, protect a wounded heart, or pollute a society.

For this reason, the Qur’an is sensitive to the kind of speech. Speech is not judged only by whether it is technically true or false; it is also weighed by its effect, justice, dignity, gentleness, firmness, and relation to truth.

Qawl can be maʿrūf: recognized as good and proper. It can be sadīd: firm, true, and corrective. It can be karīm: noble and preserving dignity. It can be layyin: gentle, even before a tyrannical power. Against this, qawl can be zūr: false and crooked speech; or qawl ʿaẓīm: a grave and weighty statement made wrongly about God or truth.

Qīla: it was said and the scene of address

Qīla in the Qur’an sometimes shows that speech has entered the scene while the focus is not on the speaker. The focus is on the address itself and the response of the audience.

When it is said, “it was said to them,” “they were asked,” “they were warned,” or “they were commanded,” the point is that the message has arrived and now the human response becomes visible. Qīla places the human being before speech. After qīla, complete ignorance no longer has the same meaning; the addressee must show what he does with the message.

In many verses, qīla is followed by qālū. This structure is important: it is said to them, and they say. Revelation, invitation, or warning reaches the human being; then the human tongue uncovers his position.

Taqawwul: fabricating speech and false attribution

Taqawwul is one of the most dangerous branches of this root. It is not merely simple lying. It is fabricating speech, weaving a claim, and attributing words without foundation to a place where one has no right to attribute.

When taqawwul is connected with God, revelation, or the Messenger, its danger becomes multiplied, because the human being fabricates speech for God and places it where revelation belongs. This is where speech moves from personal error into religious and social corruption.

In the Forqan method, taqawwul is a foundational warning. No statement, no matter how famous, old, sacred-looking, or accepted by the majority, should be attributed to God without evidence. Attributing to God requires clear proof from the Book of God. If a person takes something from himself, tradition, tribe, sect, narration, or power and imposes it on people in God’s name, he is standing in the dangerous field of taqawwul.

The qawl of God: command, promise, revelation, and realization

The qawl of God must not be understood like human speech. God has no body, tongue, need, uncertainty, or human inability. When the Qur’an speaks of God’s qawl, it must be understood in a way worthy of God and with transcendence.

God’s qawl may be revelation: guidance made clear for the human being. It may be promise: speech in which no failure occurs. It may be judgment: truthful decision. And it may be creative command. In Yā Sīn 36:82, it says:

إِنَّمَا أَمْرُهُ إِذَا أَرَادَ شَيْئًا أَن يَقُولَ لَهُ كُن فَيَكُونُ

His command, when He intends a thing, is only that He says to it, “Be,” and it is.

Here divine qawl is not merely announcement; it brings realization. But this meaning belongs to God. The human being cannot use this verse to claim independent creative power for himself. God’s qawl is inseparable from His will, knowledge, power, and absolute lordship.

Human qawl: speech and human consequence

Human qawl is not creative in the divine sense, but it is not ineffective. Through speech, the human being creates consequences within human life.

One statement can establish marriage or announce divorce. One statement can be testimony and revive someone’s right, or false testimony and destroy a right. One statement can build peace or ignite war. One statement can calm a human heart or break it. One statement can be attributed to God and pollute religion.

So human speech in the Qur’an is not merely a sound wave. Human qawl affects moral, social, legal, and religious relationships. The tongue is not a simple tool; it is a place of trust.

Qawl and its effect in reality

In the Qur’an, qawl is not completely separate from reality. Speech can reveal truth or cover reality. It can bring a person near to action or create a gap between tongue and action. It can call society toward justice or normalize lies.

From here the difference between qawl sadīd and qawl zūr becomes clear. Qawl sadīd is firm speech: speech that does not bend reality and brings the human being nearer to reform. Qawl zūr is crooked and false speech: speech by which truth is displaced and falsehood wears the clothing of words.

A society is not corrupted only by weapons, money, and power. It is also corrupted by qawl. If false speech becomes normal, if unsupported attributions to God are accepted, if slander and rumor gain a market, and if irresponsible speech breaks human dignity, language becomes an instrument of corruption.

Recognized, sound, noble, and gentle speech

The Qur’an does not speak only about saying the truth; it also speaks about the quality of saying. Truth, if spoken with injustice, humiliation, harshness, self-superiority, or lack of mercy, may lose its effect or become an instrument of ego.

Qawl maʿrūf is speech compatible with goodness, sound reason, dignity, and clean human recognition. It is not deceptive, humiliating, or merciless.

Qawl sadīd is truthful, firm, precise, and reforming speech. Being sadīd is not only verbal accuracy. Speech must be in the right place, with the right intention, and with the right effect.

Qawl karīm is noble speech, especially where the listener needs honor, kindness, and preservation of dignity. Qawl layyin is gentle speech. The Qur’an even commands Moses and Aaron to speak gently before Pharaoh. This shows that the seriousness of a message does not permit violence of tongue. Sometimes gentle speech itself is part of proof and wisdom.

False speech, grave speech, and speaking about God without knowledge

Against proper speech, the Qur’an also exposes dangerous kinds of speech. Qawl zūr is one of them. Zūr means speech that has turned away from truth: false, crooked, deceptive speech, or speech polluted by fabrication and false testimony.

Qawl ʿaẓīm in some contexts points to an extremely grave and wrongful statement: speech whose danger is as weighty as its claim. When the human being speaks without knowledge about God, angels, the Hereafter, revelation, or great truths, the error is not small.

One of the greatest dangers in the Qur’an is speaking about God without knowledge. A person may like a statement, receive it from his forefathers, hear it from a scholar or group, or see it as useful for power and identity; none of this is enough to attribute that statement to God.

In the Forqan method, every religious qawl must be measured against the Qur’an. If a statement is attributed to God, it must receive clarity from the Book of God. If it does not, it should be presented as understanding, possibility, tradition, or human opinion, not as God’s decisive ruling.

Qawl and the distance between tongue and heart

The Qur’an shows that there may be distance between tongue and heart. A person can say something that is not in his heart. He can say, “We believe,” while faith has not settled in his heart. He can make excuses with his tongue while intending something else. He can speak softly while hiding hostility inside.

So qawl alone is never enough. The Qur’an hears speech, but it weighs it with heart, action, truthfulness, and consequence. Saying faith does not replace faith. Saying reform does not replace reform. Saying obedience does not replace obedience.

Qawl and action

One of the Qur’an’s important critiques is the gap between speech and action. A person may speak well while acting against what he says. He may call people to good while staying far from it himself. He may claim faith while his actions show injustice, corruption, arrogance, or irresponsibility.

If qawl separates from action, it can become a cover for deception. But when it joins righteous action, speech gains light. True speech becomes stronger proof when it appears in action.

For this reason, qawl and action are not separate in the Qur’an. Language is the beginning of a position becoming visible, but it is not the end of responsibility. The human being must show that what he has said is also present in the direction of his life.

Methodological note

In translating the root Q W L, one should not always settle for a single English word. Sometimes “said” is enough, but in many verses, saying is not only a report of sound. According to context, qāla may mean answered, claimed, announced, declared, took a position, or attributed.

Qul should not always be understood only as “say,” even though “say” is its natural translation. In explanation, it should be made clear that qul in the Qur’an is the command to deliver, announce, and draw a revelatory boundary.

Qawl, according to context, may be speech, statement, expression, promise, judgment, claim, position, or attribution. In ethical verses, its responsibility must be seen. In verses about God, transcendence must be preserved. In verses about taqawwul and speaking about God, the danger of religious fabrication must be made clear.

Suggested rendering in Forqan

  • Qāla / qālū: he said, they said, answered, declared, announced, took a position, claimed; according to context.
  • Yaqūl / yaqūlūn: he says, they say, claims, keep saying, imagine and declare; according to context.
  • Qul: say, announce, deliver, say clearly, make the boundary clear; according to the address.
  • Qawl: speech, statement, saying, verbal position, claim, promise, judgment, responsibility-bearing word.
  • Qīla: it was said, it was said to them, the address came, it was commanded, it was warned; according to context.
  • Qāʾil / qāʾilūn: speaker, speakers, one who declares, those who say this.
  • Taqawwala / taqawwul: fabricated speech, invented a statement, falsely attributed, carved out a baseless statement, attributed speech to God or revelation without right.
  • Qawl maʿrūf: proper speech, recognized good speech, speech joined with goodness and dignity.
  • Qawl sadīd: sound speech, upright and firm speech, reforming expression.
  • Qawl karīm: noble speech, speech joined with honor.
  • Qawl layyin: gentle speech, calm and wise expression.
  • Qawl zūr: false speech, crooked statement, false testimony, deceptive and baseless expression.

Summary

ق و ل (Q W L) in the Qur’an is not only the root of saying with the tongue. It shows meaning, position, claim, command, promise, and responsibility becoming visible. Through qawl, the human being enters the field of truth and falsehood.

The qawl of God in the Qur’an is tied to command, revelation, promise, judgment, and realization; but it must be understood with transcendence and not confused with human speech. Human qawl is not creative in the divine sense, but it has real effect in human life. Through speech, the human being can reveal truth, fabricate falsehood, bind a covenant, revive a right, pollute religion, or bring society nearer to reform.

For this reason, the Qur’an does not treat language lightly. Speech in the Qur’an is a trust. Every qawl must be weighed by truth, knowledge, dignity, justice, and action.

In the Forqan method, Q W L should be understood this way: saying is not merely sound; it is making a position visible before God, people, truth, and responsibility. Every statement either helps the light of truth or places another veil over it.
