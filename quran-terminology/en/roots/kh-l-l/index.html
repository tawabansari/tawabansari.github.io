From the spaces between things and passage through them to khullah, khalīl, and close human relationships

Arabic خ ل ل encompasses a broad family concerning intervals, gaps, penetration, and close bonds. Khalal can mean a gap or opening between things; takhallul, entering and passing among them; and khilāl and khalīl, in one semantic branch, close friendship and a special relationship between people.

Two apparently different conditions occur in this family: a space remaining between things, and a relationship becoming so close that language describes it through penetration and interweaving.

Close friendship can be called khullah, but this does not mean every friendship actually arose through something penetrating something else. The image can explain a semantic connection without establishing the word’s certain historical origin.

The Qur’an uses this root in two prominent fields: spaces between things and passage through them, as among houses, gardens, and clouds; and friendship and close bonds, as in Abraham’s being a khalīl, someone’s intimate companion, and friendships tested at the Resurrection.

1. Forms and semantic core

خ ل ل is a doubled triliteral root: its second and third radicals are identical.

Arabic records numerous forms and derivatives in this family.

Khalal denotes an interval, gap, or opening between things. Khilāl can mean the space between objects or the route through them. Takhallala means entering and passing among things, and in some uses penetrating.

In physical uses, khalla concerns piercing, inserting something into another thing, or fastening two parts with a slender implement. Instrumental khilāl denotes a slender stick used to clean between the teeth or fasten two edges of a garment.

Extended khalal can mean a deficiency, lack, or defect in a task, decision, or structure.

In the branch concerning need, khallah denotes lack, want, and poverty. Certain early uses also apply khalīl to someone poor and in need.

In human relationships, khullah means a special, close friendship, and khalīl a close friend or intimate companion. Its plurals include akhillāʾ and khullān.

Mukhāllah and khilāl also describe close relationships and friendship in this branch.

Three principal groups occur in the Qur’an:

  • Khilāl: the spaces between things; and, in one occurrence, friendship.
  • Khalīl and akhillāʾ: a close friend and close friends.
  • Khullah: friendship or a special bond.

Verbs such as takhallala and khālla, and the exact noun form khalal, do not occur in the Qur’an. Their early Arabic witnesses nevertheless help establish the root’s field. There are thirteen occurrences in thirteen verses and eleven surahs: eight khilāl, seven spatial and one concerning friendship; three khalīlan and one plural al-akhillāʾ; and one khullatun.

Slenderness and intervals: Ibn Fāris’s explanation

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies a shared foundation and states:

وَمَرْجِعُ ذَلِكَ إِمَّا إِلَى دِقَّةٍ أَوْ فُرْجَةٍ

wa-marjiʿu dhālika immā ilā diqqatin aw furjatin

Meaning: “This goes back either to slenderness or to an interval or opening.”

The definition brings together two features.

First, the slenderness of something able to pass through a narrow space, such as a small stick entering between the teeth.

Second, an interval or gap between things, such as the space between two walls or cloud masses.

Ibn Fāris also connects close friendship to this field, as though two friends were joined like garment edges fastened with a slender pin.

This image can clarify a semantic relationship without being the only possible explanation or a certain history of khalīl’s origin.

Numerous Arabic witnesses also show that khalīl was a familiar word for a close friend and companion before its explanation within philosophical or religious discussions.

2. Physical uses and semantic extensions in early Arabic

The space between things

Al-khalal denotes the open space or interval between two things.

This may be between walls, houses, trees, ranks, or parts of a body.

Takhallala diyārahum, for instance, means passing among their houses.

Jalasnā khilāla al-ḥayyi describes sitting among a group’s houses or settlement.

Khilāl does not name the walls or houses themselves; it concerns the spaces between them and the area occupied or traversed by someone.

It need not mean a small hole. It can be a narrow interval between objects or a wider space among houses and gardens.

Consequently, “among” and “amid” are often more accurate than restricting the word to a hole.

A narrow path and passage among objects

Lexical sources record khall for a narrow path through accumulated sand.

It belongs to this family because it runs between parts of the terrain and provides a route through them.

Takhallala al-ramla also describes passing through a sandy expanse.

Here penetration or passage does not mean destroying an obstacle: someone moves through spaces among obstacles.

This distinguishes passing through from breaking.

Something can pass through a group of things without breaking or destroying it.

Khilāl: an implement entering a narrow interval

Arabic uses khilāl for a slender stick removing food remnants from between the teeth.

The name fits the implement’s shape and function: it must be thin enough to enter the spaces between teeth.

Khilāl also denotes an implement fastening two edges of cloth or a garment.

A slender object passes through the fabric and holds two parts together.

One physical field thus yields two different results: entering an interval to remove something, and entering to join two parts.

Neither result should become the root’s fixed meaning. The root highlights spaces and penetration into them; removing or joining depends on the implement and purpose.

Khalal as a defect or deficiency

Fī al-amri khalalun means that a task or matter has a deficiency or defect.

Lisān al-ʿArab connects khalal in affairs with weakness, corruption, and lack of firmness.

The connection with a physical gap is intelligible: an inappropriate opening in a structure that should be firm and continuous can impair its functioning.

A crack can weaken a wall; a missing essential component in a plan or argument can likewise be called khalal.

Not every interval is a defect, however. Spaces between houses or branches can be natural and necessary.

Deficiency is therefore an extended use, not a meaning to impose on every Qur’anic khilāl.

Khallah as need or lack

Early Arabic also records khallah for poverty, need, and deficiency.

Bihi khallatun shadīdatun means that someone is in severe need.

Sadda khallatahu describes meeting someone’s need or filling a lack.

Need is pictured as an empty place or gap in someone’s circumstances that requires filling.

In this branch, khalīl can mean someone poor and in need in certain early witnesses.

This matters because khalīl has not had only one meaning throughout Arabic usage.

The existence of “needy,” however, does not make it the translation in every Qur’anic verse. The preferable meaning follows from each construction and context.

Lisān al-ʿArab: spatial and instrumental khilāl, deficiency, need, and Zuhayr’s witness

Khullah and khalīl: close friendship

In human relationships, khullah denotes a special, close friendship.

Ibn Durayd explains khalīl as someone whose affection is sincere and sound.

Al-Zajjāj likewise emphasizes a friendship without defect in explaining the word.

Other sources connect it to takhallul: friendship becomes so close that it seems to penetrate a person’s interior or settle in the heart.

Another explanation is that friends meet one another’s needs and deficiencies, hence their relationship being called khullah.

These explanations can illuminate semantic links between friendship, intervals, penetration, and need. None can confidently be made the certain historical origin of all uses.

Khalīl, in particular, is not merely someone one recognizes or occasionally talks with.

Its familiar use concerns a closer, more particular relationship.

Close friendship does not, however, validate every action or belief of either party. A very close friend may lead someone toward wrongdoing.

The parties’ qualities and the direction of their friendship determine its ethical value, rather than the title khalīl itself.

3. Early Arabic witnesses

Khalīl in poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

خَلِيلَيَّ مُرَّا بِي عَلَى أُمِّ جُنْدَبِ
نُقَضِّ لُبَانَاتِ الْفُؤَادِ الْمُعَذَّبِ

khalīlayya murrā bī ʿalā ummi jundabi / nuqaḍḍi lubānāti al-fuʾādi al-muʿadhdhabi

Translation:

“My two friends, take me past Umm Jundab, so that we may satisfy the desires of the tormented heart.”

Khalīlayya is the dual of khalīl with the first-person possessive pronoun: “my two friends.”

The poet asks two close companions to help him reach the beloved.

The witness shows khalīl as a poetic designation for a close friend and companion.

It does not require every poetic khalīl to occupy the highest imaginable emotional rank. Addressing two companions in this form can also belong to poetic convention.

Transmissions vary, including nuqaḍḍi lubānāti and other components. The quotation must therefore be understood within the dīwān’s versions and the poem’s attribution to Imruʾ al-Qays.

Nawādir al-Makhṭūṭāt: Imruʾ al-Qays’s witness with a reference to the dīwān commentary

Khalīl as someone needy in poetry attributed to Zuhayr

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab transmit a verse from Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā:

وَإِنْ أَتَاهُ خَلِيلٌ يَوْمَ مَسْغَبَةٍ
يَقُولُ لَا غَائِبٌ مَالِي وَلَا حَرِمُ

wa-in atāhu khalīlun yawma masghabatin / yaqūlu lā ghāʾibun mālī wa-lā ḥarimu

Translation:

“If someone needy comes to him on a day of hunger and hardship, he says: ‘My wealth is neither out of reach nor withheld from you.’”

The lexical explanation glosses khalīl as poor and needy. It also explains ḥarim as withheld or forbidden; the translation accordingly retains the description of the wealth as not withheld.

This witness shows why not every use of khalīl should be restricted to an intimate friend.

Some transmissions give masʾalah instead of masghabah. This difference in describing the setting of a request does not remove the lexical explanation concerning need.

Such a poetic use nevertheless does not require “poor” in Al-Nisāʾ 4:125 or Al-Furqān 25:28. Their constructions concerning taking someone as a khalīl support close friendship.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: needy khalīl and ḥarim as withheld

Takhlīl as singling out in Laqīṭ ibn Yaʿmar’s poetry

A poem attributed to Laqīṭ ibn Yaʿmar al-Iyādī reads:

أَبْلِغْ إِيَادًا وَخَلِّلْ فِي سَرَاتِهِمْ
إِنِّي أَرَى الرَّأْيَ إِنْ لَمْ أُعْصَ قَدْ نَصَعَا

abligh iyādan wa-khallil fī sarātihim / innī arā al-raʾya in lam uʿṣa qad naṣaʿā

Translation:

“Convey the message to Iyād, and address their leading men in particular; I see the counsel clearly, if I am not disobeyed.”

Khallil here means directing the message particularly to a specified group. Morphologically it is an imperative of Form II khallala, rather than Form V takhallala.

The poet wants more than a general announcement: the community’s leaders are to be specifically informed.

Takhlīl in this witness can thus describe conveying a message with particular attention to one part of a group. It should not be equated simply with spatial passage or with Form V.

The witness is transmitted as pre-Islamic poetry, but its ending varies, including naṣaʿā and naṣaḥā. This does not decisively alter khallil’s meaning.

4. Qur’anic khilāl: the space between things

Seven Qur’anic uses concern spaces between things or passage among them.

Moving among houses — Al-Isrāʾ 17:5

فَجَاسُوا خِلَالَ الدِّيَارِ

fa-jāsū khilāla al-diyāri

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Then they searched and moved among the houses.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:5

Khilāla al-diyār describes spaces among houses and people’s dwellings.

Jāsū concerns movement and searching within an area; khilāl locates that movement.

Grammatically, khilāl is an adverbial of place annexed to al-diyār.

It therefore means neither the houses’ friendship nor their defect, but the spaces among them.

Moving through a group — Al-Tawbah 9:47

وَلَأَوْضَعُوا خِلَالَكُمْ يَبْغُونَكُمُ الْفِتْنَةَ

wa-la-awḍaʿū khilālakum yabghūnakumu al-fitnata

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“They would have hurried among you, seeking turmoil for you.”

Al-Tawbah 9:47

Khilālakum denotes the midst of the addressed group.

The context is conditional: had those people gone out with the believers, they would have added only trouble and worked to stir up discord among them.

Khilālakum itself does not mean fomenting discord; that comes from yabghūnakumu al-fitnah.

Nor does khilāl necessarily mean covert infiltration or spying. Those may be instances of moving within a group, but the word itself describes location and a relationship among people.

Water flowing among gardens — Al-Isrāʾ 17:91 and Al-Kahf 18:33

This excerpt occurs in Al-Isrāʾ 17:91:

فَتُفَجِّرَ الْأَنْهَارَ خِلَالَهَا تَفْجِيرًا

fa-tufajjira al-anhāra khilālahā tafjīran

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “Then make rivers flow abundantly through it.”

This belongs to a demand opponents make for accepting the Prophet. It quotes a request or proposed condition rather than necessarily reporting its fulfillment.

This excerpt occurs in Al-Kahf 18:33:

وَفَجَّرْنَا خِلَالَهُمَا نَهَرًا

wa-fajjarnā khilālahumā naharan

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “We caused a river to flow between the two.”

Khilālahumā refers to the two gardens introduced earlier.

In both verses, khilāl concerns where water flows within the garden setting.

“Within a garden” differs from “between every individual tree.” Khilāl alone does not determine the river’s exact course, distance from trees, or irrigation method.

Rivers within the earth — Al-Naml 27:61

وَجَعَلَ خِلَالَهَا أَنْهَارًا

wa-jaʿala khilālahā anhāran

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “He placed rivers within it.”

Al-Naml 27:61

The pronoun hā refers to the earth.

Khilālahā denotes the spaces within and among the earth’s parts where rivers are situated.

It can thus describe geographical structure.

The word itself does not establish the rivers’ geological origin, underground courses, or how channels formed. The verse places rivers within the earth without giving scientific details of their formation.

Rain emerging through clouds — Al-Nūr 24:43 and Al-Rūm 30:48

This excerpt occurs in Al-Nūr 24:43:

فَتَرَى الْوَدْقَ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ

fa-tarā al-wadqa yakhruju min khilālihi

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “Then you see rain emerging from within it.”

Al-Rūm 30:48 uses the same expression.

Both concern clouds. Khilālihi denotes intervals and passages among their parts from which rain emerges.

These are clear examples of the root’s physical meaning.

Khilāl denotes spaces among parts of the cloud; it does not itself mean rain or rainfall.

Al-wadq names the rain, while yakhruju expresses emerging.

Lexical sources also transmit khalalihi in this expression, relating it to gaps and openings in clouds. This variant preserves the basic field of spaces among components.

Together, the seven uses concern spaces among houses, people, gardens, parts of the earth, and clouds.

“Among” and “amid” are present throughout, but each context has a different kind of space and movement.

5. Khullah, khalīl, and Qur’anic friendship

Abraham as khalīl — Al-Nisāʾ 4:125

وَاتَّخَذَ اللَّهُ إِبْرَاهِيمَ خَلِيلًا

wa-ittakhadha allāhu ibrāhīma khalīlan

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The God took Abraham as a khalīl, a close friend.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:125

This is the principal use of khalīl concerning Abraham’s relationship with The God.

Allāh is the subject, Ibrāhīm the first object of ittakhadha, and khalīlan its second object.

The verse therefore does not say Abraham chose The God as his khalīl; it says The God took Abraham as khalīl.

This grammatical distinction matters for understanding the verse.

Khalīl’s familiar Arabic meaning is a very close friend. Because the verse concerns a divine relationship with Abraham, however, not every feature of human friendship should be transferred to it without examination.

Human friendship may involve mutual need, bodily dependence, shared feelings, and seeking help. Khalīl does not establish a divine need for Abraham.

Conversely, divine difference from human beings does not justify abandoning khalīl’s familiar meaning altogether.

Lexical sources discuss two principal interpretations: a very close friend and someone needy.

The former has stronger support here: The God takes Abraham as khalīl, while the context concerns goodness in religion, submission to The God, and following Abraham’s upright way.

“Needy” is valid in the word’s lexical history, but a base translation here would require stronger indications. Abraham’s need for The God is also common to the servant–Lord relationship, whereas the verse describes something particular to Abraham.

Lisān al-ʿArab: close-friend and needy interpretations

Does Abraham’s being khalīl imply independent legislative authority?

The same verse connects Abraham with millata Ibrāhīma ḥanīfan.

Other verses, such as Al-Baqarah 2:124, describe his leadership and divine testing.

Khalīl itself, however, does not mean imam, messenger, bearer of an independent religious law, or a necessary intermediary between The God and people.

The title describes a special relationship; Abraham’s other responsibilities and positions must be learned from verses addressing those titles.

Forqan’s existing article on imamah examines Abraham’s position in relation to testing, faithfulness, and his upright way. This Qur’anic connection helps illuminate Abraham’s character without replacing independent study of khalīl.

Khalīl in a hypothetical situation — Al-Isrāʾ 17:73

وَإِذًا لَاتَّخَذُوكَ خَلِيلًا

wa-idhan la-ittakhadhūka khalīlan

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Then they would have taken you as a close friend.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:73

The excerpt belongs to a context describing a group’s efforts to turn the Prophet from what was revealed to him.

It says they would take him as khalīl if he attributed something other than revelation to The God.

Khalīl here concerns a close relationship and social acceptance.

Crucially, the verse describes a hypothetical situation rather than confirming that such deviation occurred.

A relationship’s closeness alone therefore does not guarantee positive ethical value.

A group may seek someone’s friendship on condition that he departs from truth.

A group’s desire for friendship does not validate that group’s demands.

The relationship must be assessed by its content and the course the parties follow.

Regretting the choice of a khalīl — Al-Furqān 25:28

يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ لَيْتَنِي لَمْ أَتَّخِذْ فُلَانًا خَلِيلًا

yā waylatā laytanī lam attakhidh fulānan khalīlan

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Alas for me! If only I had not taken so-and-so as a close friend.”

Al-Furqān 25:28

Someone regrets choosing an intimate companion.

The surrounding verses explain the consequences of following a wrongful relationship.

Al-Furqān 25:29 explains the regret: that friend led the person away from the Reminder after it had reached him.

The criticism therefore concerns the relationship’s effect in turning someone from guidance, rather than close friendship itself.

Lexically, khalīl remains a close friend; ethically, this friend plays a misleading role in the account.

The distinction prevents an unwarranted conclusion that every deep friendship is dangerous or forbidden.

The verse concerns a particular friendship that led away from the Reminder.

Fulān introduces an unnamed individual. Identifying a particular historical person is not part of the word’s or verse’s direct meaning.

The fate of akhillāʾ — Al-Zukhruf 43:67

الْأَخِلَّاءُ يَوْمَئِذٍ بَعْضُهُمْ لِبَعْضٍ عَدُوٌّ إِلَّا الْمُتَّقِينَ

al-akhillāʾu yawmaʾidhin baʿḍuhum li-baʿḍin ʿaduwwun illā al-muttaqīna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Close friends on that day will be enemies to one another, except the mindful.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:67

Al-akhillāʾ is the plural of khalīl.

Even close friendship need not mean ethical stability or permanent companionship.

People closely connected in worldly life may disown one another at the Resurrection or become enemies.

The verse, however, makes an explicit exception:

إِلَّا الْمُتَّقِينَ

illā al-muttaqīna

Proposed translation of this excerpt from Al-Zukhruf 43:67: “Except the mindful.”

It should therefore not be summarized as “all friendships become enmity at the Resurrection.”

The exception shows why the relationship’s kind and ethical direction matter.

Alongside Al-Furqān 25:28 and Al-Furqān 25:29, this shows that khullah and being khalīl do not themselves guarantee guidance. Friendship may support someone on the right path or lead them away.

6. Lā khullah and lā khilāl: friendship on the day of account

This excerpt occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:254:

لَا بَيْعٌ فِيهِ وَلَا خُلَّةٌ وَلَا شَفَاعَةٌ

lā bayʿun fīhi wa-lā khullatun wa-lā shafāʿatun

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“There will be no trade in it, no friendship, and no intercession.”

This excerpt occurs in Ibrāhīm 14:31:

لَا بَيْعٌ فِيهِ وَلَا خِلَالٌ

lā bayʿun fīhi wa-lā khilālun

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“There will be no trade in it and no friendship.”

The expressions are close, but their lexical forms differ.

Khullah in Al-Baqarah 2:254 denotes friendship and a close bond.

Khilāl in Ibrāhīm 14:31 means friendship. Lexical sources record two analyses: a verbal noun of khālla/khālala, befriending, or a plural of khullah, friendships.

Either way, its contextual meaning concerns friendship rather than spaces between things.

Wa-lā khilāl should therefore not be translated as “there are no gaps or intervals.”

Al-Ṭabarī: khilāl as friendship alongside trade

Do these verses deny every friendship at the Resurrection?

If lā khullah denied the existence of every kind of friendship in the Hereafter, why would Al-Zukhruf 43:67 except the mindful from friends becoming enemies?

Answering requires attention to context and construction.

Al-Baqarah 2:254 places khullah alongside trade and intercession.

Ibrāhīm 14:31 likewise places khilāl alongside trade.

This association concerns a day when people can no longer recover what they have lost as in worldly life, through transactions, personal connections, or reliance on relationships.

In this article’s contextual synthesis, the preferable understanding is that friendship is not an independent means of transaction, compensation, or escaping accountability. This explanation results from reading the verses together; it does not make “no independent means” the direct lexical meaning of lā khullah.

This does not deny sound, enduring relationships among the mindful.

Nor does the explanation establish every kind of intercession or human relationship at the Resurrection without qualification. Those subjects have their own Qur’anic verses and conditions.

For lexical study, khullah means friendship; the scope of its negation in Al-Baqarah 2:254 follows from that verse’s context and related passages.

7. Distinctions from nearby roots

KH L L (khalal) compared with F R J (farj)

One principal meaning of ف ر ج is an opening or interval between things.

Furjah and khalal can therefore be close.

Beyond intervals, however, attested خ ل ل also concerns slenderness, penetration, passage among things, and close bonds.

The families may overlap in describing a gap between objects without being synonymous in all uses.

KH L L (takhallul) compared with N F DH (nufūdh)

ن ف ذ concerns passing through and reaching from one side of an obstacle or route to another.

Takhallul can likewise mean penetrating among things.

It highlights the spaces among components, whereas nufūdh can emphasize passage all the way through.

In fa-jāsū khilāla al-diyār, the subject is movement among houses, rather than necessarily crossing an entire city from one side to the other.

Passage among things can therefore relate to penetration, without the roots being identical throughout their uses.

KH L L (khullah) compared with W D D (wudd)

Wudd concerns affection, friendship, and positive inclination toward another.

Khullah also means friendship, but its familiar use can highlight a special, close relationship.

Someone may feel affection toward many people without making every one a khalīl.

Wudd and khullah are close within affection’s field, while being khalīl emphasizes a more particular relationship.

This does not establish a fixed ladder of emotional ranks applying in every context without independent evidence.

Study of W D D

KH L L (khullah) compared with Ṣ D Q (ṣadāqah)

Ṣadāqah and ṣadīq relate to truthfulness and friendship in Arabic.

Ṣadīq’s familiar use denotes a friend.

Khalīl is also a close friend, with lexical witnesses emphasizing a special, close relationship.

Early sources sometimes explain the words through one another, so their difference should not become an absolute opposition.

Truthfulness can ethically characterize a worthy friend, but khalīl does not guarantee someone is always truthful and faithful.

Al-Furqān 25:28 and Al-Zukhruf 43:67 illustrate this distinction.

Study of Ṣ D Q

KH L L (khalīl) compared with W L Y (walī)

Qur’anic walī encompasses a broad field of proximity, guardianship, assistance, and connection.

Khalīl also involves closeness but particularly describes close friendship.

A khalīl may also be someone’s walī or supporter in a certain respect, without every walī meaning khalīl.

Wilāyah can concern responsibility and guardianship, which are not necessary components of khalīl.

Abraham’s title therefore should not be converted into guardianship or legislative authority without independent examination.

Study of W L Y

KH L L (khalal) compared with KH L W (khuluww)

خ ل و concerns emptiness, absence, and being away from others’ presence.

خ ل ل can describe an interval between existing things.

Khilāla al-diyār presupposes houses with spaces among them.

An empty house, however, lacks a person or something within it.

The concepts may coexist in some situations but are not synonymous.

Study of KH L W

KH L L (khalal) compared with KH L Ṭ (khalṭ)

خ ل ط concerns mixing or intermingling two or more things.

خ ل ل concerns spaces between things in one field and penetration and close bonds in another.

People may mingle and associate, developing a close relationship, but association alone does not make them intimate friends.

Khalīṭ can mean companion, associate, or partner, whereas familiar khalīl means a close friend.

This distinction matters particularly for studying human and social relationships.

Study of KH L Ṭ

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Spatial khilāl must first be distinguished from khilāl concerning friendship.

In the seven spatial uses, “among,” “amid,” “through,” or “from within” fits. “Gap” is appropriate only where the context identifies a particular opening or interval.

Khilāl in Ibrāhīm 14:31 concerns friendship and should be rendered “friendship” or “a friendly relationship.”

Khullah in Al-Baqarah 2:254 likewise means friendship, but its accounting-day context must be distinguished from the general question of whether friendship exists in the Hereafter.

Khalīl in Al-Nisāʾ 4:125, Al-Isrāʾ 17:73, and Al-Furqān 25:28 approaches “close friend” or “intimate companion.” For Abraham, khalīl itself can be retained if the root explanation makes its meaning clear.

Akhillāʾ in Al-Zukhruf 43:67 is khalīl’s plural, denoting close friends.

Brief root meaning

Intervals and spaces between things; penetration and passage among them; and, in human relationships, friendship and close bonds.

Meanings in Qur’anic uses

Khilālakum — Al-Tawbah 9:47: In your midst.

Khilāla al-diyār — Al-Isrāʾ 17:5: Among the houses.

Khilālahā — Al-Isrāʾ 17:91 and Al-Naml 27:61: Within it.

Khilālahumā — Al-Kahf 18:33: Between the two.

Min khilālihi — Al-Nūr 24:43 and Al-Rūm 30:48: From among its parts.

Khilālun — Ibrāhīm 14:31: Friendship or a friendly relationship.

Khullatun — Al-Baqarah 2:254: Close friendship.

Khalīlan — Al-Nisāʾ 4:125, Al-Isrāʾ 17:73, and Al-Furqān 25:28: A very close friend.

Al-akhillāʾ — Al-Zukhruf 43:67: Close friends.

Explanatory images such as love penetrating the heart should not replace khalīl in a base translation. They can assist lexical discussion, while the verses themselves speak about a friendly relationship.

Conclusion

Arabic خ ل ل concerns intervals, openings, slenderness, and penetration among things. The family includes words for spaces between objects, narrow paths, piercing, defects, and need.

In its human branch, khullah and khalīl mean friendship and close bonds. Lexicographers explain connections through penetration into the heart, joining garment edges, or meeting a friend’s needs. These illuminate semantic links without establishing every word’s certain history.

Qur’anic khilāl is mostly spatial: intervals among houses, gardens, the earth, and clouds. The same form, however, denotes friendship in Ibrāhīm 14:31.

Qur’anic khalīl also denotes a close friend. The God takes Abraham as khalīl; a group would draw the Prophet close if he deviated; someone regrets choosing the wrong friend; and the Qur’an describes friendship’s transformation on the day of account.

A relationship’s closeness does not itself establish its truth or ethical worth. Friendship may serve truth or lead away from it. Conduct and direction determine the relationship’s value, rather than the title khalīl alone.

The root should therefore retain its two main fields: spaces between things and penetration among them, and close bonds in human relationships. Each word’s construction and context identify the intended meaning, rather than shared root letters alone. Brief root meaning: Intervals and spaces between things; penetration and passage among them; and, in human relationships, friendship and close bonds.
