Qur’anic Root KH L ʿ (خ ل ع): Removing, Separating, and Dissolving a Bond

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَاخْلَعْ
(fa-ikhlaʿ)
The sole Qur’anic form; Ṭā Hā 20:12; Form I imperative, second-person masculine singular

خَلَعَ ـ يَخْلَعُ
(khalaʿa–yakhlaʿu)
Arabic base and imperfect; these two forms do not occur in the Qur’an

خَلْع
(khalʿ)
Verbal noun: removing or separating; this form is not Qur’anic

مَخْلُوع
(makhlūʿ)
Passive participle: removed from an attachment or position; not Qur’anic

خِلْعَة
(khilʿah)
A garment taken off or bestowed; not Qur’anic

خَلِيع
(khalīʿ)
In certain uses, an outcast or solitary hunter; not Qur’anic

اِنْخَلَعَ ـ تَخَلَّعَ
(inkhalaʿa–takhallaʿa)
Forms describing separation or dislocation; not Qur’anic

خَالَعَ ـ خُلْع
(khālaʿa–khulʿ)

From taking off footwear and clothing to release from restraint, removal from office, and separation in human relationships

In Arabic, خ ل ع concerns removing, separating, or setting aside something previously connected or attached to something else. Its clearest image is taking clothing off the body or footwear off the feet, but its uses extend beyond clothing. Removing an animal’s tether, dissolving a pact, deposing someone from a social position, and marital separation also belong to this word family.

An important feature of khalʿ is the removal of an existing attachment or bond. Clothing worn on the body is taken off; a restraint holding an animal is removed; and in social uses, a previously established relationship ends.

Khalʿ does not always mean cutting, breaking, or destroying. A shoe may be taken off without harming either shoe or foot. Leaving a position or relationship likewise need not destroy either party.

The Qur’an uses this root only in a command to Moses: فَاخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ, “Take off your sandals.” This displays the root’s direct physical meaning, while early Arabic witnesses illuminate its extension into social and legal fields.

1. Forms and derivatives

The basic verb is خَلَعَ ـ يَخْلَعُ, with the verbal noun خَلْع.

Important recorded forms include khalaʿa, removing or separating; ikhtalaʿa, taking off or becoming separated; inkhalaʿa, coming out of position or becoming detached; and takhallaʿa, becoming separated, falling apart, or moving the limbs irregularly.

Makhlūʿ describes something removed from its former position or attachment. It can apply to footwear, clothing, a bodily member, or someone removed from office.

In clothing uses, khilʿah denotes a garment taken off. Dictionaries also record the related meaning of a garment bestowed on another person.

Socially, khalīʿ can describe someone cast out of the bonds and protection of family or community. Other uses apply it to someone unrestrained, rebellious, or isolated.

Khālaʿa and mukhālaʿah concern separation within marriage. Khulʿ, with ḍammah on khāʾ, is also a legal term for a kind of marital separation involving the payment or transfer of compensation.

Alongside these, certain botanical uses apply khāliʿ to ripe fruit or a plant condition in which part emerges from a former covering or attachment.

Not all these derivatives occur in the Qur’an.

The sole Qur’anic form is فَاخْلَعْ in Ṭā Hā 20:12. Ikhlaʿ is a second-person masculine singular Form I imperative with prefixed fa-. Its subject is the implicit anta, “you,” and the imperative is built on sukūn. Fa-, kāf, and other attached letters are not part of the triliteral root.

2. Semantic core and early Arabic uses

Separation from something previously attached

Ibn Fāris identifies one foundation for this root in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

مُزَايَلَةُ الشَّيْءِ الَّذِي كَانَ يُشْتَمَلُ بِهِ أَوْ عَلَيْهِ

muzāyalatu al-shayʾi alladhī kāna yushtamalu bihi aw ʿalayhi

Meaning: “Separation from something with which, or within which, one was previously enveloped.”

The earlier relationship between two things matters in this definition. Khalʿ concerns something that was in a condition of covering, attachment, or belonging, whose connection is then removed.

In خَلَعَ ثَوْبَهُ, for instance, a garment is taken off the body; in خَلَعَ نَعْلَهُ, footwear is removed from the foot; and in خَلَعَ قَيْدَهُ, a restraint binding or limiting something is removed.

Something in each use was previously attached to something else or enveloped it.

Ibn Fāris’s definition should not be narrowed to make every khalʿ concern clothing or coverings. Linguistic witnesses also use the root for displacing a bodily member at its joint, lifting restrictions, and dissolving social bonds.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: separation from a former covering or attachment

Khalʿ and forcible separation

Al-Khalīl’s Kitāb al-ʿAyn and some other dictionaries connect khalʿ with nazʿ, adding that khalʿ may involve a degree of deliberateness or unhurried action.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

كَنَزْعِهِ إِلَّا أَنَّ فِي الْخَلْعِ مُهْلَةً

ka-nazʿihi illā anna fī al-khalʿi muhlatan

Meaning: “Like pulling it off, except that khalʿ involves a degree of taking time.”

The same source, however, notes that some lexicographers make no distinction between khalʿ and nazʿ.

Slowness therefore cannot be made a certain, universal component of khalʿ. Something may be removed gently in one situation, while in another separation can be sudden or involve pressure.

Taking off footwear can be unhurried, whereas a joint may leave its position through a sudden impact or force.

What these uses share is separation from a former attachment, rather than a uniform speed or intensity.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab: deliberateness in removal and lexical disagreement

Taking off clothing and bestowing it

Early Arabic uses خَلَعَ الثَّوْبَ for taking clothing off the body.

Khilʿah likewise relates originally to a garment taken off.

In some social settings, a person takes off a garment and gives it to someone else. Khilʿah is accordingly also known for a bestowed garment or robe of honor.

Taking off a garment and giving it away are related but distinct actions. Removal follows from the verb khalʿ itself; bestowal or transfer of ownership comes from the action and social setting.

Khilʿah as a gift is thus an extended meaning. Giving must not be made an essential component of khalaʿa in every context.

Releasing an animal from restraint

Arabic records خَلَعَ قَيْدَهُ for removing a tether and خَلَعَ دَابَّتَهُ for releasing an animal from restraint.

What is removed here is the restraint or restraining device.

Once it is removed, the animal can move freely. Freedom of movement, however, is the result of removing the restriction, rather than freedom necessarily being the root’s own meaning.

This distinction matters because an action can have a positive or negative result without that result being inherent in its basic meaning.

Removing an animal’s tether can allow release and free movement. In human relationships, however, severing a bond may be beneficial, harmful, or ethically complex.

Dislocation at a joint

Arabic also uses khalʿ for a joint leaving its natural position.

For example, displacement at a joint of the arm or leg can be described as khalʿ.

The bodily member need not have been severed. A bone or joint has left its normal articulation, while the limb may remain attached to the body.

Khalʿ therefore does not necessarily mean completely cutting off or destroying bodily connections. Displacement from the natural attachment can suffice.

Takhallaʿa fī mishyatihi is also recorded for irregular movement or shaking of the limbs, as though bodily parts were departing from their usual coordination and firmness.

These uses are intelligible within the field of separation and loss of attachment, although their intensity and manner differ.

3. Early Arabic witnesses and social relationships

Removing a camel’s tether in Al-Akhnas ibn Shihāb’s poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Akhnas ibn Shihāb al-Taghlibī reads:

وَكُلُّ أُنَاسٍ قَارَبُوا قَيْدَ فَحْلِهِمْ
وَنَحْنُ خَلَعْنَا قَيْدَهُ فَهُوَ سَارِبُ

wa-kullu unāsin qārabū qayda faḥlihim / wa-naḥnu khalaʿnā qaydahu fa-huwa sāribu

Translation:

“Every group has kept its male camel on a short tether, but we have removed his tether, and he roams freely.”

Kitāb al-ʿAyn and other sources transmit this verse. Ibn Qutaybah’s Al-Maʿānī al-Kabīr also gives the opening as وَكَمْ أُنَاسٍ.

These textual variants do not affect the fundamental use of khalaʿnā qaydahu.

Ibn Qutaybah explains that the male camel’s movement can lead the herd to follow. Others restrain theirs to prevent the herd wandering away; the poet celebrates his group’s strength and security, saying they can let their camel roam.

Removing the tether is a physical action that, in the poetic image, also signals the group’s power and freedom of movement.

Khalaʿnā itself, however, does not mean power or superiority. Its direct meaning is removing the tether; power follows from the social setting and poetic image.

The witness shows that the root was familiar in pastoral and nomadic life for removing a restraining device, apart from its extensive technical uses.

Al-Maʿānī al-Kabīr: Al-Akhnas’s witness, opening variant, and the camel tether

Dissolving a protective relationship in tribal society

In certain pre-Islamic Arabic social settings, people stood within family, tribal, and communal bonds. These could involve protection, obligation, and collective responsibility.

Lexical sources call someone khalīʿ when his family or group disowns him and no longer accepts responsibility for certain actions of his.

In Kitāb al-ʿAyn, Al-Khalīl explains that a father or group might announce separation from someone feared capable of harmful or criminal conduct, so that his behavior would not be attributed to them.

This account supplies the social background of khalīʿ.

Nothing is removed from a body here; a social and protective bond is dissolved.

Certain uses accordingly also apply khalīʿ to an unrestrained or rebellious person.

Lexical meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from ethical judgment. Someone separated or excluded from a group can be called khalīʿ, but the description alone does not establish that all his conduct was wrong.

In particular historical cases, separation may result from someone’s behavior; the root’s meaning, however, concerns separation from a former bond.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: khalīʿ and the dissolution of tribal protection

A poetic witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

وَوَادٍ كَجَوْفِ الْعَيْرِ قَفْرٍ قَطَعْتُهُ
بِهِ الذِّئْبُ يَعْوِي كَالْخَلِيعِ الْمُعَيَّلِ

wa-wādin ka-jawfi al-ʿayri qafrin qaṭaʿtuhu / bihi al-dhiʾbu yaʿwī ka-al-khalīʿi al-muʿayyali

Translation:

“I crossed a deserted, uninhabited valley, like the hollow of an ass; there a wolf howled like an isolated, impoverished man with dependents to support.”

In the interpretation selected for this translation, al-khalīʿ is someone separated from ordinary social bonds and protection. This is not the only transmitted explanation: lexicographers also explain the word in this witness as a solitary hunter. Al-muʿayyal, moreover, does not merely mean having a family; Tāj al-ʿArūs explains it as someone with little property and dependents to maintain.

The solitary wolf in the wilderness is compared to such a person.

Lexical sources record the witness, but its wording, attribution, and presence in versions of the dīwān vary. It should therefore be treated as attributed evidence, rather than proof that this precise verse was composed at a particular point in Imruʾ al-Qays’s life. Tāj al-ʿArūs, for example, transmits it under Imruʾ al-Qays but also attributes it to Taʾabbaṭa Sharran, with jāwaztu baṭnahu in the first hemistich. The present witness is retained without assuming its attribution or wording to be certain.

Its value lies in showing khalīʿ as an isolated person in literary usage: in one explanation an outcast, in another a solitary hunter. The selected meaning must be presented alongside this difference of interpretation.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: disputed attribution, hunter and outcast readings, and muʿayyal

4. Extensions into government, covenants, and marriage

Removal from office

Arabic uses خُلِعَ الْوَالِي for a ruler or official removed from office.

A social or political position is pictured as something a person occupies and is then taken out of.

Khalʿ here describes ending someone’s relationship with an office; it does not itself judge the removal lawful or unlawful.

Ibn Fāris mentions a distinction between khalʿ and ʿazl within relationships of power. Word choice could thus depend on the political relationship and the parties’ relative standing.

This distinction should not be made absolute across all periods and constructions, since Arabic sources also record overlap between the words.

Dissolving a covenant

خَلَعَ الرِّبْقَةَ عَنْ عُنُقِهِ is also used for casting off a bond or obligation.

Ribqah originally concerns a device for binding and holding. Transferred to a covenant, it pictures the social relationship as a restraint connecting the parties.

Takhālaʿa al-qawmu is recorded for dissolving a pact or alliance among people.

Khalʿ can therefore concern separation from a previous commitment.

The root itself does not determine whether this is wrongful covenant-breaking or the legitimate ending of a relationship. That depends on the covenant, the reason for separation, and its circumstances.

Khalʿ in marital relationships

Khulʿ in marital separation is a familiar use of this family.

Dictionaries record خَالَعَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ زَوْجَهَا and اِخْتَلَعَتْ مِنْهُ for a woman requesting or obtaining separation from her husband by transferring money or compensation.

The marital bond is likened to something enveloping the parties; separation removes that bond.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab also invoke the clothing image in explaining the term.

In this excerpt from Al-Baqarah 2:187, the Qur’an says of wives and husbands:

هُنَّ لِبَاسٌ لَكُمْ وَأَنْتُمْ لِبَاسٌ لَهُنَّ

hunna libāsun lakum wa-antum libāsun lahunna

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “They are a garment for you, and you are a garment for them.” The installed verse translation remains unchanged.

This expression fits khalʿ’s lexical image: the spouses’ close relationship is described as a covering for each, and separation can be expressed as taking off that covering.

The term’s lexical explanation must nevertheless be distinguished from a direct Qur’anic ruling.

In this excerpt from Al-Baqarah 2:229, the Qur’an uses the following expression concerning marital separation:

فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا فِيمَا افْتَدَتْ بِهِ

fa-lā junāḥa ʿalayhimā fī-mā iftadat bihi

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “There is no blame on either of them concerning what she gives to obtain her release.” It must be read with the condition and full context of the verse; the installed translation remains unchanged.

The expression relates to certain features of what legal usage calls khulʿ, but the verse itself does not use that word.

All later rules, conditions, and legal classifications of khulʿ therefore cannot be extracted merely from خ ل ع or the Qur’an’s clothing metaphor.

The root illuminates separation from a former bond. Legal rulings and the conditions for ending marriage require independent examination of the relevant verses.

5. Other bodily and natural uses

Lexical sources also use the family for plants and fruit.

Khāliʿ, for instance, describes ripe dates whose skin separates from the flesh through moisture and ripeness.

Ibn Fāris explains this through the fruit shedding its covering.

Forms are also recorded for parts of a grain ear emerging during growth.

Lisān al-ʿArab records related forms for producing new leaves, shedding leaves, and different states of plant growth.

These are different instances: in one, peel separates from fruit; in another, leaves fall; elsewhere, a new part emerges from a plant.

What connects them with khalʿ’s general field is something emerging or separating from its previous condition or attachment.

Some of these meanings are preserved only in dictionaries, and evidence for when each circulated is uneven. They do not establish plant growth as the root’s original meaning.

Another early lexical use is al-khawlaʿ for intense fear or agitation, as though fear were so strong that the heart was torn from its place.

This is a figurative image of intense fear, showing how displacement from a natural position can describe psychological states.

Fear, however, is not a necessary component of khalʿ; it is one field to which the image was extended.

Maqāyīs, Lisān, and Tāj: fruit, plants, and fear

6. The Qur’anic use: Moses taking off his sandals

The root’s only Qur’anic occurrence is in Ṭā Hā:

إِنِّي أَنَا رَبُّكَ فَاخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ إِنَّكَ بِالْوَادِ الْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى

innī anā rabbuka fa-ikhlaʿ naʿlayka innaka bi-al-wādi al-muqaddasi ṭuwan

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“I am your Lord; so take off your sandals. You are in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā.”

Ṭā Hā 20:12

The verse occurs during Moses’s encounter with the divine call.

In Ṭā Hā 20:10, Moses sees a fire and tells his family that he may bring a burning brand from it or find guidance beside it.

In this excerpt from Ṭā Hā 20:11, when he reaches it, the call comes:

يَا مُوسَىٰ

yā mūsā

Proposed translation of this excerpt from Ṭā Hā 20:11: “O Moses!”

Then, in Ṭā Hā 20:12, The God identifies Himself to Moses and commands him to remove his sandals.

The construction and meaning of fa-ikhlaʿ

Fa-ikhlaʿ consists of two components: the particle fa- and the imperative ikhlaʿ.

Ikhlaʿ belongs to Form I khalaʿa–yakhlaʿu and commands a masculine singular addressee to remove something.

Its object is naʿlayka: your two sandals or two items of footwear.

Naʿlayka is grammatically dual and annexed to the second-person pronoun. Its accusative marker is yāʾ, while kāf identifies the owner. The dual nūn is omitted because of annexation.

The verb therefore concerns removing a specific item from the body, rather than deposing an official or dissolving a covenant.

The direct meaning is clear: Moses is commanded to take his sandals off his feet.

The command’s reason in context

This excerpt from Ṭā Hā 20:12 immediately follows the command:

إِنَّكَ بِالْوَادِ الْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى

innaka bi-al-wādi al-muqaddasi ṭuwan

Proposed translation of this excerpt: “You are in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā.”

This connection links removing the sandals to Moses’s presence in that place.

The valley is described as al-muqaddas. Al-Nāziʿāt 79:16 also names the place with الْوَادِ الْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى.

In the direct reading, Moses is in a particular place and receives a practical command in connection with its special character.

This agrees with khalʿ’s familiar use for taking off footwear.

The root itself, however, does not explain why the footwear must be removed. The reason must be examined through the command’s relationship to the setting and the place’s description.

Were Moses’s sandals impure?

Some early exegetical accounts say the sandals were made from the hide of a dead animal and that this prompted their removal.

Others identify direct contact between Moses’s feet and the sacred ground as the reason. Some understand respect and humility in that setting. Al-Ṭabarī prefers direct contact with the valley’s blessing and finds no sufficient evidence in the verse’s wording or an authoritative report for the sandals’ impurity. This exegetical preference is not the verb’s own meaning.

The verse does not identify the sandals’ material or state that they were impure.

What it explicitly states is the command to remove the sandals and Moses’s presence in the sacred valley.

The sandals’ impurity therefore cannot be established as the certain reason. Direct contact with the ground or a manner of showing respect may be examined contextually, but neither should replace the verb’s direct meaning.

The lexical meaning is clear; disagreement principally concerns the command’s reason and wisdom, rather than the act of removing footwear.

Al-Ṭabarī: competing reasons for the command and insufficient evidence for impurity

Can removing the sandals also have symbolic meaning?

In different languages and cultures, taking off clothing or footwear can accompany leaving a former condition, preparing to enter a new situation, or showing respect.

Some spiritual readings accordingly interpret Moses removing his sandals as leaving attachments or preparing to receive the divine message.

Such a reading may be examined as symbolic reflection, but it does not follow from ikhlaʿ alone.

The explicit object is naʿlayka, Moses’s sandals. A symbolic reading therefore does not displace the expression’s clear physical meaning.

The verse asks Moses to remove his sandals, rather than abandon his clothing, family, property, or all the bonds of his life.

The direct, reliable meaning of فَاخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ is thus taking off the sandals. Any further symbolic meaning requires independent indications.

A particular command or a general law?

The addressee is Moses in a specific situation.

The Qur’an relates the command to his presence in the sacred valley. This expression alone does not establish a universal, permanent rule to remove footwear in every sacred place or during every ṣalāh.

Establishing such a rule would require a broader address and evidence.

Lexically, khalaʿa al-naʿla describes only removing footwear. Its being obligatory elsewhere is not part of khalʿ’s lexical meaning.

7. Distinctions from nearby roots

KH L ʿ (khalʿ) compared with N Z ʿ (nazʿ)

The roots are close in many Arabic uses.

Nazʿ concerns pulling something out or separating it from its position or attachment.

Khalʿ can convey a similar meaning, but its familiar uses emphasize removing something worn on a body or occupying another position.

Both khalʿ al-libās and nazʿ al-libās are consequently intelligible for removing clothing.

Some lexicographers associate khalʿ with a more unhurried action than nazʿ, but others do not accept the distinction.

There can therefore be no absolute rule that nazʿ always involves force while khalʿ is always gentle.

KH L ʿ (khalʿ) compared with Q L ʿ (qalʿ)

Familiar uses of qalʿ concern uprooting or pulling something out, particularly from its place of settlement or root.

Uprooting a tree, for instance, can be expressed through qalʿ.

Taking off footwear or clothing need not involve this kind of uprooting.

Although both roots concern separation, ordinary physical khalʿ can mean removing a covering or attachment without uprooting the underlying thing from its natural place.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary, since the families approach one another in some uses.

KH L ʿ (khalʿ) compared with L B S (lubs)

One principal field of lubs concerns putting on and wearing clothing.

Khalʿ al-libās, conversely, concerns taking it off.

In ordinary clothing use, they describe opposite actions: clothing is put on, then taken off.

ل ب س has other Qur’anic uses, however, including mixing and obscuring the distinction between truth and falsehood.

The opposition between putting on and taking off should therefore not be extended to every use of the roots.

Study of L B S

KH L ʿ (khalʿ) compared with ʿ Z L (ʿazl)

ʿAzl concerns setting aside, separating, or removing something or someone from a particular position or activity.

In official positions, khalʿ and ʿazl can have the same result: the person no longer occupies the former office.

Khalʿ also carries the image of separation from a former attachment or covering, whereas familiar ʿazl emphasizes setting aside and removing from a position or group.

This does not establish an absolute boundary across all constructions. Historical and syntactic uses require individual examination.

KH L ʿ (khalʿ) compared with Ṭ L Q (ṭalq)

Ṭalq concerns release, freedom, and emerging from restraint. It also occurs in the marital field for ending the marriage bond.

Legal uses of khalʿ can likewise concern marital separation.

This overlap does not make the roots lexically identical.

Khalʿ’s principal field emphasizes separation from a former covering or attachment; many uses of ṭalq concern release and lifting restrictions.

Legal terminology can distinguish khulʿ and ṭalāq through who requests separation, compensation, and the relationship’s conditions. These features arise from legal conventions and rules, rather than all being extracted from root letters.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Natural equivalents for خ ل ع include removing, taking off, separating, and lifting off, depending on the object and context.

For clothing, taking off the body is more precise; for footwear, taking off the feet is natural. With a tether or restraint, removing the tether or releasing from restraint can convey the intended meaning.

Social uses may call for deposing, dissolving a bond, or separating from a relationship, as context requires.

The sole Qur’anic occurrence, however, does not require these extended meanings.

For this excerpt from Ṭā Hā 20:12:

فَاخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ

fa-ikhlaʿ naʿlayka

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed translation:

“Then take off your sandals.”

This preserves both the verb’s direct meaning and its explicit object.

“Take your sandals off your feet” is also clear, but “off your feet” is unnecessary for a natural translation because footwear itself identifies where it is ordinarily worn.

Brief root meaning

Removing or separating something from a covering, restraint, attachment, or bond previously connected with it.

Meaning in the verse

Fa-ikhlaʿ — Ṭā Hā 20:12: Then take your sandals off your feet.

These definitions must remain separate. The brief root meaning introduces the wider family of uses; the verse meaning is restricted to the specific command concerning Moses’s sandals.

Conclusion

Arabic خ ل ع concerns removing or separating something from a former covering, attachment, or bond.

This appears in removing footwear and clothing, lifting a restraint, dislocating a joint, and leaving a relationship or social position. Early Arabic witnesses also show uses in pastoral life, tribal relationships, and legal affairs.

Its sole Qur’anic form is fa-ikhlaʿ in Ṭā Hā 20:12. Moses is commanded to remove his sandals in connection with being in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā.

The direct meaning is clear, but the precise reason for the command, beyond its connection with the sacred setting, is not explained within the word. Reports about the sandals’ material and symbolic readings about abandoning attachments should not replace the explicit expression.

Khalʿ’s basic meaning can therefore be understood as removing and separating from a former attachment or bond, realized in the Qur’an as taking off footwear and extended in Arabic into bodily, social, and legal fields. Brief root meaning: Removing or separating something from a former covering, restraint, attachment, or bond.
