The Arabic root و ر د has several semantic branches. Its best-known branch concerns reaching a watering place, where people or animals go to obtain water. The broader sense of reaching a place or presenting oneself there is also established.

The Quran uses this root for Moses reaching Midian's water, Joseph's caravan sending its water-drawer, Pharaoh bringing his followers to the Fire, and people reaching Hell.

Two other branches also occur: وَرْدَة describes the sky's appearance when it splits, and الْوَرِيد denotes a blood vessel in the neck.

Not every derivative should be confined to “entering,” as the familiar Persian borrowing often suggests. Distinguish reaching a place, entering it, and remaining there. These may follow one another, but وَرَدَ does not necessarily express all three.

This distinction is central to understanding the passages about Hell.

1. The root in early Arabic

Reaching a watering place

Water access was essential for people and livestock in early Arabia. Caravans and herds went to wells, springs, and watering places to drink.

In this setting, وَرَدَ الْمَاءَ meant reaching water or arriving at its location.

A clear witness occurs in the Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā:

فَلَمَّا وَرَدْنَ الْمَاءَ زُرْقًا جِمَامُهُ

fa-lammā waradna al-māʾa zurqan jimāmuhu

وَضَعْنَ عِصِيَّ الْحَاضِرِ الْمُتَخَيِّمِ

waḍaʿna ʿiṣiyya al-ḥāḍiri al-mutakhayyimi

“When they reached the water, its pools clear and blue, they laid down their travelers' staffs like those who settle and pitch camp beside it.”

The poet describes a group of women traveling in a caravan, reaching water after their journey and stopping to camp.

وَرَدْنَ means that they reached the water.

Arrival is followed by settlement. The verb alone says neither that they drank nor that they entered the water itself.

They reached a destination with water; another expression then conveys their stopping and staying.

The line appears in familiar versions of Zuhayr's ode, with some differences of wording. وَرَدْنَ الْمَاءَ has a clear sense in the principal versions.

At least in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, وُرُود was thus used for reaching a watering place.

Reaching water and drinking it

Arabic dictionaries record وَرَدَ الْمَاءَ for coming to water, whether one subsequently drinks or has not yet done so.

الوِرْد can denote a watering turn, a group coming for water, or an allotted share of access to it.

مَوْرِد can name the place where water is reached or a route leading to it.

By contrast, returning from a watering place is expressed by ص د ر.

Two directions can therefore be distinguished in this vocabulary:

وُرُود: approaching and reaching water.

صُدُور: returning or leaving after reaching it.

This contrast also occurs in the Quran and helps clarify the root's historical usage.

Red coloring and the rose

Another witness from و ر د occurs in the same ode attributed to Zuhayr:

عَلَوْنَ بِأَنْماطٍ عِتاقٍ وَكِلَّةٍ

ʿalawna bi-anmāṭin ʿitāqin wa-killatin

وِرادٍ حَواشيها مُشاكِهَةُ الدَّمِ

wirādin ḥawāshīhā mushākihatu al-dami

“They raised fine coverings and curtains over the howdahs, their borders red and resembling blood.”

وِرَاد concerns red coloring here.

The poet compares the coverings' borders to blood. The subject is color, not reaching water.

Versions differ in some words, but lexical commentaries clearly relate وِرَاد to red coloring.

Arabic uses وَرْد for the familiar flower and for a color between red and yellow.

It also describes a horse of reddish coloring, potentially with a yellowish or brownish tone.

The color branch thus appears in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period as well.

Shared root letters do not establish that the flower's name or red color necessarily developed from reaching water.

Such a historical relationship requires independent evidence.

The blood-vessel branch

Classical Arabic uses وَرِيد for a blood vessel in the neck region.

The Quran also uses it.

This study has not established sufficient historical evidence to determine precisely which other image or sense, if any, gave rise to this anatomical name before Islam.

One might imagine a connection with blood flow or vessels linking bodily parts, but that alone is not proven historical etymology.

It is therefore useful to distinguish three attested branches:

Reaching and arriving, especially at water.

The rose and reddish coloring.

The vessel called وَرِيد.

2. Forms and semantic core

Important forms in this Arabic family include:

وَرَدَ: reached a place, arrived there, or came to water.

يَرِدُ: reaches or enters; the imperfect of وَرَدَ.

وُرُود: reaching, arriving, or coming to a destination.

وَارِد: someone reaching a place, especially water; in a caravan, someone sent to fetch water.

وِرْد: coming to water, a watering turn or share, or a group coming to a watering place. Some uses connect it with thirst.

مَوْرِد: a place where water is reached or a route leading to it; more broadly, a point of arrival or access.

أَوْرَدَ: brought someone or something to a destination, or caused them to enter it.

إِيرَاد: bringing or conveying something to a destination; later also used for presenting speech or information.

مَوْرُود: something or somewhere that is reached.

وَرْد: the rose; descriptively, red or reddish coloring.

وَرْدَة: one rose; also a feminine descriptive form associated with rosy coloring.

وَرِيد: a blood vessel in the neck region.

Other uses are recorded. For example, وِرْد can mean an appointed watering turn and, in later usage, a daily portion of recitation or remembrance.

That later technical sense should not be read back into the Quran, where وِرْد does not denote a daily recitation program.

Forms actually used in the Quran

The root occurs eleven times in nine verses, in seven morphological categories:

  • Basic verb: وَرَدَ and وَرَدُوهَا.
  • Form IV verb: فَأَوْرَدَهُمُ.
  • Noun وِرْد: الْوِرْدُ and وِرْدًا.
  • Active participle: وَارِدَهُمْ, وَارِدُهَا, and وَارِدُونَ.
  • Passive participle: الْمَوْرُودُ.
  • Noun وَرْدَة: وَرْدَةً.
  • Noun وَرِيد: الْوَرِيدِ.

Other forms such as مَوْرِد and اسْتَوْرَدَ, and the technical daily-recitation use of وِرْد, do not occur in the Quran.

Semantic core

In the principal verbal branch, the core is:

Reaching a destination or arriving at something, with a well-attested early use for reaching a watering place.

Arrival is central to this account.

Entering, drinking, using something, or staying may follow, but they are not necessary components of every use of the verb.

The color branch needs a separate description: the rose and rosy or reddish coloring.

وَرِيد is the attested name of a blood vessel.

These branches should not be forced into a watering-place image through an invented connection.

3. Reaching water in Quranic usage

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23 — Moses reaches Midian's water

وَلَمَّا وَرَدَ مَاءَ مَدْيَنَ وَجَدَ عَلَيْهِ أُمَّةً مِنَ النَّاسِ يَسْقُونَ وَوَجَدَ مِنْ دُونِهِمُ امْرَأَتَيْنِ تَذُودَانِ ۖ قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُمَا ۖ قَالَتَا لَا نَسْقِي حَتَّىٰ يُصْدِرَ الرِّعَاءُ ۖ وَأَبُونَا شَيْخٌ كَبِيرٌ

wa-lammā warada māʾa madyana wajada ʿalayhi ummatan mina al-nāsi yasqūna wa-wajada min dūnihimu imraʾatayni tadhūdāni qāla mā khaṭbukumā qālatā lā nasqī ḥattā yuṣdira al-riʿāʾu wa-abūnā shaykhun kabīr

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23

“When he reached Midian's water, he found a group of people watering, and apart from them two women holding back. He said: What is your situation? They said: We do not water until the shepherds drive [their flocks] away, and our father is an elderly man.”

وَرَدَ is a perfect verb from و ر د.

مَاءَ مَدْيَنَ is its object: Midian's water or watering place.

After traveling to Midian, Moses reaches a place where people and livestock have gathered for water.

The verse does not say that he entered the water itself.

After arriving, he sees people watering their animals and two women holding theirs back.

The passage clearly illustrates reaching a watering place without the verb itself asserting entry into the water.

The contrast between وَرَدَ and يُصْدِرَ

The women say in the same verse:

لَا نَسْقِي حَتَّىٰ يُصْدِرَ الرِّعَاءُ

lā nasqī ḥattā yuṣdira al-riʿāʾ

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23

“We do not water until the shepherds drive [their flocks] away.”

يُصْدِرَ belongs to ص د ر. In the quoted reading it is form IV, with an omitted object understood as the shepherds' livestock.

It concerns driving the animals away from the water. Another transmitted reading, يَصْدُرَ, is intransitive and describes the shepherds' returning.

The verse thus expresses two different directions.

Moses reaches the water: وَرَدَ.

The shepherds drive their livestock away after watering: يُصْدِرَ.

In this use, coming to water contrasts with leaving it or taking animals away from it.

This does not mean that every Quranic use of وَرَدَ involves literal water or promises a subsequent return.

Those features belong to the watering-place setting, not necessarily every extended use.

Yūsuf 12:19 — The caravan's water-drawer

وَجَاءَتْ سَيَّارَةٌ فَأَرْسَلُوا وَارِدَهُمْ فَأَدْلَىٰ دَلْوَهُ ۖ قَالَ يَا بُشْرَىٰ هَٰذَا غُلَامٌ ۚ وَأَسَرُّوهُ بِضَاعَةً ۚ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِمَا يَعْمَلُونَ

wa-jāʾat sayyāratun fa-arsalū wāridahum fa-adlā dalwahu qāla yā bushrā hādhā ghulāmun wa-asarrūhu biḍāʿatan wa-allāhu ʿalīmun bi-mā yaʿmalūn

Yūsuf 12:19

“A caravan came, and they sent their water-drawer, who lowered his bucket. He said: Good news! Here is a boy! They concealed him as merchandise, and The God knew what they were doing.”

وَارِدَهُمْ is an active participle with the pronoun هُمْ.

It names the person sent by the caravan to reach and obtain water.

“Their water-drawer” or “their water-fetcher” naturally conveys the contextual role.

The word does not itself mean owner of the well, water guard, or water seller.

The context defines his role: the caravan sends him, and he lowers his bucket into the well.

Here وَارِد is someone going to a watering place and gaining access to water.

This directly accords with the root's historical usage.

It names a caravan role, not a moral quality. The subsequent treatment of Joseph follows from other verbs and actions in the narrative.

4. Reaching Hell and arriving at a destination

In otherworldly contexts, the root's watering-place imagery extends to a different destination.

Here that destination is Hell or the Fire.

These passages raise an important translation question: does وُرُود mean reaching Hell, or necessarily entering it?

Each passage must be examined separately.

Hūd 11:98 — Pharaoh brings his people to the Fire

يَقْدُمُ قَوْمَهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فَأَوْرَدَهُمُ النَّارَ ۖ وَبِئْسَ الْوِرْدُ الْمَوْرُودُ

yaqdumu qawmahu yawma al-qiyāmati fa-awradahumu al-nāra wa-biʾsa al-wirdu al-mawrūd

Hūd 11:98

“He goes ahead of his people on the Day of Resurrection and brings them to the Fire. What an evil watering place to be brought to!”

Three forms from و ر د occur together:

«فَأَوْرَدَهُمُ»

«الْوِرْدُ»

«الْمَوْرُودُ»

Their combination has semantic and rhetorical significance.

فَأَوْرَدَهُمُ is form IV: Pharaoh brings his people to a destination.

That destination is النَّارَ, the Fire.

الْوِرْدُ evokes going to a watering place and the destination approached.

الْمَوْرُودُ is a passive participle: that which is reached.

An inverted watering-place image

Ordinarily, people or animals go to a watering place to obtain water.

Thirst or need draws them there.

Here Pharaoh brings his people to fire rather than water.

The contrast creates a powerful image.

The leader they followed takes them somewhere that, unlike water, offers no relief or deliverance.

Hūd 11:97 says that Pharaoh's people followed his command, though it was not rightly guided.

Hūd 11:98 then describes the outcome of that following.

أَوْرَدَ thus relates here to the responsibility of leader and followers.

The root itself does not mean misleading or punishing. Those implications follow from Pharaoh, his command, his people's following, and their stated destination.

بِئْسَ الْوِرْدُ الْمَوْرُودُ condemns that destination.

Retaining some watering-place imagery in translation or explanation helps preserve the effect of the three related forms.

Maryam 19:86 — Driving the guilty, thirsty, toward Hell

وَنَسُوقُ الْمُجْرِمِينَ إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ وِرْدًا

wa-nasūqu al-mujrimīna ilā jahannama wirdā

Maryam 19:86

“We drive the guilty toward Hell, thirsty.”

وِرْدًا is related to الْوِرْدُ in Hūd 11:98, but functions differently here.

Arabic can use وِرْد for a group coming to water, especially thirsty people or animals.

Hence “thirsty” is an established interpretation in this verse.

The underlying image is a group needing water and moving toward a watering place.

Here the guilty are driven toward Hell instead.

The imagery relates closely to Hūd 11:98.

There Pharaoh brings his people to the Fire; here the guilty are driven toward Hell like a thirsty group.

وِرْدًا alone does not specify the exact nature of thirst, otherworldly bodies, or the details of their movement.

The context depicts being driven toward Hell in a condition associated with thirst and a watering place.

The contrast with وَفْدًا

Maryam 19:85 describes the mindful being gathered to the Entirely Merciful as a delegation.

وَفْدًا belongs to و ف د.

Maryam 19:86 uses وِرْدًا of the guilty.

The words sound similar but have distinct roots and meanings.

وَفْد concerns a delegation presenting itself before someone.

وِرْد here evokes a thirsty group approaching water.

The paired images emphasize the different situations of the mindful and the guilty.

Rendering وِرْدًا merely as “a delegation” would erase this distinction.

Maryam 19:71 — Does reaching Hell necessarily mean entering it?

وَإِنْ مِنْكُمْ إِلَّا وَارِدُهَا ۚ كَانَ عَلَىٰ رَبِّكَ حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا

wa-in minkum illā wāriduhā kāna ʿalā rabbika ḥatman maqḍiyyā

Maryam 19:71

“There is none of you who will not reach it; this is an inevitable, determined decree with your Lord.”

وَارِدُهَا is an active participle with the feminine pronoun هَا.

The preceding context identifies the referent as Hell.

The central issue is reaching Hell.

Does this mean every addressee enters the Fire?

The lexical field must first be considered.

وَرَدَ can mean reaching a place, whether entry follows or not.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23 is a clear example: Moses reaches Midian's water, but the verb does not establish entry into the water.

Zuhayr's poetry likewise describes reaching water without the verb alone asserting drinking or entering it.

Lexically, وَارِدُهَا can therefore convey reaching Hell without necessarily specifying entry.

Conversely, it would be wrong to claim that the root never denotes entering. Some contexts involve reaching and entering a place.

Two principal readings therefore need examination:

Reaching or coming before Hell.

Entering Hell.

Both require contextual evaluation. Interpretive traditions also discuss passing over Hell; the physical details of that reading do not follow from the root alone.

Reading alongside Maryam 19:72

Maryam 19:72 says that The God saves those with taqwa and leaves the wrongdoers there on their knees.

It distinguishes two groups.

The mindful are saved.

The wrongdoers are left there.

فِيهَا matters because the wrongdoers are explicitly described as remaining in it.

For the mindful, the verb is نُنَجِّي, “We save.”

Reaching Hell and ultimately remaining there are therefore not one identical fate for everyone.

This contrast alone does not explain the full manner of rescue or the path and condition through which each person passes.

Reading alongside Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:101 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:101 says that those for whom good has already been ordained are kept far from Hell.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102 says they do not even hear its sound.

These passages matter when evaluating وَارِدُهَا.

A claim that everyone must enter Hell's fire needs to account for being kept far away and not hearing it. Proponents of entry have offered explanations distinguishing presence from suffering its punishment; the lexical argument alone does not settle the dispute.

Conversely, these verses alone do not establish precise routes or spatial distances from Hell.

This study proposes retaining the broad rendering “reach it” in Maryam 19:71 and examining each group's encounter through related verses. This is a translation choice, not proof that the entry reading is impossible.

“Will reach it” leaves further detail to the context; it should not be presented as a translation devoid of any interpretive choice.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98 — Reaching the Fire

إِنَّكُمْ وَمَا تَعْبُدُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ حَصَبُ جَهَنَّمَ أَنْتُمْ لَهَا وَارِدُونَ

innakum wa-mā taʿbudūna min dūni allāhi ḥaṣabu jahannama antum lahā wāridūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98

“You and what you worship apart from The God are fuel for Hell; you will reach it.”

وَارِدُونَ is a masculine plural active participle.

لَهَا identifies the destination.

The passage concerns a group reaching Hell together with the objects of its worship.

حَصَبُ جَهَنَّمَ further identifies their relation to the Fire.

Reaching Hell thus involves punishment in this context.

That follows from the whole sentence; وَارِدُونَ itself does not mean burning.

It should also be read alongside Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:101, which describes a group favored with good being kept far away.

The general language about false objects of worship should not override Quranic distinctions and conditions when considering beings whom others wrongfully worshipped.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:99 — Reaching Hell and remaining there

لَوْ كَانَ هَٰؤُلَاءِ آلِهَةً مَا وَرَدُوهَا وَكُلٌّ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

law kāna hāʾulāʾi ālihatan mā waradūhā wa-kullun fīhā khālidūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:99

“Had these been gods, they would not have reached it; all will remain therein.”

وَرَدُوهَا is a perfect verb with an object pronoun.

لَوْ introduces a conditional construction.

It is followed by وَكُلٌّ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ.

فِيهَا and خَالِدُونَ explicitly state remaining in Hell.

The outcome here is therefore more than reaching Hell's vicinity: presence and continued residence are expressed.

That conclusion follows from فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ, not from وَرَدُوهَا alone.

This distinction is crucial to studying the root.

The Quran uses additional words and constructions to express remaining in Hell. Arrival, entry, and enduring residence should not all be folded into a single lexical meaning.

5. Two other Quranic branches: color and the neck vessel

Al-Raḥmān 55:37 — A rosy sky

فَإِذَا انْشَقَّتِ السَّمَاءُ فَكَانَتْ وَرْدَةً كَالدِّهَانِ

fa-idhā inshaqqati al-samāʾu fa-kānat wardatan ka-al-dihān

Al-Raḥmān 55:37

“When the sky splits and becomes rosy, like oil.”

وَرْدَةً belongs to the branch associated with the rose and its coloring.

It is the predicate of كَانَتْ.

وَرْدَة can name a rose; here it describes the sky's appearance and is interpreted in relation to color.

Arabic dictionaries record وَرْد for reddish coloring, including descriptions of horses whose redness may carry yellowish or darker tones.

“Rosy” or “red-hued” can therefore render وَرْدَةً here.

The expression does not establish a literal transformation of the sky into a flower's physical shape.

The rose or its coloring supplies imagery.

The contribution of كَالدِّهَانِ

The verse also uses كَالدِّهَانِ.

This belongs to د ه ن, associated with oil and oily substances.

الدِّهَان is therefore not part of و ر د.

Rosy coloring combined with resemblance to oil portrays a transformed appearance of the sky.

Lexical and exegetical accounts discuss oil, sheen, changing colors, and, in some interpretations, colored leather in explaining الدِّهَان.

The verse does not specify the sky's chemical composition or a physical mechanism for its splitting.

It should not be restricted to a purportedly certain theory about the sky turning into oil, molten metal, or a particular scientific phenomenon.

Did وَرْدَة develop from coming to water?

Shared root letters alone do not establish that conclusion.

Early poetry attests both reaching water and red coloring.

Their coexistence does not establish the historical direction of derivation.

A relationship may have existed in the language's distant history, but it should not be invented without independent evidence.

Explain وَرْدَة through attested flower and color uses, not through a hypothetical watering-place image.

Qāf 50:16 — The neck vessel

وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ وَنَعْلَمُ مَا تُوَسْوِسُ بِهِ نَفْسُهُ ۖ وَنَحْنُ أَقْرَبُ إِلَيْهِ مِنْ حَبْلِ الْوَرِيدِ

wa-laqad khalaqnā al-insāna wa-naʿlamu mā tuwaswisu bihi nafsuhu wa-naḥnu aqrabu ilayhi min ḥabli al-warīd

Qāf 50:16

“We created the human being and know what his own self whispers to him; We are nearer to him than his neck vein.”

الْوَرِيدِ names a blood vessel in the neck region.

حَبْل relates to its cordlike shape.

حَبْلِ الْوَرِيدِ thus refers to a neck vessel, something intimately close to a person and part of the body itself.

This differs semantically from reaching water and rosy coloring.

Modern medical language uses “vein” for vessels carrying blood toward the heart. That entire modern classification should not be imposed on the historical word الْوَرِيد in this verse.

The verse names a familiar bodily vessel.

Divine nearness and the neck-vessel image

The context concerns human creation and The God's knowledge of what occurs within a person.

After mentioning that knowledge, the verse compares divine nearness with the neck vessel.

The image conveys that nothing hidden within a human being escapes divine knowledge.

It should not make The God a physical object at a measurable distance from bodily organs.

The neck vessel offers an intelligible image of extreme nearness.

That nearness appears in a context of divine knowledge of the human self, not as a determination of The God's physical location.

وَرِيد itself does not mean knowledge, awareness, or divine presence. Those ideas follow from the complete sentence.

The anatomical name's historical relation to other branches

A connection between the vessel's name and blood flow, movement, or arrival can be imagined.

Shared letters alone cannot establish that relationship conclusively.

Its attested anatomical meaning is sufficient to understand Qāf 50:16.

There is no need to claim that blood arrives like a caravan at water, or that the vessel was named for supplying water to the body, merely to unite every derivative.

Without historical evidence, such images are not lexical definitions.

6. Neighboring roots and translation challenges

و ر د compared with د خ ل

This comparison is especially important in passages about Hell.

دَخَلَ concerns entering a place or bounded area.

The principal branch of وَرَدَ concerns reaching a destination or arriving there.

A person may reach a place and then enter it.

Arrival alone does not establish entry.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23, Moses reaches Midian's water, but the verb does not say he entered the water.

In Maryam 19:71, وَارِدُهَا can lexically express reaching Hell without independently determining everyone's manner of entry.

By contrast, entry is prominent in دُخُول.

Do not translate the two identically in every passage without examining context.

و ر د compared with ب ل غ

ب ل غ concerns reaching a limit, destination, or stage.

It is close to و ر د in certain uses.

Its field is broader: reaching an age, time, measure, person addressed by a message, or destination.

وَرَدَ is particularly associated with coming to a place, especially water.

“Reach” can therefore render both verbs, although their full ranges differ.

و ر د compared with أ ت ي

أَتَى concerns coming or arriving at a person, place, or condition.

وَرَدَ can also express reaching a place.

Its early Arabic uses have a marked connection with water and a destination toward which one travels.

That background can also be recognized in Quranic images of Hell.

This does not make every Quranic instance of arrival an expression of thirst.

وِرْدًا in Maryam 19:86 emphasizes the thirsty-group image; وَارِدُهَا in Maryam 19:71 does not itself supply that description.

و ر د compared with ص د ر

At the watering place, the roots describe different directions.

وَرَدَ expresses coming to water.

صَدَرَ, in this setting, expresses returning from it.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23 clearly illustrates the contrast through وَرَدَ and the causative يُصْدِرَ.

Other branches of ص د ر include the chest and coming forward or issuing forth.

The contrast therefore applies specifically to approaching water and leaving it, not to every use of either family.

و ر د compared with س ق ي

سَقَى concerns giving water and quenching thirst.

وَرَدَ concerns reaching water.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23, Moses arrives and sees people watering.

The two verbs express different actions.

Reaching water does not itself establish drinking or giving another water.

Giving water may take place after arrival.

The distinction also matters in otherworldly imagery: reaching a destination does not necessarily mean drinking there.

و ر د compared with ر و د

The roots may be confused because wāw and rā occur in reversed order.

ر و د is an independent family containing أَرَادَ, يُرِيدُ, and رَاوَدَ.

It concerns wanting, intending, and, in certain constructions, pursuing a desired end.

The principal branch of و ر د concerns arriving at a destination.

Wanting a destination and reaching it are different actions.

أَرَادَ and وَرَدَ should not be conflated through superficial similarity.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

A single equivalent cannot cover all derivatives. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

Water-related forms

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23:

“When he reached Midian's water…”

“Reached” is more precise than “entered” here, since Moses arrives at the watering place and observes those present.

In Yūsuf 12:19:

“They sent their water-drawer…”

“Water-drawer” or “water-fetcher” is more natural than “their arriving person” and identifies the role within the caravan.

Forms concerning Hell

In Hūd 11:98:

“He goes ahead of his people on the Day of Resurrection and brings them to the Fire. What an evil watering place to be brought to!”

“Led them to the Fire” is also possible. The explanation should retain the destination and watering-place imagery.

In Maryam 19:86:

“We drive the guilty toward Hell, thirsty.”

“Thirsty” conveys the image of a group coming to water.

In Maryam 19:71:

“There is none of you who will not reach it; this is an inevitable, determined decree with your Lord.”

“Will reach it” is this study's proposed base rendering. It leaves further detail to the context without claiming to eliminate the interpretive dispute about entry.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98:

“You will reach it.”

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:99:

“Had these been gods, they would not have reached it; all will remain therein.”

The second clause independently expresses remaining in Hell.

The color form

In Al-Raḥmān 55:37:

“When the sky splits and becomes rosy, like oil.”

“Rosy” naturally conveys وَرْدَةً in this context.

“Red-hued” is also suitable.

“Turned into a flower” would restrict the imagery to an unestablished physical transformation.

The anatomical form

In Qāf 50:16:

“We are nearer to him than his neck vein.”

For Persian readers, “neck vessel” is clearer than an unexplained specialist term. English “neck vein” likewise conveys the familiar image without requiring a precise modern anatomical identification.

The root study can explain that الْوَرِيد names a vessel in the neck region.

Practical equivalents

Preserve these distinctions in future translations:

وَرَدَ: reached, came to a destination, or arrived there.

وَارِد: someone arriving; in a water context, a water-fetcher or someone coming for water.

أَوْرَدَ: brought, led to a destination, or caused to enter.

وِرْد: a watering turn, people coming for water, or a thirsty group in an appropriate context.

مَوْرُود: something or somewhere that is reached.

وَرْدَة: a rose, or descriptively rosy or red-hued.

وَرِيد: a blood vessel; in Qāf 50:16, the neck vessel.

Fit these equivalents to each sentence. The aim is to retain real distinctions rather than force every form into one repeated phrase.

8. Conclusion

و ر د has several Arabic semantic branches.

Its principal verbal branch concerns arrival, with a clear early connection to reaching water.

Poetry attributed to Zuhayr describes a caravan's arrival with وَرَدْنَ الْمَاءَ, without making drinking or entering the water necessary components of the verb.

The Quran uses the same field when Moses reaches Midian's water and Joseph's caravan sends its water-drawer.

In Hell passages, arrival remains relevant, while the destination changes from water to fire.

Hūd 11:98 makes this a forceful image: Pharaoh brings his people to fire instead of water.

Maryam 19:86 describes the guilty driven thirsty toward Hell.

In Maryam 19:71, وَارِدُهَا concerns reaching Hell; the word alone does not determine the full manner of entry. Related verses must inform consideration of the different groups.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:99 separately expresses remaining in Hell through فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ. Enduring residence should not be built into وَرَدَ itself.

Two further branches occur in the Quran.

وَرْدَةً in Al-Raḥmān 55:37 concerns the sky's rosy appearance.

الْوَرِيدِ in Qāf 50:16 names a neck vessel.

Early language also supports the red-color branch, but a certain common historical origin for every branch is not established.

The Quranic meanings can be summarized as follows:

1. Arrival: coming to a destination, especially water; more broadly, reaching a place, without entry or continued residence necessarily being part of every use.

2. Color: the rose and rosy or reddish coloring.

3. Anatomy: وَرِيد as the name of a vessel in the neck region.

The chief translation lesson is to distinguish reaching, entering, and remaining.

The modern Persian borrowing commonly suggests entering. That Persian sense should not be imposed on every Arabic use without examination.

For Forqan, this study proposes “reaching a destination” as the broad starting point for the verbal branch. Entry, drinking, stopping, or continued residence should be expressed where the Quranic context supports them; this does not deny that وَرَدَ can contextually mean entering.
