Shackles, hidden rancor, breach of trust, and secret misappropriation

The Arabic root غ ل ل has several branches, distinguished especially by their vowels. غُلّ denotes a shackle placed around a person's neck or hands to restrict movement. غِلّ denotes rancor, hostility, and ill will concealed in the heart. غُلُول denotes betrayal of a trust and secretly taking something one has no right to possess, particularly, in early usage, taking from shared property or spoils.

Arabic sources also record غِلّة for income or the yield of property or land, غُلّة for intense thirst, and غِلالة for an undergarment or something occupying an inner layer.

Some of these meanings share imagery. Shackles restrict movement; rancor can remain hidden in the heart; betrayal of a trust may involve secretly taking property. Such similarities alone, however, do not establish that all branches developed from one original historical meaning.

The root occurs sixteen times in the Quran. Its uses concern betrayal of trust, shackles, stinginess and restricted giving, rancor in the heart, and release from burdensome bonds.

Understanding this root therefore requires distinguishing physical restraints, the inward condition of rancor, and the ethical conduct of betrayal. They belong to one lexical family but are not interchangeable in translation or reflection.

1. The root in early Arabic

غُلّ: a neck shackle and restricted movement

With a ḍamma on the first letter, غُلّ denotes a restraint placed around someone's neck or hands. It may be made of iron or another material.

In its familiar use, a person's hands are bound to the neck, or a restraint around the neck restricts movement and freedom.

For example, a captive restrained at the neck and hands cannot move or use the hands as a free person can.

Arabic calls this implement غُلّ; its plural is أَغْلَال.

Here the word does not denote just any rope or cord, but an implement used to bind and restrain a person.

Lexicons also describe leather shackles that caused discomfort through prolonged contact with the body and accumulated dirt.

This usage establishes a concrete sense: an actual implement for binding and restricting a person. It does not, by itself, establish the historical order of all the root's senses.

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry: freeing a captive in Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

فَيَا رُبَّ مَكْرُوبٍ كَرَرْتُ وَرَاءَهُ

fa-yā rubba makrūbin karartu warāʾahu

وَعَانٍ فَكَكْتُ الْغُلَّ عَنْهُ فَفَدَّانِي

wa-ʿānin fakaktu al-ghulla ʿanhu fa-faddānī

Translation:

“How many a distressed man I turned back to help, and how many a captive whose shackle I released, who then declared himself my ransom!”

عَانٍ here means a captive or prisoner.

الْغُلّ is the restraint placed upon him.

فَكَكْتُ belongs to the independent root ف ك ك and concerns undoing and releasing.

The poet speaks of freeing a captive from his bonds.

The verse brings together two distinct conditions: being held in a shackle and being freed by undoing it.

Here the shackle is neither rancor nor financial betrayal. It is a physical implement restricting the captive's freedom.

The verse is recorded in the collection attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, but its precise date has not been independently established here. It witnesses the physical meaning in a tradition attributed to pre-Islamic poetry, not the exact date when the word originated.

غِلّ: hidden rancor and hostility

With a kasra on the first letter, غِلّ denotes rancor, ill will, and inward hostility.

Lexicons associate it with حقد, ضغن, عداوت, and, in some uses, envy.

Two people may behave normally toward each other outwardly while one secretly resents the other and dislikes the other's success or comfort.

Here the word denotes an inward condition that can affect one's outlook and conduct, rather than a physical shackle.

Not every discomfort, disagreement, or passing hurt should be called غِلّ.

Someone hurt by another's misconduct may complain or seek restitution. Seeking justice does not itself establish inward rancor or ill will.

In ethical usage, the term primarily concerns hostility or resentment lodged within a person.

This meaning is clear in the Quran. Its earliest appearance in pre-Islamic Arabic would require separately dated evidence; this article does not establish that chronology.

غُلُول: betrayal and secret misappropriation

غَلَّ يَغُلُّ غُلُولًا denotes betraying a trust and taking something secretly.

In its familiar military use, غلول concerns secretly taking shared property or spoils before distribution, disregarding others' rights.

A group may acquire property after a battle that must be distributed according to established rights and rules.

If someone secretly takes part before distribution, ignoring others' shares, that conduct is غلول.

The issue is not simply taking property. The person betrays a trust and the rights of others who share in it.

Some early lexicographers define the term specifically in relation to spoils. Others describe a broader field of secret betrayal involving property or a trust.

This distinction matters: the use concerning spoils is well established, but betrayal of trust should not be restricted to that instance without evidence.

An early witness: betrayal of a trust

A poem attributed to al-Namir ibn Tawlab says:

جَزَى اللَّهُ عَنَّا حَمْزَةَ ابْنَةَ نَوْفَلٍ

jazā Allāhu ʿannā Ḥamzata ibnata Nawfalin

جَزَاءَ مُغِلٍّ بِالْأَمَانَةِ كَاذِبِ

jazāʾa mughillin bi-l-amānati kādhibi

Translation:

“May God repay Ḥamza, daughter of Nawfal, on our behalf with the recompense of a liar who betrays a trust.”

Here مُغِلّ occurs alongside trust and lying. The name is also transmitted as Jamra in other sources; Ḥamza is the reading quoted in some lexicons.

The subject is not taking war spoils, but betrayal within a relationship where fidelity and honesty are expected.

The witness shows that the betrayal branch of غ ل ل can concern violation of a trust and confidence.

Al-Namir ibn Tawlab lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. Without dating this particular verse, it cannot be called a definitely pre-Quranic witness.

مُغِلّ here is understood as a form of أَغَلَّ, whereas the Quranic يَغُلَّ belongs to the basic triliteral verb. Their betrayal senses overlap, but their morphological forms differ.

غِلّة: income and the yield of property

Another branch is غِلّة, income or yield from land, a house, or property.

The Muʿallaqa attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma says:

فَتُغْلِلْ لَكُمْ مَا لَا تُغِلُّ لِأَهْلِهَا

fa-tughlil lakum mā lā tughillu li-ahlihā

قُرًى بِالْعِرَاقِ مِنْ قَفِيزٍ وَدِرْهَمِ

quran bi-l-ʿirāqi min qafīzin wa-dirhami

Translation:

“Then war will yield you a harvest such as Iraqi villages do not yield their owners in measures of produce and dirhams.”

The verse continues a description of war's destructive consequences.

With bitter irony, the poet says war will bring a yield, but its yield is loss, bloodshed, and calamity rather than the produce and money of prosperous villages.

تُغِلّ here concerns producing a yield or income.

No physical shackle or rancor is intended in this use.

This famous poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period attests the income and yield branch in early Arabic.

Sharing a root does not itself prove that the income sense derives from neck shackles or hidden rancor.

2. Semantic branches and important forms

Three related forms with three distinct meanings

Vowel differences are especially important in this family.

غُلّ: a shackle restraining the hands or neck.

غِلّ: rancor, hostility, and ill will hidden in the heart.

غُلُول: betrayal of a trust and secretly appropriating others' property or rights.

These forms share the same or related letters but have distinct meanings in Quranic usage.

When the Quran speaks of غِلٍّ in human breasts, it should not be translated as an iron chain.

When it speaks of أَغْلَال around necks, the familiar meaning is neck shackles or bonds.

When discussing غُلُول as a lexical term, the subject is betrayal and secret appropriation; that verbal noun itself does not occur in the Quran.

Preserving these distinctions is essential to understanding the root's uses.

Other branches in Arabic

Lexicons record further meanings in this family.

غُلّة and غَلِيل: intense thirst and inward heat felt by a person or animal. In some uses, غليل also denotes an inward burning associated with anger, grief, or attachment.

غِلالة: a garment worn underneath clothing or armor.

غَلَل: in some uses, water flowing among tree roots or something remaining within another thing.

غَلّ الشيءَ: in one lexical use, inserting something into another thing.

غِلّة: yield or income obtained from property or land.

These branches show the family's historical and semantic breadth.

An imagistic connection may be perceived between rancor hidden in the heart, something entering another thing, and property taken secretly.

The connection between a neck shackle and restricted freedom is also clear.

This does not justify inventing an overarching meaning such as “any entry, remaining, or restriction.” Such a formula is broad enough to cover many unrelated roots.

Preserving attested branches is more precise than imposing an unsupported unity on them.

Quranic forms and derivatives

The following forms occur in the Quran:

  • يَغُلَّ، يَغْلُلْ، غَلَّ: basic triliteral verb forms meaning betrayal and secret appropriation.
  • غُلَّتْ: a passive past form concerning hands being bound.
  • فَغُلُّوهُ: a plural imperative commanding that someone be shackled.
  • غِلّ: inward rancor and ill will.
  • أَغْلَال: shackles, the plural of غُلّ; the singular noun does not itself occur in the Quran.
  • مَغْلُولَة: a feminine passive participle meaning bound or shackled.

غَلَّ as betrayal, غِلّ as rancor, and أَغْلَال as shackles occur in distinct Quranic contexts.

Forms such as غَلِيل, غِلّة, غُلالة, غِلالة, the independent verbal noun غُلُول, and تَغَلْغُل do not occur in the Quran, though they are known in Arabic.

Quranic study should therefore center on the derivatives actually used in the text.

3. وَمَا كَانَ لِنَبِيٍّ أَنْ يَغُلَّ: betrayal of a trust

Al Imran 3:161

وَمَا كَانَ لِنَبِيٍّ أَنْ يَغُلَّ وَمَنْ يَغْلُلْ يَأْتِ بِمَا غَلَّ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ ثُمَّ تُوَفَّىٰ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَا كَسَبَتْ وَهُمْ لَا يُظْلَمُونَ

wa-mā kāna li-nabiyyin an yaghulla wa-man yaghlul yaʾti bi-mā ghalla yawma al-qiyāmati thumma tuwaffā kullu nafsin mā kasabat wa-hum lā yuẓlamūn

Āl ʿImrān 3:161

“It is not fitting for a Prophet to betray a trust. Whoever betrays a trust will bring what he misappropriated on the Day of Resurrection. Then every soul will be repaid in full for what it earned, and they will not be wronged.”

Three forms of غَلَّ occur in this verse.

يَغُلَّ is an imperfect verb in the subjunctive after أَنْ.

يَغْلُلْ is a jussive imperfect in the conditional construction وَمَنْ يَغْلُلْ.

غَلَّ is a past verb referring to that same act of betrayal.

All three concern betrayal, not binding hands and necks or harboring rancor.

Betrayal involving what?

Lexicons and reports concerning this verse connect غُلُول particularly with secret appropriation of spoils.

The preceding context of the surah also concerns battle, victory, defeat, and believers' responsibility.

The use of يَغُلَّ concerning trustworthiness with property and spoils is therefore readily understandable.

Yet وَمَا كَانَ لِنَبِيٍّ أَنْ يَغُلَّ does not name a particular item of property.

The basic translation “betray a trust” preserves the central meaning. An explanation can clarify the familiar use concerning secret appropriation of spoils or entrusted property.

Betrayal as distinct from taking property

Suppose someone is responsible for safeguarding property belonging to a group.

That person may safeguard, count, or distribute it under the applicable rules, but has no right secretly to take a portion.

Doing so violates the confidence and trust placed in that person.

The problem is not merely a transfer of property; it is betrayal of a right that should have been respected.

For example, someone distributing social assistance might secretly take part before it reaches those in need.

This conduct involves betrayal of trust even though the property is not war spoils.

The example can clarify the semantic field for a modern reader. It does not mean the verse directly discusses modern administrative institutions.

وَمَنْ يَغْلُلْ يَأْتِ بِمَا غَلَّ

This clause extends responsibility to everyone who commits that conduct.

مَنْ in the conditional construction identifies whoever performs the act.

The verse says that whoever betrays a trust will face what was misappropriated on the Day of Resurrection.

Secrecy in this world therefore does not put the act beyond divine accounting.

Someone might conceal property so effectively that nobody discovers the appropriation.

In the Quran's account, people's inability to discover the betrayal does not eliminate moral responsibility or accountability.

The verse continues that every soul receives in full the outcome of its deeds, without anyone being wronged.

This is relevant to غلول: secret appropriation may escape human discovery but does not remain hidden from divine accounting.

The readings يَغُلَّ and يُغَلَّ

In the Hafs reading, أَنْ يَغُلَّ is active: it is not fitting for a Prophet to betray a trust.

Other recognized readings have the passive أَنْ يُغَلَّ.

Two lexical explanations of that form are offered.

One is that the Prophet must not be betrayed, meaning that others must not betray a trust involving his property or the property in his charge.

The other is that the Prophet must not be accused of betrayal.

These explanations differ and should not be collapsed into one translation without examination.

The reading difference changes the direction in which the action is attributed, while the core field remains betrayal and trustworthiness.

For Forqan's translation based on Hafs, “It is not fitting for a Prophet to betray a trust” is a direct and appropriate rendering.

4. غُلّ and أَغْلَال: shackles, bonds, and restriction

A bound hand and inability to give

Isra 17:29

وَلَا تَجْعَلْ يَدَكَ مَغْلُولَةً إِلَىٰ عُنُقِكَ وَلَا تَبْسُطْهَا كُلَّ الْبَسْطِ فَتَقْعُدَ مَلُومًا مَحْسُورًا

wa-lā tajʿal yadaka maghlūlatan ilā ʿunuqika wa-lā tabsuṭhā kulla al-basṭi fa-taqʿuda malūman maḥsūrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:29

“Do not keep your hand bound to your neck, nor extend it to its fullest extent, leaving you sitting reproached and exhausted.”

مَغْلُولَة belongs to غ ل ل and means bound.

The image is clear: a hand bound to the neck cannot open to give.

The opposite extreme, extending it without limit, is also prohibited.

Together, the images concern spending and giving.

The bound hand depicts stinginess and withholding.

Unrestricted extension can depict unmeasured giving or expenditure that leaves one destitute.

مغلولة should not simply be replaced by “stingy” as its lexical meaning. It means bound; stinginess is the contextual force of the image.

Why is the hand bound to the neck?

Binding a person's hand to the neck severely restricts its movement.

It cannot give to someone else as a free hand can.

The Quran employs this physical image to describe economic conduct.

Someone may possess ample wealth yet refuse to give even when relatives or others plainly need help.

The hand is not physically bound, but the conduct resembles an inability to open it in assistance.

Another person may spend so recklessly as to become unable to meet essential personal and family needs.

The verse prohibits both conditions.

The shackle image concerns binding and restricting the hand; moderation in spending and giving follows from the whole sentence.

يَدُ اللَّهِ مَغْلُولَةٌ

In Maidah 5:64, the Quran quotes those who said that God's hand was bound.

The verse rejects that claim, speaking instead of His outstretched hands and His giving as He wills.

In this context, the bound hand points to restricted generosity or narrowness in giving.

The expression should not be treated as a physical description of God.

As the bound hand in Isra 17:29 depicts withholding, Maidah 5:64 concerns giving and generosity.

The Quran rejects the claim and says that God gives as He wills.

The response غُلَّتْ أَيْدِيهِمْ also occurs there as a rebuke to their statement. It does not itself report that every member of a people has physically bound hands throughout history.

One must distinguish a group's quoted claim, the Quran's response, and judgments about every individual bearing a religious affiliation.

Maidah itself does not describe all People of the Book as identical in conduct.

Removing الْأَغْلَال from them

Araf 7:157 describes a Messenger and Prophet who removes the heavy burden and shackles that were upon the people.

أَغْلَال is the plural of غُلّ and directly means shackles.

Its association with إِصْر and lifting heavy burdens provides a context for understanding restrictions weighing on people's lives and responsibilities.

Rules or obligations may be imposed so heavily that they make life and the fulfillment of proper responsibilities difficult.

Removing such burdens can be explained through the image of opening shackles and releasing someone from bonds.

أغلال alone does not name a particular law or rule. Identifying the restrictions intended requires examining the context and related verses.

Nor does the expression make every law or responsibility a shackle. Some obligations are necessary to preserve rights and justice; the verse concerns the burdens and restrictions it identifies.

5. Shackles in scenes of punishment and accountability

Several verses use أَغْلَال to describe those facing punishment and accountability. The image in Ya Sin 36:8 also has an interpretation concerning their present resistance to guidance.

Rad 13:5: shackles around necks

After reporting the astonishment of those denying resurrection, the verse speaks of shackles around their necks.

The neck restraint becomes associated with the fate of those resisting the truth of resurrection.

The word أَغْلَال alone does not establish the material or physical mechanism of such restraints in the Hereafter.

Its lexical meaning is a shackle or restraining implement. How it is realized in the Hereafter is a separate question.

Saba 34:33: shackles and responsibility for deeds

This verse describes placing shackles around the necks of those who disbelieved.

It then asks whether they are recompensed for anything other than what they did.

The image of shackles thus accompanies responsibility for deeds.

The punishment is related to human conduct, rather than an unfounded attribution or punishment without accounting.

The root غ ل ل itself does not mean just punishment. That conclusion comes from the whole verse and the relation between action and recompense.

Ya Sin 36:8: shackles and restricted movement

Ya Sin 36:8

إِنَّا جَعَلْنَا فِي أَعْنَاقِهِمْ أَغْلَالًا فَهِيَ إِلَى الْأَذْقَانِ فَهُمْ مُقْمَحُونَ

innā jaʿalnā fī aʿnāqihim aghlālan fa-hiya ilā al-adhqāni fa-hum muqmaḥūn

Yā Sīn 36:8

“We have placed shackles around their necks, reaching to their chins, so their heads are held up.”

The shackle creates a clear image of physical restriction.

The restraints surround the necks and reach to the chins.

The heads are consequently held in a restricted position.

مُقْمَحُون belongs to another root and describes heads held upward in a position restricting natural movement.

Its full meaning should not be transferred into أَغْلَال itself.

Nearby verses discuss those unaffected by warning and resistant to guidance.

The shackle image can thus also relate to restriction and inability to turn toward the truth.

That contextual reading should not erase the verse's image of restraints.

Conversely, no definite theory of their physical construction or bodily effects should be asserted without independent evidence.

Ghafir 40:71: shackles and chains

Here الْأَغْلَالُ appears alongside السَّلَاسِلُ.

أغلال denotes restraints placed around the neck.

سلاسل denotes chains.

The words share a semantic field but need not name the same implement.

A shackle may bind the neck or hands, while a chain connects or drags the person.

Both appear in the description of punishment.

They should therefore not be treated as perfectly interchangeable without examination.

Haqqah 69:30: the command to shackle

Haqqah 69:30

خُذُوهُ فَغُلُّوهُ

khudhūhu fa-ghullūhu

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:30

“Seize him, then shackle him.”

فَغُلُّوهُ is a plural imperative from غ ل ل.

خُذُوهُ commands seizing the person.

فَغُلُّوهُ commands shackling him.

The two actions are linguistically distinct.

Someone can be seized without yet being shackled.

After seizure, the verse commands binding and restricting the person.

غُلُّوهُ therefore means more than taking or arresting: it means shackling.

The following verses mention fire and a chain. They continue the punishment scene, but their details are not the lexical meaning of غلوه itself.

Insan 76:4: chains and shackles

Here سَلَاسِلَ and أَغْلَالًا occur alongside سَعِيرًا.

سلاسل means chains.

أغلال means shackles or restraints.

سعير means blazing fire.

The three words identify three elements of the punishment scene.

Sharing a general subject does not make their lexical meanings identical.

These uses emphasize being restrained or held in bonds. The root alone does not determine the precise bodily nature of punishment in the Hereafter.

6. غِلّ: rancor remaining in the heart

Araf 7:43: removal of rancor

God speaks of removing rancor from the breasts of the inhabitants of the Garden.

غِلّ here means neither a neck shackle nor secretly taking property.

It concerns rancor and inward hostility.

Two people may have experienced disagreements and hurt, with traces lingering after the dispute ends.

That condition can affect their relationship.

The verse speaks of God's removal of whatever rancor remains in the breasts of the Garden's inhabitants.

Their life is thus described through more than outward blessings: relationships purified of rancor and hostility are also part of it.

The verse does not require that every inhabitant previously harbored rancor toward the others.

Its expression concerns removing whatever rancor may be present.

Hijr 15:47: brotherhood without rancor

Hijr 15:47

وَنَزَعْنَا مَا فِي صُدُورِهِمْ مِنْ غِلٍّ إِخْوَانًا عَلَىٰ سُرُرٍ مُتَقَابِلِينَ

wa-nazaʿnā mā fī ṣudūrihim min ghillin ikhwānan ʿalā sururin mutaqābilīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:47

“We removed whatever rancor was in their breasts; as brothers, they face one another upon couches.”

Here غِلّ occurs with صُدُور, breasts.

نَزَعْنَا belongs to the independent root ن ز ع and concerns taking out or uprooting.

غِلّ is not the act of removal; it is what is removed from their breasts.

The verse continues with إِخْوَانًا and مُتَقَابِلِينَ.

These expressions portray brotherhood and a peaceful, close relationship among the Garden's inhabitants.

Their connection suggests that rancor and ill will conflict with peaceful, brotherly relations.

It does not mean that every disagreement necessarily amounts to rancor.

Two people can disagree in interpretation while wishing each other well and respecting each other's rights.

Inward rancor is therefore distinct from scholarly disagreement, criticism, and seeking justice.

Hashr 59:10: asking for hearts free of rancor

Hashr 59:10

وَالَّذِينَ جَاءُوا مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ يَقُولُونَ رَبَّنَا اغْفِرْ لَنَا وَلِإِخْوَانِنَا الَّذِينَ سَبَقُونَا بِالْإِيمَانِ وَلَا تَجْعَلْ فِي قُلُوبِنَا غِلًّا لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا رَبَّنَا إِنَّكَ رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ

wa-alladhīna jāʾū min baʿdihim yaqūlūna rabbanā ighfir lanā wa-li-ikhwāninā alladhīna sabaqūnā bi-l-īmāni wa-lā tajʿal fī qulūbinā ghillan li-lladhīna āmanū rabbanā innaka raʾūfun raḥīm

Al-Ḥashr 59:10

“Those who came after them say: Our Lord, forgive us and our brothers who preceded us in faith, and place no rancor in our hearts toward those who believe. Our Lord, You are Kind and Merciful.”

غِلًّا here concerns human hearts.

Those following the earlier believers seek forgiveness for themselves and their predecessors, asking God to keep their hearts free of rancor toward believers.

The prayer shows that relationships with fellow believers involve inward wishes and orientation as well as outward conduct.

Someone might behave respectfully toward a colleague while inwardly hoping for that colleague's failure or loss of a good position.

Outward conduct and inward wishes then differ.

The Quranic غِلّ belongs to this field of inward rancor and ill will.

Rancor as distinct from seeking one's rights

Freedom from rancor does not require surrendering every claim against injustice.

Someone wronged may seek restitution.

That demand is not itself a sign of vindictiveness.

A person may also criticize another's error while hoping for improvement and good.

By contrast, someone may continue resenting another's comfort and wishing for harm even after a dispute ends.

That condition is closer to غِلّ.

The plea ولا تجعل في قلوبنا غلا should not equate freedom from rancor with silence before oppression, denial of redress, or prohibition of criticism.

The verse concerns purity of heart toward fellow believers, not suspension of justice and responsibility.

7. Shackles, betrayal, and rancor: one family with several branches

A neck shackle, rancor in the heart, and betrayal of a trust initially seem far apart.

Arabic sources nevertheless record explanations connecting some of these branches.

A shackle restricts and binds a person.

Rancor remains concealed in the heart and can influence conduct from within.

In غلول, someone secretly takes something and avoids revealing the truth of the act.

Lexicons also record غَلَّ for entering or inserting something into another thing.

This use can help explain the imagery of property or rancor hidden within something.

Such similarity does not establish “concealing within” as the root's certain original historical meaning.

Nor are rancor and a physical chain, or betrayal and binding the hand, identical.

One may explain ethically that rancor traps a person in a troubled relationship. That is explanatory imagery, not the lexical meaning of غِلّ in every sentence.

A precise definition preserves the three main Quranic branches separately:

Shackles and physical restriction.

Rancor and inward hostility.

Betrayal and secretly appropriating property or rights.

These branches belong to the root's semantic network while retaining distinct meanings.

8. Comparison with related roots

غ ل ل and خ و ن: betrayal and misappropriation

Betrayal concerns violating a trust or confidence.

غُلُول is a form of betrayal, familiarly involving secret appropriation of property or something placed in one's trust or charge.

Someone may betray a financial trust by taking part of the property.

Such conduct can be غلول.

Betrayal has a broader field: it can concern a covenant, duty, confidence, or another relationship without secretly taking property.

Thus غلول is betrayal, but not every betrayal is financial misappropriation.

غ ل ل and س ر ق: secret appropriation and theft

Theft familiarly concerns taking another's property without right, often secretly.

غُلُول involving shared or entrusted property also entails abuse of access and trust.

A person might steal property from someone else's home.

By contrast, someone appointed to safeguard or distribute property might secretly take from what was entrusted to them.

Both involve wrongful taking, but the person's relationship to the property and responsibility differ.

This is not an absolute boundary: theft and betrayal of trust can coincide.

غ ل ل and ح ق د: rancor and resentment

حقد concerns rancor and inward hostility.

The Quranic غِلّ occupies that field too.

Semantic closeness does not make every form and use of the two families identical.

Besides rancor, غ ل ل includes neck shackles and betrayal.

حقد directly concerns the condition of harboring resentment.

Translating غِلّ as rancor preserves a natural equivalent, while a root study should also distinguish the wider family.

غ ل ل and ح س د: rancor and envy

Envy concerns displeasure at another's advantages and, in some uses, wishing those advantages removed.

غِلّ covers a broader field of rancor and ill will.

Envy of another's success may develop into rancor.

Rancor may also arise from an old hostility without any particular advantage or blessing at issue.

Envy can thus contribute to rancor, but the words are not fully synonymous.

غ ل ل and ش ح ح: covetousness and rancor

شح concerns covetousness and severe reluctance to give or share.

غِلّ, concerning the heart, denotes rancor and ill will.

Hashr 59:9 speaks of protection from the soul's covetousness.

Hashr 59:10 asks God not to place rancor in believers' hearts.

The concepts occur near each other but differ in meaning.

Covetousness may cause hostility and envy, but is not necessarily rancor.

Someone may harbor rancor for reasons unrelated to wealth or property.

غ ل ل and ب خ ل: a bound hand and withholding

بخل concerns withholding what it is appropriate or necessary to give.

In Isra 17:29, the hand bound to the neck depicts stinginess.

The lexical meaning of مغلولة remains bound.

Stinginess follows from the sentence's figurative use.

Not everyone who is مغلول is stingy: a person can literally be shackled without being ungenerous.

غ ل ل and ق ي د: restraints and restricted movement

قيد denotes an implement for binding and restricting movement.

غُلّ is also a restraint, but familiarly concerns the neck and sometimes hands bound to it.

A person's feet might be held in a قيد while a غل surrounds the neck.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule governing every restraint named in Arabic.

The Quran uses أَغْلَال in several descriptions of neck shackles.

غ ل ل and س ل س ل: a shackle and a chain

سلسلة denotes a chain.

غُلّ is a restraint that may be joined to a chain or another implement.

The words occur together in Ghafir 40:71 and Insan 76:4.

Their co-occurrence describes one situation without necessarily naming a single implement twice.

غ ل ل and غ ل و: shackles and excess

غ ل ل and غ ل و are independent roots.

The Quranic uses of غ ل ل concern shackles, rancor, and betrayal.

غ ل و concerns exceeding an appropriate measure or limit.

Religious excess can concern belief or conduct; it has no direct lexical identity with neck shackles or misappropriation of property.

Two shared initial letters and similar written forms do not justify merging the roots.

9. Questions of understanding and translation

Does غل always mean a chain?

No.

غُلّ with ḍamma denotes a shackle or restraining implement.

غِلّ with kasra denotes rancor and ill will.

The distinction is real and decisive in the Quran.

Rancor fits Araf 7:43 and Hijr 15:47, whereas أَغْلَال in verses concerning necks denotes restraints.

Does غلول mean only stealing spoils?

Early usage particularly associates it with secretly taking war spoils.

Lexicons also record broader betrayal of trust and secret appropriation.

In Al Imran 3:161, the military context matters, but the clause names no specific item of property.

The basic translation can retain betrayal while an explanation identifies the use concerning spoils.

Does مغلولة concerning God's hand have a bodily meaning?

Maidah 5:64 places the expression in a context of giving and generosity.

The bound hand accordingly signifies restriction or withholding in giving.

God rejects that claim within the same verse.

The expression does not warrant attributing a bodily structure or physical restriction to God.

Does the bound hand in Isra 17:29 prohibit retaining property?

No.

The verse prohibits two extremes: keeping the hand bound and extending it without limit.

It concerns measured spending and giving, rather than forbidding all provision for personal and family needs.

Is rancor in the heart simply disagreement?

No.

Differences of interpretation, criticism, and seeking justice against oppression do not themselves constitute rancor.

غِلّ concerns inward resentment and ill will.

People can disagree seriously while wishing each other well and respecting each other's rights.

Are the shackles in Hereafter verses only metaphors?

أَغْلَال directly means shackles; several verses employ images of neck restraints and chains.

Some contexts, such as Araf 7:157, more clearly present a figurative and social use involving restrictions.

Punishment verses should not have their verbal imagery erased and converted without evidence into purely psychological states.

Conversely, the material, dimensions, and physical operation of these restraints should not be asserted beyond the Quranic text.

Does rancor always imply deliberate disbelief?

No.

Rancor may develop from envy, hurt, disagreement, or other causes.

An inward feeling alone does not determine a person's entire condition of faith, intention, and responsibility.

Hashr 59:10 shows the importance of believers' inward relations through its prayer for freedom from rancor.

It does not authorize definite judgments about particular people's intentions and destinies merely from their outward disagreements.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

The root's vowel and structural distinctions should be preserved in Quran translation.

يَغُلَّ — Al Imran 3:161: “betray a trust.”

يَغْلُلْ: “betrays a trust”; in its conditional construction, “whoever betrays a trust.”

غَلَّ: “betrayed a trust,” or “what he had misappropriated,” according to the sentence structure.

مَغْلُولَةٌ — Maidah 5:64: “bound”; in the phrase concerning the hand, “the hand is bound.”

غُلَّتْ أَيْدِيهِمْ: “May their hands be bound” or “Their hands were bound,” depending on how the rebuking response is read.

مَغْلُولَةً — Isra 17:29: “bound.”

In the complete sentence: “Do not keep your hand bound to your neck.”

غِلّ: “rancor” or “rancor and ill will,” depending on context.

مِنْ غِلٍّ — Araf 7:43 and Hijr 15:47: “of rancor,” or, in the full clause, “whatever rancor was in their breasts.”

غِلًّا — Hashr 59:10: “rancor.”

أَغْلَال: “shackles,” “bonds,” or, where appropriate, “shackles and chains.”

فَغُلُّوهُ — Haqqah 69:30: “then shackle him.”

In non-Quranic usage:

غُلُول: betrayal of trust and secret appropriation of another's property or rights.

غَالّ: a betrayer or someone who has secretly appropriated property.

غِلال: in the relevant use, hidden rancors or hostilities.

غَلِيل: burning thirst; in another use, inward burning from anger or grief.

غِلّة: income or yield from property or land.

غِلالة: an undergarment or something in an inner layer.

غُلّة: intense thirst.

Each equivalent should distinguish the word's own meaning from its contextual consequence or application.

مغلولة directly means bound; stinginess follows from the image in the verse.

غِلّ means rancor; active hostility or harm is established through conduct.

غُلُول concerns betrayal and secret appropriation; the property and responsibilities involved are identified by context.

Conclusion

The Arabic root غ ل ل has several branches. Its three main Quranic uses concern shackles, hidden rancor, and betrayal of trust.

In physical use, غُلّ is a restraint around a person's neck or hands restricting movement. The verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates it through freeing a captive.

غِلّ denotes rancor, hostility, and inward ill will. Araf 7:43 and Hijr 15:47 describe its removal from the breasts of the Garden's inhabitants. In Hashr 59:10, believers ask God to keep their hearts free of rancor toward other believers.

غُلُول concerns betrayal of trust and secretly appropriating another's property or rights. Al Imran 3:161 denies such betrayal of a Prophet and addresses divine accountability for anyone committing it.

Other verses use bonds and shackles both in images of worldly restrictions and in punishment scenes.

In Maidah 5:64 and Isra 17:29, a bound hand depicts restriction or withholding in giving.

Araf 7:157 mentions removing shackles alongside lifting heavy burdens.

Rad 13:5, Saba 34:33, Ghafir 40:71, Haqqah 69:30, and Insan 76:4 use shackles and binding in scenes of accountability and punishment. Ya Sin 36:8 also presents neck shackles, in a context that can describe resistance to guidance.

Arabic also preserves غِلّة for income or yield, غُلّة for intense thirst, and غِلالة for an inner covering.

The witnesses place these meanings in one lexical family, but do not establish one certain historical origin for them all.

A final definition should not reduce the root to vague formulas such as “restriction” or “having something inside.”

The Quranic root غ ل ل has three clear branches: غُلّ, a restraining shackle; غِلّ, rancor and hostility hidden in the heart; and غَلَّ يَغُلُّ, betrayal and secret appropriation of entrusted property or rights.

Despite some shared imagery, these branches must remain distinct in translation and reflection. The decisive factors are the exact word form, its vowels, syntax, and Quranic context.
