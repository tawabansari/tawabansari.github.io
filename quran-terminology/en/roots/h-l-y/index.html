From ornaments and adornment to the embellishments of this world and the Hereafter

The Arabic root ح ل ي concerns ornaments, adornment, and adding something to the appearance of a person or object for beauty. حَلْي denotes ornaments used for adornment; حِلْيَة also denotes an ornament and, more broadly, outward form or description. حَلَّى means adorning with ornaments, while تَحَلَّى describes someone adorning himself.

Ornaments may be made of gold, silver, pearls, precious stones, or other materials. What makes something حِلْيَة is not simply its material but its use for adornment. Gold can become an ornament, and the same gold can be used to make something else.

The family extends beyond wearable ornaments. Its derivatives also express decorating a sword, describing a person’s appearance, and figurative adornment with worthy characteristics. These broader uses should not, however, be transferred into every Qur’anic verse without evidence.

ح ل ي occurs nine times in nine Qur’anic verses. Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 mentions the ornaments of Moses’ people; Al-Raʿd 13:17 concerns making ornaments from molten metal; Al-Naḥl 16:14 and Fāṭir 35:12 concern extracting ornaments from water; Al-Zukhruf 43:18 concerns upbringing amid adornment; and four other verses describe the people of the Garden being adorned with gold and silver bracelets.

Qur’anic forms: حُلِيِّهِمْ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:148; حِلْيَةٍ in Al-Raʿd 13:17; حِلْيَةً in Al-Naḥl 16:14 and Fāṭir 35:12; الْحِلْيَةِ in Al-Zukhruf 43:18; يُحَلَّوْنَ in Al-Kahf 18:31, Al-Ḥajj 22:23, and Fāṭir 35:33; and حُلُّوا in Al-Insān 76:21.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: nine occurrences and four passive verbs

Forms such as حَلْي, حَلِيَ, حَالِيَة, تَحْلِيَة, تَحَلَّى, مُتَحَلٍّ, and مُحَلًّى are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in these exact forms in the Qur’an.

1. Core Meaning and Early Arabic Uses

حَلْي and حِلْيَة: something used for adornment

Al-ʿAyn describes حَلْي as an ornament adorning a woman, sword, or something else. حِلْيَة also denotes the ornament itself and what provides adornment.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: ḥaly, ḥilyah, and ornaments of women and swords

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris examines the family beginning with ح and ل and ending with a weak consonant, distinguishing three fields: pleasantness and sweetness, embellishment, and removing or driving away in the hamzated form.

He places حَلْي in the embellishment branch, explaining it through women’s ornaments and decorating a sword.

The distinction matters: sweetness, pleasantness, and beauty may be close in human experience, but ornaments should not be identified directly with the sweetness of food.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: sweetness, embellishment, and the hamzated form

In established usage, حَلْي denotes ornaments worn or added to something for adornment; حُلِيّ is its recognized plural. Lexical sources offer differing accounts of the singular and plural relationships among حَلْي, حُلِيّ, and حِلْيَة.

حُلِيِّهِمْ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 denotes the ornaments of Moses’ people. The word is not confined to a particular item, such as a ring or bracelet.

حِلْيَة can likewise name something made or used for adornment in general.

The Qur’an uses it for ornaments extracted from water, made from molten material, and worn. Accompanying clauses establish the particular material and form.

حَلَّى: adorning with ornaments

حَلَّى الْمَرْأَةَ means adorning a woman with ornaments. Lexical sources record the verb both for putting ornaments on someone and providing ornaments for her.

حَلَّى السَّيْفَ means decorating a sword with ornamental pieces.

An ornament is added to the person’s or object’s appearance. A sword’s military function may remain unchanged while its hilt or scabbard is decorated with metal and ornamental designs.

Adorning something therefore need not change its nature or principal function.

حَلَّى itself does not specify the ornament’s material. Gold, silver, and other substances can be involved in different contexts.

Qur’anic يُحَلَّوْنَ and حُلُّوا belong to this verbal formation but are passive: people are adorned with ornaments.

تَحَلَّى: adorning oneself

تَحَلَّتِ الْمَرْأَةُ means that a woman put on ornaments or adorned herself.

The person performs the adornment upon herself in this construction.

حَلَّاهَا, by contrast, can refer to someone else providing ornaments for her or putting them on her.

This morphological distinction matters for the Qur’anic forms. The Garden descriptions use passive يُحَلَّوْنَ and حُلُّوا, focusing on people being adorned rather than establishing that they made or put on the ornaments themselves.

Arabic also extends تَحَلَّى to adornment with ethical qualities, such as someone adorning himself with a praiseworthy trait.

That figurative usage does not establish that gold and silver ornaments in the Garden verses are merely coded names for ethical virtues.

Those verses expressly mention bracelets, gold, silver, pearls, and clothing. A possible spiritual reading should not replace these words’ direct meanings.

حِلْيَة: description and outward form

حِلْيَة has another Arabic usage: the description, form, and appearance of a person or thing.

Lisān al-ʿArab records حِلْيَةُ الرَّجُلِ for someone’s form, physical features, and description.

حَلَّيْتُ الرَّجُلَ can likewise mean describing his form and features.

Wearable ornaments are not necessarily involved: the person’s physical features are conveyed so that he can be recognized.

Ornaments and outward description are intelligibly connected, since both concern how a person or thing appears. That proximity does not make them identical in every instance.

In the Qur’anic occurrences examined here, حِلْيَة concerns ornaments and adornment. “Outward description” does not directly replace ornaments in any of them.

Plant names and other forms

حَلِيّ is also recorded as a plant name. Some dictionaries describe a desert grass; others refer to its dried state.

الْحَلِيّ here names a plant or group of plants, rather than wearable ornaments.

Lexicographers disagree about the name’s scope: some restrict it to a particular plant, while others apply it to plants with similar characteristics.

Shared root letters alone do not therefore establish a common meaning.

Al-ʿAyn records حَلِيَ مِنْهُ بِخَيْرٍ for obtaining good or benefit from something. Ibn Barrī records a separate expression, مَا حَلِيتُ بِطَائِلٍ or لَمْ يَحْلَ بِطَائِلٍ, for failing to gain substantial benefit, in a negative construction. The negation restriction of that expression should not be generalized to every use concerning obtaining benefit.

This use must not become the fixed Qur’anic meaning of حِلْيَة either. Al-Raʿd 13:17 distinguishes ornaments from useful goods, but the word does not mean unrestricted benefit or economic gain.

Tāj al-ʿArūs and Lisān al-ʿArab: plurals, appearance, plant names, and benefit expressions

2. Early Poetry: Ornaments and Adornment in Arab Life

The sound of ornaments in Al-Aʿshā’s poetry

Al-Aʿshā writes:

تَسْمَعُ لِلْحَلْيِ وَسْوَاسًا إِذَا انْصَرَفَتْ
كَمَا اسْتَعَانَ بِرِيحٍ عِشْرِقٌ زَجِلُ

tasmaʿu li-l-ḥalyi waswāsan idhā inṣarafat / ka-mā istaʿāna bi-rīḥin ʿishriqun zajilu

Meaning: When the woman turns away, a delicate sound is heard from her ornaments, like the sound of dry ʿishriq stirred by the wind.

الْحَلْي denotes wearable ornaments here.

The poet describes not only their visible beauty but also the sound produced by their pieces striking one another in movement.

The witness presents ornaments as actual, familiar objects in Arab life and poetry: things worn that could produce sound during movement.

The verse is attributed to Al-Aʿshā and transmitted in commentaries on the odes and lexical sources. وَسْوَاسًا, with fatḥah on the first consonant, denotes the ornaments’ faint sound here; it should not be confused with a kasrah reading in certain verbal-noun uses.

Al-Aʿshā’s witness: waswās with fatḥah

Its value is to attest actual lexical usage. Sound is not, however, an inherent part of حَلْي, since many ornaments may make no perceptible sound when worn.

Ornaments of the neck and chest

Ṭabaqāt Fuḥūl al-Shuʿarāʾ records this description of a woman in a poem by Shabīb ibn al-Barṣāʾ, an Islamic-period poet:

حَالِي التَّرَائِبِ وَالذِّفْرَى عَقَدْنَ بِهِ
مِنْ لُؤْلُؤٍ وَجُمَانٍ غَيْرِ أَفْرَادِ

ḥālī al-tarāʾibi wa-l-dhifrā ʿaqadna bihi / min luʾluʾin wa-jumānin ghayri afrādi

Meaning: “The chest and area behind the ear are adorned; ornaments of pearls and jumān have been fastened there, not as isolated, separate beads.” Lexical explanations also use جُمَان for pearls or pearl-like beads; its material should not be restricted without evidence.

حَالِي denotes being adorned with ornaments.

تَرَائِب denotes the chest area where necklaces and ornaments lie. ذِفْرَى concerns the area behind the ear, whose ornamentation is related to earrings in explanations of the verse.

Ornaments could thus be worn at different bodily locations rather than being confined to one kind, such as bracelets.

Ṭabaqāt Fuḥūl al-Shuʿarāʾ: Shabīb ibn al-Barṣāʾ and neck and chest ornaments

Lexical sources also describe decorated swords and certain implements with ornamental designs or metal using this family.

Ornamentation is therefore not restricted to the human body. Something added to an object for beauty can also be called حِلْيَة.

3. حِلْيَة and حُلِيّ in Qur’anic Usage

Al-Aʿrāf 7:148: ornaments transformed into a calf

وَاتَّخَذَ قَوْمُ مُوسَىٰ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ مِنْ حُلِيِّهِمْ عِجْلًا جَسَدًا لَهُ خُوَارٌ أَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّهُ لَا يُكَلِّمُهُمْ وَلَا يَهْدِيهِمْ سَبِيلًا اتَّخَذُوهُ وَكَانُوا ظَالِمِينَ

wa-ttakhadha qawmu mūsā min baʿdihi min ḥuliyyihim ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwārun a-lam yaraw annahu lā yukallimuhum wa-lā yahdīhim sabīlan ittakhadhūhu wa-kānū ẓālimīna

Translation: After Moses’ departure, his people made from their ornaments a calf’s body that lowed. Did they not see that it neither spoke to them nor guided them to a way? They adopted it and were wrongdoers.

حُلِيِّهِمْ is the plural of حَلْي with the pronoun هُمْ, referring to ornaments belonging to or held by Moses’ people.

مِنْ in مِنْ حُلِيِّهِمْ concerns the material or source from which the calf’s body was made.

An ornament can thus consist of material that is reshaped and used to make something else.

حُلِيّ itself does not mean gold. The ornaments of Moses’ people are mentioned, but the word alone does not establish their precise material.

Nor does it mean a calf or deity. The calf was made from ornaments, but the material’s being ornamental and the figure’s being adopted as a deity are separate matters.

The context criticizes Moses’ people elevating something unable to speak or guide to the status of a deity.

It does not establish that possessing ornaments, using metal, or making every shape and figure is inherently associationism.

The criticism concerns their adopting as a deity a figure they had made themselves and which could not guide them.

Al-Raʿd 13:17: ornaments and useful goods from molten material

Following the image of a flood and foam on water, Al-Raʿd 13:17 states:

وَمِمَّا يُوقِدُونَ عَلَيْهِ فِي النَّارِ ابْتِغَاءَ حِلْيَةٍ أَوْ مَتَاعٍ زَبَدٌ مِثْلُهُ

wa-mimmā yūqidūna ʿalayhi fī al-nāri ibtighāʾa ḥilyatin aw matāʿin zabadun mithluhu

Meaning: From what they heat in the fire to obtain ornaments or useful goods, there is similar foam.

حِلْيَة appears alongside مَتَاع.

The distinction matters: material melted in fire can make ornaments or an implement with another everyday use.

Gold or silver, for example, may become ornaments, while other metals may become tools or needed utensils.

The clause itself does not name the metal. Identifying a particular metal as the meaning of حِلْيَة would therefore be incorrect.

حِلْيَة concerns a product made for adornment; مَتَاع here concerns something provided for use and benefit.

These fields can overlap in practice: ornaments may have economic value or social functions. Nevertheless, the verse distinguishes two purposes of manufacture.

The verse continues with foam and what benefits people, within a parable about truth and falsehood.

The lexical meaning of حِلْيَة should not become truth, falsehood, or ethical worth. Ornaments and molten material are tangible components of the image; its spiritual lesson emerges from the verse’s complete construction.

Al-Naḥl 16:14: extracting ornaments from the sea

وَهُوَ الَّذِي سَخَّرَ الْبَحْرَ لِتَأْكُلُوا مِنْهُ لَحْمًا طَرِيًّا وَتَسْتَخْرِجُوا مِنْهُ حِلْيَةً تَلْبَسُونَهَا وَتَرَى الْفُلْكَ مَوَاخِرَ فِيهِ وَلِتَبْتَغُوا مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-huwa alladhī sakhkhara al-baḥra li-taʾkulū minhu laḥman ṭariyyan wa-tastakhrijū minhu ḥilyatan talbasūnahā wa-tarā al-fulka mawākhira fīhi wa-li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi wa-laʿallakum tashkurūna

Translation: He is the One who subjected the sea so that you may eat fresh meat from it and extract ornaments that you wear. You see ships cleaving through it, and may seek His bounty, so that you may give thanks.

حِلْيَةً is the object of تَسْتَخْرِجُوا. The pronoun هَا in تَلْبَسُونَهَا refers back to the ornament.

The verse distinguishes extracting ornaments from the sea and wearing them after extraction.

Lexical and exegetical sources identify pearls and coral as familiar examples of marine ornaments.

حِلْيَة does not itself mean pearls or coral. It denotes something obtained from the sea and worn for adornment.

ل ب س here concerns wearing ornaments on the body and should not be confined to fabric clothing.

The verse juxtaposes three benefits of the sea: fresh food, ornaments, and ships’ movement.

Including ornaments among these blessings does not present the use of beautiful, decorative materials as inherently objectionable in this context.

The verse does not independently state which gender may wear which metal. Such rulings cannot be derived from ح ل ي alone.

Fāṭir 35:12: ornaments and two different waters

Fāṭir 35:12 introduces two waters: one sweet and pleasant to drink, the other salty and bitter.

It then states:

وَمِنْ كُلٍّ تَأْكُلُونَ لَحْمًا طَرِيًّا وَتَسْتَخْرِجُونَ حِلْيَةً تَلْبَسُونَهَا

wa-min kullin taʾkulūna laḥman ṭariyyan wa-tastakhrijūna ḥilyatan talbasūnahā

Meaning: From each you eat fresh meat and extract ornaments that you wear.

The wording resembles Al-Naḥl 16:14, but the context differs.

Al-Naḥl uses the singular الْبَحْر; Fāṭir contrasts two waters with different characteristics.

وَمِنْ كُلٍّ matters because it introduces human benefit from the two waters.

Early commentaries differ over whether the ornaments are directly obtained from both waters or whether the wording refers collectively to their blessings.

Translation should preserve the verse’s own construction, rather than restrict extraction to one water without justification.

حِلْيَة also does not establish that a particular marine creature or decorative stone must be intended.

The ornament’s role is clear in both verses: something is extracted from water and used for adornment.

Al-Ṭabarī, Fāṭir 35:12: the two waters and ornaments

These occurrences present ornaments among creation’s benefits, without providing a complete ethical assessment of every method of extraction, trade, or consumption.

Al-Zukhruf 43:18: being brought up amid adornment

أَوَمَنْ يُنَشَّأُ فِي الْحِلْيَةِ وَهُوَ فِي الْخِصَامِ غَيْرُ مُبِينٍ

a-wa-man yunashshaʾu fī al-ḥilyati wa-huwa fī al-khiṣāmi ghayru mubīnin

Translation: One brought up amid ornaments and adornment, yet not clear in disputation?

The context differs from the other uses of حِلْيَة.

Here the word concerns ornaments present in someone’s upbringing and life.

يُنَشَّأُ comes from ن ش أ and concerns being brought up. فِي الْحِلْيَةِ describes the condition or environment of that upbringing.

حِلْيَة itself means neither a girl, weakness, nor inability to reason.

The verse’s intended referent requires contextual examination.

Al-Zukhruf 43:16 and Al-Zukhruf 43:17 discuss attributing daughters to The God and some associators’ negative reaction to a daughter’s birth. Al-Zukhruf 43:19 criticizes attributing female sex to angels.

One recognized reading is that Al-Zukhruf 43:18 uses the social image of girls and women held by those addressees, who themselves devalued daughters while attributing female sex to The God.

Early exegesis also records another reading: the referents may be idols decorated with gold and silver, yet unable to speak or defend themselves.

These readings differ over the referent of مَنْ. Assessing their relative strength requires an independent contextual study.

In both, however, الْحِلْيَة retains its lexical meaning of ornaments and adornment.

The verse should not, without examining its argument and the audience’s views, yield a general biological claim that women are inherently unable to reason. Such a conclusion does not follow from حِلْيَة itself.

The root’s contribution is ornaments and adornment; pronoun reference and the argument’s substance are separate matters.

Al-Qurṭubī, Al-Zukhruf 43:18: women and idols as alternative referents

4. Ornaments in Descriptions of the People of the Garden

All four verbal Qur’anic occurrences concern the people of the Garden being adorned.

يُحَلَّوْنَ occurs three times and حُلُّوا once.

Both are passive Form II verbs, the former imperfect and the latter perfect.

The ornaments are not merely things extracted or manufactured in this world; they form part of the Garden’s blessings.

Al-Kahf 18:31: gold bracelets

Al-Kahf 18:31 says of the people of the Garden:

يُحَلَّوْنَ فِيهَا مِنْ أَسَاوِرَ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ

yuḥallawna fīhā min asāwira min dhahabin

Meaning: They are adorned there with bracelets of gold.

يُحَلَّوْنَ is a passive imperfect verb, third-person masculine plural.

The people being adorned occupy the verb’s subject position in this construction.

أَسَاوِر is a plural of سِوَار, bracelet; مِنْ ذَهَبٍ specifies their material.

Gold therefore comes from ذَهَب, rather than the root ح ل ي itself.

The verse continues with green clothing and different fabrics.

Being adorned with bracelets and wearing clothes are related but distinct acts, expressed through separate verbs.

Al-Ḥajj 22:23 and Fāṭir 35:33: gold and pearls

يُحَلَّوْنَ also occurs in Al-Ḥajj 22:23 and Fāṭir 35:33.

Both describe gold bracelets and pearls among the Garden’s adornments.

The act expressed by يُحَلَّوْنَ must again be distinguished from the ornaments’ materials.

The verb means being adorned; gold and pearls are identified by their independent names.

Rendering يُحَلَّوْنَ merely as “they wear gold” would merge the act with its material and confine the root to one substance.

Al-Ḥajj 22:23 expressly identifies faith and good deeds in describing the recipients. Fāṭir 35:33 follows Fāṭir 35:32, which discusses the chosen inheritors of the Book and their different groups. The two contexts should not be flattened into an identical account, nor should a dispute about the pronoun’s referent be resolved through the meaning of ornaments alone.

Ornaments themselves are not a measure of virtue or moral worth. Those qualities are established by descriptions of the Garden’s people elsewhere and by the complete context.

Al-Ṭabarī, Fāṭir 35:32: groups inheriting the Book and the following verse

Al-Insān 76:21: silver bracelets

عَالِيَهُمْ ثِيَابُ سُنْدُسٍ خُضْرٌ وَإِسْتَبْرَقٌ وَحُلُّوا أَسَاوِرَ مِنْ فِضَّةٍ وَسَقَاهُمْ رَبُّهُمْ شَرَابًا طَهُورًا

ʿāliyahum thiyābu sundusin khuḍrun wa-istabraqun wa-ḥullū asāwira min fiḍḍatin wa-saqāhum rabbuhum sharāban ṭahūran

Translation: Upon them are green garments of sundus and istabraq; they have been adorned with silver bracelets, and their Lord has given them a purifying drink.

حُلُّوا is a passive perfect Form II verb.

The preceding three occurrences use يُحَلَّوْنَ; here adornment is expressed through the perfect.

This morphological difference should not without evidence become a definite difference in the time or rank of reward. Perfect and imperfect forms can depict future scenes through different modes of expression.

The bracelets here are silver, while the earlier verses also mentioned gold.

The verses therefore do not restrict the Garden’s ornaments to a single metal.

Nor do descriptions of the Garden’s adornment by themselves prohibit particular groups from wearing gold or silver in this world.

A claimed prohibition or obligation concerning ornaments requires separate evidence. Describing adornment in the Hereafter does not, without independent textual evidence, become a restrictive ruling on worldly clothing and ornaments.

Actual adornment or solely symbolic?

Gold, silver, pearls, bracelets, and clothes have established names in Qur’anic language.

The Garden verses use these same words.

Questions about the Hereafter’s characteristics and its differences from worldly material life do not justify removing the lexical meanings of ornaments, bracelets, or the named metals at the outset.

Conversely, these verses do not provide a detailed physical account of how ornaments are made or worn in the Hereafter.

Two limits should therefore be observed: retain the direct meanings and avoid inventing unmentioned details as certain facts.

5. Distinctions from Related Roots

ح ل ي (ḥilyah) and ز ي ن (zīnah)

زِينَة has a broader field, including outward adornment, the beauty of plants, worldly splendour, the sky, and other things.

Qur’anic حِلْيَة denotes ornaments and means of adornment.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 calls the ornaments of Moses’ people حُلِيّ; Ṭā Hā 20:87, in a related account of the same event, uses زِينَةِ الْقَوْمِ.

Ornaments can thus be instances of زِينَة without the words being complete synonyms.

Al-Zukhruf 43:35 also mentions زُخْرُف and مَتَاع in connection with worldly possessions, while حِلْيَة in the same surah concerns adornment.

Every ornament is a kind of adornment, but not every adornment is a wearable ornament.

ح ل ي (ḥilyah) and ز خ ر ف (zukhruf)

زُخْرُف concerns gold and splendid adornment in Arabic.

Some uses also concern lavish decoration and embellishment.

حِلْيَة can be made of gold, but gold is not inherent in its meaning.

Silver or pearl ornaments can also be حِلْيَة, as illustrated by Qur’anic marine ornaments and silver bracelets.

حِلْيَة cannot therefore always be translated as “gold ornaments.”

Lavishness or deceptive appearance is likewise not inherent in this root.

Study of Z KH R F

ح ل ي (ḥilyah) and ج م ل (jamāl)

جَمَال concerns beauty and a pleasing appearance or condition.

حِلْيَة can be a means of adornment and added beauty.

A person or thing can be beautiful without ornaments, while possessing ornaments does not itself establish ethical or physical superiority.

Ornaments and beauty are therefore related but distinct concepts.

Qur’anic حِلْيَة concerns ornamental adornment and should not become beauty in general in every verse.

Study of J M L

ح ل ي (ḥaly) and ح ل و (ḥulw)

The families share their first two consonants, while their root forms and uses must remain distinct.

Established ح ل و concerns sweetness and pleasantness; the Qur’anic ح ل ي branch concerns ornaments and adornment.

Early dictionaries record expressions such as حَلِيَ بِعَيْنِي for something appearing pleasing or attractive to someone.

Lexicographers differ over whether this belongs to sweetness and pleasantness or is connected with ornaments and beauty.

Ibn Manẓūr also records this disagreement.

The proximity of beauty and pleasantness can be acknowledged without inserting food’s sweetness into حِلْيَة in ornament verses.

حَلَّى can likewise mean sweetening food in one construction and adorning with ornaments in another. The object, syntax, and established usage determine the sense.

ح ل ي (ḥilyah) and ح ل ل (ḥalāl)

ح ل ل concerns untying, alighting, dissolution, and, in its legal branch, permissibility.

Qur’anic ح ل ي concerns ornaments and adornment.

Their outward similarity does not establish a direct semantic connection.

حِلْيَة alone cannot determine whether an ornament is religiously permitted or forbidden.

Such rulings require expressions of permission and prohibition, rather than resemblance between the name of an ornament and حَلَال.

Study of Ḥ L L

ح ل ي (ḥilyah) and ل ب س (labisa)

In clothing contexts, ل ب س concerns wearing something.

حِلْيَة names something used for adornment.

Al-Naḥl 16:14 and Fāṭir 35:12 contain حِلْيَةً تَلْبَسُونَهَا: ornaments that you wear.

Ornaments can thus be worn, but حِلْيَة does not itself mean the act of wearing.

The Garden verses also juxtapose adornment with bracelets and wearing different clothes.

The ornament’s name, adorning with it, and wearing must therefore be distinguished.

Study of L B S

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

حُلِيِّهِمْ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 should be “their ornaments.” This preserves plurality and the attached pronoun without unjustifiably restricting the material to gold or silver.

حِلْيَةٍ أَوْ مَتَاعٍ in Al-Raʿd 13:17 is “ornaments or useful goods.” The two manufacturing purposes should remain distinct.

حِلْيَةً تَلْبَسُونَهَا in Al-Naḥl 16:14 and Fāṭir 35:12 is “ornaments that you wear,” preserving the meaning without adding an unmentioned substance.

فِي الْحِلْيَةِ in Al-Zukhruf 43:18 is “amid ornaments and adornment” or “among ornaments.” Its meaning must remain separate from deciding the referent of مَنْ.

يُحَلَّوْنَ in Al-Kahf 18:31, Al-Ḥajj 22:23, and Fāṭir 35:33 is “they are adorned,” preserving the passive construction and its human recipients.

حُلُّوا in Al-Insān 76:21 is “they were adorned” or, in a flowing depiction of the Hereafter, “they have been adorned.”

The metals and kinds of ornaments come from accompanying words, rather than حُلُّوا or يُحَلَّوْنَ themselves.

Outside the Qur’an, حَلْي is “ornaments,” تَحْلِيَة “adorning with ornaments,” تَحَلَّى “adorned himself,” and descriptive حِلْيَةُ الرَّجُلِ “the person’s outward form and features.”

Figurative تَحَلَّى بِالْفَضِيلَةِ can be “adorned himself with a virtue,” but should not replace actual ornaments in Qur’anic verses.

Conclusion

ح ل ي concerns ornaments and adornment in Arabic. حَلْي and حِلْيَة denote something used to beautify and adorn people or objects. حَلَّى means adorning with ornaments; تَحَلَّى describes someone adorning himself.

Broader حِلْيَة also describes a person’s outward form. Uses concerning sweetness, pleasantness, or plant names should not be linked to ornaments without evidence.

All nine Qur’anic occurrences belong to the ornament and adornment branch.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:148, Moses’ people make a calf’s body from ornaments. In Al-Raʿd 13:17, ornaments are products of material heated in fire. In Al-Naḥl 16:14 and Fāṭir 35:12, they are extracted from water and worn.

Al-Zukhruf 43:18 mentions ornaments in upbringing. Al-Kahf 18:31, Al-Ḥajj 22:23, Fāṭir 35:33, and Al-Insān 76:21 describe the Garden’s people being adorned with gold and silver bracelets.

Qur’anic ornaments are therefore not confined to a single material, particular shape, or ethical condition.

Brief root meaning: Ornaments and adornment; adorning people or objects with something added to their appearance for beauty.

This meaning establishes neither religious prohibition of ornaments, ethical superiority of their possessor, nor that every beauty or adornment is necessarily a wearable ornament.

For Forqan, “ornaments” suits the nominal forms and “being adorned” the verbs. Material, purpose, and significance in each verse require the accompanying words and context.

These distinctions preserve the Qur’an’s direct imagery without substituting social, legal, or symbolic interpretations for lexical meaning.
