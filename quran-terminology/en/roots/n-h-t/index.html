Carving and shaping by removing material: from stone houses to objects of worship

1. Introduction and semantic core

Arabic N Ḥ T concerns carving, cutting, and removing parts of a material to shape it. Naḥata describes producing an intended shape by cutting, carving, or reducing material such as wood or stone.

Unlike construction by adding and joining pieces, the desired form arises chiefly by removing existing material. A carpenter can carve wood smooth or shape it into a tool; a stoneworker can remove stone to produce a vessel, figure, or usable space.

Naḥt is therefore not restricted to sculpture. Its range includes carving wood, cutting and shaping stone, hollowing hard material, and creating usable space within a mountain.

Arabic nuḥātah denotes the chips and shavings separated in this work. It illustrates removal of material as a recognized feature of carving.

Not every naḥt necessarily involves a particular iron tool, powerful blows, or an artistic figure. The tool, required force, and resulting shape depend on material and use.

A defensible semantic core is carving and shaping material by cutting and removing parts of it. The purpose of carving and the value of the resulting object do not come from the root alone.

Qur’anic forms

N Ḥ T occurs four times in four Qur’anic verses, always as an imperfect verb of the basic triliteral form:

  • Wa-tanḥitūna: Al-Aʿrāf 7:74.
  • Yanḥitūna: Al-Ḥijr 15:82.
  • Wa-tanḥitūna: Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149.
  • Tanḥitūna: Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95.

The first three concern carving houses in mountains. The fourth concerns things Abraham’s people carved and worshipped.

Other Arabic forms include naḥt, nāḥit, manḥūt, minḥat, nuḥātah, naḥīt, and naḥītah. None of these occurs in the Qur’an.

All four Qur’anic verbs concern making or shaping something. None directly concerns linguistic word formation, shaping personality, or other figurative uses.

2. Early Arabic: carving wood and shaping stone

Carving and removing material

Early Arabic explanations connect naḥt with cutting, carving, and smoothing wood and stone.

The following Arabic description helps explain the action:

النُّونُ وَالْحَاءُ وَالتَّاءُ كَلِمَةٌ تَدُلُّ عَلَى نَجْرِ شَيْءٍ وَتَسْوِيَتِهِ بِحَدِيدَةٍ

al-nūnu wa-al-ḥāʾu wa-al-tāʾu kalimatun tadullu ʿalā najri shayʾin wa-taswiyatihi bi-ḥadīdatin

Translation:

“Nūn, ḥāʾ, and tāʾ indicate carving something and smoothing it with an iron implement.”

Two aspects appear: removing material and shaping or smoothing it.

A carpenter carving wood is a clear example. Mention of an iron implement describes a familiar method without making one particular tool necessary in every use.

Nashr and qashr also explain naḥt through cutting, carving, and removing a layer or portion of material.

Naḥata al-khashabata means carving a piece of wood. The aim may be smoothing its surface, reducing thickness, shaping edges, or making a particular implement.

Naḥata al-jabala concerns a much larger, harder material. Carving a mountain can involve cutting rock and creating empty space within it.

Naḥt is therefore more than shaping a surface; it can create an interior space by removing material.

This matters for mountain houses, whose usable interiors are produced by removing stone from within the mountain.

Shavings and carving tools

Nuḥātah denotes the chips and shavings separated while carving.

For example, the pieces removed when a carpenter carves wood are called nuḥātah.

Minḥat names a carving tool.

These words connect material naḥt with a familiar practical activity that progressively reshapes existing material.

They do not establish that every historical act of carving used the same tool. Wood, soft stone, and hard rock can require different tools.

Naḥītah: nature and disposition

Arabic also records naḥītah for a person’s nature, disposition, or fundamental characteristics.

Nuḥita ʿalā al-karami describes someone as though shaped upon generosity.

Material carving here supplies an image for the formation of human characteristics.

The semantic image is intelligible: as material is shaped to a form, personality can be described as having enduring qualities.

This does not prove that early Arabs understood personality as physically carved or produced by one specific process.

Nor does it imply that people cannot change their conduct or improve themselves.

Naḥītah here describes disposition, rather than establishing a theory that personality cannot change.

Wear and reduction through travel

Another recorded use concerns a human or animal body worn down by travel.

Naḥatahu al-safaru means that travel exhausted or emaciated the person or animal.

No one is carving the body with a tool. Loss of strength, flesh, or bodily condition is compared with material reduction through carving.

Naḥīt also describes worn hooves whose edges or parts have been abraded.

This intelligibly connects with reduction and removal of material.

Wear is nevertheless not necessary to every naḥt. The Qur’anic instances concern constructing houses and shaping objects, rather than bodily wasting or exhaustion.

Naḥīt: someone joining from another group

Early Arabic also records naḥīt for someone entering a community from another group or lineage.

A verse attributed to Khirniq, sister of Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, says:

وَالْخَالِطِينَ نَحِيتَهُمْ بِنُضَارِهِمْ
وَذَوِي الْغِنَى مِنْهُمْ بِذِي الْفَقْرِ

wa-al-khāliṭīna naḥītahum bi-nuḍārihim / wa-dhawī al-ghinā minhum bi-dhī al-faqri

Translation:

“Those who mingle their newcomers with their people of established lineage, and include their wealthy with their poor.”

Naḥīt here concerns someone from another community or origin incorporated into the group.

The use is recorded lexically, but the verse’s attribution is disputed; it has also been attributed to Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī.

It therefore cannot establish the social sense’s precise historical origin.

Nor should that social meaning be transferred to Qur’anic naḥt. None of the four verses concerns someone joining a tribe or community.

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry: the wells called al-Naḥāʾit

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, opening with deserted dwellings, says:

قَفْرًا بِمُنْدَفَعِ النَّحَائِتِ مِنْ
ضَفْوَى أُولَاتِ الضَّالِ وَالسِّدْرِ

qafran bi-mundafaʿi al-naḥāʾiti min / ḍafwā ulāti al-ḍāli wa-al-sidri

Approximate sense:

“The dwellings stand empty at the watercourse of the wells called al-Naḥāʾit, in the region of Ḍafwā with its ḍāl and sidr trees.”

Al-naḥāʾit here names recognized wells; cutting and carving explain their name. Their designation should therefore not be treated indiscriminately as a general noun for every carved well.

The word does not concern an artistic figure or idol here, but a practical structure for reaching water.

The witness, together with the lexical explanation of the wells’ name, connects this family with spaces made by digging and removing material. The verse itself does not state their dimensions, wall material, or exact excavation method.

Transmitted versions include forms resembling Ṣafwā and Ḍafwā. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet also differs from independently establishing the precise composition date.

It is therefore a witness from the poetic tradition attributed to Zuhayr, rather than an undisputed document establishing the word’s first use.

Carving wood and stone is independently established in Arabic, regardless of this witness’s status.

3. Thamūd: houses carved in mountains

The first three Qur’anic occurrences concern Thamūd.

All three describe houses carved from or within mountains.

Each nevertheless approaches the subject differently.

A. Al-Aʿrāf 7:74: mountain houses and buildings on the plains

وَاذْكُرُوا إِذْ جَعَلَكُمْ خُلَفَاءَ مِنْ بَعْدِ عَادٍ وَبَوَّأَكُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ تَتَّخِذُونَ مِنْ سُهُولِهَا قُصُورًا وَتَنْحِتُونَ الْجِبَالَ بُيُوتًا فَاذْكُرُوا آلَاءَ اللَّهِ وَلَا تَعْثَوْا فِي الْأَرْضِ مُفْسِدِينَ

wa-udhkurū idh jaʿalakum khulafāʾa min baʿdi ʿādin wa-bawwaʾakum fī al-arḍi tattakhidhūna min suhūlihā quṣūran wa-tanḥitūna al-jibāla buyūtan fa-udhkurū ālāʾa allāhi wa-lā taʿthaw fī al-arḍi mufsidīna

Translation:

“Remember when He made you successors after ʿĀd and settled you in the earth: you take palaces from its plains and carve the mountains into houses. Remember the gifts of The God, and do not act corruptly in the earth, spreading corruption.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74

Ṣāliḥ calls his people to remember their position and the resources made available to them.

Two uses of land appear together: establishing palaces in flat areas and carving houses in mountains.

Tattakhidhūna comes from ʾ Kh Dh and concerns taking, selecting, or providing something for use.

Tanḥitūna specifies a method of construction: carving hard material.

The verse thus reports more than possession of houses: it describes an ability to shape rock and create mountain living spaces.

Tanḥitūna al-jibāla versus tanḥitūna mina al-jibāli

Here the verb occurs without the preposition min:

وَتَنْحِتُونَ الْجِبَالَ بُيُوتًا

wa-tanḥitūna al-jibāla buyūtan

Al-Ḥijr 15:82 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 instead use mina al-jibāli.

In Al-Aʿrāf, al-jibāla follows the verb directly. It can be understood as an object identifying the carved material, with buyūtan indicating the result.

Another grammatical analysis takes al-jibāla as accusative after an omitted preposition and buyūtan as a prospective circumstantial expression: they carve the mountains so that houses result.

Either way, the general meaning is clear: they created houses in mountains.

Absence of min does not prove they converted entire mountains, from beginning to end, into houses.

The sentence describes the method of construction, rather than the exact amount of removed stone or number of houses.

Is carving mountain houses condemned?

The houses occur among the gifts and resources Ṣāliḥ reminds his people of.

The act of naḥt or ability to build stone houses is therefore not presented as intrinsically blameworthy.

The ethical prohibition comes at the end:

وَلَا تَعْثَوْا فِي الْأَرْضِ مُفْسِدِينَ

wa-lā taʿthaw fī al-arḍi mufsidīna

They are prohibited from corruption and destructive conduct on earth.

Technical ability should therefore be distinguished from its possessors’ ethical conduct.

Strong houses, architectural skill, and extensive resources do not themselves prove corruption. Equally, they do not establish integrity and responsibility.

The verse calls for remembering blessings and avoiding corruption, rather than abandoning construction skills.

B. Al-Ḥijr 15:82: carved houses and security

وَكَانُوا يَنْحِتُونَ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا آمِنِينَ

wa-kānū yanḥitūna mina al-jibāli buyūtan āminīna

Translation:

“And they used to carve houses from the mountains, while secure.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:82

Yanḥitūna is a third-person plural imperfect accompanied by kānū.

Together they describe an established or continuing activity in the past.

Mina al-jibāli identifies the material or place from which the stone is removed; buyūtan gives the result, houses emerging within the mountain.

Āminīna, from ʾ M N, describes their condition.

It does not define naḥt. They carved houses while secure, or considering themselves secure.

Secure from what?

Interpretations include protection against collapse, natural hazards, enemies, or other dangers.

Houses carved within rock may, under some conditions, be more robust than lightweight structures.

The verse does not explicitly identify the danger.

The translation should therefore not add “from the mountain collapsing,” “from enemy attacks,” or “from death.”

“Secure” suffices for the basic rendering; identifying security’s limits requires contextual examination.

Did their security last?

The following verses say:

فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الصَّيْحَةُ مُصْبِحِينَ

fa-akhadhathumu al-ṣayḥatu muṣbiḥīna

“Then the tremendous cry seized them at morning.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:83

فَمَا أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُمْ مَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ

fa-mā aghnā ʿanhum mā kānū yaksibūna

“Then what they used to acquire availed them nothing.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:84

Their security and resources did not prevent the outcome reported by the Qur’an.

That conclusion should not enter the lexical meaning of naḥt.

Naḥt concerns carving and construction; the inability to avert punishment comes from the story as a whole.

Nor does the verse say the houses necessarily collapsed during the event. That detail should remain outside the text.

C. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149: carved houses and fārihīn

وَتَنْحِتُونَ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا فَارِهِينَ

wa-tanḥitūna mina al-jibāli buyūtan fārihīna

Translation:

“And you carve houses from the mountains, being skilled in this work.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149

The construction resembles Al-Ḥijr 15:82, but its final description differs.

Al-Ḥijr 15:82 has āminīna.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 has fārihīna.

Fārihīna comes from F R H and can mean skill, proficiency, and capability.

Another recitation has farihīna without alif. Some lexical analyses connect this with elation mingled with pride, exuberance, or self-admiration.

The two forms have been explained differently. Some consider their senses close; others distinguish skill from pride.

Tanḥitūna nevertheless retains the carving sense in both recitations.

The skill reading foregrounds their proficiency in carving houses.

The pride or self-admiration reading gives greater attention to their attitude while enjoying these resources.

Context and pride

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:147 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148 mention gardens, springs, cultivated land, and palms.

The mountain houses are then recalled.

Ṣāliḥ subsequently says:

فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُونِ

fa-ittaqū allāha wa-aṭīʿūni

“So be mindful of The God and obey me.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:150

And continues:

وَلَا تُطِيعُوا أَمْرَ الْمُسْرِفِينَ

wa-lā tuṭīʿū amra al-musrifīna

“And do not obey the command of those who exceed bounds.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:151

الَّذِينَ يُفْسِدُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا يُصْلِحُونَ

alladhīna yufsidūna fī al-arḍi wa-lā yuṣliḥūna

“Those who cause corruption on earth and do not set things right.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:152

Resources and construction ability appear alongside ethical responsibility.

Even with the skill rendering of fārihīn, the context shows that technical proficiency is insufficient for integrity.

If pride is preferred, it too comes from the description and context, rather than naḥt itself.

Naḥt names an action and skill; pride, corruption, or proficiency must be identified through other words and people’s conduct.

Do carved houses necessarily indicate luxury and excess?

Al-Shuʿarāʾ prohibits following the excessive and corrupt, but this does not make every stone house or large building inherently excessive or corrupt.

Excess and corruption concern conduct, resource use, others’ rights, and the consequences of actions.

A technically advanced building may meet real needs. Construction may also involve violating rights, wasting resources, or displaying power.

Evidence from each situation must establish the distinction.

Thamūd’s story condemns corrupt conduct and rejection of Ṣāliḥ’s message, rather than declaring the carving of stone houses itself forbidden or intrinsically reprehensible.

4. Abraham: a carved object that is worshipped

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95: a-taʿbudūna mā tanḥitūna

قَالَ أَتَعْبُدُونَ مَا تَنْحِتُونَ

qāla a-taʿbudūna mā tanḥitūna

Translation:

“He said: Do you worship what you yourselves carve?”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95

This is the fourth and final Qur’anic occurrence.

The first three concern carving houses; this one concerns objects Abraham’s people carved and then worshipped.

The preceding words and actions of Abraham identify the subject.

He asks his father and people what they worship, then discusses the objects of worship they have chosen apart from God.

He subsequently turns toward them and asks:

أَلَا تَأْكُلُونَ

a-lā taʾkulūna

“Will you not eat?”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:91

مَا لَكُمْ لَا تَنْطِقُونَ

mā lakum lā tanṭiqūna

“What is wrong with you that you do not speak?”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:92

The Qur’an then describes Abraham striking them and his people confronting him.

A-taʿbudūna mā tanḥitūna occurs within that setting.

Mā tanḥitūna therefore denotes objects the people have placed in the position of a deity.

A. The meaning of mā tanḥitūna

Mā can naturally be a relative pronoun: “what” or “that which.”

Tanḥitūna is a second-person plural imperfect verb.

Its object is not overtly expressed because it refers back to mā: “what you carve.”

The sentence thus means:

“Do you worship something you yourselves have carved?”

Two distinct actions appear together:

First, carving and shaping material.

Second, worshipping the result.

Naḥt concerns the first action; worship comes from the independent verb taʿbudūna.

This distinction matters: directly making naḥt “idol-making” would restrict its range to one instance.

The carved objects here are worshipped, but neither Arabic generally nor the other Qur’anic passages restricts naḥt to making idols.

B. What does Abraham challenge?

He challenges people shaping an object with their own hands and abilities and then treating it as a deity.

The material existed before human shaping. People can alter its form, but that ability does not establish divine power or status for the resulting object.

The objection is therefore more than a matter of wood versus stone: it concerns turning a human product into an object of worship.

This follows from the whole exchange.

In the three Thamūd verses, carving houses was reported as an ability they possessed.

In Abraham’s story, the same technical action produces something people worship.

The ethical distinction comes from people’s relationship with what they make, rather than a change in naḥt’s meaning.

C. Wa-allāhu khalaqakum wa-mā taʿmalūna

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:96 says:

وَاللَّهُ خَلَقَكُمْ وَمَا تَعْمَلُونَ

wa-allāhu khalaqakum wa-mā taʿmalūna

Translation following the relative-pronoun reading:

“And The God created you and what you make.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:96

The sentence continues Abraham’s argument.

Taʿmalūna comes from ʿ M L. In this study’s preferred relative reading, mā taʿmalūna concerns the objects made for worship; in the verbal-noun reading, the action itself is the sentence’s subject matter.

Two established grammatical analyses of mā exist.

On the relative reading, mā means “what”: God created you and what you make.

On the verbal-noun reading, the expression can mean “God created you and your work.”

The second received attention in later theological debates, particularly the creation of human actions.

This study prefers the relative reading for its clear connection with the preceding sentence, which concerns objects carved and worshipped. That preference does not make the verbal-noun analysis linguistically impossible.

The argument then concerns people worshipping what they shape, although they and the material in their products are God’s creations.

This should not deny people’s actual role in making objects: the preceding verse itself attributes carving to them.

Equally, shaping material does not make a person an absolute, independent creator.

The broader relationship between divine creation and human actions is a separate question; later theological theories should not determine N Ḥ T’s lexical meaning.

Study of ʿ M L

D. Does this verse prohibit sculpture itself?

Abraham’s question in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95 concerns worshipping what his people carve.

The sentence does not ask, “Why do you carve anything?”

It asks:

أَتَعْبُدُونَ مَا تَنْحِتُونَ

a-taʿbudūna mā tanḥitūna

“Do you worship what you carve?”

Neither naḥt’s lexical meaning nor this question establishes an absolute prohibition of every kind of sculpture, stone carving, or figurative art.

Conversely, the verse does not authorize every use of figures or statues. Particular uses require their own evidence and principles.

What the verse clearly challenges is worshipping an object shaped by people themselves.

Sabaʾ 34:13 helps clarify the distinction: tamāthīl are included among things made for Solomon.

The ruling or function of every kind of tamāthīl is a separate subject. Here it suffices to distinguish carving from worshipping the result.

5. Linguistic constructions and related roots

The basic triliteral verb and Qur’anic constructions

All four Qur’anic occurrences come from basic triliteral naḥata.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:74, tanḥitūna accompanies al-jibāla: the worked material is directly mentioned, while buyūtan identifies the intended result.

Al-Ḥijr 15:82 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 use mina al-jibāli, identifying the material or location from which the houses are created.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95, mā identifies the resulting object, but its material is not explicitly named.

The verb can thus connect grammatically with either the material carved or the resulting object.

A different preposition cannot establish absolute rules about house dimensions, removed stone quantities, or tools.

N Ḥ T (نَحَتَ) and Ḥ F R (حَفَرَ): carving and digging

Arabic Ḥ F R concerns digging and creating a cavity.

This overlaps with some naḥt uses: carving stone can create empty space.

Digging does not necessarily foreground artistic shaping or smoothing. Someone may dig a well or pit without aiming at a precise form.

Naḥt more strongly foregrounds carving and reshaping material.

The distinction is a tendency, however, rather than a rule that every excavation lacks refinement and every carving is artistic.

Thamūd’s stone houses can involve both removing rock to create space and shaping it for use as a house.

The actual word chosen in the three verses is nevertheless naḥt.

Study of Ḥ F R

N Ḥ T (نَحَتَ) and J W B (جَابَ): carving and cutting rock

Al-Fajr says:

وَثَمُودَ الَّذِينَ جَابُوا الصَّخْرَ بِالْوَادِ

wa-thamūda alladhīna jābū al-ṣakhra bi-al-wādi

Translation:

“And Thamūd, who cut the rock in the valley.”

Al-Fajr 89:9

This uses J W B.

Applied to stone, it concerns cutting, cleaving, and making a cut through material.

Since the passage concerns Thamūd, it can be examined beside the three house-carving verses.

The verbs are not wholly identical.

Jābū al-ṣakhra foregrounds cutting or cleaving rock, while tanḥitūna mina al-jibāli buyūtan also states a construction result: houses carved from mountains.

The expressions can describe related aspects of technical ability without establishing a historical sequence, such as cutting rocks first and carving houses years later.

Jābū belongs to J W B and should not be listed as an N Ḥ T derivative merely because their contexts are close.

Study of J W B

N Ḥ T (نَحَتَ) and N J R (نَجَرَ): carving and carpentry

Arabic najr concerns cutting and shaping wood.

This proximity explains why naḥt is sometimes explained through najr.

Najr is especially familiar in carpentry and preparing wood, while naḥt also concerns stone and other hard materials.

“Carving” is consequently a more general equivalent for naḥt.

“Doing carpentry” would be inappropriate in the mountain-house verses, whose material is stone and mountains.

N Ḥ T (نَحَتَ) and Ṣ N ʿ (صَنَعَ): carving and making

Ṣ N ʿ concerns making, producing, and preparing something through work and skill.

Its Qur’anic uses include various activities, among them building a ship.

Hūd 11:37 says:

وَاصْنَعِ الْفُلْكَ بِأَعْيُنِنَا وَوَحْيِنَا

wa-iṣnaʿi al-fulka bi-aʿyuninā wa-waḥyinā

Translation:

“And build the ship under Our eyes and by Our revelation.”

Ṣanaʿa expresses shipbuilding, which can include cutting, carving, joining, and preparing components.

Naḥt specifies a narrower method: shaping by carving and removing material.

In construction, naḥt can be a method of making, but not all making is carving. Wear and other lexical branches must also be recognized separately.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:96 likewise distinguish a specific method from a more general action: tanḥitūna specifies carving, while taʿmalūna concerns work. The latter phrase’s exact role depends on the analysis of mā.

Study of Ṣ N ʿ

N Ḥ T (نَحَتَ) and N Q Sh (نَقَشَ): carving and engraving

Arabic naqsh concerns making a mark, image, or design on a surface.

Removing part of stone or wood can create a design.

Engraving and carving can therefore coincide.

Naḥt is nevertheless not restricted to surface images or decoration. Thamūd’s houses involve creating space within mountains, beyond marking a rock’s surface.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95 likewise concerns carved objects that are worshipped, without specifying their designs, dimensions, or shapes.

Those outward details should not be extracted from naḥt itself.

6. Interpretive questions and later usage

Linguistic naḥt: forming a word from several words

Arabic linguistic terminology also uses naḥt for forming a new word from portions of two or more words.

Morphological discussions, for example, explain basmala through combining and shortening components of بِسْمِ اللَّهِ.

Parts of existing words are taken to produce a new linguistic form.

This intelligibly relates to material carving: both involve selecting portions of existing material to form something.

The resemblance does not make Qur’anic tanḥitūna concern word formation or combining expressions.

All four Qur’anic passages concern carving material and making actual objects.

The linguistic term alone also cannot prove that every shortened Arabic form was known by this same technical term before the Qur’an.

Does Qur’anic naḥt mean creation?

Naḥt concerns shaping material already present.

Thamūd’s mountains and rock exist before people reshape them through work.

Abraham’s people likewise carve things and then worship them.

Naḥt therefore does not mean creating from nonexistence.

This does not establish that khalaqa invariably means creation from nothing in every Arabic and Qur’anic use. Kh L Q requires its own study.

Here it suffices to identify naḥt as work upon existing material that changes its form.

Study of Kh L Q

Is naḥt intrinsically positive or negative?

The four Qur’anic occurrences clarify this question.

Thamūd’s house carving shows technical ability and use of earthly resources.

Abraham’s story concerns making objects people treat as deities.

N Ḥ T therefore has no fixed ethical value by itself.

It cannot invariably mean idol-making, nor does the carver’s skill make everything carved praiseworthy.

Ethical evaluation depends on an object’s use, human conduct, and the verse’s context.

Thamūd’s corruption, disobedience, and violation of divine limits are condemned.

Abraham challenges worship of human products.

None of these is the direct meaning of naḥt.

Technical ability does not guarantee the final outcome

Reading the Thamūd verses together also suggests a broader point.

They could carve mountain houses and use earthly resources, abilities that could organize life and provide greater security.

The same society nevertheless faced corruption, rejection of Ṣāliḥ’s message, and disobedience to divine commands.

Technical and engineering advancement alone is therefore not the ultimate measure of a society’s integrity.

Conversely, the Qur’an does not present technical progress as intrinsically causing corruption.

The concern is how people use abilities and resources, with responsibility and respect for others’ rights.

This conclusion comes from the story as a whole, rather than naḥt’s lexical meaning alone.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Carving,” corresponding to Persian and Dari tarāshīdan, is a natural basic equivalent.

When reshaping material is prominent, “shaping by carving” can be used, without repeating that explanation in every verse.

Naḥata al-khashaba can be “he carved the wood.”

Naḥata al-jabala can be “he carved the mountain,” or contextually “he carved a space within the mountain.”

Nuḥātah can be “shavings” or “chips removed in carving.”

Minḥat names a carving tool; naḥītah applied to a person concerns disposition or nature.

In the four Qur’anic verses, render the verb as consistently as the construction permits.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74

وَتَنْحِتُونَ الْجِبَالَ بُيُوتًا

wa-tanḥitūna al-jibāla buyūtan

Proposal:

“And you carve the mountains into houses.”

This preserves the construction’s difference from the other two passages: the mountains follow the verb directly.

Al-Ḥijr 15:82

وَكَانُوا يَنْحِتُونَ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا آمِنِينَ

wa-kānū yanḥitūna mina al-jibāli buyūtan āminīna

Proposal:

“And they used to carve houses from the mountains, while secure.”

Security remains distinct from carving.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149

وَتَنْحِتُونَ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا فَارِهِينَ

wa-tanḥitūna mina al-jibāli buyūtan fārihīna

Proposal:

“And you carve houses from the mountains, being skilled in this work.”

The alternative recitation farihīna and possible pride or self-admiration should be examined separately, rather than silently merged into the basic translation.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95

قَالَ أَتَعْبُدُونَ مَا تَنْحِتُونَ

qāla a-taʿbudūna mā tanḥitūna

Proposal:

“He said: Do you worship what you yourselves carve?”

This preserves both carving and worshipping.

“Do you worship the idols you make?” is contextually intelligible, but “idols” is not explicit in mā tanḥitūna; the preceding verses identify the objects.

For Forqan, explain that identification in the commentary while keeping the basic translation close to the wording.

8. Conclusion

Arabic N Ḥ T concerns carving and shaping material by cutting and removing portions.

Material uses include wood, stone, and other hard substances. Nuḥātah for shavings and minḥat for a carving tool clarify this field.

Other branches concern travel wear, human disposition, and certain social relationships. They should not be reduced without historical evidence to one physical or social meaning.

The Qur’an uses the root four times, always as a basic triliteral imperfect verb.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74, Al-Ḥijr 15:82, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 concern houses Thamūd carved in mountains.

Naḥt there describes technical ability in shaping rock; security, skill, pride, and corruption come from other words and context.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95 applies the same verb to objects Abraham’s people carved and worshipped.

The comparison shows that carving is the root’s meaning; the result’s value and status depend on human use.

Mountain houses are not inherently condemned in Thamūd’s story. Abraham likewise challenges worship of a human-shaped object, rather than the ability to carve itself.

Neither idol-making, artistic creation, nor advanced engineering is the root’s necessary general meaning.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N Ḥ T means carving, cutting, and shaping material by removing portions. The work may smooth a surface, produce a tool or figure, or create space such as a house within rock.

Qur’anic tanḥitūna is a second-person plural imperfect: “you carve.” Yanḥitūna is third-person plural: “they carve.” Its combination with kānū in Al-Ḥijr 15:82 yields the past sense “they used to carve.”

The Thamūd passages concern mountain houses; Abraham’s story concerns carved objects people worshipped.

“Carving and shaping by removing material” is therefore a defensible core. The final shape, purpose, and ethical value must come from the object, other sentence components, and context.
