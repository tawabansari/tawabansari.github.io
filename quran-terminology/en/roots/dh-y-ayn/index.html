Becoming known and spreading; circulating and bringing to others’ knowledge; disclosing news or a secret

The Arabic root DH Y ʿ concerns something becoming apparent and spreading, especially when news, speech, or a matter extends beyond its initial circle and becomes known among people. In this family, adhāʿa means publishing or disclosing, idhāʿah names dissemination, and midhyāʿ describes someone who does not keep news or secrets to himself.

A significant distinction separates spreading from causing something to spread. Dhāʿa al-khabaru means “the news spread”; adhāʿa fulānun al-khabara means “someone circulated the news.” The first describes news spreading; the second attributes dissemination to an agent.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as adhāʿū. Al-Nisāʾ 4:83 describes people who circulate a report concerning security or fear when it reaches them. Had they referred it to the Messenger and those entrusted with authority among them, those capable of drawing out and examining its meaning would have known it.

The verse thus concerns more than speaking or conveying news. Its subject is circulation of a report about security or danger, where proper understanding, assessment, and handling matter.

Idhāʿah itself, however, does not lexically mean only lying, spreading a baseless rumor, or intending deception. A report may be entirely true yet harmful if circulated thoughtlessly or at the wrong time. An incorrect report can likewise spread widely and lead people to mistaken decisions and responses.

The lexical meaning of dissemination must therefore be distinguished from the report’s truth, the disseminator’s intention, and the consequences of circulation.

1. Core Meaning and Forms of the Root

Dhuyūʿ: becoming apparent and spreading

The root’s semantic center can be expressed as follows:

أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى إِظْهَارِ الشَّيْءِ وَظُهُورِهِ وَانْتِشَارِهِ

aṣlun yadullu ʿalā iẓhāri al-shayʾi wa-ẓuhūrihi wa-intishārih

That is: making something apparent, its becoming apparent, and its spreading.

This definition includes three connected aspects: something previously known within a limited circle becomes apparent; others learn of it; and knowledge of it extends further.

Arabic expressions include:

«ذَاعَ الْخَبَرُ»: the news spread.

«ذَاعَ السِّرُّ»: the secret became known.

«أَذَاعَ الْخَبَرَ»: he circulated the news.

«أَذَاعَ السِّرَّ»: he disclosed the secret.

«أَذَاعَ بِالْأَمْرِ»: he made the matter known and circulated it among people.

«رَجُلٌ مِذْيَاعٌ»: someone who does not keep a secret or report to himself, but circulates it.

Becoming apparent and spreading knowledge are central to these expressions. Nevertheless, what spreads must be distinguished from the person who spreads it.

The basic verb dhāʿa yadhīʿu usually describes news or a matter spreading. Recorded verbal nouns include dhayʿ, dhuyūʿ, dhayaʿān, and dhayʿūʿah.

Adhāʿa yudhīʿu is Form IV, with the verbal noun idhāʿah.

In this form’s established use, dissemination is attributed to a subject who conveys news or a matter to others.

Form IV alone does not establish deception as the aim, speed of dissemination, or an exceptionally large audience. These features must come from the sentence and circumstances.

Midhyāʿ: someone who does not keep secrets

Early Arabic uses midhyāʿ for someone who readily spreads news or secrets and lacks the necessary restraint in keeping them.

Its plural is madhāyīʿ.

An explanation of midhyāʿ uses «لَا يَسْتَطِيعُ كِتْمَانَ شَيْءٍ»: someone unable to keep anything concealed.

This use shows that idhāʿah can involve disclosing something meant to remain in a person’s keeping.

Midhyāʿ alone does not prove that the person lies. He may circulate a true report while failing to honor a trust or observe appropriate conditions of disclosure.

Conversely, someone who informs others accurately and at the proper time is not blameworthy merely for circulating news.

Dissemination in this family should therefore not be identified inherently with betrayal or falsehood. The conduct’s value depends on the report, the person’s responsibilities, and the circumstances.

2. Early Arabic Usage: From Disclosing Secrets to Dispersing Traces

A poetic witness: a secret made public

Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī, a poet and scholar of the early Islamic period, is credited with these lines about revealing a secret:

أَمِنْتُ عَلَى السِّرِّ امْرَأً غَيْرَ حَازِمٍ
وَلَكِنَّهُ فِي الْوُدِّ غَيْرُ مُرِيبِ

amintu ʿalā al-sirri imraʾan ghayra ḥāzimin / wa-lākinnahu fī al-wuddi ghayru murīb

أَذَاعَ بِهِ فِي النَّاسِ حَتَّى كَأَنَّهُ
بِعَلْيَاءَ نَارٌ أُوقِدَتْ بِثَقُوبِ

adhāʿa bihi fī al-nāsi ḥattā ka-annahu / bi-ʿalyāʾa nārun ūqidat bi-thaqūb

Translation: I entrusted a secret to someone lacking firmness and discretion, although I had no misgivings about his friendship. He spread it among people as though it were a fire kindled with wood on a height.

Adhāʿa bihi here means revealing a secret and conveying it to others.

The poet compares the report’s spread to a fire lit on high ground. As flames on a height are visible from afar, the secret leaves its original circle and becomes apparent to others.

The comparison matters for understanding idhāʿah. It involves more than telling something to one other person: circulated speech may quickly become widely known and escape its original owner’s control.

The lines have variant transmissions. Some give ghayra kātimin instead of ghayra ḥāzimin; the second half also has variants such as fī al-wudd and fī al-nuṣḥ.

These lines are transmitted under Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī’s name with variant readings. Lexical study should preserve those variants without inferring a securely pre-Qur’anic attribution.

Abū al-Aswad lived in the Islamic period. These lines thus illustrate early Islamic Arabic, rather than poetry that can securely be identified as pre-Islamic.

Despite that limitation, adhāʿa bihi is clear in the verse and relates directly to the same construction in Al-Nisāʾ 4:83.

Dissemination beyond news

Although DH Y ʿ is especially associated with news and speech, Arabic dictionaries also record uses beyond circulating information.

For example, dhāʿa al-jawru means injustice spread, while dhāʿa al-jarabu fī al-jildi describes a skin disease spreading across the skin.

Here, the point is more than people learning a report: a condition itself manifests across a wider area.

Adhāʿa bi-al-shayʾi can also concern carrying something off or removing its traces.

An early poetic witness states:

رَبْعٌ قَوَاءٌ أَذَاعَ الْمُعْصِرَاتُ بِهِ
وَكُلُّ حَيْرَانَ سَارٍ مَاؤُهُ خَضِلُ

rabʿun qawāʾun adhāʿa al-muʿṣirātu bihi / wa-kullu ḥayrāna sārin māʾuhu khaḍil

Translation: A deserted dwelling place whose traces rain-bearing clouds or winds, and every wandering, rain-laden night cloud, have dispersed and altered.

Adhāʿa … bihi here concerns the traces of an abandoned dwelling.

Lexical explanations take it as the dwelling’s traces being removed by wind and rain. The use therefore concerns dispersal and erasure of material remains, rather than circulating news.

The verse is also attributed to ʿUmar ibn Abī Rabīʿah, but the attribution is not uniform and it does not appear in every collection of his poetry. It cannot therefore be treated confidently as pre-Qur’anic evidence or assigned conclusively to one poet.

Its significance is the attestation of a material use of adhāʿa in the Arabic lexical and literary tradition.

A rare use concerning water

Some dictionaries also record adhāʿa al-qawmu mā fī al-ḥawḍ: the people drank all the water in the trough, leaving none.

This concerns the water supply disappearing from its original place.

The limited use does not establish consuming or exhausting something as the primary Qur’anic meaning of idhāʿah.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:83 concerns news and people’s knowledge. Spreading and making known therefore fit its construction and context.

Early Arabic sources thus attest two related fields: news or a condition becoming apparent and spreading; and, in some limited uses, the dispersal or disappearance of traces and supplies.

The witnesses examined did not yield a securely attributed pre-Qur’anic verse for this root. Islamic-period evidence and uncertain attributions should therefore not be assigned to pre-Islamic poets without qualification.

3. Adhāʿū bihi: The Root’s Sole Qur’anic Use

Al-Nisāʾ 4:83 states:

وَإِذَا جَاءَهُمْ أَمْرٌ مِنَ الْأَمْنِ أَوِ الْخَوْفِ أَذَاعُوا بِهِ وَلَوْ رَدُّوهُ إِلَى الرَّسُولِ وَإِلَىٰ أُولِي الْأَمْرِ مِنْهُمْ لَعَلِمَهُ الَّذِينَ يَسْتَنْبِطُونَهُ مِنْهُمْ وَلَوْلَا فَضْلُ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ وَرَحْمَتُهُ لَاتَّبَعْتُمُ الشَّيْطَانَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

wa-idhā jāʾahum amrun mina al-amni awi al-khawfi adhāʿū bihi wa-law raddūhu ilā al-rasūli wa-ilā ulī al-amri minhum la-ʿalimahu alladhīna yastanbiṭūnahu minhum wa-lawlā faḍlu allāhi ʿalaykum wa-raḥmatuhu la-ttabaʿtumu al-shayṭāna illā qalīlā

Translation: When a report concerning security or fear reaches them, they circulate it. Had they referred it to the Messenger and those entrusted with authority among them, those among them who draw out its meaning would have known it. Were it not for The God’s bounty and mercy upon you, you would have followed Satan, except a few.

Adhāʿū here is a Form IV perfect verb with a third person masculine plural subject.

Bihi consists of the preposition bāʾ and a masculine singular pronoun. The pronoun refers to amrun: the matter or report concerning security or fear.

Adhāʿū bihi therefore means circulating that matter among others.

Does bāʾ change the verb’s meaning?

Arabic records both adhāʿa al-khabara and adhāʿa bi-al-khabar.

One syntactic analysis treats bāʾ in adhaʿtu bihi as structurally additional, with a meaning close to adhaʿtuhu. This explains the verb’s construction rather than claiming the preposition serves no purpose in the sentence.

Both constructions, with and without bāʾ, are recorded for disclosing news and secrets.

The presence of bāʾ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:83 therefore does not establish a particular medium, manner, or speed of dissemination.

Nor does bāʾ itself indicate that the news is false.

The clear sense is making apparent and circulating the very matter that reached them.

“A matter of security or fear”: what kind of report?

At the verse’s beginning, amrun mina al-amni awi al-khawf specifies the subject of circulation.

Amr here denotes a matter or report concerning security or fear that has reached people.

The verse therefore does not treat every ordinary utterance or daily report alike. It concerns information connected with security, danger, and anxiety.

In the verse’s immediate historical setting, such a report could concern victory, defeat, a group’s security, troop movements, or a threat facing people.

The phrase itself, however, names no particular battle or event.

Amrun mina al-amni awi al-khawf should therefore neither be restricted to military news nor stripped of its setting of security and danger.

Reports about security and fear can affect decisions and conduct. Prematurely announcing victory may create unfounded confidence; news of danger may cause confusion; and disclosing sensitive information may benefit those intending harm.

These are possible consequences of dissemination, rather than separate meanings of adhāʿū itself.

Was the report necessarily false?

Nothing in adhāʿū bihi itself proves the report incorrect.

The circulated news may be false, incomplete, distorted, or not yet examined. Its substance may also be true, while the timing or manner of circulation is inappropriate.

Idhāʿah here should therefore not simply be translated as spreading lies.

The distinction matters. Inventing or conveying falsehood concerns a report’s truth and honesty; inappropriate circulation of true information can instead concern responsibility and consequences.

Conversely, not every report should be judged improper or forbidden merely because its subject is important.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:83 criticizes people who circulate a matter without following an appropriate process of examination and referral.

News circulation and the preceding verses

Al-Nisāʾ 4:81 describes people professing obedience, some of whom then leave and plan contrary to the Messenger’s words and their own profession of obedience.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:82 calls people to reflect carefully on the Qur’an.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:83 then raises the circulation of reports concerning security or fear.

This sequence places verse 83 within a setting of trust, collective conduct, knowledge, and responsibility.

Adhāʿū itself, however, does not establish that every disseminator was a hypocrite or deliberate liar.

Some early explanations identify the group as hypocrites; others also mention inexperienced people or those weak in assessing a report’s consequences.

None of these inward characteristics is part of idhāʿah’s lexical meaning.

Identifying people and assessing their responsibility requires attention to the full context, their conduct, and other evidence.

4. Dissemination Contrasted with Examination and Inference

Al-Nisāʾ 4:83 continues:

وَلَوْ رَدُّوهُ إِلَى الرَّسُولِ وَإِلَىٰ أُولِي الْأَمْرِ مِنْهُمْ لَعَلِمَهُ الَّذِينَ يَسْتَنْبِطُونَهُ مِنْهُمْ

wa-law raddūhu ilā al-rasūli wa-ilā ulī al-amri minhum la-ʿalimahu alladhīna yastanbiṭūnahu minhum

Translation: Had they referred it to the Messenger and those entrusted with authority among them, those among them who draw out its meaning would have known it.

The passage sets a different response against immediate circulation.

Instead of spreading a matter among people as soon as it arrives, it is referred to those responsible and able to examine it.

Raddūhu comes from R D D and means referring the matter back.

Yastanbiṭūnahu comes from N B Ṭ; here it concerns drawing out and understanding a matter’s truth or implication through examination.

Study of N B Ṭ

Neither word is part of idhāʿah’s meaning. They clarify the appropriate alternative to unconsidered dissemination within the sentence.

Hearing a report is not the same as understanding its truth

A report may reach many people without all of them knowing its accuracy, source, or implications.

Someone able to examine it can understand it more precisely through knowledge of circumstances and evidence.

That distinction matters in Al-Nisāʾ 4:83, which contrasts spreading news with referring it for examination.

The verse itself speaks of istinbāṭ. An entire modern research process or administrative and media system should not be presented without evidence as the word’s direct meaning.

The relationship between the clauses nevertheless shows that having a report and being able to judge it correctly are different things.

Does the verse require every report to remain concealed?

Al-Nisāʾ 4:83 does not establish that every report concerning security or danger must remain hidden forever.

It speaks of referring the matter to those able to draw out and examine its meaning. More than preventing circulation is involved: sound understanding and responsible handling also matter.

In some circumstances, informing people of danger may be necessary to protect their lives and security. In others, circulating unverified or sensitive information may harm them.

What should be published, when, and to whom depend on the report’s nature, its reliability, and actual circumstances.

This should not become unlimited authority for individuals or governments to conceal truth, prevent reasoned criticism, or silence reliable accounts of injustice.

Just as irresponsible dissemination may harm people, deliberately concealing truth may violate their rights.

The verse therefore should not yield only a principle of silence. It concerns responsibility in understanding and circulating information, rather than an absolute prohibition on reporting news.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

DH Y ʿ (dhāʿa) and SH Y ʿ (shāʿa): spreading and becoming widespread

DH Y ʿ and SH Y ʿ are close in semantic range.

Dhāʿa al-khabaru means the news spread; shāʿa al-khabaru can likewise mean it circulated and became known among people.

Al-Nūr 24:19 uses SH Y ʿ for indecency spreading among believers.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:83, adhāʿū bihi emphasizes disseminating a matter that reached people.

The difference between these verses is clear, but it should not become an absolute boundary whereby DH Y ʿ always means deliberate circulation and SH Y ʿ always spontaneous spread.

Both Arabic families can concern something spreading and becoming widespread.

Modern Persian shāyeʿah usually means an unverified or false report. Early Arabic shāʿa and its derivatives, however, were not necessarily restricted to falsehood.

DH Y ʿ (dhāʿa) and N SH R (nashara): spreading and unfolding

Study of N SH R

Arabic nashr has a broad field that includes opening, spreading out, and dispersal.

The Qur’an uses it in different situations, including expansion, dispersal, and resurrection.

The Qur’anic occurrence of DH Y ʿ, however, concerns disseminating news.

Nashr can thus concern material objects and varied conditions, while adhāʿū bihi in Al-Nisāʾ 4:83 specifically concerns information and people’s knowledge.

This distinction comes from the uses under examination, rather than DH Y ʿ being confined to news throughout Arabic.

DH Y ʿ (dhāʿa) and B TH TH (baththa): spreading and dispersing

Study of B TH TH

Arabic bathth concerns dispersing and spreading, and can apply to people, creatures, and news.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:1 uses baththa for the spread of men and women from one pair.

Some Arabic uses of baththa al-khabara also mean circulating news.

The roots therefore overlap in conveying information, while Qur’anic bathth also has uses beyond transmitting reports.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:83, adhāʿū focuses on disseminating the matter that reached the group in question.

DH Y ʿ (dhāʿa) and K T M (katama): dissemination and concealing truth

Study of K T M

Kitmān means keeping something concealed, and can stand opposite making it apparent or circulating it.

Al-Baqarah 2:42 prohibits mixing truth with falsehood and knowingly concealing the truth.

However, concealment and withholding publication are not always the same situation.

Someone may wait to circulate a report while it is examined, to honor a trust or prevent harm. This is not necessarily blameworthy concealment of truth.

Conversely, someone may knowingly hide a truth they are responsible for revealing.

Just as circulating information is not inherently virtuous, refraining from circulation is not inherently sinful or virtuous either.

In both cases, the report’s truth, personal responsibility, and circumstances matter.

DH Y ʿ (dhāʿa) and N B ʾ (nabaʾ): a report and its circulation

Study of N B ʾ

Arabic nabaʾ concerns news and information about a matter.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6 discusses receiving a report and the need to examine it under the stated conditions.

Idhāʿah, however, concerns the act of circulating and conveying a matter to others.

Receiving, examining, and disseminating news are thus distinct.

A report may be received without yet being circulated; it may also spread rapidly before its reliability is properly examined.

This distinction matters for Al-Nisāʾ 4:83: the verse begins with a matter arriving, then describes its recipients’ act of dissemination.

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

The root’s sole Qur’anic form is adhāʿū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:83.

“They circulated,” “they spread,” and “they disclosed” are defensible Arabic equivalents.

Given the report’s subject of security or fear, “they circulate it” is natural and clear.

Forqan’s current rendering is:

“When a report concerning security or fear reaches them, they circulate it.”

This is defensible for adhāʿū bihi.

“They disclose it” may also fit some circumstances, but the Persian equivalent tends to suggest prior confidentiality.

The verse does not explicitly state that the report was officially or expressly confidential. “They circulate it” therefore provides a suitable breadth of meaning.

“They spread rumors” may fit some instances of the criticized conduct, but as a direct translation risks presupposing that the report is false or baseless.

“They lie” likewise does not translate the verb: adhāʿū concerns dissemination rather than judging the report’s truth within that word itself.

The perfect adhāʿū occurs in a construction beginning with wa-idhā jāʾahum and describing recurrent conduct. A present-tense Persian rendering, corresponding to “they circulate,” is therefore natural and fits the sentence.

No change to Forqan’s current equivalent appears necessary. The danger of thoughtless circulation, responsibility to examine information, and distinction from concealing truth belong in the verse’s interpretation.

7. Conclusion

Arabic DH Y ʿ concerns something becoming apparent and spreading. Form I dhāʿa describes a report or matter spreading and becoming widespread; adhāʿa attributes making it apparent and disseminating it to an agent.

Early Arabic evidence attests the family in circulating news, disclosing secrets, and some extended material uses.

Early midhyāʿ also describes someone who does not keep news or secrets to himself, without that description alone proving him a liar.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as adhāʿū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:83.

The verse describes people circulating a matter concerning security or fear as soon as it arrives. Referring it to the Messenger and those entrusted with authority, and examination by people able to draw out its meaning, could instead have enabled better understanding and more responsible handling.

This does not establish that every circulated report is false or every dissemination prohibited. The criticism emerges from the group’s circumstances and conduct.

Nor is withholding immediate publication identical with knowingly concealing truth. Both must be assessed in light of truth, responsibility, and consequences.

Final definition: In its principal use, DH Y ʿ concerns something becoming apparent and spreading, and knowledge of a report or matter extending to others. Adhāʿa means circulating or disclosing it; midhyāʿ describes someone who readily spreads news or secrets.

Truth or falsehood, confidentiality, the disseminator’s intention, speed, and ethical value are not fixed components of the root’s meaning. They must be inferred from the report’s subject, the circumstances of dissemination, and the context.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:83, adhāʿū bihi is properly rendered “they circulated it” or, within the recurrent conditional construction, “they circulate it.” The verse’s central concern is responsibility for disseminating a report affecting security, fear, and others’ decisions.
