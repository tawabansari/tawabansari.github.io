Cutting, separating, and ending continuity: from harvesting and severing ties to firm resolve and the loss of one’s produce

The Arabic root Ṣ R M concerns cutting, separating, and ending something’s continuity. Ṣarama can describe cutting a rope, picking fruit from a tree, severing human relationships, or the ending of a period. The family also includes ṣārim, cutting or decisive; ṣarām, harvesting; and ṣarīm, something cut off or separated from its original whole.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an, all in Al-Qalam: la-yaṣrimunnahā in Al-Qalam 68:17, al-ṣarīmi in Al-Qalam 68:20, and ṣārimīna in Al-Qalam 68:22.

All three belong to the account of the garden owners, who decided to harvest their garden in the morning. Before they reached it, however, the garden suffered damage and their expected produce was lost.

A fundamental point is that cutting does not always mean destroying. Cutting may harvest produce, separate something from a whole, sever a bond, or end an undertaking. The object and context determine its result and value.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three occurrences in three forms, all in Al-Qalam

1. The semantic core: cutting and separating

Severing continuity

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الصَّادُ وَالرَّاءُ وَالْمِيمُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ صَحِيحٌ مُطَّرِدٌ، وَهُوَ الْقَطْعُ

al-ṣādu wa-al-rāʾu wa-al-mīmu aṣlun wāḥidun ṣaḥīḥun muṭṭaridun wa-huwa al-qaṭʿu

That is, the family has one sound, broadly applicable principle: cutting.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains al-ṣarm through al-qaṭʿu al-bāʾin: cutting that brings separation.

Something previously connected is separated at its point of attachment.

A cut rope loses its continuity. A branch separated from a tree loses its connection to it. Fruit picked from a cluster is detached at its point of attachment.

These examples share the ending of a bond or connection.

Cutting does not necessarily destroy the material. A cut rope still exists; picked fruit has not ceased to exist and may have been prepared for eating or storage.

Cutting must therefore remain distinct from outcomes such as destruction, corruption, or uselessness.

Does ṣarm always mean complete severance?

The lexical expression al-qaṭʿu al-bāʾin emphasizes a clear separation of two parts.

Some lexicographers also use ṣarm more broadly for cutting.

In harvesting, fruit may be separated from a tree without the tree itself being cut down or destroyed.

Ṣarama al-nakhla therefore does not establish that a palm was cut off at its roots.

The established meaning of the verb in this construction is harvesting the palm’s produce.

This distinction is crucial to the account of the garden owners in Al-Qalam.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: cutting and separation

2. Picking fruit and harvesting

Ṣarama al-nakhla: harvesting a palm’s produce

Early Arabic used ṣarama al-nakhla for picking dates from palms.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

صَرَمَ النَّخْلَ وَالشَّجَرَ وَالزَّرْعَ: جَزَّهُ

ṣarama al-nakhla wa-al-shajara wa-al-zarʿa jazzahu

That is, he cut and harvested the produce of palms, trees, or crops.

Within the family, al-ṣarām and al-ṣirām refer to harvesting fruit and sometimes its season.

Aṣrama al-nakhlu is also recorded for the arrival of the palm harvest’s time.

This shows that ṣarama had an established, practical agricultural meaning.

The cutting separates the produce from the plant, rather than destroying the plant itself.

A tree and its produce

Arabic may use a tree’s or garden’s name in place of its produce.

For example, agricultural context identifies ṣarama al-nakhla as harvesting a palm’s produce.

The same feature appears in Al-Qalam 68:17, where the garden owners swear to perform ṣarm on it in the morning.

The expression does not mean deciding to cut down every tree in the garden.

In context they wish to harvest the produce and prevent needy people from accessing it.

Harvesting here is therefore established through the verb’s known use and the verses’ context.

Ṣarām: harvest time and the act of harvesting

Lexicons record ṣarām for both harvesting and its appropriate time.

Three states must be distinguished: produce ripening, becoming ready for harvest, and the actual picking.

Aṣrama al-nakhlu can identify the arrival of harvest time; ṣarama al-nakhla describes actually picking the produce.

The name of one agricultural stage should not be substituted for another.

This distinction must also be preserved in translating Al-Qalam’s verbs.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: harvesting and the arrival of its season

3. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

Harvesting in Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s poetry

A poem by the renowned pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd reads:

أَنْتُمُ نَخْلٌ نُطِيفُ بِهِ
فَإِذَا مَا جُزَّ نَصْطَرِمُهْ

antumu nakhlun nuṭīfu bihi / fa-idhā mā juzza naṣṭarimuh

A close rendering of the sense: “You are like palms around which we circle; when their harvest time comes, we gather their produce.”

The poet compares a group with palms and uses naṣṭarimuhu.

This comes from iṣṭarama and, concerning palms, means cutting and harvesting their produce.

Juzza in the line likewise concerns cutting the produce.

The witness matters because this family’s use for harvesting is attested in early Arabic poetry independently of the Qur’anic verse.

The metaphor comparing people with palms nevertheless belongs to the poet’s image and should not be added to the root’s general meaning.

Commentary on the six pre-Islamic poets: Ṭarafah’s witness and harvest time

Severing ties in Labīd ibn Rabīʿah’s poetry

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah’s Muʿallaqah reads:

فَاقْطَعْ لُبَانَةَ مَنْ تَعَرَّضَ وَصْلُهُ
وَلَخَيْرُ وَاصِلِ خُلَّةٍ صَرَّامُهَا

fa-iqṭaʿ lubānata man taʿarraḍa waṣluhu / wa-la-khayru wāṣili khullatin ṣarrāmuhā

A close rendering of the sense: “Relinquish attachment to one whose bond is faltering; the best keeper of friendship may be someone who breaks it off when necessary.”

Wa-la-sharru is also transmitted for the second hemistich and changes the poet’s judgment. The variant must therefore remain part of the analysis.

In either reading, ṣarrām concerns cutting and severing a relationship.

The use shows how ṣarm extends beyond bodily cutting to ending human ties.

Severing ties does not necessarily mean hostility or wrongdoing.

One party may end a relationship, or someone may consciously decide not to continue it.

The decision’s value depends on its circumstances and reason, rather than ṣarm itself.

Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-ʿAshr: Labīd’s witness and the khayr/sharr variant

Ṣarama al-waṣla: severing a bond

Lexicons record ṣarama waṣlahu for breaking off a relationship and ending a bond.

Ṣāramahu also sometimes describes two people cutting off contact.

Muṣāramah and taṣārum are consequently used for reciprocal or mutual estrangement.

No actual rope, branch, or fruit is cut in these uses.

What ends is a bond between people.

The semantic extension is understandable, but does not make every Arabic use concern social relations.

4. Firm decisions and a cutting sword

Ṣārim: someone or something that cuts

Ṣārim is the active participle of ṣarama.

In direct use it can describe someone who cuts something or separates it from its place.

Arabic also uses it for a sharp sword.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

الصَّارِمُ: السَّيْفُ الْقَاطِعُ

al-ṣārimu al-sayfu al-qāṭiʿu

That is, ṣārim is a cutting sword.

The sword’s main feature here is its capacity to cut and cleave.

Ṣārim does not mean sword in every sentence: the active participle can apply to another person or thing.

Ṣarāmah: decisiveness and firm resolve

Arabic uses al-ṣarāmah for decisiveness, firmness, and holding to a decision.

Al-ṣarīmah also means a firm decision or resolve to undertake something.

Ibn Fāris explains this through cutting off obstructing attachments: a person has reached a decision that sets impediments aside.

The explanation can clarify a semantic connection between cutting and decisive resolve.

It does not make every decisive resolution the result of cutting an actual thing.

Decisiveness itself is neither a virtue nor a sin.

Someone may firmly maintain a right decision or decisively choose a wrong one.

Ṣārimīna in Al-Qalam 68:22 occurs in a harvesting context. Harvesting is therefore clearer than general decisiveness, although their resolve is also present in the context.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣārim, sword, and resolve

5. Other uses of Ṣ R M

Ṣarīm: cut off or separated

Ṣarīm follows the pattern faʿīl and sometimes describes something cut or separated.

Nakhlun ṣarīmun, for example, is recorded for palms whose produce has been picked.

Al-ṣarīmah also names land already harvested, with no standing crop remaining.

The main feature is the removal of produce or loss of the land’s former covering.

Such land is not necessarily destroyed or burned.

A field harvested normally can be cultivated again.

Harvesting must therefore be distinguished from destroying the land.

Ṣarīm: night and day

Arabic uses ṣarīm for both night and day.

The lexical explanation is that night is separated from day, and day likewise from night.

Ibn Fāris connects these uses with cutting and separation.

Lexicons also record al-aṣramāni for night and day.

Cutting off in this family thus extends beyond bodies and human relationships: the ending of one period and its separation from another can share the image.

Night and day are not interchangeable in every use of ṣarīm.

Temporal indications and context identify the intended meaning in each sentence.

Ṣarīm: a separate tract of land or sand

Lexicons record ṣarīm and ṣarīmah for a tract of sandy ground or land distinct from a larger whole.

Separation is prominent here.

Ṣarīmah may name a tract that appears separate from its surroundings through shape or position.

This relates to cutting and separation without necessarily meaning that the ground was physically cut with a tool.

Ṣirmah: a separate group

Some Arabic uses apply ṣirm and ṣirmah to a group of people or camels separate from a larger group.

The separation of part of a whole again matters.

These uses do not mean that the people or animals have literally been cut.

The sources also do not all assign the groups a uniform size.

Inṣarama and taṣarrama

Arabic inṣarama means being cut off, ending, or breaking apart.

Taṣarrama likewise sometimes describes a gradual ending or the severing of a bond.

Such forms can describe time passing, a relationship ending, or a period concluding.

Neither verb occurs in the Qur’an.

In temporal uses, ending does not destroy time itself: a period or connection comes to an end.

Early lexicons: night, day, land, groups, and ending

6. Qur’anic uses of the root

Al-Qalam 68:17: deciding to harvest the garden

إِنَّا بَلَوْنَاهُمْ كَمَا بَلَوْنَا أَصْحَابَ الْجَنَّةِ إِذْ أَقْسَمُوا لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا مُصْبِحِينَ

innā balawnāhum ka-mā balawnā aṣḥāba al-jannati idh aqsamū la-yaṣrimunnahā muṣbiḥīna

Translation: We tested them as We tested the garden owners, when they swore to harvest it in the morning.

La-yaṣrimunnahā is a Form I imperfect verb with the oath’s emphatic lām and the heavy emphatic nūn.

The construction highlights the speakers’ resolve and oath.

Hā refers to al-jannah, the garden.

Cutting the garden here contextually means harvesting its produce, rather than cutting down all its trees.

Muṣbiḥīna also identifies the morning.

The garden owners had therefore decided to harvest their produce at the day’s beginning.

Study of Ṣ B Ḥ

Is harvesting blameworthy?

No.

Picking fruit and harvesting are ordinary, necessary agricultural activities.

La-yaṣrimunnahā alone does not establish that the garden owners sinned.

The reason for blame becomes clear later in the account.

Al-Qalam 68:24 reads:

أَنْ لَا يَدْخُلَنَّهَا الْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِسْكِينٌ

an lā yadkhulannahā al-yawma ʿalaykum miskīnun

Translation: That no needy person should enter it upon you today.

This shows their intention to prevent needy people from accessing the garden and its produce.

Two actions must therefore be distinguished: harvesting, and deliberately deciding to deny needy people a share in its benefits.

The first is not inherently wrong; the second is blameworthy in the account’s context.

Ṣarama should therefore not be equated with miserliness, wrongdoing, or depriving others.

The role of wa-lā yastathnūna

Al-Qalam 68:18 reads:

وَلَا يَسْتَثْنُونَ

wa-lā yastathnūna

Translation: And they made no exception.

Early commentaries most often explain this as not saying in shāʾa allāh, “if God wills.”

Some reports also connect it with setting aside no share for needy people.

Yastathnūna belongs to another root and means making an exception.

The phrase can be examined in relation to their definite decision and refusal to allow an exception, but it should not become part of ṣarama’s meaning.

The sentence alone also does not specify every detail of a financial right or the needy people’s share.

What the later account explicitly states is their intention to prevent a needy person from entering the garden.

Al-Ṭabarī: the oath to harvest and making no exception

Al-Qalam 68:20: fa-aṣbaḥat ka-al-ṣarīmi

فَأَصْبَحَتْ كَالصَّرِيمِ

fa-aṣbaḥat ka-al-ṣarīmi

Translation on the harvested-land reading: Then it became like a reaped field.

This follows an event that struck the garden while its owners slept.

Al-Qalam 68:19 says:

فَطَافَ عَلَيْهَا طَائِفٌ مِنْ رَبِّكَ وَهُمْ نَائِمُونَ

fa-ṭāfa ʿalayhā ṭāʾifun min rabbika wa-hum nāʾimūna

Translation: Then an occurrence from your Lord passed over it while they slept.

Verse 20 then describes the garden’s condition through al-ṣarīm.

Arabic records several meanings for the word, which must be examined separately to understand the verse.

First reading: reaped land, empty of produce

On this reading al-ṣarīm refers to land already harvested.

Lexicons record al-ṣarīmah for reaped land and nakhlun ṣarīmun for palms whose produce has been picked.

The verse would thus describe the garden as though its produce had been entirely harvested and removed.

This connects directly with the Ṣ R M core of cutting and separating.

It also fits the account: the owners intended to harvest, but arrived to find none of the produce they expected.

Their intention to perform ṣarm can thus be related semantically to the garden’s becoming ṣarīm.

They wanted to separate the produce for themselves; before they could carry out their plan, the garden became empty of produce.

This connection does not mean that another person or group actually harvested it.

Ka-al-ṣarīmi is a comparison: the garden had become like something whose produce was lost or harvested.

Second reading: dark night

Arabic also uses al-ṣarīm for night.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits early commentators’ explanation of the comparison as black night.

On this reading the garden became so dark and blackened that it resembled night.

Some lexicographers and commentators explain this through the garden burning.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains that it burned and became black, like dark night.

The reading relates to the established use of ṣarīm for night.

The word’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from identifying the cause of the garden’s blackening.

Ṣarīm meaning night expresses darkness, but does not itself say how the garden became black.

The garden burning is therefore an exegetical explanation, rather than a necessary conclusion from al-ṣarīm itself.

The preceding verse also does not specify the exact event that struck the garden.

A fire, lightning strike, or particular mechanism of destruction should therefore not be presented as certain without independent evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: the overnight event and the black-night reading

Third reading: bright day

Arabic also records ṣarīm for day.

The lexical explanation is that day separates from night, just as night separates from day.

Al-aṣramāni consequently names night and day.

Some commentaries explain the comparison with day through the ground becoming bare and exposed after its vegetation was lost.

This reading requires more contextual explanation than the first two to describe the garden’s condition.

The verse only says that the garden became like al-ṣarīm; the ground becoming bright is not stated separately.

The existence of this Arabic use should nevertheless not be denied.

Which reading fits better?

The root’s semantic core and the garden owners’ account give two readings substantial support.

First, a garden become like harvested land, empty of produce.

Second, a garden so dark that it resembles black night.

The first connects more directly with la-yaṣrimunnahā in Al-Qalam 68:17 and conveys the loss of the garden’s produce central to the account.

The second also rests on an actual Arabic use of ṣarīm and early lexical reports.

They are not necessarily contradictory: a garden could be both stripped of produce and darkened in appearance.

The verse alone does not conclusively establish the ground’s final form, exact color, or the physical cause of destruction.

“Like a reaped field” is therefore defensible as the basic translation, while “like dark night” should remain in the lexical explanation.

Al-Qurṭubī: reaped land, night, day, and other readings

Is al-ṣarīm the name of a particular place?

Some exegetical reports suggest that al-ṣarīm named a particular tract of land.

The evidence for this possibility is not as strong for determining the verse’s meaning as the word’s general uses.

The Qur’an itself gives no geographical name or specific location identifying this ṣarīm.

The verse is therefore better examined first through the word’s established meanings, rather than confined to a hypothetical place.

Was the garden destroyed forever?

The verse describes the garden on the morning after the event.

Later, its owners speak of their deprivation.

The Qur’an does not specify how long the land remained unproductive or whether plants could grow again.

Al-ṣarīm therefore does not establish that the garden’s soil permanently lost all capacity for cultivation and growth.

The verse describes a situation in which the owners’ expected produce was lost.

Al-Qalam 68:22: in kuntum ṣārimīna

أَنِ اغْدُوا عَلَىٰ حَرْثِكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَارِمِينَ

ani ighdū ʿalā ḥarthikum in kuntum ṣārimīna

Translation: Go early to your field, if you intend to harvest.

Ṣārimīna is the sound masculine plural of the active participle ṣārim, in the accusative as the predicate of kuntum.

The sentence concerns their decision and plan to harvest.

Al-Qalam 68:17 used la-yaṣrimunnahā; verse 22 now presents the same action through its agents.

They are people intending to pick the produce.

Does ṣārimīna mean decisive and determined?

Arabic can use ṣārim for someone decisive and firm in a decision.

Some lexical explanations consequently suggest “if you are serious in your decision” for in kuntum ṣārimīna.

The account nevertheless gives the decision a specific object: harvesting the garden’s produce.

“Harvesters” or “those intending to pick the produce” is therefore more direct.

Their resolve is also clear through their oath in Al-Qalam 68:17 and their morning plan.

The harvesting meaning should not be removed entirely by translating only “if you are decisive.”

The role of ʿalā ḥarthikum

Ḥarth comes from Ḥ R TH and here concerns a field or its cultivated produce.

Ighdū comes from GH D W and concerns setting out in the morning.

Three distinct concepts are therefore present: morning departure, going to the field, and intending to harvest.

These should not all be incorporated into ṣārimīna’s meaning.

Study of Ḥ R TH

Why did the owners set out early?

Al-Qalam 68:23 says that they set off while speaking quietly among themselves.

Al-Qalam 68:24 makes their decision to prevent a needy person’s entry explicit.

Their early departure therefore connects with a plan to deprive needy people.

This feature comes from the account’s context, rather than ṣārimīna’s meaning.

A farmer can harvest early without doing wrong.

The blame here concerns these owners’ particular intention and conduct.

Al-Ṭabarī: harvesting and the decision to exclude the needy

7. Ṣarm within the garden owners’ account

The three Ṣ R M uses in Al-Qalam have a clear connection.

In Al-Qalam 68:17 the owners decide to pick the produce.

In Al-Qalam 68:20 the garden becomes like harvested land, or empty of its yield.

In Al-Qalam 68:22 they call one another to go to their field and harvest.

Repeating a lexical family creates a significant relationship between human intention and what actually happens.

The owners wanted to keep the produce for themselves and prevent needy people from benefiting, but were themselves deprived of it before reaching the garden.

After seeing the garden, they say in Al-Qalam 68:27:

بَلْ نَحْنُ مَحْرُومُونَ

bal naḥnu maḥrūmūna

That is, “Rather, we have been deprived.”

The sentence makes the event’s outcome clear.

The root’s lexical meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the account’s moral message.

Ṣarm basically means cutting and separating; here, harvesting.

The owners’ deprivation is the outcome of the event described.

Ṣarm should therefore not be defined directly as depriving others or losing a blessing.

Does the account concern every form of private ownership?

Owning a garden and intending to harvest are not in themselves condemned in the account.

What is explicit is the owners’ decision to prevent a needy person from entering.

They also subsequently acknowledge their own wrongdoing and transgression.

The account’s message should therefore not become a condemnation of agriculture, ownership, or harvesting itself.

Their explicit intention to deprive needy people also cannot be disregarded by reducing the entire matter to not uttering a particular phrase.

The Qur’an places their conduct, its outcome, and their acknowledgment of wrongdoing in one context.

This matters for understanding the account, but does not change Ṣ R M’s lexical definition.

Al-Ṭabarī: the garden owners’ deprivation

8. Distinctions between important forms

Ṣarama and ṣarm

Ṣarama is Form I and concerns cutting and separating.

Ṣarm is its verbal noun.

In actual use, what is cut may be a rope, produce, a relationship, or something else.

Ṣārim and ṣarrām

Ṣārim is an active participle that can describe someone who cuts, harvests produce, or acts decisively.

Ṣarrām is an intensive form, describing someone who cuts repeatedly or forcefully.

Arabic also uses ṣarrām in human relationships for someone who severs bonds.

Ṣarīm

Ṣarīm follows the pattern faʿīl.

Some uses mean cut off or something whose produce has been harvested.

It also names night, day, and a separate tract of land.

Its sole Qur’anic occurrence is Al-Qalam 68:20.

Ṣarām and ṣirām

These forms are recorded for harvesting, especially dates, and sometimes its season.

The ṣād vowel difference in some uses reflects dialect or transmitted vocalization; it should not create two entirely different actions without evidence.

Aṣrama

Aṣrama al-nakhlu means the palm’s harvest time arrived.

It differs from ṣarama al-nakhla, which expresses the actual harvesting.

Inṣarama

Inṣarama describes being cut off, ending, or breaking apart.

In temporal use it can describe a period’s ending.

Taṣārama and muṣāramah

Taṣārama is used for mutual estrangement.

Muṣāramah likewise concerns breaking off a relationship or withdrawing from one another.

These uses do not occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣarīmah and ṣirmah

Ṣarīmah may mean harvested land, a separate tract, or firm resolve.

Ṣirmah is also recorded for a group or collection separate from a larger whole.

Both should therefore not be translated as “a cutting” in every sentence; their specific meanings depend on structure and context.

9. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ R M (ṣarama) and Q Ṭ ʿ (qaṭaʿa)

Arabic qaṭaʿa has a broad meaning of cutting and separating.

Ṣarama likewise concerns cutting in its main branch.

Ibn Fāris uses qaṭʿ to define Ṣ R M’s core.

Established Ṣ R M uses also involve harvesting, severing bonds, firm resolve, and naming things separated from a whole.

The roots therefore overlap in cutting, but not all their derivatives are complete synonyms.

Study of Q Ṭ ʿ

Ṣ R M (ṣarama) and Ḥ Ṣ D (ḥaṣada)

Arabic ḥaṣada concerns reaping and harvesting crops.

Ṣarama likewise has agricultural uses, especially picking produce from palms and trees.

La-yaṣrimunnahā in Al-Qalam 68:17 concerns harvesting the garden’s produce.

Ḥaṣīdan in Yūnus 10:24 pictures land whose vegetation has gone, leaving it like a reaped crop.

The families are close in the image of harvested produce.

Ḥaṣd should not, however, be confined invariably to grain and ṣarm to dates: both have broader lexical uses.

Study of Ḥ Ṣ D

Ṣ R M (ṣarama) and J DH DH (jadhdha)

Arabic jadhdha concerns cutting and separating, including picking dates in agricultural use.

Judhādhan in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:58 describes something broken into pieces.

The root relates to cutting and separation, but some uses particularly highlight breaking into pieces.

Ṣarama in harvesting does not necessarily imply crushing or breaking fruit.

Picking may merely detach fruit from a branch.

Study of J DH DH

Ṣ R M (ṣarama) and B T R (batara)

Arabic batara concerns cutting, especially severing something’s tail or continuation.

Al-abtaru occurs in Al-Kawthar 108:3.

Ṣarama concerning produce means separating fruit from the plant, without necessarily destroying a lineage’s continuation or severing a bond forever.

The roots are therefore close in cutting, but differ in range and contextual features.

Study of B T R

Ṣ R M (ṣarama) and F Ṣ L (faṣala)

Arabic faṣala concerns separating, distinguishing, and parting things.

Ṣarama also involves separation, with cutting more prominent in material uses.

Faṣl can additionally concern conceptual separation, judgment, or distinguishing two subjects.

Those uses should not be transferred to ṣarm without evidence.

Study of F Ṣ L

Ṣ R M (ṣarama) and Ṣ M M (ṣamma)

Despite the similar letters, these are separate roots.

Ṣ R M concerns cutting and separating.

Arabic Ṣ M M concerns deafness, blocked hearing, and hardness.

Some uses of both families may involve decisiveness or closure, but this resemblance cannot establish historical kinship.

Ṣārim, cutting or decisive, and aṣamm, deaf, therefore belong to different families.

10. Challenges of interpretation and translation

The root’s main difficulty is that general cutting takes different forms in agricultural and human uses.

Translating la-yaṣrimunnahā in Al-Qalam 68:17 as cutting down the garden’s trees disregards the established harvesting meaning.

Translating ṣārimīna in Al-Qalam 68:22 only as strict people likewise removes its direct connection with harvesting.

Translating al-ṣarīm in Al-Qalam 68:20 only as dark night can also weaken its relation to harvesting and the garden’s condition.

The dark-night meaning recorded in early sources must nevertheless remain acknowledged.

Does ṣarm always have a destructive result?

No.

Picking fruit is an ordinary, useful action.

Severing ties can be right or wrong in a particular situation.

A decisive resolution can likewise be appropriate or blameworthy depending on its object and outcome.

The garden owners are condemned for deciding to deprive needy people, rather than for harvesting itself.

Must ṣarīm mean a burned garden?

No.

Burning is one established exegetical explanation of the garden’s condition.

Arabic ṣarīm can describe both harvested land and dark night.

The garden’s condition must therefore be distinguished from its exact cause.

The verse states the event’s outcome without specifying the destructive agent’s precise nature.

Is decisiveness always praiseworthy?

No.

Ṣarāmah and ṣarīmah can refer to firmness of decision.

A decision does not become right merely because it is taken decisively.

The owners pursue their harvesting plan seriously, but the plan also involves depriving needy people.

Decisive action must therefore be distinguished from the rightness of its aim and manner.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

Cutting, separating, and, where appropriate, cutting off are natural general equivalents for ṣarama.

In agriculture, picking produce and harvesting are more suitable.

In human relationships, severing ties or breaking a bond makes the meaning clearer.

In Al-Qalam 68:17:

لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا مُصْبِحِينَ

la-yaṣrimunnahā muṣbiḥīna

Preferred rendering:

“To harvest it in the morning.”

A contextual explanation identifies the object as the garden’s produce.

In Al-Qalam 68:20:

فَأَصْبَحَتْ كَالصَّرِيمِ

fa-aṣbaḥat ka-al-ṣarīmi

Preferred rendering on the harvested-land reading:

“Then it became like a reaped field.”

Another reading is “It became like dark night.”

A study must preserve both readings and their support, without adding burning directly to the root’s meaning without evidence.

In Al-Qalam 68:22:

إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَارِمِينَ

in kuntum ṣārimīna

Preferred rendering:

“If you intend to harvest.”

This preserves the active participle’s agricultural use and the owners’ resolve in context.

For forms outside the Qur’an, ṣarām can mean harvesting; ṣārim, as appropriate, cutting, harvesting, or decisive; and inṣarama, was cut off or ended.

12. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ R M basically expresses cutting and separating.

This extends through cutting a rope, harvesting, severing a relationship, a period ending, part of a whole becoming separate, and firm resolve.

In agriculture ṣarama concerns harvesting, without necessarily destroying the tree or land.

The family can likewise express severing human ties and ending a relationship.

Besides a cutting person or implement, ṣārim sometimes concerns decisiveness and firm resolve.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an, in the garden owners’ account.

In Al-Qalam 68:17 they decide to harvest.

In Al-Qalam 68:20 the garden is compared with harvested land or, on another reading, dark night.

In Al-Qalam 68:22 the owners call one another to set off and harvest.

Short definition of the root: Ṣ R M means cutting and separating something from its point of attachment or bond; extended uses can concern severing relationships, a period ending, or firmness of decision. Brief root meaning: Cutting and separating; harvesting, severing bonds, and ending continuity

The root’s main feature is severing continuity, while context identifies what is cut and its outcome.

Ṣarm in the garden owners’ account therefore means harvesting, rather than destroying the garden. Ṣarīm describes something stripped of its former yield, although its lexical use for dark night must also be considered.

The act of cutting, the intention to perform it, and its actual outcome must consequently remain clearly distinguished.
