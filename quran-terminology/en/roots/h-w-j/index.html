From need and desire to an inward wish and its fulfillment

The Arabic root «ح و ج» concerns needing, wanting, and feeling a need for something. The familiar word ḥājah belongs to this family. A person may need something to sustain life, require a tool or another person’s help to accomplish a task, or hold an inward wish they seek to fulfill.

In the Qur’an, this root is not restricted to poverty or material lack. A ḥājah may be an inward wish that a person acts to fulfill, a practical objective pursued through available resources, or a desire or sense of claim that people do not feel toward something given to others.

1. Root forms and semantic core

The root «ح و ج» occurs three times in the Qur’an, in just one form: «حَاجَةً» (ḥājatan). These occurrences are in Yūsuf 12:68, Ghāfir 40:80, and Al-Ḥashr 59:9.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three occurrences of the noun ḥājah

Its Arabic word family is broader:

  • حَاجَةٌ (ḥājah): a need or wish that someone seeks to fulfill.
  • حَاجَ يَحُوجُ (ḥāja yaḥūju): to become needy or have a need.
  • اِحْتَاجَ (iḥtāja): to need or be in need.
  • أَحْوَجَ (aḥwaja): to become needy; transitively, to cause someone to need something.
  • تَحَوَّجَ (taḥawwaja): to seek to meet a need or look for what one requires.
  • حَاجَاتٌ، حَوَائِجُ (ḥājāt, ḥawāʾij): plurals of ḥājah.
  • حَوْجَاءُ (ḥawjāʾ): another form meaning a need.
  • مُحْتَاجٌ (muḥtāj): someone in need.

These forms are recorded in Arabic lexical sources, but should not be confused with the derivatives actually used in the Qur’an.

Semantic core

The core meaning of «ح و ج» is a need or wish that a person finds necessary or desirable and may turn to an object, another person, or an action to fulfill.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains this root through «الاضطرار إلى الشيء», needing something. Lisān al-ʿArab likewise defines ḥājah through maʾrabah, a wish or purpose, and records seeking as a meaning of ḥawj.

These expressions clarify two related aspects: need and dependence on something, and wanting and seeking to obtain it.

The lexical definition’s reference to necessity should not be read so narrowly that every ḥājah must be a vital necessity, severe poverty, or a state of desperation. Qur’anic uses show that it can also be a wish capable of fulfillment or something desired inwardly.

Need and wish therefore both belong to this root’s semantic field, while each verse determines which is more precise.

2. Early Arabic usage

In early Arabic, ḥājah denoted something a person wanted and sought to obtain. That wish could be financial, social, personal, or practical.

A verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā, an Arab poet close to the period of revelation, reads:

وَإِذَا ابْتَغَيْتَ بِأَرْضِ عُكْلٍ حَاجَةً
فَاعْمِدْ لِبَيْتِ رَبِيعَةَ بْنِ حُذَارِ

wa-idhā ibtaghayta bi-arḍi ʿuklin ḥājatan / fa-ʿmid li-bayti rabīʿata bni ḥudhāri

Translation:

“If you seek to fulfill a need in the land of ʿUkl, make for the house of Rabīʿah ibn Ḥudhār.”

Here, ḥājah is something for which a person approaches someone else. The poet speaks of a wish that another person’s help may fulfill. The word itself does not establish whether it is a vital necessity or another objective.

This example belongs to poetry attributed to Al-Aʿshā, but its precise date of composition is unclear. Attribution alone therefore does not establish that this particular verse predates the Qur’an.

Al-Aʿshā’s collected verse, Abu Dhabi Poetry Encyclopedia: ʿUkl and Rabīʿah ibn Ḥudhār

Lexical sources also record qaḍāʾ al-ḥājah for fulfilling a need. In this expression, ḥājah is the need or wish itself, while qaḍāʾ expresses accomplishing or completing it. The same association occurs in the Qur’an.

Explaining «ح و ج» does not require inventing a hypothetical physical image. Need, seeking, and desire provide sufficient direct evidence for understanding its central semantic field.

3. Qur’anic uses

A. Yūsuf 12:68: a need within Jacob

وَلَمَّا دَخَلُوا مِنْ حَيْثُ أَمَرَهُمْ أَبُوهُمْ مَا كَانَ يُغْنِي عَنْهُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ إِلَّا حَاجَةً فِي نَفْسِ يَعْقُوبَ قَضَاهَا وَإِنَّهُ لَذُو عِلْمٍ لِمَا عَلَّمْنَاهُ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-lammā dakhalū min ḥaythu amarahum abūhum mā kāna yughnī ʿanhum mina allāhi min shayʾin illā ḥājatan fī nafsi yaʿqūba qaḍāhā wa-innahu la-dhū ʿilmin li-mā ʿallamnāhu wa-lākinna akthara al-nāsi lā yaʿlamūna

Proposed translation:

“When they entered from where their father had commanded them, this could not ward anything off from them against The God; rather, there was a need within Jacob that he fulfilled. He certainly possessed knowledge because of what We had taught him, but most people do not know.”

Yūsuf 12:68

In the preceding verse, Jacob advises his sons to enter through separate gates rather than one gate. He makes clear there that he cannot ward anything off from them against The God and that judgment belongs to The God (Yūsuf 12:67).

Yūsuf 12:68 explains that his sons followed his advice, but that this action had no independent power to prevent what lay within divine judgment.

In this setting appears «حَاجَةً فِي نَفْسِ يَعْقُوبَ قَضَاهَا»: a need within Jacob that he fulfilled.

Here, ḥājah is not financial lack or a need for food and the necessities of life. It is an inward wish connected with Jacob’s action and his advice to his sons.

The overall context suggests a connection with his paternal concern and planning. The Qur’an does not, however, state his precise reason for advising entry through separate gates. Definitively attributing it to fear of the evil eye, recognition, political danger, or another specific cause goes beyond the verse’s wording.

The association of ḥājatan with qaḍāhā matters. Here, a ḥājah is a wish toward whose fulfillment action is taken. Commentaries differ over the referent of the implicit subject of qaḍāhā. For example, Al-Ālūsī presents both a reading concerning Jacob’s expression of his wish and advice, and a reference to the sons’ entry, meaning that their entry fulfilled the wish. This disagreement does not alter the meaning of ḥājah itself.

Al-Ālūsī’s Rūḥ al-Maʿānī: alternative referents for the subject of qaḍāhā

Fī nafsi Yaʿqūba also identifies the wish’s inward location. This comes from combining ḥājah with fī nafs, rather than from «ح و ج» alone.

The verse ends by identifying Jacob as possessing knowledge taught by The God. It thus distinguishes knowledge and practical planning from the limits of human capacity before divine judgment. Fulfilling an inward wish does not mean controlling all the outward consequences of an action.

B. Ghāfir 40:80: attaining a need through available resources

وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا مَنَافِعُ وَلِتَبْلُغُوا عَلَيْهَا حَاجَةً فِي صُدُورِكُمْ وَعَلَيْهَا وَعَلَى الْفُلْكِ تُحْمَلُونَ

wa-lakum fīhā manāfiʿu wa-li-tablughū ʿalayhā ḥājatan fī ṣudūrikum wa-ʿalayhā wa-ʿalā al-fulki tuḥmalūna

Proposed translation:

“In them are benefits for you, and so that through them you may attain a need within your breasts; on them and on ships you are carried.”

Ghāfir 40:80

The verse concerns livestock used for riding, food, and other benefits. The preceding verse introduces this subject: «اللَّهُ الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ الْأَنْعَامَ لِتَرْكَبُوا مِنْهَا وَمِنْهَا تَأْكُلُونَ» (allāhu alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-anʿāma li-tarkabū minhā wa-minhā taʾkulūna): “The God is the One who made livestock for you, that you may ride some and eat some” (Ghāfir 40:79).

In «وَلِتَبْلُغُوا عَلَيْهَا حَاجَةً فِي صُدُورِكُمْ», ḥājah is a wish or objective that a person uses these resources to attain.

For example, someone may wish to reach a distant land, transport goods, visit family, or carry out work elsewhere. These are natural examples of using transport, rather than the exclusive meaning of ḥājah itself.

Tablughū expresses reaching, while ʿalayhā identifies the means of attaining the objective. Here, ḥājah is something a person seeks to reach.

The expression fī ṣudūrikum also occurs here. Just as ḥājah was within Jacob in Yūsuf 12:68, this verse concerns a need within people’s breasts.

Mentioning the breast does not reduce the need to a purely psychological feeling. A person may have an entirely practical objective, first desired inwardly and then pursued through action.

By mentioning livestock and ships, the verse shows that some human needs and wishes are fulfilled through material resources. Here, ḥājah is therefore more than a feeling of lack: it is also an objective pursued through effort.

C. Al-Ḥashr 59:9: a need not found within

وَالَّذِينَ تَبَوَّءُوا الدَّارَ وَالْإِيمَانَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ يُحِبُّونَ مَنْ هَاجَرَ إِلَيْهِمْ وَلَا يَجِدُونَ فِي صُدُورِهِمْ حَاجَةً مِمَّا أُوتُوا وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ وَمَنْ يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

wa-alladhīna tabawwaʾū al-dāra wa-l-īmāna min qablihim yuḥibbūna man hājara ilayhim wa-lā yajidūna fī ṣudūrihim ḥājatan mimmā ūtū wa-yuʾthirūna ʿalā anfusihim wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣatun wa-man yūqa shuḥḥa nafsihi fa-ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūna

Proposed translation:

“Those who settled in that land before them and became established in faith love those who emigrated to them. They find no need within their breasts concerning what the others were given, and prefer others over themselves even when they are in poverty. Whoever is protected from the avarice of their own self: those are the successful.”

Al-Ḥashr 59:9

The verse describes people who love the emigrants and prefer others over themselves despite their own poverty.

Two expressions must be considered together to understand ḥājatan here:

وَلَا يَجِدُونَ فِي صُدُورِهِمْ حَاجَةً مِمَّا أُوتُوا

wa-lā yajidūna fī ṣudūrihim ḥājatan mimmā ūtū

“They find no need within their breasts concerning what the others were given.”

And:

وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ

wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣatun

“Even when they are in poverty.”

The first speaks of finding no need within the breast; the second explicitly allows that they themselves may be in poverty.

Reading the first sentence as denying every material need makes it difficult to understand alongside the second. The verse does not say that these people needed nothing in life; it says that they felt no inward need concerning what others had received.

In light of the preceding verse about the emigrants (Al-Ḥashr 59:8), the recipients in this context are the emigrants. Those already settled in the land felt no claim or covetous desire toward the share the emigrants had received.

This state can also suggest an absence of envy or resentment concerning others’ benefits, but ḥājah itself does not directly mean envy. Envy is a defensible contextual interpretation of the whole expression, rather than an independent lexical meaning of this root.

Al-Ṭabarī: the emigrants’ share and the contextual explanation of envy

Fī ṣudūrihim draws attention to their inward state. Wa-yuʾthirūna ʿalā anfusihim then describes their outward conduct: despite their own need, they give others precedence.

A subtle distinction between ḥājah and khaṣāṣah also emerges here. Khaṣāṣah concerns actual poverty and lack, while ḥājah in the first clause concerns wanting or feeling a need for a share given to others.

A person may therefore be needy while holding no covetous desire or resentment toward what another has received.

This use shows that Qur’anic ḥājah need not be an objective state of poverty. It can denote a wish that may or may not arise within a person.

4. Ḥājah in relation to need, wishes, and poverty

The root’s three Qur’anic uses belong to one semantic field, approached from three different angles.

In Yūsuf 12:68, ḥājah is an inward wish connected with Jacob’s advice and his sons’ compliance. In Ghāfir 40:80, it is an objective pursued through transport. In Al-Ḥashr 59:9, it concerns wanting or feeling a need for something given to others; the verse describes its absence within a group of believers.

These differences show that the root is neither restricted to vital necessity nor identical with every fleeting, insignificant inclination.

A ḥājah is generally a wish that matters to a person and whose fulfillment, if the wish exists, is desirable. Its degree of importance must be understood from context.

Arabic words for poverty, privation, necessity, and need may overlap in some situations, but none contains the whole meaning of every other.

Ḥ W J (ḥājah) and KH Ṣ Ṣ (khaṣāṣah)

In Al-Ḥashr 59:9, khaṣāṣah concerns poverty and lack experienced by a person, while ḥājah concerns an inward wish or need directed toward something.

Someone can therefore experience khaṣāṣah while feeling no inward desire for another person’s particular wealth or benefit.

This is not an absolute division between the roots in every Arabic use, since ḥājah may also arise from actual poverty or lack. Their appearance together in one verse nevertheless shows that they should not be treated as exact synonyms without considering their complements and context.

Al-Ṭabarī: khaṣāṣah, actual need, and preferring others

Ḥ W J (ḥājah) and F Q R (faqr)

Faqr primarily expresses being in need and lacking sufficiency. Ḥājah can concern a particular thing sought to remedy a lack, achieve an objective, or fulfill a wish.

A poor person may have many needs, but needing something does not necessarily mean being poor. Even a wealthy person may need help or resources to accomplish a task, acquire knowledge, travel, or solve a problem.

This distinction matters in translation: ḥājah should not always be rendered poverty or privation.

Ḥ W J (ḥājah) and Ḥ J J (ḥajj and ḥujjah)

Ḥ W J and Ḥ J J are different roots.

Ḥajj, ḥujjah, and ḥājja belong to Ḥ J J, whereas ḥājah and iḥtāja belong to Ḥ W J.

The visual similarity of forms such as ḥāja and ḥājja should not cause confusion. The former concerns needing in lexical usage; the latter can concern disputation and argumentative engagement in Qur’anic usage.

5. Structural observations and translation proposals

In all three Qur’anic occurrences, ḥājatan is a singular accusative noun. Differences in meaning do not result from a change in the word’s form, but from its accompanying verbs, what the need concerns, and the sentence’s context.

In Yūsuf 12:68, «حَاجَةً فِي نَفْسِ يَعْقُوبَ قَضَاهَا» concerns an inward wish and its fulfillment. One suitable rendering is “a need within Jacob that he fulfilled.”

In Ghāfir 40:80, li-tablughū ʿalayhā ḥājatan expresses an objective attained through a means. “So that you may attain a need” and “so that you may fulfill a wish” fit the context, with the former closer to tablughū.

In Al-Ḥashr 59:9, lā yajidūna fī ṣudūrihim ḥājatan describes finding no inward need. A direct translation may retain “they find no need within themselves,” while the explanation clarifies that this concerns what the emigrants received.

The principal Persian equivalents of this root are niyāz, ḥājat, and khwāstah: need, a need or wish, and something desired. Depending on the construction, being in need, becoming needy, pursuing a wish, and fulfilling a need can also be appropriate.

For Forqan’s Persian text, the article recommends retaining ḥājat in all three verses as an equivalent close to the Arabic form. It is familiar in Persian and has a semantic field close to these Qur’anic uses. Essential need, an inward wish, and a practical objective can be distinguished in each verse’s explanation. In English, need, wish, or objective should be selected according to that context.

6. Conclusion

Brief root meaning: A need or wish considered necessary or desirable; material, practical, or inward.

The Qur’an uses this root in three situations: Jacob has an inward need connected with his advice and his sons’ conduct; people use livestock to attain their needs; and some believers, despite poverty, find no inward need concerning what others receive.

Ḥājah should therefore not be restricted to poverty, vital necessity, envy, or self-serving desire. None of these alone fully defines the root; each may relate to it only in an appropriate context.

Final definition: Ḥ W J denotes a need or wish that a person finds necessary or desirable. It may be material, practical, or inward. A person may seek to fulfill it, attain it, or find no such need within themselves concerning a particular thing.
