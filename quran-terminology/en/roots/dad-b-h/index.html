Audible panting and animal cries: from galloping horses and the fox’s cry to discoloration by fire and sunlight

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Ḍ B Ḥ has two established semantic branches. The first concerns sound, particularly the cries of certain animals and the sound of panting while running. The second concerns the effect of fire and heat on objects and the resulting change in their color.

In its application to horses, ḍabḥ is the sound heard when a horse gallops and breathes forcefully. Lexical explanations distinguish this sound from an ordinary neigh.

In early Arabic, ḍubāḥ also denoted the fox’s cry. The same family was used for the sounds of other animals, including owls and dogs, and even for the sound of a bow.

In the second branch, ضَبَحَتْهُ النَّارُ describes fire affecting something: heat scorches its surface, darkens it, or changes its color.

The root Ḍ B Ḥ occurs only once in the Qur’an:

ضَبْحًا: audible panting; Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1.

Important Arabic forms of the family include:

  • ضَبَحَ / يَضْبَحُ: to cry out or pant audibly; in the other branch, to scorch something or alter its color through heat.
  • ضَبْح: the sound of panting, especially while running; also the effect of heat on something.
  • ضُبَاح: an animal’s cry, particularly a fox’s.
  • ضَابِح: an animal or other being that produces the characteristic sound called ḍabḥ.
  • ضَوَابِح: plural of ḍābiḥ; those crying out or panting.
  • ضَبْحَة: one cry or instance of the sound of ḍabḥ.
  • ضَبِيح: something affected by fire or heat.
  • مَضْبُوح: heated, slightly scorched, or discolored.
  • اِنْضَبَحَ: its color changed through heat and became darker.
  • اِنْضِبَاح: discoloration, particularly darkening through fire or sunlight.
  • ضِبْح: in one lexical usage, ashes.

These forms do not all occur in the Qur’an. The only Qur’anic form is ضَبْحًا, whose meaning must be examined through the context of running and the established sound-related uses of the root.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence at Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1

2. Two semantic branches in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris’s analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two semantic principles for Ḍ B Ḥ:

أَحَدُهُمَا صَوْتٌ، وَالْآخَرُ تَغَيُّرُ لَوْنٍ مِنْ فِعْلِ نَارٍ

aḥaduhumā ṣawtun wa-al-ākharu taghayyuru lawnin min fiʿli nārin

That is, one concerns sound and the other a change of color caused by fire.

This distinction matters because an animal’s sound and wood changing color in fire are different events.

An explanation might associate an animal’s intense panting with its changed condition through fatigue or fear. Such an analogy, however, does not by itself establish that the two branches developed historically from a single original meaning.

Each branch should therefore be examined through its own evidence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: sound and discoloration as two principles

In the Qur’an, ḍabḥan belongs to the branch of sound and panting, rather than burning or discoloration.

The fox’s cry

Kitāb al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab give al-ḍubāḥ as the fox’s cry.

Arabic uses the expression:

ضَبَحَ الثَّعْلَبُ

ḍabaḥa al-thaʿlabu

That is, the fox cried out.

This usage concerns a particular animal sound and does not necessarily require the animal to be running.

A fox may produce sounds in various situations, including fear, excitement, or communication with other animals.

Early sources identify this cry as ḍubāḥ.

It should not follow that every fox sound is called ḍubāḥ or that all animal sounds are naturally identical.

The lexical designation identifies a recognized sound; it does not provide a complete classification of animal vocalizations.

A poetic witness concerning foxes

Dhū al-Rummah describes empty, remote deserts:

سَبَارِيتُ يَخْلُو سَمْعُ مُجْتَازِ خَرْقِهَا
مِنَ الصَّوْتِ إِلَّا مِنْ ضُبَاحِ الثَّعَالِبِ

sabārītu yakhlū samʿu mujtāzi kharqihā / mina al-ṣawti illā min ḍubāḥi al-thaʿālibi

Translation:

“Barren deserts where the ears of one crossing their expanse hear no sound except the cries of foxes.”

In this verse, ḍubāḥ al-thaʿālib refers to foxes’ cries.

By mentioning the only sound heard, the poet makes the desert’s silence and remoteness tangible.

This witness shows that ḍubāḥ referred to a real, audible sound in Arabic.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad poet. This verse is therefore evidence for early Islamic Arabic, rather than pre-Islamic poetry.

Kitāb al-Afʿāl: Dhū al-Rummah’s witness with kharqihā

The sounds of other animals

Lexical sources do not restrict ḍabḥ to foxes.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records forms of this family for the sounds of rabbits, certain snakes, owls, and other birds.

For example, ضَبَحَ الْبُومُ refers to the owl’s cry.

A rajaz fragment attributed to Al-ʿAjjāj reads:

مِنْ ضَابِحِ الْهَامِ وَبُومٍ بَوَّامِ

min ḍābiḥi al-hāmi wa-būmin bawwāmi

That is, from the cries of nocturnal birds and calling owls.

The exact reading of this fragment varies between copies. One version should therefore not be treated as definitive without examination.

The root is also recorded for the sound of a bow. In a description transmitted by Abū Ḥanīfah al-Dīnawarī, the bow’s sound is likened to a fox’s cry.

This usage shows how the word can extend from an animal’s sound to a similar sound produced by an instrument.

The shared audible quality matters here, although a bow is neither an animal nor something that breathes.

Consequently, not every use of ḍabḥ should be restricted to a living being’s panting.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: animal cries and the bow’s sound

3. A horse’s ḍabḥ while running

The sound of forceful breaths

In early Arabic, ضَبَحَتِ الْخَيْلُ was used of horses whose breathing makes a characteristic sound while they run.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains that a galloping horse may produce a sound through its mouth and respiratory system that differs from its ordinary neigh.

Abū Isḥāq al-Zajjāj describes horses’ ḍabḥ as a sound from within them while running.

In early language, this expression refers to the sound of panting and air moving through an animal’s breathing.

During intense activity, the rate and depth of an animal’s breathing increase. Forceful airflow may produce an audible sound.

This account is consistent with present knowledge of breathing during physical activity. The word ḍabḥ itself, however, does not name a respiratory disease, a specific disorder, or a sound with measured physical characteristics.

In the Qur’anic use, the sound of panting accompanies running.

Panting and neighing

Ṣahīl is the Arabic word for a horse’s neigh.

Some sources explicitly describe ḍabḥ as a sound distinct from both ṣahīl and ḥamḥamah.

A horse may neigh to communicate or react in different situations.

In contrast, ḍabḥ in the context of a galloping horse accompanies strenuous activity and panting.

Lexicographers have not all drawn the boundaries between these sounds identically. Some associate ḍabḥ with ḥamḥamah; others describe it as a rough, hoarse sound.

The defensible central distinction is that ḍabḥ in a running context emphasizes the sound of panting and physical effort, rather than necessarily the horse’s ordinary neigh.

A witness attributed to ʿAntarah

Lexical and exegetical sources attribute the following verse to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād:

وَالْخَيْلُ تَعْلَمُ حِينَ تَضْبَحُ
فِي حِيَاضِ الْمَوْتِ ضَبْحًا

wa-al-khaylu taʿlamu ḥīna taḍbaḥu / fī ḥiyāḍi al-mawti ḍabḥan

Translation:

“The horses know, when they pant aloud in the pools of death.”

This witness attributes ḍabḥ to horses and associates it with battle.

In this respect, its use fits the picture of a horse running and exerting itself intensely.

The transmitted wording varies: Lisān al-ʿArab reports Al-Kashshāf’s opening as والخيل تكدح rather than والخيل تعلم. This variation concerns the beginning of the verse, rather than necessarily the word taḍbaḥu.

The variant reading and the composition date of this particular verse should therefore be examined separately. Its attribution to ʿAntarah in lexical sources does not replace comparison with reliable copies of his collected poetry.

Nevertheless, numerous lexical sources record ḍabḥ for horses independently of this verse.

Lisān al-ʿArab: horse breathing and the variant opening of ʿAntarah’s verse

Is ḍabḥ exclusive to horses?

No. Recorded uses for foxes, dogs, owls, and other animals show that ḍabḥ is not exclusive to horses.

However, ḍabḥ accompanying strenuous running is particularly associated with horses in early explanations.

This distinction matters.

One should neither claim that only horses can produce ḍabḥ nor infer that the Qur’anic al-ʿādiyāt must be foxes simply because foxes also have ḍubāḥ.

The verse’s referent is examined through the relationship between al-ʿādiyāt, ḍabḥan, and the following verses.

4. Ḍabḥ and running

Does ḍabḥ itself mean galloping?

Some early sources associate ḍabḥ with a manner of fast running, as well as sound.

Abū ʿUbaydah connects ضبحت الخيل with ضبعت الخيل.

This usage is explained through the extension of the horse’s forelimbs while running and the stretching of its body in motion.

Some lexicographers likewise understand ḍabḥ as a kind of running.

This analysis is disputed, however.

Ibn Fāris considers the sound-related meaning more consistent with the root and suggests that the running and limb-extension usage may involve the proximity or substitution of letters between ḍabḥ and ḍabʿ.

Ḍabḥ and running should therefore not be treated as wholly identical without considering the distinction between the roots.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: sound and the relationship with ḍabʿ

The role of al-ʿādiyāt

In Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1, running is expressed by al-ʿādiyāt.

ʿĀdiyāt belongs to the root ʿ D W and denotes runners or chargers in this context.

Ḍabḥan belongs to Ḍ B Ḥ and expresses the audible quality accompanying their running.

Translating both words simply as “galloping” would therefore obscure ḍabḥ’s distinct contribution to the scene.

“The panting runners” preserves this distinction: running comes from al-ʿādiyāt, while audible panting comes from ḍabḥan.

5. The Qur’anic use: وَالْعَادِيَاتِ ضَبْحًا

The only occurrence of Ḍ B Ḥ appears at the opening of Al-ʿĀdiyāt:

وَالْعَادِيَاتِ ضَبْحًا

wa-al-ʿādiyāti ḍabḥan

“By the panting runners.”

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1

The verse’s construction

The opening wa- is an oath particle.

Al-ʿādiyāt is a sound feminine plural active participle from ʿ D W.

Ḍabḥan is an accusative verbal noun from Ḍ B Ḥ.

In a common grammatical analysis, ḍabḥan is the verbal noun of an implied taḍbaḥu: runners producing ḍabḥ as they run.

Another analysis treats the verbal noun as a circumstantial expression conveying the sense of ḍābiḥāt.

Both analyses yield the same central picture: beings that are running and from which the sound of ḍabḥ is heard.

Ḍabḥan itself is not an animal’s name. It expresses a condition or sound accompanying movement.

Must the referent be horses?

Early exegesis presents two principal interpretations.

In the familiar reading, al-ʿādiyāt are horses galloping swiftly and panting audibly as they run.

This interpretation fits the established use of ḍabḥ for horses.

Another reading understands al-ʿādiyāt as camels, particularly those traveling between ʿArafāt, Muzdalifah, and Minā during pilgrimage.

Reports attribute this reading to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and Ibn Masʿūd.

Other lexicographers consider the use of ḍabḥ clearer for horses and explain the movement-related camel usage through the expression ḍabʿ.

Al-Ṭabarī also prefers the interpretation referring to horses.

Historical disagreement should nevertheless not be confused with conclusive proof of either referent.

The verse itself mentions neither khayl, horses, nor ibil, camels.

Methodologically, the lexical meaning of ḍabḥ should therefore be distinguished from identifying the referent of al-ʿādiyāt.

In this context, ḍabḥ means audible panting. The horse interpretation has stronger support from lexical usage and the surah’s overall imagery.

Al-Ṭabarī: transmitted horse and camel interpretations

Its relationship to Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:2, Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3, Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4, and Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:5

The following verses read:

فَالْمُورِيَاتِ قَدْحًا
فَالْمُغِيرَاتِ صُبْحًا
فَأَثَرْنَ بِهِ نَقْعًا
فَوَسَطْنَ بِهِ جَمْعًا

fa-al-mūriyāti qadḥan / fa-al-mughīrāti ṣubḥan / fa-atharna bihi naqʿan / fa-wasaṭna bihi jamʿan

Translation:

“Then by those striking sparks; then by those raiding at dawn, raising dust thereby and entering the midst of a gathering thereby.”

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:2, Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3, Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4, and Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:5

These verses create a connected picture of swift movement, striking and sparks, a dawn raid, rising dust, and arrival within a gathering.

This picture fits galloping horses moving swiftly in a confrontation.

In particular, fa-al-mūriyāti qadḥan may refer to hooves striking stones and producing sparks.

The boundaries of each word’s meaning must nevertheless be preserved.

Ḍabḥ expresses the sound of panting; qadḥ concerns striking and sparks; naqʿ denotes dust; and mughīrāt concerns raiding and sudden movement in this context.

The sound of ḍabḥ should therefore not be equated with hoofbeats, sparks, or dust.

These are distinct components of one scene.

Is the surah about a particular battle?

Some early commentaries associate the opening verses with warhorses or a particular group of fighters.

The camel interpretation instead concerns pilgrimage and movement between ritual sites.

These five verses themselves, however, name no battle, commander, military group, or specific place.

All the details of a particular event should therefore not be imported into the words’ meanings on the basis of a disputed historical report.

At the same time, al-mughīrāt, naqʿan, and wasaṭna jamʿan show that the surah depicts more than quiet, ordinary running.

Swift movement and entry into the midst of a gathering form part of the scene.

Reading it as horses charging in a raid is therefore defensible from the words taken together. Naming a particular battle or event requires independent evidence.

The opening scene and the surah’s main subject

After the first five verses comes:

إِنَّ الْإِنْسَانَ لِرَبِّهِ لَكَنُودٌ

inna al-insāna li-rabbihi la-kanūdun

“Indeed, the human being is deeply ungrateful to his Lord.”

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:6

This sentence reveals the central judgment concerning the human being.

The continuation also speaks of the human being’s testimony to his own conduct and his intense attachment to good things or wealth.

The opening oath thus serves an argument concerning human ingratitude and responsibility.

Ḍabḥ itself, however, does not mean ingratitude, greed, or attachment to wealth.

Those concepts emerge from the subsequent verses.

An oath invoking a scene does not, by itself, morally endorse every action that might occur within that scene.

In a root study, ḍabḥ retains the meaning of audible panting. The surah’s message should be examined in its own reflection.

6. The second branch: the effects of fire and sunlight

Discoloration through heat

Arabic uses the expression:

ضَبَحَتْهُ النَّارُ

ḍabaḥat-hu al-nāru

That is, fire affected it and altered its color or surface.

Lexical sources use ḍabḥ for wood whose outer parts fire scorches or darkens.

Likewise, ضَبَحَتْهُ الشَّمْسُ describes someone or something whose color strong sunlight has altered.

Here, ḍabḥ does not necessarily mean complete combustion or the reduction of all the material to ashes.

The wood’s surface may only have been lightly heated, or human skin may have darkened in sunlight.

Early sources also record inḍibāḥ al-lawn for a change in color toward darkness.

A witness concerning meat exposed to heat

A verse attributed to Muḍarris al-Asadī reads:

فَلَمَّا أَنْ تَلَهْوَجْنَا شِوَاءً
بِهِ اللَّهَبَانُ مَقْهُورًا ضَبِيحًا

fa-lammā an talahwajnā shiwāʾan / bihi al-lahabānu maqhūran ḍabīḥan

An approximate rendering of the sense:

“When we prepared meat over the fire, with the flames having acted upon it and heated its surface.”

In this witness, ḍabīḥan describes meat affected by heat.

The lexical explanation also associates talahwuj with meat that is not yet fully cooked.

Ḍabīḥ in this context therefore refers to the effect of fire, rather than necessarily complete cooking.

This use differs from the fox’s cry or a horse’s panting.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Muḍarris’s witness and incompletely cooked meat

Wood and fire-striking stone

In Arabic, قِدْحٌ مَضْبُوحٌ describes a wooden shaft heated or darkened by fire.

Heat was used to straighten or shape wood in making and preparing certain implements.

Ḥijārah maḍbūḥah also describes fire-striking stones bearing dark or apparently scorched marks.

Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj uses the expression مَضْبُوحَ الْفِلَقْ in describing hard, dark stones.

This witness concerns stones whose split surfaces appear heated or dark in color.

Maḍbūḥ does not mean a spark here.

Sparks may arise when a fire-striking stone is struck or used, but the description maḍbūḥ concerns the effect of heat or darkness.

Consequently, ḍabḥ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1 should not be translated as burning or striking sparks merely because the next verse concerns sparks.

Do sound and heat share one origin?

Both branches of Ḍ B Ḥ are established in lexical sources.

An illustrative explanation might note that an animal changes condition through strenuous activity and fatigue, while an object changes through heat.

This general resemblance, however, is insufficient evidence that the meanings developed historically from a single origin.

Ibn Fāris identifies them as two separate principles.

For Forqan’s root study, the two branches should therefore be retained alongside one another, without reducing them to an invented common image of pressure, heat, or exhaustion.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: darkening and the effects of fire

7. Comparison with neighboring roots

Ḍ B Ḥ (ḍabḥ) and ʿ D W (ʿadw)

In its movement-related use, ʿadw denotes running or charging.

In the Qur’anic use, ḍabḥ concerns the sound of panting accompanying running.

The two families occur together in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1.

One expresses movement; the other expresses the audible condition accompanying it.

Not all running necessarily involves audible ḍabḥ, and not every ḍubāḥ occurs while running: a fox’s cry may occur independently of running in other uses.

Study of ʿ D W

Ḍ B Ḥ (ḍabḥ) and Ḍ B ʿ (ḍabʿ)

Some Arabic uses of ḍabʿ concern the extension of an animal’s forelimbs in motion.

One early explanation likewise associates ḍabḥ with a kind of running.

Abū ʿUbaydah links the two uses, while Ibn Fāris raises the possibility of letter substitution and linguistic change.

These roots should therefore not be equated without evidence.

The familiar use of ḍabḥ for a running horse emphasizes the sound of its breaths. The explanation of ḍabʿ concerns the position of its limbs and manner of movement.

Ḍ B Ḥ (ḍabḥ) and Ṣ W T (ṣawt)

Ṣawt is the general word for sound.

Ḍabḥ identifies a particular kind of sound, especially certain animal cries or panting during strenuous activity.

Every ḍabḥ is therefore a ṣawt, but not every ṣawt is a ḍabḥ.

Human speech, birdsong, and the impact of implements may all be ṣawt without all being called ḍabḥ.

Study of Ṣ W T

Ḍ B Ḥ (ḍubāḥ) and Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣiyāḥ)

Ṣiyāḥ concerns cries and loud sounds.

Established uses of ḍubāḥ denote the cries of foxes and certain other animals.

The two overlap in the field of sound, but belong to different roots.

The Qur’an also uses ṣayḥah for cries associated with the destruction of peoples and events of the Resurrection.

Those uses should not be transferred to ḍabḥ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1.

Study of Ṣ Y Ḥ

Ḍ B Ḥ (ḍabḥ) and Ṣ H L (ṣahīl)

Ṣahīl denotes a horse’s neigh.

In its familiar application to a galloping horse, ḍabḥ expresses the sound of forceful panting.

A horse may both neigh and breathe forcefully while running in one situation, but the two sounds are lexically distinct.

Ḍ B Ḥ (ḍabḥ) and Q D Ḥ (qadḥ)

In its fire-related use, qadḥ concerns striking to produce sparks.

In Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:2, qadḥan accompanies al-mūriyāt.

Ḍabḥan in the preceding verse instead names the sound of panting.

The sparks and panting are therefore different parts of the scene.

Ḍabḥ should not be translated as sparks or hooves striking stone merely because it stands near qadḥ.

Ḍ B Ḥ (ḍabaḥa) and DH B Ḥ (dhabaḥa)

These two roots are close in spelling and sound but differ in meaning.

Dhabḥ concerns killing an animal by cutting its throat.

With ḍād, ḍabḥ concerns audible panting in its Qur’anic use, and discoloration through heat in the other branch.

Ḍabḥan in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1 consequently has no direct connection with slaughtering or sacrificing an animal.

Study of DH B Ḥ

Ḍ B Ḥ (ḍabḥ) and Ṣ B Ḥ (ṣubḥ)

Ṣubḥ, from Ṣ B Ḥ, concerns the beginning of the day.

Ṣubḥan occurs in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3.

In contrast, ḍabḥan in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1 belongs to Ḍ B Ḥ.

The words’ similar sound should not cause them to be confused.

Ḍabḥan expresses audible panting; ṣubḥan expresses the time of the raid.

Study of Ṣ B Ḥ

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

ضَبَحَ: cried out or panted audibly; in the other branch, darkened or lightly scorched something through heat.

يَضْبَحُ: cries out or produces a panting sound.

ضَبْح: audible panting; in other uses, an animal cry or the effect of heat.

ضُبَاح: a cry, particularly a fox’s.

ضَابِح: crying out or panting.

ضَوَابِح: those crying out or panting.

ضَبْحَة: a single cry or instance of ḍabḥ.

مَضْبُوح: heated, darkened, or lightly scorched.

ضَبِيح: something affected by fire or heat.

اِنْضَبَحَ: its color changed through heat.

اِنْضِبَاح: discoloration and darkening through heat.

For Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1:

وَالْعَادِيَاتِ ضَبْحًا

wa-al-ʿādiyāti ḍabḥan

“By the panting runners.”

This translation is suitable because it renders al-ʿādiyāt as runners and ḍabḥan as their panting while running.

A lexical note can add that ḍabḥ in this context is forceful breathing whose sound is audible.

“Neighing” is not an exact equivalent for ḍabḥ, because the ordinary neigh has another Arabic name.

“They ran swiftly” also fails to convey the independent meaning of ḍabḥ adequately: speed is understood from al-ʿādiyāt.

“With fire and sparks” likewise concerns another branch of the root or the next verse and should not replace ḍabḥ’s meaning in the first verse.

9. Conclusion

Ḍ B Ḥ has two main branches in Arabic: sound and panting, and discoloration through fire or sunlight.

In the first branch, ḍubāḥ denotes the cries of foxes and certain other animals. In the horse-related use, ḍabḥ concerns the sound of forceful breaths while running.

The second branch uses ḍabḥ for the effect of heat on wood, meat, stone, and the color of objects.

Although recorded within one lexical family, these branches should not be derived from a common image without historical evidence.

Only the form ضَبْحًا occurs in the Qur’an.

It accompanies al-ʿādiyāt in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1, expressing the audible panting of running beings.

The galloping-horse reading has clear support from established Arabic usage and the subsequent verses. Exegetical sources also report the camel reading, but the verse itself does not explicitly name the animal.

The base translation should therefore preserve both movement and panting, while the surah’s reflection examines the precise referent.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Cries and audible panting, especially while an animal runs; in another branch, darkening or discoloration through fire and sunlight.

Meaning in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1:

ضَبْحًا: audible, forceful panting while running; a condition attributed to the runners in the verse.
