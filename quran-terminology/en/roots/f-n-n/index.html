The Qur’anic root ف ن ن (fanan)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَفْنَانٍ
(afnānin)
Branches; genitive plural noun, Al-Raḥmān 55:48

فَنَن، فَنّ، فُنُون
(fanan; fann; funūn)
A branch; a kind or manner; varieties

Tree branches, variety, and diversity: from spreading boughs to different kinds of something

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root ف ن ن has two prominent semantic branches: a tree’s branches and branching, and the different kinds or forms of something.

Fanan denotes a tree branch, particularly one extending from the trunk or a larger branch. Its plural is afnān.

Fann denotes a kind, type, manner, or one of something’s different forms. Its usual plural is funūn; afnān is also recorded in this sense.

For example, فُنُونُ النَّبَاتِ concerns different kinds of plants, while فُنُونُ الْكَلَامِ concerns types and manners of speaking.

A semantic connection is understandable: a tree’s branches extend from one trunk in different directions, while kinds of something are different forms within a larger whole.

This resemblance alone does not prove that “kind” historically developed from “tree branch.” Establishing a historical course of semantic change requires independent evidence.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to the renowned pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī contains this line:

بُكَاءَ حَمَامَةٍ تَدْعُو هَدِيلًا

مُفَجَّعَةٍ عَلَى فَنَنٍ تُغَنِّي

Bukāʾa ḥamāmatin tadʿū hadīlan / mufajjaʿatin ʿalā fananin tughannī.

“Like the weeping of a bereaved dove calling for Hadīl, singing upon a branch.”

Here fanan is the branch on which the dove sits and sings. The usage is concrete and natural, referring to a tree branch.

The line is recorded in the collection attributed to al-Nābighah. This attribution is established in Arabic literary tradition, but, as with other early poetry preserved through later transmission and compiled manuscripts, the composition date of every individual line cannot be established with certainty.

With this limitation, the witness provides important support for the early meaning of fanan as a tree branch.

Poetry attributed to the same poet also uses كُلَّ فَنٍّ in describing different manners. This accords with “kind” or “manner,” showing both semantic branches within the early Arabic poetic tradition.

Classical lexicons sometimes describe fanan as an extended, straight branch and sometimes as one branching from a larger bough. Neither straightness nor a particular size should therefore be required of every instance.

Other uses of the family

In Arabic, this family is not confined to tree branches.

Afānīn is used for numerous branches as well as different methods and manners.

تَفَنَّنَ فِي الْكَلَامِ means using different forms and manners in one’s speech.

اِفْتَنَّ فِي حَدِيثِهِ likewise concerns moving among different kinds of speech or varying one’s expression.

Faynān is recorded as a description of long, beautiful hair. A connection with branches is understandable because locks of hair can hang and spread from the head like branches.

Other usages connect fanna with driving onward, troubling, or delaying. These meanings are recorded in lexicons, but their precise historical relationship to branches or diversity is unclear.

All forms in this family should therefore not be traced back to an invented image of branching.

For the Qur’anic occurrence, “tree branches” and “different kinds” are the principal relevant meanings.

2. Forms and semantic core

Recognized Arabic forms include:

Fanan, a tree branch; afnān, branches and sometimes kinds; fann, a kind or manner; funūn, different kinds and manners; afānīn, branches or varied methods; tafannana, to employ different manners; iftanna, to vary in method or speech; and faynān, describing long, beautiful hair.

Only one form from this family appears in the Qur’an:

أَفْنَانٍ — Al-Raḥmān 55:48

It is a plural noun, indefinite and genitive in the verse.

If its singular is fanan, it means tree branches.

If understood as the plural of fann, it refers to different kinds or varieties.

Both analyses have Arabic linguistic support; selecting the appropriate meaning requires attention to the sentence and context.

In the plant-related branch, fanan designates a branching part of a tree. In the fann branch, the subject is a different type or form of something.

These semantic fields may be related, but the very general definition “any kind of expansion” should not replace their particular meanings.

3. Al-Raḥmān 55:48: two gardens possessing afnān

ذَوَاتَا أَفْنَانٍ

Dhawātā afnān.

“Both possessing tree branches.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:48

This is the only Qur’anic verse containing afnān.

To identify what dhawātā refers to, we must consult the relevant preceding verse.

Al-Raḥmān 55:46 states:

وَلِمَنْ خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِ جَنَّتَانِ

Wa-li-man khāfa maqāma rabbihi jannatān.

“For whoever fears standing before their Lord, there are two gardens.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:46

Verse 48 describes these two gardens.

Dhawātā is a feminine dual form associated with dhāt, meaning “both possessing” or “both endowed with.” Syntactically, it can qualify jannatān or serve as the predicate of an omitted subject, “the two.”

Afnānin is its genitive complement.

The subject is therefore two gardens sharing a particular feature.

First reading: gardens with tree branches

On this reading, afnān is the plural of fanan.

The two gardens have trees with numerous spreading branches.

This sense fits the established early Arabic meaning of fanan and the garden setting. Trees putting out branches, spreading boughs, and creating a green environment form a natural, intelligible image.

The word’s direct meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from its consequences.

Afnān refers to branches. Abundant foliage, extensive shade, varied fruit, and beauty may accompany the image, but the word itself does not state all these features.

For example, branches can produce shade, but fanan does not mean “shade.”

Likewise, a branch may bear fruit, but afnān does not directly name the fruit.

“Two gardens with tree branches” thus preserves this reading’s clear meaning without adding other features to the word itself.

Second reading: gardens with different varieties

The other reading treats afnān as a plural of fann.

Fann means a kind or type. Accordingly, the verse may describe two gardens containing various blessings, plants, or forms of enjoyment.

This is not merely an imaginative interpretation: fann is an established Arabic word for a kind or type.

However, ذَوَاتَا أَفْنَانٍ does not explicitly name the varieties of blessing or fruit. If afnān means varieties, what they are varieties of is understood from the setting of the gardens.

Although the usual plural of fann meaning a kind is funūn, afnān is also recorded. The second reading cannot therefore be rejected simply because the plural form differs.

For branches, meanwhile, afnān is the familiar, direct plural of fanan, well supported by the garden setting.

“Branches” is therefore the clearer choice for the basic translation. Variety remains a valid linguistic possibility to discuss in the commentary.

The images can converge in a garden: abundant boughs and diverse plants both suggest its richness. Their visual proximity does not justify combining the two separate senses with “and” in the basic translation.

4. Afnān and nearby verses in Al-Raḥmān

After describing the gardens in Al-Raḥmān 55:48, verse 50 says:

فِيهِمَا عَيْنَانِ تَجْرِيَانِ

Fīhimā ʿaynāni tajriyān.

“In both are two flowing springs.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:50

Verse 52 then states:

فِيهِمَا مِنْ كُلِّ فَاكِهَةٍ زَوْجَانِ

Fīhimā min kulli fākihatin zawjān.

“In both are two kinds of every fruit.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:52

On the branches reading, this passage describes three distinct features: tree branches, flowing springs, and varied fruits.

If afnān means tree branches, the image begins with the gardens’ boughs before introducing springs and fruit.

If afnān means different kinds, the initial description concerns the gardens’ variety, and the subsequent verses give particular examples of their blessings.

Both readings relate to the passage, but neither should cause other words’ clear meanings to be transferred into afnān.

ʿAynān names two springs, and fākihah means fruit. These words belong to other roots.

Al-Raḥmān 55:62 introduces two further gardens. Features of all the gardens in the surah should not be merged without considering pronoun reference and verse sequence.

In Al-Raḥmān 55:48, dhawātā refers to the two gardens mentioned in verse 46.

Does afnān mean beauty, shade, or abundant blessings?

Some early explanations discuss afnān in terms of the branches’ shade, spreading trees, or abundant varieties of blessing.

Such explanations can describe natural consequences of having branches or different varieties.

Yet “beauty,” “shade,” “abundant fruit,” and “an extensive garden” are not direct synonyms of fanan.

A garden may offer more shade through its many branches, or gain beauty from varied trees. These are implications of the garden image, rather than meanings all contained within afnān itself.

Translation should preserve the word’s meaning, leaving supplementary features to contextual explanation.

5. Distinctions from related roots

ف ن ن (fanan) and ش ج ر (shajar)

In its familiar sense, shajar concerns trees and woody growth.

Fanan, however, is a branch of a tree.

A tree can have a trunk, branches, leaves, and fruit. Shajar concerns the tree itself or a collection of trees, whereas fanan identifies a branching part of it.

A tree may have many branches, but the names for tree and branch are not synonymous.

Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root ش ج ر examines trees, branching, and the semantic connections of that family.

ف ن ن (fanan) and ف ر ع (farʿ)

Farʿ concerns an upper part, branch, or part extending from an origin.

In Ibrāhīm 14:24, the parable of a good tree describes its root as firm and its branch as reaching into the sky. This reference illustrates the relationship between a foundation and what extends from it.

Farʿ and fanan can overlap when describing tree branches.

Farʿ has a broader semantic field concerning an upper part or an offshoot from an origin; fanan in its botanical use names a tree branch.

This does not establish that every farʿ is necessarily a main bough and every fanan a smaller branch. Neither word absolutely determines a branch’s size or position.

ف ن ن (fanan) and غ ص ن (ghuṣn)

Ghuṣn is an Arabic word for a tree branch and one of fanan’s closest equivalents.

Both can describe tree branches.

Lexical explanations sometimes restrict fanan to an extended branch or an offshoot from a larger bough. Yet the evidence does not establish an absolute boundary covering every use of the two words.

Ghuṣn does not occur in this form in the Qur’an. This is therefore a linguistic comparison, not a comparison of two Qur’anic occurrences.

ف ن ن (fanan) and ث م ر (thamar)

Thamar concerns the fruit and produce of trees and plants.

Fanan is a tree branch, which may or may not bear fruit.

Al-Raḥmān 55:52 mentions fākihah. These fruits belong within the garden-and-branches setting, but afnān itself does not name fruits.

Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root ث م ر examines tree produce, fruit, and yield.

ف ن ن (fanan) and ف ن ي (faniya)

Despite their written resemblance, ف ن ي and ف ن ن are distinct roots.

Faniya concerns coming to an end or passing away.

Al-Raḥmān 55:26 states كُلُّ مَنْ عَلَيْهَا فَانٍ: “Everyone upon it is passing away.” The reference is to those upon the earth.

Fānin belongs to ف ن ي, not ف ن ن.

Afnān in Al-Raḥmān 55:48, by contrast, refers to branches or different varieties.

The words’ proximity within one surah does not establish a derivational relationship.

6. Funūn, tafannun, and the modern meaning of art

In modern Arabic, fann also means art and artistic skills; funūn can designate different fields of art.

Earlier usages had a broader range: kind, type, manner, or one of the different forms of an activity or subject.

For example, funūn al-kalām concerned different manners of speaking, not necessarily art in the modern sense of painting, music, or drama.

Translating afnān in Al-Raḥmān 55:48 as “arts” or “artworks” is therefore incorrect.

The modern artistic sense is a more specialized use of this family and should not be projected backward.

Arabic tafannun likewise concerns variety in method and expression, but this form does not occur in the Qur’an.

These uses help explain the family’s range; the Qur’anic meaning must nevertheless be established from afnān and the setting of the two gardens.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For botanical fanan, “tree branch” is the most suitable equivalent.

For afnān in this sense, “branches” or “boughs” is natural.

The Persian equivalent shākhsārhā can convey a broader image of branching trees, but for a concise direct translation, “branches” is clearer.

For fann in the other branch, “kind,” “type,” and “manner” are suitable.

Depending on context, funūn can mean “kinds,” “different manners,” or, in modern usage, “arts.”

Tafannana can be rendered “employed different methods” or “introduced variety into work or speech.”

These forms are absent from the Qur’an and are discussed to explain the linguistic family.

The principal proposal for Al-Raḥmān 55:48 is:

ذَوَاتَا أَفْنَانٍ

Dhawātā afnān.

“Both possessing tree branches.”

The alternative reading may be explained as follows:

Arabic afnān can also be the plural of fann, meaning a kind or type. On this reading, the phrase can refer to the two gardens’ varied blessings and plants.

The basic translation should select one sense and discuss the alternative separately. “Possessing branches and kinds of blessings” combines two lexical analyses without showing their difference.

Conclusion

Arabic ف ن ن has two prominent semantic branches: tree branches and different kinds of something.

Fanan means a tree branch, with the plural afnān. Poetry attributed to al-Nābighah illustrates this sense in its clear image of a dove sitting on a branch.

Fann means a type or manner. Its usual plural is funūn, though afnān is also recorded in this sense.

The family appears once in the Qur’an, as afnānin in Al-Raḥmān 55:48.

There, the two gardens possess afnān. Tree branches fit both the plural of fanan and the garden setting. Different varieties is also linguistically defensible, but “branches” is more precise and direct as the basic translation.

An image-based connection between a branching tree and diverse forms is understandable. It does not establish that every use of this family developed from a single historical origin.

Final definition for Forqan:

ف ن ن: a linguistic family with two main branches: fanan, a tree branch or offshoot; and fann, a kind, type, or manner of something. Fanan has the plural afnān, while the usual plural of fann is funūn; afnān is also recorded for varieties.

In Al-Raḥmān 55:48, afnānin most likely refers to the trees’ branches in the two gardens. The reading “different varieties” also has lexical support and remains a contextual possibility for discussion.
