1. Root Meaning and Word Structure

In Arabic, zukhruf denotes gold, ornament, decorative patterns, and striking adornment. It is used both for tangible beauty, such as gold, house decoration, and the colors of vegetation, and for speech adorned with pleasing words and expressions.

A central feature of this word family is the display or adornment that makes something prominent and attractive to the eye or ear. Such adornment may be genuine beauty, or a covering used to conceal the truth.

Arabic dictionaries record both “gold” and “adornment” as meanings of zukhruf. They differ over which meaning came first.

The Andalusian lexicographer Ibn Sīdah treats gold as the original meaning and explains that zukhruf was subsequently extended to every kind of ornament and adornment. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, by contrast, defines zukhruf as al-zīnah al-muzawwaqah, ornament embellished with decorative work, and explains the designation of gold from within that semantic field.

These accounts agree that both meanings exist. Their difference concerns the direction of semantic extension: did the name for gold extend to adornment generally, or was a word for adornment applied to the metal because of its brilliance and value?

The available evidence establishes both uses in Arabic. Demonstrating their historical sequence would require older, independent evidence. It is therefore preferable to recognize gold and striking adornment as two documented, closely related uses without presenting either as the certain historical source of the other.

Lisān al-ʿArab and al-Rāghib: gold and adornment

Z KH R F is a four-consonant root. Its verb zakhrafa follows the pattern faʿlala. Unlike roots such as Z L Z L or W S W S, zakhrafa does not repeat a two-consonant sequence. Its quadriliteral structure alone therefore does not establish repetition or intensity.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris places the word among items with more than three consonants and records adornment, gold, and patterns on the surface of water among its meanings.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the quadriliteral entry

Nor should Z KH R F be derived from the triliteral Z KH R without evidence. Lexical uses of zakhara concern rising water, abundance, and growing vegetation, but the shared initial three consonants alone do not prove a historical relationship.

2. Lexical Forms and Early Arabic Evidence

The noun zukhruf has a broad range of uses in Arabic, and several verbs and derivatives belong to its family.

زُخْرُف — zukhruf: Gold, ornament, or striking adornment; also something made attractive and conspicuous through decoration and patterns.

زَخَارِف — zakhārif: The plural of zukhruf: ornaments, decorative patterns, and various forms of adornment. In some early uses, it also names particular objects or creatures.

زَخْرَفَ — zakhrafa: To adorn, decorate with patterns, or beautify the appearance of something. Zakhrafa al-bayta means to decorate a house and enhance its beauty.

زَخْرَفَة — zakhrafah: The verbal noun of zakhrafa: the act of adorning, decorating, or producing ornamental patterns.

مُزَخْرَف — muzakhraf: A passive participle describing something adorned with decoration and patterns, such as a house, a piece of writing, or an object whose appearance has been embellished.

تَزَخْرَفَ — tazakhrafa: To adorn oneself or become adorned. Tazakhrafa al-rajulu describes a man adorning himself.

Arabic uses of zukhruf are not confined to gold and buildings.

Zakhārif al-māʾ denotes patterns or lines appearing on the surface of water. This use shows that the word can concern visual decoration and surface patterns even when neither gold nor a precious object is involved.

Lisān al-ʿArab and some other sources also record zakhārif as a name for small flying creatures moving over water. Kurāʿ interprets it as a kind of bird. These are early lexical explanations, insufficient to identify a species in modern zoological terms.

The following verse is attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar:

تَذَكَّرَ عَيْنًا مِنْ غُمَازَ، وَمَاؤُهَا
لَهُ حَدَبٌ تَسْتَنُّ فِيهِ الزَّخَارِفُ

tadhakkara ʿaynan min Ghumāza wa-māʾuhā / lahu ḥadabun tastannu fīhi al-zakhārifu

“He recalled a spring at Ghumāz: its water had raised ripples, in which the zakhārif moved.” This explanatory rendering follows the wording quoted; the disputed referent of zakhārif is discussed below.

Lexical transmissions and their references to the printed Dīwān record variants such as min Ghumāzata māʾuhā and ḥabab in place of ḥadab. In the wording quoted here, the poet recalls a spring whose water has movement and raised ripples, with zakhārif moving in it.

This witness is useful for the history of the word, but it should not automatically be translated as “ornaments.” Lisān al-ʿArab explains zakhārif in this verse as small creatures over water; Ibn Durayd, by contrast, connects it with the broken surface and ripples of flowing water in Jamharat al-Lughah. The verse thus attests the plural in early poetry, while its precise referent also depends on textual variants and interpretation.

Al-Mukhaṣṣaṣ: the poetic witness and variants

The same poet is also quoted using zakhārifan qushuban, “fresh ornaments” or “new decorative patterns,” in describing the remaining traces of an old dwelling place. This use is closer to the general meaning of ornament and patterned decoration.

Dīwān of Aws ibn Ḥajar, edited by Muḥammad Yūsuf Najm: fresh ornaments

Lexical sources also record zakhrafa al-kalāma: he adorned speech, giving it arrangement and an attractive appearance.

This use is significant because language, like a house or an object, can be adorned. Just as decorative patterns make a building pleasing, the choice of words, the rhythm of a sentence, and the manner of expression can make speech attractive and effective.

Yet the beauty of speech is in itself evidence neither of its truth nor of its falsehood. Evaluating it also requires attention to its content and the speaker’s purpose.

3. Qur’anic Uses: From the Earth’s Adornment to Embellished Speech

Z KH R F occurs four times in the Qur’an, each time as the noun zukhruf, with differences of case or an attached pronoun. No verb or other derivative from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four nominal occurrences

These four occurrences illuminate different aspects of the word’s semantic range.

The Earth’s Zukhruf: Natural Adornment

Yūnus 10:24

إِنَّمَا مَثَلُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا كَمَاءٍ أَنْزَلْنَاهُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَاخْتَلَطَ بِهِ نَبَاتُ الْأَرْضِ مِمَّا يَأْكُلُ النَّاسُ وَالْأَنْعَامُ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَخَذَتِ الْأَرْضُ زُخْرُفَهَا وَازَّيَّنَتْ وَظَنَّ أَهْلُهَا أَنَّهُمْ قَادِرُونَ عَلَيْهَا أَتَاهَا أَمْرُنَا لَيْلًا أَوْ نَهَارًا فَجَعَلْنَاهَا حَصِيدًا كَأَنْ لَمْ تَغْنَ بِالْأَمْسِ كَذَٰلِكَ نُفَصِّلُ الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

innamā mathalu al-ḥayāti al-dunyā ka-māʾin anzalnāhu mina al-samāʾi fa-khtalaṭa bihi nabātu al-arḍi mimmā yaʾkulu al-nāsu wa-l-anʿāmu ḥattā idhā akhadhati al-arḍu zukhrufahā wa-zzayyanat wa-ẓanna ahluhā annahum qādirūna ʿalayhā atāhā amrunā laylan aw nahāran fa-jaʿalnāhā ḥaṣīdan ka-an lam taghna bi-l-amsi kadhālika nufaṣṣilu al-āyāti li-qawmin yatafakkarūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The likeness of worldly life is only like water We sent down from the sky, with which the earth’s vegetation mingled, of what people and livestock eat. Then, when the earth had taken on its adornment and become beautiful, and its people thought themselves able to control it, Our command came to it by night or day, and We made it a harvested field, as though it had not flourished yesterday. Thus We set out the signs in detail for people who reflect.”

Here, zukhrufahā denotes the earth’s adornment: beauty arising from vegetation, flourishing growth, and the colors of nature.

The verse describes land that has reached a stage of flourishing beauty after receiving water. Plants have grown, and the scene has become so pleasing that its people imagine themselves in control of its condition.

Akhadhati al-arḍu zukhrufahā pictures the earth reaching a state of adornment and splendor. Zukhruf here is neither gold nor artificial decoration; it is the earth’s tangible, natural beauty.

The following wa-zzayyanat, “and became adorned,” is also significant. The Qur’an places two closely related expressions together: the earth has taken on its zukhruf and become adorned.

Their combination may emphasize the full appearance and flourishing of the land, but it does not prove that zukhruf and zīnah are entirely synonymous in every use. Here zukhruf highlights nature’s visible, colorful display, while izzayyanat describes its becoming adorned.

Another important point is that zukhruf has no negative meaning here. The land’s beauty is real. Its plants, colors, and flourishing are not merely a covering intended to deceive.

The warning concerns the impermanence of this condition and the assumption of absolute control over it. Even at the height of its beauty, the land can lose that condition through a sudden event.

The verse therefore does not establish that every ornament or form of beauty is inherently deceptive or false. The issue is the distinction between real beauty and a mistaken assumption people may build upon it.

Zukhruf of Speech: Adornment Used to Deceive

Al-Anʿām 6:112

وَكَذَٰلِكَ جَعَلْنَا لِكُلِّ نَبِيٍّ عَدُوًّا شَيَاطِينَ الْإِنْسِ وَالْجِنِّ يُوحِي بَعْضُهُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ زُخْرُفَ الْقَوْلِ غُرُورًا وَلَوْ شَاءَ رَبُّكَ مَا فَعَلُوهُ فَذَرْهُمْ وَمَا يَفْتَرُونَ

wa-kadhālika jaʿalnā li-kulli nabiyyin ʿaduwwan shayāṭīna al-insi wa-l-jinni yūḥī baʿḍuhum ilā baʿḍin zukhrufa al-qawli ghurūran wa-law shāʾa rabbuka mā faʿalūhu fa-dharhum wa-mā yaftarūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Thus We appointed for every prophet enemies from the devils of humankind and jinn, some of them suggesting embellished speech to others for deception. Had your Lord willed, they would not have done it. So leave them with what they fabricate.”

Here zukhruf moves from the realm of objects and tangible beauty to that of speech.

Zukhrufa al-qawli denotes speech embellished through verbal ornament, attractive expressions, and a pleasing manner of delivery. Such speech may appear orderly, beautiful, and persuasive.

The verse, however, places it alongside ghurūran, “for deception.” This qualification shows that, in this use, the adornment of speech serves to deceive and mislead.

The negative force of the expression thus does not arise solely from zukhruf. It emerges from its relation to ghurūran, to the communication between the parties, and to the setting of enmity and fabrication in the verse.

This distinction matters for translation. Zukhrufa al-qawli directly denotes “the adornment of speech” or “embellished speech.” In this verse’s context, that adornment becomes an instrument of deception.

If zukhruf is defined as “falsehood” in every use from the outset, the independent contribution of ghurūran is weakened. Such a universal definition would also fail to fit the positive or neutral use in Yūnus 10:24. The explanation given by some lexicographers of zukhruf al-qawl as speech embellished with lies concerns this particular expression and its deceptive context.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the earth’s adornment and embellished speech

The next verse, Al-Anʿām 6:113, describes the inclination of the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter, their approval of such speech, and their continuing conduct. This connection shows how verbal embellishment can help a false claim gain acceptance and spread.

The Qur’an thus distinguishes the attractive appearance of speech from the actual validity of its content.

Speech may be beautiful, rhythmic, impassioned, and apparently reasoned. None of these features alone proves it true. Evaluation requires examining the claim’s truth, its evidence, and its correspondence to reality.

This is not a condemnation of eloquence, rhetoric, or beauty of expression. True speech can also be beautiful and effective. What this verse condemns is using verbal adornment to deceive.

A House of Zukhruf: Gold and Material Splendor

Al-Isrāʾ 17:93

أَوْ يَكُونَ لَكَ بَيْتٌ مِنْ زُخْرُفٍ أَوْ تَرْقَىٰ فِي السَّمَاءِ وَلَنْ نُؤْمِنَ لِرُقِيِّكَ حَتَّىٰ تُنَزِّلَ عَلَيْنَا كِتَابًا نَقْرَؤُهُ قُلْ سُبْحَانَ رَبِّي هَلْ كُنْتُ إِلَّا بَشَرًا رَسُولًا

aw yakūna laka baytun min zukhrufin aw tarqā fī al-samāʾi wa-lan nuʾmina li-ruqiyyika ḥattā tunazzila ʿalaynā kitāban naqraʾuhu qul subḥāna rabbī hal kuntu illā basharan rasūlan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Or you have a house of gold, or ascend into the sky. Yet we will never believe in your ascent until you bring down to us a book that we can read. Say: Glory be to my Lord! Am I anything but a human messenger?”

Here zukhruf occurs in baytun min zukhrufin, “a house of zukhruf.”

The preposition min is important. In a natural reading, it indicates the material from which the house is made or by which it is characterized.

Accordingly, “gold” fits this use better than the general meaning “adornment.” The demand concerns a golden house, or one whose splendor is identified with gold.

Arabic dictionaries also cite this verse as an important witness to zukhruf meaning gold.

The context of Al-Isrāʾ 17:90, Al-Isrāʾ 17:91, Al-Isrāʾ 17:92, and Al-Isrāʾ 17:93 shows that this demand belongs to a series of conditions opponents of the Prophet proposed for accepting his call.

They place possession of a golden house alongside demands such as producing a spring, a garden, and an ascent into the sky.

The golden house is therefore an example of material splendor demanded as a condition or sign.

This use does not mean that gold or a beautiful house is itself false. The issue is making material demands and extraordinary displays the criterion for accepting prophethood.

Here zukhruf names something valuable and conspicuous. The problem with the demand emerges from the opponents’ speech in context, rather than from the lexical meaning of gold.

Zukhruf and the Luxury of Worldly Life

Al-Zukhruf 43:35

وَزُخْرُفًا وَإِنْ كُلُّ ذَٰلِكَ لَمَّا مَتَاعُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةُ عِنْدَ رَبِّكَ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ

wa-zukhrufan wa-in kullu dhālika lammā matāʿu al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-l-ākhiratu ʿinda rabbika li-l-muttaqīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“And gold and ornament. All of that is only the enjoyment of worldly life; the Hereafter with your Lord is for the God-conscious.”

This verse continues Al-Zukhruf 43:33 and Al-Zukhruf 43:34, which mention silver roofs for houses, stairways, doors, and couches on which people recline.

Within this sequence, wa-zukhrufan refers to gold and ornament, or conspicuous luxuries added to those amenities.

The mention of silver in Al-Zukhruf 43:33 also supports preferring “gold.” After silver has been named in the description of splendid houses, zukhrufan can refer to gold alongside it.

The broader sense of decoration and luxury is nevertheless intelligible here. Some lexical and exegetical accounts identify zukhruf specifically as gold in this verse, while others explain it through decorative work and the adornment of houses.

Grammatically, zukhrufan is accusative and connected to the items listed in the preceding verses. Disagreement over its precise syntactic attachment should not obscure the distinction between gold and adornment.

Al-Ṭabarī: gold, decoration, and two syntactic analyses

The context chiefly establishes the limited standing of wealth and material luxury.

Al-Zukhruf 43:33 presents these amenities conditionally: “Were it not that people would become one community, We would have made…” The point is that, without that restraint, such luxuries would have been provided even for those who reject al-Raḥmān; it is not a report that all these amenities were actually given to them. Al-Zukhruf 43:35 then emphasizes that all of this is worldly enjoyment, while the Hereafter with the Lord is for the God-conscious.

Al-Ṭabarī: the conditional account of luxury

This shows that magnificent houses, gold, silver, and material resources do not in themselves establish piety or a person’s higher standing with God.

At the same time, this verse alone does not rule that gold, beautiful architecture, or home decoration is forbidden. Its subject is the distinction between material possession and the ultimate measure of human worth.

The Qur’an’s forty-third surah is also named Al-Zukhruf, a name connected to this word and the discussion of worldly splendor and luxury.

4. Zukhruf Compared with Adornment, Gold, Luxury, and Deception

Distinguishing zukhruf from related words prevents a contextual meaning from becoming a universal definition of the root.

Zīnah: A broad term for adornment and beauty. In the Qur’an it can concern the sky, the earth, people, clothing, and other forms of beauty. Zukhruf overlaps with it, but often highlights a conspicuous display, decorative patterns, or elaborate ornament. This is a tendency in usage, rather than an absolute boundary valid in every instance.

Dhahab: The specific name of the metal gold. Zukhruf has this meaning in some uses, but cannot always be translated as gold. Zukhrufa al-qawli in Al-Anʿām 6:112 and zukhrufahā concerning the earth in Yūnus 10:24 demonstrate its wider range.

Taraf: Concerns affluence, pampered ease, and abundant resources. A person may live comfortably and also have an adorned house, but affluence and outward decoration are different concepts. Taraf primarily concerns a condition of abundance, while zukhruf concerns the display or adornment of something.

Study of T R F

Ghurūr: Deception and an assumption that leads a person away from reality. Zukhruf may serve deception, but adornment is not always deceptive. In Al-Anʿām 6:112, the connection between the two words makes the embellished speech deceptive in context.

Zūr: Concerns falsehood, what contradicts truth, and wrongful testimony or speech. False speech may be plain and unadorned; conversely, embellished speech may be entirely true. Zūr and zukhruf are therefore not synonyms, though they can occur together in deceptive speech.

These comparisons show that zukhruf concerns how something appears and is adorned, rather than its inherent truth or falsehood.

Gold can be ornament; plants can beautify the land; patterns can adorn a house; and attractive expressions can give speech a pleasing appearance.

The actual value of each must nevertheless be assessed in relation to its truth, function, and context.

5. Semantic Connections and the Limits of Etymology

The four Qur’anic uses of zukhruf initially seem different: once concerning speech, once land, and twice gold and luxury.

There is nevertheless a clear semantic connection among them.

Gold is conspicuous because of its brilliance and value. Patterns and ornaments adorn a house or object. Colorful vegetation brings the earth to a state of flourishing beauty. Carefully chosen words and expressions can likewise make speech attractive and pleasing to a listener.

All these uses concern display and adornment, although the kind of display differs.

This connection can explain the word’s semantic range without requiring the claim that pre-Qur’anic Arabs consciously created every one of these uses from the image of shining gold.

Nor does every instance of zukhruf represent a false layer concealing an ugly truth. That understanding fits only certain deceptive uses and does not fit the earth’s natural beauty in Yūnus 10:24.

There is an important distinction between adornment and deceptive adornment.

Adornment may enhance real beauty. When used to conceal weak evidence, distort the truth, or win misplaced trust, however, it becomes an instrument of deception.

The negative force of zukhruf in Al-Anʿām 6:112 should therefore not be imposed on all its other uses.

Conversely, its positive or neutral uses should not prevent recognition of the dangerous role verbal embellishment can play in deceiving people.

In the Qur’an, these distinctions emerge from the word’s connection with the other components of its sentence: zukhrufa al-qawli ghurūran, akhadhati al-arḍu zukhrufahā, baytun min zukhrufin, and wa-zukhrufan.

In each case, the word retains its basic meaning, while its referent and role are determined by context.

6. Conclusion and Translation Proposals

Z KH R F is an Arabic quadriliteral root whose family denotes gold, ornament, adornment, and conspicuous display. Lexical sources attest both gold and adornment as established meanings of zukhruf, but the certain historical priority of either remains unclear.

Arabic usage shows applications to decorative patterns in houses, the adornment of objects, the beauty of water surfaces, and embellished speech.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an, solely as the noun zukhruf.

In Yūnus 10:24 it denotes the earth’s natural beauty and colors. In Al-Anʿām 6:112, its conjunction with ghurūran makes verbal embellishment an instrument of deception. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:93, gold is prominent in baytun min zukhrufin; in Al-Zukhruf 43:35, the word concerns gold or the conspicuous luxuries of worldly life.

The root should therefore be confined neither to deception and falsehood nor to gold alone.

Brief root meaning: Gold, ornament, and striking adornment; decorative patterns and display that make something beautiful and conspicuous.

Meanings of the Qur’anic forms and translation proposals:

  • Zukhrufa al-qawli — Al-Anʿām 6:112: The adornment of speech; embellished speech used to deceive in this verse’s context. Proposed translation: “embellished speech.”
  • Zukhrufahā — Yūnus 10:24: The earth’s natural adornment and display, especially the beauty of flourishing vegetation. Proposed translation: “its adornment” or “its ornament.”
  • Min zukhrufin — Al-Isrāʾ 17:93: Of gold. Proposed translation: “of gold.”
  • Wa-zukhrufan — Al-Zukhruf 43:35: Gold and splendid ornament. Proposed translation: “and gold and ornament.”

A brief root definition should work across these uses, while each form’s translation must be determined by its sentence structure and context.

The key conclusion is that zukhruf is inherently neither falsehood nor truth; it names adornment and display. That adornment may be nature’s real beauty, a display of material splendor, or something used to deceive others.

In the Qur’an, these uses cannot be distinguished from zukhruf alone. Their differences become clear through its connection with the subject, purpose, and context of the discourse.
