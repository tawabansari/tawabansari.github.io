Root w-z-ʿ — Restraining, regulating, and inspiring action

Qur’anic and lexical forms

يُوزَعُونَ
(yūzaʿūn)
They are restrained; passive Form I

أَوْزِعْنِي
(awziʿnī)
Enable me; Form IV imperative

وَزَعَ
(wazaʿa)
He restrained; Form I

وَازِع
(wāziʿ)
Restrainer; active participle, non-Qur’anic

وَزُوع
(wazūʿ)
Inclination; non-Qur’anic lexical use

تَوْزِيع
(tawzīʿ)
Distribution; Form II verbal noun, non-Qur’anic

The Arabic root w-z-ʿ concerns restraining, preventing advance or dispersal, and controlling conduct and movement. This may concern a person and inward desires, or a group whose movement needs coordination.

Another branch concerns prompting, fostering inclination, and motivating action. In the Qur’an, it appears in Solomon’s prayer and the prayer of a person who has reached maturity, asking God for help in giving thanks and doing righteous deeds.

The root occurs five times in the Qur’an: three instances of يُوزَعُونَ and two of أَوْزِعْنِي.

The first three concern assembled groups whose movement is restrained. The other two are prayers for enabling help to give thanks and act rightly.

The distinction matters. يُوزَعُونَ does not mean “they are distributed” in the modern Persian sense of tawzīʿ; nor can أَوْزِعْنِي simply mean “stop me.”

Understanding the root requires distinguishing restraint and control from prompting and enabling action, then examining their semantic connections.

1. The root in early Arabic

Restraining and regulating conduct

A clear witness occurs in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī:

عَلَى حِينِ عَاتَبْتُ الْمَشِيبَ عَلَى الصِّبَا

ʿalā ḥīni ʿātabtu al-mashība ʿalā al-ṣibā

وَقُلْتُ أَلَمَّا أَصْحُ وَالشَّيْبُ وَازِعُ

wa-qultu a-lammā aṣḥu wa-al-shaybu wāziʿu

“When I reproached grey hair for youthful passion, I said: Have I still not come to my senses, though grey hair is a restrainer?”

The poet speaks of growing old and grey while still retaining some youthful attachments.

He asks why, despite his age, he remains inclined to conduct at odds with his experience and years.

وَازِع in this line means a restraining influence.

Grey hair does not physically force anyone to refrain. It can, however, signal the passing of life and prompt reconsideration of conduct.

Here, a wāziʿ is something that restrains continued behavior or at least calls a person to refrain from it.

The witness connects w-z-ʿ in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period with regulating conduct and checking an inclination.

Such restraint need not be physical coercion. In this line it may arise from awareness of one’s age and circumstances.

The verse appears in Al-Nābighah’s collected poetry and early Arabic grammatical works. أَلَمَّا تَصْحُ is also reported beside أَلَمَّا أَصْحُ; the variation does not change the meaning of وَازِع.

Regulating collective movement

Arabic dictionaries record وَزَعْتُ الْجَيْشَ for restraining and coordinating an army’s movement.

The point is to prevent a large body of troops from moving without control or coordination.

If those at the front outpace the others or those behind become detached, the army loses cohesion.

Some are therefore held back so that others can catch up and the whole can advance in order.

وَازِع can also designate someone charged with preventing unauthorized advance or dispersal.

This extends restraint from individual conduct to managing collective movement.

Its inclusion in later dictionaries does not establish that every detail of military organization described there existed in the same form before Islam.

The poetic witness supports restraint in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Regulating an army is also attested in classical Arabic, and the Qur’an itself offers a clear example.

Prompting and fostering inclination

Another branch of the family is represented by أَوْزَعَ and وَزُوع.

أَوْزَعَهُ الشَّيْءَ is used for inspiring or prompting something in a person.

أَوْزَعَهُ بِالشَّيْءِ is also recorded for making someone attached or inclined to something.

A reading of another verse by Al-Nābighah has يُوزِعُهُ in a hunting scene. Its wording and interpretation vary across transmissions; it should not alone establish the history of the sense “to prompt.”

The family is thus not confined to prevention: some constructions concern motivating someone toward an action.

Dictionaries also record وَزُوع for strong inclination or attachment.

These meanings do not imply that every use of awzaʿa describes forcing someone to act against their will.

Prompting, fostering inclination, and inspiring can differ according to construction and context.

The relationship between the branches

Restraint and prompting can be connected through the directing of conduct.

A person may be held back from one action and encouraged toward another.

For example, restraining harmful conduct may allow attention and effort to turn toward something beneficial.

This explains a possible semantic relationship; it does not prove that inspiration historically developed from restraint.

Lexical sources explain أَوْزِعْنِي both through inspiration and prompting and through restraining a person from other activities.

A historical account should preserve both branches rather than declaring one the certain origin of the other without evidence.

2. Forms and semantic centers

Important forms in Arabic include:

وَزَعَ: he restrained, prevented, or controlled someone’s movement or conduct.

يَزَعُ: he restrains or checks.

وَزْع: restraint, prevention, and control.

وَازِع: a restrainer; a person or influence preventing an action or departure from order.

وَزَعَة: restrainers or people entrusted with maintaining order and checking conduct.

أَوْزَعَ: in one usage, he inspired, prompted, or inclined someone toward an action.

إِيزَاع: prompting or imparting motivation; senses involving dispersal are also recorded.

وَزُوع: strong attachment or inclination.

وَزَّعَ: he divided or distributed something among people or portions.

تَوْزِيع: division and distribution among people or parts.

تَوَزَّعَ: it became divided, or a group divided something among themselves.

أَوْزَاع: separate groups or divisions of people, recorded for dispersed gatherings.

These forms are known in Arabic, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

Forms actually used in the Qur’an

The Qur’an uses only two verbal constructions from this root.

يُوزَعُونَ: the passive imperfect of Form I وَزَعَ, in Al-Naml 27:17, Al-Naml 27:83, and Fuṣṣilat 41:19.

أَوْزِعْنِي: a Form IV imperative with the object pronoun -nī, in Al-Naml 27:19 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:15.

All three instances of يُوزَعُونَ have the same form.

Both prayers beginning with أَوْزِعْنِي likewise use the same construction.

Forms such as wāziʿ, wazaʿah, tawzīʿ, and awzāʿ do not occur in the Qur’an.

Semantic centers

Two clear definitions are more useful for the Qur’anic occurrences than one overly broad definition.

The restraining branch: controlling movement or conduct and preventing advance, dispersal, or departure from the intended order.

The prompting branch: fostering motivation, inclination, or enabling help for a particular action.

They may overlap in directing conduct, but each has its own Qur’anic construction and use.

Thus يُوزَعُونَ cannot simply mean “they are inspired,” nor أَوْزِعْنِي simply “restrain me.”

3. يُوزَعُونَ: restraining and keeping groups in order

Al-Naml 27:17 — Solomon’s troops

وَحُشِرَ لِسُلَيْمَانَ جُنُودُهُ مِنَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنْسِ وَالطَّيْرِ فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ

wa-ḥushira li-sulaymāna junūduhu mina al-jinni wa-al-insi wa-al-ṭayri fa-hum yūzaʿūn

Al-Naml 27:17

“And Solomon’s troops of jinn, humans, and birds were assembled for him, and they were kept under restraint and in order.”

The verse contains two verbs from distinct roots.

حُشِرَ, from ḥ-sh-r, means being assembled.

يُوزَعُونَ, from w-z-ʿ, means being restrained and kept in order.

The distinction matters.

Assembling people does not necessarily coordinate their movement.

Many may gather in one place but move without coordination: some advance, others fall behind, and some leave the route.

Ḥashr expresses the assembling of the troops here.

Wazʿ concerns their regulation within that assembly.

Why does the distinction matter in Solomon’s army?

Three groups of troops are named: jinn, humans, and birds.

They are different kinds of beings, yet form part of Solomon’s forces.

Coordinating movement matters in such a gathering.

فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ indicates that their movement was not uncontrolled and scattered.

The verse does not specify troop numbers, commanders, or the precise formation.

Nor does the passive يُوزَعُونَ identify those directly responsible for regulating movement.

What is clear is that the troops were restrained and kept in order.

Connection with the valley of the ants

In Al-Naml 27:18, the troops reach the valley of the ants. An ant warns the others to enter their dwellings so that Solomon and his troops do not unknowingly crush them.

In Al-Naml 27:19, Solomon smiles at the ant’s words and prays to God.

The sequence describes an organized army on the move whose presence could endanger small creatures even without any intention to harm them.

The verses do not state that Solomon ordered the army to halt or relocated the ants in any particular way.

Such details should therefore not be extracted from يُوزَعُونَ.

Here the root concerns controlling and coordinating collective movement.

Al-Naml 27:83 — Groups of those who deny the signs

وَيَوْمَ نَحْشُرُ مِنْ كُلِّ أُمَّةٍ فَوْجًا مِمَّنْ يُكَذِّبُ بِآيَاتِنَا فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ

wa-yawma naḥshuru min kulli ummatin fawjan mimman yukadhdhibu bi-āyātinā fa-hum yūzaʿūn

Al-Naml 27:83

“And the day We assemble from every community a group of those who deny Our signs, and they are kept under restraint and in order.”

Ḥashr and wazʿ again occur together.

The context, however, differs from Solomon’s troops.

Al-Naml 27:17 concerned Solomon’s forces in worldly life.

Al-Naml 27:83 concerns assembling groups who deny God’s signs.

مِنْ كُلِّ أُمَّةٍ فَوْجًا matters: the sentence specifies a group from each community, rather than explicitly naming every member of every community.

فَوْجًا denotes a group of people.

The assembled groups are then described with:

فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ

fa-hum yūzaʿūn

Al-Naml 27:83

Their movement is restrained and they are kept in order.

Assembling and restraining are distinct

نَحْشُرُ expresses the act of gathering.

يُوزَعُونَ concerns the state in which the assembled group is held.

Lexical explanations include holding back those in front until those behind catch up.

This fits the regulation of collective movement.

It should not become a claim about definite formations, attendants, or methods of movement in the Hereafter.

In Al-Naml 27:84, after they arrive, they are questioned about denying the signs and their deeds.

Their restraint and gathering thus belong to a context of reckoning and accountability.

The repetition of يُوزَعُونَ here and in Solomon’s story does not imply that the two groups have the same moral standing.

The verb retains its lexical sense of restraining and regulating movement; the people, circumstances, and purpose of assembly differ.

Fuṣṣilat 41:19 — God’s enemies on the way to the Fire

وَيَوْمَ يُحْشَرُ أَعْدَاءُ اللَّهِ إِلَى النَّارِ فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ

wa-yawma yuḥsharu aʿdāʾu allāhi ilā al-nāri fa-hum yūzaʿūn

Fuṣṣilat 41:19

“And the day the enemies of The God are assembled toward the Fire, and they are kept under restraint and in order.”

The same construction فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ appears here.

The gathering is more explicitly directed: God’s enemies are assembled toward the Fire.

يُحْشَرُ is passive.

أَعْدَاءُ اللَّهِ identifies those being gathered.

إِلَى النَّارِ states the destination.

فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ then describes their restraint and order.

Fuṣṣilat 41:20 describes their arrival and the testimony of their hearing, eyes, and skins concerning their deeds.

The gathering thus belongs to the scene of accountability and the disclosure of human actions.

Does يُوزَعُونَ mean “being punished” here?

The verb itself does not mean this.

Wazʿ in this construction expresses restraint.

In Al-Naml 27:17, the same verb describes Solomon’s troops without referring to their punishment.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:19, the restrained group consists of God’s enemies and their destination is the Fire.

Punishment is understood from the group and its stated destination, not from يُوزَعُونَ itself.

The verb alone also does not specify the force applied, the guarding arrangements, or the physical manner of movement.

The semantic center of يُوزَعُونَ remains constant in all three verses: an assembled group is held back from uncontrolled movement or dispersal and kept in order.

The difference is that one concerns Solomon’s troops and the other two concern groups facing reckoning and an outcome in the Hereafter.

4. أَوْزِعْنِي: asking to be prompted toward gratitude and righteous action

Al-Naml 27:19 — Solomon’s prayer

فَتَبَسَّمَ ضَاحِكًا مِنْ قَوْلِهَا وَقَالَ رَبِّ أَوْزِعْنِي أَنْ أَشْكُرَ نِعْمَتَكَ الَّتِي أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيَّ وَعَلَىٰ وَالِدَيَّ وَأَنْ أَعْمَلَ صَالِحًا تَرْضَاهُ وَأَدْخِلْنِي بِرَحْمَتِكَ فِي عِبَادِكَ الصَّالِحِينَ

fa-tabassama ḍāḥikan min qawlihā wa-qāla rabbi awziʿnī an ashkura niʿmataka allatī anʿamta ʿalayya wa-ʿalā wālidayya wa-an aʿmala ṣāliḥan tarḍāhu wa-adkhilnī bi-raḥmatika fī ʿibādika al-ṣāliḥīn

Al-Naml 27:19

“So he smiled in amusement at her words and said: My Lord, enable me to give thanks for Your favor that You have bestowed upon me and my parents, and to do righteous work that pleases You; and admit me, through Your mercy, among Your righteous servants.”

أَوْزِعْنِي is a Form IV imperative.

The implied subject of أَوْزِعْ is “You,” referring to God, whom Solomon addresses.

The object pronoun -nī refers to Solomon himself.

The recognized meaning here is close to inspiring, prompting, and enabling.

Solomon asks God to direct him so that he can respond to blessings with gratitude and righteous action.

Does awziʿnī mean only inspiring a thought?

In modern Persian, “inspiration” can describe the sudden appearance of an idea or feeling.

Solomon is not merely asking to know a thankful phrase.

He wishes to give thanks and do righteous work that God approves.

The request therefore also concerns practical ability and inclination.

Solomon recognizes and mentions God’s favor, yet asks to respond properly to it.

Recognizing a blessing and responding with full gratitude are different.

Someone may know that they possess abundant means yet use them wrongly.

Someone who recognizes a blessing and is grateful for it should also respond appropriately in conduct and choices.

Solomon’s prayer reflects this connection: the request to give thanks is followed by doing righteous work.

Yet awziʿnī should not be equated with “righteous action.” Righteous action is what Solomon asks to be enabled to perform.

Why does Solomon offer this prayer?

In Al-Naml 27:16, Solomon speaks of being taught the language of birds and of God’s evident favors.

In Al-Naml 27:17, his diverse troops are assembled.

In Al-Naml 27:18, an ant warns of possible danger from their movement.

Solomon understands its words, smiles, and prays.

The sequence connects his special abilities and extensive power with awareness of God’s favor and his responsibility toward it.

In this prayer, he asks to be grateful and to act in a way God approves; he does not ask for increased power.

In Al-Naml 27:40, on seeing another favor, Solomon attributes it to his Lord’s bounty and speaks of being tested in gratitude or ingratitude.

Across these verses, blessings and power entail responsibility for responding rightly.

Does awziʿnī mean controlling the self?

Some lexical explanations expand the prayer as asking God to restrain a person from what distracts from gratitude and direct their attention toward giving thanks.

This connects semantically with the restraining branch.

Nevertheless, the construction has the recognized sense of inspiring and prompting.

The direct meaning should therefore be distinguished from a possible explanation of the relationship between the branches.

The direct request is:

“Enable me to give thanks.”

A broader explanation can note that such help may involve restraining wrongful desires and directing effort toward righteous action.

That explanation should not become the root’s sole, certain meaning.

Asking God for enabling help does not itself negate human agency. In the prayer, Solomon asks that he himself give thanks and act rightly.

Divine help and human action are therefore not presented as opposites in this construction.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 — A prayer at maturity

وَوَصَّيْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ بِوَالِدَيْهِ إِحْسَانًا ۖ حَمَلَتْهُ أُمُّهُ كُرْهًا وَوَضَعَتْهُ كُرْهًا ۖ وَحَمْلُهُ وَفِصَالُهُ ثَلَاثُونَ شَهْرًا ۚ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغَ أَشُدَّهُ وَبَلَغَ أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً قَالَ رَبِّ أَوْزِعْنِي أَنْ أَشْكُرَ نِعْمَتَكَ الَّتِي أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيَّ وَعَلَىٰ وَالِدَيَّ وَأَنْ أَعْمَلَ صَالِحًا تَرْضَاهُ وَأَصْلِحْ لِي فِي ذُرِّيَّتِي ۖ إِنِّي تُبْتُ إِلَيْكَ وَإِنِّي مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ

wa-waṣṣaynā al-insāna bi-wālidayhi iḥsānan ḥamalathu ummuhu kurhan wa-waḍaʿathu kurhan wa-ḥamluhu wa-fiṣāluhu thalāthūna shahran ḥattā idhā balagha ashuddahu wa-balagha arbaʿīna sanatan qāla rabbi awziʿnī an ashkura niʿmataka allatī anʿamta ʿalayya wa-ʿalā wālidayya wa-an aʿmala ṣāliḥan tarḍāhu wa-aṣliḥ lī fī dhurriyyatī innī tubtu ilayka wa-innī mina al-muslimīn

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15

“We have enjoined the human being to treat his parents well. His mother carried him with hardship and delivered him with hardship; his carrying and weaning are thirty months. When he reaches maturity and reaches forty years, he says: My Lord, enable me to give thanks for Your favor that You have bestowed upon me and my parents, and to do righteous work that pleases You; and bring about righteousness for me in my offspring. I have indeed turned back to You, and I am among those who submit.”

The same verb أَوْزِعْنِي appears here.

The wording concerning gratitude for favor and righteous action also matches Solomon’s prayer.

The contexts differ, however.

In Solomon’s story, the prayer follows the enjoyment of power and special ability.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:15, it occurs within ordinary human life: kindness to parents, maternal hardship in pregnancy and childbirth, maturity, and concern for the next generation.

Asking for help in gratitude and righteous action is thus not confined to Prophets or holders of great power.

People receive many blessings in ordinary life and bear responsibility toward them.

Gratitude for blessings bestowed on parents

Both prayers say:

الَّتِي أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيَّ وَعَلَىٰ وَالِدَيَّ

allatī anʿamta ʿalayya wa-ʿalā wālidayya

Al-Naml 27:19; Al-Aḥqāf 46:15

These are the favors God has bestowed on the person and their parents.

The expression extends attention beyond oneself.

A person’s life is not solely the result of personal effort. Life, upbringing, support, experience, and opportunities also involve the contribution of the previous generation.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 makes this clearer by recalling the mother’s hardship in pregnancy and childbirth.

This social explanation should not be inserted into the lexical meaning of awziʿnī.

The root expresses a request for prompting and enabling help. Gratitude and its connection with parents come from the following words and clauses.

The different endings of the prayers

In Al-Naml 27:19, Solomon asks:

وَأَدْخِلْنِي بِرَحْمَتِكَ فِي عِبَادِكَ الصَّالِحِينَ

wa-adkhilnī bi-raḥmatika fī ʿibādika al-ṣāliḥīn

Al-Naml 27:19

He asks God to admit him, through His mercy, among the righteous servants.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 contains a different request:

وَأَصْلِحْ لِي فِي ذُرِّيَّتِي

wa-aṣliḥ lī fī dhurriyyatī

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15

He seeks righteousness in his offspring.

He then speaks of turning back to God and submitting.

The prayers begin alike, but their continuations relate to the circumstances and subject of each verse.

Both concern gratitude and righteous action. One ends by asking to join the righteous servants; the other asks for righteousness in one’s offspring and declares return and submission.

Awziʿnī is thus a request usable in different situations, rather than a term confined to one historical event or special rank.

5. Translation challenges and related roots

Does يُوزَعُونَ mean “they are distributed”?

In modern Persian, tawzīʿ usually means dividing and distributing something among people or places.

One may say that food was distributed or books were handed out among pupils.

The word belongs to w-z-ʿ, but its modern usage must not be imposed on the Qur’anic verses.

Arabic Form II وَزَّعَ can mean dividing and dispersing.

The Qur’anic يُوزَعُونَ, however, is the passive imperfect of Form I وَزَعَ.

They are different constructions.

In all three Qur’anic occurrences, yūzaʿūna follows the gathering of a group.

Dispersing people in different directions does not fit this context of gathering and controlling movement.

“They are distributed among different places” is therefore an unsuitable translation here.

“They are kept under restraint and in order” fits the construction and context.

w-z-ʿ (wazaʿa) compared with ḥ-sh-r (ḥashara)

These roots occur together in all three verses containing يُوزَعُونَ.

Ḥashr concerns gathering people or groups.

Wazʿ here concerns controlling the movement of the gathered group and maintaining its order.

Assembling a group and keeping its movement orderly are related but distinct acts.

Yūzaʿūna should therefore not be treated as merely repeating yuḥsharūna.

Nor does wazʿ itself mean assembling all humanity on the Day of Resurrection; that must be understood from ḥashr and the scope of the people addressed in each verse.

w-z-ʿ (wazaʿa) compared with m-n-ʿ (manaʿa)

M-n-ʿ concerns preventing an action or access to something.

It is thus close to the restraining branch of w-z-ʿ.

The three Qur’anic uses of wazʿ, however, concern regulating collective movement.

Manʿ has wider applications, including withholding property, a right, or a benefit from someone.

“Restraint” may suit some uses of both roots, but their semantic fields are not identical.

w-z-ʿ (wazaʿa) compared with k-f-f (kaffa)

K-f-f also concerns restraint and refraining.

كَفَّ نَفْسَهُ is known in Arabic for restraining oneself or refraining from an action.

Here kaff and wazʿ overlap.

Yet Qur’anic wazʿ concerns an assembled group, and in another construction prompting toward gratitude.

Neither use is fully explained by “refraining” alone.

w-z-ʿ (awzaʿa) compared with l-h-m (ilhām)

Ilhām concerns imparting a meaning or inclination within a person.

Al-Shams 91:8 uses أَلْهَمَهَا concerning the human self.

أَوْزِعْنِي in the two Qur’anic prayers is also close to inspiration and prompting.

The families should not, however, be treated as wholly synonymous.

W-z-ʿ has another clear branch concerning restraint and the control of group movement, which is absent from the meaning of ilhām.

Inspiring an idea does not by itself establish that an action has occurred.

In the prayer awziʿnī, a person asks God for help so that they themselves can give thanks and act rightly.

w-z-ʿ (awzaʿa) compared with sh-k-r (shakara)

Both occurrences of أَوْزِعْنِي also contain أَشْكُرَ.

They are verbs from different roots.

Awziʿnī asks for inspiration and enabling help.

Ashkura identifies the action the speaker wishes to be enabled to perform.

W-z-ʿ does not itself mean gratitude.

In Al-Naml 27:19 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:15, gratitude is the object and direction of the request, not the lexical meaning of awzaʿa.

This matters in translation. Simply rendering awziʿnī as “make me grateful” can blur the distinction between requesting enabling help and the act of giving thanks.

w-z-ʿ (wazaʿa) compared with q-s-m (qasama)

Q-s-m concerns dividing and allotting shares.

Modern tawzīʿ may involve dividing something among people and thus overlap with qasm.

The three verses containing يُوزَعُونَ do not concern dividing property or allocating shares.

A group is assembled and its movement restrained.

The modern sense of distribution should therefore not be imposed on the Qur’anic Form I verb.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Two principal equivalents are needed: one for يُوزَعُونَ and one for أَوْزِعْنِي. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

يُوزَعُونَ

A suitable equivalent in all three verses is “they are kept under restraint and in order.”

In Al-Naml 27:17:

“And Solomon’s troops of jinn, humans, and birds were assembled for him, and they were kept under restraint and in order.”

In Al-Naml 27:83:

“And the day We assemble from every community a group of those who deny Our signs, and they are kept under restraint and in order.”

In Fuṣṣilat 41:19:

“And the day the enemies of The God are assembled toward the Fire, and they are kept under restraint and in order.”

The root explanation can say that movement is controlled so the group does not break up or its members advance and lag behind chaotically.

This explanation should not replace direct translation or be limited to a specific military formation.

“They are grouped” and “they are kept in ranks” may express some practical results, but alone do not fully convey restraint and control.

“They are distributed,” in the sense of division and dispersal, does not suit these verses.

أَوْزِعْنِي

“Enable me” is a suitable principal equivalent.

In Al-Naml 27:19, an abbreviated rendering of the request is:

“My Lord, enable me to give thanks for Your favor … and to do righteous work that pleases You.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 uses the same construction; its equivalent should remain consistent. In abbreviated form:

“My Lord, enable me to give thanks for Your favor … and to do righteous work that pleases You.”

“Prompt me” is also close to this lexical branch, although the corresponding Persian expression can suggest intense stimulation or compulsion.

“Inspire me” is another established equivalent, but it understates the request if understood only as receiving an idea.

“Enable me” is therefore a natural rendering; the root explanation can clarify its connection with inspiration, motivation, and inclination toward action.

Non-Qur’anic forms

وَازِع may mean “restrainer,” “controller,” or “restraining influence,” according to context.

وَزْع: “restraint” or “control.”

وَزَعَ الْجَيْشَ: “he restrained and coordinated the army’s movement.”

أَوْزَعَهُ الشَّيْءَ: “he inspired that action in him” or “prompted him toward it.”

وَزَّعَ in the distributive sense: “he divided” or “dispersed.”

تَوْزِيع in that sense: “division” and “distribution.”

The different forms should therefore not all receive one fixed equivalent.

7. Conclusion

W-z-ʿ has two important Arabic branches: restraining and controlling, and prompting or enabling action.

Poetry attributed to Al-Nābighah illustrates wāziʿ as a restraining influence in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Grey hair can deter continued, ill-considered youthful conduct.

The same concept also appears in managing collective movement, restraining individuals so that the group remains orderly.

يُوزَعُونَ occurs three times in the Qur’an.

In Al-Naml 27:17, Solomon’s troops of jinn, humans, and birds are assembled and kept in order.

In Al-Naml 27:83, groups who deny God’s signs are assembled and their movement restrained.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:19, God’s enemies are assembled toward the Fire and kept under restraint and in order.

Ḥashr and wazʿ express two connected but distinct concepts in all three: assembling a group and keeping it restrained and orderly.

The other branch appears in أَوْزِعْنِي.

In Al-Naml 27:19, Solomon asks God to enable gratitude and righteous action.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:15, the same request is made by a person who has reached maturity and recalls God’s favors toward himself and his parents.

Awziʿnī is not merely restraint: in this construction it concerns inspiration, prompting, and enabling help.

Restraint and prompting can be connected through directing conduct, but that connection must not become a certain historical derivation or one identical meaning for every derivative.

The Qur’anic branches can be defined as follows:

وَزَعَ / يُوزَعُونَ: controlling and restraining movement or conduct; in Qur’anic usage, keeping an assembled group orderly and preventing departure from that order.

أَوْزَعَ / أَوْزِعْنِي: inspiring, prompting, or enabling a particular action; in Qur’anic usage, asking God for help in gratitude and righteous action.

This distinction avoids two errors: translating يُوزَعُونَ with the modern sense “they are distributed,” and restricting أَوْزِعْنِي to restraint or compulsion.

For Forqan, “they are kept under restraint and in order” and “enable me” are suitable equivalents for يُوزَعُونَ and أَوْزِعْنِي respectively.

The root also illuminates two aspects of conduct: movement and behavior sometimes need restraint to preserve order, while righteous action calls for motivation, inclination, and enabling help.

The root alone does not determine what should be restrained or which action should be encouraged. The verb’s object, the context, and the Qur’an’s clear commands establish that direction and value.
