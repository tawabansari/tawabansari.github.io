From breaking an oath to grave sin and persistence in it

The Arabic root ح ن ث concerns sin, failure to fulfill a commitment, and especially failure to keep an oath. حَنِثَ فِي يَمِينِهِ describes someone who has not fulfilled an oath. In addition to this use, حِنْث also denotes sin and wrongdoing.

In Arabic, حنث is not always confined to breaking an oath. It may denote a grave sin in which someone persists. Conversely, تَحَنَّثَ in some early usages means avoiding sin and engaging in worship: a derivative’s structure can change the direction of its use.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in two verses across two surahs. In Ṣād 38:44, وَلَا تَحْنَثْ addresses Job and concerns failure to fulfill an oath. In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46, الْحِنْثِ الْعَظِيمِ denotes a grave sin in which a group persisted.

This difference matters. Breaking an oath is a specific action, whereas grave sin can have different instances. Not every occurrence of حنث should therefore be restricted to oath-breaking, nor should every broken oath be equated with the greatest sins without considering the circumstances.

Qur’anic forms: تَحْنَثْ in Ṣād 38:44 and الْحِنْثِ in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two occurrences, verbal taḥnath and nominal ḥinth

Other forms recorded in Arabic include حَنِثَ, يَحْنَثُ, حِنْث, حَنَث, حَانِث, أَحْنَثَ, حَنَّثَ, تَحَنَّثَ, أَحْنَاث, and مَحَانِث; these exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic center and early Arabic usage

حِنْث: sin and transgression

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for ح ن ث: الإِثْمُ وَالْحَرَجُ, sin and the constraint or distress associated with it.

He explains حَنِثَ فُلَانٌ as committing sin and includes breaking an oath among this family’s usages.

In Al-ʿAyn, حِنْث is explained as الذَّنْبُ الْعَظِيمُ: grave sin.

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad also records حنث for someone who has not fulfilled an oath.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise presents these two fields together: violating an oath, and sin or disobedience.

These witnesses show that حنث is not merely the name of a particular legal act. The Arabic family has a broader connection with transgression and sin.

Yet sin itself must be distinguished from its gravity. Some lexical definitions directly explain حنث as grave sin, while Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46 also adds the adjective الْعَظِيمِ.

Translation should therefore preserve the adjective عظيم as independently expressing or emphasizing the sin’s gravity. Its presence does not by itself invalidate the lexical definition “grave sin” for حنث; the precise meaning is determined through lexical evidence and context.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: sin and ḥaraj, and the explanation of taḥannuth

حَنِثَ فِي يَمِينِهِ: failing to fulfill an oath

In its established use concerning oaths, حَنِثَ يَحْنَثُ means failing to fulfill what one has sworn to do.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab explain this through لَمْ يَبَرَّ فِي يَمِينِهِ: the person did not remain faithful to the oath.

For example, if someone swears to do something but does not do it, Arabic can describe this as committing ḥinth in the oath.

Likewise, if someone swears not to do something and then does it, ḥinth may have occurred in the relationship between the oath and the subsequent conduct.

This lexical definition does not mean that every oath is religiously binding or that every oath must be fulfilled in all circumstances.

An oath’s content may conflict with a more important responsibility or with doing good. Evaluating that situation requires the relevant principles and rulings concerning oaths.

Two questions must therefore remain separate: has the person acted contrary to the oath’s content, and would fulfilling that oath in those circumstances have been right and permissible?

The root ح ن ث helps identify the first question; the answer to the second cannot be derived from this root alone.

حَانِث: someone who has violated an oath

حَانِث is the active participle of حَنِثَ.

In the use concerning oaths, it denotes someone who has not fulfilled an oath.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an, but is established in Arabic dictionaries.

أَحْنَثَهُ means causing someone else to violate an oath. حَنَّثَهُ is also explained as “made him a ḥānith” and may concern judging someone to be an oath-breaker or placing them in that position. Without considering actual usage, the two forms should not always be reduced to one kind of causation.

The violation itself, causing it, and attributing a violation to someone must therefore be distinguished.

A person may break an oath, while someone else may contribute to that violation through conduct or incitement.

Nevertheless, أحنث alone cannot determine each party’s degree of moral responsibility. That depends on their knowledge, freedom of choice, intentions, and actual circumstances.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr, and Lisān al-ʿArab: oaths and causative forms

مَحَانِث and أَحْنَاث

Lisān al-ʿArab records مَحَانِث for places or situations in which ḥinth occurs.

أَحْنَاث is also recorded as a plural for instances of ḥinth.

These usages show that the word can concern both the violation itself and multiple instances of it.

The plurality of أحناث does not mean that every violation is of the same kind or carries the same degree of responsibility.

Someone may have violated several different oaths, while each oath’s status, subject, and circumstances differ.

بُلُوغُ الْحِنْث: reaching the age of responsibility

Early Arabic also records بَلَغَ الْغُلَامُ الْحِنْثَ.

The expression refers to a child reaching the stage at which conduct is evaluated in terms of obligation and responsibility.

In Al-ʿAyn, Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad explains it as the stage when a person’s good and bad actions enter the account of responsibility.

Ibn Fāris records the same meaning.

This usage does not mean that a child necessarily commits a sin immediately upon reaching maturity.

The point is reaching a stage at which capacity and responsibility for conduct come into consideration.

The expression is conceptually connected with puberty, and lexical sources also use حنث within this expression for puberty and reaching the age of responsibility. Their explanation connects the designation with accountability for actions; it does not mean that the person has necessarily committed a sin.

Al-ʿAyn and Al-Muḥkam: bulūgh al-ḥinth and the age of responsibility

The Qur’an uses other expressions for puberty and maturity, such as بَلَغُوا الْحُلُمَ in Al-Nūr 24:59 and the discussion of sound judgment in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6.

These concepts should not be treated as identical merely because their usage is close to بلوغ الحنث.

تَحَنَّثَ: avoiding sin

One notable usage in this family is تَحَنَّثَ.

Lexical sources record تحنث as worshipping and avoiding sin.

Ibn Fāris distinguishes أَثِمَ from تَأَثَّمَ: the first concerns falling into sin, while the second, in one established usage, concerns keeping away from it.

He explains تَحَنَّثَ within this same field.

Arabic forms such as تَحَرَّجَ can likewise refer to avoiding falling into ḥaraj.

One should therefore not mechanically infer from تحنث that a person is committing sin.

Sometimes the derivative expresses the reverse conduct: striving to remain away from sin or free oneself from it.

Lexical sources also record تحنث for worshipping and withdrawing from idols.

Some lexicographers relate it to تَحَنَّفَ and propose a possible sound substitution between thāʾ and fāʾ.

This possibility alone does not establish that ح ن ث and ح ن ف were originally one root. The two families require separate examination of usage and historical evidence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: athima versus taʾaththama, and ḥarija versus taḥarraja

Limits of the historical witnesses

Lexical sources transmit the short expression مَنْ يَتَشَاءَمْ بِالْهُدَى فَالْحِنْثُ شَرٌّ to illustrate حنث in the sense of associating partners with The God. A close rendering is: “Whoever regards guidance as an ill omen, [let them know that] ḥinth is evil.” Transliteration: man yatashāʾam bi-l-hudā fa-l-ḥinthu sharrun. In explanations of this witness, ḥinth is understood as shirk.

The sources examined do not establish a reliable attribution to a particular poet or the date of composition.

It cannot therefore be presented as a certainly pre-Islamic poetic witness.

For this root, the more reliable evidence at present consists of direct reports by early lexicographers and the Qur’an’s two usages.

The absence of an independently dated poetic witness does not justify inventing an example or attributing an uncertain verse to a pre-Islamic poet.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab: the short witness without a clear attribution

2. وَلَا تَحْنَثْ in Ṣād 38:44

وَخُذْ بِيَدِكَ ضِغْثًا فَاضْرِبْ بِهِ وَلَا تَحْنَثْ إِنَّا وَجَدْنَاهُ صَابِرًا نِعْمَ الْعَبْدُ إِنَّهُ أَوَّابٌ

wa-khudh bi-yadika ḍighthan fa-ḍrib bihi wa-lā taḥnath innā wajadnāhu ṣābiran niʿma al-ʿabdu innahu awwābun

Translation: “Take a bundle in your hand, strike with it, and do not violate your oath. We found him patient. What an excellent servant! He was indeed one who repeatedly turned back.”

Ṣād 38:44

The verse continues the account of Job.

Ṣād 38:41 mentions the suffering Job faced.

Ṣād 38:42 and Ṣād 38:43 describe relief in his circumstances and the restoration of his family.

Then Ṣād 38:44 commands taking a ضغث, striking with it, and not committing ḥinth.

The structure of تَحْنَثْ

تَحْنَثْ is a second-person masculine singular imperfect verb from حَنِثَ يَحْنَثُ.

It follows prohibitive لَا and is in the jussive mood.

وَلَا تَحْنَثْ thus means “and do not commit ḥinth.”

In this context, established Arabic explanations indicate violation of an oath or failure to fulfill a commitment.

The word itself does not name the person to whom the oath related.

Nor does the verse explicitly state, for example, “Job had sworn to do such-and-such.”

The command ولا تحنث indicates an issue concerning an earlier commitment or oath, but the Qur’an does not supply all its details.

What role does ضِغْثًا play?

ضِغْث belongs to the root ض غ ث and here denotes a bundle of branches, grasses, or plant stalks.

Job is commanded to take such a bundle in his hand and strike with it.

ضغث is therefore the name of the implement specified in the command.

حنث itself does not mean taking a bundle of plants or striking with it.

The verbs خُذْ and اضْرِبْ express the commanded actions; ولا تحنث concerns avoiding violation of the commitment.

These three parts must remain lexically distinct.

Does the Qur’an say that Job should strike his wife?

Some exegetical reports state that Job, during his illness, became upset by his wife’s conduct and swore to strike her a specified number of times.

Some reports give the number as one hundred strokes.

Taking a bundle of stalks or branches is then presented as a way to fulfill the oath without administering multiple separate strokes.

These details do not appear in Ṣād 38:44 itself.

The verse does not name Job’s wife, give the number of strokes, or explain the reason for the oath.

Nor does the verse itself report anything that Job’s wife may have done.

These reports should therefore not be inserted into the translation as explicit Qur’anic wording.

The command also concerns Job and a particular situation. Without independent grounds, it cannot be turned into a general permission to inflict physical harm on a spouse or anyone else.

This distinction matters for research: lexical evidence can establish the meaning of ولا تحنث, while reconstructing every detail of the story and deriving a general ruling are separate tasks.

Al-Zajjāj’s Maʿānī al-Qurʾān: ḍighth and exegetical reports concerning Job

Avoiding violation while considering the circumstances

The verse ends by praising Job through two descriptions: صَابِرًا and أَوَّابٌ.

صابر belongs to ص ب ر, and أواب to أ و ب.

These descriptions contribute to the assessment of Job’s character, but are not the lexical meaning of حنث.

The verse gives a command that appears to specify how to act in a particular situation, without presenting all its background to the reader.

Relying on a single report therefore cannot turn the episode into a general method for evading every commitment or law.

Nor should ولا تحنث be taken to mean that a person must fulfill every oath regardless of its content.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an discusses oaths that do not incur accountability, binding oaths, expiation, and the place of goodness and reverent mindfulness.

This material needs to be examined together in a study of the rules concerning oaths.

3. الْحِنْثِ الْعَظِيمِ in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46

وَكَانُوا يُصِرُّونَ عَلَى الْحِنْثِ الْعَظِيمِ

wa-kānū yuṣirrūna ʿalā al-ḥinthi al-ʿaẓīmi

Translation: “And they persisted in grave sin.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46

This is the root ح ن ث’s second and final occurrence in the Qur’an.

Here حنث occurs as a noun, accompanied by الْعَظِيمِ.

Unlike Ṣād 38:44, this verse does not directly mention a particular person’s oath or the violation of a specific commitment.

The context concerns a group whose conduct and outcome are described in Al-Wāqiʿah.

The phrase’s structure

الْحِنْثِ is a definite noun governed by عَلَىٰ and therefore in the genitive case.

الْعَظِيمِ is its adjective, expressing the sin’s greatness and gravity.

يُصِرُّونَ belongs to ص ر ر and here refers to persisting in and continuing conduct.

The verse thus expresses three matters through distinct words: sin, its gravity, and persistence in it.

حنث alone does not mean persistence.

Nor does the use of عظيم imply that every instance of ḥinth in every situation is equally grave.

Here the subject is a grave sin that a person will not abandon.

The context: luxury, sin, and denial of resurrection

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:42, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:43, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:44 describe the companions of the left.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:45 states:

إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا قَبْلَ ذَٰلِكَ مُتْرَفِينَ

innahum kānū qabla dhālika mutrafīna

That is, they had previously lived in indulgent luxury.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46 then speaks of persistence in grave sin.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:47 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:48 also recount their words denying that they would be raised after death.

This continuity places حنث عظيم in the conduct of a group who persist in grave sin and also deny resurrection.

It does not mean that every prosperous person commits grave sin simply by possessing blessings and comfort.

In these verses, indulgent luxury, persistence in sin, and denial of being raised are particular characteristics of the group discussed.

The judgment concerning them arises from their combined conduct, rather than from possessing wealth alone.

Does حنث عظيم mean associating partners with The God?

Early exegetical reports also explain الحنث العظيم through shirk and disbelief.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the expression as “grave sin” and, in interpreting this verse, identifies that sin as associating partners with The God. This identifies the referent in the verse’s interpretation; it does not establish that حنث means only shirk in every Arabic usage.

Al-Ṭabarī: grave sin and its identification as shirk in this verse

Other accounts, including Al-Māturīdī’s commentary, connect حنث عظيم with false oaths or failing to fulfill oaths, including oaths used to deny resurrection.

Al-Naḥl 16:38 describes a group who swore emphatically that The God would not raise anyone who had died.

This verse shows that denial of resurrection sometimes accompanies oaths in the Qur’an.

It does not establish that حنث in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46 necessarily and exclusively refers to that particular oath.

Such a connection is an exegetical reading that can be examined.

Al-Māturīdī’s Taʾwīlāt: the oath reading and its connection with denial of resurrection

The broader meaning “grave sin,” meanwhile, fits both the lexical evidence and the context of persisting in wrongdoing and denying resurrection.

“Grave sin” is therefore preferable in the basic translation; identifying shirk, disbelief, or a false oath as its referent belongs in the contextual explanation.

Persistence in sin distinguished from a lapse

يُصِرُّونَ plays a decisive role in this verse.

Someone may commit an error but correct their conduct after recognizing it.

Conversely, someone may recognize the truth yet persist in wrongdoing.

These situations are not morally identical.

Āl ʿImrān 3:135 describes people who commit an indecency or wrong themselves, then remember The God, seek forgiveness, and do not knowingly persist in wrongdoing.

This verse matters for comparison with Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46.

On one side, a person can return and reform after an error; on the other, persistence in wrongdoing is emphasized.

This distinction arises from persistence and the verses considered together.

حنث itself does not mean deliberate intent or persistence. A sin may occur, but the person’s awareness, intention, and subsequent response require separate examination.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46, يصرون shows that the concern is not merely a passing occurrence, but continuing in it and refusing to stop.

4. Ḥinth, oaths, and responsibility

Is every broken oath a sin?

Lexically, ḥinth is used for failing to fulfill an oath.

Lexical meaning must nevertheless not be equated with a moral or legal ruling.

For example, in anger someone may swear never to speak to a relative again.

If, after calming down, the person realizes that continuing the estrangement is wrong, literal fidelity to the oath must be distinguished from doing what is right.

Al-Baqarah 2:224 prohibits making The God, through one’s oaths, an obstacle to goodness, reverent mindfulness, and reconciliation among people.

Al-Māʾidah 5:89 distinguishes laghw oaths from binding oaths and discusses expiation. Laghw should not be equated without explanation with every kind of invalid oath.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:2 also refers to a way of releasing oaths.

These verses show that Qur’anic treatment of oaths is not confined to literal fulfillment of every commitment regardless of its content.

The root ح ن ث should therefore not be made the basis of a ruling that compels someone to continue wrongdoing.

Responsibility for an oath, its validity, and the means of correction or reparation must be examined through the verses together.

Ḥinth and false oaths

False oaths are connected with ḥinth, but the two concepts are not entirely identical.

Someone may knowingly swear something untrue about a past event.

In that situation, the primary issue is the oath’s falsity.

In another situation, someone swears to do something in the future but later acts contrary to the oath’s content.

Here the issue is failure to fulfill a commitment.

Both can fall within transgression and sin, but their timing, subject, and type of conduct differ.

Qur’anic research must therefore distinguish false oaths, broken oaths, and the responsibilities associated with them.

The distinction becomes clearer through the roots ح ل ف, ق س م, ي م ن, and ح ن ث. يَمِين and its plural أَيْمَان in the sense of oaths belong to ي م ن, rather than أ ي م.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: yamīn and aymān under Y M N

5. Distinctions from related roots

ح ن ث (حِنْث) and ح ل ف (حَلَف)

In Qur’anic usage, حلف refers to swearing an oath.

In its use concerning oaths, حنث concerns failure to fulfill one.

Swearing an oath and breaking it are therefore different actions.

Someone may swear and fulfill an oath, or act contrary to it.

In Ṣād 38:44, ولا تحنث concerns avoiding violation of an oath; the act of swearing itself is not mentioned in the verse.

تحنث should therefore not be translated as “swear an oath” or “swear again.”

Study of Ḥ L F

ح ن ث (حِنْث) and ب ر ر (بِرّ)

Arabic uses بَرَّ فِي يَمِينِهِ for someone who fulfills an oath.

In contrast, حَنِثَ فِي يَمِينِهِ denotes violating it.

This opposition is clearly recorded in lexical sources.

But بِرّ has a wider Qur’anic field, also concerning goodness, righteousness, and proper conduct.

Not every use of بر can therefore be confined to fulfilling an oath.

Nor does literal fulfillment alone establish that an action is right if a person has sworn to do something wrong.

Study of B R R

ح ن ث (حِنْث) and ن ك ث (نَكْث)

نكث concerns breaking a covenant and undoing something that has been made firm.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:10, فَمَنْ نَكَثَ concerns breaking a pledge.

In its oath-related branch, حنث likewise concerns violating a commitment.

But نكث can concern an agreement or covenant even when no particular oath is mentioned in the sentence.

حنث في اليمين, by contrast, concerns an oath.

The two overlap in some situations but are not complete synonyms.

ح ن ث (حِنْث) and خ ل ف (خُلْف)

خُلْف concerns failing to keep a promise.

In one established use, حنث is a failure concerning an oath.

Not every broken promise, however, involves an oath.

Someone may make a promise without swearing.

Consequently, not every instance of خلف can be called ḥinth.

Study of KH L F

ح ن ث (حِنْث) and أ ث م (إِثْم)

إثم concerns sin and the responsibility arising from wrongdoing.

Ibn Fāris likewise uses الإثم to explain the principle of ح ن ث.

The two are close in the field of sin, but حنث has a more specific character in its oath-related use.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46, حنث عظيم denotes grave sin and can be connected contextually with disbelief, shirk, and denial of resurrection.

إثم itself also occurs frequently in the Qur’an and in different settings.

All uses of these two families should therefore not be confined to one offense or kind of conduct.

Study of ʾ TH M

ح ن ث (حِنْث) and ص ر ر (إِصْرَار)

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46, persistence concerns continuing in sin and refusing to abandon it.

حنث is the sin or violation itself.

Someone may commit a wrong once but refrain from repeating or continuing it.

Persistence, by contrast, shows that the person remains committed to that conduct.

Translation must therefore preserve each word’s contribution: “they persisted in grave sin.”

ح ن ث (حِنْث) and ح ن ف (حَنِيف)

حنيف belongs to ح ن ف and concerns religious orientation and Abraham’s way in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic usages of حنث concern violating an oath and sin.

Lexical sources relate تحنث and تحنف in some usages involving worship and withdrawal from idols.

Closeness in one derivative does not mean that the roots are identical.

حنث therefore cannot be translated in the Qur’an as being a ḥanīf or following Abraham’s way.

Study of Ḥ N F

ح ن ث (حِنْث) and ح ن ك (حَنَكَ)

These roots share only their first two consonants.

ح ن ك concerns the mouth and palate and, in some usages, restraining or gaining control.

ح ن ث concerns sin and violating an oath.

Sharing initial consonants is not evidence of a direct semantic connection.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Ṣād 38:44, وَلَا تَحْنَثْ can be translated:

“And do not violate your oath.”

This equivalent makes the verb’s established meaning clear.

“And do not break your oath” is also natural and defensible.

The translation should not add the name of Job’s wife, the number of strokes, or the reason for the oath, since these details do not appear in the verse.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46, الْحِنْثِ الْعَظِيمِ can be translated as “grave sin.”

A suitable rendering of the verse is therefore:

“And they persisted in grave sin.”

“Great sin” is also a defensible equivalent.

But translating it as “persistence in shirk” or “persistence in false oaths” directly substitutes one exegetical reading for the word’s meaning.

These referents can be examined in the explanation, while the broader meaning is better preserved in the basic translation.

Outside the Qur’an, حَنِثَ فِي يَمِينِهِ can be translated as “he violated his oath”; حَانِث as “an oath-breaker” or “someone who has violated an oath”; and أَحْنَثَهُ as “he caused him to violate his oath.”

In its use concerning worship and avoiding sin, تَحَنَّثَ can be rendered as “he kept away from sin” or “he engaged in worship.”

In the context of the age of responsibility, بَلَغَ الْغُلَامُ الْحِنْثَ can be translated as “the child reached the stage of responsibility for his actions.”

All these usages require preserving the distinction between violating an oath, sin, and striving to avoid sin.

Conclusion

Brief root meaning: Sin and transgression; in the use concerning oaths, failing to fulfill or breaking an oath.

Ibn Fāris identifies one principle: sin and ḥaraj. Lexical sources also record حنث for both breaking an oath and grave sin.

Within the same family, تحنث has a different usage concerning avoiding sin and worshipping. This difference shows that each derivative’s meaning must be established through its structure and actual use.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

In Ṣād 38:44, وَلَا تَحْنَثْ addresses Job and concerns avoiding violation of an oath. Yet the Qur’an does not explicitly state the reason for the oath, the number of strokes, or the identity of the person intended by the command to strike.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46, الْحِنْثِ الْعَظِيمِ concerns a grave sin in which a group persisted. The context also mentions indulgent luxury and denial of resurrection.

Lexically, حنث here falls within the field of sin and, together with عظيم, expresses grave sin. Identifying a referent such as shirk, disbelief, or false oaths requires examining the context and the verses together.

The root’s semantic center is sin and transgression; in its particular use concerning oaths, it denotes failure to fulfill a sworn commitment.

This does not mean that every oath is right, every broken oath is the greatest sin, or every occurrence of ḥinth necessarily concerns an oath.

Furthermore, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46 expresses persistence in sin through the separate verb يُصِرُّونَ. حنث itself therefore does not mean persistence, deliberate awareness, or continual repetition.

In Forqan’s translation, “violating an oath” suits the verbal usage in Ṣād 38:44, and “grave sin” suits the nominal usage in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46.

This distinction preserves each verse’s precise meaning and prevents legal rulings, historical reports, or exegetical referents from replacing the Qur’anic word itself without evidence.
