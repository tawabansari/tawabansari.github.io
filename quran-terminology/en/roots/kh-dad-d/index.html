Qur’anic Root KH Ḍ D: Bending, Breaking, and Removing Thorns

Qur’anic and lexical forms

مَخْضُودٍ
(makhḍūdin)
Sole Qur’anic derivative; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28; passive participle, genitive adjective of sidr

خَضَدَ
(khaḍada)
Lexical base verb: bent, broke, or removed thorns; this verb does not occur in the Qur’an

يَخْضِدُ
(yakhḍidu)
Lexical imperfect; does not occur in the Qur’an

خَضْد
(khaḍd)
Verbal noun for bending or breaking; not Qur’anic

خَضِيد
(khaḍīd)
Bent, broken, or cut; also soft in some uses; not Qur’anic

(inkhaḍada)

The Arabic root خ ض د concerns bending, breaking, and cutting branches and other plant structures. In some uses, a branch or piece of wood bends and sustains damage without necessarily becoming detached; other uses concern cutting a branch or removing a tree’s thorns.

The Qur’an uses this root to describe the sidr trees of Paradise. Understanding makhḍūd requires distinguishing the root’s broad meaning from its particular sense in the verse.

1. Forms of the root and its semantic core

The Qur’an contains only makhḍūd from this root, a passive participle of the Form I verb khaḍada, on the pattern mafʿūl. Its sole occurrence is in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28.

Arabic records other forms and derivatives:

  • خَضَدَ ـ يَخْضِدُ, khaḍada–yakhḍidu: bent, broke, cut, or removed something’s thorns, according to the object and context.
  • خَضْد, khaḍd: the verbal noun, meaning bending, breaking, or cutting.
  • خَضِيد, khaḍīd: something bent, broken, or cut; in some uses, soft, fresh vegetation.
  • مَخْضُود, makhḍūd: something on which khaḍd has been performed.
  • اِنْخَضَدَ, inkhaḍada: bent or broke, without necessarily becoming completely detached.
  • تَخَضَّدَ, takhaḍḍada: bent or sustained breakage and damage.
  • تَخْضِيد, takhḍīd: cutting, pruning, or removing thorns and branches.

Of these forms, only makhḍūd occurs in the Qur’an. The others are Arabic witnesses that help identify the root’s semantic family.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole passive-participle occurrence, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28, word three

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies the core as something soft bending, explaining that a branch may bend without completely breaking. He also treats cutting a tree’s thorns as an extension of this meaning.

By contrast, dictionaries such as Tahdhīb al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab record a broader range: bending, breaking without complete separation, cutting, and removing thorns.

Together these witnesses connect the semantic core with bending or breaking the structure of a branch or something similar, while actual uses can extend to cutting and separation. Every use should therefore be restricted neither to bending without cutting nor to necessarily cutting something off completely.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: soft material bending and extension to thorn removal

2. The material image and early Arabic usage

A tree branch provides a clear image. A soft branch may bend, crack, or break under pressure while remaining attached to the trunk. Greater pressure or cutting may also detach part of it.

Arabic records khaḍadtu al-ʿūda, “I bent or broke the wood.” Inkhaḍada al-ʿūdu likewise describes a branch bending without manifest breakage and complete separation.

The same semantic field includes khaḍadtu al-shajara for cutting branches or removing a tree’s thorns. What is removed is a thorn or part of the tree, rather than necessarily the tree itself.

This distinction matters for the Qur’anic verse: a tree stripped of thorns has not been destroyed; a troublesome part of its structure has been removed.

A witness from Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

In the Muʿallaqah attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, the poet describes a woman’s limbs:

كَأَنَّ الْبُرِينَ وَالدَّمَالِيجَ عُلِّقَتْ
عَلَى عُشَرٍ أَوْ خِرْوَعٍ لَمْ يُخَضَّدِ

kaʾanna al-burīna wa-al-damālīja ʿulliqat / ʿalā ʿusharin aw khirwaʿin lam yukhaḍḍadi

Translation:

“As though her anklets and armlets were hung upon an ʿushar or khirwaʿ plant whose branches had not yet been bent, broken, or pruned.”

The comparison presents a plant with its natural form and continuity intact. Lam yukhaḍḍadi indicates branches untouched by bending, breaking, or pruning. Al-Tabrīzī explains it as not having been bent, while Al-Zawzanī emphasizes branches and leaves not having been pruned; this difference in explanation should be retained.

This witness shows that early khaḍd was not restricted to removing thorns. Reducing it here to thorn removal would lose part of the poetic image and the verb’s broader usage.

Al-Tabrīzī’s Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-ʿAshr: Ṭarafah’s witness and lam yukhaḍḍadi

A witness from Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī describes flowing water:

يَمُدُّهُ كُلُّ وَادٍ مُتْرَعٍ لَجِبٍ
فِيهِ رُكَامٌ مِنَ الْيَنْبُوتِ وَالْخَضَدِ

yamudduhu kullu wādin mutraʿin lajibin / fīhi rukāmun mina al-yanbūti wa-al-khaḍadi

Translation:

“Every brimming, roaring valley supplies it with water; in it are heaps of yanbūt vegetation and broken plant debris.”

Lexical explanations connect khaḍad here with broken, accumulated vegetation or plant parts. The witness therefore fits the field of breakage and accumulated plant matter, although its exact botanical referent requires caution. Lisān al-ʿArab explains khaḍad here as broken, accumulated reeds and other moist stems or branches; “broken plant debris” is therefore clearer than leaving khaḍad untranslated.

Together with the lexicons, these early witnesses connect the root with the material condition of branches and plants. The Qur’anic sense of removing thorns is one particular use.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Mujmal al-Lughah: broken plant debris in Al-Nābighah’s witness

3. Other extensions in Arabic

Arabic dictionaries record several further uses beyond branches and trees.

Khaḍada can describe eating soft, moist food, with some reports concerning vigorous or abundant eating. Khaḍadu al-badani also denotes bodily aching, weariness, and a sense of brokenness with weakness.

The bodily use understandably connects with breakage and bending: a sore, exhausted person resembles an injured limb. This explanation nevertheless does not establish a definite historical path for every use.

An Arabic lexical family can extend into several material and figurative fields. Their existence should not place all of them inside the Qur’anic meaning of makhḍūd.

4. Qur’anic usage: فِي سِدْرٍ مَخْضُودٍ

Al-Wāqiʿah states:

فِي سِدْرٍ مَخْضُودٍ

fī sidrin makhḍūdin

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Amid sidr trees whose thorns have been removed.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28

This verse forms part of the blessings described for the companions of the right.

Arabic sidr names a familiar tree, whose familiar varieties include thorn-bearing ones. Besides shade, it has leaves and fruit; its thorns can make approaching the branches or gathering fruit difficult.

Makhḍūd follows sidr as its adjective. As a passive participle, it describes something on which khaḍd has been performed. Here it is masculine singular, indefinite, and genitive, agreeing with sidr as its attributive adjective.

In relation to sidr, khaḍada al-shawka has the established meaning of cutting or removing thorns. “Thornless sidr” is therefore concise and sound, while “sidr whose thorns have been removed” makes the participial construction clearer.

The root itself does not mean being thornless; that condition results from khaḍd applied to the thorns. Just as “broken” describes a condition resulting from breaking, makhḍūd describes a condition resulting from khaḍd.

Such a passive participle nevertheless need not report that a tree in Paradise first had thorns and someone subsequently removed them. An established linguistic description can express its final condition.

Al-Kashshāf: thornless sidr as a description, without requiring a chronology of thorn removal

Can makhḍūd describe branches bent under abundant fruit?

Early explanations also transmit another reading: the sidr is so fruitful that its branches bend under the weight.

This is understandable through the meaning of branches bending. The following verses also concern abundant fruit and paradisal blessings.

Linguistic possibility should nevertheless be distinguished from the preferred interpretation. Khaḍada al-shawka and lexical explanations concerning sidr give clearer support to thorn removal. Makhḍūd itself does not mean laden with fruit; that reading requires considering branches bending beneath its weight.

Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī: the reading concerning downward branches and abundant fruit

Thorns removed is therefore the preferred sense for the basic translation. Abundant fruit or bending branches may be discussed as an accompanying contextual possibility, rather than replacing the direct meaning without evidence.

Al-Wāḥidī’s Al-Basīṭ: thorn removal preferred and distinguished from abundant fruit

5. Connection with surrounding verses

Sidr makhḍūd appears beside several other blessings:

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29 has wa-ṭalḥin manḍūdin. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:30 mentions extended shade, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 flowing water, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:32 abundant fruit. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:33 describes the fruit as neither interrupted nor withheld.

Together these form an image of ease, enjoyment, and access to blessings. Sidr is more than an attractive tree here: a familiar troublesome feature, its thorns, is absent in its paradisal description.

Thorn removal fits this context naturally. Ease of enjoyment may be understood, but one botanical adjective should not, without evidence, generate an extensive theory of Paradise’s entire nature or the elimination of every difficulty at every level of life.

Nor should makhḍūd be equated with manḍūd in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29. Their forms look similar, but they belong to different roots.

6. Differences from neighboring roots

KH Ḍ D (khaḍd) and N Ḍ D (naḍd)

Khaḍd concerns bending, breaking, cutting, and removing part of a branch or tree.

ن ض د instead concerns arranging things upon one another in an ordered or accumulated formation.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29, manḍūd describes ṭalḥ and denotes arrangement or stacking. Qāf 50:10 likewise uses naḍīd for a palm’s fruit cluster or inflorescence.

The similar sounds of makhḍūd and manḍūd therefore do not make both mean a fruit-laden tree or stacked fruit.

KH Ḍ D (khaḍd) and Q Ṭ ʿ (qaṭʿ)

ق ط ع generally concerns cutting and separating, with extensive material and nonmaterial Qur’anic uses.

Khaḍd can also extend to cutting, but its lexical witnesses, particularly concerning branches, include bending and breakage without complete separation.

These roots can therefore overlap without being complete synonyms. Makhḍūd should not become a simple equivalent of maqṭūʿ in every context.

Study of Q Ṭ ʿ

KH Ḍ D (khaḍd) and KH Ḍ R (khuḍrah)

خ ض ر concerns greenness and the color green. Some lexical discussions also mention softness and freshness in explaining khaḍd, but similar written forms do not establish identical roots.

Greenness and freshness do not necessarily follow from خ ض د itself. If a particular use describes khaḍīd vegetation as soft and fresh, that use and its witnesses should be assessed separately.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

No single equivalent suffices for every use of خ ض د. “Bending,” “breaking,” or “bending without detaching” suits wood and branches. In pruning and removing parts of trees, “cutting branches” or “removing thorns” is more precise.

For the sole Qur’anic form, makhḍūd in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28, the proposed translation is as follows. This proposal likewise leaves the installed verse translation unchanged.

فِي سِدْرٍ مَخْضُودٍ

fī sidrin makhḍūdin

“Amid sidr trees whose thorns have been removed.”

The shorter “amid thornless sidr trees” also conveys the contextual sense acceptably.

A short root box may state:

Brief root meaning: Bending or breaking a branch; cutting or removing parts such as thorns.

Meaning in this verse: Sidr trees whose thorns have been removed and which are thornless.

Conclusion

The Arabic root خ ض د concerns bending, breaking, and cutting branches and similar things. Some uses involve breakage without complete separation, others cutting or removing part of a tree. Early Arabic witnesses show that the root cannot be restricted to removing thorns alone.

The sole Qur’anic derivative is makhḍūd in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28. The established use concerning tree thorns and the context of paradisal blessings favor “sidr whose thorns have been removed.”

Branches bending under fruit is worth considering within the root’s semantic field, but has less support for the basic translation. Distinguishing the root’s general meaning from its particular contextual sense preserves Arabic’s actual range while preventing exegetical possibilities from replacing the Qur’anic wording. Brief root meaning: Bending or breaking a branch; cutting or removing parts such as thorns.
