Qur’anic Root Q ʿ R

Depth, bottom, and base; uprooting and falling

Qur’anic forms

مُنْقَعِرٍ

Munqaʿirin

Uprooted from the base; sole occurrence, 54:20

1. Basic meaning and linguistic background

The Arabic root Q ʿ R concerns depth, the lowest part of something, and reaching its bottom. In uses concerning trees, it denotes removal from the base and falling.

Verse attributed to Labīd illustrates this family in the sense of overturning and collapse. Classical Arabic sources also record qaʿr for the bottom of a well or vessel, depth in the earth, and the base of a tree. These lexical records and poetic attributions do not by themselves establish a pre-Qur’anic date for all these uses.

Qur’anic form examined: munqaʿir, uprooted from the base and fallen.

Other Arabic forms include qaʿr, the bottom or depth of something; qaʿīr, deep; qaʿara, to reach a thing’s bottom or uproot it; taqʿīr, deepening; taqaʿʿur, becoming deep or falling; and inqiʿār, becoming uprooted. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. Material imagery and classical Arabic usage

Qaʿr names the lowest part of a well, the bottom of a vessel, or the deepest part of a depression in the ground.

The expression qaʿara al-biʾra is recorded both for deepening a well and for reaching its bottom. The verb also describes drinking all the liquid in a vessel, down to its bottom.

For trees, qaʿara al-shajarata means uprooting a tree from its base. When a tree becomes uprooted and falls, Arabic says:

“Inqaʿarat al-shajarah.”

That is, the tree became uprooted and fell.

This use can be understood through the tree’s base and attachment to the soil. Once detached from its place of growth, the tree loses the support that held it upright.

The Q ʿ R family is not restricted to trees. Lexical explanations also use its forms for the overturning of a litter and the falling of objects.

An elegy attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah for his brother Arbad includes this verse:

وَأَرْبَدُ فَارِسُ الْهَيْجَا إِذَا مَا / تَقَعَّرَتِ الْمَشَاجِرُ بِالْفِئَامِ

“And Arbad was the horseman of battle when the litters overturned with their bedding.”

The line is transmitted with variants including bi-l-fiʾāmi and bi-l-khiyāmi. The translation above follows the former and the explanation of fiʾām as a litter’s covering or bedding. Taqaʿʿarat here is Form V and describes collapse or overturning; it must not be treated as the same form as the Form VII verb inqaʿara.

Historical sources transmit this elegy in connection with Arbad’s death during the period of the divine mission. It is therefore not independent evidence of pre-Qur’anic usage. Without assigning an exact composition date, it can illustrate a non-arboreal use of this family in poetry attributed to Labīd.

Classical dictionaries also use this family for probing deeply into matters and examining obscure questions. Taqaʿʿur in speech denotes affected, elaborate expression. Its registration in a later dictionary does not establish a pre-Qur’anic date.

These uses are intelligible within the semantic domain of depth and reaching remote or inner parts of something.

3. The Qur’anic use of munqaʿir

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an. Al-Qamar describes the fate of ʿĀd after their denial of the divine warnings:

إِنّا أَرسَلنا عَلَيهِم ريحًا صَرصَرًا فى يَومِ نَحسٍ مُستَمِرٍّ

Innā arsalnā ʿalayhim rīḥan ṣarṣaran fī yawmi naḥsin mustamirr.

“We sent upon them a fierce, cold wind on a day of continuing misfortune.”

Al-Qamar 54:19

تَنزِعُ النّاسَ كَأَنَّهُم أَعجازُ نَخلٍ مُنقَعِرٍ

Tanziʿu l-nāsa ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin munqaʿir.

“Uprooting people as though they were trunks of uprooted palms.”

Al-Qamar 54:20

Two distinct roots contribute to this image.

Tanziʿu, from N Z ʿ, expresses the wind’s act of pulling people from their places. Munqaʿir, from Q ʿ R, describes palms that have become uprooted from their base and fallen.

Aʿjāz is the plural of ʿajuz and here refers to the bases or lower portions of palm trunks.

Morphology and syntax

Munqaʿir is the active participle of the intransitive Form VII verb inqaʿara. Here it describes something that has become uprooted; its active-participle form does not imply a deliberate act of uprooting.

Syntactically, munqaʿirin is a genitive adjective modifying nakhlin. Nakhl is a collective noun and can take a singular masculine or feminine adjective.

The singular form of munqaʿir therefore does not indicate only one tree, nor does its masculine form refer exclusively to male palms.

Although morphologically an active participle, munqaʿir is appropriately translated “uprooted”: inqaʿara is intransitive and describes the condition after detachment from the base.

A palm that stood firm before the wind is now uprooted and lying on the ground. The Qur’an uses this image for people unable to withstand the powerful wind.

The comparison does not establish that their bodies acquired every physical property of palm trunks or were necessarily dismembered in a particular way. Uprooting and falling are central to the image.

4. Comparison with related roots

Q ʿ R (inqiʿār) and N Z ʿ (nazʿ): becoming uprooted and pulling out

These roots appear together in Al-Qamar 54:20.

Nazʿ denotes pulling and removing something from its previous place or condition. Its uses extend beyond material objects to taking away dominion or removing resentment.

For trees, inqiʿār emphasizes the resulting condition of uprooting from the base and falling.

In Al-Qamar, the wind uproots people, whose condition is likened to uprooted palm trunks. One expression conveys the action; the other presents an image of its result.

Q ʿ R (inqiʿār) and J TH TH (ijtithāth): uprooting from the base

Ibrāhīm states:

وَمَثَلُ كَلِمَةٍ خَبيثَةٍ كَشَجَرَةٍ خَبيثَةٍ اجتُثَّت مِن فَوقِ الأَرضِ ما لَها مِن قَرارٍ

Wa-mathalu kalimatin khabīthatin ka-shajaratin khabīthatin ijtuththat min fawqi l-arḍi mā lahā min qarār.

“And the likeness of an impure word is like an impure tree uprooted from the surface of the earth, having no firm footing.”

Ibrahim 14:26

Ijtithāth and inqiʿār overlap in the sense of a tree being uprooted from its base.

J TH TH highlights cutting and pulling something out of its original place. Q ʿ R includes the idea of bottom and depth; inqiʿār, applied to a tree, describes uprooting from the base and falling.

The constructions of the two verses also differ. Ujtuththat is a passive verb reporting an act of uprooting, whereas munqaʿir is an adjective describing the resulting condition.

This morphological and semantic distinction does not mean that ijtithāth is always intentional or inqiʿār always caused by natural forces.

Q ʿ R (inqiʿār) and KH W Y (khawā): uprooting, falling, and emptiness

Al-Ḥāqqah also depicts the fate of ʿĀd through palm trunks:

سَخَّرَها عَلَيهِم سَبعَ لَيالٍ وَثَمٰنِيَةَ أَيّامٍ حُسومًا فَتَرَى القَومَ فيها صَرعىٰ كَأَنَّهُم أَعجازُ نَخلٍ خاوِيَةٍ

Sakhkharahā ʿalayhim sabʿa layālin wa-thamāniyata ayyāmin ḥusūman fa-tarā l-qawma fīhā ṣarʿā ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin khāwiyah.

“He subjected it to them for seven nights and eight consecutive days, so you see the people lying fallen therein, as though they were trunks of fallen palms.”

Al-Haqqah 69:7

Here khāwiyah, from KH W Y, occurs in place of munqaʿir.

Munqaʿir, in the palm-tree context, denotes detachment from the base. Fallen and empty are two lexical explanations of khāwiyah. This article selects “fallen” in its translation. That choice does not eliminate the other interpretation, and the two explanations need not describe two simultaneous features.

This lexical distinction does not allow hollowness to be inferred from munqaʿir; nor does an empty trunk, by itself, establish uprooting. The example clarifies meanings rather than reconstructing the event’s physical details.

The two verses portray different aspects of the same event. Al-Qamar highlights the wind’s uprooting of people; Al-Ḥāqqah describes them lying lifeless after the event.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

For munqaʿirin in Al-Qamar 54:20, “uprooted from the base” is proposed.

“Pulled out by the roots” and “uprooted” are also natural and defensible equivalents.

“Fallen” conveys part of the sense of inqiʿār but does not fully preserve detachment from the base in the palm-tree context.

“Hollow” is unsuitable for munqaʿir. It belongs to one explanation of the distinct word khāwiyah, not to the meaning of munqaʿir.

For non-Qur’anic uses of this root, suitable equivalents include bottom, depth, base, deepening, reaching the bottom, and overturning.

6. Conclusion

Q ʿ R concerns depth, the bottom or lowest part of something, reaching it, and, for trees, uprooting from the base and falling.

The Qur’an uses munqaʿir for palms uprooted from their bases. The fate of ʿĀd before the powerful wind is depicted through their condition.

Proposed Forqan definition: Depth, bottom, and base, and reaching them; for a tree, becoming uprooted from the base. Munqaʿir in Al-Qamar 54:20 describes an uprooted palm. Falling accompanies the image, but hollowness is not implied by the word itself.
