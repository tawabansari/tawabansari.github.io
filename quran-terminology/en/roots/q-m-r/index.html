1. Basic meaning and linguistic background

In poetry transmitted from Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, a pre-Islamic poet, qamar names the moon. Such evidence supports the word’s early use, without establishing the poem’s exact date or when the moon’s name first arose.

Ibn Fāris treats whiteness as central to this family and connects the moon’s name with it. This lexical analysis does not establish the historical sequence of the words. This entry focuses on the moon’s name and its Qur’anic uses.

Qur’anic form: قَمَر (qamar), the moon. It occurs 27 times in 26 verses, twice in Fuṣṣilat 41:37. Section 10 provides the complete list.

Related non-Qur’anic forms include أَقْمَار (aqmār), the plural of qamar; قُمَيْر (qumayr), its diminutive; أَقْمَر (aqmar), white or bright; and قَمْرَاء (qamrāʾ), a feminine adjective or a name for moonlight. Forms concerning gambling are treated in a separate note at the end.

2. Evidence from early Arabic

The moon and nighttime light

In a poem addressed to ʿAmr ibn Hind, Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd describes camels for whose protection he had relied on others:

وَكَانَ لَهَا جَارَانِ قَابُوسُ مِنْهُمَا وَعَمْرٌو وَلَمْ أَسْتَرْعِهَا الشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرْ

“They had two protectors, Qābūs being one and ʿAmr the other; I had not entrusted them to the sun and moon as herdsmen.”

The verse is also transmitted with a different name for one protector, while the expression concerning the sun and moon is retained.

Entrusting livestock to the sun and moon was an Arabic image for leaving them without a herdsman by day and night. The poet says he had arranged protection for his property rather than leaving it unattended.

This verse in the transmitted dīwān of Ṭarafah illustrates qamar meaning the moon. It does not establish how other senses developed.

Classical Arabic uses لَيْلَةٌ قَمْرَاءُ for a moonlit night and أَقْمَرَتِ اللَّيْلَةُ for a night becoming bright with moonlight. الْقَمْرَاء also names moonlight itself.

Whiteness and the effects of brightness

أَقْمَر and its feminine قَمْرَاء are recorded for whiteness and brightness. Another branch uses قَمِرَ for impaired sight from brightness. These uses are distinct from the celestial body’s name.

These uses belong to the field of whiteness and brightness. In the Qur’an, however, qamar names the moon, not light or a condition of human vision.

3. The moon in the created order

The moon and light

Several verses describe the sun and moon in relation to light:

هُوَ الَّذى جَعَلَ الشَّمسَ ضِياءً وَالقَمَرَ نورًا وَقَدَّرَهُ مَنازِلَ لِتَعلَموا عَدَدَ السِّنينَ وَالحِسابَ ما خَلَقَ اللَّهُ ذٰلِكَ إِلّا بِالحَقِّ يُفَصِّلُ الـٔايٰتِ لِقَومٍ يَعلَمونَ

Huwa lladhī jaʿala sh-shamsa ḍiyāʾan wa-l-qamara nūran wa-qaddarahu manāzila li-taʿlamū ʿadada s-sinīna wa-l-ḥisāb. Mā khalaqa llāhu dhālika illā bi-l-ḥaqq. Yufaṣṣilu l-āyāti li-qawmin yaʿlamūn.

“He is the One who made the sun a radiance and the moon a light, and determined stages for it so that you may know the number of years and the reckoning. The God created these only in truth. He sets out the signs in detail for people who know.”

Yunus 10:5

Here the sun is described as ضِيَاء (ḍiyāʾ) and the moon as نُور (nūr).

Surah Nūḥ also states:

وَجَعَلَ القَمَرَ فيهِنَّ نورًا وَجَعَلَ الشَّمسَ سِراجًا

Wa-jaʿala l-qamara fīhinna nūran wa-jaʿala sh-shamsa sirājā.

“And He made the moon a light among them, and made the sun a lamp.”

Nuh 71:16

Here too, the moon is described as light and the sun as a lamp.

Al-Furqān states:

تَبارَكَ الَّذى جَعَلَ فِى السَّماءِ بُروجًا وَجَعَلَ فيها سِرٰجًا وَقَمَرًا مُنيرًا

Tabāraka lladhī jaʿala fī s-samāʾi burūjan wa-jaʿala fīhā sirājan wa-qamaran munīrā.

“Blessed is the One who placed constellations in the sky, and placed in it a lamp and an illuminating moon.”

Al-Furqan 25:61

مُنِير (munīr), from N W R, describes the moon as bright or giving light.

Modern knowledge identifies the sun as a source of energy and light, while the moon’s visible light comes mainly from sunlight reflected by its surface. Describing the sun as a lamp and the moon as light is compatible with this distinction.

However, nūr and munīr in Arabic are not restricted to reflected light. Al-Aḥzāb 33:46 also applies munīr to a lamp.

Modern knowledge can deepen our understanding of the sun and moon, but “reflected light” should not be imposed as the lexical definition of qamar or nūr.

The moon and orbital motion

وَهُوَ الَّذى خَلَقَ الَّيلَ وَالنَّهارَ وَالشَّمسَ وَالقَمَرَ كُلٌّ فى فَلَكٍ يَسبَحونَ

Wa-huwa lladhī khalaqa l-layla wa-n-nahāra wa-sh-shamsa wa-l-qamar. Kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn.

“And He is the One who created the night and the day, the sun and the moon; each swims in an orbit.”

Al-Anbiya 21:33

The moon appears here alongside other phenomena of creation, in relation to orbital motion.

Qamar names the celestial body; falak expresses the course of motion; and يَسْبَحُونَ presents an image of smooth, swimming movement.

Yā Sīn also states:

لَا الشَّمسُ يَنبَغى لَها أَن تُدرِكَ القَمَرَ وَلَا الَّيلُ سابِقُ النَّهارِ وَكُلٌّ فى فَلَكٍ يَسبَحونَ

Lā sh-shamsu yanbaghī lahā an tudrika l-qamara wa-lā l-laylu sābiqu n-nahār. Wa-kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn.

“It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does the night outstrip the day; and each swims in an orbit.”

Ya-Sin 36:40

The sun and moon belong here to an ordered system, each with its course and motion. This should not be confused with their apparent alignment during a solar eclipse: apparent alignment does not mean the bodies physically reach one another.

The moon made serviceable

وَسَخَّرَ لَكُمُ الشَّمسَ وَالقَمَرَ دائِبَينِ وَسَخَّرَ لَكُمُ الَّيلَ وَالنَّهارَ

Wa-sakhkhara lakumu sh-shamsa wa-l-qamara dāʾibayni wa-sakhkhara lakumu l-layla wa-n-nahār.

“And He made the sun and moon serviceable to you, both continually at work; and He made the night and day serviceable to you.”

Ibrahim 14:33

دَائِبَيْنِ, from D ʾ B, is a dual accusative circumstantial expression referring to the sun and moon. It expresses continuity in activity.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:54 and Al-Naḥl 16:12 also relate the sun, moon, and other created phenomena to subjection and divine command.

Al-Raʿd 13:2, Luqmān 31:29, Fāṭir 35:13, and Al-Zumar 39:5 connect the sun’s and moon’s motion with an appointed term.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:61 uses this creation and subjection to expose the inconsistency between acknowledging God as Creator and turning away from truth.

The moon being made serviceable to humanity does not mean humans possess independent ownership or sovereignty over it. It belongs to an order whose regularity, motion, and effects humans can benefit from.

4. The moon’s stages and the reckoning of time

A prominent Qur’anic use of qamar connects it with recognizing and reckoning time.

فالِقُ الإِصباحِ وَجَعَلَ الَّيلَ سَكَنًا وَالشَّمسَ وَالقَمَرَ حُسبانًا ذٰلِكَ تَقديرُ العَزيزِ العَليمِ

Fāliqu l-iṣbāḥi wa-jaʿala l-layla sakanan wa-sh-shamsa wa-l-qamara ḥusbānā. Dhālika taqdīru l-ʿazīzi l-ʿalīm.

“Cleaver of daybreak; He made the night a rest, and the sun and moon a means of reckoning. That is the determining of the Mighty, the Knowing.”

Al-An'am 6:96

Al-Raḥmān also states:

الشَّمسُ وَالقَمَرُ بِحُسبانٍ

Ash-shamsu wa-l-qamaru bi-ḥusbān.

“The sun and moon follow a reckoning.”

Ar-Rahman 55:5

Ḥusbān, from Ḥ S B, concerns counting, calculation, and calculable order.

By observing the sun’s and moon’s regular changes, people can recognize the passage of days, months, years, and other periods.

The Qur’an also describes the moon’s changing appearance through a tangible image:

وَالقَمَرَ قَدَّرنٰهُ مَنازِلَ حَتّىٰ عادَ كَالعُرجونِ القَديمِ

Wa-l-qamara qaddarnāhu manāzila ḥattā ʿāda ka-l-ʿurjūni l-qadīm.

“And the moon: We determined stages for it until it returns like an old date-cluster stalk.”

Ya-Sin 36:39

مَنَازِل is the plural of مَنْزِل, from N Z L. Here it refers to the moon’s appointed stages or stations.

ʿUrjūn is the stalk bearing a cluster of dates. As it ages and dries, it curves. Comparing the moon to an old stalk depicts its slender, curved appearance near the end of the lunar cycle.

From the crescent’s first appearance through fullness and back to slenderness, the moon takes different visible forms. Its body does not grow or shrink through this cycle; the portion of its illuminated surface visible from Earth changes.

Al-Inshiqāq also states:

وَالقَمَرِ إِذَا اتَّسَقَ

Wa-l-qamari idhā ttasaq.

“By the moon when it becomes full.”

Al-Inshiqaq 84:18

اتَّسَقَ, from W S Q, concerns gathering together and becoming complete. Here it refers to the fullness of the moon’s illuminated appearance.

The Qur’an describes both the moon’s slenderness through the old stalk and its fullness through ittisāq. Qamar remains the moon in both states; the changing appearance does not alter its independent meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:189 explicitly connects lunar crescents with marking times for people and for pilgrimage.

5. The moon: a sign of creation, not a deity

Abraham’s observation of the moon

In Al-Anʿām, observing the moon occurs within Abraham’s discourse about the Lord. Interpretations differ over whether this is a search or an argument addressed to others; neither changes qamar’s lexical meaning.

فَلَمّا رَءَا القَمَرَ بازِغًا قالَ هٰذا رَبّى فَلَمّا أَفَلَ قالَ لَئِن لَم يَهدِنى رَبّى لَأَكونَنَّ مِنَ القَومِ الضّالّينَ

Fa-lammā raʾā l-qamara bāzighan qāla hādhā rabbī. Fa-lammā afala qāla laʾin lam yahdinī rabbī la-akūnanna mina l-qawmi ḍ-ḍāllīn.

“When he saw the moon rising, he said: This is my Lord. But when it set, he said: Unless my Lord guides me, I will surely be among the people who go astray.”

Al-An'am 6:77

بَازِغًا, from B Z GH, describes the moon rising and appearing; أَفَلَ, from ʾ F L, denotes setting and disappearing from view.

Despite its light and apparent splendor, the moon rises and sets and is subject to the created order.

Later in this passage, Abraham turns toward the Creator of the heavens and earth. What is created and subject to the created order cannot replace its Creator.

The prohibition of prostrating to the moon

وَمِن ءايٰتِهِ الَّيلُ وَالنَّهارُ وَالشَّمسُ وَالقَمَرُ لا تَسجُدوا لِلشَّمسِ وَلا لِلقَمَرِ وَاسجُدوا لِلَّهِ الَّذى خَلَقَهُنَّ إِن كُنتُم إِيّاهُ تَعبُدونَ

Wa-min āyātihi l-laylu wa-n-nahāru wa-sh-shamsu wa-l-qamar. Lā tasjudū li-sh-shamsi wa-lā li-l-qamari wa-sjudū li-llāhi lladhī khalaqahunna in kuntum iyyāhu taʿbudūn.

“Among His signs are the night and day, the sun and moon. Do not prostrate to the sun or to the moon, but prostrate to The God who created them, if it is Him alone you worship.”

Fussilat 41:37

The verse identifies the moon as one of God’s signs while prohibiting prostration to it.

Being a sign does not make something a deity. A phenomenon’s beauty, greatness, light, and usefulness may direct people toward its Creator, but do not make it worthy of worship.

Al-Ḥajj 22:18 includes the moon itself among those prostrating to God.

The moon is thus a created being subject to divine command, not an entity with independent authority against its Creator.

6. The moon in dreams, oaths, and events of the Resurrection

The moon in Joseph’s dream

إِذ قالَ يوسُفُ لِأَبيهِ يٰأَبَتِ إِنّى رَأَيتُ أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوكَبًا وَالشَّمسَ وَالقَمَرَ رَأَيتُهُم لى سٰجِدينَ

Idh qāla Yūsufu li-abīhi yā abati innī raʾaytu aḥada ʿashara kawkaban wa-sh-shamsa wa-l-qamara raʾaytuhum lī sājidīn.

“When Joseph said to his father: My father, I saw eleven stars, the sun and the moon; I saw them prostrating to me.”

Yusuf 12:4

The moon appears in a dream here, alongside the sun and eleven stars.

In Yūsuf 12:100, after his family gathers, Joseph speaks of the realization of his earlier dream’s interpretation.

The context indicates that the celestial bodies represented people and family relationships. The Qur’an does not explicitly identify which parent the sun represents and which the moon represents.

Qamar still means the moon lexically. Representing a family member is the moon’s interpreted role in this dream, not a separate dictionary meaning.

Oaths by the moon

The Qur’an swears by the moon in several verses:

كَلّا وَالقَمَرِ

Kallā wa-l-qamar.

“No indeed; by the moon.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:32

Al-Muddaththir 74:33 and Al-Muddaththir 74:34 then mention the night and morning.

Al-Shams states:

وَالقَمَرِ إِذا تَلىٰها

Wa-l-qamari idhā talāhā.

“By the moon when it follows it.”

Ash-Shams 91:2

The pronoun hā in تَلَاهَا refers to the sun in the preceding verse. Talā, from T L W, denotes following.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:18 likewise combines an oath by the moon with its fullness.

These uses draw attention to the order and signs of creation through the moon and its observable changes. The oath’s argumentative role is understood from each surah’s context and what follows it.

The moon splitting

اقتَرَبَتِ السّاعَةُ وَانشَقَّ القَمَرُ

Iqtarabati s-sāʿatu wa-nshaqqa l-qamar.

“The Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split.”

Al-Qamar 54:1

انْشَقَّ is a form VII past-tense verb from SH Q Q, meaning to split. الْقَمَرُ is its subject.

The predominant interpretation understands an event during Muhammad’s mission. Another reading relates it to events of the Resurrection; the two do not have equal historical prevalence.

The predominant interpretation draws on the past-tense form, Al-Qamar 54:2, and transmitted reports. The future-oriented reading draws on past tense used for a certain future event and the proximity of the Hour. The existence of that grammatical use alone does not determine this verse’s time reference.

Q M R itself does not determine when or how this event occurs. Its timing, manner, and relationship to the Resurrection require examination of the verb, context, and other Qur’anic evidence.

The moon at the Resurrection

وَخَسَفَ القَمَرُ

Wa-khasafa l-qamar.

“And the moon is darkened.”

Al-Qiyamah 75:8

وَجُمِعَ الشَّمسُ وَالقَمَرُ

Wa-jumiʿa sh-shamsu wa-l-qamar.

“And the sun and moon are gathered together.”

Al-Qiyamah 75:9

Khasf, from KH S F, concerns the moon losing its light here. Jamʿ, from J M ʿ, denotes gathering or bringing together.

These verses concern the Resurrection and changes to the familiar created order. Without supporting evidence, the darkening should not be confined to an ordinary lunar eclipse: the next verse speaks of the sun and moon being gathered together.

The verses do not specify the events’ physical mechanism.

7. Comparison with nearby roots

Q M R and H L L: the moon and the crescent

Qamar names the moon, whereas hilāl usually refers to its slender, newly appearing crescent.

Early Arabic authorities differed over how many nights the moon is called hilāl. Some uses also apply it to the slender moon at the month’s end.

Yā Sīn 36:39 still calls it qamar while describing its return to a form like an old date-cluster stalk.

Qamar in Qur’anic usage is therefore not limited to one lunar phase.

Q M R and B D R: the moon and the full moon

Badr is an Arabic name for the full moon, when the illuminated disk appears complete.

Every badr is the moon, but the moon is not called badr throughout all its phases.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:123, Badr is the proper name of the location of a battle, not a reference to the full moon.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:18 expresses the moon becoming full through qamar combined with ittisāq.

Q M R and SH H R: a celestial body and a period of time

Qamar names the celestial body; shahr denotes a period of time that Persian also calls māh.

Persian māh denotes both the moon and a month. Arabic expresses these concepts through two distinct words.

The Qur’an presents the moon’s motion and stages as means of reckoning time, whereas shahr names the time unit.

Q M R and N W R: the moon and light

Qamar names the moon; nūr denotes visible light or what makes seeing possible.

In قَمَرًا مُنِيرًا in Al-Furqān 25:61, qamar is the noun described and munīr its adjective.

Rendering qamar as brightness, moonlight, or light would erase the distinction between the celestial body and its illumination.

8. Translation recommendations for Forqan

“The moon” is proposed for all Qur’anic occurrences of qamar, corresponding to Persian ماه.

قَمَرًا مُنِيرًا may be rendered “an illuminating moon” or “a bright moon.”

الْقَمَرِ إِذَا اتَّسَقَ may be rendered “the moon when it becomes full,” preserving the distinction between the moon’s name and the description of becoming full.

“Crescent,” “full moon,” or “moonlight” should be considered only where the wording and context support that feature. None is a general substitute for qamar.

Outside the Qur’an, moonlight, whiteness, and being moonlit are suitable equivalents within the branch examined, according to the word’s form.

9. Conclusion

The main entry concerns the moon and words related to whiteness and moonlight. Gambling is discussed separately to avoid conflating meanings.

The Qur’an uses only the noun qamar from this family. The moon is a sign of creation, follows appointed motion and stages, helps reckon time, and is subject to divine command. It also appears in Joseph’s dream, oaths, and descriptions of Resurrection events.

Its lexical meaning remains stable throughout these uses; differences arise from context, accompanying verbs, and its role within the verses.

Proposed definition for Forqan: In every Qur’anic occurrence, qamar names the moon. Its light, stages, motion, and role in a dream are understood from context and do not replace its basic meaning.

11. Separate lexical note: gambling and wagering

Dictionaries record qimār and muqāmarah for wagering, and qamarahu for defeating another person in it. Grouping these words under Q M R does not prove that gambling derives historically from the moon’s name. Lexical explanations involving deception or taking advantage of inattention do not replace a documented word history.

The Qur’an does not use qimār. Examining maysir and its relationship to gambling belongs with Y S R and Al-Baqarah 2:219, Al-Māʾidah 5:90, and Al-Māʾidah 5:91. Neither its scope nor its ruling should be deduced from the moon’s name.
