1. Root Meaning and Word Structure

Arabic sunbul (سُنْبُل) names the grain-bearing head of cereals and crops producing ears. Its unit noun is sunbulah (سُنْبُلَة), and its two familiar plurals are sanābil (سَنَابِل) and sunbulāt (سُنْبُلَات). The natural Persian equivalent is khūsheh, “ear” or “head of grain.”

Lisān al-ʿArab reports from Ibn Sīdah: “السُّنْبُلُ مِنَ الزَّرْعِ وَاحِدَتُهُ سُنْبُلَةٌ”—sunbul belongs to cultivated crops, and one unit is called sunbulah.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: ears and lexical forms

The same source identifies wheat, barley, and al-dhurah (الذُّرَة) as examples. In early usage dhurah names a cereal that Lane explains as a kind of millet or sorghum; it should not automatically be identified with modern American maize. Sunbul is therefore not restricted to wheat, although a wheat ear is a familiar example.

Lane, DHRW: historical dhurah and its distinction from maize

The defining feature is the grains’ place within a particular structure. They develop there, producing a crop people can use after ripening and harvest.

The individual grain must be distinguished from the ear.

A grain can be sown or used after harvest for food or another planting. An ear is the plant structure in which grains develop.

The distinction is clear in the Qur’an too. Al-Baqarah 2:261 places ḥabbah, sanābil, and sunbulah within one image. Yūsuf 12:47 instructs that the harvested crop be left in its sunbul.

Sunbulah should therefore not be translated as the individual grain or the entire plant. It names a particular plant structure, although broader usage can employ it when referring to an ear-bearing crop.

Qur’anic morphological indexes register S N B L (س ن ب ل) as a quadriliteral entry. Early grammarians and lexicographers nevertheless disagreed over whether nūn is original or additional.

Some regard all four letters as radicals; others propose a derivational connection with S B L.

The disagreement concerns the word’s structural history, rather than the familiar, tangible meaning of an ear of grain.

2. Linguistic Forms and a Proposed Connection with S B L

Sunbul, Sunbulah, and Two Plurals

Sunbul is a general term for ears of grain; sunbulah denotes one ear.

The relationship resembles a collective noun and its unit noun. Sunbul in a field may refer to the crop’s ears collectively, while sunbulah identifies a single ear.

Arabic also has two important plurals.

On the quadriliteral analysis of S N B L, sanābil is a broken plural on the pattern faʿālil (فَعَالِل). Treating nūn as additional changes the morphological analysis.

Lane, SBL: nūn and the forms sunbul and sanābil

Sunbulāt is a plural formed by adding the ending -āt to the singular.

The Qur’an uses both: sanābil in Al-Baqarah 2:261, and sunbulāt in Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46.

Arabic grammar sometimes distinguishes plural forms associated with smaller and larger quantities. Some commentators accordingly call sunbulāt a plural of paucity and sanābil a plural of abundance.

This distinction should not become an absolute rule about the actual number of ears: the Qur’an expressly gives seven in both cases.

Al-Baqarah has sabʿa sanābila, while Yūsuf has sabʿa sunbulātin. Both expressions state seven.

Al-Samīn al-Ḥalabī discusses the difference in Al-Durr al-Maṣūn. One explanation concerns sunbulāt appearing beside baqarāt, “cows,” in Yūsuf.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn: the two plurals and sunbulāt beside baqarāt

This concerns coordination of the two constructions: the king’s dream places seven cows and seven ears together.

The morphological and rhythmic difference can therefore be recognized, without asserting that sanābil in Al-Baqarah necessarily conveys more than seven ears.

Nor must an independent hidden meaning be invented for every formal difference between the plurals. Several valid Arabic constructions can express the same stated number.

The Verb Sanbala

Sanbala al-zarʿu (سَنْبَلَ الزَّرْعُ) means that the crop or plant put forth ears.

The usage is recorded in Arabic lexicons.

The family also forms the imperfect yusanbilu (يُسَنْبِلُ) and verbal noun sanbalah (سَنْبَلَة).

The verb concerns the growth stage in which the plant’s ears emerge.

Sanbala does not occur in the Qur’an. All Qur’anic occurrences of this family are nouns.

Connection with Asbala and S B L

Tāj al-ʿArūs records sanbala al-zarʿu as a usage of Banū Tamīm, while the dialect of the Ḥijāz also uses asbala al-zarʿu (أَسْبَلَ الزَّرْعُ).

Tāj al-ʿArūs: Tamīm and Ḥijāz uses of sanbala and asbala

Both describe a crop putting forth ears.

This overlap led some lexicographers to regard nūn in sunbul as additional and connect the word with S B L.

Others regard nūn as original, citing the attested quadriliteral verb sanbala.

Both views require examination linguistically.

The two closely related verbs suggest a possible relationship between the families, but do not independently establish their historical order.

Likewise, regarding nūn as original does not establish that there was never a relationship between the constructions.

For Qur’anic study, the attested forms sunbul, sunbulah, sanābil, and sunbulāt can usefully be examined under quadriliteral S N B L, while the proposed derivation from S B L remains a separate discussion.

This should not be confused with sabīl, “way.” Some shared letters do not make the meanings of a way and an ear of grain identical.

Other Arabic Uses of Sunbul

Lexicons also use sunbul for certain aromatic plants, including what is called sunbulu al-ṭīb (سُنْبُلُ الطِّيب) or al-sunbulu al-hindī (سُنْبُلُ الْهِنْدِيّ).

These names concern plants used in preparing aromatic substances.

Sunbulah also names a familiar zodiacal sign, known in Persian as Sunbulah or Khūsheh: Virgo.

Another usage, sanbala al-rajulu thawbahu (سَنْبَلَ الرَّجُلُ ثَوْبَهُ), describes a man leaving his garment’s trailing part long and dragging it on the ground.

Lexicographers also connect this with asbala thawbahu (أَسْبَلَ ثَوْبَهُ).

Shared letters do not, however, establish a single certain historical image from which grain ears, aromatic plants, and trailing garments all developed.

For the trailing garment, the connection with asbala and the disputed status of nūn are particularly relevant.

The aromatic plant name may also have its own history.

These uses help explain the word’s lexical range without changing Qur’anic sunbulah.

The four verses examined concern ears of agricultural crops, rather than an aromatic plant, zodiacal sign, or trailing garment.

3. Early Evidence and the Tangible Meaning of an Ear

Major Arabic dictionaries record sunbul as the established name for a part of an agricultural crop.

Ibn Sīdah refers to al-sunbulu mina al-zarʿ, while al-Jawharī likewise lists sunbul and its plural among familiar Arabic words.

These definitions fit the Qur’an: its uses concern ears alongside grain, cultivation, harvest, or produce.

Early sources also record place names and personal names. For example, Sunbulah was the name of an old well in Mecca.

A rajaz line attributed to its diggers reads:

نَحْنُ حَفَرْنَا لِلْحَجِيجِ سُنْبُلَهْ

naḥnu ḥafarnā li-l-ḥajīji sunbulah

“We dug Sunbulah for the pilgrims.”

Sunbulah here is a well’s name, rather than a plant’s ear. The line cannot establish the word’s agricultural meaning.

This distinction matters when examining poetic evidence. Finding Sunbulah in an early verse does not itself show its use for grain ears.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: Sunbulah the well and the rajaz witness

For the agricultural meaning, explicit lexical definitions and the Qur’an provide clearer evidence. Among the sources examined, an independent poetic witness reliably preceding the Qur’an and unambiguously denoting an ear of grain was not clear enough to quote with certain attribution.

This limitation does not make the word unknown in early Arabic. Recording a meaning and demonstrating its antiquity through a particular poem are different questions.

An ear is a visible, familiar part of grain-bearing plants.

In plants such as wheat and barley, the grain-bearing structure develops during growth. Grains form there and become harvestable after ripening.

Grain number, head shape, and growth patterns are not identical across plants.

The precise properties of one wheat or barley variety should therefore not define every sunbulah.

The lexical meaning requires recognizing the ear-like, grain-bearing structure of an agricultural crop.

4. Qur’anic Uses: Multiplication of Grain and the King’s Dream

Al-Baqarah 2:261: One Grain and Seven Ears

Al-Baqarah 2:261

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ كَمَثَلِ حَبَّةٍ أَنْبَتَتْ سَبْعَ سَنَابِلَ فِي كُلِّ سُنْبُلَةٍ مِائَةُ حَبَّةٍ وَاللَّهُ يُضَاعِفُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

mathalu alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum fī sabīli Allāhi ka-mathali ḥabbatin anbatat sabʿa sanābila fī kulli sunbulatin miʾatu ḥabbatin wa-Allāhu yuḍāʿifu li-man yashāʾu wa-Allāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīmun

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of The God is like a grain that produced seven ears, in each ear a hundred grains. The God multiplies for whom He wills; The God is All-Encompassing, Knowing.”

Sanābil and sunbulah appear together here.

Sabʿa sanābila names seven ears; fī kulli sunbulatin, “in each ear,” considers each one separately.

The verse thus presents a clear image of increasing produce: one grain, seven ears, and a hundred grains in each ear.

The distinction between ḥabbah and sunbulah is especially clear.

Ḥabbah is the grain sown or made to grow at the image’s beginning. Sunbulah is the ear developing during growth and containing multiple grains.

The ear stands between the initial grain and the grains resulting from its growth.

Do the Numbers State an Invariable Agricultural Law?

The verse explicitly states seven ears and a hundred grains in each.

Their combination yields seven hundred grains for the image described.

The verse nevertheless begins with mathal, “example.” Its main purpose is to illustrate the multiplied effect of spending in the way of The God.

It should not become an invariable law requiring every seed in every soil and climate to produce seven ears of a hundred grains each.

Actual yield depends on the crop, soil, water, environmental conditions, and other growth factors.

In some cereals, one grain can produce a plant with several fertile stems and several ears. Grain number within each ear also varies.

University of Minnesota Extension: tillering and wheat heads

This makes the Qur’anic image understandable; understanding it does not require proving that every plant always gives exactly that yield.

Conversely, calling it a comparison does not remove the real meanings of grain and ear.

The comparison uses a tangible agricultural image. Sunbulah still means an ear of grain, while the image illustrates the increasing and multiplied effect of giving.

The later phrase wa-Allāhu yuḍāʿifu li-man yashāʾu, “The God multiplies for whom He wills,” also shows that the seven ears and hundred grains do not confine all multiplication to one fixed calculation.

The following verses, including Al-Baqarah 2:262, Al-Baqarah 2:263, and Al-Baqarah 2:264, discuss conduct after giving, reminders of generosity, harm, and showing off.

The growing-grain comparison should therefore be understood within its context of giving and ethical conditions, rather than as an unconditional promise of identical results for every outward act of generosity.

These ethical considerations do not alter sunbulah’s lexical meaning. It remains an ear, whose relationship with grain grounds the verse’s image.

Yūsuf 12:43: Green and Dry Ears in the King’s Dream

Yūsuf 12:43

وَقَالَ الْمَلِكُ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ يَا أَيُّهَا الْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِي فِي رُؤْيَايَ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ لِلرُّؤْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ

wa-qāla al-maliku innī arā sabʿa baqarātin simānin yaʾkuluhunna sabʿun ʿijāfun wa-sabʿa sunbulātin khuḍrin wa-ukhara yābisātin yā ayyuhā al-malaʾu aftūnī fī ruʾyāya in kuntum li-l-ruʾyā taʿburūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The king said: I see seven fat cows being eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears and other dry ones. O leading men, give me your judgment about my dream, if you interpret dreams.”

Sunbulāt denotes seven ears described as khuḍr, “green.”

Opposite them stand ukhara yābisātin, other dry ears.

The visible contrast is clear.

A green ear belongs to a plant retaining its freshness and green color. A dry ear represents another condition of the same kind of crop.

Green and dry do not, by themselves, establish everything about the crop’s health, maturity, or nutritional value.

For example, ears dry during the normal ripening of some cereals; dryness does not necessarily mean destruction of the crop.

Here too, yābisāt alone does not establish that every grain in the dry ears has perished.

The key point is that both groups belong to the king’s dream and gain a significance beyond their visible plant condition as the story continues.

The Qur’an relates the dream to differing periods of production and economic hardship.

That significance comes from interpreting the dream and its narrative context, rather than sunbulah originally meaning a year, famine, or abundance.

Within the dream, sunbulah remains an ear of the crop.

Yūsuf 12:46: Retelling the Same Dream

Yūsuf 12:46

يُوسُفُ أَيُّهَا الصِّدِّيقُ أَفْتِنَا فِي سَبْعِ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعِ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ لَعَلِّي أَرْجِعُ إِلَى النَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

yūsufu ayyuhā al-ṣiddīqu aftinā fī sabʿi baqarātin simānin yaʾkuluhunna sabʿun ʿijāfun wa-sabʿi sunbulātin khuḍrin wa-ukhara yābisātin laʿallī arjiʿu ilā al-nāsi laʿallahum yaʿlamūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Joseph, you most truthful one, give us your judgment about seven fat cows eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears and other dry ones, so that I may return to the people and they may know.”

The same royal dream is retold to Joseph.

Sunbulāt has the same meaning as in the earlier account; khuḍr and yābisāt retain the contrast between green and dry.

The important grammatical difference is that the king says wa-sabʿa sunbulātin in the first account, while the retelling has wa-sabʿi sunbulātin.

In the first, sabʿa is accusative and expresses part of what the king sees.

In the second, sabʿi is genitive within the construction governed by fī.

The case difference concerns the two sentence structures, without changing sunbulah’s meaning.

The narrative repetition passes the same signs from the king to Joseph, whose response then provides a practical plan for the coming years.

The two occurrences of sunbulāt should not be treated as independent meanings or different plant species.

5. Sunbulihi in Yūsuf 12:47: Keeping Grain in Its Ears

Yūsuf 12:47

قَالَ تَزْرَعُونَ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ دَأَبًا فَمَا حَصَدْتُمْ فَذَرُوهُ فِي سُنْبُلِهِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِمَّا تَأْكُلُونَ

qāla tazraʿūna sabʿa sinīna daʾaban fa-mā ḥaṣadtum fa-dharūhu fī sunbulihi illā qalīlan mimmā taʾkulūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“He said: You will cultivate for seven years continuously. What you harvest, leave in its ears, except a little of what you eat.”

Sunbulihi differs in form from the earlier occurrences.

Sunbul here is a collective or general term for the crop’s ears; the attached pronoun refers to the harvested produce.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five occurrences in four verses

It does not instruct putting the entire crop in one ear. It concerns leaving the produce within its own ears.

This is especially significant: the passage moves from a dream image to Joseph’s practical instruction concerning crops.

Harvesting versus Separating the Grain

Two actions should be distinguished.

First, ḥaṣadtum concerns harvesting the crop.

Second, fa-dharūhu fī sunbulihi commands leaving the harvested produce in its ears.

Harvesting therefore does not necessarily mean immediately separating the grains from the ears.

A crop can be harvested while its grains remain within the ear structure.

In many cereals, separating grain from the ear involves threshing or beating the harvested crop.

Joseph’s instruction emphasizes leaving it in the ears, apart from the small quantity consumed as food.

The sentence therefore says more than “store the crop”: it specifies leaving grain within its natural ear structure.

That distinction should survive translation.

Al-Ṭabarī: leaving harvested grain in its ears

The Ear and Protection of the Crop

Leaving grain in its ears can be one method of storing produce.

The natural head structure and coverings around the grain in some cereals can provide a degree of protection and reduce the need for immediate separation.

This does not mean that any crop, in any conditions, will necessarily remain sound for many years simply by remaining in its ears.

Moisture, pests, temperature, storage conditions, and the crop itself affect the duration and quality of storage.

FAO: moisture, temperature, and pests in grain storage

The verse does not specify warehouse construction, moisture levels, pest-control methods, or permissible storage periods for every cereal.

Its instruction should therefore not become a complete, universal storage manual for every climate and crop.

What is explicit is leaving the harvested produce in its ears, apart from the portion needed for food, within the historical circumstances and plan Joseph describes.

Connection with the Difficult Years

Yūsuf 12:48 describes seven difficult years consuming what has been set aside for them, except a little that is preserved.

The continuation shows that the preceding instruction forms part of preparation for future hardship.

The plan joins production in the earlier years with limited consumption and storage.

Lexically, sunbulah names the grain’s location within the plant. Contextually, retaining it within the ears serves preservation for the future.

These two levels should remain distinct.

Sunbulah itself cannot be defined as “storage,” “food security,” or “crisis management.” Those ideas arise from Joseph’s actions and instructions in relation to the future years.

Equally, the ear’s role in the practical instruction should not be reduced to a merely literary image.

The verse concerns a particular agricultural action for which the grain’s place within the ear matters.

6. Comparison with Grain, Cultivation, Produce, and Other Uses

Sunbulah and Ḥabbah

Ḥabbah (حَبَّة) is one grain.

Al-Baqarah 2:261 contrasts the initial grain with the ears developing through its growth.

The botanical distinction is clear: a grain can be planted to produce a new plant; the ear is the part in which new grains develop.

Sunbulah therefore cannot be treated as synonymous with ḥabbah.

In Al-Baqarah’s image, grain, plant, and ear belong to differing stages or components of growth.

Sunbulah and Ḥabb

Ḥabb (حَبّ) concerns grain and grain produce.

Its Qur’anic agricultural contexts can denote edible grains or grain-bearing produce.

Sunbulah instead names the plant structure in which the grains develop.

In Yūsuf 12:47 the distinction has a practical function: the grain produce is harvested but left within its ears.

Even when ḥabb and sunbulah concern the same crop, they describe different aspects.

Study of Ḥ B B

Sunbulah and Zarʿ

Zarʿ (زَرْع) concerns cultivation and the crop grown.

In Yūsuf 12:47, tazraʿūna describes cultivating; sunbulihi names a particular part of the harvested crop.

Zarʿ can encompass the whole activity or resulting crop, while sunbulah is the grain-bearing part of the plant.

Sunbulah and Nabt

Nabt (نَبْت) and the family N B T concern growth and the emergence of plants.

Al-Baqarah 2:261 uses anbatat of the grain producing ears.

The relationship shows that an ear results from growth; sunbulah itself does not mean the act of growing.

Growth is a process; the ear is part of what develops through it.

Study of N B T

Sunbulah and Thamar

Thamar (ثَمَر) concerns fruit and plant yield, with a broader field in certain uses.

Cereal grains can be considered the plant’s produce or yield as a result of growth, but sunbulah names the particular structure containing them.

Thamar and sunbulah can concern the same production process in certain instances without being lexical synonyms.

Sunbulah and Sabīl

Given the proposed additional nūn, distinctions from other words listed under S B L must also remain clear.

Qur’anic sabīl chiefly means a way or path.

Qur’anic sunbul denotes the crop’s ears.

Even accepting derivation from an agricultural branch of S B L does not establish that an ear is called a “way” in the Qur’an, or that guidance and paths belong to its direct meaning.

Possible historical derivation and a word’s actual meaning in a sentence are distinct questions.

7. Conclusion and Suggested Translation

Arabic sunbul denotes the ear-bearing part of cereal plants where grains develop. Its unit noun is sunbulah, with the familiar plurals sanābil and sunbulāt.

Lexicons include wheat, barley, and other grain crops, so the meaning should not be restricted to one cereal.

The original or additional status of nūn is disputed. Sanbala al-zarʿu and asbala al-zarʿu make the relationship worth examining without establishing their historical order conclusively.

The family occurs five times in four Qur’anic verses.

Al-Baqarah 2:261 depicts one grain producing seven ears, with a hundred grains in each, to illustrate multiplied giving in the way of The God.

Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46 include green and dry ears in the king’s dream.

Yūsuf 12:47 uses sunbulihi in a practical instruction: leave the harvested produce in its ears, apart from a small amount for food.

All these uses rest on the tangible meaning of an ear of grain.

In one verse it illustrates multiplying grain; in two it belongs to dream imagery; in another it is the natural grain-bearing structure relevant to crop storage.

None of these contextual roles should, by itself, become the root’s lexical definition.

Brief root meaning: An ear or head of grain: the grain-bearing part of a plant in which the crop’s grains develop and lie together.

Qur’anic forms and suggested translation:

سَنَابِلَ — sanābila, Al-Baqarah 2:261: Ears produced by the grain’s growth. Suggested Persian equivalent: khūsheh, “ears.”

سُنْبُلَةٍ — sunbulatin, Al-Baqarah 2:261: One of the stated ears, containing a hundred grains. Suggested translation: “ear.”

سُنْبُلَاتٍ — sunbulātin, Yūsuf 12:43: Green ears in the king’s dream, opposite the dry ears. Suggested translation: “ears.”

سُنْبُلَاتٍ — sunbulātin, Yūsuf 12:46: The same green ears in the dream retold to Joseph. Suggested translation: “ears.”

سُنْبُلِهِ — sunbulihi, Yūsuf 12:47: The harvested crop’s natural ears, in which the grains are to remain. Suggested translation in the phrase: “in its ear,” or more naturally, “in its ears.”

Persian khūsheh, “ear” or “head of grain,” is the most suitable general equivalent.

The word directly means neither multiplied reward, famine, abundance, nor storage. These are ideas and consequences connected with the ear by the verses’ contexts.

The shared core of all Qur’anic occurrences is the ear as the plant’s grain-bearing structure.

The Qur’an uses this tangible reality to illustrate increasing yield, describe a dream, and explain a method of storing grain. Understanding an ear’s physical structure helps explain these uses without inventing meanings or removing the word’s natural features.
