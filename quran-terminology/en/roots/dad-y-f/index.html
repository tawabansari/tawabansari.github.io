Guests, hospitality, and hosting: from staying with another to protecting a guest's dignity

In early Arabic, ض ي ف concerns approaching, inclining toward something, and joining or staying with another. The related noun ضَيْف denotes someone who arrives at another person's or family's home as a guest.

ضِيَافَة denotes hospitality. In the context of hosting, أَضَافَ and ضَيَّفَ mean receiving someone as a guest and providing hospitality.

Early dictionaries recognize inclination and approach among this family's central meanings. For example, ضَافَ إِلَيْهِ concerns approaching or inclining toward someone, while أَضَافَ الشَّيْءَ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ concerns joining or adding one thing to another.

In social usage, guests come to another person and spend some time in the host's surroundings or care. Hosts receive them and offer hospitality within their circumstances and means.

This relationship may involve food, accommodation, welcome, or protection. None alone exhausts the root's meaning. Guests may come for a meal, stay overnight, or need assistance and hospitality during a journey.

The root occurs six times in the Quran: five nominal occurrences of ضَيْف in different constructions and one verb, يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا.

These occurrences belong to three settings: Abraham's guests, Lot's guests, and a settlement whose people refuse hospitality to Moses and his companion.

They illuminate three aspects of the relationship: welcoming and honouring guests, protecting them from assault and abuse, and refusing hospitality when food is requested.

1. Early Arabic meaning and historical witnesses

In early Arabic, ضَيْف was an established word for someone staying with another in the position of a guest.

Desert travel and distances between settlements could make hospitality especially important: travellers might need water, food, rest, or assistance to continue their journey. This practical setting does not by itself establish the word's origin.

Being a guest does not necessarily mean being poor, helpless, or homeless. Wealthy people can also be guests. The relationship concerns arriving and being received in that capacity.

Dictionaries record several related expressions.

ضِفْتُ الرَّجُلَ means “I stayed with the man as his guest.”

أَضَفْتُهُ means “I received him as my guest.”

ضَيَّفْتُهُ also means receiving someone as a guest and offering hospitality.

The direction of the action matters. In the first construction, someone goes to another and becomes their guest. In the second and third, the host receives the visitor.

Approach and inclination

ضَافَتِ الشَّمْسُ describes the sun's inclination, while تَضَيَّفَتْ لِلْغُرُوبِ describes its approaching sunset.

ضَافَ السَّهْمُ عَنِ الْهَدَفِ is recorded for an arrow that veers from its target.

Both involve movement or inclination, but in different directions: the sun inclines toward sunset, while the arrow deviates from its target.

Early sources also record ضَيْفُ الْوَادِي for a valley's side and ضَيْفَانِ الْوَادِي for its two sides.

These uses place approach, inclination, and being beside something within the family's recorded semantic range.

Ibn Fāris takes inclination of one thing toward another as the root's central principle and relates the guest to coming toward and staying with another person.

This clarifies the semantic connection between guest and inclination. It does not prove that the guest sense historically developed later; establishing the sequence requires independently dated evidence.

Guests in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period

A poem transmitted in the collection attributed to Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī describes the speaker's treatment of guests:

وَإِنِّي لَعَبْدُ الضَّيْفِ مَا دَامَ ثَاوِيًا
وَمَا فِيَّ إِلَّا تِلْكَ مِنْ شِيمَةِ الْعَبْدِ

wa-innī la-ʿabdu al-ḍayfi mā dāma thāwiyan / wa-mā fiyya illā tilka min shīmati al-ʿabdi

Translation: “I am the guest's servant for as long as he stays; that alone is the servant's trait in me.”

الضَّيْف refers here to someone staying with a host.

ثَاوِيًا denotes remaining or residing. The guest is someone who has come to stay for a time in another person's surroundings.

Calling himself عَبْدُ الضَّيْف emphasizes service to the guest. It is a literary image of attentive care, not a claim of literal enslavement.

Poetic collections attribute this line to Ḥātim, but closely related wording is also attributed to other poets. The name Ḥātim alone does not securely date this exact line before the Quran.

The witness nevertheless shows that, in Arabic literary tradition, a guest was more than a recipient of food: staying with a host created a relationship involving hospitality and attention.

Lexical meaning and the social value of hospitality

Arabic poetry praises generosity toward guests. The meaning of ضَيْف must nevertheless be distinguished from the ethical value of hospitality.

ضَيْف does not itself mean an honoured, poor, unprotected, or needy person. It identifies a person's relationship to a host.

A host may treat a guest well or fail in their responsibilities. A guest may likewise behave well or badly.

The moral value of either person's conduct follows from their actions, not from the title ضَيْف itself.

The distinction matters in the Quran too. Abraham honours his guests, Lot resists their abuse, and a settlement refuses hospitality to two travellers. The relationship is comparable, while the conduct differs.

2. Core meaning and forms

Important recorded forms include:

ضَيْف: a guest; someone arriving at another's place in that capacity.

أَضْيَاف, ضُيُوف, and ضِيفَان: plural forms of “guest.”

ضَيْفَة: a feminine form, although ضَيْف itself can also refer to a woman.

ضَافَ: inclined, approached, or stayed with another.

ضِفْتُهُ: I became his guest or sought hospitality from him.

أَضَافَ: joined one thing to another; in hospitality, received someone as a guest.

ضَيَّفَ: offered hospitality to someone as a guest.

ضِيَافَة: hospitality and care for guests.

اسْتَضَافَ: requested hospitality or asked to become a guest.

تَضَيَّفَ: inclined toward something; socially, became someone's guest or sought hospitality.

مُضِيف: a host; someone receiving another as a guest.

مُضَاف: something added or joined to another.

إِضَافَة: adding, joining, or establishing a relationship between two things.

These forms do not mean the same thing in every use.

In Arabic grammar, إِضَافَة names the linking of nouns in constructions such as كِتَابُ زَيْدٍ, “Zayd's book.”

The first noun is related to the second, expressing possession, association, or another relationship.

This grammatical term connects with joining and dependence, but its technical meaning should not be transferred to the Quranic ضَيْف.

Similarly, مُضِيف denotes a host in modern Arabic, but not every act of “adding” is an act of hospitality.

Why can ضَيْف refer to one or several people?

Arabic ضَيْف can refer to one guest or a group of guests.

Some lexicographers explain its unchanged form through an original verbal-noun use, allowing singular and plural reference without necessarily changing the word.

This is a noteworthy linguistic explanation, not independent proof of the word's historical formation.

In its Quranic nominal occurrences, ضَيْف has a singular-looking form but refers to groups of guests.

For example, Adh-Dhāriyāt 51:24 has ضَيْفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الْمُكْرَمِينَ.

الْمُكْرَمِينَ is masculine plural, confirming that several guests are intended.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:68, Lot says هَٰؤُلَاءِ ضَيْفِي.

هَٰؤُلَاءِ is plural, while ضَيْفِي retains the form ضَيْف with an attached possessive pronoun.

The singular-looking form must therefore not be taken to mean that only one guest is present.

“Guests” is consequently the appropriate translation in these contexts.

3. Abraham's guests: hospitality and honour

Al-Ḥijr 15:51

وَنَبِّئْهُمْ عَنْ ضَيْفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ

wa-nabbiʾhum ʿan ḍayfi ibrāhīm

Al-Ḥijr 15:51

Translation: “And inform them about Abraham's guests.”

ضَيْفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ refers to the guests who came to Abraham.

ضَيْفِ is the first member of a genitive construction, and إِبْرَاهِيمَ is its second member.

The construction expresses the guest–host relationship, not Abraham's ownership of the visitors.

They are people who have come to him and occupy the position of his guests.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:52, they enter and offer peace, while Abraham expresses apprehension.

The following verses reveal that they are Messengers bringing communications from God.

They are called guests before their mission is fully disclosed.

Being a guest therefore does not depend on the host knowing one's complete identity or purpose. Visitors may occupy that position before the host fully knows who they are.

This does not establish that every stranger must be admitted without precautions. The verse describes a guest–host relationship, not every detail of receiving unfamiliar people.

Honoured guests in Adh-Dhāriyāt

Adh-Dhāriyāt 51:24

هَلْ أَتَاكَ حَدِيثُ ضَيْفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الْمُكْرَمِينَ

hal atāka ḥadīthu ḍayfi ibrāhīma al-mukramīn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:24

Translation: “Has the account of Abraham's honoured guests reached you?”

Here, ضَيْفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ is accompanied by الْمُكْرَمِينَ.

الْمُكْرَمِينَ belongs to ك ر م and describes those who are honoured.

The honour is expressed by this separate adjective. The mere fact that an adjective is added would not prove that its meaning can never overlap with the noun: adjectives can also reinforce a meaning.

The two points remain distinguishable: they are Abraham's guests, and they are described here as honoured.

In Adh-Dhāriyāt 51:25, they enter and greet Abraham. He returns the greeting and finds them unfamiliar.

In Adh-Dhāriyāt 51:26, he goes to his family and brings a fattened calf.

In Adh-Dhāriyāt 51:27, he places it near them and asks, “Will you not eat?”

These actions depict hospitality: welcome, returning a greeting, providing food, and inviting guests to eat.

Hūd 11:69 also reports that Abraham brought a roasted calf for the Messengers.

In Hūd 11:70, seeing that their hands do not reach for the food makes him apprehensive.

The Quran thus says that Abraham provided food, but does not say that the guests ate it.

Offering hospitality and accepting or eating the food are distinct actions.

What does Abraham's hospitality show?

Abraham receives the guests and provides food before knowing their true identity.

His conduct illustrates attention to guests and their needs.

The particular food should not be turned into a universal rule that every host must slaughter an animal or provide an expensive meal.

The calf is what Abraham prepares in this account, not part of the definition of ضِيَافَة or an independent command specifying everyone's hospitality.

These verses do not prescribe a length of stay, quantity of food, or number of meals.

The narrative plainly depicts Abraham treating his visitors generously and with honour.

4. Lot's guests: hosting in the face of assault

Hūd 11:78

وَجَاءَهُ قَوْمُهُ يُهْرَعُونَ إِلَيْهِ وَمِنْ قَبْلُ كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ السَّيِّئَاتِ ۚ قَالَ يَا قَوْمِ هَٰؤُلَاءِ بَنَاتِي هُنَّ أَطْهَرُ لَكُمْ ۖ فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَلَا تُخْزُونِ فِي ضَيْفِي ۖ أَلَيْسَ مِنْكُمْ رَجُلٌ رَشِيدٌ

wa-jāʾahu qawmuhu yuhraʿūna ilayhi wa-min qablu kānū yaʿmalūna al-sayyiʾāti qāla yā qawmi hāʾulāʾi banātī hunna aṭharu lakum fa-ttaqū allāha wa-lā tukhzūni fī ḍayfī alaysa minkum rajulun rashīd

Hūd 11:78

Translation: “His people came rushing toward him; previously they had been doing evil deeds. He said: My people, these are my daughters; they are purer for you. So be mindful of The God, and do not disgrace me concerning my guests. Is there not a right-minded man among you?”

ضَيْفِي means “my guests.”

The attached ي expresses Lot's relationship to them as his guests.

Unlike Abraham's meal, the immediate issue is the townspeople's threatening conduct toward Lot's visitors.

Hūd 11:77 describes Lot's distress at the Messengers' arrival. Hūd 11:78 then describes his people rushing toward him.

Lot urges mindfulness of God and says وَلَا تُخْزُونِ فِي ضَيْفِي.

That is, do not humiliate or disgrace me concerning my guests.

The preposition فِي identifies the matter in relation to which disgrace is at issue.

ضَيْفِي is thus situated in a context where Lot recognizes responsibility concerning people's treatment of his visitors.

In these circumstances, hospitality is connected with concern for people's safety and dignity.

“Safety,” “freedom,” and “protection” should not be made independent lexical meanings of ضَيْف. Those responsibilities emerge from Lot's conduct, the threat, and wider Quranic principles.

إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ ضَيْفِي

Al-Ḥijr 15:68

قَالَ إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ ضَيْفِي فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ

qāla inna hāʾulāʾi ḍayfī fa-lā tafḍaḥūn

Al-Ḥijr 15:68

Translation: “He said: These are my guests, so do not disgrace me.”

Lot again invokes their status as guests before the townspeople.

إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ ضَيْفِي emphasizes their relationship to him.

فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ then asks the people not to disgrace him.

Al-Ḥijr 15:69 continues with a call to mindfulness of God and a prohibition against humiliating him.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:70, they answer أَوَلَمْ نَنْهَكَ عَنِ الْعَالَمِينَ: “Did we not forbid you concerning other people?”

Their response indicates opposition to Lot's treatment of others and pressure on how he receives visitors.

The guests have become the focus of a conflict between their host and the townspeople.

Lot tries to protect them from abusive conduct, while the people seek to impose their will on him.

Receiving guests can therefore involve more than providing meals and accommodation: the host may also face questions of protecting their safety and dignity.

This does not imply unlimited capacity or responsibility for every host to confront every danger. Ability, danger, and responsibility require separate consideration.

Seeking access to the guests for abuse

Al-Qamar 54:37

وَلَقَدْ رَاوَدُوهُ عَنْ ضَيْفِهِ فَطَمَسْنَا أَعْيُنَهُمْ فَذُوقُوا عَذَابِي وَنُذُرِ

wa-laqad rāwadūhu ʿan ḍayfihi fa-ṭamasnā aʿyunahum fa-dhūqū ʿadhābī wa-nudhur

Al-Qamar 54:37

Translation: “They sought his guests from him, so We obliterated their eyes: taste My punishment and My warnings.”

ضَيْفِهِ refers to Lot's guests.

The attached ه refers to Lot.

رَاوَدُوهُ عَنْ ضَيْفِهِ describes their attempts to gain access to his guests through insistent solicitation.

رَاوَدَ belongs to another root and describes their approach. ضَيْف identifies the people targeted by it.

Other accounts of Lot place the demand in a context of abusive conduct.

It cannot reasonably be reduced to an ordinary request to meet the visitors or invite them elsewhere.

The noun ضَيْف does not itself supply every detail of the threatened abuse. It identifies them as Lot's guests; the threatening purpose is understood from the surrounding narrative, not from the root alone.

Comparing Abraham's and Lot's hospitality

Abraham's account emphasizes food, welcome, and honouring guests.

Lot's emphasizes defending their dignity against threats and assault.

The actions are related, but not identical.

A person might provide abundant food while remaining indifferent to others insulting or abusing their guest.

Conversely, someone with modest means might protect a guest's dignity through respectful treatment and preventing abuse.

Both accounts concern conduct toward someone who has arrived, beyond the kind of meal or the host's resources.

Hospitality in its wider social sense can thus encompass both welcome and respect for guests' safety and dignity.

The lexical meaning of ضَيْف remains “guest”; responsibilities are understood from context and conduct.

5. Refused hospitality: Moses and his companion

Al-Kahf 18:77

فَانْطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَتَيَا أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ اسْتَطْعَمَا أَهْلَهَا فَأَبَوْا أَنْ يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا فَوَجَدَا فِيهَا جِدَارًا يُرِيدُ أَنْ يَنْقَضَّ فَأَقَامَهُ ۖ قَالَ لَوْ شِئْتَ لَتَّخَذْتَ عَلَيْهِ أَجْرًا

fa-nṭalaqā ḥattā idhā atayā ahla qaryatin istaṭʿamā ahlahā fa-abaw an yuḍayyifūhumā fa-wajadā fīhā jidāran yurīdu an yanqaḍḍa fa-aqāmahu qāla law shiʾta la-ttakhadhta ʿalayhi ajrā

Al-Kahf 18:77

Translation: “So they set out until, when they reached the people of a settlement, they asked its people for food, but they refused to host them. They found there a wall about to collapse, and he set it upright. He said: Had you wished, you could have taken payment for it.”

This is the root's only verbal occurrence in the Quranic reading used here.

Moses and his companion arrive at a settlement and ask its people for food.

The people refuse them hospitality.

The construction يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا

يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا comes from the form-II verb ضَيَّفَ.

The base imperfect يُضَيِّفُ means “hosts” or “offers hospitality.”

After أَنْ, the verb is subjunctive, marked by removal of the final nūn in this conjugation.

The wāw is the plural subject pronoun, referring to the settlement's people.

هُمَا is the dual object pronoun, referring to Moses and his companion.

The construction thus means that they refused to receive those two as guests.

This differs clearly from the nominal ضَيْف.

In Abraham's and Lot's accounts, people are identified as guests. Here, يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا describes the act of offering or refusing hospitality.

ضَيْف names the guest; ضَيَّفَ describes the host's action toward them.

اسْتَطْعَمَا and يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا

اسْتَطْعَمَا belongs to ط ع م and means that the two requested food.

يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا concerns receiving them as guests.

The request for food clarifies what hospitality involves in this setting.

The verbs are nevertheless not exact synonyms.

Someone can buy food in a market without being the seller's guest. Someone else can visit a family as a guest without needing or wanting food at that moment.

The verse connects requesting food with hospitality, but does not merge the two roots.

Nor does it say that Moses and his companion necessarily requested an overnight stay.

يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا should therefore not be restricted to “allow them to sleep in their homes.”

The refusal concerns hospitality connected here with a request for food.

The refusal and the companion's conduct

After the refusal, Moses and his companion find a wall close to collapse.

Moses' companion sets it upright.

Moses observes that he could have taken payment had he wished.

Two actions stand beside each other: hospitality is refused to the travellers, while the companion repairs the wall without payment.

Al-Kahf 18:82 explains that the wall belongs to two orphans and conceals treasure for them.

The repair is therefore not simply a reward for the settlement's hospitality. It concerns preserving the two children's interests.

The story should not be reduced to a universal requirement to work without payment, even for those who have treated one badly.

Nor does one reported refusal justify condemning every inhabitant's conduct in every matter.

The verse reports refused hospitality followed by an action whose reason is disclosed later.

Does this verse prescribe obligatory hospitality?

The sentence describes refusal, but does not independently prescribe a quantity of food, duration of stay, or penalty for withholding hospitality.

The setting presents the refusal unfavourably, particularly alongside the companion's beneficial action.

A broader inquiry into practical duties would also need to consider passages about need, giving, travellers' rights, and social responsibility.

For example, Al-Isrāʾ 17:26 addresses giving relatives, the needy, and travellers their due.

Adh-Dhāriyāt 51:19 recognizes a right in wealth for the petitioner and the deprived.

Such verses matter to a wider study of responsibility toward travellers in need.

Their wider teachings should not all be inserted into the lexical meaning of ضَيْف or ضَيَّفَ.

6. Comparison with related roots

ض ي ف (ḍayf) and ن ز ل (nazala)

نَزَلَ concerns descending, alighting, or settling somewhere.

The social sense of ضَيْف identifies someone received as another's guest.

Guests may alight or stay with a host, but not every arrival is a guest relationship.

A traveller stopping somewhere is not necessarily a guest of its inhabitants.

Conversely, a guest's visit may be brief.

نُزُل can also denote provisions for reception or lodging. The Quran uses it for reception in the afterlife as well.

That connects with hospitality, but not every derivative of ن ز ل means ضِيَافَة.

ض ي ف (ḍayf) and ط ع م (ṭaʿām)

ط ع م concerns eating, food, and feeding.

Al-Kahf 18:77 places اسْتَطْعَمَا beside يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا.

Food can clearly be an important part of hospitality.

Hospitality is not limited to food, however, and not every act of feeding makes someone a guest.

One may feed a person in need on the road without hosting them at home.

Conversely, a visit may involve water, rest, or conversation without a meal.

Food and hospitality are related but distinct.

ض ي ف (ḍayf) and ق ر ي (qirā)

In early Arabic, قِرَى denotes food or hospitality prepared for guests.

It appears in poetry about generosity and hospitality.

Within this field, ضَيْف is the guest, while قِرَى is what is provided for them.

A host might receive a guest and then prepare قِرَى.

The guest, the act of hosting, and the provisions are related but distinguishable.

قِرَى should not be identified without morphological and historical examination with every use of قَرْيَة or other forms assigned to ق ر ي.

Each derivative must be understood within its own construction and context.

ض ي ف (ḍayf) and أ و ي (āwā)

أ و ي concerns seeking refuge, returning to a place of shelter, and sheltering others.

Receiving and protecting those who need refuge is an important Quranic theme.

This connects with aspects of hospitality, particularly in Lot's account.

But not every guest seeks refuge, and not everyone given refuge is a guest in the ordinary social sense.

Someone may visit friends without facing any danger.

Another person may flee war, oppression, or threats and need protection beyond a short social visit.

At-Tawbah 9:6 addresses granting protection to a polytheist who requests it and conveying him to a place of safety.

That is conceptually related to protecting Lot's guests, but does not alter the direct meaning of ضَيْف.

ض ي ف (ḍayf) and جَار (neighbour)

Guests and neighbours can both hold important places in social life.

A neighbour's relationship concerns residence or proximity; a guest's concerns arriving and being received by a host.

A neighbour may also visit as a guest, but the titles do not necessarily identify the same relationship.

An-Nisāʾ 4:36 calls for kindness to neighbours and those alongside a person.

This connects with hospitality through kindness and respect, without making “guest” and “neighbour” interchangeable.

ض ي ف (ḍayf) and ض ي ق (ḍayq)

These roots differ only in their final consonant.

ض ي ق concerns narrowness, constraint, and limited capacity.

The guest sense of ض ي ف concerns someone received by another.

Their similar spelling and sound do not erase their semantic distinction.

Guests may experience hardship, but constraint and need are not part of the noun's necessary definition.

ضَاقَ in Hūd 11:77, describing Lot's distress, should not lead to confusing ض ي ق with ض ي ف.

7. Translation guidance for Forqan

In its Quranic nominal use, ضَيْف means “guest.”

Since all these occurrences refer to several people, “guests” is more appropriate in context.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:51:

ضَيْفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ — “Abraham's guests”

In Adh-Dhāriyāt 51:24:

ضَيْفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الْمُكْرَمِينَ — “Abraham's honoured guests”

In Hūd 11:78 and Al-Ḥijr 15:68:

ضَيْفِي — “my guests”

In Al-Qamar 54:37:

ضَيْفِهِ — “his guests”

In Al-Kahf 18:77:

يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا — “host the two of them” or “offer them hospitality”

In Persian, a concise equivalent for “host them” can preserve the plural subject, although an explicit “the two of them” conveys the Arabic dual object more clearly.

“Give them food” conveys part of the setting but restricts the verb to feeding.

“Give them refuge” also lacks sufficient support here: the verse concerns food and hospitality rather than protection from a specified danger.

A proposed brief root meaning for word boxes is:

“Approaching, joining, or staying with another; becoming a guest and offering hospitality.”

Each derivative's meaning in its verse should be written separately.

For ضَيْفِي, this could be “my guests, who have come to Lot”; for يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا, “offer hospitality to Moses and his companion.”

This prevents replacing the guest noun and the hosting verb with one undifferentiated explanation.

8. Summary

In early Arabic, ض ي ف concerns inclination, approach, and joining. Its social uses concern the relationship between guest and host.

ضَيْف identifies the guest. ضَيَّفَ and أَضَافَ, in hospitality contexts, describe receiving and hosting someone.

Lexical and literary evidence records guests, stays with hosts, and hospitality. Semantic similarity alone does not establish the historical order of inclination, guesthood, and joining or adding.

The root occurs six times in the Quran.

Abraham receives guests and provides food, illustrating honourable treatment.

Lot's account centres on protecting guests from threatening conduct, linking hosting with safeguarding people's dignity.

The settlement refuses hospitality to Moses and his companion. يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا directly names the act of hosting.

These settings connect hospitality with food, respect, and protection, but no single element exhausts the lexical meaning of ضَيْف.

The final definition is:

ض ي ف: inclining, approaching, or joining; socially, coming to another as a guest, and, in the hosting verbs, receiving and offering hospitality to that person.

ضَيْف identifies the guest, while ضَيَّفَ describes offering hospitality.

The Quranic accounts show that the guest–host relationship can extend beyond meals to respect, attention to needs, and protection of human dignity.

The ethical value of these actions comes from context and Quranic principles, not from the root alone.

Being a guest is a social relationship; treatment of guests reveals a person's ethical responsibilities.
