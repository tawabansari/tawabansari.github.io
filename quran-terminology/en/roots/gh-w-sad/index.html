Diving and descending beneath the surface: from searching for pearls to the work of devils under Solomon

In early Arabic, غ و ص concerns entering water and descending beneath its surface. غَاصَ is used when a person or being enters water and goes below it. غَوْص denotes diving itself, while غَوَّاص denotes someone who dives or is known for this activity.

An important use in early Arab life was diving to find shells and retrieve pearls from the sea. The diver went underwater to reach something not visible or accessible from above the surface.

Arabic also extends this physical image to entering a difficult or hidden subject and obtaining something not easily understood or reached. Someone who carefully examines a complex question and discovers a hidden meaning can figuratively be likened to a diver.

This metaphor must not replace the basic sense. Physical diving means going underwater; retrieving pearls, finding an object, or discovering a truth are possible purposes or outcomes accompanying it in different situations.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran: as يَغُوصُونَ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82 and as غَوَّاصٍ in Ṣād 38:37.

Both occurrences concern devils assigned to work under Solomon's rule.

The verses identify diving as one activity of theirs. Ghawṣ itself does not specify the beings' nature, bodily substance, underwater movement, or what they brought up.

1. Early Arabic meanings and poetic witnesses

غَاصَ فِي الْمَاءِ means to go underwater, while غَاصَ عَلَى اللُّؤْلُؤِ means to dive in search of pearls and retrieve them.

The first construction concerns entering the depths of water.

In the second, عَلَى identifies the object sought by the diver.

Diving and pearl extraction are therefore not identical. A diver may seek something, inspect an environment, or perform another task without necessarily obtaining pearls.

Early dictionaries record both غَائِص and غَوَّاص for a diver. مَغَاص denotes a diving place, and غِيَاصَة the diver's work or activity.

These usages show that diving had a name as a recognized activity, not merely an accidental movement in water.

A poetic witness from al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

A poem attributed to the well-known pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī includes:

أَوْ دُرَّةٍ صَدَفِيَّةٍ غَوَّاصُهَا
بَهِجٌ مَتَى يَرَهَا يُهِلُّ وَيَسْجُدُ

aw durratin ṣadafiyyatin ghawwāṣuhā / bahijun matā yarahā yuhillu wa-yasjudu

Translation: “Or like a pearl in its shell, whose diver rejoices on seeing it, cries out, and bows down.”

The poet compares a woman's beauty to a precious pearl.

Durrah denotes a pearl; ṣadafiyyah connects it with its shell.

غَوَّاصُهَا denotes the person who descends into the sea to find and retrieve it.

The diver's joy also shows that reaching the pearl is a valuable achievement.

The witness is useful because its diver represents the familiar activity of seeking something underwater.

Ghawwāṣ here is neither a name for an unknown being nor someone merely absorbed in thought. It refers to the recognized activity of diving.

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī is known as a pre-Islamic poet; the verse appears in the collection attributed to him and in early literary sources. Its surviving text, however, comes through later transmission and compiled manuscripts; its exact composition date is not independently established.

The witness is useful evidence of early usage, but its first recording should not be dated with certainty.

Diving and reaching hidden things

Many objects in the sea cannot be seen from its surface.

A pearl may lie inside a shell underwater. Reaching it requires descending below the surface into a less readily accessible environment.

This image involves three matters:

First, going underwater.

Second, searching or working in an environment not easily accessible from the surface.

Third, possibly obtaining the object for which the diver entered the water.

Only the first is a clear and necessary component of physical ghawṣ. The others may be purposes or outcomes.

A diver may go underwater and find nothing, or enter to do work unrelated to extracting goods.

The root must therefore not be defined solely as “retrieving pearls.”

Figurative use for investigating difficult matters

Early Arabic sources also record غَاصَ عَلَى الْعِلْمِ الْغَامِضِ: investigating a difficult, obscure subject deeply to understand it.

Water is no longer the subject here.

As the diver descends below the surface, the investigator goes beyond a subject's appearance to reach what is not initially evident.

This image aptly describes deep reflection and careful investigation.

Its recording in classical sources does not itself establish that the same metaphor was intended throughout pre-Islamic usage or in the verses concerning Solomon.

Nor does the shared image prove that diving historically meant discovering knowledge before it meant going underwater.

The physical sense is clear; figurative usage must be identified through its own evidence and context.

2. Semantic core and root forms

غ و ص has a weak middle consonant.

In غَاصَ the root's middle wāw becomes alif, while it remains visible in the verbal noun غَوْص.

The basic verbal forms are:

غَاصَ يَغُوصُ غَوْصًا

They mean to enter water and descend beneath its surface.

The main forms include:

غَاصَ: he went underwater; figuratively, he investigated a difficult subject in depth.

يَغُوصُ: he dives or goes underwater.

غَوْص: diving and descending beneath water.

غَوْصَة: a single dive or descent into water.

غَائِص: someone who dives.

غَوَّاص: a diver; someone known for diving as an activity or characteristic.

غَوَّاصُون: plural of ghawwāṣ, a group of divers.

غَاصَة: divers, especially those diving to retrieve things from the sea.

غِيَاصَة: diving as work or an occupation.

مَغَاص: a diving place where people descend to reach things underwater.

غَوَّصَ: he made someone dive or caused them to descend into water.

تَغْوِيص: submerging or making someone dive.

Some dictionaries also record restricted usages of forms such as غائصة and متغوصة concerning concealment of a person's condition.

These do not occur in the Quran; available evidence does not make them the root's primary meaning or instances of Quranic diving.

Ghāʾiṣ and ghawwāṣ

غَائِص is the active participle of غَاصَ.

It can describe someone performing the act of diving.

غَوَّاص follows فَعَّال, a pattern often expressing a recognized or repeated activity, or a person's skill.

Someone whose occupation is diving, for example, may be called ghawwāṣ.

The pattern alone cannot specify the number of dives, level of skill, or precise intensity of activity.

It may name an occupation or duty without stating how often someone dives.

This matters in Ṣād 38:37, where غَوَّاص accompanies بَنَّاء; both can name recognized activities or work.

Quranic forms

Only two forms of this family occur in the Quran.

يَغُوصُونَ is a third-person masculine plural imperfect verb.

غَوَّاصٍ is a noun on فَعَّال, appearing in the genitive in Ṣād 38:37.

غَائِص, غَوْصَة, مَغَاص, and غِيَاصَة do not occur in the Quran.

They help explain Arabic but must not be listed as forms actually present in the Quran.

3. يَغُوصُونَ: diving under Solomon

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82

وَمِنَ الشَّيَاطِينِ مَنْ يَغُوصُونَ لَهُ وَيَعْمَلُونَ عَمَلًا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ ۖ وَكُنَّا لَهُمْ حَافِظِينَ

wa-mina al-shayāṭīni man yaghūṣūna lahu wa-yaʿmalūna ʿamalan dūna dhālika wa-kunnā lahum ḥāfiẓīn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82

Translation: “And among the devils were those who dived for him and performed other work besides that; and We were guarding them.”

This is the root's first Quranic occurrence in the order of the text.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:81 mentions Solomon and the wind that moved at his command.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82 then introduces devils who dived for him and did other work.

The pronoun in لَهُ therefore refers to Solomon.

The subject is work performed for him within the divinely granted dominion and subjection described.

The construction يَغُوصُونَ لَهُ

يَغُوصُونَ is a third-person masculine plural imperfect from غ و ص.

The sentence begins with مِنَ الشَّيَاطِينِ مَنْ.

مِنَ الشَّيَاطِينِ identifies the group from which the subjects come.

مَنْ is a relative pronoun that may refer to one person or a group.

يَغُوصُونَ is plural because its referent is semantically a plurality.

لَهُ identifies the person for whom the work is performed.

The meaning is clear: some among the devils dived for Solomon.

The verb ghāṣa here concerns going underwater and diving.

The verse does not specify the sea or river, what they obtained, or how they could work underwater.

Was the purpose retrieving pearls?

Diving for pearls was a familiar use of this root in early Arabic.

Early commentaries accordingly often understood the devils' diving for Solomon as retrieving pearls and jewels from the sea.

That fits ordinary language, but the verb's meaning must be distinguished from its particular purpose in the story.

The verse itself names neither pearls, coral, shells, nor jewels.

Al-Naḥl 16:14 mentions extracting ornaments from the sea.

Al-Raḥmān 55:22 also mentions pearls and coral.

Valuable ornaments coming from water are thus familiar in Quranic language.

Those verses do not establish that all diving for Solomon in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82 was exclusively for pearls.

That could have been one activity, but establishing it as the sole purpose requires separate evidence.

وَيَعْمَلُونَ عَمَلًا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ

After mentioning diving, the verse says they performed other work.

دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ in this context refers to work besides diving.

Their activities cannot therefore all be reduced to going underwater.

Sabaʾ 34:12 and Sabaʾ 34:13 describe jinn working under Solomon, with examples including structures, images, great vessels, and fixed cauldrons.

The work in Solomon's dominion was not a single type of activity.

دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82 does not itself name all that work; specific examples come from other verses.

It also does not necessarily rank other work as less valuable than diving. “Besides that” is more natural in this context.

The role of وَكُنَّا لَهُمْ حَافِظِينَ

The verse ends by saying that The God was guarding them.

This introduces divine control and oversight of those beings within the narrative.

Commentaries offer different explanations of what that guarding concerned, including keeping them within Solomon's command or preventing disruption of their work.

The sentence itself does not explain every detail of that oversight.

For root study, the important point is that ḥifẓ belongs to another root; it is not part of ghawṣ's meaning.

Diving is their activity; divine guarding is the subject of a different clause.

4. غَوَّاص: divers alongside builders

Ṣād 38:37

وَالشَّيَاطِينَ كُلَّ بَنَّاءٍ وَغَوَّاصٍ

wa-al-shayāṭīna kulla bannāʾin wa-ghawwāṣ

Ṣād 38:37

Translation: “And the devils, every builder and diver.”

This is the root's second and final Quranic occurrence in textual order.

In Ṣād 38:35, Solomon asks his Lord for forgiveness and a distinctive dominion.

Ṣād 38:36 describes the wind being subjected to him.

Ṣād 38:37 then identifies the devils by two activities: building and diving.

غَوَّاص names or describes someone who dives.

The construction كُلَّ بَنَّاءٍ وَغَوَّاصٍ

كُلَّ encompasses the members of the group in question.

بَنَّاءٍ belongs to ب ن ي and follows فَعَّال; it denotes a builder or someone who performs construction work.

غَوَّاصٍ follows the same pattern and belongs to غ و ص.

بَنَّاءٍ is genitive as the complement of كُلَّ; غَوَّاصٍ is conjoined to it and is also genitive.

Their juxtaposition identifies two activities: building and diving.

It does not mean every builder was incapable of diving or every diver did no other work.

The verse supplies two occupational descriptions, not an exhaustive classification of those beings' abilities.

Does غَوَّاص mean a diving specialist?

فَعَّال can describe someone who regularly or characteristically performs an activity.

بَنَّاء names building work here, and غَوَّاص names diving work.

Diving is thus presented as a distinct activity among the tasks mentioned.

The pattern does not establish how often the divers entered the water, how long they stayed, or what skills they possessed.

Those details are absent from the verse.

Connections with other Solomon passages

Sabaʾ 34:12 describes jinn working for Solomon by his Lord's permission.

Sabaʾ 34:13 gives concrete examples of their work.

Sabaʾ 34:14 shows after Solomon's death that the jinn had not known of it; had they known the unseen, they would not have remained in the humiliating torment.

These verses help explain the workers' and subjected beings' place in the narrative.

First, their activity occurs by divine permission, not through independent power Solomon possessed without it.

Second, performing unusual work does not entail unlimited knowledge of the unseen.

Third, historical tales and legends of magical tools or boundless abilities must not replace the Quran's description.

Al-Baqarah 2:102 explicitly says that Solomon did not disbelieve, but the devils did.

Their presence under his rule does not itself establish Solomon's disbelief or his power's dependence on sorcery.

These matters belong to broader research on Solomon, jinn, and devils. Studying غ و ص should clarify the diving verb and description and their use in these verses.

5. Physical diving and broader usage

Linguistically, the clear sense of ghawṣ is going underwater.

Arabic also uses it for investigating difficult matters deeply and reaching what is hidden.

Two levels should therefore be distinguished when studying the devils' diving for Solomon.

First, the physical sense: beings dived for Solomon, going underwater and performing work.

Second, a broader possible usage: entering a subject's depths or hidden aspects to do something not easily accessible to others.

Lexical sources attest that broader usage, but its lexical availability alone does not establish a figurative reading of Solomon's divers. Nor do the verses restrict their work exclusively to pearl extraction.

That possibility does not mean their diving necessarily named hidden knowledge, scientific investigation, or a wholly nonphysical activity.

The physical meaning should not be abandoned merely because the manner of their work is unknown.

Did these beings dive like humans?

The Quran describes their activity with a verb that denotes diving in human language.

These two verses say nothing about their bodily structure, need for air, tolerance of water pressure, equipment, or time underwater.

Known human bodies and present-day diving methods should therefore not be projected wholesale onto them.

Conversely, not knowing how they acted does not justify detaching يَغُوصُونَ from its familiar meaning without evidence.

The Quran can report a real action without explaining every mechanism involved.

Similarly, passages about the wind and Solomon's rule report events without giving all physical and practical details.

The lexical meaning of diving remains clear; investigation of these beings' nature and abilities must respect the limits of what is known.

Does diving mean retrieving hidden things?

In pearl-related use, diving may accompany bringing something up from the depths.

In the knowledge metaphor, deep investigation may likewise yield a truth or meaning.

The act and its result are not identical.

Someone can enter water without bringing anything out.

The root must therefore not be reduced to “extracting” or “discovering.”

Quranic يَغُوصُونَ likewise expresses diving itself.

What they did or retrieved requires another contextual indication.

This distinction prevents an interpretation of a particular instance from replacing the lexical meaning.

6. Comparison with related roots

غ و ص (Ghawṣ) and غ ر ق (Gharaq)

Gharaq in Arabic and the Quran concerns being submerged and caught in water, often with loss of the ability to escape and with destruction.

Ghawṣ also involves going underwater, but does not necessarily mean being trapped or drowning.

A diver may enter voluntarily for a specific purpose and return after completing the task.

Someone drowning, by contrast, may lose control and the ability to emerge.

Yūnus 10:90 mentions drowning in Pharaoh's story.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82 uses yaghūṣūn for work performed for Solomon.

Their diving must not be translated as their drowning or destruction.

غ و ص (Ghawṣ) and غ و ر (Ghawr)

Ghawr, when describing water, concerns its sinking into the ground and lying deep below.

Al-Mulk 67:30 uses غَوْرًا for water that may sink into the ground and become inaccessible.

Ghawṣ concerns the being or person who enters water and goes beneath its surface.

The subject of movement differs.

With ghawr, water itself may descend.

With ghawṣ, the diver descends into water.

Translating both simply as “going deep” would miss this clear difference in what moves and where.

غ و ص (Ghawṣ) and خ و ض (Khawḍ)

Khawḍ concerns entering and moving through water; figuratively, entering a conversation or subject.

Its Quranic uses mostly concern engaging in improper or idle talk.

For example, Al-Anʿām 6:68 mentions people engaging improperly in discussion of divine signs.

Physical ghawṣ emphasizes going beneath water.

Not every entry into water is diving.

Entering a discussion and investigating its depths may be close figuratively, but the images are not identical.

The negative force of some khawḍ usages must not be transferred to غ و ص.

غ و ص (Ghawṣ), خ ر ج (Kharaja), and extraction

Khurūj concerns coming out; istikhrāj concerns bringing something out.

Ghawṣ describes entering water and descending from its surface.

A diver, for example, may enter the sea and bring out a shell.

Diving and extraction are two connected actions in that event.

Diving names the entry into depth; extraction is a further action that may follow.

Al-Naḥl 16:14 mentions extracting ornaments from the sea.

This may relate to divers' work, but the verb “extract” does not specify the method of obtaining the ornament.

Not every extraction implies diving, and not every dive guarantees extraction.

غ و ص (Ghawṣ) and غ و ث (Ghawth)

غ و ث is a separate root concerning seeking help and receiving aid in distress.

Ghawṣ and ghawth share their first two consonants but differ in the last consonant and in meaning.

Ghawṣ denotes diving and descending into water.

Ghawth concerns asking for help or providing aid.

A diver might rescue someone trapped in water, combining diving with assistance.

That possible connection does not make the roots identical.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82 describes diving for Solomon, not asking him for help.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Diving” is a clear, natural equivalent for physical غَوْص.

“Going underwater” explains the direct meaning; “diving” is shorter and more suitable when the recognized activity is intended.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82

يَغُوصُونَ لَهُ

Suggested translation:

“They dived for him.”

If explanation is needed:

“They went underwater and dived for Solomon.”

The basic translation need not add “retrieved pearls,” since the Arabic does not name what was extracted.

Nor can “investigated hidden sciences” replace the direct meaning without independent evidence.

Ṣād 38:37

وَغَوَّاصٍ

Suggested translation:

“And a diver.”

In the complete phrase:

“And the devils, every builder and diver.”

This agrees with the verbal form in Al-Anbiyāʾ and the construction كُلَّ بَنَّاءٍ وَغَوَّاصٍ.

غَوَّاص should not become “someone drowning” or be restricted to “someone who has extracted pearls.”

It names or describes someone who dives.

Distinguishing the two forms in word boxes

For يَغُوصُونَ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82:

Brief root meaning: “Entering water and descending beneath its surface.”

Meaning in this verse: “They dived for Solomon.” The referent is supplied by the preceding phrase concerning the devils.

For وَغَوَّاصٍ in Ṣād 38:37:

Brief root meaning: “Diving and activity underwater.”

Meaning in this verse: “And a diver,” among those working under Solomon.

وَغَوَّاصٍ should retain its attached conjunction in the word box, while the conjunction's meaning remains distinct from the root's. These are editorial proposals; installing the study does not rewrite existing verse boxes.

Outside the Quran, غَائِص means a diver, غَوْصَة a single dive, and مَغَاص a diving place.

For the knowledge metaphor, “deep investigation” or “entering a difficult subject in depth” is suitable only when context supports it.

8. Conclusion

In early Arabic, غ و ص concerns entering water and descending beneath its surface.

غَوْص names the act; غَائِص and غَوَّاص describe those who dive.

The verse attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī illustrates pearl diving as a familiar activity in Arabic literary tradition. Pearl extraction is nevertheless a possible purpose, not the root's necessary meaning.

Arabic also uses the family for investigating difficult subjects deeply. The metaphor draws on entering depth and reaching what is hidden, but must not replace the Quranic physical sense without contextual support.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82, devils dive for Solomon and perform other work.

In Ṣād 38:37, divers appear alongside builders among those under his rule.

Both concern a shared subject, but their morphology differs: one is a plural imperfect verb and the other names or describes a diving role.

Other Quranic passages associate work under Solomon with divine permission and describe jinn and devils building things and performing various tasks.

غ و ص itself does not mean building, obedience, magic, or knowing the unseen. Where relevant, those concepts come from other words and verses.

The final definition is:

غ و ص: entering water and descending beneath its surface; in extended usage, investigating a difficult or hidden matter in depth to examine or obtain something not readily accessible from the surface.

Physically, diving is the descent itself, not necessarily retrieving pearls, finding an object, or achieving a specified result.

Figurative deep investigation uses the same image but requires linguistic evidence and a suitable context.

In the two Solomon passages, يَغُوصُونَ expresses diving, and غَوَّاص identifies its performer. How the beings dived and what they obtained are not specified by these words themselves.

The clear meaning of diving should therefore be preserved without filling unknown mechanisms with unsupported tales or invented scientific explanations.
