The Qur’anic root و د ق (wadq)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

الْوَدْقَ
(al-wadqa)
Rain; definite accusative noun, two occurrences

وَدَقَ، سَحَابَةٌ وَادِقَةٌ
(wadaqa; saḥābatun wādiqah)
It rained; a raining cloud; non-Qur’anic forms

Rain and water drops falling from clouds: from observing cloud movement to rainfall that waters the earth

1. The root in early Arabic

The triliteral Arabic root و د ق has several semantic branches. The one clearly represented in the Qur’an concerns rainfall.

وَدْق names rain, whether light and gentle or heavy and abundant. وَدَقَ الْمَطَرُ means that rain fell or its drops descended; وَدَقَتِ السَّمَاءُ means that the sky rained.

Classical Arabic explains الوَدْقُ through المَطَرُ and القَطْرُ: rain itself and its falling drops. سَحَابَةٌ وَادِقَةٌ is a cloud from which rain falls.

Rainfall intensity is not a fixed component of wadq’s meaning. It can include gentle or heavy rain. If a verse intends abundant rain, a downpour, or light rainfall, its amount and character must be established from context or a separate description.

Evidence from early Arabic poetry

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿĀmir ibn Juwayn al-Ṭāʾī reads:

فَلَا مُزْنَةٌ وَدَقَتْ وَدْقَهَا

Fa-lā muznatun wadaqat wadqahā

وَلَا أَرْضَ أَبْقَلَ إِبْقَالَهَا

Wa-lā arḍa abqala ibqālahā.

“No cloud has rained as that cloud did, nor has any land produced vegetation like that land.”

مُزْنَة denotes a rain cloud, and وَدَقَتْ is the verb for raining. وَدْقَهَا refers comparatively to the rainfall of the particular cloud being described, rather than denying that clouds rain. The verse praises abundant rain and growth; it does not describe drought or barren ground.

The usage directly connects و د ق with rainfall. The verb and noun belong to one family: a cloud rains, and what falls is called wadq.

The verse is recorded in early lexical and grammatical works and attributed to ʿĀmir ibn Juwayn al-Ṭāʾī, though other attributions and variants of the second half are reported. The reading أَرْضَ, with fatḥah, is retained here as recorded in grammatical discussions. The verse is valuable evidence for older usage, but its date of composition cannot be established conclusively.

Another witness is attributed to Zayd al-Khayl al-Ṭāʾī, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods:

ضَرَبْنَ بِغَمْرَةٍ فَخَرَجْنَ مِنْهَا

Ḍarabna bi-ghamratin fa-kharajna minhā

خُرُوجَ الْوَدْقِ مِنْ خَلَلِ السَّحَابِ

Khurūja al-wadqi min khalali al-saḥābi.

“They plunged into a dense mass and emerged from it, as rain emerges through the gaps in clouds.”

In this comparison, wadq is the rain emerging from the clouds. مِنْ خَلَلِ السَّحَابِ also resembles the Qur’anic مِنْ خِلَالِهِ in its imagery.

Since Zayd al-Khayl lived before and after Islam’s emergence, and this verse’s date is not independently established, it should not be presented without qualification as conclusively pre-Qur’anic evidence. It illustrates recognized Arabic usage and the image of rain emerging through clouds.

Other branches of the root

The Arabic family و د ق is not limited to rain.

وَدَقَ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ is recorded for approaching something. In hunting, وَدَقَ الصَّيْدُ describes game approaching a person.

وَدَقَ الْعَيْرُ إِلَى الْمَاءِ concerns an animal approaching water. The expression also occurs proverbially in Arabic.

وَدِيقَة is recorded for intense midday heat and scorching sunshine.

وِدَاق concerns mating desire in certain animals, particularly a female seeking a mate.

Lexical usages also include وَدَقَ بِهِ for becoming familiar or at ease with someone or something, and وَادِق for a sword’s sharpness and cutting ability.

These branches show why every form listed under و د ق cannot be defined by a single statement about rain.

Some lexicographers propose a connection between approaching, reaching, and raining. Rain may be pictured as moving from a cloud toward the earth and reaching it. That image alone, however, does not establish that the historical meaning “rain” developed from “approaching.”

Nor should wadīqah, intense heat, be explained solely through the image of a raindrop.

The recorded branches must be preserved in studying the root, while Qur’anic wadq clearly means rain.

2. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

The root occurs twice in two Qur’anic verses, both times in the same nominal form:

  • الْوَدْقَ in Al-Nūr 24:43.
  • الْوَدْقَ in Al-Rūm 30:48.

Both occurrences are definite and accusative. Each follows فَتَرَى as the object of the verb of seeing.

Arabic also recognizes the verbs وَدَقَ, يَدِقُ, and أَوْدَقَ, the adjective وَادِق, and the noun وَدِيقَة; these forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

This restricted Qur’anic usage presents a specific image: rain visibly emerging through clouds.

Both verses contain the same expression:

فَتَرَى الْوَدْقَ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ

Fa-tarā al-wadqa yakhruju min khilālihi.

“Then you see the rain emerging through it.”

الْوَدْقَ names the rain.

يَخْرُجُ expresses its emergence, and مِنْ خِلَالِهِ identifies where it appears and comes out, through the cloud.

Grammatically, يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ can function as a circumstantial clause describing الْوَدْقَ: the rain is seen while emerging through the cloud.

Rain itself, its movement, and the location of its emergence should be distinguished. Different words in the sentence supply these meanings.

3. Al-Nūr 24:43: rain emerging from piled clouds

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يُزْجِي سَحَابًا ثُمَّ يُؤَلِّفُ بَيْنَهُ ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ رُكَامًا فَتَرَى الْوَدْقَ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ وَيُنَزِّلُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مِنْ جِبَالٍ فِيهَا مِنْ بَرَدٍ فَيُصِيبُ بِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَصْرِفُهُ عَنْ مَنْ يَشَاءُ يَكَادُ سَنَا بَرْقِهِ يَذْهَبُ بِالْأَبْصَارِ

A-lam tara anna Allāha yuzjī saḥāban thumma yuʾallifu baynahu thumma yajʿaluhu rukāman fa-tarā al-wadqa yakhruju min khilālihi wa-yunazzilu mina al-samāʾi min jibālin fīhā min baradin fa-yuṣību bihi man yashāʾu wa-yaṣrifuhu ʿan man yashāʾu yakādu sanā barqihi yadhhabu bi-al-abṣār.

“Have you not seen that The God drives a cloud, then joins its parts, then makes it a pile, so that you see rain emerging through it? He sends down hail from the sky, from mountains within it, striking with it whom He wills and turning it away from whom He wills. The gleam of its lightning almost takes away sight.”

Al-Nūr 24:43

The verse describes a group of phenomena associated with clouds and precipitation.

First comes يُزْجِي سَحَابًا: He sets a cloud in motion or drives it along.

Next, يُؤَلِّفُ بَيْنَهُ concerns joining the cloud’s parts.

Then comes يَجْعَلُهُ رُكَامًا. رُكَام concerns piling up, with parts accumulated upon one another.

Rain is finally seen emerging through these piled clouds.

Each verb contributes its own meaning. Driving the cloud, joining it, piling it up, and rain emerging are four distinct concepts.

Wadq names the rain appearing in this process.

Rendering it as a dense cloud or the process of cloud formation would conflate the word’s meaning with the verse’s preceding descriptions.

Does wadq mean raindrops?

Arabic lexical sources relate wadq both to rain and, in explaining rainfall, to its falling drops.

When rain is visible through a cloud, what people observe is water drops falling.

Rain as a phenomenon must nevertheless be distinguished from a single drop as one unit of it.

In these verses, wadq is a generic noun for rain. “Rain” is consequently more natural than “one raindrop” or “a particular drop.”

To explain the image, we may say that rain falls from clouds in drops. This does not establish that wadq originally meant only a single small drop.

Rain, hail, and lightning

The next part of the verse mentions بَرَد: hail.

Wadq and barad are distinct terms here. The former denotes rain, the latter hail.

Hail is also a kind of precipitation, but it receives its own name in this context.

The verse ends with the gleam of lightning.

Clouds, rain, hail, and lightning occurring in one verse indicate that they can be associated within a set of atmospheric conditions.

This association does not mean that every instance of wadq must involve hail or lightning.

Nor does wadq itself specify rainfall intensity, drop size, or the complete mechanism of formation. The verse describes observable phenomena; scientific details beyond what it states should not be turned into definitive textual claims.

4. Al-Rūm 30:48: wind, cloud, and joy after rain

اللَّهُ الَّذِي يُرْسِلُ الرِّيَاحَ فَتُثِيرُ سَحَابًا فَيَبْسُطُهُ فِي السَّمَاءِ كَيْفَ يَشَاءُ وَيَجْعَلُهُ كِسَفًا فَتَرَى الْوَدْقَ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ فَإِذَا أَصَابَ بِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ إِذَا هُمْ يَسْتَبْشِرُونَ

Allāhu alladhī yursilu al-riyāḥa fa-tuthīru saḥāban fa-yabsuṭuhu fī al-samāʾi kayfa yashāʾu wa-yajʿaluhu kisafan fa-tarā al-wadqa yakhruju min khilālihi fa-idhā aṣāba bihi man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi idhā hum yastabshirūn.

“The God is the One who sends the winds, which stir up a cloud. He spreads it in the sky as He wills and makes it into portions, so that you see rain emerging through it. When He causes it to reach whom He wills among His servants, they suddenly rejoice.”

Al-Rūm 30:48

The same expression, فَتَرَى الْوَدْقَ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ, occurs here, but the surrounding image differs in some respects from Al-Nūr.

The sending of winds comes first. They stir up a cloud, which is then spread across the sky.

Next comes وَيَجْعَلُهُ كِسَفًا.

كِسَف refers to portions or pieces: the cloud is presented in masses or sections.

In Al-Nūr 24:43, رُكَامًا emphasizes clouds piling up; Al-Rūm 30:48 speaks of كِسَفًا.

This does not establish two entirely separate kinds of rain. Both verses use wadq for rain emerging through cloud.

The difference concerns the cloud’s form and condition, rather than the meaning of و د ق.

Rain’s arrival and people’s joy

The verse continues:

فَإِذَا أَصَابَ بِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ إِذَا هُمْ يَسْتَبْشِرُونَ

Fa-idhā aṣāba bihi man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi idhā hum yastabshirūn.

When rain reaches some of God’s servants, they rejoice.

Their joy results from the rain’s arrival; it is not part of wadq’s lexical meaning.

The following verse, Al-Rūm 30:49, explains that they had previously despaired. Al-Rūm 30:50 then discusses the effects of divine mercy and the earth’s revival after its death.

Reading these verses together clarifies rain’s importance for people.

Where the land is thirsty and lives depend on water, rainfall can renew hope. Farmers hope for crops, herders for green pasture, and communities for water and food.

These consequences are not the lexical meaning of wadq. The word denotes rain; the context establishes its connection with human need, joy, and the earth’s revival.

People’s joy in this verse does not mean that all rain under every condition is necessarily beneficial.

Rain may water dry ground, while in other circumstances it may cause floods and damage. Its benefits and harms also depend on intensity, quantity, timing, location, and the conditions of the land and its inhabitants.

That variation does not change wadq’s lexical meaning.

5. Differences from related roots

و د ق and م ط ر

مَطَر is a general Arabic name for rain and overlaps semantically with wadq.

Both can refer to water falling from the sky.

Their Qur’anic contexts nevertheless show a noticeable difference.

Both instances of wadq depict rain emerging through clouds.

Maṭar and related verbs occur in several verses about punishment raining upon communities, including Al-Aʿrāf 7:84 and Al-Naml 27:58.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:102, however, maṭar occurs in discussing rainy conditions during travel and military danger, without the rain itself meaning punishment.

Negative contexts in some verses therefore do not make the Arabic root inherently mean punitive rain.

Likewise, wadq’s two natural scenes do not establish a rule that wadq always means merciful rain and maṭar always punitive rain.

Both words can denote actual rain. Context determines the rainfall’s significance and consequences.

و د ق and غ ي ث

غَيْث also names rain, particularly in usages associated with the land’s need, watering, and relief after drought.

Al-Shūrā 42:28 describes ghayth being sent after people have despaired.

This clearly relates to the joy following wadq in Al-Rūm 30:48, especially given the earlier despair described in Al-Rūm 30:49.

Ghayth and wadq are nevertheless not entirely interchangeable in every respect.

Ghayth’s association with watering and relief is prominent in familiar usages; wadq foregrounds rain and its drops falling from clouds.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute boundary.

Wadq can also bring relief, and ghayth is also actual rain.

For further detail, see Forqan’s study of the root غ ي ث.

و د ق and و ب ل

وَابِل denotes heavy, intense rain in Arabic.

Al-Baqarah 2:264 and Al-Baqarah 2:265 use the word in two comparisons concerning spending.

In Al-Baqarah 2:265, wābil is contrasted with ṭall: heavy rain against lighter precipitation.

Wadq is not restricted in that way and can denote little or abundant rain.

A verse using only wadq should not therefore be translated as “a heavy downpour” without further evidence.

Wābil is a kind of rain, but not every rain is wābil.

Forqan’s study of the root و ب ل examines this distinction.

و د ق and ص و ب

صَيِّب in Al-Baqarah 2:19 refers to rain pouring from the sky.

It belongs to ص و ب, whose recognized branches include descending, reaching, and striking.

The scene in Al-Baqarah 2:19 accompanies the rain with darkness, thunder, and lightning.

Wadq also concerns rain, but does not itself mean descending toward a target or striking it.

In Al-Rūm 30:48, أَصَابَ بِهِ describes rain reaching the servants. This verb belongs to ص و ب; its meaning should not be transferred to و د ق.

Wadq thus names the rain, while aṣāba expresses its reaching or striking.

Forqan’s study of the root ص و ب explores this distinction more fully.

و د ق and س ح ب

سَحَاب names cloud and belongs to the Arabic family س ح ب.

Both Al-Nūr 24:43 and Al-Rūm 30:48 distinguish cloud from rain.

Cloud moves, spreads, or piles up, and rain emerges through it.

Saḥāb names the cloud; wadq names the rain.

Cloud characteristics cannot all be added to wadq’s meaning. For example, rukām in Al-Nūr 24:43 describes the cloud’s condition, rather than defining rain.

For cloud and its distinction from pulling or movement, see Forqan’s study of the root س ح ب.

و د ق and ب ر د

بَرَد in Al-Nūr 24:43 denotes hail: frozen pellets falling from the sky under particular atmospheric conditions.

The same verse uses wadq for rain and barad for hail.

Naming them separately distinguishes the two forms of precipitation within the description.

Both remain connected with weather and water descending from the sky.

Their distinction does not mean they are naturally unrelated; nor should hail be presented as wadq’s principal meaning.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the Qur’anic الْوَدْقَ, the principal natural equivalent is “rain.”

In Al-Nūr 24:43:

فَتَرَى الْوَدْقَ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ

Fa-tarā al-wadqa yakhruju min khilālihi.

“Then you see the rain emerging through it.”

The same rendering suits Al-Rūm 30:48 because the Arabic expression is identical.

Where explanatory expansion is needed, one may say:

“Rain falls in drops through the clouds.”

That explains the image rather than translating wadq alone word for word.

Other lexical forms absent from the Qur’an may be rendered as follows:

وَدَقَ الْمَطَرُ: “rain fell” or “the rain came down.”

وَدَقَتِ السَّمَاءُ: “the sky rained.”

سَحَابَةٌ وَادِقَةٌ: “a rain-bearing cloud” or “a raining cloud.”

وَدَقَ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ: “he approached something.”

وَدِيقَة: “intense midday heat.”

These equivalents clarify the Arabic family and should not be imposed on the verses in Al-Nūr and Al-Rūm.

Qur’anic wadq should not be restricted to “downpour,” “drizzle,” “water of mercy,” “very fine drops,” or “heavy rain.” Each adds a characteristic not supplied by the word itself.

“Rain” is the most accurate general equivalent for the noun’s two Qur’anic occurrences.

Conclusion

و د ق has several branches in Arabic, but only its noun wadq occurs in the Qur’an.

Qur’anic wadq means rain: water falling from cloud. It can include gentle or heavy rainfall and does not itself determine the rain’s quantity, intensity, or result.

In Al-Nūr 24:43, wadq appears within a description of clouds moving, joining, and piling up. Hail and lightning follow.

Al-Rūm 30:48 repeats the same expression of wadq emerging through cloud. Here the winds, the cloud’s spreading, and people’s joy when rain arrives are prominent.

The word’s meaning remains constant while each context illuminates a different aspect of rainfall.

Wadq overlaps with maṭar and ghayth in naming rain. It differs from wābil concerning intensity and from saḥāb, which names cloud. These distinctions should not be made into absolute boundaries without exceptions.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its Qur’anic branch, و د ق concerns raining and water falling from cloud. Wadq names rain, whether light and gentle or heavy and abundant. وَدَقَتِ السَّمَاءُ describes the sky raining; سَحَابَةٌ وَادِقَةٌ describes a raining cloud.

Other Arabic branches concern approaching, intense midday heat, and certain animal conditions. Their historical relationship has not been conclusively established; an invented single origin should not be assigned to them.

In both Qur’anic verses, الْوَدْقَ means “rain.” Cloud movement, rain emerging and reaching its destination, and its consequences are supplied by other words and the surrounding context.
