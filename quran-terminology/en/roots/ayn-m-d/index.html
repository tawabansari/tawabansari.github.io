Core meanings: pillars, supports, and holding something upright; intending and deliberately performing an action

The Arabic root ʿ M D has two established semantic branches: structural support, and conscious intention.

In the first branch, ʿamad, ʿimād, and ʿamūd denote something on which a structure rests and by which it remains upright, such as a tent pole or a building column.

In the second, ʿamada and taʿammada concern intending or deliberately undertaking something: the person wills the action rather than doing it accidentally. Intending the act does not, by itself, establish awareness of every consequence or ruling concerning it.

The two branches can be compared through the idea of being upright or directed toward something: a pillar holds a structure up, while a person's will is directed toward a particular act. This explanatory connection alone does not establish that one meaning historically developed from the other.

Four forms of this root occur in the Quran: عَمَدٍ, الْعِمَادِ, مُتَعَمِّدًا, and تَعَمَّدَتْ. They occur in seven verses and represent both branches.

1. The root in early Arabic

Pillars and supports in pre-Islamic Arab life

A clear use of this root in pre-Islamic Arabic is the naming of tent poles and building supports.

In tent life, a pole was more than a long piece of wood: it supported the roof or covering. Poles, ropes, and stakes driven into the ground together kept the tent standing.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Afwah al-Awdī says:

وَالْبَيْتُ لَا يُبْتَنَى إِلَّا لَهُ عَمَدٌ
وَلَا عِمَادَ إِذَا لَمْ تُرْسَ أَوْتَادُ

wa-al-baytu lā yubtanā illā lahu ʿamadun / wa-lā ʿimāda idhā lam tursa awtādu

“A house or tent cannot stand without pillars; nor can its support stand firm unless its stakes are secured.”

Here ʿamad and ʿimād refer to the actual supports of a structure, while awtād are the stakes that help secure it.

The poet uses this image to explain society's need for supporting members and cooperation. The words retain their concrete meaning: pillars supporting a building or tent.

Another witness occurs in a poem attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī:

وَخَيِّسِ الْجِنَّ إِنِّي قَدْ أَذِنْتُ لَهُمْ
يَبْنُونَ تَدْمُرَ بِالصُّفَّاحِ وَالْعَمَدِ

wa-khayyisi al-jinna innī qad adhintu lahum / yabnūna tadmura bi-al-ṣuffāḥi wa-al-ʿamad

“Subdue the jinn: I have permitted them to build Tadmur with broad stones and pillars.”

This verse offers a literary, legendary picture of the building of Tadmur. Its lexical value lies in using al-ʿamad alongside building stones, rather than in the historical accuracy of the story.

Here al-ʿamad clearly refers to building columns.

These poetic witnesses, attributed to the pre-Islamic period, connect the material branch of ʿ M D with pillars, upright structures, and architectural supports. They also illustrate its figurative use for the supporting members of society.

Intending and directing the will

Classical Arabic lexicography records ʿamada ilā al-shayʾ as intending or turning toward something.

In the same family, taʿammada means to act intentionally, while iʿtamada can mean to lean on something or make something one's aim.

The important distinction in this branch is between intentional and mistaken action.

Someone who travels toward a chosen destination has deliberately selected a course. Similarly, someone who acts intentionally intends the action; its moral value and outcome require separate consideration.

Classical lexical accounts clearly attest this branch, but assigning every form to pre-Islamic usage requires independent evidence from that period. The Quranic uses show that intention and deliberate action were established senses in the language of revelation.

2. ʿAmad: pillars and the support of the heavens

Al-Raʿd 13:2

اللَّهُ الَّذِي رَفَعَ السَّمَاوَاتِ بِغَيْرِ عَمَدٍ تَرَوْنَهَا ۖ ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ وَسَخَّرَ الشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ ۖ كُلٌّ يَجْرِي لِأَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى ۚ يُدَبِّرُ الْأَمْرَ يُفَصِّلُ الْآيَاتِ لَعَلَّكُمْ بِلِقَاءِ رَبِّكُمْ تُوقِنُونَ

Allāhu alladhī rafaʿa al-samāwāti bi-ghayri ʿamadin tarawnahā thumma istawā ʿalā al-ʿarshi wa-sakhkhara al-shamsa wa-al-qamara kullun yajrī li-ajalin musamman yudabbiru al-amra yufaṣṣilu al-āyāti laʿallakum bi-liqāʾi rabbikum tūqinūn

Al-Raʿd 13:2

“The God is the One who raised the heavens without pillars that you see; then established Himself upon the Throne and subjected the sun and moon. Each runs until an appointed term. He directs the affair and explains the signs, so that you may be certain of meeting your Lord.”

Al-Raʿd 13:2

Here ʿamad has the plural meaning of pillars or supports.

The image begins with something familiar from ordinary buildings: a roof or elevated structure generally needs supports. Yet people observe the heavens standing without such visible columns.

The grammatical role of tarawnahā matters in bi-ghayri ʿamadin tarawnahā.

Two syntactic readings merit consideration:

First, the heavens were raised without pillars, and people see them in that condition; the pronoun in tarawnahā refers to the heavens.

Second, tarawnahā qualifies the pillars, so the phrase is read as “without pillars that you see.” This reading concerns their visibility and does not by itself conclusively establish or exclude invisible supports.

In either reading, ʿamad retains the meaning of pillars or supports.

The word does not name a particular physical force. Modern scientific explanations of the motion and stability of celestial bodies do not justify translating ʿamad directly as the name of one such force.

Luqmān 31:10

خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ بِغَيْرِ عَمَدٍ تَرَوْنَهَا ۖ وَأَلْقَىٰ فِي الْأَرْضِ رَوَاسِيَ أَنْ تَمِيدَ بِكُمْ وَبَثَّ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ دَابَّةٍ ۚ وَأَنْزَلْنَا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَنْبَتْنَا فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ زَوْجٍ كَرِيمٍ

khalaqa al-samāwāti bi-ghayri ʿamadin tarawnahā wa-alqā fī al-arḍi rawāsiya an tamīda bikum wa-baththa fīhā min kulli dābbatin wa-anzalnā mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-anbatnā fīhā min kulli zawjin karīm

Luqmān 31:10

“He created the heavens without pillars that you see, placed firm mountains in the earth lest it sway with you, and scattered every kind of creature upon it. We sent down water from the sky and caused every noble kind to grow in it.”

Luqmān 31:10

The same phrase as in Al-Raʿd recurs here:

بِغَيْرِ عَمَدٍ تَرَوْنَهَا

bi-ghayri ʿamadin tarawnahā

Al-Raʿd 13:2; Luqmān 31:10

In Luqmān, the surrounding passage concerns the creation of the heavens, mountains established in the earth, creatures, water, and plant growth.

ʿAmad still denotes pillars or supports. Mentioning mountains nearby does not change the root's meaning to “stabilizing the earth.”

Rawāsiya belongs to a different lexical family and conveys firmness and stability in its own context.

A connection with stability does not make the two words synonymous.

In these two verses, ʿamad supplies the image of pillars, while the creation context expresses divine greatness and governance.

3. Al-ʿimād: pillars as signs of strength and stability

Al-Fajr 89:7

إِرَمَ ذَاتِ الْعِمَادِ

irama dhāti al-ʿimād

Al-Fajr 89:7

“Iram, possessing pillars.”

Al-Fajr 89:7

This expression follows the mention of ʿĀd in Al-Fajr 89:6.

Al-ʿimād belongs to the same family as ʿamad and ʿamūd, here in the phrase dhāti al-ʿimād.

Dhāt denotes possession in this construction, giving the direct sense “possessing ʿimād.”

The main question is what ʿimād refers to here.

In Arabic, ʿimād can denote building columns or tent poles. The phrase has therefore been interpreted through tents with poles or buildings with columns. Early interpretations also include the people's tall stature; identifying the referent is an interpretive question rather than the word's single lexical definition.

Tall, imposing columns could also suggest greatness and social standing in early language.

The phrase dhāti al-ʿimād alone does not settle whether tents or tall buildings are meant, nor does it establish the location or architecture of a particular city.

A direct translation can retain the pillar image, with the historical identification considered separately.

“Possessing tall pillars” adds information beyond what al-ʿimād alone explicitly states. Such height may be inferred from context or a historical interpretation, but no independent adjective meaning “tall” occurs in the phrase.

4. ʿAmad mumaddadah: extended pillars

Al-Humazah 104:9

فِي عَمَدٍ مُمَدَّدَةٍ

fī ʿamadin mumaddadah

Al-Humazah 104:9

“Amid extended pillars.”

Al-Humazah 104:9

This verse continues the description of the fire that, in Al-Humazah 104:8, is closed over those undergoing punishment.

ʿAmad means pillars, while mumaddadah belongs to M D D and adds the sense of extension or being stretched out.

Length and extension should therefore not be made fixed components of ʿ M D itself.

The phrase fī ʿamadin mumaddadah can evoke extended pillars within an enclosed structure.

Their exact nature and connection with the enclosing fire have received different explanations: implements of closure, confinement, or parts of the punishment's structure. The wording alone does not fully specify their material, connections, or the manner of that punishment.

The clear lexical point is that ʿamad here belongs to the material branch, not intention or deliberate action.

The Quranic readings ʿamad and ʿumud are both attested. This variation does not alter the basic meaning of pillars.

5. Taʿammada: deliberate intention contrasted with error

The root's second Quranic branch concerns human conduct and responsibility.

Its focus is deliberately performing an act, rather than supporting a building.

The Quran particularly uses this sense in contrast with error.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:5: error and deliberate intention

ادْعُوهُمْ لِآبَائِهِمْ هُوَ أَقْسَطُ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ ۚ فَإِنْ لَمْ تَعْلَمُوا آبَاءَهُمْ فَإِخْوَانُكُمْ فِي الدِّينِ وَمَوَالِيكُمْ ۚ وَلَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ فِيمَا أَخْطَأْتُمْ بِهِ وَلَٰكِنْ مَا تَعَمَّدَتْ قُلُوبُكُمْ ۚ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا

udʿūhum li-ābāʾihim huwa aqsaṭu ʿinda Allāhi fa-in lam taʿlamū ābāʾahum fa-ikhwānukum fī al-dīni wa-mawālīkum wa-laysa ʿalaykum junāḥun fīmā akhṭaʾtum bihi wa-lākin mā taʿammadat qulūbukum wa-kāna Allāhu ghafūran raḥīmā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:5

“Call them by their fathers; that is more equitable before The God. If you do not know their fathers, they are your brothers in religion and your associates. There is no blame upon you for what you do by mistake, but for what your hearts deliberately intend. The God is forgiving, merciful.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:5

Two expressions are contrasted here:

أَخْطَأْتُمْ and تَعَمَّدَتْ قُلُوبُكُمْ.

The first refers to error: someone may act incorrectly without intending the wrongful aspect of the act.

Taʿammadat, by contrast, expresses deliberate intention.

The Quran attributes this intention to qulūbukum, “your hearts.”

This construction directs attention to inward intention. Assessment concerns more than the act's outward form: whether the person acted deliberately or by mistake also matters.

The immediate subject is specific: attributing people to their fathers and distinguishing mistakes from deliberately false attribution.

It does not follow that every mistake in every legal setting entails no responsibility or compensation. The passage concerns blame and intention in this context, rather than all possible consequences of mistaken action.

Morphologically, taʿammadat is a Form V past verb. Qulūbukum is its subject: deliberate intention is attributed to the heart.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:93: intentional killing

وَمَنْ يَقْتُلْ مُؤْمِنًا مُتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاؤُهُ جَهَنَّمُ خَالِدًا فِيهَا وَغَضِبَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَلَعَنَهُ وَأَعَدَّ لَهُ عَذَابًا عَظِيمًا

wa-man yaqtul muʾminan mutaʿammidan fa-jazāʾuhu jahannamu khālidan fīhā wa-ghaḍiba Allāhu ʿalayhi wa-laʿanahu wa-aʿadda lahu ʿadhāban ʿaẓīmā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:93

“Whoever deliberately kills a believer, his recompense is Hell, remaining therein. The God is angry with him, has cursed him, and has prepared a great punishment for him.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:93

Mutaʿammidan is a Form V active participle describing the state of the person who commits the killing.

It indicates that the act is intentional, beyond the fact that a killing occurred.

The contrast with Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 matters: that verse discusses mistaken killing, while Al-Nisāʾ 4:93 concerns deliberate killing.

Here ʿamd concerns the intention to kill.

Intention and prior planning must be distinguished.

A person may have planned an act long before, or may decide to perform it at that moment. Intentional action does not by itself mean a longstanding, prearranged plan.

The verse's grave moral and otherworldly consequences arise from the killing of a believer and the divine judgment concerning it. The root itself does not invariably mean sin or crime.

Al-Māʾidah 5:95: deliberately killing game

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَقْتُلُوا الصَّيْدَ وَأَنْتُمْ حُرُمٌ ۚ وَمَنْ قَتَلَهُ مِنْكُمْ مُتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاءٌ مِثْلُ مَا قَتَلَ مِنَ النَّعَمِ يَحْكُمُ بِهِ ذَوَا عَدْلٍ مِنْكُمْ هَدْيًا بَالِغَ الْكَعْبَةِ أَوْ كَفَّارَةٌ طَعَامُ مَسَاكِينَ أَوْ عَدْلُ ذَٰلِكَ صِيَامًا لِيَذُوقَ وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِ ۗ عَفَا اللَّهُ عَمَّا سَلَفَ ۚ وَمَنْ عَادَ فَيَنْتَقِمُ اللَّهُ مِنْهُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ ذُو انْتِقَامٍ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā taqtulū al-ṣayda wa-antum ḥurumun wa-man qatalahu minkum mutaʿammidan fa-jazāʾun mithlu mā qatala mina al-naʿami yaḥkumu bihi dhawā ʿadlin minkum hadyan bāligha al-kaʿbati aw kaffāratun ṭaʿāmu masākīna aw ʿadlu dhālika ṣiyāman li-yadhūqa wabāla amrihi ʿafā Allāhu ʿammā salafa wa-man ʿāda fa-yantaqimu Allāhu minhu wa-Allāhu ʿazīzun dhū intiqām

Al-Māʾidah 5:95

“You who believe, do not kill game while in consecration. Whoever among you kills it deliberately owes compensation in livestock equivalent to what he killed, judged by two just persons among you, as an offering reaching the Kaʿbah; or an expiation of feeding the needy, or equivalent fasting, so that he may taste the consequence of his act. The God has pardoned what is past. Whoever repeats it, The God will exact retribution from him. The God is mighty, possessed of retribution.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:95

Mutaʿammidan has the same morphological form here as in Al-Nisāʾ.

The intended action differs: killing a believer in Al-Nisāʾ and killing game while in consecration in Al-Māʾidah.

This distinction shows that ʿ M D is not inherently restricted to killing humans, crime, or even sin.

The word specifies deliberate action. Its moral value and ruling depend on the act, the person's circumstances, and the verse's other instructions.

Lexically, mutaʿammidan means “deliberately” or “intentionally” in both verses.

Their rulings and consequences differ; sharing a derivative does not justify transferring the ruling of one act to the other.

6. Lexical distinctions and translation proposals for Forqan

ʿ M D and D ʿ M

These roots overlap in support and reinforcement but remain distinct roots.

The material branch of ʿ M D concerns pillars and holding a structure upright. D ʿ M concerns supporting something and preventing its fall or collapse.

A pillar can be an ʿimād and provide support, but this overlap does not make all uses of the two roots identical.

ʿ M D and W T D

A watad is a peg or stake secured in the ground or elsewhere to hold something in place.

An ʿimād is a pillar or support on which part of a structure rests.

In a tent, the pole holds the covering up, while stakes help secure and anchor it. The distinction appears in the poem attributed to al-Afwah al-Awdī.

Their occurrence together illustrates their different functions.

ʿ M D and Q Ṣ D

Qaṣada concerns turning toward a direction or destination. Its uses include intention, orientation, and moderation.

The volitional branch of ʿamada also concerns intention, while taʿammada emphasizes deliberate action, particularly in contrast with error.

Every instance of taʿammud involves intention, but not every use of Q Ṣ D denotes deliberately performing an act.

ʿ M D and KH Ṭ ʾ

In contexts such as Al-Aḥzāb 33:5 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, error is contrasted with intentional conduct.

ʿAmd emphasizes deliberate intention, while error in this contrast concerns a mistake and lack of intention regarding the wrongful aspect of the act.

Error does not invariably mean carelessness, nor does intention invariably imply prolonged planning. These details depend on the action and its context.

Translation and short word-box proposals

  • عَمَدٍ — Al-Raʿd 13:2 and Luqmān 31:10:
    Brief root meaning: pillars and support; deliberate intention in another branch.
    Contextual meaning: pillars or supports.
    Suggested rendering: “without pillars that you see,” with the alternative syntax explained separately.
  • الْعِمَادِ — Al-Fajr 89:7:
    Brief root meaning: pillars and support; deliberate intention in another branch.
    Contextual meaning: pillars, describing Iram.
    Suggested rendering: “possessing pillars.”
  • عَمَدٍ — Al-Humazah 104:9:
    Brief root meaning: pillars and support; deliberate intention in another branch.
    Contextual meaning: pillars qualified by mumaddadah.
    Suggested rendering: “amid extended pillars.”
  • تَعَمَّدَتْ — Al-Aḥzāb 33:5:
    Brief root meaning: pillars and support; deliberate intention.
    Contextual meaning: deliberately intended, in contrast with error.
    Suggested rendering: “what your hearts deliberately intend.”
  • مُتَعَمِّدًا — Al-Nisāʾ 4:93 and Al-Māʾidah 5:95:
    Brief root meaning: pillars and support; deliberate intention.
    Contextual meaning: performing an action intentionally.
    Suggested rendering: “deliberately” or “intentionally.”

“Deliberately” is a natural equivalent for both occurrences of mutaʿammidan. “With a prior plan,” “out of hostility,” and “with evil intent” introduce meanings that the derivative itself does not supply.

For the material uses, “pillar” is a suitable basic equivalent. Where context emphasizes the support it provides, that role can be explained in the commentary.

7. Conclusion

ʿ M D has two clear Quranic branches:

First, pillars and supports: something on which a structure rests and by which it remains standing. This includes ʿamad in Al-Raʿd 13:2, Luqmān 31:10, and Al-Humazah 104:9, and al-ʿimād in Al-Fajr 89:7.

Second, intention and deliberate action: intentionally undertaking something, contrasted with an act occurring by mistake. Taʿammadat in Al-Aḥzāb 33:5 and mutaʿammidan in Al-Nisāʾ 4:93 and Al-Māʾidah 5:95 illustrate this branch.

A semantic affinity can be envisaged between a pillar's firmness and the directed human will. This image should not be treated as a proven historical derivation of every form.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period attests ʿamad and ʿimād as pillars and supports. Intention is also clearly recorded in classical Arabic and the Quran, though dating its first emergence requires further independent evidence.

The principal distinction from related words is this: the material branch concerns a pillar on which something rests, while the volitional branch concerns an act that someone deliberately intends.

Final definition of ʿ M D:

“In the material branch, a pillar or support on which something remains standing; in the volitional branch, intending and deliberately undertaking an action. The moral value of intentional action and the precise referent of a pillar or support depend on the word's construction and context.”
