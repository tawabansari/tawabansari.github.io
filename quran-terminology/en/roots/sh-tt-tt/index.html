Distance, deviation, and exceeding bounds: from spatial remoteness in early Arabic to statements far from truth and unjust judgment in the Qur’an

SH Ṭ Ṭ has two principal semantic fields in early Arabic: distance or remoteness, and deviation beyond a proper limit. Shaṭṭati al-dāru describes a distant dwelling or an increased distance from it. In conduct, speech, and judgment, shaṭaṭ denotes excess or departure from truth, fairness, and justice.

Shaṭṭa can express distance; shaṭaṭ, excess; and ashaṭṭa, exceeding proper bounds in a demand, transaction, or judgment.

Early lexical accounts recognize distance and inclination. Inclination can involve turning away from the proper direction. These concepts are intelligibly related: deviation from a path creates distance from it. This relationship alone does not establish a single historical origin for every usage.

The root occurs three times: shaṭaṭan in Al-Kahf 18:14 and Al-Jinn 72:4, and tushṭiṭ in Ṣād 38:22.

All concern departure from proper bounds, but their objects differ. Al-Kahf concerns invoking another deity besides God; Al-Jinn, an outrageous statement about God; and Ṣād, litigants asking David not to depart from truth and justice in judgment.

The central Qur’anic sense is exceeding proper bounds or departing from truth and justice. These usages concern truth, fairness, and a limit to be observed, rather than merely physical distance.

1. The root in early Arabic

Shaṭṭa: distance and remoteness

Shaṭṭati al-dāru means that a dwelling is distant. It can describe distance from one’s homeland or a beloved person’s residence.

Shaṭṭati al-dāru tashuṭṭu shuṭūṭan is recorded, as is the imperfect tashiṭṭu.

The subject here is distance, without necessarily implying error or blameworthy behavior.

Two people may live in distant settlements. That separation is neither injustice nor a moral fault in itself.

Travel to a distant land likewise increases spatial distance without necessarily constituting excess or departure from truth.

The negative implications of the Qur’anic usages cannot therefore be extended to every derivative.

A poetic witness attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād

A lexical citation attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād reads:

شَطَّتْ مَزَارَ الْعَاشِقِينَ فَأَصْبَحَتْ
عَسِرًا عَلَيَّ طِلَابُكِ ابْنَةَ مَخْرَمِ

shaṭṭat mazāra al-ʿāshiqīna fa-aṣbaḥat / ʿasiran ʿalayya ṭilābuki ibnata makhram

Its general sense is:

“She has gone beyond the lovers’ visiting range; reaching you, daughter of Makhram, has become difficult for me.”

Shaṭṭat expresses distance in this verse.

The poet describes the beloved’s remoteness and the difficulty of reaching her. The verb itself does not morally judge her behavior.

The verse is attributed to ʿAntarah in early sources, but its wording varies. Some versions of the Muʿallaqah begin ḥallat bi-arḍi al-zāʾirīn rather than shaṭṭat mazāra al-ʿāshiqīn.

It is evidence from a transmitted version of his poetry. The reading with shaṭṭat should not be presented as the sole, certain wording of the Muʿallaqah.

The variant still illustrates distance clearly. Its lexical meaning should be distinguished from confidence that this precise word belonged to the poem’s earliest text.

From spatial distance to departure from a proper measure

Other usages extend beyond physical remoteness to departure from a proper limit.

Ashaṭṭa fī al-sawm describes excessive bargaining or demands concerning a price.

Shaṭṭa fī ṭalabihi can describe someone exceeding reasonable bounds in a demand.

The relevant distance is between a demand and its appropriate measure, rather than between geographical locations.

For example, someone might demand a grossly disproportionate price without justification in order to exploit another’s circumstances. This illustrates excess in a demand.

Not every high price or large demand is excessive. Scarcity, quality, production costs, and actual circumstances can matter. Identifying excess requires understanding the appropriate measure and circumstances.

Shaṭaṭ therefore means more than being large: it means exceeding a measure that ought to be observed in the matter concerned.

Lexical sources record this sense in speech, transactions, demands, and judgment.

Recording a usage in a later dictionary does not establish its precise pre-Islamic date. The attributed ʿAntarah verse offers an early witness with textual variation; this study has not established independent pre-Islamic evidence for every commercial or judicial usage.

The Qur’anic occurrences themselves provide clear evidence for the sense of exceeding bounds in the language of revelation.

Shaṭāṭ: distance and tall stature

Shaṭāṭ can denote distance or remoteness.

It also describes tall stature, sometimes with the suggestion of a well-proportioned figure.

A Hudhalī poetic citation associates makhīlah and shaṭāṭ with an imposing appearance and tall stature. The shade of meaning in makhīlah depends on context; it should not automatically become a wholly positive description.

Height and extension thus also belong to this word family.

A tall person’s upper body is farther from the ground, but this image does not prove the word’s historical origin.

Tall stature is not necessarily blameworthy.

The whole family therefore cannot be assigned a uniformly negative meaning such as injustice, arrogance, or misguidance.

Shaṭṭ: a riverbank or valley side

Shaṭṭ denotes the side of a river or valley.

Lexical accounts also call the two sides of a camel’s hump shaṭṭā al-sanām.

Both usages concern the side of something.

A riverbank lies beside the water’s bed; the hump’s sides are distinguished from its middle.

Some explanations relate this to distance from the center, others to inclination toward a side.

Being at an edge is not inherently wrong or excessive. A riverbank is a natural location without moral implications.

Shaṭṭ belongs to SH Ṭ Ṭ, whereas shāṭiʾ is studied under SH Ṭ ʾ.

Their overlap in the sense of an edge does not establish a common history for all their derivatives.

Historical assessment

Lexical evidence and early poetry associate the family with distance, edges, and departure from proper bounds.

The very broad definition “moving away from something” cannot adequately distinguish physical distance, tall stature, a riverbank, and judicial injustice.

Two principal dimensions should be retained:

First, distance or extension in a spatial sense.

Second, deviation and excess in speech, demands, conduct, or judgment.

The Qur’anic occurrences emphasize the second.

2. Important forms and derivatives

SH Ṭ Ṭ is a doubled root: its last two radicals are identical.

They merge under a shaddah in forms such as shaṭṭa, but appear separately in forms such as tushṭiṭ.

Important forms include:

  • شَطَّ / يَشُطُّ and يَشِطُّ: shaṭṭa / yashuṭṭu and yashiṭṭu; to be distant or exceed bounds, depending on context.
  • شَطّ: shaṭṭ; distance, or a riverbank or valley side.
  • شُطُوط: shuṭūṭ; remoteness; also the plural of shaṭṭ, banks.
  • شَطَط: shaṭaṭ; excess or departure from truth in speech, demands, and judgment.
  • شَطَاط: shaṭāṭ; distance, or tall and proportionate stature.
  • شِطَّة: shiṭṭah; a long distance or journey.
  • شَاطّ: shāṭṭ; remote, or unfair in a judicial context.
  • شَاطَّة: shāṭṭah; a tall or well-proportioned woman in certain usages.
  • أَشَطَّ / يُشِطُّ: ashaṭṭa / yushiṭṭu; to exceed bounds, demand excessively, or judge unjustly.
  • إِشْطَاط: ishṭāṭ; going far or exceeding bounds.
  • اشْتَطَّ: ishtaṭṭa; to exceed bounds in demands, transactions, or judgment.
  • اشْتِطَاط: ishṭiṭāṭ; excess or overstepping.
  • شَطَّطَ: shaṭṭaṭa; to act with pronounced excess.
  • تَشْطِيط: tashṭīṭ; excess or carrying something beyond bounds.
  • مُشْتَطّ: mushtaṭṭ; excessive in demands or behavior, according to context.

These are recorded Arabic forms, but not all occur in the Qur’an, and independent pre-Islamic evidence has not been established here for every form.

The Qur’an contains only two forms:

Shaṭaṭan, twice: Al-Kahf 18:14 and Al-Jinn 72:4.

Tushṭiṭ, once: Ṣād 38:22.

The first concerns speech far from truth; the second, departure from truth in judgment.

This distinction comes from syntax and context, not unrelated meanings in each verse.

3. Qur’anic usages

Al-Kahf 18:14: speech far from truth

Concerning the young people who sought refuge in the cave, the Qur’an says:

وَرَبَطْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ إِذْ قَامُوا فَقَالُوا رَبُّنَا رَبُّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ لَنْ نَدْعُوَ مِنْ دُونِهِ إِلَٰهًا لَقَدْ قُلْنَا إِذًا شَطَطًا

wa-rabaṭnā ʿalā qulūbihim idh qāmū fa-qālū rabbunā rabbu al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi lan nadʿuwa min dūnihi ilāhan la-qad qulnā idhan shaṭaṭā

Al-Kahf 18:14

“We strengthened their hearts when they stood and said: Our Lord is the Lord of the heavens and earth. We will never invoke any deity besides Him; otherwise, we would indeed have spoken far from truth.”

Shaṭaṭan describes speech the believing youths reject as wrong.

They first declare that their Lord is the Lord of the heavens and earth.

They then refuse to invoke any deity besides Him.

The following expression:

لَقَدْ قُلْنَا إِذًا شَطَطًا

la-qad qulnā idhan shaṭaṭā

Al-Kahf 18:14

Indicates that invoking another deity would amount to speech far removed from truth.

Idhan, “in that case,” is important.

It links the conclusion to the hypothetical alternative. They are not admitting to having made the statement; they say that invoking another deity would constitute such speech.

Shaṭaṭ thus characterizes a rejected hypothetical utterance.

In Al-Kahf 18:15, they say of their people:

هَٰؤُلَاءِ قَوْمُنَا اتَّخَذُوا مِنْ دُونِهِ آلِهَةً لَوْلَا يَأْتُونَ عَلَيْهِمْ بِسُلْطَانٍ بَيِّنٍ

hāʾulāʾi qawmunā ittakhadhū min dūnihi ālihatan lawlā yaʾtūna ʿalayhim bi-sulṭānin bayyin

Al-Kahf 18:15

“These people of ours have taken deities besides Him. Why do they not produce clear authority for them?”

They continue:

فَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنِ افْتَرَىٰ عَلَى اللَّهِ كَذِبًا

fa-man aẓlamu mimmani iftarā ʿalā allāhi kadhibā

Al-Kahf 18:15

“Who is more unjust than one who fabricates a lie against God?”

The next verse clarifies the connection with truth and evidence.

The youths do more than note a difference in beliefs: they demand clear grounds for the other deities their people acknowledge.

They regard an unsupported claim of this kind as departure from truth.

Disagreement must therefore be distinguished from shaṭaṭ.

Disagreement with a person or group is not inherently shaṭaṭ. People may have different information, make mistakes, or be searching for truth.

Al-Kahf 18:14 specifically concerns another deity besides the Lord of the heavens and earth, which the youths regard as a grave departure from truth.

Shaṭaṭ here means speech far beyond the bounds of truth.

Its force is illuminated by the affirmation of one God, rejection of other deities, and demand for evidence in Al-Kahf 18:15, alongside the lexical sense.

Ṣād 38:22: remaining just in judgment

The second occurrence is in David’s story.

The Qur’an says:

إِذْ دَخَلُوا عَلَىٰ دَاوُودَ فَفَزِعَ مِنْهُمْ قَالُوا لَا تَخَفْ خَصْمَانِ بَغَىٰ بَعْضُنَا عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَنَا بِالْحَقِّ وَلَا تُشْطِطْ وَاهْدِنَا إِلَىٰ سَوَاءِ الصِّرَاطِ

idh dakhalū ʿalā dāwūda fa-faziʿa minhum qālū lā takhaf khaṣmāni baghā baʿḍunā ʿalā baʿḍin fa-ḥkum baynanā bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-lā tushṭiṭ wa-hdinā ilā sawāʾi al-ṣirāṭ

Ṣād 38:22

“When they entered upon David, he was alarmed by them. They said: Do not fear. We are two disputing parties; one has wronged the other. Judge between us in truth, do not depart from what is right, and guide us to the proper way.”

Tushṭiṭ is an imperfect verb in the jussive within a prohibition.

The litigants make three related requests:

First, judgment according to truth.

Second, no departure from truth and justice in judging.

Third, guidance to the right and straight course.

These reinforce one another without being identical.

Fa-ḥkum baynanā bi-l-ḥaqq establishes the standard of judgment.

Wa-lā tushṭiṭ prohibits departure from it.

Wa-hdinā ilā sawāʾi al-ṣirāṭ requests guidance to the proper path.

Shaṭaṭ concerns departure from truth and fairness, rather than merely lacking information or making a minor mistake.

For example, a property dispute calls for examining the claims, evidence, and rights of both parties.

Ignoring a party’s rights because of kinship, friendship, social influence, or personal advantage departs from justice.

Likewise, imposing a disproportionate, unjust penalty for a limited wrong illustrates exceeding proper bounds in judgment.

A harsh judgment or an outcome disliked by one party is not necessarily shaṭaṭ.

A just judgment may be painful to someone who violated another’s rights. Severity of consequences alone does not prove injustice.

The criterion is truth and justice, rather than universal satisfaction or equal shares.

Rights in David’s story

In Ṣād 38:23, one litigant says:

إِنَّ هَٰذَا أَخِي لَهُ تِسْعٌ وَتِسْعُونَ نَعْجَةً وَلِيَ نَعْجَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ فَقَالَ أَكْفِلْنِيهَا وَعَزَّنِي فِي الْخِطَابِ

inna hādhā akhī lahu tisʿun wa-tisʿūna naʿjatan wa-liya naʿjatun wāḥidatun fa-qāla akfilnīhā wa-ʿazzanī fī al-khiṭāb

Ṣād 38:23

“This is my brother. He has ninety-nine ewes, while I have one. He said: Entrust it to me, and he overpowered me in speech.”

The issue is not simply unequal possessions.

Ninety-nine ewes versus one does not establish injustice without examining ownership and conduct.

The allegation concerns the wealthier party demanding the other’s ewe and overpowering him verbally.

Ownership, demands, and treatment of the other party are therefore at issue.

David says in Ṣād 38:24:

لَقَدْ ظَلَمَكَ بِسُؤَالِ نَعْجَتِكَ إِلَىٰ نِعَاجِهِ

la-qad ẓalamaka bi-suʾāli naʿjatika ilā niʿājih

Ṣād 38:24

“He has wronged you by demanding your ewe to add to his own.”

The account continues with David’s recognition of a trial, seeking forgiveness, and returning to God.

Understanding SH Ṭ Ṭ does not require attributing a particular sin to David through stories outside the Qur’an.

Ṣād 38:22 makes a clear request: judge by truth without departing from it.

The prohibition does not by itself establish that David had already committed the prohibited act before hearing the request.

In Ṣād 38:26, God also tells David:

فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ بِالْحَقِّ وَلَا تَتَّبِعِ الْهَوَىٰ فَيُضِلَّكَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

fa-ḥkum bayna al-nāsi bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-lā tattabiʿi al-hawā fa-yuḍillaka ʿan sabīli allāh

Ṣād 38:26

“Judge between people in truth, and do not follow desire, lest it lead you astray from God’s path.”

This connects truthful judgment with resistance to personal desires.

Judicial shaṭaṭ can arise when a judge sets truth aside for desire, advantage, or inclination.

Tushṭiṭ itself does not mean following desire; Ṣād 38:26 expresses that separately.

In Ṣād 38:22, shaṭaṭ is departure from the bounds of truth and justice in judgment.

Al-Jinn 72:4: outrageous speech about God

The third occurrence is in Al-Jinn.

Jinn who have heard the Qur’an discuss their new understanding and earlier beliefs.

Al-Jinn 72:3 says:

وَأَنَّهُ تَعَالَىٰ جَدُّ رَبِّنَا مَا اتَّخَذَ صَاحِبَةً وَلَا وَلَدًا

wa-annahu taʿālā jaddu rabbinā mā ittakhadha ṣāḥibatan wa-lā waladā

Al-Jinn 72:3

“Exalted is our Lord’s majesty; He has taken neither a consort nor a child.”

They then say:

وَأَنَّهُ كَانَ يَقُولُ سَفِيهُنَا عَلَى اللَّهِ شَطَطًا

wa-annahu kāna yaqūlu safīhunā ʿalā allāhi shaṭaṭā

Al-Jinn 72:4

“Our foolish one used to say things about God far removed from truth.”

Shaṭaṭan characterizes statements about God.

Safīhunā can designate their foolish one or, under a collective interpretation, their foolish members. Its precise referent requires separate interpretation.

Some commentaries identify a particular being, but the verse does not name him.

Lexical understanding does not require a conclusive identification of that referent.

The subject is clear: improper statements about God.

The preceding verse denies a consort or child to God. Such attributions are therefore prominent instances of the speech rejected in this context.

Al-Jinn 72:5 continues:

وَأَنَّا ظَنَنَّا أَنْ لَنْ تَقُولَ الْإِنْسُ وَالْجِنُّ عَلَى اللَّهِ كَذِبًا

wa-annā ẓanannā an lan taqūla al-insu wa-al-jinnu ʿalā allāhi kadhibā

Al-Jinn 72:5

“We had thought that humans and jinn would never tell a lie about God.”

This clarifies the connection with false and unsupported claims concerning God.

The speakers acknowledge their earlier trust that humans and jinn would not lie about Him.

Their encounter with the Qur’an leads them to reassess that trust.

The context concerns consequential claims about God that led listeners toward mistaken beliefs. The word itself does not establish that the speaker knowingly lied.

Shaṭaṭ, falsehood, and ignorance

Al-Jinn 72:4 concerns speech about God; Al-Jinn 72:5 mentions falsehood.

The concepts overlap but are not lexically identical.

Kadhib concerns falsehood or an untrue report.

Shaṭaṭ emphasizes a statement’s distance from truth and departure from proper bounds.

A statement can be both false and shaṭaṭ, particularly a grave, unsupported assertion about God.

Neither every minor mistake nor every lack of knowledge automatically warrants the description.

Someone with incomplete knowledge may make an erroneous claim. Error alone does not establish deliberate falsehood; conversely, a claim’s being shaṭaṭ does not itself prove intent or awareness.

Claiming divine authority to impose an unsupported assertion about God raises issues beyond an ordinary factual error.

A particular speaker’s knowledge and intention require evidence. Demonstrating a claim’s falsity is distinct from proving a deliberate lie.

The speakers in Al-Jinn recount a change in their understanding and reassess previous beliefs after hearing the Qur’an.

Their response illustrates correcting earlier statements or trusted authorities in light of newly recognized truth.

Shaṭaṭ in Al-Jinn 72:4 means speech far from truth about God; the neighboring verses clarify its subject.

4. From spatial distance to ethical and intellectual deviation

Distance is not identical in every usage.

Two places may be far apart.

A demand may depart from a reasonable measure.

A judgment may deviate from fairness.

A statement may stray far from truth.

The reference point differs in each case.

For spatial distance, it is another place or position.

In a transaction, it may be a fair or customary demand.

In judgment, it is truth and fairness.

In statements about God, it is truth and valid evidence.

“Excess” alone can therefore be an incomplete definition.

Excess often suggests increased amount or intensity, whereas deviation from a proper standard also matters here.

A judgment can be unfairly severe to one person and unfairly lenient to another.

An extravagant claim is not shaṭaṭ merely because it is large; its falsity and distance from truth matter.

A suitable Qur’anic definition is:

“Departure from the proper limit or standard, such that speech, demands, or judgment move away from truth and fairness.”

This explains the three occurrences while keeping geographical and ethical contexts distinct.

5. Comparison with related roots

SH Ṭ Ṭ and B ʿ D: distance

Buʿd denotes distance.

This may be spatial, temporal, or figurative.

The spatial branch of SH Ṭ Ṭ overlaps with it in remoteness from a location or reference point.

Ethical and Qur’anic usages can emphasize exceeding bounds or departing from a proper standard.

Buʿd readily describes two distant cities; an unjust judgment belongs to the relevant field of shaṭaṭ.

Distance is not inherently blameworthy, and SH Ṭ Ṭ is not always merely spatial.

SH Ṭ Ṭ and GH L W: excess

Ghuluww concerns exceeding a limit, particularly elevating something beyond its proper measure or standing.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:171 says:

لَا تَغْلُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ

lā taghlū fī dīnikum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:171

“Do not exceed bounds in your religion.”

Here the excess concerns religious belief and conduct.

Shaṭaṭ also denotes excess but is not confined to religious exaggeration.

It appears in judgment, transactions, and speech.

Ghuluww often emphasizes excessive elevation, whereas shaṭaṭ can characterize deviation in different directions.

Elevating a human to divine authority can be ghuluww. A statement made about God on that basis may also be shaṭaṭ if far removed from truth.

Overlap does not make the roots interchangeable in all contexts.

SH Ṭ Ṭ and B GH Y: wrongdoing

Baghy can denote transgression or violating another’s rights.

Both appear in Ṣād 38:22:

بَغَىٰ بَعْضُنَا عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ

baghā baʿḍunā ʿalā baʿḍ

Ṣād 38:22

“One of us has wronged the other.”

Then:

وَلَا تُشْطِطْ

wa-lā tushṭiṭ

Ṣād 38:22

“Do not depart from what is right.”

Baghy is attributed to a disputing party, while shaṭaṭ concerns judgment.

The first concerns the alleged wrong; the second, the judge’s obligation to remain just.

The two roots thus address different aspects of one dispute.

This does not establish a universal division in which baghy applies only to litigants and shaṭaṭ only to judges.

SH Ṭ Ṭ and J W R: injustice

Jawr denotes injustice or departure from fairness.

Lexical explanations use it to explain judicial shaṭaṭ.

A judge who rules wrongly in favor of a powerful person acts unjustly.

The same behavior can be shaṭaṭ insofar as it departs from truth and fairness.

The broader SH Ṭ Ṭ family also includes distance, demands, and speech.

Jawr is therefore a close judicial equivalent, not a definition of the entire family.

SH Ṭ Ṭ, ʿ D L, and Q S Ṭ: justice

ʿAdl and qisṭ concern fairness and proper measure in human relations.

The litigants in Ṣād 38:22 request truthful judgment without shaṭaṭ.

In this context shaṭaṭ opposes just judgment.

Justice does not invariably mean dividing everything equally.

Splitting property equally between its owner and an unsupported claimant is not necessarily fair.

Justice may require returning all of it to its rightful owner.

A sound judgment need not lie midway between two demands; it must follow what is right.

This matters because sawāʾ al-ṣirāṭ accompanies bi-l-ḥaqq in Ṣād 38:22.

The proper way accords with truth and fairness, rather than simply occupying a numerical midpoint.

SH Ṭ Ṭ and Ḍ L L: losing the way

Ḍalāl concerns losing or missing the right way.

Qur’anic shaṭaṭ can likewise describe departure from truth.

Their linguistic images are not identical.

Ḍalāl highlights losing the way or lacking appropriate guidance.

Shaṭaṭ highlights departure from a proper limit or standard.

Ignorance may cause a person to lose the way. Assessing a claim or action as shaṭaṭ requires identifying the relevant standard and the departure from it.

Both descriptions may apply in some cases without erasing their distinction.

SH Ṭ Ṭ, SH Ṭ ʾ, and SH Ṭ R

These roots resemble one another in form.

Shāṭiʾ, under SH Ṭ ʾ, denotes a valley’s bank in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30.

Shaṭr, under SH Ṭ R, denotes direction in Al-Baqarah 2:144.

Shaṭaṭ belongs to the doubled root SH Ṭ Ṭ and concerns departure from truth or exceeding bounds in the Qur’an.

Shaṭṭ and shāṭiʾ can both mean a riverbank without all their derivatives becoming identical.

Nor does shaṭr as direction reduce shaṭaṭ to movement in a particular direction.

These families require independent study alongside their overlaps.

6. Morphology and suggested Forqan renderings

Shaṭaṭan in Al-Kahf 18:14 and Al-Jinn 72:4

Both verses use shaṭaṭan.

It is a verbal noun from SH Ṭ Ṭ, in the accusative.

In:

لَقَدْ قُلْنَا إِذًا شَطَطًا

la-qad qulnā idhan shaṭaṭā

Al-Kahf 18:14

It relates to speaking. One grammatical analysis treats it as qualifying an omitted verbal noun, qawlan shaṭaṭan: speech far from truth.

Al-Jinn 72:4 similarly has:

كَانَ يَقُولُ سَفِيهُنَا عَلَى اللَّهِ شَطَطًا

kāna yaqūlu safīhunā ʿalā allāhi shaṭaṭā

Al-Jinn 72:4

It characterizes the kind of speech.

It does not name a person, place, or concrete object here.

Accusative case need not indicate an object undergoing a physical action; the construction concerns speech and its description.

Tushṭiṭ in Ṣād 38:22

The form is:

وَلَا تُشْطِطْ

wa-lā tushṭiṭ

Ṣād 38:22

“Do not depart from what is right.”

Tushṭiṭ is the second-person masculine singular imperfect of Form IV.

Its corresponding perfect is ashaṭṭa.

Prohibitive lā places the verb in the jussive.

The doubled radicals appear separately in this form.

It differs from Form II tushaṭṭiṭ and Form I tashṭuṭ. Other readings and lexical forms are recorded, but the Ḥafṣ reading used here has Form IV tushṭiṭ.

The prohibition addresses David, and its object is clarified by the preceding request:

فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَنَا بِالْحَقِّ

fa-ḥkum baynanā bi-l-ḥaqq

Ṣād 38:22

“Judge between us in truth.”

Wa-lā tushṭiṭ therefore prohibits departure from truth in judgment.

Suggested renderings of derivatives

شَطَّ: shaṭṭa; was distant, moved away, or exceeded bounds, according to context.

شَطّ: shaṭṭ; bank or shore spatially; distance in another usage.

شُطُوط: shuṭūṭ; remoteness, or banks as the plural of shaṭṭ.

شَطَط: shaṭaṭ; excess, departure from truth, or speech far from truth.

شَطَاط: shaṭāṭ; distance, or tall and proportionate stature.

شِطَّة: shiṭṭah; a long distance.

شَاطّ: shāṭṭ; remote, or unjust in a judicial context.

أَشَطَّ: ashaṭṭa; exceeded bounds, demanded excessively, or judged unjustly.

إِشْطَاط: ishṭāṭ; excess or exceeding bounds.

اشْتَطَّ: ishtaṭṭa; exceeded bounds in a demand or judgment.

اشْتِطَاط: ishṭiṭāṭ; excess or overstepping a proper measure.

شَطَّطَ: shaṭṭaṭa; acted with pronounced excess.

The equivalent must suit the object and syntax. Injustice fits a judicial context, but not a distant location or tall stature.

Suggested renderings of the three occurrences

Al-Kahf 18:14

لَقَدْ قُلْنَا إِذًا شَطَطًا

la-qad qulnā idhan shaṭaṭā

Al-Kahf 18:14

“Otherwise, we would indeed have spoken far from truth.”

“Far from truth” connects shaṭaṭ with invoking a deity besides God.

Ṣād 38:22

فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَنَا بِالْحَقِّ وَلَا تُشْطِطْ

fa-ḥkum baynanā bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-lā tushṭiṭ

Ṣād 38:22

“Judge between us in truth, and do not depart from what is right.”

This retains the verb’s sense and its judicial setting.

Al-Jinn 72:4

وَأَنَّهُ كَانَ يَقُولُ سَفِيهُنَا عَلَى اللَّهِ شَطَطًا

wa-annahu kāna yaqūlu safīhunā ʿalā allāhi shaṭaṭā

Al-Jinn 72:4

“Our foolish one used to say things about God far removed from truth.”

An intensifier can convey the pronounced distance from truth in this context.

Neither speech-related occurrence should be reduced lexically to polytheism, deliberate lying, or knowingly rejecting truth.

Such concepts may arise from context but are not all contained in the root itself.

“Do not act unjustly” is also intelligible in Ṣād 38:22; “do not depart from what is right” more directly retains the image of departure and its connection with the preceding request. These proposals do not replace installed verse translations.

7. Conclusion

SH Ṭ Ṭ concerns distance, deviation, and exceeding bounds.

Spatial usages concern distant dwellings; other usages concern excessive demands or departures from proper speech and judgment.

Tall stature and riverbanks show that not all derivatives carry negative moral implications.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an.

In Al-Kahf 18:14, the believing youths say invoking another deity would constitute speech far from truth.

In Ṣād 38:22, the litigants ask David to judge truthfully without injustice.

In Al-Jinn 72:4, the listening jinn recall outrageous statements about God attributed to their foolish one; the precise referent is a separate interpretive question.

All three share departure from a proper reference point.

For speech about God, that point is truth and evidence; for judgment, truth and justice.

Shaṭaṭ is not merely quantitative excess. It can describe departure from a standard even where no physical amount is involved.

The defensible Qur’anic core is exceeding proper bounds or departing from truth and justice.

“Speech far from truth” fits the two verbal contexts; “do not depart from what is right” fits judgment.

Not all distance is wrong, height excessive, disagreement deviation, or severity injustice.

The description applies where speech, demands, or judgment depart from the proper standard; assessing it responsibly requires identifying that standard.

Unsupported claims about God and judgment driven by personal advantage both replace truth with something else. Faithfulness to truth in speech and to justice in judgment are two clear contexts for avoiding shaṭaṭ in the Qur’an.
