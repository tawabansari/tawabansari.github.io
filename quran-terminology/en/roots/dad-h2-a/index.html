Resemblance and similarity: from likeness between people and statements to the recurrence of earlier beliefs

In Arabic, the root ḍ h ʾ concerns resemblance between things, people, or statements. The verb ḍāhaʾa is used when something resembles something else, or a person's speech or conduct shows a recognizable likeness to another's.

Within this family, مُضَاهَأَة means resemblance or similarity. It may concern appearance, characteristics, conduct, or the content of speech.

For example, two people may express an idea similarly, or two groups in different periods may advance beliefs with similar content. Both involve resemblance, but resemblance alone does not establish deliberate imitation.

This distinction matters for the Quranic usage: Al-Tawbah 9:30 speaks of statements resembling those of people who disbelieved before.

Arabic sources record ضَاهَأَ and ضَاهَى with the shared meaning of resemblance. Some usages and readings employ the form with hamzah, others the form without it.

In ʿĀṣim's Quranic reading, the root occurs only as يُضَاهِئُونَ. This is a Form III verb and, in context, describes resemblance between religious statements.

1. The root in early Arabic

Early Arabic sources explain مُضَاهَأَة principally through مُشَاكَلَة: resemblance or similarity between two things.

In this usage, ضَاهَأْتُ الرَّجُلَ means “I resembled that man.”

The form without hamzah, ضَاهَيْتُهُ, is recorded with the same meaning.

Early lexical accounts explain مُضَاهَاة sometimes as resemblance and sometimes as following or accompanying another.

Although these usages are related, three situations should be distinguished:

First, an existing likeness between things: they share a characteristic or condition.

Second, becoming like another: someone's speech or conduct takes a form resembling another's.

Third, following or imitating another: someone knows another's speech or conduct and follows it.

The third situation can produce resemblance, but resemblance alone does not establish conscious following.

Historical evidence for the meaning

Early lexicons show that “resemblance” was a recognized meaning for both forms, with and without hamzah.

Accounts attributed to early lexicographers place ضَاهَأْتُ الرَّجُلَ and ضَاهَيْتُهُ alongside شَابَهْتُهُ, “I resembled him.”

Al-Farrāʾ explains the form without hamzah in terms of similar statements and identifies the form with hamzah as a usage of some Arabs.

Both forms are thus recorded in the Arabic linguistic tradition; their difference is not simply a spelling mistake.

The evidence examined here does not establish an independent poetic witness for ضَاهَأَ whose composition can confidently be dated before the Quran.

We therefore cannot state with certainty which form came first historically or precisely when the form with hamzah arose.

What is clearly supported is that both forms are recorded in Arabic lexical sources and Quranic reading traditions. These records do not by themselves establish the exact date when either form originated.

Another reported meaning: treating gently

Lexicons also report another use of ضَاهَأَ.

Through Abū ʿUbayd, quoting al-Umawī, ضَاهَأَ الرَّجُلَ is recorded as رَفَقَ بِهِ: treating someone gently or with consideration.

This is not the same as resemblance.

Someone may treat another gently without resembling them in speech or conduct.

This records a gentle-treatment sense for the form with hamzah. With insufficient independently datable evidence, however, it should not be made the root's central meaning or its preferred Quranic sense.

Al-Tawbah 9:30 concerns similar statements, rather than gentle treatment of others.

2. Forms and the relationship with Ḍ H Y

Arabic sources record the following forms in this family:

ضَاهَأَ: resembled or showed likeness to another.

يُضَاهِئُ: resembles or shows likeness to something.

مُضَاهَأَة: resemblance between things or actions.

ضَاهَى: the verb without hamzah, meaning to resemble, become like, or in some usages follow another.

يُضَاهِي: the imperfect form of the verb without hamzah.

مُضَاهَاة: resemblance, similarity, or making something alike.

Some indexes discuss the forms with hamzah under ḍ h ʾ and those without it under ḍ h y.

This classification does not establish that the two families began as entirely independent historical roots.

Early grammarians differed over the morphological relationship between these verbs. Some treated the hamzated form as a linguistic variant of ḍāhā. Debates about an original or additional hamzah also concern related forms such as ضَهْيَأ and should be examined separately.

Under ḍ h y, Ibn Fāris identifies resemblance as the central meaning and records the form with hamzah alongside it.

In this Quranic study, ḍ h ʾ is the indexed root for يُضَاهِئُونَ, while its connection with ḍ h y remains important.

Does Form III mean reciprocal imitation?

Ḍāhaʾa belongs to Form III.

In many verbs, this pattern can express a relationship or action between two parties. It does not, by itself, require both parties to perform the same action toward each other.

If someone's speech resembles another's, the second person need not be consciously imitating the first.

Nor does the pattern alone establish intention or awareness.

Resemblance may result from direct following, or two statements may resemble one another without deliberate imitation.

Thus, يُضَاهِئُونَ in Al-Tawbah 9:30 does not alone establish that the speakers directly heard earlier statements and consciously repeated them.

Such a historical relationship requires separate evidence.

3. The Quranic use of يُضَاهِئُونَ

Al-Tawbah 9:30

وَقَالَتِ الْيَهُودُ عُزَيْرٌ ابْنُ اللَّهِ وَقَالَتِ النَّصَارَى الْمَسِيحُ ابْنُ اللَّهِ ۖ ذَٰلِكَ قَوْلُهُمْ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ ۖ يُضَاهِئُونَ قَوْلَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ قَبْلُ ۚ قَاتَلَهُمُ اللَّهُ ۚ أَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ

wa-qālati al-yahūdu ʿuzayrun ibnu allāhi wa-qālati al-naṣārā al-masīḥu ibnu allāhi dhālika qawluhum bi-afwāhihim yuḍāhiʾūna qawla alladhīna kafarū min qablu qātalahumu allāhu annā yuʾfakūn

Al-Tawbah 9:30

Translation: “The Jews said, ‘ʿUzayr is the son of The God,’ and the Christians said, ‘The Messiah is the son of The God.’ That is their saying with their mouths. They resemble the saying of those who disbelieved before. May The God oppose them! How they are turned away!”

This is the only Quranic occurrence of ḍ h ʾ in the reading underlying this study.

The verse reports two statements: attributing divine sonship to ʿUzayr and to the Messiah.

It then states that these sayings resemble those of people who disbelieved earlier.

The structure of يُضَاهِئُونَ

يُضَاهِئُونَ is an imperfect Form III verb.

It is third-person masculine plural and refers back to the speakers of the preceding statements.

The wāw represents the plural subject; the final nūn marks the indicative in this verbal construction.

قَوْلَ is the direct object. Some grammarians explain the comparison through an understood noun: “their saying resembles the saying of those…” This clarifies how a verb attributed to people compares their statements.

الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا refers to people who disbelieved previously, whose statement is being compared with that of the speakers in the verse.

مِنْ قَبْلُ specifies their earlier position in time.

The immediate subject of the comparison is therefore a likeness between statements.

This part of the verse does not compare faces, ancestry, language, ethnicity, or physical features.

Nor does يُضَاهِئُونَ itself mean holding every belief of the earlier group. It identifies a likeness in a particular statement.

Which statement resembles earlier speech?

The verse begins with two specific claims about ʿUzayr and the Messiah. Their common feature is the attribution of sonship to God.

That feature is the clear focus of the comparison.

When these sayings are compared with those of earlier disbelievers, attributing a child to God is consequently the most appropriate point of comparison.

This does not make the persons mentioned in the two statements identical: one concerns ʿUzayr, the other the Messiah.

Their doctrinal structure is similar: placing someone in a relationship of sonship with God.

Linguistically, statements may resemble each other in content even when their names and wording are not identical.

Who are قَوْلَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ قَبْلُ referring to?

The phrase does not explicitly name the earlier group.

Historical commentaries propose several possibilities: likeness to polytheists who attributed children or daughters to God; likeness between the Christian statement and the claim attributed to Jews; or following earlier beliefs within the same communities.

These explanations may aid historical study, but no single one should become the root's lexical meaning without independent evidence.

Ḍāhaʾa means resemblance, not the name of a people, a specific doctrine, or a historical mechanism of transmission.

The verse makes clear that the criticized statements resemble earlier sayings. Establishing every historical detail of that relationship requires separate research.

4. Resemblance in speech and deliberate following

This distinction is central to understanding the root.

Suppose two people in different places and periods arrive at the same conclusion without knowing each other.

Their statements may resemble each other, but the resemblance does not prove imitation.

If someone hears another's statement, accepts it without examination, and repeats it, there is following as well as resemblance.

Resemblance and imitation can therefore coexist, but they are not identical concepts.

In Al-Tawbah 9:30, يُضَاهِئُونَ directly expresses likeness between statements.

Direct borrowing of earlier speech is not necessarily entailed by the root itself.

Connection with Al-Baqarah 2:118

Al-Baqarah 2:118 describes similar statements by groups in different periods and then refers to their hearts being alike.

The speakers and times differ, while their kind of statement and orientation resemble one another.

This helps illuminate Al-Tawbah 9:30: the Quran can place statements from different groups together because their content or orientation is similar.

Nevertheless, the entire conclusion of Al-Baqarah 2:118 should not be transferred into the lexical meaning of يُضَاهِئُونَ.

The similarity of hearts is expressed separately in that verse. In Al-Tawbah 9:30, ḍ h ʾ concerns resemblance in speech.

Connection with following predecessors

Al-Zukhruf 43:22 describes people who make their ancestors' beliefs the basis of their following.

It clarifies the subject of adherence to inherited beliefs.

The two situations nevertheless differ:

Al-Tawbah 9:30 describes resemblance to earlier statements.

Al-Zukhruf 43:22 describes reliance on the ancestors' path and beliefs as a basis for following.

Both may occur in one historical situation, but resemblance alone does not establish the means by which a belief was transmitted.

A translation of يُضَاهِئُونَ should therefore preserve resemblance, explaining conscious imitation only where independent contextual evidence supports it.

5. Resemblance, truth, and falsehood

The root ḍ h ʾ does not have a fixed moral judgment built into its lexical meaning.

Two things can resemble each other in a good or correct characteristic, just as two false statements may resemble one another.

Resemblance alone therefore proves neither truth nor falsehood.

For example, if two people independently make a true statement, its resemblance does not make it false.

Conversely, repetition of a false claim across groups or generations does not make its frequency or age evidence of truth.

In Al-Tawbah 9:30, the censure comes from the doctrinal content and the Quran's monotheistic context, not from يُضَاهِئُونَ itself.

Al-Ikhlāṣ 112:3 denies that God begets or is begotten.

This clear Quranic principle provides the criterion for assessing claims of divine sonship.

Muḍāhaʾah should therefore not be defined as polytheism, disbelief, or false belief.

The verse compares statements whose content is criticized in context. Their evaluation follows from that content and other Quranic principles.

Does the censure extend to every Jew and Christian?

The verse uses the collective terms الْيَهُودُ and النَّصَارَى and attributes particular sayings to these communities.

Without independent evidence, this report cannot establish that every Jewish or Christian individual in every time and place held or holds exactly the same belief.

The criticism concerns the particular claims stated in the verse.

Nor does resemblance between a present belief and an earlier one establish an individual's intention, awareness, or moral responsibility.

Contemporary analysis should distinguish a belief's content, its transmission history, the person's awareness, and their responsibility.

These distinctions concern the scope of an attribution and its audience. They are not part of the lexical definition of ḍ h ʾ.

6. Related forms and lexical distinctions

Ḍ H ʾ (ḍāhaʾa) and Ḍ H Y (ḍāhā)

This is the closest form to the one under study.

Early sources record both ضَاهَأَ and ضَاهَى for resemblance and similarity.

The central formal difference is the presence or absence of hamzah. Early grammarians discussed their relationship; the status of hamzah in nearby nominal forms also requires a separate morphological analysis.

In ʿĀṣim's reading, Al-Tawbah 9:30 has يُضَاهِئُونَ; the other six of the seven established readings have يُضَاهُونَ.

Both readings convey resemblance in speech here.

The difference in hamzah should therefore not by itself be taken as evidence of two different meanings.

Conversely, recording both forms does not establish which was original and which was modified at the beginning of the language's history.

Ḍ H ʾ (ḍāhaʾa) and Sh B H (shabah)

Sh b h is a familiar root for resemblance and similarity.

It can concern appearance, characteristics, condition, or the content of two things.

Ḍāhaʾa likewise denotes resemblance in its Quranic usage.

The particular feature here is that resemblance is expressed as a relationship between statements.

This single occurrence does not establish that ḍ h ʾ always concerns speech or that sh b h always concerns outward appearance.

Arabic usages of both families are broader and overlap in the field of resemblance.

Ḍ H ʾ (ḍāhaʾa) and M Th L (mithl)

Mithl concerns likeness or equivalence in one or more respects.

The Quran uses this root in various constructions to compare people, conduct, things, and situations.

The usage studied here expresses resemblance between statements through a verb.

Two things may be mithl, alike, in a particular respect and show resemblance in that same respect.

This overlap does not make all uses of mithl and muḍāhaʾah interchangeable.

Mithl can function in various nominal and descriptive constructions, whereas يُضَاهِئُونَ here is a verb with a subject and object, expressing a relationship between the speakers and earlier speech.

Ḍ H ʾ (ḍāhaʾa) and Ḥ K Y (ḥakā)

In Arabic, ḥakā concerns recounting, reporting, and in some uses imitation.

Someone recounting another's statement may reproduce its wording or content.

Similarity between two statements, however, does not necessarily mean direct quotation or borrowing.

If someone hears another's words and repeats them, imitation or recounting may be involved.

If independent groups make similar statements, resemblance is evident while direct transmission remains unestablished.

The verb يُضَاهِئُونَ in Al-Tawbah 9:30 does not alone prove direct transmission.

Ḍ H ʾ (ḍāhaʾa) and T B ʿ (tabiʿa)

Tabiʿa concerns following or moving along another's path.

The Quran uses this family for following guidance, people, commands, and various paths.

Following can produce resemblance in conduct and speech, but the concepts differ.

One may follow another without resembling them in every respect, or resemble them without following them.

Translating يُضَاهِئُونَ as “they follow” may be discussable within some interpretations, but it is not a direct equivalent covering all uses.

Words similar in writing

Early sources also record ضَهْيَأ and ضَهْيَاء.

Some uses describe a woman who does not menstruate or whose body lacks certain developed features. Related forms also have other applications to animals and plants.

Some lexicographers explained the designation for a woman through resemblance to men.

Early grammarians nevertheless disagreed over these forms' morphology and whether their hamzah was original or additional.

Lexicons also record ضَهْيَأَ أَمْرَهُ for leaving a matter unresolved rather than settling it decisively.

These usages should not be presented as certain derivatives of the same three-consonant root ḍ h ʾ without examining morphology and historical evidence.

Understanding يُضَاهِئُونَ does not require importing illness, fertility, plants, or indecisive conduct into the Quranic meaning of resemblance.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the principal use of ضَاهَأَ, possible equivalents include “resemble,” “be similar,” and “become alike.”

In some constructions, “make alike” or “imitate” may fit, but the choice depends on context and the relationship involved.

In Al-Tawbah 9:30, the phrase:

يُضَاهِئُونَ قَوْلَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ قَبْلُ

yuḍāhiʾūna qawla alladhīna kafarū min qablu

Al-Tawbah 9:30

Has the direct sense:

“Their saying resembles that of those who disbelieved before.”

Or, retaining a verbal construction:

“They say something like what those who disbelieved before said.”

The current Persian Forqan rendering, meaning “they resemble the saying of those who disbelieved before,” also preserves the sense of resemblance.

For a shorter explanation in a word box, the following can be proposed:

يُضَاهِئُونَ

Brief root meaning: “Resemblance or similarity to something or someone.”

Meaning in this verse: “Their statements resemble those of people who disbelieved before.”

“They consciously imitate their predecessors” adds awareness and deliberate imitation beyond the verb's direct meaning.

“They repeat the same words” is not necessarily precise either: similarity of content does not entail identical wording.

“They make similar statements” or “their statements resemble” more clearly preserves the lexical meaning.

Elsewhere in Arabic, مُضَاهَأَة can be rendered “resemblance” or “similarity.” The particular reported sense of gentle treatment requires an equivalent such as “treat gently”; that uncommon meaning should not be extended to every form.

8. Conclusion

In its principal use, ḍ h ʾ concerns resemblance and similarity.

Early sources record ضَاهَأَ and ضَاهَى with the shared meaning “resemble.” Following or accompanying is also reported in some uses, but deliberate imitation is not a necessary part of the root's meaning.

Early grammarians discussed the relationship between the forms with and without hamzah. The evidence establishes the family's meaning, while determining which form arose first requires further independent evidence.

The Quran has this root only in Al-Tawbah 9:30, as يُضَاهِئُونَ in ʿĀṣim's reading. The other six of the seven established readings record the form without hamzah, with the same general meaning of resemblance.

The verse concerns the resemblance between statements attributing sonship to God and those of earlier disbelievers.

The verb directly expresses likeness in speech. Identifying the earlier groups precisely or explaining how later speakers acquired those beliefs is a historical and interpretive question.

The negative judgment comes from the statements' content and their Quranic critique. The root itself does not mean disbelief, polytheism, distortion, or following falsehood.

The final definition is:

Ḍ H ʾ: resemblance between things, people, conduct, or speech in a particular characteristic or content, without necessarily implying conscious imitation, direct transmission, or complete identity.

Al-Tawbah 9:30 applies this meaning to doctrinal statements. A belief may recur in similar forms across communities or periods, but its truth and worth must be assessed by its content and evidence, rather than its age, frequency, or repetition.
