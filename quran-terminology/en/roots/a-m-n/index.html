Qur’anic Root

A M N

Security, trust, confidence, entrusted responsibility, faithfulness, affirmation, rest from fear, and entering into a secure relation with truth.

Forms Covered from This Root

This page studies the root A M N in meaning, then explains the most important Qur’anic forms built from this root:

  • أَمِنَ / يَأْمَنُ / أَمِنتُمْ (amina / yaʾmanu / amintum) — Form I verb; to be secure, feel safe, trust, move out of fear
  • آمَنَ / يُؤْمِنُ / يُؤْمِنُونَ / آمَنُوا / آمَنَّا (āmana / yuʾminu / yuʾminūna / āmanū / āmannā) — Form IV; to believe, trust, affirm, enter into faith
  • ٱؤْتُمِنَ (uʾtumina) — Form VIII passive; to be entrusted, to be treated as trustworthy
  • أَمْن (amn) — noun; security, safety, state free from fear
  • أَمَنَة (amanah) — noun; calming security, inward rest after fear, a soothing security
  • أَمَانَة / أَمَانَات (amānah / amānāt) — noun; trust, entrusted responsibility, something to be preserved without betrayal
  • أَمِين (amīn) — adjective; trustworthy, faithful to a trust, secure in relation to responsibility
  • مَأْمَن (maʾman) — noun of place; place of safety, refuge, place of security
  • آمِن / آمِنِين / آمِنَة (āmin / āminīn / āminah) — active participle/adjective; safe, secure, at peace from fear
  • مَأْمُون (maʾmūn) — passive participle; made secure, trusted, placed in a state of safety
  • إِيمَان (īmān) — verbal noun; faith, trustful affirmation, inward assurance, fidelity to truth
  • مُؤْمِن / مُؤْمِنُون / مُؤْمِنِينَ (muʾmin / muʾminūn / muʾminīn) — Form IV active participle; believer, believers, those who trust, affirm, and remain faithful to truth
  • مُؤْمِنَة / مُؤْمِنَات (muʾminah / muʾmināt) — feminine active participle; believing woman, believing women

Reminder: the Form I verb is أَمِنَ (amina) with kasrah on the mīm, and should be distinguished from the noun أَمْن (amn) with sukūn. Also, the adjectival form is آمِن (āmin), while آمَنَ (āmana) is the Form IV past-tense verb.

Related note: مُهَيْمِن (muhaymin) is listed in the Qur’anic Arabic Corpus under the root H M N, but semantically it is closely connected with guarding, trust, security of truth, and preserving the Book. This page treats it as a semantic connection, not as a direct A M N form.

General meaning of A M N

The root A M N in the Qur’an points to security, assurance, trust, entrusted responsibility, affirmation, calm from fear, faithfulness, and entering into a secure relation with truth.

This root begins from a simple and deep truth: the human being lives either in fear, mistrust, betrayal, and insecurity, or in security, trust, entrusted responsibility, and truthfulness. Qur’anic faith rises from this field. Faith is not merely accepting a statement in the mind; it is arranging one’s relation with truth on the basis of trust, assurance, and entrusted responsibility.

From here we can see the deep connection between amn, amānah, and īmān. Whoever has faith must be faithful to truth. Whoever betrays a trust breaks the security of confidence. Whoever believes in truth must leave false fears, suspicion, and betrayal of truth behind.

So A M N does not only call the human being to belief. It calls the human being to build a secure, truthful, trustworthy, and faithful relation with God, revelation, people, and the responsibilities of life.

Amn: stability against the trembling of fear

To understand A M N, we should notice its sensory layer. Amn is a state in which heart and life move out of the trembling caused by danger and enter stability.

Against khawf, which shakes the heart and scatters the human being, amn points to firmness, calm, and freedom from collapse. This helps explain why trust, faith, and social safety all belong in the same semantic field.

Trustworthiness stabilizes a relationship. Betrayal collapses it. Faith steadies the heart in relation to truth. Suspicion and faithlessness leave the heart in insecurity and instability.

So at the root of A M N, the issue is not only absence of fear. It is stability of relationship, inward calm, and the absence of betrayal and collapse.

Amina and amn: security and departure from fear

Amina is the verb: to become secure, feel safe, or trust. Amn is the noun: security and the state of safety. Distinguishing them helps preserve precision.

Qur’anic security is not only bodily safety. Someone may be outwardly safe while the heart remains trapped in fear, doubt, betrayal, or mistrust. Another person may face outward hardship yet remain inwardly stable in relation to God and truth.

Qur’anic amn is connected to truth. True security is not merely being protected from external danger; deeper security is that the human being is on a sound path, does not betray truth, and lives before God with trustworthiness.

So amn in this root prepares the meaning of faith. Faith means leaving the insecurity of mistrust and fragmentation and entering a secure relation with truth.

Amānah: what must be preserved intact

Amānah and amānāt are major branches of this root. A trust is something entrusted to a person and must be preserved correctly, without betrayal.

Trust is not only property. Life, intellect, speech, knowledge, power, family, the Book, truth, covenant, social responsibility, and even faith can all be trusts. The human being is answerable for what has been entrusted to him.

Here the connection between trust and faith becomes clear. The believer must be trustworthy. If someone claims faith but betrays trusts, there is a gap between the tongue and the truth of life.

So amānah brings faith out of claim and into action. Faith without trustworthiness is incomplete and unstable.

Īmān: beyond mental belief

Īmān is often translated as faith or belief, but Qur’anic faith is wider than mental belief. A belief may be merely accepting a proposition. Īmān also includes trust, affirmation, fidelity, entrusted responsibility, and practical orientation.

A person may accept something mentally yet not remain faithful to it. He may say God exists, but betray truth in practice. He may say he accepts the Book, but set its criterion aside. That is not complete faith.

Īmān means truth enters the heart, creates trust, secures the human being inwardly, and appears outwardly as trustworthiness. Faith must be seen in conduct, judgment, justice, fidelity, and responsibility.

So faith in the Qur’an is not only speech. It is a living state that places the human being in a secure and truthful relation with God and truth.

Āmana / yu’minu: trusting and affirming truth

The forms āmana and yu’minu are among the most important forms of this root. They are usually translated as “to believe,” but their depth must be preserved.

Āmana means the human being trusted truth, affirmed it, entered into a relation of security with it, and left hostility, mistrust, or denial.

Coming to faith is not merely changing a name or joining a group. It means making a covenant with truth. If one affirms God, one must avoid betraying truth. If one affirms the Book, one must take it as criterion. If one affirms the Last Day, one must live responsibly.

So yu’minu means the human being does not merely hear truth; he trusts it and organizes life by it.

Yu’minūn: ongoing renewal of faith

Yu’minūn means they believe, but this is not merely a verbal or mental act. The imperfect verb also suggests continuity and renewal. Faith is not a fixed station once completed; it is a living flow that must be renewed in each encounter with truth.

They do not merely say, “We believe.” They trust God, affirm truth, do not betray revelation, and arrange their relation with truth on the basis of trustworthiness.

Yu’minūn shows that faith is living movement. The human being must remain in ongoing trust, affirmation, fidelity, and reform. If faith does not become active in life, it becomes a dry claim.

For this root page, the word means: those who choose and renew the path of trust and entrusted responsibility in relation to God, truth, the unseen, the Book, and moral accountability.

Faith and the unseen

Faith in the Qur’an is sometimes connected with the unseen. The unseen is truth hidden from direct sensory perception. But being hidden from sense does not mean being irrational or cut off from reason.

Faith in the unseen means the human being is not imprisoned inside the visible. He understands that not all truth is limited to what the eye sees or the hand touches. Through signs, revelation, nature, reason, and trust in God, he arranges his relation with hidden truth.

This is not blind faith. Qur’anic faith should not be separated from evidence. But evidence does not always mean physical seeing. Many truths are known through effect, order, sign, trustworthiness of the message, and harmony with truth.

So faith in the unseen means conscious trust in a reality hidden from sense but known through signs, revelation, and truth.

Mu’min: the secure and trustworthy human being

Mu’min is one of the most important words from this root. A mu’min is one who has faith, but that faith must be understood through security, trust, entrusted responsibility, and fidelity.

A mu’min is not merely someone who places a religious label on himself. A mu’min is someone whose relation with God, truth, people, and entrusted responsibilities is based on security and truthfulness. He must be trustworthy because faith cannot be separated from trust.

The mu’min affirms truth and does not betray it. If he accepts the Book, he must not distort or hide it. If he believes in the Last Day, he must not live irresponsibly in this world. If he believes in God, he must not divide servitude between God and powers, desires, or human lords.

So the mu’min is a human being secured in truth and trustworthy in life.

Mu’min and Muslim are not identical

In the Qur’an, mu’min is not simply identical with muslim. A muslim may be someone who has entered a state of submission, outward acceptance, or the order of obedience. But faith is a deeper rank because it must enter the heart and bring the human being inwardly into security, trust, affirmation, and fidelity.

The Qur’an makes this distinction clear in the speech of the desert Arabs:

قَالَتِ الْأَعْرَابُ آمَنَّا قُل لَّمْ تُؤْمِنُوا وَلَٰكِن قُولُوا أَسْلَمْنَا وَلَمَّا يَدْخُلِ الْإِيمَانُ فِي قُلُوبِكُمْ

qālati al-aʿrābu āmannā; qul lam tuʾminū walākin qūlū aslamnā wa-lammā yadkhuli al-īmānu fī qulūbikum

They said they had believed, but the response was not to say they had believed; rather, they should say they had submitted, because faith had not yet entered their hearts.

This shows that faith is not merely verbal declaration or outward membership. A person may submit at one level, enter the community of believers, or carry a religious appearance, while faith has not yet taken root in the heart.

So faith is deeper than outward submission. Islam may be an entrance, but īmān must settle in the heart and appear as trustworthiness in action.

Believers beyond a closed group-name

If the word believers is understood by the Qur’an’s own criterion, it is not merely the name of a closed religious group. A believer is one connected to God, the Last Day, and righteous action, arranging his relation with truth through trust, affirmation, entrusted responsibility, and fidelity.

In verses such as 2:62 and 5:69, the Qur’an places the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians before a shared criterion: faith in God, faith in the Last Day, and righteous action.

This means faith in the Qur’anic sense is an ethical and divine reality, not merely a sectarian label. Any group that carries the name of faith but is cut off from God, the Hereafter, and righteous action is far from the truth of faith. And any human being who turns toward God, final accountability, and righteous action enters the field of faith.

So mu’min should not be understood as a closed and exclusive identity. A believer is one who is trustworthy, truthful, and faithful in relation to God and truth.

Amānah and amānāt: entrusted responsibility

Amānah and amānāt show that faith is connected with responsibility. Every trust entrusted to a human being must be preserved properly.

A trust may be property, but it is not only property. Truth is also a trust. The Book is a trust. Power is a trust. Vote, testimony, judgment, family, knowledge, and speech are also trusts.

If the human being betrays a trust, he destroys the security of confidence. A society in which trusts are betrayed becomes inwardly insecure, even if its outward order looks stable.

So amānah and amānāt show that faith without trustworthiness is a rootless claim.

Amīn: trustworthy and faithful

Amīn means one who is trustworthy and preserves the trust intact. In the Qur’an, it describes those whose relation to message, responsibility, or trust is not treacherous.

Being amīn is not a small ethical quality. In the field of revelation, trustworthiness is a condition for carrying the message correctly. One who is not trustworthy cannot carry truth, because he distorts, hides, or corrupts the message.

In human life as well, trustworthiness is the foundation of justice. If people cannot trust one another, contracts, family, society, knowledge, and religion are harmed.

So amīn is a branch of faith: one who does not betray the trust and does not break confidence.

Ma’man: place of safety and the right of refuge

Ma’man means a place of safety, refuge, or a place of security. It shows that Qur’anic security is not only an inward state; it may also have a social and practical form.

To provide safety and a place of refuge means to treat even someone from outside or someone in danger with justice and security. This meaning shows that faith must not become blind violence or injustice.

If the human being is a believer, he should create security, not fear. He should be trustworthy toward truth, not one who abuses power.

So ma’man connects the root of faith with the ethics of refuge, protection, and social safety.

Āmin and āminīn: safety from fear

Āmin, āminīn, and āminah refer to a state of safety, calm, and departure from fear. They are branches of the same field of amn.

Security in the Qur’an is a great blessing, but if the human being turns security into heedlessness, arrogance, or ingratitude, that same security becomes a test.

A secure society should recognize truth more deeply, not corrupt itself through comfort. A secure person should be more grateful and responsible, not more indifferent.

So safety in this root is both a blessing and a responsibility.

Amanah: security in the middle of danger

Amanah points to calm, security, and assurance after distress. In the Qur’an, it appears in a very concrete and dangerous context: believers in the atmosphere of battle and outer fear are covered by a light, calming sleep.

This shows that amanah is not ordinary psychological comfort. It is a kind of security sent by God, a security that gives the heart and body stillness in the middle of outward danger.

When the human being is in danger, yet God places calm within him, security is not merely the absence of danger. It is the presence of divine stillness within danger.

So amanah is a branch of divine security: an inward stillness that holds the human being together amid distress.

Ma’mūn: made secure or trusted

Ma’mūn is the passive participle in this field: something or someone placed in security, trusted, or regarded as safe.

This word shows that security can also be a received state. The human being is not always the producer of security. Sometimes he himself needs to be made secure, given refuge, and placed in a trustworthy condition.

So being ma’mūn reminds us that true security is a blessing that must be recognized and not turned into betrayal, arrogance, or heedlessness.

Muhaymin: semantic connection with guarding the trust of truth

Muhaymin is analyzed in Qur’anic morphology under the root H M N. Yet some lexical explanations connect it with the field of A M N, guarding, trust, and preservation.

Semantically, muhaymin means guardian, watcher, witness, preserver, and one that keeps the trust of truth under protection. When the divine Book is described as muhaymin over previous scriptures, it preserves the trust of truth, confirms what remains true, and exposes distortion or concealment.

In this sense, muhaymin functions like a supremely trustworthy safeguarding system: a place of security for the truth of the Books, and a guardian over the authenticity of truth across history.

So although this page does not list muhaymin as a direct form of A M N, its semantic connection with trust, security, and preserving truth is important for understanding the broader field of faith.

Faith, trust, and responsibility

The root A M N leads us to a major principle: faith without trustworthiness is incomplete.

If someone believes in God, he must be faithful to truth. If he believes in the Book, he must not distort it. If he gives people a promise, he must not betray it. If he has power, he must create security and justice. If he has knowledge, he must use it rightly.

Faith is not only an inward relation with God; its effect must appear in the world. The believer should create security. His tongue should not deceive people. His power should not break trusts. His religion should not become a tool of insecurity.

So Qur’anic faith begins with security and trust, and reaches responsibility, justice, and fidelity.

Summary

The root A M N in the Qur’an calls the human being to understand security, assurance, trust, entrusted responsibility, faith, affirmation, and fidelity. It shows that faith is not merely mental belief; faith must appear in the heart, conduct, trust, and one’s relation with truth.

Amn speaks of security and calm from fear. Āmana and yu’minu speak of believing, trusting, and affirming truth. Īmān speaks of deep inward and practical faith. Mu’min speaks of the human being who enters a secure and trustworthy relation with truth. Amānah and amānāt speak of entrusted responsibility. Amīn speaks of trustworthiness. Ma’man speaks of a place of safety and refuge. Āminīn speaks of a state of security. And muhaymin, in semantic connection, points to guarding and preserving the trust of truth.

Mu’min and muslim are not identical. The Qur’an shows that one may have submitted outwardly while faith has not yet entered the heart. Faith is a deeper rank; it must settle in the heart and appear as trustworthy action.

Also, believers by the Qur’anic criterion are not merely a closed group-name. Whoever turns to God, the Last Day, and righteous action, and arranges his relation with truth through trust, responsibility, and fidelity, enters the field of faith.

So yu’minūn means those who do not merely claim faith, but continually make it alive and renewed through trust, affirmation, entrusted responsibility, and moral accountability.
