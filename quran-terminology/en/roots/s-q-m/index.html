The Arabic root S Q M concerns illness and infirmity, a condition in which normal bodily health and functioning are impaired. Saqima means “became ill”; suqm and saqam mean illness; and saqīm means ill or infirm.

This family is semantically close to M R Ḍ, but their uses are not identical. Maraḍ can concern bodily illness or a diseased heart and inner condition, whereas the primary, familiar sense of saqam is more specifically bodily ill health.

Literary usage can extend the word to inner suffering, lovesickness, or even unsound reasoning. Such uses should not replace the basic meaning of illness without contextual evidence.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran, both times as saqīmun and both in Al-Ṣāffāt.

First, Abraham looks toward the stars and says innī saqīm: “I am ill.”

Second, Jonah is cast onto open ground after being brought out of the fish, while saqīm: ill and infirm.

The word has the same form in both verses, but different settings. Jonah's illness is part of his condition after an ordeal. Abraham declares illness while opposing his people's worship of fabricated deities.

The lexical sense is clear, but the illness's kind, cause, and the speaker's intention require separate contextual consideration.

1. The root in early Arabic

Illness as opposed to health

Arabic lexical usage attests al-suqm, al-saqam, and al-saqām as names for illness.

Saqima or saquma describes someone becoming ill or being unwell.

By contrast, ṣiḥḥah and ṣaḥīḥ concern health and soundness.

In this contrast, saqam is not simply an unpleasant feeling or passing discomfort; it denotes impaired health.

The word does not identify a particular illness.

Someone with fever, illness-related weakness, pain, or prolonged illness may be called saqīm.

None of these is the root's exclusive meaning. Saqīm names no particular disease; it is a general description of someone who is unwell.

Some lexical explanations associate saqam with prolonged illness. This use does not establish that everyone called saqīm necessarily had a chronic disease.

The basic definition is therefore:

Saqam is illness or ill health; saqīm describes someone whose health is impaired and who is ill or infirm.

A witness attributed to a pre-Islamic poet

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, reads:

أَرِقْتُ وَمَا هَذَا السُّهَادُ الْمُؤَرِّقُ
وَمَا بِيَ مِنْ سُقْمٍ وَمَا بِيَ مَعْشَقُ

ariqtu wa-mā hādhā al-suhādu al-muʾarriqu / wa-mā biya min suqmin wa-mā biya maʿshaqu

“I have lain awake: what is this tormenting sleeplessness? I have no illness, nor am I troubled by love.”

The poet speaks of sleeplessness while denying two possible causes: illness and lovesickness.

Suqm here names a condition that can cause sleeplessness and discomfort.

The witness matters because it distinguishes illness, lovesickness, and sleeplessness itself.

Suqm is not a synonym for every sadness or restlessness here; illness is one possible cause of those states.

The verse is preserved in the poetic tradition attributed to al-Aʿshā and associated in literary accounts with an episode in his pre-Islamic life.

Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet alone cannot establish the precise date of composition or original wording of every line. The witness matters for early usage, but the historical claim should remain within those limits.

Another witness: illness and restored health

Another verse attributed to al-Aʿshā reads:

أَوْ كَمَا قَالُوا سَقِيمٌ فَلَئِنْ
نَفَضَ الْأَسْقَامَ عَنْهُ وَاسْتَصَحّْ

aw kamā qālū saqīmun fa-la-in / nafaḍa al-asqāma ʿanhu wa-istaṣaḥḥ

“Or, as they say, he is ill; yet if he shakes off his illnesses and becomes healthy again…”

Saqīm is contrasted with istaṣaḥḥ in this verse.

Istaṣaḥḥ concerns regaining or enjoying health; nafaḍa al-asqāma ʿanhu pictures shaking off illnesses.

Illness is placed opposite health in this poetic image. The isolated verse does not independently establish the person's actual medical condition.

The description saqīm is therefore not necessarily permanent or irreversible: someone may be ill and later recover.

The verse's precise date is also not independently established, but its meaning agrees with attested lexical usage.

Does saqam concern only the body?

One lexical distinction treats saqam as bodily illness, while maraḍ can concern both body and inner self.

This distinction usefully identifies the word's predominant sense.

Arabic nevertheless allows figurative and extended usage.

Literary language can speak of an afflicted heart or an unsound idea.

Illness is then applied to something that has departed from a sound condition, by analogy with the body.

An unsound idea has a flaw in reasoning or outcome; it does not literally suffer fever or bodily disease.

Makānun saqīm is also attested for an unsafe or frightening place.

Here illness is not literal bodily disease; an undesirable, harmful condition is described.

Such figurative uses do not justify rendering saqīm as moral, religious, or psychological distress in every Quranic occurrence.

The direct, familiar meaning is illness. A figurative interpretation requires evidence from the sentence or context.

2. Forms and derivatives

S Q M occurs twice in the Quran, both times in the same form:

Saqīmun — Al-Ṣāffāt 37:89 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145

Saqīm is an adjective on the faʿīl pattern, formed from saqima.

Both occurrences are masculine singular and indefinite.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:89, saqīmun is the predicate of inna in innī saqīm.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145, it is the predicate of huwa in wa-huwa saqīm.

In each, it describes a person's condition.

The faʿīl pattern alone does not make the illness permanent, severe, contagious, or incurable.

Arabic adjectives on this pattern can describe states that last for a time and then cease.

Other forms are also attested in Arabic.

Saqima yasqamu and saquma yasqumu mean becoming ill or being ill.

Suqm, saqam, saqām, and saqāmah are nominal forms related to illness.

Saqim and saqīm describe an ill person.

Misqām describes someone frequently or repeatedly ill.

Asqama and saqqama concern making someone ill or causing illness.

Musqam describes someone made ill.

Siqām is also attested as a plural of saqīm, referring to ill people.

Of these forms, only saqīmun occurs in the Quran.

Saqām is also recorded as the name of a valley in the Hijaz, and sawqam as a tree name. These names alone do not create new meanings for illness and should not be derived from saqīm without independent etymological evidence.

3. Innī saqīm: Abraham's statement

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:89 states:

فَقَالَ إِنِّي سَقِيمٌ

fa-qāla innī saqīm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:89

“Then he said: ‘I am ill.’”

These are Abraham's reported words.

Their intention should be considered alongside the preceding and following verses.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:84 introduces Abraham with qalbin salīm, a sound heart.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:85, he asks his father and people what they worship.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:86, he challenges their worship of fabricated deities.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:88 states:

فَنَظَرَ نَظْرَةً فِي النُّجُومِ

fa-naẓara naẓratan fī al-nujūm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:88

“Then he cast a glance at the stars.”

The next verse reports innī saqīm.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:90 states:

فَتَوَلَّوْا عَنْهُ مُدْبِرِينَ

fa-tawallaw ʿanhu mudbirīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:90

“Then they turned away from him, retreating.”

Abraham subsequently approaches their deities, exposes their inability to eat or speak, and strikes them.

His statement of illness thus occurs within opposition to idol worship, before he approaches the deities after his people depart.

Was Abraham actually ill?

Innī saqīm directly presents Abraham as declaring himself ill.

The verse does not specify symptoms, the illness's kind, or its duration.

Several readings have therefore been proposed.

On the first, Abraham was actually ill or unwell.

This agrees with saqīm's direct meaning.

The absence of a symptom description does not rule it out: a narrative need not list every sign of someone's illness.

On a second reading, Abraham speaks of inner distress and dissatisfaction at his people's idol worship.

This attends to the context of opposition to their fabricated deities.

Saqīm is then used figuratively: a person is distressed at seeing wrongful conduct.

But the sentence does not directly state this. It does not say “I am sick of your idol worship” or “my heart is distressed by your conduct.”

This can be an interpretive possibility, but should not replace “I am ill” in the direct translation without evidence.

Did looking at the stars predict illness?

Some early explanations suggest Abraham used the stars to predict the time of an illness or knew when a fever would recur.

The Quran itself presents two consecutive events: looking at the stars and saying “I am ill.”

It does not state that he diagnosed his illness from the stars, predicted its timing, or judged the future through celestial movements.

Such details require independent evidence.

Conversely, the glance at the stars should not be ignored. It belongs to the scene and may relate to the conversation and Abraham's subsequent decision.

Its precise role does not follow from S Q M.

The verse alone thus establishes neither the legitimacy nor reliability of predicting illness from stars; nor should the glance be dismissed as unrelated to the next sentence without examination.

Did Abraham make an untrue statement?

Some transmitted accounts and interpretations describe innī saqīm as untrue, as though Abraham used illness as a pretext not to accompany his people.

This Quranic passage does not itself say that he lied.

It reports his words and then his people's departure.

Whether he was physically ill, spoke of inner distress, or used an expression with multiple senses to further his purpose requires separate examination.

A reading should not be treated as a certain, unspoken fact of the verse merely because it appears in extratextual accounts. Assessing those accounts' evidence is a separate matter.

Defending Abraham's character does not require denying the clear meaning of saqīm or excluding every possibility of bodily illness.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:84 mentions a sound heart; Al-Ṣāffāt 37:89 reports saqīm.

A sound heart in faith and moral orientation is compatible with bodily illness.

Someone can have a sound heart and upright conduct while physically ill.

The apparent contrast between salīm and saqīm should therefore not be turned into a contradiction requiring an invented account outside the Quran.

Illness and moral character

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80, Abraham says:

وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ

wa-idhā mariḍtu fa-huwa yashfīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80

“And when I am ill, it is He who heals me.”

This verse uses M R Ḍ, not S Q M.

It nevertheless matters for understanding illness in prophets' lives.

Abraham speaks of illness and healing. Becoming ill is therefore not inherently incompatible with prophethood or spiritual soundness.

This does not prove that he was ill in the particular situation of Al-Ṣāffāt 37:89, but it shows that prophethood alone cannot rule out bodily illness.

“I am ill” is the appropriate basic translation for Forqan.

The precise condition or intention behind the statement is examined contextually, within the limits of the evidence.

4. Wa-huwa saqīm: Jonah's illness

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145 states:

فَنَبَذْنَاهُ بِالْعَرَاءِ وَهُوَ سَقِيمٌ

fa-nabadhnāhu bi-al-ʿarāʾi wa-huwa saqīm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145

“Then We cast him onto open ground while he was ill.”

This belongs to Jonah's story.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140 describes his departure toward a laden ship.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141 recounts the casting of lots.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142 states:

فَالْتَقَمَهُ الْحُوتُ وَهُوَ مُلِيمٌ

fa-ltaqamahu al-ḥūtu wa-huwa mulīm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142

“Then the fish swallowed him, while he was blameworthy.”

The account then speaks of his glorification and rescue, and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145 describes his condition upon being brought out of the fish.

Saqīm in this verse

In wa-huwa saqīm, huwa refers to Jonah and saqīm describes his condition.

The opening wāw links that condition to the preceding event as a circumstantial clause.

Thus he was ill when cast onto open ground.

Bodily illness is the clear, natural reading here.

Jonah had undergone an ordeal and is described as ill and infirm upon emerging from it.

The Quran nevertheless does not identify a specific disease.

It does not diagnose fever, a skin condition, digestive injury, dehydration, or another particular illness.

Such proposals go beyond the lexical statement; none is contained in saqīm itself.

The verse should therefore not be used to make a medical diagnosis.

Mulīm and saqīm

The distinction between two descriptions of Jonah matters.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142 calls him mulīmun.

This belongs to L W M and concerns blame and responsibility in that setting.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145 instead calls him saqīmun.

That concerns his illness and ill health.

Mulīm and saqīm are therefore not synonyms.

One concerns responsibility and blame; the other describes illness.

Their sequence does not establish that every illness results from the patient's sin or blameworthiness.

The story uses distinct words for different conditions. Their precise relationship must be understood from the account, not a fabricated rule making illness invariably punishment for moral failure.

Illness after rescue

Rescue from grave danger does not necessarily remove all physical effects immediately.

Someone may survive a life-threatening situation while remaining weak, injured, or ill, needing time and suitable conditions to recover.

Jonah too is rescued, yet remains saqīm when cast onto the ground.

Rescue from the fish and immediate full health are therefore distinct matters.

The verse does not explain the illness's duration, how recovery occurred, or its stages.

The yaqṭīn plant and Jonah's condition

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:146 states:

وَأَنْبَتْنَا عَلَيْهِ شَجَرَةً مِنْ يَقْطِينٍ

wa-anbatnā ʿalayhi shajaratan min yaqṭīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:146

“And We caused a plant of yaqṭīn to grow over him.”

This follows the description of Jonah as saqīm.

The plant's growth therefore occurs in the context of his condition after emerging from the fish.

A plant can provide shade, food, or other benefits. This phrase does not explicitly say that it was grown to treat a particular illness.

Yaqṭīn itself does not establish a medicine, treatment method, or definite medical property relevant to Jonah's condition.

The connection is clear: Jonah is ill, and the narrative then describes a plant growing over him.

Its precise effect on his body is not detailed in the text.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:147 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:148 describe his mission and the subsequent account of his people.

Illness is thus a condition during part of his life, not a description determining his entire identity or ultimate outcome.

5. Comparison with related roots

S Q M and M R Ḍ: illness and ill health

The closest semantic family is M R Ḍ.

Both can describe human illness in Arabic.

Maraḍ has a broader Quranic range, however.

Al-Baqarah 2:10 mentions a diseased heart in a context of human conduct and inner condition.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80 attributes illness to Abraham and contrasts it with healing.

Maraḍ can therefore concern the body or the heart and moral or psychological condition.

Saqam's familiar sense is more specifically bodily illness.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary; literary saqam can also express nonphysical suffering or disorder.

That possibility does not establish a necessarily figurative meaning for Quranic saqīm.

S Q M and W H N: illness and weakness

Wahn concerns weakness and frailty.

In Maryam 19:4, Zechariah speaks of his bones growing weak.

Weakness may accompany old age, illness, fatigue, or other conditions.

Not every weakness or incapacity is necessarily illness.

Conversely, illness need not immediately produce an obvious loss of ability.

Saqīm and wāhin should therefore not be treated as complete synonyms.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145 calls Jonah saqīm. His illness may have involved weakness, but the basic translation should not reduce the adjective to “unable” or “weak.”

S Q M and N Ṣ B: illness and fatigue

The Quranic family N Ṣ B includes suffering, fatigue, and exhaustion associated with effort or hardship.

Al-Kahf 18:62 expresses travel fatigue with naṣaban.

Fatigue and illness are distinct.

Someone may tire after strenuous activity and regain ordinary strength through rest.

Illness, by contrast, may not resolve after a brief rest.

Illness and fatigue can of course coexist.

Saqīm therefore should not invariably become “tired” or “exhausted.”

S Q M and Ḍ R R: illness and harm

Ḍarar has a broad range, including loss, harm, and affliction.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:83, Job speaks of affliction reaching him.

Harm can be physical, financial, social, or of another kind.

Saqam directly concerns illness and ill health.

Illness may involve harm and suffering, but not every loss or harm is an illness.

Jonah's saqīm should therefore not become the general “harmed,” even though his illness may have followed a harmful ordeal.

S Q M and SH F Y: illness and healing

SH F Y concerns healing and the removal of illness.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80 places illness and healing in one sentence.

Saqam expresses ill health; shifāʾ concerns recovery from illness.

The meanings complement each other.

But saqīm itself promises no recovery, just as it determines neither duration nor outcome.

Jonah's later life and mission are described, but the precise stages of his healing are not reported.

S Q M and S L M: illness and soundness

Salāmah concerns being sound and unharmed.

The Quran also uses the family in moral and spiritual contexts.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:84 describes Abraham as having qalbin salīm, a sound heart.

A few verses later, he says innī saqīm.

This juxtaposition shows why a sound heart should not be equated with perfect bodily health.

Abraham's sound heart concerns his inner orientation, whereas saqīm directly describes illness.

Both expressions can therefore apply to one person without contradiction.

The similar sounds of salīm and saqīm do not make them one root: they belong to S L M and S Q M respectively.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The natural Persian equivalent of saqīm is bīmār, “ill.”

Ranjūr, “infirm” or “ailing,” can also fit, especially when the setting emphasizes someone's difficult condition.

Ranjūr can extend beyond bodily illness to sorrow or inner exhaustion in Persian. Bīmār is therefore clearer for a direct Quranic translation.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:89:

«فَقَالَ إِنِّي سَقِيمٌ»

fa-qāla innī saqīm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:89

Suggested translation:

“Then he said: ‘I am ill.’”

This preserves the direct meaning without predetermining the illness or Abraham's possible intention.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145:

«فَنَبَذْنَاهُ بِالْعَرَاءِ وَهُوَ سَقِيمٌ»

fa-nabadhnāhu bi-al-ʿarāʾi wa-huwa saqīm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145

Suggested translation:

“Then We cast him onto open ground while he was ill.”

“Ill” is likewise suitable here; the surrounding verbs convey Jonah's particular circumstances.

Other lexical equivalents follow, with Quranic saqīm included for comparison:

Saqima and saquma: became ill or was ill.

Suqm and saqam: illness.

Saqām: illness or infirmity.

Saqīm: ill, infirm.

Misqām: frequently ill or repeatedly becoming ill.

Asqamahu al-dāʾu: the disease made him ill.

Musqam: made ill.

Without evidence, Quranic saqīm should not become “mentally ill,” “diseased in belief,” “feverish,” “chronically ill,” or “unable to move.”

These identify particular illnesses or go beyond the direct meaning.

“I will soon become ill” is not temporally identical to “I am ill.” The nominal sentence has no explicit future marker, so the latter is the natural basic translation. A future-oriented reading may be discussed separately, with contextual evidence.

Conclusion

S Q M concerns illness and ill health.

Saqīm describes someone ill; suqm, saqam, and saqām are attested names for illness.

Early poetic witnesses support this meaning. Al-Aʿshā distinguishes illness, lovesickness, and sleeplessness, while another attributed verse contrasts being ill with regaining health.

The basic meaning is clear and requires no invented image or philosophical sense.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran, both times as saqīmun.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:89, Abraham declares himself ill during his opposition to idol worship, but the verse does not specify the illness or his precise intention.

He should not be called a liar without evidence; nor should saqīm's direct meaning be discarded simply to exclude bodily illness.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145, Jonah is cast onto the ground while ill after emerging from the fish. His physical condition is described, but not a particular diagnosis or treatment.

His illness should not be equated with blame or sin merely because he was earlier called mulīm. Physical condition and moral responsibility are distinct.

A concluding definition is therefore:

S Q M denotes illness and ill health, a condition of impaired bodily health. Saqīm describes an ill or infirm person without specifying the illness's kind, cause, severity, duration, or outcome. Figurative use for inner distress or nonphysical disorder is possible, but requires independent contextual evidence.

“Ill” is the most appropriate basic equivalent for both Quranic occurrences in Forqan.

Illness itself makes no judgment about a person's moral worth.

Abraham is introduced with a sound heart and says “I am ill” in the same account. Jonah remains described as ill after rescue from grave danger.

Spiritual soundness, rescue, and bodily health are related but distinct. Someone can be upright in faith and conduct while ill, or survive danger yet need time and care to regain health.

These distinctions prevent treating people's illnesses, without Quranic evidence, as signs of sin, divine displeasure, or inferiority.
