The Qur’anic Root B R K (Barakah, Mubārak, and Tabāraka)

Enduring and abundant good; from a camel settling down and water remaining in a pool to blessed land, people, scripture, and the exaltation of The God

Qur’anic and lexical forms

بَارَكْنَا
(bāraknā)
Active verb; six Qur’anic occurrences

بَارَكَ
(bāraka)
Third-person active verb; Fuṣṣilat 41:10

بُورِكَ
(būrika)
Passive verb; Al-Naml 27:8

تَبَارَكَ
(tabāraka)
Nine Qur’anic occurrences in praise

بَرَكَات / بَرَكَاتُهُ
(barakāt / barakātuhu)
Sound feminine plural, with and without a pronoun; three occurrences

مُبَارَك
(mubārak)
Masculine passive participial description; eight occurrences

مُبَارَكَة
(mubārakah)
Feminine form; four occurrences

بَرَكَ الْبَعِيرُ
(baraka al-baʿīru)
Lexical use for a camel settling; this form is not Qur’anic

بَرَكَة
(barakah)
Lexical singular citation form; not found in this form in the Qur’an

بِرْكَة
(birkah)
Lexical pool or basin; not Qur’anic

بَرَاكَاءُ الْقِتَال
(barākāʾ al-qitāl)
Poetic persistence in battle; not Qur’anic

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

Barakah in Arabic denotes good and an abundance of benefit that is placed in something, remains in it, or arises from it. This good may appear in provision and crops, come through useful knowledge, affect human life, or relate to a particular time or place.

Qur’anic blessing is more than an increase in quantity. A book can be blessed, a person can be made blessed, land can receive blessing, and water descending from the sky can be called blessed. Barakah cannot therefore be limited to more money, larger harvests, or more children.

Nor should its meaning become so broad that every pleasant experience or success is necessarily treated as a sign of divine blessing without regard to context and evidence.

B R K occurs 32 times in the Qur’an. The corpus records five groups: eight instances of the form III verb bāraka, nine of form VI tabāraka, three of the sound feminine plural barakāt, four of mubārakah, and eight of mubārak.

The form III verbs include seven active instances and one passive, būrika. Mubārak and mubārakah are structurally passive participial descriptions in this family.

The Qur’an does not use the simple expression بَرَكَ الْبَعِيرُ, “the camel settled down,” or the singular noun بَرَكَة in that uninflected citation form. Its occurrences concern good and blessing, conferring blessing, and exalting The God.

Bāraka and tabāraka require different translations. Bāraka concerns bestowing blessing on someone or something; Qur’anic tabāraka praises and exalts The God and cannot be reduced to increasing possessions or receiving blessing from another.

A creature’s being mubārak must likewise be distinguished from tabāraka concerning The God. A creature can receive the good and blessing that The God establishes; The God is presented as its giver and source, rather than as a recipient of blessing from outside Himself.

2. The basic Arabic field: stability, settling, and abundant good

A camel settling and remaining in place

In Maqāyīs al-lughah, Ibn Fāris introduces the root’s semantic core as follows:

«الْبَاءُ وَالرَّاءُ وَالْكَافُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ ثَبَاتُ الشَّيْءِ»

al-bāʾu wa-al-rāʾu wa-al-kāfu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa thabātu al-shayʾi

Translation: “Bāʾ, rāʾ, and kāf have one underlying sense: something remaining firmly in place.”

He then gives بَرَكَ الْبَعِيرُ يَبْرُكُ بُرُوكًا as a familiar use: the camel settled down and remained in its place.

Bark also denotes the front of the camel’s body, especially the chest that reaches the ground when it settles. Its resting place is called a mabrak.

The image comes from the everyday life of camel keepers. After moving, a camel lowers its chest and forequarters to the ground and settles. It keeps that position until it rises.

The use has a clear linguistic connection with settling and remaining.

The physical action alone does not prove that every sense involving good and blessing historically developed from it. Ibn Fāris explains these uses within a semantic family; an etymological explanation is not always conclusive evidence of the historical order in which meanings arose.

Birkah: water collecting and remaining

Birkah, with an initial i vowel, denotes a hollow or pool in which water gathers and remains.

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad, as quoted by Ibn Fāris, describes a hollow made in the ground to collect water. The report speaks of pools without high walls built above ground level.

Other uses apply the same name to constructed basins and reservoirs for retaining water.

The connection with stability is clear: water that might have passed through a place collects there and remains for a time.

The image makes the connection between continuance and beneficial good tangible. In a dry environment, stored water enables drinking, watering animals, and irrigation.

It does not follow that Qur’anic barakah means a reservoir, groundwater, or an accumulation of material. The pool is a concrete use of the family; divine blessing is another established meaning with linguistic and visual connections to it.

Barakah: good that endures and grows

Lisān al-ʿArab describes barakah through al-namāʾ wa-al-ziyādah, “growth and increase.” Other explanations in the same family emphasize the continuance and endurance of what has been given.

In al-Mufradāt, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī defines barakah as follows:

«الْبَرَكَةُ: ثُبُوتُ الْخَيْرِ الْإِلَهِيِّ فِي الشَّيْءِ»

al-barakatu thubūtu al-khayri al-ilāhiyyi fī al-shayʾi

Translation: “Barakah is divine good becoming established in something.”

He calls something mubārak when this good is present in it. He also explains that divine good may appear in ways whose amount or manner people cannot fully anticipate.

The definition helps with reading the Qur’an, but two points should be distinguished.

The establishment and increase of good are related dimensions. Good may remain, its effects may spread, or its beneficial use may continue.

Not every numerical increase is blessing. Someone’s wealth may grow through oppression and corruption. Material abundance alone does not establish the blessing praised in the Qur’an.

Conversely, something small in quantity can bring lasting benefit to its owner and others.

In religious usage, barakah is therefore best understood through established, beneficial, and abundant good, without forcing all these features into every verse translation as a compulsory formula.

3. Older uses: resting camels and persistence in battle

Burūk and resting camels

After explaining a camel’s settling, Maqāyīs al-lughah quotes this rajaz:

بَرْكُ هُجُودٍ بِفَلَاةٍ قَفْرِ

barku hujūdin bi-falātin qafri

أَحْمَى عَلَيْهَا الشَّمْسَ أَبْتُ الْحَرِّ

aḥmā ʿalayhā al-shamsa abtu al-ḥarri

Translation: “Resting camels in a barren wilderness, exposed to the sun’s fierce, windless heat.”

Bark here denotes camels settled in the wilderness. Hujūd describes their resting or repose.

Ibn Fāris quotes the rajaz among Arabic usages, but the report does not identify its poet or precise date. It is a witness preserved in the lexical tradition, rather than a verse securely assigned to a particular pre-Islamic poet.

It illustrates familiar camel behavior and its vocabulary. The camel has settled and remained in place; the description itself contains no religious or theological meaning of blessing.

Barākāʾ al-qitāl: persistence in battle

A poem attributed to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim contains the following verse:

وَلَا يُنْجِي مِنَ الْغَمَرَاتِ إِلَّا

wa-lā yunjī mina al-ghamarāti illā

بَرَاكَاءُ الْقِتَالِ أَوِ الْفِرَارُ

barākāʾu al-qitāli awi al-firāru

Translation: “Nothing saves one from the engulfing perils except standing firm in battle or fleeing.”

The verse is transmitted in Bishr’s Dīwān, Maqāyīs al-lughah, and Khizānat al-adab. Its lexical explanation treats barākāʾ al-qitāl as fierce engagement in which fighters hold their position and continue fighting.

The word now concerns remaining and persisting in battle rather than a camel resting.

It shows that forms in B R K also expressed remaining established in a situation.

The word itself does not mean courage, victory, or virtue. The poet contrasts two courses: persistence in battle and flight. The circumstances and the fighters’ conduct determine the action’s ethical significance and outcome.

The witness helps identify a linguistic connection with stability and persistence, but does not make Qur’anic barakah mean fighting or military endurance.

Continuing with an undertaking

Lexical sources record بَرَكَ فُلَانٌ عَلَى الْأَمْرِ for someone who persists in an undertaking and continues it.

The sense is close to a settled camel and a fighter holding position: all three involve some form of stability or persistence.

Remaining in something must nevertheless be distinguished from its being good.

One person may persist in beneficial work and another in harmful conduct. Persistence alone does not establish whether the activity is right or wrong.

Goodness should therefore not be extracted solely from sitting and remaining. Religious barakah concerns good and benefit as well as continuance.

Other uses distinct from divine blessing

Older Arabic uses birkah for a water-retaining hollow, mabrak for a camel’s resting place, and bark for its forequarters. Some sources also record birkah for milking at a particular time of day and ibtaraka al-saḥāb for rain persisting over one place.

These uses reveal the family’s range, but none alone defines divine blessing.

For understanding the Qur’an, the relevant connection is that good and benefit can endure, remain established, and increase. This connection should not become a fixed physical image or a conclusive theory of how The God acts.

4. Bāraka and barakāt: good in land, people, and life

Blessings from heaven and earth

Al-Aʿrāf 7:96

وَلَوْ أَنَّ أَهْلَ الْقُرَىٰ آمَنُوا وَاتَّقَوْا لَفَتَحْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ بَرَكَاتٍ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَلَٰكِنْ كَذَّبُوا فَأَخَذْنَاهُمْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ

wa-law anna ahla al-qurā āmanū wa-ittaqaw la-fataḥnā ʿalayhim barakātin mina al-samāʾi wa-al-arḍi wa-lākin kadhdhabū fa-akhadhnāhum bi-mā kānū yaksibūna

Translation: “Had the people of the settlements believed and practiced taqwā, We would surely have opened blessings from heaven and earth upon them. But they denied, so We seized them for what they had been earning.”

Barakāt is the plural of barakah and denotes multiple kinds of good made available from heaven and earth.

The verse connects blessing with belief and taqwā. Barakāt itself does not mean either of these; they appear as conditions in the sentence.

“From heaven and earth” also identifies sources or settings of the people’s benefit.

Rain, vegetation, crops, and other resources of life can exemplify such good. The verse’s blessing should not be restricted to increased rainfall or harvests alone.

Nor should the verse become a simple formula according to which every wealthy society necessarily has belief and taqwā, or every poor society has necessarily suffered divine punishment.

It states a conditional relationship concerning the people in question. The word barakah alone cannot explain all economic, climatic, and historical differences between societies.

People may face drought, war, or others’ oppression despite their effort, integrity, and taqwā. Other Qur’anic verses also discuss life’s trials and differences in human circumstances.

Divine blessing should consequently not become a simple measure for judging the moral worth of rich and needy people.

Blessings upon Noah and communities after him

Hūd 11:48

قِيلَ يَا نُوحُ اهْبِطْ بِسَلَامٍ مِنَّا وَبَرَكَاتٍ عَلَيْكَ وَعَلَىٰ أُمَمٍ مِمَّنْ مَعَكَ ۚ وَأُمَمٌ سَنُمَتِّعُهُمْ ثُمَّ يَمَسُّهُمْ مِنَّا عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

qīla yā nūḥu ihbiṭ bi-salāmin minnā wa-barakātin ʿalayka wa-ʿalā umamin mimman maʿaka wa-umamun sa-numattiʿuhum thumma yamassuhum minnā ʿadhābun alīmun

Translation: “It was said: ‘Noah, descend with peace from Us and blessings upon you and upon communities from those with you. Other communities We shall grant enjoyment, then a painful punishment from Us will touch them.’”

Blessings appear alongside peace here. The pairing connects blessing with security and well-being, but the terms are not identical.

Peace and safety concern freedom from harm; blessings concern the good bestowed upon Noah and groups associated with his companions.

The verse’s ending matters too. It mentions other communities that receive worldly enjoyment but eventually face punishment.

Worldly enjoyment should therefore not be equated without evidence with the blessing praised in the Qur’an.

Blessing upon Noah and groups of his companions likewise does not exempt all their later descendants from independent moral judgment merely because of lineage. The verse itself distinguishes groups with different outcomes.

Blessings upon Abraham’s household

Hūd 11:73

قَالُوا أَتَعْجَبِينَ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ ۖ رَحْمَتُ اللَّهِ وَبَرَكَاتُهُ عَلَيْكُمْ أَهْلَ الْبَيْتِ ۚ إِنَّهُ حَمِيدٌ مَجِيدٌ

qālū a-taʿjabīna min amri llāhi raḥmatu llāhi wa-barakātuhu ʿalaykum ahla al-bayti innahu ḥamīdun majīdun

Translation: “They said: ‘Are you astonished at the command of The God? The mercy of The God and His blessings be upon you, people of this house. He is indeed praiseworthy and glorious.’”

Barakātuhu is the plural barakāt with the pronoun -hu: “His blessings.”

The blessings accompany mercy and are directed to the household being addressed.

Mercy and blessing are close concepts, but the Qur’an expresses them through independent nouns. Translation should neither omit one nor reduce both to a general word such as “happiness.”

Mercy can concern divine kindness and compassion, and blessing the good and benefit He establishes. Their distinction can be studied across the usages, while allowing overlap in their effects and instances.

Blessing upon a household does not mean that every future relative of that family will necessarily be righteous.

Another verse concerning Abraham and Isaac makes this boundary more explicit.

Blessing Abraham and Isaac without guaranteeing every descendant’s goodness

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:113

وَبَارَكْنَا عَلَيْهِ وَعَلَىٰ إِسْحَاقَ ۚ وَمِنْ ذُرِّيَّتِهِمَا مُحْسِنٌ وَظَالِمٌ لِنَفْسِهِ مُبِينٌ

wa-bāraknā ʿalayhi wa-ʿalā isḥāqa wa-min dhurriyyatihimā muḥsinun wa-ẓālimun li-nafsihi mubīnun

Translation: “We bestowed blessing upon him and upon Isaac. Among their descendants are those who do good and those who plainly wrong themselves.”

This is among the clearest Qur’anic witnesses to the limits of blessing.

The God speaks of blessing Abraham and Isaac, then immediately states that their descendants include both a doer of good and someone plainly wronging himself.

Blessing upon the two Prophets is thus no unconditional guarantee of goodness for all their children and later descendants.

Lexically, bāraknā ʿalayhi expresses bestowing blessing. The descendants’ goodness or wrongdoing is stated in the following independent clause.

The distinction prevents blessing from becoming an immutable hereditary privilege.

Nor does the verse establish that divine blessing had no effect. It shows that the presence of blessing and people’s moral responsibility are distinct matters.

5. Bāraknā fīhā: blessing land and particular places

Blessed land in Abraham and Lot’s story

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:71

وَنَجَّيْنَاهُ وَلُوطًا إِلَى الْأَرْضِ الَّتِي بَارَكْنَا فِيهَا لِلْعَالَمِينَ

wa-najjaynāhu wa-lūṭan ilā al-arḍi allatī bāraknā fīhā li-al-ʿālamīna

Translation: “We rescued him and Lot to the land in which We had placed blessing for all peoples.”

Bāraknā is a form III perfect verb with The God as its agent. Fīhā refers to the land, and li-al-ʿālamīn expresses the reach of the benefit.

Historical and exegetical sources generally identify the land with the Levant and Palestine, toward which Abraham and Lot migrated.

That geographical identification is not part of B R K’s lexical meaning. Blessing concerns good and benefit; the location is identified through the narrative and historical evidence.

The verse speaks of the land’s blessing “for all peoples,” rather than a privilege reserved exclusively for one community.

The blessing alone establishes neither one community’s perpetual political ownership, a right to oppress other inhabitants, nor the inhabitants’ moral immunity. Such claims do not follow from bāraknā.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:81 uses the same expression for the land toward which the wind flowed at Solomon’s command.

These uses make blessed land a meaningful Qur’anic description, but neither identify an owning community within the word nor determine every kind of benefit from the root alone. Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 also describes the eastern and western parts of blessed land while recounting the inheritance of a people subjected to weakness and their perseverance; blessing must be distinguished from those separately stated events and qualities.

The surroundings of al-Masjid al-Aqṣā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:1

سُبْحَانَ الَّذِي أَسْرَىٰ بِعَبْدِهِ لَيْلًا مِنَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ إِلَى الْمَسْجِدِ الْأَقْصَى الَّذِي بَارَكْنَا حَوْلَهُ لِنُرِيَهُ مِنْ آيَاتِنَا ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْبَصِيرُ

subḥāna alladhī asrā bi-ʿabdihi laylan mina al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi ilā al-masjidi al-aqṣā alladhī bāraknā ḥawlahu li-nuriyahu min āyātinā innahu huwa al-samīʿu al-baṣīru

Translation: “Glorified is the One who took His servant by night from al-Masjid al-Ḥarām to al-Masjid al-Aqṣā, whose surroundings We have blessed, to show him some of Our signs. He is indeed the Hearing, the Seeing.”

Bāraknā is accompanied here by ḥawlahu, “around it.”

This differs from bāraknā fīhā. The blessed-land verses locate blessing within the land; Al-Isrāʾ 17:1 explicitly identifies the surroundings of al-Masjid al-Aqṣā as the area blessed.

A direct translation should therefore retain “whose surroundings We have blessed” rather than omit ḥawlahu and simply say “the blessed mosque.”

This grammatical distinction does not deny other good or inviolability associated with the mosque; it preserves what this sentence explicitly says.

Blessing the surroundings also does not imply that oppression, war, or corruption cannot occur there. A land’s blessing does not automatically make every action of its inhabitants right.

Settlements and safe routes

Sabaʾ 34:18 mentions settlements between the people of Sabaʾ and the blessed settlements. The same verse speaks of measured traveling stages and safe journeys by night and day.

A land’s blessing can thus accompany people’s practical benefits, settlement, and links between regions.

The association does not make barakah itself mean road safety, a transport network, or urban development.

Those features are stated separately in the verse’s context.

Nor does the verse supply a conclusive map of every historical town and route without independent geographical evidence.

Blessing the earth alongside its sustenance

Fuṣṣilat 41:10

وَجَعَلَ فِيهَا رَوَاسِيَ مِنْ فَوْقِهَا وَبَارَكَ فِيهَا وَقَدَّرَ فِيهَا أَقْوَاتَهَا فِي أَرْبَعَةِ أَيَّامٍ سَوَاءً لِلسَّائِلِينَ

wa-jaʿala fīhā rawāsiya min fawqihā wa-bāraka fīhā wa-qaddara fīhā aqwātahā fī arbaʿati ayyāmin sawāʾan li-al-sāʾilīna

Translation: “He placed firmly set mountains upon it from above, bestowed blessing within it, and apportioned its sustenance in four days, equally for those who ask.”

Three verbs appear together: placing mountains, blessing the earth, and apportioning its sustenance.

The connection with aqwātahā, its sustenance, matters because food and provision are clear examples of the earth’s good.

Qūt and barakah nevertheless remain different words. Bāraka alone does not mean apportioning, mountains, or food.

The earth’s blessing may concern beneficial capacities and possibilities for life; the number of days and manner of creation must be understood from other parts of the verse.

Blessing at Moses’ call: būrika

Al-Naml 27:8

فَلَمَّا جَاءَهَا نُودِيَ أَنْ بُورِكَ مَنْ فِي النَّارِ وَمَنْ حَوْلَهَا وَسُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

fa-lammā jāʾahā nūdiya an būrika man fī al-nāri wa-man ḥawlahā wa-subḥāna llāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīna

Translation: “When he reached it, a call was made: ‘Blessing has been bestowed upon whoever is in the fire and whoever is around it. Glorified is The God, Lord of all worlds.’”

Būrika is a form III perfect passive verb, the only passive instance of this verbal form in the Qur’anic B R K family.

The recipients of blessing are identified through “whoever is in the fire” and “whoever is around it.”

Older exegesis differs over the precise referent of “whoever is in the fire.”

Al-Ṭabarī transmits several explanations: some connect the fire with light, and reports differ in identifying the angels and Moses and their relation to the fire and its surroundings. Al-Zamakhsharī’s al-Kashshāf also connects the blessing of the place and its event with the addressing of Moses.

Būrika itself does not mean that The God is physically inside the fire. The verb concerns blessing; the identity of those described in the phrase is a separate question.

Difficulty interpreting the phrase does not justify deleting “whoever is in the fire” from the translation.

The sentence’s direct meaning should remain, while its referents are examined separately through context, including Al-Naml 27:7, Al-Naml 27:8, and Al-Naml 27:9.

Good and blessing remain the relevant lexical field; the root does not determine the scene’s nature or the identity of its participants.

6. Mubārak and mubārakah: good in people, books, times, and things

Mubārak and mubārakah are important forms in this family.

Morphologically they are form III passive participial descriptions. Qur’anic use applies them to a person or thing endowed with blessing or in which good and benefit have been placed.

The participle alone does not determine that good’s nature. Its subject and the verse’s context show what is blessed and how it benefits.

A blessed house and guidance for all peoples

Āl ʿImrān 3:96

إِنَّ أَوَّلَ بَيْتٍ وُضِعَ لِلنَّاسِ لَلَّذِي بِبَكَّةَ مُبَارَكًا وَهُدًى لِلْعَالَمِينَ

inna awwala baytin wuḍiʿa li-al-nāsi la-alladhī bi-bakkata mubārakan wa-hudan li-al-ʿālamīna

Translation: “The first house established for people is indeed the one at Bakkah, blessed and a guidance for all peoples.”

Mubārakan describes the house in question.

It appears alongside “guidance for all peoples.” The house’s blessing is thus placed in a context that also emphasizes guidance.

Mubārak and hudā are not lexically identical; the Qur’an states both separately.

The blessing cannot be restricted to crops or economic wealth. Nor does the verse turn it into a physical force whose mere contact guarantees a moral outcome regardless of worship and human conduct.

The verse places blessing alongside the house’s role for people and guidance for all peoples. Ritual details and effects require study of the relevant verses.

Jesus: a blessed person

Maryam 19:31

وَجَعَلَنِي مُبَارَكًا أَيْنَ مَا كُنْتُ وَأَوْصَانِي بِالصَّلَاةِ وَالزَّكَاةِ مَا دُمْتُ حَيًّا

wa-jaʿalanī mubārakan ayna mā kuntu wa-awṣānī bi-al-ṣalāti wa-al-zakāti mā dumtu ḥayyan

Translation: “He has made me blessed wherever I may be, and has charged me with ṣalāh and zakāh for as long as I remain alive.”

Jesus speaks here of being made blessed.

Ayna mā kuntu, “wherever I may be,” shows that the description is not confined to his presence in one place.

Mujāhid, an early interpreter, explains mubārakan here as naffāʿan, “greatly beneficial.” Al-Ṭabarī transmits the report alongside other explanations.

The interpretation fits beneficial good. A blessed person can benefit others through guidance, knowledge, right conduct, and help.

Mubārak should not be lexically restricted to “teacher” or “healer.” Those can exemplify Jesus’ blessing, but the description has a wider range.

The verse also does not give Jesus blessing independently of The God. Jaʿalanī makes his being made blessed attributable to The God.

A human being’s blessedness therefore differs from tabāraka concerning The God. The person receives blessing and can benefit others; the verse presents The God as the source of that good.

A blessed book: guidance and reflection

Ṣād 38:29

كِتَابٌ أَنْزَلْنَاهُ إِلَيْكَ مُبَارَكٌ لِيَدَّبَّرُوا آيَاتِهِ وَلِيَتَذَكَّرَ أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

kitābun anzalnāhu ilayka mubārakun li-yaddabbarū āyātihi wa-li-yatadhakkara ulū al-albābi

Translation: “A book We have sent down to you, blessed, so that they may reflect upon its signs and those of understanding may take heed.”

Mubārak describes the book, followed by two purposes: reflection upon its signs and the taking of heed by people of understanding.

The book’s blessing is placed in a context where understanding, thought, and benefit from guidance are central.

Al-Anʿām 6:92 likewise describes the revealed book as blessed and confirming what came before it.

In Al-Anʿām 6:155, its being called blessed is followed by the command to follow it and practice taqwā.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:50 also calls the revealed reminder blessed.

Taken together, the verses make the book’s blessing more than an honorific detached from its content. Its message and guidance can be understood and practiced to benefit human life.

Mubārak itself cannot nevertheless be defined as “a book that must be reflected upon.” Reflection is stated separately in Ṣād 38:29.

Nor does the book’s blessedness establish, without Qur’anic evidence, that keeping a physical copy anywhere, repeating its words without understanding, or treating it as a magical object necessarily produces particular effects on wealth, health, or difficulties.

The verses connect the book’s blessing with revelation, guidance, reflection, following, and taqwā.

Blessed water: tangible good and growing life

Qāf 50:9

وَنَزَّلْنَا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً مُبَارَكًا فَأَنْبَتْنَا بِهِ جَنَّاتٍ وَحَبَّ الْحَصِيدِ

wa-nazzalnā mina al-samāʾi māʾan mubārakan fa-anbatnā bihi jannātin wa-ḥabba al-ḥaṣīdi

Translation: “We sent down blessed water from the sky, and through it caused gardens and grain for harvesting to grow.”

Mubārakan describes the water.

Here tangible examples of the good follow immediately: the growth of gardens and crops.

The water’s blessing thus relates to its capacity to support plant growth and provision.

Mubārak itself does not mean rain, clean water, or natural fertilizer. It describes the good present in the water; the following clause identifies its use and effect.

Nor does the verse establish that every rainfall, at every time and in every amount, benefits all people. Rain can also produce floods and harm under some conditions.

It describes water that The God calls blessed and identifies the growing of gardens and crops as its effects. This should not become an absolute claim about every weather event.

Descending to a blessed place

Al-Muʾminūn 23:29 gives a prayer to descend to a munzalan mubārakan.

Munzal expresses the place or manner of descent, and mubārak describes it.

Lexically, the request concerns a descent or landing place accompanied by good.

Mubārak should not be converted into “a large house,” “well-watered land,” or “a place of permanent safety.” Such features may belong to a particular instance but are not all components of the word itself.

A blessed tree in the parable of light

Al-Nūr 24:35 mentions shajaratin mubārakatin zaytūnatin in the parable of light.

Mubārakah describes the tree, while zaytūnah identifies it as an olive tree.

Its fruit, oil, and other uses can benefit people. Its oil also plays a role in the verse’s image of light and a lamp.

The blessing should not be restricted to a claim that every olive tree under all conditions has an extraordinary power or the same medicinal property.

Mubārakah alone also does not establish that the tree necessarily belongs to a particular historical person or place.

The verse uses the tree within a parable. The familiar physical meaning of the olive remains, while its place in the image must also be studied with the verse’s other elements.

The blessed spot at Moses’ call

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 describes al-buqʿati al-mubārakati in the scene of Moses’ call.

Buqʿah denotes a place or patch of ground, described by mubārakah.

Its blessing relates to a major event in Moses’ story. Mubārak itself does not mean tree, fire, or call; those elements are introduced separately.

Nor can the description supply every geographical detail or the physical nature of the manifestation and call.

The place has a special narrative role, while blessing retains its lexical connection with good and benefit.

A blessed night

Al-Dukhān 44:3

إِنَّا أَنْزَلْنَاهُ فِي لَيْلَةٍ مُبَارَكَةٍ ۚ إِنَّا كُنَّا مُنْذِرِينَ

innā anzalnāhu fī laylatin mubārakatin innā kunnā mundhirīna

Translation: “We sent it down on a blessed night. We have indeed been warning.”

Mubārakah describes laylah, “night.”

Blessing can therefore also be attributed to a time. The word itself identifies neither the month nor the night’s precise date.

Identification requires other verses about the Qur’an’s revelation, including Al-Baqarah 2:185 and Al-Qadr 97:1.

Mubārak should likewise not be made to mean a particular number of rewards, a specific ritual, or guaranteed effects of every action on that night without independent evidence for the claim.

A blessed greeting

Al-Nūr 24:61 speaks of a greeting described as mubārakatan ṭayyibatan upon entering houses.

This matters because the blessing concerns a linguistic and social act, rather than a physical substance.

Greetings of peace can convey goodwill, respect, and good relations.

Mubārakah and ṭayyibah remain separate descriptions: the former concerns blessing and good, the latter wholesomeness and pleasantness.

Mubārakah itself does not specify a fixed wording. The form and etiquette of greeting require the full range of relevant verses.

Qur’anic blessing is consequently not confined to material things; it can concern good human speech and conduct too.

7. Tabāraka: blessing, greatness, and praise of The God

The most important distinction within Qur’anic B R K is between bāraka and tabāraka.

With bāraka, The God establishes blessing in something or bestows it upon someone. Such constructions distinguish the giver from the recipient.

Qur’anic tabāraka expresses praise and exaltation, concerning the abundant good, glory, and elevated status of The God.

Ibn Fāris reports explanations of tabāraka llāh through magnification and glorification, and through taʿālā llāh, the exaltation of The God. Al-Rāghib instead emphasizes His being the source of good and favors.

The two explanations can complement one another.

The exaltation expressed by tabāraka relates to His good and blessing, but should not be reduced to something increasing within The God.

Tabāraka differs from bāraka

Morphologically, tabāraka follows the pattern tafāʿala.

Some Arabic verbs on this pattern express mutual participation, but not all do. The pattern can have other functions, including expressing a quality and emphasis or intensity.

The form alone therefore does not establish that The God receives blessing from another being or exchanges it reciprocally with creatures.

The Qur’an uses tabāraka to praise The God and His name.

This differs from calling creatures mubārak. The God can make a person or land blessed; tabāraka concerning Him expresses greatness and abundant good attributed to Him.

The Qur’anic uses themselves support the distinction without requiring a philosophical theory of the divine essence.

Tabāraka alladhī nazzala al-furqān: praise alongside revelation

Al-Furqān 25:1

تَبَارَكَ الَّذِي نَزَّلَ الْفُرْقَانَ عَلَىٰ عَبْدِهِ لِيَكُونَ لِلْعَالَمِينَ نَذِيرًا

tabāraka alladhī nazzala al-furqāna ʿalā ʿabdihi li-yakūna li-al-ʿālamīna nadhīran

Translation: “Great and abounding in blessing is the One who sent down the Criterion upon His servant, so that he may be a warner to all peoples.”

Tabāraka opens the surah.

The following words identify The God through sending down the Criterion. His exaltation is thus set in the context of revelation, guidance, and warning to all peoples.

Tabāraka itself does not mean revealing a book or warning. Those meanings come from nazzala and li-yakūna li-al-ʿālamīna nadhīran.

Their connection in the sentence makes the Criterion’s revelation an occasion of praise.

This accords with the book itself being called blessed in Al-Anʿām 6:92 and Ṣād 38:29.

The God is praised with tabāraka, while the book sent from Him is mubārak. The descriptions operate differently: one praises The God; the other describes a book endowed with good and guidance.

Creation and praise of The God

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14 follows the stages of human creation with fa-tabāraka llāhu aḥsanu al-khāliqīn.

Tabāraka is here associated with the creative activity of The God.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:54 uses the same verb to exalt the Lord of all worlds after mentioning creation of the heavens and earth, governance, and His possession of creation and command.

Ghāfir 40:64 likewise places fa-tabāraka llāhu rabbu al-ʿālamīn after the earth, sky, human form, and wholesome provision.

The common feature is praise of The God following a reminder of His actions and favors.

The verb itself cannot therefore mean “created” or “provided.” He is praised in connection with what the verse describes, while each verb retains its own meaning.

Al-Furqān 25:10 uses tabāraka in a context of His ability to grant better good and resources.

In Al-Furqān 25:61, it introduces mention of celestial burūj, a lamp, and an illuminating moon.

The surah thus places His blessing and greatness alongside revelation, the ability to give, and the celestial order.

Dominion and power

Al-Mulk 67:1

تَبَارَكَ الَّذِي بِيَدِهِ الْمُلْكُ وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

tabāraka alladhī bi-yadihi al-mulku wa-huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrun

Translation: “Great and abounding in blessing is the One in whose hand is dominion, and He has power over all things.”

Tabāraka accompanies the dominion and power of The God.

Bi-yadihi al-mulk attributes dominion and authority to Him in Qur’anic language. It does not make ownership or power part of B R K itself.

Mulk belongs to an independent family, and qadīr to another root.

Al-Zukhruf 43:85 gives the same exaltation alongside dominion over heaven and earth, knowledge of the Hour, and people’s return to The God.

Tabāraka is thus an expression of praise that relates to His attributes and actions in different contexts.

All those attributes should not be listed as fixed components of its lexical meaning.

Tabāraka ismu rabbika: blessing the Lord’s name

Al-Raḥmān 55:78

تَبَارَكَ اسْمُ رَبِّكَ ذِي الْجَلَالِ وَالْإِكْرَامِ

tabāraka ismu rabbika dhī al-jalāli wa-al-ikrāmi

Translation: “Exalted and abounding in blessing is the name of your Lord, Possessor of majesty and honor.”

The syntax differs from many other occurrences.

Elsewhere, tabāraka concerns The God in phrases such as tabāraka llāh and tabāraka alladhī.

Here the verb’s grammatical subject is ismu rabbika, “the name of your Lord.”

The grammatical subject of all nine occurrences is therefore not directly the noun Allāh or a pronoun referring to Him.

Here the Lord’s name is praised and described as abounding in blessing.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the name’s blessing through the mention and remembrance of the Lord. Ibn ʿĀshūr also discusses it as praise and exaltation of The God at the surah’s close.

Blessing can therefore be expressed in relation to His name and remembrance too.

The verse does not establish that the words of His names are independent beings with power separate from Him.

Nor should the name’s blessing become so abstract that the name and remembering The God cease to matter within the verse.

The Qur’an explicitly mentions the Lord’s name. Its relationship to praise, remembrance, and the named One can be studied, but those explanations do not authorize omitting “name” from the base translation.

The nine occurrences of tabāraka

The nine occurrences are Al-Aʿrāf 7:54, Al-Muʾminūn 23:14, Al-Furqān 25:1, Al-Furqān 25:10, Al-Furqān 25:61, Ghāfir 40:64, Al-Zukhruf 43:85, Al-Raḥmān 55:78, and Al-Mulk 67:1.

All praise The God or the Lord’s name.

Their distribution does not by itself establish a historical rule covering every possible Arabic use. What the Qur’an establishes is its own use of the verb.

The central distinction is that The God bestows blessing, a creature can be blessed, and Qur’anic tabāraka praises and exalts The God and His name.

8. Blessing and related words

B R K (barakah) and KH Y R (khayr)

Khayr is among the concepts closest to barakah.

Al-Rāghib defines blessing through divine good becoming established, but proximity does not make the words completely identical.

Qur’anic khayr has a broad range: goodness, benefit, superiority, or possessions, depending on context.

Major uses of barakah emphasize good marked by continuance, increase, or beneficial effects.

This should not become an absolute rule that khayr is always temporary good and barakah always everlasting, inexhaustible good.

Context determines the amount, duration, and manner of the good.

Study of KH Y R

B R K (barakah) and Z Y D (ziyādah)

Ziyādah concerns addition and increase.

Lexical sources connect blessing with increase too, but Qur’anic blessing is not restricted to numerical growth.

A water supply may increase in volume while being unsuitable for drinking or cultivation. More quantity does not by itself prove benefit.

Conversely, a book may keep the same number of pages while more people benefit from its guidance.

Increase can be an expression of blessing, but not every increase is blessing and not every blessing increases a measurable quantity.

Study of Z Y D

B R K (barakah) and R Z Q (rizq)

Qur’anic rizq concerns what beings are given and benefit from.

Blessing can relate to provision: Al-Aʿrāf 7:96 and Qāf 50:9 mention good from heaven and earth and the growth of crops.

Rizq and barakah are nevertheless not synonymous.

Someone may possess abundant wealth or food while oppressing others in using it or spending it on corruption.

The blessing of a book, greeting, or person such as Jesus also cannot be explained solely through food and possessions.

Blessing describes a quality of good that can accompany provision without being confined to provision itself.

Study of R Z Q

B R K (barakah) and R Ḥ M (raḥmah)

Mercy and blessing appear together in Hūd 11:73.

Their pairing shows a connection, but does not alone establish an absolute boundary between them.

Divine mercy can appear through kindness, support, forgiveness, and other good. Blessing concerns good and benefit established by The God.

Their effects may overlap, while translation should not remove either in favor of the other.

Study of R Ḥ M

B R K (barakah) and Q D S (quds)

Arabic and Qur’anic quds concerns purity and sanctification.

Blessing concerns good and benefit.

The two can relate to the same place or religious matter, but are not lexically identical.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 calls a spot blessed. What occurs in a holy or blessed place must be studied through the verses themselves.

Mubārak should therefore not always be rendered “holy.” A thing or place may be blessed without the verse establishing a particular rule of sanctification.

Study of Q D S

B R K (barakah) and TH B T (thabāt)

Thabāt means remaining firm, which Ibn Fāris identifies as the semantic axis of B R K.

Qur’anic blessing is nevertheless more than stability.

Something may remain for a long time without benefit. Seasonal rain, conversely, can sustain abundant life and crops within its brief season.

Religious blessing should not be reduced to mere duration; the presence of good and benefit matters too.

The image of a settling camel or collected water can explain relationships between meanings without controlling the entire Qur’anic meaning of blessing.

Study of TH B T

B R K (barakah) and B R Q (barq)

The roots share their first two consonants, but differ in the third.

Qur’anic B R K concerns blessing and good, whereas B R Q concerns lightning, gleaming, and related phenomena.

Their similarity in sound or writing does not establish a historical or semantic relationship.

Blessing should therefore not be defined through lightning, energy, or radiating light without independent evidence in a particular context.

9. Divine blessing, praying for blessing, and seeking it

Arabic records tabrīk for praying and asking for blessing. In bāraka llāhu ʿalayka, for example, someone asks The God to bless another.

Lisān al-ʿArab records this use of bāraka and also Arabic uses of tabarraka for seeking good and blessing through something.

The Qur’an does not use tabarraka for a person seeking blessing through an object or person. Its form is tabāraka, used in exalting The God.

The morphological and semantic distinction should be preserved.

The existence of an Arabic word for seeking blessing does not by itself make every later practice of touching objects, seeking blessing through people, or using places a Qur’anic command.

Such judgments require independent Qur’anic evidence.

Conversely, the absence of tabarraka does not establish that no one may pray for another’s good and blessing. Hūd 11:73 speaks of divine blessings upon a household, and Al-Nūr 24:61 mentions a blessed greeting.

Three matters remain distinct: The God bestowing blessing, asking for good and blessing for another, and attributing an independent effect to an object or person without clear evidence.

The distinction matters in understanding blessing within divine unity, but the root alone does not determine every rule concerning prayer and ritual conduct.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Blessing,” “blessed,” and “to bless” are established English terms and can serve in many contexts without inventing a new equivalent.

Barakāt can be “blessings.” In Al-Aʿrāf 7:96, “blessings from heaven and earth” retains the range of good without limiting it to particular rain or crops.

Bāraknā can be “We bestowed blessing” or “We placed blessing.” Prepositions should follow the verse’s construction: “in which We placed blessing” in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:71, “whose surroundings We blessed” in Al-Isrāʾ 17:1, and “We bestowed blessing upon him and upon Isaac” in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:113.

Būrika in Al-Naml 27:8 should retain its passive voice. “Blessing was bestowed” or “has been blessed” preserves the construction better than introducing an unexpressed agent. Uncertainty about “whoever is in the fire” belongs in explanation rather than an arbitrary alteration of the translation.

Mubārak and mubārakah can naturally be “blessed”: “made me blessed” in Maryam 19:31, “blessed water” in Qāf 50:9, and “a blessed night” in Al-Dukhān 44:3 follow the Arabic constructions.

Tabāraka requires greater care.

“Received blessing” concerning The God could suggest that He receives it from another being.

“Bestowed blessing” also collapses it into bāraka and weakens its force of praise and exaltation.

“Abounding in blessing” expresses abundant good; “great and abounding in blessing” or “exalted and abounding in blessing” can also convey the glorifying force in a suitable context.

The principal Forqan proposal is:

Tabāraka: “great and abounding in blessing,” with “exalted and abounding in blessing” available where appropriate.

Al-Raḥmān 55:78 must also retain ismu rabbika, since the Lord’s name is the grammatical subject.

Expressions such as “The God increased His blessing,” “received blessing,” or “blessed Himself” do not convey tabāraka’s established force of praise adequately.

“Most blessed” is not required either. Tabāraka is not a comparative or superlative form; expressing greatness and abundant good does not require a comparison between The God and others.

11. Conclusion

Older Arabic B R K concerns stability and remaining established. A camel settling down, persistence in a place, and water collecting in a pool are concrete uses of the family.

Another branch concerns good, abundant benefit, and the establishment of good. Lexicographers connect the uses through continuance, but this should not become a conclusive historical origin for every meaning.

The root occurs 32 times in the Qur’an in five recorded groups.

Blessing can be attributed to land, water, a house, time, a person, scripture, and a greeting. Its range is therefore wider than increased wealth and provision.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:96 links blessings from heaven and earth with belief and taqwā. Qāf 50:9 connects blessed water with gardens and crops. Maryam 19:31 attributes blessedness to Jesus, and Ṣād 38:29 places a blessed book in a context of reflection and taking heed.

Blessing can thus have material and nonmaterial effects; the word alone does not determine each effect’s nature.

The blessed-land verses also distinguish a place’s good from its inhabitants’ conduct. A land’s blessing licenses neither oppression nor exclusivity, just as blessing Abraham and Isaac does not guarantee every descendant’s goodness.

The central morphological distinction is between bāraka, mubārak, and tabāraka.

Bāraka expresses bestowing blessing; mubārak describes someone or something endowed with it; Qur’anic tabāraka praises and exalts The God and the Lord’s name.

The uses belong to one semantic family, but should not replace one another in translation or analysis.

Blessing should also not be equated with numerical increase, wealth, temporary pleasure, physical health, or ritual holiness. Each may relate to it in context, but the word has a broader range.

A suitable final definition for Forqan:

B R K is an Arabic family connected with stability and remaining established; in uses concerning good, it expresses its continuance, abundance, and benefit. Qur’anic blessing is good placed by The God in people, things, places, times, and activities, whose effects are understood through context. Bāraka expresses bestowing blessing, mubārak being endowed with it, and Qur’anic tabāraka the exaltation of The God and His name. Blessing is neither confined to quantitative increase nor an automatic guarantee of goodness, perpetual prosperity, or immunity from harm. Its precise meaning and effects require the word’s subject and the full Qur’anic context.
