Qur’anic root م ك ر

Concealed planning, plotting against others, and evil plotting returning upon its authors

1. The root in early Arabic

In Arabic, م ك ر concerns concealed planning, devising means, stratagems, and redirecting another person’s course without their awareness of the plan.

Arabic lexicons describe مَكْر as a covert stratagem that can prevent another from reaching their goal or confront them with an unexpected outcome.

Al-Raghib al-Isfahani distinguishes praiseworthy from blameworthy مَكْر. In his definition, its value depends on the purpose pursued through the plan.

Classical Arabic records expressions such as:

  • مَكَرَ بِهِ: he devised a concealed plan against him.
  • مَكَرَ الْقَوْمُ: the people formed a plan in secret.
  • رَجُلٌ مَاكِرٌ: a man who uses stratagems to achieve his purpose.
  • مَكْرَة: a stratagem, including in warfare.

Lexicons also record senses such as irrigating land, a kind of plant, coloring with reddish clay, and fullness or prominence of the lower leg.

Ibn Faris distinguishes stratagem from fullness of the leg. There is insufficient evidence to derive all these uses from a single shared semantic core.

This study does not establish an independently datable pre-Islamic example of concealed planning. The Qur’an’s own usages nevertheless show that this meaning was recognized in the Arabic of its revelation.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms in Arabic include:

  • مَكَرَ ـ يَمْكُرُ: to devise a concealed plan; plot; use a stratagem.
  • مَكْر: concealed planning; a stratagem or plot.
  • مَاكِر: someone devising a concealed plan.
  • مَاكِرُونَ and مَاكِرِينَ: plural forms of مَاكِر.
  • مَكَّار: someone who plots frequently.
  • مَكُور: highly cunning.
  • مَمْكُورٌ بِهِ: someone plotted against.
  • مَكْرَة: a stratagem or plan.
  • مُكُور: in the botanical branch, a plural of the plant name مَكْر / مَكْرَة; distinct from مَكُور, highly cunning.

Qur’anic forms examined:

مَكَرَ ـ مَكَرُوا ـ مَكَرْنَا ـ مَكَرْتُمُوهُ ـ يَمْكُرُ ـ يَمْكُرُونَ ـ لِيَمْكُرُوا ـ تَمْكُرُونَ ـ مَكْر ـ مَكْرًا ـ الْمَكْر ـ مَكْرَهُمْ ـ مَكْرُهُمْ ـ مَكْرِهِمْ ـ مَكْرِهِنَّ ـ الْمَاكِرِينَ

All Qur’anic forms in this family belong to three groups: the basic triliteral verb مَكَرَ, its verbal noun مَكْر, and the active participle مَاكِر.

The intensive form مَكَّار does not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Core meaning

The core meaning is a plan employed to affect another’s course of action or achieve an outcome concealed from them.

مَكْر differs from ordinary planning. A person may plan to build a home, grow crops, or provide for their family without devising a concealed plan directed against anyone.

مَكْر generally involves another party and how to respond to that party’s decision, intention, or conduct.

For example, someone may create a misleading appearance to appropriate another’s property: this is evil plotting. Conversely, someone may devise a plan unknown to an oppressor to prevent injustice and neutralize that oppressor’s scheme.

Both involve concealed planning, but their purposes and moral values differ.

Thus مَكْر is not inherently identical with lying, betrayal, or oppression, although human usages often involve these behaviors.

The Qur’an shows this distinction by attributing مَكْر both to humans and to The God, and through the expression الْمَكْرُ السَّيِّئُ.

4. Qur’anic usages

Human plotting and its return upon its authors

This verse speaks of plotting by people with social standing and influence.

Such people may use power, wealth, and influence to preserve their interests, mislead others, and prevent acceptance of truth.

They imagine that their plans affect only others, whereas the Qur’an returns the ultimate consequences of their plotting upon them.

This does not mean that oppressors’ schemes never harm others. Many people can become victims of conspiracy and deception. The verse means that the plotters do not escape responsibility and consequences, even when unaware of this while forming their plans.

Al-Anʿam 6:124 likewise connects the criminals’ humiliation and severe punishment with their plotting.

Plotting in the story of Joseph

This verse refers to the concealed plan of Joseph’s brothers.

At the beginning of Yusuf, the Qur’an recounts their discussions about Joseph, their decision to remove him from his father, and their subsequent return to their father.

Here مَكْر is more than a single lie: it is a coordinated series of decisions and actions directed toward a hidden purpose.

The lie the brothers told their father afterward was part of the plan, but does not exhaust the meaning of مَكْر.

Another occurrence appears in Yusuf 12:31. When the wife of Egypt’s dignitary learned of the women’s plotting, she invited them to a gathering.

Yusuf 12:30 recounts what the women said about her. The next verse describes their behavior as مَكْر. Plotting is therefore not limited to politics or warfare; it can occur in social relationships, speech, and concealed conduct between individuals.

Plotting as an accusation

When the sorcerers believed after seeing Moses’ signs, Pharaoh presented their acceptance of truth as a conspiracy against his rule.

مَكْر here is part of Pharaoh’s accusation, not The God’s direct description of the sorcerers’ behavior.

The narrative shows Pharaoh’s unwillingness to accept that people serving his power could freely abandon their previous belief after recognizing truth.

He attributed their change of conviction to a political scheme.

This usage also illustrates an important distinction: when the Qur’an quotes someone, not every claim within their speech can be attributed to The God.

Calling another’s conduct a plot does not by itself establish that plotting occurred.

Plotting against Muhammad and divine planning

The opponents’ aims are explicit here: to detain, kill, or expel Muhammad.

They sought to use their resources and power to obstruct his mission.

The Qur’an sets divine planning against theirs, employing the same root م ك ر for both parties.

Their purposes and positions, however, are not equivalent.

The opponents plan to obstruct delivery of the message and harm The God’s Messenger, whereas divine planning can neutralize their scheme and prevent their intended result.

In the reading based on concealed planning as a shared core, the distinction rests on agent, purpose, and context. Some lexical and exegetical sources instead explain divine مَكْر as recompense named after the opposing party’s act; another rhetorical analysis therefore also exists in the interpretive tradition.

Plotting against Salih

Al-Naml 27:49 describes a group’s decision to attack Salih and his household by night and then deny responsibility.

The following verse sets their plotting against The God’s planning.

They had planned for a specific outcome but encountered an entirely different one.

Al-Naml 27:53 also speaks of the deliverance of those who believed and practiced taqwa.

Taken together, these verses show that divine planning does not arise from need, inability, or rivalry with humans. Human beings plan within limited resources and awareness, without knowing every circumstance and consequence of their actions.

وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ expresses this limitation in their awareness.

Organized plotting and misleading people

Here plotting extends beyond individual relationships to relations between powerful people and those under their influence.

مَكْرُ اللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ indicates sustained planning and effort to shape people’s beliefs and behavior.

Such plotting can involve propaganda, imposed beliefs, concealed truth, intimidation, or exploitation of social and economic dependence.

Those in power can promote beliefs that obstruct recognition of truth in order to preserve their position.

Nevertheless, the verse’s conclusion preserves personal responsibility. Being under others’ influence does not, by itself, remove all responsibility for one’s conduct and choices.

The story of Noah also states:

In context, this plotting concerns the influence of people possessing wealth and children and efforts to preserve fabricated deities.

كُبَّارًا emphasizes the magnitude of their plotting. Greatness and intensity come from this qualifier, rather than being necessary components of مَكْر itself.

الْمَكْرُ السَّيِّئُ: evil plotting and its return

This is a central verse for understanding the moral evaluation of مَكْر in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an qualifies it with السَّيِّئُ. What is condemned here is planning with an evil purpose, serving arrogance, oppression, and obstruction of truth.

Together with usages concerning The God, this qualifier shows that moral evil cannot be treated as inseparable from the root’s meaning.

Another point is the connection between evil plotting returning upon its authors and The God’s established way.

An oppressor may imagine that cunning, concealment, and influence ensure immunity from consequences. Yet their conduct can generate distrust, hostility, corruption, and ultimately the collapse of their own position.

Al-Nahl 16:26 depicts this outcome through a building attacked at its foundations, whose roof collapses upon its occupants.

Fatir 35:10 also speaks of the plotting of those who devise evil coming to ruin.

Apparent, temporary success therefore does not guarantee ultimate success.

The believer from Pharaoh’s people delivered from plotting

This verse concerns the believing man among Pharaoh’s people who supported Moses.

The Qur’an connects the opponents’ plotting, the believer’s protection from its consequences, and the punishment encompassing Pharaoh’s people.

The juxtaposition of وَقَاهُ, from و ق ي, and مَكَرُوا, from م ك ر, shows that divine assistance can include protecting a person from harmful consequences of others’ plans.

5. Divine planning and the attributes of The God

وَاللَّهُ خَيْرُ الْمَاكِرِينَ

This verse occurs within the story of Jesus and opposition by those who rejected his call.

The Qur’an uses the same verb for the opponents’ behavior and for divine planning.

Understanding the verse requires distinguishing the concept of planning from its moral evaluation.

Human plotting may arise from ignorance, fear, need, greed, or an intention to oppress. Attributing مَكْر to The God does not attribute these qualities to Him.

The Qur’an presents The God as knowing, free of need, and upholding justice. Divine planning must be understood consistently with these attributes.

In Al ʿImran 3:54 and al-Anfal 8:30, divine planning stands against the opponents’ plotting. Al-Naml 27:50 expresses the same relationship while emphasizing their unawareness of it.

In these usages, divine planning concerns neutralizing evil human schemes and confronting their authors with outcomes different from those intended.

It is not, however, restricted to direct counteraction against a particular plot. The Qur’an also describes heedless people encountering consequences they did not expect.

أَفَأَمِنُوا مَكْرَ اللَّهِ: a false sense of security

The preceding verses describe people who enjoyed ease and abundance after hardship, then unexpectedly encountered the outcome of their actions.

Al-Aʿraf 7:97 and al-Aʿraf 7:98 question the security felt by communities while sleeping or occupied with daytime amusement.

A person may persist in wrongdoing for years and, seeing no immediate consequence, imagine they will never face it.

Wealth, power, and comfort can reinforce this sense of security.

Yet delayed consequences do not mean absent consequences.

This verse does not contradict hope in divine mercy. Hope for mercy and forgiveness differs from a sense of absolute immunity from the consequences of oppression and corruption.

The Qur’an does not demand a life of constant anxiety and despair. It warns against the heedlessness through which people imagine themselves exempt from responsibility.

اللَّهُ أَسْرَعُ مَكْرًا: the limits of human planning

This verse presents human plotting after renewed enjoyment of mercy and ease.

After deliverance from hardship, people may disregard a truth they recognized during distress and devise justifications for their previous behavior.

The Qur’an nevertheless speaks of human plotting being recorded.

أَسْرَعُ مَكْرًا expresses the superiority of divine planning over limited human plans.

This does not mean that The God, like a human, must formulate a fresh plan upon discovering someone else’s scheme. Divine knowledge and power do not depend on limited awareness or reactive human planning.

فَلِلَّهِ الْمَكْرُ جَمِيعًا: ultimate divine sovereignty

The verse both acknowledges earlier peoples’ plotting and attributes all planning to The God.

This does not attribute every human deceit, betrayal, or oppression to The God, nor remove the human agent’s responsibility.

The verse immediately speaks of divine knowledge of what every soul earns.

People can plan to achieve their purposes, but cannot independently and absolutely determine their plans’ outcomes.

Al-Raʿd 13:33 also describes people whose plotting is made attractive to them and who are barred from the path. A person may regard a wrongful plan as intelligence and success while that very plan leads them away from the right course.

Is الْمَاكِر a divine name?

The Qur’an uses the verb and the expressions خَيْرُ الْمَاكِرِينَ, أَسْرَعُ مَكْرًا, and مَكْرَ اللَّهِ concerning The God.

It does not, however, use الْمَاكِر as an independent divine name.

A verb attributed to The God in a particular context, a description related to that action, and an independent divine name must be distinguished.

خَيْرُ الْمَاكِرِينَ should retain its own structure and meaning. Turning it into an independent name not given in that form by the Qur’an goes beyond the text.

6. Morphology and syntax

The verb and verbal noun

مَكَرَ is a basic triliteral verb, and مَكْر its verbal noun.

The verb frequently occurs with بِ. In al-Anfal 8:30, for example, the target of the plotting is identified by the pronoun attached to this preposition.

Usages such as al-Nahl 16:45 and Fatir 35:10 connect plotting with evil deeds.

In al-Naml 27:50, مَكْرًا follows a verb of the same root as a cognate accusative.

Nuh 71:22 has the same construction, with كُبَّارًا emphasizing the scale of the plotting.

خَيْرُ الْمَاكِرِينَ

مَاكِر is an active participle on the pattern فَاعِل.

الْمَاكِرِينَ is its plural and the genitive complement in خَيْرُ الْمَاكِرِينَ.

In Al ʿImran 3:54 and al-Anfal 8:30, this construction expresses the superiority of divine planning.

Rendering it as “the best deceivers” can introduce an inappropriate moral implication, because deception is commonly understood as blameworthy and associated with lying and betrayal.

“The best of those who practise makr” retains the word makr in translation; the concealed nature of the planning and the neutralization of opponents’ schemes are clarified in the explanation.

Variant readings in Ibrahim 14:46

In Hafs’s transmission from ʿAsim, لِتَزُولَ has an initial kasra and a subjunctive verb. One recognized analysis denies that their plotting could remove mountains.

A conditional interpretation has also been proposed: even if their plotting were great enough to remove mountains, it would not lie outside divine knowledge and power.

Al-Kisa’i’s reading, لَتَزُولُ, has an initial fatha and an indicative verb, allowing an emphasis on the enormity of their plotting.

Despite these differences, a shared theme is that human plotting remains within divine knowledge and sovereignty.

Al-Nahl 16:127 and al-Naml 27:70 also counsel Muhammad against grief and distress over his opponents’ plotting. The existence of their schemes does not guarantee their success.

7. Comparison with related roots

م ك ر compared with ك ي د

كَيْد concerns devising means to achieve an objective, particularly in dealing with another party.

مَكْر and كَيْد are very close in meaning.

In Yusuf 12:76, The God uses a derivative of ك ي د for the plan that enabled Joseph’s brother to remain with him.

Al-Tariq 86:15 and al-Tariq 86:16 likewise set the opponents’ كَيْد against divine كَيْد.

In مَكْر, concealment and the other party’s unawareness are prominent; كَيْد also emphasizes devising means to achieve an intended outcome.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute boundary between the families.

م ك ر compared with خ د ع

خِدَاع concerns creating an appearance different from reality and leading someone toward a mistaken understanding.

مَكْر concerns concealed planning that affects another’s course of action and its outcome.

A person may use deception to execute a plot. Yet not every concealed plan requires a false appearance.

For example, someone may plan to prevent an oppressor’s scheme without making a false statement.

Al-Baqarah 2:9 returns the hypocrites’ deception upon themselves, just as al-Anʿam 6:123 returns the criminals’ plotting upon themselves.

م ك ر compared with د ب ر

تَدْبِير concerns considering outcomes, arranging affairs, and administering them.

Every مَكْر can be a kind of تَدْبِير, but not every تَدْبِير is مَكْر.

People can manage their lives without a hidden scheme directed against another.

Yunus 10:3 and al-Raʿd 13:2 attribute the governance of creation to The God.

Divine مَكْر is a specific use of planning in relation to human conduct, heedlessness, and schemes; divine governance as a whole is not restricted to it.

م ك ر compared with ب ي ت

In one branch of ب ي ت, تَبْيِيت denotes concealed planning and decision-making, especially by night.

Al-Nisa 4:81 describes a group planning at night something contrary to what they had said.

تَبْيِيت can be one form of مَكْر, but مَكْر is not restricted to night or any particular time.

“Plotting by night and day” in Saba 34:33 illustrates this temporal breadth.

م ك ر compared with غ ر ر

In its deception-related sense, غُرُور concerns a mistaken understanding and confidence in an appearance that does not disclose reality.

مَكْر can exploit such an understanding to achieve its purpose.

In al-Aʿraf 7:99, those who feel safe from divine planning possess a sense of security inconsistent with their actual limitations.

غُرُور can enable a plot to succeed or cause people to suffer the consequences of their own plotting.

م ك ر compared with ك ذ ب

كَذِب concerns false speech, reports, and claims.

مَكْر may employ lies as an instrument, but is not limited to verbal falsehood.

Joseph’s brothers first formed a plan, then lied to their father to conceal their action.

The lie was part of their plotting, not its entirety.

مکر is familiar in Persian and Dari and can be retained in Qur’an translation, especially where repeating the same root for humans and The God is significant.

Because the word often carries a negative moral connotation in contemporary Persian, its semantic range needs explanation to prevent misunderstanding.

Rendering divine مَكْر merely as “punishing” is also insufficient: it removes the idea of planning and the verbal connection between human and divine مَكْر. Punishment, neutralization of a scheme, or consequences returning upon the plotter can be outcomes of divine planning.

Conclusion

م ك ر concerns concealed planning, stratagems, and influencing another’s course of action without their awareness of the plan.

مَكْر is not necessarily good or evil in itself. Its value depends on purpose, means, and the outcome sought.

The Qur’an discusses human plotting in familial, social, political, and religious relationships, as well as divine planning in relation to human plotting and heedlessness.

People may devise hidden plans with limited power, knowledge, and resources, and succeed for a time; they do not control all the circumstances and consequences of their actions.

Divine planning is inseparable from divine knowledge, power, and justice. خَيْرُ الْمَاكِرِينَ emphasizes its superiority, while الْمَكْرُ السَّيِّئُ identifies evil plotting as wrong.

The return of evil plotting upon its authors is also connected with The God’s unchanging way.

Definition for Forqan:

م ك ر is a semantic family denoting concealed planning to redirect another’s course of action, prevent their purpose, or confront them with an unexpected outcome. Its moral evaluation depends on purpose and context.
