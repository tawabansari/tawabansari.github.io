The Qur’anic Root Ḥ J J

From Intending a Destination and Hajj to Argument and Reasoned Confrontation

The root Ḥ J J appears in several semantic branches in the Qur’an, two of which are particularly prominent. One concerns hajj, intending a destination, and turning toward it; the other concerns ḥujjah, presenting arguments, and confrontation between claims. Alongside these, the form حِجَج (ḥijaj) occurs once with the meaning “years.”

These branches belong to one linguistic family, but they should not all be reduced to a single image without evidence. Early lexicographers likewise recorded several semantic directions when explaining Ḥ J J. “Intending” is among the root’s oldest known meanings, while different explanations have been offered for the precise historical connection between hajj and ḥujjah. Their semantic relationship can therefore be explored, but a possible account of derivation should not be presented as the word’s established history.

An important feature of this root is that the Qur’anic meaning of ḥujjah is not confined to a sound or conclusive proof. In the Qur’an, a ḥujjah can be valid and fully effective, but it can also be the argument of a party in the wrong, or even an argument that has collapsed and lost its validity. The word “ḥujjah” itself therefore does not guarantee that an argument is sound.

Forms Examined

The root Ḥ J J occurs 33 times in the Qur’an in eight principal morphological forms:

One occurrence of the form I verb حَجَّ (ḥajja)

Twelve occurrences of the form III verb حَاجَّ (ḥājja) and its inflections

One occurrence of form VI, يَتَحَاجُّونَ (yataḥājjūna)

Nine occurrences of the noun حَجّ (ḥajj)

One occurrence of حِجّ (ḥijj)

One occurrence of حِجَج (ḥijaj)

Seven occurrences of حُجَّة (ḥujjah)

One occurrence of the active participle الْحَاجّ (al-ḥājj)

This distribution shows that a substantial portion of the root’s Qur’anic uses concern arguments and argumentative confrontation, rather than the rites of hajj.

1. The Early Core and Use in Arabic Before Islam

Intending and Turning Toward

One of the early, clear meanings of حَجَّ (ḥajja) in Arabic is to intend something and turn toward it. Lexical sources record expressions in this word family for seeking out a person, coming to that person, and repeatedly going to and from them.

Within the same semantic field, Arabic also uses المَحَجَّة (al-maḥajjah) for a clear road or a route along which a person travels toward a destination. This form does not occur in the Qur’an, but it shows that direction, a path, and a destination were present in the family’s earlier uses.

There is no need to invent a physical image for this root, such as a stone, a rope, or a particular movement. What the linguistic evidence supports with greater confidence is intending, turning toward, and going toward a destination.

Evidence from Early Arabic Poetry

Poetry attributed to al-Mukhabbal al-Saʿdī, a poet whose life spanned the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, includes:

وَأَشْهَدُ مِنْ عَوْفٍ حُلُولًا كَثِيرَةً
يَحُجُّونَ سِبَّ الزِّبْرِقَانِ الْمُزَعْفَرَا

Wa-ashhadu min ʿAwfin ḥulūlan kathīratan / yaḥujjūna sibba al-Zibriqāni al-muzaʿfarā.

Lexicographers explain يَحُجُّونَ (yaḥujjūna) in this verse as seeking out al-Zibriqān, coming to him, and repeatedly going to and from him.

This evidence matters because it shows that the verb حَجَّ (ḥajja) in early Arabic was not restricted to the Kaaba or religious rites. A person could also “ḥajj” another person or destination: seek them out and turn toward them.

The more general meaning “to intend or seek out” should therefore not be derived from the technical meaning of the pilgrimage to Mecca. Rather, hajj to the House is one specific use within this broader field of intending and turning toward.

Hajj to Mecca Before Islam

Hajj to the House itself was also known in Arabia before the revelation of the Qur’an. The season of visiting the House, circumambulation, and a set of associated practices were not unfamiliar to the Qur’an’s first audience.

This is also evident in the Qur’an’s own language. It says:

الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَّعْلُومَاتٌ

Al-ḥajju ashhurun maʿlūmātun.

Hajj is in known months.

Al-Baqarah 2:197

The expression أَشْهُرٌ مَّعْلُومَاتٌ (ashhurun maʿlūmātun) speaks of “known” months: a time familiar to the audience.

At the same time, the Qur’an makes the orientation of this hajj clear:

وَأَتِمُّوا الْحَجَّ وَالْعُمْرَةَ لِلَّهِ

Wa-atimmū al-ḥajja wa-al-ʿumrata li-llāhi.

And complete hajj and ʿumrah for The God.

Al-Baqarah 2:196

The fact that hajj was known before Islam does not mean that every belief and practice surrounding it forms part of its Qur’anic meaning. The Qur’an places a familiar word and rite within its own semantic and grammatical framework.

2. Hajj in Qur’anic Usage

حَجَّ الْبَيْتَ (ḥajja al-bayta): Performing Hajj to the House

The clearest use of the form I verb is:

فَمَنْ حَجَّ الْبَيْتَ أَوِ اعْتَمَرَ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِ أَنْ يَطَّوَّفَ بِهِمَا

Fa-man ḥajja al-bayta awi iʿtamara fa-lā junāḥa ʿalayhi an yaṭṭawwafa bihimā.

Whoever performs hajj to the House or undertakes ʿumrah incurs no blame for going around the two.

Al-Baqarah 2:158

At the level of the root’s earlier meaning, حَجَّ الْبَيْتَ (ḥajja al-bayta) is connected with seeking out and turning toward the House. In Qur’anic language, however, this expression no longer means merely “he headed for the house.” Hajj to the House names a recognized rite with its associated acts.

This distinction matters. Just as the root’s definition should not be restricted to “the Islamic rites of hajj,” the Qur’anic term hajj should not be reduced so completely to its root meaning that all its ritual content disappears.

The Qur’an also names hajj and ʿumrah separately:

الْحَجَّ وَالْعُمْرَةَ

Al-ḥajja wa-al-ʿumrata — hajj and ʿumrah.

And:

حَجَّ الْبَيْتَ أَوِ اعْتَمَرَ

Ḥajja al-bayta awi iʿtamara — performed hajj to the House or undertook ʿumrah.

Sharing a destination or some observances therefore does not make these two designations identical.

The Timing of Hajj

The Qur’an also connects hajj with a system of timekeeping:

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْأَهِلَّةِ قُلْ هِيَ مَوَاقِيتُ لِلنَّاسِ وَالْحَجِّ

Yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-ahillati qul hiya mawāqītu li-l-nāsi wa-al-ḥajji.

They ask you about the crescents. Say: They are measures of time for people and for hajj.

Al-Baqarah 2:189

And then:

الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَّعْلُومَاتٌ فَمَنْ فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ

Al-ḥajju ashhurun maʿlūmātun fa-man faraḍa fīhinna al-ḥajja fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa wa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajji.

Hajj is in known months. Whoever undertakes hajj in them: there is to be no sexual intercourse, no disobedience, and no disputing during hajj.

Al-Baqarah 2:197

The word أَشْهُر (ashhur) is the plural of “month” and must retain that meaning in translation. How later legal formulations arranged the various acts of hajj on particular days is a separate question from the direct meaning of أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ (ashhurun maʿlūmātun, known months). “Months” must not become “days” in translation.

Hajj to the House and the Ability to Reach It

In Al-Imran we read:

وَلِلَّهِ عَلَى النَّاسِ حِجُّ الْبَيْتِ مَنِ اسْتَطَاعَ إِلَيْهِ سَبِيلًا

Wa-li-llāhi ʿalā al-nāsi ḥijju al-bayti mani istaṭāʿa ilayhi sabīlan.

And owed to The God by people is hajj to the House, for whoever can find a way to it.

Al-Imran 3:97

The association of حِجُّ الْبَيْتِ (ḥijju al-bayti, hajj to the House) with سَبِيلًا (sabīlan, a way) accords with the earlier element of movement toward a destination. Hajj here is the recognized rite, but performing it is connected with being able to find a way to the House.

This image becomes clearer in Surah Al-Hajj:

وَأَذِّنْ فِي النَّاسِ بِالْحَجِّ يَأْتُوكَ رِجَالًا وَعَلَىٰ كُلِّ ضَامِرٍ يَأْتِينَ مِن كُلِّ فَجٍّ عَمِيقٍ

Wa-adhdhin fī al-nāsi bi-al-ḥajji yaʾtūka rijālan wa-ʿalā kulli ḍāmirin yaʾtīna min kulli fajjin ʿamīqin.

And proclaim hajj among the people: they will come to you on foot and on every lean camel, coming from every distant route.

Al-Hajj 22:27

Here the ritual meaning of hajj and the earlier image of intending and moving toward a destination appear together.

الْحَاجّ (al-ḥājj): The Hajj Pilgrim

At-Tawbah 9:19 states:

أَجَعَلْتُمْ سِقَايَةَ الْحَاجِّ وَعِمَارَةَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ كَمَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ

Ajaʿaltum siqāyata al-ḥājji wa-ʿimārata al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi ka-man āmana bi-llāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri.

Have you made providing water to the hajj pilgrim and maintaining al-Masjid al-Ḥarām like one who has faith in The God and the Last Day?

At-Tawbah 9:19

الْحَاجّ (al-ḥājj) is an active participle from this root. In this context, its connection with al-Masjid al-Ḥarām shows that it means someone who performs hajj to the House, rather than anyone who has set out for a destination.

يَوْمَ الْحَجِّ الْأَكْبَرِ (yawma al-ḥajji al-akbari): The Day of the Greater Hajj

At-Tawbah 9:3 states:

وَأَذَانٌ مِّنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ إِلَى النَّاسِ يَوْمَ الْحَجِّ الْأَكْبَرِ

Wa-adhānun mina Allāhi wa-rasūlihi ilā al-nāsi yawma al-ḥajji al-akbari.

And an announcement from The God and His Messenger to the people on the day of the greater hajj.

At-Tawbah 9:3

The root Ḥ J J itself does not determine exactly which day or stage of the rites يَوْمَ الْحَجِّ الْأَكْبَرِ (yawma al-ḥajji al-akbari) denotes. Identifying that day through history or transmitted reports is a separate discussion; the identification should not enter the translation as part of the meaning of hajj itself.

3. Ḥujjah and Argumentative Confrontation in the Qur’an

حُجَّة (ḥujjah) Does Not Always Mean a Sound Argument

One of this root’s most important features appears in the use of حُجَّة (ḥujjah).

The Qur’anic word “ḥujjah” should not be assumed to mean “a sound, conclusive, and unassailable proof.” Its Qur’anic uses do not carry that fixed meaning.

The Qur’an says:

وَالَّذِينَ يُحَاجُّونَ فِي اللَّهِ مِن بَعْدِ مَا اسْتُجِيبَ لَهُ حُجَّتُهُمْ دَاحِضَةٌ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ

Wa-alladhīna yuḥājjūna fī Allāhi min baʿdi mā istujība lahu ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍatun ʿinda rabbihim.

Those who argue concerning The God after His call has been answered—their argument has collapsed before their Lord.

Ash-Shura 42:16

If حُجَّة (ḥujjah) automatically meant “a sound proof,” the expression حُجَّتُهُمْ دَاحِضَةٌ (ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍatun, their argument is invalid) would lose its natural sense. The Qur’an calls something their ḥujjah and then describes that very argument as having collapsed and lost its validity.

In Al-Jathiyah it also says:

وَمَا كَانَ حُجَّتَهُمْ إِلَّا أَن قَالُوا ائْتُوا بِآبَائِنَا إِن كُنتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

Wa-mā kāna ḥujjatahum illā an qālū iʾtū bi-ābāʾinā in kuntum ṣādiqīna.

Their argument was nothing but to say: Bring our fathers, if you are truthful.

Al-Jathiyah 45:25

Here too, حُجَّتَهُمْ (ḥujjatahum, their argument) identifies only what they put forward to establish their position. Calling it a “ḥujjah” does not endorse its validity.

At its basic level, حُجَّة (ḥujjah) can therefore denote a reason, an argument, an argumentative claim, or something offered in an exchange of arguments to establish a position. Context determines its worth and validity.

Divine and Human Arguments

Al-Anam states:

وَتِلْكَ حُجَّتُنَا آتَيْنَاهَا إِبْرَاهِيمَ عَلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ

Wa-tilka ḥujjatunā ātaynāhā Ibrāhīma ʿalā qawmihi.

And that was Our argument, which We gave to Abraham against his people.

Al-Anam 6:83

And:

قُلْ فَلِلَّهِ الْحُجَّةُ الْبَالِغَةُ

Qul fa-li-llāhi al-ḥujjatu al-bālighatu.

Say: To The God belongs the argument that fully reaches its end.

Al-Anam 6:149

In the second expression, الْبَالِغَةُ (al-bālighatu) qualifies ḥujjah. This construction also shows that “fully effective and reaching its end” is not part of the meaning of حُجَّة (ḥujjah) itself; it is a description of this argument’s quality.

By contrast, the Qur’an says of people:

لِئَلَّا يَكُونَ لِلنَّاسِ عَلَيْكُمْ حُجَّةٌ

Li-allā yakūna li-l-nāsi ʿalaykum ḥujjatun.

So that people may have no argument against you.

Al-Baqarah 2:150

And:

لِئَلَّا يَكُونَ لِلنَّاسِ عَلَى اللَّهِ حُجَّةٌ بَعْدَ الرُّسُلِ

Li-allā yakūna li-l-nāsi ʿalā Allāhi ḥujjatun baʿda al-rusuli.

So that people may have no argument against The God after the Messengers.

An-Nisa 4:165

In these uses, a ḥujjah is something a person can raise against another party as a reason, a claim, or a reasoned plea.

It is therefore better to understand ḥujjah first through its place in the structure of argumentation, rather than through our conclusion about whether it is right or wrong.

حَاجَّ (ḥājja): Entering an Argumentative Confrontation

Form III of this root occurs twelve times in the Qur’an. In these uses, people confront one another with arguments about a claim or subject.

For example:

قُلْ أَتُحَاجُّونَنَا فِي اللَّهِ وَهُوَ رَبُّنَا وَرَبُّكُمْ

Qul atuḥājjūnanā fī Allāhi wa-huwa rabbunā wa-rabbukum.

Say: Do you argue with us concerning The God, while He is our Lord and your Lord?

Al-Baqarah 2:139

And:

أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى الَّذِي حَاجَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ فِي رَبِّهِ

Alam tara ilā alladhī ḥājja Ibrāhīma fī rabbihi.

Have you not seen the one who argued with Abraham concerning his Lord?

Al-Baqarah 2:258

And:

وَحَاجَّهُ قَوْمُهُ قَالَ أَتُحَاجُّونِّي فِي اللَّهِ وَقَدْ هَدَانِ

Wa-ḥājjahu qawmuhu qāla atuḥājjūnnī fī Allāhi wa-qad hadāni.

His people argued with him. He said: Do you argue with me concerning The God, when He has guided me?

Al-Anam 6:80

حَاجَّ (ḥājja) itself does not identify which party is right. The verb describes entering an argumentative confrontation; the validity or invalidity of a position must be understood through knowledge, content, and context.

Knowledge and Argumentative Confrontation

Al-Imran 3:66 is one of the most important ayahs for understanding this point:

هَا أَنتُمْ هَٰؤُلَاءِ حَاجَجْتُمْ فِيمَا لَكُم بِهِ عِلْمٌ فَلِمَ تُحَاجُّونَ فِيمَا لَيْسَ لَكُم بِهِ عِلْمٌ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

Hā antum hāʾulāʾi ḥājajtum fīmā lakum bihi ʿilmun fa-lima tuḥājjūna fīmā laysa lakum bihi ʿilmun wa-Allāhu yaʿlamu wa-antum lā taʿlamūna.

Here you are: you argued about what you had knowledge of; why, then, do you argue about what you have no knowledge of? The God knows, and you do not know.

Al-Imran 3:66

This ayah establishes a clear relationship between argumentative confrontation and knowledge. The Qur’an does not treat the mere ability to speak, debate, or present a claim as sufficient to make an argument valid; it censures arguing about something of which one has no knowledge.

This does not mean, however, that simply possessing knowledge automatically makes every argument sound. The ayah identifies lack of knowledge as an evident problem in argumentation; an argument’s ultimate validity still depends on its content as well.

The distinction matters: a person can engage in argument without knowledge, just as they can offer an argument that ultimately proves dāḥiḍah, collapsed and invalid.

يَتَحَاجُّونَ (yataḥājjūna): Mutual Argumentation

Form VI occurs only once in the Qur’an:

وَإِذْ يَتَحَاجُّونَ فِي النَّارِ فَيَقُولُ الضُّعَفَاءُ لِلَّذِينَ اسْتَكْبَرُوا إِنَّا كُنَّا لَكُمْ تَبَعًا

Wa-idh yataḥājjūna fī al-nāri fa-yaqūlu al-ḍuʿafāʾu li-alladhīna istakbarū innā kunnā lakum tabaʿan.

And when they argue with one another in the Fire, the weak say to those who acted arrogantly: We were your followers...

Ghafir 40:47

Here the form يَتَحَاجُّونَ (yataḥājjūna) itself makes the reciprocal character evident: groups raise reasons, questions of responsibility, and claims against one another.

This is the Qur’an’s only occurrence of form VI from this root, and it happens to appear in a scene of the Hereafter. One example, however, does not justify a rule that form III belongs to this world and form VI to the Hereafter. The defensible distinction in this ayah is the explicitly reciprocal nature of the argumentation.

4. Connections Between the Root’s Branches and the Temporal Use of حِجَج (ḥijaj)

Do Hajj and Ḥujjah Derive from a Single Image?

An intelligible semantic connection can be envisaged between “intending a destination” and “ḥujjah.” Early lexicographers likewise described a ḥujjah as something a person seeks out in a dispute, or uses to move toward establishing what they intend to prove.

The existence of المَحَجَّة (al-maḥajjah), meaning a clear road, also makes this explanation conceptually intelligible: just as a road leads to a destination, an argument can provide a route by which a claim reaches a conclusion.

This explanation must not, however, be presented as an established historical derivation.

A semantic resemblance between two branches does not show that we know precisely when, or through what process, one developed from the other. Even early lexicographers did not explain every use of Ḥ J J through a single simple definition.

It is therefore better to say that intending and argument admit of a semantic connection, but the precise history of that connection is uncertain.

حِجَج (ḥijaj) Meaning Years

In the account of Moses we read:

قَالَ إِنِّي أُرِيدُ أَنْ أُنكِحَكَ إِحْدَى ابْنَتَيَّ هَاتَيْنِ عَلَىٰ أَن تَأْجُرَنِي ثَمَانِيَ حِجَجٍ فَإِنْ أَتْمَمْتَ عَشْرًا فَمِنْ عِندِكَ

Qāla innī urīdu an unkiḥaka iḥdā ibnatayya hātayni ʿalā an taʾjuranī thamāniya ḥijajin fa-in atmamta ʿashran fa-min ʿindika.

He said: I wish to marry one of these two daughters of mine to you, on condition that you work for me for eight years; if you complete ten, that will be of your own accord.

Al-Qasas 28:27

ثَمَانِيَ حِجَجٍ (thamāniya ḥijajin) clearly means eight years in this context.

One early explanation is that, because hajj was a recurring season within the annual cycle, a حِجَّة (ḥijjah) could denote a period of one year. This connection is intelligible, but the exact historical origin of the use should not be asserted more firmly than the evidence allows.

حِجَّة (ḥijjah) also has a temporal use in the poetry of Labīd:

يَرُضْنَ صِعَابَ الدُّرِّ فِي كُلِّ حِجَّةٍ
وَإِنْ لَمْ تَكُنْ أَعْنَاقُهُنَّ عَوَاطِلَا

Yaruḍna ṣiʿāba al-durri fī kulli ḥijjatin / wa-in lam takun aʿnāquhunna ʿawāṭilā.

Lexicographers report both “year” and “season” for حِجَّة (ḥijjah) in this verse. That variation itself shows that the evidence is useful for identifying a temporal use, but insufficient to establish its precise origin.

In Al-Qasas 28:27, the natural rendering is eight years. Translating it as “eight hajjs” to preserve the root’s outward form would make the sentence’s meaning unclear.

5. Comparison with Related Roots and Words

Ḥ J J Compared with B R H N: Ḥujjah and Burhān

In addition to حُجَّة (ḥujjah), the Qur’an uses بُرْهَان (burhān) eight times.

For example:

قُلْ هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

Qul hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīna.

Say: Bring your proof, if you are truthful.

Al-Baqarah 2:111

And:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ قَدْ جَاءَكُم بُرْهَانٌ مِّن رَّبِّكُمْ

Yā ayyuhā al-nāsu qad jāʾakum burhānun min rabbikum.

O people, a proof has come to you from your Lord.

An-Nisa 4:174

Qur’anic usage shows a difference in emphasis. Ḥujjah can be what one party offers in argument, even if it is unsound or has collapsed. Burhān more often occupies the position of evidence that is expected actually to establish a claim, or is presented as a clear proof.

This difference is not absolute, but it matters for translation. In particular, not every حُجَّة (ḥujjah) should automatically be translated as “conclusive proof.”

Ḥ J J Compared with J D L: Argumentative Confrontation and Disputation

The root J D L also has extensive Qur’anic use in the field of discussion and dispute.

Interestingly, in the context of hajj the Qur’an itself says:

وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ

Wa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajji.

And there is to be no disputing during hajj.

Al-Baqarah 2:197

In Qur’anic usage, jidāl more strongly foregrounds the back-and-forth of debate, verbal contention, and dispute; muḥājjah is more closely connected with presenting an argument and confronting positions in an effort to establish a case.

The boundary is not absolute. Muḥājjah can be accompanied by jidāl, and jidāl can include reasoning. Muḥājjah should therefore not always be taken as calm, scholarly discussion, nor jidāl as invariably hostile conflict.

Ḥ J J Compared with KH Ṣ M: Argumentative Confrontation and Adversarial Dispute

The root KH Ṣ M presents another aspect of conflict.

The Qur’an says:

هَٰذَانِ خَصْمَانِ اخْتَصَمُوا فِي رَبِّهِمْ

Hādhāni khaṣmāni ikhtaṣamū fī rabbihim.

These are two opposing groups who disputed with one another concerning their Lord.

Al-Hajj 22:19

And in the account of David:

خَصْمَانِ بَغَىٰ بَعْضُنَا عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَنَا بِالْحَقِّ

Khaṣmāni baghā baʿḍunā ʿalā baʿḍin fa-uḥkum baynanā bi-al-ḥaqqi.

We are two opposing parties; one of us has wronged the other. Judge between us in truth.

Sad 38:22

In KH Ṣ M, the opposing parties themselves and the adversarial or disputed situation are more prominent. The root can approach the sphere of legal proceedings and the parties to a case, although it is not always restricted to a court.

In حاجّ (ḥājja), the focus is more on what a party does in a dispute: presenting an argument and confronting another person over a subject.

We can therefore say:

Khaṣm more strongly identifies the position of opposing parties;
muḥājjah more strongly foregrounds the argumentative confrontation between them.

Again, this is a difference in emphasis, not an absolute boundary.

Ḥ J J Compared with Q Ṣ D: Intending and Hajj

Lexicographers repeatedly use qaṣd, intending, to explain the earlier meaning of hajj, but the two roots are not identical in the Qur’an.

Qaṣd has a broader field involving orientation, a path, and moderation:

وَعَلَى اللَّهِ قَصْدُ السَّبِيلِ

Wa-ʿalā Allāhi qaṣdu al-sabīli.

And upon The God rests the straight way.

An-Nahl 16:9

And:

وَاقْصِدْ فِي مَشْيِكَ

Wa-iqṣid fī mashyika.

And be moderate in your walking.

Luqman 31:19

“Intending” is therefore useful for explaining the earlier core of hajj, but not every act of intending is hajj. In the Qur’an, hajj to the House names a particular rite and should not be reduced to merely “intending to go to the House.”

6. Challenges in Understanding and Restrictions of Meaning

Hajj Is Not Merely the Name of Rites from the Islamic Period

Early linguistic evidence shows that حَجَّ (ḥajja) could be used for seeking out and repeatedly visiting other people and destinations. “Performing the rites of hajj to Mecca” is therefore not the definition of the root itself.

Yet Qur’anic Hajj Should Not Be Translated Merely as “Intending”

In expressions such as الْحَجّ (al-ḥajj), حِجُّ الْبَيْتِ (ḥijju al-bayti), and الْحَاجّ (al-ḥājj), the Qur’an speaks of a recognized rite. Etymology must not erase the word’s established meaning in Qur’anic context.

“Known Months” Must Not Become “The Days of Hajj”

In الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَّعْلُومَاتٌ (al-ḥajju ashhurun maʿlūmātun), أَشْهُر (ashhur) explicitly means months. Identifying the days on which the various acts are performed is a separate matter and should not enter the translation of this expression.

يَوْمَ الْحَجِّ الْأَكْبَرِ (yawma al-ḥajji al-akbari) Does Not Obtain Its Specific Referent from the Root

The root Ḥ J J does not identify exactly which day is “the day of the greater hajj.” This must be examined separately through context and historical evidence.

Ḥujjah Does Not Always Mean a Sound Argument

The use of حُجَّتُهُمْ دَاحِضَةٌ (ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍatun, their argument is invalid) makes this clear. A ḥujjah is something offered to establish a position; its soundness does not follow from the word itself.

Engaging in Argument Does Not Mean Being Right

The Qur’an speaks of argumentation without knowledge. The ability to enter a debate or present an argument therefore does not, by itself, show that a person is right.

Knowledge Matters in Argumentation, but Its Sufficiency Has Not Been Established

Al-Imran 3:66 censures arguing about something of which one has no knowledge. This shows that lack of knowledge is a fundamental defect in argumentation. The same ayah does not, however, establish that every argument accompanied by knowledge is necessarily valid.

The Branches of Ḥ J J Should Not Be Forced into a Single Image

Intending, hajj, ḥujjah, and ḥijaj have semantic connections that can be examined, but sharing a root does not by itself establish the precise history of their development.

7. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

حَجَّ الْبَيْتَ (ḥajja al-bayta):
“He performed hajj to the House,” or “he undertook hajj to the House.”

The root discussion can explain the earlier meaning of intending and turning toward, but the translation should not reduce hajj to a simple intention.

الْحَجّ (al-ḥajj):
“Hajj.”

In the Qur’an, the word itself names a recognized rite.

حِجُّ الْبَيْتِ (ḥijju al-bayti):
“Hajj to the House.”

الْحَاجّ (al-ḥājj):
“The hajj pilgrim.”

حُجَّة (ḥujjah):
Depending on context: “ḥujjah,” “reason,” “argument,” or “the evidence invoked.”

It should not consistently be rendered as “conclusive proof.”

حُجَّتُهُمْ دَاحِضَةٌ (ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍatun):
“Their argument has collapsed,” or “their argument is invalid.”

الْحُجَّةُ الْبَالِغَةُ (al-ḥujjatu al-bālighatu):
“The fully effective argument,” or “the argument that fully reaches its end,” depending on the final translation’s style.

لِئَلَّا يَكُونَ لِلنَّاسِ عَلَى اللَّهِ حُجَّةٌ (li-allā yakūna li-l-nāsi ʿalā Allāhi ḥujjatun):
“So that people may have no argument against The God.”

The explanation can clarify that this means a reason or claim that could be raised in argument.

حَاجَّ / يُحَاجُّ (ḥājja / yuḥājju):
“He argued a case,” “he presented an argument,” or, in some contexts, “he reasoned with him.”

“He disputed” is not always the best equivalent, because it brings the expression too close to the field of J D L.

يَتَحَاجُّونَ (yataḥājjūna):
“They argue with one another,” or “they present arguments against one another.”

ثَمَانِيَ حِجَجٍ (thamāniya ḥijajin):
“Eight years.”

Conclusion

The root Ḥ J J has several branches in the Qur’an that should not be reduced to one simple meaning.

In one of its earliest Arabic uses, حَجَّ (ḥajja) concerns intending, turning toward, and repeatedly visiting a person or destination. Early Arabic poetry preserves this use outside the pilgrimage to Mecca. Within the same linguistic environment, hajj to the House was also a recognized rite before Islam, and the Qur’an uses its name for an audience familiar with the season itself.

In the Qur’an, hajj in expressions such as حَجَّ الْبَيْتَ (ḥajja al-bayta), الْحَجّ (al-ḥajj), حِجُّ الْبَيْتِ (ḥijju al-bayti), and الْحَاجّ (al-ḥājj) belongs to the recognized rite of the House. The earlier meaning of intending remains discernible behind the word, but Qur’anic hajj cannot be translated merely as “intending to go to the house.”

The other major branch is ḥujjah and muḥājjah: an argument and argumentative confrontation. A ḥujjah is what is offered in argument to establish a position, but the word itself does not guarantee its validity. The Qur’an speaks both of حُجَّتُنَا (ḥujjatunā, Our argument) and الْحُجَّةُ الْبَالِغَةُ (al-ḥujjatu al-bālighatu, the fully effective argument), and of حُجَّتُهُمْ دَاحِضَةٌ (ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍatun, their argument is invalid) and the argument of people whose reasoning is not accepted.

The same applies to muḥājjah. A person can argue while lacking knowledge. Al-Imran 3:66 clearly challenges argumentation without knowledge. In the Qur’an’s own reasoning, merely participating in debate, possessing verbal power, or offering a ḥujjah is therefore insufficient to establish the validity of a position.

Alongside these two branches, حِجَج (ḥijaj) in Al-Qasas 28:27 means years—a use probably connected with the recurring annual cycle and the hajj season, although the precise history of its emergence is uncertain.

A concise and cautious definition of this root is therefore:

Ḥ J J: A linguistic family in which one early branch denotes intending and turning toward a destination, with hajj to the House becoming the name of a particular rite within this field; another branch concerns presenting arguments and engaging in argumentative confrontation to establish a position. A ḥujjah in this usage is not necessarily sound: its validity is determined by knowledge, content, and context.

This definition preserves hajj’s earlier meaning without erasing its ritual use in the Qur’an, avoids equating ḥujjah with conclusive proof, and retains the differences in emphasis between argumentative confrontation, disputation, and adversarial conflict.
