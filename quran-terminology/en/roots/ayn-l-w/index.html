Height, superiority, elevation, and self-exaltation: from high places to divine exaltedness

The Arabic root ʿ L W denotes being high, rising, and occupying a higher position. This can be spatial and perceptible, such as the top of a building or mountain, or concern status, standing, power, and superiority. Something occupying a higher position relative to another, in space, rank, or authority, falls within this semantic field.

The Quran uses this family broadly: for high heavens, gardens and elevated places; one side’s superiority over another; divine exaltedness; and people seeking superiority through arrogance, domination, and transgression.

Height and superiority are not, by themselves, either moral virtues or sins. Their value depends on the nature, means, purpose, and context of the superiority. The God is called al-ʿAliyy and al-Mutaʿāl, while Pharaoh too is said to have ʿalā in the land. These share a lexical family, but not the same reality or moral value.

Quranic forms include ʿalā and other inflections of ʿalā–yaʿlū, such as taʿlū and la-taʿlunna; taʿālā, taʿālaw, taʿālayna, istaʿlā, ʿuluww, ʿaliyy, aʿlā, al-aʿlawn, ʿulyā, ʿulā, ʿālin, inflected forms of ʿālī, ʿālīn, ʿāliyah, al-mutaʿāl, and ʿilliyyīn/ʿilliyyūn. These are dictionary forms or families of inflected forms, not all independent Quranic spellings.

Each form requires analysis in its own construction and context. In particular, taʿālaw as an invitation to come does not have the same use as taʿālā describing exaltedness.

1. Historical meaning and witnesses attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic

Witnesses attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic attest spatial height, perceptible elevation, and elevated standing. The central meaning is relational: something is above another, moves upward, or is considered higher in rank or esteem.

Two principal fields are physical height and elevated standing.

Spatial and perceptible height

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the celebrated pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays describes a swift horse:

مِكَرٍّ مِفَرٍّ مُقْبِلٍ مُدْبِرٍ مَعًا
كَجُلْمُودِ صَخْرٍ حَطَّهُ السَّيْلُ مِنْ عَلِ

mikarrin mifarrin muqbilin mudbirin maʿan / ka-julmūdi ṣakhrin ḥaṭṭahu al-saylu min ʿali

“My horse is agile in charging and retreating, advancing and withdrawing together, like a great boulder swept down from a height by the torrent.”

Min ʿali means “from above” or “from a high place.” The poet conveys the horse’s speed and force by comparing it to a large rock hurtling down from a height.

The spatial sense is clear: this concerns upper and lower positions in perceptible space, not status, honor, or domination.

This is a familiar witness in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Recorded variants of the final form ʿali do not obscure the upward direction it denotes.

Arabic also records ʿalā al-jabala, “he climbed the mountain,” and ʿulw al-dār, “the upper part of the house.”

Elevated standing and social position

A passage attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Taʾabbaṭa Sharran reads:

وَلَا ابْنِ وَهَيْبٍ كَاسِبِ الْحَمْدِ وَالْعُلَا

wa-lā ibni wahībin kāsibi al-ḥamdi wa-l-ʿulā

“Nor Ibn Wahīb, the winner of praise and eminence.”

Here al-ʿulā accompanies al-ḥamd, “praise.” The meaning concerns eminence, greatness, and social esteem, not spatial elevation.

The witness illustrates that the semantic field in poetry attributed to the pre-Quranic period extended beyond physical height to human rank and esteem.

The poet’s period, attribution of the passage, and actual date of composition must nevertheless be distinguished. These witnesses support early meanings without independently dating the emergence of every semantic branch.

Classical Arabic also records different verbal forms: ʿalā–yaʿlū for rising, being high, and prevailing, and ʿaliya–yaʿlā for elevated standing and honor. Their overlap prevents an absolute rule that one is always spatial and the other always figurative.

Core meaning

Historical witnesses and Arabic usages support the following core:

Being high, rising, or occupying a higher position relative to something or someone else, in space, rank, standing, or power.

Prevailing and overcoming also fit this core: the prevailing party gains the upper hand in a relationship of power.

Oppression, arrogance, purity, holiness, and rightful standing are not invariant components of the root. They must be identified from the derivative, construction, and context.

2. Spatial height, elevated standing, and superiority

Quranic uses place perceptible height and superiority in rank within one family, but do not make their translations interchangeable.

Upper and lower: perceptible positions

The account of the destruction of Lot’s people says:

فَلَمَّا جَاءَ أَمْرُنَا جَعَلْنَا عَالِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا

fa-lammā jāʾa amrunā jaʿalnā ʿāliyahā sāfilahā

Hūd 11:82

“When Our command came, We made its upper part its lower part.”

Hūd 11:82

The same upper-to-lower construction recurs in al-Ḥijr 15:74.

ʿĀliyahā denotes the upper part of that place and sāfilahā its lower part. Their opposition makes the spatial relationship explicit.

No moral superiority is expressed here. Upper and lower parts do not, by those descriptions alone, acquire moral value.

Another verse says:

فِي جَنَّةٍ عَالِيَةٍ

fī jannatin ʿāliyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:22; Al-Ghāshiyah 88:10

“In an elevated garden.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:22

The same expression occurs in al-Ghāshiyah 88:10.

ʿĀliyah can express the garden’s elevated position. Exalted standing also fits the heavenly context, but the adjective alone does not specify a numerical altitude or a particular tier in a spatial system.

Al-Insān 76:21 says:

عَالِيَهُمْ ثِيَابُ سُنْدُسٍ خُضْرٌ وَإِسْتَبْرَقٌ

ʿāliyahum thiyābu sundusin khuḍrun wa-istabraq

Al-Insān 76:21

“Upon them are garments of green fine silk and brocade.”

This construction concerns clothing upon them. The family can thus describe an object’s position relative to a body or another object without denoting superiority of rank or power.

An elevated position

Maryam 19:57 says of Idrīs:

وَرَفَعْنَاهُ مَكَانًا عَلِيًّا

wa-rafaʿnāhu makānan ʿaliyyā

Maryam 19:57

“And We raised him to a high station.”

Rafaʿnāhu, from R F ʿ, expresses raising; ʿaliyyan describes the elevated station.

The two roots have distinct roles: raising and the height of the resulting position.

The verse describes Idrīs’s elevation, but makānan ʿaliyyan alone supplies no spatial coordinates.

Maryam 19:50 also says:

وَجَعَلْنَا لَهُمْ لِسَانَ صِدْقٍ عَلِيًّا

wa-jaʿalnā lahum lisāna ṣidqin ʿaliyyā

Maryam 19:50

“And We granted them an exalted, truthful renown.”

Here ʿaliyyan qualifies lisān ṣidq, not an elevated physical place. It concerns their honored remembrance and good name.

Both verses illustrate exaltedness, while the accompanying noun determines its kind.

A higher rank or position

Aʿlā is an elative form of the family and can mean higher, superior, or highest.

Al-Najm 53:7 says:

وَهُوَ بِالْأُفُقِ الْأَعْلَىٰ

wa-huwa bi-l-ufuqi al-aʿlā

Al-Najm 53:7

“While he was at the higher horizon.”

Aʿlā qualifies “horizon” and concerns position and direction.

Ṭā Hā 20:68, however, addresses Moses:

قُلْنَا لَا تَخَفْ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْأَعْلَىٰ

qulnā lā takhaf innaka anta al-aʿlā

Ṭā Hā 20:68

“We said: Do not fear; you will indeed have the upper hand.”

This concerns Moses’s confrontation with Pharaoh’s sorcerers. Superiority in that encounter is intended, not Moses standing physically above them.

Āl ʿImrān 3:139 says:

وَلَا تَهِنُوا وَلَا تَحْزَنُوا وَأَنْتُمُ الْأَعْلَوْنَ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ مُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-lā tahinū wa-lā taḥzanū wa-antumu al-aʿlawna in kuntum muʾminīn

Āl ʿImrān 3:139

“Do not weaken or grieve; you are superior, if you are believers.”

Al-aʿlawn is plural and expresses superiority. Its condition, “if you are believers,” prevents treating it as an assertion of inherent superiority for everyone adopting a Muslim or believer label.

The same form occurs in Muḥammad 47:35:

فَلَا تَهِنُوا وَتَدْعُوا إِلَى السَّلْمِ وَأَنْتُمُ الْأَعْلَوْنَ

fa-lā tahinū wa-tadʿū ilā al-salmi wa-antumu al-aʿlawn

Muḥammad 47:35

“So do not weaken and call for peace while you have the upper hand.”

This passage concerns confrontation and steadfastness. Aʿlawn expresses superiority or the upper hand, but does not by itself establish an unrestricted prohibition of peace in all circumstances.

ʿUlyā and ʿulā: higher rank

Al-Tawbah 9:40 says:

وَجَعَلَ كَلِمَةَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا السُّفْلَىٰ وَكَلِمَةُ اللَّهِ هِيَ الْعُلْيَا

wa-jaʿala kalimata alladhīna kafarū al-suflā wa-kalimatu allāhi hiya al-ʿulyā

Al-Tawbah 9:40

“And He made the word of those who disbelieved the lower, while the word of The God is the higher.”

Al-ʿulyā is feminine elative, opposed to al-suflā. The contrast concerns the standing and authority of the word, not the volume of speakers’ voices.

Ṭā Hā 20:4 says:

تَنْزِيلًا مِمَّنْ خَلَقَ الْأَرْضَ وَالسَّمَاوَاتِ الْعُلَى

tanzīlan mimman khalaqa al-arḍa wa-l-samāwāti al-ʿulā

Ṭā Hā 20:4

“A sending down from the One who created the earth and the high heavens.”

Al-ʿulā here qualifies the heavens.

Ṭā Hā 20:75 says of believers who do good:

فَأُولَٰئِكَ لَهُمُ الدَّرَجَاتُ الْعُلَىٰ

fa-ulāʾika lahumu al-darajātu al-ʿulā

Ṭā Hā 20:75

“Those will have the higher ranks.”

Here the same adjectival family describes ranks and degrees.

ʿUlyā and ʿulā thus concern height or higher rank, while the accompanying noun identifies what is high: a word, heavens, or degrees.

3. ʿUluww and istiʿlāʾ: from prevailing to oppressive self-exaltation

A major Quranic use describes power that becomes domination and rebellion.

Pharaoh’s self-exaltation in the land

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:4 says:

إِنَّ فِرْعَوْنَ عَلَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَجَعَلَ أَهْلَهَا شِيَعًا يَسْتَضْعِفُ طَائِفَةً مِنْهُمْ

inna firʿawna ʿalā fī al-arḍi wa-jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan yastaḍʿifu ṭāʾifatan minhum

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:4

“Pharaoh exalted himself in the land and divided its people into factions, weakening a group of them.”

ʿAlā is a past-tense verb from ʿ L W. Here ʿalā fī al-arḍ denotes self-exaltation and the exercise of domination in society.

Oppression is not inferred from the root alone: the verse immediately describes Pharaoh dividing people and weakening a group.

His ʿuluww is therefore not merely possession of political power or office, but self-exaltation accompanied by domination, discrimination, and the weakening of others.

Yūnus 10:83 says:

وَإِنَّ فِرْعَوْنَ لَعَالٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَإِنَّهُ لَمِنَ الْمُسْرِفِينَ

wa-inna firʿawna la-ʿālin fī al-arḍi wa-innahu la-mina al-musrifīn

Yūnus 10:83

“Pharaoh was indeed exalted in the land, and he was among those who exceeded the bounds.”

ʿĀlin is an active participle describing superiority or ascendancy. Its association with al-musrifīn identifies transgressive power.

Al-Dukhān 44:31 likewise says of Pharaoh:

إِنَّهُ كَانَ عَالِيًا مِنَ الْمُسْرِفِينَ

innahu kāna ʿāliyan mina al-musrifīn

Al-Dukhān 44:31

“He was indeed self-exalting, among those who exceeded the bounds.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:46 says of Pharaoh and his chiefs:

فَاسْتَكْبَرُوا وَكَانُوا قَوْمًا عَالِينَ

fa-stakbarū wa-kānū qawman ʿālīn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:46

“They grew arrogant and were a self-exalting people.”

Istakbarū belongs to K B R and ʿālīn to ʿ L W. Their combination connects arrogance with self-exaltation in that group without making the roots fully synonymous. Arrogance concerns magnifying oneself; ʿuluww here also conveys domination and asserted superiority.

Self-exaltation can be deliberate

Al-Naml 27:14 says:

وَجَحَدُوا بِهَا وَاسْتَيْقَنَتْهَا أَنْفُسُهُمْ ظُلْمًا وَعُلُوًّا

wa-jaḥadū bihā wa-stayqanathā anfusuhum ẓulman wa-ʿuluwwā

Al-Naml 27:14

“They denied those signs, though inwardly certain of them, out of injustice and self-exaltation.”

ʿUluwwan accompanies ẓulman, describing deliberate denial of truth.

The desire to retain superiority and domination can thus lead people to deny what they inwardly know to be true.

This cannot be generalized to every opponent or denier. Here their inward certainty is explicit. Without such evidence, disagreement or denial cannot automatically be classified as knowing rejection motivated by self-exaltation.

Self-exaltation in the land: an objectionable aim

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:83 says:

تِلْكَ الدَّارُ الْآخِرَةُ نَجْعَلُهَا لِلَّذِينَ لَا يُرِيدُونَ عُلُوًّا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا فَسَادًا

tilka al-dāru al-ākhiratu najʿaluhā li-lladhīna lā yurīdūna ʿuluwwan fī al-arḍi wa-lā fasādā

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:83

“That abode of the Hereafter We assign to those who desire neither self-exaltation in the land nor corruption.”

This follows the account of Qārūn, who displayed his wealth and splendor before the community and whose end is described in preceding verses.

Here ʿuluwwan fī al-arḍ concerns the desire for superiority and domination. Its association with corruption clarifies the objectionable aim.

Lā yurīdūna is significant: the concern is not only refraining from an outward action, but not desiring or pursuing such superiority.

Someone may lack power yet seek it to humiliate and dominate others. Conversely, responsibility, knowledge, ability, and social standing do not inherently constitute blameworthy ʿuluww.

The condemnation concerns a desire for domination and corruption, not every achievement, capacity, or higher position.

The Children of Israel: distinct usages

Al-Isrāʾ 17:4 says:

لَتُفْسِدُنَّ فِي الْأَرْضِ مَرَّتَيْنِ وَلَتَعْلُنَّ عُلُوًّا كَبِيرًا

la-tufsidunna fī al-arḍi marratayni wa-la-taʿlunna ʿuluwwan kabīrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:4

“You will surely cause corruption in the land twice and engage in great self-exaltation.”

La-taʿlunna is emphatic, and ʿuluwwan a verbal noun of the same family. Together they express the intensity of self-exaltation in the context of corruption.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:7 also says:

وَلِيُتَبِّرُوا مَا عَلَوْا تَتْبِيرًا

wa-li-yutabbirū mā ʿalaw tatbīrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:7

“And utterly destroy whatever they overcame.”

On this reading, ʿalaw denotes overcoming what they destroy. Another grammatical interpretation treats mā as introducing duration, yielding destruction for as long as they prevailed. Both differ from the preceding description of corrupt self-exaltation.

This variation shows why the root should not always be translated as arrogance.

Istiʿlāʾ: gaining the upper hand

In Ṭā Hā 20:64, the sorcerers say of their confrontation with Moses:

فَأَجْمِعُوا كَيْدَكُمْ ثُمَّ ائْتُوا صَفًّا وَقَدْ أَفْلَحَ الْيَوْمَ مَنِ اسْتَعْلَىٰ

fa-ajmiʿū kaydakum thumma ʾtū ṣaffan wa-qad aflaḥa al-yawma mani istaʿlā

Ṭā Hā 20:64

“So unite your plan, then come in ranks; today the one who gains the upper hand will succeed.”

Istaʿlā is Form X and here denotes prevailing or gaining superiority in a confrontation.

The speakers are describing competition and struggle. Their claim of success is not a Quranic moral endorsement of everyone who defeats another.

Nor does Form X alone establish that every istiʿlāʾ necessarily involves oppression or arrogance. Its moral bearing depends on speaker and context.

Solomon’s letter to the queen of Sheba says in al-Naml 27:31:

أَلَّا تَعْلُوا عَلَيَّ وَأْتُونِي مُسْلِمِينَ

allā taʿlū ʿalayya wa-ʾtūnī muslimīn

Al-Naml 27:31

“Do not exalt yourselves against me, and come to me in submission.”

In al-Dukhān 44:19 Moses says:

وَأَنْ لَا تَعْلُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ إِنِّي آتِيكُمْ بِسُلْطَانٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-an lā taʿlū ʿalā allāhi innī ātīkum bi-sulṭānin mubīn

Al-Dukhān 44:19

“And do not exalt yourselves against The God; I come to you with a clear authority.”

These constructions combine the verb with ʿalā and the party against whom superiority is asserted. This can express rebellion, confrontation, and a claim to the upper hand.

Al-ʿālīn: high standing or asserted superiority?

Ṣād 38:75 addresses Iblīs:

أَسْتَكْبَرْتَ أَمْ كُنْتَ مِنَ الْعَالِينَ

astakbarta am kunta mina al-ʿālīn

Ṣād 38:75

“Have you grown arrogant, or were you among the exalted?”

Al-ʿālīn can denote those occupying a high rank, but the word itself does not identify their nature or identity.

The opposition with astakbarta does not, without further evidence, establish a particular ontological class of superior beings with different powers or obligations.

This is a reproachful question concerning Iblīs’s disobedience and arrogance. The lexical form alone cannot establish genuine or merely claimed exaltedness.

4. Taʿālaw: an invitation to come, not to seek superiority

The imperative taʿālaw and its feminine plural taʿālayna form an important, distinct Quranic branch.

Arabic taʿāla is established as an invitation meaning “come” or “approach.” Traditional explanations connect it with calling someone to a higher place and a subsequent extension of use.

Established usage does not require the caller to stand above the person addressed. Someone below can say taʿāla to someone higher up.

It therefore cannot always be translated “come up,” “become spiritually elevated,” or “reach a higher rank.”

An invitation to a common word

Āl ʿImrān 3:64 says:

قُلْ يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ تَعَالَوْا إِلَىٰ كَلِمَةٍ سَوَاءٍ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَكُمْ

qul yā ahla al-kitābi taʿālaw ilā kalimatin sawāʾin baynanā wa-baynakum

Āl ʿImrān 3:64

“Say: People of the Book, come to a word equitable between us and you.”

Taʿālaw issues the invitation, while “to a word equitable” identifies its destination.

The direct meaning is “come.” Any movement toward truth, justice, or a shared monotheistic principle comes from the invitation’s content, not an inherent meaning of spiritual elevation in the verb.

Āl ʿImrān 3:61 uses the same verb in the invitation to mutual invocation:

فَقُلْ تَعَالَوْا نَدْعُ أَبْنَاءَنَا وَأَبْنَاءَكُمْ

fa-qul taʿālaw nadʿu abnāʾanā wa-abnāʾakum

Āl ʿImrān 3:61

“Then say: Come, let us call our children and your children.”

Again, the verb invites people to attend and undertake a shared action.

An invitation to listen or refer a matter

Al-Anʿām 6:151 says:

قُلْ تَعَالَوْا أَتْلُ مَا حَرَّمَ رَبُّكُمْ عَلَيْكُمْ

qul taʿālaw atlu mā ḥarrama rabbukum ʿalaykum

Al-Anʿām 6:151

“Say: Come, I will recite what your Lord has forbidden to you.”

Here taʿālaw introduces an invitation to listen and attend.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:61 and al-Māʾidah 5:104 similarly invite people to what The God has sent down and to the Messenger.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:5 uses taʿālaw in an invitation to come so that the Messenger may seek forgiveness for them.

Āl ʿImrān 3:167 invites a group to come and fight in the way of The God or defend, but they give a different answer. The verb itself invites coming; the following words specify the action and its value.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:28 uses the feminine plural:

فَتَعَالَيْنَ أُمَتِّعْكُنَّ وَأُسَرِّحْكُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا

fa-taʿālayna umattiʿkunna wa-usarriḥkunna sarāḥan jamīlā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:28

“Then come, I will provide for you and release you with a gracious release.”

This addresses the Prophet’s wives. Taʿālayna matches the feminine plural addressees.

The established meaning in these invitational uses is “come.” They should not be confused with taʿālā describing The God.

5. Divine exaltedness: al-ʿAliyy, al-Aʿlā, and al-Mutaʿāl

Divine uses are a major part of the root’s Quranic semantic network.

In these contexts, height concerns the superior standing, greatness, rule, and self-sufficiency of The God. The words alone do not determine a physical location.

The forms must nevertheless remain distinct: al-ʿAliyy, al-Aʿlā, al-Mutaʿāl, and the verb taʿālā are not morphologically identical and must not be arbitrarily substituted for one another.

Al-ʿAliyy: the Exalted

Al-Baqarah 2:255 says:

وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْعَظِيمُ

wa-huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-ʿaẓīm

Al-Baqarah 2:255

“And He is the Exalted, the Great.”

Al-ʿAliyy describes divine exaltedness and here accompanies al-ʿAẓīm.

The verse concerns ownership of the heavens and earth, encompassing knowledge, permission for intercession, and preservation of creation. Exaltedness is therefore understood alongside divine authority, greatness, and sovereignty.

The word itself supplies no particular location. Describing The God as exalted does not alone place Him at a point with specified physical coordinates.

Al-Ḥajj 22:62 and Luqmān 31:30 say:

وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْكَبِيرُ

wa-anna allāha huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-kabīr

Al-Ḥajj 22:62; Luqmān 31:30

“And The God is the Exalted, the Great.”

Divine exaltedness is contrasted here with the falsehood of fabricated objects of worship.

Sabaʾ 34:23, following discussion of intercession and permission, says:

وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْكَبِيرُ

wa-huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-kabīr

Sabaʾ 34:23

“And He is the Exalted, the Great.”

Al-Shūrā 42:4 places al-ʿAliyy al-ʿAẓīm alongside ownership of the heavens and earth; Ghāfir 40:12 uses Allāh al-ʿAliyy al-Kabīr.

A warning concerning human domination

Al-Nisāʾ 4:34 says:

فَإِنْ أَطَعْنَكُمْ فَلَا تَبْغُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ سَبِيلًا إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلِيًّا كَبِيرًا

fa-in aṭaʿnakum fa-lā tabghū ʿalayhinna sabīlan inna allāha kāna ʿaliyyan kabīrā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:34

“If they obey you, seek no way against them; The God is indeed Exalted and Great.”

This occurs in the context of marital relations and disagreement. Divine exaltedness and greatness follow the prohibition of seeking a way against the women.

The ending places a limit on human power. Power or responsibility within a family does not authorize pressure, revenge, or oppression. Divine exaltedness places human power within its limited and accountable position.

This conclusion comes from context. ʿAliyyan does not lexically mean “preventing a man from oppressing”; that message arises from the divine description and the preceding instruction together.

Al-Aʿlā: the Highest

Al-Aʿlā 87:1 says:

سَبِّحِ اسْمَ رَبِّكَ الْأَعْلَى

sabbiḥi isma rabbika al-aʿlā

Al-Aʿlā 87:1

“Glorify the name of your Lord, the Highest.”

Al-aʿlā is an elative form, here qualifying the Lord.

Al-Layl 92:20 says:

إِلَّا ابْتِغَاءَ وَجْهِ رَبِّهِ الْأَعْلَىٰ

illā ibtighāʾa wajhi rabbihi al-aʿlā

Al-Layl 92:20

“Only seeking the face of his Lord, the Highest.”

These describe The God, but the form aʿlā is not exclusively divine.

For example, Pharaoh claims in al-Nāziʿāt 79:24:

أَنَا رَبُّكُمُ الْأَعْلَىٰ

anā rabbukumu al-aʿlā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:24

“I am your highest lord.”

This is Pharaoh’s claim, not Quranic approval. The form can occur in a human claim; its truth or falsehood depends on speaker and context.

Al-Naḥl 16:60 and al-Rūm 30:27 use al-mathal al-aʿlā concerning The God. Aʿlā qualifies mathal and must be interpreted with that noun, rather than simply equated with al-ʿAliyy.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:8 and Ṣād 38:69 use al-malaʾ al-aʿlā. Here aʿlā describes an assembly identified as higher or exalted. The adjective does not fully specify its nature or location.

Al-Mutaʿāl: exalted and transcendent

Al-Raʿd 13:9 says:

عَالِمُ الْغَيْبِ وَالشَّهَادَةِ الْكَبِيرُ الْمُتَعَالِ

ʿālimu al-ghaybi wa-l-shahādati al-kabīru al-mutaʿāl

Al-Raʿd 13:9

“Knower of the unseen and the witnessed, the Great, the Exalted.”

Al-mutaʿāl belongs to the taʿālā family and describes The God.

Its morphology differs from al-ʿaliyy, although both concern exaltedness.

It accompanies knowledge of the unseen and witnessed and divine greatness. Exaltedness is understood alongside superior standing and encompassing knowledge and authority.

The form does not mean The God is moving from a lower to a higher rank or gradually attaining greater standing. In divine use it describes His exalted status.

Taʿālā: exalted above improper attributions

Taʿālā occurs repeatedly concerning The God.

Al-Anʿām 6:100 says:

سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَىٰ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ

subḥānahu wa-taʿālā ʿammā yaṣifūn

Al-Anʿām 6:100

“Glory be to Him; exalted is He above what they describe.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:43 says:

سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَىٰ عَمَّا يَقُولُونَ عُلُوًّا كَبِيرًا

subḥānahu wa-taʿālā ʿammā yaqūlūna ʿuluwwan kabīrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:43

“Glory be to Him; greatly exalted is He above what they say.”

Taʿālā with ʿammā expresses exaltedness above improper descriptions and attributions.

The verb also responds to associating partners and other deities with The God in al-Aʿrāf 7:190, Yūnus 10:18, al-Naḥl 16:1, al-Naḥl 16:3, al-Muʾminūn 23:92, al-Naml 27:63, al-Qaṣaṣ 28:68, al-Rūm 30:40, and al-Zumar 39:67.

Ṭā Hā 20:114 and al-Muʾminūn 23:116 say fa-taʿālā Allāhu al-maliku al-ḥaqq. Exaltedness here accompanies divine kingship and truth.

Al-Jinn 72:3 says:

وَأَنَّهُ تَعَالَىٰ جَدُّ رَبِّنَا مَا اتَّخَذَ صَاحِبَةً وَلَا وَلَدًا

wa-annahu taʿālā jaddu rabbinā mā ittakhadha ṣāḥibatan wa-lā waladā

Al-Jinn 72:3

“And exalted is the majesty of our Lord; He has taken neither companion nor child.”

Jaddu rabbinā concerns the Lord’s greatness and majesty, not the familiar Persian sense of jadd as grandfather.

Across these uses, taʿālā expresses exaltedness and superiority, but its divine context differs from describing a self-exalting human being.

6. ʿIlliyyīn and ʿilliyyūn: an elevated place or register

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:18 says:

كَلَّا إِنَّ كِتَابَ الْأَبْرَارِ لَفِي عِلِّيِّينَ

kallā inna kitāba al-abrāri la-fī ʿilliyyīn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:18

“No indeed! The record of the righteous is in ʿIlliyyīn.”

The next verse says:

وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا عِلِّيُّونَ

wa-mā adrāka mā ʿilliyyūn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19

“And what will make you know what ʿIlliyyūn is?”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19

ʿIlliyyīn and ʿilliyyūn are two case forms of one designation. It relates to ʿ L W and elevation, but functions here as a particular Quranic term.

Understanding it requires the preceding and following verses.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7 says:

كَلَّا إِنَّ كِتَابَ الْفُجَّارِ لَفِي سِجِّينٍ

kallā inna kitāba al-fujjāri la-fī sijjīn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7

“No indeed! The record of the wicked is in Sijjīn.”

The contrast between Sijjīn and ʿIlliyyīn concerns the recording of the wicked’s and the righteous’s deeds.

After mentioning ʿIlliyyūn, al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20 says:

كِتَابٌ مَرْقُومٌ

kitābun marqūm

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20

“An inscribed record.”

And al-Muṭaffifīn 83:21:

يَشْهَدُهُ الْمُقَرَّبُونَ

yashhaduhu al-muqarrabūn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:21

“Those brought near witness it.”

The passage associates ʿIlliyyīn with an exalted, distinguished standing and the record of the righteous. The lexical form alone does not conclusively establish whether it names the record’s place, an elevated register, or its rank.

Translating it directly as “the seventh heaven,” “the Throne,” a particular paradise, or a place with defined coordinates adds information absent from the word itself.

For Forqan, retaining ʿIlliyyīn is preferable, with its association with elevation explained separately.

7. Related roots and translation proposals

ʿ L W and S M W: height and elevation

S M W also concerns height and elevation, with a broad Quranic family including samāʾ and samāwāt.

Both share height and elevation. Quranic ʿ L W explicitly includes the upper hand, domination, self-exaltation, and higher rank; S M W is also prominently associated with naming the sky and heavens.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary.

ʿ L W and R F ʿ: being high and raising

R F ʿ principally expresses raising, lifting, or elevating something; ʿ L W can express being high, occupying a high rank, or gaining the upper hand.

In Maryam 19:57, rafaʿnāhu and ʿaliyyan illustrate the distinct roles of raising and the station’s height.

This does not mean ʿ L W never expresses motion: ʿalā al-jabala also means climbing the mountain.

ʿ L W and K B R: superiority and greatness

K B R concerns greatness and, in some forms, magnifying oneself. ʿ L W concerns height, higher rank, and ascendancy.

The divine expression al-ʿAliyy al-Kabīr places the descriptions together.

The human description “they grew arrogant and were a self-exalting people” likewise combines arrogance and self-exaltation.

Association does not transfer every meaning between the families. A person can hold higher social standing without arrogance, or magnify themselves without actual superiority over others.

ʿ L W and F W Q: upper position and relative superiority

Fawq expresses being above or over something else.

ʿ L W can express the same relation through verbs, adjectives, verbal nouns, and elative forms such as ʿalā, ʿuluww, ʿaliyy, and aʿlā.

Fawq and ʿuluww overlap in many situations but differ in grammatical role and range of use.

ʿ L W and Ṣ ʿ D: ascending and being above

Ṣ ʿ D chiefly expresses upward movement and ascent.

ʿ L W can express movement as well as occupying a high position, elevated standing, and prevailing.

Ṣuʿūd should not be generalized to every assertion of superiority, nor should ʿuluww always be understood as bodily upward motion.

The preposition ʿalā and root derivatives

The frequent Quranic word ʿalā is a preposition. It has a grammatical role in expressions such as ʿalā al-arḍ, ʿalayhim, and ʿalaykum.

Even where a historical semantic connection with height is discussed, the preposition is not counted among this study’s nominal and verbal root derivatives.

The verb ʿalā, “rose” or “prevailed,” is not identical to the preposition ʿalā.

This distinction is necessary for counting occurrences and analyzing morphology.

Translation proposals for Forqan

Renderings should fit the type of superiority and the sentence’s construction.

  • ʿAlā: rose, was above, prevailed, or overcame; in domination and corruption contexts, exalted oneself.
  • ʿUluww: height, elevation, superiority; in oppression contexts, self-exaltation or domination.
  • ʿAliyy: high, exalted, elevated in standing.
  • Al-ʿAliyy: the Exalted.
  • Aʿlā: higher, superior, highest, according to noun and context.
  • Al-aʿlawn: those who are superior or have the upper hand.
  • ʿUlyā/ʿulā: higher, high, exalted, according to the qualified noun.
  • ʿĀlin/ʿālī: high, upper, of superior standing; concerning Pharaoh, self-exalting.
  • ʿĀliyah: high, elevated.
  • Istaʿlā: prevailed, gained superiority or the upper hand.
  • Taʿālā: is exalted; with ʿammā, is exalted above what is attributed.
  • Al-mutaʿāl: exalted; in divine context, transcendence above deficiency may be discussed separately.
  • Taʿālaw/taʿālayna: come; an invitation to attend, refer, or act.
  • ʿIlliyyīn/ʿilliyyūn: retain the designation and explain its elevated place or register through context.

For ʿuluwwan fī al-arḍ in al-Qaṣaṣ 28:83, “self-exaltation in the land” is clearer than “height in the land,” given the desire for domination and corruption.

For wa-huwa al-ʿAliyy al-ʿAẓīm, “He is the Exalted, the Great” is natural.

For qul taʿālaw, “Say: Come” suffices; spiritual elevation should not replace the direct verbal meaning.

8. Summary

ʿ L W is a broad Quranic family centered on being high, rising, and occupying a higher position. This can concern physical, social, political, spiritual, or other ranks and standing.

Witnesses attributed to pre-Quranic Arabic illustrate both spatial height and elevated standing. The Quran uses this linguistic range in different situations.

Spatial ʿālī denotes an upper part, and ʿāliyah a high place. ʿAliyy and aʿlā can express elevated standing. Istaʿlā concerns gaining the upper hand in confrontation. Invitational taʿālaw means “come.” Divine al-ʿAliyy, al-Aʿlā, al-Mutaʿāl, and taʿālā express exaltedness.

A central distinction in human uses is that elevated standing is not identical to seeking domination.

People may attain high standing through knowledge, service, competence, responsibility, or good deeds; alternatively, they may seek superiority through oppression, humiliation, corruption, and domination. The family retains superiority in both situations, while conduct and context determine moral value.

Pharaoh’s ʿuluww accompanies dividing society, weakening a group, arrogance, and transgression. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:83 condemns the desire for self-exaltation and corruption, not every elevated position, ability, or responsibility.

Divine exaltedness concerns greatness, superiority, and authority. It should not be reduced to sitting at a physical location or taken to imply gradual movement toward a higher rank.

The root denotes being high or rising, and occupying or attaining a higher position relative to something or someone else, in space, rank, standing, or power. Physical elevation, eminence, prevailing, self-exaltation, and divine exaltedness are contextual branches; virtue, arrogance, oppression, and rightful standing do not follow from the root alone.

This distinction helps readers understand the greatness of al-ʿAliyy, recognize the danger of self-exaltation in the land, and distinguish attaining high standing from desiring domination over others.
