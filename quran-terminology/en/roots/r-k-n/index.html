Firm support, supporting power, and inclination toward what one relies upon

Brief meaning: A strong part of something that offers support; a source of backing and strength; and, in verbal usage, inclining, becoming attached, or relying upon someone or something.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The three-letter root R K N concerns firmness, support, strength, and inclination toward something trusted.

Physically, rukn denotes a corner or strong side of a building, mountain, or other object. In human and social settings, it can denote a supporter, supporting force, powerful kin group, or power on which someone relies.

The related expression رَكَنَ إِلَى means to incline, rely, or settle in reliance upon another.

This connection matters in the Qur’an: the family names both a support and, through its verbs, a person’s relationship with what they incline toward or trust.

Qur’anic uses concern both supporting power and inclination or reliance.

Lot’s story describes the need for strong support. Pharaoh turns away with his power and supporters. Hūd prohibits reliance on wrongdoers, while Al-Isrāʾ describes, conditionally, coming close to inclining toward those seeking to divert the Messenger from revelation.

These uses share a semantic field but differ in moral value.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an, through the noun rukn and the basic-form verb:

  • رُكْنٍ: An indefinite singular noun meaning support or supporting power; Hūd 11:80.
  • تَرْكَنُوا: A second-person plural imperfect basic-form verb, meaning to rely or incline; Hūd 11:113.
  • تَرْكَنُ: A second-person singular imperfect basic-form verb, meaning to incline or rely; Al-Isrāʾ 17:74.
  • بِرُكْنِهِ: Rukn with bi- and the pronoun -hi, meaning with his power or supporters; Al-Dhāriyāt 51:39.

Other recorded forms include رَكَنَ, رَكِنَ, يَرْكَنُ, يَرْكُنُ, رُكُون, رَاكِن, رَكِين, رَكَانَة, أَرْكَان, أَرْكُن, مِرْكَن, and مُرَكَّن.

They help establish the lexical family but do not constitute additional Qur’anic occurrences beyond the four identified above.

2. Semantic core and early Arabic usage

Rukn: a strong side and support

An early lexical analysis identifies one core meaning:

الرَّاءُ وَالْكَافُ وَالنُّونُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى قُوَّةٍ

al-rāʾu wa-al-kāfu wa-al-nūnu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā quwwatin

Translation: “Rāʾ, kāf, and nūn constitute one root indicating strength.”

It then describes rukn as the stronger side of something.

This account clearly relates to physical usage.

In a building, a rukn may be a corner or part contributing to its stability. The word also describes the strong, firm side of a mountain.

From this field, arkān denotes a whole’s principal parts or supports.

It does not follow that every rukn is a vertical column or separate foundation.

A building’s corner, a mountain’s side, a person’s backing, and social strength can share this designation while referring to different things.

The shared feature is firmness and the possibility of relying on something supportive.

Rakana ilā: inclining and settling in reliance upon another

Lexical accounts explain رَكَنَ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ as inclining toward something and finding reassurance in it.

Rukūn is the corresponding verbal noun.

This usage is semantically connected with rukn.

Someone may incline toward something because they regard it as supportive or reassuring.

For example, رَكَنَ إِلَى الدُّنْيَا concerns attachment or inclination to worldly life.

رَكِنَ فِي الْمَنْزِلِ describes remaining in a dwelling without leaving it; emotional attachment should not automatically be inferred.

Such uses extend beyond bodily movement toward something: inclination, attachment, or reliance may be involved.

This does not make every brief movement or moment of attention toward something an act of trust or dependence.

The object and preposition matter in verbal usage.

رَكَنَ إِلَى emphasizes inclination toward someone or something; context establishes its degree and consequences.

Rakīn and rakānah: firmness and composure

Rakīn describes someone composed, dignified, and steady in conduct.

Rakānah similarly concerns steadiness, dignity, and calm.

جَبَلٌ رَكِينٌ describes a firm, strong mountain.

These uses show firmness extending into descriptions of people.

A calm, measured person may be called rakīn.

Composure and dignity do not necessarily establish truth or justice.

Someone may appear calm and steadfast while persisting in an erroneous view.

The root therefore does not itself guarantee moral goodness. The object and direction of firmness or reliance determine their value.

Arkān: principal supporting parts

أَرْكَان and أَرْكُن are plurals of rukn.

Physically, they can denote the principal parts on which a building or other structure depends.

In later Arabic, arkān also denotes foundational components of a discipline, institution, contract, or act.

Expressions corresponding to “pillars of government,” “elements of a contract,” and “pillars of worship” are familiar in Arabic and Persian.

These are intelligible extensions of support and essential components.

Later legal terminology, including precise distinctions between an integral element and a condition, should not be imposed on the Qur’anic word.

The Qur’an does not use the plural arkān.

Without independent evidence, Hūd 11:80 cannot establish the later technical classification of the pillars of worship.

Mirkan and other physical uses

Early Arabic uses مِرْكَن for a vessel or basin used for water and washing.

ناقة مُرَكَّنَةُ الضَّرْع describes a she-camel with a prominent, full udder.

The recorded family thus includes physical descriptions and everyday objects.

Not every item requires an invented etymological story.

Recording mirkan as a washbasin does not prove that its name arose from strong corners or from someone leaning on it.

The attested sense should be retained, while historical connections should go no further than the evidence.

3. Poetry and the social use of rukn

Rukn as unmatched power

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī reads:

لَا تَقْذِفَنِّي بِرُكْنٍ لَا كِفَاءَ لَهُ
وَإِنْ تَأَثَّفَكَ الْأَعْدَاءُ بِالرِّفَدِ

lā taqdhifannī bi-ruknin lā kifāʾa lahu / wa-in taʾaththafaka al-aʿdāʾu bi-al-rifadi

Approximate translation: “Do not strike me with a power that has no equal, even if my enemies surround you, supporting one another.”

The verse belongs to a poem defending the poet and seeking pardon from al-Nuʿmān ibn al-Mundhir.

Here rukn evokes formidable power and standing rather than a building’s physical corner.

Lā kifāʾa lahu emphasizes that it has no equal.

This transmission of pre-Islamic poetry attests a social and political use of rukn for strength and superior power.

The poem’s setting concerns the poet’s relationship with a ruler.

Rukn itself does not mean king, government, or sovereignty; those referents come from the circumstances and addressee.

The second hemistich has variant readings, while the first is familiar in early transmissions.

Physical description in a verse attributed to Ṭarafah

A poetic fragment attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd reads:

وَضَرَّتُهَا مُرَكَّنَةٌ دَرُورُ

wa-ḍarratuhā murakkanatun darūru

Translation: “Its udder is full and yields abundant milk.”

Murakkanah is a physical description concerning the fullness and prominence of the part described.

The family’s poetic uses thus extend beyond social backing and attachment to another.

This sense should not be transferred without evidence to every derivative.

Rukn shadīd in Lot’s story concerns supporting strength, whereas murakkanah here describes part of an animal’s body.

Rukn and firmness in early Islamic poetry

A line attributed to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj reads:

وَزَحْمُ رُكْنَيْكَ شِدَادَ الْأَرْكُنِ

wa-zaḥmu ruknayka shidāda al-arkuni

Approximate translation: “And your two strong sides’ repelling those of powerful supports.” The transmitted reading here is shidād al-arkun, a plural; the isolated fragment does not identify all parties involved.

Ruknayka is dual and names two sides or strong parts.

Arkun is a plural of rukn. The surrounding lines concern strength in confronting an ill-tempered person; this is not exclusively a description of bodily anatomy.

Ruʾbah lived during the Umayyad and early Abbasid periods. This is evidence of early Islamic Arabic after revelation, rather than pre-Islamic poetry.

The example helps explain the root’s imagery of strength but does not determine every Qur’anic sense.

Together, the examples attest both a strong side of something and human backing or power.

4. Rukn shadīd: strong support in Lot’s story

Hūd 11:80

Verse:

قَالَ لَوْ أَنَّ لِي بِكُمْ قُوَّةً أَوْ آوِي إِلَىٰ رُكْنٍ شَدِيدٍ

qāla law anna lī bikum quwwatan aw āwī ilā ruknin shadīdin

Hūd 11:80

Translation:

“He said: If only I had strength against you, or could take refuge in a firm support.”

Rukn in this verse

Ruknin is an indefinite singular noun in the genitive after ilā.

Shadīdin qualifies it, emphasizing strength and firmness.

The phrase therefore denotes strong, dependable support.

It can describe social strength, supportive kin, or power relied upon against a threat.

Lot faces people threatening his guests and refusing to abandon their wrongdoing.

In Hūd 11:78, he calls them to mindfulness and asks them not to disgrace him concerning his guests.

In Hūd 11:79, they respond that he knows what they want.

Hūd 11:80 then expresses his lack of sufficient force or access to firm support.

Rukn shadīd thus appears in a situation of danger and need for protection.

Quwwah and rukn

Two expressions occur together:

لِي بِكُمْ قُوَّةً

lī bikum quwwatan

Hūd 11:80

and

آوِي إِلَىٰ رُكْنٍ شَدِيدٍ

āwī ilā ruknin shadīdin

Hūd 11:80

The first concerns possessing force to confront the aggressors.

The second concerns seeking refuge in strong support.

They are related but not mere repetition.

Someone may lack sufficient personal ability to defend themselves yet receive support from a powerful family, community, or group.

Rukn can therefore denote a supporting force to which one turns for refuge.

Lexical explanations sometimes give al-ʿashīrah, the kin group that supports someone, for this use.

Kin should not, however, be presented as the word’s only lexical meaning.

A kin group is one social instance of support, not its entire meaning.

Had Lot forgotten The God?

Some interpretations connect the strong support with divine protection.

Divine refuge and support clearly feature throughout the Qur’an.

In this sentence, Lot speaks of force he does not possess for practically confronting the aggressors.

Human and social support is consequently a natural reading in the immediate context.

In Hūd 11:81, the divine messengers identify themselves and assure Lot that the attackers will not reach him.

Divine protection is thus present, but Lot’s request for strong support does not establish that he had actually forgotten The God or abandoned faith.

The Qur’an itself makes no such accusation.

Nor does the verse establish that seeking human assistance contradicts trust in The God.

Lot needs strength and support to prevent assault and defend his guests.

Human means of defending right and resisting injustice do not inherently replace faith or divine reliance.

Strong support for protection

This use shows that power and support can play a beneficial role.

Lot seeks protective strength capable of resisting assault and wrongdoing.

Rukn here does not designate power sought for aggression or domination.

The context concerns backing that prevents harm.

The story illustrates a distinction: power’s value depends on its purpose and use.

That conclusion comes from context; justice and legitimate defense are not intrinsic dictionary meanings of rukn.

5. Tarkanū and tarkanu: reliance on wrongdoers and pressure to alter revelation

Hūd 11:113

Verse:

وَلَا تَرْكَنُوا إِلَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا فَتَمَسَّكُمُ النَّارُ وَمَا لَكُمْ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ مِنْ أَوْلِيَاءَ ثُمَّ لَا تُنْصَرُونَ

wa-lā tarkanū ilā alladhīna ẓalamū fa-tamassakumu al-nāru wa-mā lakum min dūni allāhi min awliyāʾa thumma lā tunṣarūna

Hūd 11:113

Translation:

“Do not rely on those who have done wrong, lest the Fire touch you. You have no protectors apart from The God; then you will not be helped.”

The meaning of tarkanū

Tarkanū is a second-person plural imperfect verb in the basic form.

After prohibitive lā it is jussive. Its indicative form is tarkanūna; the final nūn is dropped in the jussive.

Ilā alladhīna ẓalamū identifies those toward whom the inclination is directed.

The verse therefore prohibits inclination and reliance upon people who have acted unjustly.

Lexical explanations use mayl and sukūn: inclining and finding reassurance through someone or something.

This goes beyond simple contact or proximity.

Someone may converse, trade, or live in the same community with another person without making that person their support or endorsing their injustice.

Attachment to an oppressive power, trust in it, making it one’s backing, or inclination to support it constitutes a different relationship.

This is the semantic field in which rukūn becomes significant.

Does rukūn mean only slight inclination?

Some lexical and interpretive explanations emphasize slight inclination in particular contexts.

This does not establish that rukūn always means only a very small inclination throughout Arabic.

Rakana ilā can also express substantial trust and reliance.

Context and qualifiers determine degree.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:74, shayʾan qalīlan explicitly expresses slightness.

Even if slightness were inherent in every use, the qualifier could reinforce it; its presence therefore does not by itself settle that lexical question. Here the degree is expressly stated.

“Inclining and relying” is the more suitable general definition, with intensity determined in each use.

Steadfastness and avoidance of transgression

Hūd 11:112 calls the Messenger and those who have turned back with him to remain steadfast as commanded and avoid transgression.

Hūd 11:113 then prohibits reliance on wrongdoers.

This sequence matters.

Steadfastness involves more than personally avoiding oppression: one must not rely on oppressors for power, security, or advantage in a way that supports their injustice.

Rukūn here concerns a relationship with oppressive power.

The warning addresses reliance upon wrongdoers as well as direct wrongdoing.

Moral responsibility can consequently extend beyond a person’s immediate actions to their support, legitimization, or dependence involving others’ conduct.

Not every social relationship or necessary encounter with a wrongdoer amounts to endorsement.

The relevant distinctions concern the relationship’s nature and the direction of reliance.

Reliance on wrongdoers and responsibility

Someone may depend on an unjust or corrupt power to preserve position, wealth, security, or influence.

That dependence sometimes manifests as open support for injustice.

A person may knowingly defend an oppressive authority, justify its conduct, or provide resources sustaining it to protect personal interests.

These can be clear examples of blameworthy inclination and reliance.

The word rukūn does not, however, reveal everyone’s intentions.

Cooperation may arise from ignorance, fear, coercion, or error. Individual responsibility requires consideration of freedom, knowledge, role, and consequences.

These differences should not empty the prohibition of meaning.

The verse expressly prohibits reliance on wrongdoers; personal advantage or social dependence alone cannot justify supporting oppression.

Is every relationship with a wrongdoer prohibited?

Lexically, rukūn is not synonymous with meeting, conversation, trade, or coexistence.

Contact with people or institutions that have acted wrongly may serve negotiation, redress, reform, or ending injustice.

Without examining its purpose and nature, such contact does not establish reliance on or alignment with oppression.

The Qur’an distinguishes just treatment of others from support for injustice.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:8, hostility toward a group must not lead to injustice.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:135, justice must prevail even over personal interest and close family ties.

Hūd 11:113 should therefore not become a blanket prohibition of every relationship with any wrongdoer.

Conversely, the permissibility of some social contact cannot be used to exempt genuine reliance upon and support for oppressive power.

The key distinction is between contact and supportive dependence.

Fa-tamassakumu al-nār: the consequence of inclination

The verse continues:

فَتَمَسَّكُمُ النَّارُ

fa-tamassakumu al-nāru

Hūd 11:113

That is, “lest the Fire touch you.”

This warns of the consequence of reliance on wrongdoers.

Fire is not part of the lexical meaning of rukūn.

The action is first prohibited, then its consequence is stated.

The sentence further says that there are no protectors apart from The God, and that they will not be helped.

This clearly connects with the field of support.

Someone may regard oppressive power as their backing, yet the verse warns that it cannot save them from the ultimate consequence.

The inclination is therefore situated within human responsibility and final outcome, rather than portrayed as inconsequential thought.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:74

Verse:

وَلَوْلَا أَنْ ثَبَّتْنَاكَ لَقَدْ كِدْتَ تَرْكَنُ إِلَيْهِمْ شَيْئًا قَلِيلًا

wa-lawlā an thabbatnāka la-qad kidta tarkanu ilayhim shayʾan qalīlan

Al-Isrāʾ 17:74

Translation:

“Had We not made you steadfast, you would indeed have come close to inclining toward them a little.”

Tarkanu: inclination under pressure

Tarkanu is a second-person singular imperfect verb addressed here to the Messenger.

Its difference from Hūd 11:113 matters.

Hūd uses a plural prohibition, while Al-Isrāʾ 17:74 directly addresses the Messenger in the singular.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:73 describes people seeking to divert him from what was revealed so that he would attribute something else to The God.

It adds that they would then have taken him as a friend.

Ilayhim in Al-Isrāʾ 17:74 thus refers to those seeking interference with revelation and attribution of other speech to The God.

The inclination here is not an ordinary social interest or relationship.

It concerns pressure to change revelation and movement toward the demands of those seeking that change.

The roles of lawlā and kidta

Two constructions matter:

لَوْلَا أَنْ ثَبَّتْنَاكَ

lawlā an thabbatnāka

Al-Isrāʾ 17:74

and

لَقَدْ كِدْتَ تَرْكَنُ

la-qad kidta tarkanu

Al-Isrāʾ 17:74

Lawlā identifies an existing circumstance that prevented the stated consequence: divine strengthening.

Kidta expresses approaching an act, not necessarily performing it. Here even that approach is within the counterfactual clause, rather than asserted as an actual event.

The verse does not say that the Messenger accepted their demands or attributed something contrary to revelation to The God.

Nor should one derive a complete theory of human susceptibility from this sentence alone. It emphasizes divine strengthening amid pressure, without reporting an actual lapse.

The Qur’an presents his strengthening as preventing such an approach to inclination.

Divine support and the seriousness of the warning should both be retained.

Shayʾan qalīlan: a little inclination

Shayʾan qalīlan limits the degree of the inclination mentioned.

The conditional statement concerns coming close to a slight inclination, not complete acceptance of the opponents’ demands.

Even slight inclination would be serious in a context involving alteration of revelation.

This is not simply compromise over a private matter: it concerns attributing to The God something He did not reveal.

The importance and danger of an inclination are not determined solely by its size.

A small inclination in an ordinary matter may have limited effects, whereas the same degree concerning fidelity to revelation and truth may have far greater consequences.

This does not establish that every small, involuntary inclination by anyone carries the responsibility and consequence of this particular address to the Messenger.

The subject, addressee, and level of responsibility must remain distinct.

Inclination and steadfastness

Thabbatnāka in Al-Isrāʾ 17:74 belongs to TH B T.

It means making firm or steadfast.

Tarkanu ilayhim concerns inclining toward the people in question.

The words are from different roots but stand in a clear contextual relationship.

Strengthening the Messenger prevents his inclining toward the demands of those seeking to alter revelation.

The wrongful inclination concerns approaching acceptance of their demands, rather than merely maintaining contact with opponents.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:75 adds a severe warning about the consequence of such a lapse.

Fidelity to truth must not be sacrificed to compromise, even when pressure comes with promises of friendship and acceptance.

Comparing the two verbal uses

In Hūd 11:113, tarkanū occurs in a prohibition: do not rely on those who have done wrong.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:74, tarkanu occurs in a conditional warning: without divine strengthening, you would have come close to inclining a little toward their demands.

Both uses concern inclination.

The first is a plural prohibition of reliance on wrongdoers; the second addresses an individual and describes a danger prevented through divine strengthening.

Translation and analysis must preserve these distinctions.

6. Bi-ruknihi: Pharaoh’s power and supporters

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:39

Verse:

فَتَوَلَّىٰ بِرُكْنِهِ وَقَالَ سَاحِرٌ أَوْ مَجْنُونٌ

fa-tawallā bi-ruknihi wa-qāla sāḥirun aw majnūnun

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:39

Translation:

“He turned away, relying on his power, and said: A sorcerer or a madman.”

The meaning of bi-ruknihi

Bi-ruknihi combines the preposition bi-, the noun rukn, and the pronoun -hi.

The pronoun refers to Pharaoh, named in the preceding verse.

On the reading emphasized here, rukn concerns his power and backing.

Lexical accounts use rukn al-rajul for a person’s force, kin, followers, and supporting resources.

Bi-ruknihi can thus mean with his force, relying on his power, or accompanied by his supporters.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:40 mentions Pharaoh and his troops together.

This supports understanding his backing as including military and institutional power, beyond personal bodily strength.

Could rukn refer to a bodily side?

In Arabic, rukn can also denote a bodily side.

Some grammatical and rhetorical readings accordingly understand the expression as a bodily turning aside.

This imagery is possible because tawallā itself concerns turning away.

Nevertheless, Pharaoh’s story and the troops mentioned in the next verse support the reading involving power and supporters.

On that reading, Pharaoh relies on his available strength as he rejects Moses’ message.

The preposition bi- can express accompaniment or reliance here.

“He turned away, relying on his power” is therefore a natural, defensible rendering.

The preposition alone does not establish the precise bodily movement, place, or direction involved.

Social and political power

On this reading, the verse illustrates the extension of rukn from physical strength to political power.

Pharaoh possessed ruling authority, military forces, and extensive resources.

Several Qur’anic passages examine his power in relation to his conduct.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:40, for example, presents Pharaoh and his forces together in their final fate.

Rukn in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:39 can therefore denote the power he relied upon against Moses.

The root itself does not mean kingship or government.

Political power is one instance of strong support.

Nor does rukn alone mean oppression or arrogance; these come from Pharaoh’s conduct and the story.

Lot’s support and Pharaoh’s support

The two Qur’anic nominal uses differ clearly.

In Hūd 11:80, Lot lacks strong support for resisting assault and protecting his guests.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:39, Pharaoh turns away from Moses’ message with his power and supporters.

Both passages concern strength or backing.

One seeks support against injustice; the other links existing power with rejection of truth.

The noun itself therefore does not determine moral value.

Power can defend right or sustain oppression and denial.

Rukn should be understood as neither inherently sacred and good nor inherently oppressive.

Its object, purpose, and use determine the distinction.

Having power and being right

Pharaoh’s troops and political authority do not make his claim true.

The verse describes his rejection and accusation that Moses is a sorcerer or madman.

Practical power and sound evidence are different.

Power may enable defense, protection, or imposition of one’s will; it does not itself establish truth.

The contrast is visible in Lot and Pharaoh: one seeks force to resist assault, while the other uses existing power to reject a true message.

7. Related roots, translation proposals, and conclusion

R K N (rukn) and Q W Y (quwwah)

Quwwah concerns strength and ability.

The noun rukn denotes a strong side or something that can be relied upon.

Hūd 11:80 places the two fields together.

Lot speaks of having strength to confront others or finding refuge in strong support.

Quwwah can name ability itself, whereas rukn here names the backing relied upon.

The distinction is clear in this verse but does not establish that quwwah is always internal and rukn always external.

A person may rely on personal strength or receive strength from others.

R K N (rukn) and S N D (sanad)

Sanad can denote something on which a person leans or through which they remain supported.

Sanad and rukn therefore overlap in support and reliance.

Rukn’s primary field concerns a strong side or firm part of something.

Sanad may emphasize the relationship of backing and support.

The distinction is not absolute: extended uses of both can designate a supporter.

Persian equivalents meaning “backing” and “support” help explain both families but do not invariably capture every nuance.

R K N (rukn) and ʾ W Y (taking refuge)

Awā concerns seeking refuge, turning toward safety, or finding shelter. Īwāʾ is the causative verbal noun: giving shelter.

In Hūd 11:80, āwī ilā ruknin shadīd brings refuge and support together.

Āwī expresses the act of taking refuge.

Ruknin shadīd identifies the strong support in which refuge is sought.

Rukn itself does not mean taking refuge; the verb āwī expresses that action.

Translation should preserve the distinct contributions of the two words.

R K N (rukūn) and M Y L (mayl)

Mayl concerns inclination.

The verbal field of rukūn can likewise express inclining.

Reassurance, trust, or reliance on the object of inclination may also be involved.

The prohibition lā tarkanū in Hūd 11:113 should therefore not be confined to bodily movement toward oppressors.

Inclination and reliance are at issue.

Yet not every rukūn necessarily expresses complete, lasting trust.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:74, shayʾan qalīlan qualifies the inclination as slight.

Its degree and character must be examined in each passage.

R K N (rukūn) and S K N (sukūn)

Sukūn concerns settling and the subsiding of movement or disturbance.

Rukūn may involve settling in reliance on something.

The words are nevertheless distinct.

Someone may settle peacefully somewhere without attachment to another person or authority.

Conversely, a person may depend on oppressive power while remaining anxious and unsettled.

Settling can thus be an aspect of rukūn without exhausting its meaning.

R K N (rukn) and R K Z (rakz)

The roots share their first two letters but differ in meaning.

R K Z has one branch concerning planting or securing something in place and another concerning faint sound.

R K N concerns a strong side, support, and inclination toward what one relies upon.

Their fields may approach each other where firmness is involved.

Planting a spear in the ground nevertheless differs from relying on kin or political power.

Rikzan in Maryam 19:98 means faint sound and must not be confused with ruknin in Hūd 11:80.

R K N (rukūn) and W L Y (wilāyah)

Wilāyah and walī have a broad Qur’anic field involving nearness, guardianship, protection, and relationship.

Rukūn can also occur in dependence on powers or persons.

Their roots and core meanings remain different.

Hūd 11:113 pairs the prohibition of relying on wrongdoers with the absence of protectors apart from The God.

This connects the passage with support and backing.

Rukūn does not itself mean a formal pact of allegiance, unconditional obedience, or religious affiliation.

Those concepts require their own contextual evidence.

Translation proposals for Forqan

رُكْنٍ — Hūd 11:80

Suitable equivalents include “support,” “backing,” or “a strong supporter.”

For ruknin shadīd, “a firm support” is natural and accurate.

The supplied translation preserves this meaning.

تَرْكَنُوا — Hūd 11:113

With prohibitive lā, suitable equivalents are “do not rely,” “do not incline,” or, as contextual explanation, “do not attach yourselves to them and make them your backing.” The prohibition comes from lā, not tarkanū alone.

In the complete construction, “do not rely on those who have done wrong” conveys the core meaning well.

تَرْكَنُ — Al-Isrāʾ 17:74

Suitable equivalents are “incline” or “lean toward them.”

With shayʾan qalīlan, “you would have come close to inclining toward them a little” is clear, provided the preceding condition is retained.

بِرُكْنِهِ — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:39

Suitable equivalents include “with his force,” “with his supporters,” and “relying on his power.”

In the sentence, “he turned away, relying on his power” is a suitable choice.

This preserves the connection with power and support without restricting the expression to bodily anatomy.

Proposed word-box explanations for Forqan

Root: R K N

Brief meaning: A strong side and support; supporting power; inclining and relying upon someone or something.

Word: رُكْنٍ — Hūd 11:80

Meaning here: Strong support in which Lot seeks refuge to resist assault and defend his guests.

Word: تَرْكَنُوا — Hūd 11:113

Meaning here: With prohibitive lā: do not rely upon or incline toward those who have done wrong.

Word: تَرْكَنُ — Al-Isrāʾ 17:74

Meaning here: Incline toward their demands, in the conditional account of pressure to alter what was revealed to the Messenger.

Word: بِرُكْنِهِ — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:39

Meaning here: Relying on his power and supporters, describing Pharaoh’s rejection of Moses’ message. These proposals do not replace existing authored verse-box explanations.

Conclusion and final definition

R K N forms a family concerning firmness, support, power, and inclination toward what one regards as backing.

Physically, rukn is the strong side of a building, mountain, or other object.

Socially, it can denote strength, kin, troops, or power on which someone relies.

Rakana ilā concerns inclining, trusting, and relying upon someone or something.

Transmitted pre-Islamic poetry also attests the meanings of power and backing.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an.

In Hūd 11:80, ruknin shadīd names the strong support Lot needs in danger.

In Hūd 11:113, tarkanū occurs in a prohibition of reliance on wrongdoers.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:74, tarkanu belongs to the counterfactual account of approaching a slight inclination toward those seeking to divert the Messenger from revelation.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:39, bi-ruknihi concerns the power and backing with which Pharaoh rejects Moses’ message.

These uses show that the root does not itself assign a fixed moral value.

Support may defend right and resist injustice, as in Lot’s story, or supply the power for resistance to truth, as with Pharaoh.

Reliance on people can likewise be right or wrong depending on their identity and conduct.

Final definition of R K N:

The core field concerns a strong, firm side that can be relied upon, extending to power, backing, and support.

In the verbal branch, rakana ilā concerns inclining, finding reassurance in something, trusting, or relying upon a person or matter.

The two principal branches are thus:

1. Rukn: The support itself, a firm side, or a supporting force.

2. Rukūn: A person’s inclination and reliance upon what they regard as backing or an object of trust.

The Qur’anic uses connect these branches, while retaining the distinction between noun and verb.

Rukn does not intrinsically name oppression, justice, government, tribe, or faith. These may be referents or objects within contexts of power and reliance.

Rukūn does not mean every contact or relationship with others; its Qur’anic uses concern inclination and reliance toward the persons in question.

A suitable core for Forqan’s study and translation is therefore “firm support, and inclination or reliance upon it.”

This retains the relationship among the four Qur’anic uses while letting context distinguish refuge in strong support, reliance on wrongdoers, inclination under pressure, and dependence on political power.
