Qur’anic root Z F F

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Core meaning: swift, light movement, particularly advancing with hurried steps; in other uses, setting in motion, escorting, or conveying someone or something along a course.

1. The root in early Arabic and pre-Islamic poetry

The Arabic root Z F F concerns speed, lightness of movement, and advancing. زَفَّ commonly means to hurry or move swiftly. زَفِيف denotes rapid movement, sometimes described as closely spaced steps faster than ordinary walking.

One early use describes an ostrich’s movement. زَفَّ الظَّلِيمُ means that a male ostrich moved swiftly. Some lexical explanations identify this as the beginning of its run or a pace between walking and full running.

The same verb is also used for camels, individuals, and groups of people.

One early lexical analysis places خِفَّة, “lightness,” at the center of the root, associating it with an ostrich’s swift movement, people advancing, and conveying a bride to her husband.

Here lightness need not mean low bodily weight. A person or animal described with يزف may be heavy but move swiftly and nimbly.

The movement branch therefore concerns speed and lightness of advance, not the weight of the moving body.

A pre-Islamic example from the Muʿallaqah of al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah

The pre-Islamic poet al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah describes his traveling mount in his Muʿallaqah:

بِزَفُوفٍ كَأَنَّهَا هِقْلَةٌ أُمُّ رِئَالٍ دَوِّيَّةٌ سَقْفَاءُ

bi-zafūfin ka-annahā hiqlatun ummu riʾālin dawwiyyatun saqfāʾu

Translation:

“On a swift she-camel, as though she were a female ostrich, mother of chicks, dwelling in the open wilderness, tall and curved in form.”

زَفُوف describes the poet’s she-camel.

To portray its speed, he likens it to an ostrich crossing the wilderness toward its chicks, an image developed in the surrounding lines.

هقلة denotes a female ostrich; رئال is the plural of رأل, an ostrich chick.

دوّية relates to a wide, remote wilderness.

The camel covers the distance with swift, nimble movement.

زفوف here does not mean a bride, wedding procession, or ceremonial movement. Its meaning directly concerns speed and advancing.

The poet describes a camel running like an ostrich: the root’s use was not confined to the bird itself.

This transmitted pre-Islamic poetic example supports swift, light movement independently of the Qur’anic passage.

Another example attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

لَمَّا رَكِبْنَا رَفَعْنَاهُنَّ زَفْزَفَةً
حَتَّى احْتَوَيْنَا سَوَامًا ثُمَّ أَرْبَابَهُ

lammā rakibnā rafaʿnāhunna zafzafatan / ḥattā iḥtawaynā sawāman thumma arbābahu

Sense of the line:

“When we mounted, we drove them swiftly onward until we took possession of grazing livestock and then their owners.”

زفزفة describes the mounts’ rapid movement.

This belongs to the repeated quadriliteral structure Z F Z F. It is semantically related to the doubled triliteral Z F F, but is not morphologically the same root.

Lexical accounts record زفزفة for swift movement, repeated shaking, and the movement of wind.

The example supports rapid movement in the related quadriliteral family. The clearer Muʿallaqah example remains the direct evidence for the triliteral root before the Qur’an.

What the pre-Qur’anic evidence supports

The movement branch can be defined from transmitted pre-Islamic Arabic evidence as follows:

زفّ denotes swift, light advance, particularly a pace faster than ordinary walking. Arabic uses it for ostriches, camels, and humans.

The poetic witness supports this movement sense without relying on the Qur’anic verse.

Other Arabic uses concern conveying a bride, wind, and groups of people moving.

Their existence does not show that every use appeared at the same time or establish the exact derivation of one from another.

The Qur’anic occurrence belongs to the speed-and-movement branch.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَفَّ يَزِفُّ

This is a basic triliteral verb from the doubled root Z F F.

In the movement sense, it means to hurry, advance swiftly, or move quickly.

زَفَّ الظَّلِيمُ

zaffa al-ẓalīmu

That is: “The male ostrich moved swiftly.”

زَفَّ الْقَوْمُ

zaffa al-qawmu

That is: “The group advanced swiftly.”

زَفَّ الْبَعِيرُ

zaffa al-baʿīru

That is: “The camel moved quickly.”

It can describe an individual or a group. The number of movers comes from the subject, not the root’s meaning alone.

زَفّ, زَفِيف, and زُفُوف

These are recorded verbal nouns for rapid movement.

زفيف is often explained as swift movement with closely spaced steps.

For an ostrich, some explanations identify the beginning of running or a pace between walking and full running.

This does not establish a fixed speed or require short steps in every use.

The pace and manner depend on the creature and circumstances.

زَفُوف

This describes a swiftly moving creature.

ناقة زفوف means a swift she-camel.

Some lexical accounts also use زفوف for the ostrich itself, whose movement is a familiar example of زفيف.

In al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah’s verse, it describes a camel compared with an ostrich for speed.

زَافّ

This active participle denotes someone or something moving swiftly.

زَافٌّ فِي سَيْرِهِ

zāffun fī sayrihi

That is: “Swift in his movement.”

It can describe a human or an animal.

أَزَفَّ

Two uses of this verb are recorded.

One is to hurry or enter a state of swift movement.

The other is to make another move swiftly.

أَزَفَّ الْبَعِيرَ

azaffa al-baʿīra

That is: “He made the camel move swiftly.”

The distinction matters in the readings of Al-Ṣāffāt 37:94.

زَفَّ الْعَرُوسَ

In marriage-related usage, this means conveying a bride to her husband or escorting her in the bridal procession.

زَفَّ الْعَرُوسَ إِلَى زَوْجِهَا

zaffa al-ʿarūsa ilā zawjihā

That is: “He conveyed the bride to her husband.”

This use concerns conveying and escorting.

It does not require the bride to run or hurry intensely to her husband’s home.

Conveyance and accompaniment are prominent here, rather than necessarily high speed.

زِفَاف

This denotes conveying the bride to her husband and the associated ceremony.

The word is also familiar in Persian.

Present-day wedding customs should not be projected wholesale onto pre-Qur’anic Arabic uses: marriage ceremonies differ across societies and periods.

زُفَّة

Lexical accounts distinguish زَفَّة, with fatḥah, for one occasion or stage of movement, and زُفَّة, with ḍammah, for a group of people.

زُفَّة thus denotes a company or group.

Wedding-related usage also includes a group accompanying the bride; vocalization and customary usage should be distinguished from the preceding forms.

The word can denote people moving together without fixing their number.

مِزَفَّة

This denotes a litter or conveyance in which a bride is carried.

It belongs to the escorting-and-conveying use.

زِفّ

With a kasrah on the first consonant, this denotes small, soft feathers, particularly an ostrich’s down.

These are softer and lighter than larger feathers.

An early lexical explanation relates this use to lightness.

That explanation does not establish the word’s exact history. Neither speed deriving from the name of feathers nor the reverse should be asserted as certain.

أَزَفّ and زَفَّاء

These describe a bird with abundant small, soft feathers.

أزفّ is masculine and زفّاء feminine.

They describe the bird’s covering, not necessarily its speed.

زَفَّتِ الرِّيحُ

This is recorded for wind blowing or continuing to move.

Some accounts describe gentle, sustained blowing, whereas related repeated forms can refer to strong wind.

Wind intensity cannot be treated as identical throughout the word family.

زَفْزَفَ and زَفْزَفَة

These belong to the repeated quadriliteral Z F Z F and must be distinguished from the triliteral Z F F.

زفزفة is recorded for swift motion and shaking, wind sounding among plants, and birds’ wing movements.

The uses concern movement, but repeated letters alone do not prove intensity, duration, or repetition in every occurrence.

3. Physical imagery and semantic extensions

An ostrich’s movement

The ostrich provides one of the root’s clearest physical images.

Its fast movement involves successive strides and can cover considerable ground.

Arabic explanations use زفيف النعامة for a pace faster than ordinary walking.

Poets therefore compare a swift camel or mount with an ostrich.

That image is important for زفوف in al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah’s verse.

The ostrich is one example, not the only possible subject of زفيف. A human described with يزف need not reproduce the bird’s stride or bodily movement.

Groups of people moving

زفّ القوم في سيرهم means that people hastened in their movement.

They advance toward a destination, with a pace faster than ordinary walking emphasized.

A group may move in order, scattered formation, or disorder. None of these is a necessary component of the root.

People may hurry toward a destination without being frightened or angry.

Their haste may also arise from worry or anger.

Emotion and motivation therefore come from context.

Conveying a bride

زفّ العروس concerns conveying and escorting a bride.

This may involve companions, celebration, and a particular ceremony.

Joy, music, clothing, and other ceremonial details are not necessary meanings of the root itself.

This use emphasizes accompanying and conveying someone to a destination.

A derivative cannot therefore be restricted to “hurrying” in every context.

Wind movement

The word family also describes wind blowing and moving onward.

Wind may pass continuously through plants and set them moving.

Movement and flow are prominent in this use.

Neither زف nor the repeated زفزف alone specifies an exact wind speed.

4. The Qur’anic occurrence

The root Z F F occurs once in the Qur’an, in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:94.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:94

فَأَقْبَلُوا إِلَيْهِ يَزِفُّونَ

fa-aqbalū ilayhi yaziffūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:94

Translation:

“Then they came toward him in haste.”

يَزِفُّونَ is an imperfect verb from Z F F.

Its subject is the same group introduced by فَأَقْبَلُوا.

إِلَيْهِ specifies the direction: they came toward Abraham.

يَزِفُّونَ describes their manner of movement.

Two elements should therefore be distinguished:

فَأَقْبَلُوا إِلَيْهِ expresses turning and coming toward Abraham.

يَزِفُّونَ expresses their haste and speed.

Grammatically, the verbal clause يَزِفُّونَ functions circumstantially: they came toward Abraham while hastening.

The verb alone does not mean coming toward someone. Direction comes from the preceding clause and the preposition إلى.

The context of Abraham’s story

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:85, Abraham questions his father and people about what they worship.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:86 raises their worship of deities besides The God.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:91 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:92, Abraham approaches their gods and exposes their inability through questions.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:93, he turns upon the idols and strikes them.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:94 then describes people hastening toward him.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95, Abraham asks:

قَالَ أَتَعْبُدُونَ مَا تَنْحِتُونَ

qāla a-taʿbudūna mā tanḥitūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:95

Translation:

“He said: Do you worship what you carve?”

The sequence links their haste with Abraham’s treatment of the idols.

They face an act challenging their beliefs and objects of worship.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:59 likewise reports people asking who has done this to their gods.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:60 mentions Abraham in connection with speaking against them.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:62 then questions Abraham about what happened to their gods.

These verses help explain the confrontation.

The verb’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the movers’ motives.

Lexically, يَزِفُّونَ does not mean being angry, taking revenge, or attacking.

Their haste may be understood contextually as a response to the idols’ destruction, with worry or anger; those emotions do not come from the root alone.

The verse directly states that they came toward Abraham in haste.

Does يزفون mean running?

زفيف denotes swift movement; some explanations place it between walking and full running.

“Running toward him” can therefore convey the haste.

It may, however, suggest a more specific gait than the word itself establishes.

Conversely, “they came slowly and calmly” misses the principal meaning here.

“In haste” is suitable because it preserves speed without fixing the precise steps.

Does speed imply wrongdoing or falsehood?

The verse attributes haste to people confronting Abraham.

The root Z F F itself carries no necessary negative moral judgment.

Someone can hurry to do good just as someone can hurry toward wrongdoing.

Judgment of Abraham’s people comes from the broader story, idol worship, and their response to his questions.

This occurrence does not make haste inherently a sign of anger, rebellion, or corruption.

5. Readings and the meaning of يزفون

In the reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim used here, the word is يَزِفُّونَ, with fatḥah on yāʾ and kasrah on zāy.

This is from the basic triliteral زَفَّ يَزِفُّ.

Its meaning in the verse is hurrying or advancing swiftly.

The reading transmitted from Ḥamzah and Khalaf has يُزِفُّونَ, with ḍammah on yāʾ.

That form belongs to أَزَفَّ.

Two lexical explanations have been proposed.

One is entering swift movement: they began to hurry.

The other is causing someone else to hurry: members of the group urged others onward.

In the latter explanation, the object is not stated and must be understood from the construction or context.

Both explanations relate to the group’s hurried movement.

The reading difference does not alter the confrontation with Abraham, but affects the analysis of the verb and the agency behind the movement.

Other reported readings have been connected with W Z F. They should not be counted uncritically as additional derivatives of Z F F.

The form studied in the Ḥafṣ text here is يَزِفُّونَ.

6. Differences from related roots

Z F F (زَفَّ) and S R ʿ (سَرِعَ)

Both roots concern speed.

S R ʿ concerns haste and doing or happening quickly, in physical movement and other matters.

The familiar movement sense of زفّ focuses on swift advance.

In descriptions of ostriches and camels, manner and lightness of motion can also be prominent.

This is not an absolute boundary. زفّ has uses beyond spatial movement, and S R ʿ can describe running and bodily advance.

“In haste” therefore suits Al-Ṣāffāt 37:94, without making زفّ identical to S R ʿ in every use.

Z F F (زَفَّ) and Z J W (زَجَا)

Z J W concerns driving or setting something in motion.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:66 uses يُزْجِي for moving ships.

Al-Nūr 24:43 uses it for moving clouds.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:94, يزفون describes the group’s own swift movement.

These verses thus distinguish moving something from moving swiftly oneself.

The distinction comes especially from form and context; it does not make one root exclusively transitive and the other exclusively intransitive throughout Arabic.

Z F F (زَفَّ) and Z Ḥ F (زَحَفَ)

Many concrete uses of زحف describe heavy, slow, or ground-hugging advance.

Al-Anfāl 8:15 uses it in a military context.

The movement sense of زفّ, by contrast, emphasizes haste and lightness.

The roots can thus describe different ways of advancing.

Military زحف does not necessarily mean soldiers moving slowly, just as زفّ does not assign an identical, measurable speed to every instance.

Z F F (زَفَّ) and Z F R (زَفَرَ)

Their spelling is similar, but their semantic fields differ.

Qur’anic Z F F concerns swift movement.

Qur’anic Z F R concerns heavy breath, groaning, or formidable sound.

Sharing the first two consonants does not establish a historical relationship or transfer one meaning to the other.

Breath, groaning, and respiratory strain should therefore not be imported into the definition of Z F F.

Z F F (زَفَّ) and Z F Z F (زَفْزَفَ)

زفزف is a repeated quadriliteral found in descriptions of movement, wind, and birds.

Its uses concern speed, wing movement, or shaking objects.

It is semantically related to the family of زفّ but has a different morphological structure.

Repeated consonants alone do not prove that every زفزفة is stronger or longer than زفيف.

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan

The following equivalents suit the main forms. These study suggestions do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

زَفَّ يَزِفُّ: hurried or advanced swiftly and lightly.

زَفِيف: swift, light movement, sometimes with closely spaced steps.

زَفّ and زُفُوف: haste or quick movement, according to usage.

زَفُوف: swift-moving, of a camel or ostrich.

زَافّ: hurrying or moving quickly.

أَزَفَّ: hurried or made another move swiftly.

زَفَّ الْعَرُوسَ: escorted and conveyed the bride to her husband.

زِفَاف: conveying the bride to her husband and the associated ceremony.

زُفَّة: a group or company; distinguish it from زَفَّة, an occasion of movement, and wedding-related customary uses.

مِزَفَّة: a bridal litter or conveyance.

زِفّ: small, soft feathers.

أَزَفّ: having abundant small, soft feathers.

زَفَّتِ الرِّيحُ: the wind blew or continued moving.

زَفْزَفَ: in the repeated quadriliteral, moved or shook rapidly, with particular uses for wind and birds.

Suggested rendering of Al-Ṣāffāt 37:94

فَأَقْبَلُوا إِلَيْهِ يَزِفُّونَ

fa-aqbalū ilayhi yaziffūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:94

“Then they came toward him in haste.”

This preserves the direction from أقبلوا إليه and the haste from يزفون.

A brief root definition could read:

“Swift, light movement; advancing in haste.”

For the verse-specific explanation:

“They were moving swiftly; the sentence describes their approach to Abraham.”

“They attacked angrily” is inaccurate for يزفون alone because neither anger nor attack is a necessary meaning of the verb.

“They moved as a group” is also insufficient: plurality comes from the verb form, whereas its distinctive root meaning here is speed.

8. Conclusion

Transmitted pre-Islamic Arabic associates Z F F with speed and lightness of movement.

Al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah’s Muʿallaqah uses زفوف for a swift camel compared with an ostrich.

Other Arabic uses concern people moving, wind, birds, conveying a bride, and accompanying groups.

زِفّ also denotes small, soft feathers. Its relationship with the movement words does not by itself establish the history of all the senses.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs only at Al-Ṣāffāt 37:94; the Ḥafṣ reading has يَزِفُّونَ.

The verse describes people hurrying toward Abraham after his action against the idols.

يزفون describes their manner of movement, not inherently anger, threat, attack, or revenge.

Those features require evidence from the narrative context.

The Qur’anic sense is swift, light advance: hurried movement as a group approaches Abraham.

This accords with the transmitted early movement sense and preserves the verb’s meaning without importing wedding customs, a bird’s exact gait, or the people’s psychological motives.
