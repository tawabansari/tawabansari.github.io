The Qur’anic Root W J D

Finding, having access, and encountering something within one’s perception

Despite its frequent occurrence, the root و ج د has a very limited range of morphological forms in the Qur’an. Almost the entire Qur’anic family consists of the basic verb وَجَدَ and its inflections, such as وَجَدَ، وَجَدْنَا، وَجَدُوا، تَجِدُ، يَجِدُ، أَجِدُ and وُجِدَ. Alongside these verbs, the root has just one noun: وُجْد, wujd, in مِنْ وُجْدِكُمْ.

Forms such as wujūd, mawjūd, ījād, wājid, and wijdān do not occur in the Qur’an. They have important uses in Arabic, but the philosophical sense of wujūd as existence, the modern sense of wijdān as conscience, or the mystical sense of wajd should not be the starting point for understanding Qur’anic wajada.

The predominant core of wajada cannot be limited to finding something that was lost. In the Qur’an, a person may find someone, find something available or at their disposal, find someone possessing a particular quality, find a state within themselves, perceive a scent, find their deeds’ outcome present, or find a promise fulfilled.

The verb’s core can therefore be approached as follows:

Wajada: something becomes present, attained, available, or perceptible to the subject, who finds or apprehends it in that condition.

This definition is deliberately broad, since the object and sentence structure determine the kind of finding. The nominal branch وُجْد, meaning means and capacity, must nevertheless be examined separately rather than forced back into “finding” merely to create a single definition.

Forms and constructions examined

وَجَدَ
Wajada: the basic verb; finding and perceiving

وَجَدَ + وصف
Wajada + description: finding someone or something in a condition

وُجْد
Wujd: noun; means and resources

The semantic field in early Arabic

No demonstrable original physical image, such as cutting, binding, or the movement of a bodily member, is available for و ج د. The root primarily concerns the subject’s relationship to something attained, present, or perceptible to them.

An early example attributed to ʿAntarah illustrates this construction:

لَقَدْ وَجَدْنَا زَبِيدًا غَيْرَ صَابِرَةٍ
يَوْمَ الْتَقَيْنَا وَخَيْلُ الْمَوْتِ تَسْتَبِقُ

La-qad wajadnā Zabīdan ghayra ṣābiratin / yawma ltaqaynā wa-khaylu l-mawti tastabiq.

“On the day we met, we found Zubayd lacking endurance, while the horses of death raced one another.”

Wajadnā here does not merely mean locating the people of Zubayd somewhere. The object comes with a description: they found Zubayd lacking endurance. They encountered them in a particular condition and apprehended that condition in them.

The same pattern is frequent in the Qur’an: someone is found patient, defiantly disobedient, or in some other state.

The root’s emotional branch also appears in early poetry. A poem attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says:

فَوَجْدِي بِسَلْمَى مِثْلُ وَجْدِ مُرَقِّشٍ
بِأَسْمَاءَ إِذْ لَا تَسْتَفِيقُ عَوَاذِلُه

Fa-wajdī bi-Salmā mithlu wajdi Muraqqishin / bi-Asmāʾa idh lā tastafīqu ʿawādhiluh.

“My longing for Salmā is like Muraqqish’s longing for Asmāʾ, when those who reproached him would not relent.”

Wajd here is a state of intense love and inward involvement. The example shows that the root’s family in early Arabic was not confined to finding an external object.

This emotional وَجْد, wajd, should not be equated with Qur’anic وُجْد, wujd, in Al-Ṭalāq 65:6. A shared root does not make the morphological and semantic branches identical.

Arabic also records wajada ʿalayhi for anger, wajada bihi for intense love, and forms of wajada for sorrow. These usages matter for the wider linguistic family, but are not separate Qur’anic forms and should not be inserted into the verses’ basic meaning.

Finding, access, and availability

The clearest use of wajada is finding a person or thing that becomes present or accessible in one’s surroundings:

كُلَّمَا دَخَلَ عَلَيْهَا زَكَرِيَّا الْمِحْرَابَ وَجَدَ عِنْدَهَا رِزْقًا

Kullamā dakhala ʿalayhā Zakariyyā l-miḥrāba wajada ʿindahā rizqā.

“Whenever Zechariah entered the sanctuary to visit her, he found provision with her.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:37

The story of Moses also says:

فَوَجَدَا عَبْدًا مِنْ عِبَادِنَا

Fa-wajadā ʿabdan min ʿibādinā.

“Then they found a servant from among Our servants.”

Al-Kahf 18:65

And in Midian:

وَوَجَدَ مِنْ دُونِهِمُ امْرَأَتَيْنِ تَذُودَانِ

Wa-wajada min dūnihimu mraʾatayni tadhūdān.

“Apart from them, he found two women holding back [their flocks].”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23

Wajada is not confined to physical finding. In various Qur’anic rulings, not finding approaches not having something at one’s disposal or within reach:

وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ وَلَمْ تَجِدُوا كَاتِبًا فَرِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ

Wa-in kuntum ʿalā safarin wa-lam tajidū kātiban fa-rihānun maqbūḍah.

“If you are on a journey and find no scribe, then [let there be] a pledge taken into possession.”

Al-Baqarah 2:283

And:

فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا

Fa-lam tajidū māʾan fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyibā.

“And you find no water, then perform tayammum with clean earth.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43

In such constructions, not finding may concern the thing’s actual absence, lack of access to it, or its unavailability to the person. “Not having access” may therefore be a natural rendering in some verses.

This becomes clearer in verses concerning material resources:

وَلَا عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يَجِدُونَ مَا يُنْفِقُونَ حَرَجٌ

Wa-lā ʿalā lladhīna lā yajidūna mā yunfiqūna ḥaraj.

“Nor is there blame upon those who find nothing to spend.”

Al-Tawbah 9:91

Lā yajidūna mā yunfiqūna here concerns a person’s available resources. It does not follow that wajada always means “be able.” Ability arises from the object and the verse’s economic context; the verb still concerns whether something is available and accessible to the person.

The same pattern occurs in expressions about finding no protector, helper, refuge, or escape. What the person needs is not available to them.

Wujd: available means and capacity

The family’s only Qur’anic noun occurs in Al-Ṭalāq:

أَسْكِنُوهُنَّ مِنْ حَيْثُ سَكَنْتُمْ مِنْ وُجْدِكُمْ وَلَا تُضَارُّوهُنَّ لِتُضَيِّقُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ

Askinūhunna min ḥaythu sakantum min wujdikum wa-lā tuḍārrūhunna li-tuḍayyiqū ʿalayhinn.

“House them in the manner in which you yourselves live, according to your means, and do not harm them in order to constrain them.”

Al-Ṭalāq 65:6

وُجْدِكُمْ, wujdikum, does not mean finding here; it denotes the means, capacity, and resources at a person’s disposal.

“According to your means” or “within your resources” is a more natural rendering.

A semantic closeness can be seen between what is available to a person and their means and resources, but it does not establish a historical path of derivation. We do not know exactly how, or at what stage, the wujd branch separated from or developed out of the wajada branch. The semantic connection can be recognized without constructing a hypothetical derivational history.

Finding a quality, state, or existing condition

One of the verb’s most important Qur’anic constructions is:

وَجَدَ + a person or thing + a quality or state

In this construction, a person does more than locate something: they find it to be such and such.

For example:

وَمَا وَجَدْنَا لِأَكْثَرِهِمْ مِنْ عَهْدٍ ۖ وَإِنْ وَجَدْنَا أَكْثَرَهُمْ لَفَاسِقِينَ

Wa-mā wajadnā li-aktharihim min ʿahdin wa-in wajadnā aktharahum la-fāsiqīn.

“We found no faithfulness to a covenant in most of them, and We found most of them defiantly disobedient.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:102

In the second part, wajadnā does not describe locating people in space; they are apprehended as fāsiqīn, defiantly disobedient.

The same construction describes Job:

إِنَّا وَجَدْنَاهُ صَابِرًا ۚ نِعْمَ الْعَبْدُ ۖ إِنَّهُ أَوَّابٌ

Innā wajadnāhu ṣābiran niʿma l-ʿabdu innahu awwāb.

“We found him patient. What an excellent servant! He was ever returning.”

Ṣād 38:44

And when someone makes a promise:

سَتَجِدُنِي إِنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ صَابِرًا

Sa-tajidunī in shāʾa llāhu ṣābirā.

“If The God wills, you will find me patient.”

Al-Kahf 18:69

In all these cases, wajada concerns assessing or apprehending someone’s condition.

The construction also appears in the words of those who appeal to their forefathers’ way:

إِنَّا وَجَدْنَا آبَاءَنَا عَلَىٰ أُمَّةٍ وَإِنَّا عَلَىٰ آثَارِهِمْ مُهْتَدُونَ

Innā wajadnā ābāʾanā ʿalā ummatin wa-innā ʿalā āthārihim muhtadūn.

“We found our forefathers following a way, and we are guided by their footsteps.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:22

They do not mean that they located their forefathers after they had gone missing. They grew up in a society and found their forefathers following an existing way.

Elsewhere:

وَجَدْنَا آبَاءَنَا لَهَا عَابِدِينَ

Wajadnā ābāʾanā lahā ʿābidīn.

“We found our forefathers worshipping them.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:53

The verb merely reports an existing situation. Wajadnā does not itself confirm that the way is right. A person can find a tradition in existence even when that tradition is wrong.

This distinction matters: finding a situation is not the same as judging it to be right.

Finding an inward state

The same verb can move from the external world to a person’s interior:

فَلَا وَرَبِّكَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ حَتَّىٰ يُحَكِّمُوكَ فِيمَا شَجَرَ بَيْنَهُمْ ثُمَّ لَا يَجِدُوا فِي أَنْفُسِهِمْ حَرَجًا مِمَّا قَضَيْتَ وَيُسَلِّمُوا تَسْلِيمًا

Fa-lā wa-rabbika lā yuʾminūna ḥattā yuḥakkimūka fī-mā shajara baynahum thumma lā yajidū fī anfusihim ḥarajan mimmā qaḍayta wa-yusallimū taslīmā.

“No, by your Lord, they do not believe until they make you judge over what has arisen in dispute between them, then find no constraint within themselves concerning your judgment and submit fully.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:65

Here, ḥaraj is found within the self. In natural English, “feel no constraint” may convey the result more readily, but the Arabic construction uses the same verb wajada.

Al-Ḥashr also says:

وَلَا يَجِدُونَ فِي صُدُورِهِمْ حَاجَةً مِمَّا أُوتُوا

Wa-lā yajidūna fī ṣudūrihim ḥājatan mimmā ūtū.

“They find no need within their breasts concerning what has been given to them [the emigrants].”

Al-Ḥashr 59:9

This context does not concern an absence of material need. It concerns an inward state toward what the emigrants have been given, explained in early commentaries as envy or coveting. The verse continues by describing selfless preference for others despite one’s own need. This explanation belongs to the verse’s context and should not become a fixed definition of wajada.

These uses show that the boundary between outward finding and inward perception is not closed in this root. What changes is the location and nature of what a person finds.

Finding through sense perception, presence, and fulfillment

Some Qur’anic uses show that wajada is broader than seeing.

Jacob says:

وَلَمَّا فَصَلَتِ الْعِيرُ قَالَ أَبُوهُمْ إِنِّي لَأَجِدُ رِيحَ يُوسُفَ لَوْلَا أَنْ تُفَنِّدُونِ

Wa-lammā faṣalati l-ʿīru qāla abūhum innī la-ajidu rīḥa Yūsufa lawlā an tufannidūn.

“When the caravan departed, their father said: I perceive Joseph’s scent, if you will not think me senile.”

Yūsuf 12:94

أَجِدُ رِيحَ يُوسُفَ concerns smell. The verb is not restricted to a particular mode of perception. “I can smell Joseph’s scent” is natural English, but this sensing is one use of the same wajada.

The story of Dhū al-Qarnayn also says:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغَ مَغْرِبَ الشَّمْسِ وَجَدَهَا تَغْرُبُ فِي عَيْنٍ حَمِئَةٍ وَوَجَدَ عِنْدَهَا قَوْمًا

Ḥattā idhā balagha maghriba sh-shamsi wajadahā taghrubu fī ʿaynin ḥamiʾatin wa-wajada ʿindahā qawmā.

“When he reached the place of the sun’s setting, he found it setting in a dark, muddy spring, and found a people near it.”

Al-Kahf 18:86

Wajadahā taghrubu expresses what Dhū al-Qarnayn observes and experiences from his position. A theory of the world’s physical structure cannot be extracted from wajada itself. A few words later, the same verb describes finding a people in that area.

Another verse says:

وَجَدَهَا تَطْلُعُ عَلَىٰ قَوْمٍ

Wajadahā taṭluʿu ʿalā qawm.

“He found it rising upon a people.”

Al-Kahf 18:90

Wajada can thus express something together with a condition that appears or is perceived by an observer. This does not justify translating it as “it seemed to him” in every verse. In many uses, finding is entirely objective and direct.

Finding an outcome or fulfillment

Sometimes what was previously absent or promised becomes present at a later stage:

وَنَادَىٰ أَصْحَابُ الْجَنَّةِ أَصْحَابَ النَّارِ أَنْ قَدْ وَجَدْنَا مَا وَعَدَنَا رَبُّنَا حَقًّا فَهَلْ وَجَدْتُمْ مَا وَعَدَ رَبُّكُمْ حَقًّا ۖ قَالُوا نَعَمْ

Wa-nādā aṣḥābu l-jannati aṣḥāba n-nāri an qad wajadnā mā waʿadanā rabbunā ḥaqqan fa-hal wajadtum mā waʿada rabbukum ḥaqqan qālū naʿam.

“The inhabitants of the Garden will call to the inhabitants of the Fire: We have found what our Lord promised us to be true. Have you also found what your Lord promised to be true? They will say: Yes.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:44

Wajadnā … ḥaqqan here does not merely mean learning that something was true. What was promised has now been fulfilled in their experience, and they have found it as promised.

The same direction appears in this verse:

وَوَجَدُوا مَا عَمِلُوا حَاضِرًا وَلَا يَظْلِمُ رَبُّكَ أَحَدًا

Wa-wajadū mā ʿamilū ḥāḍiran wa-lā yaẓlimu rabbuka aḥadā.

“They will find what they did present, and your Lord wrongs no one.”

Al-Kahf 18:49

Deeds performed in the past are now found present. Wajada here expresses encountering something that has become present at the reckoning.

And in Āl ʿImrān:

يَوْمَ تَجِدُ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَا عَمِلَتْ مِنْ خَيْرٍ مُحْضَرًا

Yawma tajidu kullu nafsin mā ʿamilat min khayrin muḥḍarā.

“On the day when every soul finds the good it has done brought before it.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:30

This use also makes an interesting semantic connection with ق د م: a person sends something ahead and later finds it. That relationship comes from considering two Qur’anic expressions together; it is not part of wajada’s lexical definition.

Wa-wajada llāha ʿindahu

The parable of the mirage says:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَهُ لَمْ يَجِدْهُ شَيْئًا وَوَجَدَ اللَّهَ عِنْدَهُ فَوَفَّاهُ حِسَابَهُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ

Ḥattā idhā jāʾahu lam yajidhu shayʾan wa-wajada llāha ʿindahu fa-waffāhu ḥisābahu wa-llāhu sarīʿu l-ḥisāb.

“When he reaches it, he finds it to be nothing, and finds The God there, who pays him his account in full. The God is swift in reckoning.”

Al-Nūr 24:39

This construction should not be turned into a bodily location for The God. The sentence immediately continues with the settling of the account. Someone expecting water from a mirage encounters a different reality on arrival and receives his reckoning.

Wajada llāha also appears with a description in other verses:

يَجِدِ اللَّهَ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا

Yajidi llāha ghafūran raḥīmā.

“He finds The God forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:110

Here too, the point is not locating The God after He had been lost. A person encounters divine action and an attribute in a particular situation.

Wajada attributed to The God, and the limits of post-Qur’anic terminology

Al-Ḍuḥā contains three instances of a construction that could mislead if English “finding” is understood too narrowly:

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:6

أَلَمْ يَجِدْكَ يَتِيمًا فَآوَىٰ ۝

A-lam yajidka yatīman fa-āwā.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:7

وَوَجَدَكَ ضَالًّا فَهَدَىٰ ۝

Wa-wajadaka ḍāllan fa-hadā.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8

وَوَجَدَكَ عَائِلًا فَأَغْنَىٰ

Wa-wajadaka ʿāʾilan fa-aghnā.

“Did He not find you an orphan and give you shelter? And find you ḍāll and guide you? And find you in need and enrich you?”

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:6–8

In English, finding can sometimes suggest that the subject was previously unaware of something’s existence or location and discovered it after searching. This implication should not be imposed on these verses.

The Arabic construction وَجَدَكَ + a description can mean that the addressee was in a particular condition and The God found him, or dealt with him, in that condition. The verb does not itself express acquiring knowledge after ignorance.

In وَجَدَكَ ضَالًّا, the meaning of ḍāll must likewise be determined from the root ض ل ل and its own context. Wajada cannot predetermine that word’s meaning.

From wajada to wujūd

In later philosophical and theological usage, wujūd became a fundamental term for existence and being, while mawjūd was used for what exists. Extensive philosophical and theological systems developed around terms such as existence, quiddity, and necessary existence. This development of terminology should not be equated with the history of the words’ own origins.

The Qur’an does not employ this root in that system of terminology.

Wujūd as a philosophical noun does not occur in the Qur’an. Mawjūd, ījād, and wājid are not Qur’anic forms of this root either.

This does not make later Arabic usages wrong. A language can form new words and technical terms from a root over time. The issue is that a later meaning should not be projected backward to determine Qur’anic wajada.

The same caution applies to wijdān and mystical wajd. The linguistic family may develop in later periods, but a later technical use should not replace Qur’anic usage.

W J D (wajd) compared with L Q Y (laqy), R ʾ Y (raʾy), and ʿ L M (ʿilm)

Wajada approaches laqiya in some verses, since both can involve encountering a person or thing. Their perspectives, however, are not identical.

Laqiya places greater emphasis on meeting and the encounter between two parties. Wajada views something from the subject’s side: something has become findable, present, or perceptible to them.

This is why wajada can concern a scent, a state within the self, a person found patient or disobedient, or something found available. Not all these uses are explained by the simple idea of meeting.

Wajada is also distinct from raʾā. Raʾā can convey seeing or apprehension through sight and consideration, but innī la-ajidu rīḥa Yūsuf clearly shows that wajada is not dependent on the eyes.

Overlap with ʿalima is also possible, particularly when someone finds a person possessing a particular quality. Yet ʿalima emphasizes knowing, while many uses of wajada emphasize something’s presence within the subject’s experience, access, or perception.

For example:

وَجَدْنَا مَا وَعَدَنَا رَبُّنَا حَقًّا

Wajadnā mā waʿadanā rabbunā ḥaqqā.

does not merely say, “We learned that the promise was true.” They have now found that promise fulfilled and true.

These distinctions describe tendencies rather than absolute boundaries. Depending on the sentence, the roots’ semantic fields can overlap.

Translation proposals for Forqan

One fixed English equivalent is insufficient for wajada.

For a person or thing, “find” is natural:

فَوَجَدَا عَبْدًا مِنْ عِبَادِنَا

“Then they found a servant from among Our servants.”

Fa-wajadā ʿabdan min ʿibādinā.

When a means or resource is absent, “not find,” “have no access to,” or “be unavailable” may be appropriate:

فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً

“And you find no water.”

Fa-lam tajidū māʾā.

For finding a quality:

إِنَّا وَجَدْنَاهُ صَابِرًا

“We found him patient.”

Innā wajadnāhu ṣābirā.

For an inward state:

لَا يَجِدُوا فِي أَنْفُسِهِمْ حَرَجًا

“They feel no constraint within themselves.”

Lā yajidū fī anfusihim ḥarajā.

For sensory perception:

إِنِّي لَأَجِدُ رِيحَ يُوسُفَ

“I perceive Joseph’s scent.”

Innī la-ajidu rīḥa Yūsuf.

For a promise’s fulfillment:

وَجَدْنَا مَا وَعَدَنَا رَبُّنَا حَقًّا

“We have found what our Lord promised us to be true.”

Wajadnā mā waʿadanā rabbunā ḥaqqā.

And for وُجْد, wujd:

مِنْ وُجْدِكُمْ

“According to your means and resources.”

Min wujdikum.

Translation should therefore preserve the kind of thing being found and its relationship to the subject within the sentence.

Conclusion

The root و ج د appears in the Qur’an predominantly through the basic verb وَجَدَ. Its only nominal branch is وُجْد in Al-Ṭalāq 65:6.

Wajada extends beyond finding a person or object. Something may become available to someone, a person may be found with a particular quality, an inward state may be felt, a scent perceived, a deed found present, or a promise found true in its fulfillment.

The verb’s predominant core can therefore be expressed as follows:

Something becomes present within the subject’s access, experience, or perception, and the subject finds it or apprehends it in a particular condition.

This definition should not make all the root’s branches identical. Qur’anic وُجْد denotes a person’s available means and capacity, and its precise historical connection with the verbal branch wajada cannot be determined from the Qur’an itself.

The emotional branch wajd also existed in early Arabic, but should not be confused with Qur’anic wujd or with later mystical and psychological uses.

Wujūd, mawjūd, ījād, and wijdān do not occur in the Qur’an. Their later philosophical, mystical, and psychological usages should not determine the meaning of Qur’anic wajada; their absence from the Qur’an, however, does not by itself establish when the words originated.

Finally, attributing wajada to The God in constructions such as أَلَمْ يَجِدْكَ يَتِيمًا does not mean discovering something unknown after ignorance. The same linguistic construction that presents a person as possessing a particular quality here describes the addressee’s condition; the following verb shows what was done about that condition.

A concise definition of the root can be stated as follows:

W J D: in verbal usage, finding or apprehending something as present, accessible, available to experience, or discernible to the subject. Depending on context, it can therefore convey finding, encountering, having access, perceiving, or feeling. The nominal branch wujd denotes a person’s available means and capacity; its precise historical relationship to the other branches must remain an open question approached with caution.
