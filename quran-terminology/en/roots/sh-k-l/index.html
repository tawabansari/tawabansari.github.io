Resemblance, kind, manner and direction: from early Arabic usage to Qur’anic shākilah

SH K L has several related meanings in Arabic, notably resemblance, similarity in kind, correspondence, and belonging to a particular type or manner. In early usage, shakl did not denote only an object’s visible or geometric form; it also expressed likeness between things in their qualities, condition or kind.

In the same family, shākilah has been used for a manner, direction, way or characteristic with which a person’s conduct or movement corresponds. Other Arabic uses include binding and restraining, mixing colors and matters becoming ambiguous.

The Qur’an uses this root in shākilatihi and shaklihi. One concerns a person’s manner of acting; the other expresses resemblance and similarity in kind. These occurrences show why the root should not be confined to outward appearance. Conversely, shākilah should not be made to mean an unchangeable nature or predetermined destiny without supporting evidence.

1. The root in early Arabic

Resemblance and similarity in kind

An established meaning of shakl is a likeness, counterpart or equivalent. Hādhā shaklu hādhā means that one thing is of the same kind as, or resembles, another.

Such resemblance does not necessarily mean complete identity. Two things may share a kind, quality, behavior, condition or manner without being equal in every characteristic.

Lexical accounts transmit the following verse without a definite attribution to a particular poet:

فَلَا تَطْلُبَا لِي أَيِّمًا إِنْ طَلَبْتُمَا

fa-lā taṭlubā lī ayyiman in ṭalabtumā

فَإِنَّ الْأَيَامَى لَسْنَ لِي بِشُكُولِ

fa-inna al-ayāmā lasna lī bi-shukūl

Translation:

“If you seek a wife for me, do not seek an unmarried woman; unmarried women are not my counterparts.”

Shukūl is the plural of shakl and denotes compatibility or being a counterpart. The poet is not saying that the women fail to resemble him in outward shape; he does not consider them suitable matches. The line establishes this lexical use, but by itself does not explain all the speaker’s criteria or the full circumstances of the passage.

This witness records the sense of correspondence in Arabic. Since its attribution and date are uncertain, it cannot independently serve as definite evidence from the pre-Islamic period.

Shākala and tashākala are also used in early Arabic for resemblance and correspondence between two things. The central point is similarity or compatibility, not necessarily identical outward form.

Direction, side and manner

Another branch concerns direction, side and course. Shākilah is also used for the side or region of something.

A poem attributed to Tamīm ibn Muqbil, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, contains:

وَعَمْدًا تَصَدَّتْ يَوْمَ شَاكِلَةِ الْحِمَى

wa-ʿamdan taṣaddat yawma shākilati al-ḥimā

لِتَنْكَأَ قَلْبًا قَدْ صَحَا وَتَوَقَّرَا

li-tankaʾa qalban qad ṣaḥā wa-tawaqqarā

Translation:

“Deliberately she appeared that day beside the protected grounds, to wound again a heart that had recovered its composure and dignity.”

Shākilati al-ḥimā denotes the side or edge of the protected area. It concerns neither a psychological trait nor an object’s geometric appearance, but a spatial side or position.

Ibn Muqbil lived during both the pre-Islamic period and the beginning of Islam, but the precise date of this verse is not established. It is useful evidence of early shākilah without being securely datable before the Qur’an. The ending is also transmitted as wa-tanakkarā; the wording wa-tawaqqarā supplied here is attested in lexical transmission.

The spatial sense of side and direction helps explain shākilah’s relationship to orientation and manner. Saying that someone acts according to their shākilah may concern the direction their conduct takes. This semantic explanation does not establish the historical order in which the meanings developed.

Binding and restraining

Shakaltu al-dābbah means binding an animal’s legs with a particular implement. Shikāl names a tether used to restrain its movement.

This use belongs to everyday animal husbandry in Arabic-speaking settings and has a specific meaning: binding and limiting movement with a tether.

Some lexical explanations connect it with joining parts and resemblance, since the tether links the animal’s legs.

The image of movement constrained in a direction can also illustrate human shākilah. Habits, inclinations and established ways may direct conduct along particular paths.

This comparison must not become a definite historical derivation. The existence of shikāl does not establish that Qur’anic shākilah necessarily means an unbreakable bond of human nature.

Mixed colors and ambiguity

Shuklah is also recorded for one color mixed with another, such as redness mingled with whiteness.

Ashkala al-amr describes a matter that has become difficult to distinguish or understand. Features or possibilities are intermingled so that drawing clear distinctions is not easy.

These uses suggest a relationship between mixture, resemblance and difficulty in distinguishing things, without requiring all of them to derive from one original physical image.

Ishkāl, ambiguity or difficulty in understanding, and ashkāl, the plural of shakl, are different forms that look identical in unvocalized Persian writing.

The technical use of shakl for written vowel marks belongs to the history of writing and linguistic study. It should not be attributed to pre-Qur’anic Arabic without evidence or made the basis for interpreting these verses.

2. Central meaning and Qur’anic forms

The Qur’an uses two nouns from SH K L:

Shākilah in ʿalā shākilatihi in Al-Isrāʾ 17:84, and shakl in min shaklihi in Ṣād 38:58.

Forms such as shākala, tashākala, ashkala, mushkil, shikāl and tashakkul are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an.

Shākilah is a noun on the pattern fāʿilah, concerning manner and direction of action in its Qur’anic setting. Shakl in the other verse expresses resemblance in kind and quality.

These two uses support the following account of the central meaning:

Resemblance or correspondence between things in kind, quality, condition or manner; in human conduct, the direction or way with which a person’s actions accord.

This definition relates the two Qur’anic uses without making them identical.

Ṣād 38:58 concerns similarity in kind. Al-Isrāʾ 17:84 concerns the way according to which a person acts.

This account does not merge every lexical use into one image. Senses involving color, tethers, needs and outward form require their own constructions and evidence.

3. Shākilah and human conduct in the Qur’an

The principal occurrence concerning human behavior is Al-Isrāʾ 17:84:

قُلْ كُلٌّ يَعْمَلُ عَلَىٰ شَاكِلَتِهِ فَرَبُّكُمْ أَعْلَمُ بِمَنْ هُوَ أَهْدَىٰ سَبِيلًا

qul kullun yaʿmalu ʿalā shākilatihi fa-rabbukum aʿlamu bi-man huwa ahdā sabīlā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:84

Translation:

“Say: Everyone acts according to their own way; your Lord knows best who is better guided in the path.”

Shākilatihi accompanies yaʿmalu, “acts.” The concern is therefore not a person’s outward shape, but the relationship between their manner or orientation and what they do.

People may act from beliefs, aims, habits, inclinations or an adopted approach. These factors can affect conduct, but no single one exhausts the meaning of shākilah.

Early explanations include senses close to side, way, intention, nature, disposition and religion. Each illuminates part of the word’s range, but none should become the exclusive meaning of this verse without justification.

For example, “Everyone acts according to their intention” may express one aspect of behavior, but confines shākilah to inward intention. “Everyone acts according to their inborn nature” adds a meaning not necessarily conveyed by the word itself.

“According to their own way” preserves the breadth of the expression while leaving the roles of intention, disposition, inclination, belief and way of life for reflection on the verse.

Shākilah and observable conduct

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 describes the Qur’an’s different effects on believers and wrongdoers: healing and mercy for believers, but an increase only in loss for wrongdoers.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:83 addresses human responses to blessing and hardship: turning away when blessed and becoming despairing when afflicted.

After these verses comes the instruction to say that everyone acts according to their shākilah.

This sequence places shākilah in a setting where conduct and response matter. People may react differently to the same message, blessing or difficulty. Their responses can relate to the orientation and manner they have adopted in life.

The verse does not say that every action necessarily results from an inborn and unchangeable trait.

Does shākilah determine a person’s destiny?

A restrictive reading risks turning shākilah into a fixed, unchangeable personality and concluding that everyone is placed on a predetermined course from creation, without the freedom to change it.

The verse itself does not establish that conclusion.

Kullun yaʿmalu ʿalā shākilatihi concerns the relationship between action and a person’s way. It does not specify the ultimate origin of all their characteristics, declare them unchangeable or remove responsibility for choices.

Habits can change, beliefs can be corrected and people can reconsider their conduct in response to new understanding. Other verses, such as Al-Raʿd 13:11 on changes in people’s condition in relation to what they change within themselves, and Muḥammad 47:17 on increased guidance for those already guided, indicate the possibility of transformation within Qur’anic teaching.

Shākilah can therefore describe a person’s present way and orientation without becoming a deterministic judgment about their entire future.

Shākilah and judging guidance

The end of the verse states:

فَرَبُّكُمْ أَعْلَمُ بِمَنْ هُوَ أَهْدَىٰ سَبِيلًا

fa-rabbukum aʿlamu bi-man huwa ahdā sabīlā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:84

Translation:

“Your Lord knows best who is better guided in the path.”

This indicates that differing ways do not make every path equally sound.

Someone may act according to their own way, but that fact does not establish the way’s correctness. Judging which path is better guided is a separate matter.

Shākilah is thus neither praise nor blame in itself. Its value becomes clear in relation to conduct, orientation and the path followed.

The verse does not imply that acting according to nature or habit is necessarily right. Conversely, differences in people’s ways of life or characteristics alone do not prove that they are misguided.

4. Shakl as kind and resemblance in the Qur’an

The root’s other occurrence is Ṣād 38:58:

وَآخَرُ مِنْ شَكْلِهِ أَزْوَاجٌ

wa-ākharu min shaklihi azwāj

Ṣād 38:58

Translation:

“And other kinds like it.”

This continues the account of the transgressors’ outcome. Ṣād 38:57 mentions ḥamīm and ghassāq; the following verse introduces other varieties of a similar kind.

Shaklihi here means resemblance or similarity in kind, rather than outward form or geometric dimensions.

Min shaklihi means something of its kind or like it.

Such likeness does not necessarily mean complete identity in every characteristic. Two things may belong to one kind or group while differing in particular qualities.

For example, two implements may be of one type but differ in color, size or secondary functions. Being of the same kind does not make every characteristic identical.

Here too, shakl concerns similarity in kind, while azwāj denotes varieties or types.

The word’s meaning should be distinguished from the verse’s subject. In this context, resemblance concerns forms of punishment, but shakl itself does not mean punishment, intensity of pain or a specific quality of retribution.

The site’s base reading, Ḥafṣ, has the singular wa-ākharu. Another established reading has the plural wa-ukharu. The difference concerns grammatical expression, not necessarily the number of punishments: azwāj also occurs with the singular reading. Shaklihi expresses resemblance and similarity in kind in both.

The relationship between the two Qur’anic uses

At first, shākilah in Al-Isrāʾ 17:84 and shakl in Ṣād 38:58 may seem far apart in meaning.

There is, however, an intelligible relationship.

In Ṣād 38:58, one thing corresponds to another in kind and quality.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:84, human conduct corresponds to the person’s own way and orientation.

The former concerns likeness between things; the latter, correspondence between a person’s way and their actions.

This relationship clarifies a shared semantic connection. It does not establish that shākilah means exactly the same as shakl, or that all uses developed from one historically demonstrated image.

5. Related roots and distinctions

SH K L (shakl) and SH B H (shabah)

Shabah concerns resemblance in a quality or form. Such similarity can sometimes make things difficult to distinguish.

Shakl overlaps with that sense, but its use in Ṣād 38:58 emphasizes similarity in kind. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:84, shākilah concerns manner and direction of action.

The roots are therefore close in the field of resemblance, but their uses are not universally interchangeable.

SH K L (shakl) and M TH L (mithl)

Mithl is used for a likeness, counterpart, example or comparison. The root has a wide Qur’anic range, from expressing resemblance to giving examples that explain a truth.

Shakl can also express likeness, but min shaklihi focuses on similarity in kind.

Mithl and shakl may be close in many Arabic sentences. This does not justify drawing an absolute boundary between them: precise distinctions depend on construction and context.

SH K L (shakl) and Ṣ W R (ṣūrah)

Ṣūrah chiefly concerns how something appears, its configuration and formation. Qur’anic Ṣ W R also concerns giving form and the forms of created beings.

Shakl can be used for outward appearance in Arabic, but its Qur’anic uses are not confined to that sense.

Ṣād 38:58 does not discuss an object’s geometry or appearance. Nor does shākilah in Al-Isrāʾ 17:84 refer to a person’s face or bodily configuration.

Automatically translating shakl as “outward form” is therefore inadequate for both verses.

Shākilah, nature and disposition

Shākilah relates to nature, disposition, habit, intention and way of life, since these can all help orient conduct.

None alone is its complete equivalent.

“Nature” may emphasize innate characteristics; “disposition” approaches behavioral traits; “habit” concerns repeated behavior; and “intention” expresses an action’s inward aim and direction.

Shākilah in Al-Isrāʾ 17:84 offers a broader concept through which a person’s overall manner and orientation can be understood with reference to these factors.

It is therefore preferable to retain “way” or “manner” in the base translation and explain other dimensions in contextual analysis.

6. Interpretive difficulties and changing usage

In modern Persian, shakl commonly brings to mind an object’s appearance, outline or form. Expressions such as “the shape of a house,” “the shape of a tree” and “geometric shape” refer to configuration.

That usage is valid, but extending it to the entire Qur’anic root family narrows the semantic field.

Min shaklihi emphasizes kind and resemblance, not necessarily outward appearance. ʿAlā shākilatihi concerns a manner of acting, not bodily shape or physical structure.

Modern Persian also sometimes uses shākilah for the basic framework of a system or organization. That may help as a metaphor, but it should not be presented as the word’s direct meaning in Al-Isrāʾ 17:84.

“The framework of personality” may likewise refer to a collection of psychological and behavioral traits. This contemporary terminology should not make the Qur’an appear to present a particular psychological theory of fixed personality, psychological types or the biological origins of behavior.

The verse concerns human ways of acting. Psychological, social, educational and biological influences can be studied independently; they are not extracted from the word shākilah itself.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For shakl in Ṣād 38:58, “kind,” “type,” “like” and “of the same kind” are appropriate. The sentence’s construction determines which works best.

For min shaklihi, “of the same kind” or “like it” is natural and close to the verse. “Of its outward shape” is unsuitable because it confines the meaning to appearance.

For shākilah in Al-Isrāʾ 17:84, “way,” “manner” and, in some settings, “orientation” are appropriate.

The proposed rendering of the verse is:

“Say: Everyone acts according to their own way; your Lord knows best who is better guided in the path.”

This conveys shākilah without adding a theory of determinism, inborn nature or psychological structure.

A fuller explanation may describe how actions relate to an adopted way, inclination and course. The entire explanation should not be packed into the translation of one word.

For Ṣād 38:58, “And other kinds like it” is a natural rendering preserving similarity in kind and fitting the context. These proposals do not change the site’s installed verse translations.

Conclusion

SH K L has a broad Arabic semantic field, prominently including resemblance, kind, correspondence, direction and manner. Tethering animals, mixing colors and difficulty in distinguishing things are also attested in the family. More evidence would be needed to establish one historical origin for every meaning.

The Qur’an contains two occurrences of the root.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:84, shākilatihi denotes the manner and orientation of human action. Everyone acts according to their own way, but the existence of a way does not prove its correctness. The verse’s ending connects judgment about guidance with the Lord’s knowledge.

In Ṣād 38:58, shaklihi expresses resemblance in kind, referring to other varieties akin to those already mentioned.

Their semantic relationship appears in correspondence and compatibility: one thing can resemble another in kind, and a person’s actions can accord with their way and orientation.

Shākilah nevertheless means neither bodily appearance nor, by itself, an unchangeable destiny.

For Forqan’s study and translation, the concluding definition is:

In its use for resemblance, SH K L denotes correspondence in kind or quality; in shākilah, it denotes the particular manner and direction according to which a person acts.

This preserves both Qur’anic occurrences in their own settings, without imposing the outward, technical or interpretive meaning of one upon the other.
