Core meanings: Arabic S J L has several branches, including the water bucket, pouring water, turns at drawing water, and mutual competition. In writing, sijill denotes a scroll, sheet, or recorded document. The Qur’an contains al-sijill and sijjīl: the former concerns rolling up a scroll of writings, the latter stones sent down upon groups of people. Their resemblance does not establish a common historical origin, particularly since a non-Arabic origin has also been proposed for sijjīl.

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic S J L has uses concerning water, buckets, giving, competition, and recording writing.

Lexicons define al-sajl as a large bucket, especially one containing water.

Sajala al-māʾ means to pour out or cause water to flow.

Asjala lahu, in the context of giving, concerns making a generous gift.

Musājalah is explained through reciprocal drawing of water: two people draw in turns, each trying to match or surpass the other.

The word also occurs in competition and boasting; this semantic relationship alone does not establish the chronology of the uses.

Sijill as a sheet or written document and sijjīl as the material of stones present separate historical questions.

These meanings should not all be derived from a single image without evidence.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period: flowing tears

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays says:

عَيْنَاكَ دَمْعُهُمَا سِجَالُ
كَأَنَّ شَأْنَيْهِمَا أَوْشَالُ

ʿaynāka damʿuhumā sijāl / ka-anna shaʾnayhimā awshāl

Translation:

“Your eyes pour tears in succession, as though their tear channels were little streams of water.”

Sijāl here concerns repeated pouring and flowing.

Shaʾnayhimā refers to the tear channels of the two eyes.

Awshāl, the plural of washl, concerns small quantities or trickles of water.

The poet compares flowing tears to water running repeatedly.

The central image is pouring liquid and repeated flow.

Grief, weeping, and affection are not lexical meanings of sijāl itself; they arise from the description of eyes and tears.

Literary transmission attributes the poem to Imruʾ al-Qays. Early discussions also compare it with similar lines attributed to ʿAbīd ibn al-Abraṣ; resemblance by itself does not establish that this particular verse is misattributed.

It is useful evidence of early usage, although attribution alone cannot establish its exact date of composition.

Islamic-period poetry: competing at drawing water

The Islamic-period poet al-Faḍl ibn ʿAbbās ibn ʿUtbah ibn Abī Lahab says:

مَنْ يُسَاجِلْنِي يُسَاجِلْ مَاجِدًا
يَمْلَأُ الدَّلْوَ إِلَى عَقْدِ الْكَرَبِ

man yusājilnī yusājil mājidan / yamlaʾu al-dalwa ilā ʿaqdi al-karab

Translation:

“Whoever competes with me competes with a noble man who fills the bucket up to the fastening of its rope.”

Yusājilnī refers to competing with the speaker.

The poet immediately mentions filling a bucket.

This connection makes the material background clear.

Two people may fill their buckets in turns and compete in the amount of water drawn or their ability to perform the task.

This can provide an image for rivalry in generosity, prestige, and other social matters.

The poet belongs to the Islamic period. His verse is not direct pre-Islamic evidence, but illustrates the family's continuing use in later Arabic.

The Arabic branch

Early poetry and recorded lexical uses connect this family with water buckets, pouring, and drawing water.

Abundant giving and mutual competition are also recorded in this field.

In the water branch, sajl denotes a bucket containing water and sajala means pouring or causing it to flow. Musājalah describes competition explained through the image of reciprocal water-drawing.

This definition cannot automatically be transferred to sijill as a scroll or sijjīl as the material of stones.

Their Qur’anic uses and histories require separate examination.

2. Important forms and derivatives

سَجْل

In established Arabic usage, a large bucket containing water.

Some definitions emphasize fullness; others require water to be present, whether much or little.

Extended uses connect sajl with a share or gift.

This exact form does not occur in the Qur’an.

سِجَال

The plural of sajl.

In water-related usage, buckets or turns at drawing water.

Mutual competition is also a recognized use of this family.

Al-ḥarbu sijāl describes war in which victory or advantage alternates between the sides.

This does not make war or victory the original meaning of S J L.

سَجَلَ يَسْجُلُ

A Form I verb.

Sajala al-māʾ means pouring out or causing water to flow.

The use concerns water moving and being poured out.

This verb does not occur in this form in the Qur’an.

أَسْجَلَ

A Form IV verb.

Recorded meanings include generous giving, causing something to flow, and in some uses letting something go freely.

Asjala lahu can refer to abundant giving.

In some uses, isjāl concerns sending or releasing.

These senses belong to their particular constructions; their existence does not establish that Qur’anic sijjīl means something sent.

مُسَاجَلَة

The verbal noun of Form III.

In drawing water, a contest or reciprocal turns at drawing.

In social usage, competition or rivalry.

Form III alone does not establish that the participants are always equal in strength, ability, or amount of action.

Matching or competition is understood from the established usage and context.

اِنْسَجَلَ

Describes liquid being poured or flowing.

Here flowing is attributed to the water or liquid itself.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

سِجِلّ

A sheet, scroll, document, or written record.

The word also denotes formal documents in legal and administrative Arabic.

It occurs in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104.

The precise history of this written sense and its relationship to sajl, a bucket, is uncertain.

Proposed semantic links do not by themselves establish historical derivation.

سَجَّلَ يُسَجِّلُ

In writing, to record or enter something in a document.

Sajjala al-qāḍī refers in legal usage to a judge recording a ruling or decision.

This usage expanded in later administrative language.

The modern sense of recording information should not be treated, without historical examination, as the earliest meaning of the entire family.

تَسْجِيل

The verbal noun of sajjala.

Recording or entering something in writing or a document.

Familiar in modern Persian and Arabic, but absent from the Qur’an in this form.

سِجِلَّات

The plural of sijill.

Documents and written records.

This form is also absent from the Qur’an.

سِجِّيل

A word used with ḥijārah, stones, in the Qur’an.

It identifies the type or material of stones sent upon people in accounts of punishment.

Its origin is disputed in lexical and historical discussion.

One familiar explanation connects it with Persian sang, stone, and gil, clay.

This is not the only proposed account.

Other origins have been proposed, but evaluating them requires direct linguistic evidence and a demonstrable route of transmission; resemblance alone is insufficient.

Placement under S J L in Arabic indexes does not make sijjīl an established derivative of sajala.

Forms actually found in the Qur’an

The Qur’anic index groups four occurrences and two principal forms under S J L:

  • Sijjīl in Hūd 11:82, Al-Ḥijr 15:74, and Al-Fīl 105:4.
  • Al-sijill in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104.

The first three concern stones.

The fourth compares the rolling up of heaven with rolling up a scroll of writings.

This fundamental distinction guides the Qur’anic study of this family.

3. Do al-sijill and sijjīl share an origin?

The words look very similar.

Their structures, however, differ.

Al-sijill ends in a doubled lām.

Sijjīl has a doubled jīm and a long ī.

Their Qur’anic meanings also differ clearly.

Al-sijill concerns a sheet or scroll.

Sijjīl occurs in a construction concerning stones.

The familiar Persian explanation of sijjīl

The lexical tradition proposes a Persian origin.

According to this account, Persian sang and gil combined and entered Arabic as sijjīl.

Sang denotes stone; gil denotes earth mixed with water.

The resulting sense concerns stone and clay, or hardened clay material.

This fits the Qur’anic use, since the stones in a parallel account are described with ṭīn, clay.

Semantic compatibility alone does not prove every historical and phonetic stage of borrowing.

The limits of historical reconstruction

A proposed origin must distinguish semantic resemblance from evidence of borrowing.

A demonstrable historical route requires recorded forms, dates, and evidence of contact between languages.

Similarity of sound and meaning is not enough to reconstruct that route.

Where the stages of transmission remain unclear, a proposed origin should remain a hypothesis.

For reading the Qur’an, the word's contextual meaning is directly relevant.

Is sijjīl derived from sijill, a document?

Some older explanations connect sijjīl with sijill as a written record.

They suggest that the stones had been recorded or appointed in the divine decree.

This explanation faces two difficulties.

First, resemblance does not establish historical derivation.

Second, the Qur’an places sijjīl beside stones and uses ṭīn in a parallel account.

The material interpretation therefore has clearer support from direct Qur’anic comparison.

Recording a decree or determining a group's fate may be an independent topic in the punishment narrative; it should not be made the lexical meaning of sijjīl without evidence.

Al-sijill and sijjīl have distinct Qur’anic uses; a shared historical origin has not been established.

4. A scroll of writings in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104

يَوْمَ نَطْوِي السَّمَاءَ كَطَيِّ السِّجِلِّ لِلْكُتُبِ كَمَا بَدَأْنَا أَوَّلَ خَلْقٍ نُعِيدُهُ وَعْدًا عَلَيْنَا إِنَّا كُنَّا فَاعِلِينَ

yawma naṭwī al-samāʾa ka-ṭayyi al-sijilli li-al-kutubi kamā badaʾnā awwala khalqin nuʿīduhu waʿdan ʿalaynā innā kunnā fāʿilīn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104

Translation:

“The day We roll up heaven as a scroll is rolled over writings; as We began the first creation, We shall bring it back—a promise binding upon Us. We shall indeed do it.”

Al-sijill is a noun following ṭayy.

Ṭayy, from Ṭ W Y, means rolling or folding up.

Here al-sijill is a sheet or scroll bearing writings.

Li-al-kutub expresses its relationship to the writings.

How is a scroll rolled up?

In older writing practices, text could be placed on a long sheet and the sheet rolled into a scroll.

When open, its written surface is spread out.

When rolled, its extended parts gather over one another.

What was spread out becomes rolled and gathered together.

This image helps explain the comparison.

The Qur’an uses the familiar rolling of a scroll to illustrate the rolling up of heaven.

Sijill itself does not mean rolling.

That action comes from naṭwī and ṭayy.

Sijill names what is rolled in the comparison.

Does sijill mean a writer?

Some older explanations take Sijill as the name of a writer or angel.

That reading is not necessary to understand the construction.

The established written sense of sijill is a sheet or scroll.

The comparison is also clearer when something extended—heaven—is compared with another thing that can be rolled and gathered.

The scroll reading therefore fits the language and image well.

Reports about a writer or angel do not, without independent evidence, displace the established lexical sense.

The role of li-al-kutub

In ka-ṭayyi al-sijilli li-al-kutub, lām expresses a relationship between scroll and writings. In the scroll interpretation, it has also been explained in the sense of “over”: the sheet is rolled over its writing.

The lām should not automatically be treated as personal ownership or a devotional purpose.

The relationship concerns the sheet and what is written or kept on it.

The singular li-al-kitāb is also attested among Qur’anic readings alongside plural li-al-kutub.

This varies whether the writing is expressed in singular or plural form.

Both can retain the image of rolling up a sheet and its writing.

The variant alone does not make sijill necessarily a person's name in one reading and a scroll in another.

Does the verse explain heaven's physical shape?

The verse describes an immense event: heaven being rolled up.

The scroll supplies a comprehensible image of gathering something extended.

The comparison alone does not specify heaven's geometry or the physical mechanism of its transformation.

It does not establish that heaven is a material sheet of particular thickness and dimensions.

Nor can the comparison be confined without evidence to a specific theory of the universe's end, collapsing space, or celestial structure.

The verse speaks of the end of creation's present condition without defining its full nature in scientific terminology.

Connection with Al-Zumar 39:67

Al-Zumar 39:67 also describes the heavens being folded up.

Its action and imagery are close to Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104.

Sijill itself does not occur there.

That passage can illuminate the image without adding a new lexical meaning to sijill.

Creation's beginning and return

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104 continues by declaring that creation will be restored as it was first begun.

This places the rolling up of heaven within a wider context.

The subject includes restoration and renewal of creation, beyond gathering something extended.

Sijill itself does not mean re-creation.

Restoration is expressed by nuʿīduhu.

Al-sijill names the scroll or sheet of writings; ṭayy expresses rolling; restoration of creation appears in the rest of the sentence.

5. Stones of sijjīl sent upon Lot's people

Hūd 11:82 says:

فَلَمَّا جَاءَ أَمْرُنَا جَعَلْنَا عَالِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا وَأَمْطَرْنَا عَلَيْهَا حِجَارَةً مِنْ سِجِّيلٍ مَنْضُودٍ

fa-lammā jāʾa amrunā jaʿalnā ʿāliyahā sāfilahā wa-amṭarnā ʿalayhā ḥijāratan min sijjīlin manḍūd

Hūd 11:82

Translation:

“When Our command came, We made its upper part its lower part and rained upon it stones of sijjīl, arranged in layers.”

Sijjīl follows min in this verse.

The construction identifies the material or type of the stones.

Ḥijārah means stones.

Manḍūd describes arrangement, stacking, and components placed together.

What material is sijjīl?

Parallel Qur’anic uses help explain it.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33 says:

لِنُرْسِلَ عَلَيْهِمْ حِجَارَةً مِنْ طِينٍ

li-nursila ʿalayhim ḥijāratan min ṭīn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33

Translation:

“To send upon them stones of clay.”

This concerns the same narrative.

Hūd 11:82 uses ḥijāratan min sijjīl.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33 uses ḥijāratan min ṭīn.

This is one of the clearest Qur’anic clues to the meaning of sijjīl.

Both concern stones sent upon those being punished.

The latter identifies the material with ṭīn, clay.

Sijjīl here is therefore connected with clay or stone-like clay material.

This also fits the familiar historical explanation involving stone and clay.

Must sijjīl be clay baked in fire?

Some translations use baked clay or stones of baked clay.

This is understandable because hardened clay can become stone-like.

Hardening and baking in fire nevertheless need to be distinguished.

Hūd 11:82 itself mentions neither fire nor a kiln.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33 names clay, without describing a baking process.

The passages support the connection with clay stones; the precise method of hardening needs further evidence.

“Stones of hardened clay” can be suitable without asserting a particular manufacturing process.

What does manḍūd add?

Manḍūd belongs to N Ḍ D.

That family concerns arranging, ordering, and stacking.

In Hūd 11:82, manḍūd accompanies sijjīl.

It can describe the material or stones as arranged or layered.

Its sense should not be absorbed into sijjīl itself.

Sijjīl identifies material or type.

Manḍūd adds a separate description.

It does not by itself establish the number of layers, size of stones, or precise manner of their falling.

Connection with Hūd 11:83

Hūd 11:83 describes the stones as musawwamah.

From S W M, this concerns marking or designation.

Their being marked or designated is expressed by musawwamah.

It should not become the lexical meaning of sijjīl.

The verse does not explicitly say that each stone bore a particular person's name.

Marking alone does not disclose all details of the marks or the allocation of each stone.

6. Sijjīl in Al-Ḥijr 15:74 and Al-Fīl 105:4

Al-Ḥijr 15:74 says:

فَجَعَلْنَا عَالِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا وَأَمْطَرْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ حِجَارَةً مِنْ سِجِّيلٍ

fa-jaʿalnā ʿāliyahā sāfilahā wa-amṭarnā ʿalayhim ḥijāratan min sijjīl

Al-Ḥijr 15:74

Translation:

“We made its upper part its lower part and rained upon them stones of sijjīl.”

The same construction concerning stones of sijjīl appears here.

It concerns the same event involving Lot's people.

Unlike Hūd 11:82, it does not include manḍūd.

That omission does not establish a different material.

The construction's basic meaning is the same in both passages.

Stones of sijjīl in Al-Fīl

Al-Fīl 105:4 says:

تَرْمِيهِمْ بِحِجَارَةٍ مِنْ سِجِّيلٍ

tarmīhim bi-ḥijāratin min sijjīl

Al-Fīl 105:4

Translation:

“Pelting them with stones of sijjīl.”

The birds mentioned in the preceding verse are the subject of tarmīhim.

Bi-ḥijārah identifies the stones used in the pelting.

Min sijjīl identifies their material or type.

Sijjīl therefore occurs beyond the account of Lot's people.

Al-Fīl uses it for stones falling upon another group.

Is sijjīl a kind of punishment?

All three occurrences accompany stones.

Sijjīl itself does not name punishment.

Punishment and the groups' fate emerge from the narrative and its actions.

In Hūd and Al-Ḥijr, the stones occur amid the overturning of a settlement and destruction of a people.

In Al-Fīl, they appear in the account of the defeat of the elephant army's scheme.

In both accounts sijjīl describes the stones' type or material.

It should therefore not be translated as “divine punishment,” “predestined stones,” or “recorded punishment.”

Were the stones in both accounts identical?

The shared word indicates a common semantic field.

It does not establish identical size, weight, shape, geographical origin, or manner of descent.

Each account's statements should be understood in their own context.

Later accounts of very small or large stones, a particular disease, or the number carried by each bird should not be added to the word's meaning without Qur’anic evidence.

The three occurrences together

In Hūd 11:82, Al-Ḥijr 15:74, and Al-Fīl 105:4, sijjīl occurs with stones.

The clay description in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33 is the clearest clue to their material.

Qur’anic sijjīl thus concerns clay stones or hardened clay material.

This conclusion follows from comparison of the passages, without requiring a particular historical etymology.

7. Distinctions from nearby words

S J L: al-sijill, and K T B: kitāb

Qur’anic kitāb, from K T B, concerns writing, written material, and texts.

Sijill in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104 names the sheet or scroll bearing writings.

The two are related here but serve different roles.

Sijill is the sheet or scroll.

Kutub identifies the associated writings.

Al-sijill should therefore not simply be translated as the act of writing.

S J L: al-sijill, and Ṣ Ḥ F: ṣuḥuf

Ṣuḥuf concerns sheets and writings.

Sijill in the scroll passage belongs to the same general field.

Not every sheet is necessarily a scroll.

Ṭayy in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104 introduces rolling.

“Scroll” is therefore suitable here.

It clarifies the rolling of the sheet without restricting all written material to scrolls.

S J L: sijjīl, and Ḥ J R: ḥajar

Ḥajar means stone.

Ḥijārah appears in all three sijjīl passages.

It names the stones themselves.

Sijjīl identifies their material or type.

The two should not be treated as exact synonyms.

Although sijjīl concerns stone-like material here, the construction contains two distinct words.

S J L: sijjīl, and Ṭ Y N: ṭīn

Ṭīn means clay.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33 uses it for stones in the same account.

The passage directly informs the interpretation of sijjīl.

Ṭīn is not lexically identical with sijjīl.

Ṭīn names clay.

Sijjīl here concerns the material of stones connected with clay and its hardening.

The distinction should be retained.

S J L: sijjīl, and S J N: sijjīn

Sijjīn occurs in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8.

It resembles sijjīl in form and sound.

The Qur’anic index places them in different families.

Sijjīn ends in nūn.

Sijjīl ends in lām.

Sijjīn occurs with the record of the wicked in Al-Muṭaffifīn.

Sijjīl accompanies stones in Hūd, Al-Ḥijr, and Al-Fīl.

Similarity does not authorize imposing one word's meaning on the other.

Writing and recording in the context of sijjīn do not establish that sijjīl stones bore writing or personal names.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Principal meanings

Sajl: a bucket containing water; by extension, a share or gift.

Sajala: poured water or caused it to flow.

Sijāl: buckets or reciprocal turns at drawing water; by extension, competition and alternating advantage.

Asjala: gave generously; in other uses, caused to flow or released freely.

Musājalah: mutual competition or rivalry.

Insajala: was poured out or flowed.

Sijill: a sheet, scroll, or written document.

Sajjala: recorded, in writing-related usage.

Tasjīl: recording or entering something in writing.

Sijillāt: documents and written records.

Sijjīl: the material of hardened clay stones in Qur’anic usage; its etymology remains disputed.

Proposed translations of the Qur’anic forms

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104:

كَطَيِّ السِّجِلِّ لِلْكُتُبِ

ka-ṭayyi al-sijilli li-al-kutub

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104

“As a scroll is rolled over writings.”

“Scroll” suitably renders al-sijill here.

The stones of sijjīl construction in Hūd 11:82, Al-Ḥijr 15:74, and Al-Fīl 105:4 can be rendered:

“Stones of hardened clay.”

This proposal follows comparison with Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33.

Where the base translation should avoid settling the exact origin or material beyond the evidence, sijjīl can be retained and explained briefly.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meanings

For the brief family box:

“Water buckets and pouring in the Arabic branch; in the Qur’an, a scroll of writings and the material of sijjīl stones, whose historical origins are not necessarily shared.”

For Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104:

“A sheet or scroll bearing writings, which is rolled up.”

For Hūd 11:82:

“The material of stones sent upon those being punished; parallel passages connect it with hardened clay.”

For Al-Ḥijr 15:74:

“Stones of material connected with hardened clay, rained upon those being punished.”

For Al-Fīl 105:4:

“The material of the stones the birds hurled at the elephant army.”

The exact forms al-sijill and sijjīl should remain distinct in the boxes. These proposals do not replace existing authored verse explanations.

The forms should not be reduced to an invented shared meaning.

9. Conclusion

S J L has several established Arabic uses.

In the water branch, sajl is a bucket containing water and sajala al-māʾ means pouring or causing water to flow.

Reciprocal water-drawing supplies the explanatory background of musājalah as competition.

The poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays uses sijāl for repeatedly flowing tears. It connects with buckets and pouring, although attribution and dating require care.

Al-Faḍl ibn ʿAbbās's Islamic-period verse also makes the connection with competing at water-drawing clear.

None of the verbs for pouring water or competing occurs in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic forms are al-sijill and sijjīl.

Al-sijill in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104 concerns a scroll or sheet of writings.

Heaven is compared with something rolled up like a scroll.

Rolling comes from ṭayy and naṭwī, not from sijill itself.

Sijjīl accompanies stones in Hūd 11:82, Al-Ḥijr 15:74, and Al-Fīl 105:4.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33 describes the stones in the account of Lot's people with ṭīn.

This connects Qur’anic sijjīl with clay or hardened clay material.

The word alone does not specify how it hardened, the stones' size, or names supposedly written on them.

The lexical tradition proposes Persian stone and clay as the source of sijjīl. This should remain a proposed explanation unless the stages of borrowing are independently established.

Similarity to sijill, a sheet, does not establish that the stones were named after the recording of a punitive decree.

S J L should not be reduced to an invented single core meaning. Al-sijill in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104 denotes a scroll or sheet of writings; sijjīl in the other three passages concerns stone material connected with hardened clay; buckets, pouring water, and musājalah constitute established Arabic uses of this indexed family.

For Forqan, al-sijill can be translated as “scroll”; parallel passages support explaining sijjīl in relation to hardened clay stones.

These distinctions prevent resemblance of forms from replacing independent examination of language, history, and Qur’anic context.
