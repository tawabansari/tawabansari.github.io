The Qur’anic root ف ل ن (fulān)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فُلَانًا
(fulānan)
So-and-so; accusative noun, Al-Furqān 25:28

فُلَان، فُلَانَة
(fulān, fulānah)
A substitute for a person’s name; masculine and feminine forms

A substitute for an unspoken personal name: from “so-and-so” in language to regret over a chosen companion in the Qur’an

1. The word in early Arabic

Arabic فُلَان stands in place of a person’s name, much like Persian fulāni or English “so-and-so.” The speaker refers to someone without giving their actual name.

Arabic grammar calls this كِنَايَةٌ عَنِ الْعَلَمِ: an expression standing in place of a proper name.

For example, جَاءَ فُلَانٌ means “So-and-so came,” and لَقِيتُ فُلَانًا means “I met so-and-so.”

The speaker may know the person while leaving their name unspoken. The listener may also know who is intended, or may not know the name. Fulān therefore need not mean an unknown person: it substitutes for a name not stated in the utterance.

This clarifies the word’s semantic core: fulān replaces a personal name; it does not describe character, conduct, social standing, or belief.

Classical lexicons record فُلَان and فُلَانَة as substitutes for men’s and women’s names. Some older usages employ these words with the definite article al- for animals or other things, as in رَكِبْتُ الْفُلَانَ for an animal someone rode, or حَلَبْتُ الْفُلَانَةَ for an animal someone milked.

Fulān was sufficiently familiar in Arabic to stand in place of a real name without that name being introduced.

A historical witness and its limits

A line attributed to the Umayyad poet Abū al-Najm al-ʿIjlī reads:

فِي لَجَّةٍ أَمْسِكْ فُلَانًا عَنْ فُلِ

Fī lajjatin amsik fulānan ʿan fuli.

“Amid the hubbub, hold so-and-so back from so-and-so.”

Here فُلَانًا and فُلِ replace personal names. The speaker refers to people without identifying them by proper names.

Grammarians differ over فُل. Some regard it as a shortened form of fulān; others distinguish an independent expression, particularly in direct address. Its existence does not conclusively establish whether fulān historically developed from the shorter form or vice versa.

This line belongs to the poetic tradition after Islam’s emergence and is not direct evidence of pre-Qur’anic usage.

The independently datable evidence presented in this study does not suffice to reconstruct fulān’s precise pre-Qur’anic history. This limitation does not prove that the word was absent then. The Qur’an itself clearly attests its use in Arabic at the time of revelation, and lexicons preserve its function as a substitute for personal names.

Some morphological analyses treat fulān as following the fuʿāl pattern; other accounts of its earlier structure also exist. Its historical derivation from a particular verb has not been established here.

No hypothetical physical image or verb should therefore be invented for ف ل ن, with a claim that fulān originally meant hiding, losing, or making someone unknown.

Its established linguistic function is to stand in place of a name.

2. Related forms and Qur’anic usage

Familiar Arabic forms discussed with this expression include:

فُلَان for a masculine personal name; فُلَانَة for a feminine name; فُلَانِيّ for a relation to an unnamed person or name; and vocative expressions such as يا فُلُ and يا فُلَاه. Their shared function does not resolve the disagreement over whether every vocative form derives from fulān.

Among these forms, the Qur’an uses only fulānan.

The expression occurs once in the Qur’an, as فُلَانًا in Al-Furqān 25:28.

Fulānan is an accusative noun. The final written alif is associated with accusative nunation; the base form is fulān.

No verb from this family occurs in the Qur’an. It should not be treated like an extensive verbal family whose basic and derived verb forms, active participles, and passive participles provide additional meanings.

Listing ف ل ن as a root is an Arabic indexing convention for the word’s consonants. It does not itself prove a historical verb فَلَنَ with an independent meaning.

3. Al-Furqān 25:28: so-and-so taken as a close friend

يَٰوَيْلَتَىٰ لَيْتَنِي لَمْ أَتَّخِذْ فُلَانًا خَلِيلًا

Yā waylatā laytanī lam attakhidh fulānan khalīlā.

“Woe to me! If only I had not taken so-and-so as a close friend!”

Al-Furqān 25:28

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of fulānan.

Understanding it requires attention to the sentence’s construction.

لَيْتَنِي expresses a wish marked by regret: the person wishes they had not done something in the past.

لَمْ أَتَّخِذْ negates taking or choosing in the past.

فُلَانًا identifies the person being discussed; خَلِيلًا names the position in which the speaker had placed that person.

Grammatically, fulānan is the first object of attakhidh, and khalīlan its second object.

The speaker regrets choosing someone as a close friend.

Why is the person not named?

The Qur’an does not give the friend’s actual name here, using fulānan instead.

This wording allows the reader to understand the verse without knowing the person’s historical name.

A particular name might direct attention toward that individual’s biography. Fulān instead keeps the focus on the relationship the speaker regrets.

The word alone does not establish the definitive reason for leaving the name unspoken. Its lexical meaning does not specify secrecy, contempt, generalization, or another purpose.

What is clear is that the text leaves the person unnamed and that the relationship can be understood without identifying them.

Later exegetical reports propose individuals’ names for the verse. Those names are not part of its wording, and fulānan itself identifies none of them.

Forqan’s translation should therefore not replace fulān with a particular person’s name.

What is being regretted?

The surrounding verses explain the reason for regret.

Al-Furqān 25:27 portrays a wrongdoer biting both hands on the day of account, wishing to have taken a way with the Messenger.

Al-Furqān 25:28 then mentions the close friend the speaker wishes never to have chosen.

Al-Furqān 25:29 clarifies the regret: that person led the speaker away from the remembrance after it had reached them.

The condemnation does not target affection or close friendship in themselves. It concerns a friendship that draws someone away from truth and guidance.

Fulān does not itself mean “one who misleads.” The person’s conduct in the next verse supplies that description.

Khalīl is another word, from خ ل ل, concerning close friendship and relationship. Fulān refers to the person without a name; khalīl identifies their relationship with the speaker.

For a fuller account of close friendship, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root خ ل ل.

4. Fulān and responsibility in choosing a companion

The word’s occurrence is limited, but its position in the sentence matters.

The regretful person does not merely say they knew someone or once met them. They say they took that person as their khalīl.

In this construction, اتَّخَذَ concerns selecting someone for a place within a relationship.

The relationship influenced conduct, and the following verse, Al-Furqān 25:29, attributes being led away from the remembrance to that person.

Close relationships can influence decisions, beliefs, and behavior. A friend, companion, or trusted person may encourage good actions or lead someone away from the right path.

This social observation must not become the definition of fulān. The word simply stands for an unnamed person; their positive or negative influence follows from their conduct and relationship.

Al-Zukhruf 43:67 also describes close friendships changing on the day of account, excepting the God-conscious. It indicates that friendship’s final value depends on its direction and consequences, not merely on two people’s closeness.

In Al-Furqān 25:29, بَعْدَ إِذْ جَاءَنِي matters: the speaker says they were led away after the remembrance had reached them.

This qualification shows the importance of guidance reaching a person and their response to it when considering responsibility.

Everyone who befriends a misguided person cannot be assigned identical responsibility without considering their own knowledge, freedom of choice, and conduct.

These verses place responsibility for choosing a companion alongside that companion’s influence. أَضَلَّنِي, “led me astray,” belongs to ض ل ل; its meaning should not be transferred to ف ل ن.

5. Differences from related expressions

ف ل ن and أ ح د

In some constructions, أَحَد refers to one person or any member of a set.

Fulān, however, stands in place of a personal name.

لَقِيتُ فُلَانًا means “I met so-and-so,” someone whose name is not stated; مَا لَقِيتُ أَحَدًا means “I met no one.”

The first sentence substitutes for a name; the second denies meeting anyone.

The words therefore differ in function and meaning.

ف ل ن and ر ج ل

رَجُل means a man, a general designation for a male person.

Fulān is a name substitute.

جَاءَ رَجُلٌ means “A man came”; جَاءَ فُلَانٌ means “So-and-so came.”

Both sentences may leave the name unstated, but rajul identifies someone as a man, while fulān occupies the place of a proper name.

ف ل ن and مَنْ

مَنْ can function as an interrogative or relative pronoun for people.

In مَنْ جَاءَ؟, the speaker asks, “Who came?”

جَاءَ فُلَانٌ instead refers to someone whose name is not given in the sentence.

Asking about someone’s identity and substituting for their name are different functions.

ف ل ن and اِسْم

Ism is a general word for the names of people and things.

Fulān is itself an expression used in place of a particular name.

It can therefore be called a name substitute, but names in general are not fulān.

This also shows why fulān need not arise from ignorance of the name. The speaker may know it yet choose not to use it.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The most natural Persian/Dari equivalent of fulān is fulāni, corresponding to English “so-and-so.”

It preserves the Arabic function: referring to someone without giving their actual name.

An appropriate rendering of Al-Furqān 25:28 is:

“Woe to me! If only I had not taken so-and-so as a close friend!”

“So-and-so” correctly stands in place of a name without adding a description of the person’s character.

“That person” may convey the general idea, but does not mark the name-substitution function as clearly as “so-and-so.”

“An unknown person” is inaccurate: the word does not establish that the speaker did not know them.

“A bad friend” should not replace fulān either. Badness follows from the person’s contextual conduct, not the word’s lexical meaning.

In Persian, retaining the familiar forms fulān or fulāni is preferable to inventing a new equivalent; Persian speakers already understand their function.

Conclusion

ف ل ن has very limited Qur’anic usage. Its only form is fulānan in Al-Furqān 25:28.

Fulān is not the proper name of a particular individual; it stands in place of a personal name in speech. It does not necessarily mean the person is unknown to the speaker.

The verse refers to someone whom the speaker regrets having chosen as a khalīl. The following verse explains that regret through being led away from the remembrance and guidance.

Arabic also has fulānah, fulāniyy, and associated vocative forms; these do not occur in the Qur’an.

An original verb or historical physical image cannot be invented without evidence. The established function is name substitution, which translation should preserve.

Final definition for Forqan:

ف ل ن: a nominal family whose principal form is fulān, an expression standing in place of a person’s proper name without specifying their identity, moral character, or status.

Qur’anic fulānan refers to an unnamed person whom the speaker regrets having chosen as a close friend. Its natural Persian equivalent is fulāni, and its English equivalent is “so-and-so.”
