The Arabic root و ج ف concerns swift movement, motion, and agitation. This movement may appear in the rapid pace of horses and camels or in the beating and trembling of the human heart.

The Quran uses the root twice, in two different constructions: أَوْجَفْتُمْ for driving mounts swiftly, and وَاجِفَةٌ for hearts agitated on the Day of Resurrection.

These uses show that the root should not be restricted either to “riding swiftly” or to “being afraid.” Movement connects them, while each verse’s context determines its particular kind and cause.

1. The Root in Early Arabic

In early Arabic, وَجْف and وَجِيف describe a rapid gait of horses and camels. وَجَفَ الْبَعِيرُ means that a camel moved swiftly; أَوْجَفَ الرَّاكِبُ دَابَّتَهُ means that a rider urged his mount to move swiftly.

One witness attributed to the pre-Islamic period occurs in an ode ascribed to ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah, known as ʿAlqamah al-Faḥl:

إِلَيْكَ أَبَيْتَ اللَّعْنَ كَانَ وَجِيفُهَا

ilayka abayta al-laʿna kāna wajīfuhā

بِمُشْتَبِهَاتٍ هَوْلُهُنَّ مَهِيبُ

bi-mushtabihātin hawluhunna mahību

“Toward you—may you remain above reproach—was her swift passage, through bewildering routes whose terrors inspire dread.”

Describing his journey to al-Ḥārith al-Ghassānī, the poet speaks of his camel’s movement through difficult terrain.

وَجِيفُهَا refers to the camel’s swift movement, not the animal’s fear. The terrifying character of the routes is expressed by other words, especially هَوْلُهُنَّ.

This witness matters because the root’s use for a mount’s rapid movement is not merely a result of interpreting Al-Ḥashr: it also appears in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Poetry attributed to al-ʿAjjāj, an early Islamic poet, also includes:

نَاجٍ طَوَاهُ الْأَيْنُ مِمَّا وَجَفَا

nājin ṭawāhu al-aynu mimmā wajafā

“A swift camel, worn thin by fatigue from its rapid traveling.”

وَجَفَا again refers to a camel’s rapid movement. Historically, this witness belongs after the emergence of Islam and illustrates continuity of usage, rather than independently establishing a pre-Quranic meaning.

Alongside an animal’s swift movement, Arabic lexicons record وَجَفَ الشَّيْءُ for something becoming agitated or moving, and وَجَفَ الْقَلْبُ for a heart beating or becoming agitated.

The family’s two principal branches are therefore swift movement and agitated motion or throbbing.

ʿAlqamah’s witness supports rapid movement in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Establishing precisely when the heart-related use arose, or from which branch it developed, would require independent historical evidence. Recording both senses in dictionaries does not establish their chronological order.

2. Forms and Semantic Core

Recognized Arabic forms include:

وَجَفَ: Moved swiftly; also used of things and hearts for movement, agitation, or throbbing.

وَجْف, وَجِيف, and وُجُوف: Verbal nouns denoting rapid movement or agitation and throbbing, depending on usage.

أَوْجَفَ: Hastened or caused something, especially a mount, to move swiftly.

إِيجَاف: Rapid movement or urging a mount onward swiftly.

وَاجِف: Moving, throbbing, or agitated, as in an agitated heart.

مِيجَاف: A description of a mount that moves very rapidly.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but not all occur in the Quran.

Semantic Core

On the basis of recognized uses, the root’s central meaning can be described as:

Swift movement, or agitated motion and throbbing, particularly in the movement of mounts and the beating of hearts.

For horses and camels, physical movement and rapid progress are prominent; for hearts, beating and inward unrest.

Movement provides a semantic connection between these uses, but does not prove that one necessarily developed historically from the other.

Fear is not an invariant component of every derivative. A horse moving swiftly need not be afraid; a heart described as وَاجِف in a frightening situation may be beating from fear.

3. Quranic Occurrences

A. Al-Ḥashr 59:6 — Driving Mounts Swiftly

وَمَا أَفَاءَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِ مِنْهُمْ فَمَا أَوْجَفْتُمْ عَلَيْهِ مِنْ خَيْلٍ وَلَا رِكَابٍ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ يُسَلِّطُ رُسُلَهُ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۚ وَاللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

wa-mā afāʾa allāhu ʿalā rasūlihi minhum fa-mā awjaftum ʿalayhi min khaylin wa-lā rikābin wa-lākinna allāha yusalliṭu rusulahu ʿalā man yashāʾu wa-allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr

Al-Ḥashr 59:6

“Whatever The God restored to His Messenger from them, you drove neither horses nor riding camels to obtain it. But The God gives His Messengers power over whomever He wills, and The God is capable of all things.”

أَوْجَفْتُمْ is a Form IV verb addressed to a plural audience: “you caused to move swiftly,” or here, “you drove at speed.”

خَيْل refers to horses; رِكَاب here denotes camels used for riding and travel.

فَمَا أَوْجَفْتُمْ عَلَيْهِ مِنْ خَيْلٍ وَلَا رِكَابٍ thus states that the addressees had not driven horses or camels swiftly to obtain what God restored to His Messenger.

Several linguistic points matter in this construction.

First, أَوْجَفْتُمْ concerns moving mounts or urging them to haste. The verb alone does not mean fighting, winning, or taking spoils.

Second, the pronoun in عَلَيْهِ refers back to what the verse introduces as مَا أَفَاءَ اللَّهُ. The subject is therefore an effort that was not undertaken to acquire that property.

Third, مِنْ in مِنْ خَيْلٍ occurs in a negative construction and reinforces its comprehensive scope: no horses or camels were employed in such riding.

The surah’s context clarifies this. Al-Ḥashr 59:2 speaks of a group of the People of the Book leaving their homes. Al-Ḥashr 59:6 then explains that the property restored to the Messenger was not acquired through the addressees’ rapid riding of horses and camels.

Al-Ḥashr 59:7 turns to the allocation and distribution of that property, including preventing its exclusive circulation among the wealthy.

This continuity places أَوْجَفْتُمْ within a specific discussion of acquiring property. Rules governing that property and its distribution derive from the passages together, not from the lexical meaning of و ج ف alone.

The verb here therefore means “you urged mounts to move swiftly,” conveyed within the negative sentence as “you drove neither horses nor riding camels.”

B. Al-Nāziʿāt 79:8 — Trembling Hearts on the Day of Resurrection

قُلُوبٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَاجِفَةٌ

qulūbun yawmaʾidhin wājifah

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:8

“Hearts on that Day will be trembling.”

The root appears here as وَاجِفَةٌ, the feminine active participle of وَجَفَ.

Unlike Al-Ḥashr 59:6, this verse concerns the condition of hearts on the Day of Resurrection, rather than horses or camels moving swiftly.

قُلُوبٌ is the subject and وَاجِفَةٌ its predicate. يَوْمَئِذٍ specifies when this condition occurs.

In context, وَاجِفَةٌ describes hearts that are agitated and throbbing.

The nearby verses help explain the image.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:6 speaks of الرَّاجِفَةُ and its shaking. In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:7, another event follows it. Al-Nāziʿāt 79:8 then turns to the hearts of people who are agitated that Day.

The sequence portrays shaking and upheaval in events alongside unrest in hearts.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:9 describes their eyes as downcast. Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10 then presents the question of those who doubt being restored again.

The context thus supports fear and agitation before the Resurrection.

The meaning of the word should nevertheless be distinguished from the cause of the condition.

وَاجِفَةٌ expresses throbbing and agitation; the context supplies their connection with witnessing the events of Resurrection and concern about the outcome.

The verse should not be turned into a medical account of an actual pulse rate or bodily physiology at the Resurrection. It expresses the condition of hearts through an intelligible image without specifying that Day’s biological mechanisms.

وَاجِفَةٌ can consequently be rendered “trembling,” “agitated,” or, more explicatively, “throbbing with anxiety.”

4. The Two Quranic Constructions

أَوْجَفْتُمْ and وَاجِفَةٌ share a root but differ in structure and function.

The Form IV verb أَوْجَفْتُمْ denotes an action a person can perform with a mount: causing it to move swiftly.

In وَاجِفَةٌ, an active participle describes a condition attributed to the heart.

The difference is not only morphological. What the verb or noun concerns is also essential to determining its meaning.

When horses and camels are involved, rapid movement is prominent. When the subject is the human heart at the Resurrection, throbbing and agitation come to the fore.

This does not imply that Form IV throughout Arabic is always restricted to physical movement, or that this root’s active participle always denotes fear.

The Quran presents two actual uses within one word family.

Does Wajf Always Mean Irregular Movement?

Descriptions of this root sometimes use the term اضطراب, which can be misleading for a Persian reader.

In contemporary Persian, اضطراب usually means psychological anxiety; the Arabic اضطرب الشيء may simply express movement, shaking, or lack of stillness.

A camel’s rapid movement need not be irregular or uncontrolled. Likewise, وَاجِفَةٌ in the Resurrection passage does not name a particular illness or physical disorder.

Both branches should therefore remain in the definition: swift movement, and throbbing or unrest.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

W J F Compared with R J F

ر ج ف concerns trembling, shaking, and forceful movement. The Quran uses it for shaking events and, in some instances, for stirring unrest.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:6, تَرْجُفُ and الرَّاجِفَةُ belong to that family.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:8, وَاجِفَةٌ comes from و ج ف.

Their proximity matters: first a shaking event is described, then hearts that are throbbing and agitated.

رَاجِفَة and وَاجِفَة should not be treated as one word or two forms of one root.

In this context, ر ج ف concerns the shaking event, while و ج ف describes the hearts’ agitation. This is the tendency of these particular verses, not an absolute boundary between every use of the two roots.

W J F Compared with W J L

وَجَل concerns fear and inward apprehension. The Quran uses it for a person’s inward state in a frightening situation or when God is remembered.

و ج ف does not necessarily express fear. Al-Ḥashr 59:6 uses it for driving mounts swiftly, without directly expressing fear.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:8, agitation and heart-throbbing connect with the context’s apprehension and dread.

وَجَل primarily names apprehension itself, whereas و ج ف can describe the motion and throbbing accompanying that state.

The two may overlap in describing human fear, but remain distinct.

W J F Compared with W J S

و ج س concerns perceiving or sensing something, especially an unobtrusive sign. The Quranic construction أَوْجَسَ خِيفَةً concerns feeling apprehension inwardly.

By comparison, وَاجِفَةٌ from و ج ف describes throbbing and unrest in the heart.

These roots approach each other in contexts of fear, but differ: و ج س can emphasize inward perception, while و ج ف concerns movement, throbbing, or agitation attributed to something.

Their phonetic resemblance does not prove a shared historical origin or identity across all their meanings.

W J F Compared with W J B

Arabic also uses وَجِيب, from و ج ب, for throbbing or movement.

It resembles وَجِيف in form and meaning, but the roots’ final consonants differ.

The same ode attributed to ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah uses وَجِيب and وَجِيف in describing a camel’s movement and the strain of travel. Their proximity illustrates the existence of different Arabic words for movement and throbbing.

Semantic and phonetic resemblance alone does not make them one root.

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

In Al-Ḥashr 59:6, أَوْجَفْتُمْ is best translated together with خَيْل and رِكَاب:

“You drove neither horses nor riding camels to obtain it.”

This concise rendering preserves the meaning of urging mounts to move swiftly.

“You undertook a military expedition” may explain the situation, but is not the verb’s direct meaning. “You fought” alone is likewise not a precise substitute for أَوْجَفْتُمْ.

For وَاجِفَةٌ in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:8, one may write:

“Hearts on that Day will be trembling.”

To make the image of throbbing more explicit:

“Hearts on that Day will throb with anxiety.”

The first is shorter and suitable for the main translation. The second more explicitly conveys throbbing and unrest in an explanatory rendering.

Other forms also require contextual equivalents: وَجِيف concerning a mount can mean rapid movement or swift riding; وَجَفَ الْقَلْبُ means that the heart began throbbing; إِيجَافُ الدَّابَّةِ means urging a mount to move swiftly.

None of these equivalents should be generalized to every use without considering what the expression concerns and how its sentence is constructed. These are editorial proposals; installing this study does not authorize rewriting existing verse word boxes.

7. Conclusion

و ج ف has two important Arabic uses: rapid movement, especially of horses and camels, and agitated motion or throbbing, especially of the heart.

Poetry attributed to ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah illustrates وَجِيف describing a camel’s swift movement in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Lexicons also record throbbing and agitation, although the historical order in which these branches developed remains uncertain.

The Quran has only these two forms of the root:

أَوْجَفْتُمْ in Al-Ḥashr 59:6, concerning swift riding of horses and camels to acquire property.

وَاجِفَةٌ in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:8, concerning hearts agitated and throbbing on the Day of Resurrection.

The root can be defined as swift movement or agitated motion and throbbing, with two clear applications: the movement of mounts and unrest in hearts.

Not every rapid movement involves fear, nor is every fear described through this root. Each derivative’s precise meaning depends on the kind of movement, its subject or object, and the sentence’s context.

Accordingly, the proposed Forqan renderings use “drove at speed” for أَوْجَفْتُمْ in Al-Ḥashr 59:6 and “trembling” for وَاجِفَةٌ in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:8, while the root study explains their shared connection with movement and throbbing.
