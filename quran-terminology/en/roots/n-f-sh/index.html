The Root N F SH: Spreading, Opening Out, and Grazing at Night

A study of nafsh in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from roaming livestock and opened wool to the judgment of David and Solomon and the image of mountains on the Day of Resurrection

The three-letter root N F SH concerns spreading, opening out, or extending something previously gathered or closely joined. Words from this family describe separating wool fibres so that the wool becomes loose and fluffy, or livestock dispersing across a pasture.

The root has two familiar applications in Arabic. The first is opening and separating wool and cotton, a process used to prepare fibres for spinning. The second is livestock spreading out to graze at night, particularly when roaming through pasture or cultivated land without a shepherd’s supervision.

Other uses describe animals’ fur bristling, birds’ feathers fluffing out, and objects becoming scattered.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an: once in the account of David and Solomon, when a people’s sheep grazed through a cultivated field at night; and once in the description of mountains on the Day of Resurrection, when they are likened to opened wool.

Despite their different subjects, these uses share the idea of spreading or opening out. Their particular meanings must nevertheless be examined separately. Nafsh of sheep is more than their dispersal: in Arabic livestock terminology it also concerns grazing at night. Manfūsh wool, by contrast, has had its fibres opened and has lost its previous compactness.

1. Forms and Semantic Core

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • نَفَشَ: To open out or scatter something; of livestock, to roam and graze at night.
  • نَفْش: Opening and spreading wool or cotton; also, in livestock usage, dispersed night grazing.
  • نَفَش and نُفُوش: Attested forms associated with livestock dispersing and grazing at night.
  • مَنْفُوش: Opened out, carded, or fluffed, especially of wool and cotton.
  • نَفَّشَ and تَنْفِيش: Opening, separating, and spreading wool or cotton fibres.
  • اِنْتَفَشَ: To open out, fluff up, or cease to be compact.
  • تَنَفَّشَ: To fluff up and open out, especially of animals’ fur and feathers.
  • أَنْفَشَ: In livestock usage, to release animals for night grazing.
  • نَافِش and نَافِشَة: An animal that roams and grazes at night.
  • نُفَّاش and نَوَافِش: Descriptive and plural forms associated with animals roaming to graze at night.

Some lexical uses of النَّفَش refer to loose wool, scattered belongings, and even abundant talk and claims.

Only two forms from this range occur in the Qur’an:

نَفَشَتْ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:78, a Form I perfect verb.

الْمَنْفُوشِ in Al-Qāriʿah 101:5, a passive participle of the Form I verb.

The two forms share a root, but one expresses livestock roaming and grazing, while the other describes something that has been opened out.

The semantic core

One lexical analysis identifies the root’s core as “spreading”: dispersing and extending outward.

This meaning appears across the family’s different uses.

When wool undergoes nafsh, its compacted fibres separate and open out. When sheep undergo nafsh, they leave a gathered position and disperse through pasture. When a cat fluffs its fur, the hairs rise from their flat position and appear more spread out.

Spreading and opening out are common features in these uses.

Nafsh does not, however, mean every kind of dispersal. For wool, the opening of the fibres matters; for livestock, roaming to graze at night; and for fur and feathers, fluffing up and standing out.

A general definition should show how these uses relate without imposing the distinctive feature of one on the whole family.

2. N F SH in Early Arabic

Opening wool and cotton

One familiar application in everyday Arab life was the opening of wool and cotton.

In the expression:

نَفَشَ الصُّوفَ

nafasha al-ṣūfa

Translation: “He opened out the wool.”

The wool’s fibres are separated by hand or with an implement so that their compacted, clinging arrangement loosens.

Likewise:

نَفَشَ الْقُطْنَ

nafasha al-quṭna

Translation: “He carded the cotton and opened its fibres.”

This process mattered in preparing fibres for spinning and other uses in cloth and clothing.

After nafsh, wool or cotton usually appears more voluminous and lighter because the fibres separate and more space opens between them.

Apparent volume should not be confused with an actual increase in material. Opening wool does not necessarily add to its weight; it changes the fibres’ structure and arrangement.

This distinction matters in understanding the Qur’anic expression العهن المنفوش.

Fluffing animals’ fur and birds’ feathers

Arabic also uses intafasha and tanaffasha for fur or feathers fluffing up.

For example:

اِنْتَفَشَتِ الْهِرَّةُ

intafashati al-hirratu

Translation: “The cat’s fur bristled.”

A cat may raise and fluff its fur when frightened, angry, or threatened, making its body appear larger.

Likewise, تنفش الطائر describes a bird whose feathers open out and stand up.

In these uses, nafsh and intifāsh concern fur or feathers opening and spreading out.

Fear and anger are not fixed meanings of the root itself. They may cause fur or feathers to fluff up, but an animal may also spread them in other circumstances.

Forms in this family also describe the shape of facial features, such as a broad or spreading nose. Here too, an open or extended appearance is involved.

These physical uses show that nafsh is not confined to dispersal through space: it may also concern the opening of a structure.

Livestock roaming at night

In Arabic livestock terminology, nafsh has a specific use:

نَفَشَتِ الْغَنَمُ

nafashati al-ghanamu

Translation: “The sheep roamed and grazed at night.”

In this usage, sheep leave their enclosure or supervised area and spread out at night to find food.

Lexical explanations often also mention the absence of a shepherd or his being unaware of the animals’ dispersal.

Another expression is:

أَنْفَشَ الرَّاعِي غَنَمَهُ

anfasha al-rāʿī ghanamahu

Translation: “The shepherd released his sheep for night grazing.”

Here the verb is Form IV and concerns releasing or sending livestock out to graze in this way.

Nafsh in this usage is more than aimless dispersal: the animals are seeking food and grazing.

The general equivalent “they dispersed” therefore does not by itself convey the full familiar meaning of nafsh of livestock.

Is nafsh always restricted to night?

Early lexical accounts distinguish nafsh from certain terms for daytime grazing.

Early lexical explanations associate nafsh of livestock with grazing at night.

Hamal, by contrast, sometimes describes unsupervised grazing during the day, although other accounts attest it for livestock left unattended by night or day.

The nighttime qualification thus has strong linguistic support in livestock usage.

This particular use does not mean that all derivatives of N F SH must concern night in every subject. Wool can be opened at any time, and animals’ fur may fluff up by day or night.

Lexical accounts also differ over which livestock may be called nāfish. One narrower definition restricts nafsh to sheep and uses ʿashw for camels grazing at night. Other definitions also attest nafsh of camels.

This difference calls for distinguishing common usage, narrower definitions in some accounts, and an absolute rule for the language.

It creates no difficulty for the Qur’anic verse, which explicitly mentions ghanam.

Arabic poetic examples

A verse attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, reads:

نَفَشَتْ فِيهِ عِشَاءً غَنَمٌ
لِرِعَاءٍ ثَمَّ بَعْدَ الْعَتَمَهْ

nafashat fīhi ʿishāʾan ghanamun / li-riʿāʾin thamma baʿda al-ʿatamah

Translation: “Sheep belonging to shepherds roamed and grazed there in the evening, after darkness had fallen.”

The verse connects nafsh directly with sheep and nighttime.

Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt lived around the emergence of Islam, and the exact date of this verse is unclear. Its wording is also very close to the Qur’anic نفشت فيه غنم القوم. It is useful evidence for the transmitted use of the word, but cannot, without further examination, be treated as an independent and securely dated example preceding the Qur’an.

Another rajaz passage transmitted in early lexical and literary works reads:

ثَارَ عَجَاجٌ مُسْبَطِرٌّ قَسْطَلُهُ
تَنْفُشُ مِنْهُ الْخَيْلُ مَا لَا تَغْزِلُهْ

thāra ʿajājun musbaṭirrun qasṭaluhu / tanfushu minhu al-khaylu mā lā taghziluhu

Translation: “A vast, spreading cloud of dust arose; the horses card out from it what they never spin.”

The poet compares dust dispersed by moving horses to the opening and spreading of fibres.

The example shows that nafsh can extend to the dispersal of material other than wool and cotton.

The wording and attribution vary in transmission. Some accounts attribute the passage to al-ʿAjjāj, others to Abū al-Najm. It should therefore not be presented with a certain attribution or as pre-Islamic poetry.

Together, these examples and lexical accounts establish two clear uses of N F SH: opening fibres and livestock roaming at night. They do not establish which use historically came first.

3. The First Qur’anic Use: Sheep and the Judgment of David and Solomon

وَدَاوُودَ وَسُلَيْمَانَ إِذْ يَحْكُمَانِ فِي الْحَرْثِ إِذْ نَفَشَتْ فِيهِ غَنَمُ الْقَوْمِ وَكُنَّا لِحُكْمِهِمْ شَاهِدِينَ

wa-dāwūda wa-sulaymāna idh yaḥkumāni fī al-ḥarthi idh nafashat fīhi ghanamu al-qawmi wa-kunnā li-ḥukmihim shāhidīna

Translation: “And David and Solomon, when they were judging concerning the cultivated field, when the people’s sheep roamed and grazed in it at night; and We were witnesses to their judgment.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:78

The verse concerns David and Solomon judging a dispute over a cultivated field.

Three principal elements appear: the field, a people’s sheep, and judgment concerning an incident involving them.

The verb نَفَشَتْ describes the sheep’s behaviour.

The precise meaning of نَفَشَتْ

نَفَشَتْ is a feminine singular perfect verb from N F SH.

Its subject is غَنَمُ الْقَوْمِ, the people’s sheep.

Arabic collective animal nouns can take a feminine singular verb. The verb’s feminine form therefore does not prove that all the sheep were female.

Ghanam is a collective term for sheep and goats and includes males and females. The word alone therefore does not determine the sex or precise kind of every animal in this account.

Semantically, nafashat here describes the sheep spreading out to graze at night.

This agrees with the familiar Arabic use of the verb and need not be inferred solely from later exegetical narratives.

The role of فِيهِ

In the expression:

إِذْ نَفَشَتْ فِيهِ غَنَمُ الْقَوْمِ

idh nafashat fīhi ghanamu al-qawmi

The pronoun “it” in fīhi refers to al-ḥarth, the cultivated field.

The Qur’an thus describes more than sheep dispersing through a public pasture: they entered and grazed in the cultivated field at issue in the judgment.

This distinction matters for understanding the dispute.

Sheep moving within an authorized pasture and sheep entering another person’s crop or agricultural property do not have the same potential consequences.

Entering another person’s property should not, however, become a fixed part of the root’s meaning. That relationship follows from the field, the pronoun fīhi, and the setting of adjudication.

Does nafsh mean damaging the field?

Exegetical explanations commonly relate this verse to sheep entering another person’s field or garden at night and damaging it.

This reading fits the judicial setting: without a dispute or loss, the reason for David and Solomon’s judgment would not be clear in this way.

Two matters must nevertheless be distinguished.

First, nafashat itself, which here describes the sheep roaming and grazing at night.

Second, the damage to the field, understood from the dispute and the judgment.

The verse does not use verbs such as أفسدت, أتلفت, or أهلكت. Nafsh should therefore not be defined as destruction or damage in every Arabic use.

Sheep may graze at night somewhere without materially harming another person’s property. In a cultivated field, by contrast, their grazing may damage or consume part of the crop.

Nafsh identifies the animals’ behaviour here; the judicial setting shows that its consequences became a dispute between people.

Does the Qur’an specify the crop or extent of the loss?

The verse names the cultivated area al-ḥarth.

Some exegetical accounts identify it as a field, others as a vineyard.

The Qur’an does not specify the crop, the field’s size or value, or the exact extent of damage.

Understanding nafsh therefore does not require treating one of these accounts as certain.

Nor does the verse explicitly say that the owner deliberately sent the sheep into the field. Lack of supervision has a place in familiar accounts of night grazing, but proving intent to cause harm or determining the owner’s degree of negligence is a separate matter.

The verb alone consequently does not establish full legal liability or a particular measure of compensation.

Connection with Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79: understanding and judgment

فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا سُلَيْمَانَ وَكُلًّا آتَيْنَا حُكْمًا وَعِلْمًا

fa-fahhamnāhā sulaymāna wa-kullan ātaynā ḥukman wa-ʿilman

Translation: “So We gave Solomon understanding of it, and to each We had given judgment and knowledge.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79

The Qur’an describes Solomon being given understanding of the matter, while both David and Solomon possess judgment and knowledge.

The account thus concerns more than sheep: adjudication and a sound understanding of a dispute also matter.

Exegetical narratives supply details of the two judgments. In one well-known account, David awards the sheep to the field’s owner. Solomon proposes an arrangement allowing that owner to benefit from the sheep until the loss is made good, after which each party receives their property back.

This narrative can be studied as part of the history of interpretation, but its details do not appear in the wording of these two verses.

The Qur’an states the judgment, the grazing incident, and Solomon being given understanding; it does not set out every legal detail of compensation.

The expression وَكُلًّا آتَيْنَا حُكْمًا وَعِلْمًا also precludes presenting David as generally lacking judgment or knowledge.

The account indicates the importance of informed judgment and attending to disputes. A complete system of civil liability or compensation for livestock damage should not, however, be derived from nafsh alone.

4. The Second Qur’anic Use: Mountains Like Opened Wool

وَتَكُونُ الْجِبَالُ كَالْعِهْنِ الْمَنْفُوشِ

wa-takūnu al-jibālu ka-al-ʿihni al-manfūshi

Translation: “And the mountains will be like opened, carded wool.”

Al-Qāriʿah 101:5

This is the second and last Qur’anic occurrence of N F SH.

Al-Qāriʿah 101:4 states:

يَوْمَ يَكُونُ النَّاسُ كَالْفَرَاشِ الْمَبْثُوثِ

yawma yakūnu al-nāsu ka-al-farāshi al-mabthūthi

Translation: “The day when people will be like scattered moths.”

Al-Qāriʿah 101:4

The two verses offer different images: people like scattered creatures, and mountains like opened wool.

Their proximity indicates that the familiar order and condition of the world will change on that day.

Mabthūth and manfūsh nevertheless belong to different roots and should not be treated as identical.

The meaning of الْمَنْفُوشِ

الْمَنْفُوشِ is the passive participle of نَفَشَ.

In wool terminology, manfūsh describes wool whose fibres have been separated and opened by hand or with an implement.

Such wool is no longer compact. The fibres spread out, making the wool appear lighter and more voluminous.

In the verse, manfūsh modifies al-ʿihn.

It does not independently mean mountains, dust, or fire. It describes the condition of the wool to which the Qur’an likens the mountains.

What does al-ʿihn mean?

ʿIhn concerns wool in Arabic. Some lexical accounts use it particularly for dyed wool.

Al-ʿihn al-manfūsh thus presents an image of opened, spread-out wool; in some lexical readings, the wool’s different colours also contribute to the image.

Colour belongs to the study of ʿihn and its uses, rather than to N F SH.

Manfūsh describes the fibres’ open and separated condition.

Translating the expression only as “colourful wool” would lose its manfūsh quality. Calling it simply “dust” would also remove the image of wool and opened fibres.

Why are mountains compared to opened wool?

In ordinary experience, mountains appear large, hard, and firm.

Here the Qur’an likens them to wool whose fibres have opened out and which lacks the firmness and compactness of a hard body.

The contrast emphasizes the great change in the mountains’ condition at the Resurrection.

Manfūsh itself does not mean “destroyed.”

Wool that has been opened has not necessarily ceased to exist; its condition and structure have changed.

If the image suggests the mountains’ destruction or collapse, that follows from the wider resurrection passages and the comparison with wool, rather than from nafsh independently.

Connections with other passages about mountains

Al-Maʿārij 70:9 states:

وَتَكُونُ الْجِبَالُ كَالْعِهْنِ

wa-takūnu al-jibālu ka-al-ʿihni

Translation: “And the mountains will be like wool.”

This verse compares the mountains to ʿihn without using the adjective manfūsh.

Al-Qāriʿah 101:5 develops the same image with the description manfūsh.

The difference shows that manfūsh adds a particular quality to the wool image in Al-Qāriʿah.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 also state:

وَبُسَّتِ الْجِبَالُ بَسًّا ۝ فَكَانَتْ هَبَاءً مُّنْبَثًّا

wa-bussati al-jibālu bassan / fa-kānat habāʾan munbaththan

Translation: “And the mountains will be thoroughly crushed, becoming scattered dust.”

These verses describe the mountains with bussat and habāʾan munbaththan.

The descriptions relate to the image of opened wool in that both concern transformation and dispersal.

They do not establish that nafsh itself means crushing mountains or turning them into dust. Those meanings are expressed by other words.

Nor does the wool comparison by itself establish a particular physical mechanism for the mountains’ destruction, such as stone literally becoming fibres, matter turning into gas, or a scientific process that the verse does not name.

The Qur’an presents the mountains’ transformation without explaining its final material condition in scientific detail.

5. N F SH and Related Roots

N F SH and N D F

These roots are very close in uses involving wool and cotton.

Nadf concerns beating or carding wool or cotton, separating the fibres in preparation for spinning.

Nafsh is also used here for opening and spreading the fibres.

Some lexical accounts explain nafsh and nadf through one another.

An absolute division in which nadf always means only striking and nafsh only its result would therefore be misleading. The uses overlap.

In nafsh, opening and spreading the fibres is prominent; in nadf, carding and separating them.

In Al-Qāriʿah 101:5, manfūsh directly describes the wool’s condition, but the verse does not specify an implement or exact method of opening the fibres.

N F SH and N SH R

Nashr has familiar uses involving spreading, opening, and dispersing.

These meanings sometimes approach nafsh.

Nashr has a broader range, however, including unfolding a written text, disseminating news, and, in some forms, raising or restoring to life.

Nafsh, by contrast, is particularly familiar in opening fibres, fluffing fur and feathers, and livestock roaming to graze at night.

Not every use of nashr can therefore be replaced with nafsh.

A shared idea of dispersal also does not establish a common historical origin for the two roots.

Study of N SH R

N F SH and B TH TH

Bathth concerns scattering and spreading in Arabic.

Al-Qāriʿah 101:4, immediately before al-ʿihn al-manfūsh, uses al-mabthūth:

كَالْفَرَاشِ الْمَبْثُوثِ

ka-al-farāshi al-mabthūthi

“Like scattered moths.”

Here bathth concerns individuals dispersed in different directions.

In al-ʿihn al-manfūsh, however, nafsh concerns the opening and separation of wool fibres.

Both images involve dispersal, but its object and kind differ.

The distinction should therefore be retained in translation: “scattered” for mabthūth, and “opened” or “carded” for manfūsh.

Study of B TH TH

N F SH and R ʿ Y

Raʿy concerns grazing and tending grazing animals.

Nafsh of livestock describes a particular kind of grazing accompanied by dispersal at night.

Nafsh of livestock can thus involve grazing, but not all grazing is called nafsh.

A sheep may graze by day under a shepherd’s supervision in a designated pasture. Raʿy is appropriate here, while nafsh in its particular livestock sense need not apply.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:78, nafsh conveys the sheep’s grazing at night as well as their dispersal.

Restricting the translation to “they grazed,” with no indication of timing or manner of movement, would therefore omit part of its familiar meaning.

Study of R ʿ Y

N F SH and H M L

Hamal concerns being left loose or unattended and is sometimes used of livestock grazing without a keeper.

Lexical accounts commonly restrict nafsh to night grazing, whereas hamal is attested in some uses for daytime and in others for either night or day.

Lack of supervision can be shared by the two uses, but the timing and manner of grazing matter in the particular definition of nafsh.

This distinction does not establish an absolute legal rule assigning every nighttime loss to the livestock owner and every daytime loss to the field’s owner. Such a rule does not follow from N F SH itself.

N F SH and N F KH

These roots sound similar, and in some outward uses their results may resemble one another.

For example, wool becomes more voluminous and fluffy after nafsh. Something may instead swell and increase in volume after nafkh, the blowing of air.

Opening wool need not involve blowing air: the fibres can be opened by hand or with tools.

Nafkh also does not mean sheep roaming to graze at night.

Similar apparent volume or puffiness therefore does not make these roots identical or prove a historical relationship.

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For نَفَشَ applied to wool or cotton, “opened out,” “carded,” and “spread the fibres” are suitable equivalents.

For اِنْتَفَشَ describing fur or feathers, “fluffed up” or “opened out” is natural.

In livestock usage, both roaming and grazing at night should be retained.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:78

إِذْ نَفَشَتْ فِيهِ غَنَمُ الْقَوْمِ

idh nafashat fīhi ghanamu al-qawmi

Proposed translation:

“When the people’s sheep roamed and grazed in it at night.”

This conveys the familiar meaning of nafsh in livestock usage.

Writing only “the sheep grazed in it” would omit the dispersal and nighttime features.

Writing only “the sheep dispersed in it” would not make the grazing sufficiently clear.

Adding “they destroyed the entire field” to the direct translation would also be inappropriate, since the phrase does not state the extent of damage.

Al-Qāriʿah 101:5

وَتَكُونُ الْجِبَالُ كَالْعِهْنِ الْمَنْفُوشِ

wa-takūnu al-jibālu ka-al-ʿihni al-manfūshi

Proposed translation:

“And the mountains will be like opened, carded wool.”

The Persian ḥallājī-shudah, “carded,” is a familiar equivalent related to the actual practice of opening wool.

Az-ham-bāz-shudah, “opened out,” also clarifies the fibres’ condition without imposing a particular carding method on the verse.

For a shorter translation, “carded wool” is possible.

Translating manfūsh as “ash,” “dust,” or “destroyed” would replace the word’s meaning with another image or result.

Brief root meaning and meanings in the verses

Brief root meaning: Spreading, opening out, and extending; especially opening wool or cotton fibres, or livestock dispersing to graze at night.

Meaning in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:78: The sheep roamed and grazed in the cultivated field at night; a form of night grazing commonly described in Arabic as occurring without a shepherd’s supervision.

Meaning in Al-Qāriʿah 101:5: Opened or carded; describing wool whose fibres have been separated and spread out, to which the mountains on that day are likened.

These explanations keep the root’s meaning distinct from each event’s possible consequences. Damage to the field follows from the judicial setting, and the mountains’ great transformation from the complete image and related resurrection passages.

7. Conclusion

N F SH concerns spreading, opening out, and extending something in Arabic.

For wool and cotton, nafsh means opening the fibres and loosening their compact arrangement. For animals’ fur and feathers, it describes fluffing and spreading out. In livestock terminology, it has a particular application to animals roaming and grazing at night.

Lexical evidence supports these two important branches, but neither should be treated without evidence as the other’s certain historical origin.

The Qur’an uses the root in two quite different settings.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:78, a people’s sheep roam through a cultivated field at night, leading to David and Solomon’s judgment. Nafsh concerns the animals’ behaviour, and the context shows that its consequences became a subject of dispute.

In Al-Qāriʿah 101:5, mountains are compared to manfūsh wool. Nafsh here concerns wool whose fibres have been opened and spread apart.

The uses share the idea of dispersal and opening out without being identical. Night grazing belongs to the livestock use; carding and opening fibres belong to the wool use.

Nafsh itself does not mean destroying a field or the destruction of mountains. Those outcomes follow from context and accompanying words.

Final definition: At its semantic core, N F SH denotes spreading and opening out. Its fibre branch concerns wool or cotton being opened and carded; its livestock branch concerns animals dispersing to graze at night. In the Qur’an, نَفَشَتْ expresses this roaming night grazing, while الْمَنْفُوشِ describes the opened wool to which the mountains are likened on the Day of Resurrection.
