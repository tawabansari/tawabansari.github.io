Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root Ṣ AYN Q (Ṣ ʿ Q)

Thunderbolt, sudden crushing strike, severe sound and shock, and an event that kills, stuns, or disables normal function

Important Forms from This Root

صَاعِقَة (ṣāʿiqah)

ṣāʿiqah; thunderbolt, heavenly strike, crushing or destructive event.

صَوَاعِق (ṣawāʿiq)

ṣawāʿiq; thunderbolts, deadly sudden strikes.

صَعِقَ (ṣaʿiqa)

ṣaʿiqa; was stunned, fell unconscious, was struck down, died, or was disabled by a severe event, depending on context.

صَعْق (ṣaʿq)

ṣaʿq; stunning strike, disabling shock, state of being overcome.

Method Note

The root Ṣ AYN Q (Ṣ ʿ Q) should not be reduced to lightning in the sky. In the Qur’an, this root is tied to a severe and sudden event that enters from outside and affects a human being, a people, or even the order of created beings. Its effect is not only visual or auditory.

A thunderbolt is seen and heard, but more importantly, it strikes. It can terrify, make the ear react, disable the body, or destroy a people. Three layers are close in this root: severe sound, sudden strike, and destructive or disabling effect.

For this reason, صَاعِقَة (ṣāʿiqah) appears near thunder and lightning, but also in contexts of punishment, destruction, Moses falling unconscious, and the cosmic shock when the Trumpet is blown. Thunder is mainly the field of sound and trembling. Lightning is mainly the visual flash. Ṣāʿiqah is the point of impact and crushing effect.

Central Meaning

The central meaning is sudden severe impact: an impact that is not merely seen or heard, but removes the target’s ability to stand, respond, or continue in its ordinary state.

A ṣāʿiqah tears through the surrounding space, joining sound, light, and blow. It places the living being before a force beyond his power to resist. So this root is not only sound, because thunder also has sound; nor only brightness, because lightning also shines. Ṣāʿiqah is the moment when heavenly intensity or an outside strike reaches the point of effect, seizes the target, knocks it down, or removes it from normal order.

Material Image

The material image is the moment in a storm when, after darkness, thunder, and lightning, a crushing strike descends. The human being is not merely watching. His body reacts. The ear fears the sound, the eye is startled by the flash, and the self trembles before the possibility of death.

The key image is the sudden emptying of strength. Ṣaʿq is an event that gives no time to prepare. The strike comes, and the living being loses the ability to stand or continue in the ordinary state. In some contexts this becomes death; in others it becomes unconsciousness, collapse, or being overcome by the force of the event.

Classical Arabic Note

In classical Arabic, صَاعِقَة (ṣāʿiqah) refers to an event descending from the sky with severe sound, fire, light, or crushing force. Lexical usage also connects it with death, fainting, falling from intense sound, or the descent of punishment. This range is natural, because ancient people did not experience thunderbolt only through sight; they experienced it through hearing, body, fear, and what remained after it struck.

For this reason, صَعِقَ (ṣaʿiqa) can mean to faint, fall, be struck down, or die from the severity of an event. The focus is not the flash itself, but the effect of intensity on a living being.

Qur’anic Usage

Thunderbolts in the Parable of Ṣayyib

In يَجْعَلُونَ أَصَابِعَهُمْ فِي آذَانِهِم مِّنَ الصَّوَاعِقِ حَذَرَ الْمَوْتِ (yajʿalūna aṣābiʿahum fī ādhānihim mina al-ṣawāʿiqi ḥadhara al-mawti), thunderbolts appear within a heavy pouring rain from the sky, where there are darknesses, thunder, and lightning 2:19. But what they fear, and what makes them put their fingers in their ears, is the thunderbolts.

This distinction matters. Thunder creates the auditory field of fear. Lightning brings sudden seizing brightness. But thunderbolt is the threat of impact and death. In this verse, الصَّوَاعِق (al-ṣawāʿiq) are the point of danger: the moment when the storm passes from sign and sound into deadly strike.

Ṣāʿiqah as Punishment of Peoples

Surat Fuṣṣilat says: فَإِنْ أَعْرَضُوا فَقُلْ أَنذَرْتُكُمْ صَاعِقَةً مِّثْلَ صَاعِقَةِ عَادٍ وَثَمُودَ (fa-in aʿraḍū faqul andhartukum ṣāʿiqatan mithla ṣāʿiqati ʿādin wathamūda); if they turn away, say: I have warned you of a thunderbolt like the thunderbolt of ʿĀd and Thamūd 41:13.

Here, ṣāʿiqah is not only a natural phenomenon. It names a destroying event that descended upon peoples. Later in the same surah, it says: فَأَخَذَتْهُمْ صَاعِقَةُ الْعَذَابِ الْهُونِ (fa-akhadhathum ṣāʿiqatu al-ʿadhābi al-hūni); the thunderbolt of humiliating punishment seized them 41:17.

The verb أَخَذَتْهُمْ (akhadhathum) is important. The thunderbolt did not merely pass by them; it seized them in its effect. This ṣāʿiqah is presented as the consequence of their own choice against guidance.

Ṣāʿiqah and the Demand to See The God

In the story of the Children of Israel, the Qur’an says: فَأَخَذَتْكُمُ الصَّاعِقَةُ وَأَنتُمْ تَنظُرُونَ (fa-akhadhatkumu al-ṣāʿiqatu wa-antum tanẓurūna); the thunderbolt seized you while you were looking 2:55. They said they would not believe until they saw The God openly; then they met an event that took from them the very power to see and stand before it.

Another verse says: فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الصَّاعِقَةُ بِظُلْمِهِمْ (fa-akhadhathumu al-ṣāʿiqatu biẓulmihim); the thunderbolt seized them because of their wrongdoing 4:153. Here the cause is clearer: the event is read in relation to wrongdoing and transgression.

Ṣāʿiqah Against the Defiance of Thamūd

Surat al-Dhāriyāt says: فَعَتَوْا عَنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّهِمْ فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الصَّاعِقَةُ وَهُمْ يَنظُرُونَ (faʿataw ʿan amri rabbihim fa-akhadhathumu al-ṣāʿiqatu wahum yanẓurūna); they defiantly turned from the command of their Lord, so the thunderbolt seized them while they were looking 51:44.

The phrase وَهُمْ يَنظُرُونَ (wahum yanẓurūna) shows that the event happened before their eyes: not hidden, not gradual, but visible and crushing. Here the ṣāʿiqah is the end point of defiance.

Ṣaʿiqan and Moses Falling Unconscious

In فَلَمَّا تَجَلَّىٰ رَبُّهُ لِلْجَبَلِ جَعَلَهُ دَكًّا وَخَرَّ مُوسَىٰ صَعِقًا (falammā tajallā rabbuhu lil-jabali jaʿalahu dakkan wakharra mūsā ṣaʿiqan), the form صَعِقًا (ṣaʿiqan) is used of Moses 7:143. This is not an ordinary sky thunderbolt. It is the effect of encountering an overwhelming event.

The Lord manifests to the mountain, the mountain is crushed, and Moses falls from the intensity of the encounter. Moses does not die in this verse, because afterward he returns to awareness and speaks. So صَعِقًا (ṣaʿiqan) here should be understood as unconscious, struck down, or fallen from the force of the encounter, not as dead.

Ṣaʿiqa at the End of the Present World-Order

In وَنُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ فَصَعِقَ مَن فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَن فِي الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا مَن شَاءَ اللَّهُ (wanufikha fī al-ṣūri faṣaʿiqa man fī al-samāwāti waman fī al-arḍi illā man shāʾa allāh), ṣaʿiqa appears on a cosmic scale 39:68. When the Trumpet is blown, whoever is in the heavens and earth undergoes ṣaʿq, except whom The God wills.

Here ṣaʿq is no longer local or tribal. It is a comprehensive transition. The word shows that the order of created beings is removed from its ordinary state before the final command. The context can carry death, unconsciousness, collapse, or total disabling; therefore the translation should not be narrowed too quickly.

Difference from Nearby Words

Thunder is the roaring, shaking sound of the sky, but not necessarily the point of impact or destruction. Lightning is sudden brief brightness, but not by itself a deadly strike. Ṣāʿiqah can accompany thunder and lightning, but its field centers on impact, seizing, destruction, or disabling.

M W T is death, but ṣaʿq is not always death. In the case of Moses, it is not death, because he returns to awareness. In the case of some peoples, ṣāʿiqah is joined to destruction. The context decides whether ṣaʿq should be understood as death, unconsciousness, collapse, or severe disabling.

AKH DH is taking or seizing. In several verses, ṣāʿiqah comes with akhadh: فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الصَّاعِقَةُ (fa-akhadhathumu al-ṣāʿiqah). This pairing shows that the thunderbolt does not merely hit; it seizes a people or person in its effect and removes them from their previous state.

Rajfah is shaking or convulsion. Ṣayḥah is a cry or mighty sound. Both can belong to the field of communal punishment, but ṣāʿiqah is closer to heavenly strike, severe sound, light, and disabling effect.

Translation Method

The translation of ṣāʿiqah must follow the context. In the parable of al-Baqarah, صَوَاعِق (ṣawāʿiq) is close to deadly thunderbolts in the setting of thunder and lightning. In stories of peoples, ṣāʿiqah may be “a destructive strike” or “crushing punishment.” In the case of Moses, صَعِقًا (ṣaʿiqan) should not be translated as “dead”; “unconscious,” “struck down,” or “fallen from the intensity of the encounter” is better.

In al-Zumar 39:68, the translation should remain open because the verse speaks of a comprehensive ṣaʿq. If one says only “they died,” part of the semantic field may be lost. If one says only “they fainted,” the final and overwhelming force of the verse may be weakened. The wording should show removal from ordinary state and total disabling before The God’s command.

Forqan Translation Suggestions

صَاعِقَة (ṣāʿiqah): thunderbolt, heavenly strike, crushing punishment, destructive event, depending on context.

صَوَاعِق (ṣawāʿiq): thunderbolts, deadly thunderbolt-strikes.

صَعِقَ / صَعِقًا (ṣaʿiqa / ṣaʿiqan): underwent ṣaʿq, fell unconscious, was struck down, was removed from ordinary state, or died, depending on context.

مِنَ الصَّوَاعِقِ حَذَرَ الْمَوْتِ (mina al-ṣawāʿiqi ḥadhara al-mawti): because of the thunderbolts, fearing death; or from the deadly thunderbolt-strikes, fearing death.

صَاعِقَةً مِّثْلَ صَاعِقَةِ عَادٍ وَثَمُودَ (ṣāʿiqatan mithla ṣāʿiqati ʿādin wathamūda): a thunderbolt like the thunderbolt of ʿĀd and Thamūd; or a crushing destructive strike like what descended upon ʿĀd and Thamūd.

فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الصَّاعِقَةُ (fa-akhadhathumu al-ṣāʿiqah): the thunderbolt seized them; or the crushing strike overtook them.

وَخَرَّ مُوسَىٰ صَعِقًا (wakharra mūsā ṣaʿiqan): Moses fell from the intensity of it, unconscious and struck down.

فَصَعِقَ مَن فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَن فِي الْأَرْضِ (faṣaʿiqa man fī al-samāwāti waman fī al-arḍ): whoever was in the heavens and earth underwent ṣaʿq; or was removed from ordinary state and disabled, except whom The God willed.

Summary

The root Ṣ AYN Q (Ṣ ʿ Q) begins from the experience of a sudden severe event that gathers sound, strike, light, and crushing effect. In the Qur’an, ṣāʿiqah is not only a natural phenomenon. Sometimes it is part of a storm scene, sometimes the name of punishment upon peoples, sometimes the effect of an overwhelming encounter, and sometimes the cosmic disabling of the present order.

In Forqan’s reading, ṣāʿiqah is the point where warning passes beyond sound and sign and becomes impact. Thunder shakes, lightning flashes, but ṣāʿiqah seizes, strikes, knocks down, disables, or destroys.
