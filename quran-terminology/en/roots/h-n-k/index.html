The Qur’anic Root H N K

Qur’anic and lexical forms

From the palate and bridling animals to control, comprehensive taking, and acquired experience

The Arabic root ح ن ك is first recognized through حَنَك, a part of the human or animal mouth. The same family supplies verbs for restraining an animal with a rope or bridle, taking something completely, and human maturation through experience.

حَنَكَ الدَّابَّةَ means to restrain an animal through its mouth, attaching a rope or implement to the mouth or jaw to draw it in a particular direction. احْتَنَكَ الْجَرَادُ الْأَرْضَ describes locusts consuming a land’s vegetation and leaving nothing behind.

Another usage, حَنَّكَتْهُ التَّجَارِبُ, describes a person tested and matured by life’s experiences. In this field, مُحَنَّك and مُحْتَنِك can denote an experienced, seasoned person.

The meanings have connections but should not be treated as identical. Restraining an animal, completely consuming vegetation, and human maturity through experience are different situations. Their derivational relationship must be established through lexical evidence rather than an invented image.

The root ح ن ك occurs only once in the Qur’an: لَأَحْتَنِكَنَّ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:62. It appears in Iblīs’s declaration that, if granted respite until the Day of Resurrection, he will seek to bring Adam’s descendants, except a few, under his influence.

Qur’anic form: لَأَحْتَنِكَنَّ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:62; a first-person singular Form VIII imperfect, with lām introducing the answer to an oath and the heavy emphatic nūn. Direct attachment to this nūn makes the verb fixed on fatḥah.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole H N K occurrence, a Form VIII verb

Arabic also records حَنَك, أَحْنَاك, حَنَكَ, حَنَّكَ, تَحْنِيك, حِنَاك, احْتَنَكَ, اسْتَحْنَكَ, حُنْكَة, مُحَنَّك, مَحْنُوك, and مُحْتَنِك; these exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic center and early Arabic usages

حَنَك: the palate and inner mouth

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for ح ن ك: the bodily part called حَنَك.

He relates this part to the furthest interior of the human mouth and explains the other usages through it.

Lisān al-ʿArab uses حَنَك for the inner mouth of humans and animals.

Reports differ, however, on whether it specifically denotes the upper palate or lower jaw.

Some lexicographers relate it to the upper part of the mouth, others to the lower part toward the front of the jaws.

الْحَنَكَانِ can also denote both upper and lower parts of the mouth.

The variation shows that early usage did not always follow the precise boundaries of modern anatomical terminology.

“The palate and inner mouth” is therefore suitable as a general introduction, while specialized explanations should preserve the different upper and lower usages.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: ḥanak as the family’s principle

حَنَكُ الْغُرَاب: a crow’s beak

Lexical sources also record حَنَكُ الْغُرَابِ for a crow’s beak.

Ibn Fāris explains that an expression such as أشد سواداً من حنك الغراب refers to the crow’s beak.

He distinguishes حَنَك from حَلَك, since حَلَك in the sense of intense blackness belongs to another lexical family.

This distinction matters.

A crow’s beak may be black, but حنك itself does not mean the color black.

Here the name of a bodily part is used; its color is understood through knowledge of the animal.

The resemblance between حنك and حلك therefore does not make darkness an inherent meaning of ح ن ك.

حَنَكَ الدَّابَّةَ: restraining an animal

Arabic uses حَنَكَ الدَّابَّةَ for attaching a rope or bridle to an animal’s mouth to restrain and guide it.

حِنَاك also denotes a rope or strap used around the mouth and jaw for restraint.

Lisān al-ʿArab reports from Abū ʿUbayd that حنك الدابة means placing a rope in an animal’s mouth.

Ibn Sīdah connects the usage with حنك itself and explains احتنك الدابة in the same field.

For example, attaching a bridle to a horse’s mouth allows someone to change its direction by pulling.

This involves a relationship between the restraining implement, the animal’s mouth, and its direction of movement.

The name حنك itself does not mean obedience. Obedience or movement in the intended direction results from using the implement in a particular situation.

Nor does the usage always mean pulling violently or injuring an animal, although some early methods of restraint or treatment involving pressure and injury to the mouth are also recorded.

This physical usage supplies an image of forced guidance or drawing someone along a desired path.

It is one of the two important bases for explaining لأحتنكن in the Qur’an.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the mouth, ḥināk, and bridling an animal

احْتَنَكَ الْجَرَادُ الْأَرْضَ: consuming and exhausting

In early Arabic, احْتَنَكَ الْجَرَادُ الْأَرْضَ describes locusts eating and consuming the vegetation of a piece of land.

The emphasis is on complete consumption, leaving no vegetation behind.

Ibn Fāris relates the meaning to the mouth: locusts eat vegetation with their mouths and consume it.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains احْتَنَكَ الْجَرَادُ الْأَرْضَ as eating all the land’s vegetation.

In the same field, احْتَنَكَ الشَّيْءَ means taking or eliminating something completely.

For example, احْتَنَكَ فُلَانٌ مَالَ فُلَانٍ can describe someone taking another person’s wealth in its entirety.

There is no literal eating of wealth here. The image of consuming and finishing something extends to taking possession of or exhausting property.

In Majāz al-Qurʾān, Abū ʿUbaydah also records the usage for wealth, knowledge, or speech: taking something completely or pursuing it to its limit.

This does not mean that احتنك always denotes physical destruction.

In the expression concerning wealth, property may pass from one person’s control to another without being materially destroyed.

Comprehensiveness, possession, and mastery are therefore also involved in this branch.

Are restraining and eliminating one meaning?

In the usage concerning an animal, someone controls its direction through its mouth.

In the usage concerning locusts, something is eaten with the mouth until it is gone.

Both images involve the mouth, but their outcomes differ.

One concerns restraining and drawing along; the other, consuming and exhausting.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī presents both in Al-Mufradāt to explain the Qur’anic usage.

Ibn Fāris likewise locates the family’s principle in the mouth and explains complete consumption of vegetation and comprehensive taking through this field.

A derivational explanation does not mean that every occurrence of احتنك simultaneously carries every detail of both images.

One context emphasizes restraint and drawing along; another emphasizes comprehensiveness and elimination.

This distinction is decisive for translating Al-Isrāʾ 17:62.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: the two explanations, restraint and complete consumption

حُنْكَة: maturity and experience

Al-ʿAyn describes a مُحَنَّك as an experienced person whom the hardships of time have tested and matured.

حَنَّكَتْهُ التَّجَارِبُ means that experiences have seasoned a person and made them practiced.

حَنَّكَتْهُ السِّنُّ likewise describes someone matured by age and experience.

In the same field, مُحْتَنِك denotes someone whose intellect, age, or experience has reached maturity.

Ibn Fāris relates this usage to reaching a limit and completing a process.

The explanation does not mean that every elderly person is necessarily wise.

Age can provide an opportunity for experience, but sound understanding and considered judgment do not arise automatically from the passage of time.

Nor are having experience and controlling someone else the same contextual usage.

The Qur’anic لأحتنكن concerns Iblīs’s endeavor to dominate Adam’s descendants, rather than to make them mature and wise.

The meaning “to make experienced” must therefore not be transferred to this verse.

حَنَّكَ الصَّبِيَّ: rubbing something on a child’s palate

Another usage, حَنَّكَ الصَّبِيَّ, denotes rubbing a substance, such as softened dates, onto a child’s palate.

Lexical sources record the practice as تَحْنِيك.

It directly concerns the mouth and differs in meaning from restraining an animal or taking property.

Its presence in a dictionary shows that حنّك can denote an action concerning a child’s palate.

Recording a historical practice in language does not by itself constitute a religious instruction to perform it or establish its medical benefit.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an and should not be made the meaning of لأحتنكن.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: experience and taḥnīk

2. Witnesses from early poetry and language

حِنَاك: a restraint upon a person

A verse by Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī describes a captive:

إِذَا مَا اشْتَكَى ظُلْمَ الْعَشِيرَةِ عَضَّهُ
حِنَاكٌ وَقَرَّاصٌ شَدِيدُ الشَّكَائِمِ

idhā mā ishtakā ẓulma al-ʿashīrati ʿaḍḍahu / ḥinākun wa-qarrāṣun shadīdu al-shakāʾimi

The verse’s general meaning is: “Whenever he complained of his kinsmen’s or group’s injustice, a harsh bond and restraining implement bit into him and caused him pain.”

حِنَاك here denotes a bond associated with the jaw and mouth, used to restrain a person or restrict movement.

The witness shows that restraint through the mouth was an image used not only for animals but also in descriptions of people.

Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī belongs to the Islamic and Umayyad periods; the verse is therefore not a certainly pre-Islamic witness.

Lexical sources, including Lisān al-ʿArab, quote it to explain حناك.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī’s captive witness

Iḥtināk and the loss of property

In explaining Al-Isrāʾ 17:62, Al-Ṭabarī quotes the following rajaz:

نَشْكُو إِلَيْكَ سَنَةً قَدْ أَجْحَفَتْ
جَهْدًا إِلَى جَهْدٍ بِنَا فَأَضْعَفَتْ
وَاحْتَنَكَتْ أَمْوَالَنَا وَجَلَّفَتْ

nashkū ilayka sanatan qad ajḥafat / jahdan ilā jahdin bi-nā fa-aḍʿafat / wa-iḥtanakat amwālanā wa-jallafat

“We complain to you of a year that has dealt harshly with us, adding hardship to hardship and weakening us; it has consumed our property and stripped it away.”

احْتَنَكَتْ أَمْوَالَنَا here denotes the loss or exhaustion of property.

The usage is close to احتنك الجراد الأرض: just as locusts consume vegetation, drought and hardship can exhaust people’s property.

The wording and attribution of this rajaz nevertheless require caution.

The edition of Al-Ṭabarī explains that the passage is attributed to Al-Zafayān al-Saʿdī and transmitted in variant forms. The third line is also absent from one known version of the urjūzah.

The witness is useful for understanding the recorded usage of احتنك, but cannot be presented as certainly pre-Islamic poetry without further examination.

What matters is the presence of comprehensiveness and exhaustion in independent lexical accounts, rather than establishing a particular date through this poem alone.

Al-Ṭabarī and the editorial note: the property rajaz’s wording and attribution

3. لَأَحْتَنِكَنَّ ذُرِّيَّتَهُ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:62

قَالَ أَرَأَيْتَكَ هَٰذَا الَّذِي كَرَّمْتَ عَلَيَّ لَئِنْ أَخَّرْتَنِ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ لَأَحْتَنِكَنَّ ذُرِّيَّتَهُ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

qāla a-raʾaytaka hādhā alladhī karramta ʿalayya la-in akhkhartani ilā yawmi al-qiyāmati la-aḥtanikanna dhurriyyatahu illā qalīlan

Translation: “He said, ‘Do You see this one whom You have honored above me? If You grant me respite until the Day of Resurrection, I will surely draw his descendants after me, except a few.’”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:62

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of ح ن ك.

The speaker is Iblīs. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:61, he disobeys the command to prostrate before Adam and bases his objection on Adam’s creation from clay.

Then, in Al-Isrāʾ 17:62, referring to Adam’s being honored, he declares that he will bring Adam’s descendants under his influence if granted respite.

The morphology of لَأَحْتَنِكَنَّ

أحتنك is a Form VIII imperfect from ح ن ك; without emphasis, its form is أَحْتَنِكُ.

In لَأَحْتَنِكَنَّ, the verb is first-person singular, accompanied by lām introducing the answer to an oath and the heavy emphatic nūn. Direct attachment to the emphatic nūn makes the verb fixed on fatḥah.

The construction belongs to a sentence beginning لَئِنْ أَخَّرْتَنِ.

Al-Mujtabā: oath, condition, and emphatic nūn

Structurally, Iblīs speaks with strong emphasis: if allowed to remain until the Day of Resurrection, he will certainly do what he claims concerning Adam’s descendants.

Strong emphasis does not, however, guarantee fulfillment of the speaker’s claim.

The verse reports Iblīs’s words. His intention and claim must therefore be distinguished from what the Qur’an states about his actual power.

ذُرِّيَّتَهُ is the verb’s object and refers to Adam’s descendants.

إِلَّا قَلِيلًا excludes a group from Iblīs’s sweeping claim.

The exception appears within his own speech; the verse is not providing actual statistics about the numbers led astray and guided.

First reading: bridling and drawing after oneself

One lexical explanation connects لأحتنكن with restraining an animal through its mouth.

In this image, someone attaches a rope or bridle to the animal’s mouth and draws it in a desired direction.

If this is the intended image, Iblīs says he will draw Adam’s descendants after him, except a few, and influence the direction of their conduct.

In Al-Muḥarrar al-Wajīz, Ibn ʿAṭiyyah explains this through drawing along and inclining.

Ibn ʿAṭiyyah’s Al-Muḥarrar al-Wajīz: inclining and drawing along

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī likewise presents it as one of two possible explanations of the Qur’anic verb.

The central image in this reading is directing someone along a desired path by restraint and drawing along.

It does not imply that Iblīs actually possesses a human being’s freedom of choice as someone might control an animal drawn by a rope.

The comparison can express the intensity of his desire to control human conduct without transferring every feature of physical restraint to the relationship between Satan and people.

Second reading: comprehensively taking and exhausting

Another reading connects لأحتنكن with احتنك الجراد الأرض.

In that usage, locusts eat and consume all the vegetation of a piece of land.

Accordingly, احتنك الشيء can mean taking something completely or encompassing it.

If this field is intended, Iblīs declares his intention to bring Adam’s descendants broadly under his influence and divert them from the right way.

Al-Ṭabarī explains this through mastery, encompassing, and eradication.

Ibn Fāris likewise places iḥtināk within mastery and complete removal, connecting it with locusts consuming all the vegetation.

This reading emphasizes the comprehensiveness and intensity of taking control, rather than necessarily drawing along by a bridle.

Here too, removal should not be translated without regard to context as the physical destruction of all Adam’s descendants.

Iblīs speaks of leading people astray, while the following verses present invitation, deception, and influence as his methods.

Eradication in this context can therefore concern destroying a person’s right course and drawing them into error.

Is either reading certain?

Both readings are recorded in authoritative lexical and exegetical sources.

The image of restraint emphasizes directing and drawing along.

The image of consuming all vegetation emphasizes comprehensiveness and extensive domination.

Their contextual outcomes converge on Iblīs’s intention to dominate Adam’s descendants and draw them into error.

That closeness should not conceal the differences between the original images.

The verse itself does not name ropes, bridles, locusts, or vegetation.

These are Arabic witnesses used to understand the verb, rather than objects that should be added to the direct translation.

Translation can therefore convey the readings’ shared contextual intention, while the root article explains their differences.

4. Iblīs’s claim and the actual limits of his influence

لأحتنكن is Iblīs’s claim

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:62, Iblīs declares that he will bring Adam’s descendants, except a few, under his influence.

His words should not be equated with a definitive judgment about everyone’s destiny.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:63, the divine response continues with فَمَنْ تَبِعَكَ مِنْهُمْ: those among them who follow him.

This phrase matters because it places human following in relation to temptation and misguidance.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:64 presents various methods of incitement, mobilization, sharing in wealth and children, and making promises.

It ends by stating that Satan’s promises are nothing but deception.

His endeavor to draw people along his path is consequently connected with invitation, incitement, and deception.

These methods should not be understood as absolute, coercive power over human will.

إِنَّ عِبَادِي لَيْسَ لَكَ عَلَيْهِمْ سُلْطَانٌ

Al-Isrāʾ 17:65 explicitly states that Iblīs has no sulṭān over the servants of The God.

This verse provides an important boundary for understanding لأحتنكن.

Iblīs intends extensive domination and influence, but the Qur’an places limits upon that intention.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, Satan likewise says on the Day of Reckoning that he had no authority over people except that he invited them and they responded.

This clarifies the distinction between invitation and deception and coercion and removal of choice.

Even if احتنك evokes restraining an animal, it does not follow that Satan controls people in the same physical and unavoidable manner.

The comparison clarifies his intention and approach; his actual capabilities must be understood through the verses together.

The exception إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

Iblīs’s speech ends with إِلَّا قَلِيلًا.

It shows that he himself makes an exception within his sweeping claim.

The word قليلا should not be taken to provide a precise numerical ratio between those saved and those led astray.

His speech combines a threat with a claim to domination, seeking to announce his extensive influence.

Other Qur’anic verses, by contrast, set out criteria concerning faith, following, susceptibility to deception, and human responsibility.

قليلا should therefore be understood within that speech, rather than as a fixed number for every human generation.

Connection with Iblīs’s supposition

Sabaʾ 34:20 states that Iblīs found his supposition about a group confirmed, and they followed him except for a group of believers.

Sabaʾ 34:21 then discusses the limitation of his authority and the disclosure of those who believe in the hereafter and those who doubt it.

These verses show that Iblīs’s claim in Al-Isrāʾ 17:62 must be understood alongside human conduct and whether people follow or reject his invitation.

In this context, iḥtināk concerns the intention to dominate and mislead, while human responsibility for choice and conduct remains.

This also accords with Forqan’s published study concerning Satan and ṭāghūt, which distinguishes temptation, invitation, authority, and human choice.

5. Distinctions from related roots

ح ن ك (احْتَنَكَ) and ق و د (قَادَ)

In Arabic, قاد concerns leading an animal or group along a particular path.

In one usage, احتنك concerns restraining an animal through its mouth and drawing it along.

The two can have similar outcomes, but are not identical.

قاد emphasizes leading and directing movement, whereas the physical image of احتنك involves the mouth and a restraining implement.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:62, drawing along a desired path can be understood through that image, but every detail should not be inserted into the translation.

ح ن ك (احْتَنَكَ) and س و ق (سَاقَ)

ساق concerns driving or moving something in a direction.

In the usage of restraining an animal, احتنك can likewise refer to controlling its movement and drawing it along.

سوق, however, is used for moving different groups and things, while the original image of iḥtināk has a particular connection with the palate and mouth.

“Driving” can therefore be one contextual explanation, but is not the complete meaning of ح ن ك.

ح ن ك (احْتَنَكَ) and س ل ط (سُلْطَان)

In the Qur’an, سلطان concerns power, influence, authority, and also proof or evidence.

احتنك in Al-Isrāʾ 17:62 expresses Iblīs’s intention to dominate Adam’s descendants.

The words nevertheless belong to different roots.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:65 explicitly denies Iblīs authority over the servants of The God.

This shows that claiming domination and actually possessing legitimate power are not the same.

A person may wish to dominate others, but that wish alone does not establish authority to do so.

ح ن ك (احْتَنَكَ) and غ و ي (إِغْوَاء)

إغواء concerns leading astray and drawing someone toward a wrong path.

In Iblīs’s speech, احتنك is connected contextually with his intention to mislead and dominate.

The root ح ن ك itself does not always mean leading astray in Arabic.

Restraining an animal or acquiring experience, for example, is not inherently misguidance.

“Leading astray” is therefore an appropriate contextual outcome in the verse, rather than an independent definition of every derivative.

ح ن ك (احْتَنَكَ) and و س و س (وَسْوَسَ)

وسوسة belongs to the quadriliteral root و س و س and concerns concealed or inward suggestion and incitement in Qur’anic usage.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: quadriliteral W S W S

Iḥtināk in Al-Isrāʾ 17:62 depicts Iblīs’s comprehensive intention to bring Adam’s descendants under his influence.

Whispering can be one method of this influence, but احتنك itself does not mean whispering.

Understanding Satan’s methods also requires examining verses about whispering, deceptive promises, and invitation.

Study of W S W S

ح ن ك (احْتَنَكَ) and أ ك ل (أَكَلَ)

أكل means eating.

In احتنك الجراد الأرض, locusts eat the vegetation until none remains.

Eating therefore contributes to one meaning of iḥtināk.

In the use concerning taking property, however, iḥtināk no longer means physical eating.

Nor does Iblīs speak of physically eating people in Al-Isrāʾ 17:62.

The physical image of eating must therefore be distinguished from its extended use for comprehensive taking and mastery.

Study of ʾ K L

ح ن ك (حُنْكَة) and خ ب ر (خِبْرَة)

خبرة concerns experience and knowledge gained through testing.

حنكة likewise denotes maturity and being seasoned in Arabic.

They can be close, but established usage of حنكة particularly emphasizes a person’s maturity through time and varied experience.

احتنك in Iblīs’s speech nevertheless should not be translated as making Adam’s descendants experienced.

That meaning does not fit the verse’s threat and domination.

ح ن ك (حَنَك) and ف و ه (فَم)

فَم means “mouth,” and its plural is أَفْوَاه. In the root classification used by this study and the Quranic Arabic Corpus, the family appears under ف و ه rather than ف م م. The singular and plural’s distinctive morphology should be distinguished from assigning a root merely from the visible form فم.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: mouth and afwāh under F W H

حنك denotes a part of the mouth.

The words are related anatomically but are not complete synonyms.

حنك in the image of restraining an animal highlights a particular part of the mouth, while فم names the mouth generally.

ح ن ك and ح ن ف

In the Qur’an, ح ن ف concerns being a ḥanīf and orientation toward Abraham’s way.

The Qur’anic usage of ح ن ك concerns Iblīs’s intention to bring Adam’s descendants under his influence.

The roots share only their first two consonants.

لأحتنكن cannot therefore be translated as making people ḥanīfs, converting them to Abraham’s way, or morally reforming them.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:62, لَأَحْتَنِكَنَّ ذُرِّيَّتَهُ can be rendered:

“I will surely draw his descendants after me.”

Retaining the verse’s exception:

“If You grant me respite until the Day of Resurrection, I will surely draw his descendants after me, except a few.”

This expresses Iblīs’s intention to influence people and draw them along his chosen path.

“I will certainly gain mastery over his descendants, except a few” is also defensible and closer to the reading of domination.

The rendering must nevertheless be understood as Iblīs’s claim, rather than a definitive report of his absolute power.

“I will destroy all his descendants” is less suitable: in contemporary Persian, destruction commonly suggests physical elimination or killing, whereas the context concerns misguidance and influence.

“I will lead all his descendants astray” conveys the contextual meaning, but partly loses احتنك’s particular image and brings it closer to غ و ي.

For the basic translation, “I will draw them after me” is natural and suitable. The lexical explanation should present both readings: restraint and comprehensive taking or domination.

Outside the Qur’an, حَنَك can be rendered as “palate or inner mouth”; حَنَكَ الدَّابَّةَ as “he restrained the animal through its mouth”; احْتَنَكَ الْجَرَادُ الْأَرْضَ as “the locusts ate all the land’s vegetation”; and احْتَنَكَ مَالَهُ as “he took all his property” or “exhausted it.”

حُنْكَة means “experience and maturity”; مُحَنَّك, “experienced and seasoned”; حِنَاك, “a band or restraining implement associated with the mouth”; and تَحْنِيك, in the usage concerning a child, “rubbing a substance on a child’s palate.”

In every case, the verb’s object and context determine the intended semantic branch.

Conclusion

Brief root meaning: The palate and mouth; restraining and taking or eliminating completely; in another usage, maturity through experience.

Ibn Fāris identifies this bodily part as the family’s principle and explains its other usages through it.

The family includes restraining an animal through its mouth, locusts consuming all vegetation, taking or exhausting something, and human maturity through experience.

The usages can be explained through the mouth, restraint, and reaching a limit, but should not all be reduced to one fixed meaning.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as لَأَحْتَنِكَنَّ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:62.

There are two important lexical readings: restraining and drawing after oneself, and encompassing or comprehensive taking.

Both fit Iblīs’s intention to bring Adam’s descendants under his influence.

Yet these words are Iblīs’s claim. The following verses distinguish invitation from human following and explicitly state that he has no authority over the servants of The God.

Iḥtināk therefore does not establish removal of human choice or Satan’s absolute and unavoidable domination.

إِلَّا قَلِيلًا likewise appears within Iblīs’s speech and does not itself specify the number of people led astray or guided.

The family’s lexical center is the palate and mouth; its Qur’anic usage concerns the endeavor to gain control and draw others along a desired path.

For Forqan’s translation, “I will surely draw his descendants after me, except a few” is suitable and defensible.

The equivalent preserves the image of restraint and drawing along without introducing physical destruction, the certain misguidance of everyone, or removal of their choice without evidence.
