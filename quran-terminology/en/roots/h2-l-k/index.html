1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The three-letter Arabic root H L K concerns passing away, destruction, collapse, or the ending of something’s condition or existence. A person may die, a plant dry up and perish, wealth be spent and lost, someone’s authority collapse, or a society lose its former existence through a devastating event.

Each involves a loss, but what is lost differs. Human death, destroyed crops, the end of political authority, and a ruined city are four different events.

Perishing should therefore not be restricted from the outset to bodily death. Nor should every instance mean the absolute and irreversible annihilation of everything something is.

The root occurs sixty-eight times in sixty-three Qur’anic verses, in verbal, nominal, and adjectival forms. This article’s counts follow the reading of Ḥafṣ. Important forms include:

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sixty-eight occurrences, including muhlik and muhlikī

  • هَلَكَ / يَهْلِكُ: To perish, die, or pass away. Qur’anic: yes; five occurrences, including Al-Nisāʾ 4:176, Al-Anfāl 8:42, and Ghāfir 40:34.
  • أَهْلَكَ / يُهْلِكُ: To cause destruction or make someone or something perish. Qur’anic: yes; fifty-one active and passive Form IV occurrences.
  • هَالِك: Perishable, perishing, or facing destruction. Qur’anic: yes; Yūsuf 12:85 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:88.
  • مُهْلِك: One who causes destruction. Qur’anic: yes; six occurrences including مُهْلِكِي in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:59, with examples in Al-Anʿām 6:131 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:59.
  • مُهْلَك: Destroyed or made to perish. Qur’anic: yes; الْمُهْلَكِينَ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:48.
  • تَهْلُكَة: Destruction or a condition and outcome leading to destruction. Qur’anic: yes; Al-Baqarah 2:195.
  • مَهْلِك: The time, place, or event of destruction, depending on morphology and reading. Qur’anic: yes; Al-Kahf 18:59 and Al-Naml 27:49.
  • هَلَاك: Perishing and destruction. Qur’anic: no, as this independent noun.
  • هُلْك / هَلْك: Destruction and death; early verbal-noun forms. Qur’anic: no, as these independent forms.
  • هَلَكَة: Destruction or a condition connected with perishing. Qur’anic: no.
  • هَلْكَى / هُلَّاك: Those who perish or are overtaken by destruction. Qur’anic: no, in these plural forms.
  • هَلَّكَ / تَهْلِيك: To destroy or bring to destruction. Qur’anic: no.
  • اِسْتَهْلَكَ / اِسْتِهْلَاك: To consume, use up, or expend wealth and resources. Qur’anic: no, in these forms.
  • اِهْتَلَكَ / اِهْتِلَاك: To expose oneself to destruction or pursue something intensely. Qur’anic: no.
  • تَهَالَكَ: To cast oneself down, collapse, or move toward something eagerly and intensely. Qur’anic: no.
  • مَهْلَكَة / مَهْلِكَة: A place or condition of destruction; in some uses, a dangerous route or desert. Qur’anic: no, as these independent forms.
  • هَلُوك: In early usage, a woman described as swaying or bending in her walk; figuratively, a woman described as sexually promiscuous. This historical sense should not be generalized to anyone eager for something. Qur’anic: no.

The distinction between intransitive and transitive verbs matters. هَلَكَ normally concerns someone or something that perishes, whereas أَهْلَكَ in its basic construction identifies causing another to perish.

This morphological distinction does not mean that every instance of perishing lacks an external cause or that every act of destruction results from an individual’s direct and independent intention. The sentence must establish the agent, object, cause, and kind of destruction.

2. The lexical core and physical image

From breaking and falling to destruction

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains H L K through كَسْر and سُقُوط: breaking and falling.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: breaking and falling

This provides an understandable image of perishing.

Something that loses its structure or capacity to endure may collapse and become unable to continue in its previous condition.

A drying plant loses its ability to grow and bear fruit. A collapsed building no longer serves its former function. A person’s bodily life ends at death.

This image does not establish that every Qur’anic instance necessarily involves physical breaking or falling.

Someone may lose authority without any object breaking or falling from a height.

Breaking and falling are therefore useful for understanding part of the root’s lexical background; its broader practical definition concerns the loss and ending of a previously established condition or existence.

Human perishing and death

Arabic uses هَلَكَ فُلَانٌ for someone who has died.

This use is also clear in the Qur’an.

Death, however, must be distinguished from absolute annihilation.

Ordinary speech can express the ending of bodily life without pronouncing on the whole reality of human existence or the nature of the person’s fate after death.

The Qur’an also discusses death, resurrection, reckoning, and return to God in different verses.

When هَلَكَ refers to a person’s death, the verb itself therefore does not establish that every possibility of existence or future life has disappeared forever.

Conversely, this distinction does not mean that bodily death is unreal or merely an imagined change in feeling.

The meaning of death must remain intact in its own setting.

The loss of wealth and the fruits of work

Arabic uses أَهْلَكَ مَالَهُ for someone who destroys, spends, or loses wealth.

Perishing is therefore not restricted to living beings.

Lost wealth does not always mean that coins or physical objects have burned or broken.

A person may spend property and cease to own it.

The wealth then leaves that person’s control or holdings, although it may remain in someone else’s possession.

The destruction of wealth may thus be understood relative to its owner and ownership, rather than the physical annihilation of every part of the property.

This distinction is particularly important in Al-Balad 90:6.

Mahlakah: a dangerous place

Arabic also uses مَهْلَكَة for a dangerous desert or route where crossing may lead to death or destruction.

Here, the name of destruction is transferred to a place of danger.

Not every desert is a mahlakah, nor is every mahlakah necessarily a desert.

Political, military, or natural circumstances may expose someone to destruction.

The place of destruction therefore differs from the event of perishing itself.

In Al-Baqarah 2:195, التَّهْلُكَة names a destructive condition or outcome, rather than a particular desert.

3. Early Arabic usage and poetic witnesses

Hālik in poetry attributed to Ibn Jidhl al-Ṭiʿān

A verse attributed to Ibn Jidhl al-Ṭiʿān reads:

فَأَيْقَنْتُ أَنِّي ثَائِرُ ابْنِ مُكَدَّمٍ
غَدَاةَئِذٍ أَوْ هَالِكٌ فِي الْهَوَالِكِ

fa-ayqantu annī thāʾiru ibni Mukaddamin / ghadātaʾidhin aw hālikun fī al-hawāliki

English translation

I became certain that on that morning I would either avenge Ibn Mukaddam’s blood or be among those who perish.

هَالِكٌ describes someone facing the danger of being killed in a confrontation.

الْهَوَالِكِ likewise refers to people or groups overtaken by destruction.

The witness shows that poetic Arabic could use perishing for death in a dangerous situation.

Hālik itself, however, does not mean a warrior or avenger.

Those meanings come from the verse’s context.

Lexical accounts discuss the plural hawālik and its formation. That discussion concerns a plural pattern and poetic usage rather than changing the core meaning of perishing.

Early sources record the attribution, but the date of composition is not clear enough to present the verse as a certain pre-Qur’anic witness without independent investigation.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Ibn Mukaddam and the plural hawālik

Exhausting strength and resources in al-Ḥuṭayʾah’s poetry

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah has the following verse:

مُسْتَهْلِكِ الْوِرْدِ كَالْأُسْدِيِّ قَدْ جَعَلَتْ
أَيْدِي الْمَطِيِّ بِهِ عَادِيَةً رُغَبَا

mustahliki al-wirdi ka-al-usdiyyi qad jaʿalat / aydī al-maṭiyyi bihi ʿādiyatan rughabā

English translation

A route exhausting those seeking water, even like a woven cloth; the camels’ forelegs have made hollows in it like broad ancient wells.

مُسْتَهْلِكِ الْوِرْدِ describes a route whose length and difficulty exhaust those approaching or seeking water. الأُسْدِيّ denotes woven cloth, while عاديةً رُغَبًا evokes broad ancient wells in the image of the route’s hollows.

The route is described as so long or exhausting that it consumes the traveler’s and mount’s strength.

This use of the istihlāk family therefore approaches exhausting or using up strength; the verse itself contains the active participle مُسْتَهْلِك, rather than the verbal noun istihlāk.

Abū ʿAmr al-Shaybānī, Kitāb al-Jīm: woven cloth and broad wells in al-Ḥuṭayʾah’s verse

This does not by itself imply the certain death of every traveler.

Someone may expend resources or strength intensely and become exhausted without immediately dying.

This use helps show the wider field of depletion and consumption.

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating this particular poem, it should not be labeled a certain pre-Islamic witness.

Ibn al-Sikkīt, Al-Kanz al-Lughawī: jaʿalat and rughub in the poetic witness

A destructive route in al-ʿAjjāj’s rajaz

A rajaz passage transmitted in lexical sources reads:

وَمَهْمَهٍ هَالِكِ مَنْ تَعَرَّجَا
هَائِلَةٍ أَهْوَالُهُ مَنْ أَدْلَجَا

wa-mahmahin hāliki man taʿarrajā / hāʾilatin ahwāluhu man adlajā

English translation

A vast, frightening desert: whoever turns aside within it faces destruction, and its terrors overwhelm the traveler who sets out by night.

Hālik appears in a description of a dangerous route or desert.

Structurally, it can be understood of someone perishing on such a route; some lexical explanations also assign it a use close to مُهْلِك, causing destruction.

This witness shows that in some Arabic constructions the family’s intransitive and transitive forms approach one another.

That overlap should not erase the basic Qur’anic distinction between هَلَكَ and أَهْلَكَ.

Al-ʿAjjāj was an Islamic-period poet, and his witness should be presented within that historical scope.

What the early witnesses establish

These examples concern human death, depleted resources, and the danger of destruction along a route.

The root’s range therefore extends beyond bodily death.

Poetry alone, however, does not justify restricting every Qur’anic use to exhaustion, lost wealth, or death in battle.

Each verse must be understood through its own agent, object, and context.

4. هَلَكَ: death, loss, and human outcomes

Al-Nisāʾ 4:176: a person’s death and inheritance

Qur’anic text

يَسْتَفْتُونَكَ قُلِ اللَّهُ يُفْتِيكُمْ فِي الْكَلَالَةِ إِنِ امْرُؤٌ هَلَكَ لَيْسَ لَهُ وَلَدٌ وَلَهُ أُخْتٌ فَلَهَا نِصْفُ مَا تَرَكَ وَهُوَ يَرِثُهَا إِنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ لَهَا وَلَدٌ فَإِنْ كَانَتَا اثْنَتَيْنِ فَلَهُمَا الثُّلُثَانِ مِمَّا تَرَكَ وَإِنْ كَانُوا إِخْوَةً رِجَالًا وَنِسَاءً فَلِلذَّكَرِ مِثْلُ حَظِّ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ أَنْ تَضِلُّوا وَاللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

yastaftūnaka quli Allāhu yuftīkum fī al-kalālati ini imruʾun halaka laysa lahu waladun wa-lahu ukhtun fa-lahā niṣfu mā taraka wa-huwa yarithuhā in lam yakun lahā waladun fa-in kānatā ithnatayni fa-lahumā al-thuluthāni mimmā taraka wa-in kānū ikhwatan rijālan wa-nisāʾan fa-li-al-dhakari mithlu ḥaẓẓi al-unthayayni yubayyinu Allāhu lakum an taḍillū wa-Allāhu bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīmun

English translation

They ask you for a ruling. Say, “The God clarifies the ruling for you concerning kalālah: if a person dies without a child but with a sister, she receives half of what he leaves; he likewise inherits from her if she has no child. If there are two sisters, they receive two-thirds of what is left. If there are siblings, male and female, the male receives a share equal to that of two females.” The God makes this clear to you so that you do not go astray; The God knows all things.

Examining the usage

هَلَكَ is a past-tense Form I verb.

The person who dies is امْرُؤٌ. In a common grammatical analysis, this noun is the subject of an omitted verb explained by هَلَكَ; the overt verb has a pronoun subject referring to that same person.

The clear meaning of perishing here is death.

The subject is inheritance and the situation of survivors.

Halaka therefore does not mean a destroyed city, depleted wealth, or divine punishment here.

The verse concerns the death of someone whose property and family relationships matter for inheritance.

The verb itself also does not establish that the death occurred because of sin or as punishment.

Death introduces the inheritance issue rather than necessarily condemning the deceased.

This use shows that perishing does not always carry a negative ethical judgment in the Qur’an.

Ghāfir 40:34: Joseph’s death

Qur’anic text

وَلَقَدْ جَاءَكُمْ يُوسُفُ مِنْ قَبْلُ بِالْبَيِّنَاتِ فَمَا زِلْتُمْ فِي شَكٍّ مِمَّا جَاءَكُمْ بِهِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا هَلَكَ قُلْتُمْ لَنْ يَبْعَثَ اللَّهُ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ رَسُولًا كَذَٰلِكَ يُضِلُّ اللَّهُ مَنْ هُوَ مُسْرِفٌ مُرْتَابٌ

wa-la-qad jāʾakum Yūsufu min qablu bi-al-bayyināti fa-mā ziltum fī shakkin mimmā jāʾakum bihi ḥattā idhā halaka qultum lan yabʿatha Allāhu min baʿdihi rasūlan kadhālika yuḍillu Allāhu man huwa musrifun murtābun

English translation

Joseph had indeed previously come to you with clear proofs, yet you remained in doubt about what he brought. When he died, you said, “The God will never raise another messenger after him.” Thus The God leaves astray one who is excessive and doubtful.

Examining the usage

هَلَكَ again refers to a human death.

The person in question is Joseph.

This matters: a claim that Qur’anic perishing always means a death of humiliation or punishment meets a counterexample here.

The Qur’an uses this same verb for Joseph’s death.

Its mere occurrence therefore does not justify calling someone sinful or punished.

The end of Joseph’s life also does not invalidate his message.

The verse discusses people who asserted after his death that no further messenger would come.

The messenger’s death must therefore be distinguished from the people’s subsequent claim.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:29: the loss of authority

Qur’anic text

هَلَكَ عَنِّي سُلْطَانِيَهْ

halaka ʿannī sulṭāniyah

English translation

My authority has passed away from me.

Examining the usage

هَلَكَ concerns سُلْطَانِيَهْ.

The issue is not a person’s death but the loss of that person’s authority or power.

The Qur’an itself therefore demonstrates a non-bodily use of the root.

Someone who possessed power, influence, or the ability to defend a position in worldly life speaks here of losing it.

Sulṭān requires its own S L Ṭ study, since it may concern authority, influence, proof, or the capacity to defend a claim.

What halaka makes clear is the ending and loss of something previously possessed.

The root therefore cannot be translated as “die” in every verse.

Al-Anfāl 8:42: perishing and living in relation to clear evidence

Qur’anic text

إِذْ أَنْتُمْ بِالْعُدْوَةِ الدُّنْيَا وَهُمْ بِالْعُدْوَةِ الْقُصْوَىٰ وَالرَّكْبُ أَسْفَلَ مِنْكُمْ وَلَوْ تَوَاعَدْتُمْ لَاخْتَلَفْتُمْ فِي الْمِيعَادِ وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَقْضِيَ اللَّهُ أَمْرًا كَانَ مَفْعُولًا لِيَهْلِكَ مَنْ هَلَكَ عَنْ بَيِّنَةٍ وَيَحْيَىٰ مَنْ حَيَّ عَنْ بَيِّنَةٍ وَإِنَّ اللَّهَ لَسَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

idh antum bi-al-ʿudwati al-dunyā wa-hum bi-al-ʿudwati al-quṣwā wa-al-rakbu asfala minkum wa-law tawāʿadtum la-ikhtalaftum fī al-mīʿādi wa-lākin li-yaqḍiya Allāhu amran kāna mafʿūlan li-yahlika man halaka ʿan bayyinatin wa-yaḥyā man ḥayya ʿan bayyinatin wa-inna Allāha la-samīʿun ʿalīmun

English translation

When you were on the nearer bank, they on the farther bank, and the caravan below you. Had you arranged a meeting, you would have differed over the appointment. But this occurred so that The God would accomplish a matter to be fulfilled: so that whoever perished would perish upon clear evidence, and whoever lived would live upon clear evidence. The God is indeed Hearing, Knowing.

Examining the usage

The root occurs twice:

لِيَهْلِكَ and هَلَكَ.

They stand opposite يَحْيَىٰ and حَيَّ.

This contrast between perishing and living matters.

The verse belongs to an actual confrontation and battle, so death and survival are relevant contextual possibilities.

At the same time, عَنْ بَيِّنَةٍ is repeated for both outcomes.

This brings clear evidence and the disclosure of truth into the meaning.

One may therefore examine how perishing and living relate to a person’s outcome in the presence of clear proof.

The direct meanings should not be replaced without argument by “disbelieve” and “believe,” nor should the role of clear evidence be removed from the analysis.

The root itself does not mean conscious rejection.

Any such connection established by context is an interpretive conclusion, rather than the lexical definition of halaka.

Yūsuf 12:85: becoming one of those who perish

In Yūsuf 12:85, Joseph’s brothers tell their father that his continuing remembrance of Joseph may bring him to severe physical decline or death.

مِنَ الْهَالِكِينَ refers to those who pass away or are overtaken by death.

Hālikīn itself does not mean grief.

Grief and remembering Joseph are understood from the accompanying statements.

Perishing can therefore be the feared outcome of a physical and psychological situation without itself naming that psychological state.

5. أَهْلَكَ: destroying crops, property, resources, and societies

Al-Baqarah 2:205: destroying cultivation and offspring

Qur’anic text

وَإِذَا تَوَلَّىٰ سَعَىٰ فِي الْأَرْضِ لِيُفْسِدَ فِيهَا وَيُهْلِكَ الْحَرْثَ وَالنَّسْلَ وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الْفَسَادَ

wa-idhā tawallā saʿā fī al-arḍi li-yufsida fīhā wa-yuhlika al-ḥartha wa-al-nasla wa-Allāhu lā yuḥibbu al-fasāda

English translation

When he turns away, he strives through the earth to spread corruption and destroy cultivation and offspring; The God does not love corruption.

Examining the usage

يُهْلِكَ is a present-tense Form IV verb.

Its subject is the person being described, and its objects are الْحَرْثَ and النَّسْلَ.

Destruction here is not one person’s direct death; it concerns two important foundations of life.

Ḥarth concerns cultivation and agricultural production.

Nasl concerns reproduction and the continuity of offspring.

The verse therefore describes conduct damaging the foundations of continued life.

Destruction may accordingly concern damage to biological resources and a society’s capacity to sustain life.

Every contemporary environmental issue, however, should not be presented as the verb’s direct lexical meaning.

Environmental destruction, lost food resources, and harm to generations may be examined as contemporary applications, while the root’s definition remains causing something to perish.

Does destroying cultivation and offspring mean only killing people?

No.

The verse has two independent objects: cultivation and offspring.

Cultivation may be lost through destroyed land, crops, or productive resources. Harm to offspring may likewise disrupt living beings and their continuity.

Without examination, nasl should neither be restricted to one person’s children nor automatically expanded to every being in the universe.

Each object’s range requires its own root and context.

The clear point is that destruction here extends beyond a single person’s death to the foundations of life.

Āl ʿImrān 3:117: crops destroyed by wind

Qur’anic text

مَثَلُ مَا يُنْفِقُونَ فِي هَٰذِهِ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا كَمَثَلِ رِيحٍ فِيهَا صِرٌّ أَصَابَتْ حَرْثَ قَوْمٍ ظَلَمُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ فَأَهْلَكَتْهُ وَمَا ظَلَمَهُمُ اللَّهُ وَلَٰكِنْ أَنْفُسَهُمْ يَظْلِمُونَ

mathalu mā yunfiqūna fī hādhihi al-ḥayāti al-dunyā ka-mathali rīḥin fīhā ṣirrun aṣābat ḥartha qawmin ẓalamū anfusahum fa-ahlakat-hu wa-mā ẓalamahumu Allāhu wa-lākin anfusahum yaẓlimūna

English translation

What they spend in this worldly life is like a wind bearing bitter cold that strikes the crops of people who wronged themselves and destroys them. The God did not wrong them; they wrong themselves.

Examining the usage

فَأَهْلَكَتْهُ denotes the wind’s destruction of the crops.

In the physical image, the agent of destruction is the wind.

Form IV is therefore not restricted to someone consciously deciding to destroy.

A natural agent can also cause something to perish.

This matters for understanding the verb’s range.

The destroyed crop forms part of a comparison. The verse chiefly concerns the outcome of a particular group’s spending, while the image draws on actual crop destruction by wind.

The verb therefore cannot simply mean “spending that earns no reward,” since within the image it refers to destroyed crops.

Conversely, the wind image does not establish that every agricultural loss results from the landowner’s wrongdoing and direct divine punishment.

This is a particular comparison; extending it to every natural event requires independent evidence.

Al-Balad 90:6: expending abundant wealth

Qur’anic text

يَقُولُ أَهْلَكْتُ مَالًا لُبَدًا

yaqūlu ahlaktu mālan lubadan

English translation

He says, “I have expended a mass of wealth.”

Examining the usage

أَهْلَكْتُ is a first-person singular past-tense Form IV verb.

Its object is مَالًا.

Destruction here concerns consuming or losing property.

لُبَدًا emphasizes the wealth’s abundance and accumulation.

The person claims to have disposed of or spent a large amount of wealth.

The verb itself does not determine exactly what the wealth was spent on.

Nor does it alone establish that all large expenditures are objectionable.

The surah’s context examines the person’s statement and claim.

The lexical meaning of consuming or losing wealth must therefore be distinguished from judging the intention or outcome.

Destroying oneself: bringing harm upon oneself

Al-Anʿām 6:26 describes people who deter others and turn away themselves, then states that they destroy only themselves without realizing it.

Al-Tawbah 9:42 likewise contains يُهْلِكُونَ أَنْفُسَهُمْ.

Both connect destruction with the people themselves.

This construction does not necessarily mean directly killing oneself physically.

People may create the conditions of their own loss and destruction through their conduct and decisions.

Every suffering or unfavorable consequence, however, should not be called self-destruction without evidence.

The context must specify the subject and cause of harm.

Lexical conclusion: Form IV can express destroyed crops, lost property, damage to life’s foundations, or causing oneself or others to perish. The object and circumstances identify the kind of destruction.

6. التَّهْلُكَة: the danger of destruction and human responsibility

Al-Baqarah 2:195

Qur’anic text

وَأَنْفِقُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَلَا تُلْقُوا بِأَيْدِيكُمْ إِلَى التَّهْلُكَةِ وَأَحْسِنُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

wa-anfiqū fī sabīli Allāhi wa-lā tulqū bi-aydīkum ilā al-tahlukati wa-aḥsinū inna Allāha yuḥibbu al-muḥsinīna

English translation

Spend in the way of The God, and do not cast yourselves with your own hands toward destruction. Do good; The God indeed loves those who do good.

Examining the usage

التَّهْلُكَةِ is a definite noun in the H L K family.

It denotes destruction or a condition whose outcome is destruction.

The principal verb تُلْقُوا belongs to L Q Y and concerns casting or throwing.

The verse therefore prohibits casting oneself toward destruction rather than merely mentioning destruction’s existence.

بِأَيْدِيكُمْ also appears in the construction, emphasizing the addressees’ own action and responsibility.

This should not become an unsupported claim that every kind of destruction results solely from personal choice.

Other verses describe death, calamity, and groups perishing in different circumstances.

This verse concerns an action through which the addressee becomes exposed to destruction.

The connection with spending

The verse begins with وَأَنْفِقُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ.

The prohibition on casting oneself toward destruction therefore occurs in a context of spending.

Reflection must retain that connection.

One may examine how neglecting financial and social responsibilities, or misusing resources, can expose people and society to destruction.

Without evidence, however, one should not claim that anyone failing to give a particular amount directly exemplifies this prohibition.

The verse itself specifies neither an amount nor an independent taxation system.

Does the verse prohibit every kind of risk?

The wording alone does not establish such an absolute ruling.

There is a difference between considered risk-taking for a proper responsibility and deliberately casting oneself toward destruction.

A person may face danger to save another life, defend someone wronged, or perform a necessary task.

Danger alone does not establish that the action constitutes التَّهْلُكَة.

Conversely, invoking courage or responsibility cannot make an unjustified destructive action insignificant.

Assessment requires examining the issue, necessity, likely consequences, alternatives, and relevant Qur’anic commands.

The prohibition on casting oneself toward destruction is real; the noun itself, however, provides no closed list of every risky behavior.

Does tahlukah mean only bodily death?

Tahlukah concerns destruction but should not be restricted without contextual evidence to instantaneous death.

An action might destroy essential resources, expose a person to gradual destruction, or undermine a collective responsibility.

These wider possibilities must not erase the verse’s direct meaning.

The basic meaning concerns entering a destructive condition.

Identifying precise examples belongs to interpretation and application.

Lexical conclusion: Tahlukah denotes destruction or a destructive condition. Al-Baqarah 2:195 prohibits the addressees from casting themselves toward it; the danger and responsibility must be assessed through the spending context and other related verses.

7. Societies perishing: wrongdoing, clear proof, respite, and justice

What does it mean for a settlement to perish?

The Qur’an repeatedly uses أَهْلَكَ concerning settlements and generations.

The subject extends beyond one individual’s death to the disappearance of a society, generation, or structure of collective life.

A city’s buildings may be damaged, its people killed, or its social order and existence lost.

These situations are not always identical.

When the Qur’an says a settlement perished, context must determine whether this concerns its inhabitants, the physical settlement, or both.

Arabic qaryah can denote a place of habitation and, in some constructions, its people.

The verb ahlaknā alone therefore does not supply every detail of a historical event.

Al-Kahf 18:59: wrongdoing and the appointed time of destruction

Qur’anic text

وَتِلْكَ الْقُرَىٰ أَهْلَكْنَاهُمْ لَمَّا ظَلَمُوا وَجَعَلْنَا لِمَهْلِكِهِمْ مَوْعِدًا

wa-tilka al-qurā ahlaknāhum lammā ẓalamū wa-jaʿalnā li-mahlikihim mawʿidan

English translation

Those settlements We destroyed when they did wrong, and We appointed a time for their destruction.

Examining the usage

The root occurs twice:

أَهْلَكْنَاهُمْ and مَهْلِكِهِمْ.

The first expression attributes the act of destruction to God.

لَمَّا ظَلَمُوا connects its cause or setting with wrongdoing.

The second expression places مَهْلِكِهِمْ alongside مَوْعِدًا.

The verse therefore states an appointed time for destruction.

Destruction here is consequently not presented as unrelated to timing and circumstances.

أهلكناهم also contains a human plural pronoun although القرى precedes it.

This shift highlights the connection between a settlement and its people.

Variant readings of مَهْلِك

Different readings of this form are transmitted in Al-Kahf 18:59 and Al-Naml 27:49.

Ḥafṣ reads مَهْلِك, with fatḥah on the mīm and kasrah on the lām.

Other readings include مَهْلَك and مُهْلَك.

These morphological differences may affect analysis as a verbal noun or a noun of time.

The word may consequently denote destruction itself or the time at which it occurs.

In Al-Kahf 18:59, مَوْعِدًا is an important indication of destruction’s timing.

Al-Naml 27:49 concerns a group planning to claim that they did not witness the destruction of a person’s family; the reading can highlight the event itself or its temporal setting.

A single fixed morphological meaning should therefore not be imposed on both verses without considering the reading and context.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn: readings and analyses of mahlik

Hūd 11:117: a settlement’s destruction and its inhabitants’ reform

Qur’anic text

وَمَا كَانَ رَبُّكَ لِيُهْلِكَ الْقُرَىٰ بِظُلْمٍ وَأَهْلُهَا مُصْلِحُونَ

wa-mā kāna rabbuka li-yuhlika al-qurā bi-ẓulmin wa-ahluhā muṣliḥūna

English translation

Your Lord would not destroy settlements unjustly while their inhabitants were setting things right.

Examining the usage

لِيُهْلِكَ is a Form IV verb.

Here, destroying settlements is connected with بِظُلْمٍ and the condition أَهْلُهَا مُصْلِحُونَ.

The description muṣliḥūn is particularly important.

Muṣliḥ, from Ṣ L Ḥ and Form IV, concerns setting things right.

It goes beyond a mere absence of corruption or a claim of goodness, since it denotes an active role in reform.

The precise scope of بظلم nevertheless remains an interpretive question.

It may be examined in relation to the people’s wrongdoing or to denying unjust destruction by God.

Neither reading should become the only possible meaning without grammatical analysis and consideration of related verses.

The clear point is the relationship between societies perishing, wrongdoing, and reform.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:59: warning before destruction

Qur’anic text

وَمَا كَانَ رَبُّكَ مُهْلِكَ الْقُرَىٰ حَتَّىٰ يَبْعَثَ فِي أُمِّهَا رَسُولًا يَتْلُو عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتِنَا وَمَا كُنَّا مُهْلِكِي الْقُرَىٰ إِلَّا وَأَهْلُهَا ظَالِمُونَ

wa-mā kāna rabbuka muhlika al-qurā ḥattā yabʿatha fī ummihā rasūlan yatlū ʿalayhim āyātinā wa-mā kunnā muhlikī al-qurā illā wa-ahluhā ẓālimūna

English translation

Your Lord would not destroy settlements until He sent a messenger in their central settlement, reciting Our signs to them. We would not destroy settlements unless their inhabitants were wrongdoers.

Examining the usage

The root occurs in two active-participle forms:

مُهْلِكَ and مُهْلِكِي.

Both denote the role of causing destruction.

The verse is not introducing an independent divine name; it discusses how and under what conditions societies are destroyed.

First, a messenger is raised in the settlement’s center to recite the signs.

Destruction is then connected with the inhabitants’ wrongdoing.

The verse thus connects warning, conveying the message, and people’s responsibility.

Merely hearing a messenger’s name or superficially encountering information about him should not automatically be equated in every circumstance with the clear and complete communication described here.

The audience’s awareness and responsibility require the wider network of verses concerning clear proof, messengership, and wrongdoing.

Al-Anʿām 6:131: destruction and unawareness

Qur’anic text

ذَٰلِكَ أَنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ رَبُّكَ مُهْلِكَ الْقُرَىٰ بِظُلْمٍ وَأَهْلُهَا غَافِلُونَ

dhālika an lam yakun rabbuka muhlika al-qurā bi-ẓulmin wa-ahluhā ghāfilūna

English translation

That is because your Lord would not destroy settlements unjustly while their inhabitants were unaware.

Examining the usage

Here too, مُهْلِكَ is attributed to God.

The attribution occurs in a negative construction alongside the inhabitants’ unawareness.

Societal destruction should therefore not be examined apart from awareness and warning.

The verse has a clear connection with Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:59.

Al-Qaṣaṣ mentions raising a messenger and reciting the signs; Al-Anʿām focuses on the people’s unawareness.

غافلون itself, however, does not mean reformers or wrongdoers.

Each description has an independent role and requires examination in its own setting.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:16: the affluent and their transgression

Qur’anic text

وَإِذَا أَرَدْنَا أَنْ نُهْلِكَ قَرْيَةً أَمَرْنَا مُتْرَفِيهَا فَفَسَقُوا فِيهَا فَحَقَّ عَلَيْهَا الْقَوْلُ فَدَمَّرْنَاهَا تَدْمِيرًا

wa-idhā aradnā an nuhlika qaryatan amarnā mutrafīhā fa-fasaqū fīhā fa-ḥaqqa ʿalayhā al-qawlu fa-dammarnāhā tadmīran

English translation

When We intend to destroy a settlement, We command its affluent people; they then transgress within it, the pronouncement becomes due against it, and We destroy it utterly.

Examining the usage

نُهْلِكَ is Form IV.

The continuation also has another verb:

فَدَمَّرْنَاهَا تَدْمِيرًا.

دَمَّرَ, from D M R, emphasizes thorough devastation.

The words share a context of societal destruction without being lexically identical.

مُتْرَفِيهَا and فَفَسَقُوا also have important roles.

Without examination, one cannot conclude that the divine command to the affluent was a command to transgress.

Structurally, أَمَرْنَا expresses commanding, while فَفَسَقُوا expresses their transgressive conduct.

The command’s content is not stated as an explicit object within this sentence.

Supplying that content in a direct translation therefore requires a separate argument.

Contextual analysis should retain the relationship with Al-Anʿām 6:131, Hūd 11:117, and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:59 so that societal destruction remains connected with justice, warning, and responsibility.

Does every Qur’anic instance of a settlement perishing mean punishment?

Many verses connect destroyed settlements with their inhabitants’ wrongdoing, rejection, corruption, or aggression.

Examples include Yūnus 10:13, Al-Kahf 18:59, and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:208 likewise mentions warners before a settlement’s destruction.

These particular uses do not establish that every destroyed city, earthquake, flood, or epidemic in history necessarily directly punishes the local population’s sins.

Judging a particular event that way requires independent evidence.

An ethical pattern in Qur’anic narratives does not authorize attributing guilt or punishment to every natural disaster’s victims.

Conclusion: Qur’anic societal destruction relates to justice, wrongdoing, warning, clear evidence, and human responsibility. H L K itself expresses destruction; the accompanying verses establish its causes and conditions.

8. Hālik, muhlik, and the impermanence of created things

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:88: كُلُّ شَيْءٍ هَالِكٌ إِلَّا وَجْهَهُ

Qur’anic text

وَلَا تَدْعُ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا آخَرَ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ كُلُّ شَيْءٍ هَالِكٌ إِلَّا وَجْهَهُ لَهُ الْحُكْمُ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

wa-lā tadʿu maʿa Allāhi ilāhan ākhara lā ilāha illā huwa kullu shayʾin hālikun illā wajhahu lahu al-ḥukmu wa-ilayhi turjaʿūna

English translation

Do not invoke another deity alongside The God. There is no deity except Him. Everything is perishable except His wajh. Judgment belongs to Him, and to Him you will be returned.

Examining the usage

هَالِكٌ is a Form I active participle.

It describes كُلُّ شَيْءٍ.

The construction highlights things’ impermanence and perishability in contrast with what pertains to God.

Being perishable must nevertheless be distinguished from already being nonexistent.

An active participle may express a quality or outcome without necessarily establishing that the action has already fully occurred at the moment of speaking.

Something may exist while being perishable in nature or eventual outcome.

“Everything is perishable” therefore does not establish that everything has already been destroyed and no longer exists.

Perishing and absolute annihilation

The verse raises a wider issue of permanence and impermanence.

The word hālik alone, however, does not supply every philosophical detail concerning human and cosmic existence.

Bodily death, in particular, should not be equated with the absolute annihilation of every aspect of human existence.

Conversely, the lack of evidence for absolute annihilation does not itself establish a particular form of life after death.

Other verses discuss resurrection, return, reckoning, and life in the Hereafter.

Understanding hālik must be consistent with those verses, but the root itself does not express every event’s precise nature.

The role of إِلَّا وَجْهَهُ

The verse includes the exception إِلَّا وَجْهَهُ.

Wajh belongs to the independent root W J H.

In the Qur’an, it can denote a bodily face, while other uses concern direction, turning toward something, or what is attributed to God.

Its attribution to God and exception from perishing require independent reflection here.

Without evidence, وجهه cannot be turned into a bodily organ resembling a human face.

Nor should the exception be removed, reducing the verse to a statement about all things being destroyed.

Al-Raḥmān 55:26 and Al-Raḥmān 55:27 also discuss created beings passing away and the abiding wajh of the Lord.

Those verses matter for comparison, but F N Y differs from H L K.

Is muhlik one of the Most Beautiful Names?

Some verses attribute مُهْلِك to God, including Al-Anʿām 6:131 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:59.

These uses describe God in relation to destroying settlements.

An action-related description of destroying must therefore be distinguished from an independent divine name.

The presence of مُهْلِك alone does not justify adding الْمُهْلِك to a list of independent divine names.

Nor does the attribution mean that destroying describes the whole reality or standing of God.

The same verses include conditions concerning justice, wrongdoing, and warning.

The action-related description must therefore be understood in context.

مُهْلَكِينَ: those who have been destroyed

الْمُهْلَكِينَ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:48 is a plural Form IV passive participle.

It concerns people who have been destroyed.

Its difference from هَالِكِينَ matters.

Hālik comes from the unaugmented verb and concerns perishing; مُهْلَك, with fatḥah on the lām, is the Form IV passive participle and highlights destruction caused by another agent.

This distinction does not mean that no cause contributes whenever hālik occurs.

The unaugmented verb and Form IV offer different linguistic perspectives, rather than two absolute laws explaining every death.

9. Related roots and structural distinctions

H L K compared with M W T

Study of M W T

Qur’anic M W T concerns death and the end of life.

H L K can likewise refer to human death.

Its range is wider, however, also covering destroyed wealth, crops, power, or a society.

It means death in Al-Nisāʾ 4:176 and Ghāfir 40:34, but the loss of authority in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:29.

The roots are therefore not absolute synonyms.

H L K compared with F N Y

Study of F N Y

Fanāʾ concerns passing away and impermanence.

Perishing can likewise concern something passing away.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:88, Al-Raḥmān 55:26, and Al-Raḥmān 55:27 allow comparison between these fields in describing created things’ impermanence and the abiding character of what pertains to God.

Shared meaning does not establish that every use of the roots is identical.

H L K compared with D M R

Study of D M R

Arabic tadmīr concerns devastation and severe destruction.

Both families occur in Al-Isrāʾ 17:16.

Their proximity shows that perishing may accompany devastation; not every instance, however, necessarily involves a collapsed building or physical demolition of a city.

A person’s death or lost authority can also be described by H L K.

H L K compared with T L F

Arabic T L F concerns the loss and destruction of property or objects.

H L K may occur in the same field.

Its Qur’anic scope is wider, however, also including human death and societies perishing.

“Being lost” or “being destroyed” may fit some economic uses, but not every instance of H L K should be restricted to property loss.

H L K compared with B Y D

Study of B Y D

Arabic B Y D concerns passing away, disappearance, and destruction.

In Al-Kahf 18:35, the garden’s owner imagines that his garden will never perish.

This is close to the field of crops or property being destroyed.

The families remain independent roots; semantic proximity must not erase their distinctions.

H L K compared with KH S R

Study of KH S R

KH S R concerns suffering loss and losing capital or benefit.

Perishing may result from severe loss, but not every loss constitutes complete destruction.

Someone may suffer financially while retaining life and essential resources.

A destroyed society or crop may, by contrast, represent a far more severe situation than reduced profit.

Loss and perishing should therefore not be treated as absolute synonyms.

H L K compared with F S D

Study of F S D

F S D concerns corruption, lost soundness, and disruption of a desirable condition.

H L K concerns passing away and destruction.

Al-Baqarah 2:205 connects corruption with destroying cultivation and offspring.

This connection shows that corruption can lead to destruction, although not every corruption has reached complete destruction.

Nor does every Qur’anic instance of perishing, such as Joseph’s death, express the person’s ethical corruption.

H L K compared with ʿ DH B

Study of ʿ DH B

Punishment concerns suffering and retribution.

Perishing may be a consequence of punishment in some contexts.

They are nevertheless different.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:164 places destroying and severe punishment alongside one another.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:58 likewise has distinct forms, مُهْلِكُوهَا and مُعَذِّبُوهَا.

That distinction must remain intact.

A natural death should not be called divine punishment merely because halaka is used.

H L K compared with H L L

Study of H L L

H L L is an independent root represented by أُهِلَّ and أَهِلَّة in the Qur’an.

It is not the same family as H L K.

The shared first two letters must not place أُهِلَّ in food verses or أهلة in Al-Baqarah 2:189 under the root for perishing.

H L K compared with H L ʿ

Study of H L ʿ

Qur’anic H L ʿ concerns anxious impatience and intense reactions to hardship and prosperity.

It differs from H L K.

Someone may become anxious from fear of destruction, but anxiety and destruction are independent concepts.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

For هَلَكَ, first establish what the verb concerns.

“Passed away” or “died” suits Al-Nisāʾ 4:176 and Ghāfir 40:34.

“Passed away from me” or “was lost” is more natural in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:29, where the subject is authority.

In Al-Anfāl 8:42, “perish” and “live” remain close to the wording and its contrast; reflection should examine their relation to clear evidence.

For أَهْلَكَ, “destroyed,” “brought to an end,” or “caused to perish” may fit according to the agent and object.

“Destroy cultivation and offspring” suits Al-Baqarah 2:205.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:117, “destroyed it” concerns the crop.

For Al-Balad 90:6, “I have expended a mass of wealth” or “I have consumed abundant wealth” may be considered.

“We destroyed” is a clear equivalent of أهلكنا in societal-destruction verses. Context determines whether people were killed, dwellings ruined, or a social structure collapsed.

For التَّهْلُكَة in Al-Baqarah 2:195, “destruction” or “a destructive condition” is appropriate.

“Do not cast yourselves toward destruction” preserves the Arabic structure in direct translation.

For مَهْلِك in Al-Kahf 18:59 and Al-Naml 27:49, “destruction” or “time of destruction” depends on the reading and context.

For هَالِك in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:88, “perishable” or “bound to perish” is preferable to “already destroyed.”

For مُهْلِك when attributed to God, “destroying” or “one who destroys” conveys the description; it should not be made an independent divine name.

“Those who have perished” or “those destroyed” naturally translates مُهْلَكِينَ.

The governing translation principle is to avoid merging death, destroyed wealth, lost crops, collapsed authority, and societal destruction into a single undifferentiated equivalent.

11. Final synthesis

Arabic H L K concerns passing away, ending, and destruction.

This may concern a living being, property, crops, power, or a society.

Qur’anic هَلَكَ sometimes means human death, as in Al-Nisāʾ 4:176 and Ghāfir 40:34; in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:29, however, it expresses lost authority.

Form IV أَهْلَكَ highlights causing destruction, whether the agent is a corrupting person, a destructive wind, or divine power in a particular event.

Al-Baqarah 2:205 connects destroyed cultivation and offspring with corruption. Al-Balad 90:6 concerns expending abundant wealth. In Āl ʿImrān 3:117, wind destroys the crop.

Perishing therefore does not mean only human death.

Verses on societies perishing also discuss wrongdoing, rejection, warning, awareness, and an appointed time.

This connection shows that the Qur’anic fate of societies is not detached from their ethical and social responsibility.

Every natural calamity or collective death in history should nevertheless not be labeled certain divine punishment without evidence.

In Al-Baqarah 2:195, tahlukah is a destructive condition toward which people are forbidden to cast themselves.

Danger alone does not establish that an action constitutes tahlukah. Its subject and the conditions of responsibility require separate examination.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:88, كل شيء هالك إلا وجهه highlights created things’ impermanence in contrast with God’s uniqueness and permanence.

The phrase alone does not explain every stage of death, resurrection, or every being’s fate.

Being hālik is likewise different from already being nonexistent at the same moment.

When attributed to God, مُهْلِك describes His role in destroying societies; the action-related description alone does not authorize constructing the independent divine name المهلك.

Final definition: H L K denotes the loss or ending of something’s existence, condition, or effective power. This may involve death, physical destruction, lost property, or the collapse of a social structure. Form IV highlights causing such destruction.

For each use, establish what passes away, what agent or situation causes it, and whether the verse simply states destruction or also explains its cause, responsibility, and ethical consequences.

These distinctions prevent natural death from being equated with divine punishment, spending wealth with physically annihilating it, or societal destruction with absolutely annihilating every aspect of human existence.

The shared core is passing away and ending; the kind of destruction, its cause, and the fate of what perishes cannot be established from the word alone.
