The Arabic root و ر ث concerns something passing from an earlier holder or generation to another. It may be property, land, standing, family distinction, a book, or a benefit received by a later person or group.

In ordinary human usage, inheritance is chiefly associated with death and property passing to survivors. Early Arabic and Quranic usage, however, show a field wider than a deceased person's estate.

The Quran discusses family inheritance, inheriting land, the transmission of the Book across generations, Solomon inheriting from David, inheriting Paradise, and The God as inheritor. The root occurs thirty-five times in thirty-four verses.

These uses share the idea of something reaching a later person or group, or of successors occupying a position after their predecessors depart. In descriptions of Paradise and of The God, the context must determine how this inheritance imagery applies; a previous human owner should not be presumed.

Becoming an heir does not always mean acquiring a dead person's property. Each verse's context determines the inheritance, its manner of transmission, and the associated rights and responsibilities.

1. The root in early Arabic

Early Arabic uses وَرِثَ for receiving property or family distinction and standing from predecessors.

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm says:

وَرِثْنَا مَجْدَ عَلْقَمَةَ بْنِ سَيْفٍ

warithnā majda ʿalqamata bni sayfin

أَبَاحَ لَنَا حُصُونَ الْمَجْدِ دِينَا

abāḥa lanā ḥuṣūna al-majdi dīnā

“We inherited the glory of ʿAlqamah ibn Sayf; he opened the fortresses of glory to us through conquest.”

In this part of the ode, the poet boasts of his ancestors' standing and achievements.

وَرِثْنَا does not refer here to receiving specified property or land. Its object is مَجْد: greatness, honor, and glory.

The line shows that inheritance in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period was not confined to material property. Later generations could also be said to inherit their ancestors' distinction and reputation.

Another passage in the same ode reads:

وَعَتَّابًا وَكُلْثُومًا جَمِيعًا

wa-ʿattāban wa-kulthūman jamīʿan

بِهِمْ نِلْنَا تُرَاثَ الْأَكْرَمِينَا

bihim nilnā turātha al-akramīnā

“And ʿAttāb and Kulthūm together; through them we attained the heritage of the most noble.”

The word تُرَاث occurs here, a form also found in the Quran.

The poet connects inheritance with family standing and achievements. Here تُرَاث is not restricted to an estate distributed after death; it can encompass the reputation and prestige of earlier generations.

Another witness attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes a family's goodness and distinction:

فَمَا كَانَ مِنْ خَيْرٍ أَتَوْهُ فَإِنَّمَا

fa-mā kāna min khayrin atawhu fa-innamā

تَوَارَثَهُ آبَاءُ آبَائِهِمْ قَبْلُ

tawārathahu ābāʾu ābāʾihim qablu

“Whatever good they have done was already inherited by their forefathers' forefathers.”

Versions of this line differ in their wording, but تَوَارَثَ clearly concerns the transmission of good qualities and traditions across generations.

Again, the subject is not distributing a deceased person's estate, but the continuation of generosity and distinction within a family.

These witnesses belong to poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Attribution to poets of that era offers important historical evidence, although a poet's name alone cannot establish each line's exact date of composition.

What the early witnesses show

These passages reveal two significant uses:

First, something passing from predecessors to successors.

Second, property, standing, distinction, or a recognized family quality continuing into later generations.

Arabic dictionaries record وَرِثَ أَبَاهُ for inheriting from one's father and وَرِثَ مَالَهُ for receiving his property. Expressions such as وَرِثَ مَجْدَهُ also concern inheriting distinction and glory.

The root's historical meaning therefore cannot be confined to receiving property after death.

Nonfinancial uses do not, however, make financial inheritance a late development or something foreign to early Arabic. Both belong to the established semantic field.

A defensible account of the root in early Arabic is something passing from predecessors to successors, whether material property or reputation, standing, and qualities continuing across generations.

2. Forms and semantic core

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

وَرِثَ: inherited, received someone's inheritance, or obtained something after another.

يَرِثُ: inherits or becomes an heir.

وِرْث and إِرْث: inheritance; what reaches survivors from their predecessors.

وِرَاثَة: inheriting or the transmission of an inheritance.

مِيرَاث: what is inherited; in legal usage, property and financial rights transferable after death.

تُرَاث: inheritance or heritage left by earlier generations.

وَارِث: an heir; someone who inherits or comes to possess something after another.

وَرَثَة: a plural of وَارِث; heirs.

وَرِيث: heir or successor; attested in Arabic but not used in the Quran.

أَوْرَثَ: gave something as an inheritance or caused someone to inherit it.

إِيرَاث: giving as an inheritance or making someone an heir.

وَرَّثَ: made someone an heir or assigned them an inheritance share.

تَوَارَثَ: inherited across generations or successively.

مَوْرُوث: something inherited.

All these forms are known in Arabic, but not all occur in the Quran.

Quranic forms

The Quran uses five principal morphological categories from this root.

The basic verb, as in وَرِثَ, يَرِثُ, تَرِثُوا, and نَرِثُ.

The form IV verb, as in أَوْرَثْنَا, يُورِثُ, نُورِثُ, and أُورِثُوا.

The active participle and its plurals, including الْوَارِثِ, الْوَارِثُونَ, الْوَارِثِينَ, and وَرَثَةِ.

The noun مِيرَاث.

And the noun تُرَاث.

In يَرِثُ, the root's initial wāw is omitted, a familiar morphological pattern in certain Arabic verbs beginning with wāw.

Thus يَرِثُ belongs to و ر ث, not to a root consisting only of rā and thā.

مِيرَاث belongs to the same family. Traditional morphological analysis reconstructs مِوْرَاث and explains the wāw's change to yā. This is a morphological explanation, not by itself evidence for a directly attested earlier historical form.

In تُرَاث, initial tā takes the place of the root's wāw. The word is therefore not assigned to an independent root ت ر ث.

Semantic core

The family's principal semantic core is:

Receiving property, standing, a right, a benefit, or a heritage as a successor; often something passes from an earlier holder or generation, though the inheritance imagery need not imply human kinship or a previous owner's death.

This may take place within a family or extend to a people, community, book, or position.

In some Quranic passages, The God makes someone inherit something without kinship or a previous owner's death being the subject.

Death is therefore an important setting for inheritance, but not a fixed condition of every use of the root.

Nor does inheritance itself imply the heir's moral worth. Someone may rightfully inherit property, receive a book yet neglect it, or obtain a predecessor's position and become unjust.

Quranic uses make these distinctions apparent.

3. Financial inheritance and heirs' rights

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 — Property passing after death

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 specifies shares for children and parents in defined circumstances.

It includes وَوَرِثَهُ أَبَوَاهُ: “and his parents inherit from him.”

Here وَرِثَ has a clear legal sense: a deceased person's property passes to the heirs.

This accords with the familiar Arabic meaning of financial inheritance.

The root's meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from rules for distributing an estate.

وَرِثَ alone does not specify the shares of a father, mother, child, or other relative.

Those shares follow from the verse's separate provisions, family relationships, and stated conditions.

For example, the presence or absence of children affects certain shares. The verse also explicitly relates distribution to bequests and debts.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 addresses spouses and certain relatives in other circumstances. It also uses the passive يُورَثُ concerning a person whose estate is at issue.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:176 uses يَرِثُهَا for a brother inheriting from a sister under the conditions it specifies.

These verses connect the root with a particular system of financial rights, but the root itself does not contain that entire system.

Inheritance and bequest

Al-Baqarah 2:180 discusses making a bequest for parents and relatives.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 likewise relate inheritance rights to bequests and debts.

Their juxtaposition shows that inheritance and bequest are distinguishable concepts.

Inheritance concerns a right arising for heirs in specified circumstances.

A bequest concerns instructions or wishes expressed about what a person leaves behind.

An inheritance share mentioned in the Quran is therefore not identical to a bequest.

Conversely, the word “inheritance” alone does not establish that bequests must be eliminated or that an heir cannot benefit from one.

Such questions require examining together the Quran's provisions on bequests, debt, heirs' rights, and preventing harm.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 includes غَيْرَ مُضَارٍّ: “without causing harm,” qualifying the preceding discussion of bequest and debt.

Respect for financial rights thus involves more than transferring property; it also concerns preventing injury and infringement of others' rights.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:19 — Prohibiting inheriting women against their will

Al-Nisāʾ 4:19 provides a significant use of this root.

Addressing believers, the Quran says that inheriting women against their will is not lawful for them.

The object of تَرِثُوا is النِّسَاءَ, the women themselves.

This construction matters: unlike estate-distribution passages, its object is women, not their property.

The Quran prohibits this conduct.

The root's legal usage therefore cannot establish that people may be inherited as property.

Using a word to describe a social practice does not endorse that practice. The Quran can describe an existing custom in familiar language and then prohibit it.

The prohibition on inheriting women against their will must also be distinguished from women's own inheritance rights.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:7 assigns both men and women a share of property left behind.

A woman can therefore hold inheritance rights within the Quran's financial provisions; she is not property for others to inherit against her will.

Al-Baqarah 2:233 — The heir's responsibility

After addressing the rights and responsibilities of mother, father, and child, Al-Baqarah 2:233 says:

وَعَلَى الْوَارِثِ مِثْلُ ذَٰلِكَ

wa-ʿalā al-wārithi mithlu dhālik

Al-Baqarah 2:233

“And a similar obligation rests upon the heir.”

الْوَارِثِ is the active participle of و ر ث.

This phrase alone does not independently identify precisely who the heir is.

Interpretations differ about the heir's identity and the obligations involved. These questions require examining the whole verse, pronoun references, and related provisions.

For understanding the root, the clear point is that the designation “heir” can carry responsibility within a legal provision.

Being an heir is not always solely about receiving property. Particular circumstances can attach rights and obligations to that position.

This does not mean the word “heir” transfers every parental responsibility to someone else in all situations. The verse determines the provision's scope.

Al-Fajr 89:19 — Devouring inheritance greedily

وَتَأْكُلُونَ التُّرَاثَ أَكْلًا لَمًّا

wa-taʾkulūna al-turātha aklan lammā

Al-Fajr 89:19

“And you devour inheritance, gathering it all in greedily.”

التُّرَاثَ belongs to this root.

In modern Persian, تُرَاث commonly denotes cultural, historical, and scholarly heritage. Here, however, the subject is property and its improper treatment.

Al-Fajr 89:17 concerns disregard for the orphan.

Al-Fajr 89:18 concerns failing to encourage one another to feed those in need.

Al-Fajr 89:19 condemns consuming inheritance with the expression أَكْلًا لَمًّا.

Al-Fajr 89:20 speaks of intense love of wealth.

The context thus connects inheritance with greed, property, and others' rights.

أَكْلًا لَمًّا depicts consuming and gathering wealth greedily and indiscriminately.

تُرَاث itself does not mean unlawful property or wrongful appropriation. It denotes inheritance; the condemnation concerns its treatment.

This distinction matters: the Quran does not prohibit inheritance itself, but here condemns acquisitive conduct and disregard for others' rights.

4. The inheritance of the Prophets: Solomon, David, and Zechariah

Al-Naml 27:16 — Solomon inheriting from David

وَوَرِثَ سُلَيْمَانُ دَاوُودَ وَقَالَ يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ عُلِّمْنَا مَنْطِقَ الطَّيْرِ وَأُوتِينَا مِنْ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَهُوَ الْفَضْلُ الْمُبِينُ

wa-waritha sulaymānu dāwūda wa-qāla yā ayyuhā al-nāsu ʿullimnā manṭiqa al-ṭayri wa-ūtīnā min kulli shayʾin inna hādhā la-huwa al-faḍlu al-mubīn

Al-Naml 27:16

“Solomon inherited from David and said: People, we have been taught the speech of birds and given of everything. This is indeed manifest bounty.”

وَوَرِثَ is a perfect verb, with سُلَيْمَانُ as its subject.

دَاوُودَ is the verb's object.

وَرِثَ سُلَيْمَانُ دَاوُودَ means that Solomon inherited from David.

The phrase does not specify the inheritance with a noun such as property, kingship, or prophethood.

Its extent must be investigated through context.

Al-Naml 27:15 speaks of knowledge granted to David and Solomon.

Al-Naml 27:16 mentions Solomon being taught the speech of birds and receiving abundant gifts.

Al-Naml 27:17 describes his forces of jinn, humans, and birds.

This sequence makes Solomon's knowledge, standing, and rule important to the inheritance's context.

Those indications do not prove that he inherited no financial property from David.

Nor does the verb itself prove an automatic transfer of prophethood from father to son: the Quran presents prophethood as a divine gift.

Distinguish what Solomon received after David from what The God independently granted him.

The verse directly says that Solomon inherited from David. Its context emphasizes knowledge, bounty, and standing, but the verb alone cannot conclusively restrict the inheritance to any one of these.

The verse also does not confer divine legitimacy on every historical hereditary monarchy merely because its power was inherited.

Inheriting a position and being morally worthy or justified in its exercise are separate matters.

Maryam 19:6 — Zechariah's request for an heir

يَرِثُنِي وَيَرِثُ مِنْ آلِ يَعْقُوبَ وَاجْعَلْهُ رَبِّ رَضِيًّا

yarithunī wa-yarithu min āli yaʿqūba wa-ijʿalhu rabbi raḍiyyā

Maryam 19:6

“Who will inherit from me and inherit from the family of Jacob; and make him, my Lord, pleasing.”

This verse continues Zechariah's prayer.

Maryam 19:5 describes his concern about those after him and his request for a walī.

Maryam 19:6 then describes that walī as an heir.

يَرِثُنِي means “will inherit from me.”

وَيَرِثُ مِنْ آلِ يَعْقُوبَ means “and inherit from the family of Jacob.”

The two constructions differ significantly.

In the first, the suffix نِي refers directly to Zechariah.

In the second, مِنْ connects the inheritance with Jacob's family.

In the familiar reading, يَرِثُنِي and يَرِثُ are indicative. A jussive reading is also transmitted, relating the verbs differently to the preceding request. This grammatical variation alone does not determine the inheritance's nature.

Was Zechariah requesting an heir to property, or to knowledge and responsibility?

An exclusive answer should not be imposed hastily.

Arabic usage allows both financial and nonfinancial inheritance.

The prayer's context concerns the future, family, succession, and a child who is pleasing.

Maryam 19:12 commands John to hold the Book firmly and speaks of judgment granted to him.

This sequence strengthens the connection with continuing guidance, knowledge, and family responsibility.

It does not by itself prove that financial inheritance is entirely excluded.

Nor does Zechariah's request make prophethood family property passing like wealth from father to son without divine choice and bestowal.

The verse requests an heir with a position after Zechariah and in relation to Jacob's family, whom The God will make pleasing.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:89 — The God as the best of inheritors

وَزَكَرِيَّا إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ رَبِّ لَا تَذَرْنِي فَرْدًا وَأَنْتَ خَيْرُ الْوَارِثِينَ

wa-zakariyyā idh nādā rabbahu rabbi lā tadharnī fardan wa-anta khayru al-wārithīn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:89

“And Zechariah, when he called to his Lord: My Lord, do not leave me alone; You are the best of inheritors.”

This presents Zechariah's request from another angle.

لَا تَذَرْنِي فَرْدًا asks not to be left alone.

وَأَنْتَ خَيْرُ الْوَارِثِينَ addresses The God as the best of inheritors.

Even while requesting a child and heir, Zechariah recognizes that everything ultimately belongs to The God.

Human inheritance and divine inheritance should therefore not be treated as identical or placed on the same level.

After another's death, a person may receive property or a position.

The God does not acquire a previously absent ownership through another's death. His description as inheritor concerns His remaining when created beings' possession ends.

Other uses of the root concerning The God clarify this distinction.

5. Inheriting peoples' land, homes, and property

Al-Aʿrāf 7:128 — Land and making servants inherit

قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِقَوْمِهِ اسْتَعِينُوا بِاللَّهِ وَاصْبِرُوا إِنَّ الْأَرْضَ لِلَّهِ يُورِثُهَا مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ وَالْعَاقِبَةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ

qāla mūsā li-qawmihi istaʿīnū bi-allāhi wa-iṣbirū inna al-arḍa li-allāhi yūrithuhā man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi wa-al-ʿāqibatu li-al-muttaqīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:128

“Moses said to his people: Seek help from The God and remain steadfast. The earth belongs to The God; He gives it as an inheritance to whomever He wills of His servants, and the outcome belongs to the mindful.”

يُورِثُهَا is a form IV verb.

Its morphology must be distinguished from يَرِثُهَا.

يَرِثُ means inheriting.

يُورِثُ means making someone inherit or giving as an inheritance.

Here The God is the verb's subject.

The earth belongs to Him, and He can place His servants in a position to receive it.

Inheritance in the Quran is thus not limited to property passing between relatives.

Peoples and generations can also inherit land and resources after earlier groups.

Yet يُورِثُهَا مَنْ يَشَاءُ does not mean that everyone who historically gained control of land necessarily enjoyed divine moral approval for their conduct and rule.

A transfer occurring and its participants acting rightfully are separate questions.

In this same verse, Moses calls for seeking divine help and steadfastness, and connects the favorable outcome with taqwa.

يُورِثُ alone cannot authorize wrongful seizure of others' land or sanctify every political and military victory.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 — Those oppressed become heirs

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 says that The God made the people who had been oppressed inherit the eastern and western parts of the blessed land.

وَأَوْرَثْنَا is a perfect form IV verb.

The verb has two objects: the people made to inherit and the eastern and western regions they receive.

This occurs in the story of Moses, Pharaoh, and the deliverance of the oppressed people.

Inheritance here accompanies a change in a people's condition: a group formerly under pressure and domination attains a new position after that condition ends.

The root itself does not mean being oppressed, military victory, or justice.

Those features come from the narrative and other expressions in the verse.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:5 likewise describes the divine intention to favor the oppressed, make them leaders, and make them heirs.

وَنَجْعَلَهُمُ الْوَارِثِينَ means “and make them the inheritors.”

Both passages show inheritance relating to a group's changed historical position, beyond property passing within families.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:59 and Al-Dukhān 44:28 — Inheritance after another group departs

After describing the departure of Pharaoh's people and what they left behind, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:59 says that The God gave it as an inheritance to the Children of Israel.

Al-Dukhān 44:28 uses the related expression وَأَوْرَثْنَاهَا قَوْمًا آخَرِينَ: “and We gave it as an inheritance to another people.”

Inheritance can thus pass between peoples without a family relationship being the subject.

Here it concerns property, territory, and resources reaching another group after the former group's circumstances change.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:27 similarly uses وَأَوْرَثَكُمْ concerning another group's lands, homes, and possessions.

The objects inherited in these passages are clear: land, homes, and property.

In each case, the narrative establishes the transfer's manner and circumstances.

These examples should not be converted into a universal authorization to seize others' property.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:100 — Those inheriting land remain accountable

Al-Aʿrāf 7:100 speaks of people inheriting the earth after its previous inhabitants.

It also warns that their sins can bring consequences upon them.

Inheriting land therefore does not confer immunity from wrongdoing or guarantee permanent possession.

A generation may replace its predecessors and use their resources while remaining responsible for its conduct.

Inheritance of land is not itself evidence of the heirs' moral superiority.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:105 — The righteous inherit the earth

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:105 says that The God's righteous servants will inherit the earth.

يَرِثُهَا is the basic verb, with عِبَادِيَ الصَّالِحُونَ as its subject.

It relates to other passages about inheriting land, but its specific feature must be retained.

The heirs are described as صَالِحُونَ, righteous.

This quality is not contained in the root; it independently identifies the heirs intended here.

Nor does الْأَرْض alone determine the promise's precise time, place, and manner of fulfillment.

Readings concerning earthly life and those concerning the righteous's final destiny must be evaluated against context and related verses.

The promise clearly connects inheritance with righteousness, not with a people's name, a sectarian designation, or a claim of superior ancestry.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58 — The God as inheritor after settlements perish

After describing settlements destroyed for their ungrateful self-indulgence, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58 says:

وَكُنَّا نَحْنُ الْوَارِثِينَ

wa-kunnā naḥnu al-wārithīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58

“And We were the inheritors.”

The former inhabitants and householders have perished, while The God remains.

This distinguishes divine inheritance from ordinary human inheritance.

A person receives after another something they may not previously have owned.

The God already owned the earth and its contents before those people departed.

Here divine inheritance concerns His remaining and the end of human life and possession.

6. Inheriting the Book, guidance, and responsibility

Al-Aʿrāf 7:169 — Inheriting the Book without adhering to it

Al-Aʿrāf 7:169 describes a later generation inheriting the Book after its predecessors.

وَرِثُوا الْكِتَابَ means “they inherited the Book.”

The continuation criticizes their conduct: they pursue transient worldly benefits while making claims about their forgiveness.

This is an important witness to the root's broader meaning.

The Book is not divided like land or personal property, but can pass between generations.

Inheritance here means the Book reaching and becoming available to a later generation.

Receiving it does not necessarily mean understanding it correctly, following sincerely, or obeying all its commands.

A group may inherit the Book yet fail its responsibilities toward its contents.

وَرِثُوا here therefore does not mean “they acted upon the Book”; it describes the Book reaching them.

Their conduct is evaluated separately.

Fāṭir 35:32 — Giving the Book as an inheritance

ثُمَّ أَوْرَثْنَا الْكِتَابَ الَّذِينَ اصْطَفَيْنَا مِنْ عِبَادِنَا فَمِنْهُمْ ظَالِمٌ لِنَفْسِهِ وَمِنْهُمْ مُقْتَصِدٌ وَمِنْهُمْ سَابِقٌ بِالْخَيْرَاتِ بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ الْفَضْلُ الْكَبِيرُ

thumma awrathnā al-kitāba alladhīna iṣṭafaynā min ʿibādinā fa-minhum ẓālimun li-nafsihi wa-minhum muqtaṣidun wa-minhum sābiqun bi-al-khayrāti bi-idhni allāhi dhālika huwa al-faḍlu al-kabīr

Fāṭir 35:32

“Then We gave the Book as an inheritance to those We chose from Our servants. Among them are those who wrong themselves, those who take a middle course, and those foremost in good deeds by The God's permission. That is the great bounty.”

أَوْرَثْنَا is form IV.

The God gives the Book as an inheritance to a group of His servants.

The continuation distinguishes their conduct.

Some wrong themselves, some take a middle course, and some lead in good deeds.

Inheriting the Book does not by itself imply identical understanding and conduct among all its heirs.

Nor should أَوْرَثْنَا الْكِتَابَ mean transferring exclusive ownership of truth to a group, denying others the right to hear, understand, or examine it.

A group can be entrusted with the Book and bear responsibility toward it; that differs from owning others' faith and conscience.

Ghāfir 40:53 — The Children of Israel inherit the Book

Ghāfir 40:53 says that The God granted Moses guidance and gave the Book as an inheritance to the Children of Israel.

أَوْرَثْنَا again concerns the Book passing to a people.

Its inheritance can thus occur at the level of a community and across generations.

The verb alone does not make every member of that community equally knowledgeable, faithful, or righteous at every time.

Al-Shūrā 42:14 — Inheriting the Book and experiencing doubt

Al-Shūrā 42:14 uses أُورِثُوا الْكِتَابَ for people who received the Book as an inheritance.

The same verse mentions their doubt.

أُورِثُوا is the passive of form IV: “they were given as an inheritance.” Here the object received is the Book.

Receiving a religious heritage and possessing knowledge and certainty are separate matters.

A person or group may receive a book from predecessors while differing or doubting about understanding and accepting its truth.

Possessing a religious heritage therefore does not establish the correctness of every inherited belief.

Inheriting the Book and receiving revelation

Two concepts should be distinguished here.

In this discussion, revelation concerns receiving a message from The God; it does not exhaust the broader Arabic field of و ح ي.

Inheriting the Book concerns the Book and its contents reaching a later group or generation.

Those inheriting the Book need not be the original recipients of revelation.

This distinction is particularly important in studying religious history.

Receiving the Book in a later generation is not receiving a new revelation. Nor does inheriting it authorize presenting every inherited interpretation or subsequently devised ruling as revelation itself.

7. Inheriting Paradise and The God as inheritor

Al-Muʾminūn 23:10 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:11 — The inheritors of Firdaws

Al-Muʾminūn 23:10 says:

أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْوَارِثُونَ

ulāʾika humu al-wārithūn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:10

“Those are the inheritors.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:11 says:

الَّذِينَ يَرِثُونَ الْفِرْدَوْسَ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

alladhīna yarithūna al-firdawsa hum fīhā khālidūn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:11

“Who inherit Firdaws; they will abide therein.”

الْوَارِثُونَ and يَرِثُونَ belong to the same root.

The first verse names the inheritors.

The second specifies what they inherit: Firdaws.

This differs from family financial inheritance.

The Quran does not say here that Firdaws was a dead person's estate now being distributed according to kinship.

Rather, it describes the final benefit received by people whose qualities the preceding verses identify.

The qualities of successful believers are described in Al-Muʾminūn 23:1, Al-Muʾminūn 23:2, Al-Muʾminūn 23:3, Al-Muʾminūn 23:4, Al-Muʾminūn 23:5, Al-Muʾminūn 23:6, Al-Muʾminūn 23:7, Al-Muʾminūn 23:8, and Al-Muʾminūn 23:9.

Inheriting Firdaws is therefore connected here with faith and conduct, not ancestry.

The verb can express receiving a great and lasting benefit without every condition governing financial estates applying.

Paradise as inheritance elsewhere

Al-Aʿrāf 7:43 tells Paradise's inhabitants that they received it as an inheritance because of their deeds.

Maryam 19:63 describes the Garden given as an inheritance to mindful servants.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:85, Abraham asks to be among the inheritors of the Garden of bliss.

Al-Zukhruf 43:72 likewise tells Paradise's inhabitants that they inherited it because of what they used to do.

These share the idea of receiving a benefit The God has appointed for His servants.

The conditions stated in each passage must still be retained.

Some explicitly mention human action.

Another mentions taqwa.

Abraham's prayer asks to be included among Paradise's inheritors.

Inheriting Paradise is therefore not a right acquired solely through ancestry or membership of a named community, without regard to faith and conduct.

Al-Zumar 39:74 — Inheriting the earth in Paradise's context

In Al-Zumar 39:74, Paradise's inhabitants praise The God for fulfilling His promise and making them inherit the earth, then say they may settle in Paradise wherever they wish.

وَأَوْرَثَنَا الْأَرْضَ occurs in an otherworldly context.

الْأَرْض should therefore not automatically be confined to acquiring a particular territory during earthly life.

Conversely, the root's lexical meaning does not determine the hereafter's physical details or the character of its places.

The context concerns fulfillment of the divine promise and the benefit received by Paradise's inhabitants.

The God as ultimate inheritor

Al-Ḥijr 15:23 says that The God gives life and death and is the inheritor.

Maryam 19:40 says that He inherits the earth and everyone upon it, and all return to Him.

Concerning someone claiming future wealth and children, Maryam 19:80 says that The God will inherit what that person speaks of, while he comes to Him alone.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58 likewise identifies The God as inheritor after describing ruined, defiant settlements.

These passages connect divine inheritance with His remaining after created beings' lives and possession end.

People may own homes, land, and wealth, but their ownership and control are limited and end through death or changed circumstances.

The God owned the heavens and earth before human beings and remains after them.

Calling Him inheritor does not mean He first acquires after their deaths something He did not previously own.

Here divine inheritance expresses His enduring, ultimate ownership and authority in contrast with temporary human possession.

The inheritance of the heavens and earth

Āl ʿImrān 3:180 and Al-Ḥadīd 57:10 use وَلِلَّهِ مِيرَاثُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ.

“To The God belongs the inheritance of the heavens and earth.”

Both connect this expression with financial matters and people's treatment of divine gifts.

Āl ʿImrān 3:180 concerns withholding what The God has granted from His bounty.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:10 concerns spending in His way.

People should therefore not imagine the property and resources they possess to be their absolute, everlasting ownership.

What someone controls today will ultimately pass beyond their possession.

This does not invalidate legitimate human ownership during earthly life. Other Quranic verses recognize financial rights and prohibit wrongfully taking others' property.

Limited human ownership and ultimate divine ownership must thus be distinguished.

Divine descriptions from و ر ث

الْوَارِثُونَ describes The God in Al-Ḥijr 15:23, and خَيْرُ الْوَارِثِينَ occurs in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:89.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58 also uses نَحْنُ الْوَارِثِينَ.

Recognize these forms as they actually occur in the Quran, rather than changing them without reason to match later lists of divine names.

A human heir receives something after another.

In divine usage, The God remains and ultimately owns everything.

The semantic connection is retained without attributing human limitations to Him.

8. Comparison with neighboring roots

و ر ث compared with خ ل ف

خ ل ف concerns being behind, coming after another, and succession.

و ر ث also relates to coming afterward, but emphasizes receiving something from predecessors.

A people living on land after another people can be described in terms of succession.

When the former group's land, homes, or possessions pass to the later group, inheritance is also an appropriate description.

These meanings overlap but are not identical.

An heir succeeds predecessors in some respect, but not everyone who comes after another inherits their property, position, or heritage.

Being a successor does not itself convey ownership of every predecessor's rights and possessions.

و ر ث compared with ت ر ك

ت ر ك concerns leaving and leaving behind.

In inheritance passages, the two families become semantically related.

The deceased “leaves behind” property, and an heir “inherits” it.

They view the event from different perspectives.

ت ر ك emphasizes what a person leaves after them.

و ر ث emphasizes its reaching the heir.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 and other inheritance verses can therefore describe both the property left and its heir within one legal construction.

Leaving property and inheriting it express two sides of a connected event.

و ر ث compared with م ل ك

م ل ك concerns ownership, possession, and, in other uses, rule.

و ر ث concerns how something reaches a subsequent holder.

Someone may obtain property through purchase, gift, work, or inheritance.

All may establish ownership, but only one is inheritance.

Someone can also inherit a book or family distinction without owning it privately in the same sense as a house or land.

وَرِثَ should therefore not be converted to “became the owner” in every verse.

In divine contexts, The God already owns the heavens and earth before created lives end; inheritance does not mean a newly acquired ownership.

و ر ث compared with و ص ي

و ص ي concerns enjoining and entrusting an instruction or command.

In financial matters, a bequest can concern property left after death.

و ر ث concerns receiving an inheritance and the heir's position.

Bequest and inheritance may concern the same property but remain distinct concepts.

The Quran addresses heirs' rights and bequests in related passages; the root's lexical meaning alone cannot establish that either cancels the other. That is a separate interpretive question.

و ر ث compared with ن ص ب

نَصِيب concerns a person's share or portion.

Under Quranic financial provisions, an heir may have a share in property.

“Heir” names the person receiving an inheritance; نَصِيب denotes the assigned portion or benefit.

The word “heir” therefore does not determine a share's size.

Its size follows from the relevant circumstances and provisions.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Distinguish financial inheritance, nonfinancial heritage, making someone inherit, and divine usage. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

In financial provisions, “inherited” is a clear equivalent for وَرِثَ.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:11:

“And his parents inherit from him.”

“Heir” naturally renders وَارِث in human contexts; the established Persian term also needs no newly coined substitute.

In Al-Baqarah 2:233:

“And a similar obligation rests upon the heir.”

“Inheritance” suits مِيرَاث and تُرَاث. Where a deceased person's property is meant, an explanation may also refer to the estate or property left behind.

In Al-Fajr 89:19:

“And you devour inheritance, gathering it all in greedily.”

Render أَوْرَثَ and related forms as “gave as an inheritance,” “made inherit,” or “passed on as an inheritance,” according to the sentence.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:128:

“The earth belongs to The God; He gives it as an inheritance to whomever He wills of His servants.”

In Fāṭir 35:32:

“Then We gave the Book as an inheritance to those We chose from Our servants.”

In Al-Naml 27:16:

“Solomon inherited from David.”

Do not insert a specific object of inheritance without decisive evidence. Knowledge, rule, and other contextual indications belong in the explanation.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:10 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:11:

“Those are the inheritors.”

“Who inherit Firdaws.”

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:89:

“You are the best of inheritors.”

This preserves the Quran's expression. An explanation can distinguish divine permanence and ultimate ownership.

In Maryam 19:40:

“We inherit the earth and everyone upon it.”

Again, preserve the imagery of inheritance, explaining separately The God's remaining after human possession ends.

For non-Quranic forms such as تَوَارَثَ, وِرَاثَة, and مَوْرُوث, natural equivalents are “inherited across generations,” “inheritance,” and “inherited.”

“Cultural heritage” is a familiar modern expression, but should not replace التُّرَاث in Al-Fajr 89:19, where the context concerns wealth and financial rights.

10. Conclusion

و ر ث concerns something reaching successors from predecessors.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period shows inheritance extending beyond material property to family distinction, reputation, and ancestral goodness.

The Quran uses this field in several principal ways.

In financial provisions, an heir receives from property left behind under specified conditions. The relevant verses on inheritance, bequests, and debt determine shares, rights, and responsibility.

Peoples and generations can inherit land and resources after others. This does not automatically establish their moral worth or justify all their conduct.

In inheriting the Book, divine teaching and scripture reach later generations; receiving them does not necessarily mean understanding correctly or acting sincerely.

In Solomon and David's story and Zechariah's prayer, inheritance relates to succession, family, knowledge, and responsibility. The root alone cannot determine that only property, or only prophethood and knowledge, is intended.

In Paradise passages, inheritance concerns a benefit appointed for servants by The God, not a deceased person's estate divided according to kinship.

The God is the ultimate inheritor because human possession ends while He remains. This does not mean His acquiring a new ownership.

The root concerns receiving property, a right, a position, a book, or a benefit as an heir, or causing someone to inherit. It often describes transmission from predecessors to successors; in Paradise imagery, a previous human owner should not be assumed. Divine usage expresses His remaining and ultimate authority when human possession ends.

Distinguish four questions: receiving an inheritance, what is inherited, the conditions and rights of transfer, and the heir's worthiness or responsibility.

The root expresses inheritance itself; context and other Quranic provisions determine its object and the moral value of the heir's conduct.

This distinction prevents Forqan's translation from reducing every use to distributing a deceased person's estate, or conversely replacing clear financial inheritance with a vague idea of succession and responsibility.
