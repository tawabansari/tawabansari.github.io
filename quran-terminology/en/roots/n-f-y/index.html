A study of nafy in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from removing dust and impurities to denying a relationship and expelling people from a land

The three-letter root N F Y denotes removing, driving away, or separating something from a place, group, or relationship with which it is associated. Wind drives dust from the ground, a torrent carries debris away, a person is expelled from home, and a speaker may deny something’s existence or an assertion’s truth. All these uses involve setting aside, removal, or rejection of a relationship.

Familiar forms include nafy, manfī, manfā, intifāʾ, munāfāh, and nufāyah. In Persian, nafy is mainly familiar as denying or rejecting a claim, while tabʿīd denotes forcibly removing someone from their place of residence.

The Arabic root is not confined to verbal denial or punitive banishment. Physical uses include removing pebbles, dispersing dust, eliminating impurities, and driving something out of a group. In speech and thought, the same family rejects a relationship or declares something absent.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Māʾidah 5:33, as yunfaw in the expression:

أَوْ يُنْفَوْا مِنَ الْأَرْضِ

aw yunfaw mina al-arḍi

“Or be expelled from the land.”

The passage concerns people who wage war against The God and His Messenger and strive to spread corruption in the land. Expulsion is one of the stated penalties, but the word itself does not determine its procedure, duration, destination, or every condition.

Understanding this use requires distinguishing the lexical meaning of driving away, the passive form, the area from which someone is expelled, and later legal interpretations concerning banishment, pursuit, and imprisonment.

1. Forms and Semantic Core

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • نَفَى، يَنْفِي: To remove, drive away, expel, or negate.
  • نَفْي: Removal, driving away, or expulsion; in speech, denying or rejecting a claim.
  • نَفِيّ: Something removed or cast aside, such as dispersed dust or water.
  • نَفَيَان: Material dispersed or driven along by wind, a torrent, or moving water.
  • نُفَايَة: Refuse, residue, or inferior material separated from the rest.
  • مَنْفِيّ: Expelled or removed; also something negated.
  • مَنْفًى: A place of exile or a location to which someone is banished.
  • اِنْتَفَى: To move away, disappear, or separate from something; in some uses, to deny one’s relationship or affiliation with another.
  • نَافَى: To be incompatible with something or negate it.
  • تَنَافَى: For two things to be incompatible or mutually conflicting.
  • مُنَافَاة: Incompatibility or mutual exclusion.

Some lexical accounts also attest nifwah and nifyah for discarded things or the act of removal. These forms relate to familiar alternations between certain words ending in wāw and yāʾ, but formal similarity alone should not generate new historical roots without independent examination.

Only the verb yunfaw occurs in the Qur’an. Other forms such as nafy, intafā, nāfā, manfā, and nufāyah do not occur there.

The semantic core: separating and driving away

One lexical analysis explains the root through separating one thing from another and removing it.

Several uses make this meaning clear.

When wind drives dust away, the particles leave their previous location. When impurities are separated from a substance, part of a mixture is set aside. When someone is expelled from a town, they are removed from the environment they occupied.

In denying a claim, a speaker also rejects an attribute, event, or relationship asserted of a subject.

This semantic connection is intelligible, but does not mean that Arabic speakers imagined sweeping dust or ejecting a physical object in every use of N F Y.

Physical and verbal uses each have an established place in the language. Explaining their relationship helps map the root’s meanings without proving the exact history of the semantic transfer.

Generally, N F Y concerns setting aside or removing something from a place, group, or relationship with which it was associated. Whether the removal is physical or takes the form of speech or judgment depends on the particular use.

2. N F Y in Early Arabic

Removing pebbles and dispersing dust

A clear Arabic use of nafā is removing physical things.

In the expression:

نَفَيْتُ الْحَصَى عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ

nafaytu al-ḥaṣā ʿani al-ṭarīqi

Translation: “I removed the pebbles from the road.”

Someone moves material away so that this part of the road becomes clear of pebbles.

Another expression is:

نَفَتِ الرِّيحُ التُّرَابَ

nafati al-rīḥu al-turāba

Translation: “The wind dispersed and drove away the dust.”

Here wind, rather than a person, causes the removal.

Wind moves dust particles away from their previous location. The dust may collect beside a wall, a tree’s roots, or another obstacle.

Arabic dictionaries attest nafiyyu al-rīḥ for dust and particles driven by wind and gathered somewhere.

Nafy therefore does not necessarily mean destruction. Dust carried by wind does not cease to exist; it changes location.

Removing pebbles from a road also does not necessarily make them worthless. Something may be removed for convenience, order, cleaning, or another purpose.

Badness or worthlessness is thus not an invariable part of nafy.

A torrent and what it carries away

Arabic uses nafā for flowing water carrying debris and light objects away:

السَّيْلُ يَنْفِي الْغُثَاءَ

al-saylu yanfī al-ghuthāʾa

Translation: “The torrent carries the debris away.”

Ghuthāʾ concerns light, scattered material moving on water, such as plant remains and other things a torrent carries.

Nafy here expresses moving something and driving it away from its earlier position.

Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, describes a reed removed from its place of growth by flowing water:

سَبِيٌّ مِنْ أَبَاءَتِهِ نَفَاهُ
أَتِيٌّ مَدَّهُ صُحَرٌ وَلُوبُ

sabiyyun min abāʾatihi nafāhu / atiyyun maddahu ṣuḥarun wa-lūbu

Translation: “A reed taken from its bed, driven away by a rushing stream fed by water from different terrains.”

Nafāhu concerns the reed being driven away by the current.

The example illustrates nafy in describing nature. Abū Dhuʾayb experienced the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, however, and this verse’s exact date is uncertain. It is valid evidence for early Arabic, but not necessarily securely pre-Qur’anic.

Lexical accounts also attest nafayānu al-sayl for water overflowing and leaving its collecting place.

Nafayānu al-saḥāb concerns water falling from clouds, and nafiyyu al-māʾ can describe droplets splashing around when water is drawn from a well.

The family thus has a substantial place in describing the movement and separation of physical material, beyond expelling people or denying statements.

Removing impurities

Nufāyah denotes something set aside from a collection, particularly inferior, superfluous, or unwanted material.

When sound material is separated from spoiled material, the rejected portion may be called nufāyah.

Lexical accounts attest the word for inferior food, low-value dirhams, and discarded material.

The act of removal must nevertheless be distinguished from a judgment about the object’s value.

In nufāyah, being discarded may be associated with poor quality. This does not establish that every manfī, or banished person, is inherently corrupt or worthless.

Someone may be expelled unjustly, and something valuable may be discarded by mistake.

The evaluative connotations of nufāyah for inferior material should therefore not be transferred to the entire root family.

A pre-Islamic example: removal in a wool-carding comparison

Aws ibn Ḥajar, a pre-Islamic poet, satirizes a group as people removed from the paths and company of the noble.

A verse attributed to him reads:

يُنْفَوْنَ عَنْ طُرُقِ الْكِرَامِ كَمَا
تَنْفِي الْمَطَارِقُ مَا بَلِيَ الْقَرَدُ

yunfawna ʿan ṭuruqi al-kirāmi kamā / tanfī al-maṭāriqu mā baliya al-qaradu

Translation: “They are driven from the paths of the noble, as carding sticks separate tangled, inferior wool.”

The poet uses a physical process in preparing wool to describe people excluded from a social group.

Maṭāriq denotes implements used to beat wool or cotton and open the fibres. Qarad here concerns tangled, matted wool.

Nafy in the comparison therefore means separating something from a collection and driving it away.

The example matters because it joins the root’s physical and social uses in one image.

The wording varies in early transmission. Some versions have mā yalī al-qarad instead of mā balī al-qarad, and the opening verb also varies. The verse is attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar.

Despite the variants, the relevant meaning is clear: setting something aside and separating it from other components.

Expelling someone from their home

Arabic also uses nafā for driving someone from a land or town:

نَفَيْتُ الرَّجُلَ مِنْ بَلَدِهِ

nafaytu al-rajula min baladihi

Translation: “I expelled the man from his town.”

And:

نَفَيْتُهُ عَنِ الْأَرْضِ

nafaytuhu ʿani al-arḍi

Translation: “I drove him away from that land.”

Someone is removed from the environment they occupy or loses permission to remain there.

This may be a punishment, but nafā alone does not establish whether the removal is just or unjust.

Departure also need not mean destruction. Someone expelled from one town may go to another and continue living.

In this setting, manfā names a place of exile and manfī an expelled person.

This use is closer to the Qur’anic yunfaw mina al-arḍ than the branches concerning dispersed dust or water.

3. Nafy in Speech: Denial, Rejection of a Relationship, and Incompatibility

Arabic also uses N F Y for denying an assertion or rejecting a relationship ascribed to something.

For example:

نَفَى الشَّيْءَ

nafā al-shayʾa

In a verbal use, this can mean “he denied it” or “he rejected its existence or occurrence.”

Nafā nasabahu concerns denying descent or kinship.

Nothing is physically displaced in these uses. What is rejected is a relationship or claim asserted between two things.

In reasoning and grammar, nafy consequently stands opposed to affirmation.

Negation and affirmation

“This event happened” affirms a proposition.

“This event did not happen” negates its occurrence.

In Arabic grammar, particles and words such as lā, mā, lam, lan, and laysa can express negation in different constructions.

They are not, however, etymological derivatives of N F Y. Nafy names the linguistic and logical operation performed through such means.

Not every verse with a negative construction should therefore count as an occurrence of this root.

The Qur’an contains many negative sentences, but N F Y itself occurs only in Al-Māʾidah 5:33.

Does nafy always mean denying the truth?

No.

Someone may negate a false claim and speak truthfully, or deny an evident truth and speak falsely.

Nafy is thus a linguistic act or judgment whose truth must be assessed separately.

This matters when comparing nafy with jaḥd and kitmān.

Al-Naml 27:14 states:

وَجَحَدُوا بِهَا وَاسْتَيْقَنَتْهَا أَنْفُسُهُمْ ظُلْمًا وَعُلُوًّا

wa-jaḥadū bihā wa-istayqanathā anfusuhum ẓulman wa-ʿuluwwan

Translation: “They denied them, although inwardly certain of them, out of injustice and arrogance.”

Jaḥd here accompanies inner certainty and the motives of injustice and arrogance.

Not every verbal negation necessarily has these features.

Kitmān concerns concealing something, whereas nafy may openly reject a claim.

Someone may conceal a truth without explicitly denying it. Someone else may reject a claim because they sincerely consider it false.

Despite overlap in some situations, the roots should therefore remain distinct.

Incompatibility

The Arabic expression:

هَذَا يُنَافِي ذَاكَ

hādhā yunāfī dhāka

Means: “This is incompatible with that.”

Two things are presented as incompatible, or as conflicting in their realization.

This fits removal and exclusion: in a particular relationship, the two exclude one another.

Munāfāh has nevertheless acquired specialized logical and legal uses. These should not be inserted directly into the translation of Qur’anic yunfaw.

Al-Māʾidah 5:33 concerns expelling people, rather than negating a proposition or the incompatibility of two rulings.

4. The Qur’anic Use: Al-Māʾidah 5:33

إِنَّمَا جَزَاءُ الَّذِينَ يُحَارِبُونَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَيَسْعَوْنَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَسَادًا أَن يُقَتَّلُوا أَوْ يُصَلَّبُوا أَوْ تُقَطَّعَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَأَرْجُلُهُم مِّنْ خِلَافٍ أَوْ يُنْفَوْا مِنَ الْأَرْضِ ذَٰلِكَ لَهُمْ خِزْيٌ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَلَهُمْ فِي الْآخِرَةِ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

innamā jazāʾu alladhīna yuḥāribūna Allāha wa-rasūlahu wa-yasʿawna fī al-arḍi fasādan an yuqattalū aw yuṣallabū aw tuqaṭṭaʿa aydīhim wa-arjuluhum min khilāfin aw yunfaw mina al-arḍi dhālika lahum khizyun fī al-dunyā wa-lahum fī al-ākhirati ʿadhābun ʿaẓīmun

Translation: “The recompense of those who wage war against The God and His Messenger and strive to spread corruption in the land is only that they be killed, crucified, have their hands and feet cut off on opposite sides, or be expelled from the land. That is disgrace for them in this world; and in the Hereafter they will have a great punishment.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:33

The verse contains the Qur’an’s sole occurrence of N F Y.

It presents four kinds of penalty for people who wage war against The God and His Messenger and strive to spread corruption in the land.

The fourth is expressed as aw yunfaw mina al-arḍ.

The morphology of يُنْفَوْا

Yunfaw comes from the Form I verb nafā, yanfī.

It is a third-person masculine plural passive imperfect.

Its meaning is “be expelled” or “be driven away.”

The passive leaves the agent performing the expulsion unnamed in the verb.

The plural wāw refers to the group introduced at the verse’s opening. The following alif is the separating alif, rather than another pronoun.

Syntactically, the verb is coordinated with preceding penalties such as yuqattalū and yuṣallabū and is subjunctive under an. Its subjunctive marker is the deletion of the nūn from yunfawna.

It belongs to a sentence expressing possible penalties, rather than reporting a group’s expulsion in the past.

The passive does not authorize every individual or group to carry out the act. It only leaves the agent unnamed; legitimate authority and conditions of implementation are separate legal questions.

The role of مِنَ الْأَرْضِ

In yunfaw mina al-arḍ, min indicates the area from which people are expelled.

Ordinarily, expelling someone from a town or land means removing them from that place.

Al-arḍ in the Qur’an and Arabic can, depending on context, denote the earth broadly, a particular land, or the area under discussion.

Mina al-arḍ should therefore not immediately be taken to require removing someone from the entire planet.

That interpretation has no clear practical meaning in human life and does not fit the familiar usage of expelling someone from a land.

Conversely, the base translation should not replace al-arḍ with the name of a particular town, country, or region without evidence.

The verse specifies neither a destination of exile nor precise geographical boundaries.

“Be expelled from the land” retains the wording. An explanation may clarify that expelling a person concerns removing them from the land or area they occupy.

Who is subject to this penalty?

The verse describes the people concerned through two expressions:

يُحَارِبُونَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ

yuḥāribūna Allāha wa-rasūlahu

“They wage war against The God and His Messenger.”

And:

وَيَسْعَوْنَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَسَادًا

wa-yasʿawna fī al-arḍi fasādan

“And strive to spread corruption in the land.”

These descriptions matter in identifying the ruling’s subject.

Early exegetical and legal uses associate the passage with warfare, brigandage, insecurity, attacks on life and property, and other forms of violent corruption in the land.

Nafy itself defines none of those offences. It names a penalty; the conduct subject to it is identified by other parts of the verse.

Mere intellectual disagreement, criticism of a person, or opposition to a religious claim cannot, without clear evidence, be identified with the warfare and corruption addressed here.

The distinction matters: extending such classifications without criteria can subject people to penalties for differences of belief or speech, although the verse describes conduct with particular features.

A full definition of warfare and corruption requires independent study of Ḥ R B, F S D, and related passages. The N F Y article establishes only that the penalty concerns removing and expelling people.

5. Expulsion from the Land: Banishment, Pursuit, or Imprisonment?

A major interpretive question in Al-Māʾidah 5:33 is how expulsion from the land is implemented.

Exegetical and legal history records several readings.

Expulsion from a town or land

The first understands nafy as expulsion from one’s residence or the land in which the offence occurred.

The person is sent from one town or region to another place.

This fits the direct, familiar meaning of nafā: nafaytuhu min baladihi describes expelling someone from their town.

The verse nevertheless specifies neither the duration, distance, nor conditions of residence in the new location.

These details cannot therefore be determined from N F Y alone.

Pursuit and removal from an area

Another reading in early commentaries understands nafy as pursuing a fugitive so that they cannot settle in an area, remaining under pursuit until captured or driven out of that land.

Nafy here concerns driving someone away and preventing settlement.

This is related to the root’s idea of removal.

Pursuit and capture are not, however, the direct lexical meaning of nafā. They are a proposed means of removing someone or applying pressure to them.

If someone has departed by fleeing, voluntary flight must also be distinguished from nafy as a penalty.

The differences show that a lexical definition alone cannot determine implementation of the ruling.

Imprisonment

A third reading interprets expulsion from the land as imprisonment.

Its reasoning is that a prisoner loses free presence in society and movement through the land, as though excluded from all the earth except the prison’s narrow confines.

Some early legal schools proposed this interpretation.

Its semantic connection with nafy is intelligible: prisoners are separated from a wide area of ordinary life and social activity.

That connection does not make nafy and ḥabs identical in every Arabic use.

Someone may be banished from a town without being imprisoned, or imprisoned in their own town without being sent elsewhere.

Imprisonment is therefore one interpretation of how nafy is implemented in this verse, rather than the root’s certain lexical meaning.

Even those accepting imprisonment differed over its location and relationship to banishment.

Does the root itself establish one of these readings?

The direct meaning of nafā is driving away or removing.

Of these readings, expulsion from a land is closer to that meaning.

Lexical proximity to one method does not, however, resolve every legal disagreement.

For example, expelling someone from a region may remove a danger to its inhabitants while allowing that person to harm people elsewhere. Confinement may instead be more suitable for preventing harm.

These questions concern public security, individual rights, proportionality, and implementation, rather than the root’s independent meaning.

A lexical article therefore cannot, without separate legal and Qur’anic research, establish that imprisonment is always forbidden or banishment always required.

What can be stated securely is that yunfaw does not itself mean “be imprisoned.” Translating it that way makes an interpretive choice.

The role of أَوْ among the penalties

Al-Māʾidah 5:33 connects the penalties with aw, “or.”

The construction places expulsion alongside the other stated penalties.

Their relationship is nevertheless disputed in interpretation.

Some read them as alternatives determined by the offence’s kind and severity. Others understood aw as allowing a choice among penalties.

The disagreement does not follow from N F Y alone.

The verse itself also does not detail the precise conduct corresponding to each penalty.

A root article should therefore not claim either that expulsion always applies only to someone who frightened people, or that every penalty may be chosen in every case regardless of severity.

Such conclusions require independent study of the verse’s full construction, justice in punishment, and other Qur’anic principles.

Al-Māʾidah 5:34 and repentance

Immediately after the penalties, Al-Māʾidah 5:34 states:

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ تَابُوا مِن قَبْلِ أَن تَقْدِرُوا عَلَيْهِمْ فَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

illā alladhīna tābū min qabli an taqdirū ʿalayhim fa-iʿlamū anna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Translation: “Except those who repent before you gain power over them. Know, then, that The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:34

This matters in understanding the scope of the preceding penalties.

The Qur’an does not state penalties alone; it also mentions repentance before the offenders are apprehended.

Examining expulsion without this exception would therefore be incomplete.

N F Y itself says nothing about repentance, forgiveness, or the removal of liability. Those matters follow from the next verse.

Nor does repentance necessarily remove, without separate examination, the rights of people harmed by the offenders. Its relationship to public punishment and victims’ rights is a separate legal question.

For a lexical study, it is sufficient to establish that expulsion is one stated penalty and that the following verse qualifies the whole passage with an important exception.

6. N F Y and Related Roots

N F Y and Ṭ R D

Ṭard concerns driving away or expelling in Arabic.

Some uses are very close to nafy, and lexical accounts explain expelling someone from a town through ṭard.

N F Y also encompasses a broader range of setting aside and rejecting.

Denying a claim or descent is familiar, while ṭard more strongly retains the image of driving away.

The roots can therefore have close meanings when someone is expelled from a land.

That proximity does not make every nafy involve physical force or dragging someone away. The manner of acting depends on context.

Study of Ṭ R D

N F Y and KH R J

Kharaja has the familiar meaning of coming or going out.

Nafā in a transitive construction may instead describe driving someone else out.

Someone may leave a house voluntarily; kharaja is natural in that situation.

When someone drives another person from a town, nafā may be used.

The distinction should not become absolute. KH R J also has causative forms, such as akhraja, for bringing or taking someone out.

The comparison should therefore concern particular constructions, rather than treating every departure as voluntary and every nafy as compulsory.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:33, coercion follows from the passive construction and the punitive context.

Study of KH R J

N F Y and GH R B

Ghurba and taghrīb in uses concerning people relate to being away from home and going to another land.

Gharīb describes someone away from their homeland or familiar surroundings.

Nafy may concern removing someone without requiring a destination very far away.

Nafy and taghrīb sometimes approach one another in legal discussions of banishment.

That technical proximity should not restrict the Qur’anic term to sending an offender to a foreign land or a specified distance.

The verse gives no such distance.

Study of GH R B

N F Y and F R Q

Farq concerns separating and distinguishing things.

Nafy may also separate something from a collection, but its principal uses emphasize setting aside or removing.

Two things can be distinguished without either being rejected or driven away.

When impurities are removed from a substance, by contrast, part of the mixture is separated and discarded.

Physical nafy may thus involve separation, but not every distinction or separation is necessarily called nafy.

Study of F R Q

N F Y and N F R

These roots approach one another in the idea of moving away.

Nafr has familiar uses involving bolting, turning away, or setting out on an undertaking.

Transitive nafā emphasizes removing or driving away something or someone.

Someone may flee danger or set out in response to a summons, conduct that may be expressed with nafr.

Someone forcibly expelled from a town, however, is called manfī in the language of banishment.

Moving away is present in both families, but motive, agent, and grammatical relationships are not identical.

Study of N F R

N F Y and N F D

The roots differ in spelling and should not be confused.

N F Y concerns setting aside, driving away, and denying.

N F D concerns running out or coming to an end.

Al-Naḥl 16:96 states:

مَا عِندَكُمْ يَنفَدُ وَمَا عِندَ اللَّهِ بَاقٍ

mā ʿindakum yanfadu wa-mā ʿinda Allāhi bāqin

Translation: “What you have runs out, while what The God has remains.”

Yanfadu is from N F D, rather than N F Y.

Something may be removed from a place yet still exist. Something exhausted, by contrast, has reached the end of its quantity or duration.

Nafy in Al-Māʾidah 5:33 should therefore not mean destroying or annihilating a person.

Expulsion is a separate stated penalty, with killing expressed by a different verb in the same sentence.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For physical uses of nafā, “removed,” “drove away,” “dispersed,” or “expelled” may fit, depending on what is removed and from where.

For example:

نَفَتِ الرِّيحُ التُّرَابَ

nafati al-rīḥu al-turāba

“The wind dispersed the dust.”

نَفَيْتُ الْحَصَى عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ

nafaytu al-ḥaṣā ʿani al-ṭarīqi

“I removed the pebbles from the road.”

For people, “drove away” or “expelled,” and in a punitive setting “banished,” are suitable.

For intafā meaning departure, “moved away” or “withdrew” is natural; concerning descent or a claim, “denied” or “rejected the asserted relationship.”

For nufāyah, “refuse,” “residue,” or “rejected inferior material” is appropriate.

For nāfā and tanāfā, “was incompatible” and “were mutually incompatible” are familiar equivalents.

Al-Māʾidah 5:33

أَوْ يُنْفَوْا مِنَ الْأَرْضِ

aw yunfaw mina al-arḍi

The Forqan Persian rendering:

“Or be expelled from the land.”

This retains the direct meaning of the verb and preposition.

“Be expelled” reflects the passive, while “from the land” conveys the Qur’anic phrase without adding a town or particular area.

An explanation may add that expelling a person means removing them and denying their presence in the land or area to which the ruling applies.

“Be banished” is also intelligible, but may suggest a specific procedure, such as transfer to another town for a fixed period.

“Be expelled” therefore provides a more direct and less restrictive base translation.

“Be imprisoned” should not replace the phrase without explanation, since imprisonment is an exegetical and legal reading of implementation, rather than the verb’s independent meaning.

“Be destroyed” or “be annihilated” is also unsuitable: destruction differs from expulsion, and the Qur’an names killing separately.

Brief root meaning and meaning in the verse

Brief root meaning: Setting aside, driving away, and separating something from a place or group; in speech, negating or denying a relationship or claim.

Meaning in Al-Māʾidah 5:33: Be expelled; removing people from a land or area as a penalty, without the word itself specifying a destination, duration, or procedure.

8. Conclusion

N F Y concerns setting aside, separation, and removal in Arabic.

The meaning appears in wind dispersing dust, torrents carrying debris, impurities being discarded, and people being driven from home.

In speech, nafy means rejecting a claim or denying a relationship. Related uses include negation and affirmation, incompatibility, and mutual exclusion.

These uses should neither be separated by artificial boundaries nor reduced to a single original physical image. Removal and exclusion are recognizable common features, but their character and object vary across branches.

The Qur’an uses the root only once, as yunfaw in Al-Māʾidah 5:33.

The verb means “be expelled,” expressing one penalty for people who wage war against The God and His Messenger and strive to spread corruption in the land.

Exegetical history records disagreement over implementation: expulsion from a town or land, pursuit and removal from a region, or imprisonment.

The differences show that a lexical definition alone does not determine every detail of implementation.

Nor do those interpretive differences justify removing the clear sense of driving away from the translation.

N F Y itself does not determine guilt, the authority entitled to impose the penalty, its duration, or the conditions governing selection among the penalties.

These matters require the full construction, the conduct described as warfare and corruption, principles of justice, and the repentance exception in the following verse.

Final definition: At its semantic core, N F Y denotes setting aside, driving away, and separating something from a place, group, or relationship. Physical uses concern removal and dispersal; verbal uses concern denying or rejecting a relationship; uses concerning people concern expelling them from where they are present. Qur’anic yunfaw mina al-arḍ means “be expelled from the land,” stating removal without independently specifying its exact procedure, duration, or destination.
