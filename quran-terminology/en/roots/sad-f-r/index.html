1. Introduction to the Root

The root Ṣ F R in early Arabic has several known semantic branches. The branch to which all Qur'anic usages of this root belong indicates the color yellow, yellowishness, and a thing entering the state of yellowness.

Alongside this branch, the family of Ṣ F R in Arabic also had other meanings, including:

  • becoming empty or hollow;
  • yellow-colored metal, especially copper or yellow alloys;
  • whistling or a sharp sibilant sound;
  • the name of the month Ṣafar;
  • some early usages connected with hunger, emptiness of the belly, or abdominal illness.

The historical connection among all these branches is not equally clear. Some, such as the name of yellow metal, are plainly connected with color; but the connection of “yellowness” with “becoming empty,” “whistling,” or the name of the month Ṣafar cannot be stated with the same certainty. Therefore, in Qur'anic study, the color branch should be kept separate from the other branches that share the root.

The condensed definition of the Qur'anic branch is this:

Ṣ F R in the Qur'an indicates the appearance of yellow color, whether yellowness is the fixed and vivid color of something, or whether it arises in it as a result of change, ripening, drying, or wearing down.

3. The Semantic Core in Qur'anic Usage

Qur'anic usage shows two main states of yellowness:

In this state, something is described from the beginning with yellow color. This yellowness may be bright, pure, and pleasing to the eye:

بَقَرَةٌ صَفْرَاءُ فَاقِعٌ لَوْنُهَا a yellow cow whose color is vivid and intense.

In this verse, yellowness is not a sign of withering or illness; rather, it is a visible and pleasing color.

In this state, something that had previously been green and fresh becomes yellow as a result of ripening, wind, dryness, or the passage of time:

فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا then you see it yellowed.

In this usage, yellowness is not an initial fixed color, but a stage of change. The form muṣfarr expresses this movement into the state of yellow.

As a result, the root Ṣ F R itself does not have one fixed positive or negative value. In one context, yellowness pleases those who look; in another context, it is a sign of the end of a plant’s freshness; and in another, it is part of the frightening image of sparks of fire.

4. The Semantic Range of the Root in Arabic

4.1. Yellowness and Yellowishness

The principal branch used in the Qur'an is the color yellow:

  • أَصْفَر: yellow, masculine;
  • صَفْرَاء: yellow, feminine;
  • صُفْر: yellowish ones;
  • صُفْرَة: yellowness;
  • اِصْفَرَّ: it became yellow;
  • مُصْفَرّ: yellowed.

Yellowness can be bright and living, like the color of the cow in Surat Al-Baqarah; or it can be the yellowness that results from drying, like the cultivated field in Surahs Ar-Rum, Az-Zumar, and Al-Hadid.

4.2. Becoming Empty

In Arabic it is said that a vessel, house, or a person’s hand ṣafira; meaning it became empty of content, inhabitants, or wealth. Expressions such as ṣifr al-yadayn also indicate being empty-handed.

The numerical usage ṣifr also comes from this concept of emptiness: a place in which there is no quantity. This numerical usage does not appear in the Qur'an and is not connected to the color branch.

4.3. Yellow Metal

Ṣufr in some Arabic usages is the name of yellow metal, especially copper or yellow copper alloys. The connection of this meaning with the color branch is clear: the metal is named by its yellow or yellowish color.

4.4. Ṣafir and Whistling

Ṣafir is a sharp, extended sound produced by the passage of air, such as a human whistle, the cry of some birds, or the sound of wind. This branch is not used in the Qur'an.

4.5. The Month Ṣafar

Ṣafar is the name of the second month of the lunar year. Various explanations have been given for this naming, including the emptying of houses because of travel and war, or its connection with the conditions of Arab life at the time the months were named. It is difficult to determine the exact origin of this name with certainty.

5. Qur'anic Morphological Forms

5.1. Ṣafrā'

Ṣafrā' is the feminine of aṣfar. These two forms are used to express a color adjective:

  • أَصْفَر: yellow;
  • صَفْرَاء: yellow;
  • صُفْر: yellowish ones.

Ṣafrā' in Surat Al-Baqarah is the adjective of baqarah, because baqarah is grammatically treated as feminine.

5.2. Muṣfarr

Muṣfarr is formed from the verb iṣfarra:

اِصْفَرَّ الشَّيْءُ the thing became yellow.

This form does not merely state the existence of yellow color; rather, it indicates the arising of yellowness and transition into this state. For this reason, in describing a plant that becomes yellow after greenness and freshness, muṣfarr is more precise than aṣfar.

5.3. Ṣufr

Ṣufr is the plural of aṣfar. In Surat Al-Mursalat this form is the adjective of jimalat and describes a group of yellowish or yellow-brown camels.

This form should not be confused with Ṣafar, the name of the month, or ṣifr, meaning emptiness and the number zero. The first vowel of the word plays a role in distinguishing these meanings:

  • صُفْر: yellowish ones;
  • صَفَر: the name of the month;
  • صِفْر: empty and the number zero.

6. Ṣafrā' in Surat Al-Baqarah

In response to the Children of Israel’s question about the color of the cow, it says:

Al-Baqarah 2:69

قَالُوا ادْعُ لَنَا رَبَّكَ يُبَيِّنْ لَنَا مَا لَوْنُهَا قَالَ إِنَّهُ يَقُولُ إِنَّهَا بَقَرَةٌ صَفْرَاءُ فَاقِعٌ لَوْنُهَا تَسُرُّ النَّاظِرِينَ

They said: Ask your Lord for us to make clear what its color is. He said: He says it is a yellow cow; its color is vivid and intense, pleasing those who look.

6.1. The Kind of Yellowness

The word ṣafrā' by itself identifies the color yellow; but the phrase faqiʿun lawnuha adds intensity and visibility to this color.

Faqiʿ in describing yellow indicates a color that is strong, pure, visible, and striking. Therefore the intended cow is not pale yellow, sickly yellow, or whitish; rather, its yellowness is clear and eye-catching.

The phrase tasurru an-nazirin also states the visual effect of this color: seeing it is pleasing to those who look. In this context, yellowness is joined with beauty, brightness, and attracting attention.

6.2. The Grammatical Structure

In the phrase:

بَقَرَةٌ صَفْرَاءُ فَاقِعٌ لَوْنُهَا

Ṣafrā' directly describes baqarah; but faqiʿ comes in the masculine form because intensity and brightness are attributed to lawn, and lawn is masculine.

The meaning of the structure is:

a yellow cow whose color is vivid.

This way of expression moves attention from the mere name of the color to its quality and intensity.

7. Muṣfarr and the Process of Becoming Yellow

Muṣfarr appears three times in the Qur'an, and in all three cases it shows the acquired state of yellowness. The main subject in these usages is a plant or cultivated field that has passed the stage of greenness and freshness and has turned toward yellow.

7.1. Surat Ar-Rum

وَلَئِنْ أَرْسَلْنَا رِيحًا فَرَأَوْهُ مُصْفَرًّا لَظَلُّوا مِنْ بَعْدِهِ يَكْفُرُونَ

And if We send a wind and they see it yellowed, they would still, after it, cover truth ungratefully.

Ar-Rum 30:51

In the preceding verses, the speech is about wind, clouds, rain, and the earth being brought to life. Therefore, the pronoun hu in fara'awhu is usually returned to the plant or crop understood from the context: a harmful wind blows, and people see the growths yellowed after their greenness.

Here muṣfarr is the result of a change in condition. The plant has lost its first color, and the effect of wind or dryness has become visible in it.

7.2. Surat Az-Zumar

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَسَلَكَهُ يَنَابِيعَ فِي الْأَرْضِ ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُ بِهِ زَرْعًا مُخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهُ ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ حُطَامًا

Have you not seen that The God sent down water from the sky, then He made it travel as springs in the earth; then through it He brings forth cultivated growth with varied colors; then that growth reaches the stage of drying and wearing down, and you see it yellowed; then He makes it broken debris.

Az-Zumar 39:21

This verse sketches a continuous process:

  1. the coming down of water;
  1. its flowing through the earth;
  1. the sprouting of cultivated growth;
  1. diversity of colors;
  1. the plant reaching the stage of dryness;
  1. yellowing;
  1. becoming broken fragments.

Muṣfarr stands in the middle of this process. Yellowness is a stage that appears after freshness and before the complete collapse of the plant.

Ḥuṭām, from the root Ḥ Ṭ M, indicates something broken and crushed. Therefore yellowing is not itself the collapse; it is a sign of the plant’s approaching that stage.

7.3. Surat Al-Hadid

كَمَثَلِ غَيْثٍ أَعْجَبَ الْكُفَّارَ نَبَاتُهُ ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا ثُمَّ يَكُونُ حُطَامًا

Like rain whose growth amazes the farmers; then the plant reaches the stage of drying, and you see it yellowed; then it becomes broken debris.

Al-Hadid 57:20

The semantic structure of this verse is close to Surat Az-Zumar. First the plant causes wonder; then it yellows, and finally it loses its structure.

The word muṣfarr here reveals the distance between two states:

  • the initial state: growth, freshness, and visible beauty;
  • the final state: fragmentation and loss of structure.

Yellowing is the visible boundary between these two stages.

8. Difference between Ṣafrā' and Muṣfarr

The difference between these two forms is not only grammatical; it is also semantic:

FormType of indicationExample
صَفْرَاءhaving yellow as a featurea yellow cow
مُصْفَرّentering the state of yellow after changea yellowed cultivated field

Ṣafrā' answers this question:

What color is that thing?

But muṣfarr more often answers this question:

What state has that thing now entered?

Therefore, in baqaratun ṣafrā', color is part of the animal’s description; but in fatarāhu muṣfarran, yellowness is the result of a temporal process.

9. Ṣufr in Surat Al-Mursalat

In describing the sparks of the Fire, it says:

Al-Mursalat 77:32-33

إِنَّهَا تَرْمِي بِشَرَرٍ كَالْقَصْرِ كَأَنَّهُ جِمَالَتٌ صُفْرٌ

It throws sparks like great structures, as if those sparks were yellowish camels.

Ṣufr is the plural of aṣfar and the adjective of jimalat. The image of the verse is built from several elements:

  • the largeness of the sparks;
  • their movement and being hurled;
  • their continuity or gathering together;
  • their yellowish color;
  • their resemblance to a group of camels.

9.1. The Range of Color in the Description of Camels

Naming the colors of camels in early Arabic does not always match the simple color categories of modern language exactly. Aṣfar in describing camels could include a range of yellow, yellow-brown, camel-colored, and darker color mixed with yellowness.

Therefore jimalatun ṣufr can be translated “yellowish camels” or “yellow-brown camels.” The certain core in the word is the presence of yellowness or yellowishness; but determining one completely precise shade of color is not possible from the word alone.

9.2. The Suitability of the Image with Sparks of Fire

A spark of fire, when it separates, moves, and scatters, can appear yellow, reddish, orange, or mixed with dark smoke. Comparing it to yellowish camels embodies both the size and movement of the sparks and their color.

In this verse too, ṣufr is not simply the name of an isolated color; it is part of a moving and frightening image.

10. The Role of Yellowness in the Image of the Plant Cycle

In the two verses of Az-Zumar and Al-Hadid, yellow color stands inside a temporal sequence:

water ← growth ← diversity of color ← dryness ← yellowing ← brokenness

In this sequence, muṣfarr is a visible stage of material transformation. The plant still exists and can be seen, but it has lost its first freshness. After this stage, its structure breaks and it becomes ḥuṭām.

This image shows that color in the Qur'an is sometimes not only a static description; it can be the mark of a temporal and biological stage. The yellowness of the plant makes the passage from growth to dryness visible to the eye.

In Surat Al-Baqarah, this same color has another function: yellowness is not a sign of the end of a cycle, but the bright and pleasing feature of an animal. The difference between these two usages comes from the difference of word form and sentence context.

11. The Relation of Ṣ F R with the Root L W N

The root L W N expresses the general concept of color and visible appearance; but Ṣ F R is the name of a specific color or the process of that color arising.

In Surat Al-Baqarah both concepts come together:

مَا لَوْنُهَا What is its color?

Then the answer is given:

صَفْرَاءُ فَاقِعٌ لَوْنُهَا It is yellow, and its color is vivid.

Therefore:

  • lawn expresses the general category of color;
  • ṣafrā' determines the kind of that color;
  • faqiʿ shows its intensity and visible force.

In Surat Az-Zumar as well, first there is mention of mukhtalifan alwanuhu, and then one of the later stages of the cultivated growth is described with muṣfarran. Alwan is the general diversity of plant appearance; muṣfarr is a specific state that appears after that diversity.

12. Comparison with Nearby Roots

Kh Ḍ R indicates greenness and being green, and in the description of plants it is usually connected with freshness, moisture, and growth. Muṣfarr in the context of plants shows the stage in which greenness has decreased and the plant has come close to dryness.

Thus, in the plant cycle:

  • greenness is the color of the stage of growth and freshness;
  • yellowing is the color of the stage of ripening or moving toward dryness.

Ṣ F R determines the kind of color; F Q ʿ determines its intensity and vividness.

  • Ṣafrā': yellow;
  • faqiʿun lawnuha: its yellow color is intense and visible.

Faqiʿ by itself is not the name of the color yellow; rather, it describes the quality of that color.

In Surahs Az-Zumar and Al-Hadid, yahij comes before muṣfarran. This verb, about plants, indicates reaching a stage in which freshness is ending and dryness begins. Muṣfarr shows the color and visible appearance of this change.

Muṣfarr is the yellowed state of the plant; ḥuṭām is the final result of its collapse.

  • Muṣfarr: the plant’s form still remains, but it has become yellow;
  • ḥuṭām: the plant’s structure has broken and become crushed.

13. Translation Suggestions

Base translation:

yellow

Considering faqiʿun lawnuha:

deep yellow bright yellow pure and striking yellow

Precise translations:

yellowed turned toward yellowness yellowed and dried

“Yellowed” carries the process of change better than simply “yellow.”

Base translation:

yellowish ones

In the verse’s expression:

yellowish camels yellow-brown camels

The translation “yellowish” shows the color range of the word in describing camels better than one bright, uniform yellow.

14. Conclusion

The root Ṣ F R in Arabic has several historical branches, including yellowness, becoming empty, yellow metal, whistling, and the name of the month Ṣafar; but all of its Qur'anic usages belong to the branch of color.

This branch has three main forms in the Qur'an:

  • ṣafrā' for fixed, bright, and eye-pleasing yellow color;
  • muṣfarr for something that has become yellow as the result of a process;
  • ṣufr for a plural of yellowish beings, in the image of camels and sparks of fire.

The overall meaning of these usages is this:

Ṣ F R in the Qur'an expresses yellowness both as a visible color and as a visible sign of transformation.

In the cow, yellowness is a bright and pleasing color. In the cultivated field, yellowness is the stage of transition from freshness to dryness. In the sparks of fire, yellowishness is part of an image of largeness, movement, and fear. Therefore, the final meaning of each usage is not obtained from the name of the color alone, but from the morphological structure, the thing being described, and the place of the word in the context of the verse.
