Spreading and making level: daḥw in Arabic and its use concerning the earth

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic D Ḥ W concerns spreading, extending, making level, and in some uses driving or moving something aside.

One familiar expression is daḥā al-arḍ: he spread out the earth or made its expanse level and open.

Early Arabic also uses this family for rain moving pebbles, driving a stone across the ground, a horse’s forelegs moving close to the ground, and preparing an ostrich’s nesting place.

These are different actions: spreading land, moving stones, and clearing sand for eggs are not identical. Their relationship can nevertheless be examined through opening an expanse, leveling a surface, or moving things across it.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an, in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30. It concerns the earth’s spreading; subsequent verses describe bringing out water and pasture and setting mountains firmly.

Understanding this usage therefore requires more than a dictionary definition: its difference from initial creation, its relationship to spreading, extending, and preparing, and the contribution of subsequent verses matter.

Spreading the earth’s surface should also be distinguished from specifying the whole planet’s geometry. Early sources do not make daḥw itself the name of a particular geometric shape.

2. Forms and meaning in early Arabic

The Qur’anic form

D Ḥ W occurs only as daḥāhā in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30.

Daḥā is a past Form I verb; hā refers to al-arḍ, the earth.

The root ends in wāw. Some written or inflected forms show a final alif or yāʾ, but the root examined here is D Ḥ W.

Arabic records the following family members:

دَحَا يَدْحُو: spreading, extending, or leveling; in some uses driving or moving something.

دَحْو: the verbal noun, spreading or extending; elsewhere driving something across a surface.

دَاحٍ: an active participle, someone spreading, leveling, or driving something, according to context.

مَدْحُوّ: a passive participle, something spread or made level.

أُدْحِيّ and أُدْحِيَّة: an ostrich’s egg-laying place in sandy ground.

مَدْحَى: in some uses, the ostrich’s egg-laying site or the place prepared for it.

مِدْحَاة: an implement used in a game involving driving or moving something along the ground.

مَدَاحِي: stones or implements in games involving driving objects toward a target.

Lexical sources also record daḥā yadḥā as a linguistic variant alongside daḥā yadḥū.

These variants alone do not establish completely independent semantic families. Qur’anic indexing should nevertheless identify the actual form and root.

Only daḥāhā occurs in the Qur’an; the other forms illuminate Arabic usage and semantic development.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30

The semantic core: spreading and leveling

Ibn Fāris defines the root in Maqāyīs al-Lughah as:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى بَسْطٍ وَتَمْهِيدٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā basṭin wa-tamhīdin

“One principle indicating spreading and preparing a level expanse.”

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

الدَّحْوُ: الْبَسْطُ

al-daḥwu al-basṭu

“Daḥw means spreading.”

Its use concerning the earth is then explained:

دَحَا الْأَرْضَ يَدْحُوهَا دَحْوًا: بَسَطَهَا

daḥā al-arḍa yadḥūhā daḥwan basaṭahā

“He performed daḥw upon the earth: he spread it out.”

These sources also connect daḥw with making something spacious or extensive.

Concerning the earth, it therefore involves spreading and leveling rather than merely moving it between locations.

Basṭ and tamhīd are themselves distinguishable.

Basṭ foregrounds spreading and extending; tamhīd can convey leveling and preparing a surface for use.

They overlap concerning land without making every detail of surface preparation a fixed meaning of daḥā.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: spreading and preparing a level expanse

Do all uses reduce to spreading?

Ibn Fāris seeks to connect the family’s uses through spreading and leveling.

Rain moving pebbles aside can expose or level parts of a surface.

An ostrich clearing sand with its feet similarly prepares an egg-laying place.

This relationship does not make daḥw directly mean leveling in every sentence.

Driving stones matters in a game; movement of limbs over the surface matters for a horse.

The final definition should therefore explain both the dominant spreading sense and recorded senses of driving and moving, without forcing one onto every use.

3. Early Arabic witnesses and physical imagery

Spreading the earth

Dictionaries clearly establish daḥw of the earth through spreading and extension.

The early lexicographer Shamir reports that an Arab woman recited:

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي أَطَاقَا
بَنَى السَّمَاءَ فَوْقَنَا طِبَاقَا
ثُمَّ دَحَا الْأَرْضَ فَمَا أَضَاقَا

al-ḥamdu li-llāhi alladhī aṭāqā / banā al-samāʾa fawqanā ṭibāqā / thumma daḥā al-arḍa fa-mā aḍāqā

“Praise belongs to The God who had power; He built the sky above us in layers, then spread the earth without making it narrow.”

Asked about daḥā, she explained it as awsaʿahā: He made it wide and spacious.

This witness preserves spaciousness in the speaker’s own explanation.

Its composition date is unknown, however, and it cannot be called pre-Islamic without independent evidence. Its close Qur’anic themes also prevent this transmission alone from establishing an independent pre-Qur’anic use.

Its value lies in documenting the linguistic understanding of spreading and widening.

A witness attributed to Zayd ibn ʿAmr ibn Nufayl

Lexical and exegetical sources attribute a verse to Zayd ibn ʿAmr ibn Nufayl, a pre-Islamic Arab figure:

دَحَاهَا فَلَمَّا رَآهَا اسْتَوَتْ
عَلَى الْمَاءِ أَرْسَى عَلَيْهَا الْجِبَالَا

daḥāhā fa-lammā raʾāhā istawat / ʿalā al-māʾi arsā ʿalayhā al-jibālā

“He spread it; when He saw it settled upon water, He set mountains firmly upon it.”

Here spreading the earth accompanies settlement and mountains.

Text, attribution, and composition date require caution. A poet having lived before Islam does not establish that every attributed verse predates revelation. Al-Qurṭubī transmits this wording from Al-Mubarrad without naming its poet, while the form attributed to Zayd begins دَحَاهَا فَلَمَّا اسْتَوَتْ شَدَّهَا. Both textual and attribution differences should remain visible.

This illustrates the expression’s literary transmission, but does not independently establish pre-Qur’anic cosmology.

Nor should the earth resting upon water in this poem become the root’s lexical meaning or a direct Qur’anic statement.

Al-Qurṭubī: poetic text and attribution variants

Rain moving pebbles aside

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

دَحَا الْمَطَرُ الْحَصَى عَنْ وَجْهِ الْأَرْضِ

daḥā al-maṭaru al-ḥaṣā ʿan wajhi al-arḍi

“Rain swept pebbles from the ground’s surface.”

In some constructions, the root thus conveys moving and clearing something away.

Rain can shift pebbles and alter the ground by washing or driving surface material.

Here the verb concerns the pebbles, rather than a geometric enlargement of the whole earth’s surface.

Meaning should not transfer to every use without attention to the object and preposition.

A witness attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic Aws ibn Ḥajar describes rain:

يَنْزِعُ جِلْدَ الْحَصَى أَجَشُّ مُبْتَرِكٌ
كَأَنَّهُ فَاحِصٌ أَوْ لَاعِبٌ دَاحِي

yanziʿu jilda al-ḥaṣā ajashshu mubtarikun / ka-annahu fāḥiṣun aw lāʿibun dāḥī

“Roaring, pounding rain strips away the pebbled surface, like one scraping the ground or a player driving something along.”

Dāḥī denotes a player moving a stone or similar object.

Rain resembles the player because its force shifts pebbles.

Early Arabic daḥw thus concerns driving and movement across a surface as well as spreading.

Dictionaries also report ʿUbayd as the poet. Attribution to Aws should therefore remain qualified.

Al-Muḥkam and Tāj al-ʿArūs: rain and stone games

Stone games and movement along the ground

Daḥā bi-al-ḥajar can mean driving or throwing a stone.

Lexical descriptions of madāḥī games have relatively rounded or flat stones driven toward a ground hollow; reaching the target makes the player win.

Daḥw can therefore involve moving something across the ground or toward a target.

This does not make the Qur’an’s spreading of the earth mean throwing the entire planet, rotating it, or placing it in orbit.

A driving or throwing sense in one construction does not itself determine daḥā al-arḍ.

The object and evidence within the sentence must guide interpretation.

A horse’s movement

Ibn Fāris also records a use for a moving horse.

The horse throws its forelegs forward without lifting its hooves far above the ground.

This concerns movement close to the ground and its extension.

It should not define every derivative. Horse movement, driving stones, and spreading land are distinct uses requiring their own contexts.

4. Udḥiyyah: an ostrich’s egg-laying place

Two recorded lexical forms are udḥiyy and udḥiyyah; the latter has a final tāʾ.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab identify the ostrich’s egg-laying place in sand.

An ostrich does not build a branch-and-leaf nest in a tree. It prepares a shallow place on the ground and lays eggs there.

The lexical account describes the bird clearing sand with its feet to prepare that place.

The relationship to daḥw thus concerns preparing and leveling a site.

San Diego Zoo: the ostrich’s shallow nest scrape

Does udḥiyyah mean an ostrich egg?

In the usage explained by early dictionaries, it names the egg-laying place rather than the egg.

This difference is essential.

Saying the bird lays in its udḥiyyah means its eggs occupy the prepared site.

Udḥiyyah alone therefore does not make D Ḥ W mean producing an ostrich egg or making something egg-shaped.

A nesting place may be shallow, round, or elongated, but its precise shape is not part of daḥw’s meaning in every use.

The word names a prepared natural site rather than the whole earth’s geometric shape.

Early dictionaries: udḥiyyah as the nesting place

Relating the usage to the earth’s shape

Some claim that udḥiyyah’s connection with ostriches makes Qur’anic daḥāhā mean shaping the earth like an ostrich egg.

Several unproved steps intervene.

First, the cited udḥiyyah would need to name the egg, whereas early dictionaries clearly define its laying place.

Second, an egg’s geometric meaning would need to transfer into daḥā and be present when describing the earth.

Third, the verse or contemporary Arabic would need evidence confirming that particular meaning.

Sharing a root proves none of those steps.

“He made the earth ostrich-egg-shaped” is therefore not a defensible lexical translation of Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30.

This neither affirms nor denies scientific findings about the earth’s shape, which require astronomical evidence and measurement independently.

Root research identifies the word’s meanings in Arabic.

5. Qur’anic usage: Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30

وَالْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ دَحَاهَا

wa-al-arḍa baʿda dhālika daḥāhā

“And the earth—after that, He spread it out.”

This is the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

Semantically, al-arḍ is what is spread. In the common grammatical analysis of ishtighāl, an implied verb governs its accusative, explained by daḥāhā; the explicit verb’s object is hā, referring back to the earth. Baʿda dhālika is temporal. This is therefore more precise than treating al-arḍ simply as that explicit verb’s ordinary fronted object.

Daḥāhā refers to spreading the earth.

Lexically, this agrees with established early Arabic explanations of spreading and extension.

The verse’s full implication nevertheless requires its preceding and following context.

Al-Qurṭubī: al-arḍ governed by an implied verb and the meaning of daḥw

Earth after the account of heaven

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:27 asks about the creation of humans and heaven.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28 describes raising and ordering heaven.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29 describes darkening its night and bringing out daylight.

Verse 30 then says the earth was spread after that.

In ordinary syntax, baʿda dhālika thus refers to a stage following what preceded.

The verb is daḥā rather than khalaqa, created.

The verse does not expressly say the earth first came into existence after heaven’s creation. It speaks of spreading or preparing its expanse.

Distinguishing initial creation from spreading matters when comparing other verses.

Bringing out its water and pasture

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:31

أَخْرَجَ مِنْهَا مَاءَهَا وَمَرْعَاهَا

akhraja minhā māʾahā wa-marʿāhā

“He brought out its water and pasture from it.”

This immediately follows spreading the earth.

Water and pasture becoming manifest are described here.

Their presence places spreading within a larger account of provision for life and growth.

The verb’s own meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from the connected verses’ result.

Lexical daḥw does not itself mean bringing out water, growing vegetation, creating pasture, or irrigating land.

The separate verb akhraja conveys those matters.

Their proximity nevertheless presents spreading, water, and pasture as interconnected.

Context can therefore explain a prepared, extensive habitat without putting all those implications into daḥāhā’s translation.

Setting mountains firmly

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32

وَالْجِبَالَ أَرْسَاهَا

wa-al-jibāla arsāhā

“And He set the mountains firmly.”

Arsā belongs to R S W and denotes establishing the mountains firmly.

Mountains following spreading, water, and pasture show that the account does not concern an entirely flat surface without relief.

Mountains belong within the same picture of spread-out earth.

Spreading should therefore not mean removing every mountain, valley, slope, or irregularity.

Daḥw emphasizes spreading and leveling without necessarily turning the entire surface into a featureless plane.

Mountains appear in the following verse with a separate verb for establishing them.

Provision for you and your livestock

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:33

مَتَاعًا لَكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَامِكُمْ

matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum

“For your benefit and that of your livestock.”

This phrase connects the account with human and livestock benefit.

Spreading earth, bringing out water and pasture, and establishing mountains concern habitat and creatures’ provision in this context.

This helps explain why daḥw is mentioned here.

Benefit comes from matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum rather than D Ḥ W itself.

Preparing earth for life therefore follows from the whole passage; daḥāhā’s direct translation remains “He spread it out.”

Does daḥāhā specify the earth’s geometry?

Early sources explain spreading, extending, and leveling.

Those senses do not immediately establish that the verse describes the whole earth as a flat plane.

People can experience and describe an extensive surface without discussing the complete shape of the body beneath them.

Natural descriptions of a country’s extent, for example, need not describe the whole planet’s geometry.

Conversely, daḥāhā alone does not establish a spherical, ellipsoidal, or other scientifically specified shape.

Two hasty conclusions should therefore be avoided: daḥw necessarily means the whole earth is flat, or directly means it is spherical or egg-shaped.

The defensible meaning here is spreading the earth; its actual shape requires independent evidence.

After that and the order of creation

Baʿda dhālika in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30 naturally means after that.

Some exegetical explanations propose “along with” to relate this verse to others.

Such uses can be examined in some Arabic constructions, but ordinary “after” need not be abandoned here without strong contextual evidence.

One can distinguish the earth’s initial creation from its subsequent spreading.

Al-Baqarah 2:29 describes creating what is on earth, then turning to heaven. Fuṣṣilat 41:9, Fuṣṣilat 41:10, Fuṣṣilat 41:11, and Fuṣṣilat 41:12 describe stages involving earth, its provisions, and ordering the heavens.

These require careful attention to verbs, sentence order, and each stage’s scope.

The entire question of creation’s order should not be imposed on daḥw’s lexical meaning.

Daḥā itself provides no complete timetable of earth and heaven’s formation.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30 places spreading after a series of descriptions of heaven. Relating those stages cosmologically and historically requires a separate study of all creation passages.

6. Distinguishing related roots

D Ḥ W and B S Ṭ: spreading and extending

Basṭ is daḥw’s closest lexical equivalent.

Early dictionaries explain daḥā al-arḍ through basaṭahā.

Qur’anic B S Ṭ concerns many subjects, from outstretched hands and expanded provision to spread-out land.

Qur’anic daḥw concerns only the earth.

That does not restrict all Arabic daḥw to the earth, since dictionaries document other uses.

B S Ṭ has a broader variety of Qur’anic subjects; daḥw in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30 specifically concerns spreading the earth.

Distinguishing them should not introduce unsupported geometric or scientific meanings.

Study of B S Ṭ

D Ḥ W and M D D: spreading and extending

Al-Ḥijr 15:19, Qāf 50:7, and Al-Raʿd 13:3 use M D D verbs concerning the earth.

Madd can mean extending, stretching, or expansion.

Daḥw likewise concerns spreading when applied to the earth.

Their fields overlap without necessarily being identical.

Madd foregrounds extension; daḥw foregrounds spreading and leveling or opening an expanse according to established usage.

This does not establish a sequence of daḥw before madd or the reverse unless verses specify it.

Nor does madd itself mean that the earth’s extent is unlimited.

Study of M D D

D Ḥ W and M H D: spreading and preparing

M H D concerns preparing and leveling a place.

The Qur’an describes the earth through mahd and mihād, for example in Ṭā Hā 20:53 and Al-Nabaʾ 78:6.

Their meanings overlap; Ibn Fāris himself uses tamhīd to define daḥw.

M H D concerning the earth foregrounds the site’s preparedness and suitability.

Daḥw in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30 describes spreading, while subsequent verses relate it to water, pasture, and creatures’ benefit.

Preparing for life is therefore contextual, rather than an independent necessary meaning of every D Ḥ W form.

Study of M H D

D Ḥ W and S Ṭ Ḥ: the earth’s spread and surface

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20 says:

وَإِلَى الْأَرْضِ كَيْفَ سُطِحَتْ

wa-ilā al-arḍi kayfa suṭiḥat

“And at the earth—how it has been spread and made level.”

S Ṭ Ḥ concerns surface and breadth.

The verse directs attention to the earth’s surface and how it is spread or leveled.

Daḥw approaches spreading and leveling, but the roots differ.

Similarity does not make both decide the whole earth’s geometry conclusively.

Nor does extensive, level land in human experience mean every surface lacks mountains, valleys, or curvature.

Usable, observable expanses should be distinguished from the whole planetary body’s shape.

D Ḥ W and Ṭ Ḥ W: two close forms

Al-Shams 91:6 says:

وَالْأَرْضِ وَمَا طَحَاهَا

wa-al-arḍi wa-mā ṭaḥāhā

“And by the earth and that which spread it.”

Ṭaḥāhā belongs to Ṭ Ḥ W rather than D Ḥ W.

Their written forms differ only in the first letter, and dictionaries compare them.

Al-Farrāʾ and Al-Azharī associate ṭaḥā with daḥā. Lisān al-ʿArab likewise relates ṭaḥw to spreading.

This matters because the Qur’an uses two similar verbs concerning the earth.

Sound resemblance alone should not merge their Quranic index entries.

Nor can their historical direction of development be settled without sufficient evidence, for example by declaring one necessarily original and the other altered.

Both concern spreading the earth contextually, but their forms and roots require independent recording.

Study of Ṭ Ḥ W

D Ḥ W and D Ḥ Ḥ: related families

Lexical D Ḥ Ḥ concerns pressing, pushing into ground, leveling, or driving something.

Those uses overlap with some daḥw forms.

D Ḥ Ḥ is a doubled root; D Ḥ W ends in wāw.

Shared meaning alone neither unifies them nor proves historical development from one to the other.

Qur’anic daḥāhā should particularly retain D Ḥ W rather than merge with forms having another final letter.

7. Suggested translations for Forqan

Since daḥāhā is this family’s sole Qur’anic form, translation should first address that verse.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30

وَالْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ دَحَاهَا

wa-al-arḍa baʿda dhālika daḥāhā

Suggested translation:

“And the earth—after that, He spread it out.”

This is concise, natural, and consistent with recorded lexical meaning.

Spreading suitably renders daḥā. In Persian, “the earth” also conveys hā’s object without needing separate repetition.

A Persian equivalent of “flattened the earth” may evoke an overly specific flat sheet, despite its understandable connection with basṭ.

“Made the earth level” conveys one semantic aspect but could suggest removing every terrain irregularity if used exclusively.

“Prepared the earth” can be explained contextually but moves farther from the direct verb: preparation for benefit follows from Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:31, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32, and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:33.

“Made the earth ostrich-egg-shaped” lacks documented lexical support.

Moving the earth, placing it in orbit, or making it spherical should likewise not become daḥāhā’s meaning solely from Arabic stone-driving uses.

Conversely, turning the earth into a completely flat sheet also adds information beyond the direct verb.

“After that, He spread out the earth” is therefore the preferred base rendering for Forqan.

Explanation can mention spreading, widening, and leveling, linked contextually with water, pasture, and human and livestock benefit.

Those explanations need not all enter the short verse translation.

Non-Qur’anic daḥw may mean spreading, leveling, or in some contexts driving; dāḥin means one spreading or driving, madḥuww means spread out, and udḥiyyah names an ostrich’s nesting site.

8. Conclusion

Arabic D Ḥ W concerns spreading and leveling, particularly clearly in daḥā al-arḍ.

Dictionaries also record rain shifting pebbles, driving stones, horse movement, and preparing ostrich nesting places.

These relate to opening or leveling an expanse and moving objects across surfaces without reducing every use to one physical motion or geometric shape.

Qur’anic D Ḥ W occurs once, as daḥāhā in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30.

Its direct meaning is spreading the earth. Subsequent verses bring out water and pasture, establish mountains, and connect the account to human and livestock benefit.

Contextually, this is part of preparing a habitat; all the habitat’s qualities are not thereby daḥw’s lexical meaning.

Daḥw also differs from initial creation. The verse does not by itself date the earth’s first matter or every stage of creation.

Udḥiyyah names the egg-laying place rather than the egg, so it does not establish an egg-shaped rendering of daḥāhā.

Conversely, natural descriptions of spreading alone do not conclusively determine whether the whole earth is flat or spherical.

Qur’anic research should distinguish linguistic meaning, context, interpretation, and independent natural-scientific knowledge.

Proposed final definition: D Ḥ W principally denotes spreading, extending, and leveling. Some Arabic uses concern driving or moving things across a surface. Qur’anic daḥāhā most naturally means spreading the earth, without itself specifying the planet’s complete geometry, the process’s physical mechanism, or every stage of creation. Brief root meaning: Spreading, extending, and leveling; in some uses, driving or moving something aside across a surface.
