1. Word Meaning and Structure

In its familiar Arabic sense, qiṭmīr names the very thin covering on a date stone, between the stone and the fruit’s flesh. Its thinness and slight substance also made it a name for something exceedingly insignificant.

Qiṭmīr occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Fāṭir 35:13. The verse denies those invoked apart from The God ownership of even a qiṭmīr.

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ explains it through al-fūfatu allatī fī al-nawāti (الفُوفَةُ الَّتِي فِي النَّوَاةِ), a thin, delicate covering associated with the date stone.

Lisān al-ʿArab expands this: al-qishratu al-daqīqatu allatī ʿalā al-nawāti bayna al-nawāti wa-l-tamri (القِشْرَةُ الدَّقِيقَةُ الَّتِي عَلَى النَّوَاةِ بَيْنَ النَّوَاةِ وَالتَّمْرِ), the thin covering on the stone between it and the date flesh.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: membrane, groove, and spot

The definition identifies a familiar, tangible object.

Separating a date’s flesh from its stone may leave a thin layer on the stone’s surface. The familiar lexical account calls that layer qiṭmīr.

Early sources nevertheless give other explanations, including the stone’s groove and a white spot on it.

Although the thin-covering sense has stronger support, recorded disagreement about the exact referent should not be concealed.

The accounts share a reference to a very small, slight part of a date stone.

Its insignificance matters in the Qur’anic sentence, which denies even the slightest share of ownership.

This should nevertheless not erase the tangible meaning by translating qiṭmīr simply as “nothing.”

Qiṭmīr names a real thing whose insignificance emphasizes the denial of even a small amount.

Word Structure

Qur’anic morphology indexes Q Ṭ M R as quadriliteral.

The Qur’anic form qiṭmīr follows the fiʿlīl pattern.

Unlike Z L Z L and W S W S, Q Ṭ M R does not consist of two repeated letters.

Nor do the examined lexical sources provide a clear, extensive family of Qur’anic verbs from which qiṭmīr’s meaning could be extracted.

Its established primary use names a small date-stone part.

Quadriliteral structure alone does not justify inventing a verb meaning “peel,” “cover,” or “separate.”

The recorded noun meaning should take priority over unsupported derivational hypotheses.

2. Lexical Forms and Differing Early Explanations

Qiṭmīr and Qiṭmār

Arabic dictionaries record both qiṭmīr and qiṭmār.

Ibn Sīdah’s Al-Muḥkam and Ibn Manẓūr’s Lisān al-ʿArab give both in connection with date stones.

Qiṭmār differs structurally from qiṭmīr while belonging to the same attested semantic field.

Only qiṭmīr occurs in the Qur’an.

Qiṭmār should therefore be recognized as a related lexical form, rather than a Qur’anic derivative.

Al-Qāmūs al-Muḥīṭ and Tāj al-ʿArūs also record both forms and several referent explanations.

These require separate examination.

Al-Muḥkam, Al-Qāmūs, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: qiṭmīr and qiṭmār

The Thin Date-Stone Covering

The best-known account is the thin covering between stone and fruit flesh.

Al-ʿAyn mentions a covering or layer remaining attached to, or accompanying, the stone when it is removed.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ explains the same sense with fūfah.

Fūfah here concerns a very delicate layer.

This fits the tangible experience of removing a date stone: part of its thin covering may come out with it.

It does not establish that every date stone always has an identical covering in color, thickness, and shape.

Date variety, ripeness, and drying conditions may affect its appearance.

Understanding the word does not require precise modern measurements.

The principal point is a very thin, slight covering.

The Date Stone’s Groove

Another account defines qiṭmīr and qiṭmār as shaqq al-nawāh, the date stone’s groove or cleft.

Al-Muḥkam records this meaning, also transmitted by Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs.

A groove is not the same physical thing as the thin covering.

The groove is a visible depression or cleft in the stone’s structure; the covering is a layer on its surface.

These explanations therefore should not be physically equated.

Both nevertheless concern a small date-stone feature.

The difference does not undermine the Qur’anic verse’s central point: denying ownership of even something insignificant.

For precise translation, however, the more widely attested and familiar lexical meaning is preferable.

“The thin covering of a date stone” is preferable in that respect.

The Date Stone’s White Spot

Lexicons also record a third explanation.

It identifies qiṭmīr as a white spot on the stone’s back, associated in that account with the palm’s germination point.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs record this explanation too.

The white spot nevertheless differs from the surrounding membrane and the groove.

The three explanations disagree about the exact referent.

Their inclusion in one dictionary does not establish that every lexicographer described precisely the same part in three different ways.

Local usage or lexical explanation may have extended one small part’s name to nearby features.

Establishing that history, however, requires further evidence.

The sources collectively connect the word with a very small, slight part of the date stone.

That feature is central to Qur’anic usage.

3. Linguistic Evidence and Uses for Something Insignificant

Qiṭmīr in Arabic Speech

Lisān al-ʿArab records mā aṣabtu minhu qiṭmīran: I obtained nothing from him, not even a qiṭmīr’s worth.

Lisān al-ʿArab: mā aṣabtu minhu qiṭmīran

The actual transaction need not concern a date-stone membrane.

The speaker may mean property, a right, or a benefit they expected to receive.

They name a minute date-stone part to say nothing came to them.

The tangible meaning has consequently extended to an expression of insignificant quantity.

The extension does not erase the word’s original meaning.

The qiṭmīr’s smallness underlies the emphatic negation.

Likewise, Persian “not even the size of a needle’s tip” need not actually concern a needle.

The tangible image remains present, making the minute amount imaginable to the hearer.

A Witness Attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt

The familiar Masāʾil Nāfiʿ ibn al-Azraq collection attributes a verse to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt:

لَمْ أَنَلْ مِنْهُمْ فَسِيطًا وَلَا زَبَدًا
وَلَا فُوفَةً وَلَا قِطْمِيرَا

lam anal minhum fasīṭan wa-lā zabadan / wa-lā fūfatan wa-lā qiṭmīrā

Rendering following the quoted vocalization: “From them I obtained neither a nail clipping, nor froth, nor a delicate membrane, nor a qiṭmīr.” Fasīṭ lexically denotes a nail clipping or a small date part. Zabad, with a on the b, is froth; zabd, with an unvoweled b, means a gift or contribution. The overall point is that the speaker received no benefit.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: fasīṭ in the poetic witness

Some versions have fawqah instead of fūfah. Here fūfah has u on its first consonant, following the lexical vocalization for the thin covering.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the vocalization fūfah and the thin membrane

The textual difference matters because the forms and lexical meanings differ.

Lisān al-ʿArab: zabad and zabd

Several names for slight things appear together in the verse.

Qiṭmīr concludes the list, expressing the speaker’s deprivation of any benefit.

The use is semantically close to Qur’anic mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīrin.

Both place a minute thing’s name in a negative sentence.

The witness’s historical status nevertheless requires caution.

Umayyah lived around Islam’s emergence, but poems attributed to him were collected in later sources, with differing degrees of authenticity.

Masāʾil Nāfiʿ ibn al-Azraq likewise comprises lexical reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās; its compilation history and the attribution of individual parts require independent study.

The verse consequently attests recorded qiṭmīr usage in Arabic poetic and lexical tradition. Without independent evidence it should not be presented as an established, certainly pre-Qur’anic witness.

Masāʾil Nāfiʿ ibn al-Azraq: attribution to Umayyah and fawqah variant

From a Small Object to an Insignificant Amount

The relationship between tangible meaning and emphatic usage is clear.

The date-stone membrane is very thin and slight compared with the fruit or stone.

Used of ownership, benefit, or entitlement, it conveys that even the smallest amount is intended.

It does not thereby become a formal measurement unit.

The examined sources assign it no standard weight, length, or volume.

It should therefore not be treated like a mithqāl, dirham, or cubit as an exact measure.

In these expressions it is a tangible example of insignificance, rather than a fixed numerical standard.

Nor must it be the smallest possible object in the universe.

Its smallness in human experience and Arabic language suffices to emphasize a negligible amount.

4. Qur’anic Use in Fāṭir 35:13

Q Ṭ M R occurs only once in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole occurrence in Fāṭir 35:13

Fāṭir 35:13

يُولِجُ اللَّيْلَ فِي النَّهَارِ وَيُولِجُ النَّهَارَ فِي اللَّيْلِ وَسَخَّرَ الشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ كُلٌّ يَجْرِي لِأَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى ذَٰلِكُمُ اللَّهُ رَبُّكُمْ لَهُ الْمُلْكُ وَالَّذِينَ تَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِهِ مَا يَمْلِكُونَ مِنْ قِطْمِيرٍ

yūliju al-layla fī al-nahāri wa-yūliju al-nahāra fī al-layli wa-sakhkhara al-shamsa wa-l-qamara kullun yajrī li-ajalin musamman dhālikumu allāhu rabbukum lahu al-mulku wa-alladhīna tadʿūna min dūnihi mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīrin

Forqan’s existing Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“He causes the night to enter the day and the day to enter the night, and has subjected the sun and moon, each running until an appointed term. That is The God, your Lord; sovereignty belongs to Him. Those you invoke apart from Him do not own even a thin date-stone membrane.”

Qiṭmīrin in the Sentence

Qiṭmīrin is a singular, indefinite noun in the genitive.

It follows the preposition min.

Mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīrin begins with negation and denies ownership.

In such Arabic constructions, min can emphasize and universalize the negation.

The sentence therefore does more than deny the invoked beings’ ownership of one particular qiṭmīr.

It denies them ownership even to the extent of such an insignificant thing.

Persian can consequently use ḥattā, “even,” to convey the emphasis.

“They do not own even a thin date-stone membrane” naturally conveys the sentence.

“Even” does not independently translate one Arabic word; it expresses the emphatic negative construction with min.

Qiṭmīr itself, conversely, does not mean “nothing.”

It names a small part used here to emphasize the absence of even the smallest amount.

Lahu al-Mulku and Mā Yamlikūna

The verse begins with night and day entering one another and the sun and moon being subjected.

It then says dhālikumu allāhu rabbukum lahu al-mulku.

This attributes sovereignty and ownership to The God.

Immediately afterward it denies ownership to those invoked apart from Him.

The two parts consequently form a clear contrast.

On one side is The God, whose command encompasses night, day, and the movement of sun and moon.

On the other are those people invoke apart from Him, whose claimed position entails no ownership even of a qiṭmīr.

The word’s selection has rhetorical importance.

After sun, moon, and sovereignty, the verse mentions a minute date-stone part.

The contrast between comprehensive sovereignty and the qiṭmīr’s insignificance highlights the alleged deities’ inability.

Qiṭmīr itself nevertheless does not mean sovereignty or power.

It remains the small part’s name; ownership and power emerge from yamlikūna and context.

Al-Ṭabarī: sovereignty and denial of ownership even of a stone covering

What Does Denying Ownership Mean?

The verse concerns those invoked min dūnihi, apart from Him.

That qualification must be preserved.

It concerns the inability of deities and supports invoked in opposition to, or instead of, The God.

It should not be detached from context to claim that no human possesses property, usage rights, or lawful authority over anything in worldly life.

Other Qur’anic verses discuss human possessions, inheritance, transactions, rights, and financial responsibilities.

These need not conflict with all beings’ dependence on The God.

Fāṭir 35:13 principally concerns ownership and sovereignty claimed in the position of a deity or independent support apart from The God.

It denies that position to those invoked apart from Him.

The context can therefore convey denial of independent ownership and sovereignty equal to The God’s.

Direct translation should nevertheless retain “they do not own,” discussing independence or dependence within contextual analysis.

Study of M L K

Why Qiṭmīr?

The word draws on a tangible reality.

Someone familiar with dates and their stones can imagine how slight the delicate covering is.

Naming something minute conveys lack of ownership in tangible terms.

Simply saying those deities have no power would convey the general idea.

Qiṭmīr nevertheless supplies a concrete image: beings elevated to deity and support in opposition to The God lack ownership even of a date-stone membrane.

The word thus serves the verse’s monotheistic argument.

It does not follow that Arabic qiṭmīr is inherently a symbol of associating partners with The God.

Other lexical expressions also use it for something insignificant.

The verse draws on that semantic field, while association and ownership arise from the sentence and Qur’anic context.

Connection with Fāṭir 35:14

The following Fāṭir 35:14 describes the same invoked beings’ inability to hear supplication and answer it.

The discussion consequently goes beyond possessing goods or economic ownership.

Fāṭir 35:13 denies their independent ownership and sovereignty; Fāṭir 35:14 connects this with hearing and answering human requests.

The argument about reliance on and invocation of others apart from The God continues.

Together they show qiṭmīr in Fāṭir 35:13 expressing lack of power and ownership in the claimed position.

Every issue involving supplication, worship, or association should nevertheless not be imported into qiṭmīr’s lexical meaning.

The word itself names something minute.

Its role in the Qur’anic argument emerges from the ownership verb and surrounding verses.

Connection with Sabaʾ 34:22

Sabaʾ 34:22 likewise concerns beings claimed and invoked apart from The God.

It denies them even a speck’s worth of ownership in heaven and earth, then denies their partnership in sovereignty and their supporting The God.

This agrees in direction with Fāṭir 35:13.

Dharrah and qiṭmīr nevertheless differ.

Early dharrah can denote something very small; qiṭmīr’s familiar meaning is a thin date-stone membrane.

Their physical referents should therefore not be equated.

Nor does juxtaposing the verses establish qiṭmīr as necessarily smaller or larger than dharrah.

Both deny ownership of even a negligible amount.

Their tangible images nevertheless differ.

5. Distinguishing Qiṭmīr from Fatīl, Naqīr, and Related Words

Qiṭmīr and Fatīl

Fatīl belongs to F T L and familiarly denotes a fine thread in a date stone’s groove.

Some lexical explanations compare it with a thin strand formed by twisting delicate material.

Qur’anic fatīl occurs three times: Al-Nisāʾ 4:49, Al-Nisāʾ 4:77, and Al-Isrāʾ 17:71.

All three deny injustice even to that minute extent.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: all three fatīl occurrences

Fatīl and qiṭmīr can consequently both express a negligible amount.

Their referents nevertheless differ.

The familiar qiṭmīr is the thin membrane; fatīl is a fine thread in the groove or inner part of the stone.

They should not be substituted for one another without evidence.

Other fatīl explanations include a minute thing produced by rubbing or twisting the fingers.

Even fatīl should therefore not be restricted to one physical stone part without examining all the evidence.

Qiṭmīr and Naqīr

Naqīr belongs to N Q R.

In date-stone usage, it names a small depression or spot on the surface.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:53 describes people who, if they possessed a share of sovereignty, would not give others even a naqīr.

A minute thing’s name again emphasizes a negligible amount.

Naqīr and qiṭmīr nevertheless have distinct tangible meanings.

Naqīr concerns a depression or spot; the familiar qiṭmīr is the thin membrane.

Some sources also define qiṭmīr as the white spot or groove. The overlap reflects disagreement over precise referents.

That disagreement does not make all these words exact synonyms.

Each has its own name and usage history.

Are Qiṭmīr, Fatīl, and Naqīr Measurement Units?

General explanations sometimes group the three as small date-stone parts.

That comparison helps identify tangible Arabic images.

It should nevertheless not become a formal measurement system.

The Qur’an provides no table ranking them by weight, volume, or size.

Nor can one securely identify one as the smallest and another as the largest Qur’anic quantity unit.

The clear point is their use for negligible amounts.

Fāṭir 35:13 uses such an amount to deny ownership.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:49, Al-Nisāʾ 4:77, and Al-Isrāʾ 17:71 use it to deny injustice.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:53 uses the same device to describe withholding even the smallest benefit.

Each verse’s sentence structure determines the word’s function.

Qiṭmīr and Dharrah

Arabic dharrah denotes something very small.

The Qur’an mentions a dharrah’s weight in contexts of reckoning, deeds, and ownership.

Qiṭmīr can likewise express a negligible amount, but names a particular date-stone part.

Their tangible meanings consequently differ.

Qiṭmīr should not become “a speck” unless only general insignificance is being explained and its precise meaning is separately clarified.

Direct translation more accurately preserves the date-stone membrane image.

Study of DH R R

Qiṭmīr and Qishr

Arabic qishr is a skin or outer covering.

The familiar qiṭmīr is a particular thin covering belonging to a date stone.

The relationship is therefore general to specific.

Every qiṭmīr in this sense is a qishr, but not every qishr is a qiṭmīr.

Fruit skin, tree bark, and other coverings can be qishr, whereas qiṭmīr concerns the particular date-stone part.

The general equivalent “covering” alone is consequently insufficient to identify it precisely.

“The thin covering of a date stone” is more specific.

Qiṭmīr and Q Ṭ M

Arabic also has triliteral Q Ṭ M, with uses involving biting, cutting, and strong appetite.

It shares Q Ṭ M R’s first three letters.

Shared letters nevertheless do not sufficiently establish derivation of qiṭmīr from it.

One cannot simply derive qiṭmīr from qaṭm, cutting, because the membrane tears when the stone is removed.

The image may be attractive without constituting an established historical etymology.

Qur’anic study should therefore examine Q Ṭ M R in its own recorded structure.

6. Conclusions and Translation Proposal

In its familiar, widely supported Arabic meaning, qiṭmīr denotes the very thin date-stone covering.

It lies on the stone and may be visible when stone and fruit flesh are separated.

Lexicons record two other meanings: the stone’s groove and its white spot.

Their precise referents differ, but all concern a small, slight date-stone feature.

Arabic also records the nearby form qiṭmār.

Spoken and poetic qiṭmīr can express a negligible amount or receiving no benefit.

That use does not make it a formal weight or size unit.

The family occurs only once in the Qur’an, as qiṭmīrin in Fāṭir 35:13.

After divine sovereignty, the verse denies those invoked apart from The God ownership even of a qiṭmīr.

The thin date-stone covering supplies a tangible example of the slightest amount.

Mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīrin emphasizes comprehensive negation: they possess no such slight share of the ownership claimed.

Translating it merely as “nothing” would lose the precise tangible image.

Nor does qiṭmīr itself mean power, sovereignty, or association; those concepts come from the ownership verb and context.

Brief root meaning: The very thin date-stone covering; emphatically, an example of something minute and insignificant. Lexicons also record the stone’s groove and white spot as alternative referents.

Meaning of Qur’anic qiṭmīrin: the thin date-stone membrane, used in Fāṭir 35:13 to deny even the slightest amount of ownership.

Proposed word translation: “the thin covering of a date stone.”

Translation of the Qur’anic phrase:

وَالَّذِينَ تَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِهِ مَا يَمْلِكُونَ مِنْ قِطْمِيرٍ

wa-alladhīna tadʿūna min dūnihi mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīrin

“Those you invoke apart from Him do not own even a thin date-stone membrane.”

This renders Forqan’s existing Persian translation, preserving the tangible meaning and emphatic sentence.

The principal finding is that qiṭmīr names a real, minute object used in Qur’anic language to express the slightest share of ownership.

Fāṭir 35:13 contrasts divine sovereignty’s scope with the inability of those people invoke apart from Him.

Its selection consequently suits the argument.

That suitability does not make qiṭmīr inherently a religious or ethical concept.

It remains the thin date-stone membrane; denying the slightest ownership emerges from negation and the contrast with divine sovereignty.

Preserving the distinction allows faithful translation and explains the Qur’anic argument without unsupported additions.
