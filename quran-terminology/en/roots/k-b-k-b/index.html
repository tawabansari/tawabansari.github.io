1. Root Meaning and Word Structure

Arabic kabkaba means overturning, toppling, and casting something or someone down, especially when the fall involves rolling, turning over, or things landing upon one another.

Early usages concern throwing someone into a pit or chasm, overturning objects, and tumbling them together. Kabkabah also concerns a gathered or accumulated group of people or animals in some Arabic usages.

The family occurs only once in the Qur’an, as fa-kubkibū, describing groups cast headlong into the Blazing Fire.

Lisān al-ʿArab states: “كَبَّ الشَّيْءَ يَكُبُّهُ، وَكَبْكَبَهُ: قَلَبَهُ”: kabba or kabkaba a thing means overturning it.

The same source transmits Al-Zajjāj’s explanation of kubkibū: “طُرِحَ بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَى بَعْضٍ”: some were thrown upon others.

Lisān al-ʿArab: overturning and Al-Zajjāj

These explanations identify two related but distinct features.

First, overturning and losing the previous position, as when a vessel is turned over or a person falls to the ground.

Second, falling and landing upon one another, potentially accompanied by rolling and disorderly gathering.

Kabkabah should therefore not be reduced to simple movement between places.

Gently lifting something and placing it elsewhere without overturning would not necessarily be called kabkabah.

Its image involves toppling, falling, turning over, or tumbling together.

Not every use nevertheless specifies a fall from a defined height. Something may overturn on the ground or its parts fall upon one another without the sentence mentioning an elevation.

Quadriliteral Structure

K B K B is a quadriliteral root with a repeated structure. Kabkaba follows faʿlala, repeating K B.

Its repeated letters resemble those in zalzala, waswasa, and damdama.

In some Arabic repeated structures, repetition can emphasize intensity, continuity, or repeated occurrence.

Written form alone nevertheless cannot establish that interpretation for every quadriliteral verb.

For kabkaba, lexical explanations themselves connect the action with repeated or successive overturning.

Something thrown into a chasm may, for example, roll and turn over several times before reaching the bottom.

That image helps explain kabkaba.

It does not make every occurrence necessarily an endless fall or unlimited repetition.

The verb may emphasize the intensity and manner of toppling without specifying its stages.

2. K B K B, K B B, and Disputed Root Structure

Kabba: Overturning and Casting to the Ground

Arabic kabba belongs to doubled triliteral K B B.

Kabba al-shayʾa means overturning something.

Kabba al-ināʾa, for example, means turning a vessel over.

Kabbahu ʿalā wajhihi describes throwing someone face down.

Direction matters: the person leaves an upright or secure position and falls upon the face.

Inkabba likewise concerns overturning or bending over something in some uses.

Akabba ʿalā al-shayʾi can mean applying oneself intently to something.

The person has not necessarily fallen: the image is of bending over a task and directing attention toward it.

K B B thus extends from tangible overturning into bodily posture and concentrated application to a task.

That figurative application should not transfer into Qur’anic kubkibū without evidence.

The verse concerns being cast into the Blazing Fire, where tangible toppling fits more clearly.

Distinguishing Kabba and Kabkaba

In its principal use, kabba can describe overturning or casting to the ground.

Lexical kabkaba connects with more forceful overturning, rolling, or throwing things upon one another.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains kabkabah as something rolling and turning over after being thrown into a pit or chasm, until it settles.

He relates that to repeated falling and overturning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: tumbling into a pit until settling

Their imagery makes the distinction understandable.

Kabb can convey overturning itself; kabkabah has greater capacity to depict tumbling, falling together, and successive overturning.

That does not make kabba always a single occurrence or kabkaba necessarily repeated in every context.

Morphology alone supplies no exact number of occurrences.

Is K B K B an Independent Root?

Early grammarians disagreed over kabkaba’s historical structure.

One analysis treats all four K B K B letters as root consonants and the verb as an independent quadriliteral.

Another treats kabkaba as an expanded kabba, with repetition of a letter or structure intensifying the meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī identifies كببوا as the underlying form when explaining كبكبوا and discusses a repeated kāf. This reports his morphological analysis and should not silently be replaced by another form.

Al-Ṭabarī: morphological analysis and kabkabah

Al-Zajjāj likewise interprets kabkabah as repeated kabb.

Al-Samīn al-Ḥalabī’s Al-Durr al-Maṣūn transmits three morphological positions.

One derives the repeated structure from the simpler verb.

The Basran position treats all four letters as root consonants.

The Kufan position posits kabbaba, with three bāʾs, as the underlying kabkaba form, treating the third consonant kāf as a replacement for the middle bāʾ.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn: three analyses and pronoun reference

The semantic relationship between kabb and kabkaba may be clear without agreement on their precise morphological history.

Two questions should therefore be distinguished.

First, kabkaba’s established Arabic meaning involving overturning, tumbling, and casting down.

Second, its historical derivation from kabba or status as an independent quadriliteral.

The first is clear and documented for Qur’anic interpretation even if the historical analysis remains disputed.

3. Semantic Branches: Toppling, Tumbling, and Gathering

Kabkaba al-Shayʾa: Tumbling Parts Together

Kabkaba al-shayʾa describes something whose parts overturn or accumulate upon one another.

It concerns both an object’s displacement and a changed relationship among its parts.

Kabkabah can, for example, describe objects collapsing so that some land upon others.

That directly suits Al-Zajjāj’s explanation of kubkibū.

Overturning and gathering nevertheless differ.

Something may overturn without accumulating with other objects.

Conversely, objects may be stacked gently and orderly without overturning.

Some kabkabah usages bring both together: parts gather through falling and turning over.

That does not justify translating it simply as “gathering” in every context.

Kabkaba as Casting into a Chasm

The Cairo Arabic Language Academy explains kabkaba fulānan as overturning and throwing someone to the ground.

Kabkabahu fī al-huwwati concerns throwing someone into a pit or chasm.

Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ, Cairo Arabic Language Academy: kabkaba

Huwwah is a hollow or deep place into which something falls.

That helps explain fa-kubkibū fīhā, which likewise uses a passive verb and fī for being cast into the Blazing Fire.

Kabkaba itself nevertheless specifies neither the chasm’s depth nor the fall’s distance.

No definite height, depth, or duration should therefore be extracted from it.

Kabkabah: A Gathered Group

Al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab record kabkabah, كَبْكَبَة, for a group of horses. Some lexical transmissions also give kubkubah, كُبْكُبَة; the group noun’s vocalization is consequently not uniform.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: kabkabah as a group of horses

Kubbat al-khayl names a group or large body of horses; kabkabah also appears in that field.

Another use, kabkaba al-mawāshiya, means gathering scattered livestock.

The verb then concerns collecting parts or individuals.

That branch approaches accumulation or falling together without being identical to them.

Gathering livestock need not involve overturning the animals.

It does not follow that Qur’anic fa-kubkibū means only the orderly gathering of groups.

Nor should gathering be erased from the word’s history: lexical sources record it.

Different groups sharing a fate in the verse connects with gathering, while the verb retains the image of being cast headlong in the context of the Blazing Fire.

Other Linguistic Forms

Principal forms include:

كَبْكَبَ — kabkaba: quadriliteral verb; overturned, tumbled, or cast someone into a pit.

يُكَبْكِبُ — yukabkibu: the same verb’s imperfect.

كَبْكَبَة — kabkabah: verbal noun for overturning or casting down. The word also denotes a group; some transmissions vocalize that sense as كُبْكُبَة, kubkubah.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: group noun vocalizations and the verb

تَكَبْكَبَ — takabkaba: rolled, turned over, or became tumbled together.

مُكَبْكِب — mukabkib: active participle, someone overturning or casting down.

مُكَبْكَب — mukabkab: passive participle, something or someone overturned or cast down.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records mutakabkiban fī thiyābihi, someone wrapped or gathered within his clothes.

That concerns gathering and wrapping rather than necessarily falling.

Kubakib and kubākib also describe someone with a large, compactly built body.

Such usages can be compared through gathering and density without deriving them all from falling into a chasm absent historical evidence.

Only passive fa-kubkibū occurs in the Qur’an.

Kabkab as a Place Name

Geographical sources and Arabic poetry also use Kabkab for a mountain or elevated place near Mecca.

The following verse is transmitted from Imruʾ al-Qays:

غَدَاةَ غَدَوْا فَسَالِكٌ بَطْنَ نَخْلَةٍ
وَآخَرُ مِنْهُمْ جَازِعٌ نَجْدَ كَبْكَبِ

ghadāta ghadaw fa-sālikun baṭna nakhlatin / wa-ākharu minhum jāziʿun najda kabkabi

“On the morning they set out, one was taking the valley of Nakhlah, and another of them was crossing the heights of Kabkab.”

Here Kabkab is a geographical name on a group of people’s route.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Imruʾ al-Qays and place name Kabkab

The sound sequence consequently appears in early poetry.

A place name does not itself attest the verb’s overturning sense.

Nor does the verse establish that the mountain was named for its rounded shape, steep slope, or danger of falling.

Such a claim requires historical naming evidence.

Kabkab as a place name should therefore be distinguished from kabkaba as a verb.

The poetic witnesses examined did not securely establish an independent pre-Qur’anic occurrence of kabkaba itself meaning “overturn,” with clear attribution and dating.

Early dictionaries nevertheless record the verb’s meanings, supported by Qur’anic use and nearby K B B forms.

4. Qur’anic Usage in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94

K B K B occurs only once in the Qur’an.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94

فَكُبْكِبُوا فِيهَا هُمْ وَالْغَاوُونَ

fa-kubkibū fīhā hum wa-l-ghāwūna

Forqan’s existing Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Then they and those astray are cast headlong into it;”

The Morphology and Syntax of Fa-Kubkibū

Fa-kubkibū comprises fa-, passive kubkibū, and an attached plural pronoun.

It is a quadriliteral passive perfect in the third-person masculine plural.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole quadriliteral verb in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94

Fa- connects the clause to preceding events.

The final plural wāw is the passive subject, identifying those upon whom the casting-down action falls.

Fīhā follows the verb.

Feminine hā refers back to al-jaḥīm in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:91.

The context therefore identifies where they are cast.

Hum follows, commonly analyzed as an emphatic pronoun reinforcing the verb’s plural wāw.

Wa-l-ghāwūna coordinates another group under the same predication.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:95 adds wa-junūdu iblīsa ajmaʿūna, all Iblīs’s forces, to the group.

Morphologically, the action is done to them; they are not the direct agents of overturning in this verse.

A passive perfect can also narrate a future resurrection scene as an event already realized within that scene.

Natural Persian may therefore use “were cast headlong” or, given the afterlife narrative, “are cast headlong.”

Kubkibū in This Context

Several lexical features come together here.

First, toppling and being overturned.

Second, falling into the Blazing Fire.

Third, several groups share a scene, potentially involving being tumbled together or landing upon one another.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the verb as casting some upon others.

Mujāhid offers tumbling and casting down; a report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās describes their gathering there.

Al-Ṭabarī: Mujāhid, Ibn ʿAbbās, and differing explanations

Those explanations are not three wholly unrelated meanings.

Groups cast into one place may overturn, land upon one another, and gather there.

Lexical imagery should nevertheless be distinguished from the verse’s certain details.

The verse supplies no number of overturnings.

Nor does it specify an individual’s exact height of fall or duration of rolling.

“Were cast headlong” or “were overturned and tumbled together” appropriately conveys the verb without adding stages or a count of falls.

Does Repeated Structure Mean Endless Punishment?

Some lexical explanations concern repeated overturning, as when something thrown into a pit tumbles several times before settling.

That image differs from a fall that never ends.

Ibn Fāris’s explanation expressly includes reaching a resting place after tumbling.

Kabkaba’s structure alone consequently does not establish that people in the Blazing Fire forever and uninterruptedly fall, roll, and fall again.

Such a claim needs independent evidence.

Structural repetition can emphasize the action’s quality without determining its duration or number of occurrences.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94 clearly conveys toppling and casting down, but supplies no exact duration.

5. Pronoun Reference and the Context around Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94

The Blazing Fire Is Exposed to Those Astray

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:91 exposes the Blazing Fire to al-ghāwīn.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:92 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:93 then ask where the beings they worshipped apart from The God are, and whether those beings can help them or defend themselves.

Following those questions, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94 describes hum wa-l-ghāwūna being cast headlong into the Blazing Fire.

That continuity matters for hum’s referent.

One reading refers the pronoun to the deities questioned in the preceding verses.

Those worshipped apart from The God are then cast headlong into the Blazing Fire together with their misguided worshippers.

The nearby sequence supports that reading: first the deities are questioned, then “they and those astray” are cast down.

Grammatically, a masculine plural pronoun can refer to deities their worshippers regarded as beings possessing will and power.

Commentaries also record another reading.

The pronoun then refers to the associators or followers themselves, while al-ghāwūn identifies another group of misleaders or followed objects of worship.

The precise identities of both sides are therefore disputed.

Both readings nevertheless agree that those involved in misguidance and worship apart from The God receive no mutual help in the scene and share a common fate.

That conclusion comes from context, not because kabkaba itself means worshipping or misleading.

Are Al-Ghāwūn the Same as Iblīs’s Forces?

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:95 mentions junūdu iblīsa ajmaʿūna.

That introduces another group within the same scene.

Some exegetical explanations connect al-ghāwūn with devils.

The word itself nevertheless does not restrict its referents to nonhuman beings.

Qur’anic ghawā and its derivatives concern straying from the right course and can also describe humans.

Iblīs’s forces can, conversely, designate those following his course and helping misguidance.

Identifying each group precisely requires examining the relevant verses in full.

Al-Ṭabarī: pronoun readings, al-ghāwūn, and Iblīs’s forces

A lexical study of K B K B need not collapse all these designations into one group.

The verb itself describes the indicated groups being cast headlong.

Conflict after Being Cast Down

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:96 describes people disputing with one another in the Blazing Fire.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:97, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:98, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:99, they discuss their error and equating others with the Lord of all worlds.

That continuation matters.

Groups bound on earth through worship, following, or misleading encounter conflict and reproach in the afterlife scene instead of helping or saving one another.

The conflict nevertheless is not kubkibū’s lexical meaning.

Kabkabah concerns the action of casting headlong.

Dispute, reproach, and acknowledgment of misguidance appear in the following verses.

Kabkaba should therefore not directly become “collapse of religious relationships” or “exposure of leaders’ falsehood” in translation.

Such ideas may be inferred from the sequence without replacing the verb’s direct meaning.

Toppling and Losing Supposed Support

The preceding verses question the deities’ ability to help.

Those on whom people pinned hopes of support and power then appear in a scene of toppling and helplessness.

The thematic contrast is clear.

What was imagined on earth as support and superior power can neither help others here nor find an independently indicated escape.

Toppling consequently connects with the collapse of imagined power and superiority.

That contextual connection should nevertheless not become a lexical derivation.

Arabic kabkaba does not itself mean “losing social status” or “a belief becoming false.”

The word conveys overturning and casting down; context supplies the religious and ethical implications.

6. Distinguishing Kabkaba from Related Words

Kabkaba and Kabba

Kabba concerns overturning and throwing to the ground.

Kabkaba closely overlaps, but lexical usages can emphasize rolling, repeated overturning, or some falling upon others.

Al-Naml 27:90 uses kubbat wujūhuhum fī al-nār.

That concerns being cast down and falling upon the face.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94 uses quadriliteral kubkibū.

Both approach casting into fire, while differing in structure.

Al-Mulk 67:22 also uses mukibban ʿalā wajhihi for walking bent down or upon one’s face, contrasted with upright walking on a straight path.

The K B B family can therefore also appear in figurative images.

That does not make Al-Shuʿarāʾ’s kubkibū necessarily exclusively ethical or nonphysical.

Each verse requires its own structure and context.

Kabkaba and Ṭaraḥa

Arabic ṭaraḥa concerns throwing or casting something away.

Some contexts may yield a result resembling kabkabah.

Not every throwing action, however, involves overturning or tumbling.

Something may be placed gently somewhere or moved away from oneself.

Kabkaba more prominently conveys toppling and tumbling together.

Ṭarḥ can explain the result without always conveying the full kabkabah image.

Kabkaba and Alqā

Alqā covers a broad range of casting and placing.

The Qur’an uses it for casting a staff, throwing objects, and other actions.

Kabkaba is narrower, involving toppling or rolling.

Rendering fa-kubkibū simply as “they were thrown” preserves the general falling idea but weakens overturning.

“They were cast headlong” is more precise.

Study of L Q Y

Kabkaba and Nakkasa

Nakkasa concerns overturning or lowering something from its former position.

Arabic tankīs can concern the head, body, or arrangement of something.

It overlaps semantically with kabkabah.

Nakkasa need not involve tumbling or throwing several objects upon one another.

Some kabkabah usages, by contrast, include that image.

They therefore cannot be exact synonyms in every construction.

Kabkaba and Ṣaraʿa

Ṣaraʿa concerns throwing a person to the ground or bringing someone down.

In wrestling or physical confrontation, one person may ṣaraʿa another.

Kabkaba likewise can concern throwing down and toppling.

Ṣarʿ need not include rolling or falling into a pit.

Shared falling imagery does not erase differences in the action’s manner.

Kabkaba and Dahwara

Arabic dahwara concerns rolling and casting something down.

Commentaries explain kubkibū with duhwirū.

Their images of movement and falling are close.

Explaining one word through another does not make all their uses identical.

Dahwarah can emphasize rolling, while kabkabah additionally concerns overturning and gathering in other usages.

The preferred meaning therefore depends on context.

Kabkaba and Jamaʿa

Jamaʿa means gathering or bringing things together.

Some lexical kabkabah usages likewise concern gathering.

Not every gathering is kabkabah.

Objects may be arranged orderly, whereas kabkabah in falling contexts depicts tumbling together and overturning.

“They were gathered” may explain part of Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94’s result without conveying the verb’s full image.

Study of J M ʿ

Kabkaba and Kawkab

Arabic kawkab names a celestial body and occurs in Qur’anic contexts involving stars and heavenly bodies.

Kawkab and kabkaba resemble one another in form.

Some lexical explanations also use kawkab al-shayʾi for a thing’s great or prominent body.

Shared letters and gathering senses nevertheless do not establish derivation of either from the other.

The Qur’anic index records separate families.

Kubkibū in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94 consequently cannot be taken as becoming stars, celestial motion, or falling stars on that basis.

7. Conclusions and Translation Proposal

Arabic K B K B is a repeated quadriliteral root.

Its principal use involves toppling, overturning, tumbling, and casting down.

Some lexical usages also concern gathering or accumulating groups of people, horses, and other things.

Turning over, tumbling together, and gathering can connect these branches.

They should nevertheless not all be reduced to an exceedingly broad meaning such as movement or gathering.

The Qur’anic use more specifically conveys being cast headlong and tumbled together.

Grammarians disagreed over kabkaba’s history: some regard it as an expansion and repetition of kabba; others treat all four consonants as root letters.

That dispute does not fundamentally change the verb’s recorded Qur’anic meaning.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as fa-kubkibū in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94.

The verb is passive perfect, third-person masculine plural.

It describes groups being cast headlong into the Blazing Fire.

Preceding verses ask about beings worshipped apart from The God and question their ability to help worshippers or defend themselves.

They and those astray are then cast headlong; Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:95 adds Iblīs’s forces.

Lexically, kubkibū depicts falling, overturning, and possibly people landing upon one another.

The verb supplies no count, duration, or precise manner of falling.

Nor should it be reduced to “they were gathered”: toppling has clearer support in this context.

Brief root meaning: Casting headlong, overturning, and tumbling; in some uses, casting down and tumbling things upon one another.

Meaning of Qur’anic fa-kubkibū: then they were cast headlong and tumbled together; in context, into the Blazing Fire.

Proposed word translation: “then they were cast headlong.”

Forqan’s existing Persian translation of Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94, rendered here in English:

“Then they and those astray are cast headlong into it;”

It preserves the verb’s principal meaning without importing additional images specifying the number of overturnings or the fall’s precise mechanics.

Lexical explanation can also discuss rolling, tumbling together, and some falling upon others: early sources document these senses.

Direct translation nevertheless more clearly and naturally uses “being cast headlong.”

The principal finding is that Qur’anic kabkabah conveys being cast headlong and falling through a vivid image of overturning and tumbling together.

The word itself means neither sin, association, misguidance, nor collapse of a belief system.

Those ideas come from context and the situation of those cast down.

Across Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:91, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:92, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:93, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:95, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:96, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:97, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:98, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:99, people who relied on power and help apart from The God face a scene where those supports cannot save them.

Toppling in that context connects with the collapse of imagined superiority and reliability in those deities.

The contextual conclusion should nevertheless not replace the verb’s lexical meaning.

Kubkibū conveys being cast headlong; surrounding verses identify who is involved, in what position, and with what outcome.
