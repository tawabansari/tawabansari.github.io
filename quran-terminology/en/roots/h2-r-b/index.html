Harab: fleeing and withdrawing to escape danger, difficulty, or an unwanted situation

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Hā–rāʾ–bāʾ denotes fleeing and moving away from someone or something. Haraba, yahrubu, haraban describes a person or living creature seeking, through withdrawal, to get beyond danger, pursuit, or a situation in which it does not wish to remain.

Someone facing a powerful enemy may flee if confrontation seems unsuitable. An animal sensing a hunter may likewise run away.

In both cases, movement is more than ordinary travel between places: the person or animal seeks distance from something and departure from the previous situation.

Early Arabic dictionaries explain harab through firār, flight. They also state that the usage includes animals as well as humans.

Fear, however, is not indispensable to every derivative. Some lexical uses of ahraba describe hastening away, with or without fear. The action, its motive, and the fugitive’s emotional state must therefore be distinguished.

One person may flee for fear of death, another to avoid pursuit or an unwanted responsibility. Context establishes their motives; the root alone does not.

Evidence from early Arabic

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād contains:

إِذْ أَقْبَلَتْ حَدَقُ الْفُرْسَانِ تَرْمُقُنِي

idh aqbalat ḥadaqu al-fursāni tarmuqunī

وَكُلُّ مِقْدَامِ حَرْبٍ مَالَ لِلْهَرَبِ

wa-kullu miqdāmi ḥarbin māla li-al-harab

“When the cavalry’s eyes turn toward me, even a warrior foremost in battle inclines toward flight.”

The poet boasts of his martial prowess, claiming that even the boldest fighters consider fleeing when they face him.

Harab here means withdrawing from confrontation to escape danger.

Māla li-al-harab shows that flight can begin with inclination or decision: someone may turn toward escape before actually moving. This comes from the whole expression; harab itself does not mean deciding.

The poem appears in collections attributed to ʿAntarah, but this review has not independently established its attribution or date. The line therefore cannot serve as conclusive evidence of pre-Quranic usage.

Flight is clearly attested in the Quran itself, a direct witness to Arabic at the time of revelation. Later dictionaries record the same basic sense. The established early Arabic meaning can thus be described without overstating the word’s origin or the certain antiquity of a poetic witness.

A historically defensible definition: Harab is fleeing and moving away from a person, danger, or situation from which the fugitive seeks release. Fear, pursuit, and haste may accompany it, but context determines motive and outcome.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded Arabic forms include:

  • هَرَبَ: fled.
  • يَهْرُبُ: flees.
  • هَرَب: flight, fleeing.
  • هُرُوب: another verbal noun meaning fleeing.
  • هَرَبَان: a recorded verbal noun of fleeing.
  • هَارِب: one who flees; a fugitive.
  • مَهْرَب: a place or route of escape, or refuge from danger.
  • أَهْرَبَ: hastened away or made earnest efforts to flee.
  • إِهْرَاب: the Form IV verbal noun, with senses corresponding to its verb.
  • هَرَّبَ: made another flee or caused someone’s escape.
  • تَهْرِيب: causing or facilitating flight.

Not all these forms occur in the Quran. Its sole form from this family is haraban.

Semantic core

Three elements can be identified in the principal use of this root.

First, a person or creature withdraws from a situation. The movement may be rapid, but not every rapid movement is flight.

Second, distance is sought from something within whose reach the fugitive does not wish to remain: an enemy, danger, pursuer, or difficult situation.

Third, there is an effort to escape that situation.

Crucially, fleeing and attaining safety are not identical.

Someone may flee an enemy and survive, or flee and later be caught. Flight occurs in either case.

Success or failure is therefore not part of harab’s necessary definition. The subsequent event or statement supplies the outcome.

Does harab always mean rapid movement?

Flight often involves haste. Someone escaping immediate danger usually seeks distance as quickly as possible.

Speed alone, however, does not fully define the root.

A person may run quickly toward a destination without fleeing. Conversely, someone may leave slowly and secretly to escape pursuit.

What places an action within harab’s field is its direction and the intention to leave the unwanted situation, rather than speed alone.

Tahrīb and later usage

Harraba can mean making someone flee or causing another’s escape.

In contemporary Arabic, and some Persian usage, tahrīb also appears in expressions for the clandestine and unlawful movement of goods, taking people across borders, or removing someone from the reach of the law.

These later legal and economic applications do not define the root in Quranic Arabic.

Smuggling goods, for example, may involve concealment, border crossing, and legal violations. None of these features should be transferred to Quranic haraban.

3. Quranic usage: Al-Jinn 72:12

The root’s sole Quranic occurrence is in Al-Jinn.

Al-Jinn 72:12

وَأَنَّا ظَنَنَّا أَنْ لَنْ نُعْجِزَ اللَّهَ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَنْ نُعْجِزَهُ هَرَبًا

wa-annā ẓanannā an lan nuʿjiza allāha fī al-arḍi wa-lan nuʿjizahu harabā

Al-Jinn 72:12

“And we came to know that we can never render The God powerless on earth, nor can we render Him powerless by fleeing.”

The speakers are a group of jinn describing their hearing of the Quran and a change in their understanding and belief.

The surah’s opening verses recount their hearing of a wondrous Quran that guides toward right understanding and direction. They then speak of belief, abandoning association with God, and their new recognition of Him.

Al-Jinn 72:11 explains that they are not all alike: some are righteous, while others are otherwise.

Against this background, Al-Jinn 72:12 acknowledges their inability in relation to God.

The meaning of haraban

Haraban is an accusative verbal noun of haraba, yahrubu.

The independent verb haraba does not occur here. The Quran does not report that they fled; it places the verbal noun beside wa-lan nuʿjizahu.

One recognized syntactic analysis treats the verbal noun as a circumstantial expression, equivalent to:

“وَلَنْ نُعْجِزَهُ هَارِبِينَ”

“Nor can we render Him powerless while fleeing.”

In Persian, “by fleeing” conveys this naturally.

Haraban thus expresses the manner or state of attempting to get away.

It does not itself mean rendering powerless. That meaning belongs to nuʿjizahu; haraban adds the circumstance of flight.

Nuʿjiza and nuʿjizahu: getting beyond reach

Nuʿjiza belongs to Form IV of ʿayn–jīm–zāy.

The construction concerns rendering another unable, or getting beyond that party’s power and reach.

If someone escapes a pursuer so completely that the pursuer cannot catch them, the pursuer has become unable to reach that person.

In Al-Jinn 72:12, however, the speakers acknowledge that this is impossible in relation to God.

They cannot reach a place on earth beyond His power, nor can flight prevent Him from reaching them or exercising His power.

This concerns creatures’ limited capacity before God. It does not depict God as a bodily being who must traverse a particular route to catch fugitives.

“Rendering God powerless” is framed from the perspective of the fugitives’ effort and capacity: nothing they do can limit His power.

Fī al-arḍ and haraban

Two expressions follow one another:

“لَنْ نُعْجِزَ اللَّهَ فِي الْأَرْضِ”

“وَلَنْ نُعْجِزَهُ هَرَبًا”

In the first, fī al-arḍ concerns the speakers’ location and situation.

In the second, haraban expresses flight.

The two expressions therefore do more than repeat one thing in different words.

First comes their inability to escape God’s power on earth; then the statement that even attempted flight cannot change that condition.

Imagine someone seeking to escape a ruler’s territory. Crossing a border might place them beyond that ruler’s jurisdiction, because human power depends on particular circumstances, resources, and territory.

The verse, however, concerns God, whose power is not confined to an earthly ruler’s domain.

This illustration distinguishes limited human power from divine power; it does not posit a spatial boundary or bodily mechanism for God.

Ẓanannā: a change in understanding

The verse begins with ẓanannā.

From ẓāʾ–nūn–nūn, this word can express supposition or firm conviction in the Quran, depending on context.

Here the jinn’s acknowledgment of their inability before God forms part of the understanding they gained after hearing guidance.

Al-Jinn 72:13 describes their belief after hearing that guidance.

Ẓanannā thus concerns recognizing and accepting a truth about God’s power.

The meanings of harab and ẓann must nevertheless remain distinct: one concerns flight, the other the speakers’ understanding, supposition, or belief.

The connection with Al-Jinn 72:22

Later in the surah, Muhammad is instructed to declare that no one can protect him against God and that he will find no refuge apart from Him.

Al-Jinn 72:22 clearly connects with the theme of Al-Jinn 72:12.

In the earlier verse, jinn acknowledge that flight cannot take them beyond God’s power.

In the later verse, Muhammad likewise declares that no creature can protect him against God.

The inability to escape divine power is thus not confined to one group of creatures.

Flight and refuge are nonetheless different: flight is withdrawal, while refuge is a place or person sought for safety and protection.

Someone may flee and find refuge, or leave without having any refuge available.

4. Comparison with related roots

Hā–rāʾ–bāʾ and fāʾ–rāʾ–rāʾ: harab and firār

These roots are plainly close in the field of fleeing and withdrawal.

Dictionaries define harab through firār. An absolute boundary claiming that one always involves fear and the other never does would therefore be unjustified.

Their Quranic uses nevertheless differ in scope.

Haraban in Al-Jinn 72:12 concerns the inability to flee beyond God’s power.

Fāʾ–rāʾ–rāʾ occurs in a wider range of Quranic situations.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:21, Moses describes fleeing after becoming afraid of those he addresses.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:16 discusses fleeing death or being killed, declaring that such flight will not provide lasting benefit.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:8 likewise describes the death people flee, which will ultimately meet them.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:50, by contrast, commands flight toward God:

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:50

فَفِرُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ إِنِّي لَكُمْ مِنْهُ نَذِيرٌ مُبِينٌ

fa-firrū ilā allāhi innī lakum minhu nadhīrun mubīn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:50

“So flee to The God; I am a clear warner to you from Him.”

This demonstrates that Quranic flight does not always mean abandoning truth or evading responsibility.

Sometimes people flee danger or oppression; elsewhere they are commanded to flee toward God.

The action’s value depends on its direction, object, and circumstances.

Neither harab nor firār alone therefore warrants calling every fugitive cowardly, sinful, or blameworthy.

Hā–rāʾ–bāʾ and hamzah–bāʾ–qāf: general flight and departure from dependency

Abaqa, from hamzah–bāʾ–qāf, was especially used in early Arabic for an enslaved person leaving and fleeing their owner.

The Quran applies the word to Jonah.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140

إِذْ أَبَقَ إِلَى الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ

idh abaqa ilā al-fulki al-mashḥūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140

“When he fled to the laden ship.”

Abaqa here describes Jonah’s departure toward the ship.

Haraba principally denotes flight from a situation without itself specifying the legal relationship of slavery or departure from a particular position of service.

Ibāq thus characterizes flight within a particular relationship in its prominent historical use, while harab is more general.

The legal details of slavery should not, however, all be transferred to abaqa as used of Jonah.

For a fuller comparison, see Forqan’s study of hamzah–bāʾ–qāf.

Hā–rāʾ–bāʾ and shīn–rāʾ–dāl: flight and dispersal

Shīn–rāʾ–dāl concerns fleeing, bolting, and departing from a place or group.

In animal contexts, sharada can describe bolting and running away.

In Al-Anfāl 8:57, sharrid occurs in warfare and concerns scattering and driving people away through a military encounter.

Haraba can likewise describe a person or animal fleeing danger.

Quranic sharrid directs an action at dispersing others, whereas haraban in Al-Jinn 72:12 describes the speakers’ own flight.

This distinction also depends on verbal construction and context; it does not make every derivative of shīn–rāʾ–dāl causative or every derivative of hā–rāʾ–bāʾ an individual act of flight.

Hā–rāʾ–bāʾ and nūn–jīm–wāw: fleeing and attaining safety

Najāh concerns deliverance from danger, difficulty, or harmful consequences.

Harab instead names the action of fleeing.

Flight may bring safety, but does not always do so.

Safety also need not result from flight: someone may remain in place and be rescued through assistance or the removal of danger.

This matters in Al-Jinn 72:12. The speakers do not say that no creature can ever escape any danger; they acknowledge their inability to get beyond God’s power.

Haraban should therefore not be translated as attaining safety, nor should this verse’s denial of escape become a denial of all deliverance and divine mercy.

Hā–rāʾ–bāʾ and ḥāʾ–rāʾ–bāʾ: flight and war

These roots look similar, but differ in their first letter and meaning: hāʾ in one, ḥāʾ in the other. Their letter order is not the distinguishing feature.

Hā–rāʾ–bāʾ concerns fleeing.

Ḥāʾ–rāʾ–bāʾ, in its familiar Quranic use, concerns war and armed hostility.

Some may attack and others flee in the same war, but this association does not make the roots identical.

Even the line attributed to ʿAntarah places ḥarb and harab together while preserving each word’s independent meaning.

5. Interpretive and translation challenges

Is harab always a sign of fear and cowardice?

No. Flight may arise from fear, but fear and cowardice are different concepts.

Someone fleeing real danger to protect themselves or others has not necessarily acted wrongly.

Withdrawal from fire, flood, or an unjust attack can lexically constitute flight, while its ethical value depends on circumstances and motive.

Conversely, someone may flee accountability or abandon an entrusted responsibility.

Harab alone therefore supplies no ethical judgment.

Does harab mean hiding?

Fleeing and hiding can coincide, but are not identical.

Someone may flee a place and then hide. Another may hide without leaving the place at all.

The central image in Al-Jinn 72:12 is flight and attempted withdrawal, rather than merely staying out of sight.

Replacing fleeing with hiding would therefore narrow the verse’s direct meaning.

Does the verse deny the possibility of leaving earth?

No. Fī al-arḍ in Al-Jinn 72:12 concerns the speakers’ inability to escape God’s power on earth.

The phrase itself makes no ruling on whether humans or other beings can travel beyond earth.

Nor does wa-lan nuʿjizahu haraban establish a particular limit on speed, travel distance, or technology.

The subject is divine power and creatures’ inability to escape it.

Does the inability to flee negate human choice?

No. God’s power over creatures must be distinguished from their capacity to choose and act.

In the same surah, jinn speak of hearing guidance and believing. Al-Jinn 72:14 distinguishes those who submit from those who deviate from right.

Inability to escape God’s power therefore does not mean that all creatures lack choice in their conduct.

They can choose different paths, but those choices neither abolish His power nor take them outside His sovereignty.

Again, contextual inference and lexical meaning must remain separate. Harab expresses flight; choice and responsibility emerge from the wider verses.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for established forms include:

هَرَبَ: fled; ran away.

يَهْرُبُ: flees; runs away.

هَرَب: flight; fleeing.

هُرُوب: fleeing; running away.

هَارِب: fugitive; one who flees.

مَهْرَب: place or route of escape.

أَهْرَبَ: hastened away or made earnest efforts to flee, depending on context.

هَرَّبَ: made another flee; facilitated escape.

تَهْرِيب: facilitating escape; in later usage, smuggling or clandestine unlawful transport.

For the sole Quranic form, haraban, the basic equivalent is fleeing.

In Al-Jinn 72:12, “by fleeing” fits the construction.

The proposed rendering is:

“And we came to know that we can never render The God powerless on earth, nor can we render Him powerless by fleeing.”

This preserves the verbal noun’s meaning and function in the sentence.

The Persian equivalent “by running away” also works, but “by fleeing” is shorter and more natural.

“By hiding,” “by travelling far away,” and “by attaining safety” are incomplete equivalents, each replacing flight with a different feature.

“We cannot escape God” can explain the sentence’s implication fluently, but does not directly reproduce its structure: the verse speaks of rendering God powerless and connects flight with that action.

7. Conclusion

Hā–rāʾ–bāʾ denotes fleeing and withdrawing from a person, danger, or unwanted situation.

Fear, haste, and pursuit can accompany the family’s uses, but should not automatically be treated as invariant meanings of every derivative.

Harab differs from ordinary departure, hiding, and attaining safety. Fleeing is withdrawal; safety is a possible result; hiding may accompany flight or occur separately.

The Quran uses only haraban. In Al-Jinn 72:12, it expresses the circumstance or manner of flight and, beside wa-lan nuʿjizahu, shows that flight cannot render God powerless.

The verse concerns creatures’ limited power before God, rather than condemning every flight or denying all deliverance.

Final definition for Forqan:

Hā–rāʾ–bāʾ denotes fleeing and moving away from someone or a situation to escape danger, pursuit, or unwanted conditions. Fear and haste may accompany it, but successful escape is not essential to its meaning.

In its sole Quranic occurrence, haraban means fleeing. In wa-lan nuʿjizahu haraban, it expresses attempted flight that cannot limit God’s power.
