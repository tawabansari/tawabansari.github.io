Idols and worshipped images: from objects made by human hands to their being treated as deities

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

In Arabic, صَنَم (ṣanam) denotes an image or object that people treat as a deity and worship. It may be made of stone, wood, metal, or other materials. Its plural is أَصْنَام (aṣnām), normally translated as “idols.”

Two features must be distinguished here: the form and material of the manufactured object, and the status people assign to it. An image may simply be an artwork or memorial; when it is treated as a deity, however, the description ṣanam applies to it.

The root Ṣ N M occurs five times in five Qur’anic verses. Every Qur’anic occurrence is the plural aṣnām:

  • أَصْنَامًا (aṣnāman): idols; Al-Anʿām 6:74 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:71.
  • أَصْنَامٍ (aṣnāmin): idols; Al-Aʿrāf 7:138.
  • الْأَصْنَامَ (al-aṣnāma): the idols; Ibrāhīm 14:35.
  • أَصْنَامَكُمْ (aṣnāmakum): your idols; Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:57.

Neither the singular ṣanam nor verbal derivatives of this family occur in the Qur’an. Its Qur’anic study therefore rests on the noun aṣnām and people’s conduct toward idols, rather than on an assumed verb meaning “to make” or “to carve.”

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five occurrences, all the plural aṣnām

2. Ṣanam in early Arabic

An image treated as a deity

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris presents ṣanam as a word without extensive derivational branches. He describes it as something made of wood, silver, or copper and worshipped.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise applies ṣanam to something people have adopted as a deity. Ibn Sīdah mentions carving an image from wood and fashioning it from silver and copper.

These witnesses connect ṣanam, in its familiar usage, with objects made by human hands. The material alone, however, does not determine its meaning.

A carved stone, metal image, or wooden statue is not inherently a deity. Its religious status and people’s treatment of it make the object a worshipped ṣanam.

Ṣanam therefore cannot be defined merely as “a statue” or “something carved.”

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: a manufactured object that is worshipped

Must a ṣanam have human form?

Some lexicographers restrict ṣanam to an image with a definite form. Certain explanations emphasize human form; others also allow an animal’s form or that of another embodied being.

The early sources, however, do not agree on restricting ṣanam to one particular shape.

What is clearer in its familiar usage is the presence of an object or image made an object of worship.

It should therefore not be claimed that a ṣanam must resemble a human being. Equally, an honored object or something a person loves should not be called a ṣanam without supporting evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: ṣanam and differing accounts of form and material

A possible non-Arabic origin

Some lexicons, including Lisān al-ʿArab, report the possibility that ṣanam is an Arabicized form of شَمَن (shaman).

Historical linguistic studies have also noted similarities among Semitic words, including forms meaning “image” or “representation.”

Resemblance among words in several languages does not by itself establish which language used the word first or how it passed between them.

Nor does a lexicon’s report that ṣanam was Arabicized establish its precise origin without independent historical evidence.

The established Arabic meaning can therefore guide interpretation of its Qur’anic usage without making an uncertain derivational theory the basis of the definition.

Arthur Jeffery, The Foreign Vocabulary of the Qur’an, p. 199: Semitic forms and a borrowing hypothesis

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic

Verses attributed to Zayd ibn ʿAmr ibn Nufayl, a figure of pre-Islamic Arabia, state:

تَرَكْتُ اللَّاتَ وَالْعُزَّى جَمِيعًا
كَذَلِكَ يَفْعَلُ الْجَلْدُ الصَّبُورُ

فَلَا الْعُزَّى أَدِينُ وَلَا ابْنَتَيْهَا
وَلَا صَنَمَيْ بَنِي غَنْمٍ أَزُورُ

taraktu al-lāta wa-al-ʿuzzā jamīʿan / kadhālika yafʿalu al-jaldu al-ṣabūru / fa-lā al-ʿuzzā adīnu wa-lā ibnatayhā / wa-lā ṣanamay banī ghanmin azūru

Translation:

“I abandoned both Al-Lāt and Al-ʿUzzā; that is what a steadfast, patient person does. I neither profess devotion to Al-ʿUzzā and her two attributed daughters nor visit the two idols of Banū Ghanm.”

The poem is transmitted in Ibn al-Kalbī’s Kitāb al-Aṣnām. Other sources give different tribal names in the second couplet, including Banū ʿAmr and Banū Ṭasm in place of Banū Ghanm.

Its attribution and exact wording should therefore not be treated as undisputed.

If the attribution is accepted, however, the use of ṣanamay shows that ṣanam referred, in the religious setting of pre-Islamic Arabia, to particular objects that people visited and regarded with religious reverence.

The verb azūru also describes visiting those idols. Visiting alone does not mean worship; in this poem, abandoning such visits accompanies abandoning devotion to those deities.

3. The five Qur’anic occurrences of ṣanam

Al-Anʿām 6:74: treating an idol as a deity

وَإِذْ قَالَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ لِأَبِيهِ آزَرَ أَتَتَّخِذُ أَصْنَامًا آلِهَةً إِنِّي أَرَاكَ وَقَوْمَكَ فِي ضَلَالٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-idh qāla ibrāhīmu li-abīhi āzara a-tattakhidhu aṣnāman ālihatan innī arāka wa-qawmaka fī ḍalālin mubīnin

“And when Abraham said to his father Āzar: Do you take idols as deities? I see you and your people in evident error.”

Al-Anʿām 6:74

In this verse, aṣnāman and ālihatan are distinct words appearing within one construction.

Aṣnāman refers to the idols, while ālihatan states the status assigned to them.

The verb أَتَتَّخِذُ (a-tattakhidhu) also matters: Abraham asks his father whether he adopts these images as deities.

This construction shows that Abraham’s objection concerns more than the existence of manufactured objects; it concerns taking them as deities.

The following verses describe observing a star, the moon, and the sun, and questioning lordship. Abraham’s inquiry therefore extends beyond manufactured images to the status of lordship and divinity.

The breadth of that inquiry should not, however, extend the lexical meaning of aṣnām to stars, the moon, the sun, or every other possible deity. Those phenomena appear under their own names in the following verses.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:138: attending and being devoted to idols

وَجَاوَزْنَا بِبَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ الْبَحْرَ فَأَتَوْا عَلَىٰ قَوْمٍ يَعْكُفُونَ عَلَىٰ أَصْنَامٍ لَهُمْ قَالُوا يَا مُوسَى اجْعَلْ لَنَا إِلَٰهًا كَمَا لَهُمْ آلِهَةٌ قَالَ إِنَّكُمْ قَوْمٌ تَجْهَلُونَ

wa-jāwaznā bi-banī isrāʾīla al-baḥra fa-ataw ʿalā qawmin yaʿkufūna ʿalā aṣnāmin lahum qālū yā mūsā ijʿal lanā ilāhan ka-mā lahum ālihatun qāla innakum qawmun tajhalūna

“And We brought the Children of Israel across the sea. They then came upon a people attending their idols. They said: Moses, appoint a deity for us, just as they have deities. He said: You are a people acting ignorantly.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:138

Here ṣanam accompanies the verb يَعْكُفُونَ (yaʿkufūna).

The root ʿ K F concerns attending, remaining, and persistently turning to something. In this context, the people were religiously devoted to their idols and remained with them.

The verse therefore describes more than images existing in a place; it also describes people’s religious conduct toward them.

The Children of Israel’s request makes this clearer. They do not merely ask Moses to make them an artistic image; they say:

اجْعَلْ لَنَا إِلَٰهًا

ijʿal lanā ilāhan

“Appoint a deity for us.”

The central issue is therefore again divine status.

The verb ʿakafa itself does not always mean idol worship. The Qur’an also uses this family for attending the mosque and participating in religious observances. The object and direction of that attendance connect it with idol worship in this verse.

Ibrāhīm 14:35: avoiding the worship of idols

وَإِذْ قَالَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ رَبِّ اجْعَلْ هَٰذَا الْبَلَدَ آمِنًا وَاجْنُبْنِي وَبَنِيَّ أَنْ نَعْبُدَ الْأَصْنَامَ

wa-idh qāla ibrāhīmu rabbi ijʿal hādhā al-balada āminan wa-ujnubnī wa-baniyya an naʿbuda al-aṣnāma

“And when Abraham said: My Lord, make this city secure, and keep me and my children away from worshipping idols.”

Ibrāhīm 14:35

Here al-aṣnāma is the object of the verb naʿbuda.

The connection between ṣanam and worship is therefore explicit.

Despite his clear opposition to idol worship, Abraham asks God to keep him and his children away from it.

His request concerns himself and his children; without examining other verses, it should not be turned into an unconditional guarantee of the conduct of all his descendants.

The next verse states:

رَبِّ إِنَّهُنَّ أَضْلَلْنَ كَثِيرًا مِنَ النَّاسِ

rabbi innahunna aḍlalna kathīran mina al-nāsi

“My Lord, they have led many people astray.”

Ibrāhīm 14:36

The pronoun إِنَّهُنَّ (innahunna) refers to the idols.

Leading astray is attributed to the idols here. This does not necessarily mean that the stone or wood possesses independent will, knowledge, or power to guide people or lead them astray.

Elsewhere the Qur’an itself describes the inability of fabricated deities to hear, speak, or provide help.

The attribution can therefore be understood in relation to the system of belief and conduct surrounding the idols, through following which people go astray.

This explanation must nevertheless preserve the verse’s explicit wording. The verse does attribute leading astray to idols; analyzing that attribution belongs to contextual interpretation rather than changing the translation.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:57: Abraham and breaking the idols

Al-Anbiyāʾ states:

وَتَاللَّهِ لَأَكِيدَنَّ أَصْنَامَكُمْ بَعْدَ أَنْ تُوَلُّوا مُدْبِرِينَ

wa-ta-allāhi la-akīdanna aṣnāmakum baʿda an tuwallū mudbirīna

“And by The God, I will certainly devise a plan against your idols after you turn your backs and leave.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:57

The next verse states:

فَجَعَلَهُمْ جُذَاذًا إِلَّا كَبِيرًا لَهُمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ إِلَيْهِ يَرْجِعُونَ

fa-jaʿalahum judhādhan illā kabīran lahum laʿallahum ilayhi yarjiʿūna

“So he broke them into pieces, except their largest, so that they might return to it.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:58

In this account, the idols are objects that can be broken into pieces. Their material character is therefore evident within the narrative itself.

Abraham’s argument, however, aims at more than breaking several objects.

The people are subsequently asked to turn to the largest idol and question the idols themselves, if they can speak.

This argument exposes the inability of beings that people have treated as deities.

The issue is therefore the conflict between the status claimed for the idols and their actual abilities.

An account of a historical event involving Abraham does not by itself establish a general ruling about breaking every image, damaging other people’s property, or acting individually without regard for people’s rights and social circumstances. Such rulings require independent evidence.

Lexically, too, aṣnāmakum refers to the particular idols Abraham’s people possessed, rather than every manufactured object in their lives.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:71: worship and continued attendance

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ, Abraham asks his people what they worship. They reply:

قَالُوا نَعْبُدُ أَصْنَامًا فَنَظَلُّ لَهَا عَاكِفِينَ

qālū naʿbudu aṣnāman fa-naẓallu lahā ʿākifīna

“They said: We worship idols and continue attending them.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:71

This is among the clearest occurrences of ṣanam, since both the verb naʿbudu and ʿākifīna appear.

The images are therefore objects of worship, and people also devote their time and attention to attending them.

Abraham then asks:

هَلْ يَسْمَعُونَكُمْ إِذْ تَدْعُونَ
أَوْ يَنْفَعُونَكُمْ أَوْ يَضُرُّونَ

hal yasmaʿūnakum idh tadʿūna / aw yanfaʿūnakum aw yaḍurrūna

“Do they hear you when you call? Or do they benefit or harm you?”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:72 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:73

These questions examine the supposed functions of the deities: Do they hear? Can they bring benefit or harm?

Abraham’s people answer by appealing to their fathers’ conduct.

The issue thus extends beyond the idols’ existence to accepting their status as objects of worship and continuing a tradition inherited from earlier generations.

These verses do not establish that every inherited custom is wrong. The criticism concerns a tradition that treats powerless objects as deities and replaces examining the truth with following earlier generations.

4. Ṣanam compared with wathan, timthāl, and nuṣub

Ṣ N M (ṣanam) and W TH N (wathan)

Ṣanam and wathan are closely related in Arabic sources, and one is sometimes defined by the other.

Some lexicographers distinguish them: ṣanam is an image with a definite form, while wathan is something worshipped without a definite form.

Other sources report the reverse distinction. Still others use both words for objects of worship.

This disagreement prevents presenting a completely fixed, universally accepted boundary for every historical use of the language.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: competing distinctions between ṣanam and wathan

Awthān occurs three times in the Qur’an: Al-Ḥajj 22:30, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:17, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:25.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:17 states:

إِنَّمَا تَعْبُدُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ أَوْثَانًا وَتَخْلُقُونَ إِفْكًا

innamā taʿbudūna min dūni allāhi awthānan wa-takhluqūna ifkan

“You worship idols apart from The God and fabricate a falsehood.”

Wathan is likewise connected with worship here.

Ṣanam and wathan therefore share a field in Qur’anic usage, at least concerning fabricated deities.

This overlap does not mean that every historical and lexical feature of the two words is identical.

Ṣ N M (ṣanam) and M TH L (timthāl)

Timthāl belongs to the root M TH L and denotes a form or image representing the likeness of something.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:52, Abraham asks:

مَا هَٰذِهِ التَّمَاثِيلُ الَّتِي أَنْتُمْ لَهَا عَاكِفُونَ

mā hādhihi al-tamāthīlu allatī antum lahā ʿākifūna

“What are these images that you attend?”

Here the images receive the religious attendance of Abraham’s people.

The word tamāthīl also appears in another setting.

Concerning Solomon, Sabaʾ 34:13 states:

يَعْمَلُونَ لَهُ مَا يَشَاءُ مِنْ مَحَارِيبَ وَتَمَاثِيلَ

yaʿmalūna lahu mā yashāʾu min maḥārība wa-tamāthīla

“They made for him whatever he wished of chambers and images.”

Here tamāthīl appears in a list of manufactured objects without stating that they were worshipped.

This distinction matters.

Timthāl primarily concerns form and representation, whereas ṣanam in Qur’anic usage is identified through the image’s status as an object of worship.

An image should therefore not be considered a worshipped ṣanam merely because it has a form.

Equally, the presence of images in the account of Solomon does not by itself resolve every legal and ethical question concerning different kinds of pictures and images. Those matters require independent evidence.

Study of M TH L

Ṣ N M (ṣanam) and N Ṣ B (nuṣub)

Nuṣub and its plural anṣāb concern things erected or set up.

In Arab religious usage, anṣāb also designated erected stones and markers associated with sacrifice and worship.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:90, al-anṣāb appears alongside khamr, maysir, and azlām.

The expression is related to ṣanam through religious associations, but every erected stone or marker is not necessarily called a ṣanam.

Nor does standing upright make every installation or structure an object of worship.

Historical examination must establish how the object was used and what status people assigned to it.

Ṣ N M (ṣanam) and N D D (nidd)

In the Qur’an, nidd and its plural andād concern rivals that people set up alongside God.

Al-Baqarah 2:165 states:

وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يَتَّخِذُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ أَنْدَادًا يُحِبُّونَهُمْ كَحُبِّ اللَّهِ

wa-mina al-nāsi man yattakhidhu min dūni allāhi andādan yuḥibbūnahum ka-ḥubbi allāhi

“And among people are those who adopt rivals apart from The God, loving them as they love God.”

Nidd is broader than ṣanam, because setting up a rival does not necessarily require a physical image.

A person may treat an idol as a deity and thereby set up a rival to God. But not every form of setting up rivals appears as a carved image.

Equally, affection, respect, attachment, or following another person does not inherently amount to setting up a rival to God.

The specific meaning of an idol or worshipped image should therefore be retained when discussing Ṣ N M. The broader discussion of rivals, love, and loyalty in relation to divine status belongs to a separate study of andād.

5. Does ṣanam belong only to the past?

In the Qur’an’s direct usage, aṣnām are objects that people treated as deities and worshipped.

This historical, material usage is clear and should remain part of the text.

The question raised by the Qur’an’s criticism of this conduct, however, extends beyond an image’s shape: how does a person treat something unworthy of divinity as a deity?

This allows modern expressions such as “the idol of power,” “the idol of wealth,” or “the idol of personality.”

These are metaphorical uses. Such an expression becomes meaningful when power, wealth, or a person gains a status for whose sake truth and justice are set aside.

This ethical extension should nevertheless not replace the lexical meaning of ṣanam.

Merely valuing wealth, respecting a leader, loving one’s family, or using a symbol does not mean idol worship.

Applying idol worship or setting up rivals to contemporary conduct requires examining the nature of the attachment, its role in decisions, the person’s awareness, and its relationship to clear Qur’anic principles.

Historical idol worship should likewise not be reduced to a psychological metaphor. The Qur’an speaks of actual objects, actual worship, and actual social conduct.

The historical referent of ṣanam and a possible extension of the Qur’anic message to contemporary conduct must therefore remain distinct.

6. Translation suggestions for Forqan

صَنَم (ṣanam): an idol; an image or object treated as a deity.

أَصْنَام (aṣnām): idols.

أَصْنَامًا (aṣnāman): idols, with definiteness determined by the sentence.

أَصْنَامٍ (aṣnāmin): idols; in Al-Aʿrāf 7:138, those that the people attended.

الْأَصْنَامَ (al-aṣnāma): the idols; in Ibrāhīm 14:35, those whose worship Abraham seeks to avoid.

أَصْنَامَكُمْ (aṣnāmakum): your idols; in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:57.

Qur’anic examples:

أَتَتَّخِذُ أَصْنَامًا آلِهَةً (a-tattakhidhu aṣnāman ālihatan) — Do you take idols as deities?

يَعْكُفُونَ عَلَىٰ أَصْنَامٍ لَهُمْ (yaʿkufūna ʿalā aṣnāmin lahum) — They attended their idols.

أَنْ نَعْبُدَ الْأَصْنَامَ (an naʿbuda al-aṣnāma) — That we worship idols.

لَأَكِيدَنَّ أَصْنَامَكُمْ (la-akīdanna aṣnāmakum) — I will certainly devise a plan against your idols.

نَعْبُدُ أَصْنَامًا (naʿbudu aṣnāman) — We worship idols.

For a basic translation, “idol” is a natural and familiar equivalent of ṣanam.

“Statue” or “image” alone may fail to convey the word’s connection with worship. Conversely, “fabricated deity” is broader and does not necessarily indicate a physical object.

“Idol” is therefore preferable in the translation, while lexical and contextual explanation can clarify the image’s nature and how it was worshipped.

7. Conclusion

The root Ṣ N M appears in the Qur’an only as the plural aṣnām, referring to idols that people treated as deities.

In early Arabic, ṣanam was associated with images and objects made from stone, wood, and metal. Shape and material do not exhaust its meaning, however; the object’s religious status and worship also belong to the word’s familiar usage.

In the five Qur’anic occurrences, aṣnām is associated with adopting deities, attending them, worshipping, praying to avoid their worship, and Abraham’s action against his people’s idols.

These verses show that the Qur’anic criticism concerns more than an idol’s material construction; it concerns the status people assign to it.

Among related roots, wathan often overlaps with ṣanam, although lexicographers disagree over their precise boundary. Timthāl emphasizes form and representation without worship being a fixed part of its meaning. Nuṣub likewise concerns an erected object that may have a religious use.

Andād is broader than aṣnām and may encompass other forms of setting up rivals. Every human attachment or affection should nevertheless not be called a ṣanam or nidd without evidence.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

An idol; an image or object that people treat as a deity and worship.

In Qur’anic usage, aṣnām denotes worshipped idols. Each idol’s material, form, and particular associations are identified through the context and evidence relating to that occurrence.
