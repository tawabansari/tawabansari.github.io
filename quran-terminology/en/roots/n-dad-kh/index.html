Gushing, spurting and splashing liquid: from abundant springs to scattered droplets, sweat and traces left on things

1. The root and its semantic core

The Arabic root N DAD KH concerns liquid issuing forth, gushing, splashing or scattering. In one clear use, water wells up and emerges forcefully from a spring. In other uses, droplets splash onto something or leave traces on clothing, the body or other surfaces.

“نَضَخَ الْمَاءُ” can describe water gushing or spurting out. “عَيْنٌ نَضَّاخَةٌ” describes a spring from which water wells up abundantly.

The root is not restricted to water, however.

In early Arabic, this family also describes the splashing of sweat, blood, perfume, dye and other liquids.

These uses have a clear connection: a liquid leaves its source or previous location, moves, and may splash onto another surface or leave a trace.

Nevertheless, not all these stages are necessary in every use.

A spring may, for example, discharge water forcefully from the ground without the sentence mentioning droplets splashing onto clothing.

Conversely, traces of blood or perfume on clothing may result from splashing without any spring or eruption from the ground.

Thus, liquid issuing forth and scattering forms the general semantic field of this family; gushing water and the traces of splashed liquid are distinct uses that need to be distinguished.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs only in a description of two springs in the Garden.

Gushing water is therefore directly relevant to its Qur’anic meaning, while the other branches help establish the range of Arabic usage.

The Qur’anic form

The root N DAD KH occurs only once in the Qur’an:

“نَضَّاخَتَانِ” — Al-Raḥmān 55:66

The word describes two springs.

Its singular feminine form is “نَضَّاخَةٌ”, and its masculine form is “نَضَّاخٌ”.

“نَضَّاخَتَانِ” follows the pattern “فَعَّالَتَانِ” and is feminine dual nominative.

The doubled ḍād matters: naḍḍākh is a description capable of emphasis or intensification. Depending on usage, the pattern can highlight abundance, intensity or relative persistence compared with a simple description.

Here it describes two springs, emphasizing their gushing discharge.

The intensive pattern alone does not establish an exact volume of water, jet height, discharge speed or unlimited duration.

Other forms recorded in Arabic include “نَضَخَ”, “يَنْضَخُ”, “نَضْخ”, “نُضُوخ”, “نَاضِخ”, “نَضَّاخ”, “نَضَّاخَة”, “مَنْضُوخ”, “اِنْتَضَخَ” and “مُنَاضَخَة”.

None of these forms occurs in the Qur’an except “نَضَّاخَتَانِ”.

This distinction matters: not every dictionary meaning belongs to the particular Qur’anic form.

2. Early Arabic: gushing water and splashing liquids

A. Water gushing from a spring

Early lexical explanations connect naḍkh with water welling up and issuing from a spring.

One explanation states:

النَّضْخُ مِنْ فَوَرَانِ الْمَاءِ مِنَ الْعَيْنِ وَجَيَشَانِهِ

al-naḍkhu min fawarāni al-māʾi mina al-ʿayni wa-jayashānihi

Meaning:

“Naḍkh concerns water gushing and welling up from a spring.”

Two features are present here.

First, water emerges from its source.

Second, its emergence involves movement and welling up, rather than merely a little moisture appearing on the soil’s surface.

In Arabic, “عَيْنٌ نَضَّاخَةٌ” describes a spring that discharges abundant water.

“عَيْنٌ نَضَّاخَةٌ” is also explained as follows:

وَعَيْنٌ نَضَّاخَةٌ: تَجِيشُ بِالْمَاءِ

wa-ʿaynun naḍḍākhatun tajīshu bi-al-māʾi

Meaning:

“A naḍḍākh spring is one in which water wells up and emerges.”

This explanation directly relates to the Qur’anic use.

A spring’s welling up must, however, be distinguished from water boiling through heat.

In Persian, “water boils” can refer either to water reaching boiling point or to water welling up from a spring.

In “عَيْنٌ نَضَّاخَةٌ”, the point is water issuing and moving from its source, rather than becoming hot through fire.

The translation should therefore make the gushing sense clear.

B. Splashing water and other liquids

“نَضَخَ عَلَيْهِ الْمَاءَ” is also recorded for splashing water onto someone or something.

Here water moves from one location to another and may scatter as droplets.

“اِنْتَضَخَ الْمَاءُ” likewise concerns water splashing or spraying about.

This is not quite the same as a spring gushing.

In splashing, a person may set the water in motion by hand or with a tool.

In a gushing spring, water emerges from its source.

Both involve liquid moving and scattering, but the cause and manner of movement differ.

Naḍkh should therefore not always be rendered simply as “sprinkling water”.

C. Perfume, blood and dye: traces on a surface

“نَضَخَ ثَوْبَهُ بِالطِّيبِ” is recorded for splashing perfume onto clothing.

Naḍkh can also denote the trace of a liquid left on cloth or the body.

This use is explained as follows:

النَّضْخُ: الرَّدْعُ وَاللَّطْخُ يَبْقَى فِي الْجَسَدِ أَوِ الثَّوْبِ مِنَ الطِّيبِ وَنَحْوِهِ

al-naḍkhu al-radʿu wa-al-laṭkhu yabqā fī al-jasadi awi al-thawbi mina al-ṭībi wa-naḥwihi

Meaning:

“Naḍkh denotes the trace or stain left on the body or clothing by perfume or something similar.”

Attention here falls on the result as well as the splashing itself.

Droplets of blood may, for example, splash onto clothing and leave traces on the fabric.

Perfume or dye may similarly splash onto something and leave a mark.

This does not mean that every instance of naḍkh necessarily leaves a lasting stain.

A spring can discharge clear water forcefully without staining being the subject of the sentence.

Thus, a remaining trace is one recorded use of naḍkh, rather than a necessary condition of every occurrence.

D. A poetic witness attributed to Jarīr: traces of blood

A fragment of poetry attributed to Jarīr is quoted to illustrate this use:

ثِيَابُكُمُ وَنَضْخُ دَمِ الْقَتِيلِ

thiyābukumu wa-naḍkhu dami al-qatīli

Translation of the phrase:

“Your clothes and the splashed blood of the slain.”

The fragment illustrates naḍkh in connection with blood.

It concerns traces of blood on clothing, rather than water gushing from a spring.

Because the lexical quotation contains only part of a verse, another hemistich should not be invented to complete it.

Jarīr was a poet of the Islamic and Umayyad period; this is a witness to classical Arabic rather than pre-Islamic poetry.

Its lexical relevance remains clear: naḍkh can concern a liquid other than water, such as blood.

E. Naḍkh in descriptions of rain

“غَيْثٌ نَضَّاخٌ” is recorded for abundant rain.

“نَضْخَة” also denotes a fall or shower of rain in some uses.

These uses concern water being discharged and scattered through the surroundings.

Rain and springs are nevertheless different phenomena.

Rain falls from clouds, whereas a spring issues from an underground source or a place where water has collected.

“نَضَّاخَتَانِ” in Al-Raḥmān 55:66 therefore does not establish that the two springs descend from the sky like rain.

That image would require independent evidence.

3. A significant poetic witness: naḍḍākhah describing a she-camel

One of the clearest literary witnesses to this family occurs in poetry by Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr:

مِنْ كُلِّ نَضَّاخَةِ الذِّفْرَى إِذَا عَرِقَتْ
عُرْضَتُهَا طَامِسُ الْأَعْلَامِ مَجْهُولُ

min kulli naḍḍākhati al-dhifrā idhā ʿariqat / ʿurḍatuhā ṭāmisu al-aʿlāmi majhūlu

An approximate rendering of the sense:

“With a powerful she-camel whose sweat pours abundantly from behind her ears when she sweats; a camel that travels a difficult route whose landmarks are effaced and unknown.”

In this verse, “نَضَّاخَة” describes the she-camel.

“الذِّفْرَى” denotes the area behind or near the camel’s ear, noted in Arabic descriptions as a place where sweat appears.

“نَضَّاخَةُ الذِّفْرَى” describes a camel that discharges much sweat from that area.

The subject here is animal sweat rather than spring water.

The shared feature is clear, however: liquid issues abundantly from its source.

The witness is especially relevant because it contains the same “نَضَّاخَة” pattern whose feminine dual appears in the Qur’an.

It shows how this pattern can highlight abundant liquid discharge in such a setting.

Not all the camel’s qualities in the verse should, however, be incorporated into the word naḍḍākhah itself.

Strength, fitness for travel and crossing a difficult route come from the rest of the poem and its context.

Naḍḍākhah itself concerns abundant sweat discharge.

Reliability and date of the witness

This verse belongs to Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr’s famous ode and is recorded in early literary sources.

Kaʿb lived across the transition from the pre-Islamic to the Islamic period.

The ode containing this verse is attributed to his Islamic period.

It is therefore a witness to early Arabic contemporary with the language of Qur’anic revelation, but should not be presented as certainly pre-Qur’anic.

Some vowels and details of the ode’s text vary between witnesses, but “نَضَّاخَةِ الذِّفْرَى” is well attested in lexical and literary transmission.

This witness supports the semantic analysis without adding uncertain or fabricated verses.

4. N DAD KH and N DAD H: two close roots

A central issue is the distinction between N DAD KH and N DAD H.

The families differ only in their final consonant:

  • “ن ض خ” ends in khāʾ.
  • “ن ض ح” ends in ḥāʾ.

Both are recorded for liquid being splashed or scattered.

Lexicographers have not agreed on their precise distinction.

A. Is naḍkh more intense than naḍḥ?

A familiar explanation assigns naḍkh a stronger or more abundant splashing and naḍḥ a lighter or smaller amount.

This agrees with “عَيْنٌ نَضَّاخَةٌ” describing an abundant, gushing spring.

One interpretation of Al-Raḥmān 55:66 likewise treats naḍkh as stronger than naḍḥ, which is more closely associated with sprinkling.

That is not the only recorded view, however.

B. Other lexical views

Some transmitted lexical explanations conflict with one another.

One view considers naḍkh less than naḍḥ.

One report assigns unintentional splashing to naḍkh and intentional splashing to naḍḥ; another reverses the distinction. Intention cannot therefore be treated as an invariant difference between the roots.

Another explanation uses naḍḥ for the action a person performs, but naḍkh for what reaches someone through splashing.

One lexical preference also considers naḍkh greater than naḍḥ.

A further view distinguishes the splashing action from the trace it leaves.

Another report treats the two words as synonymous in some uses.

Early Arabic speakers therefore cannot be said to have agreed on one fixed, certain boundary between the words.

C. What the comparison establishes

The evidence supports three points.

First, both roots belong to the field of splashing or scattering liquid.

Second, when describing a spring, naḍkh clearly concerns gushing water.

Third, the relative intensity of the words across Arabic usage is disputed.

It is therefore unsuitable to define N DAD KH as “always more splashing than naḍḥ”.

Nor is it sound to claim that “naḍkh is always lighter than naḍḥ”.

For Al-Raḥmān 55:66, the established expression “عَيْنٌ نَضَّاخَةٌ”, describing an abundant, gushing spring, matters more than this dispute.

Disagreement about the relative intensity of the roots does not erase the clear gushing sense in the description of a spring.

5. The Qur’anic use: Al-Raḥmān 55:66

فِيهِمَا عَيْنَانِ نَضَّاخَتَانِ

fīhimā ʿaynāni naḍḍākhatāni

Translation:

“In those two are two springs, welling up and gushing forth.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:66

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Its syntax and surrounding verses both matter for an accurate reading.

A. What “فِيهِمَا” refers to

“فِيهِمَا” means “in those two”.

It refers to the two gardens introduced earlier in Al-Raḥmān 55:62:

وَمِنْ دُونِهِمَا جَنَّتَانِ

wa-min dūnihimā jannatāni

Translation:

“And besides those two are two other gardens.”

The description continues:

مُدْهَامَّتَانِ

mudhāmmatāni

“Both are deeply green and dark-hued.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:64

Al-Raḥmān 55:66 then describes two springs in those gardens.

The reference is clear: the springs are in the two gardens just mentioned.

Naḍḍākhatān itself does not name the gardens or their vegetation.

Those details come from the surrounding verses.

B. “عَيْنَانِ”: two springs

“عَيْنَانِ” is the dual of “عَيْن”.

The Arabic root AYN Y N has a broad semantic field, including eyes, springs and other uses.

Here the description naḍḍākhatān makes “springs” the appropriate meaning.

A spring in this use is a source from which liquid emerges.

In the primary lexical and interpretive reading, that liquid is water.

The meanings of ʿayn and naḍḍākhatān should nevertheless remain distinct.

“عَيْنَانِ” identifies two springs.

“نَضَّاخَتَانِ” describes their gushing discharge.

Naḍḍākhatān therefore does not itself mean “two springs”; it describes them.

Study of AYN Y N

C. The morphology of “نَضَّاخَتَانِ”

“نَضَّاخَتَانِ” is a feminine dual nominative adjective.

It agrees with “عَيْنَانِ” in number, grammatical gender and case.

Its singular is “نَضَّاخَةٌ”.

Here the pattern can intensify the description, emphasizing abundance or force.

Possible renderings therefore include “gushing”, “abundant and welling up”, or “gushing forth abundantly”.

The pattern alone does not establish a specific rate of discharge.

Its feminine gender is grammatical rather than biological. Arabic ʿayn is grammatically feminine, and its adjective agrees with it.

D. Does the water leap upward?

Some lexical explanations use naḍkh for water moving from below to above.

One lexical explanation identifies upward movement as a feature of naḍkh.

This fits the image of a spring spurting upward.

“Gushing” is therefore an appropriate rendering.

The word does not, however, establish that the water rises to a specific height like a modern artificial fountain.

Nor should the shape or course of the discharge be reconstructed in engineering or physical detail.

The clear sense is liquid actively welling or gushing out of its source.

The verse specifies no dimensions, height, speed or exact shape.

E. Do the springs never cease?

Some interpretive reports explain naḍḍākhatān as two full, unceasing springs.

One report describes them as full and denies that they cease.

This is consistent with the picture of the Garden’s gifts.

Lexical precision is still necessary, however.

Naḍḍākhatān directly describes abundant liquid welling and gushing out.

The word alone does not specify an unlimited duration.

The broader context of the Garden’s gifts may suggest continuing enjoyment, but that conclusion should not become part of the root’s independent definition.

For a basic translation, “two gushing springs” is more precise than “two springs that gush eternally without interruption”.

The latter adds details that the adjective alone does not state.

6. Different readings: springs of water or sources of blessing?

Interpretive tradition offers different explanations of what the two springs discharge.

A. The first reading: water

Several early interpretive reports understand two springs from which water gushes.

The reason for preferring this reading is that ʿayn ordinarily means a water spring in such a context.

This has clear lexical support.

“عَيْنٌ نَضَّاخَةٌ” is an established Arabic expression for an abundant, gushing spring.

Thus, water is the primary and preferable reading of the verse.

B. The second reading: goodness and blessing

Other reports speak of goodness and blessing issuing forth.

This can relate to the abundance of the Garden’s gifts.

Neither “goodness” nor “blessing”, however, is stated in “عَيْنَانِ نَضَّاخَتَانِ”.

Describing the springs as sources of goodness should therefore remain an interpretive explanation.

Naḍḍākhatān should not be translated directly as “two sources of blessing” with the gushing sense omitted.

C. The third reading: perfume, musk, ambergris and camphor

Some early reports describe the springs as spraying musk, ambergris or camphor on the Garden’s inhabitants.

This is intelligible in light of naḍkh being used for splashing perfume and fragrant liquids.

The verse itself nevertheless names none of these substances.

They therefore should not be added to the basic translation.

The possibility of using naḍkh for perfume does not itself establish perfume as the subject in Al-Raḥmān 55:66.

In the context of two springs, water has the clearer claim.

D. The fourth reading: fruit and various gifts

Another report speaks of fruit issuing from these springs.

Al-Raḥmān 55:68 also states:

فِيهِمَا فَاكِهَةٌ وَنَخْلٌ وَرُمَّانٌ

fīhimā fākihatun wa-nakhlun wa-rummānun

Translation:

“In those two are fruit, date palms and pomegranates.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:68 identifies fruit in the same two gardens; it does not explicitly say that fruit gushes from the springs.

Combining the verses therefore does not establish that the springs necessarily produce or eject fruit.

Fruit being in a garden must be distinguished from water gushing from a spring.

What these readings establish

Lexical evidence and context support “two springs gushing with water” as the primary reading.

Reports about perfume, goodness and fruit belong to interpretive tradition and should be recognized as proposed explanations.

None should displace the adjective’s direct meaning without clear evidence.

7. Naḍḍākhatān and tajriyān in Al-Raḥmān

A particularly relevant Qur’anic comparison is between two expressions in the same surah.

Al-Raḥmān 55:50 states:

فِيهِمَا عَيْنَانِ تَجْرِيَانِ

fīhimā ʿaynāni tajriyāni

Translation:

“In those two are two flowing springs.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:66, however, states:

فِيهِمَا عَيْنَانِ نَضَّاخَتَانِ

fīhimā ʿaynāni naḍḍākhatāni

Translation:

“In those two are two gushing springs.”

Both expressions contain the same “فِيهِمَا عَيْنَانِ”.

The difference lies in the description of the springs.

A. Tajriyān: flowing

“تَجْرِيَانِ” belongs to the root J R Y.

Applied to water, jary concerns flowing or moving along a course.

Water may emerge from a source and then run in a stream or channel.

Jary therefore describes water’s movement and flow.

The verb does not necessarily say that the water leaps upward as it leaves its source.

B. Naḍḍākhatān: gushing and spurting forth

Naḍḍākhatān, by contrast, emphasizes liquid welling up and actively issuing from a spring.

This is compatible with flowing water, but is not exactly the same meaning.

A spring may discharge water forcefully, after which the water flows along its course.

Gushing and flowing can therefore be two stages or features of one phenomenon.

That possibility does not establish that the two Qur’anic verses necessarily describe consecutive stages of the same flow: the passages concern two pairs of gardens.

C. Is one description superior to the other?

Interpretive tradition differs over the relative superiority of tajriyān and naḍḍākhatān.

Some interpreters consider continuing flow superior to gushing.

Others take gushing to indicate greater force and abundance.

One comparison of the expressions considers jary stronger than naḍkh.

An objection holds that the flow could be small and gentle while the gushing could be strong and abundant.

Another explanation treats the different descriptions as variety in depicting the gardens’ beauty.

The words alone therefore cannot establish that every flowing spring outranks every gushing spring, or the reverse.

This is a significant methodological point.

The descriptive difference is clear, but a ranking in value or status is not a necessary consequence of the words’ lexical meanings.

D. Do the two pairs of gardens differ in rank?

Al-Raḥmān 55:46 states:

وَلِمَنْ خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِ جَنَّتَانِ

wa-li-man khāfa maqāma rabbihi jannatāni

Translation:

“For whoever fears standing before their Lord are two gardens.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:62 also states:

وَمِنْ دُونِهِمَا جَنَّتَانِ

wa-min dūnihimā jannatāni

“And besides those two are two other gardens.”

Different readings of “مِنْ دُونِهِمَا” include being lower in rank or location, or being two further gardens in addition to the first pair.

That question requires an independent examination of D W N and the surah’s context.

The difference between tajriyān and naḍḍākhatān alone should not be treated as decisive evidence of different ranks.

Even if other evidence supports distinct ranks, naḍḍākhatān itself describes only the springs, rather than the inhabitants’ worthiness or degree of reward.

8. Comparison with related roots

N DAD KH and N DAD H: gushing and splashing

These families are close in sound and meaning.

Naḍḥ is especially familiar in descriptions of liquid being sprinkled or splashed.

Naḍkh is also used for splashing, but in a spring’s description highlights water gushing or welling up.

Their relative intensity remains lexically disputed.

A Qur’anic translation should therefore rest on naḍḍākhatān in relation to ʿaynān, rather than an alleged universal rule of one root’s superiority.

N DAD KH and N B AYN: gushing and sources

N B AYN concerns water emerging from its source in Arabic.

The Qur’an states:

فَسَلَكَهُ يَنَابِيعَ فِي الْأَرْضِ

fa-salakahu yanābīʿa fī al-arḍi

Translation:

“Then He channeled it into springs in the earth.”

Al-Zumar 39:21, part of the verse

“يَنَابِيع” belongs to N B AYN and denotes springs or places where water emerges.

Naḍḍākhatān, however, describes two springs as they gush.

The semantic fields are close but not identical.

A spring is a source of water; gushing describes how it emerges.

Study of N B AYN

N DAD KH and J R Y: gushing and flowing

Jary concerns water flowing or moving along a course.

Applied to a spring, naḍkh concerns water welling up and issuing actively.

These can occur in one spring without being complete synonyms.

Comparing Al-Raḥmān 55:50 and Al-Raḥmān 55:66 is the clearest Qur’anic witness to this distinction.

Study of J R Y

N DAD KH and F J R: gushing, splitting open and making flow

One branch of F J R concerns splitting open and water emerging.

Al-Insān 76:6 states:

عَيْنًا يَشْرَبُ بِهَا عِبَادُ اللَّهِ يُفَجِّرُونَهَا تَفْجِيرًا

ʿaynan yashrabu bihā ʿibādu allāhi yufajjirūnahā tafjīran

Translation:

“A spring from which the servants of The God drink, making it flow freely.”

“يُفَجِّرُونَهَا” here concerns making the water flow and opening a route for its emergence.

Naḍḍākhatān, however, describes the spring’s gushing state.

Tafjīr and naḍkh are thus close in the field of water, without being identical.

Naḍḍākhatān alone should not be translated as “two springs for which a path through the earth has been split open”.

Study of F J R

N DAD KH and N DAD J: gushing liquid and ripeness

These roots differ only in the final consonant.

N DAD KH concerns liquid issuing or gushing forth.

N DAD J concerns becoming cooked or ripe.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:56, “نَضِجَتْ” describes skins seared in the fire.

In Al-Raḥmān 55:66, however, “نَضَّاخَتَانِ” describes two gushing springs.

Naḍkh should not be understood as becoming cooked, nor naḍj as water gushing.

In particular, the Persian uses of “boiling” for a spring’s welling up and for water boiling through heat are distinct.

The translation must preserve that difference.

Study of N DAD J

N DAD KH and N DAD R: gushing and freshness

N DAD R concerns freshness, vitality and radiance.

The Qur’an states:

وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَاضِرَةٌ

wujūhun yawmaʾidhin nāḍiratun

“Faces that Day will be fresh and radiant.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:22

“نَاضِرَة” belongs to N DAD R, unlike “نَضَّاخَة” from N DAD KH.

Abundant water can make plants flourish, but that natural relationship does not make the roots identical.

Naḍḍākhatān should therefore not be rendered as “two fresh springs” or “two beautiful springs”, even though freshness and beauty may form part of the wider picture of the gardens.

9. Translation questions and proposals for Forqan

Is the Persian “jūshān” appropriate?

“Chashmah-ye jūshān”, a spring welling up, is a natural and familiar Persian expression.

On its own, however, jūshān can sometimes suggest heated, boiling water.

“A gushing spring” makes the intended sense clearer.

The combined Persian wording “welling up and gushing” can preserve both fluency and the Arabic’s particular meaning.

A short basic translation need not always place two close equivalents together, however.

Is “two abundant springs” sufficient?

Abundant water is an intelligible feature of naḍḍākhah.

That rendering alone, however, does not convey the movement and gushing well.

A pool can be full of water without any water gushing from it.

“Two abundant springs” therefore conveys the dynamic image less fully than “two gushing springs”.

Is “two flowing springs” appropriate?

This is closer to “تَجْرِيَانِ” in Al-Raḥmān 55:50.

Using only “flowing” in Al-Raḥmān 55:66 would partially erase the difference between the Qur’anic descriptions.

It is therefore preferable to render:

“تَجْرِيَانِ” as “they flow”.

And:

“نَضَّاخَتَانِ” as “they gush forth”.

These renderings preserve the verbal and pictorial distinction.

Does gushing mean endlessness?

No.

The word concerns the manner and abundance of the water’s discharge.

If continuing or endless enjoyment is discussed in relation to the Garden’s gifts, other evidence must support it.

“Endless” should not replace naḍḍākhatān in a translation without justification.

The proposed rendering of Al-Raḥmān 55:66 is:

“In those two are two gushing springs.”

This is concise, preserves the specific gushing sense and does not add a referent beyond the wording.

For a more expansive Persian expression, it is possible to write:

“In those two are two springs, welling up and gushing forth.”

The first expression is sufficient for a basic translation, however.

Proposal for Forqan’s word box

Word: نَضَّاخَتَانِ

Root: N DAD KH

Brief root meaning: Liquid gushing or issuing forth; splashing and scattering it.

Meaning in this verse: Gushing forth; describing two springs from which water wells up abundantly and actively. “Two springs” itself is the meaning of “عَيْنَانِ”.

This keeps the family’s general meaning separate from the verse’s particular use.

“Two endless springs”, “two perfume springs”, “two sources of blessing” and “two springs producing fruit” should not replace the word’s direct meaning without sufficient evidence.

10. Conclusion

In Arabic, N DAD KH concerns liquids issuing forth, gushing or splashing.

Applied to a spring, naḍkh describes water welling up and actively emerging from its source.

Elsewhere the family describes the splashing of water, sweat, blood, perfume and dye, and the traces they leave on clothing or the body.

Early Arabic records “غَيْثٌ نَضَّاخٌ” for abundant rain and “عَيْنٌ نَضَّاخَةٌ” for an abundant, gushing spring.

Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr’s “نَضَّاخَةِ الذِّفْرَى” shows the pattern being used for plentiful sweat issuing from a camel’s body.

The root is therefore not restricted to spring water, but its Qur’anic occurrence belongs to that branch.

Lexical sources also discuss its distinction from naḍḥ.

Some consider naḍkh stronger, others lighter; some distinguish the act of splashing from the trace left behind.

The roots’ relative intensity should therefore not become an absolute rule.

What is clear for the Qur’anic use is the gushing-water sense of “عَيْنٌ نَضَّاخَةٌ”.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as “نَضَّاخَتَانِ” in Al-Raḥmān 55:66.

It describes two springs in the two gardens mentioned in the preceding verses.

Al-Raḥmān 55:50 also describes two springs with “تَجْرِيَانِ”.

The linguistic difference is clear: one emphasizes flowing water, the other water welling up and gushing.

That difference does not establish an absolute ranking of the two pairs of gardens, a specific water volume or a physical discharge mechanism.

Interpretive tradition includes differing reports about water, goodness, perfume and fruit as what issues from the springs.

Nevertheless, water is the clearer referent and primary reading on lexical and contextual grounds.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

In its general meaning, N DAD KH denotes liquid issuing forth, gushing or splashing. This movement may involve scattered droplets or a trace left on a surface.

Early Arabic records these meanings for water, sweat, blood, perfume, rain and other liquids.

In the Qur’an, “نَضَّاخَتَانِ” describes two springs and means “gushing forth”; the whole expression “عَيْنَانِ نَضَّاخَتَانِ” can be rendered “two springs, welling up and gushing forth”.

The intensive pattern highlights abundant or forceful welling up without determining a specific jet height, exact water volume or unlimited duration.

Thus, liquid gushing and actively issuing forth is central to the Qur’anic use of N DAD KH. Its distinction from flowing water, ordinary splashing and the traces of liquids should remain clear in lexical study, while the nature and details of the Garden’s gifts require context and independent evidence.
