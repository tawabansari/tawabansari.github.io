The Qur’anic Root Q N T

From obedience and commitment to steadfastness in worship

The Qur’anic root ق ن ت concerns obedience, commitment and being in a state of submission. Sometimes this is said of all beings in the heavens and earth; sometimes of Abraham and Mary. Men and women are described as qānit and qānitāt, and in two verses this state appears in the setting of Salat and worship at night.

This range shows that qunūt cannot mean only a particular supplication during prayer. The Qur’an does not use the verbal noun قُنُوت itself; the meanings of the family’s attested forms must be understood from the sentences in which they occur.

Nor should qānit be translated simply as “standing” or “silent.” The Qur’an describes someone as qānit while both prostrating and standing. It tells Mary اقنتي لربك, then separately اسجدي واركعي. Qunūt therefore extends beyond a single bodily posture.

The root occurs 13 times in 12 verses: one imperative, اقْنُتِي, and one jussive imperfect, يَقْنُتْ, from Form I, together with eleven active participles. Eight occurrences are in the forms قانت، قانتون، قانتين, and three in the feminine plural قانتات. قَنَتَ is the perfect form used to identify the verb; that form itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

An initial definition can be stated as follows:

Q N T in Qur’anic usage: obedience and commitment to it. Applied to a person, it can describe devotion and dutiful commitment; in kullun lahu qānitūn, all are described in relation to the command of The God. Context clarifies the duration and character of this state. Standing, silence and a particular supplication are not required meanings in every occurrence.

Pre-Qur’anic Arabic: a poetic witness and the limits of the evidence

Reconstructing an earliest meaning requires evidence that shows a word in use, rather than a list of meanings. The evidence available here does not establish a single physical image as the certain origin of every use of ق ن ت.

In the collected poetry attributed to al-Shanfarā, a pre-Islamic poet, the poem beginning وإنك لو تدرين أن رب مشرب contains this verse:

وَوَادٍ بَعِيدِ الْعُمْقِ ضَنْكٍ جُمَاعُهُ
مَرَاصِدُ أَيْمٍ قَانِتِ الرَّأْسِ أَخْوَفُ

An approximate translation is: “And many a deep ravine, narrow of passage, a lurking place for a fearsome snake with lowered head.” قانت الرأس describes the snake’s head. “With lowered head” is a descriptive reading of the expression; the verse itself does not establish religious obedience or moral humility on the snake’s part.

This attributed verse shows qānit used to describe an animal outside the setting of Salat. Both its historical attribution and the expression’s semantic range nevertheless require caution. One verse cannot establish the basic meaning or the order in which every branch developed.

The Safaitic inscription AMSI 190 records qnt in the genealogy “Gd son of Ġyr son of Qnt.” This attests a personal name. It establishes neither the meaning “obedience” nor, by itself, that the name derives from this Qur’anic root. Matching consonants in a name are insufficient to establish its etymology.

The poetic witness can therefore be retained with its limits stated, but neither it nor an inscribed name securely reconstructs a pre-Qur’anic meaning of “obedience.” For Qur’anic meaning, the Qur’an’s own range of uses provides the clearest basis for this study.

Kullun lahu qānitūn: a description embracing all beings

One of the most important verses for establishing the root’s scope appears in Al-Baqarah:

وَقَالُوا اتَّخَذَ اللَّهُ وَلَدًا ۗ سُبْحَانَهُ ۖ بَلْ لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ ۖ كُلٌّ لَهُ قَانِتُونَ

“They said, ‘The God has taken a child.’ Glory be to Him! Rather, whatever is in the heavens and earth belongs to Him; all are obedient to Him.”

Al-Baqarah 2:116

Almost the same wording appears again in Ar-Rum:

وَلَهُ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ ۖ كُلٌّ لَهُ قَانِتُونَ

“Whoever is in the heavens and earth belongs to Him; all are obedient to Him.”

Ar-Rum 30:26

These verses matter because قانتون is predicated of “all.”

If qunūt meant only a particular prayer supplication, silence during Salat, or even a particular voluntary act of worship, كل له قانتون would be difficult to explain.

These two contexts highlight the relation of all beings to the command of The God. Explaining this as “not being outside His command and authority” is a reading of the verse’s encompassing statement; it should not replace the word’s meaning in every occurrence.

For a human being, qunūt can describe conscious, ethical obedience. In كل له قانتون, however, its scope extends beyond an individual believer’s voluntary obedience.

For this reason, simply translating it as “all worship Him” says more than the word itself. The Qur’an is not saying that every human being is morally obedient. The expression is broader: all are qānitūn in relation to Him.

Abraham: qunūt as an enduring quality

Of Abraham, the Qur’an says:

إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ كَانَ أُمَّةً قَانِتًا لِلَّهِ حَنِيفًا وَلَمْ يَكُ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

“Abraham was a community, obedient to The God, a ḥanīf, and he was not among those who associate partners.”

An-Nahl 16:120

Here the complement is explicit:

قَانِتًا لِلَّهِ — “obedient to The God.”

In this occurrence, qunūt is a state or quality in relation to The God.

The sentence mentions neither a particular prayer, nor silence, nor physical standing. Alongside ḥanīfan, qānitan is one of Abraham’s general qualities.

“Obedient,” “committed in obedience” or “steadfast in obedience to Him” fits this context better than “supplicating” or “standing.”

Here qānit describes Abraham and expresses his commitment. The active participle alone, however, does not establish uninterrupted permanence; the sense of steadfastness comes from the verse’s overall description of Abraham.

Mary: qunūt is not identical to prostration or bowing

Mary is addressed:

يَا مَرْيَمُ اقْنُتِي لِرَبِّكِ وَاسْجُدِي وَارْكَعِي مَعَ الرَّاكِعِينَ

“Mary, be obedient to your Lord, prostrate, and bow with those who bow.”

Aal Imran 3:43

The sentence’s structure is revealing.

First:

اقنتي لربك — “Be obedient to your Lord.”

Then:

واسجدي — “And prostrate.”

And then:

واركعي — “And bow.”

The sentence mentions qunūt, prostration and bowing separately. Coordination alone does not prove that their meanings never overlap. Read alongside Az-Zumar 39:9, however, where the qānit person is described as both prostrating and standing, it gives clear grounds for not reducing qunūt to one bodily posture.

The same Mary is described at the end of At-Tahrim:

وَمَرْيَمَ ابْنَتَ عِمْرَانَ الَّتِي أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهِ مِنْ رُوحِنَا وَصَدَّقَتْ بِكَلِمَاتِ رَبِّهَا وَكُتُبِهِ وَكَانَتْ مِنَ الْقَانِتِينَ

“And Mary, daughter of Imran, who guarded her chastity; We breathed into it of Our spirit. She affirmed her Lord’s words and His books, and she was among the obedient.”

At-Tahrim 66:12

كانت من القانتين describes her character and relationship with her Lord in general; it is not the name of a brief posture in prayer.

Qānitun ānāʾa al-layl: qunūt embraces both prostration and standing

Az-Zumar provides another example that challenges “qānit = only standing”:

أَمَّنْ هُوَ قَانِتٌ آنَاءَ اللَّيْلِ سَاجِدًا وَقَائِمًا يَحْذَرُ الْآخِرَةَ وَيَرْجُو رَحْمَةَ رَبِّهِ ۗ قُلْ هَلْ يَسْتَوِي الَّذِينَ يَعْلَمُونَ وَالَّذِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ ۗ إِنَّمَا يَتَذَكَّرُ أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

“Is one who is devoutly obedient during the hours of the night, prostrating and standing, wary of the Hereafter and hoping for his Lord’s mercy…? Say: Are those who know equal to those who do not know? Only people of understanding take heed.”

Az-Zumar 39:9

The person is qānit, and in this verse that state accompanies both:

سَاجِدًا — “prostrating,”

and:

قَائِمًا — “standing.”

The description applies through both postures.

Qānit therefore cannot merely name physical standing. Qunūt describes the person’s worship and commitment more broadly; prostration and standing are its practical expressions in this verse.

The verse also shows qunūt associated with a span of time:

آناء الليل — “hours of the night.”

آناء الليل refers to times during the night, not necessarily the entire night. This phrase specifies the time of worship in this verse. The root alone does not mean “worship throughout the night” or prescribe a particular duration.

Wa-qūmū li-llāhi qānitīn: standing in a state of qunūt

Al-Baqarah states:

حَافِظُوا عَلَى الصَّلَوَاتِ وَالصَّلَاةِ الْوُسْطَىٰ وَقُومُوا لِلَّهِ قَانِتِينَ

“Maintain the Salats and the middle Salat, and stand before The God in obedience.”

Al-Baqarah 2:238

In this setting of Salat, قوموا can be understood as rising and standing. Grammatically, قانتين is a circumstantial expression describing how that standing is undertaken.

Translating قانتين merely as “standing” would obscure an important distinction between the two words:

قوموا commands rising or standing;
قانتين describes the addressees’ state as they do so.

“Stand obediently,” “stand in a state of devotion and obedience” or “stand before The God in qunūt” better preserves this distinction.

The Qur’an’s own range of uses does not allow قانتين to be restricted to silence. The same family describes Abraham, Mary and all beings, and appears in lists of believers’ qualities.

Silence may accompany a state of worship, but possible association does not make qunūt and silence identical in meaning.

The verbal noun qunūt and the forms actually used in the Qur’an

A term used to explain a word family must be distinguished from the forms actually found in the Qur’an.

The verbal noun قُنُوت itself does not occur in the Qur’an. The attested forms are the verbs اقنتي / يقنت and the active participles قانت، قانتون، قانتين، قانتات. In this study, qunūt is therefore an explanatory name for the family’s state and action, not a word quoted directly from a verse.

The familiar name “the qunūt supplication” does not establish that every qānit in the Qur’an is someone reciting that specific prayer. Such a restriction would require evidence in the context itself.

For example:

Abraham is قانت لله;
all in the heavens and earth are له قانتون;
men and women are described as قانتين وقانتات.

None of these statements concerns that specific supplication.

Qānitīn and qānitāt: a quality shared by men and women

Al-Ahzab 33:35 is an important witness to this quality. It describes men and women in parallel, including:

القانتين والقانتات — “the obedient men and the obedient women.”

Qunūt here is a religious quality shared by men and women, just as submission, faith, truthfulness, patience, humility, charity, fasting and remembrance are presented in parallel for both.

This matters for understanding قانتات elsewhere. The feminine form does not contain a special lexical meaning such as “obedience to a husband.” It expresses the same quality as qānit, in the feminine.

At-Tahrim 66:5 likewise describes possible wives with a set of qualities:

مسلمات، مؤمنات، قانتات، تائبات، عابدات، سائحات

Again, qānitāt appears among religious qualities.

Wherever qānitāt occurs, the general meaning must first be retained; context can then establish whether a particular complement or relationship is specified.

Fa-l-ṣāliḥātu qānitāt in An-Nisa 4:34: obedient to whom?

An-Nisa 4:34 states:

فَالصَّالِحَاتُ قَانِتَاتٌ حَافِظَاتٌ لِلْغَيْبِ بِمَا حَفِظَ اللَّهُ

قانتات has no explicit complement in this sentence.

This is a significant lexical point.

When the Qur’an specifies the direction of qunūt explicitly, it can name it:

In Aal Imran 3:43:

اقنتي لربك — “Be obedient to your Lord.”

In An-Nahl 16:120:

قانتا لله — “obedient to The God.”

And concerning the Prophet’s wives in Al-Ahzab 33:31:

يقنت منكن لله ورسوله — “whoever among you is obedient to The God and His messenger.”

The word qānitāt therefore does not itself identify the party to whom obedience is directed.

An-Nisa 4:34 concerns family relationships, and that context matters for interpretation. Yet qānitāt = obedient to their husbands does not follow from the word alone. Adding “their husbands” requires a separate contextual argument.

This caution accords with the feminine form elsewhere. The same qānitāt appears within general religious qualities in Al-Ahzab 33:35 and At-Tahrim 66:5.

Qānitāt can be understood as “obedient” or “committed in obedience.” The party to whom this obedience is directed must be established from the sentence and its context; the feminine form does not identify that party by itself.

Wa-man yaqnut minkunna li-llāhi wa-rasūlihi: an explicit complement

Al-Ahzab addresses the Prophet’s wives in a more explicit construction:

وَمَنْ يَقْنُتْ مِنْكُنَّ لِلَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَتَعْمَلْ صَالِحًا نُؤْتِهَا أَجْرَهَا مَرَّتَيْنِ وَأَعْتَدْنَا لَهَا رِزْقًا كَرِيمًا

“Whoever among you is obedient to The God and His messenger and does righteous work, We shall give her reward twice over, and We have prepared a generous provision for her.”

Al-Ahzab 33:31

Here, unlike An-Nisa 4:34, the complement is explicit:

لِلَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ — “to The God and His messenger.”

The verb yaqnut expresses the relation of obedience, while the sentence can specify the party to whom it is directed.

The verse also follows qunūt with:

وَتَعْمَلْ صَالِحًا — “and does righteous work.”

Qunūt and righteous work are mentioned separately: one expresses a state of obedience, the other the doing of good. Their association shows a connection, but is insufficient to define one in place of the other.

Qunūt among virtues: its relation to truthfulness, patience and humility

Aal Imran describes believers as:

الصَّابِرِينَ وَالصَّادِقِينَ وَالْقَانِتِينَ وَالْمُنْفِقِينَ وَالْمُسْتَغْفِرِينَ بِالْأَسْحَارِ

“The patient, the truthful, the obedient, those who spend, and those who seek forgiveness before dawn.”

Aal Imran 3:17

Al-Ahzab 33:35 likewise places qānitīn alongside صادقين، صابرين، خاشعين and other qualities.

These associations show qunūt accompanying patience, truthfulness and humility. Coordination alone does not prove an absence of overlap, but neither does it permit unrestricted substitution of these words for one another.

Across the attested uses, reducing qunūt to any one of these is insufficient:

Only humility, only patience, only supplication, or only standing.

These concepts can coexist in a life of worship, while each expresses its own perspective.

Q N T and Ṭ W ʿ: obedience and qunūt

In Al-Ahzab 33:31, يَقْنُتْ ... لِلَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ expresses a relation of obedience. An-Nisa 4:59 commands obedience with أَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ. These contexts clarify the proximity of qunūt and obedience.

Nevertheless, the two should not be treated as complete synonyms without examination.

Most Qur’anic occurrences of this family are active participles describing a person or their state: Abraham is qānit; Mary is among the qānitīn; men and women are qānit; someone is qānit during times of the night.

These examples highlight a state of commitment. Yet neither that emphasis nor the active participle’s pattern makes continuity a requirement in every occurrence.

Nor do these examples establish a rule that ṭāʿah is always a single act while qunūt is always continuous. The duration and character of obedience must be examined in each sentence.

Q N T and KH SH ʿ: obedience and humility

Al-Ahzab 33:35 presents qunūt and khushūʿ as two separate qualities.

It includes both القانتين والقانتات and, later, الخاشعين والخاشعات.

A qānit person can also be khāshiʿ. Their occurrence together establishes neither complete synonymy nor a total absence of overlap.

In the uses examined, qunūt concerns obedience and commitment. This does not justify replacing it everywhere with khushūʿ; equally, the two descriptions’ association does not establish an absolute boundary between them.

Translation challenges

For اقنتي لربك, “be steadfast in obedience to your Lord” or, more briefly, “be obedient to your Lord” is appropriate. “Recite the qunūt supplication” is not.

For قانتا لله describing Abraham, “obedient and committed to The God” or “steadfast in obedience to The God” conveys the semantic field better.

For كل له قانتون, “all are obedient to Him” is more cautious than “all are His worshippers,” since the statement encompasses all beings.

For وقوموا لله قانتين, “stand obediently before The God” or “stand in a state of qunūt” is suitable. Adding “silently” requires evidence beyond the meaning of qānitīn itself.

For أمن هو قانت آناء الليل, “one who remains devoted and obedient during the hours of the night” is more natural than “one who recites the qunūt supplication all night.”

For القانتين والقانتات, “men and women committed in obedience” or “obedient men and women” is appropriate.

For فالصالحات قانتات, “righteous women are obedient” or “righteous women are committed in obedience” retains the general meaning. Adding the party to whom obedience is directed requires an argument from the context of An-Nisa 4:34, not merely from the word’s feminine form.

Summary

The Qur’anic root ق ن ت concerns obedience and commitment to it.

The verse attributed to al-Shanfarā uses qānit al-raʾs to describe a snake. Religious obedience or the origin of every meaning cannot be inferred from it without caution. Nor does the name Qnt in the Safaitic inscription AMSI 190 establish the verb’s meaning or an etymological connection with the Qur’anic word by itself.

The Qur’an’s own uses provide a clearer basis for establishing the semantic range.

كل له قانتون applies the description to all beings in the heavens and earth. It therefore cannot mean only a particular supplication or a worshipper’s bodily posture.

Abraham is قانتا لله, and Mary is told اقنتي لربك. These constructions explicitly relate obedience and commitment to the Lord.

Mary’s address places qunūt alongside prostration and bowing. In Az-Zumar, the qānit person both prostrates and stands. This second example in particular shows why qunūt should not be restricted to physical standing.

In وقوموا لله قانتين, qūmū commands standing, while qānitīn describes the addressees’ state. This grammatical distinction helps preserve the semantic difference. Silence, supplication and prolonged standing do not individually define every occurrence of the root.

The verbal noun qunūt itself does not occur in the Qur’an; there are two verbs and eleven active participles. The name “the qunūt supplication” should not be turned into the meaning of all these forms without evidence.

Masculine and feminine forms share the same meaning. Al-Ahzab 33:35 describes men and women as qānitīn and qānitāt. Accordingly, qānitāt in An-Nisa 4:34 does not lexically specify “obedient to husbands”: it does not name the object of obedience. Any particular complement must be argued from the verse’s context, not from the lexical meaning of qānitāt.

The concise definition is therefore:

Q N T in Qur’anic usage: obedience and commitment to it. Applied to a person, it can describe devotion and dutiful commitment; in kullun lahu qānitūn, all are described in relation to the command of The God. Context must establish the duration, character and direction of this obedience. Standing, silence, humility and supplication can accompany it, but none alone defines all the root’s uses.
