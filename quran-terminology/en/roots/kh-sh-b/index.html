Wood, hardness, and dryness: from a useful natural material to the image of propped-up timbers describing the hypocrites

Arabic khashab means wood: material obtained from tree trunks and branches and used to make tools, houses, beams, vessels, and other objects. Khashabah is a piece of wood, and khushub is its plural.

In early Arabic, this lexical family was not restricted to the name of wood. Its derivatives also expressed hardness, coarseness, dryness, and roughness. Akhshab, for example, described a hard, rocky mountain, while khashīb could describe a coarse or hard person, animal, or object.

The root also appeared in crafts involving the manufacture of tools. Wood not yet shaped into its final form, an unfinished arrow or bow, and even a sword at a particular stage of forging or polishing could be described with this family’s derivatives.

These uses connect the name of a natural material with physical properties such as hardness, dryness, and coarseness.

Such properties are not always negative. Hard wood may be suitable for a strong beam; a tree trunk may become a pillar or part of a roof. By contrast, a piece of wood left unused against a wall may perform no particular task in that position, despite its large, sturdy appearance.

The Qur’an uses this root only once, in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4:

كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُسَنَّدَةٌ

Meaning: “As though they were timbers propped up.”

The verse describes a group of hypocrites whose physical appearance attracts attention and whose speech seems worth hearing, but then compares them to propped-up timbers.

Understanding this image requires distinguishing three things: khushub itself, the description musannadah, and the characteristics of the group being compared to them.

Khushub means pieces of wood. Their being propped up comes from another word; the ineffectiveness or emptiness of the people’s behavior emerges from the comparison’s context.

Hypocrisy, lack of understanding, lack of faith, and worthlessness should therefore not be treated as the lexical meaning of khashab itself.

1. Forms of the root and its semantic core

The root خ ش ب occurs only once in the Qur’an, as خُشُبٌ in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4.

This noun has plural meaning and is commonly analyzed as a broken plural of khashabah. In the standard reading of Ḥafṣ, both khāʾ and shīn carry ḍammah.

Its singular is khashabah. Lexicons also record the plural forms khashab, khushub, khushb, and khushbān.

Other important Arabic derivatives include:

  • خَشَب, khashab: wood, especially thick wood used in making objects.
  • خَشَبَة, khashabah: a piece of wood.
  • خُشُب and خُشْب, khushub and khushb: established plurals for pieces of wood.
  • خَشَّاب, khashshāb: someone who works with or sells timber.
  • أَخْشَب, akhshab: hard, coarse, and rugged; geographically, a hard and rocky mountain.
  • أَخَاشِب, akhāshib: the plural of akhshab, especially of mountains.
  • خَشْبَاء, khashbāʾ: the feminine of akhshab, describing something dry, hard, or rough.
  • خَشِيب, khashīb: hard and coarse; of an implement, newly made or roughly shaped and not yet finished. Some uses also mean polished.
  • مَخْشُوب, makhshūb: something carved, fashioned, or mixed with something else.
  • خَشَبَ ـ يَخْشِبُ, khashaba–yakhshibu: in certain uses, carved, polished, or mixed one thing with another.
  • تَخَشَّبَ, takhashshaba: of camels, fed on woody material or dry branches.
  • اِخْشَوْشَبَ, ikhshawshaba: became hard, coarse, or rough; of human life, became accustomed to hardship and few comforts.
  • خَشِب, khashib: coarse, hard, or unfinished; also used of people and animals.

Lexical sources record these forms, but none except khushub occurs in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole occurrence, Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4, word ten; plural noun

The semantic core according to Ibn Fāris

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْخَاءُ وَالشِّينُ وَالْبَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى خُشُونَةٍ وَغِلَظٍ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-shīnu wa-al-bāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā khushūnatin wa-ghilaẓin

That is, the root indicates roughness, coarseness, and hardness.

He then identifies the hard mountain, a sword or arrow being made, the large coarse camel, and the dry, rugged forehead as uses of this family.

He concludes by connecting these uses with khashab, wood.

This analysis is semantically understandable. Wood, especially dry, thick wood, can be hard and coarse. Arabic also uses these properties to describe mountains, human or animal bodies, and manufactured tools.

The name of a material should nevertheless be distinguished from its properties.

Not all wood, for example, is extremely hard or rough; fresh, soft wood also exists. A stone may likewise be hard without being made of wood.

Khashab should therefore not mean merely hardness in every use.

Conversely, akhshab describing a mountain does not necessarily mean a mountain made of wood; its coarseness and hardness are prominent.

Two main directions should therefore be retained: wood as a natural material, and the properties of hardness, coarseness, and dryness extended through some derivatives of the same family.

These witnesses alone do not establish the historical sequence of every meaning. We cannot say with certainty either that the name of wood came first and generated all the descriptions, or that hardness existed first and subsequently produced the name of wood.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: wood, roughness, and coarseness

2. Early uses: wood, mountains, and manufactured implements

Wood as a building material

Arabic khashabah is a piece of wood.

Once cut, trees can become pieces used for roofs, beams, doors, tools, or other objects.

Wood here is a useful material whose value depends on its kind, size, strength, and intended use.

A thick piece may suit a roof beam. If placed unused beside a wall, it remains wood but performs no structural task in that position.

This difference matters for understanding the Qur’anic comparison.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 does not merely say they resemble wood; it says they resemble propped-up timbers.

The wood’s position therefore contributes to the image.

If wood itself were useless in every position, musannadah would not have such clear significance.

Akhshab: a hard, rocky mountain

Early Arabic used akhshab for a hard, large, rugged mountain.

Ibn Fāris and Al-Jawharī record this use.

Lexicons also identify al-Akhshabān as two mountains around Mecca.

Akhshab here does not mean a mountain made of wood.

The mountain’s hardness and massiveness are the prominent features.

To explain this meaning, Al-Aṣmaʿī transmits a line of rajaz:

تَحْسَبُ فَوْقَ الشَّوْلِ مِنْهُ أَخْشَبَا

taḥsabu fawqa al-shawli minhu akhshabā

Translation:

“You would think that above the she-camels with dwindling milk a great, rugged mountain had risen.”

The male camel is compared to a mountain in stature and bulk.

Akhshaban names a hard, massive mountain, rather than wood placed on the camels’ backs.

Abū ʿUbayd’s Gharīb al-Ḥadīth and later lexicons transmit this line, but that report does not make its author or exact date clear. Asās al-Balāghah attributes it to Ruʾbah, so it is not anonymous in every report; Abū ʿUbayd’s citation alone nevertheless does not establish its date.

It is therefore evidence of an early use of akhshab, rather than poetry whose pre-Islamic attribution is conclusively established.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Asās al-Balāghah: the akhshab rajaz and Al-Zamakhsharī’s attribution to Ruʾbah

Khashīb and makhshūb: unfinished or polished implements

One important field of this family is the making of arrows, bows, and swords.

Lexicons use sahmun khashībun for an arrow that has received its first shaping but has not yet been fully manufactured and finished.

Qawsun khashībun likewise describes a bow at a stage of manufacture.

In making such implements, wood or another raw material must be cut, shaped, straightened, and finished to obtain the desired form.

The implement may therefore have a recognizable overall shape without yet being ready for proper use.

This use connects with hardness and roughness.

Sources differ, however, over sayfun khashībun.

One explanation describes a sword whose forging or shaping has begun but which has not been properly polished.

Another uses khashīb for a polished sword.

Al-Jawharī records these as contrary uses of the word.

Ibn Fāris likewise explains that the first use concerned an initial stage of manufacture, after which the word was also applied to a polished sword.

Khashīb should therefore not always mean only unfinished or only finished and polished.

A pre-Islamic witness: makhshūbah in Aws ibn Ḥajar

The pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar says:

يُجَلْجِلُهَا طَوْرَيْنِ ثُمَّ يُفِيضُهَا
كَمَا أُرْسِلَتْ مَخْشُوبَةٌ لَمْ تُقَوَّمِ

yujaljiluhā ṭawrayni thumma yufīḍuhā / ka-mā ursilat makhshūbatun lam tuqawwami

Approximate translation:

“He shakes them twice and then lets them go, as an arrow roughly shaped but not yet straightened is released.”

Makhshūbatun lam tuqawwami refers to something that has undergone some carving or manufacture but has not yet been fully straightened and finished. The preceding verse in the Dīwān also mentions ashum, arrows, so that image matters for the comparison. Lisān al-ʿArab nevertheless quotes the verse as a description of horses; differences in wording and explanation should be retained.

Lexical explanations connect this expression with an arrow or unfinished wooden implement.

Makhshūbah here is therefore not a completely raw piece of wood; its manufacture has begun without reaching its final form.

This use shows that derivatives of خ ش ب were established in the field of making and finishing implements in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Lexical reports vary in the first hemistich, including forms such as fa-jaljalahā and fa-ḥalḥalahā.

Aws’s Dīwān records yujaljiluhā.

Makhshūbatun lam tuqawwami is nevertheless clearer in meaning and important for understanding this branch of the root.

Jamharat al-Lughah and its note on Aws’s Dīwān: yujaljiluhā and textual variants

Another early poetic witness: makhshūb in Al-Aʿshā

Al-Aʿshā describes a horse:

قَافِلٍ جُرْشُعٍ تَرَاهُ كَتَيْسِ الرَّ
بْلِ لَا مُقْرِفٍ وَلَا مَخْشُوبِ

qāfilin jurshuʿin tarāhu ka-taysi al-rabli / lā muqrifin wa-lā makhshūbi

Approximate translation:

“A lean horse, broad in the flanks, that you see like a male grazing on rabl herbage: neither of inferior lineage nor makhshūb.”

Makhshūb appears beside muqrif in this verse.

Arabic muqrif concerns an animal considered inferior in lineage, particularly on its father’s side.

The meaning of makhshūb in this particular verse is disputed.

Abū ʿUbayd explains it in terms of mixed or impure pedigree.

Ibn Khālawayh instead says it describes a horse not properly raised or trained, likening it to a wooden bowl whose manufacture is not sound or complete.

These explanations are not identical.

One emphasizes lineage; the other, finishing and training.

Both, however, occur in a context where the poet presents his horse as free of a possible defect.

The verse is recorded in Al-Aʿshā’s Dīwān and early lexicons.

Al-Aʿshā belonged to the pre-Islamic and earliest Islamic periods. This witness matters for the family’s early Arabic usage, but without dating this particular verse, its composition before Qur’anic revelation cannot be treated as certain.

The different explanations of makhshūb should nevertheless be retained. Neither should be declared its exclusive meaning here without conclusive evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Aʿshā’s witness and the pedigree and training explanations of makhshūb

Dryness and hardship in life

Arabic takhashshabati al-ibilu describes camels feeding on branches or dry herbage.

ʿAyshun khashibun is also recorded for a hard life with few comforts.

Ikhshawshaba likewise describes someone becoming hard or coarse, or accustomed to a life with few comforts.

These uses concern hardness and dryness, but do not establish that hardship is intrinsically a moral virtue or that comfort always corrupts.

The word simply describes a difficult condition with few comforts.

A person’s moral worth emerges from behavior, responsibility, and decisions, rather than how rough or simple the clothing and food are.

Khashaba al-shiʿra: unpolished speech

Ibn al-Sikkīt records another use:

خَشَبَ الشِّعْرَ

Meaning: composed poetry as it came, without careful polishing or extensive revision.

Al-Zamakhsharī’s Asās al-Balāghah uses this expression to explain the different ways Jarīr and Al-Farazdaq composed poetry.

Roughness and lack of finishing have thus extended from physical materials and implements to speech and human work.

This extension does not make every unrevised poem worthless or incorrect.

A poet may produce beautiful, sound speech without many revisions. Conversely, lengthy polishing does not necessarily produce a good work.

Early sources themselves discuss the relative merits of Jarīr’s and Al-Farazdaq’s poetry despite their different methods.

This branch illustrates Arabic’s range of use, but is not the direct meaning of khushub in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4.

Asās al-Balāghah and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: khashaba al-shiʿra and polishing speech

3. Qur’anic usage: timbers propped up

وَإِذَا رَأَيْتَهُمْ تُعْجِبُكَ أَجْسَامُهُمْ وَإِنْ يَقُولُوا تَسْمَعْ لِقَوْلِهِمْ كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُسَنَّدَةٌ يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ هُمُ الْعَدُوُّ فَاحْذَرْهُمْ قَاتَلَهُمُ اللَّهُ أَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ

wa-idhā raʾaytahum tuʿjibuka ajsāmuhum wa-in yaqūlū tasmaʿ li-qawlihim kaʾannahum khushubun musannadatun yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim humu al-ʿaduwwu fa-iḥdharhum qātalahumu allāhu annā yuʾfakūna

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“When you see them, their bodies impress you; if they speak, you listen to their words. They are like timbers propped up. They think every cry is against them. They are the enemy, so beware of them. May The God destroy them! How they are turned away from the truth!”

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

The verse concerns a group of hypocrites introduced at the surah’s beginning.

In Al-Munāfiqūn 63:1, they come to the Prophet and testify to his messengership, but the Qur’an testifies to their falsehood.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:2 speaks of their oaths as a covering, through which they turn people away from The God’s path.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:3 also speaks of their believing and then disbelieving, and concludes that they do not understand.

By the time Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 compares them to propped-up timbers, the audience is therefore already acquainted with this group’s duplicity in speech and behavior.

This background matters: the comparison is not based merely on their physical appearance or handsome faces.

تُعْجِبُكَ أَجْسَامُهُمْ: appearance attracts attention

The verse first says that, when you see them, their bodies impress you.

Ajsām comes from ج س م and concerns the human body or physical frame.

This part shows that their physical appearance can be striking.

The verse does not itself say that a handsome body, tall stature, or an attractive appearance indicates hypocrisy.

That conclusion would run against the verse’s argument.

The point is that outward appearance alone is insufficient to assess the truth of a person’s behavior and belief.

Someone may have an attractive appearance and also act uprightly.

Another person may look impressive but speak and act inconsistently with the truth he claims.

This verse concerns the second group; their identification emerges from the context and specified behavior, rather than bodily shape.

Study of J S M

وَإِنْ يَقُولُوا تَسْمَعْ لِقَوْلِهِمْ: speech that attracts a listener

The verse continues: if they speak, you listen to their words.

This shows that they can attract attention through speech too.

The expression does not itself establish that all their speech attained the highest level of grammar, argument, or eloquence.

Early commentaries discuss their eloquence and fluency. This accords with attracting a listener’s attention, but the verse itself states only that their speech is listened to.

The essential distinction is between speech that attracts a hearing and content that is true.

A person may present a claim with an engaging voice, orderly expression, and great confidence while lacking evidence for it.

Conversely, someone speaking simply and without much skill may say something true and well supported.

Eloquence and engaging delivery are therefore not independent measures of truth.

This conclusion should not erase the value of clear, beautiful speech. The verse criticizes a group with specified behavior, rather than everyone who speaks well.

كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُسَنَّدَةٌ: the central image

Kaʾanna introduces a comparison.

The pronoun hum refers to the same group.

Khushub means pieces of wood or timbers.

Musannadah describes them and comes from س ن د, rather than خ ش ب.

It denotes something placed against a support or in a supported position.

The image is of timbers propped against something.

Imagine several wooden beams placed beside a wall. They may be tall, thick, smooth, and impressive in appearance, but in that position they do not perform the function of an installed pillar or a beam used in a roof.

They may look sturdy in form, but their position indicates that they have not yet been put to work.

Al-Zamakhsharī’s Al-Kashshāf emphasizes this point: wood installed in a roof or wall contributes to the building, whereas wood merely propped against a wall is unused in that position.

This is a defensible explanation of the image.

The word’s meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from the comparison’s function.

Khushub does not mean useless timbers.

Nor does musannadah alone mean rotten or hollow.

Beside the description of the hypocrites’ appearance and speech, the image of propped-up timbers criticizes the gap between outward display and the reality of their behavior.

Are the timbers necessarily rotten or hollow?

Some early commentaries compare these timbers to rotten or internally damaged wood.

Another reading compares them to carved wooden figures.

These explanations seek to clarify the image, but the expression itself contains no word meaning rotten, hollow, or a carved figure.

The Qur’an says:

خُشُبٌ مُسَنَّدَةٌ

Meaning: timbers propped up.

Internal decay or wooden figures may therefore be discussed as explanatory readings, but should not become the word’s certain meaning or part of the basic translation.

The visible image suffices for the main point: timbers with outward form and bulk which, in their propped position, do not perform the expected function of an active, useful structure.

Al-Kashshāf: propped-up timbers, the comparison’s function, and explanatory readings

يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ: fearing every cry

After the comparison with timbers comes:

يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ

Meaning: they think every cry is against them.

This part shows another condition of the same group.

Their appearance and speech attract attention at first; the continuation describes their suspicious, apprehensive response.

This concern is understandable in the preceding verses’ context of protective oaths and duplicity.

Someone concealing the truth of his conduct may fear exposure and interpret every unexpected report or sound as directed against him.

This connection should nevertheless not become a general judgment about everyone experiencing anxiety.

Danger, illness, or unsafe circumstances may make a person sensitive to sounds without being hypocritical or deceptive.

The verse concerns a specific group whose other behavior is also identified in context.

Nor does khashab mean fear or anxiety. Suspicion is expressed by the independent sentence yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim.

هُمُ الْعَدُوُّ فَاحْذَرْهُمْ: a warning based on behavior

The Qur’an then describes their hostility and the need for caution toward them.

This warning belongs to the same context of concealing behavior behind oaths, obstructing The God’s path, and inconsistency between outward claims and actual conduct.

The propped-up timbers comparison should therefore not become a means of accusing people on the basis of their faces, posture, or manner of speaking.

Nor can every critic or opponent be identified with this verse merely because of disagreement.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:7 and Al-Munāfiqūn 63:8 also specify this group’s political and social behavior, including efforts to cut off financial assistance and claims of superiority and expelling others.

These are behavioral indications important to understanding the surah’s context.

Khashab itself, however, includes none of these actions in its lexical meaning.

4. Khushub and khushb in the readings

In the standard reading of Ḥafṣ, the word is khushub, with ḍammah on both khāʾ and shīn.

Some other readings have khushb, with sukūn on shīn.

Al-Ṭabarī regards both as established readings and explains that both are valid Arabic forms.

He compares other plurals whose middle consonant may be pronounced either with a vowel or without one.

Morphological sources offer different explanations of whether khushub is a direct plural of khashabah or a plural formed at a further stage of pluralization.

These differences do not decisively alter the verse’s central meaning.

Both forms refer to pieces of wood.

The base text following Ḥafṣ therefore retains khushub, while khushb may be noted in discussing the readings.

The vowel difference does not establish that one reading means hard wood and the other rotten or hollow wood. No such independent semantic difference is demonstrated by the readings themselves.

Al-Ṭabarī: the khushub and khushb readings and plural analysis

A grammatical point about musannadah

Khushub is a noun with plural meaning referring to nonrational things; it is commonly analyzed as a broken plural of khashabah.

In Arabic, nonrational plurals can commonly take a feminine singular adjective.

Musannadah therefore appears in the feminine singular.

This construction does not mean there is only one piece of wood.

The plural meaning should be retained in translation:

“Timbers propped up.”

Musannadah is also a Form II passive participle: timbers that have been propped up.

It should not therefore be translated as timbers that support others; a natural formulation should preserve the direction of the support.

5. Differences from neighboring roots

KH SH B (khashab) compared with SH J R (shajar): wood and trees

Arabic shajar concerns trees.

A tree is a living organism with roots, trunk, branches, and leaves, capable of growing and bearing fruit.

Khashab in its established use, however, is wooden material, especially wood separated from a tree or intended for making objects.

This difference matters for the Qur’anic image.

Khushubun musannadatun does not depict living, rooted trees.

It describes timbers propped against a support.

This does not make every piece of cut wood useless. Wood can have many uses after separation from the tree.

The timbers’ particular propped-up condition matters in this verse.

Study of SH J R

KH SH B (khashab) compared with Ḥ Ṭ B (ḥaṭab): wood and firewood

Arabic ḥaṭab concerns wood and material gathered for lighting a fire.

Khashab is a more general name for wood and wooden pieces.

A piece of wood may serve as fuel, but may also be used to build a house, door, or tool.

Khushub in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 should therefore not be translated directly as firewood.

The verse concerns propped-up timbers, rather than wood prepared for burning.

Nor does this comparison alone establish that the people are necessarily being identified as firewood for Hell. Such a claim requires independent evidence.

Study of Ḥ Ṭ B

KH SH B (khashab) compared with J DH ʿ (jidhʿ): wood and a tree trunk

Arabic jidhʿ concerns a tree’s trunk or main stem.

Maryam 19:23 and Maryam 19:25 use jidhʿi al-nakhlati for the trunk of a date palm.

Khashab, however, can refer to wooden pieces of different shapes and uses.

A tree trunk can have woody tissue, but not every piece of wood is an entire trunk.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 uses khushub, rather than judhūʿ.

“Tree trunks” may therefore restrict the image beyond what the word itself expresses.

“Timbers” or “pieces of wood” is more suitable.

Study of J DH ʿ

KH SH B (khashab) compared with KH SH N (khushūnah): wood and roughness

Arabic khushūnah concerns roughness, hardness, and unevenness in its lexical use.

Ibn Fāris likewise identifies hardness and coarseness as the shared core of خ ش ب.

These are nevertheless independent roots.

Khashab denotes wood, while derivatives such as akhshab can express roughness and hardness.

Kh-sh-n, by contrast, directly concerns roughness and hardness as properties, rather than a particular material.

Khushub in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 should therefore not be translated merely as rough objects.

The referent is timbers, whose characteristics contribute to the comparison.

KH SH B (khashab) compared with S N D (sanad): wood and propping up

These roots occur beside one another in the same Qur’anic expression.

Khashab names the material or pieces of wood.

Musannadah comes from س ن د and expresses their being propped up.

The full image therefore results from two independent words occurring together.

If khashab alone is made to mean useless and propped up, musannadah loses its role.

Conversely, retaining only wood and dropping the description loses an important part of the Qur’anic image.

Both elements should therefore be translated.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4, the most suitable equivalent for khushub is timbers or pieces of wood.

Since the word is plural, its plural meaning should be retained.

“Pieces of wood” is especially defensible if we wish to clarify that living trees are not meant.

“Dry trunks” may create an image narrower than the word itself.

“Rotten wood” and “hollow wood” should likewise not enter the basic translation, since the verse does not expressly give those descriptions.

For the full expression, a suitable proposal is:

“As though they were timbers propped up.”

An explanation may clarify that, despite their form and bulk, these timbers perform no active function in their propped position. In the verse’s context, the image criticizes the distance between the hypocrites’ impressive appearance and the reality of their behavior.

This explanation should not replace direct translation of the words.

Brief root meaning and the word’s meaning in the verse

خُشُبٌ — Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4

Root: خ ش ب

Grammar: A noun with plural meaning, indefinite and nominative, serving as the predicate of kaʾanna; commonly analyzed as a broken plural of khashabah. In Ḥafṣ’s reading it is khushub.

Brief root meaning: Wood and wooden material; other derivatives also express hardness, coarseness, and dryness as established features of this family.

Meaning in this verse: Timbers or pieces of wood, the object of comparison for the group concerned. Their propped-up condition is expressed by the independent description musannadah; criticism of their behavior and understanding emerges from the comparison’s context.

This explanation keeps the word’s own meaning distinct from musannadah and the comparison’s implications.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root خ ش ب connects with wood and the properties of hardness, coarseness, and dryness.

Lexicons use this family for wood, hard mountains, coarse bodies, difficult living, and implements undergoing shaping and finishing.

Early poetry by Aws ibn Ḥajar and Al-Aʿshā shows that its derivatives were also established in toolmaking and descriptions of animals.

The contrary meanings of khashīb for swords and the different explanations of makhshūb in Al-Aʿshā nevertheless show that every derivative should not be reduced to one narrow meaning.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4, in the form khushub.

Despite their impressive appearance and engaging speech, a group of hypocrites are compared to propped-up timbers.

The image occurs within a context of duplicity, protective oaths, and lack of proper understanding.

Khashab itself nevertheless does not mean hypocrisy, lack of understanding, or uselessness. The propped-up condition comes from musannadah; the moral criticism emerges from the full verse’s context.

Nor does the verse permit attractive appearance, a strong body, eloquence, or even worry and fear alone to determine hypocrisy. The Qur’an specifies other behavior and characteristics of the group concerned.

Final definition: In nominal use, خ ش ب denotes wood and wooden pieces; in other derivatives it connects with hardness, coarseness, and dryness. Khushub in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 means timbers and, together with musannadah, forms the image of timbers propped up. The comparison’s critical meaning emerges from the image’s relation to the appearance, speech, and behavior of the group concerned, rather than from khashab alone. Brief root meaning: Wood and pieces of timber; in other derivatives, hardness, coarseness, and dryness.
