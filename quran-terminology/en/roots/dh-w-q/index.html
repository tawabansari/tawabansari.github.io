Tasting and perceiving flavor; experiencing and undergoing a condition; causing someone to taste blessings, suffering, and the consequences of deeds

Arabic DH W Q begins with tasting and perceiving flavor, and extends to experiencing or undergoing a condition. A person may taste food, experience the bitterness of someone’s conduct, enjoy blessings and ease, or taste suffering and the consequences of their deeds.

A significant feature runs through these uses: something is not merely presented as information or imagined; the person encounters and experiences it.

Arabic uses dhāqa al-ṭaʿāma for tasting food, dhāqa al-baʾsa for experiencing hardship, and dhāqa mā ʿinda fulān for testing and discovering what another person has to offer.

The Qur’an gives this root a wide range. Adam and his wife taste the tree; every soul is described as tasting death; people taste mercy and blessing; groups experience the consequences of their deeds; and commands to “taste” express confrontation with suffering and punishment.

These uses show that Qur’anic dhawq is neither confined to food’s flavor nor always negative. Tasting also need not mean a slight or fleeting experience. It may mark an initial acquaintance, but some verses use the same word for intense suffering or a lasting condition.

DH W Q occurs 63 times in 61 Qur’anic verses. Its forms fall into four groups: 36 Form I verbs, 22 Form IV verbs, two masculine plural active participles, and three feminine active participles in dhāʾiqatu al-mawt, “tasting death.”

1. Core Meaning and Early Arabic Usage

Tasting: discovering flavor through experience

The family’s basic verb is dhāqa yadhūqu. Recorded verbal nouns include dhawq, dhawāq, madhāq, and madhāqah.

Its semantic center can be understood as testing something by tasting and perceiving its flavor; «اختبار الشيء من جهة تطعّم» expresses that connection.

Arabic expressions include:

«ذَاقَ الطَّعَامَ»: he tasted the food.

«ذَاقَ الشَّرَابَ»: he tasted the drink.

«ذَاقَ الْحُلْوَ»: he tasted sweetness.

«ذَاقَ الْمُرَّ»: he tasted bitterness.

«مَذَاقُهُ طَيِّبٌ»: its flavor is pleasant.

In these uses, tasting is a way of discovering a quality perceived through the mouth and tongue.

Someone may see food’s shape and color, or hear descriptions of its flavor, but tasting allows direct experience of its taste.

This does not establish that dhawq always produces correct and complete knowledge. Someone may taste something without knowing its ingredients, origin, or every property.

Tasting is a kind of experience, but even direct experience does not necessarily provide complete knowledge.

Does tasting always mean a small amount?

Ordinary usage often applies dhawq to a small amount sampled to discover flavor, whereas akl can concern consuming a larger amount of food.

The difference is familiar in everyday language. Someone may taste a single mouthful to discover the flavor without eating the whole dish.

However, dhawq is not restricted to small amounts; the Qur’an also uses it for severe punishment.

Thus, tasting does not necessarily mean a brief, mild, or limited experience.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:56, tasting punishment appears within a context of continuing suffering. Qur’anic expressions of tasting punishment therefore cannot always mean sampling only a small portion.

The experience’s intensity, extent, and duration must be determined from other words and the context.

Dhawq: testing and discovering things beyond food

Arabic dhāqa mā ʿinda fulān is used for trying what another person has to offer.

It need not mean eating that person’s food. One may experience their conduct, abilities, generosity, or another quality in practice.

Dhāqa al-qawsa is also recorded for testing a bow: drawing its string to discover its stiffness, flexibility, and strength.

Nothing is tasted with the mouth here. The bow is tested through contact, drawing, and observing its response.

This illustrates the established Arabic extension of dhawq from oral experience to directly testing other things.

Amrun mustadhāq is likewise recorded for something tried and known through experience.

Here dhawq concerns more than knowing a report; experiencing something contributes to knowing it.

A pre-Islamic witness: the bitterness of human conduct

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād says in his Muʿallaqah:

فَإِذَا ظُلِمْتُ فَإِنَّ ظُلْمِي بَاسِلٌ
مُرٌّ مَذَاقَتُهُ كَطَعْمِ الْعَلْقَمِ

fa-idhā ẓulimtu fa-inna ẓulmī bāsilun / murrun madhāqatuhu ka-ṭaʿmi al-ʿalqami

Translation on the reading of responding to injustice: If I am wronged, my harsh response is bitter and disagreeable, its taste like the bitterness of colocynth.

On this reading, the poet uses bitter food to describe the severity of his response to injustice.

Madhāqatuhu concerns taste and how something is tasted. ʿAlqam denotes something intensely bitter, such as colocynth.

The poet does not say that his conduct or response is literally bitter food. He compares the other person’s disagreeable experience to a bitter taste.

Two readings of ẓulmī are proposed: the injustice done to me, or my response to another person’s injustice. The syntax of madhāqatuhu is also explained differently. Both readings, however, clearly use taste and bitterness for an unpleasant experience.

This matters because it shows that extending tasting to the experience of human conduct was known in pre-Qur’anic poetry. Not every nonfood use of dhawq should therefore be treated as a meaning first introduced by the Qur’an.

The verse itself is a poetic stance on responding to injustice. It does not establish an ethical justification for every kind of revenge or severe response.

Testing a bow in Arabic poetry

Al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, describes testing a bow:

فَذَاقَ فَأَعْطَتْهُ مِنَ اللِّينِ جَانِبًا
كَفَى وَلَهَا أَنْ يُغْرِقَ السَّهْمَ حَاجِزُ

fa-dhāqa fa-aʿṭathu mina al-līni jāniban / kafā wa-lahā an yughriqa al-sahma ḥājizu

Translation: He tested it, and it gave him sufficient flexibility, yet had a resistance that prevented excessive drawing.

Fa-dhāqa in this verse means testing the bow.

By drawing the string, the person assesses the bow’s flexibility and stiffness. Dhawq here is practical experience used to discover a tool’s properties.

Transmissions differ over the vocalization of part of the second half, including an yughriqa and an yaghriqa. The essential use of fa-dhāqa for testing a bow is established in lexical accounts.

Al-Shammākh lived before and after Islam, but the date of this particular verse is uncertain. It cannot therefore be called securely pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Together with ʿAntarah’s verse, it illustrates two semantic extensions: experiencing suffering or human conduct, and testing a tool’s properties.

2. Qur’anic Forms: Tasting and Making Someone Taste

Two verbal constructions play the main role in the Qur’an.

Form I dhāqa means tasting or experiencing something. Its subject is the person who tastes or experiences it.

Form IV adhāqa means causing another to taste something. A person or agent brings about another’s experience.

In dhāqū al-ʿadhāba, for example, a group tastes punishment.

In adhāqahumu allāhu, by contrast, The God causes them to taste something.

The distinction matters syntactically: Form IV introduces the cause or agent of making someone taste.

Form IV alone, however, does not establish whether the experience is good or bad. The Qur’an uses adhāqa for both mercy and punishment.

Nor does this form establish that causing someone to taste always involves physical compulsion or direct contact with the mouth.

The nominal forms dhāʾiq and dhāʾiqah describe someone or something that tastes or experiences.

In kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu al-mawt, dhāʾiqah agrees with nafs, a grammatically feminine Arabic noun. The feminine form alone proves nothing about the soul’s biological sex or bodily nature.

3. From Tasting the Tree to Experiencing Death

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22: tasting the tree

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 states:

فَدَلَّاهُمَا بِغُرُورٍ فَلَمَّا ذَاقَا الشَّجَرَةَ بَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْآتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِنْ وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ وَنَادَاهُمَا رَبُّهُمَا أَلَمْ أَنْهَكُمَا عَنْ تِلْكُمَا الشَّجَرَةِ وَأَقُلْ لَكُمَا إِنَّ الشَّيْطَانَ لَكُمَا عَدُوٌّ مُبِينٌ

fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin fa-lammā dhāqā al-shajarata badat lahumā sawʾātuhumā wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraqi al-jannati wa-nādāhumā rabbuhumā a-lam anhakumā ʿan tilkumā al-shajarati wa-aqul lakumā inna al-shayṭāna lakumā ʿaduwwun mubīn

Translation: He drew them down through deception. When they tasted the tree, their private parts became apparent to them, and they began covering themselves with leaves from the Garden. Their Lord called to them: Did I not forbid you that tree and tell you that Satan is a clear enemy to you?

Dhāqā here is a dual past-tense verb referring to Adam and his wife.

Its object is al-shajarah, the tree.

The familiar lexical and exegetical explanation takes tasting the tree as tasting something from it, particularly its fruit or food.

The phrase itself does not name a particular tree or fruit. Identifying the fruit or its properties is therefore not part of dhāqā’s lexical meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:35 prohibits approaching the tree; Ṭā Hā 20:121 also describes the event with akalā, “the two ate.”

This comparison shows that tasting in Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 concerns the same event described elsewhere as eating.

Dhawq and akl nevertheless should not be treated as complete synonyms throughout Arabic.

Fa-lammā dhāqā highlights the event’s occurrence and the appearance of its consequence afterward. The verse does not specify how much they ate or how often they repeated it.

Dhāqā alone therefore cannot support a theory of the amount eaten, the substance involved, or the bodily mechanism of its effect on Adam and his wife.

Every soul tastes death

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:35 states:

كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَائِقَةُ الْمَوْتِ وَنَبْلُوكُمْ بِالشَّرِّ وَالْخَيْرِ فِتْنَةً وَإِلَيْنَا تُرْجَعُونَ

kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu al-mawti wa-nablūkum bi-al-sharri wa-al-khayri fitnatan wa-ilaynā turjaʿūn

Translation: Every soul tastes death; We test you with adversity and good as a trial, and to Us you will be returned.

Kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu al-mawt recurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:185, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:35, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:57.

In all three, dhāʾiqah means tasting, but its object is death.

Death is not food or drink whose flavor the tongue perceives. Tasting here concerns experiencing and encountering death.

The expression does not fully describe death’s nature, human awareness at death, or how it is experienced within or outside the body.

The verse states that every soul will taste death. Details of death, taking souls, resurrection, and return are examined in other verses.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:35, this statement accompanies testing through good and adversity and returning to The God.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:57, the statement about tasting death is followed by return to The God.

Āl ʿImrān 3:185 also speaks of receiving rewards in full on the Day of Resurrection.

In all three, tasting death concerns the present life’s finitude and the human outcome. Dhawq itself, however, does not mean annihilation, punishment, or reward.

“They do not taste death there”: negating the experience

Al-Dukhān 44:56 states:

لَا يَذُوقُونَ فِيهَا الْمَوْتَ إِلَّا الْمَوْتَةَ الْأُولَىٰ وَوَقَاهُمْ عَذَابَ الْجَحِيمِ

lā yadhūqūna fīhā al-mawta illā al-mawtata al-ūlā wa-waqāhum ʿadhāba al-jaḥīm

Translation: They do not taste death there, except the first death; and He has protected them from the punishment of the blazing fire.

This need not conflict with the general statement that every soul tastes death.

The preceding verses concern the people of the Garden. In that context, the Qur’an denies tasting death in their described condition, while mentioning the first death.

Except the first death should not be taken without reason to mean that the same first death occurs again in the Garden.

Syntactically, it can be understood as an exception distinguishing the earlier death from the state without death now described.

The lexical point is that yadhūqūna retains the meaning of experiencing in both situations. The difference arises from its object, negation, and qualifications.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:24 also states:

لَا يَذُوقُونَ فِيهَا بَرْدًا وَلَا شَرَابًا

lā yadhūqūna fīhā bardan wa-lā sharābā

Translation: They taste neither coolness nor drink there.

The next verse, Al-Nabaʾ 78:25, supplies the exception: illā ḥamīman wa-ghassāqā.

Thus, the point is not denial of every liquid without exception, but the absence of relieving coolness and drink in that condition.

This also shows that dhawq can concern experiencing coolness and relief, beyond food’s flavor.

4. Tasting Mercy and Blessing: A Positive Experience

Qur’anic dhawq does not occur only with suffering and punishment. Adhāqa is repeatedly used for mercy and blessing.

Hūd 11:9: tasting mercy and then losing it

وَلَئِنْ أَذَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ مِنَّا رَحْمَةً ثُمَّ نَزَعْنَاهَا مِنْهُ إِنَّهُ لَيَئُوسٌ كَفُورٌ

wa-laʾin adhaqnā al-insāna minnā raḥmatan thumma nazaʿnāhā minhu innahu la-yaʾūsun kafūr

Translation: If We make a person taste mercy from Us, then take it away from him, he becomes deeply despairing and ungrateful.

Adhaqnā here is Form IV and expresses making someone taste mercy.

Mercy is not food whose flavor the tongue perceives. A person receives mercy and experiences its effect in life.

That mercy is then taken away, and the human response described is despair and ingratitude.

Hūd 11:10 presents the opposite situation: someone tastes blessing after hardship and may respond with self-congratulation and pride.

Adhāqa thus concerns experiencing blessing in both verses. The response to receiving or losing it is conveyed by other words.

Tasting mercy itself does not mean ingratitude or pride.

Experiencing mercy and changing human conduct

Al-Rūm 30:36 states:

وَإِذَا أَذَقْنَا النَّاسَ رَحْمَةً فَرِحُوا بِهَا وَإِنْ تُصِبْهُمْ سَيِّئَةٌ بِمَا قَدَّمَتْ أَيْدِيهِمْ إِذَا هُمْ يَقْنَطُونَ

wa-idhā adhaqnā al-nāsa raḥmatan fariḥū bihā wa-in tuṣibhum sayyiʾatun bi-mā qaddamat aydīhim idhā hum yaqnaṭūn

Translation: When We make people taste mercy, they rejoice in it; but if adversity strikes them because of what their hands sent ahead, they suddenly despair.

This verse distinguishes tasting mercy from the arrival of an unfavorable condition.

Mercy is expressed through adhaqnā, and the adverse condition through tuṣibhum.

This does not establish that dhawq always concerns something pleasant and iṣābah always something unpleasant. Other Qur’anic uses apply dhawq to suffering and punishment too.

Al-Rūm 30:46 also states:

وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ أَنْ يُرْسِلَ الرِّيَاحَ مُبَشِّرَاتٍ وَلِيُذِيقَكُمْ مِنْ رَحْمَتِهِ وَلِتَجْرِيَ الْفُلْكُ بِأَمْرِهِ وَلِتَبْتَغُوا مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-min āyātihi an yursila al-riyāḥa mubashshirātin wa-li-yudhīqakum min raḥmatihi wa-li-tajriya al-fulku bi-amrihi wa-li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi wa-laʿallakum tashkurūn

Translation: Among His signs is that He sends the winds bearing good news, to let you taste His mercy, so that ships may sail by His command and you may seek His bounty, and so that you may give thanks.

Here, tasting mercy accompanies winds, sailing ships, and seeking The God’s bounty.

This shows that tasting mercy can involve people’s actual enjoyment of blessings and life’s resources.

Yudhīqakum itself does not mean wind blowing, rain falling, or ships sailing. Those details come from context.

Yūnus 10:21, Al-Rūm 30:33, Fuṣṣilat 41:50, and Al-Shūrā 42:48 also discuss tasting mercy and the human response to it.

Together, these uses show that Qur’anic dhawq also expresses experiencing blessing and should not be restricted to suffering and punishment.

5. Tasting Suffering, Punishment, and the Consequences of Deeds

A large share of the root’s Qur’anic uses concerns hardship, punishment, and penalty.

Āl ʿImrān 3:106, Al-Anʿām 6:30, Al-Aʿrāf 7:39, Al-Anfāl 8:35, and Al-Aḥqāf 46:34 contain expressions of “taste the punishment.”

The imperative dhūqū addresses those experiencing punishment.

Why is tasting used for punishment?

Tasting in its initial use is direct perception of flavor. In its extended use, someone actually experiences something and encounters its effects.

In dhūqū al-ʿadhāba, punishment is more than information about the future: it is a condition the addressee confronts.

However, not every Qur’anic punishment must therefore be received through the mouth, tongue, or bodily sense of taste.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:56, tasting punishment appears in a context of burning skins.

Al-Anfāl 8:50 speaks of striking faces and backs and a command to taste the punishment of burning fire.

Al-Qamar 54:48 has dhūqū massa saqar: taste the touch or harmful impact of Saqar.

These uses show that tasting punishment concerns directly experiencing suffering and is not confined to flavor perception.

This semantic breadth should not erase the verses’ explicit bodily imagery. Their descriptions of fire, skin, and contact must be examined in their own right.

“Taste what you used to do”: deeds and their consequence

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:55 states:

يَوْمَ يَغْشَاهُمُ الْعَذَابُ مِنْ فَوْقِهِمْ وَمِنْ تَحْتِ أَرْجُلِهِمْ وَيَقُولُ ذُوقُوا مَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

yawma yaghshāhumu al-ʿadhābu min fawqihim wa-min taḥti arjulihim wa-yaqūlu dhūqū mā kuntum taʿmalūn

Translation: The day punishment envelops them from above and beneath their feet, and He says: Taste what you used to do.

The object of tasting here is mā kuntum taʿmalūn: what you used to do.

Past deeds are not ordinarily food or drink. Tasting here therefore concerns experiencing what their deeds bring before them in this scene.

Al-Zumar 39:24 similarly has dhūqū mā kuntum taksibūn: taste what you used to earn.

Al-Tawbah 9:35 addresses those who hoarded gold and silver with fa-dhūqū mā kuntum taknizūn: taste what you used to hoard.

These expressions connect human conduct with experiencing its consequences.

The root itself does not establish that every deed materially transforms into a substance tasted with the tongue. Such a theory exceeds dhawq’s lexical meaning and requires a separate study of reckoning and recompense.

“So that he may taste the consequence of his affair”

Al-Māʾidah 5:95, in its discussion of hunting, includes:

لِيَذُوقَ وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِ

li-yadhūqa wabāla amrih

That is: so that he may taste the grave consequence of his action.

Wabāl concerns an adverse consequence and the burden resulting from an action.

Dhawq itself does not mean consequence; that meaning comes from wabāl.

Related expressions also occur in Al-Ḥashr 59:15, Al-Taghābun 64:5, and Al-Ṭalāq 65:9.

These uses show that tasting can concern experiencing the result of human conduct and decisions. That result sometimes occurs in worldly life and sometimes in the scene of the Hereafter.

Dhawq does not itself determine when punishment occurs.

“To make them taste part of what they did”

Al-Rūm 30:41 states:

ظَهَرَ الْفَسَادُ فِي الْبَرِّ وَالْبَحْرِ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ أَيْدِي النَّاسِ لِيُذِيقَهُمْ بَعْضَ الَّذِي عَمِلُوا لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْجِعُونَ

ẓahara al-fasādu fī al-barri wa-al-baḥri bi-mā kasabat aydī al-nāsi li-yudhīqahum baʿḍa alladhī ʿamilū laʿallahum yarjiʿūn

Translation: Corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what people’s hands have earned, so that He may make them taste part of what they have done, so that they may return.

This is an important instance of Form IV.

Corruption is connected with human conduct, and making people taste part of what they have done is linked to the prospect of return.

Baʿḍa is significant: the verse speaks of tasting part, not necessarily every consequence of every action.

Laʿallahum yarjiʿūn also introduces return as the purpose.

This does not establish that every natural or social hardship, whenever it occurs, is automatically a direct divine punishment for particular people’s sins.

The verse connects corruption and human conduct. Identifying actual instances and individual responsibility requires appropriate evidence.

The nearer and the greater punishment

Al-Sajdah 32:21 states:

وَلَنُذِيقَنَّهُمْ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ الْأَدْنَىٰ دُونَ الْعَذَابِ الْأَكْبَرِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْجِعُونَ

wa-la-nudhīqannahum mina al-ʿadhābi al-adnā dūna al-ʿadhābi al-akbari laʿallahum yarjiʿūn

Translation: We will certainly make them taste some of the nearer punishment before the greater punishment, so that they may return.

The punishment’s extent and position are specified by separate phrases: mina al-ʿadhābi al-adnā and dūna al-ʿadhābi al-akbar.

Nudhīqa alone should therefore not be understood as a small punishment. The relationship between the nearer and greater punishments comes from their descriptions.

The verse also ends with return as its purpose. That feature belongs to this context; it does not establish that every instance of making someone taste punishment throughout the Qur’an leaves an opportunity to return in worldly life.

6. “The Clothing of Hunger and Fear”: An Enveloping Experience

Al-Naḥl 16:112 states:

وَضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا قَرْيَةً كَانَتْ آمِنَةً مُطْمَئِنَّةً يَأْتِيهَا رِزْقُهَا رَغَدًا مِنْ كُلِّ مَكَانٍ فَكَفَرَتْ بِأَنْعُمِ اللَّهِ فَأَذَاقَهَا اللَّهُ لِبَاسَ الْجُوعِ وَالْخَوْفِ بِمَا كَانُوا يَصْنَعُونَ

wa-ḍaraba allāhu mathalan qaryatan kānat āminatan muṭmaʾinnatan yaʾtīhā rizquhā raghadan min kulli makānin fa-kafarat bi-anʿumi allāhi fa-adhāqahā allāhu libāsa al-jūʿi wa-al-khawfi bi-mā kānū yaṣnaʿūn

Translation: The God set forth the example of a town, secure and at ease, whose provision came abundantly from every place. It was ungrateful for The God’s blessings, so The God made it taste the clothing of hunger and fear because of what they used to do.

The verse combines two different fields of imagery:

Adhāqahā: He made it taste.

Libāsa al-jūʿi wa-al-khawf: the clothing of hunger and fear.

In its initial sense, tasting concerns flavor and the mouth, while clothing covers the body.

Here the Qur’an uses both images to describe experiencing hunger and fear.

The combination shows that dhawq concerns direct experience without being restricted to a particular bodily sense. Hunger and fear are conditions people experience in life.

Clothing can emphasize how these conditions encompass people, as a covering surrounds the body.

The phrase therefore cannot be restricted to a slight experience of hunger and fear. The context concerns a broad change in the town’s condition.

Qaryah, “town,” is also made the subject or object of human relationships. The town is described as a social community; its inhabitants’ conduct and experience are attributed to the town itself.

The plural pronoun in kānū yaṣnaʿūn at the verse’s end further clarifies that social aspect.

The construction does not establish that the town’s buildings and land possess tongues or a sense of taste.

Nor does DH W Q identify the town historically or provide details of its events. The Qur’an presents the verse as an example, and understanding tasting lexically does not depend on identifying a historical referent.

7. Tasting in Scenes of Punishment: Experience and Manner of Address

In some verses, dhūqū contributes to the manner of address as well as expressing the experience of punishment.

Al-Dukhān 44:49 states:

ذُقْ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْعَزِيزُ الْكَرِيمُ

dhuq innaka anta al-ʿazīzu al-karīm

Translation: Taste! You are indeed the mighty, the noble.

The preceding context describes someone being seized and dragged toward punishment.

In this situation, “mighty” and “noble” can carry an inverted, reproachful tone: someone who considered himself powerful and superior now encounters a very different condition.

That tone arises from the whole address and context, rather than dhuq inherently meaning mockery.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:14 also has dhūqū fitnatakum: taste your trial.

Al-Qamar 54:48 uses dhūqū massa saqar for experiencing Saqar’s touch or effect.

Ṣād 38:57 uses fa-l-yadhūqūhu concerning what is then described for those undergoing punishment.

These examples show that dhawq can accompany commands, warnings, reproach, and statements of consequences.

These features are not fixed parts of the verb itself. The same verb describes experiencing blessing in verses about mercy.

Dhawq’s positive or negative orientation therefore comes from its object and the situation of the utterance.

8. Comparison with Related Roots

DH W Q (dhāqa) and Ṭ ʿ M (ṭaʿima): tasting and food

Study of Ṭ ʿ M

Arabic ṭaʿm relates to flavor and eating or receiving food.

Dhawq likewise concerns discovering flavor in its initial sense.

Its extended uses, however, involve testing and experiencing things that are not food, such as hardship, mercy, and death.

Tasting the tree in Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 connects with eating it elsewhere in the story. But in “every soul tastes death,” death is not food and experiencing is prominent.

The roots therefore overlap without being identical in every Qur’anic use.

DH W Q (dhāqa) and ʾ K L (akala): tasting and eating

Study of ʾ K L

Akl concerns eating and consuming food.

Dhawq can involve tasting a small amount of food, but the Qur’an also uses it for experiencing suffering and mercy.

In ordinary language, someone may taste food without eating all of it.

This does not establish that every Qur’anic dhawq is slight and every akl abundant.

In Adam’s story, tasting and eating concern one event. Each verb’s precise meaning depends on its sentence and context.

DH W Q (dhāqa) and M S S (massa): tasting and contact

Study of M S S

Arabic mass concerns contact and something reaching another.

Mercy, suffering, and harm are also sometimes expressed through this root in the Qur’an.

In Hūd 11:10, ḍarrāʾa massathu describes hardship affecting someone, while adhaqnāhu naʿmāʾa expresses experiencing blessing.

This highlights contact or the arrival of a condition with mass, and experiencing it with dhawq.

However, mass cannot always be called superficial, or dhawq always profound. Each event’s intensity and extent must be inferred from context.

DH W Q (dhāqa) and B L W (balā): experience and testing

Study of B L W

Qur’anic ibtilāʾ concerns testing and exposure to a trial.

Dhawq concerns tasting and experiencing something.

Both can occur in one situation. Someone may, for example, experience mercy or hardship during a test.

But not every tasting is a test, nor is every test described through dhawq.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:35 mentions testing through good and adversity and tasting death in one verse, using two different roots.

Dhawq therefore should not consistently be translated as “being tested.” Testing can be one setting of experience, but the verb itself concerns experiencing and tasting.

9. Translation Proposals for Forqan

“Tasting” and “making someone taste” should remain the main equivalents for the Qur’anic root. Persian likewise uses these verbs both literally and in expressions such as tasting suffering or the bitterness of defeat.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:22, dhāqā al-shajarata can be rendered “they tasted the tree.” That this means eating something from it belongs in contextual explanation, without inserting the name of a particular fruit into the translation.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:185, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:35, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:57, kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu al-mawt is appropriately rendered “every soul tastes death.”

“Will experience death” is also defensible in meaning, but “tastes death” better preserves the original Arabic image.

In verses about mercy, such as Hūd 11:9 and Al-Rūm 30:36, adhaqnā can be rendered “We made taste.” The corresponding Persian verb is natural and need not imply an oral experience.

In verses about punishment, dhūqū al-ʿadhāba means “taste the punishment.”

In Al-Māʾidah 5:95, li-yadhūqa wabāla amrihi can be translated “so that he may taste the grave consequence of his action.” “Grave consequence” renders wabāl, rather than dhawq itself.

In Al-Rūm 30:41, li-yudhīqahum baʿḍa alladhī ʿamilū is better rendered “so that He may make them taste part of what they did.” Baʿḍ must be retained, rather than replaced with all the consequences of their conduct.

In Al-Naḥl 16:112, fa-adhāqahā allāhu libāsa al-jūʿi wa-al-khawf can be translated “so The God made it taste the clothing of hunger and fear.”

This preserves the verse’s imagery. A separate explanation clarifies the inhabitants’ enveloping experience of hunger and fear.

In Al-Dukhān 44:56, lā yadhūqūna fīhā al-mawta means “they do not taste death there.” The negation and exception must be retained in their respective places.

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:24, lā yadhūqūna fīhā bardan wa-lā sharābā can be rendered “they taste neither coolness nor drink there.” The next verse’s exception must not be ignored in analysis.

In all these cases, tasting is an appropriate equivalent. Other concepts, such as punishment, mercy, death, hunger, fear, and consequences of deeds, must be translated from their own words rather than all being incorporated into the meaning of DH W Q.

10. Conclusion

Arabic DH W Q begins with tasting and perceiving flavor, extending to testing and experiencing something.

Pre-Qur’anic poetry shows that using tasting for human conduct and a situation’s bitterness was already known. Other uses in the family also concern testing tools such as bows.

The Qur’an uses the root both for sensory and nonfood experiences.

Adam’s story concerns tasting the tree; dhāʾiqatu al-mawt concerns encountering death. In verses about mercy, people experience blessing and ease; in punishment verses, suffering and penalty. Al-Rūm 30:41 and Al-Māʾidah 5:95 connect tasting with experiencing the consequences of deeds.

Form IV likewise attributes making someone taste to a person or agent exposing another to a condition. The experience may be pleasant or unpleasant.

The central point is that dhawq does not itself name punishment, blessing, death, or penalty. Those concepts are specified by its object and context.

Nor does dhawq necessarily involve a small or momentary experience. Tasting sometimes begins acquaintance with something, while some Qur’anic uses concern an intense, continuing experience.

Final definition: In its sensory meaning, DH W Q concerns tasting and perceiving flavor; more broadly, it means experiencing and undergoing a condition or event. Adhāqa means making another taste or exposing them to such an experience, and dhāʾiq describes one who tastes or experiences.

Understanding the root requires distinguishing the experience itself, its object, the agent who causes it, and its consequence. Whether it is positive or negative, its intensity, duration, extent, and bodily character cannot be determined from the root alone; sentence structure and context clarify these features.
