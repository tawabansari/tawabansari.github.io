Seeking help in hardship and coming to someone's rescue: from human appeals for assistance to seeking God's help

In early Arabic, غ و ث concerns seeking help in distress and providing assistance that relieves hardship, danger, or helplessness. غَوْث means aid or rescue, اسْتِغَاثَة requesting such aid, and إِغَاثَة providing assistance and relieving distress.

The root relates to help generally, but its familiar use emphasizes hardship or urgent need. Someone in danger, overcome by thirst, or unable to face a threat may seek another's help.

Such an appeal differs from an ordinary request: the person seeks relief from distress or threatening harm, rather than simply an additional benefit.

مُغِيث denotes a helper or rescuer; مُسْتَغِيث someone seeking help.

Requesting help and receiving it are nevertheless different acts. Someone may appeal without receiving assistance. Another person may recognize distress and help without an explicit request.

Excluding the disputed form in Yūsuf 12:49 and the proper name Yaghūth, this family has five verbal occurrences in four verses: four Form X verbs requesting aid and one Form IV verb providing it. They occur in Al-Anfāl 8:9, Al-Kahf 18:29, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15, and Al-Aḥqāf 46:17.

These verses present four situations: believers seeking God's help amid the danger of war; someone asking Moses for assistance; parents appealing to God over their child's behaviour; and people in punishment calling for relief but receiving a burning, painful response.

1. The Root in Early Arabic and Historical Witnesses

Ghawth in early Arabic concerns assistance and rescue in hardship.

Recorded usage gives اسْتَغَاثَ فُلَانًا for asking someone for aid and أَغَاثَهُ for coming to their assistance or relieving their distress.

وَاغَوْثَاهُ is also a recognized Arabic cry for help.

It may come from someone facing violence, seeing no escape, or turning to another to meet an urgent need.

Early sources connect appeals for aid with crying out, but the caller's voice must be distinguished from the assistance requested. Ghawth can denote the aid itself; istighāthah the act of requesting it.

A Poetic Witness Attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

The dīwān of the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā includes the following description of a bird escaping danger:

حَتَّى اسْتَغَاثَتْ بِمَاءٍ لَا رِشَاءَ لَهُ
مِنَ الْأَبَاطِحِ فِي حَافَاتِهِ الْبُرَكُ

ḥattā istaghāthat bi-māʾin lā rishāʾa lahu / mina al-abāṭiḥi fī ḥāfātihi al-buraku

Translation: “Until she sought refuge in water requiring no well-rope, amid broad watercourses with waterbirds at its edges.”

The poet describes a sandgrouse fleeing a falcon and seeking refuge in water.

اسْتَغَاثَتْ belongs to غ و ث. Water here provides a means of escaping danger.

Istighāthah is therefore not always calling a person. Poetic language can use the verb for seeking refuge in something that offers escape.

This does not mean the bird consciously asked the water for help in a human manner. The poet describes its movement towards a means of escape through the language of seeking aid.

The verse is recorded in Zuhayr's dīwān and early lexicons. Some transmissions have اسْتَغَاثَ without the final tāʾ; other words also vary between readings.

Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet makes it useful for early usage. The precise composition date and original wording, however, have not been independently established and cannot conclusively date the word's origin.

A Poetic Witness from Abū Zubayd al-Ṭāʾī

A poem attributed to Abū Zubayd al-Ṭāʾī says:

صَادِيًا يَسْتَغِيثُ غَيْرَ مُغَاثٍ
وَلَقَدْ كَانَ عُصْرَةَ الْمَنْجُودِ

ṣādiyan yastaghīthu ghayra mughāthin / wa-laqad kāna ʿuṣrata al-manjūdi

Translation: “Thirsting, he seeks aid but receives none, though he had been a refuge for the distressed.”

يَسْتَغِيثُ and مُغَاث appear together.

يَسْتَغِيثُ describes requesting aid; غَيْرَ مُغَاثٍ indicates that the request has not resulted in assistance.

The poet distinguishes seeking relief from actually receiving it.

The second half recalls someone who had himself sheltered the distressed.

Someone who once helped others may be deprived of their assistance when he becomes distressed himself. This contrast clarifies the root's meaning.

Abū Zubayd lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Early sources transmit this verse within a poem associated with mourning his sister's son.

Its exact date has not been independently established. It illuminates the root's continuing early Arabic usage but cannot unconditionally be assigned to the period before the Quran.

What the Historical Witnesses Show

Together with early lexical records, these witnesses show three situations involving this family:

First, requesting aid in distress, such as a cry from someone overcome by danger or thirst.

Second, providing help and removing hardship, such as rescuing someone in danger.

Third, receiving no assistance despite requesting it, when an appeal finds no rescuer.

Distress and the need for relief are prominent throughout, but the root alone does not specify the hardship or kind of assistance.

The needed help might be water, food, protection from an enemy, rescue, or a way to resolve difficulty.

These lexical and poetic witnesses do not confine istighāthah to a particular religious ceremony, fixed prayer, or specific means of assistance.

2. Semantic Core and Forms

غ و ث has a weak middle radical. In some derivatives, its wāw changes to alif or yāʾ.

For example, اسْتَغَاثَ is Form X, with the present form يَسْتَغِيثُ.

Form IV has أَغَاثَ and يُغِيثُ.

Principal attested forms include:

غَوْث: aid or rescue; assistance towards relief from distress.

غَاثَ: he helped or came to someone's rescue; recorded in lexicons, although أَغَاثَ is more familiar for this sense.

أَغَاثَ: he provided aid, relieved hardship, or removed distress.

يُغِيثُ: he provides aid or comes to someone's rescue.

إِغَاثَة: the act of helping and rescuing from distress.

مُغِيث: a helper or rescuer.

مُغَاث: someone assisted; the Form IV passive participle.

اسْتَغَاثَ: he sought assistance, rescue, or relief.

يَسْتَغِيثُ: he seeks help.

اسْتِغَاثَة: requesting help in distress.

مُسْتَغِيث: someone seeking aid.

غَوَّثَ: he cried for help; in recorded usage, uttered وَاغَوْثَاهُ.

تَغْوِيث: crying out for assistance.

تَغَاوَثُوا: in some transmitted uses, people called to one another for help.

غِيَاث and غَوِيث: assistance, rescue, or a means through which a distressed person receives help.

Lexicons also record rarer forms such as مَغُوثَة. These must not all be presented as Quranic derivatives merely to fill out the family.

Requesting Aid and Providing It

In Form X, اسْتَغَاثَ describes requesting aid.

In Form IV, أَغَاثَ concerns providing it.

The two verbal constructions reflect this distinction.

For example, a drowning person who calls for help has made an appeal: istighāthah.

Someone who comes and rescues that person performs ighāthah.

An appeal may occur without assistance following.

Someone may also recognize danger and rescue another without hearing a request.

Request and response are related but not identical.

Form X here expresses requesting aid, but this function must not be generalized to every Arabic Form X verb without examination.

The Semantic Core

A concise, defensible definition is:

Requesting aid or providing assistance needed to avert danger, remove distress, or free someone from a difficult situation.

Three elements matter: distress or need, a request or action for help, and the aim of relief.

Help does not always produce the desired result. One Quranic passage describes an apparent response to an appeal as painful punishment itself.

Istighāthah means asking for help; it does not guarantee receiving it.

3. Quranic Occurrences

With Yūsuf 12:49 considered separately because of its disputed derivation, this family's verbal forms occur five times in four verses.

In Al-Anfāl 8:9, تَسْتَغِيثُونَ concerns seeking God's help.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15, فَاسْتَغَاثَهُ describes one person's appeal to Moses.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:17, يَسْتَغِيثَانِ describes parents seeking God's assistance.

In Al-Kahf 18:29, يَسْتَغِيثُوا expresses seeking relief and يُغَاثُوا the response.

Istighāthah thus occurs both between human beings and in the human relationship with God.

It also addresses different kinds of distress: warfare, a conflict between two men, deep parental concern, and punishment in the hereafter.

4. Believers Seeking God's Help

Al-Anfāl 8:9

إِذْ تَسْتَغِيثُونَ رَبَّكُمْ فَاسْتَجَابَ لَكُمْ أَنِّي مُمِدُّكُمْ بِأَلْفٍ مِنَ الْمَلَائِكَةِ مُرْدِفِينَ

idh tastaghīthūna rabbakum fa-stajāba lakum annī mumiddukum bi-alfin mina al-malāʾikati murdifīn

Al-Anfāl 8:9

Translation: “When you sought your Lord's help, He answered you: ‘I will reinforce you with a thousand angels following one another.’”

تَسْتَغِيثُونَ is a Form X present verb addressing a plural group.

رَبَّكُمْ is its direct object: they were seeking their Lord's assistance.

The syntax matters. The verb expresses the appeal, and the one addressed follows directly as its object.

The verse then says:

“فَاسْتَجَابَ لَكُمْ”

God answered their request.

Istijābah comes from another root and expresses responding. The Quran distinguishes the appeal from its answer.

Seeking Aid during War

Al-Anfāl's context concerns military confrontation, enemy danger, resources, and believers' circumstances in battle.

Al-Anfāl 8:7 mentions God's promise concerning one of two groups; Al-Anfāl 8:9 presents the appeal to their Lord.

The appeal thus relates to real danger and an urgent need for support.

Believers face an enemy and request God's help.

Reliance on God is compatible with recognizing danger and needing assistance.

A person may work to fulfil a proper responsibility while recognizing limited ability and seeking God's help.

The Response to the Appeal

In Al-Anfāl 8:9, the response promises reinforcement by a thousand angels.

Al-Anfāl 8:10 then says that God made this a source of glad tidings and reassurance, and that victory is from God.

The connection suggests that the response concerns more than external assistance: reassurance and steadfastness of heart also appear in the context.

This event does not establish that every appeal, in every time and situation, necessarily receives angels or immediate victory.

The verse describes a particular appeal and response in a historical setting.

Divine help does not negate human effort and responsibility. Al-Anfāl itself also commands preparation, steadfastness, and proper conduct in battle.

Seeking God's aid does not replace responsible action.

5. A Human Appeal to Moses

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15

وَدَخَلَ الْمَدِينَةَ عَلَىٰ حِينِ غَفْلَةٍ مِنْ أَهْلِهَا فَوَجَدَ فِيهَا رَجُلَيْنِ يَقْتَتِلَانِ هَٰذَا مِنْ شِيعَتِهِ وَهَٰذَا مِنْ عَدُوِّهِ ۖ فَاسْتَغَاثَهُ الَّذِي مِنْ شِيعَتِهِ عَلَى الَّذِي مِنْ عَدُوِّهِ فَوَكَزَهُ مُوسَىٰ فَقَضَىٰ عَلَيْهِ ۖ قَالَ هَٰذَا مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ ۖ إِنَّهُ عَدُوٌّ مُضِلٌّ مُبِينٌ

wa-dakhala al-madīnata ʿalā ḥīni ghaflatin min ahlihā fa-wajada fīhā rajulayni yaqtatilāni hādhā min shīʿatihi wa-hādhā min ʿaduwwihi fa-staghāthahu alladhī min shīʿatihi ʿalā alladhī min ʿaduwwihi fa-wakazahu mūsā fa-qaḍā ʿalayhi qāla hādhā min ʿamali al-shayṭāni innahu ʿaduwwun muḍillun mubīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15

Translation: “He entered the city at a time when its people were unaware, and found two men fighting: one of his group and the other of his enemies. The man of his group sought his help against the man of his enemies. Moses struck him with his fist and finished him. He said, ‘This is Satan's doing; he is an enemy who clearly leads astray.’”

This verse is especially important for the root's scope because the request is directed to a human being.

فَاسْتَغَاثَهُ combines اسْتَغَاثَ with the object pronoun هُ.

The pronoun refers to Moses.

One of the men therefore requested Moses's aid.

عَلَى الَّذِي مِنْ عَدُوِّهِ identifies the opposing party against whom help was sought.

Is Istighāthah Used Only for Appeals to God?

This verse shows that it is not.

In Al-Anfāl 8:9, believers appeal to God.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15, a man appeals to Moses.

Therefore, the lexical meaning of istighāthah is not restricted to requesting God's help.

The same verb can describe interaction between people.

That does not equate human and divine power or standing.

Moses is a person present at the scene and able to intervene in the conflict.

The appeal to God in Al-Anfāl involves another relationship and divine power and will.

A shared verb alone does not make all appeals equivalent in their object, the addressee's capacity, or their legitimacy.

Nor does the word establish the permissibility or efficacy of every appeal to an absent or deceased being, or one credited with alleged powers. Such questions require independent evidence.

Does Every Appeal Express a Rightful Request?

A man asks Moses for help, but the opposing man dies after Moses intervenes.

Moses then says:

“هَٰذَا مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ”

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:16, he asks his Lord for forgiveness.

The request and the consequences of intervention must therefore be examined separately.

Someone may genuinely need help, yet the kind of assistance or manner of intervention may produce an unintended or wrongful outcome.

An appeal proves neither the legitimacy of all the requester's wishes nor the correctness of every action performed as assistance.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18, the same man asks Moses again. يَسْتَصْرِخُهُ, from ص ر خ, describes crying for help.

This related use shows that the Quran employs several roots for appeals during conflict.

Istighāthah and istiṣrākh are not entirely synonymous: crying and sound are more prominent in the latter; seeking aid and relief is central to the former.

6. Parents Seeking God's Help

Al-Aḥqāf 46:17

وَالَّذِي قَالَ لِوَالِدَيْهِ أُفٍّ لَكُمَا أَتَعِدَانِنِي أَنْ أُخْرَجَ وَقَدْ خَلَتِ الْقُرُونُ مِنْ قَبْلِي وَهُمَا يَسْتَغِيثَانِ اللَّهَ وَيْلَكَ آمِنْ إِنَّ وَعْدَ اللَّهِ حَقٌّ فَيَقُولُ مَا هَٰذَا إِلَّا أَسَاطِيرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

wa-alladhī qāla li-wālidayhi uffin lakumā a-taʿidāninī an ukhraja wa-qad khalati al-qurūnu min qablī wa-humā yastaghīthāni allāha waylaka āmin inna waʿda allāhi ḥaqqun fa-yaqūlu mā hādhā illā asāṭīru al-awwalīn

Al-Aḥqāf 46:17

Translation: “As for the one who said to his parents, ‘Uff to you both! Do you promise me that I will be brought out, when generations before me have passed away?’ They both seek The God's help: ‘Woe to you! Believe! The God's promise is true.’ But he says, ‘These are only tales of the ancients.’”

يَسْتَغِيثَانِ is a dual present verb.

Its two subjects are the father and mother.

اللَّهَ is its direct object: both seek God's help.

Istighāthah is therefore not confined to immediate bodily danger.

The parents face anxiety and helplessness over their child's words and conduct.

The child denies resurrection and dismisses their words as ancient tales.

They appeal to God and call the child to believe.

Appealing amid Deep Concern

There is no warfare or physical attack here.

The issue is disagreement over belief and the parents' intense concern about their child's choice.

Distress prompting an appeal may therefore be understood by those involved as moral or spiritual.

Parents may ask God to guide their child, relieve them of wrongful behaviour, or provide a way through the situation.

The verb itself does not specify the exact wording or contents of their appeal.

Context connects it with the child's conduct and their invitation to believe, but unmentioned details must not be inserted as certainties.

Seeking Help and Human Choice

The parents appeal to God and urge their child to believe.

Yet the child continues to reject their words in the same verse.

An appeal to God does not in itself mean another person's choice disappears.

Nor does the verse authorize parents to change the child's beliefs through force, threats, or punishment.

Their invitation to believe appears alongside their appeal to God.

This example does not make every parent-child disagreement about belief identical to this situation.

The verse describes particular words and conduct. Other people must be judged with knowledge of their circumstances and behaviour.

7. An Appeal That Does Not Bring Relief

Al-Kahf 18:29

وَقُلِ الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ ۖ فَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيُؤْمِنْ وَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيَكْفُرْ ۚ إِنَّا أَعْتَدْنَا لِلظَّالِمِينَ نَارًا أَحَاطَ بِهِمْ سُرَادِقُهَا ۚ وَإِنْ يَسْتَغِيثُوا يُغَاثُوا بِمَاءٍ كَالْمُهْلِ يَشْوِي الْوُجُوهَ ۚ بِئْسَ الشَّرَابُ وَسَاءَتْ مُرْتَفَقًا

wa-quli al-ḥaqqu min rabbikum fa-man shāʾa fa-l-yuʾmin wa-man shāʾa fa-l-yakfur innā aʿtadnā li-l-ẓālimīna nāran aḥāṭa bihim surādiquhā wa-in yastaghīthū yughāthū bi-māʾin ka-l-muhli yashwī al-wujūha biʾsa al-sharābu wa-sāʾat murtafaqā

Al-Kahf 18:29

Translation: “Say, ‘The truth is from your Lord. Let whoever wills believe, and whoever wills disbelieve.’ We have prepared for the wrongdoers a fire whose enclosure surrounds them. If they seek relief, they will be ‘relieved’ with water like molten material that scorches faces. What an evil drink and dreadful resting place!”

Two forms of the same root stand together:

يَسْتَغِيثُوا, Form X, means to request help.

يُغَاثُوا, passive Form IV, means to be assisted; here it expresses the response to the appeal.

This pairing clearly illustrates the distinction between requesting aid and providing it.

Request and Response

وَإِنْ يَسْتَغِيثُوا is conditional: if they seek relief.

يُغَاثُوا states the consequence of that condition.

What they receive, however, is water like muhl: hot, burning material that scorches faces.

The verse does not describe soothing, rescuing assistance.

The response is itself described as producing greater suffering.

Bitter Reversal in the Language of Relief

Ordinarily, someone seeking help expects it to reduce their distress.

A thirsty person asking for water in severe heat expects relief from thirst.

Here the response is a scalding drink.

The contrast conveys severe punishment and deprivation of relief.

It can be understood as a bitter reversal: a word normally associated with assistance appears where the outcome is no rescue.

يُغَاثُوا must therefore not be taken as proof that those punished actually escape their suffering.

The verb retains its role in the construction, while context supplies an outcome contrary to the ordinary expectation of aid.

Reducing it to “are given water” would also lose its direct lexical connection with يَسْتَغِيثُوا.

The Quran places appeal and response together through two forms of the same root.

Does Every Appeal Receive the Desired Response?

This verse shows that it does not.

Believers in Al-Anfāl 8:9 request God's help and receive a supportive response.

In Al-Kahf 18:29, an appeal in punishment receives no rescuing relief.

No unconditional promise of rescue can therefore be derived from the word istighāthah itself.

It expresses requesting help.

Acceptance, the kind of response, and its result emerge from the verse and context.

8. Distinctions from Related Roots

غ و ث (Ghawth) and غ ي ث (Ghayth)

The two roots are close in spelling and meaning but should be distinguished in morphological analysis.

Ghayth in familiar Quranic usage denotes rain, especially rain after need and waiting that brings growth and relief.

Al-Shūrā 42:28 describes God sending rain after people despair and spreading His mercy.

Ghayth also occurs in Luqmān 31:34 and Al-Ḥadīd 57:20.

Ghawth in the sense examined here denotes aid and rescue.

The semantic relationship is clear: rain can deliver people from drought.

This closeness alone does not prove that the roots are historically identical or that one necessarily developed from the other.

يُغَاثُ النَّاسُ in Joseph's Story

Yūsuf 12:49 says:

Yūsuf 12:49

ثُمَّ يَأْتِي مِنْ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ عَامٌ فِيهِ يُغَاثُ النَّاسُ وَفِيهِ يَعْصِرُونَ

thumma yaʾtī min baʿdi dhālika ʿāmun fīhi yughāthu al-nāsu wa-fīhi yaʿṣirūn

Yūsuf 12:49

Translation: “Then after that will come a year in which people are relieved and in which they press.”

يُغَاثُ resembles يُغَاثُوا in Al-Kahf 18:29 in form and construction.

Its root analysis, however, is disputed.

One morphological analysis assigns يُغَاثُ in Yūsuf 12:49 to غ ي ث and understands relief through rain, agricultural abundance, and the end of drought. This is one analysis, rather than an undisputed historical derivation.

يُغَاثُوا in Al-Kahf 18:29 is assigned to غ و ث.

Some early lexicographers and commentators also relate يُغَاثُ النَّاسُ in Yūsuf to ghawth and ighāthah in meaning or derivation.

The similarity of the forms must not be treated as conclusive historical identity without examination.

In Yūsuf 12:49, the clear theme is a year of relief and abundance after hardship and drought.

Al-Kahf 18:29 concerns appeals for relief in punishment.

Even similar or identical surface forms may differ importantly in context and root analysis.

This article examines يُغَاثُ in Yūsuf separately and does not silently add it to the five occurrences above. If it is assigned to غ و ث, the count becomes six verbal occurrences in five verses; the present site's word box uses that assignment.

غ و ث (Ghawth) and ن ص ر (Naṣr)

Naṣr concerns support and assistance and is often connected with overcoming and victory in the Quran.

Ghawth is also help, with distress and a request for relief especially prominent in familiar usage.

Someone may support a group in a good undertaking without immediate danger being present; this can be naṣr.

When someone under threat calls for assistance, istighāthah more specifically describes the appeal.

The distinction is not absolute: naṣr can also occur during danger and distress.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 describes the man who earlier appealed to Moses through verbs concerning support and crying for help.

These terms may overlap in a situation while emphasizing different aspects.

غ و ث (Ghawth) and ع و ن (ʿAwn)

ʿAwn concerns help and cooperation in performing a task.

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 commands cooperation in goodness and reverence.

Such assistance can occur in ordinary circumstances.

Ghawth more characteristically concerns distress and a need for which relief is sought.

Two people building a house may cooperate: this is help and mutual assistance.

If someone becomes trapped beneath rubble and asks for help, the request is istighāthah.

Ghawth can thus be understood as assistance in difficulty, but ordinary cooperation is not necessarily an appeal for rescue.

غ و ث (Ghawth) and ص ر خ (Ṣarakha)

Ṣarakha concerns shouting and a loud voice.

Ibrāhīm 14:22 discusses inability to provide relief between Satan and his followers.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 uses يَسْتَصْرِخُهُ for an appeal to Moses.

Istiṣrākh can mean crying out for assistance.

Istighāthah does not itself depend on loudness.

Someone may request rescue quietly, through an urgent written message, or with a visible signal.

An appeal exists even without a loud cry.

Sound and shouting feature in some forms, but do not exhaust the meaning of ghawth.

غ و ث (Ghawth) and ن ج و (Najāh)

Najāh concerns escaping danger, harm, or distress.

Istighāthah is requesting assistance towards that relief.

Someone may appeal without being rescued.

Another may escape danger without an explicit request.

Appeal and rescue are distinct stages or aspects.

Ighāthah may denote assistance towards rescue, but the verb does not always specify the whole final outcome.

A rescuer may come to help and remove part of the danger while the situation remains unresolved.

Translation must therefore distinguish requesting help, taking action, and achieving relief.

9. Yaghūth: A Proper Name in Nūḥ

Another Quranic word with closely related letters deserves examination.

Nūḥ 71:23

وَقَالُوا لَا تَذَرُنَّ آلِهَتَكُمْ وَلَا تَذَرُنَّ وَدًّا وَلَا سُوَاعًا وَلَا يَغُوثَ وَيَعُوقَ وَنَسْرًا

wa-qālū lā tadharunna ālihatakum wa-lā tadharunna waddan wa-lā suwāʿan wa-lā yaghūtha wa-yaʿūqa wa-nasrā

Nūḥ 71:23

Translation: “They said, ‘Do not abandon your gods; do not abandon Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, or Nasr.’”

يَغُوثَ names one of the deities mentioned by Noah's people.

Its form has a notable connection with the letters of غ و ث.

It is not a present-tense verb meaning “he helps” in this verse.

It occurs in a list of proper names.

A proper name must not be treated as though it functions as an ordinary verb or derivative in the sentence.

Does Yaghūth Derive from Rescue?

Its form and semantic resemblance invite examination of a connection with helping.

Early lexicons also record Yaghūth alongside this family.

A proper name alone, however, does not conclusively establish the historical reason for naming.

Establishing that this deity was named specifically for rescuing requires independent historical evidence.

The Quran clearly identifies Yaghūth as one of the deity names mentioned by Noah's people.

The name does not establish the deity's actual ability to help.

Naming something after a quality does not prove that it actually possesses that quality.

Yaghūth therefore belongs in historical study of deity names, but not in the count of five verbal occurrences of غ و ث.

10. Appeals, Ability to Help, and Human Responsibility

An important conclusion is the distinction between requesting aid, possessing the ability to help, and the legitimacy of intervention.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15, a person asks Moses for help.

In Al-Anfāl 8:9, believers appeal to God.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:17, parents seek God's help.

In Al-Kahf 18:29, distressed people appeal but receive no rescuing response.

The same lexical family expresses the request, while its object, addressee, and outcome differ.

Requesting Human Help

People need one another's assistance in social life.

Someone ill, endangered, or unable to perform a necessary task may ask others for aid.

Those able to help may act to relieve others' distress.

Yet a request does not itself justify every possible action.

If someone in a fight asks a friend for help, the friend should assess the situation and distinguish defending the wronged from joining wrongdoing.

Moses's intervention shows that assistance can have consequences requiring responsible examination.

Requesting God's Help

The Quran presents people seeking their Lord's aid in distress.

This may occur in war, danger, anxiety, or a need for guidance.

Appealing to God does not mean abandoning effort, action, or responsibility.

Nor do these passages make every wish right and just simply because it is expressed as prayer.

Someone may want something harmful to themselves or others.

The value of the request, its manner, and its consequences require separate evaluation.

Istighāthah recognizes need and requests assistance; the wish's legitimacy and the response's nature go beyond the root's lexical meaning.

Rescue and Social Responsibility

Someone may face real danger without being able to escape.

For example, a person may be caught in a flood or a family may need urgent help because of war and displacement.

Istighāthah then requests help towards safety.

Those with the capacity and means to assist should assess their responsibility in light of circumstances, goodness, and justice.

The word ghawth itself does not constitute a complete legal ruling for everyone and every emergency.

Responsibility to help is understood through Quranic principles of goodness, justice, giving, support for the needy, and preventing oppression.

غ و ث clarifies the need and appeal, rather than every rule governing a helper's responsibility.

11. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Preserve the difference between requesting aid and providing it.

“Aid,” “rescue,” or “help towards relief” suit غَوْث.

“Sought aid,” “asked for help,” or “appealed for relief” naturally render اسْتَغَاثَ.

“Provided aid,” “came to the rescue,” or, where appropriate, “relieved distress” can render أَغَاثَ.

The choice must fit the construction.

Al-Anfāl 8:9

“تَسْتَغِيثُونَ رَبَّكُمْ”

Suggested translation:

“You sought your Lord's help.”

The wartime setting makes the urgent need for support clear.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15

“فَاسْتَغَاثَهُ الَّذِي مِنْ شِيعَتِهِ”

Suggested translation:

“The man of his group sought his help.”

The person is appealing to Moses.

An unexplained technical term for istighāthah can sound excessively religious to a Persian reader; “asked for help” conveys the direct, natural meaning.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:17

“وَهُمَا يَسْتَغِيثَانِ اللَّهَ”

Suggested translation:

“They both seek The God's help.”

The verb is dual and refers to the father and mother.

Preserve singular, dual, and plural distinctions in translation.

Al-Kahf 18:29

“وَإِنْ يَسْتَغِيثُوا يُغَاثُوا”

Suggested translation:

“If they seek relief, they will be ‘relieved.’”

This is only the beginning of the conditional construction; the following words specify the response.

The complete translation must make clear that what they receive is scalding water that burns faces.

يُغَاثُوا must therefore not be presented in isolation as guaranteed rescue or beneficial help.

Here the apparent response to an appeal offers no rescue, contrary to ordinary expectation.

Brief Root Meanings for Forqan Word Boxes

For forms requesting aid:

Brief root meaning: “Seeking assistance and relief in distress.”

For forms providing aid:

Brief root meaning: “Providing assistance and relieving distress or danger.”

Write each derivative's contextual meaning separately.

تَسْتَغِيثُونَ in Al-Anfāl 8:9 concerns believers seeking divine aid in battle.

فَاسْتَغَاثَهُ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15 is a man's appeal to Moses during a fight.

يَسْتَغِيثَانِ in Al-Aḥqāf 46:17 describes parents seeking God's aid over their child's conduct and denial.

In Al-Kahf 18:29, يَسْتَغِيثُوا requests relief from suffering; يُغَاثُوا gives the response in the form of a scalding drink.

These distinctions prevent one shared description from replacing the request, intervention, and result.

12. Conclusion

غ و ث in early Arabic concerns appeals in distress and assistance sought to remove hardship or escape danger.

Literary and lexical witnesses describe human appeals, seeking refuge in a means of escape, and cries from those in difficulty.

Excluding Yūsuf 12:49, whose derivation is disputed, there are five verbal occurrences in four verses. Including it under غ و ث gives six occurrences in five verses.

Believers seek God's help in Al-Anfāl 8:9 and receive a specific response.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15, someone appeals to Moses, demonstrating that the lexical sense is not confined to appeals to God.

Parents seek God's aid over their child's conduct in Al-Aḥqāf 46:17, showing that distress may also be nonphysical.

Al-Kahf 18:29 pairs the request with a burning, punitive response: asking does not guarantee rescuing assistance.

Yaghūth in Nūḥ 71:23 is a proper deity name, not a verb or proof of that deity's power to help.

يُغَاثُ النَّاسُ in Yūsuf 12:49 admits two recorded analyses: relief through rain under غ ي ث, or aid and release from hardship under غ و ث. These should be distinguished without claiming unanimous agreement on one.

Similar letters and related meanings do not suffice to identify every form and root as the same.

Final definition:

غ و ث: requesting or providing assistance towards relief from distress, danger, or hardship, where someone seeks rescue or another person or agent acts to meet that need.

Istighāthah requests assistance; ighāthah concerns providing it.

The Quranic uses reveal an important distinction: asking for help, having the ability to help, and achieving relief are three separate matters.

An appeal may fail to produce the desired result. Someone able to help may intervene with a wrongful consequence. Someone may appeal to God and receive the response within the circumstances the verse describes.

غ و ث should therefore neither be confined to a religious technical term nor turned into a promise of certain rescue.

In Quranic language, the family presents human need for assistance across bodily danger, social conflict, deep anxiety, and appeals for relief from suffering.

Recognizing need and requesting help is one stage; receiving proper assistance and overcoming distress is another.
