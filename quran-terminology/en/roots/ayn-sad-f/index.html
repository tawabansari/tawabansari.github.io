Core meaning: strong, forceful blowing of wind capable of moving and scattering things; in the plant branch, crop leaves, straw, and nongrain parts of cereals that may be cut, dried, broken, or dispersed.

1. The root in early Arabic

The triliteral root ع ص ف has two important branches: strong wind, forceful movement, and displacement; and the leaves, straw, chaff, and other parts of crops accompanying their grain and ears.

عَصَفَتِ الرِّيحُ means that the wind blew strongly. رِيحٌ عَاصِفٌ or رِيحٌ عَاصِفَةٌ describes a forcefully blowing wind. Here عَصْف is a verbal noun expressing strong blowing.

Such wind can lift dust, dry leaves, straw, and light objects and scatter them. Some Arabic uses consequently associate عَصْف with rapid movement and scattering as well as wind strength.

In the second branch, عَصْف names crop leaves, especially those of cereals. They may be green while the plant grows, dry after maturity, or separated from the grain-bearing parts during harvest.

عَصْفَة, عَصِيفَة, and عُصَافَة occur in the same field. Dictionaries sometimes distinguish foliage, ear coverings, straw, and broken remnants, and sometimes explain these forms through closely related meanings.

Wheat consists of more than grain: stalks, leaves, ears, and surrounding coverings also belong to the plant. After harvesting and separating the grain, remaining parts can become straw and animal fodder.

This branch of عَصْف concerns such plant parts, rather than necessarily the edible grain itself.

A natural connection exists between wind and plant material: strong winds lift and scatter light leaves and straw. This does not by itself establish that the name for foliage and straw historically derives from the verb for blowing.

Even early lexicographers did not unanimously accept that derivation as certain.

A historical definition should preserve both branches: strong blowing and forceful movement in the wind branch, and cereal foliage and straw in the plant branch. Their possible connection cannot replace independent evidence for each.

2. Early poetic evidence and variant wording

An early example of the plant branch is attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Alqamah ibn Abadah:

تَسْقِي مَذَانِبَ قَدْ مَالَتْ عَصِيفَتُهَا

tasqī madhāniba qad mālat ʿaṣīfatuhā

“It waters channels whose surrounding crop leaves have bent.”

عَصِيفَة here refers to crop foliage. The image concerns cultivated ground watered through channels, with leaves bending beside them.

This shows that agricultural عَصِيفَة need not be confined to dry, broken straw. It could also describe leaves of crops still standing in the field.

Variants include مَالَتْ and زَالَتْ. The first suggests bending; the second is explained in the lexical account as foliage having been cut, after which the crop is watered to regrow its leaves.

In both readings, عَصِيفَة concerns plant covering rather than the grain itself.

Lexical sources attribute this line to Alqamah ibn Abadah. Accepting the attribution makes it pre-Islamic evidence; the exact date of composition has not been independently established here.

For force and destruction, a line attributed to al-A'sha reads:

فِي فَيْلَقٍ جَأْوَاءَ مَلْمُومَةٍ
تَعْصِفُ بِالدَّارِعِ وَالْحَاسِرِ

fī faylaqin jaʾwāʾa malmūmatin / taʿṣifu bi-l-dāriʿi wa-l-ḥāsiri

“In a dense, dark host that sweeps away the armored and the unarmored.”

تَعْصِفُ describes an army's force rather than wind: it overwhelms and destroys people.

The verb can thus describe destructive force. Just as powerful wind dislodges and scatters objects, a great force can crush people or things.

Destruction is nevertheless a possible effect, and not every use of عَصْف entails complete annihilation.

Al-A'sha lived into the emergence of Islam. This line's date has not been independently established here; it is useful early literary evidence without establishing that this exact expression was necessarily composed before Islam.

Lexical and poetic evidence connects this family with agriculture, forceful movement, intensity, and scattering. It does not establish one certain historical origin for all these senses.

3. Lexical and Quranic forms

Important forms include عَصَفَ, يَعْصِفُ, عَصْف, عُصُوف, عَاصِف, عَاصِفَة, عَاصِفَات, عَصْفَة, عَصِيفَة, عُصَافَة, أَعْصَفَ, and اعْتَصَفَ.

Their meanings and grammatical functions differ.

Applied to wind, عَصَفَ expresses strong blowing.

In that branch عَصْف is a verbal noun for forceful blowing; in the plant branch it is a noun for cereal leaves and straw.

عَاصِف and عَاصِفَة describe something blowing or moving forcefully.

عَصِيفَة and عُصَافَة concern foliage, coverings, or plant remnants.

أَعْصَفَ is recorded for intensified movement or the growth of crop leaves.

Another classical use of اعْتَصَفَ concerns earning and seeking sustenance. Without evidence, it should not be made the root's principal Quranic meaning.

The family occurs seven times in six Quranic verses:

عَاصِفٌ in Yunus 10:22; عَاصِفٍ in Ibrahim 14:18; عَاصِفَةً in Al-Anbiya 21:81; الْعَصْفِ in Al-Rahman 55:12; الْعَاصِفَاتِ عَصْفًا in Al-Mursalat 77:2; and عَصْفٍ in Al-Fil 105:5.

عَاصِف, عَاصِفَة, and عَاصِفَات concern forceful movement. عَصْف in Al-Rahman and Al-Fil names plant material, whereas in Al-Mursalat it is the verbal noun for strong blowing.

Identical consonants, or even identical outward forms, do not imply identical grammatical roles and meanings in every verse.

4. عَاصِف: a strongly blowing wind

The first Quranic occurrence appears in Yunus:

هُوَ الَّذِي يُسَيِّرُكُمْ فِي الْبَرِّ وَالْبَحْرِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا كُنْتُمْ فِي الْفُلْكِ وَجَرَيْنَ بِهِمْ بِرِيحٍ طَيِّبَةٍ وَفَرِحُوا بِهَا جَاءَتْهَا رِيحٌ عَاصِفٌ وَجَاءَهُمُ الْمَوْجُ مِنْ كُلِّ مَكَانٍ وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُمْ أُحِيطَ بِهِمْ دَعَوُا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ لَئِنْ أَنْجَيْتَنَا مِنْ هَٰذِهِ لَنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الشَّاكِرِينَ

huwa alladhī yusayyirukum fī al-barri wa-l-baḥri ḥattā idhā kuntum fī al-fulki wa-jarayna bihim bi-rīḥin ṭayyibatin wa-fariḥū bihā jāʾathā rīḥun ʿāṣifun wa-jāʾahumu al-mawju min kulli makānin wa-ẓannū annahum uḥīṭa bihim daʿawu Allāha mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīna la-in anjaytanā min hādhihi la-nakūnanna mina al-shākirīn

Yūnus 10:22

Yunus 10:22

“He is the One who enables you to travel on land and sea. When you are aboard ships sailing with their passengers in a pleasant wind, and they rejoice in it, a fierce wind strikes the ships and waves come at them from every direction. They think they are surrounded. They call upon The God, devoting religion sincerely to Him: If You save us from this, we will surely be grateful.”

عَاصِفٌ qualifies رِيحٌ: a wind blowing strongly.

Its contrast with رِيحٍ طَيِّبَةٍ at the scene's beginning is significant.

Initially the ships sail in a pleasant wind and their passengers rejoice. Then the fierce wind arrives and the voyage changes.

The verse distinguishes wind that makes sailing pleasant from wind whose strength, together with surrounding waves, brings danger and anxiety.

عَاصِف does not merely mean moving wind, since the first wind also moves the ships. Intensity distinguishes the second.

Nor does عَاصِف itself mean waves. الْمَوْجُ expresses those separately.

The danger arises from the combination of strong wind, surrounding waves, and the ships' situation.

Why رِيحٌ عَاصِفٌ?

Here رِيحٌ is qualified by عَاصِفٌ, whereas Al-Anbiya 21:81 has الرِّيحَ عَاصِفَةً.

Arabic admits both the masculine عَاصِف and feminine عَاصِفَة in this usage, as recognized in lexical and grammatical discussions.

The difference does not establish two completely different kinds of wind.

Both describe strong blowing; grammatical form alone does not establish a difference in force, direction, or outcome.

5. يَوْمٍ عَاصِفٍ: a day of strong wind

The second occurrence appears in Ibrahim:

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِرَبِّهِمْ أَعْمَالُهُمْ كَرَمَادٍ اشْتَدَّتْ بِهِ الرِّيحُ فِي يَوْمٍ عَاصِفٍ ۖ لَا يَقْدِرُونَ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ الضَّلَالُ الْبَعِيدُ

mathalu alladhīna kafarū bi-rabbihim aʿmāluhum ka-ramādin ishtaddat bihi al-rīḥu fī yawmin ʿāṣifin lā yaqdirūna mimmā kasabū ʿalā shayʾin dhālika huwa al-ḍalālu al-baʿīd

Ibrāhīm 14:18

Ibrahim 14:18

“The likeness of those who disbelieve in their Lord: their deeds are like ashes swept fiercely by the wind on a stormy day. They have no power over anything they have earned. That is far-reaching error.”

عَاصِفٍ qualifies يَوْمٍ rather than directly qualifying الرِّيحُ.

This is natural Arabic usage. Calling a day “cold” does not give time a cold body; it describes the cold experienced that day.

Similarly, يَوْمٍ عَاصِفٍ is a day when wind blows strongly.

The accompanying اشْتَدَّتْ بِهِ الرِّيحُ also matters.

The verse describes not merely wind's presence but its intensity and effect on ashes.

Ash is light and strong wind can scatter it. Once dispersed in different directions, gathering it again becomes difficult or impossible.

The Quran uses this image for the outcome of the deeds of those who disbelieve in their Lord.

The elements of the comparison must be distinguished.

رَمَاد means ashes.

الرِّيح means wind.

عَاصِف expresses strong blowing and stormy conditions.

لَا يَقْدِرُونَ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ states the outcome concerning those people's deeds.

The root itself does not mean worthless deeds, lost reward, or error. Those ideas emerge from the complete comparison.

عَاصِف does not necessarily entail breaking large objects: wind's strength can appear in its ability to scatter light particles.

6. عَاصِفَة: powerful wind at Solomon's command

The third occurrence of this branch appears in Solomon's story:

وَلِسُلَيْمَانَ الرِّيحَ عَاصِفَةً تَجْرِي بِأَمْرِهِ إِلَى الْأَرْضِ الَّتِي بَارَكْنَا فِيهَا وَكُنَّا بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَالِمِينَ

wa-li-Sulaymāna al-rīḥa ʿāṣifatan tajrī bi-amrihi ilā al-arḍi allatī bāraknā fīhā wa-kunnā bi-kulli shayʾin ʿālimīn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:81

Al-Anbiya 21:81

“And for Solomon We subjected the powerful wind, flowing at his command toward the land We had blessed. We knew all things.”

عَاصِفَةً describes the wind's state; grammatically it is a circumstantial accusative, not an attributive adjective agreeing in definiteness. It expresses strong, forceful blowing.

The difference from Yunus 10:22 matters.

In Yunus the fierce wind appears in a dangerous scene, frightening passengers together with the waves.

In Al-Anbiya the forceful wind flows at Solomon's command.

Powerful wind is therefore not inherently dangerous to every person in every situation. Its use, circumstances, and effects are determined by context.

Water's force can likewise cause destruction or be used in a suitable structure to produce energy or accomplish useful work.

This example only distinguishes force from its outcome. The verse identifies no particular machine or technical mechanism.

Sad 38:36 also describes wind flowing at Solomon's command:

فَسَخَّرْنَا لَهُ الرِّيحَ تَجْرِي بِأَمْرِهِ رُخَاءً حَيْثُ أَصَابَ

fa-sakhkharnā lahu al-rīḥa tajrī bi-amrihi rukhāʾan ḥaythu aṣāb

Ṣād 38:36

Sad 38:36

“We subjected the wind to him, flowing gently at his command wherever he intended.”

رُخَاءً concerns gentleness and ease of movement.

These descriptions should not immediately be treated as contradictory.

One highlights wind's force, the other the gentleness of its flow. They may describe different conditions or aspects of its being subjected to him.

Both verses clearly describe wind flowing at Solomon's command.

Lexically, عَاصِفَة does not name an independent, uncontrollable power. It describes forceful movement; whether that force is controlled comes from the complete statement.

7. فَالْعَاصِفَاتِ عَصْفًا: blowing forcefully

Al-Mursalat opens:

وَالْمُرْسَلَاتِ عُرْفًا

wa-l-mursalāti ʿurfā

Al-Mursalāt 77:1

Al-Mursalat 77:1

“By those sent forth in succession.”

It then continues:

فَالْعَاصِفَاتِ عَصْفًا

fa-l-ʿāṣifāti ʿaṣfā

Al-Mursalāt 77:2

Al-Mursalat 77:2

“Then by those blowing fiercely.”

Two forms of the family appear together here.

الْعَاصِفَاتِ is a feminine plural active participle, referring to beings or things performing the action of عَصْف.

عَصْفًا is the same root's verbal noun, emphasizing the occurrence and intensity of the action.

Arabic calls this an absolute object: a cognate verbal noun used to emphasize an action or specify its manner.

الْعَاصِفَاتِ عَصْفًا thus emphasizes strong blowing or forceful movement.

What does الْعَاصِفَاتِ refer to?

The qualified noun is not stated explicitly. The Quran says الْعَاصِفَاتِ rather than الرِّيَاحُ الْعَاصِفَاتُ.

Lexically the description fits strong winds well, since other verses apply عَاصِف and عَاصِفَة to wind.

“Winds blowing fiercely” is therefore a natural reading.

The opening oaths also mention those sent forth, those spreading, those separating, and those delivering a reminder. Interpretations differ over whether these descriptions concern one group or several.

Clear uses of عَصْف for wind are important evidence, but alone do not determine every referent in the surah.

A base translation can retain “those blowing fiercely,” explaining strong winds as a prominent referent. “Blowing” itself already favors the wind reading and is not wholly neutral between interpretations.

عَصْفًا here is the verbal noun for strong blowing, not the noun for straw or foliage.

These different meanings within one family demonstrate why sentence structure matters.

8. الْعَصْف: cereal foliage and straw

The occurrence in Al-Rahman belongs to the plant branch:

وَالْحَبُّ ذُو الْعَصْفِ وَالرَّيْحَانُ

wa-l-ḥabbu dhū al-ʿaṣfi wa-l-rayḥān

Al-Raḥmān 55:12

Al-Rahman 55:12

“And grain with its straw, and fragrant plants.”

الْحَبّ concerns plant grains, particularly cereals.

ذُو الْعَصْفِ means possessing foliage or straw: grain, or a grain-bearing crop, accompanied by these plant parts.

Lexical explanations include cereal foliage, leaves around the ears, straw, chaff, and remnants left after grain separation.

This range shows that the word was not confined to a very narrowly defined plant part.

In a wheat field, edible grain is only part of the yield. Stalks, leaves, and ear remnants also have uses.

Some dry after harvest and become straw; others are cut before maturity and used as fodder.

عَصْف should therefore not always be restricted to entirely dry, useless remnants.

Grain and عَصْف

ذُو is an adjective qualifying الْحَبُّ, meaning “possessing.” The possessive construction is ذُو الْعَصْفِ: ذُو is the first term and الْعَصْفِ the genitive second term. الْحَبُّ is not directly in a possessive construction with الْعَصْفِ.

The Quran does not identify عَصْف with grain; it describes grain possessing it.

Grain-bearing parts and accompanying plant coverings belong to one plant but are not identical.

The preceding verse also mentions fruit and palms with coverings:

فِيهَا فَاكِهَةٌ وَالنَّخْلُ ذَاتُ الْأَكْمَامِ

fīhā fākihatun wa-l-nakhlu dhātu al-akmām

Al-Raḥmān 55:11

Al-Rahman 55:11

“In it are fruit and palm trees with sheaths.”

Both verses mention accompanying plant parts and coverings alongside edible produce.

أَكْمَام and عَصْف are different words and should not be reduced to one definition. Both nevertheless show that plants and their produce can have coverings and associated parts.

This matters in describing earthly blessings: fields yield more than grain and fruit. Other plant parts also serve human life, animal nourishment, and natural cycles.

These economic and environmental uses are reflections on earthly blessings, rather than the direct meaning of عَصْف.

الرَّيْحَانُ is a separate word; appearing beside عَصْف does not make it another name for straw.

“Grain with its straw” is a concise, understandable rendering for Forqan; the wider range of foliage and crop remnants can be explained in the root study.

9. كَعَصْفٍ مَأْكُولٍ: a comparison with eaten plant remnants

The second nominal occurrence of عَصْف appears in Al-Fil:

فَجَعَلَهُمْ كَعَصْفٍ مَأْكُولٍ

fa-jaʿalahum ka-ʿaṣfin maʾkūl

Al-Fil 105:5

Al-Fil 105:5

“He made them like eaten straw.”

عَصْف refers to plant material, not blowing wind.

كَـ marks a comparison: the people mentioned are likened to plant material with a particular quality.

مَأْكُولٍ states that quality.

مَأْكُول is the passive participle of أ ك ل: something eaten.

كَعَصْفٍ مَأْكُولٍ thus evokes crop foliage or straw that has been eaten and lost its former condition.

عَصْف and عَصْف مَأْكُول

عَصْف alone does not mean eaten material.

The leaves or straw may still be intact and available for fodder or other uses.

Adding مَأْكُول presents a different condition: plant material has been eaten and its former structure lost.

An animal feeding on foliage and crop remnants may break, chew, or trample portions. The result no longer resembles the intact standing plant.

That image helps explain the comparison.

The expression itself does not identify which animal ate it, or whether it was entirely swallowed and subsequently expelled as remnants.

Some lexical explanations instead understand عَصْف مَأْكُول as straw remaining after its grain has been eaten. That reading emphasizes the less valuable material left after the principal part has been removed.

Both explanations fit the field of crop foliage and straw; neither should be asserted as the only possible image without evidence.

The comparison within Al-Fil

Earlier verses describe the failure of the People of the Elephant's scheme and the sending of birds that pelt them with stones.

The final verse expresses the result through a tangible comparison: they were made like eaten straw.

It makes intelligible the disintegration and loss of effectiveness of a previously threatening force.

عَصْف itself does not mean a slain human. Plant material serves as the comparison's image.

Every feature should therefore not be imposed as an anatomical detail. The verse does not say their bodies literally became straw in composition or every body part precisely resembled chewed leaves.

The clear image is collapse, fragmentation, and loss of former condition and power.

This emerges from the full comparison and surah context, not from عَصْف alone.

10. Connecting the wind and plant branches

The Quranic uses allow examination of the relationship between these branches.

In Ibrahim 14:18, strong wind scatters ashes.

In Al-Rahman 55:12, عَصْف names crop foliage and straw.

In Al-Fil 105:5, the same plant material supplies an image of disintegration after being eaten.

Light leaves and straw are easily dispersed by wind. This could have contributed to a historical connection between the material's name and wind action.

A possibility, however, is not proof of historical derivation.

Lexicographers differ over deriving the name for straw from its being scattered by wind. Some propose the connection; others find it weak as an etymology.

It is therefore inappropriate to assert “breaking up and scattering everything” as a definitive original meaning and derive every use from it.

That account might seem attractive for wind and plant remnants, but poorly explains intact green leaves not yet broken or scattered.

Conversely, defining the entire family as “straw and chaff” cannot explain the clear wind usages.

A more accurate account recognizes two related but distinct branches: strong blowing and forceful movement; and crop foliage and straw.

11. Distinguishing related roots

ع ص ف and ر و ح

رِيح names wind, whereas عَاصِف describes the intensity of its blowing.

Not every wind is fierce. It may be calm and move air or objects gently.

Yunus 10:22 illustrates this through رِيحٍ طَيِّبَةٍ and رِيحٌ عَاصِفٌ.

عَاصِف should not simply be equated with wind: intensity matters.

ع ص ف and ق ص ف

ق ص ف concerns breaking, shattering, and force causing breakage.

Al-Isra 17:69 has قَاصِفًا مِنَ الرِّيحِ.

Both قَاصِف and عَاصِف can describe strong winds, but their emphasis differs.

عَاصِف foregrounds strong blowing; قَاصِف has a clearer connection with breaking and crushing.

A fierce wind can also break things, and a shattering wind necessarily has substantial force. This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary.

ع ص ف and ه ش م

ه ش م concerns breaking and fragmentation.

هَشِيم describes dry, broken plant remnants.

In Al-Kahf 18:45, flourishing vegetation becomes هَشِيمًا تَذْرُوهُ الرِّيَاحُ: dry fragments scattered by the winds.

The image resembles عَصْف مَأْكُول in Al-Fil, but the words differ.

Plant عَصْف names cereal foliage and straw and can apply before drying.

هَشِيم emphasizes the fragmented, broken condition.

Not every عَصْف is therefore هَشِيم, nor should every هَشِيم be called عَصْف.

ع ص ف and ر م د

رَمَاد means ashes left by burning.

In Ibrahim 14:18 strong wind scatters ashes.

Plant عَصْف concerns foliage and straw whose formation does not require burning.

Both materials can be light and dispersible, yet differ in nature and origin.

A shared capacity to be scattered does not make their names interchangeable.

ع ص ف and ز ر ع

زَرْع concerns cultivation and crops.

عَصْف does not name the entire plant or the act of cultivation; its plant branch concerns foliage and straw accompanying the yield.

الْحَبُّ ذُو الْعَصْفِ in Al-Rahman 55:12 presents عَصْف as part of, or material accompanying, a grain-bearing plant.

It should not be extended without evidence to the entire field or every agricultural product.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

The Quranic derivatives require translations respecting the two branches.

عَاصِفٌ — Yunus 10:22

Brief root meaning: strong, forceful blowing.

Meaning in this verse: fierce and forceful, describing wind.

Proposed translation:

رِيحٌ عَاصِفٌ

rīḥun ʿāṣif

Yūnus 10:22

“A fierce wind.”

عَاصِفٍ — Ibrahim 14:18

Meaning in this verse: stormy, describing a day of strong wind.

Proposed translation:

فِي يَوْمٍ عَاصِفٍ

fī yawmin ʿāṣif

Ibrāhīm 14:18

“On a stormy day.”

عَاصِفَةً — Al-Anbiya 21:81

Meaning in this verse: powerful and strongly blowing, describing wind.

Proposed translation:

الرِّيحَ عَاصِفَةً

al-rīḥa ʿāṣifah

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:81

“The powerful wind.”

الْعَصْفِ — Al-Rahman 55:12

Brief meaning in the plant branch: cereal foliage and straw.

Meaning in this verse: straw and leaves accompanying a grain-bearing crop.

Proposed translation:

وَالْحَبُّ ذُو الْعَصْفِ

wa-l-ḥabbu dhū al-ʿaṣf

Al-Raḥmān 55:12

“And grain with its straw.”

الْعَاصِفَاتِ عَصْفًا — Al-Mursalat 77:2

Meaning in this verse: those blowing strongly or moving forcefully.

Proposed translation:

“Then by those blowing fiercely.”

The explanation can identify powerful winds as a prominent referent. “Blowing” favors that reading; discussion of other referents requires examining the opening oaths as a whole.

عَصْفًا — Al-Mursalat 77:2

Meaning in this verse: strong blowing, an emphatic verbal noun.

عَصْفٍ — Al-Fil 105:5

Meaning in this verse: straw or crop remnants.

Proposed translation:

كَعَصْفٍ مَأْكُولٍ

ka-ʿaṣfin maʾkūl

Al-Fil 105:5

“Like eaten straw.”

“Eaten” corresponds to مَأْكُولٍ, not to عَصْفٍ itself.

For Forqan word boxes, a brief family meaning can be:

“Strong, forceful blowing; in the plant branch, cereal foliage and straw.”

Each derivative still needs its own contextual meaning, separately from that family summary.

Conclusion

ع ص ف has two important Arabic branches: strong blowing of wind, and crop foliage and straw.

عَاصِف and عَاصِفَة express forceful blowing. The Quran uses them in maritime danger, the scattering of ashes, and wind flowing at Solomon's command.

Wind strength is the meaning; destructiveness, usefulness, and danger depend on circumstances and context.

Plant عَصْف concerns cereal foliage, straw, and chaff. Al-Rahman 55:12 presents it among earthly plant blessings, whereas عَصْف مَأْكُول in Al-Fil 105:5 depicts collapse and disintegration.

In Al-Mursalat 77:2, عَصْف is the verbal noun for strong blowing and must be distinguished from the plant noun.

A natural connection between the branches is understandable because strong winds scatter light foliage and straw. That alone does not establish one historical origin for every sense.

Quranic study and translation should preserve both principal senses: “strong, forceful blowing” in wind passages and “cereal foliage and straw” in plant passages. Intensity, scattering, fragmentation, and destruction must each be distinguished to the extent supported by wording and context.
