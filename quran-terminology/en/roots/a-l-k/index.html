Foundational Meaning

The root A L K, at the lexical level, is connected with message, the mission of delivering a message, and mediation in transferring speech or command. Its core is not ownership or sovereignty, but conveying something entrusted from elsewhere.

The bearer of a message does not produce the message from himself. He is entrusted with transmission. From this angle, A L K is tied to trust, mediation, mission, and carrying a command from sender to receiver.

Lexical Layer

In older Arabic, words such as أَلُوك (alūk) and مَأْلُكَة (ma'lukah) are remembered in the field of message and mission. For this reason, some lexicographers related the word مَلَك (malak) and its plural مَلَائِكَة (malā'ikah) to this semantic field.

In Qur'anic work, however, two levels must be distinguished: one level is morphological and statistical classification; the other is semantic and etymological analysis. These two levels can speak to one another, but one should not replace the other.

Careful Relation to Malā'ikah

In common Qur'anic classification, مَلَك (malak) and مَلَائِكَة (malā'ikah) are placed under the root M L K, not under A L K. Therefore, in this entry, A L K is not treated as the decisive root of malā'ikah. It is studied as one lexical path for understanding the layer of message, mission, and mediation.

This caution matters because the Qur'an distinguishes words and titles. Etymology should not force all semantic layers of a word into one direction.

Angels in the Role of Messengers

At the beginning of Sūrat Fāṭir, the Qur'an says: جَاعِلِ الْمَلَائِكَةِ رُسُلًا (jāʿili al-malā'ikati rusulan), the One who makes the angels messengers.

This verse shows that angels can be rusul in the role of mission. But the verse does not say that the entire reality of angels is only message-delivery, nor does it say that every occurrence of rasūl must be returned to angels.

Rasūl is a functional title: one sent. It can be used for a human being, and it can be used for angels from the angle of mission. But rasūl is not the name of a species. It is the title of one who has been sent and entrusted with delivering or executing a matter.

Distinguishing Angels and Messengers

In verses of faith, the Qur'an distinguishes The God, angels, books, and messengers. In Al-Baqarah 2:285: كُلٌّ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَمَلَائِكَتِهِ وَكُتُبِهِ وَرُسُلِهِ (kullun āmana bi-Allāhi wa-malā'ikatihi wa-kutubihi wa-rusulihi), each has faith in The God, His angels, His books, and His messengers.

This distinction shows that malā'ikah and rusul are not fully identical titles. Some angels may be messengers in mission, but all uses of rusul do not return to angels, and the whole subject of angels is not exhausted by message-delivery.

In the Qur'an, angels have varied tasks: bowing before Adam, recording deeds, taking souls, carrying the Throne, descending with divine command, supporting believers, and being present in scenes of Resurrection.

Difference Between A L K and M L K

A L K explains the field of message, mission, and mediation. M L K, however, is connected with ownership, power, rule, mastery, and having something within one's authority.

If malā'ikah are studied under M L K, attention is drawn to their executive power and place within the order of divine command. If they are viewed from the angle of A L K, attention is drawn to message-delivery and mission. These two angles may approach each other in some cases, but they are not identical.

Therefore, referring malā'ikah to A L K clarifies the layer of message and mission; it does not erase the relation between malā'ikah and M L K. These two angles should be seen beside one another.

Angels and Execution of Command

In the Qur'an, angels are not merely silent letter-carriers. They are obedient agents and appointed workers within the order of divine command. About the keepers of Hell, At-Tahrim 66:6 says: لَا يَعْصُونَ اللَّهَ مَا أَمَرَهُمْ وَيَفْعَلُونَ مَا يُؤْمَرُونَ (lā yaʿṣūna Allāha mā amarahum wa-yafʿalūna mā yu'marūn), they do not disobey The God in what He commands them, and they do what they are commanded.

This verse shows angels in the axis of obedience and execution. So although message-delivery is one important layer in understanding them, the Qur'anic concept of angels is broader than verbal message-delivery.

They are agents of the order of command: sometimes they bring a message, sometimes they record, sometimes they support, sometimes they take, and sometimes they are present in scenes of Resurrection.

Difference From Nearby Roots

A L K is close to R S L, but not the same. R S L is the general root of sending and being a messenger, and in the Qur'an it is much broader than the discussion of angels. A rasūl can be a human being who conveys the message in society, history, language, calling, and ethical responsibility.

A L K focuses more on the message itself and the mediation of the message: something is carried from sender to receiver. R S L is the field of sending and being sent; A L K is the field of message and entrusted transmission.

A L K also differs from N B A and KH B R. Naba' and khabar focus more on the content of information: what is known or reported. A L K attends to the route of delivering the message: sender, bearer, message, and receiver. In naba', the main weight is on the report itself; in A L K, the main weight is on entrusted transmission and mediation.

Semitic Linguistic Note

From the perspective of Semitic linguistics, this semantic connection is noteworthy. In languages close to Arabic, words from this semantic family appear for messenger or envoy. This proximity shows that understanding angels from the angle of message-delivery is not rootless.

Even so, in Qur'anic work the Qur'anic text must remain the main criterion. The text places malā'ikah, at the level of common morphological classification, in the field of M L K, while functionally bringing them near to mission and message-delivery in some verses.

The Qur'anic Correction About Angels

The Qur'an itself indicates that some Arab mushrikīn held incorrect ideas about angels: they imagined angels as female, constructed a relation of offspring for The God, and sometimes placed them in a position of intercession or worshipable mediation.

In Az-Zukhruf 43:19: وَجَعَلُوا الْمَلَائِكَةَ الَّذِينَ هُمْ عِبَادُ الرَّحْمَٰنِ إِنَاثًا (wa-jaʿalū al-malā'ikata alladhīna hum ʿibādu al-raḥmāni ināthā), they made the angels, who are servants of The Merciful, female.

The Qur'an rejects this view. Angels are brought down from the position of worship, kinship with The God, and tribal imagination, and are placed in their proper position: they are servants and agents of The God, not daughters of The God and not partners with Him.

Methodological Principle

The entry A L K gives a methodological principle: wherever message and mission are discussed, the bearer of the message must be distinguished from the owner of the message. Mission does not create independent lordly identity.

If an angel brings a message, the message is not from the angel. If an agent executes a command, the command is not from that agent. The agent is a mediator, not the source.

This principle matters for understanding angels. In the Qur'an, angels are not beings independent from The God in governing the world. They are within the orbit of divine command. If M L K shows their direction of power and agency, A L K clarifies their direction of message and mission. Both directions should be seen, but neither should replace the other.

Boundary of This Entry

In this entry, discussions of Jibrīl, Rūḥ al-Qudus, and Rūḥ al-Amīn are mentioned only as boundaries of research, not as the main topic. Each of those terms needs its own article.

What matters here is that the Qur'an does not allow all unseen titles to be melted into one another. Malā'ikah, rusul, rūḥ, and specific names must each be studied in their own place and according to their own context.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For مَلَائِكَة (malā'ikah): “angels” in smooth translation; and in root-level explanation, “divine agents/envoys within the order of command.”

For جَاعِلِ الْمَلَائِكَةِ رُسُلًا (jāʿili al-malā'ikati rusulan): “the One who makes the angels messengers,” or “the One who appoints the angels as sent agents.”

For رُسُلًا (rusulan) in the context of angels: “messengers,” “sent agents,” or “bearers of mission.”

For لَا يَعْصُونَ اللَّهَ مَا أَمَرَهُمْ (lā yaʿṣūna Allāha mā amarahum): “they do not disobey The God in what He commands them.”

For وَيَفْعَلُونَ مَا يُؤْمَرُونَ (wa-yafʿalūna mā yu'marūn): “and they do what they are commanded.”

Summary

A L K is the root of message and entrusted transmission. This root is important for understanding the layer of message-delivery and mission in the Qur'anic world, but it is not enough by itself to establish the decisive root of malā'ikah.

At the level of morphological classification, malā'ikah stand in the field of M L K. At the level of function, in some verses they approach rusul. At the lexical level, A L K can help us understand their layer of message and mediation.

The right method is to see these layers beside one another without confusing them. Angels are not agents independent from The God. They are servants and workers of divine command. If they bring a message or execute a command, the message and command are not from themselves; they are from The God.
