Striking, applying force, and driving back with a direct blow

The Arabic root w-k-z denotes delivering a blow or applying direct force to someone or something. Wakaza means to strike, thrust, or hit someone with the hand; wakz names this kind of blow or forceful thrust.

In its familiar use concerning people, wakz usually describes a blow delivered with a clenched hand or fist. It is also associated with pushing, but is not equivalent to gentle pressure or touching. The blow may aim to drive someone back, overcome resistance, or cause injury.

Arabic lexical sources also record this root for striking with a pointed implement, embedding something, and several other limited uses. In its Qur’anic occurrence, however, the central meaning is striking a person.

The root w-k-z occurs only once in the Qur’an, as fa-wakazahu in al-Qasas 28:15. During a fight between two men, Moses strikes one of them, and the blow results in his death.

1. The root in early Arabic

Early lexicons record wakazahu wakzan as striking someone or driving him back with force. They explain wakz through taʿn, dafʿ, and striking with a clenched hand.

These three expressions overlap in one respect, but are not identical.

Dafʿ emphasizes driving something away or repelling it with force. Darb concerns delivering a blow. In a physical context, taʿn can denote a direct thrust or piercing, especially with a pointed implement.

In human action, wakz describes a direct blow, usually delivered with a clenched hand, which may drive the other person back or injure him.

The expression wakazahu bi-jumʿi kaffihi means striking with a clenched hand: what is ordinarily called punching.

Some lexical explanations identify the chin or chest as the point of impact. These details, however, are not fixed components of the root’s meaning. Such a blow can strike another part of the body.

For example, gently moving someone aside with an open hand is not necessarily wakz. Striking him with a fist or a sudden thrust of the hand is more readily described by this word.

Classical Arabic also records wakaza anfahu for a blow that breaks the nose. This usage shows that the blow can cause serious injury, but breaking a bone is not an essential part of the verb’s meaning.

Some lexical accounts record wakazahu bi-l-rumh for striking or piercing someone with a spear. In this usage, the direction and penetration of the thrust matter, while the sentence identifies the implement.

The semantic core of this branch may therefore be described as delivering a direct, concentrated blow or thrust against something. It may be delivered by hand or, in some usages, with an implement.

Historical evidence and its limits

In determining this root’s pre-Qur’anic meaning, we must distinguish the usage recorded in the Qur’an itself from evidence transmitted in later sources.

A half-line attributed to al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhali reads:

وَالشَّوْكُ فِي أَخْمَصِ الرِّجْلَيْنِ مَوْكُوزُ

wa-l-shawku fī akhmaṣi al-rijlayni mawkūzu

Translation: “And thorns are embedded in the arches of both feet.”

In this version, mawkuz denotes something embedded or driven into a surface.

The wording of this witness is not secure, however. Other versions read markuz, reported as the better-known reading. The attribution of the mawkuz version to al-Mutanakhkhil is also uncertain.

This half-line therefore cannot serve as independent, conclusive evidence for wakz in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Among the evidence examined here, no independent poetic witness for the meaning of striking has a securely established pre-Qur’anic date and attribution. Al-Qasas 28:15 itself directly attests this usage in the language of revelation, while early lexical accounts also support the meanings of striking, thrusting, and driving back.

This evidence is sufficient for understanding the Qur’anic usage. Establishing the root’s earliest historical meaning or the order in which its branches developed requires further evidence.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • Wakaza: he struck, thrust, or punched.
  • Yakizu: he strikes or thrusts.
  • Wakz: a direct blow, forceful thrust, or striking.
  • Wakzah: a single blow of this kind.
  • Wakiz: someone who delivers a blow.
  • Mawkuz: someone or something struck or subjected to a blow.
  • Tawakkaza: in certain recorded usages, he leaned on a staff; also, he prepared himself or became full of food.
  • Tawakkuz: the verbal noun of tawakkaza in these usages.

Some sources also record wakaza al-wiʿaʾ for filling a container. Wakkaza is also reported for running in fear, although even some early lexicographers questioned the reliability of this latter usage.

These secondary meanings should not be traced to a single historical origin without sufficient evidence. In particular, leaning on a staff, although listed under this entry in some dictionaries, must not be confused with the independent root w-k-ʾ, used in the Qur’an for Moses leaning on his staff.

Study of w-k-ʾ

None of these secondary forms occurs in the Qur’an. Its only Qur’anic form is fa-wakazahu.

This form derives from the basic triliteral verb wakaza–yakizu. Here the verb is transitive: the blow is delivered to another person.

3. Qur’anic usage: Moses strikes a man during a fight

Al-Qasas 28:15

وَدَخَلَ الْمَدِينَةَ عَلَىٰ حِينِ غَفْلَةٍ مِنْ أَهْلِهَا فَوَجَدَ فِيهَا رَجُلَيْنِ يَقْتَتِلَانِ هَٰذَا مِنْ شِيعَتِهِ وَهَٰذَا مِنْ عَدُوِّهِ ۖ فَاسْتَغَاثَهُ الَّذِي مِنْ شِيعَتِهِ عَلَى الَّذِي مِنْ عَدُوِّهِ فَوَكَزَهُ مُوسَىٰ فَقَضَىٰ عَلَيْهِ ۖ قَالَ هَٰذَا مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ ۖ إِنَّهُ عَدُوٌّ مُضِلٌّ مُبِينٌ

wa-dakhala al-madīnata ʿalā ḥīni ghaflatin min ahlihā fa-wajada fīhā rajulayni yaqtatilāni hādhā min shīʿatihi wa-hādhā min ʿaduwwihi fa-staghāthahu alladhī min shīʿatihi ʿalā alladhī min ʿaduwwihi fa-wakazahu Mūsā fa-qaḍā ʿalayhi qāla hādhā min ʿamali al-shayṭāni innahu ʿaduwwun muḍillun mubīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15

Translation: “He entered the city at a time when its people were unaware, and found two men fighting there, one from his own group and the other from his enemies. The one from his group sought his help against the one from his enemies. Moses punched the man and killed him. He said: This is of Satan’s work; he is a misleading, manifest enemy.”

In this verse, Moses encounters two men fighting each other.

One belongs to Moses’ group and the other to the opposing group. The first asks Moses for help, and Moses strikes the other man.

Fa-wakazahu consists of three components:

Fa- connects this action to the preceding event.

Wakaza is a past-tense verb denoting striking.

Hu is a masculine singular object pronoun referring to the man from Moses’ opposing group.

The subject, Musa, follows the verb.

The sentence’s structure thus makes clear that Moses struck the man.

Does wakazahu mean “he punched him”?

Early lexical explanations commonly describe wakz as striking with a clenched hand. Translating the Qur’anic usage as “he punched” therefore has a linguistic basis.

Some explanations, however, relate it to pressure from the fingers or pushing someone with the hand. Certain accounts also mention the use of a staff.

The form fa-wakazahu itself does not specify the exact point of impact. The Qur’an does not say that Moses struck the man’s chest, chin, abdomen, or another part of his body.

Nor does the sentence mention an implement such as a staff. Adding “with a staff” to the basic translation therefore has no direct basis in the verse’s wording.

Among the recorded meanings, “he punched” is familiar and clear. “He struck him” is a broader rendering that leaves the precise position of the hand unspecified.

Either way, the basic action is the same: Moses delivered a physical blow to the man.

4. The distinction between striking and killing

The most important point in understanding w-k-z is the distinction between the blow itself and its outcome.

The verse says:

«فَوَكَزَهُ مُوسَىٰ فَقَضَىٰ عَلَيْهِ»

fa-wakazahu Mūsā fa-qaḍā ʿalayhi

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15

In the first part, wakaza describes the act of striking.

In the second, qada ʿalayhi describes the ending of the man’s life.

The man’s death is therefore understood from the sentence as a whole, not from the root w-k-z alone.

If wakaza inherently meant killing, it could not describe a blow that someone survives. Yet the root’s lexical usages describe striking and driving back without making death a necessary condition of the action.

For example, someone might punch another person during a fight and merely drive him back. The blow might cause injury or unconsciousness. Under different circumstances, the same kind of blow could cause death.

The kind of action and its outcome are separate matters.

In Moses’ story, the Qur’an explicitly states that his blow resulted in the man’s death. In al-Qasas 28:33, Moses himself also speaks of having killed someone and fears the consequences.

The occurrence of death therefore cannot be denied.

But whether Moses intended to kill the man when he struck him, or only sought to stop him from continuing the fight, cannot be established from the verb wakaza itself.

Intending to deliver a blow and intending to kill are two different matters.

Wakaza describes what Moses did; fa-qada ʿalayhi indicates the result. Assessing his intention requires considering the story as a whole and his response after the incident.

5. Moses’ response and responsibility for the act

Later in al-Qasas 28:15, Moses calls the event Satan’s work and describes Satan as a misleading, manifest enemy.

In al-Qasas 28:16, Moses tells his Lord that he has wronged himself and asks for forgiveness. The Qur’an then states that he was forgiven.

In al-Qasas 28:17, Moses declares that he will no longer support the guilty.

These verses show that Moses did not regard the event as trivial. He takes the consequences of his action seriously and turns back to God.

Nevertheless, several matters must be distinguished: delivering the blow, the man’s death, Moses’ intention when intervening, and his moral judgment of what happened.

The man’s death alone does not prove that Moses acted with a prior intention to kill him. Conversely, the absence of an explicit statement of such an intention should not lead to denying the action or its actual consequence.

The Qur’an states both the death and Moses’ response and request for forgiveness.

In al-Shuʿaraʾ 26:14, Moses mentions a charge against him and fears being killed. In al-Shuʿaraʾ 26:20, when answering Pharaoh, he does not deny having committed the act.

These connections matter for understanding Moses’ responsibility, but the entire discussion of sin, intention, error, and forgiveness should not become part of the lexical meaning of wakz. Forqan’s study of the root dh-n-b examines this subject more broadly.

Lexically, the conclusion is clear: wakaza describes striking. The ultimate intention and moral consequences must be understood from other evidence.

6. Comparison with related roots

W-k-z (wakz) and d-r-b (darb): a direct blow and striking

Darb has a broad semantic range in Arabic. In physical usage, it denotes delivering a blow.

Wakz is more specific in human action, usually referring to a direct blow with a clenched hand or a forceful thrust.

Wakz can therefore be a kind of darb, but not every blow is necessarily wakz.

Someone might strike a surface with an implement or deliver a different kind of blow. Whether that action can also be called wakz depends on the kind of impact and established linguistic usage.

W-k-z (wakz) and d-f-ʿ (dafʿ): striking and driving back

Dafʿ concerns driving away, repelling, or preventing something from advancing.

Wakz may aim to drive someone back, but it also emphasizes the delivery of a direct blow.

Someone might drive another person back with sustained pressure without striking him. This can be dafʿ without necessarily being wakz.

Conversely, someone might strike another person without moving him from his place.

The other person’s retreat is therefore not a necessary result of wakz, even though it may accompany the action in some usages.

Study of d-f-ʿ

W-k-z (wakz) and n-k-z (nakz): a blow and concentrated pressure

Arabic nakz concerns striking, applying a pinching kind of pressure, or driving someone back with a blow.

Early lexicons sometimes present wakazahu and nakazahu as closely related expressions.

The distinction between the two roots is not clear and absolute in every usage. Some lexical explanations associate nakz more with a blow or pressure from the fingertips, and wakz with a clenched hand.

This distinction can be useful in certain cases, but should not become an absolute rule for the entire Arabic language without sufficient evidence.

W-k-z (wakz) and l-k-z (lakz): striking with the hand

Lakz is also used for striking with the hand.

Some sources present wakz and lakz as almost synonymous. Others distinguish the point of impact or the manner of striking.

For example, wakz is sometimes described as a blow to the chest and lakz as a blow to the back. This distinction is not constant across all usages, however.

Reports of early readings also give fa-lakazahu for al-Qasas 28:15. This report helps illustrate the closeness of the two verbs, but the Qur’anic text examined by Forqan reads fa-wakazahu, and that wording must be preserved.

W-k-z (wakz) and t-ʿ-n (taʿn): a direct blow and piercing

In physical usage, taʿn denotes a direct thrust, especially with a pointed implement.

Some lexical usages also explain wakz through taʿn, since both can involve a direct, concentrated striking movement.

Taʿn, however, is not necessarily performed with a fist, while wakz in the principal usage concerning Moses is closer to a blow delivered by hand.

For this reason, translating fa-wakazahu as “he speared him” is inappropriate when the verse itself mentions no such implement.

W-k-z (wakz) and q-t-l (qatl): striking and taking life

Qatl denotes killing or taking life, but the root itself does not specify how it is done.

Wakz denotes a striking action without including death in its meaning.

In al-Qasas 28:15, these concepts meet in one event: Moses strikes the man, and the man dies.

Wakz thus identifies the means or kind of action, whereas the fatal outcome is understood from the rest of the sentence.

This distinction matters for accurate translation and for understanding human responsibility.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

In human action, suitable renderings of wakaza are “he punched,” “he struck,” or “he struck with a clenched hand.”

For wakz, “a direct blow” is clear; in a punching context, “a punch” is also suitable.

For wakzah, “a single blow” or “one punch” is appropriate.

For wakiz, “one who strikes” is proposed; for mawkuz, “struck.” When the usage involves a pointed implement, the rendering must reflect that context.

For the phrase fa-wakazahu Musa fa-qada ʿalayhi in al-Qasas 28:15, the following translation is proposed:

“So Moses punched him and killed him.”

This rendering is concise and natural, preserving the distinction between the two verbs: first the blow, then the man’s death.

For a broader translation that does not specify the hand’s position, one may say:

“So Moses struck him, and that blow resulted in his death.”

The basic translation must not turn wakz into “he intentionally killed,” since the verb itself does not establish an intention to kill.

Nor is “he gently pushed him” appropriate: it reduces the action’s force without evidence and does not clearly fit the outcome reported in the verse.

8. Conclusion

In its principal usage, w-k-z denotes delivering a direct, concentrated blow or thrust. This is usually done with a clenched hand or fist, although some Arabic usages record striking with an implement.

Lexicons also place other meanings under this family, including embedding something, filling a container, and leaning on a staff. There is insufficient evidence to establish the historical relationship and order of development of all these usages; they should therefore not be imposed on the Qur’anic meaning.

The Qur’an contains only fa-wakazahu in al-Qasas 28:15. During a fight between two men, Moses strikes one, and the blow results in the man’s death.

Wakz itself does not mean killing. Death is understood from the continuation, fa-qada ʿalayhi. Nor can an intention to kill be inferred solely from the verb describing the blow.

This distinction neither removes Moses’ responsibility nor permits certainty about an intention that the Qur’an does not explicitly state.

The final definition of w-k-z is delivering a direct blow or thrust against someone or something; in its familiar human usage, usually a blow with a clenched hand or fist. In al-Qasas 28:15, the blow results in the man’s death, but death and any possible intention to kill are not components of the root’s meaning and must be examined separately through the context.
