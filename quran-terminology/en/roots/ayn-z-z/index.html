The Qur’anic Root ʿ Z Z

Power, firmness, invincibility, dignity, and difficulty

The Arabic root ع ز ز concerns power, hardness, firmness, and prevailing. عِزّ and عِزَّة can express strength, dignity, and a position in which a person is not easily defeated or humiliated. عَزِيز can describe someone or something powerful, esteemed, inaccessible, or difficult. The verb عَزَّ can mean to prevail over another, while عَزَّزَ denotes strengthening or reinforcing.

Not every use is confined to power and victory. Arabic also uses ʿazīz for something rare and difficult to find. When something عَزَّ عَلَيْهِ, it may be difficult or burdensome for someone. In such a construction, ʿazīz does not mean powerful or invincible.

The Qur’anic family covers a broad field. ʿIzzah can describe TheGod, human standing and strength, or pride and obstinacy in a context of criticism. ʿAzīz likewise describes TheGod, serves as a title for an official in Joseph’s story, and qualifies a book and a victory. Power, social standing, difficulty, rarity, and moral evaluation therefore need to be distinguished.

The Quranic Arabic Corpus counts 119 occurrences: one Form I verb عَزَّ, one Form II عَزَّزْنَا, one Form IV تُعِزُّ, three أَعَزّ, one عِزّ, eleven عِزَّة, and 101 forms of عَزِيز, including two instances of the plural أَعِزَّة. The proper name الْعُزَّىٰ in Al-Najm 53:19 is not included in that count; its linguistic connection with the family is examined separately here. The verbal classification in this count follows the reading of Ḥafṣ.

Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Power, hardness, and resistance to being overcome

In the Qur’an, ʿizz and ʿizzah concern strength, high standing, and resistance to humiliation. The opposition of تُعِزُّ and تُذِلُّ in Āl ʿImrān 3:26, and of الْأَعَزُّ and الْأَذَلَّ in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:8, makes this relationship clear. ʿAzīz can also describe someone powerful who is not easily overcome.

These meanings have a clear relationship. A powerful person can defend themselves; someone with adequate support is less vulnerable to defeat; and a person with influence and high standing may enjoy social dignity and prestige.

Physical strength, social influence, and moral worth are nevertheless distinct. Someone may have high social standing while acting wrongly. Another may lack outward power yet stand honorably in a moral judgment. The root alone does not determine these differences.

For the root’s physical imagery, Al-Mutalammis’s verse about the firmness of a camel’s flesh is significant. It attests a bodily use of the family. This review did not find a datable pre-Qur’anic example of أَرْضٌ عَزَازٌ, recorded in dictionaries for hard ground, so that expression is not used to reconstruct an original meaning.

A physical sense does not itself establish that all social meanings or divine descriptions developed from one particular material image. Hardness and resistance to being overcome are intelligibly related, but their historical sequence requires independent evidence.

Evidence from pre-Islamic poetry

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says:

وَلَوْ حَضَرَتْهُ تَغْلِبُ ابْنَةُ وَائِلٍ
لَكَانُوا لَهُ عِزًّا عَزِيزًا وَنَاصِرَا

Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, Sharḥ Dīwān, p. 137.

“Had Taghlib, the offspring of Wāʾil, been there with him, they would have been powerful support and a helper to him.”

Sharḥ Dīwān Ṭarafah transmits this verse with حَضَرَتْهُ and ابْنَةُ وَائِلٍ. عِزًّا عَزِيزًا emphasizes the tribe’s supporting strength; the translation follows the wording quoted here.

ʿIzz concerns the backing and force that a tribe can provide. It is more than inward respect or affection: it involves people able to defend someone and exercise effective power against others.

The verse attests a pre-Qur’anic connection between ʿizz, collective strength, and social support. This social sense should therefore not be treated as an innovation first created by the Qur’an.

Another example is attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Mutalammis:

أُجُدٌ إِذَا ضَمَرَتْ تَعَزَّزَ لَحْمُهَا
وَإِذَا تُشَدُّ بِنِسْعِهَا لَا تَنْبِسُ

Al-Mutalammis al-Ḍubaʿī; transmitted in Al-Muḥkam under ʿ Z Z. The Dīwān variant with «عنس», Cairo 1970, p. 180, is also recorded in the editorial notes to Kitāb al-Afʿāl.

“A sturdy she-camel: when she grows lean, her flesh becomes firm; when her girth is tightened, she makes no sound.” The opening أُجُدٌ describes the camel’s sturdiness. A Dīwān transmission instead begins with عَنْسٌ; both preserve تَعَزَّزَ لَحْمُهَا.

Here تَعَزَّزَ لَحْمُهَا denotes the camel’s flesh becoming hard and firm. It concerns bodily firmness rather than social rank, tribal pride, or victory over an enemy.

Together, these examples attest both collective human support and physical firmness in pre-Islamic Arabic. The fields are related, but neither alone establishes the other’s historical origin.

Rarity, difficulty, and value

Arabic dictionaries record “rare and difficult to find” for عَزَّ الشَّيْءُ and عَزِيز. This review did not find independent pre-Qur’anic evidence for that branch, and the verses quoted here do not establish it by themselves. It should therefore not be inserted into Qur’anic occurrences without contextual support.

A semantic connection with resistance is understandable: something not readily available is difficult to obtain. That relationship, however, does not establish a historical sequence of semantic development without further evidence.

In the same broad field, عَزَّ عَلَيْهِ الْأَمْرُ can mean that something became difficult or burdensome for someone. عَزَّ عَلَيَّ can accordingly mean “it was hard for me.”

Someone or something deeply valued may also be called ʿazīz. Value, rarity, and difficulty are nevertheless distinct: something can be rare without being valuable, or valuable without being rare.

These differences matter in translation. Not every ʿazīz means “beloved,” just as not every occurrence means “powerful.”

Qur’anic verbs: prevailing, reinforcing, and granting honor

“He overpowered me in speech”

In the account of the two disputants who came before David, one says:

إِنَّ هَٰذَا أَخِي لَهُ تِسْعٌ وَتِسْعُونَ نَعْجَةً وَلِيَ نَعْجَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ فَقَالَ أَكْفِلْنِيهَا وَعَزَّنِي فِي الْخِطَابِ

“This is my brother. He has ninety-nine ewes and I have one ewe. Yet he said, ‘Entrust it to me,’ and he overpowered me in speech.”

Ṣād 38:23

عَزَّنِي is the Form I verb ʿazza with an object pronoun. It expresses prevailing over someone, while فِي الْخِطَابِ specifies the field: speaking and presenting a claim.

The phrase does not necessarily imply physical defeat or direct force. One disputant says that the other prevailed over him in the discussion and in pressing his demand.

This may involve unequal standing, speaking ability, or the manner of demanding, but the verb alone does not identify the precise cause. What the wording states is one person gaining the upper hand over another in speech.

Prevailing in speech does not make a claim correct. Someone can dominate an argument or conversation while being in the wrong. This distinction matters in a story about judgment and justice.

Reinforcement by a third

Yā Sīn says:

إِذْ أَرْسَلْنَا إِلَيْهِمُ اثْنَيْنِ فَكَذَّبُوهُمَا فَعَزَّزْنَا بِثَالِثٍ فَقَالُوا إِنَّا إِلَيْكُمْ مُرْسَلُونَ

“When We sent two to them and they rejected both, We reinforced them with a third. They said, ‘We have been sent to you.’”

Yā Sīn 36:14

عَزَّزْنَا is Form II and expresses strengthening or reinforcement here. بِثَالِثٍ specifies the means: sending a third person.

Shuʿbah’s transmission from ʿĀṣim reads فَعَزَزْنَا, without doubling the first zāy, while Ḥafṣ reads فَعَزَّزْنَا, with doubling. The Form II analysis here concerns Ḥafṣ’s wording. For the lighter form, granting the upper hand or providing support is proposed; reinforcement cannot be deduced from a Form II pattern in that reading.

The verse shows that this family can express adding strength and support, as well as a state of being strong.

Form II alone does not establish that reinforcement was gradual, continuous, or performed in multiple stages. The number of messengers comes from “two” and “a third,” rather than from the verbal pattern itself.

“You grant honor to whom You will”

Āl ʿImrān says:

قُلِ اللَّهُمَّ مَالِكَ الْمُلْكِ تُؤْتِي الْمُلْكَ مَن تَشَاءُ وَتَنزِعُ الْمُلْكَ مِمَّن تَشَاءُ وَتُعِزُّ مَن تَشَاءُ وَتُذِلُّ مَن تَشَاءُ بِيَدِكَ الْخَيْرُ إِنَّكَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

“Say: O TheGod, Owner of sovereignty! You grant sovereignty to whom You will and take it away from whom You will. You grant honor to whom You will and humble whom You will. Good is in Your hand; You are capable of all things.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:26

تُعِزُّ is Form IV and concerns making another ʿazīz. It is contrasted with تُذِلُّ, bringing someone low.

The contrast clarifies the semantic field: strength, dignity, and freedom from humiliation.

Granting honor can accompany granting sovereignty, but the root is not limited to political rule. The verse mentions mulk and tuʿizzu separately, while other occurrences connect ʿizzah with support, standing, and ability. Coordination alone does not establish completely separate semantic fields.

A record of Qur’anic forms must distinguish تُعِزُّ from the grammatically possible active participle مُعِزّ. Muʿizz itself does not occur in the Qur’an: the possibility of forming a derivative does not make it an attested Qur’anic word.

Human ʿizzah: dignity, social power, and obstinacy

ʿIzzah contrasted with humiliation

Al-Nisāʾ says:

الَّذِينَ يَتَّخِذُونَ الْكَافِرِينَ أَوْلِيَاءَ مِن دُونِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ أَيَبْتَغُونَ عِندَهُمُ الْعِزَّةَ فَإِنَّ الْعِزَّةَ لِلَّهِ جَمِيعًا

“Those who take the disbelievers as patrons and protectors instead of the believers—do they seek honor and strength with them? All ʿizzah belongs to TheGod.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:139

ʿIzzah is something people may seek with others. In the context of taking patrons and protectors, that hope can concern power, support, backing, and social standing.

The verse questions seeking such ʿizzah with them and declares that all ʿizzah belongs to TheGod.

Fāṭir likewise says:

مَن كَانَ يُرِيدُ الْعِزَّةَ فَلِلَّهِ الْعِزَّةُ جَمِيعًا

“Whoever desires ʿizzah—all ʿizzah belongs to TheGod.”

Fāṭir 35:10

The rest of the verse discusses good speech, righteous action, and evil plotting. Seeking ʿizzah is thus also considered in an ethical setting.

ʿIzzah itself does not mean righteous action. Righteous action is introduced in the rest of the verse, and its relationship with ʿizzah must be understood from the full passage.

Al-Munāfiqūn gives a clear example of a claim to ʿizzah and the Qur’an’s response:

يَقُولُونَ لَئِن رَّجَعْنَا إِلَى الْمَدِينَةِ لَيُخْرِجَنَّ الْأَعَزُّ مِنْهَا الْأَذَلَّ وَلِلَّهِ الْعِزَّةُ وَلِرَسُولِهِ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

“They say, ‘If we return to the city, the mightier will certainly drive out the more abased.’ Yet ʿizzah belongs to TheGod, His messenger, and the believers, but the hypocrites do not know.”

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:8

الْأَعَزُّ and الْأَذَلَّ first appear in the speech of people who regard themselves as superior and stronger. They invoke social power and the ability to expel another group.

The Qur’an then sets this claim against the reality of ʿizzah, attributing it to TheGod, His messenger, and the believers.

Claiming ʿizzah does not establish its possession. The verse also does not imply that every believer will always surpass others in wealth, government, or military strength. It identifies where ʿizzah belongs in response to a particular group’s claim; it does not declare everyone’s outward circumstances identical across time.

ʿIzzan: support sought from deities

Maryam says:

وَاتَّخَذُوا مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ آلِهَةً لِّيَكُونُوا لَهُمْ عِزًّا

“They took deities apart from TheGod so that they might be a source of strength and support for them.”

Maryam 19:81

ʿIzzan expresses what the worshippers expect from their deities. The family’s early sense allows an understanding in terms of support, backing, protection, and strength.

The following verse rejects that expectation: those deities will disown their worship and stand against them.

ʿIzzan therefore describes the worshippers’ desire and conception, rather than a Qur’anic affirmation that those deities really provide power and protection.

This has a semantic connection with the early poetry. A tribe can provide supporting strength in the poem; here worshippers seek comparable backing from deities. The settings differ while sharing the field of support and power.

ʿIzzah joined to pride and obstinacy

Al-Baqarah says:

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُ اتَّقِ اللَّهَ أَخَذَتْهُ الْعِزَّةُ بِالْإِثْمِ فَحَسْبُهُ جَهَنَّمُ وَلَبِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ

“When he is told, ‘Be mindful of TheGod,’ pride drives him into sin. Hell is sufficient for him—what an evil resting place!”

Al-Baqarah 2:206

الْعِزَّةُ is linked with بِالْإِثْمِ. “Pride drives him into sin” conveys that relationship: the person responds obstinately to the call to taqwā and persists in his conduct. The negative sense comes from the entire expression; ʿizzah alone does not mean sin or seeking honor.

ʿIzzah can thus occur in a critical setting when joined to arrogance and persistence in sin. It does not follow that the word always means pride or sin.

Ṣād also says:

بَلِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فِي عِزَّةٍ وَشِقَاقٍ

“Rather, those who disbelieve are in pride and opposition.”

Ṣād 38:2

ʿIzzah accompanies shiqāq here. Shiqāq expresses opposition and a divided stance; their combination places ʿizzah in the field of obstinacy and self-exaltation.

Rendering ʿizzah simply as commendable dignity would ignore the criticism. Conversely, defining it as arrogance throughout the Qur’an would ignore the family’s positive uses.

Its moral value is not settled by the root alone. Its relationship to a person’s conduct, circumstances, and the truth matters.

Gentleness and firmness toward others

A group of believers is described as:

أَذِلَّةٍ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ أَعِزَّةٍ عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ

“Humble and gentle toward the believers, firm and strong toward the disbelievers.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:54

أَعِزَّة is the plural of ʿazīz; it should not be confused with the singular comparative أَعَزّ.

The contrast between أَذِلَّة and أَعِزَّة describes two ways of responding. The same group is gentle and humble toward believers and firm and strong toward disbelievers.

Aʿizzah does not itself mean oppressive, cruel, or aggressive. The verse continues with striving in the way of TheGod and not fearing reproach, which help clarify the intended picture.

Adhillah should likewise not be translated as if believers were required to be degraded or worthless before one another. The paired descriptions contrast humility and gentleness with firmness and strength.

Political and social standing

In Al-Naml, the Queen of Sheba speaks about kings entering towns:

إِنَّ الْمُلُوكَ إِذَا دَخَلُوا قَرْيَةً أَفْسَدُوهَا وَجَعَلُوا أَعِزَّةَ أَهْلِهَا أَذِلَّةً وَكَذَٰلِكَ يَفْعَلُونَ

“When kings enter a town, they ruin it and reduce its powerful inhabitants to humiliation. That is what they do.”

Al-Naml 27:34

أَعِزَّةَ أَهْلِهَا denotes inhabitants with standing, influence, or power, contrasted with أَذِلَّة.

The statement is presented in the queen’s speech and concerns royal entry and the reversal of powerful people’s status. Aʿizzah therefore need not mean good people or those morally superior.

In the account of the owner of two gardens, one man says:

أَنَا أَكْثَرُ مِنكَ مَالًا وَأَعَزُّ نَفَرًا

“I have more wealth than you and am stronger in supporters.”

Al-Kahf 18:34

Aʿazzu is comparative and is qualified by نَفَرًا. The speaker relies on the number or strength of people who can back him.

This concerns social standing and collective strength rather than affection or moral worth.

In Hūd 11:91, Shuʿayb’s opponents say that they would stone him were it not for his kin, then add وَمَا أَنْتَ عَلَيْنَا بِعَزِيزٍ: “You have no power or protective standing with us.” In the next verse he asks أَرَهْطِي أَعَزُّ عَلَيْكُم مِّنَ اللَّهِ: “Are my kin more esteemed by you than TheGod?”

ʿIzzah here concerns social backing, respect, and protection from attack. These senses must not be generalized to every occurrence of ʿazīz regardless of context.

ʿAzīz describing people, a book, victory, and difficulty

ʿAzīz as an official’s title

Yūsuf says:

وَقَالَ نِسْوَةٌ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ امْرَأَتُ الْعَزِيزِ تُرَاوِدُ فَتَاهَا عَن نَّفْسِهِ

“Women in the city said, ‘The wife of the ʿAzīz is seeking to seduce her young servant.’”

Yūsuf 12:30

Al-ʿAzīz is a title for a man with rank and influence. His wife continues to be identified through امْرَأَتُ الْعَزِيزِ in the story.

امْرَأَتُ الْعَزِيزِ also occurs in Yūsuf 12:51. In 12:78 and 12:88, Joseph’s brothers address Joseph himself as يَا أَيُّهَا الْعَزِيزُ. The title thus occurs four times in the surah.

This shift matters: ʿAzīz is not simply one person’s name in the story but a title applicable to someone in a position of authority. Translation should preserve its function as a title rather than mistake it for a personal name.

The title alone does not establish that its bearer is righteous, just, or beloved by TheGod. The word conveys social standing and authority; moral judgment depends on conduct and the story’s context.

“Your suffering weighs heavily on him”

Al-Tawbah says:

لَقَدْ جَاءَكُمْ رَسُولٌ مِّنْ أَنفُسِكُمْ عَزِيزٌ عَلَيْهِ مَا عَنِتُّمْ حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُم بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ رَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ

“A messenger has come to you from among yourselves. Your suffering weighs heavily on him; he is deeply concerned for you, and toward the believers he is compassionate and merciful.”

Al-Tawbah 9:128

This is a particularly clear example of ʿazīz meaning something other than powerful or high-ranking.

عَزِيزٌ عَلَيْهِ concerns difficulty and burden. مَا عَنِتُّمْ refers to the hardship experienced by the addressees. Their suffering is hard and burdensome for the messenger of TheGod.

“He is dear to you” would reverse the relationship. The Arabic does not say that people esteem him; it says that what causes them suffering is hard for him to bear.

Nor does ʿazīzun ʿalayhi mean “powerful over you” here. The preposition, the sentence structure, and mā ʿanittum determine the appropriate meaning.

“That is not difficult for TheGod”

Ibrāhīm 14:20 says:

وَمَا ذَٰلِكَ عَلَى اللَّهِ بِعَزِيزٍ

“That is not difficult for TheGod.”

The same wording recurs in Fāṭir 35:17.

In these verses, ʿazīz itself means “difficult,” and مَا negates that difficulty. The bāʾ in بِعَزِيزٍ reinforces the negative construction; it is not part of the word’s lexical meaning. The sentence says that the act in question is not difficult for TheGod, rather than ʿazīz itself meaning “easy.”

Rendering ʿazīz as dear or beloved would change the sense. “This is not dear to TheGod” is not equivalent to “This is not difficult for TheGod.”

Comparison with Al-Tawbah 9:128 is instructive. Human suffering weighs heavily on the messenger in one passage, while an act is not difficult for TheGod in another. Both use this family, with different subjects, structures, and outcomes.

“A mighty book”: the book’s firmness

Fuṣṣilat says:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِالذِّكْرِ لَمَّا جَاءَهُمْ وَإِنَّهُ لَكِتَابٌ عَزِيزٌ

“Indeed, those who disbelieved in the Reminder when it came to them… And it is indeed a firm, unassailable book.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:41

The next verse says:

لَا يَأْتِيهِ الْبَاطِلُ مِن بَيْنِ يَدَيْهِ وَلَا مِنْ خَلْفِهِ تَنزِيلٌ مِّنْ حَكِيمٍ حَمِيدٍ

“Falsehood does not approach it from before it or from behind it—a sending down from One who is wise and praiseworthy.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:42

Describing the book as ʿazīz does not attribute bodily strength or social office to it. Its firmness and resistance to being overcome by falsehood are in view, supported by the following verse.

“A firm, unassailable book” or “a book that falsehood cannot overcome” therefore fits the context.

The single word ʿazīz does not establish every theory about the history of writing, manuscripts, or textual transmission. Those require separate evidence. The passage describes the book and the inability of falsehood to approach it.

“A mighty victory”

Al-Fatḥ 48:3 says:

وَيَنصُرَكَ اللَّهُ نَصْرًا عَزِيزًا

“And that TheGod may aid you with a mighty victory.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:3

ʿAzīzan qualifies naṣran. It describes the victory, rather than serving here as a title for TheGod or for the victorious person.

“A mighty victory,” “powerful support,” or, where appropriate, “a firm victory” can convey the expression. Although difficulty and rarity are recorded senses of this family in Arabic, they do not outweigh power and prevailing in this setting.

Even a form frequently used to describe TheGod need not be a divine description in every Qur’anic occurrence.

Al-ʿUzzā: a deity’s name in pre-Islamic Arabia

Al-Najm says:

أَفَرَأَيْتُمُ اللَّاتَ وَالْعُزَّىٰ

“Have you considered Al-Lāt and Al-ʿUzzā?”

Al-Najm 53:19

Al-ʿUzzā is the proper name of a deity known among groups of pre-Islamic Arabs. It accompanies Al-Lāt, followed by Manāt; the subsequent verses question beliefs concerning these deities.

Morphologically, عُزَّىٰ fits the feminine pattern corresponding to أَعَزّ, as فُضْلَىٰ corresponds to أَفْضَل. This analysis connects the name with power and ʿizzah. The verse itself does not explain the history of its naming or the stages through which the name developed.

The verse nevertheless uses a proper name. Translation should retain Al-ʿUzzā rather than replace it with “the strongest woman” or “the invincible deity.” Even if the name derives from a description of strength, that derivation does not affirm the description of the being worshipped.

A deity’s name may derive from a word for power, greatness, or superiority. The existence of the name does not prove the reality of the power attributed to it by worshippers.

Al-ʿUzzā is not an ordinary feminine adjective applied to any strong woman or being here. Its occurrence should be recorded, while its particular historical use remains distinct from ʿizzah and ʿazīz elsewhere.

Al-ʿAzīz and ʿizzah in descriptions of TheGod

Power that cannot be overcome

Al-ʿAzīz frequently describes TheGod in the Qur’an. Power, prevailing, and invincibility are central in this use.

Hūd says:

إِنَّ رَبَّكَ هُوَ الْقَوِيُّ الْعَزِيزُ

“Your Lord is indeed the Strong, the Invincible.”

Hūd 11:66

Al-Qawiyy and Al-ʿAzīz occur together. Qawiyy emphasizes strength and capability, while ʿazīz also conveys authority and not being overcome.

This is a semantic tendency, not a rule that one denotes only inward strength and the other only outward victory. The descriptions overlap in strength while highlighting it from different directions.

TheGod’s strength and might also occur together in Al-Ḥajj 22:40 and 22:74, Al-Shūrā 42:19, and Al-Mujādilah 58:21.

These descriptions should not be turned into a bodily image. Divine power is not presented as dependent on muscles, weapons, or human-like physical resistance. ʿAzīz describes authority and not being overcome, rather than a hard body or a position behind an impenetrable wall.

Al-ʿAzīz Al-Ḥakīm: might and wisdom

A familiar Qur’anic pairing is Al-ʿAzīz Al-Ḥakīm:

إِنَّكَ أَنتَ الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ

“You are indeed the Mighty, the Invincible, the Wise.”

Al-Baqarah 2:129

The pairing occurs in many verses. ʿAzīz expresses authority and not being overcome, while ḥakīm concerns wisdom and sound judgment and governance.

Their conjunction brings power and wisdom together without making them identical. ʿAzīz does not itself mean wise, nor does ḥakīm simply mean powerful.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:26, TheGod is addressed as Owner of sovereignty before the granting of honor and humiliation is mentioned. Elsewhere, Al-ʿAzīz Al-Ḥakīm occurs in contexts of creation, revelation, judgment, and governance. This range shows that ʿizzah is not confined to one type of divine action.

Al-ʿAzīz Al-Raḥīm: might alongside mercy

The following phrase recurs through Al-Shuʿarāʾ:

وَإِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَهُوَ الْعَزِيزُ الرَّحِيمُ

“Your Lord is indeed the Mighty, the Invincible, the Merciful.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:9

The wording is repeated after several accounts of messengers and peoples. These contexts involve rescue and support as well as confrontation with rejection and injustice.

The pairing places might and mercy together. It does not mean that ʿazīz is severity and raḥīm is softness, or that these always represent opposing forces.

ʿAzīz expresses power and invincibility, while raḥīm independently expresses mercy. The same TheGod is described through both without either canceling the other’s meaning.

Yā Sīn 36:5 also has تَنزِيلَ الْعَزِيزِ الرَّحِيمِ, connecting both descriptions with the sending down of revelation.

Al-ʿAzīz Al-Ghaffār and other pairings

Ṣād 38:66 says:

رَبُّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا الْعَزِيزُ الْغَفَّارُ

“Lord of the heavens and the earth and what lies between them—the Mighty, the Invincible, the Oft-Forgiving.”

Ṣād 38:66

Al-ʿAzīz accompanies Al-Ghaffār. Divine might and forgiveness are expressed in two adjoining descriptions; this does not turn the lexical meaning of ʿizzah into forgiveness.

In Al-Anʿām 6:96, الْعَزِيزِ الْعَلِيمِ connects might with knowledge and determination. In Ibrāhīm 14:1, الْعَزِيزِ الْحَمِيدِ joins it with praiseworthiness; in Ṣād 38:9, الْعَزِيزِ الْوَهَّابِ brings power and giving together.

عَزِيزٌ ذُو انتِقَامٍ also occurs, as in Āl ʿImrān 3:4 and Ibrāhīm 14:47. Dhū intiqām is an additional description alongside ʿazīz; ʿazīz itself does not mean one who exacts retribution.

Likewise, عَزِيزٌ غَفُورٌ in Fāṭir 35:28 joins might and forgiveness. Defining ʿazīz inherently as punishing would fail to explain its range across these pairings.

When describing TheGod, Al-ʿAzīz retains power and invincibility. Its combinations with wisdom, mercy, forgiveness, knowledge, and judgment show that it is not limited to a particular divine action.

Definite and indefinite forms

ʿAzīz occurs with the definite article, as in الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ, and without it, as in إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ.

The presence or absence of the article is not an absolute test of a divine description. In either construction, ʿazīz can describe TheGod.

Its frequent divine use also does not mean that every occurrence refers to TheGod. Officials in Yūsuf, the book in Fuṣṣilat, and عَزِيزٌ عَلَيْهِ in Al-Tawbah demonstrate otherwise.

The comparative aʿazz is also distinct from Al-ʿAzīz. Each has its own morphological form and function.

“The mighty, the noble”: an ironic address

Al-Dukhān says:

ذُقْ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ الْعَزِيزُ الْكَرِيمُ

“Taste! You are indeed the mighty, the noble!”

Al-Dukhān 44:49

The setting is punishment, giving the address a rebuking, mocking force. Someone who imagined himself possessed of might and honor is addressed with those very titles.

This need not be a wholly new lexical meaning of ʿazīz. The word still concerns might and standing, but the utterance mocks the person’s claim to them.

The example highlights the speaker and situation. Not every description quoted in the Qur’an is an endorsement, and the lexical meaning does not simply change according to the truth or falsehood of a speaker’s claim.

Swearing by the might of TheGod

In Ṣād, Iblīs says:

قَالَ فَبِعِزَّتِكَ لَأُغْوِيَنَّهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

“He said, ‘By Your might, I will surely lead them all astray.’”

Ṣād 38:82

بِعِزَّتِكَ refers to the might of TheGod, but the oath and claim belong to Iblīs. In the next verse, Ṣād 38:83, he himself gives an exception: إِلَّا عِبَادَكَ مِنْهُمُ الْمُخْلَصِينَ, “except Your servants among them who have been made pure.” The speech is thus neither an affirmation of unlimited power on Iblīs’s part nor even a claim without an exception.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44, the magicians say before facing Moses:

بِعِزَّةِ فِرْعَوْنَ إِنَّا لَنَحْنُ الْغَالِبُونَ

“By Pharaoh’s might, we will certainly be the victors.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44

ʿIzzah can thus be invoked in an oath. Pharaoh’s might and TheGod’s might are not presented as equal in standing or reality. Swearing by someone’s power does not establish the truth of the speaker’s claims about it.

The magicians regard victory as certain, but the story’s continuation defeats that expectation. ʿIzzah in their oath refers to Pharaoh’s power and authority, not his rightfulness.

Comparison with related roots

ʿ Z Z and Q W Y: ʿizzah and strength

Quwwah concerns force and ability. ʿIzzah is also connected with power but can emphasize a position in which someone is strong, dignified, or difficult to overcome.

This should not become an absolute rule that quwwah is merely physical strength and ʿizzah merely social prestige. Both Qur’anic families can concern power and capability in different domains.

الْقَوِيُّ الْعَزِيزُ shows their proximity, while the separate descriptions invite examination of their differing emphasis.

ʿ Z Z and Gh L B: ʿizzah and prevailing

Ghalaba denotes prevailing over another and can express the outcome of a confrontation. ʿIzzah more often concerns the power or position that enables such victory or prevents defeat.

Yet عَزَّنِي فِي الْخِطَابِ shows that the verb ʿazza itself can mean to prevail. The two families cannot be separated completely.

In one use, prevailing is an event; in another, ʿizzah describes a person’s strength or standing. Their precise distinction depends on the form and sentence.

ʿ Z Z and Dh L L: dignity and being brought low

ʿIzzah and dhillah are often contrasted. Granting honor and bringing low in Āl ʿImrān 3:26, and the mightier and more abased in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:8, are clear examples.

However, أَذِلَّةٍ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ in Al-Māʾidah 5:54 shows that Dh L L likewise requires context. Humility toward believers occurs alongside firmness toward those opposed to them.

Not every use of Dh L L is moral degradation, nor every use of ʿ Z Z a moral virtue. Evaluation depends on the form, accompanying words, and context.

ʿ Z Z and K R M: ʿazīz and karīm

Karīm concerns nobility, esteem, and generosity, while ʿazīz more often concerns power, standing, and not being overcome.

Both can describe one person without being complete synonyms. Someone can possess power and rank without generosity or nobility; another may be noble without extensive political or social power.

الْعَزِيزُ الْكَرِيمُ in Al-Dukhān 44:49 combines the titles. Its mocking context prevents the phrase from being understood as straightforward praise.

ʿ Z Z and M N ʿ: ʿazīz and preventing

Manaʿa means to prevent or hold back. In some uses, ʿazīz describes someone not easily overcome, with the strength and backing to resist attack.

The concepts meet in resistance and preventing domination, but one names an act of prevention while the other can describe strength or standing.

ʿAzīz does not always mean one who prevents. Difficulty is prominent in Al-Tawbah 9:128, while social standing is prominent in Joseph’s story.

Translation proposals

For ʿizz and ʿizzah, “honor,” “dignity,” “strength,” “might,” or “powerful support” may fit, depending on context. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:139 and Fāṭir 35:10, “might and honor” can convey the word’s broad range. In Maryam 19:81, “a source of strength and support” makes the worshippers’ expected relationship with their deities clearer.

In Al-Baqarah 2:206 and Ṣād 38:2, pride and obstinacy need to be visible in the translation because the passages join ʿizzah with sin and opposition.

عَزَّنِي in Ṣād 38:23 is appropriately “he overpowered me,” while عَزَّزْنَا in Yā Sīn 36:14 is “We reinforced.” تُعِزُّ in Āl ʿImrān 3:26 can be “You grant honor” or “You make strong and dignified.”

Describing TheGod, ʿazīz can be “the Mighty, the Invincible” or, where suitable, “mighty.” “Invincible” makes not being overcome explicit, but the word’s entire range should not always be confined to victory in a confrontation.

In Yūsuf 12:30, 12:51, 12:78, and 12:88, ʿAzīz is an official’s title and should retain that function. In Al-Tawbah 9:128, عَزِيزٌ عَلَيْهِ is “hard or burdensome for him”; in Ibrāhīm 14:20 and Fāṭir 35:17, بِعَزِيزٍ belongs to a negative construction meaning “not difficult.”

كِتَابٌ عَزِيزٌ in Fuṣṣilat 41:41 can be “a firm, unassailable book,” while نَصْرًا عَزِيزًا in Al-Fatḥ 48:3 can be “a mighty victory.”

For aʿazz, “mightier,” “more honored,” or “more esteemed” may fit a comparison of strength or standing. Aʿizzah is the plural of ʿazīz and may describe powerful people, people of high standing, or those who stand firm.

الْعُزَّىٰ in Al-Najm 53:19 should remain the proper name Al-ʿUzzā. Its possible derivation belongs in linguistic explanation rather than replacing the name in translation.

Conclusion

In Qur’anic usage, ʿ Z Z concerns power, firmness, dignity, and resistance to being overcome; in certain constructions it expresses difficulty. The poetry of Ṭarafah and Al-Mutalammis attests tribal support and bodily firmness. Dictionaries record rarity, but this review did not find independent pre-Qur’anic evidence for that sense.

The family can describe power that overcomes another, a position in which someone is not easily humiliated, or something difficult to attain or bear. ʿAzīz therefore has no single equivalent across all verses. An official’s title in Joseph’s story, the burden of people’s suffering on the messenger of TheGod, the firmness of the book, and TheGod’s invincibility all belong to its actual range.

ʿIzzah does not inherently supply moral praise or blame. It can accompany power and dignity or occur with obstinacy, arrogance, and persistence in sin. Conduct, the direction of power, and the whole verse determine the difference.

The defensible semantic core concerns hardness and strength associated with firmness, prevailing, or being difficult to overcome. Social standing, esteem, and difficulty remain distinguishable in the family’s different constructions. Their relationship does not justify asserting a definite historical sequence for all of them or inserting rarity into every Qur’anic occurrence.

Understanding the Qur’an requires distinguishing true ʿizzah, claims to it, social power, a difficult undertaking, and the divine description Al-ʿAzīz. Each occurrence must be read through its form, syntax, and context, rather than one preselected meaning imposed on the entire family.
