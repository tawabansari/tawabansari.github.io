Foot movement, striking with the foot, running, and hastening

Brief meaning: Moving the foot or striking with it; swift movement, especially running. Extended uses describe rapid animal movement, wingbeats, and sudden motion or stirring.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root R K Ḍ concerns foot movement, striking, and rapid motion. A familiar use describes a rider striking a mount’s flank with the foot to start it moving or urge it faster.

رَكَضَ الرَّجُلُ دَابَّتَهُ means that someone strikes a mount with the foot to make it move or hasten. The same family describes human running and swift animal movement.

Extended usage includes رَكَضَ الفَرَسُ for a horse moving swiftly and رَكَضَ الطَّائِرُ for a bird’s rapid movement or wingbeats.

The Qur’an uses the root in two settings: a command to move or strike with the foot in Job’s story, and people hastening away when they perceive punishment in Al-Anbiyāʾ.

Not every occurrence can therefore be translated as running. Each context determines whether foot movement, striking, hastening, or fleeing is intended.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

R K Ḍ occurs three times in three verses:

  • يَرْكُضُونَ: Third-person plural imperfect, “they move swiftly” or “they run”; Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12.
  • تَرْكُضُوا: Second-person plural imperfect, made jussive by prohibitive lā; Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13.
  • ارْكُضْ: Second-person singular imperative, “strike with your foot” or “move your foot”; Ṣād 38:42.

All three belong to the basic verb رَكَضَ يَرْكُضُ.

Other recorded forms include رَكْض, رَكْضَة, رَاكِض, رَكَّاض, رَكُوض, مَرْكَض, مِرْكَض, مُرْكِضَة, ارْتِكَاض, مُرَاكَضَة, and تَرَاكُض.

These help establish the root’s semantic range but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Foot movement and striking

One early lexical analysis identifies a core of forward movement or setting something in motion.

It explains the familiar ركض الرجل دابته through a rider striking an animal to urge it onward.

The rider strikes the mount’s flank with the foot, and the animal responds by moving or accelerating.

Two actions should be distinguished: the movement and strike of the rider’s foot, and the responding animal’s movement.

Early scholars debated whether rakḍ originally concerned the rider’s foot or independently described the animal’s own movement.

One view emphasizes the rider urging the mount, while another cites Arabic examples applying the verb directly to animals or birds moving.

Even if foot-striking is a basic use, the animal-movement uses cannot therefore be excluded from Arabic.

Striking the ground or objects

Lexical records give رَكَضَ الأَرْضَ for striking the ground with the foot.

رَكَضَ الثَّوْبَ is also recorded for striking or moving cloth with the foot.

The person need not be running in such uses.

Someone may stand still while moving a foot or striking something with it.

This matters for Ṣād 38:42: ارْكُضْ بِرِجْلِكَ does not mean running along a lengthy route.

There the verb concerns foot movement and striking.

Rakḍ and rapid motion

Another use of رَكَضَ الرَّجُلُ describes running or moving swiftly.

An early lexical explanation also describes rakḍ as a person’s movement using both feet.

Linguistic evidence associates this movement with speed in many settings.

Depending on context, someone who يركض may be running or moving quickly away from a place.

The root itself does not determine direction.

A person might run toward a destination, away from danger, or in a race.

Flight may therefore be an instance of rakḍ, but not every rakḍ is flight.

A verse by Salāmah ibn Jandal

A poem by the pre-Islamic poet Salāmah ibn Jandal contains:

وَلَّى حَثِيثًا وَهَذَا الشَّيْبُ يَطْلُبُهُ
لَوْ كَانَ يُدْرِكُهُ رَكْضُ الْيَعَاقِيبِ

wallā ḥathīthan wa-hādhā al-shaybu yaṭlubuhu / law kāna yudrikuhu rakḍu al-yaʿāqībi

Translation: “Youth sped away, and this gray age pursues it—if the swift motion of the yaʿāqīb could ever catch it.”

The poet pictures departing youth and approaching old age through pursuit and rapid movement.

Rakḍ here expresses swift motion.

Yaʿāqīb has two explanations in lexical accounts: male birds of a partridge-like kind, or swift horses.

Both fit the image of speed, but R K Ḍ alone cannot identify the exact referent.

The verse illustrates swift movement in poetry attributed to a pre-Islamic poet; attribution alone does not independently establish the exact date of composition.

Speed serves a literary image of pursuit or catching up, without rakḍ itself meaning pursuit.

A verse by al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

Poetry by al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī contains:

وَالرَّاكِضَاتِ ذُيُولَ الرَّيْطِ فَنَّقَهَا
بَرْدُ الْهَوَاجِرِ كَالْغِزْلَانِ بِالْجَرَدِ

wa-al-rākiḍāti dhuyūla al-rayṭi fannaqahā / bardu al-hawājiri ka-al-ghizlāni bi-al-jaradi

Approximate translation: “And the women whose steps move the hems of their long garments, pampered by cool repose in the midday heat, like gazelles on bare ground.”

الراكضات here concerns the women’s movement and their long garments.

Early explanations connect it with garment hems trailing or being moved as the women walk.

Running or flight is not necessarily intended: foot movement and the movement of cloth are prominent.

The example shows that this root is not simply a term for great speed, even in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Some wording varies across versions, but الراكضات is recorded in recognized transmissions.

Birds in motion

Early Arabic records ركض الطائر for a bird moving rapidly and sometimes moving its wings.

An early lexical account records يركض for a bird in flight.

A verse attributed to Ruʾbah describes crows in motion using this root; the transmitted phrase is ركض غربان. The birds are crows, not vultures.

The family was therefore not confined to foot movement.

A flying bird does not run along the ground like a person, yet its swift movement or successive wingbeats can receive the same description.

This does not make flying the root’s primary meaning.

The use concerns movement of the propelling limb during flight.

Fetal movement and irtikāḍ

Arabic also records irtikāḍ for motion and stirring.

One example, ارْتِكَاضُ الصَّبِيِّ, describes fetal movement in the mother’s womb.

أَرْكَضَتِ النَّاقَةُ likewise describes a she-camel whose fetus has begun moving within her.

Running is not intended: the fetus moves or stirs in the womb.

The semantic field therefore includes movements resembling strikes and perceptible stirring.

The word alone cannot establish developmental stage, health, or the exact quality of movement.

Rakḍ, propulsion, and tools

رَكْضَة can describe a single strike or jolt.

قَوْسٌ رَكُوضٌ is recorded for a bow that forcefully propels an arrow.

The image here extends from foot-striking to force setting something in motion.

مِرْكَض is also recorded as an implement for stirring a fire.

These uses illustrate the broad connection between motion, striking, and causing movement.

They should not be reduced to one physical action. Foot-striking, wingbeats, fetal movement, and an arrow propelled from a bow are different phenomena expressed through related forms.

3. يَرْكُضُونَ: fleeing swiftly upon perceiving punishment

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12

Verse text:

فَلَمَّا أَحَسُّوا بَأْسَنَا إِذَا هُمْ مِنْهَا يَرْكُضُونَ

fa-lammā aḥassū baʾsanā idhā hum minhā yarkuḍūna

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12

Translation:

“When they perceived Our punishment, suddenly they fled swiftly from there.”

The meaning of يَرْكُضُونَ

يَرْكُضُونَ is a third-person masculine plural imperfect verb.

It expresses swift movement and running.

Here the context establishes more than speed: they move away from danger they have perceived.

“Fleeing swiftly” is therefore suitable.

Lexical and contextual meaning should remain distinct:

يركضون itself expresses rapid movement.

فَلَمَّا أَحَسُّوا بَأْسَنَا and مِنْهَا establish that they move away from the place when they perceive punishment.

Flight results from the verb’s connection with the situation, not from R K Ḍ invariably meaning flight.

مِنْهَا: where are they fleeing from?

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11 speaks of destroying a wrongdoing settlement and bringing forth other people after it.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12, the pronoun in مِنْهَا refers back to that settlement.

The sentence thus describes people hastening away from their home upon perceiving punishment.

يركضون alone does not identify the movement’s starting point or destination.

مِنْهَا specifies its origin here.

The destination of flight is not named.

Without evidence, it should not be specified as mountains, desert, another city, or any particular place.

إِذَا: the sudden reaction

إِذَا in إِذَا هُمْ مِنْهَا يَرْكُضُونَ highlights sudden occurrence.

The people are pictured running and fleeing as soon as they perceive punishment.

Their haste is connected with danger.

The verse does not specify their speed or how long they ran.

“Suddenly they fled swiftly” naturally conveys the sentence, but details such as distance or numbers lack support in the verse itself.

Conduct and consequence

The settlement in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11 is described as ظالمة, wrongdoing.

The account therefore concerns more than an accidental loss of a city or a natural event: the community’s wrongdoing forms part of its context.

When they perceive punishment, they hasten away instead of remaining where they were.

The image shows how quickly circumstances can change a human situation.

A community previously living in security and comfort tries to escape when danger appears.

This conclusion should not become the lexical definition of rakḍ.

The root describes movement and haste; the community’s wrongdoing and the consequences of conduct are identified elsewhere in the verses.

Nor does this verse establish that everyone fleeing danger is a wrongdoer.

Fleeing may be natural and entirely justified. Condemnation of this group concerns its conduct and context, not running itself.

4. لَا تَرْكُضُوا: confronting those fleeing with their deeds

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13

Verse text:

لَا تَرْكُضُوا وَارْجِعُوا إِلَىٰ مَا أُتْرِفْتُمْ فِيهِ وَمَسَاكِنِكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُسْأَلُونَ

lā tarkuḍū wa-irjiʿū ilā mā utriftum fīhi wa-masākinikum laʿallakum tusʾalūna

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13

Translation:

“Do not flee; return to the luxury you enjoyed and to your dwellings, so that you may be questioned.”

The construction لَا تَرْكُضُوا

تَرْكُضُوا is a second-person plural imperfect verb.

Prohibitive lā makes it jussive. Its indicative form is تَرْكُضُونَ; the final nūn is dropped in the jussive.

The preceding verse used the third person:

يَرْكُضُونَ — “they run.”

Here the address becomes direct:

لَا تَرْكُضُوا — “do not run” or “do not flee.”

The change matters: the people just described hastening away upon perceiving punishment now face words prohibiting continued flight.

“Do not flee” suits the context better than “do not run”: ordinary running is not at issue, but escape from the situation confronting them.

وَارْجِعُوا: returning instead of fleeing

The prohibition is followed by:

وَارْجِعُوا

wa-irjiʿū

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13

That is, “And return.”

The verbs point in different directions.

تركضوا describes hurrying away.

ارجعوا concerns returning to what they left behind.

R K Ḍ itself does not mean abandoning truth, denying, or shirking responsibility.

These can be examined in broader analysis of the scene; the verb directly describes swift movement.

The continuation identifies the destination of return: their former luxury and their dwellings.

Returning to a life of comfort

The verse says:

إِلَىٰ مَا أُتْرِفْتُمْ فِيهِ وَمَسَاكِنِكُمْ

ilā mā utriftum fīhi wa-masākinikum

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13

أُتْرِفْتُمْ belongs to T R F and concerns affluence and luxurious living.

People fleeing punishment are recalled to that former comfort and their homes.

This can be read as rebuke and irony.

They once enjoyed resources, dwellings, and ease; now they leave that same setting in haste.

The sentence then speaks of being questioned:

لَعَلَّكُمْ تُسْأَلُونَ

laʿallakum tusʾalūna

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13

That is, “So that you may be questioned.”

This relates to people whose former resources no longer let them escape their deeds’ consequences. Classical readings also mention questions about their conduct or requests for assistance or counsel. The phrase itself does not specify the subject of the questioning.

The verse does not name the speaker, so a particular person or angel should not be identified without evidence.

Its rebuking role within the destruction of the wrongdoing community is clear.

The people’s response

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:14, they acknowledge their wrongdoing.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15 describes their cry continuing until their destruction.

The connected account shows that rapid flight did not save them from the consequences of their condition.

That outcome belongs to this account.

It should not become a lexical rule that rakḍ always means futile movement or inevitable failure.

People can escape danger by running. Here flight’s failure is established by the punishment and the community’s fate.

Rakḍ and acknowledgment of error

The response in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:14 includes acknowledgment of wrongdoing.

Acknowledgment when consequences appear does not by itself resolve reform, repairing harm, or actually turning from error.

Rakḍ itself means neither denial of error nor repentance.

Running and fleeing are bodily conduct; acknowledgment and fate are expressed through other sentences.

The verses’ entire ethical message should therefore not be compressed into a single verb.

5. ارْكُضْ بِرِجْلِكَ: foot movement in Job’s story

Ṣād 38:41

Verse text:

وَاذْكُرْ عَبْدَنَا أَيُّوبَ إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ أَنِّي مَسَّنِيَ الشَّيْطَانُ بِنُصْبٍ وَعَذَابٍ

wa-udhkur ʿabdanā ayyūba idh nādā rabbahu annī massaniya al-shayṭānu bi-nuṣbin wa-ʿadhābin

Ṣād 38:41

Translation:

“Remember Our servant Job, when he called to his Lord: Satan has touched me with hardship and suffering.”

Ṣād 38:42

Verse text:

ارْكُضْ بِرِجْلِكَ ۖ هَٰذَا مُغْتَسَلٌ بَارِدٌ وَشَرَابٌ

urkuḍ bi-rijlika hādhā mughtasalun bāridun wa-sharābun

Ṣād 38:42

Translation:

“Strike the ground with your foot; here is a cool washing place and a drink.”

The meaning of ارْكُضْ

ارْكُضْ is the singular imperative of رَكَضَ يَرْكُضُ.

It appears here with بِرِجْلِكَ.

This specifies that the action is performed with the foot.

Arabic records رَكَضَ الأَرْضَ بِرِجْلِهِ for striking the ground with one’s foot.

ارْكُضْ بِرِجْلِكَ therefore concerns moving or striking with the foot here.

“Run” does not suit this context. The decisive evidence is the accompanying foot expression and the command’s setting, rather than a requirement that every expression of running name a destination.

“Strike the ground with your foot” conveys the command more aptly.

الأرض, “the ground,” is not actually present in the Arabic verse. It is the naturally understood object supplied in this rendering, not a separately expressed word.

Lexical analysis must distinguish the verse’s wording from contextual explanation.

Does rakḍ here mean a severe blow?

Some lexical explanations describe rakḍ through thrusting or striking with the foot.

The verb does not determine the strike’s precise force.

The verse specifies neither how many times Job struck nor which foot he used nor the force involved.

Some exegetical accounts mention one strike, others two.

These details are absent from the Qur’anic wording.

Foot movement and striking suffice for lexical study; physical details should not replace the word’s meaning.

Washing and drinking after the command

The verse continues:

هَٰذَا مُغْتَسَلٌ بَارِدٌ وَشَرَابٌ

hādhā mughtasalun bāridun wa-sharābun

Ṣād 38:42

This introduces something associated with washing and drinking.

مُغْتَسَل can describe a washing place or something used for washing.

بارد describes its coolness; شراب concerns a drink.

The context therefore points to water suitable for washing and drinking.

Neither mughtasal nor sharāb belongs to R K Ḍ.

Rakḍ itself should not be made to mean bringing forth water, healing, or drinking.

These meanings emerge from the following sentence and its connection with Job’s story.

Does the verse say a spring emerged from the ground?

Exegetical accounts describe water emerging after Job struck with his foot.

Some mention one spring used for washing and drinking.

Others mention two, one for washing and another for drinking.

Some even identify geographical locations and distances between springs.

Ṣād 38:42 specifies neither the number of springs nor their location.

It commands foot movement and then introduces cool washing water or a washing place, and a drink.

The sequence links the command with provision for washing and drinking, without establishing every detail in exegetical narratives.

Accounts of one or two springs should therefore not become the meaning of ارْكُضْ in a root study.

Is rakḍ a treatment method?

In Job’s story, the command follows his appeal to his Lord.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:83 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:84 likewise describe Job’s suffering, appeal for mercy, and removal of harm.

Together these verses connect the story with relief and divine mercy.

ارْكُضْ does not establish a general treatment method.

For example, the verse does not instruct every sick person to strike the ground with a foot to recover.

Nor does it establish a particular diagnosis for Job’s illness or a biological mechanism of healing.

Rakḍ names a bodily movement here. Its result belongs to the story and its divine command.

Rakḍ and ḍarb within the same story

Ṣād 38:44 contains another verb:

فَاضْرِبْ بِهِ

fa-iḍrib bihi

Ṣād 38:44

This belongs to Ḍ R B.

Ḍarb has a broad field of striking and affecting, whereas rakḍ in Ṣād 38:42 specifically concerns movement and striking with the foot.

Both can concern striking, but they are not identical.

The first command expressly names the performing limb as rijl, the foot.

The second specifies an instrument through bihi, “with it.”

The Qur’an thus uses different roots for related actions within one story.

Rakḍ should not be equated with every use of ḍarb without considering limb, object, and context.

6. Related roots and translation proposals

R K Ḍ (rakḍ) and R K L (rakl)

These roots overlap in striking with the foot.

رَكَلَ is used for kicking or striking with a foot.

رَكَضَ can also describe foot-striking, particularly a rider urging a mount.

Other uses concern swift movement and running.

Rakl and rakḍ are therefore not synonymous in every use.

Ṣād 38:42 concerns a foot-strike; Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12 concerns running and hurried flight.

R K Ḍ (rakḍ) and S ʿ Y (saʿy)

Qur’anic saʿy has a broad range, from movement and exertion to striving toward a goal.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:20 describes a man coming in haste from the city’s farthest part.

يسعى concerns rapid movement in that scene.

Rakḍ can likewise describe swift movement.

Saʿy, however, often concerns human effort and action even where bodily running is not central.

Not every saʿy can therefore be translated as running, nor every rakḍ as striving for a goal.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12 highlights swift bodily movement; Ṣād 38:42, foot movement.

R K Ḍ (rakḍ) and F R R (firār)

Firār concerns fleeing and moving away from something.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13, rakḍ appears in flight from punishment.

Fleeing is consequently a suitable translation there.

A semantic distinction nevertheless remains.

Firār emphasizes escape and withdrawal; rakḍ’s main field concerns foot movement and haste.

Someone can flee by running, but also by vehicle or another means.

Conversely, not everyone running is fleeing.

Flight in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12 is therefore established by context and direction.

R K Ḍ (rakḍ) and R K B (rukūb)

The familiar meaning of rukūb is mounting or riding an animal.

Rakḍ can describe foot movement urging that animal onward, or rapid movement by people and animals.

A person may mount a horse and then urge it faster with a foot.

Mounting and rakḍ are related but distinct actions in that scene.

Sharing two consonants does not make all the roots’ meanings identical.

R K Ḍ (rakḍ) and R K D (rukūd)

Rukūd concerns settling and stopping movement.

Rakḍ often concerns motion and haste.

Unlike the stationary ships of Al-Shūrā 42:33, people described by يركضون in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12 are moving swiftly.

This difference between particular Qur’anic uses should not become an absolute opposition between every derivative.

Rakḍ in Ṣād 38:42 may be a single foot-strike, without extended movement along a route.

Rukūd likewise concerns stopping a particular movement, not the absolute absence of all motion.

Translation proposals for Forqan

يَرْكُضُونَ — Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12

Suitable equivalents include “they run swiftly,” “they flee swiftly,” and “they escape in haste.”

In the complete sentence, “suddenly they fled swiftly from there” is natural and precise.

تَرْكُضُوا — Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13

With prohibitive lā, suitable equivalents include “do not run” and “do not flee.” The prohibition comes from lā; تَرْكُضُوا alone does not mean “do not flee.”

Given the connection between the verses, “do not flee” is preferable.

It takes the direction from context without claiming that the root always means flight.

ارْكُضْ — Ṣād 38:42

Suitable equivalents include “strike with your foot,” “move your foot,” or, for the full expression, “strike the ground with your foot.”

The last conveys the command clearly and naturally in Job’s story.

“Run” does not suit this setting, since movement along a route is not the action described.

Suggested word-box wording for Forqan

Root: R K Ḍ

Brief root meaning: Foot movement and striking with it; swift movement, running, and hastening.

Word: يَرْكُضُونَ — Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12

Meaning here: They flee swiftly, moving away from the settlement upon perceiving punishment.

Word: تَرْكُضُوا — Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13

Meaning here: With prohibitive lā, “do not flee”: a prohibition on continued hurried flight, recalling them to their homes and former life.

Word: ارْكُضْ — Ṣād 38:42

Meaning here: Strike with your foot; the command to Job following his appeal to his Lord.

The brief root summary should identify the common field of movement and striking, while each verse meaning follows its context. These proposals do not replace existing authored explanations in verse boxes.

7. Conclusions and final definition

Arabic R K Ḍ concerns foot movement, striking, and swift motion.

In early usage, a rider strikes a mount with the foot to urge it onward. The family also describes human running and rapid animal movement.

Other branches express wingbeats, fetal stirring, and sudden motion or striking.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period illustrates rapid movement as well as feet moving garment hems. The entire family cannot therefore be restricted to flight or fast running.

The Qur’an uses the root three times.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12, يَرْكُضُونَ describes people fleeing swiftly from their settlement upon perceiving punishment.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13, تَرْكُضُوا appears in a prohibition on continuing that flight.

In Ṣād 38:42, ارْكُضْ commands Job to move or strike with his foot.

The three share a field of movement but differ in contextual meaning.

Final definition of R K Ḍ:

Its principal field is foot movement, striking or setting something in motion with the foot, and rapid movement.

Extended uses include swift animal movement, wingbeats, and perceptible stirring or jolting.

Rakḍ may mean running in one context, fleeing swiftly in another, or striking with the foot in ارْكُضْ بِرِجْلِكَ.

Flight, healing, rescue, and movement toward a specified destination are not invariant meanings of the root. They emerge from the sentence and situation.

The two Qur’anic scenes must remain distinct: in Al-Anbiyāʾ, people hasten from punishment; in Ṣād, Job receives a command concerning his foot.

Preserving that distinction clarifies the language and prevents one story’s outcome or one derivative’s feature from being imposed on the whole lexical family.
