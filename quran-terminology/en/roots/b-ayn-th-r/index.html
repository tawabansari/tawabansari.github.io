Qur’anic Root B ʿ TH R

Turning over, disrupting an existing arrangement, and bringing out what has remained hidden or covered

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(baʿthara / yubaʿthiru)
Basic quadriliteral verb; active forms not Qur’anic

بَعْثَرَة
(baʿtharah)

(buʿthirat)

In Arabic, the root ب ع ث ر concerns turning something over, disrupting the position and arrangement of its parts, and scattering or bringing out what lies within it. The action may involve soil and land, belongings and objects, or something beneath a covering that becomes exposed when the covering is disturbed.

This family conveys more than moving a single object. Baʿthara often describes a situation in which something’s previous arrangement is disrupted: its parts are overturned or scattered, or what lay inside or underneath becomes exposed and is brought out.

Recorded uses include turning soil over, jumbling belongings, and demolishing a basin so that its lower part comes to the top. These examples show how scattering, displacement, and exposure of covered parts may result from one action. All these consequences need not occur together in every use.

The root appears only twice in the Qur’an, both times in connection with graves: بُعْثِرَتْ in Al-Infiṭār 82:4 and بُعْثِرَ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the feminine passive form in Al-Infiṭār 82:4

Both passages concern an event within the horizon of resurrection, but there is an important difference. In the first, the graves themselves are turned over; in the second, what is inside the graves is raised and brought out.

This difference shows why the root should not be restricted either to “being scattered” or to “the dead coming to life.” Resurrection is the two passages’ Qur’anic setting, while the verb itself conveys the disruption of a covered condition and the emergence of what lay underneath or within.

1. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Kitāb al-ʿAyn gives the following example for بَعْثَرَ:

«بَعْثَرَهُ بَعْثَرَةً إِذَا قَلَبَ التُّرَابَ عَنْهُ»

That is, he turned the soil over and removed it from above something.

Al-ʿAyn and other classical dictionaries: overturning and bringing out

This explanation supplies a tangible image of the action. Something lies under soil, and disturbing and moving the soil removes its covering. What stands out is more than scattering earth: it is overturning a covering that had kept something underneath.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah transmits uses from al-Farrāʾ and al-Zajjāj that clarify the same semantic field. With belongings and goods, بَعْثَرُوا مَتَاعَهُمْ describes turning objects over, heaping some upon others, and disrupting their arrangement.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records these expressions:

بَعْثَرَ الشَّيْءَ: he scattered and dispersed something.

بَعْثَرَ التُّرَابَ وَالْمَتَاعَ: he turned over soil and belongings.

بَعْثَرْتُ حَوْضِي: I demolished my basin and brought its lower part to the top.

The last example matters because baʿtharah here means more than moving a few objects. A previously established structure comes apart and the positions of its parts change.

Al-Muḥīṭ fī al-Lughah records similar uses for turning land over and jumbling belongings. Some lexical accounts also use بَعْثَرَ الرَّجُلُ for searching and inspecting, a use intelligibly connected with turning things over to find what lies among or beneath them.

Three semantic directions can be distinguished from these witnesses.

First, disrupting an existing arrangement, as when belongings are turned over or a structure demolished, leaving its parts displaced.

Second, scattering and dispersing, when parts leave their ordered positions after being overturned and spread in different directions.

Third, exposing and bringing out what is covered, as when soil is removed from something beneath it.

These directions are related but not fully synonymous. Something can be scattered without having been hidden; something can also be brought out from beneath soil without its parts being dispersed in different directions.

A definition should therefore encompass all three possibilities while deriving each sentence’s particular meaning from the object and circumstances of the action.

Lexical accounts also record ذَهَبَ الْقَوْمُ بَعْثَرَى for a group of people dispersing. This shows that the word family is not confined to soil and belongings: it can also express a group’s dispersal and the loss of its previous arrangement.

Al-Azharī, Tahdhīb al-Lughah: a group’s dispersal and baʿtharā

These recorded forms should not all be treated as Qur’anic forms. Only two passive forms of بَعْثَرَ occur in the Qur’an.

Nor does a dictionary’s recording of a use establish the precise date of its circulation in the pre-Islamic period. This study has not established a pre-Qur’anic poetic witness for baʿthara in this sense with reliable wording and attribution. No invented example should therefore be assigned to a pre-Islamic poet, and no expression’s antiquity should be claimed beyond the evidence.

2. The quadriliteral structure and the question of derivation

In Arabic morphology, ب ع ث ر is a four-consonant root. Its basic verb is بَعْثَرَ, its imperfect يُبَعْثِرُ, and its established verbal noun بَعْثَرَة.

The Qur’anic passive forms بُعْثِرَ and بُعْثِرَتْ are formed from this quadriliteral verb.

Its difference from ب ع ث is more than the apparent addition of a letter that would allow the two to be treated as one root without historical investigation.

In Al-Mufradāt, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains baʿthara as follows:

«أَيْ قُلِبَ تُرَابُهَا وَأُثِيرَ مَا فِيهَا»

That is, the graves’ soil was turned over and what was within them was stirred up.

He then considers the possibility that بَعْثَرَ arose from combining بُعِثَ and أُثِيرَ. In Arabic linguistic analysis, this process is understood as forming a word by combining and contracting several words.

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: the proposed contraction of baʿth and uthīra

The proposal is semantically intelligible: baʿth concerns raising and setting in motion, while uthīra refers to something being stirred up or overturned.

Yet similarity of meaning between two words does not, by itself, establish the historical derivation of one from the other.

A definitive derivation requires independent historical and linguistic evidence. Al-Rāghib’s analysis should therefore be treated as a possibility for investigation, rather than a fixed and established principle of the root.

Some lexicographers also report بَعْثَرَ and بَحْثَرَ as closely related Arabic forms. Sound substitution has been suggested in explaining their relationship. Reports of readings also transmit بُحْثِرَ; the Qur’anic text examined here, however, has بُعْثِرَتْ in Al-Infiṭār 82:4 and بُعْثِرَ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9.

Al-Farrāʾ, Maʿānī al-Qurʾān: the reported baḥthara form

These related forms show the range of linguistic usage, but the Qur’anic quadriliteral root should not automatically be equated with ب ح ث or ب ع ث.

For understanding the Qur’an, knowing the verb’s actual meaning in its quadriliteral structure and the two verses’ contexts matters more than establishing a possible derivation.

3. First use: the graves being turned over

Al-Infiṭār 82:4

Verse text:

وَإِذَا الْقُبُورُ بُعْثِرَتْ

wa-idhā al-qubūru buʿthirat

Translation:

And when the graves are turned over.

Al-Infiṭār 82:4

This verse stands at the opening of Al-Infiṭār among a series of momentous events. Al-Infiṭār 82:1 speaks of the sky splitting apart, Al-Infiṭār 82:2 of the stars scattering, and Al-Infiṭār 82:3 of the seas being opened and their waters joining. Al-Infiṭār 82:4 then describes the graves being turned over.

Within this sequence, familiar structures of the world are transformed. What had seemed stable and established loses its previous condition.

In ordinary human experience, a grave is where a dead body lies beneath soil. The soil covers what is inside, separating the exterior from the burial place.

When the Qur’an says الْقُبُورُ بُعْثِرَتْ, its linguistic image means more than soil moving or scattering. The graves themselves undergo a disruption of their previous arrangement, through which what lies under their covering can become exposed.

Explaining this verse, al-Zajjāj describes the graves’ soil being overturned and the dead within them being raised. This explanation accords with the root’s semantic field and the resurrection context.

Al-Wāḥidī, Al-Basīṭ: transmission of al-Zajjāj’s explanation

There is nevertheless a difference between the verb’s direct meaning and the event’s result.

Buʿthirat does not, by itself, name human beings coming to life again. Arabic uses the same verb for soil, belongings, and basins, things for which coming to life is not at issue.

What connects the overturning of graves with resurrection here is the subject of graves and the wider passages concerning human return after death.

Translating the verse only as “the dead came to life” removes the image of graves being overturned. Conversely, restricting it to “the graves’ soil scattered” weakens its clear connection with the dead emerging and its resurrection setting.

A direct translation should preserve “the graves being turned over,” leaving its connection with the dead emerging to the explanation.

Connection with the next verse

Al-Infiṭār 82:5

عَلِمَتْ نَفْسٌ مَا قَدَّمَتْ وَأَخَّرَتْ

ʿalimat nafsun mā qaddamat wa-akhkharat

Translation:

Every person will know what they sent ahead and what they left behind.

Al-Infiṭār 82:5 connects the outcome of the opening events with human knowledge of one’s own conduct.

The world’s transformation and the overturning of graves lead to a scene in which human beings confront what they have done or left behind.

This connection matters, but it must not change the meaning of buʿthirat into “deeds becoming manifest.” In Al-Infiṭār 82:4 the graves are the verb’s subject, while Al-Infiṭār 82:5 concerns a person’s knowledge of their conduct.

The connection is between resurrection and human responsibility and knowledge of the past, rather than identity of meaning between بُعْثِرَتْ and عَلِمَتْ.

Nor can the juxtaposition of the opening events establish their precise chronological sequence without further evidence. Repeated إِذَا places several events within one great scene, but each conjunction does not necessarily specify their moment-by-moment order.

4. Second use: bringing out what is in the graves

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9

Verse text:

أَفَلَا يَعْلَمُ إِذَا بُعْثِرَ مَا فِي الْقُبُورِ

a-fa-lā yaʿlamu idhā buʿthira mā fī al-qubūri

Translation:

Does he not know, when what is in the graves is turned over and brought out?

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9

Here, the verb is بُعْثِرَ, but its subject differs from that in Al-Infiṭār.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the masculine passive form in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9

In Al-Infiṭār 82:4, the graves, الْقُبُورُ, are themselves turned over. Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9 uses مَا فِي الْقُبُورِ: what is inside the graves.

This difference matters lexically and grammatically.

The first sentence focuses on the graves and the transformation of their condition; the second focuses directly on their contents being brought out.

مَا is a relative pronoun here, referring to what lies within the graves. In the resurrection context, the dead are the principal subject. Merely using mā rather than man should not therefore be taken as evidence that the verse is not about human beings.

Nor should the breadth of mā be taken, without contextual grounds, to mean that the verse intends to enumerate every material, object, and possession in the soil. Human destiny and accountability are central to the context.

Baʿthara here means more than disturbing soil: what lay in a covered place is raised and brought out.

“Is brought out” is consequently more suitable here than a rendering conveying only dispersal. The ideas of overturning and disruption of the previous condition should nevertheless remain in the explanation of the root.

Why does the verse use baʿthara?

Elsewhere, the Qur’an uses different expressions for the dead emerging from graves.

Al-Ḥajj 22:7 explicitly speaks of raising those in the graves. Yā Sīn 36:51 describes people emerging from graves and moving toward their Lord. Al-Maʿārij 70:43 describes their rapid departure from graves.

These passages express resurrection and the dead emerging through different verbs and images.

Baʿthara, by contrast, emphasizes transformation of the condition of something in a covered place. Its settled, covered state no longer holds, and what was within becomes exposed and is brought out.

Baʿth and baʿthara therefore draw close in resurrection contexts while remaining less than fully synonymous lexically.

Baʿth can concern raising, sending, or making someone rise. Baʿthara, beyond the possibility of bringing out, carries the image of overturning and disrupting an existing arrangement.

This difference should not be turned into a definitive chronology. The roots’ difference alone, for example, cannot establish that all graves are first overturned and human beings begin coming to life after some specific interval. Such a sequence requires independent evidence.

What the two verbs clearly show is a difference in perspective.

5. The connection between overturning graves and revealing what is in breasts

The continuation of Al-ʿĀdiyāt is particularly important for understanding the root’s place in the passage.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:10

Verse text:

وَحُصِّلَ مَا فِي الصُّدُورِ

wa-ḥuṣṣila mā fī al-ṣudūri

Translation:

And what is within breasts is gathered and made manifest.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:11

Verse text:

إِنَّ رَبَّهُمْ بِهِمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ لَخَبِيرٌ

inna rabbahum bihim yawmaʾidhin la-khabīrun

Translation:

Their Lord is indeed fully aware of them on that day.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9 concerns what is in graves being brought out; Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:10 turns to what lies within breasts.

There is a clear connection between these expressions: both concern something within a place that is subsequently dealt with in the resurrection scene.

The verbs, however, are different.

بُعْثِرَ comes from ب ع ث ر and concerns overturning, raising, and bringing out.

حُصِّلَ comes from ح ص ل and in this context concerns gathering, discerning, and making the outcome of affairs clear.

Study of Ḥ Ṣ L

The meaning of ḥaṣala should therefore not be transferred to baʿthara so as to conclude that baʿthara originally means accounting, separating good and bad deeds, or revealing intentions.

The connection can instead be understood as follows: the person brought out from the place of death will confront the reality of their conduct and what they held within.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:11 likewise emphasizes the Lord’s complete awareness of human beings.

This prevents a mistaken interpretation, as though The God needed graves to be overturned or breast contents to become manifest in order to learn people’s hidden affairs.

The Qur’an also describes The God’s knowledge of apparent and hidden matters before these events. Manifestation and gathering at the reckoning should therefore not be understood as the ending of ignorance on His part.

The scene addresses human beings who may be heedless of the consequences of their conduct in earthly life but will confront its reality and their destiny on that day.

The preceding verses of Al-ʿĀdiyāt discuss human ingratitude, awareness of one’s own conduct, and intense attachment to good or wealth. The question in verse 9 follows this context.

The overturning of graves in this surah is consequently more than an image of disturbed soil: it belongs to the account of resurrection and human responsibility. This contextual conclusion should nevertheless not replace the verb’s lexical meaning.

6. Distinguishing related roots

ب ع ث ر (baʿthara) and ب ع ث (baʿatha)

Study of B ʿ TH

Baʿth concerns raising, setting in motion, sending, and, in resurrection contexts, raising the dead.

Baʿthara concerns overturning, disrupting an arrangement, and bringing something out of a covered or settled condition.

The families draw close particularly in passages about graves, but equating them removes their differing imagery.

Baʿthara is not merely a longer version of baʿatha whose fourth consonant can be treated as ineffective.

A connection may be proposed in a derivational analysis, but a Qur’anic study should preserve baʿthara’s quadriliteral structure and independent use.

ب ع ث ر (baʿthara) and ب ح ث (baḥatha)

Study of B Ḥ TH

In physical uses, baḥth concerns digging and searching, including removing soil to find or expose something.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:31, a raven digs the ground to show the human being how to cover his brother’s body.

Baʿthara may likewise involve soil being displaced and something exposed, but it also attends to disruption and overturning.

In searching, finding something may be the action’s purpose. In baʿtharah, exposure can result from disruption and overturning even when systematic searching was not the main intention.

This distinction clarifies the families’ semantic tendencies; it does not establish an absolute boundary separating every use.

ب ع ث ر (baʿthara) and ب ح ث ر (baḥthara)

The two quadriliteral forms are reported as closely related in lexical sources.

Both have recorded senses such as jumbling belongings, scattering them, and bringing something out from beneath a covering.

Some lexicographers regard them as synonymous linguistic forms. Their closeness, however, cannot establish which came first historically without sufficient evidence.

The Qur’anic text examined here uses baʿthara, which remains the basis of translation and analysis.

ب ع ث ر (baʿthara) and ن ث ر (nathara)

Study of N TH R

Nathr concerns scattering and strewing parts. Al-Infiṭār 82:2 describes the stars scattering.

Baʿthara may also involve dispersal, but its image can include overturning, displacement of internal parts, and the emergence of what was covered.

Objects scattering does not necessarily mean that something was buried and became exposed. Graves being overturned, on the other hand, relates to the ending of their covered, settled condition.

This difference shows why the two roots in nearby verses of Al-Infiṭār should not receive wholly identical translations.

ب ع ث ر (baʿthara) and ن ب ش (nabasha)

Nabsh concerns digging into and opening the ground or a grave to bring something out.

In relation to graves, nabsh and baʿthara may describe closely related events. Baʿthara, however, is not confined to soil and graves; it also describes belongings, basins, and other things.

Disruption of an existing arrangement is also prominent in baʿthara, while nabsh can more specifically identify digging and opening a covered place.

Digging up a grave can therefore explain one instance of the overturning of graves, but it does not define the entire family ب ع ث ر.

ب ع ث ر (baʿthara) and خ ر ج (kharaja)

Study of KH R J

Khurūj primarily concerns going or coming out. It does not necessarily require the previous location to be overturned.

Baʿthara may result in something emerging, but its image involves transformation of a covered condition or an existing arrangement.

“Coming out” thus conveys a suitable result of baʿtharah in some sentences without expressing the root’s full semantic potential on its own.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For active بَعْثَرَ in general usage, “turned over,” “jumbled,” “scattered,” or “brought out from beneath a covering” can be appropriate. The choice should fit the verb’s object and the action’s circumstances.

For verbal noun بَعْثَرَة, suitable renderings may include “turning over,” “disruption,” or “scattering and exposing.” Where dispersal alone is intended, adding exposure is unnecessary.

The following translations are proposed for the two Qur’anic uses:

Al-Infiṭār 82:4 — وَإِذَا الْقُبُورُ بُعْثِرَتْ

“And when the graves are turned over.”

“Are turned over” is preferable here because the graves themselves are the verb’s subject. The transformation’s relationship to the dead emerging belongs in the explanation.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9 — أَفَلَا يَعْلَمُ إِذَا بُعْثِرَ مَا فِي الْقُبُورِ

“Does he not know, when what is in the graves is turned over and brought out?”

Here, mā fī al-qubūr is the verb’s subject. The translation should therefore make clear that what lies within the graves is brought out.

“Are scattered” for the Al-Infiṭār verse conveys part of the root’s meaning but does not clearly preserve the image of graves being overturned. Likewise, “the dead come to life” for the Al-ʿĀdiyāt verse conveys the resurrection outcome while covering the particular meaning of baʿthara with an interpretive expression.

A basic translation should preserve the word’s image and explain the broader resurrection significance in the reflection.

8. Conclusion

The quadriliteral root ب ع ث ر concerns turning over, disrupting an existing order and arrangement, scattering parts, and bringing out what lies under a covering.

Recorded uses involving soil, belongings, and basins show that it is not confined to graves and resurrection. Its proposed origin from combining baʿth and uthīra, though semantically worth examining, remains a derivational possibility rather than an established fact.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an. In Al-Infiṭār 82:4, the graves themselves are turned over; in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9, what is within the graves is brought out.

Both verses concern resurrection, but this should not reduce baʿthara to a definition of the dead coming to life. The root retains the image of a previous condition being disrupted and what was covered emerging.

In Al-Infiṭār, the event accompanies other great transformations of the world and connects with human knowledge of one’s own conduct. In Al-ʿĀdiyāt, the emergence of what is in graves is joined with gathering and revealing what is in breasts. These connections clarify resurrection and human responsibility without changing the root’s lexical definition.

Final root definition: ب ع ث ر denotes overturning and disrupting something’s ordered or covered condition, so that its parts are dispersed or what lay inside or underneath is exposed and brought out.

The family’s distinctive feature is more than dispersal: it includes transformation of the previous condition and exposure of what lay within it. In the two Qur’anic uses, “the graves being turned over” and “what is within graves being brought out” are thus two related expressions of one semantic field.
