The Qur’anic Root F N Y (فَنِيَ)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Coming to an end, perishing, and perishability: from the wearing out of life and exhaustion of possessions to the Qur’anic contrast between passing away and remaining

1. The root’s meaning in early Arabic

The three-letter root ف ن ي concerns coming to an end, passing away, and the cessation of something’s continuance. فَنِيَ الشَّيْءُ means that something ended, perished, or no longer remained in its former form or condition. فَنَاء names this ending or passing away, while فَانٍ describes something perishable or subject to an end.

A familiar classical Arabic definition of this family is الفَنَاءُ نَقِيضُ الْبَقَاءِ: passing away is the opposite of remaining.

Remaining denotes continued existence; passing away concerns the end of that continuance.

This meaning can apply to a human being, an animal, wealth, an ability, the contents of a storehouse, or a condition that does not last.

For example, فَنِيَ الْمَالُ means that wealth ran out or was lost. أَفْنَى مَالَهُ means that someone exhausted or dissipated their wealth.

Of a human being, فَنِيَ الرَّجُلُ can refer to the end of his life. Some early usages also apply فَانٍ to someone worn down by old age and approaching life’s end.

These usages show that perishing does not name only a sudden event. Something may gradually wear out and eventually end, or an event may abruptly terminate its continuance.

Nevertheless, gradualness, physical destruction, and the disappearance of every trace are not fixed requirements of the root’s meaning. What this family emphasizes is the ending or passing away of the subject under discussion.

Evidence from early Arabic poetry

A poem attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah al-ʿĀmirī says:

حَبَائِلُهُ مَبْثُوثَةٌ بِسَبِيلِهِ

وَيَفْنَى إِذَا مَا أَخْطَأَتْهُ الْحَبَائِلُ

Ḥabāʾiluhu mabthūthatun bi-sabīlihi / wa-yafnā idhā mā akhṭaʾat-hu l-ḥabāʾilu

“Death’s snares are spread along his path; even if the snares miss him, he eventually wears out and passes away.”

The poet speaks here of the human condition.

حَبَائِل denotes snares or means of entrapment; in this image it stands for mortal dangers. A person may escape these dangers, but escaping one danger does not mean permanent release from life’s end.

Here يَفْنَى concerns human decline and perishability. Early lexical explanations also cite this line as evidence for growing old and approaching the end of life.

The distinction matters: the word can describe decline that leads toward an end, rather than naming only the instant of death in every sentence.

Labīd lived both before and after the advent of Islam. Some sources identify this poem as an elegy for al-Nuʿmān ibn al-Mundhir, ruler of al-Ḥīrah. If that occasion and attribution are correct, the poem belongs near the beginning of Qur’anic revelation or earlier. Attribution to Labīd alone, however, cannot establish this particular line’s date with certainty.

With this qualification, the verse and recorded lexical usages show that human perishability and decline were recognized meanings of this family in early Arabic.

Other branches of the family

In Arabic, أَفْنَى concerns bringing something to an end or destroying it. Its difference from فَنِيَ becomes clear through the agent’s role.

In فَنِيَ الْمَالُ, the wealth has run out.

In أَفْنَى الرَّجُلُ مَالَهُ, however, the man has exhausted his wealth.

تَفَانَى الْقَوْمُ is also recorded of groups that destroy one another in war. This construction involves reciprocal action between the parties.

فِناءُ الدَّارِ, with a kasrah on the first letter, denotes the open space in front of or around a house; its plural is أَفْنِيَة.

Without vowel marks this noun looks like فَناء, “passing away,” but their meanings differ. Some lexicographers explain the space before a house in terms of reaching the house’s boundary. That explanation alone does not establish a definite historical derivation connecting the two usages.

الفَنا is also recorded as a plant name, associated with small seeds or berries. Poetry attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā uses the name in a comparison with scattered seeds. This plant name should not be interpreted as destruction merely because its written form resembles the other word.

The family should therefore be studied through the actual meaning of each form, rather than by imposing a single image on every similarly written word.

2. Forms and the Qur’anic semantic core

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

فَنِيَ, “came to an end” or “perished”; يَفْنَى, “comes to an end”; فَنَاء, “ending” or “passing away”; فَانٍ, “perishable” or “approaching an end”; أَفْنَى, “destroyed” or “brought to an end”; إِفْنَاء, “destruction”; and تَفَانَى, in usages such as mutual destruction.

In the Qur’an, however, the root occurs only once:

فَانٍ — al-Raḥmān 55:26

فَانٍ is the active participle of فَنِيَ, following the pattern فَاعِل. Its underlying morphological stem is فَانِي. As a noun with a weak final letter, indefinite and nominative here, it loses its final yāʾ and appears as فَانٍ with tanwīn.

The same formation appears in بَاقٍ from ب ق ي.

In its Qur’anic use, فَانٍ is neither a past-tense nor a present-tense verb: it is a description attributing perishability to the sentence’s subject.

This matters. فَنِيَ reports an ending, whereas فَانٍ can describe someone who is alive now but will not remain forever.

Thus the verse’s description does not necessarily mean someone who has already perished. It describes someone subject to an end, someone who will pass away.

The semantic core here is perishability and the absence of permanent continuance.

3. Al-Raḥmān 55:26: everyone on earth is perishable

كُلُّ مَنْ عَلَيْهَا فَانٍ

Kullu man ʿalayhā fān.

“Everyone upon it is passing away.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:26

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of ف ن ي.

The verse is brief, but each component helps determine its scope.

كُلُّ makes the statement comprehensive within the relevant set.

مَنْ refers to those under discussion. Ordinarily used for persons and perceiving beings, it should not be extended without examination to “every object and every particle.”

عَلَيْهَا refers back to the earth, which al-Raḥmān 55:10 has already described as established for creatures.

فَانٍ describes those upon the earth.

The direct meaning is therefore clear: everyone on earth is perishable, and their earthly life and presence will not last forever.

The perishability of humans and jinn

Sūrat al-Raḥmān addresses both humans and jinn. Al-Raḥmān 55:14 and al-Raḥmān 55:15 discuss their creation, and al-Raḥmān 55:31 addresses the two groups as الثقلان.

In this context, the perishability described in al-Raḥmān 55:26 includes jinn as well as humans.

But مَنْ عَلَيْهَا concerns those upon the earth. Without independent evidence, this expression alone cannot establish details about the perishability of every unseen being or every celestial body.

Other Qur’anic verses may discuss creation’s perishability or the transformation of the world; each verse’s scope must nevertheless be determined from its own wording and context.

Does “perishable” mean already dead?

No.

Someone described as perishable may be alive and strong: working, thinking, raising a family, and enjoying life. That condition is simply not permanent.

A young person, for example, may enjoy excellent health and strength, but these do not exempt them from the impermanence of earthly life.

Conversely, an elderly person may live for many more years. فَانٍ does not specify a date of death.

Forqan’s current Persian wording, corresponding to “will pass away,” is therefore natural and accurate. It expresses perishability without asserting that the subject has already been destroyed.

4. Passing away contrasted with remaining

Immediately after the verse on creatures’ perishability comes:

وَيَبْقَىٰ وَجْهُ رَبِّكَ ذُو الْجَلَالِ وَالْإِكْرَامِ

Wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika dhū l-jalāli wa-l-ikrām.

“And the face of your Lord, Possessor of Majesty and Honor, remains.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:27

Together these verses create a clear contrast.

In al-Raḥmān 55:26, فَانٍ expresses the perishability of those upon the earth.

In al-Raḥmān 55:27, يَبْقَىٰ speaks of the remaining of the Lord’s face.

Lexically, passing away and remaining occupy opposite sides of a contrast: one denotes the end of continuance, the other its persistence.

The nature of that persistence, however, depends on the subject.

A tree may remain for years and later wither. A house may survive its builder. A person’s words or work may endure long after them.

None of these cases establishes unlimited duration from the word “remaining” alone.

By contrast, the Lord’s remaining in al-Raḥmān 55:27 is set against creatures’ perishability. This distinguishes God’s permanence from beings’ limited, dependent duration.

Does only God’s face remain?

The verse says وَجْهُ رَبِّكَ. وَجْه belongs to و ج ه, whose meaning should be examined independently of ف ن ي and ب ق ي.

Grammatically, يَبْقَىٰ is the verb “remains,” and وَجْهُ رَبِّكَ is its subject.

This construction does not justify imagining God as a body with perishable parts, only one of which—the face—survives.

Such a reading imposes a bodily image on the divine description that the contrast between passing away and remaining does not establish.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:88 likewise speaks of the perishability of everything, with an exception for the Lord’s face. This association of “face” with exemption from destruction provides further context for studying the expression among divine descriptions.

The central point discernible here is that creatures are perishable, whereas their Lord is not subject to their destruction and passing away.

A fuller explanation of “face” belongs in that root’s study, rather than being reproduced in full in this article.

For an independent examination of remaining, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root ب ق ي.

5. Does passing away mean absolute annihilation of existence?

This question concerns the distinction between lexical meaning and philosophical inference.

In Arabic, فَنِيَ can express the ending of something itself. When wealth perishes, the point may be that someone’s possessions are exhausted. Applied to a human being, it can denote life’s end or the decline leading toward it.

These usages do not establish a single ontological definition under which every perishable thing must vanish from every mode of existence, with no possibility of return or other continuation.

If someone consumes all their provisions, for example, we can say their provisions have run out. This does not mean all the food’s constituent matter has disappeared from existence.

Likewise, when earthly life ends, that life has indeed ended. Claiming that the person’s entire existence is destroyed forever is a further proposition requiring independent evidence.

The Qur’an does not present human death as the end of a person’s relationship with God or as a denial of resurrection.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:15 speaks of death after earthly life, while al-Muʾminūn 23:16 describes being raised again on the Day of Resurrection.

Al-Baqarah 2:28 mentions death and life at different stages, and al-Zumar 39:42 discusses the taking of the self at death and during sleep.

These verses do not allow the end of earthly life to be automatically equated with a person’s absolute and permanent extinction.

Nor should this be taken to mean that the character of life after death, the constitution of the self, or awareness in the unseen world is fully known to us.

Consequently, human perishability concerns the ending of earthly life; by itself it is not a complete theory of every mode of a person’s existence before and after death.

This distinction is explored in detail in Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root م و ت.

Passing away in philosophical and mystical terminology

In later periods, some philosophical and mystical traditions gave فناء technical meanings, including “annihilation in God” and “annihilation of the self,” explained through overcoming selfishness, transformed awareness, or the disappearance of personal attachments.

These usages belong within the history of thought and its technical vocabularies.

But فَانٍ in al-Raḥmān 55:26 does not directly designate such concepts. The verse concerns the perishability of those upon earth, not a particular mystical station or spiritual experience.

Later technical usage should therefore not replace the Qur’anic meaning.

6. Distinctions from related roots

ف ن ي and ب ق ي: perishability and remaining

These two families stand in clear semantic opposition.

فَنِيَ denotes something’s ending or passing away.

بَقِيَ concerns its continuation or remaining.

Al-Raḥmān 55:26 and al-Raḥmān 55:27 state this contrast directly.

Nevertheless, something’s remaining can be temporary. What remains now may perish later.

In its general sense, remaining does not always mean eternity; perishing does not necessarily imply sudden destruction.

ف ن ي and ن ف د: perishing and exhaustion of supplies

ن ف د concerns something coming to an end, particularly when its supply, quantity, or availability for use is exhausted.

Al-Naḥl 16:96 says:

مَا عِنْدَكُمْ يَنْفَدُ وَمَا عِنْدَ اللَّهِ بَاقٍ وَلَنَجْزِيَنَّ الَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا أَجْرَهُمْ بِأَحْسَنِ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

Mā ʿindakum yanfadu wa-mā ʿinda llāhi bāqin wa-la-najziyanna lladhīna ṣabarū ajrahum bi-aḥsani mā kānū yaʿmalūn.

“What is with you runs out, and what is with The God remains; and We will surely reward those who persevered according to the best of what they used to do.”

Al-Naḥl 16:96

Here يَنْفَدُ describes the limited duration of human possessions and is contrasted with بَاقٍ.

Exhaustion and passing away overlap in the field of coming to an end.

In the Qur’anic uses of ن ف د, however, the exhaustion of a finite supply or resource is prominent, whereas فَانٍ in al-Raḥmān 55:26 describes beings’ perishability.

If a vessel’s water runs out, نَفِدَ is very natural. When discussing the ending of someone’s life, فَنِيَ may be appropriate.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule governing all Arabic.

For details, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root ن ف د.

ف ن ي and ه ل ك: passing away and destruction

The Qur’an uses ه ل ك for destruction, perishing, and the loss of persons, groups, or things.

In al-Qaṣaṣ 28:88, هَالِكٌ occurs alongside وَجْهَهُ, creating a thematic parallel to the contrast between passing away and remaining in Sūrat al-Raḥmān.

Destruction has varied Qur’anic uses: the destruction of a people, the death of individuals, or the loss and ruin of something.

Passing away emphasizes perishability and cessation. In many Qur’anic uses, هلاك highlights the event or outcome of destruction.

Yet destruction cannot be defined as always sudden and passing away as always gradual. The two roots do not establish that absolute distinction.

ف ن ي and م و ت: passing away and death

In human contexts, م و ت concerns death and the end of familiar life.

ف ن ي has a broader range: it can describe the ending of life, wealth, ability, or a supply.

Al-Raḥmān 55:26 concerns those upon the earth. In this setting, death is among the clearest instances of the ending of their earthly life.

Lexically, however, passing away and death are not the same word.

Death ends life in a particular condition; passing away concerns the ending or cessation of the subject in question. Their convergence in one instance does not make all their uses identical.

ف ن ي and ز و ل: passing away and departure

In Arabic, ز و ل concerns moving from a place, departing, or the cessation of a condition.

Someone’s anxiety, for example, may be said to have disappeared. Here a psychological condition has ended.

Passing away can also describe a condition’s ending, but its central branch emphasizes cessation and the termination of the subject’s continuance.

Departure does not always mean the destruction of the thing itself. Something may move or undergo a change of condition without being destroyed.

One should therefore not infer annihilation or nonexistence from every occurrence of زَالَ.

ف ن ي and ف ن ن: two different families

Despite their visual resemblance, ف ن ن and ف ن ي are distinct.

فَنَن denotes a tree branch, and فَنّ a kind or manner. أَفْنَان occurs in al-Raḥmān 55:48.

By contrast, فَانٍ in al-Raḥmān 55:26 belongs to ف ن ي and concerns perishability.

Both families occurring in Sūrat al-Raḥmān does not establish a derivational relationship.

Nor should modern فَنّ, “art” or “skill,” be identified with فَنَاء, “passing away.”

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The main equivalents for فَنِيَ are “came to an end,” “passed away,” and, where appropriate, “perished.”

For يَفْنَى, “comes to an end,” “passes away,” or, of a person, “perishes” may be suitable.

فَنَاء can be rendered “perishing,” “coming to an end,” “passing away,” or “cessation”; the choice depends on the subject.

For أَفْنَى, appropriate equivalents include “destroyed,” “annihilated,” or “brought to an end.”

For تَفَانَى in a military context, “destroyed one another” or “annihilated each other” can convey the construction’s meaning.

These verbal forms, however, do not occur in the Qur’an.

For the sole Qur’anic form فَانٍ, two suitable renderings are:

“Will pass away” and “is perishable.”

Forqan’s existing translation of al-Raḥmān 55:26 reads:

كُلُّ مَنْ عَلَيْهَا فَانٍ

Kullu man ʿalayhā fān.

“Everyone upon it is passing away.”

The existing translation is brief, natural, and defensible; it need not be changed.

“Is perishable” is also lexically sound. In ordinary Persian, however, the existing expression corresponding to “will pass away” is more fluent and accessible.

“Everyone has been destroyed” is incorrect: the verse does not report a completed event in the past.

“Everyone is undergoing destruction” may likewise imply an actual ongoing process more strongly than the participial construction warrants.

“Everyone on earth will die” expresses one clear instance of passing away, but confines the word’s range to death.

Forqan’s existing Persian wording should therefore be retained. Lexical commentary can clarify its sense of perishability and the absence of permanent continuance; the English “passing away” should likewise be understood in this sense.

Conclusion

In Arabic, ف ن ي concerns ending, passing away, and the cessation of something’s continuance.

The ending may concern human life, wealth, ability, or anything whose continuance is at issue.

In early Arabic this family also concerns the decline of old age. The poem attributed to Labīd shows that even someone who escapes mortal dangers remains subject to life’s end.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as فَانٍ.

Al-Raḥmān 55:26 describes the perishability of those upon earth. The following verse, al-Raḥmān 55:27, contrasts this with the remaining of the Lord’s face.

This contrast shows that creatures’ life and presence do not endure without limit, whereas God’s permanence is distinct from that perishability.

Passing away is semantically related to exhaustion, destruction, death, and departure, but their uses are not identical. Nor should the end of earthly life be taken, without evidence, to entail the absolute and permanent extinction of a person’s entire existence: the Qur’an also speaks of death, revival, resurrection, and return.

Final root definition for Forqan:

ف ن ي: coming to an end, passing away, or ceasing to continue, whether through gradual decline or an event that terminates its duration. فَانٍ describes something or someone that does not remain permanently and is subject to an end.

In al-Raḥmān 55:26, فَانٍ describes the perishability of those upon earth: they will pass away, while the following verse sets the Lord’s remaining against their impermanence.
