Giving, attribution and claims, becoming thin, and honey bees: a family with several semantic branches

1. Introduction and semantic cores

Arabic N Ḥ L has several established branches: giving something to another, attributing speech or thought, becoming thin, and the name of the honey bee.

In the giving branch, naḥala and anḥala concern giving something to someone, especially without demanding direct compensation. Niḥlah also denotes a gift or grant.

The attribution branch includes naḥalahu qawlan and intaḥala al-shiʿra. Literary uses commonly involve false attribution or claiming another’s poetry. Other uses, such as adopting a belief, are not necessarily dishonest; construction and context identify the sense.

Another branch uses naḥila for becoming lean or losing flesh. Nāḥil and naḥīl describe thin or worn humans and animals.

Naḥl, finally, names honey bees; naḥlah denotes one bee.

The branches resemble one another in writing, but should not be derived from one certain common meaning without historical evidence.

Thinness, giving, and attribution or claims can be distinguished within this family. Honey bees also appear within the lexical family, but the classification alone does not establish the historical sequence of meanings.

A lexical explanation connects the bee’s name with giving honey. This is an etymological possibility, rather than proven naming history.

No single semantic core can confidently explain every use without exception. Recognize established branches separately, relating them only as far as evidence permits.

Qur’anic forms

The root occurs twice, in two nominal forms:

  • Niḥlatan: a gift or grant; Al-Nisāʾ 4:4.
  • Al-naḥli: a honey bee or honey bees; Al-Naḥl 16:68.

Their root consonants are the same, but vocalization and meaning differ.

Niḥlah, with initial i, concerns giving in Al-Nisāʾ.

Naḥl, with initial a, names honey bees; its singular naḥlah likewise begins with a.

Qur’anic niḥlah should therefore not be confused with naḥlah, one bee.

No verb from the root occurs in the Qur’an. Naḥala, anḥala, intaḥala, tanaḥḥala, nāḥil, and naḥīl are Arabic forms, but not Qur’anic derivatives.

2. Early Arabic usage and semantic branches

Giving without demanding compensation

One clear Arabic meaning is giving something without demanding direct compensation.

Arabic records giving without asking for a return, with nuḥl for a grant and niḥlah for a gift.

For example:

نَحَلْتُهُ مَالًا

naḥaltuhu mālan

“I gave him wealth as a gift.”

Likewise:

نَحَلْتُ الْمَرْأَةَ مَهْرَهَا

naḥaltu al-marʾata mahrahā

“I gave the woman her dower.”

The latter does not mean she had no right to the dower before receiving it. It concerns transferring property appointed for her.

Giving generously differs from paying something while demanding a further return.

This does not make every gift necessarily optional or free of obligation. Property may be someone’s established right, while its payer must hand it over without reproach, extra compensation, or wrongful interference.

The distinction matters for niḥlatan in Al-Nisāʾ 4:4.

Niḥlah: gift, religious commitment, and belief

Niḥlah is also recorded for a religion or religious belief.

Mā niḥlatuka means “What is your religion or belief?”

Yantaḥilu madhhaban describes someone adopting or affiliating with a school or position.

The person regards himself as committed to or associated with that belief.

Adopting something and attributing it to oneself have an intelligible connection, but this does not establish that religious niḥlah developed directly from a financial gift.

These uses are established; their historical order requires separate study.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4 also has explanations of niḥlatan as farīḍatan or diyānatan: an appointed due or an obligation observed on religious grounds.

These differ from a gift and should not be substituted for one another without context.

Intiḥāl: attribution and claiming authorship

Another important branch attributes speech or poetry to someone.

Naḥalahu qawlan can concern attributing a statement to a person.

If someone else actually said it, the attribution is false.

Intaḥala al-shiʿra describes claiming poetry for oneself, commonly with a false claim of authorship.

This matters for Arabic literary history, since attributing verses to poets who did not compose them is an issue in assessing early poetry.

A witness from al-Aʿshā

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿshā says:

فَكَيْفَ أَنَا وَانْتِحَالِيَ الْقَوَافِيَ
بَعْدَ الْمَشِيبِ كَفَى ذَاكَ عَارَا

fa-kayfa anā wa-intiḥāliya al-qawāfiya / baʿda al-mashībi kafā dhāka ʿārā

Translation:

“How could I, in old age, claim others’ verses as my own? That alone would be shame enough.”

Intiḥāl here concerns claiming authorship or attributing another’s poetry to oneself.

It illustrates attribution and claims in early Arabic poetic language.

It does not make every intiḥāl necessarily deceitful.

Someone genuinely adopting a belief or affiliating with a group can make a true claim of belonging.

No absolute truth/falsehood boundary should be imposed between intiḥāl and tanaḥḥul. Their particular uses in attribution and claims require evidence and context.

Truth or falsity consequently comes from evidence and context, rather than the verbal form alone.

Nuḥūl: becoming thin and worn

Another branch uses naḥila for a person or animal becoming thin.

Naḥila jismuhu means “his body became thin.”

Nāḥil and naḥīl describe reduced flesh or bodily wear through illness, travel, or hardship.

Arabic also applies this description to sword edges thinned by extensive use and repeated sharpening.

Al-Aʿshā says:

مَضَارِبُهَا مِنْ طُولِ مَا ضَرَبُوا بِهَا
وَمِنْ عَضِّ هَامِ الدَّارِعِينَ نَوَاحِلُ

maḍāribuhā min ṭūli mā ḍarabū bihā / wa-min ʿaḍḍi hāmi al-dāriʿīna nawāḥilu

Translation:

“Their striking edges have grown thin and worn from long use and biting the heads of armoured warriors.”

Nawāḥil describes swords whose blades have lost thickness through use.

This has a clear figurative connection with bodily leanness.

It does not establish giving wealth as originally a kind of thinning, as though the giver’s loss necessarily supplied the name.

Without historical evidence, that remains a possible image.

Thinness and giving are established branches, but neither has proven historical priority over the other.

Al-naḥl: honey bees

Naḥl names honey bees.

Early Arabic also calls them dhubāb al-ʿasali, a winged honey-producing insect. Applying modern taxonomy to that historical wording would wrongly identify honey bees as ordinary flies.

Naḥl is collective, while one bee is naḥlah.

Honey bees were familiar in early Arab life. Honey was valued as food, and collecting it from natural nests was a human activity.

A witness from Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī

Abū Dhuʾayb says:

وَإِنَّ حَدِيثًا مِنْكِ لَوْ تَبْذُلِينَهُ
جَنَى النَّحْلِ فِي أَلْبَانِ عُوذٍ مَطَافِلِ

wa-inna ḥadīthan minki law tabdhulīnahu / janā al-naḥli fī albāni ʿūdhin maṭāfili

Translation:

“If you would grant me a word, it would be honey gathered by bees, mixed with the milk of newly calved she-camels nursing their young.”

Janā al-naḥli denotes the bees’ produce, honey.

Honey and milk express the beloved’s speech as sweet and pleasant.

Honey bees and their product thus have a recognized place in Arabic literary imagery.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This verse’s exact date is uncertain, so it is not an unqualified, conclusive pre-Qur’anic witness.

Was the bee named through giving honey?

Some explanations connect the name with honey being given to the bee, others with the bee’s benefit and its giving honey to people. These are different etymological proposals, rather than one established historical account.

They connect giving and bees conceptually, but a conceptual link is not proof of a name’s origin.

In the first, the bee receives the gift of honey; that differs from the bee giving honey to people.

The other emphasizes the bee’s usefulness and product. It too cannot establish naming history alone.

Neither explanation proves that the earliest Arabic speakers actually named the bee for that reason.

A historical relationship may exist, but semantic resemblance alone cannot establish it.

Forqan should therefore retain honey bee as an independent established sense, with the giving connection presented only as a lexical possibility.

3. First Qur’anic usage: Al-Nisāʾ 4:4 and women’s financial rights

وَآتُوا النِّسَاءَ صَدُقَاتِهِنَّ نِحْلَةً ۚ فَإِنْ طِبْنَ لَكُمْ عَنْ شَيْءٍ مِنْهُ نَفْسًا فَكُلُوهُ هَنِيئًا مَرِيئًا

wa-ātū al-nisāʾa ṣaduqātihinna niḥlatan fa-in ṭibna lakum ʿan shayʾin minhu nafsan fa-kulūhu hanīʾan marīʾan

Translation:

“Give women their dowers as a gift. If they willingly give you something of it, consume it pleasantly and agreeably.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4

Niḥlatan relates to women’s dowers.

Ṣaduqātihinna is the plural of ṣaduqah, marriage dower; -hinna attributes it to the women.

Ātū commands giving.

The verse therefore concerns giving women the dower belonging to them.

A. The meaning of niḥlatan

Niḥlah denotes a gift or grant, particularly something given without direct compensation.

It follows the command to pay the dower:

وَآتُوا النِّسَاءَ صَدُقَاتِهِنَّ نِحْلَةً

wa-ātū al-nisāʾa ṣaduqātihinna niḥlatan

Two aspects appear together: giving the dower and the character or status of that giving.

On the gift reading, the verse emphasizes transferring the dower to women, rather than to another person or in exchange for demands beyond the payer’s rights.

Other readings also exist.

B. Gift, prescribed due, or religious obligation?

Three principal explanations have been proposed.

First, a gift given without demanding compensation.

Second, a prescribed due belonging to the women.

Third, a payment made as a religious obligation.

They emphasize different aspects without necessarily excluding one another.

Gift emphasizes the manner of transfer; prescribed due emphasizes obligation; religious commitment concerns its place in religious responsibility.

Property may be someone’s established right while another must hand it over willingly, without reproach or an extra return.

The gift sense therefore does not make payment entirely optional, permitting refusal whenever the payer wishes.

Ātū clearly commands giving.

Conversely, the gift meaning should not disappear into tax, debt, or wages alone.

This study prefers gift in the basic translation, understanding the woman’s established due through the command and context.

C. The grammar of niḥlatan

Niḥlatan is accusative.

Several grammatical analyses exist.

One takes it as an accusative verbal noun expressing the kind of payment: give the dower as a gift.

Another treats it as a circumstantial verbal noun: give while transferring generously.

The prescribed-due and religious-obligation readings also have corresponding grammatical analyses.

They affect its relation to payment without altering the command itself.

All concern a dower to be given to women.

The accusative alone cannot establish optional payment or define the dower’s legal character in every circumstance.

Grammar must be considered with the lexical sense and complete sentence.

D. To whom does the dower belong?

The pronoun in ṣaduqātihinna refers to women.

The command likewise gives the dower to the women themselves.

This matters in Al-Nisāʾ’s opening context of orphans’ property and justice in family relationships.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:2 concerns returning orphans’ wealth and avoiding wrongful appropriation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:3 concerns justice and marriage.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4 then emphasizes giving women their dowers.

The sequence indicates that others should not seize the woman’s financial due for their own benefit.

Reports about pre-Islamic customs describe fathers or guardians sometimes receiving marriage property for themselves while the woman received none of her dower.

Such reports can illuminate the command’s importance, without establishing uniform practice in every family or among all pre-Islamic Arabs.

The verse itself clearly attributes the dower to women and commands giving it to them.

E. Fa-in ṭibna lakum: the woman’s own consent

The second part says:

فَإِنْ طِبْنَ لَكُمْ عَنْ شَيْءٍ مِنْهُ نَفْسًا

fa-in ṭibna lakum ʿan shayʾin minhu nafsan

Meaning:

“If they willingly give you something of it.”

Ṭibna refers to the women.

Nafsan clarifies their inner willingness.

The woman herself willingly relinquishes or transfers some of her dower.

This foregrounds her decision concerning her own property.

Genuine willingness differs from silence caused by fear, family pressure, or coercion.

An outward transfer made only to escape threats or pressure cannot be described as wholehearted consent simply because it occurred.

This comes from ṭibna ... nafsan, rather than N Ḥ L itself.

F. Fa-kulūhu hanīʾan marīʾan: using willingly transferred property

The verse ends:

فَكُلُوهُ هَنِيئًا مَرِيئًا

fa-kulūhu hanīʾan marīʾan

Meaning:

“Then consume it pleasantly and agreeably.”

Arabic eating of wealth can also mean using or benefiting from it.

The property need not therefore be food.

The recipient may use what the woman willingly relinquishes from her dower.

The sentence’s order matters: her willingness precedes permission to use the property.

The ending cannot be detached from its condition to authorize taking the woman’s dower.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:20 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:21 likewise prohibit taking back property given to a woman and describe the spouses’ firm covenant.

G. Is dower the purchase price of a woman?

Some explanations make the woman seem to become the man’s property in exchange for money.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4 concerns giving her dower, rather than buying her.

Niḥlatan fits gift and transfer, rather than presenting a woman as merchandise.

This word alone nevertheless cannot define all marital rights and responsibilities; other verses require examination.

Here the woman’s receipt of her dower and her willingness in transferring some of it are clear.

4. Second Qur’anic usage: Al-Naḥl 16:68

وَأَوْحَىٰ رَبُّكَ إِلَى النَّحْلِ أَنِ اتَّخِذِي مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا وَمِنَ الشَّجَرِ وَمِمَّا يَعْرِشُونَ

wa-awḥā rabbuka ilā al-naḥli ani ittakhidhī mina al-jibāli buyūtan wa-mina al-shajari wa-mimmā yaʿrishūna

Translation:

“Your Lord revealed to the honey bee: Take houses in the mountains, in trees, and in what they raise.”

Al-Naḥl 16:68

Al-naḥli names the honey bee.

Unlike Al-Nisāʾ 4:4, this concerns neither dower nor financial gifts between people.

Its subject is the bee, its habitat, and divine guidance concerning its behaviour.

A. Is al-naḥl singular or plural?

Naḥl is a collective generic noun, capable of denoting honey bees as a group.

Its unit form is naḥlah.

Ittakhidhī is a feminine singular imperative.

Kulī and uslukī in Al-Naḥl 16:69 have the same form.

Arabic permits feminine agreement through the unit noun naḥlah or consideration of the group of bees.

The feminine verbs therefore do not make naḥl exclusively female bees or lexically exclude males.

Modern biology identifies most food-gathering and honey-producing bees as female workers. This can relate to the passage without being the sole grammatical explanation of the feminine verbs.

Arabic had grammatical gender rules for collectives before modern biological study.

B. Revelation to honey bees

The verse says:

وَأَوْحَىٰ رَبُّكَ إِلَى النَّحْلِ

wa-awḥā rabbuka ilā al-naḥli

Revelation is attributed to the honey bee.

This does not necessarily make it the same kind of revelation as that given to prophets.

W Ḥ Y occurs in different Qur’anic settings; the addressee and context identify the communication’s character.

The context concerns natural bee behaviour: dwelling, feeding, travelling routes, and producing something beneficial.

Revelation here thus concerns divine guidance in bee behaviour.

Naḥl itself does not specify its manner or biological mechanism.

Nor does the verse establish human-like religious awareness, reception of speech, or moral responsibility in bees.

C. Bee dwellings: mountains, trees, and human structures

Three settings are named:

مِنَ الْجِبَالِ

mina al-jibāli

In the mountains.

وَمِنَ الشَّجَرِ

wa-mina al-shajari

And in trees.

وَمِمَّا يَعْرِشُونَ

wa-mimmā yaʿrishūna

And in what they raise or construct.

Yaʿrishūna comes from ʿ R Sh and concerns erecting or preparing a structure.

It can concern structures people prepare for bees, or ones offering nest sites.

It should not be restricted without evidence to modern industrial hives.

People have used vessels, built spaces, and different structures to keep bees across time.

The verse does not specify materials, shapes, or the nest’s precise structure.

Al-naḥl still means honey bees; architectural or biological explanations come from context and separate evidence.

5. The following verse: bee feeding and honey

Al-Naḥl 16:69 also needs examination in this context:

ثُمَّ كُلِي مِنْ كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ فَاسْلُكِي سُبُلَ رَبِّكِ ذُلُلًا ۚ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ بُطُونِهَا شَرَابٌ مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهُ فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ لِلنَّاسِ ۗ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

thumma kulī min kulli al-thamarāti fa-uslukī subula rabbiki dhululan yakhruju min buṭūnihā sharābun mukhtalifun alwānuhu fīhi shifāʾun li-al-nāsi inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yatafakkarūna

Translation:

“Then eat of all kinds of fruits and follow your Lord’s paths made easy. From within them emerges a drink of different colours, in which is healing for people. In that is a sign for people who reflect.”

Al-Naḥl 16:69

The verse describes feeding, travelling, and producing a drink of varied colours.

A. Kulī min kulli al-thamarāti

This concerns bee food.

Thamarāt comes from Th M R and requires independent lexical study; here it concerns plant food sources.

It does not mean every bee eats every fruit species worldwide or feeds exclusively on ripe fruit flesh.

Honey bees principally use nectar and pollen, with other resources possible according to species and conditions.

This biology helps explain behaviour without automatically replacing thamarāt with flower nectar in the basic translation.

B. Fa-uslukī subula rabbiki dhululan

This concerns following paths.

Subul is the plural of sabīl, a way or path.

Dhululan can describe easy or subdued paths. Another grammatical analysis connects it with the bee’s own submissiveness.

The disagreement concerns sentence structure, rather than the bee’s name.

The verse also does not supply every detail of navigation, memory, or communication among bees.

Those belong to biological research, rather than naḥl’s lexical definition.

C. Yakhruju min buṭūnihā sharābun

A drink of different colours emerges from within the bees.

In the honey-bee context, it refers to honey.

The sentence nevertheless uses sharāb, drink, rather than the word ʿasal.

ʿAsal also occurs in Muḥammad 47:15.

Preserve this distinction in translation: the explanation may identify honey without overlooking the Arabic drink wording.

D. Fīhi shifāʾun li-al-nāsi

Healing is attributed to the bees’ drink.

Lexically, shifāʾ concerns recovery and relief from disease.

The phrase alone does not say honey cures every known disease in every person at any quantity.

Nor does it prescribe honey as a replacement for all other treatments.

Therapeutic uses, limitations, and safety conditions require independent medical evidence.

For N Ḥ L, the bee is presented as a being yielding a beneficial product.

That benefit is not itself naḥl’s lexical meaning.

Does honey establish the bee’s naming history?

The family’s giving sense can suggest the bee was named for giving honey to people.

As noted, this explanation occurs in lexical tradition.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 nevertheless cannot establish the name’s historical origin.

It discusses the bee’s product, rather than the history of the Arabic name.

Honey in the context therefore does not turn an etymological possibility into certainty.

6. Comparison with related roots

N Ḥ L (نَحَلَ) and W H B (وَهَبَ): giving and gifts

Arabic W H B concerns giving and granting. H B B is another root and must not replace it.

Qur’anic forms include hab, wahaba, and the description wahhāb, including requests for divine gifts. The noun hibah itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

Financial niḥlah approaches hibah because both can concern giving without demanding direct compensation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4 nevertheless combines niḥlah with the command to pay dower.

The similarity does not make dower legally identical to every optional gift that can simply be withheld.

The sentence and context establish the distinction.

N Ḥ L (نِحْلَة) and Ṣ D Q (صَدُقَة): gift and marriage dower

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4 joins ṣaduqātihinna with niḥlatan.

Ṣaduqāt identifies the women’s dowers.

Niḥlah describes the gift or manner of transfer.

The words are not complete synonyms.

One identifies the property due, the other the payment’s character or status as a grant.

Dower’s entire meaning should not be derived from N Ḥ L, nor does every Arabic niḥlah necessarily mean marriage dower.

Study of Ṣ D Q

N Ḥ L (نَحَلَ) and ʿ Ṭ W (أَعْطَىٰ): giving and granting

Aʿṭā means giving and occurs in many settings.

A payment may be someone’s due, wages, a gift, or assistance.

The giving branch of naḥala more particularly tends toward giving without demanding compensation.

The distinction is not absolute: broader giving uses are also recorded.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4 expresses the command through ātū, with niḥlatan explaining its accompanying character.

The manner of transfer and the act of giving are distinguishable aspects.

N Ḥ L (نِحْلَة) and N Ḥ L (نَحْلَة): identical unvowelled writing, different meanings

This comparison concerns the same family.

Niḥlah, with initial i, concerns a gift or, elsewhere, religious belief.

Naḥlah, with initial a, is the unit form of naḥl and means one honey bee.

Unvowelled writing looks identical, but vocalization and use differ.

Identical written form therefore does not establish identical meaning.

Persian also uses niḥlah for a religion or intellectual school, belonging to belief and affiliation rather than bees.

N Ḥ L (نَحَلَ) and N Kh L (نَخَلَ): distinct roots

The roots resemble one another in sound and writing but remain distinct.

Qur’anic nakhl concerns date palms and their lexical family.

Al-naḥl in Al-Naḥl names honey bees.

Their resemblance has occasioned differing readings in some early transmissions, without proving historical kinship.

Each root requires its own evidence.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The main concern is preserving the branches’ distinctions.

Giving naḥala can be “gave” or “granted.”

Gift niḥlah is naturally “gift” or “grant.”

Religious niḥlah can be “religion,” “school,” or “religious belief.”

Literary intaḥala can be “attributed to himself,” adding “falsely” when the claim is untrue.

Bodily naḥila is “became thin,” and nāḥil “thin” or “lean.”

Naḥl is “honey bee,” or “honey bees” collectively.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4

وَآتُوا النِّسَاءَ صَدُقَاتِهِنَّ نِحْلَةً

wa-ātū al-nisāʾa ṣaduqātihinna niḥlatan

Proposal:

“Give women their dowers as a gift.”

This preserves the established gift sense.

The explanation should clarify that giving as a gift does not remove the woman’s due. The command and attribution of dower establish payment’s obligation.

Prescribed due and religious obligation can be explained as established readings without awkwardly piling gift and prescription together as competing equivalents in one sentence.

Al-Naḥl 16:68

وَأَوْحَىٰ رَبُّكَ إِلَى النَّحْلِ

wa-awḥā rabbuka ilā al-naḥli

Proposal:

“Your Lord revealed to the honey bee.”

Honey bee is the clear, established meaning.

It should not become giver of honey or another expression constructed from the gift branch.

Any proposed etymological connection belongs in lexical explanation, rather than the basic verse translation.

8. Conclusion

Arabic N Ḥ L has several branches not reducible without evidence to one meaning.

The giving branch concerns transferring something without direct compensation, with niḥlah for a grant or gift.

The attribution branch concerns attributing speech, poetry, a belief, or a claim. Such affiliation can be true or false; the word alone does not establish its validity.

Nuḥūl concerns thinness and loss of flesh or thickness, applied to humans, animals, and sword blades.

Naḥl names honey bees. A connection with giving honey has been proposed, but its historical origin is unproven.

The Qur’an uses the root twice.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4 commands payment of women’s dowers. Gift fits the lexicon and construction without making payment optional or denying the woman’s due. Some of it may be transferred only through her willingness.

Al-Naḥl 16:68 concerns guidance to honey bees about dwelling; the following verse describes feeding, movement, and a beneficial drink.

These verses do not establish the name’s origin, every biological feature, or medical details from the root meaning alone.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N Ḥ L has several established branches:

The giving branch concerns granting or transferring something, particularly without direct compensation. This study prefers that branch for niḥlah in Al-Nisāʾ 4:4, retaining prescribed due and religious obligation in the verse’s explanation.

A second branch concerns attributing speech, poetry, thought, or affiliation to someone, truthfully or falsely.

A third concerns leanness, thinning, and bodily or material reduction.

A fourth names honey bees, the meaning in Al-Naḥl 16:68.

Gift and honey bee are two Qur’anic meanings in this family, but shared consonants do not establish that one derives from the other.

Forqan therefore uses gift for niḥlatan and honey bee for al-naḥl. Possible connections and the other branches belong in lexical study, without imposing additional meaning on the Qur’anic text.
