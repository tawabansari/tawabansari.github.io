The Qur’anic Root M S S

From direct contact to the experience of good, harm, and human intimacy

The Qur’anic root م س س begins with a tangible image: one thing reaches another and contact occurs between them. Its Qur’anic uses, however, extend beyond bodily contact. Fire can touch something, and one person can touch another; harm, good, punishment, old age, fatigue, and even an influence attributed to Satan can also “touch” a person.

The semantic extension is understandable. In physical contact, the distance between two things closes and one reaches the other. Language uses the same image for something that enters human experience and makes its effects felt.

The Qur’an mainly uses the verb مَسَّ, alongside يَتَمَاسَّا, the noun مَسّ, and مِسَاس in lā misās. These forms cover physical contact, sexual intercourse, the onset of a condition, and exclusion from contact. The root occurs sixty-one times: fifty-six Form I verbs, two Form VI verbs, two instances of mass, and one of misās.

The root may initially be defined as follows:

M S S: one thing reaching and coming into contact with another; by extension, an effect, condition, or consequence reaching a person. The root alone does not determine the kind or intensity of contact, or whether it is physical. Context supplies these distinctions.

Qur’anic forms

مَسَّ
Form I; 56 occurrences

يَتَمَاسَّا
Form VI; two occurrences

مَسّ
Noun; two occurrences

مِسَاس

Contact: the physical image and early Arabic usage

Arabic lexicography records the basic meaning of مَسَّ الشيءَ as touching something. Ibn Fāris locates the root’s core in feeling something with the hand; other sources similarly explain مسست الشيء through contact made by the hand or body.

Yet the older language does not restrict mass to the hand. Two bodies can be in mumāsah, touching one another. Dictionaries also record ماء مسوس for water handled by people, although other explanations of this expression concern its taste or ability to quench thirst. The first explanation is one recorded sense, not a definition covering every use of masūs.

A poem transmitted in the Dīwān of al-Aʿshā, a poet of the pre-Islamic tradition, mentions musical instruments and a ṣanj:

وَطَنَابِيرَ حِسَانٍ صَوْتُهَا
عِنْدَ صَنْجٍ كُلَّمَا مُسَّ أَرَنّ

“And sweet-sounding lutes, beside a ṣanj that rings out whenever it is touched.”

Here مُسَّ describes actual contact with the instrument, followed by a response in sound. The ṣanj is what rings out. Al-Aʿshā is a major poet of the pre-Islamic tradition, but this particular poem has not been independently dated here. It is evidence for an early concrete use, without establishing an exact date of composition.

Lexicographers also transmit the following verse under the name of Aws ibn Maghrāʾ:

مِسْنَا السَّمَاءَ فَنِلْنَاهَا وَطَاءَ لَهُمْ
حَتَّى رَأَوْا أُحُدًا يَهْوِي وَثَهْلَانَا

The opening means “We touched the sky and reached it”; the closing presents Uḥud and Thahlān, two mountains, as falling. The intervening wording varies across the available transcriptions: وطاء لهم appears in Tāj al-ʿArūs, while a transcription of al-Akhfash’s Maʿānī al-Qurʾān has وطالهم. Without resolving that textual difference, a confident translation of the complete verse would be premature. The secure point here is the hyperbolic “touching the sky” and the shortened form مِسْنا, cited by grammarians for مَسِسْنا. Aws lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods; without dating this poem, it cannot be labelled securely pre-Qur’anic.

Taken together, early Arabic evidence establishes the concrete sense of touching or reaching. This basic meaning helps explain the wider Qur’anic uses; it does not by itself establish the historical order in which every sense developed.

Physical contact and direct reaching in the Qur’an

Some Qur’anic occurrences remain close to tangible contact.

The Light Verse says of olive oil:

اللَّهُ نُورُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ ۚ مَثَلُ نُورِهِ كَمِشْكَاةٍ فِيهَا مِصْبَاحٌ ۖ الْمِصْبَاحُ فِي زُجَاجَةٍ ۖ الزُّجَاجَةُ كَأَنَّهَا كَوْكَبٌ دُرِّيٌّ يُوقَدُ مِنْ شَجَرَةٍ مُبَارَكَةٍ زَيْتُونَةٍ لَا شَرْقِيَّةٍ وَلَا غَرْبِيَّةٍ يَكَادُ زَيْتُهَا يُضِيءُ وَلَوْ لَمْ تَمْسَسْهُ نَارٌ ۚ نُورٌ عَلَىٰ نُورٍ ۗ يَهْدِي اللَّهُ لِنُورِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۚ وَيَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْأَمْثَالَ لِلنَّاسِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

“The God is the light of the heavens and the earth. The likeness of His light is a niche containing a lamp; the lamp is in glass, and the glass is as though a radiant star, lit from a blessed tree, an olive neither eastern nor western. Its oil almost shines even though no fire has touched it. Light upon light. The God guides whom He wills to His light, and The God sets forth examples for people. The God knows all things.”

An-Nur 24:35

لَمْ تَمْسَسْهُ نَارٌ means that fire has not reached the oil: contact between them has not occurred.

Al-Waqiʿah says of a protected book:

إِنَّهُ لَقُرْآنٌ كَرِيمٌ ۝ فِي كِتَابٍ مَكْنُونٍ ۝ لَا يَمَسُّهُ إِلَّا الْمُطَهَّرُونَ ۝ تَنْزِيلٌ مِنْ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

“It is indeed a noble Qur’an, in a protected book, touched only by the purified; a sending down from the Lord of all worlds.”

Al-Waqiʿah 56:77–80

The verb يَمَسُّهُ expresses touching or reaching something. Identifying the “protected book,” “the purified,” and the precise nature of this contact requires the passage’s context; the root م س س alone does not settle them. A later legal interpretation concerning contact with a physical copy of the Qur’an should therefore not be built into the lexical definition of the verb.

The she-camel of Ṣāliḥ is also mentioned:

وَيَا قَوْمِ هَٰذِهِ نَاقَةُ اللَّهِ لَكُمْ آيَةً فَذَرُوهَا تَأْكُلْ فِي أَرْضِ اللَّهِ وَلَا تَمَسُّوهَا بِسُوءٍ فَيَأْخُذَكُمْ عَذَابٌ قَرِيبٌ

“My people, this she-camel of The God is a sign for you. Leave her to feed on the earth of The God, and do not touch her with harm, lest a punishment soon seize you.”

Hud 11:64

Here بِسُوءٍ specifies the character of the contact. Mass itself is neither good nor bad; its accompanying expression and context determine that.

When a condition “touches” a person

Many Qur’anic uses no longer concern contact by a hand or body. Harm, hardship, wounds, old age, fatigue, punishment, and good can “touch” a person. Mercy should not be added to this list as a direct Qur’anic collocation: in Yunus 10:21 and Fussilat 41:50 mercy is “tasted,” while the preceding hardship is what “touched” people.

This extension remains connected to contact. The image of reaching is retained: a condition or consequence reaches someone and enters their experience.

Al-Baqarah says of earlier communities:

أَمْ حَسِبْتُمْ أَنْ تَدْخُلُوا الْجَنَّةَ وَلَمَّا يَأْتِكُمْ مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ خَلَوْا مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ ۖ مَسَّتْهُمُ الْبَأْسَاءُ وَالضَّرَّاءُ وَزُلْزِلُوا حَتَّىٰ يَقُولَ الرَّسُولُ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مَعَهُ مَتَىٰ نَصْرُ اللَّهِ ۗ أَلَا إِنَّ نَصْرَ اللَّهِ قَرِيبٌ

“Or did you suppose that you would enter the Garden before the like of what befell those before you had come upon you? Hardship and distress touched them, and they were shaken until the messenger and those who believed with him said: When will the help of The God come? Surely the help of The God is near.”

Al-Baqarah 2:214

مَسَّتْهُمُ الْبَأْسَاءُ وَالضَّرَّاءُ means that hardship and distress reached and afflicted them.

The same construction appears with old age:

قَالَ أَبَشَّرْتُمُونِي عَلَىٰ أَنْ مَسَّنِيَ الْكِبَرُ فَبِمَ تُبَشِّرُونَ

“He said: Do you bring me good news when old age has overtaken me? What good news, then, do you bring?”

Al-Hijr 15:54

Old age does not have a hand that touches the body. مسني الكبر means that it has overtaken the person and become part of his condition.

Fatigue can occupy the same grammatical position. The inhabitants of the Garden say:

الَّذِي أَحَلَّنَا دَارَ الْمُقَامَةِ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ لَا يَمَسُّنَا فِيهَا نَصَبٌ وَلَا يَمَسُّنَا فِيهَا لُغُوبٌ

“He who, through His bounty, has settled us in the lasting home: no toil touches us there, nor does weariness touch us.”

Fatir 35:35

In English, “afflict,” “overtake,” or “reach” may be natural in these contexts, although “touch” can also preserve the Arabic image when it reads clearly.

Mass is not inherently negative: good also touches a person

Frequent expressions such as مَسَّهُ الضُّرُّ، مَسَّهُ السُّوءُ، يَمَسُّهُمُ الْعَذَابُ might suggest that the root is inherently negative. Qur’anic usage shows otherwise.

Al-Anʿam says:

وَإِنْ يَمْسَسْكَ اللَّهُ بِضُرٍّ فَلَا كَاشِفَ لَهُ إِلَّا هُوَ ۖ وَإِنْ يَمْسَسْكَ بِخَيْرٍ فَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

“If The God touches you with harm, none can remove it except Him; and if He touches you with good, He has power over all things.”

Al-Anʿam 6:17

The same verb is used for harm and good.

Al-Maʿarij also says:

إِنَّ الْإِنْسَانَ خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا ۝ إِذَا مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ جَزُوعًا ۝ وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ الْخَيْرُ مَنُوعًا

“The human being was created anxious: intensely distressed when evil touches him, and intensely withholding when good touches him.”

Al-Maʿarij 70:19–21

Mass therefore does not simply mean being harmed or afflicted. Its core is neutral: something reaches a person and affects their situation.

Nor does mass alone fix the degree or intensity of the effect. Al-Anbiya says:

وَلَئِنْ مَسَّتْهُمْ نَفْحَةٌ مِنْ عَذَابِ رَبِّكَ لَيَقُولُنَّ يَا وَيْلَنَا إِنَّا كُنَّا ظَالِمِينَ

“If a mere breath of your Lord’s punishment should touch them, they would certainly say: Woe to us! We were indeed wrongdoers.”

Al-Anbiya 21:46

The small amount here is expressed particularly by نَفْحَةٌ and the construction as a whole. Mass can help evoke initial or slight contact in some contexts, but “a light touch” is not an adequate fixed definition for all its occurrences.

Fire and punishment: contact as an experience

In several verses, fire or punishment “touches” a person.

Al-Baqarah reports a claim:

وَقَالُوا لَنْ تَمَسَّنَا النَّارُ إِلَّا أَيَّامًا مَعْدُودَةً ۚ قُلْ أَتَّخَذْتُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ عَهْدًا فَلَنْ يُخْلِفَ اللَّهُ عَهْدَهُ ۖ أَمْ تَقُولُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

“They said: The Fire will touch us only for a few numbered days. Say: Have you obtained a covenant from The God, so that The God will never break His covenant? Or do you say about The God what you do not know?”

Al-Baqarah 2:80

تَمَسَّنَا النَّارُ does not merely describe momentary contact between skin and fire. The context concerns experiencing the Fire and how long that lasts. English “the Fire will touch us” can retain this sense, provided “touch” is not taken to mean only a brief or harmless encounter.

The image of contact can thus extend to something befalling a person and becoming their experience.

Al-Qamar also says:

يَوْمَ يُسْحَبُونَ فِي النَّارِ عَلَىٰ وُجُوهِهِمْ ذُوقُوا مَسَّ سَقَرَ

“On the day they are dragged through the Fire on their faces: Taste the touch of Saqar.”

Al-Qamar 54:48

Here مَسّ is a noun. “The touch of Saqar” describes contact with its effects; the preceding clause makes the severity of the experience clear. The word does not make that experience mild, even if it can draw attention to its first onset.

Mass between a woman and a man: beyond ordinary touch

One important Qur’anic branch of this root concerns sexual intercourse.

Al-Baqarah says:

لَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِنْ طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ مَا لَمْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ أَوْ تَفْرِضُوا لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً ۚ وَمَتِّعُوهُنَّ عَلَى الْمُوسِعِ قَدَرُهُ وَعَلَى الْمُقْتِرِ قَدَرُهُ مَتَاعًا بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ۖ حَقًّا عَلَى الْمُحْسِنِينَ

“There is no blame upon you if you divorce women before having intercourse with them and before assigning them a marriage payment. Provide for them fairly: the person of ample means according to his means, and the person of restricted means according to his; a duty upon those who do good.”

Al-Baqarah 2:236

The following verse says:

وَإِنْ طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ وَقَدْ فَرَضْتُمْ لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً فَنِصْفُ مَا فَرَضْتُمْ إِلَّا أَنْ يَعْفُونَ أَوْ يَعْفُوَ الَّذِي بِيَدِهِ عُقْدَةُ النِّكَاحِ ۚ وَأَنْ تَعْفُوا أَقْرَبُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ ۚ وَلَا تَنْسَوُا الْفَضْلَ بَيْنَكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

“If you divorce them before having intercourse with them, having assigned them a marriage payment, then half of what you assigned is due, unless they waive it or the one in whose hand is the marriage bond waives it. To waive is nearer to mindfulness. Do not forget generosity between you. The God sees what you do.”

Al-Baqarah 2:237

The marriage and divorce context shows that تمسوهن is not ordinary contact by the hand or body. It refers to sexual intercourse within marriage. The translation of 2:236 follows the reading concerning divorce before intercourse and before a marriage payment has been fixed; the relation expressed by أَوْ has received other grammatical explanations. That question does not change the contextual meaning of mass.

Mary’s words clarify this use from another perspective:

قَالَتْ رَبِّ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لِي وَلَدٌ وَلَمْ يَمْسَسْنِي بَشَرٌ ۖ قَالَ كَذَٰلِكِ اللَّهُ يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَاءُ ۚ إِذَا قَضَىٰ أَمْرًا فَإِنَّمَا يَقُولُ لَهُ كُنْ فَيَكُونُ

“She said: My Lord, how can I have a child when no man has had intercourse with me? He said: Thus The God creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He only says to it: Be, and it is.”

Aal Imran 3:47

The mention of a child establishes that لم يمسسني بشر concerns intercourse that could lead to a child, rather than any bodily contact with another person.

Maryam 19:20 makes this clearer by adding وَلَمْ أَكُ بَغِيًّا: “nor have I been unchaste.”

The sexual use of mass is an established idiom. Reducing it to ordinary touch and then drawing further conclusions from that reduction obscures the language of these passages.

Yatamāssā: mutual contact in marriage

In the discussion of ẓihār, Al-Mujadilah says:

وَالَّذِينَ يُظَاهِرُونَ مِنْ نِسَائِهِمْ ثُمَّ يَعُودُونَ لِمَا قَالُوا فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ يَتَمَاسَّا ۚ ذَٰلِكُمْ تُوعَظُونَ بِهِ ۚ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

“Those who pronounce ẓihār against their wives and then go back on what they have said must free an enslaved person before the two have intercourse. This is what you are admonished to do. The God is fully aware of what you do.”

Al-Mujadilah 58:3

The next verse repeats the expression:

فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ شَهْرَيْنِ مُتَتَابِعَيْنِ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ يَتَمَاسَّا ۖ فَمَنْ لَمْ يَسْتَطِعْ فَإِطْعَامُ سِتِّينَ مِسْكِينًا ۚ ذَٰلِكَ لِتُؤْمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ ۚ وَتِلْكَ حُدُودُ اللَّهِ ۗ وَلِلْكَافِرِينَ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

“Whoever cannot find one must fast for two consecutive months before the two have intercourse; whoever is unable must feed sixty people in need. This is so that you may believe in The God and His messenger. These are the limits of The God, and for the disbelievers there is a painful punishment.”

Al-Mujadilah 58:4

يَتَمَاسَّا is Form VI, with a dual subject, placing both people in the relationship. The marital and ẓihār context establishes the sexual sense. In this usage, ẓihār is a declaration comparing one’s wife to one’s mother so as to forbid marital intimacy. The translation of “go back on what they have said” follows one established interpretation of يَعُودُونَ لِمَا قَالُوا; the root M S S does not settle that separate interpretive question.

Form VI alone should not bear more weight than this. Morphology and context together express mutuality here; they do not establish a rule for every Form VI verb. The legal scope of the prohibited intimacy is also a separate issue from identifying the expression’s central sexual sense.

Satan’s touch and al-mass: the limits of a root definition

Three occurrences connect this root with Satan. Their wording must be distinguished from explanations of the phenomena described.

Al-Aʿraf says:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا إِذَا مَسَّهُمْ طَائِفٌ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ تَذَكَّرُوا فَإِذَا هُمْ مُبْصِرُونَ

“Those who are mindful, when a passing impulse from Satan touches them, remember, and at once they see clearly.”

Al-Aʿraf 7:201

In Sad, Job says:

وَاذْكُرْ عَبْدَنَا أَيُّوبَ إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ أَنِّي مَسَّنِيَ الشَّيْطَانُ بِنُصْبٍ وَعَذَابٍ

“Remember Our servant Job, when he called to his Lord: Satan has afflicted me with hardship and torment.”

Sad 38:41

Al-Baqarah says of those who consume ribā:

الَّذِينَ يَأْكُلُونَ الرِّبَا لَا يَقُومُونَ إِلَّا كَمَا يَقُومُ الَّذِي يَتَخَبَّطُهُ الشَّيْطَانُ مِنَ الْمَسِّ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا إِنَّمَا الْبَيْعُ مِثْلُ الرِّبَا ۗ وَأَحَلَّ اللَّهُ الْبَيْعَ وَحَرَّمَ الرِّبَا ۚ فَمَنْ جَاءَهُ مَوْعِظَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّهِ فَانْتَهَىٰ فَلَهُ مَا سَلَفَ وَأَمْرُهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ ۖ وَمَنْ عَادَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ ۖ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

“Those who consume ribā do not rise except as one rises whom Satan has thrown into confusion through al-mass. That is because they said: Trade is just like ribā. Yet The God has permitted trade and forbidden ribā. Whoever receives an admonition from his Lord and desists may retain what is past, and his affair rests with The God. Those who return are companions of the Fire; they remain there.”

Al-Baqarah 2:275

The lexicons record المس for a condition described as madness or mental derangement, alongside the explanation “as though the jinn had touched him.” This established sense should be acknowledged rather than reduced entirely to physical touching.

The word alone does not explain the mechanism of the condition. طائف من الشيطان, مس الشيطان, and من المس occur in different contexts. A lexical study can identify contact, affliction, and the recorded sense of derangement without treating the root as a medical, psychological, or metaphysical theory.

Neither accepting nor rejecting an explanation of a phenomenon changes the lexical evidence. Equally, a familiar historical usage should not be erased merely to make every occurrence fit a single abstract definition.

Lā misās: exclusion from contact and association

مِسَاس occurs only once in the Qur’an:

قَالَ فَاذْهَبْ فَإِنَّ لَكَ فِي الْحَيَاةِ أَنْ تَقُولَ لَا مِسَاسَ ۖ وَإِنَّ لَكَ مَوْعِدًا لَنْ تُخْلَفَهُ ۖ وَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ إِلَٰهِكَ الَّذِي ظَلْتَ عَلَيْهِ عَاكِفًا ۖ لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ ثُمَّ لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ فِي الْيَمِّ نَسْفًا

“He said: Go, then. In this life it will be yours to say: No contact. You also have an appointment that will not fail you. Look at your deity, to which you remained devoted: we shall certainly burn it and scatter it into the sea.”

Ta-Ha 20:97

لَا مِسَاسَ belongs to the mass family and negates touching or mingling: “Neither shall I have contact with another, nor another with me.” In the familiar reading with kasrah on the mīm and fatḥah on the final sīn, misās is explained as a verbal noun in a negative construction; it should not be confused with the differently vocalized مَسَاسِ, analysed as an imperative expression in other transmitted readings.

The phrase itself does not establish later accounts of a particular disease, both parties developing fever, or the fate of the Samaritan’s descendants. Those details are not stated in the Qur’an.

The wording and context indicate a condition of no contact or mingling with other people.

“No contact” preserves the concise wording. “I have no contact or association with anyone” can explain its force, without importing details absent from the passage.

M S S and L M S: neighbouring word families

Mass and lams are close in Arabic, and early dictionaries sometimes define one through the other. Both can denote direct contact, and both extend in certain contexts to relations between women and men.

Qur’anic usage does not support a simple, absolute dividing line between them.

Al-Anʿam says:

وَلَوْ نَزَّلْنَا عَلَيْكَ كِتَابًا فِي قِرْطَاسٍ فَلَمَسُوهُ بِأَيْدِيهِمْ لَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا سِحْرٌ مُبِينٌ

“Even if We sent down to you a book on parchment and they touched it with their hands, those who disbelieved would certainly say: This is nothing but manifest sorcery.”

Al-Anʿam 6:7

لَمَسُوهُ بِأَيْدِيهِمْ makes contact with the hands explicit.

Yet L M S also appears in التمسوا نورا, “seek light,” in Al-Hadid 57:13. The link with searching by touch offers a way to understand this extension; it is not proof that the verse describes a physical search by hand. The family is not restricted to simple contact.

M S S, meanwhile, ranges widely in the Qur’an from physical contact to the arrival of good, harm, punishment, old age, and fatigue.

We cannot impose a rule that lams is exclusively bodily contact and mass exclusively an immaterial effect, or consistently rank one as stronger than the other.

A defensible distinction in tendency is that L M S includes seeking something, while M S S is used very widely in the Qur’an for contact itself and the onset of an effect or condition. Neither tendency makes the two families mutually exclusive.

Their overlap must therefore remain visible: the immediate construction and context are more reliable than an absolute distinction between the two roots.

Translation proposals for Forqan

Mass is one of the words for which mechanically using “touch” in every verse can obscure the intended sense.

For physical contact, as in لَمْ تَمْسَسْهُ نَارٌ, “even though no fire has touched it” is natural. For لَا يَمَسُّهُ إِلَّا الْمُطَهَّرُونَ, “none touch it except the purified” retains the wording, leaving the referents and nature of the contact to contextual analysis. The root alone does not identify a physical copy of a book.

In expressions such as مَسَّهُ الضُّرُّ، مَسَّهُ الْخَيْرُ، مَسَّنِيَ الْكِبَرُ, and لَا يَمَسُّنَا فِيهَا نَصَبٌ, English may use “afflict,” “come to,” “overtake,” or “touch,” depending on what reaches the person and the sentence’s natural phrasing.

For تَمَسَّنَا النَّارُ, “the Fire will touch us” is idiomatic, but it must convey exposure to the Fire rather than a momentary, mild touch. “The Fire will afflict us” makes that contextual force more explicit.

In marriage and divorce passages, mass should convey the established contextual meaning of sexual intercourse. “Before you touch them” may be read as ordinary bodily contact in contemporary English and can leave the meaning unclear.

For يَتَمَاسَّا, “before the two have intercourse” expresses both the dual subject and the contextual meaning.

For لَا مِسَاسَ, “No contact,” or the explanation “neither contact with others nor their contact with him,” preserves the relevant sense.

Conclusion

The root م س س is grounded in touching and direct reaching. Arabic lexicography explains it through contact by the hand or body, while early poetry preserves this tangible use.

The Qur’an employs the image much more widely. Fire can touch oil, but harm, good, hardship, wounds, old age, fatigue, and punishment can also touch a person. In these uses, a condition or consequence has reached the person and become part of their experience.

The root is not inherently negative. The same verb accompanies harm and evil, as well as good and a favourable occurrence, as in Aal Imran 3:120. Nor does mass by itself fix the intensity of an effect. Expressions such as nafḥah and the surrounding description establish its degree; an implication of initial contact in some passages should not be turned into a universal meaning of mildness.

Between a woman and a man, mass is an established expression for sexual intercourse. Marriage, divorce, and Mary’s words clarify that sense and prevent reducing it to ordinary touch. يَتَمَاسَّا in Al-Mujadilah likewise describes the two spouses’ intimacy.

The occurrences associated with Satan require particular care. The root and its recorded usages identify contact or affliction and, in al-mass, a condition described as derangement; they do not by themselves determine its nature or mechanism. They therefore cannot settle a medical explanation, nor prove from the root alone that the effect is exclusively physical or immaterial.

لَا مِسَاسَ belongs to the same field and conveys exclusion from contact and mingling.

M S S and L M S overlap considerably, and both can express contact. Yet M S S is used especially widely in the Qur’an for an effect or condition reaching a person. A single fixed equivalent cannot convey every occurrence equally well.

M S S: one thing reaching and making contact with another; by extension, an effect, condition, or consequence reaching a person. Contact may be physical or nonphysical, welcome or unwelcome, and in some contexts an established expression for sexual intercourse. The root alone does not determine its kind, intensity, or value; these emerge from the object, accompanying expressions, and context. Established senses such as al-mass for derangement must also be recognized in their own right.
