Throwing, sending toward a target, and directing an accusation at another person

Brief root meaning: Casting or throwing something in a direction or toward a target; in extended uses, directing speech, a claim, or an accusation at someone else. Early Arabic also uses this family for shooting, hunting, traveling toward a destination, exceeding a limit, and certain successive movements.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The triliteral root R M Y is best known in Arabic for throwing and casting. Ramā means casting something from the hand or sending it in a direction. The object may be a stone, arrow, dust, or something else.

Its range is not restricted to material projectiles. Arabic also uses this verb when someone attributes a sin, offense, or reprehensible act to another.

In the first use, a material object is sent toward a target; in the second, a statement or accusation is directed at a person.

For example, رَمَاهُ بِحَجَرٍ means throwing a stone at someone, whereas رَمَاهُ بِتُهْمَةٍ means directing an accusation at them.

This extension also appears in the Qur’an.

Al-Anfāl 8:17, Al-Mursalāt 77:32, and Al-Fīl 105:4 use the verb for throwing or casting. Al-Nisāʾ 4:112 and three verses in Al-Nūr use it for attributing wrongdoing, sin, or an accusation to people.

Not every Qur’anic occurrence can therefore be translated solely as “shooting arrows” or “slandering.”

Each use must be understood through its subject, object, preposition, and context.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

R M Y occurs nine times in seven Qur’anic verses, always as the basic verb ramā:

  • يَرْمِ: Attributes or directs against another; Al-Nisāʾ 4:112.
  • رَمَيْتَ: You threw; twice in Al-Anfāl 8:17.
  • رَمَىٰ: Threw; Al-Anfāl 8:17.
  • يَرْمُونَ: Accuse; Al-Nūr 24:4.
  • يَرْمُونَ: Accuse; Al-Nūr 24:6.
  • يَرْمُونَ: Accuse; Al-Nūr 24:23.
  • تَرْمِي: Throws; Al-Mursalāt 77:32.
  • تَرْمِيهِمْ: Throws at them; Al-Fīl 105:4.

Other Arabic derivatives include رَمْي, رِمَايَة, رَامٍ, رُمَاة, رَمِيَّة, مَرْمِيّ, مَرْمَى, مِرْمَاة, مُرَامَاة, ارْتِمَاء, تَرَامِي, and إِرْمَاء, but these particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

All Qur’anic occurrences are Form I verbs. Forms such as III, VI, and IV are not used for this family in the Qur’an.

This morphological restriction does not imply a narrow semantic range: the same basic verb denotes physical throwing in one verse and an accusation in another.

2. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Ramā: Casting and throwing something

An early lexical analysis identifies one central meaning for this root:

الرَّاءُ وَالْمِيمُ وَالْحَرْفُ الْمُعْتَلُّ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ نَبْذُ الشَّيْءِ

al-rāʾu wa-al-mīmu wa-al-ḥarfu al-muʿtallu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa nabdhu al-shayʾi

Translation: “Rāʾ, mīm, and the weak consonant have one principle: casting something.”

This definition clarifies the root’s main field.

In concrete usage, something is released from a position or a person’s hand and sent in a direction.

The following construction is also recorded:

رَمَى الشَّيْءَ رَمْيًا، وَرَمَى بِهِ

ramā al-shayʾa ramyan wa-ramā bihi

Meaning: “He threw something, or cast it.”

The act may involve a hand, a bow and arrow, or another implement.

Ramy itself, however, does not necessarily establish that the projectile hits its target.

Someone may throw a stone that falls short; an archer may release an arrow that misses.

Thus, throwing and hitting are related but distinct concepts.

Ramy expresses the throwing; arrival, impact, and eventual effect must be understood from the sentence and context.

This distinction matters particularly in Al-Anfāl 8:17.

Ramy and rimāyah: Throwing and shooting

Ramy is a verbal noun of ramā, denoting throwing.

Rimāyah also denotes archery or throwing in Arabic.

Recorded verbal nouns include ramy, rimāyah, and rimāʾ.

Rāmin denotes someone who throws or shoots; its broken plural is rumāh.

Marmiyy denotes something thrown or targeted by a throw.

Marmā can denote the place or target of throwing.

These words are related but not identical in meaning.

Rāmī denotes the agent; ramy the act; marmiyy what is thrown or targeted; and marmā the location or intended target.

In hunting usage, ramiyyah is also recorded for game struck by a shot or shot at.

This noun should be distinguished from the bare verb: a use of ramiyyah may concern struck game, while ramā alone does not always guarantee a hit.

A witness from Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays describes a hunter from Banū Thuʿal:

رُبَّ رَامٍ مِنْ بَنِي ثَعْلٍ

rubba rāmin min banī thaʿlin

Translation: “Many an archer from Banū Thuʿal…”

The poem continues with animals approaching water, the hunter preparing, and his shot.

It then says:

فَهْوَ لَا تَنْمِي رَمِيَّتُهُ
مَا لَهُ لَا عُدَّ مِنْ نَفَرِهِ

fa-huwa lā tanmī ramiyyatuhu / mā lahu lā ʿudda min nafarihi

Translation: “His stricken game does not escape from sight; what a marksman—may he not be counted among his people!” The final expression has the outward form of a curse, but here conveys astonishment at his skill; it need not express a literal wish for his death.

Ramiyyah here denotes game struck by an arrow.

In hunting usage, نَمَتِ الرَّمِيَّة describes wounded game going away carrying the arrow and disappearing from the hunter’s sight; death elsewhere is also mentioned in explanations of this usage.

The poetic phrase therefore concerns the animal’s condition after being shot.

It illustrates rāmin and ramiyyah in early poetry for an archer and the game he shoots.

Ramiyyah does not itself identify a particular species.

The game could be a bird, gazelle, or another animal, identified by context.

Some lines of the poem have variant readings, but فَهْوَ لَا تَنْمِي رَمِيَّتُهُ is familiar in early transmissions.

A witness from al-Shammākh’s poetry

Al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār describes a bow:

إِذَا أَنْبَضَ الرَّامُونَ عَنْهَا تَرَنَّمَتْ
تَرَنُّمَ ثَكْلَى أَوْجَعَتْهَا الْجَنَائِزُ

idhā anbaḍa al-rāmūna ʿanhā tarannamat / tarannuma thaklā awjaʿathā al-janāʾizu

Translation: “When the archers make its string vibrate, it sings, like the lament of a bereaved woman whom funerals have pained.”

Al-rāmūn is a plural of rāmin and denotes archers.

Early explanations connect anbaḍa with moving a bowstring and producing its sound, including when no arrow is released.

The verse concerns archers and their implement, but the phrase does not necessarily establish that an arrow is being shot at that instant.

Al-Shammākh lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This passage’s date is not independently established, so it is early Arabic evidence without necessarily being pre-Qur’anic.

A familiar proverb about preparing to shoot

An Arabic proverb says:

قَبْلَ الرِّمَاءِ تُمْلَأُ الْكَنَائِنُ

qabla al-rimāʾi tumlaʾu al-kanāʾinu

Translation: “Before shooting, the quivers are filled.”

Kanāʾin is the plural of kinānah, a container for arrows.

The proverb concerns preparing the means of a task before beginning it.

Rimāʾ denotes shooting here, while preparation is understood from the whole proverb.

The root R M Y itself therefore does not mean planning or preparation.

Those ideas arise from the figurative use of a familiar scene from Arab life.

Ramy in hunting and warfare

Archery was a skill associated with hunting and warfare in pre-Islamic Arab life.

رَمَى الصَّيْدَ concerns shooting at game or casting an arrow toward it.

In warfare, رَمَى العَدُوَّ may concern shooting arrows, throwing stones, or sending other projectiles at an enemy.

This setting includes words such as rumāh for archers and murāmāh for an exchange of shots.

R M Y does not itself name warfare, hunting, or killing.

Someone may shoot for practice or throw something at an inanimate target.

The act’s value and outcome are understood through its object and context.

Ramy in speech and accusation

Early Arabic records:

رَمَى فُلَانٌ فُلَانًا بِأَمْرٍ قَبِيحٍ

ramā fulānun fulānan bi-amrin qabīḥin

Meaning: “Someone attributed something reprehensible to another.”

No material object is thrown in this use.

An accusation or unfavorable statement is directed at another person.

The conceptual relation to throwing is clear: just as an object is sent toward a target, a claim may be directed at someone.

This conceptual explanation must not become a conclusive historical claim about the origin of the accusatory sense.

The linguistic evidence establishes that both uses exist in Arabic.

In this branch, ramy may denote attributing reprehensible conduct to someone, but the accusation’s falsity is not a necessary part of the verb itself.

A claim may be true or false. Its truth, falsity, and the speaker’s responsibility require evidence and context.

The Qur’anic uses also require this distinction.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:112 expressly identifies the accused person as innocent.

Al-Nūr 24:4 addresses producing four witnesses.

Al-Nūr 24:6 examines an accusation between spouses where other witnesses are absent.

Ramy generally denotes accusation in these passages, but their conditions and consequences differ.

Further extended uses

Lexical records include رَمَى إِلَى بَلَدٍ or similar expressions for traveling or setting out toward a destination.

تَرَامَى الأَمْرُ إِلَى كَذَا can describe an affair reaching a particular result or condition.

أَرْمَى عَلَى الْخَمْسِينَ is also used for passing the age of fifty.

These uses relate to advancing, reaching a destination, or exceeding a limit.

That relationship alone does not establish every derivative’s historical meaning.

In another branch, الرَّمِيّ names rain-bearing clouds or clouds with large drops.

An early lexical explanation relates this name to portions of cloud moving or being cast toward one another.

Such an explanation does not make ramy mean raining in every Qur’anic verse.

Each Qur’anic verb’s meaning is understood through its sentence and what it concerns.

3. Physical throwing in Al-Anfāl, Al-Mursalāt, and Al-Fīl

Al-Anfāl 8:17: وَمَا رَمَيْتَ إِذْ رَمَيْتَ

Verse text:

فَلَمْ تَقْتُلُوهُمْ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ قَتَلَهُمْ وَمَا رَمَيْتَ إِذْ رَمَيْتَ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ رَمَىٰ وَلِيُبْلِيَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ مِنْهُ بَلَاءً حَسَنًا ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

fa-lam taqtulūhum wa-lākinna Allāha qatalahum wa-mā ramayta idh ramayta wa-lākinna Allāha ramā wa-li-yubliya al-muʾminīna minhu balāʾan ḥasanan inna Allāha samīʿun ʿalīmun

Al-Anfāl 8:17

Translation:

“You did not kill them, but The God killed them. You did not throw when you threw, but The God threw, and so that He might test the believers with a good trial from Him. The God is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”

This verse uses ramā three times:

Ramayta in the first negative clause.

Ramayta in idh ramayta.

Ramā in attributing the act to The God.

All three share the root and belong to Form I.

Does the verse deny the Messenger’s act of throwing?

وَمَا رَمَيْتَ إِذْ رَمَيْتَ presents an act under two different attributions.

The act is attributed to the addressee in:

إِذْ رَمَيْتَ: “When you threw.”

Another construction then denies that verb of him and attributes it to The God.

Taking both statements in precisely the same respect would make the verse appear simultaneously to affirm and deny the throw.

The sentence itself, however, indicates a distinction between the two attributions.

Idh ramayta acknowledges the Messenger’s act.

One well-known interpretation attributes to The God the effect and outcome beyond the addressee’s independent power. This explains the relationship between the clauses rather than supplying the verb’s bare lexical meaning.

Early interpretive reports describe the Messenger throwing a handful of earth or pebbles toward the enemy, with its effect realized through divine power.

Such reports can illustrate the verse, but the verse itself does not specify the projectile.

No word for dust, sand, pebbles, or arrows occurs in this sentence.

None should therefore be inserted without qualification into the basic translation.

Performing an act and realizing its outcome

Lexically, ramy denotes throwing.

The verb alone does not guarantee that the projectile reaches its target or produces the thrower’s intended effect.

This matters in Al-Anfāl 8:17.

The Messenger can perform the throw while its effect within the wider event is attributed to The God.

The verse also says earlier:

فَلَمْ تَقْتُلُوهُمْ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ قَتَلَهُمْ

fa-lam taqtulūhum wa-lākinna Allāha qatalahum

Al-Anfāl 8:17

Translation: “You did not kill them, but The God killed them.” This clause stands alongside the statement about throwing and should be read in the context of the confrontation.

The verse thus belongs to a context of confrontation and the believers’ victory.

Attributing the outcome to The God should not erase all human action: the surrounding Qur’anic discourse also addresses human acts, commands, and responsibility.

Nor does this authorize anyone to call their own violent actions directly The God’s work and disclaim responsibility.

The passage concerns a specific event and an attribution made by the Qur’an itself.

For root research, the conclusion is clear: ramy denotes throwing; its agent, the agent’s capacity, and its ultimate effect require separate contextual analysis.

Al-Mursalāt 77:32: تَرْمِي بِشَرَرٍ

Verse text:

إِنَّهَا تَرْمِي بِشَرَرٍ كَالْقَصْرِ

innahā tarmī bi-shararin ka-al-qaṣri

Al-Mursalāt 77:32

Translation:

“It casts forth sparks like a palace.”

Tarmī here is a feminine singular imperfect verb.

Its implicit subject refers to the fire indicated by the context.

Bi-shararin identifies what is cast forth: sparks.

Tarmī therefore denotes throwing or casting here.

The projectiles are not stones or arrows, but sparks of fire.

This shows that ramy is not confined to solid objects held by a human.

Sparks separating from a fire and being cast outward can also be described by this verb.

Ka-al-qaṣr then conveys their size through a comparison.

Early explanations of qaṣr here include a large building and large pieces of wood.

That disagreement does not concern the meaning of ramy.

Tarmī expresses casting; the sparks’ size and appearance come from the following comparison.

Nor does the verb determine the physical details of otherworldly fire, the sparks’ trajectories, or that world’s structure.

The linguistic image is clear, but its ultimate reality exceeds the root’s meaning.

Al-Fīl 105:4: تَرْمِيهِمْ بِحِجَارَةٍ

Verse text:

تَرْمِيهِمْ بِحِجَارَةٍ مِنْ سِجِّيلٍ

tarmīhim bi-ḥijāratin min sijjīlin

Al-Fīl 105:4

Translation:

“They pelt them with stones of sijjīl.”

Tarmīhim combines the imperfect verb tarmī with the object pronoun hum.

The pronoun refers to the people of the elephant.

The preceding verse, Al-Fīl 105:3, describes birds sent in groups.

The birds are therefore the agents of throwing in this context.

Bi-ḥijāratin identifies the means or projectile: stones.

Unlike Al-Anfāl 8:17, this verse specifies both the target and the projectile.

The target is the people of the elephant; the projectiles are stones of sijjīl.

Ramy itself supplies no information about the stones’ material, size, or mechanism of action.

Those features require the other words and relevant evidence.

Al-Fīl 105:5 describes the outcome by comparing them to eaten straw or foliage.

The throwing in verse four is thus distinct from its result in verse five.

Tarmīhim expresses the throwing; fa-jaʿalahum gives the subsequent result.

This corresponds to the distinction between act and outcome discussed for Al-Anfāl 8:17.

4. Attributing wrongdoing to another in Al-Nisāʾ 4:112

Verse text

Al-Nisāʾ 4:112

وَمَنْ يَكْسِبْ خَطِيئَةً أَوْ إِثْمًا ثُمَّ يَرْمِ بِهِ بَرِيئًا فَقَدِ احْتَمَلَ بُهْتَانًا وَإِثْمًا مُبِينًا

wa-man yaksib khaṭīʾatan aw ithman thumma yarmi bihi barīʾan fa-qadi iḥtamala buhtānan wa-ithman mubīnan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:112

Translation:

“Whoever earns a wrongdoing or sin and then attributes it to an innocent person has borne a slander and a manifest sin.”

The meaning of yarmi

Yarmi is the jussive imperfect of ramā, yarmī.

The ordinary imperfect is yarmī, but the final weak letter is omitted in this conditional construction.

Thumma connects the verb to the preceding event.

A person first incurs wrongdoing or sin and then attributes it to someone else.

Bihi refers to the wrongdoing or sin already mentioned.

Barīʾan denotes the innocent person targeted by the accusation.

Ramy here does not mean throwing a material object.

What is directed at the innocent person is the attribution of wrongdoing or sin.

Committing wrongdoing and attributing it to another

Two distinct acts appear in the verse.

First, committing wrongdoing or sin.

Second, directing it against an innocent person.

These behaviors should not be merged.

Someone may do wrong, acknowledge it, and attempt to make amends.

Attributing that wrongdoing to another constitutes an additional act.

The verse accordingly concludes that the person bears slander and manifest sin.

Attributing wrongdoing to someone else does not transfer the perpetrator’s actual responsibility.

People may be deceived into blaming the innocent, but false attribution does not change what happened.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:111 likewise says that whoever earns a sin earns it against themselves.

This continuity shows that responsibility is not erased by blaming another.

Ramy and buhtān

Ramy here denotes the act of attributing.

Buhtān identifies the grave, false accusation contrary to truth.

The two words are therefore not semantically identical.

Ramy can denote asserting a claim, but Al-Nisāʾ 4:112 makes its falsity clear by calling the accused barīʾan, innocent.

Buhtānan and ithman mubīnan then state the act’s ethical consequence.

Yarmi should therefore not automatically be translated as “slanders” in every use.

This verse explicitly expresses slander through a separate word.

A context of truth and justice

Al-Nisāʾ 4:105 directs the Messenger to judge truthfully among people and forbids advocating for the treacherous.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:107 likewise forbids defending those who betray themselves.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:112 then addresses attributing wrongdoing or sin to the innocent.

The issue is therefore more than an incorrect sentence: an accusation can place an innocent person under suspicion while the actual perpetrator evades responsibility.

This clarifies the social and legal significance of this use of R M Y.

Like a material projectile aimed at a target, an accusation is directed at someone and may harm their standing, reputation, and rights.

These consequences come from context and actual conduct; they are not all fixed components of ramy’s lexical definition.

5. Yarmūna: Accusations against chastity in Al-Nūr

Al-Nūr 24:4: Accusation and witnesses

Verse text:

وَالَّذِينَ يَرْمُونَ الْمُحْصَنَاتِ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَأْتُوا بِأَرْبَعَةِ شُهَدَاءَ فَاجْلِدُوهُمْ ثَمَانِينَ جَلْدَةً وَلَا تَقْبَلُوا لَهُمْ شَهَادَةً أَبَدًا ۚ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْفَاسِقُونَ

wa-alladhīna yarmūna al-muḥṣanāti thumma lam yaʾtū bi-arbaʿati shuhadāʾa fa-ijlidūhum thamānīna jaldatan wa-lā taqbalū lahum shahādatan abadan wa-ulāʾika humu al-fāsiqūna

Al-Nūr 24:4

Translation:

“Those who accuse chaste women and then do not produce four witnesses—lash them eighty lashes and never accept their testimony; those are the defiantly disobedient.”

Yarmūna is a third-person masculine plural imperfect verb.

Its object is al-muḥṣanāt.

The opening context of Al-Nūr concerns accusations against chastity and sexual conduct.

Yarmūna al-muḥṣanāt therefore means making accusations of sexual misconduct against chaste women.

The meaning of muḥṣanāt

Muḥṣanāt belongs to Ḥ Ṣ N, whose Qur’anic field includes protection and chastity.

It should not be restricted in every verse to married women without examining context.

Al-Nūr 24:4 chiefly concerns accusations against women’s chastity.

“Chaste women” is therefore suitable.

“Married women” could restrict the verse’s meaning without justification.

For this root study, the main point is that the accusation’s target is identified by the object.

Yarmūna alone does not specify the alleged conduct; al-muḥṣanāt and the surah’s context clarify it.

The role of four witnesses

After yarmūna, the verse states:

ثُمَّ لَمْ يَأْتُوا بِأَرْبَعَةِ شُهَدَاءَ

thumma lam yaʾtū bi-arbaʿati shuhadāʾa

Al-Nūr 24:4

Meaning that the accusers do not produce four witnesses.

This qualification is decisive.

The subsequent ruling does not arise from ramy’s lexical meaning alone; it relates to the kind of accusation and the production of witnesses.

The particular ruling of eighty lashes should therefore not be extended to every claim, criticism, or disagreement.

Criticizing an official, complaining of financial loss, or reporting an incident does not automatically fall within this particular construction simply because ordinary language may call it an accusation.

The verse concerns a particular accusation against chastity and the conditions for substantiating it.

Lacking four witnesses and establishing that a claim is false are conceptually distinct matters.

The verse addresses the consequences of an accusation for which the prescribed witnesses are not produced. Yarmūna itself does not establish that every accuser knowingly lied from the outset.

This distinction matters for ethical and legal understanding.

Al-Nūr 24:5: Repentance and reform

The following verse addresses repentance and reform.

The accusation thus matters beyond the instant of speech; subsequent behavior also counts.

The detailed legal consequences for testimony, repentance, and enforcement are separate from the root’s lexical meaning.

For this study, the relevant point is that the verb concerns accusations against chastity, for which the passage sets particular conditions and consequences.

Al-Nūr 24:6: Accusation between spouses

Verse text:

وَالَّذِينَ يَرْمُونَ أَزْوَاجَهُمْ وَلَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُمْ شُهَدَاءُ إِلَّا أَنْفُسُهُمْ فَشَهَادَةُ أَحَدِهِمْ أَرْبَعُ شَهَادَاتٍ بِاللَّهِ ۙ إِنَّهُ لَمِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ

wa-alladhīna yarmūna azwājahum wa-lam yakun lahum shuhadāʾu illā anfusuhum fa-shahādatu aḥadihim arbaʿu shahādātin bi-Allāhi innahu la-mina al-ṣādiqīna

Al-Nūr 24:6

Translation:

“Those who accuse their spouses and have no witnesses except themselves: the testimony of each is four attestations by The God that he is indeed truthful.”

Yarmūna again means accuse.

Its object, however, changes.

Al-Nūr 24:4 referred to al-muḥṣanāt.

Al-Nūr 24:6 uses azwājahum.

The accusation is thus situated within a marital relationship.

Allegation and proof

Al-Nūr 24:6 expressly states the absence of other witnesses:

وَلَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُمْ شُهَدَاءُ إِلَّا أَنْفُسُهُمْ

wa-lam yakun lahum shuhadāʾu illā anfusuhum

Al-Nūr 24:6

Translation: “And they have no witnesses except themselves.” The phrase clarifies the absence of other witnesses.

Al-Nūr 24:7 mentions the fifth attestation.

Al-Nūr 24:8 and Al-Nūr 24:9 then describe the other party’s response and attestations.

Together, these verses do not simply quote an accuser and treat the allegation as established truth without further examination.

The accused also has an opportunity to respond.

Thus, ramy here does not mean proving an offense; it is making an accusation, followed by a procedure of testimony and response.

Yarmūna azwājahum should not be generalized to every marital disagreement or complaint.

The surah’s context concerns accusations against chastity and sexual conduct.

Al-Nūr 24:23: Accusing chaste, unsuspecting, believing women

Verse text:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَرْمُونَ الْمُحْصَنَاتِ الْغَافِلَاتِ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ لُعِنُوا فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

inna alladhīna yarmūna al-muḥṣanāti al-ghāfilāti al-muʾmināti luʿinū fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati wa-lahum ʿadhābun ʿaẓīmun

Al-Nūr 24:23

Translation:

“Those who accuse chaste, unsuspecting, believing women are cursed in this world and the Hereafter, and theirs is a tremendous punishment.”

Yarmūna again denotes making an accusation.

Its object carries additional descriptions:

الْمُحْصَنَاتِ: Chaste women.

الْغَافِلَاتِ: Unsuspecting women.

الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ: Believing women.

These descriptions must not be omitted or merged into one general category.

Al-ghāfilāt here describes women unsuspecting of, and removed from, such accusations.

Al-muʾmināt describes their faith.

These features clarify the harm of the accusation in the verse’s portrayal.

Accusation and reputation

Al-Nūr 24:11, Al-Nūr 24:12, Al-Nūr 24:13, Al-Nūr 24:14, Al-Nūr 24:15, Al-Nūr 24:16, Al-Nūr 24:17, Al-Nūr 24:18, Al-Nūr 24:19, and Al-Nūr 24:20 examine circulating allegations and speech without knowledge.

Al-Nūr 24:15 describes receiving a statement from one another’s tongues and uttering what one does not know.

The harm of an accusation therefore extends beyond its first speaker.

A statement may pass from person to person and spread through society.

Just as something thrown can travel from its starting point, an accusation may extend beyond its first speaker and affect the accused person’s life.

This comparison should not become a new lexical meaning of ramy.

Ramy remains accusation here; its social spread and the responsibility of listeners and repeaters come from other passages.

Al-Nūr 24:24 also describes tongues, hands, and feet testifying about people’s deeds.

Speech and accusation consequently belong within human responsibility.

6. Related roots and interpretive questions

R M Y (ramy) and Q DH F (qadhf)

These roots overlap in throwing and making accusations.

Qadhf concerns casting and throwing in Arabic.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 contains نَقْذِفُ بِالْحَقِّ عَلَى الْبَاطِلِ.

In that context, truth is cast against falsehood and crushes it.

Physical ramy likewise denotes throwing.

Lexical explanations sometimes use qadhf and ramy to gloss each other.

In later legal and juristic language, qadhf became a familiar term concerning accusations of sexual misconduct.

That terminology does not make every Qur’anic occurrence of ramy concern sexual accusation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:112 shows that ramy can also concern attributing wrongdoing or sin to the innocent.

The accusatory field of ramy is therefore broader than one particular legal category.

R M Y (ramy) and R J M (rajm)

In physical use, rajm concerns casting stones or stoning.

The Qur’an also uses it beyond physical threats and conduct, as in رَجْمًا بِالْغَيْبِ.

In Al-Kahf 18:22, that expression concerns speculation about what is unknown.

It approaches ramy’s branch of accusation or unsupported assertion.

The roots are not nevertheless identical in basic meaning.

Physical rajm has a specific connection with stones.

Ramy may involve any suitable projectile and is not confined to stones.

Both can concern unsupported claims in verbal uses, but each expression must be understood contextually.

R M Y (ramy) and N B DH (nabdh)

Nabdh concerns casting or putting something aside.

An early lexical definition explains ramy through نَبْذُ الشَّيْءِ.

This shows overlap in the field of casting.

Nabdh can emphasize setting aside or discarding.

Ramy can, by contrast, concern sending something toward a target.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule.

Nor can all accusatory meanings be inferred from nabdh without evidence.

R M Y (ramy) and L Q Y (ilqāʾ)

Alqā is a Form IV verb from L Q Y and denotes casting, placing, or throwing in Qur’anic usage; its initial hamzah is not a root letter.

For example, Moses’s casting of the staff is expressed by this verb.

Ramy can likewise denote throwing an object.

Ilqāʾ has broad Qur’anic applications, sometimes concerning conveying speech or placing something in a situation.

Not every ilqāʾ is consequently a forceful, directed throw.

Physical ramy in the Qur’an often depicts casting something in a direction or toward a target.

This distinction should be tested against actual usage rather than made an exceptionless rule.

R M Y (ramy) and B H T (buhtān)

Buhtān concerns false attribution that places the accused in an unjust position.

Ramy and buhtān occur together in Al-Nisāʾ 4:112.

Yarmi expresses attributing wrongdoing or sin.

Buhtān clarifies that act’s falsity and gravity.

Not every ramy is therefore buhtān.

An accusation supported by clear evidence is not shown false merely by the verb of attribution.

When an innocent person is accused, however, as in Al-Nisāʾ 4:112, the verse itself identifies it as slander.

This matters when examining social and legal accusations.

R M Y (ramy) and Q W L (qawl)

Qawl is a broad term for speech.

Accusatory ramy is a particular kind of speech attributing something to another person.

Not every statement is ramy.

Someone can ask a question, report an event, or express an opinion without accusing anyone.

Attributing sin or reprehensible conduct to a particular person can be expressed by ramy in the relevant construction.

Responsibility for speech therefore depends not merely on speaking, but also on its object, content, and manner of attribution.

Does ramy necessarily mean hitting?

The act of throwing should not be equated with its outcome in either main branch.

A physical projectile may hit or miss.

An accusation may be accepted or rejected.

Someone may make a false accusation that people do not believe.

The accusation has still been made, even if it fails to achieve its speaker’s purpose.

Thus, success or failure is not a fixed component of the root’s meaning.

This fits both hunting and archery uses and the passages concerning accusations.

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan and conclusion

Suggested translations of the Qur’anic forms

يَرْمِ — Al-Nisāʾ 4:112

Suitable equivalents: “attributes” or “directs against another.”

Within the full sentence:

“Then attributes it to an innocent person.”

This makes the verbal, accusatory use clear.

رَمَيْتَ — Al-Anfāl 8:17, twice

Suitable equivalent: “you threw.”

The singular second person should be preserved.

Using the same verb twice in وَمَا رَمَيْتَ إِذْ رَمَيْتَ preserves the sentence’s distinctive structure.

رَمَىٰ — Al-Anfāl 8:17

Suitable equivalent: “threw.”

In وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ رَمَىٰ, the verb remains throwing; its attribution to The God requires contextual examination of act and outcome.

يَرْمُونَ — Al-Nūr 24:4

Suitable equivalent: “accuse.”

With al-muḥṣanāt, this concerns accusations against women’s chastity.

يَرْمُونَ — Al-Nūr 24:6

Suitable equivalent: “accuse.”

The accusation concerns spouses, followed by a procedure of testimony.

يَرْمُونَ — Al-Nūr 24:23

Suitable equivalent: “accuse.”

The descriptions al-muḥṣanāt al-ghāfilāt al-muʾmināt must remain in the translation.

تَرْمِي — Al-Mursalāt 77:32

Suitable equivalents: “throws” or “casts forth.”

The objects here are sparks of fire.

تَرْمِيهِمْ — Al-Fīl 105:4

Suitable equivalents: “pelting them” or “throwing at them.” These preserve the act of throwing; “targeting them” alone may obscure it.

The birds cast stones toward the people of the elephant in this context.

Conclusion and final definition

R M Y forms a family of meanings concerning throwing and casting.

In its primary use, something is released from a position and sent in a direction or toward a target.

The act may involve a hand, bow and arrow, or another implement.

In speech, a statement or accusation is attributed to another person. This relates conceptually to throwing without being a literal material throw.

Early Arabic records derivatives such as rimāyah, rāmin, ramiyyah, marmā, murāmāh, and tarāmī.

Uses involving destinations, movement between places, exceeding limits, and certain cloud names illustrate the wider family.

Not all these uses should be assigned as direct meanings to every Qur’anic derivative.

The root occurs nine times in seven Qur’anic verses.

Al-Anfāl 8:17 repeats it three times. A well-known interpretation distinguishes the addressee’s throw from its power and effect attributed to The God; this distinction interprets the sentence rather than changing the verb’s lexical meaning.

In Al-Mursalāt 77:32, fire casts forth sparks.

In Al-Fīl 105:4, birds cast stones toward the people of the elephant.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:112, ramy means attributing wrongdoing or sin to the innocent.

Al-Nūr 24:4, Al-Nūr 24:6, and Al-Nūr 24:23 use the same verb for accusations against chastity.

Both physical and verbal applications thus occur in the Qur’an.

Final definition of R M Y:

In its principal field, the root denotes casting or throwing something from a position in a direction or toward a target.

In its extended verbal use, it denotes directing speech, a claim, or an accusation at another person.

Both involve something directed toward a target, but this conceptual relationship should not become a conclusive history of every meaning’s origin.

The root alone does not establish a hit, success, harm, falsity, or a realized outcome.

In physical throwing, the target and effect come from the sentence.

In accusation, its kind, truth or falsity, and the accuser’s responsibility require evidence and context.

The two main Qur’anic branches are therefore:

1. Physical ramy: Throwing or casting, as with sparks and stones.

2. Verbal ramy: Attributing an accusation or reprehensible conduct to another person.

The root itself does not assign a single ethical value to every use.

Throwing may be permissible, defensive, or harmful in different circumstances; a claim may be true or false.

What matters is the agent, object, evidence, circumstances, and actual consequences.

For Forqan’s research and translation, the most suitable short meaning is:

“Throwing and casting toward a target; in speech, directing an accusation at another.”

This preserves the linguistic relationship between Qur’anic uses without imposing a particular verse’s sense, such as sexual accusation or stone throwing, on the entire family.
