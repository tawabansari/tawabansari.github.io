Echo, thirst, turning toward, and clapping: a family with several distinct semantic branches

The Arabic family Ṣ D Y includes words used in several different domains: thirst and the need for water; echo and returning sound; turning toward and attending to something; clapping and making sound with the hands; and certain names and expressions concerning the head, birds, and the dead.

This family matters because its uses, unlike those of many roots, cannot readily be traced to one original image or meaning. In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris expressly notes the semantic distance between its uses.

Under the usual morphological classification, the root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in two forms: تَصَدَّىٰ in ʿAbasa 80:6 and تَصْدِيَةً in Al-Anfāl 8:35.

In ʿAbasa, تَصَدَّىٰ means turning toward and giving someone attention; تَصْدِيَةً in Al-Anfāl concerns clapping and producing sound with the hands.

Lexicons also discuss a possible historical relationship to the doubled root Ṣ D D for both forms. Listing them under Ṣ D Y in a Qur’anic index does not by itself prove that all their forms originally shared one distinct triliteral root.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two occurrences in two morphological forms

1. Semantic branches in Arabic

Does Ṣ D Y have one central meaning?

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris describes this family as:

كَلِمٌ مُتَبَاعِدَةُ الْقِيَاسِ

kalimun mutabāʿidatu al-qiyāsi

That is, words whose semantic relationships and analogies lie far apart.

He then examines a bird name, the head and brain, echo, thirst, care of property, turning toward, and clapping under this entry.

This is methodologically significant. Some uses may have intelligible relationships: turning toward something may relate to facing it, or clapping to reflected sound. Such resemblances do not prove that every word arose from one historical meaning.

The branches should therefore be examined separately, accepting only relationships supported by linguistic evidence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: a family of semantically distant uses

Ṣadā: echo and returning sound

One familiar meaning of صَدَى is sound that returns after encountering a mountain, rock, or other surface and is heard again.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn explains الصَّدَى as sound returning among mountains or in a similar setting, heard as a counterpart of the original sound.

For example, if someone shouts toward a mountain and shortly afterward hears a sound resembling the shout, Arabic calls that returning sound ṣadā.

Two elements matter here: the original sound and the sound returning after it.

The word does not itself necessarily express a physical theory of reflected sound waves. Its linguistic meaning is the experience of hearing a returning sound.

Nor should ṣadā be equated with every loud sound: echo concerns the return or repeated hearing of a sound, rather than loudness alone.

Ṣadā: thirst

Another important branch is صَدًى, meaning thirst, especially intense thirst.

Arabic records:

صَدِيَ يَصْدَى صَدًى

ṣadiya yaṣdā ṣadan

That is, someone became thirsty or experienced thirst.

صَدْيَان also describes a thirsty person.

Lexicons record صَدٍ and صَادٍ for someone thirsty, and صَدْيَا or صَادِيَة as feminine forms.

Some accounts distinguish degrees of thirst expressed by these forms. The distinctions are not consistent across all sources, however, and no fixed numerical degree can be assigned to each form.

Thirst here is the body’s actual need for water, rather than necessarily an emotional or spiritual longing.

Literary language may use thirst as an image of need or longing, but such an extension must be established from context.

Ṣādiyah and ṣawādī: palms and their relation to water

Some Arabic uses apply الصَّوَادِي to a group of date palms.

Lexicographers transmit two different explanations of the expression.

One describes palms that have received no water and remain thirsty.

The other describes palms whose roots have reached water and which no longer need ordinary irrigation.

The word is also recorded for tall palms.

These differences show why not every use should be explained by thirst simply because it belongs to that family. Plant type, water conditions, and local usage may help determine the meaning.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Kitāb al-ʿAyn: thirst, echo, and palms

Ṣadā: the head, brain, and the dead in old beliefs

Lexicons also record صَدَى for the brain or the part of the head associated with hearing.

Arabic expressions such as صَمَّ صَدَاهُ can concern a person’s death or falling silent.

Some explanations relate this to the silence of someone’s echo: the dead no longer speak for their voice to return.

Another explanation connects it to the head or seat of hearing.

Lexicons also report pre-Islamic Arab beliefs about a bird called ṣadā or hāmah.

According to one such belief, a bird emerged from the head or remains of an unavenged murder victim and kept crying until revenge was taken.

This report matters for the history of usage. It should not be presented as a natural fact or a teaching affirmed by the Qur’an.

Ṣadā here is a name within a set of Arabic beliefs and expressions; Ṣ D Y does not generally mean birds arising from the dead.

Ṣadā māl: knowledge of managing property

Lexicons also record the expression صَدَى مَالٍ.

It described someone knowledgeable and skilled in managing and caring for property, especially camels.

The subject here is competence in caring for possessions, rather than echo or thirst.

Ibn Fāris includes this among the family’s different meanings.

Without independent historical evidence, it cannot be concluded that property management arose from attention, thirst, or echo. The attested meaning should be retained without presenting an uncertain historical relationship as established.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the head, birds, and care of property

2. Early Arabic and poetic evidence

Echo in Imruʾ Al-Qays’s poetry

A verse attributed to Imruʾ Al-Qays, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, reads:

صَمَّ صَدَاهَا وَعَفَا رَسْمُهَا
وَاسْتَعْجَمَتْ عَنْ مَنْطِقِ السَّائِلِ

ṣamma ṣadāhā wa-ʿafā rasmuhā / wa-istaʿjamat ʿan manṭiqi al-sāʾili

Translation: “Its echo fell silent and its traces faded; it remained mute before the questioner’s speech.”

The poet describes a dwelling or campsite from which the signs of life have departed.

صَدَاهَا can refer to the echo or sound once heard from that place.

The poetic image is of a silent place that gives no answer.

The verse appears in Imruʾ Al-Qays’s collected poetry and early commentaries. Its image should not be taken as a physical claim that echoes permanently cease in a place: it poetically describes a site where its former inhabitants are no longer heard. Ibn Al-Anbārī’s commentary also explains ṣamma ṣadāhā as an imprecation against the place, so the declarative rendering above is not the phrase’s only reading.

The witness shows that returning sound was familiar in early Arabic and used in literary imagery.

Ibn Al-Anbārī’s commentary: Imruʾ Al-Qays’s witness and its optative reading

Turning toward in Al-Rāʿī Al-Numayrī’s poetry

Al-Rāʿī Al-Numayrī, a poet of the early Islamic period, says:

تَصَدَّى لِوَضَّاحِ الْجَبِينِ كَأَنَّهُ
سِرَاجُ الدُّجَى تُجْبَى إِلَيْهِ السَّوَائِرُ

taṣaddā li-waḍḍāḥi al-jabīni ka-annahu / sirāju al-dujā tujbā ilayhi al-sawāʾiru

An approximate rendering: “Turned toward the radiant-browed one, as though he were a lamp in the darkness toward which the traveling caravans converge.”

تَصَدَّى here concerns turning toward and attending to someone.

This relates to giving attention or presenting oneself before someone.

Arabic تَصَدَّى لَهُ can mean placing oneself before someone or coming forward to attract that person’s attention.

Al-Rāʿī Al-Numayrī was an Islamic-period poet, however. His verse supplies evidence of classical Arabic usage, rather than definite evidence from before the Qur’an’s revelation.

The distinction matters: a word’s occurrence in Islamic-period poetry attests its usage without establishing precisely when it arose.

Al-Rāʿī Al-Numayrī’s poem: the witness for turning toward

Thirst in Arabic poetry

Arabic poetry also uses صَادٍ, صَدْيَان, and صَوَادِي for thirst in people and animals.

For example, Dhū Al-Rummah uses صَوَادِيَ الْهَامِ in describing a state involving thirst and need for water.

This attests the word in the description of a living creature’s bodily state.

Dhū Al-Rummah was also an Islamic-period poet; his poetry should not be presented as pre-Islamic evidence.

Taken together, early and lexical witnesses support branches involving thirst, echo, and turning toward. They do not establish one definite historical path tracing them all to a single initial meaning.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: Dhū Al-Rummah’s witness for thirst

3. First Qur’anic use: ʿAbasa 80:6

فَأَنْتَ لَهُ تَصَدَّىٰ

fa-anta lahu taṣaddā

Translation: So you turn toward him and give him attention.

This verse occurs near the beginning of ʿAbasa, in a passage comparing conduct toward two kinds of recipient.

ʿAbasa 80:5 mentions someone who considers himself self-sufficient or is in a position of self-sufficiency.

ʿAbasa 80:6 describes the addressee’s conduct toward that person: فَأَنْتَ لَهُ تَصَدَّىٰ.

ʿAbasa 80:7 recalls that the addressee is not responsible for that person’s failure to purify himself.

By contrast, ʿAbasa 80:8 mentions someone who comes striving; ʿAbasa 80:9 describes his reverent fear, and ʿAbasa 80:10 describes inattention toward him.

تَصَدَّىٰ thus belongs to a contrast in conduct: attention and turning toward one person, as opposed to inattention toward another who has come to receive the message.

The meaning of تَصَدَّىٰ

Lisān al-ʿArab records تَصَدَّى لِلشَّيْءِ for turning toward something and placing oneself before it.

In some uses, a person raises the head or chest and attends to something.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains ʿAbasa 80:6 through turning toward and attending to the person.

تَصَدَّىٰ here therefore expresses more than a passing glance: it describes giving attention and presenting oneself before someone.

The verb does not necessarily mean honoring, obeying, or accepting that person’s words.

Someone may turn toward another and devote time to guidance, conversation, or invitation.

Whether that attention is appropriate in a particular situation depends on the overall conduct and circumstances, rather than on the root alone.

The role of لَهُ

In فَأَنْتَ لَهُ تَصَدَّىٰ, lahu refers to the person receiving attention.

The preposition li- marks the direction of the attention or turning toward.

“You give him attention” is therefore a natural rendering.

The preposition does not establish ownership, obedience, or full agreement with the person’s position.

The verse describes turning toward and attending; it does not necessarily place the addressee under the other person’s authority.

Does taṣaddī always mean intervening in others’ affairs?

In modern Persian, taṣaddī kardan often means assuming responsibility for a task or administering an affair.

The Arabic تَصَدَّىٰ in this verse, however, means turning toward and attending to someone.

That differs from the modern Persian administrative use of taṣaddī-ye omūr.

“You take responsibility for him” or “you manage his affairs” would therefore add a meaning absent from the direct construction.

Is attending to a self-sufficient person inherently wrong?

No.

تَصَدَّىٰ carries no fixed moral condemnation in itself.

ʿAbasa criticizes differing treatment of recipients in a particular situation.

One person has come to receive the message, striving and showing reverent fear, while attention is directed toward another described as self-sufficient.

The issue is assessing conduct through the recipient’s actual need, readiness to listen, and responsibility in conveying the message.

The verse does not establish that speaking with a wealthy, influential, or self-sufficient person is always wrong.

Nor does تَصَدَّىٰ alone reveal precisely what motivated the addressee or what inward intention was being pursued.

The passage places this conduct alongside its opposite and clarifies attention and priorities.

Al-Ṭabarī: turning toward and attention in ʿAbasa 80:6

4. Morphology and possible derivation of تَصَدَّىٰ

In its Qur’anic use, تَصَدَّىٰ is an indicative imperfect addressed to a singular second person.

The usual analysis treats it as Form V, تَتَصَدَّىٰ, with one of the two initial tāʾs omitted for ease of pronunciation.

Reading traditions also record a form with doubled ṣād, explained by assimilation of one tāʾ into ṣād.

The larger question, however, concerns its historical root.

First possibility: relation to Ṣ D D (صَدَّ)

Some grammarians and lexicographers derive تَصَدَّىٰ from تَصَدَّدَ.

In that analysis, one repeated dāl becomes a weak consonant, producing taṣaddā.

The explanation relates to ṣadad in the sense of the direction or side opposite someone.

Arabic records:

دَارِي صَدَدَ دَارِهِ

dārī ṣadada dārihi

That is, “My house is opposite his house.”

On this account, taṣaddā may originally concern placing oneself before someone or turning toward that person.

This is semantically defensible, since attending to someone relates to facing that person.

Second possibility: relation to Ṣ D Y (صَدَى)

Other sources list the form directly under Ṣ D Y.

This family records تَصَدَّى for turning toward and attending to something.

Some have tried to connect it to ṣadā as thirst, likening an attentive person to someone thirsty who approaches water.

That explanation primarily proposes an interpretive image.

The resemblance alone is insufficient to prove that taṣaddā historically derived from thirst.

It is therefore preferable to retain the established sense of turning toward and giving attention, while avoiding unwarranted certainty about its exact historical origin.

Al-Qurṭubī: consonant omission, assimilation, and proposed derivations of taṣaddā

5. Second Qur’anic use: Al-Anfāl 8:35

وَمَا كَانَ صَلَاتُهُمْ عِنْدَ الْبَيْتِ إِلَّا مُكَاءً وَتَصْدِيَةً فَذُوقُوا الْعَذَابَ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَكْفُرُونَ

wa-mā kāna ṣalātuhum ʿinda al-bayti illā mukāʾan wa-taṣdiyatan fa-dhūqū al-ʿadhāba bi-mā kuntum takfurūna

Translation: Their salat at the House was nothing but whistling and clapping. So taste the punishment for your disbelief.

تَصْدِيَةً is a verbal noun here, occurring alongside مُكَاءً.

Arabic مُكَاء denotes whistling or producing a whistle-like sound.

The predominant lexical and exegetical explanation takes تَصْدِيَة as clapping.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn explains it as striking one hand against the other to produce sound.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains taṣdiyah as clapping and transmits numerous early exegetical reports supporting that meaning.

Does taṣdiyah mean clapping?

Arabic records صَدَّى يُصَدِّي تَصْدِيَةً for clapping.

The use concerns the hands striking each other and the sound produced by that action.

Taṣdiyah in this sense is more than a silent movement or raising the hands: contact between the hands creates sound.

Both kaf-zadan and dast-zadan are natural Persian equivalents.

Dast-zadan can also mean touching or undertaking an action in other Persian combinations. Kaf-zadan therefore identifies clapping more clearly.

Does taṣdiyah mean echo?

Some lexical explanations connect taṣdiyah with ṣadā as returning sound.

Clapping, in this analysis, produces sound by bringing the hands together.

A visual or conceptual analogy can also be drawn between returning sound and repeated clapping sounds.

The Qur’anic taṣdiyah nevertheless names a particular human action: clapping.

“Echo” alone would be imprecise because the verse concerns a human action, rather than merely natural sound reflection in a setting.

Echo is one meaning within the lexical family, but it is not the preferred meaning of taṣdiyah here.

Does taṣdiyah mean preventing access to the House?

Some exegetical reports relate taṣdiyah to ṣadd as preventing.

On that reading, the criticized group’s conduct concerns barring people from the House or preventing them from following the way of The God.

The possibility relates to Al-Anfāl 8:34, which mentions preventing access to the Sacred Mosque.

Context and derivation must nevertheless be distinguished.

Al-Ṭabarī raises a specific morphological point: if taṣdiyah were formed directly as a Form II verbal noun from doubled Ṣ D D, the expected form would be تَصْدِيد, rather than تَصْدِيَة.

Arabic does attest the replacement of one repeated consonant by a weak consonant in some forms. A historical relationship cannot therefore be ruled out entirely.

That morphological possibility alone is insufficient to prefer “preventing” to “clapping.”

Clapping, moreover, has direct lexical support as a meaning of taṣdiyah.

Clapping is thus preferred in Al-Anfāl 8:35; preventing access to the Sacred Mosque is conduct independently expressed in the preceding verse.

The relationship of salat to mukāʾ and taṣdiyah

صَلَاتُهُمْ refers here to the conduct of a particular group at the House.

The actions mukāʾ and taṣdiyah then describe what they had put in the place of salat.

Early exegetical reports differ over how and under what circumstances this conduct occurred. Some mention whistling and clapping during circumambulation; others relate them to disrupting the prophet and his followers.

Those historical reports should not be added to the lexical meaning of taṣdiyah without independent examination.

The verse itself describes the criticized group’s conduct at the House through whistling and clapping.

Al-Anfāl 8:34 presents the same group as preventing access to the Sacred Mosque and claiming its guardianship.

The verse thus criticizes their conduct and religious claim.

The mention of clapping in this setting does not establish that every instance of clapping, at every time and place, is inherently forbidden or incompatible with worship.

Nor does this verse alone restrict the entire Qur’anic reality of salat to a collection of movements or sounds.

Taṣdiyah names a particular action; the Qur’anic assessment of this group comes from that action together with the context, their religious claim, and their conduct.

Al-Ṭabarī: clapping and the proposed derivation from ṣadd

6. The morphology of تَصْدِيَةً

تَصْدِيَة is the Form II verbal noun of صَدَّى.

It appears in the accusative and is conjoined with مُكَاءً.

In this construction, صَلَاتُهُمْ is the subject of kāna, and مُكَاءً وَتَصْدِيَةً occupies its predicate position.

The two verbal nouns thus characterize the group’s action.

Lexicons attest the verb ṣaddā for clapping, but that verb itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

Only its verbal noun, taṣdiyatan, occurs, in Al-Anfāl 8:35.

Its structure differs from taṣaddā in ʿAbasa 80:6.

Taṣaddā is a Form V verb, whereas taṣdiyah is a Form II verbal noun.

The morphological difference matters: shared letters and outward resemblance do not justify translating both as “attending” or both as “clapping.”

7. Other important Arabic forms

صَدِيَ and صَدْيَان

صَدِيَ is recorded for becoming thirsty, and صَدْيَان for a thirsty person.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Other Arabic words, including ʿaṭash and ẓamaʾ, can also express thirst. Semantic proximity does not make their roots identical.

صَدَى and أَصْدَاء

In its echo sense, ṣadā denotes returning sound.

Its plural is أَصْدَاء.

Elsewhere, the same form occurs as a bird name or in concepts concerning the head and the dead.

The meaning of aṣdāʾ in an Arabic text must therefore be determined from context.

أَصْدَى

أَصْدَى الْجَبَلُ is recorded for a mountain returning sound.

The verb here concerns echo.

In other expressions, the form is also related to the cessation of an echo or to death.

Sharing a verb does not make death and echo identical meanings in every use.

صَادَى and مُصَادَاة

Arabic صَادَى فُلَانًا is recorded for facing, countering, or treating someone correspondingly or reciprocally.

Some sources also explain it through gentleness, accommodation, and conciliatory treatment.

These uses show the importance of response and facing another in this branch.

Muṣādāh does not mean sound reflection or clapping in every sentence.

تَصَدَّى

تَصَدَّى لِلشَّيْءِ means turning toward something, presenting oneself before it, or attending to it.

Some uses also concern entering into a task or preparing to deal with it.

The form occurs in ʿAbasa 80:6 and expresses giving someone attention.

Modern Persian taṣaddī has also acquired administrative senses involving responsibility. Those later senses should not be imposed on the verse without evidence.

صَدَّى and تَصْدِيَة

In its familiar use, ṣaddā means clapping.

Taṣdiyah is its verbal noun.

That form occurs in Al-Anfāl 8:35.

Lexicons discuss a possible historical relationship with Ṣ D D, but clapping is clear as an independently attested linguistic use.

Ṣadā as a particular name or description

As recorded in early sources, ṣadā is also used for certain birds and in Arab beliefs concerning the dead.

These uses should be understood in their historical and linguistic settings.

They should not supply a new meaning for Qur’anic taṣaddā or taṣdiyatan without clear independent evidence.

Arabic lexicons: distinctions between forms and non-Qur’anic uses

8. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ D Y (صَدَى) and Ṣ D D (صَدَّ)

The families are close in form and in some uses.

In its main Qur’anic branch, Ṣ D D concerns turning away and preventing.

Qur’anic Ṣ D Y, however, concerns attending and clapping.

Lexicons suggest that taṣaddā or taṣdiyah may originally have arisen from forms with repeated dāl.

That possibility should be retained without becoming historical certainty.

In ʿAbasa 80:6, giving attention relates to facing a person.

In Al-Anfāl 8:35, clapping concerns the hands coming together to produce sound.

By contrast, صَدُّوا عَنِ السَّبِيلِ concerns preventing progress along a way or turning away from it.

These three uses should therefore remain distinct in translation.

Study of Ṣ D D

Ṣ D Y (صَدَى) and Ṣ W T (صَوْت)

صَوْت is the general Arabic word for sound.

In one branch, ṣadā denotes a sound that returns and is heard again.

Every echo is thus sound, but not every sound is an echo.

Taṣdiyah can also produce sound, but Qur’anically it names the act of clapping, rather than all sounds.

Ṣ D Y (صَدَى) and Ṣ F Q (صَفَقَ)

Arabic صَفَّقَ describes clapping and bringing the hands together.

Ṣaddā has a very similar meaning in uses concerning the hands.

Some dictionaries explain taṣdiyah directly through taṣfīq.

Ṣ F Q also has other uses concerning striking and contact.

The families therefore overlap in clapping without sharing all their branches.

Ṣ D Y (صَدَى) and ʿ Ṭ SH (عَطَش)

ʿAṭash is an Arabic word for thirst.

Ṣadā also relates to thirst, sometimes intense thirst, in one branch of its family.

Some sources assign ṣadā greater intensity than ordinary thirst.

That grading is not fixed across all uses.

A permanent numerical hierarchy should therefore not be constructed between the two words.

Nor should thirst be imposed on Qur’anic taṣaddā. Formal resemblance and a possible emotional analogy do not justify changing the verse’s clear meaning.

Ṣ D Y (صَدَى) and Ṣ D ʾ (صَدَأ)

Ṣ D ʾ, with hamzah, is a separate root.

Arabic ṣadaʾ concerns iron rust and certain color characteristics.

The Persian equivalent zang-zadegī likewise expresses rusting.

Ṣadā, by contrast, means echo or thirst in its familiar branches.

Their written and phonetic resemblance does not establish definite historical kinship or equal meanings.

Ṣ D Y (صَدَى) and Ṣ D F (صَدَفَ)

Arabic صَدَفَ عَنْ concerns turning or withdrawing from something.

Qur’anic تَصَدَّى لَهُ concerns turning toward and giving someone attention.

The actions can oppose each other in direction: one turns toward someone, while the other turns away from something.

The semantic contrast does not imply historical derivation of one from the other.

ʿAbasa 80:6 concerns attention to a person; يَصْدِفُونَ in Al-Anʿām 6:46 describes turning away from the signs.

Differences in root, construction, and preposition should therefore be preserved in translation.

Study of Ṣ D F

9. Challenges in understanding and translation

The main difficulty is trying to produce one definition covering every derivative.

Defining Ṣ D Y solely as returning sound, for example, does not adequately explain thirst or attending to a person.

Defining it solely as attention excludes clapping and echo.

Deriving all forms from Ṣ D D as preventing would impose an unwarranted restriction on the clear sense of taṣaddā in ʿAbasa and the sound-related taṣdiyah in Al-Anfāl.

The family’s multiple branches should therefore be accepted, examining the actual construction in each use.

Does taṣdiyah mean obstruction?

That meaning is proposed as an exegetical possibility in Al-Anfāl 8:35, but clapping has more direct lexical support.

The verse places mukāʾ alongside taṣdiyah, both referring to actions involving sound.

Replacing “clapping” with “preventing worship” is therefore unsuitable without explanation and sufficient evidence.

Conversely, the group’s obstructive conduct should not be reduced entirely to clapping: Al-Anfāl 8:34 describes preventing access to the Sacred Mosque.

Both statements can occur in one context without one word fully containing the other’s meaning.

Does taṣaddā mean thirsting for attention?

Some lexical explanations liken the person turning toward someone to a thirsty person longing for water.

The image may help explain intense attention, but the word in ʿAbasa 80:6 does not itself mean being thirsty.

“You thirst for his attention” or “you turn toward him out of longing” would add a claim about the addressee’s inward state.

The direct meaning is turning toward and attending to the person. The verb alone does not establish the degree of longing or inward motivation.

Is every instance of clapping condemned?

Taṣdiyah lexically means clapping.

The condemnation in Al-Anfāl 8:35, however, concerns a particular group’s conduct in a religious and social setting.

An action’s occurrence in a context of condemnation does not establish that it is inherently improper in every other situation.

Such a ruling would require an independent, clear command or prohibition.

Lexical study should therefore explain meaning without creating a general ruling that the word and verse themselves do not state.

10. Translation suggestions for Forqan

In ʿAbasa 80:6:

فَأَنْتَ لَهُ تَصَدَّىٰ

fa-anta lahu taṣaddā

Preferred rendering:

“So you turn toward him and give him attention.”

The shorter “So you give him attention” is also valid, but “turn toward” conveys facing and directing oneself toward the person more clearly.

In Al-Anfāl 8:35:

إِلَّا مُكَاءً وَتَصْدِيَةً

illā mukāʾan wa-taṣdiyatan

Preferred rendering:

“Nothing but whistling and clapping.”

This keeps the actions distinct without adding motivation, historical detail, or moral judgment to their direct meanings.

In non-Qur’anic uses, ṣadā in its acoustic sense can be translated “echo”; in its thirst sense, “thirst” or “intense thirst.”

Ṣadyān likewise means thirsty.

Depending on context, muṣādāh can be rendered “facing,” “reciprocal treatment,” or “conciliatory treatment.”

Uses concerning birds, the head, or the dead require each expression’s particular meaning; they should not be forced into identity with echo or thirst.

11. Conclusion

Ṣ D Y is an Arabic family with several branches for which one definite historical core cannot be established across every use.

Early sources relate it to thirst, returning sound, attention and turning toward, clapping, certain head and bird names, and skill in managing property.

Ibn Fāris likewise emphasizes the distance between these uses. Proposed semantic developments should be supported by historical and linguistic evidence, rather than by images constructed retrospectively between words.

Under the usual classification, the Qur’an contains only two forms from this family.

Taṣaddā in ʿAbasa 80:6 means turning toward and giving someone attention.

Taṣdiyatan in Al-Anfāl 8:35 concerns clapping and making sound with the hands.

Lexicons discuss a possible historical relationship with Ṣ D D for both forms. That possibility should not change their clear contextual meanings in the Qur’an.

Short definition: Ṣ D Y is a family with several branches expressing thirst, echo, turning toward, and clapping in different uses, alongside other restricted Arabic meanings. These branches cannot be reduced to one meaning without evidence. Brief root meaning: Several branches: thirst, echo, turning toward and attending, and clapping.

In the Qur’anic uses, taṣaddā is best rendered “you turn toward and give attention,” and taṣdiyatan “clapping.”

The family is best understood by acknowledging its distinct attested uses, rather than seeking one image that appears to explain every meaning.
