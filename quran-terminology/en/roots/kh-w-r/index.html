From the lowing of cattle and calves to weakness and softness: the calf’s sound in the story of Moses, and the distinction between sound, speech, and guidance

In Arabic, KH W R has two prominent semantic fields: animal sound, especially the lowing of cattle and calves; and weakness, slackness, and reduced firmness or strength. Early sources also record related words for soft ground, flexible objects, and features of coasts and seas.

In the sound branch, khuwār names the call produced by cattle and calves. Other usages include the calls of certain animals and even an arrow’s sound as it travels through the air. Khuwār is thus a recognized sound term, rather than a name for every sound, utterance, or human song.

In the second branch, khawar concerns weakness and slackness. A person who has lost strength and endurance, or a spear lacking firmness under pressure, can be described through this family.

The Qur’an uses only the sound branch. In two verses, khuwār describes the sound of the calf made among Moses’ people.

The key distinction is that having a sound is not identical to being able to speak, understand, respond, or guide. After mentioning the calf’s khuwār, the Qur’an emphasizes this distinction: the calf had a sound, but did not speak to the people, show them a way, or control benefit or harm for them.

1. Forms and semantic cores

KH W R is a triliteral root with a medial wāw: its second radical is wāw, which becomes alif in some inflected forms.

Its main Arabic forms include:

  • Khāra–yakhūru: to call or bellow, especially cattle, calves, and certain other animals.
  • Khuwār: the familiar lowing of cattle and calves; in some uses, other animal calls.
  • Khāra–yakhūru: in another branch, to become weak or slack.
  • Khawira–yakhwaru: to undergo weakness, slackness, or a decline in strength.
  • Khawar: weakness, slackness, and inability to maintain firmness.
  • Khāʾir: someone or something that has become weak or slack.
  • Khawwār: of people and some objects, very weak or lacking firmness; of certain animals, qualities such as softness, flexibility, or abundant milk.
  • Khaʾūr: weak and slack.
  • Khūr: a plural recorded for some forms concerning weakness, softness, and animal qualities.
  • Khawwārah: in particular uses, soft, level ground, a plentiful milk producer, or something soft and flexible.
  • Khawwara: to attribute weakness to someone or describe them as weak and slack.
  • Khawr: geographically, a depression, water channel, or inlet into which seawater extends.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an contains only khuwārun, in two verses: Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 and Ṭā Hā 20:88.

In both, khuwār is a verbal noun of khāra–yakhūru in the sound branch. It functions in the sentence to identify the calf’s sound.

Three similar forms must be distinguished.

Khuwār, with ḍammah on khāʾ, concerns an animal call.

Khawar, with fatḥah on khāʾ and wāw, concerns weakness and slackness.

Khawwār, with doubled wāw, often describes a weak person or something lacking firmness.

Their similar spelling should not make their meanings identical without examining vocalization and context.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two occurrences of the verbal noun khuwār

Two principles in Ibn Fāris’s account

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two principles for this root:

الْخَاءُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالرَّاءُ أَصْلَانِ: أَحَدُهُمَا يَدُلُّ عَلَى صَوْتٍ، وَالْآخَرُ عَلَى ضَعْفٍ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-rāʾu aṣlāni aḥaduhumā yadullu ʿalā ṣawtin wa-al-ākharu ʿalā ḍaʿfin

Translation:

“Khāʾ, wāw, and rāʾ have two principles: one indicates sound, the other weakness.”

This matters because, unlike roots for which he proposes a shared meaning, Ibn Fāris identifies two independent semantic fields here.

His example for the first branch is khāra al-thawru yakhūru: the bull lowed.

For the second, he gives rumḥun khawwārun and arḍun khawwāratun: a weak spear and soft ground.

These branches do not establish that a bovine call originally meant weakness, or that human weakness developed from the sound of cattle. Such a history would require independent evidence.

The fields of sound and weakness should therefore remain distinct, even though some subsidiary uses overlap in softness and reduced resistance.

2. Khuwār: animal calls in early Arabic

The lowing of cattle and calves

In familiar Arabic usage, khuwār is the sound of cattle and calves.

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes Al-Layth:

الْخُوَارُ صَوْتُ الثَّوْرِ وَمَا اشْتَدَّ مِنْ صَوْتِ الْبَقَرَةِ وَالْعِجْلِ.

al-khuwāru ṣawtu al-thawri wa-mā ishtadda min ṣawti al-baqarati wa-al-ʿijli

That is:

“Khuwār is the bull’s sound and the forceful call of the cow and calf.”

This definition associates the word with a particular bovine sound, which can be prominent or loud.

Persian and Dari equivalents include māq kashīdan, “to moo,” and bāng-e gāv, “a bovine call.” For Qur’anic translation, “a bovine call” or “the calf’s sound” is clearer and more natural.

Khuwār does not itself mean speaking words. Cattle can express hunger, discomfort, excitement, or another condition through their calls, but such a sound is linguistically distinct from human speech with meaningful, reasoned sentences.

Qur’anic khuwār should therefore not be translated as “speaking” without evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Layth’s account and bovine sound

Evidence from the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

A verse attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd reads:

فَلَيْتَ لَنَا مَكَانَ الْمَلْكِ عَمْرٍو
رَغُوثًا حَوْلَ قُبَّتِنَا تَخُورُ

fa-layta lanā makāna al-malki ʿamrin / raghūthan ḥawla qubbatinā takhūru

Translation:

“Would that, instead of King ʿAmr, we had a nursing ewe calling around our tent.”

The verse belongs to a satire about ʿAmr ibn Hind and relations at his court.

Raghūth here concerns a nursing ewe. In a disparaging comparison, the poet wishes for a useful milk animal in place of the king.

The important lexical form is takhūru.

Khuwār primarily names a bovine sound, but Ṭarafah uses it for a sheep’s call.

Commentators on his poetry note this extension, explaining that the poet applies the familiar cattle sound word to a sheep.

The witness shows that poetic khuwār can extend beyond its main referent without becoming a general word for all sounds.

The dairy animal’s economic value and the criticism of the king come from the poetic image as a whole, rather than from takhūru itself.

Tāj al-ʿArūs, R GH TH: Ṭarafah’s witness and the nursing ewe

Other animal calls and the sound of arrows

Ibn Sīdah records khuwār for cattle, sheep, gazelles, and arrows.

Poetry attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar compares arrows sounding as they travel through the air with the calls of animals that have young.

The lexical quotation reads:

يَخُرْنَ إِذَا أُنْفِذْنَ فِي سَاقِطِ النَّدَى
وَإِنْ كَانَ يَوْمًا ذَا أَهَاضِيبَ مُخْضِلَا

yakhurna idhā unfidhna fī sāqiṭi al-nadā / wa-in kāna yawman dhā ahāḍība mukhḍilā

The image continues:

خُوَارَ الْمَطَافِيلِ الْمُلَمَّعَةِ الشَّوَى
وَأَطْلَائِهَا صَادَفْنَ عِرْنَانًا مُبْقِلَا

khuwāra al-maṭāfīli al-mulammaʿati al-shawā / wa-aṭlāʾihā ṣādafna ʿirnānan mubqilā

Translation of the sense:

“The arrows sound when released through falling moisture and rain, even on a day of drenching showers, like the calls of mothers with young and mottled limbs, and of their offspring, who have found ʿIrnān green with vegetation.”

The poet compares the sound of passing arrows with animal calls.

Transmissions differ in some words and in the arrangement of the hemistichs, but the lexical sources clearly attest khuwār for arrows and the comparison with animal sounds. The translation above covers both couplets. ʿIrnān is a place name, rather than simply a general word for pasture. The quotation retains unfidhna from Lisān al-ʿArab; other transmissions have unfizna.

Khuwār therefore need not always come from an actual animal’s throat. Poetic language can use it for a comparable sound.

This figurative usage does not automatically make Qur’anic khuwār the sound of a manufactured tool or device.

The Qur’an uses it of a calf described as a body. How its sound arose is a separate question from the lexical meaning of khuwār.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Aws ibn Ḥajar’s couplets and the arrows’ sound in rain

3. Khawar: weakness, declining strength, and softness

Human weakness and loss of firmness

The root’s second branch concerns weakness and slackness.

Arabic uses khāra al-rajulu or khawira al-rajulu for a person losing strength and firmness.

Rajulun khawwārun likewise describes someone weak or lacking sufficient endurance in difficulty.

Lexically, however, weakness can be physical or behavioral.

Someone may lose bodily strength through fatigue or illness. In another situation, someone may fail to stand firm under pressure or danger.

All uses of khawar should therefore not be restricted to moral timidity or cowardice.

Some poetic witnesses contrast khawar with courage and endurance; other uses concern wood, spears, ground, or animals without a moral judgment.

Evidence from Al-Ṭirimmāḥ’s poetry

A verse attributed to the Umayyad poet Al-Ṭirimmāḥ ibn Ḥakīm reads:

أَنَا ابْنُ حُمَاةِ الْمَجْدِ مِنْ آلِ مَالِكٍ
إِذَا جَعَلَتْ خُورُ الرِّجَالِ تَهِيعُ

anā ibnu ḥumāti al-majdi min āli mālikin / idhā jaʿalat khūru al-rijāli tahīʿu

Translation:

“I am a son of the guardians of glory from the house of Mālik, when fainthearted men fall into alarm.”

Khūru al-rijāli describes weak, unsteady men who lose firmness in fear and danger.

Lexical commentary identifies the plural’s singular as khawwār.

Tahīʿu likewise concerns fear and confusion in this verse.

The contrast highlights the firmness of the poet’s family against the weakness of others.

The dīwān and lexical sources differ over some words, including the first part and the verb ending the second hemistich. The quotation here follows the familiar wording in Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab.

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ belongs to the Islamic period. This witness matters for early literary Arabic, but should not be presented as pre-Islamic poetry.

Maqāyīs and Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Ṭirimmāḥ’s witness and the plural khūr

A weak spear or arrow

Arabic rumḥun khawwārun describes a spear lacking firmness.

Sahmun khawwārun is also recorded for a weak arrow with little resistance.

Weakness here concerns a tool’s material properties, rather than morality or fear.

A spear bending excessively under pressure or unable to maintain the necessary firmness is called khawwār.

Slackness can thus describe reduced resistance and stability in an object.

Khawwār does not always mean broken or destroyed. The object may still exist and even remain usable, while being less firm than desired.

Soft ground with little resistance

Lexicons use arḍun khawwāratun for soft, yielding ground.

Such ground lacks the hardness and resistance of rocky or compacted terrain.

This connects with weakness and reduced firmness, but soft ground is not necessarily undesirable.

It may suit some activities and not others.

Khawwārah ground should therefore not always be rendered as ruined or useless.

Softness and flexibility: favorable uses of khawwār

Lisān al-ʿArab records uses of khawwār that have favorable or at least neutral associations, unlike a weak man or flimsy spear.

Nāqatun khawwāratun, for example, describes a she-camel producing abundant milk. The same description is recorded for a ewe yielding plentiful milk.

Ibn Fāris explains that hardness and constriction have departed in this usage, leaving a softer condition.

Farasun khawwāru al-ʿināni describes a horse whose neck and movement are supple and that is easily guided.

Softness here need not be blameworthy weakness; it can be a desirable animal quality.

Bakratun khawwāratun also describes a wheel whose axle turns easily. This concerns smooth, easy movement in a tool, rather than necessarily undesirable slackness or damage.

These uses show that reduced hardness and resistance are not always equivalent to inability or worthlessness.

Khawar may be undesirable in a frightened person, while softness can benefit a milk animal or an implement that needs flexibility.

The distinction follows from the kind and function of the thing described.

Lisān al-ʿArab: soft ground, milk animals, horses, and a wheel

4. Khawr: low ground and seawater extending inland

Arabic sources also record khawr, with unvoweled wāw, for coastal and marine features.

One definition in Lisān al-ʿArab is:

الْخَوْرُ: مَصَبُّ الْمَاءِ فِي الْبَحْرِ

al-khawru maṣabbu al-māʾi fī al-baḥri

That is: “Khawr is where water flows into the sea.”

Shamir offers another explanation: a tongue of seawater extending inland.

Geographical khawr can therefore denote a water channel, a small gulf, or a coastal depression into which water enters.

Khawr is also recorded for low, depressed ground between two heights.

A rajaz passage attributed to Al-ʿAjjāj describes a ship’s movement:

إِذَا انْتَحَى بِجُؤْجُؤٍ مَسْمُورِ
وَتَارَةً يَنْقَضُّ فِي الْخُؤُورِ
تَقَضِّيَ الْبَازِي مِنَ الصُّقُورِ

idhā intaḥā bi-juʾjuʾin masmūri / wa-tāratan yanqaḍḍu fī al-khuʾūri / taqaḍḍiya al-bāzī mina al-ṣuqūri

Translation:

“As it turns with its nailed breast or forward hull, at times it swoops into coastal inlets and channels like a hunting hawk.”

Al-khuʾūr is the plural of khawr here, concerning inland extensions of water or coastal channels. Juʾjuʾ evokes the ship’s breast or forward section, rather than a human forehead. The plural is also written with hamzah on wāw: khuʾūr.

Al-ʿAjjāj is a poet of the Islamic period, so this witness belongs to early Arabic of the Umayyad era.

This geographical usage should be recorded alongside sound and weakness without assuming, in the absence of evidence, that it necessarily derives from a bovine call or human weakness.

Depressed ground, advancing water, and a low location form a distinct spatial field.

Khawr in this sense is also distinct from Qur’anic khuwār. Their vowels and contexts distinguish the meanings.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān, and Tāj: khawr, khuʾūr, and Al-ʿAjjāj’s witness

5. Qur’anic usage: a calf with khuwār

Al-Aʿrāf 7:148: a body with a bovine call

وَاتَّخَذَ قَوْمُ مُوسَىٰ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ مِنْ حُلِيِّهِمْ عِجْلًا جَسَدًا لَهُ خُوَارٌ أَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّهُ لَا يُكَلِّمُهُمْ وَلَا يَهْدِيهِمْ سَبِيلًا اتَّخَذُوهُ وَكَانُوا ظَالِمِينَ

wa-ittakhadha qawmu mūsā min baʿdihi min ḥuliyyihim ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwārun a-lam yaraw annahu lā yukallimuhum wa-lā yahdīhim sabīlan ittakhadhūhu wa-kānū ẓālimīna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“After his departure, the people of Moses took from their ornaments a calf, a body with a bovine call. Did they not see that it did not speak to them or show them a way? They took it, and they were wrongdoers.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:148

Khuwārun follows ʿijlan jasadan in this verse.

ʿIjl denotes a calf, while jasad identifies its body or bodily form.

Study of ʿ J L; Study of J S D

The verse then says:

لَهُ خُوَارٌ

lahu khuwārun

That is: “It had a call” or “It had a sound like bovine lowing.”

Grammatically, lahu is the fronted predicate and khuwārun the delayed subject. The sentence describes a characteristic of the calf.

Khuwār here names a kind of sound, rather than an animal, a living being, or an act of speaking.

Significantly, Moses’ people fashioned the calf from their ornaments. The verse thus concerns something connected with materials belonging to the people.

The presence of khuwār does not itself prove that the body had become a living animal with all the bodily characteristics of cattle.

Nor does the calf’s being made from ornaments alone establish precisely how its sound was produced.

The Qur’an’s statement is clear: there was a body in calf form that had khuwār.

Lahu khuwārun does not itself explain how the sound arose.

Khuwār and speaking

The same verse continues:

أَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّهُ لَا يُكَلِّمُهُمْ وَلَا يَهْدِيهِمْ سَبِيلًا

a-lam yaraw annahu lā yukallimuhum wa-lā yahdīhim sabīlan

This phrase is particularly important for understanding the root.

The Qur’an affirms khuwār while pointing out that the calf does not speak to the people.

If khuwār here were equivalent to kalām and meaningful conversation, the contrast would be unclear.

The calf had a call, but did not speak to them.

An animal call can naturally indicate its condition. Producing sound alone, however, does not mean being able to converse, answer questions, or guide others.

After denying speech, the Qur’an also denies guidance:

وَلَا يَهْدِيهِمْ سَبِيلًا

wa-lā yahdīhim sabīlan

The criticism therefore does not rest merely on silence or inability to produce sentences. It concerns something taken as a deity that shows the people no way.

The verse distinguishes a thing’s striking appearance or sound from its fitness to be a deity and provide guidance.

This follows from the verse’s construction, rather than from khuwār’s lexical meaning alone.

Ṭā Hā 20:88: khuwār and the claim to divinity

فَأَخْرَجَ لَهُمْ عِجْلًا جَسَدًا لَهُ خُوَارٌ فَقَالُوا هَٰذَا إِلَٰهُكُمْ وَإِلَٰهُ مُوسَىٰ فَنَسِيَ

fa-akhraja lahum ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwārun fa-qālū hādhā ilāhukum wa-ilāhu mūsā fa-nasiya

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Then he brought out for them a calf, a body with a bovine call. They said: ‘This is your deity and the deity of Moses,’ but he forgot.”

Ṭā Hā 20:88

The same construction occurs here:

عِجْلًا جَسَدًا لَهُ خُوَارٌ

ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwārun

Khuwār thus has the same meaning in both verses.

The context in Ṭā Hā supplies further details of the event.

Ṭā Hā 20:85 describes the Samaritan leading Moses’ people astray.

Ṭā Hā 20:87 and Ṭā Hā 20:88 discuss the ornaments the people carried and the Samaritan’s role in bringing out the calf.

A claim about the body then follows:

هَٰذَا إِلَٰهُكُمْ وَإِلَٰهُ مُوسَىٰ

hādhā ilāhukum wa-ilāhu mūsā

That is: “This is your deity and the deity of Moses.”

The sequence places the calf’s sound within an event that led to the people’s misguidance and the attribution of divine status to a body.

The verse does not explicitly say that everyone believed in it solely because they heard khuwār.

The calf’s sound is one characteristic; the full causes and circumstances of the people’s misguidance require the whole story. Commentators also differ over the pronoun and the attribution of fa-nasiya to Moses or the Samaritan. The quoted rendering “he forgot” leaves the pronoun open, and that question is separate from khuwār’s meaning.

In Ṭā Hā 20:90, Aaron tells the people they have been tested through it and that their Lord is Al-Raḥmān.

Khuwār thus belongs to a setting of testing and religious deviation, but the word itself does not mean testing or misguidance.

Al-Ṭabarī: the ornaments and differing referents of fa-nasiya

Ṭā Hā 20:89: inability to respond

The next verse states:

أَفَلَا يَرَوْنَ أَلَّا يَرْجِعُ إِلَيْهِمْ قَوْلًا وَلَا يَمْلِكُ لَهُمْ ضَرًّا وَلَا نَفْعًا

a-fa-lā yarawna allā yarjiʿu ilayhim qawlan wa-lā yamliku lahum ḍarran wa-lā nafʿan

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Do they not see that it returns no speech to them and controls neither harm nor benefit for them?”

Ṭā Hā 20:89

This clarifies khuwār’s contextual meaning.

The calf had a sound but returned no meaningful response when people addressed it.

Lā yarjiʿu ilayhim qawlan emphasizes its inability to return speech.

The verse then denies its control over benefit or harm.

Three characteristics should be distinguished across these verses.

First, having khuwār, a sound characteristic.

Second, speaking and responding, which the verses deny of the calf.

Third, guiding and controlling benefit or harm, also denied of it.

These concepts are not synonymous.

Something can produce sound without meaningful speech. A being might also speak without that ability alone proving its fitness to guide or be worshipped.

The Qur’an therefore does more than deny a sound characteristic: it confronts the claimed divine status with the body’s actual condition.

Al-Ṭabarī: returning no speech and control over benefit and harm

Was the calf’s khuwār natural or manufactured?

Historical and exegetical accounts give different explanations of how its sound was produced.

Some describe the calf becoming a living being.

Others describe air entering the body and emerging as a bovine sound.

Neither mechanism is explicitly explained in the two Qur’anic occurrences of khuwār.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 and Ṭā Hā 20:88 describe a body with a bovine call. The following passages discuss its inability to speak, guide, or control benefit and harm.

Ṭā Hā 20:97 also describes destroying what the Samaritan and his people had taken as a deity.

These indications matter for understanding the religious claim and the body’s status, but do not establish a technical mechanism for producing sound.

Linguistically, khuwār names a sound, rather than explaining its origin.

Just as Arabic poetry can compare an arrow’s sound to an animal call, an animal sound word does not necessarily prove that the sound came from a living animal’s throat.

That poetic comparison likewise does not establish that the Samaritan’s calf necessarily produced its sound through a mechanical device.

The verses’ direct meaning should therefore remain separate from possible explanations of sound production.

Fatḥ al-Bayān: differing accounts of the sound’s origin

Sound and identifying truth

The story presents people with a body possessing a tangible, attention catching characteristic: a calf’s call.

The Qur’an directs their attention beyond that characteristic: does the body speak, show a way, or control benefit and harm?

The issue is therefore more than an astonishing phenomenon. It involves attributing status and capacities to something that lacks them.

A broader principle follows from the context: one perceptible characteristic, such as sound, does not by itself establish every other characteristic or qualification.

This conclusion should remain proportionate to the evidence. The story directly concerns the calf and its claimed divine status, rather than denying the value of all sounds, tools, or unusual phenomena.

The criticism concerns making a sounding body into a deity and source of guidance.

6. Distinctions from related roots

KH W R (khuwār) and Ṣ W T (ṣawt): bovine lowing and sound generally

Arabic ṣawt means sound broadly.

Human and animal sounds, and certain other phenomena, can be called ṣawt.

Khuwār primarily names a particular sound: the lowing of cattle and calves.

Every khuwār is therefore an audible ṣawt, but not every ṣawt is khuwār.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 and Ṭā Hā 20:88, khuwār suits the calf form.

Saying only that the body “had a sound” would leave its kind unclear. Khuwār evokes the familiar bovine call.

KH W R (khuwār) and Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣayḥah): an animal call and a cry

Ṣayḥah concerns a cry or shout and sometimes denotes an intense sound in the Qur’an.

Khuwār, however, concerns a particular animal call.

They can overlap in loudness without having identical referents.

In Yā Sīn 36:29, for example, ṣayḥah occurs in the setting of a people’s destruction. In the calf story, khuwār names the sound of the calf body.

Their possible loudness does not make them synonyms.

KH W R (khuwār) and R GH W (rughāʾ): cattle and camel calls

Arabic rughāʾ names a camel’s call.

Khuwār primarily names the call of cattle and calves.

Arabic thus had distinct words for different animal sounds.

Poetry may use one in comparing another animal’s sound, but such an extension does not erase their main naming distinctions.

KH W R (khawar) and Ḍ ʿ F (ḍaʿf): slackness and weakness generally

Ḍaʿf covers a broad field of inability and declining strength.

Khawar also concerns weakness, while often emphasizing slackness, lack of firmness, and loss of resistance.

Rumḥun khawwārun concerns a flimsy spear; khūru al-rijāli concerns weak, unsteady people in difficulty.

Khawar cannot replace every use of ḍaʿf without regard to context.

Qur’anic ḍaʿf appears in physical, social, and other circumstances, whereas the weakness branch of KH W R does not occur in the Qur’an.

KH W R (khawar) and W H N (wahn): declining strength and slackness

Wahn also concerns weakness and slackness.

Luqmān 31:14 uses wahnan ʿalā wahnin in describing a mother’s condition.

Lexical khawar can describe personal weakness or reduced firmness in an object.

The roots overlap, but their actual usages do not justify claiming that one always denotes a more severe weakness or a later stage of the other.

Nor should Qur’anic khuwār be identified with wahn: in the calf story, khuwār belongs to the sound branch.

KH W R (khawar) and KH R R (khurūr): weakness and falling

Qur’anic KH R R includes falling and collapse.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:143, for example, describes Moses through kharra mūsā ṣaʿiqan, “Moses fell unconscious.”

KH W R is a different root.

Weakness may cause someone to fall, but weakness and falling are not one concept.

Khawar as weakness should therefore not be assigned the same root as kharra, “fell.” Likewise, the calf’s khuwār has no direct lexical connection with falling to the ground.

Study of KH R R

KH W R (khuwār) and K L M (kalām): sound and speech

The Qur’an itself emphasizes this difference.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:148, the calf has khuwār, but lā yukallimuhum: it does not speak to them.

Ṭā Hā 20:89 likewise says that it returns no speech.

Khuwār concerns an animal call, while kalām and qawl in this setting concern meaningful speech and response.

This does not mean every nonhuman sound lacks all information. It shows that producing sound is not equivalent to the meaningful dialogue and guidance expected of a deity.

That distinction is central to both Qur’anic occurrences of khuwār.

Study of K L M; Study of Q W L

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

In both Qur’anic verses, khuwār concerns the calf’s sound.

Natural Persian and Dari equivalents include “a bovine call,” “the calf’s sound,” and colloquial māq kashīdan, “mooing.”

“A bovine call” is precise and clear for Qur’anic translation because it preserves the sound’s kind.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:148, the phrase:

عِجْلًا جَسَدًا لَهُ خُوَارٌ

ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwārun

Can be rendered:

“A calf, a body with a bovine call.”

This accords with khuwār’s familiar Arabic meaning.

Ṭā Hā 20:88 contains the same phrase, allowing the same meaning to be retained.

“A body with a call” conveys the general sense, but does not make khuwār’s particular sound as explicit as “a bovine call.”

“A living calf that mooed,” “a statue with a sound producing mechanism,” and “a calf that spoke” all go beyond the word’s direct meaning.

A basic translation should not substitute any of these possibilities for khuwār.

Brief root meaning

In one branch, an animal call, especially cattle and calf lowing; in another, weakness, slackness, and reduced firmness.

Meaning in the verses

Khuwārun — Al-Aʿrāf 7:148: a cattle or calf call, the sound heard from the calf body made among Moses’ people.

Khuwārun — Ṭā Hā 20:88: a cattle or calf call, the sound of the body the Samaritan brought out for the people.

The sound meaning is preferred in both verses. There is no indication favoring a translation of khuwār as weakness, inability, or softness.

Nor does the word itself establish that the body was alive or how its sound was produced.

Conclusion

KH W R has two main Arabic semantic fields: sound and weakness.

In the first, khuwār is the familiar lowing of cattle and calves. Arabic poetic witnesses show extended uses for other animal calls and even arrow sounds.

In the second, khawar concerns slackness and reduced firmness. It can describe a weak person, a flimsy spear, and soft ground. In certain animal descriptions, softness and flexibility can be desirable rather than blameworthy.

Geographical khawr is also recorded for depressed ground and coastal channels or inlets. It should not be reduced to sound or weakness without evidence.

Only khuwār occurs in the Qur’an, twice in the story of the calf of Moses’ people.

It denotes the calf’s sound, rather than speech or possession of knowledge and guidance.

After mentioning khuwār, the Qur’an describes that body’s inability to speak, return a response, guide, or control benefit and harm.

Qur’anic khuwār should therefore be understood as “a cattle or calf call.” Its connection with the people’s deception, the body’s claimed divine status, and the criticism of that claim emerges from the verses together, rather than from khuwār’s lexical meaning alone.

This distinction preserves familiar Arabic usage and the context’s central conclusion: hearing a sound does not by itself prove the ability to speak or guide, or fitness to be worshipped.
