1. Introducing the Root

The three-consonant Arabic root J N D concerns gathering, organization, and assistance. Its principal word, jund, denotes forces gathered to support a person, ruler, group, or objective. In its familiar military use it means an army or host, but its semantic range extends beyond armed soldiers.

In early Arabic, jund can also mean helpers and supporters. Some usages also call a distinct category of created beings a jund.

This distinction matters. Every crowd is not a jund. People who happen to gather in a market do not become an army or a mutually supporting force merely through their numbers. The principal use involves some companionship, connection, organization, or common direction alongside gathering.

Conversely, being a jund does not itself establish whether a group serves truth or falsehood. The Qur’an speaks of Pharaoh’s and Iblīs’s hosts as well as Solomon’s hosts and forces attributed to The God.

A jund’s moral value therefore comes from its leadership, objective, conduct, and context, rather than from the word itself.

Another important point is that jund does not necessarily designate one soldier. It is a collective noun in Arabic and can denote a body of forces. Junūd is its plural and can refer to several armies, several bodies of forces, or the forces attached to one commander.

Arabic also forms jundī to designate an individual belonging to an army, but this form does not occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an uses J N D 29 times in 27 verses across 18 surahs. Every occurrence belongs to the nominal family of jund. Verbs such as jannada and tajannada do not occur in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus:29 occurrences in27 verses

The Qur’anic usages fall into several settings: human armies such as those of Saul, Goliath, and Pharaoh; Solomon’s organized hosts of jinn, humans, and birds; groups gathered against believers; Iblīs’s hosts; and forces attributed to The God, some unseen by humans.

Translation should therefore retain the shared sense of a gathered, supporting force while preserving the differences between physical human armies, accompanying groups, and divine hosts. Brief root meaning: Forces gathered for assistance, support, and joint action; in military use, an army.

2. The Semantic Core in Early Arabic

Gathering and Assistance

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْجِيمُ وَالنُّونُ وَالدَّالُ يَدُلُّ عَلَى التَّجَمُّعِ وَالنُّصْرَةِ.

al-jīmu wa-al-nūnu wa-al-dālu yadullu ʿalā al-tajammuʿi wa-al-nuṣrati

Translation: “Jīm, nūn, and dāl indicate gathering and assistance.”

He then adds:

يُقَالُ هُمْ جُنْدُهُ، أَيْ أَعْوَانُهُ وَنُصَّارُهُ.

yuqālu hum junduhu ay aʿwānuhu wa-nuṣṣāruhu

Translation: “One says, ‘They are his jund,’ meaning his helpers and supporters.”

Maqāyīs al-Lughah:gathering and assistance

This explanation identifies two features.

First, the forces have gathered as a body.

Second, that body assists and supports a person or objective.

Both features fit the military use of jund. An army consists of individuals and groups gathered for a common objective who may act under a particular command.

The same principle can also occur in broader uses. A group supporting a leader or idea may figuratively be called that leader’s or idea’s jund, even if its members are not all professional soldiers.

Nevertheless, Ibn Fāris’s definition does not establish that every kind of social cooperation is indiscriminately called jund.

Several people may cooperate to build a house, for example, without ordinarily being considered one another’s army or jund.

The familiar use emphasizes force, support, and readiness to act.

Jund: Helpers and an Army

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْجُنْدُ: الْأَعْوَانُ وَالْأَنْصَارُ.

al-jundu al-aʿwānu wa-al-anṣāru

Translation: “Jund means helpers and supporters.”

It continues:

وَالْجُنْدُ: الْعَسْكَرُ، وَالْجَمْعُ أَجْنَادٌ.

wa-al-jundu al-ʿaskaru wa-al-jamʿu ajnādun

Translation: “Jund also means an army, and its plural is ajnād.”

Lisān and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ:helpers,army,and plurals

These definitions show two closely related Arabic uses: the broader sense of supporting forces and the familiar sense of a military army.

An army is a particularly clear instance of jund, but assistance and support are not confined to a military structure.

This breadth matters in the Qur’an. Pharaoh’s hosts are forces attached to his rule in a historical situation. In “the hosts of the heavens and the earth,” however, the word extends beyond a human army.

Both involve forces and a capacity for action, but the forces are not of the same kind.

Does Jund Mean Only an Armed Group?

In military use, jund generally includes forces organized to fight or support warfare.

The broader meanings “helpers” and “supporters,” however, show that it can also describe those providing assistance and support.

Such support may occur during war, but assistance itself is not always confined to killing or using weapons.

An army may include, alongside combatants, people who supply provisions, move equipment, maintain matériel, or support other forces.

These examples help explain the word’s range, but should not be presented as a definitive organizational inventory for every army at the time of revelation.

In military use, jund consequently denotes the body of forces attached to a common undertaking, rather than necessarily every individual directly engaged in fighting.

3. Principal Forms and Morphological Uses

Jund: A Collective Noun

Jund is a collective noun.

It can denote a body of forces without naming each member separately.

An illustrative Arabic sentence is:

“هَذَا جُنْدٌ قَدْ أَقْبَلَ” (hādhā jundun qad aqbala).

That is: “Here is an army that has arrived.”

Although the army consists of many individuals, jund is singular in form in this sentence.

The same feature appears in Ṣād 38:11, where jundun takes the singular description mahzūmun.

Its singular form therefore does not establish that only one soldier is meant.

Junūd: The Plural of Jund

Junūd is a familiar plural of jund and the most frequent form of this family in the Qur’an.

The plural can designate several armies or bodies of forces.

Al-Baqarah 2:249, for example, concerns Saul’s and Goliath’s troops.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:9 uses junūd twice: first for forces that came against the believers, then for forces The God sent that the believers did not see.

In such uses, the forces’ nature and role emerge from context.

The plural junūd itself specifies neither the number of people nor the number of groups or military units in an army.

Ajnād: Another Plural

Ajnād is another plural of jund.

Lexical sources record both junūd and ajnād.

Some Arabic plural patterns can be associated with smaller or larger numbers, but such distinctions should not be imposed on every use without contextual evidence.

Junūd occurs in the Qur’an; ajnād does not.

Discussion of the two plurals thus belongs to Arabic linguistic research and does not license adding a specified troop count to verses.

Jundī: An Individual Attached to a Jund

Jundī is a relational form denoting a person belonging to an army.

In present-day Arabic it means a soldier.

Its usual modern plurals include jundiyyūn and junūd.

In the history of the word’s formation, however, jund fundamentally designates the body, while jundī identifies an individual attached to it.

A verse using jund should therefore not be translated as “one soldier” without supporting evidence.

Jannada and Tajnīd

Jannada is a Form II verb concerning the gathering and organization of forces.

Tajnīd is its verbal noun.

In modern usage, tajnīd denotes recruiting soldiers, organizing military forces, or enlisting people for a particular objective.

Modern uses, such as administrative terminology in contemporary armies, did not necessarily exist in precisely the same form in revelation-era Arabic.

Early sources also use jannada al-junūda for gathering forces.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Tajannada: Gathering Forces

Arabic uses of tajannada concern procuring forces or preparing as a jund.

The verb can describe a person or group placing itself at the service of an undertaking or body.

Its modern sense of formally enrolling in an army should not, however, be attributed to every historical period without investigation.

Mujannad: Gathered and Organized

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

جُنْدٌ مُجَنَّدٌ: مَجْمُوعٌ.

jundun mujannadun majmūʿun

Translation: “A jund mujannad is an assembled army.”

Lisān al-ʿArab:jund mujannad

Mujannad here emphasizes the body’s gathering and organization.

The description itself does not determine whether that army fights for truth or falsehood.

That judgment concerns the forces’ objective and conduct.

4. Nonmilitary and Historical Uses of the Family

Janad: Hard, Stony Ground

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris reports another use of these consonants:

وَالْجَنَدُ: الْأَرْضُ الْغَلِيظَةُ فِيهَا حِجَارَةٌ بِيضٌ.

wa-al-janadu al-arḍu al-ghalīẓatu fīhā ḥijāratun bīḍun

Translation: “Janad is hard ground containing white stones.”

Here the vowels differ from those of jund, “army.”

The subject is a kind of hard or stony ground.

Ibn Fāris does not assert a definite relationship between this usage and gathering or assistance. He allows that it may belong to the same family or result from consonantal substitution in another root.

This caution matters.

Shared consonants alone do not establish that the name for an army originally came from the name for stony ground.

Some sources suggest such a connection, but Ibn Fāris does not present it as the word’s certain history.

Root research should therefore distinguish jund from janad and not elevate their possible historical relationship beyond the evidence.

Ibn Fāris:janad and caution about derivation

Jund as a Military District

Early Islamic administrative language also used jund for military and administrative divisions of certain territories.

A familiar example is ajnād al-Shām.

Geographical and historical sources list five well-known Syrian districts: Damascus, Ḥimṣ, Qinnasrīn, Jordan, and Palestine.

In this usage, jund does not merely name soldiers. It can designate a district to which military forces belonged and in which their administration and payments were organized.

This territorial sense is understandable in relation to the word’s military use.

The administrative division should not be attributed to pre-Islamic times or the period of revelation without historical evidence for that particular period.

This use also does not occur in the Qur’an.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ:five Syrian districts and Al-Farazdaq’s witness

Geographical Names

Janad is also recorded as the name of a district in Yemen.

Jundaysābūr is the name of a historical city.

Ajnādayn is a place in the Syrian region known from the history of the early Islamic conquests.

These names belong to linguistic and geographical history, but every proper name should not be derived from “army” without documentation.

A word resembling jund in a geographical name does not itself establish that the place was originally a military camp.

Junayd, Junād, and Junādah

Arabic sources record these forms as names of persons or groups.

They show that the lexical family can also be used in naming.

A proper name does not always retain the word’s general meaning in ordinary speech.

A personal name such as Junayd consequently establishes neither the number of military forces nor its bearer’s position in an army.

5. Poetic Witnesses from Late Pre-Islamic and Early Islamic Arabic

Jundu Kisrā in Al-Aʿshā’s Poetry

Al-Aʿshā Maymūn ibn Qays, a prominent poet of late pre-Islamic Arabia, says in a poem about the Arabs’ confrontation with Sasanian forces at Dhū Qār:

وَجُنْدُ كِسْرَى غَدَاةَ الْحِنْوِ صَبَّحَهُمْ
مِنَّا كَتَائِبُ تُزْجِي الْمَوْتَ فَانْصَرَفُوا

wa-jundu Kisrā ghadāta al-ḥinwi ṣabbaḥahum / minnā katāʾibu tuzjī al-mawta fa-inṣarafū

Translation: “At dawn at Al-Ḥinw, our battalions, driving death before them, attacked Kisrā’s army; then they withdrew.”

Jundu Kisrā here denotes forces attached to Kisrā’s rule.

The poet does not describe an unrelated, scattered crowd, but an army attached to a particular political and military power.

The second hemistich uses katāʾib.

Katībah concerns a fighting detachment or unit.

Within this verse, jund denotes a ruler’s army, while katāʾib denotes fighting groups engaged in the confrontation.

This proximity helps clarify the words’ distinction.

Jund can name the whole army, whereas katībah can designate a group or portion of its fighting forces.

This should not be converted into the precise classification of modern armies: military units differed in size and organization across historical periods.

Al-Aʿshā’s poem is recorded in collections and literary sources. Some expressions in the ode have variant readings, but jundu Kisrā and its connection to the Dhū Qār confrontation appear in familiar transmissions.

Al-Aʿshā lived through the pre-Islamic period and the beginning of Islam, making him a witness near the era of revelation. His lifespan alone, however, should not be used to establish a definite date for this ode.

Al-Aʿshā’s ode:Kisrā’s army and Dhū Qār

An Army’s Connection to Political Power

Al-Aʿshā attributes the army to Kisrā.

This relationship shows that a jund can be identified through the person or institution commanding its forces.

Attributing an army to a ruler does not mean all its members share identical beliefs, motives, or individual responsibility.

An army may consist of different groups whose members have different duties and responsibilities.

Linguistically, jundu Kisrā identifies the body attached to his rule, rather than delivering separate judgments on every member’s moral characteristics.

This distinction also matters for the Qur’anic expressions junūdu Firʿawna and junūdu Sulaymāna.

Ajnād al-Shām in Al-Farazdaq’s Poetry

Al-Farazdaq, a poet of the Umayyad period, says:

فَقُلْتُ مَا هُوَ إِلَّا الشَّامُ تَرْكَبُهُ
كَأَنَّمَا الْمَوْتُ فِي أَجْنَادِهِ الْبَغَرُ

fa-qultu mā huwa illā al-shāmu tarkabuhu / ka-annamā al-mawtu fī ajnādihi al-bagharu

Translation following the context: “I said: it is only Syria, whose road you take; as though death in its military districts were the thirst-producing disease of camels, spreading there.”

Lisān al-ʿArab:baghar in Al-Farazdaq’s witness

Ajnād here denotes the recognized Syrian districts.

This usage relates to the general military sense while extending to geographical and administrative units.

Al-Farazdaq’s verse therefore witnesses a development of jund’s use in the Islamic period.

Al-Farazdaq was not a pre-Islamic poet. His administrative use of ajnād should not be projected into pre-Islamic Arabic without evidence.

Some words in the line also have variant readings, including the verb for traveling to Syria and the final word’s reading.

The lexical transmission makes the sense of ajnād clear, but the poem’s detailed readings should be compared with the poet’s collected works.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ:the tarkabuhu reading in Al-Farazdaq’s witness

Findings from the Historical Witnesses

Al-Aʿshā’s witness shows jund used for an army attached to a ruler.

Al-Farazdaq’s witness illustrates the family’s extension to Islamic-period military and geographical units.

The uses are related, but differ in date and scope.

Lexical sources also record hard ground, place names, and various groups; these should not all be derived from a single original meaning without evidence.

For understanding Qur’anic usage, the principal finding is that jund was a familiar name for an army or body of forces attached to a commander in language close to the period of revelation.

6. Every Qur’anic Occurrence of the Root

The morphological index records 29 occurrences across 27 verses. Two verses each use the root twice: Al-Baqarah 2:249 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:9.

They can be grouped as follows to ensure that no occurrence is excluded.

Troops in historical narratives: Al-Baqarah 2:249, Al-Baqarah 2:250; Yūnus 10:90; Ṭā Hā 20:78; Al-Naml 27:17, Al-Naml 27:18, Al-Naml 27:37; Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:6, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:8, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:39, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:40; Al-Dukhān 44:24; Al-Dhāriyāt 51:40.

Armies and opposing forces confronting believers: Al-Aḥzāb 33:9; Ṣād 38:11; Al-Burūj 85:17.

Supporting forces, jund, and their relationship to assistance or inability: Maryam 19:75; Yā Sīn 36:75; Al-Mulk 67:20.

Iblīs’s hosts: Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:95.

Divine forces and their assistance or victory: Al-Tawbah 9:26, Al-Tawbah 9:40; Yā Sīn 36:28; Al-Ṣāffāt 37:173; Al-Fatḥ 48:4, Al-Fatḥ 48:7; Al-Muddaththir 74:31.

This classification only organizes the study. Some verses can be considered under several headings. Al-Aḥzāb 33:9, for instance, mentions both attacking human forces and divinely sent hosts.

Classifying a verse under human troops also does not establish that every member of the army belonged to one people or had one particular military role. Solomon’s hosts explicitly include nonhuman beings, so that narrative should not be classified as exclusively human.

7. Saul’s and Goliath’s Troops

Al-Baqarah 2:249: Jund on Both Sides of the Confrontation

Al-Baqarah 2:249 uses J N D twice.

First, for the troops traveling with Saul:

“بِالْجُنُودِ” (bi-al-junūdi).

Then for Goliath’s forces:

“جُنُودِهِ” (junūdihi).

Saul sets out with his troops and informs them about a trial at a river.

After crossing, some companions express their inability to confront Goliath and his troops.

Both occurrences therefore denote human forces preparing for battle.

Jund itself, however, determines neither their strength, troop count, nor the battle’s outcome.

The verse continues with a small group and a large group, stating that a small group can overcome a large one by The God’s permission.

A large jund thus does not itself guarantee victory.

Al-Baqarah 2:250: Facing Goliath’s Troops

وَلَمَّا بَرَزُوا لِجَالُوتَ وَجُنُودِهِ قَالُوا رَبَّنَا أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا وَانْصُرْنَا عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْكَافِرِينَ

wa-lammā barazū li-Jālūta wa-junūdihi qālū rabbanā afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabran wa-thabbit aqdāmanā wa-unṣurnā ʿalā al-qawmi al-kāfirīna

Proposed translation based on this study: “When they came out to face Goliath and his troops, they said: Our Lord, pour patience upon us, make our steps firm, and help us against the disbelieving people.”

Junūdihi here denotes Goliath’s troops.

Goliath and his forces stand on one side of the confrontation, with Saul’s companions on the other.

Jund here names the group supporting a commander.

Victory, however, is not subsequently attributed only to an army’s numbers and organization; steadfastness and divine assistance are also mentioned.

This should not alter jund’s meaning.

The army remains an army. Steadfastness, prayer, and divine assistance are accompanying subjects.

Distinguishing Jund from Fiʾah

Al-Baqarah 2:249 uses fiʾatin qalīlatin and fiʾatan kathīratan.

Fiʾah denotes a group whose members are expected to share some connection and mutual support.

Jund likewise concerns a body of supporting forces.

The words are close in a military setting, but are not complete synonyms.

In Saul’s narrative, jund names the troops, whereas fiʾah describes the confronting groups in terms of their numbers.

Not every use of fiʾah should therefore be confined to a formal army, nor every use of jund to a group of a particular size.

8. Pharaoh’s Hosts and the Responsibility of Power

Pharaoh and His Attached Forces

The Qur’an discusses Pharaoh’s hosts or attached forces in Yūnus 10:90, Ṭā Hā 20:78, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:6, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:8, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:39, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:40, and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:40.

Some verses also name Hāmān alongside Pharaoh.

This association shows that Pharaoh’s power does not reside in his person alone. He has a body of forces and supporters participating in the actions of his rule.

Junūd itself, however, does not determine that every army member bore equal responsibility for every decision and crime of the ruler.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:39: The Arrogance of Ruler and Troops

وَاسْتَكْبَرَ هُوَ وَجُنُودُهُ فِي الْأَرْضِ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُمْ إِلَيْنَا لَا يُرْجَعُونَ

wa-istakbara huwa wa-junūduhu fī al-arḍi bi-ghayri al-ḥaqqi wa-ẓannū annahum ilaynā lā yurjaʿūna

Proposed translation based on this study: “He and his troops arrogantly exalted themselves in the land without right, and supposed that they would not be returned to Us.”

Here arrogance is attributed to Pharaoh and his hosts.

In this instance, the Qur’an itself judges the forces’ conduct.

The judgment comes from istakbara and the verse’s context, rather than from jund inherently meaning an oppressive group.

Other troops may behave differently in different circumstances.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:40: The End of Pharaoh and His Army

فَأَخَذْنَاهُ وَجُنُودَهُ فَنَبَذْنَاهُمْ فِي الْيَمِّ فَانظُرْ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الظَّالِمِينَ

fa-akhadhnāhu wa-junūdahu fa-nabadhnāhum fī al-yammi fa-unẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-ẓālimīna

Proposed translation based on this study: “We seized him and his troops and cast them into the sea; look, then, at the outcome of the wrongdoers.”

Pharaoh and his forces here meet a shared event.

Junūdahu shows that the arrogant ruler is not alone: attached forces have accompanied his pursuit of power and confrontation.

The event should not be taken to establish that every soldier in every army is necessarily oppressive.

The judgment concerns a particular army within Pharaoh’s narrative.

Jund and Responsibility

Political and military power may consist of different individuals and groups.

One person may issue commands, another execute them, and other groups provide resources and support.

Assessing responsibility for an event requires examining people’s actual roles and degree of choice.

Conversely, army membership alone does not determine every detail of individual responsibility.

J N D identifies a relationship between forces and a body; each individual’s justice and responsibility are separate questions.

9. Solomon’s Hosts: A Body Beyond a Human Army

Al-Naml 27:17

وَحُشِرَ لِسُلَيْمَانَ جُنُودُهُ مِنَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنسِ وَالطَّيْرِ فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ

wa-ḥushira li-Sulaymāna junūduhu mina al-jinni wa-al-insi wa-al-ṭayri fa-hum yūzaʿūna

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “Solomon’s hosts of jinn, humans, and birds were gathered for him, and they were kept in order.”

Solomon’s hosts are identified as three groups: jinn, humans, and birds.

The Qur’an itself therefore supplies a clear instance in which jund is not confined to armed humans.

Birds are also counted among Solomon’s attached forces.

This is particularly important.

Always defining jund exclusively as “human soldiers” fails to explain this verse’s explicit use.

Conversely, the presence of birds does not establish that every animal everywhere has a recognized army with a human military structure in every context.

The Qur’an concerns Solomon and his particular forces here.

Fa-hum Yūzaʿūna: Ordering and Restraining the Body

The verse ends with fa-hum yūzaʿūna.

This verb comes from W Z ʿ and concerns restraining, controlling, or organizing a body.

In the setting of Solomon’s hosts, ordering and restraining the forces is appropriate.

The jund in this verse is therefore more than a large, disorganized crowd.

The forces have gathered, and their movements or positions are supervised.

Jund itself does not mean organizing, however; that feature comes from yūzaʿūna.

Al-Naml 27:18: An Army’s Responsibility Toward Small Creatures

In the next verse, an ant warns the other ants to enter their dwellings so that Solomon and his troops do not unwittingly crush them.

The movement of a large body of forces can thus endanger smaller creatures even if the troops are unaware of the danger.

Lexically, jund here remains the body of forces accompanying Solomon.

Their unawareness comes from wa-hum lā yashʿurūna, rather than from jund’s meaning.

Unawareness or carelessness should therefore not be built into the definition of an army.

Al-Naml 27:37: Force and the Threat of Confrontation

ارْجِعْ إِلَيْهِمْ فَلَنَأْتِيَنَّهُم بِجُنُودٍ لَّا قِبَلَ لَهُم بِهَا وَلَنُخْرِجَنَّهُم مِّنْهَا أَذِلَّةً وَهُمْ صَاغِرُونَ

irjiʿ ilayhim fa-la-naʾtiyannahum bi-junūdin lā qibala lahum bi-hā wa-la-nukhrijannahum minhā adhillatan wa-hum ṣāghirūna

Proposed translation based on this study: “Return to them: we will certainly come against them with troops they have no power to confront, and we will drive them out of there, humbled and abased.”

Bi-junūdin here denotes forces Solomon can mobilize.

Lā qibala lahum bi-hā expresses the other side’s inability to confront those forces.

The army’s strength thus emerges from the verse’s combined wording.

Jund alone does not mean an invincible army.

The verse also concerns a specific situation in Solomon’s narrative and should not become a general permission to threaten and humiliate other nations.

10. Opposing Armies and Human Forces in the Qur’an

Al-Aḥzāb 33:9: Attacking Armies and Unseen Forces

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اذْكُرُوا نِعْمَةَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ إِذْ جَاءَتْكُمْ جُنُودٌ فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ رِيحًا وَجُنُودًا لَّمْ تَرَوْهَا ۚ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū udhkurū niʿmata Allāhi ʿalaykum idh jāʾatkum junūdun fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim rīḥan wa-junūdan lam tarawhā wa-kāna Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīran

Proposed translation based on this study: “You who believe, remember The God’s favor upon you when armies came against you: We sent against them a wind and forces you did not see; The God sees what you do.”

This verse uses junūd twice.

First, it denotes forces gathered against the believers.

Second, it denotes forces The God sent that the addressees did not see.

The same word thus describes two bodies with different roles within one verse.

Being a jund consequently does not itself determine whether forces serve truth or falsehood.

The verse also mentions wind separately.

Rīḥan and junūdan should therefore not be treated as one word without explanation.

Early commentaries interpret the unseen hosts as angels. That reading can be considered alongside other verses concerning angelic assistance.

Lam tarawhā itself, however, states only that the addressees did not see the forces. Their precise identity requires other evidence.

Ṣād 38:11: A Defeated Army

جُنْدٌ مَّا هُنَالِكَ مَهْزُومٌ مِّنَ الْأَحْزَابِ

jundun mā hunālika mahzūmun mina al-aḥzābi

Proposed translation based on this study: “An army from the confederate groups, defeated there.”

Jundun is singular here and takes the singular description mahzūmun.

One soldier is not intended, however, but a body of forces.

Mina al-aḥzābi connects that body to allied groups and factions.

Defeat is expressed by mahzūmun.

Jund itself does not mean defeated.

Al-Burūj 85:17 and Al-Burūj 85:18: The Account of the Hosts

Al-Burūj 85:17

هَلْ أَتَاكَ حَدِيثُ الْجُنُودِ

hal atāka ḥadīthu al-junūdi

Proposed translation based on this study: “Has the account of the hosts reached you?”

Al-Burūj 85:18

فِرْعَوْنَ وَثَمُودَ

Firʿawna wa-Thamūda

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “Pharaoh and Thamūd?”

Al-junūd here denotes groups subsequently identified through Pharaoh and Thamūd.

Jund can thus appear in recollections of past powers and human bodies.

It does not itself establish that every member of Thamūd was a military soldier.

The context concerns the power and assembled forces of groups that confronted prophets and their call in their history.

11. Jund in the Sense of Assistance and Support

Maryam 19:75: Weakness of Supporting Forces

Maryam 19:75 concerns the outcome of groups relying on their position and power.

The verse ends with aḍʿafu jundan.

This expression concerns weakness in forces and support.

Jund here may relate to a group’s social or military strength.

Aḍʿafu itself, however, expresses weakness.

Jund names the body of forces, being neither strong nor weak by itself.

Al-Mulk 67:20: Who Is Your Supporting Army?

أَمَّنْ هَٰذَا الَّذِي هُوَ جُنْدٌ لَّكُمْ يَنصُرُكُم مِّن دُونِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ ۚ إِنِ الْكَافِرُونَ إِلَّا فِي غُرُورٍ

amman hādhā alladhī huwa jundun lakum yanṣurukum min dūni al-Raḥmāni ini al-kāfirūna illā fī ghurūrin

Proposed translation based on this study: “Or who is there to be an army for you, helping you apart from the Entirely Merciful? The disbelievers are only in delusion.”

Jundun lakum denotes a force that could serve or support the addressees.

Yanṣurukum makes the sense of assistance clearer.

This use directly relates to Ibn Fāris’s definition: gathering and assistance.

The verse’s question challenges reliance on a force independent of The God.

It does not establish that using every kind of human force or social cooperation is forbidden.

The issue is a claim to assistance independent of the Entirely Merciful, rather than a denial of all ordinary human help and cooperation.

Yā Sīn 36:75: A Host Present for the Objects of Worship

لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ نَصْرَهُمْ وَهُمْ لَهُمْ جُنْدٌ مُّحْضَرُونَ

lā yastaṭīʿūna naṣrahum wa-hum lahum jundun muḥḍarūna

Proposed translation based on this study: “They cannot help them; and they are a host brought before them.”

This follows a discussion of people who have taken objects of worship apart from The God, hoping to receive help from them.

There are two familiar readings of the referents of hum and lahum.

In the first, the worshippers stand as a host for their objects of worship: they defend them, while those objects cannot help their worshippers.

In the second, the objects or beings worshipped are brought forth on the Day of Account, but not to provide their worshippers with actual assistance.

The first reading fits the contrast between the clauses clearly: people expect their object of worship to help them, yet actually rise to support it themselves.

Al-Ṭabarī also prefers this reading.

Al-Ṭabarī:preferring worshippers’ support for their objects of worship

The disagreement over the pronouns’ referents should nevertheless be examined in an independent reflection on the verse.

Both readings preserve the lexical point: jund denotes a body that acquires a role of companionship or support in relation to another party.

This verse illustrates jund used critically for people’s loyalty to powerless objects of worship.

12. Iblīs’s Hosts

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:95

وَجُنُودُ إِبْلِيسَ أَجْمَعُونَ

wa-junūdu Iblīsa ajmaʿūna

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “And the hosts of Iblīs, all together.”

This forms part of a scene in which the misguided and false objects of worship are cast into Hell.

Junūdu Iblīsa denotes forces or groups attached to Iblīs.

Verse 95 itself does not list their members precisely.

The context and other verses may lead to discussion of devils and humans who followed Iblīs.

Jund alone does not establish that Iblīs necessarily has an army with the same organization, equipment, and ranks as a human army.

Attributing hosts to Iblīs here indicates their connection and association with him.

Being a jund also does not make following Iblīs legitimate.

The same word used for Solomon’s and divine hosts can also describe Iblīs’s hosts.

Moral value therefore emerges from the forces’ relationships and conduct rather than from the root itself.

13. Divine Hosts: Forces Beyond a Human Army

Al-Tawbah 9:26 and Al-Tawbah 9:40

Al-Tawbah 9:26 mentions the sending of hosts the addressees did not see.

Al-Tawbah 9:40 likewise describes assistance to the Prophet through unseen forces.

These usages are close to Al-Aḥzāb 33:9.

In all three, junūd denotes forces participating in divine assistance that were not visible to the addressees.

Being unseen does not itself specify every detail of the forces’ nature.

Other evidence may concern angels assisting believers, such as Al-Anfāl 8:9, Āl ʿImrān 3:124, and Āl ʿImrān 3:125.

These verses should be considered together.

Nevertheless, every Qur’anic use of junūd should not be restricted to angels.

Solomon’s hosts include jinn, humans, and birds. Pharaoh’s are human. Unseen forces must likewise be understood within their own context.

Yā Sīn 36:28: No Need to Send an Army

وَمَا أَنزَلْنَا عَلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ مِن بَعْدِهِ مِن جُندٍ مِّنَ السَّمَاءِ وَمَا كُنَّا مُنزِلِينَ

wa-mā anzalnā ʿalā qawmihi min baʿdihi min jundin mina al-samāʾi wa-mā kunnā munzilīna

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “After him, We sent down no army from heaven upon his people, nor were We to send one.”

The next verse mentions one cry that leaves that people extinguished.

This contrast matters.

The Qur’an explicitly states that no army was sent from heaven for this event.

Every divine destruction or punishment should therefore not be attributed without evidence to the presence of a distinct army of heavenly forces.

Different verses may mention wind, water, a cry, or other agents.

Calling all these junūd in every context, however, requires independent verbal evidence or explanation.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:173: Our Hosts Will Prevail

وَإِنَّ جُندَنَا لَهُمُ الْغَالِبُونَ

wa-inna jundanā la-humu al-ghālibūna

Proposed translation based on this study: “Our hosts will indeed be the ones who prevail.”

The preceding verses discuss the divine promise concerning the messengers and assistance to them.

Jundanā in this context consequently relates to forces following divine command and assistance.

The verse does not identify every member or specify their total number.

It also does not establish that every group calling itself The God’s army is necessarily victorious and right.

Claiming a relationship with The God differs from establishing it.

Victory must also be examined within the complete context and the outcome of the messengers’ call.

Believers may suffer a temporary defeat in a battle or historical period. Jundanā itself does not establish that every force professing faith will win every military confrontation.

Al-Fatḥ 48:4: The Hosts of the Heavens and the Earth

هُوَ الَّذِي أَنزَلَ السَّكِينَةَ فِي قُلُوبِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ لِيَزْدَادُوا إِيمَانًا مَّعَ إِيمَانِهِمْ ۗ وَلِلَّهِ جُنُودُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ ۚ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا

huwa alladhī anzala al-sakīnata fī qulūbi al-muʾminīna li-yazdādū īmānan maʿa īmānihim wa-li-Allāhi junūdu al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-kāna Allāhu ʿalīman ḥakīman

Proposed translation based on this study: “He sent tranquility into the believers’ hearts so that they might increase in faith alongside their faith; to The God belong the hosts of the heavens and the earth; The God is Knowing, Wise.”

The scope of the divine hosts here extends to the heavens and the earth.

This is far broader than a ruler’s human army.

The verse concerns forces belonging to The God throughout the heavens and the earth.

It does not yield an exhaustive inventory of every instance of divine hosts.

Nor does it establish that the Qur’an necessarily names every natural event a jund.

The verse expresses the breadth of forces belonging to The God; the details of each force’s role in each event require evidence concerning that subject.

Al-Fatḥ 48:7: Repeating the Hosts of the Heavens and the Earth

وَلِلَّهِ جُنُودُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَزِيزًا حَكِيمًا

wa-li-Allāhi junūdu al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-kāna Allāhu ʿazīzan ḥakīman

Proposed translation based on this study: “To The God belong the hosts of the heavens and the earth; The God is Invincible, Wise.”

The same expression for the hosts of the heavens and the earth recurs here.

The two verses end differently, however.

Al-Fatḥ 48:4 uses ʿalīman ḥakīman.

Al-Fatḥ 48:7 uses ʿazīzan ḥakīman.

The expression for divine hosts therefore accompanies different descriptions of The God in two related contexts.

Jund should not consequently be defined as “knowledge” in one verse and “invincible power” in the other.

Those descriptions concern The God and have their own meanings from independent roots.

Al-Muddaththir 74:31: The Number of Your Lord’s Hosts

Al-Muddaththir 74:31 states:

“وَمَا يَعْلَمُ جُنُودَ رَبِّكَ إِلَّا هُوَ” (wa-mā yaʿlamu junūda rabbika illā huwa).

That is: “None but He knows your Lord’s hosts.”

The phrase appears in a verse that previously mentions the guardians of the Fire and angels.

This very context therefore connects the Lord’s hosts to angelic forces.

It should not establish that all divine hosts in the Qur’an consist exclusively of that particular group.

The phrase reserves complete knowledge of the Lord’s hosts to Him.

Al-Ṭabarī:the Lord’s hosts and the guardians’ context

Claims to an exact number or complete inventory of divine hosts without clear evidence consequently do not accord with the verse’s content.

This particularly matters for studies seeking to identify every natural force, human group, or unknown phenomenon definitively as one of the divine hosts.

Such identifications may be proposed as possibilities, but establishing them requires evidence.

14. Are Divine Hosts the Same as Angels?

Some verses clearly connect divine assistance and angels.

Al-Anfāl 8:9, for example, mentions assistance through angels.

Āl ʿImrān 3:124 and Āl ʿImrān 3:125 also mention angelic aid in a battle setting.

Angels can therefore be among the forces The God employs for assistance.

Junūd’s Qur’anic meaning, however, is not limited to angels.

Al-Naml 27:17 identifies Solomon’s hosts as jinn, humans, and birds.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:39, Pharaoh’s hosts are his attached forces.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:95 mentions Iblīs’s hosts.

Al-Fatḥ 48:4 and Al-Fatḥ 48:7 attribute the hosts of the heavens and the earth to The God.

Jund consequently does not name one particular kind of being, but forces or bodies related to a commander, objective, or power.

The kinds of beings composing a jund require separate identification in each verse.

Being unseen does not itself establish that every member of a jund is an angel.

The context and other verses may support that reading, but invisibility alone does not fully define the forces’ nature.

15. Distinguishing J N D from Related Roots

J N D (Jund) and J M ʿ (Jamʿ)

Study of J M ʿ

Jamʿ concerns gathering and assembly.

Gathering also belongs to jund’s semantic core.

Not every crowd is a jund, however.

In its principal use, jund is a body providing support, assistance, or readiness for action.

People may instead gather by chance or for a nonmilitary objective.

Gathering is therefore one feature of jund, rather than its entire meaning.

J N D (Jund) and J Y SH (Jaysh)

Jaysh denotes an army or body of fighting forces in Arabic.

Jund is close to this meaning in military use.

Lexical sources, however, also define jund as helpers and supporters.

In some contexts its range can therefore extend beyond a particular combat force.

This should not become a categorical, permanent distinction: the two words can be very close in many Arabic military usages.

Jund and its plural occur in the Qur’an, whereas jaysh does not occur as a noun in its text.

J N D (Jund) and K T B (Katībah)

Study of K T B

Katībah denotes a body of fighting forces in Arabic.

Al-Aʿshā’s poetic line places jundu Kisrā and katāʾib together.

This association shows that an army may consist of several fighting detachments.

The two nouns alone do not determine an exact troop count or a fixed military hierarchy.

In many uses, jund is closer to the whole body of forces and katībah to a detachment within it.

J N D (Jund) and Ḥ Z B (Ḥizb)

Ḥizb denotes a group recognized through a shared bond, inclination, or objective.

Jund can likewise designate a body of associated forces.

Ṣād 38:11 places jund and aḥzāb in one sentence.

Their proximity shows that allied groups may form fighting forces.

Ḥizb and jund are not identical in every usage, however.

Ḥizb can designate an intellectual, social, or political group, whereas jund’s principal use more prominently concerns force and practical support.

J N D (Jund) and F ʾ Y / F ʾ W (Fiʾah)

Fiʾah denotes a group with some companionship and support among its members. Lexicographers differ over its derivation under F ʾ Y and F ʾ W; the final tāʾ marbūṭah is not a root consonant.

Lisān al-ʿArab:disagreement over fiʾah’s derivation

Al-Baqarah 2:249 uses fiʾah alongside junūd.

The two are close in a military confrontation.

Fiʾah may simply designate a group, small or large, while jund more often denotes associated forces or forces attached to a commander.

Not every fiʾah is therefore a formal army in every use.

J N D (Jund) and N Ṣ R (Nuṣrah)

Study of N Ṣ R

Nuṣrah concerns providing assistance.

Ibn Fāris likewise identifies assistance as a central feature of jund.

Assistance does not always take the form of an army, however.

People may help others through wealth, knowledge, speech, or a nonmilitary act.

Jund, by contrast, commonly denotes a body of associated forces.

Assistance can consequently be a jund’s function, but every act of assistance is not a jund.

J N D (Jund) and Q W Y (Quwwah)

Study of Q W Y

Quwwah denotes capacity and strength.

Jund can be an organized way of deploying strength.

Being a jund does not itself establish great strength.

An army may be small and weak or large and well equipped.

Maryam 19:75 speaks of weakness in supporting forces.

Jund therefore does not mean an invincible force.

J N D (Jund) and J N N (Jinn)

Study of J N N

These roots share their first two consonants but are distinct.

J N D concerns forces and supporting bodies.

One familiar branch of J N N concerns covering and concealment; jinn belongs to that family.

In Al-Naml 27:17, al-jinn is one component of Solomon’s hosts.

Their association does not mean jund derives from jinn or that all hosts consist of jinn.

The same verse also includes humans and birds among the hosts.

The words’ resemblance should therefore not cause their roots to be confused.

J N D (Jund) and J N Ḥ (Janāḥ)

Study of J N Ḥ

Janāḥ means a wing in Arabic and, in some uses, a side or flank.

Its consonants differ from those of J N D.

Some military or social structures may refer to an army’s wing, meaning one side of it.

This does not make janāḥ and jund derivatives of the same root.

Jund names the body of forces; janāḥ can denote a part or side of a body.

J N D (Jund) and ʿ S K R (ʿAskar)

ʿAskar names an army in Arabic and is also used in lexical sources to explain jund.

The two are very close in military use.

The Qur’an, however, sometimes uses jund for nonhuman forces as well.

Arabic also uses ʿaskar in expressions concerning a military camp or the place where forces are stationed.

“Army” may consequently suit both in translation, without making all their historical and morphological uses identical.

16. Questions of Understanding and Translation

Is Jund Always a Military Army?

No.

A military army is one particularly clear instance of jund.

Lexical sources also define it as helpers and supporters.

The Qur’an likewise applies jund to bodies extending beyond armed human forces.

“Forces” may therefore be more accurate than “soldiers” in some contexts.

Does Jund Mean One Soldier?

In Qur’anic use, jund is a collective noun.

It can be singular in form while identifying a body of forces.

“One soldier” consequently fails to convey the meaning in Ṣād 38:11 or Al-Mulk 67:20.

Are All Divine Hosts Angels?

No.

Some Qur’anic contexts speak of angels as assisting forces, but jund is not restricted to angels.

Al-Fatḥ 48:4 and Al-Fatḥ 48:7 attribute the hosts of the heavens and the earth to The God.

Al-Muddaththir 74:31 reserves complete knowledge of the Lord’s hosts to Him.

A definitive list of all divine hosts should therefore not be invented without Qur’anic evidence.

Is Every Natural Force One of The God’s Hosts?

All forces in the world lie within The God’s creation and power.

That principle does not establish that the Qur’an calls every natural force a jund in every instance.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:9, for example, mentions wind and hosts separately.

Yā Sīn 36:28 states that no heavenly army was sent, and the next verse introduces one cry.

Identifying a particular phenomenon as a jund in interpretation therefore requires evidence.

Does Being a Jund Establish Rightfulness?

No.

The Qur’an also speaks of Pharaoh’s and Iblīs’s hosts.

Being a jund identifies a body of associated forces.

Truth or falsehood emerges from its objective, conduct, and actual relationship to divine commands.

Can People Establish That They Are The God’s Army by Claiming It?

No.

No group establishes itself as The God’s jund merely by adopting that name.

Religious claims in the Qur’an require clear evidence.

A person or group calling itself The God’s army consequently gains no automatic authorization for obedience, war, or attributing divine legitimacy to its conduct.

Do Numerous Hosts Guarantee Victory?

No.

The narrative of Saul and Goliath mentions the possibility of a small group overcoming a large one.

Ṣād 38:11 also mentions a defeated army.

The name jund therefore guarantees neither victory nor defeat.

Were Solomon’s Hosts Exclusively Human?

No.

Al-Naml 27:17 explicitly identifies jinn, humans, and birds.

Confining jund to human soldiers is incompatible with this Qur’anic usage.

This breadth should not, however, make the roles and natures of all those groups identical.

Does “The Hosts of the Heavens and the Earth” Mean Every Being in the World?

The expression presents a very broad scope of divine forces.

Strictly speaking, however, junūd names bodies of forces rather than directly equaling every existing being.

Different creatures may fall within divine power and command, but turning every being into a soldier with a particular task requires independent evidence.

Translation should therefore retain “the hosts of the heavens and the earth,” leaving possible instances for analysis.

17. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For military jund, the principal Persian equivalents are sepāh and lashkar, both meaning an army or host.

For plural junūd, sepāhiyān (“troops”), sepāh-hā (“armies”), or nīrūhā (“forces”) can be appropriate.

The choice depends on context.

“Troops” is natural in the narrative of Saul and Goliath, which concerns human forces in military confrontation.

“Pharaoh’s troops” is likewise clear in Pharaoh’s narrative.

“Solomon’s hosts” suits Al-Naml 27:17, provided their composition of jinn, humans, and birds is recognized; “human soldiers” is incomplete.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:9, “troops” can describe the attacking forces, and “forces” or “hosts” the divinely sent junūd.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:4 and Al-Fatḥ 48:7, “the hosts of the heavens and the earth” retains the Qur’anic meaning and image. “The forces of the heavens and the earth” can also explain the broad usage, but should not erase the sense of a supporting host.

“Iblīs’s hosts” suits Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:95.

“A host brought forth” in Yā Sīn 36:75 retains the image of attachment and support.

“Army” or “supporting force” in Al-Mulk 67:20 fits the question concerning assistance.

“Your Lord’s hosts” is natural in Al-Muddaththir 74:31; a list of possible instances should not be inserted into the base translation.

Non-Qur’anic forms require differing equivalents as well.

Ajnād denotes armies in military use and districts or military divisions in historical administrative use.

Jundī ordinarily means a soldier.

Jannada, when gathering forces, can mean “assembled forces” or “mobilized.”

Tajnīd concerns gathering and organizing forces.

Jundun mujannadun means an assembled or organized army.

Janad in early descriptions of terrain means hard, stony ground.

These meanings should not replace one another without evidence.

For Persian Forqan, sepāh and sepāhiyān should be the base equivalents in human military contexts. In broader divine contexts, an explanation such as “forces under command or providing assistance” can be added when needed.

Such an explanation should not be inserted unnecessarily into the verse translation itself.

18. Conclusion

The Arabic root J N D concerns gathering and assistance.

Ibn Fāris identifies these two features as its semantic core: al-tajammuʿ wa-al-nuṣrah.

In its principal use, jund names a body of forces serving a commander, group, or objective.

That body may be a military army, but the word is not restricted to armed human soldiers.

Arabic sources also call helpers and supporters jund.

In the early Islamic period it was also used for military and administrative districts, as in ajnād al-Shām.

Alongside these uses, janad, with fatḥah on both jīm and nūn, is recorded for hard, stony ground; its historical relationship to the name for an army is uncertain.

Early poetic witnesses clarify the military meaning.

In Al-Aʿshā’s poetry, jundu Kisrā denotes an army attached to Kisrā’s rule.

In Al-Farazdaq’s poetry, ajnād describes Syria’s military districts.

These witnesses show that the military sense was familiar near the period of revelation and subsequently extended to administrative uses.

The Qur’an uses the root 29 times in 27 verses.

In Al-Baqarah 2:249 and Al-Baqarah 2:250, junūd concerns Saul’s and Goliath’s troops.

In verses about Pharaoh, junūd denotes forces attached to his rule.

In Al-Naml 27:17, Solomon’s hosts consist of jinn, humans, and birds.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:9 mentions both attacking armies and divinely sent unseen forces.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:95 mentions Iblīs’s hosts.

Al-Fatḥ 48:4 and Al-Fatḥ 48:7 attribute the hosts of the heavens and the earth to The God.

Al-Muddaththir 74:31 reserves complete knowledge of the Lord’s hosts to Him.

These differing uses lead to a clear finding: Jund does not name a particular kind of being; it identifies a body of forces through command, companionship, assistance, or a common objective.

A jund may therefore be human or nonhuman, visible or unseen, serving truth or falsehood.

These characteristics come from each verse’s context rather than from the word itself.

A numerous jund also does not itself establish invincible power. The Qur’an speaks both of defeated armies and of small groups overcoming large ones.

For Forqan, “army” and “troops” are principal equivalents in human military usages.

For broader usages, especially divine hosts, “forces” can clarify the range, provided the sense of a supporting body under command is retained.

The defensible core is forces gathered for support and common action: neither a mere crowd nor necessarily human combatants, nor a title automatically establishing a group’s rightfulness or victory.

Preserving this distinction allows every Qur’anic use of J N D, from Pharaoh’s troops to Solomon’s hosts and divine forces, to be studied without equating their natures or attributing unwritten details to the Qur’an.
