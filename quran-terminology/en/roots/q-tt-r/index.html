1. Basic meanings and linguistic background

Q Ṭ R has several semantic branches in Arabic. They cannot all be confidently traced to a single historical meaning.

One recognized branch concerns liquids dripping, drops falling, and successive movement. Another denotes a side, region, or boundary of an expanse. Different forms also name two materials: copper, particularly when molten, and tar, a thick, sticky substance obtained from certain trees.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period and the era near revelation attests this word family in connection with applying tar to camels. The sense of a side or region is also clear in the Qur’an itself. Independent evidence is nevertheless insufficient to establish when all these branches arose or how they are historically related. Their inclusion in later dictionaries does not establish that history by itself.

Qur’anic forms examined: The family occurs five times in five verses: qiṭr twice, qaṭirān once, and aqṭār twice.

  • قِطْر (qiṭr): copper; molten copper in its Qur’anic contexts.
  • قَطِرَان (qaṭirān): tar or plant-derived tar.
  • أَقْطَار (aqṭār): regions, sides, and boundaries; the plural of قُطْر (quṭr).

Other Arabic forms not used in the Qur’an include قَطَرَ (qaṭara), “to drip”; قَطْرَة (qaṭrah), “a drop”; قَطْر (qaṭr), “rain”; تَقْطِير (taqṭīr), “causing to drip”; قِطَار (qiṭār), “a train of camels”; and تَقَاطَرَ (taqāṭara), “to come in succession.”

2. Physical images and early Arabic witnesses

Dripping and successive movement

When water falls drop by drop from a leaking vessel, قَطَرَ (qaṭara) can describe that movement.

Successive drops resemble another usage in this family: a train of camels. A string of camels following one another was called قِطَار (qiṭār). This resemblance helps explain the usages, but does not by itself establish the historical order in which they developed.

Within this branch, dripping liquid and the successive arrival of objects or people are related usages.

Tar in Arab material life

Tar was a dark, sticky, pungent substance associated in lexical explanations with extracts and material obtained by heating certain trees. Applying it to camels, including those affected by mange, is a recorded use.

One transmitted version of a verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

أَتَقْتُلُنِي وَقَدْ شَغَفْتُ فُؤَادَهَا / كَمَا قَطَرَ الْمَهْنُوءَةَ الرَّجُلُ الطَّالِي

“Will you kill me when my love has penetrated the depths of her heart, as the man applying tar coats the tar-treated she-camel?”

Lexical sources transmit different versions of this verse. In the version quoted here, قَطَرَ (qaṭara) denotes applying tar. The comparison connects the burning effect of attachment with tar’s effect. This study has not established the precise date of this wording or of the poem’s composition.

In an image comparing tears to water from a bucket, Labīd ibn Rabīʿah describes a water-drawing camel:

بَكَرَتْ بِهِ جُرَشِيَّةٌ مَقْطُورَةٌ / تُرْوِي الْمَحَاجِرَ بَازِلٌ عُلْكُومُ

“A tar-coated she-camel from Jurash set to work with that bucket at dawn: a mature, sturdy camel watering the gardens.”

Labīd lived through both the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, but this verse’s precise date is unknown. مَقْطُورَة (maqṭūrah) describes a camel whose body has been coated with tar.

These witnesses present tar as a familiar material in Arab life and attest a verb for its application.

Side, flank, and region

قُطْر (quṭr) denotes a side, flank, or region. Either flank of the body can also be described with this word.

From the sense of a side or region, the plural أَقْطَار (aqṭār) is readily understood for the sides of cities, territories, and larger expanses.

3. Qiṭr in the Qur’an: molten copper

قِطْر (qiṭr) denotes copper, particularly molten copper. Some lexical explanations also include varieties of copper or copper-based metals within its semantic field.

Both Qur’anic occurrences involve the flowing or pouring of this material.

Dhū al-Qarnayn and the construction of a barrier

ءاتونى زُبَرَ الحَديدِ حَتّىٰ إِذا ساوىٰ بَينَ الصَّدَفَينِ قالَ انفُخوا حَتّىٰ إِذا جَعَلَهُ نارًا قالَ ءاتونى أُفرِغ عَلَيهِ قِطرًا

Ātūnī zubara l-ḥadīdi ḥattā idhā sāwā bayna l-ṣadafayni qāla nfukhū. Ḥattā idhā jaʿalahu nāran qāla ātūnī ufrigh ʿalayhi qiṭrā.

“Bring me pieces of iron.” Then, when he had made the space between the two mountain sides level, he said, “Blow.” Then, when he had made it fire, he said, “Bring me [copper], so that I may pour molten copper over it.”

Al-Kahf 18:96

The verse names two distinct materials: iron and qiṭr. Pieces of iron are used to build the barrier, and molten copper is then poured over them.

أُفْرِغْ (ufrigh), from F R GH, denotes emptying or pouring out. عَلَيْهِ (ʿalayhi, “over it”) specifies where the material is poured.

قِطْرًا (qiṭran) here names a metallic material, rather than the action of dripping or pouring. Its molten state fits the verb and the construction context.

The Qur’an does not specify metal ratios, chemical composition, or a complete metalworking procedure for this structure. A particular alloy cannot therefore be established as qiṭr’s definite meaning from the word alone.

Solomon and the flowing copper

وَلِسُلَيمٰنَ الرّيحَ غُدُوُّها شَهرٌ وَرَواحُها شَهرٌ وَأَسَلنا لَهُ عَينَ القِطرِ وَمِنَ الجِنِّ مَن يَعمَلُ بَينَ يَدَيهِ بِإِذنِ رَبِّهِ وَمَن يَزِغ مِنهُم عَن أَمرِنا نُذِقهُ مِن عَذابِ السَّعيرِ

Wa-li-Sulaymāna l-rīḥa ghuduwwuhā shahrun wa-rawāḥuhā shahrun wa-asalnā lahu ʿayna l-qiṭr. Wa-mina l-jinni man yaʿmalu bayna yadayhi bi-idhni rabbih. Wa-man yazigh minhum ʿan amrinā nudhiqhu min ʿadhābi l-saʿīr.

And for Solomon [We subjected] the wind: its morning journey was a month and its evening journey a month. And We caused a spring of molten copper to flow for him. And among the jinn were those who worked before him by his Lord’s permission. Whoever among them deviates from Our command, We make him taste the punishment of the blazing fire.

Saba 34:12

The expression is عَيْنَ الْقِطْرِ (ʿayna al-qiṭri). ʿAyn here denotes a spring or source, and أَسَلْنَا (asalnā), from S Y L, means “We caused to flow.”

The following verse, Sabaʾ 34:13, mentions making structures, statues, large vessels, and fixed cauldrons for Solomon. This context concerns his access to resources for construction and metalworking.

The liquid state of the metal is evident in both Qur’anic contexts. Despite its resemblance to flowing liquids, we cannot confidently claim that the Arabic name for copper historically developed from the verb “to drip.”

4. Qaṭirān: a coating of sticky, combustible material

In Sūrat Ibrāhīm, after a description of offenders bound in fetters, the text says:

سَرابيلُهُم مِن قَطِرانٍ وَتَغشىٰ وُجوهَهُمُ النّارُ

Sarābīluhum min qaṭirānin wa-taghshā wujūhahumu l-nār.

Their garments are of tar, and fire covers their faces.

Ibrahim 14:50

Tar adheres to surfaces and bodies and is combustible. For an audience familiar with coating camels with it, a garment made of that material provides a tangible image of torment.

سَرَابِيل (sarābīl) is the plural of سِرْبَال (sirbāl), a garment or bodily covering. Here مِنْ (min) identifies the material of which the garments consist.

“Plant-derived tar” is an explanatory rendering for the material familiar in these witnesses. The verse translation retains “tar,” with its character explained in the discussion, rather than restricting it to a modern industrial or petroleum product.

Knowing tar’s earthly properties does not establish the material composition or precise nature of punishment in the afterlife. The verse offers an intelligible image without supplying a physical account of that world.

A different transmitted reading

A reading attributed to some early reciters divides مِنْ قِطْرٍ آنٍ (min qiṭrin ānin) into two words: copper and intensely hot; ānin belongs to A N Y. This article follows Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim: قَطِرَانٍ (qaṭirānin) is one word and names tar.

The two readings must remain distinct. Their difference does not establish that tar and molten copper are synonymous.

5. Aqṭār: regions, sides, and boundaries

أَقْطَار (aqṭār) is the broken plural of قُطْر (quṭr). It denotes the sides or regions of an expanse. The Qur’an uses it both for a city and for the heavens and the earth.

The sides of the city

وَلَو دُخِلَت عَلَيهِم مِن أَقطارِها ثُمَّ سُئِلُوا الفِتنَةَ لَـٔاتَوها وَما تَلَبَّثوا بِها إِلّا يَسيرًا

Wa-law dukhilat ʿalayhim min aqṭārihā thumma suʾilū l-fitnata la-ātawhā wa-mā talabbathū bihā illā yasīrā.

And if it [the city] had been entered upon them from its sides, and they had then been asked for sedition, they would have yielded to it and delayed only a little in doing so.

Al-Ahzab 33:14

The preceding verse, al-Aḥzāb 33:13, mentions the people of Yathrib and a group seeking to leave on the pretext that their houses were exposed.

أَقْطَارِهَا (aqṭārihā) refers here to the city’s sides; the context supplies the city as the antecedent of hā. دُخِلَتْ (dukhilat) is passive: the city is entered, and min marks the directions of entry. Fitnah in this situation has been understood as joining unrest and the attackers, or abandoning faith. That contextual interpretation must not be confused with the meaning of aqṭār.

Aqṭār here does not mean different countries or the city’s geometric diameters. It denotes the sides and directions from which the city is entered.

The regions of the heavens and the earth

يٰمَعشَرَ الجِنِّ وَالإِنسِ إِنِ استَطَعتُم أَن تَنفُذوا مِن أَقطارِ السَّمٰوٰتِ وَالأَرضِ فَانفُذوا لا تَنفُذونَ إِلّا بِسُلطٰنٍ

Yā maʿshara l-jinni wa-l-insi ini staṭaʿtum an tanfudhū min aqṭāri l-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi fa-nfudhū. Lā tanfudhūna illā bi-sulṭān.

O company of jinn and humans, if you are able to pass beyond the regions of the heavens and the earth, then pass beyond. You cannot pass beyond except with authority.

Ar-Rahman 55:33

The construction مِنْ أَقْطَارِ (min aqṭāri) appears again, but the expanse is now the heavens and the earth rather than a city.

تَنْفُذُوا (tanfudhū), from N F DH, means to pass through or beyond. Aqṭār identifies the regions or boundaries whose crossing is in question.

Al-Raḥmān 55:31 speaks of attending to the two groups, jinn and humans. Al-Raḥmān 55:35 speaks of a flame and nuḥās being sent against them and their inability to defend themselves. The context concerns divine authority and the limits of created beings.

Sultan here concerns authority and the ability to pass beyond. Aqṭār itself expresses only regions and boundaries; it establishes neither the universe’s geometric shape nor a physical theory of its limits.

Aqṭār thus retains the sense of regions and boundaries without specifying their shape, distance, or physical extent.

6. Comparison with related roots

Q Ṭ R (quṭr) and A F Q (ufuq): region and horizon

Ufuq and quṭr overlap in denoting regions and boundaries, but have different emphases.

Ufuq often concerns the edge of the field of vision and an expanse extending into the distance. Quṭr denotes a side or region, whether at the farthest visible edge or nearer to the observer.

Thus al-Aḥzāb 33:14 uses aqṭār for the city’s sides, while al-Takwīr 81:23 describes the place of a sighting as the clear horizon.

Q Ṭ R (qaṭr) and S Y L (sayl): dripping and flowing

In the branch concerning liquid movement, qaṭr denotes drops falling or dripping, whereas sayl emphasizes a liquid flowing or running.

Water falling drop by drop from a roof illustrates qaṭr; water running in a stream illustrates the movement expressed by S Y L.

In Sabaʾ 34:12, the Qur’an uses أَسَلْنَا (asalnā) for making qiṭr flow. Qiṭr names the material; isālah expresses causing it to flow.

This family must be distinguished from Q T R, with tāʾ, and from the four-letter entry Q N Ṭ R, under which qinṭār is studied. Similar spelling does not establish identical roots or a shared historical origin.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Render قِطْر (qiṭr) in al-Kahf 18:96 and Sabaʾ 34:12 as “molten copper.”

Render قَطِرَان (qaṭirān) in Ibrāhīm 14:50 as “tar,” with “plant-derived tar” as an explanatory gloss where needed.

Render أَقْطَارِهَا (aqṭārihā) in al-Aḥzāb 33:14 as “its sides,” and أَقْطَارِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ (aqṭāri al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi) in al-Raḥmān 55:33 as “the regions of the heavens and the earth.”

For non-Qur’anic usages, suitable equivalents include dripping, causing to drip, a drop, successive movement, a side, and a region.

8. Conclusion

Q Ṭ R is an Arabic word family with several branches: dripping and successive movement; a side or region; and the names of two materials, copper and tar.

The Qur’an uses three nominal forms from this family: molten copper in the accounts of Dhū al-Qarnayn and Solomon; tar in the description of offenders’ garments in the afterlife; and aqṭār for the sides of a city and the regions of the heavens and the earth.

Proposed definition for Forqan: Dripping and successive falling; a side, region, or boundary of an expanse; and, in particular nominal forms, molten copper and tar. A common historical origin for all these branches has not been conclusively established.
