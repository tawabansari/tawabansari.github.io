Kinship and attribution: from descent and family to human responsibility toward ties of lineage

1. Introducing the root and its semantic core

Arabic N S B concerns a relationship or connection between people or things. In the familiar noun nasab, this means kinship through birth and descent; other forms can attribute a person, statement, quality, or subject to its origin or owner.

Nasab connects people through fathers, mothers, and earlier and later generations. It extends beyond parent and child: siblings, paternal and maternal uncles and aunts, and their children can be related through shared ancestors.

Arabic «نَسَبَ الشَّيْءَ إِلَى فُلَانٍ» means attributing something to someone. Attributing a statement to an author or speaker claims that it comes from or belongs to them.

That attribution may be true or false. The act of attributing does not guarantee its accuracy.

Two related ideas occur in N S B: an actual relationship between people or things, and stating or claiming such a relationship.

The relationship itself must be distinguished from a claim about it. Someone may genuinely descend from a person, while another claims the same without evidence. The family’s words can occur in both cases, but the attribution’s validity differs.

Thus, “a connection or relationship between two parties” is the family’s general axis, while Qur’anic nasab specifically concerns kinship or a claim of kinship.

Kinship alone does not establish moral superiority, truth, virtue, or merit. People’s value and responsibility must be understood from their conduct and other Qur’anic principles.

Qur’anic forms

N S B occurs only three times in the Qur’an, in two nominal forms:

  • «أَنْسَابَ»: plural of nasab; Al-Muʾminūn 23:101.
  • «نَسَبًا»: singular noun; Al-Furqān 25:54.
  • «نَسَبًا»: singular noun; Al-Ṣāffāt 37:158.

All concern kinship, but their contexts differ.

Al-Muʾminūn concerns lineage’s place on the day of resurrection.

Al-Furqān presents descent and marriage among the relationships established in human life.

Al-Ṣāffāt describes people falsely asserting kinship between The God and other beings.

No direct verb from this root, such as nasaba, yansubu, intasaba, nāsaba, or tanāsaba, occurs in the Qur’an.

Arabic also knows forms such as nisbah, nasīb, nassāb, nassābah, mansūb, munāsabah, and tanāsub, but they are not Qur’anic derivatives of this root.

Broader Arabic uses of attribution help explain the family, but should not replace the kinship meaning when studying these Qur’anic passages.

2. Early Arabic usage and semantic range

Nasab: connection through birth and descent

One lexical definition introduces N S B as follows:

النُّونُ وَالسِّينُ وَالْبَاءُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ قِيَاسُهَا اتِّصَالُ شَيْءٍ بِشَيْءٍ

al-nūnu wa-al-sīnu wa-al-bāʾu kalimatun wāḥidatun qiyāsuhā ittiṣālu shayʾin bi-shayʾin

Translation:

“This root denotes one thing being connected with another.”

In that explanation, kinship is an example of this connection.

The definition helps relate the family’s uses, but is too general to determine Qur’anic nasab by itself.

Not every connection is nasab. Joining two pieces of wood, connecting two cities, or linking two ideas is not necessarily called nasab in Arabic.

In the kinship sense, nasab is a specific relationship arising through birth and generational connections.

Another explanation identifies kinship through a shared parental connection and distinguishes two kinds: relationships along generations, such as parents and children, and relationships across branches, such as siblings’ children or cousins.

This shows that nasab extends beyond a direct father–child relationship.

Two brothers are related through shared parents, although neither is the other’s child.

The children of two sisters or two brothers can likewise be related through shared ancestors.

Defining nasab as only “paternal relationship” or “passing down a father’s surname” is therefore too narrow.

Paternal descent was more prominent in some historical and legal settings, but that social practice should not become the basic meaning of every use.

Attribution to a father, city, or occupation

Arabic nasaba is not used only for kinship.

«نَسَبْتُ فُلَانًا إِلَى أَبِيهِ» means attributing someone to his father or stating his lineage.

«نَسَبْتُ الْقَوْلَ إِلَى فُلَانٍ» means attributing a statement to someone.

A person may also be associated with a city, tribe, occupation, or residence.

City- or occupation-related descriptions can indicate where someone lived or the work for which they are known.

That association is not identical to kinship.

Someone associated with a city need not descend from every inhabitant. Attributing a poem to a poet also creates no relationship of birth between poem and poet.

Attribution here identifies or presents a relationship between a subject and its origin or owner.

N S B therefore extends beyond kinship in Arabic, while nasab itself concerns kinship in its three Qur’anic occurrences.

Intisāb: stating or claiming affiliation

Arabic intasaba concerns declaring one’s lineage or affiliation to a family or group.

Istansaba can also concern requesting a statement of lineage.

Lexical usage records expressions such as:

اِنْتَسَبَ إِلَى أَبِيهِ

intasaba ilā abīhi

Meaning: “He stated his descent from his father.”

And:

اِسْتَنْسِبْ لَنَا

istansib lanā

Meaning: “State your lineage for us, so that we may know you.”

Here, affiliation identifies a person and their family connections.

Tanassaba can in some uses concern claiming kinship, which may be true or false.

Arabic lexical tradition records the proverb:

الْقَرِيبُ مَنْ تَقَرَّبَ لَا مَنْ تَنَسَّبَ

al-qarību man taqarraba lā man tanassaba

Translation:

“A truly close person is one who shows closeness and companionship, rather than merely claiming kinship.”

This distinguishes a claimed connection from a relationship evident in conduct.

It does not make actual lineage unimportant. The proverb concerns closeness and companionship, rather than denying birth and descent.

The expression is recorded in lexical and proverbial traditions, but its earliest date is uncertain. It should not be assigned to the pre-Islamic era without historical evidence.

Lineage in Arab social life

Knowledge of descent mattered in many relationships in early Arab society.

A father’s name, family, tribe, and ancestors could identify someone, establish family connections, recognize relatives, arrange marriages, and identify social responsibilities.

Where official identity records did not exist in their present form, naming paternal ancestors and tribal affiliations was one way to identify people.

Lineage could also become a source of pride and superiority. Someone might claim rank through famous or powerful ancestors rather than personal conduct.

Yet not every social use of lineage should be reduced to tribal boasting.

It could support identification and family responsibility, just as it could be used for superiority or contempt.

Lineage’s social functions must therefore be distinguished from the relationship itself.

A poetic witness in Arabic lexical tradition

Arabic lexical tradition transmits a verse explaining the form nasb:

يَا عَمْرُو يَا ابْنَ الْأَكْرَمِينَ نَسْبًا
قَدْ نَحَبَ الْمَجْدُ عَلَيْكَ نَحْبًا

yā ʿamru yā ibna al-akramīna nasban / qad naḥaba al-majdu ʿalayka naḥban

Translation:

“O ʿAmr, son of those noblest in descent; glory has as though vowed never to leave you.”

Nasban here concerns descent and family kinship.

The poet praises the addressee through his ancestors’ and family’s standing.

Nasab could therefore appear in praise and social boasting.

The lexical transmission does not securely identify the poet or date. The vowelless sīn in nasb is also explained as required by poetic meter here.

This is evidence of recorded poetic usage, rather than certain evidence for the earliest history of nasab’s meaning.

Praise of lineage in one poem does not establish moral superiority for everyone with famous ancestors; that judgment belongs to the speaker and poetic context.

Nasīb: a relative and the love section of a poem

Arabic nasīb can mean a relative.

فُلَانٌ نَسِيبُ فُلَانٍ

fulānun nasību fulānin

Meaning:

“So-and-so is a relative of so-and-so.”

The same form has another familiar meaning in poetic tradition.

Nasīb names a passage about the beloved, love, separation, beauty, or memories of meeting.

In early Arabic odes, it could occur at the opening before praise, travel, boasting, or another subject.

Lexical explanations relate this use to the connection a poet establishes with the beloved in speech.

A poetic nasīb does not mean poet and beloved are necessarily relatives.

Nor does its name establish love as the original meaning of every N S B use.

Poetic nasīb is an established literary use, but this form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Nisbah and tanāsub: relationships between things

Broader Arabic uses of munāsabah and tanāsub concern correspondence, resemblance, or a relationship between things.

Examples include proportions between quantities or relationships between statements and qualities.

The subject is no longer human kinship.

Even here, the relationship must be of an appropriate kind. Mere simultaneity or juxtaposition does not establish a semantic or causal relationship.

Lexical usage also records naysab for a narrow, evident path whose parts are visibly connected.

A lexical explanation connects this usage with the path’s connected parts.

This does not establish a narrow path or a continuous trail of ants as the historical origin of nasab.

The image can illuminate semantic connection, but cannot replace historical etymological evidence.

3. Al-Furqān 25:54: descent and marriage in human life

وَهُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ مِنَ الْمَاءِ بَشَرًا فَجَعَلَهُ نَسَبًا وَصِهْرًا وَكَانَ رَبُّكَ قَدِيرًا

wa-huwa alladhī khalaqa mina al-māʾi basharan fa-jaʿalahu nasaban wa-ṣihran wa-kāna rabbuka qadīran

Translation:

“He is the One who created a human being from water, then established for him ties of descent and marriage; your Lord is Powerful.”

Al-Furqān 25:54

This is the clearest positive, descriptive Qur’anic use of nasab.

Nasab occurs alongside ṣihr, both concerning relationships established among humans.

A. «خَلَقَ مِنَ الْمَاءِ بَشَرًا»

The verse first speaks of human creation from water.

Māʾ directly means water.

An explanation may examine its connection with reproductive fluid, since other verses discuss the sperm-drop and human creation.

The base translation should not silently replace “from water” with “from male semen” or “from male and female reproductive cells.”

Those are contextual or biological explanations rather than the same Arabic wording.

Nor does this establish that every human relationship arises solely through one biological route.

After mentioning human creation, the verse presents two social and family relationships.

B. «فَجَعَلَهُ نَسَبًا وَصِهْرًا»

The pronoun hu refers to the human being.

Nasaban and ṣihran are accusative nouns describing human relationships or the resulting condition.

This does not mean one person physically turns first into something called nasab and then into ṣihr.

Rather, humans acquire family relationships through birth and marriage.

The construction can describe establishing humanity in a state of possessing these relationships.

“Established for him ties of descent and marriage” therefore conveys the meaning naturally.

Fa-jaʿalahu should not be restricted to every individual necessarily marrying or simultaneously possessing every kind of marital connection.

The verse concerns these relationships emerging in human life, rather than identical circumstances for everyone.

C. Distinguishing nasab and ṣihr

Nasab here concerns kinship through birth and generations.

Ṣihr concerns kinship and connection arising through marriage.

A person’s relationship with parents, children, and siblings is nasab.

Relations with a spouse’s family and relatives connected through marriage fall within ṣihr and muṣāharah.

Lexical usage differs over ṣihr’s precise extent: sometimes the spouse’s relatives, sometimes both families connected through marriage.

The principal distinction remains clear: nasab concerns generational ties, ṣihr marital ties.

This should not become a division between paternal relatives as nasab and maternal relatives as ṣihr.

Maternal kinship can also be nasab.

A mother and child have nasab, just as a father and child do.

A spouse’s father or mother, by contrast, is connected through marriage.

D. Are nasab and ṣihr entirely separate?

They differ in the relationship’s source.

In actual family networks, however, the two can be interconnected.

A marriage establishes a new connection between families. Children of that marriage then have descent-based kinship with both families.

Nasab and ṣihr are different relationships, but can be connected within family structure.

Broader Arabic kinship terminology can also overlap.

Two labels occurring together does not establish that they have no connection or overlap in any setting.

E. Are descent and marriage virtues in themselves?

The verse ends:

وَكَانَ رَبُّكَ قَدِيرًا

wa-kāna rabbuka qadīran

“Your Lord is Powerful.”

This places family relationships and human creation within divine power.

It does not say one family is morally superior to another merely through descent or marriage.

Nor does it endorse every marriage or family relationship regardless of justice and individual rights.

Nasab and ṣihr describe realities of human relationship structures here.

Conduct within them is a separate question requiring examination alongside other Qur’anic verses.

F. Descent, marriage, and milk kinship

Al-Nisāʾ 4:23 names groups with whom marriage is prohibited.

They include relatives by descent, some marital relationships, and milk relationships.

Family rulings therefore concern more than one kind of connection.

That does not make milk kinship lexically identical to kinship through birth.

A nursing relationship can have a particular place in family rulings without changing someone’s birth or lineage.

The relationship, its lexical name, and its legal effects must be distinguished.

Nasab and ṣihr alone in Al-Furqān 25:54 do not establish the entire system of marriage, inheritance, and family rulings.

4. Al-Muʾminūn 23:101: lineage on the day of resurrection

فَإِذَا نُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ فَلَا أَنْسَابَ بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَلَا يَتَسَاءَلُونَ

fa-idhā nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-lā ansāba baynahum yawmaʾidhin wa-lā yatasāʾalūna

Translation:

“When the Trumpet is blown, there will be no ties of descent among them that day, nor will they ask one another.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:101

This is important for distinguishing kinship’s reality from its function in the scene of judgment.

At first, “no ties of descent among them” might seem to mean every family relationship is destroyed and nobody has parents, children, or relatives anymore.

Such a conclusion requires examining the Qur’anic verses together.

A. The direct meaning of «فَلَا أَنْسَابَ بَيْنَهُمْ»

Ansāb is the plural of nasab.

Lā negates the entire category; ansāba is its governed noun.

Baynahum locates the relationship among the people.

The direct expression thus denies ties of descent among them that day.

What aspect of lineage is denied still needs clarification.

Does the Qur’an say the historical reality of birth and kinship disappears?

Or does lineage cease its familiar earthly functions of support, boasting, and saving people?

The rest of the verse and other passages about that scene are needed to answer.

B. The following verses: deeds as the criterion

Immediately afterward comes:

فَمَنْ ثَقُلَتْ مَوَازِينُهُ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

fa-man thaqulat mawāzīnuhu fa-ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūna

Translation:

“Those whose scales are heavy are the successful.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:102

Then:

وَمَنْ خَفَّتْ مَوَازِينُهُ فَأُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ خَسِرُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ فِي جَهَنَّمَ خَالِدُونَ

wa-man khaffat mawāzīnuhu fa-ulāʾika alladhīna khasirū anfusahum fī jahannama khālidūna

Translation:

“Those whose scales are light have lost themselves, remaining in Hell.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:103

This continuity matters.

After denying ties of descent, the verses speak of weighing and people’s outcome.

Lineage is not presented here as guaranteeing salvation or superiority.

People face their deeds and responsibility; family affiliation alone cannot replace them.

A strong reading is therefore that lineage confers no saving function or privilege in that scene.

That belongs in the explanation, rather than replacing ansāb with “family intercession” or “tribal pride” in translation.

C. Do parents and children fail to recognize one another at resurrection?

ʿAbasa 80:34, ʿAbasa 80:35, ʿAbasa 80:36, and ʿAbasa 80:37 say:

يَوْمَ يَفِرُّ الْمَرْءُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ

yawma yafirru al-marʾu min akhīhi

“The day a person flees from his brother.”

وَأُمِّهِ وَأَبِيهِ

wa-ummihi wa-abīhi

“And his mother and father.”

وَصَاحِبَتِهِ وَبَنِيهِ

wa-ṣāḥibatihi wa-banīhi

“And his spouse and children.”

لِكُلِّ امْرِئٍ مِنْهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ شَأْنٌ يُغْنِيهِ

li-kulli imriʾin minhum yawmaʾidhin shaʾnun yughnīhi

“Each of them that day has a concern sufficient to occupy him.”

Family relationships remain named in familiar terms in these verses.

The person flees from siblings, parents, spouse, and children.

If these relationships had disappeared entirely in reality and recognition, such a description would be difficult to understand.

It is more fitting to relate Al-Muʾminūn 23:101 to the failure of ordinary reliance on kinship there, rather than erasing every reality of birth and descent.

Fleeing relatives in ʿAbasa also expresses the severity of each person’s predicament.

It does not say family affection becomes inherently wrong or every human relationship turns hostile at every stage of the Hereafter.

The subject is that difficult situation and individual responsibility in the scene of judgment.

D. «وَلَا يَتَسَاءَلُونَ»: is there no conversation?

After denying ties of descent, Al-Muʾminūn 23:101 says:

وَلَا يَتَسَاءَلُونَ

wa-lā yatasāʾalūna

“Nor will they ask one another.”

But Al-Ṣāffāt 37:27 says:

وَأَقْبَلَ بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ يَتَسَاءَلُونَ

wa-aqbala baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin yatasāʾalūna

Translation:

“Some turn toward others, asking one another.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:25 also describes questions and conversation among the people of the Garden.

Are these verses contradictory?

Different situations must be considered before judging.

Qur’anic verses can describe different circumstances in resurrection, judgment, and people’s final condition.

One event’s severity may prevent asking about others; conversation and questions may occur in another setting.

Exegetical explanations include different stages of resurrection and different groups being described.

The exact timing of every conversation or a definite sequence of stages should not be fixed without evidence.

What is clear is that Al-Muʾminūn 23:101 cannot become a claim that no human speaks to another at any stage of the Hereafter.

Conversely, other conversations do not make “nor will they ask one another” meaningless.

Each statement must be understood in its own scene and context.

E. Is lineage wholly without value in the Hereafter?

Qur’anic passages distinguish lineage as a real relationship from relying on it as a guarantee of salvation.

For example, Noah says of his son:

رَبِّ إِنَّ ابْنِي مِنْ أَهْلِي وَإِنَّ وَعْدَكَ الْحَقُّ

rabbi inna ibnī min ahlī wa-inna waʿdaka al-ḥaqqu

Translation:

“My Lord, my son is of my family, and Your promise is true.”

Hūd 11:45

The response says:

إِنَّهُ لَيْسَ مِنْ أَهْلِكَ إِنَّهُ عَمَلٌ غَيْرُ صَالِحٍ

innahu laysa min ahlika innahu ʿamalun ghayru ṣāliḥin

Translation:

“He is not of your family; this is an unrighteous deed.”

Hūd 11:46, part of the verse

The relationship of father and son through birth is not denied in this account. The Qur’an itself calls him Noah’s son.

The divine response shows that family membership alone does not determine inclusion in the promise and salvation.

This accords with the principle in Al-Muʾminūn 23:101: lineage cannot replace personal responsibility.

Yet this does not abolish relatives’ earthly rights or require indifference toward family.

Other verses command goodness to parents, care for relatives, and respect for their rights.

Thus, denying lineage a saving privilege at judgment does not deny earthly family responsibility.

F. Lineage and the criterion of human honor

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 says:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ مِنْ ذَكَرٍ وَأُنْثَىٰ وَجَعَلْنَاكُمْ شُعُوبًا وَقَبَائِلَ لِتَعَارَفُوا إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ أَتْقَاكُمْ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu innā khalaqnākum min dhakarin wa-unthā wa-jaʿalnākum shuʿūban wa-qabāʾila li-taʿārafū inna akramakum ʿinda allāhi atqākum

Translation:

“People, We created you from a male and female and made you peoples and tribes so that you may know one another. The most honored of you with The God is the most mindful of you.”

Differences between groups and social affiliations can therefore support mutual recognition.

Honor with The God, however, is linked to mindfulness.

Known ancestry, a large tribe, or famous forebears does not by itself establish moral superiority.

Lacking famous ancestry or knowledge of every ancestor likewise does not diminish human worth.

This places lineage appropriately: a way to identify human connections, rather than the final criterion of value.

5. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:158: attributing kinship to The God

وَجَعَلُوا بَيْنَهُ وَبَيْنَ الْجِنَّةِ نَسَبًا وَلَقَدْ عَلِمَتِ الْجِنَّةُ إِنَّهُمْ لَمُحْضَرُونَ

wa-jaʿalū baynahu wa-bayna al-jinnati nasaban wa-la-qad ʿalimati al-jinnatu innahum la-muḥḍarūna

Translation:

“They established kinship between Him and the jinn; the jinn have certainly known that they will be brought forth.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:158

This differs fundamentally in judgment from Al-Furqān 25:54.

Al-Furqān presented lineage as a real human relationship.

Al-Ṣāffāt describes people attributing kinship to The God, which the following passage condemns.

An actual relationship must therefore be distinguished from claiming one.

A. «وَجَعَلُوا»: they established an attribution

Jaʿalū refers to people whose beliefs were criticized in the preceding context.

The verse does not say The God genuinely has kinship with the jinn.

It says that they established or attributed that kinship.

Nasab therefore concerns a claimed relationship here.

Lexically it still means kinship; the truth or falsity of the claim does not follow from the word alone.

The preceding and following verses establish the Qur’an’s position.

B. The connection with claiming offspring for The God

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:149 says:

فَاسْتَفْتِهِمْ أَلِرَبِّكَ الْبَنَاتُ وَلَهُمُ الْبَنُونَ

fa-istaftihim a-li-rabbika al-banātu wa-lahumu al-banūna

Translation:

“Ask them: are daughters for your Lord and sons for themselves?”

The next verse says:

أَمْ خَلَقْنَا الْمَلَائِكَةَ إِنَاثًا وَهُمْ شَاهِدُونَ

am khalaqnā al-malāʾikata ināthan wa-hum shāhidūna

“Or did We create the angels female while they witnessed?”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:150

Then:

أَلَا إِنَّهُمْ مِنْ إِفْكِهِمْ لَيَقُولُونَ

alā innahum min ifkihim la-yaqūlūna

“Indeed, out of their fabrication they say.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:151

And:

وَلَدَ اللَّهُ وَإِنَّهُمْ لَكَاذِبُونَ

walada allāhu wa-innahum la-kādhibūna

“The God has begotten; they are certainly liars.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:152

Together, these clarify the principal background to verse 158.

The subject is attributing offspring and family relationships to The God.

Nasaban thus concerns claimed kinship or descent, rather than every general relationship between The God and created beings.

C. Who are «الْجِنَّةِ»?

Al-jinnah belongs to J N N and in its familiar explanation concerns beings concealed from human sight.

Exegetical reports differ over its exact referent here.

Some understand it as the jinn.

Some describe a group of unseen beings with whom the claimants imagined a family relationship for The God.

Others interpret al-jinnah as angels or a group of them.

That reading is connected with the preceding claim that angels are daughters of The God.

These reports do not make al-jinnah synonymous with angels throughout the Qur’an.

Nor should it become jannah, a garden or Paradise: the construction and context concern beings with whom kinship is attributed to The God.

For the base translation, “the jinn” is close to the form and familiar use.

The contextual explanation should acknowledge differing interpretations and the importance of the preceding offspring claim.

D. Does nasaban mean marriage here?

Some exegetical accounts describe people claiming that The God married beings from the jinn and that the angels resulted from that union.

Another account describes a claim that The God and Iblīs were brothers.

These reports attempt to specify the historical content of the claimed kinship.

The verse itself does not specify its precise form.

Nasaban alone cannot establish marriage as the necessary meaning, since Arabic also has ṣihr for kinship through marriage.

Nor does the word alone necessarily establish the particular claim of brotherhood with Iblīs.

The preceding context highlights the offspring claim, giving familial descent strong contextual support.

Details about spouses, mothers, or the relationship’s form should remain exegetical reports, rather than explicit Qur’anic statements.

E. «وَلَقَدْ عَلِمَتِ الْجِنَّةُ إِنَّهُمْ لَمُحْضَرُونَ»

The second part says:

وَلَقَدْ عَلِمَتِ الْجِنَّةُ إِنَّهُمْ لَمُحْضَرُونَ

wa-la-qad ʿalimati al-jinnatu innahum la-muḥḍarūna

Knowledge is grammatically attributed to al-jinnah.

The referent of innahum and the nature of being brought forth are disputed.

One reading takes the beings as knowing that those attributing kinship to The God will be brought for judgment or punishment.

Another takes the pronoun as referring to the unseen beings themselves being brought forth.

Neither interpretation makes the clause endorse the kinship claim.

The pronoun’s exact referent cannot be derived from N S B; it requires analyzing pronouns and context.

F. The Qur’an’s position on this attribution

The next verse says:

سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ

subḥāna allāhi ʿammā yaṣifūna

“The God is exalted above what they describe.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:159

This clearly rejects the attributions under discussion.

Al-Ikhlāṣ also says:

لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ

lam yalid wa-lam yūlad

“He has not begotten, nor was He begotten.”

Al-Ikhlāṣ 112:3

Attributing offspring and ancestry to The God is therefore inconsistent with the Qur’anic description of Him.

This does not make nasab inherently falsehood or association of partners.

In Al-Furqān 25:54 it describes real human relationships attributed to divine creation.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:158 the same kinship concept occurs in a false claim about The God.

The meaning of kinship does not change between them; the relationship’s reality or falsity differs.

This illustrates the need to distinguish lexical meaning from the truth of the sentence containing the word.

6. Related roots and interpretive questions

N S B (نَسَب) and Ṣ H R (صِهْر): descent and marriage

The two occur together in Al-Furqān 25:54.

Nasab concerns birth, parents, and shared ancestors.

Ṣihr concerns the relationship established through marriage between people and their families.

Both are real human relationships and can contribute to extended family formation.

This does not make nasab exclusively paternal or ṣihr exclusively maternal.

Nor does it establish nasab as always superior to ṣihr, or ṣihr as necessarily a subsequent stage.

They identify different origins of relationships; their value concerns people’s conduct and the rights within them.

Study of Ṣ H R

N S B (نَسَب) and R Ḥ M (رَحِم): lineage and family ties

Raḥim in its primary bodily sense concerns the womb.

Arabic also uses it for kinship and family connection.

The Qur’an speaks of arḥām and ulū al-arḥām.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:6 says:

وَأُولُو الْأَرْحَامِ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلَىٰ بِبَعْضٍ فِي كِتَابِ اللَّهِ

wa-ulū al-arḥāmi baʿḍuhum awlā bi-baʿḍin fī kitābi allāhi

Translation:

“Relatives have greater claim upon one another in the ordinance of The God.”

This overlaps with nasab, since both may concern kinship through birth.

Qur’anic raḥim also connects with kinship duties and rights.

Nasab more directly concerns descent and the generational relationship itself.

This is a tendency, rather than an absolute boundary. Both may refer to the same people in a situation.

Raḥim should not always become the physical womb in translation, just as every nasab does not mean a particular legal responsibility.

Study of R Ḥ M

N S B (نَسَب) and W L D (وَلَدَ): lineage and birth

W L D concerns giving birth and being born.

Nasab can arise from that relationship.

Walad and nasab are nevertheless different.

Walad can name a child, whereas nasab describes a relationship between people.

Two brothers can have nasab through shared parents without either being the other’s child.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:152 gives the claim «وَلَدَ اللَّهُ», followed in verse 158 by nasaban.

Birth and kinship thus share a semantic field.

The act of begetting should nevertheless be distinguished from the relationship it produces.

Study of W L D

N S B (نَسَب) and Ḥ S B (حَسَب): ancestry and social standing

Arabic ḥasab can concern the standing, reputation, and familiar qualities of a person or family.

Nasab concerns descent and kinship.

They occur together in Arabic expressions such as ḥasab wa-nasab.

Famous ancestry is not identical to genuine moral standing.

Someone from a famous family may behave wrongly.

Someone from an unknown family may show great integrity and responsibility.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 places honor with The God in mindfulness.

Social pride in lineage should therefore not become the root’s inherent Qur’anic meaning.

Study of Ḥ S B

N S B (نَسَب) and ʿ Z W (عَزَا): relationship and attribution

Arabic «عَزَاهُ إِلَى فُلَانٍ» can mean attributing something to someone.

It is close in that use to «نَسَبَهُ إِلَى فُلَانٍ».

Attribution itself does not establish the claimed relationship as real.

This matters for poetry, historical reports, and claims of descent.

A poem attributed to a poet requires independent evidence for the attribution’s accuracy.

The same principle matters in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:158: claiming kinship between The God and other beings does not establish its reality.

This does not make every attribution false.

An attribution can be true; suitable evidence establishes its accuracy.

Is every spiritual or intellectual connection nasab?

Modern Persian and Arabic can describe someone’s relationship with an idea, school, or tradition.

That fits the general notion of relationship and connection.

The three Qur’anic verses, however, concern kinship.

Nasab in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:158 should not become mere “intellectual similarity,” “political cooperation,” or “spiritual affiliation” without evidence.

Al-Furqān 25:54 likewise concerns human family relationships rather than a purely intellectual or cultural connection.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:101 concerns kinship upon which people may rely in this world.

Although the root family is broad, the noun’s Qur’anic meaning is more specific in these passages.

7. Proposed Forqan renderings

N S B requires distinguishing kinship from the broader sense of attribution.

For nasab concerning birth and family, “lineage,” “descent,” or “kinship” is suitable.

Persian nasab is familiar, but “kinship” can be clearer where it might otherwise be restricted to the paternal family.

Ansāb can be rendered “ties of descent” or “kinship ties.”

General nisbah can mean “relationship” or “affiliation”; nasabahu ilā fulān means “he attributed it to so-and-so.”

Nasīb means a relative in family usage, or the love section of an ode in poetic terminology.

Nassāb and nassābah describe someone knowledgeable about genealogies.

For the three Qur’anic passages, the following are preferable.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:101

فَلَا أَنْسَابَ بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ

fa-lā ansāba baynahum yawmaʾidhin

Proposal:

“There will be no ties of descent among them that day.”

This remains close to the wording and preserves the negation.

The explanation should relate that negation to the failure of reliance on lineage and its privileges, considering judgment and other family passages, rather than necessarily abolishing the reality of birth and kinship.

“No lineage can save them” expresses a contextual consequence, but the word “save” does not occur in the phrase itself.

That consequence is better explained in the reflection, leaving the base translation concise.

Al-Furqān 25:54

فَجَعَلَهُ نَسَبًا وَصِهْرًا

fa-jaʿalahu nasaban wa-ṣihran

Proposal:

“Then established for him ties of descent and marriage.”

This preserves the distinction between the relationships.

Descent concerns kinship through birth and lineage.

Marriage concerns the connection arising through marriage.

Nasab should not be restricted to father and son, or ṣihr solely to a son-in-law.

Legal details of marriage and inheritance need not enter the translation.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:158

وَجَعَلُوا بَيْنَهُ وَبَيْنَ الْجِنَّةِ نَسَبًا

wa-jaʿalū baynahu wa-bayna al-jinnati nasaban

Proposal:

“They established kinship between Him and the jinn.”

This presents the claimed relationship without predetermining its precise form.

The explanation should examine the offspring claim and interpretations of al-jinnah.

Adding “He married daughters of the jinn” or “Iblīs was the brother of The God” to the base translation would be inappropriate: those details are not stated in the Qur’anic phrase.

Nor should nasab become “friendship” or “cooperation,” since the preceding context concerns a claimed family relationship.

8. Conclusion

Arabic N S B concerns relationships and connections between people or things.

Specifically, nasab concerns kinship through birth, parents, and shared ancestors, covering both direct generational ties and relationships across family branches.

Broader uses attribute statements, qualities, people, or things to their origins and owners.

Attributing is not proving: a relationship may be real or claimed without evidence.

Lineage mattered for identifying families, ancestry, and social position in Arabic culture. It could also become a means of boasting and asserting superiority.

These social functions should not be identified with nasab’s independent meaning.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an.

Al-Furqān 25:54 places nasab beside ṣihr as two family relationships: kinship through birth and connections through marriage.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:101 denies ties of descent on the day the Trumpet is blown. The following judgment passages and other verses naming parents, children, and siblings favor understanding this as the failure of lineage to guarantee salvation, privilege, or ordinary support, rather than erasing the historical reality of birth and kinship.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:158, people establish claimed kinship between The God and unseen beings. The preceding offspring claim explains the context, and the following verse rejects it.

Nasab here is a claimed relationship the Qur’an does not accept as true.

Together, the uses show nasab itself as neither virtue nor disbelief and misguidance.

Al-Furqān presents kinship as a human reality.

Al-Muʾminūn denies reliance on it a saving role at judgment.

Al-Ṣāffāt rejects attributing such kinship to The God.

Kinship and descent remain the meaning in all three; their value, validity, and function follow from each context.

Nasab must also be distinguished from ṣihr, raḥim, walad, and ḥasab. They can be close in some uses without being fully synonymous.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N S B generally concerns a relationship existing or being attributed between two parties.

Nasab as kinship connects humans through birth, parents, and shared descent. It can be direct, as between parent and child, or through common ancestors, as among siblings and other relatives.

Other Arabic uses attribute speech, qualities, people, or things to their origin or owner; the attribution can be true or false.

Qur’anic nasab and ansāb mean kinship or a claim of kinship.

Al-Furqān 25:54 presents real human relationships; Al-Muʾminūn 23:101 denies their function at judgment; Al-Ṣāffāt 37:158 condemns attributing kinship to The God.

Thus, “kinship through birth and descent” is the principal common meaning of the Qur’anic uses. The actual relationship, claim of relationship, and its value or function require separate attention in each verse.
