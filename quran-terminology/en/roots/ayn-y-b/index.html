Defect and deficiency, becoming defective, causing a defect, and finding fault with others

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root ʿ–y–b denotes a defect or deficiency in something, becoming or making something defective, and attributing a fault to someone or something.

Two main uses can be distinguished within this family:

First, a defect or flaw in the thing itself. A cracked wall, broken vessel, or implement that has lost some functionality may be maʿīb. The defect may be outward or affect how the object works.

Second, attributing a defect to someone or something. When a person criticizes another's conduct, speech, or characteristics, the verb ʿāba can describe this act.

The distinction matters. Having a defect is not the same as being criticized. Someone may identify a real fault or criticize another unjustly. The verb of finding fault does not itself establish whether the allegation is true.

A witness from pre-Islamic Arabic poetry

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, contains:

وَلَيْسَ مُجِيرًا إِنْ أَتَى الْحَيَّ خَائِفٌ

وَلَا قَائِلًا إِلَّا هُوَ الْمُتَعَيَّبَا

wa-laysa mujīran in atā al-ḥayya khāʾifun / wa-lā qāʾilan illā huwa al-mutaʿayyabā

Translation: If someone fearful comes to the community, he cannot grant them protection; and objectionable speech is attributed to him.

The verse describes someone without sufficient standing or power among others, who is exposed to blame.

Al-mutaʿayyabā concerns speech being regarded as faulty or objectionable. The subject is not physical breakage or a defective tool, but attributing a fault to human speech.

The verse is transmitted in early Arabic sources under al-Aʿshā's name. Its attribution to this pre-Islamic poet is recognized, although the precise date of composition has not been independently established.

Its lexical significance is clear: the family ʿ–y–b in early Arabic was not confined to defects in material objects. It also described objectionable speech and social fault-finding.

Arabic lexicons also record ʿāba al-ḥāʾiṭu, meaning that the wall became defective. ʿĀba al-shayʾa can refer either to making something defective or to finding fault with it.

These material uses are clearly recorded in classical Arabic, but their recording in later dictionaries does not establish their precise date of use in the pre-Islamic period.

The early Arabic semantic field of defect and fault-finding can therefore be recognized without claiming more than the evidence supports about the family's first historical meaning.

The semantic core

The principal branch of ʿ–y–b can be defined as follows:

The presence or creation of a deficiency in something's properties or function, and the attribution of such a deficiency to a person, speech, conduct, or object.

The fault may be real or alleged.

If a ship is damaged and its integrity or functionality is impaired, a real defect has arisen in it.

If someone accuses another of a fault, however, the accusation's truth still needs examination.

Nor does every defect amount to complete destruction. Something may be defective while retaining some of its functionality.

This distinction matters for understanding the Quranic occurrence.

2. Forms and derivatives

Arabic recognizes various forms in this family.

ʿĀba yaʿību: to become defective, make defective, or find fault, depending on the construction and the roles of subject and object.

ʿAyb: defect, deficiency, or fault.

ʿUyūb: the plural of ʿayb.

ʿĀʾib: someone who finds fault or attributes a defect to another.

Maʿīb and maʿyūb: something or someone defective or regarded as defective.

ʿAyyaba: to make defective or find fault.

Aʿāba: also recorded in Arabic for finding fault or making defective.

Taʿayyaba: to find fault or attribute a defect to something.

ʿAyyāb and ʿayyābah: someone who frequently finds fault with others.

Maʿāyib: faults and deficiencies.

These forms are known in Arabic, but not all occur in the Quran.

The distinct branch “ʿaybah”

Arabic dictionaries also record ʿaybah as a receptacle, bag, or container for keeping clothes and belongings.

Someone might, for example, put clothes or possessions in a leather container. Such a container has been called ʿaybah in Arabic.

This differs from ʿayb meaning defect.

Some early lexicographers likewise distinguished the branches: defect and deficiency on the one hand, and ʿaybah as a storage container on the other.

Shared consonants alone do not establish that the container's name developed from defect, or that defect originally meant something hidden in a container.

Likewise, the figurative use of ʿaybah for a confidant does not justify redefining every derivative of ʿ–y–b as concealing secrets.

The Quran uses the branch concerning defect, not ʿaybah as a container.

3. Quranic use of ʿ–y–b

The root occurs only once in the Quran:

Aʿībahā in Al-Kahf 18:79.

This form comes from the Form I verb ʿāba yaʿību.

The important morphological point is that the initial hamzah of aʿība marks the first-person singular imperfect: “I make defective.”

It is therefore not Form IV and should not be confused with aʿāba.

Aʿībahā combines three grammatical and semantic features: a first-person imperfect verb, subjunctive marking after an, and the attached pronoun hā referring to the ship.

In fa-aradtu an aʿībahā, the speaker expresses his intention to make the ship defective.

This construction conveys deliberate action upon the ship. The reason for that action does not come from the root itself and must be understood from the rest of the verse.

The needy people's ship and the creation of a defect

أَمَّا السَّفِينَةُ فَكَانَتْ لِمَسَاكِينَ يَعْمَلُونَ فِي الْبَحْرِ فَأَرَدْتُ أَنْ أَعِيبَهَا وَكَانَ وَرَاءَهُمْ مَلِكٌ يَأْخُذُ كُلَّ سَفِينَةٍ غَصْبًا

ammā al-safīnatu fa-kānat li-masākīna yaʿmalūna fī al-baḥri fa-aradtu an aʿībahā wa-kāna warāʾahum malikun yaʾkhudhu kulla safīnatin ghaṣbā

Al-Kahf 18:79

Translation: As for the ship, it belonged to needy people working at sea. I wanted to make it defective, for behind them was a king seizing every ship by force.

Al-Kahf 18:79

This verse explains an incident during Moses' journey with a servant to whom God had granted mercy and knowledge from Himself.

Al-Kahf 18:65 introduces the servant with these characteristics.

When Moses and that person boarded the ship, an act occurred that appeared harmful and objectionable to Moses.

Its reason was explained later: the ship belonged to needy people working at sea, and a king was seizing ships by force.

Aʿībahā here means actually making the ship defective, not verbally criticizing or disparaging it.

The speaker does not say that the ship was already defective and he merely exposed its fault. He explains that he himself intended to introduce a defect.

This clarifies the verbal use: a defect may be brought into an object through an action.

4. Piercing the ship and making it defective

To understand aʿībahā precisely, the verse describing the incident itself must also be considered.

فَانْطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا رَكِبَا فِي السَّفِينَةِ خَرَقَهَا قَالَ أَخَرَقْتَهَا لِتُغْرِقَ أَهْلَهَا لَقَدْ جِئْتَ شَيْئًا إِمْرًا

fa-inṭalaqā ḥattā idhā rakibā fī al-safīnati kharaqahā qāla a-kharaqtahā li-tughriqa ahlahā laqad jiʾta shayʾan imrā

Al-Kahf 18:71

Translation: So they set out until, when they boarded the ship, he made a hole in it. He said, “Have you made a hole in it to drown its people? You have indeed done a grievous thing.”

Al-Kahf 18:71

Here the Quran uses kharaqahā.

Kharaqa belongs to kh–r–q and here refers to piercing the ship or creating a breach in it.

In Al-Kahf 18:79, however, when the action's reason is explained, aʿībahā appears instead of a repetition of kharaqa.

The difference matters.

Kharaqahā describes the action performed: making a hole in the ship.

Aʿībahā describes the intended effect of that action upon it: making the ship defective.

Thus kharq is the kind of action, while ʿayb is the defect resulting from it.

Piercing may cause a defect, but not every defect is a hole or breach.

A hole in a boat's hull can make it defective. Yet a broken oar or malfunctioning steering equipment can also make it defective without a hole in the hull.

In the Quranic story, the earlier verse identifies the action, and the explanatory verse identifies its purpose.

Does becoming defective mean the ship was completely destroyed?

No. The root itself does not signify total destruction or the loss of all functionality.

An object may have a defect and remain usable.

The stated purpose of damaging the ship relates to preserving it from the king's seizure. The connection between the verses thus indicates that the defect was intended to help prevent confiscation.

The Quran does not, however, specify the size of the hole, the extent of damage, the repair method, or the ship's final condition.

Nor does the verse explicitly say “sound ship.” It says the king seized every ship by force. The connection between the reason and action explains why creating a defect would avert seizure, but the precise conditions of the king's decision are not given.

One should therefore not assert without evidence that the hole was small, immediately repaired, or that a specific law restricted the king's confiscations to sound ships.

For the root's meaning, this much is sufficient: making defective means introducing a defect, not necessarily destroying the object completely.

5. A real defect and the moral judgment of the act

In everyday language, defect usually has a negative sense because it names a deficiency in something or an objectionable characteristic in a person.

That does not mean every use of “making defective” independently supplies a complete moral judgment of the act.

Someone who damages another's working equipment intending harm has introduced a defect and injured its owner.

Yet identifying the purpose behind an act that creates a defect, and the actor's responsibility, is a separate question.

The ship's story contains two distinct matters:

First, the act itself: the ship was pierced and made defective.

Second, its reason and purpose: preventing the king from seizing it.

The first establishes the meaning of ʿayb. The second explains the action's reason in this particular story.

At the end of the sequence, in Al-Kahf 18:82, the servant expressly says he did not perform the acts on his own initiative.

That qualification matters: the story involves special knowledge and circumstances initially unknown to Moses.

It therefore does not grant general permission to damage others' property on the basis of conjectured future benefit.

Nor should the root be redefined as “a defect created to save something.” That purpose belongs to this incident, not to the lexical meaning of ʿayb.

Finding fault does not always establish a real fault

The other branch concerns criticizing a person, their speech, or their conduct.

Someone may criticize another's statement because they consider it incorrect. They might also condemn a correct statement out of hostility or envy.

Linguistically, fault-finding has occurred in both cases; whether a real defect exists must be examined separately.

Having a defect must therefore be distinguished from being judged defective by others.

That distinction matters in social uses, but Al-Kahf 18:79 does not concern verbal criticism. The action is directed at the ship and concerns a material defect introduced into it.

6. Comparison with related roots

ʿ–Y–B compared with Kh–R–Q (kharq)

In the ship passage, kh–r–q concerns making a hole or breach.

ʿ–y–b concerns a defect that may result from that action.

In Al-Kahf 18:71 and Al-Kahf 18:79, the words describe two aspects of one incident.

Kharq may thus cause ʿayb, but ʿayb is not limited to kharq.

ʿ–Y–B compared with N–Q–Ṣ (naqṣ)

N–q–ṣ concerns a reduction in something's quantity, size, number, properties, or completeness.

ʿAyb concerns a flaw or deficiency that makes something defective or objectionable in some respect.

Reducing the amount of water in a vessel, for example, diminishes the quantity without making the vessel itself defective.

A perforated vessel, by contrast, has a structural defect even if it happens to be empty when examined.

Naqṣ and ʿayb can nevertheless overlap. A deficiency in an essential characteristic may count as a defect. The distinction is a tendency, not an absolute boundary.

ʿ–Y–B compared with F–S–D (fasād)

Fasād concerns corruption, disruption of a proper condition, or departure from a sound and suitable state.

ʿAyb may be a limited defect that has not destroyed all functionality.

A fruit may have damaged skin while remaining mostly sound, whereas a completely spoiled fruit is no longer suitable for consumption.

Nevertheless, not every defect is minor and not every corruption is complete. The extent and severity of damage depend on the situation or context.

Quranic fasād also encompasses a broad field of corruption in conduct and human and social relations that should not be merged into the definition of ʿ–y–b.

ʿ–Y–B compared with ʿ–W–R (ʿawr)

In uses involving ʿawrah, ʿ–w–r concerns a vulnerable, unprotected place or one requiring covering.

ʿ–y–b concerns the defect itself or making something defective.

A house may be structurally sound but unprotected against danger. In that situation, ʿawrah is appropriate.

A cracked or structurally flawed wall, however, may be described as maʿīb.

These conditions can be related, but vulnerability and the presence of a defect are not one concept.

ʿ–Y–B compared with ʿ–Y–R (ʿayr)

The family ʿ–y–r includes forms such as ʿayyara for reproaching or taunting another person.

This is close to the social branch of ʿ–y–b, since both may involve criticism.

Yet the Quranic use of ʿ–y–b concerns making an object defective. Its meaning should not be confined to blaming or shaming people.

The resemblance between the consonants of ʿ–y–b and ʿ–y–r also does not make them one root.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translating ʿ–y–b requires distinguishing having a defect, creating one, and finding fault.

The Persian equivalents of ʿayb are “fault,” “defect,” or “deficiency.”

ʿĀba al-shayʾu can mean “the thing became defective” or “a defect arose in it.”

ʿĀba al-shayʾa may mean “he made it defective” or “he found fault with it,” depending on context.

In social use, ʿāba fulānan means “he criticized him” or “he reproached him.”

ʿAyyāb can be rendered “fault-finder” or “someone who frequently finds fault.”

Maʿīb and maʿyūb naturally correspond to “defective” or “faulty.”

In the storage-container branch, ʿaybah means “bag,” “container,” or “receptacle,” rather than “defect.”

Translating “aʿībahā” in Al-Kahf 18:79

Two main Persian equivalents are suitable:

“Make it defective”

and

“Cause it to have a defect.”

Both fit the verb's construction and the verse's meaning.

For the Forqan base translation, “make it defective” is precise and natural.

This expresses introducing a defect into the ship and relates to kharaqahā in Al-Kahf 18:71.

“Make a hole in it” describes the incident but is not the precise equivalent of ʿ–y–b itself: piercing is identified by a different verb in Al-Kahf 18:71.

Likewise, “destroy it” or “put it out of service” goes beyond the direct meaning of aʿībahā.

It is preferable to retain “make it defective” in the base translation and explain the distinction between piercing and the resulting defect in the discussion.

8. Conclusion

In its principal branch, ʿ–y–b concerns a defect or deficiency, becoming or making something defective, and attributing faults to others.

Early Arabic recognizes the family both for material defects and for criticism of human speech and conduct. The verse attributed to al-Aʿshā illustrates its social use, while material uses are clearly evident in lexicons and the Quran.

Arabic also records ʿaybah as a storage container. Without historical evidence, this should not be derived from or equated with defect.

The root occurs in the Quran only as aʿībahā in Al-Kahf 18:79. It is the first-person imperfect of ʿāba yaʿību and means making the ship defective.

Comparing Al-Kahf 18:71 with Al-Kahf 18:79 reveals an important distinction: kharaqahā describes piercing the ship, while aʿībahā denotes introducing a defect into it.

That defect does not necessarily mean complete destruction or loss of all functionality. Its size and nature are not supplied by the root itself.

The particular reason for damaging the ship—preventing its seizure—should likewise not be added to the lexical meaning. The verb describes creating a defect; its purpose and evaluation come from the verses' context.

Final definition of ʿ–y–b:

In its principal branch, ʿ–y–b denotes the presence or emergence of a flaw in something's properties, structure, or function. It may also mean creating such a defect or attributing a fault to a person, speech, or conduct. In Quranic aʿībahā, it means introducing a defect into the ship, rather than verbally finding fault or destroying it completely.
