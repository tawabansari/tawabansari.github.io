The Root ف ت ر — Subsiding Intensity, Slackening, and Interruptions in Continuity

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَتْرَةٍ
(fatratin)
An interval; al-Ma’idah 5:19

يَفْتُرُونَ
(yafturūna)
They flag; al-Anbiya 21:20

يُفَتَّرُ
(yufattaru)
Its intensity is reduced; al-Zukhruf 43:75

The three-consonant root ف ت ر in Arabic is associated with reduced intensity, slackening, waning strength, and interruptions in the continuation of an activity or process. Its recognized meanings include settling after intensity, becoming soft after firmness, and diminished activity after strength and movement.

The Qur’an uses this root in three forms: فَتْرَةٍ, يَفْتُرُونَ, and يُفَتَّرُ. All concern diminution or interrupted continuity, but they are not identical. One describes an interval in the sending of Messengers; another expresses unflagging glorification; the third describes punishment whose intensity is not reduced.

1. The Root’s Meaning in Early Arabic

In early Arabic, فَتَرَ is used of something whose intensity or strength diminishes. Several established uses illustrate this meaning.

فَتَرَ الْحَرُّ means “the heat subsided.”

فَتَرَ عَنِ الْعَمَلِ means that someone slackened in work after being active or reduced their previous effort.

فَتَرَ جِسْمُهُ means that his body grew weak, losing strength and firmness.

طَرْفٌ فَاتِرٌ describes a gaze lacking sharpness or intensity, associated with calmness or lowered eyelids. The expression can also describe beautiful eyes; the root itself is therefore not necessarily disparaging.

مَاءٌ فَاتِرٌ denotes water that is neither hot nor cold. When used of previously hot water, it describes heat that has subsided.

These examples show that ف ت ر is not confined to human fatigue. Heat, the body, a gaze, and activity can all be described through this root. Their common feature is diminished intensity or strength, not necessarily the complete disappearance of what existed before.

Evidence from Early Arabic Poetry

A verse attributed to Mudarris ibn Rib‘i al-Asadi reads:

نَصَبْتُ لَهُ وَجْهِي وَكَلَّفْتُ حَمِيَّهُ

Naṣabtu lahu wajhī wa-kallaftu ḥamiyyahu

أَفَانِينَ حُرْجُوجٍ بَطِيءٍ فُتُورُهَا

Afānīna ḥurjūjin baṭīʾin futūruhā.

Translation: “I faced that day and confronted its heat with the varied strides of a strong she-camel, slow to flag.”

The poet describes a she-camel whose strength is slow to fail amid difficult travel and heat. بَطِيءٍ فُتُورُهَا refers to the delayed onset of slackening, not to slow movement.

Some sources classify Mudarris ibn Rib‘i as a pre-Islamic poet, but another account associates him with al-Farazdaq, which, if accurate, places his life in a later period. The verse is therefore evidence from early Arabic poetry, but its attribution alone cannot establish a definitely pre-Qur’anic date.

Poetry attributed to Ibn Muqbil also uses فَفَتَّرَا of a weather phenomenon. Lexical commentaries connect it with settling or the cessation of rainfall. Ibn Muqbil lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, and the date of this verse has not been independently established.

Together with recorded classical Arabic usage, these examples support a semantic field of slackening after strength, diminished intensity, and subsiding. They do not establish a single, certain history of development for every branch of the family.

2. Lexical Forms and the Semantic Core

The main recognized forms include:

  • فَتَرَ: grew slack; its intensity subsided.
  • فُتُور: slackening, diminished strength, or subsiding intensity.
  • فَتْرَة: an interruption, interval, or period during which a previous process has subsided or stopped.
  • فَاتِر: something whose intensity, heat, or sharpness has diminished.
  • فَتَّرَ: reduced something’s intensity or caused it to slacken.
  • أَفْتَرَ: in some lexical uses, caused weakness or slackening.

Among these forms, the Qur’an uses only فَتْرَةٍ, يَفْتُرُونَ, and يُفَتَّرُ.

The shared semantic core of these uses can be expressed as follows:

Diminishing, or causing a reduction in, the intensity and strength of a condition; in some uses, bringing about an interruption or cessation in its continuation.

Reduced intensity must be distinguished from complete cessation. Not every instance of futūr means that an activity ends. Something may continue while lacking its initial intensity and force.

In some constructions, فَتْرَة denotes an interval during which the process in question has stopped. This particular sense must be understood from the noun and its context, rather than extending complete cessation to every derivative.

Dictionaries also record فِتْر as the span between the tips of the thumb and index finger when spread apart: a hand-based measure. It is listed under ف ت ر, but its historical connection with slackening and subsiding has not been clearly established. An image of spreading the fingers should therefore not be presented as the certain origin of every meaning of the root.

3. Qur’anic Uses

A. فَتْرَةٍ — An Interval in the Sending of Messengers

Surah al-Ma’idah 5:19

يَٰٓأَهْلَ الْكِتَٰبِ قَدْ جَآءَكُمْ رَسُولُنَا يُبَيِّنُ لَكُمْ عَلَىٰ فَتْرَةٍ مِّنَ الرُّسُلِ أَن تَقُولُوا مَا جَآءَنَا مِنۢ بَشِيرٍ وَلَا نَذِيرٍ ۖ فَقَدْ جَآءَكُم بَشِيرٌ وَنَذِيرٌ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

Yā ahla l-kitābi qad jāʾakum rasūlunā yubayyinu lakum ʿalā fatratin mina r-rusuli an taqūlū mā jāʾanā min bashīrin wa-lā nadhīrin fa-qad jāʾakum bashīrun wa-nadhīrun wa-llāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr.

Translation: “People of the Book, Our Messenger has come to you, making things clear to you after an interval in the sending of Messengers, lest you say: ‘No bearer of good news or warner came to us.’ A bearer of good news and a warner has now come to you; and The God has power over all things.”

Here فَتْرَةٍ refers to an interval in the sending of Messengers.

عَلَىٰ فَتْرَةٍ مِنَ الرُّسُلِ must be understood as a complete expression. فَتْرَة names a period or interval, while مِنَ الرُّسُلِ specifies that the interruption concerns the coming of Messengers.

It does not follow that The God had ceased guiding humanity during this period or that every trace of earlier revelation had disappeared.

The preceding verses of the same surah, especially al-Ma’idah 5:15, address the People of the Book and refer to the Book and material they possessed. An interval in the sending of Messengers is therefore compatible with the survival of earlier Messengers’ writings and teachings.

The verse does not specify the interval’s duration. Neither the number of years nor the identities of all the Messengers at its beginning and end can be derived from فَتْرَة alone.

فَتْرَةٌ مِنَ الرُّسُلِ concerns an interruption in a particular process. Turning it into a claim that all human societies lacked every form of knowledge, truth, faith, or guidance exceeds what the word and verse convey.

Another point is the connection between this interval and the responsibility of conveying the message. The verse announces that a Messenger has now come to clarify, bring good news, and warn. Its focus is the arrival of the message and the clarification of the case for its audience.

B. لَا يَفْتُرُونَ — Unflagging Glorification

Surah al-Anbiya 21:20

يُسَبِّحُونَ اللَّيْلَ وَالنَّهَارَ لَا يَفْتُرُونَ

Yusabbiḥūna l-layla wa-n-nahāra lā yafturūn.

Translation: “They glorify night and day and do not flag.”

The preceding verse, al-Anbiya 21:19, speaks of those who are with The God, who are not too arrogant to worship Him and do not become weary or exhausted.

Verse 20 continues this picture: they glorify night and day, and لَا يَفْتُرُونَ.

يَفْتُرُونَ is a form I imperfect verb in the third-person masculine plural. لَا negates slackening, not continuity: their glorification does not weaken or subside.

The expression is close in meaning to لَا يَسْتَحْسِرُونَ in the preceding verse, but the two do not express exactly the same thing.

لَا يَسْتَحْسِرُونَ concerns not growing weary or exhausted; لَا يَفْتُرُونَ emphasizes undiminished activity and continued glorification.

In human life, fatigue may reduce activity, but the two are conceptually distinct. Someone may reduce their activity without severe fatigue. Conversely, a person may continue working even as their strength diminishes.

Together, these expressions depict servants whose worship is accompanied by neither exhaustion nor slackening.

Nevertheless, لَا يَفْتُرُونَ alone cannot support a theory about their bodily or biological constitution or physiological needs. The verse describes their worship and glorification, not the structure of their being or the mechanism of their abilities.

C. لَا يُفَتَّرُ — Punishment Whose Intensity Is Not Reduced

Surah al-Zukhruf 43:75

لَا يُفَتَّرُ عَنْهُمْ وَهُمْ فِيهِ مُبْلِسُونَ

Lā yufattaru ʿanhum wa-hum fīhi mublisūn.

Translation: “Its intensity is not lessened for them, and they are despairing within it.”

The preceding verse, al-Zukhruf 43:74, describes the guilty remaining in the punishment of Hell.

يُفَتَّرُ is a passive imperfect of form II. This construction conveys reducing or diminishing something’s intensity. Because it is passive, the person or agent who might bring about that reduction is not named.

عَنْهُمْ also makes clear that the issue is relief from, or a reduction in, the pressure of punishment upon them.

The wording here does not mean that a person does not slacken in performing a task. Rather, the passive construction لَا يُفَتَّرُ and the context of punishment convey that its intensity is not reduced.

مُبْلِسُونَ belongs to a different root and expresses their despair. Despair is not itself the meaning of ف ت ر; it describes the condition of those discussed in the verse.

This verse shows that ف ت ر is not restricted to voluntary activity or human effort. The intensity of a condition can also be diminished.

Nor should the absence of relief from punishment be equated with detailed claims about its physical form, temperature, or mechanism. The verse affirms that the punishment is not abated without giving a technical explanation of its nature.

4. Morphological Differences and the Role of Context

The three Qur’anic uses share a semantic field but have different structures.

فَتْرَةٍ is a noun expressing an interval or interruption in the sending of Messengers.

لَا يَفْتُرُونَ contains an active form I verb: its subjects do not slacken in glorification.

لَا يُفَتَّرُ عَنْهُمْ contains a passive form II verb, negating a reduction in the intensity of punishment.

These differences arise from morphology, negation, the preposition, and the sentence’s context.

A shared root does not mean that فَتْرَة always denotes an interval between two Messengers, that فُتُور always means spiritual weakness, or that يَفْتُرُ always describes the complete cessation of an activity.

Conversely, emphasizing differences between derivatives should not erase their clear semantic connection. All three uses concern diminished intensity or an interruption in something’s continuity.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

ف ت ر (فَتَرَ) and ف ت أ (فَتَأَ)

These roots overlap in some expressions concerning continuation or cessation, but their usual constructions differ.

In Yusuf 12:85, تَفْتَؤُا, with negation understood from the oath construction, means “you do not stop remembering Joseph.”

In al-Anbiya 21:20, لَا يَفْتُرُونَ negates slackening and subsiding activity.

The first highlights not ceasing an activity; the second highlights undiminished strength and continued activity.

This difference is clear in the two verses, but it should not become an absolute rule separating every use of the two roots throughout Arabic.

ف ت ر (فَتَرَ) and و ن ي (وَنَى)

و ن ي is associated with slackening, falling short, and reduced effort in performing a task.

In Ta-Ha 20:42, Moses and Aaron are told: وَلَا تَنِيَا فِي ذِكْرِي — “Do not slacken in My remembrance.”

لَا تَنِيَا and لَا يَفْتُرُونَ can both be rendered through equivalents of slackening: “do not slacken” and “they do not flag.”

Nevertheless, ف ت ر covers a broad field of diminishing intensity and subsiding, whereas و ن ي in this context is closer to falling short or slackening in a duty.

ف ت ر (فَتَرَ) and و ه ن (وَهَنَ)

وَهَنَ concerns weakness in strength or firmness.

In Al ‘Imran 3:146, فَمَا وَهَنُوا describes people who did not weaken in response to what befell them in the way of The God.

Wahn can highlight diminished capacity or firmness, whereas futūr more often focuses on the subsiding intensity of a condition or activity.

This is a semantic tendency rather than an absolute boundary admitting no overlap.

ف ت ر (فَتَرَ) and ك س ل (كَسِلَ)

Kasal is associated with laziness, reluctance, or sluggishness in taking action.

Al-Nisa 4:142 describes people who rise for salat reluctantly and sluggishly.

Sluggishness may exist before an activity begins, whereas futūr in many uses concerns diminished strength and intensity after activity.

For example, someone may start work willingly and energetically, then experience futūr after a while. Another person may begin with reluctance and sluggishness from the outset.

This example only illustrates a common distinction between the concepts; it should not be treated as a categorical rule covering every use.

ف ت ر (فَتَرَ) and خ ف ف (خَفَّ)

خ ف ف is associated with lightness and reduced heaviness and, in some contexts, with alleviation or lightening a burden.

In al-Baqarah 2:86, فَلَا يُخَفَّفُ عَنْهُمُ الْعَذَابُ describes punishment that is not lightened. It is close to لَا يُفَتَّرُ عَنْهُمْ in al-Zukhruf 43:75.

Nevertheless, takhfīf highlights reducing the weight or burden of something, while taftīr in this context highlights reducing its intensity.

The overlap of the two expressions in describing punishment does not make their roots entirely identical. Each must be examined across its own range of uses.

6. Challenges in Understanding and Translation

One possible error is equating ف ت ر with fatigue. Fatigue can cause futūr, but the root is not confined to human tiredness. Subsiding heat, reduced punishment, and an interval in the sending of Messengers rule out that restriction.

Another error is equating futūr with complete cessation. In some cases an activity continues at a lower intensity; in others, such as فَتْرَةٌ مِنَ الرُّسُلِ, a particular process is interrupted. The derivative and context determine the kind of diminution or interruption involved.

Likewise, فَتْرَة in al-Ma’idah 5:19 should not be turned into an absence of all divine guidance. The verse concerns an interval in the sending of Messengers, not the disappearance of every trace of revelation or the severing of humanity’s relationship with truth.

Conversely, لَا يَفْتُرُونَ does not merely mean performing an activity without bodily rest. Its emphasis is unflagging, unabated glorification. Bodily details or the nature of angels and servants brought near cannot be derived from this root alone.

Finally, the negation of reduced punishment in al-Zukhruf 43:75 does not restrict the root’s definition to punishment in the hereafter. The same root occurs in two entirely different settings in al-Ma’idah and al-Anbiya.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Several natural equivalents are available for this root’s derivatives, chosen according to the sentence’s structure.

فَتَرَ can be translated as “grew slack,” “subsided,” “lost intensity,” or “settled.”

Depending on context, فُتُور means “slackening,” “diminished strength,” “subsiding,” or “an interruption in activity.”

In al-Ma’idah 5:19, فَتْرَة is best rendered as “an interval” or “an interruption in the sending of Messengers.” “A period without the coming of a Messenger” can serve as an explanation, but it must not suggest an absence of guidance or scripture.

For لَا يَفْتُرُونَ in al-Anbiya 21:20, “they do not flag” is concise and suitable. “They do not cease glorifying” makes continuity explicit, but may convey less clearly that the activity’s intensity does not diminish.

For لَا يُفَتَّرُ عَنْهُمْ in al-Zukhruf 43:75, “the intensity of their punishment is not reduced” is more precise than “their punishment does not stop.” The verb concerns abatement of intensity; complete cessation is not its only concern.

A derivative should not be translated solely through a fixed equivalent for its root. The verbal construction, active or passive voice, prepositions, and subject matter must inform the choice of wording.

8. Conclusion

In Arabic, ف ت ر is associated with subsiding intensity, diminished strength, slackening, and interrupted continuity. Its early uses concerning the body, heat, a gaze, and activity show that it is not limited to human fatigue or laziness.

In the Qur’an, فَتْرَة refers to an interval in the sending of Messengers; لَا يَفْتُرُونَ expresses continued glorification without slackening; and لَا يُفَتَّرُ describes punishment whose intensity is not reduced.

These three uses show that the root’s semantic core is diminishing, or reducing, intensity and strength and, in certain constructions, an interruption in a process’s continuity.

The distinction between reduced intensity and complete cessation, and between a root’s meaning and what a sentence’s construction conveys, is essential when translating this family. ف ت ر has neither an inherently positive nor an inherently negative value; the subject matter and context determine the value and implications of each use.
