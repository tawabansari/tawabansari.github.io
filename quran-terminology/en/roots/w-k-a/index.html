Qur’anic root w-k-ʾ (wakʾ)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Leaning, resting against a support, and settling into a supported position

In Arabic, the root w-k-ʾ denotes leaning on something and placing part of the body’s weight on a support. Tawakkaʾa is used of someone leaning on a staff while standing or walking; ittakaʾa describes someone leaning against something or settling into a supported sitting position.

Muttakiʾ is a person who is leaning, while muttakaʾ denotes a place or object prepared for leaning against.

The relationship between a person and a support matters in this family. A person places part of their weight on something that holds the body or steadies their sitting or standing position. This may help maintain balance, reduce fatigue, or make sitting comfortable.

The Qur’an uses this root in three principal settings: Moses leaning on his staff, preparing a reclining place for guests in Joseph’s story, and reclining on couches and bedding in descriptions of comfort and enjoyment.

1. The root in early and classical Arabic

Leaning on a staff and sitting on something that supports the body are two established uses of the w-k-ʾ family in early Arabic.

Lexicons record tawakkaʾa ʿalā al-shayʾ and ittakaʾa ʿalayhi as leaning on something. Al-tukaʾah also denotes a staff or another object on which a person leans while walking.

These uses show that leaning need not involve sitting. A person may stand and lean on a staff, or sit with part of their weight resting on a cushion, backrest, or other support.

A verse attributed to ʿAbdah ibn al-Ṭabīb, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

حَتَّى اتَّكَأْنَا عَلَى فُرُشٍ يُزَيِّنُهَا
مِنْ جَيِّدِ الرَّقْمِ أَزْوَاجٌ تَهَاوِيلُ

ḥattā ittakaʾnā ʿalā furushin yuzayyinuhā / min jayyidi al-raqmi azwājun tahāwīlu

Translation: “Until we reclined on bedding adorned with varied, beautiful woven patterns.”

The poet describes a gathering furnished with patterned bedding, drinking vessels, and equipment for entertaining guests.

Ittakaʾnā here refers to settling onto bedding and reclining at a gathering. Its combination with ʿalā furush, “on bedding,” identifies bodily reliance on a place prepared for sitting and ease.

ʿAbdah ibn al-Ṭabīb lived both before and after the advent of Islam. The same ode mentions Kufa, so it cannot establish that the verse was composed before Islam. It helps document Arabic usage near the early Islamic period but does not independently prove the date of a pre-Qur’anic usage.

Another example occurs in the poetry of Jamīl ibn Maʿmar, an Umayyad poet:

فَظَلِلْنَا بِنِعْمَةٍ وَاتَّكَأْنَا
وَشَرِبْنَا الْحَلَالَ مِنْ قُلَلِهِ

fa-ẓalilnā bi-niʿmatin wa-ittakaʾnā / wa-sharibnā al-ḥalāla min qulalihi

Translation: “We spent the day in comfort and ate, and drank the lawful drink from its jars.”

Here ittakaʾnā accompanies drinking; lexical explanations also understand it as eating or being entertained with food.

This usage relates to the practice of sitting and reclining while eating. Since people sat on bedding and supports at such gatherings, ittakaʾa could in certain expressions refer to attending a meal and eating.

This does not mean that ittakaʾa always equaled akala, “to eat.” The sense of eating depends on the setting and the established usage of the expression.

This example also belongs to the Islamic period and should not be presented as pre-Islamic poetry.

The independently datable evidence reviewed here does not suffice to establish when every branch of this root emerged before the Qur’an. Nevertheless, Qur’anic usage, classical Arabic descriptions, and poetry near the period of revelation clarify the senses of bodily support and reclining.

Some early lexicographers also associated this family with wikāʾ, the cord that closes the mouth of a waterskin or bag, using the image of firmness and securing something to explain supported sitting.

That analogy may help explain support and stability, but it does not prove that “leaning” historically developed from “tying the mouth of a waterskin.” Forms associated with closing a waterskin require separate morphological and historical investigation.

In the clear Qur’anic uses of w-k-ʾ, the principal image is leaning and placing the body on a support, rather than tying something with a cord.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • Wakaʾa: a basic form recorded for this family in lexicons.
  • Tawakkaʾa: leaned on something, especially while standing or walking.
  • Tawakkuʾ: the act of leaning or resting on a support.
  • Ittakaʾa: reclined or settled into a supported position.
  • Ittikāʾ: leaning or reclining against a support.
  • Muttakiʾ: someone who is leaning or reclining.
  • Muttakaʾ: a support, reclining place, or place prepared for sitting.
  • Al-tukaʾah: a supporting object such as a staff; in another use, someone who frequently leans on things.
  • Atkaʾa: positioned someone to recline or supplied them with a support.
  • Awkaʾa: in some lexical usages, set up or supplied a support for someone.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The w-k-ʾ family occurs eleven times in the Qur’an, in four morphological groups:

1. Atawakkaʾu, a Form V verb, in Ṭā Hā 20:18.

2. Yattakiʾūna, a Form VIII verb, in Al-Zukhruf 43:34.

3. The active participles muttakiʾīna and muttakiʾūna in eight occurrences: Al-Kahf 18:31, Yā Sīn 36:56, Ṣād 38:51, Al-Ṭūr 52:20, Al-Raḥmān 55:54, Al-Raḥmān 55:76, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:16, and Al-Insān 76:13.

4. Muttakaʾan in Yūsuf 12:31, denoting a reclining place or support.

In ittakaʾa, the initial wāw of the root is replaced by tāʾ and assimilated to the tāʾ of Form VIII, producing a doubled tāʾ. Thus ittakaʾa and muttakiʾ belong to w-k-ʾ, not to an independent root t-k-ʾ.

Muttakaʾ has the pattern shared by a passive participle and a noun of place in this form. In context it may name the place or object used for reclining; this does not make it a regularly formed instrumental noun. The sentence determines its specific meaning.

3. Qur’anic uses

1. Atawakkaʾu: Moses leaning on his staff

Ṭā Hā 20:18

قَالَ هِيَ عَصَايَ أَتَوَكَّأُ عَلَيْهَا وَأَهُشُّ بِهَا عَلَىٰ غَنَمِي وَلِيَ فِيهَا مَآرِبُ أُخْرَىٰ

qāla hiya ʿaṣāya atawakkaʾu ʿalayhā wa-ahushshu bihā ʿalā ghanamī wa-liya fīhā maʾāribu ukhrā

Ṭā Hā 20:18

Translation: “He said: It is my staff; I lean on it and beat down leaves with it for my sheep, and I have other uses for it.”

This verse belongs to the conversation between The God and Moses. In Ṭā Hā 20:17, Moses is asked what he holds in his right hand.

Moses answers that it is his staff and then describes its uses.

Atawakkaʾu ʿalayhā contains a Form V verb. The first-person singular imperfect describes Moses’ customary action: he leans on the staff.

ʿAlayhā identifies the object on which he leans. Its feminine pronoun refers back to ʿaṣāya, “my staff.”

The support here is entirely physical. While standing or walking, someone may place part of their weight on a staff to maintain balance or reduce pressure on their legs.

This does not imply a complete inability to walk. A healthy person may also use a staff on a long journey, over uneven ground, or while tending sheep.

The root w-k-ʾ itself does not express illness, old age, or physical weakness. Those characteristics require support from context or other evidence.

Moses next says that he uses the same staff to beat down leaves for his sheep and has other uses for it as well.

This shows that a single tool may serve several purposes. Leaning is one of them; it does not exhaust the meaning or use of a staff.

Later in the story, the same staff becomes an extraordinary sign at The God’s command. That event should not change the straightforward meaning of atawakkaʾu in verse 18.

Before the staff becomes an extraordinary sign, Moses speaks of its ordinary use. “I lean” retains its familiar human meaning.

2. Muttakaʾan: the place of hospitality in Joseph’s story

Yūsuf 12:31

فَلَمَّا سَمِعَتْ بِمَكْرِهِنَّ أَرْسَلَتْ إِلَيْهِنَّ وَأَعْتَدَتْ لَهُنَّ مُتَّكَأً وَآتَتْ كُلَّ وَاحِدَةٍ مِنْهُنَّ سِكِّينًا وَقَالَتِ اخْرُجْ عَلَيْهِنَّ ۖ فَلَمَّا رَأَيْنَهُ أَكْبَرْنَهُ وَقَطَّعْنَ أَيْدِيَهُنَّ وَقُلْنَ حَاشَ لِلَّهِ مَا هَٰذَا بَشَرًا إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا مَلَكٌ كَرِيمٌ

fa-lammā samiʿat bi-makrihinna arsalat ilayhinna wa-aʿtadat lahunna muttakaʾan wa-ātat kulla wāḥidatin minhunna sikkīnan wa-qālati ukhruj ʿalayhinna fa-lammā raʾaynahu akbarnahu wa-qaṭṭaʿna aydiyahunna wa-qulna ḥāsha li-llāhi mā hādhā basharan in hādhā illā malakun karīm

Yūsuf 12:31

Translation: “When she heard of their plotting, she sent for them, prepared a reclining place for them, gave each of them a knife, and said: Come out before them. When they saw him, they regarded him as magnificent, cut their hands, and said: Glory be to The God! This is no human; this is only a noble angel.”

Muttakaʾan here names something prepared for reclining.

It belongs to the family of ittakaʾa. Unlike muttakiʾ, which describes a person reclining, muttakaʾ denotes a support or place prepared for that purpose.

In Joseph’s story, the wife of the Egyptian dignitary invites the women who had spoken about her. She supplies a muttakaʾ and gives each woman a knife.

Together these actions indicate preparations for entertaining guests. The knives may also relate to food requiring cutting.

In early Arabic, muttakaʾ could also denote a gathering for hospitality and even the food prepared for it.

This usage relates to ways of sitting and eating. At such gatherings, people reclined on bedding or cushions and partook of food and drink. Consequently, the name of the reclining place could extend to the whole reception or its food.

Direct translation should distinguish these levels of meaning.

In its familiar formation, muttakaʾan relates to a support or reclining place. The sense of a reception is supported by social usage, while the knives also suggest food in the context.

Some reports of variant readings record mutkan, explained as citron or food that can be cut. This differs from muttakaʾan, with a doubled consonant and hamzah, in the reading followed here. The meaning of a different form should not be transferred to muttakaʾ without examination.

Nor does the preparation of a muttakaʾ establish how many couches or cushions were present, or what kind of carpet was used. The Qur’an does not supply these details.

“She prepared a reclining place for them” preserves the direct sense. Commentary may add that this was part of a reception and probably included food for the guests.

This use illustrates the extension of muttakaʾ from bodily support to a place of hospitality, while retaining its relationship to leaning.

3. Muttakiʾīna: reclining in comfort

Most Qur’anic instances of muttakiʾīna and muttakiʾūna describe people reclining in comfortable surroundings.

Al-Raḥmān 55:54

مُتَّكِئِينَ عَلَىٰ فُرُشٍ بَطَائِنُهَا مِنْ إِسْتَبْرَقٍ ۚ وَجَنَى الْجَنَّتَيْنِ دَانٍ

muttakiʾīna ʿalā furushin baṭāʾinuhā min istabraqin wa-janā al-jannatayni dān

Al-Raḥmān 55:54

Translation: “Reclining on bedding lined with thick silk brocade; the fruit to be gathered from the two gardens is near.”

Muttakiʾīna is the masculine plural active participle of ittakaʾa.

ʿAlā furush identifies what they recline on, while the following description specifies the material lining the bedding.

These elements should remain distinct. Reclining is expressed by muttakiʾīna, bedding by furush, and the fabric by istabraq.

The same construction occurs in Al-Raḥmān 55:76, where the furnishings are described instead as rafrafin khuḍr and ʿabqariyyin ḥisān.

Al-Raḥmān 55:76

مُتَّكِئِينَ عَلَىٰ رَفْرَفٍ خُضْرٍ وَعَبْقَرِيٍّ حِسَانٍ

muttakiʾīna ʿalā rafrafin khuḍrin wa-ʿabqariyyin ḥisān

Al-Raḥmān 55:76

Translation: “Reclining on green cushions and fine, beautiful coverings.”

Both verses express the people’s posture through the same root, but the supporting furnishings differ.

Ittikāʾ is therefore not restricted to one particular kind of couch or cushion. What matters in the verb’s meaning is the body’s reliance on a support.

In Al-Kahf 18:31 and Al-Insān 76:13, this posture accompanies ʿalā al-arāʾik: reclining on couches or adorned seating places.

Al-Ṭūr 52:20 describes reclining on sururin maṣfūfah, couches arranged in rows.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:16, another description accompanies their posture.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:16

مُتَّكِئِينَ عَلَيْهَا مُتَقَابِلِينَ

muttakiʾīna ʿalayhā mutaqābilīn

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:16

Translation: “Reclining on them, facing one another.”

The pronoun in ʿalayhā refers to the couches mentioned in the preceding verse.

Muttakiʾīna expresses reclining; mutaqābilīna indicates that they face one another.

The descriptions are distinct. Someone may recline while facing a wall or another direction. Here both reclining and the people’s orientation toward one another are stated.

The meaning of mutaqābilīna should therefore not be treated as a fixed component of w-k-ʾ.

In Ṣād 38:51, the supporting object is not explicitly introduced with ʿalā.

Ṣād 38:51

مُتَّكِئِينَ فِيهَا يَدْعُونَ فِيهَا بِفَاكِهَةٍ كَثِيرَةٍ وَشَرَابٍ

muttakiʾīna fīhā yadʿūna fīhā bi-fākihatin kathīratin wa-sharāb

Ṣād 38:51

Translation: “Reclining there, they call for abundant fruit and drink.”

This shows that the meaning of ittakaʾa does not always require an explicitly named support.

Fīhā identifies their setting, while muttakiʾīna itself expresses their reclining posture.

There is therefore no mechanical rule requiring ʿalā in every use. It identifies the support in many verses, but the verb can also appear without naming it.

4. Comfort in this world and the Hereafter: does reclining carry a moral value?

Most passages examined describe reclining alongside blessings of the Hereafter. One Qur’anic use, however, shows that the action itself is not inherently a sign of faith or divine reward.

Al-Zukhruf 43:34

وَلِبُيُوتِهِمْ أَبْوَابًا وَسُرُرًا عَلَيْهَا يَتَّكِئُونَ

wa-li-buyūtihim abwāban wa-sururan ʿalayhā yattakiʾūn

Al-Zukhruf 43:34

Translation: “And for their houses, doors and couches on which they would recline.”

This continues a passage about worldly comforts for those who reject the Entirely Merciful.

Al-Zukhruf 43:33 says that, were it not that people would become one community, those who reject would have been given silver roofs and stairways for their houses. Al-Zukhruf 43:34 mentions their doors and couches; Al-Zukhruf 43:35 clarifies that these enjoyments are provisions of worldly life.

Yattakiʾūna has the same sense of reclining here as in descriptions of blessings in the Hereafter.

The difference lies in the object and context, not in the verb’s lexical core.

In the Garden passages, reclining forms part of the image of comfort enjoyed by the righteous. In Al-Zukhruf, couches and reclining places are material amenities of worldly life that even those who reject may possess.

Thus reclining does not itself signify God-consciousness, faith, purity, or a high standing before The God.

Likewise, comfortable couches and bedding in this world do not by themselves indicate sin or corruption. A person’s conduct is evaluated through the relevant ethical criteria and context.

This distinction matters for understanding the root. A physical posture may be praised in one context and simply describe material comfort in another, without any change in its lexical meaning.

5. Leaning, sitting, and resting: three related concepts

Persian expressions for leaning are sometimes treated as equivalent to sitting, lounging, or resting.

These words do not mean exactly the same thing.

Sitting concerns the body’s seated posture. A person may sit without leaning on any support.

Leaning concerns the body’s reliance on something. Someone may sit against a backrest or stand leaning on a staff.

Resting is a purpose or result sometimes associated with sitting and leaning. Yet leaning is not always for rest: Moses leans on his staff during his daily activities.

Qur’anic uses make these distinctions clear.

In Ṭā Hā 20:18, Moses describes leaning on his staff in a context of everyday activity.

In Yūsuf 12:31, a reclining place is prepared for a gathering.

In the Garden passages, reclining appears alongside couches, bedding, food, and drink, contributing to the depiction of comfort and enjoyment.

The root’s principal sense here is bodily support; comfort, hospitality, and rest are understood from the situation and accompanying words.

6. Comparison with related roots

W-k-ʾ and w-k-l: physical support and entrusting an affair

These roots resemble one another in writing, and Persian sometimes explains both through the same expression for reliance. Their semantic fields nevertheless differ.

In Qur’anic usage, w-k-ʾ expresses bodily reliance on something. Moses leans on a staff, and people recline on couches and bedding.

W-k-l concerns entrusting an affair, delegating responsibility, and relying on someone to carry out or manage what has been entrusted.

In expressions such as ʿalā Allāhi tawakkaltu, tawakkul means trusting The God and entrusting one’s affair to Him, rather than physically leaning on Him.

The distinction matters because the resemblance between tawakkaʾa and tawakkala can lead to confusion between the two concepts.

A person may lean on a staff while walking yet place their ultimate trust in The God. Using a material support does not itself contradict tawakkul, just as tawakkul does not mean dispensing with the tools and means of life.

This conclusion about practical means and trust comes from comparing the two semantic families; the entire concept of tawakkul cannot be derived from w-k-ʾ.

W-k-ʾ and s-n-d: leaning and support

In physical usage, sanad concerns something that a person or object relies on or receives support from.

W-k-ʾ likewise concerns leaning and support.

Sanad can foreground the supporting object itself, whereas ittikāʾ more directly emphasizes the action of the person leaning on it.

In modern uses, sanad may also mean scholarly or legal support, or a document. These meanings cannot be transferred to Qur’anic muttakiʾīna without justification.

Study of s-n-d

W-k-ʾ and r-k-n: support and inclination toward something

In Qur’anic usage, rukūn may denote inclining, relying, or leaning toward someone or something.

Hūd 11:113 prohibits inclining toward those who have done wrong.

Ittikāʾ in passages about couches and a staff expresses physical leaning. Rukūn in that context concerns inclination and reliance in human and ethical relationships.

The families share an image of support, but ittikāʾ does not itself mean following a wrongdoer or surrendering one’s agency to another.

Study of r-k-n

W-k-ʾ and j-l-s: sitting and reclining

Julūs concerns sitting, while ittikāʾ emphasizes leaning on a support.

Two people may sit on the same couch, one leaning against its back and the other sitting without support.

Arabic may use muttakaʾ for a sitting gathering, but this extension does not make ittikāʾ and julūs interchangeable in every context.

Thus “gathering” in Yūsuf 12:31 may convey the word’s social usage, whereas “reclining place” makes its relationship to the root clearer.

Study of j-l-s

W-k-ʾ and ḍ-j-ʿ: reclining and lying down

Ḍujūʿ concerns lying down and placing the body in a recumbent position.

Ittikāʾ does not necessarily mean lying down.

Someone may sit leaning against a support at their side or back without lying down.

Consequently, translating every instance of muttakiʾīna as “lying down” is inaccurate. A person may sometimes recline in a partly recumbent posture, but the root alone does not establish that specific position.

7. Challenges of understanding and translation

One common difficulty is attributing a complete picture of ease, rest, and enjoyment to muttakiʾīna itself.

For example, it might be rendered as “resting peacefully and without care on luxurious couches.”

That wording may convey the broader depiction of the Garden’s blessings, but it combines several distinct meanings within a single word.

“Reclining” expresses the derivative itself. Comfort and enjoyment come from the context. Couches, fine bedding, and their qualities are expressed by other words.

A second challenge is restricting ittikāʾ to leaning on one side of the body.

Some lexical explanations describe leaning to one side; other uses also call someone muttakiʾ when firmly settled in a sitting place. An explicitly separate backrest is therefore not required in every lexical definition.

Not every Qur’anic instance can be restricted to one exact posture, such as leaning on the right or left side.

A third challenge concerns muttakaʾ in Joseph’s story.

Rendering it only as “food” obscures its relationship to a reclining place. Rendering it only as “a cushion,” however, specifies an object and shape that the verse does not identify.

“Reclining place” has a wider scope, encompassing a support, bedding, or a gathering prepared for sitting.

The invitation and knives also indicate hospitality, but the word itself does not identify the food or the number of furnishings.

8. Suggested translations for Forqan

For tawakkaʾa, “leaned” or “leaned on something” are suitable.

In Ṭā Hā 20:18, “I lean on it” is the clearest rendering, since the object is Moses’ staff.

For ittakaʾa, “reclined” or “leaned against something” is suitable. At a gathering, “sat leaning against a support” may also clarify the meaning, but not all uses should be restricted to sitting.

For muttakiʾ, “reclining” or “someone who is leaning” is natural.

For muttakiʾīna and muttakiʾūna, “reclining” or “they recline” is suggested. When ʿalā follows, the object supporting them should also be expressed naturally in translation.

For muttakaʾ, “support” or “reclining place” is appropriate. In a context of hospitality, “a gathering prepared for reclining” may also be used.

Suggested renderings of the principal Qur’anic uses follow:

Ṭā Hā 20:18

“It is my staff; I lean on it.”

Yūsuf 12:31

“And she prepared a reclining place for them.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:34

“And couches on which they would recline.”

Yā Sīn 36:56

“They and their spouses are in shade, reclining on couches.”

Ṣād 38:51

“Reclining there, they call for abundant fruit and drink.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:54

“Reclining on bedding lined with thick silk brocade.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:16

“Reclining on them, facing one another.”

All these renderings preserve leaning or reclining. Other details—comfort, food, the type of bedding, and the direction people face—come from their respective words.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root w-k-ʾ denotes leaning on something. In physical use, a person places part of their body’s weight on a staff, cushion, bedding, or another supporting place.

This can occur while standing, walking, or sitting. It is not limited to sitting or lying down.

The Qur’an uses atawakkaʾu of Moses leaning on his staff. Muttakaʾan in Joseph’s story refers to a place prepared for reclining and hospitality. Muttakiʾīna and related forms describe people reclining on couches and bedding.

In most instances, reclining contributes to the description of blessings in the Hereafter. Al-Zukhruf 43:34, however, presents the same action in worldly life, involving material amenities for those who reject. Reclining therefore carries no fixed moral value in itself.

W-k-ʾ must also be distinguished from w-k-l. Tawakkuʾ concerns reliance on a physical object or place that supports the body; tawakkul concerns trust and entrusting an affair to another. Their overlapping Persian translations do not make them the same root.

The form muttakaʾ relates to a reclining place, while hospitality and food are clarified by social usage and the story’s context.

The principal definition of w-k-ʾ is leaning and placing part of the body’s weight on a support. Lexical usage also includes settling firmly into a sitting place. This field of meaning connects Moses’ staff, the women’s reception, and the couches and bedding mentioned in the Qur’an. The purpose and value of the posture—assistance in walking, attending a gathering, or enjoying comfort—depend on each verse’s context.
