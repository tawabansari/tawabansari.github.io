1. Introduction to the Root

The root “SH Q Q” in Arabic denotes splitting something that had been whole, the emergence of a breach or rupture within it, and the formation of two distinct parts or two sides. “شَقَّ الشيءَ” means he split something; “اِنْشَقَّ” means it split open; “شِقّ” is one of the two halves or two sides of a thing; and “شِقاق” emerges when two persons or two groups stand on two opposing sides.

This root was used before the revelation of the Qur'an in several material and social fields. Al-Nabighah al-Dhubyani, in an image of the rising dawn, says:

وَقَدْ شَقَّ أَعْلَى الصُّبْحِ أَوْ كَادَ يَسْطَعُ

In this image, the light of morning seems to split the darkness and open the path of its own appearance.

Labid ibn Rabi'ah also uses “شُقَّة” for a distant land or distance of travel:

رَحَلْنَ لِشُقَّةٍ وَنَصَبْنَ نَصْبًا

The camels set out to cross a distant road and endured the hardship of travel. This use can be compared with “بَعُدَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الشُّقَّةُ” in the Qur'an.

Zuhayr ibn Janab al-Kalbi also uses “شِقاق” for an opposition in which a person continually chooses the contrary side:

أَمِيرُ شِقَاقٍ إِنْ أُقِمْ لَا يُقِمْ مَعِي
وَيَرْحَلْ وَإِنْ أَرْحَلْ يُقِمْ وَيُخَالِفُ

The line itself explains the image of shiqaq: if I stay, he goes; and if I go, he stays. Each one has taken a different “shiq,” a different side.

These witnesses show that before Islam the main branches of this word-family were already known:

  • splitting and opening;
  • rising through and making a way from within something;
  • half, side, and separated direction;
  • a distant road or land;
  • hardship that wears down human capacity;
  • opposition that places people on two opposing sides.

The path of this study also begins from this material image: first, the splitting of earth, stone, sky, and moon is examined; then the meaning reaches hardship and distant travel; and finally it moves into family, social, and creedal ruptures in “شِقاق” and “مُشَاقَّة”.

2. Morphological Structure of the Root

“SH Q Q” is a triliteral, sound, doubled root; that is, its second and third root letters are both qaf. These two qafs remain visible in some forms:

  • شَقَقْنَا;
  • يَشْقُقْ;
  • شِقَاق.

But in many constructions they merge into one another and appear as a doubled qaf:

  • شَقَّ;
  • شِقّ;
  • أَشَقّ;
  • اِنْشَقَّ;
  • شَاقَّ.

Therefore, the shaddah on the qaf in “شَقَّ” and “شِقّ” is not merely a sign of stronger pronunciation; it contains two original letters of the root.

The form “يَشَّقَّقُ” in the verse “وَإِنَّ مِنْهَا لَمَا يَشَّقَّقُ” is also an assimilated form from “يَتَشَقَّقُ”. The ta of the form tafa''ul has assimilated into the shin, producing a doubled shin.

4. The Basic Material Image

The most tangible image of this root is a relatively whole object in which a crack or split appears. This object may be wood, stone, earth, a wall, skin, or cloth.

Several stages can be recognized in this event:

  • something was previously connected or closed;
  • a force acts on it from within or from outside;
  • its continuity is broken;
  • a breach, path, or distance appears between its parts;
  • the two sides of the split become distinct from one another.

“شَقّ” does not necessarily mean the complete separation of two pieces. A body may merely crack, or a path may open in it, without its parts becoming fully separated. Therefore, the kind and size of the split must be understood from the object of the verb and the context of the sentence.

5. The Semantic Core

The semantic core of this root can be stated as follows:

The breaking of continuity and the emergence of two sides, a breach, or a distance within something that had previously been whole or connected.

The other branches of the root can be understood from this image:

  • “شِقّ” is one of the two halves or two sides that have come about;
  • “شَقِيق” is someone who shares a common origin and close bond with another;
  • “شِقاق” is the placing of persons on two opposing sides;
  • “مَشَقَّة” is hardship that wears down human capacity;
  • “شُقَّة” is a distant road or land that has placed distance between traveler and destination.

However, morphological structure and context create a specific meaning in each derivative. Not every form of this family should be translated directly back into “splitting.”

6. Lexical Branches of the Root

6.1. Splitting a Body

“شَقَّ الشيءَ” means he caused a split in something or opened it apart. This is the earliest and most material use of the root.

Splitting may be:

  • shallow or deep;
  • lead to complete separation or only to cracking;
  • arise from an external force or an internal pressure;
  • destructive, or a ground for the emergence of something new.

The splitting of the earth so that vegetation may emerge is a life-giving split; but the splitting of a firm structure may be a sign that its previous order is passing away.

6.2. Half and Side

When something is divided into two parts, each part is its “شِقّ”. From here “شِقّ” is used for half, flank, side, and direction of a thing.

This meaning is the basis for understanding “شِقاق”: two opponents each stand in a different shiq and side.

6.3. “شَقِيق”: Bond from a Shared Origin

“شَقِيق” in Arabic is used for a full brother, meaning a brother from the same father and mother. In its lexical explanation, two brothers are imagined as if each is a half or counterpart of the other, and both arise from a shared origin. For this reason, “شَقِيقُ النَّفْس” is also used for someone very close to a person.

“شَقِيق” does not occur in the Qur'an, but knowing it is useful for understanding the semantic field of the root. This word shows that “splitting” does not always mean estrangement: two halves may remain related to one another and preserve their shared origin despite distinction.

By contrast, “شِقاق” takes shape when the two sides are not only distinct from one another, but also stand on opposing sides. Therefore, the determining element in shiqaq is not mere duality; it is opposition and incompatibility between two sides.

6.4. Becoming Difficult and Exhausting

“شَقَّ عليه الأمر” means the matter became heavy and difficult for him. In this use, there is no material split; what is present is pressure and hardship that wears down a person's capacity.

The Persian word “مشقت” is taken from the Arabic “مَشَقَّة”. The word “مَشَقَّة” itself does not occur in the Qur'an, but this semantic branch is present in the forms “أَنْ أَشُقَّ عَلَيْكَ”, “أَشَقُّ”, and “بِشِقِّ الْأَنْفُسِ”.

6.5. Road and Distant Travel

“شُقَّة” is said of a journey, destination, road, or distant land. Here the distance that the traveler must cross is highlighted.

The remoteness of the destination is usually accompanied by difficulty, but “شُقَّة” in itself is not merely the name of a feeling of hardship; it points to an extension and distance that has been placed between traveler and destination.

6.6. Opposition and Social Rupture

“شِقاق” is an opposition in which the shared bond is split and the parties stand on two opposing sides. Therefore, every small difference of opinion is not “شِقاق”.

In shiqaq, these elements are usually visible:

  • the existence of a relationship or shared ground;
  • the emergence of a breach in that relationship;
  • the parties taking two opposing directions;
  • the difficulty of convergence and repair.

6.7. Derivation of Words

The technical word “اشتقاق” also belongs to this family. Lexicographers related it to taking part of one form and making from it a related form, as though one form or branch were taken from a shared material.

But “اشتقاق” as a technical term of morphology and word-formation does not occur in the Qur'an, and its technical meaning should not be imposed on the Qur'anic uses of the root.

7. Qur'anic Forms

The root “SH Q Q” appears in the Qur'an in the following forms:

  • “أَشُقَّ”: that I make difficult and heavy;
  • “شَقَقْنَا”: We split;
  • “شَاقُّوا”: they stood on the opposing side;
  • “يُشَاقِقْ، تُشَاقُّونَ”: to oppose and confront;
  • “يَشَّقَّقُ، تَشَقَّقُ”: it cracks or is split open;
  • “اِنْشَقَّ، اِنْشَقَّتْ، تَنْشَقُّ”: it split open or will split open;
  • “أَشَقُّ”: more difficult and more exhausting;
  • “شِقّ”: hardship and wearing down of capacity;
  • “شَقًّا”: the verbal noun of splitting;
  • “شُقَّة”: a distant road or distance;
  • “شِقَاق”: rupture, incompatibility, and taking the opposing side.

This family begins from a material image, but in the Qur'an it expands into the fields of hardship, travel, human relationships, and conscious taking of a position.

8. “شَقَقْنَا الْأَرْضَ شَقًّا”

The Qur'an says about the growth of food from the earth:

ثُمَّ شَقَقْنَا الْأَرْضَ شَقًّا

Abasa 80:26

After water is poured down, the earth is split and the plant emerges from within it. Here the root is used in its closest material meaning.

“شَقَقْنَا” is a transitive verb:

  • the doer of the splitting: “نا”;
  • the thing split: “الْأَرْضَ”;
  • the result shown by context: the opening of the earth for the emergence of plants.

This splitting is not destruction. The breaking of the closed surface of the earth opens the path for hidden life to appear. Therefore, the root “SH Q Q” in itself is neither positive nor negative; its value and function are known from the object of the verb and the result of the splitting.

9. The Role of “شَقًّا” in Emphasizing the Verb

In the same verse, after “شَقَقْنَا”, the verbal noun “شَقًّا” appears:

شَقَقْنَا الْأَرْضَ شَقًّا

“شَقًّا” is an absolute object, from the same root as the verb. This structure highlights and emphasizes the occurrence of the splitting itself.

Thus the point is not merely that some unspecified change happened in the earth; the earth was opened and split so that what was hidden within it could appear.

10. “يَشَّقَّقُ فَيَخْرُجُ مِنْهُ الْمَاءُ”

In the description of stones it is said:

وَإِنَّ مِنْهَا لَمَا يَشَّقَّقُ فَيَخْرُجُ مِنْهُ الْمَاءُ

Al-Baqarah 2:74

“يَشَّقَّقُ” is from the form tafa''ul and was originally “يَتَشَقَّقُ”. This form indicates the appearance of cracks within the stone itself.

The order of the sentence matters:

  • the stone cracks open;
  • the crack opens a path in its closed structure;
  • water comes out of it.

The word “SH Q Q” alone does not contain the meaning of water. The emergence of water is understood from the following clause, “فَيَخْرُجُ مِنْهُ الْمَاءُ”. The contribution of the root is the opening of a structure that had previously been closed and whole.

This verse also shows that cracking is not always a sign of ruin; sometimes the crack becomes the path for water to emerge and for life to appear.

11. “اِنْشَقَّ” and “تَشَقَّقَ”

There is a structural difference between “شَقَّ”, “اِنْشَقَّ”, and “تَشَقَّقَ”:

  • “شَقَّ الشيءَ”: he split something;
  • “اِنْشَقَّ الشيءُ”: the thing split open;
  • “تَشَقَّقَ الشيءُ”: cracks appeared in the thing, or its structure became split.

In “شَقَقْنَا الْأَرْضَ”, the agent of splitting is explicitly mentioned. But in “اِنْشَقَّ الْقَمَرُ” and “اِنْشَقَّتِ السَّمَاءُ”, the moon or the sky itself is presented as the thing in which the state of splitting has occurred.

The form tafa''ul in “تَشَقَّقُ” highlights the process or appearance of splitting within the structure of the thing. However, no physical details that the text has not stated should be extracted from the difference between these forms.

12. The Splitting of the Sky

Several verses speak of the sky being split open:

وَيَوْمَ تَشَقَّقُ السَّمَاءُ بِالْغَمَامِ

Al-Furqan 25:25

فَإِذَا انْشَقَّتِ السَّمَاءُ فَكَانَتْ وَرْدَةً كَالدِّهَانِ

Ar-Rahman 55:37

وَانْشَقَّتِ السَّمَاءُ فَهِيَ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَاهِيَةٌ

Al-Haqqah 69:16

إِذَا السَّمَاءُ انْشَقَّتْ

Al-Inshiqaq 84:1

It is also said about attributing a child to The God:

تَكَادُ السَّمَاوَاتُ يَتَفَطَّرْنَ مِنْهُ وَتَنْشَقُّ الْأَرْضُ

Maryam 19:90

In these verses, the sky or the earth, which appears to the addressee as a vast and firm structure, loses its prior continuity. “SH Q Q” builds the image of this structure splitting open; but it does not explain its form, size, or material mechanism.

In the verse of Surah Al-Haqqah, the sentence “فَهِيَ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَاهِيَةٌ” makes the accompanying state clear: on that day the sky no longer has its previous firmness. “واهية” is from another root and adds the meaning of weakness; this meaning should not be directly merged into “انشقّت” itself.

13. The Splitting of the Moon

The Qur'an says:

اقْتَرَبَتِ السَّاعَةُ وَانْشَقَّ الْقَمَرُ

Al-Qamar 54:1

“اِنْشَقَّ” in this verse indicates the realization of splitting in the moon. The lexical meaning of the verb is clear, but the root by itself does not determine the time, manner, duration, or mechanism of this event.

Root study can say that “انشقّ” means the moon's previous continuity underwent a split; but from the structure of the word alone it cannot build a physical or historical theory about its quality.

The co-occurrence of this phrase with “اقْتَرَبَتِ السَّاعَةُ” places it in the field of signs of the Hour; but the details of the event must be examined from the context itself and from independent evidence, not from the meaning of the root alone.

14. “تَشَقَّقُ الْأَرْضُ عَنْهُمْ”

In the description of people being raised, it is said:

يَوْمَ تَشَقَّقُ الْأَرْضُ عَنْهُمْ سِرَاعًا

Qaf 50:44

The particle “عَنْ” is important in this construction. “تَشَقَّقُ الْأَرْضُ” expresses the splitting of the earth itself, while “عَنْهُمْ” shows that this splitting is accompanied by the earth moving away from over them and their becoming visible.

“سِرَاعًا” adds the manner of emergence: they come out swiftly. This speed is not part of the root “SH Q Q”; it comes from the word “سِرَاعًا”.

Here too the split opens the way for emergence. The earth, which had covered the dead, opens and they come out.

15. Splitting as a Path of Emergence or a Sign of Collapse

The Qur'anic uses show that splitting is not always one thing in value:

  • the splitting of the earth in Abasa 80:26 opens the path of plant growth;
  • the cracking of stone in Al-Baqarah 2:74 opens the path of water;
  • the splitting of the earth in Qaf 50:44 opens the path of resurrection;
  • the splitting of sky or moon appears in scenes of tremendous cosmic change and the nearness of the Hour.

So the root itself gives the image of splitting, breach, and opening. Whether that split is life-giving, terrifying, destructive, or revealing must be understood from context.

16. “أَنْ أَشُقَّ عَلَيْكَ”

In the story of Musa and the righteous man of Madyan, the man says:

وَمَا أُرِيدُ أَنْ أَشُقَّ عَلَيْكَ

Al-Qasas 28:27

The meaning is:

I do not wish to make the matter hard and burdensome for you.

“أَشُقَّ” here is a verb. It is not about splitting a body; it is about imposing a hardship that would wear down the other person. The particle “على” shows the direction of burden: difficulty falls upon the person.

The sentence also has an ethical tone: the speaker wants the agreement not to become oppressive or harsh for Musa. So this form carries the sense of making a matter heavy and exhausting for someone.

17. The Difference Between “أَشُقَّ” and “أَشَقُّ”

17.1. أَشُقَّ

“أَشُقَّ” is a verb from the form “أفعل”; it means: I make something difficult, or I burden someone with hardship.

In “أَنْ أَشُقَّ عَلَيْكَ”, the speaker negates the will to impose hardship upon Musa.

17.2. أَشَقُّ

“أَشَقُّ” is the elative adjective: more difficult, more burdensome, more exhausting.

The Qur'an says:

وَلَعَذَابُ الْآخِرَةِ أَشَقُّ

Ar-Rad 13:34

The meaning is:

and the punishment of the Hereafter is more severe and more burdensome.

In this verse, “أَشَقُّ” expresses greater intensity of hardship. It is not a verb and should not be translated as “he split.”

Thus the difference in vowels and pattern completely changes the grammatical role and meaning:

  • أَشُقَّ: that I make difficult;
  • أَشَقُّ: more difficult.

18. “بِشِقِّ الْأَنْفُسِ”

The Qur'an says about livestock carrying loads:

وَتَحْمِلُ أَثْقَالَكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَلَدٍ لَمْ تَكُونُوا بَالِغِيهِ إِلَّا بِشِقِّ الْأَنْفُسِ

An-Nahl 16:7

The phrase “بِشِقِّ الْأَنْفُسِ” refers to reaching a place with great strain, hardship, and wearing down of human capacity. The meaning is not that the souls are physically split into two. The image is that the burden of the journey presses so hard that it is as if a person must expend and wear out the very self.

A cautious translation is:

You would not have reached it except with severe strain upon yourselves.

The word “شِقّ” here is in the field of hardship and strain, not the direct splitting of a material body. Yet the semantic relation with splitting remains in the background: hardship acts as though it divides and exhausts a person's strength.

19. “بَعُدَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الشُّقَّةُ”

In the verse about those who held back from going out, it is said:

وَلَٰكِنْ بَعُدَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الشُّقَّةُ

At-Tawbah 9:42

“شُقَّة” here means distant road, stretch of travel, or destination. The structure of the sentence is precise:

  • “بَعُدَتْ”: it became distant or appeared distant;
  • “الشُّقَّةُ”: the road or distance;
  • “عَلَيْهِمْ”: this distance weighed upon them.

The translation “the hardship became difficult for them” both repeats meaning and erases the image of the distant road. A more accurate translation is:

But the road and distance seemed far to them.

“شُقَّة” and “مَشَقَّة” belong to the same family, but they are not the same word. In this verse, the distance of the road is highlighted; hardship is a consequence of this distance and part of the context.

20. “شِقَاق”: Standing on Two Opposing Sides

“شِقاق” is a verbal noun of the form mufa'alah and indicates an opposition that places the parties on two opposing sides. Its lexical image is that each side stands in a “شِقّ,” a side other than the side of the other.

The Qur'an says:

فَإِنَّمَا هُمْ فِي شِقَاقٍ

Al-Baqarah 2:137

And also:

بَلِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فِي عِزَّةٍ وَشِقَاقٍ

Sad 38:2

The particle “فِي” portrays shiqaq as an encompassing condition in which they are situated. The matter is not merely one different opinion or an unresolved question; opposition has become a settled stance.

“شِقاق” can include these stages:

  • not accepting the shared path;
  • choosing the opposing side;
  • persisting in separation;
  • turning difference into rupture and confrontation.

For this reason, “difference” in some verses is a weak translation for “شِقاق”. “Breach-creating incompatibility,” “oppositional alignment,” or “rooted opposition” can convey the intended meaning more accurately.

21. “شِقَاقِي”: Opposition to the Messenger

Shu'ayb says to his people:

وَيَا قَوْمِ لَا يَجْرِمَنَّكُمْ شِقَاقِي أَنْ يُصِيبَكُمْ مِثْلُ مَا أَصَابَ قَوْمَ نُوحٍ

Hud 11:89

“شِقَاقِي” here means the people's opposition and confrontation with Shu'ayb, not that Shu'ayb himself is the initiator of conflict or rupture.

The annexation of “shiqaq” to the first-person pronoun specifies the axis of opposition:

your opposition to me and your taking the opposite side from my message.

The verb “لَا يَجْرِمَنَّكُمْ” shows that hostility or opposition to the messenger should not drag them into conduct that would bring upon them the fate of earlier peoples. Therefore, shiqaq here is not merely a personal disagreement; it is opposition to a warning that has reached them through a messenger.

22. “شِقَاقٍ بَعِيدٍ”

The Qur'an speaks several times of “شِقَاقٍ بَعِيدٍ”:

وَإِنَّ الَّذِينَ اخْتَلَفُوا فِي الْكِتَابِ لَفِي شِقَاقٍ بَعِيدٍ

Al-Baqarah 2:176

وَإِنَّ الظَّالِمِينَ لَفِي شِقَاقٍ بَعِيدٍ

Al-Hajj 22:53

مِمَّنْ هُوَ فِي شِقَاقٍ بَعِيدٍ

Fussilat 41:52

“بَعِيد” in this construction does not describe physical distance. The shiqaq has become so deep and far-reaching that the party stands at a great distance from truth, harmony, or the shared path.

Thus “shiqaq ba'id” can be understood as:

an opposition so deep that it has moved far from the possibility of convergence.

The adjective “ba'id” highlights the scope of the distance; but all of this meaning should not be placed in “shiqaq” alone. The intensity of remoteness is produced by the connection of this noun with the adjective “ba'id.”

23. “شِقَاقَ بَيْنِهِمَا” in Family Relations

The Qur'an says about husband and wife:

وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ شِقَاقَ بَيْنِهِمَا فَابْعَثُوا حَكَمًا مِنْ أَهْلِهِ وَحَكَمًا مِنْ أَهْلِهَا

An-Nisa 4:35

In this verse, “shiqaq” is not merely a passing quarrel. A relationship that should stand on bond and companionship is exposed to being split in two, and each party may stand on a separate side.

The construction “بَيْنِهِمَا” specifies the place where the split arises: distance and incompatibility have formed between the two.

The continuation of the verse speaks of “إِصْلَاحًا” and “يُوَفِّقِ”. The semantic contrast is clear:

  • shiqaq: two sides of the relationship moving apart;
  • islah: repairing what has gone wrong in the relationship;
  • tawfiq: the two sides becoming compatible and aligned again.

Sending two arbiters is not for a superficial disagreement; the aim is to prevent the stabilization of a breach that can tear apart the family bond.

24. “شَاقَّ” and “يُشَاقِقْ”

The Qur'an says:

وَمَنْ يُشَاقِقِ الرَّسُولَ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُ الْهُدَىٰ

An-Nisa 4:115

And also:

ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ شَاقُّوا اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ

Al-Anfal 8:13

“شَاقَّ” is from the form mufa'alah. The basic image is that a person places himself in a shiq, a side opposite the other party. In Qur'anic usage, this verb is more than a single act of disobedience or a failure to know the truth; it expresses position-taking and confrontation.

In the verse of An-Nisa, the phrase “مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُ الْهُدَىٰ” clarifies the boundary of meaning:

  • guidance has become clear to him;
  • nevertheless, he chooses another road;
  • and he places himself against the messenger and the path of the believers.

Thus “يُشَاقِقِ الرَّسُولَ” can be translated as:

to take a front against the messenger and pursue a path opposed to him.

The simple translation “oppose the messenger” is correct but weak, because it may suggest an ordinary difference of opinion, while “mushaqah” indicates choosing the opposing side and breaking away from the shared path.

25. The Meaning of “Mushaqah” with The God

In phrases such as “شَاقُّوا اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ”, the point is not that anyone stands equal in rank or power against The God. The meaning of the root is realized at the level of human choice and position: instead of standing in the path of command and guidance, the person chooses the opposite side.

So “mushaqah with The God” means:

  • turning away from His command;
  • standing on the opposing side from the path He has appointed;
  • and turning disobedience into a settled position and front.

The “shiq” and “opposing side” belong to the human being's choice, not to any imagined spatial side for The God.

26. “تُشَاقُّونَ فِيهِمْ”

On the Day of Resurrection it will be said to the mushrikin:

أَيْنَ شُرَكَائِيَ الَّذِينَ كُنْتُمْ تُشَاقُّونَ فِيهِمْ

An-Nahl 16:27

“تُشَاقُّونَ” expresses their position of opposition and confrontation. “فِيهِمْ” specifies the topic or axis of this confrontation: they used to stand on the opposing side regarding the claimed deities and in defense of them.

Therefore:

  • “تُشَاقُّونَ”: you were taking a front and opposing;
  • “فِيهِمْ”: regarding them or over them.

The claimed deities are not part of the meaning of the root; the preposition and pronoun determine the topic of the mushaqah.

27. The Difference Between “شِقاق” and “اختلاف”

“اختلاف” may simply be the difference of views, methods, or states. Not every difference necessarily creates hostility or rupture.

But “شِقاق” is a stronger stage:

  • ikhtilaf expresses difference;
  • shiqaq highlights two opposing sides and rupture in the relationship.

Two people may differ, yet still converse within a shared path. Shiqaq arises when their difference turns them into two confronting sides.

In Al-Baqarah 2:176, both words appear together:

الَّذِينَ اخْتَلَفُوا فِي الْكِتَابِ لَفِي شِقَاقٍ بَعِيدٍ

“اخْتَلَفُوا” expresses the occurrence of disagreement, while “شِقَاقٍ بَعِيدٍ” describes the state into which that disagreement has led or with which it has become associated. Therefore, the two words are not full synonyms.

28. Difference from Nearby Roots

28.1. SH Q Q (شَقَّ) and F L Q (فَلَقَ)

Both roots can indicate splitting. “F L Q” often highlights a split from within which something appears or is released, such as a seed being split or morning emerging from darkness.

“SH Q Q” is broader and focuses on the breaking of continuity and the appearance of a crack or two sides.

In “فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ”, the splitting of seed and date-stone for the emergence of vegetation is highlighted. In “شَقَقْنَا الْأَرْضَ شَقًّا”, the earth itself is split so that the path of growth opens.

28.2. SH Q Q (شَقَّ) and F T R (فَطَرَ)

“F T R” in one branch of its use denotes opening and bringing into being at the beginning, and in the Qur'an it is connected with creation and also with the sky coming apart.

“SH Q Q” highlights the emergence of a split in a connected structure itself. Therefore, in some contexts concerning the sky, the two roots come close to one another, but all their uses are not the same.

28.3. SH Q Q (شَقَّ) and Q T A (قَطَعَ)

“Q T A” denotes cutting and severing the extension of something and may lead to complete separation.

“SH Q Q” focuses more on the opening of a crack within or along something. A cloth may be split lengthwise, while cutting may sever it across and fully separate it into two pieces.

28.4. SH Q Q (شَقَّ) and F R Q (فَرَقَ)

“F R Q” denotes separating, distinguishing, and turning a whole into distinct parts. This root is also used in the cognitive field for distinguishing truth from falsehood.

“SH Q Q” preserves the more tangible image of breach, crack, and the two sides of a split. For this reason, “shiqaq” is not merely difference or distinction; it is alignment on two opposing sides.

28.5. Shiqaq and Isyan

“Isyan” is disobedience and departure from command. A person may fail to carry out a command without forming an opposing front or side.

“Mushaqah,” in addition to disobedience, highlights confrontation and the choosing of the opposing side. For this reason, every mushaqah is a kind of disobedience, but not every isolated disobedience is necessarily mushaqah.

29. Semantic Boundaries of the Root

Several points prevent uncontrolled expansion of the root's meaning:

First, “SH Q Q” does not always mean violence or destruction. The splitting of the earth in Surah Abasa is the ground for the growth of plants.

Second, every hardship is not “shiqaq.” Hardship belongs to the branch “شَقَّ عليه” and “مَشَقَّة”, while shiqaq belongs to rupture and opposition between two sides.

Third, every difference of opinion is not mushaqah. Mushaqah denotes taking an opposing stance and confrontation.

Fourth, “شُقَّة” in the verse of At-Tawbah does not directly mean “hardship”; it is a road or distant distance whose crossing becomes difficult.

Fifth, “شَقِيق” and “شِقاق”, despite sharing a root, do not have the same semantic result. In “shaqiq,” shared origin and closeness are highlighted; in “shiqaq,” standing on two opposing sides is highlighted.

Sixth, the splitting of the sky, moon, or earth should not be defined by scientific theories outside the text. The root expresses the occurrence of a split, not its physical mechanism.

30. Similar Words that Are Not from the Same Root

30.1. SH Q Q and SH Q W

“شَقِيّ”, “شَقُوا”, and “شَقَاوَة” are from the root “SH Q W”, not “SH Q Q”. “شَقِيّ” relates to wretchedness and an unhappy end and should not be interpreted from the image of splitting.

The similarity of the first two letters is not proof of sharing a root.

30.2. SH Q Q and SH F Q

“شَفَق”, “مُشْفِق”, and “إِشْفَاق” are from the root “SH F Q”. Ishfaq is connected with fear mixed with care and concern, and it is not from the family of “شَقّ” and “شِقاق”.

30.3. SH Q Q and SH K K

“شَكّ” is from the root “SH K K” and means doubt and unrest in judgment. In writing and pronunciation, it differs from “شَقّ”, and shiqaq should not be treated merely as a kind of mental doubt.

31. Care in Translation

For the derivatives of this root, one cannot use a single English equivalent. Translation must be aligned with morphology and context:

  • “شَقَقْنَا الْأَرْضَ”: We split the earth;
  • “شَقًّا”: a clear splitting, or splitting thoroughly;
  • “يَشَّقَّقُ”: it cracks open;
  • “اِنْشَقَّ الْقَمَرُ”: the moon split open;
  • “اِنْشَقَّتِ السَّمَاءُ”: the sky split open;
  • “أَنْ أَشُقَّ عَلَيْكَ”: that I make the matter difficult for you;
  • “أَشَقُّ”: more difficult and more exhausting;
  • “بِشِقِّ الْأَنْفُسِ”: with soul-straining hardship and great expenditure of strength;
  • “الشُّقَّةُ”: the road or distant distance;
  • “شِقَاق”: rupture and opposition that forms opposing sides;
  • “يُشَاقِقِ الرَّسُولَ”: to take a front against the messenger and pursue an opposing path.

Translating all of these forms as “hardship,” or all of them as “splitting,” destroys the semantic network of the root.

32. Conclusion

The root “SH Q Q” begins from the tangible image of splitting something that had been whole. With the appearance of a split, a connected structure is opened and two sides, halves, or a distance appear between its parts.

The Qur'an uses this image in several fields:

  • the earth is split so vegetation may grow from it;
  • stone cracks open and water comes out of it;
  • the earth opens and human beings come out of it;
  • the sky and moon undergo splitting;
  • a matter becomes heavy and exhausting for a person;
  • a distant road is called “شُقَّة”;
  • the relationship of husband and wife is exposed to “شِقاق” and division;
  • and through “mushaqah,” someone consciously chooses the side opposed to the messenger and the path of guidance.

In the material field, splitting may open a path for appearance and life, or it may announce the disruption of a previous order. In the human field too, the separation of two sides may be only the distinction of two halves from a shared origin, as seen in “شَقِيق”; or it may become opposition and rupture, as happens in “شِقاق”.

Thus the semantic core of the root may be summarized as follows:

“SH Q Q”: the breaking of continuity and the bringing about, or coming about, of a split, two separated sides, or a distance that may be material, exhausting, or social.

And its most important semantic transfer is this:

Just as a split turns a whole body into two sides, “شِقاق” also places people who could have remained within one bond or shared path on two opposing sides.
