Blazing, spreading and visible whiteness: from fire and animal markings in early Arabic to the spread of white hair in Zechariah’s prayer

In early Arabic, SH ʿ L concerns fire blazing, flames rising, and the spread of their light or effects. The family also describes white markings among darker hair and the dispersal of people or things in different directions.

Shuʿlah denotes a flame or a portion of burning material. Ishtaʿalati al-nāru describes fire catching and blazing. Shaʿal also denotes white markings in a horse’s mane, forelock or tail.

Applied to a person, ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shayban describes white hair spreading over the head, as though whiteness were a bright flame spreading among darker hairs.

Other uses describe scattered horsemen, troops or locusts. Here dispersal in different directions is prominent, without an actual fire.

Early lexicographers connected these meanings through spreading among the parts of a whole. This helps explain their relationship but does not establish the historical origin of every derivative. “Spreading” alone is also too broad: other word families express it without referring to flame, ignition or white hair.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as ishtaʿala in Maryam 19:4. In Zechariah’s prayer it describes the spread of his white hair.

The imagery evokes blazing, but the verse concerns white hair becoming widespread and the signs of age becoming visible, rather than his head physically catching fire.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Flame: fire alight and shining

Fire is one established field of this word family in early Arabic.

Shaʿala al-nāra and ashʿala al-nāra mean to kindle fire. Ishtaʿalati al-nāru describes it blazing in wood or other fuel.

Shuʿlah also denotes a flame or a portion of material visibly alight.

Several features can be distinguished: ignition, the appearance of light and flame, and possible spread to other parts of the fuel as burning continues.

These features should not be attributed equally to every derivative.

For example, shuʿlah may name a particular portion of fire, whereas ishtiʿāl names the event of catching fire and blazing.

Ignition does not necessarily mean that all available material burns or is destroyed. The amount, intensity and outcome depend on circumstances or context.

Labid’s verse: a lamp shining in darkness

Labid ibn Rabiʿah, who lived through the pre-Islamic period and the beginning of Islam, describes lightning:

أَصَاحِ تَرَى بَرِيقًا هَبَّ وَهْنًا
كَمِصْبَاحِ الشَّعِيلَةِ فِي الذُّبَالِ

aṣāḥi tarā barīqan habba wahnan / ka-miṣbāḥi al-shaʿīlati fī al-dhubāl

“Companion, do you see a flash rising in the night, like the light of a lamp with its wick alight?”

The poet compares the lightning’s brightness to a lamp.

Shaʿīlah here concerns the burning wick or fire in the lamp. Dhubāl also refers to lamp wicks.

Oil and fat lamps provided a familiar source of light at night. The poet draws on that experience to describe lightning.

The witness matters because shaʿīlah is not mere brightness: it involves a burning wick and its flame.

Lightning is neither a wick nor lamp fire. The image rests on their similar brightness.

The line is recorded in Labid’s collected poetry and early lexicons. Since he lived across both periods and this line is not securely dated, it cannot confidently be assigned to the years before Quranic revelation.

It illustrates early Arabic close to the Quranic period, without establishing an exact date of composition.

Shaʿal: whiteness among darker hair

Shaʿal had another recognized use in early Arabic.

It denoted visible whiteness in part of a horse’s tail or forelock.

Farasun ashʿalu describes a horse with a distinct white area in its tail or forelock. The feminine is shaʿlāʾ.

Early lexical accounts distinguish the location of white markings on a horse.

For example, whiteness at the side or tip of the tail is associated with ashʿal, while markings elsewhere have other descriptions.

Thus this usage does not simply mean white as a general color.

It concerns whiteness appearing in a particular part of the animal’s hair, especially where it stands out against another color.

A white marking on the forelock extending near an eye is also described as shaʿlāʾ.

As an image, such whiteness may recall a flame’s brightness against a dark background.

This resemblance does not establish that the horse-color term necessarily originated in fire terminology. Both usages are recorded, but their historical priority is uncertain.

What is clear is that visible whiteness belongs to this family, which helps explain the Quranic image of spreading white hair.

Another line from Labid: spreading white hair

Elsewhere Labid says:

إِنْ تَرَيْ رَأْسِي أَمْسَى وَاضِحًا
سُلِّطَ الشَّيْبُ عَلَيْهِ فَاشْتَعَلْ

in taray raʾsī amsā wāḍiḥan / sulliṭa al-shaybu ʿalayhi fa-ishtaʿal

“If you see my head now visibly white, grayness has gained mastery over it and spread like flame.”

This line in Labid’s collected poetry clearly applies fa-ishtaʿala to white hair.

The poet does not say his head literally caught fire. He describes white hair spreading over it.

Wāḍiḥan also relates here to visible whiteness.

Two aspects are combined: white color becoming apparent and spreading among the hairs.

This is the same field of imagery found in Maryam 19:4.

The collection includes poetry concerning different periods of Labid’s life. This line’s precise date is uncertain, and some of his poetry belongs to the Islamic period.

Its presence in the collection therefore does not itself prove that the expression was already current before revelation.

It does show that figurative ishtiʿāl for white hair was intelligible in early Arabic.

Spreading and dispersal in other uses

Early lexicons also record ashʿala al-khayla for scattering horses or horsemen across a field of movement or battle.

Katībatun mushʿilah can describe troops dispersed in different directions.

Jarādun mushʿil likewise describes numerous locusts spreading in different directions.

These usages do not involve actual fire.

A large swarm may spread across land with different groups moving in different directions. Its dispersal resembles flames spreading through different parts of fuel.

Mushʿil in this usage means scattered locusts, not burned locusts.

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddad reads:

وَلَرُبَّ مُشْعِلَةٍ وَزَعْتُ رِعَالَهَا
بِمُقَلِّصٍ نَهْدِ الْمَرَاكِلِ هَيْكَلِ

wa-la-rubba mushʿilatin wazaʿtu riʿālahā / bi-muqalliṣin nahdi al-marākili haykal

“Many a dispersed host have I checked in its advancing companies, on a powerful, swift horse.”

With kasrah on the ʿayn, mushʿilah can describe a dispersed military formation. A blazing battle is another traditional interpretation, associated with the reading mushʿalah. The two readings and explanations should remain distinct.

Riʿāl means advancing companies or groups; wazaʿtu means I checked or restrained.

The witness belongs to the imagery of battle and its scattered companies; the blazing-battle interpretation also evokes the intensity of fighting.

The word does not establish that fire was actually used or armies literally burned.

Under the battle interpretation, the expression depicts the intensity and spread of conflict, much as “war flared up” need not mean lighting fires throughout a battlefield.

The line is preserved in older collections attributed to ʿAntarah. Its attribution is relevant, but neither its precise date nor a single interpretation should be imposed on every use of the root.

What the historical witnesses show

Three significant fields are recognizable in early Arabic:

First, fire catching and blazing.

Second, whiteness appearing in animal or human hair.

Third, groups or things spreading in different directions.

Their imagery is related, but the definition should not become so broad that the family loses its specific features.

SH ʿ L is not any movement or dissemination. Fire usages concern blazing; hair usages concern visible whiteness spreading; group usages concern dispersal in different directions.

In the fire branch, a defensible core is catching fire and blazing with visible effects spreading. The recognized branches concerning white hair and dispersal must also retain their specific meanings.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Important forms in Arabic include:

Shaʿala and ashʿala: kindled fire. Elsewhere ashʿala can mean scattered or spread something widely.

Shaʿʿala: kindled or set alight.

Ishtaʿala: caught fire or blazed; of hair, whiteness spread over the head. This form occurs in the Quran.

Tashaʿʿala: blazed or flamed up.

Ishtiʿāl: ignition or blazing; figuratively, the spread or intensification of a state likened to fire.

Shuʿlah: a flame or portion of burning material.

Shuʿal: plural of shuʿlah.

Shuʿlūl: a flame; elsewhere, a scattered part of a group.

Shaʿīlah: a burning wick or lamp flame.

Mashʿal: a lamp, torch or implement in which fire is lit.

Mashʿalah: a place where fire is lit. Distinguish it from mushʿilah, used of scattered troops, and mushʿalah, describing something kindled or blazing.

Mushʿal: kindled or set alight. Mushʿil is the active participle, “kindling,” and is also recorded for dispersed groups and locusts. Vowels and context must be checked rather than treating these forms as identical.

Shaʿal: visible whiteness in part of a horse’s hair, especially the forelock or tail.

Ashʿal: a horse with a distinct white marking in its forelock or tail.

Shaʿlāʾ: the feminine of ashʿal.

Shaʿila: of animal hair, acquired a white marking.

Shaʿālīl: scattered groups or portions.

Shāʿil: a person associated with kindling fire. The lexical comparison to tāmir and lābin explains the adjectival formation; it does not give shāʿil the general meaning “owner of property.”

Mishʿal, with kasrah on the mīm, also names a leather vessel used by some desert communities for liquids or preparing a steeped drink. This is a distinct usage whose historical connection to fire is unclear; the vessel should not be identified as a lamp merely because the forms resemble one another.

Shaʿl also occurs as a personal name or epithet. A proper-name usage does not create a new meaning for every member of the family.

Only ishtaʿala occurs in the Quran.

The nouns shuʿlah, mashʿal, shaʿal and ishtiʿāl do not occur in those forms, nor do the verbs ashʿala and shaʿʿala.

3. Maryam 19:4: the head blazing with white hair

At the beginning of Maryam, the Quran recounts Zechariah’s prayer.

Maryam 19:2 introduces the Lord’s mercy toward His servant Zechariah; Maryam 19:3 describes his calling upon his Lord quietly.

Maryam 19:4 then says:

قَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّي وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا وَلَمْ أَكُنْ بِدُعَائِكَ رَبِّ شَقِيًّا

qāla rabbi innī wahana al-ʿaẓmu minnī wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shayban wa-lam akun bi-duʿāʾika rabbi shaqiyyan

Maryam 19:4

“He said: My Lord, my bones have weakened and my head blazes with white hair; and, my Lord, I have never been deprived in calling upon You.”

Zechariah names two signs of age: weakened bones and spreading white hair.

Instead of simply reporting “my hair has turned white,” the second expression creates a vivid, expansive image.

Ishtaʿala: blazing as an image of spreading

Applied directly to fire, ishtaʿala means blazed or caught fire.

Here its subject is al-raʾs, the head.

In the surface structure, therefore, the head has blazed.

The following shayban specifies what this means: spreading white hair.

The image evokes fire spreading through material, with brightness and flames appearing in different parts.

White hair may likewise first appear in limited areas and then spread more widely.

White hairs among dark ones become visible like brightness spreading across a contrasting background.

Yet the verse describes no actual fire.

The head is neither burning nor producing fire’s heat, and white hair does not mean the body is being destroyed.

Those properties belong to real fire and should not be transferred wholesale to Zechariah’s body.

The main resemblance is whiteness becoming conspicuous and spreading across the hair of the head.

Why not simply say “my hair has turned white”?

Compare two statements:

“White hair has spread over my head.”

And:

“My head has blazed with white hair.”

Both can describe the same condition but create different images.

The first simply reports the presence and spread of white hair.

The second brings the whole head into view and presents whiteness like a flame appearing in its different parts.

The Arabic wording makes a similar distinction.

In ishtaʿala shaybu al-raʾsi, white hair itself would be the subject of blazing.

Instead, the Quran says:

وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا

wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shayban

Maryam 19:4

Al-raʾs is the subject, while shayban explains the respect in which the blazing occurs.

The image extends beyond a few white strands and highlights conspicuous spread.

It does not establish that every single hair was white, with no dark hair remaining.

The verse depicts predominance and spread, without specifying a numerical ratio.

The grammatical role of shayban

Shayban comes from SH Y B and denotes white hair, especially in the process of aging.

It belongs to a different root from SH ʿ L.

In Maryam 19:4 it is indefinite and accusative.

The familiar grammatical analysis treats it as tamyīz, a specifying expression that clarifies the preceding statement.

Without it, “the head blazed” presents the listener with a head catching fire.

Shayban makes clear that the blazing concerns white hair.

In the familiar analysis, this specification is transferred from the semantic subject of a simpler construction:

Ishtaʿala shaybu al-raʾsi: the head’s white hair spread.

The Quran instead attributes blazing to the head and places white hair in the specifying position.

This has rhetorical significance, producing a broader image of whiteness spreading.

Some early grammarians proposed a verbal-noun analysis of shayban. Both readings retain the reference to white hair; this study follows the familiar tamyīz analysis.

Shayban therefore does not mean fire, flame or heat.

“Old age” alone also misses its direct meaning, since the verse names the visible sign of white hair.

Blazing and extent

Lexical usages associate ishtiʿāl with flames spreading and their effects extending.

That feature is not equally prominent in every context.

A small fire may blaze in a limited area and still constitute ishtiʿāl.

The verb therefore does not always mean complete coverage of an entire whole.

In Maryam 19:4, extent arises from three features together: the blazing image, its attribution to the head, and the specification shayban.

Together they depict the conspicuous spread of white hair.

The breadth of the image thus comes from the whole sentence, not merely a morphological rule about Form VIII.

4. The image within aging and Zechariah’s prayer

Wahana al-ʿaẓmu: bodily weakness

Before mentioning white hair, Zechariah says:

إِنِّي وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي

innī wahana al-ʿaẓmu minnī

Maryam 19:4

“My bones have weakened.”

Wahana belongs to W H N and means became weak or frail.

Al-ʿaẓm means bone.

Bone is presented as a fundamental, firm part of the body.

By describing weakened bones, Zechariah expresses diminished bodily strength and firmness.

He then mentions white hair, a visible outward sign.

Two aspects of aging appear together: reduced physical strength and outward change.

They should not be equated lexically.

Wahn denotes weakness.

Ishtiʿāl here depicts white hair spreading.

Both describe aging, but their independent meanings differ.

Does white hair mean incapacity?

White hair may accompany advancing age, but does not itself establish complete physical incapacity.

Someone relatively young may have white hair while retaining considerable strength.

An older person may retain dark hair despite declining strength.

Shayb and wahn are distinct conditions, though they may accompany one another in old age.

Maryam 19:4 mentions both, forming Zechariah’s overall description of aging.

In Maryam 19:8 he also says:

وَقَدْ بَلَغْتُ مِنَ الْكِبَرِ عِتِيًّا

wa-qad balaghtu mina al-kibari ʿitiyyan

Maryam 19:8

“I have reached extreme old age.”

This context shows that Zechariah regards himself as far advanced in age.

That conclusion comes from the surrounding verses, not from ishtiʿāl itself meaning frailty.

Aging and the request for a child

After describing his condition, Zechariah expresses concern about those after him and notes his wife’s barrenness in Maryam 19:5.

He then asks God:

فَهَبْ لِي مِنْ لَدُنْكَ وَلِيًّا

fa-hab lī min ladunka waliyyan

Maryam 19:5

“Grant me, from Yourself, a guardian.”

He continues by describing the successor he requests.

References to age and white hair establish the circumstances of this request.

Under ordinary circumstances, having a child appears difficult for him.

In Maryam 19:8 he himself asks how he can have a child given his age and his wife’s barrenness.

This does not express despair of God.

In Maryam 19:4 he says:

وَلَمْ أَكُنْ بِدُعَائِكَ رَبِّ شَقِيًّا

wa-lam akun bi-duʿāʾika rabbi shaqiyyan

Maryam 19:4

“My Lord, I have never been deprived in calling upon You.”

He thus turns to his Lord while recognizing his condition.

He mentions aging but asks for divine generosity rather than a return to youth, in circumstances he sees as difficult.

Maryam 19:9 replies that this is easy for God.

The image is therefore part of his physical self-description, not a declaration of defeat, the worthlessness of old age, or God’s inability to answer him.

Does the blazing head signify anger or sorrow?

Arabic also uses ishtiʿāl figuratively for anger.

Ishtaʿala ghaḍaban can mean that someone’s anger flared up.

This is not the meaning in Maryam 19:4, where shayban specifies the blazing.

The word does not imply that Zechariah was angry or that sorrow made his head flare up.

The subject is white hair.

The verse supplies no particular cause such as sorrow, fear or psychological strain for its whitening.

Advancing age is clear from the context, but an unusual cause would require independent evidence.

5. Distinctions from related roots

SH ʿ L compared with W Q D

W Q D concerns kindling and lighting fire.

The Quran uses that family for kindled fire and its fuel.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:17 says:

كَمَثَلِ الَّذِي اسْتَوْقَدَ نَارًا

ka-mathali alladhī istawqada nāran

Al-Baqarah 2:17

“Like someone who kindled a fire.”

Istawqada denotes bringing about or kindling fire.

Ishtiʿāl belongs to the same field but can foreground the blazing and its spread.

This tendency is not an absolute boundary; the families overlap in fire usages.

Maryam 19:4 applies blazing imagery to white hair rather than lighting a real fire.

Shuʿlah compared with lahab

Lahab denotes a flame or tongue of fire.

Al-Masad 111:3 says:

سَيَصْلَىٰ نَارًا ذَاتَ لَهَبٍ

sa-yaṣlā nāran dhāta lahab

Al-Masad 111:3

“He will enter a flaming fire.”

Lahab describes the fire’s character.

Shuʿlah can also mean a flame, but its family includes white hair and scattered groups.

The relationship between the flame terms does not make all their derivatives identical.

Although the image in Maryam 19:4 can be compared to tongues of fire, the verse itself does not use lahab.

Details of burning, heat and flames should not be transferred without evidence to Zechariah’s hair.

Ishtiʿāl compared with ḥarq

Ḥarq means burning or setting something on fire.

Some uses involve material being damaged or destroyed.

Ishtiʿāl directly expresses catching fire and blazing, without necessarily specifying the entire outcome.

A lamp wick can remain alight and provide light for some time. Being aflame does not mean the whole wick is instantly destroyed.

The distinction matters particularly here: likening white hair to blazing does not mean that hair burned or disappeared.

“My head has burned” is therefore an unsuitable translation.

SH ʿ L compared with SH Y B

The two roots occur together in Maryam 19:4 but mean different things.

SH Y B concerns hair turning white and the resulting whiteness.

SH ʿ L depicts how that whiteness spreads and becomes conspicuous.

“His hair has turned white” reports the change of color.

“White hair spread over his head like fire” emphasizes the visible extent of that change.

Shayb names the change, while ishtiʿāl supplies the image of its spread.

Shayban does not belong to SH ʿ L, nor should ishtiʿāl independently be reduced to “hair turning white.”

The words work together to form the sentence’s full meaning.

Ishtiʿāl compared with nashr

Nashr means spreading or dispersing something.

Ishtiʿāl also involves spreading in some uses.

Yet not every spreading is ishtiʿāl.

Cloth may be spread on the ground or news disseminated without any flame, ignition or white hair.

When fire spreads through wood, ishtiʿāl includes blazing as well as spread.

Its choice in Maryam 19:4 gives a more vivid image than a general verb of spreading.

Spreading is therefore one aspect of the Quranic usage, not the entire meaning of the root family.

SH ʿ L compared with SH ʿ R and SH ʿ ʿ

These roots share letters but are independent families.

SH ʿ R concerns hair and also perception or awareness in Arabic.

SH ʿ ʿ concerns rays and radiating light.

SH ʿ L includes blazing, visible whiteness in hair, and dispersal usages.

The verse concerns head hair, but the word shaʿr does not appear in its expression.

Likewise, the resemblance between flame and rays does not unite the roots of shaʿal and shuʿāʿ.

Lexical study must preserve these distinctions.

6. Morphology and translation proposals for Forqan

The Quranic form ishtaʿala

The Quranic form is:

وَاشْتَعَلَ

wa-ishtaʿala

Maryam 19:4

It consists of the conjunction wa and the verb ishtaʿala.

Ishtaʿala is a third-person masculine singular perfect verb in Form VIII.

Its base verb is shaʿala.

Form VIII can express a state or event arising or developing in its subject. Here a distinction is visible between kindling fire and fire blazing.

For example:

Ashʿala al-nāra: he kindled the fire.

Ishtaʿalati al-nāru: the fire blazed.

The form alone does not establish suddenness, spontaneity, absence of a cause, or maximum intensity.

Those features depend on the circumstances and context.

In Maryam 19:4, al-raʾsu is the explicit subject and shayban an accusative specification.

Structurally, the verse says:

“The head blazed with white hair.”

Its intended sense is white hair spreading and becoming visible over the head.

Translation choices within the family

Shaʿala al-nāra: “kindled the fire.”

Ashʿala: “kindled,” “set alight,” or, of groups, “scattered.”

Shaʿʿala: “kindled” or “made blaze.”

Ishtaʿala: “blazed,” “caught fire,” or figuratively “spread like flame.”

Tashaʿʿala: “blazed” or “flamed up.”

Ishtiʿāl: “blazing” or “ignition.”

Shuʿlah: “flame” or “a portion of burning material.”

Shaʿīlah: “burning wick” or “lamp flame.”

Mashʿal: “torch,” “lamp,” or “an implement for lighting fire.”

Shaʿal: of hair coloring, “visible whiteness in part of the hair.”

Ashʿal: of a horse, “having a white marking in the forelock or tail.”

Shaʿlāʾ: the feminine of that description.

Shaʿālīl: “scattered groups” or “separated companies.”

The translation must suit what the word describes.

For fire, “blazing” is natural.

The same imagery can be used figuratively for white hair spreading.

For troops or groups, “scattering” is more appropriate than wording that implies actual burning.

A proposed rendering of Maryam 19:4

The Quranic expression is:

وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا

wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shayban

Maryam 19:4

To preserve its imagery and structure, one may translate:

“And my head blazes with white hair.”

A more explanatory rendering is:

“And white hair has spread over my head like flame.”

The first preserves the attribution of blazing to the head and is structurally closer to the Arabic.

The second makes the image’s effect explicit but changes the subject from the head to white hair.

The first is therefore proposed for Forqan’s base translation, with spreading explained in the lexical note. This proposal does not change the installed verse translation.

“My hair has turned white” conveys the physical state but omits the blazing image and its extent.

“My head has caught fire,” without specifying white hair, is misleading because it suggests literal burning.

“I have grown old” alone also omits the particular sign chosen by the verse: white hair.

A suitable rendering should preserve the blazing image as far as possible while making clear that white hair is spreading.

7. Conclusion

In early Arabic, SH ʿ L concerns fire catching and blazing. Other recorded uses include white markings in animal hair, spreading human white hair, and scattered groups or things.

Labid’s poetry illustrates shaʿīlah for a burning lamp and ishtiʿāl for white hair. The line attributed to ʿAntarah uses a related form for dispersed military companies, also interpreted through the image of blazing battle.

The witnesses illuminate the semantic range without establishing a uniform, certain chronology for all poems or branches.

The sole Quranic form is ishtaʿala in Maryam 19:4.

Zechariah describes weakened bones and spreading white hair, two distinct signs of aging: diminished bodily firmness and visible outward change.

Instead of simply reporting white hair, the verse portrays the head blazing with it.

Here blazing is not literal fire but conspicuous white hair. It does not mean sorrow, anger, disease or bodily destruction, although other contexts can apply the image to intensified emotions.

Grammatically, al-raʾs is the subject and shayban the specification. Attributing blazing to the head creates a broader image than naming white hair alone.

In the fire branch, a defensible core is catching fire and blazing with visible effects spreading. White-hair and dispersal usages are related in imagery but must be understood through their own subjects and contexts.

The precise Quranic sense is white hair spreading over Zechariah’s head in a way likened to blazing fire.

“My head blazes with white hair” offers a clear rendering close to the structure. The lexical explanation should clarify that white hair, rather than fire, has spread.

Within his prayer, the image expresses age and physical limitations. Recognizing them, he turns to his Lord with his request.

White hair here marks the passage of life, not the end of hope, prayer or receiving divine mercy.
