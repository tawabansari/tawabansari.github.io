The Qur’anic Root A Y D

Strength, firmness, and strengthening another

The root أ ي د (ʾ Y D) forms a small, highly coherent Qur’anic family. Its core is strength and firmness, and its verbal form develops into strengthening, making firm, and supporting another.

Qur’anic forms

This root occurs only eleven times in the Qur’an: nine times as the Form II verb أَيَّدَ (ayyada) and its inflections, and twice as the noun أَيْد (ayd), meaning strength. This limited range allows its Qur’anic field to be observed with considerable precision.

أَيْد (ayd)
Strength

أَيَّدَ (ayyada)
Giving strength

One distinction is essential from the outset: ʾ Y D, meaning strength, is not the same root as Y D Y, meaning hand. The written resemblance between أَيْد (ayd) and أَيْدِي (aydī) has sometimes led to their conflation, although the Qur’an and Arabic clearly distinguish them.

Strength in early Arabic

Early Arabic records آدَ يَئِيدُ أَيْدًا (āda yaʾīdu aydan) for becoming strong and firm, and الأَيْد (al-ayd) and الآد (al-ād) for strength. The adjective أَيِّد (ayyid) was also used of someone or something strong.

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays concerning date palms says:

فَأَثَّتْ أَعَالِيهِ وَآدَتْ أُصُولُهُ
وَمَالَ بِقِنْوَانٍ مِنَ الْبُسْرِ أَحْمَرَا

Fa-aththat aʿālīhi wa-ādat uṣūluhu; wa-māla bi-qinwānin mina al-busri aḥmarā.

“Its upper branches grew dense and its roots grew strong, and it bent with clusters of red dates.”

آدَتْ أُصُولُهُ (ādat uṣūluhu) means that its roots became firm and strong. This witness shows that the family’s field of strength and firmness is not restricted to religious uses.

From this same core, أَيَّدَهُ (ayyadahu) means that he made someone strong and added to their strength. In the simple form, something itself gains strength; in Form II, an agent brings about another’s strengthening.

There is no need to construct a particular physical image for this root, such as a pillar, hand, or supporting tool. The linguistic evidence is clearer and simpler: having strength and giving strength.

“أَيْد (ayd)” is strength, not the plural of “hand”

The root’s two nominal occurrences are especially important for establishing its meaning.

Of David it is said:

وَاذْكُرْ عَبْدَنَا دَاوُودَ ذَا الْأَيْدِ إِنَّهُ أَوَّابٌ

Wa-dhkur ʿabdanā Dāwūda dhā al-aydi innahu awwābun.

“And remember Our servant David, possessed of strength; indeed, he was ever returning.”

Ṣād 38:17

ذَا الْأَيْدِ (dhā al-aydi) means possessed of strength. The word itself does not specify whether that strength is physical, political, devotional, or a combination of aspects. Such restrictions require contextual explanation. What the root itself clearly establishes is strength.

The second occurrence is in Al-Dhāriyāt:

وَالسَّمَاءَ بَنَيْنَاهَا بِأَيْدٍ

Wa-l-samāʾa banaynāhā bi-aydin.

“And the heaven We built with strength.”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:47

بِأَيْدٍ (bi-aydin) in this verse means “with strength.” It should not be identified with the plural of يد (yad), “hands.”

In the Qur’an, the ي د ي (Y D Y) family is used for the hand:

بِأَيْدِيهِمْ

Bi-aydīhim.

“With their hands.”

Al-Baqarah 2:79

This أَيْدِي (aydī) belongs to the root associated with yad. But أَيْد (ayd) in:

ذَا الْأَيْدِ

Dhā al-aydi.

“Possessed of strength.”

and:

بِأَيْدٍ

Bi-aydin.

“With strength.”

belongs to أ ي د (ʾ Y D) and means strength.

This is more than a minor translation issue. Turning bi-aydin in Al-Dhāriyāt into “with hands” conflates two independent word families and introduces a meaning that this word does not express in that construction.

“أَيَّدَ (ayyada)”: strengthening and making firm

The root’s other nine occurrences all belong to Form II and follow a highly regular construction.

Of Jesus it is said:

وَأَيَّدْنَاهُ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ

Wa-ayyadnāhu bi-rūḥi al-qudusi.

“And We strengthened him with the Spirit of Holiness.”

Al-Baqarah 2:87

The same expression recurs in Al-Baqarah 2:253, while Al-Māʾidah 5:110 addresses Jesus directly:

إِذْ أَيَّدْتُكَ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ

Idh ayyadtuka bi-rūḥi al-qudusi.

“When I strengthened you with the Spirit of Holiness.”

In these verses, أَيَّدَ (ayyada) makes the relationship clear: Jesus has been strengthened and supported, and rūḥ al-qudus, the Spirit of Holiness, enters this construction through the preposition bi-.

The root ʾ Y D itself, however, does not determine the nature of rūḥ al-qudus. The verb establishes only a relationship of strengthening and support; precisely what the Spirit of Holiness is remains a separate question.

The same construction appears in other settings, preventing taʾyīd from being restricted to one particular kind of support.

Strengthening through “naṣr”

Āl ʿImrān 3:13 is one of the most important verses for understanding this root precisely:

وَاللَّهُ يُؤَيِّدُ بِنَصْرِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ

Wa-Allāhu yuʾayyidu bi-naṣrihi man yashāʾu.

“And The God strengthens whomever He wills through His aid.”

Here the Qur’an uses أ ي د (ʾ Y D) and ن ص ر (N Ṣ R) in one construction:

يُؤَيِّدُ بِنَصْرِهِ

Yuʾayyidu bi-naṣrihi.

“He strengthens through His aid.”

Taʾyīd and naṣr are therefore not exactly the same thing. Naṣr is that through which strengthening takes place.

The same construction occurs in Al-Anfāl 8:26:

فَآوَاكُمْ وَأَيَّدَكُمْ بِنَصْرِهِ

Fa-āwākum wa-ayyadakum bi-naṣrihi.

“Then He sheltered you and strengthened you through His aid.”

And of the Prophet:

هُوَ الَّذِي أَيَّدَكَ بِنَصْرِهِ وَبِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ

Huwa alladhī ayyadaka bi-naṣrihi wa-bi-l-muʾminīna.

“He is the One who strengthened you through His aid and through the believers.”

Al-Anfāl 8:62

This verse is particularly clear because two things follow the preposition bi-:

بِنَصْرِهِ (bi-naṣrihi)
and
بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ (bi-l-muʾminīna)

Both the aid of The God and the believers play a role in strengthening and securing the Prophet’s position.

Translating أَيَّدَ (ayyada) simply as “helped” therefore erases its distinction from naṣr within the same sentence. The more precise meaning is giving strength, strengthening, and making firm.

Strengthening through unseen forces

Al-Tawbah 9:40 states:

فَأَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ سَكِينَتَهُ عَلَيْهِ وَأَيَّدَهُ بِجُنُودٍ لَمْ تَرَوْهَا

Fa-anzala Allāhu sakīnatahu ʿalayhi wa-ayyadahu bi-junūdin lam tarawhā.

“Then The God sent down His tranquility upon him and strengthened him with forces you did not see.”

Again, أَيَّدَ (ayyada) does not itself mean an army or victory. The forces are that through which strengthening occurs.

Al-Mujādilah 58:22 also says of a group of believers:

وَأَيَّدَهُمْ بِرُوحٍ مِنْهُ

Wa-ayyadahum bi-rūḥin minhu.

“And He has strengthened them with a spirit from Him.”

The means or setting of taʾyīd is therefore not identical across the verses: the Spirit of Holiness, aid, believers, forces, or “a spirit from Him” can accompany strengthening within the sentence.

The constant core in all these cases is an increase in strength and firmness.

From strengthening to prevailing over an enemy

Al-Ṣaff 61:14 states:

فَأَيَّدْنَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا عَلَىٰ عَدُوِّهِمْ فَأَصْبَحُوا ظَاهِرِينَ

Fa-ayyadnā alladhīna āmanū ʿalā ʿaduwwihim fa-aṣbaḥū ẓāhirīna.

“Then We strengthened those who believed against their enemy, and they became dominant.”

This verse clarifies another boundary.

Taʾyīd does not in itself mean “being victorious.” The sentence first says that they were strengthened, then states the result:

فَأَصْبَحُوا ظَاهِرِينَ

Fa-aṣbaḥū ẓāhirīna.

“And they became dominant.”

That is, as a result they came to occupy the position of ascendancy.

Victory or prevailing can therefore be a result of taʾyīd, but should not be inserted into ʾ Y D’s lexical definition.

The same principle applies elsewhere. Someone can be strengthened for worship, steadfastness, a mission, confronting an enemy, or another matter. The context supplies the activity in which that strength is employed.

Differences from nearby roots

ʾ Y D compared with Q W Y: strength and giving strength

The root ق و ي (Q W Y) is the Qur’an’s broad family for strength and being strong itself. The Qur’an speaks of قوة (quwwah), قوي (qawiyy), and شديد القوى (shadīd al-quwā).

ʾ Y D also approaches this field in its two nominal occurrences:

ذَا الْأَيْدِ

Dhā al-aydi.

“Possessed of strength.”

and:

بِأَيْدٍ

Bi-aydin.

“With strength.”

Both concern strength.

No artificial boundary should therefore be drawn between ayd and quwwah that the language does not establish. At the level of the noun, their overlap is considerable.

One feature of Qur’anic ʾ Y D is especially prominent, however: nine of its eleven occurrences are أَيَّدَ (ayyada), giving strength to another. The Qur’an thus uses this family particularly for transferring or adding strength to a person or group.

Strength can be a person’s existing condition; taʾyīd is an action making that person stronger and firmer.

This distinction concerns the use of the Qur’anic forms, not an absence of semantic overlap between the roots.

ʾ Y D compared with N Ṣ R: strengthening and aid

The Qur’an itself supplies the clearest distinction:

يُؤَيِّدُ بِنَصْرِهِ

Yuʾayyidu bi-naṣrihi.

“He strengthens through His aid.”

and:

أَيَّدَكُمْ بِنَصْرِهِ

Ayyadakum bi-naṣrihi.

“He strengthened you through His aid.”

Even if taʾyīd and naṣr were fully synonymous, this construction would at least show that one can function as the means of the other.

Naṣr in Qur’anic usage tends toward aid, particularly against an opponent or toward prevailing.

Taʾyīd emphasizes strengthening and making firm.

Naṣr can be one means of taʾyīd, just as believers or forces can contribute to strengthening someone.

Rendering both invariably as “aid” therefore loses part of the distinction in the verses.

ʾ Y D compared with ʾ Z R: two closely related families of strength

The root أ ز ر (ʾ Z R) also concerns strength and support in the Qur’an. Moses says of Aaron:

اشْدُدْ بِهِ أَزْرِي

Ushdud bihi azrī.

“Make my strength firm through him.”

Ṭā Hā 20:31

And Al-Fatḥ 48:29 says of a crop:

فَآزَرَهُ فَاسْتَغْلَظَ

Fa-āzarahu fa-staghlaẓa.

“Then it strengthened it, and it grew stout.”

Here the plant’s shoots strengthen one another so that the plant becomes firm.

ʾ Z R and ʾ Y D therefore genuinely overlap. Linguistic sources explain both through strength and firmness.

The evidence is insufficient, however, for an absolute boundary between them. Some uses of ʾ Z R also carry the image of the back and a supporting base, or mutual strengthening; ʾ Y D is used with considerable regularity in the Qur’an for giving strength to a person or group.

This difference is best retained as a tendency, not a rigid rule.

Taʾyīd: strengthening rather than merely confirming

The Arabic term تأييد (taʾyīd) can be approached through familiar ideas of endorsement or confirmation, but these can depart from its Qur’anic field.

When someone says:

“I endorse this statement”

the usual meaning is I consider it correct, accept it, or affirm it.

That sense must not be imposed on the Qur’an’s أَيَّدَ (ayyada).

In:

وَأَيَّدْنَاهُ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ

Wa-ayyadnāhu bi-rūḥi al-qudusi.

“And We strengthened him with the Spirit of Holiness.”

the meaning is not that The God “declared Jesus’s statement or opinion correct.” The subject is strengthening and supporting him.

Likewise:

أَيَّدَكَ بِنَصْرِهِ وَبِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ

Ayyadaka bi-naṣrihi wa-bi-l-muʾminīna.

“He strengthened you through His aid and through the believers.”

does not mean “He affirmed that you were correct.” The Prophet was strengthened and supported through aid and the believers.

For a basic English rendering, “gave strength,” “strengthened,” “made firm,” or, in some contexts, “supported” is therefore generally clearer than “confirmed” or “endorsed.”

Taʾyīd can be retained in specialized discussion, but readers should not project the sense of merely approving a statement back into the verse.

Translation guide

أَيْد (ayd) in the Qur’an’s two nominal occurrences means strength:

ذَا الْأَيْدِ

Dhā al-aydi.

“Possessed of strength.”

and:

بَنَيْنَاهَا بِأَيْدٍ

Banaynāhā bi-aydin.

“We built it with strength.”

According to context, أَيَّدَ (ayyada) is best rendered as “gave strength,” “strengthened,” “made firm,” or “supported.”

In:

وَأَيَّدْنَاهُ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ

Wa-ayyadnāhu bi-rūḥi al-qudusi.

“And We strengthened him with the Spirit of Holiness.”

In:

وَاللَّهُ يُؤَيِّدُ بِنَصْرِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ

Wa-Allāhu yuʾayyidu bi-naṣrihi man yashāʾu.

“And The God strengthens whomever He wills through His aid.”

In:

هُوَ الَّذِي أَيَّدَكَ بِنَصْرِهِ وَبِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ

Huwa alladhī ayyadaka bi-naṣrihi wa-bi-l-muʾminīna.

“He is the One who strengthened you through His aid and through the believers.”

And in:

فَأَيَّدْنَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا عَلَىٰ عَدُوِّهِمْ

Fa-ayyadnā alladhīna āmanū ʿalā ʿaduwwihim.

“Then We strengthened those who believed against their enemy.”

The final rendering can vary for fluency, but strengthening and an increase in strength must be preserved.

Conclusion

The root أ ي د (ʾ Y D) forms a small, clear Qur’anic family centered on strength and giving strength. Early Arabic likewise records آد يئيد أيدا (āda yaʾīdu aydan) for becoming strong and firm, and الأيد (al-ayd) for strength.

The Qur’an’s two nominal occurrences display this core directly. David is ذَا الْأَيْدِ (dhā al-aydi), possessed of strength, and the heaven was built بِأَيْدٍ (bi-aydin), with strength. Bi-aydin in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:47 belongs to ʾ Y D and must not be confused with أيدي (aydī), the plural of يد (yad), hand. بأيديهم (bi-aydīhim) in verses such as Al-Baqarah 2:79 belongs to another word family.

The other nine occurrences all take the form أَيَّدَ (ayyada), moving from strength to giving strength to another. Jesus is strengthened with the Spirit of Holiness; believers are strengthened through the aid of The God; the Prophet receives strength through aid and believers; and in another verse unseen forces enter the construction of taʾyīd.

The Qur’an itself also distinguishes taʾyīd from naṣr, saying يؤيد بنصره (yuʾayyidu bi-naṣrihi). Aid can therefore be the means of strengthening, and the words should not be collapsed into one meaning. Prevailing can likewise result from strengthening: Al-Ṣaff 61:14, after strengthening the believers, says that they became ظاهرين (ẓāhirīn), dominant.

This root overlaps considerably with Q W Y, since both concern strength. The distinctive feature of Qur’anic ʾ Y D is that most occurrences are in Form II, expressing adding strength to another. It is also closely related to ʾ Z R, but the evidence does not permit an absolute, artificial boundary between those families.

Care is also needed with taʾyīd in translation. The sense of affirming or considering correct is insufficient for ayyada, and in many verses can even mislead. The Qur’anic core is strengthening, making firm, and supporting.

A cautious account of the root is:

ʾ Y D: a family centered on strength and firmness. Ayd is strength itself, while ayyada means adding strength to another, making them firm, or supporting them. In different contexts, the means of strengthening may be aid, believers, forces, or spirit. Victory, confirmation, or a particular kind of support is not a fixed component of the root itself; context determines these.
