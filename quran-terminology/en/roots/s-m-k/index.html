Height, elevation of a building, and the vertical extent of a structure

The Arabic root S M K concerns height and elevation. This sense appears in buildings, roofs, poles, tall bodies, and structures extending upward from a lower level.

In the Quran, this root occurs only once, as samkahā in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28. The verse describes the creation of the sky and, alongside the verb rafaʿa, mentions its structural height.

It differs from S M W and R F ʿ: S M W covers being high and above, while R F ʿ concerns raising or elevating. Samk can name the height or elevated structure itself.

Arabic also has samak, “fish.” Lexicons record both under the same consonants, but their historical relationship is not established. The meaning “fish” should not be imported into samkahā.

1. The root in early Arabic and the dating of its witnesses

Historical meaning: tangible height

Early and classical Arabic records samaka al-shayʾa, “he raised the thing,” and intransitive samaka al-shayʾu, “the thing rose or became high.”

Samk al-bayt denotes the roof or height of a building. Some lexical explanations also describe the distance between its highest and lowest parts.

Samk is therefore not confined to the uppermost point: it can express the extent of a structure upward.

The height of a house involves two related ideas: its roof or raised upper part, and the height extending from its bottom to its top.

The following forms are recorded in this family:

  • Samaka: raised or elevated.
  • Intransitive samaka: rose or became high.
  • Samk: height, elevation, roof, or upper structure.
  • Sāmik: high or elevated.
  • Masmūk: something raised or built high.
  • Mismāk: a pole or support used to raise a structure.

These uses show that tangible height and the raising of structures form the central field of this branch.

Dating each form before Islam requires distinguishing independent historical evidence from reports in later dictionaries.

The two poetic witnesses presented here belong to Islamic times and cannot independently establish pre-Quranic usage. Their recorded meanings remain useful, but these particular witnesses should not be dated to the pre-Islamic period.

Early poetry: raising the sky

Al-Farazdaq, an Umayyad poet, says in a celebrated verse:

إِنَّ الَّذِي سَمَكَ السَّمَاءَ بَنَى لَنَا
بَيْتًا دَعَائِمُهُ أَعَزُّ وَأَطْوَلُ

inna alladhī samaka al-samāʾa banā lanā / baytan daʿāʾimuhu aʿazzu wa-aṭwalu

“He who raised the sky built us a house whose pillars are mightier and taller.”

Here samaka al-samāʾ means raising the sky.

The poet then mentions building, a house, and pillars. The house pictures his family's standing and honor; its tall pillars convey that standing's strength and elevation.

The usage connects samk with raising and structural height.

Al-Farazdaq lived in Islamic times. The line is not a pre-Islamic witness; its proximity to Quranic language also prevents treating it, without investigation, as independent pre-Quranic evidence.

Within that limit, it illustrates living Arabic usage in the first Islamic century.

An implement for raising a structure

Mismāk designated a wooden pole supporting part of a tent or building above the ground.

The Umayyad poet Dhū al-Rummah uses this image to describe an animal's long legs:

كَأَنَّ رِجْلَيْهِ مِسْمَاكَانِ مِنْ عُشَرٍ
سَقْبَانِ لَمْ يَتَقَشَّرْ عَنْهُمَا النَّجَبُ

ka-anna rijlayhi mismākāni min ʿusharin / saqbāni lam yataqashshar ʿanhumā al-najabu

“Its two legs seem like two supports of ʿushar wood: two tall stems whose bark has not yet peeled away.”

Mismākān in the comparison means two supporting or lifting poles.

The family thus names both height and an implement that holds a structure aloft.

Dhū al-Rummah also lived in Islamic times. His line cannot conclusively establish pre-Quranic use, but clarifies the family's tangible, structural sense in early Arabic.

Tall stature and bodily height

Expressions such as sanāmun sāmik, “a high camel's hump,” are recorded.

Sāmik describes something standing higher than its surroundings.

Ṭawīl al-samk can likewise describe a tall creature.

Samk is therefore not restricted to human buildings. Bodies and structures with tangible height may belong to this semantic field.

This does not establish the same meaning for every kind of moral superiority or spiritual distinction.

In these witnesses, height is primarily spatial and structural. Applying it to social standing or rank requires figurative and contextual evidence.

2. Arabic forms and Quranic usage

The root occurs only once in the Quran:

Samkahā — Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28

The form has two components:

Samk is a noun meaning height, elevation, or upper structure.

Hā is an attached feminine singular pronoun referring here to the sky, al-samāʾ.

Samkahā therefore means “its height” or “its elevated structure.”

It is the object of rafaʿa in the sentence.

Other forms, including samaka, yasmuku, sāmik, masmūk, mismāk, and sumūk, are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Quran.

Samk, “height,” and samak, “fish,” share consonants but differ in vocalization and usage.

Samak, “fish,” does not occur in the Quran. Words used for fish include ḥūt and its plural forms, as well as nūn.

The sole Quranic occurrence of S M K concerns the height and structure of the sky.

3. Rafaʿa samkahā fa-sawwāhā: the sky's height and ordering

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:27:

أَأَنْتُمْ أَشَدُّ خَلْقًا أَمِ السَّمَاءُ بَنَاهَا

a-antum ashaddu khalqan ami al-samāʾu banāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:27

“Are you harder to create, or the sky? He built it.”

The next verse continues:

رَفَعَ سَمْكَهَا فَسَوَّاهَا

rafaʿa samkahā fa-sawwāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28

“He raised its height, then ordered it.”

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29:

وَأَغْطَشَ لَيْلَهَا وَأَخْرَجَ ضُحَاهَا

wa-aghṭasha laylahā wa-akhraja ḍuḥāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29

“And He darkened its night and brought forth its daylight.”

The three verses form a clear sequence.

First comes the sky's creation, then its elevation and ordering, followed by the darkness of night and the light of day within this order.

Banāhā and samkahā

Banāhā derives from B N Y and concerns building and erecting.

Samkahā derives from S M K and emphasizes structural height or an elevated part.

They are related but not fully synonymous.

Building concerns bringing into being and constructing; samk concerns the height and extent of what is built or raised.

Following banāhā, rafaʿa samkahā highlights a feature of that creation: the sky's structural elevation.

Architectural expressions for the sky are familiar in Arabic. Buildings and roofs provide tangible images of height, stability, and something overhead.

Such language does not itself establish that the sky consists of the materials and components of an earthly house.

Does samk mean a roof?

Samk al-bayt is recorded for a house's roof.

Consequently, “He raised its roof” is a lexically possible rendering of Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28 to consider.

Yet samk is not confined to a roof: structural height is also recorded.

This matters for translation.

Translating samkahā only as “the sky's roof” may suggest a specified physical covering like a house roof.

“Its height” preserves elevation without deciding the sky's geometry or material beforehand.

Here “height” is therefore broader, while “roof” is a lexical usage illuminating the architectural imagery.

Rafaʿa and samk

In rafaʿa samkahā, the verb and object come from different roots.

Rafaʿa derives from R F ʿ and means raising or elevating.

Samk is a noun expressing the height or elevated structure itself.

The verb expresses raising, while the noun identifies what is described as raised.

This prevents treating samk simply as a synonym of the verb rafaʿa.

In Persian, some formulations of “raising its height” sound repetitive. The proposed wording uses the noun “height” with the verb “elevate” to preserve the relationship more naturally.

Fa-sawwāhā

Fa-sawwāhā derives from S W Y, not S M K.

It concerns leveling, proportioning, ordering, and completing a structure.

The verse thus mentions both the sky's elevation and its ordering.

The two concepts should remain distinct.

Something can be high without proportion or order; a structure can be proportioned and orderly without height being its sole defining feature.

Rafaʿa samkahā emphasizes elevation, while fa-sawwāhā describes ordering the same sky.

Fa-sawwāhā alone does not establish that the sky is flat, planar, or uniformly high at every point. Such structural interpretations require separate evidence.

4. The sky's height across Quranic passages

Several other passages about the sky help contextualize samk in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28.

The sky as an elevated structure

Al-Raʿd 13:2 describes raising the heavens without pillars that can be seen.

Al-Raḥmān 55:7 likewise says that God raised the sky and set the balance.

Both use R F ʿ, placing elevation in the context of the sky's creation.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28 adds samk to the verb of raising, highlighting the height or elevated structure itself.

Qāf 50:6 directs attention to the sky overhead: its construction, adornment, and absence of fissures.

Building, height, and structural order can thus belong to a connected Quranic description.

Each word nevertheless contributes its own meaning. Samk does not name every feature of the sky, and should not absorb building, adornment, proportioning, and preservation into its definition.

The sky as a roof

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 calls the sky a protected roof.

The imagery relates to the lexical use of samk for a roof or raised part of a building.

Saqf and samk remain different words.

Saqf principally concerns an upper covering or overhead structure; samk emphasizes its height and extent.

They may describe the same subject without sharing every meaning.

“Protected” in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 should not enter the lexical definition of samk. Preservation is a separate concept.

Linguistic imagery and scientific knowledge

Early observers viewed the sky from the earth. Height, expanse, stars, night, and daylight were real experiences.

Quranic language draws on this tangible field to direct attention to creation.

Modern science can reveal new dimensions of cosmic extent, celestial structure, and distance. None of these discoveries alone changes the historical meaning of samk.

Without independent evidence, rafaʿa samkahā cannot yield the universe's precise geometry, the thickness of a cosmic shell, a number of physical sky layers, or a particular cosmological mechanism.

Conversely, limited past knowledge does not justify reducing the verse to the roof of a house.

The lexical sense remains height and elevation. Identifying its referent and extent requires Quranic context and other reliable evidence.

The separate study of S M W, “Height, elevation, and sky,” discusses samāʾ and its distinction from samāwāt.

5. Samk, sumk, and thickness

Samk is recorded for height; in measuring bodies, distance between surfaces also belongs to this family.

Sumk is used for thickness in modern Arabic and in some Persian technical usage.

Height and thickness can both measure a body's extent between surfaces. The distinction concerns the direction of measurement.

A wall's height is measured upward, while its thickness can be the distance between its two faces.

This makes their semantic relationship understandable. Nevertheless, the existence of “thickness” does not establish that samkahā in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28 specifies the sky's material thickness.

Rafaʿa, the preceding banāhā, and the subject of the sky support height and elevation.

“Height” or “elevation” therefore fits this verse better than “thickness.”

6. Samak, fish: a different branch

Samak, with a vowel a on the mīm, is the general Arabic name for fish.

Its unit form is samakah; recorded plurals include asmāk, sumūk, and simāk.

Although its letters match the height branch, that similarity does not establish a single historical meaning.

Some lexical explanations attempt to connect upward movement with the name for fish. Without independent historical evidence of the naming, this remains uncertain.

For example, fish cannot be said to have been named samak because they rise in water or leave the seabed. Their movement does not itself prove the historical reason for the name.

In lexical analysis, height is presented as a central sense, while samak, fish, is distinguished as a usage not readily subsumed under that sense.

The distinction matters for Quranic study.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28 uses samkahā for the sky's height or elevated structure, not the name of a fish.

Quranic words for fish include ḥūt and its related forms, as well as nūn.

The marine animal's meaning must not be imported into samk, nor can similar spelling establish anything about the sky's nature.

7. Other derivatives and non-Quranic uses

Sāmik and masmūk

Sāmik describes something high or elevated.

Sanāmun sāmik denotes a high hump; bināʾun masmūk denotes an elevated building.

Masmūk is a passive participle for something raised or erected.

Some Arabic expressions use al-masmūkāt for the heavens, retaining the image of elevation.

These forms do not occur in the Quran and should not be presented as Quranic derivatives.

Mismāk and simāk

Mismāk, and in some usages simāk, denotes a support holding up a roof, wall, or part of a tent.

The family thus also belongs to everyday shelter construction.

Simāk also names two stars in Arabic astronomical tradition: al-Simāk al-Aʿzal and al-Simāk al-Rāmiḥ.

Some explanations connect the names to the stars' elevated positions. This does not establish a single certain historical derivation for every use of simāk.

These star names do not occur in the Quran. Here they illustrate lexical range rather than determine Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28's meaning.

8. Comparison with related roots

S M K and S M W: height and elevation

Their initial consonants and semantic fields are close, but the roots are distinct.

S M W concerns being high, elevation, and upward direction. Samāʾ belongs to that family.

The Quranic branch of S M K more particularly concerns structural height or tangible extent.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:27, al-samāʾ belongs to S M W; in the following verse, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28, samkahā belongs to S M K and its pronoun refers to that sky.

The sequence distinguishes the families: the sky is the subject, and samk expresses its height.

Sound similarity does not establish that one root derives from the other.

S M K and R F ʿ: height and raising

Rafʿ concerns raising something.

Samk can name its height or elevated structure.

In rafaʿa samkahā, the verb expresses raising and the noun identifies its object.

The sentence makes this distinction clear without requiring invented philosophical boundaries.

S M K and ʿ L W: height and superiority

ʿUluww covers being above and superiority, spatially or, in some contexts, in rank, power, and self-exaltation.

Samk in the height witnesses principally emphasizes bodily and structural features.

A tall structure can thus also relate to ʿuluww, while not every superiority is samk.

A person's moral superiority cannot be called their samk without independent evidence.

S M K and Ṣ ʿ D: height and ascent

Ascent concerns moving from below to above.

Samk chiefly describes structural height or extent.

Climbing stairs is ascent; the building's height or bottom-to-top distance can belong to samk's field.

This is a tendency, not an absolute division of all Arabic usage.

S M K and S Q F: height and overhead covering

Saqf denotes a covering structure or upper part of a building.

Samk can concern a roof, but also conveys height and vertical extent.

They overlap in describing buildings without being fully synonymous.

This prevents restricting Quranic samkahā to a roof of a specified material and construction without investigation.

S M K, B N Y, and S W Y

Building concerns constructing and erecting; samk concerns structural height; taswiyah concerns ordering and proportioning.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:27 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28 use all three in one description.

They may describe related aspects or stages of creation. Sentence order alone does not yield every temporal and physical detail of the sky's origin.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

The sole Quranic form is samkahā in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28.

Several equivalents deserve consideration.

“Its height” preserves elevation and fits the context without adding a specified shape or material.

“Its elevation” is clear as a dimensional expression, although some Persian combinations with “raise” may sound repetitive.

“Its roof” has lexical support and conveys architectural imagery, but is narrower than “height” and may suggest a definite physical covering.

“Its elevated structure” combines height and construction, but is explanatory rather than a simple equivalent and is better suited to lexical discussion.

For the complete verse:

رَفَعَ سَمْكَهَا فَسَوَّاهَا

rafaʿa samkahā fa-sawwāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28

A suitable proposal for Forqan:

“He raised its height and ordered it.”

This preserves raising from R F ʿ, height from S M K, and ordering from S W Y.

It adds no geometry, material, specified distance, or theory of the sky's physical structure.

A brief lexical explanation may read:

“Samkahā: its height or elevated structure, referring to the sky's height in the context of creation.”

This should not merge with the definitions of samāʾ or sawwāhā; each has its own root and role.

Conclusion

The height branch of S M K denotes elevation and structural extent. It appears in buildings, roofs, tall stature, high humps, and structural supports.

Early Islamic poetic and linguistic witnesses illustrate raising the sky and tall structures. Dating every usage before the Quran requires independent historical evidence; certainty cannot replace such evidence.

The root occurs once in the Quran, as samkahā in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28.

There it concerns the sky's height. Rafaʿa and the preceding banāhā reinforce structural elevation.

Fa-sawwāhā separately expresses ordering and proportioning the sky. The meanings should not be conflated.

Samak, fish, shares the consonants but has no established historical connection to height and plays no role in this Quranic occurrence.

The Quranic branch can be defined as follows:

S M K in its height branch denotes elevation, vertical extent, and raised structure. In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:28, samkahā refers to the sky's height, described as raised alongside the ordering of the sky.

This preserves the word's meaning without restricting it to a specified thickness, a particular roof shape, or a theory of cosmic structure.
