Central meaning: brightness, radiance, clarity of colour, and visible beauty; in plants, flowers and blossoms; more broadly, freshness, splendour, and attractive appearance.

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root z h r concerns brightness, beauty, clear colour, and flowering. It describes human faces, the colouring of animals, the moon and stars, firelight, and flowers.

In botanical usage, zahr denotes flowers or blossoms appearing on a plant. Zahrah can denote an individual blossom and, more broadly, the beauty or splendour of something.

Azhar describes a bright, radiant colour in people and animals, especially clear, lustrous whiteness sometimes tinged with red.

Depending on their subject, zahara and azhara concern shining, becoming bright or beautiful, and flowering.

An early lexical analysis describes the family's underlying meaning as follows:

الزَّاءُ وَالْهَاءُ وَالرَّاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى حُسْنٍ وَضِيَاءٍ وَصَفَاءٍ

al-zāʾu wa-l-hāʾu wa-l-rāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā ḥusnin wa-ḍiyāʾin wa-ṣafāʾ

“Zāy, hāʾ, and rāʾ form one basic root indicating beauty, brightness, and clarity.”

This brings together visible beauty, brightness, and clarity of colour.

Brightness here does not always mean emitting light independently. Colour, a clear appearance, or flowering may justify the description zāhir without the object producing light like a lamp.

Pre-Islamic poetry provides important evidence for earlier usage.

First poetic witness: brightness in Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm

Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm, a pre-Islamic poet of Aws, describes a woman's gait:

تَمْشِي كَمَشْيِ الزَّهْرَاءِ فِي دَمَثِ الرَّمْلِ إِلَى السَّهْلِ دُونَهُ الْجُرُفُ

tamshī ka-mashyi al-zahrāʾi fī damathi al-ramli ilā al-sahli dūnahu al-jurufu

Translation:

“She walks like the bright wild cow on soft sand, toward level ground with an eroded bank before it.”

Al-zahrāʾ here denotes a brightly coloured female wild bovine.

In this animal description, azhar is masculine and zahrāʾ feminine; more generally, these are the masculine and feminine forms of the adjective.

The salient feature is the animal's bright, lustrous colouring.

The woman's movement is compared with the animal's measured gait over soft ground and a bank. The simile concerns movement and beauty, whereas zahrāʾ itself supplies a colour description.

The witness supports the use of this family for brightness and clarity of colour in pre-Islamic Arabic.

It also shows that the root was not restricted to flowers.

Second poetic witness: coloured blossoms attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd al-ʿIbādī reads:

حَتَّى تَعَاوَنَ مُسْتَكٌّ لَهُ زَهَرٌ
مِنَ التَّنَاوِيرِ شَكْلُ الْعِهْنِ فِي اللُّؤَمِ

ḥattā taʿāwana mustakkun lahu zaharun / mina al-tanāwīri shaklu al-ʿihni fī al-luʾam

Its general sense is:

“Until dense vegetation grew together, bearing blossoms like coloured wool on decorated travelling gear.”

Zahar here concerns the plants' blossoms.

Tanāwīr also belongs to the vocabulary of flowers, while ʿihn denotes dyed wool.

The flowers' colours are compared with patterns of coloured wool.

The wording and attribution vary: the verse is also attributed to ʿAbd al-Masīḥ ibn ʿAsalah. Neither its author nor the precise circumstances of composition should therefore be treated as certain.

Despite these uncertainties, the witness presents flowers displaying their colour and beauty among plants.

It illustrates the botanical branch in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

What the early evidence supports

Taken with the limits of their transmission, the two witnesses support the following description:

The family z h r concerns visible beauty, clear colour, and striking brightness: bright colouring in descriptions of people and animals, and colourful, fresh blossoms in plants.

Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm's witness highlights brightness and colour.

The verse attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd highlights flowering and coloured vegetation.

Both involve an appearance that is bright, beautiful, or conspicuous.

They do not prove that every meaning began with flowers, or that the flower name arose from an animal's colouring.

The evidence supports both branches without establishing their historical order.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Zahara, yazharu

In the branch of brightness, this means shining, becoming bright, or showing radiance.

زَهَرَ السِّرَاجُ

zahara al-sirāj

“The lamp shone.”

زَهَرَ الْقَمَرُ

zahara al-qamar

“The moon shone.”

زَهَرَتِ النَّارُ

zaharat al-nār

“The fire glowed and shone.”

Brightness is explicit in these uses.

The verb also concerns beauty and the appearance of colour.

Zahira and zahura

These forms describe becoming beautiful, bright, or clear in colour.

زَهِرَ وَجْهُهُ

zahira wajhuhu

“His face became bright and beautiful.”

زَهُرَ النَّبْتُ

zahura al-nabtu

“The plant became beautiful in appearance.”

These uses concern beauty and clarity of colour.

Vowel differences alone do not establish completely separate kinds of beauty or brightness.

Zahr and zahar

Zahr denotes plants' flowers or blossoms.

Zahar, with a vowel on the middle consonant, is also recorded with a related meaning.

زَهْرُ النَّبَاتِ

zahru al-nabāt

“The flowers of the plant.”

Some early descriptions distinguish white nawr from yellow zahr.

Other uses have a wider range, including flowers of different colours.

Zahr cannot therefore be restricted to yellow flowers throughout Arabic usage.

Zahrah and zaharah

These denote a flower or blossom.

More broadly, they can describe beauty, splendour, freshness, or flourishing appearance.

زَهْرَةُ النَّبَاتِ

zahratu al-nabāt

“The blossom of the plant.”

زَهْرَةُ الدُّنْيَا

zahratu al-dunyā

“The bloom and splendour of worldly life.”

The second application is not restricted to actual flowers: floral imagery conveys life's beauty and appeal.

This extended sense occurs in the Quran.

Azhār and azāhīr

These are recognized plurals within the flower vocabulary.

They denote multiple flowers or blossoms.

Azāhīr is also analysed as a plural of a plural.

Both forms remain familiar in Arabic usage for flowers.

Zuhrah

One recorded meaning is brightness, beautiful whiteness, or clarity of colour.

بَيِّنُ الزُّهْرَةِ

bayyinu al-zuhrah

“Of evident brightness and whiteness.”

Its vocalization differs from zahrah, blossom, although both concern beauty and radiance.

Azhar

An adjective meaning bright, radiant, or beautifully lustrous white.

رَجُلٌ أَزْهَرُ

rajulun azhar

“A man with a bright, radiant complexion.”

Some uses concern whiteness tinged with red.

Al-azhar can also designate the moon because of its visible brightness.

The sun and moon have also been called al-azharān.

These uses extend beyond white colouring to luminous appearance.

Zahrāʾ

The feminine of azhar.

It describes a woman, animal, or grammatically feminine object with bright colouring.

دُرَّةٌ زَهْرَاءُ

durratun zahrāʾ

“A clear, radiant pearl.”

Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm's poem uses this form for a brightly coloured wild cow.

Zahrāʾ is therefore not inherently a particular person's name, although it is also used as a personal name or epithet.

Zāhir

An active participle or adjective meaning radiant, bright, or beautiful in appearance.

نَبَاتٌ زَاهِرٌ

nabātun zāhir

“A beautiful, flourishing plant.”

وَجْهٌ زَاهِرٌ

wajhun zāhir

“A bright, radiant face.”

The description can concern colour, light, or visible beauty.

Azhara, yuzhiru

This is a Form IV verb.

In botanical usage:

أَزْهَرَ الشَّجَرُ

azhara al-shajar

“The tree blossomed.”

أَزْهَرَ النَّبَاتُ

azhara al-nabāt

“The plant flowered.”

In another construction:

أَزْهَرَ النَّارَ

azhara al-nāra

“He kindled the fire.”

The verb can therefore be intransitive or transitive, depending on its construction.

Izhār

The verbal noun of azhara; in botanical usage, flowering.

إِزْهَارُ الشَّجَرِ

izhāru al-shajar

“The flowering of the tree.”

In the branch of light, it can concern kindling or producing brightness.

Zuhūr

One recorded use is a verbal noun denoting shining.

زُهُورُ السِّرَاجِ

zuhūru al-sirāj

“The shining of the lamp.”

The same vocalized form can also denote flowers as a plural in wider usage. Context, rather than spelling alone, distinguishes the meanings.

Izhārra

This is recorded for a plant flowering or displaying its blossoms.

Lexical discussions differ on its morphological analysis and relationship to three- and four-consonant structures.

That issue does not determine the meaning of Quranic zahrah.

Izdahara

This is recorded for shining or displaying radiance.

Another construction, izdahara bi-l-shayʾ, means keeping, safeguarding, or giving special attention to something.

Its origin is disputed: some analyses treat it as Arabic and connect it with attention to a desirable thing; others suggest borrowing from another language.

Safeguarding should therefore not be made the underlying meaning of the entire family without evidence.

Mizhar

A name for a stringed musical instrument.

It is registered under this family, but its precise historical connection to beauty, brightness, or flowers is uncertain.

An invented image should not be used to connect it directly to the Quranic meaning.

Al-Zuharah

The name of the planet Venus.

Its conspicuous brightness makes the semantic association with radiance understandable, without independently proving the history of the name.

The Quranic word zahrah does not name this planet.

Only the form zahrata occurs in the Quran.

3. Quranic usage

The root occurs once in the Quran, at Ṭā Hā 20:131.

Ṭā Hā 20:131

وَلَا تَمُدَّنَّ عَيْنَيْكَ إِلَىٰ مَا مَتَّعْنَا بِهِ أَزْوَاجًا مِّنْهُمْ زَهْرَةَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا لِنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ وَرِزْقُ رَبِّكَ خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ

wa-lā tamuddanna ʿaynayka ilā mā mattaʿnā bihi azwājan minhum zahrata al-ḥayāti al-dunyā li-naftinahum fīhi wa-rizqu rabbika khayrun wa-abqā

Ṭā Hā 20:131

Translation:

“Do not extend your eyes toward what We have granted groups among them to enjoy—the bloom of worldly life, through which We test them. Your Lord's provision is better and more lasting.”

Zahrata is a feminine singular noun in the accusative.

Several grammatical analyses have been proposed, including an accusative expressing censure, a circumstantial expression, or apposition, with qualifications concerning their grammatical justification.

Despite these differences, zahrat al-ḥayāt al-dunyā describes the bloom and attractive splendour of worldly life.

The reading used here, Ḥafṣ, has zahrah with an unvowelled hāʾ. A reading zaharah, with a vowel on that consonant, is also reported.

The alternative vocalization does not remove the phrase from the field of beauty and flourishing appearance.

What does zahrah mean here?

In its concrete sense, zahrah denotes a blossom.

Its colour, freshness, and beauty attract attention.

Ṭā Hā 20:131 uses this word to describe worldly enjoyments.

Actual flowers or flowering gardens alone are not the referent.

The verse concerns conspicuous and attractive benefits enjoyed by groups of people.

These can include wealth, amenities, adornment, comfort, and other worldly advantages.

The noun itself does not enumerate them; the examples follow from the phrase and context.

“The bloom of worldly life” retains the image, while “worldly splendour and beauty” explains its extended meaning.

Does zahrah mean adornment?

Zahrat al-dunyā is close in meaning to zīnat al-dunyā.

Both can describe life's beauty and splendour.

Zīnah concerns adornment or that which beautifies an appearance.

Zahrah also evokes blooming, freshness, and visible splendour.

A tree may remain throughout the year, yet gain a striking appearance when it flowers.

That image can convey the attractiveness of worldly benefits.

It does not mean that everything called zahrah necessarily exists for only a very short time.

Some blossoms are short-lived, but the verse also establishes the contrast in endurance through “Your Lord's provision is better and more lasting.”

The entire idea of transience should not be extracted from the root alone.

4. Worldly bloom and human testing

Appearance and the direction of one's gaze

The verse opens with:

وَلَا تَمُدَّنَّ عَيْنَيْكَ

wa-lā tamuddanna ʿaynayka

Ṭā Hā 20:131

“Do not extend your eyes.”

This describes directing sustained attention toward something.

Here the object is the advantages enjoyed by groups of people.

Such attention can involve desire, fascination, and longing, especially when comparing another person's circumstances with one's own.

The command should not be taken as a prohibition of every ordinary glance at beauty or possessions.

It concerns the gaze's orientation and the attachment that outward attractions can encourage.

Others' impressive circumstances may tempt someone to measure their worth and happiness solely by their resources.

The verse instead places those benefits within testing.

Benefits and testing

The verse says:

لِنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ

li-naftinahum fīhi

Ṭā Hā 20:131

“So that We may test them through it.”

This clause relates to the enjoyment granted to them: it presents those benefits as a setting for testing, not testing as the purpose of the prohibition on looking.

Testing does not occur only through poverty, illness, loss, and hardship.

Wealth, comfort, power, and abundant resources can also present ethical choices.

Someone may use these resources for good, justice, generosity, and constructive work.

The same resources may also encourage pride, excess, oppression, or neglect of responsibility.

Possessing advantages alone therefore establishes neither special divine approval nor punishment.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:35 likewise describes testing people through adversity and good.

Al-Fajr 89:15 and Al-Fajr 89:16 depict a person's assumptions about prosperity and restricted provision; Al-Fajr 89:17 rejects the preceding assumption and turns attention to conduct toward the orphan.

In Ṭā Hā 20:131, worldly bloom likewise provides a test rather than a final measure of human worth.

Are beauty and worldly benefits condemned?

The root does not carry an intrinsically negative meaning.

Flowers, blossoms, light, and beauty are ordinary and often attractive applications of this family.

Ṭā Hā 20:131 does not declare beauty or material enjoyment intrinsically forbidden.

The warning concerns attachment and longing that divert attention from more important standards.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:32 questions those who prohibit the adornment and wholesome provision of The God for His servants.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:77 combines seeking the Hereafter with not forgetting one's share of this world.

Beauty and material resources can be blessings, whose proper use need not conflict with piety.

Their being blessings does not make greater possession a measure of ethical superiority.

This distinction matters when interpreting the phrase.

Blooming appearance and lasting value

The verse ends:

وَرِزْقُ رَبِّكَ خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ

wa-rizqu rabbika khayrun wa-abqā

Ṭā Hā 20:131

“Your Lord's provision is better and more lasting.”

Worldly splendour is contrasted with better and more enduring provision.

Khayr expresses greater goodness or superiority.

Abqā emphasizes greater endurance.

Neither belongs to z h r; each supplies its own meaning.

Zahrah should therefore not be translated directly as “perishable,” “worthless,” or “misleading.”

Worldly bloom can be beautiful and desirable without constituting the final measure of good or endurance.

Al-Kahf 18:46 likewise calls wealth and children adornments of worldly life, while lasting good deeds are better in reward and hope.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:20 compares worldly life to vegetation flourishing after rain, delighting cultivators, and then changing and drying.

These passages illuminate the distinction between visible beauty and lasting worth.

They do not make every human endeavour in this world worthless.

Good work, justice, service, and responsible use of resources occur in this life and can have lasting value.

5. The surrounding verses

Connection with Ṭā Hā 20:130

The preceding verse commands the Prophet to endure others' words and glorify his Lord.

Ṭā Hā 20:131 then warns against extending his gaze toward the striking benefits enjoyed by groups of people.

External pressure and the attraction of material advantages are distinct influences on a person's direction.

Hurtful speech calls for steadfastness.

Others' impressive status and possessions call for preserving sound standards.

Ṣabr and zahrah remain separate lexical concepts; their connection arises from context, not a shared root.

Connection with Ṭā Hā 20:132

The next verse commands the Prophet to enjoin ṣalāh on his household and persevere in it.

It then speaks of divine provision and associates the good outcome with piety.

The sequence is significant.

Ṭā Hā 20:131 contrasts worldly attractions with better and more lasting provision.

Ṭā Hā 20:132 foregrounds ṣalāh, perseverance, and reliance on the Lord's provision.

The passage thus addresses practical orientation as well as inward restraint toward others' wealth.

Ṣalāh and perseverance counter the neglect of responsibility through material attractions.

These verses do not establish a duty to abandon earning a living, improving one's circumstances, or supporting a household.

The passage redirects priorities without negating provision and its enjoyment.

This connection is also considered in Forqan's study “Ṣabr in the Qur’an: Patience, Steadfastness, and What Makes Them Worthwhile.”

6. Related roots and distinctions

Z H R and N W R: blossoms and light

Nawr can denote a plant's blossoms, overlapping with zahr.

Some lexical accounts distinguish white and yellow blossoms by these names.

The distinction is not universal: broader applications include different colours.

Nūr in the Quran has a wider field encompassing physical light and guidance.

Zahrah in Ṭā Hā 20:131 concerns life's beauty and flourishing appearance.

Semantic proximity to brightness does not make it the light of guidance or identical to nūr elsewhere.

Z H R and Z Y N: adornment

Zīnah concerns adornment and what beautifies an appearance.

Zahrah can likewise convey visible beauty, with the image of bloom, freshness, and radiance.

Al-Kahf 18:46 calls wealth and children worldly adornment.

Ṭā Hā 20:131 describes some people's enjoyments as worldly bloom.

The expressions concern related subjects without being identical.

Zīnah foregrounds adornment; zahrah depicts attractive, flourishing splendour.

This is not an absolute boundary, since beauty and splendour belong to both fields.

Z H R and B H J: beauty

Bahjah concerns beauty and a delightful appearance.

Al-Naml 27:60 describes gardens of beauty.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 describes the earth stirring after receiving water and producing beautiful kinds of vegetation.

A blossom can be a conspicuous example of a plant's beauty, relating zahrah to bahjah.

Bahjah is not restricted to flowers and can describe a whole scene or arrangement.

Z H R and N Ḍ R: freshness

Naḍrah concerns freshness, vitality, and visible beauty.

This can approach the freshness of flourishing vegetation.

The Quran also applies naḍrah to faces.

Zahrah in its Quranic occurrence evokes worldly bloom and splendour.

The overlap in freshness and appearance does not make every meaning identical.

Z H R and Z KH R F: ornament

Zukhruf concerns ornament and splendour, including gold and conspicuous decoration in some uses.

The Quran sometimes places it in contexts of deceptive or outward adornment.

Zahrah in Ṭā Hā 20:131 also describes impressive worldly advantages.

Yet the words come from distinct lexical families.

Zahrah evokes blooming and radiance; zukhruf more directly concerns ornament, decoration, and patterns.

Both can describe worldly splendour without sharing a root.

Z H R and Z H D: lack of desire

These roots resemble each other in letters but differ in meaning.

Zahr concerns visible beauty and splendour.

Zuhd concerns limited desire or disinterest.

A person can have little desire for worldly bloom.

That relation in human conduct does not establish a common derivation.

Ṭā Hā 20:131 uses zahrah, not zuhd.

The verse should not be reduced to a technical command to renounce every worldly benefit.

Z H R and Z H Q: passing away

Zahaqa concerns departure, disappearance, or perishing.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 describes truth coming and falsehood passing away.

A blossom may evoke beauty that does not last forever.

The roots' resemblance does not make them identical.

In Ṭā Hā 20:131, abqā expressly conveys the greater endurance of the Lord's provision.

Disappearance cannot simply be transferred from z h q into zahrah.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following renderings distinguish important forms. These research proposals do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

Zahara: shone, became bright, or displayed radiance.

Zahira and zahura: became beautiful, bright, or clear in colour.

Zahr: flowers and blossoms; brightness in the relevant lexical use.

Zahrah: blossom; more broadly, beauty, freshness, or splendour.

Zaharah: another vocalization in the field of blossoms and beauty.

Azhār: flowers and blossoms.

Azāhīr: multiple flowers or blossoms.

Zuhrah: brightness, beautiful whiteness, or radiance.

Azhar: bright, radiant, or lustrous white.

Zahrāʾ: feminine of azhar; brightly coloured or radiant.

Zāhir: radiant, bright, or flourishing.

Azhara al-nabātu: the plant flowered.

Izhār: flowering or the appearance of blossoms.

Zuhūr: shining in its verbal-noun use.

Izdahara: shone; in another construction, safeguarded or attended to something.

Mizhar: a stringed musical instrument.

Al-Zuharah: Venus in astronomical usage.

Proposed rendering of Ṭā Hā 20:131

زَهْرَةَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا

zahrata al-ḥayāti al-dunyā

Ṭā Hā 20:131

“The bloom of worldly life.”

This retains the underlying image.

Where explanation is needed, one may add:

“The striking splendour, beauty, and freshness of worldly enjoyments.”

The basic translation need not accumulate every explanation into one long phrase.

For the brief root meaning:

“Visible beauty, brightness, and blooming.”

For the verse-specific meaning:

“The bloom and striking splendour of worldly enjoyments.”

“Wealth” alone is insufficient: the word concerns life's splendour and can extend beyond possessions.

“Perishable life” and “misleading wealth” are not direct lexical equivalents. Relevant implications should be explained from context and the other words.

8. Conclusion

Early Arabic evidence associates z h r with brightness, clear colour, visible beauty, and flowering.

Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm's witness uses zahrāʾ for a brightly coloured wild cow.

The verse attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd illustrates coloured blossoms.

They illuminate two important branches: radiant colouring and flowering vegetation.

The family also describes bright faces, shining lamps, the radiant moon, glowing fire, and flowers.

Only zahrata occurs in the Quran, at Ṭā Hā 20:131.

Within zahrat al-ḥayāt al-dunyā it depicts the bloom and attractive splendour of worldly enjoyments.

The verse presents these as a setting for testing and calls the Lord's provision better and more lasting.

Zahrah itself does not mean sin, deception, prohibition, or destruction. It conveys beauty and blooming; the verse as a whole supplies ethical implications.

Material beauty and benefits alone establish neither superiority nor inferiority. Ethical assessment concerns people's response, responsibilities, and conduct.

The principal semantic field is visible beauty and radiance, expressed as flowers in plants and as the striking bloom of worldly life in the Quran.

This preserves the lexical evidence while distinguishing it from worldly transience, divine testing, and ethical interpretations supplied by context.
