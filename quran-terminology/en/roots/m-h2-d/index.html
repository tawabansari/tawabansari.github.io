1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The three-letter Arabic root M H D concerns preparing, smoothing, and making a place suitable for settling, resting, or doing something. In physical use, someone may spread and prepare a bed or arrange a place for a child. More broadly, someone may establish conditions for an action and prepare for their own future through their conduct.

The Qur’an uses this root for Jesus’s cradle, the earth as a place prepared for life, the bed or dwelling of Hell’s inhabitants, resources provided for a prosperous person, and preparing one’s own outcome through righteous action.

These belong to one family but concern different subjects. A child’s place, the earth’s surface, living resources, and the outcome of human actions cannot be restricted without examination to one physical meaning.

M H D occurs sixteen times in fifteen verses across thirteen surahs. The form counts here follow the reading of Ḥafṣ. Important Qur’anic and lexical forms include:

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sixteen occurrences in six forms

  • مَهْد: A prepared place; for a child, a cradle or infant’s bed. Qur’anic: yes; five times. Three concern Jesus’s cradle in Āl ʿImrān 3:46, Al-Māʾidah 5:110, and Maryam 19:29; two concern the earth in Ṭā Hā 20:53 and Al-Zukhruf 43:10.
  • مِهَاد: Bedding or a spread-out place prepared for settling. Qur’anic: yes; seven times, six concerning Hell and one the earth.
  • مَهَدَ / يَمْهَدُ: To prepare and provide conditions. Qur’anic: yes; يَمْهَدُونَ in Al-Rūm 30:44.
  • مَهَّدَ / يُمَهِّدُ: To prepare, smooth, provide conditions, and make something easier. Qur’anic: yes; وَمَهَّدْتُ in Al-Muddaththir 74:14.
  • تَمْهِيد: Preparation, smoothing, and establishing conditions. Qur’anic: yes; تَمْهِيدًا in Al-Muddaththir 74:14.
  • مَاهِد: One who prepares and makes a place suitable. Qur’anic: yes; plural الْمَاهِدُونَ in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:48.
  • مُهُود: Plural of mahd: cradles or prepared places. Qur’anic: no.
  • أَمْهِدَة: A recorded Arabic plural of mihād. Qur’anic: no.
  • مُمَهَّد: Something prepared, smoothed, or made suitable. Qur’anic: no.
  • تَمَهَّدَ / تَمَهُّد: A place or conditions becoming prepared or even; in some uses, becoming established. Qur’anic: no.
  • اِمْتَهَدَ: To prepare a place for oneself; alternatively a camel’s hump spreading and rising. Qur’anic: no.
  • اِسْتَمْهَدَ: To spread a bed for oneself. Qur’anic: no.
  • مَهِيد: In early usage, pure butter or its clear, purified portion. Qur’anic: no.
  • مُهْد / مُهْدَة: Muhd denotes raised ground in one account and low sandy ground in another; muhdah denotes low, level ground. Qur’anic: no.

Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ: istamhada and lexical forms

Mahd and mihād are therefore not merely two names for one fixed object. Both concern a prepared and suitable place, but can describe a child’s place, the earth, or another setting.

The Qur’anic verb مَهَّدَ also concerns providing a person’s living conditions rather than physically spreading a bed. Every derivative should therefore not be translated only as a cradle or mattress.

2. The lexical core and its physical image

Mahd: preparing and making a place suitable

Early Arabic مَهَدَ الْفِرَاشَ means spreading and preparing a bed.

Someone prepares a place for sleeping or settling. They may smooth an uneven surface, spread something soft upon it, or establish the conditions for using it.

The core is therefore more than spreading a piece of cloth.

What matters is making the place suitable for its intended use.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the root through تَوْطِئَة and تَسْهِيل: preparing, smoothing, and making something easy.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: preparation and facilitation

This definition can account for the Qur’anic uses.

A cradle is prepared for a child. The earth is presented as a suitable place for human life and movement. Resources are provided for someone. People also prepare for their own future through their actions.

This connection must not erase the actual differences between the uses.

Does mahd mean a perfectly flat surface?

Smoothing belongs to the root’s field, but does not necessarily eliminate every elevation, depression, or irregularity from the surface concerned.

Someone may prepare a suitable place to sit or sleep on a mountainside without flattening the entire mountain.

Land can likewise suit walking, cultivation, and life while containing mountains, valleys, and other elevations.

Being prepared or made even may therefore concern usability rather than a surface’s complete geometric form.

This matters particularly in verses about the earth.

Mahd: a child’s prepared place

Arabic مَهْدُ الصَّبِيِّ denotes a place prepared for a child to lie or sleep.

“Cradle” is a natural translation.

The basic sense of a prepared place should nevertheless be distinguished from particular cradles’ features.

Cradles need not all share one shape, material, or mechanism.

A child’s place may be made from wood, fabric, or other materials. One cradle may move while another remains stationary.

Rocking or movement is therefore not an essential part of mahd.

The principal feature is a place prepared for the child.

This matters when comparing the child’s cradle with the earth described as mahd.

Mihād: a spread-out bed

Arabic mihād denotes bedding or something spread for settling or sleeping.

Some lexical explanations consider it broader than mahd.

This is understandable in usage: mahd can denote a particular child’s place while mihād also denotes a larger or more extensive bed.

The distinction should not become an absolute rule.

The Qur’an itself uses مَهْدًا for the earth in Ṭā Hā 20:53 and Al-Zukhruf 43:10, describing the place of human life.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:6 uses مِهَادًا for the same earth.

The forms’ difference therefore does not invariably restrict mahd to a small cradle and mihād to a large bed.

From a physical place to preparing an undertaking

Arabic مَهَّدَ الْأَمْرَ describes preparing something and easing the conditions for carrying it out.

No bed need be spread on the ground in this use.

Someone might arrange preparations for travel, education, or a social undertaking so that others can carry it out more easily.

The undertaking’s conditions resemble a bed prepared beforehand.

This figurative image must not erase the physical meaning of every verse.

When the Qur’an discusses a child’s cradle, it concerns a real place for the child rather than merely preparing intellectual development.

The earth described as mahd or mihād likewise concerns the real setting of human life and movement.

The shared core may be understood as preparing and making a place or conditions suitable for settling, living, or performing an action.

3. Early Arabic uses and poetic witnesses

Preparing a place in Arabic poetry

A verse attributed in grammatical sources to a man of Banū Sulaym reads:

فَمَا آبَاؤُنَا بِأَمَنَّ مِنْهُ
عَلَيْنَا اللَّاءِ قَدْ مَهَدُوا الْحُجُورَا

fa-mā ābāʾunā bi-amanna minhu / ʿalaynā allāʾi qad mahadū al-ḥujūrā

English translation

Our fathers, who prepared their laps for us, have bestowed no greater favor upon us than he has.

مَهَدُوا الْحُجُورَا concerns preparing laps as places for caring for a child.

حُجُور is the plural of حِجْر, meaning a lap or embrace here.

The lap is a place of care and nurture.

The verb thus concerns preparing a place for care, without necessarily referring to a manufactured cradle.

The verse highlights the lap’s nurturing function.

It shows that مهد can describe providing a suitable environment and place for a person.

The reviewed transmissions do not establish the poet’s precise name. This is evidence of Arabic recorded in early sources, rather than a verse confidently attributable to a particular pre-Islamic poet.

Sharḥ Alfiyyat Ibn Mālik: the verse attributed to a man of Banū Sulaym

اِمْتَهَدَ in Abū al-Najm’s poetry

A line by Abū al-Najm al-ʿIjlī reads:

وَامْتَهَدَ الْغَارِبُ فِعْلَ الدُّمَّلِ

wa-imtahada al-ghāribu fiʿla al-dummali

English translation

The camel’s upper back rose and expanded like a boil.

اِمْتَهَدَ describes a part of a camel’s body.

This matters because the root is not restricted to flattening or lowering a surface.

The hump or upper back rises and expands here, rather than merely becoming flat.

The family’s meaning therefore cannot be restricted to lowering and completely flattening everything.

Abū al-Najm was an Islamic-period poet. His witness is useful for early Arabic but should not be presented as pre-Qur’anic poetry.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: the hump rising

Preparing bedding in al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī’s poetry

Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī has the following verse:

تَمَهَّدْنَ دِيبَاجًا وَعَالَيْنَ عِقْمَةً
وَأَنْزَلْنَ رَقْمًا قَدْ أَجَنَّ الْأَكَارِعَا

tamahhadna dībājan wa-ʿālayna ʿiqmatan / wa-anzalna raqman qad ajanna al-akāriʿā

English translation

They made brocade their bedding and raised a covering called ʿiqmah above it; they let a patterned cloth hang down, concealing the lower parts of the camels’ legs.

تَمَهَّدْنَ concerns taking bedding for themselves. Al-Zamakhsharī’s explanation of the final part explicitly refers to cloth hanging over the camels’ legs.

The witness illustrates the familiar physical meaning in daily life and the use of spread-out coverings.

Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī was an Umayyad poet, so his witness should not be transferred to the pre-Islamic period without evidence.

Al-Zamakhsharī, Asās al-Balāghah: cloth over camels’ legs

What the early witnesses establish

The three witnesses concern preparing laps, the formation or expansion of a camel’s upper back, and laying out bedding.

They connect mahd with preparation, smoothing, and suitability without describing one identical physical form in every use.

When the Qur’an calls the earth mahd, one must therefore identify the feature highlighted by context rather than transfer every property of a human cradle or bed to it.

4. مَهْد: Jesus’s cradle and a child speaking

Āl ʿImrān 3:46

Qur’anic text

وَيُكَلِّمُ النَّاسَ فِي الْمَهْدِ وَكَهْلًا وَمِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

wa-yukallimu al-nāsa fī al-mahdi wa-kahlan wa-mina al-ṣāliḥīna

English translation

He will speak to people in the cradle and in maturity, and will be among the righteous.

Examining the usage

الْمَهْدِ is a definite noun in the genitive after فِي.

The surrounding verses concern Jesus son of Mary.

The phrase places speaking in the cradle alongside speaking in maturity.

Mahd here denotes the child’s place.

The linguistic significance is that speech is attributed to someone still in infancy and in a cradle.

كَهْلًا, by contrast, introduces another stage of life.

The cradle and maturity are therefore distinct conditions.

The Qur’an accordingly describes Jesus speaking at a stage when ordinary, fully developed speech would not yet be expected.

Mahd itself, however, does not mean speaking, prophethood, or a miracle.

Those meanings arise from يُكَلِّمُ, the child’s condition, and the narrative as a whole.

Maryam 19:29

Qur’anic text

فَأَشَارَتْ إِلَيْهِ قَالُوا كَيْفَ نُكَلِّمُ مَنْ كَانَ فِي الْمَهْدِ صَبِيًّا

fa-ashārat ilayhi qālū kayfa nukallimu man kāna fī al-mahdi ṣabiyyan

English translation

She pointed to him. They said, “How can we speak to someone who is a child in the cradle?”

Examining the usage

الْمَهْدِ again denotes the child’s place.

This time, the words are spoken by those to whom Mary points out the child.

They express surprise at speaking with a child in a cradle.

This reaction fits a child’s ordinary condition at that stage.

Mahd itself does not establish exactly how many days or months old the child was.

صَبِيًّا also states his childhood.

Details such as the cradle’s shape or material, the precise date of birth, or how long Jesus remained in it should therefore not be added without independent evidence.

In the next verse, Maryam 19:30, Jesus speaks and identifies himself as God’s servant.

The event’s significance becomes clear through the whole narrative.

Al-Māʾidah 5:110

In Al-Māʾidah 5:110, God likewise reminds Jesus that, supported by the Holy Spirit, he spoke to people in the cradle and in maturity.

This is the third occurrence of الْمَهْدِ concerning Jesus.

All three retain the meaning of a cradle or child’s place.

The sentences’ roles nevertheless differ.

In Āl ʿImrān, speaking in the cradle belongs to the announcement concerning Jesus.

In Maryam, it occurs during Mary’s defense and Jesus’s own speech.

In Al-Māʾidah, the ability is recalled among divine favors and supports.

One word can therefore refer to the same subject in three contexts without the root meaning changing.

Is mahd merely a metaphor for the beginning of life?

Arabic can also use mahd figuratively for something’s early development.

In these three verses, however, فِي الْمَهْدِ accompanies a child speaking.

The clear direct reading is therefore the child’s actual place.

Symbolic meanings of life’s beginning or a nurturing setting may be considered, but must not replace the verses’ linguistic and narrative reality.

Lexical conclusion: الْمَهْد in Jesus’s verses means a cradle or a child’s prepared place. The wonder of speech comes from the verb’s relationship with infancy, rather than the word itself meaning a miracle.

5. The earth as mahd and mihād: prepared for life

Ṭā Hā 20:53

Qur’anic text

الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ الْأَرْضَ مَهْدًا وَسَلَكَ لَكُمْ فِيهَا سُبُلًا وَأَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ أَزْوَاجًا مِنْ نَبَاتٍ شَتَّىٰ

alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-arḍa mahdan wa-salaka lakum fīhā subulan wa-anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhrajnā bihi azwājan min nabātin shattā

English translation

The One who made the earth a prepared bed for you, opened paths for you through it, and sent down water from the sky; through it We brought forth diverse kinds of plants.

Examining the usage

مَهْدًا is the same noun used elsewhere for a child’s cradle.

Its subject nevertheless differs.

The earth is presented as a mahd for people.

لَكُمُ matters: the earth’s preparation is described in relation to the audience’s life and needs.

Paths, water, and plants follow in the verse.

The context therefore concerns the earth as a place where human life and movement have become possible.

This connection relates mahd here to the earth’s suitability for living, moving, and benefiting from it.

The word alone, however, does not explain every feature of the earth.

Paths, for example, come from سُبُلًا and its accompanying verb; mahd itself does not mean a road.

Water and the growth of plants are likewise expressed through independent verbs and words.

Al-Zukhruf 43:10

Qur’anic text

الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ الْأَرْضَ مَهْدًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ فِيهَا سُبُلًا لَعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ

alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-arḍa mahdan wa-jaʿala lakum fīhā subulan laʿallakum tahtadūna

English translation

The One who made the earth a prepared bed for you and provided paths through it, so that you may find your way.

Examining the usage

مَهْدًا again describes the earth.

Its connection with سُبُلًا again matters.

The earth is presented as a place in which people can find their way and move.

لَعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ should nevertheless not be defined solely through M H D.

Guidance belongs to H D Y.

The phrase may be examined both through finding one’s way along earthly paths and through recognizing divine favor and guidance.

Mahd itself does not mean guidance.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:6

Qur’anic text

أَلَمْ نَجْعَلِ الْأَرْضَ مِهَادًا

a-lam najʿali al-arḍa mihādan

English translation

Have We not made the earth a prepared bed?

Examining the usage

Unlike Ṭā Hā and Al-Zukhruf, this verse uses مِهَادًا.

The subject remains the earth.

Linguistically, mihād denotes a spread-out, suitable bed.

The earth is presented here as a bed for life.

The verse is not defining the earth’s geometric form.

Mihād does not name a perfectly flat mathematical surface, just as a prepared bed or place need not lack every rise or depression throughout all its parts.

The word also does not explicitly express the earth’s motion.

Any proposed figurative connection between the cradle image and rocking motion requires contextual support in this use.

Some cradles moving does not suffice to establish the earth’s movement from mahd.

The clear basic meaning is therefore the earth’s preparation and suitability for settling and living.

The difference between مَهْدًا and مِهَادًا

Mahd and mihād are related morphological forms.

Lexical sources record several analyses of their relationship.

One explanation treats mihād as broader than mahd.

Another understands both as bedding and a prepared place.

Some lexicographers also examine mihād as a plural or a verbal-noun form related to mahd.

These differences caution against an absolute, unchanging boundary between the forms.

The Qur’an itself uses both for the earth.

“A prepared bed” may therefore suit both in translation.

Lexical reflection may explain mihād as more capable of emphasizing an extensive place, but that must not become a categorical claim about two kinds of earth or cosmic structures.

Variant readings in Ṭā Hā and Al-Zukhruf

Ṭā Hā 20:53 and Al-Zukhruf 43:10 also have variant readings.

Ḥafṣ reads مَهْدًا.

Other established readings, including Nāfiʿ’s and several others, read مِهَادًا.

This matters morphologically: the latter uses the same form as Al-Nabaʾ 78:6.

Both remain in the field of bedding and a prepared place.

The reading difference therefore does not establish contradictory theories of the earth’s shape.

A translation based on Ḥafṣ must preserve مَهْدًا in its Arabic text unless the readings are being examined independently.

Sharḥ al-Naẓm al-Jāmiʿ: Nāfiʿ’s mihādan reading in Ṭā Hā and Al-Zukhruf

Is the earth like a child’s cradle?

The linguistic comparison is understandable: a cradle is prepared for a child, and the earth is presented as suitable for human life.

Comparison or shared vocabulary does not transfer every property between the two things.

The earth is not a wooden cradle.

Other Qur’anic verses also discuss mountains, valleys, paths, seas, and different parts of the earth.

Its suitability for life therefore does not mean that all its surfaces are identical.

The expression concerns living on and benefiting from the earth; it neither denies geography nor establishes a particular theory of celestial motion.

Lexical conclusion: Mahd and mihād present the earth as a place prepared and suitable for human life. The basic meaning is neither perfect geometric flatness nor cradle-like motion.

6. الْمَاهِدُونَ: a divine description of preparing the earth

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:48

Qur’anic text

وَالْأَرْضَ فَرَشْنَاهَا فَنِعْمَ الْمَاهِدُونَ

wa-al-arḍa farashnāhā fa-niʿma al-māhidūna

English translation

We spread out the earth; how excellent We are as those who prepare and make it suitable.

Examining the usage

الْمَاهِدُونَ is a sound masculine plural active participle from Form I.

The description is attributed to God in a plural expression of majesty.

It directly relates to mahd and mihād but performs a different role.

Mahd and mihād name a place or bed.

Māhidūn describes those who prepare and make a place suitable.

God is the agent here.

The relationship with فَرَشْنَاهَا

The verse begins:

وَالْأَرْضَ فَرَشْنَاهَا.

فَرَشْنَا belongs to F R SH and concerns spreading out.

It is followed by فَنِعْمَ الْمَاهِدُونَ.

Spreading the earth and preparing it therefore form a connected expression.

This proximity links M H D with providing and making a place suitable.

It does not make M H D and F R SH complete synonyms in every use.

F R SH emphasizes spreading out, while M H D may also encompass preparing and smoothing a place or its conditions.

Both concepts combine in describing the earth here.

Is الْمَاهِدُونَ an independent divine name?

The verse uses the description to praise God and express His power.

An action-related description should nevertheless be distinguished from an independent divine name.

الماهدون concerns preparing the earth within this construction.

God can therefore be said to be praised as the One who prepares and makes the earth suitable.

This occurrence alone, however, does not establish that the singular الماهد appears in the Qur’an as an independent divine name.

That form is absent from this verse.

Nor does the plural mean several gods. It belongs to the divine expression and agrees with plural فرشناها.

Lexical conclusion: الماهدون is a Qur’anic description concerning preparing and making the earth suitable. It accompanies divine praise but must not become a newly constructed independent name without evidence.

7. يَمْهَدُونَ: preparing one’s own outcome

Al-Rūm 30:44

Qur’anic text

مَنْ كَفَرَ فَعَلَيْهِ كُفْرُهُ وَمَنْ عَمِلَ صَالِحًا فَلِأَنْفُسِهِمْ يَمْهَدُونَ

man kafara fa-ʿalayhi kufruhu wa-man ʿamila ṣāliḥan fa-li-anfusihim yamhadūna

English translation

Whoever disbelieves bears their own disbelief; those who do righteous deeds prepare for themselves.

Examining the usage

يَمْهَدُونَ is a third-person masculine plural present-tense Form I verb.

Unlike the nouns for the earth’s bed and a child’s cradle, this use concerns human action.

In فَلِأَنْفُسِهِمْ يَمْهَدُونَ, the verb accompanies لِأَنْفُسِهِمْ.

Preparation is therefore presented as benefiting the people themselves.

What is prepared is not stated as an explicit object.

The direct translation should therefore not add, without explanation, “they build palaces in Paradise” or “spread heavenly beds for themselves.”

These may be contextual or figurative interpretations, but those words are absent from the verse.

Righteous action and preparing the future

The first part returns disbelief and its responsibility to the person concerned.

The second describes righteous action and preparing for oneself.

This contrast matters.

The verse assigns responsibility for disbelief to its bearer and returns the benefit of preparation through righteous action to those performing it.

Preparation is consequently connected with human conduct.

Righteous action provides something for a person’s eventual outcome.

The verb alone does not specify the precise physical or nonphysical form of that result.

Conversely, the verse does not permit reducing righteous action to inward wishes or claims.

عَمِلَ صَالِحًا is explicit.

Preparing the future is therefore inseparable from action here.

Does M H D mean building a bed in the Hereafter?

Lexical and exegetical explanations relate the expression to providing a suitable bed or place for oneself in the Hereafter.

This fits the root’s image.

The verse nevertheless names no particular bed or place after يمهدون.

“They prepare for themselves” is therefore preferable in direct translation.

Reflection can then explain its relationship with righteous deeds and human outcomes.

This distinction prevents a lexical image from becoming a definite architectural description of the Hereafter without evidence.

Lexical conclusion: يَمْهَدُونَ in Al-Rūm 30:44 denotes preparing and providing for oneself. The context connects this with righteous action, but the verb alone does not establish the result’s precise subject or form.

8. مَهَّدْتُ لَهُ تَمْهِيدًا: providing the resources of life

Al-Muddaththir 74:14

Qur’anic text

وَمَهَّدْتُ لَهُ تَمْهِيدًا

wa-mahhadtu lahu tamhīdan

English translation

I thoroughly prepared the conditions for him.

Examining the usage

The root occurs twice:

مَهَّدْتُ and تَمْهِيدًا.

مَهَّدْتُ is a first-person singular past-tense Form II verb.

تَمْهِيدًا is that form’s verbal noun, functioning as a cognate accusative.

This repetition emphasizes the act of preparation and its thorough fulfillment.

The precise subject of preparation requires the surrounding verses.

Al-Muddaththir 74:11, Al-Muddaththir 74:12, and Al-Muddaththir 74:13 mention the person’s creation, abundant wealth, and children present around him.

The preparation in Al-Muddaththir 74:14 therefore concerns his living conditions and resources.

The verb itself does not mean wealth or children.

Those come from the preceding verses.

Form II and the cognate accusative

Arabic Form II may express making provision, emphatically performing an action, or its more extensive realization.

Morphology alone, however, does not determine the number of preparation stages or the kinds of resources.

This verse also includes the cognate accusative تمهيدا.

The construction emphasizes the verb.

“I thoroughly prepared” or “I fully provided the conditions” is therefore natural in translation.

Adding assets, political office, or social power not stated in the verses exceeds the root’s lexical meaning.

Does preparation always signal divine approval?

No.

The context concerns resources given to someone whose desires and behavior are subsequently criticized.

Possessing resources alone therefore does not establish certain divine approval or personal integrity.

Someone may have wealth, children, and favorable conditions while responding wrongly to truth.

Conversely, مهدت itself does not mean condemnation or punishment.

It expresses provided conditions; judgment of the person comes from the wider context.

The difference from Al-Rūm 30:44

In Al-Rūm 30:44, people prepare for themselves through righteous action.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:14, God describes preparing conditions for a person.

The agents therefore differ.

Al-Rūm uses the unaugmented verb يمهدون.

Al-Muddaththir uses Form II with an emphatic verbal noun.

The semantic core remains providing and preparing in both.

The context establishes its subject and value.

9. مِهَاد: Hell’s bed and the difference between a place and comfort

Al-Aʿrāf 7:41

Qur’anic text

لَهُمْ مِنْ جَهَنَّمَ مِهَادٌ وَمِنْ فَوْقِهِمْ غَوَاشٍ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي الظَّالِمِينَ

lahum min jahannama mihādun wa-min fawqihim ghawāshin wa-kadhālika najzī al-ẓālimīna

English translation

For them is a bed of Hell, and coverings above them. Thus We recompense the wrongdoers.

Examining the usage

مِهَادٌ denotes bedding or the place beneath them.

مِنْ جَهَنَّمَ specifies that bed’s substance or source.

It is followed by مِنْ فَوْقِهِمْ غَوَاشٍ.

The verse therefore depicts below and above: a bed of Hell and coverings over the people.

Mihād does not mean a comforting bed here.

The subject is a place of Hell.

The ordinary use of beds does not establish that mihād always means a place of ease, rest, and comfort.

The word can denote what someone lies or settles upon, while context establishes its quality.

Here, that quality connects with Hell and its enveloping coverings.

Al-Baqarah 2:206

Qur’anic text

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُ اتَّقِ اللَّهَ أَخَذَتْهُ الْعِزَّةُ بِالْإِثْمِ فَحَسْبُهُ جَهَنَّمُ وَلَبِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ

wa-idhā qīla lahu ittaqi Allāha akhadhat-hu al-ʿizzatu bi-al-ithmi fa-ḥasbuhu jahannamu wa-la-biʾsa al-mihādu

English translation

When he is told, “Be mindful of The God,” pride drives him into sin. Hell is sufficient for him; what an evil bed.

Examining the usage

الْمِهَادُ is definite and occurs in the expression of blame لَبِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ.

The bed or place is therefore explicitly condemned.

The important point is that mihād itself does not mean evil.

Its badness comes from لَبِئْسَ and the context of Hell.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:6 uses the same word for the earth.

The condemned use in Hell therefore does not make mihād inherently negative.

Other verses describing Hell as mihād

Āl ʿImrān 3:12 reads:

قُلْ لِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا سَتُغْلَبُونَ وَتُحْشَرُونَ إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ وَبِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ

qul li-alladhīna kafarū sa-tughlabūna wa-tuḥsharūna ilā jahannama wa-biʾsa al-mihādu

English translation

Tell those who disbelieve, “You will be defeated and gathered to Hell; what an evil bed.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:197 reads:

مَتَاعٌ قَلِيلٌ ثُمَّ مَأْوَاهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ وَبِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ

matāʿun qalīlun thumma maʾwāhum jahannamu wa-biʾsa al-mihādu

English translation

A little enjoyment; then their dwelling is Hell. What an evil bed.

Ṣād 38:56 likewise reads:

جَهَنَّمَ يَصْلَوْنَهَا فَبِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ

jahannama yaṣlawnahā fa-biʾsa al-mihādu

English translation

Hell, which they enter and whose burning they endure. What an evil bed.

Al-Raʿd 13:18 also ends a description of those who fail to respond properly to their Lord with بِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ.

These uses show mihād as a recurring word for Hell’s place or bed.

Repetition does not establish that Hell is physically made exactly like a bed of cloth, cotton, or wool.

Nor should the image of a place be erased by translating mihād simply as punishment.

Punishment and place are related but different concepts.

Mihād and maʾwā

Several verses place مَأْوَىٰ and mihād close together.

Maʾwā belongs to ʾ W Y and concerns taking refuge or lodging.

Study of ʾ W Y

Mihād belongs to M H D and denotes a bed or prepared place.

The words are close but not complete synonyms.

Maʾwā may highlight the destination or place of lodging, while mihād evokes bedding and a place to settle.

Their proximity therefore does not justify replacing both with one fixed equivalent in every verse.

Does mihād in Hell mean a peaceful resting place?

The Persian ārāmgāh can evoke rest, stillness, or even peace after death.

That connotation does not suit these verses about Hell.

“Bed” or “the place beneath” more accurately preserves the Arabic image.

The bed here must nevertheless not be understood as a source of comfort.

Lexical conclusion: Mihād in Hell’s verses means a bed or place of settling; its quality comes from the description of Hell and the construction of blame. The root itself means neither comfort nor punishment.

10. Related roots and grammatical points

M H D compared with F R SH

Study of F R SH

F R SH concerns spreading and laying something out.

M H D concerns preparing and making a place or conditions suitable.

Both families occur in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:48.

This proximity shows their semantic connection.

Not every spreading fully prepares a place, nor does every preparation necessarily involve spreading something.

Someone may prepare conditions for an undertaking without laying out physical bedding.

The roots are therefore close in some contexts without being complete synonyms.

M H D compared with W Ṭ ʾ

Study of W Ṭ ʾ

Arabic W Ṭ ʾ concerns stepping, being underfoot, and in some uses smoothing and preparing.

Lexical explanations of M H D frequently use وطأ and توطئة.

The connection is understandable because preparing bedding makes a place suitable for settling and use.

This overlap does not make every Arabic use of W Ṭ ʾ equivalent to M H D.

M H D compared with B S Ṭ

Study of B S Ṭ

B S Ṭ concerns spreading, opening, and sometimes giving breadth or abundance.

M H D may likewise concern preparing and spreading out a place.

B S Ṭ sometimes concerns increased quantity or extent, while M H D emphasizes a place becoming suitable or conditions being provided.

The earth being mihād therefore does not alone yield every meaning of B S Ṭ.

M H D compared with S W Y

Study of S W Y

Taswiyah concerns making something even, proportionate, or orderly.

Tamhīd may likewise involve smoothing and preparing.

Not every taswiyah, however, means providing a bed.

Arranging a structure or proportioning its parts may involve no physical bed or place.

The roots are close but do not share an identical semantic field.

M H D compared with B W ʾ

Study of B W ʾ

Qur’anic B W ʾ concerns lodging, settling someone, and providing a place to reside.

M H D likewise concerns a prepared place.

B W ʾ may emphasize settling someone in a particular location, while M H D emphasizes preparing the place or conditions themselves.

They are therefore close in habitation without becoming interchangeable in every use.

M H D compared with H D Y

Study of H D Y

Guidance belongs to H D Y.

Mahd belongs to M H D.

Al-Zukhruf 43:10 places the prepared earth and its paths alongside guidance.

Words appearing together does not make their roots or meanings identical.

Mahdī in its familiar religious use likewise belongs to the root for guidance, rather than M H D.

Similar spelling must therefore not place Mahdī in the M H D family.

M H D compared with M Y D

Study of M Y D

Qur’anic M Y D concerns movement, swaying, instability, or inclination.

M H D concerns a prepared place and making it suitable or even.

The roots may be discussed together regarding the earth while remaining lexically distinct.

M H D alone therefore does not establish earthly motion, nor does M Y D establish that mahd means only remaining fixed and motionless.

Each root requires examination in its own setting.

Form I and Form II

يَمْهَدُونَ in Al-Rūm 30:44 is Form I.

مَهَّدْتُ in Al-Muddaththir 74:14 is Form II.

Both involve preparing and providing conditions.

The Form II verb in Al-Muddaththir also has the cognate accusative تمهيدا, adding emphasis to preparation.

This does not restrict one form to the Hereafter and the other to worldly life.

Righteous actions in Al-Rūm and living resources in Al-Muddaththir establish the subjects.

Counting مَهْد and مِهَاد separately

The Qur’anic index records five occurrences of mahd and seven of mihād.

Some lexical sources treat mihād as a plural or broader form of mahd, while others describe both as names for bedding or verbal nouns related to preparation.

The counts should therefore remain separate, while semantic analysis must avoid an absolute boundary without exceptions.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Cradle” naturally renders الْمَهْدِ in the verses about Jesus.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:46, Al-Māʾidah 5:110, and Maryam 19:29, it fits childhood and speaking within it.

In Ṭā Hā 20:53 and Al-Zukhruf 43:10, however, مَهْدًا describes the earth. “A prepared bed” or “a suitable, prepared place” is more natural.

If “cradle” is chosen as a figurative rendering for the earth, reflection should clarify that suitability is intended rather than transferring every feature of a child’s cradle.

“A prepared bed” suits مِهَادًا in Al-Nabaʾ 78:6.

For mihād in Hell’s verses, “bed” or, where appropriate, “the place beneath” may preserve the principal image.

“For them is a bed of Hell” remains close to Al-Aʿrāf 7:41.

“What an evil bed” suits بِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ and لَبِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ.

For يَمْهَدُونَ in Al-Rūm 30:44, “they prepare for themselves” is proposed.

A specific place or reward absent from the wording should not be added to direct translation.

For وَمَهَّدْتُ لَهُ تَمْهِيدًا in Al-Muddaththir 74:14, “I thoroughly prepared the conditions for him” or “I fully provided for him” is natural.

For الْمَاهِدُونَ in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:48, “those who prepare” or “those who make suitable” is appropriate.

This description should not become an independent divine name, nor should the plural imply several independent agents.

The governing principle is to avoid merging cradle, bedding, preparing a place, and providing conditions for an undertaking into one fixed equivalent.

12. Final synthesis

Arabic M H D concerns preparing, smoothing, and making a place or conditions suitable.

This is clear in physical uses such as spreading bedding and preparing a child’s cradle.

Broader uses concern providing conditions for an undertaking or preparing a person’s eventual outcome.

The Qur’an uses mahd for Jesus’s cradle and for the earth.

The subjects differ, but their connection through a prepared place is understandable.

Mihād describes both the earth and Hell. Both involve bedding or a place, although its quality differs.

The earth appears in a context of human life and resources, while Hell’s mihād is a condemned bed.

The word itself therefore means neither certain comfort nor certain punishment; context establishes the place’s qualities.

In Al-Rūm 30:44, people prepare for themselves through righteous action.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:14, God describes providing someone’s living conditions.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:48 praises God as preparing and making the earth suitable.

Preparation may therefore concern a physical place, living conditions, or the outcome of human actions.

The root alone does not determine the nature or value of what is prepared.

The earth being mahd or mihād likewise does not yield a definitive theory of its geometric shape or motion.

The verses describe its suitability for human life and use; paths, water, plants, and other resources appear through independent vocabulary in the same context.

Final definition: M H D denotes preparing and making a place or conditions suitable for settling, living, or performing an action. Nominal mahd and mihād may denote a cradle, bed, or prepared place; verbal uses emphasize preparation and providing conditions.

For each use, establish what has been prepared, for whom or for what purpose, and whether the verse concerns a physical place or conditions and an outcome established through action.

The core is preparing and making suitable. Comfort, punishment, ethical value, and the actual form of what is prepared come from the sentence’s construction and Qur’anic context.
