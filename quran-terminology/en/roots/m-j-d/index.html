The root م ج د — Nobility and glory

Qur’anic and lexical forms

مَجِيدٌ
(majīdun)
Noble and glorious; describing God

الْقُرْآنِ الْمَجِيدِ
(al-Qurʾāni al-majīdi)
The glorious Qur’an

الْمَجِيدُ / الْمَجِيدِ
(al-majīdu / al-majīdi)
Different referents across readings

From honor, generosity, and high standing in early Arabic to descriptions of God, the Qur’an, and the Throne

The Arabic root م ج د concerns nobility, honor, dignity, glory, and high standing. Human majd may arise from virtues, good deeds, generosity, and social position. Other things can be called majīd because of their greatness, worth, and exalted rank.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an, only as majīd. It describes God once and the Qur’an twice. In the fourth occurrence, a reading difference determines whether it describes God or His Throne.

The variety matters. Of God, majīd concerns nobility, glory, and perfection; of the Qur’an, dignity and high standing; of the Throne, the glory and greatness of something belonging to God.

Majd is therefore more than fame, receiving praise, or possessing power. Someone may be famous or powerful without nobility, while good conduct and generosity can bring honor without political rule or great wealth.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Classical majd concerns honor, nobility, graciousness, and generosity.

Rajulun mājidun describes an honorable, noble, generous man.

Majīd can likewise describe someone distinguished in nobility and honor.

Lexicons explain majd as al-sharafu al-wāsiʿu: extensive, elevated honor.

They also include al-murūʾatu wa-al-sakhāʾu: honorable conduct and generosity.

Human majd does not always have one source.

Some Arab social traditions connected it with family honor and distinguished ancestors; other uses emphasize a person’s own conduct, hospitality, generosity, and service.

Both directions occur in early usage, and neither alone defines the entire root.

A verse by Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr praises Harim ibn Sinān:

«إِذَا ابْتَدَرَتْ قَيْسُ بْنُ عَيْلَانَ غَايَةً
مِنَ الْمَجْدِ مَنْ يَسْبِقْ إِلَيْهَا يُسَوَّدِ»

Idhā ibtadarat Qaysu bnu ʿAylāna ghāyatan / mina al-majdi man yasbiq ilayhā yusawwadi.

“When Qays ibn ʿAylān compete for a goal of glory, whoever reaches it first is made a leader.”

The next verse says:

«سَبَقْتَ إِلَيْهَا كُلَّ طَلْقٍ مُبَرِّزٍ
سَبُوقٍ إِلَى الْغَايَاتِ غَيْرِ مُجَلَّدِ»

Sabaqta ilayhā kulla ṭalqin mubarrazin / sabūqin ilā al-ghāyāti ghayri mujalladi.

“You surpassed every outstanding, swift contender toward that goal, one who reaches the finish without needing to be whipped.”

Majd here denotes high standing among people attained through virtue and worthy action.

The poet compares it to a destination for which people compete.

The competition exceeds seeking wealth or a title. The poem goes on to praise generosity, chastity, and respect for relatives’ rights.

The example connects pre-Islamic poetic majd with nobility and social standing, with personal conduct contributing to its attainment.

The verses appear in Zuhayr’s collection and Ibn Qutayba’s al-Shiʿr wa-al-shuʿarāʾ. Some wording in the second verse varies, but al-majd and its relationship to honor and leadership are clear.

The attribution to Zuhayr has an established literary basis, although the exact date of these verses is not independently known.

Majd and generosity in early poetry

A poem attributed to Ḥujayya ibn Muḍarrib al-Kindī says:

«يَصُونُونَ أَحْسَابًا وَمَجْدًا مُؤَثَّلًا
بِبَذْلِ أَكُفٍّ دُونَهَا الْمُزْنُ وَالْبَحْرُ»

Yaṣūnūna aḥsāban wa-majdan muʾaththalan / bi-badhli akuffin dūnahā al-muznu wa-al-baḥru.

“They preserve ancestral honors and deeply rooted glory through giving hands beside whose generosity rainclouds and the sea fall short.”

Majd connects inherited honor with generosity displayed by the present generation.

Muʾaththalan denotes something old and firmly rooted.

The poet says that established standing is preserved through giving and good conduct.

Human majd can thus involve both ancestors’ honor and practical virtues.

Al-Qālī’s al-Amālī attributes this poem to Ḥujayya ibn Muḍarrib, while other transmissions attribute it to ʿĀmir ibn Ẓarib. This study has not established its precise date or definitive authorship. It supplements the semantic evidence without decisively dating every usage before the Qur’an.

A quality or a result of public praise?

Majjadahu and amjadahu denote honoring, praising, and attributing majd to someone.

Tamajjada can concern recounting one’s own noble qualities and honors.

Possessing majd and being declared majīd should be distinguished.

People may praise someone in ways unsupported by actual conduct.

A virtuous, noble person may conversely remain unknown or insufficiently praised.

Nobility is not merely a product of others’ approval. Praise may respond to it, but the quality and what others say about it differ.

This matters especially when God and the Qur’an are called majīd: their dignity is not made dependent on popularity or human approval.

2. Other meanings in the family

Abundance and giving

Amjada ʿaṭāʾahu and majjada ʿaṭāʾahu describe making one’s gift abundant.

Amjadanā qiran describes plentiful hospitality, providing enough for guests and more besides.

These uses connect majd with abundance in giving.

They do not make every great fortune or large gift proof of moral nobility.

Giving may seek prestige, influence, or personal advantage. The amount alone determines neither intention nor moral worth.

Praiseworthy human majd can embrace nobility and generosity, while evaluating actual conduct also requires attention to motives and manner.

Pastoral usage

Majadati al-ibilu is recorded for camels reaching abundant pasture and feeding there.

Amjada al-rāʿī ibilahu concerns a herder providing ample pasture or food.

Some lexical reports connect it with satiation, others with approaching satiation.

Regional differences in the amount eaten and the verbal construction are also recorded.

The branch clearly concerns abundant food and access to pasture.

A semantic relationship with lavish generosity is imaginable, but human honor has not been shown to have historically developed from camels becoming satiated.

Some lexical explanations propose that connection as an origin; establishing the chronological development requires separate evidence.

Abundance and breadth matter without reducing majd to “filling” or “being full.”

An Arabic proverb

A traditional proverb says:

«فِي كُلِّ شَجَرٍ نَارٌ، وَاسْتَمْجَدَ الْمَرْخُ وَالْعَفَارُ»

Fī kulli shajarin nārun wa-istamjada al-markhu wa-al-ʿafāru.

“There is fire in every tree, but markh and ʿafār have a greater share.”

Markh and ʿafār are plants traditionally associated with generating fire through wood friction.

Istamjada here conveys a greater share and distinction in this property.

A quality may exist in many things while some excel in it.

This relates to abundance and excellence.

The proverb’s first date is not independently known. Its inclusion in dictionaries does not establish that it circulated unchanged throughout pre-Islamic times.

Nor should its material image be imposed on the Qur’an: divine or Qur’anic majīd does not mean possessing more fire.

3. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

The root occurs four times in one principal form:

  • Majīdun — Hūd 11:73: describing God alongside ḥamīdun.
  • Al-majīdi — Qāf 50:1: describing the Qur’an.
  • Al-majīdu — al-Burūj 85:15: in the usual reading, describing God; with the genitive ending in another reading, His Throne.
  • Majīdun — al-Burūj 85:21: describing the Qur’an.

Majīd follows the faʿīl pattern.

This pattern can express a prominent, established quality, but morphology alone does not make every faʿīl word more intense than all related forms.

Mājid is also known. Majīd is sometimes treated as more emphatic, but this should not become an unexamined absolute rule.

Majd, mājid, majāda, majjada, tamjīd, tamajjada, amjada, mumājada, and istamjada are recorded in Arabic but do not occur as such in the Qur’an.

Majīd is not reserved for God: it also describes the Qur’an.

Its presence alone does not make it a divine proper name. Each verse’s grammar determines its referent.

4. Majīd describing God

Hūd 11:73

«قَالُوا أَتَعْجَبِينَ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ رَحْمَتُ اللَّهِ وَبَرَكَاتُهُ عَلَيْكُمْ أَهْلَ الْبَيْتِ إِنَّهُ حَمِيدٌ مَجِيدٌ»

Qālū a-taʿjabīna min amri Allāhi raḥmatu Allāhi wa-barakātuhu ʿalaykum ahla al-bayti innahu ḥamīdun majīd.

“They said: Do you marvel at the command of The God? The mercy of The God and His blessings be upon you, people of this household. Indeed, He is praiseworthy and glorious.”

Majīdun describes God here.

The divine messengers respond to Abraham’s wife after announcing a child.

Hūd 11:71 announces Isaac and, after him, Jacob.

Hūd 11:72 describes her amazement at bearing a child despite her and her husband’s old age.

Hūd 11:73 then speaks of God’s command, mercy, and blessings, closing with innahu ḥamīdun majīdun.

Ḥamīd and majīd

Ḥamīd belongs to ح م د and concerns praiseworthiness.

Majīd belongs to م ج د and concerns nobility, honor, and glory.

The descriptions are related but distinct.

God is ḥamīd: worthy of praise.

God is majīd: noble, glorious, and exalted.

Translating both simply “praised” erases the lexical distinction.

Invented meanings should not be assigned merely to differentiate them. They are related qualities that may coexist.

Their conjunction with mercy and blessing matters.

The messengers describe mercy and blessings upon Abraham’s family, then call God ḥamīd and majīd.

The context accommodates gracious nobility and abundant divine favor as well as grandeur.

Majīd itself does not mean giving a child or blessing a family; these are the contextual events and gifts.

Divine glory and human family honor

Early human uses sometimes connect majd with honorable ancestry and family standing.

That restriction cannot be transferred to God.

Divine nobility does not arise from lineage, family, outside support, or a status someone else bestowed.

Hūd 11:73 attributes majīd to God Himself.

His glory does not depend on recognition or praise.

Human denial does not alter the divine quality.

The divine application thus transcends human social limitations.

5. Majīd describing the Qur’an

Qāf 50:1

«ق وَالْقُرْآنِ الْمَجِيدِ»

Qāf wa-al-Qurʾāni al-majīd.

“Qāf. By the glorious Qur’an.”

Al-majīdi qualifies al-Qurʾāni.

Both are genitive and agree grammatically.

The description concerns the Qur’an, rather than God or Muhammad.

Why is the Qur’an called majīd?

Majīd can describe something of high standing, worth, and dignity.

The surah opens by attributing this quality to the Qur’an.

It then discusses opponents amazed by a warner from among themselves and doubtful of resurrection after death.

Qāf 50:2 and Qāf 50:3 express that amazement and denial.

Qāf 50:4 mentions God’s knowledge of what the earth diminishes of people and a preserving record.

Qāf 50:5 identifies denial of the truth.

The opening therefore presents an honored, exalted discourse against the opponents’ astonishment and rejection.

The root itself does not mean proving resurrection or explaining every detail of creation; later verses address those subjects separately.

Does majīd mean outward beauty?

The Qur’an’s glory should not be reduced to calligraphy, a reciter’s voice, or decorated manuscripts.

A beautifully written codex does not define al-Qurʾāni al-majīdi.

Pleasant recitation likewise does not exhaust the message’s value and standing.

The Qur’an itself is called majīd.

Its dignity concerns the message rather than only the writing surface or voice carrying it.

Nor does the adjective independently validate every claim made about the Qur’an. Other issues require their own Qur’anic evidence.

Al-Burūj 85:21

«بَلْ هُوَ قُرْآنٌ مَجِيدٌ»

Bal huwa Qurʾānun majīd.

“Rather, it is a glorious Qur’an.”

Majīdun again qualifies the Qur’an.

The sentence’s construction differs.

Qāf 50:1 has definite al-Qurʾāni al-majīdi within an oath.

Al-Burūj 85:21 has indefinite Qurʾānun majīdun in a declarative sentence.

Majīd retains dignity and glory in both.

The description in al-Burūj’s context

Al-Burūj 85:19 describes people persisting in denial.

Al-Burūj 85:20 states God’s encompassing them.

Al-Burūj 85:21 then calls the Qur’an glorious.

The sequence sets human rejection against the Qur’an’s truth and standing.

Denial itself does not erase the message’s worth.

Al-Burūj 85:22 continues:

«فِي لَوْحٍ مَحْفُوظٍ»

Fī lawḥin maḥfūẓ.

“In a preserved tablet.”

Maḥfūẓ comes from ح ف ظ, keeping and protecting.

Majīd and maḥfūẓ are distinct.

Majīd describes Qur’anic honor and dignity.

Maḥfūẓ in the next verse concerns preservation.

The qualities may coexist without translating majīd directly as “preserved.”

The two words alone do not specify the tablet’s shape, substance, or physical structure.

6. Al-majīd: God or the Throne?

Al-Burūj 85:15

In the usual reading:

«ذُو الْعَرْشِ الْمَجِيدُ»

Dhū al-ʿarshi al-majīdu.

“Possessor of the Throne, the Glorious.”

The verse is especially important grammatically and in its variant readings.

In the usual Ḥafṣ reading, al-majīdu is nominative, ending in ḍamma.

Other recognized readings have genitive al-majīdi, ending in kasra.

The difference changes the referent of the description, rather than merely its sound.

Nominative: God is glorious

In the reading:

«ذُو الْعَرْشِ الْمَجِيدُ»

Dhū al-ʿarshi al-majīdu.

Al-majīdu is nominative.

Dhū, “possessor,” is also nominative.

Al-ʿarshi is genitive as its complement.

Al-majīdu can qualify dhū al-ʿarshi or form another predicate about the same referent.

The meaning is:

“He is Possessor of the Throne and glorious.”

Glory is directly attributed to God.

This accords with the preceding verses.

Al-Burūj 85:12 describes the Lord’s forceful seizure.

Al-Burūj 85:13 presents Him as originating and restoring.

Al-Burūj 85:14 calls Him forgiving and loving.

Al-Burūj 85:15 then describes Him as Possessor of the Throne and glorious.

Al-Burūj 85:16 says He does what He wills.

Majīd thus accompanies power, forgiveness, love, and divine freedom to act.

“Glorious” or “most noble” naturally expresses the divine description here.

Genitive: the Throne is glorious

The alternative reading has:

«ذُو الْعَرْشِ الْمَجِيدِ»

Dhū al-ʿarshi al-majīdi.

Genitive al-majīdi can qualify al-ʿarshi.

The meaning becomes:

“Possessor of the glorious Throne.”

Majīd directly describes the Throne in this reading.

The difference should remain visible: the grammatical referents differ.

In the nominative reading, God is glorious.

In the genitive reading, the Throne is described as glorious.

Both have defensible Arabic constructions and are recorded in the recitation tradition.

What does the Throne’s glory mean?

It conveys glory, greatness, and high standing.

The word does not specify dimensions, material, shape, or spatial coordinates.

In human language a throne or seat of rule may be called glorious because of its grandeur and importance.

That linguistic illustration does not determine the divine Throne’s construction.

Its majd here denotes honor and glory, not a physical blueprint.

Nor does the adjective give the Throne independent power. God is dhū al-ʿarshi: the Throne is presented in relation to its Possessor.

Translating the readings

A base text following Ḥafṣ should retain nominative al-majīdu.

A suitable translation is:

“Possessor of the Throne, the Glorious.”

For genitive al-majīdi:

“Possessor of the glorious Throne.”

The Persian wording differs only slightly, but the referent matters greatly.

A root study should identify both readings and the construction underlying each translation.

The difference does not authorize merging both meanings into the main translation through added wording. One reading supplies the base text; the other belongs in explanation.

7. Distinctions from neighboring roots

م ج د and ح م د: nobility and praiseworthiness

Ḥamd concerns praise and deserving praise.

Majd concerns honor, nobility, and exalted standing.

Hūd 11:73 describes God with both: ḥamīdun majīdun.

The words share a semantic neighborhood without requiring a repetitious identical translation.

Praise may respond to virtue; nobility is the quality recognized as worthy of esteem.

The divine descriptions emphasize praiseworthiness and glorious nobility respectively.

م ج د and ك ر م: honor and generosity

Karam concerns nobility, generosity, and precious worth.

It clearly overlaps with majd.

A majīd person may also be generous and karīm.

Majd can express broader honor and exalted nobility; karam often foregrounds generosity and worth.

Al-Burūj 85:21 calls the Qur’an majīd.

Al-Wāqiʿa 56:77 calls it karīm.

The descriptions are close but belong to different roots whose distinction should remain.

No absolute boundary makes karīm only generous and majīd only high-ranking: both have broader ranges.

م ج د and ع ز ز: nobility and power

ʿIzza concerns strength, invincibility, and standing not easily brought under another’s domination.

Qur’anic majd particularly conveys honor, nobility, and glory.

Great influence can exist without virtue.

A noble person may lack extensive political or military power.

Power and nobility are therefore distinguishable.

Al-Burūj 85:8 calls God al-ʿazīzi al-ḥamīdi, and al-Burūj 85:15 uses al-majīdu.

Together these describe related yet distinct aspects of divine power, praiseworthiness, and glory.

م ج د and ع ظ م: glory and greatness

ʿAẓama concerns greatness and importance.

Material greatness can involve size; nonmaterial greatness, importance and standing.

Majd likewise conveys glory and high rank, with nobility and honor especially prominent.

Not everything large is majīd.

A huge building’s size alone does not establish its dignity or value.

A short statement can be exalted because of its worth.

The Qur’an’s majīd quality should not be reduced to its physical bulk or number of verses.

م ج د and ج ل ل: nobility and majesty

Jalāl concerns greatness and majesty.

This approaches majd.

Both can express divine glory and exaltedness.

Majd also connects with generosity and honor, while jalāl more particularly emphasizes grandeur.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary without overlap.

م ج د and ج د د: nobility and exaltedness

Al-Jinn 72:3 says:

«وَأَنَّهُ تَعَالَىٰ جَدُّ رَبِّنَا مَا اتَّخَذَ صَاحِبَةً وَلَا وَلَدًا»

Wa-annahu taʿālā jaddu rabbinā mā ittakhadha ṣāḥibatan wa-lā waladā.

Jaddu rabbinā here concerns the Lord’s greatness, exalted standing, and independence.

The sense approaches majd.

ج د د and م ج د remain separate roots.

Similarity in divine descriptions does not establish identical lexical origins or derivatives.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

A Persian equivalent should preserve nobility, honor, and high standing as far as possible.

“Glorious,” “noble,” “honored,” and “exalted” are defensible equivalents.

No single equivalent conveys the full range equally in every sentence.

“Glorious” emphasizes grandeur.

“Noble” more closely evokes honor and graciousness.

“Honored” conveys worth and esteem.

“Exalted” emphasizes high standing.

“Glorious” and “noble” suit God; “glorious” or “honored” naturally describe the Qur’an.

Hūd 11:73

«إِنَّهُ حَمِيدٌ مَجِيدٌ»

Innahu ḥamīdun majīd.

“Indeed, He is praiseworthy and glorious.”

The translation preserves the distinction between ḥamīd and majīd.

A note may explain gracious nobility and glory in harmony with the verse’s divine mercy and blessings.

Qāf 50:1

«ق وَالْقُرْآنِ الْمَجِيدِ»

Qāf wa-al-Qurʾāni al-majīd.

“Qāf. By the glorious Qur’an.”

“Glorious” conveys its dignity and high standing.

“The honored Qur’an” is also defensible, while “glorious” preserves the oath’s grandeur more clearly.

Al-Burūj 85:15

In the usual reading:

«ذُو الْعَرْشِ الْمَجِيدُ»

Dhū al-ʿarshi al-majīdu.

“Possessor of the Throne, the Glorious.”

In the alternative reading:

«ذُو الْعَرْشِ الْمَجِيدِ»

Dhū al-ʿarshi al-majīdi.

“Possessor of the glorious Throne.”

The root article should make the differing referents explicit.

Al-Burūj 85:21

«بَلْ هُوَ قُرْآنٌ مَجِيدٌ»

Bal huwa Qurʾānun majīd.

“Rather, it is a glorious Qur’an.”

This agrees with Qāf 50:1 and attributes majīd to the Qur’an itself.

Non-Qur’anic equivalents should likewise follow the subject.

Rajulun mājidun: “a noble, honorable man.”

Majdu al-qawmi: “the people’s honor and nobility.”

Majjadahu: “He honored and praised him.”

Tamajjada can mean “He recounted his noble qualities and honors.”

None of the four Qur’anic uses means merely social fame, abundant wealth, or family prestige.

9. Synthesis

م ج د concerns honor, nobility, glory, dignity, and exalted standing.

Early poetic usage can connect it with personal virtues, generosity, ancestral honor, and social leadership.

Zuhayr’s attributed poetry presents majd as a valued goal for which people compete and which worthy conduct helps attain.

Lexicons also record abundant gifts, hospitality, and ample camel pasture. These may connect through breadth and abundance without proving the chronological development of every sense.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an, only as majīd.

Hūd 11:73 describes God as ḥamīdun majīdun: praiseworthy and glorious.

Qāf 50:1 and al-Burūj 85:21 describe the Qur’an as honored and glorious.

In al-Burūj 85:15, the reading determines whether al-majīd describes God or His Throne.

Forqan definition: م ج د principally conveys nobility, honor, and exalted dignity. Majīd describes someone or something possessing nobility, glory, or elevated worth: human virtue and generosity, divine nobility and perfection, or the Qur’an’s exalted value according to context.

Majd overlaps with ḥamd, karam, ʿizza, ʿaẓama, and jalāl, without any one being a complete substitute.

Being majīd should not be equated with fame, public praise, or outward power. The number of admirers and extent of influence do not alone determine nobility.

Qur’anic usage transcends family prestige and tribal honors: God is described through nobility and glory, and the Qur’an through its exalted worth.

The Qur’anic center is nobility and glory joined with exalted dignity, applied appropriately to God, the Qur’an, or the Throne according to its referent.
