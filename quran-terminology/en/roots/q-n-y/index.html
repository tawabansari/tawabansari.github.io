The Qur’anic Root Q N Y

Acquiring and keeping property; bestowing possessions and bringing satisfaction

Qur’anic forms

أَقْنَىٰ

Aqnā

Form IV perfect; Al-Najm 53:48

1. Basic meaning and linguistic history

The Arabic root Q N Y concerns acquiring something, possessing it, and keeping it for one’s use.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period supplies transmitted evidence for possessing resources in a wāw form of this family. This evidence is considered alongside dictionaries’ records of wāw and yāʾ forms; it cannot establish that every interpretation of aqnā existed in pre-Qur’anic Arabic.

In classical Arabic, qinyah denotes property acquired for oneself with the intention of retaining and using it. Iqtināʾ means acquiring and keeping property, and iqnāʾ means providing another with such possessions.

Being satisfied and bringing satisfaction are also recorded meanings of this family.

Qur’anic form examined: أَقْنَىٰ (aqnā), the sole occurrence under this entry, in Al-Najm 53:48. The adopted translation is “bestowed possessions”; another reading is “brought satisfaction.” Three additional complete verses provide comparison and context.

Other known Arabic forms include qanā, acquiring and keeping; qinyah, retained property; iqtināʾ, acquiring possessions for oneself; muqtanayāt, possessions and collected objects; and iqnāʾ, providing another with possessions. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. Evidence from early Arabic

Property and possessing resources

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, in a poem about suffering and life’s changes, reads:

أَلَا إِنَّ بَعْدَ الْعُدْمِ لِلْمَرْءِ قَنْوَةً وَبَعْدَ الْمَشِيبِ طُولَ عُمُرٍ وَمَلْبَسَا

“Indeed, after poverty a person may attain possessions, and after gray hair there may still be long life and enjoyment.”

Qanwah stands opposite al-ʿudm in this verse. Here ʿudm means poverty and lack of material resources, while qanwah describes possessing resources after deprivation.

The first hemistich is transmitted with different forms, including qinyah.

The verse supplies transmitted evidence for the contrast between poverty and possessions in the form qanwah. Its variants and uncertain precise date must remain acknowledged. It cannot by itself establish “bringing satisfaction” or “impoverishing” as the meaning of the Qur’anic verb.

Qinyah: property kept for use

In classical Arabic, qanaytu al-māl means acquiring and keeping property for oneself.

Lexical sources record qanaytu al-ʿanz for keeping a goat to benefit from its milk. Wāw forms are also reported in the same field of acquiring and retaining.

A home acquired to live in, land kept for cultivation, or tools retained for one’s occupation provide tangible examples of this semantic field.

An intention to keep property does not make its later sale or transfer impossible. Qinyah describes how property is acquired and held, not an immutable legal rule governing it.

Possessions and satisfaction

Arabic lexical sources record qinā for satisfaction, while aqnāhu can mean satisfying someone as well as bestowing possessions.

A semantic relationship can be seen between possessing resources and being satisfied with them. This explains a connection between meanings; it does not establish the historical development of one from the other.

Material possessions and inward satisfaction are not identical. Someone wealthy may remain dissatisfied, while someone with limited means may be content.

3. Aqnā in the Qur’an

The Qur’an uses this root in Al-Najm while describing divine actions and power:

وَأَنَّهُ هُوَ أَغنىٰ وَأَقنىٰ

Wa-annahu huwa aghnā wa-aqnā.

“And it is He who gave sufficiency and bestowed possessions.”

An-Najm 53:48

Two verbs from different roots occur together:

Aghnā, from Gh N Y, means making free of need or providing sufficiency. Aqnā, from Q N Y, means providing possessions that a person can keep for their own use.

Both verbs are masculine singular perfects in Form IV. The second is coordinated with the first, and both have The God as their subject.

The objects are not specified. The statement attributes sufficiency and possessions to The God, without identifying a recipient, amount, or type of property.

First reading: bestowing possessions and resources

In this study’s adopted reading, aqnā means “bestowed possessions”: providing someone with something to keep and use. This preference rests on the recorded meanings of qinyah and iqnāʾ.

On this reading, aghnā and aqnā differ in emphasis: making free of need and bestowing possessions. This is not a rigid division between meeting temporary needs and supplying permanent property. Possessions can provide sufficiency, and iqnāʾ does not guarantee their everlasting retention.

Second reading: bringing satisfaction

On another recorded meaning of iqnāʾ, the verse can be translated:

“And it is He who gave sufficiency and brought satisfaction.”

On this reading, aghnā concerns supplying adequate resources, while aqnā concerns satisfaction with what is received.

For a conceptual comparison between giving and satisfaction—not evidence of a shared root or proof of aqnā’s meaning—consider:

وَلَسَوفَ يُعطيكَ رَبُّكَ فَتَرضىٰ

Wa-la-sawfa yuʿṭīka rabbuka fa-tarḍā.

“And your Lord will surely give to you, and you will be satisfied.”

Ad-Duha 93:5

Giving and becoming satisfied are related but distinct here. The Lord gives, and the recipient becomes satisfied.

In explaining the satisfaction reading, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī describes iqnāʾ as giving satisfaction as though it were a possession. This is his lexical and exegetical explanation, not independent pre-Qur’anic evidence for that meaning.

The reading has lexical support, but the presence of satisfaction in the root’s semantic family does not itself prove that Al-Najm exclusively means inward contentment.

Does aqnā mean impoverishing?

Some early interpretations explain aqnā as making poor.

One proposed contextual clue is the structure of neighboring verses: Al-Najm 53:43 speaks of making laugh and weep, Al-Najm 53:44 of causing death and giving life, and Al-Najm 53:45 of creating the male and female pair.

On that basis, some interpreters also seek opposites in verse 48, placing aqnā, understood as impoverishing, opposite aghnā.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an attributes both expanding and restricting provision to The God, as in Al-Raʿd 13:26 and Al-Shūrā 42:12. That concept is therefore present in the Qur’an.

Its presence, however, does not establish it as the lexical meaning of aqnā.

This study found no clear independent evidence of pre-Qur’anic aqnā meaning “impoverishing.” Its interpretation as the opposite of aghnā is recorded, but coordination of two verbs alone does not require opposite meanings.

“Bestowed possessions” is therefore adopted, with “brought satisfaction” retained as another reading. “Impoverished” is reported as an interpretation, not a meaning made certain by the contrasts in neighboring verses.

4. Iqnāʾ and human circumstances

Attributing resources to The God can be read alongside human responsibility. However, the verses about striving in this surah concern the value of deeds and recompense; they should not be treated as a guarantee that every diligent person will become wealthy.

The Qur’an says in the same surah:

وَأَن لَيسَ لِلإِنسٰنِ إِلّا ما سَعىٰ

Wa-an laysa li-l-insāni illā mā saʿā.

“And that a human being has only their striving.”

An-Najm 53:39

Al-Najm 53:40 and Al-Najm 53:41 speak of striving being seen and fully recompensed. The example of a farmer working with land, water, and personal abilities merely illustrates how effort and available resources can coexist; it is neither the verse’s translation nor a guarantee of economic results.

Possessing wealth does not itself demonstrate divine approval of all the owner’s conduct. Al-Fajr 89:15 and Al-Fajr 89:16 report the human assumption that plentiful provision means divine honor and restricted provision means humiliation. Al-Fajr 89:17 rejects it with kallā, “No indeed.” Reporting that assumption must not be mistaken for Qur’anic endorsement.

On the adopted reading, iqnāʾ in Al-Najm 53:48 concerns giving and providing possessions. The ethical value of how property is obtained and used is a separate question, assessed through other Qur’anic teachings.

5. Comparison with related roots

Q N Y (qinyah) and Gh N Y (ghinā): property and sufficiency

Ghinā highlights sufficiency and freedom from need; qinyah highlights property kept for use. The adopted translation of Al-Najm 53:48, “gave sufficiency and bestowed possessions,” preserves these perspectives without assuming wholly separate classes of property or necessary successive stages.

Al-Ḍuḥā says:

وَوَجَدَكَ عائِلًا فَأَغنىٰ

Wa-wajadaka ʿāʾilan fa-aghnā.

“He found you in need and gave you sufficiency.”

Ad-Duha 93:8

Q N Y (iqtināʾ) and M L K (milk): acquiring property and ownership

Milk has a broad semantic field involving ownership, authority, and control over something.

Iqtināʾ more particularly emphasizes acquiring and retaining something for use.

Ownership describes a person’s relationship to property, while iqtināʾ emphasizes acquiring and taking possession of it for oneself.

Q N Y (qinyah) and M W L (māl): retained property and wealth generally

Māl is a broad term for property, encompassing money, livestock, land, goods, and other valuable resources.

Qinyah more particularly describes property acquired to retain and use for oneself.

Goods a merchant buys for resale belong to the merchant’s māl. A home and work tools retained for personal, continuing use are clearer examples of qinyah.

The distinction is prominent in lexical usage, but should not become a rigid economic or legal classification governing every type of property.

Q N Y (qinā) and Q N ʿ (qanāʿah): possessions and satisfaction

Qanāʿah, from Q N ʿ, concerns being content and satisfied with what one has or receives.

In Q N Y’s satisfaction branch, iqnāʾ can mean bringing someone satisfaction, overlapping in this respect with iqnāʿ and qanāʿah.

The two roots nevertheless have distinct linguistic structures.

The relationship between Q N Y and Q N W

Arabic sources also use qanaytu and qanawtu interchangeably for acquiring and keeping something.

Qinyah and qinwah show the same overlap. Lexicographers have differed over the underlying wāw or yāʾ in some forms.

Forqan’s index and the morphological index used in this study organize qinwān in Al-Anʿām 6:99 under Q N W and aqnā in Al-Najm 53:48 under Q N Y. This practical distinction does not assert complete historical separation of the families.

Despite their historical and lexical overlap, the date-cluster meaning of qinwān should not be imported into iqnāʾ in Al-Najm.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Bestowed possessions” is proposed for aqnā in Al-Najm 53:48.

“Brought satisfaction” is also defensible on another semantic branch and is best retained in the explanation.

Rendering both aghnā and aqnā simply as “made wealthy” obscures the distinction between sufficiency, freedom from need, and being given possessions.

Outside the Qur’an, qinyah can be translated as property kept for use; iqtināʾ as acquiring and retaining something for oneself; and muqtanayāt as possessions and collected objects.

7. Conclusion

Q N Y concerns acquiring something, keeping it for oneself, and possessing property and resources. Satisfaction and bringing satisfaction are also recognized meanings of the family.

Proposed definition for Forqan: Acquiring and keeping something for oneself and using it; in iqnāʾ, bestowing property and resources on another, and, in another branch, bringing that person satisfaction.
