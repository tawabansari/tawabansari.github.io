Decay and deterioration, repair and restoration, and other branches of this word family

Brief root meaning: This family has several distinct branches: decaying, wearing away, and falling apart; repairing and restoring what has been damaged or scattered; and, in other branches, remaining silent or moving the mouth to speak. In the Qur’an, only the branch of decay and deterioration occurs, in the word ramīm.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The triliteral root R M M is a doubled Arabic root: its final two consonants are identical. In forms such as رَمَّ and رَمّ, these two mīms are represented by a shaddah.

This family has a notable semantic feature: Arabic uses it both for deterioration and disintegration and for repairing and restoring something damaged.

For example, رَمَّ العَظْمُ in one established use refers to a bone decaying and deteriorating, whereas رَمَمْتُ الدَّارَ means “I repaired the house.”

The outcomes of these uses point in opposite directions. In one, a thing loses strength or integrity; in the other, its damaged parts are repaired and its condition improves.

However, opposing meanings within one word family do not mean that every derivative can carry both meanings regardless of context.

Ramīm occurs twice in the Qur’an: once concerning decayed bones and once in a comparison concerning things reduced by a destructive wind to a deteriorated, disintegrated condition.

Thus, the Qur’anic meaning of ramīm concerns decay and deterioration, not repair and restoration.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

The root R M M occurs twice in the Qur’an, in two verses and in one basic lexical form:

  • رَمِيمٌ: An indefinite nominative word meaning decayed and deteriorated; Yā Sīn 36:78.
  • كَالرَّمِيمِ: The same word with the comparison particle ka- and the definite article al-, meaning like something decayed and disintegrated; Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42.

Other forms known in Arabic include رَمَّ, رَمّ, رَمِيم, رِمَّة, رُمَّة, رِمَام, مِرَمَّة, أَرَمَّ, إِرْمَام, اِسْتَرَمَّ, and تَرْمِيم. The reduplicated quadriliteral form تَرَمْرَمَ is also discussed alongside this family in lexical works; it should be distinguished from direct derivatives of the triliteral root.

These forms belong to different branches and do not all have the same meaning.

Ramīm in the Qur’an describes deterioration, whereas tarmīm in familiar modern Persian and Arabic usage means repairing and restoring something damaged.

2. Semantic branches in early Arabic

Four branches in an early lexical analysis

One early lexical analysis identifies four semantic principles for this family, arranged in two opposing pairs.

The first pair comprises repair and deterioration: restoring something damaged versus its decaying and falling apart.

The second concerns remaining silent and moving the mouth to speak.

This classification matters because it shows that even early lexical scholarship did not derive every use of this family from one simple meaning.

For example, ramīm meaning decayed does not imply that tarmīm must originally mean causing decay. Nor does the restoration sense make the Qur’anic ramīm bones repaired bones.

Each branch must be understood through its own linguistic evidence and usage.

رَمَّ الشيءُ: Decaying and deteriorating

In early Arabic, رَمَّ الشيءُ describes something that has lost its strength through time, decay, or wear.

An established lexical explanation states:

رَمَّ العَظْمُ إِذَا بَلِيَ

ramma al-ʿaẓmu idhā baliya

Translation: “The bone is described by this verb when it has decayed and deteriorated.”

This use is among the clearest indications of the meaning of ramīm in the Qur’an.

After a living creature dies, a bone may change and deteriorate over time. It loses its initial condition and may become brittle, crumble, or disintegrate.

However, ramīm itself does not specify the precise stages of that process.

It does not determine how long the bone has been underground, how much of its material remains, or which biological and chemical processes have altered it.

What the language conveys is a condition of decay and deterioration.

رِمَّة: A decayed bone or thing

Rimmah is also used in Arabic for a decayed bone.

Ramīm and rimmah are closely related in this lexical branch.

Their usage, however, is not confined to bones.

For example, rummah, with a ḍammah on the rāʾ, is recorded for a piece of old, worn rope.

This shows that the family’s deterioration sense can apply to different materials.

Bone, rope, and dry vegetation differ in material and structure, but all may lose strength and integrity.

Thus, decay and deterioration have a wider scope than the decay of bones alone.

This matters when comparing the two Qur’anic uses: bones are the subject in Yā Sīn 36:78, while Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42 employs the word in a broader image of destruction and disintegration.

رَمَّ الشيءَ: Repairing and restoring

In another branch, ramma means repairing something partly damaged.

An established lexical use of ramm is repairing something partly spoiled, such as worn rope or a house needing repairs.

Familiar examples include:

رَمَمْتُ الدَّارَ

ramamtu al-dāra

Meaning: “I repaired the house.”

And:

رَمَمْتُ الحَبْلَ

ramamtu al-ḥabla

Meaning: “I repaired the rope.”

In these uses, repair acts upon something previously damaged or weakened.

Repairing is therefore distinct from making something entirely new.

A person may build a new house, but repairing an old house involves something that already exists and now needs restoration.

Arabic also records رَمَّ الأَمْرَ for putting an affair in order after its elements have become scattered or its condition disordered.

Here, repair extends from material objects to setting affairs in order.

However, this extension does not make every moral, political, or social reform a direct meaning of R M M itself.

The derivative, its construction, and what it concerns must be examined.

Did decay and repair develop from one historical image?

These meanings are related through the condition of the object.

Something may deteriorate and subsequently be repaired.

For example, rope with damaged sections may be repaired again.

Yet this practical relationship does not conclusively establish the words’ historical origin.

Such an example alone cannot prove that repair developed from decay, or conversely that decay originally meant losing a repaired condition.

In the early lexical analysis mentioned above, these are presented as two opposing principles.

Both should therefore be retained in a root study without inventing an unsupported etymological history to merge them.

اِسْتَرَمَّ: Coming to need repair

Istaramma is recorded for something whose time for repair or restoration has arrived.

For example, this family is used of a wall that has not been plastered for a long time and needs repair.

Such usage does not simply describe a completely collapsed wall.

The wall may remain standing while part of it is damaged and needs restoration.

This distinction shows that deterioration does not always equal complete destruction.

Likewise, needing repair does not itself mean that repairs have been carried out.

Istaramma thus concerns a condition requiring restoration, while transitive ramma may express the repair itself.

أَرَمَّ: Improving in bodily condition

Aramma al-baʿīr is recorded for a camel or animal that has grown fat or improved in bodily condition.

In the early lexical classification mentioned above, this use belongs to the repair and improvement branch.

This does not mean repairing an animal’s body as one repairs a house; rather, its bodily condition has improved.

This usage shows that the improvement branch was not restricted to manufactured objects.

Nevertheless, aramma in this sense should not be confused with aramma in the silence branch.

Identical verbal forms within a family do not determine meaning without analysis of construction and context.

3. Other branches and evidence from early language and poetry

رُمَّة: Old, worn rope

Rummah is recorded in Arabic for a piece of worn rope.

It is particularly associated with rope used to tether an animal or hold something in place.

After long use, the rope’s strands may separate, or only portions may remain.

In this situation, the semantic connection between rummah and deterioration is clear.

However, the name for rope does not make every use of the root concern binding or taking captive.

Rope is one material instance within the deterioration branch.

A witness from Dhū al-Rummah’s poetry

Dhū al-Rummah, an Umayyad poet, describes a tent peg with remnants of worn rope still attached:

فِيهِ بَقَايَا رُمَّةِ التَّقْلِيدِ

fīhi baqāyā rummati al-taqlīdi

Translation: “On it remain remnants of the worn fastening rope.”

Here, rummah refers to rope of which parts remain.

This use illuminates the deterioration sense by showing that the word was not restricted to decayed bones.

Dhū al-Rummah lived after the Qur’an’s revelation. This is therefore an early Arabic witness from the Umayyad period, not a pre-Islamic one.

Lexical accounts also connect the poet’s familiar epithet with this word.

However, the precise explanation of his epithet should not replace the meaning of the poetic witness itself.

بِرُمَّتِهِ: With everything belonging to it

The expressions أَخَذَهُ بِرُمَّتِهِ and أَعْطَاهُ بِرُمَّتِهِ are recorded for taking or giving something in its entirety.

An early explanation describes a camel delivered to its buyer with its tether: the camel was given together with its rope.

The expression then extended to handing over the whole thing without omission.

This explanation is semantically intelligible.

Yet the existence of the expression alone cannot establish the details of its first historical use.

What matters is that rummah here does more than describe deterioration: in the idiom, it conveys entirety.

This idiomatic meaning must not be transferred to the Qur’anic ramīm.

Ramīm in Yā Sīn 36:78 does not mean complete and undiminished; it refers to decayed bones.

Taking vegetation with the lips

The expression رَمَّتِ الشَّاةُ الحَشِيشَ describes a sheep taking and eating vegetation with its lips.

In this field, مِرَمَّة refers to the lip or mouth area of a cloven-hoofed animal with which it takes grass. The variant مَرَمَّة, with an initial fatḥah, is also recorded.

This shows that the material recorded under R M M includes a verb concerning eating vegetation.

One might imagine a connection between plucking vegetation and its deterioration.

But that image does not establish historical derivation.

This branch should therefore be recognized as a separate lexical use, without making ramīm in the Qur’an mean food or grazing.

Silence and movement of the mouth

The same early lexical classification identifies two other branches concerning silence and movement of the mouth.

In one use, أَرَمَّ القومُ means that a group became silent or fell quiet.

By contrast, تَرَمْرَمَ concerns moving the mouth to speak. It is a reduplicated quadriliteral form discussed alongside this family, rather than a direct triliteral derivative.

These two uses are close in subject while contrasting in outcome.

Someone may remain silent without speaking, or move the lips and mouth and utter words.

Neither use makes silence or speech the meaning of Qur’anic ramīm.

These branches are absent from the two Qur’anic occurrences examined here.

A witness from Aws ibn Ḥajar’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar says:

وَمُسْتَعْجِبٍ مِمَّا يَرَى مِنْ أَنَاتِنَا
وَلَوْ زَبَنَتْهُ الْحَرْبُ لَمْ يَتَرَمْرَمِ

wa-mustaʿjibin mimmā yarā min anātinā / wa-law zabanat-hu al-ḥarbu lam yataramrami

Translation: “Many marvel at the patience and deliberation they see in us; yet if war struck them hard, they would not even move their lips to speak.”

Here, lam yataramram means not moving the mouth to speak.

This witness shows that taramrama was associated with speech or mouth movement in pre-Islamic poetry.

The verse itself concerns neither decayed bones nor repairing an object.

Thus, discussing these forms together in a lexical entry does not require translating them all with one equivalent or treating them all as direct triliteral derivatives.

This distinction is especially important in families with several branches.

A poetic witness concerning decayed bones

Lexical works transmit the following verse:

أَمَا وَالَّذِي لَا يَعْلَمُ السِّرَّ غَيْرُهُ
وَيُحْيِي الْعِظَامَ الْبِيضَ وَهِيَ رَمِيمُ

amā wa-alladhī lā yaʿlamu al-sirra ghayruhu / wa-yuḥyī al-ʿiẓāma al-bīḍa wa-hiya ramīmu

Translation: “By the One who alone knows the secret and revives the white bones when they are decayed.”

This verse is sometimes attributed to Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī and sometimes to another poet.

The early transmission itself expresses uncertainty about its attribution.

Its conspicuous resemblance in wording and theme to the Qur’anic verse also prevents confidently dating it before revelation without independent research.

It is useful evidence for ramīm applied to decayed bones, but not secure evidence that this particular verse predates the Qur’an.

The meaning of bone decay is independently recorded regardless of this attribution.

4. رَمِيمٌ: Decayed bones in Yā Sīn 36:78

Verse text

Yā Sīn 36:78

وَضَرَبَ لَنَا مَثَلًا وَنَسِيَ خَلْقَهُ ۖ قَالَ مَنْ يُحْيِي الْعِظَامَ وَهِيَ رَمِيمٌ

wa-ḍaraba lanā mathalan wa-nasiya khalqahu qāla man yuḥyī al-ʿiẓāma wa-hiya ramīmun

Yā Sīn 36:78

Translation:

“He offered Us an example and forgot his own creation. He said: Who will revive the bones when they are decayed?”

The meaning of ramīm in the verse

Ramīmun here refers to bones that have decayed and deteriorated.

Morphologically, the word follows the faʿīl pattern and appears after wa-hiya in the sentence.

Al-ʿiẓām is the plural of ʿaẓm and means bones.

The pronoun hiya refers back to those bones.

The subject is therefore clear: bones that have lost their original condition and undergone decay.

Ramīm here does not mean repaired, restored, or made sound.

Selecting such a meaning would alter the entire image of the question.

The questioner raises the difficulty of reviving deteriorated bones, not bones previously repaired.

Why does ramīm lack a feminine ending?

The pronoun hiya is feminine singular here, but ramīm has no feminine tāʾ ending.

Early grammatical and interpretive discussions examine this point.

One explanation treats ramīm as a use of the faʿīl pattern whose form remains the same for masculine and feminine. This does not establish such invariance for every word on the faʿīl pattern.

Another analysis treats ramīm more as a name for a decayed condition or substance than as an adjective necessarily displaying gender agreement.

Under this nominal analysis, it names what has decayed, comparable to rimmah and rufāt, rather than requiring the feminine ending of an ordinary adjective.

The absence of tāʾ in ramīm is therefore not a grammatical error.

Nor does this feature mean that the bones have become one bone in gender or number.

Al-ʿiẓām is a nonhuman plural, to which Arabic can refer with a feminine singular pronoun.

These differing morphological analyses do not change the essential translation: the bones are decayed and deteriorated.

Does ramīm mean the complete destruction of bone?

Decay is not identical to complete and absolute destruction.

A bone may retain part of its shape while becoming severely deteriorated and brittle.

In another condition, parts of it may have crumbled and scattered.

Ramīm conveys decay and deterioration, but does not determine the amount of remaining matter or the exact stage of decomposition.

“Decayed” is consequently more precise than “completely annihilated.”

Nor does ramīm alone mean ash: ash is associated with burning, whereas bone can decay without burning.

This verse says nothing about burning bones or turning them to ash.

The questioner’s statement

Yā Sīn 36:77 describes a human created from a small quantity of reproductive fluid who has now become an open disputant.

Verse 78 then presents that person’s question about reviving decayed bones.

This matters for understanding the verse.

The words مَنْ يُحْيِي الْعِظَامَ وَهِيَ رَمِيمٌ belong to someone who considers revival unlikely.

The Qur’an quotes the question and then answers it.

Two parts of the statement should be distinguished.

First, bones can indeed decay; this is a familiar condition.

Second, the questioner uses that condition to dismiss or deny revival.

His error is therefore not observing decay but drawing a conclusion from it.

He moves from the bones’ deterioration to an alleged inability to revive them.

The Qur’an rejects that conclusion.

The Qur’anic answer: The first creation

Yā Sīn 36:79 states:

قُلْ يُحْيِيهَا الَّذِي أَنْشَأَهَا أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ ۖ وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ خَلْقٍ عَلِيمٌ

qul yuḥyīhā alladhī anshaʾahā awwala marratin wa-huwa bi-kulli khalqin ʿalīmun

Yā Sīn 36:79

Translation:

“Say: The One who brought them into being the first time will revive them; and He knows every creation.”

This answer links the ability to revive decayed bones with the first creation and The God’s knowledge.

The Qur’an does not say that bones never decay.

Nor does answering the denial of resurrection require denying actual bone decay.

Instead, it approaches the questioner’s reasoning from another direction: the One who originated creation can also revive it.

The answer shows that the matter’s deteriorated condition does not delimit the Creator’s power.

However, the root R M M itself does not make revival part of the meaning of ramīm.

Ramīm describes decay; reviving is expressed by the verb يُحْيِيهَا.

Is revival the same as repair?

The repair sense within R M M might suggest that the Qur’an deliberately plays upon the decay and repair of bones.

Such an association is conceivable as linguistic reflection, but its limits should be clear.

The verse does not use ramma in the sense of repairing.

The answer likewise uses yuḥyī, not yarummu or yurammimu.

Reviving should therefore not be presented as lexically equivalent to repairing bones.

Repairing a building or rope belongs to restoring a damaged object.

Bringing a being to life after death is a broader issue expressed through words for life, creation, and resurrection.

The family’s two opposing branches are linguistically significant, but do not establish that Qur’anic resurrection is merely repairing the former body.

Resurrection and the limits of human knowledge

Yā Sīn 36:81 also invokes the creation of the heavens and earth within this argument.

Yā Sīn 36:82 speaks of the realization of the divine command when He wills it.

Together, these statements explain revival in relation to the Creator’s power and knowledge.

However, ramīm does not disclose the biological, chemical, or physical mechanism of resurrection.

For example, the verse does not specify how every part of the former matter is gathered, how bodily structure emerges, or the nature of life after resurrection.

Those questions exceed the lexical subject of ramīm.

The word clearly means decay and deterioration; the argument for creation and revival comes from the sentences as a whole.

5. كَالرَّمِيمِ: Deteriorated and disintegrated matter in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42

Context

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41 states:

وَفِي عَادٍ إِذْ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمُ الرِّيحَ الْعَقِيمَ

wa-fī ʿādin idh arsalnā ʿalayhimu al-rīḥa al-ʿaqīma

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41

Translation:

“And in ʿĀd, when We sent against them the barren wind.”

The following verse describes that wind.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42

Verse text:

مَا تَذَرُ مِنْ شَيْءٍ أَتَتْ عَلَيْهِ إِلَّا جَعَلَتْهُ كَالرَّمِيمِ

mā tadharu min shayʾin atat ʿalayhi illā jaʿalat-hu ka-al-ramīmi

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42

Translation:

“It left nothing that it came upon without making it like decayed matter.”

The meaning of ka-al-ramīm

كَالرَّمِيمِ combines the comparison particle ka-, the definite article al-, and the noun ramīm.

Here, the word denotes something decayed, deteriorated, or disintegrated.

Unlike Yā Sīn 36:78, however, bones are not its immediate subject.

The verse describes a destructive wind reducing what lay within its destructive reach to a condition like decayed, disintegrated matter.

The sense remains deterioration and disintegration, but its application is broader.

Ramīm: Decayed bone or crumbled vegetation?

Early lexical and interpretive explanations offer several descriptions of al-ramīm here.

One connects it with vegetation that has dried and been crushed underfoot.

Another describes dry, crumbled leaves.

A further explanation compares it with decayed bone or something old and worn.

These differences need not represent unrelated meanings.

Decayed bone, dry leaves, and crumbled vegetation can share the loss of strength and integrity.

In Yā Sīn 36:78, the word explicitly concerns bones.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42 contains no such restriction; it uses the word in a general comparison for the wind’s destructive effect.

“Decayed and disintegrated matter” therefore fits this verse better than restricting the meaning to “decayed bone.”

The wind’s role in the image

The preceding verse introduces الرِّيحَ الْعَقِيمَ, the barren wind.

The present verse explains its destructive effect.

مَا تَذَرُ comes from W DH R and expresses not leaving or sparing.

أَتَتْ عَلَيْهِ concerns something the wind passes over or reaches.

جَعَلَتْهُ expresses transforming that thing’s condition.

Finally, كَالرَّمِيمِ describes the outcome through the image of deterioration and disintegration.

The verse’s meaning is thus composed of several elements.

R M M itself supplies the decayed and deteriorated condition.

The agent of destruction, its reach, and its scope come from the other expressions.

Did the wind literally make everything decay?

An Arabic comparison does not require two things to share every characteristic.

When the wind makes something “like ramīm,” that thing need not undergo the exact natural process by which bones decay.

A destructive impact may crush, break, or disintegrate something until its condition resembles decayed, crumbling matter.

The comparison in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42 emphasizes the destructive outcome, not an identical mechanism for bone decay and wind damage.

Conversely, the actual image of destruction should not be turned into a wholly abstract concept.

The verse describes a wind with a destructive effect on beings and objects.

This image also relates to other Qur’anic scenes concerning ʿĀd.

Does “anything” include every being in the universe?

مَا تَذَرُ مِنْ شَيْءٍ أَتَتْ عَلَيْهِ emphatically conveys the extensive destruction.

But the statement concerns what the wind encounters within its destructive reach.

It should not be converted into a claim that every being and object in the universe was destroyed at that time.

Nor does it establish that no historical or material trace of ʿĀd remains.

The subject is the severity and extent of destruction within the event described.

The verse’s emphatic language should thus be distinguished from an unsupported universal generalization.

Connections with other verses about ʿĀd

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6 and Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 also describe ʿĀd’s destruction by a powerful wind.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 compares the dead people to hollow palm trunks.

Al-Qamar 54:20 likewise presents the image of uprooted palm trunks.

Alongside Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42, these verses offer a clear field of destruction, uprooting, collapse, and disintegration.

Each image nevertheless has its own words and features.

In Al-Ḥāqqah and Al-Qamar, the comparison is with palm trunks.

In Al-Dhāriyāt, it is with ramīm.

Not every feature of a tree trunk should therefore be transferred to ramīm, or vice versa.

Does ramīm here mean ash?

Some interpretive reports also explain al-ramīm through ash.

This is understandable in terms of its broken-down appearance and scattered particles.

Yet ramīm and ramād belong to different roots.

Ramād names ash and concerns the residue of burning.

Ramīm concerns something decayed and deteriorated.

Both may resemble fine, disintegrated particles in a particular condition, but their direct meanings are not identical.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42 says nothing about objects burning in fire.

Ka-al-ramīm should therefore not be restricted without explanation to “like ash.”

“Like decayed and disintegrated matter” better preserves the semantic field.

6. Related roots and translation questions

R M M (ramīm) and B L Y (bilā)

Bilā in Arabic concerns deterioration and wearing out.

Lexical explanations also gloss رَمَّ العَظْمُ with بَلِيَ.

The two families are thus close in the field of decay and deterioration.

However, بَلِيَ has broad applications to worn objects and differing conditions.

Qur’anic ramīm is used for decayed bones and disintegrated matter.

Overlap does not make the two roots identical in every respect.

R M M (ramīm) and R F T (rufāt)

Rufāt is used in the Qur’an for crumbled and disintegrated remains.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:49, people find revival unlikely after becoming bones and crumbled remains.

The image clearly relates to the question in Yā Sīn 36:78.

Yet the words have different roots.

Ramīm emphasizes decay and deterioration.

Rufāt belongs to crumbling and becoming scattered remnants.

This distinction describes tendencies rather than an absolute boundary, since severe decay can also involve crumbling and dispersal.

R M M (ramīm) and N KH R (nakhir)

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11 contains the expression عِظَامًا نَخِرَةً.

Nakhirah concerns the decay and deterioration of bones.

This resembles Yā Sīn 36:78: in both, deteriorated bones appear in the speech of those who consider revival unlikely.

Nevertheless, N KH R and R M M are independent families.

Sharing the referent of decayed bones does not prove historical or etymological identity.

Nor does the overlap mean that one word always describes a specific, more advanced stage than the other.

R M M (ramīm) and H SH M (hashīm)

Hashīm is used in the Qur’an for dry, crumbled vegetation.

Al-Kahf 18:45 describes vegetation that, after growing green, becomes hashīm scattered by the winds.

This image relates to Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42 because both concern deteriorated or crumbled matter in relation to wind.

Hashīm and ramīm are nevertheless different words.

In Al-Dhāriyāt, the wind itself destroys things and reduces them to a condition like ramīm.

In Al-Kahf, vegetation first becomes hashīm and is then scattered by the winds.

The sequence of events and the imagery’s structure are consequently different.

R M M (ramīm) and R M D (ramād)

These roots share their first two letters but differ in basic meaning.

R M D concerns ash in its Qur’anic use.

R M M concerns decay and deterioration in its Qur’anic use.

Ash and decayed matter may both appear fragile and scattered, but their origins and meanings differ.

Ibrāhīm 14:18 compares a group’s deeds to ashes scattered by a strong wind.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42, the destructive wind reduces things to a condition like ramīm.

These images are close in destruction and dispersal, but the relationship operates differently.

Ramīm should therefore be translated distinctly from ramād.

R M M (ramm) and Ṣ L Ḥ (iṣlāḥ)

In the repair and restoration branch, R M M relates to the field of Ṣ L Ḥ.

Iṣlāḥ can concern human behavior, social relations, disputes, and unsound conditions.

A clear use of رَمَّ الشيءَ, however, also concerns repairing a damaged object such as a house or rope.

Repair can thus be one form of rectification, while rectification is not limited to repairing worn objects.

Ṣ L Ḥ also has extensive Qur’anic uses, whereas R M M appears only as ramīm meaning decayed.

The Qur’anic sense of ramīm must not be replaced with the general concept of reform or rectification.

Translation suggestions for Forqan

رَمِيمٌ — Yā Sīn 36:78

Suitable equivalents include “decayed,” “deteriorated,” or “decayed and crumbling.”

Within the complete sentence, “the bones when they are decayed” is natural and precise.

The supplied translation also preserves this meaning.

كَالرَّمِيمِ — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42

Suitable equivalents include “like decayed matter,” “like deteriorated and disintegrated material,” or “like crumbled and decayed remains.”

Within the complete sentence, “making it like decayed matter” is concise and defensible.

For a fuller explanation of the image, “like something deteriorated and fallen apart” is also suitable.

The word should not be restricted to bones here: its application extends to what the destructive wind encounters within its reach.

7. Conclusion and final definition

R M M forms a family with several branches in Arabic.

In the first branch, رَمَّ الشيءُ concerns decay, deterioration, and loss of strength.

Ramīm describes bone, vegetation, or matter that has decayed and deteriorated.

In the second, رَمَّ الشيءَ concerns repairing something damaged or partly disjointed.

Other recorded branches concern remaining silent, moving the mouth to speak, and certain uses involving eating vegetation.

An early lexical classification identifies four principles for this family: two concerning repair and deterioration, and two concerning silence and mouth movement.

Its meanings should not therefore be forced into a single image such as “restoration” or “falling apart.”

Early linguistic evidence also confirms this range.

In Aws ibn Ḥajar’s poetry, taramrama concerns moving the mouth to speak.

In Dhū al-Rummah’s poetry, rummah denotes worn rope.

Rimmah for decayed bone and ramma for repairing a house or rope are also recorded lexical uses.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times in the form ramīm.

In Yā Sīn 36:78, ramīm describes decayed bones. The questioner uses their decay to dismiss revival, but the Qur’anic answer rejects his conclusion by recalling the first creation.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42, ka-al-ramīm appears in a comparison involving a destructive wind. It describes deterioration and disintegration without being limited to bones.

Both verses preserve the shared sense of decay and deterioration, while differing in referent and function.

Final definition of R M M:

This word family has several distinct branches:

1. Decay and deterioration: Loss of strength and integrity until something is decayed, brittle, or disintegrated.

2. Repair and restoration: Restoring or setting in order something damaged or partly disjointed.

3. Silence and mouth movement: Uses concerning remaining silent or moving the mouth for speech.

4. Further applications: Worn rope, taking vegetation with the lips, and certain idiomatic forms.

These branches should not be derived from one another without historical evidence.

Only the first branch occurs in the Qur’an.

Ramīm means decayed and deteriorated: something that has lost its strength and former condition.

The word itself means neither absolute annihilation of matter nor repair and revival.

In Yā Sīn 36:78, bone decay is the subject of the question; the possibility of revival is understood from the Qur’an’s answer.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42, deterioration and disintegration convey the wind’s destruction.

For Forqan’s translation and research, decay, crumbling, complete destruction, repair, and revival should therefore be distinguished.

These concepts may be related in a discussion without having identical lexical meanings.

The most suitable Qur’anic equivalent is “decayed and deteriorated.” Where the context depicts severe destruction, “disintegrated and fallen apart” can also be used in explanation.
