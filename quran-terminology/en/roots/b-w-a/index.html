The Qur’anic Root ب و أ

From returning and settling to providing a place and incurring consequences

The Qur’anic root ب و أ appears in two main branches. One concerns settling, choosing a place, and providing a place for someone else, represented by بَوَّأَ، تَبَوَّأَ and مُبَوَّأ. In the other, the simple verb بَاءَ accompanies wrath, displeasure, or sin: a person incurs something through their actions, or returns bearing it.

Early Arabic knew a wider range of uses than those found in the Qur’an. باءة and مباءة denoted a dwelling or place of settlement. Another branch, بواء, expressed equality or equivalence. This latter branch has no Qur’anic occurrence.

The main Qur’anic forms are the simple verb بَاءَ / يَبُوءُ, Form II بَوَّأَ, Form V تَبَوَّأَ, and the nominal form مُبَوَّأ. Every Qur’anic occurrence of the simple verb involves something unwelcome, such as wrath, displeasure, or sin. The derived forms have no such fixed negative association: they describe earthly settlements, battle positions, houses, and places in the Garden. There are seventeen occurrences in all: six in Form I, six in Form II, four in Form V, and one of mubawwaʾ.

This family is best understood without forcing all its uses into a single artificial definition. Two clear Qur’anic centres emerge: returning with or incurring a consequence, and occupying or assigning a place. Their proximity is intelligible through returning to a place and settling there, but that resemblance alone cannot establish the precise historical relationship.

Bāʾah and mabāʾah: dwellings and places of settlement in early Arabic

In early Arabic, الباءة and المباءة named a dwelling or a place where people alighted and settled. Mabāʾah could also describe where livestock slept or rested; returning camels to their resting place was expressed through the same family.

This supplies a concrete image: a living being returns from movement, reaches its place, and settles there. It is a way of relating the uses, rather than a requirement that every occurrence describe a preceding journey.

Ibn Fāris and Ibn Manẓūr transmit the following verse from the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd:

طَيِّبُو الْبَاءَةِ سَهْلٌ وَلَهُمْ
سُبُلٌ إِنْ شِئْتَ فِي وَحْشٍ وَعِرْ

“Their dwelling is pleasant and accessible; they have paths, should you seek them, even through desolate, rugged country.”

Here الباءة is the people’s dwelling or place of settlement. The transmitted verse supports this use in poetry attributed to a pre-Islamic poet. A note in Lisān al-ʿArab records a variant, طيّب, beside the plural طيّبو used here; the central evidence is the sense of bāʾah as a dwelling.

Early lexical sources also explain تبوأ منزلا as choosing a place and settling in it, and بوأه منزلا as providing someone with a place and lodging them there.

This early lexical field corresponds closely to a large part of the Qur’anic usage.

Another early branch: bawāʾ as equivalence

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets and classical lexicons also preserve البواء, meaning equality or equivalence, particularly in blood vengeance and retaliation.

A verse attributed to Muhalhil ibn Rabīʿah says:

فَقُلْتُ لَهُ بُؤْ بِامْرِئٍ لَسْتَ مِثْلَهُ
وَإِنْ كُنْتَ قُنْعَانًا لِمَنْ يَطْلُبُ الدِّمَا

“I told him: be slain in requital for a man whose equal you are not, even if you are an acceptable match for one seeking blood.”

Here بؤ بامرئ concerns setting one person against another as an equivalent in retaliation. The poet invokes that equivalence while denying that the person slain truly matches the man being avenged.

Lexicographers likewise record bawāʾ for equality and parity. Someone’s blood could be said to be bawāʾ for another’s: equivalent for purposes of retaliation.

This use matters for the history of the family, but it does not occur in the Qur’an. Equality should therefore not be imported into Qur’anic bāʾa, bawwaʾa, or tabawwaʾa merely to construct an all-encompassing definition.

Ibn Fāris explicitly distinguishes two semantic foundations here: return and equivalence. His lexical analysis does not itself prove two historically independent origins, but it cautions against deriving every branch from a single image.

بَوَّأَ: providing a place for someone else

In the Qur’an, Form II generally presents the perspective of someone placing another person somewhere or providing a suitable place for them.

Āl ʿImrān says:

وَإِذْ غَدَوْتَ مِنْ أَهْلِكَ تُبَوِّئُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ مَقَاعِدَ لِلْقِتَالِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

“When you left your household early in the morning, assigning the believers positions for battle; The God is hearing and knowing.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:121

تُبَوِّئُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ مَقَاعِدَ لِلْقِتَالِ shows that bawwaʾa is not restricted to providing housing or permanent settlement. Here battle positions are assigned, and people are placed in their appropriate stations.

“Positioning” describes the action well; a natural translation is “assigning the believers positions for battle.”

Al-Aʿrāf says of Thamūd:

وَاذْكُرُوا إِذْ جَعَلَكُمْ خُلَفَاءَ مِنْ بَعْدِ عَادٍ وَبَوَّأَكُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ تَتَّخِذُونَ مِنْ سُهُولِهَا قُصُورًا وَتَنْحِتُونَ الْجِبَالَ بُيُوتًا ۖ فَاذْكُرُوا آلَاءَ اللَّهِ وَلَا تَعْثَوْا فِي الْأَرْضِ مُفْسِدِينَ

“Remember when He made you successors after ʿĀd and settled you in the land: you build palaces on its plains and carve the mountains into houses. Remember the favours of The God, and do not spread corruption in the land.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74

Here, being given a place involves the opportunity to settle and make use of the land.

Al-Hajj also says:

وَإِذْ بَوَّأْنَا لِإِبْرَاهِيمَ مَكَانَ الْبَيْتِ أَنْ لَا تُشْرِكْ بِي شَيْئًا وَطَهِّرْ بَيْتِيَ لِلطَّائِفِينَ وَالْقَائِمِينَ وَالرُّكَّعِ السُّجُودِ

“When We assigned Abraham the site of the House: associate nothing with Me, and purify My House for those who circle it, those who stand, and those who bow and prostrate.”

Al-Hajj 22:26

بَوَّأْنَا لِإِبْرَاهِيمَ مَكَانَ الْبَيْتِ concerns identifying and making available the site of the House.

The shared capacity of Form II is thus assigning or providing a position or place for someone, and establishing their relation to it.

مُبَوَّأَ صِدْقٍ: the place itself

Yunus places a verb and a nominal form from this family together:

وَلَقَدْ بَوَّأْنَا بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ مُبَوَّأَ صِدْقٍ وَرَزَقْنَاهُمْ مِنَ الطَّيِّبَاتِ فَمَا اخْتَلَفُوا حَتَّىٰ جَاءَهُمُ الْعِلْمُ ۚ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ يَقْضِي بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فِيمَا كَانُوا فِيهِ يَخْتَلِفُونَ

“We settled the Children of Israel in a goodly dwelling and provided them with wholesome things. They did not differ until knowledge had come to them. Your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning their disagreements.”

Yunus 10:93

In this context, مُبَوَّأ denotes a dwelling or place of settlement. Its form coincides with the Form II passive participle; a place reading and a verbal-noun reading have both been proposed. “A dwelling” is the reading followed here.

In مُبَوَّأَ صِدْقٍ, ṣidq is grammatically the second member of a construct phrase, not an adjectival modifier. Semantically it qualifies the dwelling as good or worthy, hence “a goodly dwelling.” The wider range of ṣidq requires its own lexical investigation; mubawwaʾ here identifies where the group has been settled.

This occurrence also shows that the family is not confined to an individual house. A place of settlement may be an extensive territory or environment supporting a community.

تَبَوَّأَ: choosing and occupying a place

Form V presents the perspective of someone choosing a place or settling in it.

Yunus says:

وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ وَأَخِيهِ أَنْ تَبَوَّآ لِقَوْمِكُمَا بِمِصْرَ بُيُوتًا وَاجْعَلُوا بُيُوتَكُمْ قِبْلَةً وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ ۗ وَبَشِّرِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

“We revealed to Moses and his brother: take houses for your people in Egypt, make your houses a qiblah, establish Salat, and give good news to the believers.”

Yunus 10:87

تَبَوَّآ occurs here with بيوتا, concerning the choice and provision of houses in which the people can settle.

Of Joseph, the Qur’an says:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ مَكَّنَّا لِيُوسُفَ فِي الْأَرْضِ يَتَبَوَّأُ مِنْهَا حَيْثُ يَشَاءُ ۚ نُصِيبُ بِرَحْمَتِنَا مَنْ نَشَاءُ وَلَا نُضِيعُ أَجْرَ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

“Thus We established Joseph in the land, to settle wherever he wished. We bestow Our mercy on whom We will, and We do not let the reward of those who do good be lost.”

Yusuf 12:56

The people of the Garden say:

وَقَالُوا الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي صَدَقَنَا وَعْدَهُ وَأَوْرَثَنَا الْأَرْضَ نَتَبَوَّأُ مِنَ الْجَنَّةِ حَيْثُ نَشَاءُ ۖ فَنِعْمَ أَجْرُ الْعَامِلِينَ

“They said: Praise belongs to The God, who fulfilled His promise to us and made us inherit the land; we settle wherever we wish in the Garden. How excellent is the reward of those who act.”

Al-Zumar 39:74

In these occurrences, tabawwaʾa emphasizes choosing and occupying a place.

Form V by itself does not encode permanent residence or eternal establishment. Context determines the duration and character of the settlement.

تَبَوَّءُوا الدَّارَ وَالْإِيمَانَ: place and faith in one construction

Al-Hashr contains a particularly revealing construction:

وَالَّذِينَ تَبَوَّءُوا الدَّارَ وَالْإِيمَانَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ يُحِبُّونَ مَنْ هَاجَرَ إِلَيْهِمْ وَلَا يَجِدُونَ فِي صُدُورِهِمْ حَاجَةً مِمَّا أُوتُوا وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ ۚ وَمَنْ يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

“Those who had settled in the dwelling and become established in faith before their arrival love those who migrated to them. They find in their hearts no desire for what the migrants were given, and prefer them to themselves even when they are in need. Those protected from their own avarice are the successful.”

Al-Hashr 59:9

الدار denotes a place, whereas الإيمان is not a physical location. Yet both follow تَبَوَّءُوا in the sentence.

Grammarians offer several analyses. One gives the verb the broader sense of remaining attached or committed: they held to the dwelling and to faith. Another treats faith figuratively as a place in which one settles. A third supplies an omitted verb for faith, such as “embraced” or “believed in”: they settled in the dwelling and embraced faith. Under that analysis, tabawwaʾū itself need not acquire a new non-spatial meaning.

These analyses converge on the community’s established attachment to faith, but they do not all extend the lexical meaning of the verb. “Before them” should also not automatically mean that the Helpers believed before all the Emigrants; it can refer to their settlement and faith before the migrants’ arrival.

Nor does the construction define faith literally as “the soul’s house” or a “spiritual habitat.” These may be literary images, but they go beyond what the expression establishes lexically.

More precisely, a figurative reading extends the image of settlement to their relation with faith; an ellipsis reading expresses that relation through an understood second verb.

The simple verb بَاءَ: returning with or incurring something

The simple verb follows a different pattern in the Qur’an. All six occurrences involve an adverse consequence: five concern wrath or displeasure and one concerns sin.

Al-Baqarah says:

وَضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الذِّلَّةُ وَالْمَسْكَنَةُ وَبَاءُوا بِغَضَبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ ۗ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ كَانُوا يَكْفُرُونَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَيَقْتُلُونَ النَّبِيِّينَ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ ۗ ذَٰلِكَ بِمَا عَصَوْا وَكَانُوا يَعْتَدُونَ

“Humiliation and destitution were imposed upon them, and they returned bearing wrath from The God. That was because they rejected the signs of The God and killed the prophets without right; that was because they disobeyed and continued to transgress.”

Al-Baqarah 2:61, excerpt

Āl ʿImrān says:

أَفَمَنِ اتَّبَعَ رِضْوَانَ اللَّهِ كَمَنْ بَاءَ بِسَخَطٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَمَأْوَاهُ جَهَنَّمُ ۚ وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

“Is one who follows the approval of The God like one who incurs displeasure from The God and whose refuge is Hell? What an evil destination.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:162

Arabic lexicons record “return” for باء إلى الشيء. For باء بذنبه and similar constructions they give explanations including returning with something, bearing it, incurring it, or acknowledging it.

In باء بغضب, wrath is therefore not someone’s home or spiritual dwelling. Rather, through their actions, the person returns bearing wrath, and incurs that consequence.

In English, “incurred wrath” or “brought wrath upon themselves” is generally more natural than “returned with wrath.” The latter makes the underlying Arabic image more visible: a return with a consequence attached to the person.

تَبُوءَ بِإِثْمِي وَإِثْمِكَ: a similar-looking form from a different verb pattern

In the account of Adam’s two sons:

إِنِّي أُرِيدُ أَنْ تَبُوءَ بِإِثْمِي وَإِثْمِكَ فَتَكُونَ مِنْ أَصْحَابِ النَّارِ ۚ وَذَٰلِكَ جَزَاءُ الظَّالِمِينَ

“I want you to bear the sin [of killing me] and your own sin, and thus become one of the people of the Fire; that is the recompense of wrongdoers.”

Al-Maʾidah 5:29

تَبُوءَ here belongs to the simple verb بَاءَ يَبُوءُ. It is not تَبَوَّأَ, “to settle.”

Without vowel marks, the similarity can obscure this distinction. تَبَوَّآ in Yunus 10:87, with a doubled wāw, concerns taking houses; تَبُوءَ in Al-Maʾidah 5:29 belongs to Form I and concerns sin incurred by the person.

“Bear the sin” is a natural rendering; “return with the sin” exposes the image but is less idiomatic. The bracketed words in the translation follow the contextual interpretation, transmitted by al-Ṭabarī and others, of إثمي as the sin of killing the speaker. This is an explanation of the possessive construction in context, not an additional lexical meaning of bāʾa, and the wording should not silently imply a transfer of all the victim’s sins.

The negative pattern of Form I in the Qur’an

A notable pattern is that every Qur’anic occurrence of the simple verb bāʾa/yabūʾu accompanies an adverse consequence.

The Qur’an speaks of غضب من الله، غضب على غضب، سخط من الله and إثم. It offers no occurrence in which someone uses bāʾa with a blessing, mercy, or reward.

This pattern deserves to be recorded, while remaining limited to the evidence it describes.

It does not establish that bāʾa is inherently negative throughout Arabic. Its old sense of returning is not itself negative, and its uses outside the Qur’an are not confined to a single moral association.

The more precise statement is:

In Qur’anic usage, Form I consistently accompanies an unwelcome consequence. That negative association comes from the attested contexts and the expressions linked to the verb through bi-, rather than being an established intrinsic meaning of the root throughout Arabic.

This pattern should not be transferred to the derived forms. بَوَّأَ and تَبَوَّأَ also describe good dwellings, ordinary positions, and the Garden.

Do returning and settling share a single core?

The two branches have a real semantic proximity.

A مباءة is a place where a person or animal settles, and to which they may return. Arabic records expressions for bringing animals back to their mabāʾah.

Meanwhile, باء إلى الشيء itself means returning to something.

A simple image connects these uses: movement ends, someone reaches a point, and takes up a position in relation to it.

This makes the proximity of return and settlement intelligible. It should not be converted into a philosophical definition such as “reaching a point of support and existential fixity.” Such language adds no precision to the evidence and risks artificially homogenizing the uses.

Ibn Fāris’s analysis distinguishes the branch of equivalence from that of return. This is a distinction between lexical foundations in his method, not a final historical reconstruction.

The final definition should therefore retain clear, distinct branches, rather than erase actual differences for the sake of a single elegant sentence.

ب و أ and ن ز ل: alighting and taking a place

Both families can concern coming to occupy a location, but their perspectives are not identical.

In ن ز ل, movement towards a place, alighting, and lodging are prominent. This need not mean a vertical fall in every use: nazala can also describe arriving to stay, being a guest, or coming to a location.

In the settlement branch of ب و أ, the emphasis falls more on the place and occupying or assigning it.

Āl ʿImrān 3:121 illustrates the difference. تُبَوِّئُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ مَقَاعِدَ لِلْقِتَالِ is not about bringing the believers down from above, but about assigning and arranging their battle positions.

Yunus 10:87 concerns taking houses, while in Yusuf 12:56 Joseph can settle wherever he wishes in the land.

As a tendency, ن ز ل highlights arrival and alighting at a place, while the settlement branch of ب و أ highlights choosing, occupying, or providing a place. This is a contextual tendency, not an exceptionless rule governing all Arabic usage.

بيئة: an old word with a broader modern use

The modern word بيئة belongs to this family but does not occur in the Qur’an.

In classical lexicons, bīʾah concerns the manner or condition of settlement and the place of residence. Its modern use for “environment,” particularly the natural environment, broadens that field.

The modern ecological meaning should therefore not be read back into Qur’anic bawwaʾa or tabawwaʾa. The lexical relationship is real, but the words’ historical uses are not identical.

Nor is ب و أ the same root as و ب أ in وباء, “epidemic.” The consonants occur in a different order, and the two families require separate investigation.

Translation proposals for Forqan

Form II should be translated according to the kind of place involved. بَوَّأَ may mean “settled,” “placed,” “provided a dwelling for,” or “assigned a position to.” In Āl ʿImrān 3:121, “assigning the believers positions for battle” is more natural than “housing the believers.”

Depending on context, تَبَوَّأَ can be “settled,” “took a place,” or “established oneself.” In Al-Hashr 59:9, “lived in faith” may sound excessively physical. “Had settled in the dwelling and become established in faith” conveys the relationship more clearly, while remaining an interpretive rendering of the construction.

مُبَوَّأ can be rendered “a dwelling” or “a place of settlement,” following the contextual reading adopted here.

For simple بَاءَ بِـ, “returned with” preserves the lexical image but is not always idiomatic. With wrath, “incurred wrath” or “brought wrath upon themselves” works well; in Al-Maʾidah 5:29, “bear the sin” is more natural.

Since bawāʾ in the sense of equivalence does not occur in the Qur’an, that meaning should not be inserted into the translation of any of these Qur’anic forms.

Summary

Classical Arabic and poetry transmitted from pre-Islamic poets connect ب و أ with returning, dwelling, and settlement. Bāʾah and mabāʾah designate places where people or livestock settle; the verse transmitted from Ṭarafah illustrates the dwelling sense, with the textual variant noted above.

Early Arabic also knew بواء as equivalence or parity. The verse attributed to Muhalhil illustrates this in blood vengeance. Since that sense does not occur in the Qur’an, it should be retained as a distinct historical branch, rather than incorporated into a definition of the Qur’anic uses.

In the Qur’an, بَوَّأَ generally concerns placing someone or providing them with a place. This may be a battle position, land, a dwelling, the site of the House, or chambers in the Garden. مُبَوَّأ here denotes the dwelling itself.

تَبَوَّأَ presents the perspective of someone choosing or occupying a place. Al-Hashr 59:9 brings الإيمان alongside الدار. A figurative reading extends settlement to faith; another grammatical analysis supplies a separate verb for embracing faith. Neither makes “faith is a house or spiritual habitat” a lexical definition of the verse.

The simple verb بَاءَ always accompanies an adverse consequence in the Qur’an: wrath, displeasure, or sin. A person returns with, or incurs, that consequence through their actions. This negative pattern holds across all Qur’anic Form I occurrences, but should not be extended to Arabic as a whole or to the root’s other verb patterns.

Returning and settling are semantically close: a dwelling is somewhere a person reaches, returns to, and occupies. This relationship helps explain the Qur’anic branches without establishing a single philosophical essence.

A concise definition should preserve both Qur’anic branches:

ب و أ: in one branch, returning and carrying or incurring something as a consequence of one’s actions; the Qur’an uses this construction with wrath, displeasure, and sin. In the other, occupying a position, choosing a place, or providing a place for someone else. Mubawwaʾ here denotes the dwelling. Early Arabic also had a branch expressing equivalence or parity, which does not occur in the Qur’an.
