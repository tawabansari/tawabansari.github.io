From swimming and movement in water to the year and the passage of time: ʿām and its distinction from sanah and ḥawl

The Arabic root ʿ W M has two recognized branches: swimming, floating, and moving through water; and ʿām, a year, a complete annual period. Arabic dictionaries place both under one root family, but this classification alone does not establish that the word for a year developed from swimming.

In the first branch, عَامَ يَعُومُ عَوْمًا means to swim or move through water. It describes people, vessels, and floating creatures; extended uses also describe the flowing, continuous movement of camels, horses, and even stars.

In the second branch, عَام names the unit of time called a year. Its plural is أَعْوَام and its dual forms are عَامَانِ and عَامَيْنِ. It serves to count durations, date events, organize social and agricultural affairs, and describe periods of human life.

The Quran uses only the noun عَام in singular and dual forms. Swimming verbs such as عَامَ and يَعُومُ, and the plural أَعْوَام, do not occur in it.

All nine Quranic occurrences belong to the meaning year. Their contexts nevertheless vary: a period in a person's existence, the timing of a command, a period of testing, a year of abundance and relief, or part of the count of a long lifetime.

An important question is how this word differs from سَنَة and حَوْل. Some interpretations associate sanah with hardship and ʿām with ease. The Quran itself shows that this cannot be a fixed definition applying to every occurrence.

1. Historical meaning and early Arabic usage

ʿĀm: a complete annual period

In early Arabic, الْعَامُ means a year, an annual period. The word alone does not specify the calendar; a lunar year does not coincide with a complete solar cycle of the seasons.

Early lexical definitions explain ʿām through ḥawl and describe it as including winter and summer. This presents a familiar annual span rather than a single season or a few months. It does not establish that every use of ʿām necessarily denotes a solar year.

In pre-Islamic Arab life, passing years were associated with changing seasons, the movement of herds, rainfall and drought, plant growth, ripening crops, and travel. The year measured elapsed time and intervals between events.

These natural and social associations should not become components of the lexical definition. ʿĀm does not itself mean rain, crops, migration, or summer; it names the period in which such events may occur.

A poetic witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

أَبَتْ أَجَأُ أَنْ تُسْلِمَ الْعَامَ جَارَهَا
فَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيَنْهَضْ لَهَا مِنْ مُقَاتِلِ

abat ajaʾu an tuslima al-ʿāma jārahā / fa-man shāʾa fa-l-yanhaḍ lahā min muqātil

“Aja refused to surrender its neighbor this year; let whoever wishes rise to fight against it.”

Aja is a mountain in the territory of the tribe of Ṭayyiʾ. The poet speaks of its inhabitants and their protection of a neighbor.

Here الْعَامَ means “this year,” identifying the particular period in which the people's conduct is described.

The meaning is clear: a particular year, with no reference to swimming, circulating water, or abundant crops.

Early literary sources attribute the poem to Imruʾ al-Qays, although some words have variant readings. Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet is significant evidence for the age of ʿām, but the date of this particular verse cannot be settled independently of the poem's transmission and textual history.

ʿĀm in poetry attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

A poem attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

كَمْ لِلْمَنَازِلِ مِنْ عَامٍ وَمِنْ زَمَنٍ
لِآلِ أَسْمَاءَ بِالْقُفَّيْنِ فَالرُّكُنِ

kam li-l-manāzili min ʿāmin wa-min zamanin / li-āli asmāʾa bi-l-quffayni fa-l-rukun

“How many years and stretches of time have passed over the dwellings of Asma's people at al-Quffayn and al-Rukun!”

In this verse, ʿām appears alongside zaman.

The poet describes time passing over dwellings that retain memories and traces of former life.

ʿĀm is a specific unit of time, whereas zaman has a broader range and may denote a duration or age without specifying an exact unit.

This illustrates the use of ʿām to count and conceive of passing years in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Neither witness restricts ʿām to a year of ease or abundance.

ʿAwm: swimming and floating

The other branch concerns swimming and movement through water.

عَامَ فِي الْمَاءِ means “he swam in the water.”

عَوْم names the act of swimming; عَائِم describes someone or something swimming or floating in water.

عَوَّام also describes a skilled swimmer and, in some uses, a horse whose flowing movement resembles swimming.

Classical Arabic also describes a vessel's movement on water with this family. عَامَتِ السَّفِينَةُ means that the vessel moved over the water or floated.

A rajaz line preserved in dictionaries says:

وَهُنَّ بِالدَّوِّ يَعُمْنَ عَوْمًا

wa-hunna bi-l-dawwi yaʿumna ʿawman

“And those she-camels move through the desert as though swimming.”

The camels' movement through the desert is likened to swimming. The image extends from water to flowing, continuous movement on land.

The line's precise date is unknown; without independent evidence it should not be classified as pre-Quranic Arabic.

Dictionaries sometimes describe the movement of stars with ʿawm as well. Floating can thus provide an image for movement across an extensive space.

These witnesses connect the ʿawm branch with floating and movement. They do not mean that everything described by this verb is physically floating in a liquid. The descriptions of camels and horses are clearly figurative.

Did ʿām, “year,” develop from swimming?

Since ʿām and ʿawm are classified under one root, one might imagine a connection between the annual cycle and continuous or floating movement.

A year passes continuously, followed by another period when it ends. Movement in water can likewise suggest a continuous flow.

That resemblance does not establish the word's historical origin.

Claiming that early Arabs named the year ʿām because time revolves or floats requires independent evidence for that relationship in linguistic history.

The witnesses examined do not conclusively establish such a derivation.

A historical definition should therefore distinguish the two branches: ʿām names a complete year, while ʿawm means swimming or floating.

Both belong to the Arabic root family ʿ W M, but their relative antiquity and a shared semantic origin remain uncertain.

2. Morphological forms and non-Quranic uses

ʿĀm, meaning year, is a masculine noun.

Its singular is عَامٌ and its established plural أَعْوَام.

The nominative dual is عَامَانِ; the accusative and genitive dual is عَامَيْنِ.

The Quran contains عَامٍ, عَامًا, عَامٌ, عَامِهِمْ, and عَامَيْنِ. These are inflected and grammatical forms of one noun, not derivatives with different root meanings.

عَامِهِمْ combines ʿām with the attached pronoun hum: “their year.”

عَامَيْنِ denotes two years.

Classical Arabic records further forms absent from the Quran.

عَاوَمَ can concern an annual transaction or agreement; مُعَاوَمَة is used for a transaction based on a year or future years.

In agriculture, عَاوَمَتِ النَّخْلَةُ describes a date palm bearing fruit one year and not the next.

عَوَّمَ الْكَرْمُ is recorded for a vine yielding abundantly one year and little the next.

These uses can readily be explained through ʿām, year: they concern annual cycles and changes in produce or agreements from year to year.

By contrast, عَوَّمَ السَّفِينَةَ concerns making a vessel float and belongs to the floating branch.

These related forms show why membership in a root family is insufficient: each word's object and context must establish its meaning.

None of these verbs occurs in the Quran. Its use of this root centers on the noun ʿām.

3. ʿĀm in the Quran: time, society, and human life

Al-Baqarah 2:259: a hundred years in an account of death and revival

Al-Baqarah 2:259 says:

فَأَمَاتَهُ اللَّهُ مِائَةَ عَامٍ ثُمَّ بَعَثَهُ

fa-amātahu allāhu miʾata ʿāmin thumma baʿathah

Al-Baqarah 2:259

“Then The God caused him to be dead for a hundred years, then raised him.”

Later, when the person answers a question about his stay, he is told:

قَالَ بَلْ لَبِثْتَ مِائَةَ عَامٍ

qāla bal labithta miʾata ʿām

Al-Baqarah 2:259

“He said: Rather, you have remained a hundred years.”

ʿĀm occurs twice in this verse, meaning year on both occasions.

مِائَةَ عَامٍ specifies a hundred years: miʾah supplies the quantity and ʿām the unit.

The account concerns someone passing a ruined settlement and asking how it will be brought to life after its death.

Raised after a hundred years, he supposes he has remained a day or part of a day.

The verse distinguishes the stated duration from the person's perception of elapsed time.

ʿĀm here means neither ease nor hardship. It specifies the time unit.

Nor does this use imply that ʿām always accompanies ordinary life and continuing human activity: these hundred years are described in relation to death and revival.

The verbs and the whole account convey death, remaining, and revival; ʿām itself does not.

Al-Tawbah 9:28: a particular year in a command

Al-Tawbah 9:28 says:

فَلَا يَقْرَبُوا الْمَسْجِدَ الْحَرَامَ بَعْدَ عَامِهِمْ هَٰذَا

fa-lā yaqrabū al-masjida al-ḥarāma baʿda ʿāmihim hādhā

Al-Tawbah 9:28

“So let them not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year of theirs.”

عَامِهِمْ refers to their year, which هَٰذَا identifies more specifically.

The year is a particular period used to state the command's temporal boundary.

ʿĀm itself conveys nothing independently about the Sacred Mosque, the associators, or the rule concerning approach. Other parts of the verse supply those meanings.

بَعْدَ also indicates that the command concerns the period after this year has passed.

“After this year of theirs” is a temporal expression; ʿām is not the name of a special religious period separate from a year.

Al-Tawbah 9:37: lawful one year and forbidden another

Al-Tawbah 9:37 says:

يُحِلُّونَهُ عَامًا وَيُحَرِّمُونَهُ عَامًا

yuḥillūnahu ʿāman wa-yuḥarrimūnahu ʿāman

Al-Tawbah 9:37

“They deem it lawful one year and forbidden another year.”

The verse concerns nasīʾ and altering the inviolability of months.

عَامًا means year in both clauses.

Repeating the word in contrasting clauses shows a change in their conduct between different years.

They treat the same matter as permissible in one year and forbidden in another.

This use is especially instructive: the year simply measures the temporal difference between two actions.

The first year cannot be called a year of ease merely because it accompanies يُحِلُّونَهُ, nor the second a year of hardship because it accompanies يُحَرِّمُونَهُ.

Deeming something lawful or forbidden is what people do in those years, not what the word year means.

ʿĀm can thus describe changes in decisions and rulings across successive periods without carrying a fixed moral value.

Al-Tawbah 9:126: testing every year

Al-Tawbah 9:126 says:

أَوَلَا يَرَوْنَ أَنَّهُمْ يُفْتَنُونَ فِي كُلِّ عَامٍ مَرَّةً أَوْ مَرَّتَيْنِ ثُمَّ لَا يَتُوبُونَ وَلَا هُمْ يَذَّكَّرُونَ

a-wa-lā yarawna annahum yuftanūna fī kulli ʿāmin marratan aw marratayni thumma lā yatūbūna wa-lā hum yadhdhakkarūn

Al-Tawbah 9:126

“Do they not see that they are tested once or twice every year, yet they neither turn back nor take heed?”

فِي كُلِّ عَامٍ means “in every year.”

The year measures the recurrence of an event.

مَرَّةً أَوْ مَرَّتَيْنِ specifies its frequency.

The verse describes a test occurring once or twice each year.

This matters particularly when comparing ʿām and sanah. Anyone claiming that ʿām always denotes prosperity and ease must account for its use in a verse about repeated tests and a failure to repent or take heed.

An event's being a test does not by itself prove that the entire year was painful or difficult. Yet the verse shows that ʿām is not lexically restricted to ease.

The year supplies the temporal setting; يُفْتَنُونَ identifies the event.

Yūsuf 12:49: a year in which people receive relief

After describing seven years of cultivation and seven difficult years, the surah of Joseph says:

ثُمَّ يَأْتِي مِنْ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ عَامٌ فِيهِ يُغَاثُ النَّاسُ وَفِيهِ يَعْصِرُونَ

thumma yaʾtī min baʿdi dhālika ʿāmun fīhi yughāthu al-nāsu wa-fīhi yaʿṣirūn

Yūsuf 12:49

“Then after that a year will come in which people are given relief and in which they press.”

Yūsuf 12:49

عَامٌ denotes the year following the period of hardship.

فِيهِ يُغَاثُ النَّاسُ describes relief and assistance reaching people that year.

وَفِيهِ يَعْصِرُونَ describes pressing and extracting produce. In the agricultural context, this fits the renewed possibility of benefiting from crops.

This is one of the Quran's clearest examples of a year of relief and abundance.

Nevertheless, the meaning of ʿām must be distinguished from the description of that year.

ʿĀm means year; the following phrases specify what happens in it.

In this verse, the context and the clause about relief explicitly describe the year. Their presence alone neither proves nor disproves that the word has a particular connotation; establishing its general meaning requires examining its other uses as well.

The choice of ʿām here may fit recognized tendencies in Arabic usage, but a contextually appropriate choice does not establish an absolute rule for every verse.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14: a thousand years less fifty years

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14 says:

وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا نُوحًا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ فَلَبِثَ فِيهِمْ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ إِلَّا خَمْسِينَ عَامًا فَأَخَذَهُمُ الطُّوفَانُ وَهُمْ ظَالِمُونَ

wa-la-qad arsalnā nūḥan ilā qawmihi fa-labitha fīhim alfa sanatin illā khamsīna ʿāman fa-akhadhahumu al-ṭūfānu wa-hum ẓālimūn

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14

“We certainly sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them a thousand years less fifty years; then the flood overtook them while they were wrongdoers.”

This is a particularly important comparison of ʿām and sanah.

One sentence contains both names for a year: أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ and خَمْسِينَ عَامًا.

أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ إِلَّا خَمْسِينَ عَامًا subtracts fifty years from a thousand. Its numerical result is nine hundred and fifty years.

If ʿām and sanah denoted different-sized units, this straightforward subtraction would require an explanation of their ratio.

The verse accordingly shows that both can express a comparable temporal unit.

Some have inferred from the two different forms that Noah experienced nine hundred and fifty years of hardship and fifty years of ease.

That conclusion does not follow from the sentence's construction.

The verse does not call the subtracted fifty years a period of ease, nor place them at the beginning or end of Noah's life.

The expression concerns his stay among his people.

The choice of two words invites linguistic and rhetorical reflection. Turning sanah into hardship and ʿām into ease without independent contextual support adds something to the text.

The basic translation should preserve the numerical relationship.

Luqmān 31:14: two years in connection with weaning

Luqmān 31:14 says:

وَوَصَّيْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ بِوَالِدَيْهِ حَمَلَتْهُ أُمُّهُ وَهْنًا عَلَىٰ وَهْنٍ وَفِصَالُهُ فِي عَامَيْنِ أَنِ اشْكُرْ لِي وَلِوَالِدَيْكَ إِلَيَّ الْمَصِيرُ

wa-waṣṣaynā al-insāna bi-wālidayhi ḥamalathu ummuhu wahnan ʿalā wahnin wa-fiṣāluhu fī ʿāmayni ani shkur lī wa-li-wālidayka ilayya al-maṣīr

Luqmān 31:14

“We enjoined the human being concerning his parents: his mother carried him in weakness upon weakness, and his weaning is within two years. Give thanks to Me and to your parents; to Me is the return.”

عَامَيْنِ is the dual of ʿām and means two years.

فِي عَامَيْنِ gives the period associated with fiṣāl.

Fiṣāl belongs to F Ṣ L and here concerns the child's separation from breastfeeding.

ʿĀmayn itself simply specifies the amount of time: two years.

This use directly relates to Al-Baqarah 2:233:

وَالْوَالِدَاتُ يُرْضِعْنَ أَوْلَادَهُنَّ حَوْلَيْنِ كَامِلَيْنِ لِمَنْ أَرَادَ أَنْ يُتِمَّ الرَّضَاعَةَ

wa-l-wālidātu yurḍiʿna awlādahunna ḥawlayni kāmilayni li-man arāda an yutimma al-raḍāʿah

Al-Baqarah 2:233

“Mothers nurse their children for two complete years, for whoever wishes to complete the nursing period.”

This verse uses حَوْلَيْنِ in place of ʿāmayn.

Both expressions refer to two years.

Al-Baqarah adds كَامِلَيْنِ to state explicitly that the two years are complete. لِمَنْ أَرَادَ أَنْ يُتِمَّ الرَّضَاعَةَ also specifies completion of the nursing period.

The difference between the verses follows from their full constructions; ʿām should not be treated as a natural year and ḥawl as a wholly different period.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 gives the duration of pregnancy and weaning in months. The Quran thus measures periods related to a child's development in different time units.

None of those units alone determines every rule or detail concerning breastfeeding.

4. Comparing ʿ W M with S N W and Ḥ W L

ʿĀm and sanah: two names for a year

Both ʿām and sanah can denote a year in Arabic.

Some dictionaries record differing tendencies: sanah occurs prominently in descriptions of drought and hardship, while ʿām can be prominent in contexts of abundance and ease.

Such an observation must be distinguished from an absolute semantic division.

Joseph's story provides a decisive test.

Yūsuf 12:47 says:

قَالَ تَزْرَعُونَ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ دَأَبًا فَمَا حَصَدْتُمْ فَذَرُوهُ فِي سُنْبُلِهِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِمَّا تَأْكُلُونَ

qāla tazraʿūna sabʿa sinīna daʾaban fa-mā ḥaṣadtum fa-dharūhu fī sunbulihi illā qalīlan mimmā taʾkulūn

Yūsuf 12:47

“He said: You will cultivate for seven years continuously; leave what you harvest in its ears, except a little that you eat.”

Here seven years of cultivation and harvesting are expressed with سِنِينَ.

Joseph then describes seven difficult years, followed in Yūsuf 12:49 by a year when people receive relief.

If sanah always meant a year of famine, سَبْعَ سِنِينَ for years of cultivation and harvesting would be difficult to explain.

The Quran also uses sinīn to count ordinary years of life. Yūnus 10:5 concerns knowing the number of years and reckoning time; in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:18 Pharaoh speaks of the years Moses lived among them.

These uses show that hardship is not a fixed component of sanah.

Nor does ʿām always signal ease. Al-Tawbah 9:126 connects it with recurring tests, and Al-Tawbah 9:37 uses it for changes in what people deem lawful or forbidden from year to year.

A defensible distinction is therefore as follows:

Both name the year and can express the same duration. In certain Arabic contexts, one may be more associated with hardship and the other with relief, but that tendency is not an indispensable, permanent definition.

ʿĀm and ḥawl: the year and the turning of a period

حَوْل also denotes an annual period in the Quran.

The Arabic family Ḥ W L concerns turning, change, transfer, and surroundings.

Ḥawl can therefore more readily evoke a completed period and the return of a temporal cycle.

That image does not make ḥawl a necessarily different unit of time from ʿām.

In Al-Baqarah 2:233, حَوْلَيْنِ كَامِلَيْنِ denotes two complete years.

Luqmān 31:14 uses فِي عَامَيْنِ for the period associated with weaning.

The contexts are closely related and both expressions give a duration of two years.

Al-Baqarah 2:240 also uses ḥawl in connection with a bequest and provision for wives.

In Quranic usage, ʿām and ḥawl can consequently overlap in the amount of time they denote.

Their root distinction is that ʿām names an annual period, while other derivatives of Ḥ W L also concern turning and change.

That distinction does not imply a different length of year.

ʿĀm and shahr: year and month

Shahr is a smaller time unit used in the Quran to count months.

Al-Tawbah 9:36 says:

إِنَّ عِدَّةَ الشُّهُورِ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ اثْنَا عَشَرَ شَهْرًا

inna ʿiddata al-shuhūri ʿinda allāhi ithnā ʿashara shahran

Al-Tawbah 9:36

“The number of months with The God is twelve months.”

The verse describes an annual structure of twelve months in the system under discussion.

ʿĀm itself does not require every occurrence to be translated as a particular kind of solar or lunar year.

Where the Quran states a hundred years or two years, the unit's name alone does not specify every detail of calendrical calculation.

Such details require context and independent evidence.

ʿĀm and yawm: year and day

Yawm ordinarily denotes a day, although different Quranic contexts may use it for a period or a particular time.

ʿĀm names an annual period.

Al-Baqarah 2:259 places these units together in one account: the person thinks he has remained a day or part of one, but is told that a hundred years have passed.

The contrast treats day and year as specific, different units.

It does not establish that ʿām is a subjective unit and yawm an objective one, or vice versa.

Both measure time; their differences concern duration and context.

5. Is there a conceptual link between the annual cycle and floating?

The two branches of ʿ W M can invite linguistic reflection.

A swimmer moves through an environment along a course. With passing years, people likewise experience time's continuing movement and the recurrence of annual periods.

An explanatory image must nevertheless be distinguished from historical derivation.

Flow and movement may illustrate a possible relationship between the branches. The image itself does not prove that pre-Quranic Arabs understood ʿām as time floating.

No Quranic occurrence of ʿām means movement over water, floating time, or the physical revolution of celestial bodies.

Every occurrence concerns the year as a time unit.

Waves, flow, or floating used to describe passing years would therefore be explanatory comparisons, not the direct meaning of the Quranic word.

Conversely, swimming should not be excluded from the Arabic root family merely because it does not occur in the Quran. A root study should represent Arabic's actual range while attributing only attested forms to the Quran.

The same distinction applies to modern taʿwīm. Contemporary Arabic uses it for making objects float and, in economics, for floating an exchange rate.

The economic term draws on floating, but it is a later technical use and should not be imposed on ʿām in Quranic verses.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The basic Persian equivalent of Quranic عَام is “year,” as is its English equivalent.

مِائَةَ عَامٍ in Al-Baqarah 2:259: “a hundred years.”

بَعْدَ عَامِهِمْ هَٰذَا in Al-Tawbah 9:28: “after this year of theirs,” or more idiomatically, “after their present year.”

يُحِلُّونَهُ عَامًا وَيُحَرِّمُونَهُ عَامًا in Al-Tawbah 9:37: “They deem it lawful one year and forbidden another year.”

فِي كُلِّ عَامٍ in Al-Tawbah 9:126: “in every year.”

ثُمَّ يَأْتِي مِنْ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ عَامٌ in Yūsuf 12:49: “Then after that a year will come.”

أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ إِلَّا خَمْسِينَ عَامًا in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14: “a thousand years less fifty years.”

وَفِصَالُهُ فِي عَامَيْنِ in Luqmān 31:14: “and his weaning is within two years.”

In all these passages, ʿām or ʿāmayn is translated as year or two years.

In the non-Quranic branch, عَوْم is swimming or floating, and عَائِم means floating or swimming.

عَوَّام, when describing a person, means a skilled swimmer; of a horse, it can describe flowing movement resembling swimming.

مُعَاوَمَة in contractual uses concerns a transaction or relationship based on a year.

These meanings should remain distinct.

In particular, “year of ease,” “year of blessing,” “floating cycle of time,” or “revolving period” should not replace the direct meaning of ʿām unless a modifier or contextual indication in the verse conveys that additional meaning.

7. Summary

The Arabic root ʿ W M has two major branches: swimming and floating, and the name of a complete annual period.

In the first branch, ʿawm concerns movement in water, including a vessel's movement on water. Figurative uses extend the image to the flowing movement of camels, horses, and stars.

In the second branch, ʿām means year. Poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā shows its early use to denote a temporal period and the passing of years.

Arabic dictionaries group both branches under ʿ W M, but their historical relationship is not securely established. Shared letters alone do not derive the name of the year from floating or moving time.

The Quran uses only the temporal branch: ʿām occurs nine times in seven verses, always meaning year.

Al-Baqarah 2:259 gives a hundred years in an account of death and revival. Al-Tawbah 9:28 sets a command's temporal boundary; Al-Tawbah 9:37 connects different years with changing declarations of permissibility and prohibition; Al-Tawbah 9:126 makes each year a setting for testing. Yūsuf 12:49 introduces a year of relief after hardship. Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14 subtracts fifty years from a thousand, and Luqmān 31:14 gives two years in connection with weaning.

This variety shows that ʿām does not itself name a year of ease or hardship. Those qualities come from the context and the year's events.

ʿĀm, sanah, and ḥawl can denote the same unit of time despite different root families and some differing usage tendencies. Those distinctions should not become an asserted difference in year length or an invariable rule about ease and hardship.

A final definition of ʿ W M should preserve both branches: first, swimming and floating movement in water, figuratively extended to flowing movement; second, ʿām, one year, a complete period used to count durations and date events.

In Quranic language, only the second meaning occurs. Accurate understanding depends on identifying the time unit and distinguishing it from events within the year, rather than inventing a hidden meaning for the year itself.
