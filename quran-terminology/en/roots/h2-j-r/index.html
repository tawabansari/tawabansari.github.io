From severing ties and withdrawing to leaving a homeland and moving toward a new life

The Arabic triliteral root ه ج ر concerns leaving, separating, withdrawing, and severing ties. A person may distance themselves from someone, end a relationship, abandon a behavior, or leave their home and homeland. Each involves separation, but its object and cause differ.

In the Qur’an, this root describes withdrawal from people and behaviors, abandonment of the Qur’an, and migration from one land to another. One usage concerns improper or idle speech and requires separate consideration.

Migration is therefore one branch of this word family, rather than the meaning of all its derivatives.

1. The root in early Arabic

In early Arabic, هَجْر stood in contrast to وَصْل. Waṣl denoted joining and maintaining a connection, while hajr denoted breaking it. Separation could concern social contact, conversation, affection, or companionship, without necessarily involving departure from a place.

A poetic witness attributed to Dirham ibn Zayd, a Jewish Arab poet identified among pre-Islamic poets, reads:

«هَجَرْتَ الرَّبَابَ وَجَارَاتِهَا
وَهَمُّكَ بِالشَّوْقِ قَدْ يُطْرَحُ»

hajarta al-rabāba wa-jārātihā / wa-hammuka bi-l-shawqi qad yuṭraḥu

“You have left Al-Rabāb and her neighboring women; perhaps your sorrow of longing may also be cast aside.”

Here hajr means abandoning contact and distancing oneself from the beloved. The poet is not describing migration to another land, but severing or reducing relationships with people.

The poem survives in collections compiled after Islam and is attributed to a pre-Qur’anic poet. It is useful evidence for early usage, although the date of this particular verse cannot be established independently with certainty.

Classical Arabic dictionaries likewise record هَجَرَ الشَّيْءَ as leaving something, and هَجَرَ فُلَانًا as breaking off relations with or withdrawing from someone.

The language also connects leaving a relationship with leaving one’s dwelling. Someone who leaves their original home or community for another place has separated from a spatial and social connection.

However, not every word containing ه ج ر can be confined to this branch.

Lexicons also record هُجْر for offensive, improper, or meaningless speech, and هَاجِرَة for the time of intense midday heat. هِجَار named a device used to bind and restrain a camel’s leg. These uses show distinct branches in classical Arabic; one should not invent a single derivational story for them all.

Later lexicographical records alone do not establish exactly when these latter branches emerged before Islam. Qur’anic research must identify each derivative’s actual meaning in its own construction and context.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

Important Qur’anic forms of this family include:

  • هَجَرَ: the basic verb, with imperatives such as ٱهْجُرْ, ٱهْجُرْنِي, ٱهْجُرْهُمْ, and ٱهْجُرُوهُنَّ, and the imperfect تَهْجُرُونَ.
  • هَجْر: a verbal noun of separation or withdrawal, as in هَجْرًا جَمِيلًا.
  • هَاجَرَ: the Form III verb, with forms such as هَاجَرُوا, هَاجَرْنَ, يُهَاجِرْ, يُهَاجِرُوا, and تُهَاجِرُوا.
  • مُهَاجِر: the Form III active participle, as in مُهَاجِرٌ, مُهَاجِرًا, and الْمُهَاجِرِينَ.
  • مُهَاجِرَات: the feminine plural active participle, referring to women who migrate.
  • مَهْجُور: the passive participle, occurring as مَهْجُورًا.

مُهَاجِرَات belongs to the same morphological family as مُهَاجِر; the difference concerns grammatical number and gender.

هِجْرَة and هِجْرَان are established Arabic nouns, but neither occurs in that form in the Qur’anic text. The Qur’an primarily expresses migration through هَاجَرَ and its derivative مُهَاجِر.

3. Hajr: withdrawing from a person or behavior

Gracious withdrawal

Al-Muzzammil 73:10

«وَاصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَاهْجُرْهُمْ هَجْرًا جَمِيلًا»

wa-ṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūna wa-hjurhum hajran jamīlā

“And be patient with what they say, and withdraw from them graciously.”

وَاهْجُرْهُمْ commands withdrawal from people; هَجْرًا is the same verb’s verbal noun, emphasizing the action. جَمِيلًا specifies the quality of that separation.

Hajr itself denotes distancing; the gracious character of the distancing comes from جَمِيلًا, not the root alone.

The connection with the command to exercise patience also matters. Withdrawal here should not become a pretext for revenge, insults, or harmful retaliation. One can leave a harmful environment or relationship while maintaining dignity and appropriate conduct.

This shows that hajr does not necessarily mean hostility or resentment. Sometimes distancing prevents further conflict.

A demand for distance within a family relationship

Maryam 19:46

«قَالَ أَرَاغِبٌ أَنْتَ عَنْ آلِهَتِي يَا إِبْرَاهِيمُ لَئِنْ لَمْ تَنْتَهِ لَأَرْجُمَنَّكَ وَاهْجُرْنِي مَلِيًّا»

qāla a-rāghibun anta ʿan ālihatī yā ibrāhīmu la-in lam tantahi la-arjumannaka wa-hjurnī maliyyā

“He said: Abraham, are you turning away from my gods? If you do not desist, I will surely stone you. Leave me for a long time.”

وَاهْجُرْنِي commands departure from the speaker, while مَلِيًّا indicates the duration of that separation.

The demand is made in a threatening setting. Hajr here is not merely respectful distance; it can accompany rejection and severed relations.

The contrast with Al-Muzzammil 73:10 shows that the root itself does not determine separation’s moral quality. One verse qualifies it as gracious; the other places it alongside a threat.

Withdrawal in marital relations

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:34, وَاهْجُرُوهُنَّ occurs with فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ.

This phrase limits the action to beds and the relationship associated with them. Here hajr means withdrawal in bed, not necessarily leaving the home, expelling a spouse, or ending the marriage.

The distinction between the root and the qualifying phrase is decisive. Hajr conveys distancing, while فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ specifies its location and setting.

This expression should not be equated with all marital rulings or the other commands in the same verse. Each verb and qualifier requires separate examination.

Withdrawing from rujz

Al-Muddaththir 74:5

«وَالرُّجْزَ فَاهْجُرْ»

wa-l-rujza fa-hjur

“And keep away from defilement.”

The object of hajr here is neither a person nor a place, but الرُّجْزَ.

فَاهْجُرْ still denotes leaving and distancing. What is to be left is supplied by its object.

Hajr can thus describe separation from people or abandonment of a behavior or objectionable thing. Its uses should not all be translated as geographical migration.

4. Hajr in speech and abandonment of the Qur’an

تَهْجُرُونَ: improper speech or turning away?

Al-Muʾminūn 23:67

«مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ بِهِ سَامِرًا تَهْجُرُونَ»

mustakbirīna bihi sāmiran tahjurūn

“Being arrogant on its account, speaking improperly in your evening gatherings.”

This is one of the root’s difficult occurrences.

Classical Arabic also uses هُجْر and related verbs for offensive, idle, delirious, or otherwise improper speech.

The reading تَهْجُرُونَ, with a fatḥah on tāʾ and a ḍammah on jīm, allows two semantic directions: turning away and abandoning, or uttering improper and unfounded speech. The recognized Form IV reading تُهْجِرُونَ, with a ḍammah on tāʾ and a kasrah on jīm, also supports the sense of offensive speech.

The context directly bears on this question. Al-Muʾminūn 23:66 describes people retreating when the verses were recited. The following verse describes their arrogance, evening gatherings, and تَهْجُرُونَ.

Turning away from the verses and speaking improperly about them both contribute to the passage’s meaning. Given سَامِرًا and the alternative reading, “speaking improperly” has substantial support for تَهْجُرُونَ itself, while its connection with rejecting truth should remain visible.

Rendering every derivative as “leaving” cannot adequately represent this usage’s semantic field.

مَهْجُورًا: setting the Qur’an aside

Al-Furqān 25:30

«وَقَالَ الرَّسُولُ يَا رَبِّ إِنَّ قَوْمِي اتَّخَذُوا هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنَ مَهْجُورًا»

wa-qāla al-rasūlu yā rabbi inna qawmī ittakhadhū hādhā al-qurʾāna mahjūrā

“And the Messenger said: My Lord, my people have set this Qur’an aside.”

مَهْجُورًا is a passive participle. In this construction, the Qur’an is what has been abandoned or set aside.

اتَّخَذُوا هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنَ مَهْجُورًا describes treating the Qur’an as abandoned. Such rejection may appear in refusing it, disregarding its message, or setting aside its guidance.

Another lexical interpretation connects مَهْجُورًا with improper speech, referring to false statements made about the Qur’an. This relates to opponents’ disparaging descriptions of it. In the verse’s immediate construction, however, “set aside” is the clearer and more natural reading.

Abandoning the Qur’an is not simply equivalent to failing to buy a copy or recite its words. Someone may read the text yet disregard its guidance in understanding, judgment, and action. This inference comes from the verse’s subject and the Qur’an’s role, rather than from hajr itself meaning disobedience.

5. Migration: leaving a land and a former environment

With هَاجَرَ and مُهَاجِر, separation extends beyond personal relationships to leaving a home, land, or former social environment.

Classical Arabic records migration as moving from one place of residence to another. Its connection with leaving and separation is clear: a person leaves where they have lived and sets out for another environment.

Form III alone does not prove that two parties must leave one another or that separation requires mutual hostility. Relocation and departure from the former dwelling are established by the verb’s usage and the verses’ context.

Migration toward one’s Lord

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:26

«فَآمَنَ لَهُ لُوطٌ وَقَالَ إِنِّي مُهَاجِرٌ إِلَىٰ رَبِّي إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ»

fa-āmana lahu lūṭun wa-qāla innī muhājirun ilā rabbī innahu huwa al-ʿazīzu al-ḥakīm

“Then Lot believed him. And he said: I am migrating toward my Lord; indeed, He is the Mighty, the Wise.”

إِنِّي مُهَاجِرٌ إِلَىٰ رَبِّي shows that migration can have a direction and purpose grounded in faith. The subject of “he said” is not explicitly named in this phrase: a well-known contextual interpretation identifies Abraham, while another possibility identifies Lot. The words of the root alone do not settle that question.

إِلَىٰ رَبِّي is not the name of a land or geographical destination. The Lord is identified as the movement’s direction. In the story, migration involves leaving the former environment while also having a spiritual aim.

The expression should neither be reduced to an aimless geographical journey nor used to erase actual departure from one’s dwelling because it says “toward my Lord.”

Migration in the path of The God

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

«وَمَنْ يُهَاجِرْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ يَجِدْ فِي الْأَرْضِ مُرَاغَمًا كَثِيرًا وَسَعَةً وَمَنْ يَخْرُجْ مِنْ بَيْتِهِ مُهَاجِرًا إِلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ ثُمَّ يُدْرِكْهُ الْمَوْتُ فَقَدْ وَقَعَ أَجْرُهُ عَلَى اللَّهِ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا»

wa-man yuhājir fī sabīli Allāhi yajid fī al-arḍi murāghaman kathīran wa-saʿatan wa-man yakhruj min baytihi muhājiran ilā Allāhi wa-rasūlihi thumma yudrikhu al-mawtu fa-qad waqaʿa ajruhu ʿalā Allāhi wa-kāna Allāhu ghafūran raḥīmā

“Whoever migrates in the path of The God will find many places of refuge and ample room on earth. Whoever leaves his home migrating toward The God and His Messenger, and is then overtaken by death, his reward has already become due from The God. The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

The verse connects يُهَاجِرْ with يَخْرُجْ مِنْ بَيْتِهِ. Leaving home makes the migrant’s actual movement clear, while مُهَاجِرًا إِلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ describes its direction and character.

ثُمَّ يُدْرِكْهُ الْمَوْتُ also shows that the value of migration does not depend on definitely reaching the destination. Someone who begins the journey is included in the promise even if death intervenes before arrival.

Here migration is a real action beginning with departure from home, while intention, direction, and purpose also matter.

Migration in response to oppression

Al-Nisāʾ 4:97 questions those who remained in oppressive circumstances despite having the ability to migrate. Its question about the breadth of The God’s earth and migration within it shows that remaining under oppression is not always virtuous.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:98 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:99 distinguish those lacking the actual means and route of departure. Responsibility to migrate must therefore be assessed alongside ability and opportunity.

This accords with other uses of the root. Migration is more than declaring dissatisfaction with an environment; actually leaving requires means and conditions.

Al-Naḥl 16:41 also describes migration after oppression, while Al-Naḥl 16:110 connects it with hardship, striving, and patience.

Nevertheless, هَاجَرَ does not itself mean “oppressed.” Oppression and hardship are supplied by those verses’ context, not the root’s structure.

Migrants and those expelled

Al-Ḥashr 59:8

«لِلْفُقَرَاءِ الْمُهَاجِرِينَ الَّذِينَ أُخْرِجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ وَأَمْوَالِهِمْ يَبْتَغُونَ فَضْلًا مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانًا وَيَنْصُرُونَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الصَّادِقُونَ»

li-l-fuqarāʾi al-muhājirīna alladhīna ukhrijū min diyārihim wa-amwālihim yabtaghūna faḍlan mina Allāhi wa-riḍwānan wa-yanṣurūna Allāha wa-rasūlahu ulāʾika humu al-ṣādiqūn

“For the poor migrants who were expelled from their homes and possessions, seeking favor and approval from The God and supporting The God and His Messenger: they are the truthful.”

Here الْمُهَاجِرِينَ occurs with أُخْرِجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ وَأَمْوَالِهِمْ.

هَاجَرَ presents departure from the perspective of leaving and going; أُخْرِجُوا identifies an outside force expelling people from their homes and possessions. Pressure and expulsion may make someone a migrant. Migration and forced displacement are therefore not always separate, unrelated conditions.

Poverty, expulsion, and loss of possessions are not fixed components of “migrant.” They characterize the particular migrants described here.

Al-Ḥashr 59:9 praises those who welcome and love migrants. مَنْ هَاجَرَ إِلَيْهِمْ identifies the destination through إِلَيْهِمْ: those who migrated to them.

Qur’anic migration is thus not described solely through departure from the first location. Arrival in another community and relationships with its people can also belong to the reality of migration.

Women who migrate

In Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:10, الْمُؤْمِنَاتُ مُهَاجِرَاتٍ refers to believing women arriving as migrants in the community addressed.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:50 also uses the feminine verbal form هَاجَرْنَ.

Migration in Qur’anic language is therefore not confined to men. مُهَاجِرَات expresses the same basic meaning in the feminine plural.

The rules concerning receiving these women, examining their circumstances, and their family relationships come from the construction and context of Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:10. The feminine form alone does not carry a special legal meaning.

6. Migration, faith, striving, and social responsibility

Al-Baqarah 2:218, Āl ʿImrān 3:195, Al-Anfāl 8:72, Al-Anfāl 8:74, Al-Anfāl 8:75, and Al-Tawbah 9:20 place migration alongside faith and striving in The God’s path.

This association matters, but does not make هَاجَرُوا and جَاهَدُوا synonymous.

Migration expresses leaving the former environment and relocating; jihad expresses striving and applying one’s capacity toward a goal. The two may coincide in someone’s life while remaining distinct actions.

Al-Anfāl 8:72 discusses the relationship between those who believed and migrated and those who sheltered and supported them. It separately addresses believers who had not yet migrated.

In those circumstances, migration thus affected support relationships and the believing community’s responsibilities as well as location. The verse’s specific rulings should not be generalized to every migration in every age without examining its addressees and conditions.

In Al-Tawbah 9:100 and Al-Tawbah 9:117, “the Migrants” names a recognized group alongside the Helpers. Al-Nūr 24:22 includes migrants among those discussed in connection with financial support. Al-Aḥzāb 33:6 mentions them in a context of legal and kinship relationships.

Alongside the general meaning of someone who migrates, مُهَاجِر therefore becomes a social designation for a particular community in some verses. Its identity comes from historical context and the verse’s wording, not the root alone.

Al-Ḥajj 22:58 speaks of people who migrated in The God’s path and were subsequently killed or died. This distinction itself shows that migration, being killed, jihad, and martyrdom are not one term: each has its own meaning and conditions.

7. Distinctions from nearby roots

ه ج ر and ت ر ك: distancing and leaving

تَرْك has a broad Arabic range, including leaving a person, object, action, or situation.

Hajr often overlaps with leaving, but its interpersonal uses clearly convey breaking contact, distancing, and separation.

For example, وَاهْجُرْهُمْ in Al-Muzzammil 73:10 can be rendered “withdraw from them.” The more general “leave them” does not convey the relational distance as clearly.

This is a tendency of usage, not a rule excluding every kind of leaving from hajr.

ه ج ر and خ ر ج: migration and departure

The family خ ر ج expresses going out or bringing out from a place or situation. The departure may be voluntary or caused by outside pressure.

In its migration branch, ه ج ر expresses leaving a former dwelling or community and moving toward another environment.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 contains both roots: someone leaves home as a migrant.

Al-Ḥashr 59:8 identifies migrants as people expelled from their homes and possessions.

Every migration involves leaving a previous environment, but not every departure is migration. Leaving home for daily work and returning is not migration merely because it is an exit.

ه ج ر and ف ر ر: migration and flight

ف ر ر denotes fleeing and distancing oneself from something, often in relation to threats, fear, or escape from danger.

Migration may also seek safety, but that motive is not present in every usage of ه ج ر.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:50 commands فَفِرُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ through ف ر ر; Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:26 uses مُهَاجِرٌ إِلَىٰ رَبِّي through ه ج ر.

Both give the movement a divine direction, but this does not make the verbs identical. Flight emphasizes escaping and distancing; migration in its context may express leaving an environment for a new destination.

8. Challenges of understanding and translation

A limitation of familiar translations is treating the whole family as “migration.” This suits هَاجَرُوا in many verses, but is insufficient for وَاهْجُرْهُمْ, فَاهْجُرْ, or تَهْجُرُونَ.

Another problem is confining migration to the historical movement of Muhammad and his companions from Mecca to Medina. The Qur’an also mentions migration in accounts of earlier Prophets and uses general constructions such as وَمَنْ يُهَاجِرْ.

Conversely, migration should not be reduced entirely to an inward transformation, eliminating actual relocation from verses about homes, lands, expulsion, and receiving migrants.

A spiritual reading can clarify migration’s aim and direction, but should not replace the spatial meaning supported by context.

“Migrant” alone proves neither pure intention, poverty, oppression, nor entitlement to every social benefit. Where such features occur, they arise from attributes, actions, and circumstances that the Qur’an separately states.

Migration for safety, freedom, education, or livelihood can be discussed within broader human rights and responsibilities. Those motives cannot all be made part of هَاجَرَ’s lexical meaning.

For a detailed examination of migration’s conditions, responsibilities of migrants and receiving communities, asylum seekers’ rights, and voluntary versus forced migration, Forqan’s independent study “Hijrah and Migration in the Qur’an: Preserving Life, Freedom, and Mutual Responsibilities” approaches the subject through a wider network of verses.

9. Suggested renderings for Forqan

The equivalent for the basic verb هَجَرَ should be chosen according to its object.

For people and relationships, “distance oneself,” “withdraw,” or “break off relations” may fit. For objectionable behavior or things, “desist,” “abandon,” or “keep away” may be more natural.

In Al-Muzzammil 73:10, وَاهْجُرْهُمْ هَجْرًا جَمِيلًا is best rendered “withdraw from them graciously,” preserving both separation and its appropriate manner.

In Maryam 19:46, وَاهْجُرْنِي مَلِيًّا means “leave me for a long time.”

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:34, وَاهْجُرُوهُنَّ فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ should retain the reference to beds, for example “withdraw from them in beds.”

In Al-Muddaththir 74:5, فَاهْجُرْ may be rendered “keep away” or “abandon.”

For تَهْجُرُونَ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:67, the evening-gathering context and reading evidence support “you speak improperly.” Its connection with turning away from the verses should also remain in the explanation.

مَهْجُورًا in Al-Furqān 25:30 may be rendered “set aside,” “abandoned,” or, within the sentence, “have set aside.”

For Form III هَاجَرَ, “migrate” or “emigrate” fits spatial contexts. Where general readers need clarification, “leave one’s home or homeland and go elsewhere” explains the meaning.

Render مُهَاجِر and مُهَاجِرَات as “migrant” and “women who migrate,” respectively. Do not impose additional descriptions such as poor, refugee, displaced, or war-affected unless the particular context calls for them.

10. Conclusion

In its principal Qur’anic branch, ه ج ر denotes leaving, distancing, and severing a connection. The separation may concern people, behaviors, a living environment, or a homeland.

The basic verb هَجَرَ emphasizes withdrawal and abandonment of contact or action. هَاجَرَ and مُهَاجِر bring out departure from a former environment toward another. مَهْجُور denotes something set aside or turned away from.

تَهْجُرُونَ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:67 shows that improper speech is also a branch requiring independent examination. One equivalent cannot cover every form without regard to differences in structure and context.

Final definition for Forqan: In its predominant Qur’anic usage, ه ج ر means leaving and separating from a person, behavior, or former environment. In human relations this appears as distancing and broken contact; in migration, as leaving one’s dwelling for another environment. Improper speech is a distinct usage whose meaning is determined by construction and context.

The root alone does not determine hajr’s moral value. Withdrawal may be appropriate or inappropriate; leaving may mean escape from oppression or rejection of truth; migration may be in The God’s path or for another motive. The verb’s object, the action’s purpose, and the verse’s combined evidence determine the separation’s character, the movement’s direction, and human responsibility.
