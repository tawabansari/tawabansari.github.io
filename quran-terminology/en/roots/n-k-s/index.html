A study of naks in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from inverting objects and reversing a condition to lowering the head, the return of illness, and changes in old age

The three-letter root N K S in Arabic concerns turning something upside down, reversing its previous condition, or changing its direction or order. Words of this family describe turning something on its head, lowering the head, or a change from a previously stable or desirable condition.

In physical uses, naks can refer to placing an object in reverse, overturning it, or lowering the head. In extended uses, the same family describes an illness returning after improvement, declining ability in old age, and falling back from a desirable condition.

These uses are related, but they are not identical. Reversing an arrow, lowering the head, and bodily weakening in old age are three different situations. Their connection lies in a change of direction, reversal of condition, or loss of a previous state; the precise meaning of each derivative must be established in its own setting.

The root N K S occurs three times in three Qur’anic verses: «نُكِسُوا» in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65, «نَاكِسُوا» in Al-Sajdah 32:12, and «نُنَكِّسْهُ» in Yā Sīn 36:68.

These occurrences concern three subjects: the reversal of a group’s position before Abraham’s argument, the criminals’ lowering of their heads when facing their Lord, and human change with advancing age.

The root should therefore not be defined solely as “being defeated,” “feeling ashamed,” or “growing old.” These may be situations and consequences associated with particular uses, but the root’s basic meaning is broader.

1. Forms and semantic core

Important forms of this family in Arabic include:

  • نَكَسَ: turned something upside down, inverted it, or reversed its direction.
  • نَكْس: the act of inverting something or reversing its condition.
  • نِكْس: in early usage, an arrow whose direction or parts have been reversed; also, descriptively, someone weak or falling below an expected ability or standing.
  • أَنْكَاس: plural of niks, particularly of inferior arrows and weak people.
  • نَكَّسَ and تَنْكِيس: inverted something, lowered the head, or reversed a condition.
  • اِنْتَكَسَ: became inverted or returned to an earlier, undesirable condition.
  • تَنَكَّسَ: became inverted or lost its previous condition.
  • مَنْكُوس: inverted or placed in reverse.
  • نَاكِس: someone lowering the head.
  • نَوَاكِس: one of the attested plurals of nākis in Arabic poetry.
  • مُنَكِّس: someone or something lowering or reversing another’s head or condition; of a horse, one that does not raise its head or falls behind other horses.
  • نُكَاس: an illness returning after improvement or subsiding.
  • نَكِيس: in certain uses, someone weak, incapable, or inferior to their peers.

Other forms are also recorded for the return of illness, weakness in old age, and descriptions of certain animals.

Only three forms occur in the Qur’an:

نُكِسُوا: a passive perfect verb of the basic stem.

نَاكِسُوا: the masculine plural active participle of the basic verb.

نُنَكِّسْهُ: an imperfect verb of Form II, with an attached object pronoun.

The Qur’an thus uses a passive verb, an active participle, and a transitive Form II verb, while the nouns naks, nukās, intikās, and mankūs do not occur in its text.

Semantic core: reversing a condition

The core of this root is turning or inverting something: changing its direction or order into a reversed state.

A clear example is turning an object upside down.

نَكَسَ الشَّيْءَ

nakasa al-shayʾa

Translation: “He turned the thing upside down.”

In this use, something previously having a particular direction or order is reversed.

The act may be entirely physical, such as placing an implement in reverse. It may also concern a person’s condition, such as lowering the head or returning from strength to weakness.

The root itself, however, does not mean misfortune, moral defeat, or misguidance.

Someone might, for example, turn a vessel upside down to wash or empty it. That act is not inherently wrong or harmful.

By contrast, the reversal of a person’s position or the return of illness may be unwelcome.

Thus, inversion and reversal of a previous condition form the semantic core; their value and consequences are determined by context.

2. Naks in early Arabic: from a reversed arrow to returning illness

Reversing an arrow

One specific early use of this root concerns arrows.

In Arabic, niks names an arrow whose upper part has been damaged or broken and whose parts have subsequently been reversed.

Lexical explanations describe changing the positions of the arrow’s rear end and point so that it could be used again.

Some accounts describe such an arrow as weaker or inferior to an intact one.

This use has two features: first, reversing the order or direction of the arrow’s parts; second, its possible inferiority after that change.

These features should not be equated. The reversal is clear; inferior quality and reduced effectiveness are characteristics mentioned in descriptions of some niks arrows.

From this use, ankās is known as a plural of niks.

Poetic evidence: inferior arrows and social standing

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says:

قَدْ نَاضَلُونَا فَسَلُّوا مِنْ كِنَانَتِهِمْ
مَجْدًا تَلِيدًا وَعِزًّا غَيْرَ أَنْكَاسِ

qad nāḍalūnā fa-sallū min kinānatihim / majdan talīdan wa-ʿizzan ghayra ankāsi

Translation: “They competed with us and drew from their quiver ancient glory and honor unlike inferior, reversed arrows.”

The poet uses the imagery of arrows and a quiver to express glory and social standing.

Ankās here evokes arrows considered inferior because of alteration and weakness.

By denying this description, the poet speaks of honor and glory that he regards as firm and valuable.

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah lived in both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, but the exact date of this verse is unknown. It should therefore not be presented without evidence as a definitively pre-Qur’anic witness.

The verse is useful because it shows the connection between a reversed arrow and the descriptive use of niks for weakness or inferiority.

It does not establish that every derivative of N K S necessarily has a negative moral connotation.

Lowering the head

Arabic uses the expression:

نَكَسَ رَأْسَهُ

nakasa raʾsahu

Translation: “He lowered his head.”

Also:

نَكَّسَ رَأْسَهُ

nakkasa raʾsahu

Translation: “He bowed his head.”

Here, a person inclines the head downward from its usual raised or upright position.

This movement may indicate shame, sorrow, helplessness, humility, or another condition.

The act of lowering the head does not itself establish the person’s intention.

Someone may lower their head to see something on the ground; the movement need not signify humiliation or defeat.

By contrast, when someone faces their own error or cannot answer, lowering the head may indicate shame.

The physical movement must therefore be distinguished from the psychological or moral condition accompanying it.

Arabic poetry: lowered heads and eyes

Al-Farazdaq, a poet of the Umayyad period, says:

وَإِذَا الرِّجَالُ رَأَوْا يَزِيدَ رَأَيْتَهُمْ
خُضَعَ الرِّقَابِ نَوَاكِسَ الْأَبْصَارِ

wa-idhā al-rijālu raʾaw yazīda raʾaytahum / khuḍaʿa al-riqābi nawākisa al-abṣāri

Translation: “When the men saw Yazīd, you would see them with bowed necks and downcast eyes.”

Nawākis al-abṣār in this verse refers to downcast eyes.

The poem’s setting concerns submission or deference before someone in power.

The verse shows that naks can describe bodily posture and a person’s gaze, with social or psychological implications understood from that posture.

Early grammatical discussions examine the plural nawākis here, since some grammarians regarded this form in such a position as unusual.

There are also differences in the transmitted wording and grammatical analysis. The verse is useful for understanding usage, but its particular poetic construction should not be made an absolute rule for all Arabic plural forms.

Al-Farazdaq belongs to the Umayyad period; this verse is not a pre-Qur’anic witness.

Birth in reversed presentation

In early Arabic, al-wilād al-mankūs describes a delivery in which the baby’s feet emerge before its head.

The usual order of birth is reversed in this use.

The example helps explain the root’s physical meaning, since the inversion concerns the order in which parts of the body are positioned.

Naks here is not itself the name of a disease or a definitive judgment about the baby’s survival. The main point is the reversal of its usual position or order of emergence.

This image should not be transferred to the Qur’an to conclude that «نُنَكِّسْهُ فِي الْخَلْقِ» in Yā Sīn 36:68 means the body returning to an embryonic state.

The verse concerns advancing age and human change, not a literal repetition of embryonic stages.

Illness returning after improvement

An important use of this family is nukās.

In Arabic, nukās describes an illness returning after the patient has begun to improve or its severity has lessened for a time.

In the expression:

نُكِسَ الْمَرِيضُ

nukisa al-marīḍu

Translation: “The patient suffered a return of the illness.”

The person returns from relative improvement to illness.

This meaning is related to reversal of condition, but is not the physical inversion of an object.

A verse attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh al-Hudhalī reads:

خَيَالٌ لِزَيْنَبَ قَدْ هَاجَ لِي
نُكَاسًا مِنَ الْحُبِّ بَعْدَ انْدِمَالِ

khayālun li-zaynaba qad hāja lī / nukāsan mina al-ḥubbi baʿda indimāli

Translation: “An image of Zaynab stirred in me a renewed pain of love after its wound had healed.”

Nukās here describes the return of a suffering that had subsided.

It shows how a word for the return of physical illness can extend to an emotional experience.

The transmitted wording varies. Some versions have li-ʿAbdah instead of li-Zaynab, and khabālan mina al-dāʾ instead of nukāsan mina al-ḥubb.

Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh is a poet of the Umayyad period; some biographical accounts also describe him as having lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The exact date and original wording of this verse nevertheless remain uncertain.

The verse helps establish transmitted early Arabic usage, but cannot alone prove that pre-Islamic Arabs used naks in precisely a modern psychological sense.

Returning to a previously abandoned activity

Lexical accounts record the expression:

نَكَسْتُهُ فِي ذَٰلِكَ الْأَمْرِ

nakastuhu fī dhālika al-amri

With the meaning: “I returned him to the very activity from which he had previously emerged.”

Here, reversal concerns the condition or course of an activity.

Someone may have passed through a stage, only to be returned to the earlier one.

This use helps explain the possibility of a nonphysical meaning of naks.

Some accounts also use naks for repeating a dyeing process or returning a task to its earlier state.

Repeating any activity is not necessarily called naks. The context must justify some return, reversal, or change of condition.

3. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65: reversal before Abraham’s argument

ثُمَّ نُكِسُوا عَلَىٰ رُءُوسِهِمْ لَقَدْ عَلِمْتَ مَا هَٰؤُلَاءِ يَنْطِقُونَ

thumma nukisū ʿalā ruʾūsihim la-qad ʿalimta mā hāʾulāʾi yanṭiqūna

Translation: “Then they were turned upside down upon their heads: You certainly know that these do not speak.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65

This is the first Qur’anic occurrence of N K S.

In the story, Abraham discusses with his people the objects they worship. After the idols have been broken, they ask him who did it. He responds that they should ask the idols, if they can speak.

The preceding verse says:

فَرَجَعُوا إِلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ فَقَالُوا إِنَّكُمْ أَنْتُمُ الظَّالِمُونَ

fa-rajaʿū ilā anfusihim fa-qālū innakum antumu al-ẓālimūna

Translation: “Then they returned to themselves and said: Surely you yourselves are the wrongdoers.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:64

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65 then describes their being turned upon their heads.

The meaning of «نُكِسُوا عَلَىٰ رُءُوسِهِمْ»

Nukisū is a passive perfect verb from N K S.

Its basic meaning in this construction is being overturned or turned upside down.

The question is whether the Qur’an describes a literal bodily inversion or a reversal of their position and argument.

Arabic recognizes the expression nakasa al-shayʾa ʿalā raʾsihi for turning something upside down.

When such an image is used of people in a discussion, it may refer to bodily posture, an intellectual position, or their inability to respond.

The context of Abraham’s discussion with his people matters in determining the intended sense.

Three recognized interpretations

The history of interpretation offers three related but distinct explanations of this expression.

In the first, Abraham’s people return to their previous position after a moment of reflection and self-examination, refusing to accept the conclusion of his argument.

On this interpretation, the reversal concerns the renewed change in their position.

In the second, they cannot answer Abraham’s argument and their own case turns against them.

This fits their subsequent words: they acknowledge that the idols cannot speak, an admission that strengthens Abraham’s argument.

In the third, they lower their heads in shame.

This interpretation relates to the established use of naks for lowering the head.

The verse, however, says «نُكِسُوا عَلَىٰ رُءُوسِهِمْ», not «نَكَسُوا رُءُوسَهُمْ». The first describes the people themselves as overturned; the second would directly express their lowering of their heads.

This grammatical difference gives particular weight in this context to the reversal of their condition or argument.

Had they actually turned away from their error?

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:64, the Qur’an says:

فَرَجَعُوا إِلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ

fa-rajaʿū ilā anfusihim

“Then they returned to themselves.”

The expression indicates that Abraham’s words prompted them to reconsider and examine their situation.

The meaning of «إِنَّكُمْ أَنْتُمُ الظَّالِمُونَ» is nevertheless disputed.

On one interpretation, they recognized their own wrongdoing in worshipping powerless beings.

On another, some told the others that questioning Abraham was wrong, because the idols were present and should themselves be questioned.

Given this disagreement, one cannot assert with certainty that in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:64 they embraced monotheism or firmly decided to abandon idol worship.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65, they say:

لَقَدْ عَلِمْتَ مَا هَٰؤُلَاءِ يَنْطِقُونَ

la-qad ʿalimta mā hāʾulāʾi yanṭiqūna

“You certainly know that these do not speak.”

This explicitly acknowledges the idols’ inability to speak.

That very fact, however, supports Abraham’s argument.

They can thus be understood as revealing the powerlessness of their objects of worship by saying something true, without necessarily accepting its conclusion.

One interpretation relates naks to the reversal of their argument. Others favor a return to their previous position or lowering their heads in shame.

This disagreement should not be concealed by eliminating one of the credible possibilities.

The main point in this occurrence

Here, naks expresses a reversal in their condition.

Within the discussion, the reversal of their position or argument is a strong interpretation. Lowering their heads in helplessness is also linguistically possible, but their exact physical posture is not explicitly stated.

The basic translation should therefore preserve the image of reversal, while explanation clarifies its relationship to argumentative helplessness and a possible return to their earlier position.

Nukisū should not be translated directly as only “they became disbelievers,” “they felt ashamed,” or “they were defeated.” Such renderings replace the word’s own image with a consequence or interpretation.

4. Al-Sajdah 32:12: the criminals’ downcast heads

وَلَوْ تَرَىٰ إِذِ الْمُجْرِمُونَ نَاكِسُوا رُءُوسِهِمْ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ رَبَّنَا أَبْصَرْنَا وَسَمِعْنَا فَارْجِعْنَا نَعْمَلْ صَالِحًا إِنَّا مُوقِنُونَ

wa-law tarā idhi al-mujrimūna nākisū ruʾūsihim ʿinda rabbihim rabbanā abṣarnā wa-samiʿnā fa-arjiʿnā naʿmal ṣāliḥan innā mūqinūna

Translation: “If you could see when the criminals lower their heads before their Lord: Our Lord, we have seen and heard; return us so that we may do what is right; we are certain.”

Al-Sajdah 32:12

This is the second Qur’anic occurrence of N K S.

Unlike the expression in Al-Anbiyāʾ, this construction directly concerns the criminals’ lowered heads.

The meaning of «نَاكِسُوا رُءُوسِهِمْ»

Nākisū is a masculine plural active participle.

It describes people who have lowered their heads.

Ruʾūsihim is joined to the active participle in a construct relationship. This accounts for the loss of the final nūn of the sound masculine plural nākisūn.

Nākisū ruʾūsihim therefore means “lowering their heads.”

The main subject here is not the inversion of the entire body, but the lowering of the heads.

“They lower their heads” is therefore more natural and better suited to the construction than “they are turned upside down upon their heads.”

Does naks here mean shame?

The lowering of heads concerns the criminals’ condition when facing their Lord.

They say:

رَبَّنَا أَبْصَرْنَا وَسَمِعْنَا

rabbanā abṣarnā wa-samiʿnā

“Our Lord, we have seen and heard.”

They then ask:

فَارْجِعْنَا نَعْمَلْ صَالِحًا

fa-arjiʿnā naʿmal ṣāliḥan

“Return us so that we may do what is right.”

Their words show that they now face a reality to which they did not give due attention in their previous life.

In such a situation, lowering the head may indicate shame, helplessness, regret, or involuntary submission to an evident truth.

These states are understood from context, not from the independent meaning of the active participle nākis.

Someone elsewhere might lower their head out of respect. The same bodily movement would not necessarily indicate criminality or shame.

Naks here thus describes the position of the head; the people’s moral condition is understood from their designation as criminals and from their words.

Is their request to return itself naks?

The criminals ask The God to return them to their previous life.

Their request is expressed by the verb «فَارْجِعْنَا».

Nākisū ruʾūsihim does not itself mean requesting a return.

Both ideas occur in one scene, but belong to two different roots.

Their heads are lowered, while they also wish to return and reform their deeds.

Their request alone does not establish that repentance and reform are accepted in that condition. The Qur’an here reports their words; their ultimate outcome must be examined with other verses.

Why the difference between the two verses matters

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65 uses «نُكِسُوا عَلَىٰ رُءُوسِهِمْ», a construction that can express the reversal of an argument or position within a discussion.

Al-Sajdah 32:12 uses «نَاكِسُوا رُءُوسِهِمْ», which expresses the lowering of heads more directly.

The expressions share a root, but differ in morphology and syntax.

The precise meaning in Al-Sajdah should not be transferred to Al-Anbiyāʾ without examination, or vice versa.

5. Yā Sīn 36:68: reversal of human condition in old age

وَمَنْ نُعَمِّرْهُ نُنَكِّسْهُ فِي الْخَلْقِ أَفَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ

wa-man nuʿammirhu nunakkis-hu fī al-khalqi a-fa-lā yaʿqilūna

Translation: “Whomever We grant a long life, We reverse in creation; will they not reason?”

Yā Sīn 36:68

This is the third Qur’anic occurrence of N K S.

Here, naks relates to the extension of human life.

The meaning of «نُنَكِّسْهُ»

Nunakkishu is a Form II verb.

In the widely used transmission of Ḥafṣ, the kāf is doubled, and the pronoun hu refers to the person whose life has been prolonged.

The verb in this construction is attributed to The God.

Its direct meaning is inverting or reversing the person’s condition.

What does this reversal concern?

The expression continues:

فِي الْخَلْقِ

fī al-khalqi

“In creation.”

The subject is thus change in the person’s created constitution.

Returning from strength to weakness

Al-Rūm 30:54 helps explain this expression:

اللَّهُ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ ضَعْفٍ ثُمَّ جَعَلَ مِنْ بَعْدِ ضَعْفٍ قُوَّةً ثُمَّ جَعَلَ مِنْ بَعْدِ قُوَّةٍ ضَعْفًا وَشَيْبَةً يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَاءُ وَهُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْقَدِيرُ

allāhu alladhī khalaqakum min ḍaʿfin thumma jaʿala min baʿdi ḍaʿfin quwwatan thumma jaʿala min baʿdi quwwatin ḍaʿfan wa-shaybatan yakhluqu mā yashāʾu wa-huwa al-ʿalīmu al-qadīru

Translation: “The God is the One who created you from weakness, then appointed strength after weakness, then appointed weakness and gray hair after strength. He creates what He wills, and He is the Knowing, the Powerful.”

The verse describes a recognizable course of human life: initial weakness, increasing strength, and then declining strength and old age.

In this respect, «نُنَكِّسْهُ فِي الْخَلْقِ» in Yā Sīn 36:68 accords with a return from strength to weakness.

People acquire greater physical ability during part of their life. With advancing age, bodily strength, mobility, and certain other abilities may decline.

“Reversal” here means that the course of increasing ability changes at some stage toward declining strength and weakness.

The verse does not say that the human body literally regains a childhood or embryonic form.

The subject is a change in abilities and created condition, not a physical retracing of every developmental stage in reverse order.

Do all older people become equally incapable?

No. People differ in physical condition, cognitive ability, and the course of aging.

Someone may retain considerable mental ability and practical independence at an advanced age, while another faces greater limitations.

The verse concerns the recognized course of human change with advancing age; it does not assign everyone an identical degree of incapacity.

Naks here does not itself mean dementia or complete loss of memory.

Al-Naḥl 16:70 says:

وَمِنكُم مَّن يُرَدُّ إِلَىٰ أَرْذَلِ الْعُمُرِ لِكَيْ لَا يَعْلَمَ بَعْدَ عِلْمٍ شَيْئًا

wa-minkum man yuraddu ilā ardhali al-ʿumuri li-kay lā yaʿlama baʿda ʿilmin shayʾan

Translation: “Among you are those returned to the lowest stage of life, so that after knowing they know nothing.”

The verse describes some people facing severe loss of cognitive ability in old age.

The phrase “among you are those” indicates that the condition is not described identically for everyone.

Yā Sīn 36:68 should therefore not be restricted to a particular memory disease or cognitive disorder.

Nor are Qur’anic verses about aging sufficient to diagnose a medical condition or determine the future abilities of each individual.

Does naks mean returning to childhood?

Early explanations sometimes compare severe old age with childhood.

The comparison is understandable in one respect: people may need others’ help at the beginning of life and in severe old age.

A similar need for care does not make childhood and old age completely identical.

Children are at the beginning of growth, with many abilities still developing.

Older people, by contrast, have a lifetime of experience and knowledge behind them, while some physical or mental abilities may have declined.

Returning from strength to weakness does not therefore mean literally returning to childhood.

This distinction should be preserved when explaining the verse.

The readings «نُنَكِّسْهُ» and «نَنْكُسْهُ»

Yā Sīn 36:68 has an important variation of reading.

ʿĀṣim’s reading, including the transmission of Ḥafṣ, and Ḥamzah’s reading have the following form:

نُنَكِّسْهُ

nunakkis-hu

This is a Form II verb.

The other readers among the seven read the word with an initial a, a vowelless second nūn, and an undoubled kāf with u, as follows:

نَنْكُسْهُ

nankus-hu

This form belongs to the basic verb nakasa.

Both readings concern reversing or inverting the human condition.

One explanation regards the Form II construction as fitting the stage-by-stage change in a person’s condition, preferring the doubled reading for this reason. This explains the construction’s suitability to context; it does not make gradualness a necessary meaning of every Form II verb.

Form II alone cannot establish that every naks necessarily takes place gradually over a long time.

Conversely, the basic-stem reading does not mean a sudden, instantaneous change.

The morphological difference may affect the manner of expression, but does not change the principal meaning here.

«أَفَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ»: reflecting on life’s changes

The verse ends with this question:

أَفَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ

a-fa-lā yaʿqilūna

“Will they not reason?”

It presents the change in human abilities with advancing age as something to reflect upon.

The preceding verses of Yā Sīn speak of The God’s power to change the human condition.

Yā Sīn 36:68 then points to a change observable in ordinary human life.

Naks here thus forms part of a reminder that human creation is subject to change.

The root N K S does not itself mean “proof of resurrection” or “a sign of The God’s power.” Such conclusions come from context and the closing question.

The verse should also not be taken to mean that old age is a divine punishment or a sign of moral failure.

Aging and declining ability are recognized conditions of human life. The Qur’an uses them to recall the instability of a creature’s powers, not to morally reproach older people.

6. N K S and related roots

N K S and Q L B

In physical uses, qalb concerns turning, overturning, and changing the direction of something.

Naks also belongs in this field and is sometimes explained through qalb.

In clear examples, however, naks highlights reversal of a previous direction or position, such as an arrow being turned end for end or the head being lowered.

The roots may be synonymous or very close in certain constructions, but their full ranges of use differ.

Q L B also has extensive Qur’anic uses concerning the heart and its changes.

Not every occurrence of qalb should be interpreted as naks, nor does a shared image of inversion establish historical identity between the roots.

Study of Q L B

N K S and N K Ṣ

These roots have very similar letters, but differ in meaning.

N K S concerns turning upside down, reversing a condition, or lowering the head.

N K Ṣ concerns retreating or drawing back from a previous course or action.

Al-Anfāl 8:48 says:

فَلَمَّا تَرَاءَتِ الْفِئَتَانِ نَكَصَ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ

fa-lammā tarāʾati al-fiʾatāni nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibayhi

Translation: “When the two groups came into view of one another, he turned back on his heels.”

The verb here is nakaṣa with ṣād, not sīn.

This use concerns retreat from a previous position.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65, however, nukisū has sīn and concerns reversal of condition.

Retreat may accompany a change in someone’s position, but that overlap does not make the roots identical.

N K S and R J ʿ

R J ʿ concerns returning and can be used in very diverse circumstances.

N K S also relates to returning in certain uses, such as recurring illness or changes in old age.

Not every return is naks.

Someone may return home from a journey. R J ʿ is natural here, with no need to use N K S.

By contrast, N K S is clearer when illness returns after improvement or someone’s condition changes from strength to weakness.

Naks therefore often expresses inversion or reversal of condition, rather than simply returning to a previous place.

Study of R J ʿ

N K S and KH F Ḍ

Khafḍ concerns lowering or bringing something down.

This approaches naks in its use concerning the head.

Khafḍ does not necessarily mean inversion or reversal of condition.

Lowering a voice or reducing a quantity may, for example, be expressed with KH F Ḍ, without needing N K S.

In Al-Sajdah 32:12, naks of the heads concerns their being lowered; the root’s image relates to changing the head’s direction from its usual position.

Lowering may thus be an instance of naks without making all uses of KH F Ḍ and N K S identical.

Study of KH F Ḍ

N K S and N K TH

In Arabic, nakth concerns unravelling twisted strands and breaking a covenant or oath.

Naks concerns inverting something and changing its direction or condition.

Breaking a covenant may reverse someone’s social or moral position, but that consequence does not equate the two roots’ meanings.

Naks concerns reversal.

Nakth concerns undoing a previously established bond or commitment.

The similarity of their letters should not lead to their conflation in lexical research.

Study of N K TH

7. Proposed translations for Forqan

For physical uses of nakasa, “turned upside down,” “turned end for end,” and “placed in reverse” are suitable.

For intakasa, “was overturned” or “returned to an earlier, undesirable condition” is natural, depending on context.

For nukās concerning illness, “the return of illness after improvement” is suitable.

For nākis al-raʾs, “with head lowered,” “with head bowed,” or “bowing the head” is appropriate.

The different Qur’anic constructions must nevertheless remain distinct.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65

ثُمَّ نُكِسُوا عَلَىٰ رُءُوسِهِمْ

thumma nukisū ʿalā ruʾūsihim

Proposed translation:

“Then they were turned upside down upon their heads.”

This preserves the verse’s verbal image.

Contextual explanation should clarify that the reversal occurs within Abraham’s discussion and argument, potentially referring to the defeat of their case or their turning away from a moment of reconsideration.

Lowering their heads in shame is also a recognized interpretation, but should not be presented without explanation as the expression’s only certain meaning.

Al-Sajdah 32:12

نَاكِسُوا رُءُوسِهِمْ

nākisū ruʾūsihim

Forqan’s rendering:

“They lower their heads.”

This is accurate and natural.

Nākisū is an active participle here, describing the criminals’ heads.

Explanation may add that, in the context of facing their Lord and requesting a return, lowering their heads relates to helplessness and shame.

Shame should not replace the direct meaning of lowering the head.

Yā Sīn 36:68

وَمَنْ نُعَمِّرْهُ نُنَكِّسْهُ فِي الْخَلْقِ

wa-man nuʿammirhu nunakkis-hu fī al-khalqi

Forqan’s rendering:

“Whomever We grant a long life, We reverse in creation.”

This translation retains the literal image of naks.

Explanation should clarify that reversing the created condition concerns change in ability and a return from strength to weakness.

If a smoother rendering is needed, “We turn his created condition toward weakness” conveys the contextual sense. The direct translation should not, however, depart unnecessarily from the image of naks.

Brief root meaning and meanings in the verses

Brief root meaning: turning something upside down and reversing its direction or condition; in other uses, lowering the head or returning from a previous condition to a different one.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65: reversal of a group’s condition before Abraham’s argument, with possible reference to the defeat of their case, turning away from earlier reconsideration, or lowering their heads in shame.

In Al-Sajdah 32:12: lowering their heads; in context, the criminals’ condition when facing their Lord.

In Yā Sīn 36:68: a change in human created condition with advancing age; in context, a return from strength to weakness in old age.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root N K S concerns turning upside down, reversing, and changing something’s direction or condition.

This appears in turning objects end for end, reversing an arrow, lowering the head, and certain descriptions of changes in human condition.

Other branches use naks and intikās for illness returning after improvement, declining ability, and a return to an earlier condition.

These uses have a clear semantic connection, but should not all be restricted to one moral or physical outcome.

N K S occurs three times in the Qur’an.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65 presents reversal within Abraham’s discussion with his people. The expression allows several interpretations; reversal of position or argument is particularly strong in context. Lowering the head in shame is also linguistically possible.

In Al-Sajdah 32:12, the active participle nākisū directly describes the criminals’ lowered heads. Shame and helplessness are understood from the scene of judgment and their words, not from the root alone.

In Yā Sīn 36:68, nunakkishu concerns human change with advancing age. Within the network of verses on creation and aging, this is understood as declining ability and a return from strength to weakness.

Naks in the Qur’an therefore does not always mean defeat, shame, or growing old.

The root also has no fixed moral value. An object may be inverted without moral consequences; a person may be unable to answer truth; and advancing age may bring a natural decline in ability.

The Qur’an’s use of naks should not make old age a form of punishment or divine humiliation.

Final definition: at its semantic core, N K S denotes inverting and reversing something’s direction, order, or condition. Physically, it can describe turning objects end for end or lowering the head; extended uses concern a return from a previous condition, such as recurring illness or declining ability with advancing age. The Qur’an uses it in three different settings: reversal of condition before an argument, the criminals’ lowered heads, and changing human abilities in old age. The precise meaning and implications depend on the construction and context of each verse.
