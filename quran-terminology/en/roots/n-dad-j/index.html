Qur’anic Root N Ḍ J (نَضِجَ)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(naḍijat)

(naḍija)
Became cooked or ripe; citation form

Reaching maturity: cooking food, ripening fruit, and skin becoming seared in fire

1. Introducing the root and its semantic core

Arabic N Ḍ J concerns something reaching a stage of readiness or maturity in its development or preparation.

Established uses include naḍija al-laḥmu, meat became cooked, and naḍija al-thamaru, fruit ripened.

The two processes differ.

Heat cooks meat; fruit ripens through natural development, with changes in color, taste, and other properties.

Both nevertheless move from a previous condition to a stage described in Arabic as nuḍj.

Someone reaching a considered decision after thought and examination can also be called naḍīj al-raʾy: mature and sound in judgment.

This relationship should not make nuḍj invariably an ideal perfection or beneficial result.

The Qur’an uses the root for skin exposed to fire and seared or burned by its heat.

Here it describes a change in skin under fire, rather than desirable cooking or beneficial fruit ripening.

Thus reaching a stage of maturity is an important shared meaning, while the result’s benefit or harm, and whether it comes through natural growth or heat, depend on the subject and context.

Desirable fruit ripening should not be transferred to burned skin, nor the verse’s thermal injury imposed on every Arabic use of nuḍj.

The Qur’anic form

N Ḍ J occurs only once in the Qur’an:

Naḍijat — Al-Nisāʾ 4:56

It is a Form I perfect verb, third-person feminine singular.

Its grammatical subject is julūduhum, their skins.

No other form, such as naḍaja, yanḍaju, nuḍj, nāḍij, naḍīj, anḍaja, naḍḍaja, or munḍaj, occurs in the Qur’an.

Arabic records these forms, but they should not be presented as derivatives found in the Qur’an.

2. N Ḍ J in early Arabic

A. Cooking meat and food

A main Arabic use concerns cooking food.

A lexical explanation records:

نَضِجَ نَضْجًا وَنُضْجًا

naḍija naḍjan wa-nuḍjan

“It became cooked or ripe; naḍj and nuḍj are its verbal nouns.”

The explanation then discusses meat and food brought to readiness by a cook.

Naḍija al-laḥmu means the meat became cooked.

Anḍaja al-ṭāhī al-laḥma means the cook cooked the meat, bringing it to readiness.

The morphological difference matters.

Naḍija can describe becoming cooked itself.

Anḍaja describes someone or something causing the cooking.

Lexical explanations discuss dried meat, roasted meat, and cooked meat in examining this root.

It is therefore not restricted to boiling food in water; roasting by fire also belongs to its field.

That does not make direct flame necessary in every use.

Readiness can arise under different conditions, with the process identified from context.

B. Rawness and cooking

Arabic uses naḍīj in contrast with nayyiʾ.

Nayyiʾ identifies raw or uncooked food.

Naḍīj can identify cooked, ready food.

The contrast illustrates a branch in which something previously raw reaches a cooked condition.

Cooking should nevertheless be distinguished from burning.

Appropriate heat can cook food, while excessive exposure can burn it.

Ordinary food-related nuḍj does not necessarily mean burning and destruction.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:56, burning skin follows from exposure to fire and the punishment context.

“Burning” is therefore contextually appropriate without being the whole family’s general meaning.

C. Ripening fruit

Lexicons also record nuḍj for grapes, dates, and other fruit:

وَالْعِنَبُ وَالتَّمْرُ وَالثَّمَرُ يَنْضَجُ

wa-al-ʿinabu wa-al-tamru wa-al-thamaru yanḍaju

Meaning:

“Grapes, dates, and fruit ripen.”

This concerns natural ripening.

Fruit can move from unripe to ripe, with changes in color, taste, texture, and other properties.

Anḍajahu ibbānuhu means its season or appointed time brought the fruit to maturity.

This differs importantly from cooking food.

Ripening does not necessarily mean placing fruit in fire.

“Burning through heat” therefore cannot define every N Ḍ J use.

Both heat-induced cooking and natural ripening are recorded.

Their shared vocabulary does not make the processes’ mechanisms identical.

D. Mature judgment

Arabic uses:

رَجُلٌ نَضِيجُ الرَّأْيِ

rajulun naḍīju al-raʾyi

Meaning:

“A man of mature, considered judgment.”

Also:

أَمْرٌ مُنْضَجٌ

amrun munḍajun

This can identify a matter made ready and sound through examination.

Early lexicons record this figurative use.

Its semantic relationship with cooked food is intelligible.

As a process makes food ready, thought, consultation, and assessment can make an opinion well considered.

The comparison does not mean thought is physically cooked with heat.

Naḍīj al-raʾy also does not guarantee that every decision the person makes is correct.

It describes considered, mature judgment rather than infallibility.

This branch is absent from the Qur’an and should not be transferred to Al-Nisāʾ 4:56.

E. An analysis of the root’s core

One early lexical analysis explains N Ḍ J as:

أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى بُلُوغِ النِّهَايَةِ فِي طَبْخِ الشَّيْءِ

aṣlun yadullu ʿalā bulūghi al-nihāyati fī ṭabkhi al-shayʾi

Meaning:

“A root indicating reaching completion in cooking something.”

The analysis then extends the family to things reaching a stage of soundness and completion.

This helps explain the relationship between cooked meat, ripe fruit, and considered judgment.

Two cautions nevertheless remain.

First, completion here concerns the relevant cooked condition, rather than necessarily the material’s total destruction.

Second, proposing a common core does not itself establish the actual history of every meaning.

Cooking and ripening can therefore provide the family’s core, while the subject and context determine each use’s precise features.

3. Pregnancy and reaching the time of birth

Early Arabic also applies N Ḍ J to a pregnant animal.

Naḍijat al-nāqatu bi-waladihā describes a she-camel past her usual time of birth that has not yet delivered.

The expected time being reached or exceeded is prominent. Form II naḍḍajat is also recorded and appears in the following poetic witnesses; it differs morphologically from Qur’anic naḍijat in Al-Nisāʾ 4:56.

Some lexical explanations associate this with more complete fetal development.

Two issues nevertheless differ:

Passing the expected delivery time is a temporal condition.

Fetal completeness or health is a separate matter not invariably established by the word itself.

A verse attributed to Ḥumayd ibn Thawr

A poem attributed to Ḥumayd ibn Thawr, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says:

وَصَهْبَاءَ مِنْهَا كَالسَّفِينَةِ نَضَّجَتْ
بِهِ الْحَمْلَ حَتَّى زَادَ شَهْرًا عَدِيدُهَا

wa-ṣahbāʾa minhā ka-al-safīnati naḍḍajat / bihi al-ḥamla ḥattā zāda shahran ʿadīduhā

Approximate sense:

“A reddish she-camel among them, like a great ship, whose pregnancy continued until its count exceeded the expected term by a month.”

The root concerns a she-camel’s pregnancy here.

Neither cooked meat nor ripe fruit is the subject.

Reaching or passing an expected time can nevertheless relate semantically to maturity in the family’s other uses.

Differences in wording and attribution

Sources transmit differing versions of this verse.

Some begin wa-ṣahbāʾa or li-ṣahbāʾa and attribute the verse to Ḥumayd ibn Thawr.

Other transmissions begin li-admāʾa and attribute it to Al-Ḥuṭayʾah.

Some versions have bihi al-ḥamla, others bihi al-ḥawla.

Neither one wording nor one attribution should therefore be presented as undisputed.

The example attests the pregnancy use, while identifying the original text and poet requires examination of manuscripts and transmission.

Ḥumayd and Al-Ḥuṭayʾah both lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods; this verse’s precise composition is therefore not securely dated before the Qur’an.

A verse from ʿUwayf al-Qawāfī

The Umayyad-period poet ʿUwayf al-Qawāfī says of she-camels whose deliveries were delayed:

هُوَ ابْنُ مُنَضِّجَاتٍ كُنَّ قِدْمًا
يَزِدْنَ عَلَى الْعَدِيدِ قِرَابَ شَهْرِ

huwa ibnu munaḍḍijātin kunna qidman / yazidna ʿalā al-ʿadīdi qirāba shahri

Translation:

“He is the offspring of she-camels that, in earlier times, exceeded the reckoned term by nearly a month.”

Munaḍḍijāt here belongs to Form II and describes she-camels past their usual delivery time, rather than the Form IV shape munḍijāt.

The example supports this Arabic poetic usage.

Its poet lived after the Qur’an’s revelation, so it attests classical Arabic rather than independently attesting pre-Islamic usage.

The family could therefore extend beyond food and fruit to the passage of time and maturity.

That does not make naḍijat julūduhum in the Qur’an mean that the skins’ appointed time has passed.

The verse concerns skin and fire, rather than pregnancy and birth.

4. The Qur’anic use: Al-Nisāʾ 4:56

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِآيَاتِنَا سَوْفَ نُصْلِيهِمْ نَارًا كُلَّمَا نَضِجَتْ جُلُودُهُمْ بَدَّلْنَاهُمْ جُلُودًا غَيْرَهَا لِيَذُوقُوا الْعَذَابَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَزِيزًا حَكِيمًا

inna alladhīna kafarū bi-āyātinā sawfa nuṣlīhim nāran kullamā naḍijat julūduhum baddalnāhum julūdan ghayrahā li-yadhūqū al-ʿadhāba inna allāha kāna ʿazīzan ḥakīman

Translation:

“Those who disbelieve in Our signs: We will soon expose them to a fire. Whenever their skins become seared, We replace them with other skins so that they taste the punishment. The God is Mighty, invincible, and Wise.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:56

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Several neighboring verbs and nouns help identify its meaning.

A. Nuṣlīhim nāran: the fire setting

The verse begins:

سَوْفَ نُصْلِيهِمْ نَارًا

sawfa nuṣlīhim nāran

This describes bringing people into or exposing them to burning fire.

Nuṣlīhim belongs to Ṣ L Y, rather than N Ḍ J.

The distinction is essential.

Nuḍj describes the skin’s change in condition.

Nuṣlīhim nāran supplies the fire and exposure to it.

Nuḍj alone therefore should not mean “putting into fire.”

Given that exposure, the skin’s nuḍj means becoming seared or burned by heat, rather than naturally ripening like fruit.

B. Kullamā naḍijat julūduhum

The expression means:

“Whenever their skins reach the condition described as nuḍj.”

Kullamā indicates recurrence whenever the condition is fulfilled.

Naḍijat is a Form I perfect verb.

Julūduhum is its subject.

Julūd is grammatically a nonhuman plural, for which feminine singular verbal agreement is natural in Arabic.

The feminine singular verb therefore does not make the verse concern only one skin.

It concerns the skins of the people being discussed.

Naḍijat in this setting can be explained as:

The skins have become seared and burned through the fire’s heat.

An early explanation glosses it with inshawat and iḥtaraqat: the skins became roasted and burned.

That fits the lexical roasting use and the fire context.

Seared skin nevertheless differs from complete destruction of the skin.

Nuḍj alone does not establish that no particle remains or every component becomes ash.

The verse describes skins reaching a burned condition and then their replacement.

C. Is “becoming cooked” suitable here?

Etymologically, “becoming cooked” approaches nuḍj’s general meaning.

In Persian, however, “the skin became cooked” can sound unfamiliar or insufficient for human skin in fire.

“Became seared” and “burned through fire” are more natural here.

“Became seared” stays closer to the Arabic image by retaining the relationship between thermal cooking and burning.

“Whenever their skins are completely destroyed” adds complete destruction beyond the word’s direct meaning.

“Whenever their skins ripen” could evoke ripe fruit and misrepresent the verse in Persian.

The equivalent should therefore fit the subject of skin and fire.

5. Baddalnāhum julūdan ghayrahā: replacing the skins and continuing punishment

After naḍijat julūduhum, the verse says:

بَدَّلْنَاهُمْ جُلُودًا غَيْرَهَا

baddalnāhum julūdan ghayrahā

Translation:

“We replace their skins with other skins.”

This expression does not belong to N Ḍ J.

Baddalnā belongs to B D L and concerns changing or replacing.

Julūdan belongs to J L D and identifies skins.

Study of J L D

Ghayrahā marks the replacement skins as other than the preceding ones.

Two distinct events are therefore described:

First, the skins become seared through fire.

Then other skins replace them.

Translation should preserve the distinction.

A. Are the replacement skins entirely new?

Julūdan ghayrahā indicates that the replacement skins are not the burned skins in their previous condition.

How this replacement occurs raises further questions.

Is another skin brought into existence?

Does the same skin enter a new condition after transformation and reconstruction?

Does the expression provide another image of continuing punishment?

The commentarial tradition offers different views.

One explanation observes that Arabic can call something “another thing” after reshaping or reconstruction, even when it remains connected with the earlier material.

It illustrates this through a ring broken and remade.

Other commentators describe replacement with fresh skins.

The difference concerns identity and replacement, rather than the meaning of nuḍj itself.

The wording describes replacement without detailing stages of skin formation or reconstruction.

A particular theory of cellular regeneration or recreating skin should therefore not be inserted directly into the translation.

B. Does the person experiencing punishment change?

The personal pronouns remain consistent through the verse:

نُصْلِيهِمْ

nuṣlīhim

جُلُودُهُمْ

julūduhum

بَدَّلْنَاهُمْ

baddalnāhum

لِيَذُوقُوا

li-yadhūqū

The people threatened at the beginning remain the ones tasting punishment at the end.

Replacing the skin therefore does not mean the text substitutes someone completely unrelated to the first person.

The philosophical relationship between human identity, the body, and replaced components nevertheless goes beyond a lexical study of N Ḍ J.

The verse maintains the identity of those punished without explaining every philosophical or physical detail.

C. Li-yadhūqū al-ʿadhāba: the purpose of replacement

The continuation says:

لِيَذُوقُوا الْعَذَابَ

li-yadhūqū al-ʿadhāba

“So that they taste the punishment.”

Yadhūqū belongs to DH W Q.

Its basic sense is tasting, but Qur’anic use also concerns experiencing punishment, mercy, and other conditions.

The stated purpose of replacing the skins is experiencing punishment.

Tasting punishment does not make it necessarily received through the tongue or the gustatory sense.

Arabic uses the expression for experiencing and feeling.

Li-yadhūqū alone does not explain pain perception, neural anatomy, or the precise location where pain signals are received.

Those bodily questions require independent evidence rather than being added to the verse’s meaning.

6. Does the verse concern skin nerves and pain sensation?

Some contemporary writings infer that replacing burned skins in Al-Nisāʾ 4:56 identifies skin pain receptors as a scientific fact.

Several levels of explanation should be distinguished.

First, the verse names skin, fire, searing, replacement, and tasting punishment.

Second, modern medicine identifies neural structures in the skin and underlying tissues involved in detecting harmful stimuli. Severe thermal injury can destroy nerve endings and alter sensation in the affected area.

Third, pain experience is not restricted to skin; neural networks, signaling pathways, and the central nervous system contribute.

The scientific information therefore does not make all pain originate within skin itself or every burn, regardless of depth and circumstances, completely insensate.

The verse also does not name nerves, pain receptors, neural signals, or the brain.

Thus a connection with suffering and the skin’s role is intelligible, but turning the verse into a precise neuroscientific theory goes beyond its wording and context.

It also describes an afterlife condition whose every feature should not be equated with present human biology without evidence.

For the root study, the relevant point is that the skins become seared in fire and are replaced for the continued experience of punishment.

7. Comparison with related verses and roots

A. N Ḍ J and Ṣ L Y: thermal change and exposure to fire

Both families appear together in Al-Nisāʾ 4:56.

Nuṣlīhim belongs to Ṣ L Y and concerns exposure to fire and enduring its heat.

Naḍijat belongs to N Ḍ J and describes the skin’s change under heat.

Ṣ L Y supplies the fire setting and N Ḍ J the resulting condition here.

That does not make them synonymous.

Arabic also uses nuḍj for ripe fruit, a use not necessarily involving fire.

Study of Ṣ L Y

B. N Ḍ J and Ḥ R Q: cooking and burning

Ḥ R Q concerns burning and setting something on fire.

Food-related N Ḍ J concerns becoming cooked, while skin in fire can become seared or burned.

The two roots therefore approach each other in the outcome described in Al-Nisāʾ 4:56.

Nuḍj nevertheless does not invariably mean burning.

Ripe fruit may never have been exposed to fire.

“Burning” therefore fits this verse’s context without defining the whole N Ḍ J family.

Study of Ḥ R Q

C. N Ḍ J and Ṣ H R: searing and melting

Al-Ḥajj 22:20 says:

يُصْهَرُ بِهِ مَا فِي بُطُونِهِمْ وَالْجُلُودُ

yuṣharu bihi mā fī buṭūnihim wa-al-julūdu

Translation:

“What is in their bellies, and their skins, are melted by it.”

This also concerns punishment and intense heat.

Yuṣharu belongs to Ṣ H R and concerns melting.

Naḍijat in Al-Nisāʾ 4:56, by contrast, describes skin becoming seared or cooked through fire.

The images are close without being identical.

Melting is more prominent in ṣahr.

Nuḍj concerns material reaching a heat-induced cooked condition.

Both should not be translated indiscriminately as “being destroyed.”

Study of Ṣ H R

D. N Ḍ J and B D L: a changed condition and replacement

Al-Nisāʾ 4:56 places these verbs in sequence.

Nuḍj concerns the existing skins’ change in condition.

Tabdīl concerns replacing them with other skins.

The distinction matters.

Something can change condition without being replaced.

Something can also be replaced without first becoming seared in fire.

Both events occur in this verse, but their meanings remain independent.

Study of B D L

E. N Ḍ J and Y N ʿ: ripening fruit

Al-Anʿām 6:99 says:

انْظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ

unẓurū ilā thamarihi idhā athmara wa-yanʿihi

Translation:

“Look at its fruit when it bears fruit, and at its ripening.”

Yanʿihi belongs to Y N ʿ and concerns fruit ripening.

Early lexical explanations gloss yanʿ through nuḍj and reaching maturity.

The families therefore overlap in fruit-related uses.

Qur’anic Y N ʿ nevertheless describes ripening fruit, while Qur’anic N Ḍ J occurs only for skin seared in fire.

Their semantic proximity in Arabic does not justify substituting one in the other’s verse.

F. N Ḍ J and N Ḍ R: maturity and freshness

The roots have similar consonants but independent meanings and uses.

N Ḍ J concerns cooking and maturity.

N Ḍ R concerns freshness, vitality, and radiance.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:22 says:

وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَاضِرَةٌ

wujūhun yawmaʾidhin nāḍiratun

“Faces that day are fresh and radiant.”

The description belongs to N Ḍ R, rather than N Ḍ J.

Nāḍirah should therefore not mean cooked or seared.

Nāḍij, cooked or ripe, likewise belongs to N Ḍ J rather than the family of freshness and radiance.

G. N Ḍ J and N Ḍ KH: maturity and gushing

Al-Raḥmān 55:66 says:

فِيهِمَا عَيْنَانِ نَضَّاخَتَانِ

fīhimā ʿaynāni naḍḍākhatāni

“In both are two gushing springs.”

Naḍḍākhatān belongs to N Ḍ KH.

That root concerns water gushing and bursting forth.

N Ḍ J concerns cooking and ripening.

Similar consonants should not cause the families to be confused.

H. N Ḍ J and KH L Q: mature material and creation

Qur’anic KH L Q concerns creation and formation.

Qur’anic N Ḍ J describes skin changing under heat.

Nuḍj itself therefore does not mean creating fresh skin.

The independent baddalnāhum supplies replacement in Al-Nisāʾ 4:56.

Creation or reconstruction should not automatically be added to nuḍj itself.

Study of KH L Q

8. Al-Nisāʾ 4:56 beside other verses about punishment and skin

A. Al-Nisāʾ 4:55, Al-Nisāʾ 4:56, and Al-Nisāʾ 4:57: two outcomes

Al-Nisāʾ 4:55 says:

فَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ آمَنَ بِهِ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ صَدَّ عَنْهُ وَكَفَىٰ بِجَهَنَّمَ سَعِيرًا

fa-minhum man āmana bihi wa-minhum man ṣadda ʿanhu wa-kafā bi-jahannama saʿīran

Translation:

“Some of them believed in him, while others turned away from him; Hell suffices as a blazing fire.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:56 then describes the outcome for those who disbelieve in divine signs.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:57 says:

وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ سَنُدْخِلُهُمْ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ

wa-alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti sa-nudkhiluhum jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru

Translation:

“Those who believe and do good works: We will admit them to gardens beneath which rivers flow.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:57, opening excerpt

The context places the nuḍj verse within warning and judgment about human conduct.

Nuḍj’s lexical meaning is nevertheless neither disbelief nor punishment.

Disbelief, faith, and human responsibility require separate examination of their roots and related verses.

B. Al-Isrāʾ 17:97: continuing fire

Al-Isrāʾ 17:97 says:

كُلَّمَا خَبَتْ زِدْنَاهُمْ سَعِيرًا

kullamā khabat zidnāhum saʿīran

Translation:

“Whenever it subsides, We increase the blaze for them.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, closing excerpt

Khabat belongs to KH B W and describes fire subsiding.

The image approaches Al-Nisāʾ 4:56 in its depiction of continuing punishment.

Their direct subjects nevertheless differ.

Al-Isrāʾ describes the subsiding and increasing blaze.

Al-Nisāʾ describes skins becoming seared and being replaced.

Khubūw and nuḍj should therefore not be treated as synonyms.

Study of KH B W

C. Fuṣṣilat 41:20, Fuṣṣilat 41:21, and Fuṣṣilat 41:22: skin and testimony about deeds

Fuṣṣilat 41:20 says:

شَهِدَ عَلَيْهِمْ سَمْعُهُمْ وَأَبْصَارُهُمْ وَجُلُودُهُمْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

shahida ʿalayhim samʿuhum wa-abṣāruhum wa-julūduhum bi-mā kānū yaʿmalūna

Translation:

“Their hearing, their eyes, and their skins testify against them about what they used to do.”

Skin is again a subject, but the verb naḍija does not appear.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:56 places skin within punishment and replacement.

Fuṣṣilat describes skins testifying about deeds.

The Qur’an can therefore discuss the same body part in different contexts.

Combining the passages does not make skin independently possess every memory or bear responsibility for every act apart from the person.

The medical or physical mechanisms of testimony or replacement also should not be supplied without evidence.

9. Translation questions for Forqan

The key distinction is between the general meaning of maturity and the particular use for skin in fire.

Persian “ripened” alone can suggest fruit ripening.

“Became cooked” retains the lexical meaning but may not sufficiently communicate the skin-in-fire image.

“Was completely destroyed” adds something not required by the root.

“Became seared” is therefore concise, natural, and contextually suitable in Al-Nisāʾ 4:56.

“Burned” is defensible in context, but the lexical study should retain the difference between nuḍj and ḥarq.

كُلَّمَا نَضِجَتْ جُلُودُهُمْ بَدَّلْنَاهُمْ جُلُودًا غَيْرَهَا

kullamā naḍijat julūduhum baddalnāhum julūdan ghayrahā

Proposed rendering:

“Whenever their skins become seared, We replace them with other skins.”

This preserves three points:

First, recurrence through kullamā.

Second, the skins becoming seared through naḍijat.

Third, replacing them with other skins through baddalnāhum julūdan ghayrahā.

Replacement, fire, and tasting punishment should not become N Ḍ J’s independent meaning; other words supply each concept.

Proposed Forqan word box

Word: نَضِجَتْ

Root: N Ḍ J

Brief root meaning: becoming cooked or ripe; reaching maturity.

Meaning in this verse: the skins became seared and burned through the fire’s heat.

The distinction prevents reducing the general meaning to burning or mistaking the verse’s particular meaning for fruit ripening.

10. Conclusion

Arabic N Ḍ J concerns reaching a stage of cooking or maturity.

Meat and food become cooked through heat; fruit ripens through natural growth.

Figuratively, thought and judgment can also be mature and considered.

Another recorded use describes a she-camel’s pregnancy passing its usual delivery time.

Poetry attributed to Ḥumayd ibn Thawr and ʿUwayf al-Qawāfī illustrates this branch, although one verse’s wording and attribution are disputed.

The lexical analysis discussed above foregrounds completion in cooking. This helps connect the uses, but does not make nuḍj invariably a desirable or beneficial outcome.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an:

Naḍijat in Al-Nisāʾ 4:56.

The skins of people who disbelieve in divine signs are exposed to fire; whenever they become seared, other skins replace them so they taste punishment.

Nuḍj here consequently concerns the skins becoming roasted or burned through heat.

The word itself does not establish complete destruction, conversion to ash, or reconstruction through a specified biological process.

The verse concerns skin’s role in experiencing punishment without explaining pain perception’s neuroscientific mechanisms.

Nuḍj, tabdīl, and dhawq are distinct concepts in the sentence.

Nuḍj describes the skin’s change under heat.

Tabdīl expresses replacement.

Dhawq concerns experiencing punishment.

Translation and reflection should preserve these distinctions.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N Ḍ J concerns something reaching a cooked, ripe, or mature condition. Heat, natural growth, or the passage of time can produce it; depending on the subject, it describes cooked food, ripe fruit, considered judgment, or another condition.

Qur’anic naḍijat in Al-Nisāʾ 4:56 describes skins becoming seared and burned in fire.

Thus reaching maturity is the family’s general core, while becoming seared through fire is its particular meaning in the verse.

The root alone does not identify complete skin destruction, the manner of replacement, or scientific details of pain sensation; those questions require other sentence elements or independent evidence.
