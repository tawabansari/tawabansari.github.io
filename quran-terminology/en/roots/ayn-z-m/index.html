Firm resolve, settling doubt, seriousness in carrying out a task, and perseverance in a decision

The Arabic root ʿ Z M concerns a decision that goes beyond wishing, considering, or inclining toward something: a person becomes firmly resolved to act. This family connects resolve with seriousness, decisive intention, setting aside hesitation, and perseverance in pursuing a task.

The Qur’an uses the root both for ordinary human decisions and for firmness in demanding conduct. Decisions concerning divorce, contracting marriage, deciding after consultation, fidelity to a commitment, patience under harm, and forgiving others all occur in its contexts.

This range reveals an important point: resolve is not inherently good or bad. A person may resolve upon something right or wrong. The decision’s value comes from its object, motive, and consequences, rather than determination alone.

Resolve is related to wanting, deciding, acting, and persevering, but these concepts are distinct. Someone may want something without deciding to pursue it, decide without acting, or begin an action and subsequently abandon the decision.

The Qur’an’s different uses of this root bring out these distinctions.

Forms of ʿ Z M occur in nine verses. Four occurrences are verbs: ʿazamū, taʿzimū, ʿazamta, and ʿazama. Five are instances of the verbal noun ʿazm: three in ʿazmi al-umūr, one in wa-lam najid lahu ʿazman, and one in the title ulū al-ʿazm.

Other forms, including ʿazīmah, ʿāzim, iʿtazama, taʿazzama, and ʿazmah, are known in Arabic, but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Resolve: settling hesitation

The expressions al-ṣarīmah and al-qaṭʿ are used to explain the semantic core of ʿ Z M. Here “cutting” concerns settling hesitation and making a decision definite, rather than physically severing an object.

One early definition of resolve reads:

الْعَزْمُ مَا عُقِدَ عَلَيْهِ الْقَلْبُ مِنْ أَمْرٍ أَنْتَ فَاعِلُهُ

al-ʿazmu mā ʿuqida ʿalayhi al-qalbu min amrin anta fāʿiluhu

Meaning: resolve is that upon whose performance the heart has become firmly set.

This distinguishes inclination from firm decision. Someone may consider an action, be interested in it, or even prefer it while still hesitating. Resolve becomes applicable when the decision to act is firmly established.

Arabic uses the following expressions:

ʿAzama ʿalā al-amr: he resolved to carry out a task.

ʿAzama al-amr: he decided firmly upon the matter.

Iʿtazama al-amr: he decided to undertake the matter and became firmly committed to it.

Arabic ʿazama can thus take either ʿalā or a direct object. The Qur’an has direct objects in Al-Baqarah 2:227 and Al-Baqarah 2:235; in Āl ʿImrān 3:159, the object of the decision is understood from context. The explicit construction ʿazama ʿalā, however, does not occur in the Qur’an.

ʿAzamtu ʿalayka also conveys emphatic insistence: a person earnestly asks another to act or adjures that person to do so.

This is related to personal determination, but describes a different situation. In one case someone establishes a personal decision; in the other, a request is pressed upon another person.

Resolve and firmness in action

Early Arabic also explains ʿazm through jidd: seriousness and determined engagement with a task.

ʿAzīmah likewise denotes firm decision and a determination to which a person remains committed.

For someone who keeps wavering after deciding or cannot maintain a decision, Arabic uses mā lahu ʿazīmah: that person lacks the necessary resolve and firmness.

Resolve is therefore not confined to the moment of choice. In some situations, maintaining and continuing with a decision also belongs to this semantic field.

This does not mean that every firm resolve necessarily results in completion. External obstacles, changed circumstances, or personal weakness may prevent a resolute person from reaching the intended outcome.

Decision and achieved outcome must therefore be distinguished.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes a generous, steadfast man:

فَأَقْصَرْنَ مِنْهُ عَنْ كَرِيمٍ مُرَزَّإٍ
عَزُومٍ عَلَى الْأَمْرِ الَّذِي هُوَ فَاعِلُهُ

fa-aqṣarna minhu ʿan karīmin murazzaʾin / ʿazūmin ʿalā al-amri alladhī huwa fāʿiluhu

Translation: They ceased reproaching that noble, generous man, resolute in whatever he intended to do. Murazzaʾ here describes someone whose wealth and beneficence others enjoy, rather than merely someone who has suffered misfortune.

In the preceding verse, women alternately cherish and reproach the generous man, but ultimately cease trying to restrain him.

ʿAzūm here describes someone serious in his decision, who does not readily abandon it because of others’ reproaches.

Differences of wording are reported in the transmission of this verse. The form ʿazūm is nevertheless attested and clearly conveys the meaning under discussion.

The poet describes firmness in connection with actual conduct. Resolve is not merely an inward wish here: it appears in action and resistance to reproach.

Another witness occurs in an ode attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAbd Qays ibn Khufāf al-Burjumī:

وَاسْتَأْنِ حِلْمَكَ فِي أُمُورِكَ كُلِّهَا
وَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ عَلَى الْهَوَى فَتَوَكَّلِ

wa-staʾni ḥilmaka fī umūrika kullihā / wa-idhā ʿazamta ʿalā al-hawā fa-tawakkali

Translation: Proceed with patience and deliberation in all your affairs; once you have resolved upon your desire, act with trust.

The verse occurs in some versions of his advisory ode, but comparison of the transmissions also identifies it as an additional verse in some versions. Its attribution to the earliest form of the ode is therefore uncertain.

With that qualification, ʿazamta ʿalā illustrates a recognized Arabic construction for firm resolve.

The witness places deliberation and resolve together: first careful consideration, then decision and action. This is a useful relationship, but does not mean that every instance of ʿazm in Arabic necessarily follows consultation or adequate examination.

Later technical uses

In some legal terminology, ʿazīmah stands in contrast to rukhṣah, a concession. ʿAzāʾim has also been used for certain prayers, charms, and expressions concerned with reciting devotional formulas.

Later Arabic and the colloquial speech of some communities also use ʿazūmah for an invitation to a meal or gathering.

These uses belong to the family’s semantic history, but should not be imported into Qur’anic verses. In particular, ulū al-ʿazm is not a title for people who recite charms or use a particular legal term.

2. Resolve in human decisions

Al-Baqarah 2:227: deciding upon divorce

وَإِنْ عَزَمُوا الطَّلَاقَ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

wa-in ʿazamū al-ṭalāqa fa-inna allāha samīʿun ʿalīm

Translation: If they resolve upon divorce, then The God is Hearing, Knowing.

ʿAzamū is a plural past verb, and al-ṭalāqa is its direct object.

Here resolve concerns a specific decision: ending a marital relationship through divorce.

The preceding verse, Al-Baqarah 2:226, concerns those who swear to abstain from their wives. It mentions four months of waiting and provides for returning.

Al-Baqarah 2:227 then speaks of resolving upon divorce.

Together, the verses present an important decision: returning to the relationship or becoming firmly set on separation.

The important lexical distinction is that resolving upon divorce and divorce taking place are different concepts. Resolve concerns the decision; divorce names the relevant action or legal event.

The root ʿ Z M alone does not establish that an inward decision, without any further words or action, automatically produces every legal consequence of divorce. That question requires examining the complete ruling and its relevant expressions.

Nor is resolve necessarily praise of an action. A decision to divorce can arise in different circumstances, depending on its grounds and the parties’ conduct. The word ʿazm itself expresses firmness of decision.

Al-Baqarah 2:235: resolve concerning a marriage contract

Al-Baqarah 2:235 states:

وَلَا تَعْزِمُوا عُقْدَةَ النِّكَاحِ حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ الْكِتَابُ أَجَلَهُ

wa-lā taʿzimū ʿuqdata al-nikāḥi ḥattā yablugha al-kitābu ajalah

Translation: Do not make the marriage bond definite and binding until the prescribed period reaches its end.

Taʿzimū is an imperfect verb in a prohibitive construction, marked as jussive; ʿuqdata al-nikāḥ specifies what the resolve concerns.

ʿAzama al-amr is an established Arabic construction. The preposition ʿalā need not be explicitly present for the sense of deciding to be understood.

Two closely related explanations are offered here: prohibition of a definite, binding commitment to contract marriage during the waiting period, and prohibition of actually establishing the contract before that period ends. Since this same verse permits keeping an inclination within oneself, the prohibition should not be understood as a ban on merely deciding inwardly to marry in the future. Making the contract definite and effective before the waiting period ends is central to the expression.

The distinction matters for the precise ruling, but both readings concern preventing premature contracting of marriage within the prescribed period.

The verse’s opening denies blame for indirect proposals or concealed inclination. It subsequently places restrictions on secret promises and establishing the contract.

Wa-lā taʿzimū should therefore not be turned into a prohibition of every feeling of affection, thought, or possibility of future marriage.

The passage distinguishes inclination and considering a possibility from advancing the decision to a stage that conflicts with the existing temporal and legal restriction.

Here resolve conveys something beyond simple inclination: serious determination concerning a specific action.

Āl ʿImrān 3:159: deciding after consultation

Āl ʿImrān 3:159 states:

فَبِمَا رَحْمَةٍ مِّنَ اللَّهِ لِنتَ لَهُمْ وَلَوْ كُنتَ فَظًّا غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ لَانفَضُّوا مِنْ حَوْلِكَ فَاعْفُ عَنْهُمْ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُتَوَكِّلِينَ

fa-bi-mā raḥmatin mina allāhi linta lahum wa-law kunta faẓẓan ghalīẓa al-qalbi la-infaḍḍū min ḥawlika fa-ʿfu ʿanhum wa-staghfir lahum wa-shāwirhum fī al-amri fa-idhā ʿazamta fa-tawakkal ʿalā allāhi inna allāha yuḥibbu al-mutawakkilīn

Translation: Through a mercy from The God you became gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you. Pardon them, seek forgiveness for them, and consult them in the matter. Then, when you have decided, place your trust in The God. The God loves those who trust in Him.

Resolve follows three important behaviors in this verse: pardon, seeking forgiveness, and consultation.

Wa-shāwirhum fī al-amr commands consultation in the matter; fa-idhā ʿazamta then introduces the stage of decision.

Consultation and resolve are distinct concepts. Consultation enables hearing opinions and assessing an issue, while resolve concerns establishing a firm decision about it.

Resolve is followed by the command fa-tawakkal ʿalā allāh: place your trust in The God.

The verse thus places three related concepts together: consultation, deciding, and trust.

This sequence does not make ʿazm mean “a decision after consultation” in every Arabic use. That feature comes from the context of Āl ʿImrān 3:159.

Trust also does not replace decision and action. The verse does not tell people to refrain from deciding and merely await an outcome; it introduces trust after resolve.

Here resolve marks the transition from consultation to firm decision. The word alone does not imply that decisions must never be revised in light of new evidence. Firmness in a decision differs from stubborn adherence to an exposed error.

3. Resolve in action: firmness, patience, and fidelity

Āl ʿImrān 3:186: perseverance under pressure

لَتُبْلَوُنَّ فِي أَمْوَالِكُمْ وَأَنْفُسِكُمْ وَلَتَسْمَعُنَّ مِنَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ مِن قَبْلِكُمْ وَمِنَ الَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا أَذًى كَثِيرًا وَإِن تَصْبِرُوا وَتَتَّقُوا فَإِنَّ ذَٰلِكَ مِنْ عَزْمِ الْأُمُورِ

la-tublawunna fī amwālikum wa-anfusikum wa-la-tasmaʿunna mina alladhīna ūtū al-kitāba min qablikum wa-mina alladhīna ashrakū adhan kathīran wa-in taṣbirū wa-tattaqū fa-inna dhālika min ʿazmi al-umūr

Translation: You will certainly be tested in your possessions and yourselves, and will hear much harm from those given scripture before you and from those who associate partners. If you persevere and remain mindful, that is among matters requiring firm resolve.

Here ʿazm occurs in the expression ʿazmi al-umūr.

The subject is the human response to hardship, harm, and pressure. The commended response is more than enduring pressure: the verse joins patience with mindfulness.

Patience concerns holding oneself steady under pressure; mindfulness concerns the direction and standard of conduct.

In this context, resolve thus concerns firmness in considered, responsible behavior.

If someone abandons ethical standards under harm and turns to aggression or misconduct, resistance and obstinacy alone do not constitute the conduct praised here.

Min ʿazmi al-umūr may be understood as matters requiring firm determination and seriousness. Its positive value here comes from the conduct involved and its association with patience and mindfulness.

Luqmān 31:17: firmness in responsibility

Luqmān 31:17 states:

يَا بُنَيَّ أَقِمِ الصَّلَاةَ وَأْمُرْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَانْهَ عَنِ الْمُنكَرِ وَاصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا أَصَابَكَ إِنَّ ذَٰلِكَ مِنْ عَزْمِ الْأُمُورِ

yā bunayya aqimi al-ṣalāta wa-ʾmur bi-al-maʿrūfi wa-nha ʿani al-munkari wa-ṣbir ʿalā mā aṣābaka inna dhālika min ʿazmi al-umūr

Translation: My son, establish ṣalāh, enjoin what is right, forbid what is wrong, and persevere through what befalls you. That is among matters requiring firm resolve.

These words belong to Luqmān’s advice to his son.

Resolve is connected here with practical responsibilities: establishing ṣalāh, enjoining what is right, forbidding what is wrong, and persevering through what befalls a person.

Inna dhālika min ʿazmi al-umūr concludes this series of instructions. It can refer to the instructions as a whole, particularly the perseverance required to carry them out.

This goes beyond a purely inward decision. A person must also remain steadfast through difficulties in performing an accepted responsibility.

The advice continues in Luqmān 31:18 with prohibitions of arrogance, turning one’s face proudly from others, and walking ostentatiously. Firmness in responsibility should thus not be accompanied by self-importance or contemptuous conduct.

ʿAzmu al-umūr here does not mean violence, unlimited severity, or imposing personal wishes on others.

Al-Shūrā 42:43: resolve in patience and forgiveness

Al-Shūrā 42:43 states:

وَلَمَن صَبَرَ وَغَفَرَ إِنَّ ذَٰلِكَ لَمِنْ عَزْمِ الْأُمُورِ

wa-la-man ṣabara wa-ghafara inna dhālika la-min ʿazmi al-umūr

Translation: Whoever perseveres and forgives—that is indeed among matters requiring firm resolve.

This is one of the Qur’an’s clearest ethical uses of resolve.

Al-Shūrā 42:40, Al-Shūrā 42:41, and Al-Shūrā 42:42 address proportionate response to wrongdoing, the virtue of pardon and reconciliation, and the responsibility of those who wrong others.

Al-Shūrā 42:40 states:

وَجَزَاءُ سَيِّئَةٍ سَيِّئَةٌ مِّثْلُهَا فَمَنْ عَفَا وَأَصْلَحَ فَأَجْرُهُ عَلَى اللَّهِ

wa-jazāʾu sayyiʾatin sayyiʾatun mithluhā fa-man ʿafā wa-aṣlaḥa fa-ajruhu ʿalā allāh

Translation: The recompense of an evil is an evil like it. Whoever pardons and brings about reconciliation has a reward with The God.

Al-Shūrā 42:41 also does not present seeking redress after being wronged as inherently blameworthy.

Al-Shūrā 42:43 then places patience and forgiveness among ʿazmu al-umūr.

Resolve therefore appears not only in standing firm to obtain one’s rights, but sometimes also in restraining a reaction and knowingly choosing forgiveness.

Someone capable of responding may assess the circumstances and choose reconciliation and pardon. Such a decision may require determination and firmness.

The verse does not mean that every wronged person must surrender their rights in every situation or never act to obtain justice.

This same context recognizes seeking redress for wrongdoing and the importance of preventing aggression.

Resolve in Al-Shūrā 42:43 concerns firmness in commended patience and forgiveness, rather than compulsory silence before injustice.

Forqan’s study of Ṣ B R examines patience, restraint of reactions, and perseverance in what is right.

4. Adam’s commitment and the absence of resolve

Ṭā Hā 20:115 states:

وَلَقَدْ عَهِدْنَا إِلَىٰ آدَمَ مِن قَبْلُ فَنَسِيَ وَلَمْ نَجِدْ لَهُ عَزْمًا

wa-la-qad ʿahidnā ilā ādama min qablu fa-nasiya wa-lam najid lahu ʿazmā

Translation: We had previously entrusted Adam with a commitment, but he forgot; and We found no firm resolve in him.

This verse is especially important for understanding ʿ Z M because it connects resolve with a commitment and forgetfulness.

ʿAhidnā concerns an earlier commitment or instruction entrusted to Adam. Fa-nasiya describes forgetting or neglecting it; wa-lam najid lahu ʿazman states the absence of resolve.

As the narrative continues, Satan tempts Adam in Ṭā Hā 20:120. Ṭā Hā 20:121 describes Adam and his wife eating from the tree, and Adam’s disobedience and lapse.

Ṭā Hā 20:115 cannot therefore be translated as though no error occurred. It mentions the prior commitment, forgetfulness, and the absence of resolve.

Wa-lam najid lahu ʿazman does not mean that Adam lacked all will, decision-making capacity, or responsibility throughout his life.

The absence of resolve concerns this particular commitment and its preservation. Adam did not show the required firmness regarding what had been entrusted to him.

Nor does the expression imply that, from the outset, he had firmly intended disobedience and resolved to break the commitment. Such an intention does not follow from the word ʿazm itself.

Insufficient firmness in maintaining a commitment must be distinguished from a deliberate resolve to violate it.

Ṭā Hā 20:122 also states that his Lord chose Adam, accepted his return, and guided him.

A lapse and lack of resolve in a particular situation do not eliminate the possibility of reform and return.

Forqan’s study of ʿ H D examines commitment, responsibility, preservation, and accountability. Resolve can be understood here as one capacity needed for practical fidelity to a commitment; ʿahd and ʿazm, however, are distinct words.

Study of ʿ H D

ʿAhd expresses an entrusted or accepted responsibility; ʿazm concerns firmness in deciding and maintaining that responsibility.

5. Ulū al-ʿazm: the steadfastness of messengers

Al-Aḥqāf 46:35 states:

فَاصْبِرْ كَمَا صَبَرَ أُولُو الْعَزْمِ مِنَ الرُّسُلِ وَلَا تَسْتَعْجِل لَّهُمْ كَأَنَّهُمْ يَوْمَ يَرَوْنَ مَا يُوعَدُونَ لَمْ يَلْبَثُوا إِلَّا سَاعَةً مِّن نَّهَارٍ بَلَاغٌ فَهَلْ يُهْلَكُ إِلَّا الْقَوْمُ الْفَاسِقُونَ

fa-ṣbir ka-mā ṣabara ulū al-ʿazmi mina al-rusuli wa-lā tastaʿjil lahum ka-annahum yawma yarawna mā yūʿadūna lam yalbathū illā sāʿatan min nahārin balāghun fa-hal yuhlaku illā al-qawmu al-fāsiqūn

Translation: Persevere as the messengers endowed with firm resolve persevered, and do not seek to hasten matters for them. On the day they see what they are promised, it will seem as though they had remained only an hour of a day. A proclamation! Will any but the defiantly disobedient people be destroyed?

Ulū al-ʿazm means those possessing or endowed with resolve.

Their characteristic is directly connected with ṣabara, “persevered.” The messenger is commanded to persevere as the resolute messengers persevered.

Resolve here is not merely the decision to begin a mission. It also concerns steadfastness in continuing, especially amid denial and hardship.

Wa-lā tastaʿjil lahum, “do not seek to hasten matters for them,” also connects perseverance with refraining from haste.

Resolve should therefore not be turned into rushing toward an outcome or using any means to achieve one’s desire.

Are ulū al-ʿazm only five messengers?

One well-known interpretive classification identifies five prophets as ulū al-ʿazm: Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad.

These names appear together elsewhere in the Qur’an in passages concerning the prophets’ covenant, including Al-Aḥzāb 33:7 and Al-Shūrā 42:13. However, Al-Aḥqāf 46:35 itself neither names any of ulū al-ʿazm nor specifies their number.

Linguistically, mina al-rusul has also received two analyses. If min is partitive, the resolute are a group among the messengers; if it explains the class concerned, the title can describe messengers in respect of their resolve and perseverance.

A definite list of five cannot therefore be derived solely from ʿ Z M or this verse’s construction. It requires separate Qur’anic argument.

The clear meaning is messengers possessing resolve and steadfastness, whose perseverance is presented as an example.

6. Fa-idhā ʿazama al-amr: when a matter becomes serious and definite

Muḥammad 47:21 states:

طَاعَةٌ وَقَوْلٌ مَّعْرُوفٌ فَإِذَا عَزَمَ الْأَمْرُ فَلَوْ صَدَقُوا اللَّهَ لَكَانَ خَيْرًا لَّهُمْ

ṭāʿatun wa-qawlun maʿrūfun fa-idhā ʿazama al-amru fa-law ṣadaqū allāha la-kāna khayran lahum

Translation: Obedience and fitting speech! When the matter becomes serious and definite, being true to The God would be better for them.

ʿAzama al-amr differs in construction from the verb’s usual uses.

In ʿazama al-rajul, a person is the deciding subject. Here al-amr, the matter itself, is the grammatical subject.

Arabic recognizes this construction for a matter becoming serious, settled, or definite.

One lexical explanation assigns the verb to the matter itself while the decision or obligation actually comes from those responsible for it or an issuing authority. Another understands ʿazama al-amr as the matter becoming serious and obligatory to carry out.

The preceding verse, Muḥammad 47:20, describes people asking for a surah to be revealed, whose condition changes when a decisive surah is revealed and fighting is mentioned in it.

ʿAzama al-amr here thus concerns the responsibility in question becoming serious and definite.

The verse distinguishes an expression of readiness from conduct when the actual task arrives.

The meaning of ʿazm throughout the Qur’an should not be restricted to war or fighting. Fighting belongs to this particular context, rather than being a fixed component of the root’s meaning.

The construction also shows that resolve need not always appear with a human subject. Arabic can express a matter becoming serious and definite by assigning the verb to the matter itself.

7. Distinctions from related roots

ʿ Z M (ʿazama) and R W D (arāda): wanting and firm decision

Study of R W D

Will and resolve are closely related, but not identical in every use.

Irādah can concern wanting or intending something, while ʿazm generally highlights firmness of decision and the settling of hesitation.

A person may want something without yet firmly deciding to pursue it.

This matters in marriage and divorce, where the Qur’an uses different expressions for wanting, deciding, and acting.

It does not follow that every will is weak and every resolve strong. Irādah has a much broader Qur’anic range, and its precise distinction from ʿazm requires examination in each context.

ʿ Z M (ʿazama) and H M M (hamma): initial intention and decision

Study of H M M

Hamma bi-al-shayʾ can concern intending something and turning one’s mind toward doing it.

In some uses, hamm precedes a definite decision, and the person may subsequently abandon the action.

The main use of ʿazm highlights a decision becoming firmly established.

This is a tendency, rather than a mechanically fixed boundary applying throughout Arabic texts.

ʿ Z M (ʿazama) and Ṣ B R (ṣabara): decision and perseverance

Study of Ṣ B R

The Qur’an places patience and resolve together several times.

Patience concerns holding oneself steady and persevering under pressure; resolve concerns seriousness and firmness of decision.

A person may make a right decision but need patience to maintain it in difficult circumstances.

Conversely, perseverance may support a wrong position. Resolve or patience alone therefore does not establish that its object is right.

Their connection is clear in Āl ʿImrān 3:186, Al-Shūrā 42:43, and Al-Aḥqāf 46:35, but patience and resolve are not fully synonymous.

ʿ Z M (ʿazama) and Ḥ Z M (ḥazama): resolve and prudence

Arabic ḥazm can concern firmness accompanied by assessment, planning, and caution.

ʿAzm focuses more on deciding and seriousness in pursuing the decision.

Someone may be highly determined without having assessed the consequences properly. Firmness of decision alone does not demonstrate prudence.

The command to consult before deciding in Āl ʿImrān 3:159 illustrates this distinction in a particular setting.

Ḥazm and ʿazm nevertheless overlap in Arabic. One should not be restricted entirely to thought and the other entirely to willpower.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable renderings of ʿazama and its verbal forms include “decided,” “became firmly resolved to act,” and, in some contexts, “decided firmly.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:227, ʿazamū al-ṭalāqa may be rendered “they resolved upon divorce.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:235, taʿzimū ʿuqdata al-nikāḥ concerns making the marriage bond definite and binding. “Do not make the marriage bond definite and binding” conveys the contextual sense more clearly. The prohibition’s scope requires the complete verse and the distinction between inward inclination or decision and actually contracting marriage.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:159, fa-idhā ʿazamta is simply “then, when you have decided.” Consultation and trust supply its fuller context.

In Muḥammad 47:21, fa-idhā ʿazama al-amr is better rendered “when the matter becomes serious and definite” than “when the matter decides,” since the grammatical subject is not a person.

In Ṭā Hā 20:115, wa-lam najid lahu ʿazman may be rendered “We found no firm resolve in him.” The contextual explanation identifies the issue as firmness in maintaining the commitment.

For min ʿazmi al-umūr in Āl ʿImrān 3:186, Luqmān 31:17, and Al-Shūrā 42:43, suitable renderings include “among serious, firmly established matters” or “among matters requiring firm resolve.”

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:35, ulū al-ʿazm can be rendered “those possessing resolve” or “those endowed with firm resolve.” This preserves the title’s lexical meaning without inserting a particular list of messengers into the translation without evidence.

All these uses require distinguishing decision, performance, perseverance, and their ethical value.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root ʿ Z M concerns seriousness and a decision becoming firm. Its main use describes the point at which a person goes beyond hesitation or mere wishing and establishes a decision to act.

Qur’anic resolve sometimes concerns a specific decision such as divorce or contracting marriage, sometimes accompanies consultation and trust, and sometimes describes the firmness required to maintain a commitment, perform responsibilities, persevere, and forgive.

The Qur’an also describes Adam’s lack of resolve, showing that knowing or accepting a commitment does not alone guarantee firmness in maintaining it.

ʿAzmi al-umūr concerns matters requiring serious decision and firmness; ulū al-ʿazm concerns messengers possessing this characteristic.

Resolve is distinct from being right, patience, trust, consultation, or achieving an outcome. Someone may resolutely decide upon something wrong, or decide correctly but subsequently fail to persevere.

Final definition: In its main use, ʿ Z M concerns a serious, firm decision accompanied by setting aside hesitation about acting. Extended uses can concern maintaining the decision, fidelity to a responsibility, and seriousness in carrying out a task. In ʿazama al-amr, the matter itself becomes serious and definite.

The ethical value of resolve, the correctness of a decision, and how it is carried out must all be determined from its object and context. Firmness alone guarantees neither correctness nor achievement.
