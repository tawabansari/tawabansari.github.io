The Qur’anic Root ʿ K F

Remaining in attendance, residing, and being held back

The root ع ك ف occurs nine times in the Qur’an. Its range of forms is limited but revealing: one occurrence of the verb يَعْكُفُونَ, seven of the active participle عَاكِف in its various inflections, and one of the passive participle مَعْكُوف. Neither the verb اعْتَكَفَ nor the verbal noun اعْتِكَاف occurs in the Qur’an.

These nine occurrences concern very different subjects. People remain devoted to their idols; the Israelites persist in attending the calf; Abraham asks people about their attendance upon statues; people stay in mosques; العاكف stands opposite الباد in the passage about the Sacred Mosque; and in Al-Fatḥ 48:25 the sacrificial animals are described as مَعْكُوفًا, held back.

Across these uses, remaining and maintaining an attachment to an object or place are prominent. Sometimes this takes place in a setting of worship; elsewhere the subject is residence or being prevented from proceeding.

The tangible image of the root can be seen in staying near something or remaining in a place. In its other direction, something is held and prevented from continuing on its way. Both directions are evident in the Qur’anic uses of عاكف and معكوف.

An initial definition may be stated as follows:

ʿ K F: to remain in attendance upon, near, or around something without turning away from it; and, in transitive usage, to keep or hold something back. Worship, sacredness, goodness, and evil are not supplied by the root alone: the object and context determine them.

Qur’anic forms

يَعْكُفُونَ
Form I verb; 1 occurrence

عَاكِف
Active participle; 7 occurrences

مَعْكُوف
Passive participle; 1 occurrence

Pre-Qur’anic Arabic: horses remaining beside someone

The root’s use before the Qur’an was not confined to religious settings.

In the Muʿallaqah of the pre-Qur’anic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm, after mentioning a chief whom his people have crowned, the poem says:

تَرَكْنَا الْخَيْلَ عَاكِفَةً عَلَيْهِ
مُقَلَّدَةً أَعِنَّتَهَا صُفُونَا

“We left the horses remaining beside him, their reins hanging about their necks, standing still.”

In this verse, عاكفة عليه describes the horses remaining beside the person, within a setting of confrontation and martial boasting. مُقَلَّدَةً أَعِنَّتَهَا depicts reins lying around the horses’ necks; صُفُونًا describes their standing posture, associated with a horse standing on three legs while resting the other. The verse attests a non-devotional use of عاكفة.

Poetic source: the Muʿallaqah of ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm, beginning ألا هبي بصحنك فاصبحينا, the verse beginning تركنا الخيل, in the reading عاكفة also transmitted in al-Zawzanī’s commentary. The reading عاطفة is also transmitted for this verse, so the lexical evidence here belongs specifically to the عاكفة reading. The later books serve here as sources transmitting the poem; the historical evidence is the verse attributed to the pre-Qur’anic poet.

The image in this witness is remaining in a fixed relation to a person or object. A single verse does not establish the history of every branch of the root, but it shows that the root’s range was not restricted to prayer, mosques, or worship.

Idols and the calf: persistent attendance in a context of worship

One of the most useful verses for examining the root’s meaning occurs in Abraham’s conversation with his people:

قَالُوا نَعْبُدُ أَصْنَامًا فَنَظَلُّ لَهَا عَاكِفِينَ

“They said: We worship idols and remain in attendance upon them.”

Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:71

The wording is linguistically significant. The people first say:

نَعْبُدُ أَصْنَامًا

“We worship idols.”

They then say:

فَنَظَلُّ لَهَا عَاكِفِينَ

Here نَعْبُدُ explicitly names the act of worship, while عَاكِفِينَ is the predicate of نظلّ, describing continued attendance upon the idols. The two expressions occurring together does not, by itself, prove that their meanings cannot overlap: clauses can reinforce one another. However, other uses of the root for a resident and for sacrificial animals held back show that “to worship” cannot define the entire family.

In Al-Aʿrāf, this persistent attendance appears in a narrative explicitly concerned with worship:

وَجَاوَزْنَا بِبَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ الْبَحْرَ فَأَتَوْا عَلَىٰ قَوْمٍ يَعْكُفُونَ عَلَىٰ أَصْنَامٍ لَهُمْ قَالُوا يَا مُوسَى اجْعَلْ لَنَا إِلَٰهًا كَمَا لَهُمْ آلِهَةٌ قَالَ إِنَّكُمْ قَوْمٌ تَجْهَلُونَ

“We took the Children of Israel across the sea, and they came upon a people who remained in attendance upon idols of theirs. They said: Moses, make a deity for us as they have deities. He said: You are indeed an ignorant people.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:138

يَعْكُفُونَ عَلَىٰ أَصْنَامٍ appears here in the context of idol worship, which is apparent from the narrative as a whole and from آلهة, “deities.” The contribution of يعكفون is persistent attendance and attachment. The same family describes remaining with the calf in Ṭā Hā 20:91 and 20:97, while Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:52 asks about the statues أَنْتُمْ لَهَا عَاكِفُونَ, “upon which you remain in attendance.”

Reverence and worship in these passages are understood through the object and the wider context. By contrast, العاكف in Al-Ḥajj 22:25 describes someone in terms of residence, and معكوف in Al-Fatḥ 48:25 describes sacrificial animals held back. Reverence is therefore not a constant element in every use of the family.

From the House and the mosque to ʿākif as a resident

In Al-Baqarah 2:125, Abraham and Ishmael are instructed to purify the House for several groups, including:

وَالْعَاكِفِينَ

The context is the House and worship. The عاكفون are those who remain there in attendance. Their mention alongside those circling the House, bowing, and prostrating makes the devotional setting clear; the active participle itself expresses remaining and attending.

Al-Ḥajj 22:25 brings the sense of residence into clearer focus:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَيَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَالْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ الَّذِي جَعَلْنَاهُ لِلنَّاسِ سَوَاءً الْعَاكِفُ فِيهِ وَالْبَادِ وَمَنْ يُرِدْ فِيهِ بِإِلْحَادٍ بِظُلْمٍ نُذِقْهُ مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

“Indeed, those who have disbelieved and bar access to the way of The God and the Sacred Mosque, which We have established for people, the resident there and the visitor from outside being equal in it …; and whoever intends wrongful deviation there, We shall make him taste a painful punishment.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:25

Here العاكف فيه stands opposite الباد: the person residing there and the person coming from outside. This contrast clarifies the sense “resident” for العاكف. The ellipsis in the translation reflects the unfinished opening construction إنّ الذين; nothing has been omitted from the Arabic verse quoted above.

The distinction between the people in this expression is residing and coming from outside. Here عاكف designates a resident; the kind of worship that person practices is not the basis of the contrast.

In Al-Baqarah 2:125, “those who remain in attendance at the House” likewise conveys the word’s meaning. The devotional purpose of their remaining is understood from the House, purification, bowing, prostration, and the verse as a whole.

Staying in mosques: the Qur’anic form ʿākifūn

Al-Baqarah 2:187 says: وَلَا تُبَاشِرُوهُنَّ وَأَنْتُمْ عَاكِفُونَ فِي الْمَسَاجِدِ — “And do not have sexual relations with them while you remain in attendance in the mosques.”

Here staying in mosques is accompanied by a specific rule concerning sexual relations. The devotional setting is therefore present in the Qur’an itself. The form used is also morphologically clear:

The Qur’an does not say مُعْتَكِفُونَ, and the form اعْتِكَاف does not occur in it. Its wording is عاكفون, the active participle of the basic verb عَكَفَ. The Qur’anic morphological inventory contains no Form VIII use of this root.

The absence of اعتكاف from the Qur’an does not establish when the word arose. It cannot show that the Form VIII expression, or its devotional use, necessarily originated after the Qur’an’s revelation. What can be stated directly is that this verse uses the active participle of the basic verb.

Both morphology and context contribute to understanding the expression: عاكفون describes remaining in attendance; the mosque and the rule concerning sexual relations specify the circumstances of that remaining.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 concerns remaining in mosques in a devotional setting. The expression itself does not specify a fixed duration or every detail of the practice; those details cannot be derived from the root’s three consonants.

This distinction matters especially because the same Qur’an also uses عاكفين for those who persist in attending the calf and idols.

Maʿkūf: being held back

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 says of the sacrificial animals accompanying the believers:

الْهَدْيَ مَعْكُوفًا أَنْ يَبْلُغَ مَحِلَّهُ

“The sacrificial animals, held back from reaching their destination.”

مَعْكُوف is a passive participle describing the animals as held and restrained. They have been prevented from reaching their destination; this is not voluntary attendance.

In the same verse, وَصَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ reports that the addressees were barred from the Sacred Mosque. The expression concerning the sacrificial animals occurs within this same context of obstruction and failure to reach the destination.

This use reveals the transitive branch of the root:

In intransitive use, as in يَعْكُفُونَ عَلَىٰ أَصْنَامٍ, the subject remains in attendance upon something.

In the transitive use represented by the passive participle مَعْكُوفًا, something is kept or held back.

These two uses are related. In one, the subject remains in a fixed relation to something; in the other, something is kept in that condition and prevented from proceeding.

Their semantic connection can be expressed through remaining and being made to remain. This describes their relationship in meaning; it does not, by itself, establish the historical order in which they developed.

Prepositions clarify the relation

The Qur’anic uses show why sentence structure matters when translating this root.

In يَعْكُفُونَ عَلَىٰ أَصْنَامٍ and ظَلْتَ عَلَيْهِ عَاكِفًا, على marks a close and sustained relation to an object: the person remains attached to it without turning away.

In لَهَا عَاكِفِينَ, the idols are identified as the object of that persistent attendance.

In عَاكِفُونَ فِي الْمَسَاجِدِ and الْعَاكِفُ فِيهِ, place is prominent: remaining or residing somewhere.

The preposition must be read with the verb and its object: على conveys attention and attachment toward something in these sentences, while في specifies the place of remaining. The devotional or non-devotional meaning of the whole expression comes from context.

ʿUkūf does not itself express praise or blame

One of the clearest features of this root in the Qur’an is its lack of a fixed moral evaluation.

Abraham and Ishmael purify the House for العاكفين.

People in mosques are also عاكفون.

Elsewhere, a people يعكفون upon their idols, the Israelites are عاكفين with the calf, and Abraham’s people are عاكفون in attendance upon statues.

In Al-Fatḥ, even the sacrificial animals are معكوف.

Right worship, associating partners with The God, mindfulness, sin, and even deliberate agency are therefore not constant components of the root’s meaning.

ʿUkūf describes a relation: remaining in attendance, sustained attachment, or being held in place. The value of that relation comes from its object and context.

This matters particularly in translation. Rendering عاكفين everywhere as “worshippers” or “people in devotional retreat” imports a meaning that in some passages comes from other elements of the context, and in others is not present at all.

ʿ K F alongside detention and residence

The Qur’anic uses show two related directions: residence and persistent attendance in intransitive forms, and restraint and being held in the transitive form.

This does not make عكف and حبس, “to detain,” interchangeable in every context.

In العاكف فيه, “resident” is a natural translation. The expression itself does not imply imprisonment or compulsion.

In معكوفا أن يبلغ محله, “held back” is appropriate because something has prevented the sacrificial animals from reaching their destination.

For attendance upon idols, “being imprisoned” is not a natural rendering: the point is persistent attachment and remaining.

Each form must be understood through its own sentence: residence in Al-Ḥajj 22:25, persistent attendance upon idols in Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:71, and the sacrificial animals being held back in Al-Fatḥ 48:25.

Translation proposals for Forqan

For يَعْكُفُونَ عَلَىٰ أَصْنَامٍ:

“They remain in attendance upon their idols”

Or, in more everyday English:

“They stay constantly by their idols”

These retain the root’s contribution more clearly than simply “they worship idols”; the worship is independently evident from the context.

In Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:71:

فَنَظَلُّ لَهَا عَاكِفِينَ

This can be rendered:

“And we remain in attendance upon them.”

In Ṭā Hā 20:91:

لَنْ نَبْرَحَ عَلَيْهِ عَاكِفِينَ حَتَّىٰ يَرْجِعَ إِلَيْنَا مُوسَىٰ

“We shall not cease attending it until Moses returns to us.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:125, for العاكفين:

“Those who remain in attendance at the House”

This makes the sense of remaining and attending clear. If the familiar expression “those in devotional retreat” is used, its explanation should remain tied to this Qur’anic context, without adding conditions the verse does not state.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187:

عَاكِفُونَ فِي الْمَسَاجِدِ

This can be rendered:

“You remain in attendance in the mosques”

Or, more briefly:

“You are ʿākif in the mosques”

The transliterated term then requires explanation. The devotional context is clear from the verse and can be explained separately; the first rendering does not insert “worship” as an additional word into the phrase itself.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:25:

الْعَاكِفُ فِيهِ

The most suitable rendering in context is:

“The person residing there”

Here the relevant distinction is residence, rather than participation in a devotional retreat.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:25:

الْهَدْيَ مَعْكُوفًا

“The sacrificial animals that had been held back”

Or:

“The detained sacrificial animals”

Both preserve the meaning of the passive participle.

Conclusion

In the Qur’an, ع ك ف is a family concerned with persistent attendance, remaining, and being held back.

Someone can remain near or attached to something without turning away. Thus the horses in the pre-Qur’anic poem are عاكفة, the resident at the Sacred Mosque is عاكف, and people can remain in attendance upon an idol or the calf.

In its transitive use, the same root concerns keeping and restraining. In Al-Fatḥ 48:25 the sacrificial animals are معكوف: held back from reaching their destination.

In Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:71, نَعْبُدُ names worship, while عَاكِفِينَ describes the condition of remaining in attendance upon the idols. In Al-Ḥajj 22:25, the relevant aspect of العاكف is residence. Reading these uses together clarifies the root’s semantic range.

The forms اعتكاف and معتكف do not occur in the Qur’an, but remaining in mosques in a devotional setting is expressed through عاكفون in Al-Baqarah 2:187. A form’s absence from the Qur’an does not, by itself, establish when it arose in Arabic.

ʿ K F: to remain in attendance upon something or in a place without turning away; and, in transitive usage, to keep something in place or prevent it from proceeding. This staying may be devotional or non-devotional, approved or disapproved, voluntary or imposed. Its value and direction are determined by its object and context.
