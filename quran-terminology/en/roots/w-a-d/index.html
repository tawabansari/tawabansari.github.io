Waʾd: burying a living person, covering them with earth, and depriving them of life

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Wāw–hamzah–dāl has several established Arabic uses. Its important Quranic application is burying a person alive.

Waʾada, yaʾidu, waʾdan means burying someone while they are still alive. The agent is wāʾid; the victim is mawʾūd, or feminine mawʾūdah.

The word’s familiar historical use chiefly concerns girls buried alive because of discriminatory attitudes toward daughters within families or communities.

The person is placed under earth before dying, and the act causes death. Waʾd therefore differs from burying someone already dead.

Other recorded branches include heavy movement, a tremendous sound, and slow, deliberate movement.

Some Arabic scholars explain these senses through heaviness and pressure: the buried person is weighed down by earth, while related forms describe heavy footsteps and burdensome movement.

This can illuminate connections between some uses, but should not become a certain historical origin for every meaning.

The clear, established human application of waʾd is burying someone alive. Treating “making heavy” as the common historical core of every branch requires independent evidence.

Historical and early Arabic evidence

The Umayyad poet al-Farazdaq says of a distinguished ancestor:

وَمِنَّا الَّذِي مَنَعَ الْوَائِدَاتِ

wa-minnā alladhī manaʿa al-wāʾidāti

وَأَحْيَا الْوَئِيدَ فَلَمْ يُوأَدِ

wa-aḥyā al-waʾīda fa-lam yūʾadi

“Among us was one who stopped those burying children alive and preserved the intended victim’s life, so that the child was not buried.”

He describes someone preventing this practice and rescuing children at risk of being buried alive.

Several forms of the same lexical family occur together.

Al-wāʾidāt denotes those carrying out live burial.

Al-waʾīd here denotes someone facing that fate.

Yūʾadi is passive and refers to the act being inflicted on someone.

The witness clarifies the relationship between agent and victim.

Al-Farazdaq lived in the Islamic period. His verse attests the word’s continued Umayyad use and historical memory of the practice, rather than independently dating its composition before Quranic revelation.

Lexicons report further verses and expressions concerning waʾd, but precise dating and secure attribution are unavailable for many of them.

The Quran itself directly attests mawʾūdah in the Arabic of revelation. Its association with bi-ayyi dhanbin qutilat shows that a person was killed through this act.

Live burial is thus a clear meaning at the time of revelation. Claims about the practice’s exact antiquity, the word’s origin, or prevalence across all Arab tribes should not exceed the evidence.

2. Forms and semantic branches

Important recorded forms include:

  • وَأَدَ: buried alive.
  • يَئِدُ: buries alive.
  • وَأْد: burying alive; elsewhere, a powerful sound.
  • وَائِد: someone who buries another alive.
  • مَوْءُود: someone buried alive.
  • مَوْءُودَة: feminine mawʾūd; a girl or woman buried alive.
  • وَئِيد: in one use, someone buried alive; elsewhere, a heavy sound or slow, heavy movement.
  • وَئِيدَة: a feminine form also recorded for someone buried alive.
  • تُؤَدَة: deliberateness, restraint, and unhurried conduct.
  • اتَّأَدَ: proceeded calmly and deliberately.
  • تَوَأَّدَ: moved slowly and deliberately; the related variant تَوَأْدَتْ is also recorded of earth concealing something within itself.

Arabic sources record these forms, but only al-mawʾūdah occurs in the Quran.

First branch: burying alive

This application involves three elements:

First, someone is still alive.

Second, that person is placed in and covered with earth.

Third, death results from the act.

These features distinguish it from ordinary burial of the dead.

Placing someone in a grave after death is burial; waʾd here concerns burying a living person.

Nor does every child killing lexically constitute waʾd. A child killed otherwise has been killed, but the specific term for live burial applies only where that method fits.

This matters when examining verses about killing children.

Second branch: heaviness and powerful sound

Arabic also records waʾd and waʾīd for a heavy, loud sound.

The fall of a large wall or animals’ heavy footsteps can be described this way.

Early expressions use waʾīd for sound produced by feet striking the ground heavily.

An early rajaz verse reads:

مَا لِلْجِمَالِ مَشْيُهَا وَئِيدَا

mā li-al-jimāli mashyuhā waʾīdā

أَجَنْدَلًا يَحْمِلْنَ أَمْ حَدِيدَا

a-jandalan yaḥmilna am ḥadīdā

“What is wrong with the camels, walking so heavily and slowly? Are they carrying stone or iron?”

The image connects the camels’ movement with a heavy load, emphasizing slow, weighty walking.

Lexicons transmit this rajaz, sometimes attributing it to al-Zabbāʾ. Its dating and attribution are insufficiently secure here to establish a definitive pre-Islamic witness.

It nevertheless illustrates waʾīd describing slow, heavy movement.

This does not make Quranic mawʾūdah merely “weighed down.” Its established contextual meaning is a person buried alive.

Third branch: deliberateness and restraint

Tuʾadah concerns calmness, deliberation, and avoiding haste.

Ittaʾada fī amrihi means that someone proceeded thoughtfully rather than hastily.

Early sources discuss these forms alongside wāw–hamzah–dāl, with differing explanations of their structure and historical relationship.

Slow, heavy movement resembles deliberate conduct, but that proximity does not reduce burying alive and acting thoughtfully to one simple shared meaning.

Quranic research must preserve each derivative’s meaning in its own setting.

Tuʾadah’s calmness and restraint should therefore not enter the definition of al-mawʾūdah.

3. Quranic usage: Al-Takwīr 81:8

The root occurs once in the Quran.

Al-Takwīr 81:8

وَإِذَا الْمَوْءُودَةُ سُئِلَتْ

wa-idhā al-mawʾūdatu suʾilat

Al-Takwīr 81:8

“And when the girl buried alive is asked,”

The verse forms part of images of cosmic transformation and truth revealed on the Day of Judgment.

Earlier images include the sun being folded away, stars falling, mountains moving, and souls being paired.

Amid these vast scenes, a girl buried alive suddenly comes into focus.

The shift from cosmic upheaval to a defenseless person’s fate is significant.

On that day, not only great cosmic phenomena change: human crimes also become subjects of questioning and judgment.

The construction of al-mawʾūdah

Al-mawʾūdah is the feminine passive participle of waʾada.

The masculine is mawʾūd and the feminine mawʾūdah.

A passive participle refers to the recipient of an action.

For example, qātil is one who kills, while maqtūl is one who is killed.

Likewise, wāʾid performs live burial and mawʾūdah is its victim.

Al- marks definiteness. The construction can invoke a category familiar to the audience.

Mawʾūdah is grammatically feminine, and its familiar historical application concerns girls buried alive.

This does not establish that no boy or man was ever killed that way. The verse describes a female victim, whose designation should be preserved in translation.

Why is the victim questioned?

Suʾilat is a passive perfect verb from sīn–hamzah–lām.

It places the girl buried alive in the position of being questioned.

It does not make her the accused; the next verse clarifies the question.

Al-Takwīr 81:9

بِأَيِّ ذَنْبٍ قُتِلَتْ

bi-ayyi dhanbin qutilat

Al-Takwīr 81:9

“For what sin was she killed?”

The question asks what alleged wrongdoing could have occasioned her killing.

The girl is the victim here, not the perpetrator of a crime.

The question exposes the killing’s injustice: What had this child done to be deprived of life?

Dhanb belongs to dhāl–nūn–bāʾ and concerns sin or attributable wrongdoing.

Its presence in a question does not establish the child’s guilt. Rather, the question exposes the absence of wrongdoing by the victim that could justify the act.

Qutilat belongs to qāf–tāʾ–lām and is also passive.

It explicitly states that the girl was killed. Mawʾūdah therefore denotes more than someone hidden in earth: the person lost her life through the act.

Questioning the victim does not absolve the killer. It exposes the crime from the perspective of someone powerless to defend herself.

The surah continues with records being opened and each soul recognizing what it has brought.

The question thus belongs to accountability and the disclosure of truth.

4. Girls buried alive and related verses

Al-Naḥl 16:58: resenting a daughter’s birth

Al-Naḥl 16:58 describes someone whose face darkens at news of a daughter, while inwardly restraining grief and anger.

The verse identifies an attitude that made daughters’ births unwelcome to some people in its audience’s environment.

It does not establish that every Arab family or individual held that attitude.

The Quran depicts blameworthy conduct without accusing an entire society without exception.

Al-Naḥl 16:59: keeping her in humiliation or hiding her in earth

Al-Naḥl 16:59 describes the person avoiding others and considering whether to keep the daughter in humiliation or conceal her in earth.

Am yadussuhu fī al-turāb is significant here.

Yadussuhu belongs to dāl–sīn–sīn and describes hiding or inserting something.

The phrase plainly relates to live burial, although dass and waʾd are not complete synonyms.

Dass can denote concealing something, while the human application of waʾd specifies burying alive.

The verse ends by condemning such judgment and conduct.

Al-Naḥl 16:58 and Al-Naḥl 16:59 therefore provide important social and ethical context for al-mawʾūdah.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:31: killing children for fear of poverty

Al-Isrāʾ 17:31 expressly forbids killing children out of fear of poverty.

Economic anxiety can thus become an excuse for denying a child life.

Killing children, however, is a broader category than waʾd.

Children can be killed by other methods. The prohibition still applies, but waʾd need not be the precise lexical designation.

Al-Anʿām 6:151 likewise forbids killing children because of poverty.

Min imlāqin in Al-Anʿām 6:151 differs significantly from khashyata imlāqin in Al-Isrāʾ 17:31.

The former concerns existing poverty; the latter foregrounds fear of future poverty.

Neither poverty nor economic anxiety licenses killing a child.

Al-Anʿām 6:137: child killing under religious justification

Al-Anʿām 6:137 describes people for whom killing their children was made attractive.

Justifications are therefore not confined to poverty. False beliefs and people who make harmful conduct seem desirable can also contribute.

Al-Anʿām 6:140 explicitly condemns those who killed their children in ignorance.

These verses nevertheless do not specify the method in every case.

All Quranic instances of child killing should therefore not be called live burial without evidence.

Nor should they all be attributed to one motive, such as poverty or fear of dishonor.

5. Comparison with related roots

Wāw–hamzah–dāl and qāf–tāʾ–lām: live burial and killing

Qatl is a broad Quranic term for killing and taking life.

The word alone does not determine the method.

Waʾd, in its human application, specifies burying alive.

Al-Takwīr 81:8 and Al-Takwīr 81:9 bring these concepts together.

First, the victim is designated al-mawʾūdah.

Then the Quran asks for what sin she was qutilat, killed.

Live burial is therefore a method of killing in this context, but not every killing is waʾd.

Wāw–hamzah–dāl and dāl–fāʾ–nūn: burial and live burial

Dafn means placing and covering something in earth.

One may bury a dead person or conceal an object underground.

Waʾd here specifies burying a living human.

The difference concerns the person’s condition when buried.

Burying someone after death is burial. Placing them alive under earth and causing their death is waʾd.

This distinction is essential in translating al-mawʾūdah.

Wāw–hamzah–dāl and dāl–sīn–sīn: concealment and live burial

Dass concerns hiding or inserting something.

Al-Naḥl 16:59 connects hiding the daughter in earth with the conduct it condemns.

Waʾd, by contrast, is the established designation for burying alive.

Waʾd may involve concealing a person beneath earth, but not every concealment is waʾd.

Wāw–hamzah–dāl and hamzah–wāw–dāl: different letter orders

These roots have closely related letters, but their order differs.

Wāw–hamzah–dāl underlies al-mawʾūdah.

Hamzah–wāw–dāl, as in yaʾūduhu in Al-Baqarah 2:255, concerns burdensome weight and difficulty imposed on an agent.

Wa-lā yaʾūduhu ḥifẓuhumā in Al-Baqarah 2:255 states that preserving the heavens and earth imposes no burdensome difficulty on God.

Some scholars connect the families through heaviness, but similarities of letters and meaning do not establish one historical root.

Quranic indexing and analysis must retain the actual letter order.

Wāw–hamzah–dāl and wāw–dāl–dāl: live burial and affection

Mawaddah belongs to wāw–dāl–dāl and denotes affection and friendship.

Mawʾūdah instead belongs to wāw–hamzah–dāl.

The words may look similar to an unfamiliar reader, but differ in structure and meaning.

Hamzah is a root letter in mawʾūdah; mawaddah has a doubled dāl root.

Visual resemblance therefore supplies no grounds for assigning affection or friendship to al-mawʾūdah.

6. Interpretive challenges and contemporary application

Is waʾd used only of girls?

Its familiar historical application chiefly concerns girls buried alive.

The Quran likewise uses the feminine al-mawʾūdah.

The act can nevertheless apply lexically to another human; Arabic sources also record masculine mawʾūd.

The sole Quranic form’s gender does not establish that boys never suffered such treatment.

Conversely, the feminine designation and its clear referent should not disappear into a generic translation such as “an oppressed person.”

Is Quranic waʾd a metaphor for suppressing talent or freedom?

Contemporary Arabic uses expressions such as burying freedom or talent to mean suppressing something before it flourishes.

A writer might call denying children education and suppressing their abilities “burying their talents alive.”

That image can be effective social criticism.

It is not, however, the direct meaning of al-mawʾūdah in Al-Takwīr 81:8.

The passage concerns someone killed, and the next verse makes this explicit through qutilat.

Contemporary reflection can learn from the condemned crime without dissolving the actual live burial into a vague metaphor.

Did all pre-Islamic Arabs practice this?

No such generalization follows from the Quran.

It condemns conduct found among its audience or some groups within it, without saying that all families, tribes, or Arabs behaved identically.

Nor can one poem or historical account establish its exact prevalence throughout Arabia.

What is clear is the Quranic designation, condemnation of the buried girl’s killing, and connection with other passages on discrimination and child killing.

Both denying the condemned reality and making it an accusation against every person of an era would be mistaken.

Can poverty or reputation cancel a child’s right to life?

Al-Isrāʾ 17:31 and Al-Anʿām 6:151 explicitly prohibit killing children because of poverty or fear of it.

Al-Naḥl 16:58 and Al-Naḥl 16:59 condemn contempt for a daughter’s birth.

Al-Takwīr 81:8 and Al-Takwīr 81:9 expose the killing’s injustice through the question addressed to the buried girl.

Together they establish a clear principle: no one may sacrifice a child’s life to poverty, fear of the future, family prejudice, or preference for one sex.

Forqan’s principle “The Sanctity of Children’s Lives” connects this subject with the verses prohibiting child killing.

This is more than an emotional appeal for kindness. It safeguards the life of someone unable to defend their own rights.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for the main forms include:

وَأَدَ: buried alive.

يَئِدُ: buries alive.

وَأْد: burying alive; elsewhere, a heavy, tremendous sound.

وَائِد: one who buries another alive.

مَوْءُود: someone buried alive.

مَوْءُودَة: a girl or woman buried alive.

وَئِيد: a person buried alive in that context; elsewhere, a heavy sound or slow, heavy movement.

تُؤَدَة: deliberateness; calmness; avoiding haste.

اتَّأَدَ: proceeded thoughtfully and deliberately.

For the sole Quranic form, al-mawʾūdah, “the girl buried alive” is clear and appropriate.

The proposed rendering of Al-Takwīr 81:8 is:

“And when the girl buried alive is asked,”

This preserves the established meaning and feminine form.

“The girl who was killed” omits the particular method.

“The buried girl” also falls short, failing to establish that she was alive when buried.

“An oppressed person” or “an innocent child” may fit reflection on the verse, but is not the lexical equivalent of mawʾūdah.

The established designation “the girl buried alive” therefore best suits the base translation.

8. Conclusion

In its human application, wāw–hamzah–dāl denotes burying alive: a person is placed under earth before death and loses their life through the act.

Arabic also records heavy sound, slow movement, and deliberateness. Some scholars connect these through heaviness, but semantic affinity alone does not establish every branch’s history.

The Quran uses only al-mawʾūdah.

Al-Takwīr 81:8 names the girl buried alive; Al-Takwīr 81:9 exposes the crime’s injustice by asking for what sin she was killed.

Al-Naḥl 16:58 and Al-Naḥl 16:59 condemn contempt for daughters. Al-Isrāʾ 17:31 and Al-Anʿām 6:151 explicitly forbid child killing because of poverty or fear of it.

These verses show that a child’s life must not be sacrificed to discrimination, poverty, fear, or false beliefs.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its human branch, wāw–hamzah–dāl denotes burying a living person and covering them with earth until death. Other branches concern heavy movement, powerful sound, and deliberateness, and require separate examination.

Al-mawʾūdah in its sole Quranic occurrence is the girl buried alive: someone deprived of life whose unjust killing the Quran exposes through a clear question in the judgment scene.
