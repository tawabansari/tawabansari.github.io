The meaning of ṭalaq in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from release from restraint and freedom of movement and speech to divorce, spouses’ mutual rights, and the limits of separation

The Arabic triliteral root ط ل ق concerns being unrestrained, releasing, removing restrictions, and moving after an obstacle is lifted. Members of this family describe an animal released from its tether, a person setting out, fluent speech, or a face becoming open and welcoming.

Within this root, طَلِيق denotes someone released, انْطِلَاق means movement or setting out, طَلَاقَةُ اللِّسَان denotes fluency, and طَلَاق concerns ending a marital bond.

In marriage, divorce concerns release from a bond previously established through marriage. Such release does not disregard the rights, obligations, and consequences of separation. The Qur’an discusses divorce through timing, fair treatment, prevention of harm, financial responsibility, and the possibility of reconciliation.

The root occurs twenty-three times in twenty-one Qur’anic verses. Its four main forms are the verbs طَلَّقَ and انْطَلَقَ, the noun طَلَاق, and the passive participle مُطَلَّقَات.

These forms must be distinguished. انطلق describes setting out in the account of Moses and his companion, and lack of fluency when Moses speaks about his tongue. By contrast, the marital uses of طلق and طلاق concern ending marriage.

Not every derivative of ط ل ق therefore means divorcing a woman. Equally, the established marital meaning should not be reduced to general release in a way that overlooks accompanying rules and responsibilities.

1. Root forms and core meaning

The principal forms in Arabic include:

  • طَلَقَ، يَطْلُقُ: Became free or unrestrained; in other uses, departed from a place.
  • طَلُقَ: Became open or fluent, as of a face or tongue.
  • طَلْق and طِلْق: Free or unrestricted in various uses; also something subject to no particular prohibition.
  • طَلِيق: Released, especially from captivity.
  • طُلَقَاء: Plural of طليق; released people.
  • طَلَاقَة: Openness or fluency, including a welcoming face and fluent speech.
  • طَلْقُ الْوَجْهِ and طَلِيقُ الْوَجْهِ: Open-faced and welcoming.
  • طَلْقُ اللِّسَان and طَلِيقُ اللِّسَان: Fluent and able to speak without impediment.
  • طَالِق: In marriage, a divorced woman; in animal husbandry, an animal left untethered.
  • طَلَّقَ and تَطْلِيق: Released; in marriage, divorced a wife.
  • طَلَاق: Ending a marital bond; in some lexical uses, release or separation.
  • مُطَلِّق: Someone who divorces.
  • مُطَلَّق and مُطَلَّقَة: Someone divorced.
  • مِطْلَاق: A man who frequently divorces.
  • أَطْلَقَ and إِطْلَاق: Released, removed restraint, or allowed movement.
  • اِنْطَلَقَ and اِنْطِلَاق: Set out, moved, or emerged from a stationary condition.
  • تَطَلَّقَ: Became free or moved; in some uses, a face brightened or an animal accelerated.
  • اِسْتَطْلَقَ: Requested release; in special uses, requested speed or developed looseness of the bowels.

Other uses with these letters are also recorded, including طَلْق for labor pains and for a leather tether or tightly twisted rope.

These must be examined separately. Shared letters or written forms do not prove that all developed from releasing.

Qur’anic forms

Four main structures occur in the Qur’an:

طَلَّقَ: Ten form II occurrences, all concerning marital divorce.

اِنْطَلَقَ: Nine form VII occurrences, concerning setting out, movement, or fluent speech.

طَلَاق: Two nominal occurrences, in Al-Baqarah 2:227 and Al-Baqarah 2:229.

مُطَلَّقَات: Two occurrences of the feminine plural passive participle, in Al-Baqarah 2:228 and Al-Baqarah 2:241.

Forms such as طليق, طلاقة, إطلاق, مطلاق, and طالق are established in Arabic but do not themselves occur in the Qur’an.

The core meaning: releasing and leaving free

The root’s central meaning can be expressed as:

التَّخْلِيَةُ وَالْإِرْسَالُ

al-takhliyatu wa-al-irsālu

That is, “leaving free and releasing.”

A person or thing emerges from a restraint, restriction, or obstacle limiting movement or choice.

For example:

أَطْلَقَ النَّاقَةَ مِنْ عِقَالِهَا

aṭlaqa al-nāqata min ʿiqālihā

Translation: “He released the she-camel from her tether.”

Here the restraint is literal and releasing is a physical act.

But when one says:

اِنْطَلَقَ الرَّجُلُ

inṭalaqa al-rajulu

“The man set out.”

The person need not previously have been chained or captive.

He may simply have risen and started moving.

Openness and absence of an obstacle help explain this semantic family, but actual restraints or imprisonment should not be assumed in every use.

Marital divorce likewise concerns release from a marriage bond, rather than necessarily from physical captivity.

2. Early Arabic usage: release, fluency, and openness

An untethered animal and unrestricted movement

In early Arabic, نَاقَةٌ طَالِقٌ described a she-camel allowed to graze without a tether.

بَعِيرٌ طَلْقٌ likewise described an unrestrained camel.

Here طلق describes an animal able to move freely.

An animal secured with a hobble lacks that freedom of movement.

This clearly illustrates the core sense of being unrestrained.

Even in animal husbandry, however, طالق does not always mean wholly abandoned or ownerless.

A camel may belong to someone and merely be allowed to graze or go to water.

Freedom from one restraint does not eliminate every relationship or responsibility.

This distinction also helps explain marital divorce: ending marriage does not automatically remove all financial, family, or child-related responsibilities.

Releasing a captive

طَلِيق describes a captive whose bonds have been removed and who has been freed.

Its plural is طُلَقَاء.

The person was previously under another’s control without freedom of movement, then emerged from that condition.

أَطْلَقَ الْأَسِيرَ likewise means releasing a captive.

This clearly expresses release.

It does not establish that Arabic necessarily regarded a married woman as her husband’s captive.

طليق for a freed captive and طالق for a divorced woman share a semantic field of release, but the relationships and social conditions differ.

One involves captivity; the other, marriage.

A welcoming face

Arabic uses the expression:

رَجُلٌ طَلْقُ الْوَجْهِ

rajulun ṭalqu al-wajhi

Translation: “An open-faced, welcoming man.”

Also:

طَلِيقُ الْوَجْهِ

ṭalīqu al-wajhi

“Pleasant and open-faced.”

Here طلق concerns a face free of tension and frowning.

A welcoming person contrasts with someone stern and scowling.

This has an intelligible connection with openness.

A welcoming face does not necessarily mean constant happiness or absence of grief.

Nor does طلاقة الوجه itself establish honesty or justice. Someone can be pleasant while behaving wrongly.

The word’s meaning must therefore remain distinct from a broader moral judgment of the person.

Fluency of speech

طَلْقُ اللِّسَان and طَلِيقُ اللِّسَان describe someone who speaks fluently without impediment.

This does not concern physical movement between places.

The subject is the tongue’s ability to produce speech.

Accordingly, saying that someone’s tongue is not منطلق can indicate difficulty speaking, lack of fluency, or an impediment to expression.

This sense appears directly in Moses’ speech in the Qur’an.

Fluency, however, is not identical with knowledge or truthfulness.

Someone may speak fluently but incorrectly, while someone with difficulty speaking may convey truth and a sound argument.

طلاقة اللسان concerns expression, not the truth of its content.

Mild and pleasant days and nights

يَوْمٌ طَلْقٌ describes a day without oppressive heat or cold.

لَيْلَةٌ طَلْقَةٌ can describe a calm, clear, pleasant night.

Here طلق concerns ease and absence of oppressive conditions.

In the poetry of Kuthayyir ʿAzzah, an Umayyad poet, لَيَالٍ طَوَالِقُ describes pleasant nights.

This illustrates an established classical use for ease and openness, but Kuthayyir’s poetry is not pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Openhandedness

طَلْقُ الْيَدَيْنِ describes a generous person.

The open hand contrasts with the closed hand.

The person is not restrictive or tightfisted in giving.

A poetic witness reads:

اطْلُقْ يَدَيْكَ تَنْفَعَاكَ يَا رَجُلْ
بِالرَّيْثِ مَا أَرْوَيْتَهَا لَا بِالْعَجَلْ

iṭluq yadayka tanfaʿāka yā rajul / bi-al-raythi mā arwaytahā lā bi-al-ʿajal

“Let your hands go free to benefit you, man; with deliberation, not haste, you water it.”

The first half-line calls for freeing the hands to be useful. The second contrasts deliberation with haste and mentions watering; without fuller context, the entire verse should not be confined to generosity. The established expression طَلْقُ الْيَدَيْنِ more clearly supports openhandedness.

The speaker and exact date are uncertain, so the verse cannot be presented as pre-Islamic without evidence.

Generosity is not the meaning of every use of ط ل ق. It emerges in the particular expression concerning hands and human behavior.

3. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

Al-Aʿshā: marital divorce

Al-Aʿshā, who lived through the pre-Islamic period and the beginning of the Prophet’s call, says:

أَجَارَتَنَا بِينِي فَإِنَّكِ طَالِقَةْ
كَذَاكِ أُمُورُ النَّاسِ غَادٍ وَطَارِقَةْ

ajāratana bīnī fa-innaki ṭāliqah / kadhāki umūru al-nāsi ghādin wa-ṭāriqah

Translation: “My wife, depart, for you are divorced; thus are people’s affairs: one comes in the morning, another arrives by night.”

طَالِقَة here refers to a divorced woman.

The verse matters because it attests the marital use of this family in poetry close to the period of revelation; it does not alone establish the poem’s exact date.

Poetry sometimes addresses a wife as جارة. جارتنا here should therefore not be understood simply as a next-door neighbor without regard to poetic usage.

Literary accounts relate this poem to conflict between al-Aʿshā and his wife; some connect further verses to that episode.

The verse itself does not establish those reports’ details or chronology.

What directly concerns this root study is طالقة in marital separation.

The grammar of طَالِق and طَالِقَة

A common Arabic description of a divorced woman is امْرَأَةٌ طَالِقٌ, without the feminine ending.

Al-Aʿshā instead uses طَالِقَة here.

Early linguists and grammarians discussed this difference.

One explanation treats طالق as an established nominal or status description, like certain other descriptions specific to women that lack the feminine ending.

Another associates طالقة with a description based on طَلَقَتْ or one referring to a future condition.

Others explain the poetic form through meter and rhyme.

The presence or absence of the feminine ending therefore cannot by itself settle whether divorce has occurred.

طالق was also an established marital term, rather than a meaning newly derived from general freedom in every sentence.

Al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas: a share of generosity

The pre-Islamic poet al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas says:

قَبْلَ امْرِئٍ تُرْجَى فَوَاضِلُهُ
قَدْ نَالَنِي مِنْ بَاعِهِ طَلَقُ

qabla imriʾin turjā fawāḍiluhu / qad nālanī min bāʿihi ṭalaqu

Approximate sense of the excerpt: “A man whose bounty is hoped for … a share of his openhandedness has reached me.” The relation of قَبْلَ to what precedes it requires the surrounding poem.

طَلَق here concerns a benefit received from someone’s generosity.

The poet speaks of a person’s hand or capacity to give, extended to others.

This connects with openhandedness in the family ط ل ق.

The poem thus independently illustrates a branch other than marital divorce.

It does not make طلق a gift in every context. That meaning emerges from the poetic construction and its association with فَوَاضِلُهُ and بَاعِهِ.

Special uses that must remain distinct

طَلْق is also recorded for labor pains.

طَلْقَة may denote one contraction or episode of labor pain.

Another use names a leather tether or, in some accounts, a tightly twisted rope.

This apparently differs from freedom and untying a restraint.

One should not claim that the leather tether’s name necessarily arose from releasing it merely to preserve a single underlying image.

Grouping words under the same letters does not prove a shared historical origin for every use.

Labor pains, a leather tether, and marital divorce especially require their own meanings.

Neither labor pains nor a leather tether is expressed by this root in the Qur’an.

4. Inṭilāq in the Qur’an: movement and fluency

The nine occurrences of اِنْطَلَقَ show that this root is not confined to marital separation.

Most describe movement or setting out.

One concerns Moses’ tongue and illustrates fluency and openness.

Al-Kahf 18:71, Al-Kahf 18:74, and Al-Kahf 18:77: Moses and his companion set out

Al-Kahf 18:71 reads:

فَانْطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا رَكِبَا فِي السَّفِينَةِ خَرَقَهَا

fa-inṭalaqā ḥattā idhā rakibā fī al-safīnati kharaqahā

Al-Kahf 18:71

Translation: “They both set out until, when they boarded the ship, he made a hole in it.”

Al-Kahf 18:74 also reads:

فَانْطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا لَقِيَا غُلَامًا فَقَتَلَهُ

fa-inṭalaqā ḥattā idhā laqiyā ghulāman fa-qatalahu

Al-Kahf 18:74

Translation: “They both set out until, when they met a boy, he killed him.”

And Al-Kahf 18:77:

فَانْطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَتَيَا أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ اسْتَطْعَمَا أَهْلَهَا

fa-inṭalaqā ḥattā idhā atayā ahla qaryatin istaṭʿamā ahlahā

Al-Kahf 18:77

Translation: “They both set out until, when they reached the people of a settlement, they asked its people for food.”

In all three verses, فَانْطَلَقَا is a dual perfect verb in form VII.

It describes two people moving and continuing their journey.

انطلقا itself does not mean boarding a ship, meeting a boy, or reaching a settlement; those events are expressed afterward.

Here it means setting out; destination or subsequent event comes from the rest of the sentence.

Nor does the verb establish that they were previously bound or imprisoned.

In this construction it expresses beginning or continuing movement.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:13: Moses’ tongue does not flow

وَيَضِيقُ صَدْرِي وَلَا يَنْطَلِقُ لِسَانِي فَأَرْسِلْ إِلَىٰ هَارُونَ

wa-yaḍīqu ṣadrī wa-lā yanṭaliqu lisānī fa-arsil ilā Hārūna

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:13

Translation: “My breast becomes constrained and my tongue does not flow; so send to Aaron.”

This is a different use of انطلاق.

The subject is لِسَانِي, “my tongue.”

Moses describes difficulty speaking and requests Aaron’s participation in the mission.

The expression concerns fluent speech, not the tongue traveling to another place.

In Ṭā Hā 20:27 and Ṭā Hā 20:28 Moses also says:

وَاحْلُلْ عُقْدَةً مِنْ لِسَانِي ۝ يَفْقَهُوا قَوْلِي

wa-uḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī / yafqahū qawlī

Ṭā Hā 20:27; Ṭā Hā 20:28

Translation: “Untie a knot from my tongue, so they may understand my speech.”

These verses help explain Moses’ difficulty expressing himself.

Combining the accounts does not justify assigning a specific medical diagnosis without evidence.

The Qur’an describes constriction of the breast, a tongue lacking fluency, and untying a knot from it. These expressions alone do not specify the exact speech difficulty.

Lack of fluency also does not make Moses’ message untrue or his argument weak.

Ṣād 38:6: the leaders depart

وَانْطَلَقَ الْمَلَأُ مِنْهُمْ أَنِ امْشُوا وَاصْبِرُوا عَلَىٰ آلِهَتِكُمْ

wa-inṭalaqa al-malaʾu minhum ani imshū wa-iṣbirū ʿalā ālihatikum

Ṣād 38:6

Translation: “Their leaders departed, saying: Go on, and remain steadfast to your deities.”

وَانْطَلَقَ describes a group of leaders moving or departing.

Their call to remain steadfast to their deities follows.

Setting out is therefore not itself a sign of guidance or misguidance.

Their beliefs and message are identified from context.

Movement can serve different purposes.

Al-Fatḥ 48:15: setting out for spoils

سَيَقُولُ الْمُخَلَّفُونَ إِذَا انْطَلَقْتُمْ إِلَىٰ مَغَانِمَ لِتَأْخُذُوهَا ذَرُونَا نَتَّبِعْكُمْ

sa-yaqūlu al-mukhallafūna idhā inṭalaqtum ilā maghānima li-taʾkhudhūhā dharūnā nattabiʿkum

Al-Fatḥ 48:15

Translation: “Those left behind will say, when you set out to take spoils: Let us follow you.”

The preposition إِلَىٰ introduces the destination of the movement.

Those departing are heading toward spoils.

The root itself does not mean taking spoils; it expresses setting out.

Destination and purpose are supplied by accompanying phrases.

Al-Qalam 68:23: the garden owners set out

فَانْطَلَقُوا وَهُمْ يَتَخَافَتُونَ

fa-inṭalaqū wa-hum yatakhāfatūna

Al-Qalam 68:23

Translation: “They set out, speaking quietly to one another.”

This belongs to the account of the garden owners.

They had decided to visit their garden early in the morning.

فَانْطَلَقُوا describes their movement.

Their intention to deprive the needy of a share emerges elsewhere in the narrative.

انطلاق therefore does not itself mean stinginess or depriving others.

Those actions occur in the same scene but do not alter the movement verb’s independent meaning.

Al-Mursalāt 77:29 and Al-Mursalāt 77:30: commanded movement toward punishment

Al-Mursalāt 77:29 reads:

انْطَلِقُوا إِلَىٰ مَا كُنْتُمْ بِهِ تُكَذِّبُونَ

inṭaliqū ilā mā kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūna

Al-Mursalāt 77:29

Translation: “Go toward what you used to deny.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:30 continues:

انْطَلِقُوا إِلَىٰ ظِلٍّ ذِي ثَلَاثِ شُعَبٍ

inṭaliqū ilā ẓillin dhī thalāthi shuʿabin

Al-Mursalāt 77:30

Translation: “Go toward a shadow with three branches.”

Both commands belong to form VII.

Unlike ordinary travel, however, this movement leads toward punishment.

انطلاق does not intrinsically mean pleasant freedom, escape from danger, or comfort.

Someone may move toward something distressing or punitive.

Direction and outcome must be inferred from the verb’s complement and context.

What the uses of inṭilāq show

In the Qur’an, انطلاق usually means moving, going, or continuing a journey.

Moses also uses it of a tongue that does not flow.

Openness and release from obstruction can help explain these uses, but actual bonds or release from prison should not be assumed in every verse.

Nor does انطلاق have a fixed moral value: movement may serve right action, personal gain, or lead toward punishment.

5. Divorce in the Qur’an: ending marriage and accompanying responsibilities

The other Qur’anic uses of ط ل ق concern marriage.

These passages discuss deciding on divorce, divorced women, the number of divorce occasions, return, rights, waiting, and preventing harm.

Divorce here is not simply spouses living apart.

Spouses may live in different cities for a time while remaining married.

Conversely, divorce may occur while legal and family responsibilities continue for a specified period.

Qur’anic divorce therefore concerns the status of the marital bond.

Al-Baqarah 2:227: deciding on divorce

وَإِنْ عَزَمُوا الطَّلَاقَ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

wa-in ʿazamū al-ṭalāqa fa-inna Allāha samīʿun ʿalīmun

Al-Baqarah 2:227

Translation:

“If they resolve on divorce, The God is surely Hearing, Knowing.”

This is the first occurrence of the noun الطَّلَاقَ in Qur’anic order.

It is the object of عَزَمُوا.

عزم concerns decision and firmness of intention.

The verse thus discusses deciding on divorce.

Deciding to act is distinct from accomplishing the act itself.

The preceding verse, Al-Baqarah 2:226, discusses those who swear concerning their wives and specifies four months of waiting.

Al-Baqarah 2:227 then speaks of deciding on divorce.

The relationship’s direction is being clarified: will there be return and repair, or a decision to separate?

The word divorce does not establish that merely thinking of separation creates all its legal effects.

Here divorce is the object of a decision; its realization and effects require examining the relevant verses.

Al-Baqarah 2:228: divorced women

وَالْمُطَلَّقَاتُ يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنْفُسِهِنَّ ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ

wa-al-muṭallaqātu yatarabbaṣna bi-anfusihinna thalāthata qurūʾin

Al-Baqarah 2:228

Translation:

“Divorced women shall wait by themselves for three qurūʾ.”

الْمُطَلَّقَاتُ is a feminine plural passive participle of form II.

It refers to women who have been divorced.

Divorce has already occurred, and the verse describes the subsequent situation and responsibilities.

يَتَرَبَّصْنَ belongs to ر ب ص and means waiting.

قُرُوء belongs to another root whose precise meaning requires its own study.

The meaning of مطلقات should not be merged with waiting or its prescribed duration.

Nor does it mean every woman free from every restraint; here it means divorced women.

The verse continues with mutual rights and the possibility of return for reconciliation:

وَلَهُنَّ مِثْلُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ

wa-lahunna mithlu alladhī ʿalayhinna bi-al-maʿrūfi

Al-Baqarah 2:228

“They have rights comparable to their obligations, in accordance with what is fair.”

Divorce does not license disregard for the person, rights, or reciprocal responsibilities of the other spouse.

Al-Baqarah 2:229: الطَّلَاقُ مَرَّتَانِ

الطَّلَاقُ مَرَّتَانِ فَإِمْسَاكٌ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ

al-ṭalāqu marratāni fa-imsākun bi-maʿrūfin aw tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin

Al-Baqarah 2:229

Translation:

“Divorce is twice; then retention in fairness or release with kindness.”

This is the second and last occurrence of the noun الطَّلَاق in the Qur’an.

It is accompanied by مَرَّتَانِ, “twice.”

مرتان is the dual of مرة, referring to two occasions.

The verse discusses two occasions of divorce, followed by fair retention or kind release.

The root alone does not determine every legal detail of counting divorces.

The number of occasions, return, effects of a subsequent divorce, and conditions of a renewed relationship require considering Al-Baqarah 2:230 and Al-Baqarah 2:231.

Nor should مرتان be reduced without evidence to two utterances at one instant. Its detailed timing likewise does not follow merely from the root’s meaning.

Divorce, retention, and release

Two other expressions occur in this verse:

إِمْسَاكٌ بِمَعْرُوفٍ

imsākun bi-maʿrūfin

Al-Baqarah 2:229

“Retention in fairness.”

تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ

tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin

Al-Baqarah 2:229

“Release with kindness.”

إمساك belongs to م س ك and concerns retaining.

تسريح belongs to س ر ح and concerns releasing.

These matter alongside divorce but are not exact synonyms of it.

Divorce expresses ending the marital bond.

تسريح, accompanied here by kindness, describes releasing and letting go.

إمساك concerns retaining the relationship within fair treatment.

The Qur’an names not only separation but how the other party is treated.

Fairness and kindness nevertheless retain independent meanings and should not be built into the lexical definition of divorce.

Al-Baqarah 2:230: two occurrences in one verse

فَإِنْ طَلَّقَهَا فَلَا تَحِلُّ لَهُ مِنْ بَعْدُ حَتَّىٰ تَنْكِحَ زَوْجًا غَيْرَهُ فَإِنْ طَلَّقَهَا فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا أَنْ يَتَرَاجَعَا إِنْ ظَنَّا أَنْ يُقِيمَا حُدُودَ اللَّهِ

fa-in ṭallaqahā fa-lā taḥillu lahu min baʿdu ḥattā tankiḥa zawjan ghayrahu fa-in ṭallaqahā fa-lā junāḥa ʿalayhimā an yatarājaʿā in ẓannā an yuqīmā ḥudūda Allāhi

Al-Baqarah 2:230

Translation:

“If he divorces her, she is not lawful for him afterward until she marries another husband. If he too divorces her, there is no blame on the two for returning to one another, if they think they can uphold The God’s limits.”

طَلَّقَهَا appears twice.

Its contextual subject differs between the occurrences.

The first concerns divorce following the earlier two occasions.

Marriage to another husband is then mentioned.

The second divorce is by that other husband, after which the stated conditions allow return between the woman and her first husband.

Subjects, pronouns, and the sequence of events are essential to understanding the verse.

The stages and conditions of a subsequent marriage cannot be derived from ط ل ق itself; they come from the verse’s clauses.

Nor does the verse expressly instruct arranging a sham or deceptive marriage to return the woman to her first husband.

It discusses marriage to another husband and a subsequent divorce within that marriage.

Additional judgments about the form and intention of a marriage require independent evidence.

Al-Baqarah 2:231: prohibiting harmful retention

وَإِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَبَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ سَرِّحُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا لِتَعْتَدُوا

wa-idhā ṭallaqtumu al-nisāʾa fa-balaghna ajalahunna fa-amsikūhunna bi-maʿrūfin aw sarriḥūhunna bi-maʿrūfin wa-lā tumsikūhunna ḍirāran li-taʿtadū

Al-Baqarah 2:231

Translation:

“When you divorce women and they approach the end of their term, retain them fairly or release them fairly; do not retain them to cause harm and transgress.”

طَلَّقْتُمُ is a second-person plural perfect verb.

The subject is divorcing women and responsibility for subsequent conduct.

The Qur’an discusses fair retention or fair release and prohibits retaining with the purpose of harm.

The possibility of retention or return must not become a means of abuse or keeping the other person indefinitely uncertain.

This is clear from the verse itself.

Harming is not the lexical meaning of divorce; the separate phrase ضِرَارًا لِتَعْتَدُوا expresses it.

فَبَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ also requires contextual study of waiting periods: approaching the end and fully completing it have different legal implications. The root ط ل ق alone cannot resolve this.

Al-Baqarah 2:232: not obstructing marriage

وَإِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَبَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَلَا تَعْضُلُوهُنَّ أَنْ يَنْكِحْنَ أَزْوَاجَهُنَّ إِذَا تَرَاضَوْا بَيْنَهُمْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ

wa-idhā ṭallaqtumu al-nisāʾa fa-balaghna ajalahunna fa-lā taʿḍulūhunna an yankiḥna azwājahunna idhā tarāḍaw baynahum bi-al-maʿrūfi

Al-Baqarah 2:232

Translation:

“When you divorce women and they reach the end of their term, do not prevent them from marrying their husbands when they mutually agree in fairness.”

This verse again uses the verb طلقتم.

It introduces a further issue: not preventing marriage when the relevant parties consent.

تَعْضُلُوهُنَّ belongs to ع ض ل and concerns obstruction.

It should not be equated with divorce.

Divorce concerns ending a marital bond.

عضل here means preventing a bond to which the parties consent.

Separation should not confer permanent power on one person to obstruct the other’s future life.

Exactly whom the prohibition addresses in different circumstances, and the conditions of subsequent marriage, require a separate study of the verse.

Al-Baqarah 2:236 and Al-Baqarah 2:237: divorce before marital intimacy

Al-Baqarah 2:236 reads:

لَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِنْ طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ مَا لَمْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ أَوْ تَفْرِضُوا لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً

lā junāḥa ʿalaykum in ṭallaqtumu al-nisāʾa mā lam tamassūhunna aw tafriḍū lahunna farīḍatan

Al-Baqarah 2:236

Translation:

“There is no blame on you if you divorce women before you have touched them or assigned them a prescribed provision.”

Al-Baqarah 2:237 continues:

وَإِنْ طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ وَقَدْ فَرَضْتُمْ لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً فَنِصْفُ مَا فَرَضْتُمْ

wa-in ṭallaqtumūhunna min qabli an tamassūhunna wa-qad faraḍtum lahunna farīḍatan fa-niṣfu mā faraḍtum

Al-Baqarah 2:237

Translation:

“If you divorce them before touching them, having assigned them a prescribed provision, then half of what you assigned.”

In both verses, طلق describes ending marriage before marital intimacy.

Divorce is thus not dependent on prior physical intimacy: marriage may have been established and then ended before it.

The verses nevertheless discuss financial and legal responsibilities arising from this separation.

“Release” therefore does not imply that no obligation remains after deciding to separate.

Al-Baqarah 2:241: provision for divorced women

وَلِلْمُطَلَّقَاتِ مَتَاعٌ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ حَقًّا عَلَى الْمُتَّقِينَ

wa-li-al-muṭallaqāti matāʿun bi-al-maʿrūfi ḥaqqan ʿalā al-muttaqīna

Al-Baqarah 2:241

Translation:

“For divorced women there is provision in fairness, a duty upon those mindful of The God.”

This is the second occurrence of مُطَلَّقَات.

It establishes provision in fairness for divorced women.

That right emerges from the full statement, rather than from the passive participle itself.

Root study should distinguish the status of being divorced from the rights the verses attach to that status.

This does not detach rights from Qur’anic divorce. On the contrary, the Qur’an presents separation together with responsibility.

6. Divorce in Al-Aḥzāb, Al-Ṭalāq, and Al-Taḥrīm

Al-Aḥzāb 33:49: marriage before intimacy

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا نَكَحْتُمُ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ ثُمَّ طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ فَمَا لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِنَّ مِنْ عِدَّةٍ تَعْتَدُّونَهَا فَمَتِّعُوهُنَّ وَسَرِّحُوهُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā nakaḥtumu al-muʾmināti thumma ṭallaqtumūhunna min qabli an tamassūhunna fa-mā lakum ʿalayhinna min ʿiddatin taʿtaddūnahā fa-mattiʿūhunna wa-sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan jamīlan

Al-Aḥzāb 33:49

Translation:

“You who have believed, when you marry believing women and then divorce them before touching them, you have no waiting period to count concerning them. Provide for them and release them graciously.”

Here divorce occurs after marriage but before marital intimacy.

The verse states that no waiting period applies in this situation and calls for provision and gracious release.

تطليق and تسريح therefore refer to the same separation process through distinct expressions.

Divorce changes the bond’s status, while تسريح concerns release and, with its accompanying description, the manner of letting go.

This does not make every divorce in every circumstance free of a waiting period. The verse specifies the situation before marital intimacy.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:1: timing and not expelling women from their homes

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ إِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَطَلِّقُوهُنَّ لِعِدَّتِهِنَّ وَأَحْصُوا الْعِدَّةَ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ رَبَّكُمْ لَا تُخْرِجُوهُنَّ مِنْ بُيُوتِهِنَّ وَلَا يَخْرُجْنَ إِلَّا أَنْ يَأْتِينَ بِفَاحِشَةٍ مُبَيِّنَةٍ

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu idhā ṭallaqtumu al-nisāʾa fa-ṭalliqūhunna li-ʿiddatihinna wa-aḥṣū al-ʿiddata wa-ittaqū Allāha rabbakum lā tukhrijūhunna min buyūtihinna wa-lā yakhrujna illā an yaʾtīna bi-fāḥishatin mubayyinatin

Al-Ṭalāq 65:1

Translation:

“Prophet, when you divorce women, divorce them with regard to their waiting period, count the period, and be mindful of The God, your Lord. Do not expel them from their homes, nor should they leave, unless they commit a manifest indecency.”

ط ل ق occurs twice here:

طَلَّقْتُمُ and فَطَلِّقُوهُنَّ.

The first is a second-person plural perfect verb within a conditional construction.

The second is a plural imperative.

The verse opens with singular address, يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ, then continues with plural طَلَّقْتُمُ.

This shift must remain visible in translation and analysis.

The verse discusses divorce timing, counting the waiting period, and mindfulness of The God.

It then prohibits expelling women from their homes, subject to its stated exception.

This matters lexically: divorce in the Qur’an does not mean immediately expelling a woman from her home.

Reducing divorce to “releasing and sending away” could produce a conclusion inconsistent with this very verse.

Here divorce changes the marital relationship, while residence and waiting-period rules remain matters of instruction.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:6 also commands:

أَسْكِنُوهُنَّ مِنْ حَيْثُ سَكَنْتُمْ مِنْ وُجْدِكُمْ وَلَا تُضَارُّوهُنَّ لِتُضَيِّقُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ

askinūhunna min ḥaythu sakantum min wujdikum wa-lā tuḍārrūhunna li-tuḍayyiqū ʿalayhinna

Al-Ṭalāq 65:6

Translation:

“House them where you live, according to your means; do not harm them to constrain them.”

This further clarifies the relationship between divorce and housing responsibility.

Details of duration, conditions, and individual situations require a separate study of the chapter.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:5: conditional divorce

عَسَىٰ رَبُّهُ إِنْ طَلَّقَكُنَّ أَنْ يُبْدِلَهُ أَزْوَاجًا خَيْرًا مِنْكُنَّ

ʿasā rabbuhu in ṭallaqakunna an yubdilahu azwājan khayran minkunna

Al-Taḥrīm 66:5

Translation:

“His Lord may, if he divorces you, replace you for him with wives better than you.”

طَلَّقَكُنَّ is form II with a second-person feminine plural object pronoun.

إِنْ introduces a condition.

The verse does not report divorce as an accomplished event at that moment; it presents it conditionally.

يُبْدِلَهُ is a different verb, describing replacement of spouses.

Divorce and replacement are distinct concepts.

ط ل ق itself does not mean that every divorce necessarily leads to another marriage.

7. Distinctions from related roots

Ṭ L Q and S R Ḥ

Both roots can concern release.

طَلَّقَ in the Qur’an concerns ending a marital bond.

سَرَّحَ in some verses describes releasing in a good or fair manner.

Al-Baqarah 2:229 places divorce alongside تسريح بإحسان.

They belong to the same legal context without being verbally identical.

Outside marriage, سرح also describes letting animals go to pasture.

Not every تسريح means divorce, and kindness in release should not be extracted from ط ل ق alone.

Ṭ L Q and F R Q

فرق concerns separating and separation.

Divorce can lead to spouses separating.

فرق has a broader field, including groups separating or distinctions between things.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:35 discusses reconciliation in marital conflict.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:28 uses أُسَرِّحْكُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا.

Separation, release, and divorce are related in family contexts, but are not one term with identical form and legal effects.

Ṭ L Q and ʿ Q D

عقد concerns binding and securing a connection.

Marriage establishes a bond with particular obligations.

Divorce can concern ending that bond.

Lexical explanations accordingly describe divorce as حَلُّ عُقْدَةِ النِّكَاحِ, “untying the marriage bond.”

This image does not mean an actual physical knot must be tied in marriage or untied for divorce.

The expression concerns a legal and social relationship.

Nor is ending every عقد divorce: a عقد may concern a transaction, covenant, or another obligation.

Ṭ L Q and Ḥ L L

حل can mean untying a knot or opening something.

This explains its use in describing divorce as untying the marriage bond.

حلال and the family حلل have a broader Qur’anic field, also involving permission and removal of prohibition.

Not everything freed or released is therefore religiously lawful.

For example, الطِّلْق can describe something without a particular restriction, but divine lawfulness or prohibition requires evidence concerning that subject.

The root of divorce likewise does not determine the permissibility of every relationship after separation; those questions depend on separate verses and conditions.

Ṭ L Q and KH L W / KH L Y

تخلية appears in explanations of ط ل ق with the sense of leaving free.

خَلَّى سَبِيلَهُ means leaving someone’s way open and releasing them.

This resembles freeing a captive or allowing an animal to move freely.

خلّى and طلق are not identical in every use.

For example, انطلق لسانه naturally describes fluent speech, but should not be equated with every use of خلو or تخلية.

Divorce also has an established marital meaning more specific than general release.

Ṭ L Q and R S L

أرسل commonly concerns sending or dispatching.

انطلق can describe moving or setting out.

إرسال emphasizes a sender dispatching someone or something.

انطلاق can focus on the movement itself.

Al-Kahf 18:71, for example, uses فانطلقا: “They both set out.”

The verb alone identifies no sender.

أرسل can instead describe one person sending another on a mission.

A shared field of movement does not make the roots identical.

Ṭ L Q and Ṭ L ʿ

The first two letters are shared, but the meanings differ.

ط ل ق concerns release, movement, and openness.

ط ل ع concerns appearing, rising, and becoming visible.

Sunrise belongs to ط ل ع, not ط ل ق.

Something may appear after an obstacle is opened, but that possible relationship does not merge the roots.

طَلْع, the initial blossom or fruit cluster of the date palm, also belongs to ط ل ع and does not concern divorce.

8. Challenges in understanding Qur’anic divorce

Release from marriage does not remove responsibility

The core of ط ل ق concerns release and removal of restrictions.

Qur’anic divorce nevertheless occurs within a legal and human relationship.

Release from marriage does not equal release from every resulting responsibility.

Separate verses address waiting, fairness, kindness, preventing harm, financial rights, and children.

Those instructions require examination in their own contexts.

Translating divorce simply as “freeing the woman” can mislead without the marital context, suggesting that she previously lacked human agency.

“Divorcing” or “ending a marital bond” is natural and precise in the family passages.

Divorce does not always mean final, irreversible separation

Al-Baqarah 2:228 mentions return for reconciliation.

Al-Baqarah 2:229 discusses two occasions of divorce followed by fair retention or kind release.

Al-Baqarah 2:230 introduces another situation in which return depends on further conditions.

The word divorce alone therefore does not make every separation final and irreversible.

Its effects depend on its place in the process and the conditions stated in the Qur’an.

Conversely, possible return must not license harmful retention or prolonged uncertainty: Al-Baqarah 2:231 explicitly prohibits such conduct.

Divorce and deciding on divorce differ

In Al-Baqarah 2:227, divorce is the object of عزموا.

Other verses use طلقتم or طلقها for the act of divorcing.

Decision and action are thus genuinely distinct in Qur’anic expression.

Translation should retain that distinction, while determining when a decision or pronouncement produces legal effects remains a separate question.

Not every separation is divorce

People separate for many reasons.

They may live apart, end a working relationship, or cease social contact after disagreement.

Qur’anic طلاق, however, concerns marriage.

Marriage ending through a spouse’s death also differs from divorce in terminology and stated consequences.

Al-Baqarah 2:234 discusses women whose husbands have died, whereas Al-Baqarah 2:228 concerns divorced women.

Their rules and waiting periods should not be conflated merely because both involve a marriage ending.

Divorce and human dignity

Several divorce passages use بِمَعْرُوفٍ, بِإِحْسَانٍ, and سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا.

Even when continuing married life is impossible or undesirable, the manner of separation remains an ethical responsibility.

Fairness, kindness, and dignity nevertheless require examination through their relevant roots and verses.

They should not be derived solely from ط ل ق.

Lexical study clarifies ending the bond; legal and ethical study examines the accompanying conditions and responsibilities.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

General Arabic uses can be rendered “released,” “freed,” “untied,” or “became open,” according to the construction.

For طَلِيق, “released” or “freed” is natural.

For طَلْقُ الْوَجْهِ, “open-faced” or “welcoming” is suitable.

For طَلْقُ اللِّسَان, “fluent in speech” is appropriate.

For انطلق, “set out” or “departed” is the base rendering in most Qur’anic passages.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:13, where the tongue is the subject, “the tongue flows” more precisely conveys fluency.

In marriage, طلاق is appropriately “divorce” or “marital separation.” The familiar word need not be replaced with cumbersome unfamiliar language.

مطلقات means “divorced women.”

طلقتم النساء naturally means “you divorced women.”

Brief root meaning

Releasing from restraint; in other uses, setting out, openness, and fluency; in marriage, ending the marital bond.

Meanings in Qur’anic contexts

Al-Kahf 18:71, Al-Kahf 18:74, and Al-Kahf 18:77: They both set out; two people moving and continuing their journey.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:13: Does not flow; Moses’ difficulty expressing himself.

Ṣād 38:6: They departed; movement of the community’s leaders.

Al-Fatḥ 48:15: You set out; movement toward spoils.

Al-Qalam 68:23: They set out; the garden owners’ movement.

Al-Mursalāt 77:29 and Al-Mursalāt 77:30: Go or proceed; commanded movement toward what the context describes.

Al-Baqarah 2:227: Divorce; the object of a decision to end marriage.

Al-Baqarah 2:228 and Al-Baqarah 2:241: Divorced women.

Al-Baqarah 2:229: Divorce, within a construction specifying two occasions.

Al-Baqarah 2:230: He divorced her; two occurrences with different subjects in the verse’s sequence.

Al-Baqarah 2:231 and Al-Baqarah 2:232: You divorced women; accompanying instructions concern conduct and rights after separation.

Al-Baqarah 2:236 and Al-Baqarah 2:237: Divorce before marital intimacy, distinguishing whether a financial provision has been assigned.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:49: Divorce after marriage but before intimacy.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:1: Divorcing and a command concerning its timing.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:5: If he divorces you; a conditional possibility.

The shared semantic core remains, but each derivative’s meaning depends on its construction and object.

10. Conclusion

ط ل ق concerns being unrestrained, releasing, removing restrictions, and moving without an obstacle.

Its physical uses include releasing an animal from a tether and freeing a captive.

Extended uses include setting out, fluency, a welcoming face, and openhandedness.

Early Arabic poetry attests marital divorce and openhandedness before later jurisprudential terminology became established.

The root has two prominent Qur’anic branches.

In the first, انطلق describes movement and departure, and in Moses’ speech a tongue lacking fluency. These uses have no fixed moral value: movement can serve different destinations and outcomes.

In the second, divorce concerns ending marriage. This established meaning is more specific than general release.

The divorce verses attach responsibilities to ending marriage: decision, waiting, reciprocal rights, reconciliation, preventing harm, fair release, and provision for divorced women.

Divorce should therefore not be equated with expelling a woman from her home, erasing all rights, or unilaterally escaping every obligation.

The root alone also does not determine the number of occasions, conditions of return, waiting periods, or financial details. Those emerge from the verses and their relationships.

Special uses such as labor pains and a leather tether are also recorded, but cannot all be derived from a single release image without historical evidence.

Final definition: In its main branch, ط ل ق concerns releasing, leaving free, and emerging from restraint. انطلاق describes setting out and movement, or fluent speech when applied to the tongue. In marriage, طلاق denotes ending the marriage bond. In the Qur’an, this does not erase every right and responsibility. Forqan’s study should therefore keep three related levels distinct: the general root meaning, established marital divorce, and the independent instructions governing separation.
