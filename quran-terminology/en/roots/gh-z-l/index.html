Spinning yarn, turning fibres into thread, love poetry, and the gazelle

The Arabic root gh z l has several recognized semantic branches: spinning wool, cotton, flax, and other fibres; amorous conversation, courtship, and love poetry; and ghazāl, a gazelle or young gazelle.

Arabic dictionaries record these branches under one entry, but their meanings differ. Without historical evidence, they cannot all be derived from a single original image.

In the first branch, ghazl denotes both spinning and the yarn produced by it. Mighzal is a spinning implement, and ghāzil or ghāzilah is a spinner.

In the second branch, ghazal concerns amorous speech and behaviour. In Arabic literature it also denotes expressions of love, descriptions of the beloved, and love poetry.

In the third branch, ghazāl names a gazelle and, especially in early usage, a young gazelle.

The root occurs only once in the Quran: ghazlahā in Al-Naḥl 16:92. It denotes yarn a woman has spun and then unravelled after making it firm. The Quran uses this image to illustrate the wrongfulness of breaking established covenants.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Spinning yarn and turning fibres into thread

Ghazalati al-marʾatu al-ṣūfa means that a woman spun wool.

Loose wool, cotton, or flax fibres are gradually drawn out and twisted together to make a continuous, usable thread.

For example, wool shorn from a sheep initially consists of loose fibres. To become yarn, these must be prepared, drawn out, and twisted. Spinning produces thread that can be used for weaving cloth, making cord, or other purposes.

Here ghazala is the verb “to spin,” while ghazl denotes both the activity and its product.

This distinction matters: an Arabic verbal noun can name both an action and its result.

Ghazl can therefore mean either spinning or spun yarn.

Al-Naḥl 16:92 uses the second sense, since the woman unravels something already spun.

Spinning and weaving

Spinning yarn and weaving cloth are different operations.

Spinning turns fibres into thread. Weaving arranges and interlaces threads in a pattern to produce cloth or another woven object.

Wool may first be turned into yarn and that yarn then used to weave clothing.

In its principal material use, ghazala refers to spinning, rather than every stage of textile production.

Accordingly, “her spun yarn” translates Quranic ghazlahā more precisely than “her woven cloth.”

This distinction also matters for Al-Naḥl 16:92, which describes unravelling yarn made firm by twisting fibres together.

2. Evidence from early language

The gazelle in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

بِجِيدِ مُغْزِلَةٍ أَدْمَاءَ خَاذِلَةٍ

bi-jīdi mughzilatin admāʾa khādhilatin

مِنَ الظِّبَاءِ تَرَاعِي شَادِنًا خَرِقَا

mina al-ẓibāʾi turāʿī shādinan khariqā

Translation:

“With a neck like that of a pale doe, apart from the herd, watching over her frightened fawn crouched on the ground.”

Mughzilah here describes a doe with a fawn.

It differs from mighzal, a spinning implement, although the unvowelled forms look very similar.

In this witness, mughzilah describes the gazelle and belongs to the ghazāl branch.

The verse is preserved in recognized transmissions of Zuhayr’s poetry and provides evidence of early usage concerning a gazelle and its young.

Its existence does not establish that the word for spinning derives from the animal’s name.

Spinning in Jandal al-Ṭahawī’s verse

A rajaz passage attributed to Jandal ibn al-Muthannā al-Ṭahawī says:

كَأَنَّهُ بِالصَّحْصَحَانِ الْأَنْجَلِ

ka-annahu bi-l-ṣaḥṣaḥāni al-anjali

قُطْنٌ سُخَامٌ بِأَيْدِي غُزَّلِ

quṭnun sukhāmun bi-aydī ghuzzali

Translation:

“As though that mirage on the broad, level plain were soft cotton in spinners’ hands.”

The poet compares a mirage to soft cotton held by people spinning it.

Ghuzzal here is a plural referring to spinners.

The image comes from an everyday activity: loose, soft cotton held in the hands and turned into thread.

It clearly illustrates the material activity of spinning.

Jandal al-Ṭahawī was an Umayyad poet, rather than an established pre-Islamic witness. The attribution also varies, with the lines sometimes assigned to another poet.

The passage illustrates the continued use of this sense in early Arabic, but should not be presented as certain pre-Islamic evidence.

The spinning witness cited here does not have a securely established pre-Quranic date. The Quran’s own use of ghazl nevertheless shows that this meaning was intelligible in the Arabic of its first audience.

Three branches that should not be forced into one

Some Arabic dictionaries distinguish three principal branches under gh z l:

Spun yarn, amorous conversation, and the gazelle.

One proposed connection between love poetry and the gazelle invokes the comparison of the beloved to that animal.

Another proposed image connects spinning thread with composing love poetry, as though the poet spins words into verse.

Such images may be attractive literary explanations, but do not themselves establish a word’s history.

Nor does the existence of pre-Islamic love poetry show that ghazal already had precisely the same technical literary scope as in later periods.

A lexical account should identify each branch separately and avoid unsupported claims of a common historical origin.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Recognized Arabic forms in this family include:

  • Ghazala / yaghzilu: to spin; turn fibres into yarn.
  • Ghazl: spinning; also spun yarn or thread.
  • Maghzūl: something spun.
  • Mighzal: a spinning implement.
  • Ghāzil / ghāzilah: a spinner.
  • Ghuzzal / ghawāzil: plural forms for spinners.
  • Ightazala: to spin; in the fibre-related sense, close to the simple verb.
  • Aghzala: in one lexical usage, to set a spindle turning.
  • Ghazal: amorous speech and conduct; in literature, love discourse or poetry.
  • Ghazila: to engage in amorous speech or conduct; in other uses, to become weak or unable to pursue something.
  • Ghāzala / yughāzilu: to converse or behave amorously with another.
  • Mughāzalah: amorous conversation and courtship.
  • Taghazzala: to compose love poetry or speak amorously of the beloved.
  • Ghazil: someone inclined to amorous speech and conduct; in another branch, someone weak or ineffective in a task.
  • Ghazāl: a gazelle, particularly a young one in early usage.
  • Ghazālah: a female gazelle; in another usage, a name for the sun or a portion of its morning ascent.
  • Ghizlān: plural of ghazāl.
  • Mughzil: describing a doe that has a fawn.

Of these forms, only ghazlahā occurs in the Quran.

Ghazal as love poetry, ghazāl as gazelle, and mighzal as spinning implement help explain the lexical family, but do not occur in the Quran.

Different forms and vowels

Vowel differences can distinguish meanings in Arabic.

Ghazl, with no vowel after zāy, principally concerns spinning and spun yarn.

Ghazal, with a short a after zāy, denotes amorous speech, courtship, and love discourse.

The verb ghazala, “spun,” also differs in vocalization from ghazila in the amorous sense.

Analysis must preserve these distinctions.

Quranic ghazlahā belongs to spinning. Its resemblance to Persian ghazal, love poetry, should not alter the verse’s meaning.

4. Ghazlahā in the Quran

Al-Naḥl 16:92

وَلَا تَكُونُوا كَالَّتِي نَقَضَتْ غَزْلَهَا مِن بَعْدِ قُوَّةٍ أَنكَاثًا تَتَّخِذُونَ أَيْمَانَكُمْ دَخَلًا بَيْنَكُمْ أَن تَكُونَ أُمَّةٌ هِيَ أَرْبَىٰ مِنْ أُمَّةٍ إِنَّمَا يَبْلُوكُمُ اللَّهُ بِهِ وَلَيُبَيِّنَنَّ لَكُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ مَا كُنتُمْ فِيهِ تَخْتَلِفُونَ

wa-lā takūnū ka-allatī naqaḍat ghazlahā min baʿdi quwwatin ankāthan tattakhidhūna aymānakum dakhalan baynakum an takūna ummatun hiya arbā min ummatin innamā yablūkumu allāhu bihi wa-la-yubayyinanna lakum yawma al-qiyāmati mā kuntum fīhi takhtalifūn

Al-Naḥl 16:92

“Do not be like the woman who unravelled her yarn after it had become firm, reducing it to untwisted strands, making your oaths a means of deceit among yourselves because one community is greater than another. The God only tests you through this, and on the Day of Resurrection He will surely make clear to you what you differed over.”

The meaning of ghazlahā

Ghazlahā consists of the noun ghazl and the attached pronoun hā.

Here ghazl denotes the product of spinning: spun yarn or thread.

Hā refers to the woman introduced by ka-allatī, “like the woman who.”

Grammatically, ghazlahā is the object of naqaḍat: what the woman unravelled.

Its direct meaning is therefore “her spun yarn.”

The word itself does not mean cloth, thick rope, covenant, or oath. Such ideas come from other words or belong to the analogy and reflection.

Naqaḍat: undoing what has been made firm

Naqaḍat belongs to the independent root n q ḍ and concerns undoing something previously joined or made firm.

The woman unravels yarn she has spun.

Imagine someone spending hours spinning wool into strong thread, then undoing its twists until it loses its earlier firmness and continuity.

The person’s later action undoes the work of making the yarn.

This image relates to covenant-breaking, but ghazala itself does not mean making a covenant.

Spun yarn supplies the image; breaking a covenant is what the Quran explains through it.

Min baʿdi quwwah: after becoming firm

Quwwah here concerns the firmness and strength of the spun yarn.

Correctly twisted fibres acquire a cohesion and strength they lacked before spinning.

The verse says the woman unravelled her yarn after it had attained such firmness.

Quwwah here need not denote her physical strength or social power.

The appropriate contextual meaning is the yarn’s firmness.

Ankāthan: untwisted strands

Ankāth belongs to n k th and here concerns what remains when the twists of yarn are undone.

It emphasizes the result of unravelling.

Yarn previously made firm by twisting fibres returns to a loose, disjoined state.

Untwisting yarn differs from cutting it with a tool: it does not necessarily sever individual fibres.

Ankāthan can thus be explained as “untwisted strands” or “undone twists.”

This distinction matters because the analogy concerns destroying the firmness of something previously made.

5. Why use the example of spinning yarn?

Before the analogy, Al-Naḥl 16:91 commands fulfilment of the covenant and prohibits breaking oaths after confirming them.

Al-Naḥl 16:92 then illustrates covenant-breaking through a woman unravelling yarn after making it firm.

The relationship between the verses is clear.

Spinning joins loose fibres into continuous thread through effort and order.

Making a covenant similarly involves accepting an obligation and establishing trust and responsibility between people.

Breaking a confirmed covenant, particularly through deception, resembles unravelling yarn one previously worked to make.

For example, two groups might conclude a peace agreement after prolonged conflict, creating security, trust, and opportunities for cooperation.

If one group enjoys that security and then secretly breaks its commitment on finding a stronger ally, it destroys the trust established.

This involves more than changing one’s mind: it breaks a commitment on which another party has relied.

Aymānakum dakhalan baynakum

The verse continues with oaths made into a means of deception among people.

Aymān here is the plural of yamīn, “oath,” and differs from īmān, “faith.”

Their different spellings and roots matter.

The verse concerns commitments confirmed by oaths that are subsequently used to deceive.

Its censure concerns more than revising a private decision: it concerns using an oath and commitment to deceive the other party.

An takūna ummatun hiya arbā min ummah

This phrase speaks of one community being greater than another.

A group may exceed another in numbers, power, or resources.

The verse warns against making a covenant with the weaker group a means of deceit because of this difference.

A comparable example would be someone who deceives a smaller business partner, disregarding existing commitments after receiving a more profitable offer from a larger company.

This illustration does not mean that every revision of an agreement or ending of cooperation is forbidden. Agreements may end by mutual consent, expiry, or clear and legitimate conditions.

The verse concerns breaking a confirmed commitment through deceit in pursuit of advantage with a stronger group.

This distinction prevents the spinning image from becoming an absolute rule condemning every change in a relationship or decision.

Was the woman a particular historical person?

Some exegetical reports mention a woman in Mecca who would unravel her yarn after spinning it.

The Quran gives no name, ancestry, or place of residence here.

Even if such a person existed, identifying her is unnecessary for understanding ghazlahā and the analogy.

The image itself is clear: someone works to make something, then undoes it after it becomes firm.

This behaviour illustrates the wrongfulness of covenant-breaking.

For a broader discussion of fidelity to covenants, consult Forqan’s study of covenant in the Quran.

6. Love poetry and the gazelle: other branches

Ghazal: amorous conversation

Lexical sources record ghazal for amorous conversation and behaviour between a woman and a man.

Ghāzala means to speak or behave amorously with another.

Taghazzala concerns composing love poetry, expressing love, or describing the beloved.

In Arabic literature, ghazal became a designation for love discourse and poetry.

The usage is familiar in Persian too, where ghazal also names a specific poetic form beyond its association with love.

This literary sense should not be transferred to Al-Naḥl 16:92.

The woman in that verse was spinning yarn, not composing love poetry.

Does love poetry derive its name from spinning?

A literary comparison between making thread and composing speech is possible.

As a spinner joins loose fibres into continuous thread, a poet arranges words into ordered discourse.

This analogy is not a proven historical etymology.

A claim that love poetry was named directly after spinning requires independent historical and linguistic evidence.

Until that connection is established, these recorded meanings are better presented separately.

Ghazāl: a gazelle or fawn

Particularly in early Arabic, ghazāl denotes a young gazelle. Ghazālah is its feminine form.

The beauty of a gazelle’s eyes, neck, and movements made it a poetic comparison for the human beloved.

That association is familiar in love poetry and links the gazelle’s image with the language of love.

Yet semantic resemblance and poetic imagery do not prove that either spinning or love poetry necessarily derives its name from the animal.

Ghazālah: a name for the sun

Some Arabic usages call the sun, or the sun during its morning ascent, ghazālah.

Ghazālat al-ḍuḥā concerns the sun or its position during part of the morning.

Some lexicographers have proposed a metaphorical connection to the gazelle.

The usage does not establish its historical origin or show that every form under gh z l concerns the sun.

These branches are recorded in Arabic but are absent from the root’s Quranic usage.

7. Comparison with related roots

Gh z l and f t l: spinning and twisting

F t l concerns twisting or winding something.

In textile work, ghazl denotes turning fibres into yarn, a process in which twisting is essential.

The concepts are related but distinct.

An existing cord or thread may be twisted without spinning new yarn from raw fibres.

Al-Naḥl 16:92 concerns spun yarn, with its resulting firmness expressed by min baʿdi quwwah.

Twisting and firmness should therefore be understood through the process and the phrase, rather than assigned entirely to gh z l itself.

Gh z l and n s j: spinning and weaving

Nasj concerns weaving and interlacing threads.

Ghazl concerns spinning fibres into yarn.

Spinning may precede weaving in textile production, but that practical sequence does not make the verbs identical.

Without further evidence, Quranic ghazlahā should not be translated as “the cloth she had woven.”

Its more direct meaning is spun thread or yarn.

Gh z l and n q ḍ: making yarn and undoing it

Both roots appear in the image in Al-Naḥl 16:92.

Ghazl identifies the yarn produced by spinning.

Naqḍ denotes undoing and unravelling it.

One concerns the product of spinning; the other concerns destroying its cohesion and firmness.

This contrast does not make every Quranic use of naqḍ a reference to spinning. With objects such as covenants and pledges, it takes the meaning appropriate to that object.

Gh z l and n k th: spun yarn and its unravelling

Nakth concerns undoing something twisted or made firm and, socially, breaking a covenant.

Ankāthan in Al-Naḥl 16:92 describes the yarn’s unravelled state.

This connection between material imagery and covenant-breaking clarifies the analogy.

Ghazl is the yarn itself, while nakth concerns undoing its bonds.

The meaning of covenant-breaking should therefore not be transferred to gh z l.

Gh z l and ḥ b l: spun thread and rope

Ḥabl means rope or cord and can elsewhere denote a bond or means of connection.

Ghazl names spinning or the yarn it produces.

Spun yarn may be used to make rope, but not every spun thread is a rope.

“Her rope” might be conceivable in an illustrative explanation, but “her spun yarn” is closer to the wording.

Gh z l and gh s l: independent roots

Gh z l and gh s l resemble each other in writing and sound but are different roots.

Quranic gh s l concerns washing and cleansing with water.

Quranic gh z l concerns spinning yarn.

Their shared first and last consonants, and the phonetic proximity of zāy and sīn, do not establish a semantic or historical relationship.

8. Difficulties in understanding and translation

Does ghazlahā mean her love poem?

No. Quranic ghazlahā denotes spun yarn.

Love poetry belongs to another branch and does not fit the context of unravelling yarn into ankāth.

Does it mean woven cloth?

Its more precise meaning is spun yarn or thread.

That yarn might later become cloth, but gh z l here does not denote the entire process of weaving.

Does ghazl itself mean strength?

No. Spinning can produce firm, continuous yarn, but its strength depends on the spinning process and the yarn’s quality.

Min baʿdi quwwah in Al-Naḥl 16:92 makes its firmness explicit.

This contextual feature should not be treated as inherent in every use of ghazl.

Must the woman be a specific historical person?

The verse itself does not establish such an identity.

It offers a clear image of conduct whose wrongfulness the audience can understand.

Knowing the woman’s name and biography is therefore not a condition for understanding the word or analogy.

Does the verse condemn every change of decision?

No. The spinning image occurs in the context of covenants and oaths.

The subject is a confirmed commitment broken and made into an instrument of deceit.

Correcting a mistaken decision, ending an agreement on legitimate terms, or revising it by mutual consent is not in itself the same as deceptive covenant-breaking.

The image should not become a universal ruling that people may never reconsider their decisions.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ghazlahā in Al-Naḥl 16:92, “her spun yarn” is precise and clear.

In a flowing sentence, “her yarn” also suffices because spinning is understood from the context.

Suggested rendering of the phrase:

“Do not be like the woman who unravelled her yarn after it had become firm.”

To express ankāthan within the same sentence:

“Do not be like the woman who unravelled her yarn after it had become firm, reducing it to untwisted strands.”

For non-Quranic uses, the following equivalents are proposed:

Ghazala: spun.

Ghazl: spinning or spun yarn.

Maghzūl: spun.

Mighzal: spindle or spinning implement.

Ghāzil / ghāzilah: spinner.

Ghazal: amorous conversation or discourse; in literature, love poetry.

Ghāzala: spoke or behaved amorously with another.

Taghazzala: composed love poetry or spoke amorously of the beloved.

Ghazāl: gazelle or fawn, depending on usage.

Ghazālah: female gazelle; elsewhere, a name for the sun.

Mughzil: describing a doe that has a fawn.

Translation and analysis must preserve vowel patterns, morphology, and the sentence’s subject matter. Written resemblance alone does not justify transferring one branch’s meaning to another.

Conclusion

Arabic gh z l has several recognized branches: spinning yarn, amorous speech and conversation, and the gazelle’s name.

In spinning, ghazl denotes turning fibres into thread and the resulting yarn. It is distinguishable from weaving cloth and making rope.

The amorous branch concerns love discourse and conduct and became a name for love poetry. Ghazāl names a gazelle or fawn, used poetically to describe the beloved’s beauty.

Lexical and poetic evidence establishes these branches, but does not provide sufficient historical evidence to derive them all from one original image.

The Quran uses only ghazlahā in Al-Naḥl 16:92, denoting yarn the woman has spun.

Naqaḍat denotes unravelling it; min baʿdi quwwah its previous firmness; and ankāthan its unravelled state.

The Quran uses the image to explain covenant-breaking: people confirm a commitment, then turn it into a means of deceit in pursuit of another advantage.

Thus, the direct Quranic sense of gh z l is spinning and spun yarn, not making covenants, breaking them, or composing love poetry.

Spun yarn supplies the image; fidelity to a covenant is the analogy’s ethical and social message.

This distinction preserves the word’s lexical and historical meaning while keeping the Quranic image in its proper context.
