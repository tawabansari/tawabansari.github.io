Qur’anic Root أ س ف

Deep grief, sorrow and anger: from Jacob’s lament to Moses’ distress and divine anger

Qur’anic and lexical forms

يَا أَسَفَىٰ
(yā asafā)
Qur’anic vocative with asaf and “my”; Yūsuf 12:84

أَسَفًا
(asafan)
Qur’anic accusative verbal noun: out of grief, Al-Kahf 18:6

أَسِفًا
(asifan)
Qur’anic accusative description of Moses, twice: Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 and Ṭā Hā 20:86

آسَفُونَا
(āsafūnā)
Qur’anic Form IV perfect: they provoked Us to anger, Al-Zukhruf 43:55

أَسِفَ / يَأْسَفُ
(asifa / yaʾsafu)
Lexical Form I citation forms: to grieve or become angry; these forms are not Qur’anic

آسِف
(āsif)
Lexical description of a grieving or distressed person; this form is absent from the Qur’an

أَسْفَان
(asfān)
Lexical description within the grief and anger family; not Qur’anic

أَسُوف
(asūf)
Lexical description of someone readily moved to sorrow; not Qur’anic

أَسِيف
(asīf)
Lexical description in Al-Aʿshā’s verse: grieving or angry according to context; not Qur’anic

تَأَسَّفَ
(taʾassafa)
Lexical verb: grieved or sorrowed over something; not Qur’anic

آسَفَهُ
(āsafahu)
Lexical citation of the transitive verb: he made him angry or distressed

The Arabic root أ س ف concerns grief, sorrow, distress and anger. People may experience the loss of something dear with grief and longing, or become so troubled by wrongful conduct that they grow angry. In both situations, an unwelcome matter has affected them intensely.

Early Arabic used أَسَف for both deep grief and anger. These meanings are separate in some situations and coexist in others. “Grief,” “anger” or “remorse” alone therefore cannot serve as the equivalent of every derivative.

The Qur’an uses this root in five verses. Jacob grieves at Joseph’s absence; Muhammad is so affected by his audience’s failure to believe that the Qur’an warns about the intensity of his grief; Moses returns to his people غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا; and in Pharaoh’s story, The God states that when they آسَفُونَا, We punished them.

These uses show that أَسَف does not name one simple, uniform feeling. Grief and anger belong to its semantic field; which is prominent in a verse depends on the word’s form, its object and the sentence’s context.

1. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

The root أ س ف occurs five times in the Qur’an, in three principal morphological forms.

أَسِفًا in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 and Ṭā Hā 20:86 describes Moses’ condition on returning to his people.

أَسَف occurs in يَا أَسَفَىٰ in Yūsuf 12:84 and أَسَفًا in Al-Kahf 18:6. Deep grief and sorrow are prominent in both.

آسَفُونَا in Al-Zukhruf 43:55 is a Form IV perfect verb. In the setting of Pharaoh’s conduct and that of his followers, it refers to provoking The God to anger.

The basic Arabic verb is أَسِفَ يَأْسَفُ, with the verbal noun أَسَف. Forms including آسِف, أَسِف, أَسْفَان, أَسُوف and أَسِيف are also recorded for a grieving, distressed or angry person.

تَأَسَّفَ concerns grieving and sorrowing over something; آسَفَهُ has been used for making another angry or distressed.

The basic verb أَسِفَ and the forms تَأَسَّفَ, أَسِيف and أَسْفَان do not occur in the Qur’an. Its only verbal occurrence is آسَفُونَا.

The family’s semantic core can be explained as:

Being intensely affected by an unwelcome matter, with that effect appearing as grief, sorrow, distress or anger.

This definition connects the two principal branches, but should not become a certain theory of every derivative’s historical origin. Nor is every instance of grief or anger necessarily called أَسَف. Each word and its context must establish the particular meaning.

2. Early Arabic usage: from sorrow over loss to anger

أَسِفَ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ: grief and sorrow over something

A familiar early Arabic use of أَسِفَ concerns grieving over something lost or something that occurred against a person’s wishes.

أَسِفَ عَلَى مَا فَاتَهُ means grieving and sorrowing over what one has lost. تَأَسَّفَ belongs to the same semantic field.

Early explanations sometimes describe أَسَف as intense grief, and sometimes as the sorrow and agitation arising from loss.

This use shows how grief in the root may relate to attachment to something or someone. Losing what is desired, or finding an important wish apparently impossible to fulfill, may occasion أَسَف.

This connection does not make أَسَف invariably involve remorse over one’s own wrongdoing. People may grieve over losing a loved one without having done anything wrong.

This distinction matters for يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ. Jacob suffers from separation from Joseph; the expression itself is no confession of sin or regret over a wrongful decision.

أَسِفَ عَلَيْهِ: anger and agitation

Another use, أَسِفَ عَلَيْهِ, can mean becoming angry with someone.

Here the person does more than grieve over an unwelcome occurrence: that person is stirred by conduct or a condition regarded as objectionable.

Arabic also records آسَفَهُ for making another angry.

This meaning cannot simply be extracted from sorrow and grief. Anger may respond to aggression, opposition or wrongdoing even when no direct personal loss has occurred.

Grief and anger are not always entirely separate, however. Someone may suffer because of wrongful conduct while also becoming angry with its agent.

Some early Arabic explanations describe an unwelcome effect appearing as anger when a person can confront its cause, and as grief when that person is powerless against it.

This may help explain the relationship, but is no absolute law of language or human psychology. People may be angry with someone powerful or grieve over someone weaker. Qur’anic uses likewise do not allow grief and anger to be separated solely through the parties’ relative power.

Some early writings explain this distinction through blood moving or boiling in the heart. Such accounts belong to earlier attempts to explain emotions; they are not lexical evidence that the root’s historical meaning necessarily arose from a particular bodily process.

A poetic witness from early Arabic

Al-Aʿshā, a well-known Arabic poet of the pre-Islamic and early Islamic period, says:

أَرَى رَجُلًا مِنْهُمْ أَسِيفًا كَأَنَّمَا
يَضُمُّ إِلَىٰ كَشْحَيْهِ كَفًّا مُخَضَّبَا

arā rajulan minhum asīfan ka-annamā / yaḍummu ilā kashḥayhi kaffan mukhaḍḍabā

“I see a man among them deeply distressed and agitated, as though pressing a stained hand against his sides.”

Some transmissions have مِنْكُمْ in place of مِنْهُمْ.

Several explanations of أَسِيف have been proposed here. In the anger reading, the man is likened to someone whose hand has been severed and stained with blood, leaving him angry; the image does not mean that anger itself severed the hand. Another reading emphasizes grief and sorrow resulting from injury to, or loss of, the hand.

Another explanation concerns the person being a captive.

The verse therefore attests أَسِيف in early Arabic, but should not conclusively establish the priority of either grief or anger.

It matters because أَسِيف describes an intense state of distress and agitation. Disagreement over the specific image is consistent with the word family’s known breadth.

The verse’s precise date is unknown. Although its poet belongs to the pre-Islamic period, independent evidence is needed to assert that this particular verse necessarily predates the Qur’an’s revelation.

Other uses and their limits

Some Arabic dictionaries also record أَسِيف for a sensitive person readily moved to sorrow, whose grief becomes apparent.

Uses concerning sparsely vegetated ground or an animal that scarcely grows fat are also reported within the family. Some lexicographers connect these with deprivation of something desired, such as ground deprived of plant growth.

This connection should not become the certain origin of the grief and anger senses. These uses are absent from the Qur’an; the emotional branches provide clearer support for understanding its verses.

Another issue is أَسِيف for a servant or dependent in some linguistic reports. Its initial consonant may have changed from ʿayn to hamzah, linking it with عَسِيف.

Outward resemblance to أَسِيف meaning “grieving” is therefore insufficient to establish their shared historical derivation.

3. يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ: Jacob’s grief in separation from his son

Yūsuf states:

وَتَوَلَّىٰ عَنْهُمْ وَقَالَ يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ وَابْيَضَّتْ عَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْحُزْنِ فَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ

wa-tawallā ʿanhum wa-qāla yā asafā ʿalā yūsufa wa-ibyaḍḍat ʿaynāhu mina al-ḥuzni fa-huwa kaẓīm

“And he turned away from them and said: Ah, what grief over Joseph! His eyes had become white from grief, while he held his sorrow within.”

Yūsuf 12:84

يَا أَسَفَىٰ expresses Jacob’s deep grief.

The phrase combines a vocative particle with أَسَف and the first-person possessive pronoun. Its underlying form is explained as يا أَسَفِي, with the possessive yāʾ changing to alif. Such a vocative can disclose the intensity of a feeling in Arabic.

Jacob is not calling a person named Asaf. He voices his grief in an emotional expression such as “Ah, my sorrow!” or “What sorrow over Joseph!”

عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ identifies the object of grief. Jacob grieves over separation from Joseph and his unknown fate.

الْحُزْنِ appears later in the verse as well. This pairing matters for understanding أَسَف.

أَسَف and حُزْن within this verse

أَسَف in Jacob’s cry makes the intensity of his sorrow and grief apparent.

حُزْن later expresses his state of grief, to which the whitening of his eyes is attributed.

The words occupy one semantic field, but contribute from different angles within the sentence.

يَا أَسَفَىٰ is a cry of grief and sorrow; مِنَ الْحُزْنِ gives the cause of the eyes’ condition in the verse’s language.

أَسَف here is thus more than the name of a calm, concealed state: it is associated with grief’s intense expression.

This does not make أَسَف invariably stronger than حُزْن. Lexicons report such a tendency, but the word form and context also affect the intensity of each feeling.

The verse likewise offers no medical explanation for the whitening of the eyes. The sentence does not establish a particular eye-disease diagnosis or a scientific theory of grief’s direct effect on vision.

فَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ: holding grief within

كَظِيمٌ belongs to ك ظ م and concerns suppressing or holding something inside.

Study of K Ẓ M

Jacob has deep grief; كَظِيمٌ shows that he holds it within.

This description differs from أَسَف. أَسَف expresses the grief and sorrow themselves; كَظِيمٌ describes containing and restraining them.

Holding grief within does not establish that Jacob never expresses his feeling. The verse itself quotes his cry of sorrow.

In Yūsuf 12:86, he also speaks of taking his complaint of anguish and grief to The God. Deep grief, containing it and turning to The God are therefore not presented as incompatible in this story.

Together, these passages show that أَسَف in Jacob’s story concerns neither anger nor remorse for a sin. It is deep grief over the loss and absence of someone dear.

4. أَسَفًا in Al-Kahf: the Prophet’s grief over people not believing

Al-Kahf states:

فَلَعَلَّكَ بَاخِعٌ نَفْسَكَ عَلَىٰ آثَارِهِمْ إِنْ لَمْ يُؤْمِنُوا بِهَٰذَا الْحَدِيثِ أَسَفًا

fa-laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka ʿalā āthārihim in lam yuʾminū bi-hādhā al-ḥadīthi asafā

“Perhaps, then, you would wear yourself to death in their wake, if they do not believe in this discourse, out of intense grief.”

Al-Kahf 18:6

أَسَفًا here concerns the Prophet’s inward condition in response to his audience not believing.

بَاخِعٌ نَفْسَكَ concerns destroying or bringing oneself to ruin. The sentence depicts grief so intense that the Prophet seems to exhaust and wear himself away over their failure to believe.

Study of B KH ʿ

أَسَفًا is a verbal noun here, expressing the cause or condition associated with that exhaustion.

Unlike Yūsuf 12:84, the object of grief is not a lost loved one. It is people whom the Prophet wants to accept the truth, but who turn away from belief.

أَسَف is therefore not necessarily restricted to physical loss or separation from someone dear. An important desire remaining unfulfilled may also cause deep grief.

Does أَسَفًا here mean anger?

Some early explanations also gloss أَسَفًا here as anger. The root’s lexical range permits that possibility, since أَسَف can concern anger.

The context nevertheless supports grief more strongly.

بَاخِعٌ نَفْسَكَ describes someone being worn down by intense inward suffering. Here that construction fits grief over the audience not believing more closely than anger against them.

This reading also agrees with other verses, including Fāṭir 35:8, which forbids wearing oneself away in sorrow over people who have chosen a wrongful path.

Grief should nevertheless not be converted into indifference toward their wrongdoing. The Prophet can regard their conduct as wrong while also grieving over their outcome.

Does the verse condemn the Prophet’s grief?

The construction draws attention to grief’s intensity. It does not make compassion and sorrow over people’s error inherently objectionable.

The issue is how deeply grief affects someone, to the point of wearing that person away.

أَسَفًا does not name a responsibility to force others into guidance. The Prophet is commissioned to convey the message; accepting or rejecting it also concerns his audience’s will and responsibility.

The basic translation should therefore not restrict أَسَفًا to “anger at disbelievers,” “regret over inviting them” or “a sense of failure in the mission.” The verse directly concerns intense grief over the audience not believing.

5. غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا: Moses’ anger and grief

The Qur’an describes Moses as غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا twice.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150: Moses returns and confronts his people’s conduct

وَلَمَّا رَجَعَ مُوسَىٰ إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا قَالَ بِئْسَمَا خَلَفْتُمُونِي مِنْ بَعْدِي أَعَجِلْتُمْ أَمْرَ رَبِّكُمْ وَأَلْقَى الْأَلْوَاحَ وَأَخَذَ بِرَأْسِ أَخِيهِ يَجُرُّهُ إِلَيْهِ قَالَ ابْنَ أُمَّ إِنَّ الْقَوْمَ اسْتَضْعَفُونِي وَكَادُوا يَقْتُلُونَنِي فَلَا تُشْمِتْ بِيَ الْأَعْدَاءَ وَلَا تَجْعَلْنِي مَعَ الْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-lammā rajaʿa mūsā ilā qawmihi ghaḍbāna asifan qāla biʾsamā khalaftumūnī min baʿdī a-ʿajiltum amra rabbikum wa-alqā al-alwāḥa wa-akhadha bi-raʾsi akhīhi yajurruhu ilayhi qāla ibna umma inna al-qawma istaḍʿafūnī wa-kādū yaqtulūnanī fa-lā tushmit biya al-aʿdāʾa wa-lā tajʿalnī maʿa al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn

“When Moses returned to his people angry and grieved, he said: What an evil succession you made after me! Did you hasten your Lord’s affair? He cast down the tablets and seized his brother’s head, drawing him toward himself. His brother said: Son of my mother, the people overpowered me and almost killed me; do not make the enemies rejoice at my misfortune, and do not place me among the wrongdoing people.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150

غَضْبَانَ and أَسِفًا both describe Moses’ condition.

غَضْبَانَ belongs to غ ض ب and denotes being angry.

أَسِفًا belongs to أ س ف and can express grief, distress or intense anger.

Their juxtaposition raises an important question: does the Qur’an express Moses’ anger with two words, or speak of two related but distinct states?

Are غَضْبَانَ and أَسِفًا synonyms?

أَسِف can itself mean angry in Arabic. Lexically, غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا may therefore emphasize anger’s intensity.

Since other Qur’anic uses of أَسَف concern deep grief as well, another possibility remains: Moses is both angry and grieved by his people’s conduct.

This reading fits the story’s context.

In his absence, Moses’ people have turned to worshipping the calf. On returning, he confronts wrongful conduct incompatible with their guidance and covenant.

His anger may respond to this disobedience, while his grief arises from the deviation of people for whose guidance he has worked.

This analysis does not make غَضْبَانَ always mean anger alone and أَسِفًا always grief alone. Arabic does not support such an absolute separation.

“Angry and grieved” preserves both semantic possibilities for this verse. “Angry and deeply agitated” also remains lexically possible.

The preference for the first reading is supported by context and other uses of أ س ف, rather than an absolute rule about the two words’ meanings.

Moses’ conduct and intense agitation

The verse continues with Moses casting down the tablets and seizing his brother’s head, drawing him toward himself.

His response is therefore more than a concealed inward feeling.

Each detail should not, however, be assigned to a separate root: casting down the tablets cannot be attributed exclusively to ghaḍab and seizing Aaron’s head exclusively to asaf.

Both descriptions characterize Moses’ overall condition as he confronts the event.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:154 describes Moses’ anger subsiding after further dialogue and prayer. His intense response therefore continues within the story and then settles.

That verse concerns غ ض ب directly, rather than أ س ف. Without evidence, the subsiding of anger should not be made into the necessary end of all Moses’ grief.

Ṭā Hā 20:86: the same description in another account

Ṭā Hā states:

فَرَجَعَ مُوسَىٰ إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا قَالَ يَا قَوْمِ أَلَمْ يَعِدْكُمْ رَبُّكُمْ وَعْدًا حَسَنًا أَفَطَالَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْعَهْدُ أَمْ أَرَدْتُمْ أَنْ يَحِلَّ عَلَيْكُمْ غَضَبٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ فَأَخْلَفْتُمْ مَوْعِدِي

fa-rajaʿa mūsā ilā qawmihi ghaḍbāna asifan qāla yā qawmi a-lam yaʿidkum rabbukum waʿdan ḥasanan a-fa-ṭāla ʿalaykumu al-ʿahdu am aradtum an yaḥilla ʿalaykum ghaḍabun min rabbikum fa-akhlaftum mawʿidī

“Then Moses returned to his people angry and grieved. He said: My people, did your Lord not make you a good promise? Did the interval seem long to you, or did you wish for anger from your Lord to descend upon you, so that you broke your promise to me?”

Ṭā Hā 20:86

The same descriptions, غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا, appear here, while Moses’ speech is presented differently.

He recalls the Lord’s good promise and asks why his people have broken their undertaking and appointment with him.

These questions connect his response with an assessment of their conduct.

The verse also contains غَضَبٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ. Moses’ own anger must therefore be distinguished from the anger from The God of which he warns as a consequence of their conduct.

Moses’ anger is his state on returning; divine anger in his speech concerns the consequences of the people’s behavior.

These concepts are related, but using ghaḍab in both constructions does not make every feature of human and divine anger identical.

The repeated غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا in Al-Aʿrāf and Ṭā Hā also shows that the description is not incidental. The Qur’an preserves it in two related accounts of one event.

Each verse nevertheless needs its own context. Al-Aʿrāf emphasizes Moses’ conduct and dialogue with Aaron; Ṭā Hā makes his questioning of the people and their broken promise more prominent.

The root’s meaning remains the same in both verses, while each context clarifies part of Moses’ condition.

6. فَلَمَّا آسَفُونَا: divine anger in Pharaoh’s story

Al-Zukhruf states:

فَلَمَّا آسَفُونَا انْتَقَمْنَا مِنْهُمْ فَأَغْرَقْنَاهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

fa-lammā āsafūnā intaqamnā minhum fa-aghraqnāhum ajmaʿīn

“When they provoked Us to anger, We punished them and drowned them all.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:55

This is the Qur’an’s only verbal occurrence of أ س ف.

آسَفُونَا is Form IV and means provoking to anger. Its plural subject refers to Pharaoh and his followers in the preceding context; the object pronoun نَا refers to The God.

The sentence’s direct construction therefore states that their conduct occasioned divine anger.

Why does آسَفُونَا here not mean causing grief?

Arabic آسَفَهُ can concern making someone angry or distressed. The continuation here, however, makes anger prominent.

آسَفُونَا is followed by انْتَقَمْنَا, then the drowning of Pharaoh and his companions.

This sequence presents a punitive response to wrongful conduct. “They provoked Us to anger” is therefore more suitable than “they made Us grieve.”

The preceding context supports this as well.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:51 Pharaoh speaks of his rule over Egypt. In Al-Zukhruf 43:52 he belittles Moses; Al-Zukhruf 43:54 describes his treating his people as lacking judgment and their obeying him.

Al-Zukhruf 43:55 then states that when they occasioned divine anger, punishment and drowning followed.

آسَفُونَا thus concerns conduct within a setting of defiance, opposition and disobedience.

Does The God become angry as humans do?

The Qur’an here attributes anger to The God. This attribution should neither disappear from translation nor be rewritten as though absent from the text.

Attributing an action to The God and to humans does not necessarily make its manner identical in both.

Human anger may involve bodily changes, difficulty controlling emotion, personal hurt or a hasty reaction. أ س ف itself does not establish these features for The God.

What Al-Zukhruf clearly states is the relationship between Pharaoh’s and his people’s wrongful conduct, divine anger and the following punishment.

The direct meaning of آسَفُونَا can therefore be retained while avoiding bodily imagery or attributing vulnerable human emotions to the divine being.

Nor should difficulties with the expression lead us to assign نَا without evidence to Moses, angels or righteous servants of The God. The verse’s speaker is The God, and the sentence continues an account of divine actions.

An explanation through concepts such as divine approval and disapproval must preserve the verse’s direct attribution, rather than changing the pronoun’s referent.

Anger and punishment

آسَفُونَا and انْتَقَمْنَا are different verbs.

The first concerns provoking to anger; the second describes inflicting punishment.

Study of N Q M

Rendering آسَفُونَا directly as “they made Us punish them” diminishes the distinction between the verbs.

The verse links causative conduct with the punishment that follows. Divine anger here is connected with judgment and punishment, while آسَفَ lexically concerns provoking to anger, rather than punishment itself.

This verse likewise does not establish that every harmful natural event or every instance of present human suffering signifies The God’s anger and direct punishment. It concerns a particular event in Pharaoh’s story, whose ethical relationship the Qur’an itself states.

Extending that judgment to every other event without independent evidence exceeds the verse’s scope.

7. Distinguishing أ س ف from related roots

أ س ف (أَسَف) and ح ز ن (حُزْن)

حُزْن concerns grief and appears in the Qur’an for different human states.

Study of Ḥ Z N

أَسَف also expresses grief in some uses, but early Arabic connects it with sorrow, longing and intense agitation as well.

Yūsuf 12:84 provides especially clear evidence for their relationship: Jacob says يَا أَسَفَىٰ, followed by reference to his الْحُزْنِ.

Their juxtaposition may show أَسَف expressing grief intensely and حُزْن naming the state of grieving in that context.

This should not become an absolute rule making every أَسَف stronger than every حُزْن. The words overlap in part of their semantic field.

أ س ف (أَسَف) and غ ض ب (غَضَب)

غَضَب concerns anger and responding to something objectionable.

Study of GH Ḍ B

أَسَف can also mean anger. Unlike غَضَب, however, grief and sorrow are clear uses of it too.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 and Ṭā Hā 20:86 place غَضْبَانَ and أَسِفًا side by side.

This allows Moses’ anger to be connected with his grief, but does not alone establish that the words name entirely independent states throughout Arabic.

Al-Zukhruf 43:55 uses آسَفُونَا for provoking to anger without غَضَب appearing in the same sentence.

Not every أَسَف is anger, nor must every instance of anger be expressed with أَسَف.

أ س ف (أَسَف) and ح س ر (حَسْرَة)

The Qur’an also uses حَسْرَة for sorrow, regret and an unwelcome outcome.

Study of Ḥ S R

Fāṭir 35:8 restrains the Prophet from wearing himself away in sorrow over those who have chosen the wrong path. This is close to Al-Kahf 18:6, which presents his intense أَسَف over his audience not believing.

أَسَف and حَسْرَة may therefore approach one another in the field of sorrow and suffering over an unfulfilled desire.

حَسْرَة nevertheless does not mean anger in every case, while أَسَف also has that application.

“Sorrowful regret” can therefore explain أَسَف in some verses, but is no permanent equivalent for all its derivatives.

أ س ف (أَسَف) and أ س و / أ س ي (أَسَىٰ)

Lexicons discuss the أَسَىٰ family under أ س ي, while some classifications, including Qur’anic indexes, also group it under أ س و. It resembles أ س ف in its initial consonants and semantic field, but is distinct from it.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:23 concerns grieving over what has been lost. تَأْسَوْا belongs to this family and is indexed in the Qur’anic root grouping under أ س و; this does not negate the lexical treatment of أَسَىٰ under أ س ي.

Study of A S W

This has a clear semantic relationship with Arabic أَسِفَ عَلَى مَا فَاتَهُ. Both can express grief over loss.

أ س ف also occurs in the Qur’an for provoking to anger; this meaning should not be transferred to the أَسَىٰ family without evidence.

Semantic and phonetic proximity does not establish a shared historical root.

أ س ف (أَسَف) and ك ظ م (كَظْم)

كَظْم concerns containing and restraining.

Yūsuf 12:84 contains both أَسَف and كَظِيم. The first concerns grief and sorrow; the second describes someone holding grief within.

Other Qur’anic uses can concern restraining anger as well.

كَظْم therefore does not name one particular feeling, but concerns how it is contained or restrained. Both grief and anger can be its object.

أَسَف therefore cannot simply mean “suppressed grief.” In Yūsuf, suppression is conveyed by a different word.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation of أ س ف must distinguish grief, distress and anger.

For يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ in Yūsuf 12:84, “Ah, what grief over Joseph!” is natural. “Ah, my grief for Joseph!” also preserves the emotional construction.

“Alas for my regret over Joseph” may convey intense sorrow, but can narrow the meaning if “regret” is understood as remorse over the speaker’s own wrongdoing.

“Out of intense grief” suits أَسَفًا in Al-Kahf 18:6, agreeing with the sentence and بَاخِعٌ نَفْسَكَ.

For غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 and Ṭā Hā 20:86, the principal proposal is:

“Angry and grieved.”

This preserves both descriptions and allows Moses’ grief to be understood alongside his anger. “Angry and deeply agitated” is also lexically possible, but makes their distinction less apparent.

For آسَفُونَا in Al-Zukhruf 43:55, the proposed rendering is:

“They provoked Us to anger.”

This preserves the Form IV verb’s meaning. “They occasioned Our displeasure” can help explain its divine use, but replacing the direct translation with it diminishes the intensity of آسَفَ.

“They made Us grieve” is unsuitable here, since the continuation and the setting of divine punishment support anger more strongly.

Does آسِف mean apologizing?

Modern Arabic can use آسِف when apologizing. Expressions of sorrow can likewise serve different conversational functions, including offering an apology.

This familiar modern use should not be transferred into every Qur’anic form.

يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ is no confession of wrongdoing by Jacob. أَسَفًا in Al-Kahf 18:6 does not express the Prophet’s remorse over a wrongful action. آسَفُونَا in Al-Zukhruf 43:55 likewise does not mean apologizing.

“Apologizing” and “asking pardon” are therefore not the root’s basic equivalents in these verses. Contemporary apologetic usage alone cannot establish the meaning of the Qur’anic forms; dating the emergence of that usage likewise requires independent historical evidence.

Translation should first express the particular Qur’anic form’s meaning. Later developments in usage need explanation only where relevant.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root أ س ف has two principal, related branches: grief and sorrow over loss or an unfulfilled desire, and anger and agitation at objectionable conduct or conditions.

In early use, أَسَف can express deep grief, sorrow, longing or anger. These meanings are connected, but cannot be reduced everywhere to one feeling.

The Qur’an uses the root in five verses.

In Yūsuf 12:84, أَسَف is Jacob’s deep grief over separation from Joseph.

In Al-Kahf 18:6, the same root expresses the Prophet’s grief over his audience not believing.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 and Ṭā Hā 20:86, Moses returns to his people غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا, a combination that can present intense anger alongside grief over their conduct.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:55, آسَفُونَا concerns provoking The God to anger and is connected with Pharaoh’s and his companions’ punishment.

One derivative’s meaning should therefore not be transferred to another without examination. أَسَفًا in Al-Kahf, أَسِفًا in Moses’ story and آسَفُونَا in Al-Zukhruf share a word family, but have different morphological roles and contextual meanings.

Divine anger should likewise neither be explained through human bodily features and vulnerability nor have its direct attribution to The God removed from the text. In Al-Zukhruf, anger becomes clear in relation to wrongful conduct and punitive judgment.

Forqan’s final definition of the root أ س ف:

“Intense grief, sorrow or agitation at an unwelcome matter: a state that may arise from loss or an unfulfilled desire, or appear as anger in response to objectionable conduct. In the Qur’an, أَسَف and أَسِف in human settings denote deep grief, distress or anger; آسَفَ in the divine occurrence concerns provoking to anger. The precise meaning of each form depends on the sentence’s structure, the object of the feeling and the verse’s context.”
