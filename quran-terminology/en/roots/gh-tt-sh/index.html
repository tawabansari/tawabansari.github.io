Darkness, dim sight, difficulty seeing, and making dark

The Arabic root gh-ṭ-sh is associated with darkness and reduced ability to see. Ghaṭash denotes darkness and, in another use, weak eyesight; aghṭash can describe a dark night or a person with dim sight; and ghaṭshāʾ describes a desert whose darkness or indistinct paths make it difficult to navigate.

The related verb taghāṭasha describes someone who pretends not to see or behaves as though unaware of something.

These uses share a feature: things are not clearly visible to an observer, or seeing and distinguishing them becomes difficult. Sometimes the cause lies in the surroundings, as with night-time darkness; sometimes in a person's eyesight; and sometimes the person deliberately pretends not to have seen.

These three situations are nevertheless distinct. They should not all be reduced to blindness, deliberate concealment of truth, or total darkness.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as aghṭasha in An-Naziʿat 79:29. Describing the ordering of night and daylight in creation, the verb means making the night dark.

1. The Root in Early Arabic

Arabic dictionaries explain ghaṭasha al-laylu as the night becoming dark.

Similarly, aghṭasha Allāhu al-layla means that God made the night dark.

This use concerns darkness itself: a condition in which light diminishes and objects become harder to see.

In daylight, for example, a person can distinguish a path, stones, trees, and uneven ground from a suitable distance. As night arrives and light diminishes, those same things become harder to distinguish.

Arabic can describe this condition using ghaṭsh.

Another use concerns human eyesight. Ghaṭishat ʿaynuhu describes an eye whose vision has become weak or dim.

Here, unlike night-time darkness, the surroundings may be bright while the person cannot see objects as clearly as others.

The two principal uses should therefore be distinguished:

First, environmental darkness and diminished light.

Second, weak eyesight and difficulty seeing on the person's part.

Their effects are similar, but their causes differ.

An Early Witness: The Dark Desert in Poetry Attributed to Al-Aʿsha

A verse attributed to Al-Aʿsha, a prominent poet of the late pre-Islamic period, reads:

وَيَهْمَاءَ بِاللَّيْلِ غَطْشَى الْفَلَاةِ

wa-yahmāʾa bi-l-layli ghaṭshā al-falāti

يُؤْنِسُنِي صَوْتُ فَيَّادِهَا

yuʾnisunī ṣawtu fayyādihā

Translation:

“And many a vast, trackless desert, dark by night, where the voice of its male owl kept me company.”

Yahmāʾ describes an extensive, difficult desert whose routes and landmarks are not readily discernible.

Ghaṭshā in this verse describes the desert's darkness.

The poet describes a desert where night makes paths and landmarks difficult to distinguish. In such surroundings, even a resident bird's call may reassure a lone traveller of another living presence.

Fayyād in early usage denotes the male owl; some lexical explanations also associate it with the calls of nocturnal birds.

This witness shows ghaṭshā describing environmental darkness in early poetry—a darkness that also makes desert navigation difficult.

Being lost or lacking a path is not, however, an indispensable part of the root's meaning. Here darkness accompanies a landscape without clear landmarks.

Lexical sources attribute the verse to Al-Aʿsha, but its wording varies: some transmit ʿaṭshā instead of ghaṭshā. The ghaṭshā reading illustrates darkness, while the variant must remain acknowledged.

Another Witness: A Dark Night in Poetry Attributed to Al-Aʿsha

Another verse attributed to Al-Aʿsha reads:

عَقَرْتُ لَهُمْ مُوهِنًا نَاقَتِي

ʿaqartu lahum mūhinan nāqatī

وَغَامِرُهُمْ مُدْلَهِمٌّ غَطِشْ

wa-ghāmiruhum mudlahimmun ghaṭish

Translation:

“Late at night I slaughtered my she-camel for them, while a deep, dark night enveloped them.”

Mudlahimm concerns thick, intense darkness, while ghaṭish also describes darkness.

Their combination shows that ghaṭash here concerns the darkness of night.

Their association does not establish that ghaṭash necessarily denotes absolute darkness or the deepest blackness in every use.

The other adjective also contributes to the intensity of the description.

These two witnesses, together with the Quranic occurrence, show darkness as a recognized meaning of this family in early Arabic. The precise composition date of each verse is not established here.

Determining whether the darkness sense developed from weak eyesight, or the eyesight sense from an image of darkness, requires independent historical evidence. Semantic proximity alone does not establish their order of development.

2. Core Meaning and Branches of Usage

A. Ghaṭash: Darkness and Difficulty Seeing

In this branch, ghaṭash concerns environmental darkness.

Darkness may develop gradually or intensify during part of the night. The word should not therefore be restricted to a particular hour without evidence.

Some daylight may remain on the horizon at nightfall. It diminishes over time, and the surroundings grow darker.

Darkness in this process can have different degrees.

In descriptions of night, ghaṭash conveys darkness or becoming dark; the root itself specifies neither the hour nor the degree involved.

Ghuṭāsh is also recorded for the darkness and obscurity of night.

This form describes darkness and should not be conflated with other roots for covering or sunset.

B. Aghṭash: Dark or Dim-Sighted

As an adjective, aghṭash can describe a dark night.

Laylun aghṭashu, for example, means a dark night.

The same form is also recorded for someone with weak eyesight.

What is being described determines the sense.

When aghṭash describes night, environmental darkness is involved.

When it describes a person, weak eyesight is involved.

The dim-sight sense should not be transferred literally to night, nor night-time darkness treated as an eye disorder.

The uses are related through their shared effect: difficulty seeing.

C. Ghaṭish and Ghaṭshāʾ: Weak Eyesight

Arabic sources record rajulun ghaṭishun and rajulun aghṭashu for a person with weak eyesight.

Imraʾatun ghaṭshāʾu is the corresponding feminine description.

Some explanations describe an aghṭash person as having lost part of their vision or being unable to keep their eyes properly open in strong sunlight.

Another explanation compares ghaṭash to ʿamash, a condition affecting eyesight.

These accounts place the eye-related use within the field of weak vision.

They do not establish that the word necessarily named a single specific medical condition.

Reduced vision can have different causes. Early language describes an observable condition without necessarily supplying a scientific classification of eye diseases.

Nor does aghṭash here necessarily mean total blindness.

D. Ghaṭshā: A Dark, Difficult-to-Navigate Desert

Falātun ghaṭshā describes a desert that is dark or difficult to navigate.

Darkness here interacts with the desert landscape.

Where landmarks are scarce, night-time darkness can make navigation very difficult.

Someone may distinguish a mountain, hill, or travelled route in daylight, only to lose sight of those same landmarks at night.

Ghaṭshā suits such a situation.

A dark desert and a trackless desert are not always identical, however. A desert may lack clear landmarks even in daylight.

The whole meaning of gh-ṭ-sh should therefore not be reduced to getting lost or lacking a path.

E. Taghāṭasha: Pretending Not to See

Lexical sources use taghāṭasha for someone who pretends not to see or feigns ignorance of a matter.

Someone might witness wrongdoing yet behave as though they had seen nothing to avoid responsibility.

The problem here is not necessarily physical weakness of the eyes.

The person may have seen the truth but deliberately pretend otherwise.

The image resembles weak sight, but intention and responsibility differ.

Someone with genuinely weak eyesight may be unable to see something. Someone pretending not to see may have that ability while behaving as though they lack it.

Poetry by Kuthayyir ʿAzza, an Umayyad poet, also uses the expression for disregarding a complaint or acting as though it were not heard or understood.

This witness postdates the Quran and cannot alone establish pre-Islamic antiquity for the figurative use.

3. Important Forms and Derivatives

Recognized forms in this Arabic family include:

  • Ghaṭasha / yaghṭishu: became dark, of night.
  • Ghaṭisha: one's sight became weak or dim.
  • Ghaṭash: darkness; in another branch, weak eyesight.
  • Ghuṭāsh: the darkness and obscurity of night.
  • Ghāṭish: dark, especially of night.
  • Aghṭash: dark; of a person, dim-sighted.
  • Ghaṭish: weak-sighted; in some uses, dark.
  • Ghaṭshāʾ: dark or dim-sighted, the corresponding feminine adjective.
  • Ghaṭshā: describing a dark or difficult-to-navigate desert.
  • Aghṭasha / yughṭishu: made dark; in some constructions, became dark.
  • Ighṭāsh: making dark.
  • Taghaṭṭasha: became dim or weak, of eyesight.
  • Taghāṭasha: pretended not to see or feigned ignorance.
  • Taghāṭush: feigned ignorance or pretended failure to see.
  • Miyāhu Ghuṭaysh: a recorded expression for a mirage, explained through obscured vision in intense heat.

Only aghṭasha occurs in the Quran.

Other forms such as ghaṭash, ghuṭāsh, ghāṭish, ghaṭshāʾ, and taghāṭasha are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Quranic text.

The Morphology of Aghṭasha

Aghṭasha is a Form IV perfect verb.

In An-Naziʿat 79:29 it is third-person masculine singular, referring to God as its subject.

Form IV has a causative sense here: making something dark.

Arabic distinguishes ghaṭasha al-laylu from aghṭasha Allāhu al-layla.

In the first sentence, the night becomes dark.

In the second, God makes the night dark.

The Quranic construction thus has a definite agent and object. Translating it simply as “the night became dark” would remove the attribution to God.

Form IV alone does not specify the duration, intensity, or physical mechanism of darkening.

4. “Wa-aghṭasha Laylahā” in the Quran

An-Naziʿat 79:29

وَأَغْطَشَ لَيْلَهَا وَأَخْرَجَ ضُحَاهَا

wa-aghṭasha laylahā wa-akhraja ḍuḥāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29

“And He darkened its night and brought forth its daylight.”

The verse belongs to a passage about the creation and ordering of heaven and earth.

An-Naziʿat 79:27 asks whether humankind or heaven is harder to create, then speaks of heaven being built.

An-Naziʿat 79:28 describes its height being raised and its formation ordered.

An-Naziʿat 79:29 then speaks of the night being darkened and daylight brought forth.

In An-Naziʿat 79:30, the earth becomes the sentence's separate topic, and its spreading is described.

This sequence places aghṭasha within a description of the order of creation.

Laylahā: The Night of What?

In laylahā, the pronoun -hā refers to the nearest suitable antecedent in the preceding verses: al-samāʾ, heaven.

The construction therefore speaks of heaven's night.

This association is intelligible in ordinary language: the sky is connected with daylight, night-time darkness, the sun, and other visible phenomena.

“Heaven's night” does not present heaven as a living being with eyes or eyesight.

Nor can this possessive construction alone determine the physical workings of all celestial phenomena.

The pronoun relates night to heaven; aghṭasha states the act of making that night dark.

Aghṭasha: He Darkened

The verb's direct meaning here is making dark.

Arabic also uses aẓlama for this meaning.

The gh-ṭ-sh family, however, includes reduced ability to see as well as environmental darkness.

Its application to night thus fits the human experience of darkness: diminished light makes things harder to see and distinguish.

This correspondence does not mean that night literally suffers from an eye disorder or that heaven has lost its eyesight.

The appropriate contextual meaning remains darkening the night.

“Wa-akhraja Ḍuḥāhā”: Bringing Forth Daylight

The second half uses another verb: akhraja.

It belongs to kh-r-j, concerning bringing something out or making it manifest.

Ḍuḥāhā belongs to the ḍ-ḥ-w family and here concerns daylight.

The verse thus places two actions together:

Darkening the night.

Bringing forth daylight.

The contrast gives a clear picture of two different conditions.

The meaning of akhraja should not be transferred wholesale to gh-ṭ-sh, nor should bringing forth light be extracted from ghaṭash itself.

Each verb makes its own contribution.

Does Aghṭasha Mean Absolute Darkness?

The word itself conveys making dark.

The verse does not state that night must be entirely devoid of starlight, moonlight, or every other light source.

Ordinary speech can likewise describe a dark night despite limited light from the moon or stars.

Aghṭasha should therefore not be restricted without evidence to absolute darkness, absence of all light, or complete blackness.

Conversely, its application to night here cannot be reduced to a dark corner of a room or a hidden object.

The verse specifically concerns night-time darkness within the order of creation.

Does the Verse Explain the Scientific Mechanism of Night?

The verse attributes the darkening of night and bringing forth of daylight to God.

The verb aghṭasha itself does not establish that the Quran is explaining details of Earth's motion, solar radiation, atmospheric structure, or the physical properties of space.

Present knowledge of Earth's rotation and its relation to day and night can help us understand the natural order of this phenomenon.

That knowledge does not replace the word's lexical meaning and should not be inserted into its direct translation.

Attributing the darkening of night to God in Quranic language does not itself deny natural mechanisms.

The verse addresses divine agency; the physical means of realization is a separate question.

5. Ghaṭash and Human Visual Experience

One feature of this root is that environmental darkness and weak eyesight belong to the same word family.

Ordinary human experience makes this relationship intelligible.

Consider two people looking at the same scene.

The first has healthy eyesight but stands in very dark surroundings and cannot distinguish the scene's details well.

The second stands in bright surroundings but cannot see those details clearly because of weak eyesight.

Both may experience difficulty seeing, but for different reasons.

In the first case, environmental light is limited.

In the second, the person's ability to see is reduced.

Arabic has used the gh-ṭ-sh family for both conditions.

This semantic overlap does not make night-time darkness and weak eyesight identical in nature.

Nor does it establish, without historical evidence, which condition Arabic speakers first named with this root and then extended to the other.

From Weak Sight to Pretending Not to See

Taghāṭush, pretending not to see, extends this semantic field further.

Someone may see an obvious wrong yet pretend not to notice so that they need not respond.

Here the issue is neither night-time darkness nor physical weakness of the eyes.

The behaviour outwardly resembles failure to see, although the person may have fully witnessed the truth.

This use shows how a physical image can describe deliberate human behaviour.

Such an extension must not change the meaning of Quranic aghṭasha. An-Naziʿat 79:29 concerns darkening the night, not human evasion or deliberate concealment of truth.

6. Distinctions from Related Roots

Gh-ṭ-sh Compared with Gh-s-q: Darkness and Its Arrival

Quranic gh-s-q concerns night becoming dark and darkness arriving or intensifying.

In Al-Israʾ 17:78, ghasaqi al-layli marks a temporal condition of the night.

Aghṭasha in An-Naziʿat 79:29, by contrast, concerns making the night dark.

The roots overlap in the field of darkness but play different roles in these verses.

Ghasaq in Al-Israʾ helps identify a time of night-time darkness.

Aghṭasha in An-Naziʿat expresses the act of darkening the night.

The sense of ghasaq should not therefore assign aghṭasha a particular hour of the night.

Arabic gh-s-q also has other branches, including the flowing of certain liquids, which must not be transferred to gh-ṭ-sh.

Gh-ṭ-sh Compared with Gh-b-sh: Darkness and Faint Light

Ghabash concerns darkness mingled with faint light, especially around dawn or nightfall.

Some lexical sources associate ghaṭash and ghabash within the field of darkness.

This proximity does not make them identical in every use.

Ghabash can describe a mixture of light and darkness.

The clear Quranic use of gh-ṭ-sh is making the night dark.

Nevertheless, this does not justify an absolute division whereby ghaṭash always means complete darkness and ghabash always half-darkness. Each word must be evaluated through its own evidence.

Gh-ṭ-sh Compared with Ẓ-l-m: Darkness and Darkening

Arabic ẓulma concerns darkness, and aẓlama al-laylu can describe the night becoming dark.

In that respect, aghṭasha in An-Naziʿat 79:29 is close to aẓlama.

The Quranic ẓ-l-m family also has an extensive field concerning injustice and violation of rights.

That ethical use must not lead us to translate aghṭasha as committing injustice or oppression.

Nor does proximity between darkness and injustice make every Quranic darkness a symbol of evil or corruption.

Night can be part of the order of creation and a setting for human rest.

Gh-ṭ-sh Compared with Gh-sh-w: Darkening and Covering

Quranic gh-sh-w concerns covering and enveloping.

In passages such as Ar-Raʿd 13:3, night envelops and covers day.

The Quranic occurrence of gh-ṭ-sh describes darkening the night.

These concepts may approach one another in descriptions of a natural phenomenon, but they remain distinct.

Gh-sh-w in the relevant use emphasizes covering and encompassing.

Gh-ṭ-sh conveys darkness.

Translating aghṭasha merely as “covered” would therefore obscure its clearer meaning of making dark.

Gh-ṭ-sh Compared with ʿ-m-y: Dim Sight and Blindness

ʿAmiya concerns blindness and loss of sight. The Quran uses this family for physical blindness and, in other contexts, inability to recognize truth.

Eye-related ghaṭash is chiefly associated with weak or dim vision.

Aghṭash describing a person does not therefore necessarily mean complete blindness.

Nor does figurative taghāṭush, feigning ignorance, imply that every person with low vision or blindness has an ethical failing in recognizing truth.

Physical eyesight and ethical responsibility are different matters.

Gh-ṭ-sh Compared with Gh-ṭ-w: Darkness and Covering

Arabic gh-ṭ-w concerns covering and being under a covering.

This field can approach darkness in some images, since covering a light source may darken the surroundings.

An action's result is nevertheless not identical to the meaning of the action itself.

Quranic gh-ṭ-sh expresses darkening the night.

Gh-ṭ-w in the relevant use emphasizes covering itself.

The roots should not be identified merely because they sound similar and covering may produce darkness.

7. Challenges of Understanding and Translation

Does Aghṭasha Mean Causing Blindness?

In An-Naziʿat 79:29, the verb means making the night dark.

Although adjectival aghṭash can describe weak human eyesight, the verb's object here is laylahā, its night, rather than an eye or a person.

“He blinded the night” is therefore not the verse's direct, natural meaning.

Does Ghaṭash Always Mean Intense, Absolute Darkness?

No. The family concerns darkness and dim sight but does not fix a single degree of darkness across all uses.

Some witnesses add words such as mudlahimm to intensify the description.

“Darkened” is therefore clear and sufficient for the Quranic occurrence.

Is Aghṭasha the Same as Aghshā?

No. The verbs belong to different roots.

Aghṭasha in this verse concerns making dark.

Aghshā belongs to gh-sh-w and concerns covering or enveloping.

Night's covering may accompany darkness, but this does not identify the two verbs.

Does Night-Time Darkness Represent Evil Here?

No. An-Naziʿat 79:29 concerns the creation and ordering of night and daylight.

Night-time darkness here belongs to the order of creation.

Yunus 10:67 also presents night as a time for human rest.

Darkness should therefore not be treated as evil, misguidance, or punishment in every Quranic use.

Each image's significance and function emerge from context.

Can Scientific Details of Night's Creation Be Extracted from This Verse?

Aghṭasha conveys making dark.

The precise origin of night in cosmic history, celestial mechanics, atmospheric properties, and the sources of all light cannot be derived from this verb alone.

Such questions require independent scientific evidence and examination of the relevant verses.

8. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For Quranic aghṭasha in An-Naziʿat 79:29, “He darkened” is precise, brief, and natural.

Translation of the phrase:

وَأَغْطَشَ لَيْلَهَا

wa-aghṭasha laylahā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29

“And He darkened its night.”

Translation of the full verse:

“And He darkened its night and brought forth its daylight.”

This preserves the verb's direct meaning and its distinction from akhraja.

For non-Quranic uses:

Ghaṭasha al-laylu: the night became dark.

Ghaṭash: darkness; in the eye-related use, weak eyesight.

Ghuṭāsh: night-time darkness.

Ghāṭish: dark.

Aghṭash: dark; of a person, dim-sighted or having weak eyesight.

Ghaṭish: weak-sighted; in some uses, dark.

Ghaṭshāʾ: dark or dim-sighted, according to what is described.

Falātun ghaṭshā: a dark, difficult-to-navigate desert.

Aghṭasha al-layla: he made the night dark.

Ighṭāsh: making dark.

Taghaṭṭashat ʿaynuhu: his eyesight became dim or weak.

Taghāṭasha: pretended not to see or feigned ignorance.

Miyāhu Ghuṭaysh: a recorded name for a mirage, explained through obscured vision in intense heat.

Translation should distinguish environmental darkness, weak eyesight, and deliberate evasion. They belong to one family but are not interchangeable in every sentence.

Conclusion

Arabic gh-ṭ-sh concerns darkness and reduced ability to see.

In descriptions of night, ghaṭash denotes darkness and aghṭasha denotes making dark.

In eye-related uses, ghaṭash is recorded for weak or dim vision.

Of a desert, ghaṭshā can express darkness and difficulty distinguishing the way.

Arabic also uses taghāṭasha for pretending not to see or feigning ignorance.

Poetry attributed to Al-Aʿsha illustrates the family's application to night-time and desert darkness. Variant readings and the lack of independent evidence dating every branch prevent certainty about the historical origin of all its meanings.

Only the form aghṭasha occurs in the Quran.

In An-Naziʿat 79:29, its agent is God and its object is laylahā. Daylight is expressed through another verb in the same verse.

The verse thus describes two conditions within the order of creation: night-time darkness and daylight becoming manifest.

Here aghṭasha neither means causing blindness or deliberately concealing truth, nor specifies a scientific mechanism for the origin of night.

The core field of gh-ṭ-sh is darkness and dim sight, whether darkness in the surroundings or weakness in a person's vision. Figuratively, the family can extend to pretending not to see.

Its specific Quranic meaning remains clear and limited: making the night dark.
