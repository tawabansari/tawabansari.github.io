The Qur’anic Root D W R

Turning, surrounding, circles, and a place of residence

The root د و ر (D W R) has a broad family in Arabic whose branches may initially seem far apart: turning and going around, causing something to turn, a circle and the course of events, and dār as a house, residence, or abode. Ibn Fāris identifies the root’s core as encompassing something from around it, while lexical sources record dāra yadūru for turning, going around, and returning along a course.

This core, however, should not become an image imposed on every derivative. In Arabic before the Qur’an, dār was already an established word for a place of residence, while dāʾirah could express not only a round shape but also a turn of events and a change in circumstances that comes upon someone. The shared root therefore shows the kinship of these branches, but each derivative must be translated according to its own actual usage in Arabic and the Qur’an.

Qur’anic forms

The root د و ر (D W R) occurs 55 times in the Qur’an in six main forms. The nominal family دَار (dār) and its forms, including دِيَار (diyār), occurs 48 times in total. Alongside it, دَائِرَة (dāʾirah) occurs three times, دَوَائِر (dawāʾir) once, the verb تَدُورُ (tadūru) once, the verb تُدِيرُونَهَا (tudīrūnahā) once, and دَيَّارًا (dayyāran) once.

دَار / دِيَار (dār / diyār) — nominal family, 48 occurrences

دَائِرَة (dāʾirah) — three occurrences

دَوَائِر (dawāʾir) — one occurrence

تَدُورُ (tadūru) — one occurrence

تُدِيرُونَهَا (tudīrūnahā) — one occurrence

دَيَّارًا (dayyāran) — one occurrence

This distribution shows that the Qur’an retains the direct meaning of turning while using the established branch of dār and diyār far more frequently than the basic verb.

1. Turning and surrounding in early Arabic

Lexical sources use دَارَ الشيءُ يَدُورُ (dāra al-shayʾu yadūru) for something turning, going around, or moving around something. Dāʾirah can likewise, in its basic sense, be something that encompasses another thing or has a round shape. The same family has been used for a round of movement, the passage of time, movement around something, and a reversal of circumstances.

Poetry attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād provides important evidence because it shows two branches of this root in Arabic poetic language before the Qur’an. At the opening of his Muʿallaqah, he speaks of dār ʿAblah:

يَا دَارَ عَبْلَةَ بِالْجَوَاءِ تَكَلَّمِي

Yā dāra ʿAblata bi-l-Jawāʾi takallamī.

O dwelling of ʿAblah at Al-Jawāʾ, speak!

Here dār clearly means ʿAblah’s dwelling and place of residence, not a geometric shape.

Near the end of the same ode, we also find:

وَلَقَدْ خَشِيتُ بِأَنْ أَمُوتَ وَلَمْ تَدُرْ
لِلْحَرْبِ دَائِرَةٌ عَلَى ابْنَي ضَمْضَمِ

Wa-laqad khashītu bi-an amūta wa-lam tadur / Li-l-ḥarbi dāʾiratun ʿalā bnay Ḍamḍami.

I feared that I might die before war’s turn had come upon the two sons of Ḍamḍam.

Here the verb tadur and the noun dāʾirah occur in a single construction. The point is not that a geometric shape should come upon someone, but that war should turn so that its events and outcome come back upon the poet’s two enemies. Early commentaries on the Muʿallaqah likewise understand dāʾirah in this verse as an event whose course turns and, in this context, comes upon someone unfavorably.

Before the Qur’an, then, at least two important branches of this family are clear: dār as a place of residence, and dāʾirah as a turn of events or circumstances.

Evidence from the Semitic languages

Comparative evidence is also relevant, but requires caution. In Akkadian, the close form dūru is recorded for a wall or city wall. In Aramaic, the root D W R is also used for remaining, dwelling, and residing, and nouns of the same family are recorded for a place of residence.

These witnesses show that close forms associated with encompassing, walls, and dwelling also existed in other Semitic languages. They do not, however, establish that Arabic dār necessarily developed directly from a “round wall,” or that the circular shape of tents was the historical origin of the word. There is no independent evidence for such a derivational path.

Arabic sources themselves also offer explanations of the connection between dār and dawarān, turning. Ibn Jinnī, for example, connects it with people’s frequent movement within the dār. This explanation can make the semantic relationship intelligible, but should not be presented as a certain history of the word’s emergence.

2. تَدُورُ (tadūru) and تُدِيرُونَهَا (tudīrūnahā): turning and setting in circulation

The clearest use of the basic verb occurs in Al-Ahzab 33:19:

يَنظُرُونَ إِلَيْكَ تَدُورُ أَعْيُنُهُمْ كَالَّذِي يُغْشَىٰ عَلَيْهِ مِنَ الْمَوْتِ

Yanẓurūna ilayka tadūru aʿyunuhum ka-lladhī yughshā ʿalayhi mina l-mawt.

“They look at you, their eyes turning like someone overcome by faintness from death.”

Here تَدُورُ (tadūru) directly describes the movement and turning of the eyes. Fear, agitation, and the state of near-unconsciousness are understood from the whole sentence, not from the root D W R itself. The Qur’an uses this verb only once.

The Form IV verb likewise occurs only once, in Al-Baqarah 2:282:

إِلَّا أَنْ تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً حَاضِرَةً تُدِيرُونَهَا بَيْنَكُمْ

Illā an takūna tijāratan ḥāḍiratan tudīrūnahā baynakum.

“Unless it is a present transaction that you circulate among yourselves.”

The construction أدار الشيء (adāra al-shayʾ) is this family’s causative form: to turn something or set it in circulation. In this verse, تجارة حاضرة (tijārah ḥāḍirah), a present transaction, contrasts with a debt that remains due until an appointed time and is written down. The subject is therefore a transaction that passes between the parties and is completed at that time.

This occurrence matters for semantic history because modern Arabic idārah often brings to mind the management of an organization or institution. That institutional meaning should not be read back into this verse. Here تُدِيرُونَهَا (tudīrūnahā) does not mean “managing commerce” in the modern sense; it concerns circulating a transaction between the parties and carrying out a present exchange.

A basic translation could be “you circulate among yourselves,” if retaining the root’s image is natural in context. A smoother translation, “you carry out among yourselves,” also conveys the practical result of the construction. The distinction should remain clear in translation and explanation: the former preserves more of the word’s image, while the latter is more idiomatic English.

3. دَائِرَة (dāʾirah) and دَوَائِر (dawāʾir): a turn of events that comes upon someone

Dāʾirah is a derivative whose relationship to turning remains clearly visible. Arabic dictionaries record both a round shape or object and an event that turns upon someone. In expressions such as دارت عليه الدوائر (dārat ʿalayhi al-dawāʾir), the meaning is that events and troubles have befallen someone. Lexical sources also connect دائرة السوء (dāʾirat al-sawʾ) with defeat, misfortune, and an unfavorable situation that comes back upon someone.

The Qur’an says in Al-Maidah 5:52:

فَيَقُولُونَ نَخْشَىٰ أَنْ تُصِيبَنَا دَائِرَةٌ

Fa-yaqūlūna nakhshā an tuṣībanā dāʾirah.

“They say: we fear that a turn of events may strike us.”

In this sentence, دائرة (dāʾirah) occurs with the verb تصيبنا (tuṣībanā), strike us, and the context of fear makes its unfavorable direction clear.

At-Tawbah 9:98 also describes someone who:

وَيَتَرَبَّصُ بِكُمُ الدَّوَائِرَ

Wa-yatarabbaṣu bikumu d-dawāʾir.

“Awaits adverse turns of events for you.”

The same verse then says:

عَلَيْهِمْ دَائِرَةُ السَّوْءِ

ʿAlayhim dāʾiratu s-sawʾ.

“May the adverse turn be upon them.”

The same expression, دَائِرَةُ السَّوْءِ (dāʾirat al-sawʾ), also occurs in Al-Fath 48:6. In total, the Qur’an uses dāʾirah three times and its plural dawāʾir once.

The presence of السوء (al-sawʾ) in two occurrences is significant. The root D W R does not itself mean evil, defeat, or calamity. What comes from the root is turning and a reversal of circumstances; the negative character of some verses is apparent from the context and, in two instances, also from the qualification al-sawʾ.

For this reason, consistently translating the word as “calamity” removes part of its image. At the same time, the English word “circle” does not convey this Arabic usage. Depending on the context, “an adverse turn of events,” “an unfavorable change in circumstances,” or “a misfortune” may be appropriate. The explanation should make clear that behind this usage lies the image of circumstances turning and coming back upon someone.

4. دَار (dār) and دِيَار (diyār): house, residence, and abode

The largest Qur’anic branch of the root D W R is the dār family.

In Arabic usage, dār is not merely a room or residential building. Sources describe it as a place encompassing both the building and its grounds, and also record it for a home, neighborhood, a people’s place of residence, a town or land, and even a tribe. Lisān al-ʿArab explicitly states that any place where a group settles and resides can be called their dār.

The same breadth appears in the Qur’an.

Diyār and buyūt are not identical

Al-Hashr 59:2 is among the clearest witnesses:

هُوَ الَّذِي أَخْرَجَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ

Huwa lladhī akhraja lladhīna kafarū min ahli l-kitābi min diyārihim.

“He is the One who expelled from their dwelling places those among the People of the Book who committed kufr…”

And in the same verse:

يُخْرِبُونَ بُيُوتَهُمْ بِأَيْدِيهِمْ

Yukhribūna buyūtahum bi-aydīhim.

“They were destroying their houses with their own hands.”

The Qur’an uses دِيَار (diyār) and بُيُوت (buyūt) separately within one verse. Buyūt are their particular houses; diyār is the area in which those houses and their collective life are situated. Diyār therefore cannot always mean simply “houses.”

Al-Ahzab 33:27 shows the same point from another angle:

وَأَوْرَثَكُمْ أَرْضَهُمْ وَدِيَارَهُمْ وَأَمْوَالَهُمْ

Wa-awrathakum arḍahum wa-diyārahum wa-amwālahum.

“He caused you to inherit their land, their dwelling places, and their property.”

Here arḍ, land, and diyār also appear side by side. Diyār therefore does not mean all their land and territory; it points more particularly to the inhabited area, the place where they settled and lived.

Thus “houses” is suitable in some verses, but in contexts of expulsion, migration, or collective residence, “dwelling places,” “places of residence,” or “homelands” may better preserve the word’s range. “Homeland” sometimes comes close through the context, but its modern political concept should not be inserted into the lexical definition of diyār.

Dār can be a community’s place of residence

Several Qur’anic accounts of earlier peoples contain expressions such as:

فِي دَارِهِمْ جَاثِمِينَ

Fī dārihim jāthimīn.

Lying motionless in their dwelling place.

This is how the expression is used in those accounts.

Dārihim, their dār, is singular even though an entire people is being discussed. This itself shows that dār need not be an individual’s private house: it can express a community’s place of life and settlement.

This usage accords with the established Arabic semantic range in which dār can mean a neighborhood, a people’s place of residence, and in some uses even the tribe itself.

From a place of residence to the abode of the Hereafter

The Qur’an also uses dār in broader constructions:

الدَّارُ الْآخِرَةُ (al-dār al-ākhirah) — the final abode
دَارُ السَّلَامِ (dār al-salām) — the abode of peace
دَارُ الْمُقَامَةِ (dār al-muqāmah) — the abode of lasting residence
دَارُ الْقَرَارِ (dār al-qarār) — the abode of settlement
دَارُ الْخُلْدِ (dār al-khuld) — the abode of enduring residence
دَارَ الْبَوَارِ (dār al-bawār) — the abode of ruin
سُوءُ الدَّارِ (sūʾ al-dār) — the evil of the abode
عُقْبَى الدَّارِ (ʿuqbā al-dār) — the final outcome of the abode

In these constructions, “house” is usually too narrow. Dār is a place or abode in which residence and life are envisaged. Lexical sources likewise place uses such as dār al-qarār and dār al-salām within this field of a place or position of residence.

Yet the root relationship between dār and turning or surrounding should not make the translation artificial. Dār al-ākhirah cannot be translated as “the circle of the Hereafter” or “the turning ground of the Hereafter.” By the Qur’anic period, dār was itself an established word for an abode or place of residence; its actual usage should be the basis of translation.

5. دَيَّارًا (dayyāran) in Nuh 71:26

Surah Nuh states:

وَقَالَ نُوحٌ رَبِّ لَا تَذَرْ عَلَى الْأَرْضِ مِنَ الْكَافِرِينَ دَيَّارًا

Wa-qāla Nūḥun rabbi lā tadhar ʿalā l-arḍi mina l-kāfirīna dayyārā.

“Noah said: my Lord, leave no dweller from among those who commit kufr upon the earth.”

دَيَّار (dayyār) must not be confused with دِيَار (diyār). Diyār is the plural of dār, whereas dayyār in this verse is a singular noun.

Lexical sources record the old expression:

مَا بِالدَّارِ دَيَّارٌ (mā bi-l-dāri dayyār)

With the meaning “there is no one in the dwelling.” They also derive dayyār from dāra yadūru and analyze its historical structure on the pattern fiyʿāl, reconstructing its original form as daywār.

This morphological explanation matters because it shows that dayyār is neither the plural of dār nor another form of diyār. Yet no conclusion should be drawn from it beyond what the evidence supports.

In its established use, the word denotes someone present and residing in a place: a person, a dweller, and particularly conveys “no one” in a negative construction. There is no need to give it the meaning “a being constantly in motion.” Derivation from a root of movement and turning does not mean that movement remains a necessary component of its meaning in the verse.

For translating Nuh 71:26, “not a single dweller” better displays the connection with residence, while “no one” is more natural English. Both are more precise than translations such as “not a single house” or “not a single homeland,” since dayyāran here is a person, not a place.

6. Differences from nearby words and guidance for translation

D W R (دَوَر, dawar) compared with B Y T (بَيْت, bayt): dār and bayt

Dār and bayt both concern dwelling, but they are not synonymous.

Al-Hashr 59:2 directly demonstrates this difference: the very people expelled from their diyār destroy their buyūt. In that context, bayt is the particular building or house, while diyār expresses their place and area of residence.

The general tendency can be described as follows: bayt more often means a house or a particular residential space, while dār has a broader range that can encompass a house, building and grounds, a group’s place of residence, a neighborhood, or an abode.

This boundary is not absolute. In some contexts, dār too is simply a home, and bayt can also have uses broader than an ordinary room or building. The distinction should be understood from each verse’s usage, not from constructing a rigid rule between the two roots.

D W R (دَوَر, dawar) compared with D W L (دَوَل, dawal): turning and transfer

D W R and D W L approach one another in some usages because both can express a change in circumstances. Their recorded cores are nevertheless not identical.

For D W L, Ibn Fāris identifies one basic meaning as the transfer of something from one place to another, and explains tadāwul through something passing from one person to another.

The Qur’an also says:

وَتِلْكَ الْأَيَّامُ نُدَاوِلُهَا بَيْنَ النَّاسِ

Wa-tilka l-ayyāmu nudāwiluhā bayna n-nās.

Those days We alternate among people.

And:

كَيْ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً بَيْنَ الْأَغْنِيَاءِ مِنْكُمْ

Kay lā yakūna dūlatan bayna l-aghniyāʾi minkum.

So that it does not become something circulating among the wealthy among you.

In D W L, transfer and something passing in turn between parties stand out. In D W R, turning, a reversal of circumstances, or movement around something is more prominent.

It is better, however, not to turn this distinction into the geometric image of a “closed loop” versus an “open line.” D W R does not always imply a necessary return to the starting point, nor does D W L always mean movement in one direction. The valid distinction is the linguistic tendency itself: turning in one, and transfer and passing from hand to hand in the other.

Translation guidance

A single equivalent cannot be imposed on every derivative of this family.

تَدُورُ (tadūru) in its direct use means “turns” or “rotates.” تُدِيرُونَهَا (tudīrūnahā) in Al-Baqarah 2:282 expresses setting something in circulation between parties and carrying it out; it should not be confused with the modern concept of “managing.”

Dāʾirah in the verses under discussion is not a geometric circle, but a turn of events and circumstances coming back upon someone. Since its Qur’anic use occurs in negative contexts, “an adverse turn of events” or, more idiomatically, “a misfortune” may suit the verse.

Depending on context, dār ranges from “house” and “place of residence” to “abode” and “place.” In many instances, diyār is better understood as places of residence and collective life, rather than invariably reduced to “houses.”

دَيَّار (dayyār) in Nuh 71:26 denotes a person, not a place; “dweller,” or more naturally within the negative construction, “no one,” is therefore appropriate.

Conclusion

The lexical basis of the root د و ر (D W R) is connected with turning, circulation, and encompassing from around. In the Qur’an’s clearest use of the basic verb, the eyes tadūru, turn; in Al-Baqarah, a present transaction tudāru among people: it is set in circulation and carried out between them.

The dāʾirah branch extends this background of turning to a change in circumstances and an event coming back upon someone. This usage is also seen before the Qur’an in poetry attributed to ʿAntarah. In the Qur’an, dāʾirah and dawāʾir occur in contexts where the turn of events is unfavorable, but the negative character of this use should not be attributed to the core of the entire root.

In early Arabic, the dār branch had become independently established for a place of residence. Dār can denote a building and its grounds, a home, a group’s place of residence, a neighborhood, or an abode, and the Qur’an preserves this breadth. The distinction between diyār and buyūt in Al-Hashr 59:2 shows that diyār need not be a collection of residential buildings: it can express a group’s area of habitation.

Akkadian and Aramaic evidence shows the existence of close forms related to walls, encompassing, and dwelling, but does not determine the precise historical path between these meanings. We should therefore not construct a certain story about a circular enclosure, architecture, or the shape of tents to explain dār.

Dayyār is another distinct branch within this family. In Nuh 71:26 it refers to a person or dweller and is not identical to the plural diyār. Its morphological analysis shows its connection with dāra yadūru, but that connection does not authorize inserting the concept of constant motion into the translation of the verse.

A cautious synthesis of the root is:

D W R: a word family centered on turning, circulation, and encompassing. From it developed verbs of turning and causing to turn, dāʾirah for a turn and reversal of circumstances, and the established dār branch for a place of residence or abode. The Qur’an uses each branch according to its recognized usage. The root’s meaning should therefore illuminate their relationship, rather than reduce every derivative to one geometric image or one fixed translation.
