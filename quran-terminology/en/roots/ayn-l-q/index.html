Core sense: clinging, hanging, and remaining attached to something else. Extended uses include emotional attachment; nominal senses include thick or clotted blood and the leech that attaches to a body. Attachment need not be permanent.

1. The root in early Arabic

The three-consonant root «ع ل ق» concerns clinging, hanging, joining, and remaining attached. Attachment can be physical, as with something hanging from a rope or adhering to a surface, or nonphysical, as with affection for another person or a connection between matters.

Early Arabic «عَلِقَ بِالشَّيْءِ» means to cling to something and remain attached. «عَلَّقَ الشَّيْءَ» means to hang something or attach it elsewhere.

A vessel hung from a branch is held by its cord. Clothing caught on a thorn while passing through bushes offers another example.

These are different: one object hangs from a support; the other catches on a thorn.

The root is not limited to hanging down from above. Clinging and remaining attached are also central uses.

«عَلَاقَة» and «عِلَاقَة» occur for a strap, means of attachment, or relationship, with distinctions of vocalization and usage. «مِعْلَاق» and «مِعْلَق» name implements or places from which something hangs.

In well equipment, «عَلَق» also names supporting or suspending components of the pulley and rope apparatus, whose connected parts enable the water vessel to be raised and lowered.

Nonphysical uses belong to the same semantic field.

«عَلِقَ قَلْبُهُ بِفُلَانَةٍ» means his heart became attached to that woman. No literal rope binds the heart; clinging expresses emotional attachment.

«عِلْق» also denotes a precious object that someone values or cherishes.

Not every relationship is automatically ʿalaq. Attachment, adherence, or dependence is prominent, but neither permanence nor difficulty of separation is required in every use.

Another branch uses «عَلَق» and «عَلَقَة» for thick or clotted blood. «عَلَقَة» also names the leech, which attaches to a human or animal body and feeds on blood.

A connection with clinging is intelligible, but a single original image and date of origin for every noun should not be asserted without historical evidence.

A basic definition is therefore:

Clinging and remaining attached, extending to suspension, connection, and affection, and to names associated with attachment or adherence.

2. Evidence from early Arabic poetry

A well-known example occurs in poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period:

عُلِّقْتُهَا عَرَضًا وَعُلِّقَتْ رَجُلًا
غَيْرِي وَعُلِّقَ أُخْرَى غَيْرَهَا الرَّجُلُ

ʿulliqtuhā ʿaraḍan wa-ʿulliqat rajulan / ghayrī wa-ʿulliqa ukhrā ghayrahā al-rajulu

“By chance I became attached to her; she became attached to a man other than me, and that man to another woman.”

The poet describes a chain of affections.

He loves a woman who loves another man, who in turn loves someone else.

The family thus expresses affection and emotional attachment in early poetry.

No bodily adhesion is meant; the connection occupies the person's mind and heart.

It need not be mutual.

The woman does not return the poet's affection and is attached to someone else.

The root alone therefore means neither reciprocal love nor a successful, lasting relationship; it expresses attachment.

This line appears in al-Aʿshā's famous poem and in early lexical discussions. Its precise date of composition has not been independently established here.

ʿAlūq: a she-camel's behavior

Another example is attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Afnūn al-Taghlibī:

أَمْ كَيْفَ يَنْفَعُ مَا تُعْطِي الْعَلُوقُ بِهِ
رِئْمَانَ أَنْفٍ إِذَا مَا ضُنَّ بِاللَّبَنِ

am kayfa yanfaʿu mā tuʿṭī al-ʿalūqu bihi / riʾmāna anfin idhā mā ḍunna bi-al-labani

“What use is a she-camel's affectionate nuzzling if she withholds her milk?”

«الْعَلُوق» describes a she-camel here.

In the explanation relevant to this line, she nuzzles a calf as though accepting it but gives no milk. Lexicons record other descriptions under the same word; this is not its only use.

The poet criticizes attention that appears affectionate but supplies none of the expected practical benefit.

The witness connects the root with livestock behavior and everyday life.

Here ʿalūq names a specific animal condition, not merely hanging or emotional attachment.

Deception or false affection is not the root's fixed meaning; that inference belongs to the particular behavior and poetic context.

Early poetry collections attribute the line to Afnūn. Its exact date and all textual variants have not been independently established in this study.

Implements and suspended objects

Early Arabic also uses «عَلَق» for components of well equipment, including supports to which a pulley or other parts attach.

This provides a clear physical image.

An object stays in place through attachment to, or suspension from, something else.

The family also describes clothing caught on a thorn and game caught in a trap.

Attachment need not be voluntary. Someone may deliberately hang an object, while clothing can catch unintentionally.

Volition and positive or negative value are not fixed parts of the root.

3. Lexical and Quranic forms

Forms include «عَلِقَ», «يَعْلَقُ», «عَلَق», «عَلَقَة», «عَلَّقَ», «تَعْلِيق», «تَعَلَّقَ», «تَعَلُّق», «اعْتَلَقَ», «عِلْق», «عَلَاقَة», «عِلَاقَة», «مِعْلَاق», «مُعَلَّق», and «مُعَلَّقَة».

Shared root consonants do not give them identical roles or meanings.

«عَلِقَ»: clung, caught, or became attached.

«عَلَّقَ»: hung something or attached it elsewhere.

«تَعْلِيق»: hanging or attaching.

«تَعَلَّقَ»: clung, became dependent, or became emotionally attached.

«اعْتَلَقَ»: in some uses, grasped, clung, or became attached.

«عَلَاقَة» and «عِلَاقَة»: connection, attachment, or its means, with differences in vocalization and use.

«عِلْق»: a precious or valuable object that one cherishes.

«مُعَلَّق»: something suspended or held attached.

«مُعَلَّقَة»: the feminine form, describing something or someone kept suspended or attached.

«عَلَق»: thick or clotted blood; in another use, leeches.

«عَلَقَة»: a piece of thick or clotted blood, or a leech; also a Quranic name for a stage in human creation.

Quranic forms

Three principal formations occur in the Quran:

  • «عَلَقٍ» in Al-ʿAlaq 96:2.
  • «عَلَقَة», with case and definiteness variations, in Al-Ḥajj 22:5, Al-Muʾminūn 23:14, Ghāfir 40:67, and Al-Qiyāmah 75:38.
  • «الْمُعَلَّقَةِ» in Al-Nisāʾ 4:129.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14 uses ʿalaqah twice, first indefinite and then definite.

In total, the root occurs seven times in six verses: once as ʿalaq, five times as ʿalaqah, and once as muʿallaqah.

ʿAlaq and ʿalaqah belong to passages about human creation.

Muʿallaqah concerns marriage and a woman left in an unresolved position.

The verbs «عَلِقَ», «عَلَّقَ», and «تَعَلَّقَ» do not occur as verbs in the Quran.

Common Arabic derivatives should not be presented as Quranic forms merely because they share the root.

4. ʿAlaq and ʿalaqah in the beginning of human creation

The nouns have related but distinguishable lexical senses.

Early sources record ʿalaq for blood, particularly thick or clotted blood.

ʿAlaqah can mean a piece of such blood.

It also names a water-dwelling leech that attaches to a body and feeds on blood.

These referents are not identical.

Clotted blood is material in a particular condition.

A leech is a living animal attaching to another body.

Appearance, color, or adherence may help explain shared naming; the common name does not make their natures identical.

In creation passages, ʿalaqah names a stage in human formation.

Created the human being from ʿalaq

Al-ʿAlaq says:

خَلَقَ الْإِنْسَانَ مِنْ عَلَقٍ

khalaqa al-insāna min ʿalaq

Al-ʿAlaq 96:2

Al-ʿAlaq 96:2

“He created the human being from something suspended.”

«عَلَقٍ» follows the preposition «مِنْ».

It is described as a plural or collective form associated with «عَلَقَة».

This does not require each human to originate from several separate clots or several leech-like creatures.

The human being is named generically, and a material or stage of creation is introduced.

«مِنْ» relates human creation to that material or stage without supplying every detail of formation.

The historical sense of clotted blood is established; the verse itself gives no precise color, size, shape, or biological composition.

“Something suspended” is this article's proposed rendering, foregrounding attachment in the root. It is not a conclusive lexical result: early commentaries commonly explain the noun as blood or a clot, and morphology alone does not invalidate that reading.

Suspended need not mean hanging from a rope in empty space like a lamp.

Attachment and clinging also belong to this semantic family.

ʿAlaqah among creation stages

Al-Muʾminūn says:

ثُمَّ خَلَقْنَا النُّطْفَةَ عَلَقَةً فَخَلَقْنَا الْعَلَقَةَ مُضْغَةً فَخَلَقْنَا الْمُضْغَةَ عِظَامًا فَكَسَوْنَا الْعِظَامَ لَحْمًا ثُمَّ أَنْشَأْنَاهُ خَلْقًا آخَرَ ۚ فَتَبَارَكَ اللَّهُ أَحْسَنُ الْخَالِقِينَ

thumma khalaqnā al-nuṭfata ʿalaqatan fa-khalaqnā al-ʿalaqata muḍghatan fa-khalaqnā al-muḍghata ʿiẓāman fa-kasawnā al-ʿiẓāma laḥman thumma anshaʾnāhu khalqan ākhara fa-tabāraka Allāhu aḥsanu al-khāliqīn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

“Then We formed the drop into something suspended, then formed that suspended thing into a morsel of flesh, then formed the morsel into bones and clothed the bones with flesh; then We brought him into being as another creation. Blessed is The God, the best of creators.”

The noun occurs twice.

First:

خَلَقْنَا النُّطْفَةَ عَلَقَةً

khalaqnā al-nuṭfata ʿalaqah

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

Then:

فَخَلَقْنَا الْعَلَقَةَ مُضْغَةً

fa-khalaqnā al-ʿalaqata muḍghah

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

The first is indefinite and introduces a stage following the drop.

The second has «ال» and refers back to that stage.

The definite noun points to the stage just introduced, not a wholly different material or creature.

Next comes «مُضْغَة», from M Ḍ GH, associated with chewing and a morsel-sized piece.

The passage describes change and successive formation.

All scientific knowledge of embryonic development cannot be extracted from one word.

ʿAlaqah alone does not specify an exact day count or the shape of every cell and tissue.

Nor does muḍghah mean that teeth actually chewed the embryo. It can evoke size or appearance without an act of chewing.

Must ʿalaqah mean clotted blood?

Early lexicons record a piece of thick or clotted blood, and early Quranic commentaries also use this explanation. It cannot be omitted from the word's interpretive history.

A historical explanation, however, should not automatically be equated with the exact modern medical definition of a blood clot. Comparing the concepts requires separate lexical and scientific investigation.

Clinging and attachment are also clear in the root family.

“Something clinging or suspended” is a root-oriented proposal for the stage, but it needs contextual support; an established noun's meaning does not follow automatically from its root.

This proposal should not become an unsupported scientific certainty.

Comparing the attachment image with implantation is a separate interpretive and scientific inquiry. The word alone supplies neither its precise timing, cell types, nor all mechanisms.

Likewise, the lexical leech sense does not establish that the Quran says a human is created from an actual leech.

Three matters should remain distinct:

Recorded Arabic meanings.

The noun's use for a stage of human creation.

Scientific accounts of embryonic development.

They can be related without substituting for one another.

5. Other creation passages

Al-Ḥajj 22:5: creation and resurrection

Al-Ḥajj says:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ فِي رَيْبٍ مِنَ الْبَعْثِ فَإِنَّا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ مِنْ تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ نُطْفَةٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ عَلَقَةٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ مُضْغَةٍ مُخَلَّقَةٍ وَغَيْرِ مُخَلَّقَةٍ لِنُبَيِّنَ لَكُمْ ۚ وَنُقِرُّ فِي الْأَرْحَامِ مَا نَشَاءُ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى ثُمَّ نُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا ثُمَّ لِتَبْلُغُوا أَشُدَّكُمْ ۖ وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُتَوَفَّىٰ وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرَدُّ إِلَىٰ أَرْذَلِ الْعُمُرِ لِكَيْلَا يَعْلَمَ مِنْ بَعْدِ عِلْمٍ شَيْئًا ۚ وَتَرَى الْأَرْضَ هَامِدَةً فَإِذَا أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْهَا الْمَاءَ اهْتَزَّتْ وَرَبَتْ وَأَنْبَتَتْ مِنْ كُلِّ زَوْجٍ بَهِيجٍ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu in kuntum fī raybin mina al-baʿthi fa-innā khalaqnākum min turābin thumma min nuṭfatin thumma min ʿalaqatin thumma min muḍghatin mukhallaqatin wa-ghayri mukhallaqatin li-nubayyina lakum wa-nuqirru fī al-arḥāmi mā nashāʾu ilā ajalin musamman thumma nukhrijukum ṭiflan thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum wa-minkum man yutawaffā wa-minkum man yuraddu ilā ardhali al-ʿumuri li-kaylā yaʿlama min baʿdi ʿilmin shayʾan wa-tarā al-arḍa hāmidatan fa-idhā anzalnā ʿalayhā al-māʾa ihtazzat wa-rabat wa-anbatat min kulli zawjin bahīj

Al-Ḥajj 22:5

Al-Ḥajj 22:5

“People, if you doubt the resurrection, We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from something suspended, then from a morsel of flesh formed and unformed, to make clear to you. We keep in the wombs what We will until an appointed term, then bring you forth as children, then that you may reach full strength. Some of you are taken in death, and some are returned to the lowest stage of life, so that after knowledge they know nothing. You see the earth lifeless; when We send water upon it, it stirs, swells, and grows every delightful kind.”

ʿAlaqah appears among creation stages.

The purpose is not merely to describe an embryo.

The verse begins with doubt about resurrection, then discusses initial creation, growth, life's stages, and changing earth.

The stages form part of an argument about The God's power to raise human beings.

Grammatically, «مُخَلَّقَةٍ وَغَيْرِ مُخَلَّقَةٍ» qualifies «مُضْغَةٍ».

Those descriptions should not be inserted into ʿalaqah's meaning without regard to syntax.

ʿAlaqah precedes muḍghah; formed and unformed belong to the descriptors following muḍghah.

Ghāfir 40:67: from creation to old age

Ghāfir says:

هُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ نُطْفَةٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ عَلَقَةٍ ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا ثُمَّ لِتَبْلُغُوا أَشُدَّكُمْ ثُمَّ لِتَكُونُوا شُيُوخًا ۚ وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُتَوَفَّىٰ مِنْ قَبْلُ ۖ وَلِتَبْلُغُوا أَجَلًا مُسَمًّى وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

huwa alladhī khalaqakum min turābin thumma min nuṭfatin thumma min ʿalaqatin thumma yukhrijukum ṭiflan thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum thumma li-takūnū shuyūkhan wa-minkum man yutawaffā min qablu wa-li-tablughū ajalan musamman wa-laʿallakum taʿqilūn

Ghāfir 40:67

Ghāfir 40:67

“He is the One who created you from dust, then from a drop, then from something suspended, then brings you forth as children, then that you may reach full strength, then become elderly. Some of you are taken in death before that; and that you may reach an appointed term, and perhaps reason.”

Again, ʿalaqah follows the drop.

Unlike Al-Muʾminūn 23:14, this verse does not name every intervening stage before birth.

It moves from ʿalaqah to childhood, full strength, and old age.

Each list serves its passage's purpose and context.

Omitting muḍghah here does not deny that stage, just as mentioning ʿalaqah does not describe every developmental stage.

Human change across life provides an occasion for reflection.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:38: formation and proportion

Al-Qiyāmah says:

ثُمَّ كَانَ عَلَقَةً فَخَلَقَ فَسَوَّىٰ

thumma kāna ʿalaqatan fa-khalaqa fa-sawwā

Al-Qiyāmah 75:38

Al-Qiyāmah 75:38

“Then he became something suspended; then He created and proportioned.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:37 mentions the drop.

«ثُمَّ كَانَ عَلَقَةً» names the following stage.

It continues with «فَخَلَقَ فَسَوَّىٰ».

Neither verb belongs to ʿ L Q.

Khalaqa concerns creating, and sawwā ordering and proportioning.

The noun names the stage, while separate verbs express creating and proportioning.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:39 then mentions the two sexes, male and female.

The passage concerns the course of creation and divine power, not simply a material's name.

ʿAlaqah itself does not mean sexual differentiation; that is mentioned separately.

6. Muʿallaqah: a woman left in marital limbo

The other Quranic use concerns marriage rather than embryonic formation.

Al-Nisāʾ says:

وَلَنْ تَسْتَطِيعُوا أَنْ تَعْدِلُوا بَيْنَ النِّسَاءِ وَلَوْ حَرَصْتُمْ ۖ فَلَا تَمِيلُوا كُلَّ الْمَيْلِ فَتَذَرُوهَا كَالْمُعَلَّقَةِ ۚ وَإِنْ تُصْلِحُوا وَتَتَّقُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا

wa-lan tastaṭīʿū an taʿdilū bayna al-nisāʾi wa-law ḥaraṣtum fa-lā tamīlū kulla al-mayli fa-tadharūhā ka-al-muʿallaqati wa-in tuṣliḥū wa-tattaqū fa-inna Allāha kāna ghafūran raḥīmā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:129

Al-Nisāʾ 4:129

“You will never be able to be just between women, however eagerly you seek it. So do not incline entirely to one side, leaving her like one suspended. If you set things right and are mindful, The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

Muʿallaqah is a feminine passive participle of form II.

«عَلَّقَ» primarily concerns hanging or holding something attached.

Muʿallaq describes something suspended or held in dependence.

No physical hanging of a woman is intended here.

The comparison describes a wife still bound in marriage but deprived of necessary attention and rights.

Marital limbo

Marriage establishes a bond with responsibilities and rights for both spouses.

A husband may withdraw from life with his wife without ending the marriage.

She remains his wife yet lacks a proper shared life and its benefits.

The continuing bond leaves her without the position of someone whose marriage has ended. This explains the image; it does not deny possible legal remedies for ending an injurious relationship.

The image fits a woman held in a relationship while her place and rights within it remain unresolved.

Consider a man with two wives who devotes his attention and resources to one, leaving the other neglected for a long time while refusing to resolve the relationship.

The second wife enjoys neither a functioning marriage nor release from it.

This fits the prohibition on leaving her «كَالْمُعَلَّقَةِ».

“Do not incline entirely”

Before the comparison, the verse says:

فَلَا تَمِيلُوا كُلَّ الْمَيْلِ

fa-lā tamīlū kulla al-mayl

Al-Nisāʾ 4:129

“So do not incline entirely to one side.”

After saying that they will not be able to achieve justice however eagerly they seek it, the verse forbids complete inclination. A widespread interpretation distinguishes uncontrollable affection from obligations that can be fulfilled; that distinction is explanatory, not wording added to the translation.

Feelings and affection may not be evenly divided at every moment.

That difficulty does not permit abandonment of practical responsibilities and rights.

Greater affection for one wife does not justify depriving another of care, rights, or respect.

The distinction between inward feeling and responsible conduct matters here.

Muʿallaqah itself does not mean injustice or emotional preference; these are understood from the verse's context and structure.

It describes the unresolved position produced by that conduct.

Repairing the relationship or resolving its future

Al-Nisāʾ 4:128 discusses a woman fearing her husband's ill-treatment or withdrawal and the possibility of reconciliation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:129 prohibits leaving her suspended.

The following verse says:

وَإِنْ يَتَفَرَّقَا يُغْنِ اللَّهُ كُلًّا مِنْ سَعَتِهِ ۚ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ وَاسِعًا حَكِيمًا

wa-in yatafarraqā yughni Allāhu kullan min saʿatihi wa-kāna Allāhu wāsiʿan ḥakīmā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:130

Al-Nisāʾ 4:130

“If the two separate, The God will enrich each from His abundance; The God is All-Encompassing, Wise.”

The sequence does not insist merely on the outward survival of a marriage.

Repair and reconciliation are options, while separation is also mentioned.

Leaving a woman unresolved should not be called preserving the family or fidelity to marriage.

A relationship can remain formally intact while one party lives with deprivation and pressure.

The name of marriage alone does not demonstrate that rights are respected.

Al-Baqarah 2:231 also prohibits retaining women to harm them and transgress their rights.

The marital bond should not become a means of holding and restricting another person.

The lexical image remains suspension or being held attached; marital responsibilities follow from the passages' contexts.

7. ʿAlaqah and muʿallaqah: shared root, different uses

The Quran employs this family in two very different subjects.

In creation passages, ʿalaqah names a formation stage.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:129, muʿallaqah describes a woman kept in limbo.

Attachment provides a comparison between them, but the established nominal meaning of ʿalaqah cannot be replaced solely through comparison with muʿallaqah.

The shared root does not make the two words identical.

In creation passages the noun refers to material or a stage of growth.

In Al-Nisāʾ the participle metaphorically describes a social and marital position.

Muʿallaqah is a form-II passive participle; ʿalaqah is a noun.

Their morphology differs too.

A shared root does not exhaust each derivative's meaning.

Attachment is not always good or bad

An object's attachment may hold it securely in place.

Emotional attachment can be valuable to a person.

Elsewhere, someone may remain trapped in a relationship without respected rights or a way out.

Attachment itself is neither necessarily affection and benefit nor oppression and injury.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:129, the objection follows from abandonment and unmet rights in context.

In creation passages, a stage is named; interpreting its connection to attachment must retain the lexical distinctions already discussed.

Al-Aʿshā uses the family for emotional attachment.

The moral direction and outcome of each use must be assessed separately from the root's basic meaning.

8. Comparison with related roots

ʿ L Q and L Ṣ Q

L Ṣ Q concerns adhesion and close contact.

ʿ L Q overlaps but extends to hanging, catching, and dependence.

Paper stuck to a wall clearly illustrates adhesion.

A vessel suspended by a cord need not touch the ceiling, yet belongs to ʿ L Q usage.

Surface adhesion and suspension are related but distinct.

ʿ L Q and R B Ṭ

Rabṭ concerns binding or connecting things.

ʿ L Q can describe attachment or suspension even without a binding cord.

Tying an animal with a rope illustrates rabṭ.

Clothing may catch on a thorn without anyone tying it.

Both involve connection, but the means differ.

ʿ L Q and M S K

M S K concerns grasping and holding.

ʿ L Q can also involve remaining held rather than separating.

M S K foregrounds holding; ʿ L Q foregrounds clinging, catching, or attachment.

Someone holding a vessel in a hand illustrates the former.

The vessel hanging from a cord illustrates the latter.

ʿAlaqah and nuṭfah

Nuṭfah belongs to N Ṭ F and precedes ʿalaqah in creation passages.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14 describes the transformation from one to the other.

They name distinct stages in the sentence.

The features of one cannot simply be extracted from the other's root.

Each requires its own wording and context.

ʿAlaqah and muḍghah

Muḍghah belongs to M Ḍ GH and relates to chewing and a morsel-like piece.

It follows ʿalaqah in Al-Muʾminūn 23:14.

The nouns do not identify one stage.

ʿAlaqah does not mean a chewed morsel, nor does muḍghah mean attached or suspended material.

Translation should preserve that distinction.

ʿ L Q and ʿ Q D

ʿ Q D concerns knotting, binding, and securing a connection.

ʿ L Q can express attachment without any knot.

A rope knotted to an object illustrates ʿ Q D.

A leech attaches without a knot or cord.

A knot creates a connection, but attachment need not involve knotting.

In social usage, a marriage contract has a defined legal form, whereas muʿallaqah describes a wife left unresolved within the bond.

The roots should not be merged because both can concern connection.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

ʿAlaq, ʿalaqah, and muʿallaqah should remain distinct.

«عَلَقٍ» — Al-ʿAlaq 96:2

Root: ʿ L Q

Word type: Indefinite noun governed by «مِنْ», associated with the collective or plural of ʿalaqah.

Brief meaning: Clinging, hanging, and attachment; nominally, thick or clotted blood.

Meaning here: A material or stage at the beginning of human creation, named ʿalaq.

Proposed translation:

خَلَقَ الْإِنْسَانَ مِنْ عَلَقٍ

khalaqa al-insāna min ʿalaq

Al-ʿAlaq 96:2

“He created the human being from something suspended.”

This proposal foregrounds suspension and attachment. It is not the only lexical equivalent, and the established explanation as blood or a clot should remain visible.

The historical sense should be explained without turning it automatically into a definitive modern medical statement about an embryo.

«عَلَقَةٍ» — Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Ghāfir 40:67

Word type: Indefinite singular noun governed by «مِنْ».

Meaning here: A creation stage following nuṭfah.

Proposed translation:

ثُمَّ مِنْ عَلَقَةٍ

thumma min ʿalaqah

Al-Ḥajj 22:5; Ghāfir 40:67

“Then from something suspended.”

«عَلَقَةً» — Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

Word type: Indefinite singular accusative noun.

Meaning here: The stage into which the drop is formed.

Proposed translation:

ثُمَّ خَلَقْنَا النُّطْفَةَ عَلَقَةً

thumma khalaqnā al-nuṭfata ʿalaqah

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

“Then We formed the drop into something suspended.”

«الْعَلَقَةَ» — Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

Word type: Definite singular accusative noun.

Meaning here: The already introduced stage, subsequently formed into muḍghah.

Proposed translation:

فَخَلَقْنَا الْعَلَقَةَ مُضْغَةً

fa-khalaqnā al-ʿalaqata muḍghah

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

“Then We formed that suspended thing into a morsel of flesh.”

«عَلَقَةً» — Al-Qiyāmah 75:38

Word type: Indefinite singular accusative noun, predicate of «كَانَ».

Meaning here: The stage following the drop.

Proposed translation:

ثُمَّ كَانَ عَلَقَةً

thumma kāna ʿalaqah

Al-Qiyāmah 75:38

“Then he became something suspended.”

«الْمُعَلَّقَةِ» — Al-Nisāʾ 4:129

Word type: Definite feminine passive participle of form II, governed by the comparative kāf.

Brief meaning: Hanging, attaching, and remaining dependent; in this participle, being kept suspended.

Meaning here: A wife remaining bound in marriage but deprived of a proper shared life and left unresolved.

Proposed translation:

فَتَذَرُوهَا كَالْمُعَلَّقَةِ

fa-tadharūhā ka-al-muʿallaqah

Al-Nisāʾ 4:129

“Leaving her like one suspended.”

The wording preserves the image.

Contextual explanation can clarify that she enjoys neither proper marital rights nor release from the bond to decide her future.

It should not simply mean “a divorced woman”; her unresolved position differs from a completed separation.

“A woman in limbo” is a useful explanatory equivalent, but does not fully retain the image of suspension.

Conclusion

The Arabic root concerns clinging, hanging, catching, and attachment.

Physical uses include suspension by a cord, catching on a thorn, and adhesion to a surface.

Human uses extend the image to affection.

Early poetry shows attachment that need not be mutual or commendable.

Nominal senses include thick or clotted blood and leeches. Their relationship to adherence can be examined without asserting a single historical origin.

Quranic ʿalaq and ʿalaqah name a stage of human formation.

The historical blood or clot explanation must be acknowledged without automatically identifying it with a modern medical concept.

Likewise, attachment should not become a definitive claim about implantation timing or every developmental mechanism.

Muʿallaqah in Al-Nisāʾ 4:129 describes a woman left unresolved in marriage, with the bond retained but her place and rights neglected.

Physical suspension becomes an image of social and marital limbo.

The root's core concerns clinging, hanging, and attachment. Quranic ʿalaq and ʿalaqah name a creation stage, while muʿallaqah describes a wife left in limbo.

This article proposes “something suspended” for creation passages and “one suspended” for Al-Nisāʾ 4:129. The former is a root-oriented choice alongside the established historical blood/clot explanation, not a conclusive embryological identification. Lexical, biological, and social explanations should remain distinct; installing the study does not rewrite existing verse translations.
