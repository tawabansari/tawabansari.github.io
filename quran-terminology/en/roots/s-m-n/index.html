Plumpness, flesh and fat, clarified butter, and food's capacity to build up the body

The Arabic root S M N concerns the plumpness and fleshiness of human or animal bodies. Its tangible sense concerns fullness of flesh and fat in contrast to leanness; describing a body as plump does not necessarily report a prior process of gaining weight.

Samīn denotes a plump creature, siman the condition of plumpness, and samn clarified butter. Samina expresses becoming plump; asmana in one usage means fattening or causing increased flesh and fat.

The root occurs four times in the Quran: simān twice in the Egyptian king's dream, samīn once in Abraham's hospitality, and yusminu once for food that neither fattens nor relieves hunger.

Plumpness is a bodily feature whose significance varies by context. The dream's plump cows contribute to an image of abundant and scarce harvests; Abraham's plump calf concerns feeding guests; the denial of fattening in Al-Ghāshiyah emphasizes food's ineffectiveness.

1. Early Arabic meaning and witnesses attributed to pre-Quranic times

Plumpness in contrast to leanness and wasting

The familiar central sense of siman is bodily plumpness and fleshiness, contrasted with huzāl and ḍumūr, leanness or bodily wasting.

Lexicons record samina yasmanu for becoming plump and samīn for a creature with substantial flesh and fat.

This distinction mattered in livestock husbandry. A cow, sheep, goat, or camel's condition could reflect feeding, pasture, and rearing conditions. An adequately fed animal could be described as plump compared with one depleted by shortage.

People raising animals for milk and meat under varied climatic conditions could readily recognize this difference.

Early lexical descriptions connect plumpness with flesh and fat. It is therefore not simply large apparent size: bodily size, height, and plumpness are distinct features.

One animal may be tall and lean, another shorter and plump.

This branch concerns bodily fullness in flesh and fat, not every kind of largeness.

A poetic witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays describes goats supplying milk products:

فَتَمْلَأُ بَيْتَنَا أَقِطًا وَسَمْنًا
وَحَسْبُكَ مِنْ غِنًى شِبَعٌ وَرِيُّ

fa-tamlaʾu baytanā aqiṭan wa-samnan / wa-ḥasbuka min ghinan shibaʿun wa-riyyu

“They fill our house with dried curds and clarified butter; enough wealth for you is a full stomach and quenched thirst.”

The line is transmitted among poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and in early literary sources.

Aqiṭ is a dried milk product, comparable to dried curds; samn is clarified butter derived from milk fat.

The poet names an animal-derived food rather than bodily plumpness. Both bodily condition and edible fat belong to the family.

The line connects the word with livestock and food provision. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not independently date that particular line, especially where variant wording is transmitted.

It supports early use of samn for clarified butter without establishing whether that sense or plumpness developed first.

Simān describing animals

Early literature also attributes simānu al-kilābi ʿijāfu al-fiṣāl to Imruʾ al-Qays: “plump dogs and lean young camels.”

Its explanations connect the dogs' plumpness with plentiful meat from slaughter for guests, and the young camels' leanness with milk given to guests instead.

Contrasting bodily conditions thus describe hospitality and generosity.

Simān denotes the dogs' plumpness; ʿijāf the young camels' leanness.

The expression is transmitted in early literary and rhetorical works, often as a short fragment. The citations considered here do not establish a complete text and independent date; the fragment alone cannot date every usage of the root.

Despite that limit, its contrast between plumpness and leanness is clear, as in the Egyptian king's dream.

Samn: clarified butter

Samn names clarified butter prepared from milk and its fatty products.

It is related to siman within the field of fat, but differs in vocalization and meaning.

Siman is a bodily condition; samn an edible substance.

A person or animal can be plump without clarified butter being the subject; someone can use samn in food without being plump.

Samana al-ṭaʿāma is also recorded for preparing or dressing food with clarified butter.

Sammān can name a clarified-butter seller. Istasmana can mean requesting clarified butter or considering something plump.

Arḍun samīnah describes good, fertile soil, extending bodily fullness to productive land.

The figure does not mean that soil literally contains flesh and fat like an animal body.

Some lexical reports record tasmīn for cooling in the speech of groups in Yemen and Taif. This limited usage is not the root's general meaning and lacks contextual support in its Quranic occurrences.

The principal field concerns plumpness and fat, but each derivative needs its own explanation.

2. Quranic forms and word structure

The root occurs four times in two morphological groups.

Samīn and its plural simān: an adjective for a plump or fleshy creature.

  • Simānin — Yūsuf 12:43
  • Simānin — Yūsuf 12:46
  • Samīnin — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26

Yusminu: a Form IV imperfect verb meaning to fatten, here describing food's capacity to build up bodily flesh and fat.

  • Yusminu — Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7

Singular samīn follows faʿīl and describes plumpness. Simān is its established plural.

Simān modifies baqarāt in Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46; samīn modifies ʿijl in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26.

Yusminu derives from asmana. Here Form IV is causative: something causes plumpness or increased flesh and fat.

Samina can describe the creature becoming plump; asmana can identify what causes it.

Form IV does not always have only this causative sense. Asmana is also recorded for possessing or obtaining plump livestock. The construction and context determine the sense in Al-Ghāshiyah.

Other Arabic forms include siman, samn, samīnah, tasmīn, musamman, and istismān, but these forms do not occur in the Quran.

3. Simān in the king's dream: plumpness against leanness

In Yūsuf 12:43:

وَقَالَ الْمَلِكُ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ يَا أَيُّهَا الْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِي فِي رُؤْيَايَ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ لِلرُّؤْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ

wa-qāla al-maliku innī arā sabʿa baqarātin simānin yaʾkuluhunna sabʿun ʿijāfun wa-sabʿa sunbulātin khuḍrin wa-ukhara yābisātin yā ayyuhā al-malaʾu aftūnī fī ruʾyāya in kuntum li-al-ruʾyā taʿburūn

Yūsuf 12:43

“The king said: I see seven plump cows being eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others dry. Nobles, advise me about my dream, if you interpret dreams.”

Simānin describes the cows seen in the dream.

They contrast with ʿijāf, lean cows lacking flesh.

The contrast clarifies the root: the two groups differ physically, one plump and the other lean.

Simān does not mean cows large in height or age; it describes their fleshiness.

Plumpness and plentiful feed

An animal's plumpness can relate to adequate food and suitable rearing conditions.

Conversely, shortages of fodder and feed can lead to bodily depletion.

This helps explain the dream image, but does not make simān mean “rainy years” or “years of abundant harvest.”

Within the dream it describes bodies. Abundant years emerge from interpreting the dream.

The distinction between the image's meaning and its interpreted significance must remain.

Simān repeated in Yūsuf 12:46

In Yūsuf 12:46, the king's messenger retells the dream to Joseph, mentioning sabʿi baqarātin simān.

In both verses simān means plump cows.

The second sentence also forms part of a request to interpret the king's dream.

Joseph answers with seven years of sustained cultivation and storage. In Yūsuf 12:47 he directs that the harvest remain in its ears except for what is needed to eat.

Yūsuf 12:48 describes seven subsequent hard years consuming the stores laid aside.

Yūsuf 12:49 then describes renewed relief and abundance.

The plump cows thus correspond in interpretation to productive years; the lean cows to hardship and consumption of stored reserves.

The transition from bodily image to social and economic significance comes from Joseph's interpretation, not the lexical definition of S M N.

Does plumpness always indicate wealth?

These verses do not establish that every plump person or animal lives in prosperity, or every lean person in poverty.

Plumpness is a bodily condition with multiple possible contributing factors.

The dream uses it to distinguish two periods, without creating a universal economic rule about bodies.

Nor is plumpness invariably synonymous with health. Health, nourishment, and bodily condition are related but distinct.

The verses clearly contrast tangible conditions and use that contrast in interpreting the dream.

4. ʿIjlin samīn: a plump calf in Abraham's hospitality

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26:

فَرَاغَ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ فَجَاءَ بِعِجْلٍ سَمِينٍ

fa-rāgha ilā ahlihi fa-jāʾa bi-ʿijlin samīn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26

“Then he went to his household and brought a plump calf.”

Samīnin modifies ʿijlin.

ʿIjl denotes a calf; samīn describes its bodily condition.

Unlike the king's dream, the narrative presents an actual animal rather than a dreamed symbol.

The calf's plumpness and preparing food

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:24 introduces Abraham's guests; Al-Dhāriyāt 51:25 describes their entering and greeting him.

Abraham then goes to his household and brings a plump calf.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:27 says that he sets it before his guests and invites them to eat.

The calf thus belongs to hospitality and providing food.

A plump animal has more flesh and fat and can provide a substantial meal.

This contextual significance should remain distinct from the word's definition.

Samīn does not mean “a calf designated for hospitality.” It describes plumpness; hospitality's value emerges from Abraham's behavior and the narrative.

The roasted calf in Hūd 11:69

Hūd 11:69 says that Abraham brought his guests bi-ʿijlin ḥanīdh.

Ḥanīdh concerns cooking or roasting meat.

Samīn in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26 describes plumpness; ḥanīdh in Hūd 11:69 describes preparation for eating.

They are compatible but not identical.

Samīn does not specify cooking, and ḥanīdh does not itself mean plump.

Plumpness also establishes no exact weight, age, or rearing method.

The verse states a feature relevant here: the calf was plump.

Does samīn carry a positive value?

Here the calf's condition accompanies Abraham's hospitality and can suggest plentiful, suitable food for guests.

The positive value arises from conduct and context, not an inherent meaning of generosity in S M N.

The same adjective can describe another animal without any reference to hospitality.

Its lexical sense remains plump or fleshy; the narrative's moral significance is considered separately.

5. Lā yusminu wa-lā yughnī min jūʿ: food that neither fattens nor relieves hunger

In Al-Ghāshiyah 88:6:

لَيْسَ لَهُمْ طَعَامٌ إِلَّا مِنْ ضَرِيعٍ

laysa lahum ṭaʿāmun illā min ḍarīʿ

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:6

“They have no food except ḍarīʿ.”

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7 continues:

لَا يُسْمِنُ وَلَا يُغْنِي مِنْ جُوعٍ

lā yusminu wa-lā yughnī min jūʿ

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7

“It neither fattens nor relieves hunger.”

Yusminu is a Form IV imperfect verb.

The food is the potential cause of fattening, but lā negates that effect.

Lā yusminu therefore says that the food does not produce plumpness or increase flesh and fat.

Why distinguish two effects of food?

The verse immediately adds wa-lā yughnī min jūʿ to the denial of fattening.

The second verb concerns sufficiency or relief from need; jūʿ denotes hunger.

Two effects are distinguished:

First, food that builds up bodily substance.

Second, food that relieves the need expressed by hunger.

These are not identical concepts.

A meal can relieve hunger without producing plumpness; being plump does not prevent becoming hungry.

Both effects are denied of this food.

The verse therefore goes beyond unpleasant taste: the food fails to provide the expected benefits of eating.

Does yusminu mean nourish?

Some translations render lā yusminu as “does not nourish” or “gives no strength.”

These convey part of the message, since fattening relates to supplying bodily substance.

Lexically, however, asmana is closer to “fatten,” while nourishment is broader.

Beneficial nourishment does not necessarily cause fatness.

A direct translation should preserve fattening, with explanation clarifying the food's failure to build up the body or relieve hunger.

“It neither strengthens nor relieves hunger” communicates the general point, but strengthening is not the root's precise equivalent.

What is ḍarīʿ?

Ḍarīʿ belongs to Ḍ R ʿ, not S M N.

Early Arabic records the name for an unpleasant plant unsuitable for food. Some accounts connect it to a thorny plant in its dried state.

Accounts differ over its precise identification.

The name alone cannot establish a particular species with complete scientific specifications.

Here its significance lies in food that neither fattens nor relieves hunger.

The separate study of Ḍ R ʿ discusses ḍarīʿ and its lexical family in greater detail.

Study of Ḍ R ʿ

Food and punishment in the Hereafter

Al-Ghāshiyah describes this food within an otherworldly condition.

The verse should not become merely dietary advice about earthly plants.

Something is presented as food yet fails to perform food's beneficial functions for the people described.

Lā yusminu does not specify its chemical composition, energy content, or the biology of otherworldly punishment.

Its direct meaning is clear: the food is ineffective in fattening and relieving hunger.

The exact nature of the condition exceeds this root's lexical meaning.

6. Comparison with related roots

S M N and ʿ J F: plumpness and leanness

ʿ J F concerns animal leanness and bodily depletion in the Quran.

Simān and ʿijāf contrast in Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46.

The opposition clarifies both.

Simān are plump, fleshy cows; ʿijāf are lean cows with little flesh.

The contrast is not evidence that one root derives from the other; the families are distinct.

S M N and H Z L: fleshiness and wasting

Huzāl denotes leanness or bodily wasting.

Lexicons commonly define samīn against mahzūl.

This clarifies a bodily feature rather than mere size or outward beauty.

It does not establish that every lean creature is ill or every plump one healthy.

Huzāl concerns loss of flesh; cause and health status require separate assessment.

S M N and SH Ḥ M: plumpness and fat

Shaḥm denotes animal bodily fat.

Siman describes the creature's plump condition.

Shaḥm can be a bodily substance, whereas siman describes overall condition.

Samn, clarified butter, also concerns fat, but is not identical to shaḥm.

“Having fat” explains part of samīn, but “plump” or “fleshy” is more natural.

S M N and GH N Y: fattening and sufficiency

The two roots occur together in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7:

«لَا يُسْمِنُ وَلَا يُغْنِي مِنْ جُوعٍ».

lā yusminu wa-lā yughnī min jūʿ

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7

Yusminu emphasizes food's fattening effect, while yughnī here concerns relief from hunger.

The verse negates two aspects of food's usefulness.

Elsewhere GH N Y can express broader independence or sufficiency; here min jūʿ specifies the need involved.

That qualification must remain in translation.

S M N and J W ʿ: bodily condition and hunger

Jūʿ is hunger, the need for food.

Siman is bodily plumpness.

They need not be opposites: a plump person may be hungry and a lean person satiated after eating.

Thus Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7 makes two distinct statements, denying fattening and relief from hunger.

The verse does not limit its description of ineffective food to a single feature.

S M N and S N M: plumpness and a hump

S N M includes sanām, a camel's hump or raised part of its back.

S M N concerns bodily plumpness.

A camel may be plump with a prominent hump, but the hump's name and the description of plumpness differ.

Similar consonants do not prove historical kinship.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish the adjective from the causative verb.

Simānin — Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46

Proposed equivalent:

“Plump”

For the phrase:

سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ

sabʿa baqarātin simān

Yūsuf 12:43

“Seven plump cows.”

This is concise and natural.

“Seven fat cows” also conveys the bodily sense. The Persian proposal prefers farbeh as a suitable formal description of livestock.

“Seven prosperous cows” or “seven cows of abundant years” substitutes interpretation for the dream's physical description.

Samīnin — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26

Proposed equivalent:

“Plump”

For the phrase:

فَجَاءَ بِعِجْلٍ سَمِينٍ

fa-jāʾa bi-ʿijlin samīn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26

“Then he brought a plump calf.”

“A fleshy calf” is also suitable, but “plump” stays closer to the concise adjective.

“Roasted calf” must not replace it: roasting in Hūd 11:69 comes from another word.

Yusminu — Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7

Proposed equivalent:

“Fattens”

For the phrase:

لَا يُسْمِنُ وَلَا يُغْنِي مِنْ جُوعٍ

lā yusminu wa-lā yughnī min jūʿ

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7

“It neither fattens nor relieves hunger.”

This preserves the distinction between the verbs.

A lexical note can explain the food's effect in building bodily flesh and fat.

“Does not nourish” communicates the general message but loses some specificity as a direct rendering of the root.

Non-Quranic forms

Siman: bodily plumpness or fleshiness.

Samn: clarified butter.

Samina: became plump.

Sammana: fattened.

Asmana: causatively, caused plumpness; in other usages, possessed or obtained plump livestock.

Tasmīn: fattening.

Musamman: fattened.

These illuminate the lexical range but should not be presented as forms actually occurring in the Quran.

Conclusion

S M N concerns plumpness, fleshiness, and bodily flesh and fat, notably in humans and animals.

Samn, clarified butter, is another important branch connected with fat. The poetic witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates its pastoral and dairy setting.

The root occurs four times in the Quran.

Simān in Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46 describes the king's dreamed cows. Interpretation associates them with abundant harvests, while the lexical meaning remains physical.

Samīn in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26 describes Abraham's calf for his guests: its fleshiness, not its cooking or an animal's moral value.

Lā yusminu in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7 negates fattening; wa-lā yughnī min jūʿ denies relief from hunger. These statements are related but distinct.

The root can be defined as follows:

S M N principally denotes bodily plumpness and fullness of flesh and fat. Samīn describes a plump creature, and asmana can mean to fatten. Samn names clarified butter within the field of fat and dairy products.

This explains the four Quranic occurrences without importing additional meanings.

Plumpness, nourishment, satiety, health, and prosperity are related but different. The Quran uses the tangible bodily feature in three contexts: abundance and shortage, hospitality, and food unable to meet bodily needs.

Each usage receives its significance from context, while its lexical meaning remains within plumpness and fattening.
