The mouth as an organ of eating and speech: from the human body to outward statements, unsupported claims, and the distance between words and truth

1. The root in early Arabic

The triliteral Arabic root F W H concerns the mouth, through which humans and many animals receive food and water, and which is also a principal part of the human speech apparatus.

فَوْه, فَاه, and فَم are recognized Arabic forms naming the mouth. The plural is أَفْوَاه.

Physically, the mouth admits food and drink. In speech, it is where words emerge. Related forms in this family concern speaking, opening the mouth, and uttering words.

In classical Arabic, فَاهَ بِالْكَلَامِ means uttering speech; تَفَوَّهَ بِهِ likewise concerns saying or expressing something.

For example, مَا فُهْتُ بِكَلِمَةٍ means “I did not utter a single word.”

مُفَوَّه can describe an eloquent, capable speaker. أَفْوَه, by contrast, can describe someone with a large mouth or protruding teeth.

These uses must remain distinct. A large mouth is a physical characteristic; eloquence concerns expressive ability. Neither establishes the other.

Evidence from pre-Islamic Arabic poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma contains the verse:

بَكَرْنَ بُكُورًا وَاسْتَحَرْنَ بِسُحْرَةٍ
فَهُنَّ لِوَادِي الرَّسِّ كَالْيَدِ لِلْفَمِ

Bakarna bukūran wa-staḥarna bi-suḥratin / Fa-hunna li-wādi r-rassi ka-l-yadi li-l-fam.

“They set out early, journeying at dawn; their way toward Wadi al-Rass was as sure as a hand finding the mouth.”

The poet describes a group of women traveling. They follow their route as surely as the hand knows its destination when approaching the mouth.

الفَم here names the actual human mouth. It does not mean speech, belief, or false words.

The verse has variant readings, including فَهُنَّ وَوَادِي الرَّسِّ كَالْيَدِ لِلْفَمِ. This variation does not alter the basic sense of “mouth.”

The verse belongs to the transmitted tradition of Zuhayr’s Muʿallaqah. As with other pre-Islamic poetry, attribution alone does not establish its precise date of composition.

It attests the mouth in a physical simile and shows the familiar bodily organ being named in the early poetic tradition.

How does فَم relate to F W H?

This family has a significant morphological feature: its three root letters are not visible in the common form فَم.

In a familiar analysis by Arab grammarians, the earlier form was فَوْه. Loss of final hā and changes in the word’s shape produced the shorter forms فُو, فَا, and فِي.

In the common form فَم, mīm replaces part of the earlier structure.

Grammatical explanations differ over whether mīm directly replaces wāw or was supplied after the loss of hā to complete the noun. No single analysis should be presented as a certain historical account without considering those differences.

The family connection is nevertheless clear, since Arabic records these forms:

فَم: mouth.

فُو: the nominative form in certain possessive constructions.

فَا: the accusative form.

فِي: the genitive form in the same inflectional system.

أَفْوَاه: the plural of mouth.

فُوَيْه: a diminutive that more visibly preserves the family’s fuller structure.

In Arabic grammar, فُو accordingly belongs among nouns with special case forms when attached to another noun or pronoun.

The Qur’an contains one such construction, examined below under Al-Raʿd 13:14.

Openings of objects and broader uses

Arabic also applies the name of the mouth to certain openings and apertures.

فُوهَةُ الْبِئْرِ denotes a well’s opening, while فُوهَةُ النَّهْرِ can denote the mouth or opening of a river.

The imagery is intelligible: both a human mouth and a channel opening allow something to enter or emerge.

This resemblance should not become a definitive claim about the historical order of all senses.

For Qur’anic interpretation, retain the noun’s direct sense first: the mouth as a physical organ or, rhetorically, the place where speech becomes outwardly expressed.

2. Qur’anic forms

F W H occurs thirteen times in the Qur’an.

Twelve occurrences are forms of the plural أَفْوَاه with attached pronouns and particles; one is the singular فَاهُ.

The plural occurs in Al Imran 3:118, Al Imran 3:167, Al-Ma’idah 5:41, Al-Tawbah 9:8, Al-Tawbah 9:30, Al-Tawbah 9:32, Ibrahim 14:9, Al-Kahf 18:5, Al-Nur 24:15, Al-Ahzab 33:4, Ya Sin 36:65, and Al-Saff 61:8.

The singular فَاهُ appears only in Al-Raʿd 13:14.

All are forms of the noun for mouth. Neither the verbs فَاهَ and تَفَوَّهَ nor the descriptions أَفْوَه and مُفَوَّه occur in the Qur’an.

The common form فَم also does not occur in the Qur’anic text, although أَفْوَاه and فَاه belong to the same family.

The important point is that most Qur’anic occurrences connect the mouth with speech, but the word itself does not mean lying, hypocrisy, or false speech.

The value of the statement and the speaker’s condition emerge from each verse’s verbs, descriptions, and context.

3. The physical mouth: drinking, gestures, and silence

Al-Raʿd 13:14: water that does not reach the mouth

لَهُ دَعْوَةُ الْحَقِّ وَالَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِهِ لَا يَسْتَجِيبُونَ لَهُمْ بِشَيْءٍ إِلَّا كَبَاسِطِ كَفَّيْهِ إِلَى الْمَاءِ لِيَبْلُغَ فَاهُ وَمَا هُوَ بِبَالِغِهِ وَمَا دُعَاءُ الْكَافِرِينَ إِلَّا فِي ضَلَالٍ

Lahu daʿwatu l-ḥaqqi wa-lladhīna yadʿūna min dūnihi lā yastajībūna lahum bi-shayʾin illā ka-bāsiṭi kaffayhi ilā l-māʾi li-yablugha fāhu wa-mā huwa bi-bālighihi wa-mā duʿāʾu l-kāfirīna illā fī ḍalāl.

“To Him belongs the true invocation. Those whom they call upon besides Him answer them in nothing, except as one stretching both hands toward water so that it may reach his mouth, although it will not reach it. The invocation of the disbelievers is only in error.”

Al-Raʿd 13:14

This is the family’s only singular occurrence in the Qur’an.

فَاهُ combines فَا with the suffix هُ: “his mouth.”

Grammatically, فَاهُ is the object of يَبْلُغَ and is accusative.

The alif marks the accusative in this noun’s special construction. Final hā is the possessive pronoun, not the reconstructed root’s original hā.

The verse depicts someone extending both hands toward water, expecting it to reach his mouth, but that expectation is not fulfilled.

فَاهُ has its literal bodily meaning here.

The mouth is where water must arrive for drinking. يَبْلُغَ expresses reaching, while the following clause denies that it occurs.

The word itself therefore does not mean thirst, invocation, or inability. Those ideas emerge from the whole image.

The Qur’an uses this image to explain the futility of invoking objects of worship that cannot answer.

A physical situation becomes a parable: wanting something and reaching toward it do not by themselves secure it.

This occurrence prevents restricting F W H to speaking alone. Before any rhetorical extension, the mouth is an actual bodily organ.

Ibrahim 14:9: hands and mouths

After recalling earlier peoples and the arrival of their Messengers with clear signs, Ibrahim 14:9 describes returning their hands to their mouths.

The direct construction concerns bringing or returning hands toward mouths.

There are several interpretations of the gesture’s manner and intention.

One understands opponents bringing their hands to their mouths or biting their fingers in anger or denial.

Another reads the gesture as surprise, protest, or a forceful response to the Messengers’ words.

Interpretations also differ over whether the suffix in أَفْوَاهِهِمْ refers to the opponents’ own mouths or, in another explanatory reading, to the Messengers’ mouths.

On the surface grammar, reference to the group moving their hands is more natural. Reference to the Messengers’ mouths requires further contextual support.

The rest of the verse expresses rejection of the Messengers’ message and doubt about their call. The gesture thus occurs in a context of refusal and opposition.

Yet أَفْوَاهِهِمْ alone cannot establish the exact hand movement or everyone’s psychological state.

The mouth can therefore form part of a bodily gesture whose rhetorical force emerges from the whole scene.

Ya Sin 36:65: mouths sealed

الْيَوْمَ نَخْتِمُ عَلَىٰ أَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَتُكَلِّمُنَا أَيْدِيهِمْ وَتَشْهَدُ أَرْجُلُهُمْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ

Al-yawma nakhtimu ʿalā afwāhihim wa-tukallimunā aydīhim wa-tashhadu arjuluhum bi-mā kānū yaksibūn.

“Today We seal their mouths, their hands speak to Us, and their feet testify to what they used to earn.”

Ya Sin 36:65

أَفْوَاهِهِمْ identifies the mouths of those undergoing judgment.

نَخْتِمُ expresses sealing, and أَفْوَاهِهِمْ identifies where the seal is placed.

The continuation attributes speech to hands and testimony to feet.

Ordinarily, the mouth helps people make claims, explain, and defend their conduct. Here the Qur’an describes the ordinary route of speech being closed while other bodily members testify.

Sealing the mouth does not mean destroying the organ; its speech function is restricted in that situation.

Nor does this establish a definite theory of the physical mechanism by which bodily members speak in the Hereafter.

The verse clearly states that the truth about human conduct does not depend solely on claims made with the mouth.

Hands and feet also feature in the account of judgment.

The image foregrounds words and deeds, but ethical responsibility comes from the judgment context, not the lexical meaning of أَفْوَاه.

4. Mouth and heart: outward speech and inward reality

A prominent Qur’anic use sets أَفْوَاه against the heart and what a person conceals within.

Al Imran 3:167: words absent from the heart

وَلِيَعْلَمَ الَّذِينَ نَافَقُوا وَقِيلَ لَهُمْ تَعَالَوْا قَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ أَوِ ادْفَعُوا قَالُوا لَوْ نَعْلَمُ قِتَالًا لَاتَّبَعْنَاكُمْ هُمْ لِلْكُفْرِ يَوْمَئِذٍ أَقْرَبُ مِنْهُمْ لِلْإِيمَانِ يَقُولُونَ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ مَا لَيْسَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ وَاللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَكْتُمُونَ

Wa-li-yaʿlama lladhīna nāfaqū wa-qīla lahum taʿālaw qātilū fī sabīli llāhi awi dfaʿū qālū law naʿlamu qitālan la-ttabaʿnākum hum li-l-kufri yawmaʾidhin aqrabu minhum li-l-īmāni yaqūlūna bi-afwāhihim mā laysa fī qulūbihim wa-llāhu aʿlamu bi-mā yaktumūn.

“And so that He might know those who acted hypocritically. They were told, ‘Come, fight in the way of The God, or defend.’ They said, ‘Had we known there would be fighting, we would have followed you.’ That day they were nearer to disbelief than to faith. They say with their mouths what is not in their hearts; and The God knows best what they conceal.”

Al Imran 3:167

بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ identifies the means of outward expression.

يَقُولُونَ denotes the act of saying itself.

The clause concerning their hearts shows a mismatch between what they express and what they hold within.

أَفْوَاه does not itself mean hypocrisy. Hypocrisy is recognized from their conduct, statements, and the exposed inconsistency with their hearts.

The verse also declares that The God knows best what they conceal.

Three distinct ideas are involved: speaking, holding or lacking an inward stance, and concealing the truth.

They do not share one root and should not all be reduced to “mouth” or “covering.”

The verse also addresses a particular group in a particular event. It does not make everyone declining to join a battle necessarily a liar or hypocrite; its judgment concerns those people’s conduct and condition.

Al-Ma’idah 5:41: professing faith without faith in the heart

Al-Ma’idah 5:41 speaks of people who profess faith although their hearts have not believed.

Here بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ accompanies the declaration آمَنَّا, contrasted with the absence of belief in their hearts.

The central issue is the distinction between professing faith and actually possessing it.

The mouth can utter a statement, but saying it does not conclusively establish a person’s inner condition.

This does not make every verbal declaration worthless. Truthful speech can genuinely express faith and commitment.

The verse criticizes the gap between this group’s claim and inward reality.

Al-Tawbah 9:8: pleasing with mouths while hearts refuse

Al-Tawbah 9:8 describes people who please their listeners with words while their hearts refuse.

يُرْضُونَكُمْ expresses pleasing, and بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ its means.

Pleasant words do not always indicate genuine acceptance or sincere intention.

The mouth itself, however, does not mean flattery or deception. That condition emerges from the contrast between speech and heart.

For the distinction between outward satisfaction and genuine acceptance, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root R Ḍ W.

Al Imran 3:118: hostility apparent in speech

Al Imran 3:118 describes people whose hostility has appeared from their mouths, while what their breasts conceal is greater.

The mouth is where part of an inward stance becomes visible.

The contrast with Al Imran 3:167 is clear.

In Al Imran 3:167, people say what they do not hold in their hearts.

In Al Imran 3:118, speech reveals part of their inner hostility.

The mouth can thus help conceal truth behind outward words or disclose part of an inward reality.

Its lexical meaning stays constant. The difference comes from the verbs and the relationship between speech and inner condition.

The description also concerns harmful conduct and hostility. It should not be generalized to everyone outside a group merely because of differences of belief or social identity.

5. Mouths and unsupported claims: saying does not create truth

Al-Kahf 18:5: a grave statement emerging from mouths

مَا لَهُمْ بِهِ مِنْ عِلْمٍ وَلَا لِآبَائِهِمْ كَبُرَتْ كَلِمَةً تَخْرُجُ مِنْ أَفْوَاهِهِمْ إِنْ يَقُولُونَ إِلَّا كَذِبًا

Mā lahum bihi min ʿilmin wa-lā li-ābāʾihim kaburat kalimatan takhruju min afwāhihim in yaqūlūna illā kadhibā.

“Neither they nor their fathers have any knowledge of it. Grave is the word that comes from their mouths! They say nothing but a lie.”

Al-Kahf 18:5

The verse continues the criticism of claiming that The God has a child.

The verb تَخْرُجُ and the reference to mouths describe the statement emerging.

Its gravity is conveyed by كَبُرَتْ and كَلِمَةً, while the final clause declares its falsity.

Any spoken statement can emerge from a mouth. Why, then, emphasize mouths here?

The context concerns speaking without knowledge and attributing something to The God. Emphasizing the mouth highlights the distance between asserting a claim and having grounds for it.

It does not make أَفْوَاه lexically mean an unsupported statement.

Unsupported words may emerge from a mouth, but not every spoken word is unsupported.

Al-Nur 24:15: circulating a statement without knowledge

إِذْ تَلَقَّوْنَهُ بِأَلْسِنَتِكُمْ وَتَقُولُونَ بِأَفْوَاهِكُمْ مَا لَيْسَ لَكُمْ بِهِ عِلْمٌ وَتَحْسَبُونَهُ هَيِّنًا وَهُوَ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ عَظِيمٌ

Idh talaqqawnahu bi-alsinatikum wa-taqūlūna bi-afwāhikum mā laysa lakum bihi ʿilmun wa-taḥsabūnahu hayyinan wa-huwa ʿinda llāhi ʿaẓīm.

“When you received it with your tongues and said with your mouths what you had no knowledge of, and thought it insignificant, while with The God it was grave.”

Al-Nur 24:15

أَلْسِنَتِكُمْ and أَفْوَاهِكُمْ occur in the same sentence.

لِسَان primarily names the tongue, and Qur’anic uses can also concern speech, a people’s language, and manner of expression.

أَفْوَاه means mouths.

The Qur’an first describes receiving and passing on words with tongues, then saying with mouths what the speakers do not know.

This construction depicts transmission: something is heard from one person, passed to another, and repeated without adequate knowledge of its truth.

The listeners regard the matter as minor, although it is grave with The God.

Today, too, a report or accusation can spread through repetition. People sometimes repeat it from habit or trust in the previous speaker without investigating the matter itself.

That is a contemporary application of the verse’s message; أَفْوَاه does not lexically mean media, rumor, or news transmission.

Receiving a statement must be distinguished from knowing its truth.

Lack of knowledge also does not alone establish an initial intention to lie. Responsibility must be assessed in light of what the person knew, their opportunity to investigate, and their conduct.

Al-Ahzab 33:4: spoken words and actual relationships

مَا جَعَلَ اللَّهُ لِرَجُلٍ مِنْ قَلْبَيْنِ فِي جَوْفِهِ وَمَا جَعَلَ أَزْوَاجَكُمُ اللَّائِي تُظَاهِرُونَ مِنْهُنَّ أُمَّهَاتِكُمْ وَمَا جَعَلَ أَدْعِيَاءَكُمْ أَبْنَاءَكُمْ ذَٰلِكُمْ قَوْلُكُمْ بِأَفْوَاهِكُمْ وَاللَّهُ يَقُولُ الْحَقَّ وَهُوَ يَهْدِي السَّبِيلَ

Mā jaʿala llāhu li-rajulin min qalbayni fī jawfihi wa-mā jaʿala azwājakumu llāʾī tuẓāhirūna minhunna ummahātikum wa-mā jaʿala adʿiyāʾakum abnāʾakum dhālikum qawlukum bi-afwāhikum wa-llāhu yaqūlu l-ḥaqqa wa-huwa yahdī s-sabīl.

“The God has not placed two hearts within any man. Nor has He made your wives, toward whom you pronounce zihar, your mothers; nor has He made those you call your sons your actual sons. That is your saying with your mouths. The God speaks the truth, and He guides to the way.”

Al-Ahzab 33:4

The verse clearly distinguishes a human designation from a reality not changed by saying it.

Two family matters are involved.

First is zihar, a pronouncement comparing one’s wife to one’s mother.

Second is calling someone a son who is not one’s biological son.

The Qur’an states that such words do not themselves establish actual motherhood or biological sonship.

The clause about saying with mouths shows that uttering a designation may fail to create the reality claimed.

This does not make all human speech ineffective. Words can establish commitments, covenants, testimony, or declared decisions with actual social and legal effects.

A statement’s legal effect, however, differs from changing biological reality or descent.

Someone may care for and raise a child with love and responsibility without thereby changing the child’s parentage.

The verse distinguishes actual descent from a spoken designation.

Its closing declaration that The God speaks the truth contrasts divine speech with the human claim uttered through mouths.

أَفْوَاه does not itself mean falsehood. The invalidity of the claimed relationships comes from the verse’s content.

Al-Tawbah 9:30: an uttered claim

Al-Tawbah 9:30 reports claims of divine sonship and describes them as sayings made with mouths.

Its linguistic imagery resembles Al-Ahzab 33:4.

In both, people utter a relationship that the Qur’an distinguishes from reality.

Emphasis on mouths does not imply that the speakers necessarily disbelieved their own claims.

A person may sincerely believe something that is untrue.

The issue is the claim’s validity, not whether every speaker knowingly lied.

Attributing a statement to a historical or religious designation also does not authorize assigning identical beliefs and awareness to every person bearing that designation in every age.

Asserting a claim, believing it, and its being true must remain distinct.

6. Trying to extinguish divine light with mouths

يُرِيدُونَ أَنْ يُطْفِئُوا نُورَ اللَّهِ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَيَأْبَى اللَّهُ إِلَّا أَنْ يُتِمَّ نُورَهُ وَلَوْ كَرِهَ الْكَافِرُونَ

Yurīdūna an yuṭfiʾū nūra llāhi bi-afwāhihim wa-yaʾbā llāhu illā an yutimma nūrahu wa-law kariha l-kāfirūn.

“They wish to extinguish the light of The God with their mouths, but The God refuses anything except to complete His light, even though the disbelievers dislike it.”

Al-Tawbah 9:32

The mouths form part of a figurative image.

يُطْفِئُوا concerns extinguishing.

The light of The God is what the opponents seek to extinguish.

بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ identifies the means of their attempt.

Ordinarily, a person can blow out a small flame with the mouth.

The Qur’an applies this image to those seeking to eliminate divine light.

In the surah’s context, the attempt relates to verbal opposition, false claims, and resistance to the divine message.

The mouth is an instrument of expression and opposition; divine light is not a material flame extinguished by breath.

The image recurs in Al-Saff 61:8. There the construction لِيُطْفِئُوا appears in place of the infinitive construction with أَنْ in Al-Tawbah 9:32, followed by the completion of divine light.

This grammatical difference does not change the underlying image.

Both describe opponents trying to extinguish divine light through what emerges from their mouths, while the Qur’an announces its completion.

This does not turn every criticism, question, or intellectual disagreement about religion into an attempt to extinguish divine light.

The verse concerns opposition to truth in its context. Inquiry seeking understanding differs from inventing false statements to conceal truth.

F W H itself does not mean hostility to truth. The same mouth that can speak falsely can also speak truthfully.

7. Distinctions from related roots

F W H and L S N: mouth and tongue

فَم names the mouth.

Physically, لِسَان names the tongue, the organ within the mouth involved in eating, tasting, and speaking.

The Qur’an also uses لِسَان more broadly, including for a people’s language or manner of expression.

Both terms occur together in Al-Nur 24:15.

أَلْسِنَتِكُمْ appears in receiving and transmitting speech, while أَفْوَاهِكُمْ accompanies saying what the speakers do not know.

This pairing does not establish a universal rule that the tongue always conveys truth and the mouth always conveys lies.

Both can participate in human speech.

Lexically, one names the tongue and the other the mouth.

F W H and Sh F H: mouth and lip

شَفَة denotes a lip; its plural is شِفَاه.

Lips form part of the mouth’s structure, but are not identical with the whole mouth.

Al-Balad 90:9 mentions the human tongue and two lips.

In Al-Raʿd 13:14, فَاهُ identifies the mouth that the water is supposed to reach.

Lip and mouth are thus closely connected in some images without being fully synonymous.

The modern Persian and Arabic term for “oral” derives from Sh F H and refers to spoken rather than written communication. Similarity of meaning does not merge it with F W H.

For further discussion, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root Sh F H.

F W H and Q W L: mouths and saying

قَوْل concerns speaking, stating a position, and expressing meaning.

فَم names the organ through which human speech emerges.

In a construction joining يَقُولُونَ and بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ, the verb expresses saying, while the latter form foregrounds its means or outward manifestation.

This distinction matters.

A statement can be true or false; قَوْل names the statement, and فَم the mouth.

Divine speech in the Qur’an also differs from human speech. Reporting divine words or commands does not imply that The God has a physical mouth like a human being.

References to human mouths do not authorize transferring human bodily features to The God.

See Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root Q W L for further discussion.

F W H and K L M: mouths and a word

كَلِمَة concerns a meaningful word or statement.

A mouth may be the means through which a word emerges, but the word is not identical with the bodily organ producing it.

Al-Kahf 18:5 places both together: a statement coming from mouths.

كَلِمَة is what is expressed; أَفْوَاه identifies its point of emergence in the human image.

For the semantic range of “word,” see the Qur’anic root K L M study.

F W H and Q L B: mouths and heart

Qur’anic uses of قَلْب concern inner condition, perception, inclination, faith, and decision.

أَفْوَاه denotes mouths and, in speech contexts, relates to outward expression.

They are contrasted in Al Imran 3:167, Al-Ma’idah 5:41, and Al-Tawbah 9:8.

This contrast does not mean that every outward statement is false and truth always resides in inward silence.

People can say exactly what they believe.

The issue is agreement or disagreement between speech and a person’s actual condition.

See Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root Q L B for a fuller examination.

F W H and K T M: mouths and concealment

Concealment concerns keeping hidden something a person knows or can disclose.

أَفْوَاه refers to mouths.

People may know a truth but leave it unspoken, or conceal it through other words.

Not every silence is deliberate concealment, and not every statement covers up truth.

In Al Imran 3:167, the reference to mouths accompanies divine knowledge of what people conceal.

Outward speech and concealment can therefore coexist in a person’s conduct while remaining distinct concepts.

For further discussion, see the Qur’anic root K T M study.

8. Translation recommendations for Forqan

The natural equivalent of فَم is “mouth.”

فُوه, فَا, and فِي likewise mean “mouth” in bodily uses, with attention to their differing forms and grammatical cases.

أَفْوَاه is directly and clearly rendered “mouths.”

In some Persian constructions, “with one’s own mouth” or “utter” may flow more naturally, but the mouth image should not be removed from every verse without reason.

فَاهَ بِالْكَلَامِ can be rendered “uttered words”; تَفَوَّهَ can mean “spoke” or “uttered something.”

For مُفَوَّه concerning expressive ability, “eloquent” or “capable in speech” is natural.

For the physical description أَفْوَه, “large-mouthed” is appropriate.

فُوهَة can be translated “opening” or “aperture.”

Apart from أَفْوَاه and فَاه, these forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Recommendations for Qur’anic occurrences

Al-Raʿd 13:14 — فَاهُ

“His mouth.”

The actual human mouth and water reaching it are in view.

Al Imran 3:118 — أَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“Their mouths.”

Their speech reveals hostility, but the word itself does not mean hostility.

Al Imran 3:167 — بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“With their mouths.”

This preserves the contrast between outward speech and what is in the hearts.

Al-Ma’idah 5:41 — بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“With their mouths.”

Here verbal profession accompanies the absence of faith in the heart.

Al-Tawbah 9:8 — بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“With their mouths,” or, in a more idiomatic sentence, “with their words.”

Outward appeasement and inward refusal are involved.

Al-Tawbah 9:30 — بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“With their mouths.”

The emphasis is on expressing a claim the Qur’an rejects.

Al-Tawbah 9:32 — بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“With their mouths.”

The figurative image concerns trying to extinguish divine light through speech and opposition.

Ibrahim 14:9 — أَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“Their mouths.”

The mouth forms part of a hand gesture and the opponents’ reaction to the Messengers.

Al-Kahf 18:5 — أَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“Their mouths.”

A statement emerges that the Qur’an identifies as unsupported and false.

Al-Nur 24:15 — بِأَفْوَاهِكُمْ

“With your mouths.”

Speech without knowledge and underestimating its consequences are involved.

Al-Ahzab 33:4 — بِأَفْوَاهِكُمْ

“With your mouths.”

Human speech does not by itself alter actual descent.

Ya Sin 36:65 — أَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“Their mouths.”

Mouths are sealed during judgment while other bodily members testify.

Al-Saff 61:8 — بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“With their mouths.”

The image of trying to extinguish divine light through speech appears here too.

“Mouth” should remain the primary equivalent throughout. Where context gives it a figurative connection to speaking, the explanation can clarify that sense.

Rendering every occurrence as “tongue” may sound natural in some sentences, but erases the distinction between F W H and L S N.

Nor should “mouth” itself become “lying,” “hypocrisy,” “unsupported assertion,” or “hostile speech”: these characteristics come from other parts of the verse.

Summary

F W H concerns the mouth, an organ involved in eating, drinking, and speaking.

Early Arabic records فَوْه, فَم, فَا, and فِي for this organ; its plural is أَفْوَاه. Differences among the singular forms reflect the family’s recognized morphological and phonetic variations.

The root occurs thirteen times in the Qur’an: one فَاهُ and twelve forms of أَفْوَاه.

In Al-Raʿd 13:14, the mouth has its bodily sense as the destination of water for drinking.

In Ibrahim 14:9, it forms part of a bodily response.

In Ya Sin 36:65, sealed mouths stand against the testimony of hands and feet.

Most other occurrences connect mouths with human speech.

The Qur’an sometimes contrasts outward statements with the heart’s condition, sometimes addresses unsupported and false claims, and sometimes shows that verbal naming does not create an absent reality.

Al-Tawbah 9:32 and Al-Saff 61:8 also place mouths in the image of trying to extinguish divine light.

None of these uses changes the word’s basic meaning.

A mouth is not itself truthful or deceitful. The person using it can speak truly or falsely, reveal or conceal truth, and make a supported claim or speak without knowledge.

Thus F W H relates semantically to saying, tongue, speech, heart, and concealment without any of these replacing its lexical meaning.

Final root definition for Forqan:

F W H: the mouth, receiving food and drink and participating in the production and emergence of speech. Broader Arabic uses connect the family with openings of objects and uttering words.

فَم is the common noun form, أَفْوَاه its plural, and فَاهُ an accusative form with an attached pronoun.

Qur’anic uses concern either the physical mouth or the mouth as an instrument of outward speech. Truth or falsity, agreement with the heart, a claim’s validity, and ethical consequences emerge from the verbs, the subject of speech, and each verse’s context rather than the root alone.
