Yielding, accepting and complying with another’s wish or judgment; obedience and acknowledgment of a right

The Arabic root DH ʿ N concerns yielding, acceptance and complying with another’s wish or judgment. Adhʿana describes someone who ceases resisting a request, command or right and accepts it or acts accordingly.

This meaning occurs in human and social relations and in descriptions of animals responsive to their owner’s guidance. For example, nāqatun midhʿānun is a she-camel easily led, without resisting its rein or guidance.

Acceptance and compliance do not necessarily mean inward belief in a command’s correctness or lasting loyalty to it. Someone might accept a judgment because they consider it right, or solely because its outcome benefits them.

This distinction is especially important in the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

In Al-Nūr 24:48, a group turns away when summoned for judgment; Al-Nūr 24:49 says that if the right is theirs, they come to the Messenger compliantly. Together, the verses depict acceptance dependent upon personal advantage.

Qur’anic mudhʿinīna therefore shows that compliance can appear in conduct without itself establishing a person’s commitment to right in every circumstance.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as mudhʿinīna in Al-Nūr 24:49. It is the sound masculine plural of mudhʿin, the active participle of the Form IV verb adhʿana.

1. Central meaning and usage in early Arabic

From dhaʿana to idhʿān

The central meaning can be described as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الْإِصْحَابِ وَالِانْقِيَادِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-iṣḥābi wa-al-inqiyād

That is: the root has one central meaning concerning compliance and yielding.

Adhʿana al-rajulu describes a man becoming compliant and yielding.

Dhaʿana and dhaʿina are also recorded, but the more familiar form for the meaning discussed is adhʿana yudhʿinu idhʿānan.

Arabic expressions include:

Adhʿana lahu: he yielded to the person’s wish or command.

Adhʿana li-al-ḥukmi: he accepted and submitted to the judgment.

Adhʿana bi-al-ḥaqqi: he acknowledged and accepted the right or truth.

Rajulun mudhʿinun: a man who has yielded or is compliant.

Idhʿān is the verb’s verbal noun, denoting acceptance and compliance.

These expressions relate a person to what they accept: a right, judgment, request or command.

Prepositions therefore matter. Adhʿana lahu emphasizes yielding or compliance toward another, while adhʿana bi-ḥaqqihi concerns acknowledging that person’s right.

This should not become a mechanical rule assigning each preposition an independent, immutable kind of compliance.

Idhʿān: acceptance with compliance

Adhʿana is explained by inqāda wa-salisa: he yielded and became tractable and compliant.

In this familiar use, idhʿān involves more than knowing a truth. A person can know something and still resist it. Idhʿān concerns some acceptance or compliance in their conduct or position.

Mudhʿinīna is also explained by muqirrīna khāḍiʿīna: acknowledging and yielding.

Another explanation is muṭīʿīna ghayra mustakrahīna: obedient without being compelled.

These explanations show that in some uses, including this verse’s context, idhʿān can be acceptance without direct coercion. Absence of coercion should not nevertheless become an invariant condition of every use.

The absence of external compulsion alone does not establish deep conviction or love of right. Someone can freely accept a judgment out of self-interest.

Acceptance itself must therefore be distinguished from its motive.

Is speed part of idhʿān’s meaning?

Some early explanations render mudhʿinīna as musriʿīna: hastening.

Some lexical explanations combine speed with obedience. For example, adhʿana lī bi-ḥaqqī describes someone acknowledging another’s right and responding promptly to their request.

Idhʿān can thus involve readiness and prompt acceptance.

This does not mean adhʿana must denote running quickly or rapid physical movement in every Arabic sentence.

Someone may accept a judgment and act to carry it out without their physical speed being the subject.

Accordingly, yielding and compliance provide the clearer semantic center, while promptness can accompany them in some uses or contextual explanations.

2. A witness attributed to pre-Qur’anic poetry: a tractable she-camel

An important witness occurs in a poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays:

وَخَرْقٍ بَعِيدٍ قَدْ قَطَعْتُ نِيَاطَهُ
عَلَى ذَاتِ لَوْثٍ سَهْوَةِ الْمَشْيِ مِذْعَانِ

wa-kharqin baʿīdin qad qaṭaʿtu niyāṭahu / ʿalā dhāti lawthin sahwati al-mashyi midhʿān

Translation: How many a distant, spacious desert I have crossed, riding a strong she-camel, easy and smooth in its gait and responsive to guidance.

The poet describes crossing a distant desert and characterizes his mount.

Kharq denotes a broad, difficult desert or open terrain.

Dhāti lawthin here concerns the she-camel’s bodily strength and sturdiness.

Sahwati al-mashyi describes an animal whose movement is smooth and easy.

Midhʿān describes its tractability and responsiveness to guidance.

Both lawth and lawt appear in transmissions of this verse. Sahwati al-mashyi in the quoted reading describes an easy, smooth gait. Its explanation should not be confused with a different wording of the poem. Midhʿān describes the tractable mount in this witness.

On its attribution to Imruʾ al-Qays, this witness illustrates the family’s pre-Qur’anic use for an animal responsive to its owner’s guidance. Dating the witness rests upon that attribution.

Midhʿān does not mean weak, worthless or lacking physical strength. The poet instead describes a strong she-camel that responds to its rider’s guidance despite its power.

Thus tractability and compliance should not be equated with incapacity.

This animal use also does not justify reducing human compliance to a restrained animal’s condition. Human relations involve awareness, motives, rights and responsibility.

Nāqatun midhʿānun clearly illustrates tractable behavior. When applying this concept to human relations, what is accepted and under what conditions must be examined separately.

3. Mudhʿinīna in Al-Nūr 24:49: accepting judgment when the right favors oneself

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Nūr 24:49:

وَإِنْ يَكُنْ لَهُمُ الْحَقُّ يَأْتُوا إِلَيْهِ مُذْعِنِينَ

wa-in yakun lahumu al-ḥaqqu yaʾtū ilayhi mudhʿinīn

Translation: If the right is theirs, they come to him submissively.

Mudhʿinīna is a circumstantial qualifier of the subject of yaʾtū, describing their condition as they come.

The coming itself is expressed by yaʾtū; mudhʿinīna describes their manner: they come to him accepting and compliant.

This matters because rendering mudhʿinīna solely as “hastily” obscures acceptance and yielding to judgment.

Connection with the two preceding verses

Al-Nūr 24:47 states:

وَيَقُولُونَ آمَنَّا بِاللَّهِ وَبِالرَّسُولِ وَأَطَعْنَا ثُمَّ يَتَوَلَّىٰ فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ وَمَا أُولَٰئِكَ بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-yaqūlūna āmannā bi-allāhi wa-bi-al-rasūli wa-aṭaʿnā thumma yatawallā farīqun minhum min baʿdi dhālika wa-mā ulāʾika bi-al-muʾminīn

Translation: They say, “We believe in The God and the Messenger, and we obey.” Then a group of them turns away after that. Those are not believers.

The verse first mentions a claim of faith and obedience, then the turning away of some who make that claim.

Farīqun minhum, “a group of them,” matters. It attributes the criticized conduct to a group, rather than indiscriminately treating everyone professing faith alike.

Al-Nūr 24:48 makes the situation clearer:

وَإِذَا دُعُوا إِلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَهُمْ إِذَا فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ مُعْرِضُونَ

wa-idhā duʿū ilā allāhi wa-rasūlihi li-yaḥkuma baynahum idhā farīqun minhum muʿriḍūn

Translation: When they are summoned to The God and His Messenger for him to judge between them, suddenly a group of them turns away.

The subject is dispute and judgment.

A group previously claiming faith and obedience turns away when summoned for judgment.

The following verse, Al-Nūr 24:49, presents the other condition: if the right is theirs, they appear compliantly.

The main contrast is therefore:

They turn away when judgment might oppose their wishes, but come compliantly when the right favors them.

This shows that outward acceptance of an authority’s judgment does not always arise from commitment to the criterion of right.

Someone may accept judgment because they are confident it will benefit them, yet refuse the same authority if they expect a ruling against their interests.

This distinction clarifies the verse’s central message.

Lahumu al-ḥaqqu: the right is theirs

Lahumu al-ḥaqqu in Al-Nūr 24:49 must be understood within the context of judgment.

It concerns a situation in which the right or resulting judgment favors them.

It does not necessarily mean that they are right in every dispute. It states the condition in which the right under judgment is on their side.

Yaʾtū ilayhi mudhʿinīna then describes their conduct in that condition.

Their idhʿān is therefore real as behavior: they come and show acceptance. The context reveals that this acceptance is conditional upon their advantage.

Mudhʿin should not accordingly be defined as necessarily a true believer, someone accepting right in every circumstance or a person of pure intention.

Those attributes go beyond the root and must be assessed through conduct across different situations.

Does idhʿān indicate coercion?

Some Persian translations render idhʿān by an expression meaning submission.

This is defensible, but a misunderstanding must be avoided: those showing compliance in this verse have not necessarily been brought to judgment under direct pressure or compulsion.

They instead come to the Messenger when they see the outcome as favorable.

Early lexical explanations likewise give muṭīʿīna ghayra mustakrahīna: obedient without being compelled.

“Yielding,” “compliant” or “accepting the judgment” can therefore clarify the intended aspect.

Voluntary conduct should not, however, be taken to establish sincerity or lasting commitment to right.

They can freely appear for judgment while being motivated by the favorable outcome.

Three matters must therefore remain distinct: whether attendance is compelled or voluntary, acceptance of a judgment and the ethical motive for accepting it.

4. Outward compliance and commitment to right distinguished

Al-Nūr 24:50 states:

أَفِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ أَمِ ارْتَابُوا أَمْ يَخَافُونَ أَنْ يَحِيفَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَرَسُولُهُ بَلْ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الظَّالِمُونَ

a-fī qulūbihim maraḍun ami irtābū am yakhāfūna an yaḥīfa allāhu ʿalayhim wa-rasūluhu bal ulāʾika humu al-ẓālimūn

Translation: Is there sickness in their hearts, or have they doubted, or do they fear that The God and His Messenger will wrong them? Rather, those are the wrongdoers.

The verse asks several questions about why the group turns away.

Sickness of heart, doubt and injustice are separate concepts; they should not all be reduced to compliance or its absence.

Al-Nūr 24:49 depicts conduct conditional upon advantage. Al-Nūr 24:50 then places it alongside questions about inward condition and fear of unjust judgment, finally identifying them as wrongdoers.

This connection shows that merely appearing before an authority is not the Qur’an’s criterion: the person’s relationship with right and justice matters.

Al-Nūr 24:51 describes believers’ conduct in the corresponding situation:

إِنَّمَا كَانَ قَوْلَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ إِذَا دُعُوا إِلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَهُمْ أَنْ يَقُولُوا سَمِعْنَا وَأَطَعْنَا وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

innamā kāna qawla al-muʾminīna idhā duʿū ilā allāhi wa-rasūlihi li-yaḥkuma baynahum an yaqūlū samiʿnā wa-aṭaʿnā wa-ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn

Translation: The believers’ response, when summoned to The God and His Messenger for him to judge between them, is only to say, “We hear and obey.” Those are the successful.

In Al-Nūr 24:49, acceptance of judgment was conditional upon personal advantage. Al-Nūr 24:51 describes believers’ response to the summons without making acceptance dependent upon guaranteed personal victory.

The contrast foregrounds acceptance conditional upon advantage and commitment to rightful judgment.

Al-Nūr 24:51 does not establish that anyone claiming to speak for religion possesses unlimited authority to judge others. It concerns the Messenger’s judgment within his mission. Extending that authority to other people or institutions requires independent grounds.

Al-Nūr 24:53 also mentions people strongly swearing their readiness to obey. The Qur’anic response emphasizes recognized obedience and The God’s knowledge of their conduct.

The sequence thus continually distinguishes speech, claims of obedience and actual behavior.

From this perspective, idhʿān in Al-Nūr 24:49 is outward acceptance whose motive and stability differ from the praised faith and obedience in Al-Nūr 24:51.

5. Comparison with related roots

DH ʿ N (adhʿana) and Ṭ W ʿ (ṭāʿa): yielding and obeying

Ṭ W ʿ has a broad Arabic field including ability, willing action, obedience and compliance with another’s wish.

Aṭāʿa is used in the Qur’an for obeying The God, the Messenger and other people.

Adhʿana likewise concerns compliance, with yielding and accepting another’s judgment or wish prominent in its familiar uses.

Al-Nūr 24:47 mentions a group’s claim of obedience; Al-Nūr 24:49 describes acceptance when the outcome benefits them as mudhʿinīna; and Al-Nūr 24:51 describes believers’ obedience when summoned for judgment.

Obedience and idhʿān are thus close, but the words alone guarantee neither sincerity nor the act’s justice or rightness.

For a broader discussion of obedience, following and individual responsibility, see Forqan’s study of Ṭ W ʿ.

Study of Ṭ W ʿ

DH ʿ N (adhʿana) and DH L L (dhalla): compliance and humiliation

Study of DH L L

DH L L concerns becoming tractable, being easy or accessible, and in some uses subordination and loss of honor.

Dhalūl can describe a tractable animal, just as midhʿān describes an obedient she-camel.

Sharing an animal description does not make the families entirely synonymous.

Idhʿān foregrounds compliance and yielding to a wish or judgment, whereas dhillah in some uses concerns lowliness and humiliation.

Someone can therefore accept a judgment without becoming humiliated or worthless.

In Al-Nūr 24:49, accepting a favorable judgment likewise does not establish humiliation. The subject is acceptance conditional upon advantage, rather than the person’s social rank or dignity.

DH ʿ N (adhʿana) and S L M (aslama): acceptance and submission

Study of S L M

S L M has various Qur’anic uses relating to soundness, peace and submission.

Some uses of aslama concern submitting to The God.

Adhʿana can also concern submitting to a judgment or wish.

The concepts’ proximity does not mean everyone complying with a judgment realizes the divine submission intended throughout the verses on faith and islām.

In Al-Nūr 24:49, people appearing compliantly are precisely those whose conduct the context criticizes.

Accepting one judgment in one situation is not the same as comprehensive commitment to right.

DH ʿ N (dhaʿana) and Ẓ ʿ N (ẓaʿana): independent roots

DH ʿ N, with dhāl, and Ẓ ʿ N, with ẓāʾ, are different roots.

Ẓaʿana concerns departing and moving from one place to another. Early uses of ẓaʿīnah include a woman riding in a howdah or a departing caravan.

Adhʿana, with dhāl, concerns yielding and compliance.

Similar shapes or pronunciation in some dialects do not establish a shared derivation. Qur’anic mudhʿinīna should not be understood as departing travelers.

6. Translation proposal for Forqan

The root’s sole Qur’anic form is mudhʿinīna in Al-Nūr 24:49.

“Compliantly,” “yielding” and “in acceptance” are defensible equivalents.

Forqan’s current Persian translation means:

“If the right is theirs, they come to him submissively.”

This conveys the principal meaning and fits the context. The word’s explanation should nevertheless clarify that submission does not necessarily mean inward and permanent acceptance of right or being compelled.

To emphasize behavioral compliance, the expression could also be rendered:

“If the right is theirs, they come to him compliantly.”

This assigns “they come” to yaʾtū and “compliantly” to mudhʿinīna.

“They come to him hastily” is intelligible in light of some early explanations concerning promptness. As the sole equivalent, however, it obscures yielding and accepting judgment.

“They acknowledge the right” is also incomplete here. The sentence concerns coming to the Messenger in compliance, rather than merely making a verbal acknowledgment.

The principal translation should therefore preserve acceptance and compliance. The conduct’s self-interested motive belongs in contextual explanation, rather than being introduced without grounds into the word itself.

7. Conclusion

DH ʿ N concerns yielding, acceptance and compliance with another’s wish or judgment.

Early Arabic uses adhʿana for someone accepting another’s command or right. Midhʿān describes a she-camel easily led without resisting its rein or guidance.

Some lexical uses combine idhʿān with prompt response and obedience, but physical speed is not an invariant part of the root’s meaning.

The root occurs only as mudhʿinīna in Al-Nūr 24:49.

Those who turn away when summoned for judgment appear accepting and compliant when the right favors them.

Though compliance can be real as conduct, it alone does not reveal intention, sincerity or enduring commitment to right.

Final definition: In its familiar usage, DH ʿ N concerns yielding, acceptance and practical compliance with another’s wish, right or judgment. A mudhʿin shows acceptance and compliance toward its object. The reason, sincerity and ethical value of that acceptance are not established by the word alone.

Al-Nūr 24:49 centers on the distinction between acceptance and lasting commitment to right. The criticized group accepts judgment when it benefits them, whereas the believing conduct described in Al-Nūr 24:51 does not make acceptance of right conditional upon personal advantage.
