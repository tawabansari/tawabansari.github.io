Firmness, settlement, digging, and the name of the People of al-Rass

Arabic R S S denotes becoming fixed, remaining firm, and becoming established. Recorded uses include securing buildings, embedding something in the ground, digging wells, burying, and an enduring trace.

Rass also names wells, mines, and certain places. In other uses, رَسِيس (rasīs) concerns a slight remaining trace, the first signs of a condition, or a feeling established within someone.

These uses should not be collapsed into one meaning without examination. Firmness, digging a well, burying a person, and naming a place may be connected but are different things.

The Qur’an uses this root only twice, both in أَصْحَابُ الرَّسِّ (aṣḥābu al-rassi) or its other case form. It mentions the People of al-Rass alongside peoples such as Noah’s, ʿĀd, and Thamūd, who denied the messengers and met a severe outcome.

The Qur’an does not identify their precise identity, location, prophet’s name, or manner of destruction. Historical and exegetical reports differ and should not replace the Qur’an’s explicit information.

Qur’anic form: الرَّسِّ (al-rassi) in أَصْحَابَ الرَّسِّ in Al-Furqān 25:38 and أَصْحَابُ الرَّسِّ in Qāf 50:12.

Arabic records رَسَّ (rassa), رَسّ (rass), رَسِيس (rasīs), رِسَاس (risās), رَسَّسَ (rassasa), أَرَسَّ (arassa), أَرْسَسَ (arsasa), مَرْسُوس (marsūs), and تَرَاسَّ (tarāssa). رَسْرَسَ (rasrasa) and رَسْرَسَة (rasrasah) are related repetitive quadriliteral forms, rather than simply derivatives of triliteral R S S. These forms do not occur as such in the Qur’an.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Firmness and becoming established

Lexical explanation identifies firmness as the root’s semantic core:

الرَّاءُ وَالسِّينُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى ثَبَاتٍ

al-rāʾu wa-al-sīnu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā thabātin

Meaning: “Rāʾ and sīn have one core denoting firmness and stability.”

The same explanation continues:

رَسَّ الشَّيْءُ: ثَبَتَ

rassa al-shayʾu: thabata

“Something underwent rass: it became fixed and firm.”

Rass here concerns something entering a stable condition.

Rasīs also describes something that remains firmly in place.

This firmness can concern an object, an enduring trace, or a human condition that has arisen and not disappeared.

Stability should not mean everlasting endurance or absolute immutability in every use of the family.

Something may become fixed in one situation, yet change or disappear under other conditions.

The word also does not specify how firmness is produced. An object may be embedded in earth, a building’s components secured together, or an impression retained in memory or feeling.

Al-rass and securing a building

Lexical explanation of the building use states:

الرَّسُّ: إِحْكَامُ الْبِنَاءِ مِثْلُ الرَّصِّ، وَبُنْيَانٌ مَرْسُوسٌ

al-rassu: iḥkāmu al-bināʾi mithlu al-raṣṣi wa-bunyānun marsūsun

“Rass is making a building firm, like raṣṣ; a marsūs building is one made secure.”

Rass here concerns securing and firmly positioning a building’s components.

This clearly relates to stability: components are secure when placed so that they do not easily separate.

R S S should nevertheless not always be confined to construction or arranging stones.

Marsūs describes a firm building here, while other members of the family occur in different fields.

The use’s presence in an early dictionary does not establish construction as the root’s earliest historical origin.

Rasrasah: a camel bracing its knees

رَسْرَسَ الْبَعِيرُ (rasrasa al-baʿīru) describes a camel firmly planting its knees on the ground to rise.

One lexical classification also treats this use within the field of stability.

The camel braces its knees on the ground in preparation for standing.

Firmness and stabilization can thus precede movement.

Not every use of R S S therefore means complete immobility.

Sometimes something first secures its position to perform a movement.

Rasrasah is morphologically a repetitive quadriliteral. Early sources record its semantic proximity to R S S, but repetition in its form alone establishes no particular intensity or number of movements.

Digging and inserting something

Lexical explanation also gives digging for rass:

الرَّسُّ: الْحَفْرُ

al-rassu: al-ḥafru

“Rass means digging.”

It also states:

وَكُلُّ شَيْءٍ أَدْخَلْتَهُ فَقَدْ رَسَسْتَهُ

wa-kullu shayʾin adkhaltahu fa-qad rasastahu

“Whatever you insert, you have performed rass upon it.”

This goes beyond an object’s stability to inserting or placing something within something else.

Digging a well, for example, involves excavating the ground to create a cavity.

Burying involves placing something in the ground and covering it.

Settlement in the ground can be connected conceptually with becoming fixed, but historical evidence is needed before deriving every digging and burying use from an original meaning of stability.

Lexical sources establish these uses within one family.

Russa al-mayyitu: burial

Lexical burial usage includes:

رُسَّ الْمَيِّتُ: قُبِرَ

russa al-mayyitu: qubira

“The dead person underwent rass: he was buried.”

رُسَّ (russa) is passive and concerns being buried.

Burial involves placing the body in the ground and covering it.

Rass nevertheless does not mean killing in every use.

Killing, dying, and burying differ.

Someone may die and then be buried. Burial alone does not identify the cause of death.

This matters for the People of al-Rass, since some exegetical reports say they buried their prophet in a well.

Even with such a report, rass’s lexical meaning alone does not establish that they certainly killed their prophet.

That requires independent historical evidence.

2. Wells, mines, and place names

Al-rass: a well or pit

Lexical usage records al-rass for a well or pit dug in the ground.

رَسَسْتُ رَسًّا (rasastu rassan) also means digging a well.

One lexical explanation defines al-rass as a well whose walls are lined and reinforced with stones.

Others record it for an old well or one without stone-lined walls.

A construction detail such as stone lining or its absence should therefore not become a necessary element of the root’s meaning without further examination.

The broader, clearer connection is with a well, cavity, or excavation in the ground.

رِسَاس (risās) is also recorded for wells or excavations.

Al-rass: a mine or excavation

Al-rass is also recorded for an old well or mine.

A poetic passage attributed to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī states:

تَنَابِلَةٌ يَحْفِرُونَ الرِّسَاسَا

tanābilatun yaḥfirūna al-risāsā

“Short-statured men digging wells or mine excavations.” Tanābilah here concerns short stature; translating it as lazy or unambitious from familiar Persian usage is incorrect.

Risās occurs with يَحْفِرُونَ (yaḥfirūna), they dig.

It therefore concerns something excavated in this context.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, and this verse’s date is not independently clear. It should not be called definitely pre-Qur’anic without qualification.

Nor does it establish that al-rass in the Qur’an certainly means a mine. Mining is one recorded sense, but identifying the Qur’anic referent requires context and independent evidence.

Al-Rass and al-Rusays as place names

Al-Rass and al-Rusays were known place names in early Arabic.

Lexical and geographical reports identify them as valleys or watering places in Arab lands.

Al-rass can thus be a place name as well as a common noun meaning well.

Qur’anic aṣḥābu al-rass is compatible with people identified through a place called al-Rass.

That possibility does not identify their home with a particular valley named in pre-Islamic poetry or geographical writings.

Shared place names alone do not establish historical identity.

3. Pre-Islamic poetic witnesses and the name al-Rass

Al-Rass in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā includes:

لِمَنْ طَلَلٌ كَالْوَحْيِ عَافٍ مَنَازِلُهُ
عَفَا الرَّسُّ مِنْهُ فَالرَّسِيسُ فَعَاقِلُهُ

li-man ṭalalun ka-al-waḥyi ʿāfin manāziluhu / ʿafā al-rassu minhu fa-al-rasīsu fa-ʿāqiluhu

“Whose are these fading ruins, their dwellings like faint writing? Their traces at al-Rass, al-Rasīs, and ʿĀqil have disappeared.”

Al-Rass, al-Rasīs, and ʿĀqil are place names here.

The poet describes former dwelling places whose traces have weathered and faded with time.

Al-Rass thus appears as a place name in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Transmissions differ in pronouns and some vocalizations, including minhu and minhā, and al-Rasīs or al-Rusays. The names al-Rass and al-Rasīs remain present in the transmissions discussed.

Another verse attributed to Zuhayr states:

بَكَرْنَ بُكُورًا وَاسْتَحَرْنَ بِسُحْرَةٍ
فَهُنَّ وَوَادِي الرَّسِّ كَالْيَدِ فِي الْفَمِ

bakarna bukūran wa-istaḥarna bi-suḥratin / fa-hunna wa-wādī al-rassi ka-al-yadi fī al-fami

“They set out early and traveled at dawn; they and the valley of al-Rass were like hand and mouth.” The image is explained as reaching the valley without missing its route, as the hand does not miss the mouth; mere spatial proximity does not convey the whole image.

Wādī al-Rass explicitly names a valley.

Both witnesses matter for the word’s geographical use.

They do not place the Qur’an’s People of al-Rass definitely in that same valley.

Zuhayr’s poetry establishes an early place name but not the Qur’anic people’s history or identity.

Was the name al-Rass derived from a well?

Since rass can name a well or excavation, some places may have been named for their wells or watering sites.

That is only an etymological possibility.

Establishing a place’s naming history requires independent historical evidence.

Two places can share a name acquired at different times and under different conditions.

Not every place named al-Rass must therefore be a well, nor every historically named al-Rass well the site of the Qur’anic people.

4. Rasīs: a remaining trace and the onset of a condition

Rasīs: something remaining in place

Lexical explanation of rasīs states:

الرَّسِيسُ: الشَّيْءُ الثَّابِتُ اللَّازِمُ مَكَانَهُ

al-rasīsu: al-shayʾu al-thābitu al-lāzimu makānahu

“Rasīs is something fixed and remaining in its place.”

Related uses concern an impression or feeling that persists within someone.

رَسِيسُ الْهَوَى (rasīsu al-hawā), for example, concerns a trace or remnant of affection.

A verse by Dhū al-Rummah states:

إِذَا غَيَّرَ النَّأْيُ الْمُحِبِّينَ لَمْ أَجِدْ
رَسِيسَ الْهَوَى مِنْ ذِكْرِ مَيَّةَ يَبْرَحُ

idhā ghayyara al-naʾyu al-muḥibbīna lam ajid / rasīsa al-hawā min dhikri mayyata yabraḥu

“Though distance changes lovers, I do not find the lasting trace of love from remembering Mayyah departing from me.”

Rasīsu al-hawā concerns affection’s impression remaining within the poet.

Firmness can thus extend linguistically from material conditions to inward human states.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad poet; this is not a pre-Islamic witness.

The use does not make Qur’anic rass mean love or affection.

In rasīsu al-hawā, al-hawā identifies the kind of remaining impression.

Rassu al-ḥummā: fever’s first effects

رَسُّ الْحُمَّى (rassu al-ḥummā) and rasīsuhā are also used for fever’s first signs.

Someone may feel its initial effects before a pronounced or severe fever appears.

بَلَغَنِي رَسٌّ مِنْ خَبَرٍ (balaghanī rassun min khabarin) means a slight portion or first indication of news reached me.

These uses concern a slight trace and something beginning to appear.

One lexical explanation connects the first portion of news becoming established in hearing with the meaning of stability.

This does not make beginning the meaning of R S S in every use.

Rasīsu al-ḥummā concerns fever’s initial effect, whereas russa al-mayyitu concerns burial and spatial al-rass concerns a well or place name.

The uses differ and require their own examination.

Rassa al-ḥadītha fī nafsihi

رَسَّ الْحَدِيثَ فِي نَفْسِهِ (rassa al-ḥadītha fī nafsihi) describes someone repeating speech inwardly or committing it to memory.

Repetition may establish it in memory.

تَرَاسَّ الْقَوْمُ الْخَبَرَ (tarāssa al-qawmu al-khabara) is recorded for secretly conveying news or speaking quietly among people.

These uses are semantically connected with slight news, speech’s remaining impression, or establishing it in the mind.

They do not make the Qur’anic aṣḥābu al-rass mean people of secret speech or people who established news in their minds.

Neither Qur’anic verse supplies evidence for such a reading.

5. Aṣḥāba al-rass in Al-Furqān

The root’s first Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Furqān 25:38:

وَعَادًا وَثَمُودَ وَأَصْحَابَ الرَّسِّ وَقُرُونًا بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ كَثِيرًا

wa-ʿādan wa-thamūda wa-aṣḥāba al-rassi wa-qurūnan bayna dhālika kathīran

“And ʿĀd, Thamūd, the People of al-Rass, and many generations between them.”

This belongs to a passage recalling earlier peoples and the fate of those denying the messengers.

The People of al-Rass in context

Al-Furqān 25:37 states; an excerpt:

وَقَوْمَ نُوحٍ لَمَّا كَذَّبُوا الرُّسُلَ أَغْرَقْنَاهُمْ وَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ لِلنَّاسِ آيَةً

wa-qawma nūḥin lammā kadhdhabū al-rusula aghraqnāhum wa-jaʿalnāhum li-al-nāsi āyatan

“And Noah’s people, when they denied the messengers, We drowned them and made them a sign for people.”

Al-Furqān 25:38 then mentions ʿĀd, Thamūd, the People of al-Rass, and many other generations.

Al-Furqān 25:39 states:

وَكُلًّا ضَرَبْنَا لَهُ الْأَمْثَالَ وَكُلًّا تَبَّرْنَا تَتْبِيرًا

wa-kullan ḍarabnā lahu al-amthāla wa-kullan tabbarnā tatbīran

“To each We presented examples, and each We utterly destroyed.”

The People of al-Rass thus appear among groups whose denial and destruction are recalled.

The Qur’an gives no separate, detailed account of them here.

These verses identify neither their prophet, punishment, length of prophetic calling, exact location, nor manner of destruction.

They are clearly mentioned among earlier peoples, their fate serving a warning to deniers.

Aṣḥāba: identifying a group through an association

Aṣḥāba here is the accusative plural of ṣāḥib.

It names people associated or connected with a person, place, or other thing.

For example, aṣḥābu al-kahf occurs in Al-Kahf 18:9, aṣḥābu al-aykah in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:176, and aṣḥābu al-ukhdūd in Al-Burūj 85:4.

The second term can name the place or thing through which a group is identified.

Aṣḥāb does not always express legal ownership.

The People of the Cave are not necessarily the cave’s owners, but people with a recognized association with it.

Likewise, aṣḥābu al-rass does not necessarily mean owners of a well or legal possessors of land.

The construction can identify people associated with a place named al-Rass or a well.

The exact association cannot be determined from the construction alone.

Al-rassi: a place name or the common name of a well?

Al-rassi is genitive after aṣḥāba here.

Its form belongs to doubled R S S; the doubled sīn concerns the root’s structure.

That doubling does not itself express intensity, repetition, or extraordinary size.

The article al also makes al-rass definite.

Definiteness alone does not prove a proper city name or a common noun for a well known to the audience.

Both possibilities can be examined within Arabic usage.

Lexical usage records al-rass for both wells and place names.

People of the well is therefore a possible lexical explanation. Retaining al-Rass in the basic translation avoids presenting an identity not explicitly established by the Qur’an as certain.

Were the people of al-Rass Thamūd?

In the verse in Al-Furqān, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of al-Rass appear alongside one another.

This presents them as separate designations in the list of earlier peoples.

Some exegetical accounts identify the people of al-Rass with a group from Thamūd.

The verse itself does not establish that relationship.

Mentioning the two designations separately does not by itself exclude every possible historical relationship between them either.

What can be stated confidently is that the Qur’an names the people of al-Rass separately alongside Thamūd without explaining their precise historical relationship.

6. The people of al-Rass in Qāf

The second Qur’anic occurrence is in Qāf 50:12:

كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ وَأَصْحَابُ الرَّسِّ وَثَمُودُ

kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu nūḥin wa-aṣḥābu al-rassi wa-thamūdu

“Before them, the people of Noah, the people of al-Rass, and Thamūd denied.”

Qāf 50:13 and Qāf 50:14 continue:

وَعَادٌ وَفِرْعَوْنُ وَإِخْوَانُ لُوطٍ ۝ وَأَصْحَابُ الْأَيْكَةِ وَقَوْمُ تُبَّعٍ كُلٌّ كَذَّبَ الرُّسُلَ فَحَقَّ وَعِيدِ

wa-ʿādun wa-firʿawnu wa-ikhwānu lūṭin / wa-aṣḥābu al-aykati wa-qawmu tubbaʿin kullun kadhdhaba al-rusula fa-ḥaqqa waʿīdi

“And ʿĀd, Pharaoh, and the brethren of Lot, and the people of the thicket and the people of Tubbaʿ; all denied the messengers, so My warning came true.”

Emphasis on denial

The main subject of this passage is the denial of the messengers.

In Qāf 50:12, kadhdhabat expresses denial, with several peoples named as its subjects.

In Qāf 50:14, kullun kadhdhaba al-rusula places all the groups mentioned under this shared description.

The Qur’an therefore identifies the people of al-Rass, at least, as one of the groups that denied the messengers.

This does not establish that several prophets were necessarily sent to them simultaneously.

The Qur’an can describe the rejection of a single messenger as denying the messengers; Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:105 says of Noah’s people:

كَذَّبَتْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ الْمُرْسَلِينَ

kadhdhabat qawmu nūḥin al-mursalīna

“The people of Noah denied the messengers.”

The plural al-rusul alone therefore does not establish the exact number of a people’s prophets.

Fa-ḥaqqa waʿīdi: the warning comes true

The end of Qāf 50:14 reads:

فَحَقَّ وَعِيدِ

fa-ḥaqqa waʿīdi

“So My warning came true.”

This states that the denial of these groups was followed by the fulfillment of the divine warning.

The passage in Al-Furqān likewise speaks of the destruction of earlier peoples.

These passages illuminate one another on denial and the fate of earlier peoples.

Neither passage, however, specifies the punishment or manner of destruction of the people of al-Rass.

Fa-ḥaqqa waʿīdi alone therefore does not justify assigning them a particular punishment such as being swallowed by the earth, drowning, a thunderbolt, or destruction by fire.

Different grammatical positions in the two verses

In Al-Furqān 25:38, aṣḥāba al-rassi is accusative within a list connected to the preceding sentence construction.

In Qāf 50:12, aṣḥābu al-rassi is nominative, coordinated with qawmu Nūḥin.

This difference of case concerns aṣḥāb.

Al-rassi is genitive in both verses because it is the second term of the construct phrase with aṣḥāb.

The different sentence constructions therefore do not indicate different roots or meanings for al-Rass.

Both verses use the same designation, the people of al-Rass.

7. Who were the people of al-Rass?

Early exegetical accounts offer several identifications of the people of al-Rass.

They differ over the location, ethnic identity, and details of their story.

People of a well

A familiar account holds that al-Rass was the name of a well and that its people lived beside it.

One early commentary favors this explanation, relating it to rass as a well or excavation, including a well or grave.

This is linguistically defensible because rass is indeed recorded for a well.

That fact does not locate the well or identify its inhabitants precisely.

Burying a prophet in a well

Another account says that the people of al-Rass put or buried their prophet in a well, where he died.

This connects the name al-Rass with rassa in the sense of burying.

The lexical connection is understandable, but the Qur’an itself does not say that their prophet was buried in a well.

The account should therefore not be presented as the certain meaning of the designation the people of al-Rass.

The two Qur’anic verses likewise do not establish the prophet’s name, the duration of his call, how he was killed, the well’s construction material, or the punishment that followed.

Connections with Thamūd and al-Yamāmah

Some accounts identify the people of al-Rass as survivors of, or a group from, Thamūd.

Another locates them in a settlement named Falj in al-Yamāmah.

Early commentaries preserve these differing accounts alongside one another.

Neither Qur’anic verse names al-Yamāmah, Falj, or any other specific location.

The Qur’an does not explicitly say that the people of al-Rass descended from Thamūd either.

These accounts matter for the history of interpretation but do not establish a certain identification.

Connections with Yā Sīn or the people of the trench

Some exegetical accounts connect the people of al-Rass with the group discussed in Yā Sīn or with the people of the trench.

Yā Sīn 36:13, however, calls its group the people of the settlement, not the people of al-Rass.

Al-Burūj 85:4 likewise uses aṣḥābu al-ukhdūdi, not aṣḥābu al-rassi.

Since rass is associated with digging and a pit, a well and a trench have a recognizable lexical affinity.

Similarity between the meanings of two words does not establish the identity of two historical groups.

Detailed accounts of a tree’s name, the number of settlements, month names, a river’s location, or the manner of punishment should not be presented as Qur’anic information about the people of al-Rass either.

These details are absent from the Qur’an itself.

What can reasonably be concluded about their identity?

The Qur’anic occurrences and lexical evidence establish a few points.

First, rass can designate a well or a place in Arabic.

Second, the Qur’an presents the people of al-Rass as a historical group.

Third, it includes them among peoples that denied the messengers and experienced the fulfillment of the divine warning.

Their prophet’s name, exact location, ethnic descent, and manner of destruction remain unspecified.

The most suitable approach is therefore to retain the people of al-Rass in translation and explain the possible connection with a well or place name in the lexical discussion.

This respects Arabic usage while keeping disputed historical accounts distinct from what the Qur’an certainly states.

8. Comparison with related roots

R S S (rass) and R S KH (rusūkh)

R S KH likewise concerns becoming firm and securely established in Arabic.

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 includes:

وَالرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ

wa-al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi

“And those firmly grounded in knowledge.”

Rusūkh here concerns established knowledge and a person’s firmness in it.

Rass also concerns stability in a recognized sense.

They are nevertheless different roots.

R S S is doubled, with identical second and third radicals.

R S KH has khāʾ as its third radical.

Moreover, Qur’anic R S S occurs only in the designation the people of al-Rass, whereas R S KH describes firmness in knowledge.

The people of al-Rass should therefore not be translated as those firmly grounded in knowledge or people with deeply rooted beliefs.

Study of R S KH

R S S (rass) and R S W (rusūw)

R S W also concerns becoming fixed and settled.

Arabic rasā can describe something settling or becoming stable.

Its root structure differs from R S S.

Qur’anic derivatives of R S W also concern mountains and stability.

The shared concept of stability does not justify merging the roots or assigning one root’s derivatives to the other.

Al-rass in the Qur’an should especially not be interpreted as an anchorage, a firmly established mountain, or a place where a ship settles merely on the basis of semantic resemblance.

Those meanings require independent evidence.

R S S (rass) and R Ṣ Ṣ (raṣṣ)

In early lexical explanations, rass in building firmly is compared with raṣṣ.

Raṣṣ concerns fitting components closely and tightly together.

Al-Ṣaff 61:4 includes:

كَأَنَّهُمْ بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ

ka-annahum bunyānun marṣūṣun

“As though they were a closely joined, firm structure.”

This image resembles some uses of rass in the firmness of the components.

R Ṣ Ṣ is nevertheless a different root requiring separate study.

The building sense of rass also does not establish that the Qur’anic people of al-Rass were people who built exceptionally strong structures.

The verses themselves offer no such explanation.

R S S (rass) and R S L (irsāl)

R S L also resembles R S S in writing.

They are different roots.

Qur’anic R S L concerns sending, conveying a message, and messengers.

Qur’anic R S S occurs in the designation the people of al-Rass.

The resemblance between the letters of rass and rasūl establishes no derivational relationship.

It especially does not mean that the people of al-Rass means the companions of a messenger or people who were sent.

Study of R S L

R S S (rass) and Ḥ F R (ḥafr)

Arabic ḥafr concerns digging the earth and making a pit or cavity.

Digging a well is also recorded under R S S.

The two roots can therefore overlap in the act of digging.

R S S has other uses too, including firmness, burial, a remaining trace, and place names.

The entire R S S family cannot therefore be restricted to digging.

In the designation the people of al-Rass, the possible sense of well must also be distinguished from the group’s historical identity.

Study of Ḥ F R

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translating this family requires distinguishing general Arabic uses from the Qur’anic proper designation.

رَسَّ: Rassa: became fixed or firm; in other uses, dug, inserted something, or buried.

رَسّ: Rass: firmness; also a well, excavation, mine, or place name in recorded usage, and the beginning or slight trace of something, depending on context.

رَسِيس: Rasīs: something established and remaining; a slight trace or first sign of a condition; also a place name in another use.

رِسَاس: Risās: wells or excavations, a recorded plural of rass in its uses for wells and mines.

رَسَّسَ: Rassasa: made firm; of a camel, braced its knees on the ground to rise.

رَسْرَسَ: Rasrasa: planted its knees firmly and prepared to rise, of a camel.

مَرْسُوس: Marsūs: made firm or secure; in building, a structure whose components are firmly set.

أَرْسَسَ: Arsasa: marked; left a sign to identify something, in one recorded lexical use.

تَرَاسَّ: Tarāssa: exchanged news secretly or quietly with one another.

أَصْحَابُ الرَّسِّ: Aṣḥābu al-rassi: the people of al-Rass, a group known by the name al-Rass; a possible lexical explanation is people associated with a well or a place named al-Rass.

Al-Furqān 25:38

وَعَادًا وَثَمُودَ وَأَصْحَابَ الرَّسِّ وَقُرُونًا بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ كَثِيرًا

wa-ʿādan wa-thamūda wa-aṣḥāba al-rassi wa-qurūnan bayna dhālika kathīran

The Persian Forqan rendering, translated here:

“And ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of al-Rass, and many generations between them.”

Retaining the name the people of al-Rass is suitable here.

Replacing it with the people of the well would substitute one lexical and historical possibility for the group’s name.

The explanation concerning a well or place is better given in reflection or a word note.

Qāf 50:12

كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ وَأَصْحَابُ الرَّسِّ وَثَمُودُ

kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu nūḥin wa-aṣḥābu al-rassi wa-thamūdu

“Before them, the people of Noah, the people of al-Rass, and Thamūd denied.”

Retaining the people of al-Rass is preferable here too.

A brief word explanation could read:

“Arabic rass can designate a well and certain places. The Qur’an identifies the people of al-Rass as earlier deniers without specifying their exact location or identity.”

This conveys the lexical meaning while respecting the limits of the Qur’anic information.

10. Conclusion

R S S has an established semantic core of firmness and becoming fixed in Arabic.

This is seen in uses for securing a building, bracing a camel’s knees, and a trace that remains.

Uses for digging a well, inserting something, burying, slight news, and the first signs of a condition are also recorded.

These senses can be related to firmness, settling, or a remaining trace, but they should not all be derived from a single physical image without adequate historical evidence.

Early Arabic also uses al-rass for a well, excavation, mine, and place name.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s poetry shows that al-Rass was a known place name before Islam, but this evidence does not locate the historical people of al-Rass mentioned in the Qur’an.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times in the designation aṣḥābu al-rassi.

Al-Furqān 25:38 names the people of al-Rass alongside ʿĀd, Thamūd, and many other generations. The surrounding verses discuss rejection of the prophets and the destruction of earlier peoples.

Qāf 50:12 names them alongside Noah’s people and Thamūd; the following verses describe these groups’ denial of the messengers and the fulfillment of the divine warning.

The Qur’an therefore establishes two points: they were an earlier people known by the name al-Rass, and they belonged among deniers upon whom the divine warning came true.

It does not state their prophet’s name, exact location, or manner of destruction.

Historical and exegetical accounts variously identify them as people beside a well, a group from Thamūd, inhabitants of a settlement in al-Yamāmah, or a group connected with other stories.

There are also accounts of their burying their prophet in a well.

These reports can be studied as historical and exegetical accounts; the root meaning and the two Qur’anic verses alone do not establish them as certain.

Retaining the people of al-Rass in Forqan’s translation is suitable: the people of the well or people who buried their prophet in a well could lead readers to accept an identification that the Qur’an itself does not explicitly state.

Final definition: The established semantic core of R S S concerns firmness, becoming fixed, and something settling in place. The Arabic family also includes uses for digging wells, burial, remaining traces, and certain place names. Qur’anic al-rass occurs only in the designation the people of al-Rass. A connection with a well or place is lexically defensible, but the people’s identity, location, prophet’s name, and precise fate cannot be determined from the root and these verses alone.
