Flowing and traveling through the land: from moving water to sāʾiḥūn and sāʾiḥāt in the Qur'an

1. The root in early Arabic

S Y Ḥ concerns flowing, running, and moving across an expanse. Two main uses are water flowing over the ground and people traveling through the land. The word itself does not necessarily specify a destination, duration, or motive.

Sāḥa al-māʾu yasīḥu means that water ran or flowed over the ground. Al-sayḥ denotes water flowing on the surface; suyūḥ and asyāḥ are recorded plurals.

In human usage, sāḥa fī al-arḍi yasīḥu means roaming through the land, moving from place to place, or traveling through regions. Recorded verbal nouns include sayḥ, suyūḥ, sayaḥān, and siyāḥah.

This use emphasizes movement and traversing the land. A person may travel to learn about places, seek a livelihood, worship, study, leave a previous environment, or pursue another purpose. None of these motives alone is an invariable component of the verb's general meaning.

Sāʾiḥ is the active participle and, in its movement sense, denotes someone traveling through the land.

Historical meaning and the limits of evidence

Early lexicographers, including Ibn Fāris, discuss flowing water and human movement through the land within the same semantic family. This analysis identifies a clear relationship between the two.

Early lexical accounts also record sāḥa al-ẓillu for a shadow returning or changing position. The verb was therefore not confined to people and water, but could describe movement or displacement of other phenomena.

Classical Arabic uses asāḥa al-nahra for causing a river's water to flow. A verse attributed to al-Farazdaq, a poet of the early Islamic period, reads:

وَكَمْ لِلْمُسْلِمِينَ أَسَحْتُ بَحْرِي
بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ مِنْ نَهْرٍ وَنَهْرِ

wa-kam li-al-muslimīna asaḥtu baḥrī / bi-idhni allāhi min nahrin wa-nahri

An approximate rendering of the transmitted wording is: “How many a river upon river I have made flow from my sea for the Muslims, by The God's permission.” Asaḥtu concerns causing flow. The wording asaḥtu fīhim is also transmitted; without the full poem's context, “my sea” should not be treated with certainty as a metaphor for the poet's personal generosity.

This witness belongs to the Islamic period and should not be presented as pre-Islamic poetry. Nor does an image of flowing water prove that human siyāḥah necessarily developed historically from water flowing.

To establish pre-Qur'anic meaning, one must distinguish evidence from the Qur'an and language near its period, later lexicographical accounts, and independently datable witnesses.

The witnesses considered here do not establish the historical priority of all branches. Nevertheless, the Qur'anic fasīḥū fī al-arḍ shows that movement through the land was intelligible in the language of revelation.

Flowing and moving across an open expanse thus form an important semantic center. Turning this relationship into a definite story of the root's earliest origin requires further historical evidence.

Other Arabic uses

Al-sayḥ also names a striped garment or cloak. Musayyaḥ describes fabric with differently colored lines or elongated patterns. The lines can be compared with extension across a surface, but this resemblance does not establish the historical derivation of every such use.

The image is understandable, but striped fabric should not be treated as the central meaning of every derivative.

Misyāḥ is also recorded for someone who roams about carrying harmful speech or gossip. Here movement is associated with misconduct; its negative character comes from that conduct, rather than movement itself.

The root therefore has no fixed moral value in itself. Movement may serve good or bad purposes, and judgment depends on context.

2. Forms in the Qur'an

S Y Ḥ occurs three times in three verses:

  • فَسِيحُوا (fasīḥū), Al-Tawbah 9:2: a plural imperative of sāḥa yasīḥu.
  • السَّائِحُونَ (al-sāʾiḥūn), Al-Tawbah 9:112: a masculine plural active participle.
  • سَائِحَاتٍ (sāʾiḥātin), Al-Taḥrīm 66:5: a feminine plural active participle.

They belong to one family but perform different grammatical roles.

Fasīḥū directs movement through the land. Al-sāʾiḥūn and sāʾiḥāt are masculine and feminine descriptions. Whether these two descriptions mean travelers or bear a particular devotional sense requires their contexts and the history of usage, not morphology alone.

The independent verbal noun siyāḥah does not occur in the Qur'an. Nor do asāḥa, misyāḥ, or musayyaḥ, although they are recorded in Arabic.

Fasīḥū consists of the connective fa- and imperative sīḥū. It must not be confused with F S Ḥ, the root concerning spaciousness and making room.

3. Fasīḥū fī al-arḍ: movement within a treaty context

Al-Tawbah 9:2 states:

فَسِيحُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّكُمْ غَيْرُ مُعْجِزِي اللَّهِ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ مُخْزِي الْكَافِرِينَ

fa-sīḥū fī al-arḍi arbaʿata ashhurin wa-iʿlamū annakum ghayru muʿjizī allāhi wa-anna allāha mukhzī al-kāfirīn

Al-Tawbah 9:2

Travel through the land for four months, and know that you cannot thwart The God and that The God will disgrace the disbelievers.

This is the clearest verbal use of S Y Ḥ in the Qur'an.

Fasīḥū is a plural imperative; fī al-arḍ identifies the field of movement. Arbaʿata ashhur specifies the period allowed.

The verb means moving or traveling through the land. Four months comes from the temporal phrase, not from the root itself.

The addressees and the treaty context

The preceding verse, Al-Tawbah 9:1, states:

بَرَاءَةٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ إِلَى الَّذِينَ عَاهَدْتُمْ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

barāʾatun mina allāhi wa-rasūlihi ilā alladhīna ʿāhadtum mina al-mushrikīn

Al-Tawbah 9:1

A declaration of disassociation from The God and His Messenger to those polytheists with whom you made a treaty.

Fasīḥū follows a declaration concerning treaties. Its addressees are those covered by that declaration, rather than believers generally or all humanity.

This prevents treating it simply as a general instruction to undertake tourism or travel.

Nor does fasīḥū fī al-arḍ itself mean “be expelled from the land.” It contains neither a verb of expulsion nor a specified destination for migration.

Its direct sense permits movement through the land during the stated period.

Al-Tawbah 9:4 excepts polytheists who have not breached their treaties or helped anyone against the believers, requiring their agreements to be honored until their term ends.

Al-Tawbah 9:6 also says that a polytheist requesting protection should receive it and then be conveyed to a place of safety.

These verses place Al-Tawbah 9:2 within provisions concerning treaties, security, and dealings with different groups.

Lexically, sīḥū describes movement through the land; the surrounding verses explain the duration and the addressees' social and legal position.

This use shows that worship, fasting, struggle, or migration is not a necessary meaning of every derivative: here the people addressed are those covered by the declaration concerning treaties. It does not rule out a special devotional meaning elsewhere.

4. Al-sāʾiḥūn among the descriptions of believers: traveling and fasting

Al-Tawbah 9:112 states:

التَّائِبُونَ الْعَابِدُونَ الْحَامِدُونَ السَّائِحُونَ الرَّاكِعُونَ السَّاجِدُونَ الْآمِرُونَ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَالنَّاهُونَ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ وَالْحَافِظُونَ لِحُدُودِ اللَّهِ وَبَشِّرِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

al-tāʾibūna al-ʿābidūna al-ḥāmidūna al-sāʾiḥūna al-rākiʿūna al-sājidūna al-āmirūna bi-al-maʿrūfi wa-al-nāhūna ʿani al-munkari wa-al-ḥāfiẓūna li-ḥudūdi allāhi wa-bashshiri al-muʾminīn

Al-Tawbah 9:112

Those who repent, worship, praise, travel, bow, prostrate, command what is right, forbid what is wrong, and guard the limits of The God. Give good news to the believers.

Al-sāʾiḥūn here appears among descriptions of believers.

Al-Tawbah 9:111 speaks of the believers' covenant with The God and committing their lives and possessions in His way. Al-Tawbah 9:112 then describes those who follow this course.

The description is positive in this setting. Positive evaluation alone, however, does not settle which lexical sense is intended.

The ordinary movement sense

Sāʾiḥ derives from sāḥa yasīḥu and in its movement sense denotes someone traveling through the land.

On that reading, its masculine plural al-sāʾiḥūn refers to people characterized by such movement.

If this sense is retained in the verse, traveling through the land is one characteristic of believers. It may serve religious, intellectual, or practical purposes, but a specific purpose cannot be determined from the participle alone.

Movement through the land appears elsewhere in the Qur'an. For example, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:20 states:

قُلْ سِيرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ فَانْظُرُوا كَيْفَ بَدَأَ الْخَلْقَ

qul sīrū fī al-arḍi fa-unẓurū kayfa badaʾa al-khalq

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:20

Say, “Travel through the land, then observe how He began creation.”

Movement here is linked to observation and reflection. But S Y R differs from S Y Ḥ, and the instruction to observe comes from fa-unẓurū.

The passage shows that movement through the land has a place in Qur'anic teaching, but its explicit purpose should not be transferred to al-sāʾiḥūn without evidence.

Why has al-sāʾiḥūn been interpreted as people who fast?

Early commentary explains al-sāʾiḥūn as al-ṣāʾimūn, people who fast. This reading was widespread among early interpreters and is also recorded in lexicographical works.

One explanation compares a devotional traveler, who does not always carry provisions and may abstain from eating until food is found, with a person who fasts.

This connects two conditions by analogy: the traveler may go without food, and the fasting person abstains from food and drink for a specified period.

The resemblance alone does not establish that fasting was the earliest meaning of sāʾiḥ. Nor, however, does uncertainty about the historical explanation invalidate an attested special sense.

The Qur'an also uses derivatives of Ṣ W M, including taṣūmū, ṣawm, and ṣiyām, for fasting, whereas Al-Tawbah 9:2 clearly concerns movement. Different roots do not prevent semantic overlap or a special use of sāʾiḥ in another context.

The widespread early interpretation cannot be dismissed merely because the roots differ: words can acquire figurative or conventional senses.

The task is to assess the context and transmitted evidence for a special sense together; knowing the root's general meaning does not by itself resolve the verse.

Al-Tawbah 9:112 places al-sāʾiḥūn alongside worship, praise, bowing, and prostration. That fits a devotional reading, although the juxtaposition alone does not make fasting the only possible interpretation.

“Those who fast” is a well-established early reading, while “those who travel through the land” retains the movement sense and also has interpretive support. Preference in this verse should consider usage and context, not derivation alone.

Does al-sāʾiḥūn mean migrants or those engaged in struggle?

Other interpretations include migrants, travelers seeking knowledge, and those engaged in struggle in The God's way. These should be examined alongside the fasting reading.

Migration, travel for learning, and some forms of such struggle can involve movement through the land.

That association does not itself establish lexical identity.

Migration involves movement, but not every journey is migration. Someone may travel to learn or strive for truth, but their scholarly or religious purpose requires contextual evidence and is not a necessary component of sāʾiḥ.

The general meaning should therefore be distinguished from its possible instances.

5. Sāʾiḥāt in Al-Taḥrīm 66:5

Al-Taḥrīm 66:5 states:

عَسَىٰ رَبُّهُ إِنْ طَلَّقَكُنَّ أَنْ يُبْدِلَهُ أَزْوَاجًا خَيْرًا مِنْكُنَّ مُسْلِمَاتٍ مُؤْمِنَاتٍ قَانِتَاتٍ تَائِبَاتٍ عَابِدَاتٍ سَائِحَاتٍ ثَيِّبَاتٍ وَأَبْكَارًا

ʿasā rabbuhu in ṭallaqakunna an yubdilahu azwājan khayran minkunna muslimātin muʾminātin qānitātin tāʾibātin ʿābidātin sāʾiḥātin thayyibātin wa-abkārā

Al-Taḥrīm 66:5

A proposed rendering following the movement interpretation is: “If he divorces you, perhaps his Lord will replace you with wives better than you: submitting, believing, obedient, repentant, worshipping, traveling through the land, previously married and virgins.” Forqan's currently installed translation instead has “fasting” here; the proposed alternative does not alter the verse page.

Sāʾiḥāt is the regular feminine plural of sāʾiḥah. Al-sāʾiḥūn in Al-Tawbah 9:112 is masculine; here the corresponding description applies to women.

Grammatical gender alone does not change the root's meaning.

The word among the surrounding descriptions

Sāʾiḥāt follows tāʾibāt and ʿābidāt.

The setting identifies praiseworthy qualities of the women described, but each description should retain its own contribution.

Tāʾibāt concerns returning in repentance and ʿābidāt worship. Sāʾiḥāt requires assessment of both its movement sense and its established devotional interpretation as fasting.

Appearing in one list does not by itself make sāʾiḥāt identical to ʿābidāt or ṣāʾimāt. Yet the fasting interpretation is not based only on this list: it also has early transmitted and lexical support.

Early commentary frequently interprets this word as fasting women; migration and travel readings are also recorded.

The verse does not independently use a derivative of Ṣ W M or identify a destination for migration. Absence of an explicit fasting word does not itself exclude the attested special sense of sāʾiḥāt.

“Women who travel through the land” follows the movement interpretation. “Fasting women” is also an established early interpretation and a defensible translation choice; it should not be dismissed merely because it differs from the general root sense.

Does siyāḥah mean something different for women and men?

Al-sāʾiḥūn in Al-Tawbah 9:112 and sāʾiḥāt in Al-Taḥrīm 66:5 show the description applied to both sexes.

If movement is intended, there is no basis for asserting without evidence that it is actual travel for men but only an inward or figurative condition for women.

Likewise, the fasting reading should be evaluated by the same linguistic and interpretive standards in both verses. The movement sense in Al-Tawbah 9:2 does not contradict a special sense of the participle elsewhere.

Both forms should be examined through their linguistic structure, Qur'anic uses, and contexts, rather than different assumptions about men's and women's social roles.

The verse alone does not specify women's destinations, means of transport, or travel regulations. These are beyond the word's meaning.

6. S Y Ḥ and related roots

S Y Ḥ (sāḥa) and S Y R (sāra)

Both roots concern movement through the land.

Sayr means going, traversing a route, or moving. The Qur'an repeatedly calls people to sīrū fī al-arḍ to observe earlier peoples' fate or signs of creation.

Siyāḥah also describes movement through the land, with roaming across an expanse prominent in its general lexical sense. A fixed destination is not necessary to that sense.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary. Sayr may lack a specified destination, and siyāḥah may have a definite purpose.

One cannot therefore say that sayr is always purposeful and siyāḥah always aimless.

S Y Ḥ (sāḥa) and S F R (safar)

Safar concerns leaving one's place of residence and undertaking a journey. The Qur'an also discusses travelers in passages on fasting, purification, and recording debts.

Siyāḥah concerns roaming or movement through the land and may involve travel.

Not every journey is necessarily siyāḥah in the sense of roaming through regions. Someone may go somewhere for a brief task and return, whereas siyāḥah may involve continuing movement and visiting different places.

Sāʾiḥ alone does not determine that a person is far from home or meets the legal conditions for being a traveler.

Rules concerning safar should therefore not be extended to every kind of siyāḥah solely because their meanings overlap.

S Y Ḥ (sāḥa) and H J R (hajara)

Hijrah involves leaving or separating from a previous place, situation, or environment. In migration contexts, this departure is accompanied by going elsewhere.

Siyāḥah may also entail leaving one's residence, but does not necessarily mean permanent departure, severing ties with a previous environment, or seeking refuge in another land.

Someone may journey through several regions and return home. In hijrah, leaving the previous place or situation is a prominent component.

Interpreting al-sāʾiḥūn as migrants may thus identify a particular instance of movement, but is not an automatic lexical equivalent of the root.

S Y Ḥ (sāḥa) and S Y L (sāla)

Sayḥ and sayl both concern flowing water.

Sayl names flowing water or a flood; the Qur'an describes water and floods in several passages.

S Y Ḥ and S Y L are nevertheless different roots.

Semantic similarity does not establish a single historical derivation. Human siyāḥah is known through attested use of its own root, not by automatically transferring every property of a flood to human movement.

S Y Ḥ (siyāḥah) and Ṣ W M (ṣawm)

This comparison matters because of the established fasting interpretation of al-sāʾiḥūn.

Ṣawm concerns abstaining or refraining from an activity. In Qur'anic devotional usage, ṣiyām denotes fasting, with its conditions explained in the relevant verses.

The general movement sense of siyāḥah is traveling through the land.

A traveler may go without food, just as someone fasting abstains from food and drink for a specified time. This resemblance can explain an analogical connection.

Yet going without food is not required in every use of sāḥa, nor is movement required in every use of ṣāma.

The roots should not be treated as identical in all uses. Translation must still consider attested figurative or conventional meanings in the particular verse; a difference of roots alone does not exclude them.

S Y Ḥ (sāḥa) and F S Ḥ (fasaḥa)

Qur'anic fasīḥū begins with fa-, but that letter is not part of the root.

S Y Ḥ consists of sīn, yāʾ, and ḥāʾ. F S Ḥ is a separate root concerning spaciousness, breadth, and making room.

Al-Mujādilah 58:11 states:

إِذَا قِيلَ لَكُمْ تَفَسَّحُوا فِي الْمَجَالِسِ فَافْسَحُوا

idhā qīla lakum tafassaḥū fī al-majālisi fa-ifsaḥū

Al-Mujādilah 58:11

When you are told to make room in gatherings, make room.

Tafassaḥū and fa-ifsaḥū belong to F S Ḥ; fasīḥū in Al-Tawbah 9:2 belongs to S Y Ḥ.

Their written forms are close, but their meanings should not be confused.

7. Suggested renderings for Forqan

Translation should distinguish physical movement through the land from particular devotional interpretations.

Al-Tawbah 9:2

فَسِيحُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ

fa-sīḥū fī al-arḍi arbaʿata ashhur

Al-Tawbah 9:2

A suitable rendering is:

“Travel through the land for four months.”

This retains the verb's movement sense. Commentary should explain the declaration concerning treaties that frames the instruction.

The Persian “gardesh konīd” can suggest leisure or tourism today, but the verse concerns movement through the land, not necessarily a recreational journey.

If clarification is needed, Persian alternatives meaning “move about in the land” or “travel through the land” can be considered. Forqan's current rendering is defensible in retaining movement.

Al-Tawbah 9:112

السَّائِحُونَ

al-sāʾiḥūn

Al-Tawbah 9:112

The rendering proposed here follows the movement interpretation:

“Those who travel” or “those who travel through the land.”

The term appears among believers' qualities and should be understood in that context.

“Those who fast” is also an established early interpretation and a defensible translation. This article proposes retaining movement, while explaining the alternative; that preference is interpretive, not a consequence forced by the root.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:5

سَائِحَاتٍ

sāʾiḥāt

Al-Taḥrīm 66:5

Following the movement interpretation, one could propose:

“Women who travel through the land,” or simply “those who travel through the land” within the sentence.

“Fasting women,” used in Forqan's current translation, rests on an established interpretation. Neither fasting nor actual travel is determined by morphology alone; context and usage must be considered. The proposal here does not change the installed translation.

The same standards should apply as for al-sāʾiḥūn in Al-Tawbah 9:112. Grammatical gender is not a reason by itself to change lexical meaning.

Siyāḥah today

In modern Persian and Arabic, siyāḥah often refers to tourism, visiting attractions, or travel for recreation or learning about places.

This retains part of the field of movement through the land, but the Qur'anic sense should not be restricted to modern tourism.

It does not necessarily entail recreation, hotels, historical attractions, or holiday travel. These are present-day forms of travel that should not be imposed on the root's earlier meaning.

Conclusion

S Y Ḥ concerns flowing and movement. Water runs across the ground and people travel through the land. The branches have a clear semantic relationship, but the evidence considered here does not establish their historical priority.

The root occurs three times: once as the imperative fasīḥū and twice as the participles al-sāʾiḥūn and sāʾiḥāt.

In Al-Tawbah 9:2, movement is clear and belongs to the declaration concerning treaties and a four-month period. The root itself does not mean expulsion, and the instruction is not a general travel command for everyone.

Al-sāʾiḥūn describes believers in Al-Tawbah 9:112; sāʾiḥāt describes the women envisaged in Al-Taḥrīm 66:5.

The fasting interpretation is widespread in early commentary and recorded in lexicons. Movement is also an attested sense. The proposed analogy between a traveler going without food and a fasting person does not establish the earliest meaning, but uncertainty about that analogy does not invalidate the special fasting interpretation.

Readings concerning migrants, those engaged in struggle, or seekers of knowledge may identify instances of movement, but these purposes do not necessarily follow from the root alone.

Final definition: The main movement branch of S Y Ḥ concerns flowing and moving across an expanse. Sāḥa fī al-arḍ means traveling through the land, and sāʾiḥ in that sense denotes a traveler. Destination, duration, motive, and moral value depend on context. For al-sāʾiḥūn and sāʾiḥāt, the established devotional interpretation as fasting must also be considered.

This article proposes retaining “traveling through the land,” while recognizing “those who fast” as a substantial early interpretation and a possible translation. A preference between them requires usage and context, rather than excluding one solely through the root's general sense. The site's existing verse translations remain unchanged.
