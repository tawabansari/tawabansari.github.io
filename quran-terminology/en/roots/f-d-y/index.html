The Qur’anic Root F D Y

Release in exchange for something placed in another’s stead

The root ف د ي (F D Y) in the Qur’an rests on a clear relationship: something or someone is offered in exchange for another thing or person so that someone may be released from captivity, an obligation, harm, or punishment. The words فداء (fidāʾ), فدية (fidyah), and افتدى (iftadā) belong to this field.

Ibn Fāris explains the core of this branch as placing something in another thing’s stead to protect it: for example, a person offering themselves or their property in another’s place. Other sources likewise define فدى (fadā) as releasing someone through property, a life, or something else, and فداء (fidāʾ) as what is given to secure that release.

The root’s central element is therefore more than simply “paying.” Many of its uses involve three components: a situation from which release is sought, something offered in exchange, and the release or protection expected from that substitution.

In the Qur’an, this field extends from freeing a captive to a marital situation, certain provisions concerning fasting and pilgrimage, the account of Abraham and his son, and the impossible attempt to escape punishment in the Hereafter. This breadth prevents us from reducing fidyah to the narrow legal meaning “paying a sum of money.”

Qur’anic forms

The root ف د ي (F D Y) occurs 13 times in the Qur’an, in five principal forms:

فَدَيْنَا (fadaynā), from the basic verb, once;

تُفَادُوهُمْ (tufādūhum), from Form III, once;

forms of افْتَدَى (iftadā), from Form VIII, seven times;

فِدَاء (fidāʾ) once;

and فِدْيَة (fidyah) three times.

فَدَيْنَا (fadaynā), from the basic verb, once;
تُفَادُوهُمْ (tufādūhum), from Form III, once;
forms of افْتَدَى (iftadā), from Form VIII, seven times;
فِدَاء (fidāʾ) once;
and فِدْيَة (fidyah) three times.

The Qur’an does not use forms such as مفدي (mafdiyy), تفدية (tafdiyah), or an active participle from this root.

1. The semantic core in early Arabic: one thing in another’s place

Under ف د ي (F D Y), Ibn Fāris records two very different branches. He defines the branch relevant to the Qur’an as placing something in another thing’s stead to protect it. When someone ransoms another with their own life or something else, he explains, they place that offering in exchange for the person. Another branch uses فداء (fadāʾ) in certain dialects for a store or heap of foodstuffs. Ibn Fāris himself treats this as a separate meaning; it should not be conflated with the Qur’anic branch.

Lisān al-ʿArab further clarifies the main field:

فداه بماله (fadāhu bi-mālihi) means that he released him through his property;
فداه بنفسه (fadāhu bi-nafsihi) means that he offered his own life to save him;
and الفداء (al-fidāʾ) is what is given to release a person from a situation.

What is offered in exchange need not be money. It may be property, another person, the person themselves, an action, food, fasting, or something else. Context determines the offering; the root carries the relationship of release through placing one thing in exchange for another.

A clear witness predating the Qur’an

The pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī addresses al-Nuʿmān:

مَهْلًا فِدَاءٌ لَكَ الْأَقْوَامُ كُلُّهُمْ
وَمَا أُثَمِّرُ مِنْ مَالٍ وَمِنْ وَلَدِ

Mahlan fidāʾun laka l-aqwāmu kulluhum / Wa-mā uthammiru min mālin wa-min waladi.

That is: be gentle; may all the peoples, and whatever wealth and children I have gathered, be your ransom.

The poetic use is not a real transaction. It is the language of hyperbole in honoring someone and declaring loyalty. Nevertheless, it shows that before the Qur’an, fidāʾ was a recognized way of speaking about offering people, property, and children in exchange for another person’s protection.

Another witness from pre-Islamic poetry says:

فِدًى لَكُما رِجْلَيَّ أُمِّي وَخالَتِي

Fidan lakumā rijlayya ummī wa-khālatī.

“May my mother and my maternal aunt be a ransom for you both, my two feet.”

Here the poet hyperbolically offers his mother and maternal aunt as a ransom for his two feet. Again, the central relationship is clear: one thing is put in place of another valued thing so that the latter may remain.

Evidence from Semitic languages

This field is not confined to Arabic. In Biblical Hebrew, a cognate root is used for redeeming, rescuing, and releasing in exchange for something. Comparative dictionaries also record cognate forms in Akkadian, Ethiopic, and the languages of Ancient South Arabia.

These witnesses show the great antiquity of the field of “ransom and redemption.” The existence of Semitic cognates alone, however, does not establish the precise historical development of each Arabic derivative or the order in which its meanings developed.

2. وَفَدَيْنَاهُ (wa-fadaynāhu): release through placing something else in his stead

The root’s clearest image appears in As-Saffat 37:107:

وَفَدَيْنَاهُ بِذِبْحٍ عَظِيمٍ

Wa-fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin ʿaẓīmin.

“And We ransomed him with a great slaughter-offering.”

The verse’s construction is revealing. A person facing slaughter is released through another slaughter-offering. The preposition باء (bāʾ) also introduces what serves as the ransom:

فَدَيْنَاهُ بِذِبْحٍ (fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin)

The Qur’an itself thus presents the root’s core as substitution for release. Lexical sources similarly explain this verse: the slaughter-offering was made his fidāʾ, and through it he was released from slaughter.

Two boundaries must nevertheless be maintained.

First, فدى (fadā) does not itself mean “to slaughter” or “to sacrifice.” The act of ransoming is one thing, while ذبح (dhibḥ) is what serves as its means in this verse. The distinction becomes still clearer in other verses, since the ransom need not involve slaughter at all.

Second, this single event should not be turned into a general theory in which another person or being always bears a human being’s moral burden. The verse describes a particular event: something replaces the anticipated slaughter, and the person is released from it. Neither a transfer of sin nor a general system of atonement follows from the root ف د ي (F D Y) itself.

3. From captivity to fidāʾ: the direct relationship with A S R

One of the clearest connections between two roots that the Qur’an itself places together occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:85:

وَإِنْ يَأْتُوكُمْ أُسَارَىٰ تُفَادُوهُمْ

Wa-in yaʾtūkum usārā tufādūhum.

“And if they come to you as captives, you ransom them.”

Here أ س ر (A S R) describes the person’s situation: they have been captured and placed under another’s control. ف د ي (F D Y) describes movement in the opposite direction: something is offered in exchange for their release so that they may leave that situation.

Put simply:

أسر (asr) keeps a person under another’s control;
فداء (fidāʾ) is a way of releasing them in exchange for something.

This connection does not come only from a dictionary; the Qur’an itself places the two words together in one sentence.

Arabic sources likewise treat فك الأسير (fakk al-asīr), freeing a captive, as a principal use of fidāʾ, explaining that the exchange may involve property or even one person for another.

تُفَادُوهُمْ (tufādūhum) and the question of Form III

In the widely used reading of Ḥafṣ, the verse has تُفَادُوهُمْ (tufādūhum), a Form III verb. Sources on Qur’anic readings and lexicography also report the canonically transmitted تَفْدُوهُمْ (tafdūhum) in other readings. Lexicographers record both فدى الأسير (fadā al-asīr) and فادى الأسير (fādā al-asīr) for releasing a captive.

We therefore cannot derive the verse’s entire meaning from Form III and claim that this pattern necessarily means “exchanging two captives for one another.” Mufādāh can fit naturally within a transaction or reciprocal exchange, but the root’s core remains release in exchange for something; context determines the kind of transaction.

فَإِمَّا مَنًّا بَعْدُ وَإِمَّا فِدَاءً (fa-immā mannan baʿdu wa-immā fidāʾan)

After speaking of war and securing captives’ bonds, Muhammad 47:4 names two courses:

فَإِمَّا مَنًّا بَعْدُ وَإِمَّا فِدَاءً

Fa-immā mannan baʿdu wa-immā fidāʾan.

“Then either release as a favor, or ransom.”

This contrast is especially important for understanding fidāʾ.

مَنّ (mann) in this construction is release without anything in exchange;
فداء (fidāʾ) is release in exchange for something.

The verse does not say that the offering must be money, specify an amount, or require a particular form. Fidāʾ itself indicates only that something is given in exchange for release. Details of later systems governing prisoners of war should not be incorporated into the root’s lexical definition.

4. افْتَدَى (iftadā): when a person gives something for their own release

Seven of the root’s 13 Qur’anic occurrences are in Form VIII. In this construction, a person offers something in exchange for their own release.

This direction is clear in Al-Baqarah 2:229:

فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا فِيمَا افْتَدَتْ بِهِ

Fa-lā junāḥa ʿalayhimā fīmā ftadat bihi.

“Then there is no blame upon either of them concerning what she gives to ransom herself.”

The verse concerns a marital relationship in which the parties fear that the limits of The God will not be maintained. In this situation, the woman gives something and leaves the relationship under discussion.

The lexical point is that the Qur’an uses افتدت به (iftadat bihi): she offered something in exchange for her own release.

Later legal systems developed detailed terminology and rulings for this subject, but the root ف د ي (F D Y) does not define that legal apparatus. At the lexical level, this much is sufficient: something is given in exchange for a person’s release from the existing situation.

5. Iftidāʾ in the Hereafter: when no offering for release is accepted

Most Qur’anic uses of افتدى (iftadā) appear in scenes of the Hereafter. This group provides some of the strongest evidence for the root’s range.

Ali Imran 3:91 says:

فَلَنْ يُقْبَلَ مِنْ أَحَدِهِمْ مِلْءُ الْأَرْضِ ذَهَبًا وَلَوِ افْتَدَىٰ بِهِ

Fa-lan yuqbala min aḥadihim milʾu l-arḍi dhahaban wa-lawi ftadā bihi.

“An earthful of gold will not be accepted from any of them, even if he sought to ransom himself with it.”

Al-Maidah 5:36 broadens the scope:

لَوْ أَنَّ لَهُمْ مَا فِي الْأَرْضِ جَمِيعًا وَمِثْلَهُ مَعَهُ لِيَفْتَدُوا بِهِ مِنْ عَذَابِ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ مَا تُقُبِّلَ مِنْهُمْ

Law anna lahum mā fī l-arḍi jamīʿan wa-mithlahu maʿahu li-yaftadū bihi min ʿadhābi yawmi l-qiyāmati mā tuqubbila minhum.

“If they possessed everything on earth, and as much again with it, to ransom themselves from the punishment of the Day of Resurrection, it would not be accepted from them.”

The same image recurs, with variations, in Yunus 10:54, Ar-Rad 13:18, and Az-Zumar 39:47.

These verses clarify several semantic features.

First, افتداء (iftidāʾ) is not confined to prisoners of war. A person may hypothetically wish to ransom themselves from punishment or another consequence as well.

Second, what is offered need not have a fixed value or a mathematically equivalent price. The Qur’an imagines even the whole earth and as much again. The core of ransom is therefore not an “equal price”; what matters is that something is proposed in exchange for release.

Third, ransom works when the party holding authority accepts it. In these verses, the precise point is that even the greatest thing a person could offer is not accepted.

A person even offers their closest relatives

Al-Maarij 70:11 brings the image to its height:

يَوَدُّ الْمُجْرِمُ لَوْ يَفْتَدِي مِنْ عَذَابِ يَوْمِئِذٍ بِبَنِيهِ

Yawaddu l-mujrimu law yaftadī min ʿadhābi yawmiʾidhin bi-banīhi.

“The offender wishes that he could ransom himself from the punishment of that day with his children.”

The following verses name his wife, his brother, the family that shelters him, and finally everyone on earth.

This group establishes that fidāʾ is not inherently financial. What is offered for release may even be another person. The same field was already visible in the pre-Islamic poetic pairing of “wealth and children.”

The Qur’an does not affirm that such an exchange takes place in these verses; it describes an impossible human wish. The speaker’s identity and the statement’s setting matter: these are an offender’s desperate proposals, not a system the Qur’an has prescribed for release from punishment.

6. فِدْيَة (fidyah): an offering whose form is determined by context

Fidyah occurs three times in the Qur’an, and those three uses show that it cannot be defined as “money.”

فِدْيَةٌ طَعَامُ مِسْكِينٍ (fidyatun ṭaʿāmu miskīnin)

Al-Baqarah 2:184 says:

فِدْيَةٌ طَعَامُ مِسْكِينٍ

Fidyatun ṭaʿāmu miskīnin.

“A fidyah: food for a needy person.”

Here the verse itself specifies the form of fidyah as food for a needy person. Food is therefore not part of the lexical meaning of fidyah; the verse’s explanation determines what the fidyah is to be in this particular provision.

Exactly who falls under this provision in the preceding phrase is a separate interpretive question, not one resolved by the root F D Y itself.

فِدْيَةٌ مِنْ صِيَامٍ أَوْ صَدَقَةٍ أَوْ نُسُكٍ (fidyatun min ṣiyāmin aw ṣadaqatin aw nusukin)

Al-Baqarah 2:196 is clearer still:

فَفِدْيَةٌ مِنْ صِيَامٍ أَوْ صَدَقَةٍ أَوْ نُسُكٍ

Fa-fidyatun min ṣiyāmin aw ṣadaqatin aw nusukin.

“Then a fidyah of fasting, charity, or a sacrificial rite.”

This verse alone is enough to rule out the definition “fidyah = a monetary sum.”

One form of fidyah in the Qur’an itself is fasting. Fidyah may therefore be an action involving no direct financial payment. Charity and نسك (nusuk), a sacrificial rite, are other forms.

A more suitable general definition is therefore:

Fidyah is something or an action placed in exchange for something else in a particular situation, providing a means of compensation or release.

Yet “compensation” should not become the fixed meaning of every occurrence either. With a prisoner of war, the issue is freedom; in certain ritual provisions, substitution or relief from a situation’s consequences; and in the Hereafter, an attempt to escape punishment.

لَا يُؤْخَذُ مِنْكُمْ فِدْيَةٌ (lā yuʾkhadhu minkum fidyatun)

Al-Hadid 57:15 says:

فَالْيَوْمَ لَا يُؤْخَذُ مِنْكُمْ فِدْيَةٌ وَلَا مِنَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا

Fa-l-yawma lā yuʾkhadhu minkum fidyatun wa-lā mina lladhīna kafarū.

“So today no ransom will be taken from you, nor from those who committed kufr.”

This verse again shows that fidyah is not the name of one particular act of worship. The same word appears in a scene of the Hereafter for an offering a person wishes to give for their release, but which is not accepted.

The narrow legal meaning of fidyah is therefore only one historical use of this family; the Qur’an’s lexical field is much broader.

7. فِدَاء (fidāʾ) and فِدْيَة (fidyah): do they have separate meanings?

Early sources often place فدية (fidyah), فدى (fidā), and فداء (fidāʾ) in one semantic family, sometimes explicitly treating them as synonymous.

The Qur’an itself shows an interesting distribution:

Fidāʾ occurs only once, concerning the release of prisoners of war;
fidyah appears in connection with fasting, pilgrimage, and the Hereafter.

This limited distribution should not, however, become an absolute rule that fidāʾ is exclusive to captives and fidyah to worship or the Hereafter. Linguistic sources record no such rigid boundary, and the Qur’anic occurrences are too few to establish that rule.

Their shared tendency is clearer than any possible difference: something offered in exchange for release, protection, or substitution.

8. F D Y (fadā) compared with A S R (asara): captivity and release in exchange

In Al-Baqarah 2:85, the Qur’an places these two families directly together:

أُسَارَىٰ تُفَادُوهُمْ

Usārā tufādūhum.

“Captives whom you ransom.”

أ س ر (A S R) focuses on capturing and keeping someone under another’s control.
ف د ي (F D Y) focuses on bringing them out of that situation in exchange for something.

Muhammad 47:4 illuminates the difference from another angle. After:

فَشُدُّوا الْوَثَاقَ

Fa-shuddū l-wathāqa.

meaning the securing of captives’ bonds, the Qur’an goes on to speak of:

إِمَّا مَنًّا ... وَإِمَّا فِدَاءً

Immā mannan … wa-immā fidāʾan.

“Either release as a favor … or ransom.”

as the subsequent alternatives.

We can therefore see three different stages within the Qur’an’s semantic relationships:

أسر (asr): the person has been captured;
وثاق (wathāq): their bonds have been secured;
فداء (fidāʾ): something is offered in exchange for their release.

These three words are related, but they are not synonymous.

9. F D Y (fadā) compared with DH B Ḥ (dhabaḥa) and N S K (nasaka): ransom is not necessarily sacrifice

One of the root’s most important boundaries is its distinction from the act of sacrifice or slaughter itself.

As-Saffat 37:107 says:

وَفَدَيْنَاهُ بِذِبْحٍ عَظِيمٍ

Wa-fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin ʿaẓīmin.

“And We ransomed him with a great slaughter-offering.”

If فدى (fadā) itself meant sacrificing, the distinction between the verb فدينا (fadaynā) and what follows it with باء (bāʾ) would lose its sense. Slaughter is the means of the ransom, not the meaning of ransom itself.

Al-Baqarah 2:196 makes this distinction still more definite:

فِدْيَةٌ مِنْ صِيَامٍ أَوْ صَدَقَةٍ أَوْ نُسُكٍ

Fidyatun min ṣiyāmin aw ṣadaqatin aw nusukin.

“A fidyah of fasting, charity, or a sacrificial rite.”

Nusuk is only one of several forms of fidyah; fasting is another.

Fidyah therefore cannot generally be translated as “sacrifice.” A sacrifice or nusuk may serve as fidyah, just as property, food, or another action may do so.

This distinction also prevents us from building a theological theory of sacrifice, transferred sin, or forgiveness from the root F D Y alone.

10. F D Y (fadā) compared with AYN D L (ʿadala): ransom and an equivalent substitute

In some verses concerning the Hereafter, the Qur’an uses another word in a field close to ransom:

وَلَا يُؤْخَذُ مِنْهَا عَدْلٌ

Wa-lā yuʾkhadhu minhā ʿadlun.

“Nor will any substitute be taken from it.”

Al-Baqarah 2:48

And:

وَإِنْ تَعْدِلْ كُلَّ عَدْلٍ لَا يُؤْخَذْ مِنْهَا

Wa-in taʿdil kulla ʿadlin lā yuʾkhadh minhā.

“Even if it offers every substitute, none will be taken from it.”

Al-Anam 6:70

Early lexicographers and commentators explain عَدْل (ʿadl) in these two constructions as ransom and substitute: something placed in exchange for a person, taking their place in terms of value or recompense.

The two roots nevertheless have different starting points.

With عدل (ʿadl), equivalence and substitution are prominent.

With ف د ي (F D Y), the relationship of giving that substitute to obtain release is more prominent.

This is why other Qur’anic verses describe a person wishing to use the whole earth in يفتدي به (yaftadī bihi): to secure their own release with it. What is offered functions as the substitute or exchange, while using it to obtain release is expressed by F D Y.

This is a difference in tendency, not an absolute boundary between the two words.

11. The narrowing of fidyah in later legal language

In legal literature, فدية (fidyah) gradually became a technical name for a range of substitutes and compensations relating to fasting, pilgrimage, and other provisions. This usage draws on certain Qur’anic occurrences, but the technical meaning of later systems should not be read back into every Qur’anic use of the word.

The Qur’an itself uses this family in connection with:

a captive,
a marital relationship,
a substitute slaughter-offering,
fasting,
charity,
a sacrificial rite,
gold,
all the earth’s possessions,
children and relatives,
and an attempt to escape punishment

across its different contexts.

This breadth shows that, at the root level, fidyah is not the name of a monetary sum, a religious tax, a particular charitable payment, or a specific sacrifice.

What remains constant is the relationship: something is placed in exchange for something else to make release, protection, or departure from the situation possible.

Translation guidance

English readers may encounter the Arabic term fidyah in a narrow legal sense. When retaining it in translation, some verses therefore require explanation so that this familiar technical usage does not obscure the word’s broader range.

وَفَدَيْنَاهُ بِذِبْحٍ عَظِيمٍ (wa-fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin ʿaẓīmin) can be translated “And We ransomed him with a great slaughter-offering,” or more freely, “And We released him through a great slaughter-offering.” If “released” is chosen, the relationship of substitution must remain clear in the root’s explanation.

تُفَادُوهُمْ (tufādūhum) in Al-Baqarah 2:85 means “you free them by ransom” or “you provide a ransom for their release.”

فِدَاءً (fidāʾan) in Muhammad 47:4 is “ransom” or “release in exchange for something.” “Indemnity” is too financial, and “prisoner exchange” too restrictive.

افْتَدَى بِهِ (iftadā bihi) means that someone offered something in exchange for their own release. Depending on context, it can be translated “ransomed himself with it” or “secured his release through it.”

فِدْيَة (fidyah) should not automatically become “a payment of money.” The sentence itself specifies the offering: food in one verse, fasting, charity, or a sacrificial rite in another, and a hypothetical offering for release in the Hereafter.

Conclusion

In early Arabic and the Qur’an, ف د ي (F D Y) rests on a relationship in which something is placed in another’s stead to protect or release the person or thing concerned. Ibn Fāris identifies this substitution for protection as the core of the root’s Qur’anic branch. Pre-Islamic poetry likewise uses fidāʾ for offering one’s life, property, children, or others in exchange for the person being protected.

Semitic linguistic evidence also confirms this field’s antiquity: redemption and release in exchange for something are recorded in several related languages.

The Qur’an does not use the root in only one setting. In Al-Baqarah 2:85, a captive is released through ransom; in Muhammad 47:4, fidāʾ contrasts with release without an exchange; in Al-Baqarah 2:229, a person gives something for their own release. In verses concerning the Hereafter, a person wishes to ransom themselves from punishment with gold, all the earth’s possessions, or even their closest relatives, but the offering is not accepted.

Fidyah is not restricted to money either. The Qur’an explicitly describes fidyah in the form of fasting, charity, or a sacrificial rite. Reducing the word to “an amount that is paid” narrows the root’s meaning.

As-Saffat 37:107 also shows that ransom is not identical with sacrifice. The Qur’an says فديناه بذبح (fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin): the slaughter-offering is what accomplishes the ransom, just as fasting can serve as fidyah elsewhere. F D Y names the relationship of release through an exchange, not a particular kind of offering.

Its connection with the preceding root أ س ر (A S R) is direct: a captive is someone captured and held under another’s control; fidāʾ is one way of bringing them out of that situation in exchange for something. The Qur’an itself places them together in أسارى تفادوهم (usārā tufādūhum).

A cautious synthesis of the root is:

F D Y: a family describing a person’s release or protection through placing something in exchange for them or their situation. What is offered may be property, a person, food, an action, or something else; it need not be money or a sacrifice. Fidāʾ and fidyah do not themselves determine the kind of offering; context does. The constant core is the relationship between substitution and release.
