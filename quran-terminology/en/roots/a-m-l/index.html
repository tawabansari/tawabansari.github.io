Qur’anic Root A M L

Hope, expectation, and looking toward the future: from distracting wishes to hope grounded in righteous conduct

Qur’anic and lexical forms

الْأَمَلُ
(al-amalu)
Qur’anic definite nominative noun; subject of yulhihim in Al-Ḥijr 15:3

أَمَلًا
(amalan)
Qur’anic indefinite accusative noun; specification in khayrun amalan, Al-Kahf 18:46

أَمَل / أَمْل / إِمْل
(amal / aml / iml)
Recorded lexical forms of hope; only the first occurs in the Qur’an

(āmāl)

The Arabic root A M L concerns hoping, looking toward the future, and expecting something desirable. Amal arises when someone wants something they have not yet attained. The object of hope may be near or distant, easy or difficult, attainable or uncertain.

Hope can motivate action, sustain perseverance, and keep a person open to the future. Yet it can also dominate the mind so completely that present reality, immediate responsibilities, and life’s limited opportunities are forgotten.

The Qur’an uses this root twice. In Al-Ḥijr 15:3, al-amalu occurs alongside eating and enjoying life, describing hope that distracts a group of people. In Al-Kahf 18:46, amalan describes the value of al-bāqiyātu al-ṣāliḥātu and what offers a better basis for hope.

These uses illuminate one another. The Qur’an does not condemn hope itself; it distinguishes hope that diverts a person from truth and action from hope connected with righteous deeds and a lasting outcome.

Arabic taʾammul belongs to the same lexical family but concerns close observation and considered examination. It matters for the root’s wider range, yet should not be substituted directly for amal in the Qur’anic verses.

1. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

A M L occurs twice in two Qur’anic verses, exclusively as the noun amal.

The first is al-amalu in Al-Ḥijr 15:3: a definite nominative noun functioning as the subject in wa-yulhihimu al-amalu.

The second is amalan in Al-Kahf 18:46: an indefinite accusative noun in wa-khayrun amalan.

Neither verse uses a verb from A M L.

Arabic amal denotes hope and expectation of something desirable. Aml and iml are also recorded for this meaning, with āmāl as the familiar plural.

Amala yaʾmulu concerns hoping or expecting good from something.

Ammala yuʾammilu likewise expresses placing hope in something or looking forward to it.

Āmil describes someone who hopes; muʾammal can describe a person or thing in whom or which hope is placed.

Arabic taʾammala also concerns close observation and examining something carefully.

These forms matter lexically, but the Qur’an uses only the noun amal.

The Qur’anic branch’s semantic core is:

Hope and expectation of attaining something desirable that has not yet occurred; looking toward the future while envisaging the possibility of attainment.

This definition alone does not determine whether someone’s hope is sound or mistaken, whether it will be fulfilled, or whether it encourages action or replaces it.

Those qualities come from the object of hope, the person’s conduct, and context.

2. Amal in early Arabic: hope and expectation

Amala khayrahu: expecting another person’s goodness

In early Arabic, amala khayrahu means hoping for the goodness or generosity expected from another person.

The person has not yet received that good, but looks forward to receiving it.

Someone may, for example, expect another person’s help. That hope may rest on knowledge of their generosity, or merely be a wish whose fulfillment is uncertain.

In either case, amal concerns the future.

If what a person expects is already in their possession, it is no longer an object of hope in that same sense. It is an existing benefit.

The distinction between possessing and hoping matters for understanding the word.

Amal does not name the benefit or outcome itself. It concerns looking forward to it.

Must hope concern something distant?

Some later lexical explanations, including a distinction transmitted from Al-Munāwī, associate amal especially with something whose attainment appears distant or difficult. This comparison alone cannot establish a fixed boundary in the language of the revelation period.

For example, someone facing a long journey may say they hope to reach their destination despite the considerable distance remaining.

Words such as ṭamaʿ have also been used for something closer and easier to obtain.

This is a recognized linguistic tendency, rather than an absolute rule.

Arabic amal can concern nearby things, while ṭamaʿ is not always restricted to outcomes that are certain or immediately at hand.

More significantly, amalan in Al-Kahf 18:46 concerns a value expected with the Lord. Hope can therefore concern an outcome not yet reached but worthy of hope in the Qur’an’s presentation.

Distance and uncertainty are consequently not necessary components of the root’s meaning.

Hope and expectation are not identical

“Expectation,” like Arabic intiẓār, can concern pleasant or unpleasant things.

Someone may await a friend’s arrival or expect a disturbing event.

Amal, in its familiar use, concerns expecting something the person regards as desirable.

“Hope” is therefore more precise than “expectation” alone.

Expectation nevertheless belongs to hope’s semantic structure: something not yet attained is envisaged in the future.

Amal can therefore be understood as hope accompanied by looking forward and expectation.

This does not mean that a hopeful person necessarily sits idle.

Hope may accompany effort and preparation, or take their place.

The word alone does not decide between these possibilities.

3. Taʾammul: close observation and considered examination

Early Arabic taʾammala al-shayʾa means looking closely and examining something to understand it better.

Taʾammul relates to tathabbut: avoiding hasty judgment and seeking clarity.

A person does more than simply see something: they attend to it, sustain their observation, and seek better knowledge of its reality or qualities.

An early poetic witness

A line attributed to Al-Marrār, identified in Tāj Al-ʿArūs as Al-Marrār ibn Saʿīd Al-Faqʿasī, reads as follows. He was an Umayyad poet; this should not be presented as securely dated pre-revelation verse:

تَأَمَّلْ مَا تَقُولُ وَكُنْتَ قِدْمًا
قُطَامِيًّا تَأَمُّلُهُ قَلِيلُ

taʾammal mā taqūlu wa-kunta qidman / quṭāmiyyan taʾammuluhu qalīlu

“Consider what you say; formerly you were like a hawk that gave little consideration.”

Quṭāmiyy refers to a bird of prey such as a falcon. Maqāyīs emphasizes the hawk’s ability to suffice with one glance, whereas the explanation transmitted from Thaʿlab recalls the person’s youthful impulsiveness. Little consideration should therefore not unconditionally be treated as praise of perceptiveness. Wa-kunta ḥayyan is also transmitted beside wa-kunta qidman.

Taʾammal at the line’s beginning commands careful consideration of speech before saying it.

The subject is not hope for the future, but care in understanding and judging speech.

The witness illustrates lexical taʾammul for careful examination, beyond later philosophical terminology. An Umayyad witness alone, however, does not establish a pre-revelation date.

Nor should this use be transferred to al-amal in Al-Ḥijr 15:3 or amalan in Al-Kahf 18:46.

Those verses concern hope, rather than the act of close examination.

How do hope and careful consideration relate?

Ibn Fāris treats hope and careful consideration under a branch concerning deliberation and expectation, while placing the sand ridge in a separate branch. This lexical analysis does not itself establish how every usage historically arose.

Hope can keep someone waiting for a time until something arrives.

Careful consideration can likewise involve sustained attention and avoiding hasty judgment.

This proximity is intelligible, but should not become a supposedly certain historical account deriving every use from a particular psychological behavior.

For Qur’anic study, it is enough to recognize amal and taʾammul as members of one lexical family with different meanings in use.

Forqan can likewise use “reflection” to describe considered examination of verses. That editorial title does not change amal’s meaning in its two Qur’anic occurrences.

4. Amīl: another Arabic branch

Early Arabic also records amīl for an elongated sand ridge or rise, particularly a mass distinct from the main body of dunes.

Its definition includes ḥablun mina al-ramli. Here ḥabl denotes an elongated sand ridge, rather than a rope made of sand.

The recorded plural is umul.

The lexical entry A M L therefore also contains a word concerning landforms.

Shared consonants alone cannot establish that hope arose from a sand ridge, or the ridge’s name from hope and expectation.

Early lexical explanations treat hope and careful consideration on one side, and the sand ridge on the other, as distinct semantic fields.

Āmāl, “hopes,” is the plural of amal and should not be confused with umul, “sand ridges.”

Neither amīl nor umul occurs in the Qur’an.

This branch matters for the history of Arabic, but not for directly interpreting the two Qur’anic verses.

5. Wa-yulhihimu al-amalu: hope that distracts

Al-Ḥijr 15:3 states:

ذَرْهُمْ يَأْكُلُوا وَيَتَمَتَّعُوا وَيُلْهِهِمُ الْأَمَلُ فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ

dharhum yaʾkulū wa-yatamattaʿū wa-yulhihimu al-amalu fa-sawfa yaʿlamūna

“Leave them to eat and enjoy themselves, and let hope distract them and make them heedless; they will come to know.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:3

In the muṣḥaf’s order, this is the root’s first Qur’anic occurrence. That statement alone makes no claim about chronological revelation order.

Al-amalu occurs in a sentence describing distraction and heedlessness.

Understanding it precisely requires distinguishing hope itself from the action attributed to it.

Amal is hope and expectation of something desirable in the future.

Yulhihim belongs to L H W: occupying or distracting people so that their attention is diverted from something else.

Distraction and heedlessness therefore do not come from A M L itself.

The verse concerns hope that distracts people in this particular situation.

Why is al-amalu the subject?

In wa-yulhihimu al-amalu, the verb yulhī is active.

Hum is the object pronoun. It refers to the people whom the opening command says to leave; the command’s direct addressee is Muḥammad, rather than those people.

Al-amalu is the subject: hope distracts them and makes them heedless.

This construction emphasizes hope’s role in their mental and behavioral condition.

It does not turn hope into an independent, willing being that leads people astray regardless of their choices.

Arabic can make an inner state or motive the subject of a verb.

Fear, sorrow, love, or desire can, for example, be expressed as causing behavior without having an independent personality or will.

Here, hope dominates people’s attention so completely that it diverts them from a truth they ought to consider.

Who is addressed by dharhum?

Dharhum is a singular imperative, addressed in the surah’s context to Muḥammad.

The preceding verse, Al-Ḥijr 15:2, concerns disbelievers who may at some time wish they had submitted to the truth.

The command dharhum, “leave them,” follows.

Muḥammad is therefore the command’s direct addressee; hum refers to the opponents being discussed.

This does not command everyone to abandon every misguided person or every person caught in false hopes forever, ceasing all invitation, goodwill, or assistance.

The verse concerns a particular context, a group of opponents, and the outcome of their conduct.

Extending the command to every human situation requires separate examination of other verses.

Are eating and enjoyment themselves condemned?

The verse says:

Yaʾkulū wa-yatamattaʿū: “let them eat and enjoy themselves.”

Eating and enjoying life can be legitimate and natural in various circumstances.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an itself discusses the earth’s provision and blessings and calls people to use them properly.

Yaʾkulū here therefore does not by itself make eating or enjoying life’s blessings reprehensible.

The condemned condition is one in which enjoyment and future hopes dominate people’s lives, making them heedless of truth and the consequences of their conduct.

Nor should tamattuʿ be restricted without evidence to debauchery or sexual conduct. It encompasses a broader field of benefiting and enjoyment.

Conduct’s value depends on how benefits are enjoyed and their place within life.

Does al-amalu here mean wishing for a long life?

Early explanations often connect the verse with ṭūl al-amal: long-reaching hopes that distract people from death, reckoning, or responsibility.

That interpretation is understandable in context.

Someone may be so confident about a distant future that they continually postpone today’s responsibilities.

They may assume there will always be time to improve their conduct, restore others’ rights, or reconsider their life.

Al-Ḥijr 15:3 itself, however, does not contain the expression ṭūl al-amal.

It says wa-yulhihimu al-amalu.

The direct criticism therefore concerns distraction and heedlessness through hope.

Long-reaching hopes can cause such heedlessness, but they are not al-amal’s only lexical meaning.

Even a short-term hope can divert someone from an immediate responsibility.

Conversely, hope for a distant future need not be heedless when accompanied by effort and responsibility.

Fa-sawfa yaʿlamūna: knowledge deferred to the future

The verse concludes:

Fa-sawfa yaʿlamūna: “they will come to know.”

This announces future knowledge.

Al-Ḥijr 15:4 and Al-Ḥijr 15:5 then discuss the fate of settlements and communities’ appointed terms.

Their sequence shows that present enjoyment and opportunity do not prove they are endless.

Sawfa yaʿlamūna does not, however, yield the precise date of an event.

Nor does it mean that every heedless person must suffer a misfortune in the immediate future.

The verse warns that their outcome and conduct’s consequences will become clear, without specifying the exact interval in this sentence.

6. Wa-khayrun amalan: a better basis for hope in Al-Kahf

Al-Kahf 18:46 states:

الْمَالُ وَالْبَنُونَ زِينَةُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَالْبَاقِيَاتُ الصَّالِحَاتُ خَيْرٌ عِنْدَ رَبِّكَ ثَوَابًا وَخَيْرٌ أَمَلًا

al-mālu wa-l-banūna zīnatu al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-l-bāqiyātu al-ṣāliḥātu khayrun ʿinda rabbika thawāban wa-khayrun amalā

“Wealth and children are the adornment of worldly life; but lasting righteous deeds are better with your Lord in reward, and better as a basis for hope.”

Al-Kahf 18:46

In the muṣḥaf’s order, this is A M L’s second and final Qur’anic occurrence.

Unlike Al-Ḥijr 15:3, hope is not expressed within a sentence of condemnation here.

The Qur’an says that al-bāqiyātu al-ṣāliḥātu are better in reward and in hope.

Hope can therefore be attached to something valuable and worthy.

This prevents defining amal as inherently negative.

Al-mālu wa-l-banūna zīnatu al-ḥayāti al-dunyā

The verse begins by calling wealth and children the adornment of worldly life.

Zīnah concerns beauty, adornment, and what makes life desirable and pleasant.

The verse does not say that wealth and children are inherently evil.

Nor does it say that everyone with greater wealth or children is morally more misguided than others.

Wealth can provide a means of helping others, meeting needs, and doing good.

Children too can bring love, responsibility, and goodness into life.

Possessing these adornments alone, however, is not the final measure of a person’s value and outcome.

The verse introduces another criterion: what is righteous and lasting.

Al-bāqiyātu al-ṣāliḥātu: what offers a better basis for hope?

Bāqiyāt belongs to B Q Y; ṣāliḥāt belongs to Ṣ L Ḥ.

These feminine plural descriptions can refer to righteous actions and deeds.

Early explanations name examples such as remembrance of The God, ṣalāh, and familiar formulas of remembrance.

The expression’s meaning need not be restricted to a few verbal formulas or a particular rite.

Righteous action has a broad Qur’anic range, including worship, honesty, caring for people in need, justice, fulfilling covenants, and other good conduct.

Here, al-bāqiyātu al-ṣāliḥātu therefore concerns righteous deeds whose value and outcome remain with the Lord.

It does not make everything that lasts longer in this world necessarily righteous.

Lastingness and righteousness are two descriptions, both present in the phrase.

Khayrun ʿinda rabbika thawāban

The first comparison concerns reward.

Thawāban belongs to TH W B and here concerns deeds’ outcome and reward.

The Qur’an says lasting righteous deeds are better with the Lord in reward.

This concerns what a person receives for their action.

Wa-khayrun amalan follows.

The verse therefore does more than discuss reward; it separately expresses the matter of hope.

Wa-khayrun amalan: an outcome and expecting that outcome

Amalan is accusative here and can be analyzed as a specification, tamyīz, of khayr: better with respect to hope.

Thawāban and amalan occur in parallel constructions.

The first expresses superiority in reward.

The second expresses superiority in the hope a person may place in it.

This distinction matters.

Reward is the outcome envisaged for an action.

Hope is a person’s outlook and expectation toward that outcome.

Amalan therefore cannot simply be translated as another thawāban.

Rendering wa-khayrun amalan merely “and they have greater reward” eliminates one of the sentence’s two distinct concepts.

“Better as a basis for hope” preserves both levels.

Does the verse promise that righteous action fulfills every wish?

No.

The verse does not guarantee that performing righteous deeds will secure every personal desire.

Nor does it assign a specified reward to everything someone considers righteous, irrespective of the deed’s actual nature.

Its subject is the value of righteous deeds with the Lord.

Khayrun amalan therefore concerns the greater worth of hoping for such deeds’ outcome, rather than guaranteeing every worldly wish.

Nevertheless, this hope should not become a merely subjective feeling unrelated to action.

The verse itself connects the better basis for hope with al-bāqiyātu al-ṣāliḥātu.

Hope in this context is therefore not detached from conduct.

7. Relating the verses: distracting hope and hope accompanied by action

The two Qur’anic uses of amal can be examined through a clear contrast.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:3, hope distracts people and makes them heedless.

In Al-Kahf 18:46, lasting righteous deeds are presented as a better basis for hope.

Amal’s lexical meaning remains the same in both: hope and expectation of something desirable in the future.

The object of hope and its effect on human conduct differ.

In the first verse, hope dominates minds and conduct so fully that eating and present enjoyment, accompanied by distraction and heedlessness, occupy people’s attention.

In the second, hope connects with something for which a person acts righteously, whose outcome is valuable with the Lord.

The Qur’an therefore does not prohibit hope for the future.

Conversely, not every hope is good and constructive merely because it feels pleasant.

Hope’s value becomes clear through its object and its relationship with recognizing truth and acting.

Hope that replaces action

Sometimes people construct a desirable future in their minds but do nothing to attain it.

They may repeatedly tell themselves that one day they will improve their conduct, restore a right to its holder, or perform a good deed.

Each time, however, they postpone it to an indefinite future.

Hope then becomes a means of delaying action instead of motivating it.

This accords with the warning in Al-Ḥijr 15:3, where hope distracts a person.

Not every long-term plan or future wish, however, should count as such heedlessness.

Someone may plan for years of learning, raising a child, cultivating the earth, or helping others while also fulfilling present responsibilities.

A goal’s lengthy timescale does not itself signify heedlessness.

The problem arises when imagining the future replaces understanding and acting today.

Hope that motivates action

Al-Kahf 18:46 connects hope with lasting righteous conduct.

That connection shows that hope can motivate action.

Someone may hope for the outcome of their good deed even without seeing all its effects immediately.

Helping a person in need, repairing a damaged relationship, or respecting a right can involve hope for a result not yet apparent.

Hope alone, however, does not establish an action’s righteousness.

Someone may also hope for the outcome of wrongdoing.

Hope alone is therefore not a criterion of rightness.

Ṣāliḥāt in Al-Kahf 18:46 is essential to recognizing the deeds’ value.

The worthier hope is therefore hope connected with righteous action and a valuable outcome.

8. Distinguishing A M L from related roots and concepts

A M L (amal) and R J W (rajāʾ)

Study of R J W

Arabic rajāʾ concerns hope and expectation of attaining something.

It therefore overlaps extensively with amal.

Some later lexical explanations associate amal especially with distant or difficult attainment, while rajāʾ can concern an expectation whose likelihood appears clearer to the person.

This distinction should not be absolute.

Qur’anic rajāʾ can concern The God’s mercy, meeting Him, and righteous deeds’ outcome. Such hopes need not concern something immediate or nearby.

Conversely, amal in Al-Kahf 18:46 concerns worthy hope for righteous deeds’ outcome.

It is more precise to treat them as neighboring words with contextual differences, rather than fixed stages where one is always mistaken and the other always correct.

A M L (amal) and M N Y (tamannī)

Study of M N Y

Arabic tamannī concerns wishing for or desiring something.

Amal too concerns something desirable in the future.

In some uses, tamannī may express simply wanting or imagining an outcome without a clear possibility of attaining it.

Amal more strongly suggests looking forward to fulfillment.

Nevertheless, not every wish is groundless, nor every hope firmly supported.

People may have realistic wishes or hope for something impossible.

Actual usage gives the words overlapping semantic territory.

Al-Kahf 18:46 connects hope with righteous conduct, but that connection comes from context. A M L is not necessarily founded on action in every Arabic use.

A M L (amal) and Ṭ M ʿ (ṭamaʿ)

Study of Ṭ M ʿ

Arabic ṭamaʿ also concerns desiring and looking forward to something.

Translating it as English “greed” gives it a predominantly negative moral force, suggesting excessive desire.

Its Arabic and Qur’anic range, however, is broader.

Qur’anic ṭamaʿ can also concern hope for The God’s mercy and forgiveness.

The difference cannot therefore be reduced to “hope is good, while ṭamaʿ is always bad.”

Some later lexical explanations associate ṭamaʿ with expecting something nearer and amal with expecting something more distant.

That boundary is not universal either.

The object of desire, a person’s conduct, and the sentence’s context determine the moral force of each use.

A M L (amal) and R GH B (raghbah)

Study of R GH B

Raghbah concerns inclination or turning toward something.

Amal concerns expecting to attain something desirable that has not yet occurred.

Someone can desire something even when already possessing it.

Hope, however, usually concerns what has not yet been attained.

Conversely, hope for an outcome can accompany the desire to attain it.

The concepts are close without being identical.

A M L (amal) and Y A S (yaʾs)

Study of Y A S

Yaʾs concerns despair and losing the expectation of attaining something desirable.

In that respect, it contrasts with amal.

This does not mean that every hopeful person is right, or that everyone lacking hope of fulfilling a desire is morally mistaken.

Someone may abandon an improper goal; giving up that hope is not necessarily blameworthy.

Likewise, hope for The God’s mercy may motivate someone to improve their conduct.

The moral value of hope and despair depends on their objects and the person’s circumstances.

A M L (amal) and GH R R (ghurūr)

Study of GH R R

Qur’anic ghurūr concerns deception or being deceived.

Hope may accompany deception, as when someone treats desirable possibilities as certainties and ignores contrary evidence.

Hope itself, however, is neither falsehood nor deception.

Al-Ḥijr 15:3 explicitly mentions distraction and heedlessness through hope.

Al-Kahf 18:46 connects hope with righteous deeds.

Amal therefore cannot generally be translated “self-deception.”

Self-deception may be one form of misguided hope, but is not a necessary element of the root’s meaning.

A M L (amal) and A J L (ajal)

Study of A J L

Qur’anic ajal concerns an appointed term or temporal limit.

Amal is someone’s hope and expectation about the future.

The two can be related in human life.

A person may have hopes for the future without knowing when life’s opportunity will end.

After the warning about distracting hope, Al-Ḥijr 15:4 and Al-Ḥijr 15:5 discuss communities’ appointed terms.

This sequence clearly connects human hopes with limited opportunity.

Amal itself, however, does not mean respite, nor ajal hope.

One expresses human expectation; the other concerns time and an appointed term.

A M L (amal) and A M D (amad)

Study of A M D

Amad belongs to A M D and concerns limit, duration, and temporal interval.

Amal belongs to A M L and concerns hoping for the future.

A goal may require a long time to attain, but hope for it is not that duration itself.

Al-amad in Al-Ḥadīd 57:16 concerns a period growing long.

Al-amal in Al-Ḥijr 15:3 concerns hope that distracts people.

The words’ similar appearance should not merge their meanings.

A M L (amal) and ʿ M L (ʿamal)

Study of ʿ M L

The roots differ only in their first consonant, but have distinct meanings.

Amal begins with hamzah and concerns hope and expectation.

ʿAmal begins with ʿayn and concerns work and conduct.

Al-Kahf 18:46 connects the concepts by presenting righteous deeds as a better basis for hope.

That semantic connection does not make the words share a root.

Hoping is an expectant state; acting is doing something.

Hope may accompany action or remain detached from it.

A M L (amal) and M L W (imlāʾ)

Study of M L W

The Qur’an also uses expressions such as wa-umlī lahum.

These belong to a lexical family concerning granting respite and extending time, rather than A M L, “hope.”

Arabic imlāʾ also has another use: speaking words for someone to write down, or dictation.

The resemblance of amal and umlī should therefore not be treated as evidence of a shared root.

Granting someone time may encourage hope for the future, but that behavioral or contextual relationship does not establish common derivation.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal natural English equivalent of amal is “hope.”

“Wish” may also fit some sentences, but can emphasize wanting something more than expecting its attainment.

“Hope” better preserves that expectant aspect.

“Daydreaming,” “heedlessness,” “deception,” and “groundless wishing” are not constant equivalents of the root.

Such meanings can be explained only with suitable context and supporting evidence.

Al-Ḥijr 15:3

For wa-yulhihimu al-amalu, the proposed translation is:

“And let hope distract them and make them heedless.”

In the full verse:

“Leave them to eat and enjoy themselves, and let hope distract them and make them heedless; they will come to know.”

This distinguishes hope from distraction and heedlessness.

Arabic al-amalu is singular and definite; retaining the singular therefore follows the wording more closely:

“And let hope distract them and make them heedless.”

In natural English, “their hopes” can also refer to the range of wishes and expectations dominating their minds.

A base translation preserving the Arabic structure more closely should prefer the singular.

Al-Kahf 18:46

For wa-khayrun amalan, the proposed translation is:

“And better as a basis for hope.”

In the full verse:

“Wealth and children are the adornment of worldly life; but lasting righteous deeds are better with your Lord in reward, and better as a basis for hope.”

This distinguishes thawāban from amalan.

“And better in hope” is also close to the Arabic construction, but may leave the object of hope unclear in English.

“And a worthier ground for hope” is another way to convey the meaning.

“And they have greater reward” is insufficient for this second clause because it omits hope.

Non-Qur’anic forms

Amala khayrahu can be translated “hoped for his goodness.”

Ammala al-shayʾa can be “placed hope in it” or “hoped to attain it.”

Āmil is naturally “hopeful” or “one who hopes”; muʾammal can be “hoped for” or “that in which hope is placed,” according to context.

Taʾammala can be translated “looked closely” or “examined carefully.”

In English, “reflection” can describe thought, close examination, and sometimes pausing before a decision. This relates to the early branch of care and deliberation but does not directly translate Qur’anic amal.

10. Conclusion

A M L’s principal branch concerns hope, looking forward, and expecting something desirable in the future.

Early Arabic records amal, aml, and iml for hope, alongside forms such as amala, ammala, and āmil in the same field.

Taʾammul concerns close observation and considered examination. It is historically known in Arabic, but should not be transferred into amal’s two Qur’anic uses.

Another branch has amīl, an elongated sand ridge. Its historical connection with hope or careful consideration is not established; shared consonants alone should not produce an invented common origin.

The Qur’an uses amal only twice.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:3, it occurs in a sentence describing some opponents’ distraction and heedlessness. The criticism concerns hope that diverts people from truth and responsibility, rather than every kind of hope.

In Al-Kahf 18:46, amalan occurs positively: lasting righteous deeds are presented as better with respect to hope.

The second occurrence is decisive for defining the root. Amal cannot inherently mean false wishing, self-deception, or heedlessness, since the Qur’an also uses it to express positive value.

Conversely, hope’s worth in Al-Kahf 18:46 does not come from hope alone. It concerns al-bāqiyātu al-ṣāliḥātu and their value with the Lord.

The word’s lexical meaning therefore remains stable across the verses, while its object and effect differ.

Hope may occupy someone with an imagined future and prevent action today. It can also accompany understanding, responsibility, and righteous conduct.

The desired object’s distance or nearness is not a fixed part of the root’s meaning either. Some Arabic uses favor distant objects, but this tendency cannot become an exceptionless rule.

Forqan’s final definition of A M L:

“Hope and looking forward to attaining something desirable in the future: an expectation whose desired fulfillment has not yet occurred. The Qur’an uses amal once in the context of human distraction and heedlessness, and once in presenting lasting righteous deeds as a better basis for hope. Hope itself is therefore neither virtue nor sin; its value comes from its object and relationship with recognizing truth, responsibility, and human conduct. Taʾammul, another branch of the same lexical family, concerns close observation and considered examination, rather than the direct meaning of the two Qur’anic occurrences.”
