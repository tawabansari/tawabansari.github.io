The Qur’anic Root K TH B (ك ث ب)

Gathering, heaping, and forming a mound of sand

Qur’anic forms

كَثِيبًا

1. Meaning in Arabic before the Qur’an

ك ث ب concerns gathering, heaping, pouring into one place, and proximity. كَثِيب in early Arabic names a mound or dune of accumulated sand.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Tarafah ibn al-ʿAbd reads:

وَإِذَا قَامَتْ تَدَاعَى قَاصِفٌ مَالَ مِنْ أَعْلَى كَثِيبٍ مُنْقَعِرْ

When she rises, it is as though a mass of sand collapses, leaning and slipping down from the top of a dune.

Describing his beloved’s body and movement, the poet evokes a dune from which some sand separates and falls.

كَثِيب names the dune; falling and separation are supplied by the line’s other words.

This poetic attribution indicates a longstanding literary image of a dune and its falling grains, though the precise date of composition is not independently known.

Arabic lexicons record كَثَبَ الشَّيْءَ for gathering and heaping something in one place. Its objects need not be sand; earth, grain, dates, and other materials are possible.

Ibn Faris identifies gathering and proximity within this family. Bringing scattered parts into one place helps explain their semantic relationship.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • كَثَبَ ـ يَكْثِبُ ـ يَكْثُبُ: gathered, heaped, or poured.
  • كَثْب: gathering and heaping; also proximity.
  • كَثِيب: a dune or mound of sand.
  • كُثُب، أَكْثِبَة، كُثْبَان: plurals of kathīb.
  • كُثْبَة: a small collected amount, such as milk, dates, or earth.
  • كَاثِب: one who gathers.
  • مَكْثُوب: gathered or heaped.
  • اِنْكَثَبَ: gathered or accumulated in one place.
  • أَكْثَبَ: came near; in some uses, game came within the hunter’s reach.

عَنْ كَثَبٍ means “from nearby” or “at close range.”

Form examined in the Qur’an:

كَثِيبًا

The Qur’an uses only the noun كَثِيب from this family. Its verbs and plural forms do not occur.

3. Semantic core and physical image

In the branch associated with كَثِيب, the focus is on material gathering and accumulating in one place.

When sand is poured into one location, grains settle on one another and form a mass. Continued accumulation may produce a raised mound.

Desert dunes offer a clear example. Wind transports sand grains and, under suitable conditions, gathers them together.

Unlike a rocky mountain, a sand mound consists of grains that can move relative to one another, allowing parts of it to collapse as conditions change.

Collapse, however, is not inherent in كَثِيب. A dune can be relatively stable. Its movement or falling must be expressed by another word or description.

The Qur’an makes that distinction in كَثِيبًا مَهِيلًا.

The proximity branch appears in expressions such as عَنْ كَثَبٍ. Gathering and nearness are semantically related, but their historical order is not established.

4. Qur’anic use

كَثِيبًا مَهِيلًا: mountains becoming a mound of loose, falling sand

The verse depicts a great transformation of the earth and mountains.

In ordinary experience, a mountain represents mass, hardness, and stability. A sand mound, by contrast, consists of grains that can shift and slide over one another.

Three words express distinct aspects of the transformation:

  • تَرْجُفُ, from ر ج ف: the shaking of earth and mountains.
  • كَثِيبًا, from ك ث ب: a mound of sand.
  • مَهِيلًا, from ه ي ل: poured out, loose, and falling.

The earth and mountains shake, and formerly firm mountains become a heap of loose, falling material.

Kathīb identifies the kind of mass; mahīl describes its state and quality.

Kathīb alone would evoke a dune. Mahīl adds that the mass is loose and liable to fall.

Connections with other Qur’anic descriptions of mountains

Al-Waqiʿah describes another aspect of their transformation:

The first verse describes mountains being crushed; the second their becoming scattered particles.

Al-Qariʿah also states:

Carded wool evokes the loss of the mountains’ former density and cohesion.

Al-Muzzammil 73:14 offers falling sand, Al-Waqiʿah 56:6 scattered dust, and Al-Qariʿah 101:5 carded wool.

These images share the loss of the mountains’ previous stability and structure.

Sand, dust, and wool remain distinct images. These verses specify neither a precise chronological sequence nor a physical mechanism for the transformations.

Kathīb should therefore not be translated as dust, wool, molten material, or a specific geological process. Each word’s distinct image should remain visible.

5. Morphology and syntax

كَثِيب is a noun in the pattern فَعِيل. In Al-Muzzammil 73:14, the indefinite accusative كَثِيبًا is the predicate of كَانَتْ.

الْجِبَالُ is the subject of كَانَتْ, and مَهِيلًا modifies كَثِيبًا.

Although الْجِبَالُ is plural, its predicate كَثِيبًا is singular. Arabic permits a collection to be described through a singular noun expressing its state or kind.

The singular does not establish that every mountain on earth gathers in one place to form a single dune. The sentence’s focus is the mountains’ transformed condition, not the number of resulting heaps.

The perfect كَانَتْ occurs within a future description and expresses their state on that day.

6. Comparison with related roots

ك ث ب (كَثْب) and ه ي ل (هَيْل)

Kathīb identifies a sand mound; mahīl describes its looseness and falling condition.

Sand gathering into a mound can form a kathīb. Its grains falling and sliding over one another can be described through ه ي ل.

This distinction matters particularly in كَثِيبًا مَهِيلًا: one word names the mass, the other describes it.

ك ث ب (كَثْب) and ج م ع (جَمْع)

جَمْع means bringing separate things together.

Kathb overlaps with this, but kathīb specifically names a sand mound or dune.

Every dune comprises gathered sand grains, but not every gathered collection is a kathīb.

ك ث ب (كَثْب) and ج ب ل (جَبَل)

Jabal names a mountain; kathīb names a dune or sand mound.

Both can rise above the ground, but Al-Muzzammil 73:14 foregrounds their different structure and physical state.

Mountains known for stability become loose sand. Rendering both terms simply as “mountain” or “hill” loses part of this transformation.

ك ث ب (كَثْب) and ب س س (بَسّ)

Bass in Al-Waqiʿah 56:5 concerns crushing the mountains into pieces.

Kathīb in Al-Muzzammil 73:14 names the mass they become.

One expresses the action changing their structure; the other identifies a described form of that change.

ك ث ب (كَثْب) and ب ث ث (بَثّ)

ب ث ث concerns spreading and scattering.

Kathīb evokes parts gathered in one place; مُنْبَثّ in Al-Waqiʿah 56:6 describes dispersed particles.

The images contrast gathering with dispersal, although both describe the mountains’ transformation.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Arabic word or expressionSuggested rendering
كَثَبَgathered; heaped
كَثْبgathering; accumulation; proximity
كَثِيبsand mound; dune
كُثْبَانdunes
عَنْ كَثَبٍfrom nearby
كَثِيبًا مَهِيلًاa mound of loose, falling sand

“A mound of loose, falling sand” suits Al-Muzzammil 73:14 because it conveys both kathīb and its description mahīl.

Persian and Dari can likewise use «توده‌ای از ریگ ریزان» for this image.

“A heap of earth” or “scattered dust” alone does not fully preserve the specific sand-dune image.

Conclusion

ك ث ب concerns gathering, heaping, and proximity. Kathīb belongs to the accumulation branch and names a sand mound or dune.

Al-Muzzammil 73:14 uses it for the mountains’ transformation on the Day of Resurrection.

In كَثِيبًا مَهِيلًا, kathīb identifies the sand mound, while mahīl describes its loose, falling condition.

The central image transforms mountains, symbols of hardness and stability, into sand whose parts no longer possess their earlier firmness and cohesion.
