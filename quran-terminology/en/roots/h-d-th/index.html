The Qur’anic Root Ḥ D TH

From Coming into Being to Speech That Is Recounted

Forms examined:

حَدِيث (ḥadīth):

speech, discourse, a report, or a narrative;
أَحَادِيث (aḥādīth):

reports, narratives, or matters recounted;
حَدَّثَ (ḥaddatha):

he spoke, reported, or recounted;
أَحْدَثَ (aḥdatha):

he brought something into being or introduced it;
مُحْدَث (muḥdath):

newly brought into being or newly received.

In contemporary Persian, “hadith” is usually understood as the name for a body of reports attributed to the Prophet. In the Qur’an, however, the word does not yet have this restricted technical meaning. The Qur’an calls everyday speech, private conversation, the report of an event, accounts of earlier peoples, and even the Qur’an itself “ḥadīth.”

To understand this root’s Qur’anic meaning, we must first recover its natural breadth, then distinguish it from the terms that developed in later periods within hadith studies, jurisprudence, and theology.

Part One: Etymology and the Basic Image

1. The Foundational Meaning

In Arabic, the root “Ḥ D TH” denotes something coming into being that was not previously present in that form, time, or situation.

“حَدَثَ الأمرُ” (ḥadatha al-amru) means that something happened or came into being. “حادث” (ḥādith) is something that has occurred, while “حَديث” (ḥadīth), used adjectivally, means something new in contrast to something old.

From this same field of meaning, “ḥadīth” came to be used for speech or a report that emerges in the process of speaking and enters the listener’s awareness. As it is recounted, speech becomes a linguistic event: its parts appear one after another and make a subject present to the listener.

The root therefore has two principal fields:

  • happening and coming into being;
  • speaking and recounting something that is brought before a listener.

2. A Pre-Islamic Witness

One of the odes attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays contains these lines:

أَلَااِنْعِمْ صَبَاحًا أَيُّهَا الرَّبْعُ وَانْطِقِ

Alā inʿim ṣabāḥan ayyuhā al-rabʿu wa-nṭiqi

وَحَدِّثْ حَدِيثَ الرَّكْبِ إِنْ شِئْتَ وَاصْدُقِ

Wa-ḥaddith ḥadītha al-rakbi in shiʾta wa-ṣduqi

The poet addresses the silent dwelling place and asks it to recount the “account of the caravan.” The same verse contains both the verb “حَدِّثْ” (ḥaddith) and the noun “حَدِيث” (ḥadīth):

  • “حَدِّثْ” (ḥaddith): recount;
  • “حَدِيثَ الرَّكْب” (ḥadītha al-rakb): the report or story of the caravan.

This witness shows that before the religious technical meaning became established, “ḥadīth” was used in Arabic in the general sense of speech, news, and reporting.

3. The Relationship Between an Event and Speech

The relationship between “coming into being” and “speaking” can be explained in this way: an event happens in the world, and its report comes into being in language. When the “account of Moses” is recounted, the historical event itself does not happen again; rather, a report of it becomes present in the listener’s own time.

In this respect, ḥadīth can make an absent event present to the listener through language. This explanation makes the semantic relationship between event and report tangible, but it must not become a definitive claim about the psychology of pre-Islamic Arabs or the historical origin of every derivative.

The semantic core can be expressed concisely as follows:

Something coming into being or being brought into being; and, in language, speaking and recounting speech or a report that becomes present to a listener.

4. Morphological Structures

حَدِيث (ḥadīth)

“حَدِيث” (ḥadīth) follows the pattern “فَعِيل” (faʿīl). In Arabic it can function as an adjective meaning “new,” but in the Qur’an it is used chiefly as a noun meaning speech, discourse, a report, or a narrative.

أَحَادِيث (aḥādīth)

“أَحَادِيث” (aḥādīth) is a plural form denoting reports, narratives, accounts, or matters recounted. Context determines whether its subject is a dream, an event, or the history of a people.

حَدَّثَ (ḥaddatha)

Form II, the tafʿīl pattern in “حَدَّثَ” (ḥaddatha), foregrounds the act of speaking and recounting. The content of the speech may be expressed as an object or introduced with the preposition “بـ” (bi-).

أَحْدَثَ (aḥdatha)

Form IV, the ifʿāl pattern in “أَحْدَثَ” (aḥdatha), denotes bringing something into being or introducing it. Its object may be a matter, a situation, or a reminder.

مُحْدَث (muḥdath)

“مُحْدَث” (muḥdath) is the passive participle of form IV: something newly brought into being, newly introduced, or newly received by a listener.

The basic verb “حَدَثَ” (ḥadatha) and forms such as “حادث” (ḥādith), “حادثة” (ḥādithah), “حدوث” (ḥudūth), and “حداثة” (ḥadāthah) do not occur in the Qur’an, although they are common in Arabic and Persian.

5. The Difference Between “Ḥ D TH” and “B D AYN (B D ʿ)”

Ḥ D TH, represented by “حَدَثَ” (ḥadatha), denotes something happening or coming into being. B D AYN (B D ʿ), represented by “بَدَعَ” (badaʿa), places greater emphasis on bringing something into being without a previous model.

Everything unprecedented is new, but not everything new is necessarily unprecedented. Speech presented to a listener today may be “muḥdath” even if its content existed previously.

For this reason, “مُحْدَث” (muḥdath) in Qur’anic verses should not be translated as “heretically innovative.” Newness and bidʿah, innovation in the religious sense, are not identical concepts.

Part Two: Freeing the Word from Later Technical Meanings

6. From a General Meaning to a Technical Designation

In the Qur’an, “ḥadīth” is a general term for speech, reports, and narratives. As the transmission and compilation of reports attributed to the Prophet expanded, however, the word gradually became a technical designation for such reports:

A report that attributes a statement, action, approval, or characteristic to the Prophet.

This specialization can be studied within the history of language, but it must not be projected backward and imposed on every Qur’anic use.

The Qur’an does not use expressions such as “الحديث النبوي” (al-ḥadīth al-nabawī, Prophetic hadith), “كتب الحديث” (kutub al-ḥadīth, hadith books), “علم الحديث” (ʿilm al-ḥadīth, hadith studies), “أهل الحديث” (ahl al-ḥadīth, the people of hadith), or “صحيح الحديث” (ṣaḥīḥ al-ḥadīth, sound hadith). These terms belong to periods after the Qur’an.

7. The Value of Hadith Reports for Studying Language

In the method adopted in this study, hadith reports are neither a source of religious legislation nor the determining authority for the meaning of Qur’anic verses. Nevertheless, they can be examined as evidence for the history of word usage in Arabic.

Such a study must distinguish two questions:

  • What does the word “ḥadīth” mean within a transmitted report?
  • How does the Qur’an use the word “ḥadīth”?

The answer to the first question cannot replace the answer to the second. The later technical use of hadith does not cancel its semantic breadth in the Qur’an.

8. “Hadith” and “Sunnah”

Ḥ D TH, as in “حَدِيث” (ḥadīth), and S N N, as in “سُنَّة” (sunnah), are not synonymous:

  • ḥadīth is speech, a report, or a narrative;
  • sunnah is a way, practice, or pattern that has continued over time.

A hadith may make a claim about a sunnah, but a report is not identical to the reality it reports. The existence of a narrative does not in itself establish that the reported behavior actually occurred, belonged to the Prophet, or was prescribed as a general law.

Conflating hadith and sunnah erases the distinction between a “report” and a “historical practice.”

9. The Designation “Hadith” Is Not Proof of Authority

Within the Qur’an’s lexical logic, “ḥadīth” is simply a kind of speech or report. Establishing its authority requires other criteria.

The Qur’an speaks in these terms:

  • “أَصْدَقُ ... حَدِيثًا” (aṣdaqu ... ḥadīthan): most truthful in speech;
  • “أَحْسَنَ الْحَدِيثِ” (aḥsana al-ḥadīth): the best discourse;
  • “حَدِيثًا يُفْتَرَىٰ” (ḥadīthan yuftarā): a report that is fabricated;
  • “لَهْوَ الْحَدِيثِ” (lahwa al-ḥadīth): diverting discourse;
  • “يُكَذِّبُ بِهَٰذَا الْحَدِيثِ” (yukadhdhibu bi-hādhā al-ḥadīth): denies this discourse.

Descriptions such as most truthful, best, susceptible to fabrication, and diverting show that the designation “ḥadīth” itself does not deliver a final judgment on the content.

Part Three: The Qur’an as Discourse Among Discourses

10. The Neutrality of the Word “Ḥadīth”

“Ḥadīth” is not inherently true or false, divine or human, beneficial or harmful. The word simply names speech, a report, or a narrative. Its value is determined by the speaker, content, purpose, and context.

In the Qur’an, ḥadīth may be:

  • the speech of The God;
  • the Qur’an itself;
  • an account of prophets and earlier peoples;
  • an ordinary conversation;
  • private speech;
  • discourse that entertains and misleads;
  • a report that can be fabricated.

Translating “ḥadīth” with one fixed equivalent in every verse is therefore inaccurate.

11. The Most Truthful and the Best Discourse

The Qur’an says:

«وَمَنْ أَصْدَقُ مِنَ اللَّهِ حَدِيثًا»

Wa-man aṣdaqu mina Allāhi ḥadīthan

An-Nisa 4:87

“حَدِيثًا” (ḥadīthan) here refers to speech or discourse:

Who is more truthful in speech than The God?

In this verse, the truthfulness of the ḥadīth comes from its speaker, not from the word “ḥadīth” itself. Ḥadīth names the speech, while “أصدق” (aṣdaq, more truthful) specifies its quality.

Another verse states:

«اللَّهُ نَزَّلَ أَحْسَنَ الْحَدِيثِ كِتَابًا مُتَشَابِهًا مَثَانِيَ»

Allāhu nazzala aḥsana al-ḥadīthi kitāban mutashābihan mathāniya

Az-Zumar 39:23

Here the Qur’an itself is called “أَحْسَنَ الْحَدِيثِ” (aḥsana al-ḥadīth): the best speech or the finest discourse.

The continuation of the verse calls this discourse “كِتَابًا” (kitāban), a book. Thus, in Qur’anic language, ḥadīth is not necessarily an oral report; a revealed book can also be ḥadīth.

12. “هَذَا الْحَدِيثِ” (hādhā al-ḥadīth): This Discourse

In several verses, the expression “this discourse” refers to the Qur’anic message itself:

«إِنْ لَمْ يُؤْمِنُوا بِهَٰذَا الْحَدِيثِ أَسَفًا»

In lam yuʾminū bi-hādhā al-ḥadīthi asafan

Al-Kahf 18:6

«أَفَمِنْ هَٰذَا الْحَدِيثِ تَعْجَبُونَ»

A-fa-min hādhā al-ḥadīthi taʿjabūn

An-Najm 53:59

«أَفَبِهَٰذَا الْحَدِيثِ أَنْتُمْ مُدْهِنُونَ»

A-fa-bi-hādhā al-ḥadīthi antum mudhinūn

Al-Waqiah 56:81

«فَذَرْنِي وَمَنْ يُكَذِّبُ بِهَٰذَا الْحَدِيثِ»

Fa-dharnī wa-man yukadhdhibu bi-hādhā al-ḥadīth

Al-Qalam 68:44

In these verses, “هذا الحديث” (hādhā al-ḥadīth) refers to the message that its audience hears, finds astonishing, treats with indifference, or regards as false.

Thus “this discourse,” in the Qur’an’s own language, can name the Qur’an as revealed speech.

13. “فَبِأَيِّ حَدِيثٍ بَعْدَهُ يُؤْمِنُونَ” (fa-bi-ayyi ḥadīthin baʿdahu yuʾminūn)

The Qur’an asks:

«فَبِأَيِّ حَدِيثٍ بَعْدَهُ يُؤْمِنُونَ»

Fa-bi-ayyi ḥadīthin baʿdahu yuʾminūn

Al-Araf 7:185

And again:

«فَبِأَيِّ حَدِيثٍ بَعْدَهُ يُؤْمِنُونَ»

Fa-bi-ayyi ḥadīthin baʿdahu yuʾminūn

Al-Mursalat 77:50

It also says:

«فَبِأَيِّ حَدِيثٍ بَعْدَ اللَّهِ وَآيَاتِهِ يُؤْمِنُونَ»

Fa-bi-ayyi ḥadīthin baʿda Allāhi wa-āyātihi yuʾminūn

Al-Jathiyah 45:6

“Ḥadīth” in these verses has the general meaning of speech or discourse. The question is: if the audience will not have faith in the ayahs and speech of The God, then in what discourse after it will they have faith?

Historically, the verses are not naming the collections of transmitted reports compiled in later periods, because “ḥadīth” here had not yet become the technical name of those collections. Directly translating it as “hadith reports attributed to the Prophet” is therefore incorrect.

On the other hand, the verse does not permit another discourse to occupy the position of final authority above the ayahs. The word’s historical meaning is general, but the verse’s structure returns ultimate authority to The God and His ayahs.

Two claims must therefore remain distinct:

  • the verse does not directly name the technical category “Prophetic hadith”;
  • no later discourse can become a standard above the ayahs or a replacement for them.

This distinction neither turns the verse into an instrument for rejecting every historical report on the basis of its name, nor allows any report to be placed on a level with the ayahs simply because it is called “hadith.”

14. A Discourse That Is Not Fabrication

At the end of Surah Yusuf, it states:

«مَا كَانَ حَدِيثًا يُفْتَرَىٰ وَلَٰكِنْ تَصْدِيقَ الَّذِي بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ»

Mā kāna ḥadīthan yuftarā wa-lākin taṣdīqa alladhī bayna yadayhi

Yusuf 12:111

A ḥadīth can be the object of fabrication: speech can be invented and attributed to someone else. But the verse explicitly states that this Qur’anic narrative is not fabricated speech.

“Ḥadīth” does not inherently mean correct or authoritative speech. Truthfulness or fabrication determines the report’s quality:

  • a truthful report;
  • a report that can be denied;
  • a fabricated report;
  • the best discourse.

The name “hadith” therefore does not by itself establish a report’s historical or religious authority.

15. “لَهْوَ الْحَدِيثِ” (lahwa al-ḥadīth): Diverting Discourse

The Qur’an says:

«وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يَشْتَرِي لَهْوَ الْحَدِيثِ لِيُضِلَّ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ وَيَتَّخِذَهَا هُزُوًا»

Wa-mina al-nāsi man yashtarī lahwa al-ḥadīthi li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīli Allāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa-yattakhidhahā huzuwan

Luqman 31:6

The condemnation does not arise from the word “ḥadīth” alone. Several elements appear together:

  • “لَهْوَ الْحَدِيثِ” (lahwa al-ḥadīth): discourse that entertains and diverts;
  • “لِيُضِلَّ” (li-yuḍilla): its purpose is to mislead;
  • “بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ” (bi-ghayri ʿilm): it is done without knowledge;
  • “وَيَتَّخِذَهَا هُزُوًا” (wa-yattakhidhahā huzuwan): the way or ayahs of The God are made an object of ridicule.

Some commentators have applied “lahw al-ḥadīth” to singing or music. This application identifies a particular instance through transmitted interpretation or jurisprudence; it is not a meaning that necessarily follows from the root “Ḥ D TH” itself or from the verse’s lexical structure.

The verse uses neither the word “music,” nor “singing,” nor the name of any artistic instrument. Any kind of speech, performance, narrative, or entertainment might be considered an instance of this construction when used to mislead and ridicule. But restricting the basic translation to one particular art narrows the expression without a verbal indication warranting it.

Part Four: Reports and Conversations

16. Accounts of Prophets and Events

The Qur’an says:

«وَهَلْ أَتَاكَ حَدِيثُ مُوسَىٰ»

Wa-hal atāka ḥadīthu Mūsā

Ta-Ha 20:9

«هَلْ أَتَاكَ حَدِيثُ مُوسَىٰ»

Hal atāka ḥadīthu Mūsā

An-Naziat 79:15

Here the “account of Moses” means a report or narrative concerning Moses. Attaching ḥadīth to a person’s name identifies the subject of the account; Moses is not necessarily the speaker of this ḥadīth.

Concerning Abraham’s guests, it also states:

«هَلْ أَتَاكَ حَدِيثُ ضَيْفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الْمُكْرَمِينَ»

Hal atāka ḥadīthu ḍayfi Ibrāhīma al-mukramīn

Adh-Dhariyat 51:24

And likewise:

«هَلْ أَتَاكَ حَدِيثُ الْجُنُودِ»

Hal atāka ḥadīthu al-junūd

Al-Buruj 85:17

«هَلْ أَتَاكَ حَدِيثُ الْغَاشِيَةِ»

Hal atāka ḥadīthu al-ghāshiyah

Al-Ghashiyah 88:1

In these constructions, ḥadīth is a report that makes a subject present to its audience. That subject may belong to the past, the future, or the Hereafter. The time of the reported subject does not change the word’s meaning.

17. “تَأْوِيلِ الْأَحَادِيثِ” (taʾwīl al-aḥādīth)

Surah Yusuf states:

«وَيُعَلِّمُكَ مِنْ تَأْوِيلِ الْأَحَادِيثِ»

Wa-yuʿallimuka min taʾwīli al-aḥādīth

Yusuf 12:6

«وَلِنُعَلِّمَهُ مِنْ تَأْوِيلِ الْأَحَادِيثِ»

Wa-li-nuʿallimahu min taʾwīli al-aḥādīth

Yusuf 12:21

«وَعَلَّمْتَنِي مِنْ تَأْوِيلِ الْأَحَادِيثِ»

Wa-ʿallamtanī min taʾwīli al-aḥādīth

Yusuf 12:101

In this surah’s context, “aḥādīth” is connected with dreams, their narration, and events whose outcome becomes apparent. Nevertheless, the word “aḥādīth” does not lexically mean “dreams” alone; that connection is supplied by the surah’s context.

“Taʾwīl al-aḥādīth” can be understood as the ability to recognize the outcome and fulfillment of dreams, reports, and matters recounted. Restricting the word “aḥādīth” itself to dreams, or associating it with later collections of transmitted reports, does not fit its use in this surah.

18. Peoples Becoming “أَحَادِيث” (aḥādīth)

Of destroyed communities, the Qur’an says:

«فَأَتْبَعْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ بَعْضًا وَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ أَحَادِيثَ»

Fa-atbaʿnā baʿḍahum baʿḍan wa-jaʿalnāhum aḥādītha

Al-Muminun 23:44

And of another people:

«فَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ أَحَادِيثَ وَمَزَّقْنَاهُمْ كُلَّ مُمَزَّقٍ»

Fa-jaʿalnāhum aḥādītha wa-mazzaqnāhum kulla mumazzaqin

Saba 34:19

After their collapse, they passed from being a living society to being reports and accounts that others recounted about them.

“جَعَلْنَاهُمْ أَحَادِيثَ” (jaʿalnāhum aḥādītha) does not mean physically turning them into words. Their historical and social presence ended, and what remained were the narratives and recollections concerning them.

19. Social and Private Conversation

In the etiquette of entering the Prophet’s home, it states:

«فَإِذَا طَعِمْتُمْ فَانْتَشِرُوا وَلَا مُسْتَأْنِسِينَ لِحَدِيثٍ»

Fa-idhā ṭaʿimtum fa-ntashirū wa-lā mustaʾnisīna li-ḥadīthin

Al-Ahzab 33:53

“لِحَدِيثٍ” (li-ḥadīthin) here refers to an ordinary social conversation. Some guests would remain to converse after eating. This verse clearly shows that ḥadīth is not necessarily legislative or religious speech.

The Qur’an also says:

«وَإِذْ أَسَرَّ النَّبِيُّ إِلَىٰ بَعْضِ أَزْوَاجِهِ حَدِيثًا»

Wa-idh asarra al-nabiyyu ilā baʿḍi azwājihi ḥadīthan

At-Tahrim 66:3

In this verse, the Prophet shares something confidentially with one of his wives. The mere fact that the speaker is the Prophet does not turn that speech into a universal and permanent law. The verse itself presents it as a private conversation in a family setting.

The root “Ḥ D TH” does not by itself turn any human speech into revelation or legislation.

20. Moving to Another Conversation

Concerning gatherings in which the ayahs are denied and ridiculed, the Qur’an says:

«فَلَا تَقْعُدُوا مَعَهُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَخُوضُوا فِي حَدِيثٍ غَيْرِهِ»

Fa-lā taqʿudū maʿahum ḥattā yakhūḍū fī ḥadīthin ghayrihi

An-Nisa 4:140

And:

«فَأَعْرِضْ عَنْهُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَخُوضُوا فِي حَدِيثٍ غَيْرِهِ»

Fa-aʿriḍ ʿanhum ḥattā yakhūḍū fī ḥadīthin ghayrihi

Al-Anam 6:68

“حديثٍ غيره” (ḥadīthin ghayrihi) means another discourse or subject. The condemnation concerns the subject and direction of the conversation, not conversation itself. When the subject changes, the situation of the gathering changes as well.

21. Understanding or Concealing Speech

The Qur’an says:

«فَمَالِ هَٰؤُلَاءِ الْقَوْمِ لَا يَكَادُونَ يَفْقَهُونَ حَدِيثًا»

Fa-māli hāʾulāʾi al-qawmi lā yakādūna yafqahūna ḥadīthan

An-Nisa 4:78

“حديثًا” (ḥadīthan) here means speech or discourse. The verse asks why these people can scarcely understand speech properly.

Concerning the Day of Judgment, it also states:

«وَلَا يَكْتُمُونَ اللَّهَ حَدِيثًا»

Wa-lā yaktumūna Allāha ḥadīthan

An-Nisa 4:42

“Ḥadīthan” is indefinite and falls within the scope of negation: they conceal no speech or report from The God.

Neither of these uses carries the technical meaning of a “report attributed to the Prophet.”

Part Five: The Dynamics of the Verbs

22. The Verb “حَدَّثَ” (ḥaddatha)

Al-Baqarah states:

«أَتُحَدِّثُونَهُمْ بِمَا فَتَحَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمْ لِيُحَاجُّوكُمْ بِهِ عِنْدَ رَبِّكُمْ»

A-tuḥaddithūnahum bi-mā fataḥa Allāhu ʿalaykum li-yuḥājjūkum bihi ʿinda rabbikum

Al-Baqarah 2:76

“تُحَدِّثُونَهُمْ بِمَا...” (tuḥaddithūnahum bi-mā...) means that you recount to them what has been opened to you.

The preposition “بـ” (bi-) introduces the content of the speech:

  • the speakers: the plural subject pronoun expressed by wāw;
  • the audience: “هم” (hum), them;
  • the report’s content: “بما فتح الله عليكم” (bi-mā fataḥa Allāhu ʿalaykum), what The God has opened to you.

The verb itself denotes only speaking and recounting. The motive and consequences of recounting are understood from the continuation of the verse.

Surah Ad-Duha also states:

«وَأَمَّا بِنِعْمَةِ رَبِّكَ فَحَدِّثْ»

Wa-ammā bi-niʿmati rabbika fa-ḥaddith

Ad-Duha 93:11

“فَحَدِّثْ” (fa-ḥaddith) means recount it or speak of it. The preposition “بـ” (bi-) specifies the subject of the speech: the blessing of your Lord.

This verse is not in itself a command to boast or display possessions. The direct meaning of the expression is:

Speak of the blessing of your Lord.

The root itself does not determine the form or occasion of this speaking.

23. The Earth Reporting

Concerning the earth, the Qur’an says:

«يَوْمَئِذٍ تُحَدِّثُ أَخْبَارَهَا»

Yawmaʾidhin tuḥaddithu akhbārahā

Az-Zalzalah 99:4

In this expression:

  • “تُحَدِّثُ” (tuḥaddithu): it reports;
  • “أَخْبَارَهَا” (akhbārahā): its own news.

Their occurrence together shows that ḥaddatha is the act of recounting, while khabar is the content being recounted.

The verse presents a scene of the Hereafter, but does not explain the physical manner in which the earth speaks. The root expresses reporting alone; how it happens remains among the matters left unstated.

24. The Verb “أَحْدَثَ” (aḥdatha)

In the account of Moses and that servant, it states:

«فَلَا تَسْأَلْنِي عَنْ شَيْءٍ حَتَّىٰ أُحْدِثَ لَكَ مِنْهُ ذِكْرًا»

Fa-lā tasʾalnī ʿan shayʾin ḥattā uḥditha laka minhu dhikran

Al-Kahf 18:70

“أُحْدِثَ لَكَ مِنْهُ ذِكْرًا” (uḥditha laka minhu dhikran) means until I myself bring up some account of it for you or offer an explanation. Here aḥdatha does not mean creating a being; an account comes into being that had not previously been expressed to Moses.

The Qur’an also says:

«وَصَرَّفْنَا فِيهِ مِنَ الْوَعِيدِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَّقُونَ أَوْ يُحْدِثُ لَهُمْ ذِكْرًا»

Wa-ṣarrafnā fīhi mina al-waʿīdi laʿallahum yattaqūna aw yuḥdithu lahum dhikran

Ta-Ha 20:113

“يُحْدِثُ لَهُمْ ذِكْرًا” (yuḥdithu lahum dhikran) means that it brings about a fresh reminder or awakening for them. The root “Ḥ D TH” indicates the emergence of an effect, while dhikr, remembrance or reminder, identifies the kind of effect.

In the rulings concerning separation, it also states:

«لَا تَدْرِي لَعَلَّ اللَّهَ يُحْدِثُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ أَمْرًا»

Lā tadrī laʿalla Allāha yuḥdithu baʿda dhālika amran

At-Talaq 65:1

“يُحْدِثُ” (yuḥdithu) here means bringing about a new matter after that situation. The expression “بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ” (baʿda dhālika), after that, reveals the root’s temporal dimension: a new situation takes shape after a previous one.

25. The Description “مُحْدَث” (muḥdath)

The Qur’an says:

«مَا يَأْتِيهِمْ مِنْ ذِكْرٍ مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ مُحْدَثٍ إِلَّا اسْتَمَعُوهُ وَهُمْ يَلْعَبُونَ»

Mā yaʾtīhim min dhikrin min rabbihim muḥdathin illā istamaʿūhu wa-hum yalʿabūn

Al-Anbiya 21:2

And:

«وَمَا يَأْتِيهِمْ مِنْ ذِكْرٍ مِنَ الرَّحْمَٰنِ مُحْدَثٍ إِلَّا كَانُوا عَنْهُ مُعْرِضِينَ»

Wa-mā yaʾtīhim min dhikrin mina al-Raḥmāni muḥdathin illā kānū ʿanhu muʿriḍīn

Ash-Shuara 26:5

“مُحْدَث” (muḥdath) describes dhikr, the reminder. The verb “يَأْتِيهِمْ” (yaʾtīhim), comes to them, also centers the verse on the message reaching its audience. The natural meaning of the construction is:

No newly received reminder comes to them from their Lord except that...

Newness in this context concerns the reminder’s arrival and presentation to its audience. Each time a reminder reaches them, their response is play or turning away.

26. “Muḥdath” and the Dispute over the Qur’an’s Coming into Being

The word “muḥdath” was later used in theological disputes over whether the Qur’an is eternal or created. These disputes, however, must not be imposed on the verses as ready-made frameworks.

Al-Anbiya 21:2 and Ash-Shuara 26:5 concern the temporal relationship between the reminder and its audience:

  • the reminder comes from the Lord;
  • it is newly received by them;
  • they hear it;
  • then they play or turn away.

The description muḥdath belongs to dhikr, while the verb yaʾtīhim, “comes to them,” makes its temporal context clear. This construction alone cannot yield a complete theory about the essence, knowledge, or speech of The God.

Nor does muḥdath in these verses mean “heretically innovative.” The word itself contains no such negative connotation.

Part Six: Semantic Boundaries and Translation Guidance

27. Differences from Nearby Words

حَدِيث (ḥadīth) and قَوْل (qawl)

Q W L, as in “قَوْل” (qawl), is a general term for saying and what is said. A qawl may be a short expression, a command, an answer, or a claim.

Ḥadīth more often presents speech as something recounted, or as making a report or subject present to an audience. This boundary, however, is not absolute.

حَدِيث (ḥadīth) and كَلَام (kalām)

K L M, as in “کَلَام” (kalām), denotes speech as a linguistic phenomenon. Ḥadīth can present the same speech in terms of its being recounted, newly introduced, or a report.

As a general tendency:

  • kalām: speech considered as discourse;
  • ḥadīth: speech considered as something recounted or reported.

حَدِيث (ḥadīth) and خَبَر (khabar)

KH B R, as in “خَبَر” (khabar), places more emphasis on information and awareness about something. Ḥadīth foregrounds the act or content of recounting.

In “تُحَدِّثُ أَخْبَارَهَا” (tuḥaddithu akhbārahā), the earth reports, and its news is the subject of that report.

حَدِيث (ḥadīth) and نَبَأ (nabaʾ)

N B A, as in “نَبَأ” (nabaʾ), is generally news of considerable importance and consequence. Ḥadīth has a wider range and also includes everyday conversation and private speech.

Every nabaʾ can be recounted as a ḥadīth, but not every ḥadīth is momentous news.

حَدِيث (ḥadīth) and قَصَص (qaṣaṣ)

Q Ṣ Ṣ, as in “قَصَص” (qaṣaṣ), is connected with following traces and recounting events in sequence. A story foregrounds narrative structure and following the course of events.

Ḥadīth is broader and may be a story, conversation, news, or a short statement.

حَدِيث (ḥadīth) and ذِكْر (dhikr)

DH K R, as in “ذِکْر” (dhikr), denotes reminding, remembering, or bringing something up. Ḥadīth is speech that is recounted.

In “أَوْ يُحْدِثُ لَهُمْ ذِكْرًا” (aw yuḥdithu lahum dhikran), an utterance brings about a reminder for its audience. Thus dhikr and ḥadīth are not complete synonyms.

28. The Role of Syntactic Structure

In expressions such as:

  • “حَدِيثُ مُوسَىٰ” (ḥadīthu Mūsā), the account of Moses;
  • “حَدِيثُ ضَيْفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ” (ḥadīthu ḍayfi Ibrāhīm), the account of Abraham’s guests;
  • “حَدِيثُ الْجُنُودِ” (ḥadīthu al-junūd), the account of the hosts;
  • “حَدِيثُ الْغَاشِيَةِ” (ḥadīthu al-ghāshiyah), the account of the Overwhelming Event;

the genitive construction identifies the subject of the account. The noun following ḥadīth is not necessarily its speaker; it may be what the report concerns.

In “حَدَّثَ بِـ” (ḥaddatha bi-), the preposition bi- introduces the content of the speech:

«أَتُحَدِّثُونَهُمْ بِمَا فَتَحَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمْ»

A-tuḥaddithūnahum bi-mā fataḥa Allāhu ʿalaykum

«بِنِعْمَةِ رَبِّكَ فَحَدِّثْ»

Bi-niʿmati rabbika fa-ḥaddith

In constructions such as “هَذَا الْحَدِيثِ” (hādhā al-ḥadīth), this discourse, or “أَحْسَنَ الْحَدِيثِ” (aḥsana al-ḥadīth), the best discourse, definiteness merely indicates the particular discourse intended in that context. The article “الـ” (al-) does not turn it into a designation for later hadith collections.

29. Translation Guidance

Depending on context, the following equivalents can be used for the forms of this root:

  • حَدِيث (ḥadīth): speech, discourse, a report, a narrative, an account, or conversation;
  • أَحَادِيث (aḥādīth): reports, accounts, or matters recounted;
  • حَدَّثَ (ḥaddatha): he spoke, recounted, or reported;
  • أَحْدَثَ (aḥdatha): he brought into being, introduced, or brought up;
  • مُحْدَث (muḥdath): newly brought into being, newly introduced, or newly received.

For example:

  • “هَلْ أَتَاكَ حَدِيثُ مُوسَىٰ” (hal atāka ḥadīthu Mūsā): Has the account of Moses reached you?
  • “وَلَا مُسْتَأْنِسِينَ لِحَدِيثٍ” (wa-lā mustaʾnisīna li-ḥadīthin): And do not remain seated for conversation;
  • “أَسَرَّ النَّبِيُّ... حَدِيثًا” (asarra al-nabiyyu... ḥadīthan): The Prophet shared something confidentially;
  • “تُحَدِّثُ أَخْبَارَهَا” (tuḥaddithu akhbārahā): It reports its news;
  • “ذِكْرٍ... مُحْدَثٍ” (dhikrin... muḥdathin): a newly received reminder.

Retaining the word “hadith” in every translation may inadvertently introduce its modern technical meaning into the verse.

30. Necessary Boundaries

The root “Ḥ D TH” does not by itself determine:

  • whether speech is true or false;
  • whether a historical report is reliable or fabricated;
  • whether discourse is revelation or human speech;
  • whether speech is legislative or private;
  • whether a ḥadīth is beneficial or misleading;
  • whether something new is good or bad;
  • that muḥdath means bidʿah, religious innovation;
  • that aḥādīth means dreams everywhere.

These characteristics come from the description, speaker, object, purpose, and context.

Several further points must also be kept in view:

  • In the Qur’an, ḥadīth is not the technical name of post-Qur’anic collections of reports.
  • Hādhā al-ḥadīth, “this discourse,” refers to the Qur’an itself in several verses.
  • The Qur’an calls itself “أَحْسَنَ الْحَدِيثِ” (aḥsana al-ḥadīth), the best discourse.
  • Lahw al-ḥadīth is not lexically an exclusive equivalent of music.
  • The Prophet’s private speech in At-Tahrim 66:3 is not in itself general legislation.
  • Muḥdath in Al-Anbiya 21:2 and Ash-Shuara 26:5 does not mean “heretically innovative.”
  • Taʾwīl al-aḥādīth must not be interpreted through the later technical meaning of Prophetic hadith.
  • The designation “hadith” alone does not establish a report’s truth or authority.
  • The later technical usage must not restrict the word’s Qur’anic meaning.

31. Conclusion

The root “Ḥ D TH” denotes something happening or coming into being. From this same field, ḥadīth is used for speech or a report that is recounted and makes a subject present to its audience.

In the Qur’an, ḥadīth is a general word. The Qur’an itself is called “أَحْسَنَ الْحَدِيثِ” (aḥsana al-ḥadīth), the best discourse, and in several verses “هَذَا الْحَدِيثِ” (hādhā al-ḥadīth), this discourse. The accounts of Moses, Abraham’s guests, the hosts, and the Overwhelming Event are also ḥadīth. The guests’ conversation, the Prophet’s confidential speech, and the subject discussed in a gathering can likewise be ḥadīth.

Ḥadīth is therefore inherently neither revelation nor falsehood, neither legislation nor entertainment. Its truth, value, and standing are determined by description and context. The Qur’an speaks of the most truthful discourse, the best discourse, a report susceptible to fabrication, and diverting discourse.

The verb ḥaddatha denotes speaking and recounting: a human being speaks of blessing, and the earth reports its news. Aḥdatha denotes bringing something into being or introducing it, while muḥdath in the verses concerning dhikr describes a newly received reminder. It does not, by itself, establish a theological theory about the reality of the Qur’an.

The specialized meaning of hadith as a report attributed to the Prophet became established in later periods. This usage matters for the study of linguistic history and the literature of transmitted reports, but must not be imposed on Qur’anic verses. Nor do the verses “فَبِأَيِّ حَدِيثٍ بَعْدَهُ يُؤْمِنُونَ” (fa-bi-ayyi ḥadīthin baʿdahu yuʾminūn), in what discourse after it will they have faith, directly reject a later hadith collection by name. Nevertheless, they return final authority to the speech of The God and His ayahs.

The root’s basic meaning can be expressed concisely as follows:

“Ḥ D TH”: something happening or coming into being; and, in language, speaking and recounting speech or a report that is brought before an audience and made present to it.
