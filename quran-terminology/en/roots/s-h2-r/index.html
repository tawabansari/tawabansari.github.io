Staying awake, continued wakefulness, and al-sāhirah as the earth's surface

S H R concerns staying awake, especially at night or when sleep is expected. Its uses include wakeful nights, watchful eyes, and things whose activity or flow does not cease.

Early Arabic also uses al-sāhirah for land, the earth's surface, and its open expanse. This matters especially because bi-al-sāhirah in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14 is the root's sole Quranic occurrence.

In the context of resurrection, the verse says that after a single cry they are suddenly upon the earth.

Al-sāhirah here names or describes the ground; it should not simply become “awake people” or “wakefulness.”

Lexical explanations connect the name with wakefulness in different ways: creatures staying awake on earth, continuing activity, flowing water, or growing plants. These images do not independently prove the name's historical origin.

1. Early Arabic usage and ancient poetic evidence

Sahar: staying awake rather than sleeping

Sahira yas-haru saharan means staying awake, especially not sleeping at night or being unable to sleep.

Sāhir describes someone awake; sahrān likewise describes someone who has stayed awake at night.

As-harahu means that something or someone kept a person awake or deprived him of sleep.

Wakefulness may be voluntary, as in guarding, traveling, working, or studying, or arise from worry, pain, or illness.

Sahar should not be restricted to anxiety-related or pathological sleeplessness. Its central element is remaining awake; cause and value come from context.

A pre-Islamic poetic example: a wakeful night

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

كَتَمْتُكَ لَيْلًا بِالْجَمُومَيْنِ سَاهِرًا
وَهَمَّيْنِ هَمًّا مُسْتَكِنًّا وَظَاهِرًا

katamtuka laylan bi-al-jamūmayni sāhiran / wa-hammayni hamman mustakinnan wa-ẓāhirā

“I concealed from you a wakeful night at al-Jamūmayn and two sorrows, one hidden and one apparent.”

Sāhiran here is associated with night and sorrow.

Grammatical commentaries allow two readings: a circumstantial description of the speaker, or an attributed description of the night in which wakefulness occurred.

Both clearly concern staying awake rather than sleeping.

The line attests sāhir in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The poem survives in al-Nābighah's collected poetry and major Arabic anthologies. Its established attribution differs from independently dating every line.

Semantically, the example concerns wakefulness, not land, desert, or a gathering place.

Al-sāhirah describing land

Early Arabic sources record al-sāhirah for land, its surface, and its open expanse.

A line attributed to Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī says:

يَرْتَدْنَ سَاهِرَةً كَأَنَّ جَمِيمَهَا
وَعَمِيمَهَا أَسْدَافُ لَيْلٍ مُظْلِمِ

yartadna sāhiratan ka-anna jamīmahā / wa-ʿamīmahā asdāfu laylin muẓlimi

“They seek pasture on an expanse whose low and tall vegetation resembles the darkness of a deep night.”

The line describes female wild asses.

Sāhirah is their terrain, whose vegetation is compared with nighttime darkness.

It can therefore name an expanse of land rather than a sleepless person.

Nor should sāhirah always be confined to completely bare ground: this example expressly mentions vegetation.

Abū Kabīr belongs to early Hudhali poetry, and reports discuss his proximity to the transition into Islam. Without independently dating this line, it should not be conclusively placed before the Quran's revelation.

Another terrestrial example

A line attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt also reads:

وَفِيهَا لَحْمُ سَاهِرَةٍ وَبَحْرٍ
وَمَا فَاهُوا بِهِ أَبَدًا مُقِيمُ

wa-fīhā laḥmu sāhiratin wa-baḥrin / wa-mā fāhū bihi abadan muqīmu

“There is meat from land and sea, and what they ask for remains available to them.”

Sāhirah stands opposite baḥr, “sea.”

The contrast indicates that laḥmu sāhiratin concerns the meat of land animals, while baḥr refers to those of the sea.

Sāhirah thus concerns dry land and is not limited to wakefulness.

Umayyah lived near the emergence of Islam; poetry attributed to him was transmitted and collected later, with disagreements concerning some pieces.

This line illustrates an established terrestrial use, without conclusively proving its date or historical independence from Quranic language.

Together the examples attest two clear usages: staying awake, and al-sāhirah as land or its surface.

2. Semantic core and principal forms

The principal lexical forms include:

Sahira: stayed awake, especially at night.

Yas-haru: stays awake.

Sahar: wakefulness or staying up at night.

Sāhir: someone awake, or something described figuratively as wakeful or continuously active.

Sahrān: someone who remained awake at night or could not sleep.

As-hara: kept someone awake or deprived someone of sleep.

Suharah: in one lexical use, someone frequently staying awake at night.

Al-sāhirah: land, its surface or open expanse; contextually, where people appear after being raised.

These forms do not all occur in the Quran.

Only the noun al-sāhirah occurs, once. The verbs sahira and yas-haru and nouns sahar and sahrān are not Quranic forms.

Wakefulness and continued activity

ʿAynun sāhiratun can describe a spring whose water flows continuously.

A spring does not sleep and wake like a person. Wakefulness figuratively describes its continuing activity.

Some lexical explanations likewise describe arḍun sāhiratun as land whose plants continue growing, as though it worked even while people sleep.

Wakefulness can thus extend from a human condition to phenomena whose activity or flow continues.

This image should not become a scientific definition of land or springs.

Neither human biological sleep nor human awareness is being literally attributed here. The expression figuratively concerns continued activity.

Is sāhirah derived from staying awake?

Lexical sources offer several explanations for calling land sāhirah.

One relates it to people and animals sleeping and staying awake upon the earth.

Another concerns continuing growth and activity, as though the earth remained active while people slept.

A further explanation relates open terrain to a traveler kept awake by its dangers and anxieties.

Some accounts invoke the continuing movement of a mirage over open ground.

These intelligible linguistic images are not identical.

Their variety prevents treating one as the definite historical origin without independent evidence.

Usage clearly attests wakefulness for sāhir and land or the earth's surface for al-sāhirah. Precisely when and through which image the latter arose remains unsettled.

This distinction matters for the Quranic verse.

3. Bi-al-sāhirah: the sole Quranic occurrence

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:13 says:

فَإِنَّمَا هِيَ زَجْرَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ

fa-innamā hiya zajratun wāḥidah

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:13

“It is only a single cry.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14 continues:

فَإِذَا هُمْ بِالسَّاهِرَةِ

fa-idhā hum bi-al-sāhirah

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14

“Then suddenly they are upon the earth.”

The two verses directly answer questions denying resurrection after death.

The preceding context

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10, people incredulously ask whether they will be returned to their former state.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11 continues their question with mention of decayed bones.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:12, they describe such a return as ruinous.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:13 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14 then give a concise answer: one cry, and suddenly they are upon the earth.

This concerns resurrection and reappearance after death, not ordinary waking from nighttime sleep.

Al-sāhirah must therefore be understood as the setting of their presence after that event.

The construction bi-al-sāhirah

Bi-al-sāhirah comprises the preposition bāʾ and definite noun al-sāhirah.

Bāʾ expresses presence at a location here.

Al-sāhirah is feminine singular and genitive after bāʾ.

Its form corresponds to feminine sāhir, but here it functions as an established noun for land.

The sentence therefore does not mean “they are awake people.”

Simply describing people as awake would involve a different construction or context.

Hum refers to the people; bi-al-sāhirah identifies where they suddenly appear.

The role of fa-idhā

Fa-idhā foregrounds suddenness.

This expression foregrounds their sudden appearance rather than detailing stages of bodily restoration.

After mentioning a single cry, it presents their sudden presence.

An event the opponents regarded as unlikely is portrayed as suddenly realized through divine command and power.

Suddenness alone does not explain the physical restoration of bodies, human consciousness, or every stage of resurrection.

The expression emphasizes their sudden appearance in the place called al-sāhirah.

4. The earth's surface or a particular resurrection land?

Arabic records al-sāhirah for the earth's surface and expanse.

Lexical explanations include wajh al-arḍ and al-falāh.

Wajh al-arḍ means the earth's surface.

Falāh denotes an extensive wilderness, especially open country away from habitation and water; it does not itself specify the absence of every obstacle.

These expressions are not completely identical.

The earth's surface may have vegetation and varied relief. Falāh emphasizes open country, while sāhirah can have the broader sense of an expanse of land.

Al-sāhirah should not be confined exclusively to arid desert, vegetation-free ground, or a perfectly flat surface.

The line attributed to Abū Kabīr supports this, describing sāhirah with vegetation.

Is the resurrection earth the present earth?

Ibrāhīm 14:48 speaks of a day when the earth is exchanged for another earth and the heavens also change.

Ṭā Hā 20:105, Ṭā Hā 20:106, and Ṭā Hā 20:107 describe mountains removed and a level expanse remaining.

Al-Kahf 18:47 also mentions mountains being moved and the earth exposed.

These passages matter for the wider resurrection scene.

Al-sāhirah in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14 alone does not determine whether its terrain is precisely today's surface, a transformed earth, or a setting described by the other passages.

That question requires considering the relevant verses together.

The lexical sense remains land or its exposed surface.

“The gathering plain” is consequently a contextual explanation, not the root's fixed general meaning.

Is al-sāhirah a particular place in Syria?

Some early interpretive reports identify it with Syria, Jerusalem, or a specific location.

Others describe new ground, white and level ground, or the resurrection earth.

These reports reflect differing interpretations of the precise referent.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14 itself names neither Syria, Jerusalem, a mountain, nor another geographical region.

Such identification is therefore neither the word's lexical meaning nor a necessary conclusion from the verse alone.

The construction presents people suddenly upon the earth after that cry.

Does sāhirah mean the earth awakens?

Its connection with wakefulness may inspire an interpretive image of the earth becoming awake and active at resurrection.

That image is conceivable, but the verse contains no verb stating that the earth awakens.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14, al-sāhirah names land and thus indicates location; this is not a claim that it is a morphologically derived noun of place. Hum refers to the people.

The root does not establish a direct statement about the earth's biological life, consciousness, or bodily awakening.

Reflections on the earth bearing witness, truths becoming manifest, or creation being transformed require their own evidence.

Those reflections should not obscure this verse's linguistic meaning.

5. Comparison with neighboring roots

S H R (sahar) and N W M (nawm): wakefulness and sleep

Nawm concerns sleep, sahar remaining awake.

They frequently stand in contrast.

Sahar is not merely the absence of sleep at any instant; its familiar use particularly concerns remaining awake when sleep is usual or expected.

Someone working during daytime is not necessarily sāhir merely because they are not asleep.

The Quran uses N W M for sleep and rest in several passages; S H R occurs only as al-sāhirah in its terrestrial sense.

The family N W M should be examined independently for the wider meanings of sleep.

S H R (sahar) and S H D (sahad): wakeful nights and little sleep

Sahad concerns little sleep or sleep eluding the eyes.

Sahar belongs to the same general field.

Lexicons sometimes explain one through the other.

A few examples do not justify an absolute division into voluntary wakefulness versus involuntary sleeplessness.

Both can concern lost sleep; context supplies the cause.

S H D and S H R also have different letters: semantic proximity does not prove derivation from one another.

S H R (sahar) and Y Q Ẓ (yaqaẓ): remaining awake and being awake

Y Q Ẓ concerns wakefulness as opposed to sleep.

Ayqāẓan in Al-Kahf 18:18 describes the cave companions' apparently wakeful appearance despite their being asleep.

Wakefulness can thus describe apparent condition as well as actual state.

Sahar particularly emphasizes remaining awake at night.

They are semantically close without being interchangeable in every sentence.

S H R (sahar) and B ʿ TH (baʿth): wakefulness and being raised

Quranic baʿth concerns raising, bringing out of a previous condition, and bringing into renewed movement or presence.

Some passages apply it to waking from sleep, many others to raising people after death.

Sahar itself concerns staying awake, not the act of resurrection.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14 concerns resurrection, but al-sāhirah identifies the setting of the people's presence.

The resurrection context does not make this noun synonymous with baʿth.

B ʿ TH should be studied independently to clarify the distinction between raising and the location indicated by sāhirah.

S H R (sahar) and S H L (sahl): open ground and level terrain

Sahl concerns level ground, ease, and lack of difficulty in a route or situation.

Terrestrial sāhirah can indicate open or extensive ground.

They overlap in describing some terrain.

Al-sāhirah is not a fixed synonym for “level ground,” nor does sahl fundamentally mean staying awake.

Any level terrain in the resurrection scene should be understood through the relevant verses, without making the roots wholly synonymous because of a shared referent.

S H R (sahar) and S Ḥ R (saḥar): wakefulness and predawn

Saḥar in one established usage means the final part of the night near dawn.

Sahar means remaining awake.

A person can be awake at predawn without the two roots being identical.

S Ḥ R also concerns magic in another branch, which should not be confused with S H R.

The middle letters hāʾ and ḥāʾ distinguish two different lexical families.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

The only Quranic form is bi-al-sāhirah in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14.

The verse:

فَإِذَا هُمْ بِالسَّاهِرَةِ

fa-idhā hum bi-al-sāhirah

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14

A suitable basic translation:

“Then suddenly they are upon the earth.”

This is concise and consistent with the attested terrestrial meaning.

“Then suddenly they are on the earth's expanse” also works, foregrounding the open setting.

“In a broad desert” follows one lexical possibility but restricts the terrain more narrowly.

“In the land of resurrection” conveys context, although “resurrection” is not contained within sāhirah itself.

“Then suddenly they wake up” relates to the root but loses the established noun's spatial meaning.

Direct translation should therefore retain the earth's surface, leaving the root explanation to lexical commentary.

Brief root meaning and meaning in the verse

Word in the verse: bi-al-sāhirah.

Root: S H R.

Brief meaning: remaining awake and continued wakefulness; al-sāhirah also denotes land or its exposed surface.

Meaning here: upon the earth, where people suddenly appear after a single cry.

Principal non-Quranic forms

Sahira: stayed awake.

Yas-haru: stays awake.

Sahar: staying up at night.

Sāhir: someone awake.

Sahrān: someone who has stayed awake at night.

As-hara: kept someone awake or deprived them of sleep.

Suharah: frequently wakeful at night.

ʿAynun sāhiratun: of water, a continuously flowing spring.

Arḍun sāhiratun: in some lexical explanations, land with continuing growth or activity; al-sāhirah conventionally denotes land or its surface.

These forms illuminate the family, but only al-sāhirah occurs in the Quran.

Conclusion

S H R concerns staying awake and remaining without sleep, clearly expressed by sahar, sāhir, sahrān, and related verbs.

The poem attributed to al-Nābighah illustrates nighttime wakefulness in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Alongside this, al-sāhirah denotes land, its surface, and open terrain. Examples attributed to Abū Kabīr and Umayyah illustrate that sense, although their precise dates and independence are not equally certain.

Proposed explanations connect the terrestrial name with wakeful creatures, continuing growth, flowing water, or travelers staying awake in open country.

These images make a semantic connection intelligible without proving a single historical origin.

The root occurs once in the Quran, in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14.

The context confronts deniers of resurrection with the event they regarded as unlikely: after one cry they are suddenly upon the earth.

Al-sāhirah identifies their location rather than merely describing them as awake.

The resulting definition is:

S H R primarily denotes staying awake, especially at night. Sāhirah can describe wakefulness or continuing activity and also functions as a noun for land and its surface. In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14, al-sāhirah indicates the expanse upon which people suddenly appear after being raised.

This preserves both wakefulness and the independent, established terrestrial usage.

The verse should neither be reduced to ordinary waking nor made to specify a geographical location, new physical structure of the earth, or exact mechanics of resurrection through this word alone.

Its contextual meaning is clear: after one cry, those who doubted return after death suddenly appear upon the earth.
