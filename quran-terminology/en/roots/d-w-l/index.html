Root D W L (dawl)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Circulation and changing circumstances, turns in victory and power, possessions passing between hands, and the distinction between circulation and exclusive access to resources

In Arabic, D W L concerns movement, transfer, and changing circumstances, especially something passing from one person or group to another, or from one condition to another.

Familiar forms include dawlah, dūlah, mudāwalah, and tadāwul. Early Arabic uses them for changing fortunes in war, wealth passing between people, changing times, and transferring an activity or responsibility from one person to another.

The family also has a branch concerning deterioration and slackness. One lexical analysis recognizes two distinct principles for D W L:

أَحَدُهُمَا يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَحَوُّلِ شَيْءٍ مِنْ مَكَانٍ إِلَى مَكَانٍ، وَالْآخَرُ يَدُلُّ عَلَى ضَعْفٍ وَاسْتِرْخَاءٍ.

aḥaduhumā yadullu ʿalā taḥawwuli shayʾin min makānin ilā makānin wa-al-ākharu yadullu ʿalā ḍaʿfin wa-istirkhāʾin

“One indicates something passing from one place to another; the other indicates weakness and slackness.”

The distinction matters. Changes of condition, aging, and loss of strength can resemble one another, but all uses should not be derived from a single original image without historical evidence.

D W L occurs twice in two Qur’anic verses:

  • Nudāwiluhā in Āl ʿImrān 3:140, concerning the alternation of days of victory and hardship among people.
  • Dūlatan in Al-Ḥashr 59:7, concerning possessions circulating among the wealthy.

This count agrees with the Qur’anic morphological inventory.

The first verse concerns changing circumstances as a reality of human life and confrontation. The second explicitly rejects the circulation of a particular resource exclusively among the wealthy.

Circulation itself does not mean justice or injustice. What passes between people, who receives it, and its consequences determine each expression’s full meaning.

1. Semantic branches in early Arabic

Something passing from one place to another

The first use is clear in this expression:

تَدَاوَلَ الْقَوْمُ الشَّيْءَ بَيْنَهُمْ: إِذَا صَارَ مِنْ بَعْضِهِمْ إِلَى بَعْضٍ.

tadāwala al-qawmu al-shayʾa baynahum idhā ṣāra min baʿḍihim ilā baʿḍin

“People circulated something among themselves when it passed from some of them to others.”

The central feature is transfer.

When several people take turns using an implement, it passes from one person’s control to another’s. When a responsibility circulates within a group, different people hold it at different times.

These examples do not necessarily concern ownership or victory. Transfer is broader than either.

Tadāwul can therefore concern transferring an object, changing a position, or an opportunity passing between people.

Taking turns in victory

Arabic attests the expression:

كَانَتْ لَنَا عَلَيْهِمُ الدَّوْلَةُ

kānat lanā ʿalayhimu al-dawlatu

“Our turn to prevail over them came.”

Nothing like a cup or bag of money necessarily changes hands here. What changes is the position of superiority and victory.

A group can win one confrontation and lose another. Arabic expresses that shift through this family.

Adāla allāhu fulānan min fulānin is also attested for The God supporting one party and giving it victory over another.

Victory is one instance of changing circumstances, rather than the root’s exclusive meaning.

Changing times

Dālati al-ayyāmu concerns days and life’s circumstances changing.

Day is not merely a unit of time here; it can denote a situation characterizing a period or event.

A day of victory or defeat means more than twenty-four hours passing. It evokes the event and condition by which that day is known.

This usage helps explain wa-tilka al-ayyāmu nudāwiluhā bayna al-nās.

D W L itself does not mean time, day, or calendar. Al-ayyām expresses the days, while nudāwilu expresses their changing turns and circumstances.

2. The difference between dawlah and dūlah

A major lexical question is the distinction between dawlah, with an initial a vowel, and dūlah, with an initial u vowel.

Early Arabic offers several analyses.

Distinguishing the subject matter

One lexical analysis proposes this distinction:

Dūlah concerns wealth, while dawlah concerns war and victory.

Accordingly, dūlah suits wealth passing between people, and dawlah a turn of victory passing between opposing sides.

This fits the semantic fields of the two Qur’anic uses, although Āl ʿImrān uses a verb rather than the noun dawlah.

Distinguishing the transferred thing from the act of transfer

Another analysis is also proposed.

Dūlah can name the thing being circulated, whereas dawlah denotes the act of transfer or a change of turn.

The distinction is then between what is transferred and the process, rather than simply wealth versus war.

Treating the forms as equivalent

Not every lexical account accepts the distinction.

Another explanation permits either form for both wealth and war; some accounts report no clear difference between them.

The forms belong to one family, although the familiar wealth-and-war distinction is also reported.

Dūlah for wealth and dawlah for war is therefore an attested lexical tendency, rather than a rule without exceptions accepted by every Arabic speaker.

Plural forms

Duwal, diwal, and dūlāt are attested plurals of these words.

Their different vowels also relate to the singular forms and their morphological analysis.

An unvocalized دول therefore cannot be assumed to mean a political state, military victory, or possessions without examining the sentence.

Modern Arabic also uses dawlah for a state or governmental institution. That historical extension must not automatically enter Al-Ḥashr 59:7, which concerns possessions circulating, rather than defining a modern political institution.

3. Important derivatives and uses

Dāla yadūlu: changing circumstances and turns

Dāla is a basic triliteral verb with a weak middle wāw.

Dālati al-ayyāmu describes changing times and circumstances.

Dālat lahu al-dawlatu means someone’s turn of superiority has arrived; dālat ʿalayhi can describe circumstances turning against that person.

The verb concerns changing turns and situations, rather than necessarily an object’s physical movement.

Dāwala yudāwilu: circulating something between parties

Dāwala is Form III.

In dāwala al-shayʾa baynahum, something is passed or circulated among several parties.

Its verbal noun is mudāwalah.

Nudāwiluhā in Āl ʿImrān 3:140 belongs to this form.

Form III does not always establish equal or mutually voluntary exchange. In Āl ʿImrān, The God is the subject and the days are alternated among people.

Alternation and transfer follow from the verb, but consent or equality does not follow from its pattern alone.

Tadāwala yatadāwalu: passing between hands

Tadāwala is Form VI.

Arabic attests:

تَدَاوَلَتْهُ الْأَيْدِي

tadāwalathu al-aydī

“Hands took it one after another.”

The same thing can thus come under different people’s control at different times.

The construction distinguishes dāwala from tadāwala. In dāwala al-shayʾa baynahum, the subject circulates something among others; in tadāwala al-qawmu al-shayʾa, the people themselves participate in passing it and taking turns.

They share a semantic field, but differ in the relationship between subject and action.

Adāla and idālah: giving a turn of superiority

Adāla is Form IV.

Adālahu allāhu min ʿaduwwihi is attested for someone gaining victory over an enemy through The God’s granting it.

Idālah is its verbal noun and, in this setting, denotes superiority shifting in one side’s favor.

The use is not confined to war; it can concern changing turns of power in other situations.

Idālah does not always imply moral endorsement of the victor. Prevailing does not itself establish that someone’s conduct is right.

Indāla: moving elsewhere or becoming slack and hanging down

Indāla al-qawmu is attested for people moving from one place to another.

In the other branch, indāla baṭnuhu describes a belly becoming slack and hanging down.

These uses require separate semantic examination.

Dawālayka: one after another

Dawālayka is attested in early Arabic for actions performed alternately or repeatedly.

Its lexical explanation describes one party acting and the other then repeating a corresponding action.

It helps illustrate alternation, but dawālayka itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

Dawīl: dried vegetation

In the second branch, dawīl denotes vegetation that has dried or has aged since its growth.

It names vegetation that has lost its former feeding quality.

Dāla al-thawbu: clothing becoming worn

Dāla al-thawbu means the garment became old and worn.

Figuratively, dāla wadduhu also concerns affection fading and wearing thin.

The issue is not simply something passing between people, but the loss of former strength or freshness.

These uses belong to the weakness and slackness branch.

Rare uses

Dālah is attested in one lexical use for fame or becoming known. Dūlūl is also attested for great difficulty or trouble.

Al-dawālī also has a use concerning a kind of grape in Ṭāʾif.

Similar forms, such as the plural of dāliyah, can historically belong to other roots. Appearing within one dictionary entry does not prove a common historical origin for every word.

Neither Qur’anic occurrence provides evidence for deterioration, slackness, fame, or a grape name.

4. Early Arabic witnesses and poetry

Alternation and reciprocal action in Suḥaym’s poetry

Suḥaym, known as ʿAbd Banī al-Ḥasḥās, says:

إِذَا شُقَّ بُرْدٌ شُقَّ بِالْبُرْدِ مِثْلُهُ
دَوَالَيْكَ حَتَّى لَيْسَ لِلْبُرْدِ لَابِسُ

idhā shuqqa burdun shuqqa bi-al-burdi mithluhu / dawālayka ḥattā laysa li-al-burdi lābisu

“Whenever a garment was torn, a matching garment was torn in return; one after another, until the garment had no wearer.”

The verse is explained through reciprocal behavior between two people, each tearing a garment in response to the other.

Dawālayka expresses the action’s repetition and alternation.

The witness matters because circulation here does not concern wealth, power, or military victory. What recurs is the reciprocal action itself.

The expression is also discussed in explaining constructions of repeated action.

Textual variants exist. Some transmissions read shuqqa bi-al-burdi burquʿun, and the ending ḥattā kullunā ghayru lābisi.

They do not change the idea of alternation, but should remain acknowledged in research.

Suḥaym lived in the early Islamic period. The verse is early Arabic evidence, but should not confidently be declared pre-Islamic.

Changing hardships in poetry attributed to Ḍabbāb ibn Sabʿ

This verse is attributed to Ḍabbāb ibn Sabʿ al-Ḥanẓalī:

جَزَوْنِي بِمَا رَبَّيْتُهُمْ وَحَمَلْتُهُمْ
كَذَلِكَ مَا إِنَّ الْخُطُوبَ دَوَالُ

jazawnī bi-mā rabbaytuhum wa-ḥamaltuhum / kadhālika mā inna al-khuṭūba dawālu

“They repaid me thus for raising and supporting them; indeed, life’s hardships keep changing.”

Dawāl concerns events changing and alternating.

The poet presents his experience as part of the changes in human circumstances over a lifetime.

The use is close to dālati al-ayyāmu, but does not make calamity or hardship inherent in D W L. Khuṭūb supplies the hardships that alternate here.

The date of composition is not securely established in the transmission examined. Its attribution can be reported without declaring the verse certainly pre-Islamic.

Spears alternating in poetry attributed to Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī

A verse attributed to Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī reads:

وَلَقَدْ أَشْهَدُ الرِّمَاحَ تُدَالِي
فِي صُدُورِ الْكُمَاةِ طَعْنَ الدَّرِيَّهْ

wa-laqad ashhadu al-rimāḥa tudālī / fī ṣudūri al-kumāti ṭaʿna al-dariyyah

“I have witnessed spears striking in turn in warriors’ breasts, like striking a practice target.”

Tudālī is morphologically significant.

One morphological analysis understands an intended tudāwilu with some consonants changing places.

If accepted, the verse attests a circulation-related use within a martial image.

The transmitted poetic form tudālī must be distinguished from the morphological analysis deriving it from tudāwilu.

It should therefore not be silently replaced with tadāwul when quoting the poem.

Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī is a recognized pre-Islamic poet, and the verse is transmitted as his. The account of metathesis in tudālī nevertheless remains a lexical explanation, rather than something established by the visible form alone.

Old vegetation in al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī’s poetry

Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī says:

شَهْرَيْ رَبِيعٍ لَا تَذُوقُ لَبُونُهُمْ
إِلَّا حُمُوضًا وَخَمَّةً وَدَوِيلَا

shahray rabīʿin lā tadhūqu labūnuhum / illā ḥumūḍan wakhmatan wa-dawīlan

“During the two Rabīʿ months, their milch camels taste only unwholesome salt plants and old, dry herbage.”

Dawīl names dried or old vegetation whose feeding quality has deteriorated.

The verse illustrates the second branch: deterioration and weakness.

Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī was an Islamic and Umayyad-era poet. The verse is therefore evidence of early Arabic usage after Islam.

What the witnesses establish

The poetic witnesses support two points.

First, circulation and changing turns are not confined to political power or wealth. Reciprocal action, life’s events, and even military encounters can be described through this family.

Second, deterioration and weakness are attested in early usage and must remain part of the study.

The existence of both branches does not establish that they historically arose from a single tangible image.

5. Nudāwiluhā in Āl ʿImrān 3:140

Āl ʿImrān 3:140

إِنْ يَمْسَسْكُمْ قَرْحٌ فَقَدْ مَسَّ الْقَوْمَ قَرْحٌ مِثْلُهُ ۚ وَتِلْكَ الْأَيَّامُ نُدَاوِلُهَا بَيْنَ النَّاسِ وَلِيَعْلَمَ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَيَتَّخِذَ مِنْكُمْ شُهَدَاءَ ۗ وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الظَّالِمِينَ

in yamsaskum qarḥun fa-qad massa al-qawma qarḥun mithluhu wa-tilka al-ayyāmu nudāwiluhā bayna al-nāsi wa-li-yaʿlama allāhu alladhīna āmanū wa-yattakhidha minkum shuhadāʾa wa-allāhu lā yuḥibbu al-ẓālimīna

Forqan’s installed translation:

“If a wound has touched you, a similar wound has touched the other people. These days We alternate among people, and so that The God may know those who believe and take witnesses from among you. The God does not love the wrongdoers.”

Morphology and syntax

Nudāwiluhā belongs to dāwala yudāwilu, Form III.

It has two components:

Nudāwilu is a first-person plural imperfect verb in the indicative mood.

Hā is the attached object pronoun referring to al-ayyām, the days.

The God is the subject, speaking in the first-person plural.

Bayna al-nās identifies those among whom the alternation occurs.

The construction consequently means:

“We alternate those days among people.”

The days are not the subject. The God is the verb’s subject, while al-ayyām is its object through hā.

What does alternating days mean?

The preceding clause describes a wound or injury affecting two groups.

The context therefore concerns more than calendar days passing.

One interpretation relates the verse to Muslims and polytheists at Badr and Uḥud: the Muslims prevailed at Badr and faced defeat and injury at Uḥud.

Nudāwiluhā can be explained through nuṣarrifuhā: We alternate days and circumstances of superiority and injury among people.

In this reading, al-ayyām denotes particular days of confrontation and their consequences.

Alternate is lexically appropriate because it preserves changing turns and circumstances.

Does alternation make truth and falsehood equal?

No.

This is an essential limit in understanding the verse.

The Qur’an describes days of victory and hardship alternating among people, but that does not make every side winning a battle necessarily right.

A group’s defeat likewise does not establish that all its members were wrong or unjust.

The verse itself ends with wa-allāhu lā yuḥibbu al-ẓālimīn: The God does not love the wrongdoers.

Practical superiority changing hands does not change the criterion of truth and injustice.

A person or group can possess power temporarily while continuing to act unjustly.

Someone can also suffer injury while remaining steadfast in right conduct.

The distinction follows from the verse and its context, rather than nudāwilu alone.

Does the verse promise that every defeat will become victory?

Nudāwiluhā itself contains no certain individual promise of that kind.

The verse describes alternating days and changing circumstances without fixing each group’s exact time, order, or share of victory.

It does not guarantee that each person will win as much as they previously lost, or that every society will regain its former power after weakness.

Those conclusions exceed the word and sentence’s meaning.

The verse nevertheless reminds its audience that the present situation, whether victory or injury, should not be imagined as permanent and unchangeable.

Relationship to trials and steadfastness

Āl ʿImrān 3:139 reads:

وَلَا تَهِنُوا وَلَا تَحْزَنُوا

wa-lā tahinū wa-lā taḥzanū

“Do not weaken or grieve.”

After the verse under discussion, Āl ʿImrān 3:141 concerns believers’ purification, and Āl ʿImrān 3:142 effort and patience.

This sequence does more than report military superiority changing sides. It attends to how people respond to defeat and hardship.

Mudāwalah itself does not mean purification, patience, or trial.

Those concepts arise from separate expressions in the verses and must remain distinct from the root’s lexical meaning.

6. Dūlatan in Al-Ḥashr 59:7: exclusive circulation of possessions

Al-Ḥashr 59:7

مَا أَفَاءَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْقُرَىٰ فَلِلَّهِ وَلِلرَّسُولِ وَلِذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ كَيْ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً بَيْنَ الْأَغْنِيَاءِ مِنْكُمْ ۚ وَمَا آتَاكُمُ الرَّسُولُ فَخُذُوهُ وَمَا نَهَاكُمْ عَنْهُ فَانْتَهُوا ۚ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ ۖ إِنَّ اللَّهَ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

mā afāʾa allāhu ʿalā rasūlihi min ahli al-qurā fa-li-allāhi wa-li-al-rasūli wa-li-dhī al-qurbā wa-al-yatāmā wa-al-masākīni wa-ibni al-sabīli kay lā yakūna dūlatan bayna al-aghniyāʾi minkum wa-mā ātākumu al-rasūlu fa-khudhūhu wa-mā nahākum ʿanhu fa-intahū wa-ittaqū allāha inna allāha shadīdu al-ʿiqābi

Forqan’s installed translation:

“Whatever The God restored to His messenger from the people of the towns belongs to The God, the messenger, relatives, orphans, the needy, and the traveler, so that it does not circulate only among the wealthy among you. Take what the messenger gives you, and refrain from what he forbids you. Be mindful of The God; The God is severe in punishment.”

The form dūlatan

Dūlah is a noun, with an initial u vowel and an a vowel on lām in the familiar reading.

Dūlatan is indefinite and accusative.

In the familiar analysis, it is the predicate of yakūna. The implied subject refers to the possessions under discussion: what The God restored to the messenger.

Kay expresses purpose or cause; lā expresses negation.

The expression:

كَيْ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً بَيْنَ الْأَغْنِيَاءِ مِنْكُمْ

kay lā yakūna dūlatan bayna al-aghniyāʾi minkum

clarifies the stated purpose: these possessions must not become something circulating only among the wealthy.

Differences of reading and case analysis

The familiar reading has:

كَيْ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً

kay lā yakūna dūlatan

The verb yakūna is masculine, and dūlatan is accusative.

The reader Abū Jaʿfar, however, reads:

كَيْ لَا تَكُونَ دُولَةٌ

kay lā takūna dūlatun

with the feminine verb and nominative noun.

Under the complete-verb analysis of takūna, dūlatun is the nominative subject and the expression denies such circulation occurring. Under an incomplete-verb analysis, dūlatun can be its subject and bayna al-aghniyāʾ its predicate. The nominative construction therefore allows more than one analysis.

A reading with an initial a vowel, dawlah, is also reported, although the familiar reading has u.

These differences matter grammatically and lexically, but remain within preventing these possessions from circulating exclusively among the wealthy.

Does the verse prohibit wealth from circulating?

No. It does not reject all circulation of possessions; it addresses their circulation exclusively among the wealthy.

The distinction is fundamental.

Something passing between several people can lexically be called tadāwul.

Here, however, the possessions must not circulate only among the privileged while other groups are excluded from benefiting.

In this reading, the restored possessions must not fall under the wealthy’s control to circulate among themselves as they please.

The verse concerns the range of beneficiaries of a resource, rather than merely a change of ownership.

The surrounding context

Al-Ḥashr 59:6 explains that the audience did not spur horses or camels for the restored possessions.

Al-Ḥashr 59:7 names the groups benefiting from them.

Al-Ḥashr 59:8 describes needy emigrants driven from their homes and possessions.

Al-Ḥashr 59:9 describes people preferring others to themselves despite being in need.

Kay lā yakūna dūlatan bayna al-aghniyāʾi minkum therefore stands within verses concerning possessions, deprivation, need, and access to resources.

Preventing exclusive control is an explicit purpose in arranging this particular resource.

The scope of the argument must remain precise, however.

The verse concerns mā afāʾa allāhu. It does not directly supply every detail of a modern economic system, such as tax rates, corporate ownership, or market regulation.

Its stated principle against resources being restricted to the wealthy is nevertheless clear and can inform wider study of Qur’anic economic justice.

Does dūlatan mean government here?

Modern usage commonly applies dawlah to a state or government.

That meaning must not automatically be imposed on the Qur’anic word.

Dūlah in Al-Ḥashr 59:7 concerns possessions passing between hands.

Political state or government would move the sentence away from its financial context.

The modern term can be considered a historical extension within the family of changing power and turns of rule. It must not displace the attested Qur’anic meaning.

7. Comparing the two Qur’anic uses

The two verses differ in verb and noun, but both involve transfer and circulation.

Āl ʿImrān describes changing circumstances as a reality, whereas Al-Ḥashr prevents exclusive circulation of possessions.

Reading them together does not make every kind of circulation good or every stable condition bad.

The root concerns transfer and movement. Evaluation follows each verse’s subject and context.

8. Relationships with other Qur’anic concepts

D W L and D W R: circulation and rotation

Dāra yadūru belongs to D W R and concerns turning and circling.

Dālati al-ayyāmu is lexically explained through dārat.

The roots are nevertheless distinct.

In tangible uses, D W R can concern circular movement and returning to a starting point.

Familiar D W L uses more often emphasize changing turns or something passing between different parties.

Possessions can pass from one person to another without following a circular path.

Circulation can translate either root, but resemblance in the translation does not prove identical historical origins or precise meanings.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 reads:

تِجَارَةً حَاضِرَةً تُدِيرُونَهَا بَيْنَكُمْ

tijāratan ḥāḍiratan tudīrūnahā baynakum

“A present transaction that you circulate among yourselves.”

The expression belongs to D W R and is semantically close to tadāwul, because goods or money pass between people in such trading.

The relationship is useful, but tudīrūnahā does not belong to D W L in a root count.

Study of D W R

D W L and D W M: changing turns and continuity

D W M concerns continuing and remaining established.

The main branch of D W L concerns changing positions and turns.

They are not absolute opposites.

A system of circulation can itself continue over time: participants’ turns change while the rule of taking turns remains.

Circulation and continuity can coexist while expressing different aspects.

Study of D W M

D W L and N W B: circulation and turns

N W B concerns returning, visiting, and receiving a turn in Arabic.

In work or access to something, tanāwub and tadāwul can be close in meaning.

N W B can emphasize repeatedly approaching a destination or someone’s turn arriving, while D W L emphasizes something or a situation passing between parties.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute distinction.

Study of N W B

D W L and Q S M: circulating and dividing possessions

Q S M concerns dividing and apportioning shares.

Al-Ḥashr 59:7 assigns the possessions to specified groups and prevents their exclusive circulation among the wealthy.

This concerns allocating and distributing resources.

Dūlah alone does not mean just distribution. Circulation can be exclusive or otherwise; the complete construction establishes the difference.

Q S M itself also does not guarantee fairness; something can be divided unequally.

Neither root is therefore a complete lexical equivalent of economic justice.

D W L and F Y ʾ: circulation and restored possessions

Al-Ḥashr 59:7 places dūlah and afāʾa in the same sentence.

Afāʾa belongs to F Y ʾ and concerns restoring or returning.

The context concerns possessions restored from one group into another’s sphere of control.

Fayʾ and dūlah are nevertheless distinct concepts.

Fayʾ identifies the kind of possessions in context; dūlah describes their circulation or passing between hands.

Dūlah should therefore not itself be defined as spoils, fayʾ, or war property here.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

The brief definition should acknowledge both semantic branches, while each contextual explanation emphasizes the relevant use.

Qur’anic wordBrief root meaningMeaning in this verse

دُولَةً — Al-Ḥashr 59:7Circulation and something passing between handsPossessions circulating among the wealthy

For Āl ʿImrān 3:140, Forqan’s “These days We alternate among people” is appropriate and faithful to the wording.

For Al-Ḥashr 59:7, “so that it does not circulate only among the wealthy among you” also conveys the sentence well.

The root explanation can mention alternating victory, possessions circulating, changing turns, and the deterioration branch.

A direct translation should not replace dūlah with a fixed law of alternating victory, political state, governmental system, socialism, capitalism, or punishment of the wealthy.

These are either terms from other historical settings or topics for separate study of economic and political systems.

10. Conclusion

Early Arabic attests two semantic branches of D W L.

The first concerns transfer and changing turns, including alternating victory in war, possessions passing between hands, changing times, and activities performed in turns.

The second concerns weakness, slackness, and deterioration, including worn clothing, fading affection, and bodily parts becoming slack or hanging down.

The branches can be distinguished in usage; the examined evidence does not establish a single historical origin for all of them.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

Nudāwiluhā in Āl ʿImrān 3:140 concerns days of victory and hardship alternating among people. Superiority and injury are not fixed conditions, but practical victory does not always establish rightness, nor must every defeat become victory.

Dūlatan in Al-Ḥashr 59:7 concerns possessions circulating. The context explicitly prevents these possessions from passing only among the wealthy.

The uses differ in subject while sharing transfer and circulation.

Proposed final definition for Forqan:

The main branch of D W L denotes something or a situation passing from one party to another and turns circulating among individuals or groups. Uses include possessions circulating, alternating victory, and changing times. Another branch concerns slackness and deterioration. Nudāwiluhā in Āl ʿImrān 3:140 concerns days of victory and hardship alternating among people; dūlatan in Al-Ḥashr 59:7 concerns possessions passing between hands, with their exclusive circulation among the wealthy rejected.
