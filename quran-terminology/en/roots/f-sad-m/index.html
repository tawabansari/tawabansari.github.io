Cracking, breaking, and rupture: from damage to a ring to a handhold that never breaks

The Arabic root F Ṣ M concerns cracking, breaking, and damage to something's continuity. In its distinctive physical use, it denotes a fissure or fracture that does not necessarily separate the parts completely. A cracked ring, an armlet with a split in it, or a wall whose structure has fractured illustrates this family.

Arabic lexicography recognizes a distinction between faṣm and qaṣm: the former particularly denotes cracking or fracture without complete separation, while the latter more often describes a break that entirely severs the connection between the parts. Actual usage is not always confined to this boundary: infiṣām also denotes the rupture or dissolution of a bond.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as «انْفِصَامَ», in al-Baqarah 2:256:

لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا

lā infiṣāma lahā

Al-Baqarah 2:256

“There is no break in it.”

This phrase describes «الْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ», the firmest handhold. The Quran speaks of a handhold that a person grasps and in which no fracture or rupture occurs.

Understanding this image requires distinguishing the historical meaning of faṣm, its morphological forms, its relationship to neighboring roots, and the role of negation in the verse.

1. Historical meaning and early Arabic usage

In classical Arabic, «فَصَمَ الشَّيْءَ» means to split or break something, particularly when it is fractured but its parts have not separated completely.

Faṣm names the fracture, and mafṣūm describes something that has sustained such damage.

Early Arabic dictionaries explain this family through the expression «انصداع الشيء من غير بينونة»: something's cracking without its parts becoming completely detached.

This definition relates to tangible uses of the word in everyday life.

Fractures in rings and ornaments

Rings, armlets, and anklets are familiar objects described through faṣm.

A metal ring may crack under pressure or impact while remaining a single piece. Its continuity is damaged even though its two sides have not completely separated.

Arabic can describe such a ring as «مَفْصُوم».

This image helps explain the root because a ring's usefulness depends on its continuity and strength. A cracked ring may still be grasped or used, but it may no longer offer the same assurance.

A ring's becoming unsafe is nevertheless a possible consequence of the fracture, rather than the direct definition of faṣm. The word itself concerns a fissure or damage to continuity.

Cracks and damage in buildings

Faṣm is also recorded for structural damage: «فُصِمَ جانبُ البيت» is explained as the collapse of part of a building. This usage should not be restricted to a superficial crack.

The expression «فَصْمَة» can denote a crack in a wall, another application of the family to hard structures.

The distinction between a crack, the collapse of part of a building, and the destruction of the whole building remains important. The wording and context determine the extent of the damage; the root alone does not fix it.

This branch therefore concerns damage to an interconnected structure. Some uses emphasize a fissure, while others can describe the collapse of part of that structure.

A poetic witness and its historical limits

In a poem by the Umayyad poet Dhu al-Rummah, a gazelle fawn curling its body in sleep is described as follows:

كَأَنَّهُ دُمْلُجٌ مِنْ فِضَّةٍ نَبَهٌ
فِي مَلْعَبٍ مِنْ عَذَارَى الْحَيِّ مَفْصُومُ

ka-annahu dumlujun min fiḍḍatin nabahun / fī malʿabin min ʿadhārā al-ḥayyi mafṣūm

“As though it were a silver armlet, left behind and cracked in the young women's playground.” This rendering follows the explanation of «نَبَه» as something forgotten; another recorded explanation emphasizes its conspicuous brightness.

The poet likens the sleeping fawn's curved body to a silver armlet. Its shape suggests an armlet whose normal continuity or shape has been damaged without breaking into two separate pieces.

This witness helps distinguish mafṣūm from something completely broken apart.

Dhu al-Rummah lived in the Umayyad period, during the first and early second Islamic centuries. His poetry is evidence from near the early Islamic period, rather than definite evidence of language predating the Quran.

Al-Akhṭal, another Umayyad poet, also uses faṣm in connection with an anklet. This witness supports the association with breaking or opening a ring-shaped ornament, but it too is not a pre-Islamic witness.

Independent, datable evidence would be needed to assign these particular poetic uses to the pre-Islamic period.

The historical account must therefore acknowledge the limits of its evidence. In classical Arabic and poetry near the period of revelation, faṣm concerns splitting and breaking, particularly without complete separation. Infiṣām in the Quran itself securely attests this family in the first Islamic century; later dictionaries alone cannot establish its exact meaning in earlier centuries.

Extension to ending and interruption

Other uses of this family are also recorded in Arabic.

«انْفَصَمَ الْمَطَرُ» describes rain's stopping, while «أَفْصَمَتْ عَنْهُ الْحُمَّى» means that someone's fever abated.

These uses concern neither a ring nor another hard object. What ends is the continuation of a condition or event.

Their semantic relationship to fracture is intelligible: just as a body's continuity can be damaged or lost, an event's continuation can be interrupted.

This explanation does not establish a definite historical progression from breaking rings to the cessation of rain or fever. The evidence establishes these usages, rather than necessarily the order in which they developed.

Two related applications can thus be recognized: splitting or breaking a structure, and the interruption or ending of continuity or ongoing activity.

A defensible semantic core is damage to or loss of continuity; its distinctive physical image is a crack or fracture without complete separation.

2. Morphological forms and the structure of infiṣām

Important forms recorded in Arabic include the following:

«فَصَمَ» is a transitive verb meaning to split or break something.

«فَصْم» is the basic verb's verbal noun, naming breaking or making a fissure.

«مَفْصُوم» is a passive participle describing something cracked or broken.

«فَصْمَة» denotes a crack or fissure.

«انْفَصَمَ» is a form VII verb denoting something's splitting, breaking, or coming apart.

«انْفِصَام» is the verbal noun of «انْفَصَمَ».

«تَفَصَّمَ» is also recorded for splitting or breaking apart.

Within this family, «فَصَمَ» can express the act of splitting or breaking, and «انْفَصَمَ» the occurrence of the break in the object.

For example, «فَصَمَ الْحَلْقَةَ» describes an agent breaking a ring, whereas «انْفَصَمَتِ الْحَلْقَةُ» describes the ring's breaking or coming apart.

This difference reflects a familiar function of form VII. Morphology alone, however, does not establish that the fracture happened spontaneously, without an external agent, slowly, or under a particular pressure.

Infiṣām names the event or condition of rupture. Its cause and manner must be determined from context or independent evidence.

The Quranic form

Of these forms, only the verbal noun «انْفِصَامَ» occurs in the Quran.

It appears in the expression «لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا».

Here «لَا» negates the entire category: it denies any infiṣām of the thing to which the pronoun in «لَهَا» refers.

«انْفِصَامَ» is the noun governed by this negative «لا», and the pronoun in «لَهَا» refers to «الْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ».

The sentence therefore means that there is no rupture in that handhold.

The negation is comprehensive. The Quran does not merely say that the handhold does not break under ordinary conditions or is rarely damaged; it denies infiṣām of it.

The verse also does not report a rupture occurring. The word appears in a negative construction: the very fracture or rupture that it names is denied of the firm handhold.

3. F Ṣ M and Q Ṣ M: fracture without separation and a separating break

The comparison with Q Ṣ M is among the most important lexical distinctions for this root.

The two families are close in sound and both concern breaking, but lexical explanations recognize a distinction between them.

Faṣm particularly describes a fracture whose parts have not completely separated.

Qaṣm more often describes a severe break that severs their connection.

If a ring cracks but remains a single piece, faṣm is an appropriate description. If it breaks into two detached parts, qaṣm may be more appropriate.

This distinction is established in early Arabic explanations, but should not become an exceptionless rule for every usage. Infiṣām can also describe a bond's complete severance.

Qaṣm in the Quran

Al-Anbiya 21:11 states:

وَكَمْ قَصَمْنَا مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ كَانَتْ ظَالِمَةً وَأَنْشَأْنَا بَعْدَهَا قَوْمًا آخَرِينَ

wa-kam qaṣamnā min qaryatin kānat ẓālimatan wa-anshaʾnā baʿdahā qawman ākharīn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11

“How many unjust settlements We shattered, and after them brought forth other people.”

In this verse, «قَصَمْنَا» describes the shattering and destruction of communities characterized by wrongdoing.

Its association with bringing forth another people indicates the destruction or removal of a community, rather than a minor fracture in an object.

This use accords with the semantic field of qaṣm and the severity of its breaking.

By comparison, infiṣām in al-Baqarah 2:256 concerns a handhold of which The God denies every rupture.

Why lā infiṣām matters

One explanation of the distinction holds that denying even a crack or nonseparating fracture of a handhold all the more excludes its complete breaking apart.

This is a defensible semantic explanation.

Within that image, something that develops no crack or fissure will not break into two pieces either.

The choice of infiṣām thus suits a completely secure handhold: the verse excludes every rupture, going beyond merely saying that the parts do not detach.

This rhetorical explanation should not be treated as the sole certain reason for the word's selection. Infiṣām itself can denote the rupture of rings and bonds. The Quranic expression relates both to the specific image of cracking and to the broader meaning of rupture.

4. «لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا» in al-Baqarah 2:256

The root's only Quranic occurrence appears in a verse addressing freedom of belief, the distinction of right guidance, and the choice between false authority and The God:

لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ قَدْ تَبَيَّنَ الرُّشْدُ مِنَ الْغَيِّ فَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ بِالطَّاغُوتِ وَيُؤْمِنْ بِاللَّهِ فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا وَاللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

lā ikrāha fī al-dīni qad tabayyana al-rushdu mina al-ghayyi fa-man yakfur bi-l-ṭāghūti wa-yuʾmin bi-llāhi fa-qadi istamsaka bi-l-ʿurwati al-wuthqā lā infiṣāma lahā wa-llāhu samīʿun ʿalīm

Al-Baqarah 2:256

“There is no compulsion in religion; right guidance has become distinct from error. Whoever rejects false authority and believes in The God has grasped the firmest handhold: there is no break in it. And The God is Hearing, Knowing.”

Al-Baqarah 2:256

In this verse, three semantic families precede the expression denying rupture:

«اسْتَمْسَكَ», from M S K, concerns grasping and holding firmly.

«الْعُرْوَة», from ʿ R W, names a handhold or loop that can be grasped.

«الْوُثْقَىٰ», from W TH Q, expresses the handhold's exceptional firmness and reliability.

Then «لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا» declares the absence of any rupture in that same handhold.

Together these words form a single image: a person grasping a handhold that is firm and admits no fracture or rupture.

The handhold's firmness and the act of grasping it

An important distinction is between the handhold and the person holding it.

«الْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ» is the handhold itself.

«اسْتَمْسَكَ» expresses the act of grasping it.

«لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا» concerns the handhold's firmness.

The verse thus addresses two different matters: human choice and action, and the firmness of what is chosen.

This distinction matters for understanding the sentence. An unbreakable handhold does not make it impossible for someone to let go of it.

A strong handle might never break, yet the person holding it can release their grip. The handle has not broken; the person's behavior has changed.

This example only clarifies the difference between the concepts. It should not be understood as a physical description of the reality intended by the verse.

The Quranic phrase denies infiṣām of the handhold; it does not say that human choice cannot change.

It therefore cannot be translated as “whoever believes once can never abandon their faith.”

That conclusion would shift the subject of the negation from the handhold to the person's behavior, whereas the pronoun in «لَهَا» clearly refers to «العروة الوثقى».

Firmness and informed choice

The verse begins with «لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ», then states that right guidance has become distinct from error.

It next speaks of someone who rejects false authority and believes in The God.

This sequence associates grasping the handhold with human understanding and choice.

The handhold's firmness belongs to the truth on which one relies; holding fast is the person's own act and responsibility.

External compulsion therefore cannot substitute for informed choice in the reality of faith. Coercion may produce outward behavior, but «اسْتَمْسَكَ» here follows the declaration of belief and rejection of false authority.

This becomes clearer in relation to the principle of no compulsion in religion. The Quran first denies coercion, then presents the firm handhold to a person who must take a position toward false authority and The God.

Freedom of choice is nevertheless not the lexical meaning of infiṣām. Freedom of belief follows from the opening and overall context; infiṣām names the rupture or fracture denied of the handhold.

5. Distinctions from neighboring roots

F Ṣ M and Q Ṭ ʿ: fracture and severance

Q Ṭ ʿ has a broad Quranic range, including cutting, separating, severing relationships, and ending continuity.

Al-Baqarah 2:27 states:

وَيَقْطَعُونَ مَا أَمَرَ اللَّهُ بِهِ أَنْ يُوصَلَ

wa-yaqṭaʿūna mā amara allāhu bihi an yūṣal

Al-Baqarah 2:27

“And sever what The God has commanded to be joined.”

Here «يَقْطَعُونَ» concerns severing a connection that should have remained intact.

Faṣm can likewise concern rupture, but its distinctive physical meaning also includes cracking and fracture without complete separation.

Qaṭʿ and faṣm thus overlap in some outcomes without being exact synonyms in every use.

F Ṣ M, F Ṣ L, and F L Q: fracture, distinction, and splitting

F Ṣ L in Quranic language concerns separation, distinction, differentiation, and establishing boundaries between things.

Al-Shuara 26:63 gives an example from the separate root F L Q:

فَانْفَلَقَ فَكَانَ كُلُّ فِرْقٍ كَالطَّوْدِ الْعَظِيمِ

fa-nfalaqa fa-kāna kullu firqin ka-l-ṭawdi al-ʿaẓīm

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63

“Then it split, and each part was like a mighty mountain.”

This verb belongs to F L Q, rather than F Ṣ L. It clearly illustrates splitting into distinct parts and provides a conceptual comparison.

For F Ṣ L itself, al-Shura 42:21 uses «كَلِمَةُ الْفَصْلِ»: a word or decree of decisive judgment.

Faṣl can concern separating and establishing a distinction between two things even without any physical fracture.

Faṣm, in its distinctive physical use, concerns damage to or fracture of a structure.

Sharing the first two consonants does not establish a common origin for F Ṣ M and F Ṣ L.

F Ṣ M and N Q Ḍ: fracture and undoing a bond

N Q Ḍ is also used in the Quran for breaking or undoing covenants.

Al-Rad 13:25 states:

وَالَّذِينَ يَنْقُضُونَ عَهْدَ اللَّهِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مِيثَاقِهِ

wa-alladhīna yanquḍūna ʿahda allāhi min baʿdi mīthāqih

Al-Raʿd 13:25

“And those who break The God's covenant after its confirmation.”

Here «يَنْقُضُونَ» concerns undoing an obligation that had been established.

The important distinction is that naqḍ directly concerns covenant-breaking in such a context, whereas infiṣām in al-Baqarah 2:256 names a rupture denied within the image of the handhold.

The similarity therefore does not make «لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا» directly mean “no one breaks their covenant.” That meaning would require separate contextual evidence.

F Ṣ M and K S R: breaking generally and a particular fracture

Kasr is a general Arabic word for breaking and can encompass various kinds of fracture.

Faṣm in its familiar sense describes a more particular disruption of continuity, especially a fissure whose parts have not entirely separated.

Physical faṣm therefore involves a kind of fracture, while not every fracture necessarily has this particular form.

This distinction helps explain the word's precision without requiring an unusual, specialized equivalent in every natural translation.

F Ṣ M and W TH Q: fracture and firmness

Al-Baqarah 2:256 connects «الْوُثْقَىٰ» and infiṣām within one image.

Wuthqā expresses the handhold's exceptional firmness.

Infiṣām names a fracture or rupture that could compromise a connection's strength.

The verse first describes the handhold's firmness, then adds that it admits no rupture.

These are not pointless repetitions. The adjective «الوثقى» describes firmness, while «لا انفصام لها» explicitly denies fracture or rupture.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

Translation should preserve the distinction between the familiar physical meaning, its semantic extensions, and the specific Quranic use.

In physical contexts, «فَصَمَ» can be translated as “split,” “cracked,” or “broke,” with the explanation that its distinctive fracture does not necessarily detach the parts completely.

Faṣm can be rendered “crack,” “fissure,” or “fracture.”

Mafṣūm can mean “cracked” or “broken.”

Depending on context, «انْفَصَمَ» can mean “cracked,” “broke,” “ruptured,” or “came apart.”

Infiṣām likewise can be translated as “cracking,” “fracture,” or “rupture.”

In al-Baqarah 2:256, which concerns a firm handhold and grasping it, “break” is a suitable natural equivalent.

For the expression:

لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا

lā infiṣāma lahā

Al-Baqarah 2:256

The principal recommendation is:

“There is no break in it.”

In a flowing rendering of the complete clause:

“Has grasped the firmest handhold: a handhold in which there is no break.”

This preserves the verse's image without limiting infiṣām to one particular physical fracture.

“A handhold that never breaks” is also a natural, defensible rendering, although “there is no break in it” more closely follows the Arabic negative construction.

The lexical explanation can add that infiṣām includes cracking and fracture without complete separation, as well as rupture. The basic translation need not include every one of these meanings.

Expressions such as “no one can let go of it,” “no believer separates from it,” or “faith never disappears” do not directly translate «لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا». The sentence concerns the handhold's own firmness, which should not be transferred to the behavior of everyone holding it.

Modern uses of infiṣām in psychological discourse should likewise not be imported into the verse. They cannot determine the word's meaning in its Quranic context.

7. Summary

F Ṣ M concerns cracking, splitting, breaking, and rupture. In its distinctive physical use, a fracture can occur without complete separation, as with a ring or armlet that cracks while retaining its apparent unity.

Classical Arabic evidence and Umayyad poetry support this meaning. The poetic witnesses considered here do not, however, establish its precise pre-Islamic use, and their dates should not be moved backward.

Comparison with qaṣm shows that early lexical explanations especially associate faṣm with a nonseparating fracture, and qaṣm with a separating break. Infiṣām's use for the rupture of a bond nevertheless shows that this is not an absolute, exceptionless boundary.

Only «انْفِصَامَ» occurs in the Quran, within the negative sentence «لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا».

The sentence concerns «العروة الوثقى», the firm handhold a person grasps. The Quran denies every rupture of that handhold itself.

In al-Baqarah 2:256, holding fast to it is associated with rejecting false authority and believing in The God. Its firmness expresses the reliability of what a person consciously chooses.

The handhold's firmness and a person's continuing choice remain distinct matters. Its unbreakability does not by itself establish that no one can release their grip after grasping it.

The root can be defined as splitting, cracking, or breaking something's continuity, particularly without completely detaching its parts; more broadly, it concerns the rupture or ending of a connection or continuation.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 denies this rupture of the firm handhold. «لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا» clearly indicates that what one grasps in faith is no handhold liable to crack, break, or lose its continuity. Its firmness endures; grasping and continuing to hold it remain the person's responsibility.
