Qur’anic Root J R R

Pulling and drawing something along: from ropes and animal leads to drawing a person toward oneself

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Arabic jarra (جَرَّ) means pulling something and drawing it along. It can describe pulling a rope, drawing an animal by its lead, or dragging a load along the ground. Force is applied to an object in the direction of a pull.

The family has a broad Arabic range. Jarīr (جَرِير) names an animal’s lead; jarūr (جَرُور) can describe a horse resisting guidance; jarrār (جَرَّار) describes a great army drawing numerous followers along; and jarīrah (جَرِيرَة) denotes an offense whose consequences a person draws upon himself.

Only yajurruhu (يَجُرُّهُ) occurs in the Qur’an. It appears when Mūsā returns to his people angry and grieved, takes his brother Hārūn by the head, and draws him toward himself.

The usage fits the root’s direct physical meaning. The verb alone does not quantify the force, establish that Hārūn was injured, or identify Mūsā’s precise motive. It must be read alongside Mūsā’s words, Hārūn’s reply, and the narrative’s continuation.

1. Forms and semantic core

J R R is a doubled root: its second and third radicals are the same.

The basic verb jarra contracts the two rāʾ consonants of the underlying triliteral jarara (جَرَرَ). Its imperfect is yajurru and its verbal noun jarr.

Recorded forms include jarra, jarr, jarrara (جَرَّرَ), tajrīr (تَجْرِير), injarra (اِنْجَرَّ), ijtarra (اجْتَرَّ), istajarra (اسْتَجَرَّ), jārr (جَارّ), majrūr (مَجْرُور), jarīr, jarūr, jarrār, jarīrah, jarāʾir (جَرَائِر), and jirrah (جِرَّة).

Not all occur in the Qur’an. Its only form is yajurruhu in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence of yajurruhu

Yajurru is a third-person masculine singular imperfect in Form I, and -hu is its object pronoun: the one being pulled.

The root’s primary meaning

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«الْجِيمُ وَالرَّاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ مَدُّ الشَّيْءِ وَسَحْبُهُ.»

al-jīmu wa-al-rāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun, wa-huwa maddu al-shayʾi wa-saḥbuhu

That is: “Jīm and rāʾ have one principle: extending and drawing something along.”

He illustrates it with jarartu al-ḥabla (جَرَرْتُ الْحَبْلَ): I pulled the rope.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise gives al-jarru al-jadhbu (الْجَرُّ: الْجَذْبُ): jarr is pulling or drawing.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: pulling and drawing along

Both accounts identify a clear field: an applied force draws something from its earlier position or state in another direction.

This does not make every pull necessarily violent, injurious, or morally coercive. One may gently pull a rope, labor to move a load, or draw a resisting animal along.

Jarra primarily means pulling or moving something along a pull; force, resistance, and consequences depend on circumstances and contextual evidence. Brief root meaning: Pulling and drawing something along; moving it through a pulling force.

2. Physical imagery in early Arab life

Pulling a rope

Pulling a rope is among the root’s clearest usages.

Force is transmitted through a rope when someone draws one end toward himself. An object attached to the other end may also move.

Arabic says:

«جَرَرْتُ الْحَبْلَ»

jarartu al-ḥabla

That is: “I pulled the rope.”

Both the pulling action and the thing pulled are clear in this example.

Pulling a rope need not produce a complete change of location. Resistance may merely make it taut, or the attached object may move only a little.

Jarr alone therefore does not measure displacement.

Jarīr: an animal’s lead

Jarīr names a rope placed around a camel’s or another animal’s neck for restraint and guidance.

Lexical sources sometimes specify leather.

Pulling the lead can move the animal toward the handler or along a particular route.

Pulling and guiding are related here, but are not identical meanings.

Guidance may be the purpose, while jarra itself describes the pulling action.

Whether the animal willingly follows or resists is determined by its behavior, rather than a change in the verb’s basic meaning.

Jarīr is also a personal name. That proper-name use should not be confused with the common noun for a lead.

Al-Miṣbāḥ and Lisān: jarr and the animal’s lead

Jarūr: a horse resisting the lead

Ibn Fāris explains jarūr as a horse that resists being led.

He offers two accounts of its designation.

It may describe an animal continually being pulled, whose handler must keep drawing the lead to move it.

Alternatively, the horse itself pulls its handler toward it rather than following the handler’s direction.

Maqāyīs: two accounts of the resistant horse

Both involve pulling, but the direction of force differs.

The direction cannot be settled without identifying agent and object.

The horse’s resistance belongs to this particular descriptive use, rather than every derivative of the root.

Loads and animal movement

Jarrat al-ibilu (جَرَّتِ الْإِبِلُ) is recorded for camels traveling and grazing while carrying their loads along.

Jarra al-ibila (جَرَّ الْإِبِلَ) also describes gently driving or guiding camels.

Movement in these uses may be steady and gradual.

Slowness is not essential to jarra; another context can describe a rapid pull.

Jārr al-ḍabuʿ (جَارُّ الضَّبُعِ) is also used of heavy rain or a torrent that draws a hyena out of its den.

Lisān and Asās: camels’ movement and heavy rain

The force passes from human handling to moving water in this image.

The agent need not be human: floodwater can also carry something along.

3. Early poetry and extended meanings

A rope image in Laqīṭ ibn Yaʿmur

Laqīṭ ibn Yaʿmur al-Iyādī, known as a pre-Islamic poet, says:

«جَرَّتْ لِمَا بَيْنَنَا حَبْلَ الشَّمُوسِ فَلَا
يَأْسًا مُبِينًا أَرَى مِنْهَا وَلَا طَمَعَا»

jarrat li-mā baynanā ḥabla al-shamūsi fa-lā / yaʾsan mubīnan arā minhā wa-lā ṭamaʿā

That is: “In what lies between us she has drawn the rope of resistance; I see neither clear despair nor clear hope from her.” Maqāyīs also transmits narā, “we see,” in place of arā, “I see.” The supplied Arabic witness is retained; this variation does not change the root.

Dīwān of Laqīṭ ibn Yaʿmur: the rope witness and textual variation

Shamūs describes a resistant or unmanageable creature, particularly an animal difficult to tame. The poet brings that image into a human relationship.

The rope can picture a bond between people, with its pulling expressing the relationship’s difficulty.

The scene is figurative rather than a literal act of animal husbandry: the familiar lead-pulling action describes a relationship.

It witnesses figurative jarr in early Arabic.

That does not require every Qur’anic jarr to have a nonphysical meaning.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 explicitly combines taking Hārūn by the head and pulling, supplying clear physical context.

Jarrār: a great army

Maqāyīs records jarrār for a great army.

Ibn Fāris explains it through drawing numerous followers along: a force with many troops and an extensive following.

He quotes:

«سَتَنْدَمُ إِذْ يَأْتِي عَلَيْكَ رَعِيلُنَا
بِأَرْعَنَ جَرَّارٍ كَثِيرٍ صَوَاهِلُهْ»

sa-tandamu idh yaʾtī ʿalayka raʿīlunā / bi-arʿana jarrārin kathīrin ṣawāhiluh

That is: “You will regret it when our advance force comes upon you with a vast army, its horses neighing in great numbers.”

Jarrār describes the great army.

The usage concerns the multitude and its continuity as one body; the army is not literally dragged along the ground like a rope.

The pulling image extends to accompanying and drawing along numerous forces.

Maqāyīs does not name the poet, but the editorial annotation to Al-Basīṭ at Al-Anʿām 6:27 attributes the verse to Burj ibn Mus'hir al-Ṭāʾī, citing Majāz al-Qurʾān and other sources. Attribution should be distinguished from independently dating composition; without that evidence, the verse is not presented as certainly pre-Islamic.

Editorial annotation to Al-Basīṭ at Al-Anʿām 6:27: attribution of the army witness

Jarīrah: what someone draws upon himself

Jarīrah is an important nonphysical use.

It denotes an offense whose responsibility or consequences return to its perpetrator.

Ibn Fāris explains its designation as drawing something toward oneself.

An action thus brings something along that remains attached to the person responsible.

The image matters socially and juridically, since a person’s conduct may affect himself and others.

Jarīrah alone nevertheless does not establish a rule transferring responsibility to the offender’s family or tribe.

The form does not occur in the Qur’an, which uses other vocabulary for offenses, sins, and responsibility. Those words require separate examination.

The connection with pulling is an Arabic extension, rather than the direct meaning of yajurruhu in Mūsā’s story.

Ijtarra: bringing food back up

Jirrah also names food that a camel or other ruminant brings back from its digestive tract to its mouth.

Ijtarra al-baʿīru (اجْتَرَّ الْبَعِيرُ) describes chewing the cud.

Something is brought back toward the mouth from within the digestive tract.

This can be compared with drawing and bringing back, but ijtirār has its own specific usage.

Lexical sources: jarīrah, jirrah, and ijtirār

It should not always be reduced to dragging an object on the ground.

Modern Persian also uses ijtirār for repeating or returning to thought or speech. Such technical extensions should not be imposed on Qur’anic yajurruhu.

4. The sole Qur’anic occurrence: Mūsā and Hārūn

Al-Aʿrāf, verse 150

وَلَمَّا رَجَعَ مُوسَىٰ إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا قَالَ بِئْسَمَا خَلَفْتُمُونِي مِنْ بَعْدِي أَعَجِلْتُمْ أَمْرَ رَبِّكُمْ وَأَلْقَى الْأَلْوَاحَ وَأَخَذَ بِرَأْسِ أَخِيهِ يَجُرُّهُ إِلَيْهِ قَالَ ابْنَ أُمَّ إِنَّ الْقَوْمَ اسْتَضْعَفُونِي وَكَادُوا يَقْتُلُونَنِي فَلَا تُشْمِتْ بِيَ الْأَعْدَاءَ وَلَا تَجْعَلْنِي مَعَ الْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-lammā rajaʿa Mūsā ilā qawmihi ghaḍbāna asifan qāla biʾsamā khalaftumūnī min baʿdī a-ʿajiltum amra rabbikum wa-alqā al-alwāḥa wa-akhadha bi-raʾsi akhīhi yajurruhu ilayhi qāla ibna umma inna al-qawma istaḍʿafūnī wa-kādū yaqtulūnanī fa-lā tushmit biya al-aʿdāʾa wa-lā tajʿalnī maʿa al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn

“When Moses returned to his people, angry and grieved, he said, “Evil is how you succeeded me after I left. Did you seek to hasten your Lord’s command?” He threw down the tablets and seized his brother by the head, pulling him toward himself. He said, “Son of my mother, the people overpowered me and nearly killed me. Do not make the enemies rejoice over me, and do not place me with the unjust people.””

The verse follows an account of a group among Mūsā’s people worshipping the calf.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 describes their adopting the calf image after his departure. Al-Aʿrāf 7:149 describes their remorse and recognition of having gone astray.

Mūsā then returns and confronts what has happened.

The Qur’an describes him as ghaḍbāna asifan, angry and grieved. He rebukes the people, throws down the tablets, and takes his brother by the head, drawing him toward himself.

The conjunction of these actions matters for yajurruhu. Their order of mention with wa- does not by itself establish every detail of chronological sequence.

Akhadha and yajurruhu

The verse says:

«وَأَخَذَ بِرَأْسِ أَخِيهِ يَجُرُّهُ إِلَيْهِ»

wa-akhadha bi-raʾsi akhīhi yajurruhu ilayhi

Two distinct verbs occur.

Akhadha, from A KH DH, describes taking or seizing.

Yajurruhu, from J R R, describes pulling or drawing.

Mūsā takes his brother by the head and then, or simultaneously, draws him toward himself.

Syntactically, yajurruhu ilayhi describes the circumstance accompanying the taking. They are related actions. Grammatical analyses treat the clause as circumstantial, so it should not simply be a separate action at an entirely different time.

Maḥmūd Ṣāfī, Al-Jadwal: the circumstantial clause yajurruhu

Taking and pulling nevertheless differ.

Someone may take another’s hand or head without moving the person. This verse explicitly adds pulling.

A translation giving only “took his brother by the head” therefore omits part of the action.

The role of ilayhi

Ilayhi follows yajurruhu.

The preposition and pronoun specify the direction: toward Mūsā.

Yajurruhu describes pulling, while ilayhi supplies its direction.

This matters in translation.

“He pulled him” conveys the verb; “he pulled him toward himself” better expresses the full construction.

The installed translation explains -hu in yajurruhu through Hārūn himself; the construction’s connection with raʾs, head, must also be considered. In either case, the scene concerns drawing the brother by the head. The masculine pronoun alone does not establish one exclusive antecedent.

The sentence concerns taking Hārūn by the head and drawing him, rather than severing a head or treating it as detached from the person.

Must jarr mean a violent pull?

The root does not specify a fixed degree of force.

Arabic uses the verb for ropes, guiding animals, moving loads, and drawing a person.

Mūsā’s anger and grief show that the scene is not a calm, ordinary encounter.

They nevertheless do not quantify the force or establish its physical consequences.

The verse does not say that Hārūn’s head or neck was injured, that he fell, or how far he was drawn.

“He drew him toward himself” preserves the verb’s clear meaning.

An account of Mūsā’s forceful response draws on the anger, tablets, and seizure together, rather than making jarra inherently violent in every Arabic usage.

Al-Ṭabarī: drawing Hārūn amid anger and his reply

Did Mūsā pull Hārūn’s hair or beard?

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 says bi-raʾsi akhīhi: by his brother’s head.

The verse itself does not name a beard.

In Ṭā Hā 20:94, however, Hārūn says:

قَالَ يَا ابْنَ أُمَّ لَا تَأْخُذْ بِلِحْيَتِي وَلَا بِرَأْسِي إِنِّي خَشِيتُ أَنْ تَقُولَ فَرَّقْتَ بَيْنَ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ وَلَمْ تَرْقُبْ قَوْلِي

qāla yā ibna umma lā taʾkhudh bi-liḥyatī wa-lā bi-raʾsī innī khashītu an taqūla farraqta bayna banī Isrāʾīla wa-lam tarqub qawlī

“He said, “Son of my mother, do not seize my beard or my head. I feared you would say, ‘You divided the children of Israel and did not heed my word.’””

This adds information about the exchange.

Hārūn mentions both his beard and his head.

The wording of the two verses should nevertheless remain distinguishable.

Al-Aʿrāf describes taking the head and drawing Hārūn; Ṭā Hā records his request not to seize his beard or head.

Together they associate Mūsā’s conduct with taking parts of Hārūn’s head and face, without requiring every detail to be inserted into the other verse’s translation.

Al-Ṭabarī: taking the beard and head and fear of division

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:150, “took his brother by the head and drew him toward himself” is therefore more precise than inserting “pulled his beard.”

5. Mūsā’s response, Hārūn’s explanation, and limits of judgment

Does pulling Hārūn establish an accusation as proven?

Mūsā objects to his people’s conduct in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 and then takes Hārūn by the head.

Ṭā Hā 20:92 and Ṭā Hā 20:93 also ask what prevented Hārūn from following him when he saw the people go astray, and whether he disobeyed Mūsā’s instruction.

The questions show concern and objection regarding Hārūn’s role.

Taking and pulling him alone does not establish guilt or participation in calf worship.

Ṭā Hā 20:90 explicitly reports Hārūn’s earlier warning and call for the people to follow and obey him.

That matters when evaluating his role.

Being drawn toward Mūsā does not itself prove negligence or participation in the wrongdoers’ conduct.

Hārūn’s reply: pressure from his people

Hārūn says in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150:

«إِنَّ الْقَوْمَ اسْتَضْعَفُونِي وَكَادُوا يَقْتُلُونَنِي»

inna al-qawma istaḍʿafūnī wa-kādū yaqtulūnanī

The people had treated him as weak and nearly killed him.

He describes facing serious pressure from them.

He asks Mūsā not to let enemies rejoice over him or place him among the wrongdoers.

In Ṭā Hā 20:94 he also explains his fear of dividing the Children of Israel.

These replies matter, but do not all follow from the root jarr.

Jarr describes pulling; the disagreement over Hārūn’s responsibility and response to calf worship is developed in the brothers’ conversation.

Can Mūsā’s action establish a general rule?

The verse reports an event in Mūsā and Hārūn’s lives. The report is not by itself a general command to pull or physically confront others.

It does not establish that anyone’s anger legitimizes physical force against another.

Ethical judgment requires the circumstances, responsibilities, justification, and evidence to be examined.

The story describes Mūsā’s anger, the people’s defiance, pressure on Hārūn, and their exchange, without detailing every physical aspect.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:151, Mūsā asks forgiveness and mercy for himself and his brother.

The scene does not end with pulling: their relationship and turning toward The God remain part of the narrative.

Jarra itself neither identifies the reason for that prayer nor supplies a final judgment about all their conduct.

6. Related roots and technical usages

J R R (jarr) and J DH B (jadhb)

Jadhb concerns pulling and drawing toward oneself.

Lexicographers explain jarr through jadhb.

Lexical sources: jadhb and pulling

They overlap closely in physical pulling.

Many jarr examples concern drawing something along a course, such as a rope, animal, or load.

This tendency should not become a universal rule that jarr always means ground-level dragging and jadhb always means movement in the air.

The meaning of pulling is clear in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 without constructing an elaborate physical distinction between the verbs.

J R R (jarr) and S H B (saḥb)

Study of S H B

Saḥb likewise describes pulling or drawing something along.

Al-Qamar 54:48 says yusḥabūna fī al-nār (يُسْحَبُونَ فِي النَّارِ).

The punishment context describes a group being dragged through the fire.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 uses yajurruhu in Mūsā and Hārūn’s story.

The verbs overlap in pulling, but different settings should not be converted into lexical meanings.

Saḥb does not by itself mean punishment in Al-Qamar; that follows from context.

Nor does jarr mean a definitive punishment of Hārūn: it describes the pulling movement.

J R R (jarr) and A KH DH (akhdh)

Study of A KH DH

Akhadha concerns taking and can occur without moving the object.

Jarra describes pulling.

Their juxtaposition in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 makes the distinction clear.

Mūsā takes his brother by the head and draws him toward himself.

The first verb would express taking alone; the second adds further action.

That distinction is documented in the verse without requiring a universal theory of all akhdh and jarr usages.

J R R (jarr) and J R Y (jary)

Study of J R Y

J R Y is a separate root concerning flowing or movement along a course.

The Qur’an uses tajrī for rivers, ships, and other things.

Qur’anic J R R instead concerns pulling.

Water can flow without someone drawing it along with a rope or human pulling force.

Two shared initial consonants and movement in both fields do not make the roots identical.

Yajurruhu in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 belongs to J R R, rather than J R Y.

J R R (jarr) and J R M (jurm)

Study of J R M

J R M is a separate family with Qur’anic forms including ajramū and mujrimīn.

Arabic jarīrah, an offense, is recorded within J R R.

They overlap in responsibility and wrongful conduct but remain distinct roots and formations.

Jarīrah should not automatically be assigned to J R M, nor does yajurruhu mean committing an offense in Mūsā’s story.

Jarr in Arabic grammar

Jarr, also called khafḍ, names the genitive case of nouns in Arabic grammar.

Words such as min, ilā, fī, and ʿalā are termed ḥurūf al-jarr.

The term relates to the root, but the grammatical function should not be converted into physical pulling in every sentence.

In yajurruhu ilayhi, the verb belongs to J R R, while ilā is a genitive-governing preposition in grammatical terminology.

These are different usages.

One is a Qur’anic word meaning pulling; the other is a technical term describing a preposition’s function and the case it governs.

The term ḥarf jarr does not establish that every Qur’anic preposition means pulling or applying physical force.

7. Translation proposal for Forqan

The root’s sole Qur’anic form yajurruhu means he pulls him.

The full phrase adds ilayhi, specifying direction.

“Pulling him toward himself” accurately renders yajurruhu ilayhi in the narrative.

The installed English Forqan translation of Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 reads:

“When Moses returned to his people, angry and grieved, he said, “Evil is how you succeeded me after I left. Did you seek to hasten your Lord’s command?” He threw down the tablets and seized his brother by the head, pulling him toward himself. He said, “Son of my mother, the people overpowered me and nearly killed me. Do not make the enemies rejoice over me, and do not place me with the unjust people.””

It preserves the distinction between taking and pulling and supplies direction.

“Dragged him by force” may express the response’s intensity in commentary, but carries more than the direct verb alone and need not replace the installed translation.

Al-Aʿrāf does not explicitly say pulling by the beard. That detail belongs in a separate comparison with Ṭā Hā 20:94.

A brief root explanation for Forqan can read:

“J R R: pulling and drawing something along; moving it through a pulling force.”

The verse-specific meaning can be explained as:

“Yajurruhu: he pulls him; here, Mūsā takes Hārūn by the head and draws him toward himself.”

The general root meaning and the contextual meaning thus remain distinct.

Conclusion

Arabic J R R concerns pulling, drawing along, and movement through a pulling force.

Ropes, animal leads, loads, and drawing something toward oneself make the physical meaning clear.

The field also includes jarīr for a lead, jarūr for a resistant animal, jarrār for a great army, and jarīrah for an offense whose consequences someone draws upon himself.

Each has a specific usage within one family. They should not all be reduced to physical pulling, nor should their social or ethical extensions be imposed on the basic verb.

The Qur’an uses the root only in yajurruhu in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150. Mūsā returns, confronts his people’s conduct, takes Hārūn by the head, and draws him toward himself.

The scene occurs amid anger and grief. Jarr itself does not quantify force or displacement, establish injury, or give a final judgment concerning Mūsā’s action.

Hārūn’s reply and related verses show his prior warning and the pressure he faced. Being pulled does not prove his participation in calf worship or definitive responsibility for the people’s error.

The direct, defensible Qur’anic meaning of J R R is pulling or drawing along; in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150, yajurruhu ilayhi describes drawing Hārūn toward Mūsā.

The response’s intensity, the disagreement’s cause, and each person’s responsibility require the narrative’s context rather than being inserted into the verb jarr itself.
