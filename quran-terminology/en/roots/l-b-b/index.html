1. The root and its semantic field

L B B has two recognized semantic fields in Arabic: the pure, inner, or choice part of something; and remaining, attending closely, or staying firmly in place. Lubb means the kernel, core, or inner essence of something, and in human use, reason and the faculty of understanding. Its plural is albāb.

The Qur’an uses this root only as al-albāb, always with ulū or ulī. The expression denotes people who possess reason and understanding.

The Qur’an nevertheless does not portray this understanding solely as an ability to think, know, or argue. Different contexts associate it with remembrance, reflection, recognizing truth, distinguishing good from bad, mindfulness, and following what is best. The lexical meaning of lubb must be distinguished from the qualities the Qur’an attributes to those who possess it.

2. Lexical forms and semantic core

Arabic has several important forms from this root, not all of which occur in the Qur’an.

Lubb denotes the kernel, core, pure part, or inner essence of something. When used of a human being, it refers to reason and the faculty of understanding.

Lubāb likewise means the pure or choice part of something. Lubāb al-jawz denotes the walnut’s kernel, while lubāb al-qawm denotes the choice members of a group.

Labīb describes someone who possesses reason and understanding: an intelligent, discerning person. Its familiar plural is alibbāʾ.

Albāb is the plural of lubb; in Qur’anic usage it denotes people’s powers of reason or understanding.

In the other branch, labba and alabba are also recorded for staying in a place or attending closely to something. Talabbaba, concerning clothing and the body, means gathering and securing clothing around the chest and preparing for action. Labab and labbah also denote an area of the chest and the position of a necklace or strap.

These latter uses must not be equated with reason. A shared root does not require every derivative to have one meaning in every context.

The material image: the kernel and pure part

A walnut or almond kernel provides a clear image for this branch. What lies within the shell and constitutes the desirable edible part is called lubb.

The date palm likewise has a lubb in Arabic: an inner, central part distinguished from the rest of the tree.

The word is not confined to food. The same semantic field can describe the choice members of a group, the pure part of a possession, or the essence of a matter. What matters is more than being inside: it is being the principal, pure, or choice part.

Applying the expression to human beings is intelligible: just as a fruit has a kernel or choice part, human understanding and discernment can be called lubb as among a person’s most distinguished and fundamental capacities. This comparison explains a semantic relationship; it does not by itself establish the historical order in which the meanings developed.

Classical lexicons, including Lisān al-ʿArab, explicitly record this relationship between the pure part of something and human reason.

Lisān al-ʿArab and al-Ṣiḥāḥ: L B B

Two semantic fields in early lexicons

Ibn Fāris identifies two principal meanings for this family in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

First, persistence and stability, such as remaining in a place or attending closely to a task.

Second, purity and choice quality, such as a kernel and human reason.

This classification shows why lubb should not simply be defined through persistence or remaining. Conversely, the sense of reason must not be imposed on verbs such as alabba.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: persistence, stability, purity, and choice quality

In al-Mufradāt, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains lubb as reason free from admixture, distinguishing reason in general from purified understanding. He expresses this as فَكُلُّ لُبٍّ عَقْلٌ وَلَيْسَ كُلُّ عَقْلٍ لُبًّا (fa-kullu lubbin ʿaqlun wa-laysa kullu ʿaqlin lubban): every lubb is reason, but not every instance of reason is necessarily lubb.

This is a lexicographer’s semantic analysis, rather than an independent definition stated verbatim by the Qur’an. Its validity and limits must be assessed against Qur’anic usage.

Al-Rāghib’s al-Mufradāt: lubb and reason

Early linguistic evidence and labbayka

Lexical sources transmit the following verse with this wording, attributing it to a poet named Muḍarrib ibn Kaʿb:

فَقُلْتُ لَهَا فِيئِي إِلَيْكِ فَإِنَّنِي حَرَامٌ وَإِنِّي بَعْدَ ذَاكَ لَبِيبُ

fa-qultu lahā fīʾī ilayki fa-innanī ḥarāmun wa-innī baʿda dhāka labību

Translation: “I told her: Come back to yourself, for I am in consecration, and with that I remain in attendance [or pronounce the talbiyah].”

Two explanations of labīb are recorded here: remaining or attending closely, and pronouncing the talbiyah while in consecration. Its resemblance to the familiar labīb meaning discerning therefore does not make this evidence for that mental sense. The attribution and date are also insufficiently secure to classify this verse confidently as pre-Qur’anic.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: attribution and the two explanations of labīb in the poetic witness

Some lexicographers likewise connect labbayka with remaining and attending, explaining it as responding to a call and persisting in that response. Other analyses of its structure and origin are also transmitted.

Labbayka helps show the breadth of the lexical family, but its meaning cannot be carried directly into ulū al-albāb. The Qur’anic expression uses the noun lubb, rather than a verb of answering or remaining.

3. The Qur’anic form albāb and its construction

L B B occurs sixteen times in sixteen verses across ten surahs. Every occurrence is al-albāb; the Qur’an uses no verb or singular form from this root. Lubb, labīb, lubāb, and alibbāʾ are familiar Arabic words, but do not themselves occur in the Qur’anic text.

Qur’anic morphology: sixteen instances of albāb

The Qur’anic construction has two grammatical forms:

Ulū al-albāb means “people of understanding.” Ulū is the nominative form.

Ulī al-albāb has the same meaning, but ulī is the accusative and genitive form.

Ulū and ulī do not belong to L B B. They express possession, while al-albāb is the dependent noun in the genitive construction and denotes the understanding itself. Their difference reflects grammatical position, rather than different kinds of understanding. Albāb is a broken plural on the afʿāl pattern; its singular is lubb.

In English, “people of understanding,” “those endowed with reason,” and “people of discernment” can convey the expression. “People of understanding” is both natural and close to its Arabic construction.

4. People of understanding in Qur’anic contexts

A. Listening, assessing, and following what is best

One of the clearest verses for understanding ulū al-albāb occurs in Al-Zumar:

الَّذِينَ يَسْتَمِعُونَ الْقَوْلَ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ أَحْسَنَهُ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ هَدَاهُمُ اللَّهُ ۖ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمْ أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

alladhīna yastamiʿūna al-qawla fa-yattabiʿūna aḥsanahu ulāʾika alladhīna hadāhumu Allāhu wa-ulāʾika hum ulū al-albābi

“Those who listen to what is said and follow the best of it: they are the ones whom The God has guided, and they are the people of understanding.”

Al-Zumar 39:18

This verse matters because it does more than name people of understanding: it describes their conduct.

Three actions are discernible: listening to speech, recognizing the best of it, and following it.

Listening alone is insufficient. Someone may hear many statements yet accept them unexamined or reject them all. Someone may also recognize a better statement but fail to act on it because of prejudice, self-interest, or fear of opposition. The verse concerns those who listen and then follow what is best.

Aḥsanahu also shows that their choice has a criterion. They follow what is best, rather than whatever pleases them. Lubb itself does not mean following: following the best is conduct that the verse attributes to people of understanding.

The preceding context, Al-Zumar 39:17, concerns avoiding the worship of false powers and turning to The God. Listening and choosing the best speech here thus remain connected with a person’s orientation toward guidance.

Al-Ṭabarī: ulū al-albāb in the context of guidance

This quality should be considered alongside another verse:

قُلْ لَا يَسْتَوِي الْخَبِيثُ وَالطَّيِّبُ وَلَوْ أَعْجَبَكَ كَثْرَةُ الْخَبِيثِ ۚ فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

qul lā yastawī al-khabīthu wa-al-ṭayyibu wa-law aʿjabaka kathratu al-khabīthi fa-ittaqū Allāha yā ulī al-albābi laʿallakum tufliḥūna

“Say: The impure and the wholesome are not equal, even if the abundance of the impure impresses you. So be mindful of The God, people of understanding, so that you may prosper.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:100

The issue here is more than listening: it is discerning value despite abundance. A large quantity may impress someone, but quantity alone does not establish purity, goodness, or truth.

Together, these verses show people of understanding looking beyond apparent abundance and mere hearing. They distinguish alternatives and attend to quality and value in their judgments.

B. Reflecting on the Book and benefiting from reminders

Ṣād connects a stated purpose of the Book’s revelation with people of understanding:

كِتَابٌ أَنْزَلْنَاهُ إِلَيْكَ مُبَارَكٌ لِيَدَّبَّرُوا آيَاتِهِ وَلِيَتَذَكَّرَ أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

kitābun anzalnāhu ilayka mubārakun li-yaddabbarū āyātihi wa-li-yatadhakkara ulū al-albābi

“A blessed Book that We sent down to you, so that they may reflect upon its signs and people of understanding may take heed.”

Ṣād 38:29

Reflection and taking heed occur together. Tadabbur concerns examining and thinking through the signs; tadhakkur concerns remembrance and learning from a reminder.

Their association presents reading and considering the signs as a path toward useful understanding—understanding from which a person can benefit.

Taking heed should not be reduced to memorizing sentences. Someone may know many expressions by heart yet overlook their meaning when deciding how to act. Another person may hear one verse, recognize a neglected truth, and correct their conduct accordingly.

Al-Baqarah 2:269 likewise connects wisdom with taking heed:

يُؤْتِي الْحِكْمَةَ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۚ وَمَنْ يُؤْتَ الْحِكْمَةَ فَقَدْ أُوتِيَ خَيْرًا كَثِيرًا ۗ وَمَا يَذَّكَّرُ إِلَّا أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

yuʾtī al-ḥikmata man yashāʾu wa-man yuʾta al-ḥikmata fa-qad ūtiya khayran kathīran wa-mā yadhdhakkaru illā ulū al-albābi

“He grants wisdom to whom He wills. Whoever is granted wisdom has indeed been granted abundant good. None take heed except people of understanding.”

Al-Baqarah 2:269

Wisdom and lubb are not one word here. Wisdom is what The God grants; ulū al-albāb are the people who benefit from reminders. Their presence in one verse indicates a relationship, rather than complete identity of meaning.

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 describes clear and ambiguous signs within the Book, contrasting those whose hearts incline away with those firmly grounded in knowledge. Its closing statement attributes taking heed to people of understanding.

Engaging with a text is therefore more than possessing information. A person’s orientation toward truth also matters. Someone may know the signs yet pursue what occasions discord. Firm grounding in knowledge, by contrast, appears alongside accepting the Book’s divine source and engaging responsibly with its signs.

The verse does not make people of understanding incapable of asking questions or eliminate their need to research difficult passages. Such a conclusion does not follow from lubb itself.

Ibrāhīm 14:52 likewise concerns communicating the message to people, warning them, recognizing the oneness of the deity, and people of understanding taking heed. In Ghāfir 40:54, the Book received by the Children of Israel is described as guidance and a reminder for people of understanding.

These uses show that taking heed is not confined to one example or event. People of understanding can learn from the divine message, whether it proclaims oneness or conveys the Book’s guidance.

C. Learning from human histories

The Qur’an also connects learning from history with people of understanding.

Yūsuf 12:111 describes the histories of the prophets and other figures mentioned as lessons for people of understanding.

Learning from an account is more than knowing past events. Someone may know what happened yet miss the relationship between conduct and consequences. Learning from others requires moving beyond a particular event to recognize what can benefit one’s own life.

In Ṣād 38:43, after the gift to Job and the restoration of his family and their like, the event is described as mercy from The God and a reminder for people of understanding.

Dhikrā here emerges from human experience—suffering, mercy, and relief. The Qur’anic field of taking heed therefore includes lived events, rather than being confined to theoretical propositions.

Al-Raʿd 13:19 contrasts knowing the truth with blindness:

أَفَمَنْ يَعْلَمُ أَنَّمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكَ مِنْ رَبِّكَ الْحَقُّ كَمَنْ هُوَ أَعْمَىٰ ۚ إِنَّمَا يَتَذَكَّرُ أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

a-fa-man yaʿlamu annamā unzila ilayka min rabbika al-ḥaqqu ka-man huwa aʿmā innamā yatadhakkaru ulū al-albābi

“Is someone who knows that what has been sent down to you from your Lord is the truth like someone who is blind? Only people of understanding take heed.”

Al-Raʿd 13:19

Blindness here stands opposite knowing the truth. It concerns failure to recognize truth, rather than a negative judgment on physical blindness.

The following verses, Al-Raʿd 13:20, Al-Raʿd 13:21, and Al-Raʿd 13:22, describe fulfilling commitments, maintaining the bonds The God commands, reverence, fear of reckoning, patience, establishing ṣalāh, spending, and answering evil with good.

This continuity connects recognizing truth with practical responsibility. A person of understanding does more than discuss truth: what they recognize also shapes the direction of their life.

D. Recognizing signs in the natural world

Āl ʿImrān describes signs for people of understanding in the creation of the heavens and earth and the alternation of night and day:

إِنَّ فِي خَلْقِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَاخْتِلَافِ اللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ لَآيَاتٍ لِأُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ

inna fī khalqi al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-ikhtilāfi al-layli wa-al-nahāri la-āyātin li-ulī al-albābi

“In the creation of the heavens and earth and the alternation of night and day are indeed signs for people of understanding.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:190

The next verse, Āl ʿImrān 3:191, concerns remembering The God in different postures and reflecting on the creation of the heavens and earth. People of understanding here observe the world, think about it, and do not consider creation purposeless.

These verses include thinking about nature within the exercise of understanding. Recognizing divine signs is not confined to written texts: creation itself is also an object of observation and reflection.

Al-Zumar 39:21 presents water descending from the sky, its passage through the earth, vegetation of different colors growing, and its subsequent drying and crumbling as a reminder for people of understanding.

This example is significant. An event that many people find ordinary and repetitive can become a reminder for someone who reflects.

Lubb here therefore concerns more than tackling difficult, abstract problems. Sometimes the sign is visible to everyone, but benefiting from it requires attention and thought.

E. Understanding and moral responsibility

Several verses directly address yā ulī al-albāb, “people of understanding.” These addresses show that people with understanding are also called to mindfulness.

Al-Baqarah says:

وَلَكُمْ فِي الْقِصَاصِ حَيَاةٌ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ

wa-lakum fī al-qiṣāṣi ḥayātun yā ulī al-albābi laʿallakum tattaqūna

“In qiṣāṣ there is life for you, people of understanding, so that you may be mindful.”

Al-Baqarah 2:179

In the context of qiṣāṣ, the verse connects a ruling concerning human life with life itself, and calls people of understanding to mindfulness.

The address invites a judgment that looks beyond a ruling’s first appearance. Qiṣāṣ appears to involve responding to killing and taking life, yet the verse speaks of life within it. Examining that relationship requires considering the ruling’s context and limits. People of understanding are clearly called to attend to this relationship and observe mindfulness.

Al-Baqarah 2:197 names the months of pilgrimage, its behavioral restrictions, and the value of good deeds; it then describes mindfulness as the best provision and closes by addressing people of understanding.

Here understanding connects with conduct in worship and ritual. Performing rites and attending to one’s behavior are linked within the verse’s context.

Al-Zumar 39:9 describes someone worshipping during the night, prostrating and standing, fearing the hereafter and hoping for the Lord’s mercy. It asks whether those who know and those who do not know are equal, then attributes taking heed to people of understanding.

Knowledge here accompanies worship, fear, and hope. This does not make worship the lexical meaning of lubb. It presents one setting in which knowledge, taking heed, and human conduct come together.

Al-Ṭalāq also says:

أَعَدَّ اللَّهُ لَهُمْ عَذَابًا شَدِيدًا ۖ فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا ۚ قَدْ أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ إِلَيْكُمْ ذِكْرًا

aʿadda Allāhu lahum ʿadhāban shadīdan fa-ittaqū Allāha yā ulī al-albābi alladhīna āmanū qad anzala Allāhu ilaykum dhikran

“The God has prepared a severe punishment for them. So be mindful of The God, people of understanding who have believed. The God has indeed sent down a reminder to you.”

Al-Ṭalāq 65:10

Alladhīna āmanū identifies the addressees here: people of understanding who have believed. This qualification should not be made a permanent component of lubb’s lexical meaning without justification, since belief is stated separately. A separately stated qualification alone, however, does not prove that a meaning is absent from a word; the distinction requires the full lexical and contextual evidence.

Possessing understanding also does not remove the need for warning or mindfulness. Even those addressed with this description are called to be mindful of The God.

5. Lubb, reason, the heart, and related terms

The Qur’an uses several words for human understanding and cognition. Reason, the heart, fuʾād, nuhā, and ḥijr concern recognition, perception, and judgment in different contexts. Their fields overlap, but their forms and lexical ranges differ.

Study of F ʾ D

L B B (lubb) and ʿ Q L (ʿaql)

ʿ Q L in Arabic also concerns holding, binding, and restraining, and in cognitive use extends to understanding and exercising reason.

The Qur’an uses verbs such as yaʿqilūna, taʿqilūna, and ʿaqalūhu from this root, but does not use the independent noun ʿaql.

L B B, by contrast, supplies no Qur’anic verb. It appears as the broken plural albāb in the description of people of understanding.

This grammatical difference alone does not establish reason and lubb as wholly separate faculties. It does show a difference in usage: verses with ʿ Q L verbs often emphasize the act of understanding, while ulū al-albāb describes people as possessing understanding.

Lubb can mean reason in Arabic, with the image of purity and choice quality in its lexical background. Attending to discerning understanding that benefits from reminders is therefore appropriate. The root image should not, however, become an independent classification of kinds of reason that the verses themselves do not present.

Study of ʿ Q L

L B B (lubb) and Q L B (qalb)

The Qur’anic heart is not confined to emotion. Knowledge, thought, belief, denial, sickness, and spiritual blindness are also associated with it.

Al-Ḥajj 22:46 speaks of hearts with which people reason and locates the blindness under discussion in hearts that fail to recognize truth.

A strict division assigning emotion to the heart and thought to reason therefore does not fit the Qur’an’s own language, which also attributes cognitive activity to the heart.

Lubb and qalb approach each other in this field, but their uses differ. The heart occurs in many contexts concerning human inward states; ulū al-albāb specifically designates people of understanding.

Neither expression alone determines the physical location of reason in the body or establishes a definitive theory about the brain and heart.

Study of Q L B

L B B (lubb), N H Y (nuhā), and Ḥ J R (ḥijr)

Ṭā Hā 20:54 and Ṭā Hā 20:128 speak of ulī al-nuhā. Nuhā is associated with reason and restraint from wrongful conduct.

Al-Fajr 89:5 uses dhī ḥijr. Within its lexical field, ḥijr concerns prohibition and restraint, and here denotes the capacity for understanding and judgment.

These expressions are close to ulū al-albāb in referring to people with understanding. Different lexical images nevertheless do not establish three fixed, wholly separate classes of reason in the Qur’an.

Each expression is best examined in its own context. Lubb carries the image of the pure or principal part; nuhā relates to restraint, and ḥijr to restriction and prevention. These distinctions illuminate the language without establishing rigid psychological boundaries or fixed degrees of reason.

6. Challenges of interpretation and translation

Does lubb mean intelligence or aptitude?

Modern usage may equate wisdom with high intelligence, academic aptitude, or solving complex problems. Qur’anic uses of ulū al-albāb do not adopt so narrow a criterion.

Al-Zumar 39:18 concerns listening and following the best; Al-Māʾidah 5:100, distinguishing the wholesome from the impure despite the latter’s abundance; Ṣād 38:29, reflection and taking heed; and Al-Baqarah 2:197, mindfulness.

None of these verses makes a scholarly title, educational attainment, or computational ability a lexical condition of lubb.

This does not devalue knowledge or reasoning. Al-Zumar 39:9 distinguishes those who know from those who do not. The point is that information and argumentative ability alone do not encompass all the qualities associated with people of understanding across these contexts.

Does lubb mean reason entirely free from error?

“Pure reason” may suggest someone who never makes mistakes or always has access to the whole truth.

The Qur’an does not derive that immunity from ulū al-albāb itself.

Several verses call these same people to mindfulness, while others concern their taking heed. These addresses show that the description does not remove the human need for care, learning, and correction.

The subject is understanding capable of sound assessment and benefiting from signs, rather than someone immune to every error or gap in knowledge.

Is every knowledgeable person among ulū al-albāb?

If lubb simply means possessing information, the Qur’anic distinction between knowing and benefiting from knowledge becomes obscured.

Someone may recognize a true statement yet refuse to follow it because of interests or attachments. Al-Zumar 39:18 emphasizes following the best; Al-Raʿd 13:19 contrasts knowing truth with a kind of blindness.

Every disagreement or mistaken conclusion should not, however, be taken as evidence that someone lacks lubb. Distinguishing deliberate rejection, prejudice, ignorance, and honest error requires further evidence.

Ulū al-albāb is a commendatory Qur’anic description, rather than a tool for hastily judging people’s inward states.

Does the expression refer only to men?

Ulū and ulī are grammatically masculine plural forms. That feature alone does not restrict the description of understanding to male human beings.

When the Qur’an speaks of thinking, remembering, recognizing truth, and human responsibility, it does not intrinsically assign these capacities to one sex. A natural, inclusive “people of understanding” is therefore preferable to a restrictive “men of understanding,” unless a particular context specifies its addressees.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For lubb in general lexical use, the subject determines whether “kernel,” “core,” “pure part,” “essence,” or “understanding” is appropriate. “Kernel” is natural for fruit; “pure part” or “essence” for a principal, choice element; and “understanding” or “reason” for the human capacity.

For Qur’anic ulū al-albāb and ulī al-albāb, the principal proposal is “people of understanding.” “Those endowed with reason” is also a suitable English equivalent.

Expressions such as “people of pure understanding,” “truth-seeking people of understanding,” or “those with purified reason” may help explain the word and some contexts, but are unsuitable as basic translations because they add interpretive qualifications to the Arabic.

The verse is best translated with a short, natural equivalent, while the characteristics of people of understanding are explained in its reflection and analysis.

8. Conclusion

Lubb in Arabic denotes the pure, principal, or choice part of something; in human use, it denotes reason and the faculty of understanding. The Qur’an uses only the plural al-albāb from this family, describing its possessors as ulū al-albāb or ulī al-albāb.

The word itself does not mean mindfulness, belief, reflection, obedience, or following. These are distinct concepts associated with people of understanding in different Qur’anic contexts. Keeping lexical meaning separate from contextual conduct and qualities is essential to a precise understanding of the root.

Qur’anic contexts describe people of understanding as more than people able to think. They listen and follow the best, benefit from reminders, reflect on the world’s signs, learn from others’ histories, and are called to mindfulness.

In this Qur’anic portrayal, thinking and practical life remain connected. Understanding does more than collect information: it should also affect recognizing truth and responding to it.

“People of understanding” is therefore an appropriate equivalent. Across Qur’anic contexts, its value becomes evident in sound perception and judgment, taking heed, and following what is best.
