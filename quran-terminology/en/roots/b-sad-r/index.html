The Qur’anic Root B Ṣ R

Seeing, sight, the sign becoming clear, and responsibility after seeing

The central meaning of the root

The root ب ص ر (B Ṣ R) is one of the Qur’anic roots whose meaning is partly hidden if it is translated only as “seeing.” Seeing in the Qur’an is not only the work of the eye. A human being may have eyes but not see the sign. He may look at something but fail to grasp its truth. He may see the world, history, signs, and the outcomes of actions before him, yet take no insight from them.

From here the difference between surface looking and Qur’anic baṣar becomes clear. Looking may be only contact between the eye and the outward form, but baṣar is tied to meaning becoming clear. Baṣar means that something becomes manifest to the human being, a sign is seen, an outcome is understood, and the human being becomes responsible before what he has seen.

In the Qur’an, hearing and sight often appear together. Hearing is the path by which the message enters, and sight is the path by which signs are seen. The human being hears, sees, and then weighs within his qalb or fuʾād. If the ear is closed, the message does not enter. If the eye is blind, signs are not seen. If the heart is polluted, hearing and seeing still do not become guidance.

The central meaning of this root can be understood this way: B Ṣ R in the Qur’an means seeing a sign and becoming responsible before what has become clear.

Baṣar: the faculty of seeing and responsibility of recognition

بَصَر (baṣar) means sight, vision, and the ability to see. But in the Qur’an, baṣar is not only a bodily ability. It is one of the doors of human recognition. Through baṣar, the human being sees outward signs: sky, earth, history, the ends of communities, the signs of creation, and the outcomes of actions.

Yet created sight without rising into insight is not liberation. The Qur’an speaks of people who have eyes but do not see the truth. This shows that physical seeing, if it does not reach discernment of truth, does not save the human being from misguidance.

Abṣara: seeing beyond surface looking

أَبْصَرَ / يُبْصِرُونَ (abṣara / yubṣirūn) means he saw, perceived, or observed clearly. This branch of the root is stronger than ordinary seeing. In many cases, ibṣār means that something has become clear to the human being and he can no longer claim that no sign was shown to him.

When the Qur’an says a group does not see, the meaning is not always physical blindness. Sometimes the human being has eyes but lacks ibṣār: the sign stands before him, but its truth does not enter his understanding or orientation.

Baṣīr and Al-Baṣīr: human clear-sight and God’s transcendent seeing

بَصِير (baṣīr) means seeing, aware, or one who sees with clarity. When this attribute is used for a human being, it may point to the ability to see, understand, or be aware of a situation. But when the Qur’an calls God الْبَصِير (Al-Baṣīr), we must avoid every simple resemblance to human seeing.

Al-Baṣīr is a transcendent divine attribute. Transcendent theology means speaking about God while declaring Him free from the limits, needs, likenesses, and measures of created things. When we say God sees, the meaning is not that His seeing is like human seeing through eye, light, distance, direction, or instrument. The meaning is that nothing escapes His presence, awareness, encompassing knowledge, and judgment, without making Him resemble creation.

God being Al-Baṣīr is both comfort and warning. It is comfort for the person whose good deed is not seen on earth. It is warning for the one who imagines that hidden oppression, deception, display, or secret corruption remains away from God’s seeing. Al-Baṣīr is not merely a theoretical attribute; it is God’s presence before human action. Nothing is lost in His order.

Baṣīrah and baṣāʾir: inward proof and clarification

بَصِيرَة (baṣīrah) means clear insight, manifest proof, and inward sight toward truth. بَصَائِر (baṣāʾir) are signs and clarifications that move the human being beyond surface seeing into understanding.

The Qur’an describes itself as having baṣāʾir: clarifications that are not merely for reading, but for opening the inner eye of the human being. Baṣāʾir are signs that, if a human being meets them with truthfulness and attention, make the path clear for him.

Therefore, baṣīrah in the Qur’an is not a personal claim or a feeling of spiritual superiority. Baṣīrah means seeing signs in the light of truth, recognizing the shape of the present condition, understanding future outcomes, and becoming responsible before the path that has become clear. Insight is not sectarian pride; it is a restraint against cognitive error, fanaticism, haste, and self-deception.

One very deep use of this root appears in Al-Qiyāmah 75:14:

بَلِ الْإِنْسَانُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ بَصِيرَةٌ

Rather, the human being is an insight against himself.

In this verse, the human being is placed before himself. Even if his tongue makes excuses, his being, his orientation, and the truth he knows within himself become a witness against him. Here baṣīrah is not only mental awareness; it is an inward proof from which the human being cannot always escape.

Mubṣirah and tabṣirah: the clarifying sign

مُبْصِر / مُبْصِرَة (mubṣir / mubṣirah) points to something clear, visible, or clarifying. In the Qur’an, some signs or events are mubṣirah: clear enough to open the human eye toward truth.

A clear example appears regarding the she-camel of Thamūd. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:59, it says:

وَآتَيْنَا ثَمُودَ النَّاقَةَ مُبْصِرَةً

And We gave Thamūd the she-camel as a clarifying sign.

This expression shows that the blindness was not from the side of the sign. The sign was clear. The problem was not the absence of evidence, but the closedness of human reception. When a sign is mubṣirah, denial no longer comes from lack of light; it comes from fanaticism, arrogance, or escape from responsibility.

تَبْصِرَة (tabṣirah) means clarification, making one see, or something that becomes a source of insight. This word shows that baṣar in the Qur’an is not only a natural sense; it can become training, reminder, warning, and awakening.

Seeing and not seeing

One important point in this root is that the Qur’an distinguishes between looking and seeing. A human being may look at something but not see it. He may read history but not see divine patterns. He may see signs of creation but not reach the Creator. He may see the end of oppressors but not leave oppression.

So blindness in the Qur’an is not always blindness of the eye. The more dangerous blindness is blindness before truth: where the sign is present, but the human being does not accept it. In Al-Ḥajj 22:46, it says:

فَإِنَّهَا لَا تَعْمَى الْأَبْصَارُ وَلَٰكِنْ تَعْمَى الْقُلُوبُ الَّتِي فِي الصُّدُورِ

It is not the eyes that become blind, but the hearts within the chests that become blind.

This point is deeply connected to the Forqan method. The Qur’an calls the human being to independent, awake, responsible seeing: not imitative looking, not sectarian eye-closing, and not selective seeing based on interests and biases.

Baṣar and responsibility

Just as hearing makes the human being responsible after hearing, baṣar makes him responsible after seeing. Whoever has seen the sign is no longer like someone to whom no sign has arrived. Seeing makes the proof clearer.

But this responsibility is different from coercion. The Qur’an does not force the human being to believe with closed eyes. Rather, it places signs, examples, history, and outcomes before him so that he may see, reflect, and choose his path knowingly.

Sight, hearing, and the heart

In the structure of the Qur’an, hearing, sight, and qalb or fuʾād work together. Hearing brings in the message. Sight sees the sign. The qalb or fuʾād weighs it and takes direction.

If one of these paths becomes corrupt, human recognition is harmed. If the human being does not hear, he loses the message. If he does not see, he loses the signs. If the heart is sick, even hearing and seeing do not become guidance.

For this reason, Qur’anic baṣar is not merely an open eye; it is seeing in connection with an awake heart.

Methodological note

In translating this root, one should not always be satisfied with one word. “Seeing” is enough in some verses, but in many places the difference between outward seeing, clear seeing, insight, a sign being manifest, and the transcendent divine attribute Al-Baṣīr must be preserved.

If all forms of this root are translated only as “seeing,” part of the Qur’an’s epistemic geometry is lost. The Qur’an wants to show that the human being is not merely a creature with eyes; he is a creature responsible before what he sees.

Suggested rendering in Forqan

This root should be rendered according to context.

  • baṣar: sight, vision, faculty of seeing, seeing with reception of a sign.
  • abṣara / yubṣirūn: saw, perceived, saw clearly, saw the sign; or in a negative context: they do not see, meaning they do not grasp the truth.
  • baṣīr: seeing, aware, clear-sighted.
  • Al-Baṣīr for God: The All-Seeing, the One from whom no action or truth is hidden; a transcendent divine attribute beyond human seeing.
  • baṣīrah: insight, clear insight, manifest proof, inward sight toward truth.
  • baṣāʾir: clarifications, clear signs, insight-opening proofs.
  • mubṣir / mubṣirah: clear, visible, manifest, clarifying, sight-opening.
  • tabṣirah: source of insight, clarification, awakening of sight, clarifying reminder.

Summary

ب ص ر (B Ṣ R) in the Qur’an is not only the root of seeing with the eye. It shows seeing the sign, truth becoming clear, and responsibility after seeing. The human being sees the world with his eyes, but guidance occurs only when seeing reaches insight, understanding, humility, and righteous action.

In the Forqan method, baṣar should be understood this way: seeing is the beginning of responsibility. The human eye is not only an instrument of observation; it is a window through which the signs of truth either enter the heart, or the human being passes by those same signs in blindness through heedlessness and bias.

And when this root becomes the divine name Al-Baṣīr, translation must remain transcendent. God is not merely “seeing” in a human sense. He is The All-Seeing: His seeing needs no instrument, no distance, no light, and no outward surface. Nothing of the truth of the human being or the world is hidden from Him.
