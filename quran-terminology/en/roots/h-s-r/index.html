The Qur’anic Root Ḥ S R (ح س ر)

Uncovering, exhausted strength, and regret: from bodily fatigue and a weary gaze to grief over lost opportunities

Qur’anic and lexical forms

حَسْرَة / حَسَرَات
(ḥasrah / ḥasarāt)
Qur’anic noun and plural: regret, lament and grief

يَا حَسْرَتَنَا
(yā ḥasratanā)

(yā ḥasratā)

(ḥasīr)

Arabic Ḥ S R has several related branches: removing a covering and revealing what was hidden, exhaustion and loss of strength, and intense grief or regret over something lost or an unwelcome outcome. Ḥasara (حَسَرَ) can concern uncovering or exhausting something; ḥasīr (حَسِير) describes one whose strength is depleted; and ḥasrah (حَسْرَة) is grief and regret arising from loss, failure, or the disclosure of an action’s adverse consequences.

The Qur’an uses this family both for regret over one’s earlier deeds and for intense grief over others’ fate. Elsewhere a repeatedly examining gaze becomes ḥasīr; someone spending resources without restraint remains maḥsūr (مَحْسُور); and those near The God are described as not becoming exhausted or worn out in worshipping Him.

This range prevents translating every derivative as remorse. Remorse is an important branch, but Arabic also uses the family for physical exhaustion, inability after spending strength or resources, and uncovering.

Regret need not arise from a personal sin. Someone may regret their own mistake, lament a lost opportunity, or grieve at others’ painful fate. The sentence and context establish the grief’s cause, object, and moral significance.

1. Linguistic forms and earlier Arabic usage

The root occurs twelve times in the Qur’an: nine uses of ḥasrah and its associated forms, and one each of ḥasīr, maḥsūr, and yastaḥsirūna (يَسْتَحْسِرُونَ). Its main Qur’anic forms include ḥasrah, plural ḥasarāt (حَسَرَات), the vocatives yā ḥasratanā (يَا حَسْرَتَنَا) and yā ḥasratā (يَا حَسْرَتَىٰ), the description ḥasīr, the passive participle maḥsūr, and the verb yastaḥsirūna.

Earlier Arabic also records ḥasara, ḥasira (حَسِرَ), ḥasura (حَسُرَ), ḥāsir (حَاسِر), ḥasrān (حَسْرَان), taḥassara (تَحَسَّرَ), ḥassara (حَسَّرَ), aḥsara (أَحْسَرَ), inḥasara (اِنْحَسَرَ), and istaḥsara (اِسْتَحْسَرَ). These forms do not all occur in the Qur’an, but help explain the family and distinguish its semantic branches.

Removing a covering and revealing

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris connects the root’s organizing meaning with exposing something beneath its covering. Ḥasara ʿan dhirāʿihi (حَسَرَ عَنْ ذِرَاعِهِ) means drawing back a sleeve and exposing the forearm. Ḥasara al-ʿimāmata ʿan raʾsihi (حَسَرَ الْعِمَامَةَ عَنْ رَأْسِهِ) concerns removing the turban from the head.

Something previously covered becomes visible. The covering is removed, and the part beneath it is seen.

Ḥāsir can likewise describe someone whose head or another body part is uncovered. In battle, a warrior without armor or a helmet may be called ḥāsir, in contrast with dāriʿ (دارع), someone wearing armor.

This concrete usage clearly illustrates exposure and the removal of a covering.

A poem by Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī states:

وَقَامَ بَنَاتِي بِالنِّعَالِ حَوَاسِرًا
وَأَلْصَقْنَ ضَرْبَ السِّبْتِ تَحْتَ الْقَلَائِدِ

wa-qāma banātī bi-l-niʿāli ḥawāsiran / wa-alṣaqna ḍarba al-sibti taḥta al-qalāʾidi

The line’s sense is: “My daughters rose bareheaded, beating the area of their necks and chests with leather sandals.”

Ḥawāsir (حَوَاسِر) describes the daughters’ uncovered heads. The scene concerns mourning, but ḥawāsir itself does not mean mourners or grief-stricken people. It concerns being exposed and lacking a covering.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The line appears in his collected poetry, but its exact composition date does not establish this particular witness as pre-Qur’anic. Its value lies in documenting ḥawāsir’s familiar meaning in earlier Arabic. Lisān al-ʿArab also transmits the second hemistich as fa-alṣaqna waqʿa al-sibti taḥta al-qalāʾidi (فألصقن وقع السبت تحت القلائد). The variant does not change ḥawāsir’s meaning.

Receding water and exposed ground

Arabic also records ḥasara al-māʾu (حَسَرَ الْمَاءُ): water subsided or receded, exposing ground previously beneath it.

No person removes a covering by hand here. The water itself recedes from a surface, revealing what was underneath.

The usage shows exposure within Ḥ S R occurring without a human agent.

Inḥasara also concerns a covering’s recession or something becoming exposed. This does not mean that Qur’anic ḥasrah always involves removing a literal material veil.

Exhaustion and depleted strength

Another branch concerns severe fatigue and loss of strength.

Ḥasarat al-dābbatu (حَسَرَتِ الدَّابَّةُ) means that an animal became exhausted through extensive movement or work.

Ḥasarahā al-sayru (حَسَرَهَا السَّيْرُ) likewise means that prolonged travel exhausted the animal’s strength.

In many earlier uses, ḥasīr emphasizes exhaustion intense enough to make continued movement difficult. The word itself, however, establishes neither how long the fatigue lasts nor the time needed to recover after rest.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Qays ibn Khuwaylid al-Hudhalī, known as Qays ibn ʿAyzārah, states:

إِنَّ الْعَسِيرَ بِهَا دَاءٌ مُخَامِرُهَا
فَشَطْرَهَا نَظَرُ الْعَيْنَيْنِ مَحْسُورُ

inna al-ʿasīra bihā dāʾun mukhāmiruhā / fa-shaṭrahā naẓaru al-ʿaynayni maḥsūru

The line means: “This unbroken she-camel is afflicted by an illness; the gaze of both eyes toward her is exhausted and worn out.” Shaṭrahā here means toward her, rather than half of her; lexical commentary explains al-ʿasīr as a she-camel not yet trained.

Maḥsūr describes a gaze exhausted by prolonged staring and following something with the eyes.

This helps explain ḥasīr in Al-Mulk 67:4: exhaustion of the gaze was known in Arabic and should not be treated simply as a new metaphor invented in the Qur’anic text.

Variants are reported for the line, including naẓaru al-ʿaynayni (نظر العينين) and baṣaru al-ʿaynayni (بصر العينين). The transmitted versions retain the exhausted-gaze meaning.

Ḥasrah: intense regret and grief

Ḥasira ʿalā al-shayʾi (حَسِرَ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ) describes someone lamenting something or grieving intensely over its loss.

Ḥasrah can concern remorse, regret, frustrated longing, and grief arising from a loss or an action’s unwelcome result.

There is an affinity with exhaustion: intense regret and grief may rob someone of strength and peace.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī offers several explanations connecting regret with the family’s other branches. One concerns ignorance being stripped away so a person recognizes the reality of their action. Another concerns intense grief and regret exhausting the person’s strength.

These explanations suggest intelligible relationships between meanings but should not be treated as a certain history of the word’s formation.

Someone may feel regret on recognizing their decision’s adverse result, or grieve over an event they already knew was painful.

Truth becoming apparent is therefore one occasion for regret, rather than a necessary condition of every use.

2. Ḥasīr, maḥsūr, and yastaḥsirūna: exhaustion and depleted strength

Wa-huwa ḥasīr: an exhausted gaze

Al-Mulk states:

ثُمَّ ارْجِعِ الْبَصَرَ كَرَّتَيْنِ يَنْقَلِبْ إِلَيْكَ الْبَصَرُ خَاسِئًا وَهُوَ حَسِيرٌ

thumma irjiʿi al-baṣara karratayni yanqalib ilayka al-baṣaru khāsiʾan wa-huwa ḥasīr

“Then turn your gaze back again and again; your gaze will return to you frustrated and weary.” (Al-Mulk 67:4)

Ḥasīr describes al-baṣar (الْبَصَر), the gaze.

The preceding verse invites people to turn their gaze back and look for a fissure or disharmony in creation. The fourth verse repeats the inspection and presents its outcome through khāsiʾan (خَاسِئًا) and ḥasīr.

Khāsiʾ concerns frustration and returning repelled or humbled, while ḥasīr concerns the gaze’s exhaustion and weariness.

The words are close but differ in meaning.

Lexically, ḥasīr does not mean remorseful over sin. Here a person’s gaze becomes weary through repeated examination and fails to find what it sought.

Nor does the verse say that someone’s eyes must suffer medical damage or lose their sight. The description belongs to repeated inspection and failure to find a defect.

Suitable equivalents include weary, exhausted, and worn out. Where fuller explanation is needed: a gaze returning exhausted and frustrated after repeated searching.

Malūman maḥsūran: depletion after unrestrained spending

Al-Isrāʾ states:

وَلَا تَجْعَلْ يَدَكَ مَغْلُولَةً إِلَىٰ عُنُقِكَ وَلَا تَبْسُطْهَا كُلَّ الْبَسْطِ فَتَقْعُدَ مَلُومًا مَحْسُورًا

wa-lā tajʿal yadaka maghlūlatan ilā ʿunuqika wa-lā tabsuṭhā kulla al-basṭi fa-taqʿuda malūman maḥsūrā

“Do not keep your hand bound to your neck, nor extend it without restraint, lest you remain blamed and depleted.” (Al-Isrāʾ 17:29)

Maḥsūr is a passive participle from Ḥ S R.

Two extremes face each other: binding the hand to the neck portrays severe withholding; extending it completely can represent spending or giving away resources without restraint.

The second behavior’s result is described as malūman maḥsūran (مَلُومًا مَحْسُورًا).

Malūm describes someone blamed, whereas maḥsūr concerns a depleted condition in which strength and resources are exhausted and the person remains unable to continue.

Earlier Arabic can call an animal worked until it cannot continue moving ḥasīr or maḥsūr.

Likewise, someone spending all their property may become unable to meet necessary needs or fulfill later financial responsibilities.

Maḥsūr here therefore means more than regretful. Regret may follow such conduct, but depletion and exhausted resources matter within the verse’s image.

Nor does maḥsūr necessarily mean having been poor from the outset. Someone may previously have had sufficient resources and become depleted through unrestrained spending.

The immediate context concerns the rights of relatives, people in need, and travelers, alongside avoiding wastefulness. (Al-Isrāʾ 17:26 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:27) The subject therefore concerns directing financial conduct and retaining the capacity to meet responsibilities.

The verse does not command miserliness or withholding help. The images of the bound and fully extended hand call for avoiding both extremes.

This conclusion comes from the whole verse; economic moderation is not maḥsūr’s lexical meaning itself.

Wa-lā yastaḥsirūna: not becoming exhausted in worship

Al-Anbiyāʾ states:

وَلَهُ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَمَنْ عِنْدَهُ لَا يَسْتَكْبِرُونَ عَنْ عِبَادَتِهِ وَلَا يَسْتَحْسِرُونَ

wa-lahu man fī al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-man ʿindahu lā yastakbirūna ʿan ʿibādatihi wa-lā yastaḥsirūna

“To Him belongs whoever is in the heavens and the earth; those near Him do not disdain worshipping Him and do not become exhausted.” (Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:19)

Yastaḥsirūna is a third-person masculine plural imperfect of form X, istaḥsara.

Here the verb concerns exhaustion and being unable to continue an activity.

The verse describes servants near The God who do not disdain worshipping Him or become exhausted.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:20 continues with their praise by night and day and lā yafturūna (لَا يَفْتُرُونَ).

The continuity clarifies that yastaḥsirūna concerns depleted strength or exhaustion in worship here, rather than remorse over earlier conduct.

Lā yastaḥsirūna and lā yafturūna are close but not completely synonymous. The first denies exhaustion; the second concerns flagging, reduced activity, or interruption of continuity.

“Do not grow weary” therefore suits the first verb; “do not become exhausted or worn out in worship” makes its intensity clearer.

This verb alone should not determine whether the beings described have bodies, how their capacities operate, or the ontological character of their worship. The verse describes their conduct and worship; other details require independent evidence.

3. Ḥasrah: regret over deeds, opportunities, and lost outcomes

Ḥasarātin ʿalayhim: when deeds become causes of regret

Al-Baqarah states:

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوا لَوْ أَنَّ لَنَا كَرَّةً فَنَتَبَرَّأَ مِنْهُمْ كَمَا تَبَرَّءُوا مِنَّا كَذَٰلِكَ يُرِيهِمُ اللَّهُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ حَسَرَاتٍ عَلَيْهِمْ وَمَا هُمْ بِخَارِجِينَ مِنَ النَّارِ

wa-qāla alladhīna ittabaʿū law anna lanā karratan fa-natabarraʾa minhum ka-mā tabarraʾū minnā kadhālika yurīhimu llāhu aʿmālahum ḥasarātin ʿalayhim wa-mā hum bi-khārijīna mina al-nāri

“And those who followed say, ‘If only we could return, so that we might disavow them as they have disavowed us.’ Thus The God shows them their deeds as regrets weighing upon them; they will not leave the Fire.” (Al-Baqarah 2:167)

Ḥasarāt is the plural of ḥasrah.

Here regret concerns deeds people performed in their earlier life, whose consequences they now see.

The preceding verses describe people treating others as rivals to The God and showing them love and loyalty. Upon seeing the punishment, ties between leaders and followers collapse, and the followers wish for an opportunity to return.

Deeds previously accepted and pursued now become causes of grief and remorse for those who performed them.

Ḥasarāt therefore does not itself mean evil deeds: it names the condition those deeds have brought upon someone.

The plural need not enumerate particular kinds of sin. It may express the abundance or recurrence of regrets arising from seeing deeds and their consequences.

The usage shows regret arising after conduct’s outcome becomes apparent. What someone was once attached to now causes grief.

Yā ḥasratanā: lament over earlier neglect

Al-Anʿām states:

قَدْ خَسِرَ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِلِقَاءِ اللَّهِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَتْهُمُ السَّاعَةُ بَغْتَةً قَالُوا يَا حَسْرَتَنَا عَلَىٰ مَا فَرَّطْنَا فِيهَا وَهُمْ يَحْمِلُونَ أَوْزَارَهُمْ عَلَىٰ ظُهُورِهِمْ أَلَا سَاءَ مَا يَزِرُونَ

qad khasira alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-liqāʾi llāhi ḥattā idhā jāʾathumu al-sāʿatu baghtatan qālū yā ḥasratanā ʿalā mā farraṭnā fīhā wa-hum yaḥmilūna awzārahum ʿalā ẓuhūrihim alā sāʾa mā yazirūna

“Those who denied meeting The God have lost. When the Hour suddenly comes upon them, they say, ‘Alas for us, for what we neglected regarding it,’ while bearing their burdens on their backs. How evil is what they bear!” (Al-Anʿām 6:31)

Yā ḥasratanā is a vocative construction attributing regret to the speakers themselves.

They speak of their earlier neglect. Farraṭnā (فَرَّطْنَا) belongs to F R Ṭ and concerns neglecting, failing to attend to something, or losing it through carelessness.

Regret here concerns a past opportunity whose neglected use now has visible consequences.

Ḥasrah itself does not mean neglecting. Farraṭnā expresses the neglect; ḥasrah is the resulting grief and regret.

In Al-Zumar 39:56, someone likewise uses yā ḥasratā to lament their neglect of duty toward The God and admits having been among those who mocked.

Yā ḥasratā voices personal regret and may be translated “Alas for me!” or “How I regret!”

The distinction between the plural possessive and the expression of personal regret does not change the root’s basic meaning.

Regret over spending that fails to achieve its purpose

Al-Anfāl states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ لِيَصُدُّوا عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ فَسَيُنْفِقُونَهَا ثُمَّ تَكُونُ عَلَيْهِمْ حَسْرَةً ثُمَّ يُغْلَبُونَ وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ يُحْشَرُونَ

inna alladhīna kafarū yunfiqūna amwālahum li-yaṣuddū ʿan sabīli llāhi fa-sa-yunfiqūnahā thumma takūnu ʿalayhim ḥasratan thumma yughlabūna wa-alladhīna kafarū ilā jahannama yuḥsharūna

“Those who disbelieve spend their wealth to hinder the way of The God. They will spend it; then it will become a cause of regret for them, and then they will be overcome. Those who disbelieve will be gathered to Hell.” (Al-Anfāl 8:36)

Ḥasrah here is the result of spending wealth to hinder others from the way of The God.

They spend for a particular purpose, but the expected outcome fails to materialize, and their expenditure becomes a cause of regret for them.

Regret is connected with failure and loss here, but ḥasrah does not itself mean military defeat or destroyed wealth.

Yughlabūna (يُغْلَبُونَ) states their defeat separately. Ḥasrah names the grief and regret following such expenditure and outcome.

Nor does the verse criticize every kind of spending. It concerns spending whose stated purpose is hindering the way of The God.

Spending, its aim, its financial outcome, and the feeling of regret must therefore remain distinct. Spending’s moral value follows from its purpose and context; not every expenditure must end in regret.

4. Grief for others: sorrow not necessarily arising from personal sin

Ḥasratan fī qulūbihim: grief over others’ deaths

Āl ʿImrān states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَكُونُوا كَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَقَالُوا لِإِخْوَانِهِمْ إِذَا ضَرَبُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ أَوْ كَانُوا غُزًّى لَوْ كَانُوا عِنْدَنَا مَا مَاتُوا وَمَا قُتِلُوا لِيَجْعَلَ اللَّهُ ذَٰلِكَ حَسْرَةً فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ وَاللَّهُ يُحْيِي وَيُمِيتُ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā takūnū ka-alladhīna kafarū wa-qālū li-ikhwānihim idhā ḍarabū fī al-arḍi aw kānū ghuzzan law kānū ʿindanā mā mātū wa-mā qutilū li-yajʿala llāhu dhālika ḥasratan fī qulūbihim wa-llāhu yuḥyī wa-yumītu wa-llāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīr

“You who believe, do not be like those who disbelieved and said of their brothers, when they traveled through the land or went to war, ‘Had they stayed with us, they would not have died or been killed,’ so that The God makes that a regret within their hearts. The God gives life and causes death; The God sees what you do.” (Āl ʿImrān 3:156)

Regret here settles in the hearts of people speaking about relatives or companions who died or were killed.

This shows regret connected with losing loved ones and imagining that their loss could have been prevented.

They say that those people would not have died or been killed if they had stayed. This goes beyond expressing grief: it asserts certainty about an event that did not occur.

Someone facing a loved one’s death may ask whether prevention was possible. Responsible examination of an event’s causes differs, however, from certainty that changing one factor would necessarily have prevented death.

The verse criticizes such a judgment alongside the particular group’s stance.

It must not imply that everyone grieving a loved one exemplifies that disbelief or criticism. Grief over loss is a human condition; the verse assesses a particular statement and position.

Here ḥasrah concerns grief and regret within the heart, which can intensify through repeatedly imagining the past and lost possibilities.

Fa-lā tadhhab nafsuka ʿalayhim ḥasarāt: intense grief over others’ fate

Fāṭir states:

أَفَمَنْ زُيِّنَ لَهُ سُوءُ عَمَلِهِ فَرَآهُ حَسَنًا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ يُضِلُّ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَهْدِي مَنْ يَشَاءُ فَلَا تَذْهَبْ نَفْسُكَ عَلَيْهِمْ حَسَرَاتٍ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ بِمَا يَصْنَعُونَ

a-fa-man zuyyina lahu sūʾu ʿamalihi fa-raʾāhu ḥasanan fa-inna llāha yuḍillu man yashāʾu wa-yahdī man yashāʾu fa-lā tadhhab nafsuka ʿalayhim ḥasarātin inna llāha ʿalīmun bi-mā yaṣnaʿūna

“Is someone whose evil conduct has been made attractive to them, so they see it as good, [equal to someone unlike that]? The God lets whom He wills go astray and guides whom He wills. Do not let your life ebb away in grief over them. The God knows what they do.” (Fāṭir 35:8)

This verse is especially important for understanding ḥasarāt’s range.

Muḥammad is addressed and told not to be consumed by intense grief over people who see their bad conduct as good.

Here regret need not mean Muḥammad’s remorse for a sin he committed. His grief concerns others’ condition and fate.

ʿAlayhim (عَلَيْهِمْ) clarifies the object: he grieves over them.

“Your remorse over your own sins” would therefore be incorrect here. Intense grief and lament for others fits better.

Fa-lā tadhhab nafsuka ʿalayhim ḥasarāt (فَلَا تَذْهَبْ نَفْسُكَ عَلَيْهِمْ حَسَرَاتٍ) portrays grief intense enough to consume someone’s life. Ḥasarāt emphasizes the intensity and abundance of grief, rather than necessarily several particular sins.

Grammatically, ḥasarāt can be considered in relation to the cause or condition of life ebbing away. Either way, the sentence concerns intense grief for others.

This shows regret arising from compassion and real concern for others, even when the grieving person did not cause their misconduct.

The verse does not make compassion or sadness over others’ misguidance inherently wrong. The prohibition concerns grief intense enough to consume the addressee’s life. Calling others to truth, goodwill, and responsibility for conveying the message are separate matters.

Yā ḥasratan ʿalā al-ʿibād: a cry of lament

Yā Sīn states:

يَا حَسْرَةً عَلَى الْعِبَادِ مَا يَأْتِيهِمْ مِنْ رَسُولٍ إِلَّا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ

yā ḥasratan ʿalā al-ʿibādi mā yaʾtīhim min rasūlin illā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna

“Alas for the servants! No messenger comes to them without their mocking him.” (Yā Sīn 36:30)

Ḥasrah here follows the vocative particle.

Arabic uses the construction for intense grief, lament, or astonishment. A speaker may call upon regret itself without treating it as a being with ears or the ability to answer.

English expressions such as “Alas!” or “O sorrow!” can have a comparable function.

The verse concerns servants mocking messengers, expressing lament over that conduct.

The construction alone does not identify precisely whose inward experience contains regret as a human psychological state.

Yā ḥasratan ʿalā al-ʿibādi (يَا حَسْرَةً عَلَى الْعِبَادِ) therefore does not establish that The God experiences human remorse, incapacity, or regret over His own earlier decisions.

The verse is an eloquent lament concerning the servants’ conduct and fate. Attributing a particular psychological condition to The God would go beyond the expression’s direct meaning.

Alongside Fāṭir 35:8, it shows that Qur’anic ḥasrah is not confined to remorse over one’s own sin. It can concern the fate of people losing their opportunity for guidance through their conduct.

5. The day of regret and sorrow when the outcome becomes clear

The day of regret: when the matter is decided

Maryam states:

وَأَنْذِرْهُمْ يَوْمَ الْحَسْرَةِ إِذْ قُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ وَهُمْ فِي غَفْلَةٍ وَهُمْ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

wa-andhirhum yawma al-ḥasrati idh quḍiya al-amru wa-hum fī ghaflatin wa-hum lā yuʾminūna

“Warn them of the day of regret, when the matter has been decided, while they are heedless and do not believe.” (Maryam 19:39)

Yawma al-ḥasrati (يَوْمَ الْحَسْرَةِ) associates regret with a particular day.

Regret is not itself a place name here. It is a prominent characteristic of a day when the matter is decided and people face the outcome.

Idh quḍiya al-amru (إِذْ قُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ) matters. The context connects regret with a time when the final decision and result are apparent and the earlier opportunity is no longer available in the same form.

The phrase does not establish that everyone regrets equally or for the same reason that day. The warning’s addressees and the verse’s heedlessness and failure to believe must remain in view.

Ḥasrah here concerns grief and regret connected with a disclosed result and a lost opportunity.

Wa-innahu la-ḥasratun ʿalā al-kāfirīn: what denial turns into a cause of regret

Al-Ḥāqqah states:

وَإِنَّهُ لَحَسْرَةٌ عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ

wa-innahu la-ḥasratun ʿalā al-kāfirīna

“It is indeed a cause of regret for those who disbelieve.” (Al-Ḥāqqah 69:50)

The preceding verses call the Qur’an a reminder for the mindful and mention deniers.

Grammatically, the pronoun in innahu (إِنَّهُ) may refer to the Qur’an or what has been discussed concerning it. Exegetical explanations discuss reference to the Qur’an as well as a connection with denying it.

Neither reading means that the Qur’an inherently is grief or remorse. The passage concerns a situation in which the truth or its denial becomes a cause of regret for deniers.

If the pronoun refers to the Qur’an, the regret concerns the result of denying it. If it refers to denial and the deniers’ position, that conduct itself becomes a cause of remorse when its consequences appear.

The pronoun’s referent matters but does not change ḥasrah’s meaning.

Both readings concern regret over a result that has become apparent to the person.

This is close to Al-Baqarah 2:167, where earlier deeds become causes of regret when their outcome is disclosed.

6. Distinctions from nearby roots and words

Ḥ S R (ḥasrah) compared with N D M (nadam)

Study of N D M

Nadam concerns remorse for something someone did or failed to do.

Ḥasrah can include that meaning, but its range is not confined to regretting a personal decision.

Fāṭir 35:8 warns Muḥammad against intense grief for others, while Yā Sīn 36:30 voices lament over servants. These uses concern more than admitting personal error.

When someone laments their own neglect in Al-Zumar 39:56, however, ḥasrah comes very close to nadam.

Remorse therefore fits some verses but does not cover every use.

Ḥ S R (ḥasrah) compared with Ḥ Z N (ḥuzn)

Study of Ḥ Z N

Ḥuzn concerns grief or sadness arising from many kinds of painful event.

Ḥasrah’s familiar uses more often concern loss, failure, a lost opportunity, or an unwelcome outcome.

Someone may grieve over a loved one’s illness without imagining an alterable past. Repeatedly lamenting a missed chance to help them is closer to regret.

The distinction is not absolute. In actual language, sadness and regret can coexist within one human experience.

Fāṭir 35:8 itself calls the Prophet’s intense grief for others ḥasarāt, preventing confinement of the term to reviewing one’s own past decisions.

Ḥ S R (ḥasrah) compared with A S F (asaf)

Study of A S F

Asaf can concern intense grief and, in some contexts, anger or strong distress.

In Yūsuf 12:84, Jacob says yā asafā ʿalā Yūsufa (يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ), expressing grief over Joseph’s absence.

The usage resembles ḥasrah in intensity of grief.

Ḥ S R also prominently includes exhaustion and regret over lost opportunity. Asaf in some contexts can accompany anger and displeasure.

Rendering both through “alas” or “regret” may be natural in some sentences, but the overlap should not erase their different ranges.

Ḥ S R (ḥasīr) compared with F T R (futūr)

Study of F T R

F T R concerns declining intensity, slackening, and interruption in activity.

Lā yafturūna in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:20 follows lā yastaḥsirūna in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:19.

Their juxtaposition shows that not becoming exhausted and not flagging can be related without being identical descriptions.

Yastaḥsirūna here concerns becoming exhausted or worn out in continued worship; yafturūna emphasizes reduced intensity or ceasing activity.

Ḥasīr and maḥsūr should therefore not always become simply sluggish or slack. Exhaustion and depleted strength give this family a more particular meaning.

Ḥ S R (ḥasara) compared with Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣara)

Study of Ḥ Ṣ R

The roots differ only in their middle consonant but differ in meaning.

Ḥ Ṣ R concerns limiting, constraining, surrounding, or preventing. Ḥaṣūr (حَصُور) in Āl ʿImrān 3:39 belongs to that family.

Ḥ S R, by contrast, concerns exposure, depleted strength, and regret.

Someone may be both depleted and constrained, but sharing a situation does not make maḥsūr and maḥṣūr the same root or precisely the same meaning.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:29 has maḥsūran (مَحْسُورًا), with sīn, for depletion after unrestrained spending, rather than being besieged or imprisoned.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Ḥasrah may be rendered as regret, lament, sorrow, intense grief, or, where appropriate, remorse.

Each verse should establish the object of that regret.

“A cause of regret for them” suits ḥasarātin ʿalayhim in Al-Baqarah 2:167, where their deeds have become objects of regret and remorse.

Yā ḥasratanā in Al-Anʿām 6:31 can be “Alas for us!” Yā ḥasratā in Al-Zumar 39:56 is close to “Alas for me!” or “How I regret!”

Ḥasratan in Al-Anfāl 8:36 means a cause of regret, describing the outcome of unsuccessful expenditure.

Ḥasratan fī qulūbihim (حَسْرَةً فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ) in Āl ʿImrān 3:156 is regret in their hearts. Explanation should connect it with losing loved ones and the speakers’ judgment concerning death.

Ḥasarāt in Fāṭir 35:8 concerns intense grief for others. “Consuming grief” can clarify its contextual force; a closer rendering may retain “sorrows” or “regrets.”

Yā ḥasratan ʿalā al-ʿibādi in Yā Sīn 36:30 naturally becomes “Alas for the servants!”

Yawma al-ḥasrati in Maryam 19:39 is the day of regret. The phrase need not itself contain every detail of the final judgment.

La-ḥasratun ʿalā al-kāfirīna (لَحَسْرَةٌ عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ) in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:50 can be “a cause of regret for those who disbelieve.” Contextual explanation should clarify innahu’s referent.

Weary, exhausted, or worn out suits ḥasīr in Al-Mulk 67:4. A rendering such as “a weary, frustrated gaze” must preserve the distinction between ḥasīr and khāsiʾ in analysis.

Depleted or left without resources fits maḥsūr naturally in Al-Isrāʾ 17:29. “Stripped of resources and unable to continue giving” can clarify its financial setting, but the word must not always be restricted to penniless.

Yastaḥsirūna in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:19 concerns becoming exhausted or worn out. In the verse’s negative construction, “do not become exhausted in worshipping Him” fits.

Arabic ḥasara ʿan dhirāʿihi concerns uncovering the forearm; inḥasara al-māʾu (اِنْحَسَرَ الْمَاءُ) can concern receding water exposing the surface beneath it. These forms do not occur in the Qur’anic text but help explain the root’s concrete branch.

All these translations should preserve the differences among exposure, depleted strength, exhaustion, grief, and remorse. One fixed equivalent cannot convey the entire family without attention to context.

Conclusion

Ḥ S R is an Arabic family with several branches. Uncovering and exposure, exhaustion and depleted strength, and intense regret and lament form its three main fields.

Concrete uses concern drawing back a sleeve, exposing an uncovered head, or water receding from ground. In strength-related uses, an exhausted animal or a gaze weary from repeated examination can be called ḥasīr.

Ḥasrah concerns grief and regret arising from loss, failure, earlier neglect, or an action’s disclosed result.

The branches’ relationship does not require explaining every use through one concrete image. Truth becoming apparent and depleted strength can contribute to regret, but regret need not always arise from discovering a personal error or physical exhaustion.

Ḥasīr in Al-Mulk 67:4 describes a weary gaze; maḥsūr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:29 expresses depletion through unrestrained spending; and lā yastaḥsirūna in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:19 describes servants not becoming exhausted in worship.

Other uses concern regret following earlier deeds, neglect, lost opportunities, or failure to achieve an aim. Al-Baqarah 2:167, Al-Anʿām 6:31, Al-Anfāl 8:36, and Al-Zumar 39:56 clearly illustrate this field.

In Fāṭir 35:8, however, ḥasarāt concerns intense grief for others’ fate; in Yā Sīn 36:30 it voices lament over servants. These uses show that regret is not always admitting sin or regretting a personal decision.

A highly restrictive definition therefore cannot cover every derivative of Ḥ S R.

This family’s semantic centers are uncovering and exposure, strength depleted into exhaustion, and intense grief and regret over loss or an unwelcome outcome. The third branch dominates Qur’anic ḥasrah, but each verse establishes regret’s cause and object. It must not be assumed that every instance is remorse over personal sin.
