Place, position, and source: from “wherever” to “from where one does not expect”

Arabic حَيْثُ (ḥaythu) identifies a place, position, or direction in which an event occurs, someone is situated, or something originates. Depending on the construction, Persian renders it as “where,” “wherever,” “from where,” and sometimes “from a direction in which.”

For example, حَيْثُ شِئْتَ (ḥaythu shiʾta) means where someone wishes; مِنْ حَيْثُ خَرَجْتَ (min ḥaythu kharajta) concerns the place or direction from which someone has emerged; and مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَعْلَمُ (min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamu) indicates a place or route of which someone is unaware.

These uses share one feature: ḥaythu indicates a position or source, but leaves its precise specification to the accompanying clause.

In the Qur’an, it may denote a visible, identifiable place, such as where someone lives; extend the spatial scope, as in “wherever you may be”; or indicate a source or direction beyond human knowledge and expectation.

Understanding ḥaythu therefore requires recognizing its spatial meaning without confining every use to a geographical point.

1. Qur’anic form and linguistic status

The formation of ḥaythu differs from most roots from which verbs, verbal nouns, active participles, and other derivatives are formed.

The Qur’an uses only حَيْثُ (ḥaythu), sometimes with the preposition min, sometimes with the conjunction wa, and twice with mā.

Its main constructions are:

  • حَيْثُ (ḥaythu): where, wherever, in the place where.
  • مِنْ حَيْثُ (min ḥaythu): from where, from a direction, route, or source.
  • وَحَيْثُ مَا (wa-ḥaythu mā): and wherever, in a conditional or spatially generalizing construction.

These are not independent derivatives of a verb. Min and wa are particles added to the sentence’s construction; mā also takes on a particular grammatical role with ḥaythu.

The Quranic Arabic Corpus records 31 occurrences in 29 verses for this entry. All belong to ḥaythu, although its grammatical classification distinguishes nominal or adverbial uses from conditional uses.

Quranic Arabic Corpus:31occurrences,29nominaland2conditional

Is ح ي ث a derivational root like other roots?

Lexical study must distinguish the entry used to index a word from a root producing a broad family of derivatives.

Ḥaythu is an established and largely non-derived Arabic adverbial noun of place. Unlike ك ت ب or خ ر ج, ح ي ث does not have a Qur’anic family of derived verbs and nouns.

Some early lexicographers identify حَوْثُ (ḥawthu) as the original form, with wāw changing to yāʾ. Ibn Sīdah, however, regards that explanation as weak and treats it cautiously.

The lexica also record ḥawthu as a form of the same word in certain Arabic dialects.

What can be established confidently is therefore that ḥaythu is a spatial word in the Qur’an. Reconstructing its precise history, or positing an original verb from which all its meanings derived, lacks sufficient support.

Basic meaning

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains ḥaythu as follows:

حَيْثُ عِبَارَةٌ عَنْ مَكَانٍ مُبْهَمٍ يُشْرَحُ بِالْجُمْلَةِ الَّتِي بَعْدَهُ

ḥaythu ʿibāratun ʿan makānin mubhamin yushraḥu bi-l-jumlati allatī baʿdahu

It denotes an unspecified place explained by the clause that follows it.

In ḥaythu shiʾta, for example, ḥaythu alone does not name a particular location. Shiʾta explains that the choice of location depends on the person’s wish.

The same feature appears in ḥaythu amarahum abūhum: their father’s instruction specifies the place.

“Unspecified” here does not mean nonexistent or unknowable. The word itself does not name a particular place; the accompanying clause identifies it.

2. Early Arabic use

The distinction between place and time

Arabic lexica connect ḥaythu with أَيْنَ (ayna) and أَيُّ مَوْضِعٍ (ayyu mawḍiʿin): “where?” and “which place?”

Lisān al-ʿArab, quoting Al-Jawharī, states:

حَيْثُ كَلِمَةٌ تَدُلُّ عَلَى الْمَكَانِ

ḥaythu kalimatun tadullu ʿalā al-makāni

Ḥaythu is a word denoting place.

The same source carefully distinguishes حَيْثُ (ḥaythu) from حِينَ (ḥīna).

Ḥaythu is an adverbial noun of place; ḥīna is one of time. For example:

رَأَيْتُكَ حَيْثُ كُنْتَ (raʾaytuka ḥaythu kunta) means “I saw you where you were.”

رَأَيْتُكَ حِينَ خَرَجَ الْحَاجُّ (raʾaytuka ḥīna kharaja al-ḥājju), however, means “I saw you when the pilgrims set out.”

The first sentence concerns place; the second concerns time.

Early lexicographical reports describe Al-Aṣmaʿī and Abū Ḥātim al-Sijistānī criticizing confusion between ḥaythu and ḥīna, emphasizing that ḥaythu should not replace ḥīna without justification.

These reports emphasize the prevailing distinction between spatial and temporal adverbs. Al-Akhfash and some grammarians nevertheless recognize temporal uses of ḥaythu in particular witnesses; the distinction should not become a denial of every temporal use throughout Arabic.

The distinction shows that ح ي ث and ح ي ن should not be conflated merely because their spelling and some uses are close.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Ibn Hishām’s account: the prevailing place/time distinction and exceptional witnesses

Dialectal forms: حَيْثُ and حَوْثُ

The familiar early Arabic form is ḥaythu, with a final ḍammah.

Lexicographical reports also record some tribes using ḥaytha. Some accounts describe the final vowel varying according to syntactic position.

Al-Kisāʾī reports ḥaytha among some groups of Banū Tamīm and other tribes.

The sources also transmit ḥawthu.

These differences concern pronunciation and formation. The evidence does not establish separate meanings such as near place, distant place, or hidden place for the different forms.

Neither the variation between wāw and yāʾ nor the final vowel should therefore be used to invent a new spatial meaning.

A poetic witness from Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr

A verse by Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

هُمُ الْأَصْلُ مِنِّي حَيْثُ كُنْتُ وَإِنَّنِي
مِنَ الْمُزَنِيِّينَ الْمُصَفَّيْنَ بِالْكَرَمِ

humu al-aṣlu minnī ḥaythu kuntu wa-innanī / mina al-muzaniyyīna al-muṣaffayna bi-l-karami

Translation:

“They are my stock and lineage wherever I may be; I am of Muzaynah, distinguished by generosity.”

Ḥaythu kuntu here means “wherever I may be.”

The poet affirms his connection to a particular family and tribe: changing his place of residence does not dissolve that connection.

Ḥaythu does not name a particular place here; it extends the statement to any place where the poet may be.

This resembles the Qur’anic wa-ḥaythu mā kuntum in its spatial generalization.

Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr experienced both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, but this verse’s precise date is uncertain. It should not be called securely earlier than the Qur’an without independent evidence. Al-Istīʿāb also transmits the second hemistich with الْمُضِيفِينَ لِلْكَرَمِ (al-muḍīfīna li-l-karami). This variant does not change the spatial meaning of ḥaythu kuntu.

Al-Istīʿāb: Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr and a variant of the lineage witness

A witness concerning syntax

Arabic grammatical works transmit a verse beginning:

أَمَا تَرَى حَيْثَ سُهَيْلٍ طَالِعًا
نَجْمًا يُضِيءُ كَالشِّهَابِ لَامِعًا

a-mā tarā ḥaytha suhaylin ṭāliʿan / najman yuḍīʾu ka-l-shihābi lāmiʿan

Translation:

“Do you not see the place where Suhayl rises, a star shining brightly like a flaming light?”

Works including Sharḥ Ibn ʿAqīl quote this verse, whose author is not securely identified. The commentary also transmits ḥaythu with ḍammah: variation between fatḥah and ḍammah should be distinguished from Suhayl’s genitive case and the discussion of annexation to a single noun.

The witness matters because a noun follows ḥaythu. In standard and prevailing use, a complete clause normally follows it; this witness has a different construction whose grammatical analysis is debated.

The grammatical difference does not alter the basic spatial meaning.

Since its attribution and date are uncertain, it should not be treated as a securely pre-Islamic witness.

Sharḥ Ibn ʿAqīl: the Suhayl witness and annexation to a noun

3. Spatial uses in the Qur’an

حَيْثُ شِئْتُمَا (ḥaythu shiʾtumā): wherever you two wish

وَقُلْنَا يَا آدَمُ اسْكُنْ أَنْتَ وَزَوْجُكَ الْجَنَّةَ وَكُلَا مِنْهَا رَغَدًا حَيْثُ شِئْتُمَا وَلَا تَقْرَبَا هَٰذِهِ الشَّجَرَةَ فَتَكُونَا مِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-qulnā yā ādamu uskun anta wa-zawjuka al-jannata wa-kulā minhā raghadan ḥaythu shiʾtumā wa-lā taqrabā hādhihi al-shajarata fa-takūnā mina al-ẓālimīna

Proposed translation:

“We said, ‘Adam, dwell with your spouse in the garden, and eat freely and abundantly from it wherever you wish. But do not approach this tree, lest you become wrongdoers.’”

Al-Baqarah 2:35

Ḥaythu shiʾtumā expresses a broad spatial choice within the garden.

Adam and his spouse may enjoy the garden’s provisions wherever they wish.

That breadth does not mean the absence of every restriction: the same verse prohibits approaching a particular tree.

Ḥaythu alone therefore does not declare every action lawful or permitted. Permission comes from kulā, “eat, you two,” together with ḥaythu shiʾtumā; the restriction follows later in the verse.

The same construction recurs in Al-Aʿrāf 7:19.

Al-Baqarah 2:58 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:161 likewise grant permission to eat wherever the addressees wish in the settlement they are commanded to enter.

In all these uses, their wish specifies the place, while the context establishes the overall area.

وَحَيْثُ مَا كُنْتُمْ (wa-ḥaythu mā kuntum): wherever you may be

قَدْ نَرَىٰ تَقَلُّبَ وَجْهِكَ فِي السَّمَاءِ فَلَنُوَلِّيَنَّكَ قِبْلَةً تَرْضَاهَا فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَحَيْثُ مَا كُنْتُمْ فَوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ شَطْرَهُ وَإِنَّ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ لَيَعْلَمُونَ أَنَّهُ الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ وَمَا اللَّهُ بِغَافِلٍ عَمَّا يَعْمَلُونَ

qad narā taqalluba wajhika fī al-samāʾi fa-la-nuwalliyannaka qiblatan tarḍāhā fa-walli wajhaka shaṭra al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi wa-ḥaythu mā kuntum fa-wallū wujūhakum shaṭrahu wa-inna alladhīna ūtū al-kitāba la-yaʿlamūna annahu al-ḥaqqu min rabbihim wa-mā allāhu bi-ghāfilin ʿammā yaʿmalūna

Proposed translation:

“We see your face turning toward the sky. We shall turn you toward a qiblah that pleases you. Turn your face toward the Sacred Mosque; wherever you may be, turn your faces toward it. Those given the Book certainly know that this is the truth from their Lord. The God is not unaware of what they do.”

Al-Baqarah 2:144

Wa-ḥaythu mā kuntum extends the command’s spatial scope.

It is not confined to the Prophet’s location or the place of revelation: it includes the plural addressees wherever they are.

The construction recurs in Al-Baqarah 2:150.

In ḥaythu shiʾtumā, the addressees’ wish specifies the place; in ḥaythu mā kuntum, their location, wherever it may be, falls within the command’s scope.

Mā contributes to spatial generalization. The whole expression is therefore most naturally translated as “wherever you may be.”

In Qur’anic orthography, ḥaythu and mā are separate words. Arabic grammar also recognizes their combination, ḥaythumā, as a spatial conditional expression.

Ḥaythu here does not itself mean qiblah, mosque, or direction of worship. Those meanings come from other words, particularly wujūhakum, shaṭrahu, and al-masjid al-ḥarām.

Forqan’s article “Qibla, Wajh, and Hanif in the Qur’an” separately examines the broader concept of qiblah and its relationship with the face and human orientation.

مِنْ حَيْثُ خَرَجْتَ (min ḥaythu kharajta): wherever you emerge from

Al-Baqarah 2:149 and Al-Baqarah 2:150 place min ḥaythu kharajta alongside the command to turn toward the Sacred Mosque.

Min identifies an origin or point from which movement begins.

The construction can indicate a place of departure, but the qiblah context does not confine the command to one particular point.

Whereas wa-ḥaythu mā kuntum concerns the addressees’ presence, min ḥaythu kharajta concerns emerging and beginning a movement.

The expressions differ in construction, although both extend the command spatially.

Min ḥaythu alone does not establish that the verse concerns only travel, leaving home, or a particular place within a city. Those specifications need further contextual evidence.

حَيْثُ يَشَاءُ (ḥaythu yashāʾu): a place one wishes

In Yūsuf 12:56, The God speaks of establishing Joseph in the land so that he can settle wherever he wishes.

In Ṣād 38:36, the wind is subjected to Solomon and flows gently at his command toward the direction he intends.

In Al-Zumar 39:74, the people of the garden speak of settling wherever they wish within it.

In these three uses, ḥaythu concerns a position or direction connected with someone’s wish and choice.

Yet ḥaythu does not itself mean ownership, power, or unlimited freedom. Those concepts come from the accompanying verbs and each verse’s setting.

Ḥaythu yashāʾu can therefore be rendered “wherever he wishes,” without making the phrase alone establish every limit of a person’s ability or authority.

4. From a particular place to position and source

Some Qur’anic uses concern tangible, identifiable places; others extend the notion of position and source.

اللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ حَيْثُ يَجْعَلُ رِسَالَتَهُ (allāhu aʿlamu ḥaythu yajʿalu risālatahu)

وَإِذَا جَاءَتْهُمْ آيَةٌ قَالُوا لَنْ نُؤْمِنَ حَتَّىٰ نُؤْتَىٰ مِثْلَ مَا أُوتِيَ رُسُلُ اللَّهِ اللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ حَيْثُ يَجْعَلُ رِسَالَتَهُ سَيُصِيبُ الَّذِينَ أَجْرَمُوا صَغَارٌ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَعَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ بِمَا كَانُوا يَمْكُرُونَ

wa-idhā jāʾathum āyatun qālū lan nuʾmina ḥattā nuʾtā mithla mā ūtiya rusulu allāhi allāhu aʿlamu ḥaythu yajʿalu risālatahu sa-yuṣību alladhīna ajramū ṣaghārun ʿinda allāhi wa-ʿadhābun shadīdun bi-mā kānū yamkurūna

Proposed translation:

“When a sign comes to them, they say, ‘We shall not believe until we receive the like of what the messengers of The God received.’ The God knows best where to place His message. Those who committed wrongdoing will be afflicted with humiliation before The God and severe punishment for their scheming.”

Al-Anʿām 6:124

Here ḥaythu occurs in allāhu aʿlamu ḥaythu yajʿalu risālatahu.

Confining it to a point on the earth would leave the sentence’s meaning incomplete. The subject is placing the divine message: The God knows best the position in which to place it.

The context connects that position with choosing the person entrusted with the message.

“Where” here is consequently more than a geographical address. It can indicate the appropriate position for entrusting a responsibility.

That extension must not become a new definition such as “ḥaythu means moral worthiness.”

Ḥaythu still concerns position; its suitability is understood from the relationship between aʿlam and yajʿalu risālatahu, and the subject of selecting a messenger.

This is a particularly clear Qur’anic witness to spatial meaning being used for a position beyond geography.

Al-Ṭabarī: the position of the message and the recipient’s suitability

مِنْ حَيْثُ أَمَرَهُمْ أَبُوهُمْ (min ḥaythu amarahum abūhum)

وَلَمَّا دَخَلُوا مِنْ حَيْثُ أَمَرَهُمْ أَبُوهُمْ مَا كَانَ يُغْنِي عَنْهُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ إِلَّا حَاجَةً فِي نَفْسِ يَعْقُوبَ قَضَاهَا وَإِنَّهُ لَذُو عِلْمٍ لِمَا عَلَّمْنَاهُ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-lammā dakhalū min ḥaythu amarahum abūhum mā kāna yughnī ʿanhum mina allāhi min shayʾin illā ḥājatan fī nafsi yaʿqūba qaḍāhā wa-innahu la-dhū ʿilmin li-mā ʿallamnāhu wa-lākinna akthara al-nāsi lā yaʿlamūna

Proposed translation:

“When they entered from where their father had instructed them, this could not avert anything from The God on their behalf. It was only a need within Jacob that he fulfilled. He certainly possessed knowledge because of what We had taught him, but most people do not know.”

Yūsuf 12:68

Min ḥaythu identifies the place or direction of entry.

The preceding verse explains that Jacob told his sons to enter through separate gates rather than one gate (Yūsuf 12:67).

That earlier instruction consequently specifies the place in Yūsuf 12:68.

The example shows that ḥaythu may indicate a location already introduced in the discourse. The word itself does not name a gate or particular route.

Nor does ḥaythu establish Jacob’s purpose or motivation. It simply relates their entry to the instructed place.

مِنْ حَيْثُ أَمَرَكُمُ اللَّهُ (min ḥaythu amarakumu allāhu)

Al-Baqarah 2:222 follows its discussion of menstruation and women becoming clean with faʾtūhunna min ḥaythu amarakumu allāhu.

Ḥaythu identifies a place or direction to which the divine instruction relates.

The direct sense is: “Then approach them from where The God has instructed you.”

The sentence concerns marital relations and cleanness following menstruation; its application must therefore consider all the verse’s expressions.

Ḥaythu itself, however, does not mean a bodily organ, a particular sexual act, or a detailed legal ruling.

Just as ḥaythu amarahum abūhum in Yūsuf 12:68 identifies the place specified by the father’s instruction, ḥaythu amarakumu allāhu identifies the place or direction specified by the divine instruction.

The difference in subject comes from the two contexts, rather than a change in ḥaythu’s meaning.

أَسْكِنُوهُنَّ مِنْ حَيْثُ سَكَنْتُمْ (askinūhunna min ḥaythu sakantum)

أَسْكِنُوهُنَّ مِنْ حَيْثُ سَكَنْتُمْ مِنْ وُجْدِكُمْ وَلَا تُضَارُّوهُنَّ لِتُضَيِّقُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ وَإِنْ كُنَّ أُولَاتِ حَمْلٍ فَأَنْفِقُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ حَتَّىٰ يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ فَإِنْ أَرْضَعْنَ لَكُمْ فَآتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ وَأْتَمِرُوا بَيْنَكُمْ بِمَعْرُوفٍ وَإِنْ تَعَاسَرْتُمْ فَسَتُرْضِعُ لَهُ أُخْرَىٰ

askinūhunna min ḥaythu sakantum min wujdikum wa-lā tuḍārrūhunna li-tuḍayyiqū ʿalayhinna wa-in kunna ūlāti ḥamlin fa-anfiqū ʿalayhinna ḥattā yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunna fa-in arḍaʿna lakum fa-ātūhunna ujūrahunna waʾtamirū baynakum bi-maʿrūfin wa-in taʿāsartum fa-saturḍiʿu lahu ukhrā

Proposed translation:

“House them where you yourselves live, according to your means, and do not harm them to constrain them. If they are pregnant, provide for them until they deliver. If they nurse for you, give them their payment, and consult together honorably. If you encounter difficulty with one another, another woman will nurse for him.”

Al-Ṭalāq 65:6

Min ḥaythu sakantum clearly concerns accommodation.

The phrase does not independently specify every housing condition. Min wujdikum considers a person’s means and resources, while the next clause prohibits harming women to constrain them.

The spatial meaning therefore appears alongside specific rights and responsibilities.

The phrase alone does not establish that a woman must live in exactly the same room or private space as the man. Those details are not contained in ḥaythu itself.

The verse establishes a connection between women’s accommodation and the man’s actual means, while prohibiting harm intended to pressure them.

This example shows ḥaythu indicating an everyday, actual place, while the whole verse’s ruling comes from its combined expressions.

Al-Ṭabarī: housing according to means and the prohibition of harm

5. مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ (min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūna): an unseen or unexpected source

One prominent Qur’anic use combines ḥaythu with verbs of knowledge and awareness.

In such constructions, the place or source of an event is related to the limits of human knowledge and expectation.

مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا تَرَوْنَهُمْ (min ḥaythu lā tarawnahum)

يَا بَنِي آدَمَ لَا يَفْتِنَنَّكُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ كَمَا أَخْرَجَ أَبَوَيْكُمْ مِنَ الْجَنَّةِ يَنْزِعُ عَنْهُمَا لِبَاسَهُمَا لِيُرِيَهُمَا سَوْآتِهِمَا إِنَّهُ يَرَاكُمْ هُوَ وَقَبِيلُهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا تَرَوْنَهُمْ إِنَّا جَعَلْنَا الشَّيَاطِينَ أَوْلِيَاءَ لِلَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

yā banī ādama lā yaftinannakumu al-shayṭānu ka-mā akhraja abawaykum mina al-jannati yanziʿu ʿanhumā libāsahumā li-yuriyahumā sawʾātihimā innahu yarākum huwa wa-qabīluhu min ḥaythu lā tarawnahum innā jaʿalnā al-shayāṭīna awliyāʾa li-alladhīna lā yuʾminūna

Proposed translation:

“Children of Adam, do not let Satan tempt you as he brought your parents out of the garden, stripping them of their clothing to show them their private parts. He and his company see you from where you do not see them. We have made the devils allies of those who do not believe.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:27

Min ḥaythu lā tarawnahum describes one-sided seeing: Satan and his company see people, but people do not see them.

Ḥaythu identifies the position or direction from which observation occurs.

The word itself does not mean the unseen world, invisibility, or a special power of Satan. Such notions come from the complete sentence.

Nor should the verse be taken, without evidence, to mean that Satan observes people under every condition, from every direction, or with unlimited knowledge. Those generalizations exceed ḥaythu’s linguistic meaning.

The clear linguistic point is that min ḥaythu can identify an observational position even when the other party cannot see it.

مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ (min ḥaythu lā yashʿurūna)

In Al-Naḥl 16:26, Al-Naḥl 16:45, and Al-Zumar 39:25, punishment reaches groups from where they are unaware.

The expression identifies a source or direction beyond their awareness.

Lā yashʿurūna should not be treated as the meaning of ḥaythu itself. The first part indicates position or source, while the second establishes their lack of awareness.

Natural Persian can therefore say “from where they were unaware” or “from a direction they did not expect,” although the latter receives part of its sense of unexpectedness from context.

مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ (min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūna)

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:182 and Al-Qalam 68:44, min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūna accompanies sa-nastadrijuhum.

Ḥaythu identifies a source or direction of which someone is unaware. Istidrāj is a separate concept and must be studied through its own root and uses.

Together, they describe a process advancing from a direction the person does not know or recognize.

Gradual progression, deliberate concealment, and the event’s precise form are not inherent in ḥaythu itself.

مِنْ حَيْثُ لَمْ يَحْتَسِبُوا (min ḥaythu lam yaḥtasibū)

In Al-Ḥashr 59:2, people think their fortresses will protect them, but events contradict their calculations and expectations.

Fa-atāhumu allāhu min ḥaythu lam yaḥtasibū expresses that difference between their conception and what happens.

Ḥaythu again indicates source or direction, while lam yaḥtasibū establishes that it had no place in their calculations and expectations.

The expression should not be read as bodily movement of The God from one point to another. The verse speaks of divine action and judgment reaching them, then describes the consequences.

The contextual extension from physical place to the source of an event is therefore appropriate here.

Al-Ṭabarī: divine action reaching them from beyond their expectations

وَيَرْزُقْهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَحْتَسِبُ (wa-yarzuqhu min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasibu)

وَيَرْزُقْهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَحْتَسِبُ وَمَنْ يَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ فَهُوَ حَسْبُهُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بَالِغُ أَمْرِهِ قَدْ جَعَلَ اللَّهُ لِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدْرًا

wa-yarzuqhu min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasibu wa-man yatawakkal ʿalā allāhi fa-huwa ḥasbuhu inna allāha bālighu amrihi qad jaʿala allāhu li-kulli shayʾin qadran

Proposed translation:

“He provides for him from where he does not expect. Whoever relies on The God, He suffices him. The God brings His purpose to completion; The God has appointed a measure for everything.”

Al-Ṭalāq 65:3

Min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasibu concerns provision here.

Unlike the examples concerning punishment, the same construction now describes sustenance.

This difference shows that ḥaythu inherently means neither reward nor punishment. It can occur in either context.

Lā yaḥtasibu concerns the person’s calculations and expectations. Provision reaches them from a direction they did not foresee.

The phrase does not mean that provision is causeless or that people need not make an effort. The verse makes no such claim.

Nor does ḥaythu mean an unknown time here. Someone may also be unaware when provision will arrive, but the expression directly concerns its source and direction.

Persian can render this as “from where he does not expect” or “through a way he had not anticipated.”

The second works when “way” means the source or means through which provision arrives, without adding a particular physical route to the verse.

6. Spatial breadth and the limits of a ruling

Some verses use ḥaythu in the sense of “wherever,” but spatial breadth should not be confused with an unrestricted ruling.

In verses about fighting

Al-Baqarah 2:191, Al-Nisāʾ 4:89, Al-Nisāʾ 4:91, and Al-Tawbah 9:5 use expressions such as ḥaythu wajadtumūhum and ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum.

Ḥaythu identifies where the opposing party is encountered or found.

The scope and conditions of the command cannot, however, be established from ḥaythu alone.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:190 concerns fighting those who fight the addressees and prohibits transgression. Al-Baqarah 2:191 continues the confrontation and reciprocal expulsion, and adds a particular restriction on fighting near the Sacred Mosque.

Spatial generality in that context does not authorize an unrestricted rule of fighting against every person or religious opponent.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:90 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:91 likewise require considering withdrawal from fighting, peace, and refraining from aggression when explaining the ruling.

Al-Tawbah 9:4 excepts agreements faithfully maintained by the other party from the general ruling under discussion, while Al-Tawbah 9:6 separately addresses a polytheist’s request for protection.

Those restrictions come from the context and related verses, rather than ḥaythu’s lexical meaning.

The word itself concerns the place of an action. To whom it applies, under what conditions, and within what limits it is permitted or required are separate questions.

In rites and commands to move

Al-Baqarah 2:199 commands departure from where other people depart.

Al-Ḥijr 15:65 commands Lot to set out with his companions and proceed where he is instructed.

In both uses, ḥaythu identifies a place or direction of movement; the accompanying verb and command establish the purpose and conditions.

This also shows that ḥaythu is not exclusive to warfare, pilgrimage, or travel. In different situations it identifies the place or direction relevant to the action.

حَيْثُ أَتَىٰ (ḥaythu atā): wherever he goes

Ṭā Hā 20:69 states that the magician does not prosper wherever he goes.

This is spatial generalization. Ḥaythu does not independently mean failure, falsehood, or exclusion from success.

The complete sentence establishes the statement about the magician; ḥaythu atā extends its spatial scope.

7. Comparison with related words

ح ي ث (ḥayth) and ح ي ن (ḥīn): place and time

Ḥaythu primarily identifies place or position; ḥīna concerns time.

Ḥaythu dakhalū means “where they entered,” while ḥīna dakhalū means “when they entered.”

Both expressions may concern the same event, but one identifies its location and the other its time.

Contemporary Persian sometimes confuses ḥayth and ḥīn through their similar spelling or formal uses. Qur’anic translation must preserve the distinction.

Study of Ḥ Y N

ح ي ث (ḥayth) and أ ي ن (ayn): a specified place and asking where

Ayna usually asks about place: “Where?”

In its established uses, ḥaythu is an adverbial noun referring to a place explained by its accompanying clause.

Ayna anta asks where someone is; ḥaythu anta refers to the place where they are.

Generalizing constructions such as ḥaythumā and aynamā draw the two close: both can mean “wherever.”

That proximity does not make all their grammatical functions identical.

ح ي ث (ḥayth) and م ك ن (makān): position and the noun for a place

Makān is an established noun for a place or position. Ḥaythu normally indicates a situation specified by its accompanying clause.

In ḥaythu sakantum, it points to where people live, but does not itself name the place or even its type.

Makān can occur independently as a noun; ḥaythu generally depends on a clause specifying the position.

“Where” is consequently sometimes more natural than the noun “place” in translating ḥaythu.

ح ي ث (ḥayth) and و ج ه (jihah): place and direction

Jihah can describe a direction of movement or orientation. Lexica discuss جِهَة (jihah) under و ج ه, rather than an independent root ج ه ة.

Ḥaythu can also concern direction in constructions such as min ḥaythu kharajta or ḥaythu tuʾmarūna.

Yet ḥaythu does not always mean a geometrical direction. Allāhu aʿlamu ḥaythu yajʿalu risālatahu concerns the appropriate placement of the message, rather than a geographical angle or bearing.

Direction is therefore one contextual use, not the limit of the word’s entire meaning.

Early lexica, W J H: jihah belongs to W J H

ح ي ث (ḥayth) and ع ن د (ʿind): position and proximity

ʿInda can indicate spatial proximity or being with someone; other uses express possessing something or having it with a person.

Ḥaythu refers to a place or position specified by the following clause.

ʿInda allāh should therefore not automatically be replaced with ḥaythu allāh. Each construction has its own range and grammatical features.

Ḥayth and contemporary Persian ḥaythiyyat

Contemporary Persian uses ḥayth in expressions such as az īn ḥayth, meaning “in this respect” or “from this point of view.”

This connects with the extension of spatial meaning to an aspect or consideration.

Contemporary Persian ḥaythiyyat, however, usually means someone’s reputation, standing, or social status.

That later technical meaning must not be transferred to Qur’anic ḥaythu.

Allāhu aʿlamu ḥaythu yajʿalu risālatahu, for example, does not mean that The God knows the message’s reputation best: it concerns where to place the message.

Likewise, min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasibu does not mean “from an unknown social reputation,” but identifies a source or direction the person does not expect.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Ḥaythu should be translated according to its construction and context, rather than by a single fixed equivalent everywhere.

In Al-Baqarah 2:35, ḥaythu shiʾtumā can be rendered “wherever you two wish.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:144 and Al-Baqarah 2:150, wa-ḥaythu mā kuntum is most naturally “and wherever you may be.”

In Yūsuf 12:68, min ḥaythu amarahum abūhum means “from where their father had instructed them.”

In Al-Ṭalāq 65:6, min ḥaythu sakantum can be rendered “where you yourselves live.”

In Al-Anʿām 6:124, “The God knows best where to place His message” is natural and faithful to the Arabic construction. The person’s suitability for the message becomes clear from explaining the context.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:27, min ḥaythu lā tarawnahum means “from where you do not see them.”

In Al-Ṭalāq 65:3, min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasibu can mean “from where he does not expect.”

For min ḥaythu lā yashʿurūna, “from where they are unaware” or “from a direction of which they are unaware” is appropriate.

In all these examples, the preposition and accompanying clause establish the main difference.

Min ḥaythu should not always be translated “with regard to”: in some verses, such as Yūsuf 12:68, place and the point of entry are clear.

Conversely, Al-Anʿām 6:124 should not be forced into an entirely geographical translation on the assumption that ḥaythu can indicate only a tangible point.

9. Conclusion

Arabic ḥaythu is an adverbial noun indicating a place, position, or source whose precise specification depends on its accompanying clause.

Qur’anic uses range from actual places of entry and residence to the placement of the message, a direction of observation, and sources of unseen or unexpected events.

Ḥaythu alone does not express freedom, prohibition, reward, punishment, selection for the message, or heedlessness. Those meanings come from other parts of the sentence and its context.

Nor is ḥaythu identical with ḥīna: the first primarily concerns place and position; the second concerns time.

Linguistically, Qur’anic ḥaythu is largely a non-derived word, rather than a family of derived verbs and nouns. The sources offer different accounts of ḥawthu and the word’s historical formation; none should be asserted as certain without evidence.

Final definition: Ḥaythu denotes a place, position, or source specified by its accompanying clause. With min, it can indicate the point of departure, direction, or source of an event; in generalizing constructions, it means “wherever.” The Qur’an also uses it for positions beyond geography, but each use must be understood from its own context. Brief word meaning: A place or position specified by the accompanying clause; with min, an origin or source, and in generalizing use, wherever. Ḥaythu is largely a non-derived adverbial noun.
