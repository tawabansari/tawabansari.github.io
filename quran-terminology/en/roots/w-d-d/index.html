The Qur’anic Root W D D

Wadda: affection, wanting, and wishing something to happen

The Qur’anic root و د د, W D D, has several closely related but distinct branches: the verb وَدَّ / يَوَدُّ, wadda / yawaddu, which often approaches wanting and wishing; مَوَدَّة, mawaddah, and وُدّ, wudd, in the field of affection and emotional attachment; وَدُود, wadūd, used twice to describe The God; and وَدّ, Wadd, the proper name of a deity in Nūḥ. One form III verb, يُوَادُّونَ, yuwāddūna, also occurs.

This variety shows why W D D cannot be defined solely as loving or solely as wishing. Both uses have a history in Arabic. Someone can have wudd for a person, meaning affection for them, and can say wadidtu law…, meaning “I wished that…”.

The relationship between the two branches is intelligible: in both, a person inclines toward something and desires its realization, presence, or continuation. When this inclination concerns a person, the meaning approaches affection; when it concerns a situation or event, wanting and wishing become more natural renderings.

This explanation should not, however, become a definite historical claim that one branch necessarily arose from the other. Earlier evidence records both fields.

The semantic core can be approached as follows:

W D D: an inward inclination toward a desired person, thing, or situation; in relation to a person it approaches affection and mawaddah, and in relation to an event it approaches wanting and wishing it to occur.

There is no demonstrable original physical image for this root. Images such as pulling, clinging, or bodily proximity should therefore not be invented to connect affection with wishing.

The semantic field in early Arabic

Arabic lexical sources record both affection and wanting or wishing for W D D. Poetry transmitted from the pre-Islamic period also supplies a witness to the wish construction, although each witness’s date and attribution must be assessed separately.

A verse attributed to Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī says:

وَدِدْتُ وَبَيْتِ اللَّهِ لَوْ أَنَّ أَنْفَهُ
هَوَاءٌ فَمَا مَتَّ الْمُخَاطَ عَنِ الْعَظْمِ

Wadidtu wa-bayti llāhi law anna anfahu / hawāʾun fa-mā matta l-mukhāṭa ʿani l-ʿaẓm.

Translation of the first part: “By the house of The God, I wished that his nose were hollow…”

The construction وَدِدْتُ ... لَوْ, wadidtu … law, follows the same pattern used repeatedly in the Qur’an: someone wishes for the situation expressed after law.

Lexical sources also record wudd and mawaddah in the field of affection. This establishes the emotional branch’s place in the lexicon, but without identifying a particular text or inscription, one cannot cite “pre-Qur’anic Arabic writings” as independent evidence for it.

The name وَدّ, Wadd, was also known before the Qur’an as the name of a deity in parts of Arabia. It is attested in Ancient South Arabian inscriptions, including those of the Minaean setting. These inscriptions should not be called Arabic-language texts simply because they were found in Arabia. Their relevance here is to the history of the deity’s name, not directly to the word’s meaning in Qur’anic Arabic.

Three claims must nevertheless be distinguished: the existence of a root expressing affection, the existence of the proper name Wadd, and the historical reason for naming that deity. The resemblance of the name does not establish why the deity was called Wadd or precisely what its earliest worshippers understood by the name.

Wadda: from affection to wishing something to happen

In most Qur’anic verbal uses, wadda expresses wanting or wishing for a situation, rather than direct affection for a person.

Al-Baqarah says:

وَلَتَجِدَنَّهُمْ أَحْرَصَ النَّاسِ عَلَىٰ حَيَاةٍ وَمِنَ الَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا ۚ يَوَدُّ أَحَدُهُمْ لَوْ يُعَمَّرُ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ وَمَا هُوَ بِمُزَحْزِحِهِ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ أَنْ يُعَمَّرَ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بَصِيرٌ بِمَا يَعْمَلُونَ

Wa-la-tajidannahum aḥraṣa n-nāsi ʿalā ḥayātin wa-mina lladhīna ashrakū yawaddu aḥaduhum law yuʿammaru alfa sanatin wa-mā huwa bi-muzaḥziḥihi mina l-ʿadhābi an yuʿammara wa-llāhu baṣīrun bi-mā yaʿmalūn.

“You will certainly find them the most avid of people for life, even more than those who associate partners. Each of them wishes to be granted a thousand years of life; yet being granted a long life would not remove him from punishment. The God sees what they do.”

Al-Baqarah 2:96

يَوَدُّ ... لَوْ يُعَمَّرُ, yawaddu … law yuʿammaru, does not mean “he loves him.” He wants a situation to occur: that he should live for a thousand years.

The same construction appears elsewhere:

وَدَّ كَثِيرٌ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ لَوْ يَرُدُّونَكُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ إِيمَانِكُمْ كُفَّارًا

Wadda kathīrun min ahli l-kitābi law yaruddūnakum min baʿdi īmānikum kuffārā.

“Many of the People of the Book wished to turn you back to kufr after your faith.”

Al-Baqarah 2:109

And:

يَوْمَئِذٍ يَوَدُّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَعَصَوُا الرَّسُولَ لَوْ تُسَوَّىٰ بِهِمُ الْأَرْضُ

Yawmaʾidhin yawaddu lladhīna kafarū wa-ʿaṣawu r-rasūla law tusawwā bihimu l-arḍ.

“On that day, those who rejected and disobeyed the Messenger will wish that the earth were levelled over them.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:42

And:

يَوَدُّ الْمُجْرِمُ لَوْ يَفْتَدِي مِنْ عَذَابِ يَوْمِئِذٍ بِبَنِيهِ

Yawaddu l-mujrimu law yaftadī min ʿadhābi yawmiʾidhin bi-banīh.

“The guilty person wishes he could ransom himself from that day’s punishment with his children.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:11

In these uses, wadda expresses wanting a situation to occur. What is desired may be good or evil, possible or impossible, worldly or related to the Hereafter. The root therefore has no fixed moral value in itself. The intensity of the desire must be understood from context: wadda does not always express the intensity inherent in ḥirṣ.

Sometimes أَنَّ, anna, introduces what is desired:

وَتَوَدُّونَ أَنَّ غَيْرَ ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ تَكُونُ لَكُمْ

Wa-tawaddūna anna ghayra dhāti sh-shawkati takūnu lakum.

“You wanted the group without military strength to be yours.”

Al-Anfāl 8:7

Sometimes mā is a nominalizing particle: it makes the following clause function like a verbal noun expressing what is desired:

وَدُّوا مَا عَنِتُّمْ

Waddū mā ʿanittum.

“They wished for your suffering.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:118

Wadda in this branch is therefore more than a fanciful wish: it can express an actual desire for something to occur. In waddū mā ʿanittum, mā ʿanittum means “your suffering.” The Arabic thus has a nominalized clause, rather than an explicit noun such as “suffering” as its object.

Al-Mujādilah uses another form:

لَا تَجِدُ قَوْمًا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ يُوَادُّونَ مَنْ حَادَّ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ

Lā tajidu qawman yuʾminūna bi-llāhi wa-l-yawmi l-ākhiri yuwāddūna man ḥādda llāha wa-rasūlah.

“You will not find a people who believe in The God and the Last Day maintaining affection for those who oppose The God and His Messenger.”

Al-Mujādilah 58:22

يُوَادُّونَ, yuwāddūna, is a form III verb. It concerns an emotional relationship between people, rather than wishing an event to occur. Form III can foreground this relational aspect, but its pattern alone does not necessarily establish fully reciprocal affection.

Wudd and mawaddah: affection as a relationship

In the nominal branch, wudd and mawaddah more clearly enter the field of affection.

In Surah Maryam:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ سَيَجْعَلُ لَهُمُ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وُدًّا

Inna lladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū ṣ-ṣāliḥāti sa-yajʿalu lahumu r-raḥmānu wuddā.

“Those who believe and do righteous deeds—the Raḥmān will establish wudd for them.”

Maryam 19:96

The verse does not specify precisely in whose hearts this affection is established or that it flows in only one direction. A basic translation should therefore avoid determining more than the text does:

“The Raḥmān will establish affection for them.”

“Being loved among people,” “The God’s love for them,” and “believers’ love for one another” may be readings of the verse. Yet لَهُمُ ... وُدًّا, lahum … wuddan, does not by itself make any one of these instances certain.

Mawaddah generally appears in the Qur’an in relationships between people and groups. One of the clearest examples occurs in Al-Rūm:

وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ أَنْ خَلَقَ لَكُمْ مِنْ أَنْفُسِكُمْ أَزْوَاجًا لِتَسْكُنُوا إِلَيْهَا وَجَعَلَ بَيْنَكُمْ مَوَدَّةً وَرَحْمَةً ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

Wa-min āyātihi an khalaqa lakum min anfusikum azwājan li-taskunū ilayhā wa-jaʿala baynakum mawaddatan wa-raḥmatan inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yatafakkarūn.

“Among His signs is that He created spouses for you from among yourselves, so that you might find tranquility with them, and placed affection and mercy between you. In this are signs for people who reflect.”

Al-Rūm 30:21

بَيْنَكُمْ مَوَدَّةً, baynakum mawaddatan, presents affection as a relationship between parties. It does not follow that mawaddah lexically means marital love alone. The Qur’an uses the same word for other social relationships.

For example:

عَسَى اللَّهُ أَنْ يَجْعَلَ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَ الَّذِينَ عَادَيْتُمْ مِنْهُمْ مَوَدَّةً ۚ وَاللَّهُ قَدِيرٌ ۚ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

ʿAsā llāhu an yajʿala baynakum wa-bayna lladhīna ʿādaytum minhum mawaddatan wa-llāhu qadīrun wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm.

“Perhaps The God will place affection between you and those of them with whom you have been at enmity. The God is able, and The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:7

Here mawaddah stands opposite enmity and can arise between people who were previously hostile to each other. It is therefore more than a private feeling: it can also express the character of a social relationship.

Al-ʿAnkabūt also says:

وَقَالَ إِنَّمَا اتَّخَذْتُمْ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ أَوْثَانًا مَوَدَّةَ بَيْنِكُمْ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا ثُمَّ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ يَكْفُرُ بَعْضُكُمْ بِبَعْضٍ وَيَلْعَنُ بَعْضُكُمْ بَعْضًا وَمَأْوَاكُمُ النَّارُ وَمَا لَكُمْ مِنْ نَاصِرِينَ

Wa-qāla innamā ttakhadhtum min dūni llāhi awthānan mawaddata baynikum fī l-ḥayāti d-dunyā thumma yawma l-qiyāmati yakfuru baʿḍukum bi-baʿḍin wa-yalʿanu baʿḍukum baʿḍan wa-maʾwākumu n-nāru wa-mā lakum min nāṣirīn.

“He said: You have taken idols apart from The God for affection among yourselves in worldly life. Then, on the Day of Resurrection, you will disown one another and curse one another. Your abode will be the Fire, and you will have no helpers.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:25

Although more than one analysis of mawaddah’s precise syntactic role is possible, its connection with بَيْنِكُمْ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا, baynikum fī l-ḥayāti d-dunyā, is clear. Their shared system of idol worship in this world was accompanied by a kind of social bond; the continuation depicts that bond falling apart at the Resurrection.

Al-Shūrā 42:23 also says:

إِلَّا الْمَوَدَّةَ فِي الْقُرْبَىٰ

Illā l-mawaddata fī l-qurbā.

“Except affection concerning kinship.”

Mawaddah here belongs to the same field of affection and emotional attachment. The precise referent of الْقُرْبَىٰ, al-qurbā, kinship or close relationship, is a separate question. A particular later sectarian or historical identification should not be inserted into the meaning of mawaddah itself.

Likewise, مَوَدَّةً وَرَحْمَةً, mawaddatan wa-raḥmatan, in Al-Rūm 30:21 shows that affection and mercy are not the same word. Yet their coordination alone does not justify a formula such as “mawaddah must be love expressed in action, while raḥmah must be an inward feeling.” Their precise distinction requires examining the uses of both roots.

Wadūd: a divine description with and without al-

Wadūd occurs twice in the Qur’an as a direct description of The God.

In Shuʿayb’s words:

وَاسْتَغْفِرُوا رَبَّكُمْ ثُمَّ تُوبُوا إِلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّ رَبِّي رَحِيمٌ وَدُودٌ

Wa-staghfirū rabbakum thumma tūbū ilayhi inna rabbī raḥīmun wadūd.

“Ask your Lord for forgiveness, then turn back to Him. My Lord is merciful and wadūd.”

Hūd 11:90

And in Al-Burūj:

وَهُوَ الْغَفُورُ الْوَدُودُ

Wa-huwa l-ghafūru l-wadūd.

“He is the Forgiving, the Wadūd.”

Al-Burūj 85:14

These verses establish a clear linguistic point. The same description occurs once as:

وَدُودٌ

Wadūdun.

without al-, and once as:

الْوَدُودُ

Al-wadūdu.

with al-.

In both cases the description refers to The God. The presence of al- is therefore not a condition for a word to describe Him. Definiteness and indefiniteness relate to the sentence’s syntax and manner of expression; the presence or absence of al- alone should not be made the basis of a system classifying descriptions as divine or non-divine.

Wadūd follows the faʿūl pattern, which can convey intensity or an abiding quality. Morphology alone should not be made to yield more than this. In Arabic the form belongs to the field of affection, and both the active direction of loving and the possibility of being beloved have been proposed for it.

In the two Qur’anic occurrences, its association with raḥīm, merciful, and al-ghafūr, the Forgiving, makes “loving” or “abundantly loving” a natural reading.

In Hūd 11:90 particularly, the verse speaks of seeking forgiveness and returning to The God, then says:

إِنَّ رَبِّي رَحِيمٌ وَدُودٌ

Inna rabbī raḥīmun wadūd.

Here wadūd stands alongside mercy and within the Lord’s relationship with His servants. “Loving” or “abundantly loving” is therefore more natural in translation than “beloved,” although the word’s linguistic possibilities should not be restricted beyond what the context supports.

Wadd: the proper name of a deity

In Surah Nūḥ:

وَقَالُوا لَا تَذَرُنَّ آلِهَتَكُمْ وَلَا تَذَرُنَّ وَدًّا وَلَا سُوَاعًا وَلَا يَغُوثَ وَيَعُوقَ وَنَسْرًا

Wa-qālū lā tadharunna ālihatakum wa-lā tadharunna Waddan wa-lā Suwāʿan wa-lā Yaghūtha wa-Yaʿūqa wa-Nasrā.

“They said: Do not abandon your gods; do not abandon Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, or Nasr.”

Nūḥ 71:23

وَدّ, Wadd, is a proper name here, as are Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, and Nasr. It should therefore not be translated as “affection.”

The name Wadd was also known for a deity in Arabia before the Qur’an. This evidence establishes that the name had a history in Arabia’s religious environment.

It does not establish that the Qur’an intends a play on the meaning “affection,” or that the deity must have been named for the idea of friendship. Lexical relationship is one question; the historical reason for a name is another.

Nor does the existence of a deity with this name in historical Arabia by itself explain how the name was transmitted from Noah’s people, or establish the narrative’s precise chronology. Historical gaps should not be filled with unsupported hypotheses.

W D D (wudd), Ḥ B B (ḥubb), and M N Y (tamannī): affection, love, and wishing

Wudd and ḥubb overlap broadly in the field of love and can often both be explained as affection. The Qur’an’s uses do not, however, establish rules such as “ḥubb is only an inward feeling, while mawaddah must be love manifested in action.”

There is insufficient evidence for such an absolute boundary.

A notable feature of W D D is that its verb readily enters wish constructions:

يَوَدُّ ... لَوْ

Yawaddu … law.

And:

وَدُّوا لَوْ

Waddū law.

Here “having affection” is not a natural translation; the meaning approaches “wanting or wishing that something should happen.”

Ḥ B B (ḥubb) covers the broad Qur’anic field of love and affection, whereas wadidtu law… is a notable construction of W D D.

M N Y (tamannī) is relevant here in its branch concerning wishes. Tamannī in this use focuses on wishing; this does not define every branch of M N Y. The family W D D includes both affection for a person and wanting something to occur.

As tendencies, one may say:

Ḥubb focuses more on love and affection;

tamannī focuses more on wishing;

and wudd moves between the two fields: affection for a person or something loved, and wishing a desired situation to occur.

These distinctions are not absolute. Context determines whether W D D should be translated as affection, mawaddah, wanting, or wishing in a particular verse.

Translation proposals for Forqan

For وَدَّ, wadda, in constructions with law, an, or anna, “want” or “wish” is generally more natural than “love”:

يَوَدُّ أَحَدُهُمْ لَوْ يُعَمَّرُ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ

Yawaddu aḥaduhum law yuʿammaru alfa sanah.

“Each of them wishes to be granted a thousand years of life.”

وَدُّوا لَوْ تَكْفُرُونَ

Waddū law takfurūn.

“They wish that you would reject faith.”

In the construction with nominalizing mā:

وَدُّوا مَا عَنِتُّمْ

Waddū mā ʿanittum.

“They wished for your suffering.”

For يُوَادُّونَ, yuwāddūna, “maintain affection,” “show love,” or “sustain a relationship of affection” may be appropriate, depending on context.

For مَوَدَّة, mawaddah, the Arabic term may be retained in Forqan’s specialized discussion. In a general translation, “affection” or “a bond of affection” is more natural, depending on context.

For وُدّ, wudd, in Maryam 19:96:

سَيَجْعَلُ لَهُمُ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وُدًّا

Sa-yajʿalu lahumu r-raḥmānu wuddā.

“The Raḥmān will establish affection for them.”

For وَدُود, wadūd, as a description of The God, “loving” or “abundantly loving” can more naturally convey the word’s active direction in these contexts.

وَدّ, Wadd, in Nūḥ 71:23 is a proper name and should remain Wadd, rather than being translated as “affection.”

Conclusion

W D D expresses an inward inclination toward something desired in two close branches. When the inclination concerns a person or relationship, it approaches wudd, mawaddah, and affection. When it concerns a state or event occurring, the verb wadda takes the sense of wanting and wishing. Neither branch has a fixed moral value in itself; its object and context determine its direction.

Compared with nearby roots, ḥubb lies more within the field of love and affection, while tamannī in the use considered here expresses wishing. W D D retains both sides of this field within one linguistic family. This is why a single fixed English equivalent cannot serve all its uses. Mawaddah should not be restricted to marital affection or love expressed in action, and wadūd as a description of The God must be understood from context, rather than from the faʿūl pattern or the presence or absence of al- alone.

The principal caution is not to turn the branches’ semantic closeness into a certain history of derivation. Earlier evidence records both affection and wishing within this family, but does not establish which originated from the other. Moreover, Wadd in Nūḥ is a deity’s proper name. Its historical attestation can be studied, but the reason for the name and its precise relationship to “affection” are not certain without independent evidence.

A concise definition of the root can be stated as follows:

W D D: an inward inclination toward a desired person, thing, or situation. In relation to people, this approaches affection and mawaddah; in relation to something occurring, it approaches wanting and wishing it to happen. The object and sentence structure determine the precise sense.
