Laghw in early Arabic and the Qur’an: speech and sound, nonbinding oaths, disrupting the hearing of truth, turning away from futility, and wholesome speech in the Garden

The Arabic root L GH W relates to speaking, sound, things accorded no weight or benefit, and utterances lacking validity in their purpose or result. In established uses of this family, laghw may be speech uttered without serious intent or commitment, talk unworthy of attention and pursuit, or speech set aside because it is false, improper, or hurtful.

Not every use in this family means idle talk. Early Arabic uses «لَغْوُ الطَّائِرِ» for a bird’s call and «لَغِيَ بِالشَّيْءِ» for continually speaking about or occupying oneself with something. Lugha, “language,” is also discussed within this lexical family, without language itself meaning useless speech.

This distinction requires separating the act of speaking, the sound heard, and judgments about the speech’s validity or usefulness.

The root occurs eleven times in eleven Qur’anic verses: nine instances of the noun laghw, one imperative, «وَالْغَوْا», and one instance of «لَاغِيَة».

The Qur’an uses this family in several settings: an oath that does not establish a real commitment; speech and conduct from which believers turn away; talk and noise intended to prevent the Qur’an from being heard; and idle or hurtful speech whose presence is denied in descriptions of the Garden.

Despite their differences, these uses share a feature: something lacks the weight, value, or proper effect expected in its setting. Whether that arises from lack of intent, absence of benefit, falsehood and harm, or deliberate disruption must be determined from each verse’s context.

1. Forms of the Root and Its Semantic Centers

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

لَغَا، يَلْغُو: he spoke; in another use, he spoke idly or falsely.

لَغِيَ، يَلْغَى: he spoke or became attached to and occupied with something; in some uses, he persisted in doing or saying it.

لَغْو: something accorded no weight or attention, especially idle, ineffective, or insignificant speech.

لَغَا: a form close to laghw; in some uses, sound, speech, or talk without weight.

لَغْوَى: recorded for speech or a bird’s call, and as another form in the family of speaking and laghw.

لَاغٍ and لَاغِيَة: one who speaks idly, or speech or something characterized by laghw; the Qur’anic lāghiyah concerns idle speech.

لَوَاغِي: a plural of lāghiyah, recorded for sounds and utterances.

أَلْغَى، يُلْغِي: he invalidated something, excluded it from a count, or set it aside.

إِلْغَاء: invalidating, removing, or disregarding something in an account.

مُلْغًى: something whose validity has been removed or that has been excluded from an account.

مُلَاغَاة: in some uses, conversing or joking with another person.

لُغَة: a language, or a community’s manner of speaking.

Only three principal forms occur in the Qur’an: the noun laghw, an imperative of the basic verb, and lāghiyah on the pattern fāʿilah. The grammatical analysis of the last form is discussed below.

Forms such as ilghāʾ, alghā, and lugha are known in Arabic, but these words themselves do not occur in the Qur’an.

Two semantic branches

Two main strands can be distinguished for L GH W in early Arabic.

The first concerns something not counted or accorded weight.

The second concerns persistent engagement with something and speaking about it.

The first appears in speech without weight, nonbinding oaths, and things excluded from a count.

The second appears in expressions such as «لَغِيَ بِالْأَمْرِ»: he occupied himself with a matter and persisted in it.

This division matters because defining every derivative as “speaking idly” would fail to explain the uses concerning language and birdsong.

Conversely, reducing the entire family to “speaking” would erase the important feature of laghw in oaths and speech without weight.

The family thus has two related but distinct fields: speaking and sustained engagement with it; and something lacking the expected validity, benefit, or weight.

Is laghw always meaningless?

Speech with no intelligible meaning differs from speech lacking the necessary value or weight.

A sentence may be linguistically clear, yet uttered without deliberate intent or commitment.

A person may say something intelligible, but its falsehood, hurtfulness, or irrelevance makes it unworthy of pursuit.

A group may also create so much talk and noise that others cannot hear something important.

In all three cases, the speech can carry meaning, while being deficient in intent, validity, or function.

Laghw should therefore not be restricted to completely meaningless sounds.

Nor is every utterance without immediate practical benefit necessarily blameworthy laghw. Friendly conversation, respectful humor, and mental relaxation can have reasonable places in human life.

Whether speech counts as laghw in a given situation depends on its content, purpose, circumstances, and result.

2. L GH W in Early Arabic

Something not counted

One important early use of laghw concerns things given no separate weight in a count or transaction.

Lexical accounts mention young animals sold with their mothers without a separate price being assigned to them.

The young animal exists, but receives no separate valuation in the transaction.

Within this setting, laghw denotes something not included in the relevant count.

This use shows that laghw does not mean destruction or nonexistence.

Something may exist without being separately accounted for in a legal or economic relationship.

The distinction also matters for understanding a nonbinding oath: the oath has been uttered, yet may lack the binding force and accountability of a deliberately contracted oath.

A pre-Islamic witness: al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī

The pre-Islamic poet al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī says in a poem:

أَوْ مِائَةٍ تُجْعَلُ أَوْلَادُهَا لَغْوًا
وَعُرْضُ الْمِائَةِ الْجَلْمَدِ

aw miʾatin tujʿalu awlāduhā laghwan / wa-ʿurḍu al-miʾati al-jalmadi

Translation: “Or a hundred camels whose offspring are not separately counted, those hundred camels being capable of traversing hard, stony ground.”

Describing something costly, the poet speaks of a hundred camels whose offspring are not separately included in the reckoning.

Laghwan here does not refer to idle speech.

The subject is counting and valuation.

The young camels are not denied as real creatures; they simply have no independent place in the count of a hundred camels.

This is an important witness for the sense “something not counted,” illustrating a material and economic use in pre-Qur’anic Arabic.

It also helps explain why laghw al-yamīn does not necessarily mean that no oath words were spoken. The words may have been uttered without the conditions of a binding commitment being present.

However, the witness’s economic setting should not be generalized to all uses of laghw. The Qur’an also uses it where counting property or conducting a transaction is not at issue.

Sound and speaking

Another branch of the family concerns sound or speech.

Arabic uses «لَغْوُ الطَّائِرِ» for a bird’s call.

Here the sound is not necessarily described as ugly, forbidden, or worthless; the expression refers to the bird’s actual call.

Likewise, «لَغِيَ بِالشَّيْءِ» concerns persistent engagement with something. It is recorded for someone continually occupied with a subject or repeating it.

Some uses in the family also concern drinking a great deal without becoming satisfied.

This branch shows that persistence, repetition, and occupation with speaking or another activity were also recognized within the lexical family.

Such uses do not establish that every repetition or persistent activity is futile and blameworthy.

Repeating words for instruction, for example, may be useful, while repeating words to disturb others and prevent understanding serves a different function.

An early witness: Thaʿlabah ibn Ṣuʿayr

Thaʿlabah ibn Ṣuʿayr al-Māzinī says:

بَاكَرْتُهُمْ بِسِبَاءٍ جَوْنٍ ذَارِعٍ
قَبْلَ الصَّبَاحِ وَقَبْلَ لَغْوِ الطَّائِرِ

bākartuhum bi-sibāʾin jawnin dhāriʿin / qabla al-ṣabāḥi wa-qabla laghwi al-ṭāʾiri

Translation: “I came to them early, before daybreak and before the birds’ calls, with purchased wine in a large, dark vessel.”

In this verse, laghw al-ṭāʾir concerns a bird’s call.

The poet describes a time before the birds’ morning calls have begun.

The verse is preserved in early transmissions; some give mutraʿ instead of dhāriʿ.

Its significance is that it illustrates laghw in the field of sound, beyond the sense of false or useless speech.

This makes it inappropriate to restrict the entire family L GH W to sin.

From speaking to speech without weight

Arabic «لَغَا فِي الْقَوْلِ» is recorded for speaking and, in some uses, for improper or useless talk.

Al-laghā can also denote mixed sound, commotion, and talk whose disorder or lack of weight makes it unworthy of pursuit.

Different situations must be distinguished.

Sometimes speech lacks serious purpose, as with a habitual expression.

Sometimes it is deliberate, but false, improper, or useless in content.

In another situation, speech and sound are deliberately produced to disrupt hearing and understanding.

All can fall within the uses of laghw, but the reasons they lack weight differ.

Ilghāʾ: setting validity aside

Arabic «أَلْغَى الشَّيْءَ» means invalidating or setting something aside.

For example, «أَلْغَاهُ مِنَ الْعَدَدِ» means excluding it from a count.

This relates directly to the root’s first branch: something not counted.

Here, ilghāʾ names an action, while laghw can describe something lacking the relevant weight or validity.

However, the Form IV verb alghā does not occur in the Qur’an.

The verb «وَالْغَوْا» in Fuṣṣilat 41:26 does not belong to that form either. It is a plural imperative of the basic verb and must not be confused with «أَلْغَوْا», “they invalidated.”

The relationship of lugha to L GH W

Arabic lugha denotes a language or a community’s manner of speaking.

Lexical analyses connect the word with L GH W and uses involving speech and persistent speaking.

Some explanations propose lughwa as its historical form, subsequently changed during word formation.

The precise history of this development and its relationship to forms with wāw or yāʾ must nevertheless be treated cautiously.

What is clear is that lugha means language and should not be translated as idle speech.

In particular, the etymological proximity of lugha and laghw does not establish that human language was originally regarded as worthless or useless.

The Qur’an generally uses lisān for language, as in Al-Rūm 30:22, which speaks of differences among people’s languages.

The form lugha itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Laghw in Oaths: Words and Commitment

Two Qur’anic verses apply laghw directly to oaths.

This is an especially clear example of the difference between words uttered and a commitment actually established.

Al-Baqarah 2:225

لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ قُلُوبُكُمْ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ

lā yuʾākhidhukumu allāhu bi-al-laghwi fī aymānikum wa-lākin yuʾākhidhukum bi-mā kasabat qulūbukum wa-allāhu ghafūrun ḥalīm

Translation:

“The God does not hold you accountable for laghw in your oaths, but holds you accountable for what your hearts have earned; and The God is Forgiving, Forbearing.”

The verse distinguishes laghw in oaths from what hearts have earned.

Aymān here is the plural of yamīn in the sense of an oath.

The verse therefore concerns speech in the form of an oath whose intent and accountability differ from a serious oath.

In familiar Arabic usage, a person may say expressions such as «لَا وَاللَّهِ» or «بَلَى وَاللَّهِ» in ordinary conversation, without intending to establish a real commitment.

This example fits laghw in the verse: oath words have been uttered, but there is no serious intent to commit oneself.

The verse itself, however, does not name these two expressions. They illustrate familiar language use rather than provide the Qur’an’s exclusive definition of a nonbinding oath.

“What your hearts have earned”

This phrase shows that accountability is not confined to the outward form of words.

Here the heart is connected with earning and responsibility.

The distinction between laghw and an accountable oath thus relates to a person’s inward intent and commitment.

It does not follow that someone can knowingly and deliberately say something false, then escape accountability simply by later calling it laghw.

Lying, deceiving, and violating others’ rights are also separate matters whose rulings cannot be settled solely through the expression laghw al-yamīn.

Al-Māʾidah 5:89

لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا عَقَّدْتُمُ الْأَيْمَانَ فَكَفَّارَتُهُ إِطْعَامُ عَشَرَةِ مَسَاكِينَ مِنْ أَوْسَطِ مَا تُطْعِمُونَ أَهْلِيكُمْ أَوْ كِسْوَتُهُمْ أَوْ تَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ ثَلَاثَةِ أَيَّامٍ ذَٰلِكَ كَفَّارَةُ أَيْمَانِكُمْ إِذَا حَلَفْتُمْ وَاحْفَظُوا أَيْمَانَكُمْ كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ آيَاتِهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

lā yuʾākhidhukumu allāhu bi-al-laghwi fī aymānikum wa-lākin yuʾākhidhukum bi-mā ʿaqqadtumu al-aymāna fa-kaffāratuhu iṭʿāmu ʿasharati masākīna min awsaṭi mā tuṭʿimūna ahlīkum aw kiswatuhum aw taḥrīru raqabatin fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu thalāthati ayyāmin dhālika kaffāratu aymānikum idhā ḥalaftum wa-iḥfaẓū aymānakum kadhālika yubayyinu allāhu lakum āyātihi laʿallakum tashkurūn

Translation:

“The God does not hold you accountable for laghw in your oaths, but holds you accountable for the oaths you have firmly bound. Its expiation is feeding ten people in need from the average of what you feed your families, or clothing them, or freeing a slave; whoever cannot find the means is to observe ṣiyām for three days. That is the expiation of your oaths when you have sworn. Guard your oaths. Thus The God makes His signs clear to you, so that you may give thanks.”

Here, «عَقَّدْتُمُ الْأَيْمَانَ» stands opposite laghw.

ʿAqqadtum comes from ʿ Q D and concerns firmly binding and securing a commitment.

Study of ʿ Q D

This contrast clarifies laghw in oaths.

A laghw oath does not occupy the position of a firm, contracted oath in terms of intent and commitment.

By contrast, deliberately and seriously establishing an oath can create responsibility and consequences.

Is laghw al-yamīn only an unintended oath?

Several explanations of what counts as laghw al-yamīn appear in the history of interpretation.

One view concerns oath expressions uttered habitually, without intending to contract a commitment.

Another includes an oath about a fact that a person sincerely believes to be true, but that later proves mistaken.

The two situations differ in intent.

In the first, the person does not intend to establish a serious oath at all.

In the second, the person may intend an oath, but does not intend to lie and is mistaken about its subject.

The verses clearly distinguish freedom from accountability for laghw from responsibility for what the heart has earned or for firmly bound oaths.

However, identifying every possible instance of laghw al-yamīn requires closer examination of intent, mistake, sincerity, and commitment.

Direct translation is therefore better served by retaining “laghw in oaths,” with explanations of particular instances in the study or interpretation.

Does every laghw require expiation?

No. The verse distinguishes laghw from firmly bound oaths.

Expiation appears in the discussion of firmly bound oaths and concerns making amends for breaking an oath. An oath’s seriousness alone, without its being broken, does not by itself require expiation.

Mention of expiation must therefore not be taken to mean that every ordinary, unintended phrase with the outward form of an oath creates the same responsibility.

Nor should exemption from accountability for laghw be treated as permission for careless speech or playing with oaths.

The command «وَاحْفَظُوا أَيْمَانَكُمْ» links the closing part of this discussion to guarding and observing one’s oaths.

The Qur’an thus distinguishes words without commitment from serious oaths, while emphasizing responsibility for genuine commitments.

4. Laghw in Believers’ Conduct: Turning Away without Demeaning Others

Al-Muʾminūn 23:3

وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ عَنِ اللَّغْوِ مُعْرِضُونَ

wa-alladhīna hum ʿani al-laghwi muʿriḍūn

Translation:

“And those who turn away from laghw.”

This verse appears near the beginning of Al-Muʾminūn among the qualities of believers.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:2 mentions humility in ṣalāh; Al-Muʾminūn 23:4, zakāh; Al-Muʾminūn 23:5, chastity; and Al-Muʾminūn 23:8, observing trusts and covenants.

Avoiding laghw therefore appears alongside conduct that helps order individual and social life.

Muʿriḍūn comes from ʿ R Ḍ and means turning away.

Laghw is a noun here, linked to turning away through the preposition ʿan.

The Qur’an describes people who do not turn toward laghw or occupy themselves with it.

However, the verse does not say that believers never rest, joke, or enjoy recreation.

Its subject is laghw, rather than every activity that is not directly economic work, worship, or formal instruction.

If conversation or recreation contributes to rest, healthy relationships, or reasonable benefit, a specific reason is needed for calling it laghw.

Al-Furqān 25:72

وَالَّذِينَ لَا يَشْهَدُونَ الزُّورَ وَإِذَا مَرُّوا بِاللَّغْوِ مَرُّوا كِرَامًا

wa-alladhīna lā yashhadūna al-zūra wa-idhā marrū bi-al-laghwi marrū kirāmā

Translation:

“And those who do not bear false witness, and who, when they pass by laghw, pass with dignity.”

This verse describes the servants of the All-Merciful.

Its first part mentions zūr, which concerns falsehood and untruth.

Its second part describes their response to laghw.

The two words occur in one verse, but are not identical.

Zūr can denote speech marked by falsehood or untruth.

Laghw has a wider field and may denote useless or insignificant speech, or conduct unworthy of engagement.

«مَرُّوا كِرَامًا» is also significant. The Qur’an describes both their passing on and how they do so.

Passing with dignity can involve maintaining one’s own dignity and avoiding descent into improper speech.

The verse therefore does not authorize demeaning people, aggression, or violence against anyone whose speech we consider useless.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:55

وَإِذَا سَمِعُوا اللَّغْوَ أَعْرَضُوا عَنْهُ وَقَالُوا لَنَا أَعْمَالُنَا وَلَكُمْ أَعْمَالُكُمْ سَلَامٌ عَلَيْكُمْ لَا نَبْتَغِي الْجَاهِلِينَ

wa-idhā samiʿū al-laghwa aʿraḍū ʿanhu wa-qālū lanā aʿmālunā wa-lakum aʿmālukum salāmun ʿalaykum lā nabtaghī al-jāhilīn

Translation:

“When they hear laghw, they turn away from it and say: Our deeds are ours, and your deeds are yours. Peace be upon you; we do not seek the ignorant.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:52, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:53, and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:54 describe people who previously received the Book and declare their faith on hearing the Qur’an. Their perseverance, repelling evil with good, and giving are also mentioned.

Their conduct on hearing laghw is then described.

This context connects laghw with speech that would draw them into fruitless conflict or participation in ignorant behavior.

Their response is clear: they turn away, distinguish responsibility for deeds, and withdraw with a greeting of peace.

Turning away from laghw here does not mean being unable to give an informed answer or avoiding every conversation.

They have already declared their position on faith. When talk becomes laghw, however, they do not consider continued conflict necessary.

Their response illustrates the difference between dialogue aimed at understanding truth and fruitless dispute.

Does laghw in these verses mean music?

None of Al-Muʾminūn 23:3, Al-Furqān 25:72, or Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:55 names music, musical instruments, or singing as the definition of laghw.

Laghw can concern sound and speech in Arabic, but this does not establish that every melodic sound or every kind of music is inherently laghw.

Conversely, speech may have no melody and still be an instance of laghw because of falsehood, hurtfulness, or futility.

The criterion is not simply the presence or absence of melody.

If someone uses music to deceive, hurt, or deliberately prevent others’ speech from being heard, that conduct must be examined through the act itself and its consequences.

The possibility that an activity may count as laghw under certain conditions does not restrict the word’s definition to that activity.

Luqmān 31:6 also contains lahw al-ḥadīth, from L H W rather than L GH W. That verse must likewise be examined through its own wording and context.

Calling an activity laghw or relying on a personal judgment that it is useless is not, by itself, sufficient to declare it divinely forbidden.

5. Wa-lghaw fīhi: Creating Noise to Prevent the Qur’an from Being Heard

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَا تَسْمَعُوا لِهَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ وَالْغَوْا فِيهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَغْلِبُونَ

wa-qāla alladhīna kafarū lā tasmaʿū li-hādhā al-qurʾāni wa-lghaw fīhi laʿallakum taghlibūn

Translation:

“Those who disbelieved said: Do not listen to this Qur’an, and make a commotion during it, so that you may prevail.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:26

This is the only verbal occurrence of L GH W in the Qur’an.

«وَالْغَوْا» is a plural imperative of Form I. In the reading with fatḥah on ghayn, it corresponds to the imperfect yalghā; both laghiya yalghā and laghā yalghā are recorded in this branch.

It must therefore not be confused with alghaw of Form IV.

Here the command is to produce speech and noise that prevent the Qur’an from being heard and attended to.

The verb’s relationship with “do not listen”

Their statement begins:

لَا تَسْمَعُوا لِهَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ

lā tasmaʿū li-hādhā al-qurʾān

“Do not listen to this Qur’an.”

They then say:

وَالْغَوْا فِيهِ

wa-lghaw fīh

“And make a commotion during it.”

Finally they state their purpose:

لَعَلَّكُمْ تَغْلِبُونَ

laʿallakum taghlibūn

“So that you may prevail.”

This structure shows that the commotion is not accidental or purposeless.

The speakers seek to prevent the Qur’an from being heard and to overcome its message.

Laghw here thus becomes active disruption of hearing and understanding speech.

Does wa-lghaw mean invalidating the Qur’an?

Arabic alghā can mean invalidating something.

However, the verse’s verb is not Form IV, and its construction fits the production of disruptive speech and noise.

“Invalidate the Qur’an” is therefore not an appropriate direct equivalent of wa-lghaw fīhi.

They wanted to prevent others from attending to the Qur’an through speech, noise, and disruption.

Their purpose may have been to make the Qur’an’s message ineffective among people, but that purpose is inferred from context. The verb itself does not denote legally or logically invalidating the Qur’an.

The verse clearly shows that laghw can be entirely deliberate and purposeful.

It therefore cannot be defined as unintended speech in every verse.

In nonbinding oaths, the absence of a real commitment is prominent; in Fuṣṣilat 41:26, deliberate speech and commotion disrupt hearing.

These uses belong to one family but have different contextual features.

6. Laghw in Descriptions of the Garden: No Idle or Hurtful Speech

Several verses deny the presence of laghw in the life of the Garden.

These uses matter for understanding the word’s range because laghw appears alongside peace, sinful speech or conduct, and denial.

Maryam 19:62

لَا يَسْمَعُونَ فِيهَا لَغْوًا إِلَّا سَلَامًا وَلَهُمْ رِزْقُهُمْ فِيهَا بُكْرَةً وَعَشِيًّا

lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan illā salāman wa-lahum rizquhum fīhā bukratan wa-ʿashiyyā

Translation:

“They hear no idle speech there, but peace; and their provision there is morning and evening.”

Laghw stands in contrast to salām here.

Illā salāman can be understood as a disconnected exception: after denying laghw, the verse introduces peace and wholesome speech.

The point is therefore not that salām is a kind of laghw whose hearing is permitted.

The verse depicts a setting without idle or improper speech, where words of peace have their place.

Study of S L M

Al-Ṭūr 52:23

يَتَنَازَعُونَ فِيهَا كَأْسًا لَا لَغْوٌ فِيهَا وَلَا تَأْثِيمٌ

yatanāzaʿūna fīhā kaʾsan lā laghwun fīhā wa-lā taʾthīm

Translation:

“There they pass among themselves a cup involving neither laghw nor sinfulness.”

Laghw appears alongside taʾthīm here.

Taʾthīm comes from ʾ TH M and concerns sin, including attributing sin or involvement in it.

Their pairing shows that laghw and taʾthīm are not a single concept.

Speech may be idle without itself being a grave sin. Conversely, false or hurtful speech may be both without weight and sinful.

Both conditions are denied in this verse.

The pronoun in fīhā, within lā laghwun fīhā, may syntactically relate to the cup and the circumstances of drinking it. In any case, the verse denies laghw and taʾthīm in that scene.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:25 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:26

لَا يَسْمَعُونَ فِيهَا لَغْوًا وَلَا تَأْثِيمًا ۝ إِلَّا قِيلًا سَلَامًا سَلَامًا

lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan wa-lā taʾthīman / illā qīlan salāman salāmā

Translation:

“There they hear neither idle speech nor sinful talk, but words of peace, peace.”

These two verses place laghw and taʾthīm in contrast to words of peace.

The contrast shows that the point is more than absence of sound: the quality of what is heard matters.

The Qur’an describes the Garden’s life through speech free from futility and sinfulness.

This does not establish that the Garden has no conversation, emotional expression, or communication. The verse itself speaks of hearing words of peace.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:35

لَا يَسْمَعُونَ فِيهَا لَغْوًا وَلَا كِذَّابًا

lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan wa-lā kidhdhābā

Translation:

“There they hear neither idle speech nor denial.”

Here laghw appears alongside kidhdhāban.

That word concerns denial or declaring something false.

Once again, two distinct concepts appear in one construction.

Laghw can mean speech without weight or benefit; takdhīb concerns rejecting something and declaring it false.

Their pairing clarifies laghw: it is not necessarily identical with denial, although speech may have both features in certain circumstances.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:11

لَا تَسْمَعُ فِيهَا لَاغِيَةً

lā tasmaʿu fīhā lāghiyah

Translation:

“You hear no idle speech there.”

Here lāghiyatan appears in place of the noun laghw.

Lāghiyah follows the pattern fāʿilah. One analysis treats it as a feminine active participle describing speech or a speaking group; another treats it as a verbal noun meaning laghw.

A lexical explanation takes it as kalimatun lāghiyah: an utterance characterized by laghw.

The verse thus denies laghw there. The grammatical feminine form alone does not establish that the speaker’s sex is at issue.

The difference between this form and the noun laghw concerns the manner of expression.

Maryam 19:62 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:25 use the noun laghw; Al-Ghāshiyah 88:11 expresses this semantic field through lāghiyah.

The difference should not be turned into two entirely separate meanings.

What the uses concerning the Garden show

These verses place laghw alongside peace, taʾthīm, and denial.

Taken together, they suggest speech whose quality, weight, or usefulness does not suit the wholesome, peaceful setting of the Garden.

This description should not be made into an exhaustive list of all permitted and prohibited speech in worldly life.

The verses concern the quality of speech in the Garden. Extending each instance into a legal ruling for worldly life requires independent evidence.

7. Comparison with Related Roots

L GH W (laghw) and L H W (lahw)

Arabic lahw concerns diversion and an occupation that draws someone away from another matter.

Laghw concerns something lacking expected weight or benefit, or speech without validity because of its content, intent, or setting.

The two concepts can occur in one activity.

A conversation, for example, may be useless and distract someone from an important responsibility.

But not every diversion is laghw, nor is every laghw necessarily a diversion.

The deliberate commotion preventing the Qur’an from being heard in Fuṣṣilat 41:26 is laghw whose purpose is disruption and overcoming the hearing of the message, rather than entertainment.

Lahw and laghw should therefore not be treated as complete synonyms.

L GH W (laghw) and L ʿ B (laʿib)

Laʿib concerns playing.

Play can serve practice, learning, enjoyment, and rest, although some games may be useless or harmful.

Laghw may also occur in play or entertainment, but the root L GH W does not itself mean playing.

Calling a game laghw therefore requires identifying a particular feature, such as lack of weight, futility, or an improper consequence.

An activity’s being called play does not transform its lexical meaning into laghw.

L GH W (laghw) and ʿ B TH (ʿabath)

Established uses of ʿabath concern purposeless or fruitless activity, or conduct lacking seriousness and a worthy end.

Laghw can also lack benefit.

However, the Qur’an gives laghw particular uses involving speech and oaths that cannot all be replaced with ʿabath.

For example, an oath uttered without intent to commit oneself is laghw in its binding force, but need not be a deliberately purposeless act.

Likewise, the commotion in Fuṣṣilat 41:26 pursues a specific goal; from the speakers’ standpoint, the laghw is not aimless.

This shows that “idle” in translating laghw should not always be understood as entirely unintended and purposeless.

L GH W (laghw) and Z W R (zūr)

Qur’anic zūr concerns falsehood and untruth.

Al-Furqān 25:72 places zūr and laghw in one verse.

This pairing shows that the words can overlap without being identical.

Speech may be false and therefore lack validity.

Conversely, speech may not be false but still count as laghw because it is useless, hurtful, or inappropriate to the occasion.

Laghw should therefore not be defined solely as lying.

L GH W (laghw) and B Ṭ L (buṭlān)

Study of B Ṭ L

Bāṭil in its established uses concerns falsehood, invalidity, and absence of stability or rightness.

Laghw can likewise lack validity.

But in a nonbinding oath, the issue may be lack of intent to commit oneself, rather than falsehood in the sentence’s content.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:26, laghw also concerns disrupting hearing.

Bāṭil and laghw therefore overlap in some fields, but not all their uses mean the same thing.

L GH W (laghw) and L GH B (lughūb)

The roots have similar letters, but are entirely distinct.

L GH W (laghw) concerns speech, lack of weight, and idle talk.

L GH B (lughūb) concerns fatigue and exhaustion.

Qāf 50:38 states:

وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِنْ لُغُوبٍ

wa-mā massanā min lughūb

“And no weariness touched Us.”

This word belongs to L GH B, not L GH W.

Thus, laghw should not be translated as fatigue, or lughūb as futility.

8. Translation Proposals for Forqan

One Persian equivalent cannot cover every verse: oaths, deliberate disruption, and speech without weight are different uses.

In Al-Baqarah 2:225 and Al-Māʾidah 5:89, “laghw in oaths” or “nonbinding oaths” is appropriate. Explanation should clarify that these lack a real commitment or do not have the accountability of a contracted oath.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:3, “idle speech” or “futility” is possible depending on the translation’s intended scope. Given the noun’s general use, it should not be restricted without evidence to one kind of speech or recreation.

In Al-Furqān 25:72, “futility” or “idle speech” is appropriate. The false testimony in the verse’s first part should remain distinct from laghw in its second part.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:55, “idle speech” is appropriate because the verse itself concerns hearing and turning away from speech.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:26, wa-lghaw fīhi is better rendered “make a commotion during it” or “create disruption and noise during it.” The Form I verb concerns disruptive speech and noise, rather than legally or logically invalidating the Qur’an.

In Maryam 19:62, Al-Ṭūr 52:23, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:25, and Al-Nabaʾ 78:35, “idle speech” or “laghw” is suitable according to the sentence. Accompanying words such as peace, taʾthīm, and denial should be translated separately.

In Al-Ghāshiyah 88:11, “you hear no idle speech” naturally conveys lāghiyah as speech characterized by laghw.

Brief Root Meaning and Meanings in the Verses

Brief root meaning: speech or something disregarded in an account because it lacks intent, weight, or proper benefit; in another branch, speaking and sustained engagement with speech.

Meaning in Al-Baqarah 2:225 and Al-Māʾidah 5:89: laghw in an oath—words in oath form without the real commitment and binding force of a firmly contracted oath.

Meaning in Al-Muʾminūn 23:3: laghw and futility from which believers turn away.

Meaning in Al-Furqān 25:72: speech or conduct without weight that the servants of the All-Merciful pass by with dignity.

Meaning in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:55: idle speech from which listeners turn away without entering conflict with its speakers.

Meaning in Fuṣṣilat 41:26: noise and disruptive speech intended to prevent the Qur’an from being heard.

Meaning in Maryam 19:62, Al-Ṭūr 52:23, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:25, and Al-Nabaʾ 78:35: idle or insignificant speech denied in the description of life in the Garden.

Meaning of lāghiyatan in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:11: an utterance characterized by laghw; idle or improper speech not heard there.

9. Conclusion

L GH W has two established semantic fields in Arabic: speaking and engagement with speech; and something not accorded the expected weight or value.

Early Arabic witnesses illustrate both. Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī uses laghw for something without independent standing in a count; Thaʿlabah ibn Ṣuʿayr uses it for a bird’s call.

Laghw therefore cannot always be defined solely as sinful speech, meaningless sound, or activity without benefit.

The Qur’an uses the root in four prominent settings.

In oaths, laghw concerns the difference between words uttered and a real commitment. Al-Baqarah 2:225 emphasizes what hearts have earned; Al-Māʾidah 5:89 contrasts laghw with firmly bound oaths.

In believers’ conduct, turning away from laghw means avoiding entanglement in speech and actions without weight. It can involve dignity, a greeting of peace, and refusing fruitless dispute.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:26, laghw is deliberate noise intended to prevent the Qur’an from being heard. This shows that laghw is not always unintended: a person may knowingly pursue a particular goal through speech and sound that disrupt wholesome communication and make it ineffective.

Descriptions of the Garden also deny laghw alongside taʾthīm and denial, in contrast to words of peace. These uses emphasize the quality and weight of speech, rather than the absence of conversation itself.

Laghw in the Qur’an is therefore not the name of one particular kind of music, game, or entertainment. Different activities may count as laghw under certain conditions, but such a judgment requires evidence concerning the particular activity and setting.

Nor does calling an activity laghw automatically establish a divine prohibition. Legislating and declaring something forbidden require independent Qur’anic evidence.

Final definition: In one principal branch, L GH W denotes something disregarded or uncounted in terms of weight, intent, or benefit. In another, it concerns speaking, sound, and persistent engagement with speech. In the Qur’an, laghw may, according to context, denote an oath lacking a real commitment, idle or insignificant speech, or talk unworthy of attention and pursuit. The imperative wa-lghaw concerns creating disruptive noise to prevent the Qur’an from being heard. Each use’s precise sense emerges from the sentence, the speaker’s intent, and the speech’s relationship to truth, responsibility, and human dignity.
