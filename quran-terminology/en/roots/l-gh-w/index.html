Speech or action without weight or useful result; utterances that do not establish a binding commitment; idle talk and disruptive noise

The Arabic root L GH W forms a family of words concerned with speaking, producing sound, repeatedly engaging with something, and speech or action that carries no appreciable weight or validity.

Laghw is commonly rendered in Persian as “idle speech.” This is appropriate in many Qur’anic verses, but does not cover the entire semantic field. In Arabic, laghw is not confined to useless speech: it may denote something left out of an account, an utterance that establishes no binding commitment, or talk and noise deliberately used to disrupt hearing and understanding.

These differences are apparent in the Qur’an. Laghw in oaths concerns human intention and responsibility. Descriptions of believers and the servants of the Entirely Merciful speak of avoiding idle speech and conduct. In Fuṣṣilat, people are urged to make noise to prevent the Qur’an from being heard. Descriptions of the Garden speak of the absence of idle talk.

The uses of this family should therefore not all be equated with a single restricted concept such as lying, sin, entertainment, or meaningless speech.

The root occurs eleven times in the Qur’an: nine instances of the noun laghw, one of the verb wa-lghaw, and one of lāghiyah. These relatively few occurrences reveal several important distinctions within its meaning.

1. Semantic cores and early Arabic usage

Two semantic strands within one family

Two main strands can be identified for L GH W in early Arabic.

The first concerns something disregarded or excluded from the relevant count or assessment; the second concerns engaging in, or persisting in, speech or an activity.

This distinction matters: restricting the root to futility would leave some of its uses concerning sound and speech inadequately explained.

The Arabic verb laghā yalghū may refer to speaking, making an error in speech, or uttering idle talk. In some uses, laghiya likewise concerns speaking; in others, persistent engagement with something.

For example, laghiya bi-al-shayʾ describes someone occupied with or attached to something and repeatedly engaged with it.

Alghā al-shayʾ, however, concerns setting something aside or depriving it of validity.

These uses are related, but should not be turned into a certain historical derivation. Both the strand of speaking and that of lacking weight or validity are attested in Arabic.

Laghw: something not included in the count

One material and social use of this root occurs in transactions and livestock counting.

In early Arabic, when livestock were counted or a transaction valued, the young of some animals might be excluded as separate entries from the main count and called laghw.

This does not mean that laghw necessarily lacks physical existence or intrinsic worth. The animal exists and may have real value, but is not counted independently in a particular account or agreement.

A verse by the pre-Islamic poet al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī reads:

أَوْ مِائَةٍ تُجْعَلُ أَوْلَادُهَا
لَغْوًا وَعُرْضُ الْمِائَةِ الْجَلْمَدِ

aw miʾatin tujʿalu awlāduhā / laghwan wa-ʿurḍu al-miʾati al-jalmadi

Translation: Or a hundred camels whose young are not counted separately, those hundred being capable of traversing hard, stony ground.

Here laghwan refers to young camels not counted among the principal hundred. The use concerns accounting and valuation, rather than the animals themselves being objectionable or useless.

There are transmitted differences and grammatical explanations concerning miʾatin and the phrase ʿurḍu al-miʾati al-jalmadi. The meaning of laghw in the first half remains clear: something not included in the relevant count.

This witness is also useful for understanding laghw in an oath. An utterance lacking validity may concern its not counting within a particular obligation or relationship, rather than its having no meaning whatsoever.

Laghw: sound and speaking

Another strand of the root concerns producing sound and speaking.

The expression laghwu al-ṭāʾir is attested for a bird’s song or call.

The pre-Islamic poet Thaʿlabah ibn Ṣuʿayr al-Māzinī says:

بَاكَرْتُهُمْ بِسِبَاءٍ جَوْنٍ ذَارِعٍ
قَبْلَ الصَّبَاحِ وَقَبْلَ لَغْوِ الطَّائِرِ

bākartuhum bi-sibāʾin jawnin dhāriʿin / qabla al-ṣabāḥi wa-qabla laghwi al-ṭāʾiri

Translation: I came to them early with purchased wine in a large, dark vessel, before the light of morning and before the birds’ calls.

Laghwu al-ṭāʾir here refers to birds’ calls. The poet uses their calls to mark the time of the event.

This use shows that the lexical family was also associated with sound in Arabic. It does not imply that every sound is laghw or that all speaking is futile.

Early explanations attest laghā in some constructions with the meaning of speaking. Some lexical analyses also connect lughah, “language,” with this strand.

Language as the human system of communication and expression is nevertheless distinct from laghw as speech without weight or validity. Belonging to a lexical family does not establish that all human speech was originally futile.

Laghw and depriving something of validity

Arabic also uses alghā for excluding something from an account or rendering it invalid.

For example, alghāhu mina al-ʿadad means that he excluded it from the count.

Something may remain physically present while no longer counting in the relevant calculation or decision.

This distinction between existing and being counted is an important feature of the family.

However, wa-lghaw in Fuṣṣilat 41:26 is an imperative of the basic verb. It should not be confused with the imperative of alghā, “cancel” or “invalidate.” In that verse it concerns talking and making noise, rather than a formal command to annul something.

2. Laghw in oaths: speech and responsibility

Al-Baqarah 2:225: laghw and what hearts acquire

Al-Baqarah 2:225 states:

لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ قُلُوبُكُمْ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ

lā yuʾākhidhukumu allāhu bi-al-laghwi fī aymānikum wa-lākin yuʾākhidhukum bi-mā kasabat qulūbukum wa-allāhu ghafūrun ḥalīm

Translation: The God does not hold you accountable for laghw in your oaths, but holds you accountable for what your hearts have acquired; and The God is Forgiving, Forbearing.

Here laghw is associated with oaths.

The central contrast is between al-laghwi fī aymānikum, “laghw in your oaths,” and mā kasabat qulūbukum, “what your hearts have acquired.”

The Qur’an denies accountability for laghw in oaths while connecting responsibility with what a person’s heart has acquired.

An expression taking the form of an oath is therefore not necessarily equivalent in intention and responsibility to a deliberate, binding oath.

Someone may habitually use an oath expression without deciding to undertake a particular commitment. Someone may also swear about something believed to be true, only to discover later that it was mistaken.

Both examples are discussed in early explanations of laghwu al-yamīn, laghw in an oath.

The word laghw alone, however, does not determine every rule governing oaths. The criterion of responsibility must be understood from the complete verse and the other verses concerning oaths.

The preceding verse, Al-Baqarah 2:224, states that The God and oaths should not be made an obstacle to doing good, being mindful, and reconciling people. The discussion of oaths thus takes place within a setting of ethical responsibility and social conduct.

Al-Māʾidah 5:89: laghw and firmly contracted oaths

Al-Māʾidah 5:89 repeats the same opening words:

لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ

lā yuʾākhidhukumu allāhu bi-al-laghwi fī aymānikum

Translation: The God does not hold you accountable for laghw in your oaths.

Its continuation, however, differs from Al-Baqarah 2:225:

وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا عَقَّدْتُمُ الْأَيْمَانَ

wa-lākin yuʾākhidhukum bi-mā ʿaqqadtumu al-aymān

Translation: But He holds you accountable for the oaths you have firmly contracted.

ʿAqqadtum comes from ʿ Q D and concerns binding or firmly contracting an oath.

Study of ʿ Q D

This contrast is important: laghw in an oath is distinct from an oath validly undertaken as a commitment.

The remainder of the verse sets out expiation, the ways of performing it, and the command to safeguard oaths.

Together, these two verses distinguish between using the words of an oath and undertaking a commitment that entails responsibility. Words alone do not constitute the whole commitment: intention, the undertaking of the oath, and the circumstances of the action also matter.

This does not mean that every false statement made in The God’s name is merely laghw and exempt from accountability because it is false. Deliberate lying and an oath expression that establishes no commitment are different matters.

Nor do these verses authorize disregarding genuine commitments or treating deliberate oaths irresponsibly.

In this use, laghw denotes an utterance that lacks the relevant binding force as an oath. Determining all cases falling under that description requires a full examination of the related verses.

3. Laghw in human speech and conduct

Al-Muʾminūn 23:3: turning away from laghw

Al-Muʾminūn 23:3 states:

وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ عَنِ اللَّغْوِ مُعْرِضُونَ

wa-alladhīna hum ʿani al-laghwi muʿriḍūn

Translation: And those who turn away from laghw.

This verse describes one characteristic of believers.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:1 states, “قَدْ أَفْلَحَ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ,” “The believers have succeeded.” This is followed by humility in ṣalāh in Al-Muʾminūn 23:2, turning away from laghw in Al-Muʾminūn 23:3, performing zakāh in Al-Muʾminūn 23:4, guarding chastity in Al-Muʾminūn 23:5, observing trusts and commitments in Al-Muʾminūn 23:8, and safeguarding prayers in Al-Muʾminūn 23:9.

In this context, laghw stands in contrast to responsible, purposeful living.

The word itself nevertheless does not mean all entertainment or every conversation without a religious subject.

Friendly conversation, harmless joking, or a brief rest can be unobjectionable and even socially beneficial. Informality or the absence of a devotional subject does not by itself make speech laghw.

Conversely, very serious, lengthy, formal speech may prove fruitless in relation to truth, consequences, or responsibility, and fall within the field of laghw.

The value of speech cannot therefore be judged solely from whether its tone is serious.

Muʿriḍūn comes from ʿ R Ḍ and expresses turning away. It shows that believers neither engage in laghw nor turn toward it.

The verse does not say that every instance must be stopped by force or that everyone who speaks idly deserves punishment. The conduct commended here is turning away.

Al-Furqān 25:72: passing by laghw with dignity

Al-Furqān 25:72 states:

وَالَّذِينَ لَا يَشْهَدُونَ الزُّورَ وَإِذَا مَرُّوا بِاللَّغْوِ مَرُّوا كِرَامًا

wa-alladhīna lā yashhadūna al-zūra wa-idhā marrū bi-al-laghwi marrū kirāmā

Translation: And those who do not bear witness to falsehood, and who, when they pass by laghw, pass with dignity.

Al-zūr and al-laghw appear together, but are distinct words.

Zūr concerns falsehood, untruth, or distortion of reality; laghw covers a broader field of speech or action without weight or useful result.

A lie may also be laghw, but not every instance of laghw is a lie. A statement may be true yet misplaced, fruitless, or without appreciable value in a particular situation.

Marrū kirāman, “they pass with dignity,” also matters. The verse describes not only leaving laghw, but how to respond to it.

A person passing by laghw need not descend to the same level of speech or conduct. One can retain dignity without joining in futile behavior.

This does not mean remaining silent before every injustice, harmful lie, or infringement of others’ rights. Laghw must be distinguished from the responsibility to prevent injustice or defend what is right.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:55: hearing laghw and responding peacefully

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:55 states:

وَإِذَا سَمِعُوا اللَّغْوَ أَعْرَضُوا عَنْهُ وَقَالُوا لَنَا أَعْمَالُنَا وَلَكُمْ أَعْمَالُكُمْ سَلَامٌ عَلَيْكُمْ لَا نَبْتَغِي الْجَاهِلِينَ

wa-idhā samiʿū al-laghwa aʿraḍū ʿanhu wa-qālū lanā aʿmālunā wa-lakum aʿmālukum salāmun ʿalaykum lā nabtaghī al-jāhilīn

Translation: When they hear laghw, they turn away from it and say: “Our deeds are ours and your deeds are yours. Peace be upon you; we do not seek the ignorant.”

The verse concerns a group whose receipt of scripture, faith, patience, repelling evil with good, and giving are mentioned in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:52, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:53, and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:54.

Their response has several stages: they hear laghw, turn away, and do not turn their own reply into a quarrel resembling that idle speech.

Hearing laghw is distinct from participating in it. The verse does not place a listener on the same footing as its speaker merely because the words reach that listener’s ears.

The reply salāmun ʿalaykum, “peace be upon you,” also shows that withdrawal can be accompanied by peaceful conduct.

Together with Al-Furqān 25:72, this passage shows attention to the quality of one’s response and the preservation of dignity, as well as the condemnation of laghw.

4. Wa-lghaw fīhi: making noise to prevent the Qur’an from being heard

Fuṣṣilat 41:26 states:

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَا تَسْمَعُوا لِهَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ وَالْغَوْا فِيهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَغْلِبُونَ

wa-qāla alladhīna kafarū lā tasmaʿū li-hādhā al-qurʾāni wa-lghaw fīhi laʿallakum taghlibūn

Translation: Those who disbelieved said: “Do not listen to this Qur’an, and make a commotion during it, so that you may prevail.”

Wa-lghaw is a plural imperative of Form I. In the reading with a fatḥah on ghayn, it corresponds to the imperfect yalghā; both laghiya yalghā and laghā yalghā are attested in this strand. It must not be confused with the Form IV imperative of alghā.

This is not cancellation of a document or annulment of a ruling. The verse concerns behavior during the presentation of the Qur’an intended to disrupt its being heard.

The meaning emerges from three connected phrases:

Lā tasmaʿū: do not listen.

Wa-lghaw fīhi: introduce disruptive talk and noise during it.

Laʿallakum taghlibūn: so that you may prevail.

Here laghw is deliberate and purposeful behavior. Unlike laghw in oaths, which concerns the absence of a binding commitment, a group knowingly seeks to divert listeners’ attention from the Qur’an.

Absence of intention cannot therefore be required for every form of the root.

This verse is an important example of deliberate laghw: a person or group may use noise, scattered talk, or commotion to deprive others of the opportunity to hear and assess a message.

Such conduct should not be equated with scholarly criticism, critical questioning, or reasoned disagreement.

Questioning and criticism can contribute to understanding the truth, whereas the behavior described here prevents hearing and seeks to overpower the conversation.

The distinction between disagreement and deliberate obstruction of understanding is important here.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:26, the root thus describes an active behavior: producing talk or noise that, in context, is used to frustrate listening to the Qur’an.

5. The absence of laghw in the Garden

Five verses describe the absence of laghw in the afterlife of the Garden’s inhabitants. These uses occupy a closely related semantic field, although their accompanying words differ.

Maryam 19:62: laghw and salām

لَا يَسْمَعُونَ فِيهَا لَغْوًا إِلَّا سَلَامًا وَلَهُمْ رِزْقُهُمْ فِيهَا بُكْرَةً وَعَشِيًّا

lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan illā salāman wa-lahum rizquhum fīhā bukratan wa-ʿashiyyā

Translation: They hear no idle speech there, only peace; and their provision is there for them morning and evening.

Laghw and salām occur here in an exception construction.

Illā salāman does not mean that peace is a kind of idle talk. The exception can be understood as discontinuous: idle talk is not heard; rather, what is heard is peace.

The contrast is clear in either case: idle speech has no place in that setting, while salām is present.

Salām also requires examination within its own root and usage. Reducing it to a greeting formula does not cover its entire semantic field.

Al-Ṭūr 52:23: laghw and taʾthīm

يَتَنَازَعُونَ فِيهَا كَأْسًا لَا لَغْوٌ فِيهَا وَلَا تَأْثِيمٌ

yatanāzaʿūna fīhā kaʾsan lā laghwun fīhā wa-lā taʾthīm

Translation: There they pass a cup among themselves, with neither idle talk nor sin arising from it.

Laghw is accompanied here by taʾthīm.

The words are distinct. Laghw concerns speech and conduct without weight or useful purpose, while taʾthīm concerns attributing sin or becoming involved in sinful conduct.

The verse describes enjoyment and drinking without laghw or taʾthīm.

The word laghw alone does not establish that every idle remark is necessarily sinful. The two words’ juxtaposition can also reveal their distinction.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:25: the denial of laghw and taʾthīm

لَا يَسْمَعُونَ فِيهَا لَغْوًا وَلَا تَأْثِيمًا

lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan wa-lā taʾthīmā

Translation: They hear neither idle talk nor taʾthīm there.

This closely resembles Al-Ṭūr 52:23, but here the statement directly concerns hearing.

The next verse, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:26, states:

إِلَّا قِيلًا سَلَامًا سَلَامًا

illā qīlan salāman salāmā

Translation: Only the saying: “Peace, peace.”

Together, the verses contrast the absence of idle speech with the presence of peaceful words.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:35: laghw and lying

لَا يَسْمَعُونَ فِيهَا لَغْوًا وَلَا كِذَّابًا

lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan wa-lā kidhdhābā

Translation: They hear neither idle talk nor lying there.

Here laghw is accompanied by kidhdhāban.

Kidhb concerns untruth and lying, whereas laghw is not necessarily restricted to false content.

A statement may be factually true yet count as laghw in a particular situation. Conversely, a lie may be carefully planned and purposeful from the speaker’s perspective, while remaining wrongful and harmful.

The concepts thus overlap, but are not fully synonymous.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:11: lāghiyah

لَا تَسْمَعُ فِيهَا لَاغِيَةً

lā tasmaʿu fīhā lāghiyah

Translation: You hear no idle speech there.

Lāghiyah has the pattern fāʿilah. In one analysis it is the feminine active participle of lāghin, describing a word or utterance; in another it is a verbal noun meaning laghw. Its form should therefore not be restricted to a single morphological analysis.

Lexical explanations understand it as a word or utterance containing laghw. Some explain lāghiyah as idle or objectionable speech itself.

It need not mean a woman who speaks idly, or any female person. The feminine form can be explained through an understood “idle word” or the word’s nominal use.

The verse describes a lofty Garden in which no idle speech is heard.

Together, these five verses present the absence of laghw as a feature of the Garden’s afterlife setting.

They do not imply that the Garden lacks friendly speech, joy, free conversation, or social interaction. These same descriptions include peace, speech, and enjoyment.

What is excluded is laghw, rather than speaking itself or enjoying others’ company.

6. Comparison with related roots

L GH W (laghw) and L H W (lahw): futility and diverted attention

Arabic lahw concerns an entertainment or occupation that draws attention and may divert it from something else.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:9 states:

لَا تُلْهِكُمْ أَمْوَالُكُمْ وَلَا أَوْلَادُكُمْ عَنْ ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ

lā tulhikum amwālukum wa-lā awlādukum ʿan dhikri allāh

Translation: Let neither your possessions nor your children distract you from remembrance of The God.

The point is not simply the supposed uselessness of possessions and children, but their capacity to divert someone from another responsibility.

Laghw more particularly denotes speech or action without appreciable weight, result, or value in the relevant situation.

Not every instance of lahw is therefore laghw, and not every instance of laghw is entertaining.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:26, laghw is used to disrupt hearing the Qur’an, rather than for recreation.

L GH W (laghw) and L ʿ B (laʿiba): idle conduct and play

Laʿib concerns play and activities with their own structure and purpose, which may lack the seriousness or practical outcome of other affairs.

Play can serve educational, social, and physical functions in human life, particularly for children.

Laʿib should therefore not be equated with laghw or sin in every use.

Some Qur’anic descriptions of play and diversion criticize people who strip life or religion of responsibility and truth. Such contextual criticism does not make all play laghw.

L GH W (laghw) and B Ṭ L (baṭala): lacking validity and falsehood

Study of B Ṭ L

Bāṭil may denote something opposed to truth or lacking the necessary validity and soundness.

In some uses, laghw likewise denotes something invalid or left out of an account.

In the Qur’an, however, laghw may be speech that creates no binding commitment or deserves no attention. Bāṭil covers a broader field concerning truth, soundness, and validity.

For example, laghw in oaths in Al-Baqarah 2:225 does not necessarily mean a false statement. A person may have used the words of an oath without intending a commitment.

Laghw and bāṭil should therefore not be treated as synonymous in every situation.

L GH W (laghw), Z W R (zūr), and K DH B (kadhib)

Study of K DH B

Zūr concerns distortion and untruth; kadhib concerns lying and speaking contrary to fact.

Zūr and laghw occur together in Al-Furqān 25:72; laghw and lying are also juxtaposed in Al-Nabaʾ 78:35.

These combinations show the use of different words for different kinds of objectionable speech and conduct.

Laghw may include lying, but is not restricted to it. Nor does every fruitless statement constitute false testimony.

Without contextual evidence, “lying,” “slander,” or “sin” should not replace the meaning of laghw in translation.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

A single fixed equivalent does not suit all Qur’anic uses of this root.

In Al-Baqarah 2:225 and Al-Māʾidah 5:89, the main translation may retain “laghw in oaths.” “An oath expression without binding commitment” or “an oath upon which no valid commitment has been established” can explain the contextual sense; its precise application requires examining both verses.

For Al-Muʾminūn 23:3, “those who turn away from idle speech and conduct” can convey the semantic range. “Those who turn away from laghw” also preserves the original term, provided its meaning is explained.

For Al-Furqān 25:72, “when they pass by laghw, they pass with dignity” preserves the Arabic construction. “Idle speech or conduct” is also possible for readers unfamiliar with laghw.

For Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:55, “when they hear idle speech” is suitable, since hearing is explicit and supports a verbal sense.

For Fuṣṣilat 41:26, “make a commotion” or “disrupt it with talk and noise” better conveys wa-lghaw fīhi. “Cancel the Qur’an” is unsuitable: it suggests formal annulment or invalidating a text, whereas the passage concerns obstructing listening.

In Maryam 19:62, Al-Ṭūr 52:23, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:25, and Al-Nabaʾ 78:35, “idle speech” is clear and natural. Accompanying terms such as salām, taʾthīm, and lying should be translated separately.

For Al-Ghāshiyah 88:11, “you hear no idle speech there” is suitable. The feminine form lāghiyah does not by itself indicate a female person.

8. Conclusion

L GH W has two recognized semantic fields in Arabic. One concerns something not counted in the relevant assessment, account, or outcome; the other concerns speaking, producing sound, and engagement with something.

The Qur’an mostly uses this family for idle speech or conduct, with each verse’s context determining its particular sense.

In oaths, laghw concerns the absence of a binding commitment and responsibility arising from intention. Descriptions of believers commend turning away from fruitless speech and conduct. In Fuṣṣilat 41:26, it becomes deliberate noise intended to prevent the Qur’an from being heard. Descriptions of the Garden place the absence of idle talk alongside peace and the absence of taʾthīm and lying.

Laghw is neither always intentional nor always unintentional; neither necessarily a lie nor necessarily a sin. Informal speech and entertainment do not automatically fall under it.

Final definition: In its main nominal strand, L GH W concerns speech, action, or something disregarded in the relevant assessment, account, or outcome, or lacking appreciable result. Its verbal strand also concerns speaking and producing sound, and may express idle speech or disruptive noise according to context.

To understand a particular use, ask what speech or action is being called laghw, in what respect it lacks validity or result, and whether the issue is an utterance without commitment, fruitless behavior, or deliberate disruption.

The answers emerge from the sentence and the verse’s context, rather than imposing a narrow meaning on every derivative of the root.
