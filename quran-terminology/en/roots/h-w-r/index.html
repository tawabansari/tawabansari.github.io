Return, conversation, rotation, and qualities of color: a family with several semantic branches

The Arabic root «ح و ر» has several semantic branches, most notably return, reciprocal conversation, rotation, and qualities of color. Ḥiwār, muḥāwarah, ḥawārī, and ḥūr belong to this family, but do not all mean the same thing.

In conversation, speech passes back and forth; yaḥūra concerns return; one established feature of ḥūr is the marked contrast between the dark and white parts of the eyes; and the Qur’anic ḥawāriyyūn are a group of Jesus’s companions who identify themselves as helpers of The God.

These differences show that one narrow meaning cannot be imposed on every derivative. Each branch must be understood through its own linguistic evidence and Qur’anic usage.

1. Forms and semantic fields

The root «ح و ر» occurs in the Qur’an in five lexical groups:

  • يَحُورَ (yaḥūra): return, in Al-Inshiqāq 84:14.
  • يُحَاوِرُهُ (yuḥāwiruhu): converse with him, in Al-Kahf 18:34 and Al-Kahf 18:37.
  • تَحَاوُرَكُمَا (taḥāwurakumā): the two of you conversing, in Al-Mujādilah 58:1.
  • الْحَوَارِيُّونَ / الْحَوَارِيِّينَ (al-ḥawāriyyūn / al-ḥawāriyyīn): the disciples or Jesus’s close helpers, in Āl ʿImrān 3:52, Al-Māʾidah 5:111, Al-Māʾidah 5:112, and Al-Ṣaff 61:14.
  • حُور (ḥūr): describing companions in Paradise, in Al-Dukhān 44:54, Al-Ṭūr 52:20, Al-Raḥmān 55:72, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:22.

These five groups total thirteen Qur’anic occurrences. Al-ḥawāriyyūn occurs twice in Al-Ṣaff 61:14.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: thirteen occurrences in five groups

Other Arabic forms include ḥāra, to return; ḥiwār and muḥāwarah, conversation and reciprocal conversation; ḥawar, a quality of eye color; aḥwar and ḥawrāʾ, masculine and feminine descriptions of someone with that quality; ḥawwara, used for whitening or turning; and miḥwar, an axle or support around which something turns.

Arabic also records ḥuwār for a young camel. It does not occur in the Qur’an, and its semantic connection with the other branches is less clear than the main branches’ connections.

Do all the meanings go back to one core?

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies three separate principles: color, return, and rotation.

This division matters. Treating return as the sole basic meaning might lead to forcing eye color, ḥūr, and the disciples into explanations derived from return.

Conversely, treating whiteness as the only original meaning makes muḥāwarah and yaḥūru difficult to explain.

Lexical evidence establishes a clear connection between return and reciprocal conversation, since speech and responses pass between the participants. Rotation is also close to this field as a kind of movement.

Color and whiteness, however, are an established, separate branch within the same lexical material. Sharing three root letters does not demonstrate that color developed from circular movement or return.

Ḥ W R should therefore be understood as a family with several related or shared lexical branches, rather than a word whose every form must have one common definition.

2. Early Arabic usage

Return and a change of state

Early Arabic used ḥāra yaḥūru for returning from or to something. The same verb could also express a transition from one state to another.

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah reads:

وَمَا الْمَرْءُ إِلَّا كَالشِّهَابِ وَضَوْئِهِ
يَحُورُ رَمَادًا بَعْدَ إِذْ هُوَ سَاطِعُ

wa-mā al-marʾu illā ka-l-shihābi wa-ḍawʾihi / yaḥūru ramādan baʿda idh huwa sāṭiʿu

Translation:

“A person is but like a flame and its light, turning to ashes after blazing brightly.”

Yaḥūru here concerns a change from flame to ashes. The use is connected with return and change of state, rather than a spatial return to the starting point.

This witness is also recorded in Labīd’s collected poems. He lived in both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating this particular poem, it should not be presented as securely composed before the Qur’an’s revelation.

Gharīb al-Qurʾān in Arabic poetry: Labīd’s witness and its collected-poetry reference

The early expression al-ḥawr baʿda al-kawr also describes retreat from a better state to a worse one, or decrease after increase. Decrease is nevertheless not the meaning of every use; it is one possible outcome of return or change.

Conversation and returning a response

Ḥāwarahu muḥāwaratan means conversing with someone, while ḥiwār likewise denotes reciprocal speech.

Kallamtuhu fa-mā aḥāra ilayya jawāban means “I spoke to him, but he returned no answer to me.”

This makes the connection with return clear: one person speaks, and the other returns a response. Conversation takes shape through that exchange.

Muḥāwarah is not merely repeating the other person’s words. A response may affirm, question, disagree, explain, or criticize.

Arabic dictionaries record these expressions. Their appearance in classical sources establishes recognized Arabic meanings, but does not independently date the currency of every form to the pre-Islamic period.

Color, whiteness, and contrasting eye color

Another branch concerns ḥawar. A familiar lexical explanation describes intensely white parts of the eyes alongside intensely dark parts.

Aḥwar is masculine, ḥawrāʾ feminine, and ḥūr can be the plural of either form.

Lexicographers differed over the details. Some emphasized the contrast between black and white; some associated it primarily with the dark eyes of animals such as gazelles and wild cattle; others included whiteness and brightness of color in other uses of the family.

Ḥūr should therefore not be reduced without evidence to white skin. In descriptions of eyes, contrast or the quality of eye color is an established element of meaning.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and other lexica: differing explanations of eye color

This usage also occurs in early Arabic poetry.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd, a pre-Islamic poet, describes beautiful women:

هَيَّجَ الدَّاءَ فِي فُؤَادِكَ حُورٌ
نَاعِمَاتٌ بِجَانِبِ الْمِلْطَاطِ

hayyaja al-dāʾa fī fuʾādika ḥūrun / nāʿimātun bi-jānibi al-milṭāṭi

Translation:

“Tender ḥūr beside Al-Milṭāṭ stirred the pain within your heart.”

The poet goes on to describe them as agreeable in speech and adorned. Ḥūr here describes women, rather than naming beings that belong exclusively to the afterlife.

Salāmah ibn Jandal, another pre-Islamic poet, says:

وَعِنْدَنَا قَيْنَةٌ بَيْضَاءُ نَاعِمَةٌ
مِثْلُ الْمَهَاةِ مِنَ الْحُورِ الْخَرَاعِيبِ

wa-ʿindanā qaynatun bayḍāʾu nāʿimatun / mithlu al-mahāti mina al-ḥūri al-kharāʿībi

Translation:

“With us is a fair, tender singing woman, one of the tall, graceful ḥūr, like a mahāh.”

Mahāh is recorded for a wild cow and also for crystal or a pearl. Translating it definitively as gazelle is therefore imprecise. Ibn Manẓūr connects a comparison in whiteness with crystal or a pearl, and a comparison in the eyes with the wild cow. Here, ḥūr describes women; the point of the comparison must be assessed through context and the other descriptions. Al-ḥūr al-kharāʿīb should not be treated as describing a herd of gazelles.

Lisān al-ʿArab, M H W: wild cow, crystal, pearl, and the point of comparison

These two witnesses show ḥūr in pre-Islamic poetic descriptions of human beauty. Its appearance among descriptions of the afterlife therefore does not mean the Qur’an coined an entirely new, unprecedented expression.

The edited Al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt: Salāmah ibn Jandal’s witness and mina al-ḥūri al-kharāʿībi

Knowing the word’s worldly use does not, however, independently determine the actual nature of its referent in the afterlife.

Whiteness and purity

Arabic records ḥawwara al-thawba for whitening a garment and al-ḥuwwārā for refined white flour.

Ḥawārī has also been connected with purity and whiteness through this field. That is only one lexical explanation of the disciples’ title, however, and requires separate examination in their section.

3. Return and conversation in the Qur’an

Yaḥūra: return in Al-Inshiqāq 84:14

إِنَّهُ ظَنَّ أَنْ لَنْ يَحُورَ

innahu ẓanna an lan yaḥūra

Proposed translation:

“He thought that he would never return.”

Al-Inshiqāq 84:14

Yaḥūra here is from ḥāra yaḥūru, to return.

The preceding verses describe someone receiving his record from behind his back, calling for his destruction, and entering fire. He had previously been happy among his family and supposed there would be no return (Al-Inshiqāq 84:10, Al-Inshiqāq 84:11, Al-Inshiqāq 84:12, Al-Inshiqāq 84:13, and Al-Inshiqāq 84:14).

The following verse rejects that supposition and states that his Lord had always seen him (Al-Inshiqāq 84:15).

In context, lan yaḥūra therefore concerns denying return after worldly life, a return connected with human accountability.

The verb itself means returning. Resurrection after death and answering before The God come from the surrounding verses, rather than ḥāra alone meaning resurrection or coming to life.

An lan yaḥūra also places the denial of return within that person’s supposition. It is not a statement establishing that return will not occur; it is his mistaken belief, rejected in the following verse.

Yuḥāwiruhu: the garden owner’s conversation with his companion

وَكَانَ لَهُ ثَمَرٌ فَقَالَ لِصَاحِبِهِ وَهُوَ يُحَاوِرُهُ أَنَا أَكْثَرُ مِنْكَ مَالًا وَأَعَزُّ نَفَرًا

wa-kāna lahu thamarun fa-qāla li-ṣāḥibihi wa-huwa yuḥāwiruhu anā aktharu minka mālan wa-aʿazzu nafaran

Proposed translation:

“He had fruit and produce. While conversing with his companion, he said to him, ‘I have more wealth than you and greater strength in numbers.’”

Al-Kahf 18:34

Yuḥāwiruhu here concerns reciprocal conversation.

Someone with greater wealth and resources boasts to his companion about his wealth and the number of people supporting him.

Muḥāwarah here is not necessarily calm, fair, or a search for truth. The speaker uses his greater economic and social position to boast.

A few verses later, the same verb appears again:

قَالَ لَهُ صَاحِبُهُ وَهُوَ يُحَاوِرُهُ أَكَفَرْتَ بِالَّذِي خَلَقَكَ مِنْ تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ نُطْفَةٍ ثُمَّ سَوَّاكَ رَجُلًا

qāla lahu ṣāḥibuhu wa-huwa yuḥāwiruhu a-kafarta bi-alladhī khalaqaka min turābin thumma min nuṭfatin thumma sawwāka rajulan

Proposed translation:

“His companion, while conversing with him, said, ‘Do you disbelieve in the One who created you from dust, then from a drop, and then fashioned you as a man?’”

Al-Kahf 18:37

The other participant now speaks, questioning his companion’s belief and conduct.

In both verses, yuḥāwiruhu identifies a conversational relationship. The content differs: one boasts of wealth and power, while the other questions him by recalling human creation and dependence on The God.

Muḥāwarah therefore does not lexically mean agreement, reconciliation, or mutual respect. Conversation can contain boasting, disagreement in belief, difficult questions, or frank criticism.

What makes it muḥāwarah is the relationship of speech and response.

Taḥāwurakumā: a conversation heard by The God

قَدْ سَمِعَ اللَّهُ قَوْلَ الَّتِي تُجَادِلُكَ فِي زَوْجِهَا وَتَشْتَكِي إِلَى اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ يَسْمَعُ تَحَاوُرَكُمَا إِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ بَصِيرٌ

qad samiʿa allāhu qawla allatī tujādiluka fī zawjihā wa-tashtakī ilā allāhi wa-allāhu yasmaʿu taḥāwurakumā inna allāha samīʿun baṣīrun

Proposed translation:

“The God has heard the speech of the woman who disputes with you about her husband and complains to The God. The God hears the conversation between you two. The God is Hearing, Seeing.”

Al-Mujādilah 58:1

Here, the verbal noun taḥāwur takes the pronoun kumā: the conversation between the two of you.

The verse begins with a woman disputing with its addressee about her husband and bringing her complaint to The God. It then reports The God’s hearing of their exchange.

Tujādiluka and taḥāwurakumā appear in the same verse, but do not mean precisely the same thing.

Disputation here involves the woman pursuing an argument or objection about a contested matter. Taḥāwur identifies the relationship of speech and response between the participants.

The woman has an objection or request about a particular issue, but the conversation is not one-sided. The dual pronoun in taḥāwurakumā identifies two participants in the exchange.

This construction matters for understanding conversation. Conversation may include disputation, but not every conversation is a dispute, and not every objection means hostility or antagonism.

The verse also attributes hearing the woman and the exchange to The God. That attribution comes from the sentence’s content; «ح و ر» itself does not mean hearing or addressing a complaint.

4. The ḥawāriyyūn: Jesus’s companions and the title’s origin

Al-ḥawāriyyūn is the Qur’anic title of a group of Jesus son of Mary’s companions.

Its five occurrences are in Āl ʿImrān 3:52, Al-Māʾidah 5:111, Al-Māʾidah 5:112, and Al-Ṣaff 61:14.

The principal use in Āl ʿImrān

فَلَمَّا أَحَسَّ عِيسَىٰ مِنْهُمُ الْكُفْرَ قَالَ مَنْ أَنْصَارِي إِلَى اللَّهِ قَالَ الْحَوَارِيُّونَ نَحْنُ أَنْصَارُ اللَّهِ آمَنَّا بِاللَّهِ وَاشْهَدْ بِأَنَّا مُسْلِمُونَ

fa-lammā aḥassa ʿīsā minhumu al-kufra qāla man anṣārī ilā allāhi qāla al-ḥawāriyyūna naḥnu anṣāru allāhi āmannā bi-allāhi wa-shhad bi-annā muslimūna

Proposed translation:

“When Jesus perceived disbelief from them, he said, ‘Who are my helpers in the way of The God?’ The disciples said, ‘We are The God’s helpers. We believe in The God; bear witness that we submit.’”

Āl ʿImrān 3:52

Their response clarifies their role: naḥnu anṣāru allāhi, we are The God’s helpers.

Jesus asks for help, and they declare their readiness to help in The God’s way.

In Qur’anic usage, the title therefore identifies a group related to Jesus through following and assistance. Explaining it as Jesus’s close helpers, or retaining ḥawāriyyūn with a short explanation, fits the context.

Another question remains: why were they called ḥawāriyyūn?

Lexical explanations of the title’s origin

Arabic sources record several explanations.

First, whiteness of clothing. Some lexicographers describe the disciples as people who washed and whitened garments. Their title is then derived from ḥawwara, to whiten.

Second, purity and sincerity. Others explain ḥawārī as a close, chosen, pure-hearted, loyal person. On this account, purity and whiteness extend from material qualities to a human description.

Third, assistance and companionship. Classical sources also use ḥawārī for a close, particular helper. That meaning fits its Qur’anic use clearly.

Reports likewise differ over the disciples’ original occupations, including garment whitening and fishing. The Qur’an does not identify their occupations.

Those reports alone therefore cannot establish that the title necessarily originated in the occupation of whitening garments.

Nor should verbal similarity alone make them people of conversation or those who return. Such connections require independent evidence to establish a historical derivation.

What is clear from the Qur’anic text is their role as Jesus’s helpers and their declaration of faith and support.

Al-Ṭabarī: Jesus’s helpers and different explanations of ḥawārī

The disciples’ faith and question

Al-Māʾidah 5:111 describes inspiration to the disciples to believe in The God and His messenger; they declare their belief and submission.

The next verse, however, quotes a question from them:

إِذْ قَالَ الْحَوَارِيُّونَ يَا عِيسَى ابْنَ مَرْيَمَ هَلْ يَسْتَطِيعُ رَبُّكَ أَنْ يُنَزِّلَ عَلَيْنَا مَائِدَةً مِنَ السَّمَاءِ قَالَ اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ مُؤْمِنِينَ

idh qāla al-ḥawāriyyūna yā ʿīsā ibna maryama hal yastaṭīʿu rabbuka an yunazzila ʿalaynā māʾidatan mina al-samāʾi qāla ittaqū allāha in kuntum muʾminīna

Proposed translation:

“When the disciples said, ‘Jesus, son of Mary, can your Lord send down a table from heaven upon us?’ He said, ‘Be mindful of The God if you are believers.’”

Al-Māʾidah 5:112

The title alone thus does not imply immunity from questions, a need for reassurance, or every possible error.

They explain their request next, mentioning eating, reassurance of the heart, and bearing witness to the truth of what they have been told (Al-Māʾidah 5:113).

A possible lexical association with purity should therefore not be made into a conclusion that the disciples were always incapable of error or possessed an independent divine standing.

Their portrayal must come from these verses together.

Ibn Kathīr: reports of different occupations and the meaning helper

The disciples and helping The God in Al-Ṣaff 61:14

Al-Ṣaff 61:14 calls believers to help The God and presents the disciples’ response to Jesus as an example.

The title appears twice there: when Jesus addresses them, and when they respond by calling themselves The God’s helpers.

The verse then describes one group of the Children of Israel believing, another disbelieving, and The God supporting the believers.

Helping explains the disciples’ role here, but is not the lexical meaning of the entire Ḥ W R family. The connection lies in the particular title and use of ḥawāriyyūn.

5. Ḥūr in descriptions of the afterlife’s blessings

Ḥūr occurs four times in the Qur’an, each in descriptions of Paradise’s blessings and companions.

Ḥūr in Al-Dukhān and Al-Ṭūr

كَذَٰلِكَ وَزَوَّجْنَاهُمْ بِحُورٍ عِينٍ

ka-dhālika wa-zawwajnāhum bi-ḥūrin ʿīnin

Proposed translation:

“Thus it is; We paired them with wide-eyed ḥūr.”

Al-Dukhān 44:54

The same expression occurs in Al-Ṭūr 52:20, following a description of people reclining on couches arranged in rows.

Ḥūr and ʿīn appear together in these verses but come from different roots.

Ḥūr is from Ḥ W R and concerns an established description of the eyes’ color and beauty. ʿĪn is from ʿ Y N and here concerns large, wide eyes.

Ḥūr ʿīn therefore combines two descriptions. Translation should not merge them without explanation into a vague expression such as beautiful ones.

Beautiful ones may convey the overall impression, but does not make every lexical feature of the Arabic expression visible.

Zawwajnāhum in zawwajnāhum bi-ḥūrin also concerns pairing or bringing into companionship. The relationship’s details and nature cannot be determined from that verb alone.

Study of ʿ Y N

“Ḥūrun maqṣūrātun fī al-khiyāmi”

حُورٌ مَقْصُورَاتٌ فِي الْخِيَامِ

ḥūrun maqṣūrātun fī al-khiyāmi

Proposed translation:

“Ḥūr kept within the tents.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:72

Ḥūr here takes maqṣūrātun, a feminine plural description. The construction identifies the referents described in this verse as feminine.

Maqṣūrāt is from another root and concerns being restricted or kept. Fī al-khiyāmi also identifies their location.

Being confined or kept is therefore not part of ḥūr itself. The verse adds a separate description.

This distinction matters in root study: treating every accompanying description as the word’s own meaning would erase the distinction between a root and a phrase.

“Wa-ḥūrun ʿīnun” in Al-Wāqiʿah

وَحُورٌ عِينٌ

wa-ḥūrun ʿīnun

Proposed translation:

“And wide-eyed ḥūr.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:22

The next verse compares them to protected pearls (Al-Wāqiʿah 56:23).

The comparison describes value and beauty, but ḥūr does not lexically mean pearls. Pearls belong to the comparison in the following verse and should not replace the original word’s meaning.

Does ḥūr mean only women in Paradise?

In classical Arabic, ḥūr can be the plural of aḥwar and ḥawrāʾ. The underlying adjective therefore has established masculine and feminine forms.

Qur’anic use nevertheless requires attending to the verses’ own indications. In Al-Raḥmān 55:72, maqṣūrāt is clearly feminine; the other passages place ḥūr ʿīn in the context of companions in Paradise.

The plural’s morphological possibilities do not automatically establish that every Qur’anic referent includes men and women alike. Nor should the lexical form be ignored by claiming that Ḥ W R itself concerns only the female sex.

Ḥūr also does not literally mean virgins, sexual spouses, white-skinned women, or beings of a particular age. Some such descriptions may arise in studying other verses and the broader afterlife context, but should not be added without evidence to the lexical definition.

In particular, skin color cannot replace the established description of eyes and then support a claim that the Qur’an teaches the superiority of one human color.

Early Arabic used whiteness, darkness, and contrasting eye color in describing beauty. The precise nature of existence and life in the hereafter, however, is not fully accessible to our material knowledge and experience.

A root study can therefore explain the linguistic meaning of ḥūr without attempting to derive every detail of the afterlife from it.

Is ḥūr derived from bewilderment?

Ḥūr is sometimes explained as someone whose beauty leaves the observer’s eyes bewildered.

That may be a literary description of beauty, but is insufficient to establish a lexical derivation.

Ḥayrah, bewilderment, belongs to Ḥ Y R, a different root. Similar sound and meaning alone do not establish a shared origin.

Ḥūr should therefore not simply be translated as bewildering ones: that would replace the established linguistic feature with a literary effect.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Maqāyīs al-Lughah: ḥūr, aḥwar, and ḥawrāʾ, distinct from bewilderment

6. Rotation, the axle, and its connection with return

Classical Arabic uses miḥwar for a rod or support around which something turns. Ibn Fāris identifies this as the root’s third branch.

Ḥawwara al-khubzata is also recorded for turning and preparing bread.

Circular motion is prominent in this branch. Something turns around a point or support and comes back to an earlier direction or position.

This is close to return as movement, but the two should not be equated in every use. Someone may return to an earlier place without traveling in a circle; something may rotate around an axle without any moral or social change of state being intended.

Modern technical and metaphorical Persian also uses miḥwar for the center of a subject or discussion. That meaning should not be presented without evidence as the basic meaning of every early use.

Miḥwar does not occur in the Qur’an. It helps explain the family’s lexical field, but should not be counted as a Qur’anic witness.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Tahdhīb al-Lughah: the axle and turning bread

7. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ W R (ḥawr) and R J ʿ (rujūʿ): returning and sending back

R J ʿ has broad Qur’anic uses for people returning, sending something back, returning to The God, and other kinds of return.

Ḥāra yaḥūru can likewise express return, as in Al-Inshiqāq 84:14.

Ḥ W R, however, has other Arabic branches such as eye color and conversation, outside the direct meaning of R J ʿ.

Yaḥūru and yarjiʿu overlap in meaning, but that overlap does not make their entire word families identical.

Study of R J ʿ

Ḥ W R (ḥiwār) and J D L (jadal): conversation and disputation

Muḥāwarah emphasizes reciprocal speech and response. Related uses of jadal focus more on confrontation in reasoning, debate, or disagreement.

Al-Mujādilah 58:1 illustrates the distinction: the woman disputes about her husband, and The God speaks of hearing both participants’ conversation.

A dispute can therefore occur within conversation, but not every conversation is a dispute.

The distinction between conversation and debate can also be preserved in translation without treating conversation as always positive or disputation as always negative.

Ḥ W R (ḥawar) and B Y Ḍ (bayāḍ): whiteness and color qualities

Bayāḍ denotes whiteness. Ḥawar in the eyes is more than the name of one color: its familiar definition involves the marked contrast between the white and dark parts.

Directly translating ḥūr as white ones in every verse may therefore omit an important part of its semantic field.

Uses such as whitening garments nevertheless show that whiteness is an established aspect of this family.

Study of B Y Ḍ

Ḥ W R (ḥawārī) and N Ṣ R (naṣr): a group’s title and the act of helping

Ḥawāriyyūn is the Qur’anic title of a particular group of Jesus’s companions, while naṣr denotes helping itself.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:52 and Al-Ṣaff 61:14, the disciples call themselves anṣār Allāh. This helps explain why close helpers suits an explanation of their title.

It does not make N Ṣ R and Ḥ W R synonymous throughout their uses.

Study of N Ṣ R

Ḥ W R (ḥawr) and Ḥ Y R (ḥayrah): return and bewilderment

Ḥayrah concerns bewilderment and not knowing the way or an answer. One branch of Ḥ W R expresses return, another a quality of color.

Some lexical explanations connect moving back and forth or hesitation with bewilderment, but this proximity does not justify exchanging the roots.

Precise Qur’anic study must identify each word by its own root and construction, with any proposed historical connections supported by reliable evidence.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

No single equivalent is sufficient for Ḥ W R, since its Qur’anic forms belong to different semantic branches.

In Al-Inshiqāq 84:14, yaḥūra is best rendered return. Return to life after death and accountability come from context; return itself is sufficient in a close translation.

In Al-Kahf 18:34 and Al-Kahf 18:37, yuḥāwiruhu can be rendered “while conversing with him.” This is natural and preserves the reciprocal relationship.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:1, taḥāwurakumā means “the conversation between the two of you.” Adding debate, objection, or disputation to this word’s translation is unnecessary, since the verse has a separate verb for disputing.

For al-ḥawāriyyūn, retaining the Arabic title and explaining it as Jesus’s close helpers is suitable. Disciples alone may understate their assistance; helpers alone may lose the particular group’s title.

For ḥūr, no simple, flawless Persian equivalent conveys every feature in one word. Retaining ḥūr or ḥūrān with an explanation of the eyes’ color and beauty is preferable to adding adjectives absent from the text. English can likewise retain ḥūr with an explanation.

Ḥūrun ʿīnun can be rendered wide-eyed ḥūr, explaining that ḥūr concerns a quality of eye color, and ʿīn the eyes’ size and openness.

This preserves the two roots’ distinction and prevents a paraphrase from replacing both Arabic descriptions.

9. Conclusion

Brief root meaning: Return and reciprocal speech; rotation and qualities of color and eyes; the ḥawāriyyūn, Jesus’s close helpers.

In the Qur’an, yaḥūra concerns return; yuḥāwiruhu and taḥāwurakumā describe reciprocal conversation; ḥawāriyyūn titles Jesus’s helpers; and ḥūr describes companions in Paradise through an established quality of eye color and beauty.

The disciples’ role as Jesus’s helpers is clear from the Qur’an. Their title’s precise historical origin, including the proposed connection with whitening garments, remains uncertain.

Early Arabic witnesses also place ḥūr in pre-Islamic descriptions of human beauty. Its afterlife uses nevertheless require their own verse contexts, without adding unproven details.

Final definition: Ḥ W R is a family with several branches encompassing return, reciprocal speech, rotation, and qualities of color. Each derivative must be understood through its own construction and use; sharing a root alone does not justify merging every meaning.
