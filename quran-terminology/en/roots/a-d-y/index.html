Qur’anic Root A D Y

Adāʾ: conveying something to its destination or rightful recipient, returning a trust, discharging an obligation, and handing over what must be delivered

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root A D Y concerns conveying something to its destination, handing it to a recipient, and discharging an obligation resting on someone. Addā al-shayʾa ilā fulānin describes delivering or handing something to the intended person. Addā al-dayna means paying a debt, while addā al-amānata means delivering a trust to its rightful recipient.

Within the same family, adāʾ denotes the act of conveying, delivering, or carrying something out. Taʾdiyah belongs to the same field.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs in several settings: paying a due in a passage about retribution and remission, discharging a trust, returning property to its owner, delivering trusts to those entitled to them, and Moses’ demand that Pharaoh and those around him hand over the servants of The God.

These uses share the idea of conveying something to the person who should receive it, or discharging a responsibility resting on someone.

They are not all identical, however. Paying a financial obligation differs from returning property that belonged to someone else from the outset. Likewise, handing over people under Pharaoh’s domination differs from delivering a material object.

The root’s meaning should therefore be preserved, while the context identifies what is delivered and the recipient’s entitlement.

Qur’anic forms

A D Y occurs six times in five verses in the Qur’an: five times as a Form II verb and once as a verbal noun.

Qur’anic formVerseContextual meaning
وَأَدَاءٌ (wa-adāʾun)Al-Baqarah 2:178Paying or delivering the due with kindness
فَلْيُؤَدِّ (fa-l-yuʾaddi)Al-Baqarah 2:283Then let him discharge the trust or obligation
يُؤَدِّهِ (yuʾaddihi)Āl ʿImrān 3:75He returns it to you
لَا يُؤَدِّهِ (lā yuʾaddihi)Āl ʿImrān 3:75He does not return it to you
تُؤَدُّوا (tuʾaddū)Al-Nisāʾ 4:58Deliver trusts to those entitled to them
أَدُّوا (addū)Al-Dukhān 44:18Hand over or deliver

The five verbal occurrences derive from addā, yuʾaddī, Form II.

Addū in Al-Dukhān 44:18 is a plural imperative.

Fa-l-yuʾaddi in Al-Baqarah 2:283 is an imperfect verb made jussive by the lām of command.

Yuʾaddihi in Āl ʿImrān 3:75 is an imperfect verb with the attached object pronoun -hi, “it.” It occurs twice, once in an affirmative clause and once in a negative clause. In both instances, it is jussive as the response to the conditional in, and the final yāʾ of the underlying yuʾaddī is omitted. In the second instance, lā negates the statement; it is not the lā of prohibition.

Tuʾaddū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:58 is likewise an imperfect verb, in the subjunctive after an.

All these occurrences concern conveying or handing over. In each verse, however, the object delivered, the agent, and the recipient’s entitlement need to be identified.

2. The root’s meaning in early Arabic

Conveying something to its destination

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the basis of A D Y as follows:

“إِيصَالُ الشَّيْءِ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ أَوْ وُصُولُهُ إِلَيْهِ مِنْ تِلْقَاءِ نَفْسِهِ”.

That is, conveying something to something else, or its reaching a place or condition of its own accord.

This explanation has two aspects.

In the first, an agent conveys something to a destination.

In the second, something itself reaches a stage or condition.

The Qur’an’s prevailing usage belongs to the first aspect: someone delivers something to another person or discharges an obligation.

The second aspect’s presence in the lexicons nevertheless shows that the family’s early semantic field extended beyond paying money and returning trusts.

Addā al-shayʾa: conveying and delivering

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

“وَأَدَّى الشَّيْءَ: أَوْصَلَهُ”.

That is, to perform adāʾ of something is to convey it.

Addā daynahu is also explained as paying one’s debt.

These two uses have a clear relationship.

When delivering an object, someone transfers it from one place to another or into the recipient’s hands.

When paying a debt, someone conveys a financial due for which they are responsible to the person entitled to it.

There is an important difference, however: what is delivered need not previously have been in the recipient’s possession as that very material object.

For example, someone entrusted with property may return that same property.

A debtor, however, can discharge an obligation by paying the specified amount without returning the exact original coins or objects.

Adāʾ is therefore broader than returning the identical item of property. Delivering a due and discharging an obligation also fall within its field.

A poetic example of conveying a message

In explaining this root, Ibn Fāris quotes a verse containing the following:

أَدَّى إِلَى هِنْدٍ تَحِيَّاتِهَا
وَقَالَ هَذَا مِنْ وَدَاعِي بِكِرْ

addā ilā hindin taḥiyyātihā / wa-qāla hādhā min wadāʿī bikir

“He conveyed greetings to Hind and said, ‘Among my farewells, this is a new and unprecedented one.’”

What is conveyed in this example is neither money nor a physical object, but a greeting and a message.

It shows that conveying within A D Y can also concern speech.

In Lisān al-ʿArab, this verse appears among lines attributed to Ibn Aḥmar. Its attribution should therefore not be described as entirely unknown, although the precise date of composition is unclear and this alone cannot establish that it is securely pre-Islamic. The reading bikir in Maqāyīs is a phonetic variant of bikr, referring to newness or something unprecedented, rather than a person named Bakr.

Linguistically, its importance lies in documenting the use of adāʾ for conveying a message within the Arabic lexical tradition.

This does not make every act of speaking adāʾ. In this use, speech is conveyed from one person to another.

News reaching someone

In Arabic, taʾaddā ilayhi al-khabaru means that news reached someone.

In this construction, the news is what reaches the destination.

It differs grammatically from addā al-khabara, “he conveyed the news.”

In one, someone transmits the news; in the other, the news reaches its destination.

The morphological form and the sentence’s subject therefore matter.

The independent form taʾaddā does not occur in the Qur’an.

It should not be counted among Qur’anic derivatives, although it helps establish the root’s broader Arabic usage.

Adā al-labanu: milk reaching a stage of change

Early sources record the expression adā al-labanu.

In this expression, milk reaches the stage at which it begins to thicken and set.

Ibn Fāris presents this as an example of something reaching a condition.

Lexicons nevertheless discuss certain milk-related forms under both A D Y and A D W.

Without a separate historical investigation, it should therefore not be asserted that the milk-related use belongs etymologically to precisely the same branch as adāʾ al-amānah, “discharging a trust.”

Nor should the meaning of thickening milk be transferred to the Qur’anic verses.

In Qur’anic usage, conveying something and discharging an obligation to its destination or rightful recipient is the clearer, more direct meaning.

Does adāʾ always mean performing worship?

Expressions such as adāʾ al-ṣalāh, adāʾ al-wājib, and adāʾ al-ʿamal are familiar in modern Arabic: performing prayer, fulfilling a duty, and carrying out work.

In these uses, adāʾ means carrying out an action or responsibility.

The Qur’an, however, does not use this root for ṣalāh itself.

For ṣalāh, it uses expressions such as aqīmū al-ṣalāta and aqimi al-ṣalāta, “establish ṣalāh.”

The familiarity of “performing prayer” in Persian and modern Arabic therefore does not establish that adāʾ and iqāmah are completely identical in Qur’anic language.

Adāʾ can express discharging a responsibility, but iqāmah belongs to a different root with its own semantic field.

This distinction is particularly important in an independent study of ṣalāh.

3. Discharging a trust: confidence and practical fulfillment

Fa-l-yuʾaddi alladhī uʾtumina amānatahu

Al-Baqarah, 2:283

وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ وَلَمْ تَجِدُوا كَاتِبًا فَرِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ ۖ فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُمْ بَعْضًا فَلْيُؤَدِّ الَّذِي اؤْتُمِنَ أَمَانَتَهُ وَلْيَتَّقِ اللَّهَ رَبَّهُ ۗ وَلَا تَكْتُمُوا الشَّهَادَةَ ۚ وَمَنْ يَكْتُمْهَا فَإِنَّهُ آثِمٌ قَلْبُهُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ عَلِيمٌ

wa-in kuntum ʿalā safarin wa-lam tajidū kātiban fa-rihānun maqbūḍatun; fa-in amina baʿḍukum baʿḍan fa-l-yuʾaddi alladhī uʾtumina amānatahu wa-l-yattaqi allāha rabbahu; wa-lā taktumū al-shahādata; wa-man yaktumhā fa-innahu āthimun qalbuhu; wa-allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna ʿalīm

“If you are traveling and cannot find a scribe, let there be pledges taken into possession. But if one of you trusts another, let the person entrusted discharge the trust for which he is responsible, and let him be mindful of The God, his Lord. Do not conceal testimony. Whoever conceals it has a sinful heart; and The God knows what you do.”

This is one of the root’s clearest occurrences.

Amina, uʾtumina, and amānatahu in the verse belong to A M N.

Fa-l-yuʾaddi, however, belongs to A D Y.

The two families are related in meaning but distinct.

Amina concerns trusting.

Uʾtumina describes someone who has been trusted and entrusted with something.

Amānah names a deposit or responsibility that must be safeguarded.

Yuʾaddi expresses discharging that responsibility and delivering the due to the person entitled to it.

In this verse, therefore, trust is the basis for entrusting a responsibility, and adāʾ is the practical fulfillment of that responsibility.

Trust in place of material security

The verse concerns debt, travel, written documentation, and pledges.

The preceding verse discussed recording a debt and testimony.

This verse addresses a situation in which written documentation is unavailable or the parties trust one another.

Trust does not, however, extinguish the debt or remove the responsibility.

The person considered trustworthy must still discharge the trust.

Fa-l-yuʾaddi here is therefore more than an emotional recommendation about the virtue of trustworthiness: it concerns delivering a due resting on someone.

Nor does the root itself establish that written documentation or witnesses are unnecessary in every transaction.

Those questions require considering the full set of verses on debt and financial rights.

Amānatahu: whose trust?

In fa-l-yuʾaddi alladhī uʾtumina amānatahu, the pronoun -hu in amānatahu refers to the person responsible for the trust.

The natural expression “discharge his trust” does not mean that he owns the entrusted property.

Something may belong to another person while remaining in the trustee’s possession.

Translation should therefore preserve the distinction between the person responsible for the trust and the person entitled to receive it.

The verse commands the trustee to fulfill his obligation, rather than keep the entrusted property for himself.

Trust and testimony

The same verse ends by prohibiting the concealment of testimony.

Their association shows that financial relationships extend beyond safeguarding and paying property: truthfulness in testimony also protects people’s rights.

Taktumū, however, belongs to K T M and concerns concealment.

It should not be counted as a derivative of A D Y.

Nor does the juxtaposition of testimony and a trust mean that yuʾaddī here concerns only giving testimony. The verb’s own object is amānatahu, and the main setting concerns debt and financial trust.

4. Yuʾaddihi ilayka: delivering a due and distinguishing people’s conduct

Āl ʿImrān, 3:75

وَمِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مَنْ إِنْ تَأْمَنْهُ بِقِنْطَارٍ يُؤَدِّهِ إِلَيْكَ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ إِنْ تَأْمَنْهُ بِدِينَارٍ لَا يُؤَدِّهِ إِلَيْكَ إِلَّا مَا دُمْتَ عَلَيْهِ قَائِمًا ۗ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا لَيْسَ عَلَيْنَا فِي الْأُمِّيِّينَ سَبِيلٌ وَيَقُولُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-min ahli al-kitābi man in taʾmanhu bi-qinṭārin yuʾaddihi ilayka wa-minhum man in taʾmanhu bi-dīnārin lā yuʾaddihi ilayka illā mā dumta ʿalayhi qāʾiman; dhālika bi-annahum qālū laysa ʿalaynā fī al-ummiyyīna sabīlun wa-yaqūlūna ʿalā allāhi al-kadhiba wa-hum yaʿlamūn

“Among the People of the Book are those who, if you entrust them with much wealth, return it to you. Among them are others who, if you entrust them with a single dinar, do not return it unless you persist in demanding it from them. This is because they say, ‘We bear no liability concerning the ummiyyīn’; and they knowingly attribute falsehood to The God.”

A D Y occurs twice in this verse.

First, in yuʾaddihi ilayka.

Second, in lā yuʾaddihi ilayka.

The difference between these clauses is the verb’s affirmation or negation.

Returning substantial property

The first part describes someone among the People of the Book who returns even a large amount of entrusted wealth.

Qinṭār here indicates a substantial quantity of wealth.

Yuʾaddihi ilayka expresses delivering that same property or financial due to its rightful recipient.

Adāʾ here therefore means returning a trust.

The verb itself does not mean being wealthy, being generous, or giving away one’s own property.

Someone may be wealthy or possess little and nevertheless act honestly when returning a trust.

What matters is delivering the entrusted property to its rightful recipient.

Refusing to return a small amount

The second part describes someone who will not return even a dinar unless the person entitled to it persistently demands it.

Here, too, yuʾaddihi means delivering a due to its rightful recipient.

The negative construction lā yuʾaddihi, however, indicates that the act of delivery does not take place.

The verse thus distinguishes two kinds of conduct: someone trustworthy with substantial wealth, and someone who refuses to return even a small amount.

This distinction shows that the amount of property alone does not determine trustworthiness.

Does the verse judge all the People of the Book alike?

No.

Its construction begins wa-min ahli al-kitābi man, “among the People of the Book is someone who,” and then continues wa-minhum man, “among them is someone who.”

These expressions explicitly distinguish different people among the People of the Book.

Some act honestly in returning trusts; others do not.

The condemned conduct of the second group should therefore not be attributed to all the People of the Book.

Conversely, the verse’s praise of some people’s trustworthiness should not be used to erase the reality of others’ betrayal.

The Qur’an describes people’s conduct separately here.

The claim laysa ʿalaynā fī al-ummiyyīna sabīl

The verse’s final part reports a claim through which a group rejected responsibility toward the ummiyyīn.

That claim appears in the context of withholding other people’s property.

The Qur’an itself immediately calls attributing such a rule to The God a falsehood.

This statement should therefore not be presented as a Qur’anic principle denying rights to people outside a religious community.

Nor does adāʾ here depend on the recipient’s religious identity. It means delivering property to the person entitled to it.

The condemned group’s claim attempts to exempt certain rightful recipients from the obligation to return their property; the Qur’an critiques precisely that justification.

This verse clearly illustrates that faithfulness to trusts and financial rights should not be confined to one’s fellow believers.

5. Tuʾaddū al-amānāti ilā ahlihā: delivering trusts to those entitled to them

Al-Nisāʾ, 4:58

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَأْمُرُكُمْ أَنْ تُؤَدُّوا الْأَمَانَاتِ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهَا وَإِذَا حَكَمْتُمْ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ أَنْ تَحْكُمُوا بِالْعَدْلِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ نِعِمَّا يَعِظُكُمْ بِهِ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ سَمِيعًا بَصِيرًا

inna allāha yaʾmurukum an tuʾaddū al-amānāti ilā ahlihā wa-idhā ḥakamtum bayna al-nāsi an taḥkumū bi-al-ʿadli; inna allāha niʿimmā yaʿiẓukum bihi; inna allāha kāna samīʿan baṣīrā

“The God commands you to deliver trusts to those entitled to them, and, when you judge between people, to judge with justice. How excellent is the instruction The God gives you in these matters. The God is indeed Hearing and Seeing.”

This is one of the root’s most important general uses.

Tuʾaddū is an imperfect verb addressed to a plural “you.”

Its object is al-amānāt, “the trusts,” and the destination is specified by ilā ahlihā, “to those entitled to them.”

The meaning of ahlihā

Ahl in this construction denotes those to whom the trust belongs or who are entitled to receive it.

It can therefore be rendered:

“Deliver trusts to their owners.”

Or:

“Hand trusts over to those entitled to them.”

Ahlihā does not always refer solely to the private owner of a material object.

Someone may have been entrusted with a public responsibility, and those entitled to it may be the people qualified or authorized to assume that responsibility.

Such an application can be considered within the general meaning of a trust.

The full discussion of merit, governance, and appointments should nevertheless not be included in the lexical meaning of adāʾ itself.

The verb expresses conveying or handing over what must be delivered. The subject and context determine the kind of trust and the recipient’s entitlement.

A trust is not only property

Al-amānāt is plural in the Qur’an and is not restricted to a single specified item of property.

The construction can encompass different responsibilities.

In linguistic and social usage, property, documents, authority, responsibility, and things entrusted to someone for safekeeping or care can fall within the field of a trust.

This breadth should nevertheless be applied to the verse cautiously.

Something should not be called a trust merely because it is valuable, without explanation.

For example, if knowledge or an office is considered a trust, the responsibility involved and the person entitled to receive it should be identified.

In this verse, tuʾaddū emphasizes discharging the due arising from that trust.

The relationship with justice

After commanding the discharge of trusts, the Qur’an speaks of judging between people with justice.

This association matters.

People’s rights suffer in a society where trusts are not delivered to those entitled to them or judgments are unjust.

ʿAdl, however, belongs to a different root.

Tuʾaddū should not be translated as “judge with justice,” since the command to judge justly is explicitly expressed through separate words in the verse’s second part.

The two commands are related, but each retains its own meaning.

The verse also says bayna al-nās, “between people.” This should not be restricted without evidence to judging only between members of one religious community.

6. Wa-adāʾun ilayhi bi-iḥsānin: delivering a due in the passage on retribution

Al-Baqarah, 2:178

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْقِصَاصُ فِي الْقَتْلَى ۖ الْحُرُّ بِالْحُرِّ وَالْعَبْدُ بِالْعَبْدِ وَالْأُنْثَىٰ بِالْأُنْثَىٰ ۚ فَمَنْ عُفِيَ لَهُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ شَيْءٌ فَاتِّبَاعٌ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَأَدَاءٌ إِلَيْهِ بِإِحْسَانٍ ۗ ذَٰلِكَ تَخْفِيفٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ وَرَحْمَةٌ ۗ فَمَنِ اعْتَدَىٰ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ فَلَهُ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū kutiba ʿalaykumu al-qiṣāṣu fī al-qatlā; al-ḥurru bi-al-ḥurri wa-al-ʿabdu bi-al-ʿabdi wa-al-unthā bi-al-unthā; fa-man ʿufiya lahu min akhīhi shayʾun fa-ittibāʿun bi-al-maʿrūfi wa-adāʾun ilayhi bi-iḥsānin; dhālika takhfīfun min rabbikum wa-raḥmatun; fa-mani iʿtadā baʿda dhālika fa-lahu ʿadhābun alīm

“You who have believed, retribution concerning those killed has been prescribed for you: the free for the free, the enslaved for the enslaved, and the female for the female. If someone receives a remission from his brother, pursuit of the due should be conducted appropriately and payment made to him with kindness. This is an alleviation and mercy from your Lord. Whoever transgresses after that will have a painful punishment.”

Adāʾ in this verse is the root’s only Qur’anic verbal noun.

It stands alongside fa-ittibāʿun bi-al-maʿrūfi, “appropriate pursuit of the due.”

Discharging a due and pursuing it

Early explanations generally understand the construction to mean that, after retribution is remitted and a financial due is established, the rightful recipient should pursue it appropriately and the person responsible should pay it with kindness.

In this reading, ittibāʿun bi-al-maʿrūfi concerns pursuing or demanding the due appropriately.

Adāʾun ilayhi bi-iḥsānin concerns paying or delivering it to the person entitled to it.

The lexical meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from determining the parties’ precise rights.

Adāʾ itself does not mean retribution or pardon.

Qiṣāṣ belongs to Q Ṣ Ṣ, while ʿafw belongs to ʿ F W.

Adāʾ here concerns discharging an obligation arising after remission and agreement.

Does adāʾ here mean the compensation payment itself?

Legal and exegetical explanations connect adāʾ in this setting with paying diyah, compensation concerning a killing.

This reading is defensible from the verse’s construction: after remission is mentioned, it speaks of appropriate pursuit and payment with kindness.

The word diyah itself, however, does not occur in Al-Baqarah 2:178.

Diyah belongs to a different lexical family and appears explicitly in Al-Nisāʾ 4:92.

Adāʾ should therefore not be defined lexically as diyah.

Adāʾ is the act of paying or delivering a due; diyah names a kind of financial due concerning the killing of a human being.

Adāʾ in this verse may concern paying diyah, but this contextual application does not erase the root’s general meaning.

Bi-iḥsānin: the manner of payment

The verse qualifies adāʾ with bi-iḥsānin, “with kindness.”

This qualification shows that the manner of payment matters as well as the payment itself.

Payment accompanied by harassment, humiliation, deliberate delay, or deception is incompatible with the gracious payment depicted by the verse.

Iḥsān, however, belongs to Ḥ S N.

Kindness should therefore not be made an invariable component of the lexical meaning of adāʾ.

Someone may deliver property reluctantly or with mistreatment. The transfer may have occurred, while the conduct remains far from iḥsān.

Here, the Qur’an accompanies adāʾ with an ethical qualification.

Why does the recipient matter?

In adāʾun ilayhi, the preposition ilā establishes the payment’s direction and destination.

The construction indicates that the due must reach the appropriate recipient.

There are, however, differing explanations of the precise pronoun references and the roles of those involved in remission and payment in the verse’s first part.

A final judgment on every legal detail requires a separate study of fa-man ʿufiya lahu min akhīhi shayʾun and the related verses on retribution and diyah.

These differences do not change the basic meaning of adāʾ.

In each reading, adāʾ concerns delivering a due that must be discharged.

7. An addū ilayya ʿibāda allāhi: handing over the servants of The God in Moses’ account

Al-Dukhān, 44:17

وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمَ فِرْعَوْنَ وَجَاءَهُمْ رَسُولٌ كَرِيمٌ

wa-la-qad fatannā qablahum qawma firʿawna wa-jāʾahum rasūlun karīm

“We had indeed tested Pharaoh’s people before them, and a noble messenger came to them.”

Al-Dukhān, 44:18

أَنْ أَدُّوا إِلَيَّ عِبَادَ اللَّهِ ۖ إِنِّي لَكُمْ رَسُولٌ أَمِينٌ

an addū ilayya ʿibāda allāhi; innī lakum rasūlun amīn

“Hand over the servants of The God to me. I am indeed a trustworthy messenger to you.”

This use differs from the financial occurrences.

The subjects of the imperative are the people Moses addresses.

Addū is a plural command.

In the familiar grammatical reading, ʿibāda allāhi is the verb’s object and ilayya identifies the destination of the handing over.

The sentence thus means handing over the servants of The God to Moses.

Who are ʿibād allāh?

In the setting of Moses and Pharaoh’s account, ʿibād allāh is understood as referring to the Children of Israel, the community under Pharaoh’s domination.

This reading has a clear relationship with other verses in Moses’ account.

Al-Aʿrāf, 7:105

حَقِيقٌ عَلَىٰ أَنْ لَا أَقُولَ عَلَى اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْحَقَّ ۚ قَدْ جِئْتُكُمْ بِبَيِّنَةٍ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ فَأَرْسِلْ مَعِيَ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ

ḥaqīqun ʿalā an lā aqūla ʿalā allāhi illā al-ḥaqqa; qad jiʾtukum bi-bayyinatin min rabbikum fa-arsil maʿiya banī isrāʾīl

“I am bound and committed to say nothing about The God except the truth. I have brought you clear evidence from your Lord, so send the Children of Israel with me.”

In this verse, Moses asks Pharaoh to let the Children of Israel go with him.

An addū ilayya ʿibāda allāhi in Al-Dukhān 44:18 is thus connected with the demand to release and hand over the Children of Israel.

The construction does not make the Children of Israel Moses’ personal property or possessions.

Moses asks that they leave Pharaoh’s domination and depart with him.

The expression ʿibād allāh likewise relates their position to The God, rather than to Moses’ personal ownership.

The grammatical alternatives for ʿibāda allāhi

Early explanations also propose another reading of the sentence.

In that reading, ʿibāda allāhi is a vocative: Moses addresses his audience as “servants of The God.”

Addū ilayya can then concern delivering a due, accepting the message, or paying attention and listening to his words.

Some lexical explanations cite addi ilayya samʿaka for listening and directing one’s hearing toward someone’s speech.

This possibility can be considered within Arabic usage.

In the immediate setting of Moses and the Children of Israel, however, treating ʿibād allāh as the object and understanding a demand to hand over the Children of Israel has clearer contextual support.

The proposed translation is therefore “Hand over the servants of The God to me.”

The vocative reading should remain in the discussion, but its mere existence should not displace the more direct meaning of the sentence.

Adāʾ and liberation

In this verse, adāʾ relates to the Children of Israel’s release from Pharaoh’s domination.

A D Y itself, however, does not mean liberating.

Liberation is the result and purpose understood from Moses’ account and his demand of Pharaoh.

Lexically, adāʾ expresses conveying or handing over.

The contextual explanation may therefore say that Moses sought the Children of Israel’s release and delivery to him. In the word-by-word box, however, addū is better rendered as “hand over” or “deliver.”

8. Distinctions from related roots and proposed translations

A D Y and A M N: discharging a trust and the trust itself

Study of A M N

Amānah belongs to A M N and concerns confidence, security, and being entrusted with a responsibility.

Adāʾ belongs to A D Y and expresses conveying something or bringing it to fulfillment.

In Al-Baqarah 2:283, the two families appear together.

One person trusts another; a trust becomes that person’s responsibility; the person must then discharge it.

A trust and discharging a trust are therefore related but distinct concepts.

A D Y and W F Y: delivering a due and fulfilling it

Study of W F Y

Wafāʾ concerns fulfilling an obligation completely.

Adāʾ can likewise mean discharging an obligation, but delivering the due to its recipient is prominent.

For example, paying a debt can be described as adāʾ; fulfilling the entire obligation can also be described as wafāʾ.

The two should nevertheless not be treated as absolute synonyms.

Someone may complete an action without transferring anything to another person.

Conversely, something may reach someone while a question remains about whether the entire obligation has been fulfilled.

Depending on the verse’s subject, adāʾ is therefore better rendered as “paying,” “conveying,” “delivering,” or “carrying out.”

A D Y and R D D: discharging and returning

Study of R D D

Radd concerns returning something.

Adāʾ can likewise mean returning property in the trust-related verses.

Not every adāʾ, however, is the return of the identical original thing.

In Al-Baqarah 2:178, paying a financial due can be adāʾ even though the recipient need not have previously given that same property to the payer.

Al-Dukhān 44:18 likewise concerns handing over people, rather than returning a borrowed material object.

“Returning” is therefore suitable in some verses, but it is not the root’s invariable meaning.

A D Y and A T Y: delivering and giving

Study of A T Y

Ītāʾ belongs to A T Y and frequently denotes giving property, scripture, judgment, and other things in the Qur’an.

Adāʾ also concerns conveying something.

In its principal Qur’anic uses, however, an obligation or due that must reach its rightful recipient is often involved.

Ītāʾ, by contrast, can concern giving something where the recipient did not already hold the same kind of claim against the giver.

This distinction is useful in many contexts, but should not be made an absolute rule without exceptions.

The verb, its object, and the recipient need to be examined in each verse.

A D Y and Q Ḍ Y: carrying out and completing

Study of Q Ḍ Y

Qaḍāʾ concerns judgment, bringing something to an end, and carrying something out.

In financial usage, qaḍāʾ al-dayn and adāʾ al-dayn, both involving paying a debt, are closely related.

Qaḍāʾ has a wider Qur’anic semantic field, however, and can express judgment, bringing something to an end, or the realization of a matter.

In the Qur’anic occurrences examined here, adāʾ predominantly concerns delivering a due or handing something to its destination.

All uses of qaḍāʾ should therefore not be translated as adāʾ.

A D Y and B L GH: conveying and communicating

Study of B L GH

Balāgh belongs to B L GH and concerns reaching and conveying a message.

Adāʾ can also denote conveying news or greetings in Arabic.

The two families therefore have a clear semantic proximity.

In message-related usage, balāgh is particularly associated with conveying speech or news to an audience.

Adāʾ can concern messages, property, trusts, debts, and other obligations.

Neither can therefore replace the other in every context.

A D Y and W D Y: paying diyah and diyah itself

Study of W D Y

In Al-Baqarah 2:178, adāʾ occurs in the context of retribution and remission.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, diyah names a financial due connected with killing a human being.

Diyah belongs to W D Y, however, rather than A D Y.

The similar sounds of adāʾ and diyah should therefore not lead to their being treated as one root.

Diyah is a kind of financial due; adāʾ can be the act of paying that due.

This distinction matters in legal discussions and the translation of the verses.

Proposed translations for Forqan

In Al-Baqarah 2:178, adāʾun ilayhi bi-iḥsānin is best rendered as “payment of the due to him with kindness.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:283, fa-l-yuʾaddi alladhī uʾtumina amānatahu can be rendered “Then let the person entrusted discharge the trust for which he is responsible.”

In Āl ʿImrān 3:75, since entrusted property is involved, yuʾaddihi ilayka is better rendered “he returns it to you,” and lā yuʾaddihi ilayka, “he does not return it to you.”

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:58, an tuʾaddū al-amānāti ilā ahlihā is best rendered “deliver trusts to those entitled to them” or “convey trusts to their rightful recipients.”

In Al-Dukhān 44:18, addū ilayya ʿibāda allāhi, under the preferred reading, means “Hand over the servants of The God to me.”

For the word-by-word boxes:

  • Adāʾun: discharging, paying, or delivering a due.
  • Fa-l-yuʾaddi: then let him discharge or deliver.
  • Yuʾaddihi: he conveys or returns it.
  • Lā yuʾaddihi: he does not convey or return it.
  • Tuʾaddū: discharge, hand over, or deliver to the rightful recipient.
  • Addū: hand over or deliver; in Al-Dukhān 44:18, concerning the servants of The God in Moses’ account.

Conclusion

The Arabic root A D Y concerns conveying something to its destination and fulfilling what rests on someone’s responsibility.

In financial uses, adāʾ can mean returning entrusted property or paying a due owed to another person.

In news- and message-related usage, it can mean conveying speech to an audience.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs six times in five verses.

In Al-Baqarah 2:178, adāʾ occurs in the setting of remission of retribution and paying a due with kindness.

In Al-Baqarah 2:283, the person entrusted must discharge the trust.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:75, the Qur’an distinguishes someone who returns substantial property from someone who refuses to return even a small amount. This distinction rests on people’s conduct, rather than generalizing one judgment to all the People of the Book.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:58 commands delivering trusts to those entitled to them, alongside judging between people with justice.

In Al-Dukhān 44:18, Moses asks his audience to hand over the servants of The God to him. This shows that adāʾ is not limited to transferring property: the verb can also describe handing over people who must be released from wrongful domination.

These six occurrences form a clear semantic field, while their different applications must remain distinct.

For Forqan, the basic definition of this family is: adāʾ means conveying something to its destination or rightful recipient, and discharging a responsibility that must be brought to fulfillment. Financially, it can mean paying a debt or returning a trust; socially, handing over what belongs to someone; and in message-related usage, conveying speech or news to its audience.

Adāʾ alone does not determine who the recipient is, what kind of due is involved, or the ethical quality of the act.

Those features emerge from the object, preposition, recipient, and accompanying qualifications.

In Al-Baqarah 2:178, bi-iḥsānin expresses the goodness of the payment. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:58, ilā ahlihā identifies the rightful recipients. In Al-Baqarah 2:283, uʾtumina and amānatahu establish the relationship of trust and responsibility.

Studying this root therefore involves more than asking “What was delivered?” The questions are: What was someone responsible for, to whom should it have been delivered, and did that due actually reach its destination?

This distinction separates adāʾ from a trust, complete fulfillment, giving, returning, and merely carrying out an action, clarifying each Qur’anic occurrence in its own setting.
