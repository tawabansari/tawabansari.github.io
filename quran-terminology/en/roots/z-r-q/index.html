Core meaning: blue and bluish coloration. Early Arabic usages encompass eye color, clear water, the sheen of metal, and changes in appearance. Other branches concern throwing and ejecting.

1. The root in early Arabic and pre-Islamic poetry

The color branch of ز ر ق is prominent in early Arabic. It describes human eyes, clear water, spearheads, and certain birds; the dating of each usage requires its own evidence.

أَزْرَق applied to eyes denotes a color distinct from their usual dark appearance. Lexical explanations do not restrict it to modern blue: some mention greenishness or whiteness within the dark part of the eye.

Of water, أَزْرَق can describe clarity. Of a blade or spearhead, it concerns the color and sheen of the metal.

The lexical range is therefore not a single color with precise modern boundaries. It includes related color impressions and the particular brightness of surfaces.

Pre-Islamic poetry provides important evidence for particular usages.

First example: clear, blue water in Zuhayr’s poetry

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says in his Muʿallaqah:

فَلَمَّا وَرَدْنَ الْمَاءَ زُرْقًا جِمَامُهُ
وَضَعْنَ عِصِيَّ الْحَاضِرِ الْمُتَخَيِّمِ

fa-lammā waradna al-māʾa zurqan jimāmuhu / waḍaʿna ʿiṣiyya al-ḥāḍiri al-mutakhayyimi

Translation:

“When they reached the water, its gathered pools clear and blue, they laid down their traveling staffs like those settling in a camp.”

جِمَام denotes accumulated water, while زُرْقًا describes its color or appearance.

Early commentaries explain the water’s blueness through its clarity.

This attests زُرْق describing clear water in pre-Islamic poetic usage without a negative meaning.

In this image, blueness suggests appealing clarity, not disease, fear, or punishment.

Second example: the sheen of spearheads in Imruʾ al-Qays

The pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

أَيَقْتُلُنِي وَالْمَشْرَفِيُّ مُضَاجِعِي
وَمَسْنُونَةٌ زُرْقٌ كَأَنْيَابِ أَغْوَالِ

a-yaqtulunī wa-al-mashrafiyyu muḍājiʿī / wa-masnūnatun zurqun ka-anyābi aghwāli

Translation:

“Will he kill me while my Mashrafī sword lies beside me, and sharpened blue spearheads like the fangs of ghouls are with me?”

مَسْنُونَة concerns sharpened points, explained here as spearheads, while زُرْق describes their color and metallic appearance.

Here زرق does not mean blind, sick, or thirsty. The poet describes metal points with a distinctive color or sheen.

The example also attests the root’s use for inanimate objects.

What the poetic evidence establishes

Together, these two examples establish color-related usages for water and metal in poetry transmitted from before Islam. Broader eye-color descriptions are also recorded lexically.

In its color branch, زرق describes blue or bluish appearance. The two poems attest water and metal; the broader lexical range also includes eyes and other objects.

The poems support these particular usages; they do not independently date every later-recorded sense to the pre-Islamic period.

They do not show that blue or bluish coloration inherently signified evil, disease, or punishment. Its associations depend on context and the object described.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زُرْقَة

This color noun denotes blueness or bluish coloration.

Of eyes, lexical accounts include blue, greenishness, or whiteness appearing within the dark part.

Of water and metal, it concerns their color and surface appearance.

The appearance need not be identical in every instance.

زَرِقَ يَزْرَقُ

This means being or becoming blue or bluish.

زَرِقَتْ عَيْنُهُ

zariqat ʿaynuhu

“His eye became blue or bluish,” or “his eye had that color.”

The same expression is recorded for whiteness becoming visible over the dark part of the eye.

Naturally blue eyes and an unusual change of appearance are distinct conditions and should not be conflated in every usage.

أَزْرَق

The masculine adjective means blue or bluish.

رَجُلٌ أَزْرَقُ الْعَيْنِ

rajulun azraqu al-ʿayni

“A blue-eyed man.”

مَاءٌ أَزْرَقُ

māʾun azraqu

“Clear or blue-looking water.”

نَصْلٌ أَزْرَقُ

naṣlun azraqu

“A blue-hued, gleaming blade or spearhead.”

The color description must be understood in relation to the object described.

زَرْقَاء

This feminine form of أَزْرَق describes someone or something blue or bluish.

عَيْنٌ زَرْقَاءُ

ʿaynun zarqāʾu

“A blue or bluish eye.”

It also occurs in personal names and epithets. A proper name alone does not establish its bearer’s actual features or the history of the name.

زُرْق

The plural of أَزْرَق denotes people or objects with blue or bluish coloration.

It can occur without repeating the noun described, as when people are characterized by their eyes or appearance.

Only this form occurs in the Qur’an.

اِزْرَقَّ and اِزْرِقَاق

اِزْرَقَّ means becoming blue or bluish.

اِزْرِقَاق is its verbal noun, describing the appearance or intensification of that color.

اِزْرَقَّتْ عَيْنُهُ

izraqqat ʿaynuhu

“His eye became blue or bluish.”

The form can describe a color change, but its morphology does not specify the cause.

اِزْرَاقَّ

Another recorded color verb, this means becoming blue. Its pattern alone does not establish a particular rate or gradual process of change.

Its structural difference from ازرقّ does not itself imply two entirely different colors.

زَرَقَ يَزْرُقُ

Another branch concerns throwing, casting, or forcefully ejecting something.

زَرَقَهُ بِالْمِزْرَاقِ

zaraqahu bi-al-mizrāqi

“He struck him with a short spear or threw it at him.”

This concerns movement and throwing, not blue coloration.

مِزْرَاق

A short, light spear used for throwing or striking.

The word belongs to the throwing branch.

It should not be equated with أَزْرَق describing a spearhead: one concerns throwing, the other the metal’s color or appearance.

زَرَقَ الطَّائِرُ

This verb also describes a bird ejecting droppings.

It belongs to the throwing or ejecting branch.

Its similarity to the color verb does not make the usages identical.

زَرَقَ بِعَيْنِهِ

It can also mean directing a sharp gaze or turning the eye intensely toward someone.

Here the focus is directing or casting a look.

Some usages also describe the white of the eye appearing as it turns or changes direction.

These concern the eye’s movement and condition, rather than all simply meaning blue.

3. Do all the meanings share one origin?

At least two established branches appear in ز ر ق.

First is blue or bluish coloration, evident in أزرق, زرقاء, and زرق.

Second is throwing or ejecting, as in زرق بالمزراق and زرق الطائر.

The cited evidence does not establish a historical derivational link between them.

Nor does the association of مزراق with throwing, alongside زرق describing spearheads, prove that the color arose from throwing or the name of a spear.

The usages may coexist in a military setting without establishing a history of derivation.

The color branch matters to the Qur’anic usage. In Ṭā Hā 20:102, زُرْقًا is the plural adjective of أزرق, not a derivative expressing throwing.

Driving, being thrown, or being cast away should therefore not replace the color sense in this verse.

4. Qur’anic usage

The root ز ر ق occurs once in the Qur’an.

Ṭā Hā 20:102

يَوْمَ يُنْفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ وَنَحْشُرُ الْمُجْرِمِينَ يَوْمَئِذٍ زُرْقًا

yawma yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri wa-naḥshuru al-mujrimīna yawmaʾidhin zurqan

Ṭā Hā 20:102

Translation:

“The day the Trumpet is blown, and We gather the offenders that day, bluish in appearance.”

زُرْقًا is the accusative plural of أزرق.

It functions as a circumstantial description of الْمُجْرِمِينَ.

The offenders are thus described, at their gathering, in a condition associated with blue or bluish coloration.

زرقا alone does not state whether this concerns the whole body, the eyes, or another part of their appearance.

Arabic أزرق applied to a person can refer to blue eyes. Accordingly, “blue-eyed” is a major interpretation.

In the resurrection context, however, it may describe an extraordinary and fearful condition. Early explanations connect it with altered eye appearance, blindness, thirst, or severe fear.

Choosing a rendering requires distinguishing the lexical color sense from explanations of the condition in the Hereafter.

5. Zurqan: eye color or a sign of distress?

First reading: blue or bluish eyes

زُرْقًا is morphologically the plural of أزرق, whose color sense provides the basis of this reading.

Arabic also applies the adjective to blue-eyed people.

On this reading, the verse may describe offenders gathered with blue or bluish eyes. Change is an interpretive possibility, not something the adjective alone establishes.

One distinction is essential: blue eye color has no inherent negative moral meaning in the root itself.

Blue eyes, like brown or dark eyes, are a natural human feature. Eye color alone provides no basis for assigning different moral or religious worth.

Some historical cultures associated eye color with ethnicity, fear, hostility, or ill omen. Such cultural judgments should not be made into divine rulings.

The verse identifies the group through their wrongdoing. زرقا describes their condition at the gathering; it does not make eye color a criterion of guilt in earthly life.

Second reading: coloration attributed to intense thirst

Some early explanations interpret زرقا as thirsty people, proposing severe thirst as an explanation for the appearance of their eyes. This is an account of an interpretation, not an independent medical conclusion.

In this explanation, thirst is proposed as the cause of the color rather than the root’s primary meaning.

The distinction matters.

Thirst is not the basic color meaning of زرق. Rendering the verse “thirsty” adopts an interpretive explanation in place of the color expression.

Maryam 19:86 describes offenders being driven toward Hell as وِرْدًا, an expression also explained in terms of thirst.

That verse is relevant to their condition at resurrection, but it does not by itself establish that زرقا in Ṭā Hā 20:102 must mean thirsty.

Third reading: blindness

Some lexical and exegetical explanations also render زرقا as blind people.

They associate the expression with an altered appearance of the dark part of the eye. This explains the historical reading; it does not diagnose the cause or nature of an eye condition from the verse.

The expression then concerns the eye’s appearance, without becoming fully synonymous with عمى.

This reading can be considered alongside Ṭā Hā 20:124 and Ṭā Hā 20:125, where a person is gathered blind and asks about that condition.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:97 also describes people gathered at resurrection as blind, mute, and deaf.

These verses may support considering a connection with vision.

Yet the explicit word أعمى elsewhere is not sufficient reason to substitute “blind” for زرقا without explanation.

The Qur’an uses a separate word for blindness and a different expression in Ṭā Hā 20:102. Translation should acknowledge that distinction even if the conditions are related in the Hereafter.

Fourth reading: altered appearance associated with fear

Another explanation connects the coloration with the eyes’ appearance during intense terror.

This reading emphasizes a fixed gaze and a distressed appearance. The word itself establishes no particular bodily mechanism.

It understands زرقا as part of the offenders’ dreadful condition at the gathering.

The context is compatible with anxiety: in Ṭā Hā 20:103 they speak quietly among themselves, and Ṭā Hā 20:104 contrasts estimates of their previous stay.

Fear is not the lexical meaning of زرق. It is inferred from context or proposed as an explanation of the appearance.

“Frightened” or “terrified” is therefore not a direct lexical rendering of زرقا.

6. The context of Ṭā Hā 20:102

Ṭā Hā 20:100 concerns someone turning away from the divine reminder and carrying a burden at resurrection.

Ṭā Hā 20:101 describes that burden’s grievous nature.

Ṭā Hā 20:102 then introduces the blowing of the Trumpet and the gathering of offenders described as زرقا.

In Ṭā Hā 20:103, they speak quietly and regard their earlier stay as very short.

This sequence places زرقا within the portrayal of offenders at the gathering.

Color or appearance matters as a condition accompanying their gathering and accountability.

The verse itself, however, names no cause of the coloration.

A suitable approach preserves the color sense in translation and discusses thirst, blindness, and fear as interpretive possibilities.

Does زرقا describe the whole body?

Syntactically, زرقا describes المجرمين circumstantially: the description is attributed to the offenders themselves.

Arabic commonly describes a person through one bodily feature. Someone called أزرق may be described by eye color.

Attribution to المجرمين therefore does not prove that their whole bodies are blue or bluish.

Conversely, the verse does not explicitly mention eyes, so restricting the description to the eyes alone is not conclusively established by its wording.

Both points matter: the description applies to the offenders, but the exact bodily location is not named.

Does the verse describe a permanent feature?

زرقا is a circumstantial expression.

It describes the offenders at the gathering.

This alone does not establish that the color was present from their creation or remains unchanged through every stage of the Hereafter.

Nor can earthly experiences of eyes and color conclusively determine all bodily details of that condition.

The verse identifies a condition without explaining every aspect of it.

7. Comparison with related roots

ز ر ق (زُرْقَة) and ع م ي (عَمَى)

عمى denotes blindness and is used in the Qur’an for lack of sight and figuratively for failure to perceive truth.

The color branch of زرق describes color or appearance.

An association between blindness and altered appearance in some explanations does not make the words synonymous.

Ṭā Hā 20:102 uses زرقا; Ṭā Hā 20:124 uses أعمى. Each verse should be examined with that distinction intact.

ز ر ق (زُرْقَة) and ب ي ض (بَيَاض)

بياض denotes whiteness.

Some early explanations of زرق concern whiteness becoming visible in the eye.

The roots nevertheless remain distinct.

Yūsuf 12:84 describes Jacob’s eyes becoming white from grief.

The verse shows that eye appearance can be described through color, but it does not establish that ابيضت and زرقا describe exactly the same condition.

ز ر ق (زُرْقَة) and خ ض ر (خُضْرَة)

خضرة denotes greenness.

Early lexical descriptions do not always align with modern color categories; blue and green-related descriptions both appear for some eye colors.

This overlap does not make زرق and خضر wholly identical.

Qur’anic زرقا should be translated within its own attested range, not replaced by any neighboring color mentioned in a dictionary.

ز ر ق (زُرْقَة) and ع ط ش (عَطَش)

عطش denotes thirst.

The relevant branch of زرق concerns color and appearance.

Some explanations of Ṭā Hā 20:102 propose thirst as a cause of the offenders’ eye color or appearance.

A proposed cause is not the word’s basic meaning.

A basic translation should therefore not replace زرقا with “thirsty” unless its interpretive nature is made clear.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents suit the lexical forms. These proposals do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored explanations.

زُرْقَة: blueness or bluish coloration.

زَرِقَتْ عَيْنُهُ: his eye became blue or bluish, or had that color.

أَزْرَق: blue or bluish.

زَرْقَاء: the feminine of أزرق; blue or bluish.

زُرْق: the plural of أزرق; blue or bluish people or things, depending on the noun described.

اِزْرَقَّ: became blue or bluish.

مِزْرَاق: a short, light spear for throwing or striking.

زَرَقَهُ بِالْمِزْرَاقِ: struck him with a spear or threw it at him.

زَرَقَ الطَّائِرُ: the bird ejected its droppings.

زَرَقَ بِعَيْنِهِ: directed a sharp gaze toward someone.

Proposed rendering of Ṭā Hā 20:102

For the verse:

يَوْمَ يُنْفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ وَنَحْشُرُ الْمُجْرِمِينَ يَوْمَئِذٍ زُرْقًا

yawma yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri wa-naḥshuru al-mujrimīna yawmaʾidhin zurqan

Ṭā Hā 20:102

Proposed translation:

“The day the Trumpet is blown, and We gather the offenders that day, bluish in appearance.”

This retains the color sense without conclusively restricting its location to the eyes or the whole body.

A note can explain that زرقا is the plural of أزرق and may concern the bluish appearance of the offenders’ eyes or appearance more generally.

For the brief root definition:

“Blue or bluish coloration; in another branch, throwing.”

For the verse-specific meaning:

“Bluish in appearance; describing the offenders at the gathering.”

“Thirsty,” “blind,” and “terrified” reflect interpretive readings rather than direct equivalents of the color expression.

9. Conclusion

The color branch of ز ر ق is attested in poetry transmitted from pre-Islamic Arabic.

The poems describe clear water and gleaming spearheads. Broader lexical accounts also record blue, greenishness, or whiteness within the eye’s dark part; these descriptions should not all be dated to one period without evidence.

Arabic also records زرق for throwing and ejecting, but its historical connection with the color branch is not established here.

Only زُرْقًا occurs in the Qur’an, in Ṭā Hā 20:102.

It is the plural of أزرق and describes the offenders at the gathering.

Explanations include eye coloration, blindness, thirst, and intense fear. None should replace the word’s basic meaning without explanation and evidence.

Blue eyes have no inherent negative moral value. The verse describes offenders at resurrection; it does not judge earthly people by eye color.

The relevant lexical meaning is blue or bluish coloration, and زرقا describes an appearance at the gathering. The expression alone does not conclusively identify its location or cause, or establish a change from a previous color.

This preserves the attested linguistic meaning while distinguishing it from interpretations of the condition in the Hereafter.
