Central meaning: having little desire for something, regarding it as of little worth, or turning away from it; another branch concerns small quantities, limited possessions, or scant resources.

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root z h d concerns two related ideas: littleness and lack of desire.

In one branch, zahīd describes something small in quantity, value, or benefit. Māl zahīd means little wealth, and ʿaṭāʾ zahīd a meagre gift.

In another branch, zahida fī al-shayʾ means having little desire for something, undervaluing it, or turning away from it. Someone described as zāhid in relation to something shows little interest in obtaining or retaining it.

These meanings are related but distinct.

Something may be unwanted because it is small or of little value. Yet something truly valuable may also be unwanted through ignorance, indifference, or a preference for another advantage.

A person's lack of desire therefore does not establish the object's actual worth.

An early lexical analysis identifies the root's underlying meaning as follows:

أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى قِلَّةِ الشَّيْءِ

aṣlun yadullu ʿalā qillati al-shayʾ

“A fundamental sense indicating the littleness of something.”

This analysis highlights paucity. Limited desire and low estimation can also be understood within this semantic field, without proving the historical direction of derivation.

Claims about pre-Islamic usage nevertheless require evidence from that period.

First poetic witness: poverty in al-Aʿshā

Al-Aʿshā, a pre-Islamic poet, praises people who protect a woman under their care:

وَقَوْمُكَ إِنْ يَضْمَنُوا جَارَةً
يَكُونُوا بِمَوْضِعِ أَنْضَادِهَا

wa-qawmuka in yaḍmanū jāratan / yakūnū bi-mawḍiʿi anḍādihā

“If your people take a woman into their protection, they stand as her supporters.”

فَلَنْ يَطْلُبُوا سِرَّهَا لِلْغِنَى
وَلَنْ يُسْلِمُوهَا لِإِزْهَادِهَا

fa-lan yaṭlubū sirrahā li-l-ghinā / wa-lan yuslimūhā li-izhādihā

The sense of these lines is:

“If your people take a woman into their protection, they stand as her supporters; they neither seek intimacy with her for her wealth nor surrender her because of her poverty.”

Here izhādihā concerns the woman's limited possessions and poverty.

The poet contrasts ghinā with izhād.

The former denotes wealth and the latter limited resources.

Izhād here does not mean piety, worship, or voluntary abstinence from life's benefits. A woman may be poor through circumstances without choosing poverty or lacking desire for wealth.

The verse attributed to al-Aʿshā provides pre-Islamic poetic evidence for this family's use concerning limited possessions.

It also distinguishes poverty from voluntary lack of interest. A poor person may want wealth, whereas a wealthy person may have no interest in a particular thing.

Izhād here describes an economic condition, not an ethical virtue.

Second poetic witness: low estimation in ʿAdī ibn Zayd

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd reads:

وَلَلْبَخْلَةُ الْأُولَى لِمَنْ كَانَ بَاخِلًا
أَعَفُّ وَمَنْ يَبْخَلْ يُلَمْ وَيُزَهَّدْ

wa-la-l-bakhlatu al-ūlā li-man kāna bākhilan / aʿaffu wa-man yabkhal yulam wa-yuzahhad

The sense of the line is:

“For one who is miserly, refusal at the outset is more decorous; whoever acts miserly is blamed and deemed mean.” The first clause is rendered cautiously; the lexical witness lies in the closing verb.

Yuzahhad here means being deemed miserly and mean, and thus held in low esteem.

The poet describes someone whose miserly conduct diminishes his standing in others' eyes.

This does not describe the amount of his wealth. A wealthy person can be despised for miserliness.

The witness illustrates another application: regarding a person or thing as insignificant or unworthy of esteem.

The precise date of this line is not independently established. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet should be distinguished from certain dating of the individual verse.

What the early evidence supports

These witnesses and recorded Arabic usages allow two principal strands to be distinguished:

Actual paucity: something is small in quantity, possessions, or benefit.

Low estimation and limited desire: someone regards a person or thing as undesirable or of little worth and has little wish to possess or retain it.

Al-Aʿshā's witness illustrates limited possessions; the line attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd illustrates low estimation of another person.

These witnesses alone do not establish a fully developed technical concept of ascetic worship or renunciation of worldly pleasures in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Religious and ethical terminology must be examined separately from the general root meaning and its historical development.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Zahida, yazhadu

This basic verb means having little desire, being disinclined, or turning away from something.

زَهِدَ فِي الشَّيْءِ

zahida fī al-shayʾ

“He had little desire for the thing.”

زَهِدَ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ

zahida ʿani al-shayʾ

“He turned away from the thing.”

Both constructions, with fī and ʿan, are recorded.

The former can emphasize lack of interest, and the latter turning away, but this is not an absolute rule governing every occurrence.

In many uses they occupy the same semantic field.

Zahada and zahuda

Other vocalizations of the verb, with a or u on the middle consonant, are also recorded.

These relate to paucity, disinterest, and turning away.

Differences in vocalization alone do not establish different ethical meanings or degrees of piety.

Zuhd and zahādah

These are recognized verbal nouns.

At the general semantic level, they concern limited desire. Some early lexical descriptions distinguish the religious use of zuhd from the wider application of zahādah.

The underlying lack of interest can concern ordinary objects, such as merchandise, as well as religious subjects.

In religious usage, zuhd also denotes reduced desire for worldly advantages and freedom from attachment to them.

The family's semantic range nevertheless extends beyond religious matters.

Zāhid

This is an active participle from the verb.

It describes someone who has little desire for something or does not consider it especially desirable.

زَاهِدٌ فِي الْمَالِ

zāhidun fī al-māl

“Someone with little desire for wealth.”

زَاهِدٌ فِي الشَّيْءِ

zāhidun fī al-shayʾ

“Someone who does not find the thing desirable.”

The description alone does not identify the reason for this attitude.

Knowledge, contentment, independence, ignorance, fear, or a preference for something else may account for it.

Only the plural of this participle occurs in the Quran.

Zuhhād and zāhidūn

These are plural forms of the active participle.

In religious usage, zuhhād describes people known for asceticism and restraint concerning worldly attachments.

Zāhidūn is the sound masculine plural of zāhid.

Yūsuf 12:20 uses al-zāhidīn, in the genitive after the preposition min.

Zahīd

This describes something scant or of little value.

مَالٌ زَهِيدٌ

mālun zahīd

“Little wealth.”

عَطَاءٌ زَهِيدٌ

ʿaṭāʾun zahīd

“A meagre gift.”

Applied to a person, zahīd can describe someone who eats little or is content with little.

It is also recorded for a miserly or insignificant person.

Context must distinguish these meanings.

Zahīd, describing paucity, is not identical to zāhid, describing someone with little desire.

Zahīd al-ʿayn

This describes a person satisfied with little, whose expectations are modest.

Its opposite, raghīb al-ʿayn, describes someone not satisfied with little and wanting more.

The expressions contrast limited and abundant desire.

Zahīd al-ʿayn does not necessarily imply poverty, worship, or piety.

Muzhid

This describes someone with limited wealth or resources.

رَجُلٌ مُزْهِدٌ

rajulun muzhid

“A man of limited means.”

The focus is his possessions, not his ethical choices.

A poor person may strongly desire wealth.

Muzhid in this sense is therefore not necessarily zāhid in the sense of disinterested.

Azhada, izhādan

In the financial branch, this describes having little wealth or becoming poor.

أَزْهَدَ الرَّجُلُ

azhada al-rajul

“The man's possessions were scant.”

This is the branch illustrated by izhād in al-Aʿshā's poem.

Zahhada, yuzahhidu

This is a Form II verb.

One use means making someone disinclined toward something.

زَهَّدَهُ فِي الْأَمْرِ

zahhadahu fī al-amr

“He made him disinclined toward the matter.”

Another recorded use means deeming someone insignificant or miserly.

Tazhīd can therefore concern reducing someone's desire or lowering another person's standing in one's estimation.

Tazahhada

In religious usage, this means practising asceticism or adopting an ascetic way of life.

It is also associated with worship and withdrawal from worldly attachments.

This connects with the general idea of limited desire, while acquiring a wider technical and historical application.

The features of later ascetic practice should not all be imported into al-zāhidīn in Joseph's story.

Tazāhada and izdahada

These can describe devaluing something or regarding its amount as insignificant.

اِزْدَهَدَ الْعَطَاءَ

izdahada al-ʿaṭāʾ

“He regarded the gift as scant.”

The central act here is an evaluation of quantity or value as meagre.

Zahīd al-arḍ and zahīd al-wādī

In descriptions of terrain, zahīd can denote narrow ground yielding little water, or a watercourse that takes little water.

وَادٍ زَهِيدٌ

wādin zahīd

“A watercourse that takes little water.” Some descriptions concern a hard bed where even a small amount of water produces a flow; this does not necessarily describe poor water retention or drought.

This illustrates paucity in a physical setting.

Zahād al-tilāʿ

This denotes small gullies and channels in the ground.

Smallness is again prominent.

Zahada al-nakhla

Another recorded use means estimating the yield of date palms.

It concerns assessment and estimation of quantity.

Its historical relationship to paucity and disinterest is not certain. It should not be made the origin of every meaning without evidence.

Of these forms, only al-zāhidīn occurs in the Quran.

3. Quranic usage

The root occurs once in the Quran, at Yūsuf 12:20.

Yūsuf 12:20

وَشَرَوْهُ بِثَمَنٍ بَخْسٍ دَرَاهِمَ مَعْدُودَةٍ وَكَانُوا فِيهِ مِنَ الزَّاهِدِينَ

wa-sharawhu bi-thamanin bakhsin darāhima maʿdūdatin wa-kānū fīhi mina al-zāhidīn

Yūsuf 12:20

Translation:

“They sold him for a paltry price, a few counted dirhams, and had little desire for him.”

Al-zāhidīn is the sound masculine plural of the active participle zāhid.

Wa-kānū refers to the group whose attitude the clause describes.

Fīhi consists of a preposition and a pronoun.

Mina al-zāhidīn places them among those with little desire for the referent.

Here it describes people uninterested in retaining the person concerned or benefiting from his presence, and assigning him little value.

It does not denote ascetic worship.

What does fīhi refer to?

The straightforward reading takes the pronoun as referring to Joseph.

They sold Joseph cheaply and had little desire for him.

Another grammatical and interpretive reading takes the pronoun as referring to the price.

On that reading, they had little interest in the payment and were not primarily seeking a large sum.

This is associated with the reading in which Joseph's brothers wanted to distance him from their father.

Reference to Joseph remains the clearer reading of the sentence: he is the person sold and undervalued in the transaction.

“They had little desire for him” suits the basic translation; the alternative may be discussed in commentary.

Who were al-zāhidīn?

In Yūsuf 12:19 a caravan reaches the well, and its water-drawer finds a boy while drawing water.

The passage then says they concealed him as merchandise.

Yūsuf 12:20 describes selling him for a paltry price and having little interest in him.

In Yūsuf 12:21 the Egyptian buyer tells his wife to treat him honourably: he may benefit them, or they may adopt him.

The sequence shows Joseph being traded and a group assigning him little worth.

There are two principal readings of the sellers' identity.

One identifies them as caravan members who retrieved Joseph and concealed him as merchandise.

The other assigns a role in the sale to Joseph's brothers, taking al-zāhidīn as their lack of interest in him.

Earlier verses explain the brothers' wish to remove Joseph, but do not explicitly detail every stage of the transaction.

Names, exact numbers of dirhams, or conversations from outside accounts should therefore not be inserted into the lexical meaning.

For this root study, the relevant point is the disinterest of those who treated Joseph as an object of trade.

4. Disinterest and actual worth

A significant distinction is between someone's valuation and actual worth.

In Yūsuf 12:20 some people have little interest in Joseph.

They are willing to part with him for a paltry price.

In Yūsuf 12:21, however, the Egyptian buyer sees possible benefit to his household or the prospect of adoption.

The same verse describes The God establishing Joseph in the land and teaching him the interpretation of accounts.

Yūsuf 12:22 also describes his receiving judgment and knowledge.

The sellers' disinterest was therefore no measure of Joseph's actual worth.

They may have failed to recognize his human worth, abilities, and future, or may have disregarded them.

The Quran presents a life trajectory exceeding their limited assessment.

Al-zāhidīn does not mean Joseph was actually insignificant.

It describes their lack of desire to retain him.

The distinction between a person's disinterest and the referent's actual worth is central to understanding this usage.

Lack of desire and a paltry price

Bi-thamanin bakhsin concerns a low and inadequate price.

Bakhs belongs to another root and concerns diminishing a due, giving less, or an inadequate amount.

Al-zāhidīn describes the sellers' attitude.

Two facts are thus placed side by side:

Joseph was traded for a paltry price.

Those who parted with him had little desire for him.

The two facts are related but not interchangeable.

Someone can dislike a commodity yet sell it for its actual value.

Someone can also value something highly yet part with it cheaply through need or compulsion.

Thaman bakhs and al-zāhidīn are separate components of the verse's description.

Is zuhd a virtue here?

In religious terminology, zuhd is often regarded as praiseworthy.

Yet Yūsuf 12:20 applies the participle to people who had little interest in Joseph and parted with him cheaply.

The narrative does not present this attitude as exemplary conduct or spiritual virtue.

Not every lack of desire is intrinsically commendable.

Turning away from oppression or illicit gain can be right.

Lacking interest in truth, justice, knowledge, another human being, or a responsibility is not necessarily virtuous.

The ethical value depends on the object and reason for the attitude.

The Quranic occurrence supports that distinction: being disinterested in Joseph confers no ethical superiority.

5. Lexical meaning and religious asceticism

In religious and ethical terminology, zuhd commonly denotes reducing attachment to wealth, status, comfort, and worldly pleasure.

Some traditions extend this to abandoning many amenities, stringent simplicity, or withdrawal from material enjoyment.

Others understand it as freedom from dependence on possessions, with truth and responsibility taking priority over self-interest.

These views have their place in religious thought, but should not all be imported into the Quranic participle.

Yūsuf 12:20 describes people uninterested in a person, not people renouncing possessions and pleasures to draw closer to The God.

Does poverty itself establish virtue?

In Arabic, muzhid can describe someone with little wealth.

That economic meaning differs from voluntary disinterest.

Poverty can arise from lack of opportunity, oppression, illness, loss of possessions, or social conditions.

Conversely, a wealthy person may avoid excessive attachment and use possessions responsibly.

Poverty alone does not establish moral superiority, nor wealth alone a lack of piety.

Al-Baqarah 2:201 records a prayer for good in this world and the Hereafter.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:32 questions those who prohibit the adornment and wholesome provision of The God.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:77 combines seeking the Hereafter with not forgetting one's share of this world.

These passages do not condemn worldly benefits in themselves.

Elsewhere the Quran condemns greed, miserliness, extravagance, corruption, and preferring worldly interests to truth.

The ethical question concerns acquisition, expenditure, and the place possessions occupy in life, rather than their mere presence or absence.

Asceticism and abandoning responsibility

The lexical meaning does not establish a duty to withdraw from work, production, building, family life, or lawful benefits.

Such a ruling would require independent Quranic evidence.

Someone may reject unnecessary luxury while working hard to support a family and assist others.

Another person may have abundant resources acquired properly and used beneficially.

No single economic or social lifestyle can be derived from this root as a universal religious duty.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, concerning disinterest in Joseph.

That limit must guide the study's conclusions.

6. Related roots and distinctions

Z H D and R GH B: desire

Raghbah concerns inclination and wanting something.

In its attitudinal branch, zuhd contrasts with desire.

Raghiba fī al-shayʾ means “he desired the thing.”

Zahida fī al-shayʾ means “he had little desire for it.”

Both attitudes depend on their objects.

Desire for knowledge and good action can be commendable; desire for oppression and corruption is not.

Similarly, disinterest in illicit wealth may be right, whereas disinterest in truth and justice is no virtue.

Neither root has an invariant ethical value independent of its object.

Z H D and Q N ʿ: contentment

Qanāʿah commonly concerns being satisfied with what one has.

Zuhd concerns limited desire for something.

They can coexist but are distinct.

A person may want something yet accept less because of circumstances.

Another may have no interest in it and make no effort to obtain it.

Zahīd al-ʿayn highlights satisfaction with little, but this overlap does not reduce the whole root to contentment.

Z H D and B KH L: miserliness

Bukhl concerns withholding gifts or expenditure where giving is appropriate.

Zuhd concerns limited desire.

A miser may be strongly attached to wealth while refusing to share it.

Someone with little desire for wealth may readily give it away.

The lexical use yuzahhad can mean being deemed miserly and mean; this does not make miserliness identical to disinterest.

Zāhid should therefore not be treated as a general synonym for miser.

Z H D and F Q R: poverty

Faqr concerns need and limited resources.

The attitudinal branch of zuhd concerns limited desire.

A poor person may want wealth, while a wealthy person may have no interest in something particular.

Economic circumstances and inward inclination must be distinguished.

Muzhid and izhād do concern poverty in the branch associated with limited possessions.

That overlap in particular derivatives does not make the two families identical.

Z H D and W R ʿ: scrupulous restraint

In ethical usage, waraʿ concerns avoiding conduct regarded as wrong or prohibited.

Zuhd concerns limited desire.

Someone may want to do something illicit yet refrain despite that desire.

This is restraint, but not necessarily absence of inclination.

Another may have no desire to act without being motivated by ethical restraint or knowledge of prohibition.

Zuhd and waraʿ can be related in particular situations, but are not identical.

Z H D and ʿ R Ḍ: turning away

Iʿrāḍ concerns turning away or withholding attention.

Zuhd may lead to such conduct.

Yet someone can be uninterested in something while dealing with it out of duty or necessity.

Someone can also deliberately turn away from something attractive.

Iʿrāḍ foregrounds turning away, whereas zuhd may describe the attitude of limited desire itself.

Z H D and B KH S: devaluation

These roots appear together in Yūsuf 12:20.

Bakhs concerns diminishing a due or inadequacy of amount or value.

Zāhid describes someone with little desire for the referent.

Bakhs characterizes the price, while al-zāhidīn characterizes the group's attitude.

Translation and explanation should preserve this distinction.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following renderings distinguish important forms. These research proposals do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

Zahida fī al-shayʾ: had little desire for it; did not consider it especially desirable.

Zahida ʿan al-shayʾ: turned away from it; was disinclined toward it.

Zuhd: limited desire, disinterest, or turning away.

Zahādah: limited desire; in the branch of paucity, limited amount or resources.

Zāhid: disinterested in something; in religious terminology, an ascetic.

Zuhhād: plural of zāhid; ascetics or those with little desire, depending on context.

Zahīd: scant, meagre, or insignificant.

Māl zahīd: little wealth.

ʿAṭāʾ zahīd: a meagre gift.

Zahīd al-ʿayn: satisfied with little.

Muzhid: of limited means; poor.

Azhada, izhādan: had little wealth; became poor.

Zahhadahu fī al-amr: made him disinclined toward the matter.

Tazahhada: practised asceticism; adopted an ascetic lifestyle in religious usage.

Izdahada al-ʿaṭāʾ: regarded the gift as scant.

Wādin zahīd: a watercourse that takes little water; the precise physical meaning depends on context.

Zahada al-nakhla: estimated the yield of date palms in the relevant lexical use.

Proposed rendering of Yūsuf 12:20

وَشَرَوْهُ بِثَمَنٍ بَخْسٍ دَرَاهِمَ مَعْدُودَةٍ وَكَانُوا فِيهِ مِنَ الزَّاهِدِينَ

wa-sharawhu bi-thamanin bakhsin darāhima maʿdūdatin wa-kānū fīhi mina al-zāhidīn

Yūsuf 12:20

Proposed translation:

“They sold him for a paltry price, a few counted dirhams, and had little desire for him.”

This accords with the participle and the narrative context.

For a brief root summary:

“Limited desire; in another branch, small quantity or value.”

For the verse-specific meaning:

“Those with little desire for Joseph, who did not wish to retain him.”

“Ascetics,” “the pious,” or “devout worshippers” would impose technical religious meanings on this occurrence.

“The poor” and “those of limited means” are also inappropriate here: the participle does not describe their economic circumstances.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root z h d concerns paucity and limited desire.

Its quantitative branch concerns small amounts of wealth, benefit, or value. Its attitudinal branch concerns disinterest, low estimation, or turning away.

Al-Aʿshā's poetic witness illustrates izhād as poverty, while the verse attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd illustrates deeming someone mean and insignificant.

These witnesses do not establish every later technical feature of ascetic renunciation in pre-Islamic usage.

Only al-zāhidīn occurs in the Quran, at Yūsuf 12:20.

It describes those who had little interest in Joseph and parted with him cheaply.

Their attitude is not a sign of virtue or piety in this context.

Nor does their disinterest establish Joseph's actual worth. The narrative reveals a standing and future far beyond their limited evaluation.

Limited desire for something is not necessarily equivalent to its having little actual value.

In this Quranic occurrence, the meaning is disinterest or lack of desire. It may arise from low estimation, indifference, or another preference, but establishes no ethical virtue by itself.

The Quranic usage should therefore not be equated with piety, poverty, or worship without considering its object and context. The ethical value of disinterest depends on what a person turns away from and why.
