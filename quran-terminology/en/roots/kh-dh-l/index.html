Forsaking, withdrawing help, and being left unsupported where assistance is needed

In Arabic, KH DH L concerns forsaking, withdrawing companionship, and withholding help. Khidhlān describes a situation in which someone needs another’s assistance, but the person expected to help fails to do so.

Early uses extend beyond moral betrayal. The same family describes a gazelle remaining apart from its herd, a horse stopping, or legs losing the ability to support someone’s movement. Separation, falling behind, and the discontinuation of companionship are prominent in these examples.

In human relationships, however, forsaking can acquire an ethical significance. Someone who leaves a friend or companion unsupported in need, despite an obligation and ability to help, may commit khidhlān. Here the issue is more than an absence of help: support that someone hoped for or relied upon has failed.

The Qur’an uses this root in three verses. In Āl ʿImrān 3:160, khidhlān contrasts with divine help. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:22, مَخْذُول (makhdhūl) describes someone left blameworthy and unsupported through setting another deity alongside God. In Al-Furqān 25:29, Satan is خَذُول (khadhūl): one who draws someone along a path but fails to help when its consequences arrive.

Help and abandonment feature in all three Qur’anic uses. Nevertheless, God’s withholding of help, the condition of the forsaken person, and Satan’s abandonment must not be equated in cause, responsibility, or ethical character.

1. Root forms and semantic core

KH DH L occurs three times in the Qur’an, in three morphological forms: يَخْذُلْكُمْ (yakhdhulkum) in Āl ʿImrān 3:160, مَخْذُولًا (makhdhūlan) in Al-Isrāʾ 17:22, and خَذُولًا (khadhūlan) in Al-Furqān 25:29.

Arabic also records other members of this family.

  • خَذَلَ ـ يَخْذُلُ (khadhala–yakhdhulu): to forsake someone, withdraw help, or fail to support them when assistance is needed.
  • خَذْل and خِذْلَان (khadhl and khidhlān): forsaking and withholding help.
  • خَاذِل (khādhil): someone who fails to help another and leaves them unsupported.
  • خَذُول (khadhūl): someone characterized by forsaking and withholding help; in animal usage, an animal remaining apart from the herd.
  • خُذَلَة (khudhalah): someone who frequently forsakes others or refuses to help them.
  • مَخْذُول (makhdhūl): someone unaided or deprived of support.
  • خَذَّلَ (khadhdhala): to deter someone from helping another or induce them to withdraw assistance and companionship.
  • تَخْذِيل (takhdhīl): deterring or discouraging others from helping and supporting.
  • تَخَاذَلَ (takhādhala): in human relationships, to forsake one another; in bodily usage, for limbs to become weak or unable to function.
  • تَخَاذُل (takhādhul): mutual failure to help, or weakening cooperation and support.

These forms are recorded in lexicons, but only the three forms specified above occur in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three forms, a verb, passive participle, and adjective

The semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one underlying principle for this entry:

الْخَاءُ وَالذَّالُ وَاللَّامُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَرْكِ الشَّيْءِ وَالْقُعُودِ عَنْهُ

al-khāʾu wa-al-dhālu wa-al-lāmu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā tarki al-shayʾi wa-al-quʿūdi ʿanhu

That is, the root denotes leaving something and holding back from it.

He then explains khidhlān:

فَالْخِذْلَانُ: تَرْكُ الْمَعُونَةِ

fa-al-khidhlānu tarku al-maʿūnati

That is, khidhlān means withholding assistance.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: forsaking and withholding assistance

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

الْخَاذِلُ ضِدُّ النَّاصِرِ

al-khādhilu ḍiddu al-nāṣiri

That is, khādhil stands opposite nāṣir, a helper.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains khidhlān as withholding help by someone expected to provide assistance.

These definitions indicate two related aspects: withdrawing companionship, and failing to help where assistance matters.

The two aspects do not always have the same ethical character, however.

Someone might leave a group to fulfill another responsibility, for example. Departure alone is not betrayal.

Conversely, someone might promise support in danger yet knowingly abandon the person concerned. Separation and withholding help then acquire a negative ethical meaning.

The root’s semantic core can therefore be described as forsaking, withdrawing companionship, or withholding help and support, especially where continued companionship or assistance is expected.

The reason for withdrawal, the person’s ability to help, and their ethical responsibility must be identified from context, rather than from the root alone.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: the opposition of khādhil and nāṣir and expected help

2. Early Arabic usage: from a gazelle apart from its herd to withholding help

A gazelle remaining apart from the herd

An important early Arabic use concerns gazelles and other animals remaining apart from their herd.

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes Al-Aṣmaʿī: when a gazelle falls behind the herd, it is said to have khadhala.

Khadhūl is also recorded for a female gazelle remaining apart from its companions and standing by its young.

Here the gazelle may leave the herd to care for its offspring.

What is linguistically prominent is remaining apart and no longer moving with the herd, rather than necessarily disloyalty or betrayal.

A pre-Islamic witness: khadhūl in Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s Muʿallaqah

In his celebrated Muʿallaqah, Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says:

خَذُولٌ تُرَاعِي رَبْرَبًا بِخَمِيلَةٍ
تَنَاوَلُ أَطْرَافَ الْبَرِيرِ وَتَرْتَدِي

khadhūlun turāʿī rabraban bi-khamīlatin / tanāwalu aṭrāfa al-barīri wa-tartadī

An approximate rendering of the sense:

“A gazelle remaining apart from the herd, watching a herd in a wooded place; reaching the fruit-bearing branches of the arāk, as though clothing herself in them.”

Here, khadhūl describes a female gazelle amid trees and vegetation.

Early commentaries explain it as a gazelle that has left the herd and remained beside its young.

Some commentaries give a different explanation, involving separation from its young or association with the herd. This disagreement shows that khadhūl alone cannot determine every detail of the poetic scene.

The lexical explanations nevertheless share a clear sense: separation or failure to continue an earlier association.

The poet uses the animal’s image to describe the beloved’s beauty and bearing. Khadhūl here is therefore not a moral judgment on either the woman or the gazelle.

This witness matters because neither the faʿūl pattern nor khadhūl invariably denotes a blameworthy traitor.

Al-Furqān 25:29 uses the same morphological form of Satan, but its negative force emerges from the referent and the verse’s context.

Al-Khaṭīb al-Tabrīzī’s Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-ʿAshr: Ṭarafah and the gazelle apart from its herd

Separation from a whole

Early Arabic does not restrict KH DH L to animals and human beings.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd describes a horse:

فَهُوَ كَالدَّلْوِ بِكَفِّ الْمُسْتَقِي
خَذَلَتْ عَنْهُ الْعَرَاقِي فَانْجَذَمْ

fa-huwa ka-al-dalwi bi-kaffi al-mustaqī / khadhalat ʿanhu al-ʿarāqī fa-injadham

An approximate rendering of the sense:

“He is like a bucket in the water-drawer’s hand, its supporting crosspieces having come away from it, leaving it severed.”

Here, عَرَاقِي (ʿarāqī) is the plural of عَرْقُوَة (ʿarqūwah): wooden crosspieces fastened across a bucket’s mouth to support it and help raise it, rather than the rope or straps themselves.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab, ʿ-r-q: ʿarāqī, the bucket’s wooden crosspieces

خَذَلَتْ عَنْهُ (khadhalat ʿanhu) describes their coming away.

The issue is not moral support or breaking a promise. Parts of the bucket separate, destroying the connection through which the device functioned.

This witness clarifies the root’s material field: something ceases to accompany or remain connected with a whole.

It does not establish that every instance of khidhlān involves physical separation. Someone may remain beside another person yet fail to help when needed.

Legs becoming weak

Lexicons record تَخَاذَلَتْ رِجْلَاهُ (takhādhalat rijlāhu) for someone whose legs have become weak and can no longer support the body’s movement.

Al-Aʿshā’s poetry also uses خَذُولِ الرِّجْلِ (khadhūl al-rijl): someone whose leg is weak or fails to support their movement.

In this use, khidhlān concerns bodily incapacity.

Illness, fatigue, or injury may prevent someone walking as before. Their legs have failed to perform their usual function.

The use relates to discontinued support but carries no moral charge. A person’s legs do not commit betrayal by becoming ill.

Weakness or incapacity must therefore not be equated with deliberate abandonment in human relationships.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ʿAdī’s witness, weak legs, and Al-Aʿshā’s witness

تَخْذِيل (takhdhīl): deterring others from helping

Lexicons explain خَذَّلَ عَنْهُ أَصْحَابَهُ (khadhdhala ʿanhu aṣḥābahu) as inducing someone’s companions to withhold assistance from them.

This differs from khadhala itself.

In khadhala, someone fails to provide help.

In takhdhīl, someone may deter others from helping or encourage weakness and withdrawal.

For example, someone might spread rumors when a group needs cooperation, causing members to lose trust and refuse to help one another.

Such conduct can fall within takhdhīl’s semantic field.

This example must not become a definition of khidhlān itself, however. KH DH L alone does not mean spreading rumors, deception, or creating fear; these may be means of discouraging others from helping.

3. Āl ʿImrān 3:160: withholding help in contrast with divine assistance

إِنْ يَنْصُرْكُمُ اللَّهُ فَلَا غَالِبَ لَكُمْ وَإِنْ يَخْذُلْكُمْ فَمَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يَنْصُرُكُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ وَعَلَى اللَّهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ

in yanṣurkumu allāhu fa-lā ghāliba lakum wa-in yakhdhulkum fa-man dhā alladhī yanṣurukum min baʿdihi wa-ʿalā allāhi fa-l-yatawakkali al-muʾminūna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“If The God helps you, no one can overcome you; if He forsakes you and withholds help, who then can help you after Him? In The God let the believers place their trust.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:160

Here, يَخْذُلْكُمْ (yakhdhulkum) stands directly opposite يَنْصُرْكُمُ (yanṣurkum).

This contrast is fundamental to understanding the root.

Naṣr here concerns God’s help and support.

Khidhlān, in contrast, concerns forsaking and the absence of that assistance.

The verse therefore does not mean that God directly goes to war against believers or becomes their enemy. It concerns the absence of support whose presence would prevent any enemy from overcoming them.

The conditional construction

The verse begins with two conditions:

إِنْ يَنْصُرْكُمُ اللَّهُ (in yanṣurkumu allāhu).

And:

وَإِنْ يَخْذُلْكُمْ (wa-in yakhdhulkum).

Both verbs occur within conditional constructions.

The first condition states the result of divine help: no one will overcome the addressees.

The second poses a question: if God forsakes them, who can help them after Him?

The construction sets two situations against one another.

The verse itself, however, does not say that every military, social, or personal defeat necessarily means that God has forsaken the person or group for a particular sin.

Such a judgment requires independent evidence.

The preceding context in Āl ʿImrān matters for understanding this point.

The relationship with the events at Uḥud

Āl ʿImrān 3:152 describes believers becoming weak, disputing, and disobeying after seeing what they desired.

Āl ʿImrān 3:165 likewise answers a question about the calamity they suffered by saying it came from themselves.

These verses identify human conduct and decisions as affecting events’ outcomes.

Divine help and its withdrawal in Āl ʿImrān 3:160 therefore cannot be separated from the believers’ practical responsibility.

This connection must nevertheless not become a simple rule that every victorious group enjoys God’s moral approval and every defeated group is deprived of His mercy.

The Qur’an considers victory and defeat, testing, human conduct, and divine promises across different verses. An outward outcome alone cannot determine a person’s standing before God.

This verse clearly establishes that no independent power can replace divine assistance.

Does God’s withholding help mean destroying human freedom?

The root’s meaning does not establish that conclusion.

In human usage, khidhlān may mean leaving someone unaided, but the forsaken person does not necessarily lose the ability to decide.

In relation to God too, divine help not being granted must be distinguished from someone being compelled to sin or suffer defeat.

Some early lexical and exegetical explanations describe God’s khidhlān as not granting someone enabling grace or special protection and leaving them to themselves.

This explains a relationship between divine action and human beings, rather than the independent lexical meaning of every KH DH L form.

Qur’anic verses concerning freedom, guidance, repentance, and human responsibility clarify other aspects of this subject.

Āl ʿImrān 3:160 ends:

وَعَلَى اللَّهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ (wa-ʿalā allāhi fa-l-yatawakkali al-muʾminūn).

That is, believers should place their trust in God.

The command shows that the verse is more than a statement about divine power: it also calls its addressees to a particular orientation and conduct.

Trust here concerns ultimate reliance on God. It does not mean abandoning planning or practical responsibility, as the preceding verses discuss believers’ conduct, disagreements, and disobedience.

4. Al-Isrāʾ 17:22: becoming forsaken through associating another deity with God

لَا تَجْعَلْ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا آخَرَ فَتَقْعُدَ مَذْمُومًا مَخْذُولًا

lā tajʿal maʿa allāhi ilāhan ākhara fa-taqʿuda madhmūman makhdhūlan

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Do not set another deity alongside The God, lest you remain blameworthy and unsupported.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:22

Here, مَخْذُولًا (makhdhūlan) is the passive participle of خَذَلَ (khadhala).

It denotes someone unaided or forsaken.

The verse begins with a command not to set another deity alongside God.

Its consequence is then expressed through two descriptions:

مَذْمُومًا (madhmūman) and مَخْذُولًا (makhdhūlan).

These descriptions are close but not identical in meaning.

Madhmūm belongs to DH M M and denotes someone blameworthy.

Makhdhūl belongs to KH DH L and describes someone lacking needed help and support.

One therefore concerns being blamed; the other concerns being left unsupported.

فَتَقْعُدَ (fa-taqʿuda): the consequence of setting another deity

Fa-taqʿuda belongs to Q ʿ D and, in this construction, can express remaining or coming to be in a particular state.

The verse describes that state as madhmūman makhdhūlan.

The point is therefore more than physically sitting on the ground: it concerns being in a blameworthy, unsupported condition.

Quʿūd and khidhlān remain independent roots, however. Lack of assistance comes from makhdhūlan, rather than taqʿuda itself meaning deprivation of help.

Who is forsaken?

The verse addresses a singular addressee within the Qur’an’s direct addresses to the Prophet.

That address should retain its actual position; theological assumptions alone should not detach the command from its direct addressee.

At the same time, the monotheistic prohibition against setting another deity alongside God is expressed more broadly in numerous other verses.

The verse’s general message can therefore be discussed through the network of monotheistic verses without overlooking its singular address.

Why is associating another deity connected with being forsaken?

Al-Isrāʾ 17:22 presents becoming forsaken as a consequence of setting another deity alongside God.

Two verses earlier, Al-Isrāʾ 17:20 describes the Lord’s giving as reaching different groups.

Their proximity indicates that possessing worldly blessings alone does not validate every religious or ethical choice.

Someone may possess wealth, power, many companions, or abundant resources, yet choose as their deity and ultimate reliance something unable to respond where real assistance is needed.

Becoming forsaken here is consequently connected with an unsound orientation in worship.

Makhdhūl itself does not mean a polytheist or someone associating partners with God. That association is expressed independently at the verse’s opening: لَا تَجْعَلْ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَهًا آخَرَ (lā tajʿal maʿa allāhi ilāhan ākhara).

Nor does the verse establish that everyone suffering poverty, illness, or calamity is forsaken and condemned by God.

Being forsaken here results from specified conduct and must not be generalized to every difficulty in life.

5. Al-Furqān 25:29: Satan forsaking the human being

لَقَدْ أَضَلَّنِي عَنِ الذِّكْرِ بَعْدَ إِذْ جَاءَنِي وَكَانَ الشَّيْطَانُ لِلْإِنْسَانِ خَذُولًا

la-qad aḍallanī ʿani al-dhikri baʿda idh jāʾanī wa-kāna al-shayṭānu li-al-insāni khadhūlan

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“He certainly led me astray from the reminder after it had reached me; Satan leaves the human being unsupported.”

Al-Furqān 25:29

Here, خَذُولًا (khadhūlan) describes Satan.

The preceding verses must also be considered.

Al-Furqān 25:27 describes a day when the wrongdoer bites his hands in regret, wishing he had taken a path with the messenger.

Al-Furqān 25:28 mentions choosing someone as an intimate friend and wishing that choice had not been made.

Al-Furqān 25:29 then identifies that friend as the cause of turning away from the reminder after it had arrived.

Satan is subsequently described as khadhūl.

This sequence places Satan’s abandonment within deception, following a misleading companion, and the resulting outcome.

خَذُولًا (khadhūlan): how does Satan forsake the human being?

Khadhūl follows the فَعُول (faʿūl) pattern, which can indicate a characteristic’s prominence or intensity.

Here that characteristic is forsaking and withholding help.

In human relationships, someone might encourage another person to act and promise support if trouble arises, only to abandon them when the consequences arrive.

Al-Furqān 25:29 presents something close to this meaning.

Satan leads the human being astray but fails to help when its consequences arrive.

This relates to Ibrāhīm 14:22, where Satan speaks on the Day of Resurrection of a promise he broke, reminds people of their responsibility for accepting his invitation, and says that neither he nor they can rescue one another.

In Al-Ḥashr 59:16, Satan likewise disowns the human being after urging them to disbelieve.

Al-Anfāl 8:48 also describes Satan embellishing a group’s deeds before disowning them.

These verses clarify Satan’s khidhlān: invitation and deception on one side, and the absence of assistance and support when consequences arrive on the other.

Khadhūl itself, however, does not mean whispering suggestions, deception, or false promises. Those acts are expressed elsewhere.

Khadhūl denotes the characteristic of forsaking and failing to help.

Are Satan and the misleading friend the same person?

Al-Furqān 25:28 describes someone chosen as a khalīl, an intimate friend.

Al-Furqān 25:29 continues with being misled through that relationship, then Satan’s character as khadhūl.

Two subjects must be distinguished here.

First, the companion or friend whom someone regrets following and befriending.

Second, Satan, whom the Qur’an describes as khadhūl.

Some exegetical explanations connect them, since someone inviting another to misguidance may act in a satanic role.

Al-Anʿām 6:112 also mentions devils among both human beings and jinn.

The Qur’anic network therefore does not reduce Satan solely to one narrow image of an unseen being.

Without evidence, however, one must not identify every misleading friend with the particular Satan mentioned at the end of Al-Furqān 25:29.

The verse may show both the outcome of following a misleading companion and Satan’s role behind such misguidance.

This distinction should be retained.

Is the final statement spoken by the regretful person or by God?

Early commentaries present two readings of وَكَانَ الشَّيْطَانُ لِلْإِنْسَانِ خَذُولًا (wa-kāna al-shayṭānu li-al-insāni khadhūlā).

One treats it as a continuation of the regretful wrongdoer’s speech.

The other ends that speech at بَعْدَ إِذْ جَاءَنِي (baʿda idh jāʾanī), taking the final sentence as a general divine statement about Satan’s character.

Al-Ṭabarī and several other commentators adopt the second reading.

The construction also supports this preference: the preceding speech uses first-person singular forms, أَضَلَّنِي (aḍallanī) and جَاءَنِي (jāʾanī).

The next sentence instead uses the general expression لِلْإِنْسَانِ (li-al-insān).

This shift fits a transition from personal speech to a general statement about Satan.

The change alone nevertheless does not make the first reading linguistically impossible.

The article should therefore clarify the statement’s general meaning while acknowledging the speaker disagreement in proportion to its effect on understanding.

Al-Zamakhsharī’s Al-Kashshāf: readings of Satan’s identity and the final sentence’s speaker

Satan’s abandonment and human responsibility

The regretful person says they were led astray after the reminder had reached them.

This qualification matters.

Misguidance here follows the reminder’s arrival, but the verse itself does not give one identical judgment about every human being’s understanding, freedom, and responsibility in every circumstance.

Nor does Satan’s invitation completely remove human responsibility.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, Satan declares that he possessed no coercive authority over his followers beyond inviting them.

Al-Naḥl 16:99 and Al-Naḥl 16:100 also distinguish those who believe and trust in God from those who take Satan as their patron.

Satan’s character as khadhūl consequently reveals both the danger of trusting a misleading invitation and the limits of relying upon it.

This does not mean that every deceived person knowingly and maliciously turned away from the truth.

Being deceived, stumbling, knowingly denying, and persisting in recognized error are different situations.

Distinguishing them requires each case’s context and the network of verses concerning knowledge, reform, and human responsibility.

Al-Ṭabarī: misguidance, the companion, and the meaning of khadhūl

6. Distinctions from related roots

KH DH L (khidhlān) and N Ṣ R (naṣr): withholding and providing help

The closest Qur’anic opposition is with N Ṣ R.

Āl ʿImrān 3:160 places yanṣurkum and yakhdhulkum in the same verse.

Naṣr concerns help and support.

Khidhlān concerns forsaking and withholding that help.

Not every use of khidhlān means attacking someone or making war against them, however.

Someone may refuse to help another without directly injuring them.

Conversely, someone may both withhold help and assist the other person’s enemy. That is ethically more serious, but helping the enemy is not a necessary component of khidhlān’s lexical meaning.

The direct opposition in Āl ʿImrān 3:160 clarifies the meaning of forsaking and failing to help.

Study of N Ṣ R

KH DH L (khidhlān) and T R K (tark): leaving generally and withholding assistance

Arabic tark has a broad field, including leaving, leaving something behind, or ceasing an activity.

In human usage, khidhlān concerns withdrawing companionship and assistance more particularly.

Someone might leave a book on a table, for example. This is not khidhlān in its ethical sense.

If someone fails to help a friend in need, however, khidhlān becomes relevant.

In the gazelle remaining apart from the herd, the distinction is less ethical and the general sense of separation more prominent.

Khidhlān can therefore be regarded as a kind of leaving, but not every act of leaving is khidhlān.

Study of T R K

KH DH L (khidhlān) and KH W N (khiyānah): withholding help and violating trust

Qur’anic khiyānah concerns conduct contrary to a trust or entrusted responsibility.

Human khidhlān can also violate trust, especially where someone expected help or had a particular commitment.

The two roots are nevertheless not complete synonyms.

Someone may misappropriate another person’s entrusted money. This is betrayal even without an issue of assistance in danger.

Conversely, genuine incapacity may prevent someone helping. Assistance is absent, but they cannot be called a traitor without supporting evidence.

Human khidhlān can therefore be treacherous, but betrayal is not an invariant meaning of every form in the family.

KH DH L (khidhlān) and GH D R (ghadr): forsaking and breaking a pledge

Ghadr concerns breaking a pledge and disloyalty.

Khidhlān can occur without a specific verbal promise, however.

Friendship or companionship may create an expectation of help even without a formal contract.

If someone withholds support in need, khidhlān may be considered.

If a specific pledge is broken, ghadr may also be involved.

Breaking a pledge and withholding help can therefore coincide, but not every khidhlān violates an explicit promise.

KH DH L (khidhlān) and H J R (hajr): withholding help and moving apart

Hajr concerns moving apart and separation.

In some uses, someone distances themselves from another or ends a relationship.

Khidhlān may also involve separation, but withholding assistance is more prominent.

Someone may migrate from a city while continuing to support relatives, for example. Distance alone is not necessarily khidhlān.

Conversely, someone may remain beside a friend yet refuse help when needed.

Moving apart and abandoning someone where assistance is concerned are therefore related but distinct.

Study of H J R

KH DH L (khidhlān) and DH L L (dhull): being unsupported and humiliation

These roots resemble one another in writing but are independent.

KH DH L concerns withholding help and forsaking.

Different DH L L uses may concern becoming tractable, humility, or humiliation.

Lack of support may put someone in a humiliating position, but makhdhūl itself does not mean dhalīl, “humiliated.”

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:22, madhmūm and makhdhūl are distinct descriptions. They must not be merged into a vague equivalent such as “abased” that loses the absence of assistance.

Study of DH L L

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal Persian equivalents of KH DH L are “forsaking,” “failing to help,” “leaving unsupported,” and, where appropriate, “withdrawing support.”

“Leaving alone” can sometimes work, but requires care: khidhlān need not mean physical solitude or the absence of every companion.

Someone may have many companions yet lack their support at the moment real help is needed.

يَخْذُلْكُمْ (yakhdhulkum): Āl ʿImrān 3:160

The verb stands opposite yanṣurkum here.

Proposed translation:

“If He forsakes you and withholds help, who then can help you after Him?”

Root: KH DH L.

Grammar: a third-person masculine singular jussive imperfect verb of Form I, with the attached object pronoun كُمْ (kum).

Brief root meaning: forsaking and withdrawing help.

Meaning in this verse: if God does not grant the addressees His help and support.

Help and its withdrawal are attributed to God here. The reasons and conditions for withholding help must be understood from the verses’ context.

مَخْذُولًا (makhdhūlan): Al-Isrāʾ 17:22

The verse describes someone as makhdhūl as a consequence of setting another deity alongside God.

Proposed translation:

“Lest you remain blameworthy and unsupported.”

Root: KH DH L.

Grammar: a masculine singular passive participle, indefinite and accusative.

Brief root meaning: someone forsaken and deprived of assistance.

Meaning in this verse: someone left unsupported as a consequence of associating another deity with God.

Makhdhūl’s own meaning must not be merged with madhmūm.

خَذُولًا (khadhūlan): Al-Furqān 25:29

Here, khadhūl describes Satan.

Proposed translation:

“Satan leaves the human being unsupported.”

Root: KH DH L.

Grammar: an intensive adjective on فَعُول (faʿūl), masculine singular, indefinite and accusative; the predicate of kāna.

Brief root meaning: someone characterized by withholding help and forsaking.

Meaning in this verse: Satan, who leads the human being astray but fails to help when the consequences arrive.

The intensive pattern expresses a prominent characteristic of Satan; it specifies no particular number of instances of abandonment.

Nor should khadhūlan be translated directly as “misleading”: the independent verb أَضَلَّنِي (aḍallanī) expresses leading astray in the same verse.

8. Conclusion

Arabic KH DH L concerns forsaking, withdrawing companionship, and withholding assistance. In human usage, this is particularly prominent where help or support was expected.

Early witnesses show that the root’s lexical field is not exclusively morally negative. A female gazelle remaining beside its young apart from the herd, or legs becoming too weak to move, exemplifies separation and discontinued support.

In human relationships, however, withholding help can involve disloyalty and betrayal. Its ethical force emerges from circumstances, commitments, and conduct.

The Qur’an uses this root in three forms.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:160, khidhlān contrasts with divine assistance and denotes its absence.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:22, being makhdhūl results from setting another deity alongside God.

In Al-Furqān 25:29, Satan is khadhūl: one who draws the human being along the path of misguidance but fails to help when its consequences arrive.

The shared meaning is withholding assistance and forsaking, but God’s withholding of help and Satan’s abandonment differ in agency and ethical responsibility.

Nor does khidhlān necessarily mean defeat, humiliation, betrayal, or misguidance. These concepts may accompany it in some verses, but each has an independent meaning.

Final definition: KH DH L denotes forsaking, withdrawing companionship, and withholding help. In human usage, this failure to help is especially prominent where support was expected. Qur’anic yakhdhulkum expresses the absence of divine help, makhdhūlan the condition of the unsupported person, and khadhūlan Satan’s characteristic abandonment. The cause, ethical character, and consequences of each instance must be understood from its agent and the verse’s context. Brief root meaning: Forsaking and withdrawing help or companionship, especially where support is expected.
