Slipping, losing firmness, and an argument becoming invalid

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic triliteral root D Ḥ Ḍ concerns slipping, losing firmness, and moving from a previous position. A physical use describes a foot on slippery ground that can no longer remain firm.

Related uses include moving aside, losing a position, being defeated, and an argument becoming invalid or losing credibility.

دَحَضَتْ حُجَّتُهُ does not mean that an argument physically slipped on the ground. Its supporting force and validity have failed, so it can no longer sustain the person’s claim.

This connection between physical and argumentative uses is important. Yet actual bodily slipping, losing a position or victory in an event, and an argument becoming invalid should remain distinct.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an: twice of people seeking through false argument to discredit truth, once of the outcome of lots drawn by Jonah, and once of a proof lacking standing before the Lord.

Daḥḍ therefore means more than rejecting a reason. Its broader field concerns slipping, loss of stability, and displacement from a firm position. Argumentatively, this extends to a proof becoming invalid or attempts to discredit truth.

2. Qur’anic forms and early Arabic usage

D Ḥ Ḍ occurs four times in four verses, in three principal forms:

لِيُدْحِضُوا: a subjunctive Form IV imperfect verb with the purpose particle li-, meaning to invalidate or discredit. It occurs in Al-Kahf 18:56 and Ghāfir 40:5.

الْمُدْحَضِينَ: a masculine plural Form IV passive participle, in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141, describing those who lose in drawing lots.

دَاحِضَةٌ: a feminine Form I active participle, in Al-Shūrā 42:16, describing a proof without firmness or validity.

Other forms are also recorded in Arabic:

دَحَضَ يَدْحَضُ means slipping or moving from a firm position; daḥḍ and duḥūḍ mean slipping; adḥaḍa means causing to slip, displacing, or invalidating; idḥāḍ means causing to slip or invalidating; dāḥiḍ describes something slipping or losing firmness.

Madḥaḍ and madḥaḍah are also recorded for slippery ground.

These illuminate the family, but only the first three forms occur in the Qur’an. The distinctions between Form I, Form IV, active participle, and passive participle genuinely affect each verse’s meaning.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four occurrences in three forms

The semantic core: slipping and displacement

Ibn Fāris introduces the root in Maqāyīs al-Lughah as:

أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى زَوَالٍ وَزَلَقٍ

aṣlun yadullu ʿalā zawālin wa-zalaqin

“A principle indicating displacement and slipping.”

This definition combines two closely related aspects.

Zalaq denotes slipping and inability to remain firm on a surface. Zawāl denotes moving away or a state disappearing.

Ibn Fāris illustrates this with a slipping foot, the sun moving from mid-sky, and an argument becoming invalid.

Their subjects differ, but each involves something leaving a previous state or position.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn, attributed to Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad, likewise explains daḥḍ through slipping, mentioning a camel’s foot, the sun’s movement, and an invalid argument in the same entry.

Ibn Durayd’s Jamharat al-Lughah distinguishes دَحَضَتْ رِجْلُهُ, his foot slipped, from دَحَضَتْ حُجَّتُهُ, his argument became invalid.

The connection is therefore documented in early Arabic sources, rather than merely a modern literary explanation.

These witnesses alone do not establish that every use developed from a slipping foot at one time in a particular historical sequence. What is clear is the family’s semantic relationship between slipping, displacement, and invalidity.

3. Physical uses and Arabic witnesses

Slippery ground and losing firmness

مَكَانٌ دَحْضٌ denotes slippery ground where a foot cannot easily remain firm.

Water, mud, or the surface’s condition may cause this.

Besides slipping, Lisān al-ʿArab records al-daḥḍ for water that makes ground slippery.

مَزَلَّةٌ مِدْحَاضٌ likewise denotes a place where slipping frequently occurs.

The central feature is a foot or hoof losing its usual stability on the ground.

Daḥḍ should not therefore always mean water or mud. Those may cause slipping without restricting the root to those substances.

A human or animal foot slipping

Dictionaries record دَحَضَتْ رِجْلُهُ for a foot slipping.

Of a camel they also say:

دَحَضَتْ رِجْلُ الْبَعِيرِ

daḥaḍat rijlu al-baʿīri

“The camel’s foot slipped.”

Here the verb is intransitive: the foot itself slips.

In Form IV, adḥaḍa can instead be transitive, conveying causing something to slip or lose firmness.

This morphological distinction matters in Qur’anic usage: an unstable proof differs from someone seeking to discredit a proof or truth.

A witness attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

Jamharat al-Lughah, Lisān al-ʿArab, and literary sources attribute the following verse to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah:

رَدِيتُ وَنَجَّى الْيَشْكُرِيَّ حِذَارُهُ
وَحَادَ كَمَا حَادَ الْبَعِيرُ عَنِ الدَّحْضِ

radītu wa-najjā al-yashkuriyya ḥidhāruhu / wa-ḥāda kamā ḥāda al-baʿīru ʿani al-daḥḍi

“I fell into ruin, but the Yashkurī man’s caution saved him; he turned aside as a camel turns from slippery ground.”

Al-daḥḍ here denotes slippery ground or conditions that a camel avoids to prevent falling.

Importantly, the camel need not actually slip; it avoids the slippery situation.

Daḥḍ can therefore describe slipperiness and the risk of losing stability, rather than only an actual slip.

Attribution requires caution. Although dictionaries and literary sources assign the verse to Ṭarafah, some principal collections of his poetry omit it, and variants of the first hemistich are recorded.

It is a valuable witness within early Arabic transmission, but securely assigning this exact text to the pre-Islamic period requires further manuscript research.

Jamharat al-Lughah, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: the witness attributed to Ṭarafah

The sun moving away

Lexical sources record:

دَحَضَتِ الشَّمْسُ عَنْ بَطْنِ السَّمَاءِ

daḥaḍati al-shamsu ʿan baṭni al-samāʾi

This means the sun moved from its mid-sky position.

It does not describe the sun slipping on a material surface. Language uses displacement and departure to describe its observed position.

Daḥḍ is thus not confined to suddenly falling; moving from a previous position can also belong to its field.

This expression should not establish a physical theory of solar motion or the sky’s structure.

Its dictionary transmission also does not independently establish its precise date of origin.

Al-ʿAyn and lexical sources: foot, sun, and proof

From a slipping foot to an invalid argument

Arabic says:

دَحَضَتْ حُجَّتُهُ

daḥaḍat ḥujjatuhu

“His argument became invalid and lost its firmness.”

And:

أَدْحَضَ حُجَّتَهُ

adḥaḍa ḥujjatahu

“He invalidated his argument.”

An argument is pictured as something needing a firm foundation.

When reasons are insufficient or conflict with sound evidence, the argument lacks the firmness needed to support its claim.

This image helps explain Qur’anic usage.

Yet an argument being invalid and every part of a statement being false are not always identical.

A conclusion may be true while its supporting reason is false or insufficient. That particular proof fails, but this alone does not establish the opposite conclusion.

Conversely, someone may try to discredit a clear truth without succeeding.

Qur’anic D Ḥ Ḍ allows these situations to be distinguished.

4. Trying to invalidate truth: Al-Kahf 18:56 and Ghāfir 40:5

A. Al-Kahf 18:56: arguing with falsehood to discredit truth

Al-Kahf 18:56

وَمَا نُرْسِلُ الْمُرْسَلِينَ إِلَّا مُبَشِّرِينَ وَمُنْذِرِينَ وَيُجَادِلُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِالْبَاطِلِ لِيُدْحِضُوا بِهِ الْحَقَّ وَاتَّخَذُوا آيَاتِي وَمَا أُنْذِرُوا هُزُوًا

wa-mā nursilu al-mursalīna illā mubashshirīna wa-mundhirīna wa-yujādilu alladhīna kafarū bi-al-bāṭili li-yudḥiḍū bihi al-ḥaqqa wa-ittakhadhū āyātī wa-mā undhirū huzuwan

“We send messengers only as bearers of glad tidings and warnings. Those who disbelieve argue with falsehood to discredit truth through it, and they have mocked My signs and what they were warned of.”

Li-yudḥiḍū is a Form IV verb here.

It is transitive, taking al-ḥaqq as its object. People seek through false argument to deprive truth of standing and credibility.

Initial li- expresses their conduct’s purpose.

The verse thus says more than that they utter something false: they argue with falsehood to discredit truth.

This distinction matters.

Someone may misunderstand an issue or offer a faulty argument without intending to conceal or destroy truth.

Here, however, false argument has the stated purpose لِيُدْحِضُوا بِهِ الْحَقَّ.

This intention follows from the complete construction rather than D Ḥ Ḍ alone.

The roles of bi-al-bāṭil and bihi

The verse first says:

يُجَادِلُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِالْبَاطِلِ

Then adds:

لِيُدْحِضُوا بِهِ الْحَقَّ

Bihi refers to al-bāṭil: they seek to use falsehood to discredit truth.

Falsehood is therefore more than a belief held in their minds; here it serves as a tool of dispute.

Bāṭil has its own root, whose meaning should not be incorporated into daḥḍ’s definition.

Daḥḍ conveys the intended act of discrediting; falsehood is their means of pursuing it.

Intending invalidation versus achieving it

The purpose particle expresses the opponents’ aim without establishing that they have actually invalidated truth.

Seeking to discredit truth differs from having discredited it.

Argument, propaganda, or false statements may deceive some people, but deception alone does not make truth objectively false.

“To invalidate truth” is understandable, provided explanation clarifies that the phrase conveys their goal rather than its certain fulfillment.

Dispute and honest investigation

Al-Kahf 18:54 describes humanity’s frequent disputation. Verse 56 condemns a particular kind: false argument to discredit truth.

Every critical discussion, difficult question, or intellectual disagreement should therefore not be treated as invalidating truth.

Elsewhere, for example Al-Naḥl 16:125, the Qur’an calls for disputing in the best manner.

Evidence, the purpose of discussion, and its relationship to truth matter.

Idḥāḍ in this context names a particular behavior rather than all reasoning or disagreement.

B. Ghāfir 40:5: false argument and earlier peoples

Ghāfir 40:5

كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ وَالْأَحْزَابُ مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ وَهَمَّتْ كُلُّ أُمَّةٍ بِرَسُولِهِمْ لِيَأْخُذُوهُ وَجَادَلُوا بِالْبَاطِلِ لِيُدْحِضُوا بِهِ الْحَقَّ فَأَخَذْتُهُمْ فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عِقَابِ

kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu nūḥin wa-al-aḥzābu min baʿdihim wa-hammat kullu ummatin bi-rasūlihim li-yaʾkhudhūhu wa-jādalū bi-al-bāṭili li-yudḥiḍū bihi al-ḥaqqa fa-akhadhtuhum fa-kayfa kāna ʿiqābi

“Before them, Noah’s people and later factions denied; every community intended to seize its messenger, and they argued with falsehood to discredit truth through it. Then I seized them—how was My punishment?”

Ghāfir’s وَجَادَلُوا بِالْبَاطِلِ لِيُدْحِضُوا بِهِ الْحَقَّ shares li-yudḥiḍū bihi al-ḥaqq with Al-Kahf 18:56. The disputing verb is past jādalū in Ghāfir and imperfect yujādilu in Al-Kahf; this temporal difference does not change their shared sense of invalidating.

The verb therefore has the same meaning in both occurrences.

Ghāfir adds an important context: false argument accompanies denying messengers and trying to seize them.

This is more than intellectual disagreement. Communities oppose messengers, acting against them while trying through false argument to discredit truth.

Daḥḍ here does not mean killing, imprisoning, or seizing a messenger. The intention to seize comes from the separate li-yaʾkhudhūhu. Al-Ṭabarī interprets it here as intending to kill; seizing, killing, and imprisoning should not be treated as identical lexical meanings or all as explicit information in the verb itself.

Al-Ṭabarī: seizing interpreted as intending to kill, and invalidating truth

Li-yudḥiḍū expresses the argumentative aspect of their conduct.

The verse thus juxtaposes action against the messenger with efforts to discredit truth.

They can connect without being linguistically or practically identical.

Refuting a reason versus suppressing its proponent

Sound evidence can invalidate a faulty argument. That differs as a method of seeking truth from silencing its proponent through threats or force.

Ghāfir 40:5 places attempts to seize the messenger and false argument within one context.

Daḥḍ itself nevertheless does not mean physical suppression.

Here the verb means seeking to invalidate or discredit truth; pressure on the messenger follows from another part of the verse.

Argumentative idḥāḍ may invalidate a proof. If the aim is to make truth seem false through false statements, the stated context and intention establish its ethical significance.

5. Al-mudḥaḍīn: losing in the drawing of lots

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141

فَسَاهَمَ فَكَانَ مِنَ الْمُدْحَضِينَ

fa-sāhama fa-kāna mina al-mudḥaḍīna

“He drew lots and was among those who lost the draw.”

This concerns Jonah.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140 describes his going to the laden ship; verse 141 then describes drawing lots and its result.

Fa-sāhama belongs to S H M and here means participating in lots.

Al-mudḥaḍīn belongs to D Ḥ Ḍ.

It is a masculine plural Form IV passive participle, describing those defeated in the draw or deprived of the favorable outcome.

The word itself therefore does not mean participants in lots. Drawing lots comes from sāhama; losing its outcome comes from al-mudḥaḍīn.

Why losing?

Physical daḥḍ involves slipping and losing firm standing.

In drawing lots, this extends to losing a favorable position or being defeated by the selection’s outcome.

Jonah is not physically slipping on ground here, nor is his logical proof refuted in a debate.

The subject is the draw’s result.

Early dictionaries and commentaries explain al-mudḥaḍīn as al-maghlūbīn and al-maqrūʿīn: those for whom the draw turned out unfavorably.

“Those who lost the draw” is therefore clear and natural.

Mudḥaḍ should not always mean a losing participant in lots throughout Arabic; that sense follows from its combination with sāhama.

Al-Qurṭubī: lots and defeat in the draw

Does mudḥaḍ mean sinful?

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142 says the fish swallowed Jonah while he was mulīm.

Mulīm belongs to L W M, concerning blame and conduct inviting reproach.

Mudḥaḍ belongs to another root and concerns losing in the draw.

They should not be equated.

Al-mudḥaḍīn itself does not mean sinful or deserving blame.

Discussing Jonah’s error, responsibility, and return requires the relevant Al-Ṣāffāt narrative, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87, and Al-Qalam 68:48.

An unfavorable draw alone should particularly not establish a person’s ethical wrongness.

The story gives other information about Jonah and his behavior, but that does not derive from D Ḥ Ḍ itself.

Does the Qur’an specify the draw’s details?

Verse 141 says Jonah participated and was among those defeated.

It does not specify the number of draws, exact instruments, participant count, or every reason for holding it.

Exegetical reports supply details, but outside information need not complete the story for the root’s meaning to be clear.

The relationship between participating and losing the draw suffices here.

A base translation therefore need not add details such as three repeated draws or a particular decision to throw someone overboard.

6. Ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍah: a proof lacking firmness

Al-Shūrā 42:16

وَالَّذِينَ يُحَاجُّونَ فِي اللَّهِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا اسْتُجِيبَ لَهُ حُجَّتُهُمْ دَاحِضَةٌ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ وَعَلَيْهِمْ غَضَبٌ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ

wa-alladhīna yuḥājjūna fī allāhi min baʿdi mā ustujība lahu ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍatun ʿinda rabbihim wa-ʿalayhim ghaḍabun wa-lahum ʿadhābun shadīdun

“Those who dispute concerning The God after His call has been answered—their proof is invalid before their Lord. Anger is upon them, and theirs will be a severe punishment.”

Dāḥiḍah is a feminine Form I active participle.

Ḥujjatuhum is the subject and dāḥiḍah its predicate.

Ḥujjah is grammatically feminine, explaining the participle’s feminine form.

It denotes a proof lacking firmness or validity, or one that is false.

Morphologically this differs from li-yudḥiḍū.

In Al-Kahf and Ghāfir, people seek to invalidate truth; in Al-Shūrā, their proof itself is described as dāḥiḍah.

One concerns the act or attempted act of invalidating; the other the condition of an invalid proof.

Before their Lord: the standard of validity

ʿInda rabbihim is an important qualification.

The verse does not merely say their proof is unacceptable to some people, but that it is invalid before their Lord.

True validity therefore differs from fame, follower count, or its proponent’s social power.

An argument may influence people yet lack standing before The God.

Conversely, true speech can face opposition without losing its truth.

This conclusion follows from the relationship between their proof being invalid and before their Lord, rather than daḥḍ alone.

Context and “after a response has been made to Him”

The verse describes people disputing concerning The God after He or His call has received a response.

Interpretations differ about lahu’s referent and the precise meaning of ustujība. Nearby context concerns the call to The God, upholding religion, avoiding division, and disagreement after knowledge.

Al-Shūrā 42:13, Al-Shūrā 42:14, and Al-Shūrā 42:15 should also inform a full understanding.

For root research, the essential point is that a specific kind of disputation is declared invalid.

This does not make every question about The God, scholarly discussion, or interpretive disagreement a dāḥiḍah proof.

The verse’s qualifications and context determine the people and argument concerned.

Conversely, someone merely claiming an opponent’s speech is false before The God does not replace evidence.

The verse conveys divine judgment of a particular group’s proof. People cannot simply repeat that judgment of every opponent without examining the reasons.

How does an invalid argument relate to a false claim?

A proof supports or establishes a claim.

An unstable proof has not established the intended claim through that reasoning.

Logically, however, a claim can be true while someone supplies a bad reason for it.

Refuting that reason alone does not establish that the claim is false.

Al-Shūrā 42:16 nevertheless concerns more than an isolated reasoning error. It describes particular disputation alongside divine judgment, anger, and punishment.

A general logical discussion should not replace the verse’s specific judgment.

Dāḥiḍah concerns a proof’s stability and validity. Determining the corresponding claim’s status requires the full evidence for that subject.

7. Distinguishing related roots

D Ḥ Ḍ and Z L Q: slipping and slipperiness

Z L Q is among the closest semantic families.

Both can describe a slipping foot or slippery surface.

Al-Kahf 18:40 uses ṣaʿīdan zalaqan of ground with a slippery or vegetation-free surface.

Arabic daḥḍ likewise describes slippery ground and a slipping foot.

Qur’anic daḥḍ foregrounds invalid arguments and defeat, whereas Z L Q has other Qur’anic uses.

A shared image of slipping does not make them completely synonymous.

D Ḥ Ḍ and Z L L: slipping and error

Z L L also concerns slipping and can describe lapses in conduct or guidance.

Al-Baqarah 2:209 uses zalaltum of slipping after clear signs arrive.

Qur’anic daḥḍ focuses on a proof losing validity, attempts to invalidate truth, and defeat in a particular situation.

Every ethical or behavioral lapse should not therefore be called daḥḍ.

Nor does an invalid argument alone establish deliberate ethical failure by its proponent.

Ignorance or error may produce incomplete reasoning. Intent and moral responsibility require separate examination.

Study of Z L L

D Ḥ Ḍ and B Ṭ L: invalidity and lost firmness

Argumentative daḥḍ and buṭlān approach each other closely.

Lexical sources explain daḥaḍat ḥujjatuhu with baṭalat, it became invalid.

B Ṭ L has a broad field including fruitless work, false claims, lack of validity, and other uses.

Daḥḍ in the present use carries the image of a proof slipping and losing firmness.

Preserving that image should not require awkward literal translations such as an argument slipping from under its own feet.

“His proof became invalid” or “his argument lost credibility” is clear and natural.

The lexical image can remain in the root’s explanation.

Study of B Ṭ L

D Ḥ Ḍ and D Ḥ R: invalidation and expulsion

These roots resemble each other in writing but differ in their final letter.

D Ḥ R concerns driving away and removal.

D Ḥ Ḍ concerns slipping, lost firmness, and invalidity.

Someone may be displaced after defeat, or an argument excluded after refutation. Similar results do not make the roots identical.

D Ḥ R foregrounds expelling and removing.

D Ḥ Ḍ foregrounds slipping, unstable position, or lost validity.

Al-mudḥaḍīn in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141 should not be rendered “those expelled”: its context concerns losing lots rather than expulsion.

Study of D Ḥ R

D Ḥ Ḍ and GH L B: defeat and lost position

GH L B denotes one side overcoming another.

Lexical sources explain al-mudḥaḍīn in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141 through al-maghlūbīn, the defeated.

Defeat is broader and can occur in many situations.

In Jonah’s verse, daḥḍ relates to the draw’s outcome and loss of a favorable position.

Defeat is suitable here without making every D Ḥ Ḍ use a competition between people or groups.

A proof may fail from lack of valid evidence rather than because a more powerful person defeats its proponent.

D Ḥ Ḍ and Ḥ J J: a proof and its validity

Uses of Ḥ J J concerning proof, disputation, and argument involve reasoning and presenting evidence.

Daḥḍ does not name the reason or argument itself.

Ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍah names a proof on one side and describes its invalidity on the other.

The proof is what is judged; dāḥiḍah describes its validity.

Daḥḍ should therefore not mean arguing or reasoning.

Yujādilu in Al-Kahf 18:56 and jādalū in Ghāfir 40:5 convey disputing; li-yudḥiḍū gives its purpose in both.

These semantic functions should remain distinct.

Study of Ḥ J J

8. Suggested translations for Forqan

One equivalent does not suit all four Qur’anic derivatives.

Li-yudḥiḍū

Suitable equivalents in Al-Kahf 18:56 and Ghāfir 40:5 are:

“To invalidate” or “to discredit.”

“To discredit truth through it” can preserve the difference between an intended invalidation and its achievement.

“To destroy truth” risks suggesting truth’s actual destruction.

Simply “to reject truth” can weaken the sense of seeking to deprive it of credibility.

Al-mudḥaḍīn

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141 the most suitable equivalent is:

“Those who lost the draw.”

Within the sentence:

“He drew lots and was among those who lost the draw.”

“The defeated” is lexically defensible, but without mentioning lots readers might imagine defeat in war or debate.

“The invalidated” and “those who slipped” are unnatural and imprecise here.

Dāḥiḍah

“Invalid” or “without validity” suits Al-Shūrā 42:16.

For ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍatun ʿinda rabbihim, a natural rendering is:

“Their proof is invalid before their Lord.”

“Their argument has no validity before their Lord” is also clear and accurate.

“Their proof slips” would unnaturally transfer the root’s physical image into the direct translation.

The Qur’anic family can therefore use three sets of equivalents: invalidating and discrediting, losing in lots, and an argument being invalid.

Physical Arabic uses also admit slipping, causing to slip, slippery ground, and displacement.

These equivalents relate semantically without being interchangeable in every sentence.

9. Conclusion

Arabic D Ḥ Ḍ concerns slipping and lost firmness. Related extensions include displacement, defeat, and an argument losing validity.

Early dictionaries place slipping feet and invalid arguments within one family. An argument is thereby pictured as something unable to stand without a firm foundation.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an.

Li-yudḥiḍū in Al-Kahf 18:56 and Ghāfir 40:5 conveys efforts to invalidate truth through false argument. The purpose construction does not establish that truth has actually been invalidated.

Al-mudḥaḍīn in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141 concerns losing the draw, rather than being sinful or possessing an invalid proof.

Dāḥiḍah in Al-Shūrā 42:16 describes a proof without validity or firmness before the Lord, rather than merely someone’s effort to invalidate it.

Slipping, invalidity, and defeat belong to one semantic field but acquire particular meanings in context.

An invalid argument, a false claim, a conscious effort to misrepresent truth, and an honest reasoning error should remain distinct. D Ḥ Ḍ alone does not judge them all alike.

Proposed final definition: D Ḥ Ḍ denotes slipping and loss of firmness or position. Its uses extend to physical slipping, displacement, defeat, and a proof becoming invalid. Qur’anic derivatives express efforts to invalidate truth, losing lots, and an invalid argument. Morphology and context establish each derivative’s precise meaning. Brief root meaning: Slipping and loss of firmness; defeat and an argument becoming invalid or being discredited.
