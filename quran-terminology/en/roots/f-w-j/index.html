The Qur’anic Root F W J (فَوْج)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَوْجًا / فَوْجٌ
(fawjan / fawjun)
One group; three occurrences

أَفْوَاجًا
(afwājan)
In groups; two occurrences

A group or band of people: from entering the religion in groups to the gathering and movement of groups at the Resurrection

1. The root’s meaning in early Arabic

In its main branch, the Arabic triliteral root F W J concerns people gathering together. Fawj names a band or collection of people recognized as one group, often moving together or entering a place.

Classical lexicons explain fawj through two principal descriptions:

جَمَاعَةٌ مِنَ النَّاسِ: “a group of people.”

الْجَمَاعَةُ الْمَارَّةُ الْمُسْرِعَةُ: “a group passing by swiftly.”

The first definition emphasizes the group itself; the second also highlights collective movement.

All five Qur’anic occurrences concern groups being gathered, coming, or entering. Yet speed is not stated in every verse, so rapid movement cannot be made an essential condition of the word’s meaning.

One classical lexical analysis identifies the family’s central sense as tajammuʿ: gathering together and forming a collection. This is consistent with Qur’anic usage.

Fawj does not itself specify a fixed number of people. A group may be small or larger; its size must be established from context or other information.

Nor must its members belong to one family, people, religion, or social class. The feature that makes them recognizable as a group depends on the situation and subject under discussion.

Historical usage and the limits of the evidence

Arabic also records forms of this family for groups coming to a banquet or gathering.

For example, lexicons record the following expression:

مَرَّ بِنَا فَائِجُ وَلِيمَةِ فُلَانٍ

Marra binā fāʾiju walīmati fulānin.

“A group of guests from so-and-so’s wedding banquet passed by us.”

Here fāʾij refers to people brought together by a shared occasion and passing by.

Its presence in lexicons does not, by itself, establish that this exact expression was used before Islam.

This study has not established an independent poetic example for pre-Islamic fawj whose wording, attribution, and pre-Qur’anic date can all be verified with confidence.

The Qur’an itself clearly attests the word in the Arabic of its revelation, while classical sources record the meaning of a human group. These support an understanding of its meaning, but do not justify assigning its origin to a particular period without evidence.

Does fawj derive from rapid movement?

Arabic records ifājah for hurrying and running. In some uses, the verb afāja also concerns rapid movement.

Some lexicons place these forms under F W J. Another analysis, however, assigns afāja and fayj to F Y J.

In recorded Arabic usage, fayj can also mean a courier or someone traveling on foot to deliver a message. Its history and relationship to Persian forms constitute a separate etymological question.

Phonetic similarity alone therefore does not make speed and message delivery the primary meaning of fawj.

Fāʾijah is also recorded for an open space between two elevations or hills. This usage alone does not explain the historical origin of the name for a human group.

For Qur’anic usage, the clearer, more defensible definition is:

Fawj is a group of people recognized as one band; in contexts of movement, they come, go, or enter a place together.

2. Forms and Qur’anic occurrences

Arabic records the forms fawj, afwāj, fuʾūj, afāwij, and afāwīj.

Fawj is a singular noun with a collective meaning: it denotes one group comprising several people.

Afwāj is its broken plural, referring to multiple groups.

Afāwij and afāwīj are also recorded as alternative plurals or plurals of a plural. They do not occur in the Qur’an.

F W J occurs five times in the Qur’an: three times as singular fawj and twice as plural afwāj.

The actual Qur’anic forms are:

  • فَوْجًا in Al-Naml 27:83.
  • فَوْجٌ in Ṣād 38:59.
  • فَوْجٌ in Al-Mulk 67:8.
  • أَفْوَاجًا in Al-Nabaʾ 78:18.
  • أَفْوَاجًا in Al-Naṣr 110:2.

No independent verb from this family occurs in the Qur’an. All five occurrences are nouns.

In Al-Naml 27:83, fawjan is the object of naḥshuru, “We gather.”

In Ṣād 38:59, fawjun is the predicate of the demonstrative hādhā, “this.”

In Al-Mulk 67:8, fawjun is the grammatical subject of the passive ulqiya, “is cast.”

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:18 and Al-Naṣr 110:2, afwājan is a circumstantial expression describing how people come or enter.

These grammatical differences show that the word names a group, while gathering, entering, coming, and being cast are expressed by separate verbs.

3. Al-Naml 27:83: a group from every community

وَيَوْمَ نَحْشُرُ مِنْ كُلِّ أُمَّةٍ فَوْجًا مِمَّنْ يُكَذِّبُ بِآيَاتِنَا فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ

Wa-yawma naḥshuru min kulli ummatin fawjan mimman yukadhdhibu bi-āyātinā fa-hum yūzaʿūn.

“And the day when We gather from every community a group of those who deny Our signs, and they are held in order.”

Al-Naml 27:83

Here fawjan denotes a group from a community.

The construction min kulli ummatin, “from every community,” matters: the verse does not say that every member of each community belongs to this group.

The phrase mimman yukadhdhibu bi-āyātinā identifies the group as drawn from those who deny The God’s signs.

Three points should therefore remain distinct:

Ummah identifies a broader body of people.

Fawj is a group drawn from that body.

Mimman yukadhdhibu bi-āyātinā describes those from whom this group is gathered.

This construction does not imply that every member of every community is a denier.

Gathering a group and holding it in order

At the end of Al-Naml 27:83, the state of the gathered group is described:

Yūzaʿūna: they are restrained and held in order.

This verb belongs to W Z ʿ, which concerns restraining, holding back, and keeping the members of a collection in order.

Al-Naml 27:17 uses the same expression for Solomon’s forces.

Thus naḥshuru expresses gathering, fawjan identifies the group gathered, and yūzaʿūna indicates its restraint or orderly arrangement.

These three ideas are related, but not synonymous.

Fawj does not itself mean a group forcibly gathered or under military command. Such features come from the verbs and circumstances of the verse.

In Al-Naml 27:84, the gathered group is questioned about denying the signs and about their knowledge. The gathering therefore concerns accountability for conduct as well as the movement of people.

For a fuller examination of gathering, including the distinction between general and group-specific gathering, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root Ḥ Sh R.

4. Ṣād 38:59: a group entering with others

هَٰذَا فَوْجٌ مُقْتَحِمٌ مَعَكُمْ لَا مَرْحَبًا بِهِمْ إِنَّهُمْ صَالُوا النَّارِ

Hādhā fawjun muqtaḥimun maʿakum lā marḥaban bihim innahum ṣālu n-nār.

“This is a group plunging in with you. No welcome for them! They will burn in the Fire.”

Ṣād 38:59

This verse describes an exchange and confrontation among groups whose outcome is the Fire.

Hādhā fawjun identifies a particular group.

Muqtaḥimun belongs to Q Ḥ M and expresses forceful or difficult entry.

Maʿakum establishes that the group is entering with those addressed.

Consequently, the sense of plunging in or entering forcefully comes from muqtaḥimun, not from fawj itself.

If fawj inherently meant an attacking or fighting band, that meaning would also have to apply in Al-Naṣr 110:2 and elsewhere. The Qur’an instead uses the word in quite different situations.

The group’s responsibility and individual conduct

In Ṣād 38:60, the newcomers reply to those preceding them and hold them responsible for bringing about this outcome.

The dialogue and conflict among the people of the Fire continue in Ṣād 38:61 and Ṣād 38:64.

The groups are therefore more than anonymous crowds: their members discuss one another’s conduct, roles, and responsibility.

Yet fawj itself does not mean a follower, leader, sinner, or condemned person.

It simply identifies a group.

Nor does membership in one fawj prove that everyone shared identical deeds, knowledge, or moral responsibility. Such questions must be examined through the verses concerning each person’s conduct and reckoning.

5. Al-Mulk 67:8: whenever a group is cast into the Fire

تَكَادُ تَمَيَّزُ مِنَ الْغَيْظِ كُلَّمَا أُلْقِيَ فِيهَا فَوْجٌ سَأَلَهُمْ خَزَنَتُهَا أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ نَذِيرٌ

Takādu tamayyazu mina l-ghayẓi kullamā ulqiya fīhā fawjun saʾalahum khazanatuhā a-lam yaʾtikum nadhīr.

“It almost bursts apart with rage. Whenever a group is cast into it, its keepers ask them: ‘Did no warner come to you?’”

Al-Mulk 67:8

Here fawjun denotes a group of people cast into the Fire.

Kullamā expresses repetition: whenever a group enters this situation, a question is put to them.

The scene thus extends beyond one crowd entering once; it depicts multiple groups entering.

It does not specify the number of groups, the membership of each, or the intervals between their entry.

Ulqiya is passive and expresses being cast.

Fawjun names the group subjected to that action.

Saʾalahum khazanatuhā expresses the keepers’ questioning.

None of these actions is contained in the lexical meaning of fawj itself.

Does belonging to a group remove individual responsibility?

In Al-Mulk 67:9, those questioned acknowledge that a warner came to them, but that they denied him.

In Al-Mulk 67:10, they say that had they listened or reasoned, they would not have been among the people of the Fire.

While describing collective entry, the Qur’an thus directs attention to understanding, hearing, denial, and conduct.

Being a group does not replace grounds for judgment or responsibility.

Nor does the description of their entry as a fawj establish that every group has the same size, standing, or degree of punishment.

Fawj is a collective unit describing entry, not a measure of the degree of reckoning or punishment.

6. Al-Nabaʾ 78:18: people coming in multiple groups

يَوْمَ يُنْفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ فَتَأْتُونَ أَفْوَاجًا

Yawma yunfakhu fī ṣ-ṣūri fa-taʾtūna afwājā.

“The day when the Trumpet is blown, and you come in groups.”

Al-Nabaʾ 78:18

Afwājan here is the plural of fawj.

Taʾtūna describes the addressees’ coming, while afwājan describes how they come.

People arrive as multiple groups; the wording does not identify a single fawj.

Afwājan functions circumstantially and is naturally rendered “in groups.”

Are the groups divided according to their deeds?

Some exegetical explanations describe people at the Resurrection as appearing in different groups according to deeds, faith, or condition.

Other Qur’anic verses discuss differences in people’s outcomes and stations. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:7, for example, mentions three groups.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:18 itself, however, does not state the criterion for forming each fawj.

Other verses can inform a study of these groupings, but afwājan alone does not establish that each group necessarily shares a particular rank, occupation, ethnicity, sin, or reward.

The direct meaning is coming in multiple bands.

This verse alone likewise does not establish the exact number of groups or their order of arrival.

7. Al-Naṣr 110:2: people entering The God’s religion in groups

وَرَأَيْتَ النَّاسَ يَدْخُلُونَ فِي دِينِ اللَّهِ أَفْوَاجًا

Wa-raʾayta n-nāsa yadkhulūna fī dīni llāhi afwājā.

“And you see people entering the religion of The God in groups.”

Al-Naṣr 110:2

This is one of the clearest instances of afwāj describing a social event.

Al-nāsa identifies the people whom the verse’s addressee sees.

Yadkhulūna expresses the action of entering.

Fī dīni llāh identifies what they enter.

Afwājan indicates that their entry occurs in multiple groups.

Here a fawj is not merely people gathered in one place; it is a group participating in a shared event.

Does afwāj mean that everyone enters?

The verse speaks of al-nās and describes their entry into The God’s religion.

Afwāj alone, however, does not specify the total number who entered.

Nor does it prove that every human being in the world entered the religion without exception and at the same time.

The verse concerns the observation of people entering in multiple groups.

Historical accounts connect this situation with the spread of Islam’s acceptance among Arab tribes. That setting can help explain the event, but the lexical meaning of afwāj is not limited to tribes or a particular period.

The same expression can be used whenever people enter a place or body in multiple groups.

Does collective entry establish equal faith in everyone?

Afwāj describes the manner of entry, not the extent of people’s inward faith.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:14, the Qur’an distinguishes professing Islam from faith entering the heart.

The word afwāj alone therefore does not justify judging each individual’s degree of faith, knowledge, or sincerity when a group enters the religion.

Nor does the word itself mean compelled following or submission to pressure. Whether entry is voluntary or forced is established by its circumstances and associated verbs, not by the noun fawj.

Al-Naṣr 110:2 explicitly describes people entering The God’s religion in groups.

This shows that fawj is neither inherently military nor restricted to Resurrection scenes. The same word used for collective entry into the Fire is used here for collective entry into the religion.

The direction and value of the event are determined by context.

8. Distinctions from related roots

F W J (fawj) and Z M R (zumrah): group and band

Zumrah names a band or group of people; its plural is zumar.

In Al-Zumar 39:71, those who disbelieved are driven toward Hell in groups.

In Al-Zumar 39:73, the mindful are taken toward the Garden in groups.

Zumaran and afwājan are thus close in their depiction of collective movement.

Some lexical definitions explain zumrah using fawj itself.

In Qur’anic usage, zumar can emphasize distinct bands moving separately, while fawj identifies a human group in a shared situation, often coming or entering together.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary.

It does not follow that every fawj is larger than a zumrah, or that every zumrah is necessarily formed according to its members’ moral worth.

Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root Z M R examines this distinction further.

F W J (fawj) and ʾ M M (ummah): group and community

Ummah has a broad Qur’anic range: it can refer to a community linked by something shared, a period of time, and other senses.

Fawj denotes a group of people without itself specifying their historical or religious identity.

Al-Naml 27:83 places both words in one sentence:

A group is gathered from every community.

The construction shows that a fawj can be part of an ummah.

It does not imply that every fawj is always a subset of a particular ummah, or that all members of an ummah form only one fawj.

Their relationship is specified in that sentence through the preposition min, “from.”

F W J (fawj) and J N D (jund): group and army

Qur’anic jund concerns an army, troops, or forces assembled for a mission.

Fawj is broader and need not be military.

Persian and some regional languages also use fawj for a military unit. This established usage must not determine the meaning of every Qur’anic occurrence.

In Al-Naṣr 110:2, afwāj describes groups entering the religion without any reference to a military unit.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:18 likewise identifies those appearing at the Resurrection simply in collective terms.

A fawj can therefore consist of soldiers in some situations, but military character is not an invariant part of its meaning.

For the concept of an army, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root J N D.

F W J (fawj) and J M ʿ (jamaʿa): group and gathering

Jamaʿa concerns bringing things or people together.

Fawj names a group.

People may be gathered into a fawj, but not every act of collecting forms one.

Books, stones, or property can be collected, for example, whereas Qur’anic fawj describes human groups.

Jamʿ thus expresses gathering or being gathered, while fawj denotes a collective unit of people.

For further detail, see the study of the Qur’anic root J M ʿ.

F W J (fawj) and Ḥ Sh R (ḥashara): group and gathering toward a destination

Ḥashr concerns gathering and driving together a group of beings or people.

In Al-Naml 27:83, naḥshuru is the act of gathering and fawjan is the group gathered.

Fawj does not itself name the action.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:18 also describes people coming in groups, but its verb is taʾtūna, “you come,” not tuḥsharūna, “you are gathered.”

Features of ḥashr, such as gathering toward a destination or restraining people’s movement, should therefore not all be transferred into the meaning of fawj.

F W J (fawj) and W F D (wafd)

In Arabic usage, wafd denotes a group arriving before a person or authority, especially to visit or attend that person’s presence.

In Maryam 19:85, the mindful are gathered to the Entirely Merciful as wafdan.

Both words can denote a body of people, but the established use of wafd has a particular connection with arriving before a person or authority.

Fawj does not necessarily carry that feature.

In Ṣād 38:59 and Al-Mulk 67:8, groups enter the Fire but are not called wafd on that account.

Destination and the nature of reception or attendance can therefore help distinguish their usage, without imposing an absolute boundary on every instance.

9. Translation recommendations for Forqan

The principal equivalents for fawj are “group” and “band.”

“A group of people” also conveys the meaning well in an initial explanation or when the referent is unclear.

For afwāj, “groups” and “bands” are suitable; in circumstantial constructions, use “in groups” or “in bands.”

“Army” or “troops” is inappropriate without a military indication.

The five Qur’anic occurrences

Al-Naml 27:83 — فَوْجًا

“A group of those who deny Our signs.”

“A group” should be retained because the verse describes gathering a portion of each community.

Ṣād 38:59 — فَوْجٌ

“This is a group plunging in with you.”

The sense of plunging in comes from muqtaḥimun, not fawj.

Al-Mulk 67:8 — فَوْجٌ

“Whenever a group is cast into it.”

“A group” denotes people; being cast is expressed by ulqiya.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:18 — أَفْوَاجًا

“And you come in groups.”

Afwājan describes how they come and need not be rendered “armies” or “separate communities.”

Al-Naṣr 110:2 — أَفْوَاجًا

“And you see people entering the religion of The God in groups.”

“In groups” is natural and accurate here as well.

For non-Qur’anic lexical forms such as afāwij, afāwīj, and fuʾūj, “groups” is an appropriate general equivalent.

Fāʾij, when used of people assembling, can likewise mean “a group” or “a band of people.” Related-looking forms should not all be treated as direct derivatives of one verb without examination.

Conclusion

In its main Arabic usage, F W J concerns people gathering as a group.

Fawj names one group and afwāj multiple groups. Lexicons also mention collective movement, but speed, fixed membership numbers, military leadership, and shared religious identity are not essential components of its meaning.

The family occurs five times in the Qur’an.

In Al-Naml 27:83, a fawj is a group drawn from a community and gathered for accountability.

In Ṣād 38:59, it is a group entering the Fire with others.

Al-Mulk 67:8 describes multiple groups being cast into the Fire and questioned by its keepers.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:18 describes people coming in groups at the Resurrection.

Al-Naṣr 110:2 describes people entering The God’s religion in multiple groups.

These uses show that fawj has no fixed moral value in itself. A group may participate in a commendable or blameworthy event; its direction, destination, and outcome are determined by the verbs and context.

Fawj is semantically related to zumrah, ummah, jund, and wafd, but their features should not all be transferred from one term to another.

Final root definition for Forqan:

F W J: in its principal Qur’anic usage, a group or band of people recognized collectively; in movement contexts, people who come, go, are gathered, or enter a place together.

Fawj is one group and afwāj multiple groups. Membership numbers, speed, order, destination, freedom of action, and moral value are established by sentence structure and context, not by the word alone.

“Group,” “band,” and “in groups” are the family’s principal natural equivalents in Qur’an translation.
