Filling to the brim, density and crowding: from physical uses to the description of a brimming cup

The Arabic root D H Q concerns filling a vessel, accumulation, compression and intense pressure. Its best-known expression is kaʾsun dihāqun: a cup filled with drink nearly to its rim.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as dihāqan in Al-Nabaʾ 78:34. Lexicons report three interpretations: full or brimming, successive, and clear. Their evidence is not equally strong; the fullness of the cup has more direct linguistic support.

The only Qur’anic form is dihāqan. Forms such as dahaqa, adhaqa, iddahaqa and dahq are recorded in Arabic dictionaries but do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic core and physical uses

Filling a vessel to its brim

One clear Arabic use of this root is filling a cup or vessel with liquid.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

أَدْهَقَ الْكَأْسَ: شَدَّ مَلْأَهَا

adhaqa al-kaʾsa: shadda malʾahā

That is, he filled the cup completely.

The expression adhaqtu al-kaʾsa ilā aṣbārihā is also recorded: I filled the cup to its upper edges.

In this use, both dahaqa and adhaqa express filling a vessel. Kaʾsun dihāqun likewise means a full, brimming cup.

This differs in degree from simply filling a vessel: the drink is plentiful and little empty space remains at the top.

Brimming does not necessarily mean that liquid spills over. A cup may be filled to the brim without its contents running out.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: filling a cup and emptying water

Compression and accumulation

Kitāb al-ʿAyn, among the earliest Arabic lexical collections, records iddahaqati al-ḥijāratu: stones became so closely packed that some fitted among others.

This shows that D H Q is not confined to filling vessels. Density, compression and the close arrangement of parts also belong to its semantic family.

Ibn Fāris describes the core of this entry in Maqāyīs al-Lughah as follows:

يَدُلُّ عَلَى امْتِلَاءٍ فِي مَجِيءٍ وَذَهَابٍ وَاضْطِرَابٍ

yadullu ʿalā imtilāʾin fī majīʾin wa-dhahābin wa-iḍṭirābin

That is, it denotes fullness accompanied by coming and going and movement.

He then gives filling a cup as an example.

The same discussion records dahdaqah for a large piece turning about in a boiling pot, sometimes rising and sometimes sinking.

Ibn Fāris is seeking a semantic connection among uses. His explanation does not establish that every form historically developed from a single, proven image of moving liquid or pressure.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: fullness, movement and dahdaqah

Intense pressure and emptying liquid

Lisān al-ʿArab also defines dahq as shiddatu al-ḍaghṭ: intense pressure.

Dahaqa al-māʾa and adhaqahu are also recorded for pouring out or emptying water forcefully.

At first sight, filling a cup and emptying water appear opposed. Some lexicographers therefore included this family among words with contrary uses.

This opposition requires careful interpretation.

In adhaqa al-kaʾsa, the object is the cup and the result is its filling. In adhaqa al-māʾa, the object is water and the action is pouring it out.

The object and setting therefore help determine the meaning. These uses do not imply that dihāq simultaneously means both full and empty in every sentence.

Some sources also record dahaqa al-shayʾa for pressing something hard, breaking it or cutting it. This branch concerns pressure and strong physical effects; it should not be imposed on the Qur’anic description of a cup.

2. Early Arabic usage and poetic evidence

A brimming cup in a verse attributed to Khidāsh ibn Zuhayr

An important witness for Arabic dihāq is this verse attributed to Khidāsh ibn Zuhayr al-ʿĀmirī:

أَتَانَا عَامِرٌ يَرْجُو قِرَانَا
فَأَتْرَعْنَا لَهُ كَأْسًا دِهَاقَا

atānā ʿāmirun yarjū qirānā / fa-atraʿnā lahu kaʾsan dihāqā

Translation: “ʿĀmir came to us hoping for our hospitality; so we filled a brimming cup for him.”

Some transmissions have yabghī instead of yarjū: “seeking our hospitality.” This variant does not change the relevant sense in the second half-line.

The important feature is the pairing of atraʿnā and dihāqan.

Atraʿnā expresses filling a vessel to its brim, while dihāqan describes that cup’s condition. Both expressions emphasize fullness within the same image.

This witnesses the use of dihāq for a brimming cup outside the Qur’anic verse itself.

In Al-Shiʿr wa-al-Shuʿarāʾ, Ibn Qutaybah identifies Khidāsh ibn Zuhayr as a distinguished pre-Islamic poet of Qays. The sources examined do not provide sufficient evidence that he also lived into the Islamic period. Nevertheless, a dictionary’s citation of a verse alone does not independently establish its precise composition date or the authenticity of every transmitted detail.

What this witness clearly establishes is the expression kaʾsan dihāqan in early Arabic poetry and its connection with a full vessel.

Ibn Qutaybah, Al-Shiʿr wa-al-Shuʿarāʾ: Khidāsh ibn Zuhayr

Continuity and abundance

Another use of this family describes things densely or continuously arranged together.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn uses iddahaqati al-ḥijāratu for stones packed closely together, some fitting among others.

Here the point is not a full vessel but the compression and close connection of parts.

Some lexicographers and commentators have accordingly connected dihāq, when describing a cup, with abundance and succession.

Two matters should be distinguished: the plentiful contents of a single cup and the successive arrival of cups for drinkers.

The first directly describes the vessel’s condition. The second may be a related, defensible interpretation, but does not follow equally clearly from the cup’s description alone.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: poetic witness and packed stones

3. The Qur’anic occurrence

Al-Nabaʾ 78:34

وَكَأْسًا دِهَاقًا

wa-kaʾsan dihāqan

Translation: And a brimming cup.

The verse describes blessings prepared for the mindful of God.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:31 speaks of their success. Al-Nabaʾ 78:32 mentions gardens and grapes; Al-Nabaʾ 78:33 speaks of companions of equal age; and Al-Nabaʾ 78:34 continues these blessings with a brimming cup.

In this construction, kaʾsan is a noun and dihāqan describes it.

Arabic kaʾs usually denotes a cup containing drink. The verse therefore concerns a cup with a drink, rather than merely the shape of an empty vessel.

Dihāqan expresses the condition of this cup.

Does dihāqan mean brimming or successive?

Early lexical and exegetical reports give three main interpretations.

The first interpretation: full and brimming

This is supported by the direct uses dahaqa al-kaʾsa and adhaqa al-kaʾsa.

The verse attributed to Khidāsh ibn Zuhayr, pairing fa-atraʿnā with kaʾsan dihāqan, provides independent linguistic evidence for this meaning.

“A brimming cup” is accordingly a clear rendering of the Qur’anic expression.

The second interpretation: successive and plentiful

In Jāmiʿ al-Bayān, Al-Ṭabarī transmits reports explaining dihāq as mutatābiʿah: coming in succession.

On this reading, the cup or its drink is described as supplied plentifully and repeatedly to its drinkers.

Al-Ṭabarī combines fullness and successive supply in his interpretation of the verse.

This reading has a connection with the Arabic branch concerning density and continuity. Compared with the fullness of a cup, however, it requires more contextual explanation.

Kaʾsan dihāqan itself describes a cup, without a separate expression specifying a number of cups or repeated drinking.

Succession may therefore be recognized as a lexical and exegetical interpretation, while “brimming” has more direct support for the base translation.

The third interpretation: clear

Some reports also explain dihāqan as ṣāfiyah: clear.

This may concern the clarity of the drink, but its connection with the recorded core use of filling a cup is less apparent.

Preferring “clear” would require independent linguistic evidence that dihāq described this quality in a cup without further indication.

The existence of that interpretation in some exegetical reports is not by itself sufficient to replace the meaning of fullness.

Other Qur’anic descriptions of heavenly drinks likewise do not establish that all their qualities are contained in dihāqan itself.

Such drinks may have other qualities, but these must be established through their own relevant expressions.

Al-Ṭabarī: three interpretations and fullness with succession

The preferred meaning in the verse

Weighing the evidence, “full and brimming” is preferable for dihāqan.

This rests on three grounds: the established verb for filling a cup, the Arabic poetic witness, and the meaning’s direct fit with kaʾsan.

“And a brimming cup” is consequently concise and preserves the word’s central quality.

This does not categorically reject every other use of the D H Q family. It identifies the meaning with stronger support in this particular expression.

4. Morphological and syntactic points

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:34, dihāqan describes kaʾsan syntactically and accordingly takes the accusative case.

One might ask why kaʾs is feminine in Arabic while dihāq appears masculine.

Ibn Sīdah explains that dihāq can be a verbal noun used adjectivally, like certain verbal nouns that describe masculine and feminine nouns without a change in form.

In this construction, its appearance does not necessarily mark an ordinary masculine adjective. A verbal noun can describe something’s condition.

This explanation fits the verse and prevents treating dihāqan as incompatible with the gender of kaʾs merely on the basis of its appearance.

The Quranic Arabic Corpus labels the word an accusative adjective. This identifies its grammatical role and need not conflict with a lexical discussion of the form’s verbal-noun or adjectival origin.

Only this form occurs in the Qur’an. Differences in vocalization, prefixes or non-Qur’anic lexical forms should not increase the number of Qur’anic derivatives.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: accusative adjective at the root’s sole occurrence

5. Other forms in Arabic

Dahaqa and adhaqa

These two verbs express filling when used of a vessel.

Adhaqa al-kaʾsa means he filled the cup completely.

With an object such as water, the verb may express emptying it or pouring it out forcefully.

Other recorded uses include pressing, applying pressure, breaking or cutting something.

The meaning must therefore be determined through the object and context.

Iddahaqa

Arabic iddahaqati al-ḥijāratu describes the dense packing and close connection of stones.

This relates to the family’s other branches through compression and accumulation, but does not mean filling a cup.

Dahq and dahqah

Lexical sources also record dahq for intense pressure.

Dahaq, with a vowel on the middle consonant, is also the name of an implement consisting of two pieces of wood used to compress the lower leg. This vocalization should be distinguished from dahq, intense pressure; the implement likewise illustrates the family’s connection with mechanical pressure.

Dahqah is recorded in some uses for an amount of wealth given to someone.

This concerns giving a substantial portion of wealth, but should not create a new definition of Qur’anic dihāq.

Dahdaqa and dahdaqah

Some sources record dahdaqa for breaking into pieces and dahdaqah for the movement or turning of something while boiling.

These forms have a four-consonant structure. Similar sounds and meanings alone do not establish that they all derive directly from the triliteral D H Q.

They may be examined as related forms in lexical research, without making them a proven single derivational branch.

Dihqān: a word of different origin

Arabic lexicons record dihqān and duhqān, including the meaning “merchant.” Historically, this Arabized Persian word has also denoted a landowner, a village notable or a person of local influence. It should not be identified with native D H Q formations.

Despite its resemblance to D H Q, lexical sources recognize it as an Arabized Persian word.

It should therefore not be treated without evidence as a derivative of dahaqa.

Similar consonants alone do not prove a historical relationship.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab: adjectival verbal noun, dahaq and dihqān

6. Comparison with nearby roots

D H Q (dahaqa) and M L ʾ (malaʾa)

Malaʾa means to fill and is used in various settings for filling a vessel, space or capacity.

Dahaqa also concerns filling when its object is a cup, while kaʾsun dihāqun particularly emphasizes complete fullness and contents reaching the rim.

The roots overlap when describing filling a cup, but M L ʾ has a broader family of uses for filling and fullness.

D H Q cannot, however, always be claimed to express greater intensity than M L ʾ. The distinction is evident in certain constructions, not necessarily every use of both roots.

D H Q (dahaqa) and F Y Ḍ (fāḍa)

F Y Ḍ concerns flowing, abundance and overflowing.

A dihāq cup is brimming, but this does not necessarily imply that its drink spills out.

Where overflowing is expressed, the liquid’s outward movement or spread can matter. Dihāq, when describing a cup, emphasizes the vessel’s full condition.

“A brimming cup” is therefore more accurate than “a cup with drink flowing out,” unless a separate context indicates such flowing.

D H Q (dahaqa) and K ʾ S (kaʾs)

Kaʾs names the cup or drinking vessel, whereas dihāq describes its condition.

This distinction matters in Al-Nabaʾ 78:34 because the meaning is formed by combining two words.

Kaʾsan identifies the thing being described; dihāqan gives its quality.

“Drink” or “cup” should consequently not be presented as the independent semantic core of D H Q. The root also has Arabic uses outside drinking vessels.

7. Translation challenges

Several points should guide the translation of wa-kaʾsan dihāqan.

First, dihāqan is not the name of a particular drink. The expression does not name the drink; supplying wine or another specific beverage in the direct translation would add information beyond the wording.

Second, Persian labālab conveys a vessel filled nearly to its rim. It expresses the established meaning of dihāq without a lengthy explanation.

Third, both “successive” and “clear” are reported in lexical and exegetical sources, but their grounds for preference in this construction are weaker than those for fullness. They may be explained in the study without being added to the base translation without sufficient reason.

Fourth, dihāq should not absorb every quality of heavenly drinks. Sweetness, purity, enjoyment, absence of harm and other possible qualities each need their own relevant evidence.

Translation should preserve what the word directly expresses, leaving broader explanations in their appropriate place.

8. Translation proposal for Forqan

For the expression:

وَكَأْسًا دِهَاقًا

wa-kaʾsan dihāqan

The preferred rendering is:

“And a brimming cup.”

“And a completely full cup” is also clear and accurate. “Brimming” is shorter and better conveys fullness nearly to the rim.

“And cups in succession” reflects one transmitted interpretation, but turns singular kaʾsan into a plural. For a direct translation, it is not preferable to the first meaning.

“And a clear cup” also reflects a reported interpretation, but its connection with the direct use adhaqa al-kaʾsa is no clearer than the connection of “brimming.”

“A brimming cup” is accordingly the most suitable base equivalent proposed here for Forqan.

9. Conclusion

Arabic D H Q has several related branches: filling a vessel, density and compression, intense pressure and, in certain constructions, forcefully emptying or pouring out liquid.

These uses have semantic connections, but do not establish a single, exceptionless historical image.

Only dihāqan occurs in the Qur’an. In kaʾsan dihāqan it describes a full, brimming cup. “Successive” and “clear” are also reported in early sources, but fullness has stronger direct lexical and poetic support.

Short definition: D H Q is a family of uses concerning filling, compression and intense pressure; when describing a cup, it denotes complete fullness to the brim. Brief root meaning: Filling and crowding, compression and pressure; of a cup, being full to the brim.

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:34, dihāqan is therefore best rendered “brimming,” without adding the drink’s type, successive cups or clarity to the word’s central meaning without sufficient evidence.
