Core meaning: bending, turning, or inclining something away from its initial direction; bodily uses include the side or flank, while interpersonal uses include turning toward someone in support and kindness or turning away.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

The three-consonant Arabic root ʿ ṭ f concerns bending, twisting, inclination, and changes of direction. Such a change may affect an object, such as a bending branch, or a living being's movement, such as turning the head and neck. It can also describe human relations: turning toward someone with help and kindness or turning away.

In early Arabic, ʿaṭafa al-shayʾ means bending or inclining something. ʿAṭafa raʾsahu means turning or inclining one's head. Inʿaṭafa describes something bending or changing direction.

For example, bending a flexible branch away from a straight position can be expressed with ʿaṭafa. The branch has not been destroyed or necessarily broken; its direction or shape has changed.

A river or road may likewise have a stretch that turns away from its previous course. Such a bend or turning point is called a munʿaṭaf.

This use shows that ʿaṭf need not mean moving an entire object from one place to another. The object may stay where it is while part of it bends or changes direction.

Within this semantic field, ʿiṭf denotes the side or flank of a human or animal. Lexical explanations variously include the shoulder, the side of the neck, or the side of the body from the head to the waist or hip.

ʿIṭfān denotes the body's two sides, right and left.

This designation relates to bending and turning the body toward either side. It does not prove that the bodily sense historically arose after the sense of bending.

In interpersonal usage, ʿaṭafa ʿalā fulān can mean turning toward someone with support, kindness, and attention to their circumstances.

The same family can also express turning away, showing one's back, or returning toward someone with harmful intent.

This distinction matters because modern Persian ʿāṭefeh usually evokes affection and compassion. The historical Arabic family is broader and is not confined to kindness.

The root's central semantic field is bending and changing direction. Whether the turn is toward or away from someone, and whether it involves kindness or hostility, depends on construction and context.

2. Early poetic evidence and its historical setting

An early example of this family occurs in a verse attributed to Labid ibn Rabiʿah:

وَمَجُودٍ مِنْ صُبَابَاتِ الْكَرَى
عَاطِفِ النُّمْرُقِ صَدْقِ الْمُبْتَذَلْ

wa-majūdin min ṣubābāti al-karā / ʿāṭifi al-numruqi ṣidqi al-mubtadhal

“And one overcome by lingering drowsiness, bending his cushion, yet sturdy when he puts himself to work.”

In this verse, ʿāṭif al-numruq concerns bending a cushion.

Numruq denotes a small cushion or pillow. The poet describes a drowsy person bending his cushion as he leans upon it.

Here ʿaṭf denotes neither kindness nor turning away from someone; it describes bending a physical object.

The example illuminates the root's physical sense: changing an object's shape or direction without breaking it.

Labid was a poet who lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This verse is transmitted in his collected poetry and lexical works, but its precise date has not been independently established in this study. It therefore illustrates early usage without conclusively establishing composition before the Quranic revelation.

Another example comes from Abu Wajzah al-Saʿdi, a poet of the Umayyad period:

الْعَاطِفُونَ تَحِينَ مَا مِنْ عَاطِفٍ
وَالْمُنْعِمُونَ يَدًا إِذَا مَا أَنْعَمُوا

al-ʿāṭifūna taḥīna mā min ʿāṭifin / wa-al-munʿimūna yadan idhā mā anʿamū

“They turn to give support when no supporter can be found; and when they bestow kindness, their hands are generous.”

Here al-ʿāṭifūn denotes people who return to help others in a time of need and support them.

Unlike Labid's example about bending a cushion, this use concerns human relationships.

Turning toward another is accompanied by help and support.

Support and kindness are established senses of this family; the poem's setting and the pairing of al-ʿāṭifūn with al-munʿimūn help identify the intended sense here.

Abu Wajzah lived after the emergence of Islam. His verse thus attests continued usage in early Arabic, rather than supplying independent pre-Islamic evidence.

Together, these examples show that the family can describe both physical bending and movement and human social behavior.

Lexicons also record ʿaṭafa ʿalayhi for returning toward someone with something they find unwelcome.

The family's uses should therefore not all be reduced to support and compassion. Direction and the nature of the action matter in each sentence.

3. Lexical and Quranic forms

Important forms in Arabic include ʿaṭafa, yaʿṭifu, ʿaṭf, ʿiṭf, ʿāṭif, ʿaṭūf, ʿaṭṭāf, ʿaṭīf, ʿiṭāf, ʿaṭṭafa, taʿaṭṭafa, taʿāṭafa, istaʿṭafa, inʿaṭafa, and munʿaṭaf.

Although they share a root, these forms differ in function and meaning.

Bending and changing direction

In physical usage, ʿaṭafa concerns bending, turning, and inclining.

Inʿaṭafa describes something bending or changing direction.

Munʿaṭaf can name a bend or turning point, such as a turn in a route.

The Form II verb ʿaṭṭafa also means bending. Some uses involve repetition or intensity, but Form II alone does not establish these features in every sentence.

The side or flank of the body

ʿIṭf denotes the side of the body, including the shoulder, neck area, and flank.

ʿIṭfān denotes the body's two sides.

Recorded plurals of ʿiṭf include aʿṭāf, ʿiṭāf, and ʿuṭūf.

In this branch, the word names a bodily part rather than the act of bending.

This distinction matters in the Quranic verse as well: ʿiṭf is a noun, while the sense of bending comes from an active participle belonging to another root.

Turning toward someone and showing kindness

ʿAṭafa ʿalayhi can express turning toward someone with support and compassion.

Taʿaṭṭafa ʿalayhi is used for treating someone kindly, doing good to them, and showing concern.

ʿĀṭif and ʿaṭūf can describe a compassionate, supportive person.

In social contexts, taʿāṭafū refers to people showing mutual kindness and concern.

Istaʿṭafahu can mean asking for kindness or seeking another's attention and compassion.

These meanings must not be generalized to every form. For example, ʿiṭf in Al-Hajj 22:9 does not mean affection or love.

Coverings and clothing

Arabic ʿiṭāf and miʿṭaf have been used for a mantle or covering placed over the shoulders and sides of the body.

The designation relates to a covering lying over the sides and wrapping around the body.

In modern Arabic, miʿṭaf is used for a coat or outer garment within this semantic field.

The modern meaning of miʿṭaf should not replace the historical use of ʿiṭāf, since garments and styles of clothing differ across periods.

ʿIṭāf is also recorded for a sword. Lexicographers explain the designation by the sword's being carried at the body's side, in the place of a mantle.

Such lexical explanations connect an object's name with its position against the body without making the object identical in nature to a mantle.

The Quranic form

The root occurs in the Quran only as the noun ʿiṭfihi in Al-Hajj 22:9.

This form combines ʿiṭf with the masculine singular pronoun -hi, meaning “his side or flank.”

The verbs ʿaṭafa, taʿaṭṭafa, and inʿaṭafa and nouns such as ʿāṭif and ʿāṭifah do not occur in the Quran.

A Quranic study of this root should therefore focus on ʿiṭfihi rather than imposing broader Arabic uses or the modern Persian meaning of affection upon the verse.

4. Thāniya ʿiṭfihi: turning one's side and arrogant disregard

The root's sole Quranic occurrence is in Al-Hajj.

Understanding it requires attention to the preceding verse:

وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يُجَادِلُ فِي اللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ وَلَا هُدًى وَلَا كِتَابٍ مُنِيرٍ

wa-mina al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa-lā hudan wa-lā kitābin munīr

Al-Ḥajj 22:8

Al-Hajj 22:8

“Among people is one who disputes concerning The God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating Book.”

The verse describes someone disputing concerning The God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating Book to support his speech.

This matters because the subject is not simply asking questions, discussing something, or holding a different interpretation.

It is disputation lacking a basis in knowledge and guidance.

The same person's condition is then described:

ثَانِيَ عِطْفِهِ لِيُضِلَّ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ۖ لَهُ فِي الدُّنْيَا خِزْيٌ ۖ وَنُذِيقُهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ

thāniya ʿiṭfihi li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīli Allāh; lahu fī al-dunyā khizyun wa-nudhīquhu yawma al-qiyāmati ʿadhāba al-ḥarīq

Al-Ḥajj 22:9

Al-Hajj 22:9

“Turning his side away to lead astray from the path of The God. For him is disgrace in this world, and on the Day of Resurrection We make him taste the punishment of burning.”

The expression thāniya ʿiṭfihi is central to understanding this root's Quranic use.

The expression's bodily image

Thāniya belongs to th n y and is the active participle of thanā: one who bends, turns, or folds something.

ʿIṭfihi belongs to ʿ ṭ f and denotes his side or flank.

The construction's direct sense is therefore “while turning or twisting his side away.”

The act of bending is conveyed by thāniya, while ʿiṭfihi identifies the bodily part being turned.

This morphological distinction matters.

Thāniya and ʿiṭfihi do not share a root, although together they form a single image.

Arabic thanā ʿiṭfahu is an established expression for turning away, showing one's back, and disregarding someone.

Imagine someone being addressed who turns his neck and side away instead of facing the speaker and listening.

Such behavior may signal disregard or unwillingness to hear.

Alternatively, someone may lift his head, turn his neck, and draw his body aside to display superiority.

Thus thanā ʿiṭfahu also describes turning away arrogantly.

Nevertheless, arrogance is not the direct meaning of the noun ʿiṭf.

ʿIṭf names the side or flank. Turning away comes from the expression thanā ʿiṭfahu, and arrogance from its established usage and the verse's context.

The syntax of thāniya ʿiṭfihi

Thāniya is accusative here and functions as a circumstantial expression.

It describes the same person who disputes concerning The God in the preceding verse.

The passage first identifies his speech and then presents his manner while engaging in it.

He disputes concerning The God without knowledge or guidance while twisting his side in a gesture of disregard.

Thāniya ʿiṭfihi is therefore not an independent statement about someone else; it continues the description of the disputant.

The connection places his disputation and his turning away in a single picture.

Must the verse describe a literal bodily movement?

Thāniya ʿiṭfihi draws on a familiar bodily movement, but literary usage can employ it to describe arrogant, dismissive behavior.

For example, Persian, like English, can say, “He turned his back on the truth.”

This can describe physically turning one's back, but it can also mean rejecting and disregarding the truth.

The Quranic image of the side and neck matters, yet it does not require assuming that the person literally turns his neck through a specific angle every time he argues.

The expression conveys dismissive turning away accompanied by self-importance.

The bodily image should nevertheless be retained in translation rather than reduced to “he argues arrogantly.”

That rendering conveys a contextual idea but removes the original image.

5. Turning away and leading others astray

Thāniya ʿiṭfihi is followed by:

“Li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīli Allāh.”

In the reading used for the verse here, li-yuḍilla is Form IV and means leading astray.

The passage therefore concerns more than someone personally unaware of the right way: his behavior also relates to leading astray from the path of The God.

This becomes more significant alongside the preceding verse.

Al-Hajj 22:8 describes disputation without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating Book.

Al-Hajj 22:9 adds arrogant turning away and leading astray from the path of The God.

The person's defining feature is therefore not merely lack of knowledge.

Someone may not know a subject yet ask questions, request evidence, or examine different views to understand it.

Such behavior is not inherently arrogant disregard.

Another person may insist on an unsupported claim and refuse to examine clear statements even when hearing them.

If he also uses his standing and influence to draw others away from truth, his behavior approaches the picture described in these two verses.

For example, someone may speak publicly about a subject he knows insufficiently. Another presents clear evidence correcting him, but pride and fear of losing social standing lead him to disregard the truth and discourage others from attending to it.

The issue then includes his response to evidence, beyond the initial error of knowledge.

This example does not mean that everyone who maintains a position in a discussion is arrogant or misleading.

The passage combines several features: unsupported disputation, turning away, and leading astray.

They should be read together, without making any one of them a label for condemning every opponent.

The readings li-yuḍilla and li-yaḍilla

Two forms of this verb are transmitted in recognized Quranic readings.

Li-yuḍilla, with u after the initial y, is transitive and means leading astray.

Li-yaḍilla, with a after the initial y, is Form I and means the person's own going astray.

The distinction affects how his role is understood.

The first emphasizes leading others astray; the second, his own straying.

Neither changes the image of thāniya ʿiṭfihi or its sense of turning away from truth.

The reading difference concerns ḍ l l, not ʿ ṭ f, and does not signal a change in the meaning of ʿiṭf.

In analyzing the prefixed lām, one should distinguish deliberate purpose from the outcome of conduct. The prefix alone does not establish every detail of inward intention, although li-yuḍilla clearly connects the conduct with leading others astray.

6. Kindness and turning away: different directions within one family

An important feature of this Arabic family is its use both for turning affectionately toward someone and for turning arrogantly away.

Direction and sentence structure help explain the difference.

ʿAṭafa ʿalā: turning toward someone and giving support

In a compassionate use, ʿaṭafa ʿalā fulān concerns turning toward someone and attending to their situation.

For example, someone may need help, and another, seeing their circumstances, may turn back to support them.

Here ʿaṭf involves kindness and care.

The preposition ʿalā alone does not guarantee compassion. Early Arabic also records this construction for returning toward someone with harmful intent.

Context must therefore clarify the action's nature.

ʿAṭafa ilā: inclining toward something

With ʿaṭafa ilā, direction toward a person, place, or subject comes to the foreground.

The construction can describe someone changing course toward another person.

This orientation need not imply affection: the purpose may be help, conversation, opposition, or something else.

ʿAṭafa ʿan: turning away

ʿAṭafa ʿan can describe changing direction away from something.

Here the movement draws away from its object.

The Quranic expression thāniya ʿiṭfihi itself, however, contains no preposition ʿan. Turning away is understood from the established expression thanā ʿiṭfahu and the verse's context.

A preposition absent from this expression must therefore not be presented as part of its own lexical structure.

Is ʿaṭf inherently commendable?

These uses show that the root has no single inherent moral value.

Turning toward a person in need and supporting them can be commendable.

Turning away from truth and arrogantly dismissing others' words is blameworthy.

Both involve a change of orientation, but its direction and object differ.

The modern meaning of Persian ʿāṭefeh should not lead us to read affection and compassion into every derivative in the Quran or early Arabic.

In Al-Hajj 22:9, the root occurs in a description of turning away, not of affection or support.

7. Comparison with related semantic fields

ʿ ṭ f and th n y

These two roots occur together in Al-Hajj 22:9.

Thanā concerns bending, folding, and turning something.

ʿIṭf names the side or flank of the body.

In thāniya ʿiṭfihi, bending is conveyed by the active participle from th n y, while the bodily part is named by the noun from ʿ ṭ f.

Their combination does not make them one root.

Thanā can refer to folding many kinds of objects, while ʿiṭf can name the body's side without the verb thanā.

The complete meaning of thāniya ʿiṭfihi should therefore not be treated as the definition of ʿ ṭ f itself.

ʿ ṭ f and l w y

The root l w y concerns twisting, winding, and turning.

Arabic lawā ʿunuqahu means turning or twisting one's neck.

Its bodily image is close to thanā ʿiṭfahu.

In Al-Munafiqun 63:5, the Quran describes people turning their heads:

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ تَعَالَوْا يَسْتَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ لَوَّوْا رُءُوسَهُمْ وَرَأَيْتَهُمْ يَصُدُّونَ وَهُمْ مُسْتَكْبِرُونَ

wa-idhā qīla lahum taʿālaw yastaghfir lakum rasūlu Allāhi lawwaw ruʾūsahum wa-raʾaytahum yaṣuddūna wa-hum mustakbirūn

Al-Munafiqun 63:5

Al-Munafiqun 63:5

“When they are told, ‘Come, so that the Messenger of The God may ask forgiveness for you,’ they turn their heads, and you see them turning away in arrogance.”

Its bodily image relates to Al-Hajj 22:9.

In Al-Munafiqun, however, lawwaw belongs to l w y and ruʾūsahum names their heads. In Al-Hajj, thāniya belongs to th n y and ʿiṭfihi to ʿ ṭ f.

Both expressions convey arrogant turning away, but their lexical structures differ.

ʿ ṭ f and ʿ r ḍ

The Form IV verb aʿraḍa, from ʿ r ḍ, means turning away and disregarding.

Thanā ʿiṭfahu can express similar behavior through the image of bending the side and neck.

Quranic iʿrāḍ denotes turning away and disregard without necessarily presenting a specific bodily movement.

Thanā ʿiṭfahu instead draws on a tangible bodily posture to describe the behavior.

Two expressions can thus refer to similar conduct while presenting it differently.

ʿ ṭ f and ṣ ʿ r

Arabic ṣ ʿ r relates to a crooked neck and, in human behavior, turning one's face away arrogantly.

The Quran says in Luqman 31:18:

وَلَا تُصَعِّرْ خَدَّكَ لِلنَّاسِ وَلَا تَمْشِ فِي الْأَرْضِ مَرَحًا ۖ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ

wa-lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-al-nāsi wa-lā tamshi fī al-arḍi maraḥan; inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūr

Luqmān 31:18

Luqman 31:18

“Do not turn your cheek away from people in arrogance, nor walk exultantly upon the earth. Indeed, The God does not love anyone self-important and boastful.”

Khaddaka here denotes the cheek, the side of the face.

In Al-Hajj 22:9, ʿiṭfihi concerns the side or flank of the body.

Both verses use bodily imagery to express arrogant behavior, but their bodily terms and verbs differ.

Ṣaʿʿara and ʿaṭafa must therefore not be equated.

ʿ ṭ f and ḥ n w

The root ḥ n w concerns bending and inclining; in some uses it also relates to kindness and concern for another.

Its semantic field is close to that of ʿ ṭ f in this respect.

A person bending toward a child to care for them, for example, can provide an image of attention and kindness.

The similarity does not establish that ḥanā and ʿaṭafa are synonymous in every use.

Each root has independent evidence and morphological structures, and each derivative must be understood from its own usage.

ʿ ṭ f and r ḥ m

Raḥmah concerns mercy and merciful conduct.

Interpersonal uses of ʿāṭifah can likewise involve compassion and kindness.

Raḥmah and ʿaṭf nevertheless do not have identical meanings.

In ʿaṭf, orientation and turning toward someone may be prominent; raḥmah concerns mercy in the relationship and conduct.

Mercy may prompt someone to attend to another, but attending and being merciful remain lexically distinguishable.

Moreover, mercy has a broad Quranic range, whereas ʿ ṭ f occurs only as ʿiṭfihi in a context of turning away.

Mercy should therefore not be inserted into the translation of this Quranic derivative.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The root's only Quranic form is ʿiṭfihi in Al-Hajj 22:9.

Its basic equivalent is “his side” or “his flank.”

Since it occurs in thāniya ʿiṭfihi, the complete expression must also be considered.

Word: ʿiṭfihi.

Root: ʿ ṭ f.

Word type: a singular noun with an attached third-person masculine singular pronoun; genitive in an annexation construction with thāniya, and semantically the object of its bending action.

Brief root meaning: bending and changing direction; the side or flank; turning toward or away.

Meaning in this verse: his side; in thāniya ʿiṭfihi, turning the side as an expression of turning away.

For the expression:

Thāniya ʿiṭfihi.

Suggested translation:

“While turning his side away.”

In the connected rendering of the verse, the following is also suitable:

“Turning his side away to lead astray from the path of The God.”

This preserves the bodily image, while arrogant disregard can be clarified in the explanation.

“He turns away arrogantly” also fits the contextual meaning, but omits the side and the bending body.

“He turns his neck arrogantly” is semantically defensible because ʿiṭf can include the side of the neck and shoulder. “Side,” however, better preserves the noun's range without unnecessarily narrowing it to the neck.

ʿIṭfihi here must not be translated as “his affection,” “his kindness,” or “his love.” Those senses belong to other branches of the family and do not fit the construction and context of Al-Hajj 22:9.

The following equivalents suit related Arabic expressions:

ʿAṭafa al-shayʾ: he bent or turned something.

Inʿaṭafa: it bent or changed direction.

Munʿaṭaf: a bend or turning point in a route.

ʿIṭf: a side or flank of the body.

ʿIṭāf: a mantle or covering resting on the shoulders and sides.

ʿAṭafa ʿalayhi, in a compassionate use: he turned toward him and treated him kindly.

Taʿāṭafū: they showed mutual kindness and concern.

These equivalents show that each derivative's construction and context determine its meaning; one common translation cannot cover them all.

Conclusion

The Arabic root ʿ ṭ f concerns bending, inclination, twisting, and changes of direction.

Its semantic field includes bending objects, turns in a route, the body's side or flank, coverings worn over the shoulders, and people's turning toward or away from others.

Interpersonal uses can involve support, kindness, and compassion, but the family also describes returning with harmful intent and turning arrogantly away.

ʿAṭf therefore does not inherently mean affection or commendable behavior.

The Quran contains only ʿiṭfihi, in Al-Hajj 22:9.

It occurs in thāniya ʿiṭfihi, which uses the image of turning the side or neck to describe disregard.

The context describes someone disputing concerning The God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating Book, whose arrogant turning away is connected with leading astray from His path.

This must not be generalized to every disagreement or scholarly question. The criticism concerns a particular combination of conduct, not discussion alone or merely rejecting someone else's opinion.

The central field of ʿ ṭ f is thus “bending and changing direction,” while the Quranic derivative ʿiṭfihi means “his side or flank.” In thāniya ʿiṭfihi, turning the side becomes an image of arrogant disregard.

Forqan's translation should preserve the image of the side and explain arrogance and disregard for truth contextually, since the root itself is exclusive neither to arrogance nor to kindness.
