Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root N DH R

Vow, warning, commitment, and awakening before consequence

Qur’anic forms of this root

The root ن ذ ر (N DH R) gathers two semantic currents: nadhr, vow or commitment, and indhār, warning. In the first branch, the human being binds himself before God to a commitment. In the second branch, the divine message warns the human being before consequences arrive.

The central meaning of the root

The root ن ذ ر (N DH R) is important in the Qur’an because it gathers two currents of meaning: vow and warning.

In the first branch, the human being binds himself before God to a commitment. This meaning appears in words such as نَذْر (nadhr) and نُذُور (nudhūr). In the second branch, the divine message warns the human being before consequences arrive. This meaning appears in words such as أَنذَرَ (andhara), نَذِير (nadhīr), مُنذِر (mundhir), and مُنذَرِين (mundharīn).

At first glance, vow and warning may seem like two separate meanings. But both are tied to one shared truth: before the result arrives, the human being is placed before something serious. Either he himself gives a commitment to God beforehand, or he is warned by God beforehand so that before opportunity closes, he may awaken.

So N DH R is the root of commitment and warning before consequence. It pulls the human being out of heedlessness and makes him responsible before his moral future.

The image of the nadhīr: a watcher before danger

To understand نَذِير (nadhīr), one may imagine a watcher who sees danger before it arrives and awakens his people. If a flood, attack, or disaster is coming, a compassionate watcher calls out before the danger arrives so that people still have time to take refuge.

Divine indhār is of this kind: awakening before consequence. Qur’anic warning is not meant to crush the human being; it is meant to awaken him. Indhār is a serious shock to the heedless soul, so that before the consequences of its choices surround it, it may see the path of return.

The nadhīr is not the enemy of the human being. The nadhīr is hard-spoken mercy: one who does not hide danger, because hiding danger would be betrayal of the human soul.

Nadhr: a commitment the human being places upon himself

The word نَذْر (nadhr) refers to a commitment the human being places upon himself, a commitment that should be joined with God, truthfulness, and responsibility.

In Al-Baqarah 2:270, it says:

وَمَا أَنْفَقْتُمْ مِنْ نَفَقَةٍ أَوْ نَذَرْتُمْ مِنْ نَذْرٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ يَعْلَمُهُ

Whatever you spend, or whatever vow you vow, God knows it. This verse places vow beside spending. Both involve something coming out of the human being: wealth, ability, will, or commitment. The important point is that God knows it. Therefore a vow is not merely a spoken sentence or social custom. It stands before God and must be joined with sincerity.

If a vow arises from truthfulness, gratitude, and responsibility, it may become a way of forming the self. But if it becomes bargaining with God, superstitious fear, psychological pressure, or religious display, it moves away from the Qur’anic spirit.

The vow of Mary’s mother: dedicating purity to God

One of the most beautiful examples of vow in the Qur’an is the vow of Mary’s mother. In Al-Imran 3:35, it says:

رَبِّ إِنِّي نَذَرْتُ لَكَ مَا فِي بَطْنِي مُحَرَّرًا

My Lord, I have vowed to You what is in my womb, dedicated and set free. This vow is free of bargaining logic. Mary’s mother does not say: if You give me something, I will do something in return. She places the child in her womb in the direction of servanthood and service to God.

The word مُحَرَّرًا (muḥarraran) is very important. It means set free from human ownership, selfish use, personal possession, and being trapped in worldly bonds. This vow shows that a healthy vow can be liberation, not captivity.

But this verse cannot be used as an excuse to sacrifice the freedom and dignity of human beings in the name of vow. Mary’s mother releases her child from personal benefit and family ownership; she does not turn the child into a tool of her own will.

A vow of silence for the All-Merciful

In Maryam 19:26, Mary is told that if she sees any human being, she should say:

إِنِّي نَذَرْتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ صَوْمًا فَلَنْ أُكَلِّمَ الْيَوْمَ إِنْسِيًّا

I have vowed a fast for the All-Merciful, so today I will not speak to any human being. Here vow is connected with silence. This silence is not escape from truth; it is taking refuge in God at a moment when human speech cannot carry the weight of truth.

This verse shows that vow is not always financial. Sometimes a vow is discipline of the tongue. Sometimes the human being must hold his speech before the noise of people and let God make the truth manifest.

Fulfilling the vow

In Al-Insan 76:7, regarding the people of goodness, it says:

يُوفُونَ بِالنَّذْرِ وَيَخَافُونَ يَوْمًا كَانَ شَرُّهُ مُسْتَطِيرًا

They fulfill the vow and fear a day whose harm is widespread. Here vow appears beside responsible fear of the great Day. The people of goodness do not merely have good feelings; they fulfill their commitment. Fulfilling a vow is a sign that the human being is serious before God.

But fulfilling a vow has value only when the vow itself is upon a right path. A person must not vow something that conflicts with justice, mercy, reason, or God’s limits. A vow does not make injustice lawful. A commitment bound to falsehood must not be sanctified in the name of loyalty.

Nudhūr: vows and entrusted responsibilities

The word نُذُور (nudhūr) is the plural of vow. In Al-Hajj 22:29, in connection with rites, fulfilling vows is mentioned.

This shows that vow can be part of worshipful and responsible life. But the Qur’an does not present it as dry and separate from purity, circling the House, the House itself, and remembrance of God. Vow has meaning within servanthood, not within superstition and social pressure.

A vow is healthy when it brings the human being closer to God, forms the self, and makes responsibility more serious. If it becomes blind fear, bargaining with God, or harm to oneself or others, its understanding must be revisited.

Andhara: warning before consequence

The second branch of this root is indhār: warning, making aware, and awakening before the final consequence arrives.

In Al-Anʿam 6:19, the Qur’an is presented as a means of warning the first audience and whoever the message reaches:

لِأُنْذِرَكُمْ بِهِ وَمَنْ بَلَغَ

So that I may warn you with it, and whoever it reaches. This verse shows that indhār is not limited to the first time and place of revelation. Whoever the message of the Qur’an reaches enters the circle of its warning.

In Al-Anbiya 21:45, the Prophet says:

إِنَّمَا أُنْذِرُكُمْ بِالْوَحْيِ

I only warn you by revelation. This verse is very important. The Prophet’s warning does not come from himself. He warns by revelation. Therefore Qur’anic indhār must not be turned into fabricated fear, baseless predictions, or religious pressure.

Nadhīr: the warner

The word نَذِير (nadhīr) means warner. The prophets are repeatedly introduced as bearers of good news and warners: bashīr and nadhīr.

In Al-Baqarah 2:119, it is said that the Prophet was sent with truth, as a bearer of good news and a warner. In Al-Furqan 25:1, the Furqan is sent down upon God’s servant so that he may be a warner to the worlds.

If there were only good news, the human being might fall into irresponsibility and imagined safety. If there were only warning, religion would become fear, pressure, and darkness. The Qur’an brings both together: good news for receptive hearts, and warning for hearts sunk in heedlessness or wrongdoing.

Mundhir: the one entrusted with warning

The word مُنذِر (mundhir) refers to someone entrusted with warning. In various verses, the Prophet is commanded to warn people, his near kin, or his own people. This shows that warning is part of prophetic responsibility.

The Prophet did not come to force people. He came to deliver the message clearly, give good news, warn, and awaken the human being before his choice. The mundhir does not own people’s hearts. He makes the danger clear.

Mundharīn: those who were warned

The word مُنذَرِين (mundharīn) points to those who have been warned. This word matters because the Qur’an repeatedly shows that the issue is not always lack of message. Many human beings heard the warning, but treated it lightly, mocked it, or turned away from it.

In As-Saffat 37:72, it says that warners were sent among earlier peoples. The following verses then speak of the outcome of those who did not take the warning seriously.

So when a people is mundhar, warned, it can no longer claim that no awakening reached it. Indhār makes the proof clear, but it does not force faith.

Indhār and human freedom

One deep point in the root N DH R is that warning is different from compulsion. The Qur’an warns, but it does not force the human being to believe. The Prophet warns, but he does not own people’s decisions. The nadhīr shows the danger, but choosing the path remains the human being’s responsibility.

If warning becomes a tool of control, unhealthy fear, social threat, or religious domination, it has moved away from the Qur’anic spirit. Qur’anic indhār must break heedlessness, not enslave the human being to fear.

Healthy warning opens the human being’s eyes. Unhealthy warning closes the human being’s reason. The Qur’an warns so that the human being may awaken, not so that he becomes thoughtless and will-less before other humans.

Who benefits from warning?

The Qur’an shows that not everyone benefits from warning. In Ya-Sin 36:11, it says:

إِنَّمَا تُنْذِرُ مَنِ اتَّبَعَ الذِّكْرَ وَخَشِيَ الرَّحْمَٰنَ بِالْغَيْبِ

You only warn the one who follows the Reminder and fears the All-Merciful in the unseen. Warning reaches many ears, but its real effect appears in the one who still has a relation with remembrance and reverent fear.

Therefore blockage is not always from absence of warning; sometimes it comes from the hardness and closure of the receiver. Just as rain has little effect on hard stone, indhār does not affect a heart that refuses to hear.

Warning and the Day of Regret

In Maryam 19:39, the command comes:

وَأَنْذِرْهُمْ يَوْمَ الْحَسْرَةِ

Warn them of the Day of Regret. The Day of Regret is the day when the human being sees the truth of his choices: the day when opportunities no longer return to his choice, and heedlessness, arrogance, and delay reveal themselves.

Regret is born when the opportunity for repair has closed. As long as the human being is in the world and within the field of will, the window is open: he can repent, repair, make a sound vow, do righteous action, and respond to warning. But once the consequence arrives, warning gives way to regret.

So warning about the Day of Regret is mercy. God awakens the human being before the window of will closes.

Bearer of good news and warner: balance between hope and warning

The Qur’an repeatedly introduces the Prophet as bashīr and nadhīr. These two must be understood together. Good news without warning turns religion into irresponsible comfort. Warning without good news turns religion into dark fear and despair.

The Qur’an neither puts the human being to sleep with false hope nor crushes the soul in fruitless terror. Good news is for those who return to truth, believe, do righteous action, and take the path of repair. Warning is for those who persist in injustice, heedlessness, arrogance, or association.

The balance of the divine message is this: the path of mercy is open, but the consequences of choices are also real.

Healthy vow and corrupted vow

Vow in the Qur’an can be a sign of truthfulness and commitment, but like every religious action, it can also become corrupted. A healthy vow brings the human being closer to God, forms the self, and clarifies responsibility. Such a vow comes from gratitude, truthfulness, and humility.

A corrupted vow, however, is bargaining with God. The human being wants, through the vow, to force God toward his own desire, or to place a heavy burden upon himself or others out of superstitious fear, or to build a religious image for himself.

A vow must not become a tool of harming oneself, family, the poor, children, or society. A vow cannot make something morally wrong sacred. Every commitment must be measured under the light of the Qur’an, reason, justice, and mercy.

Suggested rendering in Forqan

This root should not be forced into one dry and fixed translation. The semantic branch and the context of the verse must be considered.

The branch of human commitment

  • نَذَرَ (nadhara): vowed, made a commitment, made binding upon oneself.
  • نَذْر (nadhr): vow, commitment, covenant of servanthood.
  • نُذُور (nudhūr): vows, commitments, entrusted covenants.

The branch of divine awakening

  • أَنذَرَ (andhara): warned, made aware beforehand, gave warning.
  • نُذْر (nudhr): warning, awakening, alarm.
  • نَذِير (nadhīr): warner, awakener, one who makes danger clear.
  • مُنذِر (mundhir): warner, one entrusted with warning.
  • مُنذَرِين (mundharīn): those warned, those to whom the proof of warning has arrived.

Summary

The root ن ذ ر (N DH R) is the root of commitment and warning before consequence.

In the vow branch, the human being binds himself before God to a commitment. If this commitment arises from truthfulness, gratitude, and taqwa, it can form the self and free the human being from selfish ownership. But if it becomes bargaining, superstition, display, or pressure upon oneself and others, it moves away from the spirit of the Qur’an.

In the warning branch, God warns the human being before consequences arrive. The prophets are warners, but not compellers. They reveal danger, show the opportunity for awakening, and make the proof clear.

The religion of the Qur’an is not a religion of hunting heedless human beings; it is a path of awakening responsible souls. Before regret, warning comes. Before the opportunity closes, the path of return is shown. And if the human being is awake, he can turn warning into mercy.
