1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic F Ḥ SH concerns ugliness, reprehensibility, and offensiveness intensified beyond an acceptable degree. It can concern speech, conduct, human relationships, sexual acts, miserliness, and other objectionable matters.

Fuḥsh generally concerns grossly offensive conduct or speech. Fāḥish describes a person or thing whose objectionable character is pronounced. Fāḥishah names a grossly shameful act; faḥshāʾ belongs to the same field.

The family is not confined to sexual conduct, although important uses concern sexual relationships and transgression. The Qur’an calls zinā a fāḥishah, but uses the family elsewhere too, including faḥshāʾ in a setting of fearing poverty and withholding generosity.

The root occurs 24 times in the Qur’an, in two nominal families: seventeen instances of fāḥishah and its plural fawāḥish, and seven of faḥshāʾ. No verb or independent form such as faḥusha, fāḥish, faḥḥāsh, or tafaḥḥasha occurs.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: counts and forms

Precise study distinguishes the lexical sense of gross ugliness from the instances identified in different verses. One act may be explicitly called fāḥishah; elsewhere faḥshāʾ accompanies munkar, sūʾ, or baghy.

The family therefore names neither one specified sexual offense nor vaguely whatever an individual or society dislikes. It emphasizes gross offensiveness; Qur’anic criteria and instances must be established through the Qur’an’s own evidence.

2. The semantic core and important forms

Ugliness exceeding proper measure

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْفَاءُ وَالْحَاءُ وَالشِّينُ كَلِمَةٌ تَدُلُّ عَلَى قُبْحٍ فِي شَيْءٍ وَشَنَاعَةٍ

al-fāʾu wa-al-ḥāʾu wa-al-shīnu kalimatun tadullu ʿalā qubḥin fī shayʾin wa-shanāʿatin

“Fāʾ, ḥāʾ, and shīn indicate ugliness and gross offensiveness in something.”

He continues:

كُلُّ شَيْءٍ جَاوَزَ قَدْرَهُ فَهُوَ فَاحِشٌ

kullu shayʾin jāwaza qadrahu fa-huwa fāḥishun

“Whatever exceeds its proper measure is fāḥish.”

He qualifies this excess as concerning matters regarded as objectionable and disliked.

The qualification matters: mere abundance does not make something morally fāḥish.

Someone may be exceptionally generous or knowledgeable; abundance of knowledge or generosity is not fuḥsh merely because it is great.

Fāḥish can instead mark excessive ugliness, miserliness, aggression, or objectionable conduct.

Exceeding measure alone, detached from reprehensibility, is therefore not the root’s complete meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the definition and objectionable excess

Does fuḥsh originally name a physical movement?

Unlike roots with clear physical images, such as cutting or untying a knot, this family has no particular movement or tool recorded as its shared foundation.

Its fundamental meaning describes a thing’s offensive or reprehensible quality.

This can appear in observable conduct, including aggression, deception, or sexual misconduct; in insulting speech; or in extreme miserliness and social behavior exceeding acceptable bounds.

An invented image such as tearing modesty’s veil, exposing the body, or physically crossing a boundary must not become the foundation of every use.

Such images may describe particular instances but are not necessary components of the lexical definition.

Important Arabic forms

Form I faḥusha and faḥasha concern becoming ugly or objectionable, or speech or conduct becoming grossly offensive.

Fuḥsh is a verbal noun or noun for gross offensiveness. Modern Persian commonly associates it with swearing and obscene speech, but its Arabic field is wider.

Faḥshāʾ names gross ugliness and reprehensible conduct. It occurs seven times in the Qur’an.

Fāḥishah is the feminine form of fāḥish, established as a noun for a grossly shameful act. Its Qur’anic singular occurs thirteen times.

Fawāḥish is its plural, grossly shameful acts, occurring four times.

Fāḥish describes a person, statement, act, or thing whose reprehensibility is extreme. Arabic also uses it for excessive miserliness.

Faḥḥāsh is intensive, describing someone habitually offensive in speech or known for objectionable behavior.

Form IV afḥasha can concern uttering offensive speech or speaking abusively.

Form V tafaḥḥasha concerns manifesting offensive conduct or speech.

Form VI tafāḥasha can concern mutual abusive speech, or something becoming more grossly offensive.

Lexical sources also record faḥāshah as a noun concerning gross ugliness.

Some early uses give fuḥashāʾ as a plural of fāḥish for people of objectionable conduct. It must not be confused with faḥshāʾ, gross offensiveness.

Only the nominal families fāḥishah and faḥshāʾ from this wider Arabic family occur in the Qur’an.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: forms and faḥshāʾ versus fuḥashāʾ

A Qur’anic article must therefore distinguish attested Qur’anic words from derivatives recorded only in Arabic usage.

3. Early Arabic usage

Fāḥish: excessive miserliness

The pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s Muʿallaqah reads:

أَرَى الْمَوْتَ يَعْتَامُ الْكِرَامَ وَيَصْطَفِي
عَقِيلَةَ مَالِ الْفَاحِشِ الْمُتَشَدِّدِ

arā al-mawta yaʿtāmu al-kirāma wa-yaṣṭafī / ʿaqīlata māli al-fāḥishi al-mutashaddidi

“I see death choosing the noble and taking even the most prized wealth of the rigid miser.”

Al-fāḥish describes an extremely miserly person here.

The poet addresses death and the futility of excessive attachment to wealth: even the most possessive miser cannot ultimately preserve their possessions from death and transfer to others.

ʿAqīlata māli concerns the choicest, most prized wealth; al-mutashaddid emphasizes the person’s rigid grasping.

Fāḥish has no direct sexual reference here. It concerns financial conduct whose offensiveness has reached excessive miserliness.

The verse appears in established transmissions of Ṭarafah’s Muʿallaqah, with early commentaries explaining al-fāḥish through miserliness.

Sharḥ al-Muʿallaqāt al-Tisʿ: Ṭarafah’s witness and fāḥish

This pre-Qur’anic witness shows the family was not confined to sexual sins before later legal terminology developed.

Fuḥsh in speech: language exceeding decent bounds

Afḥasha fī al-kalāmi means uttering offensive or insulting speech.

Its content or manner may amount to swearing, humiliation, or gross discourtesy.

This must not be equated merely with explicitness.

Someone can state a difficult, unpleasant matter clearly without fuḥsh. Conversely, someone may gravely humiliate or insult another without obscene vocabulary.

Explicitness, stating an unpleasant truth, and abusive speech must remain distinct.

A statement’s content may be false or its delivery degrading; the two can coincide without being identical.

Verbal fuḥsh therefore concerns gross offensiveness in speech or verbal conduct, rather than mere loudness or explicit discussion of private matters.

Fāḥish describing an extreme condition

Arabic applies fāḥish beyond personal conduct to things exceeding usual or accepted bounds in a markedly objectionable way.

For example, legal and economic ghabnun fāḥishun concerns an unacceptable excess in price disparity or loss.

Its technical legal form must not be assigned to the pre-Islamic period without historical evidence.

Early witnesses more securely establish gross offensiveness beyond acceptable bounds; specific economic or legal uses require their own terminological history.

Fāḥish may mark a behavior’s severity without itself determining the offense, penalty, or legal prescription.

4. Qur’anic faḥshāʾ: grossly offensive conduct and speech

A. Bi-al-sūʾi wa-al-faḥshāʾi: Satan’s command

Al-Baqarah reads:

إِنَّمَا يَأْمُرُكُمْ بِالسُّوءِ وَالْفَحْشَاءِ وَأَنْ تَقُولُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

innamā yaʾmurukum bi-al-sūʾi wa-al-faḥshāʾi wa-an taqūlū ʿalā Allāhi mā lā taʿlamūna

“He commands you only to evil, indecency, and saying about The God what you do not know.”

Al-Baqarah 2:169

Al-sūʾ and al-faḥshāʾ appear together, followed by speaking about The God without knowledge.

Sūʾ belongs to S W ʾ, evil or what is objectionable; faḥshāʾ to F Ḥ SH, emphasizing gross offensiveness.

Their fields overlap; conjunction alone does not establish mutually exclusive classes of sins.

Sūʾ has a broad field of evil while faḥshāʾ emphasizes its grossly offensive quality. One act may be both.

The third object is also significant: wa-an taqūlū ʿalā Allāhi mā lā taʿlamūna.

Satan’s command here extends beyond sexual matters, explicitly including uninformed claims about The God.

Al-Baqarah 2:168 addresses lawful, wholesome food and avoiding Satan’s footsteps; Al-Baqarah 2:170 addresses following ancestral practice.

The passage therefore goes beyond private behavior to uninformed religious claims and following unsupported courses.

Speaking about The God without knowledge must nevertheless not become faḥshāʾ’s exact lexical equivalent: they are separately expressed within one context.

B. Satan promises poverty: faḥshāʾ, miserliness, and giving

Al-Baqarah reads:

الشَّيْطَانُ يَعِدُكُمُ الْفَقْرَ وَيَأْمُرُكُمْ بِالْفَحْشَاءِ ۖ وَاللَّهُ يَعِدُكُمْ مَغْفِرَةً مِنْهُ وَفَضْلًا ۗ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

al-shayṭānu yaʿidukumu al-faqra wa-yaʾmurukum bi-al-faḥshāʾi wa-Allāhu yaʿidukum maghfiratan minhu wa-faḍlan wa-Allāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīmun

“Satan threatens you with poverty and commands indecency, while The God promises you forgiveness from Him and bounty. The God is All-Encompassing, Knowing.”

Al-Baqarah 2:268

This verse particularly illustrates faḥshāʾ’s breadth.

Al-Baqarah 2:267 calls believers to give from wholesome earnings and forbids selecting inferior things to give.

Al-Baqarah 2:268 then mentions fear of poverty and Satan’s command to faḥshāʾ.

The sequence connects it here with financial conduct, miserliness, and withholding generosity.

Early Arabic offers an independent witness: Ṭarafah calls an excessively miserly person fāḥish.

The connection therefore rests on pre-Qur’anic language as well as contextual inference.

Al-faḥshāʾ should not, however, simply become “miserliness” without explanation, as though the words were always synonymous.

The root concerns gross offensiveness; wrongful withholding is an instance foregrounded by this context.

This preserves lexical meaning while identifying the verse’s role concerning giving.

Al-Ṭabarī: faḥshāʾ and miserliness in giving’s context

C. The God does not command faḥshāʾ: attributing shameful conduct to Him

Al-Aʿrāf reads:

وَإِذَا فَعَلُوا فَاحِشَةً قَالُوا وَجَدْنَا عَلَيْهَا آبَاءَنَا وَاللَّهُ أَمَرَنَا بِهَا ۗ قُلْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَأْمُرُ بِالْفَحْشَاءِ ۖ أَتَقُولُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

wa-idhā faʿalū fāḥishatan qālū wajadnā ʿalayhā ābāʾanā wa-Allāhu amaranā bihā qul inna Allāha lā yaʾmuru bi-al-faḥshāʾi a-taqūlūna ʿalā Allāhi mā lā taʿlamūna

“When they commit a grossly shameful act, they say, ‘We found our fathers following it, and The God commanded us to do it.’ Say: The God does not command indecency. Do you say about The God what you do not know?”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:28

Fāḥishatan and al-faḥshāʾ occur together.

The verse first names a grossly shameful act, then reports people attributing it to ancestral practice and divine command.

The response is explicit: The God does not command faḥshāʾ.

An objectionable practice can become inherited custom and even be attributed to The God; this matters for language and legislation.

A practice’s antiquity and ancestral observance do not alone establish divine legitimacy.

Claiming divine authorization requires knowledge; the closing question emphasizes that responsibility.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:29 identifies The God’s command with qisṭ in contrast to this claim.

Gross offensiveness here thus relates to justice, social custom, and attributing prescriptions to The God.

The Qur’an calling a practice fāḥishah differs from an individual or group finding something culturally distasteful.

Understanding Qur’anic fāḥishah requires examining the conduct condemned and the evidence supplied.

D. Faḥshāʾ, munkar, and baghy: offensiveness, reprehensibility, and aggression

Al-Naḥl reads:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَأْمُرُ بِالْعَدْلِ وَالْإِحْسَانِ وَإِيتَاءِ ذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَيَنْهَىٰ عَنِ الْفَحْشَاءِ وَالْمُنْكَرِ وَالْبَغْيِ ۚ يَعِظُكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَذَكَّرُونَ

inna Allāha yaʾmuru bi-al-ʿadli wa-al-iḥsāni wa-ītāʾi dhī al-qurbā wa-yanhā ʿani al-faḥshāʾi wa-al-munkari wa-al-baghyi yaʿiẓukum laʿallakum tadhakkarūna

“The God commands justice, doing good, and giving to relatives, and forbids indecency, wrongdoing, and aggression. He admonishes you so that you may remember.”

Al-Naḥl 16:90

Three concepts appear together: faḥshāʾ, munkar, and baghy.

Faḥshāʾ emphasizes gross offensiveness.

Munkar belongs to N K R and concerns what is reprehensible or rejected.

Baghy belongs to B GH Y and concerns aggression or transgression here.

The concepts approach one another without identity.

An act may be grossly shameful, reprehensible, and aggressive at once; another may be reprehensible in one respect without every feature of aggression.

Conjunction does not establish three distinct legal classes with fixed, nonoverlapping boundaries.

Faḥshāʾ cannot simply be confined here to sexual relations: justice, goodness, relatives’ rights, and opposition to aggression give the verse a wide social and ethical field.

E. Ṣalāh restrains indecency and wrongdoing: its effect

Al-ʿAnkabūt reads:

اتْلُ مَا أُوحِيَ إِلَيْكَ مِنَ الْكِتَابِ وَأَقِمِ الصَّلَاةَ ۖ إِنَّ الصَّلَاةَ تَنْهَىٰ عَنِ الْفَحْشَاءِ وَالْمُنْكَرِ ۗ وَلَذِكْرُ اللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ مَا تَصْنَعُونَ

utlu mā ūḥiya ilayka mina al-kitābi wa-aqimi al-ṣalāta inna al-ṣalāta tanhā ʿani al-faḥshāʾi wa-al-munkari wa-la-dhikru Allāhi akbaru wa-Allāhu yaʿlamu mā taṣnaʿūna

“Recite what has been revealed to you of the Book, and establish ṣalāh. Ṣalāh restrains indecency and wrongdoing; remembrance of The God is greater, and The God knows what you do.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:45

Faḥshāʾ and munkar again appear together.

The focus is now ṣalāh’s restraining effect rather than a direct prohibition.

Tanhā belongs to N H Y and supplies restraint or prohibition; faḥshāʾ itself does not mean restraining.

The verse connects ṣalāh with ethical conduct: worship should not be detached from action and life’s direction.

It does not establish that everyone performing ṣalāh becomes immune to every wrongful act.

Someone may worship yet retain mistakes, harmful habits, or disregard for others’ rights. This raises questions about worship’s actual effect and establishment without changing faḥshāʾ’s or ṣalāh’s meaning.

The verse again connects faḥshāʾ with broad ethical conduct rather than just one sexual sin.

F. Faḥshāʾ and munkar in following Satan’s footsteps

Al-Nūr 24:21 forbids believers to follow Satan’s footsteps and states that he commands faḥshāʾ and munkar.

Its context concerns false accusations, uninformed speech, and spreading objectionable conduct.

Faḥshāʾ must therefore not be confined to a sexual act without examining the context.

An unsupported accusation can attack someone’s reputation; repeating harmful speech can foster objectionable social conduct.

These examples fit the surah without making rumor or accusation the word’s exclusive meaning.

Faḥshāʾ concerns gross offensiveness, with the particular instance depending on each verse.

G. Al-sūʾa wa-al-faḥshāʾa in Joseph’s account

Yūsuf reads:

وَلَقَدْ هَمَّتْ بِهِ وَهَمَّ بِهَا لَوْلَا أَنْ رَأَىٰ بُرْهَانَ رَبِّهِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ لِنَصْرِفَ عَنْهُ السُّوءَ وَالْفَحْشَاءَ ۚ إِنَّهُ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا الْمُخْلَصِينَ

wa-la-qad hammat bihi wa-hamma bihā lawlā an raʾā burhāna rabbihi kadhālika li-naṣrifa ʿanhu al-sūʾa wa-al-faḥshāʾa innahu min ʿibādinā al-mukhlaṣīna

“She intended him, and he would have intended her had he not seen his Lord’s proof. Thus We turned evil and indecency away from him; he was among Our purified servants.”

Yūsuf 12:24

Faḥshāʾ concerns the relationship discussed in the preceding verses. The conditional rendering above follows one syntactic and interpretive analysis of wa-hamma bihā lawlā; other readings of lawlā’s relationship and hamm also exist, requiring separate examination.

Al-Qurṭubī: differing analyses of hamm and lawlā

Yūsuf 12:23 describes the woman of the house seeking a relationship with Joseph and shutting the doors; Joseph seeks refuge in The God.

Faḥshāʾ here clearly relates to sexual misconduct and transgressing relational limits.

This instance must not become the exclusive definition of every use.

Sūʾ and faḥshāʾ remain separate expressions: their combination can emphasize reprehensibility without proving wholly distinct classes of sins.

5. Qur’anic fāḥishah: grossly shameful acts in different contexts

A. Zinā: an explicit instance

Al-Isrāʾ reads:

وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا الزِّنَا ۖ إِنَّهُ كَانَ فَاحِشَةً وَسَاءَ سَبِيلًا

wa-lā taqrabū al-zinā innahu kāna fāḥishatan wa-sāʾa sabīlan

“Do not approach zinā; it is a grossly shameful act and an evil path.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:32

This explicitly identifies an instance of fāḥishah.

The prohibited act is first independently named al-zinā, then called fāḥishah and an evil path.

Zinā is therefore one Qur’anic instance of fāḥishah.

It does not follow that fāḥishah means only zinā throughout Arabic or the Qur’an.

Such an equation would require separate explanations for the family’s uses concerning miserliness, offensive speech, and other objectionable conduct.

Al-Isrāʾ does not condemn zinā merely because a culture dislikes it: the Qur’an explicitly identifies it as prohibited and grossly shameful.

Cultural distaste and Qur’anic prescription differ. Cultural approval varies; attribution of divine prohibition requires clear support.

B. Marrying women previously married to one’s father

Al-Nisāʾ reads:

وَلَا تَنْكِحُوا مَا نَكَحَ آبَاؤُكُمْ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ إِلَّا مَا قَدْ سَلَفَ ۚ إِنَّهُ كَانَ فَاحِشَةً وَمَقْتًا وَسَاءَ سَبِيلًا

wa-lā tankiḥū mā nakaḥa ābāʾukum mina al-nisāʾi illā mā qad salafa innahu kāna fāḥishatan wa-maqtan wa-sāʾa sabīlan

“Do not marry women whom your fathers married, except what is already past. It was a grossly shameful act, hateful, and an evil path.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:22

The prohibited act is specified: marriage to a woman previously married to one’s father.

Three expressions describe it: fāḥishatan, maqtan, and sāʾa sabīlan.

Maqt belongs to another root and concerns strong hatred or abhorrence; sāʾa sabīlan describes an evil path.

Fāḥishah thus concerns a particular prohibited family relationship here.

It shares a sexual and marital field with zinā, but the specified act differs from Al-Isrāʾ 17:32’s named act.

The Qur’an prohibits a specific marriage and calls it fāḥishah.

The phrase prohibiting marriage to those one’s fathers married must therefore remain central to identifying the instance.

C. Fāḥishah in Lot’s account

Al-Aʿrāf reads:

وَلُوطًا إِذْ قَالَ لِقَوْمِهِ أَتَأْتُونَ الْفَاحِشَةَ مَا سَبَقَكُمْ بِهَا مِنْ أَحَدٍ مِنَ الْعَالَمِينَ

wa-lūṭan idh qāla li-qawmihi a-taʾtūna al-fāḥishata mā sabaqakum bihā min aḥadin mina al-ʿālamīna

“And Lot, when he said to his people: do you commit the grossly shameful act in which no one among the worlds preceded you?”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:80

Al-fāḥishah concerns conduct for which Lot confronts his people.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:81 identifies approaching men with desire instead of women.

Al-Naml 27:54 similarly questions their committing fāḥishah while seeing; Al-Naml 27:55 explains the conduct through approaching men with desire instead of women.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:28 repeats al-fāḥishah; Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29 mentions approaching men, cutting off the road, and committing munkar in their assembly.

The context therefore identifies particular conduct and foregrounds its sexual aspect.

Several matters must nevertheless remain distinct.

Fāḥishah itself does not mean man, woman, desire, or sexual relationship; independent words supply those concepts.

Nor should every act in Lot’s account be absorbed into one word: cutting off the road and wrongdoing in the assembly have independent objects of study.

The lexical meaning alone cannot establish every legal prescription, penalty, or judgment about people with differing circumstances and conduct.

Lot’s verses identify instances, while the root’s definition remains independent of a particular one.

D. Al-fāḥishah in Al-Nisāʾ 4:15 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:25

Al-Nisāʾ 4:15 addresses women who commit al-fāḥishah, requiring four witnesses from the audience for adjudication.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:16 addresses two people committing the conduct, alongside repentance and reform.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 again places fāḥishah in marriage, iḥṣān, and punishment’s setting.

It discusses marrying believing women in the historical status of mā malakat aymānukum and, after its marriage conditions, states that committing fāḥishah after iḥṣān entails half the punishment applicable to muḥṣanāt women.

Fāḥishah can thus become a matter of legal adjudication and punishment.

This does not give every Qur’anic occurrence one fixed penalty.

The half-penalty comes from niṣfu mā ʿalā al-muḥṣanāti mina al-ʿadhābi, not the root F Ḥ SH.

The wording concerns a penalty capable of division. Identifying it and its relationship to other verses, especially Al-Nūr 24:2, requires legal and interpretive study.

Lexical meaning alone cannot settle that question.

Marriage and iḥṣān support a sexual-misconduct reading here without imposing the same legal circumstances and procedure on every occurrence.

E. Bi-fāḥishatin mubayyinatin: manifest shameful conduct in family relationships

The combination occurs in Al-Nisāʾ 4:19, Al-Aḥzāb 33:30, and Al-Ṭalāq 65:1.

All three combine fāḥishah with mubayyinah.

The adjective concerns clarity or manifestness.

It belongs to B Y N, and its meaning is not part of F Ḥ SH itself.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:19 addresses treatment of women, inheriting them against their will, and pressuring them to recover part of what was given, with a fāḥishah mubayyinah exception.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:1 addresses women remaining in their homes during the prescribed post-divorce period, prohibiting expulsion and departure with a similarly phrased exception.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:30 addresses the Prophet’s wives and the heavier consequence of fāḥishah mubayyinah.

Different contexts do not immediately establish one specific act with identical legal details.

Nor should the phrase become so vague that any unpleasant conduct, family disagreement, or refusal of another person’s wishes qualifies.

Manifestness differs from vague accusation, personal suspicion, and unproven allegations.

The exceptions in Al-Nisāʾ 4:19 and Al-Ṭalāq 65:1 do not give general authorization to harm, humiliate, or deprive women of rights. Each exception remains bounded by its sentence and related prescriptions.

For root study, the phrase combines gross offensiveness and its manifest character; the exact act and legal consequence depend on the verse.

F. Fāḥishah and responsibility after sin

Āl ʿImrān reads:

وَالَّذِينَ إِذَا فَعَلُوا فَاحِشَةً أَوْ ظَلَمُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ ذَكَرُوا اللَّهَ فَاسْتَغْفَرُوا لِذُنُوبِهِمْ وَمَنْ يَغْفِرُ الذُّنُوبَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَلَمْ يُصِرُّوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلُوا وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-alladhīna idhā faʿalū fāḥishatan aw ẓalamū anfusahum dhakarū Allāha fa-istaghfarū li-dhunūbihim wa-man yaghfiru al-dhunūba illā Allāhu wa-lam yuṣirrū ʿalā mā faʿalū wa-hum yaʿlamūna

“Those who, on committing a grossly shameful act or wronging themselves, remember The God and seek forgiveness for their sins—and who forgives sins except The God?—and do not knowingly persist in what they did.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:135

Fāḥishah accompanies wronging oneself.

The former names a grossly shameful act; the latter, a person’s wrongdoing against themselves.

One act can be both.

Aw alone does not establish nonoverlapping meanings or separate every fāḥishah from self-wronging.

The emphasis is response after wrongdoing: remembrance, seeking forgiveness, and avoiding knowing persistence.

Fāḥishah thus appears alongside possible repentance and reform.

Its commission does not alone conclusively close the way back.

Seeking forgiveness does not nullify harmed people’s rights; those rights and social consequences require the relevant verses independently.

G. An tashīʿa al-fāḥishatu: spreading shameful conduct

Al-Nūr reads:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يُحِبُّونَ أَنْ تَشِيعَ الْفَاحِشَةُ فِي الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ ۚ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنْتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

inna alladhīna yuḥibbūna an tashīʿa al-fāḥishatu fī alladhīna āmanū lahum ʿadhābun alīmun fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati wa-Allāhu yaʿlamu wa-antum lā taʿlamūna

“Those who wish the grossly shameful to spread among believers will have painful punishment in this world and the hereafter. The God knows, and you do not know.”

Al-Nūr 24:19

Al-fāḥishatu is the subject of tashīʿa.

Tashīʿa belongs to SH Y ʿ and means spreading or becoming prevalent here.

Spreading comes from the verb, not fāḥishah itself.

The preceding verses concern false accusations, testimony, and uninformed speech. Al-Nūr 24:15 criticizes passing a report from tongue to tongue and saying what one does not know.

Spreading fāḥishah here therefore relates to accusation, reputation, and circulating harmful speech.

Unsupported accusations must be distinguished from responsible reporting of harmful conduct supported by sufficient evidence.

They are different acts.

The verse does not make every discussion of an actual crime, a victim’s seeking justice, or documented reporting of corruption a spreading of fāḥishah.

Circulating unproven allegations, treating reputations as entertainment, and encouraging others’ disgrace directly relate to the context’s warnings.

Fāḥishah concerns gross offensiveness here; the social conduct intended must be understood through Al-Nūr’s connected verses.

6. Fawāḥish: openly and secretly committed shameful acts

A. What is apparent and what is hidden

Al-Anʿām reads:

وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا الْفَوَاحِشَ مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَمَا بَطَنَ

wa-lā taqrabū al-fawāḥisha mā ẓahara minhā wa-mā baṭana

“Do not approach grossly shameful acts, whether apparent or hidden.”

This is part of Al-Anʿām 6:151.

Al-fawāḥish is plural of fāḥishah.

The prohibition concerns shameful acts in the plural rather than one specified act.

Mā ẓahara minhā wa-mā baṭana distinguishes apparent and hidden instances.

This matters for the concept’s breadth.

An act may be publicly committed and visible to others, or the same or another shameful act may occur secretly.

Visibility or concealment alone does not change its ethical value.

An act the Qur’an calls grossly shameful does not become right merely by remaining unseen.

This does not authorize unjustified intrusion or unlimited investigation into private lives.

The direct concern is avoiding fawāḥish; adjudicating others’ conduct and privacy’s limits require independent consideration.

B. Fawāḥish alongside sin, aggression, and association

Al-Aʿrāf reads:

قُلْ إِنَّمَا حَرَّمَ رَبِّيَ الْفَوَاحِشَ مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَمَا بَطَنَ وَالْإِثْمَ وَالْبَغْيَ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ وَأَنْ تُشْرِكُوا بِاللَّهِ مَا لَمْ يُنَزِّلْ بِهِ سُلْطَانًا وَأَنْ تَقُولُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

qul innamā ḥarrama rabbiya al-fawāḥisha mā ẓahara minhā wa-mā baṭana wa-al-ithma wa-al-baghya bi-ghayri al-ḥaqqi wa-an tushrikū bi-Allāhi mā lam yunazzil bihi sulṭānan wa-an taqūlū ʿalā Allāhi mā lā taʿlamūna

“Say: my Lord has prohibited grossly shameful acts, apparent and hidden; sin and wrongful aggression; associating with The God what He has authorized by no proof; and saying about The God what you do not know.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:33

Fawāḥish appears alongside ithm, baghy, shirk, and uninformed claims about The God.

Each has an independent semantic field.

Ithm concerns sin and responsibility for wrongdoing; baghy here concerns wrongful aggression. Association and uninformed religious claims are likewise independent subjects.

Conjunction does not make their fields nonoverlapping.

One act may be fāḥishah, ithm, and aggression against others simultaneously; overlap is compatible with independent words.

Conversely, treating them as complete synonyms erases their distinctions.

Because fawāḥish accompanies several prohibitions, it should not simply name everything prohibited.

Fawāḥish emphasizes gross offensiveness; ḥarām, prohibited status. An act can have both descriptions without the roots meaning the same.

C. Fawāḥish and major sins

Al-Shūrā 42:37 identifies avoiding major sins and fawāḥish among believers’ characteristics.

Al-Najm 53:32 similarly describes avoiding major sins and fawāḥish, except al-lamam.

Their combination emphasizes these acts’ gross offensiveness and ethical seriousness.

It does not alone separate every fāḥishah from every major sin or establish two independent legal lists.

Al-lamam raises a separate question about limited lapses or lesser conduct.

Without studying L M M and the context, an act committed only once cannot automatically be excluded from fawāḥish.

Study of L M M

Severity, repetition, intention, knowledge, and the possibility of repentance and forgiveness are related but distinct.

7. Fāḥishah, faḥshāʾ, and related roots

Fāḥishah and faḥshāʾ: two forms in one field

Fāḥishah is feminine fāḥish, established as a noun for a grossly shameful act.

Faḥshāʾ belongs to the same family and can express gross ugliness or reprehensible conduct.

Fāḥishah often introduces a particular act, as zinā in Al-Isrāʾ 17:32 or prohibited marriage in Al-Nisāʾ 4:22.

Faḥshāʾ more often expresses gross offensiveness generally, as in Satan’s command or divine prohibition.

This is a tendency rather than an exclusive rule.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:28 uses both for the conduct people perform and the faḥshāʾ The God does not command.

The forms can thus concern one shared ethical field.

They cannot be rigidly divided into fāḥishah as physical action and faḥshāʾ as an inner or intellectual state.

“Grossly shameful act” generally suits fāḥishah; “indecency” or “gross offensiveness” suits faḥshāʾ.

F Ḥ SH (fuḥsh) and S W ʾ (sūʾ)

Sūʾ concerns evil, harm, or what is objectionable.

Fuḥsh emphasizes gross offensiveness.

Al-Baqarah 2:169 and Yūsuf 12:24 combine sūʾ and faḥshāʾ.

The pairing helps examine their difference without establishing one permanent, absolute boundary.

Sūʾ has a broader field of evil, while fuḥsh highlights severity of offensiveness.

One act can have both descriptions.

Study of S W ʾ

F Ḥ SH (fuḥsh) and N K R (munkar)

Munkar belongs to N K R, concerning what is unfamiliar, objectionable, or rejected.

Faḥshāʾ emphasizes gross offensiveness.

Al-Naḥl 16:90, Al-Nūr 24:21, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:45 combine them.

Both describe conduct people must avoid.

Linguistically, reprehensibility and gross offensiveness are nevertheless not identical.

An act may be objectionable under Qur’anic criteria without matching another act’s gross offensiveness.

Munkar must not be reduced to cultural unfamiliarity: conduct can be common yet objectionable under truth and justice.

Faḥshāʾ likewise cannot be equated with personal feeling or cultural preference.

Study of N K R

F Ḥ SH (fuḥsh) and B GH Y (baghy)

Baghy in this discussion concerns aggression or transgression against rights.

Fuḥsh emphasizes an act’s gross offensiveness.

Violating another’s rights may also be grossly shameful, bringing both concepts together.

This does not justify translating every occurrence of baghy as faḥshāʾ.

Their conjunction in Al-Naḥl 16:90 warrants study without alone proving fixed, nonoverlapping boundaries.

Study of B GH Y

F Ḥ SH (fāḥishah) and Z N Y (zinā)

The Qur’an explicitly calls zinā fāḥishah.

They are not thereby complete synonyms.

Zinā names a specified act; fāḥishah describes or names grossly shameful conduct.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:32 first names the act, then describes it.

The order matters: zinā identifies the instance and fāḥishah its gross offensiveness.

Other uses may have different referents.

Study of Z N Y

F Ḥ SH (fuḥsh) and KH B TH (khubth)

Qur’anic khubth concerns impurity or badness in food, conduct, and personal characteristics, among other settings.

Fuḥsh concerns gross offensiveness.

They can approach one another in describing conduct without identical fields.

Something called khabīth in one context is not thereby among fawāḥish in every circumstance.

Personal aversion to food or conduct must not directly become khubth or fuḥsh under divine prescription.

Study of KH B TH

F Ḥ SH (fuḥsh) and Ḥ Y Y (ḥayāʾ)

Arabic ḥayāʾ concerns restraint from shameful behavior and attentiveness to social conduct.

Fuḥsh can likewise concern grossly shameful or indecent conduct.

Their proximity does not make everything personally embarrassing a fāḥishah.

Upbringing, culture, social setting, and experience can shape shame; someone may even feel ashamed to state a necessary truth or claim a right.

Conversely, grossly reprehensible conduct may become normalized until its perpetrators feel no shame.

The presence or absence of shame alone does not establish Qur’anic faḥshāʾ.

Faḥshāʾ does not simply mean bodily exposure or discussing sexual matters. Those enter this field only where particular conduct and relevant criteria establish the offensiveness concerned.

Study of Ḥ Y Y

8. Morphological and structural notes

No verb of F Ḥ SH occurs in the Qur’an: all uses are nominal.

Other verbs express performing a fāḥishah.

Āl ʿImrān 3:135 uses faʿalū with fāḥishah as object.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:80 has interrogative a-taʾtūna. In the widespread Ḥafṣ reading, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:28 instead has emphatic declarative innakum la-taʾtūna. Both verbs belong to ʾ T Y, not F Ḥ SH.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:32, fāḥishatan is kāna’s predicate describing zinā.

Al-Baqarah 2:169 and Al-Baqarah 2:268 combine faḥshāʾ with yaʾmuru.

Al-Naḥl 16:90 and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:45 connect it with yanhā.

In Al-Nūr 24:19, al-fāḥishatu is tashīʿa’s subject.

The act and its relationship to fāḥishah therefore depend on the complete construction.

Fāḥishah and faḥshāʾ must also remain morphologically distinct.

Fāḥishah is originally a feminine adjective established as a noun for a grossly shameful matter.

Faḥshāʾ is a noun in the same field, bearing al- in all seven Qur’anic occurrences.

Fawāḥish is the recognized plural, but plurality alone specifies neither the acts’ exact kind nor number.

Mubayyinah in fāḥishatin mubayyinatin requires separate study and is not a fixed component of F Ḥ SH’s meaning.

Study of B Y N

Kabāʾira al-ithmi wa-al-fawāḥishi likewise combines independent roots. Seriousness of sin and gross offensiveness can relate while kabāʾir, ithm, and fawāḥish retain distinct roles.

Study of K B R; Study of ʾ TH M

9. Challenges in understanding and translation

Does fāḥishah always mean zinā?

No.

Zinā is called fāḥishah, but the family’s field is wider.

Pre-Islamic miserliness and Qur’anic giving show why confining it to sexual behavior lacks sufficient support.

This breadth must not erase clear sexual referents in particular verses.

Establish the general meaning, then examine each instance independently.

Is every objectionable act a fāḥishah?

No. F Ḥ SH emphasizes gross offensiveness.

Language can distinguish unpleasant conduct, limited error, sin, and grossly shameful acts.

These distinctions alone do not create an exhaustive, definitive ranking of sins.

Neither personal taste nor social custom alone sets the criterion: the Qur’an’s own verses and limits chiefly identify its intended instances.

Must an act be publicly seen to be fāḥishah?

No.

Al-Anʿām 6:151 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:33 mention apparent and hidden fawāḥish.

Concealment does not remove an act’s ethical significance.

Prohibiting hidden fawāḥish does not authorize unlimited spying or disclosure of private lives.

Are faḥshāʾ and munkar identical?

No, though they may overlap.

Faḥshāʾ emphasizes gross offensiveness; munkar, reprehensible or rejected conduct within its own field.

Repeated conjunction helps examine their differences without creating absolute, nonoverlapping boundaries.

Does calling an act fāḥishah determine its penalty?

No.

It can describe an act’s gross offensiveness without determining punishment, proof requirements, or personal responsibility.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:15 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 address adjudication and punishment through independent clauses.

One verse’s legal ruling must not be imposed without examination on every mention of fāḥishah.

Does an allegation establish the act occurred?

No.

Occurrence, allegation, and proof differ.

Al-Nūr treats accusation and circulating uninformed claims about others as serious matters.

Suspicion, rumor, or cultural judgment alone cannot justify accusing someone of fāḥishah.

This requires sound identification, sufficient evidence, and respect for rights, rather than overlooking wrongdoing or harm.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

General fuḥsh suits “gross offensiveness” or “grossly offensive conduct or speech.”

For speech, “abusive language” or contextually “obscene speech” may fit; obscenity does not cover every use of the root.

Fāḥish can be “grossly shameful,” “highly reprehensible,” or, for a person, “offensive in conduct.” In Ṭarafah’s verse, contextual “rigid miser” is clearer than an unexplained fāḥish.

Fāḥishah principally suits “a grossly shameful act.” The Arabic term can also be retained as a Qur’anic keyword with its brief meaning explained.

Fawāḥish suits “grossly shameful acts,” or may remain as a Qur’anic term in analytical prose.

Faḥshāʾ suits “indecency” or “gross offensiveness.” Modern Persian faḥshā often evokes a narrower sexual meaning, so research prose should clarify the wider field.

The following proposals fit particular verses.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:32, innahu kāna fāḥishatan: “It is a grossly shameful act.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:28, wa-idhā faʿalū fāḥishatan: “When they commit a grossly shameful act.”

Al-Baqarah 2:268, wa-yaʾmurukum bi-al-faḥshāʾi: “And commands you to indecency.” Its connection with miserliness and wrongful withholding belongs in reflection.

Al-Naḥl 16:90, wa-yanhā ʿani al-faḥshāʾi wa-al-munkari wa-al-baghyi: “And forbids indecency, wrongdoing, and aggression.”

Al-Anʿām 6:151, wa-lā taqrabū al-fawāḥisha: “Do not approach grossly shameful acts.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:19, bi-fāḥishatin mubayyinatin: “A manifestly shameful act.” The conduct’s kind requires contextual study.

Al-Nūr 24:19, an tashīʿa al-fāḥishatu: “That the grossly shameful should spread.” Reflection should explain accusation, reputation, and unsupported reports.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:45, tanhā ʿani al-faḥshāʾi wa-al-munkari: “Restrains indecency and wrongdoing.”

Basic translation must not automatically turn every fāḥishah into zinā or sexual misconduct; reflection must likewise preserve a particular verse’s clear sexual referent.

Lexical meaning, direct translation, and identifying an instance are related but distinct steps.

11. Summary

F Ḥ SH concerns gross ugliness and reprehensibility exceeding acceptable bounds, in speech, conduct, miserliness, human relations, and sexual acts.

Ṭarafah’s pre-Islamic witness shows fāḥish describing extreme miserliness before later legal terminology developed.

The Qur’an uses the root 24 times, only in nominal families fāḥishah and faḥshāʾ.

Some instances have clear sexual referents, including zinā in Al-Isrāʾ 17:32 and the conduct challenged in Lot’s account.

Others combine faḥshāʾ with evil, miserliness, social conduct, uninformed religious claims, and Satan’s command, showing its scope extends beyond one sexual sin.

The Qur’an also speaks of plural fawāḥish, openly or secretly committed, alongside ithm, munkar, baghy, and other objectionable conduct.

Fāḥishah identifies gross offensiveness without alone settling the act’s kind, personal responsibility, or penalty.

Nor is everything culturally or personally disliked necessarily Qur’anic fāḥishah: clear evidence and Qur’anic criteria are required.

Briefly, F Ḥ SH means gross ugliness and reprehensibility, where conduct or speech exceeds decent and acceptable bounds.

Its Qur’anic instances may be sexual, ethical, social, or otherwise concern reprehensible human conduct.

For Forqan, “grossly shameful act” suits fāḥishah, and “indecency” or “gross offensiveness” faḥshāʾ. Identify particular instances through each context rather than inserting them into the root’s definition from the outset.
