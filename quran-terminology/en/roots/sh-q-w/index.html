Suffering, deprivation and an unhappy outcome: from hardship in early Arabic to wretchedness in contrast with well-being, guidance and salvation in the Quran

In early Arabic, SH Q W concerns suffering, difficulty, deprivation and an adverse human condition. Shaqiya describes someone experiencing hardship or an unhappy outcome; shaqāʾ and shaqāwah name that condition, while shaqiyy describes someone deprived of ease, a desired benefit or a good outcome.

This family contrasts with S ʿ D. Saʿādah concerns well-being, good fortune and attaining a desirable outcome, whereas shaqāwah may concern failure, deprivation, suffering or an unhappy end.

Its uses are not identical. Someone may experience everyday hardship and poverty, fail to obtain a desire, or reach a harmful outcome through their choices and conduct. These conditions share a semantic field but differ in cause and moral significance.

SH Q W occurs twelve times in seven Quranic surahs. Its uses range from Adam's hardship after leaving the garden to disappointment in prayer, resistance to truth and an unhappy outcome in the hereafter.

This range is fundamental: the Quran was not sent down to cause the Prophet distress, while other passages describe as shaqiyy those who resist truth and guidance.

A defensible semantic core is being in a condition of suffering, deprivation or an undesirable outcome. Natural hardship, suffering caused by others, an unmet desire and moral or eschatological wretchedness must be distinguished. Their causes and significance come from context, not the root alone.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Wretchedness in contrast with well-being

Early dictionaries define shaqāʾ and shaqāwah in contrast with saʿādah.

This opposition extends beyond a passing feeling to one's condition in well-being, ease, success or outcome.

Shaqiya al-rajulu describes someone experiencing hardship, failure or misfortune.

Being shaqiyy need not mean being poor. Someone may possess wealth yet lack peace, security or the desired fruits of their efforts.

Another person may have limited means yet enjoy good relationships, satisfaction and a sound moral direction.

Wretchedness cannot therefore be equated with a single material measure such as wealth, house size or social rank.

Older explanations connect it both with life's hardship and with deprivation of well-being or a desirable end.

Both aspects should be retained in the historical definition.

An early poetic witness: saʿīd and shaqiyy in Labīd

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived through the pre-Islamic period and the beginnings of Islam, says in a poem:

فَمِنْهُمْ سَعِيدٌ آخِذٌ لِنَصِيبِهِ
وَمِنْهُمْ شَقِيٌّ بِالْمَعِيشَةِ قَانِعُ

fa-minhum saʿīdun ākhidhun li-naṣībihi / wa-minhum shaqiyyun bi-l-maʿīshati qāniʿ

“Among them is one fortunate, taking his share; and among them one wretched, content with his livelihood.”

The preceding lines concern life's impermanence, wealth, human destiny and the contrast between building and destroying what one builds.

The poet observes differing conditions and shares in life; the surrounding lines also permit an ethical reading of those differences.

Saʿīd is set against shaqiyy in this line.

Shaqiyy should not automatically be translated as a sinner or a person in hell here; neither should its ethical dimension be categorically excluded. The surrounding lines discuss piety, action and the transience of possessions.

The line supports the opposition between good and adverse fortune, but does not conclusively restrict wretchedness to financial hardship or prove that the poet has no ethical concern.

Labīd lived across both periods. This study does not independently establish the poem's date, so it should not be presented as certainly predating Quranic revelation.

Its value lies in illustrating the contrast between shaqiyy and saʿīd in a passage about life, conduct and human fortune.

Other uses involving difficulty and suffering

The shortened shaqā is also recorded for severity, difficulty and distress.

Shaqiya fī amrihi may describe suffering or difficulty in an undertaking.

Shāqā fulānan is used for competing with another in endurance, effort or confrontation.

Two people might compete in a demanding task, enduring fatigue or overcoming obstacles. Enduring difficulty is central, not necessarily their moral failure.

Mushāqāh is also recorded for confronting difficulty and struggling with it.

This does not make all strenuous effort blameworthy wretchedness.

A farmer working to feed a family, or someone enduring difficulty to save another, may be tired or distressed without thereby becoming morally wretched.

This distinction also matters in the Quran: some passages concern hardship, others the unhappy outcome of resistance to truth.

Is there an original physical image?

Some lexical accounts record al-shāqī for a long, inaccessible mountain ridge, and shaqā nābu al-baʿīr for a camel's tooth emerging.

A form mishqā has also been reported in connection with a comb and hamzated variants; its exact spelling and classification are not established in this study and it is not used as evidence for the Quranic meaning.

The historical relationship of these peripheral forms to suffering and an unhappy outcome is unclear.

One cannot confidently derive wretchedness from a camel's tooth, a mountain ridge or a physical splitting.

In particular, sound resemblance does not identify this family with SH Q Q, splitting and difficulty.

Suffering, distress, deprivation and the opposite of well-being have clear lexical support. An unproven original physical image need not be invented as a master meaning.

2. Important forms and derivatives

In the classification used here, SH Q W is a final-weak root whose wāw changes to yāʾ or alif in certain forms.

Its principal Arabic forms include:

Shaqiya / yashqā: to suffer, experience hardship or reach an adverse condition or outcome; the basic triliteral verb.

Shaqāʾ: suffering, difficulty, deprivation or an unhappy outcome.

Shaqāwah: wretchedness or ill fortune, contrasting with well-being.

Shaqwah and shiqwah: other forms denoting wretchedness or an adverse condition. Shiqwah occurs in the Quran.

Shaqiyy: suffering, deprived of a desired benefit or ill-fated. In ethical contexts it may describe someone whose conduct and orientation lead to an unhappy end.

Ashqā: more or most wretched, an elative form.

Ashqiyāʾ: the plural of shaqiyy, those in wretchedness or facing an unhappy outcome.

Ashqāhu: he caused him to suffer or become wretched. This Form IV verb must be distinguished from the elative ashqā.

Shāqā: competed with another in enduring hardship or strenuous effort.

Mushāqāh: struggle or engagement involving difficulty.

Shāqin / al-shāqī: recorded for a long mountain ridge that cannot be climbed. It should not be presented without evidence as the ordinary adjective for a suffering person; shaqiyy is the established form in that sense.

Four morphological categories occur in the Quran: the basic verb, the adjective shaqiyy, the elative ashqā and the noun shiqwah.

Forms IV and III, the plural ashqiyāʾ and the standalone noun shaqāwah do not occur in the site's base reading, Ḥafṣ. The variant shaqāwatunā is reported among other readings of Al-Muʾminūn 23:106.

Collecting dictionary words is not enough: forms actually used in the Quran must be distinguished from the broader Arabic lexicon.

3. Life and prayer: Maryam and Ṭā Hā

Maryam 19:4: not being disappointed in Zechariah's prayer

Zechariah prays:

رَبِّ إِنِّي وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا وَلَمْ أَكُنْ بِدُعَائِكَ رَبِّ شَقِيًّا

rabbi innī wahana al-ʿaẓmu minnī wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shayban wa-lam akun bi-duʿāʾika rabbi shaqiyyā

Maryam 19:4

“My Lord, my bones have weakened and my head has blazed with gray hair; and in calling upon You, my Lord, I have never been disappointed.”

Shaqiyyan here is qualified by bi-duʿāʾika, “in calling upon You.”

That relationship specifies the adjective's sense in prayer.

Zechariah describes physical frailty and old age, yet recalls that he has not been shaqiyy in calling upon his Lord.

He cannot mean that he has never experienced hardship, since the same verse describes weakened bones and old age.

Not being shaqiyy therefore does not mean a life without difficulty.

Bi-duʿāʾika relates the statement to prayer and its outcome.

“Not being disappointed in calling upon You” is accordingly a natural rendering.

Zechariah recalls his previous experience of turning to his Lord and makes a new request on that basis.

The verse does not establish that every prayer must be fulfilled in exactly the form, time and manner expected by the supplicant.

That question exceeds shaqiyy's lexical meaning and requires the wider passages about prayer and divine response.

Maryam 19:48: Abraham's hope of not being disappointed

After the dialogue with his father, Abraham addresses those whose worship he rejects:

وَأَعْتَزِلُكُمْ وَمَا تَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَأَدْعُو رَبِّي عَسَىٰ أَلَّا أَكُونَ بِدُعَاءِ رَبِّي شَقِيًّا

wa-aʿtazilukum wa-mā tadʿūna min dūni allāhi wa-adʿū rabbī ʿasā allā akūna bi-duʿāʾi rabbī shaqiyyā

Maryam 19:48

“I withdraw from you and what you call upon apart from God, and I call upon my Lord; I hope not to be disappointed in calling upon my Lord.”

This resembles Zechariah's prayer.

Both passages associate shaqiyy with calling upon God.

Their sentence structures nevertheless differ.

Zechariah recalls past experience:

وَلَمْ أَكُنْ بِدُعَائِكَ رَبِّ شَقِيًّا

wa-lam akun bi-duʿāʾika rabbi shaqiyyā

Maryam 19:4

“I have not been disappointed in calling upon You.”

Abraham expresses hope for the future:

عَسَىٰ أَلَّا أَكُونَ بِدُعَاءِ رَبِّي شَقِيًّا

ʿasā allā akūna bi-duʿāʾi rabbī shaqiyyā

Maryam 19:48

“I hope not to be disappointed in calling upon my Lord.”

ʿAsā introduces hope; hope is not part of shaqiyy's own meaning.

After opposition and threats, Abraham withdraws from his people and turns to his Lord.

Maryam 19:49 describes the gift of Isaac and Jacob to him.

Within the story, this shows a good outcome of his withdrawal and turning to God.

It does not guarantee identical blessings or family outcomes to everyone who leaves a harmful environment.

Lexically, not being shaqiyy concerns not being deprived of good and a desirable outcome in prayer.

Wretchedness can therefore contrast with benefit and success without necessarily referring to punishment in the hereafter.

Maryam 19:32: domineering and wretched in Jesus' description

The Quran reports Jesus saying:

وَبَرًّا بِوَالِدَتِي وَلَمْ يَجْعَلْنِي جَبَّارًا شَقِيًّا

wa-barran bi-wālidatī wa-lam yajʿalnī jabbāran shaqiyyā

Maryam 19:32

“And dutiful to my mother; He has not made me a wretched tyrant.”

Shaqiyy occurs alongside jabbār.

Jabbār here concerns tyranny and domineering behavior.

Shaqiyy concerns wretchedness and an unhappy outcome.

The descriptions are contextually related, but not complete synonyms.

A tyrant's harmful behavior can be connected with an unhappy outcome and deprivation of true good.

SH Q W itself does not make shaqiyy invariably mean tyrannical.

Maryam 19:4 concerns Zechariah's lack of disappointment in prayer, without any reference to tyranny.

Here jabbāran and shaqiyyan together clarify the moral condition and outcome being denied.

Maryam 19:14 says of John:

وَلَمْ يَكُنْ جَبَّارًا عَصِيًّا

wa-lam yakun jabbāran ʿaṣiyyā

Maryam 19:14

“He was not a disobedient tyrant.”

Jabbār is shared, but John is described with ʿaṣiyyan and Jesus with shaqiyyan.

ʿAṣiyy and shaqiyy should not therefore be equated without examination.

The former concerns disobedience; the latter can describe wretchedness or an unhappy outcome.

Ṭā Hā 20:2: the Quran was not sent to distress the Prophet

Ṭā Hā begins:

مَا أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْكَ الْقُرْآنَ لِتَشْقَىٰ

mā anzalnā ʿalayka al-qurʾāna li-tashqā

Ṭā Hā 20:2

“We did not send the Quran down to you so that you would suffer.”

Ṭā Hā 20:3 continues:

إِلَّا تَذْكِرَةً لِمَنْ يَخْشَىٰ

illā tadhkiratan li-man yakhshā

Ṭā Hā 20:3

“But as a reminder for whoever fears.”

These verses contrast the purpose of revelation with causing distress.

Li-tashqā derives from yashqā and here concerns suffering and difficulty.

It does not say that the Prophet would never encounter difficulty after revelation.

The Quran itself describes opposition, persecution, migration and warfare faced by the Prophet and believers.

Faith and prophethood are therefore not immunity from life's hardships.

What is denied is distressing the Prophet as revelation's purpose.

In its place stands a reminder beneficial to a receptive hearer.

Religion should not be portrayed as though God sent the Quran to impose pointless hardship, burden life without reason and deprive people of worldly good.

Nor is every difficult responsibility wretchedness: doing right may require effort and perseverance while pursuing a good purpose and outcome.

This also relates to ease in religion. Al-Baqarah 2:185 says:

يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ

yurīdu allāhu bikumu al-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu al-ʿusr

Al-Baqarah 2:185

“God desires ease for you and does not desire hardship for you.”

This concerns fasting rules and should not be detached from that context to make every responsibility effortless.

Alongside Ṭā Hā 20:2, it shows that purposeless suffering and difficulty for its own sake are not the aim of divine guidance.

Ṭā Hā 20:117: Adam's hardship after leaving the garden

God says to Adam:

إِنَّ هَٰذَا عَدُوٌّ لَكَ وَلِزَوْجِكَ فَلَا يُخْرِجَنَّكُمَا مِنَ الْجَنَّةِ فَتَشْقَىٰ

inna hādhā ʿaduwwun laka wa-li-zawjika fa-lā yukhrijannakumā mina al-jannati fa-tashqā

Ṭā Hā 20:117

“This is an enemy to you and your spouse; do not let him drive you both out of the garden, so that you suffer.”

Fa-tashqā concerns hardship following departure from the garden.

Ṭā Hā 20:118 and Ṭā Hā 20:119 clarify it.

Ṭā Hā 20:118

إِنَّ لَكَ أَلَّا تَجُوعَ فِيهَا وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ

inna laka allā tajūʿa fīhā wa-lā taʿrā

Ṭā Hā 20:118

“There you shall neither hunger nor be naked.”

Ṭā Hā 20:119

وَأَنَّكَ لَا تَظْمَأُ فِيهَا وَلَا تَضْحَىٰ

wa-annaka lā taẓmaʾu fīhā wa-lā taḍḥā

Ṭā Hā 20:119

“Nor thirst there or be exposed to the sun's heat.”

These describe conditions Adam enjoyed in the garden.

Hunger, nakedness, thirst and exposure to the sun illustrate bodily needs and hardships.

Fa-tashqā is thus clearly connected with life's difficulty.

Defining wretchedness everywhere as disbelief or punishment in the hereafter would fail to explain this use.

Restricting it to work and earning a living would also be too narrow: the following verses describe several bodily needs.

The grammar matters too. The dual in fa-lā yukhrijannakumā refers to Adam and his spouse, whereas fa-tashqā is singular, addressing Adam.

This does not establish that his spouse was immune to hardship after leaving the garden.

Adam is addressed and the consequence attributed to him, while departure and the subsequent living condition concern both.

This is a clear use for life's toil, not necessarily a final judgment of someone's place in the hereafter.

Ṭā Hā 20:123: following guidance and avoiding wretchedness

After Adam's story, the Quran says:

فَإِمَّا يَأْتِيَنَّكُمْ مِنِّي هُدًى فَمَنِ اتَّبَعَ هُدَايَ فَلَا يَضِلُّ وَلَا يَشْقَىٰ

fa-immā yaʾtiyannakum minnī hudan fa-mani ittabaʿa hudāya fa-lā yaḍillu wa-lā yashqā

Ṭā Hā 20:123

“When guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows My guidance will neither go astray nor become wretched.”

Wretchedness is contrasted with following guidance.

Ḍalāl, from Ḍ L L, concerns losing or departing from the right path.

Shaqāwah concerns suffering, deprivation and an adverse outcome.

Their pairing shows a relationship without identity of meaning.

Misguidance concerns one's path and orientation; wretchedness can describe the resulting condition or outcome.

Their order does not prove that every case of wretchedness follows a separately identifiable stage of misguidance. The relationship requires broader evidence.

Ṭā Hā 20:124 says:

وَمَنْ أَعْرَضَ عَنْ ذِكْرِي فَإِنَّ لَهُ مَعِيشَةً ضَنْكًا وَنَحْشُرُهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ أَعْمَىٰ

wa-man aʿraḍa ʿan dhikrī fa-inna lahu maʿīshatan ḍankan wa-naḥshuruhu yawma al-qiyāmati aʿmā

Ṭā Hā 20:124

“Whoever turns away from My remembrance will have a constricted life, and We shall gather him blind on the Day of Resurrection.”

This further connects turning away with constriction and an outcome in the hereafter.

Ṭā Hā 20:123 does not mean that following divine guidance prevents every illness, poverty, sorrow or injustice inflicted by others.

Prophets and righteous people can suffer in life.

Suffering caused by circumstances or others differs from wretchedness associated with rejecting guidance and choosing a wrong course.

Someone may lose a home or possessions through another's injustice while remaining committed to truth and justice. That loss alone does not make them morally or ultimately wretched.

A wealthy, powerful person may instead place themselves on a path to an unhappy end by rebellion and violating others' rights.

The promise should therefore be read within the Quran's teaching on guidance and responsibility, not as immunity from every natural or social hardship.

For a broader examination, Forqan's article “Trials and the Promise of Good in the Quran: Is the World the Believer's Prison and the Unbeliever's Paradise?” discusses suffering, guidance, choice and promises concerning this life and the hereafter.

4. Wretchedness and the Day of Judgment

Hūd 11:105 and Hūd 11:106: wretched and fortunate

Hūd 11:105 says:

يَوْمَ يَأْتِ لَا تَكَلَّمُ نَفْسٌ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ فَمِنْهُمْ شَقِيٌّ وَسَعِيدٌ

yawma yaʾti lā takallamu nafsun illā bi-idhnihi fa-minhum shaqiyyun wa-saʿīd

Hūd 11:105

“On the day it comes, no soul speaks except by His permission; among them are the wretched and the fortunate.”

Hūd 11:106 continues:

فَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ شَقُوا فَفِي النَّارِ لَهُمْ فِيهَا زَفِيرٌ وَشَهِيقٌ

fa-ammā alladhīna shaqū fa-fī al-nāri lahum fīhā zafīrun wa-shahīq

Hūd 11:106

“Those who become wretched are in the fire; there they have groaning exhalation and inhalation.”

Wretchedness here directly concerns the hereafter.

Shaqiyy is opposed to saʿīd.

Hūd 11:106 places the wretched in the fire; Hūd 11:108 places the contrasting group in the garden.

This differs from everyday hardship in Ṭā Hā 20:117 and disappointment in prayer in Maryam 19:4.

The subject is people's final condition on the Day of Judgment.

SH Q W itself does not mean fire; al-nār is a separate word in the following sentence.

Wretchedness describes the adverse outcome, while fire specifies this group's destination in context.

Is being wretched an unchangeable fate from birth?

The relation of wretchedness and well-being to divine foreknowledge, decree and human choice is disputed in theology. Its complete resolution cannot be derived from these two words alone.

The phrase “among them are the wretched and the fortunate” does not itself establish an imposed destiny independent of human conduct.

It describes people on the Day of Judgment, not the irrelevance of every decision and action in life.

Earlier passages in the same surah repeatedly discuss conduct, injustice, denial, rebellion and their consequences for communities.

Ṭā Hā 20:123 also connects following guidance with avoiding wretchedness.

Ignoring these verses and treating wretchedness as independent of conduct would neglect a major part of the Quran's account of guidance and responsibility.

Divine knowledge should not simply be equated with coercion: knowledge of an outcome alone does not demonstrate that human choice and responsibility are meaningless. The wider question of divine will requires separate discussion.

Shaqiyy and saʿīd describe differing final conditions in Hūd 11:105; neither adjective alone supplies a theory of compulsory sin or goodness.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:106: our wretchedness and admitted misguidance

Those undergoing punishment say:

قَالُوا رَبَّنَا غَلَبَتْ عَلَيْنَا شِقْوَتُنَا وَكُنَّا قَوْمًا ضَالِّينَ

qālū rabbanā ghalabat ʿalaynā shiqwatunā wa-kunnā qawman ḍāllīn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:106

“They said: Our Lord, our wretchedness overcame us, and we were a people astray.”

Shiqwatunā combines shiqwah with the first-person plural suffix, “our.”

It denotes their wretched condition and unhappy outcome.

They also call themselves ḍāllīn, people astray.

Wretchedness and misguidance are thus related in their statement.

Ghalabat ʿalaynā, “overcame us,” does not alone prove compulsion or absolute inability to choose rightly.

These are the punished group's own words; responsibility must be examined through the surrounding exchange.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:105 asks whether God's verses were recited to them and they denied them.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:109 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:110 recall their mockery of believers who prayed.

The Quran therefore describes their conduct and responses, not merely an unfavorable condition.

Their claim that wretchedness overcame them cannot be detached from their conduct to erase all responsibility.

Shiqwatunā must also be distinguished from shakwatunā, belonging to SH K W.

Shakwā concerns complaint; shiqwah concerns wretchedness.

They are describing their wretchedness, not using a word meaning to complain.

5. Ashqā: intensified wretchedness and human conduct

Al-Aʿlā 87:11: avoiding the reminder

The Quran says:

وَيَتَجَنَّبُهَا الْأَشْقَى

wa-yatajannabuhā al-ashqā

Al-Aʿlā 87:11

“The most wretched avoids it.”

Al-Aʿlā 87:10 says:

سَيَذَّكَّرُ مَنْ يَخْشَىٰ

sa-yadhdhakkaru man yakhshā

Al-Aʿlā 87:10

“Whoever fears will take heed.”

The contrasting recipient is then called al-ashqā.

Ashqā has the afʿal elative form: more wretched or marked by greater wretchedness.

Al-Aʿlā 87:12 identifies this person:

الَّذِي يَصْلَى النَّارَ الْكُبْرَىٰ

alladhī yaṣlā al-nāra al-kubrā

Al-Aʿlā 87:12

“The one who enters the greater fire.”

The context describes avoidance of the reminder and a destination in fire.

This should not be reduced to financial deprivation or ordinary hardship.

Without context, ashqā does not establish that someone is worse off in every respect than every human throughout history.

An elative can highlight intensity within a specified group or context.

These verses identify the person's response to the reminder and final outcome.

Al-Shams 91:12: their most wretched in Thamūd's story

Al-Shams 91:12 says:

إِذِ انْبَعَثَ أَشْقَاهَا

idhi inbaʿatha ashqāhā

Al-Shams 91:12

“When their most wretched rose up.”

This occurs in Thamūd's story.

Al-Shams 91:11 says:

كَذَّبَتْ ثَمُودُ بِطَغْوَاهَا

kadhdhabat thamūdu bi-ṭaghwāhā

Al-Shams 91:11

“Thamūd denied through its transgression.”

Al-Shams 91:13 reports God's messenger warning about the she-camel and her drinking turn.

Al-Shams 91:14 describes denial and hamstringing the she-camel.

Ashqāhā points to someone who took the initiative in that wrongdoing.

The suffix refers to Thamūd and defines the comparison's scope.

“Their most wretched” is more precise than an unrestricted “the world's most unfortunate person.”

Al-Shams 91:12 uses singular inbaʿatha, while Al-Shams 91:14 uses plural fa-ʿaqarūhā.

The initiating individual's act thus occurs within the people's collective conduct.

Responsibility should neither be confined to one individual without examination nor assigned equally to every member solely through group membership.

Lexically, ashqāhā denotes greatest wretchedness within that group.

The wrongdoing is identified through the verses about transgression, denial and hamstringing the she-camel.

Al-Layl 92:15: ashqā in contrast with atqā

Al-Layl 92:14 says:

فَأَنْذَرْتُكُمْ نَارًا تَلَظَّىٰ

fa-andhartukum nāran talaẓẓā

Al-Layl 92:14

“I have warned you of a blazing fire.”

Al-Layl 92:15 continues:

لَا يَصْلَاهَا إِلَّا الْأَشْقَى

lā yaṣlāhā illā al-ashqā

Al-Layl 92:15

“None enters it except the most wretched.”

Al-Layl 92:16 specifies:

الَّذِي كَذَّبَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ

alladhī kadhdhaba wa-tawallā

Al-Layl 92:16

“The one who denied and turned away.”

Al-ashqā is identified through two actions: denial and turning away.

These belong to separate roots and should not be incorporated into ashqā's lexical meaning.

The context nevertheless connects this group's profound wretchedness with its response to truth.

Al-Layl 92:17 calls the contrasting person al-atqā. Al-Layl 92:18, Al-Layl 92:19 and Al-Layl 92:20 describe giving wealth, purification and seeking the Lord's favor.

This opposition therefore concerns orientation and conduct, not merely two emotional states.

The restrictive statement concerns the fire described in Al-Layl 92:14. Alone, it does not settle every detail of punishment or every group affected in the hereafter.

What is explicit is the connection with denial and turning away.

6. Distinguishing related roots

SH Q W and S ʿ D: wretchedness and well-being

Saʿādah concerns good fortune, benefit and a desirable condition or outcome.

Shaqāwah contrasts with it through suffering, deprivation and an unhappy end.

Hūd 11:105 directly contrasts them:

فَمِنْهُمْ شَقِيٌّ وَسَعِيدٌ

fa-minhum shaqiyyun wa-saʿīd

Hūd 11:105

“Among them are the wretched and the fortunate.”

This opposition is important for understanding the family.

Well-being should not always be reduced to momentary happiness, nor wretchedness to passing discomfort.

Someone may be happy at one moment while following a course leading to an unhappy outcome.

Someone else may suffer while pursuing truth yet attain a good outcome through right orientation and conduct.

In passages concerning final outcomes, these terms exceed a moment's pleasant or unpleasant feeling.

SH Q W and SH Q Q: hardship

SH Q Q concerns splitting and separation, and in some uses difficulty and toil.

Al-Naḥl 16:7 says:

إِلَّا بِشِقِّ الْأَنْفُسِ

illā bi-shiqqi al-anfus

Al-Naḥl 16:7

“Except with great personal hardship.”

This describes the effort and difficulty of an undertaking.

SH Q W concerns suffering, deprivation and an adverse outcome.

The two overlap in hardship but are distinct roots.

An undertaking can be strenuous while producing good and benefit.

Its difficulty alone does not make the person morally wretched or ill-fated.

Conversely, someone with little physical toil may still lack truth, inward security or a good outcome.

Not every hardship is moral wretchedness.

SH Q W, ʿ N Y and N Ṣ B: toil and fatigue

ʿAnāʾ concerns toil, difficulty and enduring hardship. In this sense it is commonly classified under ʿ N Y, and should not simply be equated with ʿ N W, which includes submission and captivity.

Naṣab is used in the Quran for fatigue and suffering arising from effort or difficult circumstances.

In Al-Kahf 18:62, Moses speaks of travel fatigue:

لَقَدْ لَقِينَا مِنْ سَفَرِنَا هَٰذَا نَصَبًا

la-qad laqīnā min safarinā hādhā naṣabā

Al-Kahf 18:62

“We have certainly encountered fatigue in this journey.”

Fatigue is part of the journey's experience.

It does not establish that Moses had abandoned guidance or suffered final wretchedness.

Bodily suffering and fatigue may approach some worldly uses of shaqāwah without sharing a moral outcome.

Ṭā Hā 20:117 concerns life's hardship, while Hūd 11:105 focuses on the hereafter.

Shaqāʾ thus extends beyond bodily fatigue.

SH Q W and Ḍ N K: constriction

Ḍank denotes a constricted and difficult living condition.

Ṭā Hā 20:124 says:

فَإِنَّ لَهُ مَعِيشَةً ضَنْكًا

fa-inna lahu maʿīshatan ḍankā

Ṭā Hā 20:124

“He will have a constricted life.”

This relates to wretchedness in Ṭā Hā 20:123.

Ḍank describes constriction of life, whereas shaqāwah may encompass a wider field of suffering, deprivation and an unhappy end.

Its entire range should not be confined to poverty or economic difficulty.

Nor does abundant wealth establish freedom from inward or ultimate wretchedness.

SH Q W and Ḍ L L: misguidance

Ḍalāl concerns losing the path or departing from guidance.

Shaqāwah concerns an undesirable condition or outcome.

Ṭā Hā 20:123 says:

فَلَا يَضِلُّ وَلَا يَشْقَىٰ

fa-lā yaḍillu wa-lā yashqā

Ṭā Hā 20:123

“Then he neither goes astray nor becomes wretched.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:106 again pairs wretchedness and misguidance.

The pairing shows a relationship between departure from guidance and an unhappy outcome.

They are not complete synonyms.

Misguidance concerns one's path and direction; wretchedness concerns condition or outcome.

Every lack of knowledge or unintentional error should not be labeled final wretchedness without examination.

Moral responsibility concerns knowledge, choice, conduct and responses to truth.

SH Q W, KH S R and F L Ḥ: loss and success

Khusrān concerns loss of benefit or capital.

Falāḥ denotes success and salvation.

Wretchedness and loss may converge in outcome while presenting different linguistic images.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:103 describes those with light scales as having lost themselves.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:106 then reports the same group speaking of its wretchedness.

Loss emphasizes forfeiture, while wretchedness describes their adverse condition and outcome.

Success, by contrast, concerns attainment and escape from loss.

The three families can describe one outcome while emphasizing different aspects.

SH Q W and SH K W: complaint

SH K W concerns complaining and expressing suffering or discomfort.

SH Q W concerns wretchedness and an unhappy outcome.

The letters are close, but their meanings differ.

A wretched person may complain, but complaint alone proves no wretchedness.

A righteous person may complain of injustice or suffering without being morally or ultimately shaqiyy.

Shiqwatunā in Al-Muʾminūn 23:106 means our wretchedness, not shakwatunā, “our complaint.”

7. Morphology and proposed Forqan renderings

The classification followed here treats SH Q W as a wāw-final weak root.

In shaqiya, the root wāw changes to yāʾ following a kasrah.

In yashqā, the final radical appears as alif maqṣūrah.

The root wāw is visible in shaqāwah and shiqwah.

Yāʾ in shaqiya and alif in yashqā do not alone determine a different historical root. This study follows the wāw-based classification, while some indexes use SH Q Y for related forms.

Quranic forms

Shaqiyyun: a masculine singular adjective in the nominative, in Hūd 11:105.

Shaqiyyan: accusative in Maryam 19:4, Maryam 19:32 and Maryam 19:48. The prayer passages emphasize disappointment or deprivation; Jesus' description denies wretchedness alongside tyranny.

Shaqū: the third-person masculine plural perfect of the basic verb, in Hūd 11:106.

Li-tashqā: an imperfect following the purpose lām in Ṭā Hā 20:2, denying distress as revelation's purpose; its subjunctive inflection is understood on the final alif.

Fa-tashqā: an imperfect after the causal fāʾ following prohibition in Ṭā Hā 20:117; its subjunctive inflection is understood on the final alif. The suffering follows departure from the garden.

Yashqā: an imperfect in Ṭā Hā 20:123; its indicative inflection is understood on the final alif within the negative clause forming the conditional response.

Shiqwatunā: shiqwah with the suffix “our,” in Al-Muʾminūn 23:106. Other readings include shaqāwatunā; both concern wretchedness and an unhappy condition.

Al-ashqā: a definite elative in Al-Aʿlā 87:11 and Al-Layl 92:15.

Ashqāhā: an elative with a feminine singular suffix referring to Thamūd, in Al-Shams 91:12.

These morphological distinctions alone do not determine the kind of suffering or outcome; context does.

Suggested renderings

Shaqiya: suffered, experienced hardship or became wretched.

Shaqāʾ: suffering, hardship, deprivation or an unhappy outcome, according to the subject.

Shaqāwah and shiqwah: wretchedness, ill fortune or a condition of suffering and deprivation.

Shaqiyy: wretched, ill-fated, deprived or suffering, according to its qualification and context.

Ashqā: more wretched, worse-fated or most wretched in an elative use.

Ashqiyāʾ: the wretched or ill-fated.

Ashqā, as a verb: caused someone to suffer or become wretched.

Shāqā: competed in enduring hardship or struggled with difficulty.

Mushāqāh: engagement and effort involving difficulty.

One fixed equivalent does not suit every Quranic use. These proposals do not alter the site's installed verse translations.

Maryam 19:4 and Maryam 19:48 favor “not being disappointed” in the context of prayer.

Ṭā Hā 20:2 and Ṭā Hā 20:117 are more clearly rendered through suffering or hardship.

In Ṭā Hā 20:123, “become wretched” can preserve the relationship with guidance.

In Hūd 11:105 and Hūd 11:106, “wretched” and “became wretched” fit the focus on the hereafter.

“Our wretchedness” is natural in Al-Muʾminūn 23:106.

The elative force of ashqā should be retained where appropriate, especially in Al-Shams 91:12, where the suffix defines the comparison's scope.

Ashqāhā thus means “their most wretched,” rather than merely “an unfortunate person.”

8. Conclusion

In early Arabic, SH Q W concerns suffering, hardship, deprivation and an unhappy outcome, contrasting with S ʿ D.

Early poetry illustrates the opposition between shaqiyy and saʿīd in discussing life and human fortune. The exact dates of all poems by poets spanning both periods are not established, and ethical context should not be ignored.

The Quran uses this family in several settings.

In Maryam, not being shaqiyy can concern not being disappointed in prayer. In Ṭā Hā it concerns life's hardship and departure from guidance. Hūd, Al-Muʾminūn, Al-Aʿlā, Al-Shams and Al-Layl connect it with unhappy outcomes, denial, rebellion and judgment.

Its uses cannot all be reduced to one narrow sense.

A defensible core is experiencing suffering, deprivation or an undesirable condition and outcome.

That definition alone does not determine the suffering's cause or the person's responsibility for it.

Illness, natural hardship, deprivation or injustice inflicted by others can cause suffering. Such suffering alone proves neither sin nor an unhappy final destiny.

Conversely, wretchedness can relate to wrong choices, knowing denial of truth and rejection of guidance, as several verses show.

Not every sufferer is morally or ultimately wretched, and not every prosperous person is fortunate in final outcome.

Reducing wretchedness to poverty, illness or hardship risks unjustly condemning righteous victims. Restricting it entirely to punishment in the hereafter overlooks its clear applications to life's toil and disappointment in prayer.

Nor does the contrast with well-being establish a compulsory fate independent of knowledge and conduct. The Quran also discusses guidance, following, denial, turning away and responsibility.

Wretchedness may describe a condition experienced in life or an outcome related to conduct and orientation. Suffering and deprivation of a desired condition are shared, but each verse and its wider context identify the kind, cause and significance.

For Forqan, suffering, deprivation, wretchedness and an unhappy outcome are principal equivalents. Context should guide selection rather than one imposed rendering for every form.

These uses do not make suffering inherently virtuous or comfort proof of final well-being. Human life's value is considered in relation to truth, guidance, conduct and outcome, not merely the amount of hardship or ease experienced during one period.
