The Arabic and Qur’anic meanings of N DAD R: from freshness and beauty to radiant faces amid the gifts of the Hereafter

The three-consonant Arabic root N DAD R concerns freshness, vitality, beauty and radiance. Its words can describe fresh green leaves and trees, or a face showing ease, liveliness and pleasure. Other Arabic uses connect the family with purity and fine quality, including descriptions of gold.

In the Qur’an, the root describes freshness and radiance, particularly in scenes of the Hereafter. It concerns more than beauty alone: an agreeable, visible condition that the verses associate with gifts, ease and joy.

N DAD R (نضر) must be distinguished from N ZHA R (نظر). The former concerns freshness and radiance; the latter concerns seeing, looking and, in some constructions, waiting. This matters especially in Al-Qiyāmah, where successive verses contain two words similar in sound but different in root and meaning.

1. Forms and semantic core

Two forms of this root occur in the Qur’an:

  • نَاضِرَةٌ: a feminine active participle meaning fresh, radiant or flourishing in appearance; Al-Qiyāmah 75:22.
  • نَضْرَةً / نَضْرَةَ: a noun denoting freshness, beauty and radiance; Al-Insān 76:11 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:24.

Together these forms occur three times. The Form I verb and other forms, including naḍīr, naḍārah, anḍar and naḍḍara, are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

The semantic core

In its principal uses, the root describes freshness or beauty visible in something’s condition or appearance. Three fields help distinguish the meanings.

First is natural freshness and vitality. Arabic applies the root to leaves, trees and plants that are fresh, green and flourishing. Greenness alone is not the whole description; freshness also matters.

Second is beauty and radiance of the face or appearance. A face described as nāḍir or naḍīr is fresh and pleasing in appearance. This may accompany liveliness, ease or enjoyment, but does not itself identify the cause.

Third is purity and fine quality, found in some uses of nuḍār and naḍīr concerning gold. Purity and value matter in this branch, without establishing that every derivative originated in gold or necessarily includes purity.

A general account can therefore mention beauty, freshness and radiance, with purity in certain branches; each derivative still requires its own particular meaning.

2. Early Arabic uses and linguistic background

Fresh plants and beautiful faces

Early lexical accounts record the root for leaves, trees, faces and colors, including expressions such as:

نَضَرَ الوَرَقُ وَالشَّجَرُ وَالوَجْهُ

naḍara al-waraqu wa-al-shajaru wa-al-wajhu

This describes leaves, trees and faces becoming fresh and pleasing in appearance.

Likewise, in:

أَخْضَرُ نَاضِرٌ

akhḍaru nāḍirun

The expression describes a fresh, pleasing green whose vitality and radiance are apparent.

The description is not restricted to green. Lexical accounts also apply nāḍir to other colors, conveying clarity, beauty or a pleasing appearance. It should not always be rendered as green or assigned one particular color.

The evidence shows that the word can describe plants, faces and other pleasing things within its familiar linguistic field. Recording these expressions in early dictionaries does not alone establish the date of their first use.

Naḍīr and nuḍār: gold, purity and fine quality

Naḍīr and nuḍār are recorded as names or descriptions of gold. Nuḍār also denotes what is pure or of fine quality.

In some uses nuḍār names wood suitable for making cups. Both “قَدَحٌ نُضَارٌ” and “قَدَحُ نُضَارٍ” are recorded in explanations of such vessels; some lexical accounts also use the first construction for a pure, fine-quality vessel. The context and construction therefore need attention.

The family thus extends beyond faces and plants. Gold and wood usages do not, however, justify a certain origin story for all its meanings. Historical evidence is needed before claiming that it first named gold and later facial beauty, or the reverse.

A pre-Qur’anic poetic witness

A poem attributed to al-Khirniq bint Badr ibn Haffān, a pre-Islamic poet, includes:

وَالْخَالِطِينَ نَحِيتَهُمْ بِنُضَارِهِمْ
وَذَوِي الْغِنَى مِنْهُمْ بِذِي الْفَقْرِ

wa-al-khāliṭīna naḥītahum bi-nuḍārihim / wa-dhawī al-ghinā minhum bi-dhī al-faqri

Translation: “Those who mix the people attached to their group with their own established kin, and their wealthy with their poor.”

Nuḍār contrasts with naḥīt here and denotes established members of the lineage. The poet praises a group that does not separate or discriminate among its members because of differences in descent or wealth.

The wording and attribution are not uniform. Some sources also attribute the verse to Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī; some transmit lujaynahum instead of naḥītahum. It is valuable for transmitted early Arabic usage, but cannot alone establish lineage purity as the root’s historically first meaning.

Such differences remind us that freshness and radiance, and purity and fine quality, are recorded within one lexical family without necessarily reducing to one physical image.

3. The Qur’anic uses of N DAD R

A. Al-Qiyāmah 75:22: fresh faces on the Day of Resurrection

وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَّاضِرَةٌ

wujūhun yawmaʾidhin nāḍiratun

Translation: “Faces that Day will be fresh and radiant.”

Nāḍirah is a feminine active participle of N DAD R and describes the faces’ condition here.

The face is a particularly visible place for a person’s state to appear. Pleasure, ease, worry and sorrow can show in it. Nāḍirah here describes freshness and radiance on the Day of Resurrection.

Study of W J H

To understand the description’s place, Al-Qiyāmah 75:22 should be read beside Al-Qiyāmah 75:24:

وَوُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ بَاسِرَةٌ

wa-wujūhun yawmaʾidhin bāsiratun

Translation: “And faces that Day will be grim and frowning.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:24

One group thus has fresh faces and the other grim, frowning faces.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:25 says of the second group:

تَظُنُّ أَن يُفْعَلَ بِهَا فَاقِرَةٌ

taẓunnu an yufʿala bihā fāqiratun

Translation: “Expecting a backbreaking calamity to be inflicted upon it.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:25

The context connects faces with the situation and expectation of people that Day. Fear or ease comes from the context, rather than necessarily belonging to the root’s invariant lexical meaning.

Nāḍirah should not be restricted to pale skin or physical light emission. The verse describes fresh, radiant faces without specifying their exact condition in the Hereafter.

B. Al-Insān 76:11: freshness and joy as reward

فَوَقَاهُمُ اللَّهُ شَرَّ ذَٰلِكَ الْيَوْمِ وَلَقَّاهُمْ نَضْرَةً وَسُرُورًا

fa-waqāhumu allāhu sharra dhālika al-yawmi wa-laqqāhum naḍratan wa-surūran

Translation: “So The God protected them from the evil of that Day and granted them freshness and joy.”

Naḍrah here is a noun alongside surūr, joy.

The pairing matters: surūr concerns joy and happiness, while naḍrah more strongly describes visible freshness, beauty and radiance.

The verse presents two distinct but connected aspects: freshness and vitality, and joy and happiness.

This should not become an absolute rule that naḍrah always concerns only the exterior and surūr only the interior. The distinction helps explain this context without fully defining either root’s entire field.

The preceding verses describe people who fulfill their vows, fear a difficult day and feed the needy, orphans and captives. They say they do so for The God, seeking neither reward nor thanks from people.

Al-Insān 76:10 also states:

إِنَّا نَخَافُ مِن رَّبِّنَا يَوْمًا عَبُوسًا قَمْطَرِيرًا

innā nakhāfu min rabbinā yawman ʿabūsan qamṭarīran

Translation: “We fear from our Lord a grim and severe day.”

Al-Insān 76:10

The passage then describes their protection from that Day’s evil and their receiving freshness and joy.

This creates a meaningful contrast: against a day described as grim and difficult, this group receives freshness and happiness.

Al-Insān 76:11 does not explicitly locate naḍrah in faces. Its facial connection becomes clearer from the root’s other Qur’anic uses. “In their faces” need not therefore be added to the direct translation, though the connection can be explained contextually.

C. Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:24: visible radiance of delight

تَعْرِفُ فِي وُجُوهِهِمْ نَضْرَةَ النَّعِيمِ

taʿrifu fī wujūhihim naḍrata al-naʿīmi

Translation: “You recognize in their faces the freshness and radiance of delight.”

The connection with faces is more explicit here: the verse states that this condition is recognizable in them.

Naḍrat al-naʿīm combines naḍrah, freshness and radiance, with naʿīm, delight and pleasant enjoyment.

It can be understood as radiance arising from delight and enjoyment. In this reading, naʿīm is the setting or cause and naḍrah its visible manifestation.

The context reinforces that connection:

إِنَّ الْأَبْرَارَ لَفِي نَعِيمٍ

inna al-abrāra la-fī naʿīmin

Translation: “Indeed, the righteous are in delight.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:22

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:24 then speaks of naḍrat al-naʿīm. It describes more than natural facial beauty: visible freshness within an account of the righteous enjoying the gifts of the Hereafter.

The word’s meaning must remain distinct from its cause. N DAD R alone does not mean Paradise, reward for deeds or wealth; accompanying words and context identify those concepts.

Study of N ʿ M

Nor does this description of the Hereafter establish facial freshness or beauty in worldly life as certain evidence of righteousness, divine approval or reward. It concerns a particular condition in the Hereafter, rather than a universal measure for judging people here.

4. Nāḍirah and nāẓirah: similar words from different roots

Two successive verses in Al-Qiyāmah offer a particularly subtle use:

وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَّاضِرَةٌ

wujūhun yawmaʾidhin nāḍiratun

Translation: “Faces that Day will be fresh and radiant.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:22

إِلَىٰ رَبِّهَا نَاظِرَةٌ

ilā rabbihā nāẓiratun

Translation: “Looking toward their Lord.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:23

In the first verse, nāḍirah contains ḍād and belongs to N DAD R, expressing freshness and radiance.

In the second, nāẓirah contains ẓāʾ and belongs to N ZHA R, concerning looking. Its precise meaning, particularly with the preposition ilā, requires an independent examination of that root and construction.

Their similar sound should not merge their meanings. The first describes faces’ condition; the second expresses a relation through looking toward the Lord.

The verses have prompted interpretive debate, particularly about seeing God in the Hereafter. N DAD R alone neither establishes nor rules out how such seeing would occur. The second verse requires independent study of nāẓirah, syntax, context and other related verses.

Study of N Ẓ R

Structurally, nāḍirah in “وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَاضِرَةٌ” is the nominative predicate of wujūh and is feminine singular. Arabic permits such agreement with nonhuman plurals, including plurals of body parts such as faces. The feminine form does not itself add a new root meaning.

5. Related words and structural points

N DAD R and N W R: freshness and light

Both can relate to brightness in some uses, but they are not identical.

Nūr denotes light and has broad sensory and figurative Qur’anic uses. Naḍrah describes freshness, beauty and radiance, a state that may appear bright without necessarily emitting light.

Naḍrat al-naʿīm should therefore not be restricted without evidence to physical light or light of a specified nature.

Study of N W R

N DAD R and H S N: freshness and beauty

Ḥusn is broader and can express beauty, goodness and excellence in different settings.

For faces and plants, N DAD R more strongly emphasizes freshness and vitality. Something nāḍir is pleasing in that respect, while not everything described as ḥasan is naturally called nāḍir.

A deed, statement or decision can, for example, be ḥasan without nāḍir being its natural description.

This is a usage tendency, rather than an absolute boundary between all occurrences.

Study of Ḥ S N

N DAD R and S R R: freshness and joy

Al-Insān 76:11 pairs naḍrah and surūr. The former denotes freshness and radiance, the latter joy and happiness.

They can accompany one another, since happiness may appear in the face, without happiness and its visible manifestation being identical.

Rendering both simply as happiness would erase part of the Qur’anic distinction.

Study of S R R

N DAD R and B S R: fresh and grim faces

Al-Qiyāmah sets nāḍirah and bāsirah against one another.

Nāḍirah describes fresh, pleasing faces; bāsirah describes grim, frowning faces.

The contrast is clear in this scene of the Hereafter, without making the roots complete opposites in every Arabic use. It comes from their shared situation and context here.

Study of B S R

Variant readings in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:24

The familiar Ḥafṣ reading has:

تَعْرِفُ فِي وُجُوهِهِمْ نَضْرَةَ النَّعِيمِ

taʿrifu fī wujūhihim naḍrata al-naʿīmi

Taʿrifu is active and naḍrata its object: the addressee recognizes the freshness of delight in their faces.

In the readings of Abū Jaʿfar and Yaʿqūb, the verb is passive and naḍrah nominative:

تُعْرَفُ فِي وُجُوهِهِمْ نَضْرَةُ النَّعِيمِ

tuʿrafu fī wujūhihim naḍratu al-naʿīmi

In this reading, the freshness of delight is recognized in their faces.

The main difference concerns syntax and presentation. Both retain naḍrah’s basic meaning and describe the radiance of delight in the righteous people’s faces.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Persian equivalents should be chosen by derivative and context.

نَاضِرَةٌ in Al-Qiyāmah 75:22:

“Fresh”, “flourishing” or “radiant” are appropriate. “Faces that Day will be fresh and radiant” conveys both the basic sense and the setting. Radiant describes the appearance without determining the physical nature of light.

نَضْرَةً in Al-Insān 76:11:

“Freshness” or “vitality” is appropriate. Since surūr accompanies it, both should not become happiness. “Granted them freshness and joy” preserves the difference.

نَضْرَةَ النَّعِيمِ in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:24:

“The freshness of delight” or “the radiance of delight” can be used. A fluent full sentence is: “You recognize in their faces the freshness and radiance of delight.”

Naʿīm belongs to the verse’s phrase and should not be incorporated into N DAD R’s independent definition.

For plants and general Arabic, green, fresh, flourishing or full of vitality may fit the context. When naḍīr or nuḍār denotes gold or a pure material, that specific lexical sense should be retained rather than forcing a freshness rendering.

7. Conclusion

In its main descriptive uses, N DAD R concerns visible freshness, vitality, beauty and radiance. Arabic applies it to leaves, trees, faces and pleasing things. Other branches concerning gold, purity and fine quality need separate consideration.

The Qur’an uses nāḍirah and naḍrah. All three occurrences concern a favorable state in the Hereafter: fresh faces in Al-Qiyāmah, freshness and joy in Al-Insān, and the radiance of delight in the righteous people’s faces in Al-Muṭaffifīn.

Naḍrah here is therefore more than beauty unrelated to the person’s situation. In these contexts it is a pleasing freshness associated with delight and ease. The root itself, however, does not mean reward, Paradise, joy or physical light; other words and context supply those concepts.

Final definition: In its principal descriptive branch, N DAD R denotes freshness, vitality and a pleasing radiance, whether in plants or in a human face. The Qur’anic uses connect this state with faces and the righteous enjoying the gifts of the Hereafter. Other branches, especially gold and purity, are recorded uses whose historical origins should not be reduced to one another without evidence.
