Heedlessness, loss of attention, forgetfulness, and neglect of what calls for attention

Arabic S H W concerns heedlessness, loss of attention, and the mind turning away from something. Someone may forget a matter, overlook part of an activity, or neglect something requiring attention.

Sahw can be brief and unintentional, as when attention wanders during a task. In another context it can describe disregard for an important responsibility.

The root appears twice in the Quran, both times as sāhūn: in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11, describing people immersed in heedlessness regarding truth and the Day of Recompense; and in Al-Māʿūn 107:5, describing those heedless of their prayer.

Sahw does not by itself name a sin or necessarily an involuntary, excusable lapse. Its basic meaning concerns inattention; the cause, responsibility, and ethical significance emerge from context.

1. Early Arabic usage and poetic evidence

Sahw: heedlessness and diverted attention

Early Arabic sources record sahā ʿani al-shayʾ for becoming heedless of something or turning one’s attention away from it.

Sahā, yas-hū, sahwan describes someone failing to attend to a matter or whose mind moves elsewhere.

Something may be present before a person without receiving due attention.

For example, preoccupation may prevent someone from hearing or understanding part of a conversation. Someone may also begin a task and then overlook one of its components.

Both involve insufficient or diverted attention.

Sāhin describes such a person; sahwān also denotes someone heedless or forgetful.

Early explanations relate sahw to nisyān and ghaflah, without making all their uses identical.

A person may retain something in memory without attending to it at a particular moment. Something else may be forgotten so thoroughly that a simple reminder does not retrieve it.

Lexical accounts differ over the precise boundaries. No single distinction should automatically become an absolute rule for all Arabic usage.

The principal sense of sahw is heedlessness or loss of attention to a matter relevant to someone’s awareness or action.

This includes brief distraction and allows more extensive heedlessness, without itself deciding whether the person is blameworthy or excused.

An early example: neglect of responsibility

Early sources record this proverb:

إِنَّ الْمُوَصَّيْنَ بَنُو سَهْوَانَ

inna al-muwaṣṣayna banū sahwān

“Those who need admonition are the sons of heedlessness.”

The proverb contrasts someone trusted to remember a task with someone who needs reminders. It is a proverbial characterization, not a general rule that everyone needing a reminder is negligent.

The expression also occurs in verse attributed to Zirr ibn Awfā al-Fuqaymī:

لَمْ يَثْنِهَا عَنْ هَمِّهَا قَيْدَانِ
وَلَا الْمُوَصَّوْنَ مِنَ الرُّعْيَانِ
إِنَّ الْمُوَصَّيْنَ بَنُو سَهْوَانِ

lam yathnihā ʿan hammihā qaydāni / wa-lā al-muwaṣṣawna mina al-ruʿyāni / inna al-muwaṣṣayna banū sahwāni

“Neither two fetters turned those camels from their purpose, nor the instructed herdsmen; those needing admonition are the sons of heedlessness.”

The image connects heedlessness with the need for reminders.

It attests inattention to work or responsibility, beyond forgetting a word or number.

The evidence examined does not establish the poet’s dates or this verse’s composition securely enough to call it pre-Quranic. The proverb is recorded in later lexicons; being old does not establish when it originated.

It supports early usage but does not prove that the exact expression circulated before the Quran.

The two Quranic sāhūn occurrences directly attest the language of revelation. The evidence examined supplies no independently dated pre-revelation example securely establishing this particular sense; that gap should not be filled with an invented historical claim.

Sahw as calmness and gentleness

A further branch concerns calmness, softness, and easy movement.

Mashy sahw is recorded for gentle, easy walking.

Nāqah sahwat al-mashy describes a camel with a smooth, comfortable gait.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

تُهَوِّنُ بُعْدَ الْأَرْضِ عَنِّي فَرِيدَةٌ
كِنَازُ الْبَضِيعِ سَهْوَةُ الْمَشْيِ بَازِلُ

tuhawwinu buʿda al-arḍi ʿannī farīdatun / kināzu al-baḍīʿi sahwatu al-mashyi bāzilu

“An exceptional, full-fleshed, mature camel makes the land’s distance easier for me, with her gentle, comfortable gait.”

Sahwat al-mashy describes the softness and comfort of the camel’s movement.

The subject is its gait, not its forgetfulness.

The line occurs in Zuhayr’s transmitted collection and early lexical sources. Its attribution matters for early usage, though its exact date cannot be separated from the transmission tradition.

S H W was thus not confined to heedlessness.

Early dictionaries also record al-sahw for calmness and softness, and musāhāh for leniency and agreeable treatment.

One can imagine an explanatory connection between calmness, relaxed strictness, and overlooking some matters. That does not establish a historical origin for every sense.

Heedlessness and gentleness should therefore be recognized without forcing either meaning onto the other.

Other lexical uses

Al-sahwah can name part of a building or a small space between rooms. Early descriptions include a projecting area, a shelf-like recess, a small store, or a place for belongings.

Al-Suhā names a faint, difficult-to-see star among Banāt Naʿsh.

One lexical explanation relates its name to the eye overlooking its faint light.

That suggestion alone does not establish the name’s historical origin.

The architectural sahwah likewise should not be derived from forgetfulness or mental calm merely to force unity onto the family.

The family has several branches, while its Quranic occurrences concern heedlessness and inattention.

2. Quranic occurrences and the form sāhūn

The root occurs twice, in the same form:

Sāhūn — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11

Sāhūn — Al-Māʿūn 107:5

Sāhūn is the sound masculine plural of sāhin.

Sāhin is the active participle of sahā, yas-hū, describing someone heedless or inattentive.

Both occurrences are nominative and describe groups of people.

The participial form alone does not imply permanent heedlessness at every moment. Nor does the plural make everyone’s causes and moral responsibility identical.

Both verses place sāhūn with hum:

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11 has hum fī ghamratin sāhūn.

Al-Māʿūn 107:5 has hum ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn.

The constructions illuminate different aspects of the heedlessness.

The first describes people in an encompassing condition.

The second relates their heedlessness to their prayer.

Sahā, yas-hū, sahw, sahwah, sahwān, musāhāh, and as-hā are known in Arabic but do not occur in those forms in the Quran.

Quranic sāhūn is neither a religious group’s name nor a technical category for people making mistakes during prayer. It describes heedlessness whose particular sense follows from the sentence and context.

3. Heedlessness amid unsupported conjecture

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:10 says:

قُتِلَ الْخَرَّاصُونَ

qutila al-kharrāṣūn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:10

“Perish the conjecturers.”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11 continues:

الَّذِينَ هُمْ فِي غَمْرَةٍ سَاهُونَ

alladhīna hum fī ghamratin sāhūn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11

“Those who are heedless in an overwhelming oblivion.”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:12 gives their question:

يَسْأَلُونَ أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ الدِّينِ

yasʾalūna ayyāna yawmu al-dīn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:12

“They ask: When is the Day of Recompense?”

The three verses form a continuous context.

First come people making unsupported claims about the matters at issue; then their encompassing heedlessness; then their question about the Day of Recompense.

Ghamrah and sahw

Ghamrah belongs to GH M R, whose physical uses concern surrounding and covering something.

Abundant water, for example, can submerge and cover an object.

The image can describe a condition so absorbing that it affects a person’s perception and attention.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11, fī ghamrah describes their encompassing condition, and sāhūn their heedlessness.

The words are not exact synonyms.

Ghamrah emphasizes what envelops and obscures; sāhūn describes people failing to attend to truth within it.

“They are in heedlessness and are heedless” conveys part of the sense but obscures the distinct imagery.

“Immersed in oblivion, they remain heedless” better expresses the relationship.

Does sahw here mean simply not knowing?

Someone may not know a truth because the necessary information has not reached them.

Another person may possess information yet ignore evidence through preoccupation with claims, conjectures, and desires.

Sahw can include the latter condition.

Al-kharrāṣūn matters in this context: the people make unsupported claims and remain heedless.

The verse does not simply condemn every uninformed person.

Nor does it make every estimate, possibility, or hypothesis sinful. Life and research sometimes require assessing possibilities with limited information.

The contextual criticism concerns unsupported conjecture and disregard for responsible understanding, rather than every disciplined use of probability.

Asking about the Day of Recompense

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:12 reports their question about its timing.

Asking a question does not itself establish heedlessness or denial.

People may ask in order to learn.

Here, however, the question follows descriptions of conjecture and heedlessness, so it must be understood alongside the group’s conduct and stance.

The subsequent verses describe the outcome they question.

Sāhūn in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11 therefore describes heedlessness accompanying immersion in conjecture and a failure to attend seriously to the Day of Recompense.

It concerns more than a momentary memory lapse: it affects how a person encounters truth.

4. Heedless of their prayer

The second occurrence is Al-Māʿūn 107:5:

الَّذِينَ هُمْ عَنْ صَلَاتِهِمْ سَاهُونَ

alladhīna hum ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn

Al-Māʿūn 107:5

“Those who are heedless of their prayer.”

It continues the warning in Al-Māʿūn 107:4:

فَوَيْلٌ لِلْمُصَلِّينَ

fa-waylun li-al-muṣallīn

Al-Māʿūn 107:4

“So woe to those who pray.”

The passage does not end there.

Al-Māʿūn 107:6 describes showing off; Al-Māʿūn 107:7 describes withholding assistance or useful things from others.

Understanding sāhūn requires reading this alongside Al-Māʿūn 107:2, on repelling the orphan, and Al-Māʿūn 107:3, on failing to encourage feeding the needy.

Neglect of prayer or a lapse during prayer?

The preposition ʿan is significant.

The Quran does not say here:

“Fī ṣalātihim sāhūn” — inattentive during their prayer.

It says:

“ʿAn ṣalātihim sāhūn” — heedless of their prayer.

Sahā fī al-ṣalāh can describe inattention or an error while praying.

Someone may forget part of the act or briefly turn their attention elsewhere.

Sahā ʿan ṣalātihi directs attention to neglect of prayer itself: the practice or its responsibility falls outside the person’s concern.

This matters in Al-Māʿūn’s context.

Nevertheless, ʿan does not invariably mean complete, deliberate abandonment, nor does fī invariably mean an excusable lapse.

The constructions can overlap. ʿAn highlights attention moving away from its object; fī can locate the lapse within an activity.

The passage concerns more than a mistake during prayer: it describes outward worship accompanied by ostentation and withholding help.

Does sāhūn mean only abandoning prayer?

Some lexical and exegetical accounts explain sahā ʿan al-ṣalāh as abandoning or neglecting prayer.

This belongs to disregard for the practice and merits consideration in appropriate contexts.

Here, fa-waylun li-al-muṣallīn precedes sāhūn.

Those criticized are identified as people who pray.

The verse therefore cannot simply be confined to people who never pray. It describes people known as worshippers yet heedless of their prayer.

Neglect can appear as disregard for its responsibility, failure to maintain it, or merely outward performance without attention to its significance and effects.

A particular instance requires evidence about the person’s conduct.

Sahw and ostentation

Al-Māʿūn 107:6 says:

الَّذِينَ هُمْ يُرَاءُونَ

alladhīna hum yurāʾūn

Al-Māʿūn 107:6

“Those who show off.”

Yurāʾūn belongs to R ʾ Y and here concerns ostentation.

Someone acts for visibility or standing in others’ eyes.

Outward worship may remain while attention and motivation shift toward display and public approval.

This is compatible with sahw: attention is diverted from what should be central to worship.

Sahw and ostentation nevertheless differ.

Not every lapse is ostentation, and distraction does not necessarily imply an intention to show off.

Ostentation concerns a particular behavior and motive; sahw concerns inattention.

Al-Māʿūn places them together to describe the criticized group.

Sahw and withholding al-māʿūn

Al-Māʿūn 107:7 says:

وَيَمْنَعُونَ الْمَاعُونَ

wa-yamnaʿūna al-māʿūn

Al-Māʿūn 107:7

“And they withhold small acts of assistance.”

The criticism extends beyond the appearance of worship.

Al-Māʿūn 107:2 addresses the orphan and Al-Māʿūn 107:3 the needy; Al-Māʿūn 107:7 ends with withholding help and useful resources.

This continuity relates neglect of prayer to social conduct.

Someone may worship publicly while withholding a simple form of help.

Attention to outward worship does not by itself demonstrate fidelity to its substance.

The root S H W alone does not contain the full meanings of prayer, māʿūn, or social responsibility.

Sahw means heedlessness; its object and consequences emerge from the related verses.

The relationship between prayer, its establishment, and conduct requires a separate study of ṣalāh; it should not be inserted into sahw’s lexical definition.

Does every distraction in prayer fall under this warning?

Brief distraction can occur in worship, reading, conversation, or an important task.

Such a lapse can lexically be called sahw.

It differs from neglect accompanied by ostentation and disregard for others’ rights.

The passage criticizes people described through a particular combination of conduct.

It does not place every worshipper who briefly loses concentration in that same category.

Conversely, the possibility of involuntary lapses does not eliminate responsibility for every kind of neglect.

Unintended events, sustained disregard, and deliberate behavior creating the conditions for inattention must be distinguished.

The verse concerns responsibility toward prayer, rather than condemning every natural lapse of attention.

5. Sahw, choice, and responsibility

The relationship between heedlessness and choice is central to this root’s interpretation.

Sahw can describe an act performed without sufficient attention or with a forgotten component.

Its ethical significance is not always the same.

Someone may briefly lose attention despite taking due care. The lapse alone does not establish deliberate abandonment of responsibility.

Someone else may knowingly create conditions that divert attention from a duty, or remain indifferent despite repeated reminders.

The immediate lapse may be unintended while the preceding or sustaining conduct remains relevant to responsibility.

An early lexical analysis also distinguishes lapses whose causes lie outside someone’s control from those whose conditions the person creates.

This distinction is compatible with the Quran’s two occurrences.

In Al-Dhāriyāt, people are immersed in conjecture.

In Al-Māʿūn, neglect accompanies ostentation and withholding assistance.

The criticism emerges from conduct and circumstances in context, rather than an isolated mental state.

Sahw is not identical to deliberate forgetting

Everyday speech sometimes says someone deliberately forgot something.

This usually means disregarding or setting it aside, rather than erasing a memory at will.

The same distinction matters for sahw.

Heedlessness may arise from involuntary loss of attention or from someone’s conduct and orientation.

The word alone does not reveal hidden intentions.

Sāhūn therefore does not prove that the people consciously decided to forget at every moment.

Nor should their heedlessness be reduced to an involuntary lapse while ignoring the critical context.

Both errors conflate lexical meaning with ethical judgment.

6. Comparison with related roots

S H W (sahw) and GH F L (ghaflah): heedlessness and inattention

Quranic ghaflah concerns unawareness and matters falling outside attention or vigilance.

Sahw belongs to a closely related field.

Its lexical explanations often emphasize attention moving from one matter to another.

Ghaflah can describe broader unawareness, while sahw sometimes concerns a limited, brief lapse.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11, sahw accompanies ghamrah and describes an encompassing condition. Quranic sahw is not necessarily small or momentary.

S H W (sahw) and N S Y (nisyān): inattention and forgetting

Nisyān concerns forgetting or failing to remember.

Sahw can also involve forgetting, though many uses emphasize diverted attention.

Someone may retain information yet fail to attend to it during an activity. This can be sahw without complete loss from memory.

Nisyān may instead describe no longer recalling something.

Early sources differ over the precise distinction, and sometimes explain each word through the other.

Quranic nisyān is also not confined to poor memory. In Al-Tawbah 9:67, people forget The God in a context concerning conduct and disregard.

Nisyān should not be made invariably involuntary, or sahw invariably deliberate.

S H W (sahw) and L H W (lahw): heedlessness and diversion

Lahw concerns occupation or diversion that may draw attention away from another matter.

Sahw concerns the inattention itself.

Both may coincide when entertainment distracts someone from a responsibility.

Not all sahw arises from amusement, and not every diversion produces blameworthy neglect.

Fatigue or anxiety can produce a lapse without recreational distraction.

Someone can also enjoy recreation while remembering and fulfilling responsibilities.

Pleasure or amusement is therefore not a fixed component of sahw.

S H W (sahw) and ʿ R Ḍ (iʿrāḍ): inattention and turning away

Quranic iʿrāḍ often describes turning away or withdrawing from something.

It can involve a conscious decision.

Sahw does not itself express such a decision.

Someone may become inattentive without deciding beforehand to turn away.

Deliberate avoidance can nevertheless produce sustained neglect.

In Al-Māʿūn 107:5, ʿan highlights neglect of prayer, but the preposition alone does not establish every feature of deliberate avoidance.

S H W (sahw) and S M D (sumūd): inattention and dismissive diversion

Sāmidūn in Al-Najm 53:61 occurs alongside laughter and a frivolous response to a warning.

Diversion and disregard are prominent there.

Sāhūn in Al-Dhāriyāt and Al-Māʿūn concerns heedlessness and loss of attention.

The families approach each other in some descriptions of conduct, but S M D also has other Arabic branches, including calmness and continued movement.

Overlap in particular contexts does not make the roots identical.

S H W (sahw) and S H L (sahl): calmness and ease

One S H W branch concerns gentle, easy movement.

Sahl concerns levelness, softness, and reduced difficulty.

They can overlap in descriptions of a smooth gait or lenient conduct.

Heedlessness in S H W should nevertheless remain distinct from ease in S H L.

Someone may perform an easy task attentively, or suffer a lapse during a difficult one.

Similar gentle-motion senses do not prove historical derivation of one root from the other.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Only sāhūn occurs in the Quran, but the two contexts differ.

Sāhūn — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11

The verse reads:

الَّذِينَ هُمْ فِي غَمْرَةٍ سَاهُونَ

alladhīna hum fī ghamratin sāhūn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11

Suggested translation:

“Those who are heedless in an overwhelming oblivion.”

This agrees with Forqan’s current translation.

“Heedless” directly renders sāhūn. With ghamrah in the same sentence, needless repetition should be avoided where the distinction can be preserved.

“Unaware” can also convey sāhūn in the context of encompassing oblivion.

The lexical explanation should keep sāhūn’s inattention distinct from the enveloping condition expressed by ghamrah.

Sāhūn — Al-Māʿūn 107:5

The verse reads:

الَّذِينَ هُمْ عَنْ صَلَاتِهِمْ سَاهُونَ

alladhīna hum ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn

Al-Māʿūn 107:5

Suggested translation:

“Those who are heedless of their prayer.”

This preserves the relationship expressed by ʿan ṣalātihim.

Forqan’s current wording likewise says “heedless of their prayer.” A root study can retain the term ṣalāh when discussing its meaning, while examining that word’s range separately.

“Those who become distracted while praying” is unsuitable here because it overlooks ʿan and the context of ostentation and withholding help.

“Those who never pray” also restricts the sentence to complete abandonment without sufficient basis.

“Those who pray late” may express one discussed application, but sāhūn does not itself mean temporal delay.

“Heedless of their prayer” is therefore a more suitable and comprehensive rendering.

Brief root meaning and contextual meanings

Word in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11: سَاهُونَ

Root: S H W

Brief root meaning: Heedlessness, loss of attention, and the mind turning away from something.

Meaning here: People immersed in heedlessness, failing to attend to truth and the Day of Recompense.

Word in Al-Māʿūn 107:5: سَاهُونَ

Root: S H W

Brief root meaning: Heedlessness, loss of attention, and the mind turning away from something.

Meaning here: People heedless of their prayer and its responsibility, in a context of ostentation and withholding assistance.

Other forms in this family

Sahā: became heedless or turned attention away.

Yas-hū: becomes inattentive.

Sahw: heedlessness, distraction, and loss of attention; in another branch, calmness and softness.

Sāhin: a heedless or inattentive person.

Sahwān: heedless and forgetful.

As-hā: caused someone to become inattentive.

Musāhāh: leniency and overlooking some of others’ lapses in social relations.

Sahwat al-mashy: describing a gentle, easy gait.

Al-sahwah: an architectural space or place for belongings.

Al-Suhā: a faint, difficult-to-see star.

These illustrate the word family, but only sāhūn occurs in the Quran.

Conclusion

The Quranic branch of S H W concerns heedlessness, loss of attention, and the mind turning from a matter relevant to awareness or responsibility.

Arabic sahw may concern a brief unintended lapse or occur in contexts of disregard and neglected responsibility.

The word alone does not determine blame or excuse.

Another branch concerns calmness, easy movement, and leniency. Poetry attributed to Zuhayr uses sahwat al-mashy for a camel’s comfortable gait, without proving that inattention historically arose from smooth movement.

Sāhūn appears twice in the Quran.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:11 describes immersion in conjecture and heedlessness, without serious attention to truth and the Day of Recompense.

Al-Māʿūn 107:5 describes neglect of prayer, alongside ostentation and withholding assistance.

The difference concerns the object and context: encompassing inattention to truth in one passage, and neglect of prayer and its responsibility in the other.

The resulting definition is:

S H W in its heedlessness branch denotes loss of attention or the mind turning away from something. In Arabic generally, this may be brief and unintended or become sustained through conduct and orientation. Both Quranic occurrences criticize contextualized neglect; responsibility is understood through cause, object, and context rather than the word alone.

Not every lapse can be declared sinful, nor every neglect involuntary and excusable.

Both Quranic uses point beyond an ordinary memory lapse: immersion in conjecture in Al-Dhāriyāt, and neglect of worship’s substance despite its possible outward observance in Al-Māʿūn.

Someone may perform an act while neglecting its substance and responsibility, just as they may speak extensively about a subject while ignoring its evidence and truth.

The distinction between outward participation and actual attention matters for sāhūn. Its lexical sense remains heedlessness, while judgments about conduct require examining the person’s actions and responsibilities together.
