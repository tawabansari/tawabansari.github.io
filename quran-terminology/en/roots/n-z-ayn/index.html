Drawing and pulling out: from uprooting and removal to contention, disagreement, and passing something among people

1. Introducing the root and its central meaning

The Arabic root «ن ز ع» concerns drawing, pulling out, separating, and removing something from its place or condition. «نَزَعَ» can describe extracting an object, taking clothing off the body, drawing a bucket from a well, or uprooting something.

These uses involve a relationship between what is drawn and the place or direction from which it is removed. It may be physical, such as drawing a hand from beneath clothing or removing something from the ground, or nonphysical, such as taking power from someone, removing resentment from the heart, or relinquishing an earlier attachment.

Another important branch is munāzaʿah and tanāzuʿ. Drawing or pulling can occur between two or more parties, giving an intelligible image for contention, disagreement, and dispute: each party draws the issue towards its own view or wish.

Not every tanāzuʿ means hostility. Arabic also uses this family for exchanging something between people. At Al-Ṭūr 52:23, «يَتَنَازَعُونَ» concerns a cup passed among the Garden’s inhabitants. The word is therefore not inherently blameworthy or violent.

Drawing, separating, and moving something within a relationship form an important semantic centre; contention, uprooting, removal, and passing something around are different uses determined by verbal structure and context.

The family cannot be translated everywhere with one equivalent. Taking off clothing and disputing an affair are different actions, just as exchanging a cup differs from hostility.

Qur’anic forms

The root occurs 20 times in 20 verses across 16 surahs, in five morphological constructions:

  • «نَزَعَ» and its inflected forms: ten occurrences: Āl ʿImrān 3:26; Al-Aʿrāf 7:27, Al-Aʿrāf 7:43, and Al-Aʿrāf 7:108; Hūd 11:9; Al-Ḥijr 15:47; Maryam 19:69; Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:33; Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:75; Al-Qamar 54:20.
  • «نَازَعَ»: once, as «يُنَازِعُنَّكَ» at Al-Ḥajj 22:67.
  • «تَنَازَعَ»: seven occurrences: Āl ʿImrān 3:152; Al-Nisāʾ 4:59; Al-Anfāl 8:43 and Al-Anfāl 8:46; Al-Kahf 18:21; Ṭā Hā 20:62; Al-Ṭūr 52:23.
  • «نَزَّاعَةً»: once, at Al-Maʿārij 70:16.
  • «النَّازِعَاتِ»: once, at Al-Nāziʿāt 79:1.

The basic triliteral verb is most frequent, followed by Form VI, which usually concerns disagreement and contention among people.

«نَزَّاعَة» is an intensive description; «نَازِعَات» is a feminine plural active participle.

Arabic also records «نَزْع», «نُزُوع», «نِزَاع», «مُنَازَعَة», «اِنْتِزَاع», «مَنْزُوع», «نَزِيع», «مَنْزَع», and «أَنْزَع», but these nominal or verbal-noun forms do not occur in the Quran.

Each Qur’anic construction should be understood through its actual use, rather than importing every dictionary meaning into every verse.

2. Early Arabic uses

Drawing and uprooting something from its place

One lexical explanation states the semantic core as follows:

النُّونُ وَالزَّاءُ وَالْعَيْنُ أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى قَلْعِ شَيْءٍ

al-nūnu wa-al-zāʾu wa-al-ʿaynu aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā qalʿi shayʾin

Translation:

“This root denotes uprooting something.”

An example such as «نَزَعْتُ الشَّيْءَ مِنْ مَكَانِهِ» illustrates this: “I pulled the thing out of its place.”

Separation and extraction are prominent, but the verb does not always mean wrenching something out with great force.

«نَزَعَ ثَوْبَهُ», for example, means removing one’s garment, an action that can be gentle and ordinary.

Extracting something firmly attached, by contrast, may require more force.

The force involved therefore comes from the object, accompanying descriptions, or context, rather than being present in every use of the root.

Drawing a bucket from a well

A familiar Arabic use is drawing a bucket from a well:

نَزَعَ الدَّلْوَ مِنَ الْبِئْرِ

nazaʿa al-dalwa mina al-biʾri

Meaning:

“He drew the bucket up from the well.”

This concerns pulling the bucket out by its rope.

Some lexical explanations distinguish this from other methods of drawing water. It can particularly concern a bucket drawn up by hand and rope.

«مَنْزَعَة» is also recorded for a place by the well’s mouth where the water-drawer stands while lifting the bucket.

The action was therefore a familiar physical part of everyday Arab life.

The image does not make every Qur’anic use upward movement. Direction depends on the object and sentence construction.

Drawing a bowstring

Another important use concerns archery.

«نَزَعَ فِي الْقَوْسِ» is recorded for drawing a bowstring.

The archer pulls the string towards themselves to provide the force for launching the arrow.

«مِنْزَع» also names an arrow in some uses, particularly one intended for a long shot.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ, a pre-Islamic poet, says of a swift horse:

فَهُوَ كَالْمِنْزَعِ الْمَرِيشِ مِنَ الشَّوْ
حَطِ مَالَتْ بِهِ شِمَالُ الْمُغَالِي

fa-huwa ka-al-minzaʿi al-marīshi mina al-shawḥaṭi / mālat bihi shimālu al-mughālī

Translation:

“The horse is like a feathered arrow of shawḥaṭ wood, set in motion by the hand of an archer shooting far.”

Minzaʿ concerns an arrow, and the poet compares the horse’s speed to an arrow released from a bow.

The witness connects this family with drawing a bow and shooting an arrow.

The line appears in the collected poetry attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ, but some dictionaries attribute it to Al-Aʿshā. Wording also varies between «مالت به شمال» and «غالت به يمين».

Its attribution is therefore disputed, although its presence in ʿUbayd’s collection supports its use as pre-Qur’anic evidence.

The witness concerns archery and should not restrict every use of the root to an arrow’s movement from a bow.

Withdrawing from an activity

Arabic «نَزَعَ عَنِ الْأَمْرِ» means ceasing an activity or leaving it behind.

No physical object is removed; the person distances themselves from behaviour they were engaged in.

This connects with separation more generally, but the relationship differs.

In removing clothing, the garment is separated from the body.

In withdrawing from an affair, the person leaves an activity.

«عن» can clarify the direction of separation, but should not become a mechanical rule making every such use repentance or abandoning sin.

A person may stop doing something right or wrong; moral value does not follow from the root alone.

Yearning for family and homeland

«نَزَعَ إِلَى أَهْلِهِ» is also recorded for longing for one’s family and turning back towards them.

The family likewise describes a camel drawn towards its familiar home or pasture.

In some uses, nāziʿ and nuzūʿ concern a person or animal drawn towards a place or thing.

Jamīl ibn Maʿmar, a poet of the Islamic period, says:

فَقُلْتُ لَهُمْ لَا تَعْذُلُونِي وَانْظُرُوا
إِلَى النَّازِعِ الْمَقْصُورِ كَيْفَ يَكُونُ

fa-qultu lahum lā taʿdhulūnī wa-unẓurū / ilā al-nāziʿi al-maqṣūri kayfa yakūnu

Translation:

“I told them: Do not reproach me; look at the longing person restrained from his goal, and see his condition.”

Nāziʿ here concerns inward attraction and yearning.

The family can thus describe an inclination towards something, not only separation and extraction.

Jamīl lived after the period of revelation, however, so the line is not direct pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Nor does it establish that «النَّازِعَات» at the beginning of Al-Nāziʿāt must mean longing people or inwardly inclined beings. Each form requires its own structure and context.

Dispute and contention: pulling in two directions

«نَازَعَهُ الْحَبْلَ» describes two people pulling a rope from opposite sides.

This offers an intelligible physical image for disagreement: each side tries to draw the matter towards itself.

«نَازَعَهُ فِي الْأَمْرِ» means disputing or contending with someone about a matter.

Yet «نَازَعَهُ الْكَأْسَ» is also recorded for exchanging a cup with someone.

The difference is important for understanding the Quran.

A construction expressing hostility in one context can concern passing something between people in another.

Munāzaʿah and tanāzuʿ therefore cannot everywhere be confined to hostility, war, or blameworthy argument.

3. Qur’anic removal: taking, separating, and uprooting

A. Āl ʿImrān 3:26: taking away dominion

قُلِ اللَّهُمَّ مَالِكَ الْمُلْكِ تُؤْتِي الْمُلْكَ مَنْ تَشَاءُ وَتَنْزِعُ الْمُلْكَ مِمَّنْ تَشَاءُ وَتُعِزُّ مَنْ تَشَاءُ وَتُذِلُّ مَنْ تَشَاءُ بِيَدِكَ الْخَيْرُ إِنَّكَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

quli allāhumma mālika al-mulki tuʾtī al-mulka man tashāʾu wa-tanziʿu al-mulka mimman tashāʾu wa-tuʿizzu man tashāʾu wa-tudhillu man tashāʾu bi-yadika al-khayru innaka ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrun

Translation:

“Say: O The God, Owner of dominion, You give dominion to whom You will and take it from whom You will; You honour whom You will and humble whom You will. In Your hand is good; You have power over everything.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:26

«تَنْزِعُ» contrasts with «تُؤْتِي».

One concerns giving dominion; the other concerns taking it away.

«الْمُلْكَ» is the object of both verbs.

The use therefore concerns removing dominion from someone who previously possessed it.

Dominion is not a physical object drawn from a well; the verb concerns power and governmental authority.

The root does not establish that the removal necessarily occurs through war, a coup, or violence. It does not specify the means of change.

Nor does possessing power alone establish divine moral approval of everything its holder does.

The verse concerns divine ownership and power, without declaring every existing ruler just and worthy.

Natural equivalents here are “take away” or “withdraw.”

B. Al-Aʿrāf 7:27: removing Adam and his wife’s clothing

يَنْزِعُ عَنْهُمَا لِبَاسَهُمَا لِيُرِيَهُمَا سَوْآتِهِمَا

yanziʿu ʿanhumā libāsahumā li-yuriyahumā sawʾātihimā

Translation:

“Removing their clothing from them to show them their private parts.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:27, part of the verse

This phrase occurs in a warning to Adam’s descendants.

The verb appears with «عَنْهُمَا» and the object «لِبَاسَهُمَا».

The direct meaning is removing the covering from the two of them.

The purpose comes from «لِيُرِيَهُمَا»: showing what had been covered.

The verb should therefore not directly be defined as “exposing private parts.” Exposure follows from removing the covering and the rest of the sentence.

The Adam narrative also distinguishes covering, temptation, exposure, and the pair’s own behaviour; these are not all contained in one verb.

C. Mūsā drawing out his hand

Al-Aʿrāf 7:108 says:

وَنَزَعَ يَدَهُ فَإِذَا هِيَ بَيْضَاءُ لِلنَّاظِرِينَ

wa-nazaʿa yadahu fa-idhā hiya bayḍāʾu li-al-nāẓirīna

Translation:

“He drew out his hand, and suddenly it was white and shining for those watching.”

The same wording occurs at Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:33.

Both uses describe Mūsā drawing out his hand.

Another verse helps identify where the hand was placed:

اسْلُكْ يَدَكَ فِي جَيْبِكَ تَخْرُجْ بَيْضَاءَ مِنْ غَيْرِ سُوءٍ

usluk yadaka fī jaybika takhruj bayḍāʾa min ghayri sūʾin

“Put your hand into your garment’s opening; it will come out white, without harm.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32, part of the verse

This connects the hand’s withdrawal with its being placed inside the garment’s opening.

Whitening is not the meaning of nazaʿa; it is a development reported after the hand is drawn out.

Nor does the verb mean severing the hand from the body. It is drawn from a covered position, not detached from the body.

The object and context therefore determine the kind of separation.

D. Hūd 11:9: withdrawing mercy from a person

وَلَئِنْ أَذَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ مِنَّا رَحْمَةً ثُمَّ نَزَعْنَاهَا مِنْهُ إِنَّهُ لَيَئُوسٌ كَفُورٌ

wa-la-in adhaqnā al-insāna minnā raḥmatan thumma nazaʿnāhā minhu innahu la-yaʾūsun kafūrun

Translation:

“If We let the human being taste mercy from Us, then withdraw it from him, he becomes deeply despairing and ungrateful.”

Hūd 11:9

The verb here concerns mercy.

«نَزَعْنَاهَا» means “We took it away from him.”

«مِنْهُ» identifies the person from whom it is withdrawn.

Despair and ingratitude are not meanings of the verb; they are the human response the verse reports afterwards.

The next verse examines the response to renewed blessing.

The passage concerns not only changed circumstances but human conduct in receiving and losing benefits.

It does not establish that every deprivation or loss in human life must be divine punishment for a particular sin.

The verb means taking something away; it alone cannot determine the cause or wisdom of every similar event.

E. Al-Aʿrāf 7:43 and Al-Ḥijr 15:47: removing resentment

Al-Aʿrāf says:

وَنَزَعْنَا مَا فِي صُدُورِهِمْ مِنْ غِلٍّ

wa-nazaʿnā mā fī ṣudūrihim min ghillin

“We removed whatever resentment was in their breasts.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:43, part of the verse

The same phrase occurs at Al-Ḥijr 15:47, followed by:

إِخْوَانًا عَلَىٰ سُرُرٍ مُتَقَابِلِينَ

ikhwānan ʿalā sururin mutaqābilīna

“As brothers, facing one another on couches.”

The verb concerns an inward condition.

Ghill is connected with resentment and concealed hostility.

The sentence therefore means removing resentment from the Garden’s inhabitants.

It should not become the physical extraction of an object from the chest: breasts here are the seat of inward states, and removal concerns resentment.

Nor does the verse establish the erasure of every memory, personal identity, or ability to recognise the past. It speaks only of removing ghill.

Al-Ḥijr 15:47 describes brotherhood and facing one another as the resulting condition.

“Removing resentment” therefore fits this use, while peace and brotherhood come from the expression as a whole.

F. Maryam 19:69 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:75: separating people from groups

Maryam says:

ثُمَّ لَنَنْزِعَنَّ مِنْ كُلِّ شِيعَةٍ أَيُّهُمْ أَشَدُّ عَلَى الرَّحْمَٰنِ عِتِيًّا

thumma la-nanziʿanna min kulli shīʿatin ayyuhum ashaddu ʿalā al-raḥmāni ʿitiyyan

Translation:

“Then from every group We will draw out those most defiant towards the Merciful.”

Maryam 19:69

The use concerns separating people from among groups.

«مِنْ كُلِّ شِيعَةٍ» identifies the groups from which people are taken.

«أَيُّهُمْ أَشَدُّ» states the criterion: the greatest defiance.

The verb does not itself mean being defiant. That quality comes from «أَشَدُّ عَلَى الرَّحْمَٰنِ عِتِيًّا».

Al-Qaṣaṣ also says:

وَنَزَعْنَا مِنْ كُلِّ أُمَّةٍ شَهِيدًا فَقُلْنَا هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ

wa-nazaʿnā min kulli ummatin shahīdan fa-qulnā hātū burhānakum

Translation:

“From every community We brought forth a witness, then said: Bring your proof.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:75, part of the verse

Here the verb concerns separating or summoning a witness from each community.

The verses differ in their objects and subject matter.

Maryam describes people singled out for defiance.

Al-Qaṣaṣ describes a witness brought forth from each community.

The verb therefore cannot have a fixed meaning of punishment or testimony in both. Separation and bringing forth are shared; the people’s roles come from context.

G. Al-Qamar 54:20: uprooting people through a devastating wind

تَنْزِعُ النَّاسَ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ مُنْقَعِرٍ

tanziʿu al-nāsa ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin munqaʿirin

Translation:

“It tears people away, as though they were trunks of uprooted date palms.”

Al-Qamar 54:20

The verse concerns the wind sent against ʿĀd.

Al-Qamar 54:19 says:

إِنَّا أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ رِيحًا صَرْصَرًا

innā arsalnā ʿalayhim rīḥan ṣarṣaran

“We sent a fierce, devastating wind against them.”

The subject of the verb in verse 20 is that wind.

The removal involves a destructive force uprooting people.

Its force and destructiveness come from the fierce wind’s context and the image of uprooted trunks.

The verb does not mean killing or destroying in every use.

The contrast with Mūsā is clear: a hand is drawn from a position in one case, while people are torn away by wind in the other.

Drawing and separation are shared, but the events differ in manner and outcome.

Study of N KH L

4. Tanāzuʿ: disagreement, contention, and exchange

A. Āl ʿImrān 3:152: disagreement during confrontation

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فَشِلْتُمْ وَتَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ وَعَصَيْتُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا أَرَاكُمْ مَا تُحِبُّونَ

ḥattā idhā fashiltum wa-tanāzaʿtum fī al-amri wa-ʿaṣaytum min baʿdi mā arākum mā tuḥibbūna

Translation:

“Until you lost courage, disputed over the matter, and disobeyed after He had shown you what you loved.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:152, part of the verse

«تَنَازَعْتُمْ» appears with «فِي الْأَمْرِ».

The subject is disagreement within a group over carrying out an affair.

In the context of Uḥud, it accompanies loss of courage and disobedience and affects the group’s situation.

These three behaviours must nevertheless be distinguished:

«فَشِلْتُمْ» concerns losing courage.

«تَنَازَعْتُمْ» expresses disagreement and contention.

«عَصَيْتُمْ» concerns disobedience.

They are separate words, not components all contained within tanāzuʿ.

Nor does every disagreement everywhere inherently constitute disobedience. This disagreement occurs in a particular situation alongside other behaviours.

B. Al-Nisāʾ 4:59: disagreement and its point of reference

فَإِنْ تَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي شَيْءٍ فَرُدُّوهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَالرَّسُولِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ

fa-in tanāzaʿtum fī shayʾin fa-ruddūhu ilā allāhi wa-al-rasūli in kuntum tuʾminūna bi-allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri

Translation:

“If you disagree about something, refer it back to The God and the Messenger, if you believe in The God and the Last Day.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:59, part of the verse

Tanāzuʿ concerns disagreement over an issue.

«فِي شَيْءٍ» is general rather than confined to one particular issue.

The command «فَرُدُّوهُ» follows the disagreement.

It belongs to «ر د د» and concerns referring the disputed matter to the specified authority.

Tanāzuʿ names the disagreement; referring it back states how it is to be addressed.

The root itself does not determine the solution.

The verse does not make every question, investigation, or different understanding blameworthy. It recognises disagreement and specifies a procedure for reference.

Even after mentioning obedience to The God, the Messenger, and those holding authority, the verse directs disputed matters back to The God and the Messenger.

The dispute should therefore not be treated as settled simply by one party’s wishes or social standing.

The details of authority, limits of obedience, and dispute resolution require broader examination of verses on command, obedience, and judgement.

C. Al-Anfāl 8:43 and Al-Anfāl 8:46: disagreement and lost collective strength

Al-Anfāl 8:43 says:

وَلَوْ أَرَاكَهُمْ كَثِيرًا لَفَشِلْتُمْ وَلَتَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ

wa-law arākahum kathīran la-fashiltum wa-la-tanāzaʿtum fī al-amri

Translation:

“Had He shown them to you as numerous, you would have lost courage and disputed over the matter.”

The verse presents a hypothetical situation.

The disagreement might have arisen in assessing the confrontation.

Al-Anfāl 8:46 says:

وَأَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَلَا تَنَازَعُوا فَتَفْشَلُوا وَتَذْهَبَ رِيحُكُمْ وَاصْبِرُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

wa-aṭīʿū allāha wa-rasūlahu wa-lā tanāzaʿū fa-tafshalū wa-tadhhaba rīḥukum wa-iṣbirū inna allāha maʿa al-ṣābirīna

Translation:

“Obey The God and His Messenger, and do not contend with one another, lest you lose courage and your strength depart. Remain steadfast; The God is with the steadfast.”

This forbids contention that produces weakness and loss of collective strength.

«فَتَفْشَلُوا» and «تَذْهَبَ رِيحُكُمْ» state consequences of such contention.

Weakness and loss of power are not part of tanāzuʿ’s lexical meaning.

Nor does the verse establish a prohibition on every scholarly disagreement or criticism of another person’s decisions.

Its context is confrontation and the need for coordinated action. Disagreement destroying collective strength in danger differs from evidence-based examination and responsible discussion.

The kind of disagreement, circumstances, and consequences determine the blameworthy conduct.

D. Al-Kahf 18:21: disagreement over the people of the cave

إِذْ يَتَنَازَعُونَ بَيْنَهُمْ أَمْرَهُمْ

idh yatanāzaʿūna baynahum amrahum

Translation:

“When they disputed among themselves about their affair.”

Al-Kahf 18:21, part of the verse

The disagreement concerns a matter involving the people of the cave.

The rest of the verse reports proposals to build a structure over their site and another group’s statement about establishing a place of worship.

Tanāzuʿ concerns debate and disagreement among people.

The verse alone does not establish that everyone acted in anger, hostility, or violence.

Disagreement over a decision can take different forms.

Reporting a proposal for a structure or place of worship does not inherently endorse every belief and action of the speakers.

The basic rendering “they disputed” is suitable.

E. Ṭā Hā 20:62: discussion and contention among the magicians

فَتَنَازَعُوا أَمْرَهُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ وَأَسَرُّوا النَّجْوَىٰ

fa-tanāzaʿū amrahum baynahum wa-asarrū al-najwā

Translation:

“They disputed and discussed their affair among themselves, concealing their private conversation.”

Ṭā Hā 20:62

This occurs in the magicians’ confrontation with Mūsā.

They discuss their situation and decision together.

«تَنَازَعُوا أَمْرَهُمْ» can express disagreement and contention over how to address the matter.

«أَسَرُّوا النَّجْوَىٰ», however, is a separate expression concerning concealing a private conversation.

Secrecy is therefore not part of tanāzuʿ itself.

Not every confidential conversation is a dispute, nor is every dispute conducted secretly.

F. Al-Ḥajj 22:67: contention over the matter

لِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنْسَكًا هُمْ نَاسِكُوهُ فَلَا يُنَازِعُنَّكَ فِي الْأَمْرِ وَادْعُ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ إِنَّكَ لَعَلَىٰ هُدًى مُسْتَقِيمٍ

li-kulli ummatin jaʿalnā mansakan hum nāsikūhu fa-lā yunāziʿunnaka fī al-amri wa-udʿu ilā rabbika innaka la-ʿalā hudan mustaqīmin

Translation:

“For every community We appointed rites which they perform. Let them not contend with you over the matter. Call to your Lord; you are on straight guidance.”

«يُنَازِعُنَّكَ» belongs to Form III.

Others are the subject, while «كَ» addresses the Prophet.

The sentence therefore concerns their contention or argument with the Prophet over the issue raised.

Form III alone does not establish that both sides bear equal blame for the disagreement.

Nor must munāzaʿah carry the technical meaning of a particular legal proceeding.

The issue concerns rites and the Prophet’s call; its full dimensions require the surrounding verses of Al-Ḥajj.

G. Al-Ṭūr 52:23: tanāzuʿ without hostility

يَتَنَازَعُونَ فِيهَا كَأْسًا لَا لَغْوٌ فِيهَا وَلَا تَأْثِيمٌ

yatanāzaʿūna fīhā kaʾsan lā laghwun fīhā wa-lā taʾthīmun

Translation:

“There they pass a cup among themselves, with neither idle speech nor sin in it.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:23

This important occurrence prevents limiting the root to hostility and blameworthy disagreement.

«يَتَنَازَعُونَ» has the object «كَأْسًا».

The subject is passing a cup among the Garden’s inhabitants.

Arabic «تَنَازَعُوا الْكَأْسَ» can mean circulating the cup, giving and taking it among one another.

It connects with the image of drawing something between several parties, but the context contains no hostile, destructive dispute.

The rest of the sentence instead says that the drinking involves neither idle speech nor sin.

“They fight over a cup there” would therefore misrepresent the context.

Conversely, this use does not make every other occurrence merely friendly conversation.

Depending on object and context, Qur’anic tanāzuʿ can concern disagreement and contention or passing something among people.

This counterexample corrects any definition restricting the root to hostility.

5. Nāziʿāt and nazzāʿah: two uses in contexts concerning the hereafter

A. Al-Maʿārij 70:16: «نَزَّاعَةً لِلشَّوَىٰ»

Al-Maʿārij says:

كَلَّا إِنَّهَا لَظَىٰ

kallā innahā laẓā

“No indeed; it is a blazing fire.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:15

Then:

نَزَّاعَةً لِلشَّوَىٰ

nazzāʿatan li-al-shawā

“Tearing away skin and outer limbs.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:16

Nazzāʿah is a description from the root in an intensive form.

The form can convey intensity or repetition; the precise manner comes from context.

«الشَّوَىٰ» belongs to another root. Arabic records it for the body’s extremities, scalp, or parts contrasted with vital organs.

Explanations differ: some concern scalp, some hands and feet, and others further body parts.

The meanings of nazzāʿah and shawā should therefore not be merged.

Nazzāʿah describes tearing and separating; shawā identifies what undergoes that action.

Ḥafṣ’s recitation has accusative «نَزَّاعَةً»; other accepted readings have nominative «نَزَّاعَةٌ».

The difference concerns the description’s syntactic role, not its basic meaning of removal.

The verse concerns the fire of the hereafter without specifying the physical details of burning, detachment of limbs, or bodily reconstruction.

Translation should retain the image of tearing away without constructing a bodily mechanism beyond the text.

B. Al-Nāziʿāt 79:1: «وَالنَّازِعَاتِ غَرْقًا»

وَالنَّازِعَاتِ غَرْقًا

wa-al-nāziʿāti gharqan

A rendering close to the wording:

“By those that draw, forcefully and to the utmost.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:1

This is among the root’s most difficult occurrences for identifying a referent.

Nāziʿāt is the feminine plural active participle of nāziʿah, denoting those who or things which perform the drawing.

The word itself does not specify angels, stars, bows, or other beings or things.

Gharqan independently belongs to «غ ر ق». One attested explanation here is drawing to the utmost, exerting full force, comparable to drawing a bowstring to its furthest extent.

It should therefore not immediately be translated as submersion in water.

First interpretation: angels extracting souls

A familiar interpretation identifies the nāziʿāt as angels extracting human souls at death.

This connects with Arabic use of the root for extracting life or approaching death.

The verse itself, however, contains neither “angels” nor “souls.”

Their identification is interpretive, not something whose every detail follows from nāziʿāt alone.

Second interpretation: stars

Some early explanations identify stars moving from one horizon to another or disappearing from the observer’s sight.

This has been connected with broader uses of the root for movement and departure from a position.

The verse does not directly name stars.

The possibility therefore needs independent evidence before becoming a definite meaning.

Third interpretation: bows and drawn strings

Another interpretation connects nāziʿāt with bows or the drawing of bowstrings.

It has lexical support, since drawing a bow is a familiar early Arabic use.

The short expression alone does not establish it conclusively either.

Which interpretation is preferable?

The five opening verses must be examined together: Al-Nāziʿāt 79:1, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:2, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:3, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:4, and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:5:

وَالنَّازِعَاتِ غَرْقًا

wa-al-nāziʿāti gharqan

وَالنَّاشِطَاتِ نَشْطًا

wa-al-nāshiṭāti nashṭan

وَالسَّابِحَاتِ سَبْحًا

wa-al-sābiḥāti sabḥan

فَالسَّابِقَاتِ سَبْقًا

fa-al-sābiqāti sabqan

فَالْمُدَبِّرَاتِ أَمْرًا

fa-al-mudabbirāti amran

They name several descriptions and actions without explicitly naming their referents.

The angel interpretation has many supporters and accords with some explanations concerning death and the ordering of affairs.

Stars and bows are also attested early interpretations and cannot simply be dismissed on the basis of the surah’s name.

Methodologically, the lexical meaning “those that draw” should remain distinct from identifying them as angels or stars.

A close rendering with contextual explanation is preferable to adding a referent not named in the verse itself.

The feminine form also does not by itself determine the beings’ sex. Arabic feminine plurals can relate to a noun or group treated as grammatically feminine.

6. Morphological distinctions and related roots

Nazaʿa, nāzaʿa, and tanāzaʿa

The three Qur’anic verbal constructions should be distinguished.

Basic Form I usually concerns drawing, extraction, or removal.

Form III can concern contention with another person.

Form VI can express disagreement between several parties or exchanging something.

These tendencies should not become absolute laws.

Form III does not necessarily imply equal power or responsibility.

Form VI does not prove that everyone acts with hostile intentions.

At Al-Ṭūr 52:23, that very form concerns passing a cup.

Morphology provides semantic possibilities; the object and context determine the resulting sense.

N Z ʿ and Q L ʿ: drawing out and uprooting

«ق ل ع» concerns pulling out and uprooting something.

It overlaps with the physical branch of nazaʿa.

Nazaʿa is broader, also describing taking away dominion, removing resentment, and drawing out a hand.

At Al-Qamar 54:20, wind uproots people, approaching the sense of qalʿ.

At Āl ʿImrān 3:26, however, Persian “uprooting dominion” is less natural than “taking dominion away.”

The equivalent should fit the sentence’s subject matter.

N Z ʿ and J DH B: drawing and attraction

Arabic «ج ذ ب» concerns pulling something towards oneself.

It is close to nazaʿa, particularly with ropes, buckets, and bowstrings.

Qur’anic nazaʿa can foreground removal from a position, while jadhb more directly emphasises drawing towards the one pulling.

The difference is a tendency, not an absolute boundary.

Tanāzuʿ can likewise evoke pulling from several directions, but that image should not replace established meanings of disagreement or exchange.

N Z ʿ and KH L ʿ: removing clothing

«خ ل ع» concerns taking off clothing or footwear and removing something from its position.

The Quran says:

فَاخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ

fa-ikhlaʿ naʿlayka

“Take off your sandals.”

Ṭā Hā 20:12, part of the verse

This is close to removing clothing through nazaʿa.

Nazaʿa nevertheless applies to many different objects and concepts, from buckets and bows to power and resentment.

The roots overlap in some constructions without being completely synonymous.

Study of KH L ʿ

N Z ʿ and KH Ṣ M: contention and hostility

«خ ص م» concerns hostility, argument, and disputes between parties.

Tanāzuʿ likewise concerns contention in many verses.

The difference is important: Al-Ṭūr 52:23 uses tanāzuʿ for passing a cup, without hostility.

Not every use can therefore be equated with hostility.

At Al-Nisāʾ 4:59, disagreement over something requiring judgement and reference need not involve warfare or intense enmity.

“Disagreement” is therefore often more suitable than “hostility.”

Study of KH Ṣ M

N Z ʿ and N Z GH: contention and provoking discord

These roots are close in writing and sound but should not be confused.

Qur’anic «ن ز غ» concerns provocation, temptation, and disturbing relationships.

Yūsuf 12:100 says:

مِنْ بَعْدِ أَنْ نَزَغَ الشَّيْطَانُ بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَ إِخْوَتِي

min baʿdi an nazagha al-shayṭānu baynī wa-bayna ikhwatī

“After Satan stirred discord and hostility between me and my brothers.”

Nazgh concerns provoking disruption between people.

Tanāzuʿ concerns the disagreement or contention itself.

Provocation and temptation may cause disagreement without being identical to it.

Satanic temptation or provocation should not therefore enter every use of tanāzuʿ.

7. Understanding and translation proposals for Forqan

The range of uses is the chief translation challenge.

Rendering everything as “uprooting” leaves power, resentment, and disagreement inadequately expressed.

Rendering everything as “contention” distorts Mūsā’s hand, the withdrawal of mercy, and removal of Adam’s covering.

Treating every tanāzuʿ as hostility also misrepresents Al-Ṭūr 52:23.

An appropriate equivalent must be chosen for each construction and object.

Tanāzuʿ in social contexts

At Al-Nisāʾ 4:59, “you disagreed” suits «تَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي شَيْءٍ».

At Āl ʿImrān 3:152 and Al-Anfāl 8:43, disagreement during confrontation may be rendered “you disagreed and contended.”

At Al-Anfāl 8:46, “do not contend” fits the warning about collective weakness.

At Al-Kahf 18:21 and Ṭā Hā 20:62, the kind of disagreement must come from context.

At Al-Ṭūr 52:23, “they pass the cup” is clear and natural.

Persian “they quarrel,” though close to the root’s familiar form, would mislead readers about this sentence.

Nāziʿāt at the beginning of Al-Nāziʿāt

The chief question is identifying the participle’s referent.

Adding “angels” would place an interpretation in the wording’s place.

Adding “stars” would make a different interpretation certain.

A base translation should therefore retain “those that draw,” or a close expression, with a brief explanation.

Gharqan also requires separate study. In the bowstring interpretation it emphasises force and drawing to the utmost.

This should not become a rule making every Qur’anic use of «غ ر ق» mean intensity.

The relationship between the words and the oath construction produces a particular contextual meaning.

8. Conclusion

Arabic «ن ز ع» concerns drawing, extraction, separation, and removing something from its place or condition.

Physical examples include drawing a bucket, removing clothing, drawing a bowstring, and uprooting something.

Nonphysical uses concern taking power away, removing resentment, abandoning an activity, or singling people out from groups.

Munāzaʿah and tanāzuʿ connect mutual pulling with disagreement and contention.

They are not confined to hostility, since Arabic and the Quran also use them for exchanging something such as a cup.

Early Arabic evidence, particularly poetry attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ, connects the family with bows and arrows. Buckets, covering, withdrawal, and yearning for home further illustrate its range.

The Quran uses the root 20 times in five morphological constructions.

Āl ʿImrān 3:26 concerns taking dominion away.

At Al-Aʿrāf 7:27, Adam and his wife’s covering is removed.

At Al-Aʿrāf 7:108 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:33, Mūsā draws out his hand.

Hūd 11:9 concerns withdrawing mercy from a person.

At Al-Aʿrāf 7:43 and Al-Ḥijr 15:47, resentment is removed from the Garden’s inhabitants.

At Maryam 19:69 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:75, people are separated or summoned from groups.

At Al-Qamar 54:20, a devastating wind uproots people.

The tanāzuʿ verses concern disagreement over an issue, contention in danger, and responding to disagreement.

Al-Ṭūr 52:23, however, shows that the same form can concern passing a cup rather than disagreement or hostility.

At Al-Maʿārij 70:16, nazzāʿah intensively describes removal and tearing away.

At Al-Nāziʿāt 79:1, nāziʿāt denotes performers of drawing, without explicitly identifying them. Angels, stars, or bows require independent interpretive argument.

The root therefore names neither an inherently good nor an inherently bad action.

Taking power, removing resentment, drawing out a hand, uprooting people, and interpersonal disagreement differ in value and consequence. Object, syntax, and context establish those differences.

A proposed final definition for Forqan

«ن ز ع» centrally concerns drawing, extraction, and removing something from its place or condition. The action may be physical or nonphysical and, according to its subject, mean drawing out, taking away, removing, uprooting, or relinquishing.

Form III and Form VI emphasise a relationship of pulling between parties, permitting disagreement, argument, and contention. Some uses, such as Al-Ṭūr 52:23, instead concern exchange and passing something between people.

Nāziʿāt and nazzāʿah concern performing the action of drawing and separating, while intensity, object, and particular referent depend on construction and context.

Drawing and separation form a defensible shared semantic centre, while disagreement, contention, and exchange are particular uses involving several parties.

Each Forqan translation should use a natural equivalent suited to the verse, without replacing the root’s direct meaning with a moral, political, or interpretive conclusion.
