Core meanings: removal, uprooting, and destruction. Some uses emphasize complete removal, while the degree is determined by context. In financial and ethical usage, suḥt denotes wrongful, tainted wealth or income, condemned because of how it is acquired. The Qur’an uses the root once in a warning of destruction through punishment and three times to condemn consuming wrongful wealth.

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic S Ḥ T concerns uprooting, stripping away, removal, and destruction.

Saḥata al-shayʾa means destroying or uprooting something.

Saḥata al-shaḥma ʿani al-laḥmi means scraping fat off meat.

Saḥata raʾsahu describes shaving the head closely.

Asḥata mālahu concerns destroying or wasting wealth.

These uses concern removal and stripping away, which can extend to complete elimination. The root alone does not specify a fixed percentage removed in every use.

Removing some fat may be partial; in uses of saḥt, scraping can extend to complete removal.

The family can also describe wealth being consumed until it disappears.

The scraping image does not mean every use involves removing material layers.

Destruction of wealth or a group of people is not literally scraping away fat or hair.

Early poetry: loss of wealth in al-Farazdaq

The Umayyad-period poet al-Farazdaq says:

وَعَضُّ زَمَانٍ يَا ابْنَ مَرْوَانَ لَمْ يَدَعْ
مِنَ الْمَالِ إِلَّا مُسْحَتًا أَوْ مُجَلَّفُ

wa-ʿaḍḍu zamānin yā ibna Marwāna lam yadaʿ / mina al-māli illā musḥatan aw mujallafu

Translation:

“And the bite of time, O son of Marwān: wealth was either destroyed or left severely diminished.”

The poet imagines time inflicting damage that consumes wealth.

Musḥat describes what has been destroyed or stripped away.

Mujallaf here describes damaged wealth of which a remainder survives; this contrasts with musḥat.

Transmissions vary in wording and case endings, including musḥatan and musḥatun, and the case of mujallaf. These variants are discussed in grammatical works.

They affect syntactic analysis without changing the central sense of musḥat as destroyed wealth. The translation above conveys the distinction between the descriptions rather than reproducing every grammatical reconstruction.

The verse is transmitted in al-Farazdaq's collected poetry and early lexical and grammatical sources.

He lived in the Umayyad period, so it is not direct pre-Islamic evidence.

It illustrates destruction and removal in early Islamic Arabic.

Recorded Arabic uses

Classical lexicons record uses beyond complete destruction.

سَحَتَ الشَّيْءَ

saḥata al-shayʾa

Meaning: “He destroyed that thing.”

سَحَتَ الشَّحْمَ عَنِ اللَّحْمِ

saḥata al-shaḥma ʿani al-laḥmi

Meaning: “He scraped the fat off the meat.”

أَسْحَتَ مَالَهُ

asḥata mālahu

Meaning: “He destroyed his wealth.”

سَحَتَ وَجْهَ الْأَرْضِ

saḥata wajha al-arḍi

Meaning: “He scraped or stripped the surface of the ground.”

These uses emphasize the effect on the object.

It may disappear completely or be substantially stripped away.

Another use concerns hair:

سَحَتَ رَأْسَهُ

saḥata raʾsahu

Meaning: “He shaved his head closely.”

This clarifies the root's material imagery.

These expressions are mainly known through classical lexicons; their inclusion in later dictionaries does not establish a pre-Qur’anic date for each one.

Was the pre-Islamic meaning the same?

The evidence examined here does not provide a securely attributed and dated independent pre-Islamic poetic example.

Al-Farazdaq's verse is Islamic-period evidence. Scraping, stripping, and uprooting are recorded lexically, but the exact history of every use cannot be dated.

Ṭā Hā 20:61 itself supplies early Qur’anic evidence for destruction and uprooting.

This meaning has clear linguistic support, without proving that every branch existed in the same extent and historical sequence before Islam.

A historical definition

The Qur’an, early Islamic poetry, and classical lexical uses support this definition:

S Ḥ T concerns removing or destroying something, sometimes through scraping and uprooting, sometimes through the loss of its substance or much of it.

In the financial branch, suḥt denotes wrongful wealth or income.

Lexical explanations connect this with the loss of blessing, reputation, and integrity.

This explains a semantic relationship without establishing that the financial sense necessarily arose later than scraping or uprooting.

2. Important forms and derivatives

سَحَتَ يَسْحَتُ

The family's Form I verb.

It means removing, uprooting, or stripping something away to destruction.

Of the body, it can concern shaving hair or removing fat.

Of wealth, it concerns its destruction.

This verb occurs in one reading of Ṭā Hā 20:61.

سَحْت

The Form I verbal noun, denoting stripping, uprooting, or destroying.

Some lexical uses also give al-saḥt the sense of punishment or hardship.

Destruction or serious harm can underlie those uses.

This verbal noun should not be equated in every use with al-suḥt, wrongful wealth.

أَسْحَتَ يُسْحِتُ

A Form IV verb.

It means destroying, eliminating, or uprooting.

أَسْحَتَ مَالَهُ

asḥata mālahu

Meaning: “He destroyed his wealth.”

Fa-yusḥitakum in Ṭā Hā 20:61 belongs to this form in the Ḥafṣ reading followed here.

Saḥata and asḥata overlap in the sense of destroying.

Form IV alone does not establish greater speed, severity, or completeness than Form I.

The degree and extent require attention to usage and context.

إِسْحَات

The verbal noun of asḥata.

Destruction or elimination.

This verbal noun does not occur in this form in the Qur’an.

سُحْت

In financial usage, wealth, benefit, or income acquired wrongfully.

Lexicons describe unlawful income, tainted earnings, or gains that bring reproach and disgrace.

Bribery is a clear example.

The recorded meaning is not restricted to bribery.

The noun occurs three times in Al-Māʾidah.

سُحُت

A variant of suḥt with ḍammah on ḥāʾ.

Both are recorded in Arabic and Qur’anic readings.

The vowel variation does not itself create two distinct financial or ethical concepts.

مَسْحُوت

The passive participle of Form I.

Something removed or destroyed.

It can describe wealth that has been lost.

مُسْحَت

The passive participle of Form IV.

Al-Farazdaq uses it for wealth that has been destroyed.

Destruction is prominent in that use.

مَسْحُوتُ الْجَوْف

An Arabic expression describing someone with an intense, insatiable appetite.

It pictures food disappearing inside without anything remaining to satisfy the person.

This image also helps explain associations with greed and excessive eating.

It does not restrict every form of S Ḥ T to greed or gluttony.

Forms actually found in the Qur’an

The root occurs four times, in two principal forms:

  • Fa-yusḥitakum in Ṭā Hā 20:61, a Form IV imperfect verb in the base reading.
  • Li-al-suḥti in Al-Māʾidah 5:42, the noun preceded by lām.
  • Al-suḥta in Al-Māʾidah 5:62 and Al-Māʾidah 5:63, the accusative noun.

These cover destruction in Ṭā Hā and wrongful wealth or income in Al-Māʾidah.

3. Fa-yusḥitakum bi-ʿadhāb: Moses warns of destruction

قَالَ لَهُمْ مُوسَىٰ وَيْلَكُمْ لَا تَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ كَذِبًا فَيُسْحِتَكُمْ بِعَذَابٍ وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنِ افْتَرَىٰ

qāla lahum Mūsā waylakum lā taftarū ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban fa-yusḥitakum bi-ʿadhābin wa-qad khāba mani iftarā

Ṭā Hā 20:61

Translation:

“Moses said to them: Woe to you! Do not fabricate a lie against The God, lest He destroy you through punishment. Whoever fabricates has indeed failed.”

Ṭā Hā 20:61

Fa-yusḥitakum is a Form IV imperfect in the base reading.

Its implicit subject refers to God in context.

-Kum refers to the people Moses warns.

Bi-ʿadhāb expresses the means or manner of destruction.

The verb here means destroying or uprooting.

The context of Moses's warning

Ṭā Hā 20:56 says Pharaoh saw the divine signs but denied them and refused.

In the following verses he accuses Moses of magic, and a meeting is arranged to confront him.

Pharaoh assembles his resources and plan.

Moses then warns them in Ṭā Hā 20:61 against fabricating lies about God.

Fa-yusḥitakum therefore warns of the consequences of fabrication against God.

The verb does not make wrongful earnings or bribery the subject here.

Those belong to another branch of the family.

This passage concerns destruction through punishment.

Uprooting and destruction

In destructive uses, saḥt can emphasize eliminating something at its source or stripping it away completely.

Shaving the head closely differs from trimming a little hair.

Destroying wealth differs from a minor reduction.

“Uproot” can therefore convey the force of yusḥitakum.

It does not mean the verse specifies the punishment's physical form, duration, or every detail.

The verb conveys forceful destruction; bi-ʿadhāb identifies the means.

Different readings and verbal forms

Recognized readings contain two forms:

فَيُسْحِتَكُمْ

fa-yusḥitakum

Ṭā Hā 20:61

Lest He destroy you; Form IV.

فَيَسْحَتَكُمْ

fa-yasḥatakum

Lest He destroy you; Form I in another reading.

The first derives from asḥata, Form IV.

The second derives from saḥata, Form I.

Both have the established sense of destruction or uprooting.

The morphological difference is real, but does not establish two completely different kinds of punishment.

Following the prohibition lā taftarū and causal fāʾ, the verb is subjunctive.

The construction connects the warning with fabrication's consequence.

Does this concern every human mistake?

The verse says lā taftarū ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban.

Iftirāʾ, from F R Y, concerns falsely attributing something to God here.

That is distinct from an unknowing mistake, an honest question, or failure to understand correctly.

Moses addresses people in a particular confrontation with divine signs.

The warning should not be applied to every religious disagreement without examining awareness and conduct.

Saḥt itself does not mean fabrication.

Fabrication is the act prohibited; fa-yusḥitakum names the destructive consequence warned against.

4. Consuming wrongful wealth in Al-Māʾidah 5:42

سَمَّاعُونَ لِلْكَذِبِ أَكَّالُونَ لِلسُّحْتِ فَإِنْ جَاءُوكَ فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ أَوْ أَعْرِضْ عَنْهُمْ وَإِنْ تُعْرِضْ عَنْهُمْ فَلَنْ يَضُرُّوكَ شَيْئًا وَإِنْ حَكَمْتَ فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ بِالْقِسْطِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُقْسِطِينَ

sammāʿūna li-al-kadhibi akkālūna li-al-suḥti fa-in jāʾūka fa-uḥkum baynahum aw aʿriḍ ʿanhum wa-in tuʿriḍ ʿanhum fa-lan yaḍurrūka shayʾan wa-in ḥakamta fa-uḥkum baynahum bi-al-qisṭi inna Allāha yuḥibbu al-muqsiṭīn

Al-Māʾidah 5:42

Translation:

“Habitual listeners to falsehood, habitual consumers of wrongful wealth. If they come to you, judge between them or turn away from them. If you turn away, they will not harm you at all. If you judge, judge between them equitably. The God loves those who act equitably.”

Al-suḥt denotes wrongful wealth or benefit.

Akkālūn belongs to ʾ K L, eating.

Akkālūna li-al-suḥt is not confined to physically eating food.

In Qur’anic financial language, eating wealth can mean appropriating and benefiting from it.

The expression concerns people benefiting from wrongful wealth or income.

Does suḥt mean bribery?

Bribery is a clear instance.

Judgment follows the condemnation of listening to falsehood and consuming wrongful wealth.

This connection matters.

A judge who takes payment to change a ruling, overlook truth, or unjustly favour one side uses authority for wrongful gain.

Bribery can deprive people of their rights and favour whoever pays.

The example directly relates to the judicial context of Al-Māʾidah 5:42.

Suḥt's recorded meaning is nevertheless broader than bribery.

Wrongful wealth may also arise through deceit, betrayal, unjust appropriation, or abuse of entrusted authority.

Bribery can be a clear contextual example without exhausting the word's meaning.

Falsehood and wrongful wealth

The verse opens with two expressions:

سَمَّاعُونَ لِلْكَذِبِ

sammāʿūna li-al-kadhibi

Al-Māʾidah 5:42

Habitual listeners to falsehood.

أَكَّالُونَ لِلسُّحْتِ

akkālūna li-al-suḥti

Al-Māʾidah 5:42

Habitual consumers of wrongful wealth.

The first concerns falsehood.

The second concerns benefiting from wrongful wealth.

These behaviours can be connected.

Someone might accept or spread a lie for financial benefit.

A bribed judge might accept false testimony.

An official might overlook a truth relevant to a decision to protect personal interests.

Financial corruption then accompanies corruption in speech and judgment.

Suḥt itself does not mean lying.

Falsehood comes from kadhib.

Wrongful wealth comes from suḥt.

Their juxtaposition shows a social and ethical relationship, not identical meanings.

Equity in judgment

The verse tells Muhammad to judge equitably if he judges between them.

فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ بِالْقِسْطِ

fa-uḥkum baynahum bi-al-qisṭi

Al-Māʾidah 5:42

Then judge between them equitably.

This command contrasts with the opening description of falsehood and wrongful wealth.

Truth and people's rights should determine equitable judgment, rather than money, influence, or the wishes of the powerful.

Bribery can undermine that standard.

Suḥt is therefore not merely a private financial matter; it can affect others' rights and the administration of justice.

Whether a payment is legitimate depends on the transaction, the parties' rights, and their conduct.

Receiving wages, a gift, or a financial benefit is not by itself evidence of suḥt.

5. Al-suḥt in Al-Māʾidah 5:62 and Al-Māʾidah 5:63: corruption and learned responsibility

Hastening into sin, aggression, and wrongful consumption

وَتَرَىٰ كَثِيرًا مِنْهُمْ يُسَارِعُونَ فِي الْإِثْمِ وَالْعُدْوَانِ وَأَكْلِهِمُ السُّحْتَ لَبِئْسَ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

wa-tarā kathīran minhum yusāriʿūna fī al-ithmi wa-al-ʿudwāni wa-aklihim al-suḥta la-biʾsa mā kānū yaʿmalūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:62

Translation:

“You see many of them hastening into sin, aggression, and consuming wrongful wealth. How evil is what they have been doing.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:62

Al-suḥta is the object of the verbal noun akl.

Three expressions occur together:

الإِثْمِ

al-ithmi

Al-Māʾidah 5:62

Sin.

الْعُدْوَانِ

al-ʿudwāni

Al-Māʾidah 5:62

Aggression.

أَكْلِهِمُ السُّحْتَ

aklihim al-suḥta

Al-Māʾidah 5:62

Their consuming wrongful wealth.

Ithm concerns sin.

ʿUdwān concerns aggression and wrongful transgression of limits.

Akl al-suḥt concerns appropriating and benefiting from wrongful wealth.

They are connected, but should not all be reduced to the generic word sin.

The verse specifically names financial corruption alongside sin and aggression.

Is every member of a community accused?

The verse begins with kathīran minhum.

It means “many of them.”

It does not accuse every individual in that community without exception.

This qualification matters.

The condemnation concerns those hastening into these behaviours.

The verse also speaks of observing conduct.

Its social application should not accuse people solely on the basis of ethnicity, religion, profession, or status.

Conduct and evidence should guide judgment.

The responsibility of scholars and religious authorities

Al-Māʾidah 5:63 says:

لَوْلَا يَنْهَاهُمُ الرَّبَّانِيُّونَ وَالْأَحْبَارُ عَنْ قَوْلِهِمُ الْإِثْمَ وَأَكْلِهِمُ السُّحْتَ لَبِئْسَ مَا كَانُوا يَصْنَعُونَ

lawlā yanhāhumu al-rabbāniyyūna wa-al-aḥbāru ʿan qawlihimu al-ithma wa-aklihimu al-suḥta la-biʾsa mā kānū yaṣnaʿūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:63

Translation:

“Why do the godly teachers and scholars not restrain them from sinful speech and consuming wrongful wealth? How evil is what they have been doing.”

Al-suḥta again denotes wrongful wealth.

The verse's emphasis differs from the preceding one.

Al-Māʾidah 5:62 condemns people hastening into sin, aggression, and wrongful consumption.

Al-Māʾidah 5:63 asks why learned religious authorities fail to restrain them.

The Qur’an addresses both direct perpetrators and those with knowledge and influence.

Perpetrators and those who fail to restrain them

Someone who wrongfully takes another's wealth is responsible for that act.

A separate issue arises when knowledgeable or influential people can appropriately confront evident corruption but deliberately abandon their responsibility.

Al-Māʾidah 5:63 expresses this responsibility through yanhāhum.

The verb concerns forbidding and restraining wrongful conduct.

The verse specifically criticizes religious and learned authorities for failing to act against sinful speech and wrongful consumption.

This invites reflection on intellectual and social responsibility in other communities too.

If an official knowingly conceals corruption for personal gain, group loyalty, or fear of losing status, their conscious silence or complicity also warrants examination.

This does not make every scholar or official responsible for all corruption worldwide.

Knowledge, authority, capacity to act, and actual responsibility matter.

Yaʿmalūn and yaṣnaʿūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:62 ends:

لَبِئْسَ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

la-biʾsa mā kānū yaʿmalūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:62

How evil is what they have been doing.

Al-Māʾidah 5:63 ends:

لَبِئْسَ مَا كَانُوا يَصْنَعُونَ

la-biʾsa mā kānū yaṣnaʿūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:63

How evil is what they have been doing.

The wording differs noticeably.

The first uses ʿamal, the second ṣunʿ.

Ṣunʿ can in some uses describe work involving deliberate arrangement or skill.

The difference alone does not establish that every learned authority in the second verse devised an organized corruption scheme.

The context condemns corrupt conduct and the authorities' failure to restrain it.

The two groups' responsibilities differ without changing the meaning of suḥt.

6. Why is wrongful wealth called suḥt?

The relationship between destruction and wrongful wealth is a significant lexical question.

Arabic sources offer several explanations.

Loss of blessing and reputation

One explanation says wrongful wealth removes blessing and damages its possessor's reputation and integrity.

This connects with the field of destruction.

Someone gaining money through betrayal or bribery may become outwardly richer while losing trust and moral standing.

Financial corruption can also destroy rights and harm society.

A semantic connection between destruction and wrongful wealth can thus be explained.

It is not an empirical law that everyone receiving wrongful wealth must shortly become poor.

Corrupt people may acquire considerable wealth and retain it for years.

The wrongfulness of its acquisition is not determined by the amount possessed.

Greed and insatiability

Another lexical explanation associates suḥt with intense, insatiable appetite.

Masḥūt al-maʿidah, describing an excessive eater, appears in this explanation.

Someone repeatedly taking wrongful wealth is compared with a person never satisfied by food.

The image can explain greed in wrongful acquisition.

It does not establish that every recipient of wrongful wealth is psychologically insatiable.

Greed may contribute to many instances of corruption, but suḥt in Al-Māʾidah denotes wrongful wealth.

Is all unlawful wealth suḥt?

The broad lexical sense includes unlawful earnings and tainted benefit.

Qur’anic study should distinguish that general definition from the passages' particular situations.

Judgment and equity in Al-Māʾidah 5:42 foreground bribery and gain through unjustly changing a ruling.

Al-Māʾidah 5:62 and Al-Māʾidah 5:63 place wrongful wealth beside sin, aggression, and learned responsibility.

The passages concern acquiring and benefiting from illegitimate wealth.

Applying suḥt to a particular transaction requires establishing the wrongfulness of its acquisition or appropriation.

Cultural dislike or a group's disapproval alone does not establish divine prohibition.

Disagreement over a transaction does not justify accusing another party without examining rights and evidence.

Suḥt names wealth whose acquisition or appropriation is wrongful; the word does not independently provide a list of every prohibited transaction or income.

7. Distinctions from nearby roots

S Ḥ T: saḥt, and M Ḥ Q: maḥq

Maḥq concerns diminution, effacement, and destruction.

Saḥt likewise concerns destruction and uprooting in its principal branch.

Al-Baqarah 2:276 says yamḥaqu Allāhu al-ribā.

Ṭā Hā 20:61 has fa-yusḥitakum bi-ʿadhāb.

They overlap in the field of destruction.

Their objects differ.

Al-Baqarah concerns the effacement of usury.

Ṭā Hā concerns the addressees' destruction through punishment.

This does not establish that maḥq is always gradual and saḥt always sudden.

The manner requires contextual examination.

S Ḥ T: saḥt, and H L K: halāk

Halāk concerns destruction and perishing.

Saḥt can convey a similar sense.

Ṭā Hā 20:61 warns of destruction through punishment.

This is close to perishing.

Saḥt also has Arabic uses concerning scraping and stripping away.

The Qur’anic noun suḥt has a financial use.

Not every form should therefore be translated merely as destruction.

S Ḥ T: suḥt, and Ḥ R M: ḥarām

Ḥarām concerns prohibition or inviolability in the Qur’an.

Financial suḥt concerns wrongful gain and benefit.

The concepts are connected but not identical in translation.

Ḥarām can apply to food, conduct, time, place, and other matters; sacred times and places concern inviolability rather than wrongful wealth.

Suḥt in Al-Māʾidah concerns wealth and wrongful benefit.

“Wrongful wealth” is clearer here than a generic “forbidden thing.”

This does not separate suḥt from prohibition.

S Ḥ T: suḥt, and ʾ TH M: ithm

Ithm concerns sin.

Suḥt in Al-Māʾidah concerns wrongful wealth.

Both occur in Al-Māʾidah 5:62.

The conjunction shows financial wrongdoing's relationship to sin.

Ithm is broader; suḥt specifies a field of wrongful acquisition and benefit.

Translating both as sin erases the distinction.

S Ḥ T: suḥt, and ʾ K L: akl

Akl ordinarily means eating.

In Qur’anic financial usage it can express appropriation and benefit.

Al-Māʾidah 5:42 uses akkālūn.

Al-Māʾidah 5:62 and Al-Māʾidah 5:63 use aklihim.

All three concern consuming suḥt.

Akl expresses consumption or benefit; suḥt identifies the wealth's wrongful nature.

The words should remain distinct.

S Ḥ T: saḥt, and S Ḥ R: siḥr

The roots share their first letters.

The final letter differs.

S Ḥ T concerns destruction and, financially, wrongful gain.

Qur’anic S Ḥ R includes sorcery or magic.

In Ṭā Hā 20:61, Moses warns the magicians against falsely attributing things to God with fa-yusḥitakum.

Magic-related words also occur in this narrative.

A shared context does not make the roots identical.

Siḥr concerns the activity under discussion; yusḥitakum concerns the destructive consequence warned against.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Principal meanings

Saḥata: removed, stripped away, or uprooted.

Saḥt: removal, uprooting, or destruction.

Asḥata: destroyed, eliminated, or uprooted.

Isḥāt: destruction or uprooting.

Suḥt: wrongful, tainted wealth or income.

Masḥūt: removed or destroyed.

Musḥat: destroyed or lost.

Proposed translations of the Qur’anic forms

In Ṭā Hā 20:61:

فَيُسْحِتَكُمْ بِعَذَابٍ

fa-yusḥitakum bi-ʿadhāb

Ṭā Hā 20:61

“Lest He uproot you through punishment.”

“Uproot” preserves the force of destruction without adding physical details of punishment.

For al-suḥt in Al-Māʾidah 5:42, Al-Māʾidah 5:62, and Al-Māʾidah 5:63, “wrongful wealth” is suitable.

This is a proposal for the root study; existing installed verse translations remain unchanged.

Bribery and corrupt judicial gain can be explained contextually, without restricting suḥt to bribery everywhere.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meanings

For the brief root box:

“Destruction and uprooting; in financial usage, wrongful wealth and income.”

For Ṭā Hā 20:61:

9. Conclusion

Arabic S Ḥ T concerns stripping away, uprooting, and destruction.

Material uses include scraping fat from meat, shaving closely, and stripping a surface.

Of wealth, it can describe destruction and loss.

Al-Farazdaq's musḥat describes wealth destroyed by the ravages of time, contrasted with mujallaf, damaged wealth of which something survives.

That evidence is Umayyad. The examples examined do not securely date an independent poetic witness to the pre-Islamic period.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an.

Ṭā Hā 20:61 uses fa-yusḥitakum in Moses's warning against fabricating lies about God.

Destruction and uprooting are clear there.

Al-suḥt in Al-Māʾidah 5:42 denotes wrongful wealth beside listening to falsehood and questions of judgment.

Bribery is a clear instance, not the entire meaning.

Al-Māʾidah 5:62 places its consumption alongside sin and aggression.

Al-Māʾidah 5:63 criticizes learned religious authorities for failing to restrain sinful speech and wrongful consumption.

Financial corruption is thus more than personal wealth: it can involve falsehood, violated rights, corrupted judgment, and failed responsibility.

Lexical explanations connect the name suḥt with loss of blessing, reputation, and integrity.

This does not mean wrongful wealth must materially disappear or every recipient become poor at a specified time.

Nor does a group's disapproval alone make income or a transaction suḥt; wrongfulness requires independent grounds.

The principal branch of S Ḥ T concerns removal, potentially reaching destruction or uprooting. Financial suḥt denotes wealth or benefit acquired wrongfully.

For Forqan, “uproot” suits fa-yusḥitakum in Ṭā Hā 20:61, and “wrongful wealth” suits al-suḥt in Al-Māʾidah.

Destruction belongs to the verbal branch; wrongful gain is an established nominal use in finance and ethics. Their relationship can be explained without transferring the whole meaning of either to the other.
