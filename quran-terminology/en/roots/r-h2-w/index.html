Becoming calm, moving smoothly, openness, and remaining in a particular condition

Brief root meaning: Calm and stillness; gentle, smooth, untroubled movement; and, in another branch, openness and spaciousness of a place, including an open space or depression between elevations. In its Qur’anic usage, رَهْوًا describes the sea’s condition; one contextual reading understands the command as leaving it as it was after the passage opened.

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic form

The three-letter Arabic root ر ه و encompasses meanings connected with calm, ease, smooth movement, and openness.

One established use of رَهَا الشَّيْءُ means that something became calm or still. رَهْو can likewise express stillness, calm, or gentle, untroubled movement.

Early Arabic also uses رَهْو for the movement of horses and camels: movement that can be continuous and smooth, without violent, irregular jolts.

رَهْو and رَهْوَة also name certain landforms. Lexicons apply them both to open depressions between elevations and to some elevated places.

The family is therefore not limited to stillness and absence of movement.

Some uses emphasize a calm condition, others ease and smoothness of movement; spatial uses concern openness or a place’s characteristics.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Dukhān, in The God’s command to Moses about leaving the sea in a particular condition.

The context matters because other Qur’anic passages describe a passage opening through the sea, the waters separating, and the Children of Israel crossing.

رَهْوًا should consequently be considered in relation to the sea after the passage opened, rather than solely as an ordinarily calm sea.

The form examined and its Qur’anic occurrence

رَهْوًا — Al-Dukhān 44:24

This is an indefinite accusative noun or verbal noun describing the sea’s condition in the sentence.

Its lexical senses include calm and stillness. In the context of Moses and the Children of Israel crossing, the passage remaining open also helps explain the condition described.

Other forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • رَهَا يَرْهُو: Became calm or still, ceased moving or being disturbed; in another use, moved gently and smoothly.
  • رَهْو: Calm, stillness, smooth movement; spatially, an open place or a location characterized by elevation or depression.
  • رَاهٍ: Calm, at ease, or enjoying an easy, untroubled condition.
  • أَرْهَى: Left calm, allowed time, treated gently, or made something available to another.
  • إِرْهَاء: Calming something or leaving it undisturbed.
  • مِرْهَاء: An epithet for a horse or animal moving smoothly.
  • رَهْوَة: An open place, a depression, or, in some uses, a height or rise.
  • رِهَاء: A plural associated with spatial uses of the family; its sense follows the relevant use.
  • رَهَاء: Open, spacious, level ground.
  • رَهْو: In another branch, the name of a bird sometimes identified or compared with a crane.

These forms help establish the family’s range, but only رَهْوًا occurs in the Qur’an.

2. The root’s semantic branches in early Arabic

Two principal branches in an early lexical analysis

An early analysis identifies two semantic principles for ر ه و:

الرَّاءُ وَالْهَاءُ وَالْحَرْفُ الْمُعْتَلُّ أَصْلَانِ، يَدُلُّ أَحَدُهُمَا عَلَى دَعَةٍ وَخَفْضٍ وَسُكُونٍ، وَالْآخَرُ عَلَى مَكَانٍ قَدْ يَنْخَفِضُ وَيَرْتَفِعُ.

al-rāʾu wa-al-hāʾu wa-al-ḥarfu al-muʿtallu aṣlāni yadullu aḥaduhumā ʿalā daʿatin wa-khafḍin wa-sukūnin wa-al-ākharu ʿalā makānin qad yankhafiḍu wa-yartafiʿu

Translation: “Rāʾ, hāʾ, and the weak letter have two principles: one indicates ease, comfort, and stillness; the other, a place that may be low or high.”

This division is useful for studying the root.

The first branch emphasizes calm conditions and untroubled movement.

The second concerns the nature of a place and the shape of the ground.

The branches can approach one another in some uses. An open, level place, for example, can allow easy movement.

That relationship alone does not prove that calm developed from spatial openness, or vice versa.

Both branches should therefore be preserved without inventing a definite historical origin for every usage.

رَهَا الشَّيْءُ: something becomes calm

An early lexical explanation states:

رَهَا الشَّيْءُ رَهْوًا: سَكَنَ.

rahā al-shayʾu rahwan sakana

Translation: “The thing became calm or still.”

Another recorded explanation says:

وَكُلُّ سَاكِنٍ لَا يَتَحَرَّكُ رَاهٍ وَرَهْوٌ.

wa-kullu sākinin lā yataḥarraku rāhin wa-rahwun

Meaning: “Anything still and unmoving can be called rāhin or rahw.”

This demonstrates the root’s clear connection with stillness.

For example, Arabic records رَهَا الْبَحْرُ for the sea becoming calm.

Such usage describes the sea in terms of calm and reduced disturbance.

It does not follow that every use of رَهْو necessarily means the absolute absence of all physical movement.

A sea called calm in ordinary language can still contain currents.

An animal whose movement is described as رَهْو can likewise be advancing.

Stillness must therefore be understood relative to what has become calm or stable in each use.

One case emphasizes reduced movement, another the absence of disturbance and violent jolting.

أَرْهِ عَلَى نَفْسِكَ: be gentle with yourself

Arabic records this expression:

أَرْهِ عَلَى نَفْسِكَ.

arhi ʿalā nafsika

Translation: “Be gentle with yourself; do not be hard on yourself.”

Here إِرْهَاء concerns calm and gentle treatment.

Someone acting with haste, pressure, and agitation may need to ease their manner.

Lexical explanations accordingly use ارْفُقْ بِنَفْسِكَ, “be gentle with yourself,” to explain it.

This closeness does not make ر ه و and ر ف ق the same root.

رِفْق has its own word family and concerns gentleness and consideration in conduct.

رَهْو overlaps with it here but also concerns stillness, smooth movement, and spatial conditions elsewhere.

The two words should therefore be examined independently.

عَيْشٌ رَاهٍ: an easy, comfortable life

عَيْشٌ رَاهٍ describes a calm, comfortable, prosperous life.

Here the field of calm and ease concerns a person’s living conditions.

A peaceful life can involve security, comfort, and freedom from turmoil.

The adjective does not by itself establish unlimited wealth or the absence of every difficulty.

Someone can have abundant resources yet live in turmoil.

Another person may have limited means but enjoy relative calm and stability.

Comfort and prosperity are close in this usage without being wholly identical concepts.

Lexicons also record خِمْسٌ رَاهٍ for an easy watering journey or interval in the management of camels; ease is supplied by رَاهٍ, while the watering interval belongs to خِمْس.

This relates to travel and animal movement in the early Arabian environment.

Gentle, smooth movement

Lexicons record:

رَهَا فِي السَّيْرِ يَرْهُو، إِذَا رَفَقَ.

rahā fī al-sayri yarhū idhā rafaqa

Meaning: “He proceeded gently and easily in his journey.”

In describing horses and camels, رَهْو can indicate smooth, continuous, untroubled movement.

One early explanation glosses the movement sense as السَّيْرُ السَّهْلُ, “easy, smooth travel.”

The animal is not still or motionless in this usage.

It is moving, but the description concerns the movement’s gentleness and coordination.

This illustrates that calm movement is not the absence of movement.

Ordinary language likewise allows someone to walk calmly and without agitation, without implying that they stand still.

Is رَهْو also used for fast movement?

Early sources sometimes apply رَهْو to the rapid movement of horses and birds.

An early lexical explanation records this usage for fast horses and birds.

Another explanation of مِرْهَاء brings speed and calm together:

سُرْعَةٌ فِي سُكُونٍ مِنْ غَيْرِ قَلَقٍ.

surʿatun fī sukūnin min ghayri qalaqin

Translation: “Speed with steadiness, without agitation.”

In some uses, the principal consideration is therefore not speed alone.

Movement may be fast yet coordinated and free of irregular jolts.

Recorded fast and calm uses do not require assuming that رَهْو always carries two utterly contradictory meanings.

Some examples emphasize the manner and continuity of movement, while speed is also present in other contexts.

This should not become an absolute rule that رَهْو always means fast but smooth movement.

The Qur’anic occurrence does not describe a horse or camel’s motion; its appropriate meaning must be established from the sea context.

رَهْو and رَهْوَة: openness and the character of the ground

Spatial uses apply رَهْو and رَهْوَة to places with differing characteristics.

Some sources use رَهْوَة for a depression where water collects.

Another explanation identifies it as an open, level place.

An early explanation describes a hollow or open space toward which rainwater flows.

Another records a low place surrounded by higher ground.

The same word is also recorded for certain heights and rises.

Lexicographers accordingly include it among words that can refer to high or low places in different uses.

An invented unified definition should not conceal this variation.

The two spatial uses are best kept clear: a depression or open space, and, in some uses, elevated or rising ground.

رَهَاء: open, level ground

رَهَاء denotes open, level ground, often described as spacious.

Some descriptions associate its broad, level expanse with mirage.

This clearly relates to openness.

Large natural barriers or severe unevenness may be less prominent there, making the route appear more open.

The word alone does not establish that all open ground is dry, sandy, or waterless.

Other Arabic uses apply رَهْو to a place where water gathers.

Spatial openness and dryness are therefore different features.

That distinction matters for وَاتْرُكِ الْبَحْرَ رَهْوًا: an open, level passage should not be equated without evidence with the absolute absence of water.

رَهْو: an opening between two things

Lexicons transmit an expression about a camel with a conspicuous gap between its two humps.

The speaker uses رَهْو for the open space between those elevations.

The example shows that رَهْو can denote an open space or interval between two sides.

The subject is neither stillness nor movement.

It is the opening and distance between two parts.

This branch matters for one Qur’anic interpretation of رَهْوًا, since Moses’ story describes the waters separating and a passage appearing between them.

That lexical sense alone cannot supply every detail of the Qur’anic event.

The surrounding verses must guide interpretation.

رَهْو: a bird’s name

Lexicons also record رَهْو as the name of a bird.

Some identify it with كُرْكِيّ, commonly rendered “crane.”

Others describe a similar bird rather than the identical kind.

That disagreement makes a precise identification from the early accounts uncertain.

The bird’s name does not establish that every meaning of the root developed from its calm movement or spread wings.

In the Qur’an, رَهْوًا does not name a bird.

This branch belongs in the language’s history but should not be imported into Al-Dukhān’s verse.

3. Evidence from pre-Islamic and early Arabic poetry

Smooth movement in al-Quṭāmī’s verse

A verse attributed to al-Quṭāmī al-Taghlibī says:

يَمْشِينَ رَهْوًا فَلَا الْأَعْجَازُ خَاذِلَةٌ
وَلَا الصُّدُورُ عَلَى الْأَعْجَازِ تَتَّكِلُ

yamshīna rahwan fa-lā al-aʿjāzu khādhilatun / wa-lā al-ṣudūru ʿalā al-aʿjāzi tattakilu

Translation: “They walk smoothly: neither do their hindquarters fail them, nor do their forequarters depend upon their hindquarters.”

The poet describes camels moving.

رَهْوًا concerns their manner of walking.

They are moving, but their gait is described as coordinated and smooth, without weakness or agitation.

It therefore does not mean standing still or absence of movement here.

“Smooth movement” is a more suitable rendering than “motionlessness.”

Al-Quṭāmī was an Umayyad-period poet; this is evidence for early Arabic after the Qur’an’s revelation, not pre-Islamic verse.

Some transmitted commentaries attribute the line to al-Aʿshā, while it is recorded as al-Quṭāmī’s verse in his poetic tradition and early literary accounts; the latter attribution is followed here.

The attribution difference should remain visible when using the example.

Continuous movement in Labīd ibn Rabīʿah’s verse

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

يُرَيْنَ عَصَائِبًا يَرْكُضْنَ رَهْوًا
سَوَابِقُهُنَّ كَالرَّجْلِ الْقِيَامِ

yurayna ʿaṣāʾiban yarkuḍna rahwan / sawābiquhunna ka-al-rajli al-qiyāmi

Approximate translation: “They are seen in bands, running in succession; their foremost are like men standing.” يُرَيْنَ is passive, “they are seen,” rather than “they show.”

The line places رَهْو beside يَرْكُضْنَ.

رَكْض here concerns swift, hurried movement.

Thus, رَهْو can occur in the description of movement that is not slow.

In a lexical explanation, رَهْوًا means following one another. The second hemistich is also transmitted as سَوَابِقُهُنَّ كَالْحِدَإِ التُّؤَامِ, “their foremost like paired kites.” The variants should be distinguished rather than translated as one text.

This is significant.

If رَهْو meant only motionlessness, its use alongside يَرْكُضْنَ would be difficult to understand.

Recognizing its field of smooth, coordinated, continuous movement makes the verse clearer.

Labīd lived both before and after Islam’s emergence. The exact date of this passage is not established here, so it should not be presented as certainly preceding the Qur’an without evidence.

رَهْوَة in ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s verse

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm includes:

نَصَبْنَا مِثْلَ رَهْوَةَ ذَاتَ حَدٍّ
مُحَافَظَةً وَكُنَّا السَّابِقِينَا

naṣabnā mithla rahwata dhāta ḥaddin / muḥāfaẓatan wa-kunnā al-sābiqīnā

Translation: “We arrayed a battle force like Rahwah, armed with might, in defense; and we were the first.”

Early explanations associate رَهْوَة here with a height or mountain.

The poet uses the image for the battle force’s greatness and strength.

Explanations differ over whether رَهْوَة is a particular mountain’s name or a general use of the word.

An early lexical objection notes that, if it is a specific mountain’s name, this verse alone cannot establish its general application to every height.

The naming may nevertheless relate to elevation.

The example helps explain the spatial branch, but the distinction between proper name and common meaning should remain clear.

Openness and elevation in Arabic verse

In verse attributed to Dhū al-Rummah, a bird on an elevated place is described:

نَظَرْتُ كَمَا جَلَّى عَلَى رَأْسِ رَهْوَةٍ
مِنَ الطَّيْرِ أَقْنَى يَنْفُضُ الطَّلَّ أَزْرَقُ

naẓartu ka-mā jallā ʿalā raʾsi rahwatin / mina al-ṭayri aqnā yanfuḍu al-ṭalla azraqu

Approximate translation: “I looked as a hooked-beaked, bluish bird looks out from the top of a rise, shaking off the dew.”

رَهْوَة occurs with رَأْس, “top,” producing an image of an elevated place.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad-period poet; this is evidence for early Arabic after the Qur’an’s revelation.

Its importance lies in supporting the spatial usage.

It does not establish that Qur’anic رَهْو necessarily means a mountain or hill.

In Al-Dukhān 44:24, the word concerns the sea and must be understood in that context.

What the poetic evidence establishes

Early poetry demonstrates that ر ه و has more than one use.

Al-Quṭāmī’s verse emphasizes smooth movement.

Labīd’s verse illustrates continuous, coordinated movement.

The verses attributed to ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm and Dhū al-Rummah use رَهْوَة in images of elevated places.

Two important branches are consequently attested in actual usage: calm and quality of movement, and spatial characteristics.

The examples do not alone settle the precise interpretation of رَهْوًا in Al-Dukhān 44:24.

The sentence’s construction and the related passages of Moses’ story must also be examined.

4. The Qur’anic usage: وَاتْرُكِ الْبَحْرَ رَهْوًا

Al-Dukhān 44:23

Verse text:

فَأَسْرِ بِعِبَادِي لَيْلًا إِنَّكُمْ مُتَّبَعُونَ

fa-asri bi-ʿibādī laylan innakum muttabaʿūna

Al-Dukhān 44:23

Translation:

“Then take My servants by night; you will be pursued.”

Al-Dukhān 44:24

Verse text:

وَاتْرُكِ الْبَحْرَ رَهْوًا ۖ إِنَّهُمْ جُنْدٌ مُغْرَقُونَ

wa-utruk al-baḥra rahwan innahum jundun mughraqūna

Al-Dukhān 44:24

Translation:

“And leave the sea calm; they are an army to be drowned.”

These verses continue the story of Moses and Pharaoh.

In Al-Dukhān 44:22, Moses appeals to his Lord about Pharaoh’s people’s wrongdoing.

The command then comes to take The God’s servants by night.

The following verse gives the command concerning the sea.

رَهْوًا thus occurs in the setting of the Children of Israel’s departure and deliverance, Pharaoh’s pursuit, and his army’s outcome.

The construction وَاتْرُكِ الْبَحْرَ رَهْوًا

وَاتْرُكِ is an imperative from ت ر ك preceded by the conjunction wa-.

Moses is the addressee.

الْبَحْرَ is the verb’s object.

رَهْوًا is accusative and describes the sea’s condition when it is left.

A familiar grammatical analysis treats it as a verbal noun serving as a circumstantial expression: “while calm,” or “in that calm, established condition.”

Another analysis takes تَرَكَ as leaving or placing something in a condition, with رَهْوًا as its second complement.

This grammatical difference does not transform the clause’s basic sense.

On either analysis, the command is to leave the sea in the intended condition.

Lexically, اُتْرُكْ itself concerns leaving or letting be.

رَهْوًا specifies the quality or condition of what is to be left.

The sentence therefore distinguishes the act of leaving from the condition in which the sea is left.

Does رَهْو describe a calm sea?

Early lexicons record رَهَا الْبَحْرُ for the sea becoming calm.

Accordingly, many early explanations gloss رَهْوًا in Al-Dukhān 44:24 as سَاكِنًا, “still.”

The sense has clear linguistic support.

An important question nevertheless remains:

What was the condition of the sea that was to be left calm at that moment?

Read apart from the story, the phrase might suggest an ordinarily calm sea.

Other Qur’anic passages, however, describe the sea opening and a route appearing for the Children of Israel to cross.

Calm here should not be equated without evidence with returning to the sea’s ordinary condition before its parting.

Ṭā Hā 20:77: a dry passage through the sea

Verse text:

وَلَقَدْ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ أَنْ أَسْرِ بِعِبَادِي فَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ طَرِيقًا فِي الْبَحْرِ يَبَسًا لَا تَخَافُ دَرَكًا وَلَا تَخْشَىٰ

wa-laqad awḥaynā ilā Mūsā an asri bi-ʿibādī fa-iḍrib lahum ṭarīqan fī al-baḥri yabasan lā takhāfu darakan wa-lā takhshā

Ṭā Hā 20:77

Translation:

“We indeed revealed to Moses: Take My servants by night and make for them a dry passage through the sea, fearing neither pursuit nor anything else.”

This verse describes a passage being made through the sea.

طَرِيقًا names the passage.

فِي الْبَحْرِ locates it within the sea.

يَبَسًا describes the passage as dry.

The Qur’an thus describes a passage through the sea that could be crossed.

This helps explain رَهْوًا by clarifying the sea’s condition during Moses and the Children of Israel’s crossing.

But يَبَسًا belongs to ي ب س; its lexical meaning should not be made the invariant meaning of رَهْو.

Dryness characterizes the passage in Ṭā Hā 20:77.

رَهْوًا in Al-Dukhān 44:24 can relate to leaving that condition in place, but the two words differ in root and basic meaning.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63: the sea parts

Verse text:

فَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ أَنِ اضْرِبْ بِعَصَاكَ الْبَحْرَ فَانْفَلَقَ فَكَانَ كُلُّ فِرْقٍ كَالطَّوْدِ الْعَظِيمِ

fa-awḥaynā ilā Mūsā ani iḍrib bi-ʿaṣāka al-baḥra fa-infalaqa fa-kāna kullu firqin ka-al-ṭawdi al-ʿaẓīmi

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63

Translation:

“Then We revealed to Moses: Strike the sea with your staff. It parted, and each part was like a mighty mountain.”

فَانْفَلَقَ describes the sea’s parting.

كُلُّ فِرْقٍ then identifies the separated portions of water.

كَالطَّوْدِ الْعَظِيمِ compares them to a mighty mountain.

The image is of water separating and a gap appearing between its parts.

This also connects with the spatial branch of رَهْو as openness and open space.

The word رَهْو alone does not determine the number of water sections, the passage’s geometry, or the water’s height.

Such details should remain within what the verses state, without additions.

رَهْو: calm or openness?

Early lexical and interpretive sources offer several explanations of رَهْوًا in Al-Dukhān 44:24.

Some give سَاكِنًا: calm and still.

Others give مُنْفَرِجًا or مُفْتَرِقًا: open or separated.

Some explain it as طَرِيقًا: a passage.

Others mention يَبَسًا or سَهْلًا: dry or easy to traverse.

These explanations are not lexically identical.

“Calm” concerns movement and disturbance.

“Open” concerns a gap and an unobstructed route.

“Dry” concerns the absence of water on the path’s surface.

“Easy to traverse” concerns the ground or route’s accessibility and may suggest smoothness; it does not necessarily specify a completely level surface.

The four should therefore not be presented as exact synonyms.

They can nevertheless coincide in the setting of the crossing.

The sea has parted, a dry passage has appeared, and the water remains in a condition that leaves the route open.

A contextual synthesis can therefore understand رَهْوًا as leaving the sea’s opened condition undisturbed. This combines the narrative evidence; it does not erase the distinction between the lexical readings “calm” and “open.”

Does “calm” mean the waters have returned?

If the sea had already returned to normal after the Children of Israel crossed, with the waters rejoining, that would differ from the interpretation of an open passage remaining in place.

Al-Dukhān 44:24 commands leaving the sea رَهْوًا, then states that the pursuing army will be drowned.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:64 describes the other group being brought near that place.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:65 states the deliverance of Moses and his companions.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:66 states that the other group was drowned.

The passage, pursuit, and drowning belong to a connected sequence.

رَهْوًا should therefore not be made to mean immediate closure of the route after Moses’ departure without evidence.

Conversely, the verse does not explain the precise manner and timing of the waters’ return.

We should retain what the Qur’an states: a passage opened in the sea, Moses and his companions were saved, and the pursuing army was drowned.

Did Moses intend to close the sea again?

Some early interpretive reports say that, after crossing, Moses intended to strike the sea again with his staff, restoring the waters to their previous condition to prevent Pharaoh’s pursuit.

On that account, he then received the command to leave the sea رَهْوًا.

The report offers one explanation of the sequence of events.

But Al-Dukhān 44:24 itself does not say that Moses intended to strike the sea again.

The verse does not mention such a decision.

It therefore should not be presented as a definite statement of the Qur’anic narrative itself.

What the verse clearly gives is the command to leave the sea in the described condition.

Its ending also says:

إِنَّهُمْ جُنْدٌ مُغْرَقُونَ

Meaning that the pursuing soldiers will be drowned.

This describes their outcome, without explaining every detail of Moses’ decisions and actions.

إِنَّهُمْ جُنْدٌ مُغْرَقُونَ: the pursuers’ outcome

إِنَّهُمْ refers to the group pursuing Moses and his companions.

The preceding verse says:

إِنَّكُمْ مُتَّبَعُونَ

Meaning: “You will be pursued,” addressing Moses and his companions.

جُنْدٌ مُغْرَقُونَ in Al-Dukhān 44:24 consequently concerns the pursuing army.

مُغْرَقُونَ is a passive participle from غ ر ق and indicates that they will be drowned.

It does not belong to ر ه و.

رَهْوًا itself therefore should not be assigned the meaning “drowning.”

رَهْو describes the sea’s condition in the command to Moses.

مُغْرَقُونَ identifies the pursuing army’s outcome.

The concepts are connected within the story but are not lexically identical.

The sea’s calm and the army’s drowning

At first, a calm sea and a drowning army might appear to be different images.

If رَهْوًا is related to the opened condition remaining in place, their connection becomes clearer.

The sea is in a condition that has allowed a passage to appear.

The command is to leave that condition in place.

The pursuing soldiers meet the fate expressed by مُغْرَقُونَ.

The connection does not yield a precise physical mechanism, such as water flow rates, the timing of collapsing walls of water, or how quickly the passage fills.

The verse supplies none of those details.

Nor must one adopt a theory of tides, wind, or earthquakes and then alter the meaning of رَهْو to fit it.

Conversely, understanding the narrative as miraculous does not license disregarding its linguistic and concrete descriptions.

The Qur’an itself speaks of an opening passage, a dry route, separated waters, and the army’s drowning.

Those are the primary elements for understanding the context.

5. Differences from related roots

ر ه و (rahw) and س ك ن (sukūn)

سُكُون concerns becoming calm and movement subsiding.

One branch of رَهْو belongs to the same field.

Lexicons explain رَهَا الْبَحْرُ through سَكَنَ الْبَحْرُ, the sea becoming still.

Other uses of رَهْو concern smooth movement, ease, and spatial openness.

The entire family cannot therefore be restricted to stillness.

In Al-Dukhān 44:24, calm is lexically defensible, while the parting narrative points to more than ordinarily quiet water.

ر ه و (rahw) and ر ك د (rukūd)

رُكُود concerns settling and ceasing to move or advance.

In Al-Shūrā 42:33, رَوَاكِدَ describes ships that stop when the wind becomes still.

رَهْو can also concern stillness, but it has a branch of smooth, continuous movement.

A stationary ship and an animal moving رَهْوًا are therefore not in the same condition.

One has stopped advancing; the other may be moving gently.

Likewise, رَهْوًا in Al-Dukhān 44:24 should not be restricted to the sea permanently ceasing all physical movement.

ر ه و (rahw) and ر ف ق (rifq)

رِفْق concerns gentleness, consideration, and companionship.

Early Arabic explains أَرْهِ عَلَى نَفْسِكَ with ارْفُقْ بِهَا, “be gentle with yourself.”

The families therefore approach one another in gentle conduct.

Qur’anic forms of ر ف ق also concern companionship, the elbow, means of comfort, and a resting place.

The Qur’anic occurrence of ر ه و, by contrast, describes the sea.

Their overlap in gentleness does not make them one root.

ر ه و (rahw) and ف ل ق (falq)

فَلْق concerns splitting and separating.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63, فَانْفَلَقَ describes the sea’s parting.

رَهْو in Al-Dukhān 44:24 concerns the condition in which it is to be left.

Within Moses’ story, the words thus address two connected aspects.

فَلْق names the event of parting.

On the contextual reading, رَهْو describes the opened condition remaining undisturbed.

رَهْوًا should therefore not be translated “split it again”; the verse does not command another parting.

Nor should فَلْق and رَهْو be treated as two names for the same act.

ر ه و (rahw) and ف ر ق (farq)

فَرْق concerns separation and distinction between things.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63, كُلُّ فِرْقٍ denotes the separated portions of water.

Al-Baqarah 2:50 also describes the sea being parted in the Children of Israel’s story.

These passages help explain its condition in Al-Dukhān 44:24.

فَرْق emphasizes the separation of parts, while رَهْو here relates to the calm or open condition that is to be left in place.

The roots’ difference should therefore be retained.

ر ه و (rahw) and ي ب س (yubs)

يُبْس concerns dryness.

Ṭā Hā 20:77 describes the passage through the sea as يَبَسًا, “dry.”

رَهْو belongs to the fields of calm and openness.

Both features can occur in one scene.

A route may be open and its surface dry.

Openness and dryness are nevertheless different lexical meanings.

رَهْوًا alone does not establish that everything called rahw is necessarily dry.

A different Qur’anic word expresses the passage’s dryness in Moses’ story.

ر ه و (rahw) and ه و ن (hawn)

هَوْن concerns calm, gentleness, and unassuming ease.

Al-Furqān 25:63 describes people walking on the earth هَوْنًا.

Its calm, untroubled movement is close to some uses of رَهْو.

The roots remain independent in structure and semantic range.

Al-Furqān 25:63 describes human conduct in the context of the servants of the Entirely Merciful.

Al-Dukhān 44:24 concerns the sea’s condition.

رَهْوًا there cannot consequently be translated as moral humility or humble conduct.

6. Interpretive questions and translation proposals for Forqan

Is “calm” sufficient?

One proposed rendering of Al-Dukhān 44:24 is:

“And leave the sea calm; they are an army to be drowned.”

This matches a clear, recorded meaning of رَهْو.

Without the other passages, “calm” might suggest an ordinary sea whose waves have merely subsided.

The narrative, however, describes a passage opening and the waters separating.

The root study should make that condition clear.

“Calm” is defensible as a basic equivalent, while leaving the sea in its opened condition matters to a fuller understanding of the sentence.

Open or calm: which is more suitable?

Both lexical fields have evidence for رَهْو.

Calm is clearly established in early sources.

Openness and spaciousness are also recorded spatially and relate to the crossing narrative.

The lexical choice should be distinguished from its contextual explanation.

Recorded lexical readings: Calm or still; alternatively, open or separated. “Remaining established” explains the command’s context rather than replacing the distinction between these readings.

Contextual explanation: The sea is to be left as it was after the passage opened.

This distinction preserves the word’s lexical meaning while retaining the narrative setting.

If “open” is chosen in direct translation, that choice should be understood as resting on an attested lexical branch and narrative evidence.

If “calm” is chosen, it should not be equated with closing the passage or returning the sea to normal.

Does رَهْو mean drying the sea?

Some interpretive explanations gloss رَهْوًا with يَبَسًا, “dry.”

That explanation draws on the passages describing a dry route through the sea.

The root ر ه و does not generally mean drying something.

“Dry out the sea” is therefore not an appropriate direct translation of وَاتْرُكِ الْبَحْرَ رَهْوًا.

The verse’s verb is اُتْرُكْ, not جَفِّفْ or another verb meaning “dry.”

رَهْوًا describes the intended condition; it is not an independent command to create that condition.

The command to open a passage elsewhere should thus be distinguished from this command to leave the sea as described.

Proposed translation of the Qur’anic form

رَهْوًا — Al-Dukhān 44:24

Short equivalents to consider include:

“Calm,” “in that calm condition,” “open and undisturbed,” or, in contextual explanation, “with its passage remaining open.”

A proposed basic translation:

“And leave the sea calm; they are an army to be drowned.”

This is defensible on the lexical meaning of رَهْو.

The root article or a newly prepared word box can explain the context as follows:

“Leave the sea, after the passage has opened, in that calm and open condition.”

This does not mean that every word of the explanation is contained in رَهْوًا itself. It combines the word’s meaning with other passages of Moses’ story.

Proposal for a Forqan word box

Word: رَهْوًا

Root: ر ه و

Brief root meaning: Calm and stillness; gentle, smooth movement; spatially, openness and open space.

Meaning in this verse: Calm or open, according to the lexical reading; in the contextual synthesis, the sea is left in the opened, passable condition provided for the Children of Israel.

The two fields should remain separate.

The brief root meaning introduces the word family.

The verse meaning explains the sea’s particular condition in the narrative.

Bird names, horses’ movement, and elevated or depressed ground belong in the full study; their details need not be repeated in the short verse box. These proposals do not replace existing authored word-box explanations.

7. Synthesis and final definition

The Arabic root ر ه و encompasses calm, stillness, smooth movement, and openness.

In its first branch, رَهَا الشَّيْءُ describes something becoming calm.

عَيْشٌ رَاهٍ describes a calm, comfortable life.

أَرْهِ عَلَى نَفْسِكَ concerns gentleness and taking things easily with oneself.

In the movement branch, رَهْو can describe a smooth, continuous, coordinated gait.

This can be slow or, in some uses, fast, while being characterized by freedom from agitation and irregular jolting.

Spatially, رَهْو and رَهْوَة name depressions, open spaces, and, in some usages, heights and rises.

Early lexicons sometimes class these spatial uses as opposing meanings.

All branches therefore cannot be derived from a single historically certain meaning.

Arabic poetic evidence also demonstrates this range.

In al-Quṭāmī’s verse, رَهْو describes camels’ smooth gait.

Labīd’s verse emphasizes successive, coordinated movement.

In verse attributed to ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm and Dhū al-Rummah, رَهْوَة occurs in images of elevated places.

The Qur’an uses the root only once: رَهْوًا in Al-Dukhān 44:24.

The God commands Moses to leave the sea in a particular condition and then announces the pursuing army’s drowning.

Ṭā Hā 20:77 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63 describe the sea having parted and a passage appearing.

The calm reading should therefore be understood in relation to that opened condition remaining in place.

This contextual synthesis neither removes the stillness reading nor makes every physical detail of the event part of the word’s meaning.

Final definition of ر ه و:

The word family has two principal branches:

1. Calm, ease, and untroubled movement: Something becoming calm, moving gently and smoothly, or remaining undisturbed.

2. Openness and spatial characteristics: Open space, a depression between heights, level ground, and, in certain uses, an elevated place.

For the Qur’anic occurrence, calm and openness are both recorded interpretations. In the contextual reading adopted here, the command leaves the sea’s opened condition undisturbed; that explanation should not be made the word’s sole dictionary definition.

It should therefore not be restricted to absolute stillness of every part of the water, drying the entire sea, or immediate closure of the passage.

رَهْو itself does not mean drowning or destroying the army. The separate expression إِنَّهُمْ جُنْدٌ مُغْرَقُونَ states that outcome.

For Forqan’s research and translation, an appropriate brief meaning is:

“Calm and stillness; smooth, untroubled movement; and, spatially, openness and open space.”

This preserves the principal Arabic semantic fields without forcing every sense into an invented single image.

Al-Dukhān 44:24 is also clarified by distinguishing the passage opening through the sea, leaving the sea in that condition, and the pursuing army’s drowning.

The three matters belong to one story, but distinct Qur’anic words and constructions express them.

Understanding رَهْوًا accurately requires neither isolating it from context nor turning every narrative detail into its lexical meaning. It requires identifying the recorded senses and considering them alongside the passages about Moses and the Children of Israel crossing the sea.
