The back and end of something; turning away, governing affairs, and reflecting on discourse

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic triliteral root D B R begins with the meaning of the back or rear part of something. دُبُر contrasts with قُبُل: the latter denotes the front or facing side, and the former the back.

This contrast is not confined to the human body. A house, route, line, day, or period can also have a front and rear, or beginning and end. This spatial and sequential relationship underlies uses such as turning one’s back, moving backward, coming to an end, remaining at the end of a group, and considering an affair’s outcome.

Two other important branches of this family are tadbīr and tadabbur.

In established Arabic usage, tadbīr means arranging an affair with regard to its course and outcome. Tadabbur is careful reflection on speech or a subject that does not stop at its surface or beginning, but also examines the connections between its parts, implications, and consequences.

These branches are related in meaning but should not be equated. Someone may reflect on a subject without being responsible for administering it; someone may manage an affair by ordering and directing its course.

In the Qur’an, this family describes physical direction, retreat, turning away from guidance, the ending of night, eliminating the remainder of a group, divine governance, and reflection on verses.

These varied uses show that “back” and “after” are important to the root, but its derivatives should not all receive one literal equivalent. Morphology, the verb’s object, and the verse’s context determine the intended branch of meaning.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

D B R occurs 44 times in 44 Qur’anic verses, in eight morphological groups.

The main forms are:

دُبُر and أَدْبَار: the back, rear part, or what comes after something. With 18 occurrences, this is the most frequent Qur’anic group.

أَدْبَرَ: turned one’s back, turned away, or drew to an end. It occurs four times.

مُدْبِر: one turning one’s back, turning away, or departing. It occurs eight times.

دَابِر: the final part, last remainder, or what stands at the end of a group. It occurs four times in the Qur’an.

يُدَبِّرُ: an imperfect Form II verb meaning to govern or arrange affairs. It occurs four times.

يَتَدَبَّرُ and its related forms: a Form V verb meaning to reflect carefully. It occurs four times.

مُدَبِّرَات: the feminine plural active participle of Form II, in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:5.

إِدْبَار: the verbal noun of Form IV, in Al-Ṭūr 52:49.

The verbs دَبَّرَ and تَدَبَّرَ and the verbal nouns tadbīr and tadabbur are established in Arabic. These verbal nouns themselves, however, do not occur in those nominal forms in the Qur’an, which uses verbs of governing and reflecting.

Other forms recorded in Arabic include دَبِير, دُبُور, تَدَابُر, اسْتِدْبَار, and دَبْر. Some have specialized physical or technical uses, but should not be counted among Qur’anic derivatives without verification.

3. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Back in contrast to front

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris traces most uses of this root to one principle:

آخِرُ الشَّيْءِ وَخَلْفُهُ خِلَافُ قُبُلِهِ

ākhiru al-shayʾi wa-khalfuhu khilāfu qubulihi

That is, the end and back of something, in contrast to its front.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise identifies dubur as the opposite of qubul.

This definition expresses a spatial and sequential relationship. A person has a front and back; in movement, something can also be ahead or behind.

The same relationship can describe time. دُبُرُ الشَّهْر refers to the latter part of the month, and دُبُرُ النَّهَار to the end of the day.

This temporal usage does not mean that the passage of time physically possesses a front and back. Language uses a familiar spatial relationship to express temporal precedence and succession.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: the back and latter part

Examples from everyday Arab life

Arabic records دُبُرُ الْبَيْتِ for the back of a house.

In animal anatomy, دَابِرَةُ الطَّائِرِ is used for a digit at the back of a bird’s foot.

In spinning, Ibn al-Sikkīt also distinguishes qabīl from dabīr. When someone twists fibers, the direction that brings the strand toward the chest is named differently from the direction that takes it away.

These uses show that front and back in Arabic can concern a body’s position, direction of movement, and even the manner of performing a task.

In social life, turning one’s back on someone can signify severing contact, disregard, or hostility. Yet the physical movement does not always carry those meanings. Someone may depart for another destination without being hostile.

The ethical significance of turning away must therefore be determined from context.

A witness from pre-Qur’anic Arabic poetry

A verse attributed to Ṣakhr ibn ʿAmr al-Sulamī, a pre-Islamic poet and brother of Al-Khansāʾ, reads:

وَلَقَدْ قَتَلْتُكُمْ ثُنَاءَ وَمَوْحِدًا
وَتَرَكْتُ مُرَّةَ مِثْلَ أَمْسِ الْمُدْبِرِ

wa-la-qad qataltukum thunāʾa wa-mawḥidan / wa-taraktu murrata mithla amsi al-mudbiri

“I killed you two by two and singly, and left the tribe of Murrah like yesterday gone by.”

In this verse, أَمْسِ الْمُدْبِرِ refers to a past day that has elapsed and receded from the present.

The image shows that in early Arabic, D B R could describe the passing of time as well as physically turning one’s back.

The verse is recorded in early literary sources, including Al-Aghānī. Some lexical transmissions have al-dābir instead of al-mudbir; this textual variation should be preserved.

Al-Aghānī: the Ṣakhr ibn ʿAmr witness

This witness is close in meaning to the Qur’anic وَاللَّيْلِ إِذْ أَدْبَرَ: in both, time passes beyond a stage and is left behind.

Majāz al-Qurʾān: thunāʾa without tanwīn

Specialized and restricted uses

Lexical sources also record دِبَار for plots or sections of cultivated land. In certain constructions, دَبِرَ describes an animal developing sores on its back.

Ibn Fāris treats some such uses as separate from, or less closely connected to, the central meaning of back and end.

These forms caution against inventing one derivational story for every word recorded under a root.

Qur’anic research centers on derivatives actually occurring in the Qur’an. Other lexical uses matter insofar as they illuminate the word’s historical field and semantic limits.

4. Dubur: back, rear, and direction of movement

The physical back in Joseph’s story

Joseph’s story contains one of the clearest physical uses of this root.

Yūsuf 12:25

وَاسْتَبَقَا الْبَابَ وَقَدَّتْ قَمِيصَهُ مِنْ دُبُرٍ وَأَلْفَيَا سَيِّدَهَا لَدَى الْبَابِ قَالَتْ مَا جَزَاءُ مَنْ أَرَادَ بِأَهْلِكَ سُوءًا إِلَّا أَنْ يُسْجَنَ أَوْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

wa-istabaqā al-bāba wa-qaddat qamīṣahu min duburin wa-alfayā sayyidahā ladā al-bābi qālat mā jazāʾu man arāda bi-ahlika sūʾan illā an yusjana aw ʿadhābun alīmun

“They both raced toward the door, and she tore his shirt from behind. They found her husband at the door. She said: What is the recompense of someone who intended harm to your household, except imprisonment or a painful punishment?”

Here مِنْ دُبُرٍ refers to the back of the shirt.

In Yūsuf 12:26 and Yūsuf 12:27, the direction of the tear becomes important for discerning truthfulness from falsehood.

The contrast between مِنْ قُبُلٍ and مِنْ دُبُرٍ is entirely physical in this story: from the front and from the back.

This has methodological importance. Before deriving meanings of management or reflection from D B R, its direct physical meaning should be recognized.

Here dubur means neither foresight, consequences of conduct, nor ethical rejection. It simply specifies direction and the back of something.

Following behind: Al-Ḥijr 15:65

In Al-Ḥijr 15:65, Lot is commanded to move his family by night and وَاتَّبِعْ أَدْبَارَهُمْ: to follow behind them.

Here adbār refers to people’s backs or their position farther back along the route.

This use shows that being behind others does not necessarily have a negative meaning.

Lot is instructed to travel at the rear of his companions. The expression does not imply fear, wrongful flight, or abandonment of responsibility.

Whereas other passages concern battlefield retreat or turning away from guidance, here the direction of movement and someone’s position relative to companions matter.

Turning one’s back in battle

Āl ʿImrān 3:111, Al-Anfāl 8:15, Al-Aḥzāb 33:15, Al-Fatḥ 48:22, and Al-Ḥashr 59:12 contain expressions of تَوْلِيَةُ الْأَدْبَارِ.

This construction means turning one’s back or turning away, and in battle generally indicates retreat or flight.

Al-Qamar 54:45 also says:

سَيُهْزَمُ الْجَمْعُ وَيُوَلُّونَ الدُّبُرَ

sa-yuhzamu al-jamʿu wa-yuwallūna al-dubura

“The gathering will be defeated, and they will turn their backs.”

Here al-dubur is singular although a group is described. The singular does not mean that everyone shares one physical back; the expression can denote the rear direction generically.

Al-Anfāl 8:15 prohibits turning one’s back before the enemy’s ranks. The following verse, Al-Anfāl 8:16, distinguishes two situations: repositioning for battle and joining another group.

This distinction matters. Moving backward is not always wrongful flight without considering its purpose and circumstances.

The ethical and legal significance of retreat follows from the command’s context, the circumstances of battle, and the verse’s exceptions, rather than the lexical meaning of dubur alone.

In Al-Tawbah 9:25, مُدْبِرِينَ also describes retreat in battle.

Throughout these passages, the family’s basic meaning remains the rear direction or turning one’s back. The cause, ruling, and consequences of each retreat require separate examination.

Turning away from guidance

Al-Māʾidah 5:21 contains the command وَلَا تَرْتَدُّوا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِكُمْ.

Here the image of going backward can express retreat from the course of command and guidance.

Muḥammad 47:25 also says:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ ارْتَدُّوا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهِمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الْهُدَى الشَّيْطَانُ سَوَّلَ لَهُمْ وَأَمْلَىٰ لَهُمْ

inna alladhīna irtaddū ʿalā adbārihim min baʿdi mā tabayyana lahumu al-hudā al-shayṭānu sawwala lahum wa-amlā lahum

“Those who turned back after guidance had become clear to them—Satan made things attractive to them and prolonged their hopes.”

In this verse, the rear direction acquires ethical and epistemic significance. This rendering takes Satan as the subject of amlā as well, understanding it as prolonging hopes. Al-Ṭabarī instead identifies The God as its subject and understands granting respite. This interpretive difference must remain separate from the meaning of adbār; granting respite and prolonging hopes should not be presented as two independent verbs in translation.

Al-Qurṭubī: prolonging hopes and differing interpretations of agency

Yet adbār alone does not make this return blameworthy. مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الْهُدَى is decisive: guidance had become clear to them.

Al-Ṭabarī: granting respite and reading variants

The verse therefore concerns turning away after guidance becomes clear, rather than every question, doubt, or difference in understanding.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:46 also describes turning away through وَلَّوْا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهِمْ نُفُورًا.

Here the image of turning one’s back extends to thought and conduct. Its ethical meaning follows from what is rejected and the verse’s other elements.

Mudbir: someone turning away

In Al-Naml 27:10 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31, after Moses sees his staff moving, he is described by وَلَّىٰ مُدْبِرًا وَلَمْ يُعَقِّبْ.

Mudbiran here describes the state of someone who has turned away and is departing.

The context makes Moses’ fear clear. Mudbir here cannot mean an unbeliever, a misguided person, or someone knowingly rejecting truth.

By contrast, the same family occurs in Al-Muddaththir 74:23, Al-Maʿārij 70:17, and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:22 in contexts of blameworthy rejection and conduct.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:90, مُدْبِرِينَ describes people departing from Abraham.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:57, Abraham refers to a time when his audience will have turned away and departed.

In Al-Naml 27:80 and Al-Rūm 30:52, this form appears in an image of an audience turning away from the call. Ghāfir 40:33 describes turning away on the day of reckoning.

These examples show that mudbir is not inherently a name for a coward, unbeliever, or sinner. Its basic meaning is someone turning their back or departing. Context determines the reason and significance of the movement.

Front and back in one image

Al-Nisāʾ 4:47 speaks of obliterating faces and turning them عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهَا.

In Al-Anfāl 8:50 and Muḥammad 47:27, faces and backs occur together.

These constructions preserve the front–back contrast. The nature of the described events, particularly reckoning and punishment, should nevertheless not be reconstructed as a definite physical mechanism from dubur’s bodily meaning alone.

The direction is clear, but how the event takes place requires independent evidence.

5. The latter part and end of time: adbara and idbār

Night ending: Al-Muddaththir 74:33

Al-Muddaththir 74:33

وَاللَّيْلِ إِذْ أَدْبَرَ

wa-al-layli idh adbara

“And by the night as it draws to an end.”

Here adbara is used of night.

In human usage, turning one’s back may be movement away from someone or something in the opposite direction. Night, however, is linguistically described as passing away.

In this context, adbara thus means night drawing to an end and passing, rather than possessing a physical face and back like a person.

Early Arabic records both دَبَرَ and أَدْبَرَ for the ending of a day, night, or period. Alongside the reading إِذْ أَدْبَرَ in Al-Muddaththir 74:33, إِذَا دَبَرَ is also transmitted: the former has a Form IV verb, the latter Form I. The quoted verse and present count follow the reading of Ḥafṣ.

Here the spatial front–back relationship extends to temporal before and after.

Al-Ṭabarī: idh adbara and idhā dabara

Adbāra al-sujūd: Qāf 50:40

Qāf 50:40

وَمِنَ اللَّيْلِ فَسَبِّحْهُ وَأَدْبَارَ السُّجُودِ

wa-mina al-layli fa-sabbiḥhu wa-adbāra al-sujūdi

“And glorify Him during part of the night, and after prostration.”

In the reading examined here, adbār is the plural of dubur.

The expression refers to time following prostration.

Importantly, al-sujūd is a separate word. Adbār expresses only the temporal relationship of coming after it.

Exegetical explanations have applied the expression to particular prayers or practices after prayer. Yet the number of prayer units, the precise time of a particular prayer, or its ritual manner cannot be derived from adbār itself.

Lexically, the direct meaning is “after prostration.”

Idbāra al-nujūm: Al-Ṭūr 52:49

Al-Ṭūr 52:49

وَمِنَ اللَّيْلِ فَسَبِّحْهُ وَإِدْبَارَ النُّجُومِ

wa-mina al-layli fa-sabbiḥhu wa-idbāra al-nujūmi

“And glorify Him during part of the night, and when the stars disappear.”

Here idbār is the verbal noun of Form IV.

Its morphological distinction from adbār in Qāf 50:40 is real.

Adbār can be the plural of dubur, denoting latter parts or times after something. Idbār denotes the process of turning away, receding, or drawing to an end.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:49, the context concerns the time when stars disappear from sight at night’s end and daylight arrives.

This alone does not mean that stars physically cease shining or all follow one particular spatial route. Their disappearance from the observer’s view accords with the verse’s natural temporal meaning.

Qāf 50:40 also has a transmitted reading إِدْبَارِ السُّجُودِ with an initial kasrah, making the word a verbal noun. The present comparison follows Ḥafṣ: أَدْبَارَ in Qāf 50:40 is the plural of dubur, whereas إِدْبَارَ in Al-Ṭūr 52:49 is the Form IV verbal noun. This morphological difference remains, despite the related semantic fields of following and passing away.

“After prostration” for Qāf 50:40 and “when the stars disappear” for Al-Ṭūr 52:49 convey the contextual distinction naturally.

Al-Ṭabarī: plural and verbal-noun readings

6. Dābir: the final part and end of a group

Dābir belongs to the same family and lexically denotes what stands at the end or remains after others.

Of a human group, it can mean the last remainder or final part of that group.

It occurs four times in the Qur’an, each in connection with cutting off.

Cutting off the remainder: Al-Anʿām 6:45

Al-Anʿām 6:45

فَقُطِعَ دَابِرُ الْقَوْمِ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا وَالْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

fa-quṭiʿa dābiru al-qawmi alladhīna ẓalamū wa-al-ḥamdu li-llāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīna

“The last remainder of the people who had done wrong was cut off; praise belongs to The God, Lord of all worlds.”

Here قُطِعَ دَابِرُ emphasizes the ending of a group’s remainder.

Its linguistic image is clear: something is cut off so completely that not even its tail or final part remains.

The same image appears in Al-Aʿrāf 7:72, Al-Anfāl 8:7, and Al-Ḥijr 15:66.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:66, دَابِرَ هَٰؤُلَاءِ مَقْطُوعٌ concerns the final fate of a particular group.

Does dābir mean root or origin?

Some lexical sources explain cutting off the dābir as destroying a group’s root and foundation.

This explanation is understandable as a result: when the last remainder is eliminated, the group’s continuity ends.

A word’s direct meaning and the expression’s result, however, are distinct.

Dābir here denotes the end and remainder. Complete destruction follows from the combination “cutting off the dābir.”

The idiom “uprooting” should therefore not lead to claiming that dābir’s primary meaning is a plant’s root or the beginning and origin of everything.

“Cutting off the last remainder” looks toward the end in time or sequence, whereas a plant’s root suggests the starting point and source of growth.

These images may converge in complete destruction, but are not lexically identical.

Al-Anfāl 8:7 likewise concerns a specific confrontation. Dābir al-kāfirīn should not be removed from its context and treated as a promise of the physical destruction of every unbeliever in every age.

The scope is determined by that verse and its historical and Qur’anic setting.

7. Tadbīr: arranging affairs with their outcome in view

One of the most important branches of D B R is the verb دَبَّرَ.

In Arabic, tadbīr means attending to, arranging, and ordering an affair’s course with its stages and outcome in view.

Ibn Fāris explains this usage by saying that a person practicing tadbīr considers what an affair will ultimately lead to.

This clarifies the link between dubur and tadbīr. Just as dubur is something’s latter part, tadbīr involves attending to an affair’s course and outcome.

Tadbīr is not merely thinking about the future. In established usage, organizing and setting affairs in motion also matter.

Yudabbiru al-amr in the Qur’an

This verb occurs four times in the Qur’an:

  • Yūnus 10:3
  • Yūnus 10:31
  • Al-Raʿd 13:2
  • Al-Sajdah 32:5

In all four, governance is attributed to The God.

Yūnus 10:31

قُلْ مَنْ يَرْزُقُكُمْ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ وَالْأَرْضِ أَمَّنْ يَمْلِكُ السَّمْعَ وَالْأَبْصَارَ وَمَنْ يُخْرِجُ الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ وَيُخْرِجُ الْمَيِّتَ مِنَ الْحَيِّ وَمَنْ يُدَبِّرُ الْأَمْرَ فَسَيَقُولُونَ اللَّهُ فَقُلْ أَفَلَا تَتَّقُونَ

qul man yarzuqukum mina al-samāʾi wa-al-arḍi amman yamliku al-samʿa wa-al-abṣāra wa-man yukhriju al-ḥayya mina al-mayyiti wa-yukhriju al-mayyita mina al-ḥayyi wa-man yudabbiru al-amra fa-sa-yaqūlūna allāhu fa-qul a-fa-lā tattaqūna

“Say: Who provides for you from heaven and earth? Who owns hearing and sight? Who brings the living out of the dead and the dead out of the living? Who governs affairs? They will say: The God. Say: Will you not be mindful?”

Here governance is mentioned alongside provision, life, death, and ownership of human faculties.

Yudabbiru al-amr thus concerns administering and ordering affairs in creation.

Provision, life, and death should not all be folded into the meaning of yudabbiru itself. Separate expressions convey these concepts; together with governance, they present a broader picture of divine power and lordship.

Yūnus 10:3 and Al-Raʿd 13:2 use the same expression in the context of creating the heavens and earth.

In Al-Raʿd 13:2, يُدَبِّرُ الْأَمْرَ accompanies يُفَصِّلُ الْآيَاتِ. These are independent verbs: one conveys governing affairs; the other detailing and clarifying signs.

Al-Sajdah 32:5 contains يُدَبِّرُ الْأَمْرَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ إِلَى الْأَرْضِ, followed by the affair ascending to The God.

Divine governance is thus described comprehensively and continuously. Its physical manner, the spatial structure of the unseen, and its relationship to the times mentioned in the verse cannot be derived from D B R itself.

Is divine governance like human planning?

Human planning usually involves examining possibilities, weighing consequences, choosing a path, and potentially revising decisions.

People may not know something, learn it through further investigation, and change their decisions.

Those human limitations should not be imported into divine governance.

When the Qur’an says that The God governs affairs, it conveys ordering and administration—not that, like a person, He is initially unaware of an outcome and needs trial and error to discover it.

This distinction preserves tadbīr’s lexical meaning while preventing the transfer of limited human attributes to The God.

Fa-al-mudabbirāti amran: Al-Nāziʿāt 79:5

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:5

فَالْمُدَبِّرَاتِ أَمْرًا

fa-al-mudabbirāti amran

“And by those who govern affairs.”

Al-mudabbirāt is the feminine plural active participle of Form II.

In verbal meaning it relates to yudabbiru al-amr: both concern governing affairs.

There is an important difference. In the preceding four verses the subject is The God. Al-Nāziʿāt 79:5 instead has a feminine plural form without explicitly naming its precise referent.

The predominant interpretation identifies angels acting in creation at The God’s command.

This reading allows an action to be attributed to commissioned agents while preserving The God’s ultimate governance.

Nevertheless, فَالْمُدَبِّرَاتِ أَمْرًا itself does not name angels. Exegetical sources also record other views of the referents of the opening oaths as a whole.

“Those who govern affairs” is therefore a more direct translation; precise identification belongs to interpreting the surah and examining its connected verses.

Al-Ṭabarī: interpreting al-mudabbirāt

This shows why the lexical meaning of mudabbir alone cannot establish the nature of the being it describes.

Nor should this occurrence be used to insert the specific name Al-Mudabbir into a list of divine names without attention to the Qur’an’s actual wording. The Qur’an attributes the act of governing to The God; that differs from a particular definite noun occurring in the text itself.

8. Tadabbur: reflecting on the connections and consequences of discourse

Another branch of this family is تَدَبَّرَ.

This Form V verb occurs four times in the Qur’an.

In Arabic, tadabbur concerns examining something with regard to its parts’ connections, implications, and ultimate consequences.

It should not be reduced to an awkward literal rendering such as “seeing the back of a verse” or “searching for a hidden meaning behind words.”

Back and end explain the root’s semantic background, but the verb here has developed the sense of careful reflection and thorough examination.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:82: examining connections and consistency

Al-Nisāʾ 4:82

أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ الْقُرْآنَ وَلَوْ كَانَ مِنْ عِنْدِ غَيْرِ اللَّهِ لَوَجَدُوا فِيهِ اخْتِلَافًا كَثِيرًا

a-fa-lā yatadabbarūna al-qurʾāna wa-law kāna min ʿindi ghayri allāhi la-wajadū fīhi ikhtilāfan kathīran

“Do they not reflect carefully on the Qur’an? Had it been from someone other than The God, they would have found much discrepancy in it.”

This verse is among the clearest witnesses to the function of tadabbur.

Here reflection involves examining the Qur’an and assessing its consistency.

The verse does not invite a superficial encounter with the text, but asks readers to think about it and examine its interconnected parts.

This construction does not mean that tadabbur’s lexical meaning is exclusively “looking for contradictions.”

The absence of much discrepancy is one particular subject of reflection here, rather than the root’s complete definition.

Such examination can include understanding syntax, considering context, comparing related verses, recognizing different audiences and circumstances, and attending to semantic consequences.

Two rulings or expressions differing does not by itself establish contradiction. One must first determine whether their subject, audience, time, and circumstances really coincide.

Conversely, reflection does not oblige readers to invent a justification for every apparent difference at any cost. Genuine questions and difficulties require honest investigation.

Tadabbur is thus neither accepting a predetermined conclusion without examination nor searching for errors with prejudice. Its standard is careful, sustained examination of the text itself.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:68: reflection on “the discourse”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:68

أَفَلَمْ يَدَّبَّرُوا الْقَوْلَ أَمْ جَاءَهُمْ مَا لَمْ يَأْتِ آبَاءَهُمُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

a-fa-lam yaddabbarū al-qawla am jāʾahum mā lam yaʾti ābāʾahumu al-awwalīna

“Have they not reflected on this discourse, or has something come to them that did not come to their forefathers?”

Here the object of reflection is al-qawl, the discourse.

Morphologically, يَدَّبَّرُوا belongs to Form V with the tāʾ assimilated into dāl. The apparent absence of tāʾ does not establish a different root or verb form.

The context concerns speech that has reached its audience but met rejection and denial.

The verse’s question calls them to examine the discourse itself.

The accompanying mention of forefathers also matters: whether speech is familiar within ancestral tradition does not replace examining its truth.

This conclusion follows from the whole verse; tadabbur itself does not mean opposing ancestral tradition.

Tadabbur examines the content of discourse. Its result may be criticism of a common view, acceptance of a true statement, or correction of prior understanding.

Ṣād 38:29: the purpose of revealing the Book

Ṣād 38:29

كِتَابٌ أَنْزَلْنَاهُ إِلَيْكَ مُبَارَكٌ لِيَدَّبَّرُوا آيَاتِهِ وَلِيَتَذَكَّرَ أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

kitābun anzalnāhu ilayka mubārakun li-yaddabbarū āyātihi wa-li-yatadhakkara ulū al-albābi

“A blessed Book We sent down to you, so that they may reflect carefully on its verses and those of understanding may take heed.”

Here reflection is an explicitly stated purpose of revealing the Book.

لِيَدَّبَّرُوا denotes reflecting on the Book’s verses, followed by وَلِيَتَذَكَّرَ أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ.

These expressions are related but not completely synonymous.

Tadabbur concerns careful examination of verses. Taking heed involves remembering and consciously receiving a truth arising from that examination or other circumstances.

Reflection should therefore not be understood as merely repeating Qur’anic words or reading without examining meaning.

Nor does it dispense with linguistic knowledge, grammatical precision, or context. Readers who misunderstand a discourse’s structure may impose their own interpretation rather than reflect on its meaning.

Proper tadabbur is responsible attention to verses, rather than inventing unsupported hidden meanings for words.

Some readings also address a plural audience directly. This affects the grammatical addressee without altering the call to reflect on the verses. For example, Al-Ṭabarī reports an initial tāʾ in contrast to the yāʾ of لِيَدَّبَّرُوا. This report of variation should remain separate from the verse’s text in the reading of Ḥafṣ.

Al-Ṭabarī: reflection and variation in grammatical address

Muḥammad 47:24: reflection and locked hearts

Muḥammad 47:24

أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ الْقُرْآنَ أَمْ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبٍ أَقْفَالُهَا

a-fa-lā yatadabbarūna al-qurʾāna am ʿalā qulūbin aqfāluhā

“Do they not reflect carefully on the Qur’an, or are there locks upon hearts?”

Here reflection contrasts with the image of closed hearts.

In Qur’anic language, heart is not solely the name of a bodily organ in medical description. Many verses relate it to understanding, attention, inclination, and human decisions.

The image of a lock on a heart highlights an impediment to reception and acceptance.

Reflection is therefore not merely outward verbal activity. Someone approaching speech with prejudice and refusing examination may hear verses yet decline to consider them responsibly.

Still, every interpretive disagreement or difficult question should not be treated as evidence of a locked heart.

Judging people’s intentions and inner states does not follow from tadabbur’s lexical meaning. The verse describes a blameworthy condition, but identifying it in any individual requires evidence.

The nearby Muḥammad 47:25 describes people who turn back after guidance becomes clear.

This proximity is noteworthy: verse 24 concerns failure to reflect and closed hearts; verse 25 concerns retreat after clear guidance.

Both use the D B R family, but in different constructions.

In the former, the subject is careful examination of the Qur’an; in the latter, turning away from a clarified path.

Their relationship can be explored in interpretation, but should not make tadabbur identical to turning backward.

Does tadabbur mean only considering future outcomes?

Lexical explanations connect tadabbur with attention to an affair’s dubur or end.

If this becomes an exclusive, restrictive definition, however, the four Qur’anic uses will not be adequately explained.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:82 concerns assessing the Qur’an’s consistency.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:68 concerns examining discourse that has reached an audience.

Ṣād 38:29 identifies reflection on the Book’s verses as a purpose of revelation.

Muḥammad 47:24 contrasts reflection with closed hearts.

Tadabbur thus goes beyond predicting a future result. In engaging with discourse, it can involve understanding semantic dimensions, connections, implications, and consequences.

Considering outcomes is one of the root’s historical and conceptual links, rather than the complete meaning of Qur’anic tadabbur.

Nor does tadabbur mean discovering a hidden secret in every verse. Sometimes its result is clearer understanding of the direct meaning and abandoning an unsupported interpretation.

9. Distinguishing tadabbur, tadbīr, and related concepts

D B R and Q B L: back and front

In their directional meanings these roots contrast with one another.

Qubul denotes the front or facing part, and dubur the back.

In Joseph’s story, the distinction appears physically in the direction of the shirt’s tear.

Extended meanings of approaching and turning away can also relate to these directions.

Yet every use of qabūl should not be reduced to physically approaching, nor every use of D B R to physically turning one’s back. Both families have broad, distinct branches in Arabic.

Study of Q B L

D B R and F K R: reflection and thought

F K R concerns thinking, using the mind, and mental examination.

Qur’anic forms such as يَتَفَكَّرُونَ occur in connection with signs in creation and other subjects.

Tadabbur is also intellectual activity, but in its established usage focuses on careful examination with attention to connections and implications.

This distinction should not imply that tafakkur is always superficial and tadabbur always profound. Tafakkur too can be precise and extensive.

The point is that all four Qur’anic occurrences of tadabbur take the Qur’an, discourse, or verses as their object, whereas tafakkur elsewhere has varied objects.

Qur’anic tadabbur is therefore close to careful examination of revealed discourse, but this does not restrict the verb throughout Arabic to sacred texts.

D B R and ʿ Q L: reflection and reasoning

Taʿaqqul involves understanding and exercising reason.

Tadabbur also requires comprehension and assessment, but its verb denotes examining and reflecting on a particular subject.

Ṣād 38:29 mentions tadabbur and أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ in the same verse.

This association shows their close relationship, but D B R itself does not mean intellect.

Conversely, possessing reason does not mean someone has actually reflected on every subject. The capacity to understand and its careful exercise are distinguishable aspects.

Study of ʿ Q L

D B R and N Ẓ R: examination and attention

N Ẓ R can denote looking, attending, examining, and waiting.

In some uses, naẓar and tadabbur approach one another because both may concern examining a subject.

Naẓar has a broader field, however, and does not necessarily mean assessing connections between parts and the implications of discourse.

Qur’anic tadabbur highlights careful examination of a text. This tendency should not become an absolute definition making every naẓar superficial or every tadabbur successful.

Study of N Ẓ R

D B R and ʾ M R: governance and affair

These families meet in يُدَبِّرُ الْأَمْرَ.

Amr can mean an affair, matter, issue, or command.

Tadbīr concerns arranging and administering that affair.

They therefore have different semantic functions: amr names the matter at hand, and tadbīr the manner of attending to and arranging it.

Yudabbiru al-amr should consequently not be rendered merely “issues commands,” since governance here expresses more than giving an instruction.

Nor does tadbīr alone mean absolute power, ownership, or dominion. Those qualities require examination of other words and contexts.

Study of ʾ M R

D B R and ʿ Q B: back, following, and outcome

In Arabic, ʿaqib relates to the heel, behind, following, and consequences.

In some uses, D B R and ʿ Q B overlap, particularly concerning the latter part or result of an affair.

The roots are nevertheless distinct.

D B R physically emphasizes back and rear, while its tadbīr and tadabbur branches concern organizing or carefully examining.

ʿ Q B is also an independent family that must be understood from its own uses.

In expressions such as ʿāqibat al-amr, outcome is conveyed by ʿāqibah, whereas tadbīr al-amr concerns a way of attending to and arranging the affair.

This matters for translation: considering consequences is an element of tadbīr, but tadbīr is not merely knowing or predicting consequences.

Study of ʿ Q B

D B R and W L Y: turning one’s back and turning away

In many verses, D B R accompanies verbs of W L Y.

وَلَّىٰ مُدْبِرًا clearly illustrates this association.

In this context, tawliyah denotes turning or turning away, while mudbiran specifies the person’s state: turning their back and departing.

This combination does not make mudbir invariably mean fleeing in fear. As Moses’ story and other uses show, reasons for turning away vary.

The association shows that roots can converge in one image without becoming completely synonymous.

Study of W L Y

10. Suggested translations for Forqan

Translating D B R requires considering its different forms separately.

Dubur and adbār in spatial usage:

“Back,” “from behind,” “behind,” or “rear part.”

“From behind” is clear and sufficient in Yūsuf 12:25. “Behind them” is more suitable in Al-Ḥijr 15:65, where position along a route is at issue.

In battle expressions, “turn one’s back” or “retreat” is more natural according to context.

Adbara and mudbir:

“Turned one’s back,” “turned away,” “departed,” “retreating,” or “drew to an end.”

In Al-Muddaththir 74:33, night drawing to an end is suitable. In Moses’ story, “turned his back and departed” or “turned away and left” conveys the direct meaning.

Mudbir should not invariably be translated as sinner, unbeliever, or coward.

Dābir:

“Last remainder,” “final part,” or “tail.”

In cutting off the dābir, “eliminating the last remainder” preserves the image. “Uprooting” can express the result naturally in some sentences, but should not impose a plant-root meaning on dābir itself.

Adbār and idbār in temporal usage:

“After,” “at the end,” or “as something draws to an end.”

“After prostration” suits Qāf 50:40. “When the stars disappear” makes the contextual meaning of Al-Ṭūr 52:49 clearer.

Yudabbiru al-amr:

“Governs affairs,” “orders affairs,” or “administers the course of affairs.”

“Plans” alone is insufficient, potentially restricting tadbīr to decisions before execution.

Tadbīr is familiar and natural in Persian and can be retained in such contexts.

Al-mudabbirāt:

“Those who govern” or “those who arrange affairs.”

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:5, replacing the word itself with “angels” is an interpretive choice that should be distinguished from direct translation.

Yatadabbaru and yaddabbaru:

“Reflects carefully,” “considers thoughtfully,” or “contemplates meaning and connections.”

Tadabbur is familiar in Persian and may be retained when translating the relevant Qur’anic verses, provided its meaning is clear to readers.

“Careful reflection” is also suitable, but explanation should clarify that reflection on verses is more than a passing thought or repeating words.

11. Conclusion

D B R belongs to the Arabic field of back, rear, and end. Its branches concern being behind something, turning away, drawing to an end, remaining at a group’s end, organizing affairs, and examining discourse carefully.

These branches are connected, but should not all be reduced to a single restricted meaning.

In Joseph’s story, dubur denotes the physical back. In passages about retreat and rejection, the direction relates to human movement and conduct. In temporal uses, adbara and idbār convey a period passing and ending.

Dābir highlights the final part or last remainder; combined with cutting off, it conveys eliminating a group’s remainder completely.

Tadbīr concerns arranging and administering affairs and is attributed directly to The God in four verses. Al-Nāziʿāt 79:5 uses the plural mudabbirāt without naming its precise referent within the expression itself.

Tadabbur means careful examination of discourse and verses. This may include understanding meaning, assessing connections, recognizing implications, and considering consequences. It is neither merely reading words nor inventing unsupported hidden meanings.

The family can therefore be explained through a fundamental relationship, but each derivative’s final definition must fit its actual Qur’anic use.

Proposed final definition: At its core, D B R denotes the back and latter part of something. Related meanings include turning away and departing, ending, remaining at the end, arranging affairs with regard to their course and outcome, and reflecting carefully on discourse’s connections and implications. In the Qur’an, morphology and context establish each branch’s precise meaning, rather than imposing one meaning on every derivative. Brief root meaning: The back and latter part; turning away and passing; tadbīr, governing affairs; tadabbur, careful reflection on discourse.
