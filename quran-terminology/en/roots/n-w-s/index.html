Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root N W S

Al-nās, people, collective human movement, and the public field of guidance and whispering

Qur’anic forms of this root

Before entering the meaning of al-nās, one important boundary must be kept clear. In the Forqan method, al-nās is studied under the root ن و س (N W S), while insān and ins belong to the field of أ ن س (A N S). These two fields are related, but they are not the same.

Distinguishing al-nās from insān and ins

The first step in understanding al-nās is to distinguish it from insān and ins. This distinction is not wordplay. It protects the precision of the Qur’an. When the Qur’an chooses a word, that word works within its own semantic field.

Insān in the Qur’an often shows the human being as an individual in existential experience: aware, forgetful, hasty, needy, responsible, tested, and responsive to blessing and hardship. Ins belongs to the field of familiarity, presence, companionship, and visible nearness. In contrast to jinn, which is hidden and unseen, ins points toward beings that are familiar and manifest. This field is related to the human being, but it is still not identical with al-nās.

For this reason, whenever this article mentions the root أ ن س (A N S), it is referring to a separate root that should be studied in its own place.

Al-nās, however, shows the human being in the collective. Al-nās means people, human society, collective movement, social influence, public address, public fear, public imitation, public whispering, and the possibility of public guidance. Al-nās is not merely the individual human being and not merely the familiar visible creature. Al-nās is people within shared life.

The Forqan method of examining the root

The Forqan method begins from the Qur’an itself. First, we examine the contexts in which the Qur’an uses a word, the words it places beside it, the addresses in which it appears, and the semantic field it builds. The final criterion is the Qur’an itself.

In some cases, cautious reference to sister languages such as Hebrew and Aramaic can help clarify the direction of meaning. This does not mean imposing an external meaning on the Qur’an. Sister languages only help us understand the sensory and historical direction a root may have carried within the family of Abrahamic languages.

This is useful when a word has become confused in Arabic usage or in common translations. Al-nās and insān are such a case. Since these two words appear close on the surface, they must be measured from inside the Qur’an and, cautiously, against sister languages so their boundary can become clearer.

Hebrew and Aramaic as cautious witnesses

When compared cautiously with sister languages, the field of N W S comes near movement, motion, going, fleeing, shifting, and fluctuation. This is important for understanding al-nās, because in the Qur’an the people are not static. They move, come, go, follow, flee, fear, accept, turn away, gather, scatter, and are affected within society by speech, power, fear, tradition, and whispering.

By contrast, ins and insān come from another field: familiarity, presence, and the familiar human creature. Sister languages therefore support the distinction: al-nās should be seen in the field of collective and social movement, not only in the field of the individual human being.

Why al-nās is read with N W S

The connection between al-nās and N W S is defensible from several directions. First, semantically, al-nās in the Qur’an appears mostly in the collective and social field, not in the description of the human individual as an inward and existential creature.

Second, structurally, al-nās is often used with concepts that belong to collectivity and social movement: the address yā ayyuhā al-nās, a House placed for the people, the Lord of the people, the King of the people, the God of the people, and whispering in the chests of the people.

Third, in distinction from أ ن س (A N S): if al-nās is collapsed into insān, the difference between people as society and the human being as an individual becomes blurred. The Qur’an keeps these fields distinct, and that distinction requires al-nās to be studied carefully on its own.

The central meaning of the root

In the Qur’an, the root ن و س (N W S), through the word al-nās, points to people in their collective, social, and moving condition: people within life, address, guidance, testing, fear, imitation, whispering, and responsibility.

Al-nās is not merely a numerical collection of human individuals. Al-nās is human society: human beings when they live together, influence one another, help one another, wrong one another, fear one another, imitate one another, and must find their path toward God amid collective currents.

The central meaning of this root can be understood this way: N W S in the Qur’an, through al-nās, means people in the field of social movement, guidance, and whispering.

Al-nās: people in the social field of life

Al-nās in the Qur’an refers to people, humankind, the public body of human beings, or human society. According to context, it may include all people or a group of people in a specific situation. But even in more limited uses, the social weight of the word remains.

Al-nās does not deal with the isolated individual alone. It shows the human being in the collective: affected by environment, family, tribe, power, propaganda, fear, desire, tradition, and public mood. For this reason, the Qur’an addresses the people, and it also warns the human being against blind following of people.

Al-nās is both a field of invitation and a field of danger. People can carry guidance, but they can also carry fear, imitation, whispering, and misguidance.

Al-nās and the Qur’an’s public address

One of the most important uses of al-nās in the Qur’an is the address yā ayyuhā al-nās: O people. This address breaks the boundaries of tribe, race, language, sect, class, and later religious belonging. When the Qur’an says “O people,” it does not place its address under the ownership of one closed religious group.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13, it says:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَاكُم مِّن ذَكَرٍ وَأُنثَىٰ

O people, We created you from male and female. This address sees the human being before tribal, religious, historical, and social labels. The Messenger delivers the message, but people are servants of God, not the property of a prophet, jurist, tribe, state, or sect.

Al-nās and the House of God

One of the clearest semantic fields of al-nās is its relation to the House of God. In Āl ʿImrān 3:96, the Qur’an says about the first House placed for the people:

إِنَّ أَوَّلَ بَيْتٍ وُضِعَ لِلنَّاسِ لَلَّذِي بِبَكَّةَ مُبَارَكًا وَهُدًى لِّلْعَالَمِينَ

The first House placed for the people is the one at Bakkah: blessed and a guidance for the worlds.

In this verse, the House of God is for al-nās: for the people. This expression should not be reduced to tribal, family, state, racial, or sectarian ownership. If the House was placed for the people, then every religious, political, racial, or sectarian claim of possession over it must be measured against this verse.

Al-nās and shared human dignity

The word al-nās in the Qur’an shows the human being at a level before social and religious divisions. People, before they are believers, rejecters, People of the Book, Arab, non-Arab, male, female, poor, rich, ruler, or ruled, stand in the field of divine address as human beings.

The Qur’an does not erase moral and faithful differences among human beings. But those differences must not become excuses for denying the public right of people. Guidance is for the people. The House of God is for the people. The message reaches the people. Responsibility is placed upon the people.

Al-nās and social vulnerability

Al-nās is not only a positive or neutral concept in the Qur’an. People in their collective state are also vulnerable. The collective can bring the human being nearer to truth, but it can also pull him away from truth.

Fear of people, following the majority, imitating forefathers, social pressure, public mood, and hidden or open speech can press upon a person’s reason and conscience. The Qur’an repeatedly shows that the human being does not always become wiser in a crowd. Sometimes the collective normalizes error. Sometimes inherited tradition replaces revelation. Sometimes fear of people covers the fear of God.

So al-nās in the Qur’an is both a field of guidance and a field of trial. The human being must live among people, but he must not hand his conscience over to people.

Sūrat al-Nās: people seeking refuge from whispering

Sūrat al-Nās is one of the most precise places for understanding this word. In Al-Nās 114:1-6, it says:

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ النَّاسِ
مَلِكِ النَّاسِ
إِلَٰهِ النَّاسِ
مِن شَرِّ الْوَسْوَاسِ الْخَنَّاسِ
الَّذِي يُوَسْوِسُ فِي صُدُورِ النَّاسِ
مِنَ الْجِنَّةِ وَالنَّاسِ

In this sūrah, the word al-nās is repeated several times. This repetition is not accidental. The human being, in his social condition, needs refuge, because whispering does not happen only in private solitude. Whispering can enter through people, power, speech, rumor, fear, imitation, and hidden currents.

Against this danger, Sūrat al-Nās gives the human being refuge in three foundational relations with God: Rabb al-nās, Malik al-nās, and Ilāh al-nās. God is the Lord of the people, not the powers that turn people into instruments. God is the true King of the people, not systems that imagine human beings as their property. God is the God of the people, not personalities, traditions, institutions, or desires that sit in God’s place.

Ṣudūr al-nās: the inward field of people

The phrase ṣudūr al-nās, the chests of the people, is very important. The chests of the people are the place of reception, intention, fear, desire, decision, and inward orientation. Whispering works in this center.

When a society becomes polluted, only outward behavior changes. The inward field of people also comes under pressure. Fears, ambitions, fanaticisms, angers, and collective illusions settle in the chests of people. For this reason, Sūrat al-Nās is not only refuge from an individual danger. It awakens the human being to whisperings that approach his center of decision from within and without, from jinn and people, from what is hidden and what is visible.

Al-nās and the public right to revelation

Al-nās in the Qur’an reminds us that divine guidance is a public right of people. Revelation did not come to be imprisoned in the hands of religious classes, families, governments, races, or sects. God speaks to people and addresses people.

This does not deny knowledge, teaching, or guidance. Human beings can learn from one another. But teaching is not ownership. No teacher, interpreter, jurist, ruler, or group owns the people. People must be able to hear the word, measure it, and follow the best of it.

In this sense, al-nās is an anti-monopoly word. Wherever religion becomes a tool for pushing people away from the text, the House of God, understanding, questioning, justice, and responsibility, one must return to this word: al-nās.

Suggested rendering in Forqan

Al-nās should not always be rendered with one fixed English word without regard to context. In some verses, “people” is enough. In others, “humankind,” “the public,” or “human society” may be more precise.

  • Al-nās in a general context: people, humankind, the people, human beings.
  • Yā ayyuhā al-nās: O people, O humankind, O human beings; according to the rhythm and context of the address.
  • Al-nās in the context of the House of God and ḥajj: the people, all people, the public body of humankind; with attention to public right and rejection of monopoly.
  • Al-nās in social and behavioral contexts: people, human society, people in collective life.
  • Ṣudūr al-nās: the chests of the people, the inward field of people, the center of reception and orientation among people.
  • Al-nās in Sūrat al-Nās: people, humankind, the socially vulnerable human public before whispering.

Summary

ن و س (N W S) in the Qur’an, through the word al-nās, does not point only to a simple collection of individuals. Al-nās means people in the social field of life: people who are addressed by revelation, have a right of access to guidance, have a public share in the House of God, influence one another, are exposed to whispering, and must find their path toward God amid collective currents.

The difference between al-nās and insān is here. Insān more often shows the human individual in his existential and moral experience; al-nās shows the human being in the collective, in movement, in society, and in the field of public address.

In the Forqan method, al-nās should be understood this way: people, not the property of powers; addressees of God, not subjects of sects; human society, not a faceless mass. The Qur’an speaks to al-nās to bring people out of whispering, imitation, fear, and human ownership, and to return them to Rabb al-nās, Malik al-nās, and Ilāh al-nās.
