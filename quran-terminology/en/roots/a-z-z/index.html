Qur’anic Root أ ز ز

Inciting, stirring and setting in motion: from boiling and agitation to satanic incitement

Qur’anic and lexical forms

تَؤُزُّهُمْ
(taʾuzzuhum)
Qur’anic Form I imperfect, third-person feminine singular, with “them”; Maryam 19:83

أَزًّا
(azzan)
Qur’anic accusative verbal noun: a cognate accusative for emphasis, Maryam 19:83

أَزَّ
(azza)
Lexical citation form of the Qur’anic verb: he incited or set in motion

أَزِيز
(azīz)
Lexical verbal noun or noun: including the sound of boiling or thunder; not Qur’anic

أَزَاز
(azāz)
Another lexical verbal noun; absent from the Qur’an

ائْتِزَاز
(iʾtizāz)
Lexical verbal noun: associated with boiling in this word family; not Qur’anic

تَأَزُّز
(taʾazzuz)
Non-Qur’anic lexical verbal noun within the family of movement and boiling

أَزَز
(azaz)
Lexical form for crowding, constriction and compression; not Qur’anic

The Arabic root أ ز ز concerns movement, agitation, inciting and setting in motion. Such movement may appear in boiling water, vibration and sound, or in human behavior when someone incites another to act or insistently drives that person in a direction. Other uses within this word family concern crowding and compression.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Maryam 19:83, where devils are said to تَؤُزُّهُمْ أَزًّا with respect to the disbelievers. Here the meaning is not bodily movement or producing sound, but stirring and inciting people toward error and disobedience.

أَزّ does not in itself mean misleading, sin, whispering temptation or compulsion. Its core here is inciting and setting in motion. Who performs the action and the verse’s context establish its direction and ethical character.

1. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

The root أ ز ز occurs twice in the Qur’an, as two words in a single verse:

تَؤُزُّهُمْ is an imperfect verb from أَزَّ, meaning to incite them or set them in motion.

أَزًّا is the same verb’s verbal noun. It functions as a cognate accusative, emphasizing the act of incitement itself.

Both forms occur in Maryam 19:83. There are thus two word occurrences, but only one Qur’anic context for the root.

أَزَّ is a basic triliteral Form I verb with identical second and third root consonants, represented together by the shaddah. The doubled zāy should not lead us to classify it as Form II. تَؤُزُّ is the imperfect of that same basic verb.

Arabic records the verbal nouns أَزّ, أَزِيز and أَزَاز. A familiar use of أَزِيز concerns a sound arising from agitation, boiling or vibration.

Other forms known in Arabic include ائْتِزَاز, تَأَزُّز and أَزَز. The first two are recorded for a pot boiling, and the third for constriction and crowding; these forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

For its Qur’anic use, the root’s semantic core can be expressed as:

Stirring and setting someone or something in motion through incitement, pressure or agitation that prompts a reaction or movement in a direction.

This differs from ordinary movement. أَزَّهُ does more than report that movement occurs: an agent incites another or sets it in motion.

Not every lexical use in this family, however, can be restricted to deliberate human incitement. A boiling pot or the sound of thunder involves neither a human agent nor conscious intention.

2. The root in early Arabic: boiling, sound, crowding and incitement

أَزَّتِ الْقِدْرُ: a pot’s boiling and sound

One known use in early Arabic is أَزَّتِ الْقِدْرُ: the pot began boiling and churning, with the sound of its boiling.

Here أَزِيز denotes the sound of boiling water or another liquid set in motion by heat.

The word is also recorded for the distant sound of thunder. In such a use, أَزِيز denotes a sound heard from an active or powerful phenomenon.

Another expression, أَزَّ بِالْقِدْرِ or أَزَّ الْقِدْرَ, concerns providing fire and heat to bring a pot to the boil. Here an external agent sets something in motion and makes it boil.

These uses show a connection between movement, agitation and sound in part of the أ ز ز family.

They do not establish that the root necessarily began as an imitation of boiling sounds, or that all its meanings developed from a boiling pot. Such claims require independent historical evidence.

أَزِيز is a recorded linguistic use, rather than conclusive evidence of the root’s earliest historical origin.

أَزَز: crowding and compression

Another branch of this family concerns crowding and compression.

Arabic has used بَيْتٌ أَزَزٌ for a house filled with people and little free space. النَّاسُ أَزَزٌ is also recorded for a densely packed crowd.

In this use, attention shifts from intense movement to people gathering and being pressed together.

Abū Al-Najm Al-ʿIjlī, a poet of the Umayyad period, says in his rajaz verse:

أَنَا أَبُو النَّجْمِ إِذَا شُدَّ الْحُجَزْ
وَاجْتَمَعَ الْأَقْدَامُ فِي ضِيقٍ أَزَزْ

anā abū al-najmi idhā shudda al-ḥujaz / wa-ijtamaʿa al-aqdāmu fī ḍīqin azaz

“I am Abū Al-Najm when the waistbands are tightened and feet come together in a crowded, constricted space.”

أَزَز here describes a narrow, crowded space in which people have drawn close and are pressed together.

This verse shows that the أ ز ز family was used for more than the sound of boiling or psychological incitement. A tightly packed crowd was also among its applications.

Crowding should not, however, be taken as the direct meaning of تَؤُزُّهُمْ in the Qur’an. Maryam 19:83 concerns incitement by devils, rather than people physically assembling in one place.

أَزَّ الشَّيْءَ: moving something and pressing it together

Lexical reports record أَزَّ الشَّيْءَ for moving something forcefully, and also for drawing its parts close and pressing them together.

These uses may overlap in practice. People or objects moved under pressure may draw close or become compressed within a limited space.

Al-Akhṭal, a poet of the Umayyad period, says:

وَنَقْضُ الْعُهُودِ بِإِثْرِ الْعُهُودِ
يَؤُزُّ الْكَتَائِبَ حَتَّى حَمِينَا

wa-naqḍu al-ʿuhūdi bi-ithri al-ʿuhūdi / yaʾuzzu al-katāʾiba ḥattā ḥamīnā

“And breach of covenant upon breach of covenant pressed the battalions together and assembled them, until we grew heated and fierce.”

The lexical explanation of يَؤُزُّ الْكَتَائِبَ in this verse concerns assembling and pressing military units together. Its connection with agitation and conflict comes from the verse’s context.

Here the verb goes beyond natural boiling and concerns a cause that draws human groups close and assembles them into a dense mass. This witness should therefore not be treated simply as an example of psychological incitement of individuals.

Both poetic witnesses belong to Umayyad Arabic; they are not pre-Islamic verses whose composition before the Qur’an has been established. They help us understand early usage, but should not be assigned to an earlier period. This investigation did not identify an independent witness that could confidently be dated before the Qur’an.

أَزَّهُ: inciting and stirring a person

The expression closest to the Qur’anic meaning is أَزَّهُ يَؤُزُّهُ أَزًّا.

Arabic has used this expression for inciting, stirring and prompting someone to move or act.

Some lexical explanations also associate أَزّ with inducing someone by contrivance and an indirect approach: inclining a person toward an action until that person undertakes it.

This matters for human behavior, since incitement does not always operate through physical force. Speech, encouragement, arousing emotions or persistence may lead someone toward an action.

أَزَّ by itself nevertheless establishes neither deception nor complete compulsion. Such features should be attributed to a particular occurrence only when its context supports them.

Together, the lexical attestations show connections between movement, agitation, compression and incitement within this family. They do not justify a single certain historical narrative for every branch.

For the use relevant to the Qur’an, forceful incitement and stirring is the clearer and more direct meaning.

3. تَؤُزُّهُمْ أَزًّا in Maryam

The only Qur’anic verse containing this root is Maryam 19:83:

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّا أَرْسَلْنَا الشَّيَاطِينَ عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ تَؤُزُّهُمْ أَزًّا

a-lam tara annā arsalnā al-shayāṭīna ʿalā al-kāfirīna taʾuzzuhum azzā

“Do you not see that We have sent the devils upon the disbelievers, intensely inciting and stirring them?”

Maryam 19:83

The devils are the agents of incitement; the disbelievers are those upon whom it acts.

تَؤُزُّهُمْ is an imperfect verb. Its subject refers to الشَّيَاطِينَ, while the object pronoun هُمْ refers to the disbelievers.

The verb is feminine singular, agreeing here with the broken plural الشَّيَاطِينَ understood collectively. This form therefore does not refer to a particular female devil.

Syntactically, the clause تَؤُزُّهُمْ describes the devils’ condition: the devils sent upon the disbelievers incite them.

أَزًّا is the verb’s verbal noun, functioning as a cognate accusative. Placing the verbal noun after the verb emphasizes the action itself. Here the force and intensity of incitement emerge from the combination of verb, verbal noun and the root’s known meaning.

The cognate accusative alone does not establish that incitement repeats at every moment or leaves no opportunity for opposition and resistance.

Toward what do the devils incite them?

تَؤُزُّهُمْ itself does not specify the direction of incitement through a preposition or another object. The verse does not say that they incite them to one particular action.

The context of Maryam nevertheless makes its general direction clear.

Maryam 19:81 speaks of people taking deities besides The God, hoping that these will provide them with might and power.

Maryam 19:82 states that those same deities will disown their worship and turn against them.

Maryam 19:83 then describes devils sent upon the disbelievers, inciting them intensely.

In this setting, their incitement is understood to lead toward error, disobedience and persistence in falsehood.

This negative direction emerges from the verb’s relationship with الشَّيَاطِينَ and الْكَافِرِينَ and from the surrounding verses. It does not mean that أ ز ز inherently denotes incitement to sin in every Arabic use.

The same root can be used in Arabic for moving something, a pot boiling or stirring a group, without those uses necessarily carrying a negative ethical value.

Why does أَزًّا follow تَؤُزُّهُمْ?

تَؤُزُّهُمْ alone would express incitement. Adding أَزًّا emphasizes the occurrence and force of that action.

This is a familiar Arabic construction: a verb is accompanied by its own verbal noun to bring the action into prominence.

Here the linguistic image goes beyond a slight, passing provocation. The devils stir those within their sphere of influence to movement and reaction.

This construction should not be made into a conclusive theory of the psychological workings of temptation. The verse gives no separate explanation of the number of incitements, the devils’ particular messages or their precise mechanism of influence.

أَزًّا emphasizes incitement; it does not explain all its stages.

4. أَرْسَلْنَا الشَّيَاطِينَ عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ: sending and human choice

In Maryam 19:83, the Qur’an attributes the sending of the devils to The God. This part of the verse should neither be omitted nor explained away to simplify the discussion.

Linguistically, أَرْسَلْنَا الشَّيَاطِينَ عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ is not identical to sending a messenger to convey a message.

عَلَى places the devils in a relationship with the disbelievers that can suggest ascendancy and influence. By comparison, a messenger sent “to people” in other verses is associated with conveying a message and guidance.

عَلَى alone does not establish all the limits of the devils’ power or the nature of their sending. Other Qur’anic verses must inform that examination.

Al-Zukhruf 43:36 speaks of someone turning away from remembrance of Al-Raḥmān and a devil becoming that person’s companion. Al-Zukhruf 43:37 explains that these companions turn people away from the path while the latter consider themselves guided.

These verses help clarify the setting of satanic incitement: the devil’s companionship and influence are described in connection with a person turning away from remembrance of The God.

Al-Naḥl 16:99, on the other hand, denies Satan authority over those who believe and trust in their Lord. Al-Naḥl 16:100 connects his authority with those who take him as their patron, and also speaks of their associating partners.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, Satan likewise tells his followers in the Hereafter that he had no authority over them beyond inviting them and their responding to his invitation.

These verses show that satanic incitement should not be equated with a complete removal of human choice.

Satan may invite, incite, whisper temptation and make falsehood attractive. This influence does not mean that people have no part in accepting, following or resisting it.

A further distinction is needed between attributing the sending of devils to The God and The God’s ethical command to human beings. Maryam 19:83 describes the occurrence and influence of this relationship; it does not establish that disobeying The God becomes a religious command or a commendable action.

After describing the devils’ incitement, Maryam 19:84 tells the Prophet not to be hasty concerning the disbelievers, because their counting and reckoning are underway. A delayed outcome therefore does not mean that their conduct goes unaccounted for.

Questions about divine will, permission, human choice and responsibility nevertheless extend beyond the lexical definition of أ ز ز. What directly matters for studying this root is that تَؤُزُّهُمْ denotes inciting and stirring, rather than forcing people into an action over which they have no choice at all.

5. Distinguishing أ ز ز from related roots

Several other Qur’anic roots concern movement, incitement and influence over human behavior. Comparing them helps clarify أ ز ز.

أ ز ز (أَزَّ) and ه ز ز (هَزَّ)

هَزَّ concerns shaking and moving.

Study of H Z Z

In Maryam 19:25, Mary is instructed to shake the trunk of the palm tree. A physical object is being moved.

Some Arabic uses of أَزَّ also concern moving something forcefully. Its Qur’anic occurrence, however, concerns inciting human beings.

The roots are thus close in the idea of movement, but are not interchangeable in every case. هَزَّ in that verse denotes bodily shaking, whereas تَؤُزُّهُمْ in Maryam 19:83 denotes stirring and setting people in motion.

أ ز ز (أَزَّ) and ف ز ز (اسْتَفَزَّ)

In Arabic, اسْتَفَزَّ concerns stirring, dislodging or prompting movement and reaction.

Study of F Z Z

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:64, Satan is told to stir with his voice whomever he can.

This satanic setting is close to تَؤُزُّهُمْ أَزًّا. Both concern influencing people and setting them in motion.

اسْتَفْزَاز can emphasize disturbing a person’s composure, dislodging someone or provoking a particular reaction. In its Qur’anic use, أَزّ more prominently expresses forceful incitement and driving onward.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary: early Arabic explanations sometimes gloss these expressions through each other.

أ ز ز (أَزَّ) and و س و س (وَسْوَسَ)

وَسْوَسَ concerns conveying hidden, recurring speech or thought.

Study of W S W S

In Ṭā Hā 20:120, Satan whispers to Adam, drawing him toward the tree and a deceptive promise.

أَزّ in Maryam 19:83 also concerns satanic incitement, but the word itself does not specify a hidden mode of conveying thought or an inward whisper.

Whispering temptation can be one means of incitement, but the two words do not have identical meanings.

Waswasah concerns the manner of suggestion more prominently; أَزّ emphasizes stirring and driving onward.

A direct rendering of تَؤُزُّهُمْ as “they whisper temptation into their ears” therefore does not convey the root’s full meaning and adds a particular method to the expression.

أ ز ز (أَزَّ) and ن ز غ (نَزَغَ)

The Qur’an also uses نَزَغَ in settings of satanic incitement and disruption of relationships.

Study of N Z GH

In Yūsuf 12:100, Joseph speaks of Satan’s interference between himself and his brothers. Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 likewise speaks of a prompting from Satan and seeking refuge in The God when it occurs.

In this setting, nazgh can concern incitement, interference and creating conflict.

أَزّ can likewise prompt someone toward a course of action. Maryam 19:83, however, emphasizes forceful incitement by devils; the word by itself does not express the creation of discord between two people.

These roots overlap in the field of incitement, while context identifies the particular application of each.

أ ز ز (أَزّ) and ع ز ز (عِزّ)

The distinction between these roots is particularly relevant in Maryam.

Maryam 19:81 speaks of deities whose worshippers hope they will provide might and power. عِزّ belongs to the root ع ز ز.

Study of ʿ Z Z

أَزّ in Maryam 19:83, however, belongs to أ ز ز.

The words are close in sound, but have different roots and meanings. عِزّ here concerns might and the backing of power; أَزّ concerns incitement and stirring.

Their occurrence in nearby verses may make a semantic contrast noteworthy: people seek might and support, while the following passage speaks of devils inciting them.

This contextual relationship helps explain the passage’s progression. It should not be turned into a claim of shared derivation or a conclusively established verbal device.

أ ز ز is also distinct from أ ز ر, concerning strength and support. The same initial consonant and proximity in some semantic settings do not justify merging the roots.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the Qur’anic use of أ ز ز, the principal equivalents are “incite,” “stir intensely” and “set in motion.”

The first proposal for تَؤُزُّهُمْ أَزًّا in Maryam 19:83 is:

“They intensely incite and stir them.”

This conveys the verb’s meaning while reflecting some of the emphasis of its verbal noun أَزًّا.

“They drive them forcefully toward disobedience” is also defensible contextually. The direction toward disobedience nevertheless emerges from the verse’s connection with devils and disbelievers, rather than the root’s independent meaning.

“They continually whisper temptation to them” is unsuitable as a direct rendering: “whisper temptation” specifies a particular mode of influence, while “continually” introduces a definite temporal repetition not established by the verbal noun alone.

“They force them to sin” is likewise imprecise. أَزَّ expresses inciting and stirring, but by itself does not establish that the person’s choice is completely removed.

In non-Qur’anic uses, أَزِيز may mean “the sound of boiling,” “rumbling” or “a sustained sound arising from movement and vibration,” according to context. أَزَز in settings of crowding concerns compression, constriction or a densely packed crowd.

These lexical branches matter for understanding the word family, but do not justify rendering the Qur’anic تَؤُزُّهُمْ as “they make a boiling sound” or “they crowd them together.”

For Maryam 19:83 in full, the proposed rendering is:

“Do you not see that We have sent the devils upon the disbelievers, intensely inciting and stirring them?”

A contextual note can clarify that this incitement leads toward error and disobedience. The basic translation need not incorporate the entire analysis.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root أ ز ز concerns movement, agitation, inciting and stirring. Some lexical uses involve boiling and the sound arising from movement; others involve crowding and compression. In human use, it concerns inciting another or prompting movement and action.

These meanings are close within the field of movement and pressure, but no single certain historical origin for them all has been established. The sound of a boiling pot, in particular, should not become a compulsory image for interpreting every occurrence.

In the Qur’an, أ ز ز occurs only in Maryam 19:83. تَؤُزُّهُمْ denotes the devils inciting and setting the disbelievers in motion, and أَزًّا is the same verb’s emphatic verbal noun.

The negative character of this incitement comes from its agent and the verse’s context. The root itself does not inherently mean sin, deception, whispering temptation or disbelief.

Intense incitement is also distinct from absolute compulsion. Other Qur’anic verses describe Satan’s influence in relation to invitation, acceptance, following and a person’s turning away from guidance. تَؤُزُّهُمْ أَزًّا alone therefore cannot establish that people have no choice or responsibility in the face of this incitement.

Forqan’s final definition of the root أ ز ز:

“Movement and agitation; and, in transitive use, inciting another and prompting movement and reaction. This incitement can involve intensity and pressure. In the Qur’an, أَزّ refers to forceful incitement of disbelievers by devils; its misleading direction comes from context, rather than the root’s basic meaning. Other lexical uses within the family concern boiling and sound, forceful movement, crowding and compression.”
