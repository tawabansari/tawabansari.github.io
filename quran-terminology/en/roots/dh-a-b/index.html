The wolf: a predatory animal, with extended uses in Arabic

The Qur’an uses DH ʾ B only in dhiʾb, the name of the predatory animal called a wolf. It occurs three times in Joseph’s story: first when Jacob fears that a wolf may eat his son; then in the brothers’ reply, treating such an event in their presence as unlikely and a sign of failure; and finally in their report upon returning, claiming that a wolf has eaten Joseph.

Beyond the animal’s name, early Arabic records related forms describing wolf-like behavior, movement in different directions, attacks on flocks, and comparisons of human behavior to wolves. Dhuʾābah, a tuft of hair or the uppermost part of something, is also discussed under this lexical family.

None of these extended meanings replaces the direct meaning of dhiʾb in the Qur’an. All three verses concern the familiar animal. The Qur’anic importance of this root lies less in multiple meanings than in distinguishing a possible danger, a response to that danger, and a claim that the event occurred.

1. Forms and the semantic core

DH ʾ B occurs three times in the Qur’an, in a single nominal form:

Al-dhiʾbu: Yūsuf 12:13, Yūsuf 12:14, and Yūsuf 12:17.

All three are singular, definite nouns functioning as the subject. Each accompanies a verb of eating: yaʾkulahu or akalahu.

Arabic dhiʾb names the wolf, a wild carnivorous animal that hunts in natural environments and may also attack livestock.

Early Arabic sometimes explains al-dhiʾb as kalbu al-barr, a dog of the wilderness or wild environment. This is a descriptive expression used to identify the animal, rather than a modern biological classification. It does not make the wolf identical to the domestic dog.

Attested plurals include dhiʾāb, adhʾub, and dhuʾbān. The feminine form is dhiʾbah.

Some Arabic dialects ease the hamzah, producing forms such as dhīb. This pronunciation difference does not establish two independent roots. The text used for this study, following the reading of Ḥafṣ, has dhiʾb with hamzah; this does not deny other forms in the readings.

Does the root’s core mean unstable movement?

One early lexical analysis explains the core of DH ʾ B as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى قِلَّةِ اسْتِقْرَارٍ، وَأَلَّا يَكُونَ لِلشَّيْءِ فِي حَرَكَتِهِ جِهَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā qillati istiqrārin wa-allā yakūna li-al-shayʾi fī ḥarakatihi jihatun wāḥidah

Meaning: the family has a basic sense of limited stability and a thing’s movement not having a single fixed direction.

This analysis connects dhiʾb with a wolf’s movement in different directions and places expressions such as tadhaʾʾabati al-rīḥ in the same field.

This is an early explanation of the family’s semantic connections, but two issues must be distinguished.

First, dhiʾb is the established Arabic name of the wolf. Its direct meaning does not depend on this derivational explanation.

Second, one lexicographer’s semantic analysis alone cannot establish that the name first arose specifically because wolves move in different directions.

Dhiʾb cannot therefore be translated in the Qur’an as “an unsettled creature,” “one moving without direction,” or “someone attacking from every side.” Such descriptions do not replace the animal’s name, even if they occur in some accounts of wolves.

2. Early Arabic uses

The wolf in pre-Islamic poetry

One familiar poetic witness occurs in some versions of the Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays:

وَوَادٍ كَجَوْفِ الْعَيْرِ قَفْرٍ قَطَعْتُهُ
بِهِ الذِّئْبُ يَعْوِي كَالْخَلِيعِ الْمُعَيَّلِ

wa-wādin ka-jawfi al-ʿayri qafrin qaṭaʿtuhu / bihi al-dhiʾbu yaʿwī ka-al-khalīʿi al-muʿayyali

Translation following one explanation: Many a deserted, uninhabited valley, like the belly of a wild ass, have I crossed; a wolf howls there like an outcast burdened with dependants. Another explanation connects jawfi al-ʿayr with the name or proverbial description of a ruined valley. The comparison with an animal’s interior is therefore not the only attested interpretation.

The poet describes crossing an empty, uninhabited landscape. The wolf and its howl emphasize the setting’s solitude and hardship.

Yaʿwī comes from a different root and means howling. It describes the wolf’s behavior, rather than forming part of the meaning of dhiʾb itself.

Al-khalīʿi al-muʿayyali is also a literary comparison. One explanation likens the wolf’s howl to the lament or cry of someone deprived of others’ support while responsible for a family. Al-muʿayyal has also been explained as someone left free to come and go. The comparison’s details should therefore not be treated as certain or exclusive.

An old disagreement concerns these verses’ attribution. Some transmitters include them in Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah, while others attribute them to Taʾabbaṭa Sharran.

The verse is thus best presented as early poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, with disputed authorship. This disagreement does not change the use of dhiʾb to mean a wolf.

The verse shows that wolves were familiar animals in Arabic depictions of the wilderness. It does not make every Arabic reference to a wolf necessarily signify solitude, hunger, or misery.

Wolf attacks and the flock

Arabic records dhuʾiba al-rajul for someone whose flock has suffered a wolf attack.

Arḍun madhʾabah denotes land with many wolves.

Such uses connect the animal’s name with pastoral life and the danger of attacks on livestock. This danger is readily understood where livestock formed part of people’s wealth and livelihood.

That linguistic setting helps explain Jacob’s concern about Joseph being eaten by a wolf as a recognizable natural danger.

Recognizing the danger does not establish its occurrence in Joseph’s story. Arabic, like other languages, can describe a possible danger that never actually takes place.

The wolf as a comparison for human behavior

Dhuʾbānu al-ʿarab is also attested for certain thieves and raiders.

This is a comparison applied to people: some are likened to wolves because of their aggression, opportunism, or plundering.

Verbs such as tadhaʾʾaba and dhaʾuba are also used in some contexts for deceitful behavior or a person’s resemblance to a wolf.

Naming an animal and morally judging a person are nevertheless distinct matters. A wolf hunts for food and survival; that behavior does not itself carry the ethical meaning of human lying, betrayal, or corruption.

A comparison between a person and a wolf highlights a particular feature of behavior. It does not transfer every characteristic of the animal to the person.

Joseph’s brothers likewise speak of a wolf, but the animal itself is not the object of moral judgment. Responsibility for truth or falsehood belongs to the people reporting the incident.

Movement in different directions and other forms

Arabic uses tadhaʾʾabati al-rīḥ or tadhāʾabati al-rīḥ for wind blowing from different directions.

Lexical explanations connect this expression with a wolf changing direction as it approaches prey.

Dhuʾābah is also attested for a tuft or braid of hair, forelock hair or its place of growth. Extended uses concern a mountain’s summit or elevated social standing.

Different explanations connect this word with the other branches of DH ʾ B. Membership in a lexical family does not justify transferring “tuft or braid of hair,” “height,” or “nobility” to Qur’anic dhiʾb.

None of these related forms occurs in the Qur’an. Its use of this root is confined to the wolf’s name.

3. Yūsuf 12:13: the wolf as a possible danger

Yūsuf 12:13 states:

قَالَ إِنِّي لَيَحْزُنُنِي أَنْ تَذْهَبُوا بِهِ وَأَخَافُ أَنْ يَأْكُلَهُ الذِّئْبُ وَأَنْتُمْ عَنْهُ غَافِلُونَ

qāla innī la-yaḥzununī an tadhhabū bihi wa-akhāfu an yaʾkulahu al-dhiʾbu wa-antum ʿanhu ghāfilūn

Translation: He said: “It grieves me that you should take him away, and I fear that a wolf may eat him while you are inattentive to him.”

Jacob expresses two concerns: grief at Joseph’s being taken away and fear that a wolf may eat him.

Akhāfu an yaʾkulahu al-dhiʾb does not report an event. It describes a possible danger about which Jacob is concerned.

Yaʾkulahu comes from ʾ K L and concerns eating. Al-dhiʾbu is its subject: the animal that might eat Joseph.

Study of ʾ K L

The verse ends with wa-antum ʿanhu ghāfilūn, describing the brothers being inattentive to Joseph when the danger might occur.

Jacob’s concern therefore involves not only a wolf, but also a failure to watch over Joseph.

In Yūsuf 12:12, the brothers ask their father to send Joseph with them and promise to protect him. Jacob’s following reply responds to that request and promise.

Lexically, al-dhiʾbu names the animal. Fear, a possible attack, and inattention come from the sentence’s other words; dhiʾb does not itself mean fear or inattention.

Nor does the verse identify a particular known wolf. The definite form can refer to the kind of animal: a danger that a wolf might pose.

4. Yūsuf 12:14: the brothers’ reply and claimed ability to protect

Yūsuf 12:14 states:

قَالُوا لَئِنْ أَكَلَهُ الذِّئْبُ وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ إِنَّا إِذًا لَخَاسِرُونَ

qālū la-in akalahu al-dhiʾbu wa-naḥnu ʿuṣbatun innā idhan la-khāsirūn

Translation: They said: “If a wolf eats him while we are a strong group, we will indeed be losers.”

The brothers refer to the same danger Jacob feared in Yūsuf 12:13.

The construction changes, however. Jacob described his fear; the brothers reply that such an event in their group’s presence would make them losers.

La-in akalahu al-dhiʾbu is conditional. It does not report a wolf attack, but presents a hypothetical situation.

Wa-naḥnu ʿuṣbah describes the brothers’ situation. They present their numbers and collective strength as grounds for reassurance.

Al-dhiʾb still names the wolf. The full statement combines a possible attack, the group’s presence, the promise of protection, and the consequences of failing in that responsibility.

The brothers shape their response around their father’s stated concern. They accept the possibility but treat its occurrence in their presence as a major failure.

Yūsuf 12:14 thus still does not establish that a wolf attacked or that the brothers witnessed such an attack. Their words remain a response to a possibility.

5. Yūsuf 12:17: turning a possible danger into a claim about an event

Yūsuf 12:17 states:

قَالُوا يَا أَبَانَا إِنَّا ذَهَبْنَا نَسْتَبِقُ وَتَرَكْنَا يُوسُفَ عِنْدَ مَتَاعِنَا فَأَكَلَهُ الذِّئْبُ وَمَا أَنْتَ بِمُؤْمِنٍ لَنَا وَلَوْ كُنَّا صَادِقِينَ

qālū yā abānā innā dhahabnā nastabiqu wa-taraknā yūsufa ʿinda matāʿinā fa-akalahu al-dhiʾbu wa-mā anta bi-muʾminin lanā wa-law kunnā ṣādiqīn

Translation: They said: “Our father, we went to race and left Joseph by our belongings; then a wolf ate him. You will not believe us, even if we are truthful.”

This is the third and final Qur’anic occurrence of al-dhiʾb.

The main difference from the earlier two verses concerns how the event is presented.

In Yūsuf 12:13, Jacob fears a possible wolf attack.

In Yūsuf 12:14, the brothers present the event conditionally.

In Yūsuf 12:17, however, they say fa-akalahu al-dhiʾbu: “then a wolf ate him.”

This time their statement takes the form of a report about the past.

Someone’s report nevertheless differs from the truth of what is reported.

Before this report, Yūsuf 12:15 describes the brothers agreeing to place Joseph in the hidden depths of the well. Yūsuf 12:18 then mentions a shirt bearing false blood and Jacob’s reply that their own selves have made a deed seem attractive to them.

The Qur’anic narrative therefore does not confirm the claim that a wolf ate Joseph. It is part of the brothers’ explanation to their father, while other verses describe the actual events.

The falsehood of their claim does not make dhiʾb figurative. They refer to a real kind of animal but attribute to it an incident that did not happen to Joseph according to the Qur’anic account.

Some accounts outside the Qur’an add details such as an intact shirt, the absence of wolf-tooth marks, or blood taken from another animal. This passage of the Qur’an mentions the shirt and false blood without reporting those details.

Understanding the root does not require introducing such additional accounts.

The verses distinguish a recognized natural danger from human use of its name to justify conduct.

The wolf is not the actual agent of misconduct here. Those who separated Joseph from his father and then gave a false account are responsible for their own actions.

6. Distinctions from related words

DH ʾ B (dhiʾb) and S B ʿ (sabuʿ): the wolf and the predatory animal

Study of S B ʿ

Arabic sabuʿ denotes a predatory animal, a broader category than dhiʾb.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 contains wa-mā akala al-sabuʿ, concerning what a predatory animal has eaten. The expression is not restricted to wolves.

Dhiʾb, by contrast, names a particular kind of animal.

Although a wolf can be classed among predators, simply translating al-dhiʾb in Joseph’s story as “a predator” would erase the distinction between the words.

The different words in Al-Māʾidah and Yūsuf do not by themselves establish different ethical or ritual judgments concerning the animals. Their main difference is the scope of the names.

DH ʾ B (dhiʾb) and K L B (kalb): the wolf and the dog

Kalb names the dog in Arabic and occurs in the Qur’an, including the Companions of the Cave narrative in Al-Kahf 18:18 and Al-Kahf 18:22.

Wolves and dogs are closely related animals, but Arabic has separate names for them.

Explaining the wolf as kalbu al-barr does not mean every Qur’anic use of kalb includes wolves, or every dhiʾb is a domestic dog.

The word chosen in Joseph’s story is dhiʾb, whose direct translation remains “wolf.”

The animal’s name and moral comparisons

Some Arabic uses take the wolf’s hunting or movement as a basis for comparing opportunistic people or raiders to it.

This figurative usage does not make dhiʾb mean “liar,” “traitor,” or “hidden enemy” in the Qur’an.

Joseph’s story uses the animal’s name in three specific statements. None by itself turns the wolf into a definite symbol for a human group.

Ethical conclusions concerning lies, deceit, or exploiting others’ fears arise from the brothers’ conduct and the narrative as a whole, rather than from changing the lexical meaning of dhiʾb.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Wolf” is the most direct, natural English equivalent in all three Qur’anic occurrences, corresponding to Persian gorg.

In Yūsuf 12:13, wa-akhāfu an yaʾkulahu al-dhiʾbu is appropriately rendered “and I fear that a wolf may eat him.”

In Yūsuf 12:14, la-in akalahu al-dhiʾbu means “if a wolf eats him.” The conditional construction should be preserved.

In Yūsuf 12:17, fa-akalahu al-dhiʾbu means “then a wolf ate him.” This conveys the brothers’ words as spoken. Context reveals their claim’s falsehood; “they falsely claimed” should not replace the statement’s direct wording.

None of these uses requires replacing dhiʾb with interpretations such as “a cunning animal,” “a merciless beast,” or “an opportunistic predator.”

Forqan’s existing translations of these three verses also use “wolf,” corresponding to Persian gorg, and need no change to that lexical choice.

8. Conclusion

The Qur’an uses DH ʾ B only as al-dhiʾbu, always naming the familiar animal, the wolf.

Early Arabic records related forms concerning attacks on flocks, land with many wolves, movement in different directions, and comparisons of people to wolves. Some lexical explanations connect the family with changing direction, but this should not replace the animal name’s direct meaning.

The three Qur’anic occurrences form a narrative sequence: Jacob’s fear of a possible danger, the brothers’ conditional response and confidence in their protective ability, and their eventual claim that the event occurred.

One fixed name can thus perform different roles in three statements without changing its lexical meaning. Possibility, hypothesis, and an asserted report come from construction and context, rather than a change in dhiʾb itself.

Final definition: Dhiʾb names the wolf, a wild predatory animal. Arabic also uses this name as a basis for forms and comparisons concerning movement, attacks, and some human behavior. In the Qur’an, however, it refers only to the animal itself.

In Joseph’s story, the wolf represents a natural danger first raised as a possibility and later claimed by the brothers to have occurred. Whether their claim is true or false is determined from the verses together, rather than from the word’s meaning.
