Foundational Meaning

At its foundation, qatʿ means creating separation between something that had been joined, flowing, connected, or continuing. Sometimes this separation is physical, such as cutting a tree or wounding a hand. Sometimes it is social and ethical, such as cutting kinship or severing bonds that The God commanded to be joined. Sometimes it is historical and existential, as in cutting the dābir of a people. Sometimes it is mental and decisional, as in being decisive about a matter.

In classical Arabic, Q Ṭ ʿ moves in the same field: cutting with a sharp tool, separating a part from a whole, crossing and leaving a path behind, interrupting a route, severing a bond, settling a matter, and ending the continuation of a flow.

The Qur'an takes this known language field and extends it ethically and divinely: cutting kinship, cutting what should be joined, cutting the dābir of wrongdoers, cutting the road, and cutting aydī. The Qur'anic use is not foreign to Arabic usage. It applies the core meaning, severing connection, across bodily, social, ethical, legal, and existential contexts.

Therefore, Q Ṭ ʿ does not always mean physical amputation or separating a limb from the body. It can concern body, road, bond, lineage, relation, decision, time, group, the continuation of a people, or the ability of a flow to continue. Its core is severing connection, but context determines the kind of severing.

Material Layer

In the material layer, qatʿ is connected to separating part of a continuous body or flow. A qiṭʿah is a portion seen as separated from a whole. The Qur'an also speaks of قِطْعٍ مِّنَ اللَّيْلِ (qiṭʿin mina al-layl): a portion of the night, a slice of time distinguished from the whole night.

This material layer helps us see that qatʿ is not always total destruction. Sometimes it means separating a portion, ending a path, making a boundary, or stopping the continuation of a flow.

Qatʿ is also connected to decisiveness. In the verse of the Queen of Sheba, قَاطِعَةً أَمْرًا (qāṭiʿatan amrā) means someone who settles a matter and ends its suspension. Here qatʿ is not about body or blade. It is ending uncertainty and determining the path of decision.

Forms and Qur'anic Examples

The root Q Ṭ ʿ appears in several main forms. In the simple verb, such as يَقْطَعُونَ (yaqṭaʿūn), فَاقْطَعُوا (fa-iqṭaʿū), and قَطَعْنَا (qaṭaʿnā), it can mean cutting, separating, cutting a road, ending, or severing a bond. In the intensified form, such as قَطَّعْنَ (qaṭṭaʿna) and تَقَطَّعُوا (taqaṭṭaʿū), it can mean more intense cutting, tearing apart, dividing, or falling apart. In تَقَطَّعَتْ بِهِمُ الْأَسْبَابُ (taqaṭṭaʿat bihimu al-asbāb), ties and means of connection collapse. Nouns such as قِطْع (qiṭʿ) and قِطَع (qiṭaʿ) refer to portions of night or pieces of darkness, and قَاطِعَة (qāṭiʿah) points to decisive settlement.

In Al-Baqarah 2:27: وَيَقْطَعُونَ مَا أَمَرَ اللَّهُ بِهِ أَن يُوصَلَ (wa-yaqṭaʿūna mā amara Allāhu bihi an yūṣal), they cut what The God commanded to be joined.

In Ali 'Imran 3:127: لِيَقْطَعَ طَرَفًا مِّنَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا (li-yaqṭaʿa ṭarafan mina alladhīna kafarū), to cut off a side of those who covered truth.

In Al-Ma'idah 5:38: وَالسَّارِقُ وَالسَّارِقَةُ فَاقْطَعُوا أَيْدِيَهُمَا (wa-al-sāriqu wa-al-sāriqatu fa-iqṭaʿū aydiyahumā), the male thief and the female thief, cut their aydī.

In Al-An'am 6:45: فَقُطِعَ دَابِرُ الْقَوْمِ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا (fa-quṭiʿa dābiru al-qawmi alladhīna ẓalamū), the dābir of the people who did wrong was cut off.

In Al-Anfal 8:7: وَيَقْطَعَ دَابِرَ الْكَافِرِينَ (wa-yaqṭaʿa dābira al-kāfirīn), and cut off the dābir of the coverers of truth.

In At-Tawbah 9:121: وَلَا يَقْطَعُونَ وَادِيًا إِلَّا كُتِبَ لَهُمْ (wa-lā yaqṭaʿūna wādiyan illā kutiba lahum), they do not cross a valley except that it is written for them.

In Yusuf 12:31: وَقَطَّعْنَ أَيْدِيَهُنَّ (wa-qaṭṭaʿna aydiyahunna), they cut their hands. In Yusuf 12:50, the same women are recalled with قَطَّعْنَ أَيْدِيَهُنَّ (qaṭṭaʿna aydiyahunna).

In Al-'Ankabut 29:29: وَتَقْطَعُونَ السَّبِيلَ (wa-taqṭaʿūna al-sabīl), you cut the road.

In Muhammad 47:22: وَتُقَطِّعُوا أَرْحَامَكُمْ (wa-tuqattiʿū arḥāmakum), you cut your womb-bonds.

In Muhammad 47:15: فَقَطَّعَ أَمْعَاءَهُمْ (fa-qaṭṭaʿa amʿā'ahum), it tears their intestines apart.

In An-Naml 27:32: مَا كُنتُ قَاطِعَةً أَمْرًا (mā kuntu qāṭiʿatan amrā), I do not settle a matter decisively.

Qatʿ as Severing Connection

The core of this root is severing connection. Something was continuing, joined, flowing, or connected; qatʿ prevents its continuation.

In 2:27 and 13:25, qatʿ stands opposite waṣl: مَا أَمَرَ اللَّهُ بِهِ أَن يُوصَلَ (mā amara Allāhu bihi an yūṣal). This is not physical cutting. It is severing bonds The God commanded to remain joined. These bonds can be ethical, social, familial, religious, or human.

In 47:22, cutting womb-bonds appears. The meaning is clearly not cutting a physical womb or body part. It is severing kinship, responsibility, and social mercy. In 2:166, تَقَطَّعَتْ بِهِمُ الْأَسْبَابُ (taqaṭṭaʿat bihimu al-asbāb) means that the causes, ties, and means of reliance fall apart.

So in the Qur'an, qatʿ is first a semantic principle before it is a bodily image: separating something from the path of connection and continuation.

Qatʿ Against Waṣl

The contrast between qatʿ and waṣl in Al-Baqarah 2:27 is one of the most important keys to this root. The Qur'an criticizes those who break the covenant of The God after its mīthāq, then cut what The God commanded to be joined, and cause corruption in the land.

The sequence matters: first there is the covenant of The God; then that covenant reaches mīthāq and becomes firm; then the human being breaks it; the practical result is the cutting of necessary bonds; and this cutting leads to corruption in the land.

So qatʿ in this verse is not an isolated act. It is the result of the collapse of ʿahd and mīthāq. When divine commitment is opened from within, the bonds that should keep the human being and society sound are also severed: kinship, justice, trust, truth, reform, and right relation among people.

Qatʿ and Dābir

One important Qur'anic use of Q Ṭ ʿ is قَطْع دَابِر (qaṭʿ dābir). Dābir means the rear, aftermath, extension, or continuing line of a people or movement. When the Qur'an speaks of cutting dābir, it does not mean cutting a body part. It means ending the continuation of an unjust movement.

In 6:45, the dābir of the wrongdoing people is cut off. In 8:7, the cutting of the dābir of the coverers of truth is mentioned. This use shows that qatʿ can mean ending the power, continuation, and social root of a group.

This meaning matters for reading the thief verse as well, because it shows that qatʿ in the Qur'an can remove the ability of a behavior or structure to continue, not only perform a bodily cut.

Cutting the Road

In 29:29, the people of Lot are accused: وَتَقْطَعُونَ السَّبِيلَ (wa-taqṭaʿūna al-sabīl). Cutting the road means making the road unsafe, blocked, disrupted, or impossible to pass through. This is not physically slicing a road with a blade; it is disabling the route of passage and social security.

In 9:121, cutting a valley means crossing and passing through a valley. This use shows that qatʿ can also refer to traversing a space: passing through it and leaving it behind, not destroying it.

So Q Ṭ ʿ is also used in relation to path, road, and movement.

Qatʿ as Decisive Judgment

In 27:32, the Queen of Sheba says: مَا كُنتُ قَاطِعَةً أَمْرًا حَتَّىٰ تَشْهَدُونِ (mā kuntu qāṭiʿatan amran ḥattā tashhadūn). That is, I do not settle a matter decisively until you are present.

Here qatʿ is not bodily cutting. Cutting a matter means ending uncertainty, separating a decision from suspension, and reaching a final judgment. This use shows that Q Ṭ ʿ also works at the mental and legal level: ending indecision.

Cutting Hands in the Story of Joseph

One of the most important verses for understanding Q Ṭ ʿ is the story of the women of Egypt in Sūrat Yusuf. The Qur'an says: وَقَطَّعْنَ أَيْدِيَهُنَّ (wa-qaṭṭaʿna aydiyahunna). But the context shows that they did not sever their hands from their bodies. They had knives, saw Joseph, were overwhelmed, and cut or wounded their hands.

This verse shows that even when Q Ṭ ʿ appears with aydī, its meaning is not necessarily complete separation of the hand from the body. It can mean cutting, wounding, scratching, or making an incision on the hand when the context requires that.

This matters for 5:38, because the thief verse also speaks of cutting aydī. From Qur'anic language alone, seeing qatʿ with aydī does not immediately settle that the meaning must be complete amputation. Context, purpose of punishment, other Qur'anic uses, justice, and proportionality must also be considered.

The Thief Verse and Semantic Care

Verse 5:38 says: وَالسَّارِقُ وَالسَّارِقَةُ فَاقْطَعُوا أَيْدِيَهُمَا جَزَاءً بِمَا كَسَبَا نَكَالًا مِّنَ اللَّهِ (wa-al-sāriqu wa-al-sāriqatu fa-iqṭaʿū aydiyahumā jazā'an bimā kasabā nakālan mina Allāh). This is one of the most sensitive uses of Q Ṭ ʿ in the Qur'an. The famous juristic reading understands it as physically severing the hand from the body. But from the language of the Qur'an, several points must be read carefully.

First, qatʿ in the Qur'an does not always mean full severing of a limb. The example of 12:31 shows that qatʿ aydī can mean cutting or wounding hands. Second, yad in the Qur'an is not always only the physical limb. Yad can point to ability, power, agency, means of action, achievement, or effect. Expressions such as مَا قَدَّمَتْ أَيْدِيكُمْ (mā qaddamat aydīkum) show that yad can represent human action and earning, not only the palm.

Third, the verse itself says: جَزَاءً بِمَا كَسَبَا (jazā'an bimā kasabā). The recompense is tied to what the two have earned. The relation between yad and kasb matters here. The hand can be the instrument of theft, but in Qur'anic language yad can also stand for ability to act, possess, and acquire.

Fourth, verse 5:38 differs from the Pharaonic threats. Pharaoh says: لَأُقَطِّعَنَّ أَيْدِيَكُمْ وَأَرْجُلَكُم مِّنْ خِلَافٍ (la-uqaṭṭiʿanna aydiyakum wa-arjulakum min khilāf). There, hands and feet appear together, with مِّنْ خِلَافٍ (min khilāf) and the threat of crucifixion. The setting is torture and political mutilation. The thief verse does not carry that same structure and intensity.

Fifth, after the thief verse, 5:39 opens the path of repentance and repair. This means the punishment should not be read in a way that makes repair, return, and rebuilding life meaningless. From Qur'anic linguistic analysis, 5:38 has room for careful discussion. One may understand it as amputation, but that is not the only possible linguistic reading. It is valid to ask whether cutting aydī here concerns cutting the ability to steal, cutting access to the tools of crime, cutting unlawful power of disposal, or making a penal mark on the hand. These questions arise from the Qur'anic semantic field of Q Ṭ ʿ and yad.

Qatʿ, Repentance, and Repair

The verse after the theft ruling is very important: فَمَن تَابَ مِن بَعْدِ ظُلْمِهِ وَأَصْلَحَ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ يَتُوبُ عَلَيْهِ (fa-man tāba min baʿdi ẓulmihi wa-aṣlaḥa fa-inna Allāha yatūbu ʿalayh). After speaking of crime and recompense, The God leaves open the path of repentance and repair. This means the final aim is not merely destroying the offender, but restraining injustice, stopping the crime, and keeping open a path of reform.

Therefore, every reading of فَاقْطَعُوا أَيْدِيَهُمَا (fa-iqṭaʿū aydiyahumā) should be weighed with the continuation of the verse. If our reading of qatʿ practically destroys the person's ability to repair and return to a sound life, it should be examined with greater care for whether it fits the passage.

This does not deny punishment. The verse itself speaks of jazā' and nakāl. But nakāl means deterrence and warning lesson, not necessarily the destruction of a person's entire human capacity. Therefore, the meaning of qatʿ must fit deterrence, justice, proportion, and the possibility of repair.

Qatʿ as Penal Marking or Wound

Historically, some societies marked offenders through branding, penal tattooing, scratching, cutting, or a lasting bodily sign. The goal of such punishments was often to deter repeat crime, identify someone with a criminal record, or limit the ability to deceive again.

These historical examples do not prove that 5:38 definitely refers to such a method. They only show that, in the history of punishment, cutting or marking the body did not always mean severing a limb. Sometimes the goal was marking, limiting repetition, and publicly recognizing prior offense.

From this angle, if someone raises the possibility of a penal mark or incision on the hand in the thief verse, that possibility is not historically strange. But it should be presented as a linguistic and historical possibility, not as a final and certain conclusion.

Qatʿ and Proportionate Punishment

One important principle in reading the thief verse is proportion. The Qur'an treats theft as a financial and social crime, yet the next verse opens the way for repentance and repair. If a punishment is read in a way that makes repair nearly impossible, one must ask again whether that reading fits the aim of the verse and the Qur'anic field of mercy and justice.

Cutting the ability to steal can take different forms: restraining, restoring loss, blocking repetition, limiting access, public legal record, or a mark that prevents hidden repetition of the crime. In modern systems, criminal record, legal restriction, supervision, or structural restitution can play such a role.

The aim in 5:38 is جَزَاءً بِمَا كَسَبَا (jazā'an bimā kasabā) and نَكَالًا مِّنَ اللَّهِ (nakālan mina Allāh): recompense for criminal acquisition and deterrence. But determining the exact applied form of this qatʿ requires linguistic care, social justice, strict proof, room for repentance, and protection from judicial injustice.

Qatʿ and Corruption in the Land

In 2:27, cutting the bonds The God commanded to be joined appears together with corruption in the land. This shows that the Qur'an does not see qatʿ only as a physical movement. Qatʿ can break divine and human order.

When the covenant of The God is broken after mīthāq, the result is cutting right bonds and causing corruption in the land. The semantic structure is: ʿahd creates responsibility; mīthāq makes that responsibility firm; naqḍ opens that firm covenant apart; qatʿ severs bonds that should remain joined; and fasād is the social and earthly result of that severing.

So Q Ṭ ʿ is not only a root about punishment. It is one of the keys to understanding ethical and social collapse in the Qur'an.

Difference from Nearby Roots

Q Ṭ ʿ is close to N Q Ḍ, but they are not the same. Naqḍ means undoing something that had been tied, twisted, or made firm, such as breaking a covenant or mīthāq. Qatʿ means severing connection and separating a path or bond.

Q Ṭ ʿ differs from B T R. Batr points more to cutting off continuation and leaving something without posterity or aftermath. Qatʿ is broader and can include any severing, separation, or ending of connection.

Q Ṭ ʿ differs from F Ṣ L. Faṣl more often points to separation with distinction, judgment, or clear boundary-making. Qatʿ emphasizes cutting connection and ending a bond or flow.

Q Ṭ ʿ is not the same as K S R. Kasr is breaking a hard physical object. Qatʿ can mean cutting, separating, severing, ending, crossing, or deciding.

Q Ṭ ʿ also differs from J DH DH. Jadhdh points more to breaking into pieces or reducing something to fragments, as in Abraham making the idols جُذَاذًا (judhādhā). Qatʿ does not necessarily mean complete fragmentation. It may only cut connection, stop a path, or separate a bond.

Translation Suggestions

قَطَعَ / يَقْطَعُ (qaṭaʿa / yaqṭaʿu): cut, sever, separate, interrupt a bond, depending on context.

يَقْطَعُونَ مَا أَمَرَ اللَّهُ بِهِ أَن يُوصَلَ (yaqṭaʿūna mā amara Allāhu bihi an yūṣal): they cut what The God commanded to be joined; they sever necessary bonds.

قُطِعَ دَابِرُ (quṭiʿa dābiru): its continuation was cut off; that movement's line was ended.

تَقْطَعُونَ السَّبِيلَ (taqṭaʿūna al-sabīl): you cut the road; you make the route unsafe or disrupted.

قَطَّعْنَ أَيْدِيَهُنَّ (qaṭṭaʿna aydiyahunna): they cut or wounded their hands.

فَاقْطَعُوا أَيْدِيَهُمَا (fa-iqṭaʿū aydiyahumā): cut their aydī; with contextual care, this can be examined within the field of cutting ability, cutting access to criminal tools, or cutting/marking the hand.

قَاطِعَةً أَمْرًا (qāṭiʿatan amrā): one who settles a matter; a decisive decision-maker.

Summary

The root Q Ṭ ʿ in the Qur'an means severing connection. This severing may be bodily, social, ethical, historical, mental, or legal. Context determines the level of qatʿ in each verse.

Q Ṭ ʿ means cutting, separating, severing a bond, ending the continuation of a flow, cutting a road or possibility of passage, cutting kinship and social bonds, cutting dābir and ending the continuation of a people, settling a matter decisively, cutting or wounding the hand, and in some contexts physical cutting or bodily restriction.

The thief verse must be discussed with care. The Qur'an speaks of cutting aydī, but the Qur'an itself shows that cutting aydī does not always mean complete amputation. Also, yad in the Qur'an is not only the physical limb; it can indicate means of action, power, ability, and what a person has earned. Therefore 5:38 has several layers within Qur'anic language and should not be limited to one inherited juristic reading without examining root, context, justice, repentance, and repair.

The root may be summarized this way: Q Ṭ ʿ means severing connection and ending a bond, path, ability, flow, or structure that previously continued or held together. In Qur'anic usage, it can include physical cutting, social severance, cutting dābir, cutting a road, decisive judgment, and limiting the ability of crime to continue.
