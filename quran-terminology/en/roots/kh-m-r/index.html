From head coverings and concealment among things to khamr, intoxication, and obscured awareness

The Arabic root خ م ر concerns covering, concealing, and, in some applications, one thing intermingling with another. Its clearest physical image is placing a cover over something previously exposed: covering a vessel, covering the head with cloth, or hiding among dense trees.

The same family includes khimār for a head covering and khamr for an intoxicating drink. Early lexical explanations connect them by saying that khimār covers the head, whereas khamr affects reason and awareness, impairing a person’s usual judgment and self-control.

This semantic connection, however, should not produce an identical definition for every derivative. Khamr is an established name for a drink, khimār names a particular covering, and mukhāmarah in some uses means intermingling or penetration into something else.

The Qur’an uses the root in two distinct settings: khamr six times in connection with drink, and khumur once in connection with women’s clothing.

Understanding the root therefore requires distinguishing the general meaning of covering from the established meaning of each derivative. Rulings concerning drink or clothing must likewise be understood from the verses’ construction and context, rather than extracted solely from the root letters’ meaning.

1. Forms and Derivatives

خ م ر is a sound triliteral root. Early Arabic records various forms in this family:

  • خَمَرَ ـ يَخْمُرُ (khamara–yakhmuru): to cover, conceal, or prevent something from appearing.
  • خَمَّرَ ـ يُخَمِّرُ (khammara–yukhammiru): to cover; in other applications, to make khamr or prepare dough.
  • تَخْمِير (takhmīr): covering; in particular applications, preparing khamr or dough.
  • أَخْمَرَ (akhmara): to cover or conceal; in some uses, to harbor resentment.
  • خَامَرَ ـ يُخَامِرُ (khāmara–yukhāmiru): to mingle with, approach, or penetrate something.
  • مُخَامَرَة (mukhāmarah): intermingling and association with something else.
  • اِخْتَمَرَ ـ يَخْتَمِرُ (ikhtamara–yakhtamiru): in clothing, to put on a khimār; of khamr or dough, to reach maturity or fermentation.
  • خَمْر (khamr): an intoxicating drink; with a different vocalization, خَمَر (khamar), with fatḥah on both khāʾ and mīm, denotes concealment or dense trees. These vocalizations should not be conflated.
  • خِمار (khimār): a covering, particularly cloth placed on the head.
  • خُمُر (khumur): the plural of khimār.
  • خُمُور (khumūr): a familiar plural of khamr, meaning intoxicating drinks.
  • خُمار (khumār): intoxication, its lingering effects, or discomfort and headache after drinking.
  • مَخْمُور (makhmūr): someone affected by khamr or khumār.
  • خَمِير and خَمِيرَة (khamīr and khamīrah): material or dough that has changed and matured, or material added to dough to make it ferment.

The lexicons record further forms concerning concealment, crowds, fragrance, dense trees, and certain bodily conditions.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic text contains only two nouns from the family: khamr six times and khumur once. The verbs khamara, khammara, and ikhtamara do not occur in those forms.

Vowels matter: khamr has fatḥah on khāʾ and sukūn on mīm, khimār has kasrah on khāʾ, and khumār has ḍammah on khāʾ. These words have distinct meanings.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: six khamr and one khumur in seven verses

The semantic core in early sources

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one underlying principle:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى التَّغْطِيَةِ، وَالْمُخَالَطَةِ فِي سَتْرٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-taghṭiyati wa-al-mukhālaṭati fī satrin

Meaning: “One underlying principle indicating covering and intermingling under concealment.”

His definition brings two aspects together.

First, covering: something lies over another thing, or the latter remains hidden from observation.

Second, intermingling under concealment: something joins or enters another thing so that its effect emerges within or among it.

Cloth covers the head; dense trees can conceal someone among them; illness or intoxication can also enter a person’s experience and affect their bodily or mental condition.

These images help explain the semantic field, but do not establish that every derivative in every context means covering a physical object.

Khimār names a covering, khamr names a familiar drink, and mukhāmarah can express intermingling.

2. Covering and Intermingling in Early Arabic

Covering a vessel or face

Arabic records خَمَّرَ الإِنَاءَ (khammara al-ināʾa) for covering a vessel.

Something is placed over the vessel’s opening, separating or protecting its contents from the outside environment.

خَمَّرَ وَجْهَهُ (khammara wajhahu) likewise means covering one’s face.

The physical meaning is clear: something previously exposed is concealed from sight or direct contact by a covering.

These examples do not establish that a khimār as clothing necessarily covered the face in every application. “Covering the face” is a verbal construction with a specified object; khimār as a garment noun has its own established usage.

The general verb must be distinguished from the name of an implement.

Hiding among trees and terrain

Arabic also records al-khamar, with fatḥah on both khāʾ and mīm, for dense trees and other things that conceal people or animals.

Khamar al-wādī can refer to parts of a valley where trees, depressions, or natural obstacles hide something from view.

Lisān al-ʿArab records expressions concerning game hiding among the land’s natural cover.

Concealment here does not necessarily involve cloth or a vessel placed over something. The surroundings prevent it from being easily seen.

Khamār al-nās and khumār al-nās, with fatḥah or ḍammah on khāʾ, also denote a mass or crowd of people, as though someone becomes hidden among them. These vocalizations differ from khimār, the head covering.

The root thus extends beyond coverings made by people: natural surroundings and human crowds can also provide concealment.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: natural cover and the crowd forms khamār and khumār

Mukhāmarah: intermingling and penetration

Arabic uses خَامَرَهُ الدَّاءُ (khāmarahu al-dāʾu) when illness has entered and mingled with a person’s condition.

Mukhāmarah here is not limited to covering an exterior surface. It concerns something joining and affecting a person’s inner or overall condition.

Arabic poetry also uses khāmara for a memory, attachment, or grief affecting the heart.

This is not the physical mixing of liquids: a condition has entered someone’s experience and affected them.

In mental and emotional applications, “intermingling” or “entering and affecting” may therefore explain the usage better than “covering.”

These connections, however, do not establish a certain historical theory for the origin of every application. The lexicons show semantic relationships without proving the exact order in which they developed.

Fermentation and changes in materials

In food-related applications, اِخْتَمَرَتِ الْخَمْرُ (ikhtamarati al-khamru) describes a drink reaching maturity and bubbling.

اِخْتَمَرَ الْعَجِينُ (ikhtamara al-ʿajīnu) describes dough changed through fermentation and ready for use.

These uses belong to the same lexical family, but the physical process differs from covering a vessel.

In modern scientific terminology, fermentation involves biochemical changes in materials through microorganisms. This scientific definition should not be equated with the word’s meaning for pre-Islamic Arabs.

Earlier naming concerned observable maturity, changes of smell, bubbling, or transformation. Modern biochemistry explains the mechanism of those changes.

Nor is every fermented material khamr. Bread dough and some fermented foods differ in effect and use from intoxicating drink.

A shared root for khamīr and khamr consequently does not establish that every fermented food falls under the Qur’anic designation khamr.

3. Khamr in Pre-Islamic Arab Life and Poetry

A witness from ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s Muʿallaqah

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm opens his Muʿallaqah with a description of drink:

أَلَا هُبِّي بِصَحْنِكِ فَاصْبَحِينَا
وَلَا تُبْقِي خُمُورَ الْأَنْدَرِينَا

alā hubbī bi-ṣaḥniki fa-iṣbaḥīnā / wa-lā tubqī khumūra al-andarīnā

Translation:

“Rise with your bowl and give us our morning drink; leave none of the wines of Al-Andarīn.”

Khumūr here is the plural of khamr, meaning intoxicating drinks.

Later verses describe mixing the drink with water and its effect on the drinker:

تَجُورُ بِذِي اللُّبَانَةِ عَنْ هَوَاهُ
إِذَا مَا ذَاقَهَا حَتَّى يَلِينَا

tajūru bi-dhī al-lubānati ʿan hawāhu / idhā mā dhāqahā ḥattā yalīnā

Translation:

“When someone with a purpose tastes it, it turns him away from his desire until he becomes yielding toward his companions.”

Khamr is more than a pleasant liquid in this image: it affects human condition and behavior. The early commentary explains becoming yielding as joining one’s companions and abandoning one’s purpose; yalīna should therefore not necessarily be translated as physical weakness.

The witness matters because poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period uses khamr as the name of a drink that changes a person’s condition.

Khumūr here is the plural of khamr, not khimār. Head coverings are khumur; the similarity of the letters should not merge the words.

Al-Zawzanī’s Sharḥ al-Muʿallaqāt: purpose and yielding to companions

What was khamr made from?

Early Arabic particularly associated khamr with a drink made from grapes.

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits Ibn Sīdah’s restriction of khamr in its narrower sense to grapes. The same source, however, records Abū Ḥanīfah al-Dīnawarī’s use of khamr for a drink made from grains.

Other dictionaries likewise define khamr as an intoxicating drink affecting reason, without requiring a particular ingredient in their general definition.

This difference must be understood accurately.

One earlier application may specifically mean grape wine, whereas another uses the name more broadly for intoxicating drinks.

A narrower meaning does not establish that broader usage is necessarily incorrect; broader usage does not erase the historical narrower meaning.

Identifying the referent in a verse requires its context and the drink’s relevant characteristics as well as lexical meaning.

Why was the drink called khamr?

Two principal explanations appear in the lexicons.

First, it is named for its covering effect on reason: intoxication impairs a person’s usual judgment and awareness.

Second, the drink undergoes ikhtimār while being prepared and maturing: it changes, bubbles, or reaches its familiar condition.

Ibn al-Aʿrābī, as quoted in Lisān al-ʿArab, mentions its smell changing during this process.

Both explanations belong to the word’s lexical history, but neither can be established with certainty as its original naming cause.

Khamr was already the name of a familiar drink in Arabic before juridical and exegetical debates; its intoxicating effect is prominent in lexical usage.

4. Khamr in the Qur’an: Drink, Intoxication, and Responsibility

Khamr in the prisoner’s dream — Yūsuf 12:36

قَالَ أَحَدُهُمَا إِنِّي أَرَانِي أَعْصِرُ خَمْرًا

qāla aḥaduhumā innī arānī aʿṣiru khamran

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“One of the two said: I see myself pressing khamr.”

Yūsuf 12:36

This expression is lexically significant: ordinarily khamr is a drink made from something such as grapes, whereas pressing is performed on the raw material.

Early explanations offer two readings.

In the first, khamr here means grapes. Some lexicographers attribute this usage to Yemen or Oman, stating that grapes were also called khamr there.

In the second, the raw material is named after its eventual product: the person presses grapes to make khamr.

Natural language permits naming something after what will subsequently result from it.

Both readings concern preparing a drink.

The expression does not establish that every grape extract, even before any transformation, is necessarily khamr in the sense of an intoxicating drink.

The verse also recounts a prisoner’s dream. Dream language need not report every actual stage of production precisely.

Translation can retain khamr while explaining the grapes or eventual product separately in the analysis.

Al-Ṭabarī: grapes, the reported Omani usage, and the dream account

Khamr in the dream’s interpretation — Yūsuf 12:41

أَمَّا أَحَدُكُمَا فَيَسْقِي رَبَّهُ خَمْرًا

ammā aḥadukumā fa-yasqī rabbahu khamran

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“As for one of you, he will serve his master khamr.”

Yūsuf 12:41

Unlike Yūsuf 12:36, khamr here is something served to another person to drink.

Its direct meaning is therefore the drink rather than grapes.

The two verses show related stages in the narrative: the dream concerns pressing something called khamr, and Joseph’s interpretation concerns the person returning to serving his master khamr.

Rabbahu here refers to the person’s master or superior within the social structure. This use of rabb must be distinguished from its reference to The God.

The verse predicts a prisoner’s fate; it does not issue a religious ruling about drinking khamr.

The presence of khamr in this story consequently cannot by itself establish approval of its consumption or negate other Qur’anic rulings.

Khamr and maysir — Al-Baqarah 2:219

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْخَمْرِ وَالْمَيْسِرِ قُلْ فِيهِمَا إِثْمٌ كَبِيرٌ وَمَنَافِعُ لِلنَّاسِ وَإِثْمُهُمَا أَكْبَرُ مِنْ نَفْعِهِمَا

yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-khamri wa-al-maysiri qul fīhimā ithmun kabīrun wa-manāfiʿu li-al-nāsi wa-ithmuhumā akbaru min nafʿihimā

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“They ask you about khamr and maysir. Say: In both is great sin and benefits for people, but their sin is greater than their benefit.”

Al-Baqarah 2:219

Khamr is placed alongside maysir.

The Qur’an attributes both benefit and sin to them, then states that their sin exceeds their benefit.

The argument does not deny the benefits, but does not treat them as sufficient to make the practice right.

Lexically, khamr names the familiar drink. “Great sin” and the comparison of harm and benefit come from the rest of the expression rather than the root’s meaning.

Khamr should therefore not be translated as “great sin.” Great sin is the verse’s judgment about it.

Identifying what a word denotes must precede discussing how the Qur’an addresses it; this distinction matters for understanding the ruling.

The command to avoid — Al-Māʾidah 5:90

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّمَا الْخَمْرُ وَالْمَيْسِرُ وَالْأَنْصَابُ وَالْأَزْلَامُ رِجْسٌ مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ فَاجْتَنِبُوهُ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū innamā al-khamru wa-al-maysiru wa-al-anṣābu wa-al-azlāmu rijsun min ʿamali al-shayṭāni fa-ijtanibūhu laʿallakum tufliḥūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“You who have believed: khamr, maysir, sacrificial stones, and divining arrows are defilement from Satan’s work. Avoid it, so that you may prosper.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:90

Khamr appears within a group of familiar practices and implements described as رِجْسٌ مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ, “defilement from Satan’s work.”

The explicit command فَاجْتَنِبُوهُ (fa-ijtanibūhu) follows.

This instruction is from ج ن ب and means keeping away or remaining apart from something.

Study of J N B

Linguistically, three matters must be distinguished:

First, the noun khamr, denoting an intoxicating drink.

Second, its description in the verse as rijs.

Third, the practical command fa-ijtanibūhu, “avoid it.”

Avoidance is established by the full verse, rather than solely by the lexical connection between khamr and covering reason.

Nor should rijs necessarily be restricted to washable physical contamination without investigating that root separately.

Conversely, the absence of the word ḥarām does not invalidate the explicit command to avoid. Its imperative, addressees, and context must be considered.

The consequences of khamr — Al-Māʾidah 5:91

إِنَّمَا يُرِيدُ الشَّيْطَانُ أَنْ يُوقِعَ بَيْنَكُمُ الْعَدَاوَةَ وَالْبَغْضَاءَ فِي الْخَمْرِ وَالْمَيْسِرِ وَيَصُدَّكُمْ عَنْ ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ وَعَنِ الصَّلَاةِ فَهَلْ أَنْتُمْ مُنْتَهُونَ

innamā yurīdu al-shayṭānu an yūqiʿa baynakumu al-ʿadāwata wa-al-baghḍāʾa fī al-khamri wa-al-maysiri wa-yaṣuddakum ʿan dhikri allāhi wa-ʿani al-ṣalāti fa-hal antum muntahūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Satan wishes through khamr and maysir to create enmity and hatred among you and to turn you away from remembrance of The God and from ṣalāh. Will you then desist?”

Al-Māʾidah 5:91

The verse describes social and spiritual consequences of khamr and maysir.

Enmity, hatred, and being prevented from remembrance and ṣalāh are expressed by the verse’s construction.

These consequences have an understandable relationship to khamr’s intoxicating effect on awareness, but should not all become components of its lexical meaning.

The verse also treats khamr and maysir together. Their social consequences should be examined in that setting; it should not be claimed without evidence that all consequences occur immediately and equally with every instance of drinking.

Nevertheless, the preceding verse’s command to avoid is not made dependent on proving a specific injury on each occasion.

Explaining a ruling’s rationale must therefore be distinguished from turning that rationale into a condition for setting the ruling aside.

Sharāb, sakar, and khamr

In Persian and Dari, sharāb often means intoxicating drink; Arabic Qur’anic sharāb has a broader meaning.

Sharāb denotes something drunk, including water, various drinks, and the drinks of the Garden.

Khamr in its familiar worldly application, however, denotes intoxicating drink.

Every khamr is a drink, but not every drink is khamr.

Al-Naḥl 16:67 describes people obtaining سَكَرًا وَرِزْقًا حَسَنًا from dates and grapes.

Sakar belongs to another root. Its meaning here requires lexical usage, its contrast or association with “good provision,” and the verses concerning intoxication.

خ م ر itself does not occur in this verse.

Khamr, sharāb, and sakar should therefore not replace one another in translation or argument without investigating their differences.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 likewise describes the condition sukārā and forbids approaching ṣalāh until a person knows what they are saying.

This verse connects intoxication with impaired awareness, but does not contain khamr.

The conceptual connection matters, and the lexical difference must also be preserved.

5. Khamr among the Garden’s Blessings

An excerpt from Muḥammad 47:15 reads:

وَأَنْهَارٌ مِنْ خَمْرٍ لَذَّةٍ لِلشَّارِبِينَ

wa-anhārun min khamrin ladhdhatin li-al-shāribīna

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And rivers of khamr, delightful to those who drink.”

Muḥammad 47:15

This is another significant use of khamr.

Here it describes the Garden’s blessings, rather than censured practices in worldly life.

The same verse describes rivers of water, milk, khamr, and honey.

The contextual difference is clear: Al-Māʾidah 5:90 and Al-Māʾidah 5:91 concern avoiding worldly khamr and its consequences; Muḥammad 47:15 concerns the reward of the Garden.

Muḥammad 47:15 calls the Garden’s khamr “delightful to those who drink.” That expression alone does not explain its material composition or how it acts.

Other verses describe features of the Garden’s drinks.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 reads:

لَا فِيهَا غَوْلٌ وَلَا هُمْ عَنْهَا يُنْزَفُونَ

lā fīhā ghawlun wa-lā hum ʿanhā yunzafūna

Proposed translation for this article: “There is no harm in it, nor are they deprived of their senses by it.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19 also reads:

لَا يُصَدَّعُونَ عَنْهَا وَلَا يُنْزِفُونَ

lā yuṣaddaʿūna ʿanhā wa-lā yunzifūna

Proposed translation for this article: “They suffer no headache from it, nor are they deprived of their senses.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19 describe the absence of harmful effects in the Garden’s drinks. Explanations of ghawl and yunzafūna/yunzifūna include bodily or mental harm, intoxication, and, in some readings, the drink running out. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19 also explicitly denies headache. These related explanations should not all be treated as a single lexical equivalent.

Sharing the name khamr across worldly life and the Garden does not itself establish identical composition, bodily effects, or characteristics in both realms.

Conversely, a different context alone does not justify removing khamr from the Garden verse or replacing it with another drink without evidence.

The Qur’an itself uses this name for the Garden’s blessings.

The base translation should therefore retain khamr or use its familiar equivalent, discussing differences between worldly and otherworldly characteristics in the explanation.

Al-Ṭabarī: ghawl, the readings of yunzafūna/yunzifūna, and alternative explanations

6. Khimār and Khumur: Head Coverings in Arabic and the Qur’an

Khimār in early Arabic

The lexicons recognize khimār, with kasrah on khāʾ, as a head covering.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْخِمَارُ مَا تُغَطِّي بِهِ الْمَرْأَةُ رَأْسَهَا

al-khimāru mā tughaṭṭī bihi al-marʾatu raʾsahā

Meaning: “Khimār is what a woman uses to cover her head.”

The source also explains khimār through naṣīf and records the plurals akhmirah and khumur.

As clothing, khimār is thus more specific than any cloth: it denotes a familiar head covering.

In broader Arabic definition, however, khimār can denote a cover that conceals something.

A man’s turban is also called khimār in some uses because it covers the head.

The family’s general covering sense must therefore be distinguished from khimār’s specific clothing sense.

The root’s general meaning is covering; khimār’s familiar clothing meaning is a head covering.

The two propositions do not contradict one another.

Lisān al-ʿArab: khimār as a head covering and its application to a turban

Women’s clothing in pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī reads:

سَقَطَ النَّصِيفُ وَلَمْ تُرِدْ إِسْقَاطَهُ
فَتَنَاوَلَتْهُ وَاتَّقَتْنَا بِالْيَدِ

saqaṭa al-naṣīfu wa-lam turid isqāṭahu / fa-tanāwalathu wa-ittaqatnā bi-al-yadi

Translation:

“Her covering fell without her intending to drop it; she reached for it and shielded herself from our gaze with her hand.”

The Arabic lexicons explain naṣīf as a khimār or a woman’s covering.

Its precise form is disputed: some describe a head covering, others a broader covering worn over clothing.

The witness establishes the presence of familiar women’s coverings, including something lexically associated with khimār, in early poetry. It cannot reconstruct a single, certain form for all pre-Islamic women’s dress.

The poem itself also says naṣīf, not khimār. It illustrates the historical clothing setting rather than directly attesting خ م ر in that hemistich.

Lisān al-ʿArab, niṣf: Al-Nābighah’s witness and the differing definitions of naṣīf

Drawing their head coverings over their garment openings — Al-Nūr 24:31

وَلْيَضْرِبْنَ بِخُمُرِهِنَّ عَلَىٰ جُيُوبِهِنَّ

wa-l-yaḍribna bi-khumurihinna ʿalā juyūbihinna

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Let them draw their head coverings over their garment openings.”

Al-Nūr 24:31

Khumur here is the plural of khimār.

It denotes coverings known in Arabic as head coverings.

The full expression’s construction, however, is essential.

Wa-l-yaḍribna is an imperfect verb built on sukūn because of the attached feminine plural subject pronoun, nūn al-niswah. It occupies the jussive position under the imperative lām. This is the lām of command, not emphasis, and the sentence expresses an instruction.

Bi-khumurihinna specifies the means or instrument of the action: their head coverings.

ʿAlā juyūbihinna specifies where the covering is to be placed.

Juyūb is the plural of jayb. In clothing, jayb denotes the slit or opening of the garment at the neck and chest.

The expression’s direct construction is therefore:

Women are to place their khimārs over their garment openings and the corresponding area.

Khumur identifies the covering, and ʿalā juyūbihinna identifies its placement.

Iʿrāb al-Qurʾān: imperative lām and the imperfect built on sukūn in the jussive position

Study of J Y B

Does khimār simply mean any covering?

Beginning solely with the semantic core, the family can be said to concern covering.

That does not establish that khumur in Al-Nūr 24:31 means any cloth, cover, or covering implement.

A derivative can acquire an established meaning more specific than its root’s general semantic field.

In clothing, khimār is a familiar head covering.

Ignoring that usage and reducing the word to “anything covering something” consequently weakens lexical precision.

Does the verse directly command covering the head?

The noun’s meaning must be distinguished from the command’s specified object.

Khimār is a head covering in Arabic, but the imperative accompanies ʿalā juyūbihinna, specifying a location for its placement.

The verse explicitly instructs placing the khimār over garment openings.

This cannot be deleted from the translation and replaced solely with “cover their heads,” which would lose juyūbihinna and its grammatical role.

Conversely, criticizing that translation does not justify denying khimār’s familiar meaning as a head covering.

Two linguistic facts must be preserved together:

First, the specified means is khimār, a head covering in familiar clothing usage.

Second, the command specifies placing it over juyūb, the garment openings.

What broader ruling, with what limits concerning women’s heads, hair, necks, chests, and clothing, follows from this construction and other clothing verses is a separate inquiry from the root’s meaning.

Lexical study should neither impose a broader ruling on the word without argument nor change khimār’s historical, familiar meaning to evade a possible ruling.

Al-Ṭabarī: khimār, garment openings, and drawing the covering

Khimār and the modern concept of hijab

In contemporary Persian and Arabic, hijab sometimes names a collection of regulations and forms of women’s dress.

Qur’anic Arabic ḥijāb, however, is from ح ج ب, with a basic meaning concerning a barrier, curtain, or concealing separation.

Khimār is from خ م ر and, in clothing, means a head covering.

These words are not complete lexical synonyms.

The modern general expression “Islamic hijab” can encompass clothing, behavior, and social instructions, whereas khumur in Al-Nūr 24:31 names a particular kind of covering.

The full contents of a later social or juridical term should not be transferred into khimār’s lexical definition.

7. Comparison with Related Roots

KH M R (khamr) and SH R B (shurb)

ش ر ب concerns drinking in the Qur’an, and sharāb is the general name for something drunk.

Khamr in its familiar worldly application, however, is intoxicating drink.

Not every drink is khamr, although every khamr is a kind of drink when consumed.

This matters especially in Muḥammad 47:15, which mentions water, milk, khamr, and honey together.

Study of SH R B

KH M R (khamr) and S K R (sukr)

Sukr concerns intoxication and impaired awareness.

Khamr in its drinking sense, by contrast, names something capable of producing that effect.

One denotes the material or drink, the other the condition or effect of intoxication.

They are closely related but not complete synonyms.

Sakar in Al-Naḥl 16:67 also requires separate examination within its own construction and context. A word from س ك ر does not establish the occurrence of khamr there.

KH M R (khimār) and S T R (satr)

Satr concerns covering and keeping something covered.

The general branch of خ م ر also concerns covering.

Khimār as clothing names a particular implement, whereas satr can denote the act of covering anything.

Every khimār is a covering implement, but not every covering implement is called khimār.

KH M R (khimār) and Ḥ J B (ḥijāb)

Ḥajb concerns an intervening barrier or obstacle.

Khimār can conceal something from view, but in clothing denotes a head covering.

A curtain between rooms, for example, can be a ḥijāb or barrier without necessarily being a khimār.

A khimār may cover the head without covering the whole body or separating the space between people as a curtain does.

Both roots involve covering, but their forms and uses differ.

Study of Ḥ J B

KH M R (mukhāmarah) and KH L Ṭ (khalṭ)

خ ل ط concerns mixing and intermingling two or more things.

Some Arabic applications of mukhāmarah also concern something intermingling with or penetrating another thing.

خ م ر additionally has a prominent branch of covering and concealment.

In khāmarahu al-dāʾu, illness has mingled with and affected the person’s condition.

Not every application of khalṭ, however, can be called mukhāmarah, nor does every mixture necessarily produce concealment.

Study of KH L Ṭ

KH M R (khamr) and KH M L (khumūl)

Arabic khumūl concerns obscurity, diminishing fame, or being little noticed.

خ م ر can also concern concealment and hiding.

This semantic resemblance does not make the roots identical.

Khamr as intoxicating drink also has an established meaning that cannot be derived from خ م ل.

8. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Translation must keep khamr and khumur entirely distinct.

Khamr in the drinking verses is best retained as khamr or rendered “intoxicating drink.”

Modern Persian and Dari sharāb can naturally translate khamr, but the reader should understand that Qur’anic Arabic sharāb is broader.

In Yūsuf 12:36, the direct “I am pressing khamr” preserves the Arabic wording. Explaining the grapes or material becoming khamr belongs in the analysis.

In Yūsuf 12:41, khamr is the drink served to the person’s master.

In Al-Baqarah 2:219, Al-Māʾidah 5:90, and Al-Māʾidah 5:91, khamr is the drink under question and prohibition. The ethical judgment or avoidance command must be explained through the other components of the verses, rather than made part of the noun’s lexical meaning.

Muḥammad 47:15 describes rivers of khamr. It should not become the name of a different drink without evidence.

Khumur in Al-Nūr 24:31 is the plural of khimār and denotes “head coverings” in familiar clothing usage.

For the expression:

وَلْيَضْرِبْنَ بِخُمُرِهِنَّ عَلَىٰ جُيُوبِهِنَّ

wa-l-yaḍribna bi-khumurihinna ʿalā juyūbihinna

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Let them draw their head coverings over their garment openings.”

This preserves both khimār’s meaning and juyūbihinna, the command’s explicitly specified location.

Covering and concealing; in some applications, one thing intermingling with and affecting another.

Conclusion

Arabic خ م ر concerns covering, concealing, and, in some applications, intermingling under concealment.

Two significant Qur’anic words belong to this family: khamr, the familiar drink associated with intoxication and impaired awareness, and khumur, the plural of khimār, a head covering in clothing usage.

Early Arabic particularly associated khamr with grape wine, but also records broader usage for intoxicating drinks made from other ingredients. Conversely, neither every drink nor every fermented material can be called khamr solely because of a shared production process.

The Qur’an uses khamr six times in drinking contexts and khumur once in a clothing context.

Al-Baqarah and Al-Māʾidah discuss harm, benefit, and avoiding khamr; Muḥammad describes it among the Garden’s blessings. A shared name does not establish identical worldly and otherworldly characteristics.

In Al-Nūr 24:31, khumur denotes head coverings, but the instruction focuses on placing them over juyūb. Both the familiar meaning of khimār and the specified location must be preserved.

Thus, the root’s basic meaning is covering and concealing, but each Qur’anic derivative’s meaning is determined by its established usage and the verse’s context.

This distinction prevents two opposing errors: extracting every meaning and ruling about clothing or drink from the root’s general image, and setting aside derivatives’ familiar meanings to reach a predetermined interpretation. Brief root meaning: Covering and concealing; in some applications, one thing intermingling with and affecting another.
