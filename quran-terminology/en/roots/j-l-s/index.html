1. Introducing the root

The three-letter Arabic root J L S concerns sitting, a place to sit, and the gathering of people who sit together. In its familiar use, جَلَسَ (jalasa) means to sit. From the same family come مَجْلِس (majlis), a sitting place or gathering place, and جَلِيس (jalīs), someone who sits with another person and keeps that person company.

The root’s semantic range, however, extends beyond human sitting. In early Arabic, جَلْس (jals) also names elevated ground, a mountain, a large rock, or a raised part of the land. In some uses, jalasa means to go to the highland region of Najd. Forms of this family also describe a large or tall bodily frame in humans and animals.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies elevation and rising as the root’s underlying principle. He explains sitting through rising from a reclining position into a higher posture, and places jals, elevated land, within the same family.

This analysis shows that the Arabic lexicographical tradition recognized a connection between sitting and elevated ground. It does not establish that every use of jalasa necessarily means ascending, or that all sitting means taking a high seat. In ordinary usage, jalasa means to sit, whether the seat is high or low.

In the Qur’anic text according to the reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, the basis of Forqan’s current edition, J L S occurs only once: as الْمَجَالِسِ (al-majālisi) in Al-Mujādilah 58:11.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence in Al-Mujādilah 58:11

Majālis is the plural of majlis and refers to places where people sit or gather. This verse instructs believers to make room when asked to do so in gatherings, and to rise when told to rise.

This usage places sitting within social relations. A majlis is more than somewhere to position the body: people gather there, converse, learn, decide matters of shared concern, or participate in a social activity.

These social functions are understood from the use of majlis and its context. The root J L S itself does not mean knowledge, consultation, or leadership.

The study must therefore distinguish three matters: sitting as a bodily posture; the majlis as a place or gathering; and the value and responsibility of people’s conduct within it. Brief root meaning: Sitting; a sitting place and gathering; and, in other early usages, elevated ground.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Recognized forms of the Arabic J L S family include:

جَلَسَ (jalasa): A Form I verb meaning “he sat.” In some early usages, it also means going to elevated land.

يَجْلِسُ (yajlisu): The imperfect of jalasa: “he sits.”

جُلُوس (julūs): The verbal noun: sitting.

جَلْسَة (jalsah): With fatḥah on jīm, a single instance of sitting.

جِلْسَة (jilsah): With kasrah on jīm, a posture or manner of sitting, such as sitting with the legs drawn in or crossed.

جَالِس (jālis): An active participle: someone sitting.

جُلُوس (julūs) and جُلَّاس (jullās): Plurals recorded for people who are sitting.

جُلَسَة (julasah): A description of someone who sits frequently.

أَجْلَسَ (ajlasa): A Form IV verb: to seat another person or cause that person to sit.

جَالَسَ (jālasa): A Form III verb: to sit with another person or keep someone company.

مُجَالَسَة (mujālasah) and جِلَاس (jilās): Verbal nouns concerning sitting with another person and companionship.

جَلِيس (jalīs): A companion: someone present with another person in an assembly or sitting.

جُلَسَاء (julasāʾ) and جُلَّاس (jullās): Plurals for companions and people attending an assembly.

تَجَالَسَ (tajālasa): A Form VI verb: to sit together.

مَجْلِس (majlis): A sitting place, gathering place, or the gathering itself. In some usages it also refers to those attending the gathering.

مَجَالِس (majālis): The plural of majlis. This is the root’s sole Qur’anic form.

مَجْلِسَة (majlisah): Another form for a sitting place, recorded in lexicons.

جَلْس (jals): Elevated ground, a mountain, or a hard, raised area of land. In other usages it describes a person or animal with a large bodily frame.

جَلْسِيّ (jalsī): Relating to highland or an elevated region, especially in contrast with lowland.

Lexicons also record rarer forms concerning plants, honey, and parts of the eye. These usages require attention to their own vocalization and evidence; their meanings must not be transferred without justification to sitting or assemblies.

Of all these forms, the Qur’an uses only al-majālisi.

This word is a broken plural of majlis. It does not directly name the act of sitting, but identifies places or gatherings attended by people.

3. The semantic core: elevation and sitting

Ibn Fāris’s analysis

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْجِيمُ وَاللَّامُ وَالسِّينُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ وَأَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ الِارْتِفَاعُ فِي الشَّيْءِ.

al-jīmu wa-al-lāmu wa-al-sīnu kalimatun wāḥidatun wa-aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa al-irtifāʿu fī al-shayʾi

Translation: “Jīm, lām, and sīn constitute one word family with one underlying principle: elevation in something.”

Ibn Fāris: elevation and the analysis of sitting

He then explains sitting through a person’s movement from lying or reclining into a seated posture.

In such a movement, the upper body rises from the surface on which it had rested.

This provides an intelligible connection between sitting and elevation.

Sitting itself, however, is not exclusively an upward movement.

Someone may stand and then sit down. In that case, the head and upper body occupy a lower position than when standing.

Consequently, the analysis through elevation cannot serve as the direct definition of every form of jalasa without regard to actual usage.

In ordinary Arabic, sitting is a familiar bodily posture, independent of the position a person occupied beforehand.

Someone may move from reclining to sitting, sit down from standing, or settle somewhere after walking or moving about.

In all three cases, the resulting posture can be called julūs.

Distinguishing the posture from the sitting place

Julūs names the act or state of sitting.

Majlis, however, concerns the sitting place or gathering.

Someone may sit on a stone, on the ground, or on a carpet. The act is julūs.

If a place is recognized or prepared for people to sit, it can be called a majlis.

There is thus a distinction between the act and its location.

The mafʿil pattern in majlis helps identify the location of the action. This morphological relationship does not mean that a majlis must always be a dedicated building or enclosed room.

A majlis can be in a house, outdoors, at a tribal gathering place, in an educational setting, or elsewhere.

In its basic use, what matters is the presence of people sitting or gathering in a place.

Can a majlis exist without sitting?

A place’s name may remain after the associated action has ended.

A room prepared for gatherings can still be called a majlis even when nobody happens to be sitting there.

People may also attend a majlis with some standing and others seated.

The meaning of majlis has therefore extended beyond a particular moment of bodily contact with the ground.

In such usage, the place or gathering takes its name from an activity usually performed there; every individual’s being seated is not a permanent condition for using the word.

The same extension appears in the social and administrative uses of majlis.

4. Jals: elevated land and bodily characteristics

Elevated land and mountains

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَالْجَلْسُ: الْجَبَلُ.

wa-al-jalsu al-jabalu

Translation: “Jals means a mountain.”

Another explanation states:

وَالْجَلْسُ مَا ارْتَفَعَ عَنِ الْغَوْرِ.

wa-al-jalsu mā irtafaʿa ʿani al-ghawri

Translation: “Jals is land that rises above the low-lying region.”

In Arabic geographical usage, غَوْر (ghawr) refers to low or sunken land.

Jals, by contrast, can name highlands and elevated parts of the terrain.

Some accounts connect this usage with Najd, which stands in contrast with lower regions.

Thus, when an early Arabic text says someone jalasa in a context of travel and geography, going to highland may be intended.

In this usage, the verb does not necessarily refer to the person sitting down.

Jalasa al-qawmu: going to Najd

Lexicons record that جَلَسَ الْقَوْمُ (jalasa al-qawmu) can mean that a group went to elevated land.

Ibn Fāris considers this consistent with the sense of elevation.

In geographical language, moving toward lowland and moving toward highland are distinct actions.

This sense must nevertheless remain distinct from ordinary sitting.

If the text concerns a group moving toward Najd, the geographical sense merits consideration. If it concerns sitting in a house or assembly, the sitting sense is more appropriate.

The same verbal form can therefore have different uses according to its referent and context.

Large rocks and hard ground

Arabic sources also record jals for a large, hard rock and for rough, uneven ground.

Physical height and hardness are prominent in this usage.

A rock may rise above the surrounding surface, or an area may be known for its hard, uneven, stony ground.

This does not establish that every rock or every elevated tract must necessarily be called jals in Arabic.

Jals is one of the recorded names or descriptions in this field and must be understood within its actual usage.

Humans and animals with large frames

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَنَاقَةٌ جَلْسٌ وَجَمَلٌ جَلْسٌ: وَثِيقٌ جَسِيمٌ.

wa-nāqatun jalsun wa-jamalun jalsun wathīqun jasīmun

Translation: “A female or male camel described as jals is sturdy and large-bodied.”

Here, jals does not refer to the animal sitting.

The subject is its bodily character: size, height, or sturdiness of frame.

Two lexical explanations are reported. Some connect this use with height and elevation; others suggest a connection with a form of the J L Z family and its sense of compactness and firmness.

This disagreement shows that resemblance in letters and meanings alone cannot establish a certain derivational history.

Lisān: highland, bodily frame, and possible consonantal substitution

The descriptive jals belongs in the study of J L S, but must not be converted into a meaning of sitting or sitting place.

Plants and honey

Some sources also record جَلَسَ الشَّيْءُ (jalasa al-shayʾu) for something remaining or continuing in place.

Abū Ḥanīfah al-Dīnawarī discusses this usage for a plant that remains in the ground for a long period.

Some accounts also use jals for honey or an amount of honey remaining in a vessel.

These are narrower usages whose interpretation requires their particular lexical evidence.

Their presence in one dictionary entry does not mean that the Qur’anic sense of sitting concerns honey, plants, or everlasting permanence.

Jullasān as a loanword

Arabic sources record جُلَّسان (jullasān) for flowers, aromatic plants, or a place over which flowers were strewn.

Some lexicographers identify it as a Persian loanword and connect it with forms such as gulshān.

This is significant for etymological method.

A word’s placement under an Arabic root does not by itself prove that the word was formed from those three Arabic consonants.

Borrowed words may be recorded beside an Arabic word family because of similarities in sound and spelling, even though their derivational histories differ.

Jullasān must therefore not be treated without investigation as evidence for deriving flowers and gardens from the Qur’anic root J L S.

Lisān: plants, honey, and borrowed jullasān

5. The root in early Arabic poetry and language

Majlis in the poetry of Muhalhil ibn Rabīʿah

Muhalhil ibn Rabīʿah, a recognized pre-Islamic Arabic poet, says in an elegy attributed to him about Kulayb:

نُبِّئْتُ أَنَّ النَّارَ بَعْدَكَ أُوقِدَتْ
وَاسْتَبَّ بَعْدَكَ يَا كُلَيْبُ الْمَجْلِسُ

nubbiʾtu anna al-nāra baʿdaka ūqidat / wa-istabba baʿdaka yā Kulaybu al-majlisu

Translation: “I was told that after you the fire was kindled, and after you, Kulayb, the people of the assembly began exchanging insults.”

Here, al-majlisu outwardly names the meeting place, but the act of exchanging insults belongs to those present.

The place itself neither speaks nor insults.

The poet therefore uses the name of the assembly to refer to its people.

This is a recognized Arabic usage: a place’s name sometimes denotes the people present there.

One might say, for example, that the assembly disagreed over an issue. The reference is to those attending, rather than to walls and carpets.

Muhalhil’s line shows this semantic extension in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Ibn ʿĀdil: the Muhalhil witness and the people of an assembly

The majlis here is more than a physical sitting place: it is a group of people gathered in a social setting.

The word majlis itself, however, does not mean insult or disagreement. That behavior is expressed by the verb اسْتَبَّ (istabba) in the line.

Majlis and social standing in Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah’s poetry

Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah, the Arab poet and warrior, says in an elegy attributed to him:

حَرَامٌ عَلَيْهَا أَنْ تَرَى فِي حَيَاتِهَا
كَمِثْلِ أَبِي جَعْدٍ فَغُورِي أَوِ اجْلِسِي

ḥarāmun ʿalayhā an tarā fī ḥayātihā / ka-mithli Abī Jaʿdin fa-ghūrī awi ijlisī

Translation: “In her lifetime she will see no one like Abū Jaʿd, whether you go to the lowlands or the highlands.”

Here, اجْلِسِي (ijlisī) does not carry its ordinary meaning, “sit down.”

It stands opposite غُورِي (ghūrī).

Ghūrī means going to lowland, while ijlisī means going to highland.

The poet uses these geographical alternatives to express the breadth of the search: lower land and higher land.

This witness matters for understanding the root’s geographical branch.

Durayd was an early poet who lived through the pre-Islamic period and the beginning of Islam. Literary sources record this line in his elegy, but its precise composition date must be established separately from the poet’s lifetime.

The line matters because it shows that jalasa could, in an early usage, concern going to high ground.

This sense must not be transferred to the Qur’anic al-majālisi, which concerns sitting places and gatherings.

Asās al-Balāghah: the fa-ghūrī awi ijlisī witness

Ladā kulli majlisi: the assembly as a social setting

The same elegy by Durayd continues:

أَعَفَّ وَأَجْدَى نَائِلًا لِعَشِيرَةٍ
وَأَكْرَمَ مَخْلُودٍ لَدَى كُلِّ مَجْلِسِ

aʿaffa wa-ajdā nāʾilan li-ʿashīratin / wa-akrama makhlūdin ladā kulli majlisi

Here, the poet speaks of someone’s generosity and standing among his people and in social gatherings.

لَدَى كُلِّ مَجْلِسِ (ladā kulli majlisi) points to a setting where people gather and a person’s conduct and standing become known.

A majlis can therefore be a setting for speech, assessment of conduct, hospitality, and social relations.

These functions are understood from people’s lives and conduct. Majlis itself does not mean generosity or standing.

Al-Aghānī: Durayd’s elegy and the reading makhlūd

Majlis in Dhū al-Rummah’s poetry

A line attributed to Dhū al-Rummah states:

لَهُمْ مَجْلِسٌ صُهْبُ السِّبَالِ أَذِلَّةٌ
سَوَاسِيَةٌ أَحْرَارُهَا وَعَبِيدُهَا

lahum majlisun ṣuhbu al-sibāli adhillatun / sawāsiyatun aḥrāruhā wa-ʿabīduhā

Translation: “They have an assembly of red-bearded, abject men; their free and enslaved alike are equal in abasement.” Sibāl is recorded for both moustaches and beards; the verse is derogatory, rather than a celebration of social equality.

Lisān: sibāl for moustaches and beards

Here, majlis refers to a group of people attending a gathering.

The descriptions that follow concern the people themselves.

Lexicons cite this line to explain majlis in the sense of the people of an assembly.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad poet. This witness must therefore not be described as pre-Islamic poetry.

Its value lies in showing the continuation of a use also found in the poetry attributed to Muhalhil: naming the assembly to denote those attending it.

Al-Sharīf al-Murtaḍā: the derogatory context of Dhū al-Rummah’s witness

What the early witnesses establish

The poetic witnesses show three significant fields of use.

In Muhalhil’s poetry, majlis refers to a gathering and its people.

In Durayd’s poetry, ijlisī concerns movement toward highland, while majlis also denotes a social setting.

In Dhū al-Rummah’s poetry, majlis again names a group attending a gathering.

These usages show that the J L S family had bodily and spatial meanings in early Arabic and also described social relations.

They do not establish that political association, knowledge, or consultation must have been the root’s original meaning.

These things can occur in an assembly, but are not the lexical meaning of majlis itself.

6. Majlis: a sitting place and human gathering

Majlis as a place

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَالْمَجْلِسُ: مَوْضِعُ الْجُلُوسِ.

wa-al-majlisu mawḍiʿu al-julūsi

Translation: “Majlis is a place for sitting.”

This definition clarifies the direct meaning of the noun of place.

A majlis can be somewhere a person sits alone, or a place where a group gathers.

In social usage, however, an assembly usually involves several people, conversation, and relations among them.

Arabic also uses majlis for the sitting itself, such as a gathering convened to hear someone speak or consider a matter.

Majlis and majlas

Lexicons record a distinction between مَجْلِس (majlis), with kasrah on lām, and مَجْلَس (majlas), with fatḥah on lām.

In its familiar use, majlis names a sitting place.

In some morphological explanations, majlas is a mīm-prefixed verbal noun and expresses the act of sitting itself.

This distinction can be examined morphologically.

In actual language use, however, the two forms do not always maintain an entirely separate, fixed semantic boundary.

The Qur’anic al-majālisi is the plural of majlis and refers to places or gatherings.

Majlis as the people present

Arabic can also call the group seated in a place a majlis.

This appears in the poetry of Muhalhil and Dhū al-Rummah.

The place’s name is then used for those present there.

The ordinary statement “the assembly decided,” for example, concerns those who attended and made the decision, rather than the physical place.

In social usage, majlis can therefore refer to the place, the gathering, or its people.

This extension is natural in language, but must not obscure the basic sense of a sitting place.

Lisān: majlis as a place and its people

Jalīs: a companion

Jalīs describes someone who sits with another person or accompanies that person in a gathering.

Arabic جَالَسَهُ (jālasahu) likewise means to sit with someone or keep that person company.

This usage emphasizes a relationship between two or more people.

People may sit beside one another in an assembly while differing in thought, faith, ethics, or social position.

Sitting together does not by itself mean agreement, close friendship, or acceptance of all the other person’s beliefs.

Companionship can nevertheless provide an opportunity for acquaintance, conversation, education, and cooperation.

These are possible outcomes of an assembly, rather than fixed components of the lexical definition of jalīs.

7. The Qur’anic occurrence: Al-Mujādilah 58:11

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا قِيلَ لَكُمْ تَفَسَّحُوا فِي الْمَجَالِسِ فَافْسَحُوا يَفْسَحِ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ وَإِذَا قِيلَ انشُزُوا فَانشُزُوا يَرْفَعِ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مِنكُمْ وَالَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ دَرَجَاتٍ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā qīla lakum tafassaḥū fī al-majālisi fa-ifsaḥū yafsaḥi Allāhu lakum wa-idhā qīla unshuzū fa-unshuzū yarfaʿi Allāhu alladhīna āmanū minkum wa-alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma darajātin wa-Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrun

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “O you who have believed, when you are told to make room in gatherings, make room so that The God may grant you spaciousness. And when you are told to rise, rise; The God raises those among you who have believed and those who have been given knowledge by degrees. And The God is aware of what you do.”

This verse contains the root J L S’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Al-majālisi is the plural of majlis and refers to sittings and gathering places.

Addressing believers, the verse sets out two actions: making room in gatherings and rising when asked to do so.

It then speaks of divine spaciousness and the raising of the ranks of those with faith and knowledge.

Fī al-majālisi: in gatherings

فِي (fī) is a preposition and here identifies where the action occurs.

Al-majālisi refers to gatherings attended by people.

The expression is plural.

Its direct meaning is consequently not restricted to one particular building, room, or sitting.

People may need to make room for others in an educational setting, social gathering, place of discussion, or another assembly.

Linguistically, majālis can encompass such places.

Tafassaḥū: making room

تَفَسَّحُوا (tafassaḥū) comes from F S Ḥ, rather than J L S.

The verb concerns opening up and widening a space.

The verse commands people to make room for others in an assembly.

This may involve moving slightly, sitting closer together, or providing a suitable place for newcomers.

Tafassaḥū itself does not mean getting up and leaving the gathering altogether.

The distinction matters because the verse later uses another verb for rising.

Making room and rising must therefore not be treated as a single action.

Fa-ifsaḥū and yafsaḥi Allāhu lakum

After the instruction about making room, the verse states:

“فَافْسَحُوا يَفْسَحِ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ” (fa-ifsaḥū yafsaḥi Allāhu lakum).

Making space for others is thus connected with divine spaciousness for you.

There is a clear linguistic relationship between the imperative and the following verb.

People make room for someone else in the gathering, and God grants them spaciousness.

The expression does not, however, restrict the precise form of that divine spaciousness.

This sentence alone does not establish that anyone who makes room for someone else will necessarily receive more money, a higher administrative position, or a particular success that same day.

Spaciousness can have a broader range within the Qur’an’s semantic network.

That discussion concerns F S Ḥ and must not all be placed within J L S’s lexical definition.

Wa-idhā qīla unshuzū fa-unshuzū

The next part of the verse states:

“وَإِذَا قِيلَ انشُزُوا فَانشُزُوا” (wa-idhā qīla unshuzū fa-unshuzū).

انشُزُوا (unshuzū) comes from N SH Z and means to rise or get up.

The instruction to make room is followed here by an instruction to rise.

The actions are clearly distinct.

People may remain in the assembly while making room for someone else.

In another situation, however, they may need to get up from their places.

The precise reason for rising is not stated in the verb unshuzū itself.

It may be to end the gathering, provide places for others, or carry out something necessary.

This expression must therefore not be used, without supporting evidence, to derive a fixed instruction to stand before someone of higher social rank.

Unshuzū also belongs to N SH Z. Its meaning must not be attributed to elevation in Ibn Fāris’s analysis of J L S.

Yarfaʿi Allāhu … darajātin

After the instruction to rise, the verse speaks of raising the ranks of those who believe and those given knowledge.

Here, يَرْفَعِ (yarfaʿi) belongs to R F ʿ.

It concerns raising and elevation, but elevation with دَرَجَاتٍ (darajātin) also concerns standing and rank.

The presence of elevation in the lexical history of J L S does not establish that the raising of ranks in this verse is derived from majlis itself.

The Qur’an places two distinct matters beside one another: human conduct in gatherings, and the standing of faith and knowledge.

This contextual connection matters, but does not replace each root’s lexical definition.

8. Assemblies, knowledge, and social conduct

Making room and respecting others

People in an assembly may compete for seats because of social position, wealth, age, knowledge, or closeness to the host.

Newcomers and less influential people may then find it difficult to obtain a place.

The command تَفَسَّحُوا فِي الْمَجَالِسِ (tafassaḥū fī al-majālisi) concerns conduct that can prevent such crowding and exclusion.

The verse itself does not prescribe a fixed arrangement by age or social status for every gathering.

What it explicitly states is that room should be provided in gatherings.

Instead of monopolizing the available space, a person considers another’s presence as well.

This social consequence follows from the verse’s command, rather than from majlis’s lexical meaning alone.

Does majlis mean only the Prophet’s assembly?

Some exegetical reports connect this verse with crowding in Muḥammad’s gatherings.

According to these accounts, people sometimes competed to sit near the Prophet and were unwilling to make room for others.

Other explanations concern gatherings connected with warfare and people’s positions within them.

These reports show that exegetes held different views about the verse’s historical setting.

The Qur’anic form in the reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, however, is al-majālisi: gatherings.

Linguistically, this has a broader scope than one particular assembly.

Even if the command followed a specific event, that fact alone does not establish that the word’s meaning is restricted to that event.

The plural form, however, does not establish that every detail of the verse applies identically to every gathering and situation.

The command’s principle is clear, while its implementation may relate to the gathering’s actual circumstances.

The readings al-majlisi and al-majālisi

Sources on Qur’anic readings and exegesis also report the singular الْمَجْلِسِ (al-majlisi).

The singular can refer to a known assembly and, in some Arabic usages, can also denote the category of assemblies.

The plural al-majālisi, by contrast, refers to multiple gatherings.

This difference must not prompt the hasty conclusion that one reading expresses only a personal ruling and the other only a general ruling.

Assessing the command’s scope requires examining its audience, accompanying verbs, and context as well as the singular or plural form.

The base text according to the reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, and Forqan’s current edition, use al-majālisi.

The root study should therefore examine this as its principal Qur’anic form and retain the reading variation in its explanatory place.

Al-Ṭabarī: readings, assembly settings, and spaciousness

Assemblies and private conversation

The verses preceding Al-Mujādilah 58:11 discuss private conversations and people’s conduct toward one another.

Al-Mujādilah 58:8 and Al-Mujādilah 58:9 discuss confidential conversation and the need to avoid conversations involving sin and aggression.

Al-Mujādilah 58:10 addresses confidential conversation in relation to causing believers distress.

Verse 11 then discusses conduct in assemblies.

This proximity shows that part of the surah addresses human communication and conduct in one another’s presence or dealings.

Najwā and majlis are nevertheless not synonyms.

Najwā concerns the manner of conversation, while majlis concerns the place or gathering.

An assembly may involve open or private conversation. Private conversation may also occur outside a formal assembly.

The lexical meaning of majālis must therefore not be restricted to confidential or secret speech.

Assemblies and access to knowledge

The end of Al-Mujādilah 58:11 places knowledge beside faith.

This connection can matter for understanding believers’ social conduct.

Where people gather to listen, learn, or converse, making room for others can help them attend and benefit.

Majlis itself, however, does not mean a school, university, or scholarly center.

An assembly may be educational or serve another purpose.

Its focus on knowledge must therefore be understood from the gathering’s subject.

This distinction prevents every Qur’anic social gathering from being converted into an educational or religious institution.

Assemblies and social standing

In some societies, a person’s seat can indicate rank, influence, or standing.

Places nearest the host may be reserved for certain people.

In such circumstances, the instruction to make room and rise may relate to respect and preventing seats from being monopolized.

That social relationship must not become the definitive meaning of majlis.

A majlis is fundamentally a sitting place or gathering. People’s social position is a matter that may arise within it.

Al-Mujādilah 58:11 also does not establish that believers must surrender their places to anyone with power or wealth, regardless of right and justice.

The verse states no such classification.

9. Distinguishing J L S from related roots

J L S (julūs) and Q ʿ D (quʿūd)

Study of Q ʿ D

In Arabic, quʿūd refers to sitting and in many usages is close or equivalent to julūs.

Some lexicographers nevertheless distinguish them.

According to one explanation, julūs more particularly concerns rising from lying or reclining into a sitting posture, whereas quʿūd more particularly contrasts with standing.

This distinction relates to Ibn Fāris’s analysis of elevation in J L S.

In actual Arabic usage, however, jalasa and qaʿada can replace one another in many sentences.

Jalasa may, for example, also describe someone sitting down after standing.

Qaʿada likewise has extensive Qur’anic uses that are not all restricted to bodily sitting.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:95, الْقَاعِدُونَ (al-qāʿidūna) contrasts with those who strive in jihād. The context concerns nonparticipation in the activity as well as bodily position on the ground.

The distinction between julūs and quʿūd must therefore not become an absolute rule for every usage.

J L S (majlis) and Q ʿ D (maqʿad)

Study of Q ʿ D

Maqʿad belongs to Q ʿ D and denotes a sitting place.

Majlis likewise denotes a sitting place.

Their spatial meanings are close.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:121, مَقَاعِدَ لِلْقِتَالِ (maqāʿida li-al-qitāli) concerns positions assigned in preparation for battle.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:11, however, majālis appears in the instruction to make room for others.

This shows that one word concerning sitting can occur in a military setting and another in a social gathering.

It does not establish that maqʿad is always military, or majlis always educational or devotional.

Both can refer to sitting places according to context.

J L S (julūs) and Q W M (qiyām)

Study of Q W M

Qiyām concerns rising and standing.

Julūs refers to sitting.

In ordinary language, these can be two distinct bodily postures.

Standing and sitting are not always opposing moral concepts, however.

Someone may rise out of respect, for worship, or to perform a task, and at another time sit to rest or listen.

The command unshuzū in Al-Mujādilah 58:11 belongs to N SH Z, rather than Q W M.

Although “rise” is a natural translation, the verb’s lexical formation must not be identified with قَامُوا (qāmū).

J L S (julūs) and N SH Z (nushūz)

In one field of use, N SH Z concerns elevated ground and rising.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:11, unshuzū means to rise.

Al-majālisi, by contrast, identifies sitting places.

The words occur in the same verse but differ in root and role.

Someone may sit in an assembly and then rise.

Rising then continues or changes the state of sitting, but majlis itself does not mean rising.

The other sense of nushūz in family relations must also not be transferred to the command to rise in this verse.

J L S (majlis) and N D W (nādī)

Study of N D W

Arabic nādī concerns a gathering place and those present there.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:17 uses نَادِيَهُ (nādiyahu).

In context, it refers to a person’s group or meeting place.

Majlis can likewise denote a gathering place or its people.

The two words are therefore close in social usage.

Nādī and majlis nevertheless belong to different families and must not be identified in every usage.

Modern nādī also names clubs and social institutions, but this contemporary usage must not be imposed on every Qur’anic occurrence.

J L S (majlis) and J M ʿ (jamʿ)

Study of J M ʿ

Jamʿ concerns bringing together and assembling.

A majlis can be a place where people assemble.

Assembling does not necessarily mean sitting.

A group may gather while moving, working, or standing.

Majlis, by contrast, in its familiar sense concerns a sitting and its place.

An assembly can therefore be a place of congregation, but not every congregation must be called a majlis.

J L S (jalīs) and Ṣ Ḥ B (ṣāḥib)

Study of Ṣ Ḥ B

Ṣāḥib belongs to Ṣ Ḥ B and concerns companionship and relations among people.

Jalīs describes someone who sits with another person or attends an assembly with them.

A jalīs may also be someone’s ṣāḥib or companion, but the descriptions do not coincide in every usage.

Two people may, for example, sit beside one another in an assembly without friendship or enduring companionship.

Conversely, two friends may be far apart yet remain companions.

Sitting together can therefore be one form of companionship without encompassing the whole meaning of accompanying and companionship.

J L S (majlis) and SH W R (shūrā)

Qur’anic shūrā concerns consultation and participation in considering affairs.

An assembly may be a place of consultation, but majlis itself does not mean consultation.

Shūrā emphasizes the process of decision-making and people’s participation.

Majlis concerns their place or gathering.

People may consult within an assembly, but not every assembly is necessarily a decision-making council.

The political or administrative meaning of majlis must therefore not be derived from J L S alone.

10. Questions of interpretation and translation

Does jalasa always mean to ascend?

No.

Ibn Fāris identifies elevation as this family’s underlying principle and offers a lexical explanation.

Ordinary jalasa, however, means sitting.

When someone sits down from standing, this verb can still be used even though the body moves downward.

The image of elevation must therefore not replace the recognized meaning of sitting.

Must a majlis be a room or building?

No.

A majlis can be a sitting place outdoors, in a house, at a gathering place, or in another setting.

The word alone does not determine the building material, presence of a roof, type of seat, or size of the space.

Al-Mujādilah 58:11 speaks of gatherings, rather than a particular building type.

Is a majlis always a place for religious education?

No.

Al-Mujādilah 58:11 concerns faith, knowledge, and social conduct.

Arabic majlis, however, can describe many kinds of gathering.

If a majlis is convened to teach the Qur’an or discuss scholarly matters, that function follows from its subject, rather than from the word majlis itself.

Does majālis mean people of rank?

No.

Majālis is the plural of sitting places or sittings.

Some assemblies may involve people with power or knowledge, but this is not part of the word’s lexical meaning.

The instruction to make room in Al-Mujādilah 58:11 addresses believers; majlis itself names no particular social class.

Is making room the same as rising?

No.

The verse uses two separate verbs: tafassaḥū and unshuzū.

The first concerns making space in an assembly; the second concerns rising.

Someone may make room for another person without leaving the gathering.

In another situation, that person may need to rise.

These actions must therefore remain distinct in translation and analysis.

Is the raising of ranks derived from majlis itself?

No.

Yarfaʿi belongs to R F ʿ, while darajāt belongs to another family.

Al-majālis refers to gatherings.

These concepts are connected in the verse, but none is the lexical meaning of another.

Majālis therefore cannot be translated as “ranks of knowledge.”

Does majlis concern only bodily sitting?

In its primary sense, majlis relates to sitting.

In social usage, however, it can also refer to the gathering itself or the group present.

This extension appears in early Arabic poetry.

Translation must therefore distinguish the place from the social gathering.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:11, “gatherings” is appropriate because it conveys both the place of assembly and the social setting intended.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

In ordinary usage, jalasa is primarily “sat.”

Yajlisu is “sits,” and julūs is “sitting.”

Jalsah, with fatḥah on jīm, can be “one instance of sitting.”

Jilsah, with kasrah on jīm, concerns a “sitting posture” or “manner of sitting.”

Ajlasa is “seated [someone],” while jālasa is “sat with” or “kept company with.”

Jalīs means “a sitting companion.”

Majlis can be translated as “sitting place,” “gathering,” or, in a suitable context, “assembly.”

The word majlis is familiar in Persian, but some modern uses denote a legislature or formal political assembly.

For a verse concerning a general gathering, the Persian neshast, “gathering” or “sitting,” may therefore be more natural.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:11, the expression:

تَفَسَّحُوا فِي الْمَجَالِسِ

tafassaḥū fī al-majālisi

Appears in Forqan’s current Persian translation as:

“Make room in gatherings.”

This rendering is appropriate.

Majālis refers to the gatherings, while tafassaḥū expresses making room.

The complete translation must also preserve the distinction between making room and rising.

The equivalents for non-Qur’anic jals differ.

In its geographical sense, jals can be “elevated land,” “high ground,” or “mountain.”

In the context of travel to Najd, jalasa al-qawmu can be rendered “the group went to the highlands.”

The descriptive جَمَلٌ جَلْسٌ (jamalun jalsun) refers to “a sturdy, large-bodied camel.”

These meanings must not be transferred without contextual evidence to the Qur’anic al-majālisi.

12. Conclusion

The Arabic root J L S concerns sitting, a sitting place, and human gatherings. Alongside these meanings, early usages concern elevated ground, mountains, rocks, and a large bodily frame.

Ibn Fāris identifies elevation as the family’s semantic core. This fits certain usages involving sitting up from reclining and traveling to highland, but the entire root cannot be restricted to ascending.

In ordinary Arabic, jalasa means to sit, while majlis names the place or gathering.

Early poetic witnesses show that majlis was more than a physical place: it could also refer to those attending a gathering. In Muhalhil’s poetry, al-majlis concerns the people of the assembly. Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah’s poetry shows both the geographical ijlisī and majlis as a social setting.

This semantic extension shows how a place’s name can come to denote a gathering or its people without losing the basic meaning of sitting place.

The Qur’an uses this root only once, as al-majālisi in Al-Mujādilah 58:11.

Believers are instructed there to make room in gatherings when asked, and, in another situation, to rise when told to rise.

The verse then speaks of divine spaciousness and the raising of the ranks of believers and those with knowledge.

The assembly here is consequently more than a physical place: it is a setting where people’s conduct toward one another matters.

Majālis itself, however, does not mean respect, knowledge, justice, or humility. Those concepts follow from the verse’s commands and context.

Compared with quʿūd, julūs may in some usages emphasize movement from reclining to sitting, but the two overlap extensively in Arabic.

Majlis and maqʿad are likewise close in the sense of sitting place, though they belong to separate word families.

For Forqan, “sitting” is the principal equivalent associated with jalasa, while “gathering” or “assembly” suits majlis.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:11, the existing Persian rendering, “make room in gatherings,” preserves the word’s meaning and the sentence’s structure well.

The root’s operative Qur’anic sense is a sitting place and gathering. Making room for others, rising, and being raised in rank are concepts supplied by the other verbs and expressions in the same verse.

Understanding J L S thus clarifies the bodily meaning of sitting and shows how Arabic extended the name of a sitting place to the gathering itself and its people, without requiring every social and ethical meaning associated with assemblies to be imposed on the root.
