The Qur’anic Root Ḥ F R

Qur’anic and lexical forms

From digging the ground and making a pit to returning to an original state

The Arabic root ح ف ر concerns digging the ground, removing earth, and creating a hollow space. حَفَرَ means digging; حُفْرَة denotes a pit or depression produced by digging. Within the same family, حَفِير denotes a pit or excavated place, and حَافِر the hoof of certain quadrupeds.

The root also has a usage distinct from directly digging the ground. رَجَعَ عَلَى حَافِرَتِهِ means returning by the same way one came. In some expressions, al-ḥāfirah likewise concerns the beginning of a situation or an original state, as though someone has returned to the point from which they set out.

The Qur’an uses the root only twice. Āl ʿImrān 3:103 uses حُفْرَة in the image of standing on the brink of a pit of fire. Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10 uses الْحَافِرَة in the speech of people expressing disbelief at returning to life after death.

Qur’anic forms: حُفْرَةٍ and الْحَافِرَةِ.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the two nominal forms of Ḥ F R

Forms such as حَفَرَ, يَحْفِرُ, حَفْر, حَفِير, حَفِيرَة, حَافِر, حَوَافِر, احْتَفَرَ, and مِحْفَار are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

1. Semantic principles and material usages

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two principles for ح ف ر: digging or extracting downward, and أَوَّلُ الْأَمْر, the beginning of a matter. The second branch appears in expressions concerning a return to an original state.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: digging and a matter’s beginning as two principles

In the first branch, حَفَرَ describes removing earth or other material from its place, producing a hollow or empty space.

The meaning has clear everyday applications: digging a well for water, a defensive trench, a channel for water, or a burial pit.

The action of digging, the material removed, and the resulting space must be distinguished in these usages.

Ḥafara: digging and removing material

حَفَرَ الْأَرْضَ means digging the ground. Part of the earth is removed, producing a depression.

حَفَرَ الْبِئْرَ likewise means digging a well.

احْتَفَرَ is recorded for digging or providing an excavated place. In some usages, it does not differ substantially from ḥafara.

مِحْفَار names an implement used for digging.

The shared feature is removing material from its place and consequently changing the location’s former shape or condition.

Ḥafara alone nevertheless does not establish the purpose. Digging may serve water access, defense, construction, or burial; the verb’s object and context identify that purpose.

Ḥufrah: a hollow space

حُفْرَة denotes a pit produced by digging. Its plural is حُفَر.

Al-ʿAyn also explains حَفِيرَة as a pit or excavated space in the ground.

Ḥufrah names the resulting space rather than the act of digging itself.

A well dug for water, a defensive trench, and a pit in the ground can belong to a shared semantic field of shapes, although their functions differ.

Nor does ordinary ḥufrah necessarily imply a very deep or dangerous pit. Context supplies its size, depth, and danger.

A shallow pit may be made for a useful purpose, for example, while a deep one may present a risk of falling.

Ḥufrah therefore does not itself mean destruction, punishment, or Hell. Such concepts arise only in particular usages with appropriate evidence.

Ḥafr, ḥafar, and ḥafīr: digging and an excavated place

Ḥafr, with a sukūn on fāʾ, is a verbal noun of digging. Ḥafar, with a fatḥah on fāʾ, is recorded for removed earth and an excavated place. Some reports also transmit the unvowelled-middle form, but the different vocalizations must not be overlooked.

Al-ʿAyn and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ explain ḥafar, with a fatḥah on fāʾ, in connection with a trench or well.

A hemistich transmitted in lexical sources reads:

قَالُوا انْتَهَيْنَا وَهَذَا الْخَنْدَقُ الْحَفَرُ

qālū intahaynā wa-hādhā al-khandaqu al-ḥafaru

Meaning: They said, “We have reached the end, and this is the excavated trench.”

Al-ḥafar here describes or names the trench that has been dug.

The hemistich’s precise attribution and date are unclear, so it cannot be presented as certain pre-Islamic evidence without further investigation. Its value is in attesting ḥafar for an excavated place.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: ḥafr, ḥafar, and the trench witness

Ḥafīr and ḥafīrah likewise denote a pit, well, or excavated place. In some usages, ḥafīr can refer to a grave, which is also prepared by digging.

This does not mean every ḥufrah or ḥafīr should be translated as a grave.

Ḥāfir: an animal’s hoof

حَافِر names the hard part of certain quadrupeds’ feet, such as a horse’s hoof. Its plural is حَوَافِر.

Ordinarily, it concerns horses and animals such as donkeys and mules.

Its connection with digging is understandable: a hoof can move earth or leave an impression when moving and striking the ground.

This explains a semantic connection, but should not become a certain account of the name’s historical origin without evidence.

Arabic also sometimes applies al-ḥāfir to the hoofed animal itself.

This matters in expressions concerning horse trading and returning along an original route, since al-ḥāfirah within the family acquired a broader sense.

Other usages

Lexical works record حَفَر for certain conditions and deposits affecting teeth and gums. Some usages of حَفَرَ also concern making lean or wearing down.

Early sources transmit, for example, an expression describing pregnancy as a cause of leanness in certain animals.

أَحْفَرَ الْمُهْرُ concerns a stage of a foal’s growth when milk teeth fall out and others replace them.

These Arabic usages do not occur in the Qur’an. They should not all be explained solely through digging earth or assigned one certain derivational relationship.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: hooves, dental usages, and a matter’s beginning

2. Al-ḥāfirah: returning to the beginning

The second branch appears in expressions about returning to a starting point or former condition.

Arabic records:

رَجَعَ عَلَى حَافِرَتِهِ

rajaʿa ʿalā ḥāfiratihi

Meaning: He returned by the same way he had come.

Ḥāfirah here does not denote a pit itself. It concerns returning to a route or condition that existed before.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains that the expression fits returning by the same way one came; returning by a different way is not necessarily described by it.

Al-ḥāfirah is also recognized for an original state or the beginning of a matter through this usage.

Returning to a former condition

A verse transmitted by Ibn al-Aʿrābī reads:

أَحَافِرَةً عَلَى صَلَعٍ وَشَيْبٍ؟
مَعَاذَ اللَّهِ مِنْ سَفَهٍ وَعَارِ

a-ḥāfiratan ʿalā ṣalaʿin wa-shaybin / maʿādha allāhi min safahin wa-ʿāri

Meaning: With baldness and gray hair, should I return to youthful ways? I seek refuge in God from foolishness and disgrace.

The speaker discusses returning to youthful conduct after reaching old age.

Ḥāfirah here does not mean a pit, grave, or hoof, but a return to a former condition and conduct.

The verse’s wording varies. Al-Ṭabarī gives the second hemistich with مِنْ سَفَهٍ وَطَيْشٍ, whereas Lisān al-ʿArab and some other sources record مِنْ سَفَهٍ وَعَارِ.

The poet’s identity and the composition’s date are also uncertain. It can therefore be used as a transmitted literary witness, but should not confidently be dated to pre-Islamic times.

Al-Ṭabarī: the ḥāfirah witness, ʿār versus ṭaysh, and uncertain attribution

It matters because Arabic uses ḥāfirah for returning to a former condition, helping to explain Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10.

Al-ḥāfirah and the beginning of a matter

عِنْدَ الْحَافِرَةِ is also recorded for a matter’s beginning or its very first moment.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ explains الْتَقَى الْقَوْمُ عِنْدَ الْحَافِرَةِ as a group meeting at the start of an encounter.

Arabic also records the proverb النَّقْدُ عِنْدَ الْحَافِرِ or النَّقْدُ عِنْدَ الْحَافِرَةِ.

One lexical explanation connects it with trading horses: the price should be paid at the transaction itself rather than deferred.

Another connects al-ḥāfirah with a race’s first stage or the beginning of a matter.

These usages extend to performing something at its outset without delay.

Accounts differ over the proverb’s exact origin and its connection with a horse’s hoof. None should be declared the certain source of every usage of al-ḥāfirah without evidence.

For understanding the Qur’an, the important point is its attested usage for a beginning or original state.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: a former route and the payment proverb

Must the two branches have one origin?

Digging the ground and returning to a beginning initially appear different meanings.

The return expression may have developed through hoofprints, retracing a route, or former footprints. Lexical sources also propose such explanations.

Ibn Fāris nevertheless records the two fields as separate principles.

A desire for one unified image must not produce a certain claim that returning to original life developed directly from digging a pit or grave.

A possible connection through hooves and a return route can be investigated, but al-ḥāfirah’s established idiomatic meaning should be understood separately from hypotheses about its historical origin.

3. “ʿAlā shafā ḥufratin mina al-nāri”: Āl ʿImrān 3:103

وَاعْتَصِمُوا بِحَبْلِ اللَّهِ جَمِيعًا وَلَا تَفَرَّقُوا وَاذْكُرُوا نِعْمَتَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ إِذْ كُنْتُمْ أَعْدَاءً فَأَلَّفَ بَيْنَ قُلُوبِكُمْ فَأَصْبَحْتُمْ بِنِعْمَتِهِ إِخْوَانًا وَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ فَأَنْقَذَكُمْ مِنْهَا كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ آيَاتِهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ

wa-ʿtaṣimū bi-ḥabli allāhi jamīʿan wa-lā tafarraqū wa-dhkurū niʿmata allāhi ʿalaykum idh kuntum aʿdāʾan fa-allafa bayna qulūbikum fa-aṣbaḥtum bi-niʿmatihi ikhwānan wa-kuntum ʿalā shafā ḥufratin mina al-nāri fa-anqadhakum minhā ka-dhālika yubayyinu allāhu lakum āyātihi laʿallakum tahtadūna

Translation: Hold fast together to God’s rope and do not divide. Remember God’s blessing upon you: when you were enemies, He brought your hearts together, and through His blessing you became brothers. You were on the brink of a pit of fire, and He rescued you from it. Thus God makes His signs clear to you, so that you may be guided.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

حُفْرَةٍ is an indefinite noun in a genitive possessive construction after شَفَا.

Shafā concerns the pit’s edge or brink here, between firm ground and the hollow space.

عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ creates a tangible image: someone on a pit’s brink, exposed to falling inside.

مِنَ النَّارِ intensifies the danger. The pit is associated with fire, and falling into it would have grave consequences.

Direct meaning and the verse’s image

Ḥufrah retains its direct meaning of a pit here.

The sentence as a whole, however, occurs in a context about people’s condition and relationships.

Before the pit image, the verse mentions the addressees’ hostility and their transformation into brothers joined by affection.

This sequence shows the brink image explaining a dangerous former condition.

Ḥufrah does not itself mean hostility, division, or disbelief. Those concepts emerge from the verse’s context.

Replacing it merely with “destruction” loses the pit’s image. Treating the verse solely as a report of people standing beside a physical pit overlooks its figurative contextual role.

A precise translation should preserve the image, with commentary explaining its contextual significance.

The brink and danger’s proximity

عَلَىٰ شَفَا is particularly significant.

The verse places the addressees on the brink rather than inside the pit.

It thus describes proximity to a danger into which one could fall.

فَأَنْقَذَكُمْ مِنْهَا follows: God rescued you from it.

Anqadha belongs to another root and means rescuing from danger. Its meaning should not be incorporated into ḥufrah’s definition.

Minhā likewise refers back to the pit and the danger pictured. The sentence forms a coherent image: the pit’s brink, the danger of falling, and rescue.

The image can explain the addressees’ human and social circumstances, but the verse does not detail every historical event of that hostility.

Does the pit of fire concern only afterlife punishment?

Early exegetical explanations often connect ḥufratin mina al-nār with the danger of falling into the fire of the Hereafter.

This reading fits al-nār in Qur’anic warnings.

The verse nevertheless first mentions hostility and hearts brought together in affection, so the addressees’ social condition is also present in the image.

Hostility and division can lead to harmful conduct and dangerous consequences, but not every war or disagreement should be identified with the verse’s fire without independent evidence.

Nor should the image be reduced solely to a political or social problem, excluding a possible afterlife reference.

The verse combines hostility, affection, danger, and rescue to portray the addressees’ transformation. Specifying every worldly and afterlife consequence goes beyond ḥufrah’s own meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī, Āl ʿImrān 3:103: the pit’s brink, fire’s danger, and rescue

4. “A-innā la-mardūdūna fī al-ḥāfirati”: Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10

يَقُولُونَ أَإِنَّا لَمَرْدُودُونَ فِي الْحَافِرَةِ

yaqūlūna a-innā la-mardūdūna fī al-ḥāfirati

Translation: They say, “Will we really be returned to the original state?”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10

الْحَافِرَةِ is a definite noun made genitive by فِي.

Mardūdūna fī al-ḥāfirati occurs in the speech of people expressing astonishment and disbelief at returning after death.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11 continues their question: will we return when we have become decayed bones?

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:12 calls that return a loss.

The context therefore indicates a return to life after death.

The first reading: an original state

Al-ʿAyn explains al-ḥāfirah through returning to a beginning or original state.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah likewise records this as one of the family’s two principles.

Al-Farrāʾ, an early lexicographer and grammarian, explains the Qur’anic phrase as returning to the original condition and life.

This also fits the context, which concerns life after death.

On this reading, al-ḥāfirah does not name the act of returning itself, but the state to which a return is contemplated.

مَرْدُودُونَ is a plural passive participle from رَدَّ, rather than a verb. It expresses being returned; فِي الْحَافِرَةِ identifies the intended state.

The distinction matters. Translating al-ḥāfirah as “return” merges the passive participle’s meaning with its complement.

“To the original state” or “to former life” keeps the relationship clearer.

Ibn Qutaybah: al-ḥāfirah as the original condition

Does the original state mean repeating worldly life?

Al-Nāziʿāt discusses returning to life after death.

Al-ḥāfirah alone does not establish a repetition of the same worldly life in all its former detail.

It concerns the original condition of being alive, without necessarily repeating the exact time, place, age, relationships, and circumstances of the former life.

The Qur’an also expresses returning after death through words from baʿth, nushūr, and iḥyāʾ. Each family contributes its own meaning.

Al-ḥāfirah here should therefore not name an endless cycle of reincarnation or repeated returns to worldly lives.

The verse concerns deniers questioning life after death’s possibility. Details of life after resurrection must be investigated through the relevant verses rather than this word alone.

The second reading: excavated earth and the grave

Some early explanations connect al-ḥāfirah with excavated earth or a grave’s location.

On this reading, it can concern earth dug for burial, approaching maḥfūrah, something excavated.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits the excavated-earth and grave interpretation. The same reports include Mujāhid’s explanation of being raised as a new creation. Different formulations of this reading must therefore be distinguished.

The reading is understandable through the root’s connection with digging, but must be considered alongside the next verse’s question.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11 discusses returning after bones have decayed. The main subject is life after death rather than merely returning a body to a grave.

Not every grave reading should be understood as simply reburying a body. Al-Qurṭubī explains it as being returned alive in the graves. An objection based on burial’s ordinary occurrence after death therefore does not refute that form of the interpretation.

Al-Qurṭubī: the grave reading explained as returning alive in the graves

“The original state” nevertheless remains preferable for the base translation: it has support in Arabic idiom and fits resurrection and the successive questions. This preference does not imply that other readings do not exist.

The conclusion does not deny the lexical connection between ḥāfirah and excavated earth. The disagreement concerns which meaning to prefer here.

The third reading: al-ḥāfirah as fire

Some exegetical reports identify al-ḥāfirah as a name of the fire.

Its lexical and contextual support is nevertheless less clear than that of returning to an original state.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11 concern returning to life and revival after bones have decayed.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:12 likewise refers to that return.

Translating al-ḥāfirah as fire here therefore requires independent evidence not clearly supplied by the expression itself.

It is consequently not preferred for the base translation.

Al-Ṭabarī: life, excavated earth, and Ibn Zayd’s fire interpretation

Al-ḥāfirah and al-karrah

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:12 states:

قَالُوا تِلْكَ إِذًا كَرَّةٌ خَاسِرَةٌ

qālū tilka idhan karratun khāsiratun

Translation: They said, “That, then, would be a losing return.”

كَرَّة comes from ك ر ر and concerns returning and repetition.

It shares a semantic setting with al-ḥāfirah, but differs in root and function.

Al-ḥāfirah in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10 identifies the original state to which a return is contemplated; karrah in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:12 names the return itself.

Their association supports the reading of returning to life after death.

They should not thereby be considered complete synonyms. One identifies the intended condition; the other names the act or event of return.

Study of K R R

5. Differences from related roots

Ḥ F R (ḥafara) and N Q B (naqaba)

In tangible usage, N Q B concerns piercing, opening, and making a passage or opening through something.

Ḥ F R more often concerns digging and removing material to create a pit or hollow space.

Digging a well clearly belongs to ḥafr, whereas making a hole or passage in a barrier can be expressed through N Q B.

The distinction is not absolute, and some usages overlap. The roots should nevertheless not be assumed synonymous without investigation.

Ḥ F R (ḥafara) and Q B R (qabara)

Familiar Q B R usage concerns burial and a person’s burial place.

Ḥ F R concerns digging and the resulting space.

A grave may be made by digging, but not every pit is a grave. Nor are burial and digging the same action.

Ḥufrah in Āl ʿImrān 3:103 cannot be translated as grave merely through its lexical connection with digging.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10, too, grave is one transmitted reading that must be considered within resurrection’s context.

Ḥ F R (ḥafara) and R J ʿ (rajaʿa)

R J ʿ concerns returning and can describe a person, speech, a condition, or other events.

Al-ḥāfirah in return usages emphasizes an original condition or former route.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10, mardūdūna itself is a plural passive participle from ر د د expressing being returned; al-ḥāfirah identifies the intended condition.

The general meaning of return should therefore not be transferred to every derivative of ح ف ر.

Study of R J ʿ

Ḥ F R (ḥafara) and B ʿ Th (baʿatha)

B ʿ Th also occurs in the Qur’an for raising and bringing to life after death.

Al-ḥāfirah in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10 occurs in this context, but does not belong to baʿth’s root.

Baʿth can express raising or bringing forth anew, whereas al-ḥāfirah, in the preferred reading, identifies the intended original condition.

It should therefore not simply be translated as “the Resurrection.” That concept emerges from context rather than the word’s direct meaning.

Study of B ʿ Th

Ḥ F R (ḥafara) and Ḥ F Ẓ (ḥafiẓa)

Ḥifẓ concerns keeping and protecting. Ḥ F R concerns digging the ground and, in another branch, returning to a beginning.

The roots share only their first two letters and have different final letters.

A trench may be dug to protect a city, but that purpose comes from the activity’s context. Ḥafr does not itself mean protection.

Study of Ḥ F Ẓ

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Āl ʿImrān 3:103, حُفْرَةٍ means a pit.

The expression can appropriately be translated:

“You were on the brink of a pit of fire, and He rescued you from it.”

Shafā is rendered as brink and ḥufrah as pit.

This preserves the verse’s tangible image without replacing ḥufrah with hostility, disbelief, or destruction.

Commentary can explain the image’s context of former hostility and rescue.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10, الْحَافِرَةِ cannot be given the same pit meaning.

The following translation is preferable:

“They say, ‘Will we really be returned to the original state?’”

“The original state” fits attested Arabic usage and the deniers’ questions in the following verses.

“Original life” is also possible in commentary, but can suggest a repetition of all details of the previous life.

“The original state” is therefore more precise and less interpretive for the base translation, with return to life after death explained in commentary.

Outside the Qur’an, حَفَرَ means “he dug,” حُفْرَة “a pit,” حَفِير “an excavated place,” حَافِر “a hoof,” احْتَفَرَ “digging,” and رَجَعَ عَلَى حَافِرَتِهِ “he returned by the same route.”

Conclusion

The Arabic root ح ف ر has two main semantic fields.

The first concerns digging the ground and producing a hollow space. It includes ḥafr for digging, ḥufrah and ḥafīr for pits or excavated places, and other derivatives for implements and circumstances of digging.

The second concerns returning to a matter’s beginning or original condition. Expressions such as rajaʿa ʿalā ḥāfiratihi demonstrate this usage; al-ḥāfirah can identify an original state or starting point.

Both fields appear in the Qur’an.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:103, ḥufrah creates the image of a pit of fire on whose brink the addressees stand. The context concerns hostility, affection, and rescue.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10, al-ḥāfirah occurs in the speech of resurrection’s deniers. In light of lexical usage and the following verses, it concerns an original condition and returning to life after death.

Brief root meaning: Digging and removing material to create a hollow space; in another branch, the beginning of a condition and returning to it. Connections through hooves, routes, and retracing a former position may be investigated, but should not be declared the source of every meaning without certain historical evidence.

Preserving the distinction is essential in Qur’anic translation. Ḥufrah means a pit, but al-ḥāfirah in Al-Nāziʿāt is not restricted to a pit or grave. Its usage for returning to an original state shows that each derivative must be understood in its own language and context, rather than placing the entire family under one material image.
