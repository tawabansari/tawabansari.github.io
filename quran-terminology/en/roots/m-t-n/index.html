The root م ت ن — Firmness, solidity, and enduring strength

Qur’anic and lexical forms

مَتِينٌ
(matīnun)
Firm; describing divine design

الْمَتِينُ
(al-matīnu)
Firm in strength; describing God

From the back and firm ground to unfailing power and a firmly established design

The Arabic root م ت ن concerns firmness, hardness, solidity, and resistance. Material uses include the back, hard elevated ground, and strongly constructed objects. More broadly, matīn describes power, judgment, a design, or an undertaking possessing firmness and sufficient strength.

In the Qur’an the root appears only as matīn: twice describing God’s design and once as al-matīn, describing God Himself.

The distinction matters. A matīn design is firm and powerful; al-matīn as a divine description attributes firmness and perfect power to God Himself.

The root should not be reduced to bodily force, stone-like hardness, or intense exertion. Just as a building or rope may be strong, a design may possess a firmness not readily broken by opposition.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Classical matn denotes the back or a firm, prominent part of something.

Matnu al-ẓahri concerns the back; for people and animals it also denotes the muscular portions on either side of the spine.

Matnu al-arḍi describes ground higher, harder, or firmer than its surroundings.

Matnu al-sahmi and matnu al-rumḥi are recorded for the middle portion of an arrow or spear.

Thus matn is not restricted to the human back: an exposed, middle, prominent, or firm part of something can bear the name.

A verse by al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥilliza

The well-known pre-Islamic poet al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥilliza says:

«أَنَّى اهْتَدَيْتِ وَكُنْتِ غَيْرَ رَجِيلَةٍ
وَالْقَوْمُ قَدْ قَطَعُوا مِتَانَ السَّجْسَجِ»

Annā ihtadayti wa-kunti ghayra rajīlatin / wa-al-qawmu qad qaṭaʿū mitāna al-sajsaji.

“How did you find the way, though unaccustomed to traveling on foot, when the company had already crossed the firm rises of the broad terrain?”

Mitān is the plural of matn.

The terrain crossed by the travelers is described by its firmness and elevation.

Lexical explanations associate al-sajsaj with broad ground or terrain intermediate between hard and soft.

Mitān here names parts of the terrain, rather than a human or animal back.

The usage connects the family with firm, prominent natural surfaces in the pre-Qur’anic poetic tradition.

The verse is recorded in the poet’s attributed collection and early lexicons. Its pre-Islamic attribution is established in the tradition, although the exact date of this particular verse is not independently known.

A verse by Imruʾ al-Qays

Describing a powerful horse, Imruʾ al-Qays says:

«لَهَا مَتْنَتَانِ خَظَاتَا كَمَا
أَكَبَّ عَلَى سَاعِدَيْهِ النَّمِرْ»

Lahā matnatāni khaẓātā kamā / akabba ʿalā sāʿidayhi al-namir.

“It has two fleshy, prominent sides of the back, like a leopard leaning down on its forelegs.”

Matnatāni denotes the horse’s two back portions.

Khaẓātā describes their fleshy prominence. Versions differ in this form’s wording, but the reference of matnatāni to the back is clear.

The picture highlights muscular strength and prominence.

Matn itself names the body part; the accompanying description supplies its fleshiness.

Not every matn therefore means an exceptionally strong muscle. The noun can name a part, with its qualities supplied separately.

Together, these poems illustrate back portions and hard, elevated ground as two material applications.

Matāna: hardness and solidity

Matuna al-shayʾu matānatan means that something became, or was, hard, strong, and firm.

Matīn describes something possessing solidity and strength.

Watarun matīnun denotes a strong, resistant string or bowstring.

Jildun matīnun describes tough, durable leather.

Mattana al-shayʾa means strengthening or making something firm.

Lexicons connect derivatives with everyday activities such as strengthening bowstrings, preparing leather waterskins, and securing tent fittings.

Imagine a rope whose strands have been properly twisted and secured. It resists tension and does not readily break apart.

This illustrates matāna.

It does not prove that pre-Islamic Arabic first coined matīn for rope and later extended it to power and design. Historical priority requires separate evidence.

Firmness and resistance are clearly central classical features, while early poetry supports the material noun matn.

2. From material structure to power and design

Matīn is not restricted to hard objects.

Raʾyun matīnun denotes a firm, well-supported judgment.

Shiʿrun matīnun can describe poetry with strong language, reasoning, or composition.

These concern qualities resisting weakness and collapse, rather than bodily hardness.

An opinion based only on passing emotions or unsupported claims may fail at the first serious question.

One based on knowledge, careful inquiry, and sound reasons is not overturned by a simple objection.

Such a judgment can be called matīn.

Firmness does not prohibit revision when new evidence appears. Sound reasoning differs from stubborn refusal of truth.

Nor does confidently stating an opinion make it well-founded: personal confidence and actual argumentative strength differ.

Mumātana is also recorded for competition, contest, and extending an undertaking or journey over a long distance.

Sayrun mumātinun describes long or strenuous travel.

This branch involves endurance and continued movement, but its every feature should not be imported into Qur’anic matīn.

Modern Persian matānat commonly denotes dignity, composure, and measured behavior. Although connected with firmness of character, that later social sense should not be imposed on the Qur’an.

Inna kaydī matīnun does not call God’s design socially “polite and dignified”: it asserts its strength and firmness.

3. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

The root occurs three times in one principal form:

  • Matīnun — al-Aʿrāf 7:183: describing God’s design.
  • Matīnun — al-Qalam 68:45: the same description in an identical expression.
  • Al-matīnu — al-Dhāriyāt 51:58: describing God alongside His provision and power.

All three belong to matuna, being strong and firm.

Matīn follows the faʿīl pattern and describes someone or something possessing firmness and power.

In al-Aʿrāf and al-Qalam it is indefinite and nominative.

In al-Dhāriyāt it has the definite article and is read al-matīnu.

Matn, mutūn, mitān, matāna, matuna, mattana, tamtīn, and mumātana are known Arabic forms but do not occur as such in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an uses only the description matīn from this family.

Its Qur’anic applications concern divine power and design, rather than a bodily back, hard ground, or material implement.

4. Matīn: the firmness of divine design

Al-Aʿrāf 7:183

«وَأُمْلِي لَهُمْ إِنَّ كَيْدِي مَتِينٌ»

Wa-umlī lahum inna kaydī matīn.

“And I grant them respite; indeed, My design is firm.”

Matīnun describes kaydī, “My design.”

Kayd here concerns a counter-design directed toward those opposing and denying the signs.

Matīnun expresses its strength and firmness.

The preceding verse

Al-Aʿrāf 7:182 describes those denying God’s signs being led onward gradually from where they do not know.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:183 follows with respite and the firmness of divine design.

The connection matters.

People may continue opposing truth without immediately experiencing visible consequences.

They may retain power, wealth, or opportunities and assume that the absence of an obstacle proves their course right.

No immediate punishment does not mean no accountability or divine inability.

Wa-umlī lahum expresses respite, while inna kaydī matīnun emphasizes the firm design.

Respite and inability differ

A person may fail to respond to oppression because of inability.

Another may have the ability but deliberately choose to act later.

Both look similar in the absence of immediate action.

Their reasons for delay differ.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:183 combines respite with firm design, so delay should not imply weakness or loss of divine ability.

Matīn describes a design whose strength is not lost through time or continued human opposition.

The root itself does not specify when that design will be realized or the form of punishment.

Firmness and the timing of consequences are separate questions.

Does matīn mean harsh or violent?

Matīn relates to strength and intensity, but does not invariably mean violence or sudden impact.

God speaks here of granting respite.

Firm design thus accompanies delayed action.

Translating only “harsh” or “severe” loses some of the firmness and strength.

Nor does the description make God’s design equivalent to human deceit.

Kayd can describe devising a countermeasure. Its quality and value depend on agent, purpose, and context.

Here it is attributed to God against those denying His signs.

Lying, unjust deception, and violating others’ rights should not be transferred from blameworthy human plotting to divine action without evidence.

Matīn concerns the design’s firmness, not every detail of its execution.

5. The repeated expression in al-Qalam

Al-Qalam 68:45

«وَأُمْلِي لَهُمْ إِنَّ كَيْدِي مَتِينٌ»

Wa-umlī lahum inna kaydī matīn.

“And I grant them respite; indeed, My design is firm.”

The Arabic text is identical to al-Aʿrāf 7:183.

Its context nevertheless merits independent attention.

Al-Qalam 68:44 instructs Muhammad to leave those denying the divine discourse to God, then describes their being led onward gradually from where they do not know.

Al-Qalam 68:45 repeats respite and firmness.

Both passages combine two concepts:

The gradual leading onward of those denying the signs, and a respite that may mislead them about their condition.

Having time does not necessarily validate someone’s conduct.

This does not authorize hasty judgments about individuals’ lives.

A righteous person may live comfortably, while a wrongdoer may also enjoy abundant resources. Wealth, health, success, or longevity alone cannot establish reward or a punitive divine design.

These passages specifically concern those denying divine signs and opposing truth.

That contextual restriction must remain.

Firm design and passing time

In both verses, inna kaydī matīnun follows wa-umlī lahum.

Granting respite is thus compatible with firm design.

A person may see only immediate results and judge success or failure accordingly.

These verses do not restrict divine power to immediate outcomes.

Matīn retains its sense of strength and firmness not undone by passing time.

This should not become a theory that every delay in life signals a hidden punishment being prepared.

Firmness of design alone pronounces no verdict on a particular individual’s fate.

6. Al-matīn as a description of God

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:58

«إِنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ الرَّزَّاقُ ذُو الْقُوَّةِ الْمَتِينُ»

Inna Allāha huwa al-razzāqu dhū al-quwwati al-matīn.

“Indeed, The God Himself is the Provider, the Possessor of power, firm in strength.”

Al-matīnu describes God.

This differs from the previous two occurrences.

In al-Aʿrāf and al-Qalam the design is matīn.

In al-Dhāriyāt God Himself bears the description.

Its connection with provision

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:56 concerns the purpose of creating jinn and humankind.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:57 states that God seeks neither provision nor feeding from them.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:58 then identifies God as Provider and Possessor of power.

The sequence shows that divine provision does not arise from God’s need for creatures.

People need food, water, air, security, and other resources, and may depend on one another to obtain them.

God does not present Himself here as needing food or sustenance from creation.

He instead provides and possesses power.

Al-matīnu emphasizes the firmness and completeness of that power.

Providing for creatures does not wear out God’s power or make it dependent on an external force.

This conclusion arises from the description together with provision and divine independence; the root itself does not always mean providing sustenance.

Al-razzāq, dhū al-quwwa, and al-matīn

The verse contains three related but distinct expressions.

Al-razzāq, from ر ز ق, concerns divine provision.

Dhū al-quwwa conveys possession of power.

Al-matīn attributes firmness and strength to God.

They are not exact synonyms.

Provision concerns giving sustenance to creatures.

Power concerns ability to act and bring something about.

Matīn emphasizes firmness and freedom from weakness in that power.

No artificial stages should be invented: the verse does not make provision the first stage of power and matāna its final stage. It joins related descriptions in one sentence.

Together they illuminate divine provision and authority.

The grammar of al-matīnu

In the usual reading al-matīnu is nominative.

Al-quwwati is genitive and grammatically feminine.

As an ordinary direct adjective of al-quwwa, matīn would be expected to agree with it in case and gender.

In the usual reading it is analyzed as describing God, or in relation to al-razzāq and dhū al-quwwa.

The verse therefore does more than say God possesses a strong power: God Himself is al-matīn.

Some less common readings report genitive al-matīni, with analyses relating it to al-quwwa. The reading examined here is nominative and refers the description to God Himself.

This distinction should not be erased by arbitrarily altering the verse or adding an absent adjective.

Is divine matāna bodily?

Material uses of the root concern the back, hard ground, and firm structures.

They do not imply that describing God as al-matīn gives Him a back, muscles, bones, or bodily construction.

Words can describe both material objects and nonmaterial qualities.

A building and an argument can both be strong, without an argument being built of stone and iron.

The divine description concerns power and firmness rather than bodily structure.

Qāf 50:38 also states that creating the heavens, earth, and what lies between did not bring God weariness.

This accords with firm divine power, but freedom from weariness has that separate textual support; matīn alone does not specify every aspect of divine action.

Is al-matīn a divine name?

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:58 explicitly describes God as al-matīnu.

The description thus has direct Qur’anic support.

The actual verse form should be distinguished from later technical lists of divine names.

Here it carries the article: al-matīnu.

That form should be preserved.

In al-Aʿrāf 7:183 and al-Qalam 68:45, indefinite matīnun instead describes kayd, not directly a name by which God is called.

The distinction matters for root research.

7. Distinctions from neighboring roots

م ت ن and ق و ي: power and its firmness

ق و ي concerns possessing ability and power.

Matīn often likewise means strong and firm.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:58 illustrates the proximity.

Dhū al-quwwa emphasizes possessing power; al-matīn highlights its firmness and solidity.

This is not an absolute rule that qawī always means mere ability and matīn a higher stage.

Both can describe very great strength.

Their placement together clarifies the distinction in this verse.

م ت ن and ش د د: solidity and intensity

ش د د concerns intensity, hardness, firmness, and strengthening.

Shadīd and matīn can overlap in descriptions of force.

Shadīd has a broad range including punishment, pressure, suffering, power, and difficult events.

Qur’anic matīn emphasizes solidity and firmness.

A blow may be intense without proving that the structure delivering it is durable or strong.

Something may conversely be firm and resistant without constantly applying intense pressure.

This helps avoid reducing inna kaydī matīnun to “My design is harsh.”

م ت ن and ث ب ت: firmness and remaining in place

ث ب ت concerns remaining fixed, standing firm, and establishing.

Matīn can likewise describe something not readily weakened.

Thabāt particularly emphasizes remaining in a given condition or position.

Structural matāna emphasizes strength and resistance.

A wall may remain in place for a time despite internal weakness and decay.

A matīn wall has a strong structure.

Staying in place and structural strength are related but not necessarily identical.

Divine design being matīn means more than a decision remaining unchanged: its strength also matters.

م ت ن and ص ل ب: physical hardness and strength

ص ل ب concerns hardness and solidity.

In material contexts, ṣulb and matīn may both describe a strong object.

Strength does not always mean rigidity.

A strong rope can be flexible while resisting tension.

A very hard object may be brittle and crack under sudden impact.

Matāna should therefore not be restricted to physical hardness.

That restriction is also unsuitable for judgment, design, and power, which are not physical objects.

م ت ن and و ث ق: firmness and a secure bond

و ث ق concerns securing, trustworthiness, and firm connections.

Mīthāq and wathīq belong to this family.

Wathāqa and matāna overlap in some uses because both convey firmness.

In covenants and bonds, wathq also emphasizes a secure relationship and commitment.

Matīn has a wider descriptive scope covering structures, force, and designs.

A firm covenant and strong design may be conceptually close while belonging to distinct roots.

8. Interpretation and translation

One difficulty is reducing matīn to “intense.”

That may work in some contexts without fully conveying firmness and durability.

“Firm” suits all three Qur’anic occurrences, applying to both design and power.

In 51:58, however, “firm” alone may not make divine strength sufficiently clear to a Persian reader.

“Powerful and firm” can explain the description there.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:183

«وَأُمْلِي لَهُمْ إِنَّ كَيْدِي مَتِينٌ»

Wa-umlī lahum inna kaydī matīn.

“And I grant them respite; indeed, My design is firm.”

“Firm” conveys matīn.

“Design” is the selected contextual equivalent of kayd; ك ي د requires its own study.

Al-Qalam 68:45

«وَأُمْلِي لَهُمْ إِنَّ كَيْدِي مَتِينٌ»

Wa-umlī lahum inna kaydī matīn.

“And I grant them respite; indeed, My design is firm.”

Identical Arabic permits an identical basic translation.

The different contexts belong in commentary, rather than an unjustified change of lexical meaning.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:58

«إِنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ الرَّزَّاقُ ذُو الْقُوَّةِ الْمَتِينُ»

Inna Allāha huwa al-razzāqu dhū al-quwwati al-matīn.

“Indeed, The God Himself is the Provider, the Possessor of power, firm in strength.”

Al-matīn remains a description of God Himself, rather than being merged with al-quwwa into an invented noun phrase.

A lexical note may add:

“Al-matīnu: possessing firm power and strength free of weakness; here it describes God Himself.”

Other Arabic uses require context-sensitive equivalents.

Watarun matīnun: “a strong string or bowstring.”

Raʾyun matīnun: “a firm, well-reasoned judgment.”

Matnu al-arḍi: a hard, elevated portion of ground.

Matnu al-ẓahri: the back or portions on either side of the spine.

Mattana al-bināʾa: “He strengthened the building.”

None of the Qur’anic occurrences means a bodily back, elevated ground, or building material.

9. Synthesis

م ت ن concerns firmness, hardness, solidity, and resistance.

Material uses include the back, hard elevated terrain, firm portions of objects, and strong structures.

Al-Ḥārith’s mitān describes firm ground; Imruʾ al-Qays’s matnatāni describes the horse’s two back portions.

Classical usage extends to judgment, speech, design, power, and other nonmaterial matters, although the chronological development of all senses is not firmly established.

The Qur’an uses the root three times, only as matīn.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:183 and al-Qalam 68:45 describe God’s design as firm. Respite accompanies this firmness: lack of immediate action does not mean weakness or absence of accountability.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:58 describes God Himself as al-matīn alongside provision and power, emphasizing divine strength and firmness.

Forqan definition: م ت ن principally denotes firmness, solidity, and resistance. Matīn describes someone or something strong and not readily weakened or broken apart. The Qur’an applies it twice to God’s design and once to God Himself.

It should not be reduced to bodily hardness, violent intensity, or unreasonable inflexibility. Firmness can appear in structure, ability, or a considered design; context determines the precise meaning.

The two kayd verses distinguish respite from inability. Al-Dhāriyāt applies the family to divine power that provides for creatures without needing provision from them.

The Qur’anic focus is firmness of power and design, rather than bodily hardness, haste in punishment, or outward displays of force. What is matīn possesses actual strength even when its results are not immediately visible to others.
