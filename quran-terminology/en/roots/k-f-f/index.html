1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root k f f is associated with taking hold, gathering, restraining, preventing advance, and keeping something within a defined limit.

In early Arabic, kaff names the palm of the hand: the part with which a person takes hold of something, retains it, or prevents its movement.

The poetry collection attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma includes this description of a bird:

حَتَّى إِذَا مَا هَوَتْ كَفُّ الْغُلَامِ لَهَا طَارَتْ وَفِي كَفِّهِ مِنْ رِيشِهَا بَتَكُ

When the boy’s hand descended toward it, the bird flew away, leaving pieces of its feathers in his palm.

The verse illustrates the physical use of kaff for the hand or palm. Different transmitted versions vary in some wording, while retaining this meaning of kaff.

Ibn Faris relates the family’s basic meaning to taking hold and gathering, and connects the naming of the palm with its ability to grasp things.

Arabic dictionaries record the following expressions:

  • كَفَّهُ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ: he restrained him from something.
  • كَفَّ عَنِ الْأَمْرِ: he refrained from doing something.
  • كَفَّ يَدَهُ: he held back his hand.
  • كَفَّ الثَّوْبَ: he gathered and stitched the edge of a garment.
  • كُفَّةُ الثَّوْبِ: the hem or edge of a garment.
  • كِفَّةُ الْمِيزَانِ: the pan of a balance.
  • جَاءَ الْقَوْمُ كَافَّةً: all the people came.

The verse attributed to the pre-Islamic period supports the meaning “palm.” The evidence considered here does not establish when every branch of the family arose. Qur’anic usage shows that restraint and collective inclusion were also familiar meanings in the Arabic of the revelation period.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms in Arabic include:

  • كَفَّ ـ يَكُفُّ: to restrain, hold back, or prevent; also to desist.
  • كَفّ: palm; restraint or holding back.
  • كَفَّانِ and كَفَّيْنِ: two palms.
  • أَكُفّ and كُفُوف: plurals of palm.
  • كَافّ: one who restrains or holds back.
  • مَكْفُوف: restrained; also blind.
  • كُفَّة: the edge or border of something.
  • كِفَّة: a scale pan or a round, enclosing object.
  • كَافَّة: all together, collectively, or inclusively.
  • كَفَاف: sufficient means of livelihood to meet a person’s needs and keep them from asking others for help.
  • كَفْكَفَ: to hold back or restrain; in some uses, to wipe away tears.
  • اِنْكَفَّ: to stop or desist.
  • تَكَفَّفَ: to extend one’s hand to others in a request for help.

Qur’anic forms examined:

كُفُّوا ـ يَكُفَّ ـ يَكُفُّوا ـ كَفَّ ـ كَفَفْتُ ـ يَكُفُّونَ ـ كَفَّيْهِ ـ كَافَّةً

All the Qur’anic verbs in this family belong to Form I. The Qur’an also uses the dual of kaff, “palm,” and kaffatan, conveying collective inclusion.

3. The semantic core and its physical image

The family’s central meanings concern taking hold, limiting, restraining, and preventing something from passing beyond a boundary.

The palm provides a tangible image: a person can grasp and retain something with the hand, or stop its movement and advance.

A stitched garment hem also keeps the threads from spreading apart and holds them within a defined edge.

As a verb, kaffa can describe restraining someone else or refraining from an action oneself.

Holding another person’s hand back from causing harm is kaff. A person’s own withdrawal from aggression can also be expressed through this family.

In the branch represented by kaffah, the meaning is inclusion of all members or parts of a group.

Lexicographers have connected this sense with containing a group within a boundary so that none of its members remains outside.

This connection helps explain the family, but does not establish the historical order in which its branches developed.

4. Qur’anic uses

كُفُّوا أَيْدِيَكُمْ: refraining from warfare

Kuffu is the plural imperative of kaffa, and aydiyakum, “your hands,” is its object.

In this context, holding back one’s hands means refraining from armed action. The hand is an instrument of action and power, and restraining it depicts refraining from aggression.

The verse describes two different circumstances: one in which people were instructed to hold back their hands, and another in which fighting was prescribed for them.

Fighting is therefore not a duty in every circumstance, nor does refraining from fighting always mean abandoning responsibility for defending oneself and others.

The value of an action depends on its circumstances, purpose, and the limits set in the Qur’an.

Al-Nisa 4:84 also speaks of restraining the hostile might of those who reject. There, yakuffa concerns curbing their capacity to cause harm, not necessarily destroying them.

يَكُفُّوا أَيْدِيَهُمْ: refraining from aggression

Here, holding back hands appears alongside withdrawing and offering peace.

These three actions are related but not identical. Someone may speak of peace while continuing to attack and cause harm.

Yakuffu aydiyahum emphasizes actually refraining from aggression.

The preceding verse is important for understanding the limits of this passage. Al-Nisa 4:90 states that The God has given no grounds for attacking those who withdraw from fighting the believers, do not fight them, and offer peace.

The ruling in al-Nisa 4:91 addresses particular people and circumstances. It cannot be extended to all people of different beliefs without regard to the verse’s qualifications and context.

فَكَفَّ أَيْدِيَهُمْ عَنْكُمْ: preventing aggression

Two semantic families stand in contrast here: b s tt and k f f.

Extending the hand means reaching out to act and cause harm; restraining the hand means preventing that action.

The group intended to attack, but The God prevented their intention from being realized.

The verse does not say that they were necessarily destroyed or permanently deprived of the ability to cause harm. It describes preventing their aggression in that situation.

In al-Ma’idah 5:110, The God also names His restraint of the Children of Israel from Jesus, when he brought them clear proofs, among His favors to Jesus.

Al-Fath 48:20 likewise includes holding people’s hands back from the believers among divine favors.

These uses show that protection can occur by preventing a danger from materializing, without necessarily destroying its source.

Restraining the hands of both sides

Restraint is not applied only to the opponents here. The God speaks of holding back the hands of both sides from one another.

The qualification “after He had given you victory over them” is significant. The believers’ hands were restrained when they had been enabled to prevail over the other side.

Refraining from continued conflict is therefore not necessarily a sign of weakness or inability. Someone able to prevail may choose not to exercise that power, preventing further harm.

Possessing power and using it are different matters.

The verse attributes the prevention of mutual confrontation and aggression to The God. Preserving human life and preventing bloodshed can thus be expressions of divine help and favor.

يَكُفُّونَ: inability to ward off the Fire

The object of yakuffuna here is the Fire, not a person’s hand.

Qur’anic kaff is therefore not limited to stopping people from acting. It can also describe warding off danger and preventing something harmful from reaching a person.

The context describes people unable to keep the Fire away from themselves.

Their inability is conveyed by the whole sentence and its context; kaffa itself means to hold back or ward off.

كَفَّيْهِ: extending two palms toward water

Kaffayhi is the dual of kaff and means “his two palms.”

The Qur’an uses a tangible image to convey the futility of invoking other objects of worship.

A thirsty person extends his hands toward water, but the water does not reach his mouth.

The outstretched hands express seeking and expecting a result; merely extending them, however, does not achieve it.

Early explanations differ over the image’s details, but in each reading the person seeks water and extends his hands without attaining the desired result.

Kaff here names a body part. The verse’s theological meaning emerges from the comparison and its context as a whole.

يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ: turning the palms in regret

This verse belongs to the account of the owner of two gardens, who had become proud of his possessions and prosperity.

After the destruction of his produce, the Qur’an depicts his remorse through the movement of his two palms.

Yuqallibu, from q l b, describes turning the hands; kaffayhi names the palms themselves.

Regret is understood from his condition, the loss of his property, and his concluding words together.

Kaff does not itself mean regret. That meaning arises from the hand movement and the person’s situation.

5. Kaffah: all together and inclusively

ادْخُلُوا فِي السِّلْمِ كَافَّةً: a collective call to peace

Kaffatan conveys collective inclusion in this verse.

Two familiar grammatical analyses are offered.

In the first, kaffatan is a circumstantial expression referring to the plural subject of udkhulu. All those addressed are called to enter into silm.

In the second, kaffatan is connected with al-silm, expressing entry into its entirety.

Both analyses accord with the family’s sense of inclusion. The first is well supported by other Qur’anic uses of kaffatan and the verse’s plural address.

The Qur’an calls believers collectively into silm. On the reading of silm as peace, peace should not be confined to one people, denomination, or community while aggression and hostility toward others remain open.

This interpretation accords with preceding verses in al-Baqarah that prohibit transgression and define the limits of confronting aggressors, particularly 2:190, 2:193, and 2:194.

In this reading, peace is conveyed by al-silm, while the collective scope of the instruction comes from kaffatan. These two levels of meaning must remain distinct.

كَافَّةً: collective resistance to collective aggression

Kaffatan occurs twice in this verse.

Its repetition on both sides of the sentence depicts collective confrontation.

Kama, “as,” connects the manner of the believers’ response with the manner of their opponents’ fighting.

Grammatically, kaffatan can refer to those doing the fighting in both clauses. Grammatical commentaries also consider attachment to the object, encompassing the opposing group.

Collective inclusion is common to both analyses.

Kaffatan alone does not establish that every associator is subject to a command to fight, regardless of conduct, treaty commitments, peacefulness, or participation in warfare.

In the same surah, al-Tawbah 9:4 requires honoring treaties with those who remain faithful to their commitments, while 9:6 speaks of granting protection to an associator who seeks it and conveying that person to safety.

Kaffatan concerns the manner and scope of the confrontation under discussion; it does not independently determine the conditions and limits of legitimate warfare.

لِيَنْفِرُوا كَافَّةً: dividing responsibilities

This verse offers particularly clear evidence for the meaning of kaffatan.

The first part concerns all the believers setting out; the second concerns a party from each group doing so.

The contrast between kaffatan and ta’ifah shows that the former encompasses all members, while the latter identifies part of the group.

Interpretations differ over the journey’s destination and which group gains understanding of religion. This does not alter the collective meaning of kaffatan.

The verse also indicates a need to divide responsibilities within a community. Everyone need not perform the same task at the same time.

In light of this verse, the collective expression in al-Tawbah 9:36 should not be taken to require every individual believer to participate in every confrontation.

كَافَّةً لِلنَّاسِ: the universal scope of Muhammad’s mission

Kaffatan here expresses the comprehensive scope of Muhammad’s mission.

The Qur’an does not confine his mission to a particular people, race, language, or territory. Its audience is identified as al-nas, humanity.

Several grammatical analyses of kaffatan have been proposed:

One treats it as a circumstantial expression referring to the person addressed in arsalnaka, conveying the comprehensive character of the Messenger’s mission.

Another treats it as a circumstantial expression placed before al-nas, meaning that he was sent to all people.

Others understand it as qualifying an omitted verbal noun: a sending that encompasses humanity.

Despite their grammatical differences, these readings provide clear support for the mission’s universal scope.

Another verse states this meaning:

Jami‘an here confirms the same inclusiveness of the mission’s audience expressed by kaffatan in Saba 34:28.

A universal mission does not mean compelling everyone to accept it. Saba 34:28 describes the Messenger’s commission as bearing good news and giving warning.

A universal audience is compatible with freedom to accept faith, as expressed in al-Baqarah 2:256 and Yunus 10:99.

6. Morphology and grammar

The structure of kaffa

Kaffa is a Form I doubled verb: its second and third root consonants are identical.

In forms such as kaffa and yakuffu, the two fa consonants merge and appear with a shaddah.

In kafaftu in al-Ma’idah 5:110, the attached subject suffix causes the doubling to separate, so both fa consonants appear individually.

Kaffa can be transitive or intransitive:

Kafaftuhu ‘ani al-amr means “I restrained him from the action.”

Kaffa ‘ani al-amr means “he himself refrained from the action.”

In Qur’anic usage, the verb usually has an object specifying what is restrained or warded off.

The structure of kaffayhi

Kaff is a singular noun, grammatically feminine.

Its dual is kaffani in the nominative and kaffayni in the accusative and genitive.

When a possessive pronoun is attached, the dual nun is dropped, producing kaffayhi.

In al-Ra‘d 13:14, it is the genitive complement of the active participle basit.

In al-Kahf 18:42, it is the accusative object of yuqallibu.

The structure of kaffatan

In general Arabic usage, kaffah conveys all members or comprehensive inclusion.

Lexicographers and grammarians offer different accounts of its morphological origin, including analysis as a verbal noun or a form related to the active participle.

In the Qur’an it always occurs as an indefinite accusative.

When expressing collective inclusion, kaffatan does not change with the gender or number of the group’s members. It therefore retains this form when addressing masculine groups.

Its final ta does not make it a description restricted to women.

7. Comparison with related roots

K f f (kaff) and y d y (yad)

Yad is the general word for hand. In extended uses it can signify power, authority, action, or responsibility.

Kaff names the terminal part of the hand, particularly the palm together with the fingers.

Al-Ra‘d 13:14 and al-Kahf 18:42 specifically mention two palms.

Al-Nisa 4:77 and al-Fath 48:24 use aydi for the hands and derivatives of k f f for restraining them.

Thus kaff can name a body part or the act of restraint.

K f f (kaff) and b s tt (bast)

Bast concerns opening, spreading, and extending.

Verbal kaff can stand in contrast to bast.

The contrast is clear in al-Ma’idah 5:11: a group intended to extend their hands, but their hands were restrained.

In al-Ra‘d 13:14, however, kaff names the body part, and the palm itself is extended toward water.

Bast and kaff are not opposites there: one describes extension and the other names the body part.

K f f (kaff) and m n ‘ (man‘)

Man‘ concerns preventing and holding back.

Verbal kaff likewise concerns restraint and overlaps considerably with man‘.

Qur’anic uses of kaff prominently concern restraining harmful movement or action, such as hands, military force, and fire.

Man‘ has a broader range and may also concern withholding property, preventing an action, or blocking access to something.

K f f (kaff) and q b d (qabd)

Qabd concerns taking hold, gathering, and bringing something into one’s possession.

The palm is an instrument with which a person can perform qabd.

Restraining someone’s action does not necessarily require physically seizing them. Their action may be stopped through an agreement, a barrier, or removal of the circumstances enabling aggression.

Qabd and kaff overlap in grasping and limiting, but are not interchangeable in every use.

K f f (kaffah) and j m ‘ (jami‘)

Jami‘ and kaffah can both encompass all members of a group.

Saba 34:28 expresses Muhammad’s universal mission with kaffatan; al-A‘raf 7:158 expresses it with jami‘an.

Their semantic closeness in these contexts is clear.

Jami‘ belongs to the family of gathering and assembly, while kaffah belongs to a family that also includes restraint, limitation, and containment.

K f f (kaffah) and k l l (kull)

Kull conveys the entirety of a group or inclusion of all its members.

Kaffatan also conveys inclusion, but in Qur’anic usage it commonly functions as a circumstantial expression specifying how an action occurs or how comprehensively it applies.

Al-Tawbah 9:122, for example, concerns all the believers setting out.

Both families can be translated with words such as “all,” although their Arabic grammatical structures differ.

K f f (kaff) and k f y (kifayah)

These families resemble one another in sound and spelling but are distinct roots.

Kaffa concerns restraining, holding back, and desisting.

Kafa concerns being sufficient and meeting a need.

Kafaf, from k f f, describes means of livelihood sufficient to keep someone from requesting help from others. Its result is close to sufficiency.

That semantic proximity does not make the two roots identical.

In verbal uses, “restrain” or “hold back” preserves the sense of preventing movement, action, or harm.

Nominal uses require distinguishing the palm from collective inclusion.

“Completely” can be defensible under some grammatical analyses, but where all members of a group are intended, “all together” is clearer and more natural.

Conclusion

The Arabic root k f f concerns taking hold, gathering, restraining, holding back, and encompassing.

The Qur’an uses this family in three semantic branches:

First, restraining and preventing aggression, harm, or advance.

Second, the palm as a part of the human body.

Third, collective inclusion, expressed by kaffatan.

Uses involving restraint of the hands show that curbing power and preventing aggression can be divine favors and forms of help. Possessing power does not by itself make its exercise necessary.

Uses of kaffatan concern the entirety and inclusiveness of a group: a collective call to peace, the division of social responsibilities, and Muhammad’s mission to all humanity.

These branches connect with the general ideas of grasping, limiting, and encompassing, but each derivative’s precise meaning must be understood through its form and context.
