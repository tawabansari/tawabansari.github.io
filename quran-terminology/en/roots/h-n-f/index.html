The Qur’anic Root Ḥ N F

Ḥanīf: turning toward The God alone and away from shirk, with attention to inclination and the limits of historical evidence

Arabic lexical accounts relate the root Ḥ N F to inclination and orientation. In the Qur’an, ḥanīf (حَنِيف) describes someone whose religious orientation is toward The God and away from shirk—associating partners with Him. Abraham is repeatedly described this way, but the word is not restricted to him. Muhammad is commanded to set his face toward the religion as a ḥanīf, and two verses use the plural ḥunafāʾ (حُنَفَاء).

Ḥanīf is often translated as “monotheist,” “upright in faith,” or “single-minded.” Each conveys part of its Qur’anic meaning. A more precise understanding requires two distinct inquiries: what meanings the Arabic root has carried, and how the Qur’an uses the word in particular sentences, alongside other descriptions, and in contrast with particular beliefs.

All Qur’anic occurrences belong to two nominal forms: ḥanīfan (حَنِيفًا), singular, occurring ten times; and ḥunafāʾa (حُنَفَاءَ), the plural of ḥanīf, occurring twice. Arabic also has forms such as ḥanaf (حَنَف), aḥnaf (أَحْنَف), taḥannuf (تَحَنُّف), and ḥanīfiyyah (حَنِيفِيَّة), but these forms do not occur in the Qur’an. Nor does a verb from this root occur. The twelve occurrences appear in twelve verses. Ḥanīf follows the pattern faʿīl (فَعِيل), and ḥunafāʾ is its broken plural on the pattern fuʿalāʾ (فُعَلَاء).

1. Lexical meaning and the limits of early Arabic evidence

Ḥanaf: inclination and departure from an alignment

Arabic dictionaries record inclination and curvature for ḥanaf (حَنَف), including a curvature of the feet. In these accounts, rajulun aḥnafu (رَجُلٌ أَحْنَفُ) describes a man whose feet have such a curvature. These dictionaries were compiled after the period of revelation. Without a datable witness, their recording of a meaning does not by itself establish its existence before the Qur’an.

Several physical descriptions are recorded: some describe a curvature in the forefoot, others an inward turning of the foot. Their details differ, but they share the idea of inclination or a departure from an alignment.

In these accounts, the physical use of aḥnaf has no necessary religious or moral value. The Qur’an uses neither this form nor the description of curved feet. That physical meaning should therefore not be imposed as a necessary image in interpreting ḥanīf.

Verbal expressions are also recorded: ḥanafa ʿani al-shayʾi (حَنَفَ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ) for inclining or turning away from something, and taḥannafa ilā al-shayʾi (تَحَنَّفَ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ) for inclining toward it. The preposition specifies the direction of the relationship: a person may turn away from one thing or toward another.

This distinction matters. Inclination alone does not identify what someone has left or where they are heading. The direction and value of the movement must be understood from the sentence and its subject.

Why is ḥanīf also explained as straight or upright?

Alongside inclination, lexicographers record “upright” and “on the right path” for ḥanīf. At first these meanings may seem opposed: how can a word connected with curvature or inclination describe uprightness?

One lexical explanation understands a ḥanīf as someone inclining away from a wrong path toward a right one. On this account, inclination is evaluated in relation to its destination. This may explain a semantic relationship, but it does not by itself establish that one meaning developed historically from the other.

It helps to distinguish the direction of a movement from its outcome. Someone who turns from a wrong course toward a right one has both changed direction and chosen a right path. Turning away and taking the right course need not be incompatible descriptions.

This explanation does not prove the word’s historical development. A recorded physical sense concerning curved feet does not show that pre-Islamic Arabs necessarily pictured such feet whenever they heard ḥanīf. Nor can we confidently assert that its religious meaning developed directly from that physical image.

Two kinds of evidence must therefore remain distinct: dictionary accounts of inclination and curvature within the family, and the Qur’an’s direct, positive use of ḥanīf. Qur’anic sentences and contexts are the primary evidence for its Qur’anic meaning. The precise historical relationship between these fields still requires independent evidence.

Reports of pre-Islamic usage and the value of the poetic witness

Lexical accounts of pre-Islamic usage contain a noteworthy report: some groups of Arabs, even while worshipping idols, considered themselves followers of Abraham’s way and called themselves ḥunafāʾ. These reports sometimes connect the designation with pilgrimage and circumcision.

Such reports show that the lexical tradition connected ḥanīf with a heritage attributed to Abraham and with particular rites. But actual pre-Islamic usage must be distinguished from accounts written centuries later. These reports alone do not establish that all Arabs used that definition or that everyone who performed pilgrimage was linguistically and religiously considered a ḥanīf.

Arabic poetry also records mutaḥannif (مُتَحَنِّف) as a description of a worshipper. A line attributed to Jirān al-ʿAwd reads:

«وَأَدْرَكْنَ أَعْجَازًا مِنَ اللَّيْلِ بَعْدَمَا
أَقَامَ الصَّلَاةَ الْعَابِدُ الْمُتَحَنِّفُ»

“Those women reached the last part of the night after the ḥanīf worshipper had established salat.”

In the passage, women return from a nighttime meeting, and their return is timed against the salat of al-ʿābidu al-mutaḥannifu (الْعَابِدُ الْمُتَحَنِّفُ), the worshipper described as mutaḥannif. The word characterizes a person engaged in worship. However, the poem has not been independently dated before Qur’anic revelation, and accounts of the poet differ over his poetry’s relationship to the Islamic period. It is therefore not treated here as conclusive evidence of the word’s pre-Qur’anic religious meaning.

Among the poems attributed to pre-Islamic times, reports of rites, and accounts of naming preserved in later sources examined here, no independently secure attribution and date were established for a witness fixing the pre-Qur’anic religious meaning of ḥanīf. This does not establish that such usage was absent; it states the limits of the evidence examined.

Reports about pilgrimage, circumcision, or worship should therefore not become a definition claiming that ḥanīf originally meant only a pilgrim, a circumcised person, or someone performing particular rites. These are related historical uses and explanations, not an established fixed meaning for every occurrence.

2. Abraham as ḥanīf: beyond religious affiliation

Most Qur’anic occurrences are connected with Abraham. That relationship matters for the word’s semantic range, but not everything the Qur’an says about Abraham belongs to the lexical meaning of ḥanīf. Other descriptions of him make their own contributions.

Abraham’s way in response to calls for religious affiliation

Al-Baqarah 2:135 says:

«وَقَالُوا كُونُوا هُودًا أَوْ نَصَارَىٰ تَهْتَدُوا قُلْ بَلْ مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ»

“They said, ‘Become Jews or Christians and you will be guided.’ Say, ‘Rather, we follow the way of Abraham, who was ḥanīf and was not among those who associate partners with The God.’”

(Al-Baqarah 2:135)

The opening presents two religious affiliations as routes to guidance: Jewish and Christian. The response turns to millata Ibrāhīma (مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ), Abraham’s way, and describes Abraham as ḥanīfan.

Grammatically, ḥanīfan is an accusative circumstantial expression, or ḥāl, in the masculine singular. Millah (مِلَّة) is feminine and definite in this construction. Thus ḥanīfan is not its direct attributive adjective. The usual analysis relates the circumstance to Abraham: his way, with him being ḥanīf. The translation above follows this analysis.

Wa-mā kāna mina al-mushrikīna (وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ) specifies the religious boundary: Abraham was not among those who associate partners with The God. The sentence concerns more than an unspecified change of direction.

Here, being ḥanīf is connected with following Abraham’s way and rejecting shirk. The word is not presented in this verse as the name of a third sect alongside Jews and Christians. The response identifies Abraham’s way rather than merely adding another affiliation to the two already named.

Ḥanīfan musliman: two descriptions together

Āl ʿImrān 3:67 says:

«مَا كَانَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ يَهُودِيًّا وَلَا نَصْرَانِيًّا وَلَٰكِنْ كَانَ حَنِيفًا مُسْلِمًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ»

“Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but was ḥanīf and one who submitted, and was not among those who associate partners with The God.”

(Āl ʿImrān 3:67)

This verse is especially important because ḥanīfan (حَنِيفًا) and musliman (مُسْلِمًا) appear together. Both describe Abraham, but their juxtaposition alone does not establish complete lexical synonymy.

Musliman belongs to S L M and concerns submitting. Ḥanīfan belongs to Ḥ N F, whose lexical field is associated with inclination and orientation. Both descriptions apply to the same person, but come from different word families.

Abraham can be understood as directing himself toward The God and away from shirk, while also submitting to The God. The order of the two words does not establish a fixed hierarchy: that someone must first become ḥanīf and only then muslim, or that being ḥanīf necessarily ranks above submission. The verse states no such sequence.

The ending, wa-mā kāna mina al-mushrikīna, helps establish the Qur’anic sense by connecting Abraham’s being ḥanīf with not assigning partners to The God. The lexical range of shirk belongs to its own root study; its entire discussion should not be compressed into a short definition of ḥanīf.

Abraham as qānit and ḥanīf

Al-Naḥl 16:120 says:

«إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ كَانَ أُمَّةً قَانِتًا لِلَّهِ حَنِيفًا وَلَمْ يَكُ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ»

“Indeed, Abraham was an ummah, obedient to The God and ḥanīf, and was not among those who associate partners with Him.”

(Al-Naḥl 16:120)

Several descriptions stand together: ummah (أُمَّة), qānitan lillāhi (قَانِتًا لِلَّهِ), and ḥanīfan. They are followed once more by the denial of shirk.

Qānitan expresses obedience and humble devotion to The God, while ḥanīfan describes Abraham’s religious orientation. Both apply to him, but their lexical contributions differ.

Ummah—ordinarily a community, applied here to Abraham himself—should not be absorbed into the definition of ḥanīf. Both describe Abraham; that does not mean every ḥanīf is, by definition, an ummah.

Comparing these three verses reveals a clear pattern. The Qur’an calls Abraham ḥanīf while speaking of submission, obedience, following his way, and avoiding shirk. These descriptions illuminate his character and way together, while retaining their respective meanings.

3. From Abraham to following his way

Ḥanīf does more than describe a historical figure. Several verses connect Abraham’s description with an instruction to follow his way.

Āl ʿImrān 3:95 says:

«قُلْ صَدَقَ اللَّهُ فَاتَّبِعُوا مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ»

“Say, ‘The God has spoken truthfully. So follow the way of Abraham, who was ḥanīf and was not among those who associate partners with Him.’”

(Āl ʿImrān 3:95)

The verse does not merely describe Abraham: fa-ttabiʿū (فَاتَّبِعُوا), “so follow,” instructs the addressees to follow him. Their relationship to Abraham is expressed through following.

The next two verses, Āl ʿImrān 3:96–97, discuss the House at Bakkah, Abraham’s station, and pilgrimage to the House. This sequence connects Abraham’s way with the House and pilgrimage. But the lexical meaning of ḥanīf cannot be restricted to performing pilgrimage or physically facing a place. Verse 95 concerns following Abraham’s way; the following verses concern the House and pilgrimage. The subjects are connected, but the words do not have identical meanings.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:125 says:

«وَمَنْ أَحْسَنُ دِينًا مِمَّنْ أَسْلَمَ وَجْهَهُ لِلَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ وَاتَّبَعَ مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا وَاتَّخَذَ اللَّهُ إِبْرَاهِيمَ خَلِيلًا»

“Who is better in religion than someone who submits their face to The God, while doing good, and follows the way of Abraham, who was ḥanīf? The God took Abraham as a close friend.”

(Al-Nisāʾ 4:125)

Submitting one’s face, doing good, and following Abraham’s way occur together here. Ḥanīfan can be understood as a circumstance describing Abraham, as in the translation above. It can also grammatically describe the subject of ittabaʿa (اتَّبَعَ), the person following. The masculine singular form is compatible with both and does not alone settle the choice. Under either analysis, the ḥanīf orientation is connected with following Abraham’s way.

The construction brings doing good, submitting one’s face, and being ḥanīf together in one context. Each expression’s contribution must be assessed through its structure and usage. Juxtaposition alone establishes neither complete synonymy nor complete separation of meaning.

Al-Anʿām 6:161 also says:

«قُلْ إِنَّنِي هَدَانِي رَبِّي إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ دِينًا قِيَمًا مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ»

“Say, ‘My Lord has guided me to a straight path: an upright religion, the way of Abraham, who was ḥanīf and was not among those who associate partners with The God.’”

(Al-Anʿām 6:161)

Ṣirāṭin mustaqīmin (صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ), “a straight path,” dīnan qiyaman (دِينًا قِيَمًا), “an upright religion,” and millata Ibrāhīma ḥanīfan (مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا) are connected within one construction. Abraham’s ḥanīf orientation is thus presented as consistent with a straight path and upright religion.

This does not make ḥanīf and mustaqīm lexically identical. Istiqāmah, uprightness, belongs to Q W M, while lexical accounts connect ḥanīf with inclination and orientation. The Qur’an can describe a path as straight and a person following it as ḥanīf without erasing the difference between the roots.

Al-Naḥl 16:123 continues this pattern with a direct instruction to Muhammad:

«ثُمَّ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ أَنِ اتَّبِعْ مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ»

“Then We revealed to you: ‘Follow the way of Abraham, who was ḥanīf and was not among those who associate partners with The God.’”

(Al-Naḥl 16:123)

Abraham’s way is not confined to a closed chapter of the past: Muhammad is commanded to follow it. For this root study, the significance lies in the continued description of Abraham as ḥanīf and its connection with a way to be followed.

This continuity does not prove that ḥanīf was originally coined to mean “a follower of Abraham.” Following Abraham’s way clarifies this Qur’anic application, but the root’s linguistic history must also be investigated independently.

4. Human orientation: from Abraham’s face to a direct command

Wajjahtu wajhiya: turning toward the Creator

In Al-Anʿām 6:79, Abraham says:

«إِنِّي وَجَّهْتُ وَجْهِيَ لِلَّذِي فَطَرَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا أَنَا مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ»

“I have turned my face toward the One who originated the heavens and the earth, being ḥanīf, and I am not among those who associate partners with Him.”

(Al-Anʿām 6:79)

This is one of the clearest examples of the Qur’anic usage, because ḥanīfan stands alongside wajjahtu wajhiya (وَجَّهْتُ وَجْهِيَ), “I have turned my face.”

The preceding verses describe Abraham’s encounter with a star, the moon, and the sun, emphasizing their setting and transience. At the passage’s conclusion, he directs himself toward the One who originated the heavens and the earth.

Two semantic contributions should remain distinct. Wajjahtu wajhiya expresses turning and directing one’s face. Ḥanīfan describes Abraham’s state and orientation. The expressions belong to different roots, but illuminate one another in this verse.

The destination is explicit: li-lladhī faṭara al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa (لِلَّذِي فَطَرَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ), “toward the One who originated the heavens and the earth.” Creator, heavens, earth, and origination must not be extracted from Ḥ N F itself; those meanings come from other words.

The ending, wa-mā anā mina al-mushrikīna (وَمَا أَنَا مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ), establishes the boundary of this turning. Abraham does not merely choose a new direction; he also denies belonging among those who associate partners with The God.

To understand being ḥanīf here, both what a person leaves and the destination they turn toward matter. Yet the construction does not require ḥanīf always to mean someone changing beliefs at that very moment. It may also describe a person’s established orientation.

Aqim wajhaka li-l-dīni ḥanīfan

Yūnus 10:105 moves from describing Abraham to a direct command:

«وَأَنْ أَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا وَلَا تَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ»

“And set your face firmly toward the religion, being ḥanīf, and never be among those who associate partners with The God.”

(Yūnus 10:105)

Here ḥanīfan does not occur in a sentence about millata Ibrāhīma, Abraham’s way. The description must apply to the person being addressed.

The command shows that ḥanīf can describe the addressee and is not exclusive to Abraham. This usage alone does not settle whether historical groups called ḥunafāʾ existed. The verse concerns the addressee’s religious orientation.

Aqim (أَقِمْ) belongs to Q W M and expresses establishing or setting firmly. Ḥanīfan describes the circumstance in which this setting of the face is to occur. Firmness and being ḥanīf are connected here, but are not linguistically the same verb or description.

The ending again rejects shirk. This repeated association shows that Qur’anic ḥanīf has an orientation toward The God alone, rather than denoting just any intellectual change of direction or opposition to prevailing beliefs.

Ḥanīf and fiṭrah

Al-Rūm 30:30 says:

«فَأَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا فِطْرَتَ اللَّهِ الَّتِي فَطَرَ النَّاسَ عَلَيْهَا لَا تَبْدِيلَ لِخَلْقِ اللَّهِ ذَٰلِكَ الدِّينُ الْقَيِّمُ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ»

“Set your face firmly toward the religion, being ḥanīf; [adhere to] the fiṭrah of The God upon which He created people. There is no changing the creation of The God. That is the upright religion, but most people do not know.”

(Al-Rūm 30:30)

Its construction closely resembles Yūnus 10:105. Both contain aqim wajhaka li-l-dīni ḥanīfan (أَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا), “set your face toward the religion, being ḥanīf.” Al-Rūm continues with fiṭrata Allāhi (فِطْرَتَ اللَّهِ).

The translation above renders fiṭrata Allāhi with an understood call to adhere to it, marking the explanatory addition in brackets. Its connection with the preceding command is clear, but it should not be treated as a semantic substitute for ḥanīfan itself. Fiṭrah belongs to F Ṭ R and concerns creation and the nature in which people are created; ḥanīf describes the addressee’s religious orientation.

This verse alone does not resolve every question about fiṭrah: how it is recognized, how it relates to upbringing and human choice, or in what sense it is unchangeable. Those broader questions require consideration of other verses.

It does not follow that ḥanīf lexically means “innate,” or that every inward inclination is automatically ḥanīf and right. The verse concerns a particular religious orientation, placing it alongside fiṭrah and upright religion.

Comparing Yūnus 10:105 with Al-Rūm 30:30 shows that being ḥanīf accompanies the command to set one’s face and need not describe only a moment of conversion. Yet firmness, continuation, or permanent steadfastness should not be made independent components of ḥanīf without evidence. Part of that implication comes from the command and sentence context.

5. Ḥunafāʾ: a plural description extending beyond Abraham

Ḥunafāʾa lillāhi ghayra mushrikīna bihi

Al-Ḥajj 22:31 says:

«حُنَفَاءَ لِلَّهِ غَيْرَ مُشْرِكِينَ بِهِ وَمَنْ يُشْرِكْ بِاللَّهِ فَكَأَنَّمَا خَرَّ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَتَخْطَفُهُ الطَّيْرُ أَوْ تَهْوِي بِهِ الرِّيحُ فِي مَكَانٍ سَحِيقٍ»

“Be ḥunafāʾ for The God, associating no partner with Him. Whoever associates partners with The God is as though they had fallen from the sky, to be snatched by birds or swept by the wind to a faraway place.”

(Al-Ḥajj 22:31)

Ḥunafāʾa (حُنَفَاءَ) is the plural of ḥanīf. Here it describes a group and is followed by lillāhi (لِلَّهِ), “for The God.” The destination of the orientation is explicit, while ghayra mushrikīna bihi (غَيْرَ مُشْرِكِينَ بِهِ), “associating no partners with Him,” states its boundary.

This is particularly strong evidence for the Qur’anic sense. The destination need not be inferred from Abraham’s story or a historical association: the verse itself says lillāhi and immediately rejects shirk.

Ḥunafāʾ describes the people’s orientation, while ghayra mushrikīna bihi explicitly denies assigning partners to The God. The expressions are closely connected here; the latter can clarify and reinforce the former’s orientation toward Him alone. Their juxtaposition does not prove an absence of semantic overlap, and a translation should reflect both expressions.

The setting within Al-Ḥajj also matters. The preceding passage discusses sacred restrictions, sacrificial animals, and avoiding the defilement of idols and false speech. The following passage continues with sacred observances. Being ḥanīf is thus addressed amid actual provisions and rites.

This does not make “pilgrim” the lexical meaning of ḥanīf or restrict the description to pilgrimage. The verse recalls worship’s orientation toward The God alone within a passage on rites; other occurrences show that its application extends beyond pilgrimage.

Mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīna ḥunafāʾa

Al-Bayyinah 98:5 says:

«وَمَا أُمِرُوا إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ حُنَفَاءَ وَيُقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَيُؤْتُوا الزَّكَاةَ وَذَٰلِكَ دِينُ الْقَيِّمَةِ»

“They were commanded only to worship The God, making the religion exclusively His, being ḥunafāʾ, and to establish salat and give zakat. That is the upright religion.”

(Al-Bayyinah 98:5)

Mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīna (مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ) and ḥunafāʾa appear together. Ikhlāṣ, making something pure or exclusive, belongs to KH L Ṣ and concerns freedom from admixture. Being ḥanīf, as the Qur’anic examples show, is associated with orientation toward The God and avoidance of shirk.

These descriptions converge in serving The God, but are not complete synonyms. Ikhlāṣ emphasizes making the religion exclusively His; ḥanīf emphasizes orientation toward Him alone and away from assigning Him partners. This distinction follows the words’ semantic fields; it should not be converted into two chronological stages or a fixed hierarchy of devotion.

The verse continues with establishing salat and giving zakat. Being ḥanīf is therefore connected here with service and action, but the word alone does not specify every form or detail of salat or zakat.

Comparing Al-Ḥajj 22:31 with Al-Bayyinah 98:5 shows, first, that ḥunafāʾ describes a group and is not exclusive to Abraham; and, second, that it accompanies the rejection of shirk in one verse and exclusive religious devotion in the other. Both support the word’s orientation toward The God alone.

The morphology also matters. Ḥunafāʾ (حُنَفَاء), with an initial u vowel, is the plural of ḥanīf in both verses. It must not be confused with ḥanfāʾ (حَنْفَاء), with an initial a and no vowel after n, which can be the feminine of aḥnaf, describing curved feet. The unvocalized spelling is identical, but the vowels, morphological patterns, and meanings differ.

6. Semantic boundaries and comparisons with related roots

Ḥ N F (ḥanaf) and J N F (janaf)

Lexical sources compare ḥanaf with janaf. Some explain the former as an inclination from error toward uprightness and the latter as an inclination from uprightness toward deviation.

The Qur’an uses janaf in Al-Baqarah 2:182:

«فَمَنْ خَافَ مِنْ مُوصٍ جَنَفًا أَوْ إِثْمًا فَأَصْلَحَ بَيْنَهُمْ فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“Whoever fears partiality or wrongdoing on the part of a person making a bequest, and brings about a settlement among them, incurs no sin. The God is forgiving and merciful.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:182)

Janafan (جَنَفًا) here concerns deviation and improper partiality in a bequest. By comparison, ḥanīf is a positive description in every Qur’anic occurrence.

That Qur’anic contrast is clear, but does not establish two perfectly symmetrical opposites throughout Arabic’s history. The lexical explanation of inclination from error toward uprightness and the reverse can help explain usage, but each root’s basic meaning must be assessed against its own evidence.

Similarity in spelling and meaning does not itself establish a historical relationship between the roots.

Ḥ N F (ḥanaf) and M Y L (māla)

M Y L concerns inclining or leaning toward something, overlapping in that respect with lexical explanations of Ḥ N F. Inclination itself has no fixed moral direction: someone may incline toward something right or wrong.

Translating Qur’anic ḥanīf merely as “inclined” is insufficient, because the destination and the description’s positive value must be clear. Its Qur’anic orientation is connected with The God and the rejection of shirk. That feature emerges from the Qur’anic occurrences as a whole, not from the general meaning of inclination alone.

Ḥ N F (ḥanaf) and Q W M (qāma)

Istiqāmah (اِسْتِقَامَة), uprightness, and dīn qayyim (دِين قَيِّم), upright religion, belong to Q W M, a family associated with establishing, standing, and firmness. Lexical explanations of Ḥ N F, by contrast, emphasize inclination and orientation.

Al-Anʿām 6:161 and Al-Rūm 30:30 bring these fields together: a straight path and upright religion are presented, with being ḥanīf accompanying them.

Being ḥanīf is thus consistent with and accompanies uprightness in the Qur’an, but the words should not be treated as fully interchangeable. Uprightness emphasizes straightness and firmness, while Qur’anic ḥanīf foregrounds orientation toward The God and avoidance of shirk.

Ḥ N F (ḥanaf) and S L M (salima)

Āl ʿImrān 3:67 describes Abraham as both ḥanīf and muslim. Submission to The God and orientation toward Him alone come together in the same person.

Lexically, however, muslim belongs to S L M and should not simply be translated as ḥanīf. Nor does the use of both in one verse establish two obligatory, consecutive stages for everyone.

The Qur’an connects being ḥanīf with submitting, but each should be understood through its own root and uses.

Ḥ N F (ḥanaf) and KH L Ṣ (khalaṣa)

In Al-Bayyinah 98:5, being ḥanīf accompanies making the religion exclusively The God’s. Ikhlāṣ concerns removing admixture and dedicating the religion to Him; being ḥanīf concerns orientation toward Him alone.

The descriptions can complement one another in explaining service to The God, but should not be substituted for one another throughout the Qur’an. For instance, mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīna in Al-Bayyinah 98:5 has its own grammatical structure, specifying both an object and the one to whom it is dedicated. Ḥunafāʾa is a distinct description.

Does ḥanīf mean opposing every tradition and inheritance?

In Abraham’s account, the Qur’an describes his questioning and opposition to the beliefs of his people and father. Other verses criticize unexamined adherence to ancestors. These matters help illuminate his conduct, but do not reduce the lexical meaning of ḥanīf to “opponent of tradition,” “rebel,” or “freethinker.”

The direction of the turning is specific: Abraham distances himself from shirk and turns toward the Creator. Not every rejection of inherited beliefs is therefore ḥanīf; equally, following a known way does not itself negate that description. The Qur’an expressly commands people to follow Abraham’s way.

The central issue is the destination’s worth and rightness, not merely being different from society or the past.

Is ḥanīf the name of a historical sect or religion?

The Qur’an uses ḥanīf descriptively, rather than introducing a formally organized sect with its own leadership and exclusive rules. It describes Abraham and also occurs in direct addresses and in the plural.

Historical and lexical reports of people called ḥanīf before Islam matter for the word’s history, but cannot be transferred without examination into its Qur’anic definition.

Descriptive usage in these verses does not itself prove or disprove the historical existence of an organized group. This study explains the word through its Qur’anic uses and makes no judgment about the organization of historical communities.

7. Translation choices for Forqan and conclusion

No single English equivalent unambiguously carries every distinction. “Monotheist” expresses the orientation toward one God but may understate turning away and choosing a direction. “Upright in faith” emphasizes uprightness, but less clearly conveys inclination or orientation. “Single-minded” also remains ambiguous unless its object is identified.

In passages such as Al-Anʿām 6:79 and Al-Ḥajj 22:31, ḥanīf can be explained as “turned exclusively toward The God” or “turning away from shirk toward The God.” That full explanation need not be repeated in every translation, especially when the word is immediately followed by an explicit rejection of shirk.

In verses such as Al-Baqarah 2:135 and Āl ʿImrān 3:67, retaining ḥanīf with a clear explanation can be helpful because the description itself is under discussion. Once its meaning is established, context may favor “oriented toward The God alone,” “worshipping The God alone,” or ḥanīf itself. The first emphasizes orientation and belief; the second emphasizes worship. The choice should fit the particular verse.

For ḥunafāʾ in Al-Ḥajj 22:31, a natural rendering is “be turned exclusively toward The God, associating no partners with Him.” In Al-Bayyinah 98:5, “oriented toward Him alone” or ḥunafāʾ can be used, provided mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīna is translated separately and its full sense of exclusive devotion is not absorbed into ḥanīf.

Ḥanīf should not automatically be replaced with “pilgrim,” “circumcised person,” “freethinker,” or “opponent of all traditions.” Each introduces a historical or interpretive setting that the Qur’anic description does not necessarily entail.

Conclusion

Arabic dictionaries connect Ḥ N F with inclination and orientation and record curved feet among the physical senses of certain forms. The Qur’an uses ḥanīf positively in a religious setting. Those two observations alone do not prove a historical development from curved feet to religious meaning. The date and course of that relationship remain uncertain.

Ḥanīf is not restricted to Abraham, though he is the most prominent person described by it. Muhammad is also commanded to set his face toward the religion as a ḥanīf, and the plural ḥunafāʾ describes servants of The God. These uses show that it is more than a historical affiliation or inherited title: it describes a person’s religious orientation.

The verses as a whole, especially Al-Anʿām 6:79, Yūnus 10:105, Al-Ḥajj 22:31, and Al-Bayyinah 98:5, establish the direction: turning toward The God and away from shirk, in connection with making devotion exclusively His. Ḥanīf, muslim, mukhliṣ, muḥsin, and mustaqīm are related in these contexts, but each should be translated with attention to its root, structure, and uses. Their co-occurrence alone proves neither complete synonymy nor an absence of semantic overlap.

The Qur’anic meaning of ḥanīf can be summarized as being turned exclusively toward The God and away from shirk. This synthesis rests on the Qur’anic occurrences as a whole. Conscious choice, a recent change of belief, or permanent steadfastness should not be added to the word’s fixed definition without contextual evidence.

In this sense, being ḥanīf is neither merely deviating from a path nor simply standing motionless upon one. It describes someone who directs their service toward The God without associating partners with Him.
