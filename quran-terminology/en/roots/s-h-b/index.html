Qur’anic root س ح ب — S Ḥ B

Dragging, extending and cloud: from drawing something over the ground to clouds moving through the sky and people dragged in punishment

The three-letter Arabic root س ح ب (sīn ḥāʾ bāʾ) concerns pulling and extending something. Cloth drawn over the ground moves along with the person pulling it. The same linguistic family includes سَحاب (saḥāb), cloud: something spread across the sky and moved by air from one area to another.

The Qur’an has two principal uses. سَحاب (saḥāb) names cloud in passages about creation, clouds moving, rain, lightning and natural imagery. يُسْحَبُونَ (yusḥabūna) is a passive verb describing people dragged in scenes of punishment.

The uses belong to one family without sharing every feature. A person dragged in chains undergoes painful, imposed movement; clouds moving through the sky are a natural phenomenon. S Ḥ B itself means neither punishment nor rain. Sentence construction and context identify what moves, its moving agent and the outcome.

The root occurs eleven times in eleven Qur’anic verses: nine instances of سَحَاب (saḥāb) and two of يُسْحَبُونَ (yusḥabūna). سَحَابًا (saḥāban), سَحَابٌ (saḥābun) and السَّحَاب (al-saḥāb) differ in grammatical case and definiteness, rather than being separate derivatives. Both verbs have the same morphology: third-person masculine plural passive imperfect of basic Form I سَحَبَ (saḥaba).

Arabic also knows سَحَبَ (saḥaba), يَسْحَبُ (yasḥabu), سَحْب (saḥb), سَاحِب (sāḥib), مَسْحُوب (masḥūb), سَحَابَة (saḥābah), سَحَائِب (saḥāʾib), سُحُب (suḥub) and اِنْسَحَبَ (insaḥaba), none occurring in these forms in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic core and older Arabic usage

Saḥaba: drawing something along a surface

سَحَبَ الشَّيْءَ يَسْحَبُهُ سَحْبًا (saḥaba al-shayʾa yasḥabuhu saḥban) means pulling something, particularly along the ground.

A familiar example is سَحَبَ ذَيْلَهُ (saḥaba dhaylahu), trailing the hem or lower part of one’s garment on the ground. The cloth moves with the person and extends along the surface.

سَحَبَتِ الرِّيحُ التُّرَابَ (saḥabati al-rīḥu al-turāba) means the wind drew dust along and shifted it over the ground.

Something is moved along a path. A heavy object may require considerable force, but difficulty and coercion are not invariable parts of the verb. A person may gently trail a garment or laboriously drag a heavy object; saḥb can describe both.

Ibn Fāris identifies pulling and extending something spread out as the root’s semantic principle, using a garment’s hem dragged over the ground as an example.

This fits older usage, but pulling, moving and being dragged must be distinguished. When someone pulls an object, its movement depends on another agent. Not everything that moves is pulled: birds fly and people walk without saḥb describing every movement.

Insaḥaba: movement following a pull

اِنْسَحَبَ الشَّيْءُ (insaḥaba al-shayʾu) can describe something being drawn or moving along a surface.

The focus is more on the object’s movement than on whoever pulls it.

Modern Arabic also uses insaḥaba for withdrawing or retreating. That familiar contemporary use should not replace the older sense of being drawn and extending.

Insaḥaba does not occur in the Qur’an. Modern “retreat” should therefore not enter yusḥabūna or saḥāb.

Saḥābah and saḥāb: naming cloud

سَحابَة (saḥābah) denotes a cloud. سَحاب (saḥāb) likewise names cloud and can concern one cloud mass or a collection of clouds.

More precisely in grammar, saḥāb is a collective genus noun whose unit is saḥābah, although some lexical descriptions call it saḥābah’s plural. Arabic also records سَحَائِب (saḥāʾib) and سُحُب (suḥub) for clouds.

This matters because saḥāb may behave like a singular noun while encompassing a collection of cloud masses.

For example, Al-Nūr 24:43 says يُؤَلِّفُ بَيْنَهُ (yuʾallifu baynahu), bringing its parts together. بَيْنَ (bayna), “between,” fits saḥāb’s collective meaning despite the singular pronoun.

The apparently singular form should not therefore make every occurrence concern exactly one entirely undifferentiated cloud mass.

Why is cloud called saḥāb?

Older lexical explanations connect the name with cloud moving and extending through the sky. As cloth is drawn over the ground, cloud moves and extends across the sky.

Ibn Fāris uses this comparison to explain the relationship between saḥb and saḥāb.

The image is clear: cloud can spread through the sky and move between regions with air currents.

One must nevertheless distinguish a lexical explanation connecting two words from establishing the historical naming of cloud.

Comparing cloud movement with something being drawn supports examining the two uses as a semantic family. It does not prove that the pulling verb necessarily existed first and that Arabs coined the cloud name at a particular date.

Nor does saḥāb imply visible movement at every instant. A cloud may appear stationary for a time and still be called saḥāb.

A natural phenomenon’s name is not identical with every passing condition.

Saḥāb in poetry before Islam

Rajaz verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas says:

«كَأَنَّهُمْ إِذَا خَرَجُوا مِنْ عَرْعَرِ
مُسْتَلْئِمِينَ لَابِسِي السَّنَوَّرِ
نَشْءُ سَحَابٍ صَائِفٍ كَنَهْوَرِ»

“When they emerged from ʿArʿar, clad in armour, they seemed like a rising mass of dense summer cloud.”

The warriors are compared to a summer cloud. مُسْتَلْئِمِينَ (mustalʾimīna) concerns wearing military equipment; السَّنَوَّر (al-sanawwar) a protective covering or armour.

نَشْءُ سَحَابٍ صَائِفٍ (nashʾu saḥābin ṣāʾifin) pictures a cloud rising and appearing in summer.

The poet uses it for the warriors’ appearance and collective movement. Cloud names an actual phenomenon serving as the comparison’s image.

The evidence shows saḥāb established in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, in descriptions of nature and military comparisons.

Poetry collections and older geographical sources attribute the passage to al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas. Its recording in later sources does not conclusively establish every detail of attribution and wording.

For the root study, the important point is that saḥāb named cloud in Arabic imagery before present-day technical and scientific uses.

2. Cloud in creation: winds, clouds and rain

Wa-al-saḥābi al-musakhkhari

Among the signs of creation, Al-Baqarah 2:164 says:

«وَتَصْرِيفِ الرِّيَاحِ وَالسَّحَابِ الْمُسَخَّرِ بَيْنَ السَّمَاءِ وَالْأَرْضِ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْقِلُونَ»

“And in the turning and changing of winds, and clouds subjected between heaven and earth, are signs for people who use reason.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:164, closing passage)

Al-saḥāb stands alongside الرِّيَاح (al-riyāḥ), winds: distinct natural phenomena related to one another.

تَصْرِيف (taṣrīf) concerns winds turning and changing; مُسَخَّر (musakhkhar) describes cloud as operating within an order under command and direction.

Saḥāb itself does not mean subjected. Al-musakhkhar supplies the description.

Nor does the verse make clouds identical with wind. Wind is moving air; a cloud comprises tiny water droplets or ice crystals in air. Wind can affect cloud movement and condition, while the words remain linguistically distinct.

Saḥāban thiqālan: heavy clouds

Al-Aʿrāf 7:57 says:

«وَهُوَ الَّذِي يُرْسِلُ الرِّيَاحَ بُشْرًا بَيْنَ يَدَيْ رَحْمَتِهِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَقَلَّتْ سَحَابًا ثِقَالًا سُقْنَاهُ لِبَلَدٍ مَيِّتٍ فَأَنْزَلْنَا بِهِ الْمَاءَ فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ مِنْ كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ كَذَٰلِكَ نُخْرِجُ الْمَوْتَىٰ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَذَكَّرُونَ»

“He is the One who sends winds as bearers of good news ahead of His mercy. When they lift heavy clouds, We drive them towards a dead land, send water down through it and bring forth all kinds of produce with that [water]. Thus We bring forth the dead, so that you may take heed.”

(Al-Aʿrāf 7:57)

Saḥāban accompanies ثِقَالًا (thiqālan), “heavy”: heavy clouds.

Heaviness here concerns water-bearing clouds that can bring rain.

Saḥāb itself does not mean a rain-bearing cloud. Thiqālan and the continuation identify the particular cloud’s character.

سُقْنَاهُ لِبَلَدٍ مَيِّتٍ (suqnāhu li-baladin mayyitin) also matters. سَاقَ (sāqa), from س و ق (S W Q), concerns driving the cloud towards a specified destination.

It should not be identified with saḥaba. Sawq expresses driving; saḥāb names what is driven.

The earth’s revival after water arrives becomes a comparison for bringing forth the dead. It rests on a natural event, but saḥāb itself does not mean renewed creation.

Wa-yunshiʾu al-saḥāba al-thiqāla

Al-Raʿd 13:12 says:

«هُوَ الَّذِي يُرِيكُمُ الْبَرْقَ خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا وَيُنْشِئُ السَّحَابَ الثِّقَالَ»

“He is the One who shows you lightning, a cause of fear and hope, and brings heavy clouds into being.”

(Al-Raʿd 13:12)

Cloud is connected with lightning and human fear and hope.

Lightning can raise hopes of rain and concern over danger and a powerful storm.

خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا (khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan) concerns the addressees’ response to lightning, rather than saḥāb’s lexical meaning.

يُنْشِئُ (yunshiʾu), from ن ش أ (N SH ʾ), expresses bringing clouds into being.

Saḥāb alone therefore does not make cloud invariably frightening or hopeful. The kind of cloud and weather conditions affect human experience.

Together with Al-Aʿrāf 7:57, the verse connects cloud with rain without making their names identical.

Fa-tuthīru saḥāban fa-suqnāhu

Fāṭir 35:9 says:

«وَاللَّهُ الَّذِي أَرْسَلَ الرِّيَاحَ فَتُثِيرُ سَحَابًا فَسُقْنَاهُ إِلَىٰ بَلَدٍ مَيِّتٍ فَأَحْيَيْنَا بِهِ الْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا كَذَٰلِكَ النُّشُورُ»

“And The God is the One who sent the winds, and they stir up clouds. We then drove them towards a dead land and revived the earth through it after its death. Thus is resurrection.”

(Fāṭir 35:9)

The sequence includes winds being sent, cloud stirred up, driven towards a land and the earth revived after water arrives.

Each stage has a distinct verb: أَرْسَلَ (arsala), sending; تُثِيرُ (tuthīru), stirring up; and سُقْنَاهُ (suqnāhu), driving.

Saḥāb names the cloud mass to which the verbs relate.

Al-Rūm 30:48 also says:

«اللَّهُ الَّذِي يُرْسِلُ الرِّيَاحَ فَتُثِيرُ سَحَابًا فَيَبْسُطُهُ فِي السَّمَاءِ كَيْفَ يَشَاءُ وَيَجْعَلُهُ كِسَفًا فَتَرَى الْوَدْقَ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ فَإِذَا أَصَابَ بِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ إِذَا هُمْ يَسْتَبْشِرُونَ»

“The God is the One who sends the winds, and they stir up clouds. He spreads the cloud through the sky however He wills, making it into portions and masses, and you see rain emerging from within it. When He causes it to reach whichever of His servants He wills, suddenly they rejoice.”

(Al-Rūm 30:48)

فَيَبْسُطُهُ (fa-yabsuṭuhu) expresses spreading the cloud; وَيَجْعَلُهُ كِسَفًا (wa-yajʿaluhu kisafan) expresses making it into portions or masses.

These fit an image of cloud movement and extension, but saḥāb itself does not mean spreading or dividing.

Comparison shows winds connected with cloud movement and formation, and clouds contributing to rainfall.

All scientific stages of cloud formation, moisture condensation and precipitation should not be derived from these verbs alone. The Qur’an presents observable natural events among creation’s signs.

3. Cloud form, density and movement: Al-Nūr, Al-Naml and Al-Ṭūr

Thumma yuʾallifu baynahu thumma yajʿaluhu rukāman

Al-Nūr 24:43 says:

«أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يُزْجِي سَحَابًا ثُمَّ يُؤَلِّفُ بَيْنَهُ ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ رُكَامًا فَتَرَى الْوَدْقَ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ وَيُنَزِّلُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مِنْ جِبَالٍ فِيهَا مِنْ بَرَدٍ فَيُصِيبُ بِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَصْرِفُهُ عَنْ مَنْ يَشَاءُ يَكَادُ سَنَا بَرْقِهِ يَذْهَبُ بِالْأَبْصَارِ»

“Have you not seen that The God gently drives clouds, brings their parts together, then heaps them up? You see rain emerging from within them. And He sends down hail from the sky, from mountain-like [cloud masses] containing hail, striking whomever He wills and turning it away from whomever He wills. Its lightning’s brilliance almost takes away sight.”

(Al-Nūr 24:43)

Saḥāban begins a series: cloud movement, parts joining, accumulation, then rain emerging and hail descending.

يُزْجِي (yuzjī) concerns driving and setting in motion; يُؤَلِّفُ (yuʾallifu) joins parts. رُكَامًا (rukāman), from ر ك م (R K M), concerns accumulation, parts lying upon others.

The words contribute to one natural image without meaning the same thing.

Why yuʾallifu baynahu?

Saḥāb appears singular, while بَيْنَهُ (baynahu) concerns joining its parts.

Arabic can give saḥāb a collective meaning encompassing clouds or cloud portions.

Yuʾallifu baynahu thus concerns joining dispersed cloud parts.

This clearly distinguishes grammatical number from a noun’s semantic range. A singular pronoun alone does not make the verse necessarily concern one cloud body without distinct parts.

Yajʿaluhu rukāman likewise presents accumulation as an outcome attributed to cloud, rather than making saḥāb mean a piled-up cloud everywhere.

Cloud, rain and hail

Saḥāb, وَدْق (wadq) and بَرَد (barad) are distinct words in Al-Nūr 24:43.

Saḥāb is cloud; wadq here concerns rain; barad is hail.

جِبَالٍ فِيهَا مِنْ بَرَدٍ (jibālin fīhā min baradin) requires examining syntax and the portrayal of masses. A familiar interpretation understands large, mountain-like, hail-containing masses in the sky. Actual rocky mountains should not be attributed to the verse without examining the construction.

For S Ḥ B, the principal point is that cloud, rain and hail are related, rather than synonymous.

The verse describes clouds and associated events. Saḥāb itself does not mean every event described.

Min fawqihi saḥābun: cloud in an image of darkness

Al-Nūr 24:40 says:

«أَوْ كَظُلُمَاتٍ فِي بَحْرٍ لُجِّيٍّ يَغْشَاهُ مَوْجٌ مِنْ فَوْقِهِ مَوْجٌ مِنْ فَوْقِهِ سَحَابٌ ظُلُمَاتٌ بَعْضُهَا فَوْقَ بَعْضٍ إِذَا أَخْرَجَ يَدَهُ لَمْ يَكَدْ يَرَاهَا وَمَنْ لَمْ يَجْعَلِ اللَّهُ لَهُ نُورًا فَمَا لَهُ مِنْ نُورٍ»

“Or like darkness in a deep sea covered by a wave, above it another wave, above that a cloud: layers of darkness upon one another. When someone puts out a hand, they can scarcely see it. Whoever The God has not given light has no light.”

(Al-Nūr 24:40)

Cloud contributes to an image of darkness.

Deep sea, waves and cloud stand together, resulting in layers of darkness.

Saḥāb therefore does not always signal rain, fertility or mercy. Cloud can also contribute to an image of obscured light and darkness.

Saḥāb itself does not mean darkness. The image’s combined conditions produce it.

This is also a comparison within discussion of the deeds of those addressed by the Qur’an. Natural cloud and sea serve the image without the root’s meaning becoming the comparison’s ethical message.

Tamurru marra al-saḥābi: mountains moving like cloud

Al-Naml 27:88 says:

«وَتَرَى الْجِبَالَ تَحْسَبُهَا جَامِدَةً وَهِيَ تَمُرُّ مَرَّ السَّحَابِ صُنْعَ اللَّهِ الَّذِي أَتْقَنَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ إِنَّهُ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَفْعَلُونَ»

“And you see the mountains, thinking them motionless, while they pass like clouds. This is the workmanship of The God, who has perfected everything. Indeed, He knows what you do.”

(Al-Naml 27:88)

Al-saḥāb appears in a simile: mountains moving are compared with clouds passing.

تَمُرُّ (tamurru) belongs to م ر ر (M R R). مَرَّ السَّحَابِ (marra al-saḥābi) compares the manner of movement.

The linguistic point is clear: cloud movement was familiar enough to the addressees for the Qur’an to use it to explain something else moving.

Precisely which condition the mountains’ movement describes exceeds saḥāb’s lexical meaning.

The preceding verses mention the Trumpet being blown and the associated terror, leading many to understand this within Resurrection. Another interpretation relates it to the present earth and mountains moving.

S Ḥ B establishes neither interpretation. Saḥāb provides a familiar picture of movement; identifying the mountains’ movement in time and nature requires the complete context.

The simile therefore neither negates actual cloud movement nor independently establishes a particular scientific theory of the earth’s motion.

Saḥābun markūmun: the deniers’ description of what they see

Al-Ṭūr 52:44 says:

«وَإِنْ يَرَوْا كِسْفًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ سَاقِطًا يَقُولُوا سَحَابٌ مَرْكُومٌ»

“And if they see a fragment of the sky falling, they say: A piled-up cloud.”

(Al-Ṭūr 52:44)

سَحَابٌ مَرْكُومٌ (saḥābun markūmun) is spoken by people judging an evident sign.

مَرْكُوم (markūm), from ر ك م (R K M), concerns something piled together.

The sentence does not establish that what they see actually is cloud. It says that if they saw a falling fragment of sky, they would call it saḥābun markūmun.

The distinction is essential: a name people assign to an event does not necessarily determine its actual nature.

The passage concerns those who can offer a reason not to accept even a startling sign.

Saḥāb still means cloud; assigning that name to the sight is the speakers’ claim.

Comparison with Al-Nūr 24:43 reveals another distinction. There, cloud accumulation describes a natural phenomenon. In Al-Ṭūr, piled-up cloud is a name deniers assign to an event.

Similar wording can consequently occupy different roles in verses.

4. Yusḥabūna: people dragged in punishment

Alongside nine saḥāb occurrences, the verb occurs only twice, both in scenes of punishment in the hereafter.

Wa-al-salāsilu yusḥabūna

Ghāfir 40:71 and Ghāfir 40:72 say:

«إِذِ الْأَغْلَالُ فِي أَعْنَاقِهِمْ وَالسَّلَاسِلُ يُسْحَبُونَ ۝ فِي الْحَمِيمِ ثُمَّ فِي النَّارِ يُسْجَرُونَ»

“When shackles and chains are around their necks, they are dragged through boiling water; then burned in the Fire.”

(Ghāfir 40:71; Ghāfir 40:72)

Yusḥabūna is a third-person masculine plural passive imperfect: “they are dragged.” The verb itself does not name the immediate agent.

Chains and shackles also appear. Being dragged accompanies a condition in which people lack freedom of movement.

Saḥb’s physical meaning is evident: someone is pulled by another’s force.

The root means neither chains, shackles, boiling water nor fire. Separate words identify each.

“Are dragged” is more appropriate than “move,” retaining the passive and the scene’s imposed character.

The translation should not suggest people freely and calmly proceeding to their destination.

Yusḥabūna fī al-nāri ʿalā wujūhihim

Al-Qamar 54:48 says:

«يَوْمَ يُسْحَبُونَ فِي النَّارِ عَلَىٰ وُجُوهِهِمْ ذُوقُوا مَسَّ سَقَرَ»

“The day they are dragged through the Fire on their faces: Taste the burning touch of Saqar.”

(Al-Qamar 54:48)

The same verb yusḥabūna occurs, with the manner more explicit: عَلَىٰ وُجُوهِهِمْ (ʿalā wujūhihim), “on their faces.”

This is not ordinary walking. People are placed in a condition of being dragged on their faces.

فِي النَّارِ (fī al-nāri) specifies the location. ذُوقُوا مَسَّ سَقَرَ (dhūqū massa Saqara) expresses the scene’s painful consequence.

Saḥb accompanies punishment, without itself meaning burning. Being dragged is one action; burning or contact with fire is another event in the same scene.

Does saḥb inherently mean coercive dragging?

Both Qur’anic verbs occur where people are dragged with coercion and suffering.

Arabic saḥb nevertheless has a wider range. Someone can trail a garment, drag an object over the ground or move something along a path.

Coercion and punishment cannot therefore be invariable parts of the root’s definition.

In Ghāfir and Al-Qamar, the human object and punishment context give the action its harsh, imposed character.

This distinction matters: defining saḥb as invariably torturing would not explain saḥāb or ordinary dragging of cloth.

5. Saḥāb and saḥb: their relationship and neighbouring roots

Is cloud also being drawn along?

Lexical explanations compare cloud movement with something being drawn.

Qur’anic cloud movement and form also use يُزْجِي (yuzjī), سُقْنَاهُ (suqnāhu), تُثِيرُ (tuthīru) and يَبْسُطُ (yabsuṭu). In Al-Naml 27:88, tamurru directly describes mountains moving, with that movement compared to clouds passing.

Saḥāb itself does not replace every verb describing cloud movement and formation.

Cloud moved by wind may spread, its masses join or accumulate in a region. These conditions fit extension, without all requiring one verb.

Saḥāb’s relationship to being drawn explains its lexical connection to saḥb, without erasing the distinction between the cloud noun and dragging verb.

S Ḥ B (saḥaba) and J R R (jarra)

جَرَّ (jarra), from ج ر ر (jīm rāʾ rāʾ), concerns pulling and broadly overlaps with saḥaba.

Lexical sources often explain saḥb through jarr. Both verbs can describe drawing cloth over the ground.

Jarr has other grammatical and social branches. Conversely, saḥāb is an established cloud name, with no identical form in J R R.

The verbs’ proximity does not make all their derivatives identical.

S Ḥ B (saḥāb) and GH M M (ghamām)

غَمَام (ghamām), from غ م م (ghayn mīm mīm), also names cloud in the Qur’an.

Al-Baqarah 2:57 says:

«وَظَلَّلْنَا عَلَيْكُمُ الْغَمَامَ»

“And We made the cloud shade you.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:57, excerpt)

Cloud relates here to casting shade.

Saḥāb and ghamām are close within the cloud field, while belonging to different root families.

Lexical explanations sometimes connect ghamām with covering and encompassing the sky. The familiar explanation of saḥāb instead involves cloud being drawn and extending.

This can clarify their different imagery, without making an absolute rule that ghamām always means shading cloud and saḥāb always rain-bearing cloud.

Both can be translated simply as “cloud,” with their lexical distinction retained in explanation.

S Ḥ B (saḥāb) and R W Ḥ (rīḥ)

رِيح (rīḥ) denotes wind, while saḥāb is cloud.

In Al-Aʿrāf, Fāṭir and Al-Rūm, winds relate to clouds moving and being stirred up.

Wind and cloud nevertheless differ in nature. Strong wind may occur without rain-bearing cloud; in other circumstances clouds may move gently.

Saḥāb should not be translated as wind, nor is rīḥ a name for cloud itself.

S Ḥ B (saḥāb) and S W Q (sāqa)

سَاقَ (sāqa), from س و ق (sīn wāw qāf), concerns driving and advancing something.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:57 and Fāṭir 35:9 describe driving cloud towards a land.

Cloud is what is moved; sawq is the action of driving it.

Saḥāb does not lexically mean driving, just as sawq is not exclusive to clouds.

S Ḥ B (saḥāb) and R K M (rakama)

رَكَمَ (rakama), from ر ك م (rāʾ kāf mīm), concerns heaping together.

Rukāman in Al-Nūr 24:43 describes the state cloud acquires after its parts join. Al-Ṭūr 52:44 also has saḥābun markūmun in deniers’ speech.

R K M concerns how parts lie in relation to one another; saḥāb names cloud.

One can therefore discuss dispersed or piled-up cloud without changing the phenomenon’s name.

S Ḥ B (saḥaba) and Ṣ Ḥ B (ṣaḥiba)

These roots differ only in their first consonant, but have separate meanings.

صَحِبَ (ṣaḥiba), from ص ح ب (ṣād ḥāʾ bāʾ), concerns accompanying and companionship. صَاحِب (ṣāḥib) and أَصْحَاب (aṣḥāb) belong to it.

Saḥaba concerns pulling; saḥāb concerns cloud.

Similar spelling or sound does not establish a definite semantic or derivational relationship.

6. Suggested translations for Forqan

“Cloud” is natural and precise for all nine Qur’anic saḥāb occurrences.

Where it has collective meaning, “clouds” can suit English flow, as in “stirs up clouds” or “drives heavy clouds.”

Al-saḥābi al-musakhkhari in Al-Baqarah 2:164 can be “clouds subjected between heaven and earth.” Saḥāban thiqālan in Al-Aʿrāf 7:57 and al-saḥāba al-thiqāla in Al-Raʿd 13:12 mean heavy clouds.

In Al-Nūr 24:43, saḥāban is cloud; rukāman should separately convey accumulation.

Marra al-saḥābi in Al-Naml 27:88 is best expressed as “like clouds passing,” preserving the comparison’s manner of movement.

Saḥābun markūmun in Al-Ṭūr 52:44 can be “a piled-up cloud,” while clearly retaining that it is the deniers’ description.

For yusḥabūna in Ghāfir 40:71 and Al-Qamar 54:48, prefer “they are dragged.” In Al-Qamar, retain ʿalā wujūhihim: “they are dragged on their faces.”

Merely “they are taken” weakens the image of dragging.

Saḥāb should not invariably mean a rain-bearing cloud. In Al-Nūr 24:40 it contributes to darkness; in Al-Ṭūr 52:44 it is a name deniers give a phenomenon.

Retain “cloud” as the basic translation, with weight, density, movement, darkness and association with rain conveyed by the verse’s other words.

Conclusion

S Ḥ B concerns pulling and extending something. Physical uses include dragging clothing or objects over the ground. Its nominal branch saḥāb names cloud, associated in familiar lexical explanation with cloud moving and extending through the sky.

The Qur’an has two uses: nine instances of the cloud name and two of yusḥabūna for people dragged in punishment.

The cloud passages show varied aspects: wind-driven movement, masses joining, accumulation, lightning and rain, darkness and passage through the sky.

Saḥāb itself is neither rain nor movement, density or darkness. Accompanying verbs and descriptions establish those features.

Cloud undergoes movement, joining and accumulation in Al-Nūr 24:43; its movement illustrates mountains moving in Al-Naml 27:88; piled-up cloud is spoken by deniers in Al-Ṭūr 52:44. These different roles must remain distinct.

In Ghāfir 40:71 and Al-Qamar 54:48, yusḥabūna expresses people being dragged. The punishment context makes that movement imposed and painful, without the root itself meaning punishment or burning.

S Ḥ B’s semantic core is pulling and extending something along a path. Saḥāb is its established nominal branch for cloud, whose movement and spread lexical explanation connects with the image of being drawn.

This clarifies the semantic relationship without identifying natural cloud movement with humans being forcibly dragged.

Qur’anic saḥāb is an actual created phenomenon used in varied comparisons and arguments. Saḥb is a verb describing people dragged in harsh conditions in two verses. Understanding the root requires preserving both branches and the contributions of their accompanying words.
