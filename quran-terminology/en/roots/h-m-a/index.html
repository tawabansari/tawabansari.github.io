From dark mud and water sediment to the material of human creation and a muddy spring

The Arabic root ح م أ concerns dark mud, sludge, and sediment accumulating in water and damp ground. حَمَإ denotes dark, altered mud; حَمْأَة likewise names this material, particularly the dark sludge and sediment of wells and pools. حَمِئَ describes water mixed with such deposits so that its colour or smell changes.

In everyday life, the root relates to wells, pools, riverbeds, and muddy ground. Sludge can accumulate at the bottom of a well, cloud the water, and acquire an unpleasant smell as organic matter remains and changes. Arabic uses this family for such conditions.

ح م أ occurs four times in the Qur’an. Three occurrences of حَمَإٍ in Al-Ḥijr concern the material associated with human creation. In Al-Kahf 18:86, حَمِئَةٍ describes a spring in whose direction Dhū al-Qarnayn sees the setting sun.

The two uses share a material field while occupying different contexts: dark mud relates to the material of creation in Al-Ḥijr and describes a spring’s water in Al-Kahf.

Qur’anic forms: حَمَإٍ in Al-Ḥijr 15:26, Al-Ḥijr 15:28, and Al-Ḥijr 15:33; حَمِئَةٍ in Al-Kahf 18:86.

Arabic also records حَمْأَة, حَمَأَ, حَمِئَ, أَحْمَأَ, حَمِئ, and حَمْء. These exact forms do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four occurrences in four verses

1. Lexical Meaning and Material Uses

حَمَإ and حَمْأَة: dark mud

Al-ʿAyn explains حَمَإ as black, foul-smelling mud. Mud removed from a riverbed or pool also belongs to this material field, but the word is not confined to a particular location.

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī defines حَمَإ as black mud and also records حَمْأَة for it.

Lisān al-ʿArab describes it as الطِّينُ الْأَسْوَدُ الْمُنْتِنُ: dark mud with an unpleasant smell.

Two features are prominent in these established uses: the mud’s darkness and its altered condition in a wet environment.

Dark mud ordinarily results from water mixing with soil particles and other materials. It may collect in wells, pools, or riverbeds.

This does not establish that all black mud must smell unpleasant or that every foul-smelling substance is حَمَأ. Darkness and a muddy consistency matter in the lexical definition; unpleasant odour accompanies many familiar instances.

حَمَإ names this material, rather than every kind of earth or dark substance.

Charcoal, for example, is also black but differs from dark mud in substance and use. Dry soil may likewise be dark, but colour alone does not justify calling it حَمَأ without the characteristics of mud and wet sediment.

Al-Basīṭ: Al-ʿAyn’s definition and the vocalization of ḥamʾah

The distinction between حَمَإ and حَمْأَة

Lexical sources place حَمَإ and حَمْأَة very close together.

Abū ʿUbaydah identifies حَمَأ as the plural of حَمْأَة. Some dictionaries transmit the unit with a vowel on the middle consonant, حَمَأَة, comparing it with قَصَبَة and قَصَب. The unit–collective relationship must therefore be distinguished from variations in vocalizing the mīm.

On that account, حَمَأ names the material or its collective quantity, while حَمْأَة can denote a portion of it.

Some dictionaries also use both for black mud without a substantial semantic distinction.

Unsupported differences should therefore not be invented, such as claiming that حَمَأ necessarily means dry mud and حَمْأَة wet mud.

Both concern dark mud. Form and number may matter in particular usages, but “dark mud” or “dark sludge” ordinarily suits both in translation.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ḥamaʾ, ḥamʾah, and vocalization variants

حَمِئَ: water becoming mixed with mud

حَمِئَ الْمَاءُ describes water mixed with حَمْأَة and consequently clouded or made foul-smelling.

حَمِئَتِ الْبِئْرُ means that dark mud has accumulated or increased in a well.

The verb describes more than the presence of water: it expresses a change in the water or well through sediment.

بِئْرٌ حَمِئَةٌ denotes a well containing dark mud.

The Qur’anic عَيْنٌ حَمِئَةٌ follows this construction: a spring containing such mud.

حَمِئَة does not mean “spring.” عَيْن names the spring, while حَمِئَة describes it.

Al-Kahf 18:86 therefore combines two words: the water source or spring and a description of its muddy condition.

حَمَأَ and أَحْمَأَ: adding or removing sediment

Arabic dictionaries record حَمَأَ الْبِئْرَ and أَحْمَأَ الْبِئْرَ for dealing with a well’s mud.

Al-Jawharī, quoting Ibn al-Sikkīt, distinguishes them: حَمَأَ removes حَمْأَة from the well, while أَحْمَأَ puts or throws it into the well.

Al-Zamakhsharī records the same distinction in Asās al-Balāghah.

Some sources report the forms differently, however. An initial added hamzah alone does not establish that the meaning must always be adding material.

Morphology matters for meaning, but the precise sense must be checked against actual usage.

Both actions are practically conceivable: cleaning a well by removing sediment or throwing mud into it.

The material remains حَمْأَة in both cases; the action and direction of movement differ.

Asās al-Balāghah: removing and adding sediment

2. Early Arabic Witnesses and Knowledge of Mud

Sediment in wells and pools

Wells were important sources of water in Arab life. In some areas, water mixed with soil and underlying deposits, leaving mud at the bottom.

Arabic distinguishes water itself, the mud accompanying it, and removing the sediment.

حَمِئَتِ الْبِئْرُ describes a well in which حَمْأَة has become abundant.

Conversely, one recorded use of حَمَأَ الْبِئْرَ concerns removing that material.

حَمَأ was thus a familiar substance in wet environments, rather than a term invented solely to explain human creation.

These uses do not establish particular chemical or biological properties for حَمَأ in every environment.

Different wells and pools can contain mud of differing composition. The Arabic word identifies perceptible characteristics rather than a modern laboratory definition.

حَمْأَة in poetry attributed to Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī

A passage attributed to Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī reads:

فَسُقِيتُ بِالْمَاءِ النَّمِيرِ وَلَمْ
أُتْرَكْ أُلَاطِمُ حَمْأَةَ الْجَفْرِ

fa-suqītu bi-l-māʾi al-namīri wa-lam / utrak ulāṭimu ḥamʾata al-jafri

Meaning: “I was given wholesome, pleasant water to drink, and was not left struggling with the dark mud of the pit or well.” The أُلَاطِمُ transmission is used here; حَمْأَةَ is its object, and النَّمِير describes wholesome water.

الْمَاءُ النَّمِيرُ is contrasted with حَمْأَةِ الْجَفْرِ in the verse.

نَمِير describes pure, pleasant water, while حَمْأَة presents the water source’s dark, undesirable mud.

This contrast illustrates the literary use of حَمْأَة to depict unsatisfactory water conditions.

The wording varies among sources. Simṭ al-Laʾālī transmits أُلَاطِمُ حَمْأَةَ الْجَفْرِ, while some dīwān transmissions have أَوَاطِسُ حَمْأَةَ الْجَفْرِ. In the examined Al-Aghānī transmission, فَسُقِيتُ بِالْمَاءِ النَّمِيرِ وَلَمْ is followed by يُنْظَرْ إِلَيَّ بِأَعْيُنٍ خُزْرِ, “be looked at with scowling eyes.” The first transmission is adopted here; the others should be recognized as variants rather than treated as one undisputed text.

Simṭ al-Laʾālī: the ulāṭimu ḥamʾata al-jafr transmission

These differences require caution over the verse’s exact wording and vocalization. Nevertheless, earlier transmissions of حَمْأَة الْجَفْر do not justify discarding the witness merely because variants exist. It is attributed to the pre-Islamic Ḥātim; the date and precise wording of this particular verse must remain distinct from the word’s independently documented meaning.

Al-Aghānī: a different half-line in the account of Ḥātim

Dark colour and an altered material condition

Early explanations identify darkness as a familiar characteristic of حَمَأ.

Darkness must not yield an ethical meaning such as evil, spiritual impurity, or inherent baseness.

Dark mud is a natural substance. It can suit one use and be unsuitable for another, but colour and odour do not themselves create an ethical judgement.

This matters for the creation verses, where حَمَأ names material in the account of human creation.

A material’s darkness or changed odour does not mean it is worthless within creation.

Conversely, defending human worth does not justify changing the word into a luminous substance, something ritually pure, or material possessing unknown characteristics.

Its material meaning is established by Arabic usage; humanity’s significance must be understood from the complete creation passages.

3. مِنْ حَمَإٍ مَسْنُونٍ in Al-Ḥijr

حَمَإٍ occurs three times in Al-Ḥijr, always accompanied by مَسْنُونٍ.

The repeated combination concerns a particular material in the description of human creation.

حَمَأ and مَسْنُون belong to independent roots: the former names the muddy material; the latter describes it.

Al-Ḥijr 15:26: the material of human creation

وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ مِنْ صَلْصَالٍ مِنْ حَمَإٍ مَسْنُونٍ

wa-la-qad khalaqnā al-insāna min ṣalṣālin min ḥamaʾin masnūnin

Translation: We indeed created the human from dry, sounding clay, from dark, altered mud.

حَمَإٍ follows صَلْصَالٍ.

صَلْصَال comes from ص ل ص ل and commonly denotes dry clay that makes a sound when struck or brought into contact with something.

Al-Raḥmān 55:14 also has صَلْصَالٍ كَالْفَخَّارِ: dry clay resembling pottery.

حَمَأ, by contrast, concerns dark mud and wet sediment.

The words express different characteristics.

One possibility is that the verse presents two conditions of a material: wet, altered mud and material that becomes صَلْصَال after drying.

The verse itself does not, however, provide a complete chronological sequence of transformations.

Their juxtaposition does not establish a drying duration, environmental temperature, or chemical composition.

The meaning of مَسْنُونٍ

مَسْنُون comes from س ن ن, rather than ح م أ.

Lexical and exegetical sources offer several explanations.

One understands it as altered, aged, or foul-smelling.

Another reads it as shaped or formed, drawing on سُنَّة in the sense of form and configuration.

A further explanation concerns poured or cast material, based on سَنَنْتُ الْمَاءَ for pouring or causing water to flow.

These interpretations differ lexically and contextually.

“Altered” emphasizes the muddy material’s condition.

“Shaped” directs attention to its formation.

Al-Ṭabarī adopts “altered” while also transmitting “shaped” and “poured.” Reporting these positions should not turn them into one identical definition of حَمَأ itself.

For this root study, the essential point is that the meaning of مَسْنُون must not be transferred into حَمَأ itself.

حَمَأ identifies dark mud; مَسْنُون’s precise sense requires independent study of س ن ن.

“Dark, altered mud” can therefore be considered for the complete phrase, but “altered” represents a selected reading of مَسْنُون rather than حَمَأ’s exclusive meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: ḥamaʾ and interpretations of masnūn

Al-Ḥijr 15:28: creating a human being

وَإِذْ قَالَ رَبُّكَ لِلْمَلَائِكَةِ إِنِّي خَالِقٌ بَشَرًا مِنْ صَلْصَالٍ مِنْ حَمَإٍ مَسْنُونٍ

wa-idh qāla rabbuka li-l-malāʾikati innī khāliqun basharan min ṣalṣālin min ḥamaʾin masnūnin

Translation: When your Lord said to the angels, “I am creating a human being from dry, sounding clay, from dark, altered mud.”

مِنْ صَلْصَالٍ مِنْ حَمَإٍ مَسْنُونٍ is repeated here.

Al-Ḥijr 15:26 has الْإِنْسَان, whereas this verse uses بَشَرًا.

بَشَر belongs to another root and here denotes the human whose creation is announced.

The difference does not change حَمَأ’s meaning: the muddy material is introduced with the same expression in both verses.

Al-Ḥijr 15:29 then discusses fashioning and breathing of the spirit.

The creation account thus continues beyond naming an initial material. “Spirit” and “fashioning” must not, however, be inserted into حَمَأ’s lexical meaning.

حَمَأ names the material; other acts and stages have independent vocabulary.

Al-Ḥijr 15:33: Iblīs’s objection

قَالَ لَمْ أَكُنْ لِأَسْجُدَ لِبَشَرٍ خَلَقْتَهُ مِنْ صَلْصَالٍ مِنْ حَمَإٍ مَسْنُونٍ

qāla lam akun li-asjuda li-basharin khalaqtahu min ṣalṣālin min ḥamaʾin masnūnin

Translation: He said, “I am not one to prostrate to a human You created from dry, sounding clay, from dark, altered mud.”

The expression now occurs in Iblīs’s speech.

He invokes the human’s material of creation to explain his refusal to prostrate.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:12 and Ṣād 38:76, he claims superiority by comparing creation from fire with creation from clay.

He uses the difference in materials to assert his own superiority.

Al-Ḥijr 15:33 does not, however, state that dark mud is inherently inferior to fire.

That valuation is not inherent in حَمَأ. It belongs to Iblīs’s argument within the context of his disobedience.

The material description must therefore not become an ethical judgement about human worth.

The relationship with تُرَاب and طِين

Other words also describe human creation in the Qur’an.

Āl ʿImrān 3:59 uses تُرَاب.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:11 mentions طِينٍ لَازِبٍ.

Al-Raḥmān 55:14 uses صَلْصَالٍ كَالْفَخَّارِ.

These are close within the field of earthly materials, without being identical.

تُرَاب is earth or dust; طِين is earth mixed with water into clay or mud; حَمَأ is dark mud; and a prominent use of صَلْصَال concerns dry, sounding clay.

They can be examined as different descriptions of earthly material.

The names alone do not establish that they must all be fixed, consecutive stages of a biological or geological process.

Nor does حَمَأ alone establish microbes, cells, genetic material, or a particular process by which life originated.

These require independent scientific research. حَمَأ means dark mud and must not become a modern biological technical term.

4. عَيْنٍ حَمِئَةٍ: Al-Kahf 18:86

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغَ مَغْرِبَ الشَّمْسِ وَجَدَهَا تَغْرُبُ فِي عَيْنٍ حَمِئَةٍ وَوَجَدَ عِنْدَهَا قَوْمًا قُلْنَا يَا ذَا الْقَرْنَيْنِ إِمَّا أَنْ تُعَذِّبَ وَإِمَّا أَنْ تَتَّخِذَ فِيهِمْ حُسْنًا

ḥattā idhā balagha maghriba al-shamsi wajadahā taghrubu fī ʿaynin ḥamiʾatin wa-wajada ʿindahā qawman qulnā yā dhā al-qarnayni immā an tuʿadhdhiba wa-immā an tattakhidha fīhim ḥusnan

Translation: When he reached the place of the sun’s setting, he found it setting in a dark, muddy spring, and found a people near it. We said, “O Dhū al-Qarnayn, either punish them or adopt a good course among them.”

حَمِئَةٍ modifies عَيْنٍ.

عَيْن denotes a spring or water source here; حَمِئَة describes its condition.

عَيْنٌ حَمِئَةٌ is a spring whose water or bed contains dark mud.

The word does not itself mean a hot spring.

حَمِئَة: containing mud

حَمِئَة is the feminine of حَمِئ, agreeing with feminine عَيْن.

It derives from حَمِئَ, used of water or a well in which حَمْأَة has increased.

The emphasis is on the water’s dark, muddy condition.

The word does not establish that the spring was necessarily small, deep, still, or possessed particular geographical features.

The adjective likewise does not specify the sediment, degree of cloudiness, or material composition.

Its direct meaning is the presence of dark mud in the spring.

The readings حَمِئَة and حَامِيَة

The widespread reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim has عَيْنٍ حَمِئَةٍ.

Other recognized readings also have عَيْنٍ حَامِيَةٍ.

The forms differ in root and meaning.

حَمِئَة, with hamzah, belongs to ح م أ and concerns dark mud.

حَامِيَة, without hamzah, belongs in its established sense to ح م ي and denotes heat.

The first reading therefore concerns a muddy spring, the second a hot spring.

Al-Ṭabarī recognizes both readings and explains their compatibility: a spring can be hot and contain dark mud.

This establishes the possibility of combining the descriptions.

It does not make حَمِئَة and حَامِيَة lexically identical.

Nor do two readings conclusively establish that the particular spring must have possessed both characteristics.

حَمِئَة is the form relevant to ح م أ. حَامِيَة belongs in an independent study of ح م ي and the readings.

Al-Ṭabarī: the ḥamiʾah and ḥāmiyah readings

Does the verse describe the sun physically entering a spring?

وَجَدَهَا matters here.

و ج د is another root; the construction concerns what Dhū al-Qarnayn encountered or found in this way during his journey.

وَجَدَهَا تَغْرُبُ فِي عَيْنٍ حَمِئَةٍ depicts the setting sun before his eyes.

In ordinary experience, the sun may appear to disappear into the sea, behind a mountain, or in the direction of a pool.

Such observation does not establish that the sun physically enters water.

The verse concerns Dhū al-Qarnayn’s experience and situation on a journey. مَغْرِبَ الشَّمْسِ concerns the place or direction of sunset along his route.

حَمِئَة contributes only the spring’s description to this question.

ح م أ cannot therefore supply a theory of solar motion, the earth’s shape, or a cosmic location of sunset.

Conversely, the tangible scene should not be removed by treating the muddy spring as merely a spiritual symbol. The text mentions a spring and people nearby.

One established exegetical reading concerns Dhū al-Qarnayn’s visual perspective: he reached an area where the sun appeared to set in the direction of muddy water on his horizon. This reading should remain distinct from the adjective’s lexical meaning and from identifying a precise location.

The explanation draws on the complete construction and familiar experience of observing sunset, rather than حَمِئَة’s lexical meaning alone.

Ibn Kathīr: the visual perspective in the sunset description

Is the spring’s location known?

The verse does not supply geographical coordinates.

حَمِئَة does not identify a present-day country, continent, or region.

Muddy or hot springs existing in various parts of the world do not establish that any particular one is the Qur’anic spring.

A historical identification requires independent geographical and historical evidence.

Neither dark mud nor the hot-spring reading alone should therefore establish a particular place as Dhū al-Qarnayn’s certain destination.

5. Distinctions from Nearby Roots and Words

ح م أ (ḥamaʾ) and ط ي ن (ṭīn)

طِين denotes earth mixed with water into a clay-like or muddy consistency.

حَمَأ is dark mud familiar in damp environments and sediment.

Every familiar حَمَأ belongs among muddy materials, while not every طِين is necessarily حَمَأ.

طِين has a broader field in creation verses, whereas حَمَأ identifies a more particular material characteristic.

Study of Ṭ Y N

ح م أ (ḥamaʾ) and ت ر ب (turāb)

تُرَاب primarily denotes earth, dust, and earthly particles.

حَمَأ concerns dark muddy material.

Earth can mix with water and become mud, but that natural possibility does not make تُرَاب and حَمَأ synonyms.

Both must retain their linguistic characteristics in creation passages.

Study of T R B

ح م أ (ḥamaʾ) and ص ل ص ل (ṣalṣāl)

صَلْصَال concerns dry, sounding clay, compared to pottery in Al-Raḥmān 55:14.

حَمَأ concerns dark mud, commonly wet and sedimentary.

Both may describe creation material while differing in perceptible condition.

Al-Ḥijr 15:26 uses both names, whose distinction should survive translation and explanation.

Study of Ṣ L Ṣ L

ح م أ (ḥamaʾ) and س ن ن (sanan)

مَسْنُون derives from س ن ن.

In حَمَإٍ مَسْنُونٍ, حَمَأ names the material and مَسْنُون adds a description.

Explanations of مَسْنُون include altered, shaped, and poured.

None should become an inherent part of حَمَأ without independent examination.

ح م أ (ḥamaʾ) and ح م ي (ḥamiya)

In its heat-related usage, ح م ي concerns becoming hot.

حَامِيَة in the alternative reading of Al-Kahf 18:86 denotes a hot spring.

حَمِئَة in the Ḥafṣ reading belongs to ح م أ and denotes a muddy spring.

Similarity of sound and form does not merge their roots.

Muddiness does not itself establish heat.

ح م أ (ḥamaʾ) and ح م م (ḥumam)

حُمَم denotes charcoal or material blackened through burning; this plural must be distinguished from حَمَأ.

حَمِيم also occurs in the Qur’an in contexts of intensely hot water.

حُمَم and حَمِيم both belong to ح م م, a family independent of ح م أ. They should not be presented as two separate roots from one another.

Darkness and heat may accompany one another in some natural materials, but حَمَأ must not become charcoal, fire, or boiling water in translation.

ح م أ (ḥamaʾ) and ح م و (ḥamw)

Some Arabic dictionaries record hamzated حَمْء for relatives by marriage, particularly a woman’s father-in-law or other relatives of her husband.

It appears alongside حَمْو and حَمَا.

The family-related use has no established semantic connection with dark mud.

A hamzated form in some dialects or lexical accounts does not establish that this family is identical with Qur’anic حَمَأ.

Root study must distinguish formal resemblance from shared derivation.

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

حَمَإٍ in Al-Ḥijr 15:26, Al-Ḥijr 15:28, and Al-Ḥijr 15:33 should be “dark mud.”

This preserves its material character and is natural for the reader.

“Dark sludge” is also possible. In contemporary Persian, however, the equivalent of sludge often suggests greater unpleasantness or contamination. “Dark mud” is more neutral in the creation passages while conveying the essential meaning.

If “altered” is preferred for مَسْنُون in مِنْ حَمَإٍ مَسْنُونٍ, the phrase can be rendered:

“From dark, altered mud.”

“Altered” must be recognized as the chosen equivalent of مَسْنُون rather than an independent component of حَمَأ.

عَيْنٍ حَمِئَةٍ in Al-Kahf 18:86 is “a dark, muddy spring.”

This follows حَمِئَة without adding heat unjustifiably.

“A spring containing dark sludge” is also precise but less fluid in Persian.

For the alternative عَيْنٍ حَامِيَةٍ, “a warm spring” or “a hot spring” is appropriate, explaining its connection to ح م ي.

Al-Kahf’s translation should preserve وَجَدَهَا تَغْرُبُ, with Dhū al-Qarnayn’s visual perspective discussed separately.

Outside the Qur’an, حَمْأَة is “sludge” or “dark mud,” حَمِئَ الْمَاءُ means “the water became mixed with dark mud and clouded,” and بِئْرٌ حَمِئَةٌ is “a well containing dark mud.”

For adding or removing sediment, the precise meaning requires construction and context rather than reliance solely on the presence or absence of an initial hamzah.

Conclusion

ح م أ concerns dark mud and wet sediment. حَمَأ and حَمْأَة name the material, while حَمِئَ describes water mixed with it or a well in which the deposits have increased.

Darkness and sometimes unpleasant odour are familiar early characteristics. The root does not mean every black substance, damp soil, or foul-smelling thing.

The family occurs four times in the Qur’an.

حَمَإٍ مَسْنُونٍ describes human creation material in three Al-Ḥijr verses. It must be studied alongside صَلْصَالٍ, without merging dark mud with its dryness or with مَسْنُون’s description.

حَمِئَةٍ in Al-Kahf 18:86 describes a spring containing dark mud. Its distinction from the حَامِيَة reading must remain: the former concerns muddiness, the latter heat.

Brief root meaning: Dark mud and sediment in water or damp surroundings; material that can alter water’s colour, clarity, or smell.

This is recognizable both in creation material and in the muddy spring.

The root alone cannot establish scientific details of human origins, fixed stages of material becoming alive, or a theory of solar motion. Such questions exceed lexical meaning and require independent evidence.

For Forqan, “dark mud” is the most suitable basic equivalent of حَمَأ, and “a dark, muddy spring” of عَيْنٍ حَمِئَةٍ.

These renderings preserve the familiar, perceptible meaning without substituting a historical interpretation, scientific theory, or another Qur’anic reading for the actual wording.
