The Qur’anic Root Q Ṣ W

Distance, moving away, and the farthest part of a place

In early Arabic, Q Ṣ W concerns being far away, moving away, and occupying a distant position relative to a particular point of reference. Its derivatives can describe the farther part of a territory, a settlement’s remote edge, or distance between people. “Farthest” belongs to its elative forms, not to every derivative.

Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim al-Asadī, a pre-Islamic poet, says in a poem about relations between tribes:

فَحَاطُونَا الْقَصَا وَلَقَدْ رَأَوْنَا / قَرِيبًا حَيْثُ يُسْتَمَعُ السِّرَارُ

They kept their distance from us, though they had seen us close enough for whispered speech to be heard.

Al-qaṣā denotes distance. Ḥāṭūnā l-qaṣā is explained as keeping away despite proximity, not simply surrounding someone at a distance. Its contrast with qarīban, “near,” makes the spatial meaning clear.

The line is transmitted in Bishr’s collected poems, with a variant reading al-qaṣāʾ wa-qad raʾawnā. It provides poetic evidence attributed to the pre-Islamic period; the precise date of composition was not independently established.

Forms examined: قَصَا, يَقْصُو, قَصِيَ, قَصْو, قُصُوّ, قَصَاء, قَاصٍ, قَاصِيَة, قَصِيّ, أَقْصَى, قُصْوَى, أَقْصَاء, إِقْصَاء, تَقَصٍّ, and اِسْتِقْصَاء. Aqṣāʾ is a plural form for distant persons or things; iqṣāʾ is the verbal noun meaning to remove or exclude. The elative aqṣā, “farther/farthest,” must also be distinguished from the identically written verb aqṣā, “removed.”

Qur’anic forms: أَقْصَى, قُصْوَى, and قَصِيّ. The root occurs five times in five verses: aqṣā three times, quṣwā once, and qaṣiyy once. Maryam 19:16 is quoted for context, not as another occurrence of this root.

1. Core meaning and the spatial image

Qaṣā l-makānu means that a place is distant, and qaṣawtu ʿani l-qawmi means that I moved away from the people.

Qāṣin and qaṣiyy describe a person or thing far from a given point. Qāṣiyat al-balad denotes a distant district or far edge of a territory.

Aqṣā is an elative, with quṣwā as its feminine form. As akbar and kubrā express greater size, aqṣā and quṣwā express greater distance or the farthest extent, depending on the comparison and context.

One house may be far from the town centre, while another stands at the town’s farthest edge. The first can be called qaṣiyy and the second aqṣā in this comparison.

Distance is relational: a place far from one person may be near another.

In non-material uses, iqṣāʾ means removing or excluding someone, while istiqṣāʾ means pursuing a matter to its furthest extent.

The image behind istiqṣāʾ is following an inquiry through to its limits and details.

Lexicons also record qaṣawtu l-baʿīra for cutting part of the end of a camel’s ear. This concerns removing an extremity; its historical relationship to the distance-related branch is not securely established.

2. Al-Masjid al-Aqṣā: the farther place of prostration

سُبحٰنَ الَّذى أَسرىٰ بِعَبدِهِ لَيلًا مِنَ المَسجِدِ الحَرامِ إِلَى المَسجِدِ الأَقصَا الَّذى بٰرَكنا حَولَهُ لِنُرِيَهُ مِن ءايٰتِنا إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّميعُ البَصيرُ

Subḥāna lladhī asrā bi-ʿabdihi laylan mina l-masjidi l-ḥarāmi ilā l-masjidi l-aqṣā lladhī bāraknā ḥawlahu li-nuriyahu min āyātinā. Innahu huwa l-samīʿu l-baṣīr.

Glory be to Him who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to al-Masjid al-Aqṣā, whose surroundings We have blessed, to show him some of Our signs. He is indeed the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing.

Al-Isra 17:1

Al-aqṣā modifies al-masjid and describes its distance. It carries an elative sense: farther or farthest in relation to a point of reference.

The sentence places two locations in relation to one another: the Sacred Mosque as the starting point and al-Masjid al-Aqṣā as the destination.

Min marks the origin and ilā the destination, while aqṣā describes the destination’s spatial position.

The blessing of its surroundings comes from bāraknā ḥawlahu, and the purpose of the journey from li-nuriyahu min āyātinā. Neither is part of the lexical meaning of aqṣā.

Nor does aqṣā itself mean highest, holiest, or best. In this context it concerns distance, not a rank of virtue or sanctity.

Historical identification of the place, its geographical boundaries, and the manner of the journey require evidence beyond the meaning of Q Ṣ W alone. None of those questions can be settled simply by glossing aqṣā.

In translation, the designation al-Masjid al-Aqṣā can be retained and its lexical sense explained separately.

3. Al-ʿudwah al-quṣwā: the farther bank and the nearer bank

إِذ أَنتُم بِالعُدوَةِ الدُّنيا وَهُم بِالعُدوَةِ القُصوىٰ وَالرَّكبُ أَسفَلَ مِنكُم وَلَو تَواعَدتُم لَاختَلَفتُم فِى الميعٰدِ وَلٰكِن لِيَقضِىَ اللَّهُ أَمرًا كانَ مَفعولًا لِيَهلِكَ مَن هَلَكَ عَن بَيِّنَةٍ وَيَحيىٰ مَن حَىَّ عَن بَيِّنَةٍ وَإِنَّ اللَّهَ لَسَميعٌ عَليمٌ

Idh antum bi-l-ʿudwati l-dunyā wa-hum bi-l-ʿudwati l-quṣwā wa-l-rakbu asfala minkum. Wa-law tawāʿadtum la-khtalaftum fī l-mīʿādi wa-lākin li-yaqḍiya llāhu amran kāna mafʿūlan li-yahlika man halaka ʿan bayyinatin wa-yaḥyā man ḥayya ʿan bayyinah. Wa-inna llāha la-samīʿun ʿalīm.

When you were on the nearer bank and they were on the farther bank, and the caravan was below you. Had you arranged a meeting, you would have differed over the appointment. But this was so that The God might accomplish a matter destined to be done, so that whoever perished might perish upon clear evidence, and whoever lived might live upon clear evidence. The God is indeed All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

Al-Anfal 8:42

This is a particularly clear witness to the root’s meaning because quṣwā is set directly against dunyā.

Al-ʿudwah denotes the side or bank of a valley.

Dunyā is the feminine of adnā, from D N W; quṣwā is the feminine of aqṣā, from Q Ṣ W.

The two descriptions locate the groups: one on the nearer bank and the other on the farther bank.

Here dunyā does not mean worldly life or moral baseness, just as quṣwā does not mean the afterlife or an elevated rank.

Both words describe spatial positions.

Distance and proximity in these word families do not inherently assign moral worth. Conduct, rather than occupying one bank or the other, determines the ethical significance of a person’s actions.

Quṣwā preserves the root’s wāw. In forms such as dunyā and ʿulyā, a root wāw appears instead as yāʾ. Quṣyā is also recorded in Arabic and in a transmitted reading; the Qur’anic text quoted here reads quṣwā. This observation concerns morphology and transmission, not two different distances.

4. Makānan qaṣiyyan: Maryam’s withdrawal to a distant place

فَحَمَلَتهُ فَانتَبَذَت بِهِ مَكانًا قَصِيًّا

Fa-ḥamalathu fa-ntabadhat bihi makānan qaṣiyyā.

She conceived him, then withdrew with him to a distant place.

Maryam 19:22

Qaṣiyyan follows the faʿīl pattern and modifies makānan, “a place.”

The verse distinguishes the act of withdrawing from the location’s characteristic.

Intabadhat, from N B DH, expresses withdrawal; qaṣiyyan describes how distant the place is.

Earlier in Maryam’s account, the Qur’an says:

وَاذكُر فِى الكِتٰبِ مَريَمَ إِذِ انتَبَذَت مِن أَهلِها مَكانًا شَرقِيًّا

Wa-dhkur fī l-kitābi Maryama idhi ntabadhat min ahlihā makānan sharqiyyā.

Mention Maryam in the Book, when she withdrew from her family to an eastern place.

Maryam 19:16

In the earlier verse, the location is described by its eastern direction; in the pregnancy passage, by its distance.

Sharqiyyan expresses direction; qaṣiyyan expresses distance.

The verse does not name the place, quantify its distance from Maryam’s home, or say how long reaching it took.

Qaṣiyy must also be distinguished from aqṣā. The verse describes a distant place, not the farthest place possible.

5. Aqṣā l-madīnah: coming from the farthest part of the city

The Qur’an uses aqṣā l-madīnah in two different accounts.

The man who warned Moses of danger

وَجاءَ رَجُلٌ مِن أَقصَا المَدينَةِ يَسعىٰ قالَ يٰموسىٰ إِنَّ المَلَأَ يَأتَمِرونَ بِكَ لِيَقتُلوكَ فَاخرُج إِنّى لَكَ مِنَ النّٰصِحينَ

Wa-jāʾa rajulun min aqṣā l-madīnati yasʿā. Qāla yā Mūsā inna l-malaʾa yaʾtamirūna bika li-yaqtulūka fa-khruj innī laka mina l-nāṣiḥīn.

A man came running from the farthest part of the city. He said, “Moses, the chiefs are deliberating about you in order to kill you. Leave! I am one of those who wish you well.”

Al-Qasas 28:20

Min aqṣā l-madīnah states where the man’s movement began.

Yasʿā describes his coming with urgency; his subsequent words explain why he came.

The distance of his location did not prevent him from acting to warn Moses.

The verse does not identify him by name, establish his social position, or explain how he learned of the chiefs’ decision. His helpful intervention is the focus.

The man who urged his people to follow the Messengers

وَجاءَ مِن أَقصَا المَدينَةِ رَجُلٌ يَسعىٰ قالَ يٰقَومِ اتَّبِعُوا المُرسَلينَ

Wa-jāʾa min aqṣā l-madīnati rajulun yasʿā. Qāla yā qawmi ttabiʿū l-mursalīn.

From the farthest part of the city a man came running. He said, “My people, follow the Messengers.”

Ya-Sin 36:20

Its structural similarity to 28:20 is noteworthy.

In both accounts, a man comes with urgency from the farthest part of a city. In Moses’ account he warns of danger; in Yā Sīn he urges the people to follow the Messengers.

The word order differs. In al-Qaṣaṣ, rajulun, “a man,” precedes the location phrase. In Yā Sīn, min aqṣā l-madīnah precedes rajulun.

This ordering can affect the prominence of the man or his starting point, but aqṣā retains the same lexical meaning.

Aqṣā l-madīnah alone does not establish that these men were poor, lived in deprived districts, or opposed the city’s political order. Such social identities cannot be extracted from “the farthest part of the city.”

Both accounts show people covering a distance to help someone or defend what they understand to be true, acting on an ethical responsibility.

6. Comparison with related roots

Q Ṣ W (quṣuww) and B ʿ D (buʿd)

Both roots concern distance.

Buʿd has a broad Qur’anic range and can express spatial, temporal, or non-material distance.

The Qur’anic uses of Q Ṣ W concern a distant place or a farther or farthest part of a spatial domain.

Qaṣiyy and baʿīd overlap in meaning, as aqṣā and abʿad can both express greater distance.

Aqṣā is particularly used in the Qur’an to locate a distant part of an area or a place far away.

Q Ṣ W (quṣwā) and D N W (dunyā)

D N W concerns being or drawing near; Q Ṣ W concerns being or moving far away.

The opposition is explicit in 8:42: the nearer bank against the farther bank.

Just as quṣwā does not itself imply moral superiority, dunyā here does not mean baseness.

Q Ṣ W (quṣuww) and Q R B (qurb)

Qurb concerns nearness or a reduced distance between things. Q Ṣ W concerns distance and moving away.

Besides spatial and temporal proximity, the Qur’an uses Q R B for human relationships and nearness to The God.

All five Qur’anic occurrences of Q Ṣ W concern spatial distance and location.

Q Ṣ W (quṣuww) and N B DH (intibādh)

Intibādh concerns withdrawing from a setting or group, while Q Ṣ W describes the distance of a place or person.

The two families occur together in 19:22.

Maryam withdrew, and the place to which she went was distant.

Someone may withdraw from a group while remaining nearby. Withdrawal and distance are thus related but distinct concepts.

Q Ṣ W (quṣuww) and Q Ṣ R (qaṣr)

Although the roots look similar, their semantic fields differ.

Q Ṣ R concerns shortening, restricting, and not extending beyond a defined measure. Q Ṣ W concerns distance and, in some uses, reaching a farthest limit.

Q Ṣ Ṣ, associated with following a trace and recounting events, is a separate family from both.

7. Translation choices for Forqan

  • Al-aqṣā in 17:1: farther or farthest; retain the designation al-Masjid al-Aqṣā and explain its lexical sense.
  • Al-quṣwā in 8:42: farther.
  • Qaṣiyyan in 19:22: distant.
  • Aqṣā l-madīnah in 28:20: the farthest part of the city.
  • Aqṣā l-madīnah in 36:20: the farthest part of the city.

Brief root meaning: Being distant, moving away, and removing to a distance; in elative forms, occupying a farther or farthest position relative to a particular reference point.

Conclusion

Q Ṣ W concerns distance, moving away, and the farthest limits of a place.

The Qur’an uses it for al-Masjid al-Aqṣā, the farther bank of a valley, Maryam’s distant place of withdrawal, and the farthest part of a city.

Qaṣiyy means distant; aqṣā and quṣwā mean farther or farthest according to context.

Spatial distance alone implies neither deprivation nor exclusion, neither moral superiority nor inferiority. Each occurrence must be read with its reference point, location, and context in view.
