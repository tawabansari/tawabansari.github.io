From hāʾ, “take,” to hāʾum: addressing others to take and receive something

1. Meaning and background in Arabic

The entry hā–alif–hamzah in Quranic root indexes concerns the word هَاؤُمُ. Arabic uses this word to address several people with the meaning “take”: the speaker places something at their disposal or offers it to them and asks them to receive it.

Early Arabic recognizes هَا and its lengthened form هَاءَ in this semantic field. When giving something to another person, one can use this expression to say “take” or “take this.” هَاؤُمُ expresses the corresponding plural address.

A distinction is needed between the expression’s background in early Arabic and precise evidence for its use before Islam. This study does not establish an independently datable pre-Quranic witness for the particular form هَاؤُمُ. The Quran itself securely attests the word in the Arabic of its revelation, while early linguists record its various forms in Arab speech.

A verse attributed to al-Kumayt, an Umayyad poet who lived after the Quran’s revelation, reads:

وَفِي أَيَّامِ هَاتِ بِهَاءِ نُلْفَىٰ

wa-fī ayyāmi hāti bi-hāʾi nulfā

إِذَا زَرِمَ النَّدَىٰ مُتَحَلِّبِينَا

idhā zarima al-nadā mutaḥallibīnā

“In days of give and take, even when generosity dries up, we are still found pouring forth gifts.”

Here هات and هاء appear together, evoking giving and receiving. The verse attests the continued use of the expression in Islamic-era Arabic; it does not independently establish the same form in the pre-Islamic period.

The defensible meaning of this family in Arabic close to the period of revelation is therefore a direct instruction to take something offered to the addressee.

2. Linguistic forms and word structure

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • هَا: “take”; the short imperative expression.
  • هَاءَ: “take”; a lengthened form addressing one person.
  • هَاءِ: “take”; one form addressing a female.
  • هَاؤُمَا: “take”; addressing two people.
  • هَاؤُمُ: “take”; addressing a group.
  • هَاؤُنَّ: “take”; addressing a group of females.
  • هَاكَ، هَاكِ، هَاكُم: forms with the suffix of address kāf, conveying the same basic instruction to take.

Linguistic accounts record variations in vowels, hamzah, and the marking of the addressee. These are not all Quranic forms: among the forms considered in this entry, the Quran uses only هَاؤُمُ.

Is hā–alif–hamzah an ordinary verbal root?

A crucial distinction is needed here. هَاؤُمُ is not an ordinary Arabic verb for which past, present, imperative, and verbal-noun forms can be freely constructed.

In the common grammatical analysis, هَاء is an imperative verb-equivalent noun: an expression conveying an imperative verb’s meaning without the structure or inflection of an ordinary verb.

Thus hā–alif–hamzah serves primarily as an index heading identifying this word family. The heading alone does not prove that its three letters form the historical root of a fully inflecting triliteral verb.

The ending of هَاؤُمُ relates to plural address; its mīm should not be treated as part of a semantic core meaning “taking.”

This distinction prevents the invention of derivatives or an etymological history unsupported by Arabic evidence.

3. Quranic usage: هَاؤُمُ

هَاؤُمُ occurs only once in the Quran, in Al-Ḥāqqah.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19

فَأَمَّا مَنْ أُوتِىَ كِتَٰبَهُۥ بِيَمِينِهِۦ فَيَقُولُ هَآؤُمُ ٱقْرَءُوا۟ كِتَٰبِيَهْ

fa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahu bi-yamīnihi fa-yaqūlu hāʾumu iqraʾū kitābiyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19

“As for the one given his record in his right hand, he says: ‘Take it; read my record!’”

The verse depicts a scene on the Day of Reckoning. The person who receives his record of deeds in his right hand does not hide it from others; he invites them to take and read it.

The sentence contains two commands:

هَاؤُمُ means “take,” and ٱقْرَءُوا means “read.”

The first command concerns offering the record for others to receive; the second concerns reading its contents. These two imperative expressions do not mean the same thing. Together they create a clear picture: the speaker offers his record to others and invites them to learn what it contains.

In the next verse, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:20, the speaker describes his awareness that he would face his reckoning. This continuation helps explain why he does not recoil from presenting his record.

By contrast, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:25 portrays someone who receives his record in his left hand and wishes he had never been given it.

In this contrast, the first person invites others to read his record, whereas the second finds even receiving his own record unwelcome.

Joy, confidence, and readiness to disclose the record arise from the context of these verses, rather than from the lexical meaning of هَاؤُمُ alone. The expression fundamentally commands taking and can accompany different emotions in different situations.

4. Syntax and structure

Hāʾum as an imperative verb-equivalent noun

Grammatically, هَاؤُمُ functions as a command and conveys the meaning of خُذُوا, “take.”

An Arabic imperative verb-equivalent noun can perform the function of a command without sharing the morphological structure of an ordinary imperative verb.

هَاؤُمُ addresses a plural audience in this verse. The Persian singular “take” is therefore unsuitable; a plural equivalent more accurately reflects the number of addressees.

The relationship between hāʾum and iqraʾū

هَاؤُمُ and ٱقْرَءُوا concern the same object: the speaker’s record.

Grammarians offer differing accounts of how كِتَٰبِيَهْ relates to the two commands. In the analysis known as competing government, both expressions call for an object, while the second is treated as its overt governor because it is nearer. Another account treats the second clause as an explanation of the first command.

This syntactic disagreement does not decisively alter the scene’s basic meaning: the person invites others to take and read his record.

The final hāʾ in kitābiyah

In the same verse, كِتَٰبِيَهْ also ends in hāʾ. This hāʾ, however, is not the same element as hāʾ in هَاؤُمُ.

In كِتَٰبِيَهْ, yāʾ refers to the speaker, producing “my record.” Grammatically, the final hāʾ is the hāʾ of pause, associated with pausing and pronunciation.

A resemblance between the letters does not establish a shared root or identical function.

5. Comparison with related expressions

Hā–alif–hamzah (هَاؤُمُ) and hā–alif–tāʾ (هَاتُوا)

The two expressions resemble one another in form and setting of use, but need separate examination.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19, هَاؤُمُ tells the addressees to take what is being offered.

In occurrences such as Al-Baqarah 2:111, هَاتُوا means “bring” or “produce.”

The difference in the direction of the action is clear:

With هَاؤُمُ, the speaker offers something toward the addressee and asks the addressee to take it.

With هَاتُوا, the speaker asks the addressee to bring or produce something.

This difference is clear in Quranic usage. Nevertheless, the historical proximity and structural relationship of all forms of hāʾ and hāt require independent investigation; the contrast alone cannot settle their historical origins.

Hāʾum and the attention-calling hā

هَا has other functions in Arabic. In the demonstrative هَٰذَا, for example, it calls attention to what is being indicated.

In that use, hā is not itself a command to take something; it directs the addressee’s attention to the referent.

The sequence hā can also be an attached feminine pronoun referring back to a feminine noun.

These uses should not be presented as semantic derivatives of هَاؤُمُ merely because they look alike. Grammatical category, position, and function in the sentence are decisive.

Hāʾum and khudhū

خُذُوا is an imperative verb from hamzah–khāʾ–dhāl and directly expresses taking.

هَاؤُمُ is an imperative verb-equivalent noun used when offering something to an addressee.

Both can result in someone taking something, but their grammatical structures differ. Nor do all senses of hamzah–khāʾ–dhāl—taking, detaining, seizing, or holding someone accountable—transfer to هَاؤُمُ.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19, the relevant sense is simply inviting the addressees to take the record.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For this entry’s sole Quranic occurrence, the principal proposed rendering is “take it.”

This is short and natural; the Arabic addresses multiple people, although English “take” does not itself mark that distinction.

A polite Persian invitation used while offering something can suit some situations, but it does not state the instruction to take as clearly as the direct plural imperative.

“Come” might convey something of the invitation, but does not sufficiently express the receiving of the object at issue in the verse.

The phrase in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19 can therefore be rendered for Forqan as:

“Take it; read my record!”

Here “take it” conveys هَاؤُمُ, while “read my record” expresses the following command.

7. Conclusion

In Quranic root indexes, hā–alif–hamzah identifies the family of hā and hāʾ used to command taking. Its Quranic form هَاؤُمُ means “take” and addresses a group.

Grammatically, هَاؤُمُ is an imperative verb-equivalent noun. Without evidence it should not be recast as an ordinary triliteral verb with a hypothetical set of derivatives. Attention-calling hā, pronominal hā, and the related expression hātū must likewise be examined according to their actual functions and structures.

The entry’s sole occurrence is Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19, where the person receiving his record of deeds in his right hand invites others to take and read it.

The word’s basic meaning is “take.” The person’s readiness to reveal his record and confidence about his reckoning are understood from the surrounding verses, not from this word alone.

Maintaining this distinction preserves translation accuracy and allows the verse’s message to emerge without loading additional meanings onto a short, clear expression.
