Linguistic Note

The root ح ج ر (Ḥ J R) turns around hardness and restraint. In material experience, stone is hard and resistant; it can build a wall, mark the limit of land, block a road, and separate one space from another. This material image extends into the non-material uses of the root.

When something becomes ḥijr, it is no longer open and boundaryless. It is behind a barrier, restricted from entry, forbidden from free disposal, or enclosed within a protected space. This is why the root can relate to stone, chambers, prohibition, enclosure, and restraining intellect.

In this root, hardness and boundary are not separate. Stone is not only hard; it also creates limits. A wall does not only separate; it also prevents entry. Intellect, when called ḥijr, is not mere knowing; it is a power that restrains a person from crossing limits without control.

Central Meaning

The central meaning of Ḥ J R is hardness, enclosure, and restraining boundary: a limit that prevents unmeasured entry, passage, access, or disposal.

The root begins from the image of stone, but in the Qur’an it is not limited to stone. It may refer to fabricated prohibition, a closed enclosure, a place name, private rooms, a sphere of upbringing and guardianship, or an intellect that restrains the human being from crossing the limit.

Material Image

The material image is a hard and resistant stone: something used to build a wall, separate the house from the outside, block a path, or form a tangible enclosure. Stone is both hard material and a tool of boundary-making.

From this image, several meanings arise naturally. The privacy of a house resembles a stone wall: not everyone may enter. A legal restriction also resembles a wall: it prevents free disposal. A chamber is a space separated from public space, and entering it requires respect for its boundary. Restraining intellect is close to this same field because it prevents the human being from crossing the limit.

Ḥijr as Restricted and Set Apart

One clear Qur’anic use of this root is fabricated religious restriction. In 6:138, the verse says: وَقَالُوا هَٰذِهِ أَنْعَامٌ وَحَرْثٌ حِجْرٌ (wa-qālū hādhihi anʿāmun wa-ḥarthun ḥijr).

Here ḥijr means something removed from free access and placed behind a forbidden boundary. But the Qur’an does not approve this restriction. The phrases بِزَعْمِهِمْ (bi-zaʿmihim) and افْتِرَاءً عَلَيْهِ (iftirāʾan ʿalayhi) show that this boundary is not from The God; it is a fabrication of their distorted religious system.

So ḥijr should not be translated here as “stone.” The meaning is livestock and crops that they declared restricted, limiting access according to their own desire.

Ḥijran Maḥjūran: A Closed and Impassable Enclosure

The phrase حِجْرًا مَّحْجُورًا (ḥijran maḥjūran) is one of the most important Qur’anic uses of this root. The phrase intensifies prohibition, as though describing an enclosure whose entry is closed and whose access is impossible.

In 25:22, seeing the angels brings no good news for the criminals: لَا بُشْرَىٰ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِّلْمُجْرِمِينَ وَيَقُولُونَ حِجْرًا مَّحْجُورًا (lā bushrā yawmaʾidhin lil-mujrimīn wa-yaqūlūna ḥijran maḥjūran). The phrase evokes the closing of access, deprivation, and the lack of safe refuge or good news.

The same phrase appears in the natural image of two waters: وَجَعَلَ بَيْنَهُمَا بَرْزَخًا وَحِجْرًا مَّحْجُورًا (wa-jaʿala baynahumā barzakhan wa-ḥijran maḥjūran) in 25:53. Barzakh is the boundary between two things; ḥijran maḥjūran emphasizes the restraining and impassable quality of that boundary. The two waters are side by side, but not without limit or order.

The two verses share the same core, but not the same context. In 25:22, the subject is deprivation on the day the angels are seen. In 25:53, the subject is the natural order between two waters. Translation should preserve that difference.

Al-Ḥijr and the Companions of al-Ḥijr

The Qur’an mentions the Companions of al-Ḥijr: وَلَقَدْ كَذَّبَ أَصْحَابُ الْحِجْرِ الْمُرْسَلِينَ (wa-laqad kadhdhaba aṣḥābu al-ḥijri al-mursalīn) in 15:80.

Here al-Ḥijr is the name of a place or region associated with a people. Its root connection with stone, a rocky region, or an enclosed place is understandable, but in translation its proper-name function should be preserved: “the Companions of al-Ḥijr” or “the people of al-Ḥijr.”

The following verses clarify the rocky, mountainous field of this people: وَكَانُوا يَنْحِتُونَ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا آمِنِينَ (wa-kānū yanḥitūna mina al-jibāli buyūtan āminīn) in 15:82. They carved homes out of the mountains and built visible security from stone. But stone and fortification cannot replace restraining moral intellect.

Al-Ḥujurāt: Chambers and Privacy

The word al-ḥujurāt also comes from this root: إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يُنَادُونَكَ مِن وَرَاءِ الْحُجُرَاتِ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ (inna alladhīna yunādūnaka min warāʾi al-ḥujurāti aktharuhum lā yaʿqilūn) in 49:4.

Ḥujurāt is the plural of ḥujrah: private chambers or separated spaces with boundaries. The verse is not only about physical location; it is about respect for the boundary between private and public space. Calling from behind the chambers breaks the etiquette of approach and communication.

This use shows how Ḥ J R moves from stone and wall to social adab. A chamber is a space separated by a boundary, and that separation requires respect and permission.

Ḥujūrikum: Lap, Household, and Sphere of Care

Another important form is ḥujūrikum: وَرَبَائِبُكُمُ اللَّاتِي فِي حُجُورِكُم (wa-rabāʾibukumu allātī fī ḥujūrikum) in 4:23.

Ḥujūrikum here points to the lap, household, or intimate sphere of guardianship and upbringing. This meaning is understood from the same axis of enclosure, nearness, and care. Someone in a person’s ḥijr is within that person’s close domestic and protective space.

The ruling of the verse should not be reduced only to physical residence in the house; the verse concerns family relation and marriage prohibition. But at the root level, ḥujūr shows how Ḥ J R can extend from a stone wall to the sphere of upbringing and guardianship.

Dhī Ḥijr: Intellect as Restraining Power

In Surah Al-Fajr, after a series of oaths, the verse asks: هَلْ فِي ذَٰلِكَ قَسَمٌ لِّذِي حِجْرٍ (hal fī dhālika qasamun lidhī ḥijr) in 89:5.

Dhī ḥijr is usually understood as a person of intellect, but the intellect is viewed from a particular angle: its restraining quality. Ḥijr here is not merely the ability to understand; it is wisdom that sets boundaries, restrains rebellion, and prevents the self, power, desire, or haste from crossing the limit.

For translation, “a person of understanding” is smooth, but the explanation should preserve the restraining and boundary-setting aspect. “One with restraining intellect” makes the root more visible, though in fluent translation “the discerning” or “the intelligent” may sometimes read more naturally.

Legal Restriction and Protected Disposal

In later legal language, ḥajr came to mean restricting a person’s free disposal of property or rights, as in the case of childhood, mental incapacity, or lack of mature judgment. This technical legal word does not appear in that form in the Qur’an, but the semantic field is close to some Qur’anic passages.

In 4:6, concerning the property of orphans, the Qur’an commands testing them until maturity and handing over their wealth when sound judgment is recognized. Until that maturity is clear, full disposal is not handed over. This is the logic of protective boundary and restriction, not humiliation.

So later legal ḥajr is not the direct Qur’anic wording of this verse, but it is related to the root’s field: restraint and boundary-setting for the protection of a right.

Distinguishing Qur’anic Uses

The Qur’anic uses of Ḥ J R should not be flattened into one translation. In 6:138, ḥijr concerns fabricated religious restriction. In 25:22, ḥijran maḥjūran appears in the context of criminals seeing the angels and being deprived of good news. In 25:53, the same phrase appears in the natural image of two waters kept apart.

In 15:80, al-Ḥijr is the name of a people’s place. In 49:4, the root concerns private chambers. In 4:23, ḥujūrikum concerns household and guardianship. In 89:5, ḥijr points to restraining intellect.

The shared core is hardness, boundary, restriction, and enclosure, but the contexts differ. If all uses are translated only as “stone,” the social and intellectual meanings disappear. If all uses are translated only as “prohibition,” the material image of the root disappears.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

In أَنْعَامٌ وَحَرْثٌ حِجْرٌ (anʿāmun wa-ḥarthun ḥijr), ḥijr describes the livestock and crops: they have been placed in a restricted state. The phrase بِزَعْمِهِمْ (bi-zaʿmihim) shows that this restriction is their claim, not divine judgment.

In حِجْرًا مَّحْجُورًا (ḥijran maḥjūran), a verbal noun and passive participle from the same root stand together. This structure intensifies the restriction: a prohibition fully closed and kept away.

In وَجَعَلَ بَيْنَهُمَا بَرْزَخًا وَحِجْرًا مَّحْجُورًا (wa-jaʿala baynahumā barzakhan wa-ḥijran maḥjūran), the pairing of ḥijr with barzakh matters. Barzakh marks the boundary between two things; ḥijran maḥjūran emphasizes barred passage and non-penetration.

In مِن وَرَاءِ الْحُجُرَاتِ (min warāʾi al-ḥujurāt), warāʾ is important: calling from behind the chambers means approaching from outside the private boundary without proper etiquette. In فِي حُجُورِكُم (fī ḥujūrikum), fī points to being within a sphere of household care. In لِّذِي حِجْرٍ (lidhī ḥijr), dhī shows ḥijr as a quality or possession of the person.

Difference From Nearby Words

Ḥ J R is close to manʿ, but not identical. Manʿ focuses on preventing or withholding. Ḥijr includes prevention together with the image of a hard boundary, enclosure, and impassability.

Ḥ J R differs from ḥaram. Ḥaram points to sanctity, inviolability, sacred prohibition, or protected sacred space. Ḥijr can indicate prohibition and enclosure, but it does not necessarily mean divine sanctity. In 6:138, people declared something ḥijr, but the Qur’an calls it fabrication.

Ḥ J R is also close to sadd, ḥadd, and barzakh, but they are not the same. Sadd emphasizes blocking a path. Ḥadd marks a limit or measure. Barzakh is an interval or boundary between two things. Ḥijr is a harder and more restraining boundary: it not only separates but also bars entry or passage.

Ḥ J R also touches intellect, but is not identical with ʿaql. ʿAql involves understanding, binding, assessment, and restraint. When ḥijr means intellect, it emphasizes the restraining and boundary-setting aspect of wisdom. Ḥ J R also differs from ṣakhr, which more directly indicates large rock or rocky mass; ḥajar can be stone, but the root’s field expands to prohibition, enclosure, chambers, guardianship, and restraining intellect.

Forqan Translation Method

Forqan should not translate Ḥ J R as “stone” everywhere. In some contexts, such as al-Ḥijr and the Companions of al-Ḥijr, the rocky connection matters. But in many verses, the main contextual meaning is prohibition, enclosure, boundary, or restraint.

For أَنْعَامٌ وَحَرْثٌ حِجْرٌ (anʿāmun wa-ḥarthun ḥijr), “restricted” or “forbidden and set apart” is better than “stone.” For حِجْرًا مَّحْجُورًا (ḥijran maḥjūran), the translation should preserve the intensity of prohibition and closed access. For الْحُجُرَات (al-ḥujurāt), “chambers” or “rooms” is natural, with the privacy of the space explained. For حُجُورِكُم (ḥujūrikum), “your laps” or “your sphere of guardianship” may fit by context. For ذِي حِجْر (dhī ḥijr), “a person of understanding” or “one with restraining intellect” is better than literal wording.

Forqan Translation Suggestions

  • anʿāmun wa-ḥarthun ḥijr: restricted livestock and crops; or livestock and crops set apart as forbidden.
  • ḥijran maḥjūran: a closed enclosure and impassable prohibition; or a barred refuge and closed way.
  • wa-jaʿala baynahumā barzakhan wa-ḥijran maḥjūran: and He placed between them a barzakh and a closed enclosure; or a restraining boundary and impassable enclosure.
  • aṣḥābu al-ḥijr: the Companions of al-Ḥijr; or the people of al-Ḥijr.
  • al-ḥujurāt: the chambers; or the private rooms.
  • min warāʾi al-ḥujurāt: from behind the chambers; or from behind the private rooms.
  • fī ḥujūrikum: in your laps; or within your sphere of guardianship and care.
  • lidhī ḥijr: for one with understanding; or for one with restraining intellect.

Summary

The root Ḥ J R begins from the material image of stone and hardness, then expands into prohibition, enclosure, boundary, and impassability. At the material level, ḥajar is a stone or wall that blocks a path and separates space. At the social level, ḥijr can be the privacy of a house, a chamber, a sphere of guardianship, or a restriction of disposal. At the intellectual level, ḥijr is understanding that restrains a person from crossing limits recklessly.

In the Qur’an, this root appears for fabricated restrictions, the boundary between two waters, the deprivation of criminals, the rocky place of the Companions of al-Ḥijr, private chambers, family guardianship, and restraining intellect.

In short: Ḥ J R in the Qur’an points to stone, hardness, enclosure, prohibition, and restraining boundary: a limit that prevents unmeasured entry, passage, or disposal.
