Nuṭq and manṭiq in early Arabic and the Qur’an: human speech, bird calls, bodily testimony and the speaking record of deeds

The three-letter Arabic root ن ط ق has two established semantic branches. One concerns speech, expression and meaningful sound; the other concerns a garment or band fastened around the waist. Nuṭq, nāṭiq and manṭiq belong to the first branch; niṭāq and minṭaqah belong to the second.

In ordinary human usage, nuṭq means speaking. Arabic also uses this family for the calls of birds and other beings, and figuratively for writing that conveys meaning as though it spoke for itself.

The Qur’an uses the root in several settings: human speech, the expression of birds, idols’ inability to answer, bodily speech at the Resurrection, the testimony of the record of deeds, and the silence of people facing divine judgment.

This range prevents us from reducing nuṭq to movements of the human tongue. It also does not justify turning every use into a philosophical definition such as “revealing truth” or “possessing reason and awareness.” Speech may be true or false, clear or obscure, deliberate or imitative. Truth, understanding and moral responsibility do not follow from the root alone.

1. Semantic Core and Forms

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • نَطَقَ، يَنْطِقُ (naṭaqa, yanṭiqu): he spoke; he speaks.
  • نُطْق (nuṭq): speaking or uttering speech.
  • مَنْطِق (manṭiq): speech, discourse, and sometimes a mode of expression or meaningful vocalization.
  • نَاطِق (nāṭiq): a speaker, or something that speaks.
  • مِنْطِيق (minṭīq): an eloquent or capable speaker.
  • أَنْطَقَ (anṭaqa): caused to speak; granted the capacity for speech.
  • اِسْتَنْطَقَ (istanṭaqa): elicited speech from someone or asked someone to speak.
  • نَاطَقَ و تَنَاطَقَ (nāṭaqa, tanāṭaqa): spoke with another; conversed.
  • نِطَاق و مِنْطَق (niṭāq, minṭaq): a garment or band fastened around the waist.
  • مِنْطَقَة (minṭaqah): in early usage, a kind of girdle or waist covering.

All these forms are established in Arabic, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

The root occurs twelve times in eleven verses across nine surahs, in three morphological categories: nine Form I verbs, two Form IV verbs from anṭaqa, and one occurrence of the noun manṭiq.

The Speech Branch: Nuṭq and Expression

The core of this branch is speaking or producing an expression recognized as speech in the relevant language and setting.

For humans, nuṭq usually concerns uttering speech. For birds, it can refer to their vocalizations or means of communication. When writing is called nāṭiq, its content may be understood to convey a clear meaning or account.

These uses are related, but evidence is needed before treating them all as precisely the same physical process. Human speech, a bird’s call and a book’s expression need not operate in the same way.

Making a sound does not automatically make something nāṭiq in every setting. The sound of a breaking stone or two objects colliding is not ordinarily called speech without a particular contextual reason.

The Other Branch: Niṭāq and the Waist Covering

Niṭāq names a kind of garment or waist band in Arabic. Minṭaq and minṭaqah also belong to this clothing vocabulary.

Because this covering surrounds the waist, related expressions extended to the middle portions of mountains or trees and to bands of water.

Some of these words closely resemble manṭiq, “speech,” in pronunciation and spelling, but their meanings differ.

This matters in root study: shared consonants alone cannot establish that speech originally derived from girding oneself, or that niṭāq developed from the idea of speaking. Such historical claims need independent evidence.

2. Early Arabic: Humans, Birds, Writing and Clothing

Human Speech

The following expression occurs in Arabic lexical explanations of the familiar verb:

نَطَقَ النَّاطِقُ يَنْطِقُ نُطْقًا

naṭaqa al-nāṭiqu yanṭiqu nuṭqan

Translation: “The speaker spoke; he speaks.”

Here nuṭq means speaking, and manṭiq denotes speech or discourse.

Minṭīq describes someone capable and eloquent in speech.

Verbal ability does not establish truthfulness or sound knowledge. An eloquent person may speak falsely or deceptively. Eloquence and truth are separate qualities.

Birds Speaking in Early Poetry

A verse attributed to Abū Qays ibn al-Aslat, an Arab poet near the beginning of Islam, says:

لَمْ يَمْنَعِ الشُّرْبَ مِنْهَا غَيْرَ أَنْ نَطَقَتْ
حَمَامَةٌ فِي غُصُونٍ ذَاتِ أَوْقَالِ

lam yamnaʿi al-shurba minhā ghayra an naṭaqat / ḥamāmatun fī ghuṣūnin dhāti awqāli

Translation: “Nothing kept that she-camel from drinking except a dove calling among branches bearing fruit.”

The verb naṭaqat is used of the dove. Explanations relate the camel’s stopping either to being startled by its call or to a longing to return home awakened by that call. Both readings retain the use of nuṭq for a bird’s voice.

The witness shows that Arabic could apply nuṭq to a bird’s call without requiring the bird to utter sentences in a human language.

The verse is transmitted in early grammatical discussions, but its attribution is disputed. Some versions have hatafat, “called,” instead of naṭaqat. It is evidence for a transmitted Arabic usage; its wording and authorship should not be presented as undisputed.

Writing That Speaks

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

أَوْ مُذْهَبٌ جُدَدٌ عَلَى أَلْوَاحِهِ
النَّاطِقُ الْمَبْرُوزُ وَالْمَخْتُومُ

aw mudhhabun judadun ʿalā alwāḥihi / al-nāṭiqu al-mabrūzu wa-al-makhtūmu

Translation: “Or gold-adorned writing, with markings upon its tablets: expressive writing, some displayed and some sealed.”

The poet describes surviving traces of a former dwelling and compares them to writing.

Nāṭiq describes writing here: although it has no human tongue, it conveys content and means something to its reader.

This witness helps explain the Qur’anic expression kitābun yanṭiqu bi-al-ḥaqq, “a book that speaks with truth”: describing writing as speaking had an established Arabic use.

Some words in the verse have variant readings. Labīd lived both before and after the beginning of Islam. Although this poem is considered part of his early poetry, its exact composition date requires caution.

Niṭāq in Clothing and Nature

In the second branch, niṭāq denoted a garment fastened around the waist. Verbs such as intaṭaqa and tanaṭṭaqa expressed putting it on or girding oneself with it.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

يُحِيلُ فِي جَدْوَلٍ تَحْبُو ضَفَادِعُهُ
حَبْوَ الْجَوَارِي تَرَى فِي مَائِهِ نُطُقَا

yuḥīlu fī jadwalin taḥbū ḍafādiʿuhu / ḥabwa al-jawārī tarā fī māʾihi nuṭuqā

Translation: “It pours [water] into a channel whose frogs crawl like young girls; you see bands in its water.”

Nuṭuq refers here to bands or streaks in the water, compared with bands worn around the waist.

Elsewhere, Arabic used expressions related to niṭāq when water rose to the middle of a tree or mound, as though it formed a band around it.

These witnesses give the clothing branch a concrete place in Arab life and language. That image should not be forced onto the speech branch: speaking cannot be defined as “binding,” “enclosing” or “forming a belt of meaning” without evidence.

3. Human Speech in the Qur’an

Idols Unable to Speak: Abraham and His People

In the account of Abraham breaking the idols, he points to the large idol and tells his people:

قَالَ بَلْ فَعَلَهُ كَبِيرُهُمْ هَٰذَا فَاسْأَلُوهُمْ إِنْ كَانُوا يَنْطِقُونَ

qāla bal faʿalahu kabīruhum hādhā fa-sʾalūhum in kānū yanṭiqūna

Translation: “He said: Rather, this large one of theirs did it; ask them, if they speak.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:63

Yanṭiqūna concerns the ability to speak and answer here.

Abraham draws attention to the powerlessness of idols his people treat as deities. If they cannot answer or defend themselves, how can they deserve that standing?

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65, as the exchange continues, his people acknowledge:

لَقَدْ عَلِمْتَ مَا هَٰؤُلَاءِ يَنْطِقُونَ

laqad ʿalimta mā hāʾulāʾi yanṭiqūna

Translation: “You already know that these do not speak.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65

They acknowledge more than not hearing the idols at one particular moment: they themselves know the idols cannot speak.

Another account of Abraham repeats the question:

مَا لَكُمْ لَا تَنْطِقُونَ

mā lakum lā tanṭiqūna

Translation: “What is wrong with you that you do not speak?”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:92

The lack of speech marks the idols’ inability to answer in these passages. This conclusion follows from the account and the status claimed for them; nuṭq itself does not always affirm or deny divinity.

Nor do these verses prove that every being lacking human speech has no perception or communication. The Qur’an also mentions birds’ expression and bodily speech. Abraham’s argument belongs within its critique of idol worship.

“He Does Not Speak from Desire”: Speech and Its Source

وَمَا يَنْطِقُ عَنِ الْهَوَىٰ ۝ إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا وَحْيٌ يُوحَىٰ

wa-mā yanṭiqu ʿani al-hawā / in huwa illā waḥyun yūḥā

Translation: “And he does not speak from desire. It is nothing but revelation being revealed.”

Al-Najm 53:3 and Al-Najm 53:4

Nuṭq concerns speaking here, while ʿani al-hawā identifies the source or orientation of that speech.

Nuṭq alone means neither speaking from desire nor speaking free of desire. The accompanying expression determines that distinction.

The following sentence, in huwa illā waḥyun yūḥā, connects the speech under discussion with revelation.

The root should therefore not be redefined as “revealed speech.” The same verb occurs in connection with speechless idols, ordinary human speech and other settings.

The scope of the statement must also be assessed in context. The opening of Al-Najm concerns guidance, the messenger’s not being astray, and revelation. The verb in Al-Najm 53:3 alone does not establish that every ordinary, personal utterance of the messenger in every circumstance was divine revelation or legislation. That generalization requires separate evidence and does not belong in the lexical definition of nuṭq.

Human Speech as an Evident Experience

فَوَرَبِّ السَّمَاءِ وَالْأَرْضِ إِنَّهُ لَحَقٌّ مِّثْلَ مَا أَنَّكُمْ تَنْطِقُونَ

fa-wa-rabbi al-samāʾi wa-al-arḍi innahu la-ḥaqqun mithla mā annakum tanṭiqūna

Translation: “Then by the Lord of heaven and earth, it is indeed true, just as you speak.”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:23

Human speech is presented as a familiar, undeniable experience in a comparison affirming the truth of the preceding statement.

Nuṭq is not intrinsically equivalent to truth or certainty: people can speak falsely. The evident fact is that the addressees themselves speak.

The distinction matters: truth follows from the proposition and its emphatic structure, not from the meaning of tanṭiqūna.

4. Birds’ Expression and the Speech of Other Beings

“We Have Been Taught the Speech of Birds”: Solomon’s Understanding

وَوَرِثَ سُلَيْمَانُ دَاوُودَ وَقَالَ يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ عُلِّمْنَا مَنْطِقَ الطَّيْرِ وَأُوتِينَا مِنْ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَهُوَ الْفَضْلُ الْمُبِينُ

wa-waritha sulaymānu dāwūda wa-qāla yā ayyuhā al-nāsu ʿullimnā manṭiqa al-ṭayri wa-ūtīnā min kulli shayʾin inna hādhā la-huwa al-faḍlu al-mubīnu

Translation: “Solomon inherited from David and said: People, we have been taught the expression of birds and given of everything. This is indeed the evident favor.”

Al-Naml 27:16

This is the Qur’an’s only occurrence of the noun manṭiq.

Solomon describes knowledge he has been taught: manṭiq al-ṭayr, the expression of birds.

Manṭiq concerns the speech or expressive means of birds here. Since the Qur’an presents this ability among Solomon’s special favors, it cannot simply mean hearing ordinary bird sounds, something other people can also do.

The surah goes on to recount an ant speaking and the hoopoe’s exchange with Solomon. The setting concerns understanding messages and communicative content as well as hearing sounds.

For example, it says of the ant:

قَالَتْ نَمْلَةٌ يَا أَيُّهَا النَّمْلُ ادْخُلُوا مَسَاكِنَكُمْ لَا يَحْطِمَنَّكُمْ سُلَيْمَانُ وَجُنُودُهُ وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

qālat namlatun yā ayyuhā al-namlu udkhulū masākinakum lā yaḥṭimannakum sulaymānu wa-junūduhu wa-hum lā yashʿurūna

Translation: “An ant said: Ants, enter your dwellings so that Solomon and his troops do not crush you without realizing it.”

Al-Naml 27:18

The verb is qālat, not a derivative of nuṭq. The verse nevertheless illuminates the narrative setting of birds’ expression: it describes a meaningful message conveyed by a nonhuman being.

In Al-Naml 27:20, Al-Naml 27:21, Al-Naml 27:22, Al-Naml 27:23, Al-Naml 27:24, Al-Naml 27:25, Al-Naml 27:26, Al-Naml 27:27 and Al-Naml 27:28, the hoopoe also communicates with Solomon and gives a report about Sabaʾ.

Together these passages portray nonhuman beings as having means of expression and communication that Solomon can understand.

These verses alone do not specify the physical or acoustic mechanism. We should neither insist that birds used the same organs, sounds and grammar as humans nor reduce the explicit account of understanding their expression to ordinary recognition of a few bird calls.

The Qur’anic account establishes Solomon’s special knowledge of other beings’ expression. It does not explain its precise mechanism further.

“The God Made Us Speak”: Bodily Speech

وَقَالُوا لِجُلُودِهِمْ لِمَ شَهِدْتُمْ عَلَيْنَا قَالُوا أَنْطَقَنَا اللَّهُ الَّذِي أَنْطَقَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ وَهُوَ خَلَقَكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

wa-qālū li-julūdihim li-ma shahidtum ʿalaynā qālū anṭaqanā allāhu alladhī anṭaqa kulla shayʾin wa-huwa khalaqakum awwala marratin wa-ilayhi turjaʿūna

Translation: “They say to their skins: Why did you testify against us? They say: The God made us speak, He who made everything speak. He created you the first time, and to Him you are returned.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:21

Anṭaqa is a Form IV verb here. Its distinction from the simple verb is clear in this use:

Naṭaqa means to speak; anṭaqa means to make another speak or give another the capacity for speech.

The skins reply that The God made them speak.

Fuṣṣilat 41:20 describes the testimony of ears, eyes and skins about human deeds. Speaking is therefore connected with testimony here.

The concepts should still be distinguished. Nuṭq describes speaking, while testimony identifies its role as an account bearing witness.

The skins’ speech is directly attributed to divine power. We should not equate it without evidence with the familiar human vocal apparatus or assign it a particular physical mechanism.

“He who made everything speak” also concerns divine power to bring beings to speech. It does not immediately establish that every object in the present world constantly uses verbal human speech.

Yā Sīn 36:65 uses another expression for bodily speech:

وَتُكَلِّمُنَا أَيْدِيهِمْ وَتَشْهَدُ أَرْجُلُهُمْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ

wa-tukallimunā aydīhim wa-tashhadu arjuluhum bi-mā kānū yaksibūna

Translation: “Their hands speak to Us, and their feet testify to what they used to earn.”

The verse uses the root K L M for the hands’ speech and SH H D for the feet’s testimony.

Their occurrence together shows that nuṭq, kalām and testimony can be connected within a scene without having identical lexical meanings.

5. A Book That Speaks with Truth

Two important occurrences of nuṭq concern a book.

The Book and Justice in the Reckoning

وَلَا نُكَلِّفُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا وَلَدَيْنَا كِتَابٌ يَنْطِقُ بِالْحَقِّ وَهُمْ لَا يُظْلَمُونَ

wa-lā nukallifu nafsan illā wusʿahā wa-ladaynā kitābun yanṭiqu bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-hum lā yuẓlamūna

Translation: “We burden no self beyond its capacity. With Us is a book that speaks with truth, and they are not wronged.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:62

The book is described as yanṭiqu bi-al-ḥaqq, “speaks with truth.”

A book does not ordinarily articulate speech like a human. Writing can nevertheless express a truth, present an account and serve as evidence in a judgment.

The connection with truth and the denial of injustice indicate a truthful account and just judgment here.

That conclusion should not become the independent meaning of nuṭq. The book speaks with truth because the verse supplies bi-al-ḥaqq; nuṭq itself does not mean truthfulness or justice.

Nor should the book’s speech in the hereafter be restricted without evidence to silent writing or, conversely, a humanlike voice. The wording conveys testimony and the disclosure of truth but does not fully describe how it takes place.

“This Book of Ours Speaks about You with Truth”

هَٰذَا كِتَابُنَا يَنْطِقُ عَلَيْكُمْ بِالْحَقِّ إِنَّا كُنَّا نَسْتَنْسِخُ مَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

hādhā kitābunā yanṭiqu ʿalaykum bi-al-ḥaqqi innā kunnā nastansikhu mā kuntum taʿmalūna

Translation: “This book of Ours speaks about you with truth. We were recording what you used to do.”

Al-Jāthiyah 45:29

This verse makes the connection between the book’s speech and the recording of deeds clearer.

The sentence continues by describing the recording of people’s actions. The book therefore concerns an account disclosing human deeds in this setting.

The prepositional expression ʿalaykum matters as well. The book speaks about the actions of those being judged and can bear witness against them.

ʿAlā is not part of the root meaning of nuṭq. The preposition identifies the relation of the statement to its addressees and the reckoning.

Simply rendering the phrase “the book talks,” without its setting, may obscure the scene. An explanation should clarify that the book’s recording and disclosure of deeds give it a role in judgment.

These two verses do not establish that every book or piece of writing conveys truth. The truthfulness of this account follows from bi-al-ḥaqq and divine judgment, rather than being an inherent property of all writing.

6. When People Do Not Speak

“So They Do Not Speak”: Facing Judgment

وَوَقَعَ الْقَوْلُ عَلَيْهِمْ بِمَا ظَلَمُوا فَهُمْ لَا يَنْطِقُونَ

wa-waqaʿa al-qawlu ʿalayhim bi-mā ẓalamū fa-hum lā yanṭiqūna

Translation: “The decree falls upon them because of their wrongdoing, so they do not speak.”

Al-Naml 27:85

Lā yanṭiqūna describes people facing divine judgment because of their wrongdoing.

Their silence in this setting is connected with an inability to answer or defend their conduct.

This does not follow from nuṭq alone. It still means speaking, and its negation indicates an absence of speech. Why they do not speak is understood from the judgment scene and the rest of the verse.

“This Is a Day When They Do Not Speak”: Silence at the Resurrection

هَٰذَا يَوْمُ لَا يَنْطِقُونَ ۝ وَلَا يُؤْذَنُ لَهُمْ فَيَعْتَذِرُونَ

hādhā yawmu lā yanṭiqūna / wa-lā yuʾdhanu lahum fa-yaʿtadhirūna

Translation: “This is a day when they do not speak, nor are they permitted to offer excuses.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:35 and Al-Mursalāt 77:36

Silence is associated here with not being permitted to offer excuses.

The issue is thus more than physical inability to make sounds: it also concerns restricted speech in a setting of reckoning and judgment.

Does the Qur’an say that people never speak at any stage of the Resurrection?

Other passages do not support that conclusion. They recount people conversing with one another, making requests to The God and answering questions in other scenes.

For example:

وَأَقْبَلَ بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ يَتَسَاءَلُونَ

wa-aqbala baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin yatasāʾalūna

Translation: “Some of them turn toward others, questioning one another.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:27

“This is a day when they do not speak” should therefore not be turned into a ban on, or impossibility of, every kind of speech throughout the Resurrection in all circumstances.

The verses describe different situations. Some contain conversation and questions; in others a group loses the opportunity to speak or make excuses.

This does not make the uses of nuṭq contradictory. The context and circumstances of speech differ in each scene.

7. Related Roots and Later Terminology

N Ṭ Q and Q W L

Qawl has a broad Arabic semantic range, including saying, expression, reporting speech and sometimes an assertion or judgment.

Nuṭq more directly emphasizes speaking itself and the capacity for, or occurrence of, speech.

In Abraham’s account, for example, both qāla and yanṭiqūna occur. His people make a statement while acknowledging that the idols do not speak.

This contextual distinction does not establish an absolute rule that qawl is always meaningful while nuṭq is mere sound production. Manṭiq al-ṭayr and kitābun yanṭiqu bi-al-ḥaqq show that nuṭq too can convey meaningful content.

Study of Q W L

N Ṭ Q and K L M

Kalām and nuṭq both concern speech, but treating them as perfectly identical is imprecise.

Nuṭq can emphasize speaking or coming to speech; kalām, in familiar uses, concerns meaningful speech and conversation.

The Qur’an uses both families for bodily speech. Their meanings therefore overlap in some settings.

Their appearance in similar scenes alone proves neither complete difference nor absolute synonymy.

Study of K L M

N Ṭ Q and L F Ẓ

Lafẓ also concerns casting something out. In speech, it means uttering words.

Qāf 50:18 says:

مَا يَلْفِظُ مِنْ قَوْلٍ إِلَّا لَدَيْهِ رَقِيبٌ عَتِيدٌ

mā yalfiẓu min qawlin illā ladayhi raqībun ʿatīdun

Translation: “He utters no word without a ready watcher beside him.”

Lafẓ concerns the utterance emerging from the speaker, while qawl concerns what is said.

The use is related to nuṭq, but a single physical image should not be made to explain every distinction among these roots.

N Ṭ Q and SH H D

Shahādah concerns bearing witness; nuṭq concerns speaking.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:21, the skins speak after testifying about human deeds. Nuṭq is the means or mode of expression in that scene; testimony identifies its evidentiary role.

Not every testimony necessarily requires verbal speech, and not every act of speaking is testimony.

Study of SH H D

Qur’anic Manṭiq and the Science of Logic

In modern Persian and Arabic, manṭiq often means sound reasoning, rules of thought or the discipline of logic.

Manṭiq al-ṭayr in Al-Naml 27:16 does not mean philosophical logic or formal rules of inference. It concerns birds’ speech and expressive means.

The technical sense of logic became established in the history of philosophy and rational disciplines. This later usage cannot be projected back into Solomon’s account without evidence.

Philosophical definitions sometimes call the human being a “rational animal,” using nāṭiq for rational and intellectual capacity. That terminology should not determine every Qur’anic use of nuṭq.

The Qur’an itself describes birds’ expression, speaking skins and a speaking book. Restricting this family to human discursive reason does not fit that range.

8. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For نَطَقَ (naṭaqa) in ordinary human use, “spoke” or “speaks” is appropriate, according to tense.

In Abraham’s account, يَنْطِقُونَ (yanṭiqūna) is best rendered “they speak.” “They understand” or “they possess reason” is not the direct equivalent, even though understanding and the idols’ powerlessness matter to the argument.

For مَنْطِقَ الطَّيْرِ (manṭiq al-ṭayr) in Al-Naml 27:16, the Persian “manṭiq of birds” is concise and recognizable. It nevertheless needs explanation as birds’ speech and expression, rather than philosophical logic. In English, “the speech of birds” conveys that distinction directly.

The explanatory alternative “the language of birds” is also defensible, provided it does not necessarily imply human language with all its grammatical and acoustic features.

For أَنْطَقَنَا اللَّهُ (anṭaqanā Allāh) in Fuṣṣilat 41:21, “The God made us speak” naturally preserves the distinction between Form IV and the simple verb.

For كِتَابٌ يَنْطِقُ بِالْحَقِّ (kitābun yanṭiqu bi-al-ḥaqq) in Al-Muʾminūn 23:62, “a book that speaks with truth” is appropriate. An explanation can add that the book discloses a truthful account.

For كِتَابُنَا يَنْطِقُ عَلَيْكُمْ بِالْحَقِّ (kitābunā yanṭiqu ʿalaykum bi-al-ḥaqq) in Al-Jāthiyah 45:29, “our book speaks about you with truth” is appropriate. The contextual explanation can clarify its testimony about deeds.

For لَا يَنْطِقُونَ (lā yanṭiqūna) in Al-Naml 27:85 and Al-Mursalāt 77:35, “they do not speak” is the direct rendering. An inability to defend oneself or lack of permission to offer excuses should be explained from the individual context.

In the other branch, نِطَاق (niṭāq) means a girdle or waist covering. It should not be confused with manṭiq meaning speech.

For Forqan’s word boxes, these Qur’anic uses can be distinguished as follows:

Brief root meaning: in the speech branch, speaking and expression; in the other branch, a waist covering or band.

Meaning in each verse: determined by form and context, such as “they speak,” “birds’ expression,” “made to speak” or “speaks with truth.”

Each derivative can thus retain its precise meaning without imposing one vague general definition on every occurrence.

9. Conclusion

The root has two established Arabic branches: speech and expression, and niṭāq as a waist covering or band. Early usage and lexical evidence attest both. Neither the historical priority of one nor their derivation from a common image is established without independent evidence.

The Qur’an uses only the speech branch. Its derivatives concern human speech, birds’ expression, idols unable to answer, bodily speech, a book expressing truth, and silence in judgment scenes.

Nuṭq in the Qur’an is therefore not limited to human speech. Its breadth does not establish that every being speaks like a human, or that everything described as speaking possesses identical reason, choice or moral responsibility.

Nuṭq itself does not mean truthfulness, testimony, revelation or correct reasoning. These features follow from accompanying words, sentence structure and context. Yanṭiqu bi-al-ḥaqq describes speaking with truth; wa-mā yanṭiqu ʿani al-hawā in Al-Najm 53:3 denies speaking from desire. The root retains its basic speech meaning; the accompanying qualifications and negation determine its orientation.

Final definition: in its speech branch, N Ṭ Q denotes speaking and expression. Depending on speaker and setting, this may be human speech, meaningful vocalization by other beings, or content conveyed through writing. Qur’anic anṭaqa means to make speak, while manṭiq in Solomon’s account concerns birds’ speech or expressive means. The other branch, niṭāq and waist coverings, does not occur in the Qur’an and should remain distinct from the speech meanings.
