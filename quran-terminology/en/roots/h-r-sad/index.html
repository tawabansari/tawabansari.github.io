The Qur’anic Root Ḥ R Ṣ

Ḥaraṣa: intense desire and earnest concern for something sought

The root ح ر ص, Ḥ R Ṣ, occurs five times in the Qur’an: three times in inflected forms of the basic verb حَرَصَ, ḥaraṣa; once as the comparative or superlative أَحْرَص, aḥraṣ; and once as the adjective حَرِيص, ḥarīṣ. The verbal noun ḥirṣ itself does not occur. Even this small family establishes an essential distinction: its Qur’anic meaning cannot be equated with the invariably negative implications of English “greed.”

In English, “greed” suggests excessive, grasping, or insatiable desire. The Qur’an, however, uses this root both for an intense attachment to life and when describing the Prophet as حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ, ḥarīṣun ʿalaykum, and speaking of his earnest desire for people’s guidance. Elsewhere the same verb concerns the desire to maintain justice between wives.

The root itself therefore means neither blameworthy greed nor virtuous compassion. What is desired, together with the sentence’s context, determines its moral direction.

Its semantic core can be expressed as follows:

Ḥirṣ: intense desire, will, and concern directed toward attaining something sought; wanting something strongly and focusing one’s attention and desire upon it.

This intensity may become excessive attachment, may be directed toward justice, or may appear as earnest concern for other people’s welfare and guidance. The root does not itself determine any one of these directions. Nor does earnest concern necessarily entail practical effort: action taken to attain the desired end must be established from context, rather than from the root alone.

Qur’anic forms

حَرَصَ
Ḥaraṣa: basic verb; earnest desire

أَحْرَص
Aḥraṣ: comparative and superlative; more or most eager

حَرِيص
Ḥarīṣ: adjective; eager, earnestly concerned

The semantic field in early Arabic

Alongside the branch concerning intense desire, the family Ḥ R Ṣ has recorded physical uses. Lexical sources describe splitting, scratching, and peeling. Lisān al-ʿArab records ḥāriṣah and ḥarīṣah for a superficial skin wound and for a cloud whose heavy rain affects the earth’s surface. The same entry cites a verse by al-Ḥuwaydirah as evidence for the rain-related usage.

The use of ḥirṣ for intense desire is also attested in early Arabic. Lisān al-ʿArab cites a verse by Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī in which the poet expresses his earnest desire to defend his family, then acknowledges that death cannot be repelled once it arrives. This witness belongs to early Islamic poetry and should not be presented as securely pre-Qur’anic evidence.

The imagery of these two branches can be seen as related, but neither should be declared the other’s certain historical source without evidence. The force of an effect in the physical branch may bear a semantic resemblance to the intensity of desire in the psychological branch; that resemblance alone does not establish a history of derivation.

It is therefore preferable to distinguish two things:

The physical branch concerns affecting, scratching, or splitting a surface.

In the branch used by the Qur’an, the central meaning is intense inclination and concern for something sought.

The physical image should not be imported into verse translations, turning ḥirṣ into “scratching to obtain something” or “splitting a path toward a goal.”

Qur’anic uses: one core with different moral directions

The clearest way to understand Ḥ R Ṣ is to compare the Qur’an’s own uses. The same root occurs in markedly different contexts, showing that its moral force comes from its object and the sentence’s construction.

Al-Baqarah says:

وَلَتَجِدَنَّهُمْ أَحْرَصَ النَّاسِ عَلَىٰ حَيَاةٍ وَمِنَ الَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا ۚ يَوَدُّ أَحَدُهُمْ لَوْ يُعَمَّرُ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ وَمَا هُوَ بِمُزَحْزِحِهِ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ أَنْ يُعَمَّرَ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بَصِيرٌ بِمَا يَعْمَلُونَ

Wa-la-tajidannahum aḥraṣa n-nāsi ʿalā ḥayātin wa-mina lladhīna ashrakū yawaddu aḥaduhum law yuʿammaru alfa sanatin wa-mā huwa bi-muzaḥziḥihi mina l-ʿadhābi an yuʿammara wa-llāhu baṣīrun bi-mā yaʿmalūn.

“You will certainly find them the most avid of people for life, even more than those who associate partners. Each of them wishes to be granted a thousand years of life; yet being granted a long life would not remove him from punishment. The God sees what they do.”

Al-Baqarah 2:96

أَحْرَصَ النَّاسِ عَلَىٰ حَيَاةٍ, aḥraṣa n-nāsi ʿalā ḥayātin, expresses an intense desire for life. The continuation makes that intensity clear: one of them wishes to live for a thousand years.

In this context, “the most avid of people for life” is natural. A rendering with the negative force of “greedy for life” can also fit the passage’s overall direction. That negative force cannot, however, be transferred to the entire root.

In Al-Nisāʾ, the same verb appears in another setting:

وَلَنْ تَسْتَطِيعُوا أَنْ تَعْدِلُوا بَيْنَ النِّسَاءِ وَلَوْ حَرَصْتُمْ ۖ فَلَا تَمِيلُوا كُلَّ الْمَيْلِ فَتَذَرُوهَا كَالْمُعَلَّقَةِ ۚ وَإِنْ تُصْلِحُوا وَتَتَّقُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا

Wa-lan tastaṭīʿū an taʿdilū bayna n-nisāʾi wa-law ḥaraṣtum fa-lā tamīlū kulla l-mayli fa-tadharūhā ka-l-muʿallaqati wa-in tuṣliḥū wa-tattaqū fa-inna llāha kāna ghafūran raḥīmā.

“You will never be able to maintain justice [in full] between wives, however earnestly you desire it. So do not incline entirely toward one, leaving the other suspended in uncertainty. If you put things right and maintain taqwa, The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:129

Here ḥaraṣtum cannot mean “you were greedy.” The matter at issue is justice. The verb expresses the strength of the desire and concern to attain it.

This verse also shows that ḥirṣ is not identical with achieving a result. Someone may want something intensely and work to attain it, but intensity of desire alone does not guarantee that the desired end will be fully realized.

The same point becomes clearer in uses concerning the Prophet:

إِنْ تَحْرِصْ عَلَىٰ هُدَاهُمْ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَهْدِي مَنْ يُضِلُّ ۖ وَمَا لَهُمْ مِنْ نَاصِرِينَ

In taḥriṣ ʿalā hudāhum fa-inna llāha lā yahdī man yuḍillu wa-mā lahum min nāṣirīn.

“If you earnestly desire their guidance, The God does not guide those whom He lets go astray, and they have no helpers.”

Al-Naḥl 16:37

Here the object of the desire is explicit:

عَلَىٰ هُدَاهُمْ

ʿAlā hudāhum.

The Prophet earnestly desires their guidance. The intensity of his desire does not give him the power to determine the outcome within another person. The verse distinguishes wanting an outcome intensely from that outcome actually occurring.

Yūsuf also says:

وَمَا أَكْثَرُ النَّاسِ وَلَوْ حَرَصْتَ بِمُؤْمِنِينَ

Wa-mā aktharu n-nāsi wa-law ḥaraṣta bi-muʾminīn.

“Most people, however earnestly you desire [their faith], are not believers.”

Yūsuf 12:103

Here the object of ḥaraṣta is not stated explicitly; it is understood from the context as the people’s faith.

The syntax matters. بِمُؤْمِنِينَ, bi-muʾminīn, is not a complement of حَرَصْتَ, ḥaraṣta. The main sentence is:

وَمَا أَكْثَرُ النَّاسِ بِمُؤْمِنِينَ

Wa-mā aktharu n-nāsi bi-muʾminīn.

The clause

وَلَوْ حَرَصْتَ

Wa-law ḥaraṣta.

is inserted within it.

This verse therefore does not provide evidence that ḥaraṣa takes the preposition bi-; a syntactic pattern should not be constructed from the mere proximity of two expressions. This does not deny the use of ḥaraṣa with bi- elsewhere in Arabic: Lisān al-ʿArab records that construction in Abū Dhuʾayb’s verse and explains it through the sense of setting one’s mind on something.

Another use appears near the end of Al-Tawbah:

لَقَدْ جَاءَكُمْ رَسُولٌ مِنْ أَنْفُسِكُمْ عَزِيزٌ عَلَيْهِ مَا عَنِتُّمْ حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ

La-qad jāʾakum rasūlun min anfusikum ʿazīzun ʿalayhi mā ʿanittum ḥarīṣun ʿalaykum bi-l-muʾminīna raʾūfun raḥīm.

“A Messenger has certainly come to you from among yourselves. Your suffering weighs heavily upon him; he earnestly desires your welfare, and toward the believers he is compassionate and merciful.”

Al-Tawbah 9:128

Reading حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ, ḥarīṣun ʿalaykum, through “greed” alone could produce an inappropriate sense such as “greedy for you.” The surrounding clauses clarify the expression’s direction:

عَزِيزٌ عَلَيْهِ مَا عَنِتُّمْ

ʿAzīzun ʿalayhi mā ʿanittum.

Your suffering weighs heavily upon him.

And:

بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ

Bi-l-muʾminīna raʾūfun raḥīm.

Toward the believers he is compassionate and merciful.

In this context, ḥarīṣun ʿalaykum expresses the Prophet’s earnest concern for people and their welfare.

Taken together, these verses clarify the root’s semantic range. Ḥirṣ may concern life, justice, faith, guidance, or people’s welfare. The desired end and the context determine the moral value of each use.

The preposition and the object of concern

In the Qur’anic uses of this family, عَلَى, ʿalā, introduces the object of concern with both the verb and adjectival forms:

أَحْرَصَ النَّاسِ عَلَىٰ حَيَاةٍ

Aḥraṣa n-nāsi ʿalā ḥayātin.

And:

تَحْرِصْ عَلَىٰ هُدَاهُمْ

Taḥriṣ ʿalā hudāhum.

ʿAlā here identifies the matter toward which intense inclination and concern are directed.

The preposition alone does not establish a moral meaning. The same construction can concern both life and guidance.

In:

حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ

Ḥarīṣun ʿalaykum.

the people themselves are the object of concern, and the context shows that this concern is for their welfare.

In:

وَلَوْ حَرَصْتُمْ

Wa-law ḥaraṣtum.

the object is omitted because it is clear from the context: the matter is justice.

In Yūsuf 12:103, likewise, the object of the desire is understood as the people’s faith, not derived from the bi- in bi-muʾminīn.

When translating ḥirṣ, the first question is therefore what is being desired, rather than reducing the verb or preposition alone to one fixed meaning.

Ḥ R Ṣ (ḥirṣ), Ṭ M AYN (ṭamaʿ), and R GH B (raghab): earnest desire, expectation, and inclination

Ḥirṣ, ṭamaʿ, and raghbah can all approach desire or wanting in translation, but they view this experience from different angles.

Ḥ R Ṣ (ḥirṣ) emphasizes intensity of desire and concern for what is sought. The main issue is the degree and strength of the desire.

Ṭ M AYN (ṭamaʿ) more closely concerns looking toward, hoping for, or expecting to obtain something. A person regards something as desirable and expects or hopes to attain it. This root is not inherently negative in the Qur’an either, and should not always be translated as blameworthy greed.

R GH B (raghab) more closely concerns the direction of inclination and desire. The sentence’s construction and preposition can indicate turning toward something or turning away from it.

As tendencies, then:

Ḥirṣ is primarily about intensity of desire;

ṭamaʿ is primarily about looking toward attainment;

and raghbah is primarily about the direction of inclination.

These are not absolute boundaries. Their semantic fields can overlap in actual usage. Nor should the distinctions be turned into a psychological sequence in which inclination always comes first, then expectation, then intense desire. The language itself does not establish such an order.

For Qur’anic translation, the main consequence is that ḥirṣ should not simply be equated with ṭamaʿ or reduced to “greed.” The uses حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ, ḥarīṣun ʿalaykum, and تَحْرِصْ عَلَىٰ هُدَاهُمْ, taḥriṣ ʿalā hudāhum, alone show why that equation does not hold.

Translation proposals for Forqan

Because “greed” carries a negative judgment in English, one fixed equivalent cannot serve all the Qur’anic uses of this family.

In Al-Baqarah 2:96:

أَحْرَصَ النَّاسِ عَلَىٰ حَيَاةٍ

Aḥraṣa n-nāsi ʿalā ḥayātin.

“The most avid of people for life”

is appropriate, since the context also describes an intense attachment to a long life.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:129:

وَلَوْ حَرَصْتُمْ

Wa-law ḥaraṣtum.

“However earnestly you desire it.”

This is more accurate than “however greedy you are,” since it does not import an English term’s negative judgment into the verse.

In Al-Naḥl 16:37:

إِنْ تَحْرِصْ عَلَىٰ هُدَاهُمْ

In taḥriṣ ʿalā hudāhum.

“If you earnestly desire their guidance.”

In Yūsuf 12:103:

وَلَوْ حَرَصْتَ

Wa-law ḥaraṣta.

“However earnestly you desire [their faith].”

And in Al-Tawbah 9:128:

حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ

Ḥarīṣun ʿalaykum.

“He earnestly desires your welfare.”

Here “welfare” is not a literal translation of a separate word. It is understood from the context: “your suffering weighs heavily upon him” and “toward the believers he is compassionate and merciful.” Retaining a phrase such as “greedy for you” in English could lead the reader toward a meaning the verse does not convey.

“Having an intense concern” is also suitable in an explanation of the root, but is not always the most natural verse translation. The translation should remain fluent English, with the more detailed analysis provided in the root study.

Conclusion

The core of Ḥ R Ṣ is intensity of desire, will, and concern for something sought. A person does not merely want something: the desire has grown strong, and their attention is focused upon it.

The crucial distinction is that ḥirṣ has no fixed moral value of its own. The Qur’an speaks of intense desire for life, but uses the same root for the Prophet’s earnest concern for people’s guidance and welfare, and for the desire to maintain justice. “Greed,” “avarice,” and “excessive acquisitiveness” therefore cannot define the whole root. A positive or negative force arises from the object of concern and the context.

Compared with nearby roots, ḥirṣ more strongly foregrounds intensity of desire, while ṭamaʿ approaches expectation of attainment, and raghbah the direction of inclination. These are tendencies, not absolute boundaries or fixed psychological stages.

The remaining lexical caution concerns the relationship between this family’s physical and psychological branches. Arabic records uses for splitting, scratching, and affecting a surface alongside ḥirṣ for intense desire. A resemblance in imagery can be recognized, but the evidence is insufficient to determine the historical direction of derivation. The physical image should therefore not be presented as the certain origin of the Qur’anic meaning.

A concise definition of the root can be stated as follows:

Ḥ R Ṣ: intense desire, will, and concern directed toward attaining something sought; wanting something strongly. This intensity has no fixed moral value in itself: its object, motivation, and context determine its direction.
