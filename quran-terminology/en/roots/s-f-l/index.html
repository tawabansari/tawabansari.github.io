The Arabic root S F L concerns being low, occupying a lower part, and descending from a higher position. Sufl contrasts with ʿulw, just as asfal contrasts with aʿlā and al-suflā with al-ʿulyā.

The family’s primary meaning is spatial: something is lower relative to something else, such as a building’s lower part, a mountain’s lower slope, a lower position in a valley, or an object’s underside.

The relation extends beyond space to social position, rank, power, value, and outcome. Sāfil may therefore describe someone lower in position or someone regarded as base in conduct and moral standing.

The Qur’an uses this root in different settings: a lower geographical position, the overturning of Lot’s people’s settlements, the defeat and subordination of opponents of truth, the lowest level of punishment, and humanity’s return to «أَسْفَلَ سَافِلِينَ».

This diversity prevents restricting every use to moral degradation or punishment in the hereafter. A low location, a defeated group, a decline in rank, and humanity’s ultimate fall receive their particular meanings from each verse’s construction and context.

1. The root in early Arabic

Sufl: being below as opposed to being above

Arabic lexical accounts define al-sufl and al-sufūl in contrast to al-ʿulw and al-ʿuluww: being below rather than above.

This identifies the family’s central meaning.

Spatially, sufl al-dār denotes the lower part of a house, and asfal al-shayʾ an object’s lower part.

Al-sāfilah also contrasts with al-ʿāliyah for lower and upper parts of spears, rivers, and other places or objects.

Being lower is relational in these uses. Identifying the lower part requires knowing its relation to a higher part or the object’s orientation.

A building section may be lower than the floor above while itself standing above ground. It is not at the lowest point in the world; it is compared with another part of that building.

Safala and safula are recorded for being low or moving down. Tasaffala likewise concerns movement or position in a downward direction.

A lexical definition expresses the family’s basic meaning as follows:

«السِّينُ وَالْفَاءُ وَاللَّامُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ مَا كَانَ خِلَافَ الْعُلُوِّ»

al-sīnu wa-al-fāʾu wa-al-lāmu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa mā kāna khilāfa al-ʿuluwwi

That is: “Sīn, fāʾ, and lām have one semantic basis: what stands opposite to elevation.”

The definition identifies lowness without initially adding a particular moral or social judgment.

A witness from early Arabic poetry

Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī says in a poem:

بِأَطْيَبَ مِنْ فِيهَا إِذَا جِئْتَ طَارِقًا
وَأَشْهَى إِذَا نَامَتْ كِلَابُ الْأَسَافِلِ

bi-aṭyaba min fīhā idhā jiʾta ṭāriqan / wa-ashhā idhā nāmat kilābu al-asāfili

“That honey is no sweeter than her mouth when you visit by night, nor more delightful when the dogs of the lower valleys have fallen asleep.”

In this passage the poet compares his beloved’s sweetness with honey.

Al-asāfil here refers to the lower reaches of valleys inhabited by herders. The poetic explanation associates their late sleeping with tending, tethering, and milking their livestock.

Kilāb al-asāfil are thus dogs of the lower valleys, not dogs belonging to morally base people.

The witness illustrates a spatial meaning: a real geographical relation that does not require any moral judgment of the inhabitants.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived through the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. His poetry survives in the early Arabic tradition, but the precise date of this passage is unknown. It can serve as evidence from near the age of revelation without being classified conclusively as pre-Islamic.

Sāfilah: the lower part of objects and routes

Arabic records sāfilat al-rumḥ for the lower part of a spear, near its butt end.

Here sāfilah names a particular section, rather than assessing its value or quality.

Sāfilat al-nahr likewise denotes a river’s lower reaches, in contrast to ʿāliyat al-nahr.

Upstream and downstream are determined by the flow and course of the water. Being downstream does not make a location undesirable.

Sufālat al-rīḥ also expresses a position relative to the direction of the wind.

These uses show how the family identifies spatial relations and direction in concrete, everyday language.

Its primary meaning should not be converted from the outset into moral decline or worthlessness.

From spatial lowness to inferiority in rank

Arabic also records safāl and safālah for low rank and base conduct.

The derogatory expression siflat al-nās referred to people considered low in conduct or social standing.

Lowness has here extended from space to rank.

This extension is intelligible: just as people speak of higher and lower physical positions, they use height and lowness for status, power, and social value.

Two kinds of judgment must nevertheless be distinguished.

One concerns low social or economic position; the other moral baseness and objectionable conduct.

They are not necessarily the same.

Someone may be poor or lack social standing while remaining honorable and upright. A person with power and elevated status may, conversely, act oppressively.

The root does not establish that everyone with a low income or a subordinate job is morally base.

In moral uses, an unfavorable evaluation depends on conduct and the criteria of judgment, rather than social or economic position alone.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

S F L occurs ten times across nine Qur’anic verses.

The occurrences fall into three morphological groups:

أَسْفَل: the elative meaning lower or lowest. This group has six occurrences, including the singular asfal and al-asfal and the plural al-asfalīn.

السُّفْلَى: the feminine elative, contrasting with al-ʿulyā. It occurs once, in Al-Tawbah 9:40.

سَافِل: an active participle or description meaning situated below or lowly. This group occurs three times as sāfilahā and sāfilīn.

Sāfilahā occurs in Hūd 11:82 and Al-Ḥijr 15:74, and sāfilīn in Al-Tīn 95:5.

Other Arabic forms include safala, safula, sufūl, safālah, tasaffala, suflah, and asāfil; they do not occur in the Qur’an in these exact forms.

Asfal alone does not always mean an absolutely lowest rank.

In Al-Anfāl 8:42 it makes a spatial comparison between two positions. In constructions such as al-dark al-asfal, however, it identifies the lowest level within a specified domain.

Choosing “lower” or “lowest” therefore depends on the sentence’s construction.

3. Spatial lowness in the Qur’an

The caravan’s position in Al-Anfāl 8:42

Al-Anfāl 8:42 states:

إِذْ أَنْتُمْ بِالْعُدْوَةِ الدُّنْيَا وَهُمْ بِالْعُدْوَةِ الْقُصْوَىٰ وَالرَّكْبُ أَسْفَلَ مِنْكُمْ

idh antum bi-al-ʿudwati al-dunyā wa-hum bi-al-ʿudwati al-quṣwā wa-al-rakbu asfala minkum

Al-Anfāl 8:42

“When you were on the nearer bank and they on the farther bank, and the caravan was below you.”

Three spatial positions are identified.

The addressees occupy the nearer bank, their opponents the farther bank, and the caravan a lower position.

Asfala minkum expresses the caravan’s position relative to the addressees.

Asfal itself makes no moral judgment about the caravan.

Its lower route does not mark its people as inferior in worth or humanity to the other groups.

The same verse uses al-dunyā and al-quṣwā to express nearness and distance.

This contrast matters: al-dunyā belongs to D N W, whereas asfal belongs to S F L.

Here al-dunyā means nearer, while asfal indicates a lower position.

Nearness and lowness may coincide in some settings, but they remain different relations.

A place may be nearer yet higher, or more distant yet lower.

Lowness in Al-Anfāl 8:42 is thus clearly spatial and requires no added notion of defeat, humiliation, or moral decline.

An attack from above and below

Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 states:

إِذْ جَاءُوكُمْ مِنْ فَوْقِكُمْ وَمِنْ أَسْفَلَ مِنْكُمْ وَإِذْ زَاغَتِ الْأَبْصَارُ وَبَلَغَتِ الْقُلُوبُ الْحَنَاجِرَ وَتَظُنُّونَ بِاللَّهِ الظُّنُونَا

idh jāʾūkum min fawqikum wa-min asfala minkum wa-idh zāghati al-abṣāru wa-balaghati al-qulūbu al-ḥanājira wa-taẓunnūna bi-Allāhi al-ẓunūnā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:10

“When they came upon you from above you and from below you, when eyes swerved in fear and hearts reached the throats, and you entertained various thoughts about God.”

Fawqakum and asfala minkum stand opposite each other.

They identify directions or positions from which the attacking forces approached.

The context concerns confrontation and military pressure.

Asfal therefore refers to the lower direction or lower part of the geographical setting.

It does not mean the attackers were weaker, nor necessarily that one group was morally lower than another.

“From above you and from below you” conveys a threat extending from multiple directions.

The intensity of fear and difficulty comes from other parts of the verse, rather than S F L itself.

The upper and lower parts of Lot’s people’s settlements

Hūd 11:82 states:

فَلَمَّا جَاءَ أَمْرُنَا جَعَلْنَا عَالِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا وَأَمْطَرْنَا عَلَيْهَا حِجَارَةً مِنْ سِجِّيلٍ مَنْضُودٍ

fa-lammā jāʾa amrunā jaʿalnā ʿāliyahā sāfilahā wa-amṭarnā ʿalayhā ḥijāratan min sijjīlin manḍūdin

Hūd 11:82

“When Our command came, We made its upper part its lower part and rained upon it stones of layered sijjīl.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:74 likewise states:

فَجَعَلْنَا عَالِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا وَأَمْطَرْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ حِجَارَةً مِنْ سِجِّيلٍ

fa-jaʿalnā ʿāliyahā sāfilahā wa-amṭarnā ʿalayhim ḥijāratan min sijjīlin

Al-Ḥijr 15:74

“We made its upper part its lower part and rained upon them stones of sijjīl.”

In both verses, ʿāliyahā contrasts with sāfilahā.

The former denotes the upper part and the latter the lower part.

The context concerns the transformation and overturning of settlements.

“We made its upper part its lower part” conveys the severity of destruction through an inversion of upper and lower positions.

The word’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from a full account of how the event occurred.

Sāfilahā does not itself name an earthquake, ground collapse, or specific geological process.

The verse describes overturning without detailing its natural mechanism.

Nor does this usage directly give sāfil an ethical meaning.

It refers here to the settlement’s lower part. Condemnation of Lot’s people’s conduct comes from the surrounding narrative, rather than the word used for that part of the settlement.

These verses clearly retain the root’s spatial meaning.

4. Lower rank, power, and standing

The lower word and the higher word

Al-Tawbah 9:40 states:

وَجَعَلَ كَلِمَةَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا السُّفْلَىٰ وَكَلِمَةُ اللَّهِ هِيَ الْعُلْيَا

wa-jaʿala kalimata alladhīna kafarū al-suflā wa-kalimatu Allāhi hiya al-ʿulyā

Al-Tawbah 9:40

“He made the word of those who disbelieved the lower, while the word of The God is the higher.”

Al-suflā and al-ʿulyā contrast in this verse.

The comparison concerns the two sides’ word or declared position, rather than the physical height of two objects.

Low and high here concern rank, ascendancy, and standing.

Al-suflā is the feminine elative and agrees in grammatical gender with kalimah.

Al-ʿulyā is likewise a feminine elative, from ʿ L W.

The contrast demonstrates the extension from spatial lowness to subordination in rank and the outcome of a confrontation.

In context, the disbelievers’ word is made lower, and God’s word is described as higher.

Al-suflā does not itself mean lying, disbelief, or falsehood. Those characteristics depend on the speakers and the verse’s context.

Becoming lower here need not mean the physical lowering of a sound or written text: it concerns the word and position’s subordination before truth.

The defeat of those who plotted against Abraham

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:98 states:

فَأَرَادُوا بِهِ كَيْدًا فَجَعَلْنَاهُمُ الْأَسْفَلِينَ

fa-arādū bihi kaydan fa-jaʿalnāhumu al-asfalīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:98

“They intended a plot against him, so We made them the lower ones.”

The verse continues the account of Abraham and his people’s opposition.

They sought to overcome him through their plan and action, but the outcome is presented as contrary to their intention.

Al-asfalīn here conveys their subordination and defeat.

“The defeated” is a defensible contextual rendering; the construction’s more direct image is being made lower. These levels of meaning should be distinguished in explanation.

Spatially, asfal can identify a lower position. In this account, however, power and the outcome of a confrontation are at issue.

“The lower ones” retains that image, while defeat and failure can be explained through the context.

The verse does not establish that everyone who loses a confrontation is morally base.

This group’s subordination is connected with its plot against Abraham, rather than a general rule about all defeated people.

Being placed underfoot and humiliated

Fuṣṣilat 41:29 states:

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا رَبَّنَا أَرِنَا الَّذَيْنِ أَضَلَّانَا مِنَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنْسِ نَجْعَلْهُمَا تَحْتَ أَقْدَامِنَا لِيَكُونَا مِنَ الْأَسْفَلِينَ

wa-qāla alladhīna kafarū rabbanā arinā alladhayni aḍallānā mina al-jinni wa-al-insi najʿalhumā taḥta aqdāminā li-yakūnā mina al-asfalīn

Fuṣṣilat 41:29

“Those who disbelieved said: Our Lord, show us the two who led us astray from among jinn and humans, so that we may put them beneath our feet and they may be among the lowest.”

Two connected images appear.

First, taḥta aqdāminā: beneath our feet.

Second, li-yakūnā mina al-asfalīn: so that they may be among the lowest.

The first image is spatial. The speakers’ desired result, however, is the humiliation and lowering of those they regard as responsible for their misguidance.

This clearly connects spatial meaning with rank.

Placing someone underfoot depicts humiliation and subordination.

The statement is nevertheless attributed to people making this request in the hereafter.

Their words alone do not establish that every detail of their demand is fulfilled or that their judgment of others’ responsibility is complete and correct.

Al-asfalīn should not be translated as “those who misled others.” Misguidance is expressed elsewhere in the verse; lowliness is the outcome the speakers desire.

5. The lowest level of punishment and humanity’s lowest condition

Al-dark al-asfal: the Fire’s lowest level

Al-Nisāʾ 4:145 states:

إِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ فِي الدَّرْكِ الْأَسْفَلِ مِنَ النَّارِ وَلَنْ تَجِدَ لَهُمْ نَصِيرًا

inna al-munāfiqīna fī al-darki al-asfali mina al-nāri wa-lan tajida lahum naṣīrā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:145

“The hypocrites are in the lowest depth of the Fire, and you will never find a helper for them.”

Al-asfal modifies al-dark.

Dark can denote a depth or lower level; here it identifies a position in the Fire.

Al-asfal specifies that this level is the lowest.

The expression therefore means more than “low”: it identifies the lowest level in the domain under discussion.

This comes from the elative construction and its combination with dark. S F L does not necessarily denote the lowest point in every use.

Why is this usage moral and eschatological?

The verse speaks about hypocrites.

Al-asfal itself does not mean hypocrisy. Hypocrisy describes the conduct and belief of the group introduced at the beginning.

Al-asfal specifies their level of punishment.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:146 then mentions those who repent, reform, hold fast to God, and make their religion sincere for Him.

The sequence prevents separating the warning from the possibility of return and reform.

The lowest level of punishment in Al-Nisāʾ 4:145 is thus a judgment within a context of hypocritical conduct and its outcome, rather than a lexical definition equating every kind of lowness with hypocrisy.

Asfala sāfilīn: the lowest of the low

Al-Tīn 95:4 states:

لَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ فِي أَحْسَنِ تَقْوِيمٍ

la-qad khalaqnā al-insāna fī aḥsani taqwīm

Al-Tīn 95:4

“We certainly created the human being in the finest constitution.”

Then Al-Tīn 95:5 states:

ثُمَّ رَدَدْنَاهُ أَسْفَلَ سَافِلِينَ

thumma radadnāhu asfala sāfilīn

Al-Tīn 95:5

“Then We returned him to the lowest of the low.”

This is one of the root’s most significant Qur’anic uses.

Asfal is an elative and sāfilīn the plural of sāfil.

Their combination expresses extreme lowliness and the lowest position.

The expression alone does not establish whether that lowliness is bodily, moral, or eschatological. Its referent must be examined through the preceding and following verses.

First reading: declining ability in old age

An early reading understands the expression as a human decline from youthful bodily strength and stability to the weakness of old age.

It attends to the relationship between Al-Tīn 95:4 and Al-Tīn 95:5.

A human being is created in a fine constitution, grows, and gains ability, but may lose some physical and cognitive powers toward life’s end.

This is a familiar human experience.

Al-Naḥl 16:70 speaks of some people being returned to the most debilitated period of life, so that after knowing they no longer know anything.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 addresses the same subject with ardhali al-ʿumur.

The bodily reading can therefore be examined in relation to other Qur’anic verses.

It nevertheless raises an important difficulty.

Al-Tīn 95:6 states:

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ فَلَهُمْ أَجْرٌ غَيْرُ مَمْنُونٍ

illā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti fa-lahum ajrun ghayru mamnūn

Al-Tīn 95:6

“Except those who believe and do righteous deeds; theirs is an unending reward.”

If the fifth verse concerns only universal aging and bodily weakness, the exception for believers requires explanation.

Believers too grow old and may lose physical and cognitive abilities.

Under this reading, the exception can concern their continuing reward: even when age prevents some actions, the value of their earlier good deeds is not lost.

This explanation fits “an unending reward,” but not all its details are directly stated in the wording.

The aging reading cannot therefore be ruled out; nor should it imply that elderly people’s disability signals moral degradation or loss of human dignity.

Aging and illness are bodily conditions, not measures of moral worth.

Second reading: moral decline and humanity’s final outcome

Another reading connects asfala sāfilīn with humanity’s fall from a fine created condition to a low rank in conduct and final outcome.

On this understanding, aḥsani taqwīm foregrounds humanity’s fine constitution and capacities, which alone do not guarantee right conduct or choices.

People may use their abilities for goodness and reform or for oppression, corruption, and denying truth.

In such a context, asfala sāfilīn may describe the final decline of someone who misuses those capacities against truth.

The exception in Al-Tīn 95:6 connects clearly with this reading: those who believe and act righteously are excepted from this lowliness.

Al-Tīn 95:7 also speaks of al-dīn as recompense and accountability, and Al-Tīn 95:8 calls God aḥkami al-ḥākimīn.

The ending therefore concerns judgment, recompense, and human destiny.

These indications strengthen a moral and eschatological reading.

Asfala sāfilīn is nevertheless not, by itself, a name for Hell. An eschatological referent would follow from the surrounding context, not from sāfilīn lexically meaning the inhabitants of the Fire.

Which reading fits better?

Both readings have supporting considerations.

The aging reading considers the visible transition from strength to weakness and can be compared with verses about ardhali al-ʿumur.

The moral and final-outcome reading connects more directly with the exception of faith and righteous action and the conclusion about recompense and judgment.

This study therefore favors the second reading on contextual grounds. That is an interpretive preference, not the root’s definitive lexical meaning; the first reading remains open to examination alongside verses about the stages of human life.

The basic translation can retain “the lowest of the low,” leaving identification of its referent to a separate explanation.

This keeps the word’s meaning clear without replacing the Qur’anic wording with a particular interpretation.

6. Comparison with related roots

S F L and ʿ L W: being below and being above

ʿ L W is the principal semantic opposite of S F L.

ʿUluww concerns being high, superiority, and rising; sufl expresses being low and inferior position.

Hūd 11:82 contrasts ʿāliyahā with sāfilahā.

Al-Tawbah 9:40 likewise contrasts al-suflā with al-ʿulyā.

The roots operate within a shared relational field but point in opposite directions.

Physical height is not necessarily moral virtue, just as physical lowness is not necessarily moral baseness.

Moral value in social and religious usages comes from context.

S F L and D N W: lowness and nearness

D N W concerns nearness, drawing closer, and accessibility in the Qur’an.

Al-Anfāl 8:42 uses both families within one spatial description.

Al-dunyā identifies the nearer bank, whereas asfala minkum expresses the caravan’s lower position.

Dunūw and sufl thus convey distinct relations here.

Al-dunyā in al-ḥayāt al-dunyā should consequently not be translated as a base or worthless life merely because it is compared with the hereafter.

The nearness of present life and a lower rank are different concepts.

S F L and T Ḥ T: being lower and being underneath

Taḥt denotes being underneath something.

It establishes a particular spatial relation, as beneath a foot, a tree, or a building.

Asfal can instead express a lower position relative to another location, rank, or standing.

Fuṣṣilat 41:29 combines taḥta aqdāminā and mina al-asfalīn in one sentence.

The first depicts being placed underfoot; the second expresses the low position desired by the speakers.

The words are related but play different roles in the sentence.

S F L and D R K: lowliness and lower depth

D R K concerns reaching and pursuing, and in some uses depths and lower levels.

Al-dark al-asfal in Al-Nisāʾ 4:145 expresses the Fire’s lowest level.

Dark identifies the level or position, and asfal its being the lowest.

The words thus complement each other within the expression.

Its existence does not make every occurrence of asfal a reference to a level of punishment.

In Al-Anfāl 8:42 the same family simply describes the caravan’s geographical position.

S F L and KH F Ḍ: being low and lowering

KH F Ḍ concerns lowering, bringing down, and reducing height or intensity.

Al-Ḥijr 15:88 uses «وَاخْفِضْ جَنَاحَكَ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ» in a context of gentle, humble treatment of believers.

Lowering the wing here depicts humility and kindness.

S F L, by comparison, more directly concerns a low condition or inferior rank.

Someone can voluntarily show humility without becoming morally base.

Khafḍ as humility should therefore not be equated with safālah as baseness.

The image of downward movement can have positive, negative, or neutral significance according to context.

S F L and H B Ṭ: a lower position and descending

H B Ṭ concerns descending or moving down from one position to another.

Al-Baqarah 2:36 and Al-Baqarah 2:38 contain the command ihbiṭū in Adam’s story.

Movement and transfer to another position are prominent there.

S F L can simply describe being low without any movement taking place.

The caravan in Al-Anfāl 8:42 is below the addressees; asfal does not itself say it descended from a higher elevation.

A verb of hubūṭ, by contrast, brings descent or transition into view.

Being low and moving down are related but distinct concepts.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

One fixed equivalent is unsuitable for every occurrence because spatial and rank-related usages differ.

أَسْفَلَ مِنْكُمْ — Al-Anfāl 8:42: “below you.”

This preserves the spatial relation.

مِنْ أَسْفَلَ مِنْكُمْ — Al-Aḥzāb 33:10: “from below you” or “from your lower side.”

جَعَلْنَا عَالِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا — Hūd 11:82 and Al-Ḥijr 15:74: “We made its upper part its lower part.”

“Overturned” can explain the result, while the basic rendering can retain the contrast between the two words.

كَلِمَةَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا السُّفْلَى — Al-Tawbah 9:40: “made the word of those who disbelieved the lower.”

فَجَعَلْنَاهُمُ الْأَسْفَلِينَ — Al-Ṣāffāt 37:98: “so We made them the lower ones.”

“The defeated” conveys a contextually intelligible result, while “the lower ones” retains the direct image.

لِيَكُونَا مِنَ الْأَسْفَلِينَ — Fuṣṣilat 41:29: “so that they may be among the lowest.”

Humiliation here follows from its association with “beneath our feet.”

الدَّرْكِ الْأَسْفَلِ مِنَ النَّارِ — Al-Nisāʾ 4:145: “the lowest depth of the Fire” or “the lowest level of the Fire.”

أَسْفَلَ سَافِلِينَ — Al-Tīn 95:5: “the lowest of the low.”

This preserves the elative and the intensity of lowliness without replacing the words from the outset with an interpreted referent such as aging, misguidance, or Hell.

Further lexical forms may be rendered as follows; sāfil and suflā belong to families also represented in the Qur’an:

  • سُفْل: below, or the lower part.
  • سُفُول: being low or descending.
  • سَفَلَ / سَفُلَ: was situated below or became lower in rank.
  • سَافِل: situated below, lowly, or base, according to context.
  • سَافِلَة: the lower part of something.
  • سُفْلَى: lower or lowest; the feminine form.
  • تَسَفَّلَ: descended or reached a lower rank.
  • سَفَالَة: baseness or low conduct in evaluative usage.

Asfal should not always become “morally base” in Qur’anic translation.

Nor should every descent or lower position be treated as a sign of divine anger or moral failure.

In spatial verses lowness is geographical; in confrontation and judgment passages it may convey inferior power, standing, or outcome.

Conclusion

S F L concerns being low and occupying a lower position or rank.

Its concrete meaning appears in lower building sections, downstream river reaches, the undersides of objects, and geographical positions. Abū Dhuʾayb’s poetic witness also uses asāfil for lower valleys.

Arabic extends this field to status and rank, allowing lowness to concern power, social standing, the value of conduct, and human destiny.

The root occurs ten times across nine Qur’anic verses, with distinct uses.

Al-Anfāl 8:42 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 concern geographical position. In Hūd 11:82 and Al-Ḥijr 15:74, the contrast between upper and lower expresses the overturning of settlements.

Al-Tawbah 9:40 makes the disbelievers’ word lower and describes God’s word as higher. In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:98, Abraham’s opponents are made lower. Fuṣṣilat 41:29 records people wishing to place those who misled them underfoot and among the lowest.

Al-dark al-asfal in Al-Nisāʾ 4:145 denotes the Fire’s lowest level.

In Al-Tīn 95:5, asfala sāfilīn expresses extreme lowliness. Declining ability in old age and moral decline with a final adverse outcome are both readings to examine; the exception of faith and righteous action and the judgment context support the second.

The root may therefore be defined as follows:

S F L denotes being low or lower relative to another position or rank. Its direct usage is spatial and concrete; social, moral, and eschatological contexts can extend it to inferior power, standing, value, and outcome.

Being low is not intrinsically a virtue or a fault. Positive or negative value depends on the subject and context.

This matters especially when considering people. A low economic, bodily, or social position does not by itself indicate moral baseness. Physical ability, political power, and high social standing likewise do not automatically confer moral superiority.

The family’s moral Qur’anic uses concern the lowering of a false word or position, the defeat of those acting against truth, and the outcome of human conduct.

For Forqan’s translations, “lower” or “below” suit spatial uses, “lowly” or “inferior” suit rank-related uses, and “lowest” suits constructions identifying the lowest level.
