The Root ف ق د — Losing, Failing to Find, and Looking for What Is Absent

(nafqidu)
We are missing

The three-consonant Arabic root ف ق د concerns the absence, loss, or failure to find something previously in a person’s possession, reach, or awareness. فَقَدَ الشَّيْءَ is used when someone loses something, no longer finds it where expected, or becomes deprived of it.

Within the same family, تَفَقَّدَ means checking and inquiring into the condition of someone or something, particularly to establish whether what was expected to be present is still there.

An important distinction separates something being beyond a person’s reach, not knowing its location, and its actual destruction. Something may be lost but remain intact elsewhere. Someone may also be absent from a gathering without the reason yet being known.

The root occurs three times in three Qur’anic verses: تَفْقِدُونَ in Yusuf 12:71, نَفْقِدُ in Yusuf 12:72, and تَفَقَّدَ in An Naml 27:20.

The first two concern the king’s measuring cup in Joseph’s story. The third concerns Solomon’s inspection of the birds and his recognition that the hoopoe is not where expected.

The core meaning concerns something whose presence is expected being beyond a person’s reach or sight; in the branch تفقّد, it concerns checking and inquiring into its presence, absence, or condition.

1. The Meaning in Early Arabic

Losing and being deprived of something

In established Arabic usage, فَقَدَ يَفْقِدُ means to lose, be deprived of, or fail to find something.

فَقَدْتُ الشَّيْءَ means “I lost the thing” or “I can no longer find it.”

فَقْد and فِقْدَان name this condition.

Early lexicons explain it through عَدِمَهُ and ضَاعَ مِنْهُ: something has been lost to a person or is no longer found with them.

For example, someone traveling may notice that their money pouch is not in its usual place and say that they have lost it.

This does not yet establish whether the money was stolen, dropped somewhere, or placed elsewhere by its owner.

“I have lost it” describes the person’s access and awareness; the cause is a separate matter.

In Maqayis al-Lughah, Ibn Faris identifies the family’s basic meaning as the disappearance or loss of something.

Al-Raghib al-Isfahani also distinguishes the absence of something that previously existed from the nonexistence of something that has never yet existed.

This distinction is useful: we usually lose something we had or expected to have within reach.

It does not mean that the person must be the legal owner in every usage. Someone may be responsible for an object and fail to find it in its place without owning it.

Losing a person

Arabic uses فَقَدَ وَلَدَهُ of someone who has lost their child.

Early lexicons also use فَاقِد for a woman who has lost her child or husband.

In such uses, loss may accompany death, separation, and grief.

The root itself, however, does not mean death.

When someone says “I have lost my child,” the child may be alive, with only their location unknown.

When speaking of a child’s death, by contrast, loss refers to deprivation of the presence of someone whose ordinary return is no longer possible.

The kind and cause of loss are therefore determined by context.

Grief may likewise follow loss, but it is not the direct meaning of the verb.

Someone may lose a low-value object and feel little sadness; another may experience profound grief at losing a loved one.

Loss has occurred in both cases, but emotional intensity depends on what was lost, its importance, and the person’s circumstances.

2. Evidence from Pre-Islamic Poetry: Losing a Loved One and Supporter

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Muhalhil ibn Rabi‘ah, concerning the killing of his brother Kulayb, says:

كَانَ الذَّخِيرَةَ لِلزَّمَانِ فَقَدْ أَتَى
فُقْدَانُهُ وَأَخَلَّ رُكْنَ مَكَانِي

Kāna dh-dhakhīrata li-z-zamāni fa-qad atā / fuqdānuhu wa-akhalla rukna makānī.

Translation: “He was the reserve and support against time; now his loss has come and weakened a pillar of my standing.”

فُقْدَانُهُ here refers to losing someone of great importance to the poet.

The poet is not merely describing someone no longer being visible. He describes losing a person whose presence supported his life and social standing.

أَخَلَّ رُكْنَ مَكَانِي expresses the consequence: that person’s absence has undermined part of the poet’s stability.

The usage shows that early Arabic could apply loss to a person and its emotional and social consequences.

There is also an important written distinction in this line.

فَقَدْ أَتَى contains two separate elements:

فَـ is a connecting particle.

قَدْ is a particle accompanying a past-tense verb.

This part of فَقَدْ أَتَى is therefore not derived from ف ق د.

فُقْدَانُهُ, however, does derive from that root.

Identical written letters are thus insufficient to establish a root without considering vowels and sentence structure.

Historical reliability of the evidence

Al-Muhalhil is a well-known pre-Islamic poet, and early literary and historical works transmit his poetry about Kulayb’s killing.

This line occurs among poems attributed to al-Muhalhil. The second half also has variant readings, including وَأَخَلَّ رُكْنَ مَكَانِي and وَأَحَالَ رُكْنَ مَكَانِي. This variation calls for care in establishing the text.

The poem has nevertheless reached us through accounts transmitted centuries later.

Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet is known, but we do not have a copy contemporary with its composition.

The example can illuminate early use of “loss,” without making every historical detail or textual reading certain and undisputed.

Its central linguistic point is that loss can denote deprivation of a valued person’s presence without the root itself being limited to grief or death.

3. تَفَقَّدَ: Checking and Inquiring

تَفَقَّدَ الشَّيْءَ means to examine or inquire into something, particularly to establish its presence, absence, or condition.

تَفَقَّدَ أَحْوَالَ القَوْمِ can likewise mean examining people’s circumstances and learning their needs or conditions.

The person is not merely reporting a loss; they take action to establish the situation.

For example, someone responsible for a group may check its members’ attendance when an activity begins.

If someone is not seen, they may ask where that person is or why they are absent.

This differs from losing something.

Someone may conduct a check and ultimately find that nobody is absent.

Conversely, someone may lose something without taking any action to find it.

Thus, فقد expresses absence or failure to find; تفقد expresses checking and inquiring to establish the situation.

Lexicons also record اِفْتَقَدَ for missing something or looking for it when absent.

اِفْتَقَدَ and تَفَقَّدَ are not identical in every sentence.

The former can emphasize noticing an absence or seeking what is missing.

When checking people or objects, the latter is closer to inquiry into their condition.

Form V does not, however, require a prolonged, repeated, or intensely anxious search. Such features must come from context.

4. Linguistic Forms and Derivatives

The principal forms include:

  • فَقَدَ: he lost something, became deprived of it, or failed to find it within reach.
  • يَفْقِدُ: he loses or fails to find.
  • فَقْد: loss or absence from a person’s reach.
  • فِقْدَان and فُقْدَان: loss or deprivation of someone’s or something’s presence.
  • فَاقِد: someone who has lost a person or thing.
  • مَفْقُود: someone or something absent or missing.
  • فَقِيد: a person or thing that has been lost; in social usage, especially a deceased person.
  • أَفْقَدَ: he caused someone to lose something.
  • تَفَقَّدَ: he checked or inquired to establish presence, absence, or condition.
  • تَفَقُّد: checking, inspection, and inquiry.
  • اِفْتَقَدَ: he failed to find something or sought it during its absence.
  • اِفْتِقَاد: noticing an absence or seeking what is beyond reach.
  • تَفَاقَدَ: in a recorded usage, people lost one another.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Only three verbs from this family occur there:

تَفْقِدُونَ — a Form I verb.

نَفْقِدُ — a Form I verb.

تَفَقَّدَ — a Form V verb.

Thus, although common in Arabic, nouns such as فقدان, فاقد, مفقود, and فقيد are not derivatives occurring in the Qur’anic text.

5. مَاذَا تَفْقِدُونَ: What Are You Missing?

Yusuf 12:71

قَالُوا وَأَقْبَلُوا عَلَيْهِمْ مَاذَا تَفْقِدُونَ

Qālū wa-aqbalū ʿalayhim mādhā tafqidūn.

Translation: “Turning toward them, they said: What are you missing?”

تَفْقِدُونَ here is from the basic verb فَقَدَ.

The speakers are Joseph’s brothers, who turn toward the callers after hearing an accusation of theft and ask what they cannot find.

مَاذَا identifies the subject of the question.

They want to know what is missing.

Asking about the missing object, not admitting the accusation

In the preceding verse, the caravan had been called thieves.

But in verse 71, Joseph’s brothers do not ask, “What have we stolen?”

They ask instead:

«مَاذَا تَفْقِدُونَ»

Mādhā tafqidūn.

That is, “What are you missing?”

This difference matters.

Their question concerns a missing object, not an admission that they stole it.

Someone responding to an accusation may ask, “What is missing from you?”

Such a question is not a confession of theft.

In Yusuf 12:73, the brothers explicitly say they did not come to cause corruption and were not thieves.

An accusation or confession should therefore not be extracted from تَفْقِدُونَ itself when its meaning contains neither.

6. نَفْقِدُ صُوَاعَ الْمَلِكِ: The Missing Royal Measuring Cup

Yusuf 12:72

قَالُوا نَفْقِدُ صُوَاعَ الْمَلِكِ وَلِمَنْ جَاءَ بِهِ حِمْلُ بَعِيرٍ وَأَنَا بِهِ زَعِيمٌ

Qālū nafqidu ṣuwāʿa l-maliki wa-li-man jāʾa bihi ḥimlu baʿīrin wa-anā bihi zaʿīm.

Translation: “They said: We are missing the king’s measuring cup. Whoever brings it shall have a camel-load, and I guarantee it.”

نَفْقِدُ is a first-person plural imperfect verb.

The speakers announce that they cannot find the king’s measuring cup.

صُوَاعَ الْمَلِكِ is the object whose absence is being reported.

A reward is then offered to whoever brings it.

Does نَفْقِدُ mean the cup has been destroyed?

No.

Here it means that the cup is not found with the speakers or where expected.

The verb does not establish that it has broken, been destroyed, or disappeared forever.

The story itself shows that the cup still existed.

Yusuf 12:70 says that, while the brothers’ supplies were being prepared, the object was placed in Joseph’s brother’s baggage.

Yusuf 12:76 then describes its removal from that brother’s baggage.

Loss here therefore concerns the object not being where expected or within the callers’ reach, rather than its actual destruction.

Does loss prove theft?

This part of the story clarifies a fundamental distinction.

Something may not be found in its usual place.

Until the reason for its absence is examined, its being missing does not itself prove that someone stole it.

The Qur’an has already reported the placement of the object in Joseph’s story.

The reader therefore knows that this is not simply an object being accidentally lost.

At the same time, the caller in Yusuf 12:70 accuses the caravan of theft.

This accusation must not be equated with the lexical meaning of نَفْقِدُ.

“We cannot find the cup” reports the object’s situation; “You are thieves” accuses people of particular conduct.

Neither statement, by itself, establishes the other.

This distinction matters in daily life as well as in Joseph’s story.

If an object is missing at home or work, failure to find it is not sufficient grounds to accuse someone of theft.

Its location and how it was moved must first be examined.

This is an application of the passage’s meaning; the root ف ق د itself does not name a legal rule or a complete method of investigating theft.

صُوَاع and سِقَايَة

Yusuf 12:70 uses السِّقَايَة.

In Yusuf 12:72, the callers identify the same object as صُوَاعَ الْمَلِكِ.

سقاية relates to providing drink and to a drinking vessel.

صواع denotes a measure or a vessel used for measuring.

In the story, the terms can refer to one object with different uses.

Neither word, however, derives from ف ق د.

In the root discussion, نَفْقِدُ expresses only failure to find that object.

Its name, function, and ownership come from other words in the sentence.

7. وَتَفَقَّدَ الطَّيْرَ: Solomon Checks the Birds

An Naml 27:20

وَتَفَقَّدَ الطَّيْرَ فَقَالَ مَا لِيَ لَا أَرَى الْهُدْهُدَ أَمْ كَانَ مِنَ الْغَائِبِينَ

Wa-tafaqqada ṭ-ṭayra fa-qāla mā liya lā arā l-hudhuda am kāna mina l-ghāʾibīn.

Translation: “He checked the birds and said: What is the matter with me that I do not see the hoopoe? Or is he among the absent?”

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of Form V from ف ق د.

The subject is Solomon.

The object is الطَّيْرَ: the birds.

Solomon checks the birds’ condition and, during that inspection, notices that he cannot see the hoopoe.

How does تفقّد differ from فقد?

In Joseph’s story, نَفْقِدُ concerns the cup being beyond the speakers’ reach.

In Solomon’s story, تَفَقَّدَ describes an action he performs: checking the birds.

He then notices that the hoopoe is not in sight.

This sequence shows that checking need not begin only after definite knowledge that something is missing.

Someone may inspect a group routinely and identify an absence during the check.

A teacher taking attendance, for example, first checks the class and then discovers that a student is absent.

Checking precedes recognition of the absence in this case.

The wording of An Naml 27:20 presents such a sequence.

“Why do I not see the hoopoe?”

Solomon first says:

«مَا لِيَ لَا أَرَى الْهُدْهُدَ»

Mā liya lā arā l-hudhud.

That is, why do I not see the hoopoe?

The expression concerns not seeing the bird.

Someone may fail to see something because it is not present.

It may also be present but hidden by an obstacle or a limitation of vision.

Not seeing it does not, by itself, establish the full reason for its absence.

Solomon then says:

«أَمْ كَانَ مِنَ الْغَائِبِينَ»

Am kāna mina l-ghāʾibīn.

That is, or is he among the absent?

The question distinguishes not seeing the hoopoe from its actual absence.

At this point, the verse itself does not explain why the hoopoe is absent.

Its reason should therefore not be treated as known before its report is heard.

Does checking mean knowing everything?

No.

Solomon checks the birds, but at that moment he does not know where the hoopoe is.

In An Naml 27:22, the hoopoe returns and says it has learned something Solomon did not know.

It then reports news from Sheba.

Solomon’s ability to organize and inspect the group does not mean absolute knowledge of all information.

In this story, even a bird in his group can bring news not previously available to him.

This follows from the narrative context; تفقّد itself means only checking and inquiring.

8. Checking, Responsibility, and Verifying News

An Naml 27:17 describes Solomon’s forces being assembled from jinn, humans, and birds.

He subsequently checks the birds’ condition.

تفقّد الطير thus occurs in a context of managing and coordinating a group.

Someone responsible for a task may need to know who is present, what resources are available, and how work is progressing.

This responsibility is not identical with a claim to know everything or a right to treat others arbitrarily.

Solomon’s response to the hoopoe’s absence

In An Naml 27:21, Solomon speaks severely about the hoopoe, mentioning severe punishment or killing unless it brings a clear justification for its absence.

This statement should neither be omitted nor rewritten as though no threat had been made.

Yet the verse itself also allows the presentation of clear justification.

The narrative treats the absence and the hoopoe’s responsibility seriously while leaving room to explain the reason.

Testing the report’s truth

An Naml 27:27

قَالَ سَنَنْظُرُ أَصَدَقْتَ أَمْ كُنْتَ مِنَ الْكَاذِبِينَ

Qāla sa-nanẓuru a-ṣadaqta am kunta mina l-kādhibīn.

Translation: “He said: We shall examine whether you have spoken truthfully or are among the liars.”

This follows the hoopoe’s report about Sheba.

Solomon hears the report but does not immediately declare it true solely on the speaker’s assertion.

He says its truth must be examined.

This accords with the checking at the beginning of the narrative, although سَنَنْظُرُ belongs to another root.

First, Solomon checks the birds’ presence.

Then the reason for the hoopoe’s absence becomes a question.

After its return, the truth of its report is also examined.

This sequence shows that checking presence, hearing an explanation, and testing a report’s truth are three different activities.

The root ف ق د does not express all these stages by itself, but the narrative connects it with them.

9. Distinguishing Loss, Checking, and Missing

These three forms share a root without being identical in every use.

فقد

فَقَدَ emphasizes losing or failing to find something.

Someone may notice, for example, that a key is not in its usual place.

At this stage, they say they have lost it.

تفقّد

تَفَقَّدَ concerns checking and inquiring into a situation.

The same person may check pockets, a room, and likely locations to establish where the key is.

Alternatively, they may check their belongings before leaving home to see whether anything is missing.

Checking can therefore precede or follow recognition of a loss.

افتقاد

اِفْتَقَدَ can denote either failing to find something or looking for it during its absence.

When applied to people, it sometimes accompanies a sense of absence and longing.

That feeling is not required in every use of the verb.

Someone may miss an object without having any particular emotional attachment to it.

Only forms of فَقَدَ and تَفَقَّدَ occur in the Qur’an; اِفْتَقَدَ does not.

The latter’s meaning should therefore be explained lexically, without listing it as a Qur’anic derivative.

10. Comparison with Related Roots

ف ق د and و ج د

وجد often concerns finding and gaining access to something.

فقد concerns failing to find something or its passing beyond reach.

In Joseph’s story, the cup is not found where expected; Yusuf 12:76 subsequently describes its removal from the brother’s baggage.

In Solomon’s story, the hoopoe is initially unseen, but after returning it reports what it has found.

These examples show how losing and finding can represent opposite conditions.

وجد has other broad Qur’anic uses, however, not all of which simply oppose losing an object.

ف ق د and غ ي ب

غيب concerns being hidden or absent from sight or awareness.

فقد concerns someone not finding the thing within reach or where expected.

The concepts converge in An Naml 27:20.

Solomon does not see the hoopoe and asks whether it is among the absent.

Being absent may therefore cause something to be missing from someone’s sight or reach, but the concepts are not identical.

Someone may be absent while their location is known.

Another person may lose something without knowing whether it has left the place or merely been moved.

ف ق د and ض ل ل

In some Arabic usages, ضلال concerns becoming lost or passing beyond reach.

In the Qur’an, however, the root also has extensive uses concerning misguidance and departure from the right path.

The three Qur’anic occurrences of ف ق د concern a missing object or checking the birds’ condition.

Losing an object must therefore not automatically be turned into moral misguidance.

Nor does every instance of misguidance mean that someone is physically lost.

ف ق د and ب ح ث

بحث concerns searching and investigation.

Al Ma’idah 5:31 describes a raven digging in the earth.

The Qur’anic use of تفقّد concerns checking the birds and recognizing the hoopoe’s absence.

Someone may investigate in order to find something, but not every check requires digging or a prolonged search.

The roots overlap in the notion of searching but differ in their direction of use.

ف ق د and ط ل ب

طلب concerns wanting and seeking something.

فقد concerns something being absent or beyond reach.

A person may seek something they have never had.

In loss, however, something whose presence or availability was expected is usually not found.

In تفقّد, seeking connects with checking presence or absence.

ف ق د and ف ق ر

These roots differ only in their final consonant.

ف ق د concerns losing and being deprived of something.

ف ق ر concerns poverty and need.

Loss of property may impoverish someone, but not every loss is poverty, and not every poor person has necessarily lost a particular possession.

Similarity in spelling and the possible overlap of some instances do not make the roots identical.

11. Morphology and Syntax

ف ق د is a sound three-consonant root.

Its basic verb has the forms:

فَقَدَ يَفْقِدُ

These are its past and imperfect forms.

In Yusuf 12:71, تَفْقِدُونَ is a second-person masculine plural imperfect verb.

Its subject is the group whom Joseph’s brothers ask about the missing object.

In Yusuf 12:72, نَفْقِدُ is a first-person plural imperfect verb.

Its object is صُوَاعَ الْمَلِكِ.

In both verses, the Form I verb is transitive.

وَتَفَقَّدَ

In An Naml 27:20, تَفَقَّدَ is a third-person masculine singular past-tense verb in Form V.

ف is the first root consonant; the preceding ت belongs to the Form V pattern, and ق is doubled.

The object is الطَّيْرَ.

Solomon is therefore not merely thinking about the birds; he is checking their condition.

Syntactically, the next clause begins with فَقَالَ and gives Solomon’s statement after the check.

This sequence matters: checking the birds, noticing that the hoopoe is unseen, and asking about its absence.

Active and passive participles

فَاقِد is an active participle: someone who has lost or been deprived of something.

مَفْقُود is a passive participle: what has been lost or passed beyond reach.

فَقِيد is also used for a person or thing that has been lost.

This distinction also matters in Persian.

Someone who has lost a child is deprived of that presence.

The child who is missing, however, is the one described as مفقود.

The person experiencing loss must not be confused with its object.

12. Challenges of Understanding and Translation

The first error is equating loss with certain destruction.

Something may be beyond a person’s reach yet continue to exist.

The king’s measuring cup in Joseph’s story is a clear example.

The second error is equating being lost with being stolen.

In Yusuf 12:72, the speakers say they cannot find the cup; theft is a separate claim requiring relevant evidence.

The third error is limiting تفقّد to finding something already known to be lost.

Solomon checks the birds and notices the hoopoe’s absence during that process.

Checking can therefore establish a situation rather than merely follow a loss.

The fourth error is inferring Solomon’s complete knowledge from his ability to inspect.

He does not see the hoopoe and does not know why it is absent. It later brings news he did not previously possess.

The fifth error is turning checking and inquiry into permission to accuse.

The loss of an object or a person’s absence does not establish cause or intention without evidence.

The sixth error is making تفقّد a principle of constant, unlimited surveillance of others’ private lives.

The passage in An Naml concerns checking the birds within the body Solomon manages.

That specific situation does not authorize intrusion into people’s private affairs.

Al Hujurat 49:12 also prohibits spying.

Responsibly checking the condition of those engaged in a shared task differs from intruding without cause into others’ privacy.

The seventh error is identifying loss with grief.

Loss can cause grief, but the word itself does not mean crying, mourning, or longing.

The intensity and kind of response depend on what was lost and the person’s circumstances.

13. Translation Recommendations for Forqan

For general uses of فَقَدَ, suitable equivalents include “lost,” “was deprived of,” and “could not find.”

فَقْد and فِقْدَان can be rendered “loss,” “being lost,” or “deprivation of someone’s presence.”

Natural equivalents of تَفَقَّدَ include “checked,” “inspected,” and, where appropriate, “inquired to establish the condition.”

فَاقِد denotes someone who has lost something, while مَفْقُود means “missing” or “lost.”

Qur’anic forms

Yusuf 12:71 — تَفْقِدُونَ

“What are you missing?”

This rendering is brief, natural, and accurate.

Yusuf 12:72 — نَفْقِدُ

“We are missing the king’s measuring cup.”

This must not be understood as establishing theft or the cup’s destruction.

An Naml 27:20 — وَتَفَقَّدَ الطَّيْرَ

“He checked the birds.”

This is more appropriate than “He lost the birds,” since Solomon checks their condition and then notices that he cannot see the hoopoe.

General root meaning and contextual meanings

Brief root meaning: Losing and being deprived of; failing to find something where expected; checking and inquiring into its presence or condition.

تَفْقِدُونَ — Yusuf 12:71: What are you missing or unable to find? Second-person plural imperfect verb.

نَفْقِدُ — Yusuf 12:72: We cannot find the king’s measuring cup within reach; first-person plural imperfect verb.

تَفَقَّدَ — An Naml 27:20: He checked the birds’ condition to establish their presence or absence; a Form V past-tense verb.

The general root meaning must remain distinct from each form’s meaning in its verse; historical discussion and comparison of roots do not replace a brief contextual meaning.

14. Synthesis

In early Arabic, ف ق د concerns losing, being deprived of, and something being absent from a person’s reach.

The poem attributed to al-Muhalhil ibn Rabi‘ah illustrates loss of a valued person whose absence has major emotional and social consequences.

Lexical sources connect فقد with loss and تفقّد with checking and inquiring when something is absent or may be absent.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an.

In Yusuf 12:71, the brothers ask what is missing.

In Yusuf 12:72, the callers say they cannot find the king’s measuring cup.

In An Naml 27:20, Solomon checks the birds and then asks why he cannot see the hoopoe.

These uses establish a clear distinction:

فقد concerns failing to find something or its passing beyond reach; تفقّد is the action of checking and establishing its condition.

Loss itself does not disclose the reason for absence.

A lost object may not have been destroyed; an absent person may have a valid explanation; and failure to find an object does not itself prove another person’s theft.

Joseph’s story distinguishes the report of the missing cup from the fact that it had been placed in his brother’s baggage.

Solomon’s story likewise distinguishes checking the birds, identifying the hoopoe’s absence, hearing its explanation, and testing its report.

Understanding this root therefore requires distinguishing actual absence, a person’s awareness of it, its cause, and action taken to find or investigate.

This care clarifies the lexical meaning of ف ق د and prevents hasty judgments about causes and others’ responsibility merely because an object is missing or a person absent.
