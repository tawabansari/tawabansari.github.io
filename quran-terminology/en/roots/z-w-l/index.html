Core meaning: departing from a former place or condition; shifting, going away, and disappearing. Extended uses include a state ending, power or stability being lost, and attempts to change or dislodge something.

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root ز و ل concerns movement, displacement, departure from a position, and the ending of a condition.

زَالَ الشَّيْءُ عَنْ مَكَانِهِ means that something moved or departed from its place.

زَالَ الْمُلْكُ means that sovereignty ended or power was lost.

زَالَتِ الشَّمْسُ عَنْ كَبِدِ السَّمَاءِ refers to the sun passing the middle of the sky.

A shared idea is evident: something previously in a position or condition no longer remains there.

The change may be spatial, as when a stone moves from its place.

It may concern time or circumstances, as when a reign ends.

It may also entail disappearance, such as the destruction of a power or structure.

Yet زوال does not invariably mean total annihilation. Something may move without ceasing to exist.

One early lexical analysis defines the root as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَنَحِّي الشَّيْءِ عَنْ مَكَانِهِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā tanaḥḥī al-shayʾi ʿan makānihi

Meaning: “One underlying principle indicating something’s departure from its place.”

This clarifies the physical picture: something occupies a place and then leaves it.

In extended uses, the position may be a rank, condition, power, or situation that no longer continues.

First example: departure in al-Aʿshā’s poetry

Al-Aʿshā, a celebrated poet of early Arabic, says in a poem about Sumayyah’s departure:

رَحَلَتْ سُمَيَّةُ غُدْوَةً أَجْمَالَهَا
غَضْبَى عَلَيْكَ فَمَا تَقُولُ بَدَا لَهَا

raḥalat sumayyatu ghudwatan ajmālahā / ghaḍbā ʿalayka fa-mā taqūlu badā lahā

هَذَا النَّهَارَ بَدَا لَهَا مِنْ هَمِّهَا
مَا بَالُهَا بِاللَّيْلِ زَالَ زَوَالُهَا

hādhā al-nahāra badā lahā min hammihā / mā bāluhā bi-l-layli zāla zawāluhā

Translation and sense:

“Sumayyah saddled her camels in the morning, angry with you—what do you think occurred to her?” The next line questions her concern by day and her presence or imagined presence at night. Its final phrase has differing readings: in one, her apparition departs as she did; in another, the phrase is a curse wishing her disappearance.

زَالَ and زَوَال concern departure and disappearance in these interpretations.

The final hemistich has variant vocalizations and explanations.

One reading has accusative زَوَالَها, relating her apparition’s departure at night to her own departure by day.

Another has nominative زَوَالُها and explains it as a traditional imprecation, wishing her disappearance or destruction.

The variation prevents presenting every detail of the line as having one certain interpretation.

Nevertheless, departure and disappearance remain within the expression’s semantic field in these explanations.

The example illustrates usage in poetry and ordinary experiences of separation. It does not by itself establish a history of philosophical terminology or precisely date the poem before the Qur’an.

Second example: struggle in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes struggling with a restless horse:

فَبِتْنَا قِيَامًا عِنْدَ رَأْسِ جَوَادِنَا
يُزَاوِلُنَا عَنْ نَفْسِهِ وَنُزَاوِلُهُ

fa-bitnā qiyāman ʿinda raʾsi jawādinā / yuzāwilunā ʿan nafsihi wa-nuzāwiluhu

Translation:

“We spent the night standing at our horse’s head; he struggled to free himself from us, and we struggled to control him.”

The circumstantial expression in the first hemistich is transmitted as قِيَامًا, وُقُوفًا, or عُرَاةً. These are variant wordings, not identical descriptions of posture or clothing.

The second hemistich, the principal lexical evidence, expresses mutual struggle in the familiar transmissions.

يُزَاوِلُنَا describes the horse’s attempt to free himself from the people.

نُزَاوِلُهُ expresses their corresponding effort to control the horse.

مزاولة here is more than motion: it involves effort and struggle to change a situation.

The horse seeks to escape restraint while the people seek to hold him.

The example attests this branch of effort and struggle in poetry transmitted from pre-Islamic Arabic.

Uses such as undertaking work, trying to accomplish it, and confronting obstacles are intelligible within this field.

The example alone does not establish that every modern occupational sense of مزاولة العمل already existed in exactly the same form.

The meaning supported by the early examples

These examples support the following account of the root in early Arabic:

ز و ل concerns moving away or leaving a former place or condition. This may involve departure or the ending of a state. In the مزاولة branch, effort and struggle to change a situation, obtain something, or control it are also present.

Al-Aʿshā’s verse illustrates departure and disappearance in early poetic language.

Zuhayr’s verse supports the branch of effort and struggle.

These examples do not establish that every derivative recorded in later lexicons circulated before Islam with the same form and meaning.

Nor does their connection make زوال, مزاولة, and إزالة interchangeable in every sentence.

Each has its own formation and meaning.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَالَ يَزُولُ

This basic triliteral verb means moving from a place, going away, or disappearing.

زَالَ الْحَجَرُ عَنْ مَكَانِهِ

zāla al-ḥajaru ʿan makānihi

Meaning: “The stone moved from its place.”

زَالَ الْمُلْكُ

zāla al-mulk

Meaning: “Sovereignty ended.”

زَالَ الْخَطَرُ

zāla al-khaṭar

Meaning: “The danger passed.”

The first concerns spatial movement.

The second concerns the end of a position of power.

The third describes a condition passing away.

The subject and context determine the kind of departure involved.

زَوَال

A familiar verbal noun of زَالَ يَزُولُ.

It denotes going away, moving aside, changing condition, or ending.

زَوَالُ النِّعْمَةِ

zawālu al-niʿmah

Meaning: “The loss of a blessing.”

زَوَالُ الْمُلْكِ

zawālu al-mulk

Meaning: “The end of sovereignty.”

زَوَالُ الشَّيْءِ عَنْ مَكَانِهِ

zawālu al-shayʾi ʿan makānihi

Meaning: “Something’s departure from its place.”

This form occurs in Ibrāhīm 14:44.

زَوِيل, زُؤُول, and زَوَلَان

Other verbal nouns and movement-related forms are recorded in this family.

زَوِيل and زُؤُول can denote departure, going away, and movement.

زَوَلَان is also used for motion and displacement.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Their differing patterns do not alone establish that each names an entirely distinct kind of movement or destruction.

أَزَالَ يُزِيلُ

This Form IV verb means removing, displacing, or clearing away.

أَزَالَ الْحَجَرَ

azāla al-ḥajar

Meaning: “He removed the stone or moved it aside.”

أَزَالَ الْخَوْفَ

azāla al-khawf

Meaning: “He dispelled the fear.”

The subject causes something’s departure or disappearance.

Where زال can express departure itself, أزال introduces someone or something causing the removal.

إِزَالَة

The verbal noun of أزال: removing or clearing something away.

Persian also uses this borrowed word for removing or cleaning away.

This must not be imposed as the direct meaning of زوال in every Qur’anic verse.

زوال may describe departure without naming its cause, while إزالة expresses the act of removal.

زَوَّلَ يُزَوِّلُ

This Form II verb is recorded for moving or displacing something.

زَوَّلَهُ عَنْ مَكَانِهِ

zawwalahu ʿan makānihi

Meaning: “He removed him from his position.”

It overlaps with أزال, although their morphological formations remain distinct.

زَائِل

The active participle of زال.

It describes something departing, disappearing, or lacking permanence.

أَمْرٌ زَائِلٌ

amrun zāʾil

Meaning: “Something impermanent.”

نِعْمَةٌ زَائِلَةٌ

niʿmatun zāʾilah

Meaning: “A blessing that passes away.”

Elsewhere, زائل may mean only moving aside, without total destruction.

زَائِلَة and زَوَائِل

In descriptions of game and animal movement, زائلة is recorded for a moving creature or thing.

Its plural is زوائل.

This attests physical motion alongside the root’s uses for ending and disappearance.

زَوَّال

This describes someone or something that moves a great deal.

Some uses describe a person whose walking involves much bodily movement but little progress.

Movement is prominent here, rather than annihilation.

The geminated زَوَّال must be distinguished from زَوَال, the ending of a condition.

زَوْل

Early Arabic records this for movement.

Human uses include an agile, clever, refined, or remarkable man.

Its plural is أَزْوَال.

Some uses describe a courageous person.

In Sudanese Arabic, زول is familiar as a word for a person.

This modern use is comparable with some early human senses, but its entire present-day usage should not be projected onto pre-Islamic Arabic without evidence.

زَوْلَة

A feminine form in certain human descriptions.

It is used for an agile, clever, intelligent, or capable woman.

Some lexical accounts describe a woman skilled in various activities.

This can be connected semantically with agility, without establishing a certain historical derivation.

زَاوَلَ يُزَاوِلُ

A Form III verb.

Its uses include trying, struggling with something, attending to it, or seeking to obtain or change it.

زَاوَلَ الْأَمْرَ

zāwala al-amr

Meaning: “He tried to accomplish the task.”

زَاوَلَ الْخَصْمَ

zāwala al-khaṣm

Meaning: “He grappled or struggled with the opponent.”

Zuhayr’s verse illustrates the reciprocal efforts of horse and people.

مُزَاوَلَة

The verbal noun of زاول.

Modern Arabic also uses it for carrying out or engaging in work.

مُزَاوَلَةُ الْعَمَلِ

muzāwalatu al-ʿamal

Meaning: “Engaging in work.”

مُزَاوَلَةُ الْمِهْنَةِ

muzāwalatu al-mihnah

Meaning: “Practicing a profession.”

Its early uses concern effort and grappling with a task one seeks to accomplish.

تَزَاوَلَ

In reciprocal use, this denotes two parties struggling with one another.

تَزَاوَلَ الرَّجُلَانِ

tazāwala al-rajulān

Meaning: “The two men engaged in a struggle with each other.”

Participation or opposition between two parties is expressed.

زَوَالُ الشَّمْسِ

This refers to the sun passing its midday meridian.

As a time expression, it denotes the sun passing the midpoint of its apparent daily course and moving into the afternoon.

It therefore does not mean sunset.

This particular expression does not occur in the Qur’an.

Other Qur’anic expressions for times and the sun’s apparent movement should be examined under their respective roots.

مِزْوَلَة

In timekeeping, this names an instrument indicating time through shadows and the sun’s position: a sundial.

The usage concerns apparent solar movement and changing shadow positions.

The instrument’s name does not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Distinguishing زال يزول from ما زال and لا يزال

One difficulty is the resemblance between two Arabic formations:

زَالَ يَزُولُ

zāla yazūlu

And:

زَالَ يَزَالُ

zāla yazālu

The first describes departing, moving aside, or disappearing.

The second, in the incomplete verb construction with ما or لا, expresses continuation.

For example:

مَا زَالَ يَعْمَلُ

mā zāla yaʿmalu

Meaning: “He continues working.”

لَا يَزَالُ قَائِمًا

lā yazālu qāʾiman

Meaning: “He is still standing.”

These expressions describe an ongoing condition, not its cessation.

How ما زال expresses continuation

If departure is understood as a condition ending, negating that departure means that the condition has not ended.

Thus:

مَا زَالَ

mā zāla

Expresses continuation in this construction.

This semantic explanation alone does not determine the morphological root of every similar form.

Lexical and morphological analyses distinguish زالَ يَزُولُ from the incomplete verb زالَ يَزَالُ.

The morphological classification used for this article assigns the Qur’anic ما زال and لا يزال forms to ز ي ل.

Some lexical accounts propose a connection and alternation between wāw and yāʾ in these families.

Such proposals remain lexical analyses; they do not establish a proven historical sequence for every form.

Two questions must therefore remain distinct:

Semantically, departure and its negation have an intelligible relationship.

Morphologically, not every form resembling زال can be assigned to ز و ل without examination.

Qur’anic ما زال and لا يزال

Hūd 11:118 says:

وَلَا يَزَالُونَ مُخْتَلِفِينَ

wa-lā yazālūna mukhtalifīn

Hūd 11:118

Translation:

“And they will continue to differ.”

لا يزالون describes continuing disagreement.

Ghāfir 40:34 says:

فَمَا زِلْتُمْ فِي شَكٍّ مِمَّا جَاءَكُمْ بِهِ

fa-mā ziltum fī shakkin mimmā jāʾakum bihi

Ghāfir 40:34

Translation:

“Yet you remained in doubt about what he brought you.”

ما زلتم likewise expresses continuing doubt.

Al-Baqarah 2:217 says:

وَلَا يَزَالُونَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَرُدُّوكُمْ عَنْ دِينِكُمْ إِنِ اسْتَطَاعُوا

wa-lā yazālūna yuqātilūnakum ḥattā yaruddūkum ʿan dīnikum ini istaṭāʿū

Al-Baqarah 2:217

Translation:

“They will continue fighting you until they turn you back from your religion, if they can.”

لا يزالون expresses continuing conduct, not its cessation.

The same construction appears in Al-Māʾidah 5:13, Al-Tawbah 9:110, Al-Raʿd 13:31, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15, and Al-Ḥajj 22:55.

These forms clarify the distinction between departure and continuation, but should not be silently added to the ز و ل count used here.

Distinguishing زَيَّلَ and تَزَيَّلَ

Yūnus 10:28 says:

فَزَيَّلْنَا بَيْنَهُمْ

fa-zayyalnā baynahum

Yūnus 10:28

Meaning: “Then We separated them.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 uses لَوْ تَزَيَّلُوا for people becoming separated or distinct from one another.

These forms belong to ز ي ل.

Separation and distancing in that family are semantically close to ز و ل.

Semantic closeness does not make the morphological formations identical.

They are discussed here to clarify the distinction.

4. Occurrences of ز و ل in the Qur’an

The morphological classification used here identifies four occurrences:

زَوَالٍ in Ibrāhīm 14:44; لِتَزُولَ in Ibrāhīm 14:46; and تَزُولَا and زَالَتَا in Fāṭir 35:41.

They concern three subjects: people and their position passing away, mountains moving, and the heavens and earth being sustained.

A. Ibrāhīm 14:44: claiming immunity from passing away

وَأَنْذِرِ النَّاسَ يَوْمَ يَأْتِيهِمُ الْعَذَابُ فَيَقُولُ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا رَبَّنَا أَخِّرْنَا إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ قَرِيبٍ نُجِبْ دَعْوَتَكَ وَنَتَّبِعِ الرُّسُلَ أَوَلَمْ تَكُونُوا أَقْسَمْتُمْ مِنْ قَبْلُ مَا لَكُمْ مِنْ زَوَالٍ

wa-andhiri al-nāsa yawma yaʾtīhimu al-ʿadhābu fa-yaqūlu alladhīna ẓalamū rabbanā akhkhirnā ilā ajalin qarībin nujib daʿwataka wa-nattabiʿi al-rusula a-wa-lam takūnū aqsamtum min qablu mā lakum min zawāl

Ibrāhīm 14:44

Translation:

“Warn the people of the day when punishment comes to them. Those who did wrong will say: ‘Our Lord, grant us a brief delay; we will answer Your call and follow the messengers.’ Had you not sworn before that there would be no passing away for you?”

زَوَالٍ is a verbal noun.

مَا لَكُمْ مِنْ زَوَالٍ expresses the absence of departure or passing away.

The wrongdoers ask for more time when confronted with punishment.

They promise to answer the divine call and follow the messengers if given another opportunity.

Their former claim is recalled in response:

“Had you not sworn before that there would be no passing away for you?”

This exposes a mistaken claim about their condition ending or their transition beyond worldly life.

The sense of زوال here

Lexically, زوال concerns departure from a former condition.

Here the subject is human life and position in relation to death and accountability.

One reading concerns their assumption that their power and circumstances would endure without downfall.

Another concerns denying a transition from earthly life to judgment and recompense.

The latter connects with resurrection-denial passages, such as Al-Naḥl 16:38, which mentions oaths denying that the dead will be raised.

زوال itself does not mean resurrection or the Last Day.

Those concepts come from context and related passages.

The word expresses the ending of, or departure from, a former condition.

The connection with Ibrāhīm 14:45

The following verse recalls that they inhabited the dwellings of earlier wrongdoers and learned what happened to them.

This connection matters.

People confident of their own permanence lived in homes and lands previously occupied by others.

The traces of earlier lives testified that human power, property, and status are not permanent.

A people may govern a land for a time and imagine their circumstances will never change; generations nevertheless pass and others take their place.

The reflection can thus extend beyond an individual’s death to the ending of a period of power and prosperity.

These applications should not all be inserted into the word’s direct translation.

“Passing away” or “the ending of a condition” can suffice, with particular applications explained through context.

5. Ibrāhīm 14:46: can plotting move mountains?

وَقَدْ مَكَرُوا مَكْرَهُمْ وَعِنْدَ اللَّهِ مَكْرُهُمْ وَإِنْ كَانَ مَكْرُهُمْ لِتَزُولَ مِنْهُ الْجِبَالُ

wa-qad makarū makrahum wa-ʿinda Allāhi makruhum wa-in kāna makruhum li-tazūla minhu al-jibāl

Ibrāhīm 14:46

A rendering according to the concessive reading:

“They devised their plot, and their plot is with The God, even if their plot were such that mountains would move because of it.”

لِتَزُولَ is a present-tense form of زَالَ يَزُولُ.

Its subject is الْجِبَالُ, the mountains.

مِنْهُ refers back to the plot.

The expression concerns mountains departing or moving from their place.

Mountains are conspicuous examples of apparent firmness and stability.

Their movement can therefore illustrate either the magnitude of plotting or the limits of its power, depending on the reading.

The passage has differing grammatical analyses and recitations.

First reading: their plotting could not move mountains

In the reading of Ḥafṣ, لِتَزُولَ has a kasrah on the initial lām and an accusative verb ending. One familiar analysis takes إِنْ as negative, equivalent to ما, and the lām as the lām of denial.

Under this analysis, the sense is:

“Their plotting was not such that mountains could move because of it.”

It presents the inability of plotting to dislodge what is firm.

This connects with Ibrāhīm 14:47, which warns against imagining that The God will fail His promise to the messengers.

Whatever its extent, opposing plots cannot invalidate the divine promise in this reading.

Second reading: even if their plotting were vast enough to move mountains

Another analysis takes the wording conditionally or concessively, emphasizing plotting’s magnitude. This grammatical analysis must be distinguished from the separate recitation described below.

The sense would then be:

“Even if their plotting were such that mountains moved because of it.”

A transmitted recitation also has لَتَزُولُ, with an initial fatḥah and an indicative ending. In its familiar analysis, إِنْ is lightened from emphatic إِنَّ and the lām distinguishes it from negative إِنْ.

That affirmative reading emphasizes plotting’s magnitude through the image of moving mountains.

It still does not place human plots beyond divine knowledge or power.

The preceding phrase explicitly says:

وَعِنْدَ اللَّهِ مَكْرُهُمْ

wa-ʿinda Allāhi makruhum

Ibrāhīm 14:46

The following verse affirms fulfillment of the divine promise.

The alternatives differ in how they portray plotting: its inability to dislodge what is firm, or its magnitude despite its inability to overcome divine power.

Literal or figurative mountains?

The direct meaning of الجبال is mountains.

Mountains can also exemplify firmness and stability in Arabic expression.

Moving them can consequently illustrate an event’s extraordinary force.

In a figurative reading, they can evoke what is firmly established, including truth that plotting cannot overthrow.

The noun itself does not mean religion, the Qur’an, or a social structure.

Such applications require supporting evidence; they are not the direct lexical meaning.

Nor does تزول necessarily mean the mountains’ complete annihilation.

The wording describes their displacement.

Violent displacement may involve destruction or transformation, but the verb does not necessarily include every such consequence.

Mountain displacement and the divine promise

Ibrāhīm 14:47 says:

فَلَا تَحْسَبَنَّ اللَّهَ مُخْلِفَ وَعْدِهِ رُسُلَهُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ ذُو انتِقَامٍ

fa-lā taḥsabanna Allāha mukhlifa waʿdihi rusulahu inna Allāha ʿazīzun dhū intiqām

Ibrāhīm 14:47

Translation:

“Do not think The God will fail His promise to His messengers. The God is indeed Mighty, Possessor of retribution.”

This clarifies the passage’s direction.

Divine fulfillment of the promise is set against human plotting.

Even when Ibrāhīm 14:46 emphasizes the plots’ magnitude, the surrounding verses stress the limits of their power before the divine will.

Power, organization, and plotting alone are therefore neither measures of truth nor guarantees of final victory.

This does not mean deceit and oppression have no real effects on human lives.

They can cause substantial harm and suffering. The passage concerns their remaining within divine knowledge and judgment, unable to overturn His promise.

6. Fāṭir 35:41: sustaining the heavens and earth

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُمْسِكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ أَنْ تَزُولَا وَلَئِنْ زَالَتَا إِنْ أَمْسَكَهُمَا مِنْ أَحَدٍ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ إِنَّهُ كَانَ حَلِيمًا غَفُورًا

inna Allāha yumsiku al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa an tazūlā wa-la-in zālatā in amsakahumā min aḥadin min baʿdihi innahu kāna ḥalīman ghafūran

Fāṭir 35:41

Translation:

“The God holds the heavens and earth lest they pass away. If they were to pass away, no one after Him could hold them. He is indeed Forbearing, Forgiving.”

The root occurs twice here.

تَزُولَا is a dual present-tense verb, subjunctive after أَنْ with its final nūn omitted.

زَالَتَا is a feminine dual past-tense verb used in a conditional construction.

Both refer to the heavens and earth, treated as two entities.

The meaning of أَنْ تَزُولَا

The verse begins:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُمْسِكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ أَنْ تَزُولَا

inna Allāha yumsiku al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa an tazūlā

Fāṭir 35:41

The God sustains the heavens and earth so that they do not pass away.

يُمْسِكُ belongs to م س ك and means holding or preventing something from being released.

تَزُولَا concerns departure, loss of position, or loss of stability.

Together the verbs relate sustaining to passing away.

Holding prevents the departure described.

تَزُولَا does not itself specify falling, collision, explosion, or any particular mechanism of destruction.

Those details are not given.

Does this mean absolute spatial immobility?

زَالَ الشَّيْءُ عَنْ مَكَانِهِ can describe something moving from its place.

Fāṭir 35:41 alone, however, does not formulate a physical theory of an absolutely stationary earth or motionless celestial bodies.

It states that The God keeps the heavens and earth from passing away.

This can be understood as sustaining the created order’s stability and continuity.

The Qur’an also describes the sun’s and moon’s movement and ordered courses, including Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:33 and Yā Sīn 36:40.

A body moving within its course is therefore not necessarily undergoing the departure described here.

Something may move regularly while retaining its place and stability within a system.

Leaving that order or losing its stability may, by contrast, be a kind of زوال.

This explanation should not be presented as the verse’s definite description of a particular scientific process.

The verse attributes sustaining the heavens and earth to The God without detailing its mechanism.

The role of وَلَئِنْ زَالَتَا

The verse continues:

وَلَئِنْ زَالَتَا

wa-la-in zālatā

Fāṭir 35:41

Meaning:

“And if the two were to pass away.”

This is conditional.

It does not report their departure as already occurring; it supposes a situation to make a point.

It then says:

إِنْ أَمْسَكَهُمَا مِنْ أَحَدٍ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ

in amsakahumā min aḥadin min baʿdihi

Fāṭir 35:41

Meaning:

“No one after Him could hold them.”

إِنْ is negative here.

If the heavens and earth lost their stability, no other being could independently sustain them.

The point directly concerns divine oneness and creation’s dependence on The God.

The connection with Fāṭir 35:40

The preceding verse questions those who associate other beings with The God:

Have those beings created anything of the earth?

Do they have any share in the heavens?

Have they received a book supporting their claim?

Fāṭir 35:41 then attributes sustaining the heavens and earth to The God.

The subject consequently extends beyond the world’s physical stability alone.

The Qur’an contrasts the Creator’s actual power with beings to which people attribute divine status and power.

Sustaining the heavens and earth exemplifies power belonging to The God.

The claim of partners in creation and preservation is thereby challenged.

Why end with حَلِيمًا غَفُورًا?

The verse ends:

إِنَّهُ كَانَ حَلِيمًا غَفُورًا

innahu kāna ḥalīman ghafūran

Fāṭir 35:41

Meaning:

“He is indeed Forbearing, Forgiving.”

This ending connects with the surrounding verses.

Fāṭir 35:40 mentions supposed partners and the wrongdoers’ deceptive promises.

Fāṭir 35:41 presents divine power in sustaining the world.

It also mentions forbearance and forgiveness.

The One upon whose sustaining the heavens and earth depend is also forbearing toward human wrongdoing and offers forgiveness.

This connection shows that divine power and mercy need not be opposed.

Forbearance and forgiveness are nevertheless not meanings of ز و ل.

They are independent attributes completing the verse’s message.

7. Related roots

ز و ل (زَوَال) and ز ي ل (زَيْل)

These families are close in form and meaning.

ز و ل primarily concerns leaving a place or condition.

ز ي ل concerns separating and distancing things from one another.

Yūnus 10:28 uses فَزَيَّلْنَا بَيْنَهُمْ for separation.

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 uses تَزَيَّلُوا for people separating from one another.

Some lexical accounts propose a connection and alternation between their wāw and yāʾ.

The morphological classification used here nevertheless distinguishes them.

ز و ل (زَوَال) and ز ه ق (زَهَق)

Qur’anic زهق concerns life ending and falsehood vanishing.

زوال can also express ending or disappearance.

Its lexical picture particularly emphasizes leaving a former position or condition.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 uses وَزَهَقَ الْبَاطِلُ for falsehood vanishing.

Ibrāhīm 14:44 uses زوال for the ending or loss of the human condition under discussion.

The roots overlap in disappearance without sharing their entire semantic range.

A stone’s displacement can, for example, be زوال without its destruction.

ز و ل (زَوَال) and ث ب ت (ثَبَات)

ثبات concerns remaining firm or established.

زوال concerns departure from, or the ending of, that condition.

The concepts can therefore stand in opposition.

Stability does not invariably mean physical immobility.

Someone may remain steadfast in truth while physically moving.

An apparently stable structure may also change and disappear over time.

Both concepts require interpretation according to their subject.

ز و ل (زَوَال) and ب د ل (تَبْدِيل)

تبديل concerns replacing one thing with another.

زوال concerns a former condition ending or being left behind.

Both may occur together, as when one government ends and another succeeds it.

Not every departure, however, implies a specified replacement.

Ibrāhīm 14:48 describes the earth being changed into another earth.

This relates to an earlier condition ending, but تُبَدَّلُ belongs to a distinct root with its own sense.

ز و ل (زَوَال) and ح و ل (تَحَوُّل)

تحول concerns change of state and transition between conditions.

زوال can accompany such change.

In زوال, departure from or ending of the earlier state is prominent.

In تحول, the change itself is central.

A social structure may be reformed and transformed without completely disappearing.

Transformation has then occurred, but complete disappearance does not necessarily follow.

ز و ل (زَوَال) and م ي ل (مَيْل)

ميل concerns inclination and a change of direction.

Spatial uses of زوال can likewise concern moving aside.

Inclination does not necessarily mean leaving a position altogether.

Al-Kahf 18:17 uses تزاور, from ز و ر, for the sun’s inclination.

Fāṭir 35:41 uses تزولا, from ز و ل, for the heavens and earth passing away.

Their formal and semantic similarity must not erase the distinction between the roots.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents suit the principal forms. These proposals do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

زَالَ يَزُولُ: moved away, departed, disappeared, or ended.

زَوَال: departure, loss, or the ending of a condition.

زَائِل: impermanent, passing away, or departing.

أَزَالَ: removed, dispelled, or moved aside.

إِزَالَة: removing or clearing away.

زَوَّلَ: moved or displaced.

زَاوَلَ: tried, grappled with, or worked to accomplish something.

مُزَاوَلَة: effort, undertaking work, or practicing an activity.

تَزَاوَلَ: engaged in mutual struggle.

زَوَّال: moving a great deal; highly active.

زَوْل: in human uses, an agile, clever, or refined person; elsewhere, movement.

زَوْلَة: an agile, clever, or capable woman.

زَوَالُ الشَّمْسِ: the sun passing its midday meridian.

9. Conclusion

ز و ل concerns movement and departure from a former place or condition.

The poetic examples illustrate departure in al-Aʿshā and effort or struggle in Zuhayr. Each transmitted line’s dating and wording remain distinct from its lexical significance.

Extended uses include sovereignty ending, blessings being lost, danger passing, and physical displacement.

Not all these uses mean complete destruction.

The Qur’an has four occurrences in three verses under the classification used here.

Ibrāhīm 14:44 confronts a claim of permanence with the ending of the claimants’ condition.

Ibrāhīm 14:46 presents moving mountains in the context of opposing plots and the divine promise.

Fāṭir 35:41 attributes sustaining the heavens and earth to The God and contrasts their possible departure with His power.

ما زال and لا يزال express continuation, but the morphological classification used here places them under ز ي ل; they are not added to the ز و ل count.

The central sense is departure from a former place or condition, whether spatial displacement, the ending of a position, or the loss of stability and continuation.

Each verse requires identifying what passes away and what kind of change is meant.

Human circumstances ending, mountains moving, and the heavens and earth passing away are not one identical phenomenon despite their semantic connection.

This distinction preserves the attested meaning and prevents conclusions about death, history, political power, or cosmic order from replacing the word’s direct sense without evidence.
