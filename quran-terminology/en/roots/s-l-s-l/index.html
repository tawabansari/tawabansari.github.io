1. Root Meaning and Word Structure

Arabic silsilah (سِلْسِلَة) names a chain whose components join one another to form a connected whole. Its plural is salāsil (سَلَاسِل), the form used in the Qur’an for chains of punishment.

In a familiar chain, each link joins another, producing an extended connected length. It can be used to bind, restrain, pull, or keep things joined.

Early Arabic dictionaries record chains alongside other uses involving connected objects, smoothly flowing water, lightning extending through the sky, and connected ridges of sand.

The lexical field of S L S L is therefore not restricted to iron chains. Connection and continuous extension appear in several of its branches.

Al-Jawharī writes in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: “وَشَيْءٌ مُسَلْسَلٌ: مُتَّصِلٌ بَعْضُهُ بِبَعْضٍ”—something musalsal has its parts connected to one another. He then gives iron chains and lightning extending across a cloud as examples.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, p.1732: connection, chains, and extended lightning

Ibn Fāris likewise explains silsilah through connection and extension in Maqāyīs al-Lughah, associating it with S L L. In his analysis, a chain is so named because its connected components extend continuously.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the proposed connection with S L L and connected extension

This explanation is semantically understandable, but historical relationships between words must be distinguished from explanations of their meanings.

S L S L (س ل س ل) is morphologically a quadriliteral entry with the pair S L repeated. S L L (س ل ل), by contrast, is a triliteral entry with uses involving drawing out, slipping away, or something separating from something else.

Their similar letters and some related meanings may suggest a historical connection, but do not justify transferring every meaning of S L L into S L S L.

The Qur’an uses S L S L only as the noun silsilah and its plural salāsil. No verb directly from this entry appears in the text.

2. Linguistic Forms and Early Arabic Evidence

Silsilah and Salāsil: Connected Components

Singular silsilah and plural salāsil are the best-known nouns of this family.

Ordinarily, a chain is a length of joined links. This feature allows the word to describe other things with visible, connected extension.

For example, silsilatu al-barq (سِلْسِلَةُ الْبَرْقِ) describes lightning extending across a cloud in a thread-like form.

The lightning is not made of iron links. The resemblance concerns the extension and continuity of the visible shape.

Salāsilu al-raml (سَلَاسِلُ الرَّمْلِ) likewise denotes connected, extended ridges or formations of sand.

The material again differs from a chain, but connected components following one another provide the basis for the shared expression.

Silsilah can therefore extend from the name of a manufactured object to the form and arrangement of other things.

This extension should nevertheless not erase the specific Qur’anic meaning “chain.” When silsilah accompanies shackles, dragging, and binding people, a chain is the clearest sense.

Salsala and Tasalsala

Arabic also forms verbs within this family.

Salsala (سَلْسَلَ) can mean joining things or pouring liquid in a continuous stream.

Tasalsala (تَسَلْسَلَ) can describe extension and successive connection, as with water moving along a continuous course.

Lexicons record tasalsala al-māʾu (تَسَلْسَلَ الْمَاءُ) of water flowing smoothly and easily.

Musalsal (مُسَلْسَل) describes something with connected parts or a chain-like form.

In later uses it also describes events, writings, narratives, and other things following one another in sequence.

Being connected differs from being identical. Chain links can join one another without being exactly alike in size, shape, or function.

The essential feature is their connection and extension, rather than the identity of every component.

Salsal and Salsāl: Flowing, Pleasant Water

Another branch of the family concerns water and drink.

Lisān al-ʿArab records al-salsal (السَّلْسَل) and al-salsāl (السَّلْسَال) for sweet, clear, pleasant water that is easy to drink.

Here the focus is the liquid’s smooth flow and easy passage through the throat.

Māʾun salsalun (مَاءٌ سَلْسَلٌ) is water whose pleasantness and smoothness cause no difficulty or harshness when swallowed.

Some sources also record al-sulāsil (السُّلَاسِل), with an initial u vowel, for pleasant water.

This must be distinguished from salāsil, with an initial a vowel, the plural “chains.” Similar spelling does not make their meanings identical.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the distinction between salāsil and sulāsil

The following is transmitted from the Arab poet Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī:

أَمْ لَا سَبِيلَ إِلَى الشَّبَابِ وَذِكْرُهُ
أَشْهَى إِلَيَّ مِنَ الرَّحِيقِ السَّلْسَلِ

am lā sabīla ilā al-shabābi wa-dhikruhu / ash-hā ilayya mina al-raḥīqi al-salsali

“Is there no way back to youth? Its memory is sweeter to me than a smooth, pleasant drink.”

Al-salsal describes a drink here, expressing smoothness, easy flow, and pleasantness.

A verse from Labīd ibn Rabīʿah also lists sulāsil among drinks and other caravan goods. Its lexical explanation identifies pleasant water, rather than iron chains.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Abū Kabīr’s and Labīd’s witnesses concerning drink

These witnesses show that the uses concerning water and drink were established in Arabic poetry.

They do not establish that the word for a chain originally came from the name of water, or that the water term necessarily arose from the chain.

A visual connection can be drawn between continuous water flow and a chain’s connected extension. Establishing historical priority requires independent evidence.

Connection and Ease, Rather than Absolute Unbreakability

Two semantic fields approach one another in this family.

The first concerns connected, extended components, as with chains, extended lightning, and continuous ridges of sand.

The second concerns smooth, easy movement, as with continuously flowing water or a drink passing easily through the throat.

The two can be related through movement or extension without interruption.

That relationship does not make every chain necessarily smooth or easy.

A chain can be hard, heavy, and restrictive, while salsāl water can be gentle and pleasant.

Ease and pleasantness therefore belong specifically to the drink branch and should not be transferred into the definition of a chain.

Likewise, a chain’s hardness and restriction should not be added to the meaning of salsāl water.

The shared feature under consideration is connection and extension. Each referent’s material, function, and properties come from its particular use. Connected links do not entail that breaking or separating a chain is impossible; that would require separate evidence.

3. Qur’anic Uses: Chains in Scenes of Punishment

S L S L occurs three times in the Qur’an, in three verses.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three nominal occurrences

In all three, the word denotes a chain or chains in the context of punishment.

Ghāfir 40:71: Chains and Being Dragged

Ghāfir 40:71

إِذِ الْأَغْلَالُ فِي أَعْنَاقِهِمْ وَالسَّلَاسِلُ يُسْحَبُونَ

idhi al-aghlālu fī aʿnāqihim wa-l-salāsilu yusḥabūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“When shackles are upon their necks, and chains as well, they are dragged.”

Al-salāsilu is the plural of silsilah and appears beside al-aghlāl.

Aghlāl is the plural of ghull, a device for binding and restraining the body, particularly the neck and sometimes the hands.

Salāsil, by contrast, denotes chains, which can be used for connection, restraint, or pulling.

The two terms have related functions but are not identical.

A ghull can be a collar or device fastened around the neck, whereas a chain consists of joined components and can connect a shackle to another point.

Fī aʿnāqihim, “upon their necks,” specifies the location of the shackles; yusḥabūna (يُسْحَبُونَ) describes the people being dragged.

Together, these expressions place the chains in a scene of people being bound and dragged.

The verse does not specify the number of chains, their precise material, the dimensions of the links, or how each chain attaches to the body.

Grammatically, al-salāsilu is nominative in the familiar reading. There are differing analyses of its relationship to al-aghlāl and yusḥabūna: some coordinate it with the shackles, while others analyze the following clause independently or supply an understood pronoun.

These differences affect details of sentence structure, but do not change the meaning of salāsil itself.

The continuation in Ghāfir 40:72 describes their being dragged through scalding water and then burned in fire. Yusjarūna (يُسْجَرُونَ) should not be treated as merely repeating the meaning “being dragged.”

Al-Ṭabarī: the syntax of salāsil, dragging in scalding water, and burning in fire

The chain here is therefore an implement associated with binding and dragging, rather than merely an abstract symbol of intellectual dependence.

Other questions can be raised about the reality and nature of punishment in the Hereafter, but the direct meaning “chain” should not be replaced without evidence by “sin,” “attachment,” or “psychological captivity.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32: A Chain with a Stated Measure

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32

ثُمَّ فِي سِلْسِلَةٍ ذَرْعُهَا سَبْعُونَ ذِرَاعًا فَاسْلُكُوهُ

thumma fī silsilatin dharʿuhā sabʿūna dhirāʿan fa-slukūhu

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Then place him within a chain whose measure is seventy cubits.”

Silsilatin appears in the singular here.

Unlike the two plural occurrences, this verse describes a particular chain and states its measure.

Dharʿuhā (ذَرْعُهَا) concerns measuring the chain; sabʿūna dhirāʿan (سَبْعُونَ ذِرَاعًا) supplies the stated amount.

Dhirāʿ, “cubit,” was an Arabic unit of length.

A direct translation should preserve “seventy.” Whether the number also emphasizes magnitude and severity is a matter for interpretation; that possibility does not justify replacing it with “a very long chain” in the basic translation.

The verse does not identify the precise kind of cubit or its relation to modern units.

A conversion into meters is therefore not defensible without establishing the measurement standard.

Fa-slukūhu (فَاسْلُكُوهُ) also matters.

The verb belongs to S L K and concerns inserting or passing something along a path or into something else.

Fī silsilatin, “within a chain,” relates to this command: the person is to be placed or inserted within that chain.

Commentaries give differing descriptions of how this occurs, including binding the person with a chain or passing the chain through his body.

The verse itself does not specify entry and exit points or the exact binding arrangement. These additional descriptions should not be made part of silsilah’s lexical definition.

Al-Ṭabarī: the cubit measure and differing accounts of uslukūhu

The preceding context, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:30 and Al-Ḥāqqah 69:31, speaks of seizing, shackling, and placing the person in fire.

The following verses, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:33 and Al-Ḥāqqah 69:34, describe two aspects of his conduct: not believing in The God, the Magnificent, and not encouraging the feeding of the needy.

The chain is thus part of a punishment scene. Silsilah itself, however, does not mean punishment, unbelief, or depriving the needy.

Those meanings belong to the context and the stated reasons for punishment.

Al-Insān 76:4: Chains, Shackles, and Blazing Fire

Al-Insān 76:4

إِنَّا أَعْتَدْنَا لِلْكَافِرِينَ سَلَاسِلَا وَأَغْلَالًا وَسَعِيرًا

innā aʿtadnā li-l-kāfirīna salāsila wa-aghlālan wa-saʿīran

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“We have prepared for the disbelievers chains, shackles, and a blazing fire.”

Salāsilā is again plural in this verse.

It stands beside aghlālan and saʿīran.

Aghlāl denotes implements of binding and restraint; saʿīr concerns blazing fire.

This combination shows that salāsil here too denotes chains associated with bondage and restriction.

Morphologically, salāsil follows the pattern faʿālil (فَعَالِل), one of the broken-plural patterns normally treated as diptotes.

Different readings are recorded for salāsilā and its pausal form: some reciters use tanwīn and others omit it; there are also differences concerning retention of the final alif when pausing. In the transmission of Ḥafṣ it has no tanwīn in connected recitation. The written final alif should not be mistaken for a plural marker or a root letter.

Al-Marṣafī’s Hidāyat al-Qārī: the final alif of salāsilā in connected and pausal recitation by Ḥafṣ

This matters for the history of recitation and Arabic morphology, but does not alter the meaning.

The presence of salāsil beside aghlāl also shows that the Qur’an uses two different nouns. Although both concern binding and restriction, one cannot replace the other in every use.

The following verses describe the comforts and pleasant drinks of the righteous.

Al-Insān thus contrasts the condition of disbelievers and the righteous. The independent meaning of a chain should not be expanded to encompass that entire contrast.

Salāsil itself means chains; its role in describing punishment comes from the context.

4. The Relationship with Salsabīl

Salsabīl (سَلْسَبِيل) is a word to consider when studying this family.

Al-Insān 76:18

عَيْنًا فِيهَا تُسَمَّىٰ سَلْسَبِيلًا

ʿaynan fīhā tusammā salsabīlan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“A spring there called Salsabīl.”

The word resembles salsal and salsāl in form and semantic field.

Lexicons explain salsabīl as a pleasant drink passing easily through the throat. Some identify it as the name of a spring in the Garden; others allow a descriptive interpretation.

Al-Zajjāj connects salsabīl with the utmost smoothness and ease of flow; the spring’s name may relate to this quality.

Its structure is nevertheless not identical with silsilah.

Qur’anic morphological classification does not count salsabīlan among the three direct occurrences of quadriliteral S L S L. Accounts of its formation and derivation also differ.

It is therefore better treated as semantically and possibly historically related, rather than presented without explanation as a certain direct derivative of S L S L.

The segmentation of salsabīl as the sentence sal sabīlan (سَلْ سَبِيلًا), “ask for a way,” has also been rejected in lexical sources. It should not be accepted as an etymology without reliable evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: salsabīl and objections to segmentation as sal sabīlan

Al-Insān contains both salāsil and salsabīl. Their similar sound does not by itself establish an intended derivational contrast between chains of punishment and the water of the Garden.

The actual contrast is clear from the contexts: one concerns bondage and punishment; the other a spring and heavenly drink.

The smoothness and pleasantness of salsabīl cannot lexically be transferred into salāsil meaning chains.

5. Comparison with Ghull, Qayd, Ḥabl, and Related Words

Silsilah and Ghull

Ghull (غُلّ) is an implement of binding and restraint, fastened around the neck or hands in established uses.

Silsilah is a chain with connected components.

The two can form parts of the same restraining device, but are not identical.

Both appear together in Ghāfir 40:71 and Al-Insān 76:4. Their association permits related roles; it does not establish entirely separate, nonoverlapping structures and functions in every case.

Silsilah and Qayd

Qayd (قَيْد) concerns an implement or condition of binding and restricted movement.

A chain may restrain a person or animal, but not every restraint is necessarily a chain.

Restraints can have different materials and structures, while a chain is recognized by its connected components.

Qayd can therefore explain a chain’s function without always being an exact equivalent of silsilah.

Silsilah and Ḥabl

Ḥabl (حَبْل) denotes a cord or rope.

Both rope and chain can join, pull, and bind things.

Their construction differs. An ordinary chain consists of joined links or components; a rope can consist of twisted or woven strands.

Shared function does not eliminate the difference in names and structures.

For Qur’anic silsilah, Persian zanjīr, “chain,” is clearer than “rope” or “cord.”

Study of Ḥ B L

Silsilah and Salaka

Salaka (سَلَكَ) concerns entering, inserting, or following a path.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32 places this verb beside silsilah.

Salaka belongs to a different root and should not be treated as a derivative of S L S L.

Silsilah names the chain; fa-slukūhu commands placing or inserting the person within that condition.

The distinction between an implement’s name and the verb describing an action must be preserved.

S L S L and S L L

Drawing something out of something else, or its emerging from it, is an established meaning of S L L.

The Qur’an uses sulālah (سُلَالَة) and yatasallalūna (يَتَسَلَّلُونَ) from this family.

Ibn Fāris proposes a relationship with silsilah, but that analysis does not make every derivative of the two entries equivalent.

Qur’anic sulālah concerns something taken or extracted from another substance, whereas silsilah names a chain.

Connection or extension can be discussed in explaining a possible historical relationship, while each word’s direct meaning comes from its own use.

S L S L and S L S

Salis (سَلِس) describes smoothness, softness, and ease, as with fluent speech or something moving without difficulty.

This triliteral entry is semantically close to salsal and salsāl describing water and drink.

Silsilah in the punishment verses, however, is a chain used for bondage and restriction.

Smoothness and ease should therefore not be treated as necessary properties of every chain.

Modern Uses of Silsilah

Modern Persian and Arabic also use silsilah for related series: events, writings, mountain ranges, and dynasties of rulers.

No material chain is present in these uses. Connection or successive components supply the shared idea.

This semantic extension is understandable in relation to early usage involving continuity.

Modern uses should not, however, be read back into the Qur’an to claim that salāsil in punishment verses directly means chains of thoughts, traditions, or social relationships.

Such a reading could be proposed as reflection only with independent contextual evidence, rather than as the word’s basic meaning.

6. Conclusion and Suggested Translation

S L S L is an Arabic quadriliteral entry with a repeated structure.

Its principal Qur’anic forms are silsilah and plural salāsil, meaning chain and chains.

Early Arabic also uses this family for connected extension and successive components. Iron chains, extended lightning, and continuous ridges of sand exemplify this field.

Forms such as salsal and salsāl also describe smoothly flowing, pleasant water and drink.

The fields can be related through continuity and unbroken flow, but historical evidence is needed before deriving every use from one certain original image.

The root occurs three times in three Qur’anic verses.

Ghāfir 40:71 places chains beside shackles in a scene of people being dragged.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32 describes a chain measured as seventy cubits, within which the person is to be placed.

Al-Insān 76:4 lists chains beside shackles and blazing fire among what has been prepared for disbelievers.

“Chain” is clear in all three uses. No direct contextual evidence turns salāsil in these verses into flowing water, a sequence of thoughts, or abstract connections.

Brief root meaning: Connection and continuous extension of components; silsilah is a chain made of joined links or components. Related lexical uses also record smoothly flowing, pleasant water.

Qur’anic forms and suggested translation:

وَالسَّلَاسِلُ — wa-l-salāsilu, Ghāfir 40:71: Chains appearing beside shackles in a scene of people being dragged. Suggested translation: “and chains.”

سِلْسِلَةٍ — silsilatin, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32: A particular chain whose measure is stated as seventy cubits. Suggested translation: “a chain.”

سَلَاسِلَا — salāsilā, Al-Insān 76:4: Chains mentioned among the implements of punishment. Suggested translation: “chains.”

Persian zanjīr, “chain,” is the most suitable equivalent in all three Qur’anic uses.

Connection and continuity matter in the general account of this family, but should not displace the tangible meaning “chain” in direct verse translation.

This study concludes that silsilah, in the principal use examined here, names a chain with joined links or components. The Qur’an uses that chain in the context of bondage and punishment; the general idea of connection is also examined in the family’s other lexical branches.

Connection belongs to a chain’s structure. Hardness, restraint, dragging, and punishment are properties, roles, and consequences understood from particular uses in a sentence.

The related words for pleasant water also show that lexical families can develop differing branches over time. Those branches should be understood without erasing their real differences to construct a uniform definition.
