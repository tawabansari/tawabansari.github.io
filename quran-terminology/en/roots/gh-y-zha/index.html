Intense anger, inner pressure, and being provoked by others: from restraining anger to hostility, revenge, and the image of an enraged fire

In classical Arabic, غ ي ظ concerns intense anger and inner distress, usually arising in response to unwelcome conduct, speech, or circumstances and potentially prompting a forceful reaction.

غَيْظ commonly denotes anger intensifying within a person, accompanied by resentment, pressure, and agitation. غَاظَهُ means “he angered him”; اغْتَاظَ means “he became angry” or “was deeply agitated.”

Some early sources define ghayẓ as concealed anger that a person cannot express or act upon. Others describe it as intense anger or the beginning of anger’s surge.

These differences matter. Intense anger and inner pressure are familiar features of ghayẓ, but inability to respond is not a constant feature of every usage.

Someone may be furious at an action and still have the power to oppose it. Choosing restraint then differs from being unable to act.

The emotion should also be distinguished from the resulting action. A person may become angry yet avoid wrongdoing, or may harm another because of anger.

This distinction matters in the Quran: ghayẓ describes people concealing hostility, believers restraining anger, and enemies returning angry after failure.

The root occurs 11 times in 10 verses: six instances of the noun غَيْظ, three of the verb يَغِيظُ, one active participle غَائِظُون, and one verbal noun تَغَيُّظ.

These usages require distinguishing inner anger, angering someone else, outward expressions of anger, restraint, and the removal of anger.

1. Classical Arabic meaning and early evidence

In classical Arabic, غَيْظ relates to anger and the pressure of distress.

Lexicons record غَاظَنِي يَغِيظُنِي for someone or something angering me.

Ibn Fāris relates the root’s core to distress reaching a person through another.

This highlights inner distress or pressure and an external agent producing it.

Someone may betray a person, violate their rights, or say something hurtful, provoking intense anger and resentment.

The cause of anger, however, is not always actual wrongdoing by another.

A rival’s success, the disclosure of truth, or the failure of an unjust desire may also provoke anger.

The word alone therefore does not show that the angry person is wronged or that their anger has a just cause.

A poetic witness attributed to Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī

Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī, associated with the pre-Islamic period, says in a poem about conflict between groups:

فَذُوقُوا كَمَا ذُقْنَا غَدَاةَ مُحَجِّرٍ
مِنَ الْغَيْظِ فِي أَكْبَادِنَا وَالتَّحَوُّبِ

fa-dhūqū kamā dhuqnā ghadāta Muḥajjirin / mina al-ghayẓi fī akbādinā wa-al-taḥawwubi

Translation: “Taste, then, as we tasted on the day of Muḥajjir: the anger within us, and lamentation and grief.”

الغيظ appears here alongside أَكْبَادِنَا.

أَكْبَاد is the plural of كَبِد, liver. Arabic poetry may use the liver as an imagined seat of suffering and intense feeling.

The poet is not describing liver disease, but anger and grief remaining deep within people.

التَّحَوُّب here concerns lamentation, grief, and suffering after calamity.

The line appears in a setting of confrontation and retaliation, recalling earlier suffering and the wish to redress it.

Ghayẓ can thus be associated with remembered harm, hostility, and a desire to retaliate.

This does not make revenge the word’s own meaning.

Anger is an inner state; revenge is an action someone may choose because of it.

Early lexical and literary sources attribute the line to Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī. This study follows that attribution; different attributions in later collections require separate examination.

Both أَكْبَادِنَا and أَجْوَافِنَا have been transmitted as readings.

Such differences should remain visible in historical study. Although Ṭufayl is recognized as pre-Islamic, this particular line’s date and original reading have not been independently established by a contemporary witness.

A poetic witness attributed to Ḥuḍayn ibn al-Mundhir

A poem attributed to Ḥuḍayn ibn al-Mundhir, who lived in the first Islamic century, addresses his son Ghayyāẓ:

وَسُمِّيتَ غَيَّاظًا وَلَسْتَ بِغَائِظٍ
عَدُوًّا وَلَكِنَّ الصَّدِيقَ تَغِيظُ

wa-summīta Ghayyāẓan wa-lasta bi-ghāʾiẓin / ʿaduwwan wa-lākinna al-ṣadīqa taghīẓu

Translation: “You were named Ghayyāẓ, yet you anger no enemy; instead, you anger your friend.”

The poet exploits the resemblance between the name Ghayyāẓ and verbs of غ ي ظ.

غَائِظ denotes someone who angers another.

تَغِيظُ means “you anger”: the son is addressed, while الصَّدِيقَ, the friend, is the object of his anger-provoking conduct.

The poet reproaches his son for distressing friends instead of opposing an enemy.

This matters because the root names not only a person’s emotion but also an agent that provokes anger.

Someone may not be angry themselves while their conduct angers others.

Conversely, a person may be intensely angry without causing anyone else’s anger.

Ḥuḍayn belongs to the period after Quranic revelation; this is not independent pre-Quranic evidence. It illustrates the continuing, clear transitive usage in classical Arabic.

Ghayẓ applied to intense heat

The Umayyad poet al-Akhṭal uses تَغَيَّظَت for the intensification of midday heat.

The day’s heat is pictured as something inflamed by anger.

The subject is not an actual human emotion; the heat’s intensity and pressure are described through an image of anger.

This figurative usage helps illuminate two Quranic passages about fire.

Al-Akhṭal lived after revelation, however. His poem alone cannot establish that this exact imagery of fire or heat was current before the Quran.

What the historical witnesses show

Arabic evidence reveals three related directions of meaning:

First, intense inner anger and distress.

Second, provoking another’s anger through an action or event.

Third, figurative anger expressing the intensity of something such as heat.

The central meaning remains anger and the intense pressure of agitation, rather than lasting resentment, revenge, or violence.

These may accompany ghayẓ but should be examined separately.

2. Forms and derivatives

غ ي ظ is a three-consonant root with yā as its middle radical.

In the perfect غَاظَ, that radical becomes alif; it remains yā in the verbal noun غَيْظ.

The familiar verbal sequence is:

غَاظَهُ يَغِيظُهُ غَيْظًا

It means to anger someone.

Principal forms include:

غَيْظ: intense anger, agitation, and its inner pressure.

غَاظَ: angered someone else.

يَغِيظُ: angers or provokes anger.

غَائِظ: someone or something that angers another.

مَغِيظ: a person who has been angered.

اغْتَاظَ: became angry or deeply agitated.

اغْتِيَاظ: becoming angry or experiencing ghayẓ.

مُغْتَاظ: someone experiencing intense anger.

غَيَّظَ: angered another.

تَغْيِيظ: provoking anger.

تَغَيَّظَ: became angry, displayed anger, or figuratively became intensely inflamed.

تَغَيُّظ: intense agitation or the manifestation of anger; figuratively, fierce roaring or raging.

غَايَظَ: treated someone in an anger-provoking way; in some usages, reciprocal confrontation or provocation.

مُغَايَظَة: mutual angering or provocative behavior between two parties.

غَيَّاظ: an intensive description of someone who frequently angers others; also an Arabic personal name.

Lexicographers differ over أَغَاظَ.

Some early scholars rejected it in favor of غَاظَهُ, whereas Ibn al-Aʿrābī and others recorded أَغَاظَ with the meaning of angering.

Its presence in linguistic sources should be acknowledged without making unqualified claims about its historical prevalence.

Four Quranic forms

The 11 occurrences fall into four morphological groups.

غَيْظ appears six times: twice in Āl ʿImrān 3:119 and once each in Āl ʿImrān 3:134, Al-Tawbah 9:15, Al-Aḥzāb 33:25, and Al-Mulk 67:8.

The Form I verb يَغِيظُ appears three times: Al-Tawbah 9:120, Al-Ḥajj 22:15, and Al-Fatḥ 48:29.

The active participle غَائِظُون occurs once, in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:55.

The Form V verbal noun تَغَيُّظ occurs once, in Al-Furqān 25:12.

The Quran does not use غَاظَ in the perfect or the verb اغْتَاظَ.

غَيَّظَ, مُغْتَاظ, and غَيَّاظ are likewise attested Arabic forms rather than Quranic derivatives.

Becoming angry and angering someone

غَاظَهُ is transitive.

غَاظَهُ الْأَمْرُ means that the matter angered him.

اغْتَاظَ describes the person becoming angry.

Someone may, for example, become angry about an unjust decision.

The decision provokes anger, and the person experiences ghayẓ.

Their anger by itself does not establish that their response is right.

The distinction also matters in the Quran: some passages concern the cause of anger, others the angry person’s inner state.

3. Concealed anger and restraint in human conduct

Two passages in Āl ʿImrān describe very different situations involving ghayẓ.

In one, intense anger accompanies concealed hostility.

In the other, restraint characterizes the mindful and those who do good.

Moral worth therefore depends on more than the presence or absence of anger: its direction, cause, and the conduct chosen matter.

Concealed anger accompanying hostility

Āl ʿImrān 3:119

هَا أَنْتُمْ أُولَاءِ تُحِبُّونَهُمْ وَلَا يُحِبُّونَكُمْ وَتُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْكِتَابِ كُلِّهِ وَإِذَا لَقُوكُمْ قَالُوا آمَنَّا وَإِذَا خَلَوْا عَضُّوا عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَنَامِلَ مِنَ الْغَيْظِ قُلْ مُوتُوا بِغَيْظِكُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ بِذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ

hā antum ulāʾi tuḥibbūnahum wa-lā yuḥibbūnakum wa-tuʾminūna bi-al-kitābi kullihi wa-idhā laqūkum qālū āmannā wa-idhā khalaw ʿaḍḍū ʿalaykumu al-anāmila mina al-ghayẓi qul mūtū bi-ghayẓikum inna allāha ʿalīmun bi-dhāti al-ṣudūr

Āl ʿImrān 3:119

Translation: “Here you are, loving them while they do not love you, and believing in the whole Book. When they meet you, they say, ‘We believe’; when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage against you. Say: die in your rage. The God knows what is within hearts.”

غَيْظ occurs twice in this verse.

First in مِنَ الْغَيْظِ, then in بِغَيْظِكُمْ.

The first gives anger as the cause of biting fingertips.

The second attributes that anger to the people themselves.

عَضُّوا عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَنَامِلَ مِنَ الْغَيْظِ

عَضُّوا comes from ع ض ض and means biting.

الْأَنَامِل denotes fingertips.

Biting fingers can signify intense anger, regret, or helplessness in Arabic.

Here مِنَ الْغَيْظِ specifies the cause.

The image concerns inwardly intensified anger becoming visible in behavior.

It does not imply that every angry person necessarily bites their fingers.

The behavior portrays intense ghayẓ in this particular situation.

Anger alongside hostility and duplicity

Āl ʿImrān 3:118 should also be read to identify the people concerned.

It describes people who desire believers’ suffering and harm, whose speech reveals hostility while what they conceal within is greater.

Āl ʿImrān 3:119 then describes their double behavior: professing belief in company while raging in private.

The subject is therefore more than a simple disagreement.

The passage describes a mismatch between outward speech and concealed feelings or intentions.

Not everyone who opposes us or becomes angry at our conduct should be identified with this verse.

Someone’s disagreement may have sound grounds.

Their anger may also respond to actual injustice.

Recognizing concealed hostility and duplicity requires evidence beyond anger alone.

قُلْ مُوتُوا بِغَيْظِكُمْ

This means: “Say: die in your rage.”

In context, it is a sharp rebuke of enemies whose hostility makes others’ success and well-being intolerable to them.

It is not a general command to kill angry people or act violently against opponents.

The construction commands an utterance in response to that hostility, rather than physical punishment for feeling angry.

Āl ʿImrān 3:120 further explains their behavior: good reaching believers distresses them, while harm reaching believers delights them.

Believers are then advised to persist with patience and mindfulness against their schemes.

The central response here is recognizing hostility, perseverance, and mindfulness, rather than adopting the same hostile conduct.

وَالْكَاظِمِينَ الْغَيْظَ: restraining anger

Āl ʿImrān 3:134

الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ فِي السَّرَّاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَالْكَاظِمِينَ الْغَيْظَ وَالْعَافِينَ عَنِ النَّاسِ وَاللَّهُ يُحِبُّ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

alladhīna yunfiqūna fī al-sarrāʾi wa-al-ḍarrāʾi wa-al-kāẓimīna al-ghayẓa wa-al-ʿāfīna ʿani al-nāsi wa-allāhu yuḥibbu al-muḥsinīn

Āl ʿImrān 3:134

Translation: “Those who spend in ease and hardship, restrain anger, and pardon people; The God loves those who do good.”

الْغَيْظَ is the object of الْكَاظِمِينَ.

كَظْم, from ك ظ م, concerns holding and restraining something gathered within that might spill out.

Applied to anger, كَظْمُ الْغَيْظ means restraining it so that the feeling does not become a wrongful reaction.

The Quran does not say that those who do good never become angry.

Restraint implies that anger may arise while a person responds with choice and self-control.

Restraining anger differs from having none

A person deceived in a transaction may become intensely angry at the other party.

They might immediately insult, threaten, or harm them.

They could instead restrain anger and pursue their rights through appropriate means.

Anger exists in the second case, but the person does not let it determine their conduct without judgment.

This clarifies the practical meaning of restraint.

Restraint should not be equated with suppressing all emotion or enduring injustice indefinitely.

Other Quranic passages also recognize defense and a just response to wrongdoing.

Distinguishing restraint from pardon

After الْكَاظِمِينَ الْغَيْظَ, the verse says:

وَالْعَافِينَ عَنِ النَّاسِ

wa-al-ʿāfīna ʿani al-nās

Āl ʿImrān 3:134

That is, those who pardon people.

The two descriptions are not identical.

Kaẓm concerns restraining anger.

ʿAfw concerns pardoning a fault or relinquishing a demand or claim against someone.

A person may restrain anger while still pursuing their rights justly.

They may also choose to pardon another’s fault and waive their claim.

Restraint by itself therefore does not mean forgiving every wrong or being forced to surrender one’s rights.

Al-Shūrā 42:40, Al-Shūrā 42:41, Al-Shūrā 42:42, and Al-Shūrā 42:43 distinguish proportionate response, pardon and reconciliation, and perseverance against injustice.

This connection places forgiveness within justice and responsibility.

A harmed person should not be asked to surrender safety, rights, or dignity in the name of restraining anger.

The connection with doing good

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 ends:

وَاللَّهُ يُحِبُّ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

wa-allāhu yuḥibbu al-muḥsinīn

Āl ʿImrān 3:134

This connects restraint, pardon, and expenditure with iḥsān.

Iḥsān concerns doing good and acting well.

The association does not change the meaning of ghayẓ.

It remains intense anger; moral value emerges in how people deal with it and choose good conduct.

The distinction between anger, perseverance, and pardon may also be examined through Al-Shūrā 42:37 and Al-Shūrā 42:40.

4. Anger in conflict, oppression, and enemy failure

Several occurrences of the root concern warfare and confrontation between groups.

Anger does not have a single role across them.

Believers may be angry about oppression and broken agreements; enemies may rage at failure; or a group’s rightful action may anger its opponents.

These differences should remain clear.

Removing anger from believers’ hearts

Al-Tawbah 9:15

وَيُذْهِبْ غَيْظَ قُلُوبِهِمْ وَيَتُوبُ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-yudhhib ghayẓa qulūbihim wa-yatūbu allāhu ʿalā man yashāʾu wa-allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm

Al-Tawbah 9:15

Translation: “And remove the anger from their hearts; The God turns toward whomever He wills, and The God is Knowing, Wise.”

غَيْظَ is the object of يُذْهِبْ.

قُلُوبِهِمْ locates the anger within people.

Al-Tawbah 9:13 describes people who broke their agreements, sought to expel the Messenger, and initiated hostility.

Al-Tawbah 9:14 discusses confronting them and aid for believers.

Al-Tawbah 9:15 then describes removing anger from the hearts of a group of believers.

The anger belongs to a context of actual harm, broken agreements, and conflict.

It does not follow that every personal anger authorizes warfare or harming someone.

The preceding verses explain the confrontation through the other party’s specified conduct.

Removing anger differs from seeking revenge

A person may experience intense anger after being wronged.

Restoring their rights appropriately and removing danger may also calm that anger.

Reduced ghayẓ may then result from security and justice.

Seeking another’s suffering solely for enjoyment is a different matter.

The root itself cannot establish the legitimacy of revenge.

Al-Tawbah 9:15 likewise discusses relief from anger within a specific event, rather than treating anger as justification for every act.

The verse also introduces turning back and repentance at its conclusion.

Its context is therefore not confined to permanent hostility and destruction of the opponent.

Rightful action and opponents’ anger

Al-Tawbah 9:120 discusses believers’ hardships and efforts while accompanying the Messenger.

Among them is treading ground that angers the disbelievers.

يَغِيظُ here concerns angering someone.

The passage belongs to a context of effort, danger, and actual confrontation.

It does not make angering people with different beliefs every believer’s purpose.

An action for truth and justice may itself anger opponents.

Defending someone harmed by injustice, for example, may anger the oppressor.

The oppressor’s anger alone does not make the defense wrong.

Conversely, making people angry does not by itself prove an action right.

The action’s purpose and method matter, not merely the intensity of opponents’ anger.

Al-Fatḥ 48:29 also depicts believers’ growth and strength through plant imagery, then describes its angering disbelievers.

لِيَغِيظَ is a subjunctive imperfect verb following lām; it concerns provoking opponents’ anger through that growth and strength.

The description الْكُفَّار should not be applied indiscriminately to everyone with different beliefs in every period and circumstance.

Nor should it become an independent duty to insult, provoke, or anger others.

Enemies returning angry and unsuccessful

Al-Aḥzāb 33:25

وَرَدَّ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِغَيْظِهِمْ لَمْ يَنَالُوا خَيْرًا وَكَفَى اللَّهُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ الْقِتَالَ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ قَوِيًّا عَزِيزًا

wa-radda allāhu alladhīna kafarū bi-ghayẓihim lam yanālū khayran wa-kafā allāhu al-muʾminīna al-qitāla wa-kāna allāhu qawiyyan ʿazīzā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:25

Translation: “The God turned back those who disbelieved with their rage, having gained no good, and spared the believers fighting. The God is Strong, Mighty.”

بِغَيْظِهِمْ describes the enemies’ condition as they return.

They acted toward a goal but failed to obtain the expected result.

Their anger accompanies failure.

This differs from Al-Tawbah 9:15.

Al-Tawbah describes anger being removed from believers.

Al-Aḥzāb describes enemies returning with their anger.

The word alone therefore does not identify its bearer as oppressed or oppressive.

Context and conduct determine that.

5. Anger in Pharaoh’s speech and responses to frustration

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:55

وَإِنَّهُمْ لَنَا لَغَائِظُونَ

wa-innahum lanā la-ghāʾiẓūn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:55

Translation: “They indeed provoke our anger.”

غَائِظُونَ is a plural active participle from غاظ.

The identity of the speaker matters.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:53, Pharaoh sends gatherers into the cities.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:54, he describes the Israelites as a small group.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:55, he says that they provoke his people’s anger.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:56 then emphasizes his forces’ readiness and vigilance.

غَائِظُون thus belongs to Pharaoh’s description of a group leaving his control.

Anger in someone who holds power

This example shows that ghayẓ need not be the anger of a powerless person.

Pharaoh possesses political power and gathers forces to pursue the Israelites.

Yet their conduct angers him.

The definition “concealed anger of someone powerless” cannot therefore govern every Quranic use without exception.

Here anger relates to retaining control and preventing a group’s departure from his rule.

The verse does not make his anger evidence that his conduct is right.

His speech appears within the account of oppression, rebellion, and Israel’s deliverance.

Having anger and the power to act does not establish the angry person’s rightness.

Does anger always result from actual harm?

Someone may be angry because another has violated their rights.

Someone else may be angry at another’s freedom, growth, or success because it threatens their own power or interests.

In the latter case, anger may reflect an unjust desire of the powerful.

Pharaoh’s example shows why the cause requires separate examination.

Intense feeling alone does not distinguish right from wrong.

Ghayẓ in Al-Ḥajj 22:15

Al-Ḥajj 22:15

مَنْ كَانَ يَظُنُّ أَنْ لَنْ يَنْصُرَهُ اللَّهُ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ فَلْيَمْدُدْ بِسَبَبٍ إِلَى السَّمَاءِ ثُمَّ لْيَقْطَعْ فَلْيَنْظُرْ هَلْ يُذْهِبَنَّ كَيْدُهُ مَا يَغِيظُ

man kāna yaẓunnu an lan yanṣurahu allāhu fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati fa-l-yamdud bi-sababin ilā al-samāʾi thumma li-yaqṭaʿ fa-l-yanẓur hal yudhhibanna kayduhu mā yaghīẓ

Al-Ḥajj 22:15

Translation: “Whoever thinks that The God will not help him in this world and the Hereafter, let him extend a rope toward the sky, then cut; then let him see whether his scheme removes what angers him.”

يَغِيظُ concerns something that provokes a person’s anger.

مَا يَغِيظُ can be rendered “what angers him.”

Two familiar interpretations concern the preceding image. The referent of the pronoun in يَنْصُرَهُ is also disputed: one reading refers to the Messenger, another to the person discussed. “Him” in the translation does not settle that question by itself.

One reading takes السَّمَاء as a roof and سبب as a rope, understanding the image as a desperate, self-destructive response to rage. This is an exegetical explanation of the image, not practical guidance or a general instruction.

Another understands an attempt to prevent divine aid or cut off its means.

Both retain the importance of the closing question: can a person’s scheme remove what provokes their anger?

The root study need not use the meaning of ghayẓ to settle this exegetical disagreement.

Ghayẓ still concerns anger and agitation.

What angers the person and how their action is imagined require separate contextual analysis.

Intense anger does not by itself confer the power to change reality or an event’s outcome.

Someone may resent a reality without that resentment removing it.

6. تَغَيُّظ and غَيْظ in descriptions of fire

Two further Quranic occurrences concern the fire of Hell.

These verses associate the language of anger with fire’s fierce raging and sound; a figurative reading is linguistically intelligible, without this study settling every question about its reality in the Hereafter.

They show that the Quran can apply the language of anger beyond human beings.

Fire’s raging in Al-Furqān

Al-Furqān 25:12

إِذَا رَأَتْهُمْ مِنْ مَكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ سَمِعُوا لَهَا تَغَيُّظًا وَزَفِيرًا

idhā raʾathum min makānin baʿīdin samiʿū lahā taghayyuẓan wa-zafīrā

Al-Furqān 25:12

Translation: “When it sees them from a distant place, they hear its raging and roaring.”

تَغَيُّظًا is a Form V verbal noun.

تَغَيَّظَ concerns becoming agitated and manifesting intense anger.

The subject here is the fire of punishment, not a human being.

زَفِيرًا belongs to ز ف ر and concerns the sound of forceful breathing, groaning, or roaring.

Together, the words present an auditory image of the fire’s intensity.

Does fire experience human anger?

The verse speaks of the fire seeing and raging.

Without independent evidence, every psychological and bodily feature of an angry person should not be attributed to fire.

Arabic can describe something fierce and loud through words familiar from human emotions.

Persian, for example, also speaks of an “angry sea.”

This describes fierce waves and stormy conditions without necessarily attributing a human mind or feelings to the sea.

In Al-Furqān 25:12, taghayyuẓ is associated with the fire’s audible roaring.

Figurative expression through intense anger and raging is therefore readily understandable.

The verse offers no technical account of the fire’s perception in the Hereafter. It should neither become a definitive theory of the fire’s mental structure nor be reduced entirely to familiar worldly phenomena.

Almost bursting apart with rage

Al-Mulk 67:8

تَكَادُ تَمَيَّزُ مِنَ الْغَيْظِ كُلَّمَا أُلْقِيَ فِيهَا فَوْجٌ سَأَلَهُمْ خَزَنَتُهَا أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ نَذِيرٌ

takādu tamayyazu mina al-ghayẓi kullamā ulqiya fīhā fawjun saʾalahum khazanatuhā a-lam yaʾtikum nadhīr

Al-Mulk 67:8

Translation: “It almost bursts apart with rage. Whenever a group is cast into it, its keepers ask them: did no warner come to you?”

الْغَيْظِ describes the fire here.

تَمَيَّزُ belongs to م ي ز and concerns parts separating or bursting apart in this context.

The preceding Al-Mulk 67:7 describes the fire’s fierce sound and boiling.

Al-Mulk 67:8 then expresses its intensity through ghayẓ.

Two images thus appear together: boiling and roaring, and near bursting from intense rage.

The image of anger and the root’s lexical meaning

Human anger may involve inner pressure, altered voice, and forceful movements.

The Quranic portrayal of fire similarly emphasizes intensity and raging sound.

Ghayẓ itself, however, does not mean boiling or bursting apart.

تَفُورُ in Al-Mulk 67:7 belongs to another root and expresses boiling.

تَمَيَّزُ likewise concerns separation and bursting apart.

غَيْظ expresses intense agitation within this description.

The brief root definition should therefore not become “the fire’s intense heat.”

Heat and roaring are understood from the passages’ contexts; intense anger remains the word’s core.

Human ghayẓ should likewise not be reduced to bodily heat or an increase in temperature.

7. Distinctions from neighboring roots

غ ي ظ (ghayẓ) and غ ض ب (ghaḍab)

Both ghaḍab and ghayẓ concern anger.

Lexicons variously describe ghayẓ as more intense anger, concealed anger, or the onset of increasing anger.

The semantic fields are close, but no single absolute rule captures their distinction.

Quranic ghayẓ prominently involves inner pressure and intense agitation.

Ghaḍab has a broader Quranic range, describing human anger as well as divine judgment and displeasure.

Al-Shūrā 42:37 uses غَضِبُوا for people who pardon when angry.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 expresses restraint through كَظْمِ الْغَيْظ.

The passages are close in their ethical subject.

One cannot conclude that ghayẓ always remains in the heart while ghaḍab always becomes outward action.

Pharaoh openly expresses anger in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:55 through the family of ghayẓ.

Such a boundary therefore does not hold across all usages.

غ ي ظ (ghayẓ) and ك ظ م (kaẓm)

Kaẓm concerns holding and restraining something accumulated within.

Ghayẓ is the anger and agitation itself.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 places the roots together.

This distinguishes the emotion from its restraint.

A person may experience ghayẓ yet restrain it.

Kaẓm thus concerns handling anger rather than having no angry feeling.

It should not mean inability to defend oneself or forced silence under oppression.

غ ي ظ (ghayẓ) and ع ف و (ʿafw)

ʿAfw concerns pardon and foregoing a claim or accountability for a fault.

Ghayẓ is anger.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 places restraining anger alongside pardoning people.

Someone may waive a claim despite feeling angry.

They may also restrain anger while pursuing their rights justly.

Restraint therefore does not make every lawful, just pursuit of rights contrary to the Quran.

The place of pardon requires examining justice, proportionate response, reconciliation, and preventing aggression as well.

غ ي ظ (ghayẓ), ح ق د (ḥiqd), and ض غ ن (ḍighn)

Ḥiqd concerns resentment and retaining hostility within.

Quranic ḍighn likewise concerns concealed hostility and resentment.

Muḥammad 47:29 and Muḥammad 47:37 mention أَضْغَان.

These concepts relate to ghayẓ without being identical.

Ghayẓ may be an intense response to a particular event.

Resentment and ḍighn may remain within a person for a long time.

Someone may become angry at a false statement, then cease being angry once the truth becomes clear.

Another may retain resentment and turn it into lasting hostility.

Anger’s intensity and hostility’s duration are different features.

Ghayẓ accompanies concealed hostility in Āl ʿImrān 3:119, but this does not make every instance a lasting grudge.

غ ي ظ (ghayẓ) and غ ي ض (ghayḍ)

These roots differ only in their final consonant.

غ ي ظ, with ẓāʾ, concerns anger and agitation.

غ ي ض, with ḍād, concerns decrease and subsiding.

غِيضَ الْمَاءُ in Hūd 11:44 means that the water subsided.

غَيْظ in Āl ʿImrān 3:134 concerns anger.

Someone may calm down when anger subsides, but the conceptual connection does not merge the roots.

“Anger subsided” is a natural expression; etymologically, the verb of decrease and the noun of anger remain distinct families.

Root boxes and lexical studies must not interchange ghayẓ and ghayḍ.

غ ي ظ (ghayẓ) and ح ل م (ḥilm)

Ḥilm concerns forbearance, sound judgment, and avoiding hasty reactions.

Ghayẓ describes intense anger.

A forbearing person can become angry while responding thoughtfully.

Ḥilm and ghayẓ are therefore not necessarily incompatible states.

Ghayẓ concerns feeling and agitation, while ḥilm concerns a person’s considered disposition and conduct under provocation or difficulty.

Forbearance should not be equated with having no feelings or being unable to oppose wrongdoing.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For human غَيْظ, “intense anger,” “rage,” or “anger” are appropriate equivalents.

“Anger” is sufficient in fluent prose, while a lexical explanation can clarify intensity and inner pressure.

“Resentment” and “revenge” are not direct equivalents, although they may be associated in some contexts.

Āl ʿImrān 3:119

مِنَ الْغَيْظِ

mina al-ghayẓ

Āl ʿImrān 3:119

Suggested translation:

“From intense anger” or “from anger.”

مُوتُوا بِغَيْظِكُمْ

mūtū bi-ghayẓikum

Āl ʿImrān 3:119

Suggested translation:

“Die in your rage.”

Both occurrences should remain within one semantic field while preserving their different prepositions and sentence roles.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134

وَالْكَاظِمِينَ الْغَيْظَ

wa-al-kāẓimīna al-ghayẓ

Āl ʿImrān 3:134

Suggested translation:

“And those who restrain anger.”

This natural rendering preserves the distinction between kaẓm and ghayẓ.

“Those who never become angry” is incorrect: the verse concerns restraint, not absence of anger.

Al-Tawbah 9:15

وَيُذْهِبْ غَيْظَ قُلُوبِهِمْ

wa-yudhhib ghayẓa qulūbihim

Al-Tawbah 9:15

Suggested translation:

“And remove the anger from their hearts.”

Or, in fluent prose:

“And take away their hearts’ anger.”

Ghayẓ is the anger itself; يُذْهِبْ expresses its removal.

The root should not be reduced to “calming down,” which is the contextual outcome of anger being removed.

Al-Tawbah 9:120 and Al-Fatḥ 48:29

يَغِيظُ and لِيَغِيظَ

Suitable equivalents:

“Angers” or “provokes anger”; in لِيَغِيظَ, the lām must also be represented as the sentence requires, for example “so as to anger.”

These are transitive forms describing an action’s or event’s effect on someone else.

Al-Ḥajj 22:15

مَا يَغِيظُ

mā yaghīẓ

Al-Ḥajj 22:15

Suggested translation:

“What angers him.”

Precisely what angers him requires contextual study and should not be inserted into the root’s lexical meaning.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:55

لَغَائِظُونَ

la-ghāʾiẓūn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:55

Suggested translation:

“They indeed provoke our anger.”

غائظون describes agents provoking anger from the speakers’ perspective, not people who are themselves angry.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:25

بِغَيْظِهِمْ

bi-ghayẓihim

Al-Aḥzāb 33:25

Suggested translation:

“With their rage” or “while they were angry.”

Anger accompanies the enemies’ return and failure here.

Al-Furqān 25:12

تَغَيُّظًا

taghayyuẓan

Al-Furqān 25:12

Suggested translation:

For تَغَيُّظًا, “raging” or “raging anger”; in the complete phrase, تَغَيُّظًا وَزَفِيرًا, “raging and roaring,” preserving the distinct contribution of زَفِيرًا.

The word can be understood figuratively here as describing the fire’s intensity and raging.

Al-Mulk 67:8

مِنَ الْغَيْظِ

mina al-ghayẓ

Al-Mulk 67:8

Suggested translation:

“From intense anger” or “with rage.”

Anger’s intensity appears in the image of fire almost bursting apart.

Ghayẓ itself should not be defined as bursting apart.

Brief meanings for Forqan word boxes

For غَيْظ:

General root meaning: “Intense anger and inner agitation in response to something unwelcome; transitively, provoking another’s anger.”

For يَغِيظُ:

Meaning of this form: “Angering and provoking someone else’s anger.”

For غَائِظ:

Meaning of this form: “Someone or something that causes another’s anger.”

For تَغَيُّظ:

Meaning of this form: “The intensification and manifestation of anger; figuratively, fierce raging.”

Each form’s contextual meaning must be written separately; the form descriptions above do not replace the general root meaning.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:134, ghayẓ is the anger a person restrains.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:119, it is anger manifested alongside concealed hostility.

In Al-Tawbah 9:15, it is anger removed from people’s hearts.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:55, غائظون describes people who provoke anger from Pharaoh’s perspective.

In Al-Furqān 25:12 and Al-Mulk 67:8, the vocabulary describes the fire’s fierce raging.

One identical general explanation must not replace these contextual distinctions. These are editorial proposals; installing this study does not rewrite existing verse boxes.

9. Conclusion

In classical Arabic, غ ي ظ concerns intense anger and the inner pressure of agitation.

Poetic evidence associates it with suffering, hostility, frustration, and a desire to retaliate. Its verbal forms also describe provoking anger in others.

Early definitions variously present concealed anger in someone powerless, intense anger, or the onset of rising anger.

The Quranic usages do not permit restricting the family to concealed anger in the powerless.

Pharaoh is angered by the Israelites in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:55 despite possessing power.

Āl ʿImrān 3:119 associates ghayẓ with concealed hostility and double behavior.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 places restraint alongside giving and pardon among qualities of those who do good.

Al-Tawbah 9:15 describes removing anger from believers’ hearts in a confrontation with those who broke agreements.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:25, enemies return angry and unsuccessful.

Al-Tawbah 9:120 and Al-Fatḥ 48:29 describe actions and conditions that anger opponents.

Al-Ḥajj 22:15 asks whether someone’s scheme can remove what angers them.

Al-Furqān 25:12 and Al-Mulk 67:8 use ghayẓ in the portrayal of fire’s intensity and raging.

A central distinction emerges: anger alone establishes neither rightness nor oppression.

Someone may be angry at actual injustice.

Someone may resent another’s success.

A ruler may be angered by the freedom of people they wish to control.

Someone may also exercise restraint despite intense anger and avoid violating others’ rights.

Anger exists in all these situations, but its causes and the conduct’s moral worth differ.

The final root definition is:

غ ي ظ: intense anger and inner agitation, usually in response to unwelcome conduct, speech, or circumstances; transitively, angering another. Figurative uses also express fierce raging and intensity through this family.

Anger is an inner state; kaẓm is its restraint; ʿafw is pardon or waiving a claim; ghaḍab is a neighboring family of anger; resentment is hostility that may endure within.

These concepts are connected but should not be merged.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 makes a central ethical point: the Quran does not require having no angry feeling, but praises restraint and avoiding wrongful reactions alongside pardon and doing good.

Such self-control does not mean abandoning justice or being forced to accept oppression.

People can be angry at injustice and act to remove it without allowing anger to replace truth, fairness, and responsibility.

Ghayẓ is a feeling that may arise within a person; its cause and the conduct chosen in response are what matter ethically.
