In early Arabic, Q Ṣ D concerns turning toward something, aiming at it, moving in a defined direction, and following a straight, measured course. Moderation and avoiding excess or deficiency are also established meanings in this word family.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Muhalhil ibn Rabīʿah, concerning the wrongdoing of Banū Bakr and his brother’s death, contains this line:

جَارَتْ بَنُو بَكْرٍ وَلَمْ يَعْدِلُوا / وَالْمَرْءُ قَدْ يَعْرِفُ قَصْدَ الطَّرِيقِ

Banū Bakr acted unjustly and did not act fairly, although a person may know the straight course of the road.

Qaṣda l-ṭarīq here denotes the road’s straight course. The poet contrasts departure from justice with knowledge of the right path.

This transmitted poetic usage is relevant because the Qur’an uses a closely related expression in 16:9, contrasting the straight course with deviation. The exact date of the line has not been independently established; its attribution and transmission should therefore be distinguished from a securely dated contemporary record.

Forms examined: قَصَدَ, يَقْصِدُ, قَصْد, قَاصِد, مَقْصُود, مَقْصَد, مَقَاصِد, اِقْتَصَدَ, اِقْتِصَاد, مُقْتَصِد, and مُقْتَصِدَة.

Qur’anic forms: اقْصِدْ, قَصْد, قَاصِد, مُقْتَصِد, and مُقْتَصِدَة. The root occurs six times in six verses: 5:66, 9:42, 16:9, 31:19, 31:32, and 35:32. Muqtaṣid appears twice; each of the other four forms appears once. The additional verses quoted here provide context rather than further occurrences of this root.

1. Core meanings and semantic branches

The Arabic expression qaṣada ilā l-shayʾ means to turn toward something and take it as one’s aim.

Qaṣd can denote direction or the intention to reach something; maqṣad denotes an aim or destination; and maqṣūd describes what is intended or sought.

Someone may have a destination but not know the route, or know a route while pursuing a wrongful aim.

Having an intention does not in itself make the aim right. In Arabic, a person may intend injustice just as one may intend justice.

When qaṣd describes a path or a way of doing something, however, it can express straightness, measured conduct, and freedom from deviation.

Ṭarīqun qāṣid describes a straight, accessible road; safarun qāṣid describes a manageable journey without excessive distance or hardship.

In classical Arabic, iqtiṣād concerns observing a fitting measure and avoiding excess and deficiency. Someone who neither spends excessively nor withholds unduly may be called muqtaṣid.

Lexicons also record branches concerning breaking something into pieces and describing a well-fleshed camel. Qaṣīdah, a poem, belongs to the same lexical family, although its naming has received different explanations.

The historical relationship among all these branches is not conclusively established. Qur’anic usage concerns the straight course of a path, ease, moderation, and measured conduct.

2. Qaṣd al-sabīl: making the straight path known

وَعَلَى اللَّهِ قَصدُ السَّبيلِ وَمِنها جائِرٌ وَلَو شاءَ لَهَدىٰكُم أَجمَعينَ

Wa-ʿalā llāhi qaṣdu l-sabīli wa-minhā jāʾir. Wa-law shāʾa la-hadākum ajmaʿīn.

It rests with The God to make the straight path known; some paths deviate. Had He willed, He would have guided you all.

An-Nahl 16:9

Qaṣd al-sabīl here concerns the straight course of the path, contrasted with jāʾir, from J W R.

Jāʾir in this context describes a path departing from the right direction.

Qaṣd al-sabīl can denote a path’s straightness or right direction. The translation understands the full construction ʿalā llāhi qaṣdu l-sabīl as attributing the clarification of that path to The God. Making the path known is a reading of the construction, not a separate lexical meaning of qaṣd alone.

The God shows people the right path, but its existence does not remove every wrong path or force people to follow it.

The verse’s ending distinguishes these matters: had The God willed, He would have guided everyone.

Sūrat al-Insān states:

إِنّا هَدَينٰهُ السَّبيلَ إِمّا شاكِرًا وَإِمّا كَفورًا

Innā hadaynāhu l-sabīla immā shākiran wa-immā kafūrā.

We have shown him the way, whether he is grateful or deeply ungrateful.

Al-Insan 76:3

Showing the way differs from a person’s choice to take it. The God makes the way clear, while people remain responsible for their choices and conduct.

In 16:9, qaṣd does not mean taking a midpoint between opposing beliefs or roads. It concerns the straight course in contrast to deviation.

3. Safar qāṣid: a manageable journey

لَو كانَ عَرَضًا قَريبًا وَسَفَرًا قاصِدًا لَاتَّبَعوكَ وَلٰكِن بَعُدَت عَلَيهِمُ الشُّقَّةُ وَسَيَحلِفونَ بِاللَّهِ لَوِ استَطَعنا لَخَرَجنا مَعَكُم يُهلِكونَ أَنفُسَهُم وَاللَّهُ يَعلَمُ إِنَّهُم لَكٰذِبونَ

Law kāna ʿaraḍan qarīban wa-safaran qāṣidan la-ttabaʿūka wa-lākin baʿudat ʿalayhimu l-shuqqah. Wa-sa-yaḥlifūna bi-llāhi law istaṭaʿnā la-kharajnā maʿakum. Yuhlikūna anfusahum wa-llāhu yaʿlamu innahum la-kādhibūn.

Had it been a readily available gain and an easy journey, they would have followed you, but the difficult distance seemed far to them. They will swear by The God: “Had we been able, we would have gone out with you.” They destroy themselves, and The God knows that they are liars.

At-Tawbah 9:42

Qāṣidan is an active participle of qaṣada, modifying safaran, a journey.

Safar qāṣid describes a journey whose distance and difficulty are not excessive.

The verse contrasts readily available gain and an easy journey with a distant, difficult undertaking.

It concerns people willing to accompany others when profit is close and the journey easy, but who offer excuses when responsibility requires a difficult journey.

Ease itself is not condemned. The criticism concerns making duty conditional on personal benefit and convenient circumstances, then resorting to a false oath.

Verse 9:91 exempts the genuinely weak, ill, and those lacking resources to spend, while also mentioning their sincerity toward The God and His Messenger. This qualification should be retained when summarizing the verse.

Genuine inability must therefore be distinguished from a false claim of inability.

Safaran qāṣidan also shows that this root is not limited to inward intention or moral moderation: it can describe a journey’s character and degree of difficulty.

4. Wa-qṣid fī mashyik: moderation in outward conduct

وَاقصِد فى مَشيِكَ وَاغضُض مِن صَوتِكَ إِنَّ أَنكَرَ الأَصوٰتِ لَصَوتُ الحَميرِ

Wa-qṣid fī mashyika wa-ghḍuḍ min ṣawtik. Inna ankara l-aṣwāti la-ṣawtu l-ḥamīr.

Be moderate in your walking and lower your voice; surely the most disagreeable of voices is the voice of donkeys.

Luqman 31:19

Iqṣid is a form I imperative, and fī mashyika specifies the field in which the command applies.

Here qaṣd means walking in a measured and balanced way.

The preceding verse makes the ethical setting clear:

وَلا تُصَعِّر خَدَّكَ لِلنّاسِ وَلا تَمشِ فِى الأَرضِ مَرَحًا إِنَّ اللَّهَ لا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُختالٍ فَخورٍ

Wa-lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-l-nāsi wa-lā tamshi fī l-arḍi maraḥā. Inna llāha lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūr.

Do not turn your cheek away from people in arrogance, nor walk upon the earth exultantly. The God does not love any conceited boaster.

Luqman 31:18

Read together, these verses concern more than walking speed. They address how someone carries themselves and behaves among people.

Walking can express arrogance, ostentation, and a sense of superiority. A loud voice can likewise become a way to impose one’s presence and wishes on others.

Luqmān calls his son to measured conduct, without a need for display or an unnecessarily raised voice to assert personal importance.

The command does not prescribe a fixed walking speed or vocal volume. Someone hurrying to rescue another or shouting a warning is not necessarily departing from moderation.

The fitting measure of conduct is understood in light of circumstances, need, and purpose.

5. Muqtaṣid and muqtaṣidah: three different contexts

Muqtaṣid is a form VIII active participle, and muqtaṣidah is its feminine form.

The Qur’an uses this derivative in three different settings. Their comparison shows why it cannot everywhere be reduced to a person of merely average performance.

An upright community among the People of the Book

وَلَو أَنَّهُم أَقامُوا التَّورىٰةَ وَالإِنجيلَ وَما أُنزِلَ إِلَيهِم مِن رَبِّهِم لَأَكَلوا مِن فَوقِهِم وَمِن تَحتِ أَرجُلِهِم مِنهُم أُمَّةٌ مُقتَصِدَةٌ وَكَثيرٌ مِنهُم ساءَ ما يَعمَلونَ

Wa-law annahum aqāmū l-Tawrāta wa-l-Injīla wa-mā unzila ilayhim min rabbihim la-akalū min fawqihim wa-min taḥti arjulihim. Minhum ummatun muqtaṣidah. Wa-kathīrun minhum sāʾa mā yaʿmalūn.

Had they upheld the Torah, the Gospel, and what was sent down to them from their Lord, they would have eaten from above them and from beneath their feet. Among them is a community following a measured, upright course, but many of them do evil in what they do.

Al-Ma'idah 5:66

Muqtaṣidah modifies ummah, describing this community’s way and conduct.

Here the community is contrasted with those who act badly. The context supports upright conduct, adherence to a sound path, and avoidance of deviation.

The verse begins by speaking of upholding the Torah, the Gospel, and what was sent down to them from their Lord.

The Qur’an does not present all the People of the Book as alike. Even while criticizing the conduct of many, it explicitly recognizes an upright community among them.

Verse 3:113 likewise speaks of a steadfast community among the People of the Book.

Moderation here does not mean selecting a point between truth and falsehood or compromising with wrongdoing. It concerns avoiding deviation and following the right path with sound judgment.

Muqtaṣid after deliverance from danger

وَإِذا غَشِيَهُم مَوجٌ كَالظُّلَلِ دَعَوُا اللَّهَ مُخلِصينَ لَهُ الدّينَ فَلَمّا نَجّىٰهُم إِلَى البَرِّ فَمِنهُم مُقتَصِدٌ وَما يَجحَدُ بِـٔايٰتِنا إِلّا كُلُّ خَتّارٍ كَفورٍ

Wa-idhā ghashiyahum mawjun ka-l-ẓulali daʿawu llāha mukhliṣīna lahu l-dīna fa-lammā najjāhum ilā l-barri fa-minhum muqtaṣid. Wa-mā yajḥadu bi-āyātinā illā kullu khattārin kafūr.

When a wave like canopies covers them, they call upon The God, devoting religion sincerely to Him. When He delivers them to land, some of them follow a measured, upright course. None rejects Our signs except every treacherous, deeply ungrateful person.

Luqman 31:32

The verse describes people calling upon The God sincerely when confronted by great danger.

After deliverance, however, their conduct does not remain the same in every case.

There are two principal readings of muqtaṣid in this context.

In the first, the person remains on the right path after rescue and does not wholly abandon the sincerity shown in distress.

In the second, the person offers only a limited measure of gratitude and right conduct after witnessing such a sign, failing to respond as fully as the experience warrants.

This study prefers the first reading because of the contrast with treacherous, ungrateful rejection at the end of the verse. That contextual preference does not remove the alternative reading of limited commitment or moderation in practice.

Verse 29:65 describes people who call upon The God sincerely when in danger at sea, but associate partners with Him again after rescue to land.

Sincerity is not revealed only at the moment of danger. Conduct after deliverance also shows how deeply the experience has affected understanding and action.

Muqtaṣid between self-wronging and leading in good deeds

ثُمَّ أَورَثنَا الكِتٰبَ الَّذينَ اصطَفَينا مِن عِبادِنا فَمِنهُم ظالِمٌ لِنَفسِهِ وَمِنهُم مُقتَصِدٌ وَمِنهُم سابِقٌ بِالخَيرٰتِ بِإِذنِ اللَّهِ ذٰلِكَ هُوَ الفَضلُ الكَبيرُ

Thumma awrathnā l-kitāba lladhīna iṣṭafaynā min ʿibādinā. Fa-minhum ẓālimun li-nafsihi wa-minhum muqtaṣidun wa-minhum sābiqun bi-l-khayrāti bi-idhni llāh. Dhālika huwa l-faḍlu l-kabīr.

Then We gave the Book as an inheritance to those We chose from among Our servants. Some of them wrong themselves, some follow a middle course, and some are foremost in good deeds by permission of The God. That is the great bounty.

Fatir 35:32

The verse identifies three conditions among those who inherit the Book:

  • Wronging oneself.
  • Following a measured, middle course.
  • Being foremost in good deeds.

Here muqtaṣid occupies the position between the one who wrongs themselves and the one foremost in good deeds.

The verse gives no numerical criteria or list of deeds defining the precise boundaries of the three groups. The word alone cannot supply every detail of their duties or ranks in the Hereafter.

The Qur’an does not describe being foremost in good deeds as an excess opposed to moderation.

One person may live rightly and with balance, while another goes further in doing good, helping people, and making good use of their abilities.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an commands people to vie in good deeds:

وَلِكُلٍّ وِجْهَةٌ هُوَ مُوَلِّيهَا فَٱسْتَبِقُوا۟ ٱلْخَيْرَٰتِ أَيْنَ مَا تَكُونُوا۟ يَأْتِ بِكُمُ ٱللَّهُ جَمِيعًا إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

Wa-li-kullin wijhatun huwa muwallīhā. Fa-stabiqū l-khayrāt. Ayna mā takūnū yaʾti bikumu llāhu jamīʿā. Inna llāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr.

Each has a direction toward which they turn. So vie with one another in good deeds. Wherever you may be, The God will bring you all together. The God has power over all things.

Al-Baqarah 2:148

Moderation therefore does not mean limiting goodness or avoiding leadership in doing good.

Being foremost in good deeds is a virtue to which the Qur’an calls people, provided it does not involve violating rights, causing harm, or exceeding rightful limits.

6. Iqtiṣād: the earlier meaning and modern terminology

In earlier Arabic, iqtiṣād meant observing a fitting measure and avoiding excess or deficiency.

In managing a livelihood, a muqtaṣid regulated spending judiciously, neither squandering nor withholding unduly.

The Qur’an expresses a related principle with other vocabulary:

وَالَّذينَ إِذا أَنفَقوا لَم يُسرِفوا وَلَم يَقتُروا وَكانَ بَينَ ذٰلِكَ قَوامًا

Wa-lladhīna idhā anfaqū lam yusrifū wa-lam yaqturū wa-kāna bayna dhālika qawāmā.

Those who, when they spend, are neither excessive nor tight-fisted, but maintain a balanced course between the two.

Al-Furqan 25:67

In modern Arabic, iqtiṣād is also the term for the economy and economics: production, distribution, exchange, and consumption, and the study of these activities.

This modern technical use must be distinguished from the older sense of measured conduct. The lexical resemblance alone does not establish the full historical route of the technical term’s development.

None of the three Qur’anic occurrences of muqtaṣid or muqtaṣidah means an economist, financial specialist, or merely a thrifty consumer.

The later expression maqāṣid al-sharīʿah draws on the branch concerning aims and purposes. The Qur’an does not use that expression as the name of the later theoretical framework.

Later technical terminology must not replace the meaning of a word in its Qur’anic context.

7. Comparison with related roots

Q Ṣ D (qaṣd) and W S Ṭ (wasaṭ)

Wasaṭ concerns a position between two sides, a middle point, and, in some uses, measured judgment and moderation.

Qaṣd concerns aiming, direction of movement, and following a measured course.

The roots overlap in moderation, but qaṣd does not always mean occupying a midpoint.

In 16:9, the subject is the straight course of the path, not a midpoint among several different roads.

Q Ṣ D (qaṣd) and ʿ D L (ʿadl)

In its justice branch, ʿadl concerns respecting rights, proportion, and fair judgment.

In its ethical branch, qaṣd concerns upright conduct and avoidance of deviation and excess.

Someone may spend moderately yet judge unjustly between two people. Establishing justice may also require action far greater in extent than ordinary behaviour.

Moderation and justice may accompany one another, but they are not identical concepts.

Q Ṣ D (qaṣd) and Q W M (istiqāmah)

Istiqāmah concerns remaining straight, upright, and steadfast.

In the path-related branch, qaṣd emphasizes the right direction and a course without deviation.

Knowing the right direction differs from remaining steadfast in it. Someone may know the right path and intend to follow it, yet turn away under pressure or through attachment to other things.

Q Ṣ D (qaṣd) and H D Y (hidāyah)

Hidāyah concerns showing the way, guiding, and leading; qaṣd concerns direction and, in the appropriate context, the straight course of the path.

Both word families occur together in 16:9.

People are shown the right path, while following it and remaining steadfast involve human choice and responsibility.

Q Ṣ D (qaṣd) and R W D (irādah)

Irādah concerns wanting something and willing an action; qaṣd places greater emphasis on direction toward a defined aim.

A person may wish to reach a certain condition without yet having identified a specific goal and route to it.

Irādah and qaṣd overlap in Arabic. Wanting, setting a destination, moving toward it, and reaching it are distinguishable ideas, but these distinctions should not be treated as rigid boundaries for every use of the two words.

8. Translation choices for Forqan

  • Ummah muqtaṣidah in 5:66: a community following a measured, upright course.
  • Safaran qāṣidan in 9:42: an easy, manageable journey.
  • Qaṣd al-sabīl in 16:9: the straight course of the path.
  • Wa-qṣid fī mashyika in 31:19: be moderate in your walking.
  • Muqtaṣid in 31:32: one following a measured, upright course.
  • Muqtaṣid in 35:32: one following a middle course.

Brief root meaning: Turning toward a defined aim; straightness of direction and course; and, in conduct and livelihood, observing a fitting measure while avoiding deviation, excess, and deficiency.

Conclusion

Q Ṣ D concerns aims, direction, the course of a path, and measured conduct.

The Qur’an uses it for the straight course of the path, ease of travel, moderation in walking, and people whose conduct follows an upright or middle course.

Qur’anic iqtiṣād is not confined to financial matters. Moderation does not always mean compromising between opposing views: sometimes the right course lies between excessive behaviours, and sometimes it requires standing clearly against a deviant course.

Nor does the Qur’an set moderation in opposition to being foremost in good deeds.

Qaṣd can express right direction and a fitting measure of conduct, but measured conduct must not become an excuse for doing too little good.
