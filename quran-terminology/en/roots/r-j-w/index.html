Hope, expectation, edges, and their distinction from postponement

The Arabic family R J W has a principal branch concerning hope and looking forward to something happening. A person anticipates something not yet realized, sometimes desiring it and sometimes simply keeping its occurrence or consequences in view.

Not every form means hope. Rajā, the maqṣūr form without a final hamzah, also means the side or edge of something; its plural is arjāʾ. Qur’anic forms such as turjī and murjawn concern postponement and awaiting a decision, a usage related to the hamzah-final root R J ʾ. Calling rajā maqṣūr does not mean its final vowel is short: it differs from rajāʾ by lacking a final hamzah.

Three fields should therefore be distinguished: hope and expectation, edges and sides, and postponing or deferring. Shared letters or similar forms do not justify deriving all these meanings from one etymological image.

The Qur’anic morphological index, including the postponement forms without hamzah, records 28 occurrences in 27 verses across 21 surahs: 22 Form I verbs concerning hope and expectation, three Form IV verbs meaning postponement, and three nominal or participial occurrences, arjāʾihā, marjuwwan, and murjawna. This is the index’s classification; it does not erase the lexical distinction between postponement and hope.

Qur’anic forms examined: yarjūna, tarjūna, yarjū, tarjū, tarjūhā, urjū, marjuwwan, arjāʾihā, arjih, turjī, and murjawna.

Rajāʾ does not occur as an independent verbal noun in the Qur’an, though verbs from this branch occur frequently. Other recorded Arabic forms include rajāwah, rajiyyah, rajawān, irtajā, and tarajjā; these exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. Semantic centers and early Arabic witnesses

Rajāʾ: hope and expectation concerning what has not yet happened

Rajā al-shayʾ means hoping for something and anticipating its occurrence. It is close to amal and contrasts with yaʾs, despair.

Lexical explanations distinguish two centers: hope, and the edge or side of something. Keeping them separate prevents either from being presented as the other’s certain historical origin without independent evidence.

In the first branch, rajawtu al-amra arjūhu means that I hope for or expect something to happen.

The important element is a person’s relationship to something not yet realized for them, or whose outcome is not yet clear.

Rajāʾ does not necessarily imply certainty. Someone may hope for something whose occurrence is uncertain. Nor does the word establish that their hope rests on sufficient evidence.

Rajāʾ may accompany effort and understanding, or become a weak, unsupported expectation. The circumstances and evidence surrounding the hope establish this difference, rather than the root alone.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim includes:

فَرَجِّي الْخَيْرَ وَانْتَظِرِي إِيَابِي
إِذَا مَا الْقَارِظُ الْعَنَزِيُّ آبَا

fa-rajjī al-khayra wa-intaẓirī iyābī / idhā mā al-qāriẓu al-ʿanaziyyu ābā

“Then hope for good and await my return, when that ʿAnazī Qāriẓ returns.”

The poem addresses the poet’s daughter. In the literary account surrounding it, he is wounded and near death.

The ʿAnazī Qāriẓ in Arabic story and proverb is someone who left and never returned. The condition therefore has an allusive force, evoking a very distant or impossible return.

The witness matters because rajjī concerns hoping, yet appears in a setting where fulfillment is highly unlikely.

Consequently, rajāʾ cannot be said always to mean a realistic expectation, a guaranteed outcome, or something highly likely to happen in basic Arabic usage.

Rajāʾ names the hope or expectation itself; its soundness and likelihood of fulfillment are separate matters.

Can rajāʾ mean fear?

Another recorded usage involves rajā meaning fear or paying heed to something’s consequences.

For example, Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī says:

إِذَا لَسَعَتْهُ النَّحْلُ لَمْ يَرْجُ لَسْعَهَا
وَخَالَفَهَا فِي بَيْتِ نُوبٍ عَوَاسِلِ

idhā lasaʿathu al-naḥlu lam yarju lasʿahā / wa-khālafahā fī bayti nūbin ʿawāsili

“When the bees sting him, he does not fear their sting and makes his way into the hive of honey-making bees.”

Lam yarju lasʿahā cannot sensibly mean here that he “did not hope the bees would sting him.” It means he does not fear or heed their sting.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This particular verse is not independently dated. It is therefore safer evidence for classical Arabic usage than an unqualified, certainly pre-Qur’anic witness.

Lexical accounts particularly report rajāʾ meaning fear in negative constructions, such as lā yarjū, “he does not fear.”

This observation should not become an absolute rule. Not every negative sentence containing yarjū means “does not fear.”

The Qur’an also clearly uses lā yarjūna for not expecting an occurrence. Fear or disregard must therefore be assessed through context and the verb’s object.

Rajā: edge and side

Another branch is rajā, the maqṣūr form without a final hamzah, meaning the side or edge of something.

Arabic uses it for a well’s edge, its two sides, and the surroundings of a place.

Its dual is rajawān and its plural arjāʾ.

This differs from rajāʾ, “hope.” Pronunciation and word structure matter: rajā, “side,” and rajāʾ, “hope,” are distinct forms.

In classical Arabic, Dhū al-Rummah describes the desert with bayna al-rajā wa-al-rajā: “between this edge and that edge.”

This is post-Islamic poetry and should not be presented as pre-Islamic. Together with the lexical accounts, however, it confirms the spatial usage.

One may imagine comparisons between a spatial edge and hope, such as someone awaiting something looking towards a horizon. Such an image is not itself etymological evidence.

The two recorded meanings should remain distinct unless sound historical evidence establishes a derivational connection.

2. Hope for The God’s mercy alongside belief and effort

One of the clearest Qur’anic settings is hoping for The God’s mercy.

Belief, migration, and striving in The God’s way

Al-Baqarah 2:218 states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَاجَرُوا وَجَاهَدُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ أُولَٰئِكَ يَرْجُونَ رَحْمَتَ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

inna alladhīna āmanū wa-alladhīna hājarū wa-jāhadū fī sabīli allāhi ulāʾika yarjūna raḥmata allāhi wa-allāhu ghafūrun raḥīm

“Those who believed, and those who migrated and strove in The God’s way: they hope for The God’s mercy, and The God is forgiving, merciful.”

Yarjūna accompanies belief, migration, and striving in The God’s way.

The verse describes people who do more than claim hope: they have acted on their belief and accepted its difficulties.

Rajāʾ should nevertheless not be lexically redefined as “belief and righteous deeds.” Belief, migration, and striving come from other parts of the verse. Rajāʾ expresses their hope and expectation of The God’s mercy.

The distinction matters: hope can motivate effort, but yarjūna does not mean striving.

Nor does the verse give a conclusive statement of every individual’s final outcome. They hope, and The God is described as ghafūr, raḥīm, forgiving and merciful.

Hope is connected with mercy and forgiveness here, without its lexical meaning becoming a guarantee of the result.

Hope in The God amid shared difficulties

Part of Al-Nisāʾ 4:104 reads:

إِنْ تَكُونُوا تَأْلَمُونَ فَإِنَّهُمْ يَأْلَمُونَ كَمَا تَأْلَمُونَ وَتَرْجُونَ مِنَ اللَّهِ مَا لَا يَرْجُونَ

in takūnū taʾlamūna fa-innahum yaʾlamūna ka-mā taʾlamūna wa-tarjūna mina allāhi mā lā yarjūn

“If you suffer, they suffer as you suffer; and you hope from The God for what they do not hope for.”

The two groups share suffering and pain, but their hope and expectation differ.

Tarjūna mina allāh shows that the hope concerns something from The God.

This clearly demonstrates that rajāʾ does not mean the absence of pain, fear, or difficulty. A person may suffer while hoping for a different future.

Hope here connects with continuing and maintaining direction. Steadfastness or victory should nevertheless not become part of R J W’s lexical definition.

Hope and fear together

Part of Al-Isrāʾ 17:57 reads:

وَيَرْجُونَ رَحْمَتَهُ وَيَخَافُونَ عَذَابَهُ

wa-yarjūna raḥmatahu wa-yakhāfūna ʿadhābahu

“And they hope for His mercy and fear His punishment.”

This is one of the clearest witnesses to the distinction between rajāʾ and khawf in the Qur’an.

Yarjūna and yakhāfūna occur alongside each other in one construction.

Rajāʾ concerns The God’s mercy, and khawf His punishment.

They are distinct states here. Hope for mercy should not be turned into fear of punishment without evidence.

Part of Al-Zumar 39:9 likewise reads:

يَحْذَرُ الْآخِرَةَ وَيَرْجُو رَحْمَةَ رَبِّهِ

yaḥdharu al-ākhirata wa-yarjū raḥmata rabbihi

“He is wary of the Hereafter and hopes for his Lord’s mercy.”

Yaḥdharu and yarjū appear alongside each other.

Their association shows hope and apprehension coexisting in one person and influencing their conduct.

These verses do not establish a numerical proportion, fixed sequence, or conclusive psychological formula between hope and fear. They show two orientations together without fixing their measure in every situation.

Hope for mercy in human relationships

Al-Isrāʾ 17:28 states:

وَإِمَّا تُعْرِضَنَّ عَنْهُمُ ابْتِغَاءَ رَحْمَةٍ مِنْ رَبِّكَ تَرْجُوهَا فَقُلْ لَهُمْ قَوْلًا مَيْسُورًا

wa-immā tuʿriḍanna ʿanhumu ibtighāʾa raḥmatin min rabbika tarjūhā fa-qul lahum qawlan maysūrā

“And if you turn away from them, seeking mercy from your Lord that you hope for, then speak to them an easy, gentle word.”

Tarjūhā refers to raḥmah, mercy.

A person awaits mercy from their Lord while still having a duty to speak appropriately to those asking something of them.

Rajāʾ concerns something not yet realized. What the person awaits is not presently available to them, or has not yet reached the desired outcome.

This shows hope extending beyond the Hereafter. A person can expect mercy from their Lord in this life as well.

3. Hope for an outcome and expectation of the future

Trade that will not fail

Fāṭir 35:29 states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَتْلُونَ كِتَابَ اللَّهِ وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَنْفَقُوا مِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ سِرًّا وَعَلَانِيَةً يَرْجُونَ تِجَارَةً لَنْ تَبُورَ

inna alladhīna yatlūna kitāba allāhi wa-aqāmū al-ṣalāta wa-anfaqū mimmā razaqnāhum sirran wa-ʿalāniyatan yarjūna tijāratan lan tabūr

“Those who recite The God’s Book, establish prayer, and spend secretly and openly from what We have provided them hope for a trade that will not fail.”

Yarjūna concerns expecting a desirable result.

Tijāratan lan tabūr is what they hope for: an outcome that will not perish or prove fruitless.

Neither trade nor profit is the meaning of R J W itself. The verse uses commercial imagery to express the relationship between conduct and outcome.

Here too hope accompanies action, while rajāʾ lexically remains an expectation of the result.

Hope others place in a person

In Ṣāliḥ’s account, part of Hūd 11:62 reads:

قَالُوا يَا صَالِحُ قَدْ كُنْتَ فِينَا مَرْجُوًّا قَبْلَ هَٰذَا

qālū yā ṣāliḥu qad kunta fīnā marjuwwan qabla hādhā

“They said: Ṣāliḥ, before this you were someone among us in whom we placed hope.”

Marjuwwan is a passive participle of rajā, describing someone in whom others have placed hope or expectation.

Unlike many other verses, this concerns a community’s expectation of a person among them, rather than human hope in The God.

The verse shows rajāʾ occurring in human relationships too.

Marjuwwan alone does not establish that Ṣāliḥ was necessarily expected to become a political leader, wield power, succeed the elders, or represent the people’s social interests.

These may be contextual possibilities to examine, but are not contained in marjuww itself.

Its brief meaning is “one in whom hope is placed.” Establishing the substance of that hope requires more evidence.

Something whose occurrence was not expected

Part of Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:86 reads:

وَمَا كُنْتَ تَرْجُو أَنْ يُلْقَىٰ إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابُ إِلَّا رَحْمَةً مِنْ رَبِّكَ

wa-mā kunta tarjū an yulqā ilayka al-kitābu illā raḥmatan min rabbika

“And you did not expect the Book to be conveyed to you, except as a mercy from your Lord.”

Tarjū means expecting here.

This matters because the subject is not hope for an ordinary advantage or a personal wish, but an event the addressee had not anticipated.

Rajā can therefore express expecting something to happen without necessarily emphasizing intense desire or a heartfelt wish.

4. Expecting encounter, reckoning, and resurrection: testing the definition

Some verses associate rajāʾ with matters that cannot simply be understood as hoping for a pleasant event.

These cases are particularly important for testing the root’s definition.

Lā yarjūna liqāʾanā

Part of Yūnus 10:7 reads:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ لَا يَرْجُونَ لِقَاءَنَا وَرَضُوا بِالْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَاطْمَأَنُّوا بِهَا

inna alladhīna lā yarjūna liqāʾanā wa-raḍū bi-al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-iṭmaʾannū bihā

“Those who do not expect to meet Us, and are content with worldly life and have settled into it…”

This expression also appears in Yūnus 10:11, Yūnus 10:15, and Al-Furqān 25:21.

Here, translating lā yarjūna liqāʾanā only as “do not hope to meet Us” may suggest that they believe in the encounter but lack hope for a favorable outcome from it.

The verses describe people who do not take the divine encounter seriously in their reckoning of life, or do not expect it to happen.

“Do not expect to meet Us” therefore conveys a broader sense closer to the construction in these cases.

A contextual explanation may discuss disregard for reckoning and its consequences. Such disregard follows from the situation and attitude described, rather than a fixed meaning of the root itself.

Lā yarjūna nushūrā

Part of Al-Furqān 25:40 reads:

بَلْ كَانُوا لَا يَرْجُونَ نُشُورًا

bal kānū lā yarjūna nushūrā

“Rather, they did not expect a resurrection.”

Nushūran concerns being raised.

The issue is not simply a lack of hope for reward. They have not included resurrection within their expectations and outlook.

If rajāʾ always meant only desiring something pleasant, this verse would be difficult: resurrection does not necessarily have a desirable outcome from every person’s perspective.

The definition must therefore also accommodate expecting an occurrence.

Lā yarjūna ḥisābā

Al-Nabaʾ 78:27 states:

إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا لَا يَرْجُونَ حِسَابًا

innahum kānū lā yarjūna ḥisābā

“They did not expect a reckoning.”

The next verse, Al-Nabaʾ 78:28, states:

وَكَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا كِذَّابًا

wa-kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā kidhdhābā

“And they emphatically denied Our signs.”

The context concerns not expecting a reckoning and denying the signs.

Rajāʾ should not mean hoping to benefit from reckoning here. The subject is expecting it to happen.

This is one of the clearest witnesses that rajāʾ does not always coincide with the outcome’s desirability for the person.

Hope for encounter on the other side

By contrast, part of Al-Kahf 18:110 reads:

فَمَنْ كَانَ يَرْجُو لِقَاءَ رَبِّهِ فَلْيَعْمَلْ عَمَلًا صَالِحًا وَلَا يُشْرِكْ بِعِبَادَةِ رَبِّهِ أَحَدًا

fa-man kāna yarjū liqāʾa rabbihi fa-l-yaʿmal ʿamalan ṣāliḥan wa-lā yushrik bi-ʿibādati rabbihi aḥadā

“So whoever hopes to meet his Lord should do righteous work and associate no one in worship of his Lord.”

Rajāʾ connects here with an orientation of belief and righteous conduct.

Part of Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:5 likewise reads:

مَنْ كَانَ يَرْجُو لِقَاءَ اللَّهِ فَإِنَّ أَجَلَ اللَّهِ لَآتٍ

man kāna yarjū liqāʾa allāhi fa-inna ajala allāhi la-āt

“Whoever hopes to meet The God: The God’s appointed time is certainly coming.”

These verses show rajāʾ concerning encounter taking a positive sense of hope.

That positive usage does not establish that lā yarjūna liqāʾanā elsewhere merely means an absence of longing.

The object concerns encounter in both groups of verses, but the speakers’ or addressees’ positions and attitudes differ.

Rajāʾ concerning the Last Day

In Shuʿayb’s account, part of Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:36 reads:

وَارْجُوا الْيَوْمَ الْآخِرَ

wa-urjū al-yawma al-ākhir

“And expect the Last Day.”

The command accompanies a call to worship The God and avoid spreading corruption in the land.

Part of Al-Aḥzāb 33:21 also reads:

لِمَنْ كَانَ يَرْجُو اللَّهَ وَالْيَوْمَ الْآخِرَ

li-man kāna yarjū allāha wa-al-yawma al-ākhir

“For whoever hopes in The God and the Last Day.”

The same construction occurs in Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:6.

Rajāʾ connects here with a person’s orientation towards The God and a future reckoning.

The verb’s own meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from the doctrinal detail concerning the Last Day. Rajāʾ expresses hope and expectation; belief in reckoning, recompense, and resurrection comes from its object and other verses.

5. Rajāʾ, fear, and disregard in difficult usages

Mā lakum lā tarjūna li-allāhi waqārā

In Noah’s account, Nūḥ 71:13 states:

مَا لَكُمْ لَا تَرْجُونَ لِلَّهِ وَقَارًا

mā lakum lā tarjūna li-allāhi waqārā

“What is wrong with you that you give no regard to The God’s dignity and greatness?”

The construction differs from many other occurrences.

Waqāran is the object, and li-allāh has an important place in the sentence.

Lexical and exegetical accounts offer several explanations.

Some understand not fearing The God’s greatness; others, paying no regard to His greatness and standing. Another reading retains ordinary hope, relating the expression to expecting good from honoring The God.

The disagreement shows why one mechanical equivalent cannot conclusively settle the verse’s meaning.

Consider Noah’s invitation and the next verse, Nūḥ 71:14:

وَقَدْ خَلَقَكُمْ أَطْوَارًا

wa-qad khalaqakum aṭwārā

“Though He created you in stages.”

The reading concerning disregard for The God’s greatness and standing clearly accords with the context.

Waqār nevertheless belongs to a different root. Greatness or weight of standing does not come from R J W itself.

Study of W Q R

Nor should the lexical explanation through fear in this construction make every negative Qur’anic usage of rajāʾ mean fear.

Lā yarjūna ayyāma allāh

Part of Al-Jāthiyah 45:14 reads:

قُلْ لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا يَغْفِرُوا لِلَّذِينَ لَا يَرْجُونَ أَيَّامَ اللَّهِ

qul li-alladhīna āmanū yaghfirū li-alladhīna lā yarjūna ayyāma allāh

“Tell those who believe to forgive those who do not expect The God’s days.”

Lā yarjūna again concerns expectation and regard for an occurrence.

Ayyāma allāh should be understood through the Qur’an’s uses of that expression. Ibrāhīm 14:5 likewise mentions reminding people of ayyāmi allāh, “The God’s days.”

The expression can refer to momentous events in which The God’s power, judgment, or governance becomes manifest.

Lā yarjūna ayyāma allāh should therefore not simply mean “they have no hope for happy days.”

Not expecting or taking The God’s days seriously better fits this context.

“Do not fear” may also convey part of the point, especially concerning consequences of divine judgment. Fear should not, without evidence, replace the expression’s entire semantic range.

Across these cases, rajāʾ sometimes expresses positive hope and sometimes expecting an occurrence. At times, negation and context foreground disregard or lack of fear.

The shared meaning should therefore not be restricted to hoping for good, nor should every rajāʾ be turned into fear.

6. Arjāʾihā: the heaven’s edges

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:17 states:

وَالْمَلَكُ عَلَىٰ أَرْجَائِهَا وَيَحْمِلُ عَرْشَ رَبِّكَ فَوْقَهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ ثَمَانِيَةٌ

wa-al-malaku ʿalā arjāʾihā wa-yaḥmilu ʿarsha rabbika fawqahum yawmaʾidhin thamāniyah

“And the angels are at its edges, and that day eight bear your Lord’s throne above them.”

Arjāʾihā is the plural of rajā, “edge” or “side.”

The preceding verse, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16, helps identify the pronoun hā:

وَانْشَقَّتِ السَّمَاءُ فَهِيَ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَاهِيَةٌ

wa-inshaqqati al-samāʾu fa-hiya yawmaʾidhin wāhiyah

“And the heaven splits, and that day it is frail.”

Arjāʾihā therefore refers to the heaven’s edges and sides.

This differs from rajāʾ, hope, and should not be rendered as “the heaven’s hopes” or anything similar.

Nor should “the heaven’s edges” immediately become a physical theory of the universe’s geometric shape, measurable limits, or cosmic structure.

The verse pictures an event of the Resurrection. Arjāʾ denotes sides and edges; the word alone does not specify their exact nature.

Rajā meaning edge is thus an established branch that should not be omitted merely because hope is more familiar.

7. Turjī and murjawn: postponement

Arjaʾa al-amra means postponing a matter.

Its form without hamzah, arjā or arjaytu, is also recorded with the sense of postponing.

An important distinction is involved: early lexical analysis recognizes hamzah-final R J ʾ separately from R J W meaning hope. In some morphological forms and Qur’anic readings, however, postponement forms with and without hamzah converge.

Morphological indexes may consequently place the forms without hamzah under R J W. This classification does not establish hope and postponement as necessarily arising from one historical meaning.

Arjih wa-akhāhu

In the account of Moses and Pharaoh, Al-Aʿrāf 7:111 states:

قَالُوا أَرْجِهْ وَأَخَاهُ وَأَرْسِلْ فِي الْمَدَائِنِ حَاشِرِينَ

qālū arjih wa-akhāhu wa-arsil fī al-madāʾini ḥāshirīn

“They said: Defer him and his brother, and send gatherers into the cities.”

A similar expression occurs in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36:

قَالُوا أَرْجِهْ وَأَخَاهُ وَابْعَثْ فِي الْمَدَائِنِ حَاشِرِينَ

qālū arjih wa-akhāhu wa-ibʿath fī al-madāʾini ḥāshirīn

“They said: Defer him and his brother, and dispatch gatherers into the cities.”

Both verses concern postponing a decision or action regarding Moses and his brother.

Arjih does not mean “place hope in him” in this context.

Established Qur’anic readings include forms with hamzah, such as arjiʾhu, and forms without it, such as arjih.

The difference matters to root study: the written and spoken form may vary while the contextual meaning remains postponement.

Murjawna li-amri allāh

Al-Tawbah 9:106 states:

وَآخَرُونَ مُرْجَوْنَ لِأَمْرِ اللَّهِ إِمَّا يُعَذِّبُهُمْ وَإِمَّا يَتُوبُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-ākharūna murjawna li-amri allāhi immā yuʿadhdhibuhum wa-immā yatūbu ʿalayhim wa-allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm

“And others are deferred pending The God’s command: either He punishes them or turns towards them, and The God is knowing, wise.”

Murjawna is a Form IV passive participle meaning postponement here.

It describes people whose case is deferred to a later time or another command.

Two possible outcomes are stated: punishment or acceptance of repentance.

Murjawna should therefore not be confused with marjuww, one in whom hope is placed.

Marjuww in Hūd 11:62 describes someone in whom others had placed hope. Murjawna in Al-Tawbah 9:106 describes those whose case is deferred.

The different morphology and context establish distinct meanings.

The hamzah form murjaʾūna is also established in Qur’anic readings. This connects the postponement usage with the hamzah-final root.

Turjī man tashāʾ

Part of Al-Aḥzāb 33:51 reads:

تُرْجِي مَنْ تَشَاءُ مِنْهُنَّ وَتُؤْوِي إِلَيْكَ مَنْ تَشَاءُ

turjī man tashāʾu minhunna wa-tuʾwī ilayka man tashāʾ

“You defer whichever of them you wish and bring close to you whichever you wish.”

Turjī contrasts with tuʾwī.

Turjī concerns postponing or temporarily setting aside; tuʾwī concerns bringing close and lodging.

Turjī does not mean “you give hope” or “you hope” here either.

The extent and practical application of this choice in the Prophet’s family relationships require separate interpretation. The verb’s lexical sense here is nevertheless deferring or postponing.

The hamzah form turjiʾu is also established in Qur’anic readings.

Arjih, murjawna, and turjī should therefore be examined within the field of postponement, rather than hope for a desirable outcome.

8. Comparison with nearby roots

R J W (rajāʾ) and M N Y (tamannī)

Both can concern something not yet realized that a person desires.

Tamannī tends to emphasize wanting or imagining something desirable; rajāʾ generally concerns expectation and hope for its occurrence.

Al-Najm 53:24 states:

أَمْ لِلْإِنسَانِ مَا تَمَنَّىٰ

am li-al-insāni mā tamannā

“Or does a human being have whatever they wish for?”

The verse distinguishes human desire from its fulfillment.

Rajāʾ also offers no guarantee of fulfillment. There should therefore be no absolute division making tamannī always fanciful and rajāʾ always realistic.

Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim’s poetic witness also shows rajāʾ applying to something highly unlikely.

The principal difference is a semantic tendency, rather than a categorical division between wrong wishes and right hope.

Study of M N Y

R J W (rajāʾ) and ʾ M L (amal)

Amal also concerns hope and expectation of the future.

Part of Al-Ḥijr 15:3 reads:

ذَرْهُمْ يَأْكُلُوا وَيَتَمَتَّعُوا وَيُلْهِهِمُ الْأَمَلُ

dharhum yaʾkulū wa-yatamattaʿū wa-yulhihimu al-amal

“Leave them to eat and enjoy themselves, and let hope distract them.”

Amal appears in a context of distracting heedlessness.

That does not make amal an invalid fancy in every Arabic usage.

Rajāʾ too may describe commendable hope or a misguided expectation, depending on its object and context.

The terms overlap, but neither should be made inherently negative and the other inherently positive.

Study of ʾ M L

R J W (rajāʾ) and Ṭ M ʿ (ṭamaʿ)

Ṭamaʿ concerns expectation and desire to obtain something.

Part of Al-Aʿrāf 7:56 reads:

وَادْعُوهُ خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا

wa-udʿūhu khawfan wa-ṭamaʿā

“And call upon Him with fear and expectation.”

Part of Al-Sajdah 32:16 likewise reads:

يَدْعُونَ رَبَّهُمْ خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا

yadʿūna rabbahum khawfan wa-ṭamaʿā

“They call upon their Lord with fear and expectation.”

Ṭamaʿ may emphasize desiring and expecting to obtain something, overlapping with rajāʾ in that respect.

In some verses, however, rajāʾ concerns expecting something not necessarily desirable, as in lā yarjūna ḥisābā.

Its Qur’anic range therefore cannot be restricted to desiring a benefit.

Study of Ṭ M ʿ

R J W (rajāʾ) and KH W F (khawf)

Khawf concerns fear and apprehension about an event or consequence that a person dreads.

Rajāʾ generally concerns hope and expectation, but certain Arabic constructions also use it for fear or regard for consequences.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57 clearly distinguishes them by placing yarjūna raḥmatahu alongside yakhāfūna ʿadhābahu.

When rajāʾ concerns expecting reckoning or divine events, however, fear should neither be excluded nor imposed merely to preserve that contrast.

There is a lexical distinction, but the boundaries of usage require examining all the evidence.

Study of KH W F

R J W (rajāʾ) and R J ʾ (irjāʾ)

This comparison is especially important morphologically and etymologically.

Rajāʾ concerns hope and expectation, while irjāʾ in its established usage means postponing something.

Arabic has postponement forms with and without hamzah, including some appearing in Qur’anic readings.

Phonetic and morphological proximity does not establish that hoping and postponing necessarily share one original meaning.

Expecting something may conceptually resemble keeping something waiting, but this resemblance does not establish a history of derivation.

Nor should the post-Qur’anic designation Murjiʾah and associated theological debates replace irjāʾ’s lexical meaning. First examine postponement in the language and the verses themselves; subsequent terminology can then be studied separately.

Study of R J ʾ

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

One fixed equivalent does not suit every form in this family.

Rajā / yarjū: hoped, expected, or anticipated something happening.

Rajāʾ: hope, expectation, or anticipation of something not yet realized. This exact verbal noun does not occur in the Qur’an.

Yarjūna raḥmata allāh: they hope for The God’s mercy.

Yarjū liqāʾa rabbihi: hopes to meet his Lord, or expects to meet his Lord.

Lā yarjūna liqāʾanā: they do not expect to meet Us.

Lā yarjūna ḥisābā: they do not expect a reckoning.

Lā yarjūna nushūrā: they do not expect resurrection.

Lā yarjūna nikāḥā: they do not expect marriage, or have no hope of marriage; in part of Al-Nūr 24:60.

Marjuww: one in whom hope is placed.

Rajā: the edge, side, or margin of something, semantically distinct from rajāʾ, hope.

Arjāʾ: edges, sides, or margins.

Arjāʾihā: its edges; in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:17, the heaven’s edges.

Arjih: defer him; postpone a decision or action concerning him.

Turjī: you defer or postpone.

Murjawna: people whose case is deferred or left pending another command.

Forqan should preserve the possibility of “expecting an occurrence” in verses concerning encounter, reckoning, and resurrection. Rendering every case as hope may narrow the meaning of verses concerning denial of reckoning.

In passages concerning mercy, “hope” is more natural. Passages concerning delay require “postpone,” “defer,” or their appropriate forms.

Given the established lexical alternatives in Nūḥ 71:13, “Why do you give no regard to The God’s dignity and greatness?” can convey the contextual point. It should remain clear that this rendering follows the verb, its object, and the context together, rather than the independent meaning of R J W.

10. Conclusion

R J W and the nearby Qur’anic forms do not constitute an entirely uniform family with just one meaning.

The first and best-known branch is rajāʾ, hope and expectation. It appears in hope for The God’s mercy, expecting a result or a future event, and hope placed in another person.

Passages concerning encounter, reckoning, and resurrection show that rajāʾ cannot always mean desiring a pleasant event. A person may expect something or, conversely, leave it out of their reckoning without the issue simply being liking or disliking it.

Certain negative Arabic constructions also use rajāʾ for fearing or heeding consequences. Mā lakum lā tarjūna li-allāhi waqārā is important for this range. It should not be generalized to all negative constructions.

The second branch is rajā, edge and side. Its plural arjāʾ occurs in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:17 concerning the heaven’s edges. This should be studied separately from hope, without unsupported imagery making either the other’s certain origin.

Postponement, irjāʾ, is a related but distinct third field examined here. Its forms without hamzah connect with Arabic forms containing hamzah, appearing in Al-Aʿrāf 7:111, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36, Al-Tawbah 9:106, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:51. Morphology and variant readings show that this usage should not be equated with hope.

Final definition: In R J W’s principal branch, rajāʾ denotes hope or expectation concerning something not yet realized or a result not yet reached. Desirability is prominent in many usages but is not a constant condition. The spatial rajā denotes an edge or side. Postponement forms require separate recognition through their relationship with R J ʾ and variant readings. Each form’s meaning follows its structure, the verb’s object, and the verse’s context.
