Central Quranic meaning: Sirāj denotes a lamp or means of illumination that gives light and illuminates its surroundings. In the Quran, it describes the sun and, figuratively, Muhammad as a messenger illuminating the way to God. Arabic lexicons also group sarj, a saddle for a horse or riding animal, under this family. The historical relationship among all these forms is nevertheless uncertain; an Iranian origin or transmission through another language has also been proposed for sirāj itself.

1. Introduction and meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic family S R J has several established uses, chiefly lamps and illumination, saddles, and, in some expressions, beauty and adornment.

Al-sirāj denotes a lamp used to illuminate a place.

Asraja al-sirāja means “he lit the lamp.”

Al-sarj denotes a saddle placed on the back of a horse or riding animal.

Asraja al-dābbata means “he saddled the animal.”

In some Arabic uses, sarraja al-shayʾa also means making something beautiful or adorned.

These meanings are recorded in Arabic lexicons, but their shared letters alone do not establish a single historical origin.

The lamp and saddle branches should be examined separately through early Arabic evidence.

First pre-Islamic example: the horse saddle in Salama ibn Jandal

The pre-Islamic poet Salama ibn Jandal says:

وَشَدُّ كُورٍ عَلَى وَجْنَاءَ نَاجِيَةٍ
وَشَدُّ سَرْجٍ عَلَى جَرْدَاءَ سُرْحُوبِ

wa-shaddu kūrin ʿalā wajnāʾa nājiyatin / wa-shaddu sarjin ʿalā jardāʾa surḥūbi

Translation:

“And fastening a camel saddle upon a sturdy, swift she-camel, and fastening a saddle upon a short-haired, tall horse.”

The verse places two types of riding equipment side by side.

Kūr refers to the camel’s saddle and equipment.

Sarj is the horse’s saddle.

Wajnāʾ describes a sturdy, powerful she-camel.

Nājiya conveys swiftness and the ability to cover distance.

Jardāʾ here describes a horse with short, sparse hair.

Surḥūb refers to the horse’s tall build.

The poet describes mounts being made ready and distinguishes a camel saddle from a horse saddle.

Sarj does not mean a lamp here.

Nor does sarj itself mean speed, strength, or travel; those features come from other words in the verse.

This example shows sarj, “horse saddle,” in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The verse is transmitted among Salama ibn Jandal’s poems. Like other pre-Islamic poetry, it survives through later transmission; its attribution and place in his established corpus nevertheless provide significant evidence for historical usage.

Second early example: the oil lamp in poetry attributed to al-Nabigha al-Jaʿdi

A verse attributed to al-Nabigha al-Jaʿdi reads:

يُضِيءُ كَضَوْءِ سِرَاجِ السَّلِيطِ
لَمْ يَجْعَلِ اللَّهُ فِيهِ نُحَاسَا

yuḍīʾu ka-ḍawʾi sirāji al-salīṭi / lam yajʿali Allāhu fīhi nuḥāsā

Translation:

“It shines like the light of an oil lamp in which God has placed no smoke.”

Sirāj here denotes an illuminating lamp.

Al-salīṭ refers to oil used to fuel the lamp.

Nuḥās in this old expression means smoke.

The poet thus evokes an oil-fueled lamp with bright, smokeless light.

Brightness and radiance are central to the image.

This usage shows that sirāj was a familiar means of illumination in early Arabic.

It also recalls everyday experience: a lamp, unlike an object merely reflecting light, would illuminate its surroundings when lit.

The image does not make a particular fuel, wick material, or physical mechanism part of the word’s lexical definition.

The lamp’s name concerns its illuminating function.

This verse is attributed to al-Nabigha al-Jaʿdi in some transmissions, while other attributions also occur. He lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, and this verse’s date is uncertain. It illustrates early Arabic usage but cannot establish a definitely pre-Islamic date.

A historical definition of sirāj

Recorded Arabic usage and the poetic example support the meaning of a lamp that gives light when lit and illuminates its surroundings.

The Quranic use for the sun also makes this sense clear.

Sirāj is fundamentally the name of a lighting device, but Arabic can apply it to something else with an illuminating role.

The sun, for example, can be called a sirāj because it illuminates the world.

A person can similarly be compared to a lamp through clarifying truth and guiding others.

This extension should not displace the basic meaning.

A defensible definition of sirāj in Arabic around the time of revelation is a lamp or illuminating device whose light brightens its surroundings; figuratively, something or someone serving to illuminate and guide.

Does sirāj derive from Persian cherāgh?

Historical linguistic studies have proposed a relationship between Arabic sirāj and words for “lamp” in Middle Iranian languages.

Forms related to cherāgh are known in pre-Islamic Iranian languages.

Aramaic also records a related-looking word for a lamp.

Accordingly, some researchers regard Arabic sirāj as a loanword related to Iranian cherāgh.

Two main transmission routes have been proposed: direct borrowing into Arabic from an Iranian language, or transmission through Aramaic.

These possibilities warrant historical study; establishing the route and every sound change requires independent evidence.

What is clear is that sirāj is established in Quranic Arabic.

Even if it originated in another language, its Quranic meaning must be understood through Arabic at the time of revelation and the verses’ contexts.

Its possible origin does not establish the same history for sarj, “horse saddle.”

The lexical analysis through beauty and adornment

One lexical analysis identifies beauty, adornment, and pleasing appearance as a common theme of S R J.

On this account, a lamp’s light adds beauty and splendor, while a saddle can adorn an animal.

This makes a semantic relationship between the uses intelligible.

A semantic explanation in a dictionary does not by itself establish etymological history.

Words can be grouped together in Arabic because of their similar forms despite different historical backgrounds.

Beauty therefore cannot be treated as the certain original meaning of every derivative without separate investigation.

In the Quran, this family directly concerns illumination, not saddles or outward adornment.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Sirāj

The noun for a lamp or illuminating device.

Ordinarily, it denotes a lamp that is lit and gives light.

It occurs four times in the Quran.

Three verses concern the sun and heavenly illumination.

One describes Muhammad figuratively as God’s messenger.

Suruj

The plural of sirāj, meaning lamps.

A canonical reading of al-Furqan 25:61 has surujan instead of sirājan.

This variation matters for number and reference and is discussed with that verse.

Sarj

A saddle for a horse or riding animal.

It occurs in Salama ibn Jandal’s poem.

Its plural is surūj.

This use differs from sirāj, “lamp.”

Asraja, yusriju

A Form IV verb.

It has two familiar Arabic uses.

أَسْرَجَ السِّرَاجَ

asraja al-sirāja

“He lit the lamp.”

أَسْرَجَ الْفَرَسَ

asraja al-farasa

“He saddled the horse.”

The verb has the same form in both constructions, but its object determines the meaning.

Asraja cannot invariably be translated either “light” or “saddle” without considering the object.

Isrāj

The verbal noun of Form IV.

With a lamp, it denotes lighting it.

With a riding animal, it denotes saddling it.

This verbal noun does not occur in the Quran in this form.

Misraja

A device holding a wick and oil for illumination.

In its familiar use, it is an oil-lamp vessel.

Masraja, with an initial a vowel, is also recorded for a lamp’s stand or support.

Arabic thus distinguishes between a lamp and its vessel or supporting structure.

Sarrāj

In the saddle branch, a maker or seller of saddles.

The trade is also called sirāja.

The term identifies an occupation.

It does not imply that everyone bearing the title also made lamps.

Sārij and musarraj

Sārij is recorded in some lexical uses for something bright or shining.

Musarraj also occurs in descriptions of beauty and adornment.

For example, a verse by Ruʾba ibn al-ʿAjjaj uses musarrajan to describe a nose.

Its interpretation is disputed: some relate it to beauty and brightness, others to resemblance to a kind of sword.

This restricted example should not determine the entire family’s meaning.

Other restricted uses

Lexical records also include forms concerning adornment, fabrication of false speech, and certain features of character or disposition.

These uses do not occur in the Quran.

They matter here only as evidence of the range of Arabic usage.

Falsehood or outward adornment should not be transferred without evidence into Quranic sirāj.

Forms actually used in the Quran

In the Hafs transmission from ʿAsim underlying this site’s Quranic text, S R J occurs four times, always as the noun sirāj:

  • Al-Furqan 25:61
  • Al-Ahzab 33:46
  • Nuh 71:16
  • Al-Nabaʾ 78:13

Al-Ahzab has wa-sirājan, with the conjunction wa-.

The other three have sirājan.

All four are indefinite and accusative.

Al-Furqan 25:61 also has a canonical reading with the plural surujan.

The Quranic forms thus concern lamps and illumination, not saddles, saddle-making, or the verb for lighting a lamp.

3. Wa-jaʿala al-shamsa sirājan: the sun as a lamp

وَجَعَلَ الْقَمَرَ فِيهِنَّ نُورًا وَجَعَلَ الشَّمْسَ سِرَاجًا

wa-jaʿala al-qamara fīhinna nūran wa-jaʿala al-shamsa sirājan

Nūḥ 71:16

Translation:

“And He made the moon among them a light, and made the sun a lamp.”

Nuh 71:16

This verse is among the clearest evidence for Quranic sirāj.

Its referent is explicitly named:

الشَّمْسَ

al-shamsa

Nūḥ 71:16

The sun

The lamp in this verse therefore refers to the sun.

Why is the sun called a sirāj?

In human experience, a lamp illuminates its surroundings.

When it is lit, people can see things previously hidden in darkness.

The sun is likewise the principal source of daylight in human life.

Its light illuminates the earth and sustains wide-ranging conditions for life.

Calling it a sirāj therefore presents an intelligible image.

The Quran is not saying that the sun is a lamp made with oil and a wick.

It uses the name of a familiar device to express the sun’s illuminating role.

The distinction between moon and sun

The verse contains two expressions:

الْقَمَرَ ... نُورًا

al-qamara ... nūran

Nūḥ 71:16

The moon ... a light

الشَّمْسَ سِرَاجًا

al-shamsa sirājan

Nūḥ 71:16

The sun a lamp

The moon is described with nūr and the sun with sirāj.

This verbal distinction should be preserved.

Nūr denotes light.

Sirāj denotes a lamp or something serving to illuminate.

The two words are therefore not identical.

Their difference alone does not yield every scientific detail about the sources of lunar and solar light.

We now know that the sun produces its radiation through internal physical processes, while the moon’s visible brightness comes mainly from reflected sunlight.

This knowledge is compatible with the different roles of sun and moon in the verse.

Nevertheless, nūr does not itself mean reflected light.

Nor does sirāj invariably mean a body with an independent mechanism of energy production.

Establishing such a scientific distinction requires natural observation and research, not merely different nouns.

Connection with Yunus 10:5

Yunus 10:5 describes the sun with ḍiyāʾ and the moon with nūr.

This is semantically related to Nuh 71:16.

In one verse the sun is a sirāj.

In the other, ḍiyāʾ describes the sun.

Both associate the moon with nūr.

This vocabulary shows the Quran using different expressions for the illumination of heavenly bodies.

Ḍiyāʾ, nūr, and sirāj should not be treated as complete synonyms without examining all their uses.

Nor should absolute physical boundaries be imposed upon them without support in the language.

Sirāj introduces the image of a lamp and illumination.

Ḍiyāʾ and nūr each have their own semantic range and require separate study.

4. Sirājan wahhājan: the blazing lamp in al-Nabaʾ 78:13

وَجَعَلْنَا سِرَاجًا وَهَّاجًا

wa-jaʿalnā sirājan wahhājan

Al-Nabaʾ 78:13

Translation:

“And We made a brilliantly blazing lamp.”

Here sirājan is a noun and wahhājan its adjective.

Wahhāj belongs to W H J.

That family concerns blazing radiance, intense shining, and heat.

Sirāj identifies the lamp, while wahhāj expresses its intense brilliance.

Does the lamp here mean the sun?

Al-Nabaʾ 78:13 does not explicitly name the sun.

Nuh 71:16, however, clearly calls the sun a sirāj.

This supplies a clear connection between the blazing lamp in al-Nabaʾ and the sun.

The context reinforces that reading.

The preceding verses mention earth, mountains, human creation, sleep, night, day, and the heavens.

The following verses concern rain and plant growth.

Sirāj wahhāj thus appears among signs of creation and the conditions supporting life.

Distinguishing sirāj from wahhāj

Their meanings should not be merged.

Sirāj denotes the lamp.

Wahhāj expresses intense blazing brilliance.

Translating only “lamp” would omit the adjective.

Conversely, defining sirāj itself as intense heat obscures wahhāj as a separate modifier.

Together they depict a brilliantly blazing lamp.

The sun gives light, and its radiation and heat affect life on earth.

This is compatible with the Quranic image.

Wahhāj does not specify the sun’s nuclear reactions, temperature, or energy output.

The verse speaks of blazing brilliance; its physical mechanisms belong to natural science.

5. Sirājan wa-qamaran munīran: heavenly illumination in al-Furqan 25:61

تَبَارَكَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ فِي السَّمَاءِ بُرُوجًا وَجَعَلَ فِيهَا سِرَاجًا وَقَمَرًا مُنِيرًا

tabāraka alladhī jaʿala fī al-samāʾi burūjan wa-jaʿala fīhā sirājan wa-qamaran munīran

Al-Furqān 25:61

Translation:

“Blessed is He who placed constellations in the heaven and placed in it a lamp and a luminous moon.”

Sirājan appears beside qamaran munīran.

The verse makes the lamp’s illuminating role apparent.

Nuh 71:16 supplies direct evidence for its referent by explicitly calling the sun a sirāj.

The singular reading therefore refers to the sun.

Why is the moon called munīr?

Munīran belongs to N W R.

It describes the moon as luminous and giving light.

Munīr does not by itself mean a reflector of light.

Another use clarifies this point.

In al-Ahzab 33:46, Muhammad too is described as sirājan munīran.

Munīr is thus not used only for the moon and cannot be an exclusive designation for a body reflecting another’s light.

Its general meaning concerns illumination; how that illumination occurs depends on the subject and context.

The readings sirājan and surujan

Alongside the singular sirājan, al-Furqan 25:61 has the canonical plural reading surujan.

The readings of Hamza, al-Kisaʾi, and Khalaf have the plural.

Suruj is the plural of sirāj, meaning lamps.

The difference matters semantically.

In the singular, Nuh 71:16 supports identifying the lamp with the sun.

The plural can include heavenly lamps such as the shining stars.

This relates to maṣābīḥ for heavenly lights in Fussilat 41:12 and al-Mulk 67:5.

The singular–plural difference is real and should remain visible.

Neither reading removes the basic sense of lamps and illumination.

The plural alone supplies neither an exact number of stars nor a scientific classification of every body referred to.

Does burūj mean the same thing as sirāj?

The verse begins with burūjan.

It then mentions the lamp and moon.

Burūj belongs to B R J and has a separate semantic range.

Their proximity does not make sirāj mean a constellation or station of heavenly bodies.

Each word must be understood in its own grammatical position.

Sirāj expresses the lamp’s illuminating role.

Qamar is the moon.

Munīr describes its luminosity.

Burūj is a separate subject requiring study through its own root and uses.

6. Wa-sirājan munīran: Muhammad as an illuminating lamp

وَدَاعِيًا إِلَى اللَّهِ بِإِذْنِهِ وَسِرَاجًا مُنِيرًا

wa-dāʿiyan ilā Allāhi bi-idhnihi wa-sirājan munīran

Al-Aḥzāb 33:46

Translation:

“And a caller to The God by His permission, and an illuminating lamp.”

Al-Ahzab 33:46

Wa-sirājan appears among descriptions of Muhammad.

In al-Ahzab 33:45, God says He sent him as a witness, a bearer of good news, and a warner.

Al-Ahzab 33:46 adds calling to God and being an illuminating lamp.

Unlike the three heavenly uses, this concerns a human being and his prophetic mission.

How can a lamp describe a person?

A lamp makes it possible to see and recognize the way in darkness.

It is not itself the road.

It illuminates the road and its obstacles for the observer.

Figuratively, someone clarifying truth for others can be compared to a lamp.

Alongside his role of calling to God, Muhammad is here called an illuminating lamp.

This concerns conveying revelation, making truth clear, and guiding his audience.

It does not imply that his body produces light like the sun or has a different physical nature from other humans.

Other verses explicitly affirm his humanity.

In al-Kahf 18:110, he is instructed to say that he is a human being like his audience, receiving revelation.

Sirāj munīr should therefore be understood alongside that statement, within the sphere of messengership and illumination.

Bi-idhnihi: the authority behind the call

The same verse says:

دَاعِيًا إِلَى اللَّهِ بِإِذْنِهِ

dāʿiyan ilā Allāhi bi-idhnihi

Al-Aḥzāb 33:46

A caller to The God by His permission

Muhammad calls to God, and that calling is by His permission.

Bi-idhnihi directly qualifies the calling to God, specifying the authority for the mission; the illuminating-lamp description appears within that same mission context.

Muhammad does not call people to himself as an independent deity; he calls them to God.

His role as a lamp belongs within this framework.

The mission’s illumination concerns revelation and guidance given to him by God.

Sirāj munīr should not become a claim of independent control over hearts, knowledge of all the unseen, or divinity.

Conversely, God’s being the source of revelation does not negate Muhammad’s real role in conveying, explaining, and clarifying it.

The Quran presents him as a real addressee, witness, bearer of good news, warner, and caller.

Connection with Ibrahim 14:1

Ibrahim 14:1 presents the Book sent down to Muhammad so that he may bring people from darkness into light by their Lord’s permission.

This has a clear connection with al-Ahzab 33:46.

Ibrahim concerns the Book and the mission of bringing people from darkness into light.

Al-Ahzab describes Muhammad as a caller and illuminating lamp.

The image can thus be understood through the relationship among revelation, explanation, and guidance.

Sirāj does not lexically mean the Quran or the Book itself.

The lamp supplies the image of illumination; the Book, revelation, and prophetic mission are understood through the relevant verses.

Is the Prophet an independent source of light like the sun?

Nuh 71:16 calls the sun a sirāj.

Al-Ahzab 33:46 calls Muhammad a sirāj munīr.

Sharing a description does not make the subjects identical in nature.

In creation passages, the sun is a heavenly body giving light and radiation.

In al-Ahzab, Muhammad is a human sent by God to witness, bring good news, warn, and call.

Illumination is the image’s common feature.

Its kind differs.

The sun concerns physical illumination.

The prophetic mission concerns clarification and human guidance.

Nor does munīr establish the Prophet as an independent source of all truth and guidance apart from revelation and God’s permission.

That claim would conflict with bi-idhnihi and other verses about his mission.

Does an illuminating lamp compel people to accept guidance?

A lamp gives light; seeing and using it is a further matter.

In the verse’s figurative use, the Prophet explains truth and illuminates the way.

This does not mean he can make every person believe against their will.

Al-Qasas 28:56 denies his independent control over the outcome of guidance: God guides whom He wills. Yunus 10:99 separately questions compelling people to become believers. These are distinct points and should not both be inferred from the word sirāj alone.

The mission to illuminate and call is therefore different from independent ownership of guidance’s outcome.

This neither diminishes the mission’s value nor removes the Prophet’s real role.

It keeps that role within the bounds expressed in the Quran.

In al-Ahzab 33:46, sirāj munīr figuratively describes Muhammad’s illuminating role in conveying guidance and calling to God; it neither defines his physical nature nor grants him independent divine authority.

7. Distinctions from related roots

S R J (sirāj) and Ṣ B Ḥ (miṣbāḥ)

Miṣbāḥ denotes a lamp or lighting device in Arabic.

Sirāj belongs to the same semantic field.

Arabic usage directly explains sirāj through miṣbāḥ.

Al-Nur 24:35 places miṣbāḥ in an image of a lamp, glass, and oil.

That verse helps illustrate oil lamps and their illuminating use.

Miṣbāḥ belongs to Ṣ B Ḥ, whereas sirāj belongs to S R J.

Similar usage does not establish identical etymological histories.

Nor should every feature of the lamp in al-Nur 24:35 be transferred to the sun or the Prophet in the sirāj verses.

S R J (sirāj) and N W R (nūr)

Nūr denotes light.

Sirāj denotes a lamp or that which illuminates.

Nuh 71:16 describes the moon as nūr and the sun as sirāj.

In al-Ahzab 33:46, munīr qualifies sirāj.

The families are related within the field of illumination.

Nūr expresses light itself, while sirāj primarily names a lamp.

This does not make all Quranic nūr reflected light, or every sirāj something burning a particular material.

Each subject and context requires separate examination.

S R J (sirāj) and Ḍ W ʾ (ḍiyāʾ)

Ḍiyāʾ concerns light and radiance.

Yunus 10:5 describes the sun with ḍiyāʾ.

Nuh 71:16 calls it a sirāj.

Both thus refer to the same subject.

Their form and linguistic function nevertheless differ.

Ḍiyāʾ expresses brightness and radiance.

Sirāj introduces the image of a lamp giving light.

This semantic tendency should be preserved without turning it into an absolute physical classification.

S R J (sirāj) and W H J (wahaj)

Wahaj concerns blazing brilliance and heat.

Sirāj denotes a lamp.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:13 combines them.

Sirājan identifies what is being described.

Wahhājan expresses its intense brilliance.

The adjective’s full meaning should not be absorbed into sirāj itself.

S R J (sirāj) and Z Y N (zīna)

Zīna concerns beauty and adornment.

A lamp’s light can enhance the beauty of a place.

One lexical analysis proposes beauty as a common feature of some uses of S R J.

Beauty alone does not exhaust the meaning of a lamp.

A lamp may adorn a place, but its familiar function is illumination.

Quranic sirāj should therefore not be reduced to “adornment” or “something beautiful.”

S R J (sirāj) and S J R (sajr)

Their letters are similar but differently ordered.

S R J has rāʾ in the second position.

S J R has jīm in the second position.

Quranic sirāj concerns lamps and illumination.

Quranic S J R concerns filling or kindling.

A lamp may be lit with fire, but this practical overlap does not make the roots identical.

Sujjirat in al-Takwir 81:6 belongs to S J R and should not be confused with sirāj.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Across the four Quranic uses, sirāj retains the lexical sense of a lamp and illumination.

Its referent differs between heavenly descriptions and the Prophet.

The same equivalent, “lamp,” can be used in the three solar verses; differences in their accompanying descriptions should be conveyed separately.

The key distinctions are physical versus figurative usage and sirāj itself versus munīr and wahhāj.

Principal meanings in the family

Sirāj: a lamp; an illuminating device.

Suruj: lamps, the plural of sirāj.

Sarj: a horse’s or riding animal’s saddle.

Asraja al-sirāja: he lit the lamp.

Asraja al-farasa: he saddled the horse.

Misraja: an oil-lamp vessel or device.

Sarrāj: a saddle-maker or saddle-seller, as an occupation.

Musarraj: adorned or made beautiful in some lexical uses.

Translating the Quranic uses

In al-Furqan 25:61, Nuh 71:16, and al-Nabaʾ 78:13, sirāj can be translated “lamp.”

The sun is its evident referent in these three verses, although the plural surujan in al-Furqan 25:61 also allows reference to heavenly lamps.

In al-Nabaʾ 78:13, wahhājan should be conveyed separately as “brilliantly blazing.”

In al-Ahzab 33:46:

وَسِرَاجًا مُنِيرًا

wa-sirājan munīran

Al-Aḥzāb 33:46

“And an illuminating lamp.”

This preserves the lamp image and communicates the Prophet’s illuminating role within his mission.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meaning

For a brief root box:

“A lamp or illuminating device; figuratively, an illuminator and guide.”

For al-Furqan 25:61:

“A heavenly lamp; in the singular reading, the sun illuminating heaven and earth.”

For Nuh 71:16:

“The sun, presented in the verse as an illuminating lamp.”

For al-Nabaʾ 78:13:

“The sun depicted as a brilliantly blazing lamp; its intense brilliance is expressed by wahhāj.”

For al-Ahzab 33:46:

“A figurative description of Muhammad illuminating the way to God through calling and conveying revelation.”

The exact forms sirājan and wa-sirājan should be preserved in the boxes.

The brief root meaning must remain distinct from the meaning in the particular verse.

9. Conclusion

S R J has several established Arabic uses, chiefly lamps, saddles, and certain expressions of beauty and adornment.

The pre-Islamic poetry of Salama ibn Jandal uses sarj for a horse saddle.

In poetry attributed to al-Nabigha al-Jaʿdi, sirāj denotes an oil lamp whose light forms an image of brightness.

These examples distinguish saddle from lamp, although the second example’s precise date and attribution are not wholly certain.

Arabic records asraja both for lighting a lamp and saddling an animal.

Other uses relate the family to beauty and adornment.

These meanings should not be made into an invented common historical origin.

For sirāj, “lamp,” a historical relationship with Middle Iranian words related to cherāgh and similar Aramaic forms has been proposed.

Its history may therefore be more complex than its three-letter classification in Arabic lexicons.

S R J occurs four times in the Quran.

Nuh 71:16 explicitly calls the sun a sirāj.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:13 speaks of a sirāj wahhāj, a brilliantly blazing lamp.

Al-Furqan 25:61 places sirāj beside the luminous moon. The alternative plural surujan also encompasses heavenly lamps.

Al-Ahzab 33:46 calls Muhammad a sirāj munīr.

This last use extends physical illumination into illumination through the prophetic mission.

Muhammad is presented as an illuminating lamp calling people to God.

The description should not be turned into physical light, divinity, or independent control over human guidance.

Bi-idhnihi directly qualifies the call and identifies the mission’s authority.

Nūr, ḍiyāʾ, munīr, wahhāj, and sirāj must also remain distinct.

They belong to the field of illumination but are not identical in meaning.

Their differences reveal distinct images and roles; the physical mechanisms of solar and lunar light and the nature of prophetic illumination require their own evidence.

In Quranic usage, S R J denotes a lamp and that which gives light to its surroundings. Applied to the sun, it concerns physical illumination; applied to Muhammad, it concerns clarification and guidance within his prophetic mission.

For Forqan, “lamp” in the heavenly passages and “illuminating lamp” in al-Ahzab 33:46 are natural, defensible renderings.

The central distinction is that the shared description sirāj indicates a shared illuminating role, not identical nature, source of illumination, or mode of existence.
