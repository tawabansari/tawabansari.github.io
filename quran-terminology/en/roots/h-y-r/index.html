Bewilderment, inconclusive movement back and forth, and the inability to find a clear way

The Arabic root Ḥ Y R concerns bewilderment, movement back and forth, and the inability to find a clear route or decision. When people lose their way and do not know which direction to take, or become so entangled in a question that they cannot discern the right course, the word ḥayrah describes their condition.

This root does not concern human thought and decision alone. In early Arabic, ḥāʾir also named a place where water collects without finding an outlet. The water remains within an area and moves about, but its flow does not continue along a definite course.

Both uses involve a failure to reach a clear course. Water circulates within a bounded space; bewildered people likewise fail to find their way despite their attention, searching, or movement.

The Qur’an uses this root only in the form ḥayrāna, in Al-Anʿām 6:71. There, a person has become bewildered through the pull of devils, while companions call that person toward guidance.

To understand this use, three situations should be distinguished: not knowing the truth and searching for it; becoming bewildered under competing influences and calls; and knowingly turning away after recognizing the right way. These situations may overlap in a person’s life, but they are not equivalent in knowledge or moral responsibility.

1. Forms of the root and its semantic core

The root Ḥ Y R occurs only once in the Qur’an, in a single form: ḥayrāna in Al-Anʿām 6:71.

Ḥayrān is an adjective on the pattern faʿlān. Its feminine form in Arabic is ḥayrā, and a recognized plural is ḥayārā.

Other important forms of this family in Arabic include:

  • حَارَ ـ يَحَارُ (ḥāra–yaḥāru): to become bewildered, failing to find a clear route or decision.
  • حَيْرَة (ḥayrah): bewilderment and the inability to discern a route or decision.
  • حَيْر and حَيَر (ḥayr and ḥayar): verbal nouns associated with bewilderment and perplexity.
  • حَائِر (ḥāʾir): a bewildered person or someone without a clear direction or decision.
  • حَيْرَان (ḥayrān): a bewildered or perplexed person.
  • حَيْرَى (ḥayrā): the feminine form of ḥayrān.
  • حَيَارَى (ḥayārā): a plural denoting bewildered people.
  • حَيَّرَ (ḥayyara): to bewilder or perplex someone.
  • تَحَيَّرَ (taḥayyara): to become bewildered, be at a loss over an issue, or fail to find the way.
  • اِسْتَحَارَ (istaḥāra): in some uses, to become bewildered; in others, concerning water or accumulated things, to collect or become full.
  • مُتَحَيِّر (mutaḥayyir): someone in a state of bewilderment or perplexity.
  • حَائِر (ḥāʾir): in a spatial use, a pool or hollow where water collects and does not readily escape.

These forms are recorded in Arabic lexicons, but they should not all be presented as Qur’anic derivatives. The only Qur’anic form of this root is ḥayrāna.

The semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies the central meaning as «التَّرَدُّدُ فِي الشَّيْءِ» (al-taraddudu fī al-shayʾ): movement back and forth within something.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: movement back and forth and a pool

Here, taraddud does not mean only doubt about a proposition. Its broader sense is movement back and forth that has not yet reached a definite direction, result, or settled position.

People who lose their way may move about repeatedly, try different paths, and still fail to find the right route. In an intellectual question, they may likewise move among several possibilities without reaching a conclusion.

This differs from asking a question or not knowing an answer. People may lack knowledge of something yet know how to investigate it. Their lack of knowledge is then not necessarily bewilderment.

Bewilderment becomes prominent when a person cannot discern a clear path toward an answer, decision, or destination.

Taken together, the evidence supports this semantic core for Ḥ Y R: being in a state of inconclusive movement back and forth or bewilderment, unable to find a clear route, direction, or decision.

This condition may be spatial, mental, or practical. Yet all these dimensions should not be imported equally into every use: losing one’s way and being perplexed by an intellectual issue are different instances within the same semantic field.

2. The physical image and early Arabic usage

A pool in which water remains without an outlet

One tangible use of Ḥ Y R in Arabic concerns water and the place where it collects.

Ḥāʾir named a pool into which rainwater or surrounding streams gathered. Some lexical explanations describe it as a hollow with a low center and raised edges.

Water reaching this hollow may collect there, turn about, and lack a clear outlet.

In the expression «تَحَيَّرَ الْمَاءُ» (taḥayyara al-māʾ), water has collected or remained within an area.

This image shows that the root does not concern only ignorance or doubt. In its physical use, water has no awareness or decision to make; nevertheless, the same word family describes its movement and remaining in place.

What matters here is the water’s movement back and forth or its restricted flow, rather than mental incapacity.

It cannot be asserted with certainty that the psychological sense of human bewilderment necessarily arose from the name of a pool. Both uses may draw on the recognized idea of movement back and forth without reaching a clear course. The evidence establishes a semantic connection, rather than a certain historical order of development.

A witness from the poetry of Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm

Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm, a pre-Islamic poet of Aws, describes a beautiful woman in the following verse:

تَخْطُو عَلَى بَرْدِيَّتَيْنِ غَذَاهُمَا
غَدِقٌ بِسَاحَةِ حَائِرٍ يَعْبُوبِ

takhṭū ʿalā bardiyyatayni ghadhāhumā / ghadiqun bi-sāḥati ḥāʾirin yaʿbūbi

A translation close to the sense:

“She steps upon two legs like two papyrus stems, nurtured by abundant water beside a brimming pool.”

In the early commentary on this verse, bardiyyatayn is understood as an image for the woman’s two legs: like two stems of papyrus, an aquatic plant, in their whiteness, smoothness, and proportion. This explains the commentators’ reading of the poet’s image.

Ibn al-Anbārī, Sharḥ al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt: the Qays witness and papyrus

Here, ḥāʾirin yaʿbūbi refers to a pool full of water.

This witness matters because ḥāʾir does not name a bewildered person. It concerns a place in which water gathers.

The spatial branch of the root thus appears in pre-Islamic Arabic poetry. It cannot be treated merely as an exegetical meaning invented after the Qur’an to explain ḥayrān.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: attribution to Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm and comparison with his Dīwān

At the same time, the verse does not show that every pool necessarily bewilders a person. Here, ḥāʾir names or describes a place, rather than reporting the psychological condition of someone looking at it.

Water-filled ground and arrested flow

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah reads:

حَتَّى تَحَيَّرَتِ الدِّبَارُ كَأَنَّهَا
زُلَفٌ وَأُلْقِيَ قِتْبُهَا الْمَحْزُومُ

ḥattā taḥayyarati al-dibāru ka-annahā / zulafun wa-ulqiya qitbuhā al-maḥzūmu

A translation close to the sense:

“Until the cultivated plots filled with water, as though they were reservoirs; and the water-drawing camel’s firmly fastened saddle was taken off after its work was done.”

Here, taḥayyarati al-dibāru concerns agricultural plots that have filled with water.

In the lexical explanation, dibār refers to cultivated plots, while zulaf denotes basins or places where water collects. Ibn al-Anbārī explains the verse as describing a water-drawing camel irrigating a crop; he also explains ulqiya qitbuhā as the removal of its saddle after the work is finished.

Ibn al-Anbārī, Al-Zāhir: irrigation and removing the saddle after work

Here, taḥayyur does not mean that the land loses its way or entertains doubts. It concerns water accumulating and remaining there.

This use shows how movement back and forth and gathering can extend, within the physical branch of the root, to becoming full and accumulating.

Labīd was a mukhaḍram poet, one who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Since the precise date of this verse is not established, it cannot confidently be presented as a witness predating the Qur’an. It is, however, recorded in the lexicons as evidence of early usage.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Labīd’s witness for water gathering

Bewildered vision

In Arabic, «حَارَ بَصَرُهُ» (ḥāra baṣaruhu) describes a person looking at something whose gaze then becomes disturbed or dimmed, preventing clear sight of it.

This sometimes happens before something intensely bright, astonishing, or beyond expectation.

In this use, bewilderment is connected with a failure of visual discrimination. People see, yet their seeing does not produce a clear recognition of what is before them.

This resembles intellectual bewilderment in structure. In both situations, a means of knowing or discerning fails to reach a clear result.

Yet this resemblance does not show that all intellectual bewilderment arises from weak eyesight, or that seeing must be the root’s original meaning.

Bewilderment in decision and conduct

The lexicons record «حَارَ فِي أَمْرِهِ» (ḥāra fī amrihi) for someone who is bewildered about an undertaking and does not know how to decide.

«رَجُلٌ حَائِرٌ» (rajulun ḥāʾirun) likewise describes someone without a clear direction.

In the expression «حَائِرٌ بَائِرٌ» (ḥāʾirun bāʾirun), two descriptions stand together. Ḥāʾir conveys bewilderment, while bāʾir concerns ruin or failure.

Their combination does not mean that ḥāʾir by itself always denotes someone ruined or unsuccessful. Bewilderment may lead to failure, but bewilderment and destruction are distinct concepts.

In Arabic usage, people may become bewildered and subsequently find their way. Bewilderment is therefore not necessarily permanent or irreversible.

Other uses

In classical Arabic, mustaḥīr also describes something that has become very full or has remained in one place for a time.

For example, a rain-laden cloud remaining over one area and continuing to shed rain may receive this description.

Under Ḥ Y R, Lisān al-ʿArab also records maḥārah for a shell and cup-like bodily hollows. Yet lexicons do not classify it uniformly: the Cairo Arabic Language Academy’s Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ refers its maḥārah entry under Ḥ Y R to Ḥ W R. These forms should therefore not be presented, without noting this difference, as certain derivatives of Ḥ Y R or established extensions of mental bewilderment.

Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ: referring maḥārah from Ḥ Y R to Ḥ W R

Al-Ḥīrah was also the name of a historical city near Kufa. This place-name does not mean that every use of ḥayrah refers to that city, or that the city’s name necessarily derives from intellectual bewilderment.

3. The Qur’anic use: a person who has lost the way

قُلْ أَنَدْعُو مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ مَا لَا يَنْفَعُنَا وَلَا يَضُرُّنَا وَنُرَدُّ عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِنَا بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَانَا اللَّهُ كَالَّذِي اسْتَهْوَتْهُ الشَّيَاطِينُ فِي الْأَرْضِ حَيْرَانَ لَهُ أَصْحَابٌ يَدْعُونَهُ إِلَى الْهُدَى ائْتِنَا قُلْ إِنَّ هُدَى اللَّهِ هُوَ الْهُدَىٰ وَأُمِرْنَا لِنُسْلِمَ لِرَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

qul a-nadʿū min dūni allāhi mā lā yanfaʿunā wa-lā yaḍurrunā wa-nuraddu ʿalā aʿqābinā baʿda idh hadānā allāhu ka-alladhī istahwat-hu al-shayāṭīnu fī al-arḍi ḥayrāna lahu aṣḥābun yadʿūnahu ilā al-hudā iʾtinā qul inna hudā allāhi huwa al-hudā wa-umirnā li-nuslima li-rabbi al-ʿālamīna

Proposed translation for this study:

“Say: Shall we invoke besides The God something that neither benefits nor harms us, and be turned back on our heels after The God has guided us? Like someone whom devils have drawn after them through the land, bewildered, while companions call that person toward guidance, saying: Come to us. Say: The God’s guidance is indeed the guidance, and we have been commanded to submit to the Lord of the worlds.”

Al-Anʿām 6:71

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of Ḥ Y R.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence, Al-Anʿām 6:71, word 23

The verse opens with a question: after divine guidance, should we turn again toward something that does not hold our real benefit or harm in its power?

To explain such a situation, it then presents an image of someone whom devils have drawn after them through the land, leaving that person bewildered.

This image is not confined to losing one’s way in a desert. Within the verse, spatial bewilderment illustrates a situation concerning guidance and the choice of a path.

Ḥayrāna: the person’s condition after being drawn away

In «كَالَّذِي اسْتَهْوَتْهُ الشَّيَاطِينُ فِي الْأَرْضِ حَيْرَانَ» (ka-alladhī istahwat-hu al-shayāṭīnu fī al-arḍi ḥayrāna), the verb istahwat-hu and the description ḥayrāna come from different roots.

Istahwat-hu belongs to H W Y and in this construction concerns drawing someone away or leading someone after oneself.

Ḥayrān, however, belongs to Ḥ Y R and expresses the person’s condition: bewildered and unable to find a clear way.

The two words should therefore not both be rendered simply as “they led him astray.” One describes the act of drawing away, and the other describes the resulting condition of the person.

Ḥayrān does not itself mean possessed by devils or under their control. The relationship to the devils is supplied by the preceding verb and its subject.

In this verse, devils are the agents drawing the person away; bewilderment is the condition of the person caught in that pull.

Why does the verse mention the land?

Fī al-arḍi, “in the land,” establishes the setting of the occurrence.

In the verse’s immediate image, the person is bewildered in the land. This fits the example of someone who has lost the way in a region and can no longer discern a clear route.

In early Arabic, losing one’s way across broad stretches of land and in deserts was a familiar image of bewilderment.

Nevertheless, the verse does not use the word “desert.” A desert may provide a useful example for understanding the image, but should not replace al-arḍ in the direct translation.

Nor does the verse specify the kind of terrain, the distance from the person’s companions, or the precise pattern of movement.

What is clear is the person’s bewilderment in relation to the way of guidance.

Lahu aṣḥābun: the person is not alone

The Qur’an continues:

«لَهُ أَصْحَابٌ يَدْعُونَهُ إِلَى الْهُدَى ائْتِنَا» — “That person has companions calling toward guidance: Come to us.” (Lahu aṣḥābun yadʿūnahu ilā al-hudā iʾtinā.)

This part is essential to understanding the image.

The bewildered person has companions who call toward guidance, saying: Come to us.

The image therefore does not concern merely someone receiving no call to return to the right way. While the person is caught in the pull of the devils, another call is present, inviting that person toward guidance.

This distinction matters, since two situations can be envisaged.

In the first, someone does not know the way and has no available sign or guide for finding it.

In the second, someone is bewildered but also receives a call toward the right way.

The verse in Al-Anʿām depicts the second situation.

Nevertheless, ḥayrān alone does not establish that the person has fully recognized the entire truth and then knowingly rejected it. Such an intention requires additional evidence.

The presence of a call toward guidance and a person’s full, conscious recognition of the truth are different matters.

Does bewilderment mean that two paths are equally valid?

No.

People may become bewildered between two calls, but their bewilderment does not establish that both calls are equally true or authoritative.

In the verse itself, one call is associated with devils, while the other is identified as al-hudā, guidance.

The verse then states the criterion clearly:

«قُلْ إِنَّ هُدَى اللَّهِ هُوَ الْهُدَىٰ» — “Say: The God’s guidance is indeed the guidance.” (Qul inna hudā allāhi huwa al-hudā.)

The verse does not assert the equality of two paths or the absence of truth. It describes someone who, despite the presence of guidance, has encountered an influence that has caused bewilderment.

This distinction matters for understanding Ḥ Y R. Bewilderment is a person’s condition in relation to paths, rather than a judgment that all those paths are correct.

Is a bewildered person necessarily a knowing disbeliever?

The word ḥayrān alone does not convey that meaning.

In Al-Anʿām 6:71, the context concerns turning back after guidance and invoking something besides The God. Bewilderment thus appears here within a setting of belief and moral choice.

Yet bewilderment must be distinguished from conscious disbelief.

One person may become bewildered through lack of knowledge or deception. Another may recognize the way but turn away because of interests, fear, or attachments. Someone else may return and make amends after recognizing a mistake.

These situations are not equivalent in moral responsibility.

The Qur’an distinguishes ignorance, stumbling, deception, conscious concealment, and rejection after the truth has become clear. These matters can, for example, be examined in their different settings in Al-Baqarah 2:36, Al-Baqarah 2:37, Al-Baqarah 2:159, and Al-Baqarah 2:209.

Being bewildered should therefore not become a conclusive moral label.

In Al-Anʿām 6:71, the central issue is following, after guidance, a call that takes a person away from a clear course.

Does the image describe a particular historical event?

Some early commentaries explain the image through a story of a traveler in the desert. Misleading beings draw the traveler after them, while companions call from another direction toward the way.

This explanation can show how earlier commentators understood the image, but its narrative details should not be added to the Qur’anic text.

The verse does not name the person, the location, the date, or the precise appearance of the devils.

Nor is it necessary to establish the historical reality of legendary beings or desert stories in order to understand ḥayrān.

The verse’s own construction is sufficient: someone is bewildered in the land, an influence has drawn that person from the way, and companions call toward guidance.

This image serves the verse’s central question about turning back after guidance.

4. Distinguishing bewilderment from related words

Ḥ Y R (ḥayrah) and T Y H (tīh): bewilderment and losing one’s way in the land

The Arabic root T Y H concerns wandering and losing one’s way.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:26, «يَتِيهُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ» (yatīhūna fī al-arḍi) describes a people wandering in the land.

This clearly overlaps with ḥayrāna in Al-Anʿām 6:71.

Yet in the Qur’anic use of T Y H, wandering movement through the land is prominent, whereas ḥayrān describes the condition of someone unable to discern a clear way.

Put simply, tīh in the verse in Al-Māʾidah shows movement through the land, while ḥayrān in Al-Anʿām describes the condition of a person who has become bewildered after being drawn away.

This comparison should not become an absolute rule that tīh is always purely bodily and bewilderment always purely mental. The lexicons record broader uses for both families.

The distinction follows from their particular uses in these two verses.

For a broader examination of tīh, see Forqan’s separate study of the root T Y H.

Study of T Y H

Ḥ Y R (ḥayrah) and Ḍ L L (ḍalāl): bewilderment and losing the way

In Qur’anic language, ḍalāl has a broad field of meaning that can include losing the way, departing from the right course, or being in a state of misguidance.

Bewilderment is related to this meaning, but it is not exactly the same concept.

People may have lost the right way yet continue along another with misplaced confidence. They are not necessarily bewildered at every moment.

Conversely, someone may be bewildered about the right way because it is still unclear which route to choose.

Misguidance and bewilderment can therefore accompany one another, but they do not always describe the same condition.

In Al-Anʿām 6:71, the person’s bewilderment appears alongside a call toward guidance. Here, bewilderment belongs within the semantic network of guidance and misguidance; nevertheless, ḥayrān itself does not mean disbeliever or denier of truth.

Study of Ḍ L L

Ḥ Y R (ḥayrah) and Sh K K (shakk): bewilderment and doubt about a proposition

Shakk concerns a lack of certainty about whether something is true or false.

Bewilderment may be broader, leaving a person at a loss over a course of action or decision.

For instance, people may doubt whether a historical report is true or false. They can consult sources to investigate it and have a clear route for their inquiry.

Such people are not necessarily bewildered.

Conversely, someone may be unable to discern the right direction among several practical routes and become bewildered, despite certainty about some surrounding facts.

Doubt may therefore contribute to bewilderment, but the two are not complete synonyms.

Morally, asking questions and examining a claim should not in themselves be counted as misguidance. The Qur’an calls people to listen, reflect, weigh matters, and avoid following without knowledge, as can be seen in Al-Isrāʾ 17:36 and Al-Zumar 39:18.

The search for truth should therefore not be equated with the censured bewilderment of Al-Anʿām 6:71 merely because it involves questions or temporary uncertainty.

Ḥ Y R (ḥayrah) and R Y B (rayb): bewilderment and doubt accompanied by unease

In many Qur’anic uses, rayb concerns doubt or suspicion about the truth of something.

Ḥ Y R places greater emphasis on the condition of bewilderment and failure to reach a clear route or decision.

People may experience rayb about something and become bewildered as a result. That relationship, however, is not necessary in every instance.

In Al-Anʿām 6:71, the central description concerns someone who has become bewildered through a misleading pull. The verse uses ḥayrān to describe that person, rather than murīb or murtāb.

Rendering it “skeptical” or “suspicious” without attending to the verse’s full image therefore restricts its basic meaning.

Study of R Y B

Ḥ Y R (ḥayrah) and Ḥ Y D (ḥayd): bewilderment and turning aside

Ḥ Y D concerns turning aside or changing course in relation to something.

In Qāf 50:19, taḥīdu describes a person’s avoidance of an encounter with death.

Ḥ Y R, however, concerns bewilderment and the inability to find a clear way.

People may knowingly and deliberately turn aside from something. Their change of direction then does not amount to bewilderment.

Conversely, someone may become bewildered without yet having moved from the current position.

Turning aside and being bewildered are thus different concepts, although they may occur together in some situations.

Study of Ḥ Y D

Ḥ Y R (ḥayrah) and Ḥ W R (ḥawr): movement back and forth and returning

One recognized branch of Ḥ W R concerns returning and movement back and forth.

Movement back and forth is also present in the family Ḥ Y R.

This semantic proximity deserves attention, but the two roots do not have identical radicals.

In Ḥ W R, some derivatives describe a return or turning that reaches a clear result, such as yaḥūra in Al-Inshiqāq 84:14, which concerns returning.

Ḥayrān in Al-Anʿām 6:71, however, describes someone unable to find a clear way.

The presence of movement back and forth in both families does not justify treating them as one root.

Study of Ḥ W R

5. Bewilderment, inquiry, and later uses

In present-day Persian and Arabic, bewilderment can sometimes have a positive sense.

For example, someone may be struck with wonder before the grandeur of nature, the complexity of creation, or a new discovery, and say: I was left bewildered.

In mystical literature, bewilderment has also sometimes described a condition in which people confront the greatness of truth or their inability to comprehend it fully.

These are recognized uses in language and literature, but they should not be transferred without evidence into the meaning of ḥayrān in Al-Anʿām 6:71.

In that verse, a person becomes bewildered through a misleading influence. This differs from admiration of beauty, scientific astonishment, or humility before what remains unknown.

Conversely, bewilderment before a difficult question is not inherently sinful.

People may sincerely acknowledge not knowing an answer and work to understand it. In such a situation, not knowing and asking questions can begin a path toward knowledge.

What stands out in Al-Anʿām 6:71 is bewilderment arising from following an influence that takes people away from guidance, rather than the condemnation of every question, doubt, or lack of knowledge.

This distinction matters in an ethical examination of the verse. Nevertheless, the lexical meaning of Ḥ Y R expresses the state of bewilderment itself. Its cause, a person’s responsibility, and the possibility of returning to the right way are understood from the context and other verses.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For general uses of Ḥ Y R, suitable equivalents include bewilderment, perplexity, losing one’s way, and being at a loss over a decision.

Describing a person, ḥāʾir and ḥayrān can be rendered bewildered or perplexed. In present-day Persian, however, ḥayrān sometimes means only astonished, which may not clearly convey the verse’s basic meaning to a reader.

In Al-Anʿām 6:71, ḥayrāna appears in a sentence describing being drawn away by devils and being called toward guidance by companions.

In this setting, “bewildered” is the most suitable basic equivalent.

A proposed rendering of «كَالَّذِي اسْتَهْوَتْهُ الشَّيَاطِينُ فِي الْأَرْضِ حَيْرَانَ» (ka-alladhī istahwat-hu al-shayāṭīnu fī al-arḍi ḥayrāna) is:

“Like someone whom devils have drawn after them through the land, while that person is bewildered.”

This rendering keeps the meanings of istahwat-hu and ḥayrān distinct.

“Bewildered and perplexed” can also be used in explanatory prose, but a direct translation needs only one clear equivalent.

“Skeptical” is unsuitable because it restricts bewilderment to theoretical doubt.

“Misguided” likewise does not convey the full meaning: it can express departure from the way without making the person’s bewilderment clear.

“Mad” or “unreasoning” are also incorrect renderings, since those meanings do not follow from ḥayrān.

For physical uses, such as ḥāʾir for a pool, “a place where water collects” or “pool” is suitable. “A bewildered place” would be unnatural.

Forqan’s translation should therefore distinguish human bewilderment, indecision, and the gathering of water without disregarding their semantic connection.

7. Conclusion

In Arabic, Ḥ Y R concerns movement back and forth, bewilderment, and the failure to reach a clear direction or decision. In physical uses, this field of meaning appears in a pool where water gathers without a definite outlet. In human uses, a bewildered person cannot find a clear route or decision.

The Qur’an uses this root only in ḥayrāna, in Al-Anʿām 6:71. The word describes someone whom devils have drawn after them through the land, while companions call that person toward guidance.

Ḥayrān itself does not mean disbelief, knowing denial, demonic possession, or theoretical doubt. It expresses the person’s bewilderment, whose cause and direction the context makes clear.

Sincere inquiry and the effort to recognize truth should likewise not be equated with bewilderment arising from following a misleading influence.

Final definition: Ḥ Y R denotes movement back and forth and bewilderment accompanied by the inability to find a clear route, direction, or decision. In Arabic, this meaning appears both in the movement and gathering of water and in people’s mental and practical condition. Ḥayrāna in Al-Anʿām 6:71 describes someone who has become bewildered in relation to guidance through a misleading pull. Brief root meaning: Movement back and forth and bewilderment; failure to find a clear route, direction, or decision; in physical use, water gathering and remaining in place.
