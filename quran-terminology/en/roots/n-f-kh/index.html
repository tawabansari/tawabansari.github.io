The meaning of nafkh in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from blowing on fire and the appearance of life to blowing into the ṣūr and the events of resurrection

The three-consonant root ن ف خ in Arabic is best known for blowing: expelling air from the mouth or directing a current of air toward or into something. A person may blow to kindle a fire, sound a wind instrument or inflate a container. Each use has a different result: fire flares up, sound is produced or a container fills with air.

Other branches of this family include intifākh for inflation or swelling, minfākh for a blowing instrument, and nafkhah for one act of blowing. Figurative uses extend to corpulence, prominence, pride and certain conditions of growth and increase.

In the Qur’an, the root appears in several settings: blowing on fire while Dhū al-Qarnayn builds a barrier, Jesus blowing into a birdlike form made of clay, the expression of blowing from the spirit in human creation and the story of Mary, and blowing into the ṣūr during the events of resurrection.

These uses differ in outcome and setting. In one, blowing helps kindle fire; in another, it accompanies a sign of divine power; in resurrection scenes, it is associated with fear, ṣaʿq, gathering and the rising of the dead.

Understanding this root requires distinguishing the meaning of blowing, what is blown into, the agent and the result stated by the verse. Especially where the action is attributed to The God, the bodily image of human blowing must not, without evidence, be treated as a description of how the divine action occurs.

1. Forms and Semantic Range

Important forms in Arabic include:

  • نَفَخَ، يَنْفُخُ: Blew; blows.
  • نَفْخ: Blowing; directing air.
  • نَفْخَة: One act of blowing; in other uses, inflation or swelling.
  • نَفَخَات: Plural of nafkhah, according to context.
  • نَافِخ: A blower; someone who blows.
  • نَفِيخ: In some early uses, a person responsible for blowing on a fire.
  • مِنْفَاخ: A blowing instrument, such as a smith’s bellows.
  • نَفَّخَ: In some uses, blew repeatedly or forcefully.
  • اِنْتَفَخَ: Became inflated, protruded or swelled.
  • مُنْتَفِخ و مَنْفُوخ: Inflated, swollen or corpulent.
  • نُفَّاخ و نُفَّاخَة: Words associated with swelling, tumours and inflated things.
  • نَفْخَاء: In some Arabic uses, ground rising above its surroundings.

The root occurs twenty times in the Qur’an: nineteen instances of the basic verb and one of the noun نَفْخَة. The verbal forms include perfect, imperfect and imperative, in active and passive constructions.

All these occurrences belong to the branch of blowing. Forms such as انتفخ, منفاخ and نافخ, meaning a blower, do not occur in the Qur’anic text, although they are known in Arabic.

Core Meaning: Blowing and Moving Air

In direct human use, nafkh means expelling air from the mouth.

For example:

نَفَخَ فِي النَّارِ

nafakha fī al-nāri

Translation: “He blew on the fire.”

And:

نَفَخَ فِي الْبُوقِ

nafakha fī al-būqi

Translation: “He blew into the trumpet.”

In the first expression, a current of air helps the fire flare up. In the second, air enters an instrument that can produce sound.

Both involve blowing, but neither flaring up nor producing sound is an essential part of the independent meaning of nafkh. Those results depend on the instrument and circumstances.

A person may also blow into something and fill it with air. Here, the result is an increase in volume or a bulging shape.

This connection makes the branch of intifākh significant within the family.

Intifākh: Inflation and Swelling

One lexical analysis proposes a core meaning involving انتفاخ و علو: inflation and rising.

This analysis draws on several uses in the family. A leather container inflates as air enters it, a belly may swell with gas, and certain areas of ground are described with words from the same family because they rise above their surroundings.

Arabic also records انتفخ النهار for the advancing day, after part of the morning has passed.

The expression نفخة الربيع refers to the flourishing growth of spring vegetation, as though the ground becomes full and raised with plants.

These uses show that N F KH is not confined to expelling air from the mouth.

Nevertheless, not every Qur’anic occurrence of the verb means “inflate” or “raise.” In the story of Dhū al-Qarnayn, for example, the clear meaning is blowing, rather than swelling the iron.

Nor can we establish historically whether inflation preceded blowing as a meaning, or the reverse. Their semantic connection is clear, but their historical sequence requires independent evidence.

2. Early Arabic Uses: Fire, Tools, Dispersal and Pride

Blowing on Fire and the Smith’s Bellows

Blowing on fire was a familiar use of nafkh in Arabic life.

In smithing, an instrument drove air toward the fire to intensify it. This instrument was called a مِنْفَاخ.

Arabic also records نفيخ for someone regularly responsible for blowing on a fire.

A poem of uncertain attribution describes the colour of Mars in the morning:

فِي الصُّبْحِ يَحْكِي لَوْنَهُ زَخِيخُ
مِنْ شُعْلَةٍ سَاعَدَهَا النَّفِيخُ

fī al-ṣubḥi yaḥkī lawnahu zakhīkhu / min shuʿlatin sāʿadahā al-nafīkhu

Translation: “In the morning, the glow of embers from a flame strengthened by the fire-blower mirrors its [Mars’s] colour.”

Here, النفيخ is someone who makes the fire flare up by blowing.

The fragment is transmitted in early lexical records, but its author and precise date are uncertain. It therefore cannot be presented as securely dated pre-Islamic poetry.

Its value lies in illustrating the familiar use of blowing to strengthen fire, a use with a clear Qur’anic example as well.

Nafkh and Dispersal

Al-Quṭāmī, a poet of the Umayyad period, says:

أَلَمْ يُخْزِ التَّفَرُّقُ جُنْدَ كِسْرَى
وَنُفْخُوا فِي مَدَائِنِهِمْ فَطَارُوا

a-lam yukhzi al-tafarruqu junda kisrā / wa-nufkhū fī madāʾinihim fa-ṭārū

Translation: “Did division not disgrace the army of Kisrā? They were blown about in their cities and scattered as though carried into the air by a breath.”

The verse uses blowing figuratively to describe the dispersal of soldiers.

The poet draws on the image of something set in motion and scattered by blowing. Such a use need not describe an actual act performed with the mouth.

The verse is also transmitted in early grammatical discussions of the passive verb. Its نُفْخوا is understood as a shortened form of نُفِخوا, with the middle consonant’s vowel omitted to ease pronunciation.

This verse belongs to the Islamic period. It witnesses figurative usage in classical Arabic, without establishing the same expression in the pre-Islamic period.

Everyday Expressions Involving Fire

Arabic records the expression:

مَا بِالدَّارِ نَافِخُ ضَرَمَةٍ

mā bi-al-dāri nāfikhu ḍaramatin

With the meaning “There is nobody in the house.”

Literally, نَافِخُ ضَرَمَة is someone who blows on a flame or ember to kindle fire.

The expression uses a simple, familiar everyday activity to convey the absence of anyone from the house.

Nafkh remains connected with blowing on fire, while the complete expression acquires the idiomatic meaning “nobody being there.”

This does not make the verb nafakh itself mean living, being present or lighting a house. Those meanings arise from the idiomatic use of the whole expression.

Inflation, Corpulence and Pride

Arabic also uses انتفخ of an inflated belly or bodily swelling.

The same image gives rise to uses for pride and self-importance. An arrogant person may be described with a swollen chest or cheeks, as though making themselves appear excessively large.

In recorded expressions, رجل ذو نفخ can describe an arrogant person, and نفخ شدقيه can refer to self-important behaviour.

These figurative uses must not be extended to every derivative. Nafkh in creation or resurrection does not acquire a meaning of pride merely because it shares the root.

Words for swelling, corpulence, raised ground and plant growth likewise show the family branching into different uses. Each branch must be understood through its own usage.

3. Blowing on Fire and the Appearance of a Bird: Two Different Qur’anic Uses

A. Dhū al-Qarnayn and Blowing on Fire

آتُونِي زُبَرَ الْحَدِيدِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا سَاوَىٰ بَيْنَ الصَّدَفَيْنِ قَالَ انْفُخُوا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَعَلَهُ نَارًا قَالَ آتُونِي أُفْرِغْ عَلَيْهِ قِطْرًا

ātūnī zubara al-ḥadīdi ḥattā idhā sāwā bayna al-ṣadafayni qāla unfukhū ḥattā idhā jaʿalahu nāran qāla ātūnī ufrigh ʿalayhi qiṭran

Translation: “Bring me pieces of iron. When he had levelled the space between the two mountain sides, he said: Blow. When he had made it like fire, he said: Bring it to me, that I may pour molten metal over it.”

Al-Kahf 18:96

Here, Dhū al-Qarnayn is building a barrier between two mountain sides.

After placing the pieces of iron, he commands:

انْفُخُوا

unfukhū

“Blow.”

This is a plural imperative from N F KH, showing that the command addresses a group.

The word انفخوا does not explicitly name what they should blow into. The context supplies it: pieces of iron and fire used to heat them.

The command therefore concerns blowing to intensify fire and heat iron.

This is a clear example of a concrete human use of nafkh in the Qur’an.

Arabic allows blowing with the mouth or with an instrument such as bellows. The verse itself, however, does not name a minfākh, specify the fuel, describe a furnace or provide other technical details.

We can therefore understand blowing on fire from the context without attributing to the text a particular technique it does not describe.

Does Nafkh Mean Kindling?

Here, blowing results in greater heat and brings the iron to a fiery state.

Yet “kindling” is not the whole independent meaning of nafkh.

When someone blows on fire, blowing is the action, while flaring up is a result that occurs under suitable conditions.

The basic translation should therefore retain “Blow.” An explanation can add that it means blowing on fire to increase the heat.

This illustrates the distinction between a root’s meaning and the purpose or outcome of an action in context.

B. Jesus Blowing into a Birdlike Form

أَنِّي أَخْلُقُ لَكُم مِّنَ الطِّينِ كَهَيْئَةِ الطَّيْرِ فَأَنْفُخُ فِيهِ فَيَكُونُ طَيْرًا بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ

annī akhluqu lakum mina al-ṭīni ka-hayʾati al-ṭayri fa-anfukhu fīhi fa-yakūnu ṭayran bi-idhni Allāhi

Translation: “I make for you from clay something in the form of a bird; then I blow into it, and it becomes a bird by permission of The God.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:49

Here, Jesus describes a sign that begins with making a form from clay.

First he makes something birdlike from clay. He then blows into it, and it becomes a bird by permission of The God.

Three matters must be distinguished: forming the clay, Jesus blowing into it, and its becoming a bird by permission of The God.

أَنْفُخُ here is the first-person imperfect of the basic verb نَفَخَ. Its initial hamzah marks the first person; it does not make this a Form IV verb.

The direct meaning of فَأَنْفُخُ فِيهِ is therefore “Then I blow into it.”

The Role of بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ

The continuation attributes the clay form’s becoming a bird to permission of The God.

This qualification matters: the result must not be attributed to an independent, unlimited power inherent in the act of blowing.

Ordinary blowing into a clay form does not make it alive. The Qur’an describes a special sign whose fulfilment depends on permission of The God.

Nafkh therefore does not, by itself, mean giving life here. Blowing is the stated action, and the appearance of the bird is the result linked to divine permission.

The same event is recounted in Al-Māʾidah 5:110:

وَإِذْ تَخْلُقُ مِنَ الطِّينِ كَهَيْئَةِ الطَّيْرِ بِإِذْنِي فَتَنْفُخُ فِيهَا فَتَكُونُ طَيْرًا بِإِذْنِي

wa-idh takhluqu mina al-ṭīni ka-hayʾati al-ṭayri bi-idhnī fa-tanfukhu fīhā fa-takūnu ṭayran bi-idhnī

Translation: “And when you made from clay something in the form of a bird by My permission, then blew into it, and it became a bird by My permission.”

Here, بِإِذْنِي is connected both with making the form and with its becoming a bird.

The difference between فِيهِ in Āl ʿImrān and فِيهَا in Al-Māʾidah must also be examined within each verse’s syntax. In a familiar grammatical reading, the feminine pronoun may refer to هيئة, while the masculine pronoun refers to the object made or the birdlike form. This difference alone does not establish two events or two different methods of creation.

4. Nafkh and Spirit in Human Creation and the Story of Mary

One major Qur’anic setting for N F KH consists of expressions connecting blowing with روح.

These uses require particular care: nafkh involves moving air in human experience, but attributing it to The God does not by itself establish a bodily manner of divine action.

Human Creation: Fashioning and Blowing

فَإِذَا سَوَّيْتُهُ وَنَفَخْتُ فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِي فَقَعُوا لَهُ سَاجِدِينَ

fa-idhā sawwaytuhu wa-nafakhtu fīhi min rūḥī fa-qaʿū lahu sājidīna

Translation: “When I have fashioned him and blown into him from My spirit, fall before him in prostration.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:29

This expression is repeated in Ṣād 38:72.

Both verses mention fashioning first and then blowing.

Taswiyah concerns ordering and completing the form. It is followed by نفخت فيه من روحي, and then by the command that the angels prostrate.

This sequence places blowing within the account of human creation and the human being’s special condition after being fashioned.

The verb alone, however, does not mean producing life, awareness or free choice. Those concepts must be examined through the creation passages as a whole, their relation to spirit and other human characteristics.

Al-Sajdah 32:9 also says:

ثُمَّ سَوَّاهُ وَنَفَخَ فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِهِ وَجَعَلَ لَكُمُ السَّمْعَ وَالْأَبْصَارَ وَالْأَفْئِدَةَ قَلِيلًا مَّا تَشْكُرُونَ

thumma sawwāhu wa-nafakha fīhi min rūḥihi wa-jaʿala lakumu al-samʿa wa-al-abṣāra wa-al-afʾidata qalīlan mā tashkurūna

Translation: “Then He fashioned him and blew into him from His spirit, and gave you hearing, eyes and hearts. Little do you give thanks.”

Here too, blowing is connected with fashioning. Hearing, sight and hearts are mentioned afterward.

This connection helps locate blowing within the creation account, but the root itself must not be made to mean reason, perception or the creation of sensory organs.

Those features are conveyed by other phrases in the verse.

Does The God Blow in the Human Manner?

Ordinary human blowing involves expelling air from the mouth. When the Qur’an attributes nafkh to The God, however, we must not, without evidence, imagine a mouth, lungs, air or a bodily process like a human one for The God.

The Qur’an says of The God:

لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ

laysa ka-mithlihi shayʾun

“Nothing is like Him.”

Al-Shūrā 42:11

Retaining “blowing” in translation therefore does not establish a bodily mode of action.

Conversely, avoiding a bodily conception does not justify deleting nafkh and replacing it in the basic translation with only “created,” “gave life” or “gave awareness.”

The Qur’an uses the verb nafakh. Translation should preserve that expression while explaining that its manner of fulfilment in divine action cannot be determined from the verb’s material sense alone.

Does مِنْ رُوحِي Mean a Portion of the Divine Essence?

In مِن رُّوحِي, the first-person pronoun attributes spirit to The God.

This attribution must not immediately be taken to mean that part of the divine essence separates and enters a human being.

In Qur’anic language, attributing something to The God can express ownership, creation, special association or a distinctive status. For example, ناقة الله, “the she-camel of The God,” does not make the camel a portion of His essence.

The precise meaning of spirit and the role of من require an independent study of R W Ḥ and all Qur’anic uses of spirit.

Study of R W Ḥ

For N F KH, the defensible conclusion is that these verses connect blowing with spirit and give it an important place in human creation. The root itself does not define the nature of spirit or its ontological relationship with The God.

Mary’s Story: Two Different Expressions

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:91 says:

وَالَّتِي أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهَا مِن رُّوحِنَا وَجَعَلْنَاهَا وَابْنَهَا آيَةً لِّلْعَالَمِينَ

wa-allatī aḥṣanat farjahā fa-nafakhnā fīhā min rūḥinā wa-jaʿalnāhā wa-ibnahā āyatan li-al-ʿālamīna

Translation: “And the woman who guarded her chastity: We blew into her from Our spirit, and made her and her son a sign for all peoples.”

Al-Taḥrīm 66:12 also says:

وَمَرْيَمَ ابْنَتَ عِمْرَانَ الَّتِي أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِنَا

wa-maryama ibnata ʿimrāna allatī aḥṣanat farjahā fa-nafakhnā fīhi min rūḥinā

Translation: “And Mary, daughter of ʿImrān, who guarded her chastity: We blew into it from Our spirit.”

The first verse has فِيهَا and the second فِيهِ.

This pronoun difference must be acknowledged. In a direct grammatical reading, the feminine pronoun in Al-Anbiyāʾ refers to Mary, whereas the masculine pronoun in Al-Taḥrīm may refer to فرجها.

Early exegetical explanations offer accounts of how this blowing occurred, including an angel blowing into part of Mary’s garment. Such details do not follow from the verb nafakh itself.

Maryam 19:17, Maryam 19:18, Maryam 19:19, Maryam 19:20 and Maryam 19:21 introduce a messenger sent by The God to Mary, who speaks of granting her a pure son. These verses matter for understanding the story, but they do not explain the precise bodily manner of blowing.

The passages as a whole attribute Jesus’s coming into being to the power and direction of The God, affirm Mary’s chastity and present the event as a divine sign.

The expression of blowing must not be taken to mean that Jesus is part of the divine essence, or that his creation necessarily followed a particular physical method that the Qur’an does not state.

The blowing in Mary’s story must also remain distinct from Jesus blowing into the birdlike form. One passage describes divine action in Jesus’s coming into being; the other describes Jesus himself blowing into a clay form, with permission of The God.

5. Blowing into the Ṣūr: One State Ends and Another Begins

Most Qur’anic occurrences of N F KH concern blowing into the ṣūr.

The expression appears in Al-Anʿām 6:73, Al-Kahf 18:99, Ṭā Hā 20:102, Al-Muʾminūn 23:101, Al-Naml 27:87, Yā Sīn 36:51, Al-Zumar 39:68, Qāf 50:20, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:13 and Al-Nabaʾ 78:18. Al-Zumar 39:68 uses the verb twice for two successive events.

The Meaning of نُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ

In this expression, نُفِخَ is a passive perfect verb: “was blown.”

فِي الصُّورِ specifies what is blown into.

In a familiar Arabic explanation, ṣūr here is a horn or trumpet into which one blows.

Another interpretation in the history of exegesis relates ṣūr to the plural of صورة. Nevertheless, the interpretation as a blowing instrument has a strong and established place in lexical and exegetical tradition.

This disagreement concerns ṣūr, rather than the meaning of N F KH itself.

A final assessment of the nature of the ṣūr requires a separate study of its root and uses. For N F KH, it is enough to establish that the Qur’an speaks of blowing into it and associates this with the great events of resurrection.

Study of Ṣ W R

Even if ṣūr is understood as a special trumpet or horn, the Qur’an does not explain its material, size, exact shape or mechanism.

Nor do these verses themselves name a particular angel as the blower. Identifying the agent by name through external reports is a separate matter and must not become the literal meaning of nafkh or an addition to the basic translation.

Blowing and Ṣaʿq

وَنُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ فَصَعِقَ مَن فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَن فِي الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا مَن شَاءَ اللَّهُ

wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-ṣaʿiqa man fī al-samāwāti wa-man fī al-arḍi illā man shāʾa Allāhu

Translation: “And the ṣūr was blown into; then whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on earth suffered ṣaʿq, except those whom The God wills.”

Al-Zumar 39:68

Here, blowing is associated with ṣaʿq.

The root Ṣ ʿ Q in the Qur’an concerns events such as fainting, loss of consciousness or death through an overwhelming occurrence. Its precise meaning must be studied separately in each verse.

For N F KH, the crucial point is that ṣaʿq is expressed by a different verb. Blowing itself does not mean dying or being destroyed.

Blowing is the event followed here by the reported ṣaʿq.

إِلَّا مَن شَاءَ اللَّهُ is also an explicit exception. The passage therefore cannot be made to say that absolutely every being perishes without exception.

Another Blowing and Rising

The same verse continues:

ثُمَّ نُفِخَ فِيهِ أُخْرَىٰ فَإِذَا هُمْ قِيَامٌ يَنظُرُونَ

thumma nufikha fīhi ukhrā fa-idhā hum qiyāmun yanẓurūna

Translation: “Then it was blown into again; and suddenly they are standing, looking.”

Al-Zumar 39:68

In this part, blowing is associated with rising rather than ṣaʿq.

The difference matters for understanding the root’s semantic capacity.

If nafkh inherently meant destruction, it could not also accompany beings rising in the same verse. Likewise, if it always meant giving life, that meaning alone would not explain the first occurrence associated with ṣaʿq.

The result of each blowing must therefore remain distinct from the root’s independent meaning.

Both expressions mean blowing, but the consequence is stated differently at each stage.

Yā Sīn 36:51 also says:

وَنُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ فَإِذَا هُم مِّنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَنسِلُونَ

wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-idhā hum mina al-ajdāthi ilā rabbihim yansilūna

Translation: “And the ṣūr was blown into; suddenly they are hastening out of the graves toward their Lord.”

Here, the result is expressed as emerging from graves and moving toward their Lord.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:18 also says:

يَوْمَ يُنفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ فَتَأْتُونَ أَفْوَاجًا

yawma yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri fa-taʾtūna afwājan

Translation: “The day the ṣūr is blown into, and you come in groups.”

Here too, blowing is connected with people gathering in the resurrection scene.

The verses are close in subject, while each highlights part of the event and its outcome.

Blowing and Fear

Al-Naml 27:87 says:

وَيَوْمَ يُنفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ فَفَزِعَ مَن فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَن فِي الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا مَن شَاءَ اللَّهُ

wa-yawma yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri fa-faziʿa man fī al-samāwāti wa-man fī al-arḍi illā man shāʾa Allāhu

Translation: “And the day the ṣūr is blown into, whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on earth becomes terrified, except those whom The God wills.”

This verse expresses the result through fazaʿ.

Fazaʿ concerns fear and agitation on encountering an event.

Fear is not part of the lexical meaning of nafkh. The Qur’an states the beings’ response through the separate verb faziʿa.

The same distinction holds elsewhere: Ṭā Hā 20:102 mentions gathering the guilty; Al-Muʾminūn 23:101, the ineffectiveness of kinship ties in that situation; and Al-Kahf 18:99, gathering humanity.

These outcomes form parts of the description of resurrection, rather than separate meanings of the root nafkh.

نَفْخَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ: One Act of Blowing

فَإِذَا نُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ نَفْخَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ

fa-idhā nufikha fī al-ṣūri nafkhatun wāḥidatun

Translation: “Then, when the ṣūr is blown into once.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:13

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of the noun nafkhah from N F KH.

Nafkhah follows the pattern فَعْلَة, here expressing one occurrence of the action.

Wāḥidah reinforces the singleness of that occurrence.

Grammatically, in the common reading, نَفْخَةٌ is nominative and serves as the passive subject of نُفِخَ.

Does “one act of blowing” mean that there is only one blowing throughout all the events of resurrection?

That conclusion would conflict with the explicit wording of Al-Zumar 39:68, which describes one blowing and then another.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:13 describes one particular occurrence. Mentioning one there does not by itself exclude another.

Nor should these verses be combined, without examining all the scenes, to specify the number of resurrection stages or their entire sequence in detail beyond the text.

Al-Zumar 39:68 clearly describes two distinct blowings. Later labels such as the blowing of fear, ṣaʿq and resurrection, and the final number of blowings, belong to a separate exegetical discussion.

Must Blowing into the Ṣūr Have a Particular Physical Sound?

Ordinarily, blowing into a trumpet or horn can create sound. If ṣūr is understood as such an instrument, its association with sound is natural.

The cited verses, however, do not explain the sound wave’s shape, loudness, range or the physical mechanism of blowing.

They must therefore not be turned into a theory about sound waves, cosmic energy or the physical mechanism of resurrection.

Nor should physical questions be avoided by deleting the meaning of blowing and translating it only as “issuing a command” or “beginning a transformation.”

Such descriptions may explain the event’s role or consequence, but do not replace the verb’s literal meaning.

6. Comparison with Related Roots

N F KH and N F Ḥ

These roots are close in sound and some uses, but differ in meaning.

Nafkh primarily concerns blowing and directing air, whereas nafḥ has a wider range including wafting, diffusion, an effect reaching someone, giving and striking.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:46, نَفْحَةٌ مِنْ عَذَابِ رَبِّكَ concerns a portion of punishment reaching people. In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:13, however, نَفْخَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ expresses one act of blowing.

The words differ only in their final consonant, but must remain distinct in translation and root analysis.

In particular, nafḥah in Al-Anbiyāʾ must not become nafkhah, nor should nafkhah in Al-Ḥāqqah be understood as a waft of punishment.

Study of N F Ḥ

N F KH and N F TH

In familiar Arabic usage, nafth concerns expelling air or blowing accompanied by a little moisture from the mouth.

The Qur’an uses this root in:

وَمِن شَرِّ النَّفَّاثَاتِ فِي الْعُقَدِ

wa-min sharri al-naffāthāti fī al-ʿuqadi

Translation: “And from the evil of those who blow into knots.”

Al-Falaq 113:4

Nafkh and nafth overlap in expelling air, but moisture from the mouth is more prominent in familiar uses of nafth.

This must not become an absolute, mechanical rule that every nafth involves a specific quantity of saliva and every nafkh occurs without any moisture.

The meaning of nafth must not be imported without evidence into Qur’anic nafkh, such as blowing on fire or into a birdlike form.

Study of N F TH

N F KH and N F S

Nafas, in the sense related to respiration, concerns inhalation and exhalation.

Ordinary nafkh also involves expelling air from the mouth, which brings the concepts close together.

Yet not every exhalation is called nafkh. Directed air toward something is usually more prominent in nafkh, while nafas can refer to ordinary breathing.

Nafs, meaning a person, self or soul, also has other uses that must not be derived from N F KH merely because the consonants are close.

Study of N F S

N F KH and N F DH

The principal uses of nafadh concern passing through, crossing, penetrating or being carried through to completion.

Nafkh, by contrast, denotes blowing and directing air.

Air may penetrate something after blowing, but penetration is a possible result rather than the constant meaning of nafkh.

Expressions such as نفخت فيه must therefore not automatically be translated as complete penetration, indwelling or bodily mixture.

This caution is especially relevant to verses concerning spirit.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan and Conclusion

For نَفَخَ in human use, the basic equivalent is “blew”; for يَنْفُخُ, “blows”; and for the imperative انْفُخُوا, “blow.”

Al-Kahf 18:96 should retain “Blow,” explaining that the purpose is to intensify fire and heat iron.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:49:

فَأَنْفُخُ فِيهِ فَيَكُونُ طَيْرًا بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ

fa-anfukhu fīhi fa-yakūnu ṭayran bi-idhni Allāhi

“Then I blow into it, and it becomes a bird by permission of The God.”

“I blow” renders the verb, while becoming a bird is the result stated in the next clause.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:29:

وَنَفَخْتُ فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِي

wa-nafakhtu fīhi min rūḥī

“And I blew into him from My spirit.”

This preserves the Qur’anic expression without determining a bodily mode of divine blowing. The nature of spirit requires its own independent study.

In Al-Zumar 39:68:

وَنُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ

wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūri

“And the ṣūr was blown into.”

The basic translation can retain ṣūr, or use an approved equivalent once its meaning has been established through independent study.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:13:

نَفْخَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ

nafkhatun wāḥidatun

“One act of blowing,” or, in the complete construction, “is blown into once.”

The singular form and the qualifier wāḥidah must remain visible.

For non-Qur’anic Arabic branches, intafakha may mean “became inflated,” “swelled” or “rose,” according to context. Minfākh is the blowing instrument.

Brief Root Meaning and Meanings in the Verses

Brief root meaning: Blowing and directing a current of air; other branches involve inflation, swelling and increased volume.

In Al-Kahf 18:96: Blow; in context, to intensify fire and heat iron.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:49 and Al-Māʾidah 5:110: Jesus blowing into the clay form that becomes a bird by permission of The God.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:29, Al-Sajdah 32:9 and Ṣād 38:72: Blowing from the spirit in human creation, in a manner not established by the bodily use of nafkh alone.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:91 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:12: Blowing from the spirit in Mary’s story and the coming into being of Jesus.

In the verses concerning the ṣūr: Blowing into the ṣūr in resurrection scenes, with the consequences stated separately by each verse.

Conclusion

N F KH is known in Arabic for blowing. Its ordinary use involves expelling air and directing it toward or into something. Other branches extend to inflation, swelling, increased volume and figurative uses such as pride.

The Qur’an uses the root in four main settings: blowing on fire, Jesus blowing into a birdlike form, blowing associated with spirit in creation, and blowing into the ṣūr during resurrection.

In all these uses, the act of blowing must remain distinct from its result.

In Dhū al-Qarnayn’s story, blowing accompanies the intensification of fire and heating of iron. In Jesus’s story, it is linked to a bird appearing by permission of The God. In the creation passages, it connects with spirit and the coming into being of humanity or Jesus. In resurrection scenes, it precedes different outcomes, including ṣaʿq, fear, rising and gathering.

These differences show that the root itself does not mean creating, giving life, causing death, terrifying or raising the dead. Those concepts follow from the complete verse constructions and accompanying verbs.

Ordinary human blowing is bodily, but that feature must not be attributed to divine action without evidence. Preserving the literal expression does not specify the physical manner of unseen events.

Final definition: N F KH primarily denotes blowing and directing a current of air. Other branches of the family also involve inflation, rising and increased volume. The Qur’an uses nafkh in tangible and unseen settings, but the root alone does not determine its result: the agent, the object, accompanying words and each verse’s context do. “Blowing” is therefore an appropriate basic equivalent for Forqan, while its manner and consequences require separate examination in each verse.
