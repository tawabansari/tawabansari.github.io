From driving and controlling to gaining mastery and bringing under one’s control

The Arabic root «ح و ذ» concerns driving, gathering, controlling, and gaining mastery over something. Early uses describe driving livestock forcefully and controlling their movement. Gathering and driving animals so that they do not leave the intended course or direction establishes a form of control over their movement.

In Arabic, this semantic field also extends to human relationships and influence over others’ decisions and conduct. Istiḥwādh in such uses means gaining the upper hand, prevailing, and bringing something under one’s influence or control.

The Qur’an uses this root both for the hypocrites’ influence over another group and for Satan’s mastery over people. Distinguishing these situations matters for understanding the word precisely.

1. Root forms and semantic core

The root «ح و ذ» occurs in two Qur’anic verses, in two verbal forms: «نَسْتَحْوِذْ» in Al-Nisāʾ 4:141 and «اسْتَحْوَذَ» in Al-Mujādilah 58:19. Both are Form X inflections of istaḥwadha–yastaḥwidhu.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two Form X verbs

Other forms in this Arabic family are also established:

  • حَاذَ ـ يَحُوذُ (ḥādha–yaḥūdhu): driving, gathering, controlling, or gaining mastery over something.
  • حَوْذ (ḥawdh): driving or directing onward, especially forcefully or rapidly.
  • أَحْوَذَ (aḥwadha): gathering something, bringing it under control, or managing it skillfully.
  • إِحْوَاذ (iḥwādh): forceful or rapid driving; also controlling and bringing under one’s authority.
  • اِسْتَحْوَذَ ـ يَسْتَحْوِذُ (istaḥwadha–yastaḥwidhu): gaining mastery over someone or something and bringing it under one’s influence or control.
  • أَحْوَذِيّ (aḥwadhī): an agile, capable person skilled in managing affairs.

These forms are recorded in lexical sources, but apart from istaḥwadha and nastaḥwidh, the other listed forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Semantic core

Understanding the semantic core requires considering two related branches: driving onward forcefully or rapidly, and gathering, controlling, or gaining mastery.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris describes this root through «الخفة والسرعة وانكماش في الأمر»: agility, speed, and energetic application to a task. He gives forcefully driving livestock as an example and also explains Satan’s mastery within this family.

Sources such as Tahdhīb al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab likewise emphasize gathering, bringing under control, and prevailing.

The two directions of meaning are related, but an imagined physical picture alone cannot establish that every historical meaning necessarily developed from one into the other.

What stands out in the Qur’anic uses is mastery and control over the course of affairs or another’s conduct. Speed and force occur in early witnesses, but need not appear independently in every translation of istiḥwādh.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Tahdhīb al-Lughah, and Lisān al-ʿArab: driving, speed, and mastery

2. Early Arabic usage

Driving and controlling livestock

Driving animals is one clear early use of this root.

Ḥādha ibilahu yaḥūdhuhā ḥawdhan describes someone driving camels forcefully. This involves more than setting them in motion: the driver keeps them on course and prevents them from scattering.

When explaining ḥawdh in Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib Al-Iṣfahānī describes driving an animal from the rear and the two sides of its body. This provides a picture of controlling and directing the animal onward.

Mentioning an animal’s body parts in a lexical explanation does not, however, establish those parts’ names as the historical origin of every meaning of the root.

A witness from Labīd’s poetry

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, a poet who lived in both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

إِذَا اجْتَمَعَتْ وَأَحْوَذَ جَانِبَيْهَا
وَأَوْرَدَهَا عَلَى عُوجٍ طِوَالِ

idhā ijtamaʿat wa-aḥwadha jānibayhā / wa-awradahā ʿalā ʿūjin ṭiwāli

Translation following the reading that identifies legs:

“When the she-asses gathered, [the male ass] controlled them from both sides and brought them to water on long, curved legs.”

Aḥwadha jānibayhā here indicates that the male animal gathered the females from both sides and controlled their movement.

Gathering and controlling are connected in this use. The animals are held together as a group, with their movement directed toward the driver’s intended destination.

The verse is transmitted in Labīd’s collected poetry. Although he lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, this verse’s precise date is unclear. It is therefore an early Arabic witness attributed to Labīd, rather than one securely dated before the Qur’an’s revelation.

Two interpretations of ʿūjin ṭiwāli are recorded: the animals’ long, curved legs, or tall, bending palms beside the water. The translation above reflects the former. This disagreement does not alter the sense of gathering and controlling in aḥwadha jānibayhā.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah, ʿ W J: two interpretations of Labīd’s witness

Agility and mastery of a task

Classical Arabic also records aḥwadhī for an agile, capable person who carries out affairs skillfully and energetically.

The family «ح و ذ» therefore does not always concern domination over people or animals. It can also describe someone’s ability to manage a task well.

Mastery in this root is thus not inherently injustice, deception, or wrongful domination. Its positive or negative value depends on the object and manner of exercising power.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Asās al-Balāghah: aḥwadhī, an agile, capable person

3. Qur’anic uses

A. Al-Nisāʾ 4:141: istiḥwādh in relations between hypocrites and disbelievers

الَّذِينَ يَتَرَبَّصُونَ بِكُمْ فَإِنْ كَانَ لَكُمْ فَتْحٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ قَالُوا أَلَمْ نَكُنْ مَعَكُمْ وَإِنْ كَانَ لِلْكَافِرِينَ نَصِيبٌ قَالُوا أَلَمْ نَسْتَحْوِذْ عَلَيْكُمْ وَنَمْنَعْكُمْ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ فَاللَّهُ يَحْكُمُ بَيْنَكُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ وَلَنْ يَجْعَلَ اللَّهُ لِلْكَافِرِينَ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ سَبِيلًا

alladhīna yatarabbaṣūna bikum fa-in kāna lakum fatḥun mina allāhi qālū a-lam nakun maʿakum wa-in kāna li-l-kāfirīna naṣībun qālū a-lam nastaḥwidh ʿalaykum wa-namnaʿkum mina al-muʾminīna fa-allāhu yaḥkumu baynakum yawma al-qiyāmati wa-lan yajʿala allāhu li-l-kāfirīna ʿalā al-muʾminīna sabīlan

Proposed translation:

“Those who wait to see what happens to you: if victory from The God comes to you, they say, ‘Were we not with you?’ If the disbelievers have a share, they say, ‘Did we not have the upper hand over you and protect you against the believers?’ The God will judge between you on the Day of Resurrection, and The God will never grant the disbelievers a way over the believers.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:141

The verse describes people who maintain no consistent, honest position toward the two groups, instead awaiting the outcome so they can associate themselves with the side benefiting from victory.

If the believers prevail, they say a-lam nakun maʿakum: “Were we not with you?”

If the disbelievers gain a share, however, they say: «أَلَمْ نَسْتَحْوِذْ عَلَيْكُمْ وَنَمْنَعْكُمْ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ» (a-lam nastaḥwidh ʿalaykum wa-namnaʿkum mina al-muʾminīna).

Nastaḥwidh is a first-person plural verb here and concerns the speakers’ own claim. They seek to impress their audience with their role and influence.

Istiḥwādh here means mastery, having the upper hand, or exercising influence over the other side. In the common reading, the following wa-namnaʿkum mina al-muʾminīna claims protection for the audience against the believers. The manner of influence or protection must be understood from the whole statement and its context.

Two approaches have been offered: influence over the disbelievers’ decisions and conduct, or assistance and protection against the believers through the hypocrites’ actions. In either case, the claim concerns an effective role in managing or altering the course of affairs.

Al-Basīṭ: influence, protection, and readings of the hypocrites’ speech

The statement is quoted as the hypocrites’ own speech. Its use of the verb therefore should not be taken as the Qur’an confirming either the truth or the actual extent of their claimed influence.

They claim a part in shaping the other side’s situation and use that claim to associate themselves with those gaining an advantage.

Istiḥwādh here has a social and political setting: influence capable of affecting decisions, orientations, and relations between groups.

ʿAlaykum identifies those over whom mastery or influence is claimed. This relationship comes from the construction istaḥwadha ʿalā, rather than an independent meaning of ʿalā supposedly fixed across every expression.

Al-Ṭabarī: mastery, Labīd’s witness, and the hypocrites’ quoted speech

B. Al-Mujādilah 58:19: Satan’s mastery over people

اسْتَحْوَذَ عَلَيْهِمُ الشَّيْطَانُ فَأَنْسَاهُمْ ذِكْرَ اللَّهِ أُولَٰئِكَ حِزْبُ الشَّيْطَانِ أَلَا إِنَّ حِزْبَ الشَّيْطَانِ هُمُ الْخَاسِرُونَ

istaḥwadha ʿalayhimu al-shayṭānu fa-ansāhum dhikra allāhi ulāʾika ḥizbu al-shayṭāni a-lā inna ḥizba al-shayṭāni humu al-khāsirūna

Proposed translation:

“Satan gained mastery over them and made them forget the remembrance of The God. They are Satan’s party. Surely Satan’s party are the losers.”

Al-Mujādilah 58:19

Here, istaḥwadha concerns Satan’s mastery over a group of people.

Understanding this mastery requires the preceding verses. Al-Mujādilah 58:14, Al-Mujādilah 58:15, Al-Mujādilah 58:16, Al-Mujādilah 58:17, and Al-Mujādilah 58:18 describe people who swear falsely, make their oaths a shield, turn others away from The God’s path, and continue their manner of swearing even when raised on the Day of Resurrection.

Al-Mujādilah 58:19 therefore follows a description of deliberate, recurring conduct.

In istaḥwadha ʿalayhimu al-shayṭānu, the verb retains the meaning of mastery and control, while its setting shifts from human relationships to Satan’s influence over human conduct and orientation.

The verse immediately states the result:

فَأَنْسَاهُمْ ذِكْرَ اللَّهِ

fa-ansāhum dhikra allāhi

That is, Satan made them forget the remembrance of The God.

This clause is essential to understanding the verse. Satan’s mastery is associated with people turning away from remembering The God. Making them forget is nevertheless not the lexical meaning of istaḥwadha itself; it is a consequence expressed through the separate verb fa-ansāhum.

Failure to distinguish these clauses may lead to equating istiḥwādh with forgetting, heedlessness, or abandoning remembrance. The first word concerns mastery, while the following clause explains its consequence.

Al-Ṭabarī: Satan’s mastery and causing forgetfulness of remembrance

Does Satan’s mastery mean a loss of human agency?

To answer, Al-Mujādilah 58:19 should be read alongside other verses explaining how Satan exerts influence.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, Satan declares on the Day of Resurrection that he had no authority over people except that he called them and they responded.

Al-Naḥl 16:99 and Al-Naḥl 16:100 distinguish those who believe and trust in their Lord from those who take Satan as a patron.

Al-Ḥijr 15:42 denies Satan’s authority over The God’s servants, except those among the erring who follow him.

These verses indicate that Satan’s mastery should not be read as unlimited power to compel people. He may call, deceive, embellish falsehood, and gain influence over those who follow him, without removing their responsibility for choosing.

Istiḥwādh in Al-Mujādilah 58:19 therefore describes real mastery over orientation and conduct, rather than necessarily external compulsion or the destruction of agency.

Consider someone who, through self-interest, prejudice, or attachment to a group, repeatedly asserts and defends a falsehood. This conduct may gradually dominate their decisions so that they continue the same course in new situations. This is merely an illustration of influence and mastery in human experience, rather than an exclusive interpretation of Satan in the verse.

Al-Mujādilah 58:19 also places ḥizbu al-shayṭāni after mastery and forgetting the remembrance of The God. Their affiliation with this party comes from their position and orientation within the context; istaḥwadha itself does not mean forming a party or joining a group.

4. Morphology and the two uses

Both Qur’anic verbs are Form X, but differ in tense, person, and function within the sentence.

Istaḥwadha in Al-Mujādilah 58:19 is a third-person masculine singular perfect verb, with al-shayṭānu as its subject. The verse reports Satan’s mastery over a particular group.

Nastaḥwidh in Al-Nisāʾ 4:141 is a first-person plural imperfect verb, made jussive by lam. It appears within the question a-lam nastaḥwidh ʿalaykum and belongs to the hypocrites’ quoted speech.

The distinction between a direct report and a group’s quoted claim matters for understanding the two uses. It comes from the sentence’s construction and speaker, rather than from the root itself.

Why does istaḥwadha retain wāw?

Ḥ W DH is a hollow root with wāw as its middle radical.

In many comparable verbs, morphological sound changes turn wāw into alif, as in istaqāma from Q W M.

Following this common pattern, istaḥādha in the perfect and yastaḥīdhu in the imperfect would be expected. These forms are also recognized in lexical and morphological discussions.

The two Qur’anic uses, however, retain the root’s original wāw: istaḥwadha and nastaḥwidh.

Al-Ṭabarī and several early linguists explain this as retaining the verb’s original form without the usual weak-letter alteration.

The Qur’anic istaḥwadha is therefore not a morphological error. It is an established, eloquent Arabic form, even though it differs from the common alteration found in many hollow verbs.

This feature also identifies wāw as part of the root material. The verb should not be assigned to a different root merely because it resembles other words.

5. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ W DH (ḥawdh) and Ḥ W Z (ḥawz): control and possession

Ḥ W Z concerns gathering, separating, taking possession, and bringing something within one’s own domain.

Early Arabic records ḥādha al-ibila and ḥāza al-ibila with close meanings in some situations. Some poetic witnesses even vary between transmissions with dhāl and zāy.

This proximity shows an overlap in usage, but does not independently establish a single historical origin for the two roots.

Ḥ W DH highlights driving, controlling, and mastering movement or conduct. In Ḥ W Z, gathering and bringing within one’s domain of possession are more prominent.

The distinction is not absolute, since either root can mean mastery or possession in some uses.

Ḥ W DH (ḥawdh) and Ḥ W Ṭ (ḥawṭ): mastery and encompassing

Ḥ W Ṭ concerns surrounding, encompassing, and, in some contexts, safeguarding or encircling.

Surrounding from all sides is prominent in Ḥ W Ṭ. In Ḥ W DH, driving, controlling, and directing movement or the course of affairs are important attested uses.

Istaḥwadha ʿalayhim can therefore be translated in some contexts as “he gained mastery over them,” whereas aḥāṭa bihim emphasizes encompassing them from all sides.

These concepts may coincide: someone who controls another from all sides may gain mastery as well. That semantic connection does not make the roots identical.

Ḥ W DH (ḥawdh) and GH L B (ghalab): mastery and victory

Ghalabah denotes prevailing or gaining mastery over another, including in competition, conflict, or struggle.

Istiḥwādh may be close to ghalabah, while its established uses also involve bringing the other side under control or influence.

Someone might prevail in a competition without thereafter controlling the other person’s decisions or conduct. Istiḥwādh over someone, however, also conveys influence or mastery over them.

This difference should not be made absolute, since ghalabah can itself lead to lasting domination in some contexts.

Ḥ W DH (ḥawdh) and S L Ṭ (sulṭān): influence and authority

Depending on context, Qur’anic sulṭān can concern power, control, authority, or proof.

Istiḥwādh, however, is a verbal noun naming the act of gaining mastery. The verbal form istaḥwadha expresses the occurrence of that mastery or influence over someone.

This distinction matters when discussing Satan. Al-Mujādilah 58:19 describes his mastery over a group, while Al-Naḥl 16:99, Al-Naḥl 16:100, and Ibrāhīm 14:22 clarify the limits of his authority.

The occurrence of istaḥwadha alone therefore does not establish independent, unlimited power over human will.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translating «ح و ذ» requires distinguishing the basic meaning of mastery from explanations supplied by each verse’s context.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:141, a-lam nastaḥwidh ʿalaykum can be rendered “Did we not have the upper hand over you?” or “Did we not have mastery over you?”

Having the upper hand is natural in this context, particularly in speech seeking to impress others with the speakers’ influence. Being in control of you is also close to the established meaning, but may suggest complete rule, which the clause alone does not establish.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:19, “Satan gained mastery over them” is clear and appropriate for istaḥwadha ʿalayhimu al-shayṭānu. “Satan gained control over them” is also defensible, provided that control is not understood as absolute compulsion or loss of agency.

Longer expressions such as “Satan completely took their thought and will out of their own hands” are unsuitable for direct translation. They add details that require separate support from other verses.

Similarly, translating the whole expression istaḥwadha ʿalayhimu al-shayṭānu fa-ansāhum dhikra allāhi as merely “Satan distracted them from remembering The God” weakens the element of mastery and merges two distinct verbs.

A basic translation should retain both actions: first Satan’s mastery, then his causing them to forget the remembrance of The God.

7. Conclusion

Brief root meaning: Driving and controlling, gathering, and gaining mastery; in Qur’anic usage, mastery and influence over another.

The Qur’an contains nastaḥwidh and istaḥwadha. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:141, hypocrites speak of their influence and upper hand over another group. In Al-Mujādilah 58:19, Satan’s mastery concerns people whose conduct involves lies, deceptive oaths, and turning others away from The God’s path.

Both uses are close to mastery and bringing under one’s influence or control. One occurs in human relations and the hypocrites’ claim; the other concerns Satan’s influence over human conduct and orientation.

Satan’s mastery should also be understood through verses concerning invitation, following, and the limits of his authority. Istiḥwādh itself means neither absolute compulsion nor forgetting the remembrance of The God. The latter is a consequence expressed separately in Al-Mujādilah.

Final definition: Ḥ W DH concerns driving and controlling and, in extended uses, gaining mastery and bringing under one’s influence. Qur’anic istiḥwādh means mastery and influence over another; its nature, manner, and limits must be understood from the sentence’s construction and the verse’s context.
