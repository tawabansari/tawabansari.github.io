Naʿl in early Arabic, its human and animal uses, and the command to remove footwear in Moses’ account

The Arabic root ن ع ل is chiefly known through naʿl: something beneath the foot during walking that protects it from direct contact with the ground and its hazards. In ordinary use it denotes footwear, especially the simple, light sandals covering the sole familiar in early Arabic.

The family extends beyond human footwear. Naʿl also names a protective piece beneath an animal’s hoof or foot, the lower fitting of a sword scabbard, certain hard, stony ground, and figuratively things placed underneath or trodden upon.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an, in the command to Moses:

فَاخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ

fa-khlaʿ naʿlayka

“So take off your sandals.”

Ṭā Hā 20:12

This command opens the divine conversation with Moses in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā. The Qur’an explicitly commands removing two shoes and then recalls the sacred location. It does not separately specify their material, ritual purity or the detailed reason for the command.

The study should first establish the concrete meaning of naʿl, then distinguish the explicit command from interpretations of its reason and wisdom.

1. Forms and Semantic Core

Important Arabic forms include:

  • نَعْل (naʿl): footwear; also protective covering beneath an animal’s foot and certain similar objects.
  • نَعْلَة (naʿlah): another form for footwear; in some uses a part or piece resembling it.
  • نِعَال و أَنْعُل (niʿāl, anʿul): plurals of naʿl.
  • نَعِلَ (naʿila): put on footwear.
  • اِنْتَعَلَ و تَنَعَّلَ (intaʿala, tanaʿʿala): wore shoes or sandals.
  • نَاعِل (nāʿil): having or wearing footwear; of certain animals, having hard hooves.
  • مُنْتَعِل (muntaʿil): wearing footwear.
  • أَنْعَلَ و نَعَّلَ (anʿala, naʿʿala): fitted footwear or a protective covering to an animal’s foot.
  • تَنْعِيل (tanʿīl): fitting footwear or protection, particularly to an animal’s foot.
  • نَعْلُ السَّيْف (naʿl al-sayf): the protective fitting at the bottom of a sword scabbard.
  • نَعْلُ الأَرْض (naʿl al-arḍ): a hard, stony and sometimes elevated patch of ground.

None of this family’s verbs occurs in the Qur’an. Its sole form is naʿlayka: the dual of naʿl with an attached second-person pronoun.

Semantic Core

In its main familiar use, naʿl is what the foot rests on, separating it from the ground.

Two features stand out: position beneath the foot, and a covering or protective role while walking.

The image of an underside or point of contact also appears in extended names, such as the bottom scabbard fitting and animal hoof protection.

One lexical analysis relates this family to being low or sunken. It fits some uses, but historical examination is needed before treating it as the certain origin of every meaning.

Hard, stony ground is not explained by “being underneath” alone: some descriptions make it slightly elevated. Definitions such as “anything low” or “anything underneath something else” erase the word’s precision.

For the clothing branch, the clearer definition is footwear beneath the foot that protects it.

2. Early Arabic Usage

Footwear in Everyday Life

Early lexical accounts describe naʿl as protecting the foot from direct ground contact.

Its form could vary: some were simple and thin, others layered or attached with straps.

Arabic accordingly had terms for footwear components, including straps and stitched pieces.

Naʿila and intaʿala express putting on footwear. Words from the ḥafā family, by contrast, describe going without it.

Rajulun nāʿilun denotes a man having or wearing footwear; rajulun ḥāfin denotes a barefoot man.

This contrast establishes a familiar, concrete everyday object. Its use is not inherently moral or religious: it can protect the feet, make walking comfortable or suit the ground.

Some expressions make thin, fine footwear a sign of prosperity. This does not make footwear inherently aristocratic; material, quality and use vary with context.

Animal Shoes and Hoof Protection

In animal-related usage, naʿl denotes protection fitted beneath a hoof or foot.

Tanʿīl of a horse, for example, describes fitting a protective piece such as iron beneath its hoof to guard against hard ground.

Leather protection for part of a camel’s foot is also recorded.

Anʿala al-dābbata and naʿʿalahā belong to this setting. Lexical accounts differ over particular verb forms, with some preferring anʿala.

Nāʿil can also describe a wild ass with very hard hooves, as though their natural hardness supplied protective footwear.

The family can thus describe something not actually wearing a shoe, but likened to footwear in resistance and protection.

The literal protective object and the comparison should be distinguished. Hoof hardness is not the basic meaning of naʿl; it motivates this figurative description.

A Sword’s Naʿl and Other Protective Fittings

Naʿl al-sayf named an iron or silver fitting at the lower end of a sword scabbard.

It could protect the scabbard’s end, much as footwear sits beneath a foot.

A verse attributed to the Umayyad poet Dhū al-Rummah says:

تَرَى سَيْفَهُ لَا تَنْصُفُ السَّاقَ نَعْلُهُ
أَجَلْ لَا وَإِنْ كَانَتْ طِوَالًا مَحَامِلُهْ

tarā sayfahu lā tanṣufu al-sāqa naʿluhu / ajal lā wa-in kānat ṭiwālan maḥāmiluh

Translation: “You see his sword: its lower scabbard fitting does not reach halfway down his shin—indeed not, even with long suspension straps.”

Naʿl here denotes the scabbard’s lower fitting, not human footwear.

The use illustrates extension from footwear to a fitting at an object’s lower end.

This poet lived after the pre-Islamic period; the verse should not be presented as direct evidence predating the Qur’an.

Hard Ground

Naʿl also named certain hard, stony patches of land, sometimes slightly above the surrounding ground, with hard, shining stones.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

كَأَنَّهُمْ حَرْشَفٌ مَبْثُوثٌ
بِالْجَوِّ إِذْ تَبْرُقُ النِّعَالُ

ka-annahum ḥarshafun mabthūthun / bi-al-jawwi idh tabruqu al-niʿālu

Translation: “They seem like scattered locusts in the open ground, when the hard, stony patches gleam.”

Al-niʿāl here is not human footwear, but the plural of naʿl in its terrain sense.

The description compares men or riders with scattered locusts. Lexical accounts differ over the precise referent of the comparison.

Some versions transmit bi-al-safḥ instead of bi-al-jaww. Despite this variant, the terrain sense of al-niʿāl is clear in lexical accounts.

Naʿl in pre-Islamic poetry was thus not confined to footwear; it also described particular ground features.

This does not make Qur’anic naʿlayka mean two patches of land. Each word’s sense depends on its construction and context.

Figurative Uses

Arabic sometimes uses naʿl for a degraded, subordinate person whom others tread upon as they do footwear.

This image draws on position underfoot; humiliation is not part of the basic meaning of a shoe.

Some lexical accounts also record naʿl and naʿlah figuratively for a wife, showing extension into social contexts.

Its registration in a lexicon does not make “wife” preferable in every Arabic text. That reading needs evidence and should not be transferred to Qur’anic verses without it.

3. Qur’anic Usage: Removing Moses’ Footwear

إِنِّي أَنَا رَبُّكَ فَاخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ إِنَّكَ بِالْوَادِ الْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى

innī anā rabbuka fa-khlaʿ naʿlayka innaka bi-al-wādi al-muqaddasi ṭuwan

Translation: “I am your Lord, so take off your sandals. You are in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā.”

Ṭā Hā 20:12

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

In Ṭā Hā 20:10, Moses sees a fire while traveling and tells his family he may bring something from it or find guidance there. In Ṭā Hā 20:11, a voice addresses him when he reaches it.

The God identifies Himself and commands Moses to remove his footwear, connecting this with his presence in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā.

Ṭā Hā 20:13 describes Moses being chosen and instructed to listen to revelation.

The Form Naʿlayka

Naʿlayka contains three grammatical components:

The base naʿl means footwear; -ay- marks the accusative or genitive dual; and -ka expresses their belonging to Moses. Here the word is the command’s object and is accusative. The dual’s nūn is omitted before the attached pronoun.

Its direct meaning is “your two shoes,” naturally rendered “your sandals” in English.

The dual matters: the verse concerns a pair of shoes for two feet. Two spiritual concerns, attachments or stages of knowledge cannot be extracted from it without contextual evidence.

The Verb Fa-khlaʿ

Fa-khlaʿ belongs to KH L ʿ, not N ʿ L.

Khalʿ here concerns detaching or taking off something connected to the body or another object.

Together, the verb and noun make the instruction clear: Moses is to remove the footwear on his feet.

This is an ordinary Arabic construction whose direct understanding requires no assumed figurative meaning.

Naʿl names the object; khalʿ expresses its removal.

Study of KH L ʿ

Fa- also links the command to the preceding statement: the instruction follows the Lord’s self-identification.

Why Was Moses Told to Remove His Footwear?

The verse ends:

إِنَّكَ بِالْوَادِ الْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى

innaka bi-al-wādi al-muqaddasi ṭuwan

“You are in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā.”

This is the clearest Qur’anic contextual indication concerning the command.

The sacred location immediately follows the instruction. The command’s connection with the place’s special standing and sanctity can therefore be inferred from the construction.

The verse nevertheless does not specify the exact practical reason for this instruction.

Historical interpretations offer several explanations: footwear made from a dead animal’s skin and therefore impure; direct contact with the sacred ground; or humility and respect while receiving revelation.

These explanations do not have identical evidential support.

The material claim does not follow from the Qur’an itself. It names no animal, mentions no carrion skin and does not explicitly call the footwear impure.

Respect for the sacred place fits the context more closely, since the valley’s sacredness is immediately stated. The precise custom or manner expressing that respect is still not explained.

The command is therefore connected with Moses’ presence in a sacred place, but a particular unstated reason should not be attributed to the Qur’an as certain.

4. Is Removing the Footwear Symbolic?

Some mystical or allusive readings interpret the two shoes as two attachments or concerns, such as this world and the hereafter, or family and possessions.

They may understand the removal as relinquishing attachments in readiness for undivided attention to The God.

Such readings may have ethical reflective value for some readers, but should remain separate from the verse’s direct meaning.

Linguistically, naʿlayka refers to two shoes. Two follows from the dual form; the noun itself contains no independent reference to two spiritual attachments.

The continuation commissions Moses to listen to revelation, receive signs and go to Pharaoh. None requires his footwear in Ṭā Hā 20:12 to represent the world, family or earlier ideas.

A symbolic reading, if offered, should be labeled a possible reflection, rather than replacing translation and lexical meaning.

Other Qur’anic Accounts of the Event

The Qur’an recalls this event elsewhere.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 says:

فَلَمَّا أَتَاهَا نُودِيَ مِن شَاطِئِ الْوَادِ الْأَيْمَنِ فِي الْبُقْعَةِ الْمُبَارَكَةِ مِنَ الشَّجَرَةِ أَن يَا مُوسَىٰ إِنِّي أَنَا اللَّهُ رَبُّ الْعَالَمِينَ

fa-lammā atāhā nūdiya min shāṭiʾi al-wādi al-aymani fī al-buqʿati al-mubārakati mina al-shajarati an yā mūsā innī anā allāhu rabbu al-ʿālamīna

Translation: “When he reached it, he was called from the right side of the valley, in the blessed place, from the tree: Moses, I am The God, Lord of the worlds.”

This recounts the event with emphasis on the call’s location and divine self-identification, without repeating the footwear instruction.

Al-Naml 27:8 and Al-Naml 27:9 also describe the divine call and self-identification.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:16 says:

إِذْ نَادَاهُ رَبُّهُ بِالْوَادِ الْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى

idh nādāhu rabbuhu bi-al-wādi al-muqaddasi ṭuwan

Translation: “When his Lord called him in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā.”

These verses confirm the event and the sacred location without providing another reason for removing the footwear.

The instruction’s absence elsewhere does not establish that it was unreal or purely symbolic. Accounts of one event may emphasize different aspects.

Ṭā Hā 20:12 explicitly contains the instruction; its direct meaning should be preserved.

A General Rule for Every Sacred Place?

The verse addresses Moses and identifies a particular place: the sacred valley of Ṭuwā.

It conveys respect for a place The God calls sacred. This verse alone does not establish an obligation for everyone in every age to remove footwear in every sacred place.

That generalization requires independent evidence.

Nor does it say that footwear is inherently impure, disrespectful or sinful. It is ordinary protection for the feet, with a particular removal instruction in this account.

The specific command to Moses in a particular setting should be distinguished from a universal rule.

5. Related Words and Roots

N ʿ L and Ḥ DH W: Ḥidhāʾ

Ḥidhāʾ means footwear and also became a general term for types of shoes in later usage.

Early naʿl more often denotes footwear beneath the foot, usually simpler than a fully enclosing shoe.

“Shoe” is therefore natural Persian wording, while “sandal” or “light footwear” can explain its historical form more precisely in certain settings.

This tendency does not make naʿl one fixed sandal design throughout history.

N ʿ L and KH F F: Khuff

Khuff concerns both the foot of certain animals, including camels, and footwear that can cover more of the foot than a naʿl.

As clothing, khuff is often a leather covering enclosing more of the foot than a simple sandal.

Naʿl is usually the lighter footwear or covering beneath the foot.

Their boundaries are not identical in every historical or regional usage. The footwear type and expression’s context matter.

Study of KH F F

N ʿ L and Ḥ F W: Barefootedness

The foot-related ḥafā family concerns having no footwear and being barefoot.

If naʿl covers and protects the foot, ḥāfī describes someone walking without that protection.

The natural result of fa-khlaʿ naʿlayka is Moses taking off his footwear and becoming barefoot.

Barefootedness results from following the command; it is not the independent meaning of naʿl, which remains the object’s name.

Study of Ḥ F W

N ʿ L and KH L ʿ

Khalʿ concerns detaching or removing something, such as taking off clothing.

Naʿl names the object; khalʿ describes removing it.

Together they form a clear expression but remain separate roots.

Detachment and removal should therefore not be transferred from khalʿ into the core of N ʿ L.

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For ordinary نَعْل (naʿl), “shoe,” “sandal” or “footwear” can fit the context.

“Sandal” can explain a simple, light form of footwear, but every occurrence should not be restricted to one design.

نِعَال (niʿāl) means shoes or sandals; اِنْتَعَلَ (intaʿala) means put on footwear.

For animals, “protective hoof covering,” or “horseshoe” for a horse, may fit.

The terrain usage has its own sense: hard, stony ground. Translating that use as shoes is incorrect.

For Ṭā Hā 20:12, Forqan’s existing translation is appropriate:

فَاخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ

fa-khlaʿ naʿlayka

“So take off your sandals.”

It preserves the dual and direct meaning without adding material or an exact reason for the instruction.

For Forqan’s word box:

Brief root meaning: footwear; protection beneath the foot, extended to hoof protection and lower fittings of some objects.

Meaning in Ṭā Hā 20:12: Moses’ two shoes, which he is commanded to remove.

Context can explain his presence in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā. Purity, humility, renouncing the world or readiness for revelation should not be inserted as lexical meanings of naʿl itself.

7. Conclusion

N ʿ L is chiefly associated with footwear beneath the foot, protecting it while walking.

The family also names hoof protection, a lower scabbard fitting and certain hard, stony ground. Figurative uses can draw on humiliation and being trodden upon.

The Qur’an contains only naʿlayka, “your two shoes,” in the direct command to Moses in Ṭā Hā 20:12.

The setting connects that command with the sacred valley of Ṭuwā, but adds no further specification of the footwear’s material, impurity or the precise wisdom behind the instruction.

Dead-animal skin explanations, direct ground contact and symbolic relinquishment of attachments should not be presented as the direct meaning of naʿl.

Nor does this command alone establish a universal rule for every sacred place. Moses’ particular situation and the specific address should remain in view.

Final definition: N ʿ L chiefly denotes footwear placed beneath the foot for protection. Extended uses name certain protective coverings, lower fittings and hard ground. Qur’anic naʿlayka means “your two shoes”; fa-khlaʿ naʿlayka directly commands Moses to remove his footwear in connection with his presence in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā.
