Tears and their flow: distinguishing the visible phenomenon, the reason for weeping, and its moral significance

1. Introduction and semantic field

The triliteral root D M ʿ concerns tears and liquid flowing from the eye. Damʿ names tears, while damʿah means a single drop. Damaʿat al-ʿaynu is used when the eye sheds tears.

This meaning was clear and familiar in early Arabic.

However, the family was not confined to human tears. Dictionaries also record moisture seeping from the ground, falling raindrops, sap exuding from plants, and a vessel overflowing.

These uses share a visible image: liquid collects somewhere or emerges from a place, becoming apparent as drops or a stream.

This does not mean that damʿ simply denotes any flowing liquid in every use. Its principal familiar meaning is tears; the rain, plant, and vessel uses relate to that image.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times as al-damʿi.

Al-Māʾidah 5:83 describes people whose eyes overflow with tears on hearing what was revealed to the Messenger, because they recognize the truth.

Al-Tawbah 9:92 describes people seeking the means to travel and participate, but lacking the necessary resources. They turn away sorrowfully, their eyes streaming with tears.

Both verses describe the same visible phenomenon: tears flowing from the eyes.

Their causes, however, differ.

The first concerns recognizing and responding to the truth; the second, sorrow at being unable to obtain the means to participate.

The root itself therefore does not mean faith, recognition of truth, sorrow, remorse, or fear. Tears may accompany these states, but their cause is identified by context.

Tears alone do not conclusively establish sincerity or the correctness of a person’s position. In Joseph’s story, the Qur’an also describes people coming to their father weeping, although their account of Joseph was untrue.

Tears themselves, their cause, and the moral assessment of a person’s conduct must therefore be distinguished.

2. Forms in the Qur’an and early Arabic

The Qur’anic form

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an, in two verses, only as a noun:

Al-damʿi — Al-Māʾidah 5:83

Al-damʿi — Al-Tawbah 9:92

Both occurrences are definite and genitive, following the preposition min.

Al- is the definite article, not part of the root.

Min is also a separate preposition.

The underlying form being studied is therefore damʿ.

No independent verb such as damaʿat, nor any form such as damʿah, dumūʿ, or dāmiʿah, occurs from this root in the Qur’an.

This distinction matters when counting occurrences. The many Persian and Arabic words concerning tears must not be counted as Qur’anic D M ʿ occurrences without attention to their actual morphology.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two nouns in Al-Māʾidah and Al-Tawbah

Familiar Arabic forms

Dictionaries record the following important forms:

Damʿ: tears; a generic noun for the liquid issuing from the eye.

Damʿah: a single teardrop.

Dumūʿ and admuʿ: recorded plurals of damʿ.

Damaʿat al-ʿaynu: the eye shed tears.

Damiʿat al-ʿaynu: another vocalization of the verb recorded in certain varieties of Arabic.

Damaʿān and dumūʿ: verbal nouns concerning the shedding of tears.

Dāmiʿ: tearful or shedding tears.

Damūʿ and dammāʿah: a person or eye that sheds tears abundantly or readily.

Damiʿah and damīʿ: descriptions of someone who readily sheds tears or weeps much.

Madmaʿ: a place where tears collect or a channel along which they flow around the eye.

Madāmiʿ: the plural of madmaʿ; in dictionaries, regions and corners of the eye associated with the flow of tears.

Dumāʿ and dimāʿ: these vocalizations require separate treatment in their particular uses. Dumāʿ denotes a tear trace on the cheek or eye fluid in differing explanations; dimāʿ denotes a brand along the tear track on a camel’s face. Both dumāʿ and dummāʿ are also recorded for vine exudation. These meanings should not be conflated without context.

Admaʿa al-ināʾa: he filled the vessel until its contents overflowed.

These forms are established in Arabic, but they do not all mean the same thing.

Damʿah, for example, is a teardrop, madmaʿ its associated place or channel, and admaʿa al-ināʾa an extended use concerning a vessel overflowing.

All derivatives should therefore not simply be translated as “weeping.”

3. The semantic core in early dictionaries

Ibn Fāris’s definition

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris writes:

الدَّالُ وَالْمِيمُ وَالْعَيْنُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى مَاءٍ أَوْ عَبْرَةٍ

al-dālu wa-al-mīmu wa-al-ʿaynu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā māʾin aw ʿabratin

“Dāl, mīm, and ʿayn have a single basic sense indicating water or tears.”

He then explains:

فَمِنْ ذَلِكَ الدَّمْعُ مَاءُ الْعَيْنِ، وَالْقَطْرَةُ دَمْعَةٌ

fa-min dhālika al-damʿu māʾu al-ʿayni wa-al-qaṭratu damʿatun

“From this family is damʿ, the water of the eye; a drop of it is a damʿah.”

This definition establishes two points.

First, damʿ denotes a particular liquid: the water of the eye.

Second, the name for tears is distinguished from a single drop.

Just as Arabic can name water generally and identify one drop separately, damʿ may name the liquid while damʿah denotes a particular drop.

The expression māʾ al-ʿayn, however, does not establish that tears have exactly the same natural composition as pure water.

This is a linguistic definition based on observable experience, not a detailed biological description.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: tears, madmaʿ, and dumāʿ variants

Al-Jawharī and Ibn Manẓūr

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ states:

الدَّمْعُ: دَمْعُ الْعَيْنِ، وَالدَّمْعَةُ الْقَطْرَةُ مِنْهُ

al-damʿu damʿu al-ʿayni wa-al-damʿatu al-qaṭratu minhu

“Damʿ is the tears of the eye; a damʿah is a drop of it.”

Lisān al-ʿArab gives the same definition and records the plurals admuʿ and dumūʿ.

Ibn Manẓūr then discusses the verbal variants damaʿat and damiʿat.

These authoritative dictionaries thus show no substantial disagreement about the basic meaning of tears.

Differences mainly concern certain morphological forms and subsidiary uses.

Qur’anic translation consequently need not replace the simple, clear meaning “tears” with a more complicated term.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: definitions and verbal forms

Tears and the act of weeping

Linguistically, tears and weeping are closely related, but they are not identical.

Damʿ names the liquid issuing from the eye.

Weeping, by contrast, is behavior that may involve tears, facial changes, sound, or other signs.

A person may shed tears silently.

A person may also weep aloud.

The name for tears does not necessarily indicate the sound of crying or the intensity of the response.

This matters in the Qur’an: both occurrences of al-damʿ concern eyes and flowing tears, rather than the verb bakā.

“Shed tears” therefore preserves the verse’s direct image better than an expression saying only “wept.”

4. Concrete uses and semantic extension in early Arabic

A tearful eye

Arabic ʿaynun dāmiʿatun denotes an eye shedding tears.

ʿAynun damūʿun also describes an eye whose tears are abundant or readily flow.

Here the adjective describes the eye.

A tearful eye alone does not establish that its owner is sad, happy, afraid, or a believer.

Tears may have various causes.

In ordinary speech, a person may shed tears from sorrow, joy, emotional response, or even a physical stimulus.

The lexical meaning of damʿ remains the same in all these circumstances.

Madmaʿ and the location of tears

In Ibn Fāris’s report, Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad defines madmaʿ as the place where tears collect around the eye.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains madāmiʿ in terms of the eye’s corners and tear channels.

This is significant: the family names not only a liquid but also its associated place or channel.

Early lexical terms for the eye’s regions should not, without separate investigation, be equated with every specific component of the lacrimal system in modern anatomy.

The lexical definition identifies the observable location of tears.

Water seeping from the ground

Dictionaries use tharan damūʿun or tharan dammāʿun for ground that exudes moisture or nearly produces drops of water.

The ground is here likened to an eye.

Just as tears emerge from the eye, moisture can become visible on the ground’s surface.

This does not mean that the soil literally weeps or possesses human feelings.

The image of tears is transferred to a natural phenomenon.

Such a description does not invariably imply a spring or river; slight dampness or a few drops may be intended.

Rain and falling drops

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

“Damaʿa al-maṭaru.”

This means that rain flowed or fell in drops.

Yawmun dammāʿun is also recorded for a day of drizzle and moisture.

Here raindrops are compared to tears.

Damʿ should nevertheless not be taken to mean rain in every situation.

In both Qur’anic occurrences, the subject is explicitly aʿyun, “eyes,” so the meaning of tears is clear.

Plant sap and the grapevine

Early sources record both dumāʿ al-karm and dummāʿ al-karm for liquid exuding from the grapevine in spring. Ibn Fāris records both the light and doubled consonant, while Tāj al-ʿArūs explains the differing vocalizations in the sources.

This use also relates to the image of tears seeping out.

Liquid emerges from part of the plant and may appear in drops.

The grapevine does not literally have eyes or sorrow here.

Nor should this liquid be equated without investigation with pure water, fruit juice, or a drink made from grapes.

The word concerns liquid exuding from the plant.

Tāj al-ʿArūs and Lisān al-ʿArab: vine exudation and vocalization variants

A vessel overflowing

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَأَدْمَعَ الْإِنَاءَ إِذَا مَلَأَهُ حَتَّى يَفِيضَ

wa-admaʿa al-ināʾa idhā malaʾahu ḥattā yafīḍa

“He admaʿa the vessel when he filled it until it overflowed.”

Qadaḥun damʿānu is also recorded for a vessel so full that liquid runs down its sides.

This use helps explain the relationship between damʿ and fayḍ.

The two roots, however, are distinct.

In its principal use, damʿ names tears.

Fayḍ concerns overflowing and flowing as a result of abundance.

A vessel may overflow with water or an eye brim with tears.

Both involve overflow, but the liquid and its location differ.

This distinction must be maintained in Al-Māʾidah 5:83 and Al-Tawbah 9:92.

Lisān al-ʿArab: overflowing vessels and moist ground

5. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

Tears in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says in his famous Muʿallaqah:

فَفَاضَتْ دُمُوعُ الْعَيْنِ مِنِّي صَبَابَةً
عَلَى النَّحْرِ حَتَّى بَلَّ دَمْعِيَ مِحْمَلِي

fa-fāḍat dumūʿu al-ʿayni minnī ṣabābatan / ʿalā al-naḥri ḥattā balla damʿiya miḥmalī

“Then the tears of my eyes poured forth from longing and attachment, flowing onto my chest until my tears soaked my sword’s shoulder strap.”

The verse contains both dumūʿ and damʿī.

Ṣabābah identifies the emotional cause of the tears.

Fāḍat expresses their flow and abundance.

Three concepts are thus separate: the tears, their flow, and their emotional cause.

This witness closely resembles the construction in the two Qur’anic verses.

The Qur’an likewise places tafīḍu alongside al-damʿ, while identifying the cause of tears separately in each verse.

In the Muʿallaqah, the cause is expressed by ṣabābah.

Al-Māʾidah 5:83 concerns recognition of the truth.

Al-Tawbah 9:92 states sorrow.

Shared words and the image of flowing tears therefore do not make their causes identical.

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary on Imruʾ al-Qays: ṣabābah and miḥmal

Tears and generosity in Labīd’s poetry

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says:

وَلَكِنَّ مَالِي غَالَهُ كُلُّ جَفْنَةٍ
إِذَا حَانَ وِرْدٌ أَسْبَلَتْ بِدُمُوعِ

wa-lākinna mālī ghālahu kullu jafnatin / idhā ḥāna wirdun asbalat bi-dumūʿi

“But every great serving bowl has consumed my wealth: when guests arrive, its plentiful food sends fat streaming down like tears.”

Here dumūʿ does not describe actual tears from an eye.

The poet likens fat flowing from a bowl full of food to tears.

The image portrays generosity and hospitality.

A concrete image of tears is used to describe another liquid.

This witness thus shows that figurative dumūʿ was familiar in Arabic.

It does not mean that damʿ always denotes food fat or abundant wealth.

Labīd lived before and after Islam. This particular verse’s date is uncertain, so it is better treated as evidence of early Arabic without assigning it a definite date.

Al-Muḥkam: Labīd’s witness and fat flowing from the serving bowl

The value of poetic witnesses

Both witnesses help establish the root’s linguistic field.

Imruʾ al-Qays’s poem illustrates actual tears and their flow.

Labīd’s poem shows the figurative extension of tears to another liquid.

Poetry alone, however, does not determine why tears flow in each Qur’anic verse.

The cause and significance of people’s responses must be understood from the verses themselves.

6. The first Qur’anic use: Al-Māʾidah 5:83

Al-Māʾidah 5:83

وَإِذَا سَمِعُوا مَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَى الرَّسُولِ تَرَىٰ أَعْيُنَهُمْ تَفِيضُ مِنَ الدَّمْعِ مِمَّا عَرَفُوا مِنَ الْحَقِّ يَقُولُونَ رَبَّنَا آمَنَّا فَاكْتُبْنَا مَعَ الشَّاهِدِينَ

wa-idhā samiʿū mā unzila ilā al-rasūli tarā aʿyunahum tafīḍu mina al-damʿi mimmā ʿarafū mina al-ḥaqqi yaqūlūna rabbanā āmannā fa-uktubnā maʿa al-shāhidīna

“When they hear what has been sent down to the Messenger, you see their eyes overflowing with tears because of the truth they have recognized. They say: Our Lord, we believe; so record us among the witnesses.”

Al-damʿi is a genitive noun here.

Aʿyunahum identifies their eyes.

Tafīḍu expresses flowing and overflowing.

Mimmā ʿarafū mina al-ḥaqqi connects the cause of their response with recognizing the truth.

Damʿ itself therefore means only tears. Recognition of truth and overflowing are expressed by separate words.

Context: people who are not arrogant

In Al-Māʾidah 5:82, the Qur’an mentions people who call themselves Christians.

That verse mentions priests and monks among them, and their lack of arrogance.

Verse 83 then describes their response on hearing the revelation.

This connection matters.

Their tears appear in the context of hearing, recognizing the truth, humility, and professing faith.

The Qur’an therefore does not make mere moisture in the eyes a criterion independent of people’s understanding and conduct.

Nor should this verse be taken to mean that everyone who calls themselves Christian responds identically to every religious statement.

The verse gives a group’s characteristics and then describes their conduct.

Their lack of arrogance should also not be reduced to a merely claimed inward feeling. Hearing and accepting the truth stand alongside it in this context.

Tafīḍu mina al-damʿ: why do the eyes overflow?

In ordinary speech, tears flow from the eye.

Here, however, the Qur’an says that their eyes tafīḍu mina al-damʿ.

This expression can emphasize the intensity of the flow, as though the eyes have filled with tears until they overflow.

Early grammatical explanations differ concerning the role of min.

Some understand it as identifying the substance or contents with which the eyes overflow.

Others explain it in relation to the origin or cause of the tears’ flow.

In all these readings, the clear sense is abundant tears flowing from the eyes.

“Their eyes overflow with tears” is therefore appropriate.

Damʿ itself, however, does not mean overflowing. That image arises from its combination with tafīḍu.

Al-Tibyān fī Iʿrāb al-Qurʾān: min in Al-Māʾidah 5:83

Mimmā ʿarafū mina al-ḥaqq: the cause of the tears

The Qur’an explicitly states the cause of their response:

“Mimmā ʿarafū mina al-ḥaqqi.”

Their tears in this context thus relate to recognizing the truth.

This does not imply that everyone recognizing a truth must necessarily shed tears.

People differ in their emotional responses to understanding and being moved.

Someone may be deeply moved by recognizing the truth without visible tears.

Someone may also shed tears on hearing moving speech without yet understanding its content correctly.

Tears alone are therefore no conclusive measure of understanding or faith.

In this verse, tears accompany recognition and a profession of faith. The response’s significance emerges from that combination.

Tears and professing faith

The verse ends:

“Rabbanā āmannā fa-uktubnā maʿa al-shāhidīna.”

In the following verse, Al-Māʾidah 5:84, they also speak of believing in God and in the truth that has reached them.

Their response is therefore not confined to tears.

Hearing, recognizing the truth, professing faith, and asking to be counted among the witnesses form a connected whole.

The root D M ʿ itself, however, must not be defined as believing or bearing witness.

These are independent actions and positions expressed in the same context.

Al-Ṭabarī: tears, hearing revelation, and recognizing truth

7. The second Qur’anic use: Al-Tawbah 9:92

Al-Tawbah 9:92

وَلَا عَلَى الَّذِينَ إِذَا مَا أَتَوْكَ لِتَحْمِلَهُمْ قُلْتَ لَا أَجِدُ مَا أَحْمِلُكُمْ عَلَيْهِ تَوَلَّوْا وَأَعْيُنُهُمْ تَفِيضُ مِنَ الدَّمْعِ حَزَنًا أَلَّا يَجِدُوا مَا يُنْفِقُونَ

wa-lā ʿalā alladhīna idhā mā atawka li-taḥmilahum qulta lā ajidu mā aḥmilukum ʿalayhi tawallaw wa-aʿyunuhum tafīḍu mina al-damʿi ḥazanan allā yajidū mā yunfiqūna

“Nor upon those who, when they came to you for mounts and you said, ‘I find nothing on which to carry you,’ turned away, their eyes streaming with tears from sorrow at finding nothing to spend.”

Here too al-damʿi follows min and occurs with tafīḍu.

Its verbal image is very close to Al-Māʾidah 5:83.

The cause of tears, however, differs here.

Inability to obtain transport

The verse begins with people coming to Muḥammad to obtain a mount or means of travel.

The answer they receive, however, is:

“Lā ajidu mā aḥmilukum ʿalayhi.”

No means of carrying them is available.

They then turn away.

Their sorrow is subsequently connected with finding nothing to spend toward the necessary costs.

These tears therefore do not simply arise from an unfulfilled personal wish or loss of comfort.

The context introduces people who seek to participate but lack the means.

Damʿ itself, nevertheless, does not mean poverty or financial inability.

That situation is established by the verse’s other expressions.

Ḥazanan: the cause of the tears

The word ḥazanan appears explicitly in this verse.

It belongs to Ḥ Z N and concerns sorrow.

The tears here are therefore caused by sorrow concerning the inability to obtain the necessary resources.

Tears and sorrow are nevertheless not identical.

Tears are a phenomenon issuing from the eye.

Sorrow is an emotional state that may cause tears to flow.

The root D M ʿ should therefore not be defined simply as grief and sorrow.

Al-Māʾidah 5:83 also mentions tears, but contains no ḥazanan.

This distinction shows how the Qur’an uses one word for two different emotional responses in separate contexts.

The preceding verse: no blame for those unable to participate

Al-Tawbah 9:91 mentions the weak, the sick, and those who cannot find the means to spend.

In that setting it removes blame, provided that they remain sincere and well-disposed.

Al-Tawbah 9:92 introduces a closely related example: people intending to participate but lacking transport and resources.

Their tears thus occur in a context of genuine inability and willingness to participate.

Tears themselves, however, are not a condition for removing blame.

The preceding verse concerns inability and sincerity, not a requirement to weep before one’s excuse is accepted.

One should therefore not conclude that an incapable person is excused only when their tears are visible.

The relationship with Al-Tawbah 9:93

Al-Tawbah 9:93 introduces another group who refrain from participation despite being wealthy.

This contrast matters.

In verse 92, people want to participate but lack resources.

In verse 93, people stay behind despite their wealth.

Moral assessment is therefore not based only on the visible outcome of participating or not participating.

People’s ability, intention, and conduct also matter in context.

Again, however, all these meanings must not be assigned to D M ʿ itself.

Tears in verse 92 indicate their emotional state; the actual conditions of responsibility emerge from the surrounding verses together.

Al-Ṭabarī: lack of transport and the sorrow of those unable to participate

8. Comparing the two Qur’anic uses

The two verses have closely similar basic constructions:

Al-Māʾidah 5:83

تَرَىٰ أَعْيُنَهُمْ تَفِيضُ مِنَ الدَّمْعِ

tarā aʿyunahum tafīḍu mina al-damʿi

Al-Tawbah 9:92

وَأَعْيُنُهُمْ تَفِيضُ مِنَ الدَّمْعِ

wa-aʿyunuhum tafīḍu mina al-damʿi

Both identify the eyes as the place where tears become visible.

Both use tafīḍu to express abundant flow.

In both, al-damʿ is the familiar liquid of tears.

There are, however, two principal distinctions.

First, in Al-Māʾidah, the cause is recognizing the truth.

Second, in Al-Tawbah, the cause is sorrow at being unable to obtain the necessary means.

The word “tears” does not, in itself, determine a particular psychological state.

In Al-Māʾidah tears also accompany a profession of faith; in Al-Tawbah, a sorrowful departure from a situation in which participation has not become possible.

Each scene’s significance and message thus emerge from the combination of conduct and circumstances.

Are tears always a sign of faith and sincerity?

No. The Qur’an itself supplies an example preventing such a generalization.

Yūsuf 12:16 states:

وَجَاءُوا أَبَاهُمْ عِشَاءً يَبْكُونَ

wa-jāʾū abāhum ʿishāʾan yabkūna

“They came to their father in the evening, weeping.”

This verse belongs to the story of Joseph’s brothers.

They come to their father weeping and report Joseph’s fate in a way inconsistent with their actual conduct.

Weeping here therefore does not, by itself, establish the truth of their words.

Yūsuf 12:16 does, of course, use the verb yabkūna rather than damʿ.

It is consequently not a direct occurrence of D M ʿ, although it matters when examining tears, weeping, and sincerity.

One should therefore not judge someone’s truthfulness or falsehood conclusively from tears alone.

Tears can accompany sincerity, but assessing sincerity also requires evidence from conduct and the truth of what is said.

Does the absence of tears indicate hardness of heart?

No such rule follows from the two verses under discussion.

People differ in their emotional and physical responses.

Someone may be deeply moved without visible tears.

Someone may also shed tears because of a physical condition or environmental stimulus, without a particular emotional experience.

Tears should therefore not be treated as a conclusive measure of faith, or their absence as a conclusive measure of unbelief.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:83, the Qur’an itself identifies the cause; in Al-Tawbah 9:92, the sorrow and inability are also clear.

Judging others’ tears in unfamiliar circumstances requires comparable understanding.

9. Distinguishing D M ʿ from related words

D M ʿ (damʿ) and B K Y (bakā): tears and weeping

Damʿ names tears.

Bukāʾ and its verbal forms concern weeping.

They accompany one another in many situations, but are not complete synonyms.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:109 describes people falling upon their chins, weeping, and increasing in humility.

Maryam 19:58 likewise places prostration and weeping together.

These verses concern emotional and behavioral responses, but do not contain the word damʿ.

B K Y and D M ʿ must therefore remain separate in the root index.

Shedding tears does not necessarily reveal whether crying is audible.

Study of B K Y

D M ʿ (damʿ) and F Y Ḍ (fāḍa): tears and overflowing

These families occur alongside each other in both Qur’anic D M ʿ occurrences.

Damʿ names the liquid.

Fayḍ concerns flowing and overflowing.

In tafīḍu mina al-damʿ, their combination creates an image of abundant tears.

Damʿ itself does not mean overflowing, just as fayḍ is not confined to tears.

Water may overflow from a vessel, or tears flow abundantly from an eye.

Translation should therefore preferably preserve both tears and their flow or overflow.

Study of F Y Ḍ

D M ʿ (damʿ) and DH R F (dharafa): tears and their shedding

Arabic dharf concerns falling or flowing, particularly tears.

Dharafat al-ʿaynu damʿahā refers to tears flowing from the eye.

Here dharf expresses the shedding, while damʿ names what flows.

Their semantic roles thus differ.

Nor does the use of dharf in a text establish that every instance of tears must flow with that same intensity or manner.

D M ʿ (damʿ) and S J M (sajama): tears and continuous flow

Arabic sajm concerns liquid pouring and flowing.

Dictionaries record this family for both rain and tears.

Damʿ, in its principal meaning, is the tears themselves.

Sajm can emphasize the pouring or continuity of the flow.

An eye may therefore have tears whose manner of flowing is described by another verb such as sajama.

This relationship does not make the roots identical.

D M ʿ (damʿ) and ʿ B R (ʿabrah): tears and emotional response

Arabic ʿabrah, with fatḥah on ʿayn, concerns tears and weeping in one of its uses. Its vocalization and use should be distinguished from ʿibrah, with kasrah, meaning a lesson or admonition.

Ibn Fāris also uses ʿabrah when defining damʿ.

The ʿ B R family has other uses, including passing, crossing, and taking a lesson.

Its entire semantic field should therefore not be confined to tears.

Learning from an event is also not identical to shedding tears because of it.

A person may learn from an event without weeping.

The proximity of some ʿabrah and damʿ uses must therefore not lead to identifying the families’ entire semantic fields.

D M ʿ (damʿ) and Ḥ Z N (ḥazina): tears and sorrow

Al-Tawbah 9:92 places al-damʿ and ḥazanan in the same sentence.

Their association shows that sorrow can cause tears.

Tears and sorrow nevertheless remain independent concepts.

Al-Māʾidah 5:83 also mentions tears, but explains their cause through recognition of the truth.

Damʿ itself therefore cannot be translated as sorrow.

Sorrow can also exist without visible tears.

Study of Ḥ Z N

D M ʿ (damʿ) and D M W (dam): tears and blood

Their letters are similar, but these are distinct roots.

D M ʿ has a final ʿayn and concerns tears in its principal use.

D M W is the Qur’anic index’s family for dam, “blood.”

Early Arabic also records shajjatun dāmiʿatun for a type of wound from which reddish fluid or blood issues.

Dictionaries differ in explaining the distinction between dāmiyah and dāmiʿah in wound classification.

This technical, figurative use does not identify tears with blood.

In the two Qur’anic verses, al-damʿ concerns eyes and means tears.

It should not be changed to blood or bleeding.

Study of D M W

D M ʿ (damʿ) and ʿ Y N (ʿayn): tears and the eye

Both verses place aʿyun alongside al-damʿ.

Aʿyun is the plural of ʿayn and belongs to ʿ Y N.

Damʿ names the liquid issuing from the eye.

Their subject matter is related, but their roots differ.

Morphological analysis must therefore not classify aʿyun as a derivative of D M ʿ.

The eye also has other Qur’anic meanings and uses requiring examination in its own root study.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

Only al-damʿi occurs from this root in the Qur’an.

Its principal, natural equivalent is “tears”; the Persian equivalent is ashk.

This equivalent suits both verses; the word’s own meaning need not change.

Al-Māʾidah 5:83

تَرَىٰ أَعْيُنَهُمْ تَفِيضُ مِنَ الدَّمْعِ

An appropriate rendering is:

“You see their eyes overflowing with tears.”

This preserves aʿyun and conveys the image of tafīḍu.

“Their eyes shed tears” is also natural, but conveys the emphasis on overflow less strongly.

“They weep from the intensity of faith” is an interpretive explanation: the cause is expressed separately by mimmā ʿarafū mina al-ḥaqqi.

Al-damʿ should also not be translated as truth, faith, or humility.

Forqan’s existing Persian “their eyes overflow with tears” is appropriate.

Al-Tawbah 9:92

وَأَعْيُنُهُمْ تَفِيضُ مِنَ الدَّمْعِ حَزَنًا

An appropriate rendering is:

“While their eyes streamed with tears from sorrow.”

Here ḥazanan expresses the emotional cause.

“Streamed with tears” naturally renders tafīḍu mina al-damʿ.

To emphasize the Arabic image’s intensity, one could say:

“While their eyes overflowed with tears from sorrow.”

The existing complete Persian translation in Forqan is nevertheless fluent and preserves the principal meaning.

There is therefore no need to change it.

Non-Qur’anic equivalents

Damʿ: tears.

Damʿah: a single teardrop.

Dumūʿ: tears, in the plural.

Damaʿat al-ʿaynu: the eye shed tears.

ʿAynun dāmiʿatun: a tearful eye.

ʿAynun damūʿun: an eye that sheds tears abundantly or readily.

Madmaʿ: a place where tears collect or a channel along which they flow.

Admaʿa al-ināʾa: he filled the vessel until it overflowed.

Tharan damūʿun: ground or soil exuding moisture.

These equivalents must be chosen contextually. Figurative rain and plant uses should not replace the clear meaning of tears in translating the two Qur’anic verses.

11. Conclusion

In Arabic, D M ʿ denotes tears and liquid issuing from the eye.

Ibn Fāris explains it through māʾ al-ʿayn and ʿabrah. Al-Jawharī and Ibn Manẓūr likewise define damʿ as tears and damʿah as a single drop.

Early Arabic also records moisture seeping from the ground, falling rain, plant exudation, and a vessel overflowing.

These uses relate to the image of tears and flowing liquid, without reducing the word’s principal meaning to any liquid or discharge.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times as al-damʿ.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:83, tears appear in the context of hearing revelation, recognizing truth, and professing faith.

In Al-Tawbah 9:92, tears appear in the context of sorrow and inability to obtain the resources needed to participate.

In both verses, tafīḍu expresses the intensity and flow of tears, but that verb belongs to the independent root F Y Ḍ.

Damʿ itself therefore does not mean overflowing.

The tears’ causes also differ: recognition of truth in one verse, sorrow in the other.

This distinction shows that tears have no fixed moral significance in themselves.

Weeping may accompany sincerity, understanding, sorrow, joy, or other states. Its cause and significance must be assessed through a person’s words, conduct, and circumstances.

In Joseph’s story too, the brothers’ arrival before their father weeping did not establish the truth of their report.

Tears therefore cannot conclusively measure faith, sincerity, or righteousness, just as their absence does not in itself establish hardness of heart or unbelief.

Proposed final definition: D M ʿ, in its familiar core, denotes tears: liquid issuing from the eye. Related concrete imagery extends to seeping, dripping, and overflowing of other liquids. In both Qur’anic occurrences, al-damʿ means tears. Their cause, however, is recognition of the truth in Al-Māʾidah 5:83, and sorrow at being unable to obtain the necessary resources in Al-Tawbah 9:92. The root itself therefore determines neither the emotional cause of tears nor their moral significance. Brief root meaning: Tears and their flow; in extended uses, liquid seeping and overflowing.
