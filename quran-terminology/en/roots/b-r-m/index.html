1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

In one of its principal Arabic uses, B R M concerns making something firm and secure. The sense is familiar in twisting a rope and also occurs in speech about decisions and plans. In abrama al-amr, someone makes an undertaking or decision firm and settled; the verb alone does not establish that it is well considered or right.

Within this family, ibrām denotes making something firm or settled, and mubrim describes the person doing so.

The Arabic family is not confined to this sense. Older lexicographers also record weariness and irritation, the mingling of two colors, names of plants and their fruits, and everyday implements. These uses deserve recognition, but should not all be reduced to firm decision-making without evidence.

B R M occurs twice in the Qur’an, both times in one verse:

  • أَبْرَمُوا (abramū): form IV active perfect verb, third-person masculine plural.
  • مُبْرِمُونَ (mubrimūn): sound masculine plural active participle of the same form.

Both occur in Al-Zukhruf 43:79. Other forms such as barama, ibrām, mubram, barima, and tabarrama do not occur in the Qur’an.

The verse uses the root twice in a contrast: first of people making an undertaking firm, then of The God firmly determining His plan. The lexical sense remains in both parts, while the agent, direction, and outcome differ.

2. The semantic core and older Arabic usage

Making a rope firm

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad, as quoted by Ibn Fāris in Maqāyīs al-lughah, explains the use concerning an undertaking as follows:

«أَبْرَمْتُ الْأَمْرَ أَحْكَمْتُهُ»

abramtu al-amra aḥkamtuhu

Translation: “I made the undertaking firm and secure.”

Ibn Fāris also writes concerning a rope:

«أَبْرَمْتُ الْحَبْلَ إِذَا فَتَلْتُهُ مَتِينًا»

abramtu al-ḥabla idhā fataltuhu matīnan

Translation: “I abrama the rope: I twisted it firmly.”

In this use, strands are twisted to form a strong, resistant bond. A well-twisted rope contrasts with a loose cord or thread not yet sufficiently strengthened.

Lisān al-ʿArab gives a more precise account. Abū Ḥanīfah al-Dīnawarī describes a method of ibrām in which a cord is formed from two strands and then twisted together. Mubram and barīm are recorded for rope made by joining twisted strands.

The image illuminates the relationship between the physical use and the use concerning decisions.

Just as twisting strengthens a loose cord, a decision may reach firmness and resolution after consideration and planning.

This relationship does not establish that every instance of abrama necessarily involves several stages of consultation, agreement among several people, or combining several decisions. Those details belong to the rope image, rather than the verb’s necessary meaning.

Even a twisted rope may eventually unravel or break. Lexical ibrām means making firm, rather than an absolute guarantee that something will never come apart.

Mubram and saḥīl

Older Arabic uses saḥīl for a thread that has not become a firmly twisted cord of several strands. Mubram, by contrast, describes firmly twisted thread or rope.

Ibn Fāris explains the difference through saḥīl consisting of one strand and mubram being twisted from two.

The distinction also appears in pre-Islamic poetry and provides important evidence for the connection between a concrete meaning and the use in decisions and plans.

A witness from Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s Muʿallaqah

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s Muʿallaqah contains the following verse:

يَمِينًا لَنِعْمَ السَّيِّدَانِ وُجِدْتُمَا

yamīnan la-niʿma al-sayyidāni wujidtumā

عَلَى كُلِّ حَالٍ مِنْ سَحِيلٍ وَمُبْرَمِ

ʿalā kulli ḥālin min saḥīlin wa-mubrami

Translation: “By my oath, you two have proved excellent lords in every circumstance, whether easy or difficult.”

The verse praises two men who worked to end the war between ʿAbs and Dhubyān.

Saḥīl and mubram are used figuratively for easy and difficult circumstances.

Saḥīl is thread without the twist and strength of a firm cord; mubram is well twisted and strong. The poet draws on this tangible contrast to express different circumstances.

The older commentary Sharḥ al-qaṣāʾid al-sabʿ al-ṭiwāl al-jāhiliyyāt likewise explains saḥīl through an easy or unsecured undertaking and mubram through a difficult, firm one.

The witness matters historically because it is transmitted in a recognized poem by a pre-Islamic poet. It connects mubram with firmness and difficulty of undoing.

The poetic and Qur’anic uses nevertheless differ. Zuhayr uses mubram descriptively in contrast with saḥīl; Al-Zukhruf 43:79 uses abramū for firmly settling an undertaking.

Firmness is shared, but the Qur’anic verse itself does not mention a rope.

3. Other Arabic branches of the family

Barima: becoming weary or irritated

In Maqāyīs al-lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies four semantic fields: making firm, weariness and inability, the mingling of two colors, and certain plants and their fruits.

For the second branch, he writes:

«بَرِمْتُ بِالْأَمْرِ: عَيِيتُ بِهِ»

barimtu bi-al-amri ʿayītu bihi

That is: “I became weary and unable to cope with the undertaking.”

Al-Khalīl also explains barimtu bi-kadhā through ḍajirtu bihi: becoming irritated or fed up with something.

This use differs from making a decision firm.

Barima bi-al-amr means becoming weary of an undertaking; abrama al-amr means making it firm and settled.

Even abrama in another construction, such as abramanī, can mean making a person weary or irritated. The object is then the person troubled by someone’s conduct.

The consonants or even the verbal form are therefore insufficient to identify a sentence’s meaning. The object, preposition, and setting must also be examined.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:79, the combination of abramū with amran supports the familiar sense of making a decision firm, rather than becoming weary of an undertaking.

Barīm: two strands or mingled colors

Arabic also uses barīm for a cord twisted from two strands, sometimes of different colors.

The term is also recorded for things containing two mingled colors.

Ibn Fāris quotes a verse attributed to ʿAlqamah comparing tears and kohl in the eyes with two mingled colors. Barīmayn there concerns tears and kohl, rather than making a decision firm.

Lexical sources also record barīmu al-ṣubḥ for dawn, when daylight mingles with the night’s remaining darkness.

The image can be compared with a two-colored cord, but does not make Qur’anic ibrām al-amr mean combining two opinions, parties, or decisions.

Barīm also names a cord or ornament worn by women around the waist or upper arm in some uses. The family thus appears in everyday life, dress, and adornment.

Baram: plant parts and fruits

Older Arabic records al-baram and al-barmah for certain stages of flower or fruit on trees such as ṭalḥ, salam, ʿurfuṭ, and arāk.

Ibn Fāris treats this botanical field as a separate branch of B R M.

Lisān al-ʿArab also describes the color, scent, and ripening stages of these fruits.

The available evidence does not show that firmly settling a decision necessarily developed from a flower’s shape, a fruit’s color, or these plants’ growth stages.

The branch matters for the Arabic range, but does not enter the definition of abramū amran.

Burmah: a cooking pot

Burmah names a kind of pot, especially a stone cooking vessel. Its plural is birām.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī attempts to relate the vessel’s name to its firm construction. Ibn Fāris, by contrast, places it among uses whose connection to the family’s other underlying senses is unclear.

The explanation of the pot’s name should therefore not be presented as conclusive historical fact.

Baram: someone refusing to contribute

Lexicons also record baram for someone who does not join others in gambling or its associated expenses, but wants to eat when the food has been provided.

Some older social expressions associate this conduct with stinginess or meanness.

Lisān al-ʿArab records the use with its social setting, and al-Rāghib also mentions the sense.

The report illustrates historical usage rather than endorsing the practice itself. Refusing to gamble is not inherently blameworthy. The conduct condemned in this use is benefiting from others’ expenditure while declining to contribute.

Nor does the use make Qur’anic abramū mean being stingy or refusing to participate.

The Qur’an uses the root in a different construction.

4. The Qur’anic use: firm human resolve and divine planning

Al-Zukhruf 43:79

أَمْ أَبْرَمُوا أَمْرًا فَإِنَّا مُبْرِمُونَ

am abramū amran fa-innā mubrimūna

Proposed translation: “Or have they firmly settled an undertaking? We too are firmly determining Our plan.”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic verse. Its two forms occur in one short sentence and produce a contrast.

Abramū amran

Abramū is a form IV active perfect verb, third-person masculine plural. The final wāw marks its plural subject.

Amran is the verb’s accusative object.

The direct meaning is consequently making an undertaking firm and settled.

Amr should also be distinguished from abrama. Amr belongs to ʾ M R and can denote an undertaking, matter, command, or issue to be decided. Here the sense of decision arises from the noun’s combination with abrama.

Abramū amran thus means that they have firmly decided upon an undertaking or brought it to resolution.

Abramū alone does not establish whether the decision is right, wrong, just, or oppressive. Its moral direction follows from context.

Abrama can accordingly also describe a just contract or beneficial decision. Firmness is not the same as rightness.

Study of ʾ M R

The opening am

The verse begins with am.

The common grammatical analysis treats it as am munqaṭiʿah, moving the discourse to a new question with a force of challenge or warning.

English can convey this with “Or have they …?” or “Rather, have they …?”

The question does more than seek information about a hidden decision. Within divine speech, it exposes the opponents’ conduct and places a warning against their planning.

Am itself does not mean conspiracy or plotting. Such ideas follow from the subject and its connection to neighboring verses.

Fa-innā mubrimūn: two determinations contrasted

Mubrimūn is a sound masculine plural active participle of form IV.

It is the predicate of inna and is nominative with wāw as a sound masculine plural. The pronoun -nā in innā is inna’s subject.

The construction fa-innā mubrimūn emphasizes the firm determination of the undertaking by The God.

The parts differ morphologically: the opponents are described with the perfect verb abramū, whereas the divine statement uses the active participle mubrimūn.

This can help express the firmness of divine resolve and planning. The participle alone does not establish that the action must continue without temporal limit or have no particular time.

In such a construction, an active participle can express a quality, readiness, intention, or occurrence according to context.

The object of mubrimūn is also unexpressed.

The verse does not say that The God strengthens the opponents’ own undertaking for them. The contrast presents His determination as firm in response to theirs.

Repeating the root therefore does not make the subject matter, method, or moral value of both plans identical.

The context clarifies the distinction further.

5. Context: hidden planning and divine knowledge

The verses immediately before and after Al-Zukhruf 43:79 are especially relevant.

Aversion to truth

Al-Zukhruf 43:78

لَقَدْ جِئْنَاكُمْ بِالْحَقِّ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَكُمْ لِلْحَقِّ كَارِهُونَ

la-qad jiʾnākum bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-lākinna aktharakum li-al-ḥaqqi kārihūna

Translation: “We certainly brought you the truth, but most of you were averse to the truth.”

The verse describes some hearers’ aversion to truth.

The next verse asks am abramū amran about an undertaking they have firmly settled.

The connection places that decision in opposition to truth, rather than discussing every human act of planning and deciding.

Al-Ṭabarī relates Al-Zukhruf 43:79 to making a plan firm against the truth and the Prophet. He transmits Qatādah’s ajmaʿū amran, “they resolved upon an undertaking.” From Mujāhid he transmits mujmiʿūn for the second part, with a response to harmful plotting. Ibn Zayd explains abramū through aḥkamū amran, “they made an undertaking firm.”

The older explanations agree in their semantic core: securing, resolving upon, and making an undertaking firm.

Their accounts of the opponents’ precise decision nevertheless differ.

Some stress planning against the Prophet, others persisting in denial of truth, and others intending harm or arranging a hidden plan.

Al-Qurṭubī transmits a report from Muqātil relating the verse to the opponents’ meeting in Dār al-Nadwah and their decision to kill the Prophet. The historical account contains details that the verse itself does not state.

The Qur’anic text names neither Dār al-Nadwah, the participants, nor a particular method of execution.

Such reports may be studied historically, but should not enter abramū’s direct lexical meaning.

What is clearer from context is a firmly settled decision in opposition to truth.

The God knows secrets and private conversation

Al-Zukhruf 43:80

أَمْ يَحْسَبُونَ أَنَّا لَا نَسْمَعُ سِرَّهُمْ وَنَجْوَاهُمْ ۚ بَلَىٰ وَرُسُلُنَا لَدَيْهِمْ يَكْتُبُونَ

am yaḥsabūna annā lā nasmaʿu sirrahum wa-najwāhum balā wa-rusulunā ladayhim yaktubūna

Translation: “Or do they suppose that We do not hear their secrets and private conversation? Indeed We do, and Our messengers are there with them, writing.”

This provides an important contextual clue to the preceding decision.

After mentioning the firm settlement of an undertaking, the Qur’an speaks of the opponents’ secrets and private conversation.

Their decision is thus connected with arrangements that were not necessarily open or public.

Deciding, concealing the decision, and carrying it out should nevertheless be distinguished.

Abramū concerns firmly settling the decision. Sirrahum wa-najwāhum concerns their hidden affairs and conversations. Execution is a separate event that neither expression alone establishes.

Abramū alone therefore does not prove that their plan was carried out or succeeded.

Verse 80, by describing the hearing and recording of secrets, rejects the idea that the planning remains hidden from The God.

The contrast in verse 79 is consequently more than two plans: limited human determination, with an expectation of secrecy, stands against the planning of The God, who knows even what is hidden.

This follows from the three verses together, rather than B R M itself meaning knowledge of the unseen.

Does fa-innā mubrimūn mean plotting?

Some interpretations call the opponents’ plan kayd or makr and explain the divine planning as its response.

In al-Kashshāf, al-Zamakhsharī describes the opponents’ plotting against the Prophet and the planning of The God in response.

This explanation is defensible in context.

Ibrām and makr are nevertheless not lexical synonyms.

Ibrām concerns firmly settling an undertaking. Makr and kayd concern ways of planning and devising, especially in opposition or conflict.

Someone may firmly settle a hidden plan; the act then concerns both ibrām and concealed planning. Makr’s meaning still does not come from B R M itself.

The divine response to wrongful planning also does not equate the opponents’ methods morally with the divine action. The Qur’an can describe both sides through related vocabulary without equating motive, rightness, or outcome.

Mubrimūn should consequently not be rendered “deceivers” or “conspirators.”

The central sense here is firmly determining a plan. Opposition and judgment follow from context.

6. Related roots and distinctions

B R M (ibrām) and Ḥ K M (iḥkām)

Iḥkām is among the concepts closest to ibrām.

Older lexicographers repeatedly explain abramtu al-amr through aḥkamtuhu.

Both can concern an undertaking that has become secure rather than weak or unfinished.

Qur’anic Ḥ K M nevertheless has a broad family covering judgment, decision, wisdom, and firmness. Qur’anic B R M concerns firmly settling a decision.

Not every sense of judgment and wisdom can therefore be transferred into ibrām.

Al-Zukhruf 43:79 clearly concerns firm decision-making, but the verb alone does not mean a judicial judgment or religious ruling.

Study of Ḥ K M

B R M (ibrām) and ʿ Q D (ʿaqd)

Concrete ʿaqd concerns tying and joining, and human uses can concern covenants and contracts.

Concrete ibrām concerns firmly twisting rope, while human uses concern firmly settling a decision or undertaking.

The families can approach one another in contractual language, but are not completely identical.

A covenant may be concluded before a firm decision about its execution. Someone can also strengthen a decision without having concluded a contract with another.

Abramū amran should not therefore be restricted to “they concluded a covenant” without evidence.

Study of ʿ Q D

B R M (ibrām) and ʿ Z M (ʿazm)

ʿAzm concerns serious resolve and intention.

Ibrām overlaps with it in decisions, while emphasizing the image of making an undertaking firm.

The Qur’an uses ʿazm for human determination and persistence and, in some settings, other matters. Ibrām appears only in Al-Zukhruf 43:79.

That limited distribution does not establish that ʿazm is always a first stage and ibrām always the final, irreversible stage of every decision.

Ibrām may place stronger emphasis on settlement in some Arabic constructions; a fixed hierarchy between the roots would require broader evidence.

Study of ʿ Z M

B R M (ibrām) and N Q Ḍ (naqḍ)

These can contrast in contexts of making firm and undoing.

Ibrām of rope means twisting it firmly; naqḍ can mean undoing or unraveling something joined or secured.

Al-Naḥl 16:92 describes a woman undoing her spun thread after strengthening it. The image helps explain the contrast between securing and unraveling.

That verse does not use B R M. The forms should not therefore be identified morphologically.

A human decision can likewise be made firm and later abandoned. Ibrām expresses strengthening, rather than making every decision it describes inherently irreversible.

Study of N Q Ḍ

B R M (ibrām) and F T L (fatl)

Fatl concerns twisting strands or winding something.

Ibrām can refer to the firmness of that twisting, but Qur’anic ibrām concerns a decision rather than a physical cord.

“Twisting a plan” may illustrate the lexical image in explanation; “they twisted their affair” is not a natural direct English rendering of the verse.

English should express firmly settling the decision while explaining the rope image separately.

Study of F T L

B R M (ibrām) and K Y D (kayd)

Kayd concerns devising and planning, especially in opposition.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:79, opposition to truth and private conversations support the possibility of hostile planning.

Devising a plan and firmly settling an undertaking are nevertheless not identical acts.

Someone may be planning without having reached a firm decision. A decision can also be firmly settled without secrecy or hostility.

A base translation should consequently retain firm determination and explain the kind of plan through context.

Study of K Y D

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Natural equivalents for ibrām concerning an undertaking or decision include “making firm,” “settling,” and “firmly determining.”

“Reaching a firm decision” is also suitable, especially with amr as the object.

“Planning” alone does not convey the whole sense. Someone can devise a plan without yet settling upon it.

“Making doubly sure” has a broad, sometimes conversational English use, but is not especially suitable as a direct rendering here.

The following distinctions matter for the Qur’anic forms:

Abramū is a perfect verb and may be rendered “they made firm” or “they firmly settled.”

Mubrimūn is a plural active participle concerning making an undertaking firm or settled. English should preserve the action without forcing an awkward noun phrase such as “the firm determiners of decisions.”

Two renderings of Al-Zukhruf 43:79 can be defended.

A rendering closer to the wording:

“Or have they firmly settled an undertaking? We too are making firm.”

This retains repetition of the root’s meaning, while leaving the second clause’s object unexpressed.

A more natural rendering in context:

“Or have they reached a firm decision? We too are firmly determining Our plan.”

“Our plan” clarifies the participle’s omitted object. It suits the context and older explanations, but no independent noun meaning “plan” occurs in the Arabic second clause.

The first option is preferable if preserving the wording’s structure is the priority. The second is clearer where naturalness and clarification of the English sentence are also criteria.

Neither permits adding “a plan to kill the Prophet” or a particular punishment to the direct translation, since the verse states neither detail.

8. Conclusion

Older Arabic B R M has several semantic fields; its Qur’anic branch concerns making an undertaking firm and secure.

Concrete abrama al-ḥabl means firmly twisting rope. Pre-Islamic poetry, especially Zuhayr’s contrast between saḥīl and mubram, shows that the distinction between a loose thread and a firm cord was familiar.

Within that field, abrama al-amr expresses firmly settling a decision.

The lexical family also includes weariness, mingled colors, plant and fruit names, a stone pot, and expressions about social participation. Shared consonants alone do not transfer these senses into the verse.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both in Al-Zukhruf 43:79.

Abramū amran first concerns the opponents’ firm decision. Fa-innā mubrimūn then contrasts this with the firmness of divine planning.

The neighboring verses place that decision in a context of aversion to truth and private conversations. Abramū itself nevertheless names neither the plan, its execution, nor a specific historical outcome.

The contrast also does not equate the opponents’ action morally or in nature with the planning of The God. Making firm is shared, while context identifies each agent, direction, and standing.

Firmness of decision is not itself evidence of rightness. People can resolve firmly upon wrongdoing. Ibrām’s lexical meaning should therefore remain distinct from moral judgment about the decision’s subject.

A suitable final definition for Forqan:

In a principal Arabic branch, B R M concerns making something firm and secure, including the firm twisting of rope. In decisions, ibrām denotes firmly settling an undertaking. The Qur’an uses the root only in Al-Zukhruf 43:79, as abramū and mubrimūn, concerning the opponents’ decision and divine planning. The root itself means neither plotting nor hostility and does not determine whether a decision is right or wrong; those features follow from agent, object, and context.
