Vessels and measures: from ṣāʿ and drinking cups to dispersal, disruption, and movement in early Arabic

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

Arabic Ṣ W ʿ has several semantic branches. Its best-known Qur’anic use concerns a vessel used for drinking or measuring substances, particularly food and grain.

Ṣāʿ and ṣuwāʿ name a vessel or measure. Other uses of the family concern scattering, dispersing, rapid movement, and breaking apart.

Ṣ W ʿ occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Yūsuf 12:72:

صُوَاعَ (ṣuwāʿa): a vessel or measure; here, the king’s measuring vessel.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • صُوَاع (ṣuwāʿ): a drinking or measuring vessel.
  • صَاع (ṣāʿ): a measure for grain and similar substances; also a drinking vessel in some uses.
  • أَصْوُع and أَصْوَاع (aṣwuʿ and aṣwāʿ): plurals of ṣāʿ.
  • صِيعَان (ṣīʿān): another plural of ṣāʿ or ṣuwāʿ.
  • صَاعَ / يَصُوعُ (ṣāʿa / yaṣūʿu): scattered; in another use, gathered or approached from several directions.
  • صَوَّعَ (ṣawwaʿa): scattered; also, in particular uses, prepared and leveled an area.
  • تَصَوَّعَ (taṣawwaʿa): dispersed, broke apart, or had its parts separate.
  • اِنْصَاعَ (inṣāʿa): went quickly, returned, or turned from the previous course.
  • صَاعَة (ṣāʿah): a prepared, level area, including a place for preparing cotton for carding.
  • صَاع (ṣāʿ): in another use, a ground depression or hollow.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but only ṣuwāʿ occurs in the Qur’anic text.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one nominal occurrence in Yūsuf 12:72

2. Semantic cores in early Arabic

Two branches in Ibn Fāris’s analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two principal branches: dispersal and breaking apart, and a vessel.

In the first, taṣawwaʿū describes people dispersing. Taṣawwaʿa al-shaʿr also describes hair or strands that have split, changed, or become scattered.

In the second, ṣāʿ and ṣuwāʿ name a vessel for drinking or measuring a substance.

This distinction matters because the branches’ historical relationship is not entirely clear.

One might picture gathering material in a vessel and scattering it as opposite movements. That explanation alone does not prove both meanings developed from one original use.

The definition should therefore preserve the two attested branches rather than artificially deriving a vessel from dispersal or dispersal from a vessel.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the dispersal and vessel branches

Ṣāʿ: a vessel and measure

Arabic ṣāʿ was an established vessel for measuring grain and other substances.

Before modern measuring devices became widespread, people used containers of known capacity to measure grain, dates, and similar goods.

A vessel holding a given quantity of wheat could become a basis for measurement and exchange.

Ṣāʿ thus names both the measuring implement and, in established usage, a particular quantity or capacity.

Sources concerning Medina’s measures describe one ṣāʿ as four mudds.

That historical size should not be extended to every place and period without investigation. Local vessels and standards may have differed.

Ṣāʿ is fundamentally a capacity measure, not a fixed unit of weight. A ṣāʿ of wheat need not weigh the same as that volume of another substance.

Yūsuf 12:72 also does not specify the capacity of the king’s ṣuwāʿ.

Its name therefore does not establish that its size was exactly the same as Medina’s ṣāʿ in later periods.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Medina’s ṣāʿ, four mudds, and differing local standards

A drinking vessel

In Kitāb al-ʿAyn, Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad explains ṣuwāʿ as a vessel from which people drink.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ also records ṣuwāʿ as a form of ṣāʿ and mentions its use as a drinking vessel.

Ṣuwāʿ therefore does not name only a grain measure.

A vessel made for drinking may also be used to measure food.

The functions are compatible.

Everyday vessels can likewise serve both drinking and measuring liquids or other substances, although precise measuring vessels require a defined capacity standard.

This overlap of drinking and measuring functions matters in Joseph’s story.

Al-ʿAyn and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: ṣāʿ and ṣuwāʿ as drinking vessels and measures

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

Al-Aʿshā describes vessels and hospitality equipment in a poem:

وَحُورٌ كَأَمْثَالِ الدُّمَى وَمَنَاصِفٌ
وَقِدْرٌ وَطَبَّاخٌ وَصَاعٌ وَدَيْسَقُ

wa-ḥūrun ka-amthāli al-dumā wa-manāṣifun / wa-qidrun wa-ṭabbākhun wa-ṣāʿun wa-daysaqu

An approximate rendering:

“Beautiful women like figurines, attendants, a cooking pot and cook, a ṣāʿ, and a serving tray.”

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: manāṣif, plural of minṣaf, means attendants

Ṣāʿ here accompanies vessels and equipment for eating and drinking.

Lexical explanations likewise connect daysaq with a vessel or large serving tray.

The combination shows that pre-Qur’anic Arabic could use ṣāʿ for an actual vessel in people’s lives.

The witness matters because the word was not known only in later legal and measurement discussions: early poetry already places it among hospitality equipment.

The verse does not, however, determine the king’s ṣuwāʿ’s material, size, or precise shape in Joseph’s story.

Safar al-Saʿādah: Al-Aʿshā’s witness and daysaq

3. Scattering and breaking apart

Ṣāʿa: scattering

Arabic records صَاعَ الغَنَمَ (ṣāʿa al-ghanama) for scattering sheep.

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Azharī explains its use when a shepherd separates and disperses the flock in pasture.

Ṣāʿa al-kamiyyu aqrānahu describes a warrior charging his opponents and dispersing their ranks.

Some lexical explanations instead connect the verb with gathering or approaching from several directions.

Lexicographers consequently disagree about the action’s direction: toward gathering or toward dispersing?

Both explanations are recorded. Their opposing meanings should nevertheless not both be inserted into every sentence at once.

In the flock example, for instance, dispersal is clearer.

An early Arabic poetic witness

Lexical sources attribute the following verse to Aws ibn Ḥajar:

يَصُوعُ عُنُوقَهَا أَحْوَى زَنِيمٌ
لَهُ ظَأْبٌ كَمَا صَخِبَ الْغَرِيمُ

yaṣūʿu ʿunūqahā aḥwā zanīmun / lahu ẓaʾbun kamā ṣakhiba al-gharīmu

An approximate rendering:

“A dark he-goat with a pendant appendage scatters the young she-goats, making a call like a clamorous creditor.”

Yaṣūʿu concerns the he-goat’s movement among the females and scattering them.

The attribution is disputed, however: Ibn Barrī assigns the verse to Al-Muʿallā ibn Jamāl al-ʿAbdī.

It therefore cannot be presented as an indisputably pre-Islamic witness by Aws without investigating the attribution.

The usage is clearer than the poet’s identity: yaṣūʿu can concern scattering animals.

Lisān al-ʿArab: disagreement about scattering and the goat witness’s attribution

Taṣawwaʿa: dispersing

تَصَوَّعَ القَوْمُ (taṣawwaʿa al-qawmu) means that a group of people dispersed.

Dhū al-Rummah writes:

عَسَفْتُ اعْتِسَافًا دُونَهَا كُلَّ مَجْهَلٍ
تَظَلُّ بِهَا الْآجَالُ عَنِّي تَصَوَّعُ

ʿasaftu iʿtisāfan dūnahā kulla majhalin / taẓallu bihā al-ājālu ʿannī taṣawwaʿu

An approximate rendering:

“To reach her I traversed every unknown wilderness, where herds of animals kept scattering away from me.”

Taṣawwaʿu here concerns animal herds dispersing.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad-period poet. This is therefore a witness to early post-Islamic Arabic, rather than pre-Islamic verse.

Other uses record taṣawwaʿa al-shaʿr for hair strands dispersing or changing condition, and taṣawwaʿa al-nabāt for vegetation drying and becoming worn.

Dispersal and breaking apart can thus concern people, animals, hair, and plants without all being reduced to exactly the same material state.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Dhū al-Rummah’s witness and taṣawwuʿ

Inṣāʿa: rapid movement and return

Arabic inṣāʿa also concerns rapid movement, returning, and turning aside.

Inṣāʿa al-qawmu may describe people going quickly.

In some uses, it also means turning back or withdrawing from a situation.

This branch therefore concerns movement and changes of direction rather than vessels and measures.

Modern Arabic also uses inṣāʿa li-amrihi for obeying or accepting someone’s command.

That usage should not be transferred to Qur’anic ṣuwāʿ: Yūsuf 12:72 concerns neither obedience nor movement and dispersal.

4. A hollow in the ground and resemblance to a vessel

Early sources also record ṣāʿ for a depression or hollow in the ground.

Children might play with a ball in such a hollow.

The pre-Islamic poet Al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas describes a camel’s forelegs:

مَرِحَتْ يَدَاهَا لِلنَّجَاءِ كَأَنَّمَا
تَكْرُو بِكَفَّيْ لَاعِبٍ فِي صَاعِ

mariḥat yadāhā li-al-najāʾi ka-annamā / takrū bi-kaffay lāʿibin fī ṣāʿi

An approximate rendering:

“Her forelegs moved briskly in flight, like the hands of someone playing with a ball in a hollow in the ground.”

Ṣāʿ here denotes a ground depression, not a grain measure.

The poetic transmissions include both lāʿib and māqiṭ. This does not change the basic image of playing and striking a ball in a hollow.

Its shape may evoke a vessel, since both contain something within them.

Similarity of shape alone does not prove that the measure was named after the hollow, or the reverse.

Lexical sources also record ṣāʿah for a prepared cotton-carding area or a level surface cleared of pebbles.

These uses belong to Ṣ W ʿ’s wider Arabic family, but are not ṣuwāʿ’s direct meaning in Joseph’s story.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Musayyab’s witness, ground hollows, and carding areas

5. The Qur’anic use: ṣuwāʿa al-maliki

Ṣ W ʿ’s only Qur’anic occurrence is in Yūsuf:

قَالُوا نَفْقِدُ صُوَاعَ الْمَلِكِ وَلِمَنْ جَاءَ بِهِ حِمْلُ بَعِيرٍ وَأَنَا بِهِ زَعِيمٌ

qālū nafqidu ṣuwāʿa al-maliki wa-li-man jāʾa bihi ḥimlu baʿīrin wa-anā bihi zaʿīmun

“They said: We are missing the king’s measuring vessel. Whoever brings it will receive a camel-load, and I guarantee it.”

Yūsuf 12:72

The meaning of ṣuwāʿ

Ṣuwāʿ is a noun denoting a particular vessel here.

Ṣuwāʿa al-maliki is a genitive construction: the king’s ṣuwāʿ.

The relationship distinguishes this vessel from others.

Lexical and exegetical sources record two functions: drinking and measuring food.

In Joseph’s story, measuring also fits the receiving and distribution of food.

“The king’s measuring vessel” is therefore a natural, defensible rendering.

The expression does not establish that it was necessarily made exclusively for grain or used only for drinking.

Al-siqāyah and al-ṣuwāʿ

Yūsuf 12:70 states:

فَلَمَّا جَهَّزَهُمْ بِجَهَازِهِمْ جَعَلَ السِّقَايَةَ فِي رَحْلِ أَخِيهِ

fa-lammā jahhazahum bi-jahāzihim jaʿala al-siqāyata fī raḥli akhīhi

“When he had equipped them with their provisions, he placed the cup in his brother’s baggage.”

Here the vessel is called al-siqāyah.

Yūsuf 12:72 calls the same vessel ṣuwāʿa al-maliki.

The lexical difference matters.

Siqāyah belongs to S Q Y and concerns giving drink or a drinking vessel.

Ṣuwāʿ belongs to Ṣ W ʿ and here denotes a vessel that could also serve as a measure.

Two different names may therefore refer to one object without their roots having identical meanings.

Ordinary language does this too: a container may be called a cup for its drinking function and a measure for its measuring function.

The story also presents one particular vessel as the subject of events: it is placed in Joseph’s brother’s baggage, then reported missing, and subsequently searched for.

Thumma istakhrajahā min wiʿāʾi akhīhi

Yūsuf 12:76 states:

ثُمَّ اسْتَخْرَجَهَا مِنْ وِعَاءِ أَخِيهِ

thumma istakhrajahā min wiʿāʾi akhīhi

“Then he brought it out of his brother’s container.”

The hā in istakhrajahā is feminine and refers back to al-siqāyah in Yūsuf 12:70.

By contrast, ṣuwāʿ in Yūsuf 12:72 accompanies a masculine pronoun in wa-li-man jāʾa bihi.

One object may thus be introduced through two names, each with its own grammatical agreement.

Different masculine and feminine pronouns do not require two separate vessels in the story.

Wiʿāʾ in verse 76 also names the container holding the ṣuwāʿ, rather than necessarily being another name for the ṣuwāʿ itself.

The three words therefore have different roles: siqāyah and ṣuwāʿ denote the sought vessel; wiʿāʾ denotes the container from which it is brought out.

Ṣuwāʿ and measuring food

Yūsuf describes measuring food even before this event.

Yūsuf 12:59 states:

أَلَا تَرَوْنَ أَنِّي أُوفِي الْكَيْلَ

a-lā tarawna annī ūfī al-kayla

“Do you not see that I give full measure?”

In Yūsuf 12:88, the brothers also ask Joseph:

فَأَوْفِ لَنَا الْكَيْلَ

fa-awfi lanā al-kayla

“Give us full measure.”

These verses show that measuring food is an important subject in the story.

Ṣuwāʿ may therefore be a measuring vessel in this setting.

This does not mean it was exclusively a grain-measuring implement throughout Arabic history: early sources also record its use as a drinking vessel.

The ṣuwāʿ’s shape and material

Exegetical reports describe the king’s ṣuwāʿ with differing shapes and materials.

Some describe an elongated vessel resembling a makkūk.

Others mention silver, gold, copper, or a gilded vessel.

Their disagreement shows that the construction’s details do not follow from the verse itself.

The Qur’an does not specify how many handles it had, its size, or exactly which metal it was made from.

“A golden cup,” “a silver measure,” or a particular vessel shape should therefore not be presented as ṣuwāʿ’s definite meaning in translation or lexical definition.

The defensible meaning is the established vessel or measure.

Al-Ṭabarī: drinking vessel, measure, and reports of shape and material

Wa-li-man jāʾa bihi ḥimlu baʿīrin

The verse continues:

وَلِمَنْ جَاءَ بِهِ حِمْلُ بَعِيرٍ

wa-li-man jāʾa bihi ḥimlu baʿīrin

“Whoever brings it will receive a camel-load.”

This states the reward for finding and returning the vessel.

Ḥimlu baʿīrin is nevertheless not lexically identical to ṣuwāʿ.

Ṣuwāʿ names the missing vessel; ḥimlu baʿīrin is the quantity of a load carried by a camel.

The verse does not establish that one ṣuwāʿ equaled a full camel-load.

Nor does this expression specify that load’s exact weight or capacity.

Wa-anā bihi zaʿīmun at the end expresses the speaker’s guarantee of the promise.

This concerns the speaker’s responsibility and commitment, not Ṣ W ʿ’s meaning.

6. The missing vessel and the accusation of theft

Yūsuf 12:70 says the vessel was placed in Joseph’s brother’s baggage, after which a caller accused the caravan of theft.

Yūsuf 12:72 responds that the king’s ṣuwāʿ is missing.

In Yūsuf 12:76, the vessel is brought out of that brother’s baggage, and the Qur’an describes an arrangement for Joseph.

The event should therefore not simply be treated as an ordinary theft.

Three matters must be distinguished: placing the vessel in the baggage, reporting it missing, and the caller’s accusation against the caravan.

Story characters’ statements are not necessarily the Qur’an’s independent judgment that everyone accused is guilty.

Joseph’s brothers explicitly deny stealing here.

Lexically, ṣuwāʿ merely names the sought object. The accusation’s truth, the arrangement’s execution, and its ethical and legal questions require examining the whole story.

Ṣuwāʿ should therefore not be defined as a stolen object: theft is an allegation within the story, not a fixed part of the word’s meaning.

7. Reported readings and distinct roots

The established Qur’anic reading is:

صُوَاعَ الْمَلِكِ (ṣuwāʿa al-maliki).

Early sources also transmit other forms.

Al-Ṭabarī mentions ṣāʿa al-maliki with the wāw omitted, and ṣawʿa al-maliki.

Ṣawgha al-maliki, with ghayn, is also reported.

The latter matters for root analysis: ṣawgh belongs to Ṣ W GH and concerns making and fashioning, particularly in metalwork.

Ṣawgh should therefore not be counted as a derivative of Ṣ W ʿ.

These transmitted readings do not all have equal standing in the standard reading.

The common Qur’anic text has ṣuwāʿ, which should remain the root study’s principal basis.

Historical reports nevertheless show some readers or lexicographers using differing forms to explain the vessel, measure, or manufactured object.

These differences should neither alter the verse’s standard form nor cause distinct roots to be merged.

Al-Ṭabarī: the standard reading, omission of wāw, and ṣawgh with ghayn

8. Distinguishing related roots

Ṣ W ʿ (ṣuwāʿ) and S Q Y (siqāyah)

Siqāyah concerns giving drink or a means of drinking.

Ṣuwāʿ concerns a vessel and measure.

Joseph’s story uses both for one vessel.

Sharing a referent does not make the roots’ meanings identical.

A drinking vessel may also measure food.

“Cup” and “measure” may therefore describe one object in different ways.

Study of S Q Y

Ṣ W ʿ (ṣuwāʿ) and K Y L (kāla)

Qur’anic kayl concerns measuring substances by capacity.

Qur’anic ṣuwāʿ names a vessel or implement that can be used for measuring.

Kayl therefore denotes the measuring act, while ṣuwāʿ denotes its implement.

Yūsuf 12:59 and Yūsuf 12:88 concern giving full measure; Yūsuf 12:72 names a particular vessel.

Ṣuwāʿ alone should therefore not be translated as fairness in measurement or fully giving others their due.

These concepts come from the verbs and context of the kayl verses.

Study of K Y L

Ṣ W ʿ (ṣuwāʿ) and W ʿ Y (wiʿāʾ)

Wiʿāʾ denotes a vessel or container holding something.

In Yūsuf 12:76, the ṣuwāʿ is brought out of Joseph’s brother’s wiʿāʾ.

Wiʿāʾ there is consequently the container holding the other vessel.

Both words may denote a kind of container, yet their functions and referents differ in the sentence.

Ṣuwāʿ names the sought vessel; wiʿāʾ names what held it.

Ṣ W ʿ (ṣuwāʿ) and Ṣ W GH (ṣawgh)

Ṣawgh belongs to Ṣ W GH and concerns making and shaping something, particularly metals.

Sources on readings also transmit ṣawgha al-maliki.

This is not identical to ṣuwāʿa al-maliki in the standard text.

A gold or silver vessel may be a worked object, but its being made of metal does not turn ṣuwāʿ’s lexical meaning into “a manufactured metal object.”

Ṣ W ʿ (ṣāʿa) and F R Q (faraqa)

In Ṣ W ʿ’s verbal branch, ṣāʿa al-ghanama can mean scattering sheep.

Farraqa also concerns separating and scattering in Arabic.

The verbs overlap in some uses, but do not share every meaning.

Ṣ W ʿ also has the vessel-and-measure branch, which F R Q itself does not supply.

Shared dispersal meanings should therefore not restrict the whole Ṣ W ʿ family to separation.

9. Translation suggestions for Forqan

صُوَاع (ṣuwāʿ): a drinking or measuring vessel; in Yūsuf 12:72, a measure.

صَاع (ṣāʿ): a measure; in established usage, a unit of capacity.

أَصْوَاع / أَصْوُع (aṣwāʿ / aṣwuʿ): measures.

صَاعَ (ṣāʿa): scattered; in other uses, gathered or approached from several directions.

صَوَّعَ (ṣawwaʿa): scattered; in another use, prepared an area.

تَصَوَّعَ (taṣawwaʿa): dispersed or broke apart.

اِنْصَاعَ (inṣāʿa): went quickly, returned, or withdrew.

صَاعَة (ṣāʿah): a prepared, level area; also a place for preparing cotton for carding.

صَاع (ṣāʿ): in its ground-related use, a depression or hollow.

A suitable rendering for Yūsuf 12:72 is:

قَالُوا نَفْقِدُ صُوَاعَ الْمَلِكِ

qālū nafqidu ṣuwāʿa al-maliki

“They said: We are missing the king’s measuring vessel.”

“Measuring vessel” fits this context better than “cup” because of the story’s measurement and distribution of food.

The drinking-vessel meaning should nevertheless remain in the lexical explanation.

“The king’s golden cup” or “the king’s silver measure” inserts details the verse itself does not state.

No definite weight or volume should be added to ṣuwāʿ’s translation either.

10. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ W ʿ has two principal branches: vessels and measures, and dispersal or breaking apart.

In the first, ṣāʿ and ṣuwāʿ denote vessels for drinking or measuring substances. Historically, ṣāʿ also became a specific measure’s name.

In the second, verbs and derivatives such as ṣāʿa, ṣawwaʿa, and taṣawwaʿa concern scattering, dispersing, and disruption.

Early sources also record a ground hollow and a work area prepared for use. Some uses’ resemblance to a vessel may help explain their meanings, but a shared historical origin for all has not been established.

Only ṣuwāʿ occurs in the Qur’an. Yūsuf 12:72 uses it for the vessel or measure reported missing in the story.

Yūsuf 12:70 calls the same vessel al-siqāyah. Both words may refer to one object, one emphasizing drinking and the other its role as a vessel or measure.

Ṣuwāʿ alone does not determine the vessel’s material, precise size, monetary value, or manufacture. The theft accusation and the associated arrangement belong to the story, not to the word’s lexical meaning.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

A vessel or measure for drinking or measuring; in another branch, scattering, dispersing, and breaking apart.

Meaning in Yūsuf 12:72:

صُوَاعَ (ṣuwāʿa): the king’s measuring vessel; a vessel known in Joseph’s story for drinking or measuring and reported missing.
