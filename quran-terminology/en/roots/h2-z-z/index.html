Hazz: shaking, setting in motion, and the movement of something previously still or settled

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Hā–zāy–zāy concerns shaking and setting something in motion. Hazza, yahuzzu, hazzan means shaking, moving, or bringing something out of stillness into motion.

When someone grasps and shakes a branch, it moves from its previous position. Wind likewise sets branches and leaves moving.

Two situations must be distinguished: shaking something and that thing coming into motion.

A person shaking a branch causes the movement. When wind moves the branch, however, the sentence may focus on the branch’s movement itself.

Hazza usually expresses causing movement, while ihtazza describes coming into motion. The latter does not establish that motion occurs independently of all external causes.

Early Arabic sources use the family for moving a spear or branch, a caravan’s movement, plants stirring, flowing water, and even a falling star’s movement through the sky.

Other forms denote sounds accompanying movement, joy, and the stirring of human feelings.

A poetic witness attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhalī contains:

قَدْ حَالَ بَيْنَ دَرِيسَيْهِ مُؤَوِّبَةٌ

qad ḥāla bayna darīsayhi muʾawwibatun

مِسْعٌ لَهَا بِعِضَاهِ الْأَرْضِ تَهْزِيزُ

misʿun lahā bi-ʿiḍāhi al-arḍi tahzīz

“A night wind has come between his two worn garments—a fierce wind shaking the land’s thorn trees.”

Tahzīz here concerns wind-driven movement of branches and thorny vegetation.

The poet depicts a natural process: wind acts on plants and sets them in motion.

The witness matters because the plants’ movement is not voluntary. Their branches are shaken by wind.

Early sources attribute the line to al-Mutanakhkhil, while some transmissions assign it to Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī. Readings also vary between dūna and bayna, and nisʿ and misʿ. The wording here follows the bayna and misʿ reading in Lisān al-ʿArab.

Despite its place in the early poetic tradition, the line’s exact date and attribution require qualification. Its use of tahzīz for wind shaking branches nevertheless has a clear meaning consistent with other linguistic evidence.

A witness attributed to Taʾabbaṭa Sharran

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Taʾabbaṭa Sharran reads:

أَهُزُّ بِهِ فِي نَدْوَةِ الْحَيِّ عِطْفَهُ

ahuzzu bihi fī nadwati al-ḥayyi ʿiṭfahu

كَمَا هَزَّ عِطْفِي بِالْهِجَانِ الْأَوَارِكِ

kamā hazza ʿiṭfī bi-al-hijāni al-awārik

“Through my praise I stir him to delight in the clan’s assembly, just as his gift of prized camels stirred me to delight.”

Hazz here extends beyond physical movement to arousing joy and eagerness.

The poet says that, just as the other man’s generosity delighted him, his public praise brings that man joy and animation.

Bodily movement provides a tangible image for the stirring of feelings.

Commentaries on early poetry transmit this line, with some differences in wording. The meaning of hazz is clear, although the precise dating of this particular textual form is not certain.

The two witnesses illustrate physical movement and inward stirring or excitement.

A historically defensible definition: In its principal branch, hā–zāy–zāy denotes causing movement and being shaken. An external factor may cause this movement; extended uses describe animation, eagerness, and excitement.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • هَزَّ: shook; moved something.
  • يَهُزُّ: shakes.
  • هَزّ: shaking; setting in motion.
  • هَزَّة: a shake or movement; sometimes excitement.
  • هِزَّة: animation, eagerness, or excited movement.
  • اِهْتَزَّ: stirred; shook; came into motion.
  • اِهْتِزَاز: stirring or shaking.
  • هَزِيز: a sound accompanying movement, such as wind or a moving object.
  • تَهْزِيز: shaking or moving something.
  • هَزْهَزَ: shook repeatedly.
  • تَهَزْهَزَ: stirred or shook repeatedly.
  • هَزَاهِز: in some lexical uses, upheavals and hardships that unsettle people.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but not all occur in the Quran. Hazhaza and tahazhaza are related reduplicated quadriliteral forms, rather than ordinary conjugations of the triliteral verb.

The root occurs five times in the Quran, in three written forms:

Wa-huzzī in Maryam 19:25; tahtazzu in Al-Naml 27:10 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31; and ihtazzat in Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Fuṣṣilat 41:39.

Semantic core

The principal uses concern stirring or moving something previously relatively still.

Directly shaking an object, such as a branch, can cause this movement.

Something can also stir through another influence, as branches move in wind.

In descriptions of plants, movement may accompany growth and emergence.

Three situations should therefore be distinguished:

Shaking: an agent acts on something and moves it.

Coming into motion: the movement itself is expressed without necessarily identifying its cause in the verb.

Excitement: physical movement supplies an image for human animation, eagerness, or responsiveness.

These senses are related, but should not all receive one fixed translation.

Does hazz always mean violent trembling?

No. Shaking may be gentle or vigorous.

Someone can gently move a branch or shake it hard enough for its fruit to fall.

Hazz expresses movement, but the root does not invariably specify force, speed, duration, or intensity.

Ihtizāz likewise need not mean an earthquake or destructive shaking. A leaf moving in wind, a stirring branch, or a plant’s movement during growth may belong to the same semantic field.

3. Quranic uses

Maryam 19:25: shaking the palm trunk

وَهُزِّي إِلَيْكِ بِجِذْعِ النَّخْلَةِ تُسَاقِطْ عَلَيْكِ رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا

wa-huzzī ilayki bi-jidhʿi al-nakhlati tusāqiṭ ʿalayki ruṭaban janiyyā

Maryam 19:25

“And shake the palm trunk toward yourself; it will drop fresh, ripe dates upon you.”

Wa-huzzī is an imperative from hā–zāy–zāy addressed to one woman.

Its addressee is Mary, whose difficult childbirth is described in the preceding verses.

In Maryam 19:23, labor pains bring her to the palm trunk.

In Maryam 19:24, a voice tells her not to grieve and announces water provided beneath her.

Maryam 19:25 then commands her to shake the trunk so that fresh, ripe dates fall upon her.

Wa-huzzī is a practical instruction: Mary is to move the trunk.

Bi-jidhʿi al-nakhlah identifies the object being moved, the date-palm trunk.

Ilayki specifies movement toward Mary.

Hazza can take a direct object or the preposition bāʾ. Here bāʾ accompanies jidhʿ. Some scholars explain the construction through grasping and pulling toward oneself; others describe it as an established way of using the verb with bāʾ.

In either case, the practical meaning is clear: Mary is instructed to shake the trunk toward herself.

Shaking and the falling dates

The continuation uses tusāqiṭ, from sīn–qāf–ṭāʾ.

That word concerns the dates falling, whereas huzzī concerns shaking the trunk.

The sentence therefore contains two distinct events:

Mary shakes the tree, and dates fall upon her.

The construction connects moving the tree with the fruit’s fall.

Hazz itself does not mean making dates fall. The other verb and the whole sentence supply that result.

The verse also does not specify the palm’s exact variety or size, Mary’s exertion, or how the dates became available.

Without Quranic evidence, one cannot insist that the palm was dry, uprooted, or cut down. Nor should the whole event be reduced to Mary’s bodily strength while disregarding the context of divine provision.

A command to perform a real movement accompanies provision for Mary. This should not become an absolute rule that every divine gift depends on physical effort by its recipient.

The root’s lexical meaning remains shaking.

Al-Naml 27:10: the movement of Moses’ staff

وَأَلْقِ عَصَاكَ فَلَمَّا رَآهَا تَهْتَزُّ كَأَنَّهَا جَانٌّ وَلَّىٰ مُدْبِرًا وَلَمْ يُعَقِّبْ يَا مُوسَىٰ لَا تَخَفْ إِنِّي لَا يَخَافُ لَدَيَّ الْمُرْسَلُونَ

wa-alqi ʿaṣāka fa-lammā raʾāhā tahtazzu ka-annahā jānnun wallā mudbiran wa-lam yuʿaqqib yā mūsā lā takhaf innī lā yakhāfu ladayya al-mursalūn

Al-Naml 27:10

“And cast down your staff. When he saw it moving as though it were a serpent, he turned away and did not return. Moses, do not fear; the Messengers do not fear in My presence.”

Tahtazzu is a Form VIII imperfect verb.

It expresses the movement of the thing being described.

Moses is instructed to cast down his staff and then reacts when he sees it moving.

Tahtazzu concerns the staff’s movement, which differs from its previous state as a staff held in Moses’ hand.

Ka-annahā jānnun clarifies the image.

Jānn here denotes a serpent, so Moses sees something moving like a snake.

Tahtazzu does not itself mean serpent; it expresses movement.

The comparison with a serpent belongs to ka-annahā jānnun.

Does tahtazzu mean only trembling in place?

In Persian, “trembling” can suggest an object remaining in place while making small, rapid movements.

Arabic ihtizāz can also denote conspicuous movement and is not confined to slight vibration at one point.

In Al-Naml, the serpent comparison shows that this is more than a faint tremor of an otherwise motionless stick.

“It moves” therefore preserves the breadth of the movement without specifying its mechanics beyond the text.

Moses then turns away without returning, before being told not to fear.

His reaction shows that the sight startled and frightened him. His fear comes from context, not the meaning of hā–zāy–zāy.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31: the same sign in another context

وَأَنْ أَلْقِ عَصَاكَ فَلَمَّا رَآهَا تَهْتَزُّ كَأَنَّهَا جَانٌّ وَلَّىٰ مُدْبِرًا وَلَمْ يُعَقِّبْ يَا مُوسَىٰ أَقْبِلْ وَلَا تَخَفْ إِنَّكَ مِنَ الْآمِنِينَ

wa-an alqi ʿaṣāka fa-lammā raʾāhā tahtazzu ka-annahā jānnun wallā mudbiran wa-lam yuʿaqqib yā mūsā aqbil wa-lā takhaf innaka mina al-āminīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31

“And cast down your staff. When he saw it moving as though it were a serpent, he turned away and did not return. Moses, come forward and do not fear; you are among those who are safe.”

This verse recounts the same event within another presentation of Moses’ story.

The construction fa-lammā raʾāhā tahtazzu ka-annahā jānnun is identical in both verses.

Both use tahtazzu for the staff’s evident movement, with the serpent comparison clarifying what was seen.

An important difference follows in the address to Moses.

Al-Naml 27:10 mentions the divine Messengers after the command not to fear.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31 instead tells Moses to come forward and assures him of safety.

The root’s meaning remains unchanged; the subsequent speech and contextual emphasis differ.

Repeating the same lexical family for this movement does not itself reveal further physical details about its nature.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5: earth stirring after rain

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ فِي رَيْبٍ مِنَ الْبَعْثِ فَإِنَّا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ مِنْ تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ نُطْفَةٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ عَلَقَةٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ مُضْغَةٍ مُخَلَّقَةٍ وَغَيْرِ مُخَلَّقَةٍ لِنُبَيِّنَ لَكُمْ وَنُقِرُّ فِي الْأَرْحَامِ مَا نَشَاءُ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى ثُمَّ نُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا ثُمَّ لِتَبْلُغُوا أَشُدَّكُمْ وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُتَوَفَّىٰ وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرَدُّ إِلَىٰ أَرْذَلِ الْعُمُرِ لِكَيْلَا يَعْلَمَ مِنْ بَعْدِ عِلْمٍ شَيْئًا وَتَرَى الْأَرْضَ هَامِدَةً فَإِذَا أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْهَا الْمَاءَ اهْتَزَّتْ وَرَبَتْ وَأَنْبَتَتْ مِنْ كُلِّ زَوْجٍ بَهِيجٍ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu in kuntum fī raybin mina al-baʿthi fa-innā khalaqnākum min turābin thumma min nuṭfatin thumma min ʿalaqatin thumma min muḍghatin mukhallaqatin wa-ghayri mukhallaqatin li-nubayyina lakum wa-nuqirru fī al-arḥāmi mā nashāʾu ilā ajalin musamman thumma nukhrijukum ṭiflan thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum wa-minkum man yutawaffā wa-minkum man yuraddu ilā ardhali al-ʿumuri li-kaylā yaʿlama min baʿdi ʿilmin shayʾan wa-tarā al-arḍa hāmidatan fa-idhā anzalnā ʿalayhā al-māʾa ihtazzat wa-rabat wa-anbatat min kulli zawjin bahīj

Al-Ḥajj 22:5

“People, if you doubt the resurrection, We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from something clinging, then from a morsel of flesh, formed and unformed, to make things clear to you. We retain in the wombs what We will until an appointed term, then bring you forth as children, then that you may reach your full strength. Some of you die, and some are returned to the frailest stage of life, so that after knowing they know nothing. You see the earth lifeless; when We send water upon it, it stirs and rises and brings forth every delightful kind.”

Ihtazzat is a third-person feminine singular Form VIII perfect verb.

It refers to al-arḍ, the earth.

The earth is first described as hāmidah: seemingly motionless, dormant, and without growth.

Its condition changes when water descends.

Ihtazzat expresses stirring.

Rabat belongs to rāʾ–bāʾ–wāw and here concerns rising and increase.

Anbatat belongs to nūn–bāʾ–tāʾ and expresses bringing forth vegetation.

Together these verbs picture earth transformed by water: it emerges from dormancy, rises, and produces varied plants.

Does ihtazzat mean an earthquake?

No. Here the earth’s movement accompanies water and plant growth.

The stirring belongs to renewed life and growth, rather than a destructive earthquake.

Imagine dry ground with no visible vegetation for a time. Water arrives, seeds germinate, roots spread, and plants emerge.

This transformation gives a tangible image of earth stirring and leaving its previous state.

The verse does not explain the exact physics, chemistry, or biology of growth. Ihtazzat should therefore not be restricted to particular soil-particle movements, pressure changes, or one scientific mechanism.

What the context makes clear is the earth’s transformation after water, with visible growth and plant life.

Resurrection after death

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 begins by addressing those who doubt resurrection.

It then discusses stages of human creation and the earth’s transformation after water descends.

The plant-growth image is therefore more than an agricultural account; it contributes to an argument about creation and resurrection.

Al-Ḥajj 22:6 connects these signs with God’s truth, His giving life to the dead, and His power over everything.

This broader message comes from the verses together. Ihtazzat alone means stirring, not resurrecting the dead.

Fuṣṣilat 41:39: earth stirring and life becoming apparent

وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ أَنَّكَ تَرَى الْأَرْضَ خَاشِعَةً فَإِذَا أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْهَا الْمَاءَ اهْتَزَّتْ وَرَبَتْ إِنَّ الَّذِي أَحْيَاهَا لَمُحْيِ الْمَوْتَىٰ إِنَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

wa-min āyātihi annaka tarā al-arḍa khāshiʿatan fa-idhā anzalnā ʿalayhā al-māʾa ihtazzat wa-rabat inna alladhī aḥyāhā la-muḥyī al-mawtā innahu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr

Fuṣṣilat 41:39

“Among His signs is that you see the earth subdued; when We send water upon it, it stirs and rises. The One who gave it life will surely give life to the dead; He has power over all things.”

This verse likewise uses ihtazzat wa-rabat.

Unlike Al-Ḥajj 22:5, which calls the earth hāmidah, it describes it as khāshiʿah.

Khushūʿ, from khāʾ–shīn–ʿayn, here evokes quiet, settled, motionless earth.

Water changes that condition: the earth stirs and rises.

The verse’s ending directly connects this change with giving life to the dead.

The earth after rain thus serves as a sign of God’s power over life and renewed creation.

The two earth verses compared

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 explicitly mentions varied vegetation after ihtazzat wa-rabat.

Fuṣṣilat 41:39 turns directly from stirring and rising to the earth’s revival and God’s power to revive the dead.

Ihtazzat has the same meaning in both; their subsequent argument and emphasis differ.

It also shares a semantic element with the movement of Moses’ staff, without making the events identical.

Moses’ story concerns the staff’s movement and an extraordinary sign.

The earth passages concern nature’s transformation through water and growth.

A shared root indicates a shared idea of movement, rather than identical causes, mechanisms, or events.

4. Hazz, ihtizāz, and hazīz

Hazz: causing movement

Hazza is principally transitive: an agent shakes something.

Maryam 19:25 instructs Mary to shake the palm trunk.

The agent and the thing moved are distinguishable.

Ihtizāz: coming into motion

Ihtazza describes the subject’s own movement.

In Al-Naml 27:10 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31, the staff is seen moving.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Fuṣṣilat 41:39, the earth stirs after receiving water.

The uses show that ihtizāz can describe a living thing, an inanimate object, or earth undergoing growth.

The verb alone therefore does not prove biological life or independent will.

Form VIII likewise does not establish motion without an external cause.

Both earth verses place the descent of water before the stirring and make it part of the context.

Hazīz: sound accompanying movement

Dictionaries record hazīz for sounds of movement, such as wind moving trees or something rotating.

This use focuses on the audible aspect.

Wind passing through branches may move them and produce a sound.

Hazīz should not, however, replace every form in the family.

The five Quranic occurrences concern movement primarily, rather than sound production.

Excitement and animation

Human applications may express enthusiasm, eagerness, and animation.

When kind words delight someone, movement and stirring can picture that response.

Such usage should not turn ihtazzat al-arḍ into literal human joy experienced by the earth.

Nor does tahtazzu in Moses’ story mean the staff becoming joyful or delighted.

Each derivative’s meaning depends on its construction and context.

5. Comparison with related roots

Hā–zāy–zāy and ḥāʾ–rāʾ–kāf: stirring and movement

Movement has a broad Arabic meaning encompassing displacement and changes in bodily position.

Hazz principally emphasizes shaking or stirring.

Someone walking from home to the market moves, but walking alone is not the same as shaking an object.

Someone shaking a branch instead causes a particular movement in it.

This does not make hazz invariably small movement and ḥarakah invariably large displacement. Their semantic ranges overlap.

Hā–zāy–zāy and zāy–lām–zāy–lām: stirring and violent shaking

Zalzalah belongs to zāy–lām–zāy–lām and denotes severe shaking in the Quran.

Al-Ḥajj 22:1 mentions the tremendous shaking of the Hour.

Al-Zalzalah 99:1 likewise speaks of the earth being shaken.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5, however, uses ihtazzat of earth stirring and producing vegetation after receiving water.

Both families concern movement, but these contexts differ.

Zalzalah here emphasizes great, violent shaking, while ihtizāz can describe gentler movement accompanying growth.

Rendering ihtazzat as an earthquake occurring is therefore inappropriate in the earth-growth verses.

Hā–zāy–zāy and rāʾ–jīm–fāʾ: stirring and trembling

Quranic rajf concerns severe trembling and agitation.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:78 uses rajfah in a community’s punishment.

Quranic hazz describes movement that can occur without destruction or upheaval.

A shaken branch may remain intact, and the earth may stir through plant growth.

Not every ihtizāz is therefore rajfah, nor every rajfah an ordinary branch’s movement.

The difference concerns kind and context of movement, rather than an absolute division between natural and supernatural events.

Hā–zāy–zāy and rāʾ–bāʾ–wāw: stirring and rising

These roots appear together in Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Fuṣṣilat 41:39.

Ihtazzat expresses stirring.

Rabat here concerns rising and increase.

The concepts are close but distinct.

Something may move without increasing in volume or height. Conversely, material may slowly expand without conspicuous shaking.

The earth verses combine both features in an image of transformation and growth.

Ihtazzat should therefore not simply become “increased in volume,” nor rabat merely “shook.”

Hā–zāy–zāy and hamzah–zāy–zāy: shaking and strong incitement

These roots resemble each other in sound and structure but remain distinct.

Maryam 19:83 uses hamzah–zāy–zāy for strong incitement or urging.

Quranic hā–zāy–zāy concerns shaking or coming into motion.

The families can overlap in stimulation and excitement, but phonetic and semantic resemblance does not prove a shared historical derivation.

Urging someone to act and shaking an object are distinct applications and should not replace one another without contextual grounds.

Hā–zāy–zāy and hā–zāy–hamzah: moving and mocking

Hā–zāy–hamzah denotes mocking and ridicule in the Quran.

Hā–zāy–zāy concerns movement and shaking.

Persian words such as hazl, hazz, and huzʾ may sound similar, but their roots differ.

That resemblance does not justify translating hazz as mocking or making light of something.

6. Interpretive and translation challenges

Does ihtizāz always indicate life?

No. Movement alone does not prove that something is a living organism.

Wind can move a branch, and flowing water can be disturbed by obstacles.

Moses’ staff is described as moving and compared to a serpent, but tahtazzu alone does not disclose the event’s full nature.

The earth’s stirring accompanies water, growth, and plant life. This important contextual connection should not change the root’s lexical meaning to becoming alive.

Is the earth’s stirring specifically soil-particle movement?

Water and plant growth involve many physical and biological changes.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Fuṣṣilat 41:39 do not provide their scientific details.

Germinating seeds, growing roots, and plants emerging from soil can illustrate the image as observable processes.

Ihtazzat should not, however, be restricted to one mechanism or claimed to refer necessarily to cells, molecules, or soil particles.

The clear contextual meaning is earth stirring as it changes and grows after receiving water.

Does hazz necessarily imply repetition?

Many tangible instances of shaking involve back-and-forth movement, as when a branch sways from side to side.

The root itself does not establish a number, duration, or exact pattern of movements.

Maryam 19:25 commands shaking the trunk without specifying how many shakes or how forcefully.

In Al-Naml 27:10 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31, tahtazzu alone likewise does not determine the staff’s exact movement; the serpent comparison clarifies part of the image.

“Shaking” and “coming into motion” are therefore better equivalents than restrictive inventions such as “shaking three times” or “continuously rotating.”

Is every movement positive?

No. Hazz principally expresses movement without independently assigning moral value.

Maryam 19:25 connects shaking the palm with food being provided.

The earth verses connect stirring with growth and life.

In Moses’ story, the observed movement initially frightens him.

Other lexical uses likewise describe excitement in varied circumstances.

The value and message of each occurrence therefore cannot come from the root alone.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents include:

هَزَّ: shook; set in motion.

يَهُزُّ: shakes.

هَزّ: shaking; moving something.

هَزَّة: a shake; a movement.

هِزَّة: animation or excitement, where context supports it.

اِهْتَزَّ: stirred; shook; came into motion.

اِهْتِزَاز: stirring; shaking.

هَزِيز: a movement’s sound, rumble, or whir, according to context.

تَهْزِيز: shaking; setting in motion.

هَزْهَزَ: shook repeatedly.

هَزَاهِز: unsettling upheavals and hardships, in that particular branch.

For the Quranic forms:

“Shake.”

This preserves the direct command to Mary and the action of moving the palm trunk.

Al-Naml 27:10 — تَهْتَزُّ

“It moves.”

These proposed renderings preserve the distinction between causing movement and coming into motion across the five occurrences.

8. Conclusion

In early Arabic, hā–zāy–zāy concerns shaking, moving something, and coming into motion.

Poetic and lexical witnesses show physical movement, such as wind shaking branches, alongside extended uses for human animation and excitement.

The root occurs five times in the Quran.

Maryam 19:25 uses wa-huzzī as a command to shake the palm trunk.

Al-Naml 27:10 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31 use tahtazzu for Moses’ staff moving.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Fuṣṣilat 41:39 use ihtazzat for the earth stirring after water arrives.

Movement remains central, while its subject and cause differ.

The root itself does not fix a cause, force, physical mechanism, or ethical value. Construction and context establish those features.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its principal branch, hā–zāy–zāy denotes shaking and setting something in motion. Hazza usually expresses causing movement; ihtazza describes the subject moving, whether through an external influence or with the cause left unstated.

Extended uses include animation, excitement, and movement-related sound. All five Quranic instances, however, center on shaking or coming into motion.
