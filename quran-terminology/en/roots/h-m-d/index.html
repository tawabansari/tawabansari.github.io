Forms covered from this root

In this page, the root H M D is studied through its semantic field. Then the most important Qur’anic forms, or Qur’an-related forms built from this field, are explained separately.

  • ḥamida — past-tense verb; a base form for understanding the root
  • yaḥmadu — present-tense verb; a base form for understanding the occurrence or continuation of ḥamd
  • yuḥmadu / yuḥmadū — passive present-tense verb; to be praised or to be made the object of ḥamd
  • al-ḥamd / ḥamd — verbal noun / noun; praise, gratitude, and acknowledgement of worthiness
  • ḥamīd — noun / adjective; praised, worthy of ḥamd, and possessing a perfection that calls for ḥamd
  • ḥāmidūn — active participle, plural; those who praise, or the people of ḥamd
  • maḥmūd — passive participle; praised, approved, or worthy of ḥamd
  • Muḥammad — proper name, on the pattern mufaʿʿal from Form II; much-praised / possessing traits worthy of ḥamd
  • Aḥmad — elative form / proper name; more praised, or more distinguished in the field of ḥamd

A note of caution: these forms do not all appear in the same way in statistical listings of the Qur’anic root H M D. Some, such as al-ḥamd, ḥamīd, ḥāmidūn, maḥmūd, and yuḥmadū, are direct Qur’anic forms counted under this root. Others, such as ḥamida and yaḥmadu, are base forms useful for understanding the morphology of the root. Muḥammad and Aḥmad are Qur’anic proper names connected to the semantic field of ḥamd and should be explained carefully in their own category.

The general meaning of the root H M D

The root H M D in the Qur’an points to the semantic field of praise, gratitude, acknowledgement of goodness, and recognition of worthiness for ḥamd. It is often translated as “praise” or “thanks,” but neither word alone carries the full field of meaning.

Ḥamd is not merely a verbal reaction to a blessing. Ḥamd takes shape when a person recognizes goodness, perfection, beneficence, wisdom, mercy, justice, or worthiness, and does not deny it. In this sense, ḥamd is connected to recognition, acknowledgement, gratitude, and praise.

When a person performs ḥamd toward someone, he is not merely saying, “I thank you.” He is, in some way, acknowledging that he has seen something worthy of praise in the other. That may be a blessing he received, a good quality, a right action, or a station worthy of acknowledgement.

In relation to The God, ḥamd takes on a deeper meaning. Every goodness, every mercy, every nurturing act, every guidance, every opening, every truth, and every real beauty ultimately comes from Him or returns to Him. Divine ḥamd, then, is not merely a devotional phrase; it is a recognition of the place of The God before existence, humanity, blessing, guidance, and truth.

This page is not a short explanation of one word. It is the reference page for the root H M D. Whenever a reader meets a Qur’anic word from this root, they can return to this page and see that form in its proper place.

Ḥamd: beyond simple thanks

In everyday language, people usually give thanks when they receive a blessing or when someone does good to them. But ḥamd in the Qur’an is broader than ordinary thanks.

Thanks usually focuses on a blessing that reached me. Ḥamd can focus on worthiness itself, on perfection, goodness, and truth, even before a person places personal benefit at the center.

For this reason, ḥamd moves the human being away from self-centeredness. If a person is grateful only when something happens according to his desire, his gaze is still circling around his own want. True ḥamd means recognizing goodness and truth, even when one does not grasp every detail of wisdom.

In the Qur’an, the ḥamd of The God is connected to creation, Lordship, mercy, guidance, forgiveness, judgement, ownership, and the final return. So ḥamd is not merely the uttering of a phrase; it is an inner orientation that turns the human being toward knowing The God, receiving blessing with gratitude, accepting truth, and standing humbly before the Lord.

The difference between ḥamd, shukr, madh, and thanāʾ

To understand the root H M D more precisely, it should be distinguished from several nearby words.

Shukr usually means response to blessing. When a blessing reaches a person, the person gives shukr. Shukr can be with the tongue, the heart, and action.

Madh can mean praise, but it does not always carry the same weight of true recognition and sound acknowledgement as ḥamd. A person may praise someone out of habit, benefit, fear, exaggeration, or even hypocrisy. Madh does not always guarantee that the praise is based on truth and sound recognition.

Thanāʾ is closer to repeated praise and favorable mention. Thanāʾ may contain ḥamd, but the root H M D itself emphasizes the recognition of worthiness and acknowledgement of goodness.

Ḥamd is deeper than all of these. It can include shukr, it can take the form of praise, and it can be joined with thanāʾ; but at its core, ḥamd means recognizing goodness and worthiness, and acknowledging it.

For this reason, Forqan should not reduce ḥamd to “thanks” alone. “Gratitude” carries part of the meaning, but “conscious praise,” or “praise joined with gratitude and acknowledgement,” comes closer to the field of the root.

al-ḥamd: true and comprehensive praise

One of the most important forms of this root in the Qur’an is al-ḥamd. This word appears at the opening of Sūrah Al-Fātiḥah:

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ al-ḥamdu lillāhi Rabbi al-ʿālamīn

Here the speech is not merely about one scattered act of praise. Al-ḥamd comes with al; praise is presented as definite, comprehensive, and known. This may point to the principle of true praise: every praise that is truly upon truth ultimately returns to The God.

This verse does not only say, “Praise The God,” even though praising The God is its natural result. The sentence is deeper: praise truly belongs to The God.

So al-ḥamd in Al-Fātiḥah is the doorway into knowing The God. Before asking for guidance, the human being recognizes the place of The God. Before saying, “Guide us,” the reader learns that the source of Lordship, mercy, ownership of the Day of Judgement, and guidance is that One Deity worthy of worship.

al-ḥamdu lillāh: returning praise to its true source

The expression al-ḥamdu lillāh is one of the deepest Qur’anic expressions. Lillāh shows that ḥamd, in the end, is for The God and belongs to The God.

A person may be grateful to a father, a mother, a messenger, a teacher, a physician, a friend, or any doer of good. The Qur’an does not deny human goodness. But if the gaze continues all the way to the source, every real good is dependent on The God: He is the One who placed possibility, life, intellect, mercy, guidance, ability, opportunity, and effect within existence.

Therefore, al-ḥamdu lillāh does not mean denying gratitude toward creation. Rather, it places gratitude toward creation in its proper place. The human being may be grateful to the means of good, but must not forget the source of good.

In this sense, ḥamd is tawḥīdic. It takes blessing back from idols, powers, individuals, groups, means, and the human ego, and returns it to the true source.

Ḥamīd: praised, worthy of ḥamd, and perfectly free of need

Ḥamīd is one of the most important divine descriptions in the Qur’an. It is often translated as “praised,” but its meaning is not only that others praise The God.

Ḥamīd means the One who is intrinsically worthy of ḥamd, whether people praise Him or not. The praisedness of The God does not depend on the tongues of servants. If all creation were silent, The God would still be Ḥamīd, because His perfection, freedom from need, wisdom, mercy, justice, and Lordship are from Himself.

This protects the human being from a misunderstanding: The God does not need our ḥamd. Our ḥamd adds nothing to the perfection of The God. We do not make The God more complete by praising Him; rather, through ḥamd we bring ourselves closer to truth.

When the Qur’an describes The God as Ghaniyyun Ḥamīd, the connection is very important. The God is free of need and at the same time Ḥamīd. He is worthy of ḥamd not because He needs praise, but because He is truly complete, free of need, giving, and worthy.

So the Ḥamīd-ness of The God preserves tawḥīd, breaks human arrogance, and clarifies the meaning of worship: worship is not for the need of The God; it is for the awakening, correction, and salvation of the human being.

Maḥmūd: a station or description worthy of praise

Maḥmūd is a passive participle from the root H M D. It means something or someone that is the object of ḥamd, or a station that is praised and approved.

In the Qur’an, the expression maqāman maḥmūdan shows that ḥamd may also relate to a station: a station whose result is praised, recognized, and worthy of acknowledgement.

Being maḥmūd is not the same as outward fame. People may praise something, while that praise is built on falsehood, benefit, partisanship, or ignorance. In the Qur’anic view, being maḥmūd should be connected to truth, guidance, the approval of The God, and a worthy result.

So maḥmūd shows that the field of ḥamd is not limited to speech. A path, a station, a result, or an effect can also be maḥmūd, if it is truly worthy of ḥamd.

yuḥmadū: seeking praise from people

One important Qur’anic use of this root is the passive form yuḥmadū: to be made the object of ḥamd, or to be praised.

This use appears in a warning context: there are those who love to be praised for what they did not do. This is very important, because it shows that ḥamd, if separated from truth, can become an instrument of self-display, deception, and arrogance.

Sound ḥamd must be based on truth. If someone did not do something, but loves to be praised for it, he has removed ḥamd from the place of truth and turned it into a tool of the ego.

This verse protects us from seeking misplaced praise. A person should not merely seek to appear maḥmūd and praised in the eyes of people. What matters more is that the action be sound before The God and according to the measure of truth.

So the same root that turns the human being toward tawḥīd in al-ḥamdu lillāh warns the human being, in yuḥmadū, against hypocrisy, fame-seeking, and false desire for praise.

ḥāmidūn: those who live in ḥamd and acknowledgement

Ḥāmidūn is a plural active participle: those who praise, those who are in a state of ḥamd, or those who have become people of ḥamd.

This use shows that ḥamd is not merely a verbal sentence. It can become the quality of a group or an inner state of the human being. A ḥāmid is one who recognizes blessing, truth, Lordship, mercy, and the worthiness of The God, and acknowledges it.

Being among the people of ḥamd means that a person does not remember The God only in moments of ease. In the path of faith, repentance, worship, movement, hardship, and return, he preserves his gaze toward the source of good.

The true people of ḥamd are not those who merely speak beautiful phrases. Ḥamd must appear in one’s gaze, choices, ethics, and direction of life. If the tongue says ḥamd but the heart attributes blessing to the ego, tribe, power, or independent means, the reality of ḥamd has not yet become complete.

Muḥammad and Aḥmad: proper names and their connection to the field of ḥamd

Muḥammad and Aḥmad appear in the Qur’an as proper names and are connected to the semantic field of the root H M D. But they must be handled carefully.

Muḥammad is on the pattern mufaʿʿal, and in Arabic it can carry the meaning of one who is much praised or one who possesses qualities worthy of ḥamd. Aḥmad may be understood as more praised, or one more distinguished in relation to ḥamd.

Yet these names must not be used to move the Messenger beyond his human station. The Messenger, although he carries the message and is a great ethical example, is a servant and messenger of The God. Any praise that is right for him is dependent, limited, and within the frame of servanthood. The source of ḥamd is The God.

This distinction is important. By bringing names from the field of ḥamd, the Qur’an honors the place of the Messenger, but does not remove tawḥīd. The Messenger is praised because in servanthood, trustworthiness, delivering the message, and character, he is worthy of praise; not because he possesses absolute perfection independently of The God.

So Muḥammad and Aḥmad should be mentioned in the root page of H M D, but with tawḥīdic discipline: the Messenger’s names belong to the field of ḥamd, while al-ḥamdu lillāh remains the final criterion.

Divine ḥamd and human ḥamd

In the Qur’an, ḥamd can be performed by the human being, but its final and complete object is The God.

The human being performs ḥamd because he sees, understands, receives blessing, makes mistakes, returns, seeks guidance, and stands before truth. Human ḥamd is a sign of need, awareness, and humility.

But the ḥamd-worthiness of The God is not dependent on the human being. The God is Ḥamīd, even if human beings are heedless. He is worthy of ḥamd not because of our voices, but because of His own reality.

At the same time, the human being can be praised in a limited sense. Messengers, doers of good, the patient, reformers, and people of truth may be praised because of right action and worthy character. But this praise must never take the place of ḥamd for The God.

Human praise is healthy when it brings the human being closer to The God, not when it presents the created being as independent, absolute, or free of need.

Ḥamd, Lordship, mercy, and guidance

In Sūrah Al-Fātiḥah, ḥamd is immediately connected to Lordship:

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ al-ḥamdu lillāhi Rabbi al-ʿālamīn

This order is very important. The God is worthy of ḥamd, and the first description after that is Rabb al-ʿālamīn. This means that the ḥamd of The God is not separate from His Lordship. He is not merely a distant creator; He is the One who nurtures, preserves, orders, and carries the worlds toward their stages.

So ḥamd in Al-Fātiḥah does not begin with a dry image of power. After Rabb al-ʿālamīn comes al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm. This means the Lordship of The God is understood within the field of mercy, and His mercy is connected to guidance, return, and the Day of Judgement.

This structure teaches the human being that ḥamd is the beginning of conscious servanthood. First, the human being recognizes The God as the source of praise, the Lord of the worlds, The Merciful, The Compassionate, and Owner of the Day of Judgement; then he asks Him for guidance.

Therefore, ḥamd in Al-Fātiḥah is not merely the opening sound of the sūrah. Ḥamd sets the direction of the whole sūrah: knowing The God, acknowledging Lordship, hoping in mercy, becoming aware of accountability, and asking for guidance.

Suggested translation in Forqan

For al-ḥamd, translations such as “praise,” “gratitude,” or “praise and gratitude” each carry part of the meaning. But one must be careful not to reduce ḥamd to simple thanks.

al-ḥamd: true and comprehensive praise

Summary

The root H M D in the Qur’an invites the human being toward recognizing worthiness, acknowledging goodness, giving gratitude for blessing, and praising upon truth.

Ḥamd is not merely thanks. Ḥamd means that the human being recognizes good, perfection, mercy, Lordship, guidance, and truth, and returns them to their true source.

At the opening of Al-Fātiḥah, al-ḥamdu lillāh forms the foundation of the whole sūrah. Before asking for guidance, the human being learns that true praise belongs to The God: the Lord of the worlds, The Merciful, The Compassionate, and Owner of the Day of Judgement.

The Ḥamīd-ness of The God shows that The God is worthy of ḥamd whether people praise Him or not. Maḥmūd shows that praise must be connected to truth and worthiness. Yuḥmadū warns that seeking misplaced praise can pull a person into hypocrisy and deception. Ḥāmidūn shows that ḥamd can become a quality of the people of faith and acknowledgement. Muḥammad and Aḥmad also belong to the field of this root, but within the frame of servanthood and messengership, not in the station of absolute ḥamd.

So when ḥamd is understood correctly, it corrects the tongue, the gaze, gratitude, and tawḥīd. Ḥamd means taking praise back from the ego, idols, powers, intermediaries, and supposedly independent means, and returning it to the true source:

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ al-ḥamdu lillāhi Rabbi al-ʿālamīn
