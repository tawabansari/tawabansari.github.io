Decay and deterioration to the point of crumbling: from rotten wood and bones to the sound of air passing through cavities

1. Introducing the root and its main meanings

The Arabic root «ن خ ر» has two prominent semantic branches: the decay and deterioration of material, and a sound produced through the nose or by air passing through cavities.

In the first branch, the verb «نَخِرَ» describes something that has lost its strength through time or deteriorating conditions and has become rotten and liable to crumble. Rotten wood, deteriorated bones, and material that breaks apart under touch or pressure are familiar examples.

In the second branch, «نَخَرَ» refers to producing a sound through the nose. «نَخِير» names this sound, while «مَنْخِر» denotes a nostril or nasal passage.

These branches can converge in the image of rotten bone or wood: material whose interior has partly disappeared, leaving cavities, may make a sound when air passes through it.

This image, however, should not become a definite claim about linguistic history. Not every rotten bone makes a sound, nor is every sound from the nose related to decay.

Understanding this root therefore requires distinguishing the decay and weakening of material, its becoming hollow, and the sound of air passing through it. These features meet in some uses, but they are not identical.

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence concerns decayed bones.

The Qur’anic form

The root «ن خ ر» occurs only once in the Quran, at Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11, in this form:

«نَخِرَةً»: decayed and deteriorated.

The word is an adjective derived from the basic triliteral verb «نَخِرَ», on the pattern «فَعِلَة».

Other accepted recitations also have «نَاخِرَةً». Its morphological structure and some lexical explanations differ from those of «نَخِرَةً»; these differences are discussed below.

Other Arabic forms in this family include «نَخَرَ», «نَخِرَ», «نَخِير», «نَاخِر», «نَخِر», «مَنْخِر», «نُخْرَة», «نَخُور», and «تَنْخِير».

Their uses concern decay, sound, the nose, and certain animal behaviours. None occurs in another Qur’anic verse: the sole location has «نَخِرَةً» in the recitation of Ḥafṣ and «نَاخِرَةً» in other recitations.

2. Early Arabic uses and semantic branches

The decay of wood and bones

One clear Arabic use of this root is for the decay of something that was previously strong.

Arabic lexical descriptions record:

نَخِرَتِ الْخَشَبَةُ

nakhirati al-khashabatu

“The piece of wood became rotten.”

And:

نَخِرَ الْعَظْمُ

nakhira al-ʿaẓmu

“The bone decayed and deteriorated.”

Here decay describes a condition in which material gradually loses its strength and cohesion.

A piece of wood may retain its outward shape while becoming weak inside and crumbling under pressure.

Bones, too, can deteriorate, break, and crumble over time.

Arabic uses both «نَخِر» and «نَاخِر» for such material.

Lexical explanations describe decayed wood as losing its strength and crumbling when touched.

This shows that decay is not confined to changes in colour or outward ageing. In wood and bones, deterioration can affect the strength and structure of the material itself.

Nevertheless, the word does not establish that the material has completely disappeared or turned into dust.

Rotten wood may still retain some of its shape; similarly, a deteriorated bone can remain recognisable before it has completely crumbled.

Decay and susceptibility to crumbling are established meanings of this branch; complete destruction is not a necessary implication of every use.

Nakhr and nakhīr: a sound from the nose

The root’s other branch concerns sound.

Arabic uses «نَخَرَ الْإِنْسَانُ» for a person producing a sound through the nose.

The same verb is also recorded for animals such as horses and donkeys.

«نَخِير» denotes an audible, extended sound accompanying the movement or expulsion of breath through the nasal passages.

«مَنْخِر» or «مَنْخَر» denotes a nostril.

The lexical relationship is clear: one word names the sound, the other the place from which it emerges.

Nasal sound should nevertheless be distinguished from ordinary breathing. Not all breathing is called nakhīr; the word concerns a sound heard as someone breathes or expels air.

In contemporary Persian, the appropriate equivalent may be “snoring,” “snorting,” or “a nasal sound,” depending on the particular sound.

Arabic nakhīr is not confined to human snoring during sleep, since it also describes sounds made by certain animals.

Nukhrah: the nose or its front part

«نُخْرَة» is recorded for the nose itself, its front part, or the nostrils.

Its precise scope varies: some explanations concern the tip or front of the nose, others the nostril or nasal passage.

Thus «نُخْرَة» differs from «نَخِرَة», “decayed.”

Vowel marks and the word’s position in a sentence matter for identifying its meaning.

For example, the Qur’anic «عِظَامًا نَخِرَةً» concerns decayed bones, not a nose or respiratory passage.

Similar spelling alone should not lead to confusing these meanings.

Nakhūr: a use in tending and milking camels

Early Arabic also uses «نَخُور» for a she-camel, particularly one that does not readily let down her milk and whose nose or nostrils the milker touches to stimulate her.

In this use, «تَنْخِير» refers to rubbing or stimulating the camel’s nose.

This makes sense within Arab pastoral life, where animals’ behaviour and responses during milking were familiar practical concerns.

It has no direct connection with decay; its relationship with the nasal branch is clearer.

Nor does this example mean that every verb formed from the root necessarily produces a sound. Sometimes the action concerns the nose without the nasal sound itself being central.

Nukhrah of the wind: a strong blowing

Arabic records «نُخْرَةُ الرِّيحِ» for the wind’s strong blowing.

Here the wind is characterised by forceful movement and sound.

An audible resemblance between wind and nakhīr can be recognised, particularly when air passes through a narrow opening.

This resemblance does not mean that every strong wind must pass through a cavity, or that nukhrah always names the sound of wind.

The Qur’anic verse does not mention wind at all. The possible sound of wind in hollow bone is one lexical explanation of «ناخرة» and should not immediately be added to the verse itself.

The relationship between decay and the sound of passing air

One lexical analysis treats sound as the connection between the root’s branches.

In this analysis, nakhīr names a sound from the nose, while mankhir is the place from which that sound emerges.

It then connects the decay of wood and bone with hollowness: when their interior deteriorates, air can pass through the cavities and produce a sound resembling nakhīr.

The explanation is conceptually understandable, but needs caution.

First, not all rotten bones or pieces of wood necessarily have cavities that make a sound when wind passes through them.

Second, decay is independently attested as a meaning in Arabic. Understanding «نَخِرَتِ الْخَشَبَةُ» does not require assuming that the wood makes a sound.

Third, the analysis proposes a semantic relationship between the branches; that proposal alone does not establish the word’s actual historical development.

Thus hollowness and the sound of passing air offer an intelligible image connecting some uses, but should not be presented as the proven historical origin of the entire root family.

Poetic evidence and its historical standing

Other uses associated with this family are recorded in Arabic poetry.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd, a poet from before Islam, says in a poem about changes of power in Yemen:

بَعْدَ بَنِي تُبَّعٍ نَخَاوِرَةً
قَدِ اطْمَأَنَّتْ بِهِمْ مَرَازِبُهَا

baʿda banī tubbaʿin nakhāwiratan / qadi iṭmaʾannat bihim marāzibuhā

A rendering close to the sense:

“After the descendants of Tubbaʿ came nobles on whom the land’s governors and leaders relied.”

Some Arabic dictionaries place «نَخَاوِرَة» under this entry and explain it as nobles or leading men; other explanations associate it with pride and arrogance.

Its derivational relationship with decay or nasal sound is not clear.

The line is also not evidence for «نَخِرَة» describing rotten bone. Its value is as another form that lexicographers have recorded under this family.

Another witness occurs in a rajaz verse associated with the battle of Al-Qādisiyyah:

مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا صِرْتَ عِظَامًا نَاخِرَهْ

min baʿdi mā ṣirta ʿiẓāman nākhirah

Meaning:

“After you have become decayed bones.”

This line matters for understanding «ناخرة». Since it is associated with Al-Qādisiyyah, after the Quran’s revelation, it is not an independent pre-Qur’anic witness. Its wording also plainly echoes Al-Nāziʿāt, so it cannot unreservedly establish the word’s earliest meaning independently.

Overall, reliable lexical evidence supports both decay and nasal sound. This study, however, has not identified a definite, independent pre-Qur’anic poetic witness using precisely «نَخِرَة» for decayed bone.

This limitation should not be filled by inventing a line or attributing an unsupported phrase to a pre-Islamic poet.

3. The Qur’anic use: Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11

أَءِذَا كُنَّا عِظَامًا نَّخِرَةً

a-idhā kunnā ʿiẓāman nakhiratan

Translation:

“Even when we have become decayed bones?”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Here «نَخِرَةً» qualifies «عِظَامًا».

«عظام» is the plural of «عظم», “bone,” and «نخرة» describes the condition of those bones.

The meaning concerns bones that have deteriorated and decayed after death and the passage of time.

The verse is not, however, an independent, complete statement about the occurrence of decay. It forms part of the speech of people questioning the possibility of being brought back to life.

A. The surrounding verses: questioning a return to life

The preceding verse says:

يَقُولُونَ أَئِنَّا لَمَرْدُودُونَ فِي الْحَافِرَةِ

yaqūlūna a-innā la-mardūdūna fī al-ḥāfirati

Translation:

“They say: Will we really be returned to our former state?”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:10

They then ask:

أَءِذَا كُنَّا عِظَامًا نَّخِرَةً

a-idhā kunnā ʿiẓāman nakhiratan

“Even when we have become decayed bones?”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11

And continue:

قَالُوا تِلْكَ إِذًا كَرَّةٌ خَاسِرَةٌ

qālū tilka idhan karratun khāsiratun

“They said: That, then, would be a losing return.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:12

These three verses form a connected utterance.

A group questions or objects to returning to life after death, invoking decayed bones as grounds for finding such a return unlikely.

The role of «نخرة» is therefore clear: the speakers point to deteriorated bodily remains to emphasise the apparent difficulty of human re-creation.

Two matters must nevertheless be distinguished.

First, bone decay is a condition that ordinary language can describe.

Second, the speakers’ conclusion that resurrection is impossible or unlikely is a separate claim.

The Quran reports their speech here; it does not endorse their conclusion.

The validity of “decayed bones” as a description does not validate the argument of those denying re-creation.

B. The morphology and syntax of «نَخِرَةً»

«نَخِرَةً» is an adjective on the pattern «فَعِلَة».

Its structure can be compared with other words describing a thing’s condition or quality.

In the sentence, «عِظَامًا» is accusative, and «نَخِرَةً» is its accusative adjective.

Arabic nonhuman plurals can take a feminine singular adjective. The feminine singular form does not mean that the verse concerns one female bone.

It describes the collection of decayed bones.

Here «نخرة» describes bones that have themselves decayed, not something causing others to decay. Even «ناخرة», despite its active-participle structure, can derive from an intransitive verb; being an active participle does not itself give it the meaning “causing decay.”

This distinction matters in translation.

“Decay-causing bones” or “bones that cause others to rot” would be incorrect.

The natural, accurate rendering is “decayed bones.”

C. Does nakhr mean the complete disappearance of bone?

In Arabic, this branch concerns decay, deterioration, weakening, and crumbling.

The word itself does not establish that the bones have completely disappeared.

The verse still uses «عظام»: the speakers envisage human remains as bones.

If bones have completely turned to dust, other words may be used to describe them.

It is therefore preferable to understand nakhr as a condition of deterioration and decay, rather than necessarily the final stage of bodily destruction.

The verse concerns the possibility of human re-creation when even a person’s bones have decayed.

The speakers emphasise the great distance between former life and the present state of bodily remains.

The word alone cannot establish the length of decay, the biological process involved, or the precise amount of material remaining.

D. The Quran’s response to the denial of re-creation

The passage continues:

فَإِنَّمَا هِيَ زَجْرَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ

fa-innamā hiya zajratun wāḥidatun

“It is only a single cry.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:13

فَإِذَا هُمْ بِالسَّاهِرَةِ

fa-idhā hum bi-al-sāhirati

“And suddenly they are on the earth’s open surface.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14

This response challenges the deniers’ argument at its foundation.

They imagine bone decay as an obstacle to renewed life; the Quran presents the return as something posing no difficulty before the divine command.

These verses should not, however, be used to derive a physical theory of how bones and other bodily organs will be reconstructed.

The Quran speaks of the power to re-create, without explaining its biological or chemical stages.

Nor is «زَجْرَة» the meaning of «ن خ ر». It belongs to another root and occurs in the passage’s answer.

«نخرة» should therefore not be translated as the cry, command, or sound of resurrection.

4. The recitational variants «نَخِرَة» and «نَاخِرَة»

A major linguistic issue is the recitational variation at Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11.

Ḥafṣ’s transmission from ʿĀṣim reads «نَخِرَةً».

The recitations of Ḥamzah and Al-Kisāʾī, and Shuʿbah’s transmission from ʿĀṣim, have «نَاخِرَةً», with an alif after the nūn.

Both readings are established and deserve examination of their structure and meaning.

Nakhirah: decayed and deteriorated bone

«نَخِرَة» follows the pattern «فَعِلَة».

Lexical explanations connect this form with the decay and crumbling of bone.

This reading emphasises the material’s condition: a bone that has lost its former strength through deterioration.

The meaning is attested in Arabic for both wood and bones.

Thus nakhirah can be rendered “decayed,” “deteriorated,” or, more fully, “decayed and weakened.”

Nākhirah: decayed, or hollow and sounding

«نَاخِرَة» follows the pattern «فَاعِلَة».

There are two main explanations of its meaning.

In the first, «ناخرة» and «نخرة» are closely related or synonymous forms, both referring to decayed bone.

Arabic sometimes uses descriptions on both «فَعِل» and «فَاعِل» for one quality, as with «طَمِع» and «طَامِع», or «حَذِر» and «حَاذِر».

In the second explanation, the two forms are distinguished.

On this account, «نخرة» describes a thoroughly decayed and weakened bone, while «ناخرة» can describe a hollow bone that produces a sound resembling nakhīr when wind passes through its cavities.

This explanation connects with the root’s sound branch.

The image of passing wind should not be treated as the only possible meaning of «ناخرة», since reliable lexical explanations also accept the two forms as synonymous.

Nor should the morphological difference alone imply that one necessarily indicates more decay or must precede the other.

Such gradations are not uniform across lexical explanations and cannot be derived from the pattern alone.

Does either reading change the verse’s meaning?

Both forms fit the context of denying re-creation.

With «نخرة», the speakers emphasise the bones’ decay and deterioration.

With «ناخرة», the condition can also be pictured as hollow, deteriorated bones.

In either case, the subject remains the same: people who find resurrection unlikely point to the deteriorated state of bodily remains.

The variation matters linguistically, but should not become two conflicting theories about death or how resurrection occurs.

For Forqan’s base translation, “decayed” is suitable and compatible with both readings. The lexical explanation can discuss the attested distinction concerning hollowness and the sound of passing air.

Do similar verse endings establish a preference for «ناخرة»?

Nearby verse endings include «الْحَافِرَة», «خَاسِرَة», and «السَّاهِرَة».

Some lexical and interpretive explanations therefore regard «ناخرة» as better aligned with the sound of those endings.

Similarity in verse endings alone cannot establish semantic correctness or the historical priority of a linguistic form.

Both forms are known in the recitational tradition. Their assessment requires reliable transmission, linguistic evidence, and context.

The beauty of the sound cannot replace lexical examination.

5. Related roots and parallel verses

N KH R and R M M: decay and deterioration

The closest Qur’anic comparison is with «ر م م».

Yā Sīn says:

وَضَرَبَ لَنَا مَثَلًا وَنَسِيَ خَلْقَهُ ۖ قَالَ مَنْ يُحْيِي الْعِظَامَ وَهِيَ رَمِيمٌ

wa-ḍaraba lanā mathalan wa-nasiya khalqahu qāla man yuḥyī al-ʿiẓāma wa-hiya ramīmun

Translation:

“He set forth an example for Us, forgetting his own creation. He said: Who will bring the bones to life when they have decayed?”

Yā Sīn 36:78

Here «رَمِيمٌ» belongs to «ر م م» and concerns the deterioration and decay of bones.

The image closely resembles «عِظَامًا نَخِرَةً» in Al-Nāziʿāt.

Both verses report people who invoke bodily decay as a reason to find renewed life unlikely.

This does not mean that nakhr and ramīm have exactly the same meaning throughout Arabic.

Lexical uses of nakhr concern weakening, crumbling, and sometimes the material’s becoming hollow.

Ramīm likewise concerns deterioration and decay and can denote something’s deteriorated remains.

The fields overlap, but the evidence does not justify a fixed sequence in which nakhr always precedes ramīm, or ramīm always follows nakhr.

Both verses concern the denial of re-creation and the Quran’s answer.

Yā Sīn 36:79 says:

قُلْ يُحْيِيهَا الَّذِي أَنْشَأَهَا أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ ۖ وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ خَلْقٍ عَلِيمٌ

qul yuḥyīhā alladhī anshaʾahā awwala marratin wa-huwa bi-kulli khalqin ʿalīmun

Translation:

“Say: The One who brought them into being the first time will bring them to life; He knows every creation.”

The answer rests on the power of the Creator who accomplished the first creation.

This Qur’anic argument should not be built into the lexical meaning of either ramīm or nakhr.

N KH R and R F T: decay and crumbled remains

Al-Isrāʾ says:

وَقَالُوا أَءِذَا كُنَّا عِظَامًا وَرُفَاتًا أَءِنَّا لَمَبْعُوثُونَ خَلْقًا جَدِيدًا

wa-qālū a-idhā kunnā ʿiẓāman wa-rufātan a-innā la-mabʿūthūna khalqan jadīdan

Translation:

“They said: When we have become bones and crumbled remains, will we really be raised in a new creation?”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:49

«رُفَاتًا» belongs to «ر ف ت» and refers to crumbled, disintegrated remains.

The general difference in emphasis is:

Nakhr concerns decay and the material’s loss of strength.

Rufāt concerns the small, scattered remnants of something.

Decay may lead to crumbling, and a material can be both decayed and approaching the condition described by rufāt.

This connection does not make the roots identical.

Nor does the pairing of bones and rufāt establish two universal, fixed biological stages of bodily decomposition.

The verse draws on familiar conditions of deterioration and bodily remains to convey the deniers’ question.

N KH R and B L Y: wearing out and decay

In Arabic, «ب ل ي» concerns ageing, wearing out, and the loss of something’s former condition.

Clothing, for example, can wear out through use and time.

Wood and bone can also decay and weaken with time or environmental conditions.

In this respect, baliya and nakhira come close in some uses.

Baliya has a broader semantic field, however, extending beyond the decay of hard material.

In its Qur’anic use, nakhirah specifically describes decayed bone.

“Deteriorated” can therefore serve as an explanatory equivalent, while “decayed” conveys the condition of the bones more precisely.

N KH R and KH W Y: decay and becoming empty

«خ و ي» concerns becoming empty, remaining vacant, or collapsing and being emptied inside.

This comes close to the image of hollow bone in some explanations of «ناخرة».

Not everything hollow is necessarily decayed.

A sound vessel, for instance, can be hollow without any deterioration.

Conversely, decayed material can lose its strength before large cavities develop.

Emptiness and decay can therefore occur together but remain distinct qualities.

This distinction prevents reducing «نخرة» in Al-Nāziʿāt to “hollow” without explanation.

Study of KH W Y

N KH R and SH KH R: two kinds of sound

Arabic also uses «شَخِير» for a sound resembling snoring.

Some lexical explanations distinguish the two, assigning nakhīr to the nose and shakhīr to the throat.

The distinction helps explain the sound branch but should not become an absolute rule for all uses of both words.

In ordinary life, breathing sounds may involve both nose and throat, and descriptions do not always draw a precise boundary.

In any case, this comparison bears on the Qur’anic «نخرة» only through the recitational variation and the image of hollow bone.

The verse itself is not about a person or animal snoring.

6. Translation issues and proposals for Forqan

The main translation challenge is to avoid two restrictions.

First, «نخرة» should not be limited to “hollow” or “sounding,” with bone decay omitted.

Second, it should not be made to mean complete destruction or the bones’ definite transformation into dust: the word alone does not establish this.

For the deterioration branch, suitable equivalents include:

«نَخِرَ الْعَظْمُ»: the bone decayed and deteriorated.

«عَظْمٌ نَخِرٌ»: a decayed, weakened bone.

«خَشَبَةٌ نَخِرَةٌ»: a rotten piece of wood.

«نَاخِر»: decayed; in some uses, hollow and producing the sound of passing air.

For the sound branch:

«نَخِير»: snoring, snorting, or an audible nasal sound.

«مَنْخِر»: a nostril.

«نُخْرَة»: the nose or its front part, depending on usage.

In the camel-related use, «نَخُور» describes an animal whose nose is stimulated to encourage the release of milk.

The Qur’anic expression itself, however, is simply:

أَءِذَا كُنَّا عِظَامًا نَّخِرَةً

a-idhā kunnā ʿiẓāman nakhiratan

Three translation choices can be considered.

“Even when we have become decayed bones?”

This is concise and natural, retaining the established meaning of the word in Ḥafṣ’s recitation.

“Even when we have become decayed and deteriorated bones?”

This is clear in meaning but combines two close terms and is longer than the first version.

“Even when we have become decayed and hollow bones?”

This also incorporates the image in some explanations of «ناخرة», but adds more detail than needed for a base translation of Ḥafṣ’s recitation.

The preferable proposal for Forqan is therefore:

“Even when we have become decayed bones?”

A lexical note may explain that a decayed bone has lost its strength through deterioration and may have become weak, friable, or hollow.

The recitational discussion can also explain the interpretation of «ناخرة» as a bone through which air passes, producing a sound.

That image should not be imposed on the base text.

Renderings such as “destroyed bones,” “bones turned to dust,” or “bones reduced to nothing” are unsuitable because they go beyond decay to complete destruction.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root «ن خ ر» has two prominent semantic branches.

The first concerns decay, deterioration, and weakening of material. Wood and bone can lose so much strength that they crumble under touch or pressure.

The second concerns a sound produced through the nose or by air passing through passages. Nakhīr names the sound; mankhir denotes a nostril.

Some lexical explanations connect decay and sound through the material’s becoming hollow. The image may illuminate some uses but does not establish a definite historical origin for every meaning.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, at Al-Nāziʿāt 79:11, as «نَخِرَةً».

The verse forms part of the speech of people who find renewed life after death unlikely. They point to decayed bones to question the possibility of human re-creation.

The Quran, however, rejects their argument in the following verses.

Bone decay and the impossibility of re-creation are separate claims. The first is a material condition described by the root; the second is the deniers’ conclusion, which the Quran does not accept.

Accepted recitations have both «نخرة» and «ناخرة». Some lexical explanations treat them as synonymous; others distinguish decayed bone from hollow bone that makes a sound.

The distinction matters but does not change the overall subject of the verse.

Parallel verses, such as Yā Sīn 36:78 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:49, also use words including ramīm and rufāt to describe deterioration and bodily remains in the speech of those denying re-creation.

These words share a related semantic field but should not, without evidence, be converted into fixed, orderly stages of bodily decomposition.

A proposed final definition for Forqan

In its material branch, «ن خ ر» denotes the decay, deterioration, and loss of strength of something such as wood or bone: a condition that can involve weakness, crumbling, or hollowness.

In its sound branch, «نَخَرَ» means producing an audible sound through the nose, while «نَخِير» names that sound. The branches’ definite historical relationship has not been established, although hollowness and the sound of passing air can explain connections between some uses.

In the Quran, «نَخِرَةً» in «عِظَامًا نَخِرَةً» means “decayed bones.”

The adjective describes the bone’s deteriorated condition, neither its complete disappearance nor the impossibility of its being brought back to life.

Thus “decayed and deteriorated” suitably explains the Qur’anic use, while the sound branch and the image of hollow bone belong in separate lexical and recitational discussion.
