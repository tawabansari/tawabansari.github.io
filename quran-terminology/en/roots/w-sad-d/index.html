The Arabic root w-ṣ-d concerns doorways, thresholds, and entrance areas, and in another branch, closing or shutting firmly. وَصِيد can denote the threshold or area before a house or shelter; أَوْصَدَ means to close or shut a door.

This study considers three Qur’anic occurrences together: الْوَصِيد in the account of the cave companions, and مُؤْصَدَة describing fire in Al-Balad and Al-Humazah. The hamzated form is also analyzed under ʾ-ṣ-d; the distinction is explained below.

In the cave account, their dog stretches its forelegs at the entrance. The other two verses describe fire closed in upon those within it.

These uses belong to a related semantic field without being identical. Waṣīd names an entrance area; muʾṣadah describes a closed condition.

Understanding the family requires distinguishing the threshold or doorway, the area before an entrance, and the act of closing access. A description of closure does not supply details of doors and walls in the hereafter.

1. The root in early Arabic

Waṣīd: a doorway and the area before it

Early Arabic uses waṣīd for the doorway, threshold, and area near a house’s entrance.

فِنَاءُ الدَّارِ denotes the space before a house, where someone stands before entering its interior.

It may be a courtyard, open area, or part of the entrance zone.

Arabic dictionaries record three related explanations: doorway, threshold, and the area before a house.

They are related but not exactly identical.

A doorway is a place of entry and exit.

A threshold is a particular part of the entrance.

The area before a house may extend beyond the threshold itself.

A witness from old poetry

A verse transmitted in early Arabic sources says:

بِأَرْضٍ فَضَاءٍ لَا يُسَدُّ وَصِيدُهَا

bi-arḍin faḍāʾin lā yusaddu waṣīduhā

عَلَيَّ وَمَعْرُوفِي بِهَا غَيْرُ مُنْكَرِ

ʿalayya wa-maʿrūfī bihā ghayru munkari

“In open country whose entrance is not shut against me, and where my kindness is not unrecognized.”

Waṣīd here concerns a doorway or point of entry.

The poet describes open country whose entrance is not closed against him.

The image concerns access and the absence of an obstructed entrance. Just as a closed door can prevent entry, an open doorway permits passage. Some explanations understand the open country as having no door there to be shut at all.

This does not make waṣīd itself mean freedom, social acceptance, or generosity. Such implications arise from the complete poetic image and its figurative doorway.

Attribution varies: the verse is assigned to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, to ʿUbayd ibn Wahb al-ʿAbsī, and in another transmission to al-Akhṭal.

Those attributions have different historical implications, so the verse cannot confidently be assigned to a particular pre-Islamic poet.

It nevertheless witnesses waṣīd as a doorway in the Arabic literary tradition. Disputed attribution alone does not establish its precise antiquity.

Waṣīdah: an enclosure for animals

Waṣīdah is also recorded for a pen or enclosure for livestock.

Some explanations describe a place built among stones or on a mountain for sheep and other livestock.

A distinction is recorded between waṣīdah, especially a stone structure, and ḥaẓīrah, which may be made from branches and other materials.

This is not an absolute rule governing every use, but it associates waṣīdah with a defined enclosure for keeping animals.

Both the enclosure’s boundaries and access matter here.

That sense does not establish that al-waṣīd in the cave account was a livestock pen.

Waṣīdah and waṣīd are related forms whose referents must be determined separately in each sentence.

Awṣada: closing and shutting

The other branch concerns closing a door and securing an entrance.

أَوْصَدَ الْبَابَ means “he closed the door.”

أَوْصَدَ الْقِدْرَ is also recorded for covering or closing the top of a pot.

This shows that closure is not limited to a house’s door.

With a pot, the opening of a vessel is closed.

With a house, the route of entry and exit is closed.

In both, an opening or means of access is shut.

Another poetic witness says:

تَحِنُّ إِلَى أَجْبَالِ مَكَّةَ نَاقَتِي

taḥinnu ilā ajbāli Makkata nāqatī

وَمِنْ دُونِهَا أَبْوَابُ صَنْعَاءَ مُؤْصَدَةٌ

wa-min dūnihā abwābu Ṣanʿāʾa muʾṣadah

“My camel longs for Mecca’s mountains, while the gates of Ṣanʿāʾ stand closed between her and them.”

Muʾṣadah describes the gates as closed.

This agrees with the Qur’anic sense of the word.

The verse’s attribution and date are not sufficiently clear to make it a certain pre-Islamic witness. Early lexical and literary sources quote it to explain the closing of doors.

The evidence thus supports entrance and closure in Arabic tradition; deciding which was historically first would require older, independent evidence.

2. Forms and semantic branches

Important Arabic forms include:

وَصِيد: doorway, threshold, or the area before a house; in other uses, an enclosure for livestock.

وَصِيدَة: a pen-like enclosure for keeping animals.

وُصُد and وَصَائِد: recorded plurals for waṣīd and associated enclosures.

أَوْصَدَ: he closed or shut a door or entrance.

إِيصَاد: closing or shutting.

مُوصَد: closed or shut.

مُوصَدَة: the feminine form describing something closed or shut.

وِصَاد: in some Arabic uses, something that closes an opening or passage.

اسْتَوْصَدَ: in livestock usage, he made or provided a waṣīdah enclosure.

Dictionaries also record other senses for وَصَدَ, including remaining fixed and inserting the weft into the warp in weaving.

وَصَّاد is also recorded for a weaver.

Waṣīd can additionally describe plants growing close together at their bases.

These senses can be related to stability, proximity, and connection, but the evidence does not establish that all developed from one original historical image.

Arabic also records أَصِيد alongside وَصِيد for an entrance.

This variation between initial wāw and hamzah matters when examining Qur’anic readings.

Actual Qur’anic forms

The present grouping considers three occurrences, while retaining the morphological question concerning ʾ-ṣ-d:

بِالْوَصِيدِ in Al-Kahf 18:18.

مُؤْصَدَةٌ in Al-Balad 90:20.

مُؤْصَدَةٌ in Al-Humazah 104:8.

The latter two have the same form and meaning but different grammatical functions.

Waṣīdah, waṣāʾid, wiṣād, and the independent verb awṣada do not occur in the Qur’an.

Semantic center

Two definitions are needed for these Qur’anic uses:

Waṣīd: a doorway, threshold, or area near an entrance.

Muʾṣadah: closed or shut; describing a condition in which an opening or access is closed.

The connection is understandable: an entrance can be open or closed, and īṣād concerns closing it.

That connection does not make waṣīd always mean a closed door. A doorway can be open, with someone at its threshold.

Muʾṣadah, conversely, expresses closure rather than merely naming an entrance.

3. Al-Kahf 18:18 — The cave companions’ dog at the entrance

وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ ۚ وَنُقَلِّبُهُمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ ۖ وَكَلْبُهُمْ بَاسِطٌ ذِرَاعَيْهِ بِالْوَصِيدِ ۚ لَوِ اطَّلَعْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ لَوَلَّيْتَ مِنْهُمْ فِرَارًا وَلَمُلِئْتَ مِنْهُمْ رُعْبًا

wa-taḥsabuhum ayqāẓan wa-hum ruqūdun wa-nuqallibuhum dhāta al-yamīni wa-dhāta al-shimāli wa-kalbuhum bāsiṭun dhirāʿayhi bi-al-waṣīdi lawi iṭṭalaʿta ʿalayhim la-wallayta minhum firāran wa-la-muliʾta minhum ruʿbā

Al-Kahf 18:18

“You would think them awake, though they are asleep. We turn them to the right and to the left, while their dog stretches its forelegs at the threshold. Had you looked upon them, you would have turned away from them in flight and been filled with dread of them.”

بِالْوَصِيدِ consists of the preposition bi- and the definite noun al-waṣīd.

وَكَلْبُهُمْ refers to the cave companions’ dog.

بَاسِطٌ ذِرَاعَيْهِ describes its posture: its forelegs are stretched out.

بِالْوَصِيدِ specifies its location.

Waṣīd here names a location or entrance area, rather than the act of closing or the condition of a closed door.

Threshold or the area before the cave?

Lexical explanations offer three related senses: threshold, doorway, and the area before an entrance.

In context, all concern the cave’s entrance zone.

The companions are inside, while their dog is described at the entrance.

Al-Kahf 18:17 speaks of فَجْوَةٍ مِنْهُ, a spacious area within the cave.

Al-Kahf 18:18 locates the dog through al-waṣīd.

Fajwah and waṣīd should therefore remain distinct.

Fajwah concerns the spacious interior area in that verse.

Waṣīd concerns the threshold or entrance here.

“The cave’s threshold” is therefore a clear, suitable explanation of this use.

Did the cave have a constructed door?

Ordinary references to a threshold or doorway may evoke a building with a wooden or metal door.

A cave need not have such a door.

Its natural opening can also have a threshold or entrance: the place where the exterior meets the interior.

Al-waṣīd therefore does not establish a manufactured door, wooden frame, or locked entrance.

The Qur’an identifies the dog’s location in the entrance area.

This matters lexically: “doorway” should not be restricted to the components of a modern house.

Was the dog guarding them?

A dog with its forelegs stretched at a cave entrance may evoke guarding and watchfulness.

Dogs have been used as guards, and their position at an entrance can fit that role.

The verse itself, however, does not say the dog was assigned guard duty or deliberately blocked others’ entry.

بَاسِطٌ ذِرَاعَيْهِ describes its posture.

بِالْوَصِيدِ describes its location.

Guarding can be a reflection on the scene, but it is not waṣīd’s lexical meaning.

The verse’s final clause says an observer would flee in fear.

That fear is expressed separately and should not be attributed solely to the dog without evidence.

Taking refuge in the cave

In the companions’ discussion in Al-Kahf 18:16, they are urged to take refuge in the cave; this should not automatically be presented as a separate direct divine command.

The following verses describe their situation and divine protection.

The entrance forms part of the scene identifying their refuge.

Waṣīd alone does not establish that the cave was sealed on every side, locked, or impossible to leave.

Al-Kahf 18:19 describes their decision after waking to send one of their number into the city.

That continuation does not fit an inescapable, completely sealed prison.

Waṣīd in Al-Kahf 18:18 should retain its spatial meaning of threshold or entrance.

4. Al-Balad 90:20 — Fire closed in upon its occupants

عَلَيْهِمْ نَارٌ مُؤْصَدَةٌ

ʿalayhim nārun muʾṣadah

Al-Balad 90:20

“Upon them is a fire closed in.”

مُؤْصَدَةٌ modifies نَارٌ here.

Nār is grammatically feminine, and its adjective is feminine too.

عَلَيْهِمْ refers to those mentioned in the preceding verse.

The verse describes fire closed in upon that group.

The meaning of muʾṣadah

Muʾṣadah concerns being closed or firmly shut.

Arabic associates it with closing doors and shutting entrances.

Here it describes fire.

The image is fire closed in upon its occupants, with no open route of release in the situation described.

The word does not specify wall materials, the number of doors, or the kind of lock.

Those details cannot be extracted from its lexical meaning.

The context of Al-Balad

The preceding verses are needed to understand this passage.

Al-Balad 90:11 concerns undertaking the difficult ascent.

Al-Balad 90:13 mentions freeing a person from bondage.

Al-Balad 90:14 describes feeding others on a day of hunger and hardship.

Al-Balad 90:15 and Al-Balad 90:16 identify an orphan who is a relative and a destitute person.

Al-Balad 90:17 brings together faith and mutual encouragement to steadfastness and mercy.

Al-Balad 90:18 identifies the companions of the right, أَصْحَابُ الْمَيْمَنَةِ.

Al-Balad 90:19 describes those who deny God’s signs as أَصْحَابُ الْمَشْأَمَةِ.

Al-Balad 90:20 portrays the latter group’s outcome through fire closed in upon them.

The passage is more than an image of an enclosed place: its wider context concerns conduct, faith, social responsibility, and their outcome.

Muʾṣadah itself does not mean denying signs, lacking mercy, or refusing to help those in need.

Those concerns belong to the preceding verses.

Muʾṣadah describes the fire’s closed condition.

Does being closed mean remaining so forever?

A closed passage may remain closed briefly or for a long time.

The act of closing does not itself specify duration.

“The door was shut” does not, in ordinary language, mean it can never reopen.

Al-Balad 90:20 describes fire as closed in; the duration and ultimate character of that condition are separate from the root’s lexical meaning.

Punishment’s duration requires studying all relevant verses about the same group, their temporal qualifications, and other Qur’anic expressions.

Muʾṣadah alone cannot establish a complete theory of the duration of every punishment in the hereafter.

Conversely, the explicit image of closure should not be made vague or ineffective.

The verse describes a situation in which no open route of release is presented.

5. Al-Humazah 104:8 — Al-Ḥuṭamah closed in upon them

إِنَّهَا عَلَيْهِمْ مُؤْصَدَةٌ

innahā ʿalayhim muʾṣadah

Al-Humazah 104:8

“Indeed, it is closed in upon them.”

Muʾṣadah has the same form as in Al-Balad 90:20.

The sentence structure differs.

Al-Balad says:

عَلَيْهِمْ نَارٌ مُؤْصَدَةٌ

ʿalayhim nārun muʾṣadah

Al-Balad 90:20

Al-Humazah says:

إِنَّهَا عَلَيْهِمْ مُؤْصَدَةٌ

innahā ʿalayhim muʾṣadah

Al-Humazah 104:8

The pronoun -hā refers to what has already been introduced: al-Ḥuṭamah, God’s kindled fire.

Muʾṣadah functions as the predicate of inna, expressing the closed condition.

The context of Al-Humazah

Al-Humazah 104:1 condemns faultfinding and derision.

Al-Humazah 104:2 describes someone who gathers and counts wealth.

Al-Humazah 104:3 presents his belief that his wealth has made him immortal.

Al-Humazah 104:4 rejects that belief and speaks of his being cast into al-Ḥuṭamah.

Al-Humazah 104:6 identifies it as نَارُ اللَّهِ الْمُوقَدَةُ, God’s kindled fire.

Al-Humazah 104:7 describes that fire reaching the hearts.

Al-Humazah 104:8 then states that it is closed in upon its occupants.

The sequence matters.

First the person’s conduct and belief are introduced.

Then his outcome is stated.

Finally the fire’s condition is described.

Muʾṣadah is only one description in the scene; it does not exhaust al-Ḥuṭamah’s meaning or all the punishment’s characteristics.

Al-mūqadah and muʾṣadah

The words resemble one another in sound and writing but have different roots and meanings.

الْمُوقَدَةُ belongs to w-q-d and concerns the kindling of fire.

Muʾṣadah, in the family examined here, concerns being closed in.

The surah thus presents two distinct characteristics:

The fire is kindled.

It is closed in upon its occupants.

Kindled does not mean closed, just as closed does not itself mean burning.

Translation must preserve the distinction.

What does fī ʿamadin mumaddadah add?

Al-Humazah 104:9 contains فِي عَمَدٍ مُمَدَّدَةٍ.

It speaks of extended pillars and relates to the preceding image.

The exact grammatical and interpretive connection with the fire’s closure is a separate question.

Muʾṣadah alone does not establish iron nails, particular locks, or doors constructed according to familiar earthly architecture.

Al-Humazah 104:9 does not explain every material component of the scene either.

The Qur’an’s verbal image should be preserved without completing the hereafter’s physical details through conjecture or storytelling.

6. W-ṣ-d, ʾ-ṣ-d, and morphology

An important issue is the existence of two related Arabic forms:

أَوْصَدَ — awṣada.

And:

آصَدَ — āṣada.

Both are recorded for closing and shutting.

The noun denoting an entrance also has two recorded forms:

وَصِيد — waṣīd.

And:

أَصِيد — aṣīd.

Al-waṣīd and al-aṣīd can both denote a doorway or entrance area.

Their proximity raises an etymological question.

Did hamzah replace wāw, or are these related forms with separate histories?

Lexicons record both, but similarity of meaning alone cannot establish their precise historical origins.

The readings muʾṣadah and mūṣadah

Recognized Qur’anic readings have the adjective both with and without hamzah.

One form is:

مُؤْصَدَةٌ — muʾṣadah.

The other is:

مُوصَدَةٌ — mūṣadah.

The transmission of Ḥafṣ reads the hamzated muʾṣadah.

Other readings have the form without hamzah.

Both mean closed or shut.

Morphologically, the form without hamzah is associated with awṣada from w-ṣ-d.

The hamzated form is associated with āṣada and ʾ-ṣ-d.

Sources also discuss phonetic substitution and the easing of hamzah as possible explanations of the variation.

A reading’s surface form should therefore not be used without explanation to claim either completely independent histories or an undisputed single root.

Closure is clear in both readings.

The variation does not change the Al-Balad and Al-Humazah passages from closing to opening or to some unrelated condition.

Is muʾṣadah a passive participle?

Morphologically it is the feminine passive-participle form.

It describes something that has been closed or is in a closed state.

In Al-Balad 90:20 it modifies fire.

In Al-Humazah 104:8 it is the predicate of inna.

The different grammatical roles do not change the word’s central meaning.

The passive participle alone identifies neither who closes it, nor the precise time, nor the instruments used.

Any such details require independent evidence from other verses; absent that evidence, the description should not be supplemented.

7. Comparison with related roots

w-ṣ-d (waṣd) and gh-l-q (ghalq)

Gh-l-q concerns closing and being closed.

Awṣada likewise means to close or shut a door.

The meanings can approach one another in particular contexts.

W-ṣ-d also includes waṣīd, naming a doorway, threshold, or area before an entrance.

Not all its derivatives can therefore be explained simply as closing.

Saying a door was closed does not identify a lock, nail, stone, or other means of closure.

The same restraint applies to Qur’anic muʾṣadah.

w-ṣ-d (waṣd) and ṭ-b-q (ṭabaq)

One branch of ṭ-b-q concerns covering and placing one thing over another.

أَطْبَقَ الْبَابَ can mean closing a door firmly.

Lexicons also explain muʾṣadah through muṭbaqah.

Closure can thus involve covering an opening completely.

Ṭ-b-q and w-ṣ-d do not share every use.

Ṭabaq includes layers, coverings, and other senses; waṣīd names an entrance area.

Explaining muʾṣadah through muṭbaqah clarifies closure without identifying the roots as one.

w-ṣ-d (waṣīd) and b-w-b (bāb)

Bāb concerns a door or route of entry and exit.

Waṣīd can likewise denote a doorway or entrance area.

A doorway and a door are not exactly the same.

There may be a manufactured door with someone standing at its threshold.

A cave may instead have a natural entrance without a separate constructed door.

Al-waṣīd in Al-Kahf 18:18 fits such an entrance area.

“Doorway” or “threshold” is therefore more accurate than “wooden door” or “closed door.”

w-ṣ-d (waṣīd) and f-j-w (fajwah)

Fajwah concerns a spacious area or open interval.

In Al-Kahf 18:17, the companions occupy فَجْوَةٍ مِنْهُ.

In Al-Kahf 18:18, their dog is described at al-waṣīd.

The Qur’an uses two different words for two locations within one scene.

Fajwah concerns the cave’s spacious interior.

Waṣīd concerns its entrance and threshold.

That distinction does not determine the cave’s exact dimensions or the distance between the dog and the companions.

w-ṣ-d (waṣd) and ṣ-d-d (ṣadd)

Ṣ-d-d concerns barring, turning away, or preventing access.

Closing a passage can accordingly prevent someone from passing.

Ṣadd and waṣd nevertheless belong to different roots.

The Qur’anic expression ṣadd ʿan sabīl Allāh can concern obstructing movement or guidance along a path.

Muʾṣadah describes a closed condition.

Similar consonants should not merge the meanings into one root.

A closed door alone does not establish the closer’s moral intention. It may be shut for protection, order, or safety.

In punishment passages, the adverse meaning comes from the context and the fire, rather than every linguistic use of closing.

w-ṣ-d (waṣd) and w-ṭ-d (waṭd)

W-ṭ-d concerns making something firm or stable.

The two families are close in sound, and early sources report nearby forms with variant consonants in particular transmissions.

Making something firm and closing an entrance are not the same meaning.

A door can be firmly shut without making the two roots interchangeable in all their uses.

Waṭd’s association with stabilization likewise does not justify adding a nail or a particular fastening device to muʾṣadah’s meaning merely because the roots sound similar.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The three passages require two principal equivalents: one for the entrance noun al-waṣīd, another for the adjective muʾṣadah. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

Al-Kahf 18:18 — bi-al-waṣīd

Suggested rendering:

“Their dog stretches its forelegs at the threshold.”

This rendering has several strengths.

It preserves waṣīd’s spatial meaning.

It identifies the threshold or entrance.

It assumes neither a manufactured nor a closed door without evidence.

“The cave’s entrance” is also suitable:

“Their dog stretches its forelegs at the cave’s entrance.”

The preferred Persian expression explicitly identifies the entrance threshold for a general reader.

“The cave’s courtyard” is less precise because a cave need not have a constructed courtyard.

“The cave’s closed door” is incorrect: waṣīd does not express closure here.

Al-Balad 90:20 — muʾṣadah

Suggested rendering:

“Upon them is a fire closed in.”

“A fire is closed in upon them” is also concise and defensible.

It retains closure without inventing door or wall materials.

“A fire from which there is no escape” can explain the described condition, but should not replace the direct rendering of muʾṣadah: it substitutes a consequence for the image of closure.

Al-Humazah 104:8 — muʾṣadah

Suggested rendering:

“Indeed, it is closed in upon them.”

The pronoun -hā refers to the fire, al-Ḥuṭamah, introduced earlier.

The basic translation need not repeat “fire” in place of the pronoun.

“Indeed, it has been shut upon them” is also defensible; the preferred Persian wording conveys the complete closure more naturally.

The wording should remain consistent with Al-Balad 90:20 because both use the same adjective.

Other equivalents in the family

Waṣīd in general usage may be a doorway, threshold, or area before an entrance.

Waṣīdah may be a livestock enclosure or stone pen.

Awṣada al-bāb means “he closed the door” or “shut it firmly.”

Awṣada al-qidr means “he covered the pot” or “closed its opening.”

Mūṣad means closed or shut.

Forms involving stability or weaving require equivalents fitting their uses; they cannot simply be rendered as doorway or closing a door.

9. Conclusion

Arabic w-ṣ-d concerns doorways, thresholds, and entrances, and in another branch, closing and shutting.

Waṣīd is recorded for the area before a house, its threshold, or doorway. Waṣīdah describes a pen-like enclosure, particularly a stone livestock pen.

Awṣada is also established for closing doors and openings.

The poetic witnesses support these uses, but some attributions and precise dates are uncertain. They cannot establish a definitive history of every semantic branch.

This study groups three Qur’anic occurrences together, retaining the morphological relationship between w-ṣ-d and ʾ-ṣ-d.

Al-waṣīd in Al-Kahf 18:18 locates the companions’ dog near the entrance. It does not itself establish a manufactured door or lock.

Muʾṣadah in Al-Balad 90:20 describes fire closed in upon its occupants.

Al-Humazah 104:8 uses the same description of al-Ḥuṭamah, God’s kindled fire.

Closure is clear in both, but the word alone does not determine the architecture of the hereafter, the number of doors, fastening materials, or the condition’s final duration.

The recognized readings muʾṣadah and mūṣadah also show the linguistic proximity of w-ṣ-d and ʾ-ṣ-d. The precise historical origin of the wāw–hamzah variation should not be declared certain without evidence.

The Qur’anic uses can be defined as follows:

Waṣīd: the threshold, doorway, or area near an entrance.

Muʾṣadah: closed or shut; describing something whose opening or access is closed.

They can be understood through a related image of an entrance and its closure, without substituting one meaning for the other.

Al-Kahf 18:18 describes a location.

Al-Balad 90:20 and Al-Humazah 104:8 describe a closed condition.

For Forqan, “threshold” for al-waṣīd and “closed in” for muʾṣadah are suitable equivalents.

The central distinction is that being at a threshold, an entrance being closed, and being confined within a closed space are different situations. Each passage has its own construction and context, and translation must preserve those differences.
