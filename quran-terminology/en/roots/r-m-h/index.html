The spear, spear thrusts, and striking with an implement or limb

Brief meaning: A spear, a long implement for striking and driving its point into a target; spearing, and in another use an animal’s hind-leg kick. Extended uses describe things resembling a spear in shape or function.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The three-letter root R M Ḥ is best known through the spear and its use. Rumḥ names an ancient hunting and fighting implement, generally with a long shaft and sharp point, enabling a person to strike beyond the reach of the hand.

Ramaḥa can mean to strike with a spear. Rāmiḥ describes someone who spears, but can also describe someone equipped with or carrying a spear.

The family extends beyond human weapons. Early Arabic uses ramaḥa for an animal kicking with its hind legs. Spear imagery also appears in plant awns, animal horns, and a star name.

This family has a familiar concrete noun at its center; other uses often relate to a spear’s shape, impact, or function.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Māʾidah, where believers are tested with accessible game.

The form examined and Qur’anic derivative

وَرِمَاحُكُمْ — Al-Māʾidah 5:94

The expression has three overt components: the conjunction wa-, the noun rimāḥ, and the second-person plural pronoun -kum. The noun and pronoun form a possessive construction; that relationship is not a fourth morpheme.

Rimāḥ is a broken plural of rumḥ, meaning spears.

The expression means “and your spears.”

Other recorded members of the family include:

  • رُمْح: A spear.
  • رِمَاح and أَرْمَاح: Plurals of spear.
  • رَمَحَ يَرْمَحُ: To strike with a spear; also an animal’s kicking with its hind legs.
  • رَمْح: Spearing or striking in verbal usage.
  • رَامِح: A spear bearer or user; in some uses, a horned animal.
  • رَمَّاح: A spear maker or someone associated with spears.
  • رِمَاحَة: The craft of making spears.
  • مُرَامَحَة: Spear fighting.
  • تَرَامَحُوا: They fought one another with spears.
  • رُمَيْح: The diminutive of rumḥ, a small spear.

These establish the lexical field, but only wa-rimāḥukum occurs in the Qur’an.

2. Semantic core and early Arabic usage

Rumḥ: a familiar implement

One early lexical analysis begins with the familiar noun rumḥ, then explains its derivatives and related uses, rather than proposing an extensive abstract definition.

This matters: a concrete object can provide the clearest starting point for understanding a lexical family.

Rumḥ names a weapon used to strike and thrust its point into a target.

A typical spear has a long shaft and sharp point, often a metal head attached to the shaft in familiar military constructions.

The noun alone does not specify exact length, wood, point shape, or manufacturing method.

“Spear” is consequently a clear, sufficient translation. Iron construction, a particular size, or other specifications require separate evidence.

Ramaḥa: striking with a spear

An early lexical account records:

رَمَحَهُ يَرْمَحُهُ رَمْحًا

ramaḥahu yarmaḥuhu ramḥan

That is, “he struck him with a spear.”

The verb here describes an action performed using a spear.

It is directly related to the implement’s name.

The name of the implement and the action performed with it remain distinct.

Rumḥ is the spear itself; ramaḥa can express spearing.

Rāmiḥ can also designate someone carrying a spear, without saying that they are striking at that moment.

Possessing a spear therefore does not establish fighting, hunting, or injury.

The Qur’anic rimāḥukum names implements, rather than reporting a spear thrust.

The spear and human reach

A spear extends a person’s reach.

An unassisted hand reaches only within its immediate range; a spear can strike farther away.

This has mattered to hunters and fighters.

Its length does not mean that a spear is always used from a great distance or thrown.

It is commonly used for thrusting, and may also be thrown in some circumstances. Its name alone does not determine its movement.

Al-Māʾidah 5:94 names a familiar means of reaching game, without prescribing one technical method of spear use.

Rāmiḥ and rammāḥ: carrying and making spears

Rāmiḥ can designate a spear bearer or someone striking with one.

Lexical accounts distinguish these uses.

A person can hold a spear without yet using it; rāmiḥ does not invariably describe an ongoing thrust.

Rammāḥ denotes a maker of spears or someone equipped with them.

Rimāḥah names the craft of spear making.

Spears thus belonged not only to combat but also to recognizable activities of manufacture, maintenance, carrying, and use.

The implement or occupation does not itself determine someone’s moral character.

A spear can serve hunting, defense, or aggression; rāmiḥ alone does not judge conduct as good or bad.

An animal kicking with its hind legs

Ramaḥathu al-dābbah describes an animal kicking a person or another animal with its hind legs.

This is particularly familiar with horses, mules, and donkeys.

One early lexical explanation compares the animal’s strike to a spear thrust.

The analogy is understandable.

A spear user drives a long implement toward a target, while an animal can thrust its hind legs toward something and strike it.

The analogy does not mean that the animal literally uses a spear or that every kick matches a spear thrust in movement and effect.

The attested fact is that the same verb names two different actions.

Rimāḥ also names an animal’s kicking habit or defect in some usages. This has the same form as the plural “spears”; context distinguishes them.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:94, “your hands and your spears” in a hunting context clearly denotes human implements, not animal kicking.

Insect movement and other uses

Ramaḥa al-jundub is also recorded.

Jundub names a jumping insect; this expression describes its striking pebbles with its leg or legs.

The branch concerning movement and striking thus also applies to animal actions.

Some lexical accounts use the family for lightning flashing in short, closely spaced bursts.

This limited usage should not become the entire root’s primary definition.

Qur’anic rimāḥukum has no direct connection with insect movement or lightning.

Plant awns: the spear as protective imagery

Of a grass such as buhmā, with sharp awns, Arabic says:

أَخَذَتْ رِمَاحَهَا

akhadhat rimāḥahā

That is, “it took up its spears.”

Its sharp parts have dried and become difficult for grazing animals to approach or eat.

The figurative expression compares sharp awns to spears.

It does not assign the plant literal weapons or a human intention to defend itself.

Well-conditioned, desirable camels are likewise said to have “taken up their spears”: their owner refrains from slaughtering them because of their value or attractiveness.

Preventing or deterring an action is prominent here.

These uses do not make “preventing” the meaning of rumḥ in every context.

A spear may deter, but deterrence is a function or consequence, not the implement’s entire definition.

3. Pre-Islamic poetry and spear usage

Spears in battle: ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād

The transmitted Muʿallaqah of the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah includes:

يَدْعُونَ عَنْتَرَ وَالرِّمَاحُ كَأَنَّهَا
أَشْطَانُ بِئْرٍ فِي لَبَانِ الْأَدْهَمِ

yadʿūna ʿantara wa-al-rimāḥu ka-annahā / ashṭānu biʾrin fī labāni al-adhami

Translation: “They call ʿAntar, while the spears in the black horse’s breast are like the ropes of a well.”

The verse describes battle.

Rimāḥ is the plural of spear.

Ashṭān means well ropes, labān the breast, and al-adham here the poet’s black horse.

The spears directed into the horse’s breast are compared in length and arrangement to well ropes; the image is not merely of weapons standing at a harmless distance.

This transmitted pre-Islamic verse clearly uses rimāḥ for actual weapons.

The word here means neither arrows, swords, nor an animal’s kicks.

The poetic image draws on the spears’ shape and placement.

The surrounding scene also describes the horse’s encounter with the spears and its response, linking them with confrontation and injury.

Rimāḥ itself still names the implements; wounds, bleeding, and battle intensity come from the wider description.

The spear and its point

The same poem also uses al-asinnah.

Sinān names a spearhead; asinnah is its plural.

The distinction matters.

Rumḥ names the whole spear, while sinān commonly names its pointed head.

Both may describe the same battle scene without being lexically identical.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:94, rimāḥukum denotes the audience’s spears, not only their metal points.

Rammāḥah in poetry attributed to Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī contains rammāḥah:

بِرَمَّاحَةٍ تَنْفِي التُّرَابَ كَأَنَّهَا
هِرَاقَةُ عُقٍّ مِنْ شُعَيْبَى مُعَجَّلِ

bi-rammāḥatin tanfī al-turāba ka-annahā / hirāqatu ʿuqqin min shuʿaybā muʿajjali

This verse is preserved in lexical accounts. Approximately: “With a spear thrust scattering dust, like bitter water poured hurriedly from a waterskin.” Its transmission varies, including shuʿayb in place of shuʿaybā; the translation does not establish a single certain reading.

One explanation takes rammāḥah as a spear thrust and suggests that it stands for ramḥah, a single act of spearing.

Its morphological explanation is therefore not entirely straightforward.

It attests a use connected with spearing but cannot establish a rule for every derivative of R M Ḥ.

The word’s form, its interpreted meaning, and uncertainty in its lexical explanation should remain distinct.

A star name: al-Simāk al-Rāmiḥ

Arabic star names include al-Simāk al-Rāmiḥ, “the spear-bearing Simāk.”

The naming explanation refers to another star apparently ahead of it, imagined as a spear.

It is contrasted with al-Simāk al-Aʿzal, the Simāk imagined without such a weapon.

Spear imagery thus also appears in celestial naming.

The star has no material spear; the explanation draws on apparent positions and visual imagery.

The name cannot establish a theory of stellar composition or true distances.

Rumḥ therefore names both a real implement and a source of analogy and naming in early Arabic.

4. Wa-rimāḥukum in Al-Māʾidah 5:94

The verse

Al-Māʾidah 5:94

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَيَبْلُوَنَّكُمُ اللَّهُ بِشَيْءٍ مِنَ الصَّيْدِ تَنَالُهُ أَيْدِيكُمْ وَرِمَاحُكُمْ لِيَعْلَمَ اللَّهُ مَنْ يَخَافُهُ بِالْغَيْبِ ۚ فَمَنِ اعْتَدَىٰ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ فَلَهُ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū la-yabluwannakumu allāhu bi-shayʾin mina al-ṣaydi tanāluhu aydīkum wa-rimāḥukum li-yaʿlama allāhu man yakhāfuhu bi-al-ghaybi fa-man iʿtadā baʿda dhālika fa-lahu ʿadhābun alīmun

Al-Māʾidah 5:94

Translation:

“You who believe, The God will surely test you with some game within reach of your hands and spears, so that The God may know who fears Him in the unseen. Whoever transgresses after that will have a painful punishment.”

The meaning of wa-rimāḥukum

Wa-rimāḥukum consists of the conjunction wa-, the plural noun rimāḥ, and the pronoun -kum.

Rimāḥ denotes spears the audience could use in hunting.

Grammatically, rimāḥukum is coordinated with aydīkum.

Aydīkum is the subject of tanāluhu; rimāḥukum is coordinated with it and shares its nominative status.

Tanāluhu belongs to N Y L and means reaching or attaining something.

The phrase directly means:

“Game that your hands and spears can reach.”

The hand and spear reach in different ways.

A hand can take accessible game directly; a spear extends the ability to reach and strike it.

This practical difference does not authorize adding hunting details that the verse does not state.

Hands and spears: two means of access

Early lexical and interpretive explanations distinguish aydīkum from rimāḥukum.

Hands can reach smaller or easily caught game, such as young birds or animals unable to escape.

Spears can reach larger or more distant animals.

This fits familiar hunting implements.

It is not an absolute rule that small game is caught only by hand and large game only with a spear.

A large animal might sometimes be caught by hand, or a small one with a spear.

The pairing expresses the range of available means rather than two fixed animal categories.

The clear point is that game becomes accessible for capture or injury.

Does “spears” mean every weapon here?

Interpretations sometimes present spears as an example of hunting implements.

Arrows and other implements may also be mentioned in explaining the setting.

That practical extension is understandable because the verse concerns access to game, and the spear is one familiar means.

Lexically, however, rimāḥ specifically means spears.

A direct translation should not replace it with “all weapons,” “hunting implements,” or “military power.”

Mention of other implements must be identified as contextual extension, not the noun’s meaning.

The spear appears here in hunting, not combat against people.

Naming a weapon does not make the verse a military instruction.

Why is easy access part of the test?

After addressing believers, the verse says la-yabluwannakumu Allāh: The God will surely test you.

It then identifies the test: game within reach of their hands and spears.

This relationship matters.

If game is inaccessible, not catching it does not necessarily show a deliberate decision to respect a prohibition.

When an action is possible but prohibited in a particular situation, refraining has a different significance.

The person faces a choice involving practical ability and responsibility.

This follows from the verse as a whole; rimāḥ alone names the implements.

Easy access does not make everyone equally interested in hunting; some may have little interest.

The test concerns a person facing an action that has become possible.

Li-yaʿlama Allāhu man yakhāfuhu bi-al-ghayb

The purpose of the test is expressed as:

لِيَعْلَمَ اللَّهُ مَنْ يَخَافُهُ بِالْغَيْبِ

li-yaʿlama allāhu man yakhāfuhu bi-al-ghaybi

Al-Māʾidah 5:94

The phrase connects testing with fearing The God bi-al-ghayb. One established reading is fearing Him without seeing Him; another emphasizes sincere fear unaffected by other people’s presence or absence.

Game being accessible when no other person is watching is one illustration of the latter reading, not the only meaning of bi-al-ghayb.

The person’s conduct nevertheless remains subject to the divine command.

The test should not be presented as implying prior divine ignorance; other Qur’anic passages describe comprehensive divine knowledge.

One contextual explanation is that testing brings conduct into actuality and makes it manifest: reverence is demonstrated in action rather than remaining a verbal claim.

This interpretation is separate from the lexical meaning of rumḥ.

The spear is one means making the tested action possible.

Fa-man iʿtadā: crossing the limit

The verse concludes:

فَمَنِ اعْتَدَىٰ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ فَلَهُ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

fa-man iʿtadā baʿda dhālika fa-lahu ʿadhābun alīmun

Al-Māʾidah 5:94

This concerns transgression after the warning and statement of the rule.

Iʿtadā belongs to ʿ D W and means exceeding a boundary or transgressing.

The act’s blameworthiness and consequence come from this clause and the regulatory context.

Rimāḥukum does not itself mean sin or transgression.

A spear usable for permitted hunting in one setting can become the means of a prohibited act during iḥrām.

The act’s status depends on the applicable circumstances and command.

That distinction is essential to understanding the verse.

5. Hunting and restrictions during iḥrām

The connection with Al-Māʾidah 5:1

Al-Māʾidah 5:1, in its discussion of food and covenants, says not to treat game as lawful while in the state described as ḥurum.

The restriction on hunting during iḥrām therefore appears earlier in the same chapter.

The test concerns a particular religious and practical situation.

Possession of hunting equipment should not itself be treated as prohibited.

Having a spear, being able to hunt, and actually hunting are three different states.

Al-Māʾidah 5:94 describes access, while related verses explain the restriction’s scope.

Al-Māʾidah 5:95: the prohibition of killing game during iḥrām

Al-Māʾidah 5:95 tells believers not to kill game while in iḥrām.

It also addresses compensation and consequences for deliberate killing.

This is important context for rimāḥukum.

A spear can kill game, but the prohibition concerns conduct in specified circumstances rather than the name or material of an implement.

Using an implement other than a spear does not by itself remove the act from the verse’s scope.

Conversely, the spear’s appearance here does not make it forbidden in every situation.

The distinction between implement and conduct must remain clear.

Al-Māʾidah 5:96: land and sea game

Al-Māʾidah 5:96 permits sea game and its food while prohibiting land game during the state described as ḥurum.

It further clarifies the rule’s scope.

The restriction during iḥrām should not become a permanent, universal ban on all hunting.

Nor does mention of a spear establish identical rules for every sea creature or method of taking animals.

The person’s condition and the type of game also matter, beyond the implement alone.

This illustrates a principle of reading Qur’anic regulations: an action may be permitted in one situation and prohibited in another without a change in the implement’s or animal’s nature.

Ability and responsibility

Access to game forms part of the test in Al-Māʾidah 5:94.

Being able to do something does not itself make it permissible.

Someone may be able to hunt, take property, or injure another person; the legitimacy of each action depends on its rules and circumstances.

Nearby game and available spears demonstrate practical ability.

The command concerning hunting during iḥrām limits its exercise.

Easy access to something therefore does not establish an unlimited right to use it.

This general principle should nevertheless not be inserted into the root’s lexical definition.

Rumḥ names a spear; the surrounding verses establish responsibility and restrictions on its use.

6. Related roots and terms

R M Ḥ (rumḥ) and Ṭ ʿ N (ṭaʿn)

These families can be related in fighting and hunting.

Rumḥ names the spear; ramaḥa can express striking with it.

Ṭaʿn concerns thrusting a point into a target or spearing; extended uses also include criticism and verbal attack.

Ṭaʿn therefore names an action rather than the spear itself.

Someone may perform ṭaʿn with a spear, but its uses are not limited to that implement.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:94, rimāḥukum names implements without reporting a thrust.

R M Ḥ (rumḥ) and R M Y (ramy)

Ramy concerns throwing or casting.

Qur’anic uses range from throwing objects to making accusations.

Rumḥ, however, names a spear.

A spear may be thrown, but rumḥ does not mean throwing.

Shooting an arrow from a bow differs from using a spear, though both can serve hunting or warfare.

The verse names a specific implement, so “spears” should not become “arrows” or “throws.”

R M Ḥ (rumḥ) and N B L (nabl)

Nabl names arrows, generally shot from a bow.

Arrows and spears can both serve hunting or fighting but differ in construction and typical use.

Early explanations of Al-Māʾidah 5:94 sometimes mention arrows alongside spears.

Both belong to the field of hunting equipment.

Rimāḥ in the verse does not thereby acquire the lexical meaning of nabl.

Their comparison concerns implements, not identical roots or words.

Rumḥ and qanā

Qanā concerns reeds or long canes and can also denote spears or spear shafts.

In transmitted pre-Islamic poetry it appears with horses, warriors, and battle scenes.

Qanā and rimāḥ can therefore refer to the same kind of implement in some settings.

Qanā also belongs to the field of long canes and reeds, whereas rumḥ directly names the familiar weapon. This does not establish a precise historical sequence of derivation.

The distinction is not absolute; Arabic weapon terminology overlaps.

Qur’anic rimāḥukum explicitly means the audience’s spears and requires no substitute term.

R M Ḥ (rumḥ) and Ṣ Y D (ṣayd)

Ṣayd concerns hunting or the game hunted.

Rumḥ names one hunting implement.

Both occur in Al-Māʾidah 5:94.

Al-ṣayd is what can be reached; rimāḥukum is one means of reaching it.

Hunting and possessing a spear are thus distinct.

Qur’anic ṣayd is not confined to one technique.

The rules must be read through passages on game type, iḥrām, and conduct, not through an implement’s name alone.

R M Ḥ (rumḥ) and S L Ḥ (silāḥ)

Silāḥ is a broader designation for fighting and defensive equipment.

A spear can be a weapon.

Not every weapon is a spear.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 uses asliḥatahum in a context of precaution and readiness for danger.

Al-Māʾidah 5:94 uses rimāḥukum in a hunting context.

An implement may serve different purposes in human life.

Mention of a spear does not always make a passage about warfare, just as hunting usage does not mean spears were used only for hunting.

7. Translation issues, Forqan proposals, and conclusion

The implement and the act of spearing

The principal translation distinction is between rumḥ and ramaḥa.

Rumḥ names the spear.

Ramaḥa can express spearing or, in another branch, an animal’s kick.

The Qur’anic rimāḥ in Al-Māʾidah 5:94 is the plural of spear.

It should not become “blows,” “your spearing,” or “kicking.”

The verse names the implements; tanāluhu expresses the act of reaching.

Does rimāḥ mean military power?

Spear imagery can symbolize power, support, or deterrence.

Here, its association with hands and game identifies a concrete implement.

“Your military power can reach it” is therefore unsuitable.

A community may possess military power, but this passage concerns access to game during a test.

Symbolic power or deterrence should not replace the implement’s name.

Does the word specify a hunting technique?

A spear can hunt animals, but its name does not describe a complete method.

Rimāḥukum does not specify a particular spear type, distance, or body part to strike.

Nor does the verse say every addressee was holding a spear at that moment.

The pronoun -kum relates the implements to the audience, while the sentence depicts potential access to game.

Technical hunting details should therefore not enter the lexical definition.

Translation proposal for Forqan

Word: وَرِمَاحُكُمْ — Al-Māʾidah 5:94

Direct equivalent: “and your spears.”

In the complete phrase:

تَنَالُهُ أَيْدِيكُمْ وَرِمَاحُكُمْ

tanāluhu aydīkum wa-rimāḥukum

Al-Māʾidah 5:94

A suitable rendering is:

“Which your hands and spears can reach.”

The supplied Persian rendering:

“Which your hands and spears can reach.”

It conveys the lexical meaning clearly and requires no substantive alteration on this point.

Explanation can distinguish direct capture by hand from reaching a target through an implement.

Such explanation belongs in the article or word box, rather than replacing the noun in translation.

Proposed word-box explanation for Forqan

Word: وَرِمَاحُكُمْ

Root: R M Ḥ

Brief meaning: A spear, a long pointed implement for striking a target. Related forms also express spearing and an animal’s kick.

Meaning here: And your spears: implements for reaching accessible game, in the test involving hunting during iḥrām.

The general root meaning and this Qur’anic form’s contextual meaning remain distinct. These proposals do not replace existing authored verse-box explanations.

Plant “spears,” animal horns, and the star name belong in the fuller study; they need not enter this brief contextual explanation.

Conclusion and final definition

The familiar center of the R M Ḥ family is rumḥ, the spear.

A spear is a long pointed striking implement; ramaḥa can describe striking with it.

The verb also describes an animal kicking with its hind legs, an association understandable through movement and impact.

Rāmiḥ can mean a spear bearer or user, rammāḥ a spear maker, and rimāḥah the craft of making spears.

Extended uses compare sharp awns, certain animal horns, and apparent stellar arrangements to spears.

ʿAntarah’s transmitted pre-Islamic verse uses rimāḥ for actual battlefield weapons. Other lexical evidence places the family in everyday life, hunting, animal keeping, and figurative naming.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an: wa-rimāḥukum in Al-Māʾidah 5:94.

It names the audience’s spears and, alongside their hands, describes access to game.

The verse concerns practical ability in circumstances subject to a divine restriction.

The spear itself means neither hunting nor sin. Its use is evaluated according to conduct and circumstances.

Final definition of R M Ḥ:

The central field concerns the spear as a long pointed implement for striking a target, and the act of spearing.

Related uses include an animal striking with its hind legs. Figurative uses compare the shape or function of things such as awns and horns to spears.

These connections should not become an excessively broad definition such as “any movement,” “any blow,” or “any defensive implement.”

The Qur’anic sense is specific: rimāḥ, the plural of rumḥ, means spears.

The appropriate rendering in Al-Māʾidah 5:94 is “and your spears.”

The spear provides access to game; the moral test arises through the relationship between ability and observance of the divine command.

Accurate understanding preserves three distinctions: the implement itself, the action performed with it, and the rule or consequence assigned by context.

These keep the lexical meaning clear, without turning warfare, hunting, defense, aggression, or prohibition into a fixed component of the noun itself.
