The Qur’anic Root B S Q (Busūq and Bāsiq)

1. The root and its Qur’anic form

In early Arabic, B S Q concerns growing tall, gaining height, and extending upward. Its familiar application is to the date palm: when its trunk grows and reaches a considerable height, the palm is described as بَاسِق (bāsiq).

The word is not confined to trees. Arabic sources also use forms of this family for a mountain’s height, a cloud’s elevation, and a person’s distinction or prominence in knowledge and excellence.

These applications nevertheless require distinction. A tree’s height is a physical characteristic; distinction in knowledge or social position extends the same semantic field. In reference to people, the root itself supplies no judgment about a person’s moral worth.

B S Q occurs only once in the Qur’an:

Qāf 50:10 — بَاسِقَاتٍ (bāsiqātin)

The word is the sound feminine plural of بَاسِقَة (bāsiqah), an active participle of the basic triliteral verb بَسَقَ (basaqa, form I). In the verse it describes date palms.

The verb بَسَقَ (basaqa), verbal noun بُسُوق (busūq), singular بَاسِق (bāsiq), and plural بَوَاسِق (bawāsiq) are recorded in lexicons, but these particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Unlike roots appearing in several Qur’anic settings, this root has only one direct Qur’anic witness. Its semantic range should therefore be studied by starting with the verse’s clear application and then assessing independent evidence from older Arabic.

2. The semantic core in older Arabic

Height and elevation

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«الْبَاءُ وَالسِّينُ وَالْقَافُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ ارْتِفَاعُ الشَّيْءِ وَعُلُوُّهُ»

al-bāʾu wa-al-sīnu wa-al-qāfu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa irtifāʿu al-shayʾi wa-ʿuluwwuhu

Translation: “Bāʾ, sīn, and qāf have one semantic basis: a thing’s elevation and height.”

He then quotes al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad:

«بَسَقَتِ النَّخْلَةُ بُسُوقًا: إِذَا طَالَتْ وَكَمُلَتْ»

basaqati al-nakhlatu busūqan idhā ṭālat wa-kamulat

Translation: “The date palm grew tall: it attained height and completed its growth.”

This definition combines two closely related aspects: rising and growing tall, and attaining a considerable height.

A date palm is short at the beginning of its growth. As its trunk grows, the crown stands farther above the ground and the tree rises high. The description bāsiq identifies that height.

However, bāsiq does not necessarily mean old or at the very end of a plant’s life. The lexical explanation طالت وكملت (ṭālat wa-kamulat) concerns developed growth and height; it does not imply that the tree will never grow again or has reached its final stage of life.

Nor does the root, by itself, express fruit bearing. A tree can be tall without having fruit at that particular time. The Qur’anic verse introduces the clusters or emerging palm growth in a separate phrase.

The height of trees, mountains, and clouds

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

«بَسَقَ الشَّيْءُ يَبْسُقُ بُسُوقًا: تَمَّ طُولُهُ»

basaqa al-shayʾu yabsuqu busūqan tamma ṭūluhu

That is: “A thing grew tall: its height became fully developed.”

The same source records bāsiq for something that has attained striking height. A tall mountain can also receive this description.

Ibn Fāris transmits from Abū Zayd, on the authority of al-Muntajiʿ ibn Nabhān, the expression غَمَامَةٌ بَاسِقَةٌ (ghamāmatun bāsiqatun): a white, high cloud. بَوَاسِقُ السَّحَابِ (bawāsiqu al-saḥāb) likewise refers to the high parts of clouds.

These applications show that busūq is not restricted to date palms. What the palm, mountain, and cloud share is height and visibility in an elevated position.

Physical height must nevertheless be distinguished from other applications. A palm grows from the ground; a mountain is an elevated landform; a cloud stands in the sky. Bāsiq describes height, rather than its cause or the process that produced it.

Distinction in knowledge and excellence

In classical Arabic, بَسَقَ فُلَانٌ عَلَىٰ أَصْحَابِهِ (basaqa fulānun ʿalā aṣḥābihi) can describe someone who surpasses his companions in knowledge or excellence.

Ibn Fāris explains بَسَقَ فِي عِلْمِهِ (basaqa fī ʿilmihi) as rising to distinction in knowledge.

This application does not concern bodily height. Instead, a social or intellectual quality is expressed through the image of elevation.

It does not follow that B S Q itself means knowledge, piety, or moral excellence. Those concepts arise from what the expression concerns and from its context.

In the Qur’an, bāsiqāt describes palms. The direct, physical sense of height therefore takes precedence over a metaphorical reading.

3. Poetry and other older Arabic usages

Distinction among tribes

In his explanation of Qāf 50:10, al-Ṭabarī quotes a verse by Abū Nawfal praising Ibn Hubayrah’s family:

يَا ابْنَ الَّذِينَ بِفَضْلِهِمْ

yā ibna alladhīna bi-faḍlihim

بَسَقَتْ عَلَى قَيْسٍ فَزَارَهْ

basaqat ʿalā Qaysin Fazārah

Translation: “O son of those through whose distinction Fazārah rose above Qays.”

Here بَسَقَتْ (basaqat) refers to a tribe. The tribe has clearly not grown physically taller like a tree or mountain. The poet speaks of its superior standing and social renown.

This use illustrates the extension of physical height into human relationships.

The witness’s date matters. The verse is transmitted in praise of a family connected with Ibn Hubayrah and belongs to poetry of the Islamic period; it cannot be treated as securely preceding the Qur’an’s revelation.

Here, too, basaqa does not by itself mean moral excellence. Distinction is expressed separately by بِفَضْلِهِمْ (bi-faḍlihim), while basaqat portrays rising above others in standing.

Tall palms and date produce

Exegetical and literary sources transmit the following two verses:

لَنَا خَمْرٌ وَلَيْسَتْ خَمْرَ كَرْمٍ

lanā khamrun wa-laysat khamra karmin

وَلَكِنْ مِنْ نِتَاجِ الْبَاسِقَاتِ

wa-lākin min nitāji al-bāsiqāti

كِرَامٌ فِي السَّمَاءِ ذَهَبْنَ طُولًا

kirāmun fī al-samāʾi dhahabna ṭūlan

وَفَاتَ ثِمَارُهَا أَيْدِي الْجُنَاةِ

wa-fāta thimāruhā aydiya al-junāti

Translation: “We have wine, though it is not grape wine; it comes from the produce of the lofty palms—noble palms that have stretched high into the sky, their fruit beyond the fruit gatherers’ reach.”

In this witness, الباسقات (al-bāsiqāt) clearly refers to tall date palms. The final hemistich also depicts their fruit’s height and its distance from the gatherers’ hands.

The witness appears in al-Qurṭubī’s commentary and Al-Durr al-Maṣūn, but the transmissions examined do not securely identify or date its poet. It cannot therefore be considered a certainly pre-Islamic witness.

The verse helps establish the meaning of bāsiqāt, because it explicitly describes the palms’ height.

Even within this image, however, the fruit’s being beyond reach results from the tree’s height; it is not the definition of bāsiq itself.

Accordingly, Qur’anic bāsiqāt should not be translated as “palms whose fruit nobody can reach.” The verse does not state that characteristic.

Laden palms: evidence for another reading

Exegetical sources also transmit another verse:

فَلَمَّا تَرَكْنَا الدَّارَ ظَلَّتْ مُنِيفَةً

fa-lammā taraknā al-dāra ẓallat munīfatan

بِقُرَّانَ فِيهِ الْبَاسِقَاتُ الْمَوَاقِرُ

bi-Qurrāna fīhi al-bāsiqātu al-mawāqiru

Translation: “When we left the dwelling, it remained lofty at Qurrān, where stood the tall, laden palms.” In this reading, Qurrān is a place name; this verse alone does not establish its precise location.

Al-Amākin, entry Qurrān

This witness places الباسقات (al-bāsiqāt) beside المواقر (al-mawāqir). The latter concerns palms heavy with produce.

Some commentators cite the verse to support reading bāsiqāt as fruit-laden palms. Yet the two descriptions together can also mean that the palms are both tall and laden.

The sources examined do not establish the poet’s identity or the verse’s precise date. An invented attribution must not turn it into supposedly dated historical evidence.

The example raises an important question: does bāsiqāt in Qāf 50:10 concern height alone, or can it convey fertility and abundant produce? The answer requires separate examination of the verse and the expression أَبْسَقَتِ النَّاقَةُ (absaqati al-nāqah).

Absaqati al-nāqah: milk appearing before birth

Lisān al-ʿArab records أَبْسَقَتِ النَّاقَةُ (absaqati al-nāqah) for a she-camel in whose udder the first milk, or colostrum, appears before she gives birth.

The form مُبْسِق (mubsiq) and plural مَبَاسِيق (mabāsīq) are also recorded for such she-camels. Similar usages are transmitted for ewes and certain other animals.

This meaning must be distinguished from a palm’s height.

In one application, basaqa concerns elevation and growing tall; in another, أبسقت (absaqat, form IV) concerns milk appearing before birth.

An imagined connection between a filling udder and its increased volume is possible, but the available evidence does not establish a shared historical origin for all these applications.

Conversely, the mere existence of this usage for she-camels does not prove that bāsiqāt must mean fruit-laden when applied to palms: both the morphological form and the sentence’s subject differ.

The Qur’anic description of the palms is accompanied by the separate clause لَهَا طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ (lahā ṭalʿun naḍīd). That clause itself concerns the tree’s produce or reproductive growth and matters when determining the meaning of bāsiqāt.

Busāq and buṣāq: does saliva share this semantic core?

Arabic also records بُساق (busāq) for saliva and بَسَقَ (basaqa) for spitting it out.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains that basaqa in this usage is a variant of بَصَقَ (baṣaqa). A form with zāy, بَزَقَ (bazaqa), is likewise recorded in this group of phonetic variants.

Ibn Fāris explicitly distinguishes this usage from the semantic basis of height. He excludes saliva from that core of B S Q and treats sīn here as a substitute for the ṣād of B Ṣ Q.

This explanation matters: sharing three letters in a written form does not establish a common semantic origin for every application.

Thus, bāsiqāt should not be explained through an image of saliva shooting out, nor should a palm’s height be claimed to derive from that action.

In the Qur’anic application, height and elevation have direct, clear support. The usage concerning saliva is a separate question in the history of Arabic phonetic variation.

4. Qāf 50:10: tall palms and closely layered palm growth

Qāf 50:10

وَالنَّخْلَ بَاسِقَاتٍ لَهَا طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ

wa-al-nakhla bāsiqātin lahā ṭalʿun naḍīdun

Translation: “And the lofty date palms, with closely layered emerging flower clusters.”

The construction wa-al-nakhla bāsiqātin

النَّخْلَ (al-nakhla) is a collective generic noun referring to date palms.

It is accusative here, coordinated with جَنَّاتٍ (jannātin) and حَبَّ الْحَصِيدِ (ḥabba al-ḥaṣīd) in the previous verse. The governing verb is فَأَنْبَتْنَا (fa-anbatnā) in Qāf 50:9: “so We caused to grow.”

بَاسِقَاتٍ (bāsiqātin) is a sound feminine plural and syntactically an accusative circumstantial expression (ḥāl) describing al-nakhl. Its accusative marker is kasrah, because the sound feminine plural takes kasrah in place of fatḥah in the accusative.

The final kasrah must therefore not lead us to classify the word as genitive in this verse. Established grammatical analyses identify it as an accusative ḥāl. Al-Qurṭubī and Al-Durr al-Maṣūn also record this analysis; syntax, rather than the final vowel’s appearance alone, must determine the case.

Al-Qurṭubī: the syntax of bāsiqāt

In meaning, the verse describes the palms as tall and rising high.

This description suits the date palm’s natural form: it typically has a tall, upright trunk with leaves gathered at its upper part.

However, bāsiqāt does not by itself mean that the trunk is straight. Height and uprightness can occur together, but the root’s principal meaning is elevation.

Qatādah and ʿAbd Allāh ibn Shaddād explain the word through the palms’ height and upright stature. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr explains it with مستويات (mustawiyāt).

Al-Ṭabarī transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, and others the explanation طوال (ṭiwāl), “tall.”

Does bāsiqāt mean fruit-laden palms?

Exegetical sources also record another reading.

Al-Māwardī and al-Qurṭubī transmit from commentators including al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and ʿIkrimah the explanation مَوَاقِيرَ حَوَامِلَ (mawāqīra ḥawāmila): palms heavy with produce.

This reading has a basis. As noted above, أبسقت (absaqat) is recorded for milk appearing in a she-camel’s udder, and some lexicographers discuss related forms concerning fertility and produce.

Furthermore, the same verse follows bāsiqāt with لَهَا طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ (lahā ṭalʿun naḍīd), concerning the palms’ emerging growth and its closely layered arrangement.

Nevertheless, a possible reading is not necessarily the preferred one.

Height appears in the root’s principal definition in Ibn Fāris and al-Khalīl and is familiar in non-Qur’anic applications to palms, clouds, and mountains.

Against this broader evidence, fruit bearing has more limited support as the meaning of bāsiqāt.

The verse also introduces the palms’ produce in a separate clause. Reading bāsiqāt as tall palms yields two distinct characteristics: the trees’ height and their closely layered emerging clusters.

Translating it simply as “fruit-laden,” however, removes the familiar sense of height and merges part of the second clause’s meaning into the first description.

After reporting the disagreement, al-Qurṭubī likewise states that the sense of height is more widespread and better known in Arabic.

The palms’ lofty stature is therefore the preferred reading. The fruit-laden interpretation should remain in the record of the study, but has insufficient grounds to replace height in the basic translation.

What does lahā ṭalʿun naḍīd add to the picture?

طَلْع (ṭalʿ) belongs to Ṭ L ʿ. In reference to palms, it concerns the newly emerging reproductive growth from which flowers, followed by fruit, develop.

نَضِيد (naḍīd) belongs to N Ḍ D and concerns things arranged and stacked on one another.

The clause therefore describes another characteristic of the palms: emerging clusters whose components are closely packed in layers.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains naḍīd with منضود بعضه على بعض (manḍūdun baʿḍuhu ʿalā baʿḍ): components laid one upon another.

Lexically, the three expressions need to be distinguished:

النخل (al-nakhl) names the date palms; باسقات (bāsiqāt) describes their height; لها طلع نضيد (lahā ṭalʿun naḍīd) concerns their emerging clusters and their arrangement.

Thus, bāsiqāt should not be defined as “having stacked clusters.” That characteristic comes from the following phrase.

Nor does ṭalʿ necessarily mean fully ripe dates. Arabic also uses the word for the early stages of the palm’s reproductive growth. Rendering it directly as “ripe dates ready for harvesting” is therefore inaccurate.

Height and fruit bearing

The verse places two striking features side by side: the palms’ tall trunks and stature, and their orderly, closely layered emerging clusters.

The tree grows upward from the ground while also producing parts involved in its reproductive process.

This combination suffices to describe the palm’s beauty and usefulness.

It does not follow that a taller palm necessarily produces more fruit. Yield also depends on other factors, including variety, age, water, soil, pollination, and environmental conditions.

Nor does bāsiqāt itself mean a large number of palms. One palm may be tall, as may a group of them.

Plurality comes from the word’s morphology; height comes from the root’s meaning.

The form bāṣiqāt with ṣād

Some sources on recitation and exegesis also report باصقات (bāṣiqāt) with ṣād.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn connects this form with a report from Quṭbah ibn Mālik and a dialectal usage in which sīn before certain consonants is replaced by a sound close to ṣād. Al-Qurṭubī, in contrast, questions the accuracy of attributing this form to the transmitted recitation.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn: the reported ṣād pronunciation

The disagreement should be treated as a report about pronunciation and the transmission of a recitation.

The Qur’anic text examined here reads بَاسِقَاتٍ (bāsiqātin) with sīn. A possible dialectal pronunciation with ṣād does not by itself give the tall palms a different meaning.

The root study therefore rests on B S Q and its familiar sense of height.

5. Tall palms in the context of Sūrat Qāf

Verse 10 forms part of Sūrat Qāf’s account of creation and plant growth.

Qāf 50:9

وَنَزَّلْنَا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً مُبَارَكًا فَأَنْبَتْنَا بِهِ جَنَّاتٍ وَحَبَّ الْحَصِيدِ

wa-nazzalnā mina al-samāʾi māʾan mubārakan fa-anbatnā bihi jannātin wa-ḥabba al-ḥaṣīdi

Translation: “And We sent down blessed water from the sky, then caused gardens and grain for harvesting to grow through it.”

Qāf 50:11

رِزْقًا لِلْعِبَادِ وَأَحْيَيْنَا بِهِ بَلْدَةً مَيْتًا ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ الْخُرُوجُ

rizqan li-al-ʿibādi wa-aḥyaynā bihi baldatan maytan kadhālika al-khurūju

Translation: “As provision for the servants; and through it We brought a dead land to life. Such, too, is the coming forth.”

The first verse concerns water and plant growth. The second introduces tall palms and their emerging clusters. The third connects this growth with provision for the servants and the revival of dead land, then speaks of “coming forth.”

The palms’ height is therefore not an isolated, purely aesthetic description. It forms part of a picture of creation, growth, and provision emerging from the earth.

Nevertheless, bāsiqāt itself does not mean provision, the land’s revival, or resurrection after death.

Bāsiqāt describes the palms’ lofty stature; رزقا للعباد (rizqan li-al-ʿibād) expresses the produce’s usefulness; كذلك الخروج (kadhālika al-khurūj) connects the revival of the land with resurrection.

This distinction matters. The conclusion of the sūrah’s argument must not replace a word’s lexical meaning.

Does the tall palm symbolize a believer here?

Elsewhere, the Qur’an uses trees in examples and parables. In Ibrāhīm 14:24, for instance, a good word is likened to a good tree.

In Qāf 50:10, however, no independent indication turns النخل باسقات (al-nakhla bāsiqātin) into believers, prophets, rulers, or another group of people.

The verse directly concerns date palms, their growth, and their produce.

The trees’ height and fertility can inspire ethical or literary reflections, but those reflections should not be presented as the word’s meaning or the verse’s certain intent.

The natural, concrete meaning is clear and sufficient in this context.

6. Distinctions from related roots

B S Q (busūq) and Ṭ W L (ṭūl)

Ṭūl concerns length and extension. In reference to palms, it is among the closest expressions to busūq.

Lexicographers and commentators repeatedly explain bāsiqāt through طوال (ṭiwāl), “tall.”

However, Ṭ W L also has a broader range of applications concerning duration, physical dimensions, or ability and resources.

In its principal use, busūq emphasizes a thing’s height and elevation.

For Qāf 50:10, “tall” or “lofty” therefore works better than “long” alone: it more naturally conveys a tree’s upward extension.

Study of Ṭ W L

B S Q (busūq) and ʿ L W (ʿuluww)

ʿUluww concerns height and superiority, whether in physical position or in social and evaluative applications.

Older Arabic likewise uses busūq for physical height and distinction in knowledge or standing.

This proximity does not mean that every application of ʿuluww can be replaced with busūq.

For example, Qur’anic ʿuluww can appear in contexts of domination and rebellion. Bāsiqāt in Qāf 50:10 itself conveys no moral meaning of arrogance or seeking superiority.

Study of ʿ L W

B S Q (busūq) and SH H Q (shuhūq)

Arabic uses شاهق (shāhiq) for great height, particularly a high mountain or building.

For a palm, bāsiq and shāhiq can be very close. Some commentators describe the bāsiq palms as طوالا شاهقات (ṭiwālan shāhiqāt), “tall and lofty.”

Nevertheless, shāhiq can suggest particularly striking height in both Arabic and Persian.

Without supporting evidence, an extraordinary or unusual height should therefore not be assumed for every bāsiq palm.

“Tall” or “lofty” preserves the principal meaning.

Study of SH H Q

B S Q (busūq) and B S Ṭ (basṭ)

These roots share their first two consonants, but have different meanings.

B S Ṭ concerns opening and spreading, whether in breadth or in the scope of what someone enjoys.

In its Qur’anic use, B S Q concerns the palms’ height and elevation.

A tree can have spreading branches and a tall trunk, but the two features are lexically distinct.

Accordingly, bāsiqāt should not be translated as “broad, spreading palms” without evidence for that description.

Study of B S Ṭ

B S Q (busūq) and Ṭ L ʿ (ṭalʿ)

In Qāf 50:10, ṭalʿ stands alongside bāsiqāt.

Yet bāsiq concerns a palm’s height, while ṭalʿ concerns its newly emerging growth associated with flowers and fruit.

Although the words describe the same tree, they express different features.

Their connection with plant growth does not justify defining busūq as fruit bearing or ṭalʿ as the trunk’s growing tall.

Study of Ṭ L ʿ

B S Q (busūq) and N Ḍ D (naḍd)

Naḍd concerns stacking and arranging things together.

In Qāf 50:10, naḍīd describes ṭalʿ, rather than directly describing the palms’ height.

Thus, the palm is bāsiq and its emerging cluster is naḍīd.

This grammatical and semantic distinction keeps the verse’s image from being conflated without justification.

Study of N Ḍ D

B S Q (busūq) and B Ṣ Q (buṣāq)

In some Arabic dialects, basaqa with sīn concerns expelling saliva. Ibn Fāris, however, treats it as a phonetic variant of baṣaqa with ṣād.

This usage must therefore not be treated as an instance of a palm’s elevation or used to define bāsiqāt.

A shared written form does not always imply a shared historical semantic core.

7. Translation proposal and conclusion for Forqan

For the root’s principal application, “growing tall,” “attaining height,” and “rising high” are suitable renderings.

For bāsiq, however, its active participial form matters: it describes something that has attained height or is tall.

In Qāf 50:10, “tall date palms,” “lofty date palms,” and “palms rising high” are defensible translations.

Among them, “lofty date palms” is both natural and faithful to the root’s clear meaning.

The principal proposed translation of the whole verse is:

“And the lofty date palms, with closely layered emerging flower clusters.”

In this rendering, “date palms” names the trees, “lofty” conveys bāsiqāt, and “closely layered emerging flower clusters” renders the separate phrase ṭalʿun naḍīd.

A research note can clarify ṭalʿ as the newly emerging growth associated with the palm’s flowers and fruit. Such explanation should not unnecessarily displace a direct rendering of the verse’s wording.

Nor should bāsiqāt be confined to “fruit-laden palms.” Some early commentaries record that reading, but the lexical evidence and verse’s context favor height.

Conclusion

In Arabic, B S Q concerns growing tall and gaining height. A tall palm, a high mountain, and an elevated cloud exemplify its physical applications. Extended applications of the family concern a person’s distinction in knowledge or social position.

The Qur’an uses this root only once, in Qāf 50:10, in the form بَاسِقَاتٍ (bāsiqātin), concerning palms.

The verse expresses the palms’ height separately from the presence and arrangement of their emerging clusters. The principal meaning of bāsiqāt is therefore lofty stature, although some commentators also transmit the fruit-laden reading.

The saliva usage of basaqa is also recorded in Arabic, but Ibn Fāris connects it with phonetic substitution involving baṣaqa and distinguishes it from the semantic core of height.

Root research should therefore not use similar consonants alone to force unrelated meanings into an artificial common image.

A suitable final definition for Forqan:

B S Q concerns growing tall, gaining elevation, and attaining considerable height. Physical applications include tall palms, mountains, and clouds; extended applications concern a person’s elevated standing or renown. The Qur’an uses it only in Qāf 50:10, as بَاسِقَاتٍ (bāsiqātin), “lofty date palms.” Closely layered emerging clusters are a separate description in that verse and must not be merged into the meaning of bāsiqāt itself. Fruit bearing remains an early interpretation worth examining, but the lexical evidence favors height and elevation.
