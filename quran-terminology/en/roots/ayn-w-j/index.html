Core meaning: bending, crookedness, and departure from an alignment or path; in nonphysical and behavioral contexts, deviation from the way, truth, or a sound standard.

The Arabic root ʿ W J denotes crookedness, curvature, and deviation. This may be visible in a bent piece of wood, a leaning wall, or uneven ground.

The same family applies to paths, thought, conduct, speech, and religion. These uses concern deviation from a particular course or standard rather than bodily curvature.

ʿAwija means to become crooked; aʿwaj describes something crooked; and ʿawwaja means to bend or divert something.

Arabic also records a distinction between ʿawaj, with fatḥah, and ʿiwaj, with kasrah: the former is often associated with physical crookedness, the latter with nonphysical deviation in religion, conduct, or speech.

This distinction is not absolute. The Quran itself uses ʿiwaj for uneven ground, and early Arabic poetry uses the same form for a crooked body part.

The root occurs nine times in nine Quranic verses, always as the noun ʿiwaj. Its three broad fields are deviation concerning The God's way, the absence of crookedness in the Quran, and its absence from the ground or the course of following the caller at the Resurrection.

1. The root in early Arabic

Physical crookedness and curvature

Early Arabic uses ʿawaj for physical crookedness and curvature.

For example, ʿūdun aʿwaj describes a piece of wood that bends along its length rather than being straight.

Ḥāʾiṭun fīhi ʿawaj similarly describes a wall departing from its upright alignment.

Crookedness here does not mean that the object has completely broken.

A branch may be intact but grow bent; a wall may remain standing despite part of it leaning out of alignment.

ʿAwaj therefore identifies crookedness or deviation itself, without necessarily implying breakage, destruction, or collapse.

Imagine a long stick. If perfectly straight, it extends in one direction; if it bends in the middle, that line changes.

This altered alignment is the basic physical image of ʿawaj.

A witness from Labid ibn Rabiah

A poem by Labid ibn Rabiah says:

فِي نَابِهِ عِوَجٌ يُجَاوِزُ شِدْقَهُ
وَيُخَالِفُ الْأَعْلَى وَرَاءَ الْأَسْفَلِ

fī nābihi ʿiwajun yujāwizu shidqahu / wa-yukhālifu al-aʿlā warāʾa al-asfal

“His tusk bends beyond the corner of his mouth, and the upper one crosses behind the lower.”

The poet describes an animal with a curved tusk departing from the usual alignment.

Nāb denotes a canine tooth or tusk; shidq denotes the corner of the mouth.

ʿIwaj here thus describes actual crookedness in a body part.

This matters because the transmitted reading here has kasrah, ʿiwaj, although some lexical explanations reserve that vowel for nonphysical matters. Other lexical transmissions give fatḥah or different wording, so this particular vocalization should not be treated as unanimous.

The poetic reading illustrates why that division should not be treated as exceptionless.

Labid lived through both the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. The verse appears in his collected poetry, but its precise composition date is not independently established; it cannot securely be assigned to the pre-Islamic period.

This early Arabic witness nevertheless illustrates a reading in which ʿiwaj describes perceptible crookedness.

ʿAwaj and ʿiwaj: two vocalizations

The Arabic lexical tradition records a distinction between ʿawaj and ʿiwaj.

A familiar explanation assigns ʿawaj to upright objects such as walls, sticks, and spears.

ʿIwaj, by contrast, applies to nonphysical matters such as religion, opinion, and conduct, and also to land and roads.

This is more precise than a simple physical/nonphysical division: even accounts making the distinction include land under ʿiwaj.

Another account accepts ʿiwaj in both physical and nonphysical contexts.

Two witnesses illustrate the limitations of a rigid division.

First, the quoted reading of Labid's verse applies ʿiwaj to a curved tusk.

Second, Ta Ha 20:107 applies it to the ground.

The two vocalizations therefore exhibit usage tendencies, but the vowel alone cannot always determine whether a physical object or an abstract matter is meant.

ʿĀja: bending and changing direction

Within this family, ʿāja concerns bending or turning something.

Dictionaries record ʿāja raʾsa al-baʿīr for turning a camel's head with its rein.

Imagine a camel traveling toward a destination. Pulling its rein to one side turns its head and may change its direction.

Here the issue is an act of turning, rather than merely a crooked shape.

ʿĀja ilā al-makān concerns turning or inclining toward a place.

ʿĀja bi-l-makān is also recorded for stopping or staying somewhere for a while.

This can be related to turning aside: a traveler may leave a direct course, turn toward a place, and stop there.

Stopping at a place and an object's crookedness are nevertheless distinct uses. Their possible connection does not mean every stop denotes wrongful deviation.

From physical crookedness to deviation in conduct and thought

Arabic also uses ʿiwaj concerning religion, opinion, conduct, and affairs of life.

Fī raʾyihi ʿiwaj, for example, can describe crookedness or deviation in someone's opinion.

An opinion is not a bent object. The point is its failure to accord with a standard or direction considered sound.

ʿIwaj al-dīn similarly concerns deviation in religion.

The image of a path makes this use understandable.

Someone seeking a destination may take the right route or stray from it.

In ethical and religious language, truth and guidance are compared to a way people should recognize and follow.

Physical crookedness must, however, be distinguished from moral judgment.

A curved branch may be entirely natural and free of any defect.

A winding road may also lead correctly to its destination.

ʿIwaj therefore does not invariably mean sin or corruption. Negative judgment arises when the subject is deviation from truth, justice, or the right way.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms in the ʿ W J family include:

ʿAwija: became crooked, bent, or departed from alignment.

ʿAwaj: crookedness and curvature, especially concerning physical or upright objects.

ʿIwaj: crookedness and deviation, widely applied to paths, land, religion, behavior, and thought.

Aʿwaj: crooked, curved, or out of alignment.

ʿAwjāʾ: the feminine form of aʿwaj.

ʿŪj: plural of aʿwaj, describing crooked or bent things or persons according to context.

Iʿwajja: became crooked or departed from straightness.

Iʿwijāj: bending, curvature, and deviation.

Muʿwajj: something that has become crooked or bent.

ʿAwwaja: bent something or diverted it from its alignment.

Taʿwīj: bending, curving, or diverting.

Taʿawwaja: became crooked or took on a curved shape.

Inʿāja: bent, turned, or inclined to one side.

ʿĀja: turned or inclined something; also turned toward a place or stopped there.

Aʿwaj was also the name of a famous horse, with related horses called Aʿwajī. This proper-name use should not be transferred to every derivative.

Only ʿiwaj among these forms appears in the Quran.

It usually occurs in the accusative as ʿiwajan; Ta Ha 20:108 has ʿiwaja after categorical lā, and Al-Zumar 39:28 has the genitive ʿiwajin.

Verbs and adjectives such as aʿwaj, ʿawwaja, and muʿwajj do not occur in the Quran.

3. ʿIwaj: seeking crookedness in The God's way

A substantial group of Quranic uses concerns The God's way and efforts to divert it or represent it as crooked.

These occur in Al Imran 3:99, Al-Aʿrāf 7:45, Al-Aʿrāf 7:86, Hūd 11:19, and Ibrāhīm 14:3.

None is restricted to the physical curvature of an earthen road; they concern the way of guidance and truth.

Al Imran 3:99: obstructing others and seeking a crooked way

قُلْ يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ لِمَ تَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ مَنْ آمَنَ تَبْغُونَهَا عِوَجًا وَأَنْتُمْ شُهَدَاءُ ۗ وَمَا اللَّهُ بِغَافِلٍ عَمَّا تَعْمَلُونَ

qul yā ahla al-kitābi lima taṣuddūna ʿan sabīli allāhi man āmana tabghūnahā ʿiwajan wa-antum shuhadāʾu wa-mā allāhu bi-ghāfilin ʿammā taʿmalūn

Āl ʿImrān 3:99

“Say: People of the Book, why do you bar those who believe from the way of The God, seeking to make it crooked, while you are witnesses? The God is not unaware of what you do.”

Al Imran 3:99

ʿIwajan appears here beside two important verbs:

Taṣuddūna and tabghūnahā.

Taṣuddūna belongs to Ṣ D D and concerns barring or obstructing.

Tabghūnahā belongs to B GH Y and here expresses seeking or wanting something.

ʿIwajan names what they seek concerning The God's way: crookedness and deviation.

The verse thus describes two related behaviors.

First, barring others from The God's way.

Second, seeking crookedness in that way.

These are not identical acts.

Someone may prevent travelers from entering a route; another may misdescribe it so that travelers themselves take the wrong road.

Either may keep people from their destination, but the methods differ.

In religion, someone may openly forbid following truth, or misrepresent it so that the path of guidance appears crooked and misleading.

Does The God's way actually become crooked?

This question matters to understanding the expression.

The verse does not say that The God's way is inherently crooked or wrong.

It describes people who want it crooked.

Someone may seek to alter truth, conceal part of it, or misrepresent it.

Wanting such a change does not mean truth itself changes accordingly.

If a guide deliberately gives wrong directions, the actual route need not have changed; what has changed is the information supplied to the traveler.

Tabghūnahā ʿiwajan can therefore concern attempts to lead people away from the way or make it appear crooked to others.

Both possibilities fit the context of obstruction, while ʿiwaj itself conveys crookedness or deviation.

The significance of wa-antum shuhadāʾ

The verse describes its addressees as shuhadāʾ, witnesses.

This description emphasizes their responsibility.

The criticism is not merely of a difference of opinion: people obstruct and seek a crooked way while standing as witnesses.

Earlier verses, including Al Imran 3:71, also discuss mixing truth with falsehood and knowingly concealing truth.

The context thus raises responsibility toward truth.

The address to People of the Book should not be extended into a claim that every follower of another scripture or religion always knowingly misrepresents truth.

The verse condemns a particular behavior: barring The God's way and seeking it crooked.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:45 and Hūd 11:19: a recurring pattern

Al-Aʿrāf 7:45 likewise describes people barring The God's way and seeking it crooked.

Denial of the Hereafter is added in the same verse.

Hūd 11:19 repeats this pattern.

The preceding verse, Hūd 11:18, describes those who invent lies about The God.

Verse nineteen then describes obstruction, seeking a crooked way, and denial of the Hereafter.

This sequence connects misrepresenting the way with falsely attributing statements to The God.

For example, someone who invents a command, attributes it to The God, and deprives people of a right on that basis may both distort truth and obstruct others' proper following.

Such an instance requires evidence of the person's actions; disagreement over a verse does not by itself prove deliberate fabrication about The God.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:86: obstruction along the roads

وَلَا تَقْعُدُوا بِكُلِّ صِرَاطٍ تُوعِدُونَ وَتَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ مَنْ آمَنَ بِهِ وَتَبْغُونَهَا عِوَجًا ۚ وَاذْكُرُوا إِذْ كُنْتُمْ قَلِيلًا فَكَثَّرَكُمْ ۖ وَانْظُرُوا كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الْمُفْسِدِينَ

wa-lā taqʿudū bi-kulli ṣirāṭin tūʿidūna wa-taṣuddūna ʿan sabīli allāhi man āmana bihi wa-tabghūnahā ʿiwajan wa-udhkurū idh kuntum qalīlan fa-kaththarakum wa-unẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mufsidīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:86

“Do not sit on every path, threatening and barring from the way of The God those who believe in Him, seeking to make it crooked. Remember when you were few and He increased you; and see what became of the corrupters.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:86

The image of roads and obstruction is more explicit here.

Bi-kulli ṣirāṭ refers to paths.

Tūʿidūna expresses threatening.

Taṣuddūna describes barring others.

Tabghūnahā ʿiwajan expresses seeking The God's way crooked.

The verse belongs to Shuayb's address to his people.

Nearby verses discuss fair measures and scales, respecting people's rights, and avoiding corruption in the land.

The issue is therefore more than a theoretical disagreement about a way: threats, obstruction, and social corruption are involved.

A group might, for example, control routes and use threats to prevent access to trade or free activity.

Those in power may also use their influence to prevent people from following truth.

The road imagery can evoke such practical barriers, but sabīl Allāh explicitly concerns guidance and The God's way.

The entire sentence should not be reduced to the physical crookedness of a road.

Ibrāhīm 14:3: preferring this world and seeking a crooked way

Ibrāhīm 14:3 also speaks of seeking The God's way crooked.

It describes people who prefer worldly life to the Hereafter, bar others from The God's way, and seek it crooked.

Their behavior is thus connected to their own choices and preferences.

Someone may reject a truth to protect wealth, power, or social position.

If that person also obstructs others or distorts the way, the behavior has social consequences beyond the person's own choice.

ʿIwaj here does not mean love of wealth or worldliness.

Deviation concerning the way comes from the word; preferring this world to the Hereafter comes from a separate expression.

Seeking a crooked way versus personally straying

These five verses do not focus only on someone who has personally left the right way.

They also concern barring others and seeking the way crooked.

This distinction matters.

A person may take the wrong route through ignorance or error.

Another may know that their advice affects others' lives and still deliberately lead them astray.

The latter goes beyond individual error to responsibility toward others.

Deliberately distorting truth nevertheless requires evidence; a different interpretation alone does not justify an accusation of concealment or intentional distortion.

ʿIwaj lexically means crookedness or deviation. Judgments about knowledge, intention, and responsibility depend on context and conduct.

4. No crookedness in the Book and Quran

Two verses use ʿiwaj to deny crookedness in the divine Book rather than describe opponents' behavior.

Al-Kahf 18:1: a Book in which no crookedness was placed

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ عَلَىٰ عَبْدِهِ الْكِتَابَ وَلَمْ يَجْعَلْ لَهُ عِوَجًا

al-ḥamdu li-llāhi alladhī anzala ʿalā ʿabdihi al-kitāba wa-lam yajʿal lahu ʿiwajā

Al-Kahf 18:1

“Praise belongs to The God, who sent down the Book upon His servant and placed no crookedness in it.”

Al-Kahf 18:1

ʿIwajan here occurs in a negative construction.

Lam yajʿal lahu ʿiwajan means that He placed no crookedness in the Book.

The subject is the revealed Book, rather than a physical object or earthly road.

Crookedness thus concerns deviation in its content and direction of guidance.

The verse describes the Book itself as free of crookedness.

This should not be extended without grounds to every human interpretation of it.

A text may be sound while a reader misunderstands part of it or attributes something absent from the text to it.

The reader's error alone does not establish that the original text is crooked or wrong.

Conversely, describing a text as free of crookedness does not automatically validate every human reading.

The Book must therefore be distinguished from people's understanding of it.

The relationship with qayyiman

Al-Kahf 18:2 describes the Book as qayyiman.

The word belongs to Q W M and concerns uprightness and sound establishment.

Two related descriptions therefore appear at the surah's opening:

First, the denial of crookedness in the Book.

Second, its description as upright.

Together they present it as both free of deviation and firmly directed toward what is right.

The two roots must nevertheless remain distinct.

ʿIwaj expresses crookedness and departure from alignment.

Qayyim belongs to a family concerning standing, firmness, and sustaining order.

Denying an undesirable quality and affirming a positive one are related but independent statements.

Does freedom from crookedness remove the need for reflection?

No.

The absence of ʿiwaj does not mean every verse is equally clear to every reader at first sight.

The Quran itself discusses clear and ambiguous verses and calls for reflection, reasoning, and examination of its verses.

Crookedness in the Book must therefore be distinguished from difficulty or possible error in understanding it.

A question may require studying several verses, knowing the language precisely, or examining context. That need for research does not itself demonstrate crookedness in the text.

Nor can all questions of comprehensiveness, clarification, or clear and ambiguous verses be derived from ʿiwaj's lexical meaning alone; they require their own evidence and principles.

Al-Zumar 39:28: an Arabic Quran without crookedness

قُرْآنًا عَرَبِيًّا غَيْرَ ذِي عِوَجٍ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَّقُونَ

qurʾānan ʿarabiyyan ghayra dhī ʿiwajin laʿallahum yattaqūn

Al-Zumar 39:28

“An Arabic Quran, without any crookedness, so that they may be mindful.”

Al-Zumar 39:28

ʿIwajin appears in ghayra dhī ʿiwajin.

Dhī expresses possessing something, and ghayr negates it.

The expression thus means that the Quran possesses no crookedness.

Al-Zumar 39:27 speaks of presenting varied examples for people so that they may remember.

The present verse then describes the Quran as Arabic and without crookedness.

Varied examples and modes of expression are thus compatible with its firm direction of guidance.

A truth may be explained through several examples differing in form and subject, all helping illuminate that truth.

Differences among examples do not by themselves constitute deviation or inconsistency.

Does ghayra dhī ʿiwajin mean no apparent difference of any kind?

Its direct meaning is the absence of crookedness or deviation.

Other Quranic verses address coherence and the absence of discrepancy, including Al-Nisāʾ 4:82.

ʿIwaj and ikhtilāf, however, belong to different lexical families.

Two statements may concern different situations and appear different without that alone indicating crookedness.

A command for ordinary circumstances may differ from one concerning necessity.

Each expression's subject, audience, and conditions must then be examined.

“Without any crookedness” therefore preserves the phrase, while the coherence of verses belongs to a separate study.

5. ʿIwaj in the ground and in following the caller

The two occurrences in Ta Ha differ significantly in meaning and syntax.

One concerns the ground after the mountains' transformation.

The other concerns following the caller on the Day of Resurrection.

Ta Ha 20:107: ground without crookedness or unevenness

لَا تَرَىٰ فِيهَا عِوَجًا وَلَا أَمْتًا

lā tarā fīhā ʿiwajan wa-lā amtā

Ṭā Hā 20:107

“You will see in it neither crookedness nor elevation.”

Ta Ha 20:107

This verse continues the discussion of mountains.

Ta Ha 20:105 describes scattering the mountains.

Ta Ha 20:106 describes the resulting condition as qāʿan ṣafṣafan: an open, level plain.

The present verse then states that neither ʿiwaj nor amt is visible there.

ʿIwaj here concerns crookedness and unevenness of the ground.

Unlike the uses concerning The God's way or the Book, this subject is material and visible.

Is ʿiwaj restricted to nonphysical matters?

This verse shows that it is not.

Some lexical explanations associate the kasrah form chiefly with religion, opinion, and nonphysical matters.

Ta Ha 20:107 nevertheless applies it to the ground.

A kasrah therefore does not necessarily indicate intellectual or moral deviation.

This agrees with the quoted reading of Labid's verse, where ʿiwaj describes a curved tusk.

The familiar distinction between ʿawaj and ʿiwaj is thus a usage tendency, not an absolute boundary without exceptions.

ʿIwaj and amt

The verse places ʿiwajan and amtan side by side.

Amt belongs to the independent root ʾ M T and is recorded for elevation, irregularity, or differences of level.

Some explanations associate ʿiwaj with depressions and amt with elevations.

This division is not uniformly and conclusively established across all lexical accounts.

The sequence clearly denies unevenness in the ground described after the mountains' transformation.

ʿIwaj emphasizes crookedness or departure from levelness, while amt conveys another aspect of unevenness or differing levels.

The words are close in meaning but have distinct roots.

The expression alone cannot determine the dimensions, geometry, or scientific mechanism of all the earth's transformations.

It portrays level ground rather than providing a geological or geometrical formula.

Ta Ha 20:108: following the caller without deviation

يَوْمَئِذٍ يَتَّبِعُونَ الدَّاعِيَ لَا عِوَجَ لَهُ ۖ وَخَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا

yawmaʾidhin yattabiʿūna al-dāʿiya lā ʿiwaja lahu wa-khashaʿati al-aṣwātu li-l-raḥmāni fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsā

Ṭā Hā 20:108

“That day they follow the caller; there is no deviation in relation to him. Voices fall quiet before the Entirely Merciful, and you hear only a faint sound.”

Ta Ha 20:108

ʿIwaja follows the categorical negation lā.

Lā ʿiwaja therefore denies crookedness.

The important issue is the referent of lahu and the relationship in which deviation is denied.

The nearest explicit antecedent is al-dāʿī, the caller.

On this reading, the phrase can deny deviation in his call, which reaches those addressed without favoring one group over another.

Another explanation concerns the people's response: they follow the caller without turning aside from answering him.

On that reading, lā ʿiwaja lahu concerns undeviating following and the absence of escape from the summons.

Both directions are discussed in Arabic lexical and grammatical explanations.

They should not be collapsed into one certain conclusion without examining them.

The difference from the preceding verse

Ta Ha 20:107 concerns unevenness of the ground.

Ta Ha 20:108 uses the word in the context of following the caller.

The same word thus concerns two different subjects in successive verses.

The first concerns the ground.

The second concerns following and calling.

Both retain the central concept of crookedness or departure from alignment.

One concerns material alignment and levelness.

The other concerns the course or relationship of following.

ʿIwaj here does not itself mean submission: the quieting of voices is separately expressed by khashaʿati al-aṣwāt.

The root's meaning should not be identified with every condition described in the resurrection scene.

6. Comparison with neighboring roots

ʿ W J (ʿiwaj) and Q W M (istiqāmah)

ʿIwaj denotes crookedness and departure from alignment.

Istiqāmah belongs to Q W M and, concerning paths and conduct, emphasizes straightness, firmness, and maintaining the right direction.

The two often stand in opposition.

Someone may recognize the right way yet depart from it.

Knowing the way and remaining on it are then distinct matters.

ʿIwaj expresses deviation; istiqāmah emphasizes remaining straight in the right direction.

Al-Kahf 18:1 and Al-Kahf 18:2 pair the denial of crookedness with the Book's uprightness.

Their pairing shows a close conceptual relationship, not complete synonymy between the roots.

ʿ W J (ʿiwaj) and Z Y GH (zaygh)

Zaygh also concerns deviation or inclination away from the right direction.

Al Imran 3:7 describes people with zaygh in their hearts who pursue ambiguous passages.

In the verses concerning The God's way, ʿiwaj describes crookedness in the course or in the image presented of it.

Both roots concern deviation, but their objects may differ.

Al Imran 3:7 attributes zaygh to hearts.

Al Imran 3:99 relates ʿiwaj to The God's way.

This contextual distinction should not become an absolute rule restricting one root to hearts and the other to paths.

ʿ W J (ʿiwaj) and M Y L (mayl)

Mayl concerns inclination or leaning to one side.

ʿIwaj likewise concerns altered alignment and crookedness.

Not every inclination is blameworthy crookedness.

A branch may lean toward light, or a person may develop an interest in beneficial work.

Inclination in these uses does not itself mean corruption.

Physical ʿiwaj is also not necessarily blameworthy, although deviation becomes prominent when contrasted with the right, upright way.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:27 uses maylan ʿaẓīman concerning those who want believers to incline into great deviation.

The negative judgment arises from the subject and context, not inclination alone.

ʿ W J (ʿiwaj) and Ḥ N F (ḥanīf)

Quranic ḥanīf concerns turning toward truth and the right way in opposition to associating partners with The God.

The Arabic Ḥ N F family also has uses concerning inclination and the condition of the feet.

Related physical imagery alone does not make ḥanīf and aʿwaj synonymous.

The Quran uses ḥanīf for Abraham's orientation against such association.

ʿIwaj in verses concerning The God's way expresses departure from the right course.

They can be compared in religious orientation, but each must be understood through its own usage.

ʿ W J (ʿiwaj) and Ṣ D D (ṣadd)

Ṣadd concerns barring and obstructing.

ʿIwaj denotes crookedness and deviation.

The two families occur together in five verses.

Preventing entry onto a route illustrates obstruction.

Misdescribing that route so travelers go elsewhere is closer to misrepresenting the way.

Obstruction and making crooked are related but distinct acts.

Their pairing may show that leading people astray does not require physical barriers; misrepresenting truth and misleading guidance may also do so.

ʿ W J (ʿiwaj) and ʾ M T (amt)

Amt accompanies ʿiwaj in Ta Ha 20:107.

Both concern uneven ground.

ʿIwaj concerns crookedness and departure from alignment, while lexical accounts also associate amt with elevation or differences of level.

Their pairing describes the ground through the denial of two forms of irregularity or unevenness.

Coordination alone, however, cannot conclusively establish every boundary between their meanings.

7. Proposed translations for Forqan

In all its Quranic uses, ʿiwaj concerns crookedness and deviation.

Translation must nevertheless distinguish the way, the Book's freedom from crookedness, uneven ground, and deviation in following.

ʿIwajan — Al Imran 3:99

Brief root meaning: bending and departure from alignment or a path; in nonphysical matters, deviation from the right direction.

Meaning in this verse: crookedness sought for The God's way, or an effort to portray it that way to others.

Proposed rendering: “crookedness.”

Rendering the phrase:

تَبْغُونَهَا عِوَجًا

tabghūnahā ʿiwajā

Al-Zumar 39:28

“Without any crookedness.”

The Persian kajī or kazhī, “crookedness,” is a natural basic equivalent throughout. “Deviation” can clarify certain contexts, but lies, sin, association, contradiction, or deliberate distortion should not replace the word in direct translation without contextual grounds.

8. Conclusion

ʿ W J denotes crookedness, curvature, and departure from alignment or a path.

This can be physical, as in a bent stick, a leaning wall, or uneven ground.

It can also concern paths, speech, thought, conduct, and religion, where departure from a sound course or standard is at issue.

Arabic records distinctions between ʿawaj and ʿiwaj, but not an absolute division. The ground in Ta Ha 20:107 and the quoted kasrah reading of Labid's curved tusk illustrate perceptible crookedness with ʿiwaj.

Every Quranic occurrence is the noun ʿiwaj.

Five verses concern people who bar others from The God's way and seek it crooked: deviation in guidance and attempts to divert or misrepresent the true way.

Al-Kahf 18:1 and Al-Zumar 39:28 deny crookedness in the Book and Quran.

Ta Ha 20:107 concerns level ground without crookedness.

Ta Ha 20:108 denies deviation in the context of following the resurrection caller.

These uses retain the root's central meaning while changing its object.

Crookedness is not always a moral judgment. A curved branch or winding road need not be wrong or harmful. Blame concerns departure from truth, justice, or guidance.

Final definition of ʿ W J:

“Bending, crookedness, and departure from alignment or a path, whether in a body's form and extension or in a person's course and orientation. Quranic ʿiwaj expresses crookedness and deviation: according to context, it may concern uneven ground, seeking The God's way crooked, the Quran's freedom from crookedness, or undeviating following. Its moral value and precise instance arise from the verse's subject and construction, not from the root alone.”
