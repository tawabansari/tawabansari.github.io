The three-consonant Arabic root ف ظ ظ concerns harshness, rough speech, unpleasant conduct, and hurtful treatment of others. فَظّ describes someone whose speech or social behavior is rough, sharp, and inconsiderate.

فَظَاظَة denotes this disposition and conduct. It may appear in tone of voice, treatment of others, reproach, humiliation, or disregard for their feelings and dignity.

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as فَظًّا in Al Imran 3:159. The God reminds Muhammad that, through divine mercy, he was gentle with the believers: had he been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around him.

The important distinction is that harshness is not the same as candor, firmness, or insistence on truth. A person can speak clearly, even critically, without humiliating the listener. Conversely, someone may be right but undermine the possibility of being heard through rough behavior.

The verse calls neither for abandoning truth nor for accepting people’s demands unconditionally. It joins gentleness with pardon, seeking forgiveness, consultation, decision, and trust.

1. The Root in Early Arabic

Roughness in speech and social dealings

Early lexicons define الفَظّ as:

الْخَشِنُ الْكَلَامِ

That is, someone whose speech is harsh and rough.

They also use the expression:

رَجُلٌ فَظٌّ

That is, a man who is harsh, ill-mannered, and severe in his dealings.

Explanations also include terms such as جافٍ, غليظ, and سيّئ الخُلُق.

جافٍ describes someone who treats others with coldness and inconsideration.

غليظ, in this semantic field, concerns roughness and hardness.

سيّئ الخُلُق describes someone with bad manners and an unpleasant disposition.

These terms are close in meaning without being identical.

Someone may be strict when stating a ruling while speaking respectfully and fairly. Another person may treat a simple matter so humiliatingly and hurtfully that others avoid discussion with them.

The latter aspect is prominent in the social use of فظ: roughness and unpleasantness in the manner of dealing with others.

Evidence from early Arabic poetry

Ru’bah ibn al-‘Ajjaj says in one of his rajaz poems:

لَمَّا رَأَيْنَا مِنْهُمُ مُغْتَاظَا
تَعْرِفُ مِنْهُ اللُّؤْمَ وَالْفِظَاظَا

Lammā raʾaynā minhumu mughtāẓā / taʿrifu minhu l-luʾma wa-l-fiẓāẓā.

Translation: “When we saw an angry man among them, you could recognize his baseness and harshness.”

Here الفِظاظ denotes harshness and rough conduct.

It occurs alongside اللؤم, meaning baseness and ignoble behavior.

مغتاظ describes a person who has become angry.

This association does not make anger and harshness identical.

A person may become angry but restrain that anger and refrain from insulting someone else.

Conversely, someone may show no obvious anger yet consistently treat others coldly, contemptuously, and roughly.

Harshness is therefore not simply a feeling of anger; it concerns how a person acts and treats others.

The historical position of this evidence

Ru’bah ibn al-‘Ajjaj was an Islamic-era poet, known in the late Umayyad and early Abbasid periods. He died in 145 AH.

His poetry must therefore not be presented as conclusive pre-Qur’anic evidence or as pre-Islamic verse.

This rajaz passage is useful evidence for early Arabic usage, but historically it follows the period of Qur’anic revelation.

For the meaning of harshness itself, فَظًّا in Al Imran 3:159 is direct linguistic evidence from the period of revelation.

This example illustrates post-Qur’anic usage. By itself, it is not evidence for establishing the word’s meaning in pre-Islamic poetry.

A later poem should not be labeled pre-Islamic merely to give the root an apparently complete history.

What can be stated confidently is that the meaning of harshness and rough conduct occurs in Qur’anic Arabic and is also clearly recorded in early lexical and literary works.

2. الفَظّ: An Unpleasant Fluid

Lexicons record another, material use of فَظّ.

الفَظّ denoted fluid in an animal’s rumen, especially a camel’s, sometimes extracted during severe water shortages.

The Arabic expression اِفْتَظَّ الْكَرِشَ referred to extracting this fluid from the rumen.

Mixed with the contents of the rumen, it was not a pleasant drink.

In Maqayis al-Lughah, Ibn Faris connects this family with unpleasantness and aversion, then explains that some lexicographers compared a person’s harshness to the unpleasantness of this fluid.

This explanation makes semantic sense.

Just as drinking an unpleasant fluid is difficult, dealing with a rough and hurtful person can be unpleasant.

A historical distinction must nevertheless be maintained.

The existence of two uses under one root does not, by itself, prove that the meaning of harshness developed from the name of rumen fluid.

Ibn Faris himself attributes the connection to certain language scholars.

A more careful formulation is therefore:

Early sources record فظّ both for a harsh person and for unpleasant rumen fluid. Lexicographers proposed a connection through unpleasantness, but their historical order of emergence is not conclusively established.

This material branch does not occur in the Qur’an.

The meaning of فَظًّا in Al Imran 3:159 must therefore not be turned into rumen fluid or drinking it.

3. Linguistic Forms and Core Meaning

ف ظ ظ is a doubled root: its second and third consonants are both ظ.

In some derived forms, they merge into a single consonant marked with shaddah.

The principal recorded forms include:

  • فَظّ: harsh, rough, and inconsiderate in speech and dealings.
  • فَظَاظَة: harshness and rough conduct.
  • فَظَظ: roughness in conduct or speech.
  • فِظَاظ: another form denoting harshness.
  • أَفْظَاظ: the plural of فَظّ; harsh people.
  • أَفَظّ: harsher or rougher.
  • فَظِظَ: he became harsh or behaved roughly in speech and conduct.
  • أَفْظَظَ: in certain lexical uses, he prevented someone from obtaining what they wanted.
  • اِفْتَظَّ: in the material branch, he extracted rumen fluid.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Only فَظًّا occurs there.

فظّ functions here as an adjective describing a person’s disposition and behavior.

Its Qur’anic core meaning can be defined as:

Harshness in speech or treatment, such that a person’s conduct is hurtful and alienating to others.

This definition concerns conduct, not necessarily whether the content of what someone says is true or false.

4. Qur’anic Usage: “Had You Been Harsh and Hard-Hearted”

Al Imran 3:159

فَبِمَا رَحْمَةٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ لِنْتَ لَهُمْ ۖ وَلَوْ كُنْتَ فَظًّا غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ لَانْفَضُّوا مِنْ حَوْلِكَ ۖ فَاعْفُ عَنْهُمْ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ ۖ فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُتَوَكِّلِينَ

Fa-bi-mā raḥmatin mina llāhi linta lahum wa-law kunta faẓẓan ghalīẓa l-qalbi la-nfaḍḍū min ḥawlika fa-ʿfu ʿanhum wa-staghfir lahum wa-shāwirhum fī l-amri fa-idhā ʿazamta fa-tawakkal ʿalā llāhi inna llāha yuḥibbu l-mutawakkilīn.

Translation: “So through mercy from The God you were gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you. So pardon them, seek forgiveness for them, and consult them in the matter. Then, when you have resolved, put your trust in The God. Indeed, The God loves those who put their trust in Him.”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

The verse begins by attributing Muhammad’s gentleness to the mercy of The God.

It then presents a hypothetical situation: had he been harsh and hard-hearted, people would have dispersed from around him.

Three commands follow: pardon them, seek forgiveness for them, and consult them.

The conclusion addresses resolution and trust.

This sequence shows that the verse is about more than pleasant conversation or outward etiquette. It concerns dealing with people, maintaining social bonds, and fulfilling responsibility under difficult conditions.

“Through mercy from The God you were gentle with them”

لِنْتَ comes from ل ي ن.

This root concerns softness and flexibility.

Here Muhammad’s gentleness is connected with divine mercy.

لَهُمْ indicates that this conduct is directed toward the people addressed in the surrounding context.

The verse does not merely say that Muhammad felt kindly inwardly; it describes how he treated others.

A person may inwardly wish someone well, yet speak so sharply and humiliatingly that this goodwill is not evident in practice.

Gentle conduct, by contrast, can help a listener hear something difficult without feeling that their dignity has been disregarded.

“Had you been harsh”

Here فَظًّا describes a hypothetical state of Muhammad.

لَوْ كُنْتَ means “had you been.”

The verse does not say that Muhammad was harsh in that situation.

Its opening describes his actual gentleness and then explains the hypothetical consequence of the opposite behavior.

فَظًّا should therefore not be taken as an actual description of Muhammad’s character.

Harshness here is the condition contrasted with gentleness.

5. Distinguishing فَظًّا from غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ

The verse places two expressions together:

«فَظًّا»

Faẓẓan.

and

«غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ»

Ghalīẓa l-qalb.

Their association matters.

فظ chiefly points to outward roughness in speech and social dealings.

غليظ القلب concerns inward hardness, reduced tenderness, and disregard for others’ circumstances.

For example, someone may speak very sharply yet feel remorse upon recognizing the harm they have caused.

Another person may speak calmly while remaining indifferent to others’ suffering and rights.

In the first case, outward roughness is prominent.

In the second, inward hardness and lack of compassion may be more important.

This example should not establish an absolute boundary between the words.

Harsh conduct can arise from hard-heartedness, and hard-heartedness can become evident in conduct.

The two descriptions together give a clearer picture of a rough and unkind person.

Is hard-heartedness the same as firmness?

No.

Firmness concerns the ability to decide and remain committed to a decision.

Hard-heartedness here concerns roughness and a lack of gentleness and mercy toward others.

The verse itself concludes:

«فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ»

Fa-idhā ʿazamta fa-tawakkal ʿalā llāh.

That is, once you have resolved, put your trust in The God.

The Qur’an thus rejects harshness and hard-heartedness while affirming firm decision-making.

If firmness were identical with hard-heartedness, these two directions within one verse would make no sense.

A person can therefore be firm in respecting rights and fulfilling responsibility without treating people roughly or humiliating them.

6. “They Would Have Dispersed from Around You”: The Consequence of Harshness

The verse continues:

«لَانْفَضُّوا مِنْ حَوْلِكَ»

La-nfaḍḍū min ḥawlik.

That is, they would have dispersed from around you.

اِنْفَضُّوا comes from ف ض ض, not ف ظ ظ.

These roots are similar in writing and sound, but remain distinct.

ف ظ ظ concerns harshness and rough conduct.

ف ض ض here denotes dispersing and separating from a gathering.

The verse therefore connects conduct with a possible consequence:

Harshness and hard-heartedness could have caused people to disperse from around Muhammad.

Does people’s departure always prove that someone is wrong?

No.

Someone may state an unwelcome truth, and some listeners may distance themselves because they refuse to accept it.

The Qur’an itself describes people opposing and turning away from Messengers.

A reduction in followers or dissatisfaction among listeners does not, by itself, prove that the speaker is harsh or wrong.

Al Imran 3:159, however, concerns a specific situation: if Muhammad had treated the believers roughly and hard-heartedly, that conduct could have dispersed them from around him.

This relationship should not become an absolute rule that everyone whom people leave must have behaved badly.

Nor should truth be concealed or every demand accepted merely to keep people present.

The verse recommends gentleness, not manipulating listeners into staying.

7. Context: Dealing with People Who Have Faltered

Al Imran 3:159 follows verses about difficulties in the believers’ confrontation with the enemy.

Al Imran 3:152 mentions weakness, disagreement, and disobedience among some believers.

The Qur’an explains that some desired this world and others the Hereafter.

The same verse also recalls divine pardon.

Al Imran 3:155 describes those who turned away and faltered when the two groups met; divine pardon is mentioned again.

In this context, verse 159 addresses Muhammad’s treatment of people among whom mistakes and weakness had occurred.

This connection is important.

Gentleness here is not reserved for times when everyone has behaved correctly and no problem has occurred.

The issue is continuing relationships and responsibility after a difficult experience.

Pardon without denying wrongdoing

The first command is:

«فَاعْفُ عَنْهُمْ»

Fa-ʿfu ʿanhum.

That is, pardon them.

This does not mean that no wrongdoing took place.

The preceding verses explicitly mention weakness, disagreement, and disobedience.

One can recognize a mistake clearly and discuss its consequences while refraining from prolonged reproach and vindictive conduct.

Pardoning past conduct is not the same as regarding it as acceptable.

Seeking forgiveness

The second command is:

«وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ»

Wa-staghfir lahum.

That is, seek forgiveness for them.

This shows that people’s past conduct and moral responsibility are still taken seriously.

Without wrongdoing or responsibility, seeking forgiveness in this context would have no clear meaning.

Kindness is therefore not identical with ignoring accountability.

Consulting those same people

The third command is:

«وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ»

Wa-shāwirhum fī l-amr.

That is, consult them in the matter.

This part is particularly significant.

Despite the experience of disagreement and disobedience, the Qur’an does not order everyone permanently excluded from discussion and the formation of decisions.

Consultation can help views be heard, past mistakes understood, and better ways found to continue fulfilling responsibility.

For example, if a group has made a mistake in a shared task, its leader can examine the mistake and then ask those same people how to prevent its recurrence.

This does not endorse the past mistake; it creates an opportunity for correction and learning from experience.

The verse itself does not, of course, say that every suggestion must be accepted.

Consultation and the final decision are two different stages.

Deciding after consultation

The conclusion says:

«فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ»

Fa-idhā ʿazamta fa-tawakkal ʿalā llāh.

That is, once you have resolved, put your trust in The God.

The Qur’an therefore does not call Muhammad to indecision or to keeping everyone satisfied at all times.

It asks him to be gentle with people, pardon their mistakes, seek forgiveness for them, consult them, and then decide.

This sequence shows that gentleness, consultation, and firmness in decision-making can coexist.

8. Does Gentleness Mean Compromising Truth?

One misconception is that avoiding harshness requires avoiding candid discussion of mistakes, injustice, or false statements.

The Qur’an does not say this.

In Ta Ha 20:44, Moses and Aaron are instructed to speak gently to Pharaoh.

Ta Ha 20:44

فَقُولَا لَهُ قَوْلًا لَيِّنًا لَعَلَّهُ يَتَذَكَّرُ أَوْ يَخْشَىٰ

Fa-qūlā lahu qawlan layyinan laʿallahu yatadhakkaru aw yakhshā.

Translation: “So speak gently to him, that he may take heed or be in awe.”

The Qur’an portrays Pharaoh as oppressive, rebellious, and arrogant.

Nevertheless, Moses and Aaron are instructed to speak gently.

This does not endorse Pharaoh’s behavior.

They must convey the message of The God and state the truth against his claims.

Gentle speech and a candid message are therefore compatible.

In An Nahl 16:125, inviting others is likewise joined with wisdom, good counsel, and discussion in the best manner.

At Tawbah 9:123, by contrast, addresses severity toward enemies confronting the believers.

These uses must be examined in their respective circumstances.

Severity in confronting an enemy and resisting aggression is not the same as harshness and humiliation in conversation and social dealings.

An instruction concerning a wartime situation therefore cannot authorize rough treatment of every intellectual opponent or questioner.

Nor can the command to be gentle mean remaining inactive in the face of oppression and aggression.

The criterion is understanding one’s responsibility and choosing the appropriate manner of response in that situation.

9. Comparison with Related Roots

ف ظ ظ and غ ل ظ

These occur together in Al Imran 3:159.

فظّ chiefly concerns harshness and outward roughness in conduct and speech.

غليظ relates to hardness, roughness, and thickness; غليظ القلب expresses hard-heartedness.

The verse’s context supports this distinction, but it should not become an absolute boundary for every Arabic usage.

A harsh person may also be hard-hearted, just as hard-heartedness may appear in rough speech and conduct.

ف ظ ظ and ق س و

قساوة concerns hardening and reduced softness and responsiveness.

When applied to the heart, it connects with rejecting reminders and being hard-hearted.

فظاظة is more evident in how someone treats others.

Hardness of heart may lead to harsh conduct, but not every sharp remark warrants a definitive judgment about the speaker’s entire inward condition.

Someone may also be outwardly polite while acting ruthlessly toward others’ rights.

A person’s character should therefore be understood through their speech, conduct, and responses together.

ف ظ ظ and ل ي ن

The two are contrasted in the same verse.

لِين concerns softness.

فظاظة here concerns roughness in treatment.

Softness does not mean lacking principles or decisions.

The verse ends with a command to resolve and trust.

Gentle conduct can therefore accompany firmness in decision.

ف ظ ظ and ر ف ق

رفق concerns gentleness, consideration, and measured, accommodating conduct.

In the ethical field, this concept is close to gentleness.

It does not, however, mean indifference to wrongdoing.

Sometimes the proper response to someone who has erred is to explain the mistake respectfully and clearly, allowing an opportunity to correct it.

Harshness, by contrast, may make that opportunity more difficult through humiliation and hurt.

ف ظ ظ and ف ض ض

These roots appear in one sentence in Al Imran 3:159 but differ in meaning and structure.

فَظًّا derives from ف ظ ظ and means harsh.

لَانْفَضُّوا derives from ف ض ض and means “they would have dispersed.”

The verse thus connects harshness with people dispersing.

In Al Jumu‘ah 62:11, انْفَضُّوا إِلَيْهَا also describes people leaving a gathering and going toward trade.

This shows that dispersal does not itself mean roughness or bad temper.

People may disperse from a gathering for different reasons.

In Al Imran 3:159, Muhammad’s hypothetical harshness and hard-heartedness are identified as the cause of that dispersal.

ف ظ ظ and غ ي ظ

غيظ concerns intense anger and inward agitation.

فظاظة concerns rough treatment.

Someone may restrain their anger and refrain from hurtful behavior despite being upset.

Al Imran 3:134 places restraining anger alongside pardoning people and doing good.

This verse helps distinguish the feeling of anger from a person’s conduct.

Anger may arise, but the subsequent response remains the person’s responsibility.

Harshness should not be inferred merely from the presence of anger; actual treatment must also be examined.

10. Morphology and Syntax

ف ظ ظ is a doubled three-consonant root.

In فَظّ, the two instances of ظ merge into one consonant marked with shaddah.

In Al Imran 3:159, فَظًّا is indefinite and accusative.

In the construction:

«وَلَوْ كُنْتَ فَظًّا»

Wa-law kunta faẓẓan.

فَظًّا is the predicate of كُنْتَ.

The sentence therefore concerns a hypothetical quality and condition of the addressee.

غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ is a further description alongside it; syntactically, it can be understood as a supplementary description or another predicate referring to the same addressee.

In either case, both expressions describe the person’s hypothetical condition.

The function of لَوْ

لَوْ introduces a conditional sentence here.

Its consequence is:

«لَانْفَضُّوا مِنْ حَوْلِكَ»

La-nfaḍḍū min ḥawlik.

That is, they would have dispersed from around you.

The construction describes a hypothetical consequence of harshness and hard-heartedness.

It does not say that Muhammad actually behaved that way and that people dispersed.

On the contrary, it states his gentleness and then shows what could have resulted from its absence.

Adjective and verb

فَظًّا in the Qur’an is an adjective, not the verb فَظَّ.

This matters when recording the word’s form in the root index.

لِنْتَ is a past-tense verb from ل ي ن.

لَانْفَضُّوا is a past-tense verb from ف ض ض.

فَاعْفُ is an imperative from ع ف و.

وَشَاوِرْهُمْ is an imperative from ش و ر.

Several lexical families with different functions therefore occur together in one verse.

The meanings of gentleness, pardon, consultation, and trust should not all be placed directly inside the definition of ف ظ ظ.

They are related concepts in the verse’s context, not derivatives of the same root.

11. Challenges of Understanding and Translation

The first error is equating harshness with candor.

Speech may be direct and clear while remaining respectful.

Conversely, someone may behave insultingly and contemptuously without discussing anything important.

Harshness is therefore not determined solely by whether the listener dislikes the content; the manner of treatment also matters.

The second error is assuming that gentleness means endorsing everything people do.

The verses preceding Al Imran 3:159 explicitly describe weakness and disobedience.

Pardon and gentleness therefore do not mean concealing a mistake or refusing to regard it as wrong.

The third error is turning gentleness into indecision.

At the end of the verse, Muhammad is called to decide and trust.

Gentleness and firmness can therefore coexist.

The fourth error is claiming that whenever people leave someone, that person must have been harsh.

The Qur’an also describes people turning away from an invitation to truth.

The actual reason for their distancing must therefore be examined.

The fifth error is using gentleness to prevent criticism and questions.

Consultation appears in this very verse. Genuine consultation requires room for differing views.

Someone who listens only to supporters and humiliates opponents into silence has departed from participation in discussion.

The sixth error is equating a calm appearance with real mercy.

Someone may speak politely while violating others’ rights in practice.

The verse is not merely describing a speaking technique: it joins gentleness with pardon, seeking forgiveness, and consultation.

Finally, rough treatment should not be regarded as a necessary means of teaching religion or reforming society.

Harshness may cause a listener to withdraw before having the opportunity to understand an argument.

This does not guarantee that gentle speech will secure acceptance of truth; it shows that verbal roughness can create an additional obstacle to communication and understanding.

12. Translation Recommendations for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for فَظّ include:

“Harsh in manner”

“Harsh-tempered”

“Rough in speech and conduct”

“Ill-mannered”

“Inconsiderate in social dealings”

فَظَاظَة can be translated as “harshness,” “rough conduct,” or “hurtful treatment.”

“Hard-hearted” alone is not a precise equivalent of فَظّ, since the verse separately includes غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ.

For Al Imran 3:159:

«وَلَوْ كُنْتَ فَظًّا غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ»

Wa-law kunta faẓẓan ghalīẓa l-qalb.

Suggested translation:

“Had you been harsh and hard-hearted…”

This brief, clear, natural rendering preserves the distinction between rough conduct and hard-heartedness.

“Had you been rough and hard-hearted” is also defensible. In Persian, the preferred equivalent more clearly identifies a behavioral disposition and manner of dealing with others.

For the complete verse, the following rendering is suitable:

“So through mercy from The God you were gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you. So pardon them, seek forgiveness for them, and consult them in the matter. Then, when you have resolved, put your trust in The God. Indeed, The God loves those who put their trust in Him.”

Distinguishing the general meaning from the verse’s meaning

Word form: فَظًّا

Root: ف ظ ظ

Brief root meaning: Harshness, rough speech and social conduct, hurtful treatment.

Meaning in this verse: Harsh and rough in dealing with people; a hypothetical description contrasted with Muhammad’s gentleness.

Consultation, pardon, and the social consequences of roughness belong to the verse’s context; they should not all be incorporated into the brief root definition.

13. Synthesis

The Arabic root ف ظ ظ concerns harshness, rough speech, and unpleasant treatment of others.

Early lexical sources describe فظّ as a person whose speech and dealings are rough and hurtful.

A material usage for unpleasant rumen fluid is also recorded. Some lexicographers proposed a connection through unpleasantness, but the historical derivation of harshness from that fluid is not conclusively established.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as فَظًّا in Al Imran 3:159.

The verse attributes Muhammad’s gentleness to divine mercy and explains that, had he been harsh and hard-hearted, people would have dispersed from around him.

It then commands pardon, seeking forgiveness, consultation, decision, and trust.

This sequence shows that gentleness is more than making speech sound pleasant. It concerns actual conduct toward others’ mistakes and the continuation of shared responsibility.

The root’s core meaning is harshness in speech and dealings: conduct that can hurt others and distance them from communication and cooperation.

Harshness must nevertheless be distinguished from candor, firmness, and defending what is right.

Someone can openly criticize a mistake, reject oppression, and remain committed to a sound decision without resorting to humiliation and mistreatment.

Conversely, someone who conceals truth or evades responsibility in the name of preserving relationships has not necessarily fulfilled the command to be gentle.

In Al Imran 3:159, gentleness accompanies pardon and consultation, but also leads to decision and trust.

Thus, the central message of this usage is that steadfastness in truth does not require rough conduct, just as kindness and respect do not mean abandoning truth and responsibility.
