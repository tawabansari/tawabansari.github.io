From hujūd, nighttime sleep, to tahajjud: waking and setting sleep aside for worship

1. Meaning in early Arabic

In early Arabic, hā–jīm–dāl concerns sleep and wakefulness at night. Hajada and its verbal noun hujūd commonly mean sleeping, especially nighttime sleep. Hājid likewise denotes someone asleep.

Some uses of the same family concern remaining awake and worshipping at night. Arabic linguists therefore include this entry among words with opposite senses: depending on form and context, they can concern sleeping or remaining awake.

Understanding the distinction requires separating hujūd from tahajjud.

Hujūd principally refers to sleep, while tahajjud, in its night-worship usage, means rising from sleep or setting sleep aside to remain awake and worship.

The connection is understandable in ordinary life. People normally reserve night for rest and sleep, but sometimes forgo sleep or wake after sleeping to attend to something. In religious usage, this wakefulness may be devoted to prayer, Quran recitation, remembrance of The God, and worship.

This explanation does not imply that every derivative has always had only one of these two meanings. The recorded differences are real, and each form must be examined separately.

An early Arabic poetic witness

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, whose life spanned the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

قُلْتُ هَجِّدْنَا فَقَدْ طَالَ السُّرَى

qultu hajjidnā fa-qad ṭāla al-surā

وَقَدَرْنَا إِنْ خَنَا الدَّهْرِ غَفَلْ

wa-qadarnā in khanā al-dahri ghafal

“I said: let us sleep, for the night journey has grown long; we can continue on our way if the ravages of time leave us alone.”

Al-surā denotes travel by night. After a long night journey, the verse turns to sleep and rest. Commentators differ over what “we can” refers to in the second hemistich, including continuing the journey or reaching water; this does not affect the sleep-related sense of hajjidnā.

Hajjidnā here means putting someone to sleep or allowing time to sleep, rather than waking them for worship.

The witness confirms an actual sleep-related branch in this family. Labīd lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, and the precise date of the passage is unclear; it cannot securely be assigned to the pre-Islamic period.

Arabic linguistic sources also record hājid and hujūd for sleeping people. Together with Quranic usage, these witnesses show that the association with sleep and nighttime wakefulness belonged to the family’s recognized semantic field around the period of revelation.

Defensible historical definition: the principal branch of hā–jīm–dāl concerns sleeping, particularly at night; another branch concerns remaining awake or rising from sleep, especially for nighttime worship.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded forms include:

  • هَجَدَ: slept; in some uses, remained awake or worshipped at night.
  • هُجُود: sleeping, especially at night.
  • هَاجِد: asleep; in some recorded uses, someone worshipping at night.
  • هُجَّد and هُجُود: plural or descriptive forms for sleepers and, in some uses, nighttime worshippers.
  • هَجَّدَ: in different uses, put someone to sleep or woke them.
  • تَهَجَّدَ: rose from sleep or remained awake at night for worship.
  • تَهَجُّد: nighttime wakefulness and worship, especially after waking from sleep.
  • مُتَهَجِّد: someone who wakes or stays awake at night for worship.
  • أَهْجَدَ: in some uses, slept, put someone to sleep, or found them asleep.

Linguistic sources report these forms, but the Quran uses only the imperative تَهَجَّدْ with an attached fāʾ.

Why do sleep and wakefulness both occur in this family?

Arabic linguists offer several explanations.

One analysis takes hajada as originally meaning to sleep and tahajjada as setting hujūd aside: the person casts off sleep and wakes.

Some Arabic formations on the tafaʿʿul pattern concern removing or relinquishing something. On this basis, tahajjud has been explained as leaving sleep behind.

Another analysis recognizes both sleeping and remaining awake as attested uses within the family, with form and context determining the intended sense.

Neither explanation should be turned into a certain historical claim. The tafaʿʿul pattern alone cannot prove that a word always means removing what the basic verb denotes.

In Quranic usage, the direction of tahajjad toward nighttime wakefulness is clear.

Must tahajjud follow sleep?

Early Arabic explanations often associate tahajjud with rising after sleep. Someone who sleeps for part of the night and then wakes for worship is accordingly a clear example of a mutahajjid.

Other lexical accounts explain tahajjud more broadly as staying awake at night for prayer or remembrance of The God.

Two questions must therefore be distinguished. Worship after sleep is a recognized use of tahajjud; excluding every night vigil without prior sleep requires an argument beyond the general meaning of nighttime wakefulness.

When examining the Quranic verse, one must likewise ask whether the sentence itself stipulates earlier sleep or simply speaks of wakefulness and worship during part of the night.

3. Quranic usage: Al-Isrāʾ 17:79

The root occurs only once in the Quran.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:79

وَمِنَ اللَّيْلِ فَتَهَجَّدْ بِهِ نَافِلَةً لَكَ عَسَىٰ أَنْ يَبْعَثَكَ رَبُّكَ مَقَامًا مَحْمُودًا

wa-mina al-layli fa-tahajjad bihi nāfilatan laka ʿasā an yabʿathaka rabbuka maqāman maḥmūdā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:79

“And during part of the night, keep vigil with it, as an addition for you; perhaps your Lord will raise you to a praised station.”

The verse directly addresses Muhammad and follows the instruction concerning the establishment of prayer and dawn recitation in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78.

The meaning of fa-tahajjad

فَتَهَجَّدْ is a form-V imperative addressing one male.

The initial fāʾ connects the command to the preceding discourse. Tahajjad calls the addressee to nighttime wakefulness.

In context, this does not mean remaining sleepless for any activity whatever. The command continues the discussion of worship, prayer, and Quran recitation.

Its appropriate contextual sense is therefore remaining awake or rising from sleep for worship.

It must not be replaced by hujūd in the sense of sleeping. The person addressed is not being told to sleep, but to devote part of the night to worship.

Wa-mina al-layl: part of the night

مِنَ اللَّيْلِ indicates that the command concerns part of the night.

The expression neither fixes the exact length of that part nor names a particular hour.

The word and sentence alone therefore do not specify a number of prayer units, a precise waking time, or a fixed schedule for every night.

Those details require independent evidence.

Bihi: keeping vigil with what?

The pronoun in بِهِ is masculine singular.

The preceding verse, Al-Isrāʾ 17:78, mentions قُرْآنَ الْفَجْرِ, dawn recitation. Referring the pronoun to the Quran or its recitation is consequently a clear, contextually appropriate reading.

On this reading, the command is not merely to remain awake, but to keep vigil with the Quran in worship.

The connection also accords with Al-Muzzammil, where rising at night accompanies measured Quran recitation.

Nevertheless, the lexical meaning of tahajjud should not be restricted to “reading the Quran.” The Quran provides the object and content of the vigil here; the verb concerns nighttime wakefulness.

Nāfilatan laka: an addition for you

Nāfilah comes from nūn–fāʾ–lām and denotes an addition beyond an original amount.

In this verse, nāfilatan laka presents the night worship as an addition for the addressee.

Laka matters because it directs the address to Muhammad. This verse alone therefore does not establish that the command, with every feature it has here, binds all people or adherents of all religious dispensations in the same way.

Conversely, the particular addressee does not exclude others from worshipping at night. Other Quranic verses describe believers keeping night vigils too.

The direct recipient of this command must therefore be distinguished from the general value of nighttime worship.

Maqāman maḥmūdan: a praised station

The verse ends with the prospect of Muhammad being raised to a praised station.

This expresses a promise and a prospect for the addressee, but “a praised station” is not part of tahajjud’s lexical meaning.

The phrase itself does not explicitly name intercession or detail an otherworldly station. Such interpretations require separate examination alongside other verses; they should not define the word tahajjud.

4. Connections with other Quranic verses

Although the root occurs only once, nighttime wakefulness and worship appear elsewhere in the Quran.

Al-Muzzammil 73:2: rising at night

قُمِ اللَّيْلَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

qumi al-layla illā qalīlā

Al-Muzzammil 73:2

“Stand through the night, except a little.”

This is a direct command to rise at night.

Al-Muzzammil 73:4 connects measured Quran recitation with that command. Nighttime wakefulness thus accompanies Quran recitation here, as it does in Al-Isrāʾ 17:79.

Qum, however, comes from qāf–wāw–mīm; it is not a derivative of hā–jīm–dāl.

Al-Muzzammil 73:20: differing human capacities

Al-Muzzammil 73:20 speaks of Muhammad and a group of his companions standing during different portions of the night.

It then recognizes human limitations, illness, travel to seek sustenance, and other responsibilities, and commands recitation of as much of the Quran as is manageable.

The passage shows that Quranic night vigils are not considered without regard to human capacity and circumstances.

The companions’ participation also shows that this practice was not confined to Muhammad personally, although the scope of each command must be determined from its own address.

Al-Furqān 25:64: the night in prostration and standing

وَالَّذِينَ يَبِيتُونَ لِرَبِّهِمْ سُجَّدًا وَقِيَامًا

wa-alladhīna yabītūna li-rabbihim sujjadan wa-qiyāmā

Al-Furqān 25:64

“And those who spend the night before their Lord, prostrating and standing.”

The verse describes servants of the Entirely Merciful spending the night prostrating and standing before their Lord.

It does not contain tahajjud, but its subject of nighttime worship connects with that word’s use.

The verse also shows that nighttime worship may take the practical form of prostration and standing. Tahajjud should therefore not be restricted to mental alertness or reflection without any act of worship.

For a broader examination of prayer and its forms in the Quran, see the article “Salat in the Quran: Its Meaning, Establishment, and Practice.”

5. Comparison with related roots

Hā–jīm–dāl and hā–jīm–ʿayn: hujūd and hujūʿ

Both Arabic roots concern nighttime sleep, but their forms and ranges of usage are distinct.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17 reads:

كَانُوا قَلِيلًا مِنَ اللَّيْلِ مَا يَهْجَعُونَ

kānū qalīlan mina al-layli mā yahjaʿūn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17

“They used to sleep little of the night.”

Yahjaʿūn comes from hā–jīm–ʿayn and denotes sleeping.

The verse describes doers of good whose nighttime sleep is little. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:18 continues by describing their seeking forgiveness before dawn.

Hajaʿa and hajada are close in the sense of nighttime sleep, but semantic proximity does not make their roots or every usage identical.

The clear Quranic contrast is that yahjaʿūn refers to sleeping itself, while fa-tahajjad commands wakefulness for worship.

The contrast follows from the derivative’s form and context, not from an absolute rule opposing every derivative of the two families.

Hā–jīm–dāl and nūn–wāw–mīm: nighttime sleep and sleep generally

Nawm denotes sleep generally in the Quran and is not restricted to night.

Al-Rūm 30:23 presents human sleep by night and day as a divine sign.

The principal witnesses for hā–jīm–dāl, however, emphasize the nighttime setting.

Nawm is thus the broader word for sleep, while hujūd in the usage examined here is especially associated with sleeping at night.

This distinction should not be presented as ruling out every other possible Arabic use of hajada.

Hā–jīm–dāl and qāf–wāw–mīm: tahajjud and standing at night

Qiyām derives from qāf–wāw–mīm and fundamentally concerns rising and standing.

The command قُمِ اللَّيْلَ places that verb in a context of nighttime worship.

Tahajjud in Al-Isrāʾ likewise concerns wakefulness for worship.

Someone rising from sleep at night for prayer or Quran recitation may both have become awake and stand in worship.

This overlap does not make the roots identical. Qiyām has many other Quranic uses unrelated to sleep or night vigils.

Hā–jīm–dāl and jīm–hā–dāl

The roots contain similar letters, but their order differs.

Hā–jīm–dāl concerns sleep and nighttime wakefulness; jīm–hā–dāl concerns effort, exerting ability, and enduring hardship.

Tahajjud may require effort and overcoming the urge to sleep, but that difficulty does not make the roots lexically identical.

Jihād derives from jīm–hā–dāl and should not be assigned to hā–jīm–dāl merely because they share letters.

6. Challenges in understanding and translation

Does tahajjud simply mean night prayer?

“Night prayer” is a familiar name for nighttime worship, but a customary name for a practice must be distinguished from a word’s lexical meaning.

Quranic tahajjud concerns nighttime wakefulness for worship. Al-Isrāʾ 17:78, Al-Isrāʾ 17:79, and passages in Al-Muzzammil clarify its connection with prayer and Quran recitation.

“Night prayer” may therefore identify a familiar instance of tahajjud, without exhausting the word’s analysis.

In a base translation, “remain awake for worship” or, where appropriate, “keep vigil” more clearly expresses the word’s structure.

Is remaining awake without worship Quranic tahajjud?

Some Arabic uses of the family concern staying awake at night, but Al-Isrāʾ 17:79 is not about sleeplessness alone.

Someone may spend the entire night travelling, keeping guard, or working. Such a person has remained awake, but that fact alone does not fully exemplify the Quranic command فَتَهَجَّدْ بِهِ.

The context connects the wakefulness with the Quran and worship.

“Remain awake” is therefore a suitable basic rendering, but its devotional content must be retained in the contextual explanation.

Does tahajjud specify a precise time or number of prayer units?

The word itself states no number of prayer units, particular hour, or fixed duration.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:79 mentions only part of the night. Al-Muzzammil discusses different portions of the night and then human capacity.

Details of timing or prayer-unit counts should therefore not be presented as the root’s lexical meaning.

Nor should the distinction between worship after sleep and worship without prior sleep yield an entire system of binding rules without Quranic evidence.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Equivalents for the different derivatives must follow form and context.

Hajada in its sleep-related sense may be translated “slept.”

Hujūd in that branch means “sleep” or “nighttime sleep.”

Hājid means “asleep” in a sleep-related context, although some lexical uses also denote a nighttime worshipper.

Tahajjada in a devotional context means “kept vigil at night” or “rose from sleep for worship.”

For the Quranic fa-tahajjad, the following rendering is suitable:

“And during part of the night, keep vigil with it, as an addition for you; perhaps your Lord will raise you to a praised station.”

Here “keep vigil” conveys the command’s practical meaning, while “with it” preserves the connection with the object supplied by context.

“Rise for night prayer” is also an understandable interpretive explanation. In a direct translation, however, “keep vigil” avoids confining the word to a specific ritual form whose details the sentence itself does not state.

8. Conclusion

Early Arabic hā–jīm–dāl concerns sleep and nighttime wakefulness. Hujūd principally denotes sleeping, while some forms also concern remaining awake and worshipping at night.

In its familiar devotional usage, tahajjud means setting sleep aside or rising from it for worship. Worship after sleep is a clear instance, but Al-Isrāʾ 17:79 itself specifies neither the duration of prior sleep nor detailed timing and practices.

The root’s sole Quranic form is fa-tahajjad. The command follows discussion of prayer and Quran recitation and addresses Muhammad. Its contextual meaning is therefore wakefulness during part of the night for worship, clearly connected with the Quran.

Final definition for Forqan:

The principal branch of hā–jīm–dāl concerns sleeping, especially at night; its wakefulness branch concerns setting sleep aside or remaining awake at night. Quranic tahajjad commands nighttime wakefulness for worship, without the word itself specifying an exact time, duration, or complete form of worship.

This definition preserves the recognized sense of hujūd and explains tahajjud’s distinct usage, without substituting contextual implications or ritual details for lexical meaning.
