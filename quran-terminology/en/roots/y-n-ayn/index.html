Fruit ripening and maturity: the change from newly formed to ripe fruit and its Qur’anic use

In Arabic, Y N ʿ principally concerns fruit ripening: the stage at which fruit matures after forming and growing. Arabic expresses this condition through yanaʿa and aynaʿa, and descriptions such as yāniʿ and yanīʿ.

Two explanatory words are especially common in early Arabic: nuḍj and idrāk. The former concerns ripeness or maturity; the latter, in this setting, means reaching the fruit’s stage of maturity and full development.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as wa-yanʿihi in Al-Anʿām 6:99.

The word appears near the end of a description of rain, plant growth, grain, date-palm clusters, grapes, olives, and pomegranates:

انظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ

unẓurū ilā thamarihi idhā athmara wa-yanʿihi

“Look at its fruit when it bears fruit and at its ripening.”

This rendering agrees with Forqan’s installed translation of Al-Anʿām 6:99.

The crucial distinction is between athmara and yanʿihi. Bearing fruit and having ripe fruit are different stages. Fruit may already have formed while remaining unripe and undergoing further changes before maturity.

Root study must preserve this distinction. Yanʿ does not itself mean creating fruit, splitting a seed, necessarily becoming sweet, harvesting, or the end of a plant’s life, although some related phenomena may enter discussion of fruit ripening.

1. Important Arabic forms and derivatives

Yanaʿa yaynaʿu: fruit ripening

Yanaʿa describes fruit passing from an unripe condition into ripeness.

The following Arabic forms are attested:

يَنَعَ الثَّمَرُ يَيْنَعُ وَيَيْنِعُ يَنَعًا وَيُنْعًا وَيُنُوعًا

yanaʿa al-thamaru yaynaʿu wa-yayniʿu yanaʿan wa-yunʿan wa-yunūʿan

Their meaning is explained as:

أَدْرَكَ وَنَضِجَ

adraka wa-naḍija

That is, “It reached maturity and ripened.”

The fruit itself is the verb’s subject. Yanaʿa al-thamaru means the fruit ripened, rather than someone picking or harvesting it.

Both yaynaʿu and yayniʿu are attested imperfect forms. The root’s initial yāʾ remains in this construction. Their vowel difference should not be mistaken for a difference in the root’s meaning.

Aynaʿa yūniʿu: becoming ripe

Aynaʿa is Form IV and, in familiar usage, likewise denotes fruit ripening.

For example:

أَيْنَعَتِ الثَّمَرَةُ

That is, “The fruit ripened.”

Its verbal noun is īnāʿ.

Lexical descriptions regard aynaʿa as more frequent than yanaʿa.

Form IV does not itself establish that someone deliberately caused the fruit to ripen. In aynaʿa al-thamaru, the fruit itself reaches ripeness.

A morphological pattern alone must therefore not impose causation or direct intervention by an outside agent on the sentence.

Yanʿ, yunʿ, and yunūʿ: ripeness and maturity

Several verbal nouns are attested for the state or process of ripening.

Familiar forms include yanʿ with an initial a vowel, yunʿ with an initial u vowel, and yunūʿ.

In fruit contexts, they denote ripeness and maturity.

Under the familiar analysis, Qur’anic wa-yanʿihi belongs to this verbal-noun family.

Yāniʿ, yanīʿ, and mūniʿ: ripe fruit

Yāniʿ describes fruit that has ripened.

Yanīʿ has the same meaning. The relationship between yāniʿ and yanīʿ can be compared with nāḍij and naḍīj.

Mūniʿ is another description in the family, attested alongside aynaʿa.

For example, rummānun yanīʿun denotes ripe pomegranates.

Some lexical analyses also treat yanʿ as a plural of yāniʿ, comparable to ṣāḥib and ṣaḥb.

This analysis must remain distinct from taking yanʿ as a verbal noun. The difference matters when examining the Qur’anic word.

Of these verbs, verbal nouns, and descriptions, only yanʿ with a conjunction and pronoun occurs in the Qur’an.

2. Does Y N ʿ concern only fruit ripening?

Fruit ripening is this family’s principal field, but its uses are not confined to fruit. Other attested extensions help clarify the root’s semantic limits.

Redness and changes of color

In the color-related use, the following is attested:

دَمٌ يَانِعٌ: شَدِيدُ الْحُمْرَةِ

damun yāniʿun: shadīdu al-ḥumrati

That is, “intensely red blood.”

Yanaʿa al-shayʾu is also attested for something whose color has become a deep red.

Yāniʿ is also attested for redness, and yunūʿ for the redness of blood.

This can be compared with the color changes of many ripening fruits. Dates and pomegranates, for example, can change color noticeably during growth or maturation.

The relationship must not be extended beyond the evidence.

Ripening does not always mean becoming red. Some fruit becomes yellow, remains green, or takes another color when ripe. Even a ripe fruit’s color depends on its type and variety.

Although yāniʿ can mean deeply red in some Arabic uses, redness cannot be the exclusive definition of Y N ʿ.

Yanaʿah: a red bead or stone

Early usage attests yanaʿah for a red bead and also a kind of agate.

Al-yanaʿ is likewise attested as a name for a kind of agate.

Fruit is no longer the subject here; the word names an object of a particular color.

A relationship with redness is understandable, but the precise history of the name is unclear. Resemblance to ripe fruit may have contributed, yet this possibility must not become an established fact.

Ripeness in figurative use

Īnāʿ also has an attested use concerning cooked food.

For example, thoroughly cooked and ready food can be described figuratively with aynaʿa.

The common feature is clear: fruit reaches maturity through growth, while food becomes ready through cooking.

Natural fruit ripening and cooking food with heat are different processes. In some uses, language describes them with related expressions.

Persian pokhtegī in translating this root must therefore be understood as natural fruit maturity, rather than cooking with fire.

Reaching the stage of picking

A sermon attributed to al-Ḥajjāj ibn Yūsuf includes:

إِنِّي لَأَرَى رُؤُوسًا قَدْ أَيْنَعَتْ وَحَانَ قِطَافُهَا

innī la-arā ruʾūsan qad aynaʿat wa-ḥāna qiṭāfuhā

“I see heads that have ripened, and their time of picking has come.”

The threatening expression compares human heads to fruit ready to be picked.

It contains two distinct expressions: aynaʿat for ripening and ḥāna qiṭāfuhā for the arrival of picking time.

Ripening and picking can be closely associated, but they are different actions.

Yanʿihi in Al-Anʿām 6:99 therefore cannot become harvesting without contextual evidence.

3. Evidence from early Arabic poetry

Ripe olives in a verse of disputed attribution

One familiar witness reads:

فِي قِبَابٍ حَوْلَ دَسْكَرَةٍ
حَوْلَهَا الزَّيْتُونُ قَدْ يَنَعَا

fī qibābin ḥawla daskarah / ḥawlahā al-zaytūnu qad yanaʿā

“In pavilions or buildings around a settlement, with its surrounding olives already ripe.”

Yanaʿā is a verbal form expressing the olives’ ripening.

The verb directly concerns olives, rather than an object’s redness or an ethical concept.

The attribution is disputed: the verse has been assigned to al-Aḥwaṣ, Yazīd ibn Muʿāwiyah, or ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Ḥassān, and other attributions are also reported.

It should not be confidently assigned to one poet. Nor does the available evidence justify confidently identifying it as pre-Islamic poetry.

Semantically, it attests yanaʿa for fruit ripening, while the precise date and original speaker remain unclear.

Ripe pomegranates in poetry attributed to ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib

A verse attributed to ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib reads:

كَأَنَّ عَلَى عَوَارِضِهِنَّ رَاحًا
يُفَضُّ عَلَيْهِ رُمَّانٌ يَنِيعٌ

ka-anna ʿalā ʿawāriḍihinna rāḥan / yufaḍḍu ʿalayhi rummānun yanīʿun

“As though on their side teeth there were wine over which ripe pomegranates are broken open.”

The image uses rummānun yanīʿun, ripe pomegranates.

Yanīʿ qualifies pomegranates and is close in meaning to yāniʿ.

The verse illustrates the formal difference and semantic proximity of yanīʿ and yāniʿ.

ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. Without dating this particular verse, however, it cannot be treated as certain evidence preceding Qur’anic revelation.

Color in poetry attributed to Suwayd ibn Kurāʿ

This verse is attributed to Suwayd ibn Kurāʿ:

وَأَبْلَجَ مُخْتَالٍ صَبَغْنَا ثِيَابَهُ
بِأَحْمَرَ مِثْلِ الْأُرْجُوَانِيِّ يَانِعِ

wa-ablaja mukhtālin ṣabaghnā thiyābahu / bi-aḥmara mithli al-urjuwāniyyi yāniʿi

“And a bright-browed, swaggering man whose clothes we dyed with a deep red like crimson.”

The image concerns a deep red color appearing on clothing.

Yāniʿ describes the intensity of redness here, rather than fruit.

The witness establishes a literary Arabic use concerning color.

It does not, however, make Qur’anic yanʿ mean fruit becoming red. Al-Anʿām 6:99 concerns fruit and its ripening, with no explicit evidence restricting the meaning to redness.

What the witnesses establish

The examined witnesses illustrate three uses: fruit ripening, describing ripe fruit, and extending the description to intense redness.

No pre-Islamic poetic witness with sufficiently secure attribution and wording has been established here to reconstruct the root’s earliest history with certainty.

Arabic and Qur’anic usage establish the principal meaning of fruit ripening. The precise history of its extension to color and human imagery requires caution.

4. Qur’anic wa-yanʿihi in Al-Anʿām 6:99

Al-Anʿām 6:99

وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ نَبَاتَ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ فَأَخْرَجْنَا مِنْهُ خَضِرًا نُّخْرِجُ مِنْهُ حَبًّا مُّتَرَاكِبًا وَمِنَ النَّخْلِ مِن طَلْعِهَا قِنْوَانٌ دَانِيَةٌ وَجَنَّاتٍ مِّنْ أَعْنَابٍ وَالزَّيْتُونَ وَالرُّمَّانَ مُشْتَبِهًا وَغَيْرَ مُتَشَابِهٍ ۗ انظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكُمْ لَآيَاتٍ لِّقَوْمٍ يُؤْمِنُونَ

wa-huwa alladhī anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhrajnā bihi nabāta kulli shayʾin fa-akhrajnā minhu khaḍiran nukhriju minhu ḥabban mutarākiban wa-mina al-nakhli min ṭalʿihā qinwānun dāniyatun wa-jannātin min aʿnābin wa-al-zaytūna wa-al-rummāna mushtabihan wa-ghayra mutashābihin unẓurū ilā thamarihi idhā athmara wa-yanʿihi inna fī dhālikum la-āyātin li-qawmin yuʾminūna

Forqan’s translation:

“He is the One who sent down water from the sky. With it We brought forth the growth of everything; from it We brought forth greenery from which We produce clustered grain. From the date palm, from its spathes, come low-hanging clusters; and gardens of grapes, olives, and pomegranates, alike and unlike. Look at its fruit when it bears fruit and at its ripening. In these are signs for people who believe.”

The morphology and syntax of wa-yanʿihi

The form has three components: wa, the conjunction; yanʿi, a noun concerning fruit ripeness; and hi, an attached pronoun.

In the familiar reading, yanʿ has an initial a vowel and a vowelless nūn.

Under the verbal-noun analysis, it denotes ripeness and maturity. Yanʿihi is genitive and coordinated with the preceding thamarihi.

The construction is therefore:

انْظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ ... وَيَنْعِهِ

That is, “Look at its fruit ... and its ripening.”

Unẓurū commands looking, while yanʿ names the state or process of fruit ripening.

Wa-yanʿihi is not an imperfect verb. It should not be translated as “and it ripens” with an independent subject and tense. In the familiar analysis, it is a noun related to the fruit.

A lexical difference: verbal noun or plural of yāniʿ?

Two early analyses of the word are proposed.

The first takes yanʿ as a verbal noun, comparable to nuḍj for ripeness or maturity.

This yields a natural meaning: look at the fruit when it forms and at its ripening.

Another analysis takes yanʿ as a plural of yāniʿ, comparable to tājir and tajr or ṣāḥib and ṣaḥb.

The emphasis then falls on ripe fruits or the fruit’s ripe condition.

Both analyses belong to the same semantic field, but differ morphologically. The first names the process of ripening; the second examines a plural description of ripe fruit.

“Its ripening” is an appropriate translation, fitting both the coordination and the broadly shared meaning.

Reported reading variants

Two other forms are reported alongside wa-yanʿihi.

Wa-yunʿihi, with an initial u vowel, is attributed to Ibn Muḥayṣin, Ibn Abī Isḥāq, and other readers.

Wa-yāniʿihi is attributed to Muḥammad ibn Sumayqiʿ and, in other reports, to additional readers.

The initial u vowel is attested among some Arabic speakers of Najd.

Explanations of these forms concern ripeness and maturity.

Despite differences of structure and vocalization, the variants do not produce unrelated meanings. All concern fruit ripeness.

The reported wa-yāniʿihi does not, however, justify treating familiar wa-yanʿihi as the same singular descriptive form without explanation.

5. The difference between athmara and yanʿihi

This is the most significant semantic distinction in the root’s Qur’anic use.

In the expression:

انظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ

unẓurū ilā thamarihi idhā athmara wa-yanʿihi

Two stages or conditions of fruit receive attention.

Athmara belongs to TH M R and concerns bearing fruit or its formation.

Yanʿ, however, denotes fruit ripening and maturity.

A pomegranate tree can, for example, have small unripe pomegranates after flowering. The fruit has formed, but has not yet ripened.

Over time it grows and, according to its type and conditions, reaches maturity.

The interval between formation and ripeness is observable, and the verse draws attention to it.

The expression should not be treated as a scientific list or botanical classification of every stage of fruit development.

The verse names two salient conditions and invites the audience to look.

Does ripening mean becoming sweet?

Not necessarily.

Many fruits become sweeter as they ripen, but yanʿ does not specifically mean an increase in sugar.

Some ripe fruit remains sour, bitter, or otherwise distinctive in flavor.

Yanʿihi should therefore not be translated as “its becoming sweet.”

Does ripening mean becoming red?

Redness is an attested use of yāniʿ, but it must not be generalized to every fruit.

Ripening can involve color changes, but the resulting color is not the same for every fruit.

Does ripening mean becoming ready for picking?

They are often closely associated, but are not identical meanings.

The appropriate harvest time can vary with the crop, its intended use, and the desired stage of maturity.

Some fruit is harvested before becoming fully ripe on the tree and undergoes further changes after picking.

Lexically, qaṭf and yanʿ are also distinct concepts.

Ripening is therefore the more precise equivalent of yanʿ; harvesting requires another word or contextual indication.

6. Why does the Qur’an invite looking at fruit and its ripening?

Al-Anʿām 6:99 begins with water descending from the sky and the emergence of vegetation.

Green growth, layered grain, date-palm clusters, vineyards, olives, and pomegranates follow.

The command unẓurū then appears.

This order invites more than viewing fruit as an isolated object. It draws attention to its formation and ripening within the varied processes of nature.

The verse concludes:

إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكُمْ لَآيَاتٍ لِّقَوْمٍ يُؤْمِنُونَ

inna fī dhālikum la-āyātin li-qawmin yuʾminūna

“In these are signs for people who believe.”

Within the Qur’anic context, changes in plants and fruit are signs inviting reflection and understanding.

Yanʿ itself still means fruit ripening. Knowing The God, attending to creation, and recognizing signs emerge from the whole verse, rather than this word alone.

Relationship to Al-Anʿām 6:95

Al-Anʿām 6:95 reads:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ

inna allāha fāliqu al-ḥabbi wa-al-nawā

“The God is the splitter of grain and fruit stones.”

That verse concerns grain and fruit stones. Al-Anʿām 6:99 describes plant growth, fruit formation, and ripening.

The verses can be considered together as part of the Qur’an’s attention to the emergence and continuation of plant life.

Fāliq, ḥabb, nawā, and yanʿ are not four identical lexical concepts. Each describes a different stage or aspect of the whole.

Relationship to Al-Naḥl 16:11

Al-Naḥl 16:11 reads:

يُنْبِتُ لَكُم بِهِ الزَّرْعَ وَالزَّيْتُونَ وَالنَّخِيلَ وَالْأَعْنَابَ وَمِن كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ

yunbitu lakum bihi al-zarʿa wa-al-zaytūna wa-al-nakhīla wa-al-aʿnāba wa-min kulli al-thamarāti

“With it He grows for you crops, olives, date palms, grapes, and every kind of fruit.”

Its naming of plants and fruits is close to Al-Anʿām 6:99.

Yanʿ does not occur in it, however.

In Al-Naḥl, yunbitu concerns causing growth; in Al-Anʿām, yanʿ concerns fruit ripening.

Not all plant-related words should therefore be merged into a vague definition of growth.

Relationship to Al-Anʿām 6:141

Al-Anʿām 6:141 reads:

وَآتُوا حَقَّهُ يَوْمَ حَصَادِهِ

wa-ātū ḥaqqahu yawma ḥaṣādihi

“Give its due on the day of its harvest.”

Ḥaṣād concerns harvesting here, rather than the fruit’s own ripening.

Reading it alongside Al-Anʿām 6:99 clarifies two matters: crop maturity and the time when the right associated with harvesting becomes relevant.

They can be closely related in agriculture while retaining distinct lexical meanings.

7. Distinctions from related roots

Y N ʿ and N Ḍ J: ripeness and maturity

Naḍija can describe ripeness and maturity in Arabic. It can concern fruit or other contexts, such as cooking through heat.

Yanʿ is often explained through nuḍj.

Its Qur’anic use, however, specifically concerns fruit.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:56, naḍijat julūduhum describes skins in fire. It concerns the effect of heat, rather than fruit ripening.

Semantic overlap does not make every use of the roots identical.

Study of N Ḍ J

Y N ʿ and TH M R: fruit formation and ripening

TH M R concerns fruit and fruit bearing.

Y N ʿ describes fruit ripeness.

Al-Anʿām 6:99 directly illustrates the distinction, placing both roots within one expression.

Bearing fruit is not sufficient for the fruit to be ripe.

Study of TH M R

Y N ʿ and R Ṭ B: ripeness and moisture

Raṭb concerns moisture and freshness. In date contexts, ruṭab also identifies a familiar stage of ripeness.

Maryam 19:25 reads:

تُسَاقِطْ عَلَيْكِ رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا

tusāqiṭ ʿalayki ruṭaban janiyyan

“It will drop fresh, ripe dates upon you.”

The verse concerns dates and their condition.

Yanʿ is not confined to dates; Arabic uses it for olives, pomegranates, and other fruit too.

Nor does every ripening fruit reach a condition equivalent to ruṭab dates.

Yanʿ therefore cannot always be translated as becoming ruṭab.

Y N ʿ and Q Ṭ F: ripening and picking

Qaṭf concerns picking fruit.

Yanʿ concerns its ripening.

The sermon attributed to al-Ḥajjāj illustrates the difference: the fruit is first described as ripe, and its time of picking is then mentioned.

Ripeness can prepare for picking, but ripening is not itself picking.

Y N ʿ and N ʿ Y

Naʿy belongs to the independent root N ʿ Y and concerns reporting or announcing a death.

Although the consonants are similar, their order and semantic fields differ.

Yanʿ in Al-Anʿām 6:99 concerns fruit ripeness and must not be confused with announcing death or conveying news.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence, the principal equivalent is ripening or ripeness.

In the expression:

انظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ

unẓurū ilā thamarihi idhā athmara wa-yanʿihi

Forqan’s existing rendering is appropriate:

“Look at its fruit when it bears fruit and at its ripening.”

It preserves the word’s direct meaning and distinguishes fruit bearing from ripening.

9. Conclusion

In early Arabic, Y N ʿ is principally associated with fruit ripening and maturity.

Yanaʿa and aynaʿa both describe fruit ripening. Yāniʿ, yanīʿ, and mūniʿ describe ripe fruit.

Extended uses concern intense redness, especially blood, and red objects such as beads and agate. Redness is nevertheless not a necessary property of every ripe fruit.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as wa-yanʿihi in Al-Anʿām 6:99. It is commonly analyzed as a verbal noun, although some lexical analyses take it as a plural of yāniʿ.

Other reported readings include wa-yunʿihi and wa-yāniʿihi. All concern fruit ripeness and maturity.

The main Qur’anic distinction is between athmara, bearing fruit, and yanʿihi, the fruit’s ripening after formation.

Al-Anʿām 6:99 places these stages alongside rain, plant growth, grain, and different kinds of fruit, inviting people to look at them.

This reflective message must not be transferred into the root’s lexical definition.

Proposed final definition for Forqan:

Y N ʿ denotes fruit ripening and maturity after formation and growth. Yanaʿa and aynaʿa describe ripening, while yāniʿ and yanīʿ describe ripe fruit. Intense-redness uses are also attested, but redness is not essential to every derivative. In Al-Anʿām 6:99, wa-yanʿihi means “and its ripening,” presented alongside and distinguished from fruit bearing.
