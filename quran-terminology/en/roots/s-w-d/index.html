Blackness and darkening, sawād and siyādah, mastery and leadership

The Arabic root S W D has two prominent branches: blackness and darkening in color, and mastery, leadership, and social prominence. Lexical sources also record sawād for a body or silhouette and a mass of people, and siwād for quiet, private conversation.

The root occurs ten times in the Quran. Seven instances concern blackness or darkening; the remaining three, in forms of sayyid and sādah, concern mastery and leadership.

The distinction matters. Blackness is a color characteristic found in nature, objects, and human bodies. Mastery, however, concerns social relationships and a person's standing among others.

In the Quran, blackness is sometimes a natural color, as in night and portions of mountains. Elsewhere a darkening face expresses an unhappy condition or the outcome of deeds. Sayyid likewise appears as a praiseworthy description of a prophet, a designation for a husband with household standing, and a name for leaders who misled their followers.

Thus blackness alone does not signify evil, nor does the title sayyid alone establish truth or virtue. Lexical meaning must remain distinct from the value and outcome associated with it in each context.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Sawād: blackness in contrast to whiteness

In early Arabic, sawād denotes blackness and dark coloring, in contrast to bayāḍ, whiteness.

Aswad describes something black; sawdāʾ is its feminine form.

Sūd and sūdān are recorded plurals of this adjective.

Iswadda means to become black or dark; sawwada, in its color use, means to blacken something or make it black.

This meaning applies to various things and phenomena: black hair, dark night, dark stone, and ground darker than its surroundings.

Lexical usage sometimes extends sawād beyond strict blackness to nearby dark colors. Dense trees and palms, for example, may be described by sawād because their concentrated greenery looks dark from a distance.

This extension does not mean that every color other than white is called black in every Arabic usage.

Blackness is this branch's established core; extension to other dark colors requires usage evidence and contextual support.

Pre-Islamic poetry: blackness as beauty

Imruʾ al-Qays, a pre-Islamic poet, describes his beloved's hair in his famous Muʿallaqah:

وَفَرْعٍ يَزِينُ الْمَتْنَ أَسْوَدَ فَاحِمٍ
أَثِيثٍ كَقِنْوِ النَّخْلَةِ الْمُتَعَثْكِلِ

wa-farʿin yazīnu al-matna aswada fāḥimin / athīthin ka-qinwi al-nakhlati al-mutaʿathkili

“And hair adorning her back, black, coal-dark and abundant, like the branching cluster of a date palm.”

Aswada describes the hair's color; fāḥim intensifies its blackness.

Athīth describes its thickness and abundance.

The poet presents black hair as one of his beloved's beautiful features.

This evidence matters because blackness is not inherently blameworthy in early poetic language and culture. A color used elsewhere in images of sadness or gloom here describes beauty.

The line is transmitted in the Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and its recognized commentaries. Some elements of the comparison have variant readings, but aswad and its color meaning are clear.

Color usage is thus well supported in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, although an individual line's date cannot independently be established apart from the poem's transmission and compilation history.

Siyādah: mastery and social standing

Another major branch consists of sāda yasūdu and the noun sayyid.

Sāda qawmahu means that someone attained leadership among his people or became their chief.

Sayyid describes a person of prominence, influence, mastery, or leadership among others.

Siyādah, suʾdud, and saydūdah are also recorded for mastery, honor, distinction, and prominent social standing.

In early usage, a sayyid might be a tribal chief, influential person, distinguished individual, or someone others approach because of his standing.

Some uses associate the sayyid with forbearance, generosity, liberality, and bearing people's burdens.

These qualities should not be made necessary components of every usage. Someone may be recognized as a sayyid through power, lineage, or social position without possessing every moral virtue.

Early poetic evidence: sayyid and social status

Al-Aʿshā, the well-known poet of the late pre-Islamic period, praises a prominent man:

وَإِنْ فَحَصَ النَّاسُ عَنْ سَيِّدٍ
فَسَيِّدُكُمْ عَنْهُ لَا يُفْحَصُ

wa-in faḥaṣa al-nāsu ʿan sayyidin / fa-sayyidukum ʿanhu lā yufḥaṣu

“If people search for a chief, your chief needs no searching out.”

Sayyid here denotes someone the poet considers worthy of leadership and distinction.

The word does not concern skin color or blackness. It concerns the person's standing among others and the esteem the poet accords him.

This line occurs in a poem attributed to al-Aʿshā that begins Aʿalqam qad ṣayyartani al-umūr.

Al-Aʿshā belongs to the leading poets of the late pre-Islamic period and lived near the emergence of Islam. The poem's precise date is not independently established, however. It supports early usage of sayyid for distinction and leadership, but cannot establish the title's exact historical origin.

Together, the examples from Imruʾ al-Qays and al-Aʿshā show that both blackness and mastery occur in the early poetic tradition.

How these branches became historically related is a separate question.

2. The historical relationship between sawād and siyādah

One early lexical analysis treats blackness as the family's common foundation and relates other meanings to it.

In this account, sawād can denote not only black coloring but a body's form or silhouette, especially when seen as a dark shape at a distance.

Sawādu al-qawm is also established for a community or mass of people.

Sawādu al-nās can denote the general populace; al-sawādu al-aʿẓam is used for a very large body of people or the majority.

One explanation relates mastery to this usage by describing the sayyid as someone people approach, gather around, and rely upon personally.

People gathering around an individual is thus proposed as a semantic connection between sawād and siyādah.

The explanation is intelligible linguistically and socially, but does not prove that sayyid historically arose from blackness or a human silhouette.

Another explanation associates mastery with distinction, elevated standing, and forbearance.

Such analyses show lexicographers attempting to connect the family's branches; none should be treated as their certain historical origin without independent evidence.

For Quranic study, the essential point is that blackness and mastery are distinct, actual senses within sentences.

Aswad can describe color, and sayyid social standing.

Neither meaning can be inferred from the other without a contextual indication.

Blackness, bodily form, and private conversation

Arabic sources also record sawādu al-shayʾ for the body or form of something.

Sāwada and siwād are also used for speaking quietly and privately.

One explanation observes that people draw their bodies close when speaking confidentially; one person's sawād approaching the other's thus provides an image for private conversation.

This offers an explanatory connection between bodily form and personal proximity.

Musāwadah in the sense of private conversation does not occur in the Quran and should not be imposed upon its color or leadership usages.

Sawād is also recorded for large groups of people, masses of trees and palms, and, in later usage, cultivated and agricultural districts.

These meanings demonstrate the family's breadth, rather than proving that all its Quranic derivatives have one identical sense.

3. Quranic forms

S W D occurs ten times in nine Quranic verses.

The occurrences fall into two main branches.

Forms concerning color and darkening

Aswad: an adjective meaning black, in Al-Baqarah 2:187.

Sūd: plural of aswad, meaning black, in Fāṭir 35:27.

Taswaddu: a Form IX imperfect verb meaning to become black or dark, in Āl ʿImrān 3:106.

Iswaddat: a past-tense verb of the same form, meaning became black, in Āl ʿImrān 3:106.

Muswaddan: an active participle of iswadda describing something that has become black or dark, in Al-Naḥl 16:58 and Al-Zukhruf 43:17.

Muswaddah: the feminine form of that participle, in Al-Zumar 39:60.

Iswadda belongs to Form IX, ifʿilāl, a pattern used notably for colors and certain bodily characteristics.

Here the verb expresses becoming black or taking on a dark color.

The morphological pattern alone does not express fear, shame, sin, or an unhappy outcome. Those associations are determined by each verse's context.

Forms concerning mastery and leadership

Sayyidan: a chief or distinguished person, in Āl ʿImrān 3:39.

Sayyidahā: her master, contextually her husband, in Yūsuf 12:25.

Sādatanā: our masters or leaders, in Al-Aḥzāb 33:67.

Morphological analysis connects sayyid with sāda yasūdu.

Grammatical sources discuss differing analyses of its underlying structure and precise pattern, including accounts of wāw changing to yāʾ and consonant assimilation.

Sādah is a recognized plural of sayyid.

Quranic sādatanā combines that plural with the first-person plural pronoun.

Forms such as sawād, siyādah, suʾdud, sawwada, sāʾid, and sayyidah are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Quran in those forms.

The title sayyid applies to human beings in the Quran. None of its three occurrences names The God; later theological usages should not make it an exclusively divine title within the Quranic text.

4. Blackness in nature and the perceptible world

The black thread and the white thread

Al-Baqarah 2:187 states:

وَكُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَكُمُ الْخَيْطُ الْأَبْيَضُ مِنَ الْخَيْطِ الْأَسْوَدِ مِنَ الْفَجْرِ

wa-kulū wa-ishrabū ḥattā yatabayyana lakumu al-khayṭu al-abyaḍu mina al-khayṭi al-aswadi mina al-fajr

Al-Baqarah 2:187

“Eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct to you from the black thread.”

Aswad here qualifies al-khayṭ, the thread.

Abyaḍ belongs to B Y Ḍ and means white.

Two colors thus stand in contrast: white and black.

The passage is not comparing two actual threads of different colors.

Mina al-fajr relates the white and black thread image to dawn becoming apparent and its light becoming distinguishable from night.

Al-khayṭ al-abyaḍ represents emerging dawn light; al-khayṭ al-aswad relates to the darkness remaining before daylight appears.

Blackness here forms part of a natural image of night and dawn.

It carries no negative moral judgment and does not determine anyone's destiny.

Night and darkness are natural phenomena; their recognizable contrast with light is used to mark time.

A fuller discussion of this temporal sign in fasting belongs to the separate reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:187.

Blackness in mountain formations

Fāṭir 35:27 states:

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ ثَمَرَاتٍ مُخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهَا وَمِنَ الْجِبَالِ جُدَدٌ بِيضٌ وَحُمْرٌ مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهَا وَغَرَابِيبُ سُودٌ

a-lam tara anna allāha anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhrajnā bihi thamarātin mukhtalifan alwānuhā wa-mina al-jibāli judadun bīḍun wa-ḥumrun mukhtalifun alwānuhā wa-gharābību sūd

Fāṭir 35:27

“Have you not seen that The God sent down water from the sky, and with it We brought forth fruits of differing colors? And in the mountains are white and red streaks of differing colors, and intensely black portions.”

Sūd is the plural of aswad.

Gharābīb is the plural of ghirbīb and, when describing color, expresses intense blackness.

Aswadu ghirbīb is an Arabic expression for very deep black.

Gharābību sūd has received several grammatical analyses, including treating sūd as explanatory or appositional to gharābīb.

Its semantic result is clear: the verse speaks of mountain portions with an intensely black color.

Judad denotes streaks or colored paths in mountains and belongs to J D D, not S W D.

Blackness is one of the earth's natural colors here.

White and red appear alongside it.

Does blackness carry a value judgment here?

Fāṭir 35:27 discusses the varied colors of fruits and mountains.

The following verse, Fāṭir 35:28, also mentions diversity of color among people, animals, and livestock.

This continuity presents created diversity of color as something to observe and understand.

None of these colors is identified in itself as a sign of moral superiority or inferiority.

Fāṭir 35:28 connects the discussion with knowledgeable servants' reverent fear of The God: recognizing creation's signs can lead to a deeper awareness of the Lord.

That does not make a stone's blackness, a mountain streak's whiteness, or human bodily color a measure of moral worth.

No human hierarchy based on color can therefore be derived from Fāṭir 35:27.

Sūd has its straightforward meaning here: black.

5. Darkening faces: human condition and the outcome of deeds

A prominent Quranic use of this root concerns a human face becoming dark or black.

The face can manifest a person's condition in Quranic language. Joy, anxiety, grief, fear, shame, and other states may become visible there.

A face becoming black in these usages is not identical to naturally black skin.

Several verses clarify the distinction.

A darkening face at news of a daughter's birth

Al-Naḥl 16:58 states:

وَإِذَا بُشِّرَ أَحَدُهُمْ بِالْأُنْثَىٰ ظَلَّ وَجْهُهُ مُسْوَدًّا وَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ

wa-idhā bushshira aḥaduhum bi-al-unthā ẓalla wajhuhu muswaddan wa-huwa kaẓīm

Al-Naḥl 16:58

“When one of them is given glad tidings of a female, his face darkens while he suppresses his anger and grief.”

Muswaddan describes the face's condition here.

Kaẓīm concerns suppressing an intense feeling, such as anger or grief.

The darkening face accompanies the person's unhappy response to news of a daughter's birth.

In Al-Naḥl 16:59, the Quran describes him hiding from people and deliberating whether to keep her in humiliation or conceal her in the earth.

This context clarifies the grounds of criticism.

The Quran does not condemn a naturally black face; it criticizes the behavior and outlook that make someone consider a daughter's birth a source of shame and anger.

The darkening face indicates his inner condition here.

The recurring image in Al-Zukhruf

Al-Zukhruf 43:17 likewise states:

وَإِذَا بُشِّرَ أَحَدُهُمْ بِمَا ضَرَبَ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ مَثَلًا ظَلَّ وَجْهُهُ مُسْوَدًّا وَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ

wa-idhā bushshira aḥaduhum bi-mā ḍaraba li-al-raḥmāni mathalan ẓalla wajhuhu muswaddan wa-huwa kaẓīm

Al-Zukhruf 43:17

“When one of them is given glad tidings of what he has attributed as a likeness to the Entirely Merciful, his face darkens while he suppresses his anger.”

The passage criticizes people who attribute to The God something they themselves dislike in their own lives.

The surrounding verses address their attribution of female offspring to The God and the inconsistency of their conduct.

They make such attributions to The God, yet feel shame and anger when the same thing comes to them.

The darkening face again concerns personal condition and behavior.

Muswaddan has the same meaning in these two passages, while the object and direction of criticism emerge from their contexts.

Both describe a social and moral reaction connected with disparaging daughters and inconsistency between belief and behavior.

Faces becoming black on the day of accounting

Āl ʿImrān 3:106 states:

يَوْمَ تَبْيَضُّ وُجُوهٌ وَتَسْوَدُّ وُجُوهٌ فَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ اسْوَدَّتْ وُجُوهُهُمْ أَكَفَرْتُمْ بَعْدَ إِيمَانِكُمْ فَذُوقُوا الْعَذَابَ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَكْفُرُونَ

yawma tabyaḍḍu wujūhun wa-taswaddu wujūhun fa-ammā alladhīna iswaddat wujūhuhum a-kafartum baʿda īmānikum fa-dhūqū al-ʿadhāba bi-mā kuntum takfurūn

Āl ʿImrān 3:106

“On a day when some faces become white and others become black. Those whose faces have become black will be asked: Did you disbelieve after your faith? Then taste the punishment for your disbelief.”

Two forms of the root occur here:

Taswaddu: become black.

Iswaddat: became black.

Tabyaḍḍu, from B Y Ḍ, expresses becoming white in the same sentence.

The passage thus contrasts two groups of faces.

Āl ʿImrān 3:107 then describes those whose faces have become white as being in The God's mercy.

By contrast, Āl ʿImrān 3:106 concerns those who disbelieved after faith and face punishment.

Faces becoming black or white here are related to the outcome of people's deeds.

Is this about natural human skin color?

No; the context does not support that meaning.

The Quran does not say that people born with black skin are condemned and people with light skin receive mercy.

The subject is faces changing on the day of accounting.

Tabyaḍḍu, taswaddu, and iswaddat express that change of condition.

The passage is therefore not about natural, inherited skin color.

Nor should one claim without evidence that the change is necessarily only a literary metaphor with no actual manifestation on faces.

The verse reports faces becoming white and black. The precise nature of that transformation on the day of accounting exceeds what the verb's lexical meaning determines.

What is clear is its relation to people's outcome, rather than their worldly race, ethnicity, lineage, or skin color.

Dark faces and falsely attributing things to The God

Al-Zumar 39:60 states:

وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ تَرَى الَّذِينَ كَذَبُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ وُجُوهُهُمْ مُسْوَدَّةٌ أَلَيْسَ فِي جَهَنَّمَ مَثْوًى لِلْمُتَكَبِّرِينَ

wa-yawma al-qiyāmati tarā alladhīna kadhabū ʿalā allāhi wujūhuhum muswaddatun a-laysa fī jahannama mathwan li-al-mutakabbirīn

Al-Zumar 39:60

“On the Day of Resurrection you will see those who lied about The God with their faces blackened. Is there not a dwelling in Hell for the arrogant?”

Muswaddah is the predicate of wujūhuhum and describes the condition of their faces.

The subject is people who lied about The God.

The verse also mentions the arrogant at its close.

Blackening faces here are thus connected with lying about The God and arrogance.

This matters particularly in Forqan's research, since attributing a statement, ruling, or authority to The God is a serious matter.

Anyone presenting a claim in The God's name is responsible for explaining its supporting evidence.

The verse should not, however, be extended without justification to mean that everyone holding an erroneous interpretation has knowingly lied about The God.

Mistaken understanding, ignorance, imitation, and knowingly attributing a falsehood to The God must be distinguished.

Muswaddah in all cases describes faces that have become dark or black; the cause of that condition is supplied by the verse's account of their behavior.

The broader relationship between the face and the manifestation of a person's condition should be examined separately under W J H.

6. Mastery and leadership in the Quran

The root's other three occurrences concern a person's standing among others, rather than color.

They show that sayyid can carry different contextual senses and evaluations.

Wa-sayyidan: Yaḥyā and praiseworthy standing

Āl ʿImrān 3:39 states:

فَنَادَتْهُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَهُوَ قَائِمٌ يُصَلِّي فِي الْمِحْرَابِ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يُبَشِّرُكَ بِيَحْيَىٰ مُصَدِّقًا بِكَلِمَةٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَسَيِّدًا وَحَصُورًا وَنَبِيًّا مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

fa-nādat-hu al-malāʾikatu wa-huwa qāʾimun yuṣallī fī al-miḥrābi anna allāha yubashshiruka bi-yaḥyā muṣaddiqan bi-kalimatin mina allāhi wa-sayyidan wa-ḥaṣūran wa-nabiyyan mina al-ṣāliḥīn

Āl ʿImrān 3:39

“The angels called to him while he stood performing ṣalāh in the sanctuary: The God gives you glad tidings of Yaḥyā, confirming a word from The God, a distinguished leader, restrained, and a prophet among the righteous.”

Sayyidan is one of Yaḥyā's descriptions here.

It accompanies confirming a word from The God, being ḥaṣūr, and being a prophet among the righteous.

Yaḥyā's distinction is therefore presented in a praiseworthy context.

Lexically, sayyid can denote an esteemed person, leader, notable, or person of standing.

“Leader” or “a person of distinguished standing” is suitable here.

The word alone does not establish that Yaḥyā possessed political kingship, a governmental territory, or sovereignty over everyone.

Those claims require independent evidence.

Here mastery concerns Yaḥyā's praiseworthy character and position.

Nor does calling Yaḥyā sayyid mean that everyone later bearing the title possessed the same spiritual standing.

The title's value here is understood from the person and his accompanying descriptions.

Sayyidahā: the woman's husband in the story of Yūsuf

Yūsuf 12:25 states:

وَاسْتَبَقَا الْبَابَ وَقَدَّتْ قَمِيصَهُ مِنْ دُبُرٍ وَأَلْفَيَا سَيِّدَهَا لَدَى الْبَابِ قَالَتْ مَا جَزَاءُ مَنْ أَرَادَ بِأَهْلِكَ سُوءًا إِلَّا أَنْ يُسْجَنَ أَوْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

wa-istabaqā al-bāba wa-qaddat qamīṣahu min duburin wa-alfayā sayyidahā ladā al-bābi qālat mā jazāʾu man arāda bi-ahlika sūʾan illā an yusjana aw ʿadhābun alīm

Yūsuf 12:25

“They both raced to the door, and she tore his shirt from behind; they found her husband at the door. She said: What is the recompense for someone who intended evil toward your household, except imprisonment or a painful punishment?”

Sayyidahā refers here to the woman's husband.

The pronoun hā refers to the woman, and bi-ahlika in the next sentence further clarifies the family relationship.

Sayyid could denote a husband in Arabic, particularly in a social setting where he held household authority and responsibility.

This does not make every husband the lexical owner of his wife's person and existence.

A social title in a historical community must be distinguished from establishing absolute ownership over another human being.

Given the story, sayyidahā is appropriately translated “her husband.”

“Her master” might suggest that the woman was another person's property, whereas the story identifies her as the wife of the Egyptian dignitary.

The contextual meaning is therefore determined by the relationships within the story.

Sādatanā: leaders who misled others

Al-Aḥzāb 33:67 states:

وَقَالُوا رَبَّنَا إِنَّا أَطَعْنَا سَادَتَنَا وَكُبَرَاءَنَا فَأَضَلُّونَا السَّبِيلَا

wa-qālū rabbanā innā aṭaʿnā sādat-anā wa-kubarāʾanā fa-aḍallūnā al-sabīlā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:67

“They said: Our Lord, we obeyed our masters and our great ones, and they led us astray from the way.”

Sādatanā consists of the plural of sayyid and the pronoun “our.”

Alongside it, kubarāʾanā belongs to K B R and refers to great ones or persons of prominent standing.

These titles may overlap in their referents, but their roots and semantic ranges are not identical.

Sādah emphasizes mastery and leadership; kubarāʾ highlights greatness or elevated social standing.

Both are placed in a blameworthy context here.

On the day of accounting, the followers say they obeyed their great ones and masters, who caused them to go astray.

The title sayyid is therefore not itself evidence of guidance or truth.

The word used praiseworthily of Yaḥyā in Āl ʿImrān 3:39 appears in Al-Aḥzāb 33:67 for masters who misled others.

This is significant Quranic evidence that the title alone does not guarantee moral worth.

Does following masters remove personal responsibility?

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:66, those undergoing punishment wish they had obeyed The God and the messenger.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:67, they identify following masters and great ones as the cause of their error.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:68, they ask for greater punishment for those leaders.

These passages do not collapse the responsibilities of leader and follower into one.

A leader may use influence and position to call others down a wrong path and bear responsibility for misleading them.

Followers must also answer for their choices, obedience, and behavior.

Sayyid therefore neither legitimizes unconditional obedience to a person nor lets followers transfer all their responsibility to him.

This point also relates to the distinction between holding leadership and being morally fit for it.

In both discussions, leadership position must be distinguished from the truth of the path to which someone calls others.

Mastery, lineage, and hereditary titles

In later Arabic and Persian usage, sayyid acquired particular social and genealogical senses.

Some communities use the title for families claiming descent from a particular person or lineage.

This belongs to the title's social history but cannot determine every Quranic usage.

The Quran calls Yaḥyā sayyid; sayyidahā identifies the woman's husband in Yūsuf's story; and other people recall their misleading masters and great ones.

The Quranic meaning is therefore not restricted to a particular family descent.

Genealogical affiliation itself is a separate historical question requiring its own evidence.

An established lineage does not by itself prove the truth of someone's thought or moral superiority.

In Al-Baqarah 2:124, the Quran says that the divine covenant concerning Ibrāhīm's leadership does not reach wrongdoers; lineage alone therefore does not guarantee participation in that covenant.

This accords with the uses of sayyid: social standing and titles should not replace truth and justice as criteria.

7. Distinctions from related roots

S W D (sawād) and B Y Ḍ (bayāḍ): blackness and whiteness

B Y Ḍ concerns whiteness and becoming white.

The color branch of S W D concerns blackness and darkening.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, the colors appear in the image of dawn light and night darkness.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:106, faces becoming white and black are contrasted in the setting of accounting.

The contrast demonstrates their difference in color meaning.

Moral value, however, does not follow from color itself.

In nature, both black and white can display creation's beauty and diversity; in the accounting context, facial coloring can signal a person's outcome.

S W D (sawād) and Ẓ L M (ẓulmah): blackness and darkness

Ẓulmah concerns absent or reduced light and a state of darkness.

Sawād primarily denotes the color black.

An object can be black in daylight, just as a night can be partly illuminated by moonlight and stars and remain night.

An object's blackness and its surroundings' darkness are therefore distinct.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, these fields approach one another through the black-thread image and night darkness. That association does not make aswad mean darkness in every verse.

S W D (sawād) and L Y L (layl): dark color and nighttime

Layl names the period of night.

Sawād describes color or dark appearance.

Reduced light can make night describable as black, but night is not simply a color.

In Fāṭir 35:27, blackness concerns mountain portions; in Al-Baqarah 2:187, it figures in distinguishing night from dawn.

A fuller account of night as a period of time belongs to the separate study of L Y L.

S W D (sayyid) and ʾ M M (imām): mastery and leadership

In human applications, imām can mean someone who goes ahead and is followed by others.

Sayyid identifies someone with mastery, distinction, or authority among people.

Both titles may apply to one person without having identical meanings.

Someone may be his people's sayyid and socially influential while calling them to an erroneous path.

Imām can also designate a book or nonhuman reference in the Quran, whereas the three sayyid occurrences concern people.

Not every sayyid is therefore a leader of guidance, nor is every imām called sayyid.

S W D (sayyid) and R B B (rabb): mastery and lordship

Rabb concerns care, nurture, ownership, and administration; depending on context, it applies to The God or, in certain usages, to people.

Sayyid can likewise designate someone holding mastery among others.

In some social uses, both titles refer to the same person.

That overlap does not make every sayyid a rabb or lord over others.

In Yūsuf 12:25, sayyidahā identifies the woman's husband and his household standing in the story.

This usage should not be converted into divine ownership, lordship, or a right to legislate.

S W D (sayyid) and M L K (malik): mastery and kingship

Malik, in one of this family's uses, denotes a king or ruler.

Sayyid can also describe an influential or prominent person.

Being a sayyid does not necessarily mean being a king.

Yaḥyā is called sayyid in Āl ʿImrān 3:39, but the verse says nothing of political kingship.

Someone may also be recognized as a sayyid within a family or tribe without governing a territory.

Sayyid should therefore not always be translated “king.”

S W D (sayyid) and K B R (kibar): mastery and greatness

Sādatanā and kubarāʾanā appear together in Al-Aḥzāb 33:67.

Their association indicates semantic proximity.

Kubarāʾ emphasizes greatness and prominence; sādah emphasizes mastery and leadership.

Social distinction and moral arrogance are also different.

Someone may be prominent and respected without arrogance, or consider himself superior without possessing real standing.

The evaluation of each title follows from behavior and context.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Aswad — Al-Baqarah 2:187

A suitable rendering:

“Black”

In the phrase:

الْخَيْطِ الْأَسْوَدِ

al-khayṭi al-aswad

Al-Baqarah 2:187

“The black thread”

The full translation should preserve its connection with dawn and night darkness.

Sūd — Fāṭir 35:27

A suitable rendering:

“Black”

In the phrase:

وَغَرَابِيبُ سُودٌ

wa-gharābību sūd

Fāṭir 35:27

“And intensely black portions”

Gharābīb also supplies the intensity. Sūd itself need not be defined as “intensely black”; the qualification emerges from the combination.

Taswaddu and iswaddat — Āl ʿImrān 3:106

Taswaddu: become black.

Iswaddat: became black.

Translation should retain the change of color or condition.

“Became ashamed” and “became sinful” are not direct equivalents of these verbs, though such associations may arise from context.

Muswaddan — Al-Naḥl 16:58 and Al-Zukhruf 43:17

A suitable rendering:

“Darkened” or “blackened”

In the phrase:

ظَلَّ وَجْهُهُ مُسْوَدًّا

ẓalla wajhuhu muswaddan

Al-Naḥl 16:58; Al-Zukhruf 43:17

“His face darkens.”

“Dark” is more natural in Persian in these passages, expressing a person's unhappy response to news.

The lexical explanation should nevertheless make clear that the root concerns blackness and becoming black.

Muswaddah — Al-Zumar 39:60

A suitable rendering:

“Blackened”

In the phrase:

وُجُوهُهُمْ مُسْوَدَّةٌ

wujūhuhum muswaddah

Al-Zumar 39:60

“Their faces have become black.”

This concerns Resurrection and the outcome for those who lied about The God. Natural skin color should not replace this image.

Sayyidan — Āl ʿImrān 3:39

A suitable rendering:

“A distinguished leader” or “a person of standing”

Yaḥyā receives praiseworthy descriptions here.

“Leader” preserves the core of mastery, while “distinguished” makes his praiseworthy contextual standing clearer.

Sayyidahā — Yūsuf 12:25

A suitable rendering:

“Her husband”

This fits the relationships in the story.

Although sayyid lexically concerns mastery, the person in this sentence is the woman's husband.

“Her master” might misleadingly reduce their relationship to ownership of the woman.

Sādatanā — Al-Aḥzāb 33:67

A suitable rendering:

“Our masters” or “our leaders”

In the phrase:

أَطَعْنَا سَادَتَنَا وَكُبَرَاءَنَا

aṭaʿnā sādat-anā wa-kubarāʾanā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:67

“We obeyed our masters and our great ones.”

This preserves the verse's two related but distinct titles.

“Our religious leaders” is too restrictive as a direct translation: sādah is not limited to religious leadership and can include socially and politically influential people.

Conclusion

The Quranic root S W D has two main branches: blackness and darkening, and mastery or leadership.

In the color branch, aswad and sūd denote black color; taswaddu, iswaddat, and muswadd describe becoming black or acquiring a dark appearance.

Poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays shows blackness describing beauty in early Arabic. In the Quran, blackness forms part of night and dawn imagery in Al-Baqarah 2:187 and appears among mountain colors in Fāṭir 35:27.

By contrast, darkening faces in Al-Naḥl 16:58 and Al-Zukhruf 43:17 concern people's unhappy response to a daughter's birth. In Āl ʿImrān 3:106 and Al-Zumar 39:60, blackened faces appear in the setting of accounting and the outcome of deeds.

A natural black color is thus distinct from a face becoming black in a moral or eschatological event.

In the second branch, sayyid denotes a master, prominent person, or leader.

The poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā illustrates this early usage.

The Quran praises Yaḥyā as sayyid; Yūsuf's story uses sayyidahā for the woman's husband; and in Al-Aḥzāb 33:67, masters and great ones have misled their followers.

Mastery therefore names a position, but the position itself guarantees neither truth nor moral worthiness.

Lexical sources propose connections between blackness and mastery through sawād as bodily form and a mass of people. These explanations suggest intelligible relationships but do not establish a certain derivational history.

The final definition should therefore preserve the family's several branches:

S W D concerns blackness, dark coloring, and becoming black in its first branch. Through sāda, sayyid, and sādah, its second branch concerns mastery, leadership, and prominent social standing. Arabic also records sawād for bodily form and masses of people, and siwād for private conversation; these senses should not be reduced to a single certain origin without historical evidence.

The Quranic passages clarify two fundamental distinctions:

First, color and moral value are different. Blackness may be a natural and beautiful characteristic, or may serve in a particular context to express an unhappy condition or outcome.

Second, mastery and truth are different. A sayyid may be a worthy, distinguished person or a leader who misleads others. Judgment rests on the truth of his speech, his deeds, and the direction of his leadership, rather than his title or position alone.

Both distinctions should be maintained when translating and understanding every Quranic occurrence of S W D.
