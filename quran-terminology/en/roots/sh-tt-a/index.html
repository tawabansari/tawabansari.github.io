Banks and sides, shoots and lateral growth: from the valley in Moses’ story to the growth and strength of a crop in the Qur’an

Early Arabic has two familiar branches of SH Ṭ ʾ: the side or edge of something, especially a river or valley; and fresh growth emerging beside or around an existing plant.

Shāṭiʾ denotes the bank or edge of a valley, river, or sea, distinct from its bed or central area. Shaṭʾ denotes fresh shoots growing beside or around a plant’s base. One plural is ashṭāʾ.

Early lexical explanations sometimes connect them through the idea of a side: shoots grow beside the parent plant, just as a bank lies beside a watercourse or valley.

Other lexicographers distinguish the two without deriving them from one original meaning. We cannot securely establish that shoots were named after riverbanks or the reverse.

The root occurs twice: shāṭiʾi in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 and shaṭʾahu in Al-Fatḥ 48:29.

The first describes the right bank of the valley from which Moses hears a call. The second depicts a plant putting out shoots, growing strong, and standing firmly on its stems, illustrating the growth and strength of believers accompanying the Prophet.

Both have an intelligible connection with a side or edge, but each meaning must be established from the word and its context.

1. The root in early Arabic

Shāṭiʾ: the side of a valley or river

Shāṭiʾ al-wādī means a valley’s side or bank; shāṭiʾ al-nahr, a riverbank; and shāṭiʾ al-baḥr, a seashore.

It denotes the edge or margin of a natural expanse or course.

Imagine a valley between two elevations. Its middle may carry water, while its sides are distinguishable from the bed. Either side can be called shāṭiʾ.

A riverbank similarly marks the meeting of the water’s bed and surrounding land.

Shāṭiʾ itself does not mean water. A dry or seasonally flowing valley still has sides.

Shāṭiʾ al-wādī should therefore not invariably become the bank of a full river. Wādī can denote a valley or natural watercourse even when dry.

Shāṭaʾtu fulānan is also recorded for walking on one bank while another person walks on the opposite bank.

The two move along a course while occupying different sides.

The expression shows that a bank can be an extended side of a natural course, not merely a single point beside water.

A poetic witness concerning banks

A verse recorded in early Arabic sources reads:

وَتَصَوَّحَ الْوَسْمِيُّ مِنْ شُطْآنِهِ
بَقْلٌ بِظَاهِرِهِ وَبَقْلُ مِتَانِهِ

wa-taṣawwaḥa al-wasmiyyu min shuṭʾānihi / baqlun bi-ẓāhirihi wa-baqlu mitānihi

The growth of the early rains withered along its margins: the plants of its open ground and those of its firm uplands.

Shuṭʾān here denotes the sides or margins of the terrain described.

Wasmī concerns the season’s early rains and the vegetation they produce. Taṣawwaḥa means withering or drying out.

The poet describes vegetation in different parts of the land, naming its margins alongside other areas.

Shāṭiʾ and related plural forms thus feature in descriptions of terrain and vegetation.

The lexical citation examined here does not clearly identify the poet. It cannot securely establish either a pre-Islamic attribution or the date of composition.

Its value is evidence for shuṭʾān meaning sides or margins, rather than proof of a precisely dated pre-Islamic usage.

Shaṭʾ: fresh growth beside the parent plant

The second branch concerns plants.

Shaṭʾ al-zarʿ denotes fresh growth emerging beside or around an existing plant.

Crops such as wheat and barley may develop additional stems from their lower parts after the first stem emerges. Their growth makes the plant fuller.

Such shoots illustrate shaṭʾ.

Trees can likewise produce fresh growth around their base or trunk; early Arabic records shaṭʾ for such growth too.

Some lexical explanations call these shoots firākh al-zarʿ.

Firākh is the plural of farkh, commonly a chick or offspring. In this botanical usage, it means fresh growth produced by the parent plant.

The expression should not be understood as animal reproduction or the birth of something entirely independent of the parent plant.

Nor does shaṭʾ mean a newly planted seed. A seed may initiate growth, while shaṭʾ denotes growth emerging from an existing plant or base.

This distinction matters in Al-Fatḥ 48:29.

A poetic witness: shoots emerging from the soil

An early Arabic poetry collection attributes this verse to al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām:

يَخْرُجُ الشَّطْءُ عَلَى وَجْهِ الثَّرَى
وَمِنَ الْأَشْجَارِ أَفْنَانُ الثَّمَرْ

yakhruju al-shaṭʾu ʿalā wajhi al-tharā / wa-mina al-ashjāri afnānu al-thamar

“Shoots emerge upon the soil’s surface, and fruit-bearing branches from the trees.”

Shaṭʾ denotes growth appearing above the ground, alongside the mention of tree branches.

The verse illustrates the botanical use of the word.

Al-Zubayr was a contemporary of the Prophet, but this verse’s exact date and attribution are not independently established here. It should not be presented as securely pre-Islamic evidence.

Some lexical citations also give the verse without naming a poet, with variation in the opening verb.

If the attribution is accepted, it would be evidence close to the Qur’anic period. Without independently confirming it, the verse supports the lexical usage but not a definite date.

Al-Fatḥ 48:29 itself provides clear Qur’anic-period evidence for shaṭʾ as plant growth.

Do shaṭʾ and shāṭiʾ share one original meaning?

Some lexical accounts connect plant shoots with shāṭiʾ because shoots emerge around the parent plant’s sides.

This gives an intelligible image linking them:

A bank lies at a valley’s side; a lateral shoot grows beside the parent plant.

The semantic comparison is understandable.

Another early lexical analysis lists the plant and valley usages as distinct. That classification does not itself establish or disprove a historical derivation between them.

Semantic resemblance and proof that one usage historically developed from the other are separate claims.

The resemblance can be explained; historical derivation needs independent evidence.

A precise definition should therefore retain both “side or bank” and “lateral plant growth,” without imposing an unproven common historical origin.

2. Important forms and derivatives

The following forms are recorded in this family.

شَطْء: shaṭʾ; a plant’s fresh lateral growth, especially around its base. A geographical sense of side or bank is also recorded.

شُطُوء and أَشْطَاء: shuṭūʾ and ashṭāʾ; plurals of shaṭʾ, referring botanically to shoots or lateral growth.

شَطَأَ الزَّرْعُ: shaṭaʾa al-zarʿu; the plant produced its shoots.

أَشْطَأَ الزَّرْعُ: ashṭaʾa al-zarʿu; the plant put out shoots or developed lateral growth.

مُشْطِئ: mushṭiʾ; a plant that has produced shoots.

شَاطِئ: shāṭiʾ; a riverbank, valley side, or seashore; the edge of a natural expanse or course.

شَوَاطِئ and شُطْآن: shawāṭiʾ and shuṭʾān; plural forms concerning banks. Lexical accounts differ on the relationship of some plurals to shāṭiʾ or shaṭʾ.

شَاطَأَ: shāṭaʾa; walked on one bank while another person walked on the opposite bank.

شَطَأَ في شاطئ الوادي: shaṭaʾa fī shāṭiʾ al-wādī; walked along a valley or riverbank.

Mushṭiʾ, applied to a valley, can describe water flowing along its sides. Context distinguishes this from a plant producing shoots.

Other recorded verbal uses concern fastening a camel’s load, being able to bear a load, burdening someone, or cutting a strip lengthwise from hide or a hump.

Their historical relation to banks or shoots is unclear, and independently dated pre-Qur’anic evidence is not available here for every usage. They should not be forced into an invented unifying image.

Only two forms occur in the Qur’an:

  • شَاطِئِ, shāṭiʾi, in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30: a bank or side.
  • شَطْأَهُ, shaṭʾahu, in Al-Fatḥ 48:29: its shoots or lateral growth.

Shaṭaʾa, ashṭaʾa, shāṭaʾa, ashṭāʾ, and shawāṭiʾ do not occur there in those forms.

3. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30: the valley’s bank in Moses’ story

The Qur’an states:

فَلَمَّا أَتَاهَا نُودِيَ مِنْ شَاطِئِ الْوَادِ الْأَيْمَنِ فِي الْبُقْعَةِ الْمُبَارَكَةِ مِنَ الشَّجَرَةِ أَنْ يَا مُوسَىٰ إِنِّي أَنَا اللَّهُ رَبُّ الْعَالَمِينَ

fa-lammā atāhā nūdiya min shāṭiʾi al-wādi al-aymani fī al-buqʿati al-mubārakati mina al-shajarati an yā mūsā innī anā allāhu rabbu al-ʿālamīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30

“When he reached it, he was called from the right bank of the valley, in the blessed place, from the tree: O Moses, I am The God, Lord of all worlds.”

This follows Moses’ departure after completing his period of residence and work with his wife’s family.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:29, he sees a fire toward the mountain and asks his family to wait while he seeks news or a firebrand with which they might warm themselves.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30, he reaches the place and is called.

Shāṭiʾ: a bank, rather than the water itself

Shāṭiʾ al-wādī denotes the valley’s side or bank.

Wādī belongs to another root and denotes a valley or natural course through which water may flow.

Shāṭiʾ names its side.

The phrase therefore should not be translated as “from within the river” or “from the middle of the water.”

It concerns the valley’s bank, not its central bed.

The verse does not state that water was flowing then. Valleys may be dry or flowing at different times, so shāṭiʾ alone establishes no full river in this scene.

Al-ayman: identifying the right side

The phrase reads:

شَاطِئِ الْوَادِ الْأَيْمَنِ

min shāṭiʾi al-wādi al-ayman

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30

“The right bank of the valley.”

Al-ayman belongs to Y M N and, in the familiar spatial reading, identifies the right side.

That description distinguishes it from the opposite side.

The verse itself does not securely identify it as the valley’s east or west side.

Right and left commonly depend on orientation and the observer’s position. Converting them into a fixed compass direction requires independent evidence.

“The right bank of the valley” therefore avoids adding an unstated geographical direction.

The blessed place and the tree

The verse continues:

فِي الْبُقْعَةِ الْمُبَارَكَةِ مِنَ الشَّجَرَةِ

fī al-buqʿati al-mubārakati mina al-shajarah

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30

“In the blessed place, from the tree.”

These phrases further locate the call.

Buqʿah denotes a place or tract of land, qualified here as blessed.

The tree also appears in the call’s spatial description.

The verse combines several locational indications: the valley’s bank, the right side, the blessed place, and the tree.

They should not all be incorporated into shāṭiʾ itself.

Shāṭiʾ denotes the bank. The place’s blessedness, the tree, and the call come from other parts of the verse.

Mentioning a tree and a location does not by itself imply that The God physically inhabits the tree or is confined to a tract of land. The passage describes where Moses receives the call, rather than defining a bodily location for The God.

Ṭā Hā 20:12 also places Moses in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā, while Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:29 mentions the mountain’s side.

These passages clarify the spatial setting without authorizing additional geographical details unsupported by the text.

The first occurrence: its lexical result

Shāṭiʾ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 is a spatial word.

It means neither a plant shoot, a necessarily water-filled shore, nor a bodily location of The God.

Shāṭiʾ al-wādī means the valley’s bank or side.

Al-ayman identifies the side, while the other phrases further locate the event.

4. Al-Fatḥ 48:29: shoots and the growing crop

The second occurrence is at the end of Al-Fatḥ.

After identifying Muḥammad as The God’s messenger and describing those with him, the Qur’an states:

وَمَثَلُهُمْ فِي الْإِنْجِيلِ كَزَرْعٍ أَخْرَجَ شَطْأَهُ فَآزَرَهُ فَاسْتَغْلَظَ فَاسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِ يُعْجِبُ الزُّرَّاعَ لِيَغِيظَ بِهِمُ الْكُفَّارَ

ka-zarʿin akhraja shaṭʾahu fa-āzarahu fa-staghlaẓa fa-stawā ʿalā sūqihi yuʿjibu al-zurrāʿ

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“Their likeness in the Gospel is like a crop that puts forth its shoots, then strengthens it, grows thick, and stands firmly on its stems, delighting the cultivators, so that through them He enrages the disbelievers.”

The image depicts emergence and increasing strength.

Akhraja shaṭʾahu: putting forth lateral shoots

The phrase reads:

كَزَرْعٍ أَخْرَجَ شَطْأَهُ

akhraja shaṭʾah

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“Like a crop that puts forth its lateral shoots.”

Zarʿ concerns cultivation or a growing crop.

Akhraja means bringing out or causing to emerge.

Shaṭʾahu refers to shoots emerging beside or around the parent plant.

Lateral stem growth from the lower parts of cereals such as wheat and barley illustrates the image in agricultural terms.

A wheat seed produces a plant with an initial stem. Further stems may then emerge near its base and grow alongside the main stem, making the plant fuller.

This makes the meaning tangible.

The verse names neither wheat nor barley. Zarʿ is broader and should not be confined to one species without evidence.

Some early explanations also apply shaṭʾ to ears, fresh leaves, or other newly emerged plant parts.

Across the principal explanations, fresh plant growth is prominent; the familiar explanation identifies lateral shoots around the base.

The familiar interpretation is therefore best conveyed by “shoots,” rather than “seed,” “root,” “fruit,” or “finished harvest.” The reported alternatives should still be acknowledged.

A seed initiates growth, a root is part of the plant’s structure, and ripe fruit or grain belongs to another growth stage.

Here shaṭʾ concerns new growth emerging from the parent plant and participating in its subsequent development.

Fa-āzarahu: strengthening through support

After the shoots emerge, the verse says:

فَآزَرَهُ

fa-āzarah

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“Then strengthened it.”

The verb belongs to ʾ Z R and concerns support or strengthening.

Both the understood subject and the object pronoun matter in interpreting the relationship between plant and shoots.

In one reading, the parent plant strengthens its new growth.

In another, the new shoots help strengthen the plant as a whole.

Both describe growth and reinforcement within the plant.

This does not establish that every lateral shoot always strengthens every plant. Species, growing conditions, and competition between stems vary.

The verse depicts successful, sturdy growth, rather than an exhaustive, exceptionless botanical rule.

The relevant image is fresh growth beside the parent plant, followed by increasing strength of the whole.

In the description of the Prophet’s companions, that image acquires a social meaning.

It can be read as a small, initially weak group becoming stronger through new participants and sustained joint effort.

Shaṭʾ itself does not mean cooperation, assistance, or unity. Those associations come from āzarahu and the whole simile.

Fa-staghlaẓa: becoming thick and sturdy

The next stage is:

فَاسْتَغْلَظَ

fa-staghlaẓ

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“Then grew thick.”

The verb belongs to GH L Ẓ and here concerns increasing thickness and sturdiness.

A young plant may have thin, fragile stems that become thicker and stronger as it develops.

Istaghlaẓa describes growth beyond that initial thinness.

Thickness is not itself the meaning of SH Ṭ ʾ.

Shaṭʾ names the new growth; istighlāẓ describes becoming thick.

Keeping them distinct prevents the noun from absorbing every later feature of the plant’s development.

Fa-stawā ʿalā sūqihi: standing on its stems

The Qur’an then says:

فَاسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِ

fa-stawā ʿalā sūqih

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“Then stood firmly on its stems.”

Sūq here is the plural of sāq, referring to plant stems.

It should not be confused with sūq, “market,” a meaning also known in early Arabic. The botanical context identifies stems.

Istawā, from S W Y, concerns the plant attaining a firm, upright condition on its stems.

Together, growing thick and standing on its stems depict a developed plant no longer resembling its first fragile growth.

Istawā here does not lexically mean moral superiority or absolute perfection. At the physical level, the plant stands on its stems.

The social and moral application comes from the verse’s context and its description of the group.

Yuʿjibu al-zurrāʿa: delighting the cultivators

The verse continues:

يُعْجِبُ الزُّرَّاعَ

yuʿjibu al-zurrāʿ

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“It delights the cultivators.”

Al-zurrāʿ denotes those who cultivate crops.

The plant’s development has reached a stage that attracts their admiration.

A cultivator recognizes the difference between a weak initial shoot and a full, sturdy plant. Successful growth can therefore inspire both admiration and pleasure.

The image moves beyond emergence to the results of growth.

The cultivators’ response is not the lexical meaning of shaṭʾ. It is expressed by yuʿjibu in relation to the plant’s condition.

The verse then says:

لِيَغِيظَ بِهِمُ الْكُفَّارَ

li-yaghīẓa bihimu al-kuffār

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“So that through them He enrages the disbelievers.”

The pronoun in bihim refers contextually to those accompanying the Prophet.

The plant image thus concerns the group’s development and strength, unwelcome to the opponents described in the passage.

It does not imply that any action angering an opponent is automatically right or divinely approved.

The verse begins by describing the companions’ mutual mercy, worship, and pursuit of divine favor and approval. Its conclusion connects forgiveness and great reward with faith and righteous action.

The image praises strength within that description; it does not authorize any group to validate its conduct simply by claiming association with the Prophet.

The social image: growth within a whole

The plant produces new growth from itself.

Shaṭʾ in its familiar botanical sense is growth connected with an existing plant.

The image can illustrate a small group expanding as people join, internal bonds strengthen, and the whole becomes more stable.

On this reading, growth is more than increased numbers: reinforcement, thickness, and firm standing also feature.

Both numerical growth and increasing collective strength are present in the image.

It is not an exceptionless sociological law that a larger membership makes every group more truthful, just, or strong.

A group may grow numerically while remaining weak in trustworthiness, justice, or ethical conduct.

The verse connects growth with the qualities of a particular group. The praised strength should be understood within that description.

The promise is connected with faith and righteous action, rather than an external claim of proximity alone. It should not be separated from the qualities stated in the verse.

The conclusion reads:

وَعَدَ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ مِنْهُمْ مَغْفِرَةً وَأَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

waʿada allāhu alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti minhum maghfiratan wa-ajran ʿaẓīmā

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“The God has promised those among them who believe and do righteous deeds forgiveness and a great reward.”

The wording foregrounds faith and righteous action. The role of min in minhum has been interpreted both partitively and explanatorily; this lexical study does not settle that independent grammatical and interpretive question.

The plant image conveys collective growth and strength, while moral judgments about people and groups must be assessed through the Qur’an’s own criteria.

5. Distinctions from related roots

SH Ṭ ʾ compared with N B T

N B T concerns plants growing or emerging.

It has many botanical Qur’anic uses, including Ṭā Hā 20:53, which describes bringing forth diverse vegetation.

Shaṭʾ is more specific in its familiar sense: new growth beside or around an existing plant’s base.

A seed germinating can be described through N B T. When the established plant puts out lateral shoots or stems, shaṭʾ is more specific.

In this interpretation, every shaṭʾ is plant growth, but not all plant growth is shaṭʾ.

Al-Fatḥ 48:29 depicts new shoots emerging from an existing crop, giving a particular image of development.

SH Ṭ ʾ compared with F R ʿ

Farʿ concerns a branch or something arising from a source.

The physical images overlap, since both can denote something arising from an original body.

Botanical shaṭʾ is particularly associated with fresh lateral growth.

Farʿ is broader: a tree branch or a branch of a larger whole.

A large branch high on a trunk is a farʿ without necessarily being a fresh shoot near the plant’s base.

Not every branch should therefore be called shaṭʾ, nor should shaṭʾ be confined to a large tree branch regardless of stage or location.

SH Ṭ ʾ compared with J N B

Jānib denotes a side or aspect.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:29 mentions the mountain’s side, while Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 mentions the valley’s bank.

The words overlap in the field of sides and orientation.

Shāṭiʾ is especially associated with valleys, rivers, and seas, while jānib can concern people, places, and other matters more generally.

“Bank” or “side of the valley” therefore fits shāṭiʾ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30.

SH Ṭ ʾ compared with S Ḥ L

Sāḥil denotes the shore of a sea or broad body of water.

It overlaps with shāṭiʾ in that usage.

Shāṭiʾ can also denote a valley’s side without stating that water is flowing there.

The Persian word for a seashore typically suggests land meeting the sea, whereas a valley’s edge may border a dry bed.

“The right bank of the valley” is therefore a more natural Persian rendering than using the term associated with a seashore.

The translation must fit the word’s referent.

SH Ṭ ʾ compared with SH Ṭ Ṭ

The roots are distinct despite their similar spelling.

Shaṭṭ can also mean a riverbank and overlap with shāṭiʾ.

Other uses of shaṭaṭ concern exceeding a limit, transgression, and injustice.

Ṣād 38:22, for example, states:

فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَنَا بِالْحَقِّ وَلَا تُشْطِطْ

fa-ḥkum baynanā bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-lā tushṭiṭ

Ṣād 38:22

“Judge between us in truth and do not exceed the bounds of justice.”

The verb belongs to SH Ṭ Ṭ, not SH Ṭ ʾ.

Shāṭiʾ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 means the valley’s bank and carries no inherent meaning of injustice.

Geographical overlap alone does not establish a shared historical origin for every branch of these roots.

SH Ṭ ʾ compared with SH Ṭ R

Shaṭr concerns direction, a side, or a portion or half.

Al-Baqarah 2:144 states:

فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ

fa-walli wajhaka shaṭra al-masjidi al-ḥarām

Al-Baqarah 2:144

“Turn your face toward the Sacred Mosque.”

Shaṭr here specifies direction.

Shāṭiʾ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 identifies the valley’s bank, rather than simply a direction toward it.

Their semantic fields overlap in orientation and sides, but their precise functions differ in context.

6. Morphology and translation proposals for Forqan

Shāṭiʾ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30

The Qur’anic phrase reads:

مِنْ شَاطِئِ الْوَادِ الْأَيْمَنِ

min shāṭiʾi al-wādi al-ayman

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30

“From the right bank of the valley.”

Shāṭiʾ is a masculine noun on the fāʿil pattern, denoting a bank or side here.

It is genitive after min and forms a construct phrase with al-wād.

Al-ayman identifies the right side in the spatial reading; grammatical accounts differ on whether it directly qualifies the bank or the valley.

“The right bank of the valley” is clear and natural.

“The right bank of the river” adds an identification of the valley as a river that the phrase itself does not establish.

“The right side of the valley” is also intelligible, while “bank” more specifically conveys its edge or margin.

Shaṭʾahu in Al-Fatḥ 48:29

The Qur’anic phrase reads:

أَخْرَجَ شَطْأَهُ

akhraja shaṭʾah

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“It put forth its lateral shoots.”

Shaṭʾ is a masculine noun functioning as the accusative object of akhraja.

The suffix hu refers to zarʿ, the crop.

The crop or plant produces its own shoots.

In the Ḥafṣ reading used here, ṭāʾ has no following vowel. Some recognized readings vocalize it with a fatḥah.

That variation does not change the principal botanical meaning.

The familiar explanation concerns lateral shoots emerging beside the parent plant.

“Its lateral shoots” is precise, though somewhat technical for some readers.

“Its shoots” is more fluent but does not itself specify their lateral location.

A basic translation can use “its shoots,” with the lexical note explaining growth from the plant’s sides or base. These proposals do not change installed verse translations.

Translation proposals

Shaṭʾ: “lateral shoot,” “fresh growth beside the plant,” or “young side shoot.”

Ashṭāʾ: “lateral shoots” or “fresh growth.”

Shaṭaʾa al-zarʿu: “the plant put forth its shoots.”

Ashṭaʾa al-zarʿu: “the plant developed lateral shoots.”

Mushṭiʾ: “producing shoots” or “having lateral growth,” botanically.

Shāṭiʾ: “bank,” “edge,” or “shore,” according to the geographical setting.

Shawāṭiʾ and shuṭʾān: “banks,” “edges,” or “shores.”

Shāṭaʾa: “walked on the opposite bank” or “walked along opposite sides,” according to the construction.

For the two Qur’anic occurrences, these renderings are suitable:

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30

نُودِيَ مِنْ شَاطِئِ الْوَادِ الْأَيْمَنِ

nūdiya min shāṭiʾi al-wādi al-ayman

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30

“He was called from the right bank of the valley.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

كَزَرْعٍ أَخْرَجَ شَطْأَهُ

ka-zarʿin akhraja shaṭʾah

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“Like a crop that put forth its shoots.”

The accompanying explanation should identify fresh lateral growth, rather than seeds, fruit, or every part of the plant.

7. Conclusion

SH Ṭ ʾ has two clear early Arabic branches: sides or banks, and plant shoots or lateral growth.

Shāṭiʾ denotes a valley’s side, a riverbank, or a seashore; shaṭʾ denotes new growth beside or around the parent plant.

A semantic connection through “side” is imaginable, but the evidence here does not establish a definite common historical origin or direction of development.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30, shāṭiʾ identifies the valley’s bank where Moses receives the call. This spatial meaning should not absorb unstated details about water, compass direction, or the nature of divine speech.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:29, shaṭʾ denotes shoots within the image of a crop growing strong, thickening, and standing firmly on its stems.

The image illustrates the believers’ collective development and strength. Supporting, thickening, and standing firmly are not all contained in shaṭʾ itself; each is expressed separately.

The definition should preserve both branches: the side or edge of a natural expanse or course, and fresh growth beside or around an existing plant.

“Bank” suits shāṭiʾ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30, while “its lateral shoots,” or more fluently “its shoots,” suits shaṭʾahu in Al-Fatḥ 48:29.

This distinction preserves each word’s actual use without turning resemblance into unproven historical etymology or importing other verbs’ meanings into the root.
