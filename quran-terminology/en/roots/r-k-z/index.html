A faint sound; planting and securing something in place

Brief root meaning: This family has two important branches: a faint, low-intensity sound; and planting or fixing something in the ground or a particular position. The second branch also includes leaning, remaining firm, a place of stationing, and buried things.

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic form

The three-consonant Arabic root R K Z forms a family whose words initially appear to belong to two different fields: sound and hearing on one side, and inserting or securing something on the other.

رِكْز denotes a quiet, faint sound, perhaps heard from a distance or so weak that the listener merely detects its presence.

In another use, رَكَزَ الرُّمْحَ means planting a spear firmly in the ground. The same family supplies مَرْكَز for a fixed position, رِكَاز for wealth or mineral material hidden underground, and ارْتَكَزَ for leaning on something.

These meanings cannot immediately be reduced to a single definition. Faint sound, a planted spear, and a place of stationing each have their own specific uses.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in the branch concerning sound.

Forms examined and the Qur’anic derivative

رِكْزًا — Maryam 19:98

This indefinite accusative noun follows تَسْمَعُ, “you hear.” It means a quiet or faint sound reaching the ear.

Other Arabic forms in this family include:

  • رَكَزَ: Planted and secured something in the ground or another position.
  • رَكْز: The act of planting or fixing something.
  • رَكَّزَ: Secured or fixed something.
  • مَرْكُوز: Something planted or fixed in place.
  • ارْتَكَزَ: Leaned on something or remained firm through its support.
  • مَرْكَز: A place of stationing or a particular position.
  • رَكِيزَة: Something fixed in the ground that can serve as a base or support.
  • رِكَاز: Buried treasure or valuable material hidden underground, with variation in lexical usage.
  • رِكْز: A quiet, faint sound.
  • رِكْزَة: In some lexical uses, an indication of steadiness or firmness.

These forms help explain Arabic’s wider range, but only رِكْزًا occurs in the Qur’an in the form listed here.

2. Semantic cores in early Arabic

Two semantic branches, without forcing a single image

An early lexical analysis identifies two principal meanings for R K Z.

The first is securing something in another thing by inserting it into it.

The second is sound.

This division matters: even early lexical analysis did not reduce every use to one simple meaning.

The first branch emphasizes an object’s relationship to the position in which it is secured. The second concerns audible sound.

There may be a historical relationship between the branches. Some interpretive explanations, for example, compare faintness of sound with the concealed part of a spear planted in the ground.

That comparison does not prove that the meaning “sound” developed from planting a spear.

A root study should therefore examine each branch through its own evidence and avoid declaring a historical relationship without adequate proof.

رَكْز: planting and securing something

Arabic lexical records explain رَكَزَ الرُّمْحَ as planting a spear in the ground.

Part of the spear enters the ground so that it remains secure.

Two features appear: entering a position and becoming fixed there.

Merely laying a spear on the ground does not necessarily match this image. In the expression’s clear use, the spear is planted into the ground.

The image also appears in names for other objects, such as stakes driven into the ground to support something.

مَرْكُوز describes something secured, while رَكِيزَة denotes something set in place as a base or firm element.

Firmness and insertion must nevertheless be examined separately in each use. Not everything stationary has been driven into the ground.

مَرْكَز: a place of stationing

Early Arabic uses مَرْكَزُ الجُنْد for a position soldiers were ordered to hold and not abandon.

مَرْكَزُ الرَّجُل can likewise refer to a person’s position.

The central relationship here is remaining at a particular location.

Someone can occupy a military station or designated position without physically being embedded in the earth.

The planting branch can therefore extend to settling and securing a position.

In geometry, مَرْكَزُ الدَّائِرَة denotes the center of a circle.

The modern geometrical and administrative senses of “center” should not become the definition of the entire root family.

The Qur’anic رِكْزًا does not mean a geometrical center, a center of power, or an administrative station.

Leaning on something

A recorded lexical expression is:

ارْتَكَزَ عَلَى قَوْسِهِ

irtakaza ʿalā qawsihi

Meaning: “He leaned on his bow.”

The explanation describes placing the bow’s end on the ground and leaning against it.

The bow thus becomes a means of supporting the body.

This connects with fixing and becoming firm: the person places weight on something capable of supporting it.

This use of irtikāz is not identical to رِكْز meaning sound.

Irtikāz should therefore not be restricted in every context to mental concentration, deep thought, or psychological attention. Such extensions can be examined separately while preserving the established bodily use.

رِكَاز: what lies hidden underground

Lexical records use رِكَاز for buried wealth or treasure and, in some usages, valuable mineral deposits.

Lexicographers and jurists have differed over its scope.

Some have chiefly associated it with treasure buried by people; others have included mines and naturally occurring valuable deposits.

Its connection with being embedded or hidden in the earth is clear.

Something concealed beneath the soil may remain there until someone extracts it.

This connection does not make رِكَاز lexically identical to رِكْز meaning a faint sound.

The legal use of rikāz and rules concerning buried wealth are separate questions from this root’s Qur’anic occurrence.

The Qur’an does not use رِكَاز, and رِكْزًا in Maryam 19:98 does not establish a rule about treasure, mining, or ownership.

Steadiness of mind and other extended uses

The expression رِكْزَةُ عَقْل is also recorded in early Arabic.

One lexical report quotes a member of Banū Asad saying that he saw no رِكْزَة of reason in someone: he found no steadiness or firmness of mind.

Physical firmness is extended here to steadiness of mind and judgment.

The expression concerns a stable, soundly ordered mind, rather than mere persistence in an opinion. It does not by itself establish complete knowledge or the correctness of every judgment a person makes.

Stability and correctness should therefore be distinguished.

Lexical records also use مَرَاكِز for the places where teeth grow or are set, and مُرْتَكِز for a dry plant stem remaining after its leaves have fallen.

These uses concern remaining and being fixed, but should not all be presented as the proven historical sources of one another.

3. رِكْز: faint sound and early poetic evidence

A sound heard with difficulty

In the second branch, رِكْز denotes a faint or low-intensity sound.

An early lexical explanation describes a sound heard from afar, such as a hunter speaking quietly to his dogs.

The subject is neither inserting something into the ground nor remaining in place, but a sound reaching a listener.

It can come from a person or another being and be so quiet that the listener only detects its presence.

It therefore differs from clear, loud speech.

It does not mean complete silence. It denotes an audible sound, however weak.

Nor should it be restricted to a scientifically specified frequency or to sound necessarily below the human hearing threshold.

Ordinary linguistic usage concerns a faint sound a listener may perceive.

Evidence from Labīd ibn Rabīʿah

A poem attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

وَتَوَجَّسَتْ رِكْزَ الأَنِيسِ فَرَاعَهَا
عَنْ ظَهْرِ غَيْبٍ وَالأَنِيسُ سَقَامُهَا

wa-tawajjasat rikza al-anīsi fa-rāʿahā / ʿan ẓahri ghaybin wa-al-anīsu saqāmuhā

Translation: “She detected the faint sound of people, and it frightened her from beyond her sight; those people were her affliction.” The image describes hearing unseen humans whose hunting threatens the animal.

The verse describes an animal detecting human sounds without seeing the people.

تَوَجَّسَتْ concerns cautious listening and perception of a hidden sound in this scene.

رِكْزَ الأَنِيسِ in this transmitted wording is the faint sound of people.

Hearing and seeing are distinct: the animal has not yet seen the people but senses their presence through a weak sound.

The wording varies across copies and quotations. Some transmit رِكْز, others رِزّ or other forms.

The line is useful evidence for the reported usage, but its wording should not be treated without qualification as certain proof that this exact term stood in the original pre-Qur’anic poem.

Labīd lived across both periods, and dating a particular poem requires independent evidence.

Evidence from Dhū al-Rummah

Dhū al-Rummah, an Umayyad poet, writes:

وَقَدْ تَوَجَّسَ رِكْزًا مُقْفِرٌ نَدِسٌ
بِنَبْأَةِ الصَّوْتِ مَا فِي سَمْعِهِ كَذِبُ

wa-qad tawajjasa rikzan muqfirun nadisun / bi-nabʾati al-ṣawti mā fī samʿihi kadhibu

Translation: “A solitary, keen-sensed creature has detected a faint sound; its hearing does not err in discerning it.”

In the poem’s commentary, this passage describes a wild bull; some lexical quotations identify the perceptive listener as a hunter. The account of the full poem should be distinguished from an isolated lexical quotation.

In the animal reading, مُقْفِر suggests isolation in the wilderness, while نَدِس expresses acuity in perceiving signs.

On that reading, the animal detects danger through a weak sound.

رِكْزًا is the faint sound being heard; نَبْأَةِ الصَّوْتِ also concerns the sound perceived.

This example is clearer as evidence for the word itself, since the poem’s commentaries explain رِكْز as a faint sound.

Dhū al-Rummah lived after the Qur’an’s revelation. This is evidence from Umayyad Arabic, not a certainly pre-Islamic example.

Its significance is confirmation of the established use of رِكْز.

Evidence concerning planted spears

An early lexical quotation gives the following verse concerning spears set firmly in place:

وَأَشْطَانُ الرِّمَاحِ مُرَكَّزَاتٌ
وَحَوْمُ النَّعْمِ وَالْحَلَقُ الْحُلُولُ

wa-ashṭānu al-rimāḥi murakkazātun / wa-ḥawmu al-naʿmi wa-al-ḥalaqu al-ḥulūlu

Translation of the relevant phrase: “And the long spear-shafts are planted firmly.” مُرَكَّزَات describes the spears set and secured in position; the following phrase also mentions livestock and settled groups.

This belongs to the physical branch, not faint sound.

The quotation examined does not establish the poet or date with certainty, so it cannot be assigned to a pre-Islamic poet without evidence.

Together these examples document both the sound branch and the planting branch, but do not establish a definite historical path from one to the other.

4. The Qur’anic occurrence: رِكْزًا in Maryam 19:98

Verse text

Maryam 19:98

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُم مِّن قَرْنٍ هَلْ تُحِسُّ مِنْهُم مِّنْ أَحَدٍ أَوْ تَسْمَعُ لَهُمْ رِكْزًا

wa-kam ahlaknā qablahum min qarnin hal tuḥissu minhum min aḥadin aw tasmaʿu lahum rikzan

Maryam 19:98

Translation:

“How many generations We destroyed before them! Do you perceive any one of them, or hear even a faint sound from them?”

The meaning of رِكْز here

رِكْزًا here denotes a faint sound.

تَسْمَعُ makes hearing explicit. The branch concerning planting or securing something in the earth is therefore inappropriate here.

The sentence’s structure also matters:

أَوْ تَسْمَعُ لَهُمْ رِكْزًا

aw tasmaʿu lahum rikzan

Maryam 19:98

That is: do you hear even a quiet sound from them?

رِكْزًا is the accusative object of تَسْمَعُ.

Its indefiniteness within a question conveying negation emphasizes the absence of even the faintest discernible sound.

“A faint sound” or “a quiet voice” conveys this well.

“A shout,” “a loud call,” or “clear speech” would lose the feature of faintness.

“Silence” is not an accurate translation of رِكْز itself: the noun means a sound. Its absence results from the rhetorical question, not the noun’s lexical meaning.

هَلْ تُحِسُّ and أَوْ تَسْمَعُ

The verse places two expressions side by side:

هَلْ تُحِسُّ مِنْهُم مِّنْ أَحَدٍ

hal tuḥissu minhum min aḥadin

Maryam 19:98

And:

أَوْ تَسْمَعُ لَهُمْ رِكْزًا

aw tasmaʿu lahum rikzan

Maryam 19:98

The first concerns perceiving or sensing the presence of even one of them.

The second concerns hearing a sound from them.

Together the questions extend perception from the presence of people to their faintest audible trace.

Someone’s presence may be detected by sight, sensation, or other signs. Hearing is explicitly named in the second clause.

The Qur’an therefore asks more than whether the people are visible: do you perceive any of them, or hear even a faint sound from them?

This sequence strengthens the image of past generations’ destruction.

The question’s force: not even a faint sound

The question is not seeking an unknown answer.

The context indicates that the expected answer is negative.

The generations that have perished are no longer present among the living, and their voices are not heard.

The choice of رِكْزًا matters here.

Had the verse mentioned only shouting or loud speech, the absence of loud sound might have been at issue. Denying even a faint sound makes the image of their vanished living presence more complete.

This denial should not become a theory that every historical trace of the past has been erased.

Buildings, inscriptions, tools, or other remains may survive a destroyed community. Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:35 itself speaks of leaving a clear sign from a community’s history.

Maryam 19:98 therefore does not mean that no material or historical trace remains of any destroyed generation.

The image emphasizes their own passing and the silencing of their living presence: none of those people remains among the living to be heard.

Connection with Maryam 19:74

Maryam 19:74 also speaks of earlier generations being destroyed.

It describes some past generations as superior to the original audience in furnishings and outward appearance.

This connection matters for understanding the surah’s final verse.

Power, resources, wealth, and outward splendor do not by themselves guarantee a society’s survival.

A community may once have been populous, powerful, and prosperous, yet its generations pass away and their voices cease to be heard.

This conclusion must not be added to the lexical meaning of رِكْز.

The word expresses faint sound; the impermanence of power and possessions comes from reading the verse within the surah.

Connection with Maryam 19:97

Maryam 19:97 describes making the Qur’an easy in the addressed messenger’s language, to give good news to the mindful and warn a contentious people.

Maryam 19:98 then closes with a reminder of earlier generations’ fate.

The connection links the warning to communities that actually lived and passed away, rather than leaving it as an isolated verbal threat.

رِكْزًا supplies a short, perceptible image in this ending.

One can imagine a city once full of life, movement, and voices, from whose former people even a quiet sound is now unheard.

The image shows the transience of human presence without making رِكْز itself mean death, destruction, or punishment.

Does the verse describe the permanent annihilation of all human existence?

رِكْز itself concerns audible sound in the audience’s world.

The absence of that sound alone does not prove that human existence has entirely ended or that no stage follows death.

Nor can this word determine the nature of awareness, speech, or life after death.

The verse concerns past generations’ fate and their lack of perceptible presence among the living.

Other verses discuss resurrection, accountability, and humanity’s ultimate outcome, but these are not parts of the lexical meaning of رِكْز.

The verse should therefore be understood within its own field: past communities have perished, and even their people’s faintest sound is no longer heard in the present world.

5. Comparison with related roots

R K Z (rikz) and H M S (hams)

In some uses both concern quiet sounds.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 uses هَمْسًا in a scene of resurrection.

Hams can concern a very quiet sound, a murmur, or the faint noise of movement.

رِكْز likewise means a faint sound in Maryam 19:98.

Their proximity does not make them fully identical in every use.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 presents voices subsiding in a particular scene; Maryam 19:98 asks whether even a faint sound remains from destroyed generations.

Hams is heard in one verse, while even rikz is not heard from those people in Maryam 19:98.

The difference in outcome follows from context, not an assumed absolute boundary between the roots.

R K Z (rikz) and Ḥ S S (ḥiss)

Maryam 19:98 also contains تُحِسُّ.

Ḥiss concerns perception and sensation, including becoming aware of something’s presence.

Rikz in its Qur’anic use is the sound that might be heard.

تُحِسُّ is therefore an act of perceiving, whereas رِكْزًا names a faint sound.

Although related within human perception, they differ in lexical role and meaning.

This clarifies the sentence: no person’s presence is perceived, and no sound from them reaches the ear.

R K Z (rikz) and R M Z (ramz)

The roots resemble each other in their letters but differ in meaning.

Ramz concerns signaling and conveying a message by signs.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:41, رَمْزًا occurs in the context of not speaking to people and can refer to communication through gesture.

رِكْز in Maryam 19:98 is a faint sound.

Letter similarity cannot make rikz mean silent gesture or restrict ramz to quiet sound.

Someone may communicate by gesture without speaking in one situation, while a very faint sound is heard in another.

The acts may have related communicative results, but their words and formations differ.

R K Z (rikz) and KH F T (khaft)

Khaft in some uses concerns lowering the voice and speaking quietly.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:110, تُخَافِتْ contrasts with تَجْهَرْ.

That context distinguishes raising and lowering the voice.

رِكْز in Maryam 19:98 names a faint sound, rather than issuing a command to lower the voice.

Mukhāfatah can be the act of speaking softly; rikz is the weak sound itself.

This matters in translation: رِكْزًا should not become “speaking quietly” unless the context supplies that action.

R K Z (rakz) and TH B T (thabāt)

In its physical branch, rakz concerns securing something.

Thabāt likewise concerns firmness and remaining stable.

But uses such as planting a spear in the ground describe a particular way of securing something.

Thabāt has a wider field, encompassing opinion, faith, footing, position, and other conditions.

Ibrāhīm 14:27, for example, speaks of keeping believers firm.

That does not mean they are physically planted in the ground.

Conversely, رَكَزَ الرُّمْحَ does not contain every meaning of faith or moral steadfastness.

Sharing the field of firmness does not make all uses identical.

R K Z (rakz) and R K N (rukn)

Rukn concerns a side, a support, and a source of strength to rely upon.

The physical branch of rakz concerns planting and fixing something; irtakaza can concern leaning on something.

The families can converge in reliance and stability.

Rukn does not itself mean driving something into the earth, and rakz does not always mean a shelter or supporting power.

In Hūd 11:80, رُكْنٍ شَدِيدٍ belongs to the field of strong support and reliance.

That use has no direct semantic connection to رِكْزًا as faint sound in Maryam 19:98.

Similar letters should therefore not cause the two roots to be conflated.

6. Interpretive challenges and translation proposals for Forqan

Distinguishing رِكْز and رَكْز

A key distinction is between established uses of رِكْز with kasrah on the rāʾ and رَكْز with fatḥah.

The Qur’anic رِكْز concerns faint sound.

رَكْز in expressions such as رَكَزَ الرُّمْحَ concerns planting and securing something.

The difference in vowel and meaning should be preserved.

Some lexical sources record vowel variants and overlapping uses, so the distinction should not become an exceptionless rule for all historical Arabic.

In Maryam 19:98, تَسْمَعُ also confirms the sound-related meaning.

Why “a faint sound” is preferable

Several Persian equivalents can be considered for رِكْزًا:

Their senses are “a quiet sound,” “a faint sound,” “a weak voice,” and “a low-intensity sound.”

The Persian equivalent of “a quiet sound” is natural in general prose.

“A faint voice” can also work, though it is somewhat more literary.

“Murmur” can approach the meaning in some settings but may suggest speech produced by the mouth or a continuous human vocal sound.

Since rikz can be a faint sound whose details are indistinct, “a faint sound” is broader and less restrictive than “murmur.”

“Rustling” also specifies a particular kind of sound and cannot serve as the main equivalent in every use.

Proposed verse translation

Maryam 19:98

“How many generations We destroyed before them! Do you perceive any one of them, or hear even a faint sound from them?”

This preserves the word’s meaning and the sentence’s structure.

In particular, “a faint sound” shows that not even the slightest audible presence of those generations remains.

The supplied translation accords with this meaning and requires no compulsory lexical replacement.

Proposed Forqan word-box explanation

Word: رِكْزًا

Root: R K Z

Brief root meaning: A faint sound; in another branch, planting and securing something in a position.

Meaning in this verse: A faint, low-intensity sound from destroyed generations, within a question expressing the absence of even such a sound.

In the word box, the spear, station, and buried-wealth branch needs only brief mention in the general root meaning.

Its fuller discussion belongs in the root article, since رِكْزًا directly concerns hearing a sound here.

7. Conclusion and final definition

R K Z has two important Arabic branches.

The first concerns planting and securing something. رَكَزَ الرُّمْحَ means planting a spear firmly in the ground. Related uses include markaz for a station, irtakaza for leaning, and rikāz for valuable things buried or hidden underground.

The second concerns faint sound. Rikz may be heard from afar or be so weak that a listener only detects its presence.

Early lexical analyses distinguish these branches. There is no need to derive faint sound from a planted spear, or planting from sound, simply to preserve an apparent unity.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an: رِكْزًا in Maryam 19:98.

The meaning is plainly faint sound. The verse concerns generations that passed away before the audience: none of their people’s presence is sensed, nor is even a quiet sound from them heard.

This depicts the transience of generations’ living presence. It does not establish that all historical traces have vanished or that all human existence after death is annihilated.

Final definition of R K Z:

The family has two distinct branches:

1. Planting and securing something in a particular position, especially inserting something such as a spear into the ground; by extension, remaining firm, leaning, and a place of stationing.

2. A faint, low-intensity sound, perhaps heard from a distance or with difficulty.

Only the second occurs in the Qur’an.

رِكْزًا in Maryam 19:98 therefore means “a faint sound.” It does not itself mean silence, destruction, support, a center, or a buried object.

Understanding the root requires preserving the distinction between branches and determining each derivative’s meaning from actual usage, without inventing an unproven historical image for the whole family.
