1. Introducing the root

The triliteral Arabic root ج ن ف concerns inclining, bending, and departing from an alignment or previous course. Related forms include جَنَف, crookedness or deviation; أَجْنَف, describing a person or thing with a bend in its structure; and تَجَانَفَ, to incline or turn to one side.

Bodily usage can concern a frame whose sides differ in shape or alignment. Spatial usage concerns movement or departure from a course. In human relationships, the same semantic field also concerns partiality, unfair conduct, and departure from right.

For example, someone may lean to one side while walking. That bodily condition alone establishes no moral judgment about them.

If someone judging between two people departs from fairness in favor of one, however, inclination and deviation operate in an ethical and legal context.

The issue is then no longer bodily alignment but departure from right and justice.

This relationship does not make every inclination wrongful or sinful. People may leave an incorrect path and turn toward a correct one. Bodily or spatial inclination itself has no fixed moral value.

The principal meaning of ج ن ف is inclination and deviation; context determines whether this is departure from right or simply a change of direction.

The Qur’an uses the root twice in two different forms:

Quranic Arabic Corpus:two occurrences and two forms

  • In Al-Baqarah 2:182, جَنَفًا concerns possible departure from right and fairness in a bequest.
  • In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, مُتَجَانِفٍ in غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ concerns not inclining toward sin in necessity.

In the first use, the deviation concerns bequests and people’s rights.

In the second, someone is compelled by severe hunger but must not deliberately incline toward sin.

Both uses share inclination and deviation, while differing in grammatical construction and subject.

Another important distinction is between جَنَفًا and إِثْمًا in Al-Baqarah 2:182. Many early commentators explain janaf there as error or unintended deviation, and ithm as deliberate sin.

That contextual distinction is defensible, but must not become an absolute definition of the root: Arabic janaf can also describe knowing deviation and deliberate injustice.

Understanding this root therefore requires separate attention to lexical meaning, morphology, and its role in each verse.

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

الْمَيْل: inclination and bending

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْجِيمُ وَالنُّونُ وَالْفَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ الْمَيْلُ.

al-jīmu wa-al-nūnu wa-al-fāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa al-maylu

Translation: “Jīm, nūn, and fā have one principle: inclination.”

Maqāyīs:inclination and bending

This short definition identifies the family’s center. Brief root meaning: Inclination and departure from alignment; in human affairs, deviation from right and fairness.

Something can follow a course or alignment, then incline away from it.

This inclination may occur in bodily structure, movement, speech, decisions, or conduct.

On a road, a person departs from a previous course.

In the body, one side differs in alignment from the other.

In judgment, someone departs from fairness and right.

مَيْل does not always mean injustice, however. Someone may turn from a dangerous road toward a safe one.

To understand janaf’s moral significance, one must therefore ask what the person or thing inclines away from and toward.

Bodily inclination and moral deviation

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

الْجَنَفُ: الْمَيْلُ وَالْجَوْرُ.

al-janafu: al-maylu wa-al-jawru

Translation: “Janaf is inclination and injustice, or departure from right.”

The definition contains two semantic levels.

الْمَيْل concerns bending or inclining.

الْجَوْر concerns departure from justice and right.

Their association shows that Arabic uses the semantic field of bodily inclination for unjust conduct as well; it does not by itself establish a precise historical sequence.

For example, sound judgment between two people can require examining evidence and respecting each person’s rights.

A judge who favors one side because of kinship, hostility, bribery, or personal advantage departs from justice.

That situation can be called janaf.

If the judge awards one side its due after examining the evidence, however, unequal outcomes alone do not make the result janaf.

Fairness does not always mean equal division; it concerns respecting actual rights.

In legal usage, deviation must therefore be assessed against right and justice.

Is janaf always deliberate?

The root itself does not establish that conclusion.

Ignorance, a calculation error, or misunderstanding may cause departure from a correct course.

Another person may knowingly cause the same deviation to benefit one side.

Both can result in departure from right, while the actors’ responsibility differs.

Three matters must therefore be distinguished: the deviation, awareness of it, and intention in acting.

Janaf in its general sense expresses the first.

Qualifications and context establish awareness and intent.

This matters especially in Al-Baqarah 2:182, where جَنَفًا and إِثْمًا appear together.

3. Bending and asymmetry in bodily structure

Unequal alignment of the body’s sides

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

الْجَنَفُ فِي الزَّوْرِ: دُخُولُ أَحَدِ شِقَّيْهِ وَانْهِضَامُهُ مَعَ اعْتِدَالِ الْآخَرِ.

al-janafu fī al-zawri: dukhūlu aḥadi shiqqayhi wa-inhiḍāmuhu maʿa iʿtidāli al-ākhari

Translation: “Janaf in the chest is the inward recession and depression of one side while the other remains straight.”

Lisān:zawr and asymmetry of the chest

زَوْر here concerns the chest region and must not be confused with زُور meaning false speech.

Janaf describes a difference between the sides of a physical structure.

One side of the chest or body may lean or be depressed relative to the other.

This is a tangible example of bending and asymmetry.

The description itself does not identify its cause.

The difference may have been present from the beginning or developed later through injury or another condition.

Bodily janaf alone is also no sign of weak character, sin, or wrongful conduct.

أَجْنَف and جَنْفَاء

Arabic أَجْنَف describes a man, and جَنْفَاء a woman or feminine referent, with some inclination or bending in bodily structure.

Ibn Fāris explains in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

وَرَجُلٌ أَجْنَفُ إِذَا كَانَ فِي خَلْقِهِ مَيْلٌ.

wa-rajulun ajnafu idhā kāna fī khalqihi maylun

Translation: “An ajnaf man is one whose bodily structure has an inclination.”

He then reports that the description sometimes concerns someone tall and bent.

أَجْنَف could therefore concern asymmetry between the body’s sides or a bent stature.

The two need not be the same bodily condition.

Someone may have a bent stature without asymmetry of the chest.

Conversely, the sides may differ while the person stands upright.

These uses concern bodily bending; the particular witness establishes the referent.

جُنَافِيّ: an inclined manner of walking

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes Shamir explaining جُنَافِيّ as someone who inclines in walking, moving with a swagger or strut.

He expressly says he heard the form only in a witness from Al-Aghlab al-ʿIjlī.

That rajaz fragment says:

غِرٍّ جُنَافِيٍّ جَمِيلِ الزِّيِّ

ghirrin junāfiyyin jamīli al-ziyyi

Translation close to context: “An inexperienced or guileless young man, walking with an inclined swagger, handsome in appearance.”

جُنَافِيّ concerns the manner of walking.

Al-Aghlab al-ʿIjlī lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This particular fragment is not independently dated and should not be presented as securely pre-Islamic.

Al-Ḥayawān:biographical note on Al-Aghlab al-ʿIjlī

Shamir himself also notes the scarcity of evidence for the form.

It is consequently a limited use within the family, not the general meaning of all ج ن ف derivatives.

4. Important forms and derivatives

جَنِفَ: bent or departed from a course

جَنِفَ is a Form I verb, with the imperfect يَجْنَفُ.

Its familiar verbal noun is جَنَف.

Bodily usage can concern bending or having an inclined posture.

Ethical and legal usage concerns departure from right and fairness.

For example, جَنِفَ عَلَيْهِ in dispute or judgment can mean showing unfair partiality against someone.

جَنَف: deviation

جَنَف is a noun or verbal noun meaning inclination and crookedness.

In bodily usage it concerns asymmetry or departure from alignment.

In bequests and judgment, it concerns departure from right and fairness.

This is the Qur’anic form in Al-Baqarah 2:182.

Grammatically, جَنَفًا is accusative there, the object of خَافَ in the sense of fearing deviation.

جُنُوف: another verbal noun

Some sources also record جُنُوف as a verbal noun or noun concerning inclination and deviation.

It does not occur in the Qur’an.

Its existence shows that the family can express inclination through more than one verbal-noun form.

Different verbal nouns must not, without evidence, generate two independent ethical or legal rulings.

أَجْنَفَ: acted with deviation

أَجْنَفَ is Form IV.

Arabic uses it for someone whose judgment or conduct displays janaf.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ records:

وَيُقَالُ: أَجْنَفَ الرَّجُلُ، أَيْ جَاءَ بِالْجَنَفِ.

wa-yuqālu: ajnafa al-rajulu ay jāʾa bi-al-janafi

Translation: “One says that a man ajnafa: that is, he acted with janaf.”

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ:ajnafa as acting with janaf

The verb here concerns conduct departing from right.

Form IV itself does not necessarily mean causing someone else to deviate.

Some Arabic Form IV verbs can express displaying or acquiring a quality.

A verb’s construction must therefore be assessed through its actual attestation.

جَانِف: inclining or deviating

جَانِف is an attested active participle in this family.

In relation to rights, it concerns someone departing from fairness.

Elsewhere, however, simple inclination or departure from a course may be intended.

Jānif should therefore not automatically become “deliberate oppressor” in every sentence.

مُجْنِف: departing from right

مُجْنِف is the Form IV active participle.

Lexical sources use it for someone inclining away from right or showing janaf in conduct and judgment.

Some witnesses apply it to someone departing from fairness in dispute and hostility.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

تَجَانَفَ: inclined to one side

تَجَانَفَ is Form VI.

Arabic uses it for inclining, moving aside from one direction, or turning toward another.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

وَجَنِفَ عَنْ طَرِيقِهِ وَجَنَفَ وَتَجَانَفَ: عَدَلَ.

wa-janifa ʿan ṭarīqihi wa-janafa wa-tajānafa: ʿadala

Translation: “He janifa, janafa, or tajānafa from his road: that is, he departed from it.”

Tajānaf can therefore concern an actual change of direction.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, however, its object is إِثْم, so the inclination concerns sin.

مُتَجَانِف: someone inclining

مُتَجَانِف is the Form VI active participle.

It occurs once in the Qur’an as مُتَجَانِفٍ.

It describes someone inclining toward something or departing from a direction.

The preposition and its complement establish the kind of inclination.

تَجَانَفَ عَنْ can express turning away from something.

تَجَانَفَ لِـ can express inclining toward something.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, لِإِثْمٍ makes the direction or object of inclination clear.

جِنَاف: a condition of deviation

Some sources record جِنَاف for a condition of partiality or blameworthy estrangement.

It does not occur in the Qur’an.

Certain expressions concern persisting in wrongful conduct or keeping away from one’s family.

That limited use must not become the meaning of every form of the root.

Proper names

Some sources record جُنَفَى and جَنْفَاء as place names; the former has an open nūn, and additional transmitted vocalizations are also reported.

Lisān:place names and vocalization variants

The presence of such names in dictionaries is not by itself evidence for the root’s moral or legal meaning.

Proper names may have independent histories.

They must therefore not be used to draw unsupported conclusions about Qur’anic جَنَفًا or مُتَجَانِفٍ.

5. Pre-Islamic poetry and early Arabic witnesses

Turning from a course in Al-Aʿshā’s poetry

Al-Aʿshā Maymūn ibn Qays, a recognized poet of the late pre-Islamic period, says in praise of Hawdhah ibn ʿAlī al-Ḥanafī:

تَجَانَفُ عَنْ جَوِّ الْيَمَامَةِ نَاقَتِي
وَمَا عَدَلَتْ مِنْ أَهْلِهَا لِسِوَائِكَا

tajānafu ʿan Jawwi al-Yamāmati nāqatī / wa-mā ʿadalat min ahlihā li-siwākā

Translation: “My she-camel turns away from Jaww al-Yamāmah, yet she has not turned from its people for anyone other than you.”

تَجَانَفُ concerns changing direction and inclining.

The movement is not itself presented as blameworthy or sinful in the poem.

The poet means that Hawdhah ibn ʿAlī is his true destination.

Departure from the route or inclination concerns the journey and destination, not oppression or unfair judgment.

This witness matters because early Arabic تَجَانَفَ could describe an actual change of direction without negative moral significance.

Some copies of Maqāyīs al-Lughah transmit the first hemistich as عَنْ جُلِّ الْيَمَامَةِ; Lisān al-ʿArab and poetic or grammatical discussions also transmit عَنْ جَوِّ الْيَمَامَةِ.

Maqāyīs:the jull reading in Al-Aʿshā’s witness

Jaww was a known name for the Yamāmah area.

This textual variation must remain visible when citing the witness.

Lisān:the Jaww reading of the same witness

Al-Aʿshā lived near the beginning of Islam, and his poetry is important evidence for the language of that period.

Janaf in kinship relations

A poem attributed to ʿĀmir al-Khaṣafī says:

هُمُ الْمَوْلَى وَإِنْ جَنَفُوا عَلَيْنَا
وَإِنَّا مِنْ لِقَائِهِمْ لَزُورُ

humu al-mawlā wa-in janafū ʿalaynā / wa-innā min liqāʾihim la-zūru

Translation: “They are our kinsmen, even though they have wronged us and shown unfair partiality against us; we turn away from confronting them.”

جَنَفُوا عَلَيْنَا concerns departure from fairness toward another group.

الْمَوْلَى here concerns kinsmen linked by tribal relationship.

The poet distinguishes kinship from unfair conduct.

People may be close in descent while treating someone wrongly.

Janaf then concerns injustice, not bodily crookedness.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and other lexical discussions record the line.

The composition date and poet’s biography are not sufficiently established by the evidence examined to label it securely pre-Islamic without qualification.

Al-Ṭabarī:ʿĀmir al-Khaṣafī and kinship

Its value lies in attesting janaf for oppression and partiality in human relationships.

Deviation in dispute: Labīd’s poetry

A line attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

إِنِّي امْرُؤٌ مَنَعَتْ أَرُومَةُ عَامِرٍ
ضَيْمِي وَقَدْ جَنَفَتْ عَلَيَّ خُصُومِي

innī imruʾun manaʿat arūmatu ʿĀmirin / ḍaymī wa-qad janafat ʿalayya khuṣūmī

Translation: “I am a man whom the root and lineage of ʿĀmir have shielded from wrong, though my adversaries and disputants have acted unjustly against me.”

جَنَفَتْ عَلَيَّ خُصُومِي concerns unfair conduct by opponents in a dispute.

خُصُوم is the plural of khaṣm, people in dispute or opposition to someone.

ضَيْم likewise concerns oppression and violation of rights.

Here janaf operates in the field of human conduct and violated rights, rather than bodily alignment; the semantic relationship alone does not date the development of either sense.

Labīd was a mukhaḍram poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods.

Dating this particular line requires examination of its position in the collected poetry and historical evidence.

It is consequently evidence for early Arabic, but its date should not be asserted without documentation.

Gharīb al-Ḥadīth and Lisān:Labīd’s witness

Deviation in a disputant’s words and conduct

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes a line by Abū al-ʿIyāl al-Hudhalī:

أَلَا دَرَأْتَ الْخَصْمَ حِينَ رَأَيْتَهُمْ
جَنَفًا عَلَيَّ بِأَلْسُنٍ وَعُيُونِ

a-lā daraʾta al-khaṣma ḥīna raʾaytahum / janafan ʿalayya bi-alsunin wa-ʿuyūni

Translation: “Why did you not repel the adversaries when you saw them unfairly turning against me with tongues and eyes?”

Janaf concerns the hostile conduct and orientation of a group.

Lexically, جَنَفًا may here be a plural of جَانِف: people inclining away from fairness.

Alternatively, the verbal noun may stand for an omitted possessive expression, ذَوِي جَنَفٍ: those possessing deviation.

Lisān al-ʿArab reports both analyses.

Lisān:two analyses of janafan in Abū al-ʿIyāl’s line

Both readings nevertheless concern unfair deviation and partiality in the disputants’ conduct.

The line also attests the family’s use for judgment and human behavior alongside bodily crookedness, without proving a historical order of derivation.

What the early witnesses show

Al-Aʿshā’s poetry shows that tajānaf can describe changing direction toward a different destination without the act itself being blameworthy.

The poetry attributed to ʿĀmir al-Khaṣafī, Labīd, and Abū al-ʿIyāl clarifies the ethical and legal field: partiality and departure from fairness.

Early Arabic thus had two related fields: inclination or change of direction, and departure from right.

They should not be assumed identical from the outset.

6. First Qur’anic use: Al-Baqarah 2:182

فَمَنْ خَافَ مِن مُّوصٍ جَنَفًا أَوْ إِثْمًا فَأَصْلَحَ بَيْنَهُمْ فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

fa-man khāfa min mūṣin janafan aw ithman fa-aṣlaḥa baynahum fa-lā ithma ʿalayhi inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English:

“If anyone fears deviation from right or sin from a testator, and brings reconciliation among them, there is no sin upon him. The God is indeed forgiving and merciful.”

This is the root ج ن ف’s first Qur’anic occurrence.

جَنَفًا concerns deviation in a bequest.

Understanding it precisely requires examining the two preceding verses.

Connection with Al-Baqarah 2:180

Al-Baqarah 2:180 discusses a bequest for parents and close relatives, qualified by بِالْمَعْرُوفِ.

That qualification matters.

A bequest is not solely someone’s decision about their property: the verse also includes what is recognized as right and responsibility toward close relatives.

One must therefore distinguish the testator’s personal wishes from the rights of those affected.

The root ج ن ف itself does not occur in Al-Baqarah 2:180.

Its relationship to maʿrūf comes from the verses’ context.

Connection with Al-Baqarah 2:181

Al-Baqarah 2:181 condemns altering a bequest after hearing it and attributes the sin of alteration to those who change it.

This instruction matters for preserving the trust of a bequest.

Someone who hears another’s bequest and then changes it for personal advantage, hostility, or partiality breaches that trust.

Al-Baqarah 2:182 introduces another situation: possible deviation from right or sin in the bequest itself.

Here the way to correction is opened.

The two instructions do not conflict.

Changing a sound bequest for personal benefit is one act.

Correcting a bequest that risks violating rights or causing sin is another.

Faithfulness to a trust does not require preserving every error and injustice.

فَمَنْ خَافَ: fearing deviation

The verse begins فَمَنْ خَافَ.

خَافَ comes from خ و ف and concerns fear or apprehension.

The concern here is possible janaf or sin by the testator.

In ordinary lexical usage, apprehension differs from complete certainty. Some early commentaries also explain khāfa here through knowing or recognizing the deviation; that exegetical reading should not be concealed.

Al-Qurṭubī:apprehension and the reading as knowledge

Someone may see signs giving reason to fear an unfair bequest, even before it is implemented.

In one analysis, Al-Ṭabarī consequently emphasizes correction before the dispute has fully occurred.

Other exegetical reports also discuss correction after the testator’s death.

The Qur’anic wording does not provide a separate explicit qualification fixing the time of correction.

The concern about deviation and the action to reconcile are clear.

The verse therefore should not be confined without examination to a fixed time before or after death.

مِن مُّوصٍ: the testator

مُوصٍ is an active participle from و ص ي.

It identifies someone making a bequest.

Janaf here concerns that person’s conduct or decision regarding the bequest.

The expression does not make every testator necessarily sinful or unjust.

The Qur’an presents a conditional situation: if there is concern about janaf or sin from a testator.

A bequest’s existence alone is therefore no sign of janaf.

جَنَفًا: deviation in a bequest

جَنَفًا concerns departure from right and fairness in the bequest.

The testator may misunderstand certain needs or rights.

They may miscalculate their assets.

Age-related changes, illness, psychological pressure, or misinformation may lead to an inappropriate decision about relatives.

Someone may also knowingly seek to violate another person’s rights through hostility or partiality.

All these situations can involve departure from fairness in a bequest.

Their causes and levels of responsibility differ.

Lexical janaf therefore means deviation; deliberateness or inadvertence requires separate examination.

جَنَفًا أَوْ إِثْمًا: error or deliberate sin?

Early commentaries distinguish the expressions.

Al-Ṭabarī reports explanations of janaf as error in a bequest and ithm as knowing, deliberate deviation.

This reading includes two situations.

First, the testator may unintentionally depart from right.

Second, they may knowingly commit sin and injustice.

The distinction is defensible in context because both can create a need to correct the bequest.

An important qualification must nevertheless remain.

Lexically, janaf does not always mean unintended error.

In poetry about judgment and dispute, it can describe unfair partiality that may be deliberate.

The distinction between error and intention in Al-Baqarah 2:182 is therefore a contextual reading of جَنَفًا beside إِثْمًا, not the roots’ fixed definitions throughout Arabic.

Al-Ṭabarī:error and intention in the bequest

Janaf’s direct meaning is deviation.

Does أَوْ introduce wholly separate conditions?

أَوْ stands between two designations.

It does not establish that janaf and ithm have no overlap.

A deviation may itself be sinful.

An error may, conversely, differ in intention and responsibility from deliberate sin.

The verse can thus encompass related situations without drawing an entirely non-overlapping boundary.

This prevents treating every janaf as innocent and every ithm as necessarily one particular kind of behavior.

فَأَصْلَحَ بَيْنَهُمْ: reconciliation among those concerned

فَأَصْلَحَ comes from ص ل ح and concerns setting right or reconciling.

Here the setting is a bequest.

بَيْنَهُمْ refers to people concerned by the bequest and its resulting rights.

Disagreement or potential disagreement may arise among the testator, beneficiaries, and others affected by the assets and rights.

Reconciliation can help clarify rights, prevent injustice, or resolve disagreement.

The word “correction” itself does not permit arbitrary alteration of a bequest.

Someone who changes another’s share for personal advantage under the name of correction must still have their action assessed against right and maʿrūf.

فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ: removing sin from the reconciler

The main clause concludes that there is no sin upon the reconciler.

This matters in relation to the preceding verse.

Al-Baqarah 2:181 condemns wrongful alteration.

Al-Baqarah 2:182 distinguishes correction in response to janaf or sin.

The Qur’an thus differentiates wrongful alteration from just correction.

Claiming to correct something does not entitle anyone to change another’s bequest without grounds.

The verse itself presents correction in response to apprehended janaf or ithm.

The grounds and direction of the corrective action therefore matter.

7. Janaf in bequests: error, partiality, and protecting rights

Janaf can arise in different ways in a bequest.

A testator may have incorrect information about a relative’s finances and decide on that basis.

Elsewhere, strong attachment to one child may cause someone to overlook others’ actual needs.

Hostility toward a relative may also lead a testator knowingly to deprive them of a right.

These examples differ in cause and responsibility.

In the first, error may result from ignorance.

In the second, emotional partiality can influence the decision.

In the third, an intention to wrong someone is more evident.

All must nevertheless be assessed through actual evidence.

Giving more to someone with particular needs is not necessarily janaf.

With sound knowledge of needs and within maʿrūf, the testator may have made a just decision.

Conversely, equal division alone does not establish justice in every circumstance.

Janaf should therefore not be attributed to every difference in bequest amounts or every surviving relative’s dissatisfaction.

The criterion is departure from right and fairness.

Forqan’s studies of Al-Baqarah 2:180, Al-Baqarah 2:181, and Al-Baqarah 2:182 examine bequests in relation to responsibility toward parents and relatives, taking account of their different circumstances.

This background helps explain janaf.

The root ج ن ف itself does not determine each person’s exact share in every situation.

That requires the bequest’s wording, assets, debts, people’s rights, and other relevant verses.

Illness or old age alone does not establish inability to make decisions.

An elderly person may decide with full awareness and fairness.

A young, healthy person may conversely commit janaf for personal advantage.

The criterion should be the decision’s actual circumstances and respect for rights, not prejudice about age or bodily condition.

Does janaf concern only bequests?

No.

Qur’anic جَنَفًا in Al-Baqarah 2:182 concerns a bequest.

Early Arabic also uses janaf for judgment, speech, dispute, and bodily structure.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 uses a derivative of the same root in food-related necessity.

Reducing the entire root to “injustice in a bequest” is therefore incorrect.

Bequests are one Qur’anic application, not the definition of the whole family.

8. Second Qur’anic use: Al-Māʾidah 5:3

حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةُ وَالدَّمُ وَلَحْمُ الْخِنزِيرِ وَمَا أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ بِهِ وَالْمُنْخَنِقَةُ وَالْمَوْقُوذَةُ وَالْمُتَرَدِّيَةُ وَالنَّطِيحَةُ وَمَا أَكَلَ السَّبُعُ إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ وَمَا ذُبِحَ عَلَى النُّصُبِ وَأَن تَسْتَقْسِمُوا بِالْأَزْلَامِ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمْ فِسْقٌ ۗ الْيَوْمَ يَئِسَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِن دِينِكُمْ فَلَا تَخْشَوْهُمْ وَاخْشَوْنِ ۚ الْيَوْمَ أَكْمَلْتُ لَكُمْ دِينَكُمْ وَأَتْمَمْتُ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعْمَتِي وَرَضِيتُ لَكُمُ الْإِسْلَامَ دِينًا ۚ فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ فِي مَخْمَصَةٍ غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ ۙ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

ḥurrimat ʿalaykumu al-maytatu wa-al-damu wa-laḥmu al-khinzīri wa-mā uhilla li-ghayri Allāhi bihi wa-al-munkhaniqatu wa-al-mawqūdhatu wa-al-mutaraddiyatu wa-al-naṭīḥatu wa-mā akala al-sabuʿu illā mā dhakkaytum wa-mā dhubiḥa ʿalā al-nuṣubi wa-an tastaqsimū bi-al-azlāmi dhālikum fisqun al-yawma yaʾisa alladhīna kafarū min dīnikum fa-lā takhshawhum wa-ikhshawnī al-yawma akmaltu lakum dīnakum wa-atmamtu ʿalaykum niʿmatī wa-raḍītu lakumu al-islāma dīnan fa-mani uḍṭurra fī makhmaṣatin ghayra mutajānifin li-ithmin fa-inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English:

“Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, that over which a name other than The God has been proclaimed, the strangled, the beaten to death, the fallen from a height, the gored, and what a predator has eaten, except what you slaughter, and what has been slaughtered on sacrificial stones, and seeking portions by arrows. This is disobedience. Today those who have denied have despaired of your religion; do not fear them, but fear Me. Today I have completed your religion for you, fulfilled My favor upon you, and approved Islam as your religion.

If anyone is compelled by hunger, without inclining toward sin, The God is indeed forgiving and merciful.”

The verse contains the root ج ن ف’s second and final Qur’anic occurrence.

مُتَجَانِفٍ appears near the end, where necessity is discussed after food prohibitions.

Its subject consequently differs from Al-Baqarah 2:182.

Al-Baqarah concerns possible deviation in a bequest.

Al-Māʾidah concerns someone compelled by severe hunger whose inclination must not be toward sin.

فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ: being compelled

اضْطُرَّ comes from ض ر ر and concerns compulsion under pressure of circumstances.

The person’s ordinary options for choosing food have become limited.

That condition differs from personal preference.

Someone may dislike one food and prefer another; preference alone is not necessity.

In dangerous hunger without ordinary food available, however, a person may be compelled to preserve life through an option normally forbidden.

Necessity must therefore be understood through its own root.

ج ن ف alone does not mean hunger or compulsion.

فِي مَخْمَصَةٍ: severe hunger

مَخْمَصَة belongs to خ م ص and concerns severe hunger.

It makes the setting of compulsion clearer.

The person genuinely needs food.

مَخْمَصَة alone does not establish an exact food quantity, number of hours without eating, or fixed numerical threshold.

People’s actual circumstances can differ.

Recognizing necessity requires examining danger, access to food, and the person’s actual circumstances.

An independent Qur’anic study of lawful and forbidden food provides a broader setting for this discussion.

غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ: without inclining

غَيْرَ negates the described condition of the person.

مُتَجَانِفٍ is the Form VI active participle.

The expression therefore concerns someone in necessity who is not inclining toward something.

The object of inclination follows:

لِإِثْمٍ.

The complement completes the sentence’s meaning.

The person must not incline toward sin.

The role of لِإِثْمٍ

Arabic تَجَانَفَ لِـ can express inclining toward something.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3 its object is إِثْم.

The verse does not merely prohibit all inclination: it specifies inclination toward sin.

This distinction matters.

Someone in necessity may need food to remain alive.

That need is not itself the same as inclination toward sin.

Someone who uses necessity as a pretext to cross prescribed bounds without genuine need presents a different issue.

Here مُتَجَانِفٍ concerns knowing inclination toward sin.

Does مُتَجَانِفٍ always imply deliberate intention?

Lisān al-ʿArab explains the expression as:

مُتَمَايِلٌ مُتَعَمِّدٌ.

mutamāʾilun mutaʿammidun

That is: “One inclining deliberately.”

This explanation fits Al-Māʾidah 5:3.

The root’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from meaning supplied by context.

تَجَانَفَ itself concerns inclination.

Because the object is sin and the person is in necessity, deliberate inclination toward sin becomes relevant.

The verse can therefore be understood as concerning someone who, in using the concession for necessity, does not intend sin or transgression.

Al-Ṭabarī:deliberate inclination in necessity

This does not make tajānaf mean intending to sin throughout Arabic, regardless of its object.

Al-Aʿshā uses the same verb for his camel’s change of direction, with no sin involved.

Genuine necessity and misuse of a concession

The verse places necessity alongside not inclining toward sin.

Two different matters are involved.

First, the person truly faces a compelling situation.

Second, that situation must not become a pretext for sin.

Someone in severe hunger may use normally forbidden food to preserve life.

Another may claim necessity through mere preference despite having permissible food available.

The situations differ.

The qualification غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ must nevertheless not require a starving person to perform impossible or obsessive calculations before saving their life.

The Qur’an itself recognizes necessity.

The qualification does not dismiss genuine need; it guards against turning necessity into a pretext for knowingly inclining toward sin.

Connection with other necessity passages

Al-Baqarah 2:173, Al-Anʿām 6:145, and Al-Naḥl 16:115 also discuss necessity in food regulations.

They contain غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ.

These qualifications have received several explanations, including not seeking prohibited food for its own sake and not exceeding necessity; some commentaries offer other readings. Their precise scope belongs to the study of those verses.

Al-Ṭabarī:readings of ghayra bāghin wa-lā ʿādin

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 instead contains غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ.

The expressions have related meanings.

The roots ب غ ي, ع د و, and ج ن ف must nevertheless not be made fully synonymous.

In one reading, seeking forbidden food without necessity is at issue.

Another qualification concerns exceeding the necessary limit.

Al-Māʾidah negates inclination toward sin.

These aspects can coexist in wrongful behavior, while each word expresses its own aspect.

A precise study must preserve each root’s meaning before explaining their relationship within the network of verses.

9. Comparing the two Qur’anic uses of ج ن ف

In Al-Baqarah 2:182, جَنَفًا concerns departure from right in a bequest.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, مُتَجَانِفٍ concerns someone in necessity who must not incline toward sin.

The difference can be examined in several respects.

First, the morphological structures differ.

جَنَفًا is a noun or verbal noun naming the deviation itself.

مُتَجَانِفٍ is an active participle describing the person inclining.

Second, the objects differ.

Al-Baqarah concerns bequests and people’s rights.

Al-Māʾidah concerns using a concession for necessity while avoiding sin.

Third, error and intention have different roles.

Al-Baqarah allows a contextual reading of janaf as unintended error beside deliberate ithm.

In Al-Māʾidah, لِإِثْمٍ concerns knowing inclination toward sin.

Fourth, the ethical and legal outcomes differ.

Al-Baqarah opens the way to correcting the bequest.

Al-Māʾidah qualifies necessity by not inclining toward sin.

The shared core nevertheless remains clear: inclination and departure from a right alignment.

The root itself is not a definition of bequest, necessity, sin, or justice.

The relevant words and verses establish those concepts.

10. Distinguishing related roots

ج ن ف (جَنَف) and م ي ل (مَيْل)

Arabic مَيْل concerns inclining and changing direction.

Janaf’s semantic core likewise involves inclination.

Ibn Fāris uses that very word to define it.

Mayl can nevertheless occur in very broad and neutral uses.

Janaf in judgment, bequests, and human conduct can concern departure from right and fairness.

This distinction should not be absolute.

Al-Aʿshā’s tajānaf concerns changing direction, without sin or injustice.

Inclination is therefore an appropriate explanation of the core, while each use’s moral direction requires separate assessment.

ج ن ف (جَنَف) and ح ن ف (حَنِيف)

Study of Ḥ N F

These roots are close in their letters.

ج ن ف begins with jīm.

ح ن ف begins with ḥā.

Bodily حَنَف can also concern bending or inclination of the feet.

Qur’anic حَنِيف, however, concerns Abraham’s religious orientation and departure from associationism.

Janaf in Al-Baqarah 2:182 concerns departure from right in a bequest.

Shared inclination does not make the two roots identical.

Religious ḥanīf is positive in the Qur’an, whereas janaf in the bequest passage is a concern that may need correction.

Conversely, bodily inclination in ح ن ف is not always good, nor in ج ن ف always bad.

Its value depends on the course and object of movement.

ج ن ف (جَنَف) and ح ي ف (حَيْف)

Arabic حَيْف concerns departure from justice and right.

Legal janaf approaches the same field.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn distinguishes them: ḥayf concerns injustice in judgment, whereas janaf has a broader range.

Al-Azharī rejects confining ḥayf to official judgment and explains that other people’s unjust conduct can also be ḥayf.

Al-ʿAyn and Al-Azharī’s criticism:ḥayf and janaf

This disagreement matters.

A narrow definition must not become a fixed rule while lexical criticism of it is concealed.

In actual usage janaf and ḥayf can be very close in unjust deviation and partiality.

Qur’anic جَنَفًا nevertheless concerns a bequest, so that particular choice requires contextual evidence.

ج ن ف (جَنَف) and ج و ر (جَوْر)

Arabic جَوْر concerns departure from justice and oppression.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains janaf through الميل والجور.

They are consequently close in oppression and wrongful partiality.

Bodily janaf can nevertheless mean only crookedness or a change of course.

Ethical and legal jawr more clearly conveys unfairness.

Not every bodily bend can therefore be called jawr, nor every change of course oppression.

ج ن ف (جَنَف) and ع د ل (عَدْل)

Study of ʿ D L

Qur’anic عَدْل concerns justice, equality consistent with right, and in some uses turning aside or setting something as an equivalent.

Janaf in Al-Baqarah 2:182 concerns departure from right and fairness.

In that context it contrasts with just conduct.

Arabic ع د ل has broader uses, however, which should not all be made the opposite of janaf.

In particular, عَدَلَ عَنْ can mean changing course, the very sense used to explain tajānaf.

One root can thus concern justice in one use and turning from a path in another.

This demonstrates context’s importance.

ج ن ف (جَنَف) and أ ث م (إِثْم)

Study of ʾ TH M

These occur close together in both Qur’anic uses of ج ن ف.

Al-Baqarah 2:182 contains جَنَفًا أَوْ إِثْمًا.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 contains مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ.

إِثْم comes from أ ث م and concerns sin and culpable wrongdoing.

Janaf concerns inclination and deviation.

A deviation may also be sinful because of its intention and outcome.

An error may conversely differ in moral responsibility from deliberate sin.

The words are therefore related, not fully synonymous.

ج ن ف (جَنَف) and ب غ ي (بَغْي)

Study of B GH Y

Arabic بَغْي concerns seeking and wanting, and in some uses transgression and oppression.

Legal janaf can likewise accompany oppression.

Baghy can emphasize seeking or transgression, whereas janaf concerns inclination and deviation from alignment or right.

غَيْرَ بَاغٍ is a familiar qualification in food-necessity passages.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 has غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ instead.

Their contextual proximity matters but must not merge the roots.

ج ن ف (جَنَف) and ج ن ب (اجْتِنَاب)

Study of J N B

ج ن ب concerns flank, side, distancing, and avoidance.

The Qur’an uses اجْتَنَبَ for avoiding sin and wrongful conduct.

ج ن ف concerns inclination and deviation.

Some behaviors involving these roots can have similar outcomes.

For example, someone may avoid sin and depart from an incorrect course.

Ijtināb nevertheless concerns standing aside and refraining, whereas tajānaf concerns inclining or departing from a direction.

Written similarity therefore does not make غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ morphologically identical to اجْتَنِبُوا الْإِثْمَ.

ج ن ف (جَنَف) and ض ر ر (ضَرَر)

Study of Ḍ R R

ضَرَر concerns harm or injury.

Janaf in a bequest can indicate deviation resulting in violated rights or someone being harmed.

Janaf itself does not mean every kind of harm.

Harm can occur without partiality or deliberate deviation.

Conversely, a decision can be unjust in its orientation before all its harmful effects materialize.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 prohibits causing harm in relation to bequests and debts.

That verse matters for studying bequest rights, but does not contain ج ن ف.

11. Questions of understanding and translation

Does janaf always mean an unintended error?

No.

Many early commentators explain it in Al-Baqarah 2:182 as unintended error contrasted with ithm.

Arabic janaf can nevertheless concern deliberate partiality and injustice.

Error in that verse is therefore a defensible contextual reading, not the root’s fixed meaning throughout Arabic.

Is janaf always injustice?

In legal usage it can concern injustice and departure from fairness.

Al-Aʿshā’s tajānaf, however, describes his camel’s change of direction.

Bodily أَجْنَف also concerns a bent frame.

Injustice is consequently not the constant meaning of all forms.

Is every difference in a bequest a sign of janaf?

No.

People may differ in needs, circumstances, and rights.

Fairness need not mean equal division in all situations.

Assessing janaf requires establishing an actual departure from right and maʿrūf.

Does correcting a bequest permit arbitrary alteration?

No.

Al-Baqarah 2:181 condemns wrongful alteration.

Al-Baqarah 2:182 permits correction when janaf or sin is feared.

Correction and self-serving manipulation therefore differ.

Does خَافَ establish certain proof of janaf?

The verb’s basic sense concerns apprehension; some commentators understand it here as knowledge or recognition of deviation.

Someone may recognize signs of deviation before harm occurs.

Apprehension must nevertheless not become a pretext for unsupported accusation.

Corrective action must therefore rest on genuine understanding and protecting rights.

Does مُتَجَانِفٍ in Al-Māʾidah mean a sinner?

Not by itself.

مُتَجَانِف describes someone inclining or deviating.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3 its object is لِإِثْمٍ; the combination establishes inclination toward sin.

The complement must not be omitted and the whole expression’s meaning assigned to the participle alone.

Does necessity permit any act without limits?

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 qualifies necessity with غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ.

Related passages also contain qualifications about not transgressing.

The Qur’an thus distinguishes genuine necessity from inclination toward sin.

That distinction must nevertheless not make saving someone in distress difficult through invented, impossible conditions.

Does tajānaf always mean inclination toward sin?

No.

Arabic تَجَانَفَ عَنْ can concern moving away from a course or direction.

Al-Aʿshā uses the verb for his camel’s movement.

In Al-Māʾidah, لِإِثْمٍ specifies the direction of inclination.

Sin’s meaning comes from the complement and context.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

For general جَنَف, “crookedness,” “inclination,” and “deviation” are suitable equivalents.

For bodily usage, “being bent” or “asymmetry between the body’s sides” may be clearer.

For a person described as أَجْنَف, context may support “bent,” “having a bend in bodily structure,” or ethically “departing from fairness.”

In judgment, جَانِف can mean “unfairly partial” or “departing from right.”

Legal مُجْنِف likewise describes someone who has departed from fairness.

Spatial تَجَانَفَ can be rendered “inclined,” “turned aside from the course,” or “changed direction.”

In conduct and intention, “inclined toward something” may be more precise.

Al-Baqarah 2:182

The expression:

فَمَنْ خَافَ مِن مُّوصٍ جَنَفًا أَوْ إِثْمًا

fa-man khāfa min mūṣin janafan aw ithman

The current Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English, says:

“If anyone fears deviation from right or sin from a testator…”

This rendering is appropriate.

“Deviation from right” makes janaf clear in the bequest context without prejudging whether it is deliberate or unintended.

Translating جَنَفًا only as “unintended error” narrows the lexical meaning.

Rendering it solely as “deliberate oppression” also removes the possibility of inadvertent error and deviation.

“Deviation from right” is therefore broader and more faithful.

Analysis can add that many early commentators distinguish error from deliberate sin in this context.

That explanation should not unnecessarily be inserted into the direct translation.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3

The expression:

غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ

ghayra mutajānifin li-ithmin

The current Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English, says:

“Without inclining toward sin.”

This rendering is also appropriate.

مُتَجَانِف concerns inclination.

لِإِثْمٍ specifies its direction.

“Without inclining toward sin” thus preserves the expression’s construction.

Analysis can explain that, in necessity, the qualification guards against knowingly misusing need and deliberately inclining toward sin.

The basic translation should nevertheless not directly turn مُتَجَانِف into “transgressor,” “glutton,” “pleasure-seeker,” or “someone falsely claiming necessity.”

Those are possible instances or interpretations, which should remain explanatory.

The existing Forqan translations of both Qur’anic occurrences are consequently precise and defensible concerning the root’s meaning.

13. Conclusion

The Arabic root ج ن ف concerns inclination, crookedness, and deviation.

Ibn Fāris identifies its semantic core as الْمَيْل.

It appears in bodily, spatial, and behavioral uses.

In bodily structure, janaf can describe bending or asymmetry between the sides.

In travel and movement, tajānaf can describe changing direction or leaving a previous course.

In judgment and human relationships, the family describes wrongful partiality and departure from right.

Early Arabic poetic witnesses show this breadth.

Al-Aʿshā uses tajānaf for his camel’s change of direction, not itself blameworthy in that context.

Poetry attributed to ʿĀmir al-Khaṣafī, Labīd, and Abū al-ʿIyāl uses janaf for unfair conduct in human relationships and dispute.

Inclination itself does not therefore always mean oppression: moral significance depends on the object and context.

The Qur’an uses the root only twice.

In Al-Baqarah 2:182, جَنَفًا indicates possible departure from right in a bequest.

Following the command to bequeath according to maʿrūf and the prohibition of wrongful alteration, the verse permits correction against the risk of janaf or sin.

Many early commentators explain janaf here as unintended error and ithm as deliberate injustice.

This contextual distinction must not become the root’s permanent definition: Arabic janaf can be deliberate or unintended.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, مُتَجَانِفٍ occurs in food-related necessity.

The verse concerns someone compelled by severe hunger who must not incline toward sin.

لِإِثْمٍ establishes the object of inclination.

Tajānaf here therefore concerns knowing inclination toward sin, although the verb does not have that meaning in every Arabic use.

Among related roots, ج ن ف approaches م ي ل in inclination, ح ي ف and ج و ر in unfairness, and ح ن ف in the general image of changing direction.

The roots are nevertheless not fully synonymous.

In particular, janaf in Al-Baqarah 2:182 and Qur’anic ḥanīf concerning Abraham’s way must not be equated merely because both involve inclination.

For Forqan, “deviation from right” appropriately renders جَنَفًا in Al-Baqarah 2:182.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, “without inclining toward sin” faithfully renders غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ.

The root’s semantic core is inclination and departure from alignment. One use may be bodily and neutral; another concerns unfair partiality; in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 it concerns inclination toward sin.

Preserving these differences allows janaf in bequests, possible error and injustice, fair correction, and not inclining toward sin in necessity to be examined without merging meanings or adding unwritten rulings to the root.
