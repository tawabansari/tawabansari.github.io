Sexual speech, marital intercourse, and their limits during fasting and pilgrimage

The Arabic root R F TH concerns speaking about sexual matters, expressing them explicitly and sometimes indecently, and sexual intercourse and conduct themselves. This word family denotes speech whose open expression was conventionally considered improper or embarrassing, and also sexual relations between spouses.

These uses are close but distinct. Speaking about sex is not intercourse itself, and marital intercourse need not involve offensive, degrading, or shameless speech.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times in Al-Baqarah, in different settings.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum concerns permission for marital intercourse on the nights of fasting.

In Al-Baqarah 2:197, fa-lā rafatha is one of three restrictions during pilgrimage, alongside fusūq and jidāl.

The meanings are related, but the rulings differ: one permits marital relations at a specified time, while the other prohibits rafath during pilgrimage.

Understanding the root requires distinguishing lexical meaning, construction, use in each verse, and rulings understood from the verses together.

Qur’anic forms: al-rafathu in Al-Baqarah 2:187 and rafatha in Al-Baqarah 2:197.

Both are forms of the noun rafath. Their different endings reflect grammatical roles rather than different roots or wholly independent meanings.

Arabic records rafatha, yarfuthu, rafitha, rafutha, rafth, rafath, rufūth, arfatha, irfāth, taraffatha, and taraffuth. Only the noun rafath occurs in the Qur’anic text examined here. The verb rafatha must not be confused with the noun bearing a fatḥah in Al-Baqarah 2:197.

1. Semantic core and early Arabic usage

Speech whose open expression was considered improper

One early definition of this root reads:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ كُلُّ كَلَامٍ يُسْتَحْيَا مِنْ إِظْهَارِهِ

aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa kullu kalāmin yustaḥyā min iẓhārihi

“A single principle: any speech that one is ashamed to express openly.”

The same explanation also includes nikāḥ and indecent speech among the family’s uses.

This definition shows that speech was an important domain of rafath.

But what is meant by speech considered improper to express openly?

Lexical usage particularly concerns sexual speech, references to intercourse, and explicit expression of matters ordinarily mentioned indirectly.

Rafath should therefore not mean simply any unpleasant statement or any word someone dislikes hearing.

Sexual subject matter is central to the root’s clear applications.

This definition alone does not establish that every explicit discussion of sexual matters is improper or prohibited in every setting.

Sexual matters may be discussed clearly and respectfully in private, educational, or medical conversations.

The historical meaning of rafath cannot establish an identical moral judgment for all these situations without examining context and purpose.

Al-rafath: sexual speech and intercourse itself

One explanation of the word’s uses states:

الرَّفَثُ: الْجِمَاعُ وَغَيْرُهُ مِمَّا يَكُونُ بَيْنَ الرَّجُلِ وَامْرَأَتِهِ

al-rafathu al-jimāʿu wa-ghayruhu mimmā yakūnu bayna al-rajuli wa-imraʾatihi

“Rafath is intercourse and other things occurring between a man and his wife.”

Kissing, flirtation, and other conduct associated with sexual intimacy are also mentioned in this explanation.

An explanation of the indirect usage’s origin adds:

وَأَصْلُهُ قَوْلُ الْفُحْشِ

wa-aṣluhu qawlu al-fuḥshi

“Its origin is indecent and explicit speech.”

Two domains of use appear together here:

First, speaking about sexual relations.

Second, sexual relations themselves and some associated conduct.

Another explanation connects rafath with speech about intercourse and its preliminaries whose explicit expression was conventionally considered improper.

The same explanation also identifies the word’s indirect use for intercourse.

Indirect expression is therefore significant in understanding this family.

A word may originally, or in one established use, denote speech about an act and then also name the act itself.

This should not become a definite historical claim that every use of rafath in all periods first concerned speech alone and only later shifted to intercourse.

Early definitions include nikāḥ among this root’s basic applications alongside embarrassing speech.

The evidence clearly establishes two uses in early Arabic: sexual speech and sexual intercourse.

Determining their exact historical priority requires more evidence.

Rafatha fī kalāmihi

Arabic uses rafatha fī kalāmihi of someone who employs indecent or explicit expressions in speech.

Arfatha is also recorded with a similar meaning.

One lexical explanation reads:

رَفَثَ يَرْفُثُ، وَأَرْفَثَ يُرْفِثُ، إِذَا أَفْحَشَ فِي شَأْنِ النِّسَاءِ

rafatha yarfuthu wa-arfatha yurfithu idhā afḥasha fī shaʾni al-nisāʾi

“Rafatha and arfatha are said when someone speaks indecently and explicitly concerning women.”

This definition shows that rafath can characterize speech.

In this use, no sexual relationship between the speaker and another person need have occurred.

The speech may merely concern a sexual subject.

Lexical study should therefore distinguish three situations:

Speaking about sexual relations, requesting or alluding to them, and engaging in them.

These may be connected, but they are distinct events.

Rafatha and arfatha

Several verbal forms are recorded, including:

رَفَثَ يَرْفُثُ

rafatha yarfuthu

“He spoke explicitly or indecently; he speaks.”

رَفِثَ يَرْفَثُ

rafitha yarfathu

“He spoke explicitly or indecently; he speaks.”

رَفُثَ يَرْفُثُ

rafutha yarfuthu

“He spoke explicitly or indecently; he speaks.”

Arfatha–yurfithu is also recorded as Form IV.

These uses involve speaking indecently or explicitly, particularly about sexual matters.

Lexical explanations distinguish rafatha and arfatha in verbal structure, while also reporting that their uses overlap and that they can substitute for one another.

The difference between the basic verb and Form IV should not be turned into two wholly unrelated meanings.

Neither of these verbs occurs in the Qur’an.

Both Qur’anic occurrences are the noun rafath.

Rafth, rafath, and rufūth

Rafth and rafath are recorded as verbal-noun or nominal forms in Arabic.

Rufūth is another form found in lexical records and reports of readings.

For Al-Baqarah 2:187, al-rufūth is reported in a reading attributed to ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd.

This report should not be equated with the familiar Qur’anic reading.

The Qur’anic text examined here has al-rafathu.

Lexical forms and reported readings deserve acknowledgment, but should not lead to altering the verse’s text.

2. Early Arabic poetry: rafath in pilgrims’ speech

One clear poetic example appears in the work of al-ʿAjjāj, a poet associated with the Umayyad period:

وَرُبَّ أَسْرَابِ حَجِيجٍ كُظَّمِ
عَنِ اللَّغَا وَرَفَثِ التَّكَلُّمِ

wa-rubba asrābi ḥajījin kuẓẓami / ʿani al-laghā wa-rafathi al-takallumi

“How many groups of pilgrims restrained their tongues from idle speech and indecent sexual talk.”

Several words matter in this verse.

Asrāb denotes groups.

Ḥajīj means pilgrims.

Kuẓẓam describes those who restrain their speech and refrain from saying something.

Al-laghā concerns idle or inappropriate speech.

Rafathi al-takallum denotes speech of the rafath kind.

The example is significant because rafath is explicitly joined to takallum, speaking.

The reference here is therefore to indecent or explicit speech, rather than intercourse itself.

The poet also places laghā and rafath together.

They can belong to a common sphere of behavior without having identical meanings.

Laghā concerns speech’s futility or inappropriateness; rafath here highlights its explicit sexual aspect.

Historical position of the example

These two lines are preserved in al-ʿAjjāj’s collected poetry and lexical quotations.

Al-ʿAjjāj lived through pre-Islamic and Islamic times into the Umayyad period. This particular example is not independently dated.

It should therefore not be labeled pre-Islamic without dating evidence.

Its value lies in demonstrating an established early Arabic usage, not in proving these particular lines predate the Qur’an.

The examples examined here provide no securely attributed and dated pre-Islamic poem that could serve as an unqualified foundation for this root’s study.

The poet’s lifespan should therefore be distinguished from the example’s unknown date, avoiding unsupported attribution of anonymous or uncertain poetry to pre-Islamic poets.

Rafath in another example

Lexical and exegetical writings also quote the following verse:

وَيُرَيْنَ مِنْ أُنْسِ الْحَدِيثِ زَوَانِيًا
وَلَهُنَّ عَنْ رَفَثِ الرِّجَالِ نِفَارُ

wa-yurayna min unsi al-ḥadīthi zawāniyan / wa-lahunna ʿan rafathi al-rijāli nifāru

“In pleasant conversation they appear inclined toward affection, yet they recoil from men’s sexual advances.”

This verse describes pleasant conversation alongside women’s response to men’s sexual advances.

Rafathi al-rijāl here concerns men’s sexual conduct or requests.

The usage may extend beyond speech alone to sexual approaches or their preliminaries.

The examined records do not establish a definite attribution to a particular poet.

It must not be presented as pre-Islamic evidence with an invented author.

The example establishes a use connecting rafath with sexual conduct and approaching another person.

Does poetry settle the Qur’anic meaning?

Poetry and dictionaries are important for understanding Arabic’s expressive possibilities.

A word may have one meaning in poetry and another established use from the same family in a Qur’anic verse.

For example, rafathi al-takallum in al-ʿAjjāj’s poem concerns speech.

Al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum in Al-Baqarah 2:187, however, occurs in the context of marital intercourse.

Al-ʿAjjāj’s poem alone cannot establish that Al-Baqarah 2:187 merely permits talking about sexual relations.

Each occurrence must be understood through its own construction and context.

3. “Intercourse with your wives is permitted for you on the night of fasting”: Al-Baqarah 2:187

The first Qur’anic occurrence reads:

أُحِلَّ لَكُمْ لَيْلَةَ الصِّيَامِ الرَّفَثُ إِلَىٰ نِسَائِكُمْ هُنَّ لِبَاسٌ لَكُمْ وَأَنْتُمْ لِبَاسٌ لَهُنَّ

uḥilla lakum laylata al-ṣiyāmi al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum hunna libāsun lakum wa-antum libāsun lahunna

Al-Baqarah 2:187

“Intercourse with your wives is permitted for you on the night of fasting. They are clothing for you, and you are clothing for them.”

Rafath here occurs in the context of marital relations.

Its precise meaning requires attention to the construction and the continuation of the verse.

The grammar of al-rafathu

Al-rafathu is definite and nominative here.

Uḥilla is passive.

In the usual grammatical analysis, al-rafathu is its passive subject: rafath is what is declared permissible.

Lakum identifies those addressed by this permission.

Laylata al-ṣiyām specifies its time.

The opening thus permits an act at a specified time.

Rafath itself does not lexically mean permissibility.

Permission comes from uḥilla.

Rafath identifies the act to which that permission applies.

The significance of ilā nisāʾikum

The Arabic construction al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum is significant.

If the word meant only indecent speech, one might expect a construction referring to rafath in speech or with another person.

The Qur’an instead uses al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum.

Lexical explanations connect ilā here with approaching and ifḍāʾ.

In this analysis, rafath incorporates the sense of ifḍāʾ, explaining its combination with ilā.

In the context of spouses, ifḍāʾ concerns intimacy and private bodily relations.

Ilā nisāʾikum is therefore an important indication of marital intercourse.

The preposition ilā alone should not be made to supply every detail of sexual relations.

It shows the relation and direction expressed in the construction; the meaning emerges from the whole phrase and the continuation of the verse.

Fa-al-āna bāshirūhunna

The same verse continues:

فَالْآنَ بَاشِرُوهُنَّ وَابْتَغُوا مَا كَتَبَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ

fa-al-āna bāshirūhunna wa-ibtaghū mā kataba Allāhu lakum

Al-Baqarah 2:187

“Now have intimate relations with them, and seek what The God has prescribed for you.”

This clarifies al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum.

Bāshirūhunna comes from B SH R, one use of which concerns skin contact and bodily intimacy.

In this verse, mubāsharah concerns sexual relations between spouses.

The verse therefore does not merely permit speaking about sexual matters at night.

It permits marital relations.

This agrees with the opening uḥilla … al-rafathu.

Early explanations also understand rafath here as an indirect designation for intercourse.

This conclusion can also be examined through the Qur’an’s context, without requiring an external report as an independent source of the ruling.

4. Does rafath in Al-Baqarah 2:187 mean offensive speech?

The question matters because lexical explanations include qawl al-fuḥsh among the word’s main meanings.

But this verse declares rafath with spouses permissible on the night of fasting.

It continues by describing spouses as clothing for one another and permitting intimate relations.

Its direct meaning cannot therefore be reduced to permission for offensive or insulting speech.

Lexical usage and moral judgment

An Arabic word may concern indecent speech in one use and designate a legitimate relationship in another.

This does not transfer every characteristic of the first use to the second.

Here the Qur’an permits marital relations on the night of fasting.

Rafath’s association with fuḥsh in some lexical uses does not mean that the Qur’an declares intercourse between spouses inherently indecent.

That conclusion does not fit the verse’s wording.

The Qur’an says:

هُنَّ لِبَاسٌ لَكُمْ وَأَنْتُمْ لِبَاسٌ لَهُنَّ

hunna libāsun lakum wa-antum libāsun lahunna

Al-Baqarah 2:187

“They are clothing for you, and you are clothing for them.”

This describes a close and reciprocal relationship.

Al-Rūm 30:21 also speaks of tranquility, affection, and mercy between spouses.

The marital bond should therefore not be equated with shamelessness or moral corruption on the basis of one use of rafath.

Rafath and the range of sexual conduct

Besides intercourse, lexical records use rafath for related conduct such as kissing, flirtation, and sexual speech.

Its semantic range is broader than one particular bodily act.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, however, marital intercourse is the clearest intended instance.

Which other acts fall within the verse’s temporal ruling requires careful examination of this verse and other relevant verses.

A lexical list alone cannot establish identical rulings for every affectionate and bodily interaction between spouses.

Permission for intercourse on the night of fasting also does not imply permission for harmful, coercive, or rights-violating conduct.

The verse permits marital relations at a specified time; it does not negate spouses’ mutual rights and agency.

5. The timing of fasting and the place of rafath

Al-Baqarah 2:187 permits rafath with spouses during laylata al-ṣiyām.

It then says concerning eating and drinking:

وَكُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَكُمُ الْخَيْطُ الْأَبْيَضُ مِنَ الْخَيْطِ الْأَسْوَدِ مِنَ الْفَجْرِ

wa-kulū wa-ishrabū ḥattā yatabayyana lakumu al-khayṭu al-abyaḍu mina al-khayṭi al-aswadi mina al-fajri

Al-Baqarah 2:187

“Eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinguishable to you from the black thread.”

Then it says:

ثُمَّ أَتِمُّوا الصِّيَامَ إِلَى اللَّيْلِ

thumma atimmū al-ṣiyāma ilā al-layli

Al-Baqarah 2:187

“Then complete the fasting until night.”

This sequence distinguishes daytime abstention from the time when certain acts are permitted.

Forqan’s published translation and reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:187 likewise understand the nighttime permission for rafath as marital intercourse.

Does rafath mean fasting?

No.

Ṣiyām comes from Ṣ W M and concerns abstaining and refraining.

Study of Ṣ W M

Rafath comes from R F TH and here concerns marital relations.

They appear within the same set of rulings but are not one concept.

The verse specifies the limits of abstention in fasting.

Ṣiyām should not be lexically reduced to abstaining from intercourse, just as rafath must not mean abstaining from food and drink.

Each word has an independent role.

Wa-lā tubāshirūhunna wa-antum ʿākifūna fī al-masājid

Toward the end of the verse:

وَلَا تُبَاشِرُوهُنَّ وَأَنْتُمْ عَاكِفُونَ فِي الْمَسَاجِدِ

wa-lā tubāshirūhunna wa-antum ʿākifūna fī al-masājidi

Al-Baqarah 2:187

“Do not have intimate relations with them while you are in retreat in the mosques.”

This expresses another restriction.

The beginning permits marital relations on the night of fasting.

The ending separately addresses intimate relations during retreat in the mosques.

This distinction matters.

General nighttime permission does not establish that the same permission applies without exception in every situation.

Nor should the restriction during retreat become a permanent prohibition of every affectionate relationship between spouses.

The subject is intimate relations in the specific situation of retreat.

The ruling’s limits must be understood through the Qur’an’s stated words and conditions.

6. “No rafath, disobedience, or disputing during pilgrimage”: Al-Baqarah 2:197

The root’s second Qur’anic occurrence reads:

الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ فَمَنْ فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ يَعْلَمْهُ اللَّهُ وَتَزَوَّدُوا فَإِنَّ خَيْرَ الزَّادِ التَّقْوَىٰ وَاتَّقُونِ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ

al-ḥajju ashhurun maʿlūmātun fa-man faraḍa fīhinna al-ḥajja fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa wa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajji wa-mā tafʿalū min khayrin yaʿlamhu Allāhu wa-tazawwadū fa-inna khayra al-zādi al-taqwā wa-ittaqūni yā ulī al-albābi

Al-Baqarah 2:197

“Pilgrimage is in known months. Whoever undertakes pilgrimage in them, there shall be no rafath, disobedience, or disputing during pilgrimage. Whatever good you do, The God knows it. Take provisions, for the best provision is taqwā; and be mindful of Me, people of understanding.”

Rafath appears alongside two other forms of conduct.

The context concerns behavioral restrictions during pilgrimage.

The grammar of fa-lā rafatha

In the reading of Ḥafṣ, the wording is:

فَلَا رَفَثَ

fa-lā rafatha

Al-Baqarah 2:197

“There shall be no rafath.”

Lā here negates the whole category.

Rafatha is its noun, built on fatḥah without nunation.

The negative construction expresses prohibition.

The verse is not reporting that no pilgrim ever engages in rafath.

Within the pilgrimage rulings, it requires refraining from rafath.

Another form also occurs in recognized readings.

Ibn Kathīr and Abū ʿAmr read rafathun and fusūqun with nominative endings and nunation, while jidāla retains its fatḥah in the same construction.

The reading difference matters grammatically but does not change the root’s basic meaning.

In all these readings, rafath belongs to the same semantic family concerning sexual speech and conduct.

Does rafath during pilgrimage mean intercourse?

Early interpretations offer two main explanations.

One understands rafath here as sexual intercourse.

Another includes intercourse, explicit sexual speech, sexual allusions, and some associated conduct.

One early analysis favors the broader meaning: because Arabic records several close uses, the word should not be restricted to just one without clear evidence.

This interpretive difference demonstrates the word’s semantic range; it does not automatically make every ordinary discussion of family or sexual matters prohibited rafath.

Connection with Al-Baqarah 2:187

Al-Baqarah 2:187 uses this same root for marital intercourse on the night of fasting.

This is important evidence for understanding fa-lā rafatha during pilgrimage.

Restricting the pilgrimage verse to nonsexual offensive speech and excluding intercourse would overlook the clear connection between the two Qur’anic uses.

Conversely, restricting rafath during pilgrimage to intercourse alone and denying its lexical capacity for related sexual speech and conduct would discard part of its established range.

A more precise reading is therefore: sexual intercourse is a principal and clear instance of rafath here, while the Arabic word can also encompass associated sexual speech and conduct.

The prohibition’s exact scope requires attention to the verse and linguistic evidence.

7. Does fa-lā rafatha prohibit every discussion of sexual relations?

This question arises from the root’s speech-related meaning.

Arabic rafath can denote explicit and indecent expression of sexual matters.

But sexually provocative speech, offensive or insulting speech, and respectful explanation of a human subject are distinct.

For example, discussing a family or medical matter is not necessarily sexual flirtation or a request for intercourse.

Respectful discussion between spouses about their shared rights and responsibilities should not be classified as prohibited rafath during pilgrimage without evidence.

The word alone does not establish that every sexual reference, whatever its purpose and manner, carries the same ruling.

Conversely, its established meaning involving sexual speech cannot be removed entirely.

Rafath is not only a name for a bodily act; it can encompass related speech and allusion.

Judging particular instances thus requires attention to the kind of speech, audience, purpose, and connection with sexual conduct.

Pilgrimage restrictions and married life

Al-Baqarah 2:197 limits the prohibition through this phrase:

فِي الْحَجِّ

fī al-ḥajji

Al-Baqarah 2:197

“During pilgrimage.”

The verse concerns the state of pilgrimage.

This restriction must not become a permanent rule for all married life.

Al-ḥajju ashhurun maʿlūmātun also places pilgrimage in known months.

That does not mean every person is subject to every pilgrimage restriction throughout those months, even before undertaking pilgrimage.

The verse itself says:

فَمَنْ فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ

fa-man faraḍa fīhinna al-ḥajja

Al-Baqarah 2:197

“Whoever undertakes pilgrimage in them.”

The pilgrimage months must therefore be distinguished from the situation of someone who has undertaken and is performing pilgrimage.

The exact beginning and end of each restriction belong to a separate study of pilgrimage rulings; the root R F TH alone does not determine them.

8. Rafath, fusūq, and jidāl: three different concepts

Al-Baqarah 2:197 places three words together:

فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ

fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa wa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajji

Al-Baqarah 2:197

“There shall be no rafath, disobedience, or disputing during pilgrimage.”

Their juxtaposition must not merge three roots into one meaning.

Rafath: sexual speech and conduct

As discussed, rafath concerns intercourse, sexual speech, and some associated conduct.

The pilgrimage context prohibits conduct in this domain.

Fusūq: departing from proper limits

Fusūq comes from F S Q and in Qur’anic usage concerns departure from proper limits and disobedience.

Study of F S Q

Here it stands alongside rafath.

Rafath itself should therefore not mean every kind of fusūq.

Conduct can be morally wrong without being lexically connected to sexual matters.

It cannot be called rafath merely because it is improper.

Jidāl: argument and contention

Jidāl comes from J D L and has different Qur’anic applications.

Study of J D L

Its prohibition during pilgrimage should be understood in relation to peace, taqwā, and the circumstances of this gathering.

Jidāl is not necessarily improper in every Qur’anic use.

Al-Naḥl 16:125 commands argument in the best manner.

The pilgrimage prohibition should therefore not imply that every question, scholarly conversation, or examination of disagreement is forbidden in all circumstances.

Likewise, rafath must not become a term for every unpleasant or conflict-provoking statement.

Each root has its own meaning.

Why do the three appear together?

In pilgrimage, these three behaviors are connected with mindfulness and observing limits.

The Qur’an associates pilgrimage with remembering The God, the House’s security, people’s gathering, and benefits for them.

In this setting, restraining sexual conduct, avoiding disobedience, and refraining from inappropriate contention can preserve pilgrimage’s purpose and sanctity.

This conclusion comes from the verses together and the pilgrimage context.

Rafath itself does not mean security, taqwā, or peace.

9. Rafath and related Qur’anic words

R F TH (rafath) and F Ḥ SH (fuḥsh)

Study of F Ḥ SH

Fuḥsh and fāḥishah concern gross impropriety in conduct or speech.

Lexical explanations sometimes define rafath through indecent speech.

The two roots are not complete synonyms.

Fuḥsh can apply to various domains of improper conduct and speech.

Rafath in its clear applications is more closely connected with sexual matters.

Not every improper act should therefore be called rafath.

Their semantic proximity also does not make legitimate marital intercourse, permitted in Al-Baqarah 2:187, inherently a fāḥishah.

The verse does not support that judgment.

R F TH (rafath) and B SH R (mubāsharah)

Study of B SH R

Mubāsharah comes from B SH R and in its basic application concerns direct contact and bodily intimacy.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 places al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum and fa-al-āna bāshirūhunna in one context.

Their association shows that mubāsharah clarifies the marital meaning in this verse.

B SH R and R F TH are nevertheless lexically distinct.

In different uses, mubāsharah can refer to contact or directly undertaking something.

Rafath belongs to the sphere of sexual speech and conduct.

Their overlap in one verse does not make all uses of the roots identical.

R F TH (rafath) and N K Ḥ (nikāḥ)

Study of N K Ḥ

Arabic nikāḥ concerns marriage and, in some early uses, intercourse.

Lexical explanations also use nikāḥ to explain rafath.

The marriage bond itself must nevertheless be distinguished from sexual relations.

Many Qur’anic occurrences of nikāḥ concern marriage and its legal relationships.

By contrast, al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum in Al-Baqarah 2:187 denotes intercourse with spouses.

Rafath here must not mean contracting a new marriage on the night of fasting.

The verse already identifies the relationship through nisāʾikum, your wives.

Its subject is marital relations, not establishing the marriage bond itself.

R F TH (rafath) and F Ḍ W (ifḍāʾ)

Afḍā is Form IV of F Ḍ W. One Qur’anic use concerns the private intimacy between spouses.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:21 says:

وَقَدْ أَفْضَىٰ بَعْضُكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ

wa-qad afḍā baʿḍukum ilā baʿḍin

Al-Nisāʾ 4:21

“When you have already been intimate with one another.”

This is semantically connected with al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum.

Lexical explanations likewise explain rafath’s combination with ilā through the meaning of ifḍāʾ.

But the words come from different roots.

Ifḍāʾ can emphasize approaching and reaching another person, while rafath concerns sexual speech and conduct.

All the meanings of one must not be transferred to the other.

R F TH (rafath) and L GH W (laghw)

Study of L GH W

Al-ʿAjjāj’s poem places laghā and rafath together.

Laghw in the Qur’an also concerns idle and fruitless speech or conduct.

For example:

وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ عَنِ اللَّغْوِ مُعْرِضُونَ

wa-alladhīna hum ʿani al-laghwi muʿriḍūna

Al-Muʾminūn 23:3

“And those who turn away from idle matters.”

Laghw and rafath are not identical in meaning.

Speech may be idle without any sexual connection.

A conversation about sexual relations may also have a clear and useful purpose in an appropriate setting.

Rafath should not mean all futility, nor laghw all sexual speech.

10. Explicit speech and respectful scholarly discussion

One difficulty in translating rafath into Persian is that some equivalents are too broad.

For example, “shamelessness” may describe any statement or behavior that a society or individual dislikes.

Social standards for discussing sexual matters differ across times and cultures.

“Intercourse,” by contrast, expresses only one established use of the rafath family.

Neither equivalent alone covers the root’s entire semantic range in every use.

Explicitness does not always mean impropriety

Some Arabic lexical explanations use qawl al-fuḥsh to explain rafath.

This does not establish that every clear reference to the body, marriage, or sexual relations is offensive in every setting.

Clear expression may be necessary for education, treatment, protecting rights, or explaining a ruling.

Manner, purpose, and audience must be distinguished in such situations.

Conversely, someone may use indirect language to demean, harass, or inappropriately provoke another person.

A statement’s impropriety cannot be determined solely by whether its words are explicit or indirect.

This matters for understanding rafath during pilgrimage: restricting sexual conduct and related speech should not become an unsupported prohibition of every respectful conversation about necessary matters of life.

Legitimate relations and the sanctity of time

Al-Baqarah 2:187 permits marital intercourse on the night of fasting.

Al-Baqarah 2:197 prohibits rafath during pilgrimage.

An act can thus be legitimate in the underlying relationship while restricted in a particular devotional state.

A temporal or ritual restriction does not imply that the relationship itself is inherently indecent.

Conversely, a relationship’s legitimacy at one time does not eliminate every limit or moral responsibility in its conduct.

Both verses require distinguishing the act itself, its time, and the circumstances in which it occurs.

11. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Translation must distinguish the root’s general meaning from each verse’s particular use.

Rafatha: spoke explicitly or indecently about sexual matters; in some uses, engaged in sexual relations.

Yarfuthu: employs explicit or indecent sexual speech; according to context, engages in conduct within the domain of rafath.

Rafth: explicit sexual speech; in some uses, the name of a sexual act or behavior.

Rafath: sexual speech or conduct; in the context of spouses, marital intercourse.

Rufūth: a recorded form of this family with a meaning close to rafath.

Arfatha: engaged in explicit or indecent sexual speech.

Irfāth: speaking in the manner of rafath; a speech-related use concerning sexual matters.

Taraffatha: engaged in speech or conduct of the rafath kind, in recorded Arabic usage.

Taraffuth: engaging in rafath; in speech-related use, explicit sexual talk.

Al-Baqarah 2:187

أُحِلَّ لَكُمْ لَيْلَةَ الصِّيَامِ الرَّفَثُ إِلَىٰ نِسَائِكُمْ

uḥilla lakum laylata al-ṣiyāmi al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum

Al-Baqarah 2:187

A suitable rendering:

“Intercourse with your wives is permitted for you on the night of fasting.”

This fits the construction, rafath’s combination with ilā nisāʾikum, and the continuation fa-al-āna bāshirūhunna.

“Offensive speech to your wives is permitted” would lose the verse’s principal intended meaning.

“Marital relations,” though natural, can have a broader meaning than intercourse in Persian.

“Intercourse with spouses” is therefore more precise for this passage.

Al-Baqarah 2:197

فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ

fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa wa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajji

Al-Baqarah 2:197

Forqan’s existing rendering:

“During pilgrimage there shall be no rafath, fusūq, or jidāl.”

Retaining rafath avoids prematurely restricting it to one instance.

For a reader unfamiliar with Arabic, however, its meaning needs explanation.

A suitable brief explanation is:

“Rafath: sexual intercourse and associated sexual speech or conduct; here, one of the restrictions during pilgrimage.”

For a rendering that translates rafath into Persian, one may say:

“During pilgrimage there shall be no intercourse or sexual conduct, no fusūq, and no jidāl.”

This makes the sexual-speech component less explicit.

Conversely, “no abusive speech” is unsuitable because it can exclude intercourse and sexual conduct, which are basic applications of the root.

Retaining rafath with precise explanation is therefore defensible.

12. Conclusion

Arabic R F TH concerns sexual speech, explicit and sometimes indecent expression of matters relating to intercourse, and sexual relations themselves.

Early lexical explanations describe rafath through indecent speech, intercourse, and some associated conduct.

These belong to one semantic domain but must not be substituted for each other without an indication.

Al-ʿAjjāj’s poetry clearly demonstrates the speech-related use:

“ʿAni al-laghā wa-rafathi al-takallum”: “from idle talk and indecent sexual speech.”

The poet lived into the Umayyad period, but this particular poem is undated and must not be confidently presented as pre-Islamic.

Other examples concern rafath as sexual conduct and intimacy, though some lack clear historical attribution.

Root study must rely on verifiable usage and avoid inventing pre-Qur’anic evidence.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum concerns permission for marital intercourse on the night of fasting.

Fa-al-āna bāshirūhunna and other parts of the verse support this meaning.

Rafath here must not be translated merely as offensive speech, since the subject is actual relations between spouses.

The word’s association with indecent speech in some settings does not make legitimate marital intercourse inherently indecent.

The same verse describes spouses as clothing for one another, expressing intimacy and a reciprocal bond.

In Al-Baqarah 2:197, fa-lā rafatha is one of three pilgrimage restrictions alongside fusūq and jidāl.

Intercourse is a clear instance of rafath here; the word’s lexical range also permits associated sexual speech and conduct.

This range does not make every respectful or necessary discussion of sexual matters prohibited rafath regardless of purpose and circumstances.

The pilgrimage restriction must not become a permanent ruling about marital relations.

Together, the two verses show that the kind of conduct, its time, and its circumstances must be distinguished.

Distinctions also matter when comparing related roots.

Fuḥsh concerns gross impropriety; mubāsharah, intimacy and direct contact; nikāḥ, marriage and in some uses intercourse; ifḍāʾ, private intimacy; and laghw, futility.

These concepts may relate to rafath in some situations, but none is its complete and permanent synonym.

Two reductions must therefore be avoided: making rafath any offensive speech without its sexual context, and restricting every use to intercourse while ignoring its speech-related range.

Final definition: Arabic R F TH denotes explicit or indecent sexual speech, intercourse, and associated conduct. In the Qur’an, al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum in Al-Baqarah 2:187 refers to marital intercourse permitted on the night of fasting; fa-lā rafatha in Al-Baqarah 2:197 prohibits rafath during pilgrimage. Precise meaning, the scope of conduct covered by the ruling, and the distinction between speech and action must be determined from each verse’s construction and context rather than one narrow, fixed equivalent for every use.
