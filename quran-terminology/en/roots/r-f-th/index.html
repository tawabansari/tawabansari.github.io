1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The triliteral Arabic root R F TH concerns explicit speech about sexual matters, especially speech regarded as indecent or shameful in customary language, as well as sexual intercourse and related conduct.

This root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times in Al-Baqarah and both as the noun rafath. In Al-Baqarah 2:187, al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum occurs in the context of permitting marital intimacy during fasting nights. In Al-Baqarah 2:197, fa-lā rafatha appears alongside fusūq and jidāl among the restrictions of pilgrimage.

The two occurrences belong to one semantic family but play different roles in their sentences. The first permits the relationship; the second prohibits rafath during pilgrimage.

Important forms in this family include:

  • رَفَث (rafath): explicit, indecent sexual speech; more broadly, sexual intercourse and related conduct. Qur’anic use: yes, twice, in Al-Baqarah 2:187 and Al-Baqarah 2:197.
  • رَفَثَ / يَرْفُثُ (rafatha / yarfuthu): to utter indecent sexual speech or, in an appropriate context, engage in sexual conduct. Qur’anic use: no, as a verb.
  • رَفِثَ / يَرْفَثُ (rafitha / yarfathu): another verbal form recorded in lexical sources. Qur’anic use: no.
  • رَفُثَ (rafutha): another verbal form for speaking indecently, recorded in some lexical accounts. Qur’anic use: no.
  • أَرْفَثَ / يُرْفِثُ (arfatha / yurfithu): to utter or engage in indecent sexual speech. Qur’anic use: no.
  • رُفُوث (rufūth): a verbal noun associated with rafath and indecent speech. Qur’anic use: no.
  • رَافَثَ / مُرَافَثَة (rāfatha / murāfathah): to engage another person in sexual or indecent speech. Qur’anic use: no.
  • تَرَفَّثَ (taraffatha): to engage in rafath or utter indecent speech. Qur’anic use: no.
  • تَرَافَثَ (tarāfatha): to exchange sexual or indecent speech. Qur’anic use: no.

No verb from this root occurs in the Qur’an. Nor does rafath function in the Qur’anic text as an adjective describing a person or an independent designation for a human group.

The Qur’anic study therefore rests on these two nominal occurrences. Non-Qur’anic forms help explain the wider Arabic language, but must not be presented as vocabulary actually used in the Qur’an.

2. The linguistic core: sexual speech and intercourse

Speech ordinarily expressed indirectly

Arabic lexical sources record al-rafath for speech that a person would ordinarily be ashamed to utter openly, especially speech about sexual intercourse and associated matters.

Some older definitions explain it as al-fuḥshu mina al-qawl: indecent or unreserved speech.

Fuḥsh in this explanation nevertheless covers a wide field of objectionable speech, while rafath in its familiar use is particularly associated with sexual matters.

Every objectionable statement or insult should therefore not be called rafath without supporting context.

Insulting someone about wealth, lineage, or an incapacity, for example, may be ugly speech without necessarily falling within rafath’s specific semantic field.

Speech about intercourse expressed in an explicit, sexually provocative, or indecent manner has a more direct connection with this root’s familiar usage.

A distinction must nevertheless be maintained between the sexual subject of speech and an indecent manner of expression.

Someone may speak respectfully and necessarily about marriage, pregnancy, sexual health, or marital relations. A sexual subject alone does not establish that speech is rafath in the indecent, objectionable sense.

Older usage requires attention to the manner of speaking, the audience, the situation, and the purpose.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Miṣbāḥ, and Al-Mufradāt: sexual speech and intercourse

From sexual speech to intercourse itself

In Arabic, rafath has not remained limited to speech.

It also denotes intercourse and related conduct, including intercourse itself, sexually provocative conversation between spouses, and some behavior associated with sexual intimacy.

This semantic extension is understandable: a language may use a word associated with speaking about an action as an indirect expression for the action itself.

Speech and action are then related but remain distinct.

A person may speak about intercourse without performing it. Intercourse can likewise occur without indecent speech.

Two fields must therefore be retained: rafath in speech, and rafath as an indirect expression or designation for intercourse.

The context of Al-Baqarah 2:187 clearly foregrounds the second meaning.

In Al-Baqarah 2:197, both fields matter when examining the prohibition’s scope. Neither should be declared the only possible meaning while dismissing the other without argument.

Does rafath inherently mean an unlawful act?

No.

The Qur’an itself makes this clear.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, The God declares al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum lawful during the fasting night.

The word rafath therefore cannot always denote an unlawful act or an inherent sin.

In some linguistic uses it can carry a negative connotation, especially when denoting indecent speech. In the context of marital intercourse, however, its legal status depends on the ruling and circumstances of that relationship.

A relationship lawful in one situation may face temporal or ritual restrictions in another.

The Qur’an clearly illustrates this difference between fasting nights and pilgrimage.

Thus an action’s lexical meaning is not identical to its ruling under every circumstance.

3. Older Arabic evidence and its limits

Rafath in the poetry of al-ʿAjjāj

One clear poetic witness occurs in al-ʿAjjāj’s verse:

وَرُبَّ أَسْرَابِ حَجِيجٍ كُظَّمِ
عَنِ اللَّغَا وَرَفَثِ التَّكَلُّمِ

wa-rubba asrābi ḥajījin kuẓẓami / ʿani al-laghā wa-rafathi al-takallumi

English translation

And many a company of pilgrims restraining their tongues from idle speech and indecent sexual talk.

Rafathi al-takallumi explicitly concerns speech here.

The poet describes groups of pilgrims refraining from al-laghā and rafath in their speech.

Al-laghā and rafath stand together but are not complete synonyms.

Laghā concerns idle or inappropriate talk, while rafath here highlights indecent speech with a sexual subject.

The line directly shows that rafath does not mean intercourse alone.

The phrase rafath al-takallum is particularly significant: the construction specifically locates rafath in language and speech.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: transmission of al-ʿAjjāj’s witness

Al-ʿAjjāj lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, his life extending into the Umayyad era. This particular witness is not independently dated, however, so we do not present it as securely pre-Qur’anic.

Umm Al-Qura University poetry encyclopedia: al-ʿAjjāj’s life

The material examined did not yield a clear, reliable pre-Qur’anic poetic witness with securely established date and attribution. A line should not be invented to fill this section, nor should later evidence be labeled pre-Islamic.

Recorded Arabic expressions

Lexical sources record expressions such as rafatha fī kalāmihi for speaking indecently.

Rafatha ilā imraʾatihi, by comparison, is discussed in the field of intimacy and intercourse.

The difference in preposition matters.

Fī kalāmihi places the matter within speech; ilā imraʾatihi expresses a directed relationship with another person and can foreground approaching one’s wife in marital intercourse.

Arabic also records forms for sexual interaction or an exchange of sexual speech.

Each non-Qur’anic form must nevertheless be examined on its own terms. A verb’s presence in a later dictionary does not itself prove that the same form and meaning were common before the Qur’an or at its revelation.

Asās al-Balāghah: rāfatha, taraffatha, and tarāfatha

Is rafath always inappropriate speech?

Lexical explanations often connect rafath with speech ordinarily expressed indirectly.

This should not become a rule declaring every explicit discussion of intercourse immoral in every setting.

Necessary conversation between spouses, medical discussion, explaining marital rights, and stating rulings on intercourse may be explicit in subject. Explicitness alone does not establish vulgarity or violation of another person’s dignity.

The Qur’an itself discusses marital relations and bodily matters.

A statement’s sexual subject and the indecency of its expression or conduct are therefore separate questions.

This boundary matters when studying R F TH, especially the prohibition of rafath in pilgrimage.

4. Al-Baqarah 2:187: permitting rafath during the fasting night

Verse text

أُحِلَّ لَكُمْ لَيْلَةَ الصِّيَامِ الرَّفَثُ إِلَىٰ نِسَائِكُمْ هُنَّ لِبَاسٌ لَكُمْ وَأَنْتُمْ لِبَاسٌ لَهُنَّ عَلِمَ اللَّهُ أَنَّكُمْ كُنْتُمْ تَخْتَانُونَ أَنْفُسَكُمْ فَتَابَ عَلَيْكُمْ وَعَفَا عَنْكُمْ فَالْآنَ بَاشِرُوهُنَّ وَابْتَغُوا مَا كَتَبَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ وَكُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَكُمُ الْخَيْطُ الْأَبْيَضُ مِنَ الْخَيْطِ الْأَسْوَدِ مِنَ الْفَجْرِ ثُمَّ أَتِمُّوا الصِّيَامَ إِلَى اللَّيْلِ وَلَا تُبَاشِرُوهُنَّ وَأَنْتُمْ عَاكِفُونَ فِي الْمَسَاجِدِ تِلْكَ حُدُودُ اللَّهِ فَلَا تَقْرَبُوهَا كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ آيَاتِهِ لِلنَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَّقُونَ

uḥilla lakum laylata al-ṣiyāmi al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum; hunna libāsun lakum wa-antum libāsun lahunna; ʿalima Allāhu annakum kuntum takhtānūna anfusakum fa-tāba ʿalaykum wa-ʿafā ʿankum; fa-l-āna bāshirūhunna wa-btaghū mā kataba Allāhu lakum; wa-kulū wa-shrabū ḥattā yatabayyana lakumu al-khayṭu al-abyaḍu mina al-khayṭi al-aswadi mina al-fajri; thumma atimmū al-ṣiyāma ilā al-layli; wa-lā tubāshirūhunna wa-antum ʿākifūna fī al-masājidi; tilka ḥudūdu Allāhi fa-lā taqrabūhā; kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu āyātihi li-l-nāsi laʿallahum yattaqūna

English translation

Sexual intimacy with your wives has been made lawful for you during the fasting night. They are a covering for you, and you are a covering for them. The God knew that you were betraying yourselves, so He turned toward you and pardoned you. Now have intercourse with them and seek what The God has prescribed for you. Eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct to you from the black thread; then complete the fast until night. Do not have intercourse with them while you are in retreat in the mosques. These are the boundaries of The God, so do not approach them. Thus The God makes His signs clear to people, so that they may be mindful.

Why does rafath denote intercourse here?

The main expression reads:

“Sexual intimacy with your wives has been made lawful for you during the fasting night.”

Al-rafathu is definite and grammatically the subject of the passive verb uḥilla, “has been made lawful.”

Laylata al-ṣiyāmi is an adverbial expression of time; ilā nisāʾikum connects the subject to the addressees’ wives.

Rafath considered in isolation could raise the meanings of sexual speech or intercourse. Several features of this sentence, however, foreground intercourse.

First is ilā nisāʾikum. Here ilā is associated with approaching and turning toward the other person.

Second is “They are a covering for you, and you are a covering for them,” which describes the spouses’ intimacy and reciprocal relationship.

Third is the subsequent verb bāshirūhunna.

Fourth is the prohibition “Do not have intercourse with them while you are in retreat in the mosques,” which restricts this relationship during retreat.

Together, these features show that the verse concerns intercourse and marital intimacy itself, rather than merely permitting discussion of sexual matters at night.

The role of ilā

When rafath means indecent speech, Arabic can use constructions such as rafatha fī kalāmihi.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 instead has al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum.

Some lexicographers explain this construction as incorporating the sense of al-ifḍāʾ ilā al-marʾah: approaching a woman in intercourse.

This explanation fits the verse’s context.

It should not, however, become a claim that ilā necessarily produces the meaning of intercourse in every Arabic sentence.

A preposition helps specify meaning alongside its associated verb or noun and the context.

Here intercourse is identified from the combined expressions, not a preposition alone.

Al-Mufradāt and Lisān al-ʿArab: incorporating ifḍāʾ and the preposition ilā

“They are a covering for you, and you are a covering for them”

This sentence helps explain rafath.

After permitting intimacy, the Qur’an describes woman and man as coverings for one another.

In its physical meaning, libās is something covering the body. Here it describes the spouses’ relationship.

The image conveys closeness, connection, and mutual covering.

Its reciprocity matters.

The Qur’an does not only say that women are men’s covering; it immediately adds that men are women’s covering.

The construction resists reducing marital relations in the verse to a man’s one-sided use of a woman.

All marital rights and responsibilities should nevertheless not be derived from the clothing image alone.

The lexical meaning of rafath still concerns intercourse; the clothing image clarifies that relationship within the spouses’ reciprocal bond.

Fa-al-āna bāshirūhunna: rafath and direct intimacy

The verse continues:

“Now have intercourse with them.”

Bāshirūhunna belongs to B SH R and Form III.

In this context it denotes physical intimacy and intercourse with wives.

Mubāsharah and rafath derive from different roots but concern the same marital subject in this verse.

This proximity does not make every Arabic use of B SH R and R F TH synonymous.

B SH R has a wider family associated with skin, human beings, and direct contact.

Rafath in Qur’anic usage is associated with sexual speech and conduct.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, however, the two share a contextual field and clarify one another.

Is bāshirūhunna a universal command to have intercourse?

Bāshirūhunna is grammatically an imperative, but its function must be understood through the preceding sentences.

The verse first announces that rafath has been made lawful.

It then mentions self-betrayal, divine turning and pardon, and uses fa-al-āna to permit intimacy in the stated circumstances.

The imperative’s surface form should therefore not become a claim that every individual must have intercourse on every fasting night.

The verse’s context does not establish such an obligation.

Here the imperative follows the removal of a restriction and the declaration of permission.

Its appropriate contextual sense is therefore permission and relief, rather than making the action obligatory for everyone.

Takhtānūna anfusakum and the limits of historical conclusions

The verse says that The God knew the addressees were betraying themselves, then mentions divine turning and pardon.

This indicates a situation of transgression or conduct inconsistent with an acknowledged boundary before the verse’s declaration.

The sentence itself nevertheless does not establish every detail of a preceding legal system.

For example, the verse does not explicitly supply the details of a prior restriction connected with particular hours after breaking the fast, sleep, or the night ṣalāt.

Historical and exegetical reports discuss such explanations, but without independent evidence they should not become the lexical meaning of rafath or the verse’s direct wording.

Nor can this expression alone prove the necessity of naskh within the Qur’an.

The verse directly establishes permission for rafath during the fasting night and states its present boundaries.

Fasting, night, and retreat

The verse permits eating and drinking until dawn becomes clear, then commands completing the fast until night.

The ruling on intercourse thus also belongs to a specified temporal structure.

The fasting night differs from the duration of the fast itself.

At the end, however, the verse states a further restriction:

“Do not have intercourse with them while you are in retreat in the mosques.”

The prohibition here concerns the state of retreat.

Nightfall therefore cannot be said to remove every restriction on intercourse regardless of any other circumstance.

Nor should this prohibition imply that marital intercourse is objectionable or opposed to mindfulness at all times.

The Qur’an declares that same relationship lawful at the verse’s opening.

Lexical conclusion: Al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum in Al-Baqarah 2:187 denotes marital intercourse. This meaning emerges from the word, its construction with ilā, the clothing image, and the verbs bāshirūhunna and lā tubāshirūhunna.

5. Al-Baqarah 2:197: prohibiting rafath in pilgrimage

Verse text

الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ فَمَنْ فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ يَعْلَمْهُ اللَّهُ وَتَزَوَّدُوا فَإِنَّ خَيْرَ الزَّادِ التَّقْوَىٰ وَاتَّقُونِ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ

al-ḥajju ashhurun maʿlūmātun fa-man faraḍa fīhinna al-ḥajja fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa wa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajji wa-mā tafʿalū min khayrin yaʿlamhu Allāhu wa-tazawwadū fa-inna khayra al-zādi al-taqwā wa-ittaqūni yā ulī al-albābi

English translation

Pilgrimage is in known months. Whoever undertakes pilgrimage in them, let there be no rafath, no fusūq, and no jidāl during pilgrimage. Whatever good you do, The God knows it. Take provisions, for the best provision is mindfulness. Be mindful of Me, people of understanding.

The grammar of fa-lā rafatha

In this verse, rafatha follows lā.

In the reading quoted in this article, lā negates the entire category and rafath is its noun.

Fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa wa-lā jidāla outwardly negates these behaviors in pilgrimage; as a ruling, however, it functions as a prohibition and restraint.

This manner of expression is familiar in Arabic: rather than only saying “do not engage in rafath,” it says “let there be no rafath during pilgrimage.”

The construction emphasizes setting aside the behavior in the state of pilgrimage.

Grammar alone nevertheless does not identify every instance of rafath. That requires examining lexical meaning and context.

Is rafath in pilgrimage limited to intercourse?

Arabic uses rafath for intercourse, speech, and some related behavior.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 shows that the noun can specifically denote intercourse.

Al-ʿAjjāj’s poetic witness and older lexical explanations show that indecent sexual speech also belongs to its field.

Unlike the fasting verse, Al-Baqarah 2:197 does not include ilā nisāʾikum.

Without examination, rafath here therefore cannot be said to have precisely the same restricted scope of intercourse, excluding every other kind of speech or behavior.

A broader reading is lexically defensible: refraining from intercourse and speech or conduct directly associated with it.

This breadth nevertheless has limits.

Rafath alone does not establish that every respectful discussion of marriage, every expression of family affection, or every necessary statement about sexual health is prohibited during pilgrimage.

Nor can every ordinary contact or nonsexual behavior be placed under rafath without independent evidence.

The lexical evidence supports the field of sexual conduct and speech, not a prohibition on every form of human interaction.

Why does rafath stand alongside fusūq and jidāl?

The verse mentions three distinct designations:

Rafath, fusūq, and jidāl.

Fusūq belongs to F S Q, and jidāl to J D L.

Despite their connection in the pilgrimage context, these three roots are not synonymous.

Rafath concerns the sexual field.

Fusūq concerns departing from boundaries and disobedience.

Jidāl concerns disputation and verbal conflict.

All three should therefore not be translated simply as “bad speech.”

If rafath meant merely every objectionable statement, translation would blur its boundary with some forms of jidāl or fusūq.

Locating rafath within the sexual field preserves their thematic distinctions more clearly.

Their appearance together shows that human conduct in pilgrimage is assessed through more than performing ritual movements.

Intercourse, ethical conduct, and the manner of addressing others also matter within this worship.

This contextual conclusion should nevertheless not become a new lexical definition. Rafath does not itself mean every form of unethical conduct.

Rafath and mindfulness

The verse ends with:

“The best provision is mindfulness.”

This sentence connects conduct in pilgrimage with taqwā.

In the Qur’an, taqwā concerns mindfulness, respecting boundaries, and responsibility before The God, beyond observing one particular prohibition.

Refraining from rafath forms part of this care in pilgrimage.

It does not follow that intercourse is inherently opposed to taqwā.

The same Qur’an permits marital intercourse during the fasting night.

Mindfulness here therefore involves recognizing and respecting each situation’s appropriate boundary, rather than absolutely denying natural human needs.

A lawful marital relationship may involve conduct restricted during a period of special responsibility.

Observing that restriction makes sense through understanding the circumstances and relevant command.

Does the prohibition cover all pilgrimage months?

The verse begins with “Pilgrimage is in known months,” then states:

“Whoever undertakes pilgrimage in them.”

The prohibition thus concerns someone who has undertaken pilgrimage within the known months.

This matters because the verse speaks of accepting the responsibility of pilgrimage as well as the calendar.

Fa-lā rafatha alone therefore does not make intercourse unlawful throughout all pilgrimage months for everyone, including those who have not entered pilgrimage at all.

Determining the restriction’s precise beginning and end for someone undertaking pilgrimage requires an independent examination of farḍ al-ḥajj, the consecrated state, and the stages of the rites.

The root rafath does not determine that timetable.

Does rafath invalidate pilgrimage or require a specific expiation?

Al-Baqarah 2:197 prohibits rafath in pilgrimage.

This prohibition is real and should not be minimized.

The verse itself nevertheless does not supply a list of expiations, compensatory days, or separate invalidation rulings for every instance of rafath.

A detailed legal system therefore cannot be built from the root’s meaning alone.

Indecent sexual speech and actual intercourse, for example, are lexically distinguishable. Their respective legal consequences when the prohibition is violated are a separate question.

None of those consequences should be attributed to The God without clear textual support.

Conversely, the absence of a detailed list of expiations in this particular verse does not make its command unimportant.

Refraining from rafath, fusūq, and jidāl during pilgrimage is expressly stated.

Al-Ṭabarī on Al-Baqarah 2:197: readings of rafath’s scope

6. Relating the two occurrences: one word in different situations

Reading Al-Baqarah 2:187 and Al-Baqarah 2:197 together reveals several significant differences.

Verse 187 permits al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum during the fasting night. The noun’s construction with ilā and the accompanying sentences foreground intercourse between spouses.

Verse 197 has fa-lā rafatha without naming an object or partner. The broader field of rafath therefore remains relevant to its examination.

In the first verse, al-rafathu is definite and the subject of the passive verb uḥilla. In the second, rafatha follows lā negating the entire category.

This grammatical difference accompanies different rulings: permission in specified circumstances, and exclusion or prohibition in another setting.

Do the verses conflict?

No.

They do not address identical circumstances.

One concerns the fasting night; the other concerns pilgrimage.

Conduct permitted within marital life can be restricted during a particular worship without creating a lexical or logical contradiction.

In ordinary life, too, something normally permitted may face restrictions while a specified responsibility is being performed.

This example only clarifies the difference in circumstances; it should not become a means of inventing new rulings.

Does rafath always mean intercourse alone?

No; that restriction does not fit all the lexical evidence.

The opposite answer is also incorrect: rafath does not always mean every kind of sexual speech.

Intercourse is the prominent meaning in Al-Baqarah 2:187.

In Al-Baqarah 2:197, the prohibition can encompass a broader field of sexual conduct and associated indecent speech.

Translation must therefore distinguish the word’s lexical field from the particular sense selected by each context.

Is everything permitted during fasting prohibited in pilgrimage?

These two verses establish no such rule.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 also permits eating and drinking at night.

Al-Baqarah 2:197 nevertheless does not establish that eating and drinking are prohibited during pilgrimage.

The pilgrimage prohibition names particular categories, which must be examined as such.

All rulings for the two forms of worship cannot therefore be transferred between them by comparing one word.

Each responsibility has its own boundaries and conditions.

7. Distinguishing R F TH from nearby words

R F TH (rafath) compared with F Ḥ SH (faḥusha)

Study of F Ḥ SH

In Arabic, fuḥsh denotes severe ugliness or indecency in speech or conduct.

Rafath in its main usage has a particular connection with sexual matters and speech about them.

Rafath-like speech may therefore also be fuḥsh, but not every fuḥsh is necessarily rafath.

A nonsexual insult, for example, may be ugly without concerning intercourse.

Nor can lawful marital intercourse in Al-Baqarah 2:187 be treated as inherently ugly or sinful simply because rafath is used.

Moral evaluation and sexual subject matter must therefore be examined separately.

R F TH (rafath) compared with B SH R (bāshara)

Study of B SH R

Bāshara concerns direct contact and physical intimacy.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, it denotes intercourse.

Rafath concerns intercourse in that verse as well, but its lexical family also encompasses sexual speech.

The two are close within that context without being absolute synonyms.

Bāshirūhunna also does not make every Qur’anic use of B SH R mean intercourse.

R F TH (rafath) compared with F Ḍ W (afḍā)

Study of F Ḍ W

Afḍā belongs to F Ḍ W and Form IV. In Arabic, it can denote reaching, gaining access, or approaching another directly.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: afḍā from F Ḍ W

Al-Nisāʾ 4:21 states:

“You have already approached one another intimately.”

This expression occurs in the context of marriage and spouses’ financial rights.

Semantically, afḍā ilā is related to al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum in Al-Baqarah 2:187.

Ifḍāʾ itself, however, is not restricted to intercourse throughout Arabic.

Each verse requires examining how the subject and context specify its meaning.

R F TH (rafath) compared with N K Ḥ (nakaḥa)

Study of N K Ḥ

Qur’anic nikāḥ concerns marriage and marital relations in different constructions.

Rafath in Al-Baqarah 2:187 denotes intercourse.

The two words are nevertheless distinct.

Nikāḥ can concern establishing marriage and its rights, whereas rafath concerns sexual speech or conduct.

Every occurrence of nikāḥ should therefore not be translated as rafath, nor should rafath supply all contractual conditions and marital rights.

R F TH (rafath) compared with L M S (lamasa)

Study of L M S

Lams concerns contact and touching.

Some uses of contact in Arabic and the Qur’an can occur in a marital context.

Not every physical touch, however, means intercourse.

Nor does rafath’s inclusion of some preliminary sexual behavior in lexical explanations turn every human contact into rafath.

The kind of contact and its purpose must be identified from context.

R F TH (rafath) compared with F S Q (fasaqa)

Study of F S Q

Fisq concerns departing from boundaries and disobedience.

Al-Baqarah 2:197 places rafath alongside fusūq.

Their proximity permits recognizing rafath in pilgrimage as prohibited conduct, but does not make the word itself mean unrestricted fusūq.

Lawful sexual conduct in its appropriate setting is not inherently fusūq.

Each behavior’s ruling must therefore come from the relevant command and boundaries.

R F TH (rafath) compared with J D L (jādala)

Study of J D L

Jidāl concerns reasoned discussion, disputation, or verbal conflict.

Disputation may be objectionable without its subject being sexual.

Rafath and jidāl are distinct categories in pilgrimage.

This difference prevents translating both merely as “bad speech.”

Nor should every scholarly discussion or critical question be treated as prohibited pilgrimage disputation simply because it involves disagreement. Its scope requires examining its own root and context.

R F TH (rafath) compared with L GH W (laghā)

Study of L GH W

Laghw concerns idle or fruitless speech or action.

Al-ʿAjjāj’s line places al-laghā alongside rafath al-takallum.

The combination shows that idle speech and indecent sexual talk can be distinct subjects.

Not every idle statement is rafath, just as not every use of rafath can be explained merely through futility.

8. Grammar and the boundaries of interpretation

Although the root has only two Qur’anic occurrences, their sentence structures create an important difference.

Al-rafathu in Al-Baqarah 2:187 is definite and occurs with the passive verb uḥilla. The subject is permission for intercourse within a specified time.

Rafatha in Al-Baqarah 2:197 is indefinite and follows lā negating the entire category. In the context of a ruling, this conveys prohibition.

Ilā nisāʾikum also identifies the partner in the first verse, whereas the second names none.

This difference can affect the intended scope without independently determining every instance.

Permission for an act also differs from a command requiring its performance. Bāshirūhunna in Al-Baqarah 2:187 follows the lifting of a restriction and should not become a universal obligation to have intercourse.

Likewise, prohibiting conduct differs from specifying a punishment or expiation for violating it. The prohibition in Al-Baqarah 2:197 is clear, but the same expression does not detail every violation’s legal consequences.

These distinctions are necessary for faithful translation and reflection.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum in Al-Baqarah 2:187, “sexual intimacy with your wives” or “sexual intercourse with your spouses” is appropriate.

Simply translating it as “speaking indecently to women” restricts the meaning excessively and does not adequately fit fa-al-āna bāshirūhunna.

“Indecent behavior toward women” is also unsuitable because it obscures the particular meaning of intercourse.

For fa-lā rafatha in Al-Baqarah 2:197, the direct translation may retain rafath, with a separate reflection explaining its field of intercourse and indecent sexual speech or conduct.

A more explanatory translation might use “neither intercourse nor indecent sexual speech,” but must acknowledge that it expands the word’s scope rather than translating several separate Arabic nouns one for one.

Rafath should also not become “insult,” “quarrel,” “immorality,” or “every form of affection toward a spouse” without justification.

Both translations must preserve the different rulings: intercourse is declared lawful during the fasting night, whereas rafath is prohibited in the state of pilgrimage.

The principle is to retain direct, contextual meaning in translation and discuss borderline cases, lists of restrictions, and legal consequences in a separate reflection.

10. Final synthesis

Arabic R F TH concerns explicit and sometimes indecent speech about sexual matters, as well as intercourse itself and closely related conduct.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times as the noun rafath.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum, the image of spouses as coverings for one another, and the command bāshirūhunna point to marital intercourse.

The Qur’an permits this relationship during the fasting night while distinguishing the state of retreat.

Rafath itself therefore does not always mean unlawful or inherently objectionable conduct.

Fa-lā rafatha in Al-Baqarah 2:197 occurs alongside fusūq and jidāl. Here rafath is conduct to be avoided by someone in the state of pilgrimage.

Lexical evidence shows that the prohibition can encompass intercourse and indecent sexual speech or conduct. It does not justify treating every respectful discussion of marital relations or every nonsexual touch as rafath.

Rafath, fusūq, and jidāl are also distinct categories and should not be reduced to the general idea of “bad speech.”

The verses do not conflict because they address different circumstances. Something lawful during the fasting night can face a particular restriction while performing pilgrimage.

This difference should nevertheless not become a means of inventing new rulings. Precise timing, borderline cases, and consequences of violation require other verses and independent argument.

Final definition: R F TH belongs to the field of sexual speech and conduct, especially explicit or indecent speech about intercourse and, in broader or indirect usage, intercourse itself and associated behavior.

The meaning of marital intercourse is clear in Al-Baqarah 2:187, while the broader field of rafath must be considered when understanding the pilgrimage prohibition in Al-Baqarah 2:197.

Three boundaries must be retained: sexual speech differs from intercourse itself; lexical meaning differs from the ruling of lawfulness or unlawfulness in particular circumstances; and Qur’anic prohibition differs from legal or historical details not stated in the verse.

This approach neither makes lawful marital relations inherently objectionable nor reduces the Qur’anic prohibition of rafath in pilgrimage to an ineffective statement or a general recommendation to behave pleasantly.
