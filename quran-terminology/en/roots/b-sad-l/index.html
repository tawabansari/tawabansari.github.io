The Qur’anic Root ب ص ل

Baṣal: an ancient name for onions, with a limited Qur’anic range

The root ب ص ل has a very limited presence in the Qur’an. It occurs only once, as the noun بَصَل, in Al-Baqarah 2:61. The Qur’an uses no verb or other derivative from this family.

بَصَل is the familiar Arabic word for onions. It is a collective generic noun: it can name onions as a kind of plant, while a single onion is called بَصَلَة. Thus وَبَصَلِهَا refers to the earth’s onion crop, rather than necessarily to one individual onion.

Unlike many Qur’anic roots, this one does not require us to construct an abstract core such as forming layers, having skins, or peeling. Historical evidence shows that baṣal is itself a very old name for this plant.

A concise definition of its Qur’anic use is straightforward:

ب ص ل: baṣal, onions—an edible plant whose name has a long history in the Semitic languages. The Qur’an uses this family only in that botanical sense.

An ancient name in the Semitic languages

The history of baṣal extends well beyond its Qur’anic occurrence. Comparable names for onions are recorded in other Semitic languages. Brown–Driver–Briggs lists Hebrew בָּצָל, its plural in Numbers 11:5, and Aramaic בּוּצְלָא, alongside the Arabic word. This comparison supports the antiquity of the plant name; a precise reconstructed form and the status of each South Arabian example require separate comparative documentation.

The published reading of the Cairo Phoenician papyrus, KAI 51, includes bṣl, “onions,” in lines 3–4 of its front, among foodstuffs. The papyrus has been dated on palaeographic grounds to around 300 BCE. Parts of the document and the descriptions following “onions” remain uncertain. The relevant evidence is the plant name itself, not a definitive interpretation of every item in this damaged text.

This historical distribution makes an explanation based solely on a secondary Arabic verb or image inadequate. It is more secure to understand baṣal as an ancient Semitic plant name also preserved in Arabic vocabulary.

The comparison does not establish that Arabic necessarily borrowed the word from Hebrew, Phoenician, or another particular language. Its presence in several Semitic branches indicates an old word family, but resemblance alone cannot determine the precise direction of borrowing or inheritance.

Usage in early Arabic

Classical Arabic dictionaries introduce baṣal without an elaborate definition: البصل معروف، الواحدة بصلة—onions are familiar, and one onion is called baṣalah.

A verse attributed to Labīd says:

فَخْمَةً ذَفْرَاءَ تُرْتَى بِالْعُرَى
قُرْدُمَانِيًّا وَتَرْكًا كَالْبَصَلِ

“A great host, pungent with the smell of its iron armour, clothed and fastened with straps, in qirdamānī coats of mail and helmets like onions.”

The verse describes a body of troops and its equipment. Its helmets are compared to onions. Classical explanations relate the comparison to their form, including the raised central part of an iron helmet. The onion is the familiar object through which the equipment’s appearance is described.

Labīd lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Since the date of this particular verse has not been independently established here, it should not confidently be labelled pre-Islamic. Its value lies in illustrating the early, natural use of baṣal for a familiar object whose shape could serve as a comparison for other things.

This investigation did not locate an Arabic occurrence of baṣal whose attribution and pre-Islamic date could both be independently established with confidence. That does not mean the word was absent from pre-Islamic Arabic, particularly given its much longer history in neighbouring Semitic languages. Evidence from another language, or an undated line of poetry, should nevertheless not be presented as securely dated pre-Islamic Arabic evidence.

The onion’s image and related Arabic expressions

An onion has several layers and overlapping skins. Arabic dictionaries record figurative expressions drawing on these features. قشر متبصل describes a covering with many layers, and بَصَّلْتُ الرجلَ من ثيابه is recorded for stripping someone of their clothes, as though removing layers. Their attestation in later dictionaries does not by itself establish when these expressions first arose.

بصلة is also used for certain objects resembling an onion. Arabic dictionaries mention an iron helmet or head covering compared to an onion because of its shape.

These uses show that the onion itself could supply an image for comparison and word formation. The proverb أَكْسَى مِنَ الْبَصَلِ, “more heavily clothed than an onion,” also draws on its many skins; its recording in a dictionary does not alone establish a pre-Islamic date.

The reasoning should not be reversed. The existence of these expressions does not prove that the original meaning of ب ص ل was peeling or having layers, with the plant name subsequently derived from it. The early Semitic evidence directly attests the plant name; a hypothesis about an earlier verbal source needs independent evidence.

For this study, the concrete image is the plant itself, rather than a hypothetical verb behind its name.

The sole Qur’anic occurrence: وَبَصَلِهَا

The Qur’an uses the word in the Israelites’ request to Moses:

وَإِذْ قُلْتُمْ يَا مُوسَىٰ لَنْ نَصْبِرَ عَلَىٰ طَعَامٍ وَاحِدٍ فَادْعُ لَنَا رَبَّكَ يُخْرِجْ لَنَا مِمَّا تُنْبِتُ الْأَرْضُ مِنْ بَقْلِهَا وَقِثَّائِهَا وَفُومِهَا وَعَدَسِهَا وَبَصَلِهَا ۖ قَالَ أَتَسْتَبْدِلُونَ الَّذِي هُوَ أَدْنَىٰ بِالَّذِي هُوَ خَيْرٌ ۚ اهْبِطُوا مِصْرًا فَإِنَّ لَكُمْ مَا سَأَلْتُمْ ۗ وَضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الذِّلَّةُ وَالْمَسْكَنَةُ وَبَاءُوا بِغَضَبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ ۗ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ كَانُوا يَكْفُرُونَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَيَقْتُلُونَ النَّبِيِّينَ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ ۗ ذَٰلِكَ بِمَا عَصَوْا وَكَانُوا يَعْتَدُونَ

“When you said: O Moses, we will not endure one kind of food; call upon your Lord for us, so that He may bring forth for us some of what the earth grows—its greens, its cucumbers, its fūm, its lentils, and its onions. He said: Would you exchange what is better for what is lower? Go down to a town, and you will have what you asked for. Humiliation and destitution were imposed upon them, and they incurred wrath from The God. That was because they rejected the signs of The God and killed the prophets without right; that was because they disobeyed and continued to transgress.”

Al-Baqarah 2:61

وَبَصَلِهَا is the final item in the list of foods requested from the earth’s produce. There is no substantial lexical uncertainty about baṣal itself: it means onions.

Within the same list, the meaning of فوم has been disputed; proposals include wheat and garlic. It is retained as fūm in the quotation above to keep that separate question open. The disagreement does not extend to baṣal, one of the clear words in this verse, whose botanical meaning is also supported by Semitic comparisons.

Does the Qur’an call onions “lowly”?

After the list of foods comes the question:

أَتَسْتَبْدِلُونَ الَّذِي هُوَ أَدْنَىٰ بِالَّذِي هُوَ خَيْرٌ

This sentence should not be incorporated into the lexical definition of baṣal.

أدنى does not define the word baṣal itself. The verse does not say البصل أدنى, “onions are lower,” and the root ب ص ل contains no lexical sense of baseness, evil, or moral disvalue.

The sentence concerns exchanging what they had for the requested alternative. Precisely what grounds the comparison made by adnā is a question for interpretation of the whole verse. Lexically, however, one point is clear: an onion has not acquired a negative moral meaning simply by being an onion.

This boundary matters because the context of a verse can mistakenly be folded into the meaning of one of its words. Just as “captive” does not become morally bad merely through its occurrence in a verse, baṣal does not acquire the lexical meaning “base food” through its place in this comparison.

ب ص ل and ب ق ل: a particular plant and a broader category

In the same verse, بَقْلِهَا precedes بَصَلِهَا.

In this food context, baql is a broader term for greens and edible plants, whereas baṣal names a particular plant. The two therefore do not operate at the same semantic level: one can designate a broader class of edible vegetation, while the other identifies a particular kind. Outside this context, the lexical range of baql is not confined to edible plants.

Their juxtaposition should not be taken to mean that the Qur’an is presenting a precise botanical taxonomy. The verse lists produce requested from the earth; its purpose is not to define a scientific relationship between baql, qiththāʾ, lentils, and onions.

For this root, the essential point is simple: baṣal specifically names onions.

Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Baqarah 2:61, وَبَصَلِهَا can straightforwardly be rendered:

“and its onions.”

Here “its” refers to the earth mentioned earlier in the sentence.

Because baṣal is a collective generic noun, the English plural “onions” naturally conveys the crop or kind of produce. It need not imply a counted group of individual bulbs, and “its onion” would misleadingly suggest a single specimen in this context.

Renderings such as “a bulbous plant,” “spring onions,” or “garlic” require evidence that is lacking here. The Arabic word and its historical comparisons point to the familiar onion.

Nor should an imagined root image lead to a rendering such as “the layered plant.” “Onions” is both the most natural and the most accurate English choice.

Summary

ب ص ل has a very limited Qur’anic range. It occurs only once, as the noun بَصَل, in Al-Baqarah 2:61. The Qur’an uses no verb or other derivative from this family.

Baṣal means onions, and a single onion in Arabic is called baṣalah. This botanical meaning has a long history: comparable names in Hebrew, Aramaic, and the Phoenician evidence discussed above support its status as an ancient Semitic plant name. Establishing the precise history of each form requires more than resemblance alone.

In early Arabic, the onion itself could serve as an image. In a verse attributed to Labīd, helmets or iron head coverings are compared to onions. Since Labīd lived across both periods and the date of this line has not been independently established, it should not be called definitively pre-Islamic. No other securely dated pre-Islamic Arabic example of baṣal was located in this investigation. Stating this limit is preferable to inventing evidence or assigning a certain date to an undated verse.

Expressions such as قشر متبصل and descriptions of stripping away layers show that the onion’s shape and skins could provide images for comparison and word formation. Their documentation in later sources does not establish when they originated, nor does it prove that peeling or layering was the root’s earliest meaning.

In its sole Qur’anic occurrence, بصلها is one of the foods requested from the earth’s produce. The subsequent phrase الذي هو أدنى does not change the lexical meaning of baṣal or make an onion intrinsically lowly or morally negative.

ب ص ل: baṣal, onions—an ancient Semitic name for this plant. In the Qur’an, the family appears only as this noun in its botanical sense. Related Arabic images involving layers, skins, and stripping can be explained through the onion itself; they should not be projected into the past as the root’s established original meaning.
