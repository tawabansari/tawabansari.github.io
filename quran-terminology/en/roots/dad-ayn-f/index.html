Weakness and diminished power: from natural human frailty and imposed powerlessness to doubling and increased reward or punishment

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

Arabic Ḍ ʿ F has two main semantic branches with different uses.

The first concerns weakness, inability, and insufficient strength. A person may be physically weak, lack sufficient financial resources, have limited decision-making capacity, or lack adequate means of defense against a stronger force.

In this branch, weakness contrasts with strength.

The second concerns doubling, increasing an amount, and multiplication. Arabic uses taḍʿīf, iḍʿāf, and muḍāʿafah when one or more equivalents of an amount are added to the original.

These branches should not be confused. Ḍaʿīf indicates weakness, whereas muḍāʿaf may concern an increase in wealth, number, reward, or punishment.

The root occurs 52 times in the Qur’an.

Writing without vowel marks can mislead readers. For example, ḍaʿf concerns weakness, whereas the familiar use of ḍiʿf concerns multiplication.

Likewise, aḍʿafu in Maryam 19:75 and Al-Jinn 72:24 means weaker, while aḍʿāfan in Al-Baqarah 2:245 concerns multiplication.

Shared consonants alone therefore do not determine the meaning. Vowels, word structure, and context must be examined.

2. Two semantic principles in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris’s analysis

Ibn Fāris says in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الضَّادُ وَالْعَيْنُ وَالْفَاءُ أَصْلَانِ مُتَبَايِنَانِ، يَدُلُّ أَحَدُهُمَا عَلَى خِلَافِ الْقُوَّةِ، وَيَدُلُّ الْآخَرُ عَلَى أَنْ يُزَادَ الشَّيْءُ مِثْلَهُ

al-ḍādu wa-al-ʿaynu wa-al-fāʾu aṣlāni mutabāyināni, yadullu aḥaduhumā ʿalā khilāfi al-quwwati, wa-yadullu al-ākharu ʿalā an yuzāda al-shayʾu mithlahu

Meaning:

“This root has two distinct principles: one indicates the opposite of strength, and the other indicates adding something’s like to it.”

This distinction is fundamental for Qur’anic research.

In the first sense, a person or thing lacks sufficient capacity.

In the second, the amount of something increases.

For example, a person may be weak while their wealth multiplies. Their weakness and increased assets are different matters.

A group may also be numerous but weak in organization or ability to resist.

More people do not necessarily mean more power, just as a lack of power does not necessarily mean few people.

The branches should not be assigned a single definite origin through invented images such as splitting strength in two or piling weaknesses on one another.

Ibn Fāris himself regards them as two distinct principles.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: weakness and adding an equivalent as distinct principles

3. Ḍaʿf and ḍuʿf: insufficient capacity

Weakness contrasted with strength

Arabic ḍaʿf and ḍuʿf contrast with quwwah, strength.

Ḍaʿufa describes a person or thing lacking necessary capacity or losing strength.

But weakness is not always absolute.

Someone may be physically strong but financially unable.

Another may have sufficient knowledge but lack the means to defend against an armed force.

A group may have enough capacity for an ordinary task but insufficient resources for a much greater one.

Weakness should therefore often be understood relative to a particular capacity, responsibility, danger, or task.

Weakness in one field does not establish inability in every field.

The difference between ḍaʿf and ḍuʿf

Early sources record both ḍaʿf, with fatḥah, and ḍuʿf, with ḍammah.

Some lexicographers associate ḍuʿf more with physical weakness and ḍaʿf more with weakness of judgment and thought.

This division is not universally accepted.

Lisān al-ʿArab states that either form can concern bodily weakness or weak judgment.

Both vowel readings are also reported for Al-Rūm 30:54.

The difference in vowels should therefore not become a semantic rule without exceptions.

In Persian, both nātavānī, inability, and ḍaʿf, weakness, can fit, according to context.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ḍaʿf, ḍuʿf, and their range

Ḍaʿīf: describing a person or thing

Ḍaʿīf describes someone or something without sufficient capacity.

The adjective itself does not specify the kind of weakness.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:76 states:

إِنَّ كَيْدَ الشَّيْطَانِ كَانَ ضَعِيفًا

inna kayda al-shayṭāni kāna ḍaʿīfan

“Satan’s plotting has indeed been weak.”

Weakness is attributed here to plotting, rather than Satan’s body or muscular strength.

Weakness can therefore concern a plan’s power and effectiveness.

Maryam 19:75 states:

فَسَيَعْلَمُونَ مَنْ هُوَ شَرٌّ مَكَانًا وَأَضْعَفُ جُنْدًا

fa-sa-yaʿlamūna man huwa sharrun makānan wa-aḍʿafu jundan

“They will know who has a worse position and a weaker host.”

Aḍʿaf is comparative here: weaker.

Al-Jinn 72:24 also mentions aḍʿafu nāṣiran, meaning someone with a weaker helper.

Weakness can thus concern an individual, group, army, supporter, or plan.

4. Early Arabic witnesses and semantic extensions

Bodily weakness and weakness of judgment

Early dictionaries do not restrict weakness to the body.

Rajulun ḍaʿīfu al-raʾy describes someone with insufficient capacity for judgment or decision-making.

Ḍaʿf al-badan, by contrast, concerns insufficient bodily strength.

The two can coexist in one person, but need not.

An older person may lack their former physical strength while having extensive knowledge and experience.

Conversely, a strong young person may struggle to understand a matter or make a sound decision.

Physical weakness should therefore not automatically become intellectual or moral incapacity.

Aḍʿafahu al-maraḍ: illness weakened him

Arabic aḍʿafahu al-maraḍ means that illness reduced a person’s strength.

Illness is the cause of weakness in this construction.

This shows that weakness is not always fixed or congenital: an external event can cause it.

Ḍaʿʿafahu al-sayr also describes a person or animal exhausted and weakened by prolonged travel.

Weakness can therefore be temporary, gradual, or dependent on circumstances.

Muḍʿif: two different uses

Early sources sometimes use muḍʿif for someone whose riding animal is weak.

The adjective need not concern the person’s own body: it describes their means of travel.

In the root’s other branch, however, muḍʿif can describe someone whose wealth or reward has multiplied.

The forms look similar but differ in meaning.

Al-muḍʿifūn in Al-Rūm 30:39 belongs to increase, rather than a weak riding animal or bodily frailty.

A witness from Labīd’s poetry

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

وَعَالَيْنَ مَضْعُوفًا وَفَرْدًا سُمُوطُهُ
جُمَانٌ وَمَرْجَانٌ يَشُكُّ الْمَفَاصِلَا

wa-ʿālayna maḍʿūfan wa-fardan sumūṭuhu / jumānun wa-marjānun yashukku al-mafāṣilā

An approximate rendering:

“They put on ornaments in multiple and single strands, strings of silver-colored beads and coral joining the intervening parts.”

Maḍʿūf contrasts with fard in this verse.

Maḍʿūf concerns something layered or multiplied, while fard means single.

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab quote this witness.

Labīd lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The precise date of this verse is unknown, but its use in early poetry is clear.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: Labīd’s witness on maḍʿūf and fard

Muḍāʿafah: layered armor

Ibn Fāris and other lexicographers also record al-muḍāʿafah for armor whose rings are woven in double or multiple layers.

The increased number of layers matters here.

The armor changes from a simple weave to a layered structure.

This does not mean that every use of muḍāʿafah concerns adding just one layer.

Arabic uses this family for increases in amount, number, or intensity in various fields.

Figuratively, aḍʿāf al-kitāb also concerns the middle parts, margins, or layers of writing.

These extensions belong to increase and layering, rather than weakness and inability.

Early lexicons: weak mounts, layered armor, and aḍʿāf al-kitāb

5. Natural human weakness: beginning, strength, and old age

Al-Rūm states:

اللَّهُ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ ضَعْفٍ ثُمَّ جَعَلَ مِنْ بَعْدِ ضَعْفٍ قُوَّةً ثُمَّ جَعَلَ مِنْ بَعْدِ قُوَّةٍ ضَعْفًا وَشَيْبَةً

allāhu alladhī khalaqakum min ḍaʿfin thumma jaʿala min baʿdi ḍaʿfin quwwatan thumma jaʿala min baʿdi quwwatin ḍaʿfan wa-shaybatan

“The God is the One who created you from weakness, then placed strength after weakness, then placed weakness and gray hair after strength.”

Al-Rūm 30:54

This is among the clearest witnesses to weakness contrasted with strength.

Human life begins in weakness and dependence.

A child needs others’ care and support. Physical and mental development bring greater abilities.

Later in life, some capacity may decline through aging.

The verse does not mean that everyone reaches the same capacity at a particular age or loses every ability equally in old age.

Physical, mental, social, and economic capacities vary.

Nor does weakness at a stage of life mean a lack of worth, knowledge, or dignity.

Here weakness is a natural human condition, rather than a moral accusation.

Wa-khuliqa al-insānu ḍaʿīfan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:28 states:

يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ أَنْ يُخَفِّفَ عَنْكُمْ وَخُلِقَ الْإِنْسَانُ ضَعِيفًا

yurīdu allāhu an yukhaffifa ʿankum wa-khuliqa al-insānu ḍaʿīfan

“The God wishes to lighten things for you; the human being was created weak.”

Human weakness accompanies the divine intention to make things easier.

Lexically, ḍaʿīfan describes limited human capacity.

The word itself does not decide whether only bodily weakness, human inclinations, natural needs, or limited decision-making capacity is meant.

The preceding verses’ discussion of human relations and rules of life also matters.

It is more fitting to read human weakness here as drawing attention to limits and need, rather than absolute powerlessness or inherent corruption.

Being created weak does not mean that people bear no responsibility.

Other Qur’anic verses assess responsibility in relation to actual capacity.

6. Weakness, responsibility, and obligation

Al-Anfāl 8:66: a lighter burden in recognition of weakness

Al-Anfāl states:

الْآنَ خَفَّفَ اللَّهُ عَنْكُمْ وَعَلِمَ أَنَّ فِيكُمْ ضَعْفًا

al-āna khaffafa allāhu ʿankum wa-ʿalima anna fīkum ḍaʿfan

“Now The God has lightened things for you and knows that there is weakness among you.”

Al-Anfāl 8:66

The context is military confrontation.

The preceding verse states a more demanding ratio for standing firm against the enemy.

The requirement is then lightened in view of weakness among the believers.

This shows that weakness can matter in assessing a group’s actual capacity.

The verse does not say that their weakness must result only from insufficient faith.

Physical strength, numbers, preparation, resources, and actual circumstances can affect a group’s capacity.

The word weakness itself does not identify one exclusive cause.

Al-Baqarah 2:286: responsibility within capacity

Al-Baqarah states:

لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا

lā yukallifu allāhu nafsan illā wusʿahā

“The God obliges no soul beyond its capacity.”

Al-Baqarah 2:286

No word from Ḍ ʿ F occurs here, but a related principle is clear: responsibility should be understood in relation to human capacity.

Someone unable to perform a task is not in the same position as someone able to do it.

This principle can be examined across different Qur’anic rulings.

Yet not every difficulty or unpleasantness means complete inability.

A difficult task differs from one beyond a person’s capacity.

Recognizing the difference requires attention to the individual’s actual circumstances.

Al-Tawbah 9:91: the weak and exemption

Al-Tawbah states:

لَيْسَ عَلَى الضُّعَفَاءِ وَلَا عَلَى الْمَرْضَىٰ وَلَا عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يَجِدُونَ مَا يُنْفِقُونَ حَرَجٌ

laysa ʿalā al-ḍuʿafāʾi wa-lā ʿalā al-marḍā wa-lā ʿalā alladhīna lā yajidūna mā yunfiqūna ḥarajun

“There is no constraint upon the weak, the sick, or those who find nothing to spend.”

Al-Tawbah 9:91

Three groups appear together: the weak, the sick, and those without the necessary financial resources.

These groups can overlap, but naming them separately shows that inability can have different causes and forms.

The verse continues by discussing sincere goodwill and right conduct.

Exemption from a responsibility beyond someone’s capacity therefore does not make honesty and responsible conduct unimportant.

7. Weakness and protecting vulnerable people

Al-Baqarah 2:282: weakness in managing a financial obligation

The verse about recording debt states:

فَإِنْ كَانَ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِ الْحَقُّ سَفِيهًا أَوْ ضَعِيفًا أَوْ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُ أَنْ يُمِلَّ هُوَ فَلْيُمْلِلْ وَلِيُّهُ بِالْعَدْلِ

fa-in kāna alladhī ʿalayhi al-ḥaqqu safīhan aw ḍaʿīfan aw lā yastaṭīʿu an yumilla huwa fa-lyumlil waliyyuhu bi-al-ʿadli

“If the person bearing the obligation is lacking in judgment, weak, or unable to dictate personally, their guardian must dictate justly.”

Al-Baqarah 2:282

Ḍaʿīfan concerns a person’s capacity to arrange or state a financial obligation here.

This weakness should not be restricted solely to physical or solely to mental inability.

Safīhan, ḍaʿīfan, and lā yastaṭīʿu are expressed separately, and their distinctions should not be erased.

The verse provides a response when someone cannot properly state what is due: the guardian must act justly.

A person’s weakness does not license exploiting their property or rights.

Limited capacity accompanies another person’s responsibility to uphold justice.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:9: weak offspring

Al-Nisāʾ states:

وَلْيَخْشَ الَّذِينَ لَوْ تَرَكُوا مِنْ خَلْفِهِمْ ذُرِّيَّةً ضِعَافًا خَافُوا عَلَيْهِمْ

wa-lyakhsha alladhīna law tarakū min khalfihim dhurriyyatan ḍiʿāfan khāfū ʿalayhim

“Let those be mindful who, if they left weak offspring behind, would fear for them.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:9

Ḍiʿāfan is a plural of ḍaʿīf.

It describes offspring who might remain without adequate support.

Their weakness may concern age, dependence, and insufficient resources.

The verse does not blame them for weakness: it describes circumstances toward which others should act responsibly.

It then commands mindfulness and sound speech.

Al-Baqarah 2:266: weak offspring and the need for support

Al-Baqarah 2:266 describes someone who has grown old and has weak offspring:

وَأَصَابَهُ الْكِبَرُ وَلَهُ ذُرِّيَّةٌ ضُعَفَاءُ

wa-aṣābahu al-kibaru wa-lahu dhurriyyatun ḍuʿafāʾu

“Old age has reached him, and he has weak offspring.”

This image shows two dependent generations: a father who has lost former capacity and offspring not yet able to meet their own needs adequately.

Losing their source of livelihood could have grave consequences.

Weakness is therefore more than an individual description: it also concerns economic vulnerability and the need for support.

Yet ḍuʿafāʾ itself does not mean orphans, poor people, or sick people; these conditions may accompany weakness in particular cases.

8. Istiḍʿāf: considering weak and imposing weakness

The meaning of istaḍʿafa

Istaḍʿafa belongs to Form X.

Lexical sources relate it to finding or considering someone weak.

Some uses go further, concerning exploitation of another’s weakness, humiliation, and keeping them powerless.

For example, depriving people of resources, political power, or means of defense may create a condition of istiḍʿāf.

This differs from natural weakness.

A child may be weak because of their stage of development, whereas capable adults may be placed under others’ pressure and domination so that they cannot use their abilities.

Istiḍʿāf then concerns a power relationship and the conduct of those exercising domination.

Nevertheless, Form X does not invariably mean deliberately weakening someone. It sometimes expresses only considering or finding them weak.

The precise meaning must therefore be understood through the verb’s structure and context.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Asās al-Balāghah: finding weak and exploiting weakness

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:4: istiḍʿāf in Pharaoh’s policy

Al-Qaṣaṣ states:

إِنَّ فِرْعَوْنَ عَلَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَجَعَلَ أَهْلَهَا شِيَعًا يَسْتَضْعِفُ طَائِفَةً مِنْهُمْ يُذَبِّحُ أَبْنَاءَهُمْ وَيَسْتَحْيِي نِسَاءَهُمْ

inna firʿawna ʿalā fī al-arḍi wa-jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan yastaḍʿifu ṭāʾifatan minhum yudhabbiḥu abnāʾahum wa-yastaḥyī nisāʾahum

“Pharaoh exalted himself in the land and divided its people into factions, keeping one group weak, slaughtering their sons and keeping their women alive.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:4

Istiḍʿāf is part of Pharaoh’s exercise of rule here.

First comes his self-exaltation, then his division of the people, and then his weakening of one group.

This is more than a mental judgment that a group seemed weak to him.

The context describes conduct severely restricting that group’s survival, security, and future.

Slaughtering sons and keeping women alive belong to this same account.

Yastaḍʿifu here thus approaches exercising power to keep a group of people weak.

Yet istiḍʿāf itself does not name every method of repression: the verse specifies particular methods afterward.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:5: reversal of the oppressed people’s condition

The following verse states:

وَنُرِيدُ أَنْ نَمُنَّ عَلَى الَّذِينَ اسْتُضْعِفُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَنَجْعَلَهُمْ أَئِمَّةً وَنَجْعَلَهُمُ الْوَارِثِينَ

wa-nurīdu an namunna ʿalā alladhīna ustuḍʿifū fī al-arḍi wa-najʿalahum aʾimmatan wa-najʿalahumu al-wārithīna

“We wished to bestow favor upon those kept weak in the land, make them leaders, and make them inheritors.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:5

Ustuḍʿifū is passive here.

The focus is those placed in a condition of istiḍʿāf.

The verse continues by describing their becoming leaders and inheritors.

This contrast shows that imposed weakness is not an unchanging human identity or destiny.

A group under pressure may leave that situation and acquire another responsibility or position.

But the context is Moses and Pharaoh. Without examining other verses, this should not become an unconditional promise of political victory for every group calling itself oppressed.

Nor does being oppressed alone ensure the correctness of every act and claim of an individual or group.

Truth and justice require independent examination.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137: the oppressed receive the land

Al-Aʿrāf states:

وَأَوْرَثْنَا الْقَوْمَ الَّذِينَ كَانُوا يُسْتَضْعَفُونَ مَشَارِقَ الْأَرْضِ وَمَغَارِبَهَا

wa-awrathnā al-qawma alladhīna kānū yustaḍʿafūna mashāriqa al-arḍi wa-maghāribahā

“We made the people who had been kept weak inherit the eastern and western parts of the land.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137

Kānū yustaḍʿafūna indicates a continuing past condition.

The group had experienced more than one humiliating act: their oppression had continued.

The verse continues with the fulfillment of the Lord’s good promise and the destruction of Pharaoh’s and his people’s constructions.

It thus reports a change in a historical situation.

Istiḍʿāf itself, however, does not mean landownership or a right to rule; other verbs express those matters.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150: istaḍʿafūnī

In Aaron’s conversation with Moses, we read:

إِنَّ الْقَوْمَ اسْتَضْعَفُونِي وَكَادُوا يَقْتُلُونَنِي

inna al-qawma istaḍʿafūnī wa-kādū yaqtulūnanī

“The people considered me weak and almost killed me.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150

Aaron is the person subjected to istiḍʿāf here.

He is not presented as ignorant or lacking prophetic standing: he was among people who pressured him and nearly killed him.

Istiḍʿāf therefore need not mean inherent personal weakness.

Someone may have knowledge, honesty, or moral standing but insufficient scope to exercise authority in a social or political situation.

This is among the clearest witnesses distinguishing inherent inability from being weakened within a power relationship.

9. The oppressed, responsibility for rescue, and actual capacity

Al-Nisāʾ 4:75: helping those held under oppression

Al-Nisāʾ states:

وَمَا لَكُمْ لَا تُقَاتِلُونَ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَالْمُسْتَضْعَفِينَ مِنَ الرِّجَالِ وَالنِّسَاءِ وَالْوِلْدَانِ

wa-mā lakum lā tuqātilūna fī sabīli allāhi wa-al-mustaḍʿafīna mina al-rijāli wa-al-nisāʾi wa-al-wildāni

“What is the matter with you that you do not fight in The God’s path and for the men, women, and children kept weak?”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:75

These oppressed people then ask to be brought out of an unjust settlement and given a protector and helper.

Here istiḍʿāf concerns insecurity and difficulty leaving an oppressive environment.

The verse also shows that the oppressed are not limited to one age or sex: it names men, women, and children.

But this verse alone is not an unlimited license to initiate any violence in the name of supporting the oppressed.

Understanding the context of fighting, limits of confrontation, prohibition of transgression, and actual means of rescue requires examining the relevant verses together.

Lexically, mustaḍʿafīn are those placed under weakness and pressure; the word itself does not determine the action needed to support them.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:97 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:98: claimed weakness and actual inability

Al-Nisāʾ 4:97 states:

قَالُوا كُنَّا مُسْتَضْعَفِينَ فِي الْأَرْضِ

qālū kunnā mustaḍʿafīna fī al-arḍi

“They said: We were oppressed in the land.”

They are answered:

أَلَمْ تَكُنْ أَرْضُ اللَّهِ وَاسِعَةً فَتُهَاجِرُوا فِيهَا

a-lam takun arḍu allāhi wāsiʿatan fa-tuhājirū fīhā

“Was The God’s earth not spacious enough for you to migrate in it?”

The next verse, however, makes an exception:

إِلَّا الْمُسْتَضْعَفِينَ مِنَ الرِّجَالِ وَالنِّسَاءِ وَالْوِلْدَانِ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ حِيلَةً وَلَا يَهْتَدُونَ سَبِيلًا

illā al-mustaḍʿafīna mina al-rijāli wa-al-nisāʾi wa-al-wildāni lā yastaṭīʿūna ḥīlatan wa-lā yahtadūna sabīlan

“Except the men, women, and children kept weak who have no means and cannot find a way.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:98

Together, these verses clarify an important distinction.

Someone may describe themselves as oppressed while retaining a real possibility of leaving the oppressive situation.

Another person may genuinely lack any practical route, means, or capacity to leave.

Verse 98 gives two explicit criteria:

لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ حِيلَةً: they have no means of finding a solution.

وَلَا يَهْتَدُونَ سَبِيلًا: they cannot find a way.

The label oppressed is therefore insufficient for assessing responsibility: actual possibilities for choice and action also need examination.

Yet this distinction should not license blaming those with no way out, those in danger, or those needing others’ help to escape.

The Qur’an itself excepts those genuinely unable.

The separate study “Migration in the Qur’an” also examines this through actual limitations of routes, resources, and others’ responsibilities.

Al-Ṭabarī: lack of means and knowledge of the route in the migration exception

10. Does being oppressed always establish being right?

The Qur’an sometimes presents the oppressed as victims of pressure and repression; elsewhere, people described with the same term also bear responsibility for following misguided leaders.

Sabaʾ 34:31, Sabaʾ 34:32, and Sabaʾ 34:33: the oppressed and the arrogant

Sabaʾ reports a conversation between those kept weak and those who acted arrogantly.

The first group says:

لَوْلَا أَنْتُمْ لَكُنَّا مُؤْمِنِينَ

lawlā antum la-kunnā muʾminīna

“Were it not for you, we would have been believers.”

Sabaʾ 34:31

The arrogant group responds by asking whether they prevented them from guidance after it came.

The oppressed then describe plotting by night and day and pressure to disbelieve and assign equals.

This conversation shows that power relationships can influence human belief and conduct.

But the Qur’an also raises personal responsibility in this scene.

Mustaḍʿaf therefore does not always mean a person with no choice or responsibility.

Conversely, this conversation should not be used to deny actual social pressures.

Someone may be under others’ pressure while retaining choice within a range of their conduct.

One social label cannot determine the extent of freedom and responsibility.

Ibrāhīm 14:21 and Ghāfir 40:47

These verses also describe conversations between the weak and the arrogant.

The weak tell those they followed that they were their followers and ask whether they can now remove some punishment from them.

The verses show that following powerful people may accompany dependence and social weakness.

But social weakness alone does not remove every individual responsibility.

Nor does a person’s or group’s arrogance establish the legitimacy of their commands.

11. Ḍiʿf: increase and multiplication

The second branch’s meaning

Ḍiʿf, with kasrah on ḍād, concerns increasing an amount by its like.

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad, quoted by Ibn Fāris, explains iḍʿāf, taḍʿīf, and muḍāʿafah as adding a like amount to the original so that the result is twice as much or more.

This differs from weakness and inability.

For example, an increase from ten to twenty is doubling.

If increase continues, the amount may multiply further.

Aḍʿāf is the plural of ḍiʿf and denotes multiples of an amount.

Does ḍiʿf always mean exactly twice as much?

Lexical sources offer different explanations of the precise reference of ḍiʿf, ḍiʿfayn, and aḍʿāf.

In familiar usage, ḍiʿf al-ʿasharah refers to twenty.

In some constructions, however, lexicographers distinguish the amount added from the sum of the original and the addition.

Ḍiʿf also denotes an unspecified increase in some contexts.

Each verse therefore requires determining whether it specifies a number or merely expresses multiplication.

For example, ḍiʿfayn concerns doubling, while aḍʿāfan kathīratan expresses abundant increase without fixing one final amount.

Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr and Lisān al-ʿArab: the added amount and resulting total

12. Multiplication in spending and reward

Al-Baqarah 2:245: abundant increase

Al-Baqarah states:

مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يُقْرِضُ اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا فَيُضَاعِفَهُ لَهُ أَضْعَافًا كَثِيرَةً

man dhā alladhī yuqriḍu allāha qarḍan ḥasanan fa-yuḍāʿifahu lahu aḍʿāfan kathīratan

“Who will lend The God a good loan, so that He may multiply it for them many times over?”

Al-Baqarah 2:245

Fa-yuḍāʿifahu is Form III and expresses an increase in amount.

Aḍʿāfan kathīratan emphasizes the abundance of this increase.

The expression itself does not specify a number.

This verse alone does not mean that every act of spending in every situation is multiplied exactly ten, a hundred, or seven hundred times.

The nature and extent of reward require examining the verses together.

A good loan here concerns spending and good action in The God’s path; it does not mean that The God borrows like a needy person to cover expenses.

Al-Baqarah 2:261: seven ears and a hundred grains

Al-Baqarah states:

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ كَمَثَلِ حَبَّةٍ أَنْبَتَتْ سَبْعَ سَنَابِلَ فِي كُلِّ سُنْبُلَةٍ مِائَةُ حَبَّةٍ

mathalu alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum fī sabīli allāhi ka-mathali ḥabbatin anbatat sabʿa sanābila fī kulli sunbulatin miʾatu ḥabbatin

“Those who spend their wealth in The God’s path are like a grain growing seven ears, each containing a hundred grains.”

Al-Baqarah 2:261

It continues:

وَاللَّهُ يُضَاعِفُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ

wa-allāhu yuḍāʿifu li-man yashāʾu

“The God multiplies for whomever He wills.”

The image takes one grain to seven hundred.

Yet the verse is a comparison concerning the expansion and increase of spending’s outcome.

The image itself should not become a fixed economic formula guaranteeing a measurable material return on every act of giving.

The following verses also condemn reminders of generosity, causing hurt, and ostentation in spending.

Multiplied reward does not make intention and manner of conduct unimportant.

Al-Baqarah 2:265: ḍiʿfayn

Another comparison states:

فَآتَتْ أُكُلَهَا ضِعْفَيْنِ

fa-ātat ukulahā ḍiʿfayni

“It yielded its produce twice over.”

Al-Baqarah 2:265

Ḍiʿfayn describes increased garden produce here.

This shows that the root’s increase branch is not limited to reward in the hereafter: it can concern natural produce.

But ḍiʿfayn itself does not mean rain, blessing, or plant growth; the sentence as a whole identifies the increase in produce.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:40: multiplying good

Al-Nisāʾ states:

وَإِنْ تَكُ حَسَنَةً يُضَاعِفْهَا

wa-in taku ḥasanatan yuḍāʿifhā

“If there is a good deed, He multiplies it.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:40

Muḍāʿafah concerns a good deed and its reward here.

Al-Anʿām 6:160 also states that whoever brings a good deed receives ten like it.

These verses can be studied together thematically, while retaining their differences in wording.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:40 gives no specified amount; Al-Anʿām 6:160 gives the number ten.

Al-Rūm 30:39: al-muḍʿifūn

Al-Rūm states:

وَمَا آتَيْتُمْ مِنْ زَكَاةٍ تُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَ اللَّهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُضْعِفُونَ

wa-mā ātaytum min zakātin turīdūna wajha allāhi fa-ulāʾika humu al-muḍʿifūna

“Whatever you give in zakat, seeking The God’s pleasure, those are the ones receiving manifold increase.”

Al-Rūm 30:39

Muḍʿifūn belongs to the increase branch here.

It means people who receive increase and multiplied reward through their deeds.

It does not mean weak people in this context.

The verse begins with ribā and increase sought in people’s wealth that does not increase with The God.

Zakat given seeking The God’s countenance is then contrasted with it.

It distinguishes two kinds of increase: that sought in others’ wealth and that with The God for good action.

This distinction comes from context; Ḍ ʿ F itself expresses only increase here.

Al-Ṭabarī: al-muḍʿifūn and increased reward

13. Multiplication in ribā and harmful increase

Āl ʿImrān states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَأْكُلُوا الرِّبَا أَضْعَافًا مُضَاعَفَةً

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā taʾkulū al-ribā aḍʿāfan muḍāʿafatan

“You who have believed, do not consume ribā in repeatedly multiplied amounts.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:130

Aḍʿāfan and muḍāʿafah belong to the same family here.

Aḍʿāfan denotes multiples.

Muḍāʿafah describes them and emphasizes addition and repeated increase.

Historical and exegetical accounts associate this expression with increasing a debt in exchange for extending the payment deadline.

For example, a debt could increase at maturity and receive another increase if repayment was postponed again.

The question of ribā should not be resolved solely through the meaning of Ḍ ʿ F.

Al-Baqarah 2:275 also prohibits ribā independently.

Al-Baqarah 2:279 speaks of returning to the principal and neither wronging nor being wronged.

Without examining these verses, aḍʿāfan muḍāʿafah should not be restricted to the conclusion that only multiplied ribā is prohibited and other forms have no restrictions.

Conversely, muḍāʿafah itself does not name ribā.

Increase can concern the reward for good or a garden’s produce, just as it can occur in an unjust financial relationship.

Its legal and moral value depends on its subject and context.

14. Multiplied punishment and responsibility

Al-Aʿrāf 7:38: requesting increased punishment

Al-Aʿrāf states:

رَبَّنَا هَٰؤُلَاءِ أَضَلُّونَا فَآتِهِمْ عَذَابًا ضِعْفًا مِنَ النَّارِ

rabbanā hāʾulāʾi aḍallūnā fa-ātihim ʿadhāban ḍiʿfan mina al-nāri

“Our Lord, these led us astray; give them multiplied punishment from the Fire.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:38

The answer states:

لِكُلٍّ ضِعْفٌ وَلَٰكِنْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

li-kullin ḍiʿfun wa-lākin lā taʿlamūna

“For each there is an increase, but you do not know.”

Ḍiʿf concerns increased punishment here.

The root itself, however, does not explain the reason for the increase.

The context concerns misleaders, followers, and each group’s responsibility.

Increased punishment here must therefore be considered in connection with conduct and responsibility, rather than a numerical rule independent of behavior.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:30: ḍiʿfayn

Al-Aḥzāb states:

مَنْ يَأْتِ مِنْكُنَّ بِفَاحِشَةٍ مُبَيِّنَةٍ يُضَاعَفْ لَهَا الْعَذَابُ ضِعْفَيْنِ

man yaʾti minkunna bi-fāḥishatin mubayyinatin yuḍāʿaf lahā al-ʿadhābu ḍiʿfayni

“Whoever among you commits a manifestly indecent act will have her punishment doubled.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:30

Both the verb of multiplication and ḍiʿfayn appear here.

But the important qualification is fāḥishah mubayyinah, a manifestly indecent act.

Increased punishment is connected to a specified act.

The root itself does not mean that every person’s every error receives punishment increased at one rate in every situation.

Al-Ṭabarī: ḍiʿfayn, multiplication, and differing numerical analyses

Al-Aḥzāb 33:68: requesting doubling

The same surah states:

رَبَّنَا آتِهِمْ ضِعْفَيْنِ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ

rabbanā ātihim ḍiʿfayni mina al-ʿadhābi

“Our Lord, give them twice the punishment.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:68

This request concerns people whom others followed and through whom they went astray.

Again, the added amount must be distinguished from the reason for requesting it.

Ḍiʿfayn concerns doubling; leaders’ and followers’ responsibility comes from context.

Hūd 11:20, Al-Furqān 25:69, and Al-Isrāʾ 17:75

These verses also describe multiplied punishment.

Hūd 11:20 places it in the context of people who obstruct The God’s path.

In Al-Furqān 25:69, increased punishment concerns acts named in the preceding verses.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:75 uses a conditional construction about a hypothetical inclination toward something contrary to revelation.

These uses show that muḍāʿafah may concern reward or punishment.

But muḍāʿaf alone names neither mercy nor punishment.

It expresses only an increase in amount or intensity.

15. Related roots

Ḍ ʿ F and Q W Y

Strength contrasts with weakness.

Strength may be physical, social, economic, or concern the ability to perform a task.

Al-Rūm 30:54 uses both to describe human stages of life.

Weakness should therefore not always mean muscular inability, nor strength always bodily power.

Study of Q W Y

Ḍ ʿ F and W H N

Wahn also concerns frailty and reduced firmness.

Āl ʿImrān 3:146 states:

فَمَا وَهَنُوا لِمَا أَصَابَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَمَا ضَعُفُوا وَمَا اسْتَكَانُوا

fa-mā wahanū li-mā aṣābahum fī sabīli allāhi wa-mā ḍaʿufū wa-mā istakānū

“They did not falter because of what befell them in The God’s path, nor weaken, nor submit.”

Wahn, ḍaʿf, and istikānah appear together here.

Their proximity shows connection without complete identity.

Wahn emphasizes faltering, ḍaʿf reduced capacity, and istikānah becoming subdued and yielding in this context.

Ḍ ʿ F and ʿ J Z

ʿAjz concerns inability to perform a task.

Ḍaʿf can mean insufficient capacity even when the task has not become entirely impossible.

Someone may be weak but still able to do limited work.

Weakness is therefore not necessarily complete inability.

This distinction matters in assessing responsibility and actual human capacity.

Ḍ ʿ F and DH L L

Dhull can concern abasement or being placed in a subordinate position.

Ḍaʿf indicates insufficient capacity.

A weak person may preserve their dignity and honor.

Conversely, someone with substantial resources may act in a degrading way.

Weakness and abasement should therefore not be treated as synonyms.

In imposed oppression, social subordination may accompany weakness, but the concepts remain distinct.

Study of DH L L

Ḍ ʿ F and Ẓ L M

Ẓulm concerns injustice and placing rights or things wrongly.

Istiḍʿāf can be one method of injustice, as Pharaoh’s conduct in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:4 shows.

Yet not every weakness results from injustice.

Childhood weakness or declining capacity in old age can be natural conditions of life.

Injustice can also take forms not directly limited to weakening another person.

Ḍ ʿ F and K TH R

Kathrah concerns abundance in number or amount.

Muḍāʿafah concerns increase relative to an earlier amount.

Something may be abundant without our knowing how many times it has increased from the original.

A small amount may also double while remaining smaller than another amount.

Aḍʿāf and kathīr are therefore not synonyms.

Al-Baqarah 2:245 combines the concepts in aḍʿāfan kathīratan: multiplication and its abundance.

Study of K TH R

16. Translation proposals for Forqan

The weakness and inability branch

ضَعُفَ: became weak; lost strength.

ضَعْف / ضُعْف: weakness, inability, or insufficient capacity, according to context.

ضَعِيف: weak or unable.

ضُعَفَاء: the weak; plural of ḍaʿīf.

ضِعَاف: weak people or those lacking capacity.

أَضْعَفُ: weaker.

اِسْتَضْعَفَ: considered weak; in domination contexts, weakened or kept powerless.

يَسْتَضْعِفُ: weakens or considers weak.

اُسْتُضْعِفُوا: were considered weak or placed in weakness.

مُسْتَضْعَف: someone considered weak or kept powerless.

مُسْتَضْعَفُون: the oppressed; people subjected to imposed weakness or limited capacity.

For mustaḍʿaf, “kept weak” is more precise than simply “weak” in many verses because it preserves the role of external agents.

Each verse, however, requires establishing whether it emphasizes being considered weak or being kept weak.

The increase and multiplication branch

ضِعْف: an added equivalent or double, according to the construction.

ضِعْفَيْن: twice as much.

أَضْعَاف: multiples.

ضَاعَفَ: multiplied.

يُضَاعِفُ: multiplies.

يُضَاعَفُ: is multiplied.

مُضَاعَفَة: increased and multiplied.

مُضْعِفُون: people receiving manifold increase.

تَضْعِيف: adding and multiplying; in another use, considering weak or attributing weakness.

مُضَاعَف: multiplied; in another use, layered.

Verse translation must distinguish weakness from ḍiʿf meaning doubling.

Likewise, aḍʿaf in aḍʿafu jundan means a weaker host, rather than multiplication of the army.

17. Conclusion

Arabic Ḍ ʿ F has two distinct semantic branches.

The first concerns weakness and insufficient capacity.

Weakness may be physical, mental, social, economic, or concern the ability to perform a particular task.

Ḍaʿīf, ḍuʿafāʾ, and istiḍʿāf belong to this family.

Istiḍʿāf sometimes concerns considering weak; elsewhere it concerns pressuring people and keeping them powerless.

The Qur’an shows this distinction through Pharaoh and the Children of Israel, Aaron’s situation, support for the oppressed, and migration.

It also connects natural human weakness with responsibility proportionate to capacity.

In Al-Rūm 30:54, weakness is part of the natural stages of life.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:28 presents human limits alongside divine easing.

In Al-Anfāl 8:66, existing weakness leads to a lighter burden of responsibility.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 combines a person’s weakness with the need for justice in stating rights and financial obligations.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:4, by contrast, exemplifies imposed oppression created by the conduct of those exercising domination.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:97 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:98 also distinguish claiming to be oppressed from actual inability to find escape.

The second branch concerns doubling and multiplication.

This increase may concern produce, wealth, reward, or punishment.

Al-Baqarah 2:245 and Al-Baqarah 2:261 describe increased reward and the result of spending.

Al-Rūm 30:39 compares the increase sought in ribā with that connected to zakat given seeking The God’s countenance.

Āl ʿImrān 3:130 places multiplication in the context of ribā.

Other verses connect muḍāʿafah with punishment and responsibility.

Increase itself is therefore neither inherently good nor bad: its subject, cause, and manner determine its context.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Insufficient capacity and weakness contrasted with strength; Form X concerns considering weak or placing in weakness. An independent branch concerns adding an equivalent to an original amount and doubling or multiplying it.

Meaning in the main Qur’anic uses:

ضَعْف / ضُعْف: weakness and insufficient capacity.

ضَعِيف / ضُعَفَاء: weak; the weak.

مُسْتَضْعَف: someone considered weak or kept powerless.

ضِعْف / أَضْعَاف: doubling or multiplication.

يُضَاعِفُ: multiplies the amount or outcome of something.

Each use must distinguish natural weakness, circumstantial weakness, imposed oppression, and increased amount: although recorded within one lexical family, they are not one concept.
