1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The triliteral Arabic root «ف ي ض» (F Y Ḍ) has yāʾ as its weak middle radical. Its central meaning concerns flowing, streaming, and pouring forth. Abundant water flows from its channel or vessel, tears stream from an eye, groups of people set out from a place, and discussion of a subject develops.

Although these uses concern different subjects, they share a recognizable idea: something leaves its previous condition of rest or confinement and enters a state of flow or movement.

In the Qur’an, this root concerns tears, water, people departing from ʿArafāt, engaging in an activity, and sustained discussion. All its occurrences are verbs. The nine occurrences appear in eight verses across seven surahs: two in Form I and seven in Form IV.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: nine occurrences in eight verses across seven surahs

Important forms in this family include:

  • فَاضَ / يَفِيضُ (fāḍa / yafīḍu): to flow, stream, overflow, or increase until flowing. Qur’anic use: yes, as «تَفِيضُ» in Al-Māʾidah 5:83 and Al-Tawbah 9:92.
  • أَفَاضَ / يُفِيضُ (afāḍa / yufīḍu): to cause to flow, pour, set out, or carry forward discussion or activity. Qur’anic use: yes, seven occurrences in various forms.
  • أَفَضْتُمْ (afaḍtum): you set out, or entered into a subject and developed discussion. Qur’anic use: yes, in Al-Baqarah 2:198 and Al-Nūr 24:14.
  • أَفِيضُوا (afīḍū): set out, or pour and cause to flow. Qur’anic use: yes, in Al-Baqarah 2:199 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:50.
  • أَفَاضَ (afāḍa): he set out or flowed forth; in discussion, he engaged with a subject. Qur’anic use: yes, in Al-Baqarah 2:199.
  • تُفِيضُونَ (tufīḍūna): you enter into, pursue, or discuss a subject. Qur’anic use: yes, in Yūnus 10:61 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:8.
  • فَيْض (fayḍ): abundant flow, overflowing, or abundance flowing forth. Qur’anic use: no, as an independent noun.
  • فَيَضَان (fayaḍān): increasing and overflowing water. Qur’anic use: no.
  • إِفَاضَة (ifāḍah): causing to flow, pouring forth, or setting out; in pilgrimage, departing from a station of standing. Qur’anic use: no, as an independent verbal noun.
  • فَائِض (fāʾiḍ): flowing or overflowing; in some uses, exceeding a need or specified amount. Qur’anic use: no.
  • فَيَّاض (fayyāḍ): very generous, giving abundantly, or possessing abundant flow or generosity. Qur’anic use: no.
  • مُفِيض (mufīḍ): one who causes something to flow or engages in discussion of a subject. Qur’anic use: no.
  • مُفَاض (mufāḍ): something caused to flow or poured out. Qur’anic use: no.
  • اِسْتَفَاضَ / يَسْتَفِيضُ (istafāḍa / yastafīḍu): to spread, become widely known of a report, or engage extensively in discussion. Qur’anic use: no.
  • مُسْتَفِيض (mustafīḍ): widespread, widely known, or a report circulating among people. Qur’anic use: no.
  • فُيُوض (fuyūḍ): another verbal noun of fāḍa; also the plural of fayḍ meaning a river. Qur’anic use: no.

Lisān al-ʿArab: root forms, fuyūḍ, and poetic evidence

The Qur’an uses afāḍa for human movement as well as water and discourse. Form IV in this root therefore cannot be restricted to making someone else pour something: in some uses, the subject itself sets out. The seven Form IV occurrences are the total represented by the subsequent Qur’anic forms in the list above, rather than seven additional occurrences.

Likewise, expressions concerning widely circulating reports, spiritual effusion, or technical mystical concepts developed in the history of language and thought should not be presented as the direct meanings of Qur’anic verses.

2. The linguistic core: from flowing water to movement and discourse

Fāḍa al-māʾ: water flowing

In Arabic, «فَاضَ الْمَاءُ» (fāḍa al-māʾ) describes water flowing and increasing.

One clear use describes water increasing within a vessel, river, or channel until it begins to flow or passes its usual limits.

In «فَاضَ الْإِنَاءُ بِمَا فِيهِ» (fāḍa al-ināʾu bi-mā fīhi), a vessel becomes full and its contents overflow.

Applied to a river or floodwater, fāḍa can likewise describe rising water and its flow.

Two features stand out in this image: abundance or accumulation, and movement or flow.

These features, however, are not equally prominent in every derivative.

Water can flow without overflowing a particular vessel. People can also depart from a place without their number having exceeded its capacity.

Overflowing is therefore an important image within this root family, but it should not become its only possible definition.

Is the root’s core abundance or flow?

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris defines the root’s core as «جَرَيَانِ الشَّيْءِ بِسُهُولَةٍ» (jarayāni al-shayʾi bi-suhūlah): something flowing easily.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: flow and al-Rāʿī’s verse

This definition emphasizes flow.

Other lexical accounts also highlight water increasing until it spills forth.

These definitions are compatible, although their focus differs.

Abundance can cause flow; nevertheless, flow does not always require proof of a very large quantity.

For example, «أَفِيضُوا عَلَيْنَا مِنَ الْمَاءِ» (afīḍū ʿalaynā mina al-māʾ) in Al-Aʿrāf 7:50 requests some water. The verb afīḍū does not establish that the speakers necessarily seek an enormous flood or an overflowing vessel.

In Qur’anic study, therefore, causing to flow or pouring forth should remain central, while intensity and quantity are determined by context.

Departing from a place: people setting out

Classical Arabic uses «أَفَاضَ الْقَوْمُ» (afāḍa al-qawm) for people setting out from a place.

For example, afāḍa describes the collective departure of people from ʿArafāt.

The image connects this use with flowing water. As water flows out of a channel, a group moves from its gathering place toward a destination.

This explanation does not imply that the people must move extremely fast, disorderly, or like a destructive flood.

In Arabic, collective ifāḍah can describe continuous, extensive movement; the verb alone does not specify its exact speed.

Setting out is also different from arriving. A person or group can depart from a place while arrival is expressed by another verb or sentence.

This distinction matters in Al-Baqarah 2:198, which describes departure from ʿArafāt followed by remembrance of The God at Al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām.

The flow of speech and engagement with a subject

Another expression is «أَفَاضَ فِي الْحَدِيثِ» (afāḍa fī al-ḥadīth).

It describes a person or group entering into a subject, developing discussion of it, or continuing a conversation.

Here, the image of flow extends from tangible material to linguistic activity.

Speech proceeds and develops like flowing water.

This image does not establish that every use of afāḍa fī necessarily describes pointless, false, or exceptionally lengthy speech.

A person may discuss a true and beneficial subject at length, or discuss an unfounded subject and repeat its claims.

Thus, the value of speech is determined by its subject, evidence, and content, rather than by the verb ifāḍah itself.

Al-Nūr 24:14 concerns harmful speech and a false accusation, whereas Yūnus 10:61 uses the verb in describing The God’s comprehensive knowledge of human affairs and activities.

These uses show that the root itself carries no fixed negative moral value.

3. Early Arabic evidence: flow, movement, and scattering

Setting divining arrows in motion in Abū Dhuʾayb’s poetry

A verse by Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī reads:

وَكَأَنَّهُنَّ رِبَابَةٌ وَكَأَنَّهُ
يَسَرٌ يُفِيضُ عَلَى الْقِدَاحِ وَيَصْدَعُ

wa-kaʾannahunna ribābatun wa-kaʾannahu / yasarun yufīḍu ʿalā al-qidāḥi wa-yaṣdaʿu

English translation

They seem a bundle of divining arrows, and he a player who sets the arrows in motion and separates them.

The poet describes a group of female wild asses and their accompanying male, comparing them to divining arrows and the person handling them.

In this use, al-ribābah concerns a collection of divining arrows or their container.

Yasar here designates someone operating those arrows.

In this verse, «يُفِيضُ عَلَى الْقِدَاحِ» (yufīḍu ʿalā al-qidāḥ) concerns moving or turning the arrows. Some early grammatical explanations understand ʿalā here in a sense close to bi-.

This example of ifāḍah concerns moving objects in a recognized practice, rather than water or pilgrims departing.

It shows that the root family can describe the movement of things set in motion by a person.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived both before and after the emergence of Islam. Without dating this particular ode, its verse cannot confidently be called pre-Qur’anic evidence, although it is preserved in early Arabic poetry collections.

Al-Mufradāt and Asās al-Balāghah: moving arrows and poetic evidence

Ifāḍah in camels bringing up cud

A verse by al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī reads:

وَأَفَضْنَ بَعْدَ كُظُومِهِنَّ بِجِرَّةٍ
مِنْ ذِي الْأَبَارِقِ إِذْ رَعَيْنَ حَقِيلَا

wa-afaḍna baʿda kuẓūmihinna bi-jirratin / min dhī al-abāriqi idh raʿayna ḥaqīlā

English translation

After holding back their cud, they brought up a mouthful of it: fodder from Dhū al-Abāriq, when they grazed at Ḥaqīl.

Here, jirrah concerns a camel bringing food back up from its stomach to chew it again.

Kuẓūm describes holding back or restraining this movement.

Thus, afaḍna describes bringing something out from within the animal or setting it in motion.

Its physical image is close to flowing liquid, although its subject is not river water.

Versions of the verse differ over the place name Dhī al-Abāriq or Dhī al-Abāṭiḥ. The variant does not substantially alter the meaning of afaḍna, but it should be acknowledged when identifying the evidence.

Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī was an Umayyad poet. This verse is therefore evidence of early Arabic usage after the Qur’an’s revelation, rather than securely pre-Islamic poetry.

Asās al-Balāghah and other lexicons: al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī’s verse

Reports circulating and discussion developing

Arabic uses «اسْتَفَاضَ الْخَبَرُ» (istafāḍa al-khabar) for a report spreading and becoming known among people.

Likewise, «أَفَاضُوا فِي الْحَدِيثِ» (afāḍū fī al-ḥadīth) can describe extensive discussion of a subject.

These uses are close in meaning but differ in construction.

In istafāḍa al-khabar, the report itself is what spreads. In afāḍa al-qawmu fī al-ḥadīth, people engage in discussing a subject.

Istafāḍa and afāḍa should therefore not be treated as interchangeable in every sentence without examination.

A report becoming widespread also does not establish its truth.

A report may circulate widely and still be false, just as a true report may be known to very few people.

The F Y Ḍ family therefore provides no independent criterion for establishing the truth of a report.

Finding from the evidence: flowing water or liquid, setting objects in motion, collective movement, and developing discourse are recognized fields of this Arabic root. Their connection can be explained, but the subject and sentence construction determine each use.

4. Tafīḍu: tears flowing in two different situations

Al-Māʾidah 5:83: tears upon recognizing truth

Verse text

وَإِذَا سَمِعُوا مَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَى الرَّسُولِ تَرَىٰ أَعْيُنَهُمْ تَفِيضُ مِنَ الدَّمْعِ مِمَّا عَرَفُوا مِنَ الْحَقِّ يَقُولُونَ رَبَّنَا آمَنَّا فَاكْتُبْنَا مَعَ الشَّاهِدِينَ

wa-idhā samiʿū mā unzila ilā al-rasūli tarā aʿyunahum tafīḍu mina al-damʿi mimmā ʿarafū mina al-ḥaqqi yaqūlūna rabbanā āmannā fa-uktubnā maʿa al-shāhidīna

English translation

When they hear what has been sent down to the Messenger, you see their eyes streaming with tears because of the truth they have recognized. They say, “Our Lord, we believe; so record us among the witnesses.”

Examining the use

Tafīḍu is an imperfect verb in Form I.

The subject of tafīḍu is the implicit pronoun hiya, referring back to aʿyunahum; aʿyunahum itself is the object of tarā. Semantically, the flow is attributed to their eyes.

The Qur’an does not simply say that tears flow: it describes their eyes streaming with tears.

This construction presents tears as something flowing from the eyes.

«مِنَ الدَّمْعِ» (mina al-damʿ) connects the flow with tears. Its grammatical analysis has several explanations, including an understood “from the abundance of tears”; the resulting meaning is the flowing of tears.

Al-Jadwal on Al-Māʾidah 5:83: the object of tarā and subject of tafīḍu

Thus, tafīḍu mina al-damʿ means tears flowing from the eyes, rather than the eyes themselves spilling out of their place.

In the linguistic image, the eye resembles a vessel or source from which tears flow.

The boundary between imagery and direct meaning should nevertheless be maintained.

The Qur’an describes actual weeping and flowing tears. The verb’s metaphorical or imagistic extension does not negate the reality of the weeping.

The cause of weeping in the verse

«مِمَّا عَرَفُوا مِنَ الْحَقِّ» (mimmā ʿarafū mina al-ḥaqq) makes the cause or setting of this reaction clear.

They have recognized something of the truth and shed tears in response.

In this verse, therefore, flowing tears are connected with recognition and belief.

This connection does not mean that every instance of weeping indicates recognition of truth.

People may weep from grief, pain, fear, joy, helplessness, or other causes.

The Qur’an itself presents another instance of flowing tears with a different cause in Al-Tawbah 9:92.

The meaning of tafīḍu thus remains the same in both verses, while the cause of weeping changes.

Nor does this verse establish that anyone who does not weep on hearing the Qur’an has failed to recognize truth.

It describes the condition of a particular group, rather than making tears the sole measure of everyone’s belief.

Al-Tawbah 9:92: tears from grief and lack of resources

Verse text

وَلَا عَلَى الَّذِينَ إِذَا مَا أَتَوْكَ لِتَحْمِلَهُمْ قُلْتَ لَا أَجِدُ مَا أَحْمِلُكُمْ عَلَيْهِ تَوَلَّوْا وَأَعْيُنُهُمْ تَفِيضُ مِنَ الدَّمْعِ حَزَنًا أَلَّا يَجِدُوا مَا يُنْفِقُونَ

wa-lā ʿalā alladhīna idhā mā atawka li-taḥmilahum qulta lā ajidu mā aḥmilukum ʿalayhi tawallaw wa-aʿyunuhum tafīḍu mina al-damʿi ḥazanan allā yajidū mā yunfiqūna

English translation

Nor is there blame upon those who came to you for mounts and to whom you said, “I can find nothing on which to carry you.” They turned back, their eyes streaming with tears, grieving that they could find nothing to spend.

Examining the use

Here too, tafīḍu comes from the same Form I verb.

In wa-aʿyunuhum tafīḍu, aʿyunuhum is the nominal subject, and the verbal clause tafīḍu is its predicate; the verb’s subject is an implicit pronoun referring back to the eyes. Mina al-damʿ connects this flow with tears.

Al-Jadwal on Al-Tawbah 9:92: the nominal subject and predicate

This time, however, the cause of the tears is explicitly stated as ḥazanan: out of grief.

The verse concerns people who needed transport or resources to participate in a responsibility but could not obtain what was needed.

They consequently turned back in grief.

This verse shows that inability to do something differs from unwillingness to do it.

The people described wished to participate but lacked the necessary means.

Their tears thus occur in the context of grief at being unable to fulfil a responsibility.

Tafīḍu itself, however, does not mean grief or inability.

Grief is conveyed by ḥazanan, and the shortage of resources by the preceding clauses.

This distinction is essential when defining the root.

Comparing the two verses

In Al-Māʾidah 5:83, tears arise from recognition of truth and a response of belief.

In Al-Tawbah 9:92, tears flow from grief at lacking necessary resources.

In both, eyes stream with tears.

Therefore, fayḍ in these two verses describes the flow of tears, rather than its psychological cause or moral value.

Recognition of truth is prominent in the first; grief and lack of resources in the second.

These uses show why a specialized meaning such as “tears of faith” or “weeping from humility” should not be extracted from the root itself.

Tafīḍu should also not be reduced to “their eyes were full of tears”: the verb conveys flowing and streaming, rather than merely the presence of tears in the eye.

5. Afīḍū ʿalaynā: pouring forth and providing water

Al-Aʿrāf 7:50

Verse text

وَنَادَىٰ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ أَصْحَابَ الْجَنَّةِ أَنْ أَفِيضُوا عَلَيْنَا مِنَ الْمَاءِ أَوْ مِمَّا رَزَقَكُمُ اللَّهُ قَالُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ حَرَّمَهُمَا عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ

wa-nādā aṣḥābu al-nāri aṣḥāba al-jannati an afīḍū ʿalaynā mina al-māʾi aw mimmā razaqakumu Allāhu qālū inna Allāha ḥarramahumā ʿalā al-kāfirīna

English translation

The inhabitants of the Fire call to the inhabitants of the Garden: “Pour upon us some water, or some of what The God has provided you.” They say, “The God has forbidden both to the disbelievers.”

Examining the use

Afīḍū is a Form IV imperative.

In this verse, the command addresses the inhabitants of the Garden, while the request comes from the inhabitants of the Fire.

ʿAlaynā specifies the direction of pouring or delivery, and mina al-māʾ identifies what is requested.

The verb here therefore means pouring or causing water to flow toward those requesting it.

This use remains very close to the root’s physical core.

Afīḍū does not, however, establish that the speakers necessarily request a large quantity of water.

Mina al-māʾ is compatible with a request for some water.

Conversely, the verb cannot be reduced to “bring water,” because ifāḍah emphasizes pouring or causing to flow.

“Pour upon us some water” better preserves the Arabic image.

Is the request only for drinking water?

The expression itself contains no verb meaning to drink.

The verse requests water or something from the provision of the Garden’s inhabitants.

The need for water and its benefit are clear from the condition of the Fire’s inhabitants, but afīḍū itself does not specify how the water will be used.

Consequently, translating it directly as “give us water to drink” introduces an action not expressed in the Arabic wording.

That may be a contextual interpretation, but it is not the verb’s exact equivalent.

Mina al-māʾ and mimmā razaqakumu Allāh

The verse distinguishes two objects of request using aw: “or.”

First comes water, then something of the provision The God has given the inhabitants of the Garden.

The entire request therefore cannot be restricted to water.

Nor should provision be incorporated into the lexical meaning of fayḍ.

Rizq belongs to a separate root and identifies the request’s second object here.

This verse alone also does not yield every detail of the physical condition of the Fire’s inhabitants or the nature of water and provision in the Garden.

What F Y Ḍ makes clear is a request to cause something to flow or reach them.

Ifāḍah and generosity

In extended Arabic uses, fayḍ and ifāḍah can also be connected with abundant giving.

For example, fayyāḍ may describe someone who gives generously.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:50, however, the request for water does not by itself insert the full meaning of generosity into the verb.

The next clause establishes the response of the Garden’s inhabitants.

The request to receive something, the action of pouring or delivering it, and the ruling on giving or withholding it should therefore be distinguished.

Lexical finding: in Al-Aʿrāf 7:50, afīḍū means “pour” or “cause to flow.” Mina al-māʾ identifies the requested substance; the verb itself determines neither its precise quantity nor its use.

6. Departure from ʿArafāt: actual human movement in pilgrimage

Al-Baqarah 2:198

Verse text

لَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ أَنْ تَبْتَغُوا فَضْلًا مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ فَإِذَا أَفَضْتُمْ مِنْ عَرَفَاتٍ فَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ عِنْدَ الْمَشْعَرِ الْحَرَامِ وَاذْكُرُوهُ كَمَا هَدَاكُمْ وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ مِنْ قَبْلِهِ لَمِنَ الضَّالِّينَ

laysa ʿalaykum junāḥun an tabtaghū faḍlan min rabbikum fa-idhā afaḍtum min ʿArafātin fa-udhkurū Allāha ʿinda al-mashʿari al-ḥarāmi wa-udhkurūhu ka-mā hadākum wa-in kuntum min qablihi la-mina al-ḍāllīna

English translation

There is no blame upon you for seeking bounty from your Lord. Then, when you set out from ʿArafāt, remember The God at Al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām, and remember Him as He has guided you, although before that you were among those astray.

Examining the use

Afaḍtum is a Form IV perfect verb.

Its subject is the plural addressees, and min ʿArafāt specifies the departure point.

The verb’s clear meaning here is therefore setting out or moving forth from ʿArafāt.

Whereas Al-Aʿrāf 7:50 concerns water, this verse concerns people moving from a specified place.

This semantic extension is recognized in early Arabic and should not be treated as merely a new metaphor introduced by an interpreter.

Does ifāḍah mean leaving ʿArafāt?

In this context, yes: departure from ʿArafāt is the expression’s clear meaning.

The verb’s meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from all the details of the rites.

Afaḍtum min ʿArafāt describes departure from ʿArafāt. The verb itself specifies neither the exact hour and minute of departure, the required length of standing at ʿArafāt, nor the means of transport.

These matters cannot be derived from the root itself.

Nor does ifāḍah establish that the movement must be extremely fast.

Some early lexical accounts associate collective ifāḍah with large numbers and haste, but these features do not amount to a requirement to hurry in every Arabic use.

Here, actual movement and its direction are what matter.

The relationship between ʿArafāt and Al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām

The verse places two locations within a connected construction: ʿArafāt and Al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām.

Min ʿArafāt gives the point of departure, while ʿinda al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām gives the place to which the command to remember The God applies.

Presence at ʿArafāt and remembrance of The God at Al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām are therefore two actual matters in the verse.

Afaḍtum does not, however, establish that the Qur’an has specified every detail of the route, every intermediate stopping place, or the caravan’s manner of travel.

Conversely, departure from ʿArafāt should not be reduced to a “spiritual movement from recognition to awareness.”

The name ʿArafāt may invite separate linguistic and historical investigation, but this verse concerns a place and people’s movement.

Its direct meaning should therefore be preserved.

Al-Baqarah 2:199

Verse text

ثُمَّ أَفِيضُوا مِنْ حَيْثُ أَفَاضَ النَّاسُ وَاسْتَغْفِرُوا اللَّهَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

thumma afīḍū min ḥaythu afāḍa al-nāsu wa-istaghfirū Allāha inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

English translation

Then set out from the same place from which the people have set out, and seek forgiveness from The God. Indeed, The God is forgiving and merciful.

Examining the use

F Y Ḍ appears twice in this verse.

First comes afīḍū, a plural imperative, then afāḍa, a third-person perfect verb.

The verse thus connects the addressees’ movement with that of al-nās: the people.

The main construction is:

«ثُمَّ أَفِيضُوا مِنْ حَيْثُ أَفَاضَ النَّاسُ» (thumma afīḍū min ḥaythu afāḍa al-nās).

Ḥaythu concerns a place or position, while min marks the point of departure.

The verse therefore tells the addressees to depart from the same place from which the people departed.

Its direct command concerns movement and its starting point, rather than pouring water, speaking, or abundant wealth.

Does this verse concern equality in pilgrimage?

Contextually, one can ask why the Qur’an connects the addressees’ movement with that of the people.

Historical and exegetical reports discuss differences between groups’ positions in pilgrimage practices and their social privileges.

These reports can provide material for historical research, but this expression in the verse names no particular tribe, privileged group, or custom.

All the details of such accounts should therefore not be imported into the verse without independent evidence.

Nevertheless, the command min ḥaythu afāḍa al-nās is clear: the addressees’ movement is connected with that of the people, rather than with a privilege inherent in the word ifāḍah.

That connection can matter in reflection on pilgrimage and people’s common position, but such a conclusion should be drawn from the whole context.

Ifāḍah and seeking forgiveness

The verse continues:

«وَاسْتَغْفِرُوا اللَّهَ» (wa-istaghfirū Allāh): seek forgiveness from The God.

Departure from the designated place is thus accompanied by seeking forgiveness from The God.

Afīḍū itself, however, does not mean seeking forgiveness.

Movement is one action; asking forgiveness is another.

Their juxtaposition also does not establish that movement automatically brings forgiveness irrespective of a person’s intention and conduct.

The verse places two distinct but related commands together.

Lexical finding: in Al-Baqarah 2:198 and Al-Baqarah 2:199, ifāḍah means departing from a specified place. This use connects with flow in the root’s central meaning, while describing an actual pilgrimage action that should not be reduced to a mental image or metaphorical movement.

7. Tufīḍūna fīhi: engaging and proceeding in activity and discourse

Yūnus 10:61

Verse text

وَمَا تَكُونُ فِي شَأْنٍ وَمَا تَتْلُو مِنْهُ مِنْ قُرْآنٍ وَلَا تَعْمَلُونَ مِنْ عَمَلٍ إِلَّا كُنَّا عَلَيْكُمْ شُهُودًا إِذْ تُفِيضُونَ فِيهِ وَمَا يَعْزُبُ عَنْ رَبِّكَ مِنْ مِثْقَالِ ذَرَّةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا فِي السَّمَاءِ وَلَا أَصْغَرَ مِنْ ذَٰلِكَ وَلَا أَكْبَرَ إِلَّا فِي كِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-mā takūnu fī shaʾnin wa-mā tatlū minhu min qurʾānin wa-lā taʿmalūna min ʿamalin illā kunnā ʿalaykum shuhūdan idh tufīḍūna fīhi wa-mā yaʿzubu ʿan rabbika min mithqāli dharratin fī al-arḍi wa-lā fī al-samāʾi wa-lā aṣghara min dhālika wa-lā akbara illā fī kitābin mubīnin

English translation

You are engaged in no affair and recite no portion of the Qur’an, and you perform no deed, without Our being witnesses over you when you enter into it and engage in it. Nothing of an atom’s weight on earth or in heaven is hidden from your Lord, nor anything smaller or greater, without its being in a clear record.

Examining the use

Tufīḍūna is a second-person plural imperfect verb in Form IV.

Here, the verb takes the preposition fī and the pronoun -hi:

«إِذْ تُفِيضُونَ فِيهِ» (idh tufīḍūna fīhi).

This combination differs in meaning from afaḍtum min ʿArafāt.

Al-Baqarah 2:198 specifies a physical departure point. Yūnus 10:61 concerns entering into an activity or subject and pursuing it.

Before this expression, the verse mentions an affair, reciting the Qur’an, and performing a deed.

Tufīḍūna fīhi therefore should not be restricted, without explanation, to “you speak at length.”

Ifāḍah in speech is a recognized Arabic use; here, however, the range of human work and activity is also present.

Consequently, “entering into and engaging in an activity” or “proceeding in what you do” suitably conveys the construction.

Does the verb concern the activity itself or discussion of it?

Grammatical analysis can examine more than one possible antecedent for the pronoun in fīhi.

It may refer to the deed or affair mentioned in the sentence; another reading relates it to a subject a person enters into and discusses.

Given the verse’s broad construction, restricting it to speech alone does not account for all the evidence.

The verse describes the Prophet’s affairs, Qur’anic recitation, and the addressees’ deeds, then emphasizes The God’s witnessing when they enter into these activities.

A broad, defensible meaning is therefore engaging in an activity or subject and pursuing it.

This fits the root’s field: flow extends from physical movement to the course of activity.

It should not, however, be used to derive a philosophical theory of the flow of all beings or the universe’s perpetual motion.

The verse concerns The God’s knowledge of, and witnessing over, human deeds.

The role of The God’s witnessing

Illā kunnā ʿalaykum shuhūdan shows that human activity remains within The God’s knowledge and witnessing.

Tufīḍūna itself, however, does not mean witnessing.

Shuhūd belongs to the separate root SH H D.

Nor does tufīḍūna necessarily identify a reprehensible action.

The verse includes recitation, work, and various activities; The God’s witnessing thus concerns both right and wrong conduct.

This matters when defining the root: ifāḍah here means entering into and proceeding within an activity or subject; the verb itself does not determine that activity’s value.

8. Ifāḍah in harmful speech and unfounded claims

Al-Nūr 24:14

Verse text

وَلَوْلَا فَضْلُ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ وَرَحْمَتُهُ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ لَمَسَّكُمْ فِي مَا أَفَضْتُمْ فِيهِ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

wa-lawlā faḍlu Allāhi ʿalaykum wa-raḥmatuhu fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati la-massakum fī mā afaḍtum fīhi ʿadhābun ʿaẓīmun

English translation

Were it not for The God’s bounty toward you and His mercy in this world and the Hereafter, a tremendous punishment would have touched you because of what you entered into and discussed.

Examining the use

Afaḍtum is a second-person plural perfect verb in Form IV.

Here, the verb occurs in the construction fī mā … fīhi.

The construction emphasizes the subject the addressees entered into and discussed.

The preceding verses of Al-Nūr concern accusation, the circulation of speech, and repeating statements about which the speakers lacked sufficient knowledge.

Afaḍtum fīhi here therefore concerns entering into and developing discourse with serious moral consequences.

That conclusion comes from the context; it does not mean that afāḍa always means lying.

Does ifāḍah in this verse mean fabricating rumors?

The conduct criticized here involves circulating harmful speech and unsupported claims.

Afāḍa should nevertheless not be restricted in a dictionary to “fabricating rumors.”

The same verb describes departure from ʿArafāt in Al-Baqarah 2:198 and pouring water in Al-Aʿrāf 7:50.

Its central meaning in Al-Nūr 24:14 is thus entering into a subject and carrying forward or developing speech about it.

The surrounding verses establish that the discourse is unfounded rumor.

The connection with Al-Nūr 24:15 and Al-Nūr 24:16

Al-Nūr 24:15 describes receiving and passing on speech with the tongue, and saying what one has no knowledge of.

Al-Nūr 24:16 likewise calls the addressees to a response that rejects repeating an outrageous assertion.

This continuity shows that the issue extends beyond merely speaking at length.

Someone may discuss a truth at length and act rightly, while a single short, unsupported statement can harm another person’s dignity and rights.

The basis of criticism here is consequently the falsehood and consequences of speech, rather than its length alone.

This distinction is particularly relevant to accusation and testimony.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:8

Verse text

أَمْ يَقُولُونَ افْتَرَاهُ قُلْ إِنِ افْتَرَيْتُهُ فَلَا تَمْلِكُونَ لِي مِنَ اللَّهِ شَيْئًا هُوَ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا تُفِيضُونَ فِيهِ كَفَىٰ بِهِ شَهِيدًا بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَكُمْ وَهُوَ الْغَفُورُ الرَّحِيمُ

am yaqūlūna iftarāhu qul in iftaraytuhu fa-lā tamlikūna lī mina Allāhi shayʾan huwa aʿlamu bi-mā tufīḍūna fīhi kafā bihi shahīdan baynī wa-baynakum wa-huwa al-ghafūru al-raḥīmu

English translation

Or do they say, “He has fabricated it”? Say, “If I have fabricated it, you have no power to protect me at all against The God. He knows best what you discuss and engage in. He suffices as a witness between me and you; and He is forgiving and merciful.”

Examining the use

Tufīḍūna fīhi here is the same verbal form found in Yūnus 10:61.

Its context, however, differs.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:8, opponents claim that the Prophet made up the Qur’an and falsely attributed it to The God.

The verse then describes The God’s knowledge of what they are discussing.

Ifāḍah here is therefore connected with their discussion and claims concerning revelation.

The verb itself, however, does not mean fabrication.

Iftarāhu belongs to F R Y and expresses the claim of making something up falsely.

Tufīḍūna concerns engaging in and developing discussion of a subject.

These two verbs occur in one context, but their meanings should not be merged.

Comparing the two uses of tufīḍūna fīhi

In Yūnus 10:61, the verb occurs within a broad construction concerning deeds, activities, and The God’s witnessing.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:8, it concerns the opponents’ speech and accusation.

The same verbal form thus occurs in two different situations.

This difference shows that afāḍa fī can describe entering into and pursuing a subject or activity; its particular referent may be a deed, speech, discussion, or a specified claim.

In Al-Nūr 24:14, the same family also occurs in the context of harmful speech.

All three verses should therefore not be translated simply as “speaking a great deal.”

One use may concern the activity itself; another, entering a discussion; another, developing unfounded discourse.

Lexical finding: Qur’anic afāḍa fī concerns entering into and pursuing a subject, activity, or discourse. Context determines its subject and value. The verb itself means neither fabrication nor necessarily pointless speech, nor invariably a commendable activity.

9. Morphology and distinctions from related roots

Distinguishing fāḍa and afāḍa

The Qur’an’s two Form I occurrences, tafīḍu, describe flowing tears.

In both, the flow is semantically attributed to the eyes, and tears are described as streaming from them.

The seven Form IV occurrences cover a broader range.

Afāḍa can mean causing water to flow, but it can also describe people themselves setting out.

Form IV in this root should therefore not be assumed always to be transitive with a direct object.

In Al-Baqarah 2:198, afaḍtum min ʿArafāt describes the subjects’ own movement.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:50, afīḍū ʿalaynā mina al-māʾ concerns providing water to those requesting it.

In Yūnus 10:61, tufīḍūna fīhi describes engagement with a subject or activity.

These differences show that the Form IV pattern alone does not determine the kind of flow involved.

The role of prepositions

Prepositions help establish the precise meaning of this root’s uses.

Min in afaḍtum min ʿArafāt marks the point of departure.

In tafīḍu mina al-damʿ, flow is connected with tears; the precise grammatical function of min has more than one analysis.

ʿAlā in afīḍū ʿalaynā specifies the direction of pouring or delivering water.

Fī in tufīḍūna fīhi identifies the subject or field that people enter into or engage with.

The various forms therefore cannot all be rendered by one identical sentence without attention to the preposition.

F Y Ḍ (fāḍa) compared with GH Y Ḍ (ghāḍa)

Study of GH Y Ḍ

GH Y Ḍ is particularly useful for comparison with F Y Ḍ.

Fāḍa concerns flowing, increasing, and overflowing.

Ghāḍa, by comparison, concerns decreasing, subsiding, and diminishing.

Hūd 11:44 reads:

«وَغِيضَ الْمَاءُ» (wa-ghīḍa al-māʾ).

That is, the water subsided.

The contrast is clear: in one field water becomes abundant and flows; in the other, its quantity or flow decreases.

Nevertheless, every use of these roots should not be reduced to numerical increase and decrease.

The Qur’an also uses F Y Ḍ for discourse and human movement, whereas GH Y Ḍ occurs in connection with receding water and the condition of wombs.

The lexical contrast concerning water is valid, but it does not make the entire ranges of the two families identical.

F Y Ḍ (fāḍa) compared with S Y L (sāla)

Study of S Y L

Arabic S Y L concerns flowing and the movement of liquid, especially water.

Fayḍ is also close to flowing water, but can give greater prominence to abundance and overflowing.

This does not mean that every sayl is destructive or that every fayḍ exceeds a vessel’s capacity.

The subject and context must be examined to determine the flow’s intensity and consequences.

Afāḍa al-nās also shows that the image of flowing water can explain collective human movement; this does not mean that people literally become a flood.

F Y Ḍ (fāḍa) compared with Ṣ B B (ṣabba)

Study of Ṣ B B

Ṣabba concerns pouring liquid or something else from one place to another.

Afāḍa likewise means pouring water in Al-Aʿrāf 7:50.

Fayḍ, however, retains a clear connection with flowing and streaming within its linguistic family.

“Pour” can therefore suit both in some contexts, but they are not absolute synonyms in every use.

For example, departure from ʿArafāt is expressed with afāḍa, rather than ṣabba in its direct sense.

F Y Ḍ (fāḍa) compared with S K B (sakaba)

Study of S K B

Arabic S K B concerns pouring liquid and causing it to flow.

Fayḍ likewise belongs to the field of flow and overflow.

Their imagery and usage are nevertheless not completely identical.

In fāḍat al-ʿaynu mina al-damʿ, the eyes stream with tears. Uses of sakaba may give greater prominence to the action of pouring liquid or causing it to flow.

A suitable translation should therefore consider the subject, the substance flowing, and the type of verb.

F Y Ḍ (fāḍa) compared with F Ḍ L (faḍl)

Study of F Ḍ L

These two roots appear together in Al-Baqarah 2:198.

The verse begins with faḍlan min rabbikum, then has afaḍtum min ʿArafāt.

Faḍl belongs to F Ḍ L and concerns increase, distinction, and giving.

Afaḍtum belongs to F Y Ḍ and here means departure from ʿArafāt.

Sharing the letters fāʾ and ḍād therefore does not make the two roots identical.

Likewise, faḍlu Allāh and afaḍtum appear together in Al-Nūr 24:14.

The first concerns The God’s bounty; the second, the addressees’ engagement in particular discourse.

These words should not be confused in translation or root indexes.

F Y Ḍ (fāḍa) compared with F W Ḍ (fawwaḍa)

Study of F W Ḍ

Arabic F W Ḍ concerns entrusting or handing over an affair.

It differs from F Y Ḍ.

In Ghāfir 40:44, «وَأُفَوِّضُ أَمْرِي إِلَى اللَّهِ» (wa-ufawwiḍu amrī ilā Allāh) belongs to F W Ḍ, rather than F Y Ḍ.

Entrusting one’s affair to The God is therefore distinct from pouring water, people setting out, or tears flowing.

The distinction matters in both spelling and meaning: the middle radical is yāʾ in F Y Ḍ and wāw in F W Ḍ, identifying two separate families.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Māʾidah 5:83, tafīḍu mina al-damʿ can be translated as “streaming with tears.”

The same equivalent suits Al-Tawbah 9:92.

In these verses, the flow of tears should be preserved, rather than reducing the verb’s meaning to the eyes merely becoming full.

In Al-Baqarah 2:198, fa-idhā afaḍtum min ʿArafāt is close to “then, when you set out from ʿArafāt.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:199, thumma afīḍū min ḥaythu afāḍa al-nās can be rendered “then set out from the same place from which the people have set out.”

In these uses, “set out” is more natural for readers than “overflow.”

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:50, afīḍū ʿalaynā mina al-māʾ is best rendered “pour upon us some water.”

Here the verb concerns water and should be distinguished from pilgrims’ movement.

In Yūnus 10:61, idh tufīḍūna fīhi is close to “when you enter into it and engage in it.” This preserves its range of work and activity without restricting it to speech.

In Al-Nūr 24:14, fī mā afaḍtum fīhi can be translated as “because of what you entered into and discussed.”

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:8, bi-mā tufīḍūna fīhi can be rendered “what you discuss and engage in.”

In both verses, the falsehood and harm of the discourse should be explained from context, rather than translating afāḍa itself directly as “fabricate” or “spread a fabricated rumor.”

Conversely, afāḍa fī al-ḥadīth in Arabic also conveys extensive discussion, so that aspect should remain part of lexical reflection.

A central translation principle is to understand flow, movement, and development in each verse according to its own subject. Flowing tears, pouring water, people setting out, and sustained discussion all belong to one root’s field, but they are not one identical action.

11. Final synthesis

Arabic F Y Ḍ concerns streaming, beginning to flow, and overflowing.

In physical uses, water and tears flow. In extended uses, people depart from a place, and individuals engage with a subject or develop a discussion of it.

This semantic extension is recognized in early Arabic, and examples occur in the Qur’an itself.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:83 and Al-Tawbah 9:92, tafīḍu describes eyes streaming with tears.

The tears have different causes in the two verses: recognition of truth and belief in one; grief over a lack of necessary resources in the other.

Tafīḍu itself therefore means neither belief nor grief. It means flowing, while other expressions establish the cause.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:50, afīḍū requests the pouring or flowing forth of water.

In Al-Baqarah 2:198 and Al-Baqarah 2:199, this family describes people departing from ʿArafāt and the connection with remembrance of The God and seeking forgiveness.

In these verses, ifāḍah is an actual action in pilgrimage, rather than merely a spiritual image or a symbol of inward human movement.

The root alone, however, does not yield the exact departure time, route details, or all associated historical and ritual rulings.

In Yūnus 10:61, tufīḍūna fīhi concerns entering into and pursuing an activity or subject.

In Al-Nūr 24:14 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:8, this family occurs in contexts of engagement in and development of discourse.

These uses show that ifāḍah in speech is neither good nor bad in itself. Speech may clarify truth or circulate accusations and unfounded claims. Its value is determined by its subject, evidence, and the speakers’ conduct.

Morphological construction must also be respected in each case. Qur’anic fāḍa describes flowing tears, whereas afāḍa can mean causing something to flow, the subject’s own movement, or entry into a subject.

Final definition: F Y Ḍ describes something streaming or entering into flow. This may involve abundance and overflowing and, in extended uses, collective movement or progress in discourse and activity.

Equivalents such as “flow,” “stream,” “pour forth,” “set out,” and “engage in discussion or pursue a subject” should therefore be chosen according to form and context.

To understand each use, distinguish three matters: what is flowing or moving, where it moves from and toward, and whether the verse expresses movement alone or also specifies its intensity, cause, and value through other expressions.

These distinctions prevent actual flowing tears from becoming one fixed psychological condition, departure from ʿArafāt from becoming mere metaphor, or engagement in discourse from always meaning falsehood and rumor.

The root’s core is flow and streaming; what flows, and the meaning of that flow in each situation, are established by sentence construction and Qur’anic context.
