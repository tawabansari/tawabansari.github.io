Root DH K W (dhakā)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

ذَكَّيْتُمْ
(dhakkaytum)
You slaughtered; the sole Qur’anic form; Form II

ذَكَّى
(dhakkā)
Slaughtered; kindled; base Form II verb

تَذْكِيَة
(tadhkiyah)
Form II verbal noun; slaughter; non-Qur’anic

ذَكَاة
(dhakāh)
Noun for slaughter; non-Qur’anic

ذَكَتْ تَذْكُو
(dhakat tadhkū)
Fire blazing; non-Qur’anic

ذَكِيّ
(dhakiyy)
Quick-witted; also describing strong fragrance; non-Qur’anic

مُذَكٍّ / مَذَاكِي

Blazing and intensification; sharp understanding; full development of strength; tadhkiyah and animal slaughter

The Arabic root DH K W forms a family used in several fields: fire blazing and heat intensifying, sharp and quick understanding, an animal reaching full physical strength, the intensity and diffusion of fragrance, and slaughtering an animal in a recognized manner.

At first sight, kindling fire, human intelligence, a horse’s maturity, and animal slaughter concern different subjects. Early lexical explanations connect them through intensity, sharpness, penetration, and completeness. None of these ideas should become an unexamined, unchanging meaning for every derivative.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as ذَكَّيْتُمْ. In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, it concerns animals exposed to strangling, blows, falling, goring, or a predator’s attack, in connection with an exception for slaughter.

Here the meaning is slaughtering an animal, but the verse’s context adds an important condition: tadhkiyah must take place while slaughtering the animal remains possible. Cutting parts of an animal already dead does not turn its earlier death into the slaughter of a living animal.

A precise study distinguishes the lexical meaning of tadhkiyah, the animal’s condition, the act of slaughter, and the ruling on food. This root must also be distinguished from Z K W, the family of zakāh and purification of the self.

1. Core meaning and forms

Intensity, sharpness, and completeness

One lexical analysis proposes a principle for DH K W related to حِدَّة and نَفَاذ: sharpness, intensity, and penetrating effectiveness.

It explains fire, sunlight, sharp understanding, an animal’s full maturity, and animal slaughter within this field.

Another analysis gives greater emphasis to completeness: dhakāʾ in understanding concerns fully developed understanding; in age, full strength; and in fire, complete ignition.

These explanations need not exclude each other. Growth or reinforcement may bring something to intensity and fullness, as fire blazes more strongly or a horse’s physical strength matures.

This shared pattern does not establish a definite history in which all these words first concerned fire and then passed directly into understanding, maturity, and slaughter.

The linguistic evidence establishes several recognized branches within one lexical family.

Important forms

ذَكَتِ النَّارُ تَذْكُو is used for fire blazing.

ذَكَّى النَّارَ means that someone kindled a fire or strengthened its flame.

ذَكِيَ الرَّجُلُ concerns someone becoming sharp and intelligent; ذَكِيّ describes quick, penetrating understanding.

ذَكَّى الْفَرَسُ is used for a horse reaching a recognized stage of maturity and strength.

ذَكَّى الذَّبِيحَةَ means that someone slaughtered the animal.

تَذْكِيَة is the verbal noun of Form II. In the animal-related use it denotes slaughtering; ذَكَاة is also recorded for animal slaughter.

The family also includes nouns and descriptive forms such as ذَكَاء, ذُكْوَة, ذَكِيّ, مُذَكٍّ, and مَذَاكِي.

Of these forms, only ذَكَّيْتُمْ occurs in the Qur’an: a Form II perfect verb in the second person masculine plural.

It belongs to ذَكَّى يُذَكِّي. In ذَكَّيْتُمْ, the attached subject pronoun تُمْ refers to the plural addressees: “you slaughtered.”

In the classification using a final wāw, this family is indexed under DH K W, although a yāʾ appears in certain inflections. The imperfect تَذْكُو also displays the wāw.

Accordingly, this study records the root of ذَكَّيْتُمْ as DH K W. The verb’s surface yāʾ alone does not warrant changing that classification, and its dhāl must not be confused with the zāy of Z K W.

2. Uses in early Arabic

Dhakā: fire blazing

Arabic uses the following expressions:

ذَكَتِ النَّارُ: the fire blazed up.

أَذْكَيْتُ النَّارَ: I kindled the fire or strengthened its flame.

ذَكَّيْتُ النَّارَ: I increased the fire’s blaze.

ذُكْوَة is also recorded for a glowing ember or something, such as fuel, used to strengthen a fire.

These uses emphasize a flame intensifying and becoming more vigorous. A fire may already be burning and blaze more strongly when fuel is added.

أَذْكَى الْحَرْبَ, “he kindled war,” belongs to the same field. War is pictured as fire intensified through fuel or incitement.

ذَكَّى does not mean setting fire to something in every context. Its sense depends on its object: with fire, kindling is meant; with an animal for slaughter, another established use applies.

Dhukāʾ: a name for the sun

ذُكَاء, with a ḍammah on the dhāl, is one Arabic name for the sun.

Lexical explanations associate it with the sun’s intense light and heat. ابن ذُكَاء, “son of Dhukāʾ,” is also used in literary language for morning, whose light is connected with sunrise.

Thaʿlabah ibn Ṣuʿayr al-Māzinī, a poet assigned to the pre-Islamic period, says of a male and a female ostrich:

فَتَذَكَّرَا ثَقَلًا رَثِيدًا بَعْدَمَا
أَلْقَتْ ذُكَاءُ يَمِينَهَا فِي كَافِرِ

fa-tadhakkarā thaqalan rathīdan baʿdamā / alqat dhukāʾu yamīnahā fī kāfir

Translation: Then the two remembered their eggs laid together, after the sun had cast its right hand into darkness.

Here ذُكَاء names the sun, and the image concerns sunset and the arrival of darkness.

In this image, ثَقَلًا رَثِيدًا refers to ostrich eggs described as goods laid together, rather than actual travel baggage. كَافِر refers to the covering night, rather than an unbelieving person.

The witness illustrates ذُكاء as a name for the sun in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Literary attribution should be distinguished from independently establishing the exact date of composition.

The name does not establish that the sun is involved in every use of the root, or that slaughtering an animal lexically means burning it.

Dhakiyy: sharp understanding

ذَكِيّ describes a person of sharp understanding.

قَلْبٌ ذَكِيّ means a heart with quick, penetrating understanding.

الذَّكَاء also concerns sharpness and speed of perception.

In this branch, a dhakiyy person grasps a subject readily and quickly.

This is not simply possessing a great deal of information. Someone may know many facts yet struggle to understand connections or grasp a problem quickly.

Intelligence and quick understanding do not themselves establish moral integrity. Mental ability may be used for good or wrongdoing.

In this branch, dhakiyy describes the ability and quality of understanding; it does not by itself guarantee faith, God-consciousness, or moral purity.

Dhakiyy: intense fragrance

مِسْكٌ ذَكِيّ and مِسْكٌ ذَاكٍ describe musk with a strong, diffusing fragrance.

Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm, a poet of the pre-Islamic tradition, describes the fragrance of a woman’s clothing:

كَأَنَّ الْقَرَنْفُلَ وَالزَّنْجَبِيلَ
وَذَاكِي الْعَبِيرِ بِجِلْبَابِهَا

ka-anna al-qaranfula wa-al-zanjabīla / wa-dhākī al-ʿabīri bi-jilbābihā

Translation: It is as though cloves, ginger, and strongly fragrant perfume are mingled in her outer garment.

ذَاكِي الْعَبِيرِ refers here to perfume with a strong, noticeable fragrance.

The word concerns the intensity or penetration of a scent, rather than slaughter, understanding, or moral purity.

This witness illustrates a branch of the root in early Arab daily life. The poet uses aromatic plants and perfume to describe clothing and expresses the scent’s intensity with dhākī. Calling the poet pre-Islamic does not by itself date this particular verse precisely in relation to the beginning of Qur’anic revelation.

Dhakāʾ and an animal’s full strength

المُذَكِّي is used for a horse that has reached a recognized stage of physical maturity and strength.

Early explanations apply it to a horse one year or more beyond the stage called قُرُوح, having reached maturity.

Its plural is مَذَاكِي.

In a verse about a male and a female wild ass, the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes maturity of age and strength:

يُفَضِّلُهُ إِذَا اجْتَهَدَا عَلَيْهِ
تَمَامُ السِّنِّ مِنْهُ وَالذَّكَاءُ

yufaḍḍiluhu idhā ijtahadā ʿalayhi / tamāmu al-sinni minhu wa-al-dhakāʾ

Translation: When the two strive over difficult ground, his full age and mature strength give the male an advantage over the female.

In this verse, الذَّكَاء accompanies تَمَامُ السِّنِّ, “fullness of age.”

On the reading concerning mature age, the point is a stage of growth and physical strength that equips the animal for movement and exertion, rather than intelligence as human reasoning. Sharp perception has also been proposed for dhakāʾ in explanations of this verse; the age-related interpretation should therefore not be presented as the only possibility.

This use shows that dhakāʾ also has a connection with completeness and maturity in Arabic.

It should not be transferred to ذَكَّيْتُمْ in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 to suggest raising an animal until adulthood. The Qur’anic passage concerns slaughter, rather than setting an appropriate age for the animal.

3. Tadhkiyah: slaughtering an animal

ذَكَّى الشَّاةَ means slaughtering a sheep.

التَّذْكِيَة and الذَّكَاة are recorded for animal slaughter. In later jurisprudential usage, tadhkiyah became a broader category under which slaughter, naḥr, and certain forms of hunting were considered as ways of taking an animal’s life for food.

The lexical use and the legal technical term should be distinguished.

In its animal-related lexical use, tadhkiyah concerns slaughtering and completing this recognized act. The root’s structure alone does not determine all technical conditions, tools, sequences, or detailed rulings.

Why is slaughter called tadhkiyah?

One lexical explanation of Al-Māʾidah 5:3 connects tadhkiyah with completion.

In that explanation, dhakāʾ in age concerns reaching full strength; in understanding, sharp and complete comprehension; and in fire, full ignition.

Accordingly, ذَكَّيْتُ الذَّبِيحَةَ is explained as completing slaughter.

This lexical analysis is significant, but must be used accurately.

“Completing slaughter” does not mean that every kind of killing or every action ending in death is called tadhkiyah.

If an animal dies through strangulation, its death alone is not tadhkiyah. If it falls from a height and dies, death does not establish that slaughter took place.

There is therefore a distinction between the animal’s death and the act of tadhkiyah.

Completeness should not be expanded into the idea that tadhkiyah means completing an animal’s natural lifespan or perfecting its existence. Those formulations go beyond the verb’s specific slaughter-related use.

In this branch, the clear, established equivalent of “performing tadhkiyah” is “slaughtering.”

4. “Except what you slaughter”: the Qur’anic occurrence

The root’s only occurrence is in Al-Māʾidah 5:3.

Part of the verse reads:

وَالْمُنْخَنِقَةُ وَالْمَوْقُوذَةُ وَالْمُتَرَدِّيَةُ وَالنَّطِيحَةُ وَمَا أَكَلَ السَّبُعُ إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ

wa-al-munkhaniqatu wa-al-mawqūdhatu wa-al-mutaraddiyatu wa-al-naṭīḥatu wa-mā akala al-sabuʿu illā mā dhakkaytum

Translation: And the strangled, the beaten to death, the fallen from a height, the gored, and what a predator has eaten, except what you slaughter.

The verse begins by listing carrion, blood, pork, and what has been dedicated to someone other than The God. It then names further conditions in which an animal may die from strangling, blows, falling, goring, or a predator’s attack.

These are followed by إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ.

Death through an accident and slaughter

The exception requires distinguishing several situations.

An animal may be injured by a fall yet remain alive. Another animal may gore it, or a predator may attack it, yet a person may reach it before it dies.

In such circumstances, slaughter remains possible.

If it has already died through the accident, cutting its neck or other parts cannot change that past event into the slaughter of a living animal.

The exception مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ therefore concerns reaching the animal while it is still alive and tadhkiyah remains possible.

Early lexical and exegetical explanations likewise connect إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ with an animal reached before its life has ended and then slaughtered.

The phrase itself does not specify exact signs of life or medical details for determining whether the animal remains alive.

Whether a particular animal is alive or already dead is a factual question open to investigation. Empirical signs or later legal criteria should not be presented without explanation as the Qur’an’s exact wording.

The scope of the exception

Grammatically, إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ is an exception following several different categories.

Early explanations differ on its scope. Some connect it with all preceding cases capable of slaughter; others discuss whether particular categories fall within it.

One shared point remains clear: an exception cannot turn an already dead animal into one subsequently slaughtered while alive.

Slaughter alone does not remove every other qualification concerning the kind of animal, mentioning The God’s name, hunting circumstances, or ritual acts.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 continues with وَمَا ذُبِحَ عَلَى النُّصُبِ: what has been slaughtered on ritual stones.

In the verse itself, an act qualifying as slaughter and permission to eat the resulting food are not identical without qualification. An act may be called slaughter by its manner, yet fall under prohibition because of its object or ritual dedication.

This prevents treating tadhkiyah as any act by which a person kills an animal while disregarding every other ruling.

Does tadhkiyah mean purification?

Some exegetical explanations connect animal slaughter with purification or making the meat permissible to eat.

The verb’s meaning should be distinguished from its legal or hygienic consequences.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, ذَكَّيْتُمْ refers to slaughter. The conditions under which that act permits consumption are understood from the verse as a whole and other food-related rulings.

It should therefore not be translated only as “you purified,” which conceals the specific act of slaughter.

Nor does every slaughtered animal become hygienically safe simply by being slaughtered. Contamination, spoilage, and bodily harm concern the actual food and its storage and consumption conditions.

Conversely, hygienic knowledge should not be presented without evidence as part of the lexical meaning of dhakāh or an explicit Qur’anic instruction.

Other verses on food and hunting

Al-Māʾidah 5:4 discusses hunting with trained animals and mentioning The God’s name over what they catch for people.

Al-Ḥajj 22:36 discusses sacrificial livestock, mentioning The God’s name over them as they stand in rows, their flanks then falling to the ground, and eating from them and feeding others.

These verses show that, in obtaining meat and food, the Qur’an considers the animal’s condition, human action, and the purpose of the act together.

A specific technical method not explicitly stated in the verse should not be declared the sole method commanded by The God merely on the basis of tadhkiyah.

This caution does not mean that the Qur’an pays no attention to how animals are killed, hunted, or prepared for consumption.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 itself shows that the distinctions between carrion, an injured animal, and one slaughtered in time matter to the ruling on food.

5. Distinctions from related roots

DH K W and Z K W: tadhkiyah and tazkiyah

Study of Z K W

The two roots may be confused because their pronunciation is close in Persian.

DH K W, with dhāl, includes blazing, sharp understanding, full strength, and animal slaughter.

Z K W, with zāy, concerns purity, growth, and flourishing. Its Qur’anic forms include زَكَاة, زَكَّى, and زَكِيّ.

Al-Shams 91:9 provides an example:

قَدْ أَفْلَحَ مَنْ زَكَّاهَا

qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā

Translation: Successful indeed is the one who purifies it and enables it to flourish.

زَكَّاهَا belongs here to Z K W, rather than DH K W.

Similarly, غُلَامًا زَكِيًّا in Maryam 19:19 uses zāy and belongs to the family of purity and growth.

ذَكَّيْتُمْ in Al-Māʾidah 5:3, however, uses dhāl and concerns animal slaughter.

The similar pronunciation must not cause animal dhakāh to be identified with financial zakāh or purification of the self.

Likewise, ذَكِيّ, “quick-witted,” and زَكِيّ, “pure” or “flourishing,” belong to different roots despite their similar appearance.

DH K W and DH B Ḥ: tadhkiyah and dhabḥ

Study of DH B Ḥ

DH B Ḥ concerns cutting the throat or front of the neck of a living being.

In its animal-related use, ذَكَّى concerns carrying out and completing slaughter.

The two verbs are close in many Arabic uses, and early lexical explanations also explain tadhkiyah through dhabḥ.

Their families nevertheless differ in range.

Dhabḥ names the specific act of cutting the throat. In the history of its usage, tadhkiyah also appears in terminology concerning naḥr and certain other ways of preparing an animal for consumption.

Dhabḥ can also refer in the Qur’an to killing humans by cutting their throats, as in the passages concerning Pharaoh’s people and the Children of Israel.

ذَكَّيْتُمْ in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 occurs in the context of animals and food.

The uses of dhabḥ should not all be restricted without reason to food rulings, nor should every form of killing be called tadhkiyah.

DH K W and N Ḥ R

Naḥr concerns killing an animal by an incision or thrust in the lower neck where it joins the chest.

In established Arabic usage, it particularly concerns camels.

وَانْحَرْ in Al-Kawthar 108:2 belongs to this root.

Dhabḥ and naḥr can both concern recognized ways of taking an animal’s life, but differ in location and manner of the act.

Later legal usage can place them under the broader category of tadhkiyah. Translating Al-Māʾidah 5:3 does not require substituting all those jurisprudential classifications for ذَكَّيْتُمْ.

DH K W and Q T L: slaughter and killing

Study of Q T L

Qatl concerns killing and ending life, which may occur by different means.

In its animal-related use, tadhkiyah names a specific act in the field of slaughter and preparation for consumption.

An animal may die from a fall, a blow, or a predator’s attack. Death has occurred, but that alone does not establish tadhkiyah.

This distinction is fundamental in Al-Māʾidah 5:3.

ذَكَّيْتُمْ should therefore not be translated merely as “you killed.” “You slaughtered” conveys the established meaning more accurately.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence, Al-Māʾidah 5:3, the phrase is:

إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ

illā mā dhakkaytum

Suitable translation: “Except what you slaughter.”

This preserves the established sense and is natural in Persian.

The Persian equivalent of “except what you perform tadhkiyah on” is close to the Arabic structure, but may be technical and obscure for general readers and require explanation.

“Except what you purify” is unsuitable because it turns slaughter into a general concept.

“Except what you kill” is too broad and obscures the distinction from death by strangling, blows, or falling.

Forqan’s current Persian wording, meaning “except what you slaughter,” agrees with the lexical findings and requires no change.

The contextual explanation should clarify that the exception concerns an animal reached before death while slaughter remains possible.

Technical details and supplementary conditions should not be added to the word’s meaning without clear evidence.

7. Conclusion

DH K W has several Arabic branches: blazing fire, sharp understanding, intense fragrance, an animal reaching full strength, and animal slaughter.

Early lexical explanations connect them through intensity, penetration, and completeness. These analyses help explain the family’s semantic field without making any one idea a compulsory definition of every derivative.

Early poetic witnesses use dhākī for penetrating fragrance and dhakāʾ for maturity of age and strength; sharp perception is also proposed in explanations of the second witness. The sun’s name and descriptions of blazing fire are recorded within this family as well.

Only the animal-slaughter branch occurs in the Qur’an.

ذَكَّيْتُمْ in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 denotes slaughtering an animal, in an exception following several conditions involving injury or death.

It illustrates the difference between an animal dying and being slaughtered. Tadhkiyah is an act performed before its life ends, rather than a term for every form of death or for cutting performed afterward.

Final definition: DH K W is connected with intensity, sharpness, and reaching completeness or full development in several semantic branches. In its particular Qur’anic use, ذَكَّى means slaughtering an animal, and ذَكَّيْتُمْ means “you slaughtered.”

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, that recognized act is the lexical meaning. Its timing, the animal’s condition, and the legal consequence are understood from the verse as a whole and other passages concerning food and hunting.

This root is distinct from Z K W, the family of zakāh, purity, and growth. Similar pronunciation should not lead to conflating their meanings or rulings.
