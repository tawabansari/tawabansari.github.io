The root R Ḥ B (raḥb)

Breadth, spaciousness, and making room for another

Arabic R Ḥ B denotes breadth, spaciousness, and being wide. This is readily seen in descriptions of places, land, houses, and open spaces, extending to expressions of friendliness, accepting others, and agreeable reception.

Raḥb describes a spacious place, one that is not narrow or confined. Applied to human relationships, the concept acquires a sense of welcoming, as though someone has entered a spacious, receptive setting.

The Qur’an contains four occurrences in two forms, four verses, and two surahs: raḥubat in Al-Tawbah 9:25 and Al-Tawbah 9:118, and marḥaban in Ṣād 38:59 and Ṣād 38:60.

The first two concern the earth’s spaciousness, contrasted in both with experienced constriction. The other two occur in exchanges among the people of the Fire, where a familiar welcome is negated, expressing rejection and refusal of reception.

Qur’anic forms: raḥubat and marḥaban.

Other recorded Arabic forms include raḥb, ruḥb, raḥābah, raḥīb, raḥbah, riḥāb, arḥaba, raḥḥaba, and tarḥīb. They help establish the family’s semantic range, but these exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usages

Spaciousness in places and objects

Raḥuba al-makānu means that a place is spacious. Arḍun raḥbah describes broad land and baladun raḥb a spacious territory.

The clear semantic center is al-saʿah: breadth and spaciousness.

This sense is also recorded across Arabic dictionaries. Raḥubat al-dāru means that a house became spacious or was spacious. Raḥīb describes something broad and wide.

The space may be limited, such as a courtyard, or extensive, such as a territory. Absolute size or a particular measurement is therefore not a constant component of the root: it describes breadth in contrast to narrowness.

Raḥbah denotes an open, spacious area, including the grounds of a house or mosque.

Riḥāb is another established plural in this family. In spatial expressions it denotes broad areas and spaces.

Other recorded usages include qidrun ruḥāb, a broad cooking pot, and raḥīb al-jawf for someone or something with a spacious belly or interior.

Spaciousness is thus not confined to land. A vessel, body, road, or place may be described with this family’s words.

This does not make R Ḥ B always mean increasing in size or expanding. Raḥuba may simply express a state of spaciousness without any change in dimensions.

Spaciousness in dealing with others

Raḥb al-ṣadr and raḥīb al-ṣadr describe a patient, forbearing person.

Bodily breadth of the chest is extended here to capacity in patience, conduct, and acceptance.

Someone patient enough to listen and tolerate others can be described as raḥb al-ṣadr. Conversely, a constricted chest may express pressure, distress, or unease.

The semantic connection with spatial breadth is understandable, but linguistic imagery should be distinguished from an ethical definition.

Raḥb alone does not mean patience, kindness, or generosity. In expressions such as raḥb al-ṣadr, these qualities follow from the combination and its established usage.

Every occurrence of raḥb therefore cannot be turned into a moral virtue, nor every broad space into a sign of kindness.

Marḥaban: welcoming through an image of spaciousness

One familiar expression from this root is marḥaban.

Saying marḥaban welcomes another person, conveying that there is ample, receptive room for them.

Lexical explanations paraphrase it as atayta saʿatan: “you have reached spaciousness,” or “you have come to a spacious place.”

Marḥaban consequently often accompanies ahlan and sahlan.

In this combination, ahlan concerns familiarity and being among people who receive someone; sahlan concerns ease and absence of difficulty; marḥaban concerns spaciousness and room to receive them.

This does not mean the speaker necessarily discusses a house’s actual dimensions. The expression has become an established greeting.

Morphologically, marḥab follows the mafʿal pattern. Lexical and grammatical analyses relate it to a spacious place, offering different syntactic reconstructions for accusative marḥaban in the greeting.

Their shared point is its connection with spaciousness and reception, becoming a welcome in ordinary usage.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī reads:

وَبِالسَّهْبِ مَيْمُونُ الْخَلِيقَةِ قَوْلُهُ
لِمُلْتَمِسِ الْمَعْرُوفِ أَهْلٌ وَمَرْحَبُ

wa-bi-al-sahbi maymūnu al-khalīqati qawluhu / li-multamisi al-maʿrūfi ahlun wa-marḥabu

“At al-Sahb lies a man of good disposition, whose words to one seeking kindness were welcome and acceptance.”

Literary explanations treat this as an elegy for a man buried at al-Sahb. Marḥab accompanies ahl, expressing his receptive treatment of someone seeking kindness and help.

The witness matters because it places marḥab as welcoming and receiving others in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Some transmissions have al-naqībah instead of al-khalīqah. This does not change the meaning of marḥab under discussion.

The verse concerns friendliness and reception, rather than a house’s physical spaciousness.

Marḥab’s social usage therefore has a history in Arabic and should not be treated as arising only in later periods.

2. Bi-mā raḥubat: a spacious earth experienced as narrow

Both Qur’anic occurrences of raḥubat are in Al-Tawbah.

The following abbreviates their shared construction in Al-Tawbah 9:25 and Al-Tawbah 9:118; the ellipsis marks omitted wording:

ضَاقَتْ ... الْأَرْضُ بِمَا رَحُبَتْ

ḍāqat … al-arḍu bi-mā raḥubat

“The earth, despite all its spaciousness, became narrow.”

This expression is particularly important linguistically and semantically.

Raḥubat and the construction

Raḥubat is a third person feminine singular Form I perfect. Its grammatical subject is the implicit pronoun hiya, referring to al-arḍ, the earth.

The verb means “was spacious” or “was broad” here.

By contrast, ḍāqat belongs to Ḍ Y Q and means becoming narrow.

Two contrasting meanings thus appear together: the earth’s breadth and its becoming constricted for people.

Mā in bi-mā raḥubat can be understood as converting the clause into a verbal-noun expression, with bāʾ expressing accompaniment. The resulting sense is “despite its breadth” or “with all its spaciousness.”

The construction does not make the earth’s spaciousness the cause of its constriction.

Nor does the verse say that the earth’s actual area decreased or its natural structure changed.

The earth remains spacious, but people in difficult circumstances experience that broad earth as constricted and without refuge.

The distinction between the place’s actual condition and the human experience follows from ḍāqat and raḥubat appearing together in this construction.

First occurrence: the day of Ḥunayn

Al-Tawbah 9:25 states:

لَقَدْ نَصَرَكُمُ اللَّهُ فِي مَوَاطِنَ كَثِيرَةٍ وَيَوْمَ حُنَيْنٍ إِذْ أَعْجَبَتْكُمْ كَثْرَتُكُمْ فَلَمْ تُغْنِ عَنْكُمْ شَيْئًا وَضَاقَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَرْضُ بِمَا رَحُبَتْ ثُمَّ وَلَّيْتُمْ مُدْبِرِينَ

laqad naṣarakumu allāhu fī mawāṭina kathīratin wa-yawma ḥunaynin idh aʿjabatkum kathratukum fa-lam tughni ʿankum shayʾan wa-ḍāqat ʿalaykumu al-arḍu bi-mā raḥubat thumma wallaytum mudbirīn

“The God certainly helped you in many situations, and on the day of Ḥunayn, when your great number impressed you, yet availed you nothing. The earth, despite all its spaciousness, became narrow for you; then you turned your backs in retreat.”

Raḥubat describes the earth’s breadth, while the situation concerns battle and retreat.

The Qur’an first mentions the people’s great number, which impressed them but did not provide the expected benefit.

It then describes the earth’s constriction.

This sequence shows spatial breadth alone providing no guarantee of security, calm, or a way of escape.

Land may actually be broad, yet someone experiencing fear, defeat, or turmoil finds it constricted.

Fear, defeat, and flight nevertheless do not come from raḥubat itself. They follow from other parts of the verse, particularly fa-lam tughni ʿankum shayʾan and thumma wallaytum mudbirīn.

Raḥubat still means the earth’s spaciousness here.

Nor does the verse make a large number intrinsically blameworthy. Their numbers inspired an admiration and reliance that failed to deliver the expected result at a difficult moment.

The contextual lesson concerns the limits of relying on numbers and apparent power. It should not become R Ḥ B’s lexical definition.

3. A broad earth and constricted selves: Al-Tawbah 9:118

The second raḥubat appears in a different situation in Al-Tawbah 9:118:

وَعَلَى الثَّلَاثَةِ الَّذِينَ خُلِّفُوا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا ضَاقَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الْأَرْضُ بِمَا رَحُبَتْ وَضَاقَتْ عَلَيْهِمْ أَنْفُسُهُمْ وَظَنُّوا أَنْ لَا مَلْجَأَ مِنَ اللَّهِ إِلَّا إِلَيْهِ ثُمَّ تَابَ عَلَيْهِمْ لِيَتُوبُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ التَّوَّابُ الرَّحِيمُ

wa-ʿalā al-thalāthati alladhīna khullifū ḥattā idhā ḍāqat ʿalayhimu al-arḍu bi-mā raḥubat wa-ḍāqat ʿalayhim anfusuhum wa-ẓannū an lā maljaʾa mina allāhi illā ilayhi thumma tāba ʿalayhim li-yatūbū inna allāha huwa al-tawwābu al-raḥīm

“And towards the three who were left behind, until the earth, despite all its spaciousness, became narrow for them, and their own selves became constricted for them. They realized there was no refuge from The God except towards Him. Then He turned towards them so that they might repent. The God is the One who repeatedly accepts repentance, the merciful.”

The verse concerns three people in a difficult situation, eventually describing their return and the acceptance of their repentance.

Here too, the earth is actually broad, while they experience their situation as constriction with no escape.

How this differs from the day of Ḥunayn

Both verses contain ḍāqat … al-arḍu bi-mā raḥubat, but their situations differ.

Al-Tawbah 9:25 concerns difficulty on the battlefield and retreat.

Al-Tawbah 9:118 concerns the pressure of the three people’s situation, their inward constriction, and turning towards The God.

In the first verse, the earth’s constriction is followed by turning away and retreating.

In Al-Tawbah 9:118, the same expression is followed by another clause:

وَضَاقَتْ عَلَيْهِمْ أَنْفُسُهُمْ

wa-ḍāqat ʿalayhim anfusuhum

“And their own selves became constricted for them.”

This emphasizes their inward pressure and unease.

The Qur’an thus uses a shared image for two different situations.

The resemblance does not establish identical causes, intensity, or outcomes for the two events.

Does the earth narrow, or do people find themselves constrained?

The earth is the sentence’s subject, while ʿalayhim shows constriction being described in relation to the people.

This matters to understanding the expression.

Ḍāqat al-arḍu alone might be understood as a place’s space decreasing. But ḍāqat ʿalayhimu al-arḍu bi-mā raḥubat emphasizes the apparent contradiction between the earth’s actual breadth and the constriction people experience.

This does not establish that every Qur’anic use of breadth and narrowness is purely psychological or figurative.

Raḥubat still denotes the earth’s actual spaciousness. Context shows the human situation affecting the experience of that same earth.

From constriction to return

After describing constriction of earth and selves, Al-Tawbah 9:118 states:

وَظَنُّوا أَنْ لَا مَلْجَأَ مِنَ اللَّهِ إِلَّا إِلَيْهِ

wa-ẓannū an lā maljaʾa mina allāhi illā ilayhi

“And they realized there was no refuge from The God except towards Him.”

The verse then mentions The God turning towards them and their repentance.

The constriction is therefore not the end of the account. The difficult situation is followed by the possibility of return and accepted repentance.

Return, repentance, and seeking refuge come from other words in the verse. None is part of raḥubat’s lexical meaning.

The root should therefore not be defined as basically meaning repentance, release, forgiveness, or divine relief.

Its semantic center is spaciousness, here contrasted with experienced constriction.

4. Lā marḥaban: denying welcome in the scene of the Fire

The root’s other two occurrences are in Ṣād.

They concern an exchange between groups facing one another in punishment, rather than the earth’s spaciousness.

Lā marḥaban bihim

Ṣād 38:59 states:

هَٰذَا فَوْجٌ مُقْتَحِمٌ مَعَكُمْ لَا مَرْحَبًا بِهِمْ إِنَّهُمْ صَالُو النَّارِ

hādhā fawjun muqtaḥimun maʿakum lā marḥaban bihim innahum ṣālū al-nār

“This is a group rushing in with you. No welcome to them, for they burn in the Fire.”

Marḥaban occurs in the negative construction lā marḥaban bihim.

Marḥaban is an Arabic expression of welcome and reception. Negation with lā turns it into rejection, an unwelcome greeting, or a hostile wish against another’s arrival.

This connects with the underlying image of spaciousness: a welcomed person is as though given ample room, while lā marḥaban denies that reception.

The expression should not simply be understood as reporting that the place is small or lacks physical space.

It is a social expression in speech. One group resents another’s entry and speaks of them with rejection.

Bal antum lā marḥaban bikum

The next verse, Ṣād 38:60, states:

قَالُوا بَلْ أَنْتُمْ لَا مَرْحَبًا بِكُمْ أَنْتُمْ قَدَّمْتُمُوهُ لَنَا فَبِئْسَ الْقَرَارُ

qālū bal antum lā marḥaban bikum antum qaddamtumūhu lanā fa-biʾsa al-qarār

“They said: Rather, you yourselves—no welcome to you. You brought this upon us beforehand. What an evil resting place.”

The second group directs the same expression back at the first.

Instead of accepting reproach, they blame the other side for their predicament.

This response connects with the continuation of Ṣād 38:60:

أَنْتُمْ قَدَّمْتُمُوهُ لَنَا

antum qaddamtumūhu lanā

“You brought this upon us beforehand.”

The harshness thus goes beyond a simple lack of welcome. Each side reproaches the other and attributes responsibility for its outcome to the other group.

Reproach, responsibility, and misleading others should nevertheless not all be inserted into marḥaban itself. They come from other clauses and the dialogue’s setting.

A negated welcome without a negative root meaning

Marḥaban is fundamentally a positive expression of reception.

In the Qur’an, this form occurs only in the negative construction lā marḥaban.

That does not make marḥab itself mean rejection, enmity, or punishment.

The negative force comes from lā and the speakers’ hostile situation.

As “welcome” can become rejecting in “no welcome to you,” negation gives marḥaban an opposite force.

The word’s meaning should therefore be distinguished from that of the negative expression.

Does lā marḥaban mean the Fire is spatially cramped?

That does not follow from the expression alone.

Although marḥab connects with spaciousness, its usage here is an established expression in speech.

It should not produce a theory of the Fire’s physical dimensions or each group’s allocated space.

Nor does its repetition establish that neither side is responsible for its own deeds, with all guilt transferred to the other.

The dialogue continues in Ṣād 38:61, Ṣād 38:62, and Ṣād 38:63, and is described as the people of the Fire disputing in Ṣād 38:64.

At the passage’s end, Ṣād 38:64 states:

إِنَّ ذَٰلِكَ لَحَقٌّ تَخَاصُمُ أَهْلِ النَّارِ

inna dhālika la-ḥaqqun takhāṣumu ahli al-nār

“This is certainly true: the disputing of the people of the Fire.”

Repeating lā marḥaban forms part of that dispute, rather than a new definition of R Ḥ B.

5. Distinguishing R Ḥ B from nearby roots

R Ḥ B (raḥb) and W S ʿ (wusʿ)

The roots overlap in breadth and capacity.

Raḥb in its clear usages emphasizes spaciousness in a place or condition.

Wasiʿa may concern a place’s breadth, but also capacity and encompassing something.

For example, part of Al-Aʿrāf 7:156 reads:

وَرَحْمَتِي وَسِعَتْ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ

wa-raḥmatī wasiʿat kulla shayʾ

“And My mercy encompasses everything.”

Wasiʿat concerns the extent of mercy, rather than a material space’s dimensions.

By contrast, raḥubat in Al-Tawbah 9:25 and Al-Tawbah 9:118 directly describes the earth’s breadth.

These are tendencies of usage, rather than absolute boundaries. Both can describe breadth, and R Ḥ B itself extends beyond physical space in expressions such as raḥb al-ṣadr.

R Ḥ B should therefore not be declared exclusively material and W S ʿ exclusively nonmaterial.

Study of W S ʿ

R Ḥ B (raḥb) and F S Ḥ (fasḥ)

Fasḥ also concerns spaciousness and making room.

Part of Al-Mujādilah 58:11 reads:

إِذَا قِيلَ لَكُمْ تَفَسَّحُوا فِي الْمَجَالِسِ فَافْسَحُوا يَفْسَحِ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ

idhā qīla lakum tafassaḥū fī al-majālisi fa-ifsaḥū yafsaḥi allāhu lakum

“When you are told to make room in gatherings, make room, and The God will make room for you.”

Tafassaḥū and fa-ifsaḥū concern creating room and providing others with more space.

Raḥubat in Al-Tawbah, by contrast, describes the earth’s state of spaciousness.

In these witnesses, F S Ḥ connects more directly with making room and R Ḥ B with describing breadth.

This should not become a categorical rule for every derivative. Arabic also records transitive R Ḥ B forms meaning to broaden or expand something.

Tarḥīb, welcoming, and fasḥ, making room for others, are socially related. One nevertheless names reception, while the verse in Al-Mujādilah commands practically making room.

R Ḥ B (raḥb) and Ḍ Y Q (ḍīq)

This comparison matters because the Qur’an places the meanings together. The following abbreviates the expression in Al-Tawbah 9:25 and Al-Tawbah 9:118:

ضَاقَتْ ... الْأَرْضُ بِمَا رَحُبَتْ

ḍāqat … al-arḍu bi-mā raḥubat

“The earth, despite all its spaciousness, became narrow.”

Ḍīq contrasts with breadth, concerning narrowness, restriction, and difficulty.

Al-Tawbah uses that contrast for a situation in which the earth’s actual extent does not prevent experienced constriction.

The Qur’an also applies narrowness to the chest; part of Al-Anʿām 6:125, for example, reads:

يَجْعَلْ صَدْرَهُ ضَيِّقًا حَرَجًا

yajʿal ṣadrahu ḍayyiqan ḥarajā

“He makes his chest constricted and distressed.”

This shows narrowness extending beyond a place’s material dimensions to describe a person’s inward condition.

The contrast does not make R Ḥ B always mean mental calm, or Ḍ Y Q always mean sadness.

Broad land, spacious places, and welcoming each have their own usage, with their precise meanings determined by sentence and context.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The family requires distinguishing the direct sense of spaciousness from the established greeting.

Raḥuba: was spacious, broad, or ample.

Raḥubat: was spacious or broad; both Qur’anic occurrences concern the earth.

Raḥb: spacious, broad, or open.

Ruḥb: breadth, spaciousness, or extent.

Raḥābah: breadth or spaciousness.

Raḥīb: spacious, broad, or open.

Raḥbah: an open area or spacious grounds.

Riḥāb: broad areas and spaces.

Raḥḥaba bihi: received or welcomed him.

Tarḥīb: reception or welcoming.

Marḥaban: welcome.

Lā marḥaban: no welcome; may there be no welcome.

For the shared expression in Al-Tawbah 9:25 and Al-Tawbah 9:118, the proposed rendering is as follows; the ellipsis marks omitted wording:

ضَاقَتْ ... الْأَرْضُ بِمَا رَحُبَتْ

ḍāqat … al-arḍu bi-mā raḥubat

“And the earth, despite all its spaciousness, … became narrow.”

“For you” in the first verse and “for them” in the second must be preserved in accordance with the text.

This keeps two points clear: the earth really is spacious, yet in that state it becomes constricted for the people described.

For Ṣād 38:59, an appropriate rendering is:

لَا مَرْحَبًا بِهِمْ

lā marḥaban bihim

“No welcome to them.”

And for Ṣād 38:60:

بَلْ أَنْتُمْ لَا مَرْحَبًا بِكُمْ

bal antum lā marḥaban bikum

“Rather, you yourselves—no welcome to you.”

These preserve the negative expression’s rejecting force in speech without turning it into a report of the Fire’s physical breadth or narrowness.

7. Conclusion

R Ḥ B’s semantic center is breadth, spaciousness, and being wide. Arabic uses it to describe land, places, houses, grounds, and objects.

The same field yields expressions of openness in receiving others. Marḥaban became an established greeting and occurs in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The Qur’an uses raḥubat twice for the earth’s spaciousness. In both, breadth accompanies the constriction people experience in difficulty. The situations differ: one concerns Ḥunayn, the other the three who were left behind.

These usages show a place’s actual breadth not always coinciding with human experiences of relief or constraint. They do not make raḥb itself mean calm, security, or release.

Ṣād twice negates marḥaban. Lā marḥaban in the people of the Fire’s dialogue conveys rejection and an unwelcome reception. Its negative force comes from negation and the disputing context, rather than the root independently.

R Ḥ B thus describes perceptible spaciousness and forms an established expression of welcoming. All characteristics of either usage should not be transferred to the other.

Final definition: R Ḥ B denotes breadth, spaciousness, and being wide. Spatially, it describes the earth or something else being broad. In marḥaban, spaciousness becomes an expression of welcome and reception. Constriction, anxiety, rejection, and reproach in the relevant verses follow from negation, accompanying words, and context, rather than the root alone.
