Qur'anic Root

Qur'anic Root R J Z

Pollution, disturbance, trembling, instability, and a heavy, painful consequence.

1. Short Introduction to the Root

The root ر ج ز in Arabic is connected with unsettled motion, trembling, disturbance, and the repeated or broken movement of a thing that does not remain firm. In lexical usage, rijz is associated with agitation and a motion that disrupts stability; from there the word is also used for an affliction or punishment whose severity throws a person or society into disturbance. The same root, in the form rajaz, also became the name of one of the meters of Arabic poetry.

Even so, the image of trembling should not be treated as the final definition of every Qur'anic use. The Qur'an uses rijz or rujz in different settings: an affliction sent down upon wrongdoers, something attributed to Satan, something from which one must separate, and a description connected with a painful punishment. The precise meaning in each place must come from the full structure of the verse, not only from the first material image of the root.

The root ر ج ز occurs in the Qur'an ten times and only in two noun forms: rujz once, and rijz nine times. No conjugated verb from this root appears in the Qur'an.

2. Important Forms and Derivatives of the Root

رِجْز

This is the dominant Qur'anic form of the root. In most cases, it points to an affliction or hard consequence that descends upon wrongdoers or is lifted from them. In Al-Anfal 8:11 it appears in the phrase "رِجْزَ الشَّيْطَانِ", and its meaning must be studied in light of purification, firmness of hearts, and the stabilizing of feet.

رُجْز

This form appears once, in Al-Muddaththir 74:5:

وَالرُّجْزَ فَاهْجُرْ

Rujz in this verse is something from which one must distance oneself and separate. Lexical explanations mention meanings such as pollution, sin, conduct leading to punishment, shirk, an idol, or Satanic whispering; but the verse itself does not restrict the word to one specific object or action.

رَجَز

Rajaz is not the Qur'anic form used from this root, but in Arabic it names one of the poetic meters. Lexical sources connect that naming with alternating movement and stillness, the shortness of the metrical units, or the trembling of a camel's leg. This usage helps us recognize the lexical field of the root, but it should not be imposed directly on every Qur'anic occurrence.

4. The Semantic Core of the Root

The defensible core of the root ر ج ز may be stated as follows:

An unsettled movement or condition that disrupts ordinary stability and is connected with a hard or unpleasant consequence.

In early Arabic, this core appears in the trembling of a camel's hind legs, repeated movement, the continuous sound of thunder, and the broken meter of rajaz poetry. In Qur'anic use, this field extends to an affliction that descends upon a people, disrupts their ease, and places them in hardship.

But the lexical core and the contextual result must be kept distinct. The root by itself does not determine that rijz must be plague, earthquake, disease, fear, shirk, or one specific kind of punishment. Such identifications are acceptable only when the verse itself, or a clear linguistic witness, supports them.

5. The Material Image of the Root

One of the clearest recorded images for this root is the condition of a camel whose thighs or rear legs tremble for a time when it rises, before they stretch out and regain steadiness. In lexical descriptions, rajaz is the name of this condition, and arjaz or rajza' can describe a camel affected by it.

This image has three elements:

  • movement from stability into unease
  • repetition or succession in the motion
  • difficulty in maintaining or recovering firmness

This image can help us understand the general field of the root, but it is not the complete definition of Qur'anic rijz. One cannot conclude from the trembling of a camel's leg that every Qur'anic rijz is necessarily a wave, a physical vibration, a neurological disease, or an unknown force.

6. Use of the Root in Early Arabic

In early Arabic, ر ج ز was not limited to affliction and punishment. Several important branches of usage are reported.

First, it was used for the movement and trembling of a camel's leg, especially when the camel could not immediately regain balance while rising.

Second, rajaz became the name of a well-known Arabic poetic meter. This meter is built from short, recurring units and was used in work chants, camel-driving chants, improvisation, and martial verse. Lexical sources do not give one single explanation for the naming: some connect it with alternating motion and stillness in the meter, some with the shortness of its parts, and some with resemblance to the trembling of a camel. The link with the camel's condition is known and possible, but it should not be treated as the only certain explanation.

Third, derivatives of this root were also used for repeated or continuous sounds, such as thunder or waves of the sea. These uses show that continuity, repetition, and unrest are present in the lexical field of the root.

7. Challenges in Understanding and Translation

One difficulty in translating rijz is that a single fixed equivalent is often chosen for every occurrence. Words such as punishment, affliction, pollution, or whispering may each be suitable in some settings, but none of them covers the whole field of the word in every verse.

A second difficulty is the mixing of ر ج ز with ر ج س. Their closeness in sound and meaning has sometimes caused meanings of one root to be transferred to the other. Lexical sources also bring them near each other in some definitions, but semantic closeness does not prove that the two roots are identical or that their Qur'anic uses are fully interchangeable.

A third difficulty is the restriction of rujz in Al-Muddaththir 74:5 to one specific object. An idol, shirk, sin, or pollution may be among possible applications, but the verse itself does not restrict the word to a stone idol. Translation should preserve the range of the sentence, while possible applications can be discussed separately.

At the same time, an excessive reaction against narrow translations is also incorrect. Rijz should not be turned into modern physical, biological, or psychological terminology. Words such as wave, frequency, amplification, electricity, neural interference, or cellular collapse are not present in the Qur'anic text and cannot be inserted into the translation or base definition without independent evidence.

8. Methodological Note

In studying this root, four levels must be kept separate:

Lexical meaning: unrest, repeated movement, disturbance, and what disrupts stability.

Qur'anic use: affliction, hard consequence, a matter attributed to Satan, something to be abandoned, or a description connected with painful punishment.

Historical referent: the kind of affliction that came upon a particular people. In these verses the Qur'an does not always explain its material nature.

Contextual reflection: a broader observation that may be drawn from the relationship of rijz with wrongdoing, fisq, Satan, purification, and firmness.

Contextual reflection must not replace direct meaning. Likewise, the Qur'an's silence about the material nature of an affliction must not be filled with external reports or scientific theories.

9. Qur'anic Uses of the Root

Al-Baqarah 2:59

فَبَدَّلَ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا قَوْلًا غَيْرَ الَّذِي قِيلَ لَهُمْ فَأَنْزَلْنَا عَلَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا رِجْزًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ بِمَا كَانُوا يَفْسُقُونَ

Then those who wronged changed the saying into other than what had been said to them; so We sent down upon those who wronged an affliction from the sky because they were departing from command.

Al-Baqarah 2:59

In this verse, rijz appears with the verb "أَنزَلْنَا", the preposition "عَلَى", and the phrase "مِنَ السَّمَاءِ". From the root itself, one understands a hard and destabilizing consequence; its descent from the sky and its relation to wrongdoing and fisq come from the full structure of the verse. The verse does not specify the material type of this affliction.

Al-A'raf 7:134

وَلَمَّا وَقَعَ عَلَيْهِمُ الرِّجْزُ قَالُوا يَا مُوسَى ادْعُ لَنَا رَبَّكَ بِمَا عَهِدَ عِندَكَ لَئِن كَشَفْتَ عَنَّا الرِّجْزَ لَنُؤْمِنَنَّ لَكَ وَلَنُرْسِلَنَّ مَعَكَ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ

And when the rijz fell upon them, they said: O Musa, call upon your Lord for us by what He has pledged with you. If you remove this affliction from us, we will surely have faith in you and we will surely send the Children of Israel with you.

Al-A'raf 7:134

Here rijz is something that falls upon them and can be lifted from them. The translation "affliction" is natural in this setting, because the verse speaks about an outward distress that can be removed. Its intensity, type, and mechanism are not made clear by the word alone.

Al-A'raf 7:135

فَلَمَّا كَشَفْنَا عَنْهُمُ الرِّجْزَ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ هُم بَالِغُوهُ إِذَا هُمْ يَنكُثُونَ

Then when We removed the rijz from them until a term they were to reach, suddenly they were breaking the pledge.

Al-A'raf 7:135

This verse shows that rijz could be lifted temporarily. Kashf here means uncovering, removing, or taking away. Their breaking of the pledge is the result of their behavior after the affliction was lifted; it is not part of the lexical meaning of rijz.

Al-A'raf 7:162

فَبَدَّلَ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مِنْهُمْ قَوْلًا غَيْرَ الَّذِي قِيلَ لَهُمْ فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ رِجْزًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ بِمَا كَانُوا يَظْلِمُونَ

Then the wrongdoers among them changed the saying into other than what had been said to them; so We sent upon them an affliction from the sky because of the wrong they were doing.

Al-A'raf 7:162

Structurally this verse is close to Al-Baqarah 2:59. The difference between "فَأَنزَلْنَا" and "فَأَرْسَلْنَا" should not be turned, without evidence, into two different scientific or physical types of affliction.

Al-Anfal 8:11

إِذْ يُغَشِّيكُمُ النُّعَاسَ أَمَنَةً مِّنْهُ وَيُنَزِّلُ عَلَيْكُم مِّنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً لِّيُطَهِّرَكُم بِهِ وَيُذْهِبَ عَنكُمْ رِجْزَ الشَّيْطَانِ وَلِيَرْبِطَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِكُمْ وَيُثَبِّتَ بِهِ الْأَقْدَامَ

When He covered you with drowsiness as security from Him, and sent down upon you water from the sky, to purify you with it, to remove from you the rijz of Satan, to bind firmly upon your hearts, and to make the feet firm thereby.

Al-Anfal 8:11

This verse attributes rijz to Satan, but does not define its nature by another noun. Around it, the verse speaks of security, purification, firmness of hearts, and firmness of feet. From this contrast one may understand that the rijz of Satan is connected with disturbance, instability, or a polluting matter that disrupts readiness and firmness.

Translations such as Satanic whispering, Satanic pollution, Satanic unrest, or Satanic disturbance each reflect part of the setting. But turning it into electricity, a wave, a neural shock, or a physical phenomenon goes beyond the text. The verse presents rain as a means of purification and stabilization, but it does not describe an unknown scientific mechanism for removing rijz.

Al-'Ankabut 29:34

إِنَّا مُنزِلُونَ عَلَىٰ أَهْلِ هَٰذِهِ الْقَرْيَةِ رِجْزًا مِّنَ السَّمَاءِ بِمَا كَانُوا يَفْسُقُونَ

We are sending down upon the people of this settlement an affliction from the sky because they were departing from command.

Al-'Ankabut 29:34

Rijz in this verse is again a consequence sent down upon a society. The ethical cause stated in the verse is their fisq, but the kind of affliction is not explained.

Saba 34:5

وَالَّذِينَ سَعَوْا فِي آيَاتِنَا مُعَاجِزِينَ أُولَٰئِكَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ مِّن رِّجْزٍ أَلِيمٌ

And those who strive against Our ayat, seeking to make them powerless: for them is a painful punishment from rijz.

Saba 34:5

In this verse, "عَذَابٌ" is the main word and "مِن رِّجْزٍ" is part of its description. This structure shows that rijz and punishment are not completely identical in the sentence. Rijz is presented as a quality, kind, or source connected with the punishment; but this structure alone does not determine its physical type.

Al-Jathiyah 45:11

هَٰذَا هُدًى وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِآيَاتِ رَبِّهِمْ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ مِّن رِّجْزٍ أَلِيمٌ

This is guidance; and those who covered truth regarding the ayat of their Lord, for them is a painful punishment from rijz.

Al-Jathiyah 45:11

The structure of this verse clearly resembles Saba 34:5. In both, punishment is described with "مِن رِّجْزٍ". The adjective "أَلِيمٌ" expresses the painfulness of the punishment; therefore pain should not be placed by itself inside the definition of the root ر ج ز.

Al-Muddaththir 74:5

وَالرُّجْزَ فَاهْجُرْ

And from rujz, separate yourself.

Al-Muddaththir 74:5

The verb "فَاهْجُرْ" speaks of leaving and separating. Rujz here is something from which one must break away. Because the verse does not name the specific referent, translations such as "abandon pollution" or "keep away from what is polluted and corrupting" preserve the breadth of the expression better than restricting it to one particular idol.

This verse does not allow every unpleasant factor, lifestyle, disturbing sound, or unstable phenomenon to be automatically called rujz. Semantic expansion must be supported by Qur'anic or linguistic evidence.

10. Examination of the Two Qur'anic Forms

رِجْز

Rijz appears nine times in the Qur'an. In six places, it is connected with an affliction that descends upon a group or is lifted from them: Al-Baqarah 2:59, Al-A'raf 7:134 twice, Al-A'raf 7:135, Al-A'raf 7:162, and Al-'Ankabut 29:34.

In one place, it is attributed to Satan: Al-Anfal 8:11.

In two places, it appears in the structure "عَذَابٌ مِّن رِّجْزٍ أَلِيمٌ": Saba 34:5 and Al-Jathiyah 45:11.

This set shows that the dominant Qur'anic use of rijz is connected with affliction and hard consequence, while the phrase "رِجْزَ الشَّيْطَانِ" extends the range to a matter attributed to Satan.

رُجْز

Rujz appears only once: Al-Muddaththir 74:5. The vowel difference between rujz and rijz is recorded, but one should not build two independent and certain theories about their nature from that sound difference alone. In the Qur'an, the context of Al-Muddaththir is more decisive than the vowel pattern by itself.

11. Grammatical and Structural Notes

In most cases, rijz is a noun placed in different syntactic structures:

  • "رِجْزًا مِّنَ السَّمَاءِ" is the object of verbs of sending down or sending.
  • "وَقَعَ عَلَيْهِمُ الرِّجْزُ" makes rijz the subject of the verb "وَقَعَ".
  • "كَشَفْنَا عَنْهُمُ الرِّجْزَ" makes rijz the object of what is lifted from them.
  • "رِجْزَ الشَّيْطَانِ" is an iḍafah construction that attributes rijz to Satan.
  • "عَذَابٌ مِّن رِّجْزٍ" is a structure in which "min" may convey kind, type, or descriptive source of the punishment.
  • "وَالرُّجْزَ فَاهْجُرْ" places the object first, emphasizing the abandonment of rujz.

The absence of a conjugated verb from this root in the Qur'an is a morphological fact only. From it one cannot conclude that rijz is always externally imposed, never voluntary, or always one specific bodily state.

Likewise, the patterns fi'l or fu'l by themselves do not determine intensity, repetition, materiality, or moral direction. These features must be confirmed by usage and context.

12. Comparison with Near Roots

ر ج ز (RAJAZ) Compared with ر ج س (RAJAS)

In its dominant Qur'anic use, rijz is connected with affliction, hard consequence, and a disturbing matter. Rijs is more often used in the field of impurity, pollution, polluted action, or an undesirable condition.

Even so, the boundary between them is not absolute. In Al-Muddaththir 74:5, rujz is something that must be abandoned and can approach the field of pollution. Some lexical explanations also refer one of these words to the other. It is better to say:

ر ج ز in the Qur'an leans more toward affliction and severe disruption; ر ج س leans more toward impurity and pollution. This is a tendency in usage, not an absolute law of language.

One should not invent for rijz the meaning of a "dynamic wave" and for rijs the meaning of a "static deposit". Such a division is not established by Qur'anic and lexical evidence.

ر ج ز (RAJAZ) Compared with ر ج ف (RAJAF)

ر ج ف in the Qur'an has a more direct connection with shaking and visible trembling, such as "الرَّجْفَةُ" for a severe quake. ر ج ز may historically and imagistically be connected with unrest and trembling, but its Qur'anic use is mainly the name of an affliction or hard consequence.

Therefore translating rijz as "trembling" in every verse erases the boundary between it and ر ج ف.

ر ج ز (RAJAZ) Compared with ع ذ ب ('ADHAB)

Punishment is the general name for suffering or chastisement that comes upon someone. Rijz in some verses comes close to affliction, but in Saba 34:5 and Al-Jathiyah 45:11 it appears inside the structure "عَذَابٌ مِّن رِّجْزٍ". This co-occurrence shows that the two words are not fully identical.

At the same time, these two verses do not prove that rijz is always one specific physical type of punishment. The verse does not define such a type.

ر ج ز (RAJAZ) Compared with و س و س (WASWAS)

Waswasah concerns hidden and repeated suggestion. In Al-Anfal 8:11 the Qur'an speaks of "رِجْزَ الشَّيْطَانِ", not "the whispering of Satan". Whispering may be one route by which the rijz of Satan appears, but the verse does not define this relationship as a fixed chain.

So one cannot say that whispering is always the method and rijz is always its result. This distinction may be useful in one explanatory context, but it is not a stable rule for the language of the Qur'an.

ر ج ز (RAJAZ) Compared with خ و ف (KHAWF)

Khawf is fear before a known or possible danger and can lead to caution and proper response. Rijz is not necessarily synonymous with fear. In Al-Anfal 8:11, the surrounding field includes the need for security, firmness of heart, and stability of feet; but the word rijz itself should not be reduced to fear without evidence.

13. The Relation of Poetic Rajaz to Qur'anic Rijz

Poetic rajaz and Qur'anic rijz come from the same root, but they do not have one identical meaning.

In poetic rajaz, shortness, repetition, and successive movement of metrical units are prominent. In Qur'anic rijz, we mostly face affliction, hard consequence, and a disturbing matter. The connection between the two can be seen in the general idea of unstable or repeated movement.

But the structure of the poetic meter cannot be used to extract the material nature of Qur'anic punishment. Rajaz poetry is a witness to the historical field of the root, not a physical map of the rijz sent down from the sky.

14. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

Translation of rijz must be verse-based.

In Al-Baqarah 2:59, Al-A'raf 7:134-135, Al-A'raf 7:162, and Al-'Ankabut 29:34

Base suggestion:

affliction

In some contexts, punishment may also be possible, but affliction better preserves the unknown nature of the event.

In Al-Anfal 8:11

Base suggestion:

the unrest of Satan

or:

the pollution of Satan

"Disturbance of Satan" is also possible, though it sounds somewhat more psychological. It is better to preserve the word rijz in the word-by-word explanation so the reader knows that no single English equivalent carries the full field.

Suggested verse rendering:

"and to remove from you the rijz of Satan"

Then in a short explanation:

"that unrest or pollution attributed to Satan which disrupts the firmness of heart and feet."

In Saba 34:5 and Al-Jathiyah 45:11

Base suggestion:

a painful punishment from rijz

It is better to preserve the word rijz, because translating it as punishment creates a meaningless repetition and erases the difference in the structure "عَذَابٌ مِّن رِّجْزٍ".

In explanation one may write:

"a punishment from the kind of, or accompanied by, rijz; a hard and destabilizing consequence whose precise quality is not stated in the verse."

In Al-Muddaththir 74:5

Base suggestion:

and abandon pollution

or, preserving the word:

and from rujz, separate yourself

Then in explanation:

"Rujz is something polluted, corrupting, or abandonment-worthy; the verse does not restrict it to one specific referent."

15. Conclusion

The root ر ج ز in Arabic is connected with unrest, repeated motion, trembling, and the disruption of stability. The known image of a camel whose legs tremble when rising, and the use of rajaz for a broken poetic meter, clarify this lexical field. Yet these images should not be turned into a certain physical definition for every Qur'anic use.

In the Qur'an, this root occurs ten times and only in the forms rijz and rujz. The dominant use of rijz is an affliction that descends upon wrongdoers or is lifted from them. In Al-Anfal 8:11, rijz is attributed to Satan and appears in a context of purification, security, firmness of heart, and firmness of feet. In Saba and Al-Jathiyah, rijz appears in the description of a painful punishment. Rujz in Al-Muddaththir is something that must be abandoned.

A cautious final definition is therefore:

ر ج ز indicates a hard, disturbing, and destabilizing matter or condition that disrupts stability and ease; but its referent, from outward affliction to a matter attributed to Satan or a pollution to be abandoned, must be recognized separately in each verse from the context.

Rijz should not be treated as identical with rijs, even though there is overlap between them. It should also not be restricted to physical trembling, a wave, disease, psychological anxiety, or one specific type of punishment unless the verse itself or a reliable witness supports that restriction. The base translation should remain natural and concise, while the image of the root, the difference of forms, and interpretive possibilities should be offered in separate explanation.
