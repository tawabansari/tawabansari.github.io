1. Introducing the root

The triliteral Arabic root J Z ʿ has several branches. One concerns cutting, severing, and crossing a place from side to side; another concerns distress and losing the ability to endure hardship. Jazʿ also names a variegated ornamental stone known in early Arabic and pre-Islamic poetry.

In describing human behavior, jazaʿ contrasts with ṣabr, patience or endurance. Pain, calamity, fear, or adversity can overwhelm a person’s composure and bring about the distress called jazaʿ.

Jazaʿ should nevertheless be distinguished from sadness, fear, crying, or expressing pain as such. A person can grieve a loved one’s loss, fear danger, or seek help while retaining endurance and responsible conduct.

The distinction concerns a diminished or broken capacity to endure and regulate one’s response to hardship, consistent with the familiar contrast with ṣabr. Brief root meaning: Distress and diminished or broken endurance under hardship, contrasted with patience.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an:

  • أَجَزِعْنَا (a-jaziʿnā) in Ibrāhīm 14:21: a Form I perfect verb preceded by the hamzah of equivalence in sawāʾ … am, “whether we become distressed.”
  • جَزُوعًا (jazūʿan) in Al-Maʿārij 70:20: an adjective on the faʿūl pattern, intensely distressed or readily overcome by adversity.

Both occurrences concern distress, rather than cutting objects, crossing a valley, or ornamental stone.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two occurrences

The other branches remain relevant: they establish distinct early Arabic usages and help assess proposed semantic connections.

2. Semantic principles in early Arabic

Cutting and severing

Ibn Fāris identifies two principles for J Z ʿ in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْجِيمُ وَالزَّايُ وَالْعَيْنُ أَصْلَانِ، أَحَدُهُمَا الِانْقِطَاعُ، وَالْآخَرُ جَوْهَرٌ مِنَ الْجَوَاهِرِ.

al-jīmu wa-al-zāʾu wa-al-ʿaynu aṣlāni aḥaduhumā al-inqiṭāʿu wa-al-ākharu jawharun mina al-jawāhir

Translation: “Jīm, zāy, and ʿayn have two principles: severance, and a kind of precious stone.”

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs 1:453: two principles

He explains the first through crossing a route transversely, passing from one side of a valley to the other.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records jazaʿa al-mawḍiʿa for crossing a place from side to side.

A clear example is jazaʿa al-wādiya, crossing a valley transversely.

This does not divide a physical object in two: it traverses a route from one side of the valley to the other.

Arabic cutting expressions can thus concern either separating a body or crossing a route. Persian similarly uses “cutting across a road” without implying physically cutting the ground with a tool.

A rope breaking

Injazaʿa al-ḥablu describes a rope that has broken.

Jazaʿtu al-ḥabla can likewise mean cutting a rope.

Physical severance is clearer here: a formerly continuous rope breaks at a point and loses its continuity.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib treats cutting a rope as a basic image in this family, using it to explain human jazaʿ.

This does not prove that emotional distress historically developed from cutting a rope. The image offers an intelligible explanation, but similarity alone does not establish a chronology of meanings.

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: rope and severance

Jazaʿ in human behavior

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْجَزَعُ نَقِيضُ الصَّبْرِ.

al-jazaʿu naqīḍu al-ṣabr

Translation: “Jazaʿ is the opposite of ṣabr.”

Al-Jawharī records the same opposition in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ.

The subject is now a person’s response to adversity, rather than a rope or a route.

Ibn Fāris explains:

وَالْجَزَعُ نَقِيضُ الصَّبْرِ، وَهُوَ انْقِطَاعُ الْمُنَّةِ عَنْ حَمْلِ مَا نَزَلَ.

wa-al-jazaʿu naqīḍu al-ṣabri wa-huwa inqiṭāʿu al-munnati ʿan ḥamli mā nazal

Translation: “Jazaʿ is the opposite of patience: the breakdown of one’s strength to bear what has befallen one.”

Maqāyīs: inqiṭāʿ al-munnah and endurance

Munnah here means strength or capacity. Inqiṭāʿ al-munnah concerns losing endurance, rather than interrupting a favor or gift.

Jazaʿ is therefore more than an unpleasant feeling: it concerns the capacity to face hardship.

Someone may be sad yet able to decide, work, and act responsibly. Another person facing the same situation may become so distressed that composure breaks down.

Hardship, individual capacity, and circumstances vary. The lexical definition alone cannot determine each person’s moral responsibility without understanding their situation.

Do all the meanings derive from one principle?

Cutting a rope, crossing a valley, and losing endurance can be connected through an image of interruption or severance.

That connection does not establish that every derivative began with a single physical experience.

Ibn Fāris treats ornamental jazʿ as a separate principle. Al-Rāghib, by contrast, proposes explaining the stone’s contrasting colors and related uses through interruption and separation.

These approaches differ: one separates recognized branches, while the other proposes semantic connections among them.

For Qur’anic study, the reliable conclusion is that human jazaʿ concerns distress and impaired endurance. The cutting image may help explain it without becoming a compulsory definition of every derivative.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Jaziʿa and jazaʿ

Jaziʿa is Form I, with imperfect yajzaʿu and verbal noun jazaʿ.

The verb describes someone becoming distressed by something.

Jaziʿa mina al-shayʾi means becoming distressed by something. Other constructions can use different prepositions to identify the calamity or concern.

The verb expresses the distressed state or reaction. Crying, shouting, disturbed behavior, or inability to continue an activity must be learned from context.

Jāziʿ

Jāziʿ is an active participle describing someone experiencing jazaʿ.

It can describe either a particular situation or a behavioral trait.

The participle alone does not establish permanence. Duration and intensity depend on the sentence and accompanying evidence.

Jazūʿ

Jazūʿ is an adjective on the faʿūl pattern.

Lexical sources apply it to someone whose distress is frequent or pronounced.

Ibn al-Aʿrābī connects the distinction between ordinary distress and jazūʿ with its frequency.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Ibn al-Aʿrābī on frequent distress

This accords with the adjectival pattern, but does not mean that the person is distressed every moment or incapable of any endurance.

In Al-Maʿārij 70:20, jazūʿan concerns the specific encounter with adversity. The adjective must be read within that situation and the continuing passage.

Ajzaʿa

One use of ajzaʿa is causing another person distress.

Ajzaʿahu al-amru means that an event distressed him.

This construction emphasizes the cause of distress.

It should not be confused with Qur’anic a-jaziʿnā in Ibrāhīm 14:21. The latter combines the hamzah of equivalence, in interrogative form, with jaziʿnā; it is not Form IV.

The distinction matters: the Qur’anic speakers discuss their own distress, rather than causing others distress.

Jazaʿa and jizʿ

In the physical branch, jazaʿa can express cutting or crossing a place transversely.

Jizʿ, with kasrah on jīm, describes a section, bend, or crossing place in a valley.

Its familiar plural is ajzāʿ.

Jizʿ can also denote a group’s encampment beside a valley.

These senses occur in early Arabic poetry, but not in the Qur’an.

Jazʿ: ornamental stone

Jazʿ, with fatḥah on jīm and sukūn on zāy, names an ornamental stone or bead with contrasting colors, particularly black and white.

Arabic dictionaries also describe it as Yemeni stone or beads.

Collective and individual forms are recorded for the ornamental beads.

Its contrasting colors make it a poetic comparison for animals’ eyes.

This sense should not be merged with jazaʿ, distress.

Mujazzaʿ

Mujazzaʿ describes things whose sections differ in color or condition.

For instance, it can describe a date that is partly ripe and partly unripe.

Lexical accounts differ over the proportion: some mention half, others a greater or smaller amount.

Ṣiḥāḥ, Mufradāt, and Tāj: date vocalization and ripeness

The basic usage concerns contrasting sections, rather than a fixed numerical ratio.

It also describes meat and other things with two contrasting colors.

Juzʿah and jizʿah

Some Arabic uses of juzʿah and jizʿah concern a little water, milk, or property, or part of the night.

For example, the form can describe a small remainder of water in a vessel.

Lexical estimates vary; it should not always be restricted to half, a third, or another fixed measure.

This branch concerns portions and remainders, rather than distress.

4. Pre-Islamic Arabic usage

Distress and patience in Al-Aʿshā of Bāhilah’s elegy

A clear witness attributed to Al-Aʿshā of Bāhilah, mourning Al-Muntashir ibn Wahb’s death, is:

فَإِنْ جَزِعْنَا فَإِنَّ الشَّرَّ أَجْزَعَنَا
وَإِنْ صَبَرْنَا فَإِنَّا مَعْشَرٌ صُبُرُ

fa-in jaziʿnā fa-inna al-sharra ajzaʿanā / wa-in ṣabarnā fa-innā maʿsharun ṣubur

Translation: “If we become distressed, the calamity has distressed us; if we endure, we are a patient people.”

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān: Al-Aʿshā of Bāhilah’s witness

Jaziʿnā and ṣabarnā are opposed here.

The poet mourns someone dear. The calamity may overwhelm the survivors, yet they also affirm their capacity for endurance.

The poet does not deny sorrow itself; he relates possible distress to the severity of the loss.

Ajzaʿanā is causative: the calamity distressed us.

The short witness thus includes distress, its cause, and its contrast with patience.

It shows jazaʿ and ṣabr as established Arabic responses to affliction before the Qur’an.

The line alone does not establish whether the poet considered distress always permissible or always blameworthy. In this elegy, he describes the loss’s severity and the survivors’ endurance.

Jāziʿ in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads as follows. The wording quoted here occurs in Muʿjam al-Buldān; Lisān al-ʿArab has sālikun baṭna Nakhlah instead of qāṭiʿ, while some other transmissions exchange jāziʿ and qāṭiʿ. These variants do not alter the sense of crossing.

Yāqūt, Muʿjam al-Buldān: qāṭiʿ and jāziʿ

فَرِيقَانِ مِنْهُمْ قَاطِعٌ بَطْنَ نَخْلَةٍ
وَآخَرُ مِنْهُمْ جَازِعٌ نَجْدَ كَبْكَبِ

farīqāni minhum qāṭiʿun baṭna Nakhlatin / wa-ākharu minhum jāziʿun najda Kabkabi

Translation: “They formed two groups: one crossing the bed of Nakhlah’s valley, the other traversing the upland route of Kabkab.”

Jāziʿ here means crossing a route, rather than being distressed.

Nakhlah and Kabkab are known Arabian place names.

The poet describes two groups taking different routes: one through the valley bed, the other through the Kabkab area.

Jāziʿ can therefore derive from traversing a route, without directly concerning emotional distress.

The object and context determine its meaning.

Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān: variants of the Imruʾ al-Qays witness

Ajzāʿ in Labīd’s Muʿallaqah

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says in his celebrated Muʿallaqah:

حُفِزَتْ وَزَايَلَهَا السَّرَابُ كَأَنَّهَا
أَجْزَاعُ بِيشَةَ أَثْلُهَا وَرِضَامُهَا

ḥufizat wa-zāyalahā al-sarābu ka-annahā / ajzāʿu Bīshata athluhā wa-riḍāmuhā

Translation: “They were urged onward, and the mirage parted from them: as though they were the reaches of Bīshah, its tamarisks and heaped rocks.” Al-Tabrīzī explains that the caravan’s camels, revealed as the mirage recedes, resemble trees moving in the wind or rocky hills; athluhā is an appositional substitute for ajzāʿ. Another explanation of zāyalahā describes movement from one mirage to another.

Al-Tabrīzī, Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-ʿAshr: caravan comparison and athluhā

Ajzāʿ is the plural of jizʿ, denoting reaches or bends of a valley.

Bīshah is a known valley; athl is a kind of tree, and riḍām concerns large or heaped rocks.

The poet describes a caravan and a landscape affected by mirage. The comparison draws on the valley’s natural features.

This illustrates the root’s spatial branch.

Ajzāʿ here names sections of a landscape, rather than distress.

Labīd was a mukhaḍram poet who lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The verse belongs to his transmitted Muʿallaqah, but a poet’s pre-Islamic life should be distinguished from the precise date of each line.

Ornamental jazʿ in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays says:

كَأَنَّ عُيُونَ الْوَحْشِ حَوْلَ خِبَائِنَا
وَأَرْحُلِنَا الْجَزْعُ الَّذِي لَمْ يُثَقَّبِ

ka-anna ʿuyūna al-waḥshi ḥawla khibāʾinā / wa-arḥulinā al-jazʿu alladhī lam yuthaqqabi

Translation: “The wild animals’ eyes around our tent and saddles are like jazʿ beads not yet pierced.”

Ṣiḥāḥ and Asās: jazʿ and animals’ eyes in Imruʾ al-Qays

Al-jazʿ names the ornamental stone or beads.

The image compares eyes with a stone’s dark and light areas.

Lam yuthaqqabi indicates beads not yet pierced for threading or ornament making.

This establishes the ornamental-stone branch in literary Arabic.

Jazʿ was therefore not solely an ethical or emotional term. Related or identically written forms also concerned natural places, routes, and ornaments.

What the early witnesses establish

The witnesses present three different fields.

Al-Aʿshā of Bāhilah’s elegy describes a human response to calamity.

Imruʾ al-Qays and Labīd use other forms for traversing routes and valley bends.

The comparison with animals’ eyes names ornamental jazʿ.

Shared consonants alone therefore cannot reduce every form to one meaning.

The witnesses also establish distress versus patience before the Qur’an, which uses familiar language in its own particular contexts.

5. Qur’anic usage: distress versus patience

Ibrāhīm 14:21: distress when there is no escape

وَبَرَزُوا لِلَّهِ جَمِيعًا فَقَالَ الضُّعَفَاءُ لِلَّذِينَ اسْتَكْبَرُوا إِنَّا كُنَّا لَكُمْ تَبَعًا فَهَلْ أَنْتُمْ مُغْنُونَ عَنَّا مِنْ عَذَابِ اللَّهِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ ۚ قَالُوا لَوْ هَدَانَا اللَّهُ لَهَدَيْنَاكُمْ ۖ سَوَاءٌ عَلَيْنَا أَجَزِعْنَا أَمْ صَبَرْنَا مَا لَنَا مِنْ مَحِيصٍ

wa-barazū li-Allāhi jamīʿan fa-qāla al-ḍuʿafāʾu li-alladhīna istakbarū innā kunnā lakum tabaʿan fa-hal antum mughnūna ʿannā min ʿadhābi Allāhi min shayʾin qālū law hadānā Allāhu la-hadaynākum sawāʾun ʿalaynā a-jaziʿnā am ṣabarnā mā lanā min maḥīṣ

Proposed translation based on this study: “They all appeared before The God. The weak said to those who had been arrogant: We followed you; can you avert anything of The God’s punishment from us? They said: Had The God guided us, we would have guided you. It is all the same to us whether we become distressed or endure: we have no escape.”

The verse depicts followers confronting arrogant leaders on the Last Day.

The followers ask those they followed whether they can avert any of the divine punishment.

The leaders reply that, had The God guided them, they would have guided their followers. They then say:

“Sawāʾun ʿalaynā a-jaziʿnā am ṣabarnā.”

A-jaziʿnā and ṣabarnā express different states.

The first combines the hamzah of equivalence with the perfect jaziʿnā; the second is a perfect verb from Ṣ B R.

Al-Darwīsh: the hamzah of equivalence in sawāʾ … am

The two are opposed: becoming distressed or enduring.

In this scene, however, their different reactions do not change the speakers’ outcome.

The role of mā lanā min maḥīṣ

The verse concludes:

“Mā lanā min maḥīṣ.”

That is: “We have no escape.”

This explains why distress and endurance are treated as equivalent here.

The speakers can no longer escape their actions’ consequences. Distress will not save them; patience at that moment will not open an escape either.

The verse does not make patience and distress equivalent in every situation or deny patience’s value.

Their equivalence concerns the speakers’ particular inescapable outcome.

Many Qur’anic verses value patience. Ibrāhīm 14:21 instead concerns people facing their conduct’s settled consequences and acknowledging that they cannot escape.

Al-Ṭabarī: followers, leaders, and no escape

Is all distress a sign of denial or disbelief?

Here jazaʿ occurs in the speech of people facing their actions’ consequences on the Last Day.

That setting does not make the Arabic word inherently mean disbelief, polytheism, or denying truth.

The elegy of Al-Aʿshā of Bāhilah likewise describes distress at loss and calamity.

The behavior’s moral value requires attention to circumstances and the person’s response.

Ibrāhīm 14:21 principally concerns followers and leaders unable to escape their outcome. Distress and endurance are different responses to that same situation.

6. Distress as a pattern of human response

Al-Maʿārij 70:20: distress when adversity strikes

إِذَا مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ جَزُوعًا

idhā massahu al-sharru jazūʿan

Proposed translation based on this study: “When adversity touches him, distressed.”

Jazūʿan describes a person’s response to al-sharr, adversity.

The faʿūl adjective emphasizes the trait’s intensity and prominence here.

This is not an isolated sentence; the preceding and following statements must also be examined.

Al-Maʿārij 70:19: halūʿ

إِنَّ الْإِنسَانَ خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا

inna al-insāna khuliqa halūʿan

Proposed translation based on this study: “The human being was indeed created anxious.”

Halūʿan describes the human being.

Halaʿ concerns restlessness, intense anxiety, and an unstable response to life’s conditions.

The following verses describe two manifestations: encountering adversity and receiving good.

In the first situation the person is jazūʿ; in the second, manūʿ.

Halaʿ in this passage therefore concerns a broader pattern of response to hardship and prosperity.

This association does not make H L ʿ, J Z ʿ, and M N ʿ lexically identical.

Each word describes a different aspect of behavior.

Al-Maʿārij 70:21: withholding when good arrives

وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ الْخَيْرُ مَنُوعًا

wa-idhā massahu al-khayru manūʿan

Proposed translation based on this study: “And when good touches him, withholding.”

The person is now described while enjoying good.

Manūʿan belongs to M N ʿ and concerns withholding or denying.

The passage places two responses alongside each other.

Adversity brings distress; receiving good brings refusal to share it or convey it to others.

The first concerns hardship, the second prosperity.

Both can indicate limited capacity to face changing circumstances, but they belong to different roots.

Jazaʿ is not withholding wealth, and manʿ is not distress at calamity.

Is distress inevitable for human beings?

Khuliqa halūʿan describes a human trait, but the continuing passage does not present it as everyone’s unalterable fate.

Al-Maʿārij 70:22 introduces an exception: illā al-muṣallīn, “except those who pray.”

The following verses describe their sustained ṣalāh, recognition of a specified right in their wealth for the needy and deprived, belief in the Day of Recompense, awareness of responsibilities, trustworthiness, and faithful testimony.

The exception shows that human responses are not presented as compulsory and incapable of reform in every circumstance.

Distress may therefore be a human tendency under adversity without making it inevitable in every calamity or denying people’s ability to grow and regulate their responses.

Al-Ṭabarī: halaʿ, jazaʿ, manʿ, and the exception

Distress and social responsibility

The pairing of jazūʿan and manūʿan adds another point.

The verses concern more than inner emotion: alongside hardship, they examine conduct when good arrives.

The passage subsequently recalls the needy and deprived person’s right in one’s wealth.

This suggests that composure under hardship and responsibility in prosperity both matter to improving conduct.

That is a contextual inference, rather than part of J Z ʿ’s lexical definition.

Jazūʿ itself means distressed; financial and social obligations follow from the passage’s other statements.

7. Distress, sorrow, and expressing suffering

Is jazaʿ the same as sorrow?

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib describes jazaʿ as stronger than ḥuzn: sorrow may become so intense that it interrupts what a person is doing.

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: jazaʿ and ḥuzn

This identifies a real distinction.

Ḥuzn can name sorrow arising from loss, disappointment, or adversity.

Jazaʿ concerns a reaction affecting endurance and self-regulation under hardship.

Not every sorrow is therefore jazaʿ.

Someone may deeply grieve a loved one’s death yet fulfill their responsibilities; another may become so overwhelmed that composure fails.

Jazaʿ more readily describes the latter.

This does not establish a fixed numerical or psychological threshold. Arabic distinguishes the meanings, while each state’s intensity and form require actual evidence.

Jacob: sorrow without abandoning hope

Jacob’s story in Yūsuf helps clarify the distinction.

Yūsuf 12:84 describes his intense sorrow and his eyes becoming white.

In Yūsuf 12:86, however, he brings his anguish and sorrow to The God; in Yūsuf 12:87, he commands his sons to search for Joseph and his brother and forbids despair.

Deep grief, expressing suffering, and continuing in hope and responsibility can thus coexist.

Every tear, sorrow, or complaint should not immediately be labeled blameworthy jazaʿ.

Jacob’s story does not mean distressed behavior can never involve crying. The distinction is between sorrow itself and a breakdown of endurance.

Job: expressing suffering while remaining patient

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:83, Job mentions his affliction and turns toward divine mercy.

Ṣād 38:44 also identifies him as patient.

Expressing pain and requesting relief are therefore compatible with patience.

If patience meant absolute silence under suffering, every expression of pain would become evidence of jazaʿ.

The Qur’an does not support that generalization.

Seeking help or treatment, protesting injustice, and trying to change difficult conditions do not in themselves constitute a breakdown of endurance.

Distress and fear

Khawf concerns fear in response to actual or possible danger.

Jazaʿ can accompany fear without being identical to it.

Someone can fear danger yet act thoughtfully and steadily. Intense fear, however, can also disturb decision-making and composure.

In that latter situation, fear may accompany jazaʿ.

Jazūʿ should consequently not always be rendered simply “afraid.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:20 concerns al-sharr, adversity that befalls a person. Fear, loss, or other difficulties may accompany it, but jazaʿ names the distressed response.

8. Jazaʿ and ṣabr: related but distinct concepts

Patience involves more than bearing pain

Qur’anic ṣabr concerns self-restraint, endurance, steadfastness, and persistence under difficulty or in fulfilling responsibility.

Some verses concern calamity; others concern continuing a call, worship, upholding rights, resisting falsehood, or refraining from misconduct.

Ṣabr is therefore broader than bearing suffering.

Qur’anic jazaʿ, particularly in Al-Maʿārij 70:20, contrasts with endurance and regulating one’s response.

It does not follow that jazaʿ opposes every use of ṣabr in every field.

The contrast is explicit in Ibrāhīm 14:21, where the words name two reactions to the same situation.

Other fields, such as persistence in truth or restraining desire, may have counterparts expressed by different roots and concepts.

Persistence in falsehood is also possible

Some Qur’anic verses describe people persevering in wrongful beliefs or behavior.

Persistence is therefore not invariably praiseworthy.

Jazaʿ likewise does not lexically mean disbelief or a particular sin. It names distress; responsibility and value depend on circumstances and conduct.

The speakers in Ibrāhīm 14:21 treat distress and endurance as equivalent when facing their outcome. They do not establish either response’s absolute value in every situation.

Al-Maʿārij 70:20 places distress under adversity within a critical description of human behavior.

The lexical meaning should therefore be distinguished from its moral position in a particular context.

Patience and continuing responsibility

If jazaʿ concerns impaired endurance, one possible consequence is inability to continue a task for which a person is responsible.

Distress might, for instance, lead to a hasty decision or abandoning something essential.

These are possible consequences, rather than fixed components of the lexical definition.

Continuing an activity is not always proper patience either. A person may need to leave harmful circumstances, seek help, or oppose injustice.

Qur’anic patience should not become insistence on suffering harm or maintaining injustice.

For a fuller examination of this distinction and verses about steadfastness in truth, see Forqan’s separate study, “Ṣabr in the Qur’an: Patience, Steadfastness, and What Makes Them Worthwhile.”

Study of ṣabr in the Qur’an

9. Comparisons with related roots

J Z ʿ (jazaʿ) and Ṣ B R (ṣabr)

Study of Ṣ B R

Ibrāhīm 14:21 explicitly contrasts them.

Jazaʿ concerns distress and diminished or broken endurance under hardship.

Ṣabr here concerns retaining endurance and regulating one’s response.

Its broader Qur’anic field also includes persistence in truth, worship, and responsibility.

Jazaʿ thus opposes a central field of ṣabr without defining the latter’s entire meaning by its absence.

J Z ʿ (jazaʿ) and H L ʿ (halaʿ)

Study of H L ʿ

Halaʿ concerns restlessness and intense reactions to adverse or favorable changes in life.

Halūʿ describes the human being in Al-Maʿārij 70:19; the next two verses explain its manifestations through jazūʿ and manūʿ.

Halaʿ therefore concerns a broader behavioral pattern in this passage.

Jazaʿ describes distress when adversity arrives; manʿ concerns withholding when good arrives.

The three are connected contextually without being lexically identical.

J Z ʿ (jazaʿ) and Ḥ Z N (ḥuzn)

Study of Ḥ Z N

Ḥuzn concerns sorrow arising from loss, disappointment, or calamity.

Jazaʿ emphasizes distress and impaired endurance or composure.

A person can be sad without experiencing jazaʿ.

Jazaʿ may accompany sorrow but is not confined to it.

“Sorrow” alone is therefore insufficient; “distress” better indicates the distinction.

J Z ʿ (jazaʿ) and KH W F (khawf)

Study of KH W F

Khawf concerns fear of danger or an adverse outcome.

Fear may cause jazaʿ, but jazaʿ does not name fear itself.

Qur’anic fear can accompany faith, vigilance, and responsibility. It is not invariably weakness or distress.

Jazaʿ more readily describes a response that loses composure under hardship.

J Z ʿ (jazaʿ) and F Z ʿ (fazaʿ)

Study of F Z ʿ

Fazaʿ concerns alarm, agitation, or an intense reaction to something frightening.

Its sudden and fearful character can be prominent.

Jazaʿ and fazaʿ may coexist without being identical.

Fazaʿ can follow a sudden event; jazaʿ emphasizes impaired endurance or composure under hardship.

This does not make fazaʿ invariably brief or jazaʿ invariably prolonged. Neither root alone fixes duration.

J Z ʿ (jazaʿ) and Y ʾ S (yaʾs)

Study of Y ʾ S

Yaʾs concerns despair.

Someone may lose hope of one outcome while retaining composure or continuing another responsibility.

Conversely, someone may remain hopeful yet become distressed by a calamity.

Jazaʿ and yaʾs can therefore accompany each other without being synonyms.

In Ibrāhīm 14:21, no escape remains, connecting the situation with lost hope of deliverance. A-jaziʿnā itself still names distress.

J Z ʿ (jazaʿ) and J Z ʾ (juzʾ)

Study of J Z ʾ

J Z ʿ and J Z ʾ are distinct roots.

J Z ʿ records cutting, severance, distress, and certain spatial and ornamental-stone senses.

J Z ʾ has juzʾ for part or share and forms such as ajzaʾa for sufficing.

Cutting and partitioning can bring their concepts close, but that similarity does not merge the roots.

Qur’anic jazūʿan contains no sense of a share, financial portion, or sufficiency.

J Z ʿ (jazaʿ) and J Z Y (jazāʾ)

Study of J Z Y

Jazāʾ belongs to J Z Y, concerning a response to action, reward, punishment, and a corresponding outcome in Qur’anic usage.

Qur’anic jazaʿ means distress.

Similar initial letters do not establish a shared meaning.

Jazaʿ in Ibrāhīm 14:21 is a response to the consequences of conduct, rather than the recompense or punishment itself.

The outcome and punishment follow from the verse’s context.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

For human behavior, the principal Persian equivalent of jazaʿ is bī-tābī, distress or loss of composure.

It is more precise than “sorrow,” since jazaʿ is more than sadness, and broader than “fear,” since loss or other adversity can also cause distress.

Jaziʿa can be rendered “became distressed.”

Yajzaʿu means “becomes distressed”; jāziʿ may be “distressed” or “one who becomes distressed,” according to the sentence.

In Ibrāhīm 14:21, a-jaziʿnā am ṣabarnā can be rendered:

“Whether we become distressed or endure.”

This preserves the opposition between the verbs.

In Al-Maʿārij 70:20, jazūʿan can be “distressed” or “intensely distressed.”

Idhā massahu al-sharru jazūʿan can retain its adjectival structure as “when adversity touches him, distressed.”

Persian bī-tāb and bī-qarār can both work naturally, provided the root explanation distinguishes distress under hardship from mere movement or ordinary restlessness.

The non-Qur’anic branches require different equivalents.

Jazaʿa al-wādiya means “he crossed the valley transversely.”

Jizʿ al-wādī denotes a bend, reach, or crossing place in a valley.

Injazaʿa al-ḥablu means “the rope broke.”

For ornamental jazʿ, the name can be retained with “variegated ornamental stone” as a brief explanation.

Mujazzaʿ describes dates or similar things whose parts differ in color or ripeness.

These meanings should not be transferred to the Qur’anic distress occurrences.

11. Conclusion

J Z ʿ has several Arabic branches: cutting, severing, and crossing routes; distress under hardship; and variegated ornamental stone. Small portions, contrasting colors, and valley sections are also recorded.

Ibn Fāris separates severance and ornamental stone as two principles. Al-Rāghib proposes connections through cutting and interruption. These explanations may help without establishing a definite historical derivation of every word.

Early poetry establishes both traversing valleys and distress at calamity before the Qur’an. Al-Aʿshā of Bāhilah’s elegy directly contrasts jazaʿ and ṣabr.

The Qur’an uses the root twice, both times for distress.

In Ibrāhīm 14:21, those facing their actions’ consequences say distress and patience are equivalent because they cannot escape. This particular situation does not negate patience’s value elsewhere.

In Al-Maʿārij 70:20, jazūʿan describes responding to adversity. It accompanies halūʿan and manūʿan, before the passage identifies people excepted from that pattern.

Qur’anic jazaʿ therefore concerns distress and diminished or broken endurance and self-regulation under hardship.

It should not be generalized to all sadness, fear, crying, or requests for help. Jacob and Job show that expressing sorrow and seeking relief can accompany steadfastness.

Bī-tābī is consequently the principal suitable Persian equivalent. Bī-qarārī can also convey it in context, provided it is distinguished from ordinary, passing restlessness.

The study’s central distinction is between experiencing suffering and losing endurance. Pain and sorrow may befall a person; jazaʿ concerns the response. This distinction helps situate the Qur’anic word without imposing psychological, ethical, or religious claims beyond the evidence.
