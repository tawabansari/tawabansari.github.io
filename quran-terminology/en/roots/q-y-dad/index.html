Providing, appointing, bringing into companionship, equivalence, exchange, and breaking a shell

The Arabic root ق ي ض has several semantic branches. قَيَّضَ means to provide, prepare, or make something or someone available to another. When applied to people, it can mean bringing someone into companionship with another or appointing someone for them.

In another branch, قَيْض denotes an equivalent or substitute, and مُقَايَضَة denotes exchanging one thing for another. قَيْض also names the outer shell of a bird's egg, while قَاضَ الْفَرْخُ الْبَيْضَةَ describes a chick breaking through its shell.

These uses are recorded within one lexical family, but historical evidence is needed before reducing them all to a single original meaning. Breaking an eggshell, exchanging goods, and providing a companion are different actions.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran, both times as the Form II verb قَيَّضَ:

In Fuṣṣilat 41:25, وَقَيَّضْنَا concerns providing companions for a group of people.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:36, نُقَيِّضْ concerns appointing a devil as companion to someone who turns away from remembrance of the Entirely Merciful.

The God is the subject in both verses. This attribution does not itself mean compelling people to go astray. Understanding divine action in relation to human choice requires examining the stated condition and consequence, together with the people's conduct.

1. The root in early Arabic

قَيْض: the outer shell of an egg

One established early meaning is the hard outer shell of a bird's egg.

Arabic sources record قَيْضُ الْبَيْضَةِ for the outer shell, particularly the broken shell remaining after a chick has emerged.

قَاضَ الْفَرْخُ الْبَيْضَةَ means that the chick broke through the eggshell.

تَقَيَّضَتِ الْبَيْضَةُ describes an egg whose shell has broken into pieces.

For example, when a chick has developed inside an egg and is ready to emerge, it breaks the shell from within. Shell fragments remain around the nest after it emerges.

Here, qayḍ concerns the shell or its breaking, rather than companionship, exchange, or destiny.

An early poetic witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays contains these lines:

تَرَوَّحَ مِنْ أَرْضٍ لِأَرْضٍ نَطِيَّةٍ

tarawwaḥa min arḍin li-arḍin naṭiyyatin

لِذِكْرَةِ قَيْضٍ حَوْلَ بَيْضٍ مُفَلَّقِ

li-dhikrati qayḍin ḥawla bayḍin mufallaqi

Translation:

“It returns from one land to a distant land, remembering shells around broken eggs.”

The preceding lines describe a bird of the ostrich family.

نَطِيَّة denotes a distant land.

بَيْضٍ مُفَلَّقِ refers to broken eggs.

قَيْض denotes the shell fragments remaining after the eggs have split.

The image is of a bird returning from afar to its eggs.

The verse appears in the well-known poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays that begins أَلَا انْعَمْ صَبَاحًا أَيُّهَا الرَّبْعُ وَانْطِقِ.

Some transmitted versions differ in the opening verb and surrounding words, but قَيْضٍ حَوْلَ بَيْضٍ مُفَلَّقِ appears in familiar versions.

This witness shows that qayḍ was known as a term for broken eggshell in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Attributing a poem to Imruʾ al-Qays does not independently establish the exact date of every verse.

More importantly, this witness supports only the eggshell sense. It cannot establish that “providing a companion” had the same history or was used in that period.

قاضَ: splitting and opening a shell

Another use of قَاضَ concerns splitting something.

For example, a chick breaks the shell as it emerges.

Some lexical accounts also record the verb for cutting through rock when digging a well.

The action here opens a passage through something hard.

This sense of splitting should not be transferred to the Quranic verb قَيَّضَ.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:25 and Al-Zukhruf 43:36, nothing is split; companions are provided for people.

قَيْض: equivalent and substitute

Another branch concerns equivalence and substitution.

Arabic uses هُمَا قَيْضَانِ of two things or people that are counterparts or equivalents.

Here قَيْض can denote something that takes another thing's place or is given in exchange for it.

For example, someone may exchange one horse for another.

Each horse then constitutes the consideration given for the other.

The related قَايَضَ and مُقَايَضَة are recorded for exchanging goods for goods.

Early accounts even give the example of exchanging one horse for two others.

Being a counterpart or an exchange item therefore need not mean complete equality of value or qualities.

What matters is one side of the transaction being given in exchange for the other.

A possible conceptual link connects this with providing a companion: both involve placing something opposite or alongside something else.

Their historical relationship remains uncertain. Qayḍ should not be defined as necessarily meaning exchange or replacement in every derivative.

قَيَّضَ: provided and made available

Arabic dictionaries explain قَيَّضَ اللَّهُ فُلَانًا لِفُلَانٍ as The God providing one person for another or bringing that person within their path or reach.

قَيَّضَ اللَّهُ لَهُ قَرِينًا likewise concerns providing a companion for someone.

Explanations use expressions such as هَيَّأَ, سَبَّبَ, and أَتَاحَ.

These concern preparing the circumstances, causes, or opportunity for companionship.

For example, a person may meet someone along their way who later becomes a close companion.

Such companionship arises through a combination of circumstances and events.

The verb alone does not determine whether the association was accidental, forced, desirable, or undesirable.

The sentence identifies the kind of companion and the outcome of the relationship.

Both Quranic occurrences involve misguidance, but this does not make “misguiding” the root's independent meaning.

The antiquity of this branch needs care: providing a companion is clear in the Quran and early lexical explanations, but this review has not established an independently datable pre-Quranic poetic witness for this construction.

The meaning is therefore securely attested in Arabic at the time of revelation; without evidence, it should not be claimed for a particular earlier poem in precisely this form and sense.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Recorded forms in the ق ي ض family include:

  • قَيْض: the hard outer shell of an egg; in another use, an equivalent or substitute.
  • قَاضَ / يَقِيضُ: split a shell or something else; in the exchange branch, gave one thing in return for another.
  • تَقَيَّضَ: broke into fragments; when applied to a person, came to resemble another.
  • اِنْقَاضَ: cracked or split without necessarily breaking into separate pieces.
  • مَقِيض: the place of eggshells; its precise referent depends on the lexical witness.
  • قَايَضَ / يُقَايِضُ: exchanged goods for other goods.
  • مُقَايَضَة: barter, the exchange of goods for goods.
  • قَيْضَان: two counterparts or equivalents.
  • قِيَاض: an exchange or substitute in early usage.
  • قَيَّضَ / يُقَيِّضُ: provided or prepared something or someone for another.
  • تَقْيِيض: providing and arranging; in another branch, splitting or opening a fissure.
  • مُقَيَّض: something provided for someone, or, in another use, split open.
  • اِقْتَاضَ: in some uses, took something in exchange; elsewhere, uprooted or eliminated something.
  • قَيِّض: in another use, a small stone heated for cauterization in treating certain livestock diseases.

This list records Arabic forms; it does not imply that all occur in the Quran.

Only the Form II verb قَيَّضَ occurs in the Quran, in two forms.

وَقَيَّضْنَا: the past tense

وَقَيَّضْنَا in Fuṣṣilat 41:25 is a Form II past-tense verb.

Its subject is the pronoun نَا, referring to The God.

قُرَنَاءَ is the verb's object.

لَهُمْ specifies for whom these companions were provided.

The sentence thus says: We provided companions for them.

نُقَيِّضْ: a jussive imperfect verb

نُقَيِّضْ in Al-Zukhruf 43:36 is a first-person plural Form II imperfect verb.

It is jussive because it expresses the consequence of a condition.

وَمَنْ يَعْشُ ... نُقَيِّضْ first states the person's situation and then its consequence.

The two Quranic forms do not differ in the meaning of the root.

قَيَّضْنَا expresses provision in the past tense.

نُقَيِّضْ expresses the same meaning within a conditional construction.

Neither verse concerns breaking an eggshell or exchanging goods.

3. وَقَيَّضْنَا لَهُمْ قُرَنَاءَ: providing companions

Fuṣṣilat 41:25

وَقَيَّضْنَا لَهُمْ قُرَنَاءَ فَزَيَّنُوا لَهُمْ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ وَحَقَّ عَلَيْهِمُ الْقَوْلُ فِي أُمَمٍ قَدْ خَلَتْ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ مِنَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنْسِ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا خَاسِرِينَ

wa-qayyaḍnā lahum quranāʾa fa-zayyanū lahum mā bayna aydīhim wa-mā khalfahum wa-ḥaqqa ʿalayhimu al-qawlu fī umamin qad khalat min qablihim mina al-jinni wa-al-insi innahum kānū khāsirīn

Fuṣṣilat 41:25

“And We provided companions for them, who made what was before them and behind them seem fair to them; and the word became due against them among communities of jinn and humans that had passed before them. They were losers.”

The verse links three matters:

The provision of companions.

Those companions making things appear attractive.

The realization of a harmful outcome for the people concerned.

These are not expressed by a single verb.

قَيَّضْنَا expresses providing companions.

زَيَّنُوا expresses what the companions do.

حَقَّ عَلَيْهِمُ الْقَوْلُ expresses the fulfillment of the divine judgment or word concerning their outcome.

This distinction matters: qayḍ should not be equated with making falsehood attractive or carrying out punishment.

قُرَنَاءَ: who are the companions?

قُرَنَاء is the plural of قَرِين, from the separate root ق ر ن.

A qarīn is someone or something closely associated with another.

Such association can develop through a shared life, conduct, beliefs, or activity.

Here, the companions make things appear attractive to the group concerned.

The word قُرَنَاء alone does not establish that all were necessarily humans or all were jinn.

Al-Anʿām 6:112 mentions devils among humans and jinn.

Across the Quran, deceiving and making falsehood attractive are therefore not confined to one kind of being.

However, مِنَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنْسِ near the end of Fuṣṣilat 41:25 describes earlier communities; it does not by itself identify the exact composition of this group's companions.

These parts of the sentence should not be conflated without grammatical examination.

فَزَيَّنُوا لَهُمْ: making things appear beautiful

زَيَّنُوا comes from ز ي ن and concerns adornment and making things seem beautiful.

Here, the companions make certain things appear attractive to those concerned.

For example, someone may seek power and superiority over others.

Companions with the same ambitions may present oppression as courage, corruption as cleverness, and humiliating others as authority.

In such an environment, the person stops measuring conduct against truth and justice, continually hearing approval from those who portray it favorably.

This example illustrates how companionship can reinforce a mistaken path.

The verb qayḍ itself does not mean this beautification.

That action is expressed by زَيَّنُوا.

مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ

Literally, this expression refers to what is before them and behind them.

In context, it can be related to worldly affairs, the future, the past, actions, and their consequences.

Some readings associate what lies before them with worldly life and what lies behind them with the hereafter.

The wording alone does not conclusively determine that division.

The clear point is that the companions make a range of matters appear appealing and desirable to those concerned.

A person may justify a wrongful past, continue wrongdoing in the present, and imagine its future favorably.

This breadth is a significant consequence of companionship with people who continually approve errors instead of correcting them.

The context: prior human choices

Fuṣṣilat 41:22 and Fuṣṣilat 41:23 concern people who thought The God did not know much of what they did; that assumption brought them to loss.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:26, those who disbelieve tell one another not to listen to the Quran and to make a disturbance during its recitation.

They are thus not portrayed simply as passive recipients of imposed companionship.

They hold beliefs, assumptions, and patterns of conduct opposed to truth.

The provision of companions should be understood in relation to that intellectual and behavioral course, rather than as an account of The God first forcing innocent people into corruption.

4. نُقَيِّضْ لَهُ شَيْطَانًا: condition and consequence

Al-Zukhruf 43:36

وَمَنْ يَعْشُ عَنْ ذِكْرِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ نُقَيِّضْ لَهُ شَيْطَانًا فَهُوَ لَهُ قَرِينٌ

wa-man yaʿshu ʿan dhikri al-raḥmāni nuqayyiḍ lahu shayṭānan fa-huwa lahu qarīn

Al-Zukhruf 43:36

“And whoever turns away from remembrance of the Entirely Merciful, We appoint a devil for him, who becomes his companion.”

This verse offers the clearest Quranic construction for understanding qayḍ in relation to human choice.

The sentence begins with وَمَنْ يَعْشُ.

Its consequence follows: نُقَيِّضْ لَهُ شَيْطَانًا.

The verse therefore states a conditional relationship.

First, the person turns away from remembrance of the Entirely Merciful.

Then a devil is provided who becomes that person's companion.

يَعْشُ: turning away from remembrance

يَعْشُ comes from ع ش و, associated with dim vision and, in another use, turning away or disregarding.

Here the verb takes the preposition عَنْ.

يَعْشُ عَنْ ذِكْرِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ therefore refers to turning away from or disregarding remembrance of the Entirely Merciful.

This should not simply be equated with physical blindness.

Someone may have healthy eyes and hear truthful speech yet refuse to consider or reflect on it.

The issue in such a case is not impaired physical sight.

Conversely, not everyone who has yet to recognize a truth or has questions about it should be classified under this verse.

Questioning, sincere inquiry, and lack of knowledge differ from turning away from truth available to someone.

Why does The God appoint a devil?

The verse attributes the appointment to The God.

That attribution must be preserved.

It does not establish that The God forces someone into misguidance without cause or any role for human choice.

The verse identifies the preceding condition: disregarding remembrance of the Entirely Merciful.

Al-Ṣaff 61:5 states a similar order concerning the deviation of hearts.

Al-Ṣaff 61:5

وَإِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِقَوْمِهِ يَا قَوْمِ لِمَ تُؤْذُونَنِي وَقَدْ تَعْلَمُونَ أَنِّي رَسُولُ اللَّهِ إِلَيْكُمْ فَلَمَّا زَاغُوا أَزَاغَ اللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْفَاسِقِينَ

wa-idh qāla mūsā li-qawmihi yā qawmi lima tuʾdhūnanī wa-qad taʿlamūna annī rasūlu allāhi ilaykum fa-lammā zāghū azāgha allāhu qulūbahum wa-allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-fāsiqīn

Al-Ṣaff 61:5

“And when Moses said to his people: My people, why do you hurt me when you know that I am the Messenger of The God to you? Then, when they deviated, The God caused their hearts to deviate; and The God does not guide the defiantly disobedient people.”

Their own deviation comes first; the deviation of their hearts is then attributed to The God.

This connection is relevant to understanding Al-Zukhruf 43:36.

Someone who continually turns away from reminders of truth may follow a course in which companions who endorse that deviation become available.

Human choice and its consequences are connected in this situation.

Does the devil eliminate human choice?

The next verse makes the companions' role clearer.

Al-Zukhruf 43:37

وَإِنَّهُمْ لَيَصُدُّونَهُمْ عَنِ السَّبِيلِ وَيَحْسَبُونَ أَنَّهُمْ مُهْتَدُونَ

wa-innahum la-yaṣuddūnahum ʿani al-sabīli wa-yaḥsabūna annahum muhtadūn

Al-Zukhruf 43:37

“And they surely turn them away from the path, while they think themselves rightly guided.”

Two matters appear here.

First, the companions turn people away from the path.

Second, those people believe they have found the right way.

The danger is therefore more than an overt invitation to wrongdoing.

A person may become so immersed in an intellectual and behavioral environment that a wrong path seems right.

For example, someone may join a group whose members regard financial corruption as cleverness.

Hearing this continually, the person may eventually feel no shame about such conduct and may even consider it admirable.

The companions have influenced that person's judgment.

Influencing judgment does not necessarily amount to complete compulsion.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, the devil states at the reckoning that he had no coercive authority over people: he invited them, and they responded.

Al-Naḥl 16:99 and Al-Naḥl 16:100 discuss the devil's authority in relation to those who take him as an ally.

Across these verses, temptation and influence remain connected to human responsibility rather than replacing it.

Is such companionship irreversible?

Al-Zukhruf 43:38

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَنَا قَالَ يَا لَيْتَ بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَكَ بُعْدَ الْمَشْرِقَيْنِ فَبِئْسَ الْقَرِينُ

ḥattā idhā jāʾanā qāla yā layta baynī wa-baynaka buʿda al-mashriqayni fa-biʾsa al-qarīn

Al-Zukhruf 43:38

“Until, when he comes to Us, he says: If only there were the distance of the two easts between me and you! What an evil companion!”

At the reckoning, the person repudiates his companion.

An association that may have seemed desirable or acceptable in worldly life becomes unwelcome when its outcome is revealed.

The verse does not say that at every stage in worldly life the person was forever incapable of reform or return.

Nor should an unlimited opportunity be inferred from it.

Its subject is the outcome of someone who remained on that course and now faces its consequences.

Other Quranic passages address return and reform before the reckoning. Qayḍ cannot therefore be defined as the irrevocable closure of every way back.

5. Qayḍ, companionship, and human responsibility

Is every association a conscious choice?

No.

People do not choose their birthplace, family, many social circumstances, or everyone they encounter early in life.

A child may grow up amid violence or dishonesty without having helped create that environment.

Every unsuitable companion or difficult circumstance cannot be attributed to someone's prior wrongdoing.

Al-Zukhruf 43:36 concerns a specific situation: disregarding remembrance of the Entirely Merciful and the companionship that follows.

It should not become a rule for judging all unwanted circumstances in people's lives.

How can companionship reinforce a course of conduct?

People are influenced by those with whom they associate.

Continual association with people who justify lying, oppression, or selfishness may gradually weaken sensitivity to these behaviors.

Conversely, associating with upright people who seek truth can improve judgment and conduct.

Such influence is observable in social life.

These examples explain how companionship can influence people; they do not make qayḍ the direct Quranic name of a specific psychological or social process.

The word means providing or appointing.

How companions exert influence is learned from the verses' other verbs and from human experience.

Association and submission

Two people may share a workplace or living environment yet disagree on important matters.

Merely associating with someone who behaves wrongly does not prove that another person accepts that conduct.

Al-Zukhruf 43:37 describes more than a companion's presence: they turn people away from the path while those people think themselves guided.

Here, companionship involves influence on discernment and conduct.

For example, someone may be among people who speak ill of others yet refuse to participate.

Another person may join in and gradually regard such speech as right.

These situations are different.

Human and nonhuman devils

Al-Zukhruf 43:36 uses شَيْطَانًا.

This word is assigned here to ش ط ن. Across the Quran, a devil may be a jinn or a human, as Al-Anʿām 6:112 mentions devils among humans and jinn.

This does not mean Al-Zukhruf 43:36 identifies the precise nature of every accompanying devil.

What is clear is the companion's role in turning people away from the path and reinforcing their mistaken belief that they are guided.

Reflection can therefore consider both devilish temptation and deceptive human influence without treating either as an exhaustive substitute for the other.

This relates to Forqan's studies of the devil and ṭāghūt, as well as the sealing of the heart and the pattern of guidance and misguidance.

6. Comparison with related roots

ق ي ض and ه ي أ: providing and preparing

هَيَّأَ concerns preparation and making circumstances ready.

In Quranic usage, قَيَّضَ likewise concerns providing or appointing a companion.

The verbs are close in this semantic area.

This proximity does not make all derivatives of the two roots identical.

Qayḍ also has branches concerning eggshells and exchange.

“Preparing a companion” explains the Quranic verb rather than defining the entire root family.

ق ي ض and ق ر ن: providing and accompanying

In both verses, qayḍ occurs alongside a word from ق ر ن.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:25, قُرَنَاء is the object of قَيَّضْنَا.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:36, نُقَيِّضْ لَهُ شَيْطَانًا is followed by فَهُوَ لَهُ قَرِينٌ.

This association is important for understanding the root's Quranic meaning.

Qayḍ expresses providing or appointing.

Qarīn identifies the accompanying person or being.

Qarīn thus names the companion involved; it does not mean that ق ي ض itself denotes “companion” in every use.

ق ي ض and ج ع ل: placing and providing

جَعَلَ has broad Quranic uses, including placing, making, and bringing about a situation.

In these two verses, qayḍ has the more specific sense of providing a companion.

For example, jaʿala can describe placing something in a particular position.

قَيَّضَ لَهُ قَرِينًا concerns providing someone with a companion.

“We placed” can convey part of the idea, but “We provided” or “We appointed” better clarifies the verb's particular relationship to the companion.

ق ي ض and ز ي ن: providing companions and adorning falsehood

Fuṣṣilat 41:25 uses two distinct verbs.

قَيَّضْنَا expresses providing companions.

زَيَّنُوا expresses the companions' own conduct.

Qayḍ should therefore not be translated as deceiving or making falsehood attractive.

A companion may be provided, while the nature of that companion's influence is known through speech and conduct.

The verse separately expresses that influence through زَيَّنُوا.

ق ي ض and ق ض ي: providing and judging or settling

قَضَى belongs to ق ض ي.

Its Quranic meanings involve judging, completing, fulfilling, or decreeing.

قَيَّضَ belongs to ق ي ض and here means providing a companion.

Despite their similar letters, these roots differ.

نُقَيِّضْ in Al-Zukhruf 43:36 should therefore not be translated merely as “We decree” or “We judge.”

The appointment may be understood within divine decree, but that reflection differs from the root's independent meaning.

ق ي ض and ق ي ظ: shell and intense heat

قَيْظ, with ظ, concerns intense heat and the hot season.

قَيْض, with ض, concerns eggshells and, in other branches, exchange and provision.

Their written similarity should not transfer the sense of heat to ق ي ض.

ق ي ض and ق و ض: splitting and dismantling

ق و ض concerns dismantling or bringing down a building or tent.

The eggshell branch of ق ي ض concerns splitting the shell.

Early lexical sources sometimes place forms concerning splitting or collapsing walls close to one another.

Dismantling a tent, splitting a shell, and providing a companion nevertheless have distinct meanings.

Such similarities do not establish a definite historical derivation or an invented common meaning.

7. Questions of interpretation and translation

Does qayḍ always mean a bad companion?

No.

Both Quranic occurrences concern companions in contexts of misguidance and deviation.

The Arabic verb itself concerns providing and preparing.

The companion's harmful nature comes from description, conduct, and context, not from the root's necessary meaning.

Does نُقَيِّضْ mean The God forces people to sin?

No such meaning is contained in the verb itself.

Al-Zukhruf 43:36 first describes disregarding remembrance of the Entirely Merciful, then presents the appointment of a devil as its consequence.

The next verse describes companions turning people away from the path while those people think they are guided.

Other Quranic passages distinguish the devil's invitation and temptation from compulsion and preserve human responsibility.

Appointing a companion should not be equated with forcing acceptance of everything the companion says or does.

Does qayḍ mean replacing one friend with another?

The muqāyaḍah branch involves exchange and substitution.

The two Quranic verses, however, use the Form II verb for providing a companion.

Neither says a previous companion must have been removed and replaced by a new person.

Replacement may occur in some circumstances but is not essential to the Quranic meaning.

Is the devilish companion necessarily invisible?

Across Quranic usage, “devil” concerns a misleading role and is applied to devils among humans and jinn.

Al-Zukhruf 43:36 does not separately specify the exact kind of accompanying being.

It should therefore not be restricted without evidence to one particular human or exclusively to an invisible being.

Can every bad friend be called a devil appointed by The God?

No.

Someone may encounter an unsuitable person in unwanted circumstances and even resist their conduct.

Al-Zukhruf 43:36 describes a conditional situation beginning with turning away from remembrance of the Entirely Merciful.

It should not be used to accuse particular people or label every unsuitable association a divine punishment.

Is qayḍ the same as economic barter?

Not in the Quran.

مُقَايَضَة concerns exchanging goods for goods.

قَيَّضَ in these verses concerns providing a companion.

A shared root does not mean that The God is trading one thing for another in these verses.

Is the eggshell sense certainly the origin of all derivatives?

That has not been established.

The poetic witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates the eggshell meaning.

Early dictionaries also record exchange and providing a companion.

Determining which came first and how the others developed requires independent historical evidence.

These branches are best defined separately, with only defensible conceptual connections explained.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Fuṣṣilat 41:25, وَقَيَّضْنَا means “We provided” or “We appointed.”

Rendering of the phrase:

وَقَيَّضْنَا لَهُمْ قُرَنَاءَ

“And We provided companions for them.”

This preserves the verb's meaning and its object.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:36, نُقَيِّضْ means “We appoint” or “We provide.”

Rendering of the phrase:

نُقَيِّضْ لَهُ شَيْطَانًا

“We appoint a devil for him.”

Rendering of the full verse:

“And whoever turns away from remembrance of the Entirely Merciful, We appoint a devil for him, who becomes his companion.”

For non-Quranic uses:

قَيْض: the hard eggshell; in another branch, an equivalent or substitute.

قَاضَ الْفَرْخُ الْبَيْضَةَ: the chick broke through the shell.

تَقَيَّضَتِ الْبَيْضَةُ: the eggshell broke into fragments.

اِنْقَاضَ: cracked or split.

مَقِيض: the place of eggshells; its precise referent depends on the lexical witness.

قَايَضَ: exchanged one thing for another.

مُقَايَضَة: barter.

قَيْضَان: two counterparts or equivalents.

قَيَّضَ لَهُ شَيْئًا: provided or prepared something for someone.

قَيَّضَ لَهُ قَرِينًا: provided someone with a companion.

تَقْيِيض: providing or appointing, when applied to companionship.

تَقَيَّضَ فُلَانٌ أَبَاهُ: someone came to resemble his father.

“Splitting,” “exchanging,” “replacing,” and “appointing a companion” should not be substituted for one another when translating this family.

In Quranic usage, deceptive influence is expressed through other verbs and should not be added to qayḍ itself.

Conclusion

The Arabic root ق ي ض has several established semantic branches.

In its material branch, qayḍ denotes the outer eggshell and qāḍa its splitting. Poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates broken eggshells in the pre-Islamic poetic tradition.

In the exchange branch, qayḍ denotes an equivalent or substitute and muqāyaḍah the exchange of goods.

In the companionship branch, qayyaḍa means providing, arranging, or making someone or something available to another.

Only this last sense occurs in the Quran.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:25, The God speaks of providing companions who made the group's conduct and affairs appear attractive to them.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:36, providing a devil as companion follows a person's disregard of remembrance of the Entirely Merciful.

The next verse identifies the companions' role: they turn people away from the path while those people believe they are guided.

Qayḍ therefore does not mean deceiving or compelling. Providing the companion is the divine action stated; obstructing the path and adorning falsehood are the companions' actions.

The condition in Al-Zukhruf 43:36 also prevents this companionship from being construed as initial, causeless divine compulsion without regard to prior human conduct.

Conversely, not every hardship or unsuitable companion should be called divine punishment. People do not choose many circumstances or those around them; responsibility must be considered in light of knowledge, ability, and actual choices.

The Quranic core of ق ي ض is providing or preparing a companion or cause for someone, bringing that companion into the person's course of life or conduct.

Both Quranic occurrences involve misleading companionship, but the companions' harmful nature comes from their conduct and context rather than the root itself.

“Providing” and “appointing” are therefore suitable equivalents for the Quranic uses of ق ي ض, while providing a companion is distinct from compelling someone to follow that companion.
