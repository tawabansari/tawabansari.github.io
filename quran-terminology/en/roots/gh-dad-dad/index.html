Reducing, lowering, and restraining: from gaze and voice to freshness and tenderness

The root غ ض ض has two established semantic branches in early Arabic: reducing, lowering, restraining, or limiting something; and the freshness, tenderness, and vitality of something that has not lost its freshness.

In the first branch, غَضَّ is used when someone lowers their gaze or voice, reduces a quantity, or restrains an action. These uses do not invariably mean eliminating something altogether; they concern reducing, limiting, or checking its continuation in a particular state.

For example, غَضَّ بَصَرَهُ means to lower or restrain one's gaze, while غَضَّ مِنْ صَوْتِهِ means to lower one's voice. غَضَّ مِنْ حَقِّهِ can also mean to diminish someone's due.

In the second branch, غَضّ describes something fresh, soft, and tender, such as newly grown vegetation, a pliant branch, or a young person who retains youthful vitality.

These branches should not be forced into a single image. One may imagine an association between reducing the intensity of gaze or voice and the tenderness of vegetation, but that resemblance alone does not establish that one meaning developed from the other.

The root occurs four times in the Quran, always as a Form I verb: twice concerning the gaze, in Al-Nūr 24:30 and Al-Nūr 24:31, and twice concerning the voice, in Luqmān 31:19 and Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3.

All Quranic occurrences concern reduction and lowering. Although the branch involving freshness and tenderness is recorded in Arabic, no separate derivative with that sense occurs in the Quran.

1. The root in early Arabic

In early Arabic, غَضَّ is used for lowering, reducing, and restraining something.

غَضَّ الطَّرْفَ means that someone lowered or restrained their gaze.

غَضَّ الصَّوْتَ means that someone lowered their voice.

غَضَّ مِنْ قَدْرِهِ means that someone diminished another person's standing or reputation.

These uses show that reduction can concern something perceptible, such as sound, or something intangible, such as a person's standing and dignity.

The root itself does not determine whether this reduction is right or wrong. Lowering one's voice out of respect can be commendable, whereas diminishing another person's rights is unjust.

A poetic witness attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād

The collected poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet and warrior ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād includes these lines:

وَأَغُضُّ طَرْفِي مَا بَدَتْ لِي جَارَتِي
حَتَّى يُوَارِيَ جَارَتِي مَأْوَاهَا

wa-aghuḍḍu ṭarfī mā badat lī jāratī / ḥattā yuwāriya jāratī maʾwāhā

إِنِّي امْرُؤٌ سَمْحُ الْخَلِيقَةِ مَاجِدٌ
لَا أَتْبَعُ النَّفْسَ اللَّجُوجَ هَوَاهَا

innī imruʾun samḥu al-khalīqati mājidun / lā atbaʿu al-nafsa al-lajūja hawāhā

Translation: “I lower my gaze whenever my woman neighbour appears before me, until her dwelling conceals her from sight. I am a generous-natured, honourable man; I do not follow the desires of my obstinate self.”

Here, أَغُضُّ طَرْفِي means controlling and lowering the gaze.

The poet does not say that he closes his eyes forever or ceases to see anything. He describes restraint in a particular situation: when his woman neighbour comes into view.

The second line connects this behaviour with restraining personal desire.

Lowering the gaze here is therefore a deliberate act undertaken to respect another person's dignity.

This witness illustrates the literary Arabic use of غض طرف for controlling the gaze and refraining from inappropriate looking. Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not, by itself, establish its date relative to later legal discussions.

Nevertheless, attribution to ʿAntarah, a pre-Islamic poet, must be distinguished from proof of when these particular lines were composed. They survive in later collections and literary sources; their independent date of composition is uncertain.

The witness is therefore relevant to usage attributed to pre-Quranic Arabic, but alone does not prove that the surviving wording was composed exactly as transmitted in the pre-Islamic period.

A witness from Jarīr: lowering the gaze as humiliation

During the Umayyad period, Jarīr said in a satire against the tribe of Numayr:

فَغُضَّ الطَّرْفَ إِنَّكَ مِنْ نُمَيْرٍ
فَلَا كَعْبًا بَلَغْتَ وَلَا كِلَابَا

fa-ghuḍḍa al-ṭarfa innaka min numayrin / fa-lā kaʿban balaghta wa-lā kilābā

Translation: “Lower your gaze, for you are of Numayr; you have attained neither the standing of Kaʿb nor that of Kilāb.”

Here too, غُضَّ الطَّرْفَ means lowering the gaze.

Yet the speaker's purpose differs from that of the poem attributed to ʿAntarah.

In the first poem, lowering the gaze expresses self-restraint and respect for a neighbour. In this line, the poet contemptuously tells his addressee to look down, as someone supposedly unworthy of holding his head high.

A similar bodily action can thus acquire different ethical and social implications in different situations.

This line belongs to the Umayyad period, after the Quran's revelation, and cannot establish pre-Quranic meaning. Its value lies in showing the continued and varied use of غض طرف in Arabic.

The comparison shows that غض does not inherently mean modesty or chastity, nor humiliation or contempt. These implications arise from context and the speaker's purpose.

Reducing a quantity or someone's due

Arabic records غَضَضْتُ السِّقَاءَ for reducing the contents of a waterskin or container.

غَضَّ مِنْ حَقِّهِ likewise means diminishing or withholding part of someone's due.

In لَا أَغُضُّكَ دِرْهَمًا, the speaker declares that they will not deduct even one dirham from what belongs or is owed to the addressee.

These uses show that the root's scope is not confined to the eyes or voice. Reducing a quantity or diminishing something is a broader meaning applicable to different subjects.

This breadth does not mean every derivative necessarily denotes a numerical, measurable reduction. Lowering the gaze and restraining conduct are not always measured by a specific quantity.

2. Semantic core and root forms

غ ض ض is a geminate root: its second and third consonants are identical.

In غَضَّ, the two root consonants ض appear in the usual contracted form as one doubled consonant marked by a shaddah.

The basic verb paradigm is:

غَضَّ يَغُضُّ غَضًّا

It means to lower, reduce, restrain, or check.

The main forms and uses in this family include:

غَضَّ: reduced something, restrained it, or lowered it.

يَغُضُّ: reduces, lowers, or restrains.

غُضَّ: a command to lower or reduce, in the contracted imperative form.

اُغْضُضْ: another imperative form, with both root consonants ض separately visible.

غَضّ: reduction, lowering, or restraint; in its corresponding adjectival use, fresh and tender.

غَاضّ: someone who lowers or restrains their gaze, voice, or something else.

مَغْضُوض: something lowered, limited, or reduced.

غَضِيض: lowered or downcast when used of the gaze; fresh and tender in the other branch.

غَضَاضَة: deficiency, humiliation, or diminished standing in some uses; freshness and tenderness in another recorded use, although some lexicographers disputed that latter use.

غُضُوضَة: softness, tenderness, and freshness.

غَضَّة: the feminine adjective for something fresh and tender.

Lexicons also record the reduplicated form غَضْغَضَ concerning reduction or diminution.

Its morphological structure differs from the triliteral غَضَّ, and it should not be presented without explanation as one of the root's Quranic forms.

Two branches that must remain distinct

In the first branch, غض concerns reduction, restraint, and lowering.

In the second, غَضّ denotes something fresh and tender.

In Arabic, نَبْتٌ غَضٌّ describes fresh, soft vegetation. شَبَابٌ غَضٌّ is likewise used for fresh and vigorous youth.

In some uses, غَضِيض also denotes a blossom or newly emerging part of a date palm.

These uses emphasise freshness and tenderness, rather than reduction in quantity or lowering the gaze.

Early sources sometimes record the two branches together, and some lexicographers recognise them as two independent semantic foundations within one family.

This distinction is more precise than claiming that fresh vegetation must mean a reduction in hardness and that all meanings therefore developed from one image.

Such an explanation is conceivable, but it requires independent evidence before it can become a historical claim.

Only the first branch occurs in the Quran.

3. Lowering the gaze in Al-Nūr

Al-Nūr 24:30

قُلْ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ يَغُضُّوا مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِمْ وَيَحْفَظُوا فُرُوجَهُمْ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ أَزْكَىٰ لَهُمْ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا يَصْنَعُونَ

qul li-l-muʾminīna yaghuḍḍū min abṣārihim wa-yaḥfaẓū furūjahum dhālika azkā lahum inna allāha khabīrun bi-mā yaṣnaʿūn

Al-Nūr 24:30

Translation: “Tell the believing men to restrain their gaze and guard their private parts; that is purer for them. Indeed, The God is aware of what they do.”

Here, يَغُضُّوا is a third-person masculine plural imperfect verb from غ ض ض.

Following قُلْ, it functions as a response to a command and carries directive force. Grammatically it is jussive, with the final nūn omitted according to the pattern of the five inflected verb forms.

أَبْصَارِهِمْ belongs to ب ص ر and refers to their sight or eyes.

The action therefore concerns human looking.

The meaning of يَغُضُّوا مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِمْ

In this expression, غض means reducing or restraining the gaze.

Looking is an activity over which people have control in many situations. They can attend to something, continue looking, stare at a subject, or turn their gaze away.

Within this semantic field, غض بصر concerns limiting the gaze and refraining from inappropriate looking.

It should not be equated with losing one's sight or permanently closing one's eyes.

Nor does the verb غض itself entail keeping one's head lowered in every situation or never looking at anyone while speaking.

In Arabic usage, restraining the gaze can involve lowering the eyes, limiting attention, or turning away from a particular object of sight.

The role of مِنْ

The preposition مِنْ is significant in مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِمْ.

Early grammatical analyses offer several explanations of its role.

On one reading, مِنْ is partitive: it concerns limiting some looking or refraining from certain looks, rather than abandoning all seeing.

On another reading, مِنْ is connected with the construction of غض itself and completes the sense of reducing one's gaze.

Other discussions consider it grammatically supplementary or assign it other syntactic roles.

This disagreement shows why the presence of مِنْ alone cannot determine a precise numerical amount of permitted or prohibited looking.

Nevertheless, the sentence's clear core in the principal readings is control and restriction of the gaze.

The distinction between ordinary and inappropriate looking also relates to the context, since the command to restrain the gaze accompanies the safeguarding of private parts.

The verse therefore concerns more than the eye's physical activity: it also addresses how people use their gaze in relationships and conduct involving chastity.

Is every look at another person prohibited?

The lexical meaning of غض does not establish such a comprehensive ruling.

People need sight and looking to understand their surroundings, converse, work, learn, and participate in society.

In ordinary human relationships, looking can also be necessary for following another person's speech, assessing a situation, or carrying out a responsibility.

There is nevertheless a difference between looking for ordinary understanding and interaction, and staring that intrudes on another person's privacy or carries an intention to violate it.

Someone may look at another person's face during conversation to understand their words better. By contrast, someone may stare persistently at another person's body in a way that disregards their dignity and privacy.

Seeing occurs in both situations, but the use made of the gaze differs.

The Quranic command to restrain the gaze relates to this human responsibility.

At the same time, all legal and social details concerning looking cannot be determined from غ ض ض alone. They require study of the full context of Al-Nūr and other relevant verses.

The connection with guarding private parts

In Al-Nūr 24:30, يَغُضُّوا مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِمْ accompanies وَيَحْفَظُوا فُرُوجَهُمْ.

غض concerns control of the gaze, whereas حفظ concerns keeping and safeguarding something.

These verbs are not identical.

By giving two separate instructions, the Quran distinguishes visual behaviour from responsibility for guarding private parts.

Their ethical relationship can be understood from context, but the whole meaning of غض should not be reduced to guarding private parts, nor the whole meaning of حفظ to restraining the gaze.

The verse ends with ذَٰلِكَ أَزْكَىٰ لَهُمْ. This conduct is thus placed within the believers' purification and ethical growth.

The God is also described as aware of what they do.

Controlling the gaze is therefore not merely an outward movement; intention and actual conduct also matter to ethical responsibility.

4. Women's gaze and shared responsibility

Al-Nūr 24:31 gives the same instruction to believing women.

The Arabic verb form is:

يَغْضُضْنَ

It belongs to the same root, غ ض ض.

Its difference from يَغُضُّوا concerns morphology and the subject.

يَغُضُّوا is masculine plural, while يَغْضُضْنَ is feminine plural.

In the feminine form, both root consonants ض are visible, and the final nūn is the feminine plural subject pronoun. The verb is fixed on sukūn because of that pronoun and occupies a jussive position as the response to the command.

The forms' visible difference therefore does not entail a difference in their semantic core.

Both concern lowering and controlling the gaze.

Different addressees do not create different lexical meanings

Al-Nūr 24:30 addresses believing men.

Al-Nūr 24:31 addresses believing women.

These addresses show that responsibility for controlling the gaze is not assigned to one sex alone.

The Quran addresses men first and then directs women to the same principle.

One therefore cannot claim that women alone are responsible for their clothing while men bear no responsibility for how they look.

Nor can these verses establish that only a man's gaze at a woman can be inappropriate, while a woman's gaze at a man lies outside ethical responsibility.

Both groups are instructed to control their gaze.

Verse 31 also discusses adornment and clothing. These are distinct subjects to be examined through that verse's own vocabulary and constructions.

A study of غ ض ض should not substitute rules about clothing, displaying adornment, or bodily privacy for the verb يغضضن's lexical meaning.

The verb still concerns reducing and restraining the gaze.

Does restraining the gaze mean ignoring another person's presence?

No such conclusion follows from the root itself.

A person can notice someone, speak with them, and participate in ordinary social relationships while restraining intrusive or inappropriate looking.

Respecting another person's dignity does not, in itself, imply that looking to identify danger, provide help, teach, or carry out a legitimate responsibility is forbidden.

For example, a physician may need to observe part of a patient's body to assess their condition. That purpose differs from prying or intrusive looking.

This example illustrates differences in situation and purpose; it does not derive a complete medical or legal ruling from غض itself.

Both verses in Al-Nūr address responsibility in using one's gaze. The conduct's ethical significance is understood through context, purpose, and relevant limits.

5. Lowering the voice in Luqmān

Luqmān 31:19

وَاقْصِدْ فِي مَشْيِكَ وَاغْضُضْ مِنْ صَوْتِكَ ۚ إِنَّ أَنْكَرَ الْأَصْوَاتِ لَصَوْتُ الْحَمِيرِ

wa-qṣid fī mashyika wa-ghḍuḍ min ṣawtika inna ankara al-aṣwāti la-ṣawtu al-ḥamīr

Luqmān 31:19

Translation: “Be moderate in your walking and lower your voice; indeed, the most disagreeable of voices is the voice of donkeys.”

Here, وَاغْضُضْ is an imperative from غ ض ض.

It addresses one person and forms part of Luqmān's advice to his son.

The initial وَ connects this instruction with the preceding advice about walking.

اغْضُضْ means to lower or reduce.

مِنْ صَوْتِكَ identifies what is to be reduced: the person's voice.

The meaning of وَاغْضُضْ مِنْ صَوْتِكَ

The subject here is no longer the gaze, but the voice.

A person can raise or lower their voice and, in many situations, control its intensity.

The command اغضض here concerns lowering the voice and refraining from raising it needlessly.

It does not mean complete silence.

Silence could be expressed with words and constructions directly concerning remaining quiet or not speaking.

Here, the instruction concerns vocal intensity and manner of expression.

The connection with moderation in conduct

The verse begins with وَاقْصِدْ فِي مَشْيِكَ.

In this context, اقصد concerns moderation and balance in walking.

The instruction to lower the voice follows.

Together these recommendations address how a person behaves and presents themselves among others.

Luqmān 31:18 also forbids turning away arrogantly from people and walking with conceit and self-display.

The advice about the voice therefore continues the discussion of humility and avoiding self-display.

Nevertheless, the lexical meaning of غض should not be replaced with humility.

Humility is the ethical implication understood from the advice's context; the verb itself means reducing or lowering the voice.

Why mention the voice of donkeys?

The verse's ending describes the voice of donkeys as the most disagreeable of voices.

The comparison concerns a sound whose loudness and quality can be harsh and unpleasant.

The advice directs attention to how people use their own voices.

This example does not entail that the animal bears ethical responsibility or that its natural sound is a sin.

A donkey produces sounds according to its nature and is not, like a human being, addressed by an ethical instruction regulating speech.

People, by contrast, can regulate their voices in many situations and avoid needlessly distressing others.

Nor does the verse itself establish a prohibition of every loud sound.

Someone may need to raise their voice to warn of danger, request help, or reach a large audience.

That differs from raising one's voice to humiliate others, show off, or cause distress.

The verse should therefore be understood within considered conduct and appropriate use of the voice, rather than as a rule requiring people to speak very softly in every situation.

The role of مِنْ صَوْتِكَ

Just as Al-Nūr 24:30 uses مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِمْ, this verse uses مِنْ صَوْتِكَ.

In both constructions, مِنْ accompanies the verb of reducing and lowering.

The similarity shows that غض can be used with مِنْ to express reducing something.

The preposition's presence does not mean the Quran specifies a particular volume or a fixed duration of looking.

The appropriate measure of voice and gaze is understood through situation, purpose, and other relevant indications.

6. Lowering the voice before the Messenger

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَغُضُّونَ أَصْوَاتَهُمْ عِنْدَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ امْتَحَنَ اللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ ۚ لَهُمْ مَغْفِرَةٌ وَأَجْرٌ عَظِيمٌ

inna alladhīna yaghuḍḍūna aṣwātahum ʿinda rasūli allāhi ulāʾika alladhīna imtaḥana allāhu qulūbahum li-l-taqwā lahum maghfiratun wa-ajrun ʿaẓīm

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3

Translation: “Indeed, those who lower their voices before the Messenger of The God are those whose hearts The God has tested for mindfulness; theirs is forgiveness and a great reward.”

Here, يَغُضُّونَ belongs to the same root, غ ض ض.

It is a third-person masculine plural imperfect in the indicative, retaining its final nūn. In Al-Nūr 24:30, يَغُضُّوا is jussive with nūn omitted; in Al-Nūr 24:31, يَغْضُضْنَ is fixed on sukūn and occupies a jussive position.

أَصْوَاتَهُمْ is its direct object.

The expression مِنْ أَصْوَاتِهِمْ is not used here.

The construction is:

«يَغُضُّونَ أَصْوَاتَهُمْ»

It means that they lower their voices.

This difference shows that غض can take either a direct object or a construction with مِنْ.

Both constructions retain the meaning of reducing and lowering.

The verse's context

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2, believers are instructed not to raise their voices above the Prophet's voice or address him as loudly as they commonly address one another.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3 then commends those who lower their voices before the Messenger of The God.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:4 and Al-Ḥujurāt 49:5 discuss those who call to Muhammad from behind the chambers and remind them that waiting patiently for him to come out would have been better for them.

These verses therefore concern conduct in Muhammad's presence and in conversation with him.

Those present must respect the conversation and the standing of his mission as Messenger.

Does غض صوت mean unquestioning obedience?

No such conclusion follows from the root itself.

Here, غض concerns lowering the voice, rather than abandoning reason, questions, or human judgement.

A person may speak softly yet say something false. Someone else may state a truth loudly; loudness alone does not make that statement false.

In this context, lowering the voice is part of respectful conduct before the Messenger and is connected with mindfulness.

That relationship should not become a rule that every religious or social authority is immune to questions or criticism by virtue of their position.

The verse concerns the Messenger of The God and the conduct of those in his presence.

Extending the principle to respectful conversation with others may be ethically useful, but the standing of the divine mission cannot be transferred to every other authority without independent textual support.

Nor should this verse be taken to require every person to speak very softly in every situation.

The clear principle in this use is control of the voice and respect for conversation in a particular setting.

7. Comparison with neighbouring roots

غ ض ض (غَضَّ) and خ ف ض (خَفَضَ)

خفض concerns lowering and bringing something down.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:24 uses وَاخْفِضْ لَهُمَا جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ concerning humility toward one's parents.

غض can likewise describe lowering the gaze or voice.

The roots overlap in some uses but are not complete synonyms.

The Quranic uses of غ ض ض foreground restraining and reducing gaze or voice.

خفض, by contrast, has a broader range of lowering and bringing down, including position, condition, or conduct.

غض صوت can therefore be translated as “lowering the voice,” but not every use of خفض means reducing voice or gaze.

غ ض ض (غَضَّ) and ك ف ف (كَفَّ)

كف concerns restraining and preventing the continuation of an action.

For example, someone can restrain their hand from harming another person.

Some uses of غض likewise concern restraining the gaze or controlling a particular action.

غض foregrounds reduction and lowering, whereas كف can indicate preventing an action altogether.

The distinction is not absolute. Depending on context, lowering the gaze can also mean completely refraining from looking at a particular object.

Nevertheless, the meaning of كف does not establish that غض always means shutting down sight or voice completely.

غ ض ض (غَضَّ) and غ م ض (غَمَضَ)

غمض in Arabic concerns closing the eyes or eyelids.

It is close to غض in some physical uses, but their meanings are not identical.

Someone may close their eyes without necessarily respecting another person's dignity or restraining an inappropriate gaze.

Conversely, someone can turn their gaze away from something without fully closing their eyes.

The Quran uses غ م ض in Al-Baqarah 2:267, in تُغْمِضُوا فِيهِ.

There, the subject is overlooking deficiencies or making concessions in accepting something undesirable, rather than a command to control one's gaze.

The resemblance between closing the eyes and lowering the gaze should therefore not lead to conflating the roots.

غ ض ض (غَضَّ) and ب ص ر (بَصَر)

بصر concerns seeing and sight.

In Al-Nūr 24:30 and Al-Nūr 24:31, أبصار identifies what the action of غض concerns.

بصر expresses the capacity or act of seeing, while غض expresses how it is limited or restrained.

The concepts must remain distinct.

Seeing something and deciding whether to continue looking are not necessarily a single action.

Something may enter a person's field of vision unintentionally. They can then decide whether to continue looking or turn away.

The meaning of غض relates to this possibility of control and limitation.

This distinction should not be used to extract an absolute rule about all first or subsequent looks. Such rulings require broader examination of context and responsibility for conduct.

غ ض ض (غَضَّ) and غ ض ب (غَضَب)

غ ض ب is a separate root concerning anger.

Although its first two consonants match غ ض ض, the families have different meanings.

In Quranic usage, غض concerns reducing and restraining gaze or voice, while غضب concerns anger.

Someone may raise their voice in anger and then lower it; lowering the voice does not, by itself, mean that the anger has ended.

Nor does lowering the gaze necessarily mean controlling anger.

The roots' visual resemblance must not lead to mistaken definitions.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Across the four Quranic occurrences, suitable equivalents for غ ض ض include “lower,” “reduce,” and “restrain.”

The precise choice depends on what the verb concerns.

Al-Nūr 24:30

«يَغُضُّوا مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِمْ»

Suggested translation:

“That they reduce their looking.”

Or:

“That they restrain their gaze.”

The first Persian rendering, meaning “reduce their looking,” makes the sense of reduction more explicit; the alternative highlights restraint.

The second rendering is also natural and emphasises controlling and checking the gaze.

“That they close their eyes” is not precise, since fully closing the eyes is not a necessary component of the verb's meaning.

Al-Nūr 24:31

«يَغْضُضْنَ مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِنَّ»

Suggested translation:

“That they reduce their looking.”

Or:

“That they restrain their gaze.”

The feminine verb's difference from the preceding masculine form should not be treated as two different lexical instructions.

Luqmān 31:19

«وَاغْضُضْ مِنْ صَوْتِكَ»

Suggested translation:

“And reduce the volume of your voice.”

Or:

“And lower your voice.”

Both equivalents are valid.

“Be silent” is imprecise, since the verse concerns reducing the voice rather than giving up speech altogether.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3

«يَغُضُّونَ أَصْوَاتَهُمْ»

Suggested translation:

“They lower their voices.”

This translation matches the direct-object construction.

Here too, neither complete silence nor a prohibition of every loud sound needs to be added to the root's meaning.

A brief root meaning for Forqan word boxes

For Quranic uses, this brief definition is suitable:

“Reducing, lowering, or restraining something, such as the gaze or voice.”

The contextual meaning in each verse should be written separately.

In Al-Nūr 24:30: “Restraining and lowering the gaze of believing men.”

In Al-Nūr 24:31: “Restraining and lowering the gaze of believing women.”

In Luqmān 31:19: “Reducing vocal loudness and avoiding an unpleasantly loud manner of speaking.”

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3: “Lowering the voice in the presence of the Messenger of The God.”

In non-Quranic uses of the second branch, غَضّ requires equivalents such as “fresh,” “soft,” “tender,” or “vibrant.”

For example, نَبْتٌ غَضٌّ means fresh, soft vegetation, rather than vegetation whose quantity has been reduced.

This distinction shows why each derivative's meaning must be understood through its form and actual usage.

9. Conclusion

The root غ ض ض has two semantic branches in early Arabic.

The first concerns reducing, lowering, and restraining something.

The second concerns freshness, tenderness, and vitality.

Arabic poetic witnesses show غض طرف used for controlling the gaze in different situations. Sometimes it expresses self-restraint and respect for another person; elsewhere, contemptuous speech uses it to make someone lower their head.

The action's ethical significance therefore comes from purpose and context, rather than from the root alone.

The Quran uses غ ض ض four times, always in the branch involving reduction and restraint.

In Al-Nūr 24:30 and Al-Nūr 24:31, believing men and women are instructed to restrain their gaze.

In Luqmān 31:19, lowering the voice forms part of advice about considered conduct and avoiding self-display.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3, those who lower their voices before the Messenger are commended.

These uses show that controlling gaze and voice can, in different situations, relate to chastity, respect for others, moderation, and courtesy.

These ethical values should not replace the root's lexical meaning.

غض does not itself mean chastity, humility, obedience, or silence. Such ideas are implications or features understood through context and the kind of conduct involved.

The branch involving freshness and tenderness must also remain distinct: without historical evidence, all uses involving fresh vegetation and vigorous youth cannot be derived from reduction and restraint.

The final root definition is:

غ ض ض:

1. Reducing the quantity or intensity of something, lowering or restraining it: for example, lowering the voice, controlling the gaze, or diminishing someone's due or a quantity.

2. The freshness, tenderness, and vitality of something retaining its softness and freshness, such as new vegetation or vigorous youth.

The Quranic occurrences use the first meaning.

The four verses share the point that people can observe measure and limits in exercising their abilities. Seeing and speaking are natural human capacities, but their use can affect others' dignity, the quality of relationships, and ethical responsibility.

These verses do not reject sight or speech itself. They call people to control and regulate their conduct in situations where inappropriate looking or speaking can conflict with chastity, respect, and considered behaviour.
