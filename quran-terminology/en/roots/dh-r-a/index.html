Bringing into being, creation and the multiplication of creatures; sowing seed; whiteness and changes in color

The Arabic root DH R ʾ has several recognized branches of meaning. Its principal branch concerns bringing into being and creation, especially where the emergence, multiplication and spread of living creatures are involved. The family also relates to sowing seed and growth. Another branch concerns white hair, the onset of graying, and certain color markings in animals.

The root occurs six times in the Qur’an, exclusively as Form I verbs. The objects differ: crops and livestock, diverse earthly creatures, humans and jinn, and the multiplication of generations in connection with pairs.

These differences show that dharaʾa should not be restricted to a single instance of creation, such as human birth or plant growth. Nor should it simply be identified with dharā from DH R W, since the Qur’an uses forms from that other root for scattering by wind.

Three matters should remain distinct: the verb’s Arabic meaning, its object in each verse, and the theological or ethical conclusions drawn from the verse as a whole. For example, dharaʾnā in Al-Aʿrāf 7:179 concerns bringing into being. The expression li-jahannama and the descriptions of the group raise further questions arising from the sentence and its context, rather than from the root itself.

The Qur’anic forms are dharaʾa in Al-Anʿām 6:136 and Al-Naḥl 16:13; dharaʾnā in Al-Aʿrāf 7:179; dharaʾakum in Al-Muʾminūn 23:79 and Al-Mulk 67:24; and yadhraʾukum in Al-Shūrā 42:11.

1. Semantic branches and usage in early Arabic

Creation and sowing seed

Two related uses of dharaʾa are recorded in early Arabic vocabulary:

Dharaʾa allāhu al-khalqa: The God brought the creatures into being.

Dharaʾnā al-arḍa: we sowed seed in the ground or cultivated it.

The first concerns bringing creatures into being; the second concerns sowing seed in the ground and providing for growth.

Two semantic branches are recognized for DH R ʾ: whiteness, and sowing or scattering seed. Dharaʾa allāhu al-khalqa is also considered in analyzing the connection between creation and the second branch.

This analysis identifies an intelligible connection between sowing seed and the emergence of creatures. Just as sowing can lead to growth and multiplication in plants, creation can involve the emergence and multiplication of generations.

The resemblance alone does not prove that the meaning of creation historically developed directly from agriculture. That conclusion would require independent historical evidence.

Dharaʾnā al-arḍa is recorded for sowing seed in the ground, and zarʿun dharīʾun for a newly sown crop.

Dharaʾa allāhu al-khalqa is also explained by khalaqahum: He created them.

Some early explanations connect dharaʾa with increasing the number of creatures. This aspect becomes clearer in verses concerning pairs and generations.

Accordingly, bringing into being and creation provide the reliable central meaning in Qur’anic usage, while multiplication and increase become more prominent in certain contexts.

Dharʾ and the manifestation of creatures

One lexical explanation describes dharʾ as The God making manifest what He has brought into being.

A comparison of dharʾ and khalq can likewise emphasize a creature’s becoming manifest: something that did not previously exist appears through creation.

This explanation can illuminate part of the semantic field. It should not become an absolute rule that khalq means only making something secretly, while dharaʾa means only its becoming visible to others.

The Qur’an uses dharaʾa for bringing various creatures into being, but not every verse includes a qualification concerning their visibility to humans.

Making manifest can therefore be a lexical explanation of this family, but it is neither a general nor a sufficient rendering for all its Qur’anic uses.

Dhurʾah: white hair and color markings

Another branch of DH R ʾ concerns whiteness.

Dhurʾah denotes white hair or a mixture of black and white hair. Some explanations emphasize the onset of graying or white hair at the front of the head.

Dhariʾa shaʿruhu means that his hair turned white.

Rajulun adhraʾu describes a man whose hair has begun to turn white; imraʾatun dharʾāʾu describes a woman with that quality.

Jadyun adhraʾu and ʿanāqun dharʾāʾu are also recorded for young goats with whiteness or distinctive color markings on the head or ears.

These uses describe coloring, rather than creation or multiplication.

Milḥun dharʾāniyyun is also recorded for intensely white salt.

These examples show that DH R ʾ has more than one semantic branch. One can mentally connect something’s appearing with the appearance of whiteness, but that resemblance alone does not establish a common historical origin for every meaning.

A poetic witness concerning white hair

In a rajaz poem, Abū Nukhaylah al-Saʿdī describes signs of his aging:

وَقَدْ عَلَتْنِي ذُرْأَةٌ بَادِي بَدِي
وَرَثْيَةٌ تَنْهَضُ فِي تَشَدُّدِي

wa-qad ʿalatnī dhurʾatun bādī badī / wa-rathyatun tanhaḍu fī tashaddudī

Translation: White hair has appeared upon me from the outset, and my knees and joints have grown so weak that I rise only with effort and difficulty.

Dhurʾah denotes white hair here, while rathyah concerns weakness or pain in the joints. The poet describes changes in hair color and declining physical strength with age.

This poem is transmitted in early lexical material. Some words have variant readings, and the expression bādī badī has been explained in different ways.

Abū Nukhaylah was an Islamic-era poet. This poem should therefore not be presented as securely pre-Islamic evidence. Its value lies in documenting dhurʾah in early Arabic verse.

A poetic passage containing thumma dharaʾti fīhi hawāki is also transmitted for the creation and sowing branch. Its wording has an important variant, however, and dharayti without hamzah has been judged preferable. The passage therefore cannot securely establish the use of dharaʾa with hamzah.

The poetic witnesses discussed above do not provide securely pre-Islamic attribution for creation and multiplication. This limitation does not mean that no other witness exists. The lexical uses and the Qur’an’s own six occurrences provide a clearer basis for the present analysis.

Subsidiary uses

Adhraʾa fulānan bi-al-shayʾi is also recorded for stirring someone’s interest in, or making someone attached to, something.

Dharʾun min khabarin denotes a small amount or part of a report.

These uses exist in Arabic but are not central to understanding the six Qur’anic occurrences. They should not be forced into a single definition of creation or white hair.

2. Creating plants, livestock and diverse earthly creatures

Al-Anʿām 6:136: what The God has brought into being

Al-Anʿām 6:136 states:

وَجَعَلُوا لِلَّهِ مِمَّا ذَرَأَ مِنَ الْحَرْثِ وَالْأَنْعَامِ نَصِيبًا فَقَالُوا هَٰذَا لِلَّهِ بِزَعْمِهِمْ وَهَٰذَا لِشُرَكَائِنَا فَمَا كَانَ لِشُرَكَائِهِمْ فَلَا يَصِلُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَمَا كَانَ لِلَّهِ فَهُوَ يَصِلُ إِلَىٰ شُرَكَائِهِمْ سَاءَ مَا يَحْكُمُونَ

wa-jaʿalū li-allāhi mimmā dharaʾa mina al-ḥarthi wa-al-anʿāmi naṣīban fa-qālū hādhā li-allāhi bi-zaʿmihim wa-hādhā li-shurakāʾinā fa-mā kāna li-shurakāʾihim fa-lā yaṣilu ilā allāhi wa-mā kāna li-allāhi fa-huwa yaṣilu ilā shurakāʾihim sāʾa mā yaḥkumūn

Translation: They assigned to The God a share of the crops and livestock He had brought into being, saying according to their own claim, “This is for The God, and this is for our partners.” What belongs to their partners does not reach The God, while what belongs to The God reaches their partners. How badly they judge.

Dharaʾa concerns crops and livestock in this verse.

Al-ḥarth concerns cultivation and produce; al-anʿām concerns the particular livestock people used for food and other needs.

This fits bringing living creatures into being and their increase. Plants grow and livestock produce offspring. It does not follow that dharaʾa denotes only biological reproduction: the verse concerns the whole of what The God has brought into being.

The context’s main concern is the conduct of people who allocate shares from these crops and livestock to The God and their objects of worship. The Qur’an condemns their division and judgment.

The condemnation therefore concerns the assignment of shares and the establishment of partners for The God, rather than the creation of plants and livestock itself.

Mimmā dharaʾa shows that what they divide belongs to The God’s creation. Dharaʾa itself does not mean dividing, sacrificing or assigning shares. Those ideas arise from jaʿalū, naṣīban and the continuation of the sentence.

Al-Naḥl 16:13: the variety of earthly creatures

Al-Naḥl 16:13 states:

وَمَا ذَرَأَ لَكُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ مُخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهُ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِقَوْمٍ يَذَّكَّرُونَ

wa-mā dharaʾa lakum fī al-arḍi mukhtalifan alwānuhu inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yadhdhakkarūn

Translation: And what He has created for you on earth, diverse in its colors—surely in this is a sign for people who take heed.

Dharaʾa concerns what has been brought into being on earth.

Mukhtalifan alwānuhu describes the varied colors of these creatures. The phrase expresses visible variety. Alwān should not be omitted from a direct translation and replaced solely with “kinds” or “biological species.”

Al-Naḥl 16:10 mentions rain, Al-Naḥl 16:11 the growth of crops and trees, and Al-Naḥl 16:12 night, day, the sun, moon and stars. In this context, Al-Naḥl 16:13 directs attention to the variety of what has been brought into being on earth.

Here dharaʾa concerns creation and bringing into being; color diversity comes from the separate phrase mukhtalifan alwānuhu.

Variety of color should therefore not be made an invariant part of the root’s meaning. Elsewhere the same verb concerns humans and jinn without any description of color.

These two verses show that dharaʾa is used for creating diverse things, from crops and livestock to other earthly creatures.

3. Bringing humans into being and their spread on earth

Al-Muʾminūn 23:79: from creation to gathering

Al-Muʾminūn 23:79 states:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي ذَرَأَكُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَإِلَيْهِ تُحْشَرُونَ

wa-huwa alladhī dharaʾakum fī al-arḍi wa-ilayhi tuḥsharūn

Translation: He is the One who brought you into being on earth, and to Him you will be gathered.

Dharaʾakum is a past-tense verb with the object pronoun kum.

Humans themselves are the object of creation, rather than only the food, livestock or plants created for them.

Fī al-arḍi specifies the place of this emergence and life.

Some lexical and contextual explanations understand the phrase as creating and multiplying humans across the earth’s regions. It can accordingly convey the emergence and spread of human generations on earth.

Dharaʾa alone, however, does not name a particular process such as birth, migration, departure or distributing people among different places. Those features arise from the verb’s object, the location phrase and related verses on human origins.

The preceding verse, Al-Muʾminūn 23:78, mentions bringing hearing, sight and hearts into being. The following verse, Al-Muʾminūn 23:80, recalls giving life, causing death, and the alternation of night and day.

Dharaʾakum fī al-arḍi thus belongs to a sequence concerning human creation, life, death and resurrection.

The verse concludes, wa-ilayhi tuḥsharūn.

This speaks of gathering humans to The God. The verse thus connects the beginning of human life on earth with its eventual gathering.

Gathering is not part of dharaʾa itself. The two verbs belong to different roots and describe different events.

Study of Ḥ SH R

Al-Mulk 67:24: the same expression in a similar context

Al-Mulk 67:24 states:

قُلْ هُوَ الَّذِي ذَرَأَكُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَإِلَيْهِ تُحْشَرُونَ

qul huwa alladhī dharaʾakum fī al-arḍi wa-ilayhi tuḥsharūn

Translation: Say: He is the One who brought you into being on earth, and to Him you will be gathered.

The main expression matches Al-Muʾminūn 23:79. The difference lies in the opening: here there is the command qul, “Say.”

Al-Mulk 67:23 likewise mentions bringing humans into being and providing hearing, eyes and hearts. In Al-Mulk 67:25, the opponents ask when the promised resurrection will occur.

As in Al-Muʾminūn, the subject is human creation and the future gathering.

This repetition matters for examining the root. Dharaʾakum fī al-arḍi has the same structure in both verses. The wording itself supplies no reason to assign “created” to one and an entirely independent meaning such as “scattered” to the other.

Human creation on earth can, of course, involve multiplying generations and their spread across different regions. “Scattered” may convey part of the contextual picture, but should not be regarded as the verb’s sole lexical meaning.

“Brought into being” or “created” is the clearer shared meaning. Where the spread of generations matters, it can be explained separately.

Both verses emphasize human existence’s dependence on The God and humanity’s return to the same Creator.

4. Yadhraʾukum fīhi: creation and the increase of generations

Al-Shūrā 42:11 states:

فَاطِرُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ جَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ أَنْفُسِكُمْ أَزْوَاجًا وَمِنَ الْأَنْعَامِ أَزْوَاجًا يَذْرَؤُكُمْ فِيهِ لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ وَهُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْبَصِيرُ

fāṭiru al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi jaʿala lakum min anfusikum azwājan wa-mina al-anʿāmi azwājan yadhraʾukum fīhi laysa ka-mithlihi shayʾun wa-huwa al-samīʿu al-baṣīr

Translation: The Originator of the heavens and earth; He made for you mates from yourselves, and pairs among livestock. In this He brings you into being and multiplies you. Nothing is like Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing.

This is one of the root’s most significant occurrences for understanding the connection between creation and multiplication.

The opening describes The God as fāṭiru al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi. It then mentions making pairs among humans and livestock.

Yadhraʾukum follows these two statements.

Azwājan in the context shows the importance of the emergence and multiplication of generations. Humans and livestock continue through successive generations, and that continuity relates to pairing.

Early explanations render yadhraʾukum both as yakhluqukum and as yukaththirukum: He creates you and He multiplies you.

The two explanations need not conflict here. Creating new generations can be the very process through which creatures increase in number.

The referent of fīhi

Yadhraʾukum fīhi requires grammatical care.

Fīhi consists of the preposition fī and a masculine singular pronoun. Azwājan previously appears in the plural. Connecting the pronoun with this part of the sentence requires a semantic explanation, such as reference to making pairs or to the arrangement of creation, rather than direct agreement with the plural word.

Several explanations have been offered.

One reading takes the pronoun to refer to the arrangement of creation described in the sentence: The God brings creatures into being within this arrangement of pairs and multiplying generations.

Another understands fī as close in meaning to the preposition bāʾ: The God increases generations through making pairs.

Another explanation relates the pronoun to the setting of birth, such as within a mate or womb. This too is an interpretation, since such a referent has not previously been named in those terms in the sentence.

Connecting the expression with pairs and the continuation of generations fits the immediate context. This explanation should nevertheless not silently replace fīhi itself in a direct translation.

The clear lexical point is that yadhraʾukum here concerns bringing into being and the increase of generations.

Kum has the form used to address humans, but pairs among livestock have also just been mentioned. In one analysis, this form encompasses both groups by giving precedence to the human mode of address. In either case, both groups’ presence in the sentence clarifies the connection between pairs and the continuity of generations.

The conclusion, laysa ka-mithlihi shayʾun, also emphasizes The God’s uniqueness. It prevents the existence of pairs among creatures from implying the same need or structure in the Creator’s being.

Dharaʾa here thus describes creating and multiplying creatures. The nature or attributes of The God should not be deduced from the biological mechanisms of creatures.

5. Wa-laqad dharaʾnā li-jahannama: creation and the human outcome

Al-Aʿrāf 7:179 states:

وَلَقَدْ ذَرَأْنَا لِجَهَنَّمَ كَثِيرًا مِنَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنْسِ لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ أَعْيُنٌ لَا يُبْصِرُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ آذَانٌ لَا يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا أُولَٰئِكَ كَالْأَنْعَامِ بَلْ هُمْ أَضَلُّ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْغَافِلُونَ

wa-laqad dharaʾnā li-jahannama kathīran mina al-jinni wa-al-insi lahum qulūbun lā yafqahūna bihā wa-lahum aʿyunun lā yubṣirūna bihā wa-lahum ādhānun lā yasmaʿūna bihā ulāʾika ka-al-anʿāmi bal hum aḍallu ulāʾika humu al-ghāfilūn

Translation: We have certainly brought into being for Hell many jinn and humans. They have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear. They are like livestock—indeed, further astray. They are the heedless.

Dharaʾnā is a first-person plural past-tense verb denoting bringing into being.

Its object is kathīran mina al-jinni wa-al-insi: many jinn and humans.

The occurrence shows that dharaʾa concerns jinn and humans as well as visible material creatures such as plants and livestock.

The meaning of li-jahannama

Li-jahannama consists of the preposition lām and the name Hell.

The sentence connects bringing a group of jinn and humans into being with Hell.

Two well-known analyses of this lām understand it as expressing purpose or as expressing eventual outcome.

The first reading refers to creation for Hell.

The second emphasizes the eventual destination of those who ultimately enter Hell.

These analyses concern sentence structure and the relation between creation and outcome. They do not differ over the verb dharaʾa itself.

In direct translation, “We brought into being for Hell” preserves the Arabic structure. Working out all its philosophical and theological implications requires studying the related verses on guidance, choice, divine knowledge, responsibility and recompense.

The root DH R ʾ alone does not establish that some humans were created compelled to error and punishment regardless of their knowledge and conduct.

Conversely, the explicit li-jahannama should not be removed from the text or replaced without argument with a sentence that expresses no relationship between creation and outcome.

The group’s characteristics

After li-jahannama, the verse describes the group:

They have hearts but do not understand with them, eyes but do not see with them, and ears but do not hear with them.

It then describes them as like livestock, indeed further astray, and concludes, ulāʾika humu al-ghāfilūn.

The verse therefore does more than mention creating a group for Hell: it describes their cognitive and behavioral condition.

These descriptions must be retained in understanding the verse. They cannot be ignored to derive an unqualified judgment about all humans or all jinn from the opening phrase.

Kathīran also matters: the Qur’an speaks of “many,” rather than all jinn and humans.

The preceding verse, Al-Aʿrāf 7:178, concerns guidance and error. Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:176 describe someone who received divine signs but detached himself from them and followed his desire.

This connection places the discussion among verses concerning knowledge, guidance, turning away and the consequences of conduct.

In lexical analysis, dharaʾnā accordingly still means “We brought into being.” The deeper relation between creation and human outcome belongs in an independent reflection on the verse, rather than being imposed upon the root’s meaning in advance.

6. Comparison with related roots

DH R ʾ (dharaʾa) and KH L Q (khalaqa): bringing into being and creation

Study of KH L Q

Khalq has a broad Qur’anic field covering the creation of beings and bringing them into being with particular measure and structure.

Dharaʾa also denotes creation, but certain contexts, especially Al-Shūrā 42:11, emphasize multiplication and the continuity of generations.

This should not become an absolute rule that khalq denotes only initial creation, while dharaʾa denotes only subsequent reproduction.

The Qur’an itself uses dharaʾa for crops, livestock, humans, jinn and diverse earthly creatures.

The roots therefore overlap, while some uses of dharaʾa draw particular attention to creatures’ emergence and increase.

DH R ʾ (dharaʾa) and B R ʾ (baraʾa): two related verbs of creation

Study of B R ʾ

Baraʾa also concerns creation in the Qur’an.

Al-bāriʾu is a divine attribute in Al-Ḥashr 59:24.

Some lexical explanations relate baraʾa to bringing creatures into being and separating or distinguishing them.

Comparisons of dharaʾa, khalaqa and baraʾa can emphasize different aspects of bringing into being. Such differences should not become absolute, immutable definitions without testing the uses.

The relevant point here is that dharaʾa concerns creation and, in some contexts, the increase of creatures. It need not contain every feature of baraʾa.

Al-dhāriʾ is also recorded as a description of the Creator, but that particular form does not occur in the Qur’an.

DH R ʾ (dharaʾa) and DH R W (dharā): creation and scattering

The similar letters and proximity of some uses can lead these roots to be confused.

DH R ʾ, with hamzah, concerns bringing into being and creation in its six Qur’anic occurrences.

DH R W, with wāw, occurs in uses such as wind scattering dust or chaff.

Al-Kahf 18:45 states:

فَأَصْبَحَ هَشِيمًا تَذْرُوهُ الرِّيَاحُ

fa-aṣbaḥa hashīman tadhrūhu al-riyāḥ

Translation: Then it became dry, crumbled vegetation that the winds scatter.

Tadhrūhu belongs to DH R W, rather than DH R ʾ.

Here the verb denotes scattering something already in existence, while Qur’anic dharaʾa concerns creation and creatures’ coming into being.

The emergence of generations can, of course, involve their spread on earth. This contextual overlap does not make the roots historically or morphologically identical.

DH R ʾ (dharaʾa) and DH R R (dharra): the question of dhurriyyah

Some Arabic lexical analyses assign dhurriyyah to DH R ʾ. On that account, the original word contained hamzah, which was subsequently dropped in usage.

Other analyses connect dhurriyyah with dharr and other forms.

The morphological classification used in Forqan’s root index places dhurriyyah under DH R R. This is one analysis of the word and does not by itself settle the derivational disagreement.

The disagreement shows that a morphological classification or a lexicographer’s analysis alone does not conclusively establish a word’s historical origin.

The clear Qur’anic meaning of dhurriyyah is offspring or descendants. Semantic proximity to multiplying creatures does not warrant transferring all its occurrences to DH R ʾ.

The matter is examined in Forqan’s separate DH R R study.

Study of DH R R

DH R ʾ (dharaʾa) and B TH TH (baththa): bringing into being and spreading

Study of B TH TH

Baththa also denotes scattering and spreading creatures or things in the Qur’an.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:1 states:

وَبَثَّ مِنْهُمَا رِجَالًا كَثِيرًا وَنِسَاءً

wa-baththa minhumā rijālan kathīran wa-nisāʾā

Translation: And from the two He spread many men and women.

This expression relates to the spread of human generations.

Dharaʾa likewise concerns the emergence and increase of humans in some verses, but its direct use foregrounds creation.

Baththa, by comparison, emphasizes spreading and dispersing its object.

This distinction matters for dharaʾakum fī al-arḍi: human emergence on earth may involve their spread, but the two verbs should not be assumed to be entirely synonymous.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Bringing into being” and “creating” are the principal defensible equivalents for the six Qur’anic occurrences. In some contexts, “increasing” and “multiplying” also clarify aspects of the intended sense.

In Al-Anʿām 6:136, mimmā dharaʾa mina al-ḥarthi wa-al-anʿāmi can be rendered “from the crops and livestock He has brought into being.” This preserves creation without attributing the division of shares to the root itself.

In Al-Naḥl 16:13, wa-mā dharaʾa lakum fī al-arḍi means “and what He has brought into being for you on earth.” The varied colors must be translated from the following expression.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:179, wa-laqad dharaʾnā li-jahannama means “We have certainly brought into being for Hell.” Explaining the relation between creation and the hereafter concerns the sentence and its context, rather than changing the verb’s meaning.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:79 and Al-Mulk 67:24, the identical dharaʾakum fī al-arḍi should preferably receive the same principal rendering: “He brought you into being on earth.” Any multiplication and spread of generations can be explained contextually.

In Al-Shūrā 42:11, yadhraʾukum fīhi can, given pairs and successive generations, be explained as “in this He brings you into being and multiplies you.” A direct translation should nevertheless preserve the distinction between yadhraʾukum and fīhi.

In none of the six verses does dharaʾa itself denote white hair, pallor or animal color markings.

8. Conclusion

DH R ʾ has two important Arabic branches: creation, bringing into being and, in some contexts, the increase of creatures; and white hair or certain color characteristics.

A recognized lexical connection exists between creation and sowing seed. It can help illuminate the emergence and increase of creatures but should not be presented as a proven historical account of every meaning of the root.

Only the creation and bringing-into-being branch occurs in the Qur’an.

Al-Anʿām 6:136 and Al-Naḥl 16:13 concern diverse earthly creatures, crops and livestock. Al-Muʾminūn 23:79 and Al-Mulk 67:24 concern bringing humans into being on earth. Al-Shūrā 42:11 connects creation and multiplying generations with pairs. Al-Aʿrāf 7:179 uses the verb for many jinn and humans.

These uses show that dharaʾa cannot be restricted to reproduction, scattering, plant growth or creatures’ becoming visible. Each idea may relate to creation in a particular context, but none is the verb’s necessary meaning in every occurrence.

Final definition: In Qur’anic usage, DH R ʾ denotes bringing creatures into being and creation. Contexts concerning pairs and generations foreground multiplication and increase. Another Arabic branch concerns white hair and color characteristics; that branch is not used with this root in the Qur’an.

Each verse’s precise sense depends on the verb’s object and accompanying qualifications. Creation, multiplication, spread on earth and the eventual outcome in the hereafter are related concepts, but should not be substituted for one another without evidence.
