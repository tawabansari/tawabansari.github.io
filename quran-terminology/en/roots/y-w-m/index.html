Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root Y W M

Day, the time when a matter becomes manifest, an appointed period, the moment of account, and the field where the result of deeds appears

Forms covered from this root

This page studies the root ي و م semantically, then explains the most important Qur’anic forms and usages built from this root.

  • yawm / يَوْم — noun; a day, a specific time, or a defined period
  • al-yawm / الْيَوْم — definite noun; today, that day, or a particular day within the verse’s context
  • yawman / yawmin / yawmu / yawma / yawmi — case forms of yawm in different grammatical positions
  • yawmayn / يَوْمَيْنِ — dual; two days
  • ayyām / ayyāman / ayyāmin / al-ayyām — plural; days, appointed days, or defined periods
  • yawmaʾidhin / يَوْمَئِذٍ — adverbial compound; on that day, at that time
  • yawmihim / yawmikum / yawmahum and attached-pronoun forms — their day, your day, their appointed time, or the day related to them

Note: forms such as عَاوَمَ or يُعَاوِمُ may have a semantic connection with yearly or periodic timing in Arabic, but they are not Qur’anic forms of the root ي و م. This page focuses on Qur’anic words and usages of the root ي و م.

Also, يَوْمَانِ may be known grammatically as the nominative dual form in Arabic, but within the Qur’anic usage being treated here, the prominent form to mention is يَوْمَيْنِ.

General meaning of the root Y W M

The Qur’anic root ي و م points to the semantic field of day, appointed time, period, moment of disclosure, and a setting in which something becomes manifest, measured, or completed.

The word يَوْم is usually translated as “day,” but in the Qur’an it should not always be reduced to an ordinary twenty-four-hour day. Sometimes it refers to a calendar day. Sometimes it refers to a specific moment in history. Sometimes it points to the Day of Resurrection. Sometimes it refers to a period connected with creation, respite, punishment, deliverance, or accountability. And sometimes it marks the moment when a hidden truth becomes manifest.

From here, it becomes clear that yawm in the Qur’an is not only a unit of time. It can be the vessel in which truth appears: the day when something happens, when a promise is fulfilled, when a people are tested, when a veil is removed, or when a human being sees the result of what they have done.

In Sūrat al-Fātiḥah, the phrase يَوْمِ الدِّينِ uses this same field of meaning. The Day of Dīn is not merely a date in the future; it is the day when accountability, judgment, recompense, and the true ownership of God become manifest.

Yawm: beyond a calendar day

In ordinary speech, a “day” usually means a portion of time: from morning to night, or a full day and night. In the Qur’an, however, يَوْم sometimes carries that sense and sometimes carries a wider meaning.

The Qur’an speaks of days in which God makes a sign manifest, delivers a people, seizes a people because of their wrongdoing, or makes a truth clear to human beings. In such usages, yawm is not merely a quantity of time; it is a moment of meaning.

For this reason, yawm can be a day of trial, punishment, rescue, victory, regret, or account. The weight of that day is not only in its duration, but in the truth that becomes manifest within it.

This view awakens the human being toward time. Time in the Qur’an is not empty or meaningless. Every day can become a vessel of choice, responsibility, repentance, reform, or collapse. The human being lives within time, and time itself becomes a witness over the human being.

Yawm and al-yawm: the specific day in context

The form يَوْم sometimes appears indefinite, and sometimes with the definite article as الْيَوْم. Both must be understood through the context of the verse.

Sometimes يَوْم points to a day unknown from the human perspective: a day that will come, a promised day, or a day in which an event will occur. At other times it points to a specific day made clear by the surrounding context.

الْيَوْم often points to a more definite day: sometimes “today” in contrast with past or future, and sometimes a special day on which something is declared, completed, or made manifest.

In both forms, yawm directs the human being toward responsible time. The Qur’an reminds the human being that days do not pass without consequence. Today is connected to tomorrow, and the days of this world are not separate from the Day of Account.

So يَوْم and الْيَوْم are not merely temporal words. They call the human being to presence, recognition of opportunity, and awareness of the consequence of action.

Yawmayn: two days and the measuring of time

The form يَوْمَيْنِ refers to two days. This usage shows that time in the Qur’an can be measured, counted, and given meaning in relation to events.

When the Qur’an speaks of two days or several days, the number itself is not the only point. The deeper point is that time stands under divine measure and decree. Creation is not without measure. Respite is not without measure. Trial is not without measure. Punishment and deliverance are not outside God’s order.

يَوْمَيْنِ and other temporal forms remind the human being that the world has not been abandoned to chaos. Time has measure. Respite has measure. Responsibility has measure. God has appointed measure, limit, and term for everything.

In this sense, the root ي و م is connected with divine order. Days pass, but in the Qur’anic view, their passing is not meaningless.

Ayyām: days, periods, and appointed times

أَيَّام is the plural of يَوْم: days. But in the Qur’an, these days are not always merely a collection of ordinary calendar days. They can refer to appointed days, defined periods, or times in which God manifests blessing, trial, deliverance, or warning.

When the Qur’an speaks of أَيَّام, it may call the human being to remember God’s days, past days, or appointed days. Days in this sense carry memory. History is not merely story; the days of history are signs.

أَيَّام teaches the human being not to view time superficially. Every people, every person, and every society has days in which something about them becomes manifest: a day of blessing, trial, arrogance, repentance, collapse, or deliverance.

So days in the Qur’an are not merely calendrical counting; they are fields of reflection. Whoever reads the days of the past properly understands their own today more clearly.

Yawmaʾidhin: that day when truth becomes manifest

يَوْمَئِذٍ means “on that day” or “at that time.” This compound often appears in very serious Qur’anic settings: where a great truth becomes manifest, human beings face the result of their actions, or a veil is lifted from reality.

In يَوْمَئِذٍ, the day is not merely time; it is a scene of disclosure. On that day, claims gain weight, hidden matters become exposed, relationships change, and the human being realizes that many worldly supports were not lasting.

This expression is especially important in verses about Resurrection, account, fear, regret, or the manifestation of truth. In this world, the human being can hide, justify, flee, or deceive the self. But يَوْمَئِذٍ is not a day of escape from truth.

So يَوْمَئِذٍ awakens the human being: there will be a day when what remains hidden today becomes manifest.

Attached-pronoun forms: your day, their day, and their appointed time

In the Qur’an, يَوْم is sometimes attached to a pronoun, such as يَوْمِكُمْ, يَوْمِهِم, or يَوْمَهُم. These forms show that a day can be related to a particular group, people, or audience.

When the Qur’an speaks of “your day” or “their day,” it refers to a time connected to them: a day of encounter, promise, account, punishment, or the day when the truth about them becomes manifest.

This usage is important because it makes time personal and accountable. The human being is not only facing a universal Resurrection; every human being and every people also has its own days: the day opportunity arrives, the day warning comes, the day respite ends, and the day the result becomes clear.

So attaching yawm to a pronoun brings the message closer. The day is not merely “a day.” It may be my day, your day, our day, or their day.

Yawm al-Dīn in Sūrat al-Fātiḥah

In Sūrat al-Fātiḥah, the phrase يَوْمِ الدِّينِ appears in the statement مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ:

مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ

Here, يَوْم is joined with الدِّين. Dīn here carries the sense of account, recompense, judgment, and the final result of deeds. So يَوْمِ الدِّينِ means the Day of Account, the Day of Judgment, the day when the truth of actions becomes manifest, and the day when the human being faces the result of their path.

This phrase in al-Fātiḥah is precise. The human being first recognizes God as Rabb al-ʿālamīn. Then the human being recognizes His mercy. Then, through مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ, the human being learns that mercy is not separate from responsibility.

The Day of Dīn is the day when apparent ownerships collapse. No human being, group, power, wealth, lineage, rank, or intercessory claim owns the final judgment. God is the Owner of that Day.

So يَوْمِ الدِّينِ in al-Fātiḥah is not only a reference to the future; it is a formation of the human being today. Whoever is aware of the Day of Dīn does not see today as without account.

Yawm, account, and the manifestation of results

One of the most important connections of the root ي و م in the Qur’an is its connection with account and the result of actions. Many Qur’anic days are days in which the truth of deeds becomes manifest.

In this world, a human being may do something and not immediately see its result. A wrong may remain hidden, an intention may remain unseen, a right may be violated, or falsehood may present itself as truth for a time. But the Qur’an reminds us that there is a day for manifestation.

On that day, the human deed is not merely a memory from the past; it becomes present, weighed, and consequential. This view removes the human being from irresponsibility.

So yawm in the Qur’an is connected with justice. Without the Day of Account, many wrongs would remain unanswered and many hidden acts of good would remain unseen. But يَوْمِ الدِّينِ shows that nothing is lost with God.

Yawm in this world and yawm in the Hereafter

The word يَوْم is used in the Qur’an both for days of this world and for the Day of the Hereafter. These two are not disconnected; they are bound together.

The days of this world are the field of choice. In them, the human being believes or rejects, reforms or corrupts, gives or wrongs, learns or denies, repents or persists. But the Day of the Hereafter is the field in which the result of those choices becomes manifest.

This world has days of respite. The Hereafter has the Day of Account. This world has days of concealment. The Hereafter has the day of disclosure. This world has days of claim. The Hereafter has the day of truth.

So the human being should not see the days of this world as separate from the Day of the Hereafter. Every day of this world can be part of the human answer to the Day of Dīn.

Today, that day, and human responsibility in time

Through words of time such as today, day, that day, and their day, the Qur’an makes the human being responsible within time. Time is not merely something that passes away; time is the vessel of trust.

Today, the human being has opportunity. Today one can understand, reform, return, forgive, ask, learn, and change direction. But a day will come when the opportunity for action ends and the time of account begins.

This view pulls the human being out of heedlessness. Whoever takes يَوْمِ الدِّينِ seriously does not treat today lightly. They know that today is connected to that Day.

So the root ي و م is not only about time; it is about wakefulness within time. Every day is an opportunity to move closer to truth before the day of truth’s manifestation arrives.

Summary

The Qur’anic root ي و م invites the human being to understand time, day, respite, account, and the manifestation of the result of deeds. This root does not refer only to a calendar day; it refers to moments and periods in which a truth appears.

يَوْم speaks of a day or appointed time. الْيَوْم highlights a specific day. يَوْمَيْنِ shows the measuring of time. أَيَّام recalls days and periods of reflection. يَوْمَئِذٍ points to that day when truth becomes manifest. And attached-pronoun forms bring the day close to the destinies of human beings and communities.

In Sūrat al-Fātiḥah, يَوْمِ الدِّينِ is the central point of this root for our path. This phrase shows that worship is not complete without awareness of account. God is The Merciful and The Compassionate, but He is also the Owner of the Day of Dīn.

So when the root ي و م is understood correctly, the human view of time is corrected. The human being learns that today is a trust, tomorrow is unknown, and the Day of Dīn is certain. The days of this world pass, but their trace is not lost before God:

مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ
