1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic triliteral root J D R has several semantic branches. The best known concerns walls, enclosures, and barriers separating spaces. Another expresses suitability, fitness, or deservingness in relation to a person or situation. Other recorded usages concern skin swellings, the first emergence of vegetation, and something appearing above the ground.

Two important Qur’anic words are jidār and ajdar. The first names a physical wall. The second is the elative of jadīr and expresses being more fitting, deserving, or likely in relation to a situation.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris gives two principles for this root: the wall and associated building or strengthening, and something becoming apparent, as with emerging plants or a swelling on the skin.

This classification matters: all J D R derivatives cannot be reduced to “wall,” “firmness,” or “appearance.” Each branch requires independent witnesses, and possible relationships should be examined cautiously. Brief root meaning: A wall, its base, and enclosure; in jadīr and ajdar, suitability and fitness; in another branch, emerging and becoming apparent.

Qur’anic forms

The root occurs four times: three instances of the noun jidār in singular and plural forms, and one of the elative adjective ajdar. The text contains the following four inflected forms:

  • «أَجْدَرُ» (ajdaru): in Al-Tawbah 9:97, an elative meaning more fitting, deserving, or liable to a condition.
  • «جِدَارًا» (jidāran): in Al-Kahf 18:77, an indefinite singular noun, “a wall.”
  • «الْجِدَارُ» (al-jidāru): in Al-Kahf 18:82, the same singular noun with the definite article, “the wall.”
  • «جُدُرٍ» (judurin): in Al-Ḥashr 59:14, the plural of jidār, “walls.”

Jidāran and al-jidāru occur at two stages of one narrative: first a wall about to collapse, then the explanation for setting it upright.

Judur in Al-Ḥashr denotes walls providing protection in a confrontation involving fighting.

Ajdar in Al-Tawbah, unlike the other three occurrences, does not name a physical object. It concerns a group’s situation and their greater likelihood of lacking knowledge.

The Qur’an uses no verb from this root, nor its forms concerning plant growth, skin swellings, or smallpox.

2. The semantic principles in early Arabic

The wall: making a barrier or boundary

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْجِيمُ وَالدَّالُ وَالرَّاءُ أَصْلَانِ، فَالْأَوَّلُ الْجِدَارُ، وَهُوَ الْحَائِطُ، وَجَمْعُهُ جُدُرٌ وَجُدْرَانٌ.

al-jīmu wa-al-dālu wa-al-rāʾu aṣlāni fa-al-awwalu al-jidāru wa-huwa al-ḥāʾiṭu wa-jamʿuhu judurun wa-judrān

Translation: “Jīm, dāl, and rāʾ have two principles. The first is jidār, the wall; its plurals are judur and judrān.”

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: the two principles

Jidār denotes an upright structure separating two areas, limiting a space, or obstructing passage or sight.

A wall may belong to a building, surround a house or garden, provide protection, or simply divide spaces.

These uses do not change the noun’s basic meaning. Jidār remains a wall; its role comes from the setting and surrounding structure.

Jadr: the base or lower part of a wall

Arabic also records jadr with a silent dāl. Ibn Fāris relates it to the wall’s foundation:

وَالْجَدْرُ أَصْلُ الْحَائِطِ.

wa-al-jadru aṣlu al-ḥāʾiṭ

Translation: “Jadr is the foundation or base of the wall.”

This usage identifies the wall’s lower or supporting part. A wall needs a base to stand, and the base holds its structure in place.

In agricultural usage, jadr can also denote raised edges or barriers retaining water within an area.

Miṣbāḥ, Al-Muḥkam, and Lisān: foundations and water-retaining banks

This connects walls, boundaries, and retaining something within a defined space.

Jadr should nevertheless be distinguished from jadhr, “root,” with dhāl. These belong to different roots; the conceptual resemblance between a foundation and a root does not establish an identical history.

Building and strengthening a wall

Lexical sources record jadara for wall-building or enclosing.

Jadara al-jidāra can describe raising or making a wall. Jaddara likewise appears in usages concerning building or strengthening a structure.

Ijtadara bināʾahu is recorded for constructing and strengthening a building.

Qāmūs and Tāj: ijtadara bināʾahu and jaddarahu

J D R therefore includes more than the noun “wall”: Arabic also has verbs for creating or reinforcing one.

Jidār itself does not necessarily mean a strong, high, or impenetrable wall. In Al-Kahf 18:77, it describes a wall close to collapse.

Firmness and weakness are conditions of the wall, rather than two separate meanings of the noun.

An enclosure or enclosed place

In Kitāb al-ʿAyn, one physical sense of jadīr denotes a place with a wall built around it.

Al-ʿAyn: jadīr as an enclosed place

Jadīrah can likewise denote an enclosure or pen for sheep. Some lexical accounts distinguish stone enclosures from those made with mud or branches, making the material relevant to the particular naming.

This connects walls with defining boundaries and containing animals.

Jadīr here names an enclosed place rather than describing a suitable person. The written form is similar, but the meanings and sentence roles differ.

Every occurrence of jadīr therefore requires identifying whether it concerns an enclosure or someone or something considered fitting for a purpose.

3. Suitability and fitness: jadīr and ajdar

Jadīr in Arabic

Another significant branch appears in expressions such as jadīrun bi-kadhā.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

هُوَ جَدِيرٌ بِكَذَا وَلِكَذَا أَيْ خَلِيقٌ لَهُ.

huwa jadīrun bi-kadhā wa-li-kadhā ay khalīqun lahu

Translation: “He is jadīr for that: fitting or suited to it.”

Lisān: suitability and fitness

This concerns the relationship between a person or situation and the matter attributed to them.

Calling someone jadīr for a task means that the task fits the person’s abilities, qualities, or position.

This does not always express moral praise. Someone may be fit for a good task or, because of their circumstances, more liable to an undesirable outcome.

Jadīr should therefore not be restricted to “worthy of respect” or “virtuous.” Its precise meaning depends on its complement and context.

Jadārah: suitability and fitness

Jadura is used for being fitting or deserving; its verbal noun is jadārah.

In this branch, jadīr describes someone or something considered fitting for a matter. Its feminine is jadīrah, and established plurals include jadīrūn and judarāʾ.

Ajdar is the corresponding elative: comparatively more fitting, deserving, or likely in relation to something.

These senses do not always produce the same Persian equivalent.

Where praise or a positive responsibility is at issue, “more worthy” or “more deserving” may suit. Where the complement concerns ignorance, harm, or an undesirable result, “more liable to” or “more likely” can convey the meaning better.

This distinction is especially significant in Qur’anic ajdaru allā yaʿlamū.

Did suitability develop from the image of a wall?

Ibn Fāris places jadīrun bi-kadhā within the first branch, concerning walls and building. His explanation concerns someone about whom the relevant matter can be established or on whom it can be founded.

Maqāyīs: the proposed connection between building and suitability

The proposed connection is intelligible: just as walls and foundations involve firmness, an attribute or outcome may be considered established or fitting for someone.

That explanation should not immediately become a definite historical account of the word’s origin.

Related usages show that lexicographers perceived a connection. They do not alone establish that “wall” came first and “suitability” subsequently developed from it.

Proving a chronological development requires independent evidence for when and how each form was used.

Both wall and suitability should consequently remain in the final definition, without reducing either to the other without evidence.

4. The other branch: protrusion and emergence

Ibn Fāris also gives a second principle for J D R:

وَالْأَصْلُ الثَّانِي ظُهُورُ الشَّيْءِ، نَبَاتًا وَغَيْرَهُ.

wa-al-aṣlu al-thānī ẓuhūru al-shayʾi nabātan wa-ghayrahu

Translation: “The second principle is something becoming apparent, whether vegetation or something else.”

This branch appears in words concerning plants and skin swellings.

The first emergence of plants

Kitāb al-ʿAyn describes plants growing in hard, stony ground. When their tips emerge at the beginning of spring, expressions such as ajdarat al-arḍu and ajdarat al-shajaratu are used.

Al-ʿAyn: emerging growth and ajdarat al-shajarah

The plant or shoot has newly appeared, emerging above ground or from a branch.

The central image is emergence from a surface. It does not yet describe a tall tree or ripe crop, but the first appearance of growth.

Jadr is also recorded as the name of a small plant.

This family’s Arabic range is therefore not limited to buildings and walls.

Skin swellings and smallpox

Lexical sources use jadar and jadarah or judarah for skin protrusions, eruptions, swellings, and marks of blows.

Judarī names smallpox, characterized by skin eruptions and blisters.

Lisān al-ʿArab gives several descriptions of protrusions on human or animal skin. Some concern disease eruptions, others swellings, and others marks left by blows or wounds.

Their shared feature is something appearing or projecting above the skin’s surface.

Forms such as jādir and jadrāʾ are also recorded concerning swellings on the necks of certain animals.

These belong to the emergence-and-protrusion branch; visual resemblance to other family members does not make them mean wall or suitability.

Do both branches go back to one original meaning?

One might perceive a resemblance between a wall rising above ground and a plant or skin eruption emerging above a surface.

Ibn Fāris nevertheless gives two separate principles, and the evidence does not establish one historical development linking everything.

Wall, suitability, and protrusion are real usages. A formula as broad as “something becoming prominent and fixed” cannot replace them all without difficulty.

Such a definition is too broad and could apply to many other roots.

Qur’anic research should therefore identify the branches clearly and propose only relationships supported by evidence.

5. Early poetic witnesses and the limits of pre-Islamic attribution

Jadīrūn in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

The well-known pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes warriors:

بِخَيْلٍ عَلَيْهَا جِنَّةٌ عَبْقَرِيَّةٌ
جَدِيرُونَ يَوْمًا أَنْ يَنَالُوا فَيَسْتَعْلُوا

bi-khaylin ʿalayhā jinnatun ʿabqariyyatun / jadīrūna yawman an yanālū fa-yastaʿlū

Translation: “With horses bearing warriors like the jinn of ʿAbqar: men likely one day to attain their aim and prevail.” Jinnah here has kasrah on jīm and denotes jinn, used in a comparison describing the warriors. It should not be confused with junnah, a shield or protective covering.

Commentary on Zuhayr’s witness: jinnah denotes jinn and depicts the warriors

The poet describes warriors’ capacity for attaining victory and ascendancy. ʿAlayhā jinnah depicts those riders through the image of jinn, rather than describing armor or protective coverings on the horses.

Zuhayr’s Dīwān: the riders and warriors in context

Jadīrūna is the plural of jadīr and here concerns their fitness and preparedness for attaining the intended result.

This witness establishes that the suitability sense existed before Islam; it is not merely a later technical term.

Fitness here concerns people’s abilities and situation. The poet describes their military qualities as fitting them to achieve their purpose.

Jadīr is thus more than a legal or moral concept: it can express the likelihood of success and the fit between a person’s qualities and an outcome.

This helps explain Qur’anic ajdar.

Jadīr as an enclosed place

Kitāb al-ʿAyn quotes the following half-line without naming a poet; Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab attribute it to Al-Aʿshā:

Al-ʿAyn unnamed; Al-Muḥkam and Lisān attribute it to Al-Aʿshā

وَيَبْنُونَ فِي كُلِّ وَادٍ جَدِيرًا

wa-yabnūna fī kulli wādin jadīran

Translation: “They build a walled enclosure in every valley.”

Jadīr here names a place surrounded by a wall.

This differs from Zuhayr’s verse: jadīrūn there concerns suitable people; jadīr here concerns an enclosed location.

A shared written form therefore does not determine the meaning. The noun’s type, sentence structure, and complement require examination.

The lexical account examined quotes only part of Al-Aʿshā’s verse. That is sufficient to establish the usage, without inventing a missing half-line and attributing a complete verse to him.

Mujtadar in Arabic poetry

Lexical sources also quote this fragment:

تَشْيِيدُ أَعْضَادِ الْبِنَاءِ الْمُجْتَدَرِ

tashyīdu aʿḍādi al-bināʾi al-mujtadar

Translation: “Strengthening the supports of the firmly constructed building.”

Mujtadar here concerns a building that has been constructed and strengthened.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab attribute the fragment to Ruʾbah. The editorial note in Kitāb al-ʿAyn, however, says it is absent from Ruʾbah’s Dīwān and occurs in that of his father, Al-ʿAjjāj, volume two, page 21. The disputed attribution should remain explicit.

Editorial note in Al-ʿAyn: Ruʾbah versus Al-ʿAjjāj

This witness should therefore neither be attributed without explanation to one particular poet nor confidently called pre-Islamic.

Its lexical use nevertheless remains clear: J D R also concerns constructing and strengthening buildings.

What the early witnesses establish

The literary evidence establishes jadīr for suitability before revelation. Words for walls, enclosures, and building also occur in records of early Arabic.

The witnesses differ in date and attribution. Zuhayr’s verse belongs to pre-Islamic Arabic; other fragments survive only in lexical accounts or have disputed attribution.

These differences prevent historical evidence being made to prove more than it supports.

6. Qur’anic occurrences

Al-Tawbah 9:97: ajdar and the likelihood of lacking knowledge

الْأَعْرَابُ أَشَدُّ كُفْرًا وَنِفَاقًا وَأَجْدَرُ أَلَّا يَعْلَمُوا حُدُودَ مَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

al-aʿrābu ashaddu kufran wa-nifāqan wa-ajdaru allā yaʿlamū ḥudūda mā anzala Allāhu ʿalā rasūlihi wa-Allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm

Proposed translation based on this study: “The desert Arabs are more intense in disbelief and hypocrisy, and more liable not to know the limits of what The God has revealed to His Messenger. The God is Knowing, Wise.”

Ajdar is the elative of the jadīr family, expressing a greater fit between a group and the condition attributed to it.

Its complement is allā yaʿlamū: not knowing the limits of what The God revealed to His Messenger.

Syntactically, ajdaru is coordinated with ashaddu. Both are elatives: ashaddu describes the intensity of disbelief and hypocrisy, while ajdaru concerns the greater likelihood or conditions for lacking knowledge.

Persian “more deserving not to know” may suggest that their lack of knowledge is desirable or worthy of approval or reward.

Ajdar in this construction need not imply that positive judgment. The circumstances are more conducive to an outcome that is undesirable here.

“More liable to lack knowledge” or “more likely not to know” can therefore convey the statement more naturally.

Al-Ṭabarī: ajdar and revelation’s limits

Al-aʿrāb and the scope of the judgment

Al-aʿrāb here denotes desert-dwelling Arabs. The verse strongly describes greater disbelief and hypocrisy, followed by greater likelihood of ignorance concerning revelation’s limits.

Analysis should not conceal the force of that criticism: the Qur’an explicitly speaks of stronger disbelief and hypocrisy and greater liability to ignorance.

Yet Al-Tawbah 9:99 identifies desert Arabs who believe in The God and the Last Day and regard their spending as a means of nearness to Him.

The judgment in verse 97 should therefore not become an absolute statement about every desert dweller at every time. The surrounding Qur’anic passage itself differentiates among them.

Ajdaru allā yaʿlamū concerns ignorance of revelation’s limits, rather than inherent lack of intelligence or natural inability to understand.

Distance from where the message was received and taught, living conditions, and access to knowledge may help explain the situation. Those explanations require contextual and independent historical evidence.

J D R itself does not mean distance from knowledge, lack of culture, or geographical deprivation.

Ajdar here thus retains its meaning of greater fitness or likelihood, with ignorance of revelation’s limits as its particular subject.

Al-Kahf 18:77: a wall about to collapse

فَانطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَتَيَا أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ اسْتَطْعَمَا أَهْلَهَا فَأَبَوْا أَن يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا فَوَجَدَا فِيهَا جِدَارًا يُرِيدُ أَن يَنقَضَّ فَأَقَامَهُ ۖ قَالَ لَوْ شِئْتَ لَاتَّخَذْتَ عَلَيْهِ أَجْرًا

fa-nṭalaqā ḥattā idhā atayā ahla qaryatin istaṭʿamā ahlahā fa-abaw an yuḍayyifūhumā fa-wajadā fīhā jidāran yurīdu an yanqaḍḍa fa-aqāmahu qāla law shiʾta la-ttakhadhta ʿalayhi ajran

Proposed translation based on this study: “They set out until they reached the people of a town and asked them for food, but the people refused to host them. There they found a wall about to collapse, and he set it upright and firm. He said: If you wished, you could have taken payment for it.”

Jidāran is an indefinite singular noun identifying a particular wall in that town.

Its significant contextual feature is instability. The wall was about to collapse, and Moses’ companion set it upright.

Arabic yurīdu an yanqaḍḍa can describe something on the verge of undergoing an event.

The expression alone does not require attributing human volition to the wall. Natural language can describe impending conditions with expressions primarily associated with willing or inclination.

“A wall about to collapse” therefore naturally conveys its condition.

Fa-aqāmahu also matters. Moses’ companion brought the wall out of its unstable condition and kept it standing. The verse does not specify whether this required complete reconstruction, repairing damaged parts, or another method.

Technical repair details should not be added without evidence.

The wall and payment

At the verse’s end, Moses remarks that his companion could have taken payment for the work if he wished.

The townspeople had refused hospitality to the travelers, yet Moses’ companion strengthened the wall.

Payment comes from ittakhadhta and ajran, rather than jidār itself.

The wall is associated with doing good, hospitality, and payment in the narrative, while its lexical meaning remains “wall.”

Al-Kahf 18:82: protecting the property of two orphans

وَأَمَّا الْجِدَارُ فَكَانَ لِغُلَامَيْنِ يَتِيمَيْنِ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ وَكَانَ تَحْتَهُ كَنزٌ لَّهُمَا وَكَانَ أَبُوهُمَا صَالِحًا فَأَرَادَ رَبُّكَ أَن يَبْلُغَا أَشُدَّهُمَا وَيَسْتَخْرِجَا كَنزَهُمَا رَحْمَةً مِّن رَّبِّكَ وَمَا فَعَلْتُهُ عَنْ أَمْرِي ذَٰلِكَ تَأْوِيلُ مَا لَمْ تَسْطِع عَّلَيْهِ صَبْرًا

wa-ammā al-jidāru fa-kāna li-ghulāmayni yatīmayni fī al-madīnati wa-kāna taḥtahu kanzun lahumā wa-kāna abūhumā ṣāliḥan fa-arāda rabbuka an yablughā ashuddahumā wa-yastakhrijā kanzahumā raḥmatan min rabbika wa-mā faʿaltuhu ʿan amrī dhālika taʾwīlu mā lam tasṭiʿ ʿalayhi ṣabran

Proposed translation based on this study: “As for that wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and beneath it lay a treasure belonging to them. Their father was righteous. Your Lord wanted them to reach maturity and strength and bring out their treasure: a mercy from your Lord. I did not do this of my own accord. This is the explanation of what you could not bear patiently.”

Al-jidāru has the definite article because it refers back to the wall introduced in Al-Kahf 18:77.

Jidāran and al-jidāru are not separate derivatives or lexical meanings: the first introduces a wall, and the second refers back to it.

Its restoration is now explained: it belonged to two orphan boys, whose treasure lay beneath it.

The wall has a significant protective role. Keeping it standing relates to preserving their property until they reach maturity.

Protecting property is nevertheless not jidār’s lexical definition. The wall first appears in Al-Kahf 18:77 as a structure close to collapse; its protective role becomes clear in Al-Kahf 18:82.

One object can have different contextual roles without its name changing meaning.

The wall is associated with the orphans’ ownership and preserving their resources. The verse does not specify the treasure’s material, how it was hidden, or the wall’s technical protective mechanism.

Al-Ḥashr 59:14: a wall as shelter and barrier

لَا يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ جَمِيعًا إِلَّا فِي قُرًى مُّحَصَّنَةٍ أَوْ مِن وَرَاءِ جُدُرٍ ۚ بَأْسُهُم بَيْنَهُمْ شَدِيدٌ ۚ تَحْسَبُهُمْ جَمِيعًا وَقُلُوبُهُمْ شَتَّىٰ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَّا يَعْقِلُونَ

lā yuqātilūnakum jamīʿan illā fī quran muḥaṣṣanatin aw min warāʾi judurin baʾsuhum baynahum shadīdun taḥsabuhum jamīʿan wa-qulūbuhum shattā dhālika bi-annahum qawmun lā yaʿqilūn

Proposed translation based on this study: “They do not fight you together except in fortified, protected towns or from behind walls. Their hostility among themselves is fierce. You think them united, while their hearts are divided. That is because they are a people who do not reason.”

Judur is the plural of jidār and means “walls.”

Min warāʾi judurin identifies a position behind walls. A wall physically separates opposing sides and may obstruct sight, passage, or direct contact.

Qurā muḥaṣṣanah also describes towns provided with fortifications or protective arrangements.

These images are related without being identical. A fortified town is a broader defensive setting; “from behind walls” specifies a position relative to walls.

Judur here thus denotes real walls serving as barriers or defensive cover in a confrontation.

Al-Ṭabarī: walls and singular/plural readings

Walls and divided hearts

After the fortified towns and walls, the verse describes fierce hostility among them and their divided hearts.

The context contrasts external defenses with a lack of inward cohesion: they appear behind fortifications but are not inwardly united.

That image matters to understanding the whole verse.

Judur itself does not mean fear, hypocrisy, division, or weakness of will. The wall is a physical barrier; other parts of the verse establish the people’s moral and social qualities.

Nor does using a wall or defensive position in any conflict inherently constitute blameworthy fear. The verse concerns a particular group and situation; its judgment cannot be extended to everyone who uses walls or defenses for protection.

Judur retains the ordinary plural meaning “walls.” The verse’s social significance arises from its combination with the other expressions.

7. Comparison with related roots

J D R (jidār) and B N Y (bināʾ)

Study of B N Y

Jidār names a particular component or kind of structure. B N Y concerns building and erecting structures more broadly.

Every wall is built, but every structure is not necessarily a wall. A building can include foundations, a roof, columns, and other components.

Al-Kahf 18:77 calls the object jidār; aqāmahu describes setting it upright.

Confusing them could make J D R seem to mean constructing every kind of building. Its Qur’anic nominal usage specifically identifies a wall.

J D R (jidār) and Ḥ J R (ḥajar)

Study of Ḥ J R

Ḥajar means stone. A wall may be made of stone, but its material is not part of jidār’s meaning.

Walls may also be made of mud, brick, or other materials.

Ḥ J R also has usages concerning prohibition and restriction, without making the full ranges of the two families identical.

A wall can obstruct and protect in Al-Ḥashr 59:14, but its direct meaning is neither “stone” nor “to prohibit.”

J D R (jidār) and Ḥ Ṣ N (ḥiṣn)

Study of Ḥ Ṣ N

Ḥiṣn concerns a fortress or protected place; its family relates to fortification and protection.

Al-Ḥashr 59:14 places qurā muḥaṣṣanah alongside judur.

Walls and fortification can work together in protection without being synonymous.

Jidār is a wall; ḥiṣn may encompass barriers, buildings, and protective arrangements.

A wall may form part of a fortification. Not every wall is a fortress, nor does every fortress consist of just one wall.

J D R (jadīr) and Ḥ Q Q (ḥaqīq)

Study of Ḥ Q Q

Jadīr and ḥaqīq can overlap, describing what is fitting, deserving, or established for a person or situation.

Ḥ Q Q nevertheless has a broader range concerning right, truth, realization, and establishment.

Adjectival jadīr emphasizes the fit between a person or thing and a particular matter.

Ajdar in Al-Tawbah 9:97 concerns a greater predisposition to ignorance. Rendering it “more right” or “having greater entitlement” without considering its complement could mislead.

Lexical proximity therefore does not authorize replacing one word with the other everywhere.

J D R (jadīr) and W L Y (awlā)

Study of W L Y

Awlā often concerns greater fitness, nearness, or deservingness and can overlap with ajdar.

Awlā relates to nearness, connection, and priority within its own family, whereas ajdar belongs to the family recording suitability. Elative awlā in this sense belongs to W L Y; it should not be merged with A W L and beginning or taʾwīl.

Ṣiḥāḥ, Miṣbāḥ, and Tāj: awlā belongs to W L Y

Both may be rendered “more deserving” in a suitable context, while their different roots and structures remain distinct in research.

Al-Tawbah 9:97 concerns a situation more conducive to an outcome, rather than moral precedence or a legal entitlement. “More liable to ignorance” is consequently more natural.

J D R (jadr) and J DH R (jadhr)

Jadr can denote a wall’s foundation or lower part. Jadhr, with dhāl, belongs to a different root and denotes a plant’s root or something’s origin.

Both can relate to Persian “foundation” or “base,” but similar meanings do not establish identical roots.

Jadr and jadhr should therefore remain separate in lexical indexes and Qur’anic analysis.

8. Challenges of understanding and translation

Is jadīr always positive?

Persian shāyistah, “worthy,” often conveys praise, suggesting moral virtue, competence, or entitlement.

Arabic jadīr is not restricted to praise. It can describe how a person or situation fits an outcome, desirable or otherwise.

Ajdar in Al-Tawbah 9:97 takes not knowing revelation’s limits as its complement. Translation should convey greater likelihood or liability to ignorance, rather than presenting ignorance as a virtue.

A familiar Persian equivalent can be correct in some settings without fitting every occurrence independently of complement and syntax.

Does jidār always signify protection?

A wall can protect, but its meaning is not restricted to protection.

In Al-Kahf 18:77 it is close to collapse, without an independently stated protective function. Al-Kahf 18:82 reveals its relationship with preserving the orphans’ treasure. Al-Ḥashr 59:14 places walls in a defensive situation.

These occurrences show that context determines a wall’s role.

“Defense,” “shelter,” or “defensive position” may help explain particular contexts, but none is jidār’s fixed equivalent.

Does ajdar directly mean “firmer”?

Because Ibn Fāris connects walls and suitability, someone might render Qur’anic ajdar as “firmer.”

Its complement in Al-Tawbah 9:97, however, concerns not knowing revelation’s limits. “They are firmer not to know” is unnatural and fails to clarify the sentence.

Even if the historical connection is correct, translation must convey the derivative’s established contextual meaning.

A proposed root image cannot replace a natural rendering.

Distinguishing the root from a verse’s moral significance

Al-Ḥashr places walls alongside divided hearts. Al-Kahf connects a wall with the orphans’ rights and preserved property. Al-Tawbah relates ajdar to a group’s knowledge of revelation’s limits.

These associations help explain the verses without independently defining the root.

Qur’anic J D R includes both physical and adjectival applications. Each verse’s moral and social significance arises from its words, syntax, and context together.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Wall” is the principal equivalent for jidār.

Jidāran in Al-Kahf 18:77 means “a wall.” Jidāran yurīdu an yanqaḍḍa can naturally be rendered “a wall about to collapse.”

Al-jidāru in Al-Kahf 18:82 refers to the already introduced wall. “As for that wall” makes the connection clear.

Judur in Al-Ḥashr 59:14 is “walls”; min warāʾi judurin is naturally “from behind walls.”

Ajdar in Al-Tawbah 9:97 requires a contextual equivalent. “More fitting,” “more deserving,” and “more likely” are established possibilities. In ajdaru allā yaʿlamū, “more liable not to know” or “more likely not to know” makes the meaning clearer.

In non-Qur’anic usage, jadīrun bi-kadhā can mean “worthy of it,” “deserving it,” or “suited to the task.”

The emergence-and-growth branch requires equivalents specific to each derivative. Judarī names smallpox, jadarah can denote a skin protrusion or eruption, and ajdarat al-arḍu concerns vegetation first emerging above ground.

These senses should not be transferred without evidence to Qur’anic jidār and ajdar.

10. Conclusion

Arabic J D R has several established branches. Its principal physical branch concerns walls, foundations, building, and enclosures. Jadīr and ajdar express suitability and greater fitness. Other usages concern skin swellings and emerging plants.

Ibn Fāris organizes the family under wall and emergence. The lexical tradition explains a connection between wall and suitability, without independently establishing a definite historical origin.

Early poetry establishes suitability as a pre-Islamic sense of jadīr. Lexical records also preserve enclosed places and firmly constructed buildings.

The Qur’an uses the root four times. Al-Kahf introduces a real wall close to collapse and later explains its restoration through preserving two orphans’ treasure. Al-Ḥashr names walls providing barriers and cover during fighting. Al-Tawbah uses ajdar for a greater likelihood or liability to ignorance of revelation’s limits.

The family therefore cannot be defined by a single broad word such as “firmness” or “barrier.” Jidār names a wall, while ajdar is an elative concerning suitability.

For Forqan, “wall” is jidār’s principal equivalent, and “walls” renders judur. Ajdar must fit its complement: in Al-Tawbah 9:97, “more liable to ignorance” is more precise than a rendering that gives ignorance moral worth.

The central distinctions are these: walls are understood through physical structures; suitability through an adjective’s relation to its complement; and moral or social significance through context. Preserving them prevents unwarranted extensions and allows every Qur’anic usage to be understood in its own setting.
