Pelting with stones, threats, conjectural speech, and being driven away

In its clear material sense, Arabic R J M denotes throwing stones at someone or something. The same family also has recorded usages involving stoning, threatening injury or death, hurling abuse, speaking conjecturally, and driving or casting away.

In the Qur’an, the root appears in several distinct settings: threats to stone prophets and believers, speaking without knowledge about hidden matters, describing Satan as rajīm, and using rujūm for what is hurled at devils.

These usages should not all be reduced to one narrow sense. Rajman bi-al-ghayb in Al-Kahf does not mean throwing actual stones, while al-shayṭān al-rajīm does not always simply describe a being struck with material stones. Conversely, figurative usages do not justify removing actual stoning from explicit Qur’anic threats.

Qur’anic forms: la-rajamnāka, yarjumūkum, la-arjumannaka, la-narjumannakum, tarjumūni, rajman, rujūman, rajīm, and al-marjūmīn.

These forms occur fourteen times in fourteen verses across twelve surahs: five Form I verbs, six occurrences of rajīm, one plural passive participle al-marjūmīn, the verbal noun rajman, and the noun rujūman. Differences in the article, case, or syntactic position of rajīm do not create separate derivatives.

Other recorded Arabic forms include rājama, tarājama, rajjama, rijām, rujmah, mirjam, and murajjam. They help establish the family’s semantic range.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic witnesses

Pelting with stones: the material sense

Rajamahu bi-al-ḥijārah means targeting someone with stones.

Rajama is the verb, rajm its verbal noun, and marjūm describes someone subjected to the action.

Lexical explanations treat throwing stones as the family’s principal center, relating its speech, conjecture, and exclusion usages to extensions of that sense. This clarifies semantic connections without independently establishing when each usage arose.

In this action, a stone is thrown from the thrower towards a target. The aim may be injury, driving away, threatening, or killing; the verb alone does not specify every throw’s outcome.

When applied to people, particularly as a threat or punishment, rajm can nevertheless mean stoning to death. This recorded lexical usage should not be equated with a harmless toss of a stone.

Three things should therefore be distinguished: throwing stones, intending harm, and the possible result of death. They often occur together but are not semantically identical.

Collected stones and everyday implements

Rijām and rujmah are also recorded for collected stones.

One familiar use of rujmah denotes a heap of stones placed on a grave or gathered as a marker.

Another use of rijām denotes a stone tied to a rope and lowered into a well to stir the mud at the bottom, or to help the water-drawing apparatus descend.

These recorded lexical examples belong to the family’s material range. Their inclusion in later dictionaries, however, does not establish that all their details were current in the same form in pre-Islamic times.

Nor should a heap of stones or a well implement be imposed on the Qur’anic verb rajama. Sharing a root does not make every usage present in each derivative.

Hurling words instead of stones

Rajm also extends to speaking ill of someone, insulting, reproaching, and hurling hurtful words.

Rajamahu bi-al-kalām means making someone the target of harsh, abusive speech.

Here, hurling speech is likened to throwing stones. Instead of a material object, the person directs hurtful words at another.

The extension is understandable, but does not establish that every Qur’anic threat of rajm means verbal abuse. Each instance requires examining its setting and any indicators of actual stoning, threatened killing, or verbal harm.

Conjectural speech: a pre-Islamic poetic witness

An important usage concerns speech grounded in guesswork rather than knowledge and observation.

The celebrated Muʿallaqah of the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā includes:

وَمَا الْحَرْبُ إِلَّا مَا عَلِمْتُمْ وَذُقْتُمُ
وَمَا هُوَ عَنْهَا بِالْحَدِيثِ الْمُرَجَّمِ

wa-mā al-ḥarbu illā mā ʿalimtum wa-dhuqtumu / wa-mā huwa ʿanhā bi-al-ḥadīthi al-murajjami

“War is only what you have known and tasted; what I say about it is no conjectural, unsupported account.”

Zuhayr refers his audience to the actual experience of war. He says his account rests on observation and experience, rather than guessing and speculative talk.

Al-ḥadīth al-murajjam denotes an account formed from conjecture without firm support.

The witness matters because it shows a nonmaterial use in poetry attributed to the period before the Qur’an. Conjectural speech should therefore not be treated simply as an interpretive invention devised to explain Al-Kahf.

The image of throwing stones can explain the connection with the material sense, but this resemblance alone does not date the figurative sense’s emergence precisely.

2. Rajm in the prophets’ accounts: threats of violence

Several verses put the verb rajama in the speech of opponents of the prophets, or of people seeking to turn believers from their belief.

These instances show rajm functioning as a means of threat and pressure, without inherently implying justice or the execution of a divine ruling.

Shuʿayb threatened by his people

Hūd 11:91 states:

قَالُوا يَا شُعَيْبُ مَا نَفْقَهُ كَثِيرًا مِّمَّا تَقُولُ وَإِنَّا لَنَرَاكَ فِينَا ضَعِيفًا وَلَوْلَا رَهْطُكَ لَرَجَمْنَاكَ وَمَا أَنْتَ عَلَيْنَا بِعَزِيزٍ

qālū yā shuʿaybu mā nafqahu kathīran mimmā taqūlu wa-innā la-narāka fīnā ḍaʿīfan wa-lawlā rahṭuka la-rajamnāka wa-mā anta ʿalaynā bi-ʿazīz

“They said: Shuʿayb, we do not understand much of what you say, and we certainly see you as weak among us. Were it not for your clan, we would certainly have stoned you; you are not held in honor by us.”

La-rajamnāka is a threat connected with Shuʿayb’s social position.

His opponents say that without his clan’s protection they would subject him to rajm. This setting clearly accords with threatened bodily violence and stoning.

The verb itself does not detail how the threat would be carried out, how many stones would be thrown, or its certain outcome.

Rajm also appears in the opponents’ speech. The Qur’an reports the threat; reporting a threat does not endorse it.

Noah threatened

In Noah’s account, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:116 states:

قَالُوا لَئِن لَّمْ تَنتَهِ يَا نُوحُ لَتَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْمَرْجُومِينَ

qālū laʾin lam tantahi yā nūḥu la-takūnanna mina al-marjūmīn

“They said: Noah, if you do not desist, you will certainly be among those stoned.”

Al-marjūmīn is the sound masculine plural of marjūm, the passive participle of rajama.

Noah’s people threaten that if he does not stop his call, he will share the fate of those subjected to rajm.

This accords with threatened stoning or killing with stones. The verse does not report that the threat was actually carried out.

The believing companions of the cave threatened

In the account of the companions of the cave, Al-Kahf 18:20 states:

إِنَّهُمْ إِن يَظْهَرُوا عَلَيْكُمْ يَرْجُمُوكُمْ أَوْ يُعِيدُوكُمْ فِي مِلَّتِهِمْ وَلَن تُفْلِحُوا إِذًا أَبَدًا

innahum in yaẓharū ʿalaykum yarjumūkum aw yuʿīdūkum fī millatihim wa-lan tufliḥū idhan abadā

“If they gain power over you, they will stone you or return you to their creed, and then you will never prosper.”

Yarjumūkum stands alongside yuʿīdūkum fī millatihim, “return you to their creed.”

The believers face two dangers: violence against them or being returned to the dominant society’s creed.

This contrast strengthens the bodily-threat reading. Rajm here does not merely name unpleasant speech; it can mean stoning and killing.

The verse also shows rajm potentially serving religious coercion and depriving people of freedom of belief.

This follows from the threatening people’s conduct and the account’s setting, rather than the root inherently meaning religious persecution.

Abraham threatened by his father

In Abraham’s account, Maryam 19:46 states:

قَالَ أَرَاغِبٌ أَنتَ عَنْ آلِهَتِي يَا إِبْرَاهِيمُ لَئِن لَّمْ تَنتَهِ لَأَرْجُمَنَّكَ وَاهْجُرْنِي مَلِيًّا

qāla a-rāghibun anta ʿan ālihatī yā ibrāhīmu laʾin lam tantahi la-arjumannaka wa-uhjurnī maliyyā

“He said: Abraham, are you turning away from my objects of worship? If you do not desist, I will certainly subject you to rajm; leave me for a long time.”

La-arjumannaka has two established lexical readings in this verse.

The first is a threat of stoning and bodily violence.

The second is a threat of insults, severe reproach, and verbal harm.

Both senses are recorded in Arabic. The context establishes a threat, but does not by itself eliminate every uncertainty about the kind of rajm.

The continuation, wa-uhjurnī maliyyan, asks Abraham to leave. La-arjumannaka should therefore not simply be rendered “leave me” without evidence, merging it with the next command.

The translation should preserve the threat, with stoning and verbal assault explained separately as possible readings.

The messengers threatened

Yā Sīn 36:18 states:

قَالُوا إِنَّا تَطَيَّرْنَا بِكُمْ لَئِن لَّمْ تَنتَهُوا لَنَرْجُمَنَّكُمْ وَلَيَمَسَّنَّكُم مِّنَّا عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

qālū innā taṭayyarnā bikum laʾin lam tantahū la-narjumannakum wa-la-yamassannakum minnā ʿadhābun alīm

“They said: We take you as a bad omen. If you do not desist, we will certainly stone you, and a painful punishment from us will certainly touch you.”

The threat of rajm accompanies a promise of painful harm.

This setting emphasizes violence and the threat’s severity. Nevertheless, ʿadhābun alīm is a separate expression; all its content should not be merged into rajm itself.

Moses seeks refuge

In Al-Dukhān 44:20, Moses says:

وَإِنِّي عُذْتُ بِرَبِّي وَرَبِّكُمْ أَن تَرْجُمُونِ

wa-innī ʿudhtu bi-rabbī wa-rabbikum an tarjumūni

“And I have sought refuge with my Lord and your Lord against your subjecting me to rajm.”

Tarjumūni can concern a bodily threat and stoning. Early lexical explanations also propose insults and verbal harm for this expression.

The verse clearly describes Moses seeking refuge against harmful conduct, but the verb alone does not conclusively identify the exact threat.

Taken together, the threats against people show rajm potentially describing overt violence against opponents. In some verses, however, the distinction between stoning and severe verbal assault should be handled carefully.

3. Rajman bi-al-ghayb: speaking without knowledge

A particularly distinct usage occurs in part of Al-Kahf 18:22:

سَيَقُولُونَ ثَلَاثَةٌ رَّابِعُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ وَيَقُولُونَ خَمْسَةٌ سَادِسُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ رَجْمًا بِالْغَيْبِ وَيَقُولُونَ سَبْعَةٌ وَثَامِنُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ قُل رَّبِّي أَعْلَمُ بِعِدَّتِهِم مَّا يَعْلَمُهُمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلٌ

sa-yaqūlūna thalāthatun rābiʿuhum kalbuhum wa-yaqūlūna khamsatun sādisuhum kalbuhum rajman bi-al-ghaybi wa-yaqūlūna sabʿatun wa-thāminuhum kalbuhum qul rabbī aʿlamu bi-ʿiddatihim mā yaʿlamuhum illā qalīl

“They will say: Three, their dog the fourth; and they say: Five, their dog the sixth—guessing about the unseen. And they say: Seven, their dog the eighth. Say: My Lord knows their number best; only a few know them.”

Rajman is an accusative verbal noun. In rajman bi-al-ghayb it denotes speaking from conjecture without sufficient knowledge.

Al-ghayb concerns a matter outside the speakers’ direct knowledge. They discuss the companions’ number, but lack clear grounds for some of their claims.

The connection with the material sense is understandable: as someone hurls a stone towards a target, speech may be flung towards an unseen subject without being grounded in knowledge.

The image should not replace the expression’s actual meaning. The verse concerns guesswork and claims to know something about which the speakers lack sufficient knowledge, rather than throwing stones.

Study of GH Y B

Distinguishing guesswork from research

Rajman bi-al-ghayb does not mean that every scientific hypothesis or possibility is forbidden.

A person may ask about an unknown matter, formulate a hypothesis, collect evidence, and state a provisional conclusion with a clear degree of confidence.

What this context criticizes is presenting insufficiently supported speech about a subject whose reality is unclear to the speaker.

There is an important difference between these statements:

“I think this may be the case, and here is my evidence.”

And:

“This certainly happened,” when the speaker lacks sufficient evidence.

Merely saying “probably” does not turn a wholly unsupported assertion into valid research. A hypothesis needs real evidence, with the limits of knowledge and ignorance made clear.

Later in Al-Kahf 18:22, the Qur’an says:

قُل رَّبِّي أَعْلَمُ بِعِدَّتِهِم مَّا يَعْلَمُهُمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلٌ

qul rabbī aʿlamu bi-ʿiddatihim mā yaʿlamuhum illā qalīl

“Say: My Lord knows their number best; only a few know them.”

This redirects attention from unsupported assertions towards actual knowledge.

Nor should the placement of rajman bi-al-ghayb after the first two reports, without further examination, establish the third as certainly correct. After reporting the third, the verse likewise attributes full knowledge of their number to The God.

The core meaning here is therefore casting out speech conjecturally and without sufficient knowledge, rather than throwing stones or every form of thinking about unknown matters.

4. Rajīm: driven away, accursed, and excluded

Rajīm occurs six times in the Qur’an, always describing Satan or Iblīs.

Morphologically, rajīm follows the faʿīl pattern and has a passive sense here: one subjected to rajm, or driven and cast away.

It is close in meaning to marjūm, though its morphological form differs.

The faʿīl pattern alone does not establish intensity, eternal duration, or a particular kind of punishment. These require evidence from the verses’ contexts.

Iblīs commanded to leave

Al-Ḥijr 15:34 and Al-Ḥijr 15:35 state:

قَالَ فَاخْرُجْ مِنْهَا فَإِنَّكَ رَجِيمٌ ۝ وَإِنَّ عَلَيْكَ اللَّعْنَةَ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ

qāla fa-ukhruj minhā fa-innaka rajīm / wa-inna ʿalayka al-laʿnata ilā yawmi al-dīn

“He said: Then leave it, for you are cast out; and the curse is upon you until the Day of Recompense.”

Ṣād 38:77 and Ṣād 38:78 likewise state:

قَالَ فَاخْرُجْ مِنْهَا فَإِنَّكَ رَجِيمٌ ۝ وَإِنَّ عَلَيْكَ لَعْنَتِي إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ

qāla fa-ukhruj minhā fa-innaka rajīm / wa-inna ʿalayka laʿnatī ilā yawmi al-dīn

“He said: Then leave it, for you are cast out; and My curse is upon you until the Day of Recompense.”

Both passages place three elements together: the command to leave, the description rajīm, and the declaration of a curse.

This context supports “driven away” or “cast out” for rajīm.

“Stoned,” if understood as the execution of a bodily punishment with stones, does not convey the passage’s full point. The Qur’an describes Iblīs being expelled and placed under a curse.

Rajīm and laʿnah nevertheless have different roots. Their occurrence together shows a semantic connection without erasing every lexical distinction.

Seeking refuge from Satan the outcast

In the account of Mary’s mother, part of Āl ʿImrān 3:36 reads:

وَإِنِّي أُعِيذُهَا بِكَ وَذُرِّيَّتَهَا مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ الرَّجِيمِ

wa-innī uʿīdhuhā bika wa-dhurriyyatahā mina al-shayṭāni al-rajīm

“And I place her and her offspring under Your protection from Satan the outcast.”

Al-Naḥl 16:98 likewise states:

فَإِذَا قَرَأْتَ الْقُرْآنَ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ الرَّجِيمِ

fa-idhā qaraʾta al-qurʾāna fa-istaʿidh bi-allāhi mina al-shayṭāni al-rajīm

“Then when you recite the Qur’an, seek refuge with The God from Satan the outcast.”

Al-rajīm describes Satan here in relation to his standing before The God.

The description does not name a particular form of bodily harm here. The reader seeks refuge from Satan; the verse does not command throwing stones at a material being.

Satan the outcast and the heaven

Al-Ḥijr 15:17 states:

وَحَفِظْنَاهَا مِن كُلِّ شَيْطَانٍ رَّجِيمٍ

wa-ḥafiẓnāhā min kulli shayṭānin rajīm

“And We guarded it from every outcast devil.”

The continuation in Al-Ḥijr 15:18 states:

إِلَّا مَنِ اسْتَرَقَ السَّمْعَ فَأَتْبَعَهُ شِهَابٌ مُّبِينٌ

illā mani istaraqa al-samʿa fa-atbaʿahu shihābun mubīn

“Except one who steals a hearing, whereupon a clear flame follows him.”

Rajīm appears in a context of guarding the heaven and preventing devils’ access.

These verses show exclusion accompanied by an image of repelling and pursuing. The material nature of the shihāb and its relationship with the beings mentioned are not determined by rajīm alone.

Shayṭānin rajīm and the Qur’an’s origin

Al-Takwīr 81:25 states:

وَمَا هُوَ بِقَوْلِ شَيْطَانٍ رَّجِيمٍ

wa-mā huwa bi-qawli shayṭānin rajīm

“And it is not the speech of an outcast devil.”

Rajīm appears in a context denying that the Qur’anic message is attributable to a devil.

Its appropriate sense is therefore Satan’s familiar description: driven away and cast out. There is no indication that the expression concerns only bodily stoning.

Overall, rajīm is a condemnatory Qur’anic description of Satan associated with exclusion, expulsion, and a curse. The Qur’an’s own usages support this meaning, rather than an external interpretation alone.

5. Rujūman li-al-shayāṭīn: what is used for pelting

Al-Mulk 67:5 states:

وَلَقَدْ زَيَّنَّا السَّمَاءَ الدُّنْيَا بِمَصَابِيحَ وَجَعَلْنَاهَا رُجُومًا لِّلشَّيَاطِينِ وَأَعْتَدْنَا لَهُمْ عَذَابَ السَّعِيرِ

wa-laqad zayyannā al-samāʾa al-dunyā bi-maṣābīḥa wa-jaʿalnāhā rujūman li-al-shayāṭīni wa-aʿtadnā lahum ʿadhāba al-saʿīr

“And We certainly adorned the nearest heaven with lamps and made them means of driving away and targeting the devils, and We prepared for them the punishment of the blazing fire.”

Rujūman belongs to the rajm family. In a common morphological explanation it is the plural of rajm meaning something used for pelting: things hurled at a target, rather than necessarily a launching device or implement. An alternative analysis treats it as a pluralized verbal noun referring to kinds of rajm.

Lexically, this connects with the material sense of casting something towards a target.

The verse does not simply concern earthly stones; it speaks of the heaven’s maṣābīḥ, “lamps,” and their relationship with devils.

Several points should be distinguished.

First, in the common reading, the pronoun hā in jaʿalnāhā refers to maṣābīḥ in the preceding clause. The relationship between pelting and the heavenly lamps is therefore present in the verse’s construction and should not disappear from translation. Determining how that relationship works is a separate interpretive question.

Second, rujūman describes a use for repelling and targeting, without explaining its physical mechanism.

Third, other verses mention shihāb. Part of Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10, for example, reads:

فَأَتْبَعَهُ شِهَابٌ ثَاقِبٌ

fa-atbaʿahu shihābun thāqib

“Then a piercing flame pursued him.”

And part of Al-Ḥijr 15:18 reads:

فَأَتْبَعَهُ شِهَابٌ مُّبِينٌ

fa-atbaʿahu shihābun mubīn

“Then a clear flame pursued him.”

These verses matter to the connection between rajm and shihāb. Taken together, however, they do not justify a conclusive theory, without independent evidence, about stars breaking away, large celestial bodies moving, devils’ physical composition, or how they are struck.

Conversely, difficulty understanding the event’s mechanism does not justify removing the Qur’anic image completely and translating rujūman merely as “conjectures” or “thoughts.”

Rajm meaning conjectural speech in Al-Kahf 18:22 does not establish the suitability of that same meaning in Al-Mulk 67:5. Al-Mulk concerns maṣābīḥ and targeting devils; Al-Kahf concerns human statements about an unknown matter.

“What is used for pelting and targeting,” or “projectiles,” therefore accords with this lexical reading. Such wording does not independently determine the event’s physical nature.

6. Legal usage and distinctions from nearby roots

A familiar use of rajm in post-Qur’anic jurisprudence is stoning as a punishment for zinā, unlawful sexual intercourse.

Lexically, this relates to the root’s material sense: stoning is a severe form of throwing stones at a person.

Its presence in language or jurisprudence, however, does not establish that the Qur’an itself prescribes it as a punishment for zinā.

Reporting conduct is different from commanding it

In the accounts of Shuʿayb, Noah, Abraham, the cave’s companions, the messengers, and Moses, rajm appears in settings of threats, harm, or fear of violence.

These usages show that the Qur’an recognizes rajm and uses it to express threats against people.

None of this root’s Qur’anic occurrences commands believers to stone a woman or man for zinā.

By contrast, part of Al-Nūr 24:2 explicitly addressing punishment for zinā reads:

الزَّانِيَةُ وَالزَّانِي فَاجْلِدُوا كُلَّ وَاحِدٍ مِّنْهُمَا مِائَةَ جَلْدَةٍ

al-zāniyatu wa-al-zānī fa-ijlidū kulla wāḥidin minhumā miʾata jaldah

“The woman and the man who commit zinā: lash each of them a hundred lashes.”

The word rajm is absent. The prescribed action is expressed by fa-ijlidū, “lash,” with a specified amount.

Study of J L D

The distinction between reporting a threat of stoning and issuing a legislative command matters. A people threatening to stone their prophet does not establish that The God authorizes stoning as a religious punishment.

Equally, lexical study should not replace complete legal and Qur’anic examination of punishment for zinā.

Forqan’s separate article, “Stoning: A Qur’anic Ruling or a Rule Derived from Reports?”, studies this in detail through verses concerning zinā, testimony, mutual oaths, iḥṣān, and punishments.

The lexical and Qur’anic conclusion here is limited and clear: rajm occurs in the Qur’an, but none of its occurrences commands stoning a person for zinā.

R J M (rajm) and R M Y (ramy)

Ramy denotes casting or throwing and can concern objects, shooting arrows, or making an accusation.

Part of Al-Nūr 24:4 reads:

وَالَّذِينَ يَرْمُونَ الْمُحْصَنَاتِ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَأْتُوا بِأَرْبَعَةِ شُهَدَاءَ

wa-alladhīna yarmūna al-muḥṣanāti thumma lam yaʾtū bi-arbaʿati shuhadāʾ

“And those who accuse chaste women, then do not bring four witnesses…”

Yarmūna belongs to R M Y and means accusing here, rather than stoning.

This shows throwing and making accusations sharing a field of imagery in Arabic. The two roots nevertheless differ in structure and range of usage.

In its principal material sense, rajm concerns throwing stones. Ramy is more general; context specifies the kind of throwing or the thing thrown.

R J M (rajm), L ʿ N (laʿn), and Ṭ R D (ṭard)

Rajīm as a description of Satan relates to a curse and expulsion, but the three words differ in root and structure.

Al-Ḥijr 15:34 and Al-Ḥijr 15:35 place the command to leave, the description rajīm, and the declaration of a curse alongside each other.

This association shows rajm in an extended sense connected with exclusion and driving away.

It does not make rajm synonymous with laʿn in every instance. Threatening Shuʿayb or Noah with stoning is a different usage from seeking refuge from Satan the outcast.

Study of L ʿ N; Study of Ṭ R D

R J M (rajm) and Ẓ N N (ẓann)

Ẓann concerns supposition or belief and, depending on context, can involve different degrees of confidence or conception.

Rajman bi-al-ghayb, however, concerns casting out conjectural speech about an unknown matter.

Not every ẓann can therefore be called rajm. Al-Kahf concerns claims about the cave’s companions’ number, some of which lack sufficient support.

Rajm here concerns the manner of speaking and the lack of sufficient knowledge. It does not mean the root denotes only conjecture in every usage.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish material action, speech usages, and Satan’s description.

Rajama: threw stones at, targeted with stones, or stoned.

Rajm: pelting with stones or stoning; in speech usages, conjectural talk or insults and verbal assault.

Marjūm: stoned, or someone subjected to rajm.

Rajīm: driven away, cast out. In descriptions of Satan, “Satan the outcast” is appropriate.

Rujūm: things hurled for pelting and targeting; in the common morphological reading, the plural of rajm in the sense of something used for pelting.

Rajman bi-al-ghayb: guessing about the unseen; speaking without sufficient knowledge.

Rijām and rujmah: collected stones or a heap of stones, with particular uses in marking places and everyday implements. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Murajjam: in expressions such as al-ḥadīth al-murajjam, speech grounded in conjecture. This form likewise does not occur in the Qur’an.

Threats should be translated according to context. La-rajamnāka in Hūd 11:91, for example, is naturally rendered “we would certainly have stoned you.”

For Maryam 19:46 and Al-Dukhān 44:20, the explanation should also preserve the possibility of verbal assault, avoiding certainty about what the context alone does not settle.

In Al-Kahf 18:22, rajman bi-al-ghayb should convey conjecture, rather than stoning.

In Al-Mulk 67:5, rujūman li-al-shayāṭīn concerns means of pelting and repelling devils. It should not be equated with conjecture in Al-Kahf.

8. Conclusion

In its clear material sense, R J M denotes throwing stones at a target. The family also has established Arabic usages involving threats, insults, driving away, and conjectural speech.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s pre-Islamic poetic witness shows a history of conjectural-speech usage in Arabic. It was not devised solely to explain a verse.

The root has several distinct Qur’anic usages.

In the prophets’ accounts and that of the cave’s companions, rajm concerns threatened violence and pressure against people. Stoning is often clear or highly plausible, though some expressions also permit severe verbal assault.

In Al-Kahf 18:22, rajman bi-al-ghayb means speaking about something unknown without sufficient knowledge. This should not be equated with material stoning.

In passages concerning Iblīs and Satan, rajīm describes a state connected with expulsion, exclusion, and a curse. It should be understood through the command that Iblīs leave and the other related verses.

In Al-Mulk 67:5, rujūman denotes means of targeting devils. The root itself does not specify the event’s physical mechanism.

In discussing legal provisions, lexical meaning must likewise be distinguished from divine command. The Qur’an recognizes rajm and uses it in accounts, but does not command it as a punishment for zinā. This distinction should remain in lexical study, without the root article replacing a separate legal inquiry.

Final definition: In its material sense, R J M denotes throwing stones and targeting with stones. Extended usages concern hurling hurtful speech, unsupported conjecture, and exclusion or expulsion. Each Qur’anic derivative’s precise meaning follows its structure and context. Stoning, conjectural speech, and being driven away should not be substituted for one another without evidence.
