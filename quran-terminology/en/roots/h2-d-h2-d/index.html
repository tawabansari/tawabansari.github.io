The hoopoe: a bird’s name and its lexical connections with sound and movement

H D H D is a four-letter Arabic root known through the bird’s name هُدْهُد (hudhud) and the verb هَدْهَدَ (hadhada). In the Qur’an, this lexical family occurs only once, in Al-Naml 27:20, as الْهُدْهُدَ. It names a particular bird that plays a role in the account of Solomon.

In Arabic, hudhud is the familiar bird with a conspicuous crest, a slender and relatively long bill, and black-and-white wing markings. In Persian and Dari it is also called hudhud; the Persian name shāneh-be-sar, “comb on the head,” is used as well.

Naturalis, Birds of Europe: the hoopoe’s appearance and call

Lexically, alongside the bird’s name, uses involving repeated sound, moving something, and bringing something downward are recorded. These linguistic connections should not turn the Qur’anic meaning of hudhud into singer, moving creature, informant, or an abstract concept. In the verse, the word is first of all a bird’s name.

1. Lexical forms and the root’s semantic field

هُدْهُد (hudhud): the familiar bird’s name. Its morphological pattern is فُعْلُل (fuʿlul), and its four-letter structure repeats the sequence H D.

هَدَاهِد (hadāhid) and هَدَاهِيد (hadāhīd): recorded plurals of hudhud. Hadāhid with initial fatḥah, when used as a plural, must be distinguished from هُدَاهِد (hudāhid) with initial ḍammah. In witnesses such as Al-Rāʿī’s verse, the latter is a singular bird name. Reports disagree over its identification, ranging from the hoopoe to kinds of pigeon. These uses cannot therefore all be treated as plurals of the same particular species without attending to vocalization and context.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt and Lisān al-ʿArab: hadāhid versus hudāhid

هَدْهَدَ ـ يُهَدْهِدُ ـ هَدْهَدَةً (hadhada — yuhadhidu — hadhadatan): a four-letter verb with several recorded uses:

  • Of a bird: it repeated its call or made a cooing-like sound.
  • Of a mother and child: she gently and repeatedly rocked the child to sleep.
  • Of something brought down from a height: someone lowered it or moved it downward.

In هَدْهَدَتِ الْأُمُّ صَبِيَّهَا, the meaning is a mother’s gentle, regular rocking of her child to put the child to sleep. This use is connected with the cradle and gentle, repeated movement.

هَدْهَدَة (hadhadah) is the verbal noun of hadhada. Depending on context, it may refer to a bird’s repeated call, a pigeon’s voice, or rocking a child.

These uses are recorded in lexical sources including Tahdhīb al-Lughah, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ.

Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ: bird calls, rocking a child, and lowering

Was the hoopoe named after its call?

The connection between the name hudhud and this bird’s repeated call is noteworthy. The repeated H D sequence in the word is also compatible with sound-imitative names in Arabic.

Nevertheless, phonetic resemblance must be distinguished from a certain historical derivation. The fact that hadhada is used for repeated bird calls does not by itself establish that the verb came first and the bird was subsequently named after it, or the reverse.

We can therefore discuss the family’s connection with sound without claiming that the hoopoe’s name certainly originated as an imitation of its call.

2. Early Arabic witnesses and distinctions from related roots

Tahdhīb al-Lughah, transmitting from Al-Layth, states that hadhadah is the hoopoe’s voice. This report shows that the word was also used for the bird’s own call. The same passage describes hudāhid as a pigeon-like bird and quotes Al-Rāʿī’s verse.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: Al-Layth’s report on hudhud and hudāhid

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise describes hudhud as a familiar bird among those that make repeated calls. Besides bird calls, the dictionary records rocking a child and lowering something within the same lexical family.

A witness from Arabic poetry

A verse attributed to Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī reads:

كَهُدَاهِدٍ كَسَرَ الرُّمَاةُ جَنَاحَهُ
يَدْعُو بِقَارِعَةِ الطَّرِيقِ هَدِيلًا

ka-hudāhidin kasara al-rumātu janāḥahu / yadʿū bi-qāriʿati al-ṭarīqi hadīlan

“Like a bird whose wing the archers have broken, calling and lamenting along the road.”

The verse is quoted in Tahdhīb al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab. Hudāhid is a singular name here, and the bird’s identification is disputed; the verse is therefore not decisive evidence for a particular hoopoe species. Ibn Barrī takes هَدِيلًا as expressing the bird’s voice and explains that the person compared to the broken-winged bird is a man whose camels the alms collector has taken. The image expresses his helplessness.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Rāʿī’s verse and Ibn Barrī’s explanation

Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī was an Umayyad-period poet. This verse is therefore evidence from early Islamic Arabic and should not be presented as pre-Islamic poetry without justification. This study did not establish a separate pre-Islamic poetic witness for hudhud whose dating and attribution were sufficiently secure.

The distinction from H D D

The four-letter H D H D should not be equated with the three-letter root H D D.

H D D is associated in Arabic with breaking, collapse, and severe destruction. A Qur’anic example is هَدًّا in Maryam 19:90.

Although some early dictionaries also place words of the hudhud family under H D D because of their repeated letters, this dictionary arrangement does not establish that the bird’s name means destroying, breaking, or collapsing.

Likewise, the use of هَدْهَدَ الشَّيْءَ for lowering something does not justify defining the bird’s name as “one that brings down.”

When studying reduplicated four-letter roots, shared letters, possible historical connections, and the actual meaning of each word must be kept distinct.

Lisān al-ʿArab: entry arrangement and distinct lexical uses

3. The Qur’anic use of hudhud

Al-Naml 27:20

وَتَفَقَّدَ الطَّيْرَ فَقَالَ مَا لِيَ لَا أَرَى الْهُدْهُدَ أَمْ كَانَ مِنَ الْغَائِبِينَ

wa-tafaqqada al-ṭayra fa-qāla mā liya lā arā al-hudhuda am kāna mina al-ghāʾibīna

Suggested translation: “And he inspected the birds, then said: What is the matter with me that I do not see the hoopoe? Or is he among the absent?”

الْهُدْهُدَ is the only Qur’anic occurrence of this family. Grammatically, it is a definite singular noun and the object of أَرَى; it therefore has the accusative fatḥah ending.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence in Al-Naml 27:20

The definite article and the sentence لَا أَرَى الْهُدْهُدَ show that Solomon is speaking about a particular, identifiable bird among those he was inspecting. This usage fits a bird’s species name, without requiring it to be a code name for a person, group, or office.

The hoopoe’s place in Solomon’s story

To understand this use, the verse should be read together with the passage about Solomon in Al-Naml.

In Al-Naml 27:16, Solomon says that they have been taught the language or mode of expression of birds. In Al-Naml 27:17, birds are listed alongside humans and jinn among Solomon’s forces.

The inspection of the birds and the hoopoe’s absence in Al-Naml 27:20 therefore occur within this narrative setting.

In Al-Naml 27:21, Solomon raises the matter of bringing clear justification for the absence. The account then continues:

Al-Naml 27:22

فَمَكَثَ غَيْرَ بَعِيدٍ فَقَالَ أَحَطتُ بِمَا لَمْ تُحِطْ بِهِ وَجِئْتُكَ مِن سَبَإٍ بِنَبَإٍ يَقِينٍ

fa-makatha ghayra baʿīdin fa-qāla aḥaṭtu bi-mā lam tuḥiṭ bihi wa-jiʾtuka min sabaʾin bi-nabaʾin yaqīnin

Suggested translation: “Then, after only a short delay, he said: I have gained knowledge of something you have not encompassed, and I have brought you certain news from Sabaʾ.”

The hoopoe goes on to describe a land ruled by a woman whose people prostrate to the sun. Solomon does not accept the report without examination.

Al-Naml 27:27

قَالَ سَنَنظُرُ أَصَدَقْتَ أَمْ كُنتَ مِنَ الْكَاذِبِينَ

qāla sa-nanẓuru a-ṣadaqta am kunta mina al-kādhibīna

Suggested translation: “He said: We shall see whether you have spoken truthfully or are among the liars.”

Al-Naml 27:28

اذْهَب بِكِتَابِي هَٰذَا فَأَلْقِهْ إِلَيْهِمْ ثُمَّ تَوَلَّ عَنْهُمْ فَانظُرْ مَاذَا يَرْجِعُونَ

idhhab bi-kitābī hādhā fa-alqih ilayhim thumma tawalla ʿanhum fa-unẓur mādhā yarjiʿūna

Suggested translation: “Take this letter of mine and cast it to them; then withdraw from them and see what response they return.”

These verses show the hoopoe first reporting news about Sabaʾ and then being tasked with delivering Solomon’s letter. His narrative role is clear, but these roles are not the lexical meaning of hudhud.

Informant and letter-carrier describe what the bird does in the story. They do not establish that hudhud fundamentally meant spy, intelligence officer, or messenger in Arabic.

Did the hoopoe have a special ability to find water?

Some early commentaries report that the hoopoe could detect water hidden underground and that Solomon needed him for that reason.

These reports are recorded in exegetical sources, but the Qur’an attributes no such ability to the hoopoe in Al-Naml 27:20, Al-Naml 27:21, Al-Naml 27:22, Al-Naml 27:23, Al-Naml 27:24, Al-Naml 27:25, Al-Naml 27:26, Al-Naml 27:27, or Al-Naml 27:28. The verses themselves concern the bird’s absence, his news about Sabaʾ, and his mission to deliver a letter.

The ability to find underground water therefore cannot be made part of the lexical meaning of hudhud or treated as a Qur’an-established characteristic of the bird. Assessing the historical or natural validity of such a claim is a separate matter.

4. Distinguishing the bird’s name from the story’s concepts

An important feature of the Qur’anic use of hudhud is that the Qur’an gives the bird a significant narrative role without changing the meaning of his name.

The hoopoe brings fresh news. Knowledge and information, however, come from أَحَطتُ بِمَا لَمْ تُحِطْ بِهِ and نَبَإٍ يَقِينٍ, not from the letters H D H D.

Solomon tests the truth of the report. Examination and verification come from سَنَنظُرُ أَصَدَقْتَ, not from the bird’s name.

The hoopoe is tasked with carrying a letter. Delivering it comes from the command اذْهَب بِكِتَابِي هَٰذَا, not from the lexical meaning of hudhud.

This distinction is especially necessary when examining animal and personal names in the Qur’an. A being may carry a message or exemplify a behavior in a story without its name lexically coming to mean that message or behavior.

Solomon’s story also has another noteworthy feature: the hoopoe’s report includes a claim to certain knowledge, yet Solomon still treats it as open to examination. This emerges from the connection between Al-Naml 27:22 and Al-Naml 27:27 and illustrates the distinction between claiming certainty and assessing a report’s truth.

This conclusion concerns understanding the story, rather than defining the root H D H D.

5. A translation proposal for Forqan

For the Qur’anic الْهُدْهُدَ, the precise and natural Persian and Dari equivalent is hudhud, the hoopoe.

There is no need in Al-Naml 27:20 to replace it with messenger bird, crested bird, or the name of an official. If readers are unfamiliar with the bird, its appearance can be described in the word explanation.

For the Qur’anic word box:

Word: الْهُدْهُدَ

Root: H D H D

Brief root meaning: The hoopoe’s name and related uses concerning repeated calls and moving something.

Meaning in this verse: The hoopoe: the particular bird Solomon did not see when inspecting the birds.

The full root study can also explain verbal uses such as hadhada, but the verse’s short word box should give priority to the noun’s clear meaning.

6. Conclusion

H D H D is a four-letter Arabic family encompassing the hoopoe’s name and verbal uses concerning repeated sound and moving something. Its different forms are recorded in early dictionaries, but only the noun hudhud occurs in the Qur’an.

In Al-Naml 27:20, the word names a familiar bird. His role in Solomon’s story, from bringing news about Sabaʾ to delivering the letter, is understood from the verses’ context and should not be transferred into the basic meaning of the bird’s name.

The possible connection between the hoopoe’s name and its call can be examined, but a historical derivation should not be treated as certain without evidence. Nor is the four-letter root H D H D identical to the three-letter H D D and its concept of destruction.

A short root definition for Forqan:

Brief root meaning: Hudhud names a crested bird with a distinctive call. The lexical family H D H D also records uses concerning repeated sounds and moving something. In the Qur’an, the word occurs only as the name of a bird in Solomon’s story.
