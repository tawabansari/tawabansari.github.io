Core meaning: in the covering branch, wrapping something in cloth and covering it; in another branch, carrying loads and sharing their transport. Early Arabic also records distinct uses concerning sound and a particular movement.

1. Introduction and meaning in pre-Islamic Arabic

The Arabic root Z M L has several semantic branches, chiefly covering and wrapping in cloth, carrying loads, companionship in transport and travel, and sound.

زَمَّلَ means wrapping someone or something in cloth and covering it. تَزَمَّلَ means wrapping oneself in clothing or a covering.

In another branch, زَمْل concerns carrying loads. زَامِلَة denotes a camel or other animal used to carry baggage and travel supplies. In an early use, زَمِيل denotes someone sharing a mount or participating in carrying loads and working with another person.

One early lexical analysis proposes two origins for this family: carrying a heavy load, and sound.

In that analysis, زَامِلَة illustrates carrying, while أَزْمَل denotes sound. Wrapping in clothing is connected with carrying because a wrapped object resembles a bundle or load.

This explanation establishes a conceptual connection between carrying and covering. It does not, without historical evidence, prove that the clothing sense developed from carrying loads.

Independent poetic evidence matters when identifying meanings in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Pre-Islamic evidence: wrapping in the poetry of Imruʾ al-Qays

Imruʾ al-Qays says in his Muʿallaqah:

كَأَنَّ ثَبِيرًا فِي عَرَانِينِ وَبْلِهِ
كَبِيرُ أُنَاسٍ فِي بِجَادٍ مُزَمَّلِ

ka-anna thabīran fī ʿarānīni wablihi / kabīru unāsin fī bijādin muzammali

Translation:

“Thabir, amid the torrents of rain descending upon it, seems like an elder of a people wrapped in a mantle.”

Thabir is a mountain; وَبْل denotes heavy, large-droplet rain.

بِجَاد was a thick, usually striped covering or mantle made from wool or animal hair.

The poet compares the rain-covered mountain to an elder wrapped in a mantle.

Here مُزَمَّل means covered or wrapped in clothing. The final kasrah in this transmitted wording is traditionally explained by grammatical attraction to the neighboring noun; the description concerns the elder.

This is direct evidence that the family was known before the Quran for covering the body and wrapping in cloth.

In this image, the word does not mean asleep, frightened, ill, or weak. The subject is being wrapped in a mantle.

The poet also draws on an image familiar to his audience. Wrapping in a covering was an independently intelligible meaning in that language.

Further evidence: zummayl in the poetry of Uhayhah ibn al-Julah

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Uhayhah ibn al-Julah reads:

لَا وَأَبِيكَ مَا يُغْنِي غَنَائِي
مِنَ الْفِتْيَانِ زُمَّيْلٌ كَسُولُ

lā wa-abīka mā yughnī ghanāʾī / mina al-fityāni zummaylun kasūlu

Translation:

“No, by your father, no feeble, lazy youth can accomplish what I accomplish.”

Here زُمَّيْل describes a weak, inactive person.

Some lexical analysis connects this use with the carrying branch: a person overwhelmed by difficulty wraps himself up like a bundle. This is a proposed semantic explanation, not an established history of the word.

This disparaging sense must not be extended to every derivative.

In another use, زَمِيل denotes a traveling companion or coworker and does not necessarily imply weakness or laziness.

The quotation thus illustrates one human description, not a definition of the whole family.

What the pre-Quranic evidence establishes

These quotations allow the following account, while keeping their evidential scope distinct:

The covering branch denotes enveloping something in cloth. The cited pre-Islamic poems directly support wrapping and a description of weakness. Carrying loads is also recorded in the lexical family, but these two verses alone do not date every use of that branch.

Imruʾ al-Qays directly supports being wrapped in a covering.

Uhayhah illustrates a description of weakness and limited ability.

Other meanings, including travel companionship, the sound of bows, and a rapid movement, occur in lexical records; these two quotations cannot establish when all of them arose.

The Quran uses the covering sense.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَمَلَ يَزْمُلُ

In the carrying branch, this means taking up or carrying a load.

زَمَلَ الشَّيْءَ

zamala al-shayʾa

“He carried the thing.”

The covering and concealment sense is recorded particularly for the doubled form زَمَّلَ.

زَمَّلَ الشَّيْءَ

zammala al-shayʾa

It can mean “He covered or concealed it.”

Form and context therefore help distinguish the verb’s meaning.

زَمْل and زِمْل

In the carrying branch, زَمْل denotes carrying, while زِمْل denotes the load carried.

زِمْل can also denote a heavy load.

In travel uses, it concerns the equipment and provisions a person carries.

A different vocalization, زَمَل, is recorded for a chant or rajaz recitation accompanying work.

This sound sense must be distinguished from a load.

زَامِلَة

An animal, especially a camel, used to carry equipment, food, and other travel baggage.

زَامِلَةُ الْقَوْمِ

zāmilatu al-qawm

“The group’s pack animal.”

What matters is its load-carrying role, not an implication that it is weak or slow.

Its plural is زَوَامِل.

The word is also used figuratively for bearers of something.

زِمَالَة

This denotes travel equipment and baggage, and can encompass the mount and supplies accompanying travelers.

Shared equipment and a shared mount can provide a setting for companionship, but this does not make the noun identical to the action of sharing.

The action of sharing a mount or load is expressed more directly by مُزَامَلَة.

زَمِيل

In early Arabic, someone sharing a mount or participating in carrying loads and working with another person.

زَمِيلُهُ فِي السَّفَرِ

zamīluhu fī al-safar

“His traveling companion.”

Some lexical accounts distinguish زَمِيل from رَفِيق: the former may share work or carrying, whereas the latter is a broader term for a companion.

This distinction is not an absolute rule governing every Arabic use.

In modern Arabic, زَمِيل can mean a colleague sharing a workplace or task. This relates to companionship and participation, but its modern details cannot simply be attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic.

زَمَّلَ يُزَمِّلُ

A Form II verb meaning to wrap someone or something in a covering.

زَمَّلَهُ بِثَوْبِهِ

zammalahu bi-thawbihi

“He wrapped him in his garment.”

زَمَّلَ الشَّيْءَ

zammala al-shayʾa

“He covered the thing and wrapped it up.”

Here the subject performs the covering, and the object is what is covered.

تَزَمَّلَ يَتَزَمَّلُ

A Form V verb meaning to wrap oneself in clothing or a covering.

تَزَمَّلَ بِثِيَابِهِ

tazammala bi-thiyābihi

“He wrapped himself in his clothes.”

Here the person is himself in the wrapped state.

The verb does not inherently mean sleeping. Someone may wrap up for sleep, cold, rest, illness, or while awake.

Nor does the verb itself establish fear or sadness.

The reason and circumstances must come from context or independent evidence.

مُتَزَمِّل and مُزَّمِّل

مُتَزَمِّل is the active participle of تَزَمَّلَ.

“Someone who has wrapped himself in a covering.”

مُزَّمِّل is its assimilated form.

This is the Quranic form.

The Form V tāʾ assimilates into zāy, changing الْمُتَزَمِّل to الْمُزَّمِّل.

This is a phonological and morphological change; the root remains Z M L.

مُزَمَّل

With fatḥah on the second mīm, this differs morphologically from مُزَّمِّل.

مُزَمَّل can be the passive participle of زَمَّلَ: someone or something wrapped in a covering.

This form occurs in the verse of Imruʾ al-Qays.

In the Hafs transmission, the Quranic الْمُزَّمِّلُ has doubled zāy and kasrah on the second mīm, deriving from مُتَزَمِّل.

The two structures should remain distinct, despite their shared image of being covered.

اِزْدَمَلَ

Recorded for taking up and carrying something.

اِزْدَمَلَ الْحِمْلَ

izdamala al-ḥimla

“He took up the load.”

Here the Form VIII tāʾ changes to dāl after zāy.

The dāl is therefore not an original root consonant.

مُزَامَلَة

Companionship and sharing a load or mount.

In travel, زَامَلَهُ means sharing carrying or riding with someone.

This relates to زَمِيل in the companionship branch.

زَمِل, زَمِيل, and زُمَّيْل

In some Arabic uses, these describe a weak, cowardly, or inactive person.

The description concerns someone lacking strength or energy in a difficult task.

Yet زَمِيل is also recorded for companionship and participation.

Without context, one cannot interpret every زَمِيل as weak or every زَمِيل as a traveling companion.

أَزْمَل

In one lexical branch, sound, particularly mixed sound and the resonance of certain objects.

For example, أَزَامِيلُ الْقِسِيِّ denotes the sounds of bows.

In another use, زَمَل denotes a chant or rajaz accompanying work and movement.

This sound branch is distinct from covering and carrying loads.

زَمَال and زَمَلَان

A kind of running or brisk walking with the body leaning toward one side.

This movement is recorded for animals, especially camels and other quadrupeds.

The movement matters here, rather than carrying or wearing clothing.

إِزْمِيل

A sharp implement used in different settings for carving or cutting.

It is recorded alongside the Z M L family, but its historical connection with covering or carrying is not firmly established.

An unsupported derivation should therefore not be invented.

Of all these forms, only الْمُزَّمِّلُ occurs in the Quran.

3. Quranic usage

The root occurs once in the Quran, at Al-Muzzammil 73:1.

Al-Muzzammil 73:1

يَا أَيُّهَا الْمُزَّمِّلُ

yā ayyuhā al-muzzammil

Al-Muzzammil 73:1

Translation:

“O you who have wrapped yourself in a covering.”

الْمُزَّمِّلُ describes the person addressed.

يَا أَيُّهَا introduces a direct address.

الْمُزَّمِّلُ describes someone wrapped in a covering.

Morphologically, it is a Form V active participle whose tāʾ has assimilated into zāy.

Its direct meaning is therefore “someone who has wrapped himself in clothing or a covering.”

The verse does not specify the garment’s material, color, thickness, or the reason for wrapping up.

Nor does the word here mean prophet, messenger, frightened, asleep, or appointed to carry a heavy load, although the addressee is the prophet who receives the following commands.

The descriptive word’s meaning must remain distinct from the addressee’s identity.

Why begin the address with this description?

The Quran addresses the person through his condition.

Rather than beginning with a personal name, it uses a description connected with his covered state.

Arabic can address someone by a current condition: sitting, standing, or wrapped in clothing.

The word is therefore not necessarily a permanent name or a title independent of the person’s state.

Since the Quran uses it in addressing him, it can also serve as a recognized designation in discussing the surah.

A description becoming a later designation does not change its original meaning.

Does muzzammil mean asleep?

The next verse says:

قُمِ اللَّيْلَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

qumi al-layla illā qalīlā

Al-Muzzammil 73:2

“Stand during the night, except a little.”

The command calls the addressee to rise and act during the night.

The juxtaposition of الْمُزَّمِّل and قُمْ does not establish that the word itself meant asleep in Arabic.

Someone may be wrapped up while sleeping, but being covered is not identical with sleeping.

An instruction to rise can also address someone seated, resting, or in another condition.

What Al-Muzzammil 73:1 establishes linguistically is wrapping in a covering.

Sleeping, if accepted, explains the addressee’s circumstances rather than the root’s meaning.

Does muzzammil mean frightened?

Some interpretations connect the Prophet’s wrapping up with fear associated with experiencing revelation.

This reading relies on an account of his circumstances when revelation began.

Al-Muzzammil 73:1 itself contains no word meaning fear or anxiety.

The Quranic covering form does not itself mean becoming frightened; other derivatives describing weakness or cowardice should not be transferred to it.

People wrap up for many reasons, including cold, rest, sleep, or a need for covering.

One possible cause cannot replace the lexical definition.

For direct translation, “O you who have wrapped yourself in a covering” is sufficient.

Any account of the cause must be examined separately from the word’s meaning.

4. Relationship to the opening commands

Al-Muzzammil 73:2: standing at night

The address يَا أَيُّهَا الْمُزَّمِّلُ is followed by قُمِ اللَّيْلَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا.

This sequence moves from a covered state to rising and carrying out a nighttime task.

Al-Muzzammil 73:3 and Al-Muzzammil 73:4 explain its duration and manner.

Al-Muzzammil 73:4 says:

أَوْ زِدْ عَلَيْهِ وَرَتِّلِ الْقُرْآنَ تَرْتِيلًا

aw zid ʿalayhi wa-rattili al-qurʾāna tartīlā

Al-Muzzammil 73:4

Translation:

“Or add to it, and recite the Quran with measured clarity.”

Standing at night is thus connected with reading and attending to the Quran.

Here the description is the starting point of the address, not the subject of every command.

The Quran calls the person wrapped in clothing to rise, read, and prepare for an important responsibility.

This follows from the sequence, and should not be added to the root’s lexical meaning.

Al-Muzzammil 73:5: a weighty word

Al-Muzzammil 73:5 says:

إِنَّا سَنُلْقِي عَلَيْكَ قَوْلًا ثَقِيلًا

innā sa-nulqī ʿalayka qawlan thaqīlā

Al-Muzzammil 73:5

Translation:

“Indeed, We shall cast upon you a weighty word.”

This announces a weighty word.

It can relate to the great responsibility of receiving and conveying the divine message.

The lexical family also has a branch concerning carrying loads and bearing weight.

This proximity may invite literary reflection: a wrapped person is called to rise and then told of a weighty word.

That literary connection does not make الْمُزَّمِّل directly mean bearer of revelation or one shouldering the prophetic mission.

In Al-Muzzammil 73:5, weight comes from ثَقِيلًا.

In Al-Muzzammil 73:1, the description directly concerns wrapping in a covering.

Reflection on the surah may connect them, but translation should not substitute one for the other.

Al-Muzzammil 73:6: the place of night

Al-Muzzammil 73:6 discusses a quality of rising and activity at night.

It relates to the preceding commands about standing at night and reciting the Quran.

The word muzzammil itself does not name a nighttime practice or a particular act of worship.

Nor does it specify a time or number of prayers.

Timing and manner come from the subsequent commands.

Al-Muzzammil 73:20 also discusses the addressee and his companions’ actual situation, the duration of nighttime standing, life’s difficulties, and divine accommodation.

It helps explain the scope and practice of the opening command without changing the lexical meaning of الْمُزَّمِّل.

5. Outward covering and spiritual responsibility

The opening uses a concrete human condition to address the Prophet.

Wrapping in clothing is an ordinary action, whether for rest, warmth, or covering the body.

The Quran addresses him in this condition, then commands rising and reading the Quran.

The sequence can invite reflection on moving from personal comfort to fulfilling responsibility.

This does not condemn rest or clothing themselves.

The Quranic description is not disparaging; being wrapped in clothing is not inherently a sign of laziness, fear, or irresponsibility.

The separate use of زَمِيل for someone weak or inactive should not be transferred to الْمُزَّمِّل.

The command to rise at night does not deny the human need for sleep and rest. The surah’s ending recognizes differing circumstances and abilities.

The opening should therefore be read alongside the surah’s commands and explanations, without a moral judgment based merely on being wrapped up.

6. Distinctions from related roots

Z M L (تَزَمَّلَ) and D TH R (تَدَثَّرَ)

Al-Muddaththir 74:1 says:

يَا أَيُّهَا الْمُدَّثِّرُ

yā ayyuhā al-muddaththir

Al-Muddaththir 74:1

Translation:

“O you who have wrapped yourself in a covering.”

الْمُدَّثِّر belongs to D TH R and here concerns clothing and wrapping in a covering.

The direct meanings of the two descriptions are very close.

Both address someone through his covered condition.

Their roots nevertheless differ.

D TH R has established uses for a covering or garment placed over clothing or the body.

Z M L also has branches concerning loads and sharing their transport.

That lexical difference alone does not prove that the two surahs refer to entirely different kinds of clothing.

Al-Muddaththir 74:2 commands قُمْ فَأَنذِرْ: “Rise and warn.”

Al-Muzzammil 73:2 likewise commands rising at night.

The two addresses are close in imagery and in their movement toward action.

This does not merge their roots or make all the commands in the two surahs identical.

Z M L (تَزَمَّلَ) and L B S (لِبَاس)

لِبَاس concerns clothing and covering the body, with figurative Quranic uses as well.

تَزَمُّل denotes wrapping oneself in a covering.

Someone may wear clothing without wrapping it around the whole body.

Ordinarily, wearing clothing and wrapping oneself in it are not entirely identical actions.

In Al-Muzzammil 73:1, “wrapped yourself in a covering” is more precise than “put on clothes,” preserving the verb’s particular sense.

Z M L (زَمْل) and H M L (حَمْل)

حَمْل denotes taking up and carrying something and has a broad range of uses.

One branch of Z M L also concerns carrying loads.

This is clear in زَامِلَة, an animal used to carry equipment.

Sharing this sense does not make every derivative of both roots identical.

The Quranic use concerns wrapping in a covering, not carrying an object.

The carrying branch should not become the primary translation of الْمُزَّمِّل.

Z M L (زَمِيل) and R F Q (رَفِيق)

رَفِيق denotes a companion.

زَمِيل may also denote a companion, with shared travel, riding, or carrying prominent in early uses.

The two words are consequently close in some settings.

Yet not every companion must be a زَمِيل, and the latter does not always imply friendship or affection.

Even today, two people can be colleagues without being friends.

Shared work and emotional companionship are related but distinct concepts.

Z M L (زَمْل) and S T R (سَتْر)

سَتْر concerns covering and concealing.

تَزَمُّل also concerns covering the body.

سَتْر extends to different kinds of concealment, from covering an object to hiding a matter from others.

The Quranic تَزَمُّل presents the specific image of a person wrapped in a covering.

Being covered is shared ground; wrapping in cloth is the more specific feature of the Quranic form.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents distinguish the principal forms. These research proposals do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

زَمَلَ: carried a load. زَمَّلَ: wrapped or concealed something in the covering branch.

زَمْل: carrying; زِمْل: a load; زَمَل: a chant or rajaz in a separate use.

زَامِلَة: a pack animal, especially a camel carrying travel equipment.

زَوَامِل: pack animals; figuratively, bearers of something.

زِمَالَة: travel equipment, baggage, and the accompanying mount and supplies.

زَمِيل: a companion or partner in travel or work; in other uses, a weak or inactive person.

زَمَّلَ: wrapped someone or something in a covering.

تَزَمَّلَ: wrapped himself in clothing or a covering.

مُتَزَمِّل: someone who has wrapped himself in a covering.

مُزَّمِّل: the assimilated form of مُتَزَمِّل, retaining the wrapping sense; its form alone does not establish freedom of choice or motivation.

مُزَمَّل: wrapped in a covering; the corresponding passive participle.

اِزْدَمَلَ: took up and carried a load.

مُزَامَلَة: sharing a load or mount.

أَزْمَل: sound or mixed sound in the auditory branch.

زَمَال and زَمَلَان: a movement or run with the body leaning toward one side.

إِزْمِيل: a sharp carving or cutting implement in the recorded use.

Proposed translation of Al-Muzzammil 73:1

يَا أَيُّهَا الْمُزَّمِّلُ

yā ayyuhā al-muzzammil

Al-Muzzammil 73:1

“O you who have wrapped yourself in a covering.”

This preserves the word’s direct meaning.

A shorter “O wrapped one” is possible, but the first wording makes the reflexive sense clearer.

A brief root box can say:

“Wrapping in a covering; in another branch, carrying loads.”

For the verse-specific meaning:

“Someone who has wrapped himself in clothing or a covering.”

“O sleeper,” “O frightened one,” or “O bearer of the prophetic burden” should not replace the direct translation.

Sleep, fear, or weighty responsibility, when discussed contextually, require their own evidence.

8. Conclusion

Early Arabic records several branches: wrapping in a covering, carrying loads, companionship in transport and travel, and sound.

The pre-Islamic quotation from Imruʾ al-Qays clearly describes someone wrapped in a mantle or covering.

This meaning predates the Quran and need not be derived from accounts of revelation’s beginnings.

In another branch, زَامِلَة denotes a pack animal, and زَمِيل can denote a companion sharing travel or loads.

Uses concerning weakness, sound, and movement are also recorded, but should not all be assigned a single definite historical origin without evidence.

Only الْمُزَّمِّلُ occurs in the Quran, at Al-Muzzammil 73:1.

Morphologically, it is an assimilated form of الْمُتَزَمِّلُ, describing someone wrapped in a covering.

The surah then calls him to rise at night, read the Quran, and prepare to receive a weighty word.

This context invites reflection on moving from a covered state to responsibility without altering the word’s own meaning.

In its Quranic use, the root concerns wrapping oneself in a covering and being in that covered state.

The word does not inherently mean asleep, frightened, lazy, or bearer of revelation. Such concepts require independent evidence when explaining the addressee’s circumstances or reflecting on the surah.

Keeping this distinction preserves the attested pre-Quranic meaning and prevents historical narrative, figurative interpretation, or another lexical branch from replacing direct translation.
