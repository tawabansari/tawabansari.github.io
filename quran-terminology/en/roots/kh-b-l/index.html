From harm and corruption to damage to the soundness and cohesion of a person or group

The Arabic root خ ب ل concerns causing harm, corrupting something, and impairing its soundness or functioning. This harm may affect the human body, disturb a person’s thinking or behavior, or appear within a group as corruption, disorder, and loss of coordination.

Early Arabic used خَبْل for bodily injury, wounds, and damage disabling a limb. Derivatives also described impaired reason and behavior. When someone corrupts other people’s affairs or brings their condition to ruin, Arabic also speaks of خَبال.

These uses share more than disorder: something with a particular structure, function, or condition suffers damage. A limb may lose mobility, someone’s thinking may become impaired, or a group may lose cohesion and its capacity to cooperate through others’ destructive conduct.

The Qur’an uses this root twice, in Āl ʿImrān 3:118 and Al-Tawbah 9:47. Both concern harm and corruption that people bring, or seek to bring, into a group through their conduct.

Āl ʿImrān warns about people admitted to the believers’ trusted inner circle who spare no effort to harm them. Al-Tawbah describes people who, had they joined the believers’ collective undertaking, would have added only corruption and disorder while seeking turmoil among them.

Qur’anic khabāl therefore concerns corruption and damage within a group. The accompanying clauses identify how the harm arises, the perpetrators’ intentions, and its particular consequences.

1. Forms and semantic core

The Qur’an uses خ ب ل only as خَبَالًا. In both verses it is an accusative noun.

Arabic also records other derivatives of this family:

  • خَبَل and خَبْل: Corruption, harm, and impairment; in some uses, a wound or disabling injury to a limb.
  • خَبَال: Corruption, ruin, and impairment affecting the body, reason, behavior, or a group’s affairs.
  • خَبِلَ: He became impaired or injured; خَبَلَهُ: he harmed him or impaired his condition.
  • خَبَّلَ: He harmed, corrupted, or impaired something’s functioning.
  • اِخْتَبَلَ: He became impaired, or caused someone harm or disturbance; another early branch records borrowing something for use.
  • مَخْبُول and مُخَبَّل: A person or thing suffering impairment or injury; of a person, someone whose reason or behavior is impaired.
  • خَابِل: Harmful or corrupting; in some early uses, the name of a being to whom harm or disturbance was attributed.
  • خُبُول: In bodily contexts, wounds and injuries to limbs.
  • إِخْبَال: In a separate Arabic usage, lending livestock to someone for temporary use of its benefits.

These forms do not all occur in the Qur’an. Its only form is خَبَالًا; the other derivatives help establish the root’s semantic field.

Semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one basic principle for خ ب ل:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى فَسَادِ الْأَعْضَاءِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā fasādi al-aʿḍāʾi

That is, the root signifies damage or impairment to bodily members.

He then explains khabl in relation to impaired reason and behavior as well as bodily injury.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains khabl as corruption and reports Ibn Sīdah’s description of damage to bodily members severe enough to impair walking.

These explanations have two important aspects: damage to a member or structure itself, and impairment of its normal functioning.

A hand, for example, may lose its previous capacity through injury. The damage is not merely a change in appearance: it affects what the hand can do.

Within a human group, corruption can likewise impair cooperation, trust, or the performance of duties.

This relationship does not establish that every historical use began with the human body and later moved into society. Ibn Fāris takes bodily impairment as a basic principle, but the precise sequence in which all branches developed requires further historical evidence.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī describes khabāl as corruption affecting a living being and causing disturbance, including damage affecting reason and thought.

This explanation is close to Qur’anic usage, although the Qur’anic object of harm extends from the individual to a human group.

Taken together, the evidence supports this semantic core: corruption or harm impairing something’s soundness, capacity, or order.

This does not itself determine whether the harm is physical, mental, or social. Its nature follows from the word’s object and the sentence’s context.

2. Early Arabic usage

Bodily injury and disabling a limb

Lexicons record khabl for injury to bodily members.

For example, خَبِلَتْ يَدُهُ describes a hand that has been injured or lost its ability to move.

The expression لَنَا فِيهِمْ دِمَاءٌ وَخُبُولٌ also distinguishes bloodshed from injuries to bodily members.

Here خُبُول concerns wounds and injuries that may involve severed or disabled hands and feet.

Khabl therefore need not be a superficial scratch or passing pain: it can seriously impair a limb’s capacity and soundness.

Nevertheless, the root’s entire range should not be confined to severing limbs. Amputation is one clear instance of severe injury, not the definition of the whole family.

A poetic witness attributed to Maʿqil ibn Khuwaylid

Lisān al-ʿArab attributes this verse to Maʿqil ibn Khuwaylid al-Hudhalī:

نُدَافِعُ قَوْمًا مُغْضَبِينَ عَلَيْكُمُ
فَعَلْتُمْ بِهِمْ خَبْلًا مِنَ الشَّرِّ خَابِلَا

nudāfiʿu qawman mughḍabīna ʿalaykumu / faʿaltum bihim khablan mina al-sharri khābilā

Translation:

“We defend you against a people angry with you—a people upon whom you have inflicted devastating evil.”

Here خَبْلًا is harm inflicted by one group upon another.

The combination of خَبْلًا and خَابِلَا emphasizes the harm’s severity and destructive character.

This shows that khabl does not only describe an individual’s bodily condition: it can also describe harm one group inflicts on another.

Maʿqil ibn Khuwaylid is identified as a Hudhalī poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The lexicons attribute the verse to him, but its exact composition date is not independently established. It is therefore evidence of early Arabic, rather than a witness with a conclusively established pre-Qur’anic date.

Umm Al-Qura University poetry encyclopedia: Maʿqil ibn Khuwaylid as a poet spanning the two periods

Impaired reason and behavior

Arabic used مَخْبُول for someone whose reason or behavior was impaired.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains خَبَل, خُبْل, and خَبَال through junūn, madness, in some usages.

These usages should not be treated as precise medical diagnoses. Early terms such as junūn and khabl sometimes covered different conditions of severe disturbance, impaired behavior, or loss of discernment.

Sources also record literary descriptions attributing a person’s khabl to grief, love, illness, or the hardships of time.

For example, خَبَلَهُ الْحُزْنُ describes someone whose condition has been severely disturbed by grief.

This expression does not establish that every experience of grief or every mental illness is khabl.

Nor is Qur’anic khabāl a diagnosis of mental illness. The verses concern corruption in a human group’s relationships and affairs.

Damage to property and produce

Lexicons also record khabl for damage to fruit and produce.

Damaged produce may lose value or usefulness: part may perish, or it may cease to suit its previous use.

This shows that خ ب ل is not confined to the human body.

Not every reduction in the amount of produce can, without further indication, be called khabl. Some may be harvested or consumed without corruption or wrongful damage.

Harm and spoilage are prominent in this branch.

A different branch: ikhbāl, lending livestock for use

Lexicons also record an إِخْبَال usage distinct from corruption and injury.

Here someone lends camels, a she-camel, or other livestock for a period so that another person may benefit from its milk, wool, or other uses before returning the animal.

This resembles lending something for temporary use.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

هُنَالِكَ إِنْ يُسْتَخْبَلُوا الْمَالَ يُخْبِلُوا
وَإِنْ يُسْأَلُوا يُعْطُوا وَإِنْ يَيْسِرُوا يَغْلُوا

hunālika in yustakhbalū al-māla yukhbilū / wa-in yusʾalū yuʿṭū wa-in yaysirū yaghlū

A translation close to the sense:

“There, if asked to lend their livestock for use, they lend; if asked for a gift, they give; and when they take part in maysir, they choose fat, costly camels.” In the final hemistich, يَيْسِرُوا refers to maysir, the early practice of gambling over camel meat, rather than ordinary trade. The poet depicts it in praise of generosity; quoting the description does not morally endorse maysir.

Al-Aʿlam al-Shantamarī’s commentary on Zuhayr: lending livestock and the verse’s maysir

This verse occurs in the collected poetry of Zuhayr, a pre-Islamic poet.

Its first verb has variant readings: some transmit يُسْتَخْبَلُوا, while others give the similar forms يُسْتَخْوَلُوا or يُسْتَخْلَبُوا.

The transmission of Zuhayr’s verse and wording variants in the notes to Al-Ṭabarī

Despite this variation, early sources clearly explain ikhbāl as lending livestock for use.

This usage should not be read as “corrupting property.” The poetic context praises generosity and helping petitioners, rather than damaging other people’s possessions.

Sharing three root letters does not therefore justify imposing corruption on every derivative.

The precise historical relationship between this lending sense and harm or impairment remains unclear. A Qur’anic root study can record the usage without inventing an origin for it.

3. Āl ʿImrān 3:118: corruption from trusted intimates

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَتَّخِذُوا بِطَانَةً مِنْ دُونِكُمْ لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا وَدُّوا مَا عَنِتُّمْ قَدْ بَدَتِ الْبَغْضَاءُ مِنْ أَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَمَا تُخْفِي صُدُورُهُمْ أَكْبَرُ قَدْ بَيَّنَّا لَكُمُ الْآيَاتِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tattakhidhū biṭānatan min dūnikum lā yaʾlūnakum khabālan waddū mā ʿanittum qad badati al-baghḍāʾu min afwāhihim wa-mā tukhfī ṣudūruhum akbaru qad bayyannā lakumu al-āyāti in kuntum taʿqilūna

Proposed translation for this root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“You who have believed, do not take as your trusted inner circle people from outside your own who spare no effort to ruin your affairs. They desire your hardship and suffering. Hatred has already appeared from their mouths, and what their breasts conceal is greater. We have made the signs clear to you, if you reason.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:118

Here خَبَالًا occurs in لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا.

Understanding it first requires attending to two accompanying words: بِطَانَةً and يَأْلُونَكُمْ.

Biṭānah comes from ب ط ن. Here it concerns people admitted to someone’s or a group’s close inner circle, who may learn private affairs and important decisions.

Yaʾlūnakum comes from أ ل و. Within lā yaʾlūnakum khabālan, it concerns sparing no effort to bring harm or corruption.

Khabālan does not itself mean the effort: it is the corruption or harm they seek to cause.

Study of B Ṭ N; Study of ʾ L W

Why do closeness and trust matter?

The verse concerns people placed in a close, trusted position who spare no effort to harm the group trusting them.

This differs from open hostility in a confrontation.

Someone opposing a group from outside does not necessarily know its internal information, decisions, or relationships.

Someone admitted to its inner circle, however, may gain information and opportunities relevant to harming it from within.

For example, a member of an organization may misuse confidential information to harm others, create mistrust, or deliberately steer collective decisions toward failure instead of honoring trust.

These are understandable examples of corruption in group relationships. They do not make khabāl itself mean espionage, disclosure of information, or administrative sabotage.

The verse does not specify the precise method of corruption. It describes sparing no effort to cause harm and ruin.

وَدُّوا مَا عَنِتُّمْ: desiring other people’s hardship

After lā yaʾlūnakum khabālan, the verse says:

“وَدُّوا مَا عَنِتُّمْ”

That is, they desire the addressees’ suffering and hardship.

ʿAnat belongs to another root and concerns hardship, suffering, and distress.

Khabāl and ʿanat are consequently related here without being complete synonyms.

Khabāl expresses corruption and damage; ʿanat concerns the hardship and suffering someone experiences.

Corruption within a group may cause its members suffering, but this does not establish that every hardship results from another person’s malicious intention or destructive action.

In this verse, desiring others’ hardship clearly indicates the direction of the group’s conduct.

Open hostility and what remains hidden

The verse next says that hatred and hostility have appeared in their speech, while what they conceal within is greater.

This further clarifies their conduct’s negative direction.

The issue is more than different beliefs or disagreement: hostility, intent to harm, and a desire for the other side’s suffering are involved.

The following verse, Āl ʿImrān 3:119, also describes a contrast between outward conduct and a hidden state: some profess belief when meeting the believers but bite their fingers in rage against them when alone.

Together, these verses reveal a pattern of double dealing and concealed hostility.

They do not establish that everyone with a different belief, culture, or social affiliation necessarily has such intentions.

The verse describes people whose particular hostility and efforts to harm are stated.

Applying it to social relationships therefore requires distinguishing different identities or beliefs from demonstrated destructive conduct.

No human being learns all another person’s hidden intentions simply from that person’s religious or social label.

The verse presents signs in conduct and speech and calls for reasoning; it does not authorize attributing malicious intentions without evidence to anyone who is different.

Al-Ṭabarī: trusted intimates, sparing no effort to corrupt, and the addressees’ hardship

4. Al-Tawbah 9:47: corruption and disorder in a collective undertaking

لَوْ خَرَجُوا فِيكُمْ مَا زَادُوكُمْ إِلَّا خَبَالًا وَلَأَوْضَعُوا خِلَالَكُمْ يَبْغُونَكُمُ الْفِتْنَةَ وَفِيكُمْ سَمَّاعُونَ لَهُمْ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِالظَّالِمِينَ

law kharajū fīkum mā zādūkum illā khabālan wa-la-awḍaʿū khilālakum yabghūnakumu al-fitnata wa-fīkum sammāʿūna lahum wa-allāhu ʿalīmun bi-al-ẓālimīna

Proposed translation for this root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Had they gone out among you, they would have added only corruption and disorder, hurrying about among you while seeking turmoil for you. Among you are people who listen to them. The God knows the unjust.”

Al-Tawbah 9:47

This verse also uses خَبَالًا, but its setting differs from Āl ʿImrān 3:118.

Āl ʿImrān concerns a close, trusted inner circle.

Al-Tawbah concerns participation in a collective undertaking and the consequences of admitting people whose intentions and conduct conflict with its purpose.

Why the preceding verse matters

Al-Tawbah 9:46 says that, had they genuinely intended to go out, they would have prepared for it.

Al-Tawbah 9:47 then says that, had they gone out among the believers, they would have added only khabāl.

Verse 47 thus states a hypothetical situation: had these people participated, their presence would have increased corruption and disorder rather than strengthened the group.

This matters because the verse does not say that they actually joined that undertaking and performed every action described.

Instead, it states the consequences of their unrealized participation in a conditional sentence, then describes conduct and motives connected to those consequences.

مَا زَادُوكُمْ إِلَّا خَبَالًا: adding corruption rather than capacity

The verb zādū comes from ز ي د and concerns adding.

An increase does not always mean an improvement.

A group’s membership may increase while its actual capacity decreases.

For example, if more people join a team but deliberately create disagreement, spread false information, or obstruct sound decisions, greater numbers do not necessarily mean greater capability.

The verse likewise says that, had these people joined, they would have added only khabāl.

Here khabāl therefore concerns corruption and impairment within the group’s functioning and relationships.

The word does not itself mean insufficient numbers, poor equipment, or inevitable military defeat. These may be consequences of corruption, but the verse uses other expressions to explain the people’s behavior.

وَلَأَوْضَعُوا خِلَالَكُمْ: hurrying among the group

The verse next describes their swift movement among the believers.

أَوْضَعُوا comes from و ض ع in a usage concerning rapid movement; خِلَالَكُمْ expresses the spaces among the group’s members.

This movement is accompanied by يَبْغُونَكُمُ الْفِتْنَةَ.

It is therefore not neutral movement or mere relocation: the verse identifies its purpose as seeking fitnah.

Fitnah comes from ف ت ن and has its own semantic field. Here it can concern turmoil, discord, and endangering the group’s cohesion.

Khabāl may result from such conduct, but khabāl itself does not mean rapid movement or fitnah.

The verse expresses those concepts through independent verbs and words.

وَفِيكُمْ سَمَّاعُونَ لَهُمْ: influencing others

At the end of the descriptive passage, the verse says:

“وَفِيكُمْ سَمَّاعُونَ لَهُمْ”

Lexically, this expression concerns people who listen to them.

Commentaries discuss two possibilities: people receptive to their words and provocations, and people collecting news or passing information to them.

The first reading concerns destructive speech influencing some group members.

The second concerns information reaching people who intend harm.

Both relate to the verse’s warning, but sammāʿūn alone does not establish organized espionage without further indication.

For direct translation, “people who listen to them” stays closer to the wording; the other possibility can be discussed separately.

Khabāl in relation to fitnah

This verse offers a particularly clear example of the distinction between khabāl and fitnah.

Khabāl is corruption and harm arising within a group.

Fitnah here concerns the turmoil and discord those people seek to create.

Seeking fitnah can therefore be one means of producing khabāl.

The two words should not, however, be merged in translation.

Translating khabālan simply as fitnah weakens the verse’s distinction between damage to a group and actions that stir turmoil.

“Corruption and disorder” better renders khabālan, allowing fitnah to remain in the separate clause where the Qur’an places it.

Al-Ṭabarī: khabāl, hurried movement, and the two readings of sammāʿūna lahum

Why could their absence benefit the group?

Together, Al-Tawbah 9:46, Al-Tawbah 9:47, and Al-Tawbah 9:48 show that some people’s absence from a collective undertaking does not necessarily weaken it.

If someone does not intend to help and their presence would generate discord, turmoil, or diminished collective capacity, their absence may prevent greater harm.

This should not become a general rule for excluding every critic or dissenter.

Honest criticism, reporting mistakes, opposing an unsound decision, and asking for reform are not in themselves khabāl.

Al-Tawbah 9:47 concerns particular people’s intention to create turmoil, their previous conduct, and their presence’s destructive consequences.

The distinction between constructive criticism and deliberate efforts to ruin a group must remain clear.

5. Distinctions from related roots

خ ب ل (khabal) and خ ب ط (khabṭ): impairment and irregular collision

Arabic khabṭ concerns striking, beating, and, in some uses, irregular movement.

Al-Baqarah 2:275 uses يَتَخَبَّطُهُ in an image of disturbed rising.

Khabal, however, concerns harm and corruption affecting the body, reason, or a group’s functioning.

These concepts may overlap in some situations. Someone with bodily or neurological damage may move unevenly, but this does not make the roots synonymous.

Khabṭ fundamentally concerns striking and collision, whereas khabal concerns damage and impairment.

The Qur’anic distinction is also clear: yatakhabbaṭuhu occurs in a movement comparison; khabālan in Āl ʿImrān and Al-Tawbah concerns corruption and harm in group relationships.

Study of KH B Ṭ

خ ب ل (khabāl) and ف س د (fasād): general corruption and impairing harm

Qur’anic fasād has a broad field, including corruption in conduct, human relationships, the earth, the living environment, and social structures.

Khabāl is also a kind of corruption, but its lexical evidence connects it to harm and impaired functioning.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:118 and Al-Tawbah 9:47, this harm concerns group relationships and cohesion.

“Corruption” is consequently a defensible rendering of khabāl, while a root explanation should also retain its harmful and impairing aspect.

No absolute boundary should be invented between the words, such as asserting that every minor corruption is khabāl or that every khabāl must destroy a group completely.

The sentence and context establish the harm’s actual extent.

Study of F S D

خ ب ل (khabāl) and ف ت ن (fitnah): damage and stirring turmoil

These words occur together in Al-Tawbah 9:47.

Depending on context, fitnah may concern testing, pressure, turmoil, or placing someone in difficulty.

Here it relates to the intention and efforts of people seeking to stir turmoil among the believers.

Khabāl is the corruption their presence and behavior would have added to the group.

Stirring fitnah may therefore produce khabāl in this context, but the roots remain distinct.

Study of F T N

خ ب ل (khabāl) and ع ن ت (ʿanat): corruption and hardship

Āl ʿImrān 3:118 contains both khabālan and ʿanittum.

ʿAnat concerns suffering and hardship.

Khabāl concerns corruption and damage.

Someone may cause others hardship by creating corruption, but not all suffering originates in others’ corruption.

The verse uses both concepts to describe a particular group’s conduct and desires: they spare no effort to cause harm and desire the addressees’ hardship.

These expressions illuminate each other without becoming mere repetitions of one meaning.

خ ب ل (khabal) and ج ن ن (junūn): impairment and the condition of reason

Early Arabic used some خ ب ل derivatives for impaired reason and behavior.

Lexicons also sometimes explain makhbūl through majnūn.

This proximity belongs to only one branch of the root’s usage.

Qur’anic khabālan does not mean people’s mental illness.

“Madness” and “disease of reason” are therefore unsuitable for Āl ʿImrān 3:118 or Al-Tawbah 9:47: these verses concern corruption in group relationships and functioning.

Nor should destructive conduct be attributed to mental illness without evidence. Moral responsibility for an action and a person’s mental health are different questions.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable Persian equivalents for Qur’anic خَبَالًا include fasād, “corruption”; tabāhī, “ruin”; and “harm and disorder.”

“Corruption” is concise and close to lexical explanations, although contemporary Persian may associate fasād chiefly with administrative or financial corruption.

Tabāhī can convey severe harm, but some sentences may suggest complete destruction.

Āshuftagī, “disorder,” approaches the disruption of relationships, but alone may weaken the element of harm and corruption.

Depending on context, the short, natural combination “corruption and disorder” can therefore be used.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:118, لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا may be rendered:

“They spare no effort to ruin your affairs.”

This conveys corruption in natural Persian phrasing without retaining khabāl as an unfamiliar loanword.

In Al-Tawbah 9:47, مَا زَادُوكُمْ إِلَّا خَبَالًا may be rendered:

“They would have added only corruption and disorder.”

Here “disorder” clearly connects to the subsequent account of provocative movement among the group.

Both passages should avoid “madness,” “insanity,” or “impaired reason.” Although these meanings occur in one Arabic branch, they do not suit the two Qur’anic occurrences.

Brief root meaning and meaning in each verse

خَبَالًا — Āl ʿImrān 3:118

Root: خ ب ل

Grammar: Indefinite accusative noun in لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا.

Brief root meaning: Harm, corruption, and impairment of something’s soundness or functioning.

Meaning in this verse: The corruption these people spare no effort to cause in the believers’ affairs and relationships.

خَبَالًا — Al-Tawbah 9:47

Root: خ ب ل

Grammar: Indefinite accusative noun in مَا زَادُوكُمْ إِلَّا خَبَالًا.

Brief root meaning: Harm, corruption, and impairment of something’s soundness or functioning.

Meaning in this verse: The corruption and disorder those people’s presence would have created or increased among the believers.

Both occurrences share a brief root meaning; each verse’s context identifies what is harmed and how that harm appears.

7. Conclusion

Arabic خ ب ل concerns corruption, harm, and impairment. This can affect the body, impair reason and behavior, or disrupt a human group’s soundness and functioning.

Wounds and disabling injuries to bodily members are clear lexical uses. Evidence concerning conduct and group relationships also shows that the root’s corruption and ruin extend beyond the body.

Early Arabic also records ikhbāl for lending livestock for temporary use. This should not be forced into corruption; its precise historical relationship to the main branch remains unclear.

The Qur’an uses this root twice, only as khabālan.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:118, khabāl concerns corruption that hostile people with harmful intentions seek to cause in the affairs of a group trusting them.

In Al-Tawbah 9:47, it concerns corruption and disorder that people intending to stir turmoil would have created or intensified within a group.

Qur’anic khabāl is therefore more than outward disorder or disagreement: it is corruption and harm threatening the soundness and cohesion of relationships.

Khabāl itself does not mean espionage, hypocrisy, differences of belief, or stirring turmoil. Those features and actions are established through the verses’ contexts.

Final definition: خ ب ل signifies corruption or harm impairing something’s soundness, capacity, or functioning. Qur’anic خَبَالًا concerns harmful corruption and disorder within a human group’s relationships and functioning. The nature of the harm, its perpetrators, and their intentions must be established through each verse’s construction and context, rather than the root’s independent meaning. Brief root meaning: Harm, corruption, and impairment of something’s soundness, capacity, or functioning.
