Opposition, incompatibility, and confrontation: from contrary qualities and standing against another to supposed supporters becoming opponents

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Ḍ D D concerns opposition, contrast, and incompatibility. Words from this family can describe two things opposed in their qualities or states, or two people whose wishes and conduct conflict.

In its familiar use, ḍidd is something that stands against or is incompatible with something else.

For example, blackness and whiteness oppose each other as colors. Night and day also replace one another, as different conditions of light, at the same place and time.

In human relations, being opposed can go beyond a simple difference and develop into confrontation, active opposition, and enmity.

Yet not every difference is an opposition. Two people may have different tastes without standing against one another.

Besides an opponent, early sources sometimes use ḍidd for an equal or rival. Another branch of the same consonantal root, with different vowel marking, concerns filling a container.

The root Ḍ D D occurs only once in the Qur’an:

ضِدًّا: opposed and standing against; Maryam 19:82.

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • ضِدّ: an opponent, opposite, or incompatible thing; in some uses, an equal or rival.
  • أَضْدَاد: plural of ḍidd; opponents or contrary things.
  • ضَدِيد: an opposite, opponent, or rival.
  • ضَدِيدَة: another form for an opposite, recorded in early sources.
  • ضَادَّ / يُضَادُّ: to oppose or stand against another.
  • مُضَادَّة: opposition and confrontation.
  • مُضَادّ: opposing, incompatible, or standing against another.
  • تَضَادَّ: two things or people were opposed to one another.
  • تَضَادّ: opposition and incompatibility.
  • مُتَضَادّ: contrary to or incompatible with another.
  • ضَدَّ: in one use, to prevail over another in a dispute; in another branch, to fill a container.
  • أَضَدَّ: in an early use, to become angry.
  • ضُدَد: in a particular use, people who filled others’ vessels with water.

These derivatives are recorded in Arabic, but only the nominal form ḍiddan appears in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole nominal occurrence, Maryam 19:82

2. Two semantic branches in Ibn Fāris’s analysis

Ibn Fāris identifies two independent branches for this root in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الضَّادُ وَالدَّالُ كَلِمَتَانِ مُتَبَايِنَتَانِ فِي الْقِيَاسِ

al-ḍādu wa-al-dālu kalimatāni mutabāyinatāni fī al-qiyāsi

That is, this family has two uses that are separate in semantic analysis.

The first branch: opposition and incompatibility

Ibn Fāris explains the first branch as follows:

وَالْمُتَضَادَّانِ: الشَّيْئَانِ لَا يَجُوزُ اجْتِمَاعُهُمَا فِي وَقْتٍ وَاحِدٍ

wa-al-mutaḍāddāni: al-shayʾāni lā yajūzu ijtimāʿuhumā fī waqtin wāḥidin

That is, two contraries are two states that cannot coexist at the same time in the relevant respect.

He gives night and day as an example.

This definition concerns uses such as blackness and whiteness, death and life, and other opposing states.

However, the inability to coexist concerns the same respect and the same subject.

For example, two different surfaces may be black and white respectively. There is no contradiction in this.

What cannot simultaneously hold at one point and in one specified respect is that point’s being completely black and completely white in the same sense.

Thus, opposition is more than simply differing: it involves incompatibility in a shared attribute or condition.

The second branch: filling a container

Ibn Fāris continues:

وَالْكَلِمَةُ الْأُخْرَى الضَّدُّ، وَهُوَ الْمَلْءُ

wa-al-kalimatu al-ukhrā al-ḍaddu, wa-huwa al-malʾu

That is, the other use of this family concerns filling.

Arabic records the expression:

ضَدَّ الْقِرْبَةَ

ḍadda al-qirbata

Meaning: “He filled the waterskin.”

A qirbah is a vessel, usually made from an animal’s skin, for holding water.

Here the verb describes pouring or introducing water until the vessel is full.

This meaning is clearly different from opposition and enmity.

Someone may fill a water vessel for another person; such a statement involves no opposition or hostility.

To construct a single semantic core, one should therefore not reinterpret filling a vessel as defeating an enemy or placing two things opposite each other.

Ibn Fāris himself presents the two branches separately.

In Maryam 19:82, ḍiddan belongs to the branch of opposition, rather than filling a vessel.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the separate branches of opposition and filling

3. Ḍidd: opposition, contrast, and rivalry

Ḍidd compared with khilāf

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes Al-Layth:

الضِّدُّ كُلُّ شَيْءٍ ضَادَّ شَيْئًا لِيَغْلِبَهُ

al-ḍiddu kullu shayʾin ḍādda shayʾan li-yaghlibahu

That is, a ḍidd is something that stands against another thing and contends with it.

Al-Layth then gives blackness and whiteness, death and life, and night and day as examples.

This explanation emphasizes opposition and incompatibility.

However, not all uses of ḍidd entail a conscious intention to prevail.

Blackness has no will to defeat whiteness; the two are opposed in their color qualities.

By contrast, when one person opposes another, they may intend to defeat them or prevent their wish from being fulfilled.

Opposition between qualities must therefore be distinguished from deliberate human opposition.

Is every difference an opposition?

Arabic khilāf covers a wider field of differing and opposing.

Two people may differ in their choice of food, clothing, or means of travel.

Such a difference does not necessarily mean enmity or confrontation.

Ḍidd is more fitting when two wishes, actions, or states stand against each other.

For example, if one person wants a gate opened and another tries to keep it closed, their wishes are opposed.

But if one likes apples and the other grapes, that difference alone does not establish that they are opponents.

Qur’anic research should therefore not describe every disagreement as enmity or opposition.

An equal or rival

Besides the sense of an opponent, Ibn Sīdah in Al-Muḥkam and Ibn Manẓūr in Lisān al-ʿArab record ḍidd meaning a like or equal.

Arabic also records:

لَا ضِدَّ لَهُ وَلَا ضَدِيدَ لَهُ

lā ḍidda lahu wa-lā ḍadīda lahu

Meaning: “He has no equal or rival.”

Here the word does more than emphasize opposition: it may indicate someone capable of standing against another.

For example, two wrestlers may be closely matched in strength and face one another in a contest.

In this situation, rivalry and opposition are connected.

This use does not establish that ḍidd means a friend, identical counterpart, or partner in every sentence.

In Maryam 19:82, ʿalayhim and the contrast with the preceding verse make the confrontation clear.

Ḍadīd and ḍadīdah

Early sources record ḍadīd and ḍadīdah meaning an opponent or opposite.

Ibn Sīdah transmits ḍadīdah from Thaʿlab.

These forms are close in meaning to ḍidd, but are less widely used.

Some sources also use ḍadīd for a counterpart or equal.

Where these words occur, it is therefore necessary to establish whether the subject is incompatibility and opposition or equality and rivalry.

Neither form occurs in the Qur’anic text.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab: an opponent, equal, ḍadīd and ḍadīdah

4. Active opposition and human conflict

Ḍāddahu: he stood against another

Arabic uses ḍāddahu of someone who opposes another person and stands against their wishes or conduct.

Abū al-Haytham gives the example of two people, one wanting length and the other shortness, or one wanting darkness and the other light.

In this situation, their wishes are incompatible.

Yet active opposition does not always mean personal enmity.

Two people may disagree about how to reach a shared goal.

Until one person’s conduct actually stands against the other’s, a difference of opinion alone should not lead us to call their relationship hostile.

By contrast, if one tries to establish something and the other destroys it, their practical opposition is clearer.

Al-Ṭabarī also mentions such a relationship in explaining Maryam 19:82: one person puts something right and another acts against them.

Ḍadd and prevailing in a dispute

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

ضَدَدْتُ فُلَانًا ضَدًّا

ḍadadtu fulānan ḍaddan

Meaning: “I prevailed over him in a dispute.”

In this use, opposition is connected with the outcome of a confrontation.

However, victory and opposition are different concepts.

Someone may stand against another and be defeated.

A verb concerning prevailing does not therefore mean that every ḍidd necessarily wins.

In Maryam 19:82, ḍiddan itself does not mean victory in battle; it concerns the opposition of those expected to provide support.

Anger and opposition

Some Arabic dictionaries record aḍadda al-rajulu meaning that the man became angry.

Anger may cause opposition and confrontation, but is not itself identical to opposition.

A person can be angry about an event without entering a conflict with anyone.

They may also oppose misconduct without acting out of anger.

This subsidiary use should therefore not reduce the main meaning of ḍidd to anger or rage.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: prevailing, anger, and conflicting wishes

5. Witnesses from poetry and early Arabic

Ḍidd expressing a contrast

A poem by Ibn Ḥayyūs, a fifth-century AH poet, contains the following:

وَجَارُكَ ضِدُّ مَالِكَ مُنْذُ أَمَّا
مَحَلَّكَ ذَا تُعِزُّ وَذَا تُهِينُ

wa-jāruka ḍiddu mālika mundhu ammā / maḥallaka dhā tuʿizzu wa-dhā tuhīnu

An approximate rendering:

“Since coming to your abode, your neighbor has stood in contrast to your wealth: you honor the one and hold the other in low esteem.”

The poet describes the different treatment of a neighbor and wealth, expressing the contrast with ḍidd.

This witness is useful for the literary use of the word to express opposition or contrast.

However, Ibn Ḥayyūs was a fifth-century AH poet, so his poetry is not direct evidence for pre-Islamic Arabic or the period of Qur’anic revelation.

This verse alone cannot establish the age of a use in pre-Qur’anic Arabic.

The meaning of opposition and contrast is also independently recorded in early dictionaries.

Ibn Ḥayyūs’s poetry: the contrast between a neighbor and wealth

A witness concerning the proper name Ḍidd

In Jamharat al-Lughah, Ibn Durayd quotes a verse by ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib al-Zubaydī:

وَذُو النُّونَيْنِ مِنْ عَهْدِ ابْنِ ضِدٍّ
تَخَيَّرَهُ الْفَتَى مِنْ قَوْمِ عَادِ

wa-dhū al-nūnayni min ʿahdi ibni ḍiddin / takhayyarahu al-fatā min qawmi ʿādi

An approximate rendering:

“And Dhū al-Nūnayn, the sword, is from the time of Ibn Ḍidd; the young warrior selected it from the people of ʿĀd.”

Ibn Durayd explains that the sword’s name was Dhū al-Nūn; the poet used the dual form Dhū al-Nūnayn to meet the requirements of the verse.

Ibn Ḍidd also concerns a personal or group name in Arabic accounts.

This witness shows the presence of Ḍidd in early proper names.

But it is not evidence for the meaning “opponent,” because Ḍidd is a proper name here.

Nor does connecting the name with the people of ʿĀd, by itself, establish the sword’s actual date or the historical existence of the person named.

Root research must therefore distinguish a witness to a word’s form, its semantic use, and a historical claim.

Jamharat al-Lughah: the name Dhū al-Nūn and its poetic dual form

The limits of poetic evidence

The sources examined did not clearly establish direct, undisputed pre-Islamic witnesses using ḍidd precisely for opposition.

This lack does not show that the word is new, since it occurs in the Qur’an and is familiar in early dictionaries.

Historical research should not fill gaps with invented, uncertain poetry or assign later examples to pre-Islamic times without dating them.

For this root, reliable lexical evidence and the Qur’an’s own use provide clearer and firmer grounds for understanding the semantic core.

6. The Qur’anic use: wa-yakūnūna ʿalayhim ḍiddan

The root’s only occurrence is in Maryam:

كَلَّا سَيَكْفُرُونَ بِعِبَادَتِهِمْ وَيَكُونُونَ عَلَيْهِمْ ضِدًّا

kallā sa-yakfurūna bi-ʿibādatihim wa-yakūnūna ʿalayhim ḍiddan

“By no means! They will disavow their worship and will be against them.”

Maryam 19:82

The meaning of ḍiddan

Ḍiddan is an accusative noun serving as the predicate of yakūnūna.

In the sentence’s structure:

وَيَكُونُونَ: and they will be.

عَلَيْهِمْ: against them, standing in opposition to them.

ضِدًّا: opposed or standing against another.

The sentence thus concerns people or things that will stand against another group.

The preposition ʿalā is important here because it specifies the direction of the opposition.

Ḍidd is more than a neutral difference here: it is confrontation with those who had relied on these deities.

The preceding verse

Maryam 19:81 states:

وَاتَّخَذُوا مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ آلِهَةً لِيَكُونُوا لَهُمْ عِزًّا

wa-ittakhadhū min dūni allāhi ālihatan li-yakūnū lahum ʿizzan

“They have taken deities besides The God so that they might be a source of might for them.”

Verse 82 rejects this expectation.

The contrast between the two verses is as follows:

In the first, people expect their deities to provide might for them.

In the second, those same deities are said to stand against them.

This reversal is also clear in the Arabic construction:

لَهُمْ عِزًّا, “might for them,” contrasts with عَلَيْهِمْ ضِدًّا, “opposed to them.”

The opposition is therefore not only between the nouns ʿizz and ḍidd; lahum and ʿalayhim also mark the change in the relationship’s direction.

What was supposed to benefit them turns out contrary to their expectations.

Does ḍidd mean an enemy in this verse?

Some exegetical accounts explain ḍidd as ʿaduww, an enemy.

This suits the context, because the opposition works to the worshippers’ detriment.

Yet ḍidd and ʿaduww are not the same root.

Ḍidd emphasizes opposition and incompatibility.

In human relations, ʿaduww highlights enmity and hostile conduct.

In Maryam 19:82, the opposition can amount to enmity; nevertheless, “will be against them” is a fitting translation that preserves lexical precision with fewer additions.

Does ḍidd mean a helper?

Reporting early views, Al-Ṭabarī quotes exegetes explaining ḍidd as ʿawn.

This may initially seem surprising, since ʿawn usually means help.

However, those same reports explain that it means helping against the worshippers, rather than helping them.

For example, Mujāhid says that they will assist against their worshippers, dispute with them, and deny their claim.

This explanation is thus consistent with opposition.

It does not mean that ḍidd becomes a friend or supporter of the worshippers here.

Instead, those who were supposed to support them stand on the other side.

Why is ḍiddan singular?

The verb yakūnūna is plural, but its predicate ḍiddan has a singular form.

Early grammatical sources discuss this difference.

Al-Akhfash explains that ḍidd can refer to one person or a group, like certain nouns with collective reference.

Another expression is:

الْقَوْمُ عَلَى ضِدٍّ وَاحِدٍ

al-qawmu ʿalā ḍiddin wāḥidin

Meaning that a group shares one opposition or confrontation.

The singular form of ḍidd therefore does not mean that only one deity stands against the worshippers.

The word can describe the shared position of a group of opponents.

Thus, the Persian “will be against them” is natural and does not need “one enemy” added.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Akhfash on singular and collective reference

7. Disavowing worship: the supporter becomes an opponent

The relationship between sa-yakfurūna and ḍiddan

Verse 82 begins:

سَيَكْفُرُونَ بِعِبَادَتِهِمْ

sa-yakfurūna bi-ʿibādatihim

Meaning that they will disavow their worship.

This phrase uses the root K F R and concerns denying or rejecting the attribution of worship in this context.

The confrontation is then expressed:

وَيَكُونُونَ عَلَيْهِمْ ضِدًّا

wa-yakūnūna ʿalayhim ḍiddan

“And they will be against them.”

These two phrases are connected, but do not mean the same thing.

Disavowing the worship is a position taken toward the worshippers’ claim.

Being ḍidd expresses a relationship of opposition and confrontation.

Someone may reject another’s claim, but rejection does not in every situation mean complete enmity.

Here, because of the preceding expectation of obtaining might through these deities, their disavowal becomes a harmful confrontation.

Must the pronouns refer to manufactured idols?

Many early commentaries take the plural pronouns in sa-yakfurūna and yakūnūna to refer to the deities mentioned in the preceding verse.

This reading suits the structure and context of the two verses.

However, ālihah is expressed generally in the verse itself.

Other Qur’anic verses show that various beings may wrongly be treated as deities, including angels or humans who did not themselves desire such worship.

The verse should therefore not be taken to concern only stone and wood shaped into idols.

Nor should one claim without evidence that all supposed deities will react in exactly the same way, with the same kind of speech, at the Resurrection.

The details require examination of related verses.

The meaning of ḍidd remains clear through these possibilities: standing against those who had relied on these deities.

Different exegetical accounts

Al-Ṭabarī transmits several explanations of ḍidd here:

  • Helpers against the worshippers;
  • enemies and opponents;
  • companions who disavow one another;
  • a source of affliction and harm.

These explanations share one feature: the relationship’s outcome is contrary to the benefit and might that the worshippers expected.

However, they are not all independent lexical definitions of the word.

Affliction may be a consequence of opposition.

Quranāʾ, companions, may concern sharing an outcome.

ʿAwn ʿalayhim describes the opponents’ role.

The basic meaning of ḍidd remains opposition and confrontation.

The translation should therefore not substitute all these explanations for one word.

Al-Ṭabarī: disavowing worship, help against worshippers, and other explanations

8. Related verses: reading the Qur’an through the Qur’an

Al-Aḥqāf 46:5 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:6: the deities become enemies

Al-Aḥqāf states:

وَإِذَا حُشِرَ النَّاسُ كَانُوا لَهُمْ أَعْدَاءً وَكَانُوا بِعِبَادَتِهِمْ كَافِرِينَ

wa-idhā ḥushira al-nāsu kānū lahum aʿdāʾan wa-kānū bi-ʿibādatihim kāfirīna

“When people are gathered, they will be enemies to them and will disavow their worship.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:6

This verse is very close in content to Maryam 19:82.

Both concern deities besides The God and the disavowal of worship.

Yet Al-Aḥqāf uses aʿdāʾ, while Maryam uses ḍiddan.

This overlap shows that ḍidd in Maryam’s context can express opposition amounting to enmity.

Nevertheless, the distinction between the words must be retained.

The verse can clarify a case of enmity, but does not make all Arabic uses of ḍidd exact synonyms of ʿaduww.

Fāṭir 35:14: disavowing the association of partners

Fāṭir states:

إِنْ تَدْعُوهُمْ لَا يَسْمَعُوا دُعَاءَكُمْ وَلَوْ سَمِعُوا مَا اسْتَجَابُوا لَكُمْ وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ يَكْفُرُونَ بِشِرْكِكُمْ

in tadʿūhum lā yasmaʿū duʿāʾakum wa-law samiʿū mā istajābū lakum wa-yawma al-qiyāmati yakfurūna bi-shirkikum

“If you call them, they do not hear your call; even if they heard, they would not answer you. On the Day of Resurrection they will disavow your association of partners.”

Fāṭir 35:14

This verse explains the groundlessness of relying on deities besides The God in two respects.

First, it describes their inability to answer requests.

It then describes their disavowal, on the Day of Resurrection, of the worshippers’ association of partners.

In Maryam 19:82 too, the deities expected to provide might stand against the worshippers, contrary to their expectations.

The two verses illuminate one another.

But ḍidd itself does not mean failing to hear a prayer: Fāṭir expresses this with other verbs.

Yūnus 10:28 and Yūnus 10:29: disavowing worship

Yūnus reports the words of those supposed to be partners of The God:

مَا كُنْتُمْ إِيَّانَا تَعْبُدُونَ

mā kuntum iyyānā taʿbudūna

“You were not worshipping us.”

Yūnus 10:28

The following verse continues:

فَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ شَهِيدًا بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَكُمْ إِنْ كُنَّا عَنْ عِبَادَتِكُمْ لَغَافِلِينَ

fa-kafā bi-allāhi shahīdan baynanā wa-baynakum in kunnā ʿan ʿibādatikum la-ghāfilīna

“The God suffices as a witness between us and you: we were unaware of your worship.”

Yūnus 10:29

These verses show that treating others as deities does not mean that they themselves accept that worship.

A person or being may be raised to the status of a deity without having commanded or accepted it.

This concerns the disavowal of worship in Maryam 19:82.

The worshippers’ claim must be distinguished from the position of those worshipped.

Attributing divine status to someone does not itself show that they wanted that position.

Sabaʾ 34:40 and Sabaʾ 34:41: rejecting the worship of angels

In Sabaʾ, The God asks the angels:

أَهَٰؤُلَاءِ إِيَّاكُمْ كَانُوا يَعْبُدُونَ

a-hāʾulāʾi iyyākum kānū yaʿbudūna

“Were these people worshipping you?”

Sabaʾ 34:40

The angels answer:

سُبْحَانَكَ أَنْتَ وَلِيُّنَا مِنْ دُونِهِمْ

subḥānaka anta waliyyunā min dūnihim

“Glory be to You! You are our protector, rather than them.”

Sabaʾ 34:41

This answer shows that the angels do not approve of humans worshipping them.

Opposing wrongful worship thus does not necessarily mean a prior personal enmity between the worshipper and the one worshipped.

The confrontation may appear when the truth becomes manifest: one party has made a claim and the other rejects it.

Maryam 19:82 also presents such a reversal.

Al-Baqarah 2:166 and Al-Baqarah 2:167: ties of following are severed

Al-Baqarah states:

إِذْ تَبَرَّأَ الَّذِينَ اتُّبِعُوا مِنَ الَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوا

idh tabarraʾa alladhīna uttubiʿū mina alladhīna ittabaʿū

“When those who had been followed disavowed those who followed them.”

Al-Baqarah 2:166

This verse does not use ḍidd, but describes the severing of the relationship between followers and those they followed.

This relates to Maryam 19:81 and Maryam 19:82.

In both passages, a person relies on a relationship expected to bring benefit and support; when the outcome is revealed, that relationship does not work to their advantage.

Nevertheless, distinctions between following, worship, and friendship must remain.

Not every instance of following or affection is worship.

These verses criticize dependence and relationships founded on false support rather than truth and responsibility.

9. Ḍidd in linguistic and logical terminology

Contraries and contradictories

Language and logic also use taḍādd for the relationship between two contrary qualities or states.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī mentions the view that two contraries share a genus but are incompatible in their particular qualities, such as blackness and whiteness.

Logic, however, distinguishes contrariety from contradiction.

For example, blackness and whiteness are contrary colors, but something may be neither black nor white, having a different color instead.

By contrast, saying “it exists” and “it does not exist” about one subject in the same specified respect gives contradictory propositions that cannot both be true under the same conditions.

This distinction helps explain the technical term taḍādd.

However, the developed philosophical and logical terminology of later periods should not be imported directly into the meaning of Maryam 19:82.

That verse concerns the relationship between supposed deities and their worshippers, rather than an abstract definition of two logical concepts.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: contrary things within one genus

Al-aḍdād in Arabic linguistic scholarship

In Arabic linguistic scholarship, al-aḍdād also has another technical use.

Here it denotes words used in the language with two opposing meanings.

For example, in some dialects and lexical accounts, one word may be used for both a light and a dark color.

In these cases, alfāẓ al-aḍdād concerns opposing senses within one word.

This technical use must not be confused with aḍdād meaning opponents or enemies.

The first concerns the relationship between two senses of a word.

The second concerns two people or things standing against one another.

Maryam 19:82 concerns the latter: the confrontation of supposed deities with their worshippers.

10. Related roots

Ḍ D D (ḍidd) and KH L F (khilāf)

Khilāf concerns difference and opposition.

In its main use, ḍidd highlights opposition and incompatibility.

Two people may disagree on a subject without ever confronting one another.

Not every khilāf therefore necessarily means enmity.

In Maryam 19:82, the opposition exceeds a simple difference, since those expected to provide might stand against the worshippers.

Study of KH L F

Ḍ D D (ḍidd) and ʿ D W (ʿaduww)

In human relations, ʿaduww means an enemy.

Ḍidd emphasizes opposition and standing on the other side.

Two people may oppose one another on one issue but cooperate on another.

Enmity, by contrast, can be a more lasting and extensive relationship than opposition over one matter.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:6 uses aʿdāʾ, while Maryam 19:82 uses ḍiddan.

Their uses overlap, but their roots and semantic emphasis differ.

Study of ʿ D W

Ḍ D D (ḍidd) and N D D (nidd)

In Arabic, nidd concerns an equal, rival, or something set against another.

The Qur’an uses andād in contexts of assigning equals to The God.

Some sources also record ḍidd meaning an equal or rival.

In its main use, however, ḍidd emphasizes opposition and incompatibility more strongly.

Nidd can instead indicate something placed in a position of equality or rivalry, even if active opposition is not the subject of that sentence.

Andād in verses such as Al-Baqarah 2:165 should therefore not be translated simply as “enemies” without a reason.

Nor does ḍidd in Maryam 19:82 mean equals of The God: it describes supposed deities turning against their worshippers.

Study of N D D

Ḍ D D (ḍidd) and Ṣ D D (ṣadd)

Arabic ṣadd concerns preventing, turning away, and obstructing.

The Qur’anic expression ṣadd ʿan sabīl Allāh concerns preventing access to The God’s path.

In its main use, ḍidd concerns opposition and confrontation.

These can come close in human conduct, since an opponent may also try to stop another person.

But not every opposition necessarily involves active obstruction.

Some early dialectal reports also record ḍaddahu ʿan al-amr meaning to gently dissuade someone from a course of action.

This limited use does not make Ṣ D D and Ḍ D D the same root.

Study of Ṣ D D

Ḍ D D (ḍidd) and KH Ṣ M (khaṣm)

In Arabic, khaṣm concerns a party to a dispute, contention, or conflict.

A ḍidd can be someone standing against another.

In a dispute, the two parties may be both opponents and disputants.

Khaṣm emphasizes the dispute and its parties more, whereas ḍidd also covers a broader field of opposition between qualities and objects.

Blackness and whiteness may be contraries, but they are not disputants in the human or legal sense.

In Maryam 19:82, the gloss khaṣm can clarify an instance of opposition, but the root’s full meaning is not limited to legal disputes.

Study of KH Ṣ M

Ḍ D D (ḍidd) and B GH Ḍ (bughḍ)

Bughḍ concerns hatred and dislike.

Ḍidd refers to opposition and confrontation.

Someone may hate another and also stand against them.

But inward hatred and active opposition are different matters.

A person can also oppose someone’s misconduct without personal hatred.

Ḍidd should therefore not always be rendered simply as someone who hates.

Study of B GH Ḍ

ضِدّ: an opponent, opposite, or incompatible thing, according to context.

أَضْدَاد: opponents, contrary things, or rivals.

ضَدِيد: an opponent, opposite, or rival.

ضَدِيدَة: another form for an opponent or opposite.

ضَادَّ: opposed or stood against another.

مُضَادَّة: opposition and confrontation.

مُضَادّ: opposing or incompatible.

تَضَادّ: opposition and incompatibility.

مُتَضَادّ: contrary or incompatible.

ضَدَّ الْقِرْبَةَ: filled the waterskin, an independent branch distinct from opposition.

أَضَدَّ: became angry.

ضُدَد: people who filled others’ vessels, a particular early use.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: gentle dissuasion and people filling vessels

For Maryam 19:82, Forqan’s current Persian translation is suitable:

كَلَّا سَيَكْفُرُونَ بِعِبَادَتِهِمْ وَيَكُونُونَ عَلَيْهِمْ ضِدًّا

kallā sa-yakfurūna bi-ʿibādatihim wa-yakūnūna ʿalayhim ḍiddan

“By no means! They will disavow their worship and will be against them.”

The word explanation can add:

Ḍiddan: opposed and standing against; those who, contrary to the expectation of support and might, stand against their worshippers.

“They will become their enemies” is also defensible from the context and parallel verses, but emphasizes enmity more than the basic meaning does.

“They will become a source of their punishment” or “they will testify against them” are exegetical explanations that may be examined through related verses; they should preferably not directly replace ḍiddan itself in translation.

12. Conclusion

Arabic Ḍ D D has two independent branches.

The main, familiar branch concerns opposition, contrast, and incompatibility.

Here two things may be opposed in their qualities, like blackness and whiteness, or two people may oppose one another in their wishes and conduct.

In human uses, this opposition may develop into rivalry, contention, and enmity.

Some early sources also record ḍidd as an equal or rival. This is understandable through the possibility of two sides confronting one another, but should not replace “opponent” in every sentence.

The other branch is ḍadd meaning to fill a vessel. Ibn Fāris identifies it as independent of opposition, and there is no evidence for historically unifying the two branches.

Only ḍiddan occurs in the Qur’an.

In Maryam 19:82, it contrasts with lahum ʿizzan in the preceding verse.

People had taken deities besides The God to provide them with might. The next verse states, however, that these deities will disavow their worship and stand against them.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:6, Fāṭir 35:14, Yūnus 10:28, Yūnus 10:29, Sabaʾ 34:40, and Sabaʾ 34:41 also clarify different aspects of disavowing and dissociating from wrongful worship.

Ḍidd here thus means more than difference or theoretical disagreement: it presents the reversal of a relationship from which a person expected support.

What was expected to support the person stands against them.

This message is understood from the verse’s context; the root’s own lexical meaning remains opposition, contrast, and incompatibility.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Opposition, contrast, and incompatibility; in human relations, standing against another and sometimes enmity. An independent branch concerns filling a vessel.

Meaning in Maryam 19:82:

Ḍiddan: opposed and standing against; deities that, contrary to their worshippers’ expectations, will stand against them instead of supporting them.
