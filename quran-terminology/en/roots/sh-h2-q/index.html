Loud breathing, a prolonged groan and roaring: early Arabic usage and the sounds of the Fire’s inhabitants and of the Fire itself in the Quran

The Arabic root SH H Q concerns two important fields: height and elevation, and breathing accompanied by a loud, prolonged or harsh sound. In the latter field, shahīq denotes breath and sound arising in intense breathing, groaning, weeping or crying out.

Shahiqa can describe someone drawing breath forcefully into the chest or producing a long, loud sound from the throat amid pain or weeping. The word also has an established use for animal sounds, particularly those of a donkey.

In the other branch, shāhiq describes something very tall or elevated, such as a mountain rising above the surrounding land.

The fields can be related through height: physical elevation in one, and the rising or extension of sound in the other. That resemblance alone does not prove the sound meaning developed from the meaning of elevation.

The Quran uses only the noun shahīq from this root. In Hūd 11:106, shahīq accompanies zafīr and is attributed to those in the Fire. In Al-Mulk 67:7, the Fire itself is described as having shahīq, heard by those thrown into it.

This distinction matters. The Quranic shahīq does not exclusively describe human breathing; it also conveys a terrifying, intense sound of the Fire. Its meaning cannot therefore be confined to a particular biological phase of respiration.

1. The root in early Arabic

Prolonged breathing and a sound from the chest

Early Arabic records shahiqa and shahaqa, with different middle vowels, for forceful breathing and its accompanying sound.

Shahīq concerns a sound produced in drawing breath or intense groaning. Shahqah also denotes a sudden, forceful breath or a cry-like sound.

For example, someone startled or weeping intensely may draw breath forcefully and produce a loud sound in the chest and throat. This differs from quiet, ordinary breathing and may be audible, broken or trembling.

Lexical explanations associate shahīq with drawing breath back into the chest, loud breathing and intense groaning.

These uses do not necessarily describe a single situation.

Some concern the direction of breath; others the loudness and duration of sound; still others a groan arising from pain or weeping.

Defining shahīq therefore requires distinguishing respiration from the sound that may accompany it.

Modern technical Arabic uses shahīq for inhalation generally. The early uses examined here emphasize breath or sound distinguished by intensity, loudness or audible quality; not every quiet inhalation conveys that entire description.

Shahīq in a donkey’s voice

An important Arabic use describes a donkey’s voice.

Lexical explanations associate shahīq al-ḥimār with a part of the donkey’s call and contrast it with zafīr.

Some accounts identify zafīr as the beginning of the call and shahīq as its ending. Others relate shahīq to drawing breath inward and zafīr to breathing outward.

The words thus do more than name two simple, silent movements of air. Prolonged, harsh sounds and stages of breathing belong to their field of usage.

A donkey’s bray may have several parts differing in intensity and quality. Arabic employs particular words to describe them.

Shahīq is not exclusively a donkey’s call: it also describes humans, and the Quran uses it for the Fire of the hereafter.

An early poetic witness: tashhāq before braying

A verse attributed to Ḥanẓalah ibn Sharqī, known as Abū al-Ṭamḥān al-Qaynī, reads:

بِضَرْبٍ يُزِيلُ الْهَامَ عَنْ سَكِنَاتِهِ

bi-ḍarbin yuzīlu al-hāma ʿan sakanātihi

وَطَعْنٍ كَتَشْهَاقِ الْعِفَا هَمَّ بِالنَّهْقِ

wa-ṭaʿnin ka-tashhāqi al-ʿifā hamma bi-l-nahqi

Translation:

“With blows that dislodge heads from their resting places, and thrusts like the drawn, harsh breathing of a donkey foal about to bray.”

The poet describes blows and spear thrusts, using the sound of an animal about to bray to convey the thrusts’ intensity and audible quality.

Al-ʿifā here denotes a donkey foal, while nahq means braying.

Tashhāq belongs to SH H Q and concerns a prolonged, forceful sound of breathing.

The poet draws on a recognizable sensory sound to form an image. The use is not merely air entering lungs: it has a distinct audible quality that can be compared with another sound.

Abū al-Ṭamḥān is classified as a mukhadram poet, having lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. This study has not independently established the date of this particular verse.

It is valuable evidence for early usage but cannot be dated with certainty before Quranic revelation without further evidence.

Another witness for zafīr and shahīq

A verse attributed to al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār, also a mukhadram poet, describes a wild ass’s call:

بَعِيدُ مَدَى التَّطْرِيبِ أَوَّلُ صَوْتِهِ

baʿīdu madā al-taṭrībi awwalu ṣawtihi

زَفِيرٌ وَيَتْلُوهُ شَهِيقٌ مُحَشْرَجُ

zafīrun wa-yatlūhu shahīqun muḥashraju

Translation:

“Far-reaching in its drawn-out modulation, its voice begins with zafīr, followed by a rattling, constricted shahīq.”

Zafīr and shahīq describe two parts of a prolonged animal call.

Muḥashraj concerns a sound emerging from the throat or chest with constriction and a rattle.

The words can thus describe one continuing sound without having identical roles or qualities.

Their order in this verse does not establish that zafīr must always occur first and shahīq afterward in every Arabic use.

Al-Shammākh also lived across the two periods; this study has not established the verse’s precise date. It clarifies older usage rather than proving a chronological sequence of pre-Quranic meanings.

Shahīq in weeping and distress

Arabic also associates shahīq with intense groaning and weeping.

Someone weeping intensely may repeatedly draw breath forcefully into the chest, sometimes with a broken, prolonged or loud sound.

Lexical explanations accordingly include a loud and intense groan.

The cause must nevertheless remain distinct from the sound itself.

Shahīq may arise amid sadness, fear, pain or severe strain, but the word alone need not identify which caused it.

Forceful audible breathing might follow weeping, fright or physical strain. Hearing the sound without knowing the situation does not establish its cause.

In the Quranic passages, suffering and terror come from the context of the Fire. “Punishment,” “remorse” or “weeping” should not replace shahīq’s lexical meaning without contextual grounds.

2. Shuhūq: height and elevation within the same family

The other branch concerns height and elevation.

Shahaqa al-jabalu describes a mountain’s elevation; jabalun shāhiqun denotes a strikingly high mountain.

Shawāhiq is also an established plural for high mountains or elevations.

The usage extends to tall buildings and other very high things.

Here the meaning is physical and spatial: being high and rising aloft.

Shāhiq alone does not mean dangerous, sacred, stable or beautiful. Two very high mountains may differ in structure and material; elevation is the feature described.

Height and the sound of shahīq

Early lexical explanations relate a high mountain to shahīq: just as the mountain rises greatly, shahīq can be an elevated breath or a prolonged, rising sound.

This helps explain a semantic relationship between the uses.

Historically, however, resemblance between physical height and heightened sound does not by itself establish which meaning came first.

Arabic can apply height to physical elevation or the intensity and extension of sound. Such a relation is intelligible here, but cannot be made the certain origin of every derivative without independent evidence.

Shāhiq in descriptions of anger

Dhū shāhiq is also recorded for a person whose anger has intensified.

A comparable use describes a male animal producing a forceful inner sound when excited or aroused.

The relation to loud sound and agitation is clearer in this branch.

Anger is not essential to every derivative: a high mountain is not angry, and a person’s shahīq need not arise from wrath.

Each derivative must therefore be examined in its own context.

Limits of pre-Quranic evidence

Elevation, intense breathing and prolonged sound are recorded in Arabic sources. The Quranic shahīq establishes the sound meaning in the language of revelation.

The poetic witnesses illustrate the field in early poetry, but uncertainty about their dates prevents assigning every detail of their usage to the pre-Islamic period with certainty.

The early meanings can be understood, while the precise historical sequence of shāhiq, shuhūq and shahīq remains unestablished by the evidence examined here.

3. Quranic forms and core meaning

The root occurs twice in two Quranic verses, only as the noun shahīq.

Hūd 11:106 has wa-shahīqun: wa- is a conjunction, and shahīq is indefinite and nominative.

Al-Mulk 67:7 has shahīqan, indefinite and accusative.

These are forms of one word; their different grammatical cases do not create a new derivative.

Shahiqa, shahaqa, shahqah, shuhūq, shāhiq, shawāhiq and tashhāq are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Quran.

Both Quranic uses concern sound, but the source of the sound differs.

In Hūd it is attributed to those in the Fire; in Al-Mulk the Fire itself is described as having shahīq.

The core meaning of shahīq in these passages can therefore be stated as:

A loud, prolonged and harsh sound associated with forceful breathing, groaning or roaring.

In humans it can accompany intense breathing. In the Fire’s description, its sound and roar are prominent, without requiring a human respiratory system to be imagined for it.

This should remain distinct from general height in shāhiq. Height helps explain the semantic relationship, but the Quranic shahīq does not name a mountain or physical elevation.

4. The inhabitants’ shahīq in Hūd 11:106

Hūd 11:106 states:

فَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ شَقُوا فَفِي النَّارِ لَهُمْ فِيهَا زَفِيرٌ وَشَهِيقٌ

fa-ammā alladhīna shaqū fa-fī al-nāri lahum fīhā zafīrun wa-shahīq

Hūd 11:106

Translation:

“As for those who became wretched, they are in the Fire; there they have heavy, groaning breaths and long, drawn-out gasps.”

The verse continues the description of the day when people are gathered and their outcomes are discussed.

Hūd 11:105 distinguishes shaqiyy and saʿīd in their outcomes. Hūd 11:106 describes the first group, while Hūd 11:108 speaks of the other.

Three matters are distinct: those with an unhappy outcome, their presence in the Fire, and the zafīr and shahīq they have there.

Attributing shahīq to the Fire’s inhabitants

The structure lahum fīhā zafīrun wa-shahīq is significant.

Lahum refers to the group introduced at the verse’s beginning: those in the Fire.

Fīhā specifies their location.

Zafīr and shahīq thus concern the inhabitants’ condition.

This differs from Al-Mulk 67:7, where shahīq is attributed directly to the Fire in the sentence’s apparent reading.

Hūd focuses on those within the Fire and describes their difficult, groaning breaths.

Zafīr and shahīq

Zafīr belongs to Z F R and concerns heavy breathing, sighing and difficult respiratory sound.

Shahīq belongs to SH H Q and is associated with long, drawn-out breath and its intense sound.

Many lexical explanations identify zafīr with exhaling and shahīq with inhaling.

The accounts are not uniform in assigning precise directions. Some describe different sequences or locations of the sounds; in audible usage the words can also describe distinct parts of a long, strained groan.

A rigid division into two physiological phases therefore does not exhaust the verse’s linguistic meaning.

Together the words indicate intense, unusual breaths and sounds associated with the inhabitants’ distress.

In this proposed rendering, zafīr evokes heavy, sighing breath, while shahīq emphasizes the long, loud sound of difficult breathing. This is a contextual rendering, not an absolute boundary between all uses of the two words.

Forqan’s study of Z F R examines the first word’s historical and Quranic branches separately.

Study of Z F R

Does the word order establish a respiratory sequence?

Zafīr precedes shahīq in the verse.

The conjunction wa- does not by itself establish a temporal order.

Joining two nouns with wa- does not necessarily mean the first event must happen before the second.

The order therefore does not establish that exhalation precedes inhalation in the Fire, or that the verse defines a specific breathing cycle.

It may serve the sound imagery and the pairing of related states; a definite physiological sequence would require independent evidence.

Does shahīq mean punishment?

Hūd 11:106 places shahīq in the context of the Fire and an unhappy human outcome.

The sound is therefore associated with suffering and distress.

The word itself does not mean punishment, penalty, remorse or wretchedness.

A person may also experience shahīq while weeping, frightened or in pain in this life. The meaning remains; the cause and circumstances differ.

Fa-fī al-nār and other expressions establish the afterlife setting, while zafīr and shahīq describe an aspect of that condition.

Translating shahīq as “severe punishment” or “endless remorse” removes its sound meaning and substitutes an interpretive result.

5. The Fire’s shahīq in Al-Mulk 67:7

The second occurrence is in Al-Mulk 67:7:

إِذَا أُلْقُوا فِيهَا سَمِعُوا لَهَا شَهِيقًا وَهِيَ تَفُورُ

idhā ulqū fīhā samiʿū lahā shahīqan wa-hiya tafūr

Al-Mulk 67:7

Translation:

“When they are thrown into it, they hear from it a long, loud roar as it boils.”

The verse concerns those thrown into the Fire.

Unlike Hūd 11:106, the apparent construction here attributes shahīq to the Fire itself.

The significance of samiʿū lahā

In samiʿū lahā shahīqan, the pronoun in lahā refers to the Fire.

The construction attributes the heard sound to it.

The direct translation should therefore preserve lahā, rather than silently turning it into “they hear their own shahīq” as they are thrown into the Fire.

Interpreting the sound as coming from its inhabitants requires an additional interpretive explanation; it is not the same direct grammatical relation.

In the apparent construction, people are the listeners and the Fire is the entity to which the sound is attributed.

This grammatical distinction is a central point when comparing the root’s Quranic uses.

Shahīq and the Fire’s boiling

The verse continues:

وَهِيَ تَفُورُ

wa-hiya tafūr

Al-Mulk 67:7

“As it boils.”

Tafūru belongs to F W R and concerns boiling or surging.

Shahīq thus occurs alongside the Fire’s boiling.

The combination creates powerful auditory imagery: a boiling Fire from which a loud, terrifying sound is heard.

In ordinary experience, large fires, strong airflow and boiling materials may produce sound. Such natural resemblance cannot determine the exact nature of the Fire of the hereafter.

The verse describes shahīq and surging, not respiratory organs, chemical composition or airflow within the Fire.

The attribution should be retained as stated without inventing an unestablished physical mechanism.

Is the Fire’s shahīq merely a comparison?

Arabic can extend a word familiar from a living being’s breath or voice to describe the sound of something else.

The sea’s roar or the wind’s howl, for example, does not necessarily give them human vocal and respiratory organs.

Al-Mulk 67:7 likewise attributes shahīq to the Fire.

The use can be understood through resemblance between its sound and intense breathing or groaning.

That rhetorical explanation should not be taken to make the verse purely imaginary or deny a heard sound in the described scene.

The Quran says those thrown into the Fire hear its shahīq.

The clear textual point is an intense sound attributed to the Fire; its complete nature and physical properties are not explained.

The following verse

Al-Mulk 67:8 states:

تَكَادُ تَمَيَّزُ مِنَ الْغَيْظِ

takādu tamayyazu mina al-ghayẓ

Al-Mulk 67:8

Translation:

“It almost bursts apart with rage.”

Ghayẓ belongs to GH Y Ẓ and concerns intense anger.

This continues the description: Al-Mulk 67:7 mentions shahīq and boiling, while Al-Mulk 67:8 describes anger and almost bursting apart.

Together they present a terrifying image of the Fire of the hereafter.

Anger is expressed through ghayẓ, not through SH H Q itself. Shahīq describes the audible quality.

Shahīq therefore cannot be lexically defined as anger or revenge.

6. Connections with other sounds of the Fire

The Fire’s own zafīr in Al-Furqān

Al-Furqān 25:12 states:

إِذَا رَأَتْهُمْ مِنْ مَكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ سَمِعُوا لَهَا تَغَيُّظًا وَزَفِيرًا

idhā raʾathum min makānin baʿīdin samiʿū lahā taghayyuẓan wa-zafīrā

Al-Furqān 25:12

Translation:

“When it sees them from a distant place, they hear its rage and heavy roaring.”

The verse has a clear connection with Al-Mulk 67:7.

Both attribute a sound to the Fire itself.

Al-Mulk speaks of its shahīq; Al-Furqān of its taghayyuẓ and zafīr.

The Quran thus describes the Fire’s sound with more than one word.

Zafīr and shahīq are related but each has its own semantic field.

Zafīr applied to the Fire in Al-Furqān cannot always be restricted to human exhalation, just as shahīq in Al-Mulk is not exclusively human respiration.

The inhabitants’ zafīr in Al-Anbiyāʾ

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100 states:

لَهُمْ فِيهَا زَفِيرٌ وَهُمْ فِيهَا لَا يَسْمَعُونَ

lahum fīhā zafīrun wa-hum fīhā lā yasmaʿūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100

Translation:

“There they have zafīr, and there they do not hear.”

The structure resembles Hūd 11:106: lahum fīhā zafīr concerns the inhabitants’ condition.

Al-Anbiyāʾ names only zafīr, not shahīq.

The omission of shahīq does not itself establish the absence of every other sound or make shahīq impossible in that situation.

Nor should lā yasmaʿūn be used, without examining context, to build a general theory of permanent loss of hearing among the Fire’s inhabitants.

Each verse describes part of the condition in its own setting.

Different sources of sound

Comparing the verses reveals an important distinction.

Hūd 11:106 uses zafīr and shahīq for the condition of those in the Fire.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100 likewise attributes zafīr to its inhabitants.

Al-Furqān 25:12 describes the Fire’s zafīr, while Al-Mulk 67:7 describes its shahīq.

The sound meanings therefore occur in two applications: the inhabitants’ sound and the Fire’s own sound.

Their shared subject matter must not erase that distinction.

7. Distinctions from related roots

SH H Q and Z F R

Shahīq concerns forceful breathing and its loud, prolonged sound.

Zafīr is associated with heavy breath, sighing and groaning sound.

Some lexical accounts identify shahīq as inhalation and zafīr as exhalation. This division is not identical in every account and does not explain all the words’ auditory uses.

Hūd 11:106 pairs them as related qualities of strained, groaning sound.

Al-Mulk 67:7 and Al-Furqān 25:12 separately apply the two words to the Fire itself.

Translation should preserve their distinction as far as possible without asserting a definite direction of airflow in a particular respiratory apparatus.

SH H Q and N H Q

Nahīq denotes a donkey’s bray.

Shahīq can describe part of that call but is not exclusive to the animal.

Early poetry sometimes places shahīq and nahīq together when describing a long, forceful sound.

Nahīq names the donkey’s characteristic call, while shahīq has a wider field of audible breathing, groaning and roaring.

Luqmān 31:19 mentions ṣawtu al-ḥamīr, “the voice of donkeys,” but the word nahīq itself does not occur in the Quran.

Lexical comparisons between shahīq and a donkey’s voice do not make the Quran identify humans or the Fire as donkeys in their nature.

Comparing a quality of sound does not equate the beings producing it.

SH H Q and Ṣ Y Ḥ

Ṣayḥah concerns a cry or loud sound.

Shahīq may also be loud and intense, but its established uses are more closely associated with breathing and prolonged groaning.

Not every cry is shahīq. A person may utter something brief and loud without any groaning breath.

Conversely, shahīq may occur without a word or definite message.

The roots overlap in loudness but remain distinct.

SH H Q and ʾ N N

Anīn concerns groaning and sounds made during pain, illness or grief.

Shahīq can accompany such groaning, with long breath and prolonged sound particularly prominent.

A groan can be quiet or intense, whereas the familiar uses of shahīq are closer to a conspicuous, unusual sound.

“A long, loud groan” may therefore suit some contexts, while “groan” alone may not convey its full quality.

SH H Q and SH M KH

Shāhiq belongs to SH H Q; shāmikh to SH M KH.

Both can describe a high mountain in Arabic.

Their fields are close in that use, expressing elevation and rising aloft.

Sharing a description of mountains does not make their entire lexical families identical.

SH H Q gives shahīq for intense breath and prolonged sound, whereas SH M KH is associated with elevation and rising high.

Nor should the arrogance found in some human uses of shumūkh be transferred to shahīq.

SH H Q and Z H Q

Zuhūq belongs to Z H Q and concerns passing away, perishing or the departure of life.

Shahīq belongs to SH H Q and concerns breath and intense sound.

A person may make a gasp-like sound near death, but that association does not make the roots identical.

Z H Q describes passing away or departure; SH H Q in its sound branch concerns a drawn breath and its accompanying sound.

Their phonetic similarity does not establish derivational kinship.

8. Questions of understanding and translation

Does shahīq always mean inhalation?

Modern Persian has clear terms for inhalation and exhalation as directions of airflow in respiration.

Many modern uses likewise identify shahīq as taking air in and zafīr as breathing it out.

These physiological definitions should not be made the complete and exclusive meanings in Quranic language without considering the sound field and older evidence.

Hūd 11:106 pairs shahīq with zafīr to describe the inhabitants’ difficult condition.

Al-Mulk 67:7 presents shahīq as heard from the Fire itself.

An exclusively anatomical definition, “air entering lungs,” would fail to preserve the auditory use in the second verse. A figurative use would not require lungs for the Fire, but should be recognized as an extension of meaning rather than a statement of its anatomy.

The definition must therefore retain the word’s capacity to describe sound.

Is shahīq only a sound of suffering?

Both Quranic occurrences are in the context of the Fire, associating the sound with terror and punishment.

Arabic also uses shahīq for animal calls and forceful human breath.

Suffering and punishment are therefore not necessary elements of the lexical meaning.

Shāhiq applied to a high mountain likewise contains no inherent suffering.

The entire family must not be presented as vocabulary exclusively for punishment, torture or the groans of the Fire’s inhabitants.

Is the Fire’s shahīq real sound or rhetorical imagery?

In Al-Mulk 67:7, samiʿū explicitly concerns hearing, and lahā shahīqan attributes a sound to the Fire.

At the textual level, a sound is heard.

Its quality may be described through familiar human language of breathing or roaring. That rhetorical possibility should not negate the report itself.

Conversely, a respiratory word does not confer every human bodily feature on the Fire.

The appropriate boundary is to retain the known meaning without treating unmentioned details as certain.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the human use of shahīq, “long, drawn-out breath,” “a groaning gasp” or “a long, loud groan” may be suitable.

“Inhalation” alone may understate the sound. An unqualified “groan” may likewise lose its loudness and duration.

Hūd 11:106 places shahīq beside zafīr, so their difference should be retained.

Proposed rendering, without changing the installed verse translation:

“As for those who became wretched, they are in the Fire; there they have heavy, groaning breaths and long, drawn-out gasps.”

Here “heavy, groaning breaths” renders zafīr, and “long, drawn-out gasps” renders shahīq.

For Al-Mulk 67:7, where the Fire is the source of the sound, “a long, loud roar” is more suitable.

Proposed rendering:

“When they are thrown into it, they hear from it a long, loud roar as it boils.”

This avoids imposing human respiratory anatomy on the Fire while preserving the sound’s intensity.

“Bellow” can convey loudness in some contexts but may evoke a particular human or animal voice too strongly. For the Fire, the Persian equivalent of “roar” is more natural.

Non-Quranic derivatives should be rendered according to context:

Shāhiq applied to a mountain means “high” or “very elevated.”

Shawāhiq denotes high elevations or mountains.

Shahqah may be rendered “gasp,” “a sudden, loud breath” or, where appropriate, “a breathy cry.”

These meanings should not replace one another without contextual grounds.

Concluding synthesis

SH H Q has two important early Arabic fields: height and elevation, and breathing or sound distinguished by intensity, duration and loudness.

Shāhiq describes the elevation of a mountain or building. Shahīq concerns prolonged breath and an intense, sometimes groaning sound.

Early poetic witnesses use shahīq and related forms for a donkey’s call and forceful audible breathing. They establish older usage, while this study has not independently dated the particular verses of the mukhadram poets.

The Quran uses only shahīq from this family.

In Hūd 11:106 it accompanies zafīr in describing those in the Fire: their difficult, groaning breaths and sounds.

In Al-Mulk 67:7, the Fire itself is described as having shahīq. Those thrown into it hear its roar as it boils.

The difference shows that Quranic shahīq is not confined to human biological respiration. Its sound quality can also describe the Fire’s roar.

Shahīq is semantically related to zafīr, nahīq, ṣayḥah and anīn, but none is an exact synonym of another in every use.

The final definition is therefore:

In its elevation branch, SH H Q denotes height and rising aloft. In its sound branch, shahīq denotes a loud, prolonged, intense breath or sound associated with difficult breathing, groaning or roaring. The Quran applies this sound meaning once to the Fire’s inhabitants and once to the Fire itself.

For Forqan’s Persian translation, “a long, drawn-out breath” for the human use and “a long, loud roar” for the Fire preserve the distinction between the contexts.
