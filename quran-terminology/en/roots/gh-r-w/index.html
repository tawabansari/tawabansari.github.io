Adhering, persistence, attachment, stirring up, and incitement

Arabic gh r w has several branches, chiefly concerning adhesion, persistence, intense attachment, and stirring someone towards something. Ghirāʾ names an adhesive; ghariya bi-l-shayʾ means becoming attached or devoted to something; and social aghrā concerns stirring up, inciting, or setting people against one another.

Early gharw also means wonder or astonishment, while ghariyy in some uses means beautiful or handsome. Lexicons group these branches together, but their historical relationship is not fully established.

The Quran uses gh r w twice, both in Form IV: fa-aghraynā in Al-Māʾidah 5:14 and la-nughriyannaka in Al-Aḥzāb 33:60.

Their construction is shared but their objects and outcomes differ. The first concerns hostility and hatred stirred among a group; the second concerns stirring Muhammad against groups continuing harmful conduct.

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic sources record three important semantic fields.

First is adhesion and joining things. Ghirāʾ was an adhesive prepared from materials such as animal hides, other animal parts, or fish. Gharawtu al-jilda means attaching a hide to something with glue.

Second is persistence and attachment. Ghariya bi-l-shayʾ describes someone attached, devoted, or captivated, continually attending to something or refusing to leave it.

Third is incitement. Aghraytuhu bi-l-shayʾ means stirring someone towards something, directing their attention and pursuit towards it.

The fields are close but not identical.

Adhesion is physical; attachment can be psychological or behavioural. Incitement is an action producing an impulse in another to pursue a target or become involved with it.

Aghraytu al-kalba bi-l-ṣayd, for example, means setting a hunting dog after prey.

The dog is not glued to the prey: it is stirred to focus on and pursue it.

Adhesion can help illustrate the connection between meanings, but the actual verb here denotes incitement and setting in motion.

Was adhesion historically the root’s first meaning?

Lexicons explain the family’s connections in two different ways.

One places adhesion and persistence at the centre. Intense attachment resembles clinging, while incitement makes someone pursue something without leaving it.

Another emphasizes beauty, liking, and wonder: something is admired, attracts a person, and may produce attachment.

Both suggest conceivable connections between certain uses, but neither alone proves the historical order of all meanings.

The recorded meaning of each word, possible connections between branches, and their historical origins must therefore be distinguished.

2. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

Gluing arrows in Aws ibn Ḥajar’s poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar describes a hunter preparing his arrows:

لِأَسْهُمِهِ غَارٍ وَبَارٍ وَرَاصِفُ

li-ashumihi ghārin wa-bārin wa-rāṣifu

“For his arrows he applies glue, pares the shafts, and binds their parts firmly.”

Ghārin describes applying adhesive to arrows; bārin means paring the shafts, and rāṣif describes binding their parts firmly.

Arrow preparation required the parts to be securely joined, including keeping the feathers firmly in place.

The sense is entirely material: assembling and joining a tool’s parts, rather than deception, infatuation, or inciting people.

Sources on hunting equipment and arrow-making attribute this passage to Aws ibn Ḥajar. It witnesses an early adhesive use, but attribution alone should not determine the precise date of each morphological form.

Gharw as wonder in Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s poetry

A poem attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd includes:

فَلَا غَرْوَ إِلَّا جَارَتِي وَسُؤَالَهَا

fa-lā gharwa illā jāratī wa-suʾālahā

“There is nothing to wonder at except my neighbour and her question.”

Gharw means wonder or astonishment here.

Lā gharwa is also used for “there is no cause for wonder.”

The witness places this branch within poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

It does not establish that gharw, wonder, was the historical source of ighrāʾ, incitement.

Beauty or wonder may attract attention and attachment, but historical derivation requires independent evidence.

Attachment and persistence in Arabic

The Umayyad-period poet Kuthayyir ʿAzzah says:

إِذَا قُلْتُ أَسْلُو غَارَتِ الْعَيْنُ بِالْبُكَا

idhā qultu aslū ghārati al-ʿaynu bi-l-bukā

غِرَاءً وَمَدَّتْهَا مَدَامِعُ حُفَّلُ

ghirāʾan wa-maddathā madāmiʿu ḥuffalu

“Whenever I say I will forget my attachment, my eye persists in weeping, supplied by abundant tears.”

The weeping and its continuation are expressed through a member of gh r w.

Lexicons differ over the precise analysis of ghārat, while connecting it with persistence and sustained behaviour.

This poem postdates the Quran, rather than belonging to the pre-Islamic period. It illustrates the family’s continuing and extended use in Arabic.

The witnesses show physical adhesion, behavioural persistence, and attachment expressed within this family. Quranic ighrāʾ still requires independent analysis of its own constructions and contexts.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Recognized forms include:

  • Gharā / yaghrū: in some uses, to adhere or coat something; elsewhere, to wonder.
  • Gharawtu al-shayʾ: I attached something with glue.
  • Ghirāʾ: adhesive.
  • Ghariya bi-l-shayʾ: became attached, devoted, or persistent in relation to something.
  • Ghariyy: intensely attached or absorbed.
  • Aghrā / yughrī: stirred up, incited, or drew someone towards something.
  • Ighrāʾ: incitement, stimulation, or setting someone against a target.
  • Mughrin: inciting or stimulating.
  • Mughran: someone incited or deeply attached.
  • Ghārā / yughārī: in some lexical uses, to persist, engage, or continue a behaviour.
  • Gharw: wonder or astonishment.
  • Ghariyy: in another branch, beautiful or handsome.
  • Maghruww and maghriyy: something glued or coated with adhesive.

Dictionaries place some forms under gharā or ghariya. With final-weak verbs, the final radical can appear as wāw, yāʾ, or alif in different formations.

This study groups the two Quranic verbs under gh r w and discusses forms with yāʾ within the same lexical family.

Forms such as ghariya and mughrī do not justify assigning each an independent root. Conversely, a shared lexical classification does not prove one historical origin for every branch.

Quranic forms

Only two Form IV forms occur in the Quran.

Fa-aghraynā: first-person plural past tense, preceded by the connective fa-.

La-nughriyannaka: first-person plural imperfect, with emphatic lām, doubled emphatic nūn, and the singular second-person object suffix -ka.

Ghirāʾ, gharw, ghariyy, and gharā in the adhesive sense do not occur independently in the Quran.

4. Fa-aghraynā: stirring hostility and hatred

Al-Māʾidah 5:14

وَمِنَ الَّذِينَ قَالُوا إِنَّا نَصَارَىٰ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَهُمْ فَنَسُوا حَظًّا مِمَّا ذُكِّرُوا بِهِ فَأَغْرَيْنَا بَيْنَهُمُ الْعَدَاوَةَ وَالْبَغْضَاءَ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ وَسَوْفَ يُنَبِّئُهُمُ اللَّهُ بِمَا كَانُوا يَصْنَعُونَ

wa-mina alladhīna qālū innā naṣārā akhadhnā mīthāqahum fa-nasū ḥaẓẓan mimmā dhukkirū bihi fa-aghraynā baynahumu al-ʿadāwata wa-l-baghḍāʾa ilā yawmi al-qiyāmati wa-sawfa yunabbiʾuhumu allāhu bi-mā kānū yaṣnaʿūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:14

“From those who said, ‘We are Christians,’ We took their covenant, but they forgot a portion of what they had been reminded of. So We stirred hostility and hatred among them until the Day of Resurrection. The God will inform them of what they used to do.”

Fa-aghraynā here means stirring, bringing about, or setting hostility and hatred in motion among people.

Al-ʿadāwah and al-baghḍāʾ are separate nouns, both governed by aghraynā.

Baynahum makes the relationship between people or groups the setting.

The verb is not physical gluing here, but the stirring and establishment of a hostile social condition.

The root and its result

Aghraynā does not itself mean hostility or hatred.

Hostility comes from al-ʿadāwah, hatred from al-baghḍāʾ.

With different objects, the sense of stirring could remain while what is stirred changes.

Ordinary speech can describe urging someone towards useful work or inciting them against another person.

Both involve stirring someone, while their moral value depends on purpose, method, and outcome.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:14, the objects are hostility and hatred, making the resulting relationship hostile.

The connection with forgetting the covenant

The verse first mentions taking a covenant, then the group’s forgetting or neglecting part of what they were reminded of.

Fa-aghraynā follows.

Fa- connects that situation with the stirring of hostility and hatred.

The hostility is thus not introduced without a setting: it follows neglect of part of the reminder and covenant.

When shared principles of justice, trustworthiness, and responsibility are abandoned, conflicting interests, prejudice, and rivalry may become hostility and hatred.

Two groups may initially agree to cooperate and respect each other’s rights. Later abandonment of those obligations in pursuit of power and self-interest may turn differences into lasting hostility.

This illustrates the social connection but cannot replace the Quran’s direct statement.

The God is the subject of aghraynā. The wording should not be recast as merely a human event with no divine attribution.

That attribution alone does not specify how it occurred: through social consequences, emerging disputes, or other factors. Such details require independent evidence.

The explicit connection is between forgetting part of the covenantal reminder and hostility and hatred being stirred among them.

The referent of baynahum

The nearest antecedent of -hum is “those who said, ‘We are Christians.’”

The more immediate reading places the hostility and hatred within that group.

Because preceding verses also mention the Children of Israel and other groups, a broader reading concerning hostility between religious communities has also been proposed.

It should not displace the nearest antecedent without supporting evidence.

The verse concerns those whose covenant was taken and who forgot part of the reminder. It does not establish that every person called Christian today personally committed that conduct or must be hostile to others.

Until the Day of Resurrection

This specifies the temporal horizon of the hostility and hatred.

It does not mean that any two individuals from the groups must hate one another at every moment of their lives.

A continuing condition among groups need not have uniform intensity at every time.

Its scope is understood through the subject, pronoun reference, and Quranic evidence about repairing relationships.

Fuṣṣilat 41:34 speaks of hostility being transformed into warm, close friendship.

Human hostility should therefore not be presented as an absolute inability to reform conduct or rebuild relationships.

5. La-nughriyannaka: stirring against harmful groups

Al-Aḥzāb 33:60

لَئِنْ لَمْ يَنْتَهِ الْمُنَافِقُونَ وَالَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ وَالْمُرْجِفُونَ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ لَنُغْرِيَنَّكَ بِهِمْ ثُمَّ لَا يُجَاوِرُونَكَ فِيهَا إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

laʾin lam yantahi al-munāfiqūna wa-lladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍun wa-l-murjifūna fī al-madīnati la-nughriyannaka bihim thumma lā yujāwirūnaka fīhā illā qalīlan

Al-Aḥzāb 33:60

“If the hypocrites, those with sickness in their hearts, and those spreading alarm in the city do not desist, We will certainly stir you against them; then they will remain your neighbours there only briefly.”

This is also Form IV, but the construction differs from Al-Māʾidah 5:14.

-Ka in la-nughriyannaka refers to Muhammad; bi-him identifies those against whom he is to be stirred.

The verb here concerns stirring a person against groups continuing specified conduct, rather than creating hostility between groups.

The condition’s role

The verse begins laʾin lam yantahi: if they do not desist.

This is a decisive condition.

La-nughriyannaka bi-him is therefore a conditional warning, not an unrestricted command detached from their behaviour.

Emphatic lām and doubled emphatic nūn stress the warning’s certainty and force.

They do not themselves specify the action’s form, manner, or every condition.

Those require the whole sentence and surrounding verses.

Who is warned?

Three descriptions occur: hypocrites, those with sickness in their hearts, and people spreading alarm in the city.

Their referents may overlap. Separate mention does not prove three entirely independent groups.

Al-murjifūn concerns creating anxiety, spreading alarming reports, and disturbing communal peace.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:58 also condemns harming believing men and women without wrongdoing on their part as slander and manifest sin.

The warning thus occurs amid actual harm and harmful behaviour.

Ighrāʾ itself should not be redefined as hypocrisy, spreading rumours, or causing harm: those are the conditions and conduct addressed by the warning.

Can la-nughriyannaka mean granting power over them?

Lexicons and explanations of the verse gloss la-nughriyannaka bi-him with inciting, stirring up, and granting power over them.

These meanings approach one another in context.

Someone stirred against a group may also gain power to act against it, making control part of the resulting situation.

Control should not, however, be made a necessary meaning of every aghrā.

Someone can be incited to act while lacking the ability to succeed. Incitement has occurred without mastery or success.

“Stir you against them” preserves a more direct sense in Al-Aḥzāb 33:60. “Give you power over them” draws support from the construction and intended consequence.

Action and its consequence

“Then they will remain your neighbours there only briefly” describes a consequence following Muhammad’s being stirred against them.

It is the ending or severe limitation of their residence as neighbours in the city.

Aghrā does not itself mean expulsion, killing, or destruction. Those questions require other wording and its own context.

A conditional warning about specified conduct should not be turned into unrestricted permission to harm anyone accused of hypocrisy or any social or religious opponent.

The lexical meaning is stirring up. The warning’s subject, condition, and consequences come from the whole verse.

6. The connection between the two Quranic uses

Al-Māʾidah 5:14 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:60 initially concern different subjects.

Al-Māʾidah describes hostility and hatred stirred among a group.

Al-Aḥzāb describes Muhammad stirred against groups continuing harmful conduct.

They share the creation or intensification of an active orientation between parties.

In the first, it appears as hostility and hatred between people or groups.

In the second, a person is stirred against a group.

Quranic ighrāʾ is thus not simply an inward feeling: it is an action that stirs or directs conduct and relationships.

The constructions’ differences also matter.

Al-Māʾidah 5:14 has aghraynā baynahumu al-ʿadāwata wa-l-baghḍāʾ.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:60 has la-nughriyannaka bi-him.

In the first, hostility and hatred are the verb’s direct objects; baynahum locates the relationship.

In the second, the second-person pronoun is its object; bi-him identifies the opposing party.

They should not be mechanically rendered with an identical sentence.

7. Distinctions from related roots

Gh r w and gh r r: incitement and deception

Quranic gh r r concerns deceiving, being deceived, and unfounded confidence or expectations.

The two Quranic gh r w uses concern stirring and incitement.

Both may occur in one action but are distinct.

A false promise might incite one group against another, combining deception and incitement.

Not every incitement deceives, and being deceived need not produce action against another.

Similar radicals do not justify merging the roots.

Gh r w and ḥ r ḍ: incitement and encouragement

Some Quranic ḥ r ḍ forms concern encouraging and urging action.

Al-Anfāl 8:65 commands ḥarriḍi al-muʾminīna ʿalā al-qitāl.

Ḥarriḍ directly concerns urging believers to fight.

Aghrā can likewise incite someone against another, but its lexical family also includes persistence and attachment.

This does not mean every ighrāʾ is stronger, more dangerous, or more lasting than every taḥrīḍ.

Context determines the incitement’s degree and character.

Gh r w and ʾ z z: stirring and driving into motion

Maryam 19:83 uses taʾuzzuhum for devils forcefully inciting disbelievers.

Its effect approaches incitement.

The lexical field of azz includes driving, setting in motion, and forceful stimulation. Quranic gh r w belongs to a family also known for persistence, attachment, and stirring.

Similar results do not make every feature identical.

Gh r w and ʿ d w: stirring hostility and hostility itself

Al-Māʾidah 5:14 combines aghraynā with al-ʿadāwah.

ʿAdāwah concerns a hostile relationship or stance.

Ighrāʾ is the action that stirs that hostility here.

One expresses bringing about or stirring a condition; the other names the hostile condition.

Rendering aghraynā simply as “made enemies” risks overlooking al-ʿadāwah’s separate contribution.

Gh r w and b gh ḍ: incitement and hatred

Quranic baghḍāʾ denotes hatred and intense aversion.

It accompanies hostility in Al-Māʾidah 5:14.

Aghraynā expresses stirring; baghḍāʾ specifies one condition stirred.

Hostility and hatred are not exact synonyms either: ʿadāwah emphasizes the hostile relationship and conduct, while baghḍāʾ emphasizes hatred and aversion.

They may coexist, but hatred need not become hostile action, nor must every hostile encounter arise from deep personal hatred.

Gh r w and gh r m: persistence and affliction

Gh r m includes obligation and persistence in debt, financial commitment, and severe affliction.

Gh r w also includes persistence and adhesion.

This shared concept does not merge the roots.

Quranic gh r m concerns debt, loss, and affliction; aghrā concerns stirring and incitement.

Gh r w and gh r q: independent roots

Quranic gh r q concerns drowning and submersion in water.

Forms such as aghraqa resemble aghrā in writing but differ in root and meaning.

Aghraynā belongs to gh r w; aghraqnā to gh r q.

Their appearance should not lead to confusion.

8. Questions of interpretation and translation

Is ighrāʾ always negative?

No. Arabic can describe stirring towards different aims.

Someone may encourage beneficial work or incite against another.

Both Quranic aghrā occurrences involve social tension and confrontation, but this comes from their contexts, not a rule making every use intrinsically negative.

Is ighrāʾ the same as gluing?

Gluing is a recognized lexical branch, but Quranic aghrā is not physical adhesion.

Adhesion can illustrate the connection with persistence and attachment, but should not produce an unnatural translation such as “We glued hostility among them.”

“We stirred” is more natural and appropriate in Al-Māʾidah 5:14.

Is gharw, wonder, the same as ghurūr?

No. Gharw is recorded as wonder and belongs to gh r w.

Quranic ghurūr, deception, belongs to gh r r.

Wonder and delusion may approach one another in some situations without sharing a root.

Does aghraynā in Al-Māʾidah command hostility?

The verse says The God stirred hostility and hatred among the group discussed.

The verb does not itself constitute a prescriptive command for humans to act hostilely.

Attributing an occurrence to The God differs from issuing a moral command to be hostile.

Al-Māʾidah 5:8 explicitly warns that hostility towards a people must not prevent justice.

Reporting an event or social consequence does not independently authorize oppression or injustice.

Does la-nughriyannaka authorize action against every opponent?

No. Al-Aḥzāb 33:60 conditions the warning on the specified groups continuing harmful conduct.

It does not address every intellectual opponent, every person of different belief, or everyone accused of hypocrisy.

Ighrāʾ itself does not specify the action’s details.

The verb’s meaning, warning’s conditions, parties’ identity and conduct, and rules governing action must be distinguished.

9. Translation suggestions for Forqan

“So We stirred” suits fa-aghraynā in Al-Māʾidah 5:14.

The complete phrase:

“So We stirred hostility and hatred among them.”

This preserves the verb and the separate contributions of al-ʿadāwah and al-baghḍāʾ.

For la-nughriyannaka bi-him in Al-Aḥzāb 33:60, “We will certainly stir you against them” is suggested.

“We will certainly give you power over them” is also defensible contextually, but includes more explanation of the outcome than the verb’s basic meaning.

Outside the Quran:

Ghirāʾ: glue or adhesive.

Gharawtu al-shayʾ: I glued it.

Ghariya bi-l-shayʾ: became attached, devoted, or unwilling to leave it.

Ighrāʾ: inciting, stirring, or setting against another, depending on the object.

Mughrin: inciting or stimulating.

Mughran: incited or deeply attached.

Gharw: wonder or astonishment.

Lā gharwa: there is no cause for wonder.

Ghariyy: beautiful or handsome in the recorded descriptive use.

Each form must retain its actual constructional meaning. Gluing, infatuation, incitement, and wonder are not freely interchangeable equivalents.

Conclusion

Gh r w has branches for adhesion and connection, persistence and attachment, stirring and incitement, and elsewhere wonder and beauty.

Witnesses attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic show adhesive arrow-making and gharw as wonder. Lexicons also record connections between adhesion, persistence, and attachment.

Shared classification and related images do not prove one historical source for every meaning.

Only two Form IV forms occur in the Quran.

Fa-aghraynā in Al-Māʾidah 5:14 concerns hostility and hatred stirred among people who forgot part of their covenantal reminder.

La-nughriyannaka in Al-Aḥzāb 33:60 concerns stirring Muhammad against groups warned about continuing harmful conduct.

Stirring and directing a relationship or behaviour is central to both. The object, parties, and consequences come from the sentence and context.

The Quranic use can thus be summarized:

Ighrāʾ means stirring or inciting someone, or bringing about a condition directing people towards confrontation, pursuit, or engagement with someone or something.

This connects intelligibly with adhesion and persistence, but physical gluing should not replace the verb’s social use.

Nor do the two Quranic occurrences make every incitement inherently objectionable or every resulting confrontation legitimate or illegitimate without examining conditions and responsibility.

The root identifies the action; its moral value, conditions, and consequences emerge from context and the wider Quranic evidence.
