Going forth, releasing, and opening the way for movement

The Arabic root S R Ḥ concerns going forth, moving freely, and releasing. In everyday Arab life, particularly livestock keeping, it was used for animals going out to pasture or being sent there. Other uses express dispatching, opening, letting go, and removing an obstacle to movement.

In the Qur’an, the root appears in two clear settings: livestock moving toward pasture, and marital separation with due regard for rights and proper conduct. Sending forth and leaving the way open connect these uses, but the ethical meaning of the second comes from the complete verses and qualifications such as بِمَعْرُوفٍ, بِإِحْسَانٍ, and جَمِيلًا.

1. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

The root occurs seven times in five verses: Al-Baqarah 2:229, Al-Baqarah 2:231, Al-Naḥl 16:6, Al-Aḥzāb 33:28, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:49.

Its Qur’anic forms are تَسْرَحُونَ, from the basic verb; تَسْرِيحٌ, the verbal noun of Form II; سَرِّحُوهُنَّ and أُسَرِّحْكُنَّ, Form II verbs; and سَرَاحًا, used alongside the verb of releasing. These fall into four morphological groups: سَرَاحًا appears in two verses, and Form II verbs occur in three places.

One early lexical analysis identifies الانطلاق as the core: setting out and moving without obstruction. It also records أمر سريح for an affair involving neither delay nor obstruction, and groups livestock, marital separation, and unhindered movement within this semantic field.

On this basis, the main meaning can be formulated as follows:

Moving or sending forth with the way open, so that movement is not held back.

Three situations should nevertheless be distinguished. Sometimes the subject moves, as livestock go to pasture. Sometimes someone sends another forth, as a shepherd sends out a flock. Elsewhere the expression concerns ending a relationship or condition that prevents the other party from leaving and acting independently. This last use is prominent in verses about marital separation.

2. Usage in early Arabic

Livestock, pasture, and free movement

In Arabic, سَرَحَتِ الماشيةُ means that livestock went out to pasture and grazed. سَرَحَ الماشيةَ describes someone sending livestock out to graze.

Related recorded uses include السَّرْح for grazing livestock, السَّارِحَة for animals going to pasture, السَّارِح for a shepherd or person associated with the flock, and المَسْرَح for a grazing place.

Here, sending forth does not mean aimless abandonment or forgetting responsibility for the animals. The flock leaves its enclosure, moves through the pasture, and can return. Opening the way for movement is central; permanently severing a connection is not.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward illustrates this early usage:

يُرِيحُ عَلَيَّ اللَّيْلُ أَضْيَافَ مَاجِدٍ
كَرِيمٍ وَمَالِي سَارِحًا مَالُ مُقْتِرِ

yurīḥu ʿalayya al-laylu aḍyāfa mājidin / karīmin wa-mālī sāriḥan mālu muqtiri

Translation: “Night brings me the guests of a noble, generous man, while my grazing wealth is the wealth of a man of little means.”

Here سَارِحًا describes wealth in livestock that goes out to pasture. The poet speaks of limited possessions and hospitality. This is a poetic illustration of the root in the pastoral and economic life of the Arabs; its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet should be distinguished from independently establishing the date of the particular verse.

Poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā also contains بجلالة سرح, describing a large, swift she-camel. This illustrates smooth, agile movement, although attribution to the poet alone does not establish the precise date of composition. The phrase is presented without vowel marks because transmitted vocalizations differ.

Other linguistic uses

The root was not confined to livestock. Lexical records include سَرَحَ السَّيْلُ for a flood flowing freely, and سَرَّحَ الشَّعْرَ for loosening and combing hair. In the latter, tangled strands are separated and opened so that the comb can pass through them.

سَرَّحَ الرَّسُولَ also meant sending a messenger on a task.

Movement, dispatching, or the removal of constriction and obstruction connects these uses. This shared feature does not make them identical: flowing water, sending a messenger, and combing hair each have their own construction and subject matter.

سَرْحَة as a tree

In early Arabic, سَرْحَة also named a large, tall tree. ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād says in his celebrated Muʿallaqah:

بَطَلٍ كَأَنَّ ثِيَابَهُ فِي سَرْحَةٍ
يُحْذَى نِعَالَ السِّبْتِ لَيْسَ بِتَوْأَمِ

baṭalin ka-anna thiyābahu fī sarḥatin / yuḥdhā niʿāla al-sibti laysa bi-tawʾami

Translation: “A warrior whose garments seem to hang upon a tall tree; he is shod in sandals of tanned leather and was not born a twin.”

The comparison with a tall tree depicts the warrior’s imposing, sturdy physique. Some versions read سِلَاحَهُ, “his weapons,” instead of ثِيَابَهُ, “his garments”; سَرْحَة remains a tall tree in either reading. The reference to not being a twin belongs to the poem’s portrayal of bodily strength, rather than establishing a biological rule.

An early lexical explanation expresses uncertainty about connecting سَرْحَة with الانطلاق, suggesting that the name might relate to branches spreading in different directions. This is a lexical proposal, not a demonstrated historical origin of the tree’s name.

Thus, although سَرْحَة belongs to the recorded vocabulary of this root, the image of a tree should not be imported into the Qur’anic meanings of tasrīḥ or sarāḥ.

3. First Qur’anic setting: sending livestock forth

Al-Naḥl 16:6

وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا جَمَالٌ حِينَ تُرِيحُونَ وَحِينَ تَسْرَحُونَ

wa-lakum fīhā jamālun ḥīna turīḥūna wa-ḥīna tasraḥūna

Al-Naḥl 16:6

Translation: “And in them there is beauty for you when you bring them back and when you send them forth.”

The preceding verse speaks about الأنعام, livestock. They are therefore the referent of فيها and the object of the two actions.

The verse places two movements alongside one another:

تُرِيحُونَ refers to bringing livestock back from pasture to their enclosure; تَسْرَحُونَ refers to sending them from there toward pasture.

In pastoral life, these movements generally occurred at two times of day: departure at the beginning and return at the end. The verse highlights جمال, the beauty of these movements.

Morphologically, تَسْرَحُونَ is a second-person plural imperfect verb in the basic form. Its subjects are the owners of the livestock, so here it describes sending the animals forth. The word alone is neither the name of a pasture nor the act of eating grass; the setting identifies pasture as the destination.

“You send them out to pasture” is therefore a natural and precise translation here. Grazing should not, however, be treated as the root’s only possible meaning throughout the Qur’an.

This verse also shows that sarḥ need not mean permanent release. The same animals that are sent out are brought back. Whether a relationship continues or ends cannot be inferred from the verb alone.

4. Second Qur’anic setting: release in marital relations

Al-Baqarah 2:229

الطَّلَاقُ مَرَّتَانِ ۖ فَإِمْسَاكٌ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ

al-ṭalāqu marratāni fa-imsākun bi-maʿrūfin aw tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin

Al-Baqarah 2:229

Translation: “Divorce is twice; then either retaining in a proper manner or releasing with kindness.”

In this verse, tasrīḥ stands opposite imsāk.

Here imsāk means retaining and continuing the marital bond; tasrīḥ means releasing that bond and leaving the way open for separation.

Both are qualified: continuing the relationship must be بِمَعْرُوفٍ, in a proper manner, and separating must be بِإِحْسَانٍ, with kindness.

The contrast shows that the verse addresses more than whether separation occurs: it also concerns conduct in either situation.

The rest of the verse restricts taking back property given to women and discusses divine limits. Releasing with kindness therefore appears in a setting involving financial rights, justice, and observance of limits. Kindness is not merely a pleasant feeling or gentle words; conduct at separation must accord with it.

تَسْرِيح is the verbal noun of Form II. Here this form expresses performing an act of releasing or sending forth. Its morphology alone does not establish repeated action or a particular stage in the number of divorces.

Early interpretation transmits two readings of releasing with kindness here: a third divorce, or allowing the period for return to end without resuming the relationship. This difference concerns the phrase’s relationship to “divorce is twice” and the surrounding verses. It does not make the number three or a particular legal procedure an intrinsic meaning of S R Ḥ.

Al-Baqarah 2:231

وَإِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَبَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ سَرِّحُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ

wa-idhā ṭallaqtumu al-nisāʾa fa-balaghna ajalahunna fa-amsikūhunna bi-maʿrūfin aw sarriḥūhunna bi-maʿrūfin

Al-Baqarah 2:231

Translation: “When you divorce women and they approach the end of their appointed period, retain them in a proper manner or release them in a proper manner.” Here approaching the end is meant, since return remains an option; Al-Baqarah 2:232 then concerns completion of the period.

The verse then says:

وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا لِّتَعْتَدُوا

wa-lā tumsikūhunna ḍirāran li-taʿtadū

Al-Baqarah 2:231

“Do not retain them to cause harm and transgress.”

This makes the meaning of release clearer by contrasting it with harmful retention.

When a marital relationship reaches the point of deciding whether it continues or ends, retaining a woman must not become a means of abuse, pressure, or violation of her rights. If continuation within proper conduct is not possible, release is provided for.

سَرِّحُوهُنَّ here is therefore more than a report of ending a relationship. It is a command contrasted with harmful retention and qualified by بِمَعْرُوفٍ.

The verse does not imply that every continuation is wrong or every separation automatically good. Conduct in either situation is the criterion. Continuing married life may accord with what is proper, as may ending it. Retention involving harm is expressly prohibited.

The following verse, Al-Baqarah 2:232, also prohibits obstructing women’s marriage after the appointed period ends. This connection indicates that separation should not leave a woman’s future choices and path in life restricted by others’ obstruction after the relationship has ended.

This conclusion comes from the connected verses, not from treating sarḥ alone as an expression of all rights concerning marriage and separation.

5. سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا: gracious separation

Al-Aḥzāb 33:28

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ قُل لِأَزْوَاجِكَ إِن كُنتُنَّ تُرِدْنَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا وَزِينَتَهَا فَتَعَالَيْنَ أُمَتِّعْكُنَّ وَأُسَرِّحْكُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu qul li-azwājika in kuntunna turidna al-ḥayāta al-dunyā wa-zīnatahā fa-taʿālayna umattiʿkunna wa-usarriḥkunna sarāḥan jamīlan

Al-Aḥzāb 33:28

Translation: “O Prophet, tell your wives: If you desire the worldly life and its adornment, then come, so that I may provide for you and release you graciously.”

The Prophet is instructed to present two courses to his wives: the first appears here, and the other in the next verse.

For this root study, the pairing of أُمَتِّعْكُنَّ and أُسَرِّحْكُنَّ is significant.

أُمَتِّعْكُنَّ means providing a benefit, while أُسَرِّحْكُنَّ means releasing and ending the bond. In this expression, separation is accompanied by provision for the other party.

سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا describes the quality of the separation. Jamīl belongs to another root and adds goodness and beauty to sarāḥ. Beauty should therefore not be made an invariant part of S R Ḥ itself.

Grammatically, أُسَرِّحْكُنَّ is a Form II imperfect verb with a first-person singular subject and a feminine plural object. سَرَاحًا functions as an absolute object, standing in for the verbal noun tasrīḥ; جَمِيلًا specifies the manner of release.

A natural Persian rendering corresponds to “release you graciously,” rather than mechanically repeating “releasing” and “release” in a cumbersome sentence.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:49

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا نَكَحْتُمُ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ ثُمَّ طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ مِن قَبْلِ أَن تَمَسُّوهُنَّ فَمَا لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِنَّ مِنْ عِدَّةٍ تَعْتَدُّونَهَا ۖ فَمَتِّعُوهُنَّ وَسَرِّحُوهُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā nakaḥtumu al-muʾmināti thumma ṭallaqtumūhunna min qabli an tamassūhunna fa-mā lakum ʿalayhinna min ʿiddatin taʿtaddūnahā fa-mattiʿūhunna wa-sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan jamīlan

Al-Aḥzāb 33:49

Translation: “O you who have believed, when you marry believing women and then divorce them before marital contact, you have no waiting period to count against them. So provide for them and release them graciously.”

The verse concerns separation before marital contact and expressly states that there is no waiting period for the men to count against the women in this situation.

طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ comes before سَرِّحُوهُنَّ. This order matters: the verse first speaks of divorce having occurred, then commands provision and gracious release.

Here tasrīḥ is consequently more than another way of reporting divorce; it also concerns how the woman is treated following separation.

This sequence should not become an absolute lexical rule making divorce and release invariably separate, successive acts. Arabic also records تسريح المرأة as divorcing a woman. The precise distinction must be established from each verse’s setting.

Here, as in Al-Aḥzāb 33:28, provision accompanies release. Ending the relationship should not become a pretext for disregarding rights or good conduct.

سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا is not simply a poetic expression about parting. It accompanies a practical command to provide for the other party. Gracious separation must therefore appear in conduct, not merely words.

6. Related roots and translation challenges

S R Ḥ (sarḥ) and Ṭ L Q (ṭalāq)

Both roots occur in marital separation, but their semantic images are not entirely identical.

In marital usage, ṭalāq names the act of dissolving the marriage bond. Tasrīḥ emphasizes sending forth, letting go, and leaving the way open after or during the ending of a relationship.

Al-Baqarah 2:229 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:49 show both terms used in the same legal field.

Nevertheless, the words can overlap in Arabic: tasrīḥ can itself mean divorcing. A rigid boundary necessarily applying to every sentence and every period of the language would therefore be unjustified.

S R Ḥ (sarḥ) and F R Q (farq)

Farq has a broad field of separating, putting distance between things, and distinguishing. In marital usage, firāq means separation.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:2 says:

فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ فَارِقُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ

fa-amsikūhunna bi-maʿrūfin aw fāriqūhunna bi-maʿrūfin

Al-Ṭalāq 65:2

“Then retain them in a proper manner or part from them in a proper manner.”

The construction clearly resembles Al-Baqarah 2:231: سَرِّحُوهُنَّ in one verse and فَارِقُوهُنَّ in the other stand opposite أَمْسِكُوهُنَّ.

This illustrates the closeness of tasrīḥ and mufāraqah in marital separation. Firāq may place more emphasis on parting, while tasrīḥ highlights letting go and leaving the way open. This is a semantic tendency, not an impassable boundary.

S R Ḥ (sarḥ) and M S K (mask)

The contrast between these two roots is especially clear in the Qur’an.

In Al-Baqarah 2:229 and Al-Baqarah 2:231, retaining and releasing are opposing options: one continues the bond, and the other lets it go.

Yet the verses supply an ethical criterion for both. Letting go is not invariably good, nor retaining invariably bad. Conduct is assessed in relation to intention, manner, the other party’s rights, and the prescribed limits.

Thus, “complete freedom from every obligation” cannot be extracted from sarḥ itself, just as imsāk cannot establish the legitimacy of every form of retention or restriction.

The challenge of translating tasrīḥ as “divorcing”

Rendering tasrīḥ as divorcing is lexically defensible in some sentences, since classical Arabic records that use.

However, consistently translating it only as divorcing, without regard for sentence structure, may obscure its connection with retaining and its image of letting go. This matters particularly in Al-Aḥzāb 33:49, where both طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ and سَرِّحُوهُنَّ occur.

Conversely, consistently using the Persian equivalent of “setting free” could misleadingly suggest that the wife had been her husband’s personal property. Such a meaning should not be imposed on human relations from the linguistic image of releasing livestock.

Depending on sentence structure, expressions corresponding to “letting go,” “parting graciously,” and “opening the way for separation” are more suitable here.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan and conclusions

In Al-Naḥl 16:6, “you send forth” suits تَسْرَحُونَ; a contextual explanation can specify sending livestock to pasture.

In Al-Baqarah 2:229, تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ can be rendered “releasing with kindness.” In Al-Baqarah 2:231, “release them in a proper manner” expresses سَرِّحُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ well.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:28, أُسَرِّحْكُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا can be rendered “release you graciously”; in Al-Aḥzāb 33:49, فَمَتِّعُوهُنَّ وَسَرِّحُوهُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا becomes “so provide for them and release them graciously.”

Final definition of the root

S R Ḥ expresses going or sending forth with the way open and movement unobstructed. Its uses extend to livestock movement, dispatching people, loosening tangles, and releasing a bond.

In the Qur’an, تَسْرَحُونَ describes sending livestock toward pasture, while tasrīḥ and sarāḥ in marital relations concern releasing and ending the bond within the specified rights and standards of conduct.

The root itself contains neither the ethical meaning of kindness nor all the rules of marital separation. Those meanings come from accompanying words and the verses’ settings.

Across these verses, marital separation does not authorize abuse, deprivation of rights, or obstruction of the other party’s future life. Alongside continuing the relationship properly, the Qur’an describes ending it with propriety, kindness, and good conduct.

In this Qur’anic usage, sarḥ therefore retains its lexical meaning of release within a setting that also clarifies human responsibility for how a relationship ends.
