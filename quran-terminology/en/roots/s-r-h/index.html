Central meaning: sending forth, releasing, and leaving the way open for movement. In transitive uses, this can release someone or something from restraint; an intransitive use need not imply earlier confinement. In early pastoral usage, saraḥat al-māshiya means that livestock went out to pasture, while sarraḥahā means sending them out. In marriage passages, tasrīḥ concerns releasing a wife and bringing the marital bond to an end. The Quran uses the family once for sending livestock to pasture and six times in expressions concerning the release of women. Maʿrūf, iḥsān, and jamīl specify the appropriate manner of separation.

1. Introduction and meaning in early Arabic

S R Ḥ concerns going forth, moving freely, releasing, and removing obstacles to departure.

Saraḥat al-māshiyatu means “the livestock went out to graze.”

Sarraḥa al-rāʿī al-māshiyata means “the shepherd sent the livestock to pasture.”

Al-sarḥ can denote livestock that go out to graze.

Al-masraḥ denotes pasture or the place to which livestock go for grazing.

More broadly, sarraḥa fulānan means sending someone forth or allowing them to leave.

Their common feature is going or allowing movement. Some uses involve release from restraint, but the verb alone does not establish previous confinement in every instance.

Such release does not necessarily mean abandoning care or ending every responsibility.

A shepherd sending sheep to pasture may still watch over them and bring them back at sunset.

They have been allowed to move and graze without ending ownership, care, or the shepherd’s responsibility.

Sending forth, allowing movement, and abandoning care must therefore be distinguished.

A transmitted poetic example: al-sarḥ in verse attributed to ʿAntara ibn Shaddad

A verse attributed to ʿAntara ibn Shaddad reads:

نُزَاحِفُ زَحْفًا أَوْ نُلَاقِي كَتِيبَةً
تُطَاعِنُنَا أَوْ يَذْعَرُ السَّرْحَ صَائِحُ

nuzāḥifu zaḥfan aw nulāqī katībatan / tuṭāʿinunā aw yadhʿaru al-sarḥa ṣāʾiḥu

Translation:

“We advance in battle, or meet a troop that exchanges spear thrusts with us, or a shouter frightens the grazing livestock.”

Al-sarḥ here denotes livestock out at pasture.

Yadhʿaru means frightening or alarming.

Ṣāʾiḥ denotes someone shouting.

Fear comes from yadhʿaru and shouting from ṣāʾiḥ.

Sarḥ itself does not mean fear, flight, or dispersal.

It identifies the grazing animals sent out to pasture.

The verse is transmitted among poems attributed to ʿAntara, but its wording and attribution are disputed. Some versions have al-sarb instead of al-sarḥ, and the authenticity of the group of verses has been questioned.

This precise reading cannot therefore be presented without qualification as certain pre-Islamic evidence. Its value lies in illustrating al-sarḥ in the Arabic poetic tradition and the meaning associated with that usage.

Another pre-Islamic example: the sarḥa tree in ʿAntara’s Muʿallaqa

ʿAntara ibn Shaddad’s famous Muʿallaqa includes:

بَطَلٌ كَأَنَّ ثِيَابَهُ فِي سَرْحَةٍ
يُحْذَى نِعَالَ السَّبْتِ لَيْسَ بِتَوْأَمِ

baṭalun ka-anna thiyābahu fī sarḥatin / yuḥdhā niʿāla al-sabti laysa bi-tawʾami

Translation:

“A warrior so tall that his clothes seem to rest on a great tree; he wears sandals of tanned leather and is no twin.”

Sarḥa here names a large, tall tree.

The poet compares the warrior to a tall tree to express his stature.

This occurrence belongs to ʿAntara’s established Muʿallaqa and has clearer support for its poetic attribution.

Sarḥa here does not mean grazing livestock or sending someone forth.

Al-sarḥ is also recorded as a name for a large, spreading tree.

Some lexical analyses propose a connection between its spreading branches and the ideas of release and extension.

That image makes a semantic relationship intelligible but does not establish derivation from the verb for sending animals to pasture.

The two uses show that early Arabic S R Ḥ was not confined to one application.

Historical definition of the main branch

The pastoral usage is clearly recorded in classical lexical descriptions:

Saraḥat al-māshiyatu means “the livestock went out to graze.”

Sarraḥahā means “he sent them forth.”

Expressions for bringing animals back in the evening contrast with sending them out at the beginning of the day.

The Quran itself uses this contrast, clarifying the meaning around the time of revelation.

A defensible historical definition of the main branch is therefore:

In movement contexts, S R Ḥ denotes going forth along a route; transitively, it denotes sending forth and leaving the way open for departure. Its familiar pastoral use concerns sending livestock to pasture and their free movement there.

Arabic also extends this usage to releasing people and ending marriage, directly attested in the Quran.

The prominence of a physical image in lexical accounts does not establish a definite period when every other use arose from it.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Saraḥa, yasraḥu

The basic, Form I verb.

With livestock, it means going out to graze.

سَرَحَتِ الْغَنَمُ

saraḥati al-ghanamu

“The sheep went out to pasture.”

It can be intransitive, attributing the movement to the animals themselves.

The transitive saraḥa al-māshiyata is also recorded for sending livestock to pasture.

Tasraḥūna in al-Nahl 16:6 belongs to this Form I verb.

Sarḥ and surūḥ

In pastoral usage, sarḥ denotes grazing livestock or animals going out to pasture.

Surūḥ is also a verbal noun for going forth.

These forms are known in Arabic but do not occur as independent nouns in the Quran.

Sāriḥ

The active participle of Form I.

In pastoral contexts, it can denote an animal out grazing.

Some uses apply sāriḥ to a shepherd or someone taking animals to pasture.

The sentence must establish its referent.

Sāriḥa

An animal or group of livestock going out to graze.

Some uses particularly concern camels and sheep.

Masraḥ

A place noun denoting pasture or where livestock go forth.

مَسْرَحُ الْمَاشِيَةِ

masraḥu al-māshiyati

“The livestock’s grazing ground.”

In later Arabic it also denotes a place of theatrical performance.

The modern sense “theater” is not the root’s Quranic meaning and should not be projected back onto revelation-era usage.

Sarraḥa, yusarriḥu

The Form II verb.

It means sending forth, releasing, and permitting departure.

سَرَّحَ الْمَاشِيَةَ

sarraḥa al-māshiyata

“He sent the livestock to pasture.”

سَرَّحَ الرَّجُلَ

sarraḥa al-rajula

“He sent the man forth or allowed him to leave.”

In marriage contexts:

سَرَّحَ الْمَرْأَةَ

sarraḥa al-marʾata

“He released the woman from the marital bond.”

This use occurs more frequently in the Quran than the Form I verb.

Tasrīḥ

The verbal noun of Form II.

It denotes sending forth and releasing.

In marriage contexts, it concerns ending the marital relationship and releasing the woman.

Al-Baqarah 2:229 uses this verbal noun.

Tasrīḥ contrasts there with imsāk, retaining.

Sarāḥ

An action noun in this family.

In release contexts it is close in meaning to tasrīḥ.

Sarāḥan jamīlan occurs twice in the Quran.

The expression denotes release and ending a relationship in a good manner.

Sarāḥ here names neither an object nor a place; it expresses the act of release and separation.

Sarīḥ

Lexical uses describe something easy, unimpeded, or proceeding smoothly.

أَمْرٌ سَرِيحٌ

amrun sarīḥun

“An easy, unimpeded matter.”

This concerns the absence of obstruction and difficulty.

It should not be made the direct meaning of every form without evidence.

Insaraḥa and tasarraḥa

These forms occur for being released, going out, and emerging from restriction.

اِنْسَرَحَ الرَّجُلُ

insaraḥa al-rajulu

“The man left the place or went forth.”

Some uses of tasarraḥa also approach the sense of proceeding at ease.

Tasrīḥ al-shaʿr

With hair, tasrīḥ denotes arranging and combing it.

A comb separates tangled strands and aligns them along their length.

This has a semantic connection with untangling and allowing things to run freely.

It does not make Quranic tasrīḥ mean combing or tidying married life.

Sarḥa

The name of a kind of large tree.

It is the word in ʿAntara’s poem.

Some descriptions identify a tall, spreading tree giving useful shade.

This use does not occur in the Quran.

Sirḥān

Also a name for a wolf in Arabic.

Other uses, including for a lion, occur in some varieties of the language.

A historical connection with the verb of movement requires independent evidence.

Similarity of form alone does not prove that the animal was named for its speed or freedom of movement.

Forms actually found in the Quran

S R Ḥ occurs seven times in five verses.

  • Tasraḥūna in al-Nahl 16:6: Form I verb.
  • Tasrīḥun in al-Baqarah 2:229: Form II verbal noun.
  • Sarriḥūhunna in al-Baqarah 2:231: imperative.
  • Wa-usarriḥkunna in al-Ahzab 33:28: first-person singular imperfect verb.
  • Sarāḥan in al-Ahzab 33:28: action noun.
  • Wa-sarriḥūhunna in al-Ahzab 33:49: imperative.
  • Sarāḥan in al-Ahzab 33:49: action noun.

These fall into two fields: sending livestock to pasture and releasing women when marriage ends.

3. Wa-ḥīna tasraḥūna: sending livestock to pasture in al-Nahl 16:6

وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا جَمَالٌ حِينَ تُرِيحُونَ وَحِينَ تَسْرَحُونَ

wa-lakum fīhā jamālun ḥīna turīḥūna wa-ḥīna tasraḥūna

Al-Naḥl 16:6

Translation:

“And in them you have beauty, when you bring them home and when you take them out to pasture.”

Tasraḥūna is a second-person plural imperfect Form I verb.

The subject matter is the livestock introduced in al-Nahl 16:5.

Here it means taking or sending the animals out to graze.

Distinguishing turīḥūna and tasraḥūna

The verse uses two different verbs:

تُرِيحُونَ

turīḥūna

Al-Naḥl 16:6

You bring back; referring to livestock returning from pasture.

تَسْرَحُونَ

tasraḥūna

Al-Naḥl 16:6

You take out to pasture.

Turīḥūna belongs to R W Ḥ and here concerns bringing animals back from pasture.

Tasraḥūna means sending them out to pasture.

In familiar pastoral practice, animals generally go out to graze early in the day and return to their holding place in the evening.

The verbs express two directions of a daily routine:

Going out to pasture and returning from it.

This distinction is also familiar in early Arabic poetry and usage.

Rendering both simply as “going” or “moving” would erase it.

Does tasraḥūna mean abandoning care?

No. The animals belong to their owners and are being sent out to graze.

Al-Nahl 16:5 mentions the warmth, benefits, and food they provide.

Al-Nahl 16:7 also mentions their carrying loads.

They are part of people’s lives and livelihood.

Sending them to pasture does not itself end ownership or responsibility for their care.

A shepherd may accompany them.

This clarifies sarḥ: the way is opened for movement, while the relationship and responsibility depend on context.

The beauty of livestock going out and returning

Al-Nahl 16:6 expresses their beauty with jamāl.

This can concern the sight of a moving herd, healthy or abundant livestock, and the appearance of pastoral life.

Tasraḥūna itself does not mean beauty.

Beauty comes from jamāl.

Tasraḥūna expresses sending the animals forth.

Their occurrence in one verse does not make adornment or beauty the root meaning of sarḥ.

In al-Nahl 16:6, sarḥ denotes sending livestock out to graze, contrasted with bringing them back from pasture.

4. Tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin: release with kindness in al-Baqarah 2:229

Al-Baqarah 2:229 says:

الطَّلَاقُ مَرَّتَانِ فَإِمْسَاكٌ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ

al-ṭalāqu marratāni fa-imsākun bi-maʿrūfin aw tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin

Al-Baqarah 2:229

Translation:

“Divorce is twice; then retention in an acceptable manner or release with kindness.”

Tasrīḥun is the verbal noun of Form II.

It stands opposite imsāk.

Imsāk belongs to M S K and means holding or retaining.

Tasrīḥ belongs to S R Ḥ and means releasing and leaving the way open to depart.

This contrast is particularly clear evidence for its Quranic meaning.

Retaining or releasing

After mentioning two occasions of divorce, the Quran presents two courses.

Either continue the relationship acceptably or release with kindness.

They differ in the resulting state of the relationship.

With imsāk, marriage is maintained or continued.

With tasrīḥ, the woman is released from the bond being brought to an end.

Tasrīḥ is therefore more than pleasant words at divorce.

Release must also become a reality.

Claiming separation while keeping a woman dependent through threats, pressure, or deprivation of her rights is incompatible with release.

In this context, tasrīḥ opens the way to real separation.

What does bi-iḥsānin add?

Iḥsān belongs to Ḥ S N.

Its Quranic uses concern goodness and acting well.

Tasrīḥ bi-iḥsān joins release to a good manner of acting.

The two components should be distinguished.

Tasrīḥ expresses release.

Iḥsān specifies how it is carried out.

If release involves humiliation, revenge, betrayal, or violation of rights, ending the relationship alone does not fulfill the command’s whole purpose.

The continuation of al-Baqarah 2:229 also prohibits taking back what was given to the women except in the situation specified there.

Release with kindness is thus connected with financial rights and God’s limits.

Tasrīḥ itself does not mean paying a dower, giving property, or fulfilling every financial requirement.

Those responsibilities are understood from other commands in the passage.

Is tasrīḥ identical to divorce?

In marriage contexts, tasrīḥ concerns ending the marital bond.

The words nevertheless have different lexical imagery.

Ṭalāq belongs to Ṭ L Q, associated with release from constraint.

The principal movement branch of tasrīḥ concerns sending forth and allowing departure.

Both can refer to the end of marriage.

Ṭalāq names the legal process of separation, while tasrīḥ, opposite imsāk, highlights release and ending retention.

This distinction alone does not establish tasrīḥ as a legal event always separate from divorce; all relevant verses must be considered.

Nor should translation erase the different word choices by making them indistinguishable.

Tasrīḥ and ending uncertainty

Someone may refuse to continue married life while also preventing its clear conclusion.

A spouse may be left with neither a proper shared life nor a way to build an independent future.

The relationship is nominally retained while its responsibilities are neglected.

This conflicts with the contrast between acceptable retention and release with kindness.

The verse qualifies retention with maʿrūf and release with iḥsān.

Neither permits keeping another person in suffering or depriving them of legitimate freedom.

In al-Baqarah 2:229, tasrīḥ means releasing a wife and ending her retention in the marital bond; iḥsān specifies the good manner of that separation.

5. Sarriḥūhunna bi-maʿrūfin: releasing without causing harm

Al-Baqarah 2:231 says:

فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ سَرِّحُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ

fa-amsikūhunna bi-maʿrūfin aw sarriḥūhunna bi-maʿrūfin

Al-Baqarah 2:231

Translation:

“Then retain them in an acceptable manner or release them in an acceptable manner.”

Sarriḥūhunna is a Form II imperative.

Its subject is the plural addressee.

Hunna refers to the women whose divorce is mentioned at the verse’s beginning.

The waiting-period context

Al-Baqarah 2:231 concerns divorced women approaching the end of their waiting period. In this context, balaghna ajalahunna is understood as nearing its end while resumption remains possible, not as establishing a right of return after the period has fully expired.

Here the Quran presents acceptable retention or acceptable release.

Tasrīḥ is thus connected with time, possible continuation of the relationship, and the end of waiting.

It is not merely an inward decision.

It must affect the woman’s actual situation and ability to leave the relationship.

The prohibition of harmful retention

The same verse continues:

وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا لِتَعْتَدُوا

wa-lā tumsikūhunna ḍirāran li-taʿtadū

Al-Baqarah 2:231

Translation:

“And do not retain them to cause harm and transgress.”

This qualifies the concept of retention.

Retaining the relationship accords with the command only when grounded in maʿrūf.

Using return or continuation to hurt, retaliate against, or restrict the woman conflicts with the verse.

Tasrīḥ then offers a way to end a harmful dependency.

That conclusion follows from release together with maʿrūf and the prohibition of harm.

S R Ḥ itself does not mean justice, prohibition of oppression, or financial rights; those criteria come from other parts of the verse.

Comparing al-Baqarah 2:229 and al-Baqarah 2:231

Al-Baqarah 2:229 uses the verbal noun tasrīḥun.

Al-Baqarah 2:231 uses the imperative sarriḥūhunna.

The first presents two courses as a principle: acceptable retention or release with kindness.

The second directly commands action concerning divorced women and their waiting period.

The first also has bi-iḥsānin and the second bi-maʿrūfin.

These qualifications belong to different roots.

Maʿrūf concerns recognized, acceptable conduct.

Iḥsān concerns goodness and acting well.

Both prevent the end of a relationship from being exploited.

Without examining their uses, one should not assign either a definitively lower or higher rank.

Both verses require proper conduct in continuing marriage and forbid turning its end into an instrument of oppression or harm.

6. Sarāḥan jamīlan: gracious separation in al-Ahzab

Sarāḥan jamīlan occurs twice in al-Ahzab.

Both connect release with providing for the woman and parting well.

Their addressees and legal situations differ.

Al-Ahzab 33:28: the choice offered to Muhammad’s wives

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ قُلْ لِأَزْوَاجِكَ إِنْ كُنْتُنَّ تُرِدْنَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا وَزِينَتَهَا فَتَعَالَيْنَ أُمَتِّعْكُنَّ وَأُسَرِّحْكُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu qul li-azwājika in kuntunna turidna al-ḥayāta al-dunyā wa-zīnatahā fa-taʿālayna umattiʿkunna wa-usarriḥkunna sarāḥan jamīlan

Al-Aḥzāb 33:28

Translation:

“O Prophet, say to your wives: If you desire worldly life and its adornment, come, I will provide for you and release you graciously.”

Wa-usarriḥkunna is a first-person singular imperfect verb, jussive in response to the preceding call to come.

Its subject is Muhammad.

Kunna refers to his wives.

Sarāḥan is an accusative action noun specifying the kind of release.

Jamīlan qualifies it.

Choice and ending the relationship

Muhammad is instructed to convey a particular statement to his wives.

If they desire worldly life and its adornment, provision and gracious separation are offered.

Al-Ahzab 33:29 presents the other choice: desiring God, His messenger, and the Hereafter, with a great reward promised to those among them who do good.

The verses therefore concern a particular choice for Muhammad’s wives.

Tasrīḥ in the first means releasing them from marriage.

It does not say that all his wives were divorced then.

The command concerns presenting a choice and its consequences.

Umattiʿkunna and usarriḥkunna

The verse contains two verbs.

Umattiʿkunna belongs to M T ʿ and means providing enjoyment or material provision.

Usarriḥkunna belongs to S R Ḥ and concerns release from the relationship.

They should not be merged.

Providing for them is a distinct responsibility.

Tasrīḥ concerns ending the bond and releasing.

Sarāḥ itself does not determine a financial amount; its amount and limits require the relevant provisions.

The verse nevertheless explicitly associates provision with release.

Al-Ahzab 33:49: separation before marital intimacy

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا نَكَحْتُمُ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ ثُمَّ طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ فَمَا لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِنَّ مِنْ عِدَّةٍ تَعْتَدُّونَهَا فَمَتِّعُوهُنَّ وَسَرِّحُوهُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā nakaḥtumu al-muʾmināti thumma ṭallaqtumūhunna min qabli an tamassūhunna fa-mā lakum ʿalayhinna min ʿiddatin taʿtaddūnahā fa-mattiʿūhunna wa-sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan jamīlan

Al-Aḥzāb 33:49

Translation:

“O you who have believed, when you marry believing women and then divorce them before touching them intimately, you have no waiting period to count against them. So provide for them and release them graciously.”

Wa-sarriḥūhunna is a Form II imperative.

Sarāḥan is an action noun.

Jamīlan is its adjective.

The situation differs from al-Ahzab 33:28.

It concerns women divorced after marriage but before marital intimacy.

No waiting period and freedom to leave

The Quran states that the men have no waiting period to count against the women in this situation.

It then commands provision and gracious release.

This sequence matters.

The woman should not be kept waiting under a restriction not imposed upon her in this situation.

Release must be real.

Sarāḥan jamīlan here concerns opening the way out of marriage while respecting the woman’s rights.

Sarāḥ itself does not mean the absence of a waiting period.

That comes from fa-mā lakum ʿalayhinna min ʿiddatin taʿtaddūnahā: “you have no waiting period to count against them.”

Provision comes from fa-mattiʿūhunna: “so provide for them.”

What does jamīl add to release?

Jamīl belongs to J M L.

Its relevant uses concern beauty and goodness.

The Quran also uses expressions such as ṣabr jamīl, ṣafḥ jamīl, and hajr jamīl: beautiful patience, gracious overlooking, and gracious withdrawal.

In these expressions, jamīl qualifies how the action is performed.

Sarāḥ jamīl joins release with goodness and propriety.

In marriage, this may involve dignity, avoiding harm, safeguarding rights, and ending the relationship respectfully.

Jamīl does not mean only saying a few gentle, polite words.

Pleasant words are insufficient if the woman’s rights are violated or her legitimate freedom is denied.

Nor does jamīl alone determine all financial, legal, and temporal details.

Those must be understood from commands addressing the particular situation.

Does gracious release mean separation without sadness?

Ending a marriage may be difficult for either or both spouses.

It may involve sadness, anxiety, or a sense of loss.

Sadness alone does not make separation wrong.

Nor does a well-conducted separation guarantee the disappearance of every disagreement or practical difficulty.

Sarāḥ jamīl concerns the manner of separation, not the absence of every difficult human feeling.

Someone may grieve the relationship’s end while respecting the other person’s rights and avoiding harm or revenge.

Both al-Ahzab passages concern releasing and ending marriage well; al-Ahzab 33:28 addresses the Prophet’s wives’ choice, while al-Ahzab 33:49 concerns separation before marital intimacy.

7. Distinguishing tasrīḥ, ṭalāq, firāq, and imsāk

The root’s relationship to other Quranic terms for continuing or ending marriage is important.

S R Ḥ (tasrīḥ) and Ṭ L Q (ṭalāq)

Ṭalāq belongs to Ṭ L Q.

This family has established uses for release from constraint.

In the Quran, ṭalāq names a specific process for ending marriage.

Tasrīḥ belongs to the same subject area but emphasizes sending forth and releasing.

Both appear in al-Baqarah 2:229.

First al-ṭalāqu marratāni, “divorce is twice,” then tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin, “release with kindness.”

Al-Ahzab 33:49 likewise has ṭallaqtumūhunna, “you divorced them,” followed by sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan jamīlan, “release them graciously.”

Their proximity shows related subject matter but different roles in the sentence.

Ṭalāq can express the decision and legal act ending marriage.

Tasrīḥ highlights release and leaving the way open for separation.

This does not establish two wholly independent legal events in every case, one called divorce and the other release.

Yet tasrīḥ should not disappear into a translation that obscures the woman’s actual release.

S R Ḥ (tasrīḥ) and F R Q (firāq)

F R Q concerns separating and putting distance between things.

Al-Talaq 65:2 says:

فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ فَارِقُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ

fa-amsikūhunna bi-maʿrūfin aw fāriqūhunna bi-maʿrūfin

Al-Ṭalāq 65:2

Then retain them acceptably or part from them acceptably.

Fāriqūhunna contrasts with amsikūhunna in this verse.

In al-Baqarah 2:231, sarriḥūhunna contrasts with amsikūhunna.

Both expressions thus occur in the ending of marriage.

Firāq emphasizes the parties separating from one another.

Tasrīḥ introduces release and leaving the way open to depart.

This is a linguistic tendency.

It should not be turned without evidence into two entirely different systems for ending marriage.

S R Ḥ (tasrīḥ) and M S K (imsāk)

In marriage contexts, imsāk concerns retaining and continuing the relationship.

Tasrīḥ concerns release from it.

They contrast directly in al-Baqarah 2:229 and al-Baqarah 2:231.

This does not make every retention good and every release bad, or the reverse.

The Quran qualifies both ethically.

Retention must accord with maʿrūf.

Release must accord with iḥsān or maʿrūf.

The action’s value depends on its manner and purpose, not simply on retaining or releasing.

S R Ḥ (tasrīḥ) and R S L (irsāl)

Irsāl concerns sending and dispatching.

Tasrīḥ can also mean sending someone or something forth.

The families overlap here.

The Quran uses irsāl for sending messengers, winds, rain, and other things.

In marriage contexts, tasrīḥ emphasizes release from a bond and opening the way for separation.

Not every use of irsāl therefore means tasrīḥ.

S R Ḥ (surūḥ) and S R B (surūb)

One use of S R B concerns going along a route.

Pastoral S R Ḥ also concerns going forth and moving freely.

Lexical descriptions bring them close in describing livestock movement.

Quranic sārib in al-Raʿd 13:10, however, describes a person going about by day.

Tasraḥūna in al-Nahl 16:6 concerns sending animals to graze.

Semantic proximity should not erase their different subjects and constructions.

S R Ḥ (sarḥ) and Ṣ R Ḥ (ṣarḥ)

Their spellings are close but their first letters differ.

S R Ḥ begins with sīn.

Ṣ R Ḥ begins with ṣād.

Quranic ṣarḥ denotes a palace or lofty building.

Quranic S R Ḥ concerns sending animals to pasture and releasing women when marriage ends.

Similar spelling does not make the roots identical.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The important distinction is between sending livestock forth and releasing a spouse.

In marriage passages, tasrīḥ should also be distinguished from its qualifications bi-iḥsān, bi-maʿrūf, and sarāḥan jamīlan.

Important meanings in the family

Saraḥa: went forth; in pastoral usage, went out to graze.

Sarraḥa: sent forth, released, permitted departure.

Sarḥ: grazing livestock or animals out at pasture, in the relevant use.

Surūḥ: going forth.

Sāriḥ: going or moving; in pastoral usage, a grazing animal or shepherd.

Masraḥ: pasture or where livestock go forth.

Tasrīḥ: sending forth and releasing; in marriage, ending the spouse’s retention in the relationship.

Sarāḥ: release; with jamīlan, gracious separation.

Insaraḥa: was released, went out, or went forth.

Tasrīḥ al-shaʿr: combing and arranging hair.

Sarḥa: a kind of large, tall tree.

Rendering the Quranic forms

Tasraḥūna in al-Nahl 16:6 means “you take out to pasture.”

Tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin in al-Baqarah 2:229 means “release with kindness.”

Sarriḥūhunna bi-maʿrūfin in al-Baqarah 2:231 means “release them in an acceptable manner.”

Sarāḥan jamīlan in al-Ahzab 33:28 and al-Ahzab 33:49 means “a gracious release,” or naturally within the sentence, “release graciously.”

There is no need to pile up near equivalents such as “free, release, and separate” in one rendering.

One natural equivalent suffices; legal and contextual distinctions belong in the explanation.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meaning

For a brief root box:

“Going forth and sending forth; releasing and leaving the way open to depart.”

For al-Nahl 16:6:

“Sending livestock to pasture, contrasted with bringing them back.”

For al-Baqarah 2:229:

“Releasing a wife and ending her retention in the marital relationship, with kindness.”

For al-Baqarah 2:231:

“Releasing divorced women acceptably, contrasted with retaining them acceptably.”

For wa-usarriḥkunna in al-Ahzab 33:28:

“Releasing the Prophet’s wives from marriage if they choose the option stated in the verse.”

For sarāḥan in that verse:

“Release whose gracious manner is specified by jamīl.”

For wa-sarriḥūhunna in al-Ahzab 33:49:

“Releasing women divorced before marital intimacy, without imposing a waiting period not required of them in this situation.”

For sarāḥan in that verse:

“Releasing in a good manner, alongside the command to provide for the women.”

The exact Quranic tokens, including attached pronouns and particles, should be retained in the boxes.

Brief root meaning and meaning in the particular verse should remain separate fields.

9. Conclusion

Arabic S R Ḥ concerns going forth, sending forth, and releasing.

In early pastoral use, saraḥa denotes animals going out to graze and sarraḥa denotes sending them out.

Poetry attributed to ʿAntara ibn Shaddad uses al-sarḥ for grazing animals, though that passage’s attribution and wording are disputed.

His established Muʿallaqa also uses sarḥa for a large, tall tree.

These examples reveal different early Arabic uses that should not be forced into an invented single historical meaning.

The root occurs seven times in the Quran.

Tasraḥūna in al-Nahl 16:6 concerns sending livestock to graze.

Their outward movement contrasts with bringing them back.

Sending forth does not necessarily mean abandoning their care.

Al-Baqarah 2:229 contrasts tasrīḥ bi-iḥsān with imsāk bi-maʿrūf.

It expresses proper continuation of married life or bringing it to an end well.

Al-Baqarah 2:231 repeats this principle through the imperative sarriḥūhunna.

Its prohibition of harmful retention shows that continuing the relationship must not become an instrument of harm or restriction.

Usarriḥkunna sarāḥan jamīlan in al-Ahzab 33:28 concerns the choice offered to Muhammad’s wives.

Sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan jamīlan in al-Ahzab 33:49 concerns separation before marital intimacy.

The absence of a waiting period in that situation and the command to provide for the women accompany gracious release.

Marriage uses thus concern actual release and an open way to separation, not merely a verbal ending.

All financial and legal rules cannot nevertheless be extracted from S R Ḥ alone.

Maʿrūf, iḥsān, jamīl, mattiʿūhunna, and waiting-period provisions each contribute independently to the proper manner of ending a relationship.

Tasrīḥ should also remain distinct from ṭalāq and firāq.

Ṭalāq names the legal process of divorce.

Firāq emphasizes the parties’ separation.

Tasrīḥ highlights release and leaving the way open to depart.

All three can concern one legal situation without identical linguistic imagery.

The principal branch of S R Ḥ denotes going forth and sending forth; transitively, it denotes releasing and allowing movement. In marriage, tasrīḥ concerns releasing a spouse from the bond and enabling real separation.

“Take out to pasture” in al-Nahl 16:6 and “release” in marriage contexts are natural equivalents for Forqan.

The central distinction is that release is not the same as abandonment of responsibility. Sending animals forth need not end their care; ending marriage likewise does not terminate every ethical and legal responsibility toward the other person.

The Quran neither treats retention at any price as virtuous nor licenses oppression through separation. In the provisions using this root, continued married life is joined to maʿrūf, and its ending to iḥsān, maʿrūf, and gracious conduct.
