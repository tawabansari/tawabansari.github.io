The Arabic root و ر ق has several semantic branches, two of which occur in the Qur’an: وَرَق means tree leaves, while وَرِق means silver or silver money.

These words have the same root letters, but differ in the vowel on the letter rāʾ and in meaning. One denotes plant leaves; the other denotes money with which food can be bought.

Tree leaves occur in three verses: once to express the extent of God’s knowledge, and twice in the account of Adam and his spouse. Silver money occurs once in the account of the Companions of the Cave.

Other derivatives are recorded in Arabic for trees putting forth leaves, paper and writing sheets, possession of wealth, and even the gray coloring of certain animals. These meanings cannot all be derived from one simple definition or imposed on Qur’anic usage.

To understand this root, we must first clarify the meanings of leaves and silver money in early Arabic, then examine the setting in which the Qur’an uses each.

1. The root’s meanings in early Arabic

Tree and plant leaves

In early Arabic, وَرَق denoted the leaves of trees and plants: the structures that grow from branches and usually have broad, thin surfaces.

وَرَقَة denotes one leaf, while وَرَق can denote leaves collectively.

This meaning also appears in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

In a verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, the stars are described as follows:

وَقَدْ رَكَدَتْ وَسْطَ السَّمَاءِ نُجُومُهَا

wa-qad rakadat wasṭa al-samāʾi nujūmuhā

رُكُودَ نَوَادِي الرَّبْرَبِ الْمُتَوَرِّقِ

rukūda nawādī al-rabrabi al-mutawarriqi

“Its stars had come to rest in the middle of the sky, like scattered groups of wild cattle resting as they browse on leaves.”

The poet compares the apparently motionless stars to wild animals feeding on plant leaves.

In this verse, الْمُتَوَرِّقِ describes an animal that eats leaves.

The root therefore occurs both in the name of the leaf and in a derivative describing an animal feeding on leaves. The quoted form is an active participle; the corresponding verb also occurs in Arabic.

This witness is transmitted as a verse of Imruʾ al-Qays and agrees with the lexical explanation of تَوَرَّقَ الظَّبْيُ as an animal eating leaves. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not, by itself, establish the exact date of composition and transmission of every line.

Its semantic implication is clear: the botanical branch of و ر ق in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period concerns actual tree and plant leaves.

Another witness occurs in poetry attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar:

كَأَنَّ جِيَادَنَا فِي رَعْنِ قُفٍّ

ka-anna jiyādanā fī raʿni quffin

جَرَادٌ قَدْ أَطَاعَ لَهُ الْوَرَاقُ

jarādun qad aṭāʿa lahu al-warāqu

“Our horses on that rocky rise are like locusts for which green pasture has become available.”

Here الوَرَاق concerns an expanse of green ground and fresh vegetation.

Transmissions of the line differ, including the place description and the forms جيادنا and جيادهن. Some versions also name the poet as Aws ibn Zuhayr.

This witness concerns the nearby sense of greenery and plant growth, rather than a single leaf.

الوَرَاق in this line should therefore not simply be treated as the plural of ورقة without considering its particular meaning.

Silver money and wealth

The second branch is وَرِق, recorded for silver, especially silver used as money.

Classical Arabic records several related forms:

وَرِق, وَرْق, and وِرْق.

Lexicons use these forms for silver and silver dirhams.

Some lexicographers restrict the word to minted dirhams; others also accept its use for silver, whether minted or not.

This variation calls for a distinction between the metal and its monetary form.

In the account of the Companions of the Cave, the context makes clear that the money is taken to buy food. The monetary sense is therefore prominent in that verse.

Lexicons also record the broader sense “wealth and property” for وَرَق with fatḥah on rāʾ, even when the property consists of livestock or other assets.

This broader usage existed in Arabic poetry and speech, but should not be equated with the specific sense of وَرِق in Al-Kahf 18:19.

This study has not established an independently datable poetic witness that fixes the pre-Islamic history of the silver-money sense. A meaning recorded in classical lexicons should not be presented as definite evidence from pre-Islamic poetry merely because the lexicons are old.

Nevertheless, the Qur’anic use of بِوَرِقِكُمْ itself shows that the monetary sense was known in the language of the revelation period.

Was silver money named after tree leaves?

Lexical explanations have sought to connect tree leaves with wealth.

For example, abundant leaves have been compared with abundant property, and falling leaves or a leafless tree have been used to explain a person becoming impoverished.

Such analogies can make the relationship between the branches more tangible.

A semantic analogy alone, however, does not establish a word’s history.

We cannot confidently claim that Arabs first named silver money after its resemblance to a leaf, or that silver acquired this name because wealth becomes plentiful like leaves.

Such conclusions require independent historical evidence.

The two Qur’anic meanings are best defined separately, without constructing a definitive account of their common origin.

2. Forms and semantic branches

Important forms in this Arabic family include:

وَرَق: a tree leaf or leaves collectively.

وَرَقَة: one leaf; the unit noun used to count leaves.

أَوْرَاق: leaves in the plural; also book or manuscript sheets in writing contexts.

أَوْرَقَ: put forth leaves or became leafy, as a tree grows new leaves.

وَرَّقَ: put forth leaves or became leafy. Lexicons also record the basic verb وَرَقَ الشَّجَرَةَ for taking a tree’s leaves; this should not be confused with the doubled form.

وَارِق: leafy; describing a tree that has leaves.

وَرِيق: a description of a plant or tree with abundant or attractive foliage in some lexical usages.

تَوَرَّقَ: in an early animal-related usage, ate leaves or fed on plant foliage.

وَرِق: silver and silver money, especially silver dirhams.

وَرَّاق: in one usage, a person with abundant silver and wealth; in another, someone whose work involves sheets, writing, copying, or books.

وِرَاقَة: the occupation of preparing, writing, or copying books and sheets.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

The gray-color branch

Arabic sources also use أَوْرَق for a color between black and white, or a gray, dusty coloring.

An أَوْرَق camel, for example, has a color tending toward gray or darkness mixed with white.

وَرْقَاء is the corresponding feminine description for some animals and also occurs in names for doves.

This branch differs in meaning from green leaves and silver money.

An image-based relationship might be imagined between the color of some leaves, silver, and gray coloring, but this possibility cannot establish a common historical origin.

The color branch does not occur in the Qur’an and should not be added to the meanings of وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ or بِوَرِقِكُمْ.

Writing sheets and paper

Arabic also uses وَرَق for surfaces on which writing is placed.

This usage can include thin writing sheets and, in later periods, paper and book leaves.

Plant leaves and thin writing sheets share a visible resemblance.

The modern Persian use of “varaq,” which may immediately suggest paper, must not change the meaning of Qur’anic verses.

The Qur’an does not use this root to speak of book sheets or paper.

The spread of paper and occupations involving manuscript copying also has a different history from the use of the word for plant leaves.

We should distinguish the early botanical sense, the writing-related usage recorded in Arabic, and professional terminology of later periods.

The semantic core

This family cannot be reduced to a phrase such as “a thin, broad thing” without losing precision.

Such a description could apply to a leaf, paper, or even a metal sheet, but it is insufficiently specific and does not establish the historical connection among all the branches.

Two clear definitions are needed for Qur’anic usage:

وَرَق and وَرَقَة: tree and plant leaves, collectively or as one individual leaf.

وَرِق: silver or silver money used in contexts of trade and payment.

These branches should be recognized together without imposing the properties of one on the other.

3. Qur’anic forms of the root

The root و ر ق occurs four times in four Qur’anic verses, in three nominal forms.

وَرَقَةٍ in Al-Anʿām 6:59 means a single leaf.

وَرَقِ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121 means plant leaves.

بِوَرِقِكُمْ in Al-Kahf 18:19 means “with your silver money.”

The Qur’an uses no verb from this root.

Verbs such as أَوْرَقَ, وَرَّقَ, and تَوَرَّقَ exist in Arabic, but sharing the root does not make them Qur’anic forms.

The plural أَوْرَاق likewise does not occur in the Qur’an.

In the two verses about Adam and his spouse, وَرَق is a collective noun conveying the meaning “leaves.”

4. Tree leaves in the Qur’an

Al-Anʿām 6:59 — No leaf is hidden from God’s knowledge

وَعِنْدَهُ مَفَاتِحُ الْغَيْبِ لَا يَعْلَمُهَا إِلَّا هُوَ ۚ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا فِي الْبَرِّ وَالْبَحْرِ ۚ وَمَا تَسْقُطُ مِنْ وَرَقَةٍ إِلَّا يَعْلَمُهَا وَلَا حَبَّةٍ فِي ظُلُمَاتِ الْأَرْضِ وَلَا رَطْبٍ وَلَا يَابِسٍ إِلَّا فِي كِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-ʿindahu mafātiḥu al-ghaybi lā yaʿlamuhā illā huwa wa-yaʿlamu mā fī al-barri wa-al-baḥri wa-mā tasquṭu min waraqatin illā yaʿlamuhā wa-lā ḥabbatin fī ẓulumāti al-arḍi wa-lā raṭbin wa-lā yābisin illā fī kitābin mubīn

Al-Anʿām 6:59

“With Him are the keys of the unseen; none knows them except Him. He knows what is on land and in the sea. No leaf falls without His knowing it, nor is there a grain in the darkness of the earth, nor anything moist or dry, except in a clear record.”

Here وَرَقَةٍ is an indefinite singular noun: one leaf.

It occurs, however, within a negative construction:

وَمَا تَسْقُطُ مِنْ وَرَقَةٍ إِلَّا يَعْلَمُهَا

wa-mā tasquṭu min waraqatin illā yaʿlamuhā

Al-Anʿām 6:59

No leaf falls without God knowing it.

In this construction, مِنْ emphasizes the comprehensive scope of the negation.

The statement is therefore not about one particular leaf: it includes every leaf that falls.

Why a tree leaf?

A falling leaf is an ordinary natural event.

Trees and plants have countless leaves. Many fall in places where no human sees them.

A leaf may fall deep in a forest, on a mountain, or in a remote place without anyone becoming aware of it.

The verse says that even such an event lies within God’s knowledge.

Other examples in the same verse convey this extent of knowledge: what is on land and in the sea, a grain in the darkness of the earth, and everything moist or dry.

The leaf is thus part of the expression of God’s comprehensive knowledge; the word itself does not give leaves a special hidden meaning distinct from other parts of nature.

The meaning of وَرَقَة in this verse

وَرَقَة means a leaf.

Falling is expressed by تَسْقُطُ.

God’s knowledge is expressed by يَعْلَمُهَا.

We should therefore not define the root as “something that falls” or “a sign of hidden divine knowledge.”

A leaf may remain on a tree, grow, or fall. In this verse, falling is an event involving the leaf.

The verse explicitly speaks of God’s knowledge. Its closing expression كِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ is a separate subject to be examined in the context of knowledge, recording, and clarity.

No physical mechanism or method for recording all events in the universe can be deduced from و ر ق itself.

One leaf and the extent of knowledge

The choice of a leaf as an example is readily understandable.

A person might count the leaves of one tree, but cannot know about every falling leaf on earth at every moment.

The verse distinguishes the limits of human knowledge from the extent of God’s knowledge.

This meaning comes from the complete statement and agrees with the ordinary meaning of “leaf.”

5. Garden leaves in the account of Adam and his spouse

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 — Trying to cover themselves with leaves

فَدَلَّاهُمَا بِغُرُورٍ ۚ فَلَمَّا ذَاقَا الشَّجَرَةَ بَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْآتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِنْ وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ ۖ وَنَادَاهُمَا رَبُّهُمَا أَلَمْ أَنْهَكُمَا عَنْ تِلْكُمَا الشَّجَرَةِ وَأَقُلْ لَكُمَا إِنَّ الشَّيْطَانَ لَكُمَا عَدُوٌّ مُبِينٌ

fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin fa-lammā dhāqā al-shajarata badat lahumā sawʾātuhumā wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraqi al-jannati wa-nādāhumā rabbuhumā a-lam anhakumā ʿan tilkumā al-shajarati wa-aqul lakumā inna al-shayṭāna lakumā ʿaduwwun mubīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22

“Thus he brought them down through deception. When they tasted the tree, their private parts became apparent to them, and they began piecing garden leaves together over themselves. Their Lord called to them: ‘Did I not forbid you that tree and tell you that Satan is a clear enemy to you?’”

Here وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ means the leaves of the garden.

وَرَق is a collective noun here: leaves as a group.

Its unit noun is وَرَقَة, which occurred in Al-Anʿām 6:59.

The leaf’s role differs between these two verses.

In Al-Anʿām 6:59, a leaf exemplifies beings and events that are not hidden from God’s knowledge.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:22, leaves are the material Adam and his spouse use to cover their bodies.

The role of يَخْصِفَانِ

In وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِنْ وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ, the verb يَخْصِفَانِ comes from خ ص ف.

It concerns joining, placing together, or patching one thing onto another.

The verse does not simply say that they picked up a leaf: it describes leaves being put together to cover the body.

وَطَفِقَا expresses the beginning of this action.

These features come from خ ص ف and the sentence structure, rather than from و ر ق itself.

“Leaf” retains its ordinary botanical meaning.

Were they leaves of a particular tree?

Some reports and historical explanations identify the tree or type of leaves used by Adam and his spouse.

The Qur’an does not name the plant in this verse.

It mentions neither fig leaves nor any other particular plant.

Nor does it say that the leaves used as a covering necessarily came from the tree they had tasted.

A particular plant or leaf type should therefore not be inserted into the translation without Qur’anic evidence.

“Leaves of the garden” is more precise because it conveys what the text says.

Ṭā Hā 20:121 — The same image repeated

فَأَكَلَا مِنْهَا فَبَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْآتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِنْ وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ وَعَصَىٰ آدَمُ رَبَّهُ فَغَوَىٰ

fa-akalā minhā fa-badat lahumā sawʾātuhumā wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraqi al-jannati wa-ʿaṣā ādamu rabbahu fa-ghawā

Ṭā Hā 20:121

“They both ate from it, and their private parts became apparent to them. They began piecing garden leaves together over themselves. Adam disobeyed his Lord and went astray.”

The same expression occurs here:

مِنْ وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ

min waraqi al-jannati

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22; Ṭā Hā 20:121

The principal difference from Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 concerns the context and continuation of the account, rather than the meaning of “leaves.”

In Al-Aʿrāf, their Lord calls to them after this event and recalls His earlier command.

In Ṭā Hā, the description of covering themselves with leaves is followed by mention of Adam’s disobedience.

Ṭā Hā 20:122 then speaks of God choosing Adam, turning toward him, and guiding him.

Both accounts show the leaves being used for the same purpose: covering the body after the private parts become apparent.

Do the leaves symbolize something else?

Because leaves can serve as a covering, a reader might develop an image concerning the concealment of wrongdoing, shame, or human return.

Such a reflection may be considered in its proper place and in light of the account’s context.

The word وَرَق itself, however, does not mean “covering sin,” “concealing truth,” or “repentance.”

The covering action is understood from يَخْصِفَانِ, while the preceding clause describes the exposure of the private parts.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:26, the Qur’an discusses clothing and bodily covering, then introduces the “garment of taqwa.”

This connection may support a broader reflection on covering and human responsibility, but it does not change the lexical meaning of “leaf.”

The root study should therefore preserve the literal meaning of leaves and distinguish it from ethical reflection.

6. Silver money in the account of the Companions of the Cave

Al-Kahf 18:19 — بِوَرِقِكُمْ هَٰذِهِ

وَكَذَٰلِكَ بَعَثْنَاهُمْ لِيَتَسَاءَلُوا بَيْنَهُمْ قَالَ قَائِلٌ مِنْهُمْ كَمْ لَبِثْتُمْ ۖ قَالُوا لَبِثْنَا يَوْمًا أَوْ بَعْضَ يَوْمٍ ۚ قَالُوا رَبُّكُمْ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا لَبِثْتُمْ فَابْعَثُوا أَحَدَكُمْ بِوَرِقِكُمْ هَٰذِهِ إِلَى الْمَدِينَةِ فَلْيَنْظُرْ أَيُّهَا أَزْكَىٰ طَعَامًا فَلْيَأْتِكُمْ بِرِزْقٍ مِنْهُ وَلْيَتَلَطَّفْ وَلَا يُشْعِرَنَّ بِكُمْ أَحَدًا

wa-kadhālika baʿathnāhum li-yatasāʾalū baynahum qāla qāʾilun minhum kam labithtum qālū labithnā yawman aw baʿḍa yawmin qālū rabbukum aʿlamu bi-mā labithtum fa-ibʿathū aḥadakum bi-wariqikum hādhihi ilā al-madīnati fa-l-yanẓur ayyuhā azkā ṭaʿāman fa-l-yaʾtikum bi-rizqin minhu wa-l-yatalaṭṭaf wa-lā yushʿiranna bikum aḥadā

Al-Kahf 18:19

“Thus We awakened them so that they might question one another. One of them asked, ‘How long have you remained?’ They said, ‘We have remained a day or part of a day.’ They said, ‘Your Lord knows best how long you have remained. Send one of you to the city with this silver money of yours. Let him see who has the purest food and bring you some provision from it. Let him act discreetly and make no one aware of you.’”

بِوَرِقِكُمْ consists of three components:

The preposition بِـ, the noun وَرِق, and the plural pronoun كُمْ.

Together they mean “with your silver money.”

Unlike Al-Anʿām 6:59, Al-Aʿrāf 7:22, and Ṭā Hā 20:121, this usage does not refer to plant leaves.

Why does the word mean silver money here?

The context is clear.

After awakening, the Companions of the Cave discuss how long they have remained. They then decide that one of them should go to the city to obtain food.

They make money available to him for that purpose.

وَرِق is therefore a means of payment for buying food here.

Its recorded Arabic meaning agrees with that context: silver and silver money.

“This silver money of yours” is consequently a natural and precise rendering.

“This silver of yours” is also lexically acceptable, but makes its monetary function less clear to the reader.

Must it have consisted of minted coins?

Arabic sources sometimes use وَرِق for minted dirhams.

“Silver coins” is therefore also a common rendering in explanations of this verse.

We should nevertheless distinguish the recorded meaning of the word from what the verse itself establishes.

The verse tells them to take silver money and obtain food with it.

This indicates that silver served as a recognized means of payment in that community.

The expression بِوَرِقِكُمْ هَٰذِهِ does not specify weight, number, coin designs, or the precise method of minting.

“Silver money” is therefore preferable as the base translation: it preserves both material and function without adding unstated historical details.

Vowel differences in the readings

In the familiar transmission of Ḥafṣ, the word is read:

بِوَرِقِكُمْ

With kasrah on rāʾ.

Other readings also record بِوَرْقِكُمْ, with sukūn on rāʾ.

Lexicons also report وِرْق, with kasrah on wāw and sukūn on rāʾ, for the same meaning of silver.

These differences concern pronunciation and word form. They do not, by themselves, change the verse’s subject from silver money to tree leaves.

Both the word’s form and the context of buying food support its monetary meaning.

Does it mean paper or paper money?

No. That reading is unsupported by the linguistic evidence and context.

In modern Persian, “varaq” may suggest paper, and paper money is a familiar means of payment.

In Al-Kahf 18:19, however, the recorded meaning of وَرِق is silver and silver money.

Modern paper currency should not be projected into the setting of the Companions of the Cave.

The fact that both leaves and paper are thin also does not establish that the money mentioned in the verse was made of paper.

This example shows how present-day familiarity with a word can lead to mistranslation of an older text if its semantic history and form are not examined.

Silver money and the companions’ conduct

Later in the verse, the person sent to obtain food is instructed to act discreetly and avoid revealing their presence.

Al-Kahf 18:20 expresses their fear of being harmed or forced back into the others’ religion.

Silver money serves their practical need for food in this account; it is not the central subject of their faith or guidance.

Nor does the word itself establish that possessing silver money made them wealthy.

The amount is unspecified.

What is clear is that they had some silver money available for buying food.

7. Related roots and interpretive challenges

و ر ق (وَرَق) compared with ش ج ر (شَجَر)

In its botanical usage, ش ج ر concerns trees.

وَرَق is part of a tree or plant.

These words occur together in the account of Adam and his spouse.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 first mentions tasting the tree, then describes the use of garden leaves to cover the body.

This distinguishes الشجرة from ورق الجنة as separate expressions.

The verse does not establish that the leaves necessarily came from the tree that was tasted.

و ر ق (وَرَق) compared with خ ص ف (خَصْف)

وَرَق names the leaf.

خَصْف concerns joining, patching, or placing things together to form a covering.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121, leaves are the material used as a covering, while يَخْصِفَانِ expresses the action of putting them together.

The two words should not be translated as though they meant the same thing.

“Leaf” does not itself mean “covering,” although leaves are used to cover in these verses.

و ر ق (وَرِق) compared with ف ض ض (فِضَّة)

فِضَّة names the metal silver.

In monetary usage, وَرِق can denote silver, particularly silver money.

The meanings overlap in some settings.

The name of the metal alone, however, does not establish that money or coins are intended.

In Al-Kahf 18:19, the monetary function is evident from the purchase of food.

“Silver money” suits that verse, but not every use of فِضَّة should be restricted to dirhams or coins.

و ر ق (وَرِق) compared with د ر ه م (دِرْهَم)

دَرَاهِمَ occurs in Yūsuf 12:20.

The dirham was a recognized currency unit or coin.

In its monetary branch, وَرِق denotes silver as a material or silver money.

The words are thus closely related in trading contexts, without being identical.

Dirhams can be a particular form of silver money, but وَرِق does not necessarily denote a specified number of dirhams.

Al-Kahf 18:19 gives no particular number or value for the companions’ money.

و ر ق (وَرَق) compared with ر ق ق (رَقّ)

Their letters and writing-related uses may lead to confusion between these roots.

و ر ق includes plant leaves and, in other uses, writing sheets.

ر ق ق is an independent root associated with thinness and becoming thin.

Al-Ṭūr 52:3 contains the expression فِي رَقٍّ مَنْشُورٍ.

رَقّ here denotes parchment or a prepared writing surface.

Its semantic proximity to a writing sheet does not make it a derivative of و ر ق.

Al-Anʿām 6:7 also uses قِرْطَاس for a writing sheet.

This word likewise does not share the root of وَرَقَة.

Several materials serving as writing surfaces need not have the same root or linguistic history.

Are leaves always thin and green?

Many plants have thin, green leaves, but these features should not become a definition of every derivative of the root.

A leaf may be yellow, red, dry, or thick.

Al-Anʿām 6:59 mentions a falling leaf without specifying its color or size.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121 likewise do not specify the type or size of the leaves.

The image of a broad, thin leaf can help convey its usual form, but a particular color or thickness is not a fixed requirement of the word’s meaning.

Silver and silver money likewise cannot be defined simply by thinness.

8. Proposed renderings for Forqan

“Leaf” or “leaves” suits the three botanical occurrences. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

In Al-Anʿām 6:59, وَرَقَةٍ is singular:

“No leaf falls without His knowing it.”

“A leaf” under negation is more precise here than a plural expression, since the Arabic uses an indefinite singular within a negative construction.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:22:

“They began piecing garden leaves together over themselves.”

In Ṭā Hā 20:121:

“They began piecing garden leaves together over themselves.”

وَرَق is collective in both verses, and “leaves of the garden” is a natural equivalent.

“Fig leaves,” “leaves of the forbidden tree,” or “a finished garment” should not be inserted into the direct translation. Claims identifying such details belong in a separate discussion supported by independent evidence.

A suitable rendering of بِوَرِقِكُمْ هَٰذِهِ in Al-Kahf 18:19 is:

“With this silver money of yours.”

“With this silver of yours” is also lexically defensible.

“Silver money,” however, makes its role in buying food clearer.

“Paper money,” “your leaves,” and “your financial documents” are unsuitable equivalents: those meanings are unsupported by the word’s form and context.

For non-Qur’anic forms too, equivalents should fit the particular usage.

أَوْرَقَ الشَّجَرُ means that the tree put forth leaves.

شَجَرَةٌ وَارِقَةٌ means a leafy tree.

تَوَرَّقَ الظَّبْيُ in early usage means that the gazelle fed on leaves.

In the color branch, أَوْرَق means grayish or having a color mixing darkness and white.

Depending on historical context, وَرَّاق may denote a person with abundant silver money or someone working with writing and preparing sheets.

Each form’s meaning must therefore be determined from its structure and actual usage.

9. Conclusion

و ر ق has two clear Qur’anic branches: tree leaves and silver money.

In the botanical branch, وَرَق denotes leaves, while وَرَقَة denotes one leaf.

The poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates the family in the context of animals browsing on leaves. Lexical evidence also connects it with the emergence of leaves and plants becoming leafy.

In Al-Anʿām 6:59, وَرَقَة expresses the extent of God’s knowledge: no leaf falls without His knowing it.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121, وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ denotes the garden leaves Adam and his spouse use to cover their bodies.

The covering action comes from يَخْصِفَانِ, rather than from و ر ق itself.

The second branch, وَرِق, occurs in Al-Kahf 18:19 for the silver money of the Companions of the Cave.

This agrees with the monetary use of silver in classical Arabic and is supported by the context of buying food.

The verse does not determine the number, weight, or precise shape of the coins.

Arabic also records gray coloring, writing sheets, and the occupational title وَرَّاق. These uses do not occur in the Qur’an, and a definite historical relationship linking all of them to leaves or silver money has not been established here.

The Qur’anic meanings can be defined as follows:

وَرَق and وَرَقَة: tree or plant leaves, collectively or as one individual leaf.

وَرِق: silver and silver money, especially silver used for trade and payment.

Shared root letters should not cause leaves and silver money to be conflated in translation.

Nor should the modern meaning “paper,” or a philosophical image of a leaf as a covering or sign of life, replace the verses’ direct meaning.

For Forqan, “leaf,” “leaves,” and “silver money” clearly convey all the root’s Qur’anic uses, while the root study preserves the history and differences among its semantic branches.
