The Qur’anic Root R Gh B

Desiring, turning toward, turning away, and the direction of human desire

The Arabic root ر غ ب concerns wanting, inclination, and seeking, but the direction of that inclination differs across constructions. رَغِبَ فِي الشَّيْءِ expresses desiring or being interested in something, whereas رَغِبَ عَنْهُ expresses turning away from it or not wanting it. رَغِبَ إِلَيْهِ is used when someone directs a wish or hope toward another and seeks something from them.

The Qur’an clearly displays these differences. Abraham turns away from his people’s deities; another passage discusses turning away from Abraham’s millah; elsewhere, people direct their hopes and requests toward TheGod. All use the same family, but some passages will be mistranslated if the differences in construction are ignored.

The root occurs eight times in eight verses: four Form I verbs, three active participles, and one verbal noun. The verbs are يَرْغَبُ, تَرْغَبُونَ, يَرْغَبُوا, and فَارْغَبْ. The active participle occurs once as singular رَاغِب and twice as plural رَاغِبُونَ; the verbal noun رَغَبًا occurs once. Other Arabic forms include رَغْبَة, رَغِيبَة, رَغَائِب, رَغَّبَ, and ارْتَغَبَ, but these particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Qur’anic forms

رَغِبَ
Form I verb; 4 occurrences

رَاغِب / رَاغِبُونَ
Active participle; 1 singular and 2 plural occurrences

رَغَبًا
Verbal noun; 1 occurrence

Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Desiring and turning away

With فِي, raghiba expresses wanting something or inclining toward it. A person sees something as desirable and feels drawn to it. The object may be wealth, a position, marriage, or something else. The word itself does not determine whether the desire is right or wrong.

In contrast, رَغِبَ عَنْ expresses turning away from or setting aside something. The person does not want it or distances themselves from it. This construction therefore cannot be translated as “be interested in,” as though it were raghiba fī.

The difference is more than a superficial change of preposition. These are established Arabic constructions with different meanings: raghiba fī expresses wanting something, while raghiba ʿan expresses not wanting or turning away from it.

A third construction, رَغِبَ إِلَى, directs inclination and a request toward someone. The person turns toward another to seek something or place hope in them. This explains its place in the language of prayer and petition.

The relationship can be understood through three possibilities: wanting something, turning away from something, and directing a request toward someone. The preposition should nevertheless be understood within the established verbal construction; it does not independently create the entire sentence’s meaning.

Evidence from early Arabic poetry

A verse attributed to Al-Namir ibn Tawlab, a poet who lived through both the pre-Islamic period and the beginnings of Islam, says:

وَإِذَا تُصِبْكَ خَصَاصَةٌ فَارْجُ الْغِنَى
وَإِلَى الَّذِي يُعْطِي الرَّغَائِبَ فَارْغَبْ

Al-Namir ibn Tawlab; transmitted in Khizānat al-Adab, ed. Muḥammad Nabīl Ṭurayfī, p. 313.

“If poverty strikes you, hope for prosperity, and turn with your request toward the one who gives generous gifts.”

Other transmissions have مَتَى instead of إِذَا, and يَهَبُ instead of يُعْطِي. The wording quoted here is recorded in Khizānat al-Adab. These variants do not change the use of al-raghāʾib or the construction ilā … fa-rghab.

Al-raghāʾib denotes generous gifts or desirable things, while fa-rghab is linked to ilā. The poet urges someone in poverty to retain hope and turn toward a giver.

The date of this particular verse has not been independently established. Although the poet also lived before Islam, that does not prove the verse predates the Qur’an. It remains evidence of early Arabic usage, but its date must not be presented with greater certainty than the evidence allows.

The example attests raghiba ilā as a way of directing a request toward someone. The construction establishes the direction of the request; who is asked for what depends on the rest of the sentence and its setting.

Spaciousness and capacity in the same family

Arabic dictionaries also record a branch concerning spaciousness and capacity. رَغِيب can describe something with ample capacity, such as a large basin or vessel. أَرْضٌ رَغَابٌ is recorded for soft, broad ground that takes in much water. This review found no independently dated pre-Qur’anic attestation for the basin, vessel, or ground examples; their inclusion in a dictionary does not itself date their use.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah’s poetry also describes dark camels as الدُّهْمِ الرِّغَابِ. The Dīwān and the transmission in Lisān al-ʿArab explain al-righāb here in terms of numerous camels. Labīd lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, and this review did not independently establish the date of the poem.

وَيَوْمًا مِنَ الدُّهْمِ الرِّغَابِ كَأَنَّهَا
أَشَاءٌ دَنَا قِنْوَانُهُ أَوْ مَجَادِلُ

“And one day, of the numerous dark camels, as though they were palms with clusters hanging near, or lofty palaces.” Al-righāb qualifies the camels; the comparison with palms and palaces depicts their stature and appearance.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, Dīwān, ed. Ḥamdū Ṭammās, p. 86; also Lisān al-ʿArab, entry R Gh B.

This branch shows that the Arabic family was not confined to desire and petition. The existence of a spaciousness sense, however, does not establish that human desire originally developed from an image of an empty vessel or a hungry stomach.

Capacity, receptiveness, and seeking abundance can be connected imaginatively, but such a connection does not prove an etymological history. For Qur’anic usage, the attested meanings of wanting, turning away, and requesting provide a firmer basis.

Raghiba ʿan: turning away and not wanting

Turning away from Abraham’s millah

Al-Baqarah says:

وَمَن يَرْغَبُ عَن مِّلَّةِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ إِلَّا مَن سَفِهَ نَفْسَهُ وَلَقَدِ اصْطَفَيْنَاهُ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَإِنَّهُ فِي الْآخِرَةِ لَمِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

“Who turns away from Abraham’s millah except one who has made a fool of himself? We certainly chose him in this world, and in the Hereafter he will be among the righteous.”

Al-Baqarah 2:130

يَرْغَبُ عَنْ expresses turning away, with مِلَّةِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ as its object.

The construction means leaving or not wanting Abraham’s millah, rather than being interested in it. The preposition ʿan is decisive in identifying this sense.

Raghiba ʿan does not itself mean foolishness or lack of judgment. That evaluation comes from the separate phrase إِلَّا مَن سَفِهَ نَفْسَهُ. The verse connects the act of turning away with this judgment, but the judgment should not be inserted into the root’s lexical definition.

Abraham’s millah must likewise be understood through the surrounding verses and other occurrences of the expression. R Gh B expresses turning away; it does not independently define the content of that millah.

The following verses describe Abraham’s submission to his Lord and the counsel he and Jacob gave their children. This context matters for understanding what is being rejected, while yarghabu ʿan retains its established meaning.

“Are you turning away from my deities?”

Maryam records Abraham’s father replying:

قَالَ أَرَاغِبٌ أَنتَ عَنْ آلِهَتِي يَا إِبْرَاهِيمُ لَئِن لَّمْ تَنتَهِ لَأَرْجُمَنَّكَ وَاهْجُرْنِي مَلِيًّا

“He said, ‘Abraham, are you turning away from my deities? If you do not desist, I will surely stone you. Leave me for a long time.’”

Maryam 19:46

أَرَاغِبٌ combines an interrogative hamzah with the active participle rāghib. Again, ʿan clarifies the direction of its meaning.

Abraham’s father is questioning his son’s rejection of the deities, not asking whether he desires them. The ensuing threat shows that this rejection is unacceptable to the speaker.

Lexically, rāghib ʿan describes someone turning away from or not wanting something. It does not itself identify the cause: understanding, dislike, indifference, or another choice.

Abraham’s reasons for opposing his father’s deities emerge from the wider conversation. His entire argument cannot be reduced to the meaning of one derivative of R Gh B.

Comparison with Al-Baqarah 2:130 is instructive. One passage criticizes turning away from Abraham’s millah; the other describes Abraham turning away from his father’s deities. Raghiba ʿan expresses turning away in both, while its evaluation depends on the object and context.

Turning away is therefore not inherently a virtue or a sin. What matters is what a person turns away from and why.

Preferring one’s own life

Al-Tawbah 9:120 says:

وَلَا يَرْغَبُوا بِأَنفُسِهِمْ عَن نَّفْسِهِ

“Nor should they prefer their own lives to his.”

Al-Tawbah 9:120

The phrase concerns the people of Medina and the desert dwellers around it, who were not to remain behind the messenger of TheGod.

Here the distinctive construction is بِأَنفُسِهِمْ عَن نَّفْسِهِ. The sentence concerns more than turning away from the messenger personally. They should not prefer their own lives and comfort to his or preserve themselves from hardships that he faces.

This differs from turning away from Abraham’s millah. In Al-Baqarah, a millah or way is rejected; in Al-Tawbah, people prefer their own lives in a particular situation.

بِأَنفُسِهِمْ cannot be ignored. The Arabic construction can indicate that someone claims a value or privilege for themselves and consequently holds themselves apart from an activity or situation.

This does not make selfishness, fear of death, or evasion of responsibility the inherent meaning of raghiba. Those features emerge from the people’s circumstances and conduct in context.

The verse also addresses a historical situation and responsibility for accompanying the messenger of TheGod. The lexical meaning alone does not authorize extending every call to take risks or disregard human life to all times and circumstances.

Raghiba ilā: directing desire and hope

“Toward TheGod we direct our hopes”

Al-Tawbah says:

وَلَوْ أَنَّهُمْ رَضُوا مَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ وَرَسُولُهُ وَقَالُوا حَسْبُنَا اللَّهُ سَيُؤْتِينَا اللَّهُ مِن فَضْلِهِ وَرَسُولُهُ إِنَّا إِلَى اللَّهِ رَاغِبُونَ

“If only they had been content with what TheGod and His messenger gave them and had said, ‘TheGod is sufficient for us. He will give us from His bounty, and His messenger too. Toward TheGod we direct our desires and hopes’…”

The verse begins with law and leaves the consequence of the condition unstated; the ellipsis in the translation preserves that open conditional structure.

Al-Tawbah 9:59

Rāghibūn is the plural active participle of rāghib, here with ilā Allāh. The phrase expresses turning toward TheGod and directing desire and hope toward Him.

The contrast with rāghib ʿan is clear. In Maryam 19:46, rāghib ʿan ālihatī concerns rejecting deities; here, ilā Allāhi rāghibūn expresses turning toward TheGod and placing hope in Him.

The verse also uses رَضُوا, expressing contentment. Contentment and desire are related but distinct: a person can be content with what they have received while hoping for further generosity and provision.

حَسْبُنَا اللَّهُ declares the sufficiency of TheGod. The verse then mentions His bounty and what is given through His messenger, before identifying TheGod as the ultimate direction of desire.

Receiving, being content, and hoping are distinguishable in this sequence. Raghbah is neither contentment with the past nor simply the name of something received; it expresses the direction of desire and hope.

The messenger is also named in connection with giving, but إِلَى اللَّهِ رَاغِبُونَ explicitly identifies TheGod as the destination of desire. That distinction belongs to this sentence’s construction. Rāghibūn alone does not explain how bounty is received or every relationship between giver and recipient.

Rāghibūn in the story of the garden owners

Al-Qalam says:

عَسَىٰ رَبُّنَا أَن يُبْدِلَنَا خَيْرًا مِّنْهَا إِنَّا إِلَىٰ رَبِّنَا رَاغِبُونَ

“Perhaps our Lord will replace it with something better. Toward our Lord we direct our desires and hopes.”

Al-Qalam 68:32

Rāghibūn again accompanies ilā, but the speakers are garden owners who have lost their crop.

They had planned to prevent any needy person from entering at harvest. After the garden’s destruction, they recognized their conduct, reproached one another, and acknowledged that they had been unjust and transgressive. They then directed their hope for a better replacement toward their Lord.

Rāghibūn expresses desire and hope here. The object of their hope appears immediately before it: أَن يُبْدِلَنَا خَيْرًا مِّنْهَا.

The word should therefore not be translated as freedom from needing wealth or complete renunciation of worldly wishes. They still hope to receive something better than the lost garden.

Their desire follows an acknowledgment of injustice and transgression. This context may indicate a reassessment of their conduct, but rāghibūn itself does not mean repentance or moral reform.

These are also the garden owners’ words. The Qur’an reports them, but the statement alone does not establish that they received another garden in this world. Hope and petition are distinct from certain fulfillment.

“Toward your Lord direct your desire”

Al-Sharḥ ends:

فَإِذَا فَرَغْتَ فَانصَبْ

“When you have finished, exert yourself.”

Al-Sharḥ 94:7

وَإِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ فَارْغَبْ

“And toward your Lord direct your desire.”

Al-Sharḥ 94:8

Fa-rghab is an imperative from R Gh B. إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ again specifies the direction of desire.

The construction commands the addressee to direct inclination, desire, and hope toward the Lord.

Placing إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ before the verb foregrounds that direction. The verse specifies the destination rather than merely commanding desire in general.

Fa-rghab does not independently mean performing a particular rite, reciting a specified prayer, or requesting a particular object. Those require contextual or other Qur’anic evidence. The verb expresses turning toward someone with desire and a request.

In the preceding verse, فَرَغْتَ belongs to the separate root F R Gh and concerns finishing an activity. Its sound resemblance to fa-rghab should not blur the roots: one concerns finishing, the other desire and turning toward.

The two verses place exertion and turning toward the Lord together. Without further evidence, their sequence should not become an absolute rule that desire begins only after all of a person’s work has ended.

Raghaban wa rahaban: desire alongside fear

After recounting the answer to Zechariah’s prayer, Al-Anbiyāʾ says:

فَاسْتَجَبْنَا لَهُ وَوَهَبْنَا لَهُ يَحْيَىٰ وَأَصْلَحْنَا لَهُ زَوْجَهُ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا يُسَارِعُونَ فِي الْخَيْرَاتِ وَيَدْعُونَنَا رَغَبًا وَرَهَبًا وَكَانُوا لَنَا خَاشِعِينَ

“We answered him, granted him John, and set his wife right for him. They used to hasten to good deeds and call upon Us with hope and fear, and they were humble before Us.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:90

Raghaban is a verbal noun expressing desire and hope. Alongside it, rahaban comes from R H B and concerns fear or apprehension.

Both words relate to يَدْعُونَنَا. The petitioners called upon TheGod with raghbah and rahbah: desire and hope, together with fear, were present in their calling.

Grammatically, the verbal nouns can describe the state or motivation of the petitioners. For understanding the passage, the central point is that they characterize the manner and motive of prayer, rather than name two separate ceremonies.

R Gh B expresses inclination and hope, while R H B retains its own meaning. Fear should not be incorporated into the lexical meaning of raghbah, nor should their conjunction imply that every human desire necessarily involves fear.

The verse continues with خَاشِعِينَ, concerning humility and a person’s bearing before TheGod. It thus combines three related descriptions: desire, fear, and humility. Their relationship does not make them synonyms.

يُسَارِعُونَ فِي الْخَيْرَاتِ describes their activity and effort. Desire and fear accompany good deeds here, but R Gh B itself does not mean hastening to do good.

The example places desire within prayer and worship. Its lexical range nevertheless extends beyond prayer: in Arabic, someone may desire wealth, marriage, standing, or another object.

“Wa targhabūna an tankiḥūhunna”: an important ambiguity in Al-Nisāʾ

Al-Nisāʾ says:

وَيَسْتَفْتُونَكَ فِي النِّسَاءِ قُلِ اللَّهُ يُفْتِيكُمْ فِيهِنَّ وَمَا يُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ فِي الْكِتَابِ فِي يَتَامَى النِّسَاءِ اللَّاتِي لَا تُؤْتُونَهُنَّ مَا كُتِبَ لَهُنَّ وَتَرْغَبُونَ أَن تَنْكِحُوهُنَّ وَالْمُسْتَضْعَفِينَ مِنَ الْوِلْدَانِ وَأَن تَقُومُوا لِلْيَتَامَىٰ بِالْقِسْطِ وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ بِهِ عَلِيمًا

“They ask you for a ruling concerning women. Say: TheGod gives you a ruling concerning them, as does what is recited to you in the Book concerning orphan women whom you do not give what is prescribed for them and whom you desire to marry; and concerning vulnerable children, and that you uphold justice for orphans. Whatever good you do, TheGod knows it.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:127

The translation above renders وَتَرْغَبُونَ أَن تَنْكِحُوهُنَّ as desiring marriage. Linguistic analysis also considers turning away from marriage by understanding ʿan. These are possible interpretations of one expression, not two different Qur’anic recitations. Their grammatical possibility alone does not establish equal contextual weight.

Targhabūna appears here without fī or ʿan, followed by أَن تَنْكِحُوهُنَّ.

Raghiba fī an yankiḥa expresses wanting to marry, while raghiba ʿan an yankiḥa expresses not wanting or turning away from marriage. The preposition is unstated in this verse, allowing both possibilities to be considered linguistically.

With fī understood, the phrase describes a desire to marry women whose prescribed rights the addressees are withholding. The verse places these two behaviors together; targhabūna itself does not reveal the people’s precise motives.

With ʿan understood, the phrase describes not wanting to marry those women. Withholding their rights remains explicit in this interpretation, too. Preventing them from marrying others, or the detailed motives for such conduct, is not stated in this expression itself.

Both possibilities relate to the verse’s concern with orphan women’s rights and justice. Lexically, however, they differ and should not be joined by “and” in a basic translation, as though targhabūna simultaneously meant both wanting and not wanting within one construction.

This analysis supplies fī or ʿan in understanding the sentence, although neither is explicit. Translators should explain their choice through context and distinguish the alternative from their selected translation.

Was the preposition omitted to convey both meanings?

One might ask whether leaving fī and ʿan unstated was intended to keep both meanings open.

An omitted preposition can permit more than one interpretation linguistically. That possibility alone does not prove an intention to express both opposing senses simultaneously.

Such a conclusion requires examination of syntax, context, and reliable evidence. Two grammatical possibilities do not by themselves establish a particular rhetorical purpose in the Qur’an.

The verse explicitly concerns withholding orphan women’s prescribed rights and the requirement of justice. وَأَن تَقُومُوا لِلْيَتَامَىٰ بِالْقِسْطِ makes this central concern still clearer.

Disagreement about the precise direction of targhabūna therefore cannot set aside justice. Both interpretations remain within a situation in which orphan women’s rights must be respected.

مَا كُتِبَ لَهُنَّ must also be examined in the context of their prescribed rights. It should not be confined without evidence to either a marriage payment or inheritance, then used to make one interpretation of targhabūna certain on that assumption.

The verse illustrates how a verb’s meaning can require examination of its accompanying prepositions and construction. Where uncertainty remains, acknowledging it matters more than selecting a translation hastily.

Comparison with related roots

R Gh B and R J W: desire and hope

Rajāʾ concerns hoping for and expecting something desirable. Raghbah likewise involves wanting and inclination, but often foregrounds the direction of desire, seeking something, or turning toward someone.

In Al-Qalam 68:32, the garden owners hope that their Lord will give them something better, then say إِنَّا إِلَىٰ رَبِّنَا رَاغِبُونَ. Expecting a desirable outcome and directing a request toward TheGod are closely related here.

It does not follow that rajāʾ always means passive expectation and raghbah always practical action. Both can accompany desire and effort; their distinction depends on the particular use.

R Gh B and Ṭ M ʿ: desire and expectation of gain

Ṭamaʿ concerns hoping or expecting to obtain something and can express greed or excessive desire in some contexts. Raghbah can also convey strong desire but does not inherently mean blameworthy greed.

These semantic fields are close in early Arabic. Nevertheless, not every raghbah is ṭamaʿ, just as not every ṭamaʿ should be translated as morally objectionable greed.

The object and manner of seeking determine the evaluation.

Raghiba ʿan and Z H D: turning away and lack of interest

Zuhd concerns little interest, indifference, or not wanting something. In Yūsuf 12:20, مِنَ الزَّاهِدِينَ describes people who showed little interest in Joseph.

Raghiba ʿan can likewise express not wanting and turning away, but the constructions and uses are not identical. It establishes a relationship of turning away from something, while Z H D can emphasize low valuation or lack of interest.

Later technical uses of zuhd for a spiritual discipline or renunciation of the world should not be imposed on every raghiba ʿan. Abraham’s rejection of his father’s deities is not simply ascetic conduct in that later technical sense.

Raghaban and rahaban: desire and fear

The roots occur together in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:90. R Gh B concerns desire and hope; R H B concerns fear and apprehension.

Their juxtaposition shows that someone can pray with hope for a desirable outcome and fear of an unwelcome one. It does not imply that every desire requires fear or that the two must always exist in equal measure.

The verse attributes both states to the petitioners. Turning them into a fixed psychological formula or a system of stages in worship would require further evidence.

Translation proposals

Depending on context, raghiba ʿan may be rendered “turn away from,” “not want,” “distance oneself from,” or “set aside.” “Turn away from” conveys the construction well in Al-Baqarah 2:130 and Maryam 19:46.

In Al-Tawbah 9:120, وَلَا يَرْغَبُوا بِأَنفُسِهِمْ عَن نَّفْسِهِ must be translated as a complete construction. “Nor should they prefer their own lives to his” is more accurate and natural than a word-for-word rendering such as “desire with their lives away from his life.”

For raghiba ilā, suitable renderings include “turn toward with desire and hope,” “seek from someone,” and “direct one’s request toward.” In Al-Tawbah 9:59 and Al-Qalam 68:32, directing desire and hope toward TheGod makes the relationship clear.

In Al-Sharḥ 94:8, “direct your desire toward your Lord” naturally preserves the verb’s direction. Fa-rghab should not be confined to “love” or “wish,” since إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ specifies where that desire is directed.

Raghaban in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:90 can be rendered “with hope” or “with desire”; wa rahaban adds “and with fear.” “With hope and fear” therefore translates the pair, not raghaban alone.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:127, understanding fī yields “whom you desire to marry,” while understanding ʿan yields “whom you are unwilling to marry.” The translation above adopts the first possibility. The other belongs in explanation, rather than combining opposing meanings as the word’s certain sense. A final choice requires contextual analysis; grammatical possibility alone does not establish equal weight.

Conclusion

The central use of R Gh B concerns inclination, wanting, and seeking. Raghiba fī expresses wanting something; raghiba ʿan expresses turning away from or not wanting it; raghiba ilā directs desire and hope toward someone. Arabic also records a branch concerning spaciousness and capacity, but its historical relationship to desire is not conclusively established.

Qur’anic uses do not make raghbah invariably commendable or blameworthy. Turning away from Abraham’s millah is criticized, while Abraham’s rejection of his father’s deities belongs to a different setting. Elsewhere people direct desire and hope toward TheGod, and one passage combines raghbah with rahbah to describe petitioners.

The defensible semantic core is wanting and inclination, whose direction is specified by established constructions: toward something desirable, away from something, or toward a person. The exact meaning therefore requires attention to syntax and accompanying prepositions.

The central translation concern is preserving the differences among raghiba fī, raghiba ʿan, and raghiba ilā. Al-Nisāʾ 4:127 also shows that an unstated preposition can leave more than one linguistic possibility, without allowing one to be declared certain without evidence. Attending to these differences helps clarify the verses’ meaning.
