Central meanings: roasting and scorching with heat; in another branch, the scalp, extremities, and peripheral parts of the body.

The Arabic root SH W Y has two important semantic branches, both used in the Quran.

In the first, شَوَى means exposing something to heat to cook or roast it. Meat or another substance changes under the heat of a fire. The same verb can also describe scorching skin and faces.

In the second, شَوَى denotes the scalp, bodily extremities, and parts such as hands and feet. Related Arabic uses concern injuries away from vital organs, small remainders, and treating something as relatively insignificant.

Sharing the same root letters does not automatically make these branches identical. The association of the verb شَوَى with roasting does not mean that the noun الشَّوَى always denotes something roasted.

The root occurs twice in the Quran: as the verb يَشْوِي in Al-Kahf 18:29 and as the noun الشَّوَىٰ in Al-Maʿārij 70:16.

1. The root in early Arabic

شَوَى: cooking and roasting with heat

In early Arabic, شَوَى اللَّحْمَ meant roasting meat.

In this process, meat is exposed to the heat of fire and cooked by it.

Someone might place a piece of meat over a fire or near glowing coals. Heat reaches it and changes its color, texture, and condition.

Arabic calls this action شَيّ and the resulting roasted food شِوَاء.

Here the meaning concerns the effect of heat on an exposed object. When food is involved, the usual purpose is cooking and preparing it to eat.

In another use, the same verb describes scorching with intense heat, without any intention of preparing food.

Roasting and scorching are therefore clearly related, but the purpose and outcome must be understood from context.

An early Arabic poetic example

A poem by Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

وَغُلَامٍ أَرْسَلَتْهُ أُمُّهُ
بِأَلُوكٍ فَبَذَلْنَا مَا سَأَلْ

أَوْ نَهَتْهُ فَأَتَاهُ رِزْقُهُ
فَاشْتَوَى لَيْلَةَ رِيحٍ وَاجْتَمَلْ

wa-ghulāmin arsalat-hu ummuhu / bi-alūkin fa-badhalnā mā saʾal / aw nahat-hu fa-atāhu rizquhu / fa-ishtawā laylata rīḥin wa-ijtamal

“Many a boy his mother sent with a request, and we gave what he asked; or she held him back, yet his provision reached him, and on a windy night he roasted meat and rendered fat.”

In this poem, فَاشْتَوَى belongs to this family and refers to preparing roasted food.

Labīd lived through both the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. This particular passage is not independently dated, so it cannot confidently be presented as a pre-Islamic witness.

Nevertheless, it illustrates the use of this family for roasting meat in Arabic close to the Quranic period.

It also agrees with the meanings of شَيّ and شِوَاء recorded in classical lexicons.

الشَّوَى: skin and bodily extremities

The other branch refers to particular parts of human and animal bodies.

Arabic lexicons record الشَّوَى for hands, feet, bodily extremities, and, in some uses, the scalp.

شَوَاة also means the skin of the head, with شَوَى recorded as its plural.

In descriptions of quadrupeds, شَوَى الفَرَسِ refers to a horse’s legs.

For example, فَرَسٌ عَبْلُ الشَّوَى describes a horse with thick, strong legs.

Here الشوى identifies its limbs; it does not concern roasting the horse or the heat of fire.

A poetic example from Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī

A poem attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī includes this line; its second hemistich has variant readings:

إِذَا هِيَ قَامَتْ تَقْشَعِرُّ شَوَاتُهَا
وَيُشْرِقُ بَيْنَ اللِّيتِ مِنْهَا إِلَى الصُّقْلِ

idhā hiya qāmat taqshaʿirru shawātuhā / wa-yushriqu bayna al-līti minhā ilā al-ṣuql

“When that doe rises, her skin shivers, and a gleam appears from the side of her neck toward her flank.”

Lisān al-ʿArab explains شَوَاتُهَا in this example as the outer surface of the skin as a whole.

That explanation connects the word with the span from the side of the neck to the flank. The lexicon’s version reads وَتُشْرِفُ, while the version quoted here reads وَيُشْرِقُ; the difference should remain visible.

Thus the broader sense of skin is supported by a recorded lexical explanation, rather than being inferred solely from the juxtaposition of body parts in the line.

Abū Dhuʾayb also lived through the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. This particular poem is not independently dated here, but it illustrates early Arabic usage.

The nominal branch شَوَى therefore concerns skin and bodily extremities, a meaning relevant to Al-Maʿārij 70:16.

شَوَى and parts whose injury is not necessarily fatal

Other Arabic uses contrast شَوَى with vital parts of the body.

Accordingly, رَمَاهُ فَأَشْوَاهُ describes shooting at someone and hitting an extremity without striking a vital spot.

For example, an arrow might hit an animal’s leg without reaching its heart or another vital organ.

The arrow has struck, but the injury has not necessarily been fatal.

Arabic expresses this distinction with forms from SH W Y.

This does not mean that injuries to hands, feet, or the head can never be fatal. The expression contrasts a direct hit to a vital spot with one that misses it.

From this image, أَشْوَى الرَّامِي also acquired the broader sense of the archer missing or failing to strike the intended target.

شَوَى: something slight or left over

Arabic sources also record الشَّوَى for something slight, insignificant, or remaining.

The sense of relative insignificance is associated with the image of a nonfatal hit.

Someone who survives a major danger may regard a smaller injury as negligible by comparison.

A poem attributed to al-Burayq al-Hudhalī says:

وَكُنْتُ إِذَا الْأَيَّامُ أَحْدَثْنَ هَالِكًا
أَقُولُ شَوًى مَا لَمْ يُصِبْنَ صَمِيمِي

wa-kuntu idhā al-ayyāmu aḥdathna hālikan / aqūlu shawan mā lam yuṣibna ṣamīmī

“Whenever the days brought death or calamity, I would say: it is slight, so long as they have not struck my very core.”

Here شَوَى contrasts with صَمِيم.

صميم denotes the core or central part of something; شوى here is what is regarded as relatively insignificant beside that core.

Without independent dating of this particular passage, it should not be presented as a securely pre-Islamic witness.

Its semantic contrast is clear: the language connects peripheral parts with something considered less significant than the essential or vital core.

Lexicons also record شَوَى for inferior livestock and شُوَايَة for a small portion or remainder of something.

These uses help map the wider family, but neither Quranic occurrence means inferior livestock or a small remainder.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

شَوَى: exposed meat or something else to heat and roasted it.

يَشْوِي: roasts or scorches with intense heat.

شَيّ: the verbal noun of شَوَى; roasting.

شِوَاء: roasted food.

شَوِيّ and مَشْوِيّ: something roasted.

شِوَاءَة: a piece of roasted food.

شَوَّى: roasted something in a transitive use; in another use, gave people roasted food or meat to roast.

اشْتَوَى: prepared roasted food; some authorities also record its use for meat becoming roasted.

انْشَوَى: became roasted.

الشَّوَى: the scalp, extremities, and peripheral body parts; in other uses, something slight or remaining.

شَوَاة: the scalp; in certain poetic explanations, the outer skin more broadly.

أَشْوَى: in archery, hit a nonvital part or missed the intended vital target.

شُوَايَة: a small piece or remainder of something.

Of these forms, the Quran uses only يَشْوِي and الشَّوَى.

يَشْوِي is an imperfect verb, whereas الشَّوَى in Al-Maʿārij is a noun.

The noun الشَّوَى should therefore not be translated as “roasted people” or “roasted things” merely because it shares this root.

3. Quranic usage: scorching faces

Al-Kahf 18:29

وَقُلِ الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ ۖ فَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيُؤْمِنْ وَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيَكْفُرْ ۚ إِنَّا أَعْتَدْنَا لِلظَّالِمِينَ نَارًا أَحَاطَ بِهِمْ سُرَادِقُهَا ۚ وَإِنْ يَسْتَغِيثُوا يُغَاثُوا بِمَاءٍ كَالْمُهْلِ يَشْوِي الْوُجُوهَ ۚ بِئْسَ الشَّرَابُ وَسَاءَتْ مُرْتَفَقًا

wa-quli al-ḥaqqu min rabbikum fa-man shāʾa fa-l-yuʾmin wa-man shāʾa fa-l-yakfur innā aʿtadnā li-l-ẓālimīna nāran aḥāṭa bihim surādiquhā wa-in yastaghīthū yughāthū bi-māʾin ka-l-muhli yashwī al-wujūha biʾsa al-sharābu wa-sāʾat murtafaqā

Al-Kahf 18:29

“And say: The truth is from your Lord; so whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him deny. We have prepared for the wrongdoers a fire whose enclosing canopy surrounds them. If they seek relief, they will be given relief with water like molten metal that scorches faces. What an evil drink, and what an evil resting place!”

Al-Kahf 18:29

Here يَشْوِي is an imperfect verb from SH W Y.

Its contextual meaning is scorching with intense heat.

الْوُجُوهَ, “faces,” is the verb’s object.

Thus يَشْوِي الْوُجُوهَ means “scorches faces,” or, preserving the roasting image more literally, “roasts faces.”

Its relation to بِمَاءٍ كَالْمُهْلِ

The verb يَشْوِي follows بِمَاءٍ كَالْمُهْلِ.

مَاء denotes water or liquid. Classical explanations of مُهْل include molten metal and oil dregs; “like molten metal” follows the former explanation, rather than exhausting the word’s recorded meanings.

The following يَشْوِي الْوُجُوهَ states its scorching effect.

The scorching of faces belongs to the intense heat of the liquid described.

Very hot water, for example, can burn skin on contact; intense heat from molten substances can also injure skin before direct contact.

This example only clarifies the effect of heat. The verse specifies neither an exact temperature nor the physical mechanism involved.

The lexical point is that يَشْوِي does not mean preparing food here.

The verb used in Arabic for roasting meat describes scorching faces in this context.

Its meaning must therefore not be limited to food preparation.

The root meaning and the complete image of punishment

Several distinct images occur together in the verse.

نَارًا names a fire.

سُرَادِقُهَا concerns its enclosing canopy or surrounding enclosure.

يَسْتَغِيثُوا expresses seeking relief.

يُغَاثُوا describes the response to that request.

كَالْمُهْلِ characterizes the liquid through comparison.

يَشْوِي specifies its scorching effect on faces.

The root’s meaning here is scorching with heat. Enclosure by fire, the request for help, and the character of the drink come from other words in the verse.

The opening presents belief and denial as choices in a context of warning, then describes the wrongdoers’ outcome. Mentioning the choice does not amount to approving denial.

A warning about an outcome in the hereafter is not, by itself, a command for people to burn or punish those who hold different beliefs.

Lexically, يَشْوِي describes the effect of heat; it does not establish an earthly rule for treating others.

4. Quranic usage: the scalp and bodily extremities

Al-Maʿārij 70:16

نَزَّاعَةً لِلشَّوَىٰ

nazzāʿatan li-l-shawā

Al-Maʿārij 70:16

“Stripping away the scalp.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:16

This follows Al-Maʿārij 70:15, which speaks of the blazing fire لَظَىٰ.

نَزَّاعَةً belongs to N Z ʿ and expresses forceful pulling out or stripping away.

الشَّوَىٰ denotes what that action affects.

The word الشَّوَىٰ itself therefore does not mean stripping or burning; it identifies what is stripped away.

Which body part does الشَّوَىٰ denote?

Lexicons record several related meanings of الشَّوَى.

One is the scalp.

Another is the hands, feet, and other bodily extremities.

Certain poetic explanations also extend the word to the outer skin.

Some early lexical explanations of this verse take الشوى as the plural of شواة, meaning the skin of the head.

Other accounts include the hands, feet, fingertips, and parts of the head.

These uses are related in that they concern bodily surfaces or extremities rather than internal organs such as the heart or stomach.

Yet they cannot all be reduced to one identical anatomical definition.

The scalp is one particular body part; hands and feet are others; outer skin can cover a much wider area.

It is therefore better to recognize the recorded senses separately and then choose an appropriate rendering for the verse.

Why “the scalp” is a defensible translation

In Al-Maʿārij 70:16 the word accompanies نَزَّاعَةً.

Here the root N Z ʿ evokes pulling or stripping something off.

The scalp can be described as the object of such stripping.

“Stripping away the scalp” consequently agrees with a recognized meaning of الشوى and clearly conveys the action of نَزَّاعَةً.

It is not, however, the word’s only recorded lexical sense.

Taking الشوى as skin and bodily extremities would give the image a broader scope.

The recorded meanings also allow that reading.

A base translation may choose “the scalp,” while the lexical discussion explains the broader range.

Does الشَّوَىٰ here mean something roasted?

It names a bodily part, rather than the result of roasting.

Although يَشْوِي in Al-Kahf 18:29 concerns scorching and roasting, الشَّوَىٰ in Al-Maʿārij 70:16 is a noun naming body parts.

The sentence structures also show the difference.

In Al-Kahf, يَشْوِي is the verb and الْوُجُوهَ its object.

In Al-Maʿārij, نَزَّاعَةً expresses stripping away and الشَّوَىٰ denotes what undergoes it.

الشوى in Al-Maʿārij 70:16 should therefore not be translated as “roasted meat.”

Conversely, يَشْوِي in Al-Kahf 18:29 should not be rendered as injuring hands and feet: its object is explicitly الْوُجُوهَ, “faces.”

These two verses illustrate why the root, the particular form, and its function in the sentence must be distinguished.

5. The relationship between the two semantic branches

Are roasting and skin or extremities historically connected?

Both are recorded within the lexical family SH W Y.

Skin and bodily surfaces can also be exposed to heat and burned.

This association may help a reader imagine a connection between the two domains.

It does not establish that شواة originally meant roasted skin and subsequently became a name for the scalp.

Nor does it establish that the roasting verb necessarily arose from the name of skin or extremities.

The branches coexist in lexical records, but their precise historical origins and relationship are not independently established here.

It is therefore better to present SH W Y through its two main branches than impose one artificial definition on every use.

From extremities to something relatively insignificant

The association between extremities and some other uses is easier to understand.

When شوى describes an arrow hitting a nonvital part, the contrast is between a peripheral injury and a fatal hit.

Against this background, its use for something slight beside the central concern is understandable.

For example, someone may lose part of their possessions while their life and family remain safe, and regard the loss as slight compared with a greater injury.

This illustrates the word’s comparative evaluation.

Such an evaluation is not inherent in the material meaning “scalp”; it belongs to a broader linguistic use.

Neither Quranic occurrence of this root has the meaning “insignificant.”

6. Comparison with related roots

SH W Y (شَيّ) and Ḥ R Q (حَرْق)

حَرَقَ concerns burning with fire or heat.

شَوَى can also describe scorching by heat, but in food preparation it refers to roasting.

Cooking meat over a fire is شَيّ; when something burns and is destroyed, حرق may be more appropriate.

In Al-Kahf 18:29, the domains of burning and intense heat overlap.

Yet يَشْوِي evokes the effect on faces through a verb from the roasting family.

“Scorches” is a natural translation, while the more literal “roasts” preserves that particular Arabic image more explicitly.

SH W Y (شَيّ) and Ṣ L Y (صَلْي)

Quranic uses of Ṣ L Y concern exposure to fire and undergoing its burning heat.

شَوَى in Al-Kahf 18:29 describes the effect that heat has on faces.

Something may be inside a fire or be exposed to intense heat from outside and burned.

These situations can coincide but are not identical.

يَشْوِي should therefore not be rendered merely as “makes enter the fire.”

The verb itself expresses scorching or roasting; the person’s or object’s location comes from context.

SH W Y (الشَّوَى) and J L D (جِلْد)

جِلْد is a general word for skin.

One recognized use of شَوَاة is specifically the scalp.

Some explanations of poetic شَوَى extend it to the outer skin more broadly.

The two families overlap in reference to skin, but are not wholly identical.

جلد can denote skin on any body part, whereas شواة has a specific scalp sense and شوى can also denote hands and feet.

Rendering الشوى as “the scalp” in Al-Maʿārij 70:16 selects a particular recorded use; it does not identify every meaning of جلد with every meaning of شوى.

SH W Y (الشَّوَى) and N Z ʿ (نَزْع)

نَزَعَ concerns pulling, extracting, or separating something.

شَوَى in Al-Maʿārij 70:16 names bodily parts.

نَزَّاعَةً لِلشَّوَىٰ therefore combines two distinct meanings: stripping away, and what is stripped away.

The distinction matters in translation.

Defining الشوى alone as “skinning” would merge the action with what undergoes it.

The stripping comes from N Z ʿ; the scalp or extremities come from SH W Y.

SH W Y (شَيّ) and SH W H (تَشْوِيه)

شَوَى and شَوَّهَ resemble one another in spelling and sound but belong to different roots.

شَوَى, ending in the root letter yāʾ, concerns roasting.

شَوَّهَ belongs to SH W H and commonly means making something ugly or misshapen.

تَشْوِيه can also be used figuratively for distorting or misrepresenting a truth.

This is not a direct meaning of SH W Y.

Severe burning may alter bodily appearance, but that consequence does not make شوى and شوّه the same root.

7. Suggested Forqan translations

يَشْوِي — Al-Kahf 18:29

Brief root meaning: roasting and scorching with heat; in another branch, skin and bodily extremities.

Meaning in this verse: the scorching effect of an intensely hot liquid on faces.

Suggested translation: “scorches”; more literally, “roasts.”

Rendering of the phrase:

يَشْوِي الْوُجُوهَ

yashwī al-wujūh

Al-Kahf 18:29

“Scorches faces.”

This rendering agrees with the verb’s Arabic meaning.

“Burns faces” also conveys the sense naturally; “roasts faces” retains the specific heat image of شوى more explicitly.

الشَّوَىٰ — Al-Maʿārij 70:16

Brief root meaning: the scalp and bodily extremities; in another branch, roasting with heat.

Meaning in this verse: the scalp, with a possible broader reference to skin and bodily extremities.

Suggested translation: “the scalp.”

Rendering of the phrase:

نَزَّاعَةً لِلشَّوَىٰ

nazzāʿatan li-l-shawā

Al-Maʿārij 70:16

“Stripping away the scalp.”

This rendering agrees with one recognized meaning of الشوى.

A lexical explanation can retain the wider range so that readers do not assume that الشوى always means only the scalp.

“Stripping away” translates نَزَّاعَةً and must not be incorporated into the brief definition of SH W Y itself.

8. Conclusion

SH W Y has two recognized semantic branches in Arabic.

In the first, شَوَى means exposing something to heat and roasting it. The verb also describes scorching with intense heat.

In the second, شَوَى concerns the scalp, extremities, and peripheral body parts.

Related uses concern hitting nonvital parts, missing an intended target, and regarding something as slight beside the central concern.

In the Quran, يَشْوِي in Al-Kahf 18:29 describes scorching or roasting faces.

الشَّوَىٰ in Al-Maʿārij 70:16, however, denotes the scalp or, more broadly, skin and bodily extremities.

These uses show that sharing a root does not make all its forms semantically identical.

A possible association between heat and skin must not be turned into a certain historical account of the branches’ origins.

Final definition of SH W Y:

“A family with two main branches: first, roasting and scorching something with heat; second, the scalp and bodily extremities. Broader uses of the second branch include hitting nonvital parts and regarding something as relatively insignificant. In the Quran, يَشْوِي describes scorching faces, while الشَّوَىٰ denotes the scalp or peripheral parts of the body.”
