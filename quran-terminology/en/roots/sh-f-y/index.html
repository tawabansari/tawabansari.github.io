Recovery, release from illness, and relief of suffering: from bodily healing to healing hearts and breasts in the Qur’an

Early Arabic SH F Y concerns recovering from illness, relieving suffering, and attaining health and ease. Shifāʾ denotes release from illness or harm that disrupts well-being; shafā describes removing that illness or suffering.

This extends beyond bodily illness. Arabic also uses healing for sorrow, inward pain, anger, and suffering caused by hostility. People may recover from illness, be relieved of heavy grief, or regain peace after injustice and harm have been removed.

These uses share a feature: a harmful or painful condition exists, and healing concerns its removal or effective relief.

Shifāʾ sometimes denotes recovery itself and sometimes the remedy or means bringing it about. These related uses are distinct: medicine is a means of treatment, whereas recovery is the hoped-for result.

The Qur’an uses two verbal forms, يَشْفِ and يَشْفِينِ, from شَفَى, and the noun شِفَاء.

One verse records Abraham speaking of healing when ill. Another describes healing the breasts of a believing people. The Qur’an also mentions healing in the bee’s drink and healing inward states through divine guidance and revelation.

Healing is therefore broader than bodily treatment in the Qur’an, but its uses should not all be reduced to one illness or method of treatment.

1. Early Arabic usage

Recovery from illness and suffering

شَفَى اللَّهُ الْمَرِيضَ means that The God healed the sick person and their illness was relieved.

شِفَاء is recorded both for recovery and for medicine used in treatment.

اسْتَشْفَى concerns seeking healing: pursuing treatment and relief from illness or suffering.

For example, someone may experience fever, pain, or weakness and subsequently recover health and strength through treatment and time. Healing concerns recovery from illness; the disappearance of a single symptom does not by itself establish complete recovery.

The word does not specify an immediate restoration of every former ability. Recovery may be gradual, and effects of an injury may remain for a time.

Beginning treatment, the process of recovery, and the resolution of illness must be distinguished. Shifāʾ relates to healing but should not automatically be equated with taking medicine or undergoing a procedure.

Imruʾ al-Qays: relief of grief and blood vengeance

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

أَلَا يَا لَهْفَ هِنْدٍ إِثْرَ قَوْمٍ

a-lā yā lahfa hindin ithra qawmin

هُمُ كَانُوا الشِّفَاءَ فَلَمْ يُصَابُوا

humu kānū al-shifāʾa fa-lam yuṣābū

Translation:

“Alas for Hind’s anguish over those people! They would have been the remedy, but they were not reached.”

The literary accounts accompanying this poem connect it with the killing of the poet’s father and his pursuit of vengeance. The verse alone does not independently establish every detail of that narrative.

Shifāʾ here does not mean medicine or bodily treatment. The poet speaks of grief he believes would be relieved by reaching those he regards as responsible for the harm.

Healing thus concerns relief of inward pain and pressure.

The witness shows that poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period could use shifāʾ beyond bodily recovery.

The word’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the action the poet envisages as a way to obtain relief.

Blood vengeance is imagined here as a route to ease. This does not make “revenge” or “killing an enemy” the lexical meaning of shifāʾ.

Believing revenge will bring relief does not change the word’s definition.

Nor does this image in early poetry establish the moral legitimacy of every act of vengeance.

The attribution is familiar in transmitted versions of the poet’s collection, but this particular verse has not been independently dated here. It supports an early use without conclusively dating the first emotional sense of healing.

Al-Asʿar al-Juʿfī: recovery from love’s pain

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Asʿar al-Juʿfī says:

هَلْ بَانَ قَلْبُكَ مِنْ سُلَيْمَى فَاشْتَفَى

hal bāna qalbuka min sulaymā fa-shtafā

وَلَقَدْ غَنِيتَ بِحُبِّهَا فِيمَا مَضَى

wa-la-qad ghanīta bi-ḥubbihā fīmā maḍā

Translation:

“Has your heart parted from Sulaymā and found relief, although you once spent your days loving her?”

فَاشْتَفَى concerns release from the pain of attachment and the attainment of ease.

The poet asks about the heart and an emotional relationship, not bodily illness or taking medicine.

Love and separation may involve inward pain and disturbance; recovery describes that suffering subsiding.

The witness shows healing applied to emotional experience in early poetry.

اشْتَفَى is Form VIII and here concerns reaching healing or relief.

The opening is transmitted as هَلْ بَانَ and هَلْ دَانَ. This article follows the cited bāna reading; its interpretation of separation should not be transferred unchanged to every variant.

The poem is attributed to a pre-Islamic poet, but an independent date for this particular verse is not established here.

Together with the Imruʾ al-Qays witness, it supports a broader early field of healing that includes inward pain.

Healing and the subsiding of anger

شَفَى غَلِيلَهُ describes quenching inward thirst, anger, or suffering arising from an unwelcome situation.

Ghalīl can concern intense thirst and, emotionally, inward burning or anger.

Someone harmed by injustice may feel relief when it ends and their rights are restored.

The ending of inward pressure may then be described through healing.

But relief of suffering must be distinguished from the means chosen to obtain it.

Suffering may lessen through correcting injustice, restoring rights, removing danger, or clarifying the truth. Another person may pursue revenge in anger.

Healing cannot be equated with revenge or punishment across all these situations.

This matters particularly in Al-Tawbah 9:14, where healing appears amid opposition to hostility and aggression.

Healing: result and means

Arabic records shifāʾ for recovery and for a healing remedy or means.

اسْتَشْفَى بِالدَّوَاءِ concerns seeking healing through medicine or using it for treatment.

أَشْفَيْتُهُ دَوَاءً is recorded for providing or giving a remedy used in treatment.

The distinction matters semantically.

“The patient recovered” describes the result.

“There is healing in this drink” describes something’s healing property or capacity.

A healing property does not itself establish equal benefit for every person, illness, and quantity.

The same distinction must be preserved in reading the Qur’an.

2. Semantic core and Qur’anic forms

SH F Y occurs six times in two morphological groups: forms of the basic verb shafā and the noun shifāʾ.

Al-Tawbah 9:14 has وَيَشْفِ. This present-tense verb is coordinated with يُعَذِّبْهُمُ, which is jussive in response to the command قَاتِلُوهُمْ. The final weak root letter is consequently omitted.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80 has يَشْفِينِ, “He heals me.” The root yāʾ remains, followed by the protective nūn. The first-person object yāʾ is omitted in the Ḥafṣ reading and spelling, with the final kasrah indicating it. The God is the subject in this sentence.

The noun occurs in Yūnus 10:57, Al-Naḥl 16:69, Al-Isrāʾ 17:82, and Fuṣṣilat 41:44.

Forms including شَافٍ, مَشْفِيّ, اسْتَشْفَى, اشْتَفَى, تَشَفَّى, and the plural أَشْفِيَة are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

شَافٍ is an active participle meaning a healer or something healing. مَشْفِيّ describes someone healed.

The independent divine name or epithet الشَّافِي does not occur in the Qur’an. What explicitly occurs is the verb of healing in Abraham’s words and in Al-Tawbah.

The Qur’anic semantic core is:

Removing or relieving illness, harm, or suffering that disrupts bodily or inward well-being, bringing recovery and release from that condition.

This covers bodily and inward healing.

The illness, treatment method, recovery time, and direct or intermediary agent cannot be established from the root alone.

3. Healing illness in Abraham’s words

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80 says:

وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ

wa-idhā mariḍtu fa-huwa yashfīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80

Translation:

“And when I fall ill, He is the one who heals me.”

This forms part of Abraham’s account of the Lord of the worlds.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:78 mentions creation and guidance, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:79 attributes food and drink to The God. Healing follows; Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:81 mentions death and life, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:82 forgiveness.

Healing is thus situated among blessings and aspects of human life.

Illness and healing are distinct

مَرِضْتُ belongs to M R Ḍ and concerns becoming ill.

يَشْفِينِ belongs to SH F Y and concerns relieving illness and restoring health.

The two words contrast within one sentence.

Illness is an impaired condition; healing concerns its removal.

This contrast does not establish a single cause of all illness or a single method of all recovery.

Why does Abraham say مَرِضْتُ?

Previously, Abraham attributes his creation, guidance, food, and drink to The God.

Here he says وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ: “when I fall ill.”

He then says فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ.

This difference deserves attention, but does not establish that Abraham regarded illness as necessarily caused by sin, a wrong decision, or his own independent will.

مَرِضْتُ states that he becomes ill without identifying the cause.

فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ identifies The God as the healer.

The emphasis in fa-huwa directs attention to The God and places healing within His power and governance.

Does divine healing exclude human treatment?

Attributing healing to The God does not itself exclude medicine, medical knowledge, or other means of treatment.

The same passage attributes food and drink to The God, while humans obtain, prepare, and consume food.

Attributing provision to Him does not eliminate the need to eat or obtain food.

Similarly, divine healing need not mean abandoning knowledge of illness or appropriate treatment.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 also describes healing in the bee’s drink: the Qur’an itself connects healing with a natural substance.

There is therefore no necessary contradiction between divine healing and natural means of treatment.

Healing and forgiveness

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:82, Abraham expresses hope that his fault will be forgiven on the Day of Recompense.

This follows illness, healing, death, and life.

Healing and forgiveness nevertheless occupy different semantic fields.

Healing concerns relieving illness or harm.

Maghfirah concerns forgiveness in the context of sin and moral responsibility.

Their proximity does not make every bodily illness punishment for sin or every recovery forgiveness of sin.

They are distinct concepts; any connection in a particular case requires separate evidence.

4. Healing believers’ breasts in Al-Tawbah 9:14

Al-Tawbah 9:14 says:

قَاتِلُوهُمْ يُعَذِّبْهُمُ اللَّهُ بِأَيْدِيكُمْ وَيُخْزِهِمْ وَيَنْصُرْكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ وَيَشْفِ صُدُورَ قَوْمٍ مُؤْمِنِينَ

qātilūhum yuʿadhdhibhumu allāhu bi-aydīkum wa-yukhzihim wa-yanṣurkum ʿalayhim wa-yashfi ṣudūra qawmin muʾminīn

Al-Tawbah 9:14

Translation:

“Fight them; The God will punish them through your hands, disgrace them, help you against them, and heal the breasts of a believing people.”

Healing appears here in the context of fighting a group of opponents.

The surrounding verses must be considered.

Al-Tawbah 9:12 concerns people who break their oaths after covenanting and attack the religion.

Al-Tawbah 9:13 mentions broken oaths, an attempt to expel the Messenger, and their initiation of hostility.

Al-Tawbah 9:14 therefore concerns confrontation with covenant-breaking and aggression.

Healing the breasts

Ṣudūr is the plural of ṣadr and here concerns inward human states.

In Qur’anic Arabic, the breast can represent concern, grief, anger, doubt, belief, and other inward conditions.

يَشْفِ صُدُورَ قَوْمٍ مُؤْمِنِينَ concerns relief of suffering and pressure within a believing people.

It does not describe treatment of a named bodily disease.

Nor does it say their chests were anatomically injured and medically repaired.

It concerns inward relief connected with hostility and earlier harm.

Healing and the removal of anger

Al-Tawbah 9:15 says:

وَيُذْهِبْ غَيْظَ قُلُوبِهِمْ وَيَتُوبُ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-yudhhib ghayẓa qulūbihim wa-yatūbu allāhu ʿalā man yashāʾu wa-allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm

Al-Tawbah 9:15

Translation:

“And remove the anger of their hearts; The God turns toward whomever He wills, and The God is Knowing, Wise.”

This connects directly with the previous verse.

Al-Tawbah 9:14 mentions healing the breasts.

Al-Tawbah 9:15 mentions removing the hearts’ ghayẓ.

Ghayẓ concerns intense anger and inward pressure.

The expressions are related but lexically distinct.

Healing describes relief of suffering or harm; anger subsiding is one condition that may occur in that process.

Healing must not always be restricted to extinguishing anger.

Nor does juxtaposition establish a fixed, universal sequence between healing and the disappearance of anger.

Does healing mean revenge here?

The poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays imagines vengeance as a means of inward relief.

Al-Tawbah 9:14 also places healing amid confrontation with an enemy.

This resemblance supports a recognized Arabic use for suffering caused by hostility.

But the Qur’an does not thereby define healing as revenge.

Al-Tawbah describes confronting covenant-breakers and aggressors. The outcome may remove harm and threats and bring relief to believers who suffered.

The word cannot justify every act of violence, personal vengeance, or harm to an enemy.

Al-Tawbah 9:15 also speaks of divine turning and repentance, so the passage cannot be reduced to a feeling of vengeance.

Healing describes relief from suffering and pressure; the legitimacy and limits of combat must be established from the Qur’anic commands and conditions as a whole.

Why “a believing people”?

Healing concerns صُدُورَ قَوْمٍ مُؤْمِنِينَ, the breasts of a believing people.

This should not automatically be extended to all believers in every age.

The verse describes people benefiting from harm being removed and their enemies defeated in that situation.

Healing alone does not establish that everyone psychologically recovers after every military victory or that all suffering ends completely.

The harm and recovery must be understood in context.

5. Healing what is in the breasts: the Qur’an and inward reform

Yūnus 10:57 says:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ قَدْ جَاءَتْكُمْ مَوْعِظَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ وَشِفَاءٌ لِمَا فِي الصُّدُورِ وَهُدًى وَرَحْمَةٌ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu qad jāʾatkum mawʿiẓatun min rabbikum wa-shifāʾun li-mā fī al-ṣudūri wa-hudan wa-raḥmatun li-l-muʾminīn

Yūnus 10:57

Translation:

“O people, admonition has come to you from your Lord, healing for what is in the breasts, and guidance and mercy for believers.”

The verse begins by addressing people.

It brings together four expressions: admonition, healing, guidance, and mercy.

Each has its own semantic field; they should not be reduced to one meaning.

شِفَاءٌ لِمَا فِي الصُّدُورِ

Healing here concerns recovery and relief of harm.

لِمَا فِي الصُّدُورِ specifies its object: what is in the breasts.

In Qur’anic language, the breast is more than the bodily cavity containing heart and lungs; its uses also concern beliefs, intentions, worries, and inward states.

Al-Baqarah 2:10 describes disease in hearts, while Al-Anʿām 6:125 connects constriction of the breast with misguidance.

Al-Sharḥ 94:1 concerns opening the breast.

These uses employ heart and breast imagery to describe inward conditions.

Healing what is in the breasts in Yūnus 10:57 may therefore concern harms affecting understanding, belief, inclination, or peace.

Misconceptions, unfounded fears, or misleading beliefs can cause distress and confusion; clarification of truth can remedy part of this condition. Sincere questioning should not thereby be treated as a moral disease.

Such examples are not a definitive list of the verse’s intended illnesses.

Is the Qur’an healing only for believers?

The verse begins يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ, addressing people generally.

It then describes admonition and healing for what is in the breasts, and guidance and mercy for believers.

This construction requires careful preservation.

Its general address must not be erased, or taken to mean nobody can benefit from Qur’anic admonition or truth before believing.

Conversely, لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ must not be ignored.

The Qur’an can offer truth to all while people’s benefit depends on their response.

One person may listen, reflect, and correct a mistaken belief; another may reject the same words without examination.

Receiving a message and benefiting from it are distinct.

Healing belief and conduct

Yūnus 10:57 places healing alongside admonition and guidance.

This connects it with inward reform and human orientation.

For example, unquestioning allegiance to a leader may lead someone to violate another’s rights. Clear standards of justice offer an opportunity to reconsider belief and conduct.

Accepting truth and correcting wrongdoing may constitute inward improvement.

But healing does not itself mean completing every stage of repentance, compensation, or social reform.

Someone may recognize truth yet refuse it because of interests or prejudice. Hearing alone then does not establish healing.

Guidance’s healing effect should not become an automatic process independent of understanding and behavior.

6. Healing in the bee’s drink

Al-Naḥl 16:69 says:

ثُمَّ كُلِي مِنْ كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ فَاسْلُكِي سُبُلَ رَبِّكِ ذُلُلًا يَخْرُجُ مِنْ بُطُونِهَا شَرَابٌ مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهُ فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ لِلنَّاسِ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

thumma kulī min kulli al-thamarāti fa-slukī subula rabbiki dhululan yakhruju min buṭūnihā sharābun mukhtalifun alwānuhu fīhi shifāʾun li-l-nāsi inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yatafakkarūn

Al-Naḥl 16:69

Translation:

“Then eat from all the fruits and follow your Lord’s ways made easy. From their bellies comes a drink of varying colors, in which is healing for people. Indeed, in this is a sign for people who reflect.”

The verse continues the account of the bee.

Al-Naḥl 16:68 describes divine inspiration to make homes in mountains, trees, and human structures.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 describes its food, movement along provided paths, and the drink emerging from within it.

The Qur’an then says:

فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ لِلنَّاسِ

fīhi shifāʾun li-l-nās

Al-Naḥl 16:69

“In it is healing for people.”

The reference of فِيهِ

In the immediate construction, the pronoun in فِيهِ refers to شَرَابٌ, the drink; this is the reading followed here.

The drink is thus the subject of the healing property.

This matters for translation.

The sentence does not explicitly name honey; it describes a drink produced by bees with varying colors.

The drink is understood as honey, but a direct rendering can preserve “drink” for sharāb and explain honey contextually.

Sharāb here does not mean an intoxicating beverage. Arabic has a broader sense, so not every drink should be reduced to alcohol.

فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ: a healing property

The verse does not state that this drink cures all diseases.

Nor does it specify that every quantity benefits every person in every situation.

فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ لِلنَّاسِ attributes a healing property to it.

The illness, quantity, method, and conditions of effect are not specified in this phrase.

A therapeutic effect is distinct from curing every illness.

A natural substance may help one condition without helping another, and inappropriate use may cause harm.

A healing property does not remove the need to understand conditions of use.

Divine healing and natural substances

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80, Abraham attributes healing to The God.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 describes healing in the bee’s drink.

The statements need not contradict each other.

One identifies The God as healer; the other describes a therapeutic property in a natural creation.

The ultimate source of a blessing can be distinguished from a natural means of recovery.

Someone may recover through medicine, suitable nutrition, or medical care. These factors do not themselves exclude attributing health and healing to The God.

Nor does that attribution justify abandoning medical knowledge and experience.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 ends by inviting reflection on this phenomenon.

This is compatible with observing and investigating nature’s properties.

Claims of healing cannot excuse disregard for evidence about effect, amount, and safety.

7. The Qur’an as healing and mercy

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 says:

وَنُنَزِّلُ مِنَ الْقُرْآنِ مَا هُوَ شِفَاءٌ وَرَحْمَةٌ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَلَا يَزِيدُ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا خَسَارًا

wa-nunazzilu mina al-qurʾāni mā huwa shifāʾun wa-raḥmatun li-l-muʾminīna wa-lā yazīdu al-ẓālimīna illā khasārā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82

Translation:

“We send down from the Qur’an what is healing and mercy for believers; it increases wrongdoers only in loss.”

The Qur’an is described through healing and mercy.

The same verse states that wrongdoers do not benefit equally and experience increased loss.

This contrast is central to understanding Qur’anic healing.

Healing and mercy are distinct concepts

Healing concerns relieving illness, harm, or suffering.

Mercy has a broader field that can include kindness, beneficence, relief, and enjoyment of good.

They occur together in Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 without being fully synonymous.

Healing may be an expression of mercy, but not all mercy treats an illness.

Yūnus 10:57 also places healing alongside admonition and guidance.

These associations describe different effects on life and understanding. They do not establish fixed stages, with admonition invariably first, healing second, and mercy last.

Word order and juxtaposition alone do not establish such a system.

The meaning of مِنَ الْقُرْآنِ

The preposition min in وَنُنَزِّلُ مِنَ الْقُرْآنِ مَا هُوَ شِفَاءٌ deserves grammatical attention.

Grammatical explanations consider it as identifying a kind or as partitive.

This preposition alone cannot establish that only a few specified verses heal while the rest have no role in reform or guidance.

Conversely, the construction should not be disregarded or assigned a meaning it does not explicitly express without examination.

What is clear is the attribution of healing and mercy to what is revealed of the Qur’an and the connection of benefit with faith.

Why do audiences respond differently?

The second part contrasts wrongdoers with believers.

A message’s effect depends on the audience’s situation and response as well as its content.

For example, the same truthful statement about justice may reach two people.

One corrects their wrongdoing on hearing it.

Another benefits from that wrongdoing, resists truth, and persists in injustice to protect their interests.

The truth is the same; the responses differ.

This example can illuminate healing and loss in Al-Isrāʾ 17:82.

It does not follow that anyone failing to feel immediately calm after reading a verse is necessarily a wrongdoer.

Nor does failure to recover physically after recitation itself establish weak faith or an improper intention.

The verse concerns guidance, faith, injustice, and response to revelation; it should not be turned without evidence into a medical or psychological test.

Healing and guidance in Fuṣṣilat 41:44

Fuṣṣilat 41:44 says:

قُلْ هُوَ لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا هُدًى وَشِفَاءٌ

qul huwa li-lladhīna āmanū hudan wa-shifāʾ

Fuṣṣilat 41:44

Translation:

“Say: ‘For those who believe, it is guidance and healing.’”

The continuation describes unbelievers with heaviness in their ears, for whom it is blindness.

Guidance and healing appear together.

Hudā belongs to H D Y and concerns guidance and showing the right way.

Shifāʾ concerns relieving harm or an unhealthy condition.

Ignorance, mistaken belief, or misguidance may lead people to harm themselves and others. Recognizing truth and correcting conduct can remedy part of that harm.

Guidance and healing are nevertheless distinct.

Guidance can show the right way; healing concerns removing harm.

Fuṣṣilat primarily concerns the Qur’an and its audience’s response.

Healing here should not be restricted without evidence to directly curing every physical disease through hearing or reading words.

Nor should understanding and accepting the Qur’an be reduced to a private soothing experience. Its message also concerns belief, behavior, justice, relationships, and social responsibility.

8. Distinctions from related roots

SH F Y and M R Ḍ: healing and illness

Maraḍ concerns illness and impaired health.

Healing concerns relief of illness or harm and recovery.

They occur together in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80.

Illness may be bodily, while other Qur’anic uses describe the heart or beliefs.

Healing likewise may concern bodily recovery or inward reform.

This does not make every dissatisfaction, disagreement, or question a disease.

Bodily illness should not be attributed without evidence to moral corruption.

SH F Y and B R ʾ: healing and recovery

In relation to illness, burʾ concerns recovery and freedom from it.

Āl ʿImrān 3:49 has أُبْرِئُ, “I heal,” while Al-Māʾidah 5:110 has تُبْرِئُ, “you heal.” Both describe healing people with specified bodily conditions.

Shifāʾ can also describe the removal of illness.

The roots overlap in recovery, but B R ʾ also concerns creation and disavowal or freedom from something.

Not every derivative of B R ʾ means healing, and not every healing usage means disavowal.

In therapeutic uses both may concern recovery, with precise differences determined by construction and context.

Healing, medicine, and treatment

Dawāʾ names a remedy or means of treatment; mudāwāh concerns treating someone and applying such means.

Shifāʾ more prominently concerns illness being relieved and recovery occurring, though it can also name a remedy.

A patient may receive medicine without yet recovering.

Treatment has occurred, but healing has not yet been achieved.

Conversely, recovery may involve rest, time, or bodily changes without a particular medicine.

Treatment and healing are closely related but not always identical in meaning.

SH F Y and K SH F: healing and removal

Kashf concerns uncovering and disclosure and, in some contexts, removing harm or distress.

Al-Anʿām 6:17 says:

وَإِنْ يَمْسَسْكَ اللَّهُ بِضُرٍّ فَلَا كَاشِفَ لَهُ إِلَّا هُوَ

wa-in yamsaska allāhu bi-ḍurrin fa-lā kāshifa lahu illā huwa

Al-Anʿām 6:17

Translation:

“If The God touches you with harm, none can remove it but Him.”

Kashf here concerns removing harm.

Healing likewise involves relief of illness or harm.

But not every kashf is healing: its object may be a covering, hidden matter, or a difficulty other than illness.

Health and recovery are important in Qur’anic healing, while kashf has a broader field.

SH F Y and S K N: healing and tranquility

Sukūn concerns becoming still or disturbance subsiding.

Healing concerns recovery from illness or harm.

Healing may bring calm, as when someone becomes calmer after severe pain ends.

But not every calm state is healing.

Someone may feel temporarily calm while illness remains.

Conversely, bodily recovery may begin while emotional concern persists.

Healing therefore should not always be translated as calm or sakīnah.

SH F Y and R Ḥ M: healing and mercy

Mercy concerns beneficence, kindness, and relief.

Healing can express mercy, but not all mercy is healing.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 places شِفَاءٌ وَرَحْمَةٌ together.

The Qur’an can thus remedy inward harm and be a source of mercy and good for believers.

This does not mean every mercy follows illness or that healing is merely a feeling of kindness.

SH F Y and SH F W: healing and an edge

An important distinction is between شِفَاء, healing, and شَفَا, an edge or brink.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103 says:

وَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ

wa-kuntum ʿalā shafā ḥufratin mina al-nār

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

Translation:

“You were on the brink of a pit of fire.”

Al-Tawbah 9:109 has شَفَا جُرُفٍ هَارٍ for the edge of an unstable, collapsing bank.

Shafā in these verses denotes an edge, not recovery from illness.

Qur’anic morphological indexes treat this form under SH F W.

Some older dictionaries group healing, an edge, and approaching an end under a broader entry. Reaching the boundary of an illness’s end has also been proposed as a semantic link.

That explanation does not establish a definite derivational history.

Forqan should therefore keep the clear edge sense of shafā distinct from healing shifāʾ.

Study of SH F W

Likewise shafah, lip, and shafāʿah, intercession, require their own roots and forms despite similarities in writing.

9. Healing and responsibility toward suffering

Bodily healing and access to treatment

Distinguishing healing from its means shows why faith or trust in The God should not be invoked to dismiss all ways of understanding and treating illness.

The Qur’an speaks of divine healing while also attributing healing properties to a natural substance.

Knowledge, experience, and therapeutic means need not conflict with divine healing.

Someone ill may need assessment of the cause, appropriate treatment, and care.

Prayer and hope may matter in their life without justifying ignoring danger or abandoning necessary treatment.

Failure to recover despite treatment and prayer does not, without evidence, establish weak faith or that the person caused their own illness.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80 attributes healing to The God but does not supply a universal rule for every illness’s cause, recovery time, or treatment outcome.

Inward healing and repairing harm

Healing hearts and breasts may involve relief of suffering, correction of mistaken beliefs, and remedying inward harm.

Someone may have suffered injustice for years. Ending it, restoring rights, establishing truth, and securing safety can help lessen suffering.

An injured person should not be expected to lose every effect of past suffering merely on hearing a few sentences.

Healing must not be invoked to deprive them of redress or repair.

Al-Tawbah 9:14 connects healing with relief from hostility; Yūnus 10:57 places it alongside admonition and guidance.

Relief may thus involve changes in outward conditions or inward understanding.

This does not make all human suffering the result of the sufferer’s own mistaken beliefs.

People can be harmed by others’ injustice and corruption. That harm should not be attributed to the injured person’s sin or moral defect.

Qur’anic healing and the audience’s response

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 and Fuṣṣilat 41:44 describe healing for believers and differing responses to the message of truth.

Truth may provide one person an opportunity to reform, while another’s persistence in error brings greater loss to light.

A person profiting from financial corruption may hear a command of justice and restore people’s rights.

If they knowingly persist instead, that same truth may make their responsibility clearer.

Healing here is more than personal calm; it may involve changed belief and corrected behavior.

But shifāʾ itself does not mean repentance, compensation, or executing justice. Those are distinct actions and outcomes that may contribute to reform.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

The primary Persian equivalent of shifāʾ is shafā, a familiar, natural word requiring no artificial replacement in most Qur’anic uses. “Healing” is the primary English equivalent.

“Recovery” is also suitable, especially for the result of treatment or resolution of illness.

“Treatment” fits some contexts but can name a process or means, so it should not replace every use of healing.

Shafā may be rendered “heal,” “bring recovery,” or, where appropriate, “relieve suffering.”

A natural rendering of Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80 is:

“And when I fall ill, He is the one who heals me.”

In Al-Tawbah 9:14:

“And heal the breasts of a believing people.”

Explanation may clarify relief of inward suffering connected with hostility, but should not substitute for the word healing in a direct translation.

In Yūnus 10:57:

“And healing for what is in the breasts.”

In Al-Naḥl 16:69:

“In it is healing for people.”

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:82:

“We send down from the Qur’an what is healing and mercy for believers.”

In Fuṣṣilat 41:44:

“Say: ‘For those who believe, it is guidance and healing.’”

These preserve healing while leaving its object and kind to each verse’s context. These proposals do not alter the installed verse translations.

For Arabic shāfin, “healing” or “healer” is appropriate; istashfā may mean “sought healing” or “pursued treatment.”

Some emotional uses of tashaffā concern relief of pain or anger, including contexts of revenge. It should not be restricted to revenge without contextual evidence.

Finally, healing shifāʾ must remain distinct from edge shafā. Similar writing does not make their meanings identical.

11. Conclusion

Early Arabic SH F Y concerns recovering from illness, relieving suffering, and attaining health and ease.

Physically, it concerns relief of illness or injury impairing health. Emotionally, it may describe soothing grief, inward pressure, or suffering caused by hostility.

Poetic witnesses attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and al-Asʿar al-Juʿfī support an early inward use. They do not make revenge, love, or calm the root’s exclusive meaning.

The Qur’an uses two verbal forms and the noun shifāʾ.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80, Abraham attributes healing when ill to The God.

Al-Tawbah 9:14 describes healing a believing people’s breasts amid relief from hostility; Al-Tawbah 9:15 also mentions removing their hearts’ anger.

Yūnus 10:57 concerns healing what is in the breasts, alongside admonition, guidance, and mercy.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 describes healing in the bee’s drink. It attributes a healing property without establishing a cure for every illness in every situation and quantity.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 and Fuṣṣilat 41:44 identify the Qur’an as healing within guidance, faith, mercy, and inward reform. This should not become an unsupported claim of automatic cures for every bodily or psychological illness.

Divine healing need not conflict with natural means: Abraham identifies The God as healer, and the Qur’an also describes healing in a natural substance.

Healing is thus a broad concept of recovery and relief, while its object and manner differ by verse.

The resulting definition is:

SH F Y denotes relieving or removing illness, harm, and suffering that impair bodily or inward well-being. Shifāʾ names this recovery and can also designate a remedy or healing means. The illness, agent, method, and outcome must be established from context rather than the root alone.

Forqan’s primary Persian equivalents are shafā and shafā dādan, “healing” and “to heal.” “Recovery” and “relief of suffering” can clarify different contexts without restricting the root’s broader meaning to one use.
