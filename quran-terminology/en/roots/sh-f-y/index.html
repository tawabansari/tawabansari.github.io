Healing, recovery and relief from suffering: from bodily illness to inward wounds and the Quran's role in guidance

In early Arabic, SH F Y concerns healing, recovery and the removal of illness or suffering. Shifāʾ denotes recovery and release from illness; transitive shafā means to heal or bring about recovery.

Physically, healing concerns an illness, pain or injury impairing health. In inward and emotional usage, the same family describes grief subsiding, inward suffering easing and distress being relieved.

Poetry traditionally attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah uses healing for inward suffering without referring to bodily illness. This supports an established nonphysical usage, although the individual line has not been independently dated here; the Quranic application to hearts and breasts need not be treated as a newly invented meaning.

Healing does not always mean happiness, tranquility or personal satisfaction. Someone may enjoy an event without a real injury being healed. Its central sense concerns removing illness or suffering and attaining a better condition.

SH F Y occurs six times in the Quran: Al-Tawbah 9:14, Yūnus 10:57, Al-Naḥl 16:69, Al-Isrāʾ 17:82, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80 and Fuṣṣilat 41:44.

These uses concern two main settings: bodily illness and inward injury or distress. One verse identifies God as the one who heals the sick; another describes a drink from bees containing healing for people. Other verses describe the Quran as healing for what is within breasts and as guidance and mercy for believers.

The semantic core is the removal of illness or suffering and the recovery of health or improvement. The relevant illness, healing agent and outcome must be identified from the object and context of each use.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Healing: release from illness and pain

Early dictionaries explain shifāʾ through recovery from illness: release from a condition impairing health.

Shafāhu Allāhu min maraḍihi means that God healed him of his illness.

Healing here directly concerns illness. A sick person may experience pain, weakness or impaired bodily function, followed by relief from that condition.

For example, someone who actually recovers from an illness may be described as healed. The disappearance of fever or some symptoms alone does not necessarily establish complete recovery.

Treatment and recovery should nevertheless be distinguished.

Treatment is an intervention intended to improve illness, whereas healing concerns recovery or the outcome of treatment.

A doctor may prescribe medicine while the illness persists. Treatment has then been undertaken, but recovery cannot be asserted without evidence of its outcome.

Conversely, the body may recover naturally from some illnesses without receiving a particular drug treatment.

Healing is therefore not merely a name for medicine or treatment; it also concerns the outcome of recovery.

Dawāʾ denotes medicine or something used for treatment, whereas shifāʾ may denote either recovery or its means.

This distinction matters in Al-Naḥl 16:69: the verse does not merely label the bees' drink a medicine, but states that it contains healing for people.

A poetic witness: inward healing in ʿAntarah's poetry

In the celebrated Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād:

وَلَقَدْ شَفَى نَفْسِي وَأَبْرَأَ سُقْمَهَا
قَوْلُ الْفَوَارِسِ وَيْكَ عَنْتَرُ أَقْدِمِ

wa-la-qad shafā nafsī wa-abraʾa suqmahā / qawlu al-fawārisi wayka ʿantaru aqdim

“The horsemen's cry, ‘Come on, ʿAntar, advance!’ healed my soul and relieved its sickness.”

The poet describes a battle in which horsemen call upon him to advance.

Their call expresses reliance on his courage and ability. Relating it to specific experiences of social humiliation requires evidence beyond the line itself.

He says that these words healed his soul and relieved his inward suffering.

Some versions read adhhaba saqamahā, “removed its sickness,” instead of abraʾa saqamahā. Both qawl al-fawāris and qīl al-fawāris are transmitted in the second hemistich.

These variants do not change the central meaning of shafā nafsī, “healed my soul.”

Here healing is not the treatment of bodily illness. The poet does not say that a physical wound closed or an organ recovered.

The subject is inward distress and human feeling.

Hearing the horsemen's call relieves his distress; specifying its biographical causes goes beyond the line's direct meaning.

This line supports the established application of healing to inward suffering in early Arabic. Its traditional attribution is relevant, but does not independently establish the exact date of this individual line.

The Quranic expression “healing for what is within breasts” thus fits the known semantic range of healing in Arabic.

This does not make every military honor or victory a healing. In ʿAntarah's poem, a particular event relieves inward suffering.

Inward healing concerns the injury and what removes it, rather than happiness or a sense of victory alone.

Relief of anger and distress

Tashaffā min ghayẓihi describes someone's anger or distress subsiding, or a sense of release from what provoked it.

Ghayẓ concerns intense anger and distress.

For someone suffering injustice or humiliation, ending that situation and restoring rights may ease anger.

In this respect, inward healing may be linked to removing its cause.

Healing and revenge must nevertheless be distinguished.

Someone may expect relief from harming an enemy, but that feeling alone proves neither the justice of the action nor actual recovery from distress.

Tashaffī can even describe satisfaction at an enemy's injury; the root itself does not determine the action's moral value.

This matters in Al-Tawbah 9:14, where healing believers' breasts occurs in the context of confronting an aggressive, treaty-breaking group.

That usage does not make every personal revenge a commendable healing.

Other Arabic uses

Istashfā means to seek healing or treatment.

Istashfā bi-l-dawāʾ means to use medicine in seeking recovery.

Istashfā can also describe attaining recovery; its precise sense depends on sentence structure.

Tashaffā can concern attaining relief after anger, suffering or distress.

Ashfā is also recorded for providing medicine or a means of healing, as when someone supplies another with a suitable remedy.

Ashfā ʿalā al-mawt, however, means to approach death. It belongs to the edge or threshold branch and should not simply be equated with recovery.

Older dictionaries sometimes discuss these usages together because of the proximity of SH F W and SH F Y forms.

Al-ishfā also names a pointed tool used to pierce leather and sew certain leather articles.

Some dictionaries classify it under SH F Y, although its relationship to healing is unclear.

Shared letters alone cannot establish one historical origin for the piercing tool, recovery from illness and the edge of a pit.

In the main Quranic branch of SH F Y, the established meaning concerns healing and the removal of illness or suffering.

2. Important forms and derivatives

SH F Y is a final-weak root: its final yāʾ changes or disappears in certain inflections.

Its principal forms include:

Shafā / yashfī: to heal or bring about recovery. Two imperfect forms occur in the Quran.

Shifāʾ: recovery and release from illness or suffering; also its means. The noun occurs four times in the Quran.

Ashfiyah: a lexical plural of shifāʾ, referring to remedies or healing agents.

Ashāfin: a further plural recorded for ashfiyah.

Shāfin: a healer or something bringing about recovery.

Mashfiyy: someone healed or recovered.

Shufiya: the passive of shafā, meaning was healed or recovered. It must be distinguished from active shafā and the two transitive Quranic verbs of this root.

Istashfā: to seek healing or treatment; in another use, to attain recovery.

Istishfāʾ: seeking or requesting healing.

Tashaffā: to feel relief from suffering, anger or distress.

Tashaffin: distress subsiding or satisfaction after its cause is removed. Some contexts concern revenge and satisfaction at an enemy's injury.

Ashfā: in the treatment branch, to supply medicine or a means of recovery. Ashfā ʿalā al-halāk instead concerns approaching destruction and should be examined separately.

Modern Arabic uses mashfā for a hospital or treatment facility. Without historical evidence, this particular noun should not be assigned that established sense in pre-Quranic Arabic.

Only the basic triliteral verb and the noun shifāʾ occur in the Quran.

Istashfā, tashaffā, shaffāʾ and mashfā do not occur in those forms in the Quran.

Nor does a derivative occur as a standalone divine name in the Quran. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80 identifies God as the one who heals, but does not contain the name al-Shāfī.

Attributing healing to God must be distinguished from the presence of a particular divine-name form in the Quranic text.

3. Bodily healing: illness and the bees' drink

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80: Abraham speaks of healing

The Quran reports Abraham saying:

وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ

wa-idhā mariḍtu fa-huwa yashfīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80

“And when I become ill, it is He who heals me.”

This occurs among Abraham's statements about his Lord.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:78 he says:

الَّذِي خَلَقَنِي فَهُوَ يَهْدِينِ

alladhī khalaqanī fa-huwa yahdīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:78

“The one who created me, and who guides me.”

And in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:79:

وَالَّذِي هُوَ يُطْعِمُنِي وَيَسْقِينِ

wa-alladhī huwa yuṭʿimunī wa-yasqīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:79

“And the one who feeds me and gives me drink.”

After healing, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:81 mentions death and renewed life.

These verses place human life in relation to creation, guidance, food, drink, illness, healing, death and renewed life.

In wa-idhā mariḍtu, becoming ill is attributed to Abraham himself.

In fa-huwa yashfīn, healing is attributed to God.

This grammatical distinction matters.

The verse does not say “when He makes me ill,” but “when I become ill.”

The healer is then identified with the emphatic pronoun huwa, “He.”

This structure does not exclude God from creation or bodily events; neither does it directly attribute an act of causing illness to God in this sentence.

What it explicitly attributes to Him is healing.

Does divine healing conflict with human treatment?

People may use medicine, suitable food, care or medical assistance to treat illness.

These means do not inherently conflict with Abraham's attribution of healing to God.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 itself describes a drink from bees containing healing for people.

One verse attributes healing to God; another describes a healing benefit in a created substance.

These two statements can both hold.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:79 attributes feeding and giving drink to God, while people use food, water, agriculture and other natural means. Likewise, healing may occur through created means and resources.

Attributing healing to God therefore does not negate medicine, medical knowledge or human efforts at treatment.

Conversely, the existence of medicine and treatment does not by itself negate God's role in life and recovery.

Lexically, yashfīn concerns healing and recovery. How this occurs in each illness is a separate question.

Al-Naḥl 16:69: healing in the bees' drink

The Quran says:

ثُمَّ كُلِي مِنْ كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ فَاسْلُكِي سُبُلَ رَبِّكِ ذُلُلًا ۚ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ بُطُونِهَا شَرَابٌ مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهُ فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ لِلنَّاسِ ۗ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

thumma kulī min kulli al-thamarāti fa-slukī subula rabbiki dhululan yakhruju min buṭūnihā sharābun mukhtalifun alwānuhu fīhi shifāʾun li-l-nāsi inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yatafakkarūn

Al-Naḥl 16:69

“Then eat from all the fruits and follow your Lord's paths, made easy. From their bellies comes a drink of varying colors, in which there is healing for people. Surely in this is a sign for people who reflect.”

Al-Naḥl 16:68 precedes this verse and describes inspiration to bees concerning the construction of their dwellings.

The pronoun in buṭūnihā thus refers to the bees.

In context, the drink concerns a substance produced by bees.

Honey is its familiar referent, although this passage uses sharāb, “drink,” rather than the noun “honey.”

Sharāb denotes something drinkable; mukhtalifun alwānuhu describes its varying colors.

The phrase relevant to SH F Y is:

فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ لِلنَّاسِ

fīhi shifāʾun li-l-nās

Al-Naḥl 16:69

“In it there is healing for people.”

What does shifāʾ mean here?

It concerns a natural substance's healing benefit.

Unlike the passages about the Quran and breasts, this verse concerns the bees' drink.

Without contextual evidence, “healing for people” should not be recast as curing misguidance, correcting belief or eliminating disbelief.

The context concerns bees, their paths, food and the drink they produce.

The natural sense here is bodily treatment and recovery.

The word itself does not establish that this drink cures every illness or has the same effect for everyone, at every quantity and under all conditions.

The Quran says:

فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ لِلنَّاسِ

fīhi shifāʾun li-l-nās

Al-Naḥl 16:69

“In it there is healing for people.”

It does not say that it contains a cure for every disease.

Nor does the phrase specify dosage, method, illness or treatment conditions.

Its statement of a healing benefit should not be expanded into claims the verse does not make.

This preserves the statement's actual scope rather than diminishing it.

“In it is healing” and “it is healing”

The actual construction locates healing in the drink; it does not simply identify the drink as healing.

It says:

فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ

fīhi shifāʾ

Al-Naḥl 16:69

“In it is healing.”

Fī here expresses a healing property or benefit present in the drink.

The two constructions differ grammatically, but neither “in it is healing” nor “it is healing,” by itself, asserts a cure for every disease, person and circumstance.

Nor does fī imply that only a tiny portion of the drink is beneficial. That conclusion also exceeds the construction.

What is stated is the presence of healing in this drink for people.

The verse ends:

إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yatafakkarūn

Al-Naḥl 16:69

“Surely in this is a sign for people who reflect.”

The Quran draws attention to a natural phenomenon as a subject of reflection.

Honey's precise composition, bodily effects and therapeutic conditions may be studied empirically. None of these details can be derived from SH F Y itself.

4. Healing breasts: inward suffering and Quranic guidance

Yūnus 10:57: healing for what is within breasts

The Quran says:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ قَدْ جَاءَتْكُمْ مَوْعِظَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ وَشِفَاءٌ لِمَا فِي الصُّدُورِ وَهُدًى وَرَحْمَةٌ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu qad jāʾatkum mawʿiẓatun min rabbikum wa-shifāʾun li-mā fī al-ṣudūri wa-hudan wa-raḥmatun li-l-muʾminīn

Yūnus 10:57

“O people, an admonition from your Lord has come to you, and healing for what is within breasts, and guidance and mercy for believers.”

It begins with a general address:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ

yā ayyuhā al-nās

Yūnus 10:57

“O people.”

The initial address is to humanity, not only one nation or religious community.

What comes from the Lord receives four descriptions: admonition, healing, guidance and mercy.

These concepts are related but should not be treated as complete synonyms without examination.

Admonition is counsel and reminder drawing attention to truth and the consequences of conduct.

Healing concerns the removal of illness or injury.

Guidance directs toward the right path.

Mercy concerns good and benefit reaching people from God.

Healing has a specified object here:

لِمَا فِي الصُّدُورِ

li-mā fī al-ṣudūr

Yūnus 10:57

“For what is within breasts.”

This qualification is decisive for understanding its meaning.

What is within breasts?

Ṣadr can denote the bodily chest; in passages about inner life it concerns thoughts, intentions, inclinations, fears and other inward states.

For example, Āl ʿImrān 3:29 says:

إِنْ تُخْفُوا مَا فِي صُدُورِكُمْ أَوْ تُبْدُوهُ يَعْلَمْهُ اللَّهُ

in tukhfū mā fī ṣudūrikum aw tubdūhu yaʿlamhu allāh

Āl ʿImrān 3:29

“Whether you conceal what is within your breasts or disclose it, God knows it.”

The subject is something people can conceal within themselves.

Al-Baqarah 2:10 also says:

فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ فَزَادَهُمُ اللَّهُ مَرَضًا

fī qulūbihim maraḍun fa-zādahumu allāhu maraḍā

Al-Baqarah 2:10

“In their hearts is an illness, so God increased their illness.”

The Quran thus uses illness imagery for an inward condition judged wrong.

Healing in Yūnus 10:57 may concern misguidance, false beliefs, concealment of truth, prejudice and other inward injuries. Honest uncertainty and sincere questioning should not thereby be classified as moral illness.

These examples are not all fixed components of the root's meaning.

Healing denotes recovery; the relevant illness is identified through context and other evidence.

Is every sorrow an illness?

An important distinction applies to nonphysical healing.

People may grieve after bereavement, injustice or life's difficulties.

The word healing does not make every sorrow, fear or emotional response a moral illness or sign of misguidance.

Prophets too experience sorrow, fear and suffering in the Quran.

Jacob's intense sorrow is described in Yūsuf 12:84; in Yūsuf 12:86 he says:

إِنَّمَا أَشْكُو بَثِّي وَحُزْنِي إِلَى اللَّهِ

innamā ashkū baththī wa-ḥuznī ilā allāh

Yūsuf 12:86

“I complain of my anguish and sorrow only to God.”

This sorrow does not itself imply disbelief or inward corruption.

Healing what is within breasts does not justify labeling every natural unpleasant emotion a religious illness.

In Yūnus 10:57, healing accompanies admonition, guidance and mercy. The main concern is removing obstacles and injuries related to recognizing truth, guidance and inward orientation.

5. The Quran as healing: the message and human responses

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82: healing and mercy for believers

The Quran says:

وَنُنَزِّلُ مِنَ الْقُرْآنِ مَا هُوَ شِفَاءٌ وَرَحْمَةٌ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَلَا يَزِيدُ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا خَسَارًا

wa-nunazzilu mina al-qurʾāni mā huwa shifāʾun wa-raḥmatun li-l-muʾminīna wa-lā yazīdu al-ẓālimīna illā khasārā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82

“We send down of the Quran what is healing and mercy for believers; it increases the wrongdoers only in loss.”

Healing is attributed to the Quran here.

The preceding context matters too.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 says:

وَقُلْ جَاءَ الْحَقُّ وَزَهَقَ الْبَاطِلُ إِنَّ الْبَاطِلَ كَانَ زَهُوقًا

wa-qul jāʾa al-ḥaqqu wa-zahaqa al-bāṭilu inna al-bāṭila kāna zahūqā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81

“Say: Truth has come and falsehood has passed away; surely falsehood is bound to pass away.”

Healing in Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 thus follows the arrival of truth and the passing of falsehood.

Recognizing truth may enable someone to correct false belief, prejudice or a misleading interpretation.

For example, unexamined adherence to a tradition may lead someone to declare an act divinely obligatory or forbidden without valid evidence.

Recognizing that false attribution through clear Quranic verses and correcting one's behavior removes an inward obstacle to guidance.

This can illustrate Quranic healing.

Healing does not mean that all intellectual errors are automatically corrected. Recognition, acceptance and human responses to truth matter too.

Does “of the Quran” restrict healing to only part of it?

The verse begins:

وَنُنَزِّلُ مِنَ الْقُرْآنِ

wa-nunazzilu mina al-qurʾān

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82

“And We send down of the Quran.”

The preposition min can receive different grammatical analyses here.

One reading treats it as explanatory of kind: what is sent down belongs to the Quran and possesses healing and mercy.

Another reads it partitively: from the Quran is sent down what is healing and mercy.

This preposition alone does not establish that only particular verses heal while others play no role in guidance or healing.

Nor should the sentence's structure be ignored to claim that each verse, independently of subject and context, works identically for every human difficulty.

Fuṣṣilat 41:44 describes the Quran generally as guidance and healing for believers.

It therefore helps clarify the scope of Al-Isrāʾ 17:82.

The Quran's healing character is affirmed; benefiting from it should be considered alongside guidance, faith and human response.

Why healing for one group and increased loss for another?

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 continues:

وَلَا يَزِيدُ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا خَسَارًا

wa-lā yazīdu al-ẓālimīna illā khasārā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82

“It increases the wrongdoers only in loss.”

Encountering the same message does not necessarily produce the same result in everyone.

One person may hear a truth, reflect and reform their conduct.

Another may hear the same truth and resist through prejudice, self-interest or persistence in injustice.

The clear message may enable guidance and release for the first, while the second person's response deepens their loss.

Someone accepting the Quran's prohibition of violating others' rights can correct their conduct.

Someone knowingly persisting in injustice despite recognizing those rights incurs further responsibility and loss through their continued behavior.

The verse should not be taken to give the Quran two contradictory essences, one healing and the other inherently harmful.

The different outcomes also concern the recipients' conditions and responses.

Disagreement with our interpretation alone does not establish that someone belongs to the wrongdoers described. Such a judgment requires examination of the truth at issue, its evidence and the person's conduct.

Fuṣṣilat 41:44: guidance and healing, heaviness and blindness

The Quran says:

وَلَوْ جَعَلْنَاهُ قُرْآنًا أَعْجَمِيًّا لَقَالُوا لَوْلَا فُصِّلَتْ آيَاتُهُ أَأَعْجَمِيٌّ وَعَرَبِيٌّ قُلْ هُوَ لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا هُدًى وَشِفَاءٌ وَالَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ فِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرٌ وَهُوَ عَلَيْهِمْ عَمًى أُولَٰئِكَ يُنَادَوْنَ مِنْ مَكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ

wa-law jaʿalnāhu qurʾānan aʿjamiyyan la-qālū lawlā fuṣṣilat āyātuhu a-aʿjamiyyun wa-ʿarabiyyun qul huwa li-lladhīna āmanū hudan wa-shifāʾun wa-alladhīna lā yuʾminūna fī ādhānihim waqrun wa-huwa ʿalayhim ʿaman ulāʾika yunādawna min makānin baʿīd

Fuṣṣilat 41:44

“Had We made it a non-Arabic Quran, they would have said: Why have its verses not been explained in detail? Non-Arabic and Arabic? Say: For those who believe, it is guidance and healing. Those who do not believe have heaviness in their ears, and it is blindness for them. They are called from a distant place.”

For believers the Quran receives two descriptions:

هُدًى وَشِفَاءٌ

hudan wa-shifāʾ

Fuṣṣilat 41:44

“Guidance and healing.”

These words occur together but belong to different roots.

Guidance directs toward the right path.

Healing concerns removing illness or injury.

In this setting, the Quran can show the right path and remove inward obstacles and false beliefs that prevent acceptance of truth.

These two effects should not be equated lexically.

Someone may recognize the right path while still needing effort and reform to abandon an old habit or prejudice.

Someone else may abandon a false belief without yet adequately understanding every aspect of the right path.

Guidance and healing can therefore complement one another without sharing a single meaning.

Heaviness and blindness: contrasting images

The verse says of those who do not believe:

فِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرٌ وَهُوَ عَلَيْهِمْ عَمًى

fī ādhānihim waqrun wa-huwa ʿalayhim ʿamā

Fuṣṣilat 41:44

“In their ears is heaviness, and it is blindness for them.”

These expressions concern hearing and accepting the Quran.

Waqr denotes heaviness and an obstacle to hearing.

ʿAmā denotes blindness.

The passage need not concern bodily diseases of the ear or eye.

Someone may physically hear words or see writing yet resist understanding and accepting their meaning.

Such images repeatedly describe cognitive and spiritual states in the Quran.

Healing here concerns removing obstacles to guidance and correcting inward conditions; the verse does not promise to cure all eye and ear diseases.

It also addresses the language of the message and its recipients' responses.

Some would have objected even if the message had been delivered in a non-Arabic language.

Their difficulty was thus not merely the language's outward form, but their response to its truth.

This context emphasizes understanding, attention and response to the message. It does not by itself establish automatic universal healing through repeating words, nor settle every question about recitation's effects.

6. Al-Tawbah 9:14: healing believers' breasts amid aggression

The Quran says:

قَاتِلُوهُمْ يُعَذِّبْهُمُ اللَّهُ بِأَيْدِيكُمْ وَيُخْزِهِمْ وَيَنْصُرْكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ وَيَشْفِ صُدُورَ قَوْمٍ مُؤْمِنِينَ

qātilūhum yuʿadhdhibhumu allāhu bi-aydīkum wa-yukhzihim wa-yanṣurkum ʿalayhim wa-yashfi ṣudūra qawmin muʾminīn

Al-Tawbah 9:14

“Fight them: God will punish them through your hands, disgrace them, help you against them and heal the breasts of a believing people.”

This is the only Quranic healing verb directly connected with warfare.

Its preceding verses are essential to interpretation.

Al-Tawbah 9:12 describes people breaking their oaths and attacking the religion.

Al-Tawbah 9:13 says:

أَلَا تُقَاتِلُونَ قَوْمًا نَكَثُوا أَيْمَانَهُمْ وَهَمُّوا بِإِخْرَاجِ الرَّسُولِ وَهُمْ بَدَءُوكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ

a-lā tuqātilūna qawman nakathū aymānahum wa-hammū bi-ikhrāji al-rasūli wa-hum badaʾūkum awwala marrah

Al-Tawbah 9:13

“Will you not fight people who broke their oaths, intended to expel the Messenger and began against you the first time?”

This identifies the group under discussion.

Al-Tawbah 9:14 is therefore not a general instruction to fight every doctrinal opponent; its context identifies treaty-breaking and hostile action.

What does healing breasts mean?

In the phrase:

وَيَشْفِ صُدُورَ قَوْمٍ مُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-yashfi ṣudūra qawmin muʾminīn

Al-Tawbah 9:14

“And heal the breasts of a believing people.”

Healing is applied to breasts.

It does not mean curing a bodily chest illness here.

The context concerns injury, fear, injustice and conflict.

Some believers have suffered from aggressors; victory and removal of the threat can relieve their distress.

Al-Tawbah 9:15 clarifies this:

وَيُذْهِبْ غَيْظَ قُلُوبِهِمْ وَيَتُوبُ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-yudhhib ghayẓa qulūbihim wa-yatūbu allāhu ʿalā man yashāʾu wa-allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm

Al-Tawbah 9:15

“And remove the anger of their hearts; God turns toward whomever He wills, and God is knowing, wise.”

Ghayẓ, intense anger and distress, is explicitly mentioned.

Healing breasts in Al-Tawbah 9:14 thus concerns relief from the suffering and anger caused by injustice and aggression.

Yashfi itself does not mean to kill, defeat or take revenge.

Those actions, where present, come from other verbs and the military context.

Healing expresses the outcome of recovery and release from distress.

Is revenge always healing?

The verse does not entitle everyone angry with another person to heal that anger by harming them.

It concerns a specified group and circumstances of aggression and treaty-breaking.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 also praises those who restrain anger and pardon people:

وَالْكَاظِمِينَ الْغَيْظَ وَالْعَافِينَ عَنِ النَّاسِ

wa-al-kāẓimīna al-ghayẓa wa-al-ʿāfīna ʿani al-nās

Āl ʿImrān 3:134

“Those who restrain anger and pardon people.”

Relieving anger therefore does not always require revenge.

Reform, ending injustice, restoring rights or deliberate forgiveness may help bring suffering to an end.

In Al-Tawbah 9:14, healing breasts is an outcome within resisting aggression, not a universal definition for every human relationship.

Al-Tawbah 9:15 also speaks of God turning toward whomever He wills.

Even in this confrontation, the Quran's discourse is not confined to endless hostility or denial of every possibility of return.

Healing here concerns relief of an injured group's inward suffering. The legitimacy of ways of confronting aggression must be established through the Quran's broader rules and limits of warfare, not from SH F Y itself.

7. Distinguishing nearby roots

SH F Y and B R ʾ: recovery

Burʾ, in relation to illness, denotes release and recovery from it.

Shifāʾ likewise belongs to the field of recovery.

Both families occur in ʿAntarah's line:

وَلَقَدْ شَفَى نَفْسِي وَأَبْرَأَ سُقْمَهَا

wa-la-qad shafā nafsī wa-abraʾa suqmahā

“It healed my soul and relieved its sickness.”

Their pairing shows semantic proximity.

Their order does not establish healing as invariably the first stage and burʾ as invariably the final stage of treatment.

The poet uses two close expressions to emphasize relief from inward suffering.

Shifāʾ and burʾ can explain one another in many contexts, but their usage differences require a range of evidence.

Natural Persian equivalents for healing and recovery suffice; unfamiliar terms need not be invented to preserve an unproven distinction.

SH F Y and M R Ḍ: illness

Maraḍ denotes illness or impaired health.

Healing concerns removal of that condition.

Both appear in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80:

وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ

wa-idhā mariḍtu fa-huwa yashfīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80

“And when I become ill, it is He who heals me.”

Illness and healing describe different conditions.

Healing should not simply be defined as the absence of every possible illness.

Someone can recover from one illness while experiencing another bodily problem.

Ordinary language says “he recovered from this illness” without claiming lifelong immunity from all diseases.

Healing concerns the particular illness or suffering, not necessarily complete, eternal health in every respect.

SH F Y and D W Y: medicine

Dawāʾ names something used for treatment.

Shifāʾ can denote recovery itself or a healing property.

A medicine may be prescribed, but the label alone does not guarantee recovery before its effects are established.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 describes a healing benefit in the bees' drink.

It connects a natural substance with healing without naming every illness that might benefit.

Medicine and healing thus share a semantic field without being complete synonyms.

SH F Y, ʿ F W and R Ḥ M: pardon and mercy

ʿAfw in ethical and legal usage concerns pardon or relinquishing a claim or demand for accountability.

Mercy encompasses goodwill, forgiveness and conveying benefit.

Healing concerns the removal of illness or suffering.

Pardon and mercy may sometimes relieve distress, but this does not make the three roots identical.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 pairs healing and mercy:

شِفَاءٌ وَرَحْمَةٌ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

shifāʾun wa-raḥmatun li-l-muʾminīn

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82

“Healing and mercy for believers.”

This pairing can describe the Quran as enabling relief from injury and bringing believers good and mercy.

The conjunction alone does not establish healing as always the first stage and mercy as always the next stage of a fixed process.

Each concept must be understood within its own semantic field.

SH F Y and H D Y: guidance

Guidance directs toward the right path.

Healing concerns removing illness or injury.

They occur together in Yūnus 10:57 and Fuṣṣilat 41:44.

For example, a false belief may have led someone away from truth.

Recognizing the right path is guidance.

Abandoning the prejudice, fear or misplaced attachment preventing its acceptance may fall within healing's semantic field.

This illustrates their relationship without establishing a universal rule that guidance must follow a separate treatment stage.

Recognizing truth and being freed from a false belief sometimes occur in one interconnected process.

Guidance and healing may complement one another without being lexically identical.

SH F Y and SH F W: an edge

Shafā belongs to SH F W and denotes an edge or margin in the Quran.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103 says:

وَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ

wa-kuntum ʿalā shafā ḥufratin mina al-nār

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

“And you were on the edge of a pit of fire.”

Shifāʾ belongs to SH F Y and denotes recovery.

The spellings are close, but their Quranic meanings differ.

Some older dictionaries gather edge and healing usages in one entry and attempt to explain them through a common image.

One lexical analysis connects healing with overcoming illness and reaching its end.

That semantic account does not establish historical priority or a certain derivational relationship between the branches.

For Forqan, edge and healing should therefore be examined according to their respective roots and Quranic uses.

SH F Y, SH F H and SH F ʿ: lip and intercession

Shafah, belonging to SH F H, means a lip.

Al-Balad 90:9 says:

وَلِسَانًا وَشَفَتَيْنِ

wa-lisānan wa-shafatayn

Al-Balad 90:9

“And a tongue and two lips.”

Shifāʾ instead concerns healing and recovery.

Shafāʿah belongs to SH F ʿ and concerns joining and intercession.

Despite similar initial letters, these families have different meanings and usages.

Healing's root does not itself yield intercession, mediation or the lips' role in speech.

Sound resemblance alone provides no reason to identify Quranic healing with intercession.

8. Morphology and proposed Forqan renderings

Yashfi in Al-Tawbah 9:14

The Quranic form is:

وَيَشْفِ صُدُورَ قَوْمٍ مُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-yashfi ṣudūra qawmin muʾminīn

Al-Tawbah 9:14

“And heal the breasts of a believing people.”

Yashfi is a third-person masculine singular imperfect of the basic triliteral verb, in the jussive.

It is coordinated with yuʿadhdhibhum, which is jussive as the response to the imperative qātilūhum.

As the root is final-weak with yāʾ, that final weak letter is dropped in the jussive, producing yashfi.

The conjunction wa- is attached at its beginning.

God is the subject referent; ṣudūra, “breasts,” is the object.

Healing concerns a specified believing people's breasts, not everyone in every circumstance.

Yashfīn in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80

The Quranic form is:

فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ

fa-huwa yashfīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80

“It is He who heals me.”

Yashfīn is an indicative imperfect of the basic triliteral verb.

Its implicit third-person singular subject refers to God.

The first-person singular object refers to Abraham.

The root yāʾ remains; nūn is the protective nūn. The object-pronoun yāʾ is omitted in the written form and in the Ḥafṣ reading, with the final kasrah indicating it.

The verb means “He heals me,” not “He is healed.”

Abraham is the ill recipient of healing, while God is its agent.

The noun shifāʾ

In the other four occurrences, shifāʾ is indefinite and nominative.

In Yūnus 10:57 it is coordinated with mawʿiẓah, “admonition.”

In Al-Naḥl 16:69, fīhi is the fronted predicate and shifāʾ the postponed subject.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:82, shifāʾ is the predicate of huwa within mā huwa shifāʾun wa-raḥmah.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:44, it is coordinated with hudan; both describe the Quran for believers.

The presence or absence of a conjunction does not create a new derivative.

All these uses belong to the same noun and lexical family.

Suggested equivalents

Shafā: healed, treated or brought about recovery.

Shifāʾ: healing or recovery; remedy when referring to a healing agent.

Shāfin: a healer or healing agent.

Mashfiyy: healed or recovered.

Istashfā: sought healing or treatment.

Istishfāʾ: seeking healing or treatment.

Tashaffā: found relief from suffering or distress; in a revenge context, took satisfaction in an enemy's injury.

Tashaffin: relief of distress; in a condemnatory usage, revenge intended to appease anger.

Renderings of the six Quranic uses

Al-Tawbah 9:14

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82

Fuṣṣilat 41:44

“Say: For those who believe, it is guidance and healing.”

Retaining the familiar loanword shifā in Persian/Dari is natural in these passages. These proposals do not alter the site's installed verse translations.

Each explanation should identify what is being healed.

Bodily healing in Al-Shuʿarāʾ and Al-Naḥl does not have the same referent as healing what is within breasts.

The context of healing believers' breasts in Al-Tawbah also differs from Quranic healing in Yūnus, Al-Isrāʾ and Fuṣṣilat.

These differences do not require unrelated root meanings: recovery and relief of suffering remain common to them.

9. Conclusion

In early Arabic, SH F Y denotes healing, recovery and relief from illness or suffering.

Physically, it concerns recovery from illness. Inwardly, it can concern grief, anger, distress and injuries involving belief or inclination.

The poetry traditionally attributed to ʿAntarah supports early usage of inward healing, although this study does not independently date the individual line. Healing the soul or breast should not simply be treated as a late invention.

The root occurs six times in the Quran.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80, Abraham attributes recovery from illness to God.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 describes the bees' drink as containing healing for people.

Yūnus 10:57 relates healing to what is within breasts, alongside admonition, guidance and mercy.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 and Fuṣṣilat 41:44 describe the Quran as healing for believers, while other groups may respond differently.

Al-Tawbah 9:14 concerns healing a believing people's breasts amid confrontation with aggression and treaty-breaking; Al-Tawbah 9:15 describes removing their hearts' anger.

Healing should neither be confined to bodily treatment nor reduced entirely to psychological calm or religious correction.

The defensible semantic core is removing illness, pain or suffering and attaining recovery.

Each use requires identifying the injury and healing agent separately.

Bodily treatment may involve medicine and natural means. Inward healing may accompany recognizing truth, correcting belief, ending injustice or relieving distress.

Not every medicine necessarily heals, every pleasant feeling constitutes recovery, or every angry response appropriately treats suffering.

In passages about the Quran, healing accompanies guidance, admonition and mercy. The divine message can therefore help correct understanding, belief and inward orientation, beyond conveying reports and commands.

The Quran also describes differing responses: one person accepts truth and benefits, while another persists in injustice and denial and deprives themselves of that benefit.

Shifāʾ should also be distinguished from shafā, “edge,” shafah, “lip,” and shafāʿah, “intercession.” Similar letters alone do not prove a shared historical meaning.

For Forqan, healing, recovery and the verb “to heal” are natural equivalents. Explanations of bodily illness, inward suffering and guidance belong to each verse's context and should not be built into the word's lexical definition.

The Quran presents healing in bodily and inward life. Both share release from an injurious condition and attainment of recovery; the verses identify the particular object, manner and outcome.
