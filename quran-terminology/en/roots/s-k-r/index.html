The Arabic root S K R concerns intoxication, disordered perception, obstructed flow, and blocked passage. Sakira means “became intoxicated”; sukr denotes intoxication and impaired awareness; sakrān describes someone in that state.

Alongside this meaning, Arabic records sakara al-nahara for blocking a watercourse, sakarat al-rīḥu for the wind becoming still, and sukkirat al-abṣāru for sight being blocked or impaired.

These uses have a meaningful connection: something's ordinary flow or operation encounters an obstruction or disturbance. Water is held back, wind subsides, vision is impaired, and an intoxicated person's ordinary discernment and judgment are disrupted.

This explanatory connection does not establish that all the uses historically developed from blocking water. Early sources record intoxication, bewilderment, obstruction, and stillness; their historical sequence requires independent evidence.

The Quran uses the root in different settings: intoxication and awareness during ṣalāt, intoxicating drink, impaired sight, behavioral bewilderment, the terror of resurrection, and the stupor of death.

Sukr therefore does not name only a state caused by drinking wine. Wine is one familiar cause, but the root can also express disturbance and impaired perception arising from other causes.

1. The root in early Arabic

Intoxication and impaired discernment

Classical Arabic contrasts al-sukr with al-ṣaḥw.

Ṣaḥw concerns sobriety and being free of intoxication, whereas sukr describes a state in which awareness and ordinary discernment are impaired.

Sakira al-rajulu means that the person became intoxicated.

Sakrān describes an intoxicated person; sukārā is its plural.

Intoxication here is more than an emotional change. A person's speech, recognition of their situation, judgment, behavioral control, and understanding of events around them may be impaired.

The intensity is not identical in every person or circumstance.

Intoxication can range from limited impairment of judgment to severe disturbance of awareness and behavior. The word alone does not specify the amount consumed, precise severity, or duration.

Arabic records askarahu al-sharābu for a drink causing someone to become intoxicated.

The drink is the cause of the state; sukr names the state itself.

Distinguishing cause from result matters. Not every drink intoxicates, and not every disturbance of awareness arises from drinking.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

A poem by the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Qamīʾah says:

يَا رُبَّ مَنْ أَسْفَاهُ أَحْلَامُهُ
أَنْ قِيلَ يَوْمًا إِنَّ عَمْرًا سَكُورْ

yā rubba man asfāhu aḥlāmuhu / an qīla yawman inna ʿamran sakūr

إِنْ أَكُ مِسْكِيرًا فَلَا أَشْرَبُ
وَغْلًا وَلَا يَسْلَمُ مِنِّي الْبَعِيرْ

in aku miskīran fa-lā ashrabu / waghlan wa-lā yaslamu minnī al-baʿīr

The verses convey: “Many a person's judgment led him into rashness when it was said that ʿAmr often becomes drunk. If I am a frequent drinker, I do not drink as an uninvited guest; nor does a camel escape me.” The final phrase suggests slaughtering camels for hospitality, rather than merely drinking at others' expense.

Sakūr and miskīr describe someone who becomes intoxicated often or repeatedly.

The witness shows that intoxication from drinking was expressed through this word family in early Arabic poetry.

The poet also treats it as social conduct that others might judge or criticize.

His evaluation of drinking is not part of sukr's lexical meaning. The word denotes intoxication; judging the behavior is a separate matter.

ʿAmr ibn Qamīʾah is recognized as a pre-Islamic poet who lived before Quranic revelation. The poem is preserved in the collection attributed to him and in literary compilations.

It provides significant evidence for the early sense of intoxication, subject to the usual limitations in dating the recording of individual verses and establishing every word of transmitted pre-Islamic poetry.

Blocking a watercourse

Sakara al-nahara means blocking the watercourse and preventing its flow.

Al-sakr denotes the act of blocking; al-sikr names the barrier or dam placed in the water's path.

For example, someone may obstruct a flowing stream or channel to direct its water elsewhere.

The water's ordinary flow is then stopped or restricted.

Lexicons also record al-sikr for a dam or embankment built to retain water or prevent it spreading.

This usage concerns farming and irrigation, where controlling water is important for supplying different fields.

Here the root concerns blocking a passage and holding back flow, not intoxication.

A semantic connection can be drawn: just as a barrier stops water, intoxication may disrupt ordinary perception and judgment.

That comparison alone does not prove that the word for intoxication historically derived from a water barrier's name.

One early lexical explanation takes bewilderment as the core meaning and explains obstructed water through its being held and left without onward movement.

Other lexical explanations emphasize blocking and restraint to elucidate intoxication and impaired perception.

Even within the lexicographic tradition, the connection among the branches was thus not confined to a single explanation.

Wind stopping and flow becoming still

Arabic records sakarat al-rīḥu for wind becoming still after blowing.

Laylatun sākirah also describes a night whose wind has become calm.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar says:

تُزَادُ لَيَالِيَّ فِي طُولِهَا
فَلَيْسَتْ بِطَلْقٍ وَلَا سَاكِرَهْ

tuzādu layāliyya fī ṭūlihā / fa-laysat bi-ṭalqin wa-lā sākirah

“My nights grow longer; they are neither mild and pleasant nor calm and windless.”

Sākirah here concerns a night's calm with respect to wind.

It does not describe human intoxication, but a natural condition in which wind has subsided.

The verse is attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar and is transmitted in early sources with variants in its first half-line.

It shows that stillness and interrupted flow were also expressed through S K R in early Arabic.

The presence of both intoxication and stillness in pre-Islamic poetry does not determine which developed first.

Three documented fields can therefore be identified: intoxication and disturbed awareness, obstruction of flow, and stillness or subsiding movement.

Their conceptual connection is intelligible, but a common historical origin should not be asserted without firm evidence.

2. Quranic forms and derivatives

The root occurs seven times in six Quranic verses.

Its four principal forms are:

Sukārā: The plural of sakrān, meaning intoxicated people. It occurs three times: once in Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 and twice in Al-Ḥajj 22:2.

Sukkirat: A Form II passive perfect verb, third-person feminine singular. In Al-Ḥijr 15:15 it concerns sight being blocked or impaired.

Sakrah: A noun denoting a state or instance of intoxication or stupor. It occurs twice: with the pronoun “their” in Al-Ḥijr 15:72 and in sakrat al-mawt in Qāf 50:19.

Sakar: A noun for something causing intoxication, especially an intoxicating drink. It occurs in Al-Naḥl 16:67.

Arabic also has other derivatives in this family:

Sakira: Became intoxicated.

Sukr: Intoxication.

Sakrān: Intoxicated.

Sakrā: The feminine adjective for intoxicated.

Sikkīr and sakūr: Someone who becomes intoxicated frequently or repeatedly.

Askara: Caused intoxication.

Muskir: Intoxicating.

Tasākara: Pretended to be intoxicated.

Sakkara: In certain uses, blocked, obstructed, or caused impairment.

Sikr: A dam or barrier to flowing water.

Sākir: A still or stopped condition, as of wind or water.

These exact forms do not occur in the Quran. Sakkara nevertheless belongs to the same Form II pattern as the Quranic passive sukkirat and should not be treated as an unrelated pattern.

Sukkar, the sweetening substance called shekar in Persian, is described in lexicons as a Persian loanword. Similar consonants alone do not establish it as a derivative of intoxication or obstruction.

Sakaran in Al-Naḥl 16:67 does not mean edible sugar.

3. Intoxication and awareness in Al-Nisāʾ 4:43

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 says:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَقْرَبُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَنْتُمْ سُكَارَىٰ حَتَّىٰ تَعْلَمُوا مَا تَقُولُونَ وَلَا جُنُبًا إِلَّا عَابِرِي سَبِيلٍ حَتَّىٰ تَغْتَسِلُوا وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ مَرْضَىٰ أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ أَوْ جَاءَ أَحَدٌ مِنْكُمْ مِنَ الْغَائِطِ أَوْ لَامَسْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا فَامْسَحُوا بِوُجُوهِكُمْ وَأَيْدِيكُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَفُوًّا غَفُورًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā taqrabū al-ṣalāta wa-antum sukārā ḥattā taʿlamū mā taqūlūna wa-lā junuban illā ʿābirī sabīlin ḥattā taghtasilū wa-in kuntum marḍā aw ʿalā safarin aw jāʾa aḥadun minkum mina al-ghāʾiṭi aw lāmastumu al-nisāʾa fa-lam tajidū māʾan fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban fa-imsaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum inna Allāha kāna ʿafuwwan ghafūrā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43

“You who have believed, do not approach ṣalāt while intoxicated, until you know what you are saying; nor in a state of janābah, except when passing through, until you wash. If you are ill or on a journey, or one of you comes from relieving himself, or you have touched women and find no water, then perform tayammum with clean earth and wipe your faces and hands. Indeed, The God is Pardoning, Forgiving.”

Sukārā describes the addressees' state.

The prohibition on approaching ṣalāt is qualified by wa-antum sukārā: while you are intoxicated.

The verse then gives a limit for this state in relation to ṣalāt:

«حَتَّىٰ تَعْلَمُوا مَا تَقُولُونَ».

ḥattā taʿlamū mā taqūlūn

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43

“Until you know what you are saying.”

This phrase is particularly important for understanding Quranic sukr.

The relationship between intoxication and awareness

The verse does more than prohibit approaching ṣalāt while intoxicated: it also mentions knowing what one says.

Intoxication here is accordingly connected with impaired understanding and awareness.

Someone whose intoxication has impaired their discernment and understanding of their own speech is in a state incompatible with conscious performance of ṣalāt.

This does not mean that every intoxicated person necessarily understands no words or is completely unconscious.

The severity varies, and “until you know what you are saying” gives a clear criterion for the situation under discussion.

Merely recognizing words without understanding their meaning also differs from the awareness to which the verse draws attention.

A person may repeat memorized words while being inattentive to their content and to the situation.

The result may involve diminished awareness, but ordinary distraction should not therefore be called intoxication in its direct lexical sense without justification.

Is sukr caused only by drinking wine?

Intoxication caused by drink is among the clearest and best-known Arabic uses of sukr.

The root also concerns disturbed awareness and is used in the Quran beyond the consumption of drink.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:2, for example, people appear intoxicated although the verse explicitly says they are not.

Al-Ḥijr 15:72 describes the sakrah of Lot's people in the context of their conduct and bewilderment.

The root's range therefore extends beyond intoxication caused by wine.

An essential distinction remains: substance-induced intoxication, severe disturbance from terror, and ordinary mental inattention are neither the same instance nor the same degree of impairment.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 presents knowing what one says as a criterion for approaching ṣalāt.

The substance's name is consequently not the only issue; the person's actual awareness also matters.

This connection can inform the study of awareness in ṣalāt. Lexical analysis must nevertheless maintain the distinction between intoxication and other forms of reduced concentration.

Intoxication and janābah are different states

The verse continues with wa-lā junuban.

Janābah and intoxication are distinct descriptions and should not be equated.

Sukārā concerns intoxication and impaired awareness, whereas junuban identifies another state, with which the verse associates washing.

The two prohibitions appearing together does not make their lexical meanings identical.

Nor does the verse establish that everyone who is slightly tired or distressed has precisely the same status as someone in janābah or intoxication.

Each description must be understood according to its own meaning and the verse's qualifications.

4. Sakaran in Al-Naḥl 16:67: An intoxicating product of fruit

Al-Naḥl 16:67 says:

وَمِنْ ثَمَرَاتِ النَّخِيلِ وَالْأَعْنَابِ تَتَّخِذُونَ مِنْهُ سَكَرًا وَرِزْقًا حَسَنًا إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِقَوْمٍ يَعْقِلُونَ

wa-min thamarāti al-nakhīli wa-al-aʿnābi tattakhidhūna minhu sakaran wa-rizqan ḥasanan inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yaʿqilūn

Al-Naḥl 16:67

“From the fruits of date palms and grapevines you obtain intoxicating drink and good provision. Surely in that is a sign for people who reason.”

Sakaran is a noun placed beside rizqan ḥasanan.

Tattakhidhūna describes what people do with the products of palms and grapevines.

Arabic records al-sakar for intoxicating drink, especially that made from dates and grapes.

Some lexical accounts also mention unfermented drink, vinegar, or food. The intoxicating sense nevertheless has stronger support in the word family's connection with intoxication and established Arabic usage.

Does sakar here simply name wine?

In this context, sakaran refers to a product made from fruit and associated with intoxication.

Khamr is also used in the Quran for a substance associated with covering and impairing the intellect.

In uses concerning intoxicating drinks, both terms may refer to the same product, but their roots and semantic perspectives differ.

Sukr highlights intoxication and sakar something that causes that state.

Khamr belongs to a separate lexical family involving covering and being covered.

Shared reference does not make every derivative of the two families identical in meaning.

“Intoxicating drink” for Al-Naḥl 16:67 clearly fits the setting of fruit and the preparation of a product.

Distinguishing sakaran from rizqan ḥasanan

The verse places two expressions together:

Sakaran and rizqan ḥasanan.

Grammatically, ḥasanan modifies rizqan, not sakaran.

A translation should therefore not say “good intoxicating drink and good provision,” as though the adjective applied to both.

Conversely, the absence of “good” after sakar does not alone settle the entire question of permissibility or prohibition.

The verse describes products that people obtain from fruit.

Reporting an existing practice differs from commanding it.

Tattakhidhūna means “you make” or “you obtain.” The verb itself does not declare every use of the product religiously permissible or commendable.

Sakar and the ruling on khamr

Al-Baqarah 2:219 describes benefit and great sin in khamr and maysir, explicitly stating that their sin is greater than their benefit.

Al-Māʾidah 5:90 and Al-Māʾidah 5:91 command avoidance of khamr and describe its harmful effects in hostility, hatred, and obstruction of remembrance of The God and ṣalāt.

These verses should be read alongside Al-Naḥl 16:67.

People's making an intoxicating product from dates and grapes does not by itself establish permission to consume it.

Understanding the verse also does not require replacing the intoxicating sense of sakar with ordinary food to remove an apparent conflict with other verses.

Each verse has its own structure: Al-Naḥl reports what people produce, Al-Baqarah evaluates the benefit and sin of khamr, and Al-Māʾidah commands avoidance.

Distinguishing a report from an ethical judgment and a command also matters when examining claims that Quranic verses conflict or supersede one another.

For this root study, the lexical conclusion is clear: sakar here denotes an intoxicating product, while the religious ruling on consumption cannot be derived from that noun alone.

5. Sukr in vision, conduct, and the terror of resurrection

Sukkirat abṣārunā: Impaired seeing

Al-Ḥijr 15:15 says:

لَقَالُوا إِنَّمَا سُكِّرَتْ أَبْصَارُنَا بَلْ نَحْنُ قَوْمٌ مَسْحُورُونَ

la-qālū innamā sukkirat abṣārunā bal naḥnu qawmun masḥūrūn

Al-Ḥijr 15:15

“They would certainly say: Our eyes have only been blocked and impaired; rather, we are a bewitched people.”

Sukkirat is a Form II passive perfect verb.

Abṣārunā is the entity to which the impairment is attributed.

The root here does not refer to drinking wine.

The expression may indicate obstructed sight, vision being covered, or the eyes entering a state in which a person cannot properly discern what they see.

Some recognized readings have sukirat without doubling the kāf. Both readings concern blocked or impaired sight, though their morphological structures differ.

Lexicons likewise explain the combination through blocking, covering, and disturbed vision.

The verse has an essential contextual feature: it reports the deniers' hypothetical words.

Al-Ḥijr 15:14 says that even if a gate in heaven were opened and they kept ascending through it, they would still speak in this way.

Al-Ḥijr 15:15 therefore does not report that their eyes really were blocked. It presents a claim they would make to deny what they observed.

S K R and S Ḥ R are distinct roots

Sukkirat and masḥūrūn occur together in the verse.

Sukkirat belongs to S K R; masḥūrūn belongs to S Ḥ R.

The former concerns the condition or impairment of sight; the latter expresses a claim of being bewitched.

Their occurrence together does not make their roots or lexical meanings identical.

The speakers first describe their eyes as blocked or impaired, then attribute their situation to sorcery.

Their claim, its alleged cause, and what actually happened must be distinguished.

Sakratihim: The bewilderment of Lot's people

Al-Ḥijr 15:72 says:

لَعَمْرُكَ إِنَّهُمْ لَفِي سَكْرَتِهِمْ يَعْمَهُونَ

la-ʿamruka innahum la-fī sakratihim yaʿmahūn

Al-Ḥijr 15:72

“By your life, they were wandering blindly in their intoxication.”

This verse occurs in the story of Lot's people.

In the preceding verses, the townspeople come rejoicing and anticipating misconduct. Lot asks them not to disgrace him concerning his guests and to remain mindful of The God.

Their state is then described as sakratihim.

The Quran does not mention drinking wine here.

The word sakrah alone therefore cannot establish that the group had necessarily become intoxicated through drink.

The context concerns domination by conduct and desire that prevented clear judgment and withdrawal from wrongdoing.

Yaʿmahūn also concerns bewilderment and movement without clear discernment.

Two concepts appear together: intoxication or disturbance, and wandering blindly within that state.

Behavioral intoxication and human responsibility

A person may become so absorbed in desire, anger, prejudice, or pleasure-seeking that they fail to judge their conduct properly.

Intoxication can then function as an expression extending beyond wine consumption.

This figurative usage should not be equated in every respect with medically understood intoxication or actual substance-induced impairment.

Lot's people's conduct is described as bewildered; their moral responsibility is understood from the story's context.

Sakrah itself does not mean sexual wrongdoing or oppression. Those actions are established elsewhere in the story.

The word describes their agitated and disordered state.

People appearing intoxicated on the day of resurrection

Al-Ḥajj 22:2 says:

يَوْمَ تَرَوْنَهَا تَذْهَلُ كُلُّ مُرْضِعَةٍ عَمَّا أَرْضَعَتْ وَتَضَعُ كُلُّ ذَاتِ حَمْلٍ حَمْلَهَا وَتَرَى النَّاسَ سُكَارَىٰ وَمَا هُمْ بِسُكَارَىٰ وَلَٰكِنَّ عَذَابَ اللَّهِ شَدِيدٌ

yawma tarawnahā tadhhalu kullu murḍiʿatin ʿammā arḍaʿat wa-taḍaʿu kullu dhāti ḥamlin ḥamlahā wa-tarā al-nāsa sukārā wa-mā hum bi-sukārā wa-lākinna ʿadhāba Allāhi shadīd

Al-Ḥajj 22:2

“On the day you see it, every nursing woman will be distracted from what she nurses, and every pregnant woman will lay down her burden. You will see people intoxicated, although they are not intoxicated; but the punishment of The God is severe.”

Sukārā occurs twice in this verse.

First, people are portrayed as appearing intoxicated.

The text immediately adds:

«وَمَا هُمْ بِسُكَارَىٰ».

wa-mā hum bi-sukārā

Al-Ḥajj 22:2

They are not intoxicated.

This negation is essential to the verse's meaning.

The Quran does not say that people have drunk wine that day or that an intoxicating substance has produced their condition.

The day's terror and disturbance are so intense that people's appearance and behavior resemble those of intoxicated people.

The image of intoxication alongside its denial

If sukārā had precisely the same application in both clauses, “they are not intoxicated” would be unintelligible.

The verse distinguishes outward resemblance from the reality of their condition.

They look intoxicated, but their state is not caused by intoxicating drink.

The end of the verse explains its intensity:

«وَلَٰكِنَّ عَذَابَ اللَّهِ شَدِيدٌ».

wa-lākinna ʿadhāba Allāhi shadīd

Al-Ḥajj 22:2

Sukārā thus has figurative force here, expressing severe disturbance of perception and behavior.

Intoxication can serve in Arabic as a model for describing terror, bewilderment, and the loss of ordinary awareness.

The comparison does not establish that everyone will experience an identical medical or chemical condition that day.

The verse describes the event's severity and people's response.

6. Sakrat al-mawt: The stupor and terror of death

Qāf 50:19 says:

وَجَاءَتْ سَكْرَةُ الْمَوْتِ بِالْحَقِّ ذَٰلِكَ مَا كُنْتَ مِنْهُ تَحِيدُ

wa-jāʾat sakratu al-mawti bi-al-ḥaqqi dhālika mā kunta minhu taḥīd

Qāf 50:19

“The stupor of death has come with the truth: this is what you were trying to evade.”

Sakrah here denotes an intense, overwhelming state associated with death's arrival.

It belongs to the same family as intoxication, but intoxicating drink is not the subject.

Al-mawt, in the possessive construction with sakrah, identifies the setting and kind of state.

Sakrat al-mawt therefore concerns the intense, transformative experience of approaching death, which may involve disturbance, stupor, and changed awareness.

Death's stupor and diminished awareness

Human dying may involve changes in bodily function and awareness.

Some people lose awareness in their final hours or moments, some experience confusion, and others retain communication and recognition of their surroundings until close to the end.

These medical differences should not be extracted directly from sakrah.

Qāf 50:19 does not explain a disease, brain mechanism, or bodily stages of dying.

The word expresses the overwhelming, transformative experience of death arriving.

Nor does it establish that everyone must become medically unconscious when dying.

Sakrat al-mawt is a Quranic expression for confronting death, not a specific diagnosis with identical signs in everyone.

Death's stupor and truth

The verse says:

«وَجَاءَتْ سَكْرَةُ الْمَوْتِ بِالْحَقِّ».

wa-jāʾat sakratu al-mawti bi-al-ḥaqq

Qāf 50:19

It then adds:

«ذَٰلِكَ مَا كُنْتَ مِنْهُ تَحِيدُ».

dhālika mā kunta minhu taḥīd

Qāf 50:19

This is what you were trying to evade.

Death's stupor here is linked to a reality that a person cannot evade indefinitely.

Qāf 50:20 describes the trumpet being blown; Qāf 50:21 describes every soul arriving with a driver and a witness.

Qāf 50:22 then describes the covering being removed and sight becoming sharp.

The sequence concerns more than bodily functions ending: the person encounters truth, judgment, and return.

Sakrah alone does not imply that everyone sees every detail of the unseen world at death.

Death's stupor should likewise not be equated with complete annihilation of human existence. That question belongs to broader Quranic inquiry into death, taking of souls, resurrection, and life with the Lord.

The root's meaning here remains an intense state of stupor and disturbance associated with death's arrival.

7. Comparison with related roots

S K R and KH M R: Intoxication and covering the intellect

KH M R in Arabic concerns covering and concealing.

Khamr, when used for intoxicating drink, is associated with the intellect being covered and impaired.

Sukr in its familiar sense denotes intoxication and disturbed perception.

The two roots approach a related phenomenon from different perspectives: covering and the state of intoxication.

Intoxication may be caused by khamr, but Quranic S K R is not restricted to wine.

Al-Ḥajj 22:2 portrays people as appearing intoxicated without being intoxicated.

Al-Ḥijr 15:72 also uses sakrah in a setting of behavioral bewilderment.

Khamr and sukr may overlap in particular situations, but they are not complete synonyms.

S K R and S Ḥ R: Impaired perception and sorcery

S Ḥ R is a separate family used in the Quran concerning sorcery and related phenomena.

Al-Ḥijr 15:15 places sukkirat abṣārunā and naḥnu qawmun masḥūrūn in the same utterance.

S K R concerns the state of impaired sight, whereas S Ḥ R concerns the cause alleged by the speakers.

Closeness in some uses does not make the roots identical.

Nor does every perceptual disturbance or visual illusion prove sorcery has occurred.

S K R and S K N: Interrupted flow and stillness

S K N concerns stillness, calm, and settledness.

Arabic can also describe wind that has stopped through S K R.

This overlap does not make all their derivatives equivalent.

Sukūn may denote comfort and calm, whereas sukr in its intoxicating sense denotes impaired awareness.

Not every calm state is intoxication, and not every stillness involves impaired perception.

S K R and GH F L: Intoxication and heedlessness

Heedlessness concerns inattention or unawareness of something that warrants attention.

Intoxication can cause heedlessness by impairing discernment and attention.

Ghaflah and sukr nevertheless have different meanings.

Someone may be entirely sober while remaining heedless of a responsibility or truth.

Conversely, an intoxicated person may still recognize some things while being impaired in others.

Not every instance of heedlessness should therefore be called intoxication in its lexical sense.

Figuratively, one may speak of intoxication with power, wealth, or prejudice, but these should be distinguished from actual drink-induced intoxication.

S K R and ʿ M H: Intoxication and bewilderment

ʿ M H in Arabic and the Quran concerns bewilderment and movement without clear discernment.

Al-Ḥijr 15:72 combines sakratihim with yaʿmahūn.

Intoxication and bewilderment may thus be present together in one pattern of conduct.

ʿAmah itself does not mean drinking an intoxicant.

Sakrah and ʿamah also contribute different meanings: one emphasizes intoxication and disturbance, the other wandering blindly within that state.

Translating one in place of the other loses the distinction within the Quranic expression.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The derivative and its context should govern translation.

Sukārā — Al-Nisāʾ 4:43

“Intoxicated.”

Within the clause:

“Do not approach ṣalāt while intoxicated, until you know what you are saying.”

Sukkirat abṣārunā — Al-Ḥijr 15:15

“Our eyes have been blocked or impaired.”

“Our eyes have been blocked” is a natural basic rendering; the sense of impaired vision can be explained separately.

Fī sakratihim yaʿmahūn — Al-Ḥijr 15:72

“They were wandering blindly in their intoxication.”

Intoxication here should not be restricted to drinking wine without evidence.

Sakaran — Al-Naḥl 16:67

“Intoxicating drink.”

This fits the product obtained from dates and grapes.

Sukārā wa-mā hum bi-sukārā — Al-Ḥajj 22:2

“You see them intoxicated, although they are not intoxicated.”

Both the figurative description and the denial of intoxication must be preserved.

Sakrat al-mawt — Qāf 50:19

“The stupor of death,” or the retained expression “sakrat al-mawt.”

The Persian equivalent “death's stupor” is more immediately intelligible, while retaining sakrah preserves the direct connection with Arabic.

For other lexical expressions, the following equivalents are suitable; the final entry is a related construction based on a Quranic verb:

  • Sakira: Became intoxicated.
  • Sukr: Intoxication.
  • Sakrān: Intoxicated.
  • Sakūr / sikkīr: Frequently intoxicated; someone repeatedly becoming intoxicated.
  • Askarahu: Made him intoxicated.
  • Muskir: Intoxicating.
  • Sakara al-nahara: Blocked the flow of a watercourse.
  • Al-sikr: A dam or barrier to water flow.
  • Sakarat al-rīḥu: The wind subsided.
  • Laylatun sākirah: A calm, windless night.
  • Tasākara: Pretended to be intoxicated.
  • Sukkirat al-abṣāru: Sight was blocked or impaired.

Quranic sukr should not invariably become “wine-drinking,” nor should actual intoxication be erased from the verses concerning drink and ṣalāt.

Death's stupor should also not be restricted to one medical disease or inevitable unconsciousness in everyone who dies.

Conclusion

S K R in Arabic concerns intoxication, disturbed perception, obstructed flow, and subsiding movement.

Early Arabic used sukr for intoxication caused by drink. ʿAmr ibn Qamīʾah's poem illustrates sakūr and miskīr in this setting.

Blocking flowing water and calming wind are also expressed through the family. Aws ibn Ḥajar's verse attests sākirah for a windless night.

A semantic connection can be drawn through interrupted flow or ordinary function, but independent historical evidence is needed before treating every branch as a proven development from one original sense.

The Quran uses the root seven times in six verses.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 links intoxication with impaired awareness and makes knowing what one says an important criterion for approaching ṣalāt.

In Al-Naḥl 16:67, sakaran is an intoxicating product of dates and grapes. The verse reports people making it; that report alone is not permission to consume it.

Al-Ḥijr 15:15 uses sukkirat in an allegation of blocked or impaired sight. These hypothetical words of deniers do not establish that it actually occurred.

Al-Ḥijr 15:72 describes Lot's people's bewilderment through sakrah, without mentioning wine consumption in that context.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:2, people appear intoxicated from terror, while the verse explicitly says they are not intoxicated.

In Qāf 50:19, sakrah concerns the intense, transformative experience of death arriving.

A defensible central definition is therefore:

In human uses, S K R denotes intoxication and impairment of awareness, ordinary perception, and judgment. Other branches concern obstructing flow or movement subsiding. These uses are semantically connected through disrupted ordinary function, but their historical order is not conclusively established.

For Forqan, “intoxication” is the basic human sense; “intoxicating drink” suits sakaran, “blocked or impaired sight” suits sukkirat abṣārunā, and “the stupor of death” suits sakrat al-mawt.

The root highlights the relationship between awareness, conduct, and responsibility. Intoxication may impair judgment; terror may make people appear intoxicated; overpowering desire or prejudice may leave them bewildered in wrongdoing.

The cause, severity, and responsibility involved in each state require separate assessment. These states are not identical in cause or consequence.

Quranic sukr should therefore not be reduced to a drink's name, nor should every emotion or distraction be treated as intoxication without evidence.

The human uses emphasize awareness and discernment: capacities whose impairment can transform speech, conduct, and decisions.
