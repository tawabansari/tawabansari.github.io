Foundational Meaning

The root ش ج ر fundamentally points to branching, ramification, interweaving, and complex extension from a single origin. Its most famous meaning is "tree," but the tree is only one natural instance of this core: something that begins from a root or trunk, branches out, spreads in different directions, and forms an interwoven network of root, branch, leaf, shade, and fruit.

From this same field, the meaning of dispute and argument can also be understood. Some conflicts are not simple and direct. They branch among several parties, become entangled, and require clear judgment.

Qur'anic Forms of the Root

The Qur'anic forms of this root are limited. شَجَر and شَجَرَة in the Qur'an mostly refer to a tree, trees, or a tree-like structure.

The only verbal use of the root in the Qur'an is شَجَرَ in the phrase فِيمَا شَجَرَ بَيْنَهُمْ. Forms such as tashajur and mushajarah in Arabic relate to quarrel and the interweaving of speech or claims, but those forms do not appear in the Qur'an. For Qur'anic work, direct Qur'anic usage should therefore be distinguished from wider lexical extension.

Linguistic Note

The linguistic center of ش ج ر is branching and interweaving. A tree is not a simple straight-line structure. It begins from one origin, but it does not remain on one direct path. Branches separate from the trunk, each branch may produce further branches, and the whole body forms a broad, multidirectional structure.

This image allows the root to function both in nature, meaning a tree, and in human relations, meaning an issue that has become complex and branched among people.

Material Image of the Root

The material image of this root is a tree with root, trunk, branch, leaf, shade, fruit, and sometimes thorn or bitterness. A tree may provide food, shade, oil, fuel for fire, or, in a Qur'anic parable, carry the meaning of purity or corruption.

Some lexical reports also connect shajar with the intermingling of parts, sticks, or points of contact and crossing. If supported by a firm early witness, this can help explain شَجَرَ بَيْنَهُمْ: an affair between people has not merely become "a disagreement"; it has become knotted and branched.

Older and Classical Arabic Usage

In classical Arabic usage, شَجَر relates to trees and branching growth. From this same field, mushajarah is used for a quarrel in which words and claims become entangled.

When something is said to have shajara between people, an issue has emerged that has moved beyond simplicity, gained branches, and made the main path difficult to discern. In a final version, a reliable witness from early Arabic poetry or old usage may be added if one is found.

Tree in Nature and Provision

The first major Qur'anic field of this root is shajar in nature and the system of provision:

هُوَ الَّذِي أَنزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً لَّكُم مِّنْهُ شَرَابٌ وَمِنْهُ شَجَرٌ فِيهِ تُسِيمُونَ

He is the One who sent down water from the sky; from it you have drink, and from it comes tree-growth in which you pasture. An-Nahl 16:10

In this verse, shajar is connected with water, growth, pasture, and provision. It is not merely an upright object in nature; it is part of a system of nourishment, life, and relation between sky, earth, human being, and animal.

Another verse says:

وَالنَّجْمُ وَالشَّجَرُ يَسْجُدَانِ

And the najm and the tree prostrate. Ar-Rahman 55:6

Here shajar appears beside najm. Many have understood najm in this context as low or stemless vegetation, and shajar as stemmed, branching growth. On that reading, the verse places two kinds of growth together: what spreads over the ground and what rises with branches. Both are subject to The God within the order of creation.

Shajar may also appear as a source of benefit and hidden capacity in nature:

وَشَجَرَةً تَخْرُجُ مِن طُورِ سَيْنَاءَ تَنبُتُ بِالدُّهْنِ وَصِبْغٍ لِّلْآكِلِينَ

And a tree that comes out from Mount Sinai, growing with oil and relish for those who eat. Al-Mu'minun 23:20

الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُم مِّنَ الشَّجَرِ الْأَخْضَرِ نَارًا فَإِذَا أَنتُم مِّنْهُ تُوقِدُونَ

The One who made for you, from the green tree, fire; then from it you kindle. Ya-Sin 36:80

In this verse, the green tree is connected with fire. The point is not only a literary contrast between greenness and fire; it shows hidden capacity within natural matter. What signals moisture and life may, in certain conditions, become a means of kindling fire.

Tree as a Parable for Speech

The Qur'an also uses the tree to explain the structure of speech and truth:

أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ ضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا كَلِمَةً طَيِّبَةً كَشَجَرَةٍ طَيِّبَةٍ أَصْلُهَا ثَابِتٌ وَفَرْعُهَا فِي السَّمَاءِ

Have you not seen how The God strikes a parable? A good word is like a good tree: its root is firm and its branch is in the sky. Ibrahim 14:24

In this verse, origin, branch, firmness, height, and fruit-bearing are gathered into one image. A good word is not merely a beautiful sentence; it is a rooted, living structure that produces effect.

In contrast, the Qur'an says:

وَمَثَلُ كَلِمَةٍ خَبِيثَةٍ كَشَجَرَةٍ خَبِيثَةٍ اجْتُثَّتْ مِن فَوْقِ الْأَرْضِ مَا لَهَا مِن قَرَارٍ

And the parable of a corrupt word is like a corrupt tree, uprooted from above the earth; it has no stability. Ibrahim 14:26

Here the corrupt tree has a tree-like appearance, but it has no rootedness or settledness. Through the image of the tree, the Qur'an distinguishes between speech that is rooted and speech that has no stable ground.

The Tree in Adam's Test

In the story of Adam, the tree becomes a clear point inside the boundary of a test:

وَلَا تَقْرَبَا هَٰذِهِ الشَّجَرَةَ فَتَكُونَا مِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ

And do not approach this tree, lest you become among the wrongdoers. Al-Baqarah 2:35

The Qur'an does not identify the botanical species of the tree. What matters is that the shajarah is something specific, pointed to, and bounded. Approaching it is entering the zone of disobedience.

In Surat Ta-Ha, Satan's speech about this same test is quoted:

فَوَسْوَسَ إِلَيْهِ الشَّيْطَانُ قَالَ يَا آدَمُ هَلْ أَدُلُّكَ عَلَىٰ شَجَرَةِ الْخُلْدِ وَمُلْكٍ لَّا يَبْلَىٰ

Then Satan whispered to him. He said: O Adam, shall I direct you to the tree of everlasting life and a dominion that does not wear away? Ta-Ha 20:120

Here the naming comes from Satan: شَجَرَةِ الْخُلْدِ, the tree of everlasting life. The Qur'an reports this expression as the wording of temptation, not as the true definition of the tree.

Tree in the Context of Trial and Punishment

The Qur'an also speaks of الشَّجَرَةَ الْمَلْعُونَةَ فِي الْقُرْآنِ: "the cursed tree in the Qur'an." Al-Isra 17:60

The verse does not fully explain the exact identity of this shajarah. In connection with the verses on zaqqum, however, one may cautiously say that the Qur'an speaks of a tree with a negative and testing force:

أَذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ نُّزُلًا أَمْ شَجَرَةُ الزَّقُّومِ

Is that better as hospitality, or the tree of zaqqum? As-Saffat 37:62

إِنَّهَا شَجَرَةٌ تَخْرُجُ فِي أَصْلِ الْجَحِيمِ

Indeed, it is a tree that comes out in the root of the blazing Fire. As-Saffat 37:64

طَلْعُهَا كَأَنَّهُ رُءُوسُ الشَّيَاطِينِ

Its emerging fruit is as if it were the heads of devils. As-Saffat 37:65

إِنَّ شَجَرَتَ الزَّقُّومِ طَعَامُ الْأَثِيمِ

Indeed, the tree of zaqqum is the food of the sinful. Ad-Dukhan 44:43-44

In this use, shajarah is no longer a sign of shade, pure food, or good fruit. It is a bitter and infernal structure. This contrast shows that shajarah in the Qur'an can carry two completely different directions: pure and fruitful growth, or corrupt and punishment-bearing growth.

Non-Botanical Use of the Root

The non-botanical verbal use of this root appears in the verse of judgment:

فَلَا وَرَبِّكَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ حَتَّىٰ يُحَكِّمُوكَ فِيمَا شَجَرَ بَيْنَهُمْ

No, by your Lord, they do not believe until they make you judge concerning what has become entangled / disputed between them. An-Nisa 4:65

This is the only verbal use of the root in the Qur'an. شَجَرَ بَيْنَهُمْ is not merely a simple disagreement. It means that an issue has emerged between them, branched out, mixed lines of claim and interest, and reached the point where judgment is needed. In this context, the Messenger's judgment opens the complexity and brings truth out from among the intertwined branches of dispute.

Distinguishing Similar Uses

The uses of shajar should be distinguished. Shajar in An-Nahl 16:10 is natural growth and pasture. Shajar in Ar-Rahman 55:6 is part of the submitting order of creation. The olive tree in Al-Mu'minun 23:20 is a beneficial, provision-bearing tree. The green tree in Ya-Sin 36:80 is natural matter with a hidden capacity for fire.

The good tree in Ibrahim 14:24 is a parable for rooted good speech. The corrupt tree in Ibrahim 14:26 is a parable for speech without rootedness or stability. Adam's tree is a point of testing. The tree of everlasting life is a deceptive title used by Satan in temptation. The tree of zaqqum is a tree in the context of punishment. And شَجَرَ بَيْنَهُمْ is a branched and entangled dispute among people.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

The structural difference between شَجَر and شَجَرَة matters. Shajar may refer to the genus of trees or tree-like growth. Shajarah usually points to a specific tree, an identifiable instance, or a parabolic structure.

In هَٰذِهِ الشَّجَرَة, the reference is to a specific tree. In كَشَجَرَةٍ طَيِّبَة, the discussion is a parabolic instance. In شَجَرَةُ الزَّقُّوم, the genitive connection to zaqqum gives the tree a specific identity. But in مِنْهُ شَجَر, the genus and collective field of tree-growth is meant.

Difference from Nearby Roots

ن ب ت focuses on growing and coming out of the earth, not necessarily on a branching structure. غ ر س relates to planting, not to the tree-body itself. ث م ر points to fruit and result, while shajar is the living structure that may or may not bear fruit.

أ ص ل relates to root and foundation, and ف ر ع to branch and upward extension; but shajarah shows the whole relation of root and branch in a single body. خ ص م relates to dispute and adversarial confrontation, but shajar in شَجَرَ بَيْنَهُمْ more specifically shows the issue becoming complex and the lines of dispute becoming entangled. ن ز ع indicates pulling, removing, or struggle, while shajar more clearly images branching and knotting of the issue.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

In natural contexts, shajar may be translated as "trees," "tree," or sometimes "tree-growth." In a specific context, shajarah is "tree," but in the parables of Ibrahim the structure of root and branch should be preserved: "a good tree, with a firm root and a branch in the sky."

شَجَرَةِ الْخُلْد is best rendered "the tree of everlasting life," with attention to the fact that this name appears in Satan's speech. شَجَرَةُ الزَّقُّوم should remain "the tree of zaqqum."

فِيمَا شَجَرَ بَيْنَهُمْ may be rendered "concerning what became disputed between them," "concerning what became entangled between them," or "concerning the branched dispute that arose among them." For smooth Qur'an translation, "what became disputed between them" is simpler; for root analysis, "the entangling and branching of dispute" is more precise.

Linguistic Summary

The root ش ج ر points to branching, ramification, and interweaving from a single origin. Its material image is the tree: a rooted, trunked, branching, extended body.

The Qur'an uses this root for the natural tree, the provision-bearing tree, the parabolic tree, the tree of testing, the tree of punishment, and complex dispute among people. Its difference from n-b-t is that n-b-t focuses on growth, while shajar focuses on the branched and interwoven structure. Its difference from kh-s-m and n-z-' is that shajar, in the context of dispute, shows the complexity and branching of the issue, not merely hostility or struggle.
