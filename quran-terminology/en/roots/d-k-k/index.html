The Qur’anic Root D K K (dakk)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Pounding down, demolishing, and removing elevation

D K K is an Arabic geminate root: its second and third radicals are identical. It describes breaking and collapsing solid structures, pounding and leveling ground, and removing a surface’s raised features.

In the Qur’an, this family particularly describes a mountain collapsing, a barrier losing its firmness, and the earth being transformed. Dakk means more than simply breaking: in many uses, collapse accompanies reduced height, a flattened prominence, or leveling.

Its Qur’anic forms are dakkan, dakkāʾa, fa-dukkatā, dakkatan, and dukkati, occurring in four verses. There are seven occurrences altogether: two passive verbs, three instances of the verbal noun dakk, one dakkah, and one dakkāʾ.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: seven occurrences in four verses

1. Semantic core and scope

Arabic dictionaries record two semantic directions for D K K:

First, lowering a prominence and leveling a surface. Something elevated, raised, or uneven subsides or becomes level.

Second, pounding, breaking, and demolishing. Impact or destructive force removes something’s cohesion and firmness.

Ibn Fāris treats these directions separately in Maqāyīs al-Lughah. For the first, he uses taṭāmun wa-insiṭāḥ, indicating lowering and spreading or flattening. For the second, he compares dakka with daqqa: pounding and crushing.

This distinction matters: not every use can be restricted to disintegration into tiny particles. Sometimes something is broken and pounded down; sometimes its prominence disappears; sometimes both aspects occur together.

Their proximity nevertheless does not prove that one historically developed from the other. The evidence establishes both uses within the family.

2. Concrete imagery and Arabic usage

Leveling ground

In Jamharat al-Lughah, Ibn Durayd records dakka al-arḍa for leveling the ground’s rises and hollows, including preparation for cultivation or other work.

Here dakk does not necessarily destroy the earth’s material. It changes the surface, reducing its unevenness.

Dakka al-turāba likewise concerns compacting, pounding, and leveling soil.

Jamharat al-Lughah: leveling ground for cultivation and other purposes

Pounding down a wall or mountain

Lisān al-ʿArab explains dakk of walls and mountains through breaking and demolition.

This is more than leveling a patch of ground: a firm structure or elevated mass breaks apart and collapses under force.

The distinction from generic “breaking” becomes clearer. Not every break is dakk. With a mountain or wall, collapse and loss of its elevated, firm shape are significant.

A humpless camel and a flattened prominence

Nāqatun dakkāʾu provides a clear lexical example.

Dakkāʾ describes a she-camel with no hump, or one whose hump has subsided so far that no obvious elevation remains on its back. The related masculine form is adakk.

Dakkāʾ therefore need not describe something broken by impact. The relevant feature can be an absent or subsiding prominence.

Akamatun dakkāʾu also denotes a broad-topped or low hill. Dakdāk describes pebbly or sandy ground whose material lies spread out without rising much.

Levelness in this family should therefore not always mean a perfectly smooth surface without the slightest rise or hollow. Reduced prominence, spreading, and subsiding also belong to its field.

Covering and filling

Some early uses record dakka al-rakiyyata for filling a well with earth. The root also describes heaping and compacting earth over a burial place.

This need not mean breaking the well. Filling a hollow changes its form, again showing the relationship with compacting, heaping, and leveling.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Maqāyīs: walls, mountains, camels and earth

A poetic witness

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī states:

فَأَبْقَى بَاطِلِي وَالْجِدُّ مِنْهَا
كَدُكَّانِ الدَّرَابِنَةِ الْمَطِينِ

fa-abqā bāṭilī wa-al-jiddu minhā / ka-dukkāni al-darābinati al-maṭīni

A sense translation is: “My riding for pleasure and serious business left of that she-camel a back like the doorkeepers’ clay-plastered platform.” Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not by itself establish this particular line’s composition date.

The remaining shape of the camel’s back is pictured through a clay-plastered platform. Dukkān here is a structure with an upper surface suitable for sitting or placing things.

Dictionaries differ, however, over dukkān’s derivation: some connect it with D K K, others with D K N. The line thus attests the word and its image in early poetry, rather than conclusively deriving it from D K K.

3. Qur’anic uses

Al-Aʿrāf 7:143 — dakkan

وَلَمَّا جَاءَ مُوسَىٰ لِمِيقَاتِنَا وَكَلَّمَهُ رَبُّهُ قَالَ رَبِّ أَرِنِي أَنظُرْ إِلَيْكَ ۚ قَالَ لَن تَرَانِي وَلَٰكِنِ انظُرْ إِلَى الْجَبَلِ فَإِنِ اسْتَقَرَّ مَكَانَهُ فَسَوْفَ تَرَانِي ۚ فَلَمَّا تَجَلَّىٰ رَبُّهُ لِلْجَبَلِ جَعَلَهُ دَكًّا وَخَرَّ مُوسَىٰ صَعِقًا ۚ فَلَمَّا أَفَاقَ قَالَ سُبْحَانَكَ تُبْتُ إِلَيْكَ وَأَنَا أَوَّلُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-lammā jāʾa mūsā li-mīqātinā wa-kallamahu rabbuhu qāla rabbi arinī anẓur ilayka qāla lan tarānī wa-lākini unẓur ilā al-jabali fa-ini istaqarra makānahu fa-sawfa tarānī fa-lammā tajallā rabbuhu li-al-jabali jaʿalahu dakkan wa-kharra mūsā ṣaʿiqan fa-lammā afāqa qāla subḥānaka tubtu ilayka wa-anā awwalu al-muʾminīna

Translation: When Moses came to Our appointed meeting and his Lord spoke to him, he said: “My Lord, show Yourself to me so that I may look at You.” He said: “You will never see Me, but look at the mountain. If it remains firm in its place, then you will see Me.” When his Lord manifested Himself to the mountain, He pounded it down, and Moses fell unconscious. When he recovered, he said: “Glory be to You! I turn back to You, and I am the first of the believers.”

Jaʿalahu dakkan concerns the mountain.

The mountain is initially a firm mass whose remaining stable in place is in question. Jaʿalahu dakkan then describes that same mountain’s transformation.

Here dakk suits the collapse of its firmness and raised structure.

Dakkan is a verbal noun used in jaʿalahu dakkan to express the resulting transformation: the mountain being pounded down or flattened.

The verse does not explain the physical mechanism. The word alone does not establish that the mountain necessarily became fine soil, dust, or particles of a particular size.

The principal point here is the loss of the mountain’s stability and elevated structure.

Al-Ṭabarī: the mountain’s dakk and differing readings

Al-Kahf 18:98 — dakkāʾa

قَالَ هَٰذَا رَحْمَةٌ مِّن رَّبِّي ۖ فَإِذَا جَاءَ وَعْدُ رَبِّي جَعَلَهُ دَكَّاءَ ۖ وَكَانَ وَعْدُ رَبِّي حَقًّا

qāla hādhā raḥmatun min rabbī fa-idhā jāʾa waʿdu rabbī jaʿalahu dakkāʾa wa-kāna waʿdu rabbī ḥaqqan

Translation: He said: “This is a mercy from my Lord. When my Lord’s promise comes, He will level it, and my Lord’s promise is true.”

Dhū al-Qarnayn says this after building the barrier mentioned in the preceding verses. The nearest contextual referent of -hu in jaʿalahu is that constructed barrier.

Dakkāʾ is a feminine adjective belonging to the adakk family, denoting something without an obvious prominence or whose prominence has subsided. An understood noun such as arḍan dakkāʾa, “level ground,” has also been proposed to explain its feminine form here. It should therefore not be treated merely as an adjective in overt agreement with masculine radm.

Dhū al-Qarnayn describes a time when the firm barrier will not withstand his Lord’s promise and will lose its elevated, obstructing form.

This differs from Al-Aʿrāf 7:143: there dakkan is a verbal noun, while here dakkāʾa describes the resulting state.

They belong to one semantic field but are morphologically different.

Established readings also vary between dakkan and dakkāʾa. In Ḥafṣ’s reading, Al-Aʿrāf 7:143 has dakkan and Al-Kahf 18:98 dakkāʾa. Other readings differ in one or both places.

This variation underscores attention to the actual form, although both readings connect contextually with collapse and the removal of elevation.

Al-Ṭabarī: the barrier, dakkāʾ and leveling

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:14 — fa-dukkatā dakkatan wāḥidatan

وَحُمِلَتِ الْأَرْضُ وَالْجِبَالُ فَدُكَّتَا دَكَّةً وَاحِدَةً

wa-ḥumilati al-arḍu wa-al-jibālu fa-dukkatā dakkatan wāḥidatan

Translation: The earth and mountains are lifted up, and both are crushed with a single crushing.

Fa-dukkatā is a feminine dual passive perfect, referring to al-arḍ wa-al-jibāl: the earth and the mountains as two groups.

The early grammarian Al-Farrāʾ likewise explains the dual: the earth constitutes one group, and the mountains the other.

Dakkatan is a noun of a single occurrence, one act of crushing, functioning here as a cognate accusative. Wāḥidatan explicitly reinforces its oneness.

Unlike uses describing only a level state, this verse foregrounds the crushing event. Earth and mountains undergo a tremendous transformation.

Both pounding and breaking, and the collapse of the earth’s and mountains’ previous form, suit the root’s semantic field here.

Dakkatan wāḥidatan explicitly states a single crushing; wāḥidah should not be set aside by supposing several independent crushings. The phrase nevertheless does not explain the physical mechanism, agent, or internal details of that single event.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: Al-Farrāʾ on the dual and shaking

Al-Fajr 89:21 — dukkati al-arḍu dakkan dakkan

كَلَّا إِذَا دُكَّتِ الْأَرْضُ دَكًّا دَكًّا

kallā idhā dukkati al-arḍu dakkan dakkan

Translation: No indeed! When the earth is pounded down, pounding after pounding.

Dukkati is a passive perfect, and dakkan the corresponding verbal noun.

Repeating dakkan dakkan emphasizes intensity, repetition, or comprehensiveness. Persian can convey it through repeated pounding down or being crushed severely and repeatedly.

The repeated verbal noun does not specify a particular number of stages or blows.

The earth itself is subjected to dakk, not merely a mountain, wall, or structure standing upon it.

The verse describes the transformation of the earth’s previous form and firmness. The precise manner and final outcome require examination of the verses describing that scene, rather than D K K alone.

Al-Ṭabarī: the earth being pounded down

4. Distinguishing important forms and constructions

The Qur’anic forms have several different roles.

Dakkan: the verbal noun of dakka. It expresses the mountain’s resulting transformation in Al-Aʿrāf 7:143, and its repetition emphasizes the earth being pounded down in Al-Fajr 89:21.

Dakkāʾa: an adjective belonging to adakk, describing the barrier’s resulting state in Al-Kahf 18:98. Its main contribution is the removal of elevation and leveling.

Dukkati and fa-dukkatā: passive forms of dakka, respectively feminine singular and feminine dual. The passive centers the crushing without explicitly naming an agent as the grammatical subject of that verb.

Dakkatan: one crushing event in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:14. It differs from dakkāʾa; superficial resemblance must not make them morphologically or syntactically equivalent.

Arabic also records adakk, dakkāʾ, dakdāk, indakka, and tadakdaka. Dakkāʾa does occur in the Qur’an, but the other forms in this list do not. Dictionary attestation does not make all of them Qur’anic forms.

5. Comparison with related roots

D K K (dakk) and D Q Q (daqq)

Daqq denotes pounding and crushing, sometimes reducing material to fine or soft particles.

Dakk can likewise involve pounding and breaking, but removing elevation, subsiding, and leveling have a distinctive role in many witnesses.

Ibn Fāris notes their proximity, but semantic resemblance or possible sound substitution does not establish complete historical identity.

Crushing a stone need not level the surface beneath it, for example, whereas a wall collapsing and losing its height can constitute dakk.

D K K (dakk) and S W Y (sawiya)

In uses concerning surface and shape, S W Y relates to levelness, proportion, and becoming equal.

D K K can denote an action lowering a prominence and leveling a surface.

Levelness may therefore result from dakk, but not every level surface results from pounding and collapse.

The roots can concern similar results in certain uses without having identical semantic fields.

Study of S W Y

D K K (dakk) and N S F (nasf)

Ṭā Hā 20:105 states that the Lord will subject the mountains to nasf. This root concerns uprooting and scattering.

Dakk centers pounding down, collapse, and reduced elevation; nasf can emphasize the uprooting and dispersal of material.

Both may describe a great transformation, but neither should replace the other completely without evidence.

D K K (dakk) and Z L Z L (zalzala)

Zalzalah denotes severe shaking and movement; dakk concerns pounding down, collapse, or leveling.

Shaking may cause collapse, but shaking and collapsing are lexically different.

Al-Farrāʾ connects dakk with shaking when explaining Al-Ḥāqqah 69:14. This helps illuminate the scene without identifying the roots’ definitions in every use.

6. Challenges in understanding and translation

A translation difficulty is choosing among pounding, shattering, collapsing, and leveling.

Rendering every dakk as merely “crushing” reduces the aspect of flattened prominence and leveling, especially in dakkāʾa.

Rendering every occurrence merely as “leveling” may fail to convey the force and collapse apparent in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:14 and Al-Fajr 89:21.

The root alone also does not establish complete annihilation leaving no material trace. A structure collapsing differs from all its constituent matter being destroyed.

Conversely, dakkāʾ should not extend indiscriminately to every slightly level surface. Its Qur’anic context concerns a firm barrier losing its elevated, obstructing form.

Each derivative’s appropriate meaning therefore depends on its actual form, the action’s object, and the sentence’s construction.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Pounding down and leveling” can convey dakk’s general field. Where breaking and loss of the previous form are prominent, “crushing” or “demolishing” is more suitable.

The following equivalents can be considered for the four Qur’anic verses:

  • Al-Aʿrāf 7:143, jaʿalahu dakkan: “He pounded it down” or “He demolished and leveled it.”
  • Al-Kahf 18:98, jaʿalahu dakkāʾa: “He will level it” or “He will bring it down and level it.”
  • Al-Ḥāqqah 69:14, fa-dukkatā dakkatan wāḥidatan: “Both are crushed with a single crushing.”
  • Al-Fajr 89:21, dukkati al-arḍu dakkan dakkan: “The earth is pounded down repeatedly.”

These equivalents should not be expanded through details such as becoming dust, disappearing entirely, or melting unless the verse’s other words and context supply that meaning.

8. Conclusion

Arabic D K K has two related semantic directions: pounding and demolishing, and lowering elevation and becoming level. They accompany one another in some uses, but not every derivative can be restricted to a single physical image.

In the Qur’an, the root describes a mountain collapsing, a barrier losing its firmness, and the earth and mountains being pounded down. Dakkan, dakkāʾa, dakkatan, and the passive verbs each express an aspect of this field through their particular constructions.

Brief root meaning: Pounding down and demolishing something, or lowering its elevation and leveling it.

Qur’anic dakk generally concerns the severe transformation of a firm or raised structure, its previous shape and height collapsing or disappearing. The degree of fragmentation, final surface, and physical mechanism cannot nevertheless always be determined from this root alone.
