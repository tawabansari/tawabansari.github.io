The Qur’anic Root Q M Ḥ (ق م ح)

Raising the head, being prevented from lowering it, and restricted head movement

Qur’anic forms

مُقْمَحُونَ

Muqmaḥūn

Their heads held up; 36:8

1. Basic meaning and linguistic background

Classical Arabic dictionaries record Q M Ḥ for a camel raising its head from water and ceasing to drink. Recording this usage in later sources does not by itself establish its precise date of origin before the Qur’an.

Lexicographers mention different causes, such as satiation, cold water, or illness. These explain situations in which the word is used; none is a necessary component of its meaning in every occurrence.

In classical Arabic, this family also describes someone whose head is held up by an obstruction around the neck, preventing it from being lowered easily.

The Qur’anic form examined: مُقْمَحُونَ (muqmaḥūn), those whose heads are forcibly held up.

Other recorded forms include قَمَحَ (qamaḥa), raise the head; قُمُوح (qumūḥ), raising the head and ceasing to drink; قَامِح (qāmiḥ), a camel that has raised its head from water; إِقْمَاح (iqmāḥ), holding the head up; and مُقْمَح (muqmaḥ), one whose head is held up. The last occurs in the Qur’an in the plural.

2. Physical imagery and dictionary accounts

Dictionaries illustrate this family with a camel raising its head when it stops drinking.

قَمَحَ and قَامَحَ are recorded in this use for raising the head and refraining from drinking. This lexical account must be kept distinct from interpreting human conduct in the Qur’anic verse.

Some lexical definitions combine a raised head with a lowered gaze. Head position and gaze direction differ: a raised head can accompany a downward gaze. Iqmāḥ should therefore not be understood simply as lowering the head.

Lexical sources report شَهْرَا قِمَاحٍ for two cold months, explaining the name through cold water and camels ceasing to drink. This explanation of a name does not independently establish when the word arose.

Another expression describes a neck shackle preventing someone from lowering their head:

أَقْمَحَهُ الْغُلُّ (aqmaḥahu l-ghull).

That is, the shackle held the person’s head up.

Unlike a camel that may stop drinking of its own accord, the head’s position here results from an external restraint. This sense is central to the Qur’anic occurrence.

Other semantic branches

قَمْح (qamḥ) also means wheat in Arabic. قَمِحَ (qamiḥa) and اِقْتَمَحَ (iqtamaḥa) are recorded for eating dry, flour-like food poured into the mouth without mixing it with water.

Some lexicographers connect pouring dry food into the mouth with raising the head. This semantic explanation does not establish a shared historical origin for the name of wheat and the head-raising branch.

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence concerns neither wheat nor eating.

3. The Qur’anic use of مُقْمَحُونَ

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Yā Sīn 36:8. Its meaning is examined first within the verse’s physical image, then within the surrounding verses.

إِنّا جَعَلنا فى أَعنٰقِهِم أَغلٰلًا فَهِىَ إِلَى الأَذقانِ فَهُم مُقمَحونَ

Innā jaʿalnā fī aʿnāqihim aghlālan fa-hiya ilā l-adhqāni fa-hum muqmaḥūn.

“Indeed, We have placed shackles around their necks, reaching to their chins, so their heads are forcibly held up.”

Ya-Sin 36:8

Three words from distinct roots contribute to this image:

أَعْنَاق (aʿnāq), from ʿ N Q, means necks; أَغْلَال (aghlāl), from GH L L, means shackles or restraining bonds; and أَذْقَان (adhqān), from DH Q N, means chins.

Shackles encircling the neck and reaching the chin prevent the head from being lowered naturally. Muqmaḥūn describes the resulting condition.

In this image, a raised head does not signify freedom, dignity, or ability; it results from restricted movement.

Morphology and syntax

مُقْمَحُونَ is the sound masculine plural of مُقْمَح, the passive participle of form IV أَقْمَحَ (aqmaḥa).

The distinction between قَامِح (qāmiḥ) and مُقْمَح (muqmaḥ) matters here: the former describes a person or animal raising its head; the latter describes someone whose head has been held up.

In فَهُمْ مُقْمَحُونَ, hum is the subject and muqmaḥūn its nominative predicate, marked by the wāw of the sound masculine plural. Fa links the resulting condition to the shackles and their extension to the chins.

The feminine singular pronoun هِيَ in the preceding clause naturally refers to أَغْلَال: Arabic can refer to a nonhuman plural with a feminine singular pronoun.

Some lexical and exegetical explanations take the pronoun to refer to hands bound to the neck. This fits certain physical uses of shackles, but the verse does not name hands. Referring the pronoun to the shackles in direct translation follows the verse’s explicit wording more closely.

4. Iqmāḥ in the context of Yā Sīn

The preceding verse states:

لَقَد حَقَّ القَولُ عَلىٰ أَكثَرِهِم فَهُم لا يُؤمِنونَ

La-qad ḥaqqa l-qawlu ʿalā aktharihim fa-hum lā yuʾminūn.

“The word has indeed come true against most of them, so they do not believe.”

Ya-Sin 36:7

After the shackles and heads held up, the Qur’an presents another image:

وَجَعَلنا مِن بَينِ أَيديهِم سَدًّا وَمِن خَلفِهِم سَدًّا فَأَغشَينٰهُم فَهُم لا يُبصِرونَ

Wa-jaʿalnā min bayni aydīhim saddan wa-min khalfihim saddan fa-aghshaynāhum fa-hum lā yubṣirūn.

“And We placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them, and We covered them, so they do not see.”

Ya-Sin 36:9

The next verse clarifies the relationship of these images to divine warning:

وَسَواءٌ عَلَيهِم ءَأَنذَرتَهُم أَم لَم تُنذِرهُم لا يُؤمِنونَ

Wa-sawāʾun ʿalayhim a-andhartahum am lam tundhirhum lā yuʾminūn.

“It is the same for them whether you warn them or do not warn them: they do not believe.”

Ya-Sin 36:10

Shackles, heads held up, barriers ahead and behind, and covering form a set of images of restriction. In context, they concern people who receive warning but do not respond to it.

As an illustration, restricted head movement can be compared with a restricted ability to reconsider beliefs. This comparison is interpretation: the verse does not explicitly name prejudice or mental attachment here.

Prejudice, arrogance, and unresponsiveness to truth are not independent lexical meanings of iqmāḥ. Nor can every refusal or question be classified as an instance of this image; an interpretive judgment requires context and evidence.

Neck shackles also occur in descriptions of punishment in the Hereafter, including Ghāfir 40:71. This supports a reading concerning the deniers’ eventual fate. By contrast, the connection of Yā Sīn 36:7, Yā Sīn 36:8, Yā Sīn 36:9, and Yā Sīn 36:10 with unbelief and ineffective warning supports a figurative reading. Choosing between these readings involves contextual analysis, not the root’s historical meaning alone.

In either reading, the lexical meaning of muqmaḥūn remains: people whose heads are held up and whose natural head movement is restricted.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

Q M Ḥ and GH L L: the head’s condition and the restraining instrument

These roots occur together in Yā Sīn 36:8.

Ghull names an instrument used to bind and restrict movement; iqmāḥ describes the particular head position resulting from such restriction.

Not every shackle necessarily holds the head up. In this verse, the shackles’ extension to the chins produces iqmāḥ.

“Those in bonds” therefore does not convey the whole meaning of muqmaḥūn: being bound has already been expressed by aghlāl.

Q M Ḥ and R F ʿ: holding the head up and raising

Rafʿ has a broad range, including raising objects, elevating status, removing something, and other uses.

Iqmāḥ in the branch examined here specifically concerns the head’s position.

Someone may raise their head to look at the sky without being muqmaḥ. In Yā Sīn 36:8, the head is held up because of a restraint.

“Those with uplifted heads” alone is therefore insufficient: it may suggest dignity, pride, or a voluntary raising of the head.

Q M Ḥ and N K S: heads held up and heads bowed

Al-Sajdah describes the wrongdoers through a different image:

وَلَو تَرىٰ إِذِ المُجرِمونَ ناكِسوا رُءوسِهِم عِندَ رَبِّهِم رَبَّنا أَبصَرنا وَسَمِعنا فَارجِعنا نَعمَل صٰلِحًا إِنّا موقِنونَ

Wa-law tarā idhi l-mujrimūna nākisū ruʾūsihim ʿinda rabbihim rabbanā abṣarnā wa-samiʿnā fa-rjiʿnā naʿmal ṣāliḥan innā mūqinūn.

“If only you could see when the guilty bow their heads before their Lord: Our Lord, we have seen and heard, so send us back; we will do righteous deeds. Indeed, we are now certain.”

As-Sajdah 32:12

نَاكِسُو رُءُوسِهِمْ describes those bowing their heads, whereas muqmaḥūn describes those whose heads are held up.

In Al-Sajdah, lowered heads accompany acknowledgment and regret. In Yā Sīn, heads held up accompany restricted movement and, within the surrounding context, unresponsiveness to warning.

These images show that head position alone does not determine moral worth. A raised head does not always mean dignity or arrogance, nor a lowered head commendable humility. The ethical significance comes from context.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

For مُقْمَحُونَ in Yā Sīn 36:8, the proposed rendering is “their heads are forcibly held up.”

“Their heads remain raised” is close, but makes the restraint and the passive participle less clear.

“Arrogant,” “haughty,” or “rebellious” may reflect a figurative interpretation, but would remove the word’s central image and its relationship to the neck shackles.

“Those with bowed heads” is also inaccurate. Some lexical explanations describe someone whose gaze is lowered while the head remains raised. Lowering the gaze is not the same as lowering the head.

7. Conclusion

One principal branch of Q M Ḥ concerns raising the head, especially of camels ceasing to drink water.

In iqmāḥ, the family also describes holding the head up through a restraint.

Muqmaḥūn describes people whose neck shackles reach their chins and prevent their heads from being lowered naturally. In the context of Yā Sīn, this image is connected with restricted sight and unresponsiveness to divine warning.

Wheat and eating dry food are other recorded meanings in this lexical family, but they are absent from its Qur’anic occurrence.

Proposed definition for Forqan: Raising the head, especially when ceasing to drink; in the iqmāḥ branch, holding the head up and preventing its natural downward movement.
