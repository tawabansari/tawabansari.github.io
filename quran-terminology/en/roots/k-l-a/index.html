Qur’anic root ك ل أ

Watching over, guarding, and keeping safe from danger

Qur’anic forms

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root ك ل أ concerns watching over, guarding, keeping watch, and protecting someone or something against danger.

Ibn Faris identifies watchfulness and attentive observation as fundamental to this branch: a person entrusted with another’s protection watches over them and remains alert to possible dangers.

A verse attributed to Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr, a poet who lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

أَنَخْتُ بَعِيرِي وَاكْتَلَأْتُ بِعَيْنِهِ وَآمَرْتُ نَفْسِي أَيَّ أَمْرَيَّ أَفْعَلُ

I made my camel kneel and kept watch by its eye; I consulted myself about which of my two courses to take.

اِكْتَلَأْتُ here concerns caution, watchfulness, and alertness to possible danger.

Early dictionaries preserve variant wordings of this verse. Since Kaʿb also lived in the Islamic period and the date of composition has not been independently established, it cannot serve as conclusive evidence of pre-Qur’anic usage.

The following expressions are also recorded in classical Arabic:

  • كَلَأَ الْقَوْمَ: he guarded the people.
  • كَلَأَهُ مِنَ الْخَطَرِ: he kept him safe from danger.
  • كَلَأَكَ اللَّهُ: may The God protect you.
  • اِكْتَلَأَ مِنْهُ: he took precautions against him.
  • عَيْنٌ كَلُوءٌ: a wakeful, watchful eye.
  • فِي كِلَاءَةِ اللَّهِ: in The God’s protection and care.

In another branch, كَلَأ denotes vegetation eaten by animals, whether green or dry. This word was significant in Arab pastoral life.

Vegetation and fodder are not the same sense as guarding. Ibn Faris likewise treats these as distinct semantic principles.

The evidence considered here does not establish an independently datable pre-Islamic example of the guarding sense. The Qur’an itself and evidence from Arabic close to its period nevertheless show that watchfulness and protection were recognized meanings of this family.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms in Arabic include:

  • كَلَأَ ـ يَكْلَؤُ: to watch over, guard, and keep safe.
  • كَلْء: keeping and protection.
  • كِلَاءَة: watchfulness, guarding, and protection.
  • كَالِئ: a guard or protector.
  • مَكْلُوء: someone or something under protection.
  • كَلُوء: wakeful and watchful, especially of an eye.
  • اِكْتَلَأَ: to keep watch and take precautions.
  • اِكْتِلَاء: watchfulness and precaution against danger.
  • كَالَأَ: to watch over or observe.
  • مُكَالَأَة: watching over or keeping under observation.
  • كَلَّاء: a harbor or shelter for ships.
  • مَكْلَأ: a sheltered place or refuge for ships.

The vegetation and grazing branch also includes:

  • كَلَأ: fodder and vegetation grazed by animals.
  • أَكْلَأَتِ الْأَرْضُ: the land became abundant in grazing vegetation.
  • أَرْضٌ مُكْلِئَةٌ: land abundant in fodder.

In some early commercial usages, كَالِئ also denotes a debt or obligation deferred to a later date. This belongs to the sense of postponement, and is distinct from “guard.”

The form examined in the Qur’an:

يَكْلَؤُكُمْ

The family’s only Qur’anic occurrence is the imperfect verb يَكْلَؤُ with the attached pronoun كُمْ in al-Anbiya 21:42.

3. Core meaning and concrete image

In the branch represented by the Qur’anic occurrence, the core meaning is watching over someone or something to keep them safe from danger and harm.

Imagine a caravan stopping to rest at night. While others sleep, someone stays awake and watches the surroundings, ready to warn them of approaching danger or prevent harm from reaching them.

This behavior fits the meaning of كِلَاءَة.

Guarding here is not merely a response to danger that has already occurred. It can begin before danger arises and prevent it from materializing.

The use of كَلَّاء for a shelter for ships is also connected with this idea. A ship is better protected there from strong winds and surrounding hazards.

Nevertheless, كِلَاءَة is not restricted to night watch or bodily protection. It can apply whenever someone undertakes to watch over and keep another safe.

The word itself does not promise absolute immunity or permanently guaranteed safety. The extent and quality of protection depend on the protector’s ability and the circumstances.

4. Qur’anic usage

يَكْلَؤُكُمْ: who keeps you safe by night and day?

This verse directs human attention to the limits of human ability and dependence on The God.

People face many dangers throughout day and night. Some dangers are removed before reaching them, perhaps without their ever becoming aware of them.

People use various means to protect their lives: building homes, safeguarding possessions, seeking treatment for illness, and drawing on knowledge and cooperation.

None of these resources, however, grants unlimited power to place oneself outside The God’s authority and sovereignty.

The verse challenges the belief that human beings can possess an independent, absolute refuge against divine will and punishment.

The meaning of مِنَ الرَّحْمَٰنِ

The verse does not ask who protects people from the dangers of night and day. It asks who protects them by night and day from the Entirely Merciful.

The surrounding verses are essential to understanding this expression.

Earlier verses in al-Anbiya discuss people who challenged the fulfillment of the divine promise and mocked The God’s Messengers.

Al-Anbiya 21:39 describes their inability to ward fire off their faces and backs. Al-Anbiya 21:41 recalls the fate of those who mocked earlier Messengers.

The verse immediately following يَكْلَؤُكُمْ states:

This clarifies the preceding question. Those who turn away from remembrance of their Lord cannot escape divine power and punishment by seeking refuge in other deities.

Thus مِنَ الرَّحْمَٰنِ in al-Anbiya 21:42 occurs in the context of being protected against divine punishment and command.

Some early explanations also understand مِنْ here in a substitutive sense: who guards you in place of the Entirely Merciful?

Although this reading also accords with human dependence on The God, the context of punishment and other deities’ inability to prevent it supports the first interpretation.

The choice of الرَّحْمَٰن in this context deserves attention. The very Lord whose mercy and blessings sustain human beings possesses the power against which no other being can have absolute independence.

Night and day: the span of protection

Al-Anbiya 21:42 uses the expression بِاللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ.

Night and day together encompass the whole span of human life.

At night, while asleep and unaware of much that happens nearby, a person still needs protection. By day, despite being awake, active, and using their abilities, they remain exposed to life’s dangers and limitations.

Mentioning night and day therefore emphasizes the continuing human need for care and protection.

This temporal comprehensiveness comes from بِاللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ, not from the inherent meaning of يَكْلَؤُ.

Security and human heedlessness

The same passage continues:

Long enjoyment of life’s resources can lead people to imagine that their security and power are permanent.

Someone who has enjoyed health, wealth, or power for years may attribute these resources to independent personal ability and forget their limitations.

Al-Anbiya 21:42 describes turning away from remembrance of the Lord, while al-Anbiya 21:44 addresses prolonged enjoyment and imagined supremacy.

Being granted time and resources does not mean permanent immunity from the consequences of one’s actions.

5. Connections with other Qur’anic verses

No independent force against the Entirely Merciful

This verse is closely related in meaning to al-Anbiya 21:42.

Al-Anbiya asks who can protect a person against the Entirely Merciful; al-Mulk asks what force can come to their aid against Him.

One concerns watchfulness and protection, the other assistance and defensive power.

Both reject the existence of an independent power capable of placing people outside The God’s sovereignty.

Human protection and responsibility

Al-Raʿd 13:11 discusses protection of human beings and the principle governing change in a people’s condition. It also states that when The God wills harm for a people, nothing can turn it back.

This theme connects with al-Anbiya 21:42.

Dependence on divine protection does not remove human responsibility for life. The Qur’an also connects a change in people’s condition with change in what is within themselves.

In the story of Joseph, Jacob says when considering entrusting his other son to the brothers:

Jacob speaks both of his previous experience and possible danger, while recognizing The God as the best guardian.

In Yusuf 12:66 and Yusuf 12:67, he also takes precautions to protect his son.

Care, caution, and human resources are therefore compatible with trust in divine protection.

6. Morphology and syntax

يَكْلَؤُكُمْ is a third-person masculine singular active imperfect verb from the basic triliteral form كَلَأَ.

It is a hamzated verb because the root’s final consonant is hamza.

Classical Arabic also records forms with a lightened hamza. These dialectal forms do not establish that the Qur’anic word belongs to a different root.

In al-Anbiya 21:42, مَنْ is interrogative; the implicit subject pronoun in يَكْلَؤُ refers back to it.

كُمْ is the attached second-person plural object pronoun, identifying those being protected.

بِاللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ specifies the time of guarding; مِنَ الرَّحْمَٰنِ identifies the one against whom the question asks about protection.

مَنْ يَكْلَؤُكُمْ does not seek the name of an unknown guard. It is a rhetorical question exposing the inability of any other power to stand against The God.

The first part directly addresses the audience with كُمْ; the second refers to them in the third person with هُمْ.

This shift moves from direct questioning to describing their conduct: turning away from remembrance of the Lord.

7. Comparison with related roots

ك ل أ compared with ح ف ظ

حِفْظ concerns keeping, care, and preventing loss, forgetting, or damage.

كِلَاءَة is close to it in the sense of protection and guarding.

حِفْظ nevertheless has a wider range: retaining speech in memory, preserving a book, observing divine limits, and keeping a covenant all belong to this family’s uses.

كِلَاءَة more particularly emphasizes watching over someone or something against danger.

Yusuf 12:64 identifies The God as the best guardian. Al-Anbiya 21:42 presents others’ inability to protect people against divine command and punishment.

ك ل أ compared with ح ر س

حِرَاسَة and كِلَاءَة are very close lexically; Arabic lexicographers use the former to define the latter.

Here حَرَس denotes a body of guards.

By contrast, يَكْلَؤُكُمْ in al-Anbiya 21:42 is a verb describing the act of guarding and keeping safe.

These families overlap extensively in protection; an absolute boundary cannot be drawn between them.

ك ل أ compared with ر ق ب

ر ق ب concerns observation, keeping watch, and waiting.

Someone observing another may seek to understand their behavior, await an event, or prevent danger.

Not every act of observation aims to protect the person observed.

For example, someone may monitor an opponent’s behavior without intending to protect them.

Keeping and safeguarding are more prominent in كِلَاءَة.

ك ل أ compared with ر ع ي

رِعَايَة concerns care, attention, keeping, and attending to what is entrusted to a person.

A shepherd caring for a flock does more than prevent danger: they also seek grazing land and meet the animals’ needs.

رِعَايَة can encompass this wider responsibility.

كِلَاءَة is more particularly concerned with guarding and keeping safe.

Care for trusts and covenants appears in al-Mu’minun 23:8 and al-Maʿarij 70:32. The sole Qur’anic occurrence of ك ل أ concerns protection against danger.

ك ل أ compared with و ق ي

وِقَايَة concerns preventing harm and keeping someone or something safe from danger.

Both كِلَاءَة and وِقَايَة can denote protection.

Watchfulness and guarding are prominent in كِلَاءَة; preventing harm is prominent in وِقَايَة.

A person watching over another may anticipate danger and provide suitable means to prevent injury. Both concepts can apply in such circumstances.

ك ل أ compared with م ن ع

مَنْع concerns restraining or preventing something from happening.

كِلَاءَة may also be accomplished by preventing danger from reaching someone.

Al-Anbiya 21:42 speaks of keeping people safe; al-Anbiya 21:43 asks about deities supposedly able to protect them against The God.

In these verses, كِلَاءَة emphasizes protection, while مَنْع emphasizes restraining and preventing.

In al-Anbiya 21:42, “keep safe” or “protect” suits يَكْلَؤُكُمْ.

“Observe” alone is insufficient, because it does not fully convey protection and prevention of harm.

Conclusion

ك ل أ has distinct semantic branches in Arabic, notably watchfulness and guarding, and vegetation suitable for grazing.

The Qur’an employs this family in the sense of watching over and keeping safe.

In al-Anbiya 21:42, it asks who can keep people safe by night and day against the Entirely Merciful.

The context shows that despite power, knowledge, wealth, and protective resources, people cannot secure an independent refuge from The God.

The core of this Qur’anic usage is watching over and guarding someone to keep them safe from danger and harm.
