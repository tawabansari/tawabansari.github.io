Root ف ر غ — Becoming empty, finishing a task, pouring out, and turning to another matter

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(fārighan)

(afrigh / ufrigh)

The three-consonant Arabic root ف ر غ concerns becoming empty, remaining vacant, and finishing an occupation. فَرَغَ is used when something loses its contents, a person finishes a task, or someone becomes free from an occupation and has an opportunity to attend to another matter.

Within this family, أَفْرَغَ means emptying a vessel by pouring out its contents. Material uses concern pouring water or molten metal; figuratively, the verb can express pouring patience and steadfastness upon someone.

The root occurs six times in six Qur’anic verses, in three morphological forms: the basic verb فَرَغَ, the Form IV verb أَفْرَغَ, and the active participle فَارِغ.

These uses range from the troubled heart of Moses’ mother and the completion of a task to requests for patience, pouring molten metal, and a warning concerning the reckoning of humans and jinn.

Farāgh does not always mean idleness or ease. A person’s mind may be emptied of everything except one concern; someone may finish one task and become ready for another responsibility; or a vessel’s contents may be poured onto something.

The family’s central meaning concerns becoming empty, or emptying something, of what it contained or what occupied it. Related constructions express finishing an occupation, pouring out contents, and attending to another matter.

1. The root in early Arabic

Becoming empty and being vacant

In established Arabic usage, فَرَغَ الْإِنَاءُ describes a vessel whose contents have been emptied.

إِنَاءٌ فَارِغٌ means a vessel containing nothing.

فَرَغَ الْمَكَانُ likewise refers to a place becoming empty.

The underlying idea is the absence of something previously present, or expected to be present, in the vessel or place.

Emptiness does not necessarily mean the contents have been destroyed. Water poured out of a jug may collect in another vessel.

Emptying something must therefore be distinguished from the absolute destruction of its contents.

Finishing an occupation

In another construction, فَرَغَ مِنَ الْعَمَلِ means becoming free from a task or completing it.

The person has not necessarily become idle; their involvement in that particular task has ended.

For example, a carpenter may finish making a table and then begin making something else.

The first task is finished, but this does not mean all activity has ceased.

Similarly, completing a duty may give someone an opportunity to attend to another matter.

Thus تَفَرَّغَ لِلْأَمْرِ is known in the sense of devoting one’s time and attention to a task.

In one construction, farāgh can express the end of a previous occupation; in another, it can express the availability of time and attention for a new task.

Openness and breadth

Early lexical sources record another branch concerning breadth and spaciousness.

طَرِيقٌ فَرِيغٌ describes a broad road.

In some uses, فَرَسٌ فَرِيغٌ describes a horse with a wide stride and flowing movement.

طَعْنَةٌ فَرْغَاءُ and ضَرْبَةٌ فَرِيغَةٌ are also recorded for the breadth or openness of a wound caused by a thrust or blow.

These uses show that the semantic field is not confined to the absence of contents; it also concerns open space and room for movement.

Nevertheless, this relationship does not establish that every branch necessarily developed from one particular material image.

Evidence from early poetry

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

وَنَحَتْ لَهُ عَنْ أَرْزِ تَالِئَةٍ
فِلْقٍ فِرَاغٍ مَعَابِلِ طُحْلِ

Wa-naḥat lahu ʿan arzi tāliʾatin / filqin firāghin maʿābili ṭuḥli

The verse describes archery and its equipment. Lexical explanations relate فِرَاغ to broad arrowheads or the breadth of the bow.

The sense here is breadth and openness, rather than freedom from work.

The verse is preserved in the collection attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and in early lexical citations. Some readings differ, as does the identification of what فِرَاغ describes: the bow or broad arrowheads.

It therefore supports the branch concerning breadth, but does not directly establish the senses of emptying a vessel or finishing work.

Emptying a vessel and pouring its contents

Early lexical sources give أَفْرَغَ الْمَاءَ in the sense of pouring water from a vessel.

أَفْرَغَ الدَّلْوَ means pouring out the bucket’s water.

فَرْغُ الدَّلْوِ also denotes the part of a bucket through which water flows out.

فِرَاغ is recorded as a name for a vessel; فِرَاغُ الدَّلْوِ denotes the side from which its water is poured. The specific term for a broad vessel is الفَرْغَان.

In metalworking, أَفْرَغَ الذَّهَبَ and أَفْرَغَ الْفِضَّةَ were used for pouring molten gold and silver.

These uses show that ifrāgh is not merely emptying: in many constructions, pouring and causing the contents to flow are prominent.

Their inclusion in dictionaries compiled in the Islamic period does not, by itself, establish the precise pre-Islamic date of every example.

The Qur’anic أُفْرِغْ عَلَيْهِ قِطْرًا directly attests that the pouring sense was known in the Arabic of the revelation period.

2. Linguistic forms and the central meaning

The principal established forms include:

  • فَرَغَ: became empty; became free from a task or finished it.
  • فَرَاغ: emptiness, leisure, or absence of occupation.
  • فُرُوغ: another verbal noun for becoming free from an occupation.
  • فَارِغ: empty, vacant, or unoccupied.
  • فَرَّغَ: emptied something of its contents.
  • تَفْرِيغ: emptying; in other uses, freeing time and attention.
  • أَفْرَغَ: poured out contents or poured them onto something.
  • إِفْرَاغ: pouring and emptying contents.
  • تَفَرَّغَ: became free from an occupation, or prepared and devoted oneself to a task.
  • اِسْتَفْرَغَ: emptied something completely; in استفرغ جهده, expended all one’s effort.
  • مُفْرَغ: emptied, or cast by pouring material into a mould.
  • فَرِيغ: broad or spacious; sometimes describing a road, movement, or wound.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The basic verb occurs twice, the Form IV verb three times, and the active participle once.

The basic verb appears in سَنَفْرُغُ and فَرَغْتَ.

Form IV appears in أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا and أُفْرِغْ عَلَيْهِ قِطْرًا.

The active participle appears as فَارِغًا, describing the heart of Moses’ mother.

These three branches differ in structure and use.

فَرَغَ concerns becoming empty or finishing an occupation.

أَفْرَغَ foregrounds pouring out and causing contents to flow.

فَارِغ describes the condition of something empty or vacant.

3. فَارِغًا: the heart of Moses’ mother after separation from her child

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:10

وَأَصْبَحَ فُؤَادُ أُمِّ مُوسَىٰ فَارِغًا ۖ إِنْ كَادَتْ لَتُبْدِي بِهِ لَوْلَا أَنْ رَبَطْنَا عَلَىٰ قَلْبِهَا لِتَكُونَ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

Wa-aṣbaḥa fuʾādu ummi Mūsā fārighan in kādat latubdī bihi lawlā an rabaṭnā ʿalā qalbihā litakūna mina l-muʾminīn

Translation: “And the heart of Moses’ mother became empty. She nearly disclosed him, had We not strengthened her heart so that she might be among the believers.”

This verse describes Moses’ mother after she placed her child in the water.

In al-Qaṣaṣ 28:7, God had instructed her to nurse Moses and, when she feared for his life, to place him in the water. She was also promised that her child would be returned.

Placing a newborn in the water was hardly easy, especially while Pharaoh was killing the sons of the Children of Israel.

Despite receiving the divine promise, Moses’ mother faced a difficult situation.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:10 describes her inner state as follows:

«وَأَصْبَحَ فُؤَادُ أُمِّ مُوسَىٰ فَارِغًا»

Wa-aṣbaḥa fuʾādu ummi Mūsā fārighan

Of what had her heart become empty?

فَارِغًا basically means empty or vacant.

When applied to a human heart or fuʾād, however, the circumstances of that emptiness must be considered.

Several interpretations are possible here.

Her heart may have been emptied of composure and endurance: the pressure of anxiety brought her close to revealing the secret.

Alternatively, her mind and heart may have lost attention to everything else, with all her concern directed towards Moses.

These readings are compatible. Intense worry about a loved one can both prevent attention to other matters and destroy one’s composure.

For example, a mother whose child has disappeared may still have a home, work, and other responsibilities, while all her thought and feeling are directed towards the child.

In that situation, an “empty heart” does not mean absence of feeling. On the contrary, concern can become so intense that it leaves no room for anything else.

This image fits the situation of Moses’ mother.

Does فَارِغًا mean emptied of faith?

The verse does not explicitly say this.

It continues:

«لَوْلَا أَنْ رَبَطْنَا عَلَىٰ قَلْبِهَا»

Lawlā an rabaṭnā ʿalā qalbihā

That is, “had We not strengthened her heart.”

This shows that Moses’ mother needed inner firmness and reinforcement.

Needing support in a difficult situation does not itself mean that a person’s faith has been destroyed.

Someone may believe God’s promise while fearing a real danger to their child.

In this story, God had told Moses’ mother not to fear or grieve; nevertheless, the Qur’an shows her experiencing profound human anxiety.

Faith must therefore not be defined as the absence of every feeling of fear, grief, or concern.

The difference between فُؤَاد and قَلْب

Two words occur in the same verse:

فُؤَادُ أُمِّ مُوسَىٰ

and

قَلْبِهَا

This distinction matters when examining the sentence.

The first part describes her fuʾād as empty and troubled.

The second speaks of strengthening her qalb.

A relationship can be seen between disturbed inner experience and restored steadiness.

These words alone do not establish that the Qur’an is identifying two physical organs or two independent centres of consciousness.

In Qur’anic usage, both fuʾād and qalb concern inner life, perception, and response. Their precise differences require separate studies of their respective roots.

The outcome of a strengthened heart

In al-Qaṣaṣ 28:11, Moses’ mother asks his sister to follow him.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:13 then describes his return, so that his mother might be comforted, cease grieving, and know that God’s promise is true.

This connection shows that فَارِغًا in al-Qaṣaṣ 28:10 describes a difficult condition preceding reassurance and the fulfilment of the promise.

For the direct translation, “her heart became empty” preserves the lexical image. Distress and being emptied of attention to other matters belong in the contextual explanation, rather than being treated as independent meanings of every use of فَارِغ.

4. أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا: pouring out patience and steadfastness

This expression occurs twice in the Qur’an.

In both cases, people face a serious threat and ask God for patience.

A. Al-Baqarah 2:250: patience when facing Goliath’s army

Al-Baqarah 2:250

وَلَمَّا بَرَزُوا لِجَالُوتَ وَجُنُودِهِ قَالُوا رَبَّنَا أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا وَانْصُرْنَا عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْكَافِرِينَ

Wa-lammā barazū li-Jālūta wa-junūdihi qālū rabbanā afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabran wa-thabbit aqdāmanā wa-nṣurnā ʿalā l-qawmi l-kāfirīn

Translation: “When they came out against Goliath and his troops, they said: Our Lord, pour patience upon us, make our feet firm, and help us against the disbelieving people.”

A group of believers facing the enemy asks God for three things:

Patience, firm footing, and help.

The requests are related but not identical.

Patience concerns steadfastness and restraining oneself from collapsing under pressure.

ثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا concerns remaining firm in the confrontation.

وَانْصُرْنَا is a request for help and victory.

Here أَفْرِغْ evokes pouring something upon them.

The verb draws on pouring a vessel’s contents, but its object is patience, which is not a material liquid such as water or molten metal.

The Qur’an thus uses a linguistic image to express a request for abundant patience.

Patience is imagined as something poured over people and encompassing them.

This should not be understood as a physical vessel of patience or an invisible liquid flowing over human bodies.

B. Al-Aʿrāf 7:126: patience before Pharaoh’s threat

Al-Aʿrāf 7:126

وَمَا تَنْقِمُ مِنَّا إِلَّا أَنْ آمَنَّا بِآيَاتِ رَبِّنَا لَمَّا جَاءَتْنَا ۚ رَبَّنَا أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا وَتَوَفَّنَا مُسْلِمِينَ

Wa-mā tanqimu minnā illā an āmannā bi-āyāti rabbinā lammā jāʾatnā rabbanā afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabran wa-tawaffanā muslimīn

Translation: “You resent us only because we believed in our Lord’s signs when they came to us. Our Lord, pour patience upon us and take us in death as ones who submit.”

These are the words of people who believed in God after recognising Moses’ signs.

In al-Aʿrāf 7:124, Pharaoh threatens them with severe punishment.

Their answer in al-Aʿrāf 7:126 shows that they recognise the danger but do not abandon their faith.

They ask God:

«أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا»

Afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabran

The same expression appears in al-Baqarah 2:250.

The two groups’ circumstances differ, however.

In al-Baqarah, the request comes during military confrontation, alongside requests for firm footing and help against the enemy.

In al-Aʿrāf, it comes before the threat of torture and death, accompanied by a request to die in submission.

This shows that Qur’anic patience is not confined either to fighting or to enduring suffering.

People may need patience to stand against an enemy, preserve faith, resist submitting to injustice, or endure a grave threat.

Why أَفْرِغْ rather than simply أَعْطِ?

أَعْطِ can express a request to give something.

أَفْرِغْ foregrounds the image of pouring and supplying abundantly.

Rather than merely asking for a little patience, these supplicants use the image of pouring to request ample patience.

This comes from the verb’s construction and the familiar image of pouring.

It does not establish a numerical quantity or infinite patience.

Nor does patience here mean silence before injustice. Both groups stand against a threat and ask God for strength to persevere.

5. أُفْرِغْ عَلَيْهِ قِطْرًا: pouring molten metal

Al-Kahf 18:96

آتُونِي زُبَرَ الْحَدِيدِ ۖ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا سَاوَىٰ بَيْنَ الصَّدَفَيْنِ قَالَ انْفُخُوا ۖ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَعَلَهُ نَارًا قَالَ آتُونِي أُفْرِغْ عَلَيْهِ قِطْرًا

Ātūnī zubara l-ḥadīdi ḥattā idhā sāwā bayna ṣ-ṣadafayni qāla unfukhū ḥattā idhā jaʿalahu nāran qāla ātūnī ufrigh ʿalayhi qiṭrā

Translation: “Bring me pieces of iron.” When he had levelled the gap between the two mountainsides, he said: “Blow.” When he had made it fiery, he said: “Bring it to me, so that I may pour molten copper over it.”

The verse concerns Dhū al-Qarnayn’s construction of a strong barrier.

أُفْرِغْ has a clear material use here.

It describes pouring molten material over a structure made of iron pieces.

قِطْرًا here denotes molten copper.

أُفْرِغْ عَلَيْهِ قِطْرًا therefore means “I may pour molten copper over it.”

Distinguishing أَفْرِغْ from أُفْرِغْ

The difference in vocalisation matters.

In al-Baqarah 2:250 and al-Aʿrāf 7:126, أَفْرِغْ is an imperative.

The speakers ask God to pour patience upon them.

In al-Kahf 18:96, أُفْرِغْ begins with an u-vowel and is a first-person singular imperfect verb.

It means “I may pour,” or “so that I may pour over it.”

Although both forms share a root and derived stem, their grammatical roles differ.

In the two prayers, God is addressed with the request.

In al-Kahf, Dhū al-Qarnayn asks his companions to bring the necessary materials so that he can perform the pouring.

Material and figurative ifrāgh

These three verses clarify the relationship between material and figurative meanings.

In al-Kahf 18:96, an actual substance is poured over a structure.

In al-Baqarah 2:250 and al-Aʿrāf 7:126, patience is poured upon people.

All three evoke pouring and conveying something onto a destination.

The verb’s object differs, however.

Molten copper is physical material; patience is a human capacity and state of steadfastness.

The material use in al-Kahf does not imply that patience must also be a physical substance.

Conversely, the clear pouring sense in al-Kahf should not be replaced with an abstraction such as inspiration, power, or guidance.

Each use takes its meaning from its context.

Does ifrāgh mean filling?

أَفْرَغَ fundamentally concerns emptying and pouring out contents.

In a construction such as أفرغ عليه, attention falls on the action directed from a vessel or source towards a destination.

When water is poured from a jug onto the ground, the jug empties and the water reaches the ground.

In al-Kahf, the prominent action is likewise pouring molten copper over the structure.

“Pouring” is therefore more precise than “filling.”

If the poured material eventually covers something or fills a gap, that is an outcome of the action, not the verb’s fixed meaning in every construction.

6. سَنَفْرُغُ لَكُمْ: attending to the reckoning of humans and jinn

Al-Raḥmān 55:31

سَنَفْرُغُ لَكُمْ أَيُّهَ الثَّقَلَانِ

Sanafrughu lakum ayyuha th-thaqalān

Translation: “We shall attend to you, O two weighty communities.”

This is an important and distinctive use of ف ر غ.

سَنَفْرُغُ is a Form I imperfect verb prefixed by سَـ, which points to the future.

لَكُمْ identifies those addressed as the object of this attention.

الثَّقَلَانِ names the two communities addressed, identified from the surah’s context as humans and jinn. Rendering it “burdened” misleadingly suggests that they carry a burden; here it designates the two weighty communities.

Does God first finish another task?

In human speech, فرغت لك can mean that someone has finished another task and can now attend to the addressee.

In al-Raḥmān 55:31, however, the expression concerns God.

It does not imply that God was previously occupied and lacked time to attend to humans and jinn.

That conclusion would conflict with other verses concerning divine knowledge, power, and governance.

For example, al-Raḥmān 55:29 says:

«كُلَّ يَوْمٍ هُوَ فِي شَأْنٍ»

Kulla yawmin huwa fī shaʾn

This describes God as continually engaged in affairs and governance.

سَنَفْرُغُ لَكُمْ should therefore be understood in its linguistic construction and warning context: the time of their reckoning will come.

The expression can also warn or threaten in human speech, as when someone says: “I shall deal with what you have done.”

The main point is not necessarily finishing every other task, but emphasising serious attention to the matter.

Connection with the following verses

Al-Raḥmān 55:32 brings the surah’s repeated question about the Lord’s blessings.

Al-Raḥmān 55:33 addresses humans and jinn and speaks of their inability to pass beyond the domains of the heavens and earth without authority.

Al-Raḥmān 55:35 likewise describes the consequences of confronting divine power.

سَنَفْرُغُ لَكُمْ thus appears within a warning about accountability and the impossibility of escaping reckoning.

It does not describe everyday leisure after work; it uses this construction to express serious and certain attention to those addressed.

Relation to the underlying meaning

Finishing one task can allow a person to direct attention to another.

In فرغ له or فرغ إلى الأمر, attending and turning to a matter become prominent.

Al-Raḥmān 55:31 uses this semantic direction in a distinctive construction.

It should not be restricted to God needing time, leisure, or release from another occupation.

“We shall attend to you” suits the verse; in its context, this means attending to their reckoning.

7. فَإِذَا فَرَغْتَ فَانْصَبْ: finishing one task and undertaking further effort

Al-Sharḥ 94:7

فَإِذَا فَرَغْتَ فَانْصَبْ

Fa-idhā faraghta fa-nṣab

Translation: “So when you have finished, exert yourself.”

فَرَغْتَ is a second-person masculine singular perfect verb, referring to the surah’s direct addressee, Muhammad.

فَإِذَا connects the clause with a future circumstance: when you have finished.

The task or occupation being finished is not named in the verse itself.

The Qur’an does not say to do this only after one particular act of worship or one specific responsibility.

The sentence’s direct meaning is therefore broader than a single restricted instance.

What does فَانْصَبْ mean?

فَانْصَبْ belongs to ن ص ب, not ف ر غ.

Here it concerns exertion, taking trouble, and applying oneself seriously.

The verse places two stages together:

Finishing a task and undertaking effort.

The end of one responsibility can thus be the beginning of further exertion.

Someone might, after completing daily duties, devote some time to learning, serving others, or another worthwhile activity.

فَرَغْتَ itself does not mean beginning a new task. That comes from the following command, فَانْصَبْ.

Does the verse prohibit rest?

The phrase does not establish that people have no right to rest or sleep.

فَرَغْتَ expresses finishing an occupation, while فَانْصَبْ commands exertion within this address.

Determining the command’s scope requires considering other verses about human capacity, natural needs, and responsibilities.

The Qur’an presents sleep and repose as blessings of creation; it also states that people are not charged beyond their capacity.

This verse should therefore not become a theory of ceaseless work that deprives people of rest.

Conversely, فَانْصَبْ should not be reduced without justification to vague, ineffective advice.

The verse calls for purposeful use of opportunity and continued effort.

Connection with al-Sharḥ 94:8

Al-Sharḥ 94:8

وَإِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ فَارْغَبْ

Wa-ilā rabbika fa-rghab

Translation: “And direct your longing towards your Lord.”

This follows immediately after فَإِذَا فَرَغْتَ فَانْصَبْ.

فَارْغَبْ belongs to ر غ ب. Its visual resemblance to فَرَغْتَ does not make it a derivative of ف ر غ.

The verses are nevertheless connected in context.

The first speaks of finishing and exertion; the next identifies the direction of longing and turning towards the Lord.

Freedom from an occupation here does not lead to abandoning responsibility or aimlessness; it accompanies effort and orientation towards God.

This conclusion comes from the two verses together, not from the lexical meaning of ف ر غ alone.

8. Distinguishing freedom from occupation, emptiness, and full attention

The six Qur’anic uses share a semantic relationship, but do not describe one identical situation.

In al-Qaṣaṣ 28:10, the heart of Moses’ mother becomes فَارِغ. This emptiness occurs amid intense concern and the possibility of revealing her child’s secret.

In al-Sharḥ 94:7, a person finishes a task and is called to further effort.

In al-Raḥmān 55:31, the expression concerns serious attention to the addressees’ reckoning.

In the three Form IV occurrences, something is poured upon something else.

Farāgh can thus concern a state, the end of an occupation, or emptying contents.

Nor is it always accompanied by peace.

Moses’ mother experiences concern and distress when her fuʾād becomes empty.

Someone who completes a task, however, may be satisfied and calmly turn to the next responsibility.

Calm, anxiety, satisfaction, and concern are therefore not independent meanings of the root.

They are identified from context and the person’s circumstances.

One must also distinguish becoming empty of one thing from becoming empty of everything.

“I have finished my work” concerns a particular task; it does not mean the person no longer has thoughts, responsibilities, or activities.

Likewise, فَارِغًا in al-Qaṣaṣ 28:10 should not be interpreted as the destruction of all the mother’s feelings, knowledge, or perceptual capacity.

9. Comparison with neighbouring roots

ف ر غ (فَرَغَ) and خ ل و (خَلَا)

خ ل و concerns emptiness, becoming vacant, and the absence of something from a particular place or situation.

ف ر غ shares this field, but its Qur’anic uses also include finishing an occupation and pouring out contents.

For example, فَرَغْتَ in al-Sharḥ 94:7 concerns finishing a task, whereas خَلَا elsewhere may simply express someone’s or something’s absence.

The distinction is not absolute: the roots overlap in emptiness.

ف ر غ (فَرَغَ) and ت م م (تَمَّ)

ت م م concerns completion within a specified measure or extent.

فَرَغَ مِنَ الْعَمَلِ can likewise express finishing a task.

A task’s completion and a person’s becoming free from it nevertheless differ in semantic focus.

A task may reach completion while its responsible person still has related duties.

Someone may also finish a task whose result is unsatisfactory.

فَرَغْتَ in al-Sharḥ 94:7 therefore does not itself guarantee that the previous task was perfect in every respect.

ف ر غ (أَفْرَغَ) and ص ب ب (صَبَّ)

صَبَّ concerns pouring and causing something to flow.

Material ifrāgh also denotes pouring contents, but prominently relates to emptying the vessel or source.

In al-Kahf 18:96, أُفْرِغْ عَلَيْهِ قِطْرًا concerns pouring molten copper.

Al-Baqarah 2:250 and al-Aʿrāf 7:126 apply the same image to patience.

The roots are therefore close in pouring, but ف ر غ has other branches concerning emptiness and finishing an occupation.

ف ر غ (أَفْرَغَ) and س ك ب (سَكَبَ)

س ك ب concerns pouring and the flow of liquid.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 uses مَاءٍ مَسْكُوبٍ.

This evokes water flowing and being poured forth.

أَفْرَغَ can also describe pouring liquid; in the Qur’an it describes molten metal and, figuratively, patience.

Both roots can concern pouring, but the verbal form, object, and context determine the intended image.

ف ر غ (فَرَغَ) and ر غ ب (رَغِبَ)

The roots appear beside each other in al-Sharḥ 94:7 and al-Sharḥ 94:8:

فَرَغْتَ

and

فَارْغَبْ

Despite their resemblance, their letters and meanings differ.

ف ر غ concerns becoming empty and finishing an occupation.

ر غ ب concerns desire, longing, and turning towards something.

The two verses connect finishing a task with turning towards the Lord, but that contextual relationship does not establish a common root.

ف ر غ (فَرَغَ) and ف ر ج (فَرَجَ)

ف ر ج concerns gaps, openings, and intervals between things.

Some lexical uses of ف ر غ likewise concern empty space and breadth.

Yet Qur’anic ف ر ج also concerns fissures in the sky and sexual privacy, whereas ف ر غ concerns emptiness, finishing, and pouring out.

Similar letters and related material images should not lead to merging the roots.

10. Difficulties in understanding and translation

The first error is equating farāgh with idleness and laziness.

In Arabic, becoming free from a task can mean completing an important responsibility.

In al-Sharḥ 94:7, it accompanies a command to exert oneself.

It therefore need not mean aimlessness or absence of responsibility.

The second error is equating فَارِغًا in al-Qaṣaṣ 28:10 with absence of feeling.

The mother’s concern for her child occupies her entire attention.

Her heart’s emptiness here should not be interpreted as lack of affection, feeling, or concern.

The third error is treating أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا materially.

The prayer uses pouring to request abundant patience. Patience is not a substance like water or molten metal poured from a physical vessel onto the body.

The fourth error is removing the material meaning of ifrāgh from al-Kahf 18:96.

That verse concerns pouring molten copper over an iron structure; it should not be replaced with simply granting spiritual strength or extending mercy.

The fifth error is attributing a need for leisure or available time to God in al-Raḥmān 55:31.

The verse warns of reckoning. It expresses serious and certain attention to those addressed, rather than the completion of God’s other tasks.

The sixth error is restricting فَإِذَا فَرَغْتَ to one particular duty without explicit evidence in the verse.

The previous task is not named. A particular interpretation may be investigated contextually, but must not become the verb’s lexical definition.

Nor should the command to exert oneself be extended into prohibiting sleep or rest, or charging people beyond their ability.

Finally, farāgh need not mean complete, absolute emptiness. A vessel may be empty of water, someone free from a task, or a mind emptied of attention to other matters.

The sentence’s subject matter and accompanying prepositions establish these distinctions.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

For general فَرَغَ, appropriate equivalents include “became empty,” “became vacant,” “became free from a task,” and “finished a task.”

فَارِغ can be “empty,” “vacant,” or “unoccupied.”

Material أَفْرَغَ naturally becomes “poured,” “poured forth,” or “poured out the contents.”

For figurative patience, “pouring patience” preserves the original image.

Qur’anic forms

In al-Baqarah 2:250:

“Our Lord, pour patience upon us.”

In al-Aʿrāf 7:126:

“Our Lord, pour patience upon us and take us in death as ones who submit.”

In al-Kahf 18:96:

“Bring it to me, so that I may pour molten copper over it.”

In al-Qaṣaṣ 28:10:

“And the heart of Moses’ mother became empty.”

In al-Raḥmān 55:31:

“We shall attend to you, O two weighty communities.”

In al-Sharḥ 94:7:

“So when you have finished, exert yourself.”

These renderings do not impose one equivalent on every derivative.

“Empty” in al-Qaṣaṣ describes an inner state.

“Pouring” in the patience verses is figurative.

“Pour” in al-Kahf conveys a material action.

“We shall attend to you” in al-Raḥmān receives its reckoning sense from the warning context.

“Have finished” in al-Sharḥ expresses the end of an occupation.

12. Conclusion

In early Arabic, ف ر غ concerns emptiness, becoming vacant, and finishing an occupation. Other branches record breadth and openness.

Early poetic evidence illustrates breadth and openness. Lexical sources also identify emptying a vessel, finishing work, and pouring contents as established meanings.

The root occurs six times in the Qur’an.

In al-Qaṣaṣ 28:10, فَارِغًا describes Moses’ mother’s heart after separation from her child: a condition accompanied by intense concern and a need for firmness.

In al-Baqarah 2:250 and al-Aʿrāf 7:126, people ask God to pour patience upon them.

In al-Kahf 18:96, أُفْرِغْ denotes pouring molten copper over the structure.

In al-Raḥmān 55:31, سَنَفْرُغُ لَكُمْ announces serious attention to the addressees’ reckoning.

In al-Sharḥ 94:7, finishing an occupation is linked with a command to exert oneself.

The root’s central meaning concerns becoming empty, or emptying something, of its contents or occupation. Related meanings include finishing work, becoming available, pouring contents, and attending to another matter.

This shared relationship must not erase differences between uses. Emptying a vessel, the emptiness of a heart, finishing work, and pouring patience differ in subject matter and construction.

In translating the Qur’an, each derivative must be understood through its root, morphological structure, preposition, and context together.

Farāgh is not always ease, idleness, or distress. Each verse requires identifying what has become empty, what task has ended, what is poured out, or towards what attention is directed.
