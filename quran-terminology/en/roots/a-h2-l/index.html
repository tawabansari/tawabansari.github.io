The Qur’anic Root ʾ H L

From family and the people of a household to community, belonging, and worthiness

The root أ ه ل appears in the Qur’an through the noun أَهْل. The Qur’an does not use verbs such as taʾahhala or ahhala from this family, but ahl itself acquires a broad range through its combinations: a person’s ahl, أهل البيت، أهل القرية، أهل المدينة، أهل الكتاب، أهل الإنجيل، أهل الذكر, and even أهل التقوى and أهل المغفرة.

Ahl cannot mean only “family” in all these uses. Sometimes it denotes relatives and family, sometimes the inhabitants of a place, sometimes a community associated with a book or field, and sometimes someone to whom something belongs or who is worthy of it.

What brings these uses together is belonging and an appropriate relationship to something. A person’s ahl are those closely connected with that person; the ahl of a house are its occupants; the ahl of a town are those who live there; the People of the Book are a community associated with scripture. In the sense of worthiness, ahl describes someone for whom something is fitting or appropriate.

An initial definition can be stated as follows:

ʾ H L: a person or group with a close, recognized relationship to a person, household, place, community, field of knowledge, or matter. This relationship underlies such meanings as family, inhabitants, adherents, rightful recipients, and those qualified or worthy in relation to a matter.

Home, family, and an ancient semantic field

The use of ahl for family and those belonging to a household or group was known well before the Qur’an.

Pre-Islamic Safaitic inscriptions attest ʾhl for family. In KRS 1991, the writer longs for his family and asks for a reunion. In Al-Mafraq Museum 24, another writer looks out for his family and prays for safety and reunion. The Hismaic inscription KJC 42 asks the deity to remember the whole family, although the inscription is damaged and the reading of “family” is partly restored. Together these witnesses attest the word in ancient North Arabian settings for family and a person’s close group.

Poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays also illustrates this use:

فَلَوْ أَنَّ أَهْلَ الدَّارِ فِيهَا كَعَهْدِنَا
وَجَدْتُ مَقِيلًا عِنْدَهُمْ وَمُعَرَّسَا

“If the people of that dwelling were still there as we once knew them, I would find among them a place to rest at midday and a place to stop for the night.”

Here أهل الدار denotes those who belong to and inhabit the dwelling or settlement.

Another poem attributed to the same poet contains the following phrase:

تَذَكَّرْتُ أَهْلِي الصَّالِحِينَ

“I remembered my worthy family and kin.”

The two uses that we frequently encounter later in the Qur’an—a person’s ahl and the ahl of a place—were thus already familiar before the Qur’an.

Comparative Semitic evidence also points to an old connection between a dwelling and those who belong to it. Hebrew אֹהֶל, ʾōhel, means “tent”; the Brown–Driver–Briggs lexicon compares it with Arabic ahl, “fellow-dwellers, family.” Such comparisons support the antiquity of the association between a dwelling and its inhabitants, without proving one precise route of semantic development.

The exact historical development should nevertheless remain open. These similarities alone cannot establish that Arabic ahl originally meant only “tent” and then shifted to “family.” What is clearer is the longstanding connection between dwelling place, resident group, and family in this linguistic field.

Arabic lexicographical sources preserve the same range: a person’s ahl are their closest people, the ahl of a house its inhabitants, and the ahl of a religion those who belong to it.

A person’s ahl: family, spouse, and close relatives

One of the clearest Qur’anic uses of ahl is a person’s family and close relatives.

When Moses sees a fire, he says:

إِذْ رَأَىٰ نَارًا فَقَالَ لِأَهْلِهِ امْكُثُوا إِنِّي آنَسْتُ نَارًا لَعَلِّي آتِيكُمْ مِنْهَا بِقَبَسٍ أَوْ أَجِدُ عَلَى النَّارِ هُدًى

“When he saw a fire, he said to his family: Stay here; I have perceived a fire. Perhaps I can bring you a burning brand from it, or find guidance at the fire.”

Ta-Ha 20:10

Ahlahu here denotes those travelling with Moses who belong to his family.

In the same surah, Moses says of Aaron:

وَاجْعَلْ لِي وَزِيرًا مِنْ أَهْلِي ۝ هَارُونَ أَخِي

“And appoint for me a helper from my family—Aaron, my brother.”

Ta-Ha 20:29–30

Here a brother is explicitly included in أهلي, “my family.”

In Joseph’s story, the wife of the Egyptian official says to her husband:

قَالَتْ مَا جَزَاءُ مَنْ أَرَادَ بِأَهْلِكَ سُوءًا إِلَّا أَنْ يُسْجَنَ أَوْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

“She said: What should be the recompense of someone who intended harm against your household, except imprisonment or a painful punishment?”

Yusuf 12:25

Here أهلك refers to the woman herself. Ahl al-rajul can therefore include a man’s wife, a use also recorded in older Arabic.

Ahl is consequently not the technical name of one narrowly defined family arrangement. Context determines the relationship: a spouse, brother, child, and other close relatives can all belong to a person’s ahl.

Ahl occurs 127 times in the Qur’an, all as nominal forms. Alongside forms such as أهلي and أهله, its plural also appears with a pronoun: أَهْلُونَا in Al-Fath 48:11 and أَهْلِيكُمْ in Al-Tahrim 66:6. These are plural forms of the same noun, not verbs or separate roots.

Ahl al-bayt: those belonging to a household

The expression أهل البيت preserves the word’s basic household association particularly clearly: those belonging to a home and family.

In Abraham’s story, when his wife is astonished by the announcement of a child, the messengers say:

قَالُوا أَتَعْجَبِينَ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ ۖ رَحْمَتُ اللَّهِ وَبَرَكَاتُهُ عَلَيْكُمْ أَهْلَ الْبَيْتِ ۚ إِنَّهُ حَمِيدٌ مَجِيدٌ

“They said: Are you astonished at the command of The God? The mercy of The God and His blessings be upon you, people of this household. He is indeed praiseworthy and glorious.”

Hud 11:73

The direct address to Abraham’s wife plainly shows that a woman of the household can be included in أهل البيت.

The same expression appears in Al-Ahzab 33:33, within a passage addressing the Prophet’s wives. In later periods, extensive theological and sectarian debates developed over the precise scope of this designation.

A root study cannot settle that historical dispute through a lexical definition alone. Ahl al-bayt itself expresses belonging to a home and household. Who falls within its scope in a particular passage must be examined through that passage and its relevant evidence, rather than through the root’s meaning alone.

The expression should therefore not first be treated as the technical name of a later religious group and that specialized sense then projected onto every Qur’anic occurrence.

“My son is of my family”: does every relative count as ahl in every context?

Noah’s story contains some of the most important passages for understanding the flexibility of this word.

Before the flood, Noah is told:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَ أَمْرُنَا وَفَارَ التَّنُّورُ قُلْنَا احْمِلْ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلٍّ زَوْجَيْنِ اثْنَيْنِ وَأَهْلَكَ إِلَّا مَنْ سَبَقَ عَلَيْهِ الْقَوْلُ وَمَنْ آمَنَ ۚ وَمَا آمَنَ مَعَهُ إِلَّا قَلِيلٌ

“Until, when Our command came and the oven overflowed, We said: Carry into it a pair of each kind, and your family except those against whom the word has already gone forth, and those who have believed. Only a few had believed with him.”

Hud 11:40

Later, Noah says concerning his son:

وَنَادَىٰ نُوحٌ رَبَّهُ فَقَالَ رَبِّ إِنَّ ابْنِي مِنْ أَهْلِي وَإِنَّ وَعْدَكَ الْحَقُّ وَأَنْتَ أَحْكَمُ الْحَاكِمِينَ

“Noah called to his Lord and said: My Lord, my son is of my family, and Your promise is true, and You are the wisest of judges.”

Hud 11:45

The reply comes:

قَالَ يَا نُوحُ إِنَّهُ لَيْسَ مِنْ أَهْلِكَ إِنَّهُ عَمَلٌ غَيْرُ صَالِحٍ فَلَا تَسْأَلْنِ مَا لَيْسَ لَكَ بِهِ عِلْمٌ ۖ إِنِّي أَعِظُكَ أَنْ تَكُونَ مِنَ الْجَاهِلِينَ

“He said: Noah, he is not of your family; he is an unrighteous deed. So do not ask Me about what you have no knowledge of. I admonish you lest you be among the ignorant.”

Hud 11:46

The text still calls him ابن, Noah’s son. “He is not of your family” therefore cannot simply be turned into a denial of biological relationship.

The context concerns the promise to save the ahl, and an exception has already been stated: إِلَّا مَنْ سَبَقَ عَلَيْهِ الْقَوْلُ, “except those against whom the word has already gone forth.” In this situation, ahl is not merely a list of biological relatives; inclusion within the promise concerning the family also determines its scope.

These passages reveal an important feature of the word: family relationship is one basis of ahl, but context can restrict or specify whom it includes.

The ahl of a place: its inhabitants

Ahl combines with a place just as it does with a person.

Abraham prays:

وَإِذْ قَالَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ رَبِّ اجْعَلْ هَٰذَا بَلَدًا آمِنًا وَارْزُقْ أَهْلَهُ مِنَ الثَّمَرَاتِ مَنْ آمَنَ مِنْهُمْ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ ۖ قَالَ وَمَنْ كَفَرَ فَأُمَتِّعُهُ قَلِيلًا ثُمَّ أَضْطَرُّهُ إِلَىٰ عَذَابِ النَّارِ ۖ وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

“When Abraham said: My Lord, make this a secure town, and provide its people with fruits—those among them who believe in The God and the Last Day. He said: And whoever disbelieves, I will grant him enjoyment for a little while, then compel him to the punishment of the Fire. What an evil destination!”

Al-Baqarah 2:126

Here أَهْلَهُ means the people and inhabitants of the town.

The Qur’an also speaks of أهل القرية، أهل القرى، أهل المدينة، أهل يثرب, and أهل مدين: the people of a settlement, settlements, the city, Yathrib, and Midian. In each case the primary relationship is belonging to and living in a place.

This use is connected with the family sense. Just as the ahl of a house are those gathered and living there, the ahl of a settlement or town are those belonging to that place.

This shows why “family” cannot serve as an unchanging translation of ahl. Ahl al-qaryah does not mean “the family of the village,” but its people or inhabitants.

People of the Book, the Gospel, and the Reminder: belonging without kinship

An important extension of ahl in the Qur’an connects it with a text, religion, and knowledge.

أهل الكتاب, “People of the Book,” occurs repeatedly. Here the defining relationship is not a shared house, town, or family. A community is identified through the Book, and ahl expresses its connection with that Book.

The title does not itself impose one moral judgment upon every member of that community. The Qur’an says:

لَيْسُوا سَوَاءً ۗ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ أُمَّةٌ قَائِمَةٌ يَتْلُونَ آيَاتِ اللَّهِ آنَاءَ اللَّيْلِ وَهُمْ يَسْجُدُونَ

“They are not all alike. Among the People of the Book is an upright community, reciting the signs of The God during the hours of the night while they prostrate.”

Al Imran 3:113

The designation أهل الكتاب thus identifies an association with a religious and scriptural domain; it does not predetermine the goodness or wrongdoing of every individual.

Al-Ma’idah 5:47 also uses أهل الإنجيل, “People of the Gospel.” Here too, ahl relates people to a text and tradition.

The same construction appears in أهل الذكر:

وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا قَبْلَكَ إِلَّا رِجَالًا نُوحِي إِلَيْهِمْ ۖ فَاسْأَلُوا أَهْلَ الذِّكْرِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

“Before you We sent only men to whom We revealed. So ask the people of the Reminder if you do not know.”

Al-Anbiya 21:7

Ahl in this expression conveys their relationship to ذكر, dhikr, the Reminder. Precisely which text or community dhikr refers to in this context must be examined through the passage and related verses.

A direct translation of ahl al-dhikr as “the clerical class,” or as any later religious institution, does not follow from the root itself. What is clear is that those who do not know are directed to people acquainted with the subject in question.

“To their ahl”: rightful recipients and those to whom something belongs

A use in Al-Nisa extends ahl beyond family, place, and community:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَأْمُرُكُمْ أَنْ تُؤَدُّوا الْأَمَانَاتِ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهَا وَإِذَا حَكَمْتُمْ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ أَنْ تَحْكُمُوا بِالْعَدْلِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ نِعِمَّا يَعِظُكُمْ بِهِ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ سَمِيعًا بَصِيرًا

“The God commands you to return trusts to those entitled to them, and when you judge between people, to judge with justice. Excellent is the admonition The God gives you. The God is indeed hearing and seeing.”

Al-Nisa 4:58

أَهْلِهَا here does not mean “the family of the trusts.” It means those to whom the trusts belong and who have the right to receive them.

This use clearly illustrates the sense of appropriate belonging. The ahl of something can be the person to whom it rightfully belongs.

The same branch appears in the Arabic expression أهل لهذا الأمر: someone qualified for or worthy of a particular matter or task.

“He is ahl of taqwa and forgiveness”: worthiness and fitness

The clearest Qur’anic occurrence of this branch appears at the end of Al-Muddaththir:

وَمَا يَذْكُرُونَ إِلَّا أَنْ يَشَاءَ اللَّهُ ۚ هُوَ أَهْلُ التَّقْوَىٰ وَأَهْلُ الْمَغْفِرَةِ

“They will not take heed unless The God wills. He is worthy of being held in reverent regard and worthy of granting forgiveness.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:56

Here ahl is no longer the name of a group of people. هُوَ أَهْلُ التَّقْوَى means that He is the One toward whom taqwa is due and fitting, while أَهْلُ الْمَغْفِرَةِ means that forgiveness is fittingly granted by Him.

This use shows why the core of ahl cannot be reduced to “members of a family.” The wider field includes belonging, fitness, and worthiness.

The relationship expressed by saying that a trust should reach its rightful ahl also allows someone to be described as ahl of a matter: the matter is fitting for that person, or that person has the appropriate entitlement or qualification.

English uses different expressions for these relationships: “people of learning,” “members of a profession,” or “someone qualified for the task.” These renderings show why kinship alone cannot convey this branch of Arabic ahl; the intended relationship may be affiliation or competence.

ʾ H L (ahl) compared with āl

Ahl and āl can both be translated as “family,” but their Qur’anic uses are not identical.

Ahl has a very broad range: a person’s family, a household, inhabitants of a settlement or town, People of the Book, people of the Gospel or Reminder, rightful recipients of trusts, and even worthiness in relation to taqwa and forgiveness.

Āl is more restricted in the Qur’an, generally denoting a group associated with a named person or household: آل فرعون، آل إبراهيم، آل عمران، آل يعقوب، آل لوط، آل موسى، آل هارون, and آل داود.

In Qur’anic usage, ahl is therefore a more general way of expressing belonging, whereas āl more often concerns a group attached to a person or lineage.

There have been different views of the historical relationship between these two words. Some early lexicographers connected āl with ahl, while other analyses treat them separately. Similarity of meaning alone cannot establish a historical derivation.

Understanding the Qur’an does not require settling that dispute first. More immediately relevant is that the Qur’an does not use the two words in identical fields. Ahl al-kitāb and ahl al-qaryah cannot simply be replaced by āl al-kitāb and āl al-qaryah.

Translation proposals for Forqan

The translation of ahl must follow the noun to which it is attached and the context.

When أهلي، أهلك، أهله concern personal relationships, “my/your/his family,” “household,” or, where the context warrants it, “wife and family” may be appropriate.

For أهل البيت, “people of the household” or “household” is natural English. The transliteration Ahl al-Bayt can also be retained when explained, but its later technical associations should not automatically be carried into every passage.

For أهل القرية، أهل المدينة، أهل يثرب, natural renderings are “the people,” “inhabitants,” or “residents” of the named place.

For أهل الكتاب, “People of the Book” is an established English rendering, provided it is not treated as a uniform moral judgment on the whole community.

أهل الإنجيل can be “People of the Gospel,” and أهل الذكر “people of the Reminder,” with the precise referent of Gospel or Reminder explained from the context.

In تُؤَدُّوا الْأَمَانَاتِ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهَا, “return trusts to their rightful recipients” or “to those entitled to them” is clearer English than retaining an unexplained ahl. The lexical discussion should still explain why ahl conveys that relationship here.

In هُوَ أَهْلُ التَّقْوَى وَأَهْلُ الْمَغْفِرَةِ, a close rendering is “He is worthy of being held in reverent regard and worthy of granting forgiveness.” The explanation should make clear that ahl expresses worthiness or fitness, rather than membership in a community of the mindful or the forgiven.

Summary

The Qur’anic family أ ه ل can be approached through the household, but it is not confined to the family.

Pre-Qur’anic evidence, from Safaitic and Hismaic inscriptions to poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, shows the antiquity of ahl for family, close associates, and inhabitants of a place. Comparison with other Semitic languages also supports an old connection between dwelling, community, and family, although the exact course of development cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

In the Qur’an, a person’s ahl can include a spouse, brother, child, and other close relatives. أهل البيت denotes those belonging to a home and household, but its exact scope must be determined from each passage.

Noah’s story shows that ahl is not always a mechanical list of biological relatives. His son remains his ibn, yet in the context of the promise of rescue he is told, “He is not of your family.” The type of relationship and the scope of the statement therefore help determine whom ahl includes.

With a place, ahl denotes inhabitants: أهل القرية، أهل المدينة، أهل يثرب. With a text or religious domain, it denotes the community associated with it: أهل الكتاب، أهل الإنجيل، أهل الذكر. Ahl itself does not confer a positive or negative moral value in these constructions; the Qur’an explicitly says that the People of the Book are not all alike.

In Al-Nisa 4:58, أهلها denotes those to whom the trusts belong and who have the right to receive them. In Al-Muddaththir 74:56, ahl expresses worthiness and fitness: هُوَ أَهْلُ التَّقْوَى وَأَهْلُ الْمَغْفِرَةِ.

These uses show that the semantic field is broader than “family.” What connects the branches is belonging and an appropriate relationship.

ʾ H L: a person or group closely and appropriately connected with a person, household, place, text, community, or matter. This field includes family and close relatives, inhabitants of a place, a community associated with a book or domain, a rightful recipient, and someone qualified or worthy in relation to a matter. Ahl alone does not identify the exact relationship; the accompanying noun and the context of each passage make it clear.
