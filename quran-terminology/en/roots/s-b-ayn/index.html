1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The triliteral Arabic root S B ʿ has two familiar fields: seven and its related forms, and predatory animals. Despite shared consonants, their direct meanings are distinct.

Most Qur’anic uses concern counting: seven heavens, ears of grain, years, nights, gates, and seas, or seventy people or units. One verse uses al-sabuʿ for an animal that attacks prey and eats from it.

The family’s important forms include:

  • سَبْع: seven, the numerical form generally used with a feminine noun. Qur’anic: yes.
  • سَبْعَة: seven, the form generally used with a masculine noun. Qur’anic: yes.
  • سَبْعُون / سَبْعِينَ: seventy. Qur’anic: yes, in three verses.
  • سَابِع: seventh, an ordinal. Qur’anic: no.
  • سُبْع: one seventh of an amount. Qur’anic: no, in this independent form.
  • أَسْبَاع: sevenths, or portions each constituting a seventh. Qur’anic: no.
  • سَبَعَ / يَسْبَعُ: to become a group’s seventh member or take a seventh of something. Qur’anic: no.
  • سَبَّعَ / تَسْبِيع: to make seven, divide into seven, or perform an action seven times. Qur’anic: no.
  • أَسْبَعَ: to make seven; in another use, to expose something to a predator or make it prey. Qur’anic: no.
  • أُسْبُوع / سُبُوع: a seven-day period. Qur’anic: no.
  • سَبُع: a predatory animal that attacks and tears its prey. Qur’anic: yes, in Al-Māʾidah 5:3.
  • سِبَاع: predatory animals, plural of sabuʿ. Qur’anic: no, in this plural form.
  • مَسْبَعَة: a region abounding in predatory animals. Qur’anic: no.
  • مَسْبُوع: something or an animal attacked or taken by a predator. Qur’anic: no.

No independent verb from this root occurs in the Qur’an. The uses are numbers or the noun for a predator. Altogether the root occurs 28 times in 24 verses across 16 surahs.

The distinction matters: sabʿ meaning seven does not directly supply the meaning of predatory animal.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: twenty-eight occurrences of S B ʿ

2. Two semantic fields in early Arabic

Seven and units of counting

Sabʿ and sabʿah name a definite quantity: seven units.

Related forms express a seventh, the seventh person, performing something seven times, or bringing a group’s membership to seven.

In the early expression sabaʿtu al-qawma, for example, a person can mean joining six people and becoming their seventh.

The family also concerns taking a seventh of property or dividing something into seven portions.

Seven itself provides the measure in these uses.

Arabic can use seven for literal counting, emphasis, comparison, or abundance, but these functions differ.

“Seven people” first means seven humans. An emphatic or abundant reading requires support from context or established linguistic convention.

Seven should not become “many” or “complete” in every use.

Sabuʿ: a predatory animal

Sabuʿ, with ḍamma on the bāʾ, denotes a predator.

Early Arabic includes lions, wolves, leopards, and other animals that attack prey among its familiar examples.

The focus is predation and tearing prey, not merely possessing teeth or claws or eating meat.

Lexical definitions differ in scope. Some emphasize a fanged animal that tears prey; other uses encompass a broader group of hunters.

A single bodily feature, such as canine teeth, cannot alone fix every boundary of the word.

Nor does “predator” lexically mean that the animal’s meat is lawful or forbidden.

An animal can be called predatory while the permissibility of eating it remains a separate question, not settled by sabuʿ alone.

Do seven and predator share one origin?

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two independent origins for S B ʿ: seven and a predatory animal.

This separation matters.

Attempts to connect them sometimes name the predator through its supposedly complete strength or collection of abilities. Without independent evidence, that does not establish the word’s historical origin.

Nor is there sufficient support for deriving seven from a predator’s body parts or traits.

The two fields are better examined independently.

The Qur’anic distinction is clear: sabʿa samāwāt counts heavens; mā akala al-sabuʿ concerns what a predator has eaten from.

Neither expression contains the other’s semantic features.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: number and predator as two origins

3. Early Arabic witnesses and extended uses

Counting and division into seven

Sabbaʿa al-shayʾa can mean making something seven or dividing it into seven.

Sabbaʿa al-ināʾa is also recorded for washing a vessel seven times.

A number can thus form a verb; that does not make performing every action seven times inherently a religious ruling.

Usbūʿ names a period of seven days.

The word does not occur in the Qur’an, although seven appears in various Qur’anic durations.

Related early Arabic forms also designate intervals between watering camels, connected with the days between visits to water.

Seven therefore served everyday counting, time division, and repeated actions.

A seven-day period in Arabic does not mean that all Qur’anic time-related rulings follow a seven-day system.

Predator country in Labīd’s poetry

A rajaz passage attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah states:

إِلَيْكَ جَاوَزْنَا بِلَادًا مَسْبَعَهْ
إِذِ الْفَلَاةُ أَوْحَشَتْ فِي الْمَعْمَعَهْ

ilayka jāwaznā bilādan masbaʿah / idhi al-falātu awḥashat fī al-maʿmaʿah

That is: “To reach you, we crossed lands abounding in predators, when the desert grew desolate and forbidding in the intense heat.”

Al-Muḥkam, maʿmaʿ: intense heat

Masbaʿah describes land inhabited by predatory animals.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Tāj al-ʿArūs: masbaʿah in the Labīd witness

It has no direct numerical meaning here: the poet does not claim that the region contained exactly seven predators.

Labīd lived before and after Islam. The passage is transmitted in accounts of his youth and appearance before Al-Nuʿmān ibn al-Mundhir; the historical attribution and transmitted wording remain distinct from certainty about its date of composition.

The witness records masbaʿah, predator country, in early poetic language.

Sabuʿ in the image of a harmful tongue

A line transmitted in Arabic literary collections states:

لِسَانُ الْفَتَى سَبْعٌ عَلَيْهِ شَذَاتُهُ
فَإِنْ لَمْ يَزَعْ مِنْ غَرْبِهِ فَهُوَ آكِلُهْ

lisānu al-fatā sabʿun ʿalayhi shadhātuhu / fa-in lam yazaʿ min gharbihi fa-huwa ākiluh

That is: “A person’s tongue is a predator whose harm returns upon him; if he does not restrain its fierceness, it will devour him.”

Here sabʿ is the contracted form of sabuʿ, a predatory animal, to which the tongue is compared.

Predation can thus be transferred to human behavior as an image; the tongue does not literally become an animal.

The poet and date are not clear enough to call this a certain pre-Islamic witness.

It illustrates figurative use of the predatory field for severe harm.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: sabʿ for predator and the tongue witness

That image should not be imposed on Al-Māʾidah 5:3, which concerns an actual predator eating from an animal.

Seven and seventy as emphasis

Arabic sometimes uses numbers for abundance or emphasis.

Seventy, for example, can emphasize multiplicity without always making the seventieth unit an exact endpoint.

That is not an absolute rule.

Selecting seventy men may mean exactly seventy. Repeating an action seventy times elsewhere may emphasize extensive but fruitless repetition.

Construction and context determine the function.

4. Seven heavens: counting creation and the limits of scientific interpretation

Al-Baqarah 2:29

Verse text

هُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ لَكُمْ مَا فِي الْأَرْضِ جَمِيعًا ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ إِلَى السَّمَاءِ فَسَوَّاهُنَّ سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

huwa alladhī khalaqa lakum mā fī al-arḍi jamīʿan thumma istawā ilā al-samāʾi fa-sawwāhunna sabʿa samāwātin wa-huwa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīmun

English translation

He is the One who created for you everything on earth, then turned toward the heaven and fashioned them as seven heavens. He knows all things.

Usage analysis

Sabʿa samāwāt combines a number and its counted noun.

Sabʿa gives seven; samāwāt is the plural of samāʾ.

The number counts heavens. It cannot become seven days, stages of human life, or moral qualities without evidence.

Seven itself specifies no shape, size, distance, or physical structure of the heavens.

Fa-sawwāhunna concerns fashioning and arranging. The nature of that structure requires study of the verses concerning the heavens.

Al-Mulk 67:3

Verse text

الَّذِي خَلَقَ سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ طِبَاقًا مَا تَرَىٰ فِي خَلْقِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ مِنْ تَفَاوُتٍ فَارْجِعِ الْبَصَرَ هَلْ تَرَىٰ مِنْ فُطُورٍ

alladhī khalaqa sabʿa samāwātin ṭibāqan mā tarā fī khalqi al-Raḥmāni min tafāwutin fa-irjiʿi al-baṣara hal tarā min fuṭūrin

English translation

The One who created seven heavens in layers. You see no inconsistency in the creation of the Entirely Merciful. Look again: do you see any fissures?

Usage analysis

The seven heavens are described as ṭibāqan.

Ṭ B Q concerns layers, correspondence, and things placed over one another.

The Qur’an gives both a count and a description of the heavens’ arrangement and relation.

Ṭibāqan does not alone establish exactly seven atmospheric layers.

That identification needs independent evidence; seven does not prove it.

Seven heavens likewise does not mean that the universe contains only seven celestial bodies, planets, or galaxies.

Samāʾ and samāwāt have their own semantic fields. Seven counts the heavens in the verse without independently fixing their physical referents or limits.

Fuṣṣilat 41:12: seven heavens and each heaven’s command

Verse text

فَقَضَاهُنَّ سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ فِي يَوْمَيْنِ وَأَوْحَىٰ فِي كُلِّ سَمَاءٍ أَمْرَهَا وَزَيَّنَّا السَّمَاءَ الدُّنْيَا بِمَصَابِيحَ وَحِفْظًا ذَٰلِكَ تَقْدِيرُ الْعَزِيزِ الْعَلِيمِ

fa-qaḍāhunna sabʿa samāwātin fī yawmayni wa-awḥā fī kulli samāʾin amrahā wa-zayyannā al-samāʾa al-dunyā bi-maṣābīḥa wa-ḥifẓan dhālika taqdīru al-ʿazīzi al-ʿalīmi

English translation

He completed them as seven heavens in two days and revealed to each heaven its command. We adorned the nearest heaven with lamps and made provision for protection. That is the determination of the Mighty, the Knowing.

Usage analysis

Alongside seven, the verse mentions kulli samāʾ, “each heaven.”

The heavens form a creation described as a set in which each heaven receives individual consideration.

Al-samāʾ al-dunyā also means the nearest heaven.

This matters for proposals equating the seven heavens with familiar atmospheric layers.

The same verse associates lamps with the nearest heaven; Al-Ṣāffāt 37:6 and Al-Mulk 67:5 clarify this connection.

A certain identification with seven atmospheric layers is not defensible without a coherent account of those verses.

Rejecting an unsupported scientific identification should not, conversely, deny the Qur’an’s own affirmation of seven heavens.

Do seven heavens also mean seven earths?

Al-Ṭalāq 65:12 states:

اللَّهُ الَّذِي خَلَقَ سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ وَمِنَ الْأَرْضِ مِثْلَهُنَّ يَتَنَزَّلُ الْأَمْرُ بَيْنَهُنَّ لِتَعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ قَدْ أَحَاطَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عِلْمًا

Allāhu alladhī khalaqa sabʿa samāwātin wa-mina al-arḍi mithlahunna yatanazzalu al-amru baynahunna li-taʿlamū anna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrun wa-anna Allāha qad aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilman

English translation

The God is the One who created seven heavens, and of the earth their like. The command descends among them, so that you may know that The God has power over all things and encompasses all things in knowledge.

Seven explicitly counts the heavens.

The phrase wa-mina al-arḍi mithlahunna follows.

It speaks of a likeness in the earthly realm to those heavens. Numerical likeness can be considered: as there are seven heavens, a numerical correspondence may also concern the earth.

The verse nevertheless does not directly use the phrase sabʿa araḍīn, “seven earths.”

Mithl alone does not determine that likeness’s geometry, physical structure, or exact nature.

Numerical correspondence should neither be dismissed without reason nor turned into a certain map of seven earths, planets, or terrestrial layers.

Other sevenfold heavenly expressions

Al-Isrāʾ 17:44 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:86 use al-samāwātu al-sabʿu and al-samāwāti al-sabʿi.

Nūḥ 71:15 also describes seven heavens as ṭibāqan.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:17 uses sabʿa ṭarāʾiq. Ṭ R Q concerns ways, paths, and arrangements involving routes or layers. Fawqakum, “above you,” supports examining a heavenly referent, but ṭarāʾiq is not lexically synonymous with samāwāt.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:12 also states:

وَبَنَيْنَا فَوْقَكُمْ سَبْعًا شِدَادًا

wa-banaynā fawqakum sabʿan shidādan

English translation

We built above you seven mighty [structures].

The counted noun is not explicitly stated after sabʿan. Context and other verses support a connection with the heavens.

Adding “heavens” clarifies an unstated referent, rather than translating an explicit noun following sabʿan.

Together these passages show that seven heavens recur throughout the Qur’an. Seven alone cannot yield a complete scientific model of the universe.

5. Seven in Joseph’s story: definite counts in dreams and planning

Yūsuf 12:43: the king’s dream

Verse text

وَقَالَ الْمَلِكُ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ يَا أَيُّهَا الْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِي فِي رُءْيَايَ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ لِلرُّءْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ

wa-qāla al-maliku innī arā sabʿa baqarātin simānin yaʾkuluhunna sabʿun ʿijāfun wa-sabʿa sunbulātin khuḍrin wa-ukhara yābisātin yā ayyuhā al-malaʾu aftūnī fī ruʾyāya in kuntum li-al-ruʾyā taʿburūna

English translation

The king said, “I see seven fat cows being eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others dry. Council, give me an answer concerning my dream, if you interpret dreams.”

Usage analysis

Seven occurs several times.

The dream presents seven fat cows, seven lean cows, and seven green ears of grain.

These are counts within the dream’s imagery.

Yūsuf 12:46 repeats these elements in the request for Joseph’s interpretation.

Seven’s recurrence is integral to the story, appearing in both the dream and its interpretation.

The dream image must still be distinguished from its external referent.

The seven cows correspond to seven years in the interpretation; baqarāt does not therefore lexically mean years.

Interpreting a dream is distinct from the lexical meanings of what is seen.

Yūsuf 12:47: seven years of cultivation

Verse text

قَالَ تَزْرَعُونَ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ دَأَبًا فَمَا حَصَدْتُمْ فَذَرُوهُ فِي سُنْبُلِهِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِمَّا تَأْكُلُونَ

qāla tazraʿūna sabʿa sinīna daʾaban fa-mā ḥaṣadtum fa-dharūhu fī sunbulihi illā qalīlan mimmā taʾkulūna

English translation

He said, “You will cultivate continuously for seven years. Leave what you harvest in its ears, except a little that you eat.”

Usage analysis

Sabʿa sinīn denotes seven actual years in an agricultural plan.

Seven is no longer only a dream image; it explains the future and planning for it.

Preserving the produce in its ears relates to that period.

Seven years is a definite duration, not merely “several plentiful years”: the count matters to the plan.

Yūsuf 12:48: seven difficult years

Verse text

ثُمَّ يَأْتِي مِنْ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ سَبْعٌ شِدَادٌ يَأْكُلْنَ مَا قَدَّمْتُمْ لَهُنَّ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِمَّا تُحْصِنُونَ

thumma yaʾtī min baʿdi dhālika sabʿun shidādun yaʾkulna mā qaddamtum lahunna illā qalīlan mimmā tuḥṣinūna

English translation

Then seven difficult years will follow, consuming what you have stored beforehand for them, except a little of what you preserve.

Usage analysis

The phrase is sabʿun shidād.

The counted noun is not repeated but follows from sabʿa sinīn in the preceding verse.

Seven difficult years are intended.

Seven itself does not mean hardship; shidād describes the years.

The first and second periods have equal lengths but different economic and agricultural conditions.

The same number can occur in opposing circumstances without changing its meaning.

Distinguishing the count from dream interpretation

Seven cows and seven ears of grain become connected with seven years.

Joseph’s interpretation establishes that connection within the story.

It does not establish a general rule making every Qur’anic seven involving animals, plants, or heavens necessarily mean seven years or historical periods.

Neither Arabic usage nor the story supports that generalization.

The passage interprets a particular dream with particular conditions and consequences.

Conclusion: Seven is a definite count in Joseph’s dream and economic planning. Its meaning remains; the subject counted moves from dream images to actual years.

6. Seven and seventy: measure, abundance, and responsibility

Al-Baqarah 2:261: seven ears and the increased yield of spending

Verse text

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ كَمَثَلِ حَبَّةٍ أَنْبَتَتْ سَبْعَ سَنَابِلَ فِي كُلِّ سُنْبُلَةٍ مِائَةُ حَبَّةٍ وَاللَّهُ يُضَاعِفُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

mathalu alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum fī sabīli llāhi ka-mathali ḥabbatin anbatat sabʿa sanābila fī kulli sunbulatin miʾatu ḥabbatin wa-llāhu yuḍāʿifu li-man yashāʾu wa-llāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīmun

English translation

Those who spend their property in the way of The God are like a grain that grows seven ears, with a hundred grains in each. The God multiplies for whom He wills; The God is encompassing and knowing.

Usage analysis

Seven ears with a hundred grains each create a numerical image for the increased yield of giving.

Seven times a hundred is seven hundred.

The verse then states:

“The God multiplies for whom He wills.”

Seven hundred therefore cannot establish a certain ceiling for the reward of all giving.

Nor is this an agricultural law making every seed produce exactly seven ears with a hundred grains under every condition.

The verse’s subject is a likeness or simile.

A number can thus express increase within an image while retaining its mathematical meaning.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:155: seventy men

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:155, Moses selects seventy men from his people for the appointed meeting.

Sabʿīna rajulan combines a number and counted noun.

Seventy counts the people selected.

The verse provides no evidence for changing it into seventy spiritual stages, qualities, or an unlimited number.

Al-Tawbah 9:80: seeking forgiveness seventy times

Verse text

اسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ أَوْ لَا تَسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ إِنْ تَسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ سَبْعِينَ مَرَّةً فَلَنْ يَغْفِرَ اللَّهُ لَهُمْ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ كَفَرُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْفَاسِقِينَ

istaghfir lahum aw lā tastaghfir lahum in tastaghfir lahum sabʿīna marratan fa-lan yaghfira Allāhu lahum dhālika bi-annahum kafarū bi-Allāhi wa-rasūlihi wa-Allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-fāsiqīna

English translation

Seek forgiveness for them, or do not seek it. If you seek forgiveness for them seventy times, The God will not forgive them. That is because they disbelieved in The God and His Messenger; The God does not guide the defiantly disobedient people.

Usage analysis

Seeking forgiveness seventy times occurs in an emphatic construction.

The issue is its fruitlessness for the group whose conduct and condition are described in context.

Sabʿīna marratan does not mean that reaching seventy-one requests would automatically change the judgment.

Such a number can emphasize extensive but fruitless repetition in Arabic.

That particular use does not make seventy men in Al-Aʿrāf 7:155 merely “many people.”

Context determines the function.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7: seven nights and eight days

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 describes the destructive wind upon ʿĀd through sabʿa layālin wa-thamāniyata ayyām, seven nights and eight days.

The construction places seven nights beside eight days.

Neither quantity should be changed to seven days, nor should the difference between night and day disappear in translation.

Seven itself establishes neither the wind’s natural cause or speed nor the manner of the people’s destruction.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32: seventy cubits

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32 gives a chain’s measure as sabʿūna dhirāʿan, seventy cubits.

A cubit is a unit of length.

Seventy forms part of a measurement in the image.

The number and unit alone do not establish the chain’s material, its precise dimensions in contemporary units, or every bodily feature of the punishment.

Does seven always symbolize abundance?

No.

Some constructions use seven or seventy emphatically, but many verses concern a definite count.

Joseph’s seven years, Moses’ seventy men, ʿĀd’s seven nights, and seven fasting days after returning from pilgrimage should not lose their numerical meaning without reason.

In the spending parable, the numerical image expresses increase and blessing.

A number can play different roles, determined by context rather than a changed basic meaning of sabʿ.

7. Seven gates, seven seas, and seven of the mathānī

Al-Ḥijr 15:44: seven gates of Hell

Verse text

لَهَا سَبْعَةُ أَبْوَابٍ لِكُلِّ بَابٍ مِنْهُمْ جُزْءٌ مَقْسُومٌ

lahā sabʿatu abwābin li-kulli bābin minhum juzʾun maqsūmun

English translation

It has seven gates; for each gate, an allotted portion of them.

Usage analysis

Sabʿatu abwāb combines seven with the plural of bāb, gate.

The verse counts gates and then assigns a portion of people to each.

Seven alone does not establish the gates’ form, spatial arrangement, or the nature of their respective groups.

Nor should seven gates alone become seven particular sins or seven definite tiers of Hell without independent Qur’anic evidence.

The number counts the gates; it does not list all their characteristics.

Luqmān 31:27: seven seas

Verse text

وَلَوْ أَنَّمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ مِنْ شَجَرَةٍ أَقْلَامٌ وَالْبَحْرُ يَمُدُّهُ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ سَبْعَةُ أَبْحُرٍ مَا نَفِدَتْ كَلِمَاتُ اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-law anna-mā fī al-arḍi min shajaratin aqlāmun wa-al-baḥru yamudduhu min baʿdihi sabʿatu abḥurin mā nafidat kalimātu Allāhi inna Allāha ʿazīzun ḥakīmun

English translation

If all the trees on earth were pens, and the sea were ink, replenished after it by seven more seas, the words of The God would not be exhausted. The God is mighty and wise.

Usage analysis

Sabʿatu abḥur belongs to an imagined supposition.

Trees become pens, the sea is imagined as ink, and seven further seas replenish it.

The point is the inexhaustibility of God’s words relative to finite material writing resources.

The verse is not a geographical census of earth’s seas.

“Seven seas” does not establish that exactly seven seas exist or that the Qur’an lists seven particular oceans.

Seven enlarges the hypothetical resources, yet even that abundance cannot exhaust God’s words.

The contrast with Joseph’s seven years is clear.

There seven forms a schedule; in Luqmān 31:27, seven seas contribute to a supposition comparing the finite with the inexhaustible.

Al-Ḥijr 15:87: seven of the mathānī

Verse text

وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَاكَ سَبْعًا مِنَ الْمَثَانِي وَالْقُرْآنَ الْعَظِيمَ

wa-la-qad ātaynāka sabʿan mina al-mathānī wa-al-qurʾāna al-ʿaẓīma

English translation

We have indeed given you seven of the mathānī and the great Qur’an.

Usage analysis

The verse has particular syntactic and interpretive significance.

Sabʿan means seven, but no independent counted noun follows it explicitly.

Mina al-mathānī identifies that to which the seven belong.

Al-mathānī belongs to TH N Y, not S B ʿ, and requires its own root study.

Classical readings include Al-Fātiḥah’s seven verses, seven long surahs, and groups or portions of revelation involving repetition and retelling.

Reporting these readings is distinct from proving them through the phrase alone.

The verse names neither Al-Fātiḥah nor a list of seven surahs.

Min also requires examination: does it designate part of the mathānī or explain the kind of seven?

Wa-al-qurʾāna al-ʿaẓīm follows. Their pairing permits different syntactic and semantic analyses; seven itself does not settle them.

Al-Zumar 39:23 describes the Qur’an as a book, mutashābihan mathāniya, important for understanding mathānī’s field.

Shared mathānī does not settle every detail of Al-Ḥijr 15:87 without further examination.

Direct translation should retain the phrase’s open referent, leaving identification for a separate reflection.

Lexical conclusion: Sabʿan means seven, but S B ʿ alone does not decide whether these are verses, surahs, or other portions.

Al-Ṭabarī on Al-Ḥijr 15:87: transmitted readings of the mathānī

8. Seven in the cave story: a number among differing claims

Al-Kahf 18:22

Verse text

سَيَقُولُونَ ثَلَاثَةٌ رَابِعُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ وَيَقُولُونَ خَمْسَةٌ سَادِسُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ رَجْمًا بِالْغَيْبِ وَيَقُولُونَ سَبْعَةٌ وَثَامِنُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ قُلْ رَبِّي أَعْلَمُ بِعِدَّتِهِمْ مَا يَعْلَمُهُمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلٌ فَلَا تُمَارِ فِيهِمْ إِلَّا مِرَاءً ظَاهِرًا وَلَا تَسْتَفْتِ فِيهِمْ مِنْهُمْ أَحَدًا

sa-yaqūlūna thalāthatun rābiʿuhum kalbuhum wa-yaqūlūna khamsatun sādisuhum kalbuhum rajman bi-al-ghaybi wa-yaqūlūna sabʿatun wa-thāminuhum kalbuhum qul rabbī aʿlamu bi-ʿiddatihim mā yaʿlamuhum illā qalīlun fa-lā tumāri fīhim illā mirāʾan ẓāhiran wa-lā tastafti fīhim minhum aḥadan

English translation

They will say, “Three, their dog the fourth,” and say, “Five, their dog the sixth,” guessing at the unseen; and say, “Seven, their dog the eighth.” Say, “My Lord knows their number best; only a few know them.” Do not dispute concerning them except in an evident, limited discussion, and do not ask any of these people for an answer about them.

Usage analysis

Seven occurs among differing claims about the cave companions’ number.

Three, five, and seven are three counts reported from speakers.

The verse then directs the addressee to acknowledge God’s superior knowledge of their number.

Sabʿah itself simply means seven, establishing neither the truth of one claim nor the falsity of the others.

Some readings favor seven because the third statement differs syntactically from the first two.

That possibility belongs to analysis of the sentence, rather than an unequivocal explicit Qur’anic declaration.

After reporting the disagreement, the verse stresses limited human knowledge and restraint from unsupported dispute.

It creates no rule making seven certainly true whenever it comes last among several proposed numbers.

Thāminuhum belongs to the root for eight, not S B ʿ; the eighth member is the dog.

The human count and the total including the dog must remain distinct when reporting the claims.

This illustrates the boundary between certain Qur’anic information and claims reported within the Qur’an.

The Qur’an can report someone’s speech without presenting it as its final judgment.

Al-Ṭabarī on Al-Kahf 18:22: reported counts of the cave companions

9. Al-sabuʿ: the predator in the food passage

Al-Māʾidah 5:3

Verse text

حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةُ وَالدَّمُ وَلَحْمُ الْخِنْزِيرِ وَمَا أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ بِهِ وَالْمُنْخَنِقَةُ وَالْمَوْقُوذَةُ وَالْمُتَرَدِّيَةُ وَالنَّطِيحَةُ وَمَا أَكَلَ السَّبُعُ إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ وَمَا ذُبِحَ عَلَى النُّصُبِ وَأَنْ تَسْتَقْسِمُوا بِالْأَزْلَامِ ذَٰلِكُمْ فِسْقٌ الْيَوْمَ يَئِسَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ دِينِكُمْ فَلَا تَخْشَوْهُمْ وَاخْشَوْنِ الْيَوْمَ أَكْمَلْتُ لَكُمْ دِينَكُمْ وَأَتْمَمْتُ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعْمَتِي وَرَضِيتُ لَكُمُ الْإِسْلَامَ دِينًا فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ فِي مَخْمَصَةٍ غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

ḥurrimat ʿalaykumu al-maytatu wa-l-damu wa-laḥmu al-khinzīri wa-mā uhilla li-ghayri llāhi bihi wa-l-munkhaniqatu wa-l-mawqūdhatu wa-l-mutaraddiyatu wa-l-naṭīḥatu wa-mā akala al-sabuʿu illā mā dhakkaytum wa-mā dhubiḥa ʿalā al-nuṣubi wa-an tastaqsimū bi-l-azlāmi dhālikum fisqun al-yawma yaʾisa alladhīna kafarū min dīnikum fa-lā takhshawhum wa-khshawnī al-yawma akmaltu lakum dīnakum wa-atmamtu ʿalaykum niʿmatī wa-raḍītu lakumu al-islāma dīnan fa-mani uḍṭurra fī makhmaṣatin ghayra mutajānifin li-ithmin fa-inna llāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

English translation

Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, swine flesh, that over which an invocation has been raised for other than The God, the strangled, the beaten, the fallen, the gored, and that from which a predator has eaten, except what you have subjected to tadhkiyah; also what has been slaughtered upon the stone altars, and dividing or determining by divining arrows. These are disobedience. Today those who disbelieve have despaired of your religion, so do not fear them; be mindful of Me. Today I have completed your religion for you, fulfilled My blessing upon you, and approved Islam for you as religion. Whoever is compelled by severe hunger without inclining toward sin: The God is forgiving and merciful.

Usage analysis

Al-sabuʿ denotes a predatory animal.

The construction is:

“That from which the predator has eaten.”

Mā refers to what is eaten from; akala is the verb “ate”; al-sabuʿ is its subject.

The phrase therefore concerns something a predator has eaten from.

The distinction is crucial.

It does not say wa-laḥmu al-sabuʿ, “the predator’s flesh.”

It speaks instead of what the predator has eaten from.

Translation and argument should not equate these meanings.

Does the verse forbid every predator’s meat?

That conclusion does not follow from this phrase alone.

In mā akala al-sabuʿ, the predator performs the eating; it is not directly the object of human consumption.

The ruling concerns an animal or something attacked and eaten from by a predator.

This phrase alone cannot establish a list of predators whose meat is then declared explicitly forbidden by this verse.

Such a claim requires independent textual evidence.

The difference between “predator meat” and “what a predator has eaten from” is a syntactic distinction, independent of personal conclusions about an animal’s lawfulness.

Relation to the other animal conditions

The preceding list mentions the strangled, beaten, fallen, and gored.

These concern an animal’s condition and how it was injured or died.

Wa-mā akala al-sabuʿ belongs to the same sequence.

Context therefore centers on the condition of an animal attacked and eaten from by a predator.

That connection does not place every detail of slaughter, food safety, or care of injured animals inside S B ʿ itself.

Those questions require akala, dhakkā, and the verse’s other terms.

The role of illā mā dhakkaytum

The exception illā mā dhakkaytum follows several animal conditions.

The ruling concerning an injured animal must therefore consider the possibility of tadhkiyah.

Sabuʿ itself does not determine how or under which conditions that is possible.

The object remains what the predator has harmed or eaten from, not the predator as a separate kind of food.

“What the predator has eaten from” is consequently more accurate than “predator meat.”

Predation, prohibition, and differing food habits

Sabuʿ names a predatory animal.

It describes hunting and behavior, not whether all people find its meat appealing.

One society may avoid certain animals while another eats them because of geography, food availability, or tradition.

Different food habits alone do not establish a divine prohibition.

Conversely, permissibility or absence of prohibition does not require anyone to eat or enjoy a food.

Health, contamination, disease risk, conservation, and animal-related laws are separate subjects; they should not be equated with sabuʿ’s lexical meaning or a religious judgment.

Lexical conclusion: Al-sabuʿ in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 is a predator, but wa-mā akala al-sabuʿ concerns what it has eaten from. The phrase itself does not independently rule on the meat of every predatory animal.

Al-Ṭabarī on Al-Māʾidah 5:3: mā akala al-sabuʿ and tadhkiyah

10. Morphology and related roots

Sabʿ and sabʿah

The form of seven relates to the counted noun’s grammatical gender.

Sabʿa samāwāt accompanies a feminine counted noun, whereas sabʿatu abwāb accompanies a masculine one.

The grammatical distinction does not change the quantity.

Both mean seven.

Some verses leave the counted noun unstated and recoverable from context, as in sabʿun shidād in Yūsuf 12:48.

Its identification must follow the preceding phrases or context, not unrestricted conjecture.

Sabʿ and sabuʿ

Sabʿ, with a vowelless bāʾ, is seven.

Sabuʿ, with ḍamma on bāʾ, is a predator. Lexicons also record the contracted sabʿ for the animal; final identification therefore requires context, not vowels alone.

This distinction matters greatly in Arabic.

The two words share S B ʿ but differ in direct meaning.

In unvocalized text, context and syntax help identify the sense.

Sabʿa samāwāt clearly gives a number; in mā akala al-sabuʿ, a predator performs the eating.

Sabʿ compared with ʿ SH R (ʿasharah)

Study of ʿ SH R

ʿ SH R is the family of ten, also including a tenth and association.

The numerical field of S B ʿ concerns seven and related forms.

Al-Baqarah 2:196 uses both: seven days after returning, and ten complete days in total.

Ten is the sum of three and seven.

Seven and ten are independent numbers, not interchangeable words for perfection.

S B ʿ compared with S B GH (sabagha)

Study of S B GH

S B GH concerns expansiveness, abundance, and completeness of covering or blessing.

It differs from S B ʿ.

Shared initial consonants do not justify defining seven through sabagha as expansiveness or completeness.

Seven’s symbolism of abundance or totality in some cultures and expressions requires examination independently of S B GH.

S B ʿ compared with S B Ḥ (sabbaḥa)

Study of S B Ḥ

Qur’anic S B Ḥ concerns movement, swimming, and glorification.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:44 places al-samāwātu al-sabʿu alongside tusabbiḥu.

Al-sabʿu and tusabbiḥu nevertheless belong to different roots.

Sound resemblance does not make seven mean glorification, or mean that the heavens are seven only because they glorify.

S B ʿ compared with S B T (sabt)

Study of S B T

Qur’anic sabt concerns the Sabbath and its associated matters.

Sabʿ is seven.

A seven-day calendar connects with the days of a week, but the two lexical roots remain independent.

Not every Sabbath ruling can be derived from seven.

The Qur’an also speaks of creating the heavens and earth in six days. Seven in the names of some periods cannot make it a Qur’anic creation in seven days.

S B ʿ compared with F R S (farasa)

Arabic farasa, in hunting, concerns tearing and bringing down prey.

Sabuʿ names the predator.

A predator may tear prey, but an animal’s name differs from the action of tearing.

That matters in Al-Māʾidah 5:3: what the predator has eaten and the animal’s behavior contribute to context.

Every sense of tearing or hunting should not be imported into the noun sabuʿ.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

The basic numerical equivalent is seven.

Sabʿa samāwāt is “seven heavens”; sabʿa sanābil, “seven ears of grain”; sabʿa sinīn, “seven years.”

Sabʿatu abwāb and sabʿatu abḥur should retain “seven gates” and “seven seas,” respectively.

Sabʿūn and sabʿīn directly mean seventy. Where context makes the count emphatic, the reflection should explain that without removing the number from direct translation.

Sabʿun shidād in Yūsuf 12:48 can be “seven difficult years.” Years follows from the preceding context, naturally supplying the omitted counted noun.

Sabʿan shidādan in Al-Nabaʾ 78:12 can be “seven mighty [structures].” If “heavens” is chosen, its contextual identification should remain clear.

Sabʿan mina al-mathānī in Al-Ḥijr 15:87 is best kept close to “seven of the mathānī,” leaving the referent for separate reflection.

Sabʿah in Al-Kahf 18:22 means seven people, but belongs to a reported claim and should not automatically become the Qur’an’s certain final judgment.

Al-sabuʿ in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 should be “predatory animal,” or naturally “predator.”

Wa-mā akala al-sabuʿ is accurately “and what the predator has eaten from.”

“Predator meat” is inaccurate here, turning the subject of eating into its object.

Translation must preserve the vowels, number construction, counted noun, and syntactic role.

12. Final synthesis

S B ʿ has two principal Arabic fields: seven and the predatory animal.

Seven names a definite quantity used to count people, periods, objects, and structures.

The Qur’an counts seven heavens, years, ears of grain, nights, gates, seas, and other things. Seventy counts people, repetitions, and measures.

Seven does not have one unvarying function throughout the Qur’an.

It can be a definite count, as in Joseph’s years or Moses’ seventy men; part of a simile, as in the ears of grain; part of an expanded supposition illustrating God’s inexhaustible words, as in the seas; or part of differing reported claims, as in the cave story.

In heavenly passages, seven counts the heavenly structures presented by the Qur’an. It does not determine their exact physical nature or correspondence to modern scientific classifications.

Seven should not become a fixed symbol of perfection, sanctity, abundance, or hidden stages without evidence.

In the other field, al-sabuʿ denotes a predator.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, it performs the eating. Wa-mā akala al-sabuʿ therefore concerns what it has eaten from, not its own flesh.

Preserving syntax matters in translation and the study of Qur’anic rulings.

A shared root does not prove a single historical origin for sabʿ and sabuʿ. Classical lexical analyses likewise distinguish their fields.

Final definition: The numerical branch of S B ʿ concerns seven and related forms such as seventy and one seventh. In its other branch, sabuʿ names a predatory animal that hunts and tears prey. Available evidence does not establish a certain historical connection between the two fields.

Each Qur’anic use requires asking whether the word is a number or an animal; what the number counts or how it is constructed; and whether the verse gives a definite count, a simile, or reported speech.

These distinctions prevent unsupported numerical symbolism and prevent animal or food rulings from arising through changes to a word’s meaning.
