1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic S F R concerns becoming visible, removing a covering, and revealing what was hidden. Related uses concern morning’s brightness, an uncovered face, travel, writing and reading books, and conveying messages.

Safar ordinarily means journeying away from one’s residence. Asfara can describe morning becoming bright; sifr names a book or recorded writing; safarah concerns scribes or transmitters of writing and messages.

Travel, a brightening sky, and writing may initially seem unrelated. Arabic recognizes a connection: travelers leave their residence, morning dispels darkness, and writing visibly records what was in thought or speech.

This does not give every form one identical contextual meaning. Qur’anic safar may concern an actual journey rather than discovering truth; asfār means journeys in one verse and books in another.

The root occurs twelve times, in five morphological groups: seven safar, two asfār with different senses, one safarah, one Form IV asfara, and one participle musfirah.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: twelve occurrences and five groups

Four Qur’anic subjects can be distinguished: traveling and the traveler’s circumstances; becoming visibly bright; written books; and those who preserve or convey writing and messages.

2. The semantic core and important forms

Becoming visible and removing a covering

Ibn Fāris defines the core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

السِّينُ وَالْفَاءُ وَالرَّاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الِانْكِشَافِ وَالْجَلَاءِ

al-sīnu wa-al-fāʾu wa-al-rāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-inkishāfi wa-al-jalāʾi

“Sīn, fāʾ, and rāʾ have one foundation indicating uncovering and becoming clear.”

He discusses travel, sweeping a house, morning’s brightness, an uncovered face, and resolving disputes within this family.

Two aspects appear: what was hidden becomes visible, and an obstacle to seeing or knowing is removed.

Wind moves clouds aside to reveal the sky; morning light dispels darkness to reveal the landscape; removing a facial covering reveals the face.

The image of uncovering is clear in these examples.

Ordinary travel is nevertheless an established meaning beyond uncovering. Likewise sifr is an established noun for a book, not something requiring the rendering “revealer” each time.

Ibn Fāris’s proposed core relates the family without replacing each word’s independent meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: uncovering and becoming visible

A physical image: cleaning and revealing

Early safara al-bayta means sweeping a house or clearing its dust and dirt.

What covered or accumulated on the surface is removed.

Safarati al-rīḥu al-ghayma describes wind moving clouds aside, revealing previously covered sky.

Early al-safīr can also name fallen leaves or what wind moves across the ground.

Revealing is therefore present in physical language and daily life as well as abstract thought.

This does not reduce every safīr to fallen leaves: elsewhere it names a messenger or mediator.

Important Arabic forms

Form I safara can concern uncovering, sweeping, revealing, and in some uses traveling or mediating, depending on its construction.

Safar, with a in both syllables, ordinarily means travel away from one’s residence. It occurs seven times in the Qur’an.

Form III sāfara means traveling; its verbal noun is musāfarah. This verb does not occur in the Qur’an.

Musāfir is its active participle, a traveler. It too is absent from the Qur’an, though safar occurs.

Asfār can be plural of safar, journeys, or sifr, books. Both uses occur in the Qur’an.

Form IV asfara can mean morning becoming bright and visible. This verb occurs in the Qur’an.

Musfir and feminine musfirah are Form IV active participles describing what has become bright, visible, or radiant. The Qur’an uses the feminine form for faces.

Sufūr concerns uncovering or becoming visible, familiarly revealing a face.

Sāfir can concern a traveler, a scribe or transmitter of writing, or someone uncovering a face. Context distinguishes the uses.

Safarah is a plural of sāfir. The Qur’an uses it for those holding the written sheets; Arabic relates it to scribes and message bearers.

Sifr, with initial i, names a book or written work, especially a large book or recorded contents. Its plural is asfār.

Safīr names a messenger, emissary, or mediator; elsewhere it can name scattered leaves.

Sifārah concerns mediation, conveying messages, and representing parties.

Sufrah can name provisions prepared for a traveler or a cloth on which food is placed.

Sifār names a camel’s nose-band, bridle, or device placed on its nose or head.

The forms differ: safar as travel, sifr as book, sufrah as travel provisions, and safarah as scribes are not interchangeable.

Vowels and word formation matter as much as the shared root letters in Qur’anic translation.

Lisān al-ʿArab, al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and al-Miṣbāḥ: forms and vowels

3. Early Arabic usage

Travel across the land: Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā writes:

كَخَنْسَاءَ سَفْعَاءِ الْمَلَاطِمِ حُرَّةٍ
مُسَافِرَةٍ مَزْؤُودَةٍ أُمِّ فَرْقَدِ

ka-khansāʾa safʿāʾi al-malāṭimi ḥurratin / musāfiratin mazʾūdatin ummi farqadi

“Like a short-nosed wild cow with dark cheeks, free-ranging and moving across the land, startled, the mother of a calf.”

The poet describes an animal moving through the wilderness, ranging from place to place in search or flight.

Musāfirah concerns the animal’s movement across the land, rather than ritual travel, trade, or learning.

Mazʾūdah separately expresses its fright; fear is not part of musāfirah’s meaning.

The verse is recorded in Zuhayr’s poetry and witnesses pre-Qur’anic movement across land. A different transmission reads mushāfirah with shīn. Its use as evidence for S F R depends on musāfirah with sīn; the readings must not be assigned the same root.

Sharḥ mā yaqaʿu fīhi al-taṣḥīf wa-al-taḥrīf: sīn and shīn readings of Zuhayr

Travel in early Arabic thus extends beyond human movement for a special purpose. Ordinary human safar concerns leaving one’s residence and covering a route.

Travel revealing a person’s condition

Early lexical explanations observe that leaving familiar circumstances may reveal otherwise unseen behavior or ability.

Some lexicographers explain the naming of travel through this revealing of a traveler’s qualities.

The image is interesting without becoming travel’s essential definition.

Travel may reveal no new personal trait; a crisis or responsibility at home may reveal hidden qualities.

Such disclosure can result from some journeys without being a necessary component of safar.

Its direct meaning is leaving one’s residence and traversing a route.

Sifārah: making speech clear between parties

Safartu bayna al-qawmi can mean mediating and seeking reconciliation between groups.

A mediator can clarify each side’s position and explain misunderstanding or disagreement.

Safīr as messenger or mediator belongs to this field.

Lexical sources transmit a verse with this meaning:

وَمَا أَدَعُ السِّفَارَةَ بَيْنَ قَوْمِي
وَمَا أَمْشِي بِغِشٍّ إِنْ مَشَيْتُ

wa-mā adaʿu al-sifārata bayna qawmī / wa-mā amshī bi-ghishshin in mashītu

“I do not abandon mediation among my people; when I go among them, I do not walk deceitfully.”

The first hemistich varies and the poet’s attribution is uncertain; the verse cannot confidently be called pre-Islamic.

Independent lexical accounts nevertheless record sifārah as mediation.

Al-Basīṭ: the mediation witness and variant transmissions

This matters for safarah in ʿAbasa, potentially concerning transmission of writing or messages.

Modern diplomacy’s political and administrative institutions must not be projected onto early Arabic.

Sifr: a book or written work

Arabic sifr names a book, especially a large book in some early accounts.

Al-Zajjāj relates its naming to revealing content: writing records knowledge or speech in a readable form.

Sifr thus has a semantic connection with becoming clear.

Ordinary book does not simply mean revealer: its contents need not always be true, clear, or understood.

Nor is it confined to the Torah, although Al-Jumuʿah 62:5 places books in that context.

Related Semitic languages also have similar words for books or writing. This helps historical study without alone determining a borrowing’s source or date.

The established meaning is book; precise historical origins require independent evidence.

4. Travel in the Qur’an: actual movement and differing circumstances

A. ʿAlā safarin: being on a journey

Seven Qur’anic instances mean travel. Five occur as ʿalā safarin: Al-Baqarah 2:184, Al-Baqarah 2:185, Al-Baqarah 2:283, Al-Nisāʾ 4:43, and Al-Māʾidah 5:6.

The construction concerns being in a state of travel rather than merely intending to go somewhere.

It specifies no numerical distance, duration, transport, or purpose.

Arabic safar concerns leaving residence and traversing a route. Conditions for a particular traveling concession require the connected verses and evidence.

The root alone cannot establish one distance or duration for every prescription.

B. Travel in the fasting verses

Al-Baqarah 2:184 and Al-Baqarah 2:185 mention travel alongside illness.

Both address the ill or traveling person through an equivalent number of days at another time.

Al-Baqarah 2:185 explicitly states the principle of ease:

«يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ». (Yurīdu Allāhu bikumu al-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu al-ʿusra): “The God intends ease for you and does not intend hardship.”

The concern is a person’s actual circumstances in worship.

Travel can change routines, fatigue, access to food and water, weather, and opportunities to rest.

These do not all belong to safar’s meaning: a journey may be easy or difficult without the word requiring exhaustion, hunger, or danger.

Travel is mentioned independently alongside illness; neither a traveler nor an ill person must necessarily be the other.

Al-Baqarah 2:184’s alladhīna yuṭīqūnahu is structurally distinct from the ill and traveling.

That distinction matters for fasting, but disputes about ransom and ability cannot be inserted into S F R’s meaning.

Lexically, travel is actual movement away from residence; the fasting verses establish its relevant prescription.

C. Travel, unavailable water, and purification

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 and Al-Māʾidah 5:6 mention travel in purification and tayammum.

Both also mention illness, janābah, and lāmastum al-nisāʾ, before inability to find water. Lāmastum has been interpreted as bodily touching or as an expression for intercourse; this is independent of safar’s meaning.

Travel can thus matter for access to water and purification.

Safar does not itself mean lack of water: a traveler may have enough, while someone at home may have none.

Travel and the condition of unavailable water remain distinct.

The verses’ full wording determines tayammum and washing or wiping; safar alone cannot establish their details.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing interpretations of lāmastum

D. Travel and recording financial obligations

Al-Baqarah 2:283 mentions travel in recording obligations and debt.

After Al-Baqarah 2:282 addresses writing debts and testimony, the next verse addresses travel without finding a scribe.

Travel connects with possible difficulty finding recording materials or a writer.

It does not make writing impossible: the verse separately states inability to find a scribe.

Travel identifies the person’s condition; absence of a scribe identifies a practical recording difficulty.

Rights and trust in financial relationships are concerns supplied by the verse’s other words and constructions.

Safar keeps its ordinary meaning without acquiring a new independent legal sense.

E. Safaran qāṣidan: a moderate journey

Al-Tawbah reads:

لَوْ كَانَ عَرَضًا قَرِيبًا وَسَفَرًا قَاصِدًا لَاتَّبَعُوكَ وَلَٰكِنْ بَعُدَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الشُّقَّةُ وَسَيَحْلِفُونَ بِاللَّهِ لَوِ اسْتَطَعْنَا لَخَرَجْنَا مَعَكُمْ يُهْلِكُونَ أَنْفُسَهُمْ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ إِنَّهُمْ لَكَاذِبُونَ

law kāna ʿaraḍan qarīban wa-safaran qāṣidan la-ittabaʿūka wa-lākin baʿudat ʿalayhimu al-shuqqatu wa-sa-yaḥlifūna bi-Allāhi lawi istaṭaʿnā la-kharajnā maʿakum yuhlikūna anfusahum wa-Allāhu yaʿlamu innahum la-kādhibūna

“Had it been a nearby gain and a moderate, manageable journey, they would have followed you; but the distance seemed far and difficult to them. They will swear by The God, ‘Had we been able, we would have gone out with you.’ They destroy themselves, and The God knows they are liars.”

Al-Tawbah 9:42

Safaran is qualified by qāṣidan.

Qāṣid belongs to Q Ṣ D, here a moderate, direct, or manageable journey.

Manageability comes from the adjective rather than safar itself.

Distance and difficulty contrast easy and difficult travel here.

This does not make difficult journeys inherently objectionable or easy ones more valuable. The verse addresses people weighing the route and immediate benefit when invited to accompany someone.

Travel can carry different descriptions; difficulty, brevity, and ease are not fixed components of safar.

Study of Q Ṣ D

F. Min safarinā hādhā naṣaban: fatigue from a journey

Al-Kahf reads:

فَلَمَّا جَاوَزَا قَالَ لِفَتَاهُ آتِنَا غَدَاءَنَا لَقَدْ لَقِينَا مِنْ سَفَرِنَا هَٰذَا نَصَبًا

fa-lammā jāwazā qāla li-fatāhu ātinā ghadāʾanā la-qad laqīnā min safarinā hādhā naṣaban

“When they passed beyond, he said to his young companion: bring our meal; we have certainly encountered fatigue from this journey.”

Al-Kahf 18:62

Safarinā concerns two people’s journey in Moses’ account.

Naṣaban belongs to N Ṣ B, here fatigue or strain from effort.

The words relate without identity.

Journeying is the activity; fatigue may result.

The verse explicitly states fatigue without establishing that every journey necessarily causes severe exhaustion.

Travel is actual movement with bodily needs such as food and rest, rather than every use being merely spiritual metaphor.

Study of N Ṣ B

5. Asfārinā: journeys and distances

Sabaʾ uses plural asfār for journeys:

فَقَالُوا رَبَّنَا بَاعِدْ بَيْنَ أَسْفَارِنَا وَظَلَمُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ فَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ أَحَادِيثَ وَمَزَّقْنَاهُمْ كُلَّ مُمَزَّقٍ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِكُلِّ صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ

fa-qālū rabbanā bāʿid bayna asfārinā wa-ẓalamū anfusahum fa-jaʿalnāhum aḥādītha wa-mazzaqnāhum kulla mumazzaqin inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūrin

“They said: our Lord, increase the distance between our journeys. They wronged themselves, so We made them tales and scattered them completely. In that are signs for everyone patient and grateful.”

Sabaʾ 34:19

Asfārinā is plural of safar, journey, rather than sifr, book.

Sabaʾ 34:18 describes visible settlements along the route, measured travel stages, and safe movement by night and day.

The subject is journeys, roads, and distances between stopping places.

The next verse reports speech about distances between journeys, followed by self-wronging and dispersal.

Early readings and explanations differ concerning bāʿid and the statement’s interpretation, including requesting greater distances or complaining ungratefully about prior conditions.

This does not turn journeys into books.

Safety, comfort, and route length come from the surrounding verses and words rather than asfār alone.

Asfārinā lexically means “our journeys”; circumstances depend on context.

6. Asfāran: books being carried

Al-Jumuʿah uses the same plural shape differently:

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ حُمِّلُوا التَّوْرَاةَ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا كَمَثَلِ الْحِمَارِ يَحْمِلُ أَسْفَارًا بِئْسَ مَثَلُ الْقَوْمِ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الظَّالِمِينَ

mathalu alladhīna ḥummilū al-tawrāta thumma lam yaḥmilūhā ka-mathali al-ḥimāri yaḥmilu asfāran biʾsa mathalu al-qawmi alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyāti Allāhi wa-Allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-ẓālimīna

“The likeness of those entrusted with the Torah who then did not bear its responsibility is an ass carrying books. Evil is the likeness of people who deny The God’s signs; The God does not guide wrongdoing people.”

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5

Asfāran is plural of sifr, book.

The Torah and an animal physically carrying written books make the sense clear.

Translating it as journeys would distort the comparison.

Physical carrying and accepting a book’s responsibility

The verse repeats Ḥ M L in several constructions.

First come those entrusted with the Torah.

Then their failure to bear it.

An ass physically carrying books follows.

Carrying an object on one’s back differs from accepting and applying its contents.

An animal may carry many books without understanding them.

A person can receive and read a book, bearing responsibility to understand and act.

The verse criticizes those entrusted with the Torah who did not properly fulfill that responsibility and denied divine signs.

The comparison concerns possessing scripture versus benefiting from its guidance, beyond merely numerous books.

Asfār itself does not mean misunderstood books; failure to understand or benefit comes from the comparison.

The verse does not condemn studying, keeping books, or learning generally. It criticizes possession without responsibility toward truth and guidance.

Asfār’s two Qur’anic uses show one plural spelling belonging to nouns differentiated by vowels and meaning: safar, journey, and sifr, book.

7. Asfara and musfirah: becoming visibly bright

A. Wa-al-ṣubḥi idhā asfara: morning’s brightness

Al-Muddaththir reads:

وَالصُّبْحِ إِذَا أَسْفَرَ

wa-al-ṣubḥi idhā asfara

“By the morning when it brightens.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:34

Asfara is a Form IV perfect describing morning becoming bright and clear.

This closely approaches uncovering and becoming visible.

Light increases in the transition from night, making previously dark surroundings visible.

Asfara concerns this brightening.

It specifies no exact minute of dawn or quantity of light.

Those details depend on observation, solar position, and other temporal expressions.

Asfara must not become morning traveling: the shared root does not make the uses identical.

The subject is morning light rather than spatial movement.

B. Wujūhun yawmaʾidhin musfiratun: bright faces

ʿAbasa reads:

وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ مُسْفِرَةٌ

wujūhun yawmaʾidhin musfiratun

“Faces that day will be bright and radiant.”

ʿAbasa 80:38

Musfiratun is a feminine Form IV active participle.

It describes wujūh, faces.

ʿAbasa 80:39 adds laughter and rejoicing; ʿAbasa 80:40 and ʿAbasa 80:41 describe other faces as dusty and dark.

Musfirah therefore concerns facial brightness.

Joy comes from the next verse; musfirah itself does not mean laughing.

Other words describe the second group’s dust and darkness.

The contrast reinforces the sense of brightness.

Musfirah versus sāfirah

Arabic sāfirah can describe a woman who has uncovered her face.

ʿAbasa 80:38 instead reads musfiratun, not sāfiratun.

It is Form IV, describing radiant faces here.

“Uncovered faces” is therefore an inaccurate rendering of this use.

Lexical sufūr concerning an exposed face does not alone establish a clothing prescription.

Vocabulary does not automatically prescribe clothing or a prohibition; that requires the relevant verses and constructions.

The verse concerns faces’ brightness and condition on the Last Day.

8. Bi-aydī safaratin: scribes or message bearers?

ʿAbasa supplies an important use.

Read the consecutive verses:

كَلَّا إِنَّهَا تَذْكِرَةٌ

kallā innahā tadhkiratun

“No indeed; it is a reminder.”

ʿAbasa 80:11

فَمَنْ شَاءَ ذَكَرَهُ

fa-man shāʾa dhakarahu

“Whoever wills may remember it.”

ʿAbasa 80:12

فِي صُحُفٍ مُكَرَّمَةٍ

fī ṣuḥufin mukarramatin

“In honored written sheets.”

ʿAbasa 80:13

مَرْفُوعَةٍ مُطَهَّرَةٍ

marfūʿatin muṭahharatin

“Raised and purified.”

ʿAbasa 80:14

بِأَيْدِي سَفَرَةٍ

bi-aydī safaratin

“In the hands of safarah.”

ʿAbasa 80:15

كِرَامٍ بَرَرَةٍ

kirāmin bararatin

“Noble and righteous.”

ʿAbasa 80:16

The passage concerns remembrance, written sheets, and those holding them.

Safarah is plural of sāfir, with two important explanations: scribes and transmitters of writing or messages.

First reading: scribes and recorders

Arabic records safara al-kitāba meaning writing.

Sāfir as scribe and safarah as scribes belong to this field.

The reading fits the surrounding ṣuḥuf, written sheets.

Sheets, writing, and those dealing with them form a connected field.

Here safarah are those responsible for writing, preserving, or recording the written material.

The word alone determines neither their precise writing, tools, nor number.

Those details are not stated.

Second reading: transmitters and intermediaries

Arabic safīr names a messenger or mediator.

Some early lexicographers and interpreters connect safarah with sifārah.

They are thus people who convey the writing or message to its audience.

This too fits revelation and honored sheets.

Messenger and scribe are not identical: one person can write, another carry, and another read.

These tasks should not be collapsed without explanation.

Who are the safarah?

Early explanations identify angels, scribes, or readers.

A well-known reading identifies angels connected with revelation and written sheets.

Kirāmin bararatin and the sheets’ status suit that reading.

ʿAbasa 80:13, ʿAbasa 80:14, ʿAbasa 80:15, and ʿAbasa 80:16 do not explicitly use the word angels.

Lexical meaning must therefore be distinguished from identifying the referents.

Angels may be defended through the evidence without claiming safarah lexically names only angels.

Likewise “scribes” alone does not determine whether they are human or angelic.

The word identifies a task or relationship; identity requires further study.

Al-Basīṭ: scribes, intermediaries, and identifying safarah

9. Distinctions from related roots

S F R (safara) and K SH F (kashafa)

Kashf concerns removing a covering and revealing.

One field of S F R shares that image.

The roots are not synonymous in every use.

Kashf can remove harm or reveal something, while Qur’anic S F R also concerns travel, morning light, books, and scribes.

Asfara’s temporal use in Al-Muddaththir 74:34 does not give every kashf that same sense.

Study of K SH F

S F R (safar) and R Ḥ L (riḥlah)

Riḥlah and safar approach each other in leaving residence and traveling.

R Ḥ L also concerns migration, preparing travel loads, and movement between places.

Safar ordinarily concerns leaving residence and covering a route.

One event may take both descriptions, while R Ḥ L retains uses concerning loading and moving camp.

Quraysh 106:2 names the winter and summer journeys.

The lexical distinction alone does not make safar always longer or shorter than riḥlah.

Study of R Ḥ L

S F R (safar) and S Y R (sayr)

Sayr concerns movement and proceeding.

Sabaʾ 34:18 uses al-sayr before asfārinā in the next verse.

Their connection helps distinguish them.

Sayr concerns movement itself; safar concerns a journey’s state away from residence.

This is not absolute: travel commonly involves sayr, with overlapping fields.

Study of S Y R

S F R (asfara) and Ṣ B Ḥ (ṣabāḥ)

Ṣubḥ names the beginning of day.

Asfara describes becoming bright and visible.

Al-Muddaththir 74:34 combines morning and its brightening.

Asfara names a condition of morning rather than morning itself.

“Morning becoming bright” preserves the verb better than simply “dawn.”

Study of Ṣ B Ḥ

S F R (asfara) and N W R (nūr)

Nūr names light.

Asfara may describe morning or a face becoming bright.

The entire root does not therefore mean light.

Physical light is no direct component of safar as travel or sifr as book.

Musfirah in ʿAbasa 80:38 concerns bright faces without defining every derivative.

Study of N W R

S F R (sifr) and K T B (kitāb)

Kitāb belongs to K T B, writing and recording.

Sifr also names a book or written work.

The nouns approach each other while belonging to different roots.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5’s asfār are books carried on an animal.

The word alone does not guarantee truth, wisdom, or sound knowledge in their contents.

Content’s validity depends on its subject and source, beyond its name.

Study of K T B

S F R (safarah) and R S L (rasūl)

Rasūl concerns being sent and conveying a message.

One lexical explanation of safarah concerns transmitters of writing or messages.

Someone could be a rasūl and lexically a safīr or message bearer.

The words are nevertheless not complete synonyms.

Safarah in ʿAbasa 80:15 accompanies written sheets and concerns writing or transmission.

Qur’anic rasūl has its own field of being sent and commissioned.

Identifying the safarah requires context rather than substituting rusul.

Study of R S L

10. Morphological and structural notes

Safar occurs seven times; five follow ʿalā, expressing a state of travel.

Al-Tawbah 9:42 qualifies it with qāṣidan.

Al-Kahf 18:62’s safarinā attaches the first-person plural pronoun, identifying a particular journey.

Sabaʾ 34:19’s asfārinā is plural of safar, our journeys.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5’s asfāran is plural of sifr, books.

The spellings match while the singulars’ vowels and meanings differ.

Al-Muddaththir 74:34’s asfara is a Form IV perfect with al-ṣubḥ as subject.

ʿAbasa 80:38’s musfiratun is a feminine Form IV active participle describing wujūhun.

ʿAbasa 80:15’s safaratin is plural of sāfir in bi-aydī safaratin.

Bi-aydī expresses possession or location in hands without identifying every task or the holders’ identity.

Safarah differs from sufrah, travel provisions; musfirah, bright, differs from musāfirah, traveling.

Shared radicals do not erase derivatives’ morphological and semantic differences.

11. Challenges in understanding and translation

Must Qur’anic travel be difficult and exhausting?

No.

Al-Tawbah 9:42 describes moderate travel, while Al-Kahf 18:62 explicitly states fatigue.

Difficulty and fatigue can result from travel without constituting its fixed meaning.

Fasting and purification verses identify travel independently; their wording establishes the concession.

Does travel require a specified numerical distance?

The root alone specifies none.

Arabic safar concerns leaving residence and traversing a route.

Distance, duration, and relevant legal criteria do not follow from the noun alone.

Modern short and long journeys cannot be arbitrarily excluded from Qur’anic Arabic without examination.

Does asfār always mean books?

No.

Sabaʾ 34:19 means journeys.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5 means books.

The singular’s vowel and context distinguish plural safar from plural sifr.

One sense cannot be transferred into the other verse.

Must the safarah in ʿAbasa be angels?

Angels are a significant and defensible interpretation.

The word lexically concerns scribes or transmitters of writing and messages.

The verse does not explicitly name angels; identifying them requires Qur’anic evidence.

This distinguishes meaning from identity without rejecting angels as the referent.

Is sufūr the same as Qur’anic isfār?

No.

Arabic sufūr concerns uncovering. This noun itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

Isfār is the Form IV verbal noun, also absent as such from the Qur’an. The Qur’an instead uses asfara for morning and musfirah for bright faces.

They approach each other in becoming visible while differing in form and use.

Facial sufūr alone does not name a religious clothing prescription.

Does sifr guarantee clear and true contents?

No.

Its lexical explanation relates a book to recording and clarifying contents.

A book may be true or false, understood or misunderstood.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5 itself distinguishes physically carrying a book from understanding and applying it.

Sifr names a book; truth, understanding, and responsibility require independent contextual evidence.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

Safar ordinarily suits “travel” or “journey.”

ʿAlā safarin in Al-Baqarah 2:184, Al-Baqarah 2:185, Al-Baqarah 2:283, Al-Nisāʾ 4:43, and Al-Māʾidah 5:6 suits “on a journey.”

Al-Tawbah 9:42’s safaran qāṣidan suits “a moderate, manageable journey”; the adjective’s contribution remains separate.

Al-Kahf 18:62’s min safarinā hādhā naturally means “from this journey of ours.”

Sabaʾ 34:19’s asfārinā means “our journeys.”

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5’s asfāran means “books,” not journeys. “Large books” may fit where warranted, without arbitrarily making physical size the comparison’s focus.

Asfara in Al-Muddaththir 74:34 suits “became bright and clear.”

Musfirah in ʿAbasa 80:38 suits bright, radiant, or bright and open; “uncovered faces” is inaccurate here.

Safarah in ʿAbasa 80:15 may be “scribes” or “transmitters of writing,” following contextual judgment. Retaining safarah with a short explanation can suit an unresolved identification.

Non-Qur’anic safīr suits emissary, messenger, or mediator.

Sifārah suits mediation or conveying messages. Modern diplomacy draws on this field without its entire institutional structure belonging to early usage.

Basic translation conveys the established sense; root explanation supplies the image of uncovering.

13. Summary

S F R concerns uncovering, becoming visible, and revealing what was hidden.

Related uses include brightening morning, uncovered faces, traveling, writing and recording books, and conveying messages.

It occurs twelve times in the Qur’an.

Seven uses concern actual human travel, alongside fasting, purification, financial obligations, difficult routes, or fatigue; none of those accompanying conditions is always part of travel itself.

Sabaʾ 34:19’s plural means journeys, while Al-Jumuʿah 62:5 means books. Singular vowels and context determine the sense.

Al-Muddaththir 74:34 describes morning brightening; ʿAbasa 80:38 describes radiant faces.

ʿAbasa 80:15 concerns those holding honored written sheets, lexically connected with scribes and transmitters; their precise identity requires further evidence.

The brief root meaning can therefore be stated as:

Uncovering and becoming visible; established branches include traveling, brightening, books, and conveying writing or messages.

This does not make every journey a discovery of truth, every book guiding scripture, or musfirah always a physical covering removed.

Each derivative retains its established meaning. Root study clarifies their connections while preserving their actual differences.
