1. Introducing the root: does qatl always mean killing?

The Arabic three-consonant root Q T L, in its familiar meaning, concerns killing and ending a living being’s life. Its derivatives and usages do not all mean actual killing, however. Arabic distinguishes qatala, killing; qātala, fighting and confrontation; iqtatala, becoming engaged in conflict; and figurative or imprecatory expressions such as qātalahumu Allāh.

This distinction is fundamental to understanding the Qur’an. Qātilū does not necessarily command killing everyone on the opposing side: it concerns fighting and confrontation. Fighting may result in deaths, but engaging in battle differs from actually killing. Two groups may fight and then end the conflict through retreat, cessation, or peace.

Conversely, where uqtulū occurs in a clear context of actual killing, it should not be converted without evidence into mere struggle, conversation, or disagreement.

The distinction is clear in Al-Baqarah 2:191 and Al-Baqarah 2:193. In verse 191, wa-uqtulūhum comes from Form I qatala and directly means “kill them”; in verse 193, wa-qātilūhum is Form III and means “fight them.”

Verse 191 itself places both forms together: first wa-uqtulūhum, then wa-lā tuqātilūhum and ḥattā yuqātilūkum. These differing choices distinguish killing from fighting within one military context.

“Slaughter” and “struggle” also require distinction. Persian koshtār emphasizes actual killing, often of many people. Struggle can confront an enemy, prevent aggression, remove oppression, or pursue a goal without necessarily causing anyone’s death.

Persian mobārezeh, “struggle,” is broader than qitāl. It can be intellectual, political, social, or armed, whereas qitāl in clearly military Qur’anic contexts concerns actual fighting. Qitāl should therefore neither be reduced to intellectual debate nor invariably converted into slaughter and annihilation of the opposition.

This matters particularly in the context of Al-Baqarah 2:190, Al-Baqarah 2:191, Al-Baqarah 2:192, and Al-Baqarah 2:193. The Qur’an first commands fighting those who fight you and forbids transgression. It then discusses killing, expulsion, and fitnah, conditions fighting at the Sacred Mosque on the opposing side’s fighting there, and considers the end of fitnah and aggression.

Although wa-uqtulūhum means actual killing here, its scope cannot be separated from the conditions of battle, its pronoun’s referent, the prohibition of transgression, and the instruction concerning cessation.

The central point is that Q T L is a linguistic family with several related usages, while “killing,” “fighting,” “slaughter,” and “struggle” do not have one meaning. Accurate translation requires the verb’s morphology, object, accompanying particles, and verse context. Brief root meaning: Killing and ending life; qitāl: fighting and confrontation, with figurative and condemnatory uses in particular contexts.

2. Uqtulūhum and qātilūhum in Al-Baqarah 2:190, Al-Baqarah 2:191, Al-Baqarah 2:192, and Al-Baqarah 2:193

Al-Baqarah 2:190: fighting and the prohibition of transgression

وَقَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُعْتَدِينَ

wa-qātilū fī sabīli Allāhi alladhīna yuqātilūnakum wa-lā taʿtadū inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu al-muʿtadīna

Proposed translation based on this study: “And fight in the way of The God those who fight you, and do not transgress; indeed, The God does not love transgressors.”

Qātilū and yuqātilūnakum are both Form III.

The first commands fighting; the second identifies those who fight against the addressees.

Wa-lā taʿtadū follows. Fighting is therefore not presented here as unconditional or unlimited.

The prohibition shows that a military confrontation does not itself authorize every treatment of the opposing side.

Qātilū here also does not mean “kill them.” Fighting can result in killing, but the verb emphasizes the act of fighting.

Al-Baqarah 2:191: killing in a military context

وَاقْتُلُوهُمْ حَيْثُ ثَقِفْتُمُوهُمْ وَأَخْرِجُوهُم مِّنْ حَيْثُ أَخْرَجُوكُمْ ۚ وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَشَدُّ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ ۚ وَلَا تُقَاتِلُوهُمْ عِندَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ حَتَّىٰ يُقَاتِلُوكُمْ فِيهِ ۖ فَإِن قَاتَلُوكُمْ فَاقْتُلُوهُمْ ۗ كَذَٰلِكَ جَزَاءُ الْكَافِرِينَ

wa-uqtulūhum ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum wa-akhrijūhum min ḥaythu akhrajūkum wa-al-fitnatu ashaddu mina al-qatli wa-lā tuqātilūhum ʿinda al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi ḥattā yuqātilūkum fīhi fa-in qātalūkum fa-uqtulūhum kadhālika jazāʾu al-kāfirīna

Proposed translation based on this study: “And kill them wherever you encounter them, and expel them from where they expelled you; fitnah is more severe than killing. Do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque until they fight you there. If they fight you, then kill them. Such is the recompense of the disbelievers.”

The distinction between the two verbal constructions is evident here.

Wa-uqtulūhum concerns killing, while wa-lā tuqātilūhum concerns fighting.

Ḥattā yuqātilūkum fīhi follows: until they fight you there.

Then comes fa-in qātalūkum fa-uqtulūhum: if they fight you, then kill them.

Within this verse, the Qur’an thus distinguishes the opposing side’s initiating fighting from permission to kill during that confrontation.

This does not permit killing under every circumstance.

The preceding verse concerns fighting those who fight and forbids transgression. Verse 191 itself mentions the addressees’ expulsion, fitnah, and the prohibition on initiating fighting at the Sacred Mosque.

The pronoun hum cannot therefore be extended without this context to everyone with different beliefs.

Conversely, restricting violence does not require converting the clear uqtulūhum into “talk to them” or “merely struggle.” The Qur’an itself uses qātilū for fighting.

The distinction is that permission to kill in a military situation does not command annihilating everyone on the opposing side irrespective of their conduct and circumstances.

Al-Baqarah 2:192: the possibility of cessation

فَإِنِ انتَهَوْا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

fa-ini intahaw fa-inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Proposed translation based on this study: “But if they desist, then indeed, The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

This short verse matters for understanding the verses immediately before and after it.

Intahaw belongs to N H Y and here concerns desisting or bringing an action to an end.

The preceding context concerns fighting, expulsion, fitnah, and transgression. Desisting should not be detached from that setting without reason. Intahaw itself leaves the object of desisting unstated; commentaries such as Al-Ṭabarī also discuss desisting from fighting and disbelief. This study’s reading of ending hostility should remain distinct from that exegetical account.

Al-Ṭabarī: desisting from fighting and disbelief

The verse indicates that a military situation can end.

If these verses intended the exceptionless killing of the opposing side, the instruction concerning cessation and ending conflict would lose its meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:193: fighting until fitnah ends

وَقَاتِلُوهُمْ حَتَّىٰ لَا تَكُونَ فِتْنَةٌ وَيَكُونَ الدِّينُ لِلَّهِ ۖ فَإِنِ انتَهَوْا فَلَا عُدْوَانَ إِلَّا عَلَى الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-qātilūhum ḥattā lā takūna fitnatun wa-yakūna al-dīnu lillāhi fa-ini intahaw fa-lā ʿudwāna illā ʿalā al-ẓālimīna

Proposed translation based on this study: “And fight them until there is no fitnah and religion is for The God. If they desist, there is no aggression except against wrongdoers.”

Qātilūhum appears again, rather than uqtulūhum.

This distinction matters.

The Qur’an describes continued fighting until a situation called fitnah ends. It then again considers the opposing side’s desisting and states that aggression should not continue once that situation ends.

The command does not literally specify killing every opponent as its goal. Qitāl is presented with an objective and conditions for its end.

Fitnah requires separate study. Fighting, expulsion from homes, and religious pressure appear in this context, supporting a reading involving persecution, pressure, and obstruction of religion. Al-Ṭabarī and a number of other commentators, however, interpret fitnah here as polytheism. This article’s persecution-and-pressure reading is therefore a contextual analysis, rather than the sole lexical meaning or an undisputed interpretation.

Al-Ṭabarī: fitnah as polytheism, distinguished from this article’s contextual reading

Wa-yakūna al-dīnu lillāh should also not be separated from the Qur’anic principle of no compulsion in religion. Ending pressure upon people’s faith differs from forcing a belief upon them by the sword.

A complete examination must also consider Al-Baqarah 2:256, Yūnus 10:99, Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8, and Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:9.

The verse should nevertheless not be reduced to intellectual struggle alone. Qātilūhum here concerns actual fighting, with an objective and limits.

A distinction translation must preserve

These four verses contain several separate concepts.

Qātilū commands fighting; uqtulūhum commands killing in a military setting; akhrijūhum concerns expulsion; intahaw concerns desisting and cessation; and lā taʿtadū sets an ethical and practical limit on conduct.

These words cannot replace one another without reason.

Rendering them all as “kill” erases the distinction between fighting and killing. Rendering them all as “struggle” removes the explicit meaning of actual killing.

Both lexical distinctions and contextual continuity should therefore be preserved.

This prevents unlimited extension of killing commands while also avoiding denial of actual violence in verses concerning warfare and killing.

3. Qur’anic forms and morphology

Q T L occurs 170 times in eight morphological categories. Its principal forms are Form I qatala, Form II qattala, Form III qātala, Form VIII iqtatala, and nouns or verbal nouns such as qatl, qitāl, and qatlā. The eighth category is the verbal noun taqtīl. The Quranic Arabic Corpus classifies 83 Form I verbs, four Form II verbs, 54 Form III verbs, four Form VIII verbs, 13 qitāl nouns, one qatlā noun, 10 qatl verbal nouns, and one taqtīl verbal noun.

Quranic Arabic Corpus:170 occurrences in eight categories

Qatala: killing

In its primary use, the Form I verb concerns ending a living being’s life.

Qatala is active perfect; yaqtulu is its imperfect.

Qutila is passive and concerns being killed.

The verb’s basic meaning is killing, but the agent, object, and circumstances follow from the sentence.

Qattala: the doubled form

Qattala is Form II.

The Qur’an uses it in contexts emphasizing intense killing or collective slaughter, such as killing the sons of the Children of Israel in Al-Aʿrāf 7:127 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:141.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:61 also contains quttilū taqtīlan.

The doubled verb with an emphatic verbal noun can emphasize the act’s intensity.

Form II alone does not establish a particular number of victims or a fixed number of repetitions.

Victim numbers, repetition, and extent require contextual evidence.

Qātala: fighting

Qātala is Form III.

In familiar Qur’anic usage, it concerns fighting and confrontation in qitāl.

Fighting can result in killing, but qātala alone does not establish that a killing occurred.

Al-Baqarah 2:190 uses it for fighting those who fight the addressees.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9 uses it for confronting the transgressing group.

In both, fighting relates to the opposing side’s conduct and the circumstances of confrontation.

Iqtatala: becoming engaged in conflict

Iqtatala is Form VIII.

Qur’anic uses concern conflict between people or groups, as in Al-Baqarah 2:253, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15, and Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15, two men are fighting, but one man’s killing is expressed later by another verb.

Iqtatala and qatala therefore differ in this context.

Qatl and qitāl

Qatl is the verbal noun of killing.

Qitāl is Form III’s verbal noun and means fighting.

This is a real phonetic and morphological distinction in Arabic.

Al-qatl in Al-Baqarah 2:191 concerns killing; al-qitāl in Al-Baqarah 2:217 concerns fighting.

Translation must preserve the distinction.

Qatlā: those killed

Qatlā is a plural referring to people who have been killed.

Al-Baqarah 2:178 uses it in discussing qiṣāṣ.

The word alone does not determine whether the killing was intentional or accidental, who the killer was, or what response the offense requires.

These matters depend on context and other legal evidence.

4. The semantic core in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْقَافُ وَالتَّاءُ وَاللَّامُ أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى إِذْلَالٍ وَإِمَاتَةٍ.

al-qāfu wa-al-tāʾu wa-al-lāmu aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā idhlālin wa-imātatin

Translation: “Qāf, tāʾ, and lām form a sound principle indicating subduing or overpowering, and causing death.”

This identifies uses concerning overpowering or eliminating something’s strength or intensity, alongside ending life.

It does not make every killing mean merely humiliating.

Someone unjustly killed is not thereby morally degraded. The killer may instead be committing injustice.

Idhlāl in Ibn Fāris’s definition thus matters for some uses without replacing the clear meaning of killing.

Ibn Fāris: subduing and causing death

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

الْقَتْلُ مَعْرُوفٌ، قَتَلَهُ يَقْتُلُهُ قَتْلًا.

al-qatlu maʿrūfun qatalahu yaqtuluhu qatlan

Meaning: “Qatl is familiar: he killed him, he kills him, killing.”

Other explanations describe causing death through a blow, stone, poison, or another cause.

A specific method is therefore not an invariant part of the root’s meaning.

Death can occur through a blow, poison, or another means, with qatala still applicable.

The root itself also does not specify intent. Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:93 add qualifications distinguishing accidental from intentional killing.

Lisān: killing, differing means, and intent

5. Figurative uses and pre-Islamic Arabic witnesses

Love that kills in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays states:

أَغَرَّكِ مِنِّي أَنَّ حُبَّكِ قَاتِلِي
وَأَنَّكِ مَهْمَا تَأْمُرِي الْقَلْبَ يَفْعَلِ

a-gharraki minnī anna ḥubbaki qātilī / wa-annaki mahmā taʾmurī al-qalba yafʿali

Translation: “Has it made you proud that love of you lays me low, and that my heart does whatever you command?”

Early commentary on Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah: love that kills

Qātilī here belongs to the language of love.

The poet describes love’s intense effect, rather than actual death from a blow or weapon.

Love is pictured as powerful enough to lay him low.

This attests figurative usage in pre-Islamic Arabic.

It does not remove physical killing from every other text.

A heart laid low

Another verse in the same Muʿallaqah states:

وَمَا ذَرَفَتْ عَيْنَاكِ إِلَّا لِتَضْرِبِي
بِسَهْمَيْكِ فِي أَعْشَارِ قَلْبٍ مُقَتَّلِ

wa-mā dharafat ʿaynāki illā li-taḍribī / bi-sahmayki fī aʿshāri qalbin muqattali

Translation: “Your eyes shed tears only so that you might strike, with your two arrows, the parts of a heart laid low.”

Muqattal describes a heart under love’s influence in the poet’s image.

Early commentaries also explain it as subdued or laid low by love.

This is not a heart physically cut apart with weapons. Love, eyes, and the arrows of a glance establish the figurative meaning.

The poem’s own texture therefore distinguishes direct from figurative usage.

Al-Basīṭ: muqattal heart and Arabic witnesses

A trained animal in Zuhayr’s poetry

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

كَأَنَّ عَيْنَيَّ فِي غَرْبَيْ مُقَتَّلَةٍ
مِنَ النَّوَاضِحِ تَسْقِي جَنَّةً سُحُقًا

ka-anna ʿaynayya fī gharbay muqattalatin / mina al-nawāḍiḥi tasqī jannatan suḥuqan

Translation: “As though my two eyes were in the two great buckets of a trained, docile camel, one of the water-drawing camels irrigating a grove of tall palms.”

Muqattalah describes a living, experienced camel.

It cannot mean that the animal was actually killed: the animal is working within the image.

Repeated work has made it trained and accustomed to its task.

This shows certain early derivatives extending beyond ending life to overpowering, pressure, or taming. Suḥuqan here concerns tall palms, rather than a distant garden; the two buckets picture the abundance of the poet’s tears.

Al-Basīṭ: two buckets, a trained camel, and tall palms in Zuhayr’s witness

Reducing a drink’s strength

Arabic records qatala al-sharāba or qatala al-khamra for mixing a drink with water and reducing its potency.

No living being is killed here.

An intensity is reduced and, figuratively, eliminated.

This fits the overpowering-and-subduing branch in Ibn Fāris’s explanation.

It does not make killing humans in the Qur’an merely reducing their power or intensity.

Thorough knowledge of a subject

Lisān al-ʿArab records qataltu al-shayʾa khubran wa-ʿilman.

This concerns knowing a subject thoroughly and comprehensively.

Persian too can describe someone as investigating a matter until nothing remains unexplored.

The verb extends here from the physical field to mastery and comprehensive knowledge.

This matters for Al-Nisāʾ 4:157, but should not be transferred without evidence to every qatala.

Lisān: diluted drink and comprehensive knowledge

Findings from the early witnesses

Imruʾ al-Qays and Zuhayr indicate that, before the Qur’an, Q T L was familiar both for actual killing and for figurative love, being overcome, and taming.

Figurative usage nevertheless requires contextual indications.

In “her love killed me,” the setting clarifies the metaphor.

In an account of someone killed in battle, a child’s killing, or death from a blow, actual killing takes precedence unless clear evidence indicates otherwise.

6. Human killing, accidental killing, and intentional killing

Killing in the account of Adam’s two sons

Al-Māʾidah 5:30

فَطَوَّعَتْ لَهُ نَفْسُهُ قَتْلَ أَخِيهِ فَقَتَلَهُ فَأَصْبَحَ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

fa-ṭawwaʿat lahu nafsuhu qatla akhīhi fa-qatalahu fa-aṣbaḥa mina al-khāsirīna

Proposed translation based on this study: “Then his own self made killing his brother easy for him; so he killed him and became one of the losers.”

The verbal noun qatl and the verb fa-qatalahu appear together.

Killing first appears in relation to the person’s decision, then its occurrence is stated.

Nothing indicates a mere difference of opinion or defeat in debate.

The subject is ending the brother’s life.

Accidental killing

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 discusses a believer killed by mistake and specifies responsibilities and reparations.

Khaṭaʾan is a decisive qualification.

A killing occurred without an intention to kill.

Accidental killing does not mean an absence of death: the distinction concerns intent and circumstances.

Intentional killing

Al-Nisāʾ 4:93

وَمَن يَقْتُلْ مُؤْمِنًا مُّتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاؤُهُ جَهَنَّمُ خَالِدًا فِيهَا وَغَضِبَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَلَعَنَهُ وَأَعَدَّ لَهُ عَذَابًا عَظِيمًا

wa-man yaqtul muʾminan mutaʿammidan fa-jazāʾuhu jahannamu khālidan fīhā wa-ghaḍiba Allāhu ʿalayhi wa-laʿanahu wa-aʿadda lahu ʿadhāban ʿaẓīman

Proposed translation based on this study: “Whoever intentionally kills a believer, his recompense is Hell, remaining therein; The God is angry with him, curses him, and has prepared a great punishment for him.”

Mutaʿammidan states that the killing is intentional.

This qualification identifies the type of killing. Yaqtul alone cannot determine the agent’s intention; the presence of a qualification does not, however, invariably rule out emphasis or clarification of a meaning already present.

Comparing Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 with Al-Nisāʾ 4:93 distinguishes the event of killing from the agent’s intent.

This is fundamental to legal assessment.

Killing children

The Qur’an discusses or prohibits killing children in Al-Anʿām 6:140, Al-Anʿām 6:151, Al-Isrāʾ 17:31, and Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12.

Some of these verses mention fear of poverty as a motive.

Motive is not part of qatl’s lexical meaning.

Qatl ends life; fear of poverty and other motives concern its circumstances.

Killing should therefore not be restricted to one particular method or motive.

7. The inviolability of life, qiṣāṣ, and individual responsibility

Al-Māʾidah 5:32: the importance of human life

Al-Māʾidah 5:32, in a ruling prescribed for the Children of Israel, compares unjustly killing one person with killing all people.

This does not state an actual victim count.

Someone who killed one person has physically killed one human being, but the Qur’an places the act in a wider relationship to express the gravity of violating life.

The verse also contains bi-ghayri nafsin aw fasādin fī al-arḍ.

That qualification should remain part of the analysis.

Its presence does not allow any individual or group to accuse someone of corruption and take their life without establishing an offense.

Right, establishing an offense, and responsibility require independent examination.

Al-qatlā in Al-Baqarah 2:178

Al-Baqarah 2:178

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْقِصَاصُ فِي الْقَتْلَى ۖ الْحُرُّ بِالْحُرِّ وَالْعَبْدُ بِالْعَبْدِ وَالْأُنْثَىٰ بِالْأُنْثَىٰ ۚ فَمَنْ عُفِيَ لَهُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ شَيْءٌ فَاتِّبَاعٌ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَأَدَاءٌ إِلَيْهِ بِإِحْسَانٍ ۗ ذَٰلِكَ تَخْفِيفٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ وَرَحْمَةٌ ۗ فَمَنِ اعْتَدَىٰ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ فَلَهُ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū kutiba ʿalaykumu al-qiṣāṣu fī al-qatlā al-ḥurru bi-al-ḥurri wa-al-ʿabdu bi-al-ʿabdi wa-al-unthā bi-al-unthā fa-man ʿufiya lahu min akhīhi shayʾun fa-ittibāʿun bi-al-maʿrūfi wa-adāʾun ilayhi bi-iḥsānin dhālika takhfīfun min rabbikum wa-raḥmatun fa-mani iʿtadā baʿda dhālika fa-lahu ʿadhābun alīmun

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “O you who have believed, qiṣāṣ concerning those killed has been prescribed for you: the free for the free, the enslaved for the enslaved, and the female for the female. Whoever has something remitted to him by his brother, pursuit should be according to what is right and payment to him with kindness. This is a lightening from your Lord and a mercy. Whoever then transgresses will have a painful punishment.”

Al-qatlā refers to those killed.

Al-qiṣāṣ belongs to a different root and should not be equated with qatl.

In legal usage, qiṣāṣ concerns pursuing an offense, establishing responsibility, and responding proportionately. In its familiar legal sense, it also denotes commensurate retaliation for an injury; it should not be reduced merely to any investigation or pursuit. The verse itself mentions pardon and payment alongside qiṣāṣ.

Al-qiṣāṣu fī al-qatlā should therefore not be reduced without examination to a command for reciprocal killing in every circumstance.

Conversely, al-qatlā means those killed, rather than merely injured people or participants in a conflict.

This distinction matters for justice.

After a killing, it is necessary to establish who died, who acted, whether the killing was intentional or accidental, and each person’s degree of responsibility.

Qatl itself does not answer all these questions.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:33 also prohibits excess in killing.

Even where the victim’s and survivors’ rights are at issue, the Qur’an does not permit unlimited revenge.

Forqan examines this in detail in “Qiṣāṣ and Forgiveness in the Qur’an: Justice, Individual Responsibility, and the Path to Pardon.”

Study: Qiṣāṣ and Forgiveness in the Qur’an

8. Qitāl, struggle, and confronting oppression

Fighting in the way of The God

Many verses accompany qitāl with fī sabīli Allāh.

Adding this phrase does not change qātala’s lexical meaning.

In a military setting, qātala still means fighting; fī sabīli Allāh specifies the action’s direction and relationship.

Assessing its legitimacy requires the aim, addressees, circumstances, and limits of conduct.

No individual or government can justify every form of violence merely by calling its war “the way of The God,” without Qur’anic evidence.

Supporting the oppressed

Al-Nisāʾ 4:75 connects fighting with supporting men, women, and children trapped in an oppressive setting who seek rescue and help.

Fighting can thus be presented as removing oppression and protecting people under pressure.

The root itself does not mean supporting the oppressed.

Supporting them is the purpose identified for fighting in that verse.

Fighting and reconciliation between two groups

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9

وَإِنْ طَائِفَتَانِ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ اقْتَتَلُوا فَأَصْلِحُوا بَيْنَهُمَا ۖ فَإِنْ بَغَتْ إِحْدَاهُمَا عَلَى الْأُخْرَىٰ فَقَاتِلُوا الَّتِي تَبْغِي حَتَّىٰ تَفِيءَ إِلَىٰ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ ۚ فَإِنْ فَاءَتْ فَأَصْلِحُوا بَيْنَهُمَا بِالْعَدْلِ وَأَقْسِطُوا ۖ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُقْسِطِينَ

wa-in ṭāʾifatāni mina al-muʾminīna iqtatalū fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā fa-in baghat iḥdāhumā ʿalā al-ukhrā fa-qātilū allatī tabghī ḥattā tafīʾa ilā amri Allāhi fa-in fāʾat fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā bi-al-ʿadli wa-aqsiṭū inna Allāha yuḥibbu al-muqsiṭīna

Proposed translation based on this study: “If two groups of believers fight one another, reconcile them. If one transgresses against the other, fight the transgressing group until it returns to the command of The God. If it returns, reconcile them justly and act equitably; indeed, The God loves the equitable.”

The verse uses iqtatalū and fa-qātilū.

First it describes two groups fighting and commands reconciliation.

If one group transgresses against the other, confrontation with the transgressor is commanded.

The objective is a return from transgression, rather than annihilating every group member.

After cessation, just reconciliation is commanded again.

This clearly illustrates fighting as a means of confronting aggression in a particular situation, giving way to reconciliation when aggression ends.

Is qitāl always direct self-defense?

Al-Baqarah 2:190 connects fighting those who fight the addressees with a prohibition of transgression.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:75 concerns helping the oppressed.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9 concerns confronting a transgressing group to restore justice.

These settings differ.

Al-Tawbah 9:29 also mentions fighting a group of the People of the Book until payment of jizyah. That verse has its own construction and subject: all the conditions of Al-Baqarah 2:190 cannot be attributed to its wording without examining it.

Conversely, it cannot establish permission to fight every person with different beliefs regardless of conduct or circumstances.

Qitāl’s meaning is clear, while its conditions and limits require all relevant verses.

Fighting and freedom of faith

Al-Baqarah 2:256 states the principle of no compulsion in religion.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8 and Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:9 distinguish those who fought because of religion and expelled people from their homes from those who did not.

Religious disagreement and a state of war therefore differ.

Someone may oppose another’s belief, but disagreement alone is not the same Qur’anic situation as aggression, expulsion, and warfare.

Qitāl should therefore not become an instrument for imposing faith upon human conscience.

Forqan examines this separately in “There Is No Compulsion in Religion; the Messenger’s Duty Is Delivery.”

Study: No Compulsion and the Duty of Delivery

9. Fighting, treaties, and ending warfare

Al-Tawbah 9:5 and its treaty context

Al-Tawbah 9:5 uses fa-uqtulū.

Its direct meaning is killing.

The opening verses concern treaties, the declaration of disassociation, and relations with different groups of polytheists.

Al-Tawbah 9:4 exempts those faithful to their treaties from the preceding situation.

Al-Tawbah 9:6 commands giving protection to a polytheist who seeks it and conveying him to safety.

Al-Tawbah 9:12 and Al-Tawbah 9:13 also concern broken treaties and initiating hostility.

Fa-uqtulū therefore cannot be detached from this context and made a universal command to kill every polytheist in every era.

Rejecting that generalization does not require changing killing into conversation or intellectual struggle.

The verb’s meaning remains, while the verses’ conditions determine its scope.

Fighting and an offer of peace

Al-Anfāl 8:61 discusses the other side’s inclination to peace and commands the addressee to incline to it too.

Withdrawal from fighting and an offer of peace also matter for conduct toward the opposing group in Al-Nisāʾ 4:90.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91 presents different circumstances: a group refusing withdrawal, peace, and restraint of its hands.

These differences show the opposing side’s situation matters for continuing conflict.

A command of killing or fighting therefore cannot be generalized to every circumstance without examining cessation of hostility, peace, and treaties.

Fa-in intahaw and the end of hostility

Al-Baqarah 2:192 and Al-Baqarah 2:193 concern ending the conduct at issue.

In verse 193, fa-in intahaw is followed by no aggression except against wrongdoers.

Fighting thus has an endpoint in this context.

Fighting until fitnah ends differs from killing every opponent.

Ending fighting does not erase every responsibility arising from injustice and crime. Justice and accountability may remain at issue.

Ending warfare and addressing violated rights are therefore distinct matters too.

10. Being killed in the way of The God, distinguished from fighting

Al-Nisāʾ 4:74

فَلْيُقَاتِلْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ الَّذِينَ يَشْرُونَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا بِالْآخِرَةِ ۚ وَمَن يُقَاتِلْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ فَيُقْتَلْ أَوْ يَغْلِبْ فَسَوْفَ نُؤْتِيهِ أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

fa-l-yuqātil fī sabīli Allāhi alladhīna yashrūna al-ḥayāta al-dunyā bi-al-ākhirati wa-man yuqātil fī sabīli Allāhi fa-yuqtal aw yaghlib fa-sawfa nuʾtīhi ajran ʿaẓīman

Proposed translation based on this study: “Let those who sell worldly life for the Hereafter fight in the way of The God. Whoever fights in the way of The God and is killed or prevails, We shall soon give him a great reward.”

This is particularly clear evidence for the distinction between qitāl and qatl.

Yuqātil first appears, meaning fighting.

Two possible outcomes follow: being killed or prevailing.

Someone can therefore fight without being killed.

If yuqātil itself meant being killed, that distinction within the same sentence would make no sense.

The verse clearly identifies qitāl as the action of fighting, rather than death as its outcome.

Being killed and dying

Al-Ḥajj 22:58 describes people who emigrated in the way of The God, then were killed or died.

The expressions occur separately.

Mawt is the end of life, while qatl concerns life ending through another agent or action.

Every killing involves death, but not every death is killing.

Those killed in the way of The God

Al-Baqarah 2:154 and Āl ʿImrān 3:169 discuss those killed in the way of The God.

They do not deny physical killing; they concern the reality of those people’s life with their Lord.

The nature of life after death does not follow from Q T L alone.

“Killed in the way of The God” should also not be equated without examination with every use of SH H D and shahīd.

Qutila means being killed; fī sabīli Allāh specifies its direction and relationship.

11. Killing animals

Al-Māʾidah 5:95 prohibits killing game while in iḥrām.

The same verse makes intentional killing of game a matter of responsibility and compensation.

Qatala is therefore not used only for humans in the Qur’an.

Killing an animal and killing a human nevertheless differ legally and contextually.

Qatl and dhabḥ

Arabic dhabḥ concerns a particular way of slaughtering an animal.

Naḥr likewise identifies a particular method in animal-related usage.

Qatl is the broader term for ending life.

An animal may be killed by dhabḥ, but not every animal killing is dhabḥ.

For example, an animal killed by an arrow while hunting can linguistically be described through qatl without that act being called dhabḥ.

This distinction matters for studies of permitted and forbidden food.

Qatl’s lexical meaning alone, however, does not determine the complete ruling on whether the meat is permissible.

12. Imprecatory and condemnatory expressions

Qutila al-insānu

ʿAbasa 80:17

قُتِلَ الْإِنْسَانُ مَا أَكْفَرَهُ

qutila al-insānu mā akfarahu

Proposed translation based on this study: “May man perish! How ungrateful and denying he is!”

Qutila is passive perfect.

The sentence nevertheless belongs to a setting of severe condemnation.

Arabic can use such a construction for an imprecation or curse.

It should not be treated merely as a historical report that a particular person was killed.

Persian equivalents meaning “may he perish” or “may he be destroyed” better convey this function.

Similar uses in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:10, Al-Muddaththir 74:19, Al-Muddaththir 74:20, and Al-Burūj 85:4 also require their own contexts.

Qātalahumu Allāh

Al-Tawbah 9:30

وَقَالَتِ الْيَهُودُ عُزَيْرٌ ابْنُ اللَّهِ وَقَالَتِ النَّصَارَى الْمَسِيحُ ابْنُ اللَّهِ ۖ ذَٰلِكَ قَوْلُهُم بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ يُضَاهِئُونَ قَوْلَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِن قَبْلُ ۚ قَاتَلَهُمُ اللَّهُ أَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ

wa-qālati al-yahūdu ʿUzayrun ibnu Allāhi wa-qālati al-naṣārā al-masīḥu ibnu Allāhi dhālika qawluhum bi-afwāhihim yuḍāhiʾūna qawla alladhīna kafarū min qablu qātalahumu Allāhu annā yuʾfakūna

Proposed translation based on this study: “The Jews said, ‘ʿUzayr is the son of The God,’ and the Christians said, ‘The Messiah is the son of The God.’ That is their saying with their mouths, resembling what earlier disbelievers said. May The God destroy them; how they are turned away from the truth!”

Qātalahumu Allāh is Form III.

The combination is nevertheless a recognized Arabic expression of imprecation and severe condemnation.

It should not be translated as physical warfare between God and humans.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains it through imprecation, cursing, or destruction.

Lisān: imprecatory and condemnatory expressions

Form III therefore does not always express actual warfare between two parties.

Conversely, this imprecatory use does not make qātilū in Al-Baqarah 2:190 or Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9 merely cursing.

Each combination’s meaning follows from its own construction and context.

13. Wa-mā qatalūhu yaqīnan: killing and comprehensive knowledge

Al-Nisāʾ 4:157

وَقَوْلِهِمْ إِنَّا قَتَلْنَا الْمَسِيحَ عِيسَى ابْنَ مَرْيَمَ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ وَمَا صَلَبُوهُ وَلَٰكِن شُبِّهَ لَهُمْ ۚ وَإِنَّ الَّذِينَ اخْتَلَفُوا فِيهِ لَفِي شَكٍّ مِّنْهُ ۚ مَا لَهُم بِهِ مِنْ عِلْمٍ إِلَّا اتِّبَاعَ الظَّنِّ ۚ وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ يَقِينًا

wa-qawlihim innā qatalnā al-masīḥa ʿĪsā ibna Maryama rasūla Allāhi wa-mā qatalūhu wa-mā ṣalabūhu wa-lākin shubbiha lahum wa-inna alladhīna ikhtalafū fīhi la-fī shakkin minhu mā lahum bihi min ʿilmin illā ittibāʿa al-ẓanni wa-mā qatalūhu yaqīnan

Proposed translation based on this study: “And because of their saying, ‘We killed the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, the messenger of The God’—whereas they neither killed nor crucified him, but it became confused for them. Those who differed about him are surely in doubt concerning it; they have no knowledge of it, only the following of conjecture; and certainly they did not kill him.”

Killing appears several times in this verse.

The beginning concerns a claim to have killed Jesus and the denial of that claim.

The final wa-mā qatalūhu yaqīnan, however, follows discussion of doubt, knowledge, and conjecture.

Lexical sources record qataltu al-shayʾa ʿilman for thoroughly knowing a subject.

Some lexicographers have therefore considered whether the final expression in Al-Nisāʾ 4:157 concerns comprehensive knowledge and attaining certainty.

Another familiar reading treats it as emphasizing the denial of Jesus’s killing.

These readings differ in subject.

The first emphasizes the absence of certain knowledge concerning the claim.

The second emphasizes a definitive denial of the killing’s occurrence.

Assessing them requires yaqīnan’s role, the pronoun’s referent, and the final sentence’s connection with the statements about knowledge and conjecture.

For a root study, the reliable finding is that figurative qatl for comprehensive knowledge is attested in Arabic and matters for examining this verse.

Al-Naḥḥās, Maʿānī al-Qurʾān: the comprehensive-knowledge reading at Al-Nisāʾ 4:157

It does not authorize converting every occurrence of killing in the verse into knowing or defeating.

14. Comparison with related roots

Q T L (qatl) and M W T (mawt)

Study of M W T

Mawt concerns life ending.

Qatl concerns ending life, or an event in which a being is killed through another agent or action.

Every killing results in death, but not every death is killing.

Al-Ḥajj 22:58 distinguishes being killed from dying.

Q T L (qatl) and S F K (safk)

Study of S F K

Safk concerns pouring liquid and, in Qur’anic use, shedding blood.

Qatl concerns ending life.

Killing may involve bloodshed, but not every killing necessarily causes visible bleeding.

Conversely, bleeding can occur without death.

Forqan’s separate S F K study examines this distinction.

Q T L (qitāl) and Ḥ R B (ḥarb)

Arabic ḥarb concerns warfare and a state of hostility.

Military qitāl concerns the act of fighting and confrontation.

They can be close without always playing the same role.

Ḥarb may describe the wider state of war, while qitāl concerns engagement within it.

This should nevertheless not become an exceptionless rule.

Q T L (qitāl) and J H D (jihād)

Arabic jahd concerns exertion and using one’s ability.

Qur’anic jihād can relate to warfare in some contexts, but its uses are not all restricted to armed fighting.

Qitāl in clearly military Qur’anic settings, however, concerns fighting.

Not every jihād is necessarily qitāl, nor is every qitāl legitimate in the way of The God merely because it bears that name.

An action’s direction and worth require context and Qur’anic criteria.

Q T L (qatl) and Ḍ R B (ḍarb)

Study of Ḍ R B

Ḍarb has a broad semantic field, including striking in one use.

A blow can cause killing, but not every blow is killing.

Killing can also occur without a direct blow.

The roots can connect in particular events without being complete synonyms.

Q T L (qitāl) and D F ʿ (dafʿ)

Dafʿ concerns driving away, repelling, or preventing something’s advance.

Āl ʿImrān 3:167 places qātilū and idfaʿū in one expression.

Their proximity shows fighting and repelling can connect in a military situation.

Dafʿ is nevertheless not always restricted to armed warfare.

Someone can repel a danger through a nonlethal action too.

Q T L (qatl) and Q Ṣ Ṣ (qiṣāṣ)

Study of Q Ṣ Ṣ

Qiṣāṣ belongs to Q Ṣ Ṣ.

In legal usage, it concerns pursuing an offense, establishing responsibility, and responding proportionately.

Killing can be the subject of qiṣāṣ, but the words differ in meaning.

Killing is the event in which a human life ends; qiṣāṣ concerns addressing it and responding justly.

Q T L (qatl) and SH H D (shahīd)

Study of SH H D

Shahīd belongs to SH H D and concerns witnessing and presence.

Qatīl and qatlā concern those killed.

In later religious terminology, shahīd often names someone killed in the way of The God.

This terminological use should not be imposed on every Qur’anic shahīd.

The roots require independent study.

15. Translation proposals for Forqan

Forms within Q T L require distinction.

Qatala primarily means “killed,” and yaqtulu means “kills.”

Qutila means “was killed.”

Qatl concerns “killing.”

Qatlā means “those killed.”

Military qātala means “fought,” and qātilū means “fight.”

Qitāl means “fighting,” or “warfare” where context supports it.

Iqtatalū can mean “fought one another” or “became engaged in conflict,” according to context.

For qattala, “carried out slaughter” is suitable where context establishes killing’s intensity or extent.

That intensity should not be added from the verbal pattern alone, without evidence.

Imprecatory expressions require different equivalents.

Qutila al-insānu in ʿAbasa 80:17 can be rendered “May man perish,” given its condemnatory setting.

Qātalahumu Allāh in Al-Tawbah 9:30 is imprecatory; “May The God destroy them” conveys its meaning.

For figurative qatala al-shayʾa ʿilman, “knew a subject thoroughly” is defensible.

That usage should not become every verb in the family’s fixed meaning.

Translating uqtulūhum and qātilūhum

Wa-uqtulūhum in Al-Baqarah 2:191 is rendered “and kill them” in the basic text.

Wa-qātilūhum in Al-Baqarah 2:193 is rendered “and fight them.”

This distinction must remain.

Uqtulūhum in a military setting concerns actual killing, but the conditions for the command follow from the verses together.

Qātilūhum concerns fighting without necessarily meaning killing every opponent.

Persian “struggle” may fit some contextual explanations. Since struggle can be unarmed, however, “fight” is more precise for qātilū in military verses.

“Slaughter” should likewise not become qitāl’s fixed equivalent: slaughter emphasizes people’s being killed, while qitāl names confrontation and fighting themselves.

16. Conclusion

Arabic Q T L concerns killing, ending life, overpowering, and, in some figurative uses, eliminating something’s strength or intensity.

Its forms nevertheless differ in meaning.

Qatala primarily concerns killing; military qātala concerns fighting and confrontation; iqtatala concerns parties engaging in conflict; and doubled qattala in some contexts emphasizes slaughter’s intensity or extent.

Pre-Islamic witnesses also attest figurative usage alongside actual killing. Imruʾ al-Qays compares love with killing and being overcome, while Zuhayr uses a derivative for a trained, experienced animal.

These usages are real, but their figurative meanings depend on their own indications.

The Qur’an uses the root 170 times. Some verses concern killing humans or animals; others concern fighting. Qutila al-insānu and qātalahumu Allāh also show imprecatory and condemnatory constructions.

The distinction between qatl and qitāl is especially clear in Al-Baqarah 2:190, Al-Baqarah 2:191, Al-Baqarah 2:192, and Al-Baqarah 2:193.

Al-Baqarah 2:190 commands fighting those who fight you and forbids transgression.

Al-Baqarah 2:191 discusses killing in a military setting, expelling those who expelled the addressees, and the prohibition on initiating fighting at the Sacred Mosque.

Al-Baqarah 2:192 considers desisting from the conflict.

Al-Baqarah 2:193 connects fighting with the end of fitnah and forbids continued aggression.

Together these passages distinguish fighting from killing. Permission to kill in a military situation also differs from an unlimited command to slaughter every opponent.

Conversely, explicit killing in verses actually concerning it should not be converted into intellectual struggle or conversation alone.

Qatl also differs from mawt. Mawt expresses life ending; qatl concerns life ended through another action or agent. Qualifications and context establish intent and responsibility, as in the Qur’an’s distinction between accidental and intentional killing.

Legal and ethical distinctions also separate killing, fighting, qiṣāṣ, bloodshed, defense, revenge, and martyrdom. They can connect in one event without any taking the place of all the others’ meanings.

For Forqan, “killing” is qatala’s primary equivalent, and “fighting” is qātala’s in military usage. “Slaughter” fits actual killing and, where indicated, its extent. “Struggle” is broader than qitāl and should not replace actual fighting without evidence.

Qatl, qitāl, and slaughter are not one concept. Killing ends life; qitāl concerns fighting and confrontation; slaughter emphasizes actual killing, particularly on a large scale. Each action’s legitimacy, limits, and outcome require context and clear Qur’anic instructions.

Preserving these distinctions makes verses concerning warfare and human life more precise, without turning every struggle into slaughter or every killing command into a merely figurative expression.
