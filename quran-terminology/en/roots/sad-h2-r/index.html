Kinship through marriage and melting through heat: two semantic branches in Arabic and the Qur’an

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Ṣ H R has two established semantic branches. One concerns kinship formed between people and families through marriage; the other concerns melting, liquefying, and the effects of intense heat on matter.

The branches share their written form and root letters, but have different meanings in their Qur’anic uses.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in two forms:

  • يُصْهَرُ (yuṣharu): is melted or liquefied; Al-Ḥajj 22:20.
  • صِهْرًا (ṣihran): kinship through marriage; Al-Furqān 25:54.

Other derivatives are also known in Arabic:

  • صِهْر (ṣihr): a relative through marriage; someone who becomes connected by marriage.
  • أَصْهَار (aṣhār): plural of ṣihr; relatives through marriage.
  • صُهَرَاء (ṣuharāʾ): another, less common plural of ṣihr.
  • صَاهَرَ (ṣāhara): formed a marriage connection with a family or group.
  • مُصَاهَرَة (muṣāharah): kinship and family connection arising through marriage.
  • أَصْهَرَ (aṣhara): established kinship through marriage; in some early uses, drew close or formed a bond carrying mutual regard.
  • صَهَرَ (ṣahara): melted or liquefied something through heat.
  • اِنْصَهَرَ (inṣahara): melted or became liquid.
  • صَهِير (ṣahīr): something melted or cooked through heat.
  • صُهَارَة (ṣuhārah): melted substance, particularly liquefied fat.
  • اِصْطَهَرَ (iṣṭahara): concerning fat, melted it or used the melted fat.

Not all these derivatives occur in the Qur’an. Its only forms are yuṣharu and ṣihran.

2. Two semantic branches in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris’s analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two semantic principles for Ṣ H R: kinship and melting something.

This distinction matters. Unlike roots whose varied branches can be traced to one basic meaning through clear evidence, this family has two different, established uses.

In the first, a relationship forms between people. In the second, heat changes a material’s condition.

Bringing two families together might be compared figuratively with blending two things. Such an analogy alone, however, does not establish that kinship-related ṣihr historically arose from the verb for melting.

Both semantic fields should therefore remain in the definition without forcing one to derive from the other.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: kinship and melting as two principles

Family connections through marriage

In early Arabic, صِهْر (ṣihr) denotes kinship arising through marriage.

When a man marries a woman, for example, new bonds form between him and her family, and between her and his family.

These differ from kinship through birth.

Two brothers may share a father and mother, whereas a father-in-law and son-in-law become connected through marriage.

Both are family relationships, but their origins differ.

Arabic therefore distinguishes nasab from ṣihr.

The scope of ṣihr

Early sources differ in exactly whom they call aṣhār.

An explanation transmitted from Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad distinguishes relatives on the two sides of a marriage, using different terms for the woman’s and man’s families.

Al-Aṣmaʿī, by contrast, treats ṣihr as a broad term embracing relatives on both sides.

Arabic also records aḥmāʾ and akhtān for relatives of a wife and husband, although lexicographers differ over their precise scope.

Ṣihr should therefore not be said to mean only a father-in-law or only a son-in-law throughout Arabic.

Its broader usage can include relatives on both sides of the marriage.

Persian likewise distinguishes a son-in-law, a wife’s father and mother, a husband’s father and mother, men married to sisters, and a relative through marriage, each naming a particular connection.

Arabic ṣihr, however, can be a more general term for kinship through marriage.

Muṣāharah: a connection between families

صَاهَرَ (ṣāhara) concerns establishing a marriage connection with a family or group.

صَاهَرَ القَوْمَ (ṣāhara al-qawma), for example, means that he became related to them through marriage.

Muṣāharah names this relationship.

Two people marry, thereby bringing their families into new kinship relationships as well.

Marriage and muṣāharah are nevertheless not precisely the same concept.

Marriage concerns the spouses’ marital relationship. Muṣāharah also emphasizes the kinship arising from it.

Lexical sources sometimes apply أصهر بهم (aṣhara bihim) to establishing a bond carrying mutual regard through neighborhood, descent, or marriage.

This broader usage shows that approaching others and forming respected relationships belongs to the family’s range. Its familiar family-related sense, however, remains kinship through marriage.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ, and Al-Muḥkam: ṣihr and relatives on both sides of marriage

3. Early Arabic witnesses concerning kinship

A witness from Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes people’s standing and distinction:

قَوْدُ الْجِيَادِ وَإِصْهَارُ الْمُلُوكِ وَصَبْرٌ
فِي مَوَاطِنَ لَوْ كَانُوا بِهَا سَئِمُوا

qawdu al-jiyādi wa-iṣhāru al-mulūki wa-ṣabrun / fī mawāṭina law kānū bihā saʾimū

Translation:

“Leading fine horses, forming kinship with kings, and steadfastness in situations where others would have wearied.”

Iṣhāru al-mulūki here concerns marriage and kinship connections with royal families.

Such a connection could mark social standing and relationships among influential families in Arab society.

The lexical meaning of iṣhār is not itself kingship, political power, or moral superiority. Those associations come from the verse’s subject.

The witness shows that Ṣ H R was known for family connections through marriage in pre-Islamic Arabic poetry.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: Zuhayr’s witness concerning kinship with kings

Family and social connections

In tribal societies, marriage could create a bond between families or groups as well as between husband and wife.

This sometimes led to cooperation, support, visiting, and new social responsibilities.

In such settings, muṣāharah could help strengthen relationships between families.

That social outcome should not become the word’s fixed meaning. Muṣāharah primarily names a kinship relationship. Whether it leads to friendship, cooperation, disagreement, or rivalry is another matter.

Kinship through marriage alone likewise does not make one family superior to another.

4. Ṣahara: melting and liquefying

Melting fat and other substances

In the second branch, صَهَرَ (ṣahara) denotes melting or liquefying something through heat.

Early Arabic uses the expression:

صَهَرَ الشَّحْمَ (ṣahara al-shaḥma)

It means that he melted the fat.

Solid or semisolid fat softens and becomes fluid when heated.

Melted fat is also called صُهَارَة (ṣuhārah).

اِنْصَهَرَ (inṣahara) likewise means that something melted or was liquefied.

Heat causes the material’s change of condition in this usage.

Ṣahr should not be restricted to melting metals. Early sources clearly attest melted fat, connecting the word with daily life, food preparation, and the use of fat.

Modern usage for metals and industrial substances draws on the same semantic field.

Ṣahr and cooking with heat

Some Arabic uses also connect this family with cooking or roasting meat.

Ṣahīr may describe something cooked or melted by heat.

The boundary between melting and intense heat’s effects in language has therefore not always been as restricted as modern scientific definitions.

Fat may melt, for example, whereas meat may cook, burn, or undergo structural changes under heat.

Both conditions could fall within this family’s range in early Arabic.

Every use should therefore not automatically be restricted to a solid’s complete conversion into liquid in the modern scientific sense.

A witness from Ibn Aḥmar

The poet Ibn Aḥmar al-Bāhilī, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, describes a young bird on open, level ground:

تَرْوِي لَقًى أُلْقِيَ فِي صَفْصَفٍ
تَصْهَرُهُ الشَّمْسُ فَمَا يَنْصَهِرْ

tarwī laqan ulqiya fī ṣafṣafin / taṣharuhu al-shamsu fa-mā yanṣahir

Approximate translation:

“She brings water to a chick cast upon an open plain; the sun melts it, yet it endures and does not melt.”

تَصْهَرُهُ الشَّمْسُ (taṣharuhu al-shamsu) here concerns intense sunlight and heat.

The poet compares the heat to melting, without the chick literally turning to liquid.

Ṣahr can therefore express heat’s intense effect even without physical melting in a strict sense.

Ibn Aḥmar experienced both pre-Islamic and Islamic times. The date of this particular verse is not independently established, however, so it should not be assigned certainly to the pre-Islamic period without evidence.

The witness helps establish the breadth of ṣahr’s early Arabic usage.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Muḥkam, and Lane: Ibn Aḥmar’s witness and the reading tarwī

Study of Ṣ F Ṣ F

Does ṣahr always mean destruction?

Melting may change a material’s original form, but that change does not necessarily mean its complete destruction.

Melted fat still exists as a substance, although its condition has changed.

Molten metal can likewise take shape again after cooling.

Intense heat may, by contrast, burn, damage, or destroy an object’s structure.

Melting, burning, structural damage, and complete destruction should therefore be distinguished.

Ṣahr alone does not establish all these consequences in every use. The material, heat’s intensity, and sentence context determine the particular result.

5. First Qur’anic occurrence: yuṣharu in Al-Ḥajj 22:20

Al-Ḥajj states:

يُصْهَرُ بِهِ مَا فِي بُطُونِهِمْ وَالْجُلُودُ

yuṣharu bihi mā fī buṭūnihim wa-al-julūdu

“What is in their bellies, and their skins, are melted by it.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:20

The formation of yuṣharu

يُصْهَرُ (yuṣharu) is a Form I passive imperfect verb.

The construction foregrounds what undergoes melting, rather than the agent performing it.

Bihi refers to something in the preceding verse.

Al-Ḥajj 22:19 states:

يُصَبُّ مِنْ فَوْقِ رُءُوسِهِمُ الْحَمِيمُ

yuṣabbu min fawqi ruʾūsihimu al-ḥamīmu

“Boiling water is poured from above their heads.”

The pronoun in bihi therefore refers to al-ḥamīm: intensely hot, boiling liquid.

The following verse describes its effect on what is in their bellies and on their skins.

The source of heat is thus clear in context, even though yuṣharu is passive.

Study of Ḥ M M

Melting in this verse

Ṣahr here concerns the extreme effects of heat.

Mā fī buṭūnihim denotes what is inside their bellies.

Al-julūdu denotes the skins.

Both relate to yuṣharu in the sentence.

The word alone should not yield every physical detail of this punishment.

In natural science, heat does not affect fat, skin, living tissue, and other materials identically. Some melt, some burn, some change structure, and some decompose at different temperatures.

The Qur’an here portrays melting through intense heat, rather than supplying a laboratory report on temperatures required to liquefy every bodily tissue.

“Are melted” preserves the central meaning. A specified temperature, the liquid’s chemical composition, or each tissue’s transformation cannot be obtained from the verse itself.

The surrounding verses

Al-Ḥajj 22:19 introduces two opposing groups, then describes punishment for those who denied.

Verse 20 continues that description through the image of melting.

Al-Ḥajj 22:21 also mentions iron maces.

Yuṣharu thus forms part of a connected depiction of punishment.

The root itself does not mean denial, punishment, or moral judgment. Those concepts come from the verses’ context.

When ṣahr describes melting fat in ordinary Arabic, that act has no fixed moral value in itself.

Here the same field of melting appears in a description of punishment.

6. Second Qur’anic occurrence: nasaban wa-ṣihran

Al-Furqān states:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ مِنَ الْمَاءِ بَشَرًا فَجَعَلَهُ نَسَبًا وَصِهْرًا وَكَانَ رَبُّكَ قَدِيرًا

wa-huwa alladhī khalaqa mina al-māʾi basharan fa-jaʿalahu nasaban wa-ṣihran wa-kāna rabbuka qadīran

“He is the One who created a human being from water, then gave him ties of descent and marriage; and your Lord is powerful.”

Al-Furqān 25:54

The meaning of ṣihran

صِهْرًا (ṣihran) is an accusative noun here, appearing alongside نَسَبًا (nasaban).

They express two kinds of human relationship.

Nasab concerns family connections through birth and lineage.

Ṣihr concerns a bond arising through marriage.

A child’s connection with their parents through birth, for example, is one of descent.

A son-in-law’s connection with his wife’s family, or a daughter-in-law’s with her husband’s family, arises through marriage and belongs to muṣāharah.

The verse thus concerns more than reproduction and children coming into being; it also describes family ties forming.

Fa-jaʿalahu nasaban wa-ṣihran

The pronoun hu in fa-jaʿalahu refers to basharan in this construction.

The verse says that God created a human being from water and gave him ties of descent and marriage.

It presents humans as beings situated within a network of family relationships, rather than living only as isolated individuals.

Birth connects a person with parents and relatives by descent.

Marriage also creates other relationships that may connect two families.

Nasab and ṣihr are distinct categories here, but do not necessarily denote two completely separate groups of people.

One person can have ties of descent to their children and ties through marriage to their spouse’s family at the same time.

The verse therefore expresses two kinds of relationship rather than dividing people into two fixed, separate classes.

Is nasab exclusive to men and ṣihr to women?

Some commentarial explanations relate nasab to men and ṣihr to women.

The verse’s construction itself, however, does not state such exclusivity.

Women and men both have ties of descent through birth.

Both may likewise become related to their spouse’s relatives through marriage.

Restricting nasab to men and ṣihr to women therefore requires evidence beyond the words themselves.

The direct, defensible sense is the existence of two kinds of family bond.

Are marriage and muṣāharah identical?

In the Qur’an, marriage concerns a relationship between spouses, whereas muṣāharah also denotes the kinship arising from it.

When two people marry, their connection extends beyond themselves: their families may gain relationships such as father-in-law, mother-in-law, son-in-law, and daughter-in-law.

Ṣihr itself does not specify the quality of married life, degree of affection, financial rights, or how marital disagreements should be resolved.

These subjects require examination of other verses.

“Ties of marriage” in Forqan’s translation is therefore defensible. Lexical explanation should clarify its breadth as kinship arising through marriage.

Creation from water and the emergence of relationships

The verse begins:

خَلَقَ مِنَ الْمَاءِ بَشَرًا

khalaqa mina al-māʾi basharan

“Created a human being from water.”

This expression concerns the origin of human creation.

Early commentaries often relate the water here to reproductive fluid. Other Qur’anic verses also describe the relationship between water and life.

For Ṣ H R itself, the central point is the transition from creating human beings to establishing ties of descent and marriage.

From a source of life arise people who form relationships across generations.

Birth creates ties of descent, and marriage enables new family connections.

The verse presents these relationships as signs of God’s power, ending with وَكَانَ رَبُّكَ قَدِيرًا (wa-kāna rabbuka qadīran), “and your Lord is powerful.”

Ṣihr itself, however, does not mean creation from water or reproduction. It names one kind of bond arising among people.

Al-Ṭabarī: creation from water, descent, and marriage kinship

7. Muṣāharah and Qur’anic marriage rulings

Al-Nisāʾ 4:23 identifies groups of relatives whom a person is prohibited from marrying.

Some relationships concern descent, such as a mother, daughter, or sister.

Others concern marriage, such as a spouse’s mother or a son’s wife.

The same verse also discusses relationships arising through breastfeeding.

These verses show that family relationships can have different origins and different implications for marriage rulings.

Ṣihr in Al-Furqān 25:54 should nevertheless not be converted directly into the complete list of prohibited marriage partners.

Ṣihr names a kinship relationship. Prohibitions on marrying particular people are separate legal rulings to be derived from the relevant verses.

Nor should every relationship through breastfeeding be called muṣāharah lexically merely because it appears in the same body of rulings.

Early commentaries sometimes divide lists of prohibited marriages into nasab and ṣihr. Those legal classifications do not eliminate every lexical distinction among descent, kinship through marriage, and breastfeeding.

For root study, the essential point is that muṣāharah denotes kinship arising through marriage; its legal consequences require separate examination.

8. Distinctions from related roots

Ṣ H R (ṣihr) and N S B (nasab)

Nasab concerns kinship through birth and lineage.

Ṣihr in family usage denotes a bond arising through marriage.

The words appear together in Al-Furqān 25:54.

Their proximity shows the Qur’an distinguishing two kinds of family relationship.

This does not mean families related by birth and marriage are completely separate. A marriage can connect families, and its children have ties of descent to both sides.

Ṣ H R (ṣihr) and Z W J (zawj)

Z W J concerns pairing in the Qur’an, with uses extending beyond family relationships.

Al-Rūm 30:21 describes spouses being created, finding tranquility with them, and affection and mercy established between them.

Zawj here concerns the spousal relationship.

Ṣihr also relates to marriage, but denotes the kinship arising from it.

Marriage can therefore establish muṣāharah, while the words remain lexically distinct.

Study of Z W J

Ṣ H R (ṣihr) and R Ḥ M (raḥim)

Raḥim in its material sense denotes the womb, where a fetus develops within the mother’s body.

The same family also concerns kinship and familial bonds.

Ṣihr, by contrast, concerns a connection arising through marriage.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:1 speaks of regarding kinship through al-arḥām.

This word should not be equated with muṣāharah without contextual examination.

One family may include both ties of the womb and ties through marriage, but their origins and names differ.

Study of R Ḥ M

Ṣ H R (ṣahara) and DH W B (dhāba)

Dhawb in Arabic concerns something becoming liquid or fluid.

Ṣahr in its melting branch also concerns liquefaction, particularly when heat changes a material’s condition.

In صهرت الشحم (ṣahartu al-shaḥma), the act of causing melting is specified.

Dhāba, however, can express the material’s change of condition without always specifying its method or cause in the verb itself.

This difference is apparent in ordinary usage but should not become an absolute rule for all Arabic.

Ṣahr can also describe intense sunlight figuratively without actual melting.

Ṣ H R (ṣahara) and Ḥ R Q (ḥaraqa)

Ḥ R Q concerns burning.

Ṣ H R in its heat-related branch concerns melting and liquefying.

Intense heat may cause both melting and burning or damage, but these are different outcomes.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:20, “are melted” preserves the verb’s direct meaning.

“Are destroyed” or “are completely burned,” by contrast, may exceed the root’s meaning unless the context supports that explanation.

9. Translation suggestions for Forqan

صِهْر (ṣihr): a relative through marriage; family connection arising through marriage.

أَصْهَار (aṣhār): relatives through marriage.

صَاهَرَ (ṣāhara): formed a connection with a family through marriage.

مُصَاهَرَة (muṣāharah): kinship arising through marriage.

أَصْهَرَ (aṣhara): became related to a family through marriage; in other uses, formed a bond or drew close to someone.

صَهَرَ (ṣahara): melted or liquefied; in some uses, burned or cooked through intense heat.

يُصْهَرُ (yuṣharu): is melted or liquefied.

اِنْصَهَرَ (inṣahara): melted or became liquid.

صَهِير (ṣahīr): melted or cooked through heat.

صُهَارَة (ṣuhārah): melted substance, particularly melted fat.

For the two Qur’anic occurrences:

Al-Ḥajj 22:20

يُصْهَرُ بِهِ مَا فِي بُطُونِهِمْ وَالْجُلُودُ

yuṣharu bihi mā fī buṭūnihim wa-al-julūdu

“What is in their bellies, and their skins, are melted by it.”

Al-Furqān 25:54

فَجَعَلَهُ نَسَبًا وَصِهْرًا

fa-jaʿalahu nasaban wa-ṣihran

“Then He gave him ties of descent and marriage.”

Both renderings are suitable; there is no need to merge their meanings.

“Are melted” in Al-Ḥajj 22:20 conveys the effect of heat.

“Ties of marriage” in Al-Furqān 25:54 conveys the principal meaning. Lexical explanation should clarify that it includes kinship connections arising through marriage.

10. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ H R has two established semantic branches: kinship through marriage and melting through heat.

In the first, ṣihr denotes a bond forming between individuals and families through marriage. This differs from nasab, established through birth and lineage.

In the second, ṣahr concerns liquefying and melting. Early uses apply it to melting fat, intense sunlight, and cooking through heat.

Each branch occurs only once in the Qur’an.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:20, yuṣharu describes punishment and the effect of boiling liquid on the body.

In Al-Furqān 25:54, ṣihran appears alongside nasaban, presenting two kinds of family bond.

Families coming together can be compared figuratively with two things blending. That image does not sufficiently establish a shared historical origin for the branches, however. The definition should therefore retain both attested meanings.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Kinship and family connection through marriage; in another branch, melting or liquefying something through heat.

Meaning in Al-Ḥajj 22:20:

يُصْهَرُ (yuṣharu): is melted; concerning what is in their bellies and their skins, subjected to intense heat through boiling liquid.

Meaning in Al-Furqān 25:54:

صِهْرًا (ṣihran): kinship through marriage; alongside descent, one of the family relationships among people.
