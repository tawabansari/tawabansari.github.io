Decrease, subsiding, and loss of quantity: from water and tears to changes in the womb and measure in creation

In classical Arabic, غ ي ض concerns decreasing, subsiding, and a reduction in quantity. غَاضَ is used when water, another liquid, or something else diminishes from its previous amount or subsides. Gradual change is possible, but the verb alone specifies no rate.

A familiar expression is غَاضَ الْمَاءُ: water decreased, subsided, or disappeared from its previous location. غَاضَ الدَّمْعُ concerns diminishing tears, and غَاضَ ثَمَنُ السِّلْعَةِ a fall in a commodity’s price.

These usages show that the root is not confined to water sinking into the ground. Water may disappear from the surface through infiltration, but diminishing tears, milk, or prices need not involve anything entering the earth.

Its semantic core is therefore a reduction in quantity or subsiding relative to a previous condition. Disappearance, drying up, or becoming inaccessible may follow such reduction, but none is necessary in every use.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran: غِيضَ in Hūd 11:44 and تَغِيضُ in Al-Raʿd 13:8.

The first passage describes water subsiding after Noah’s flood. The second concerns what wombs diminish and what they increase.

The passages share a semantic core but differ in what diminishes. Noah’s story concerns water; Al-Raʿd concerns decrease and increase in gestation and what is within wombs.

1. Meaning in classical Arabic

غاض الماء is a familiar Arabic expression for diminishing water, whether in a spring, pool, stream, or another place where water collects.

If a pool loses some water and its level falls during a dry period, its water can be described as having subsided.

Early lexicons explain غاض through نَقَصَ and نَضَبَ: decreased and receded.

Some explanations also use غارَ فَذَهَبَ: sank into the ground and disappeared from its previous location.

These expressions are related without being identical.

Decrease is a change in quantity.

Sinking into the ground is one process that may reduce surface water.

Drying up can result from a severe reduction or the exhaustion of water in a particular place.

غاض alone therefore does not establish that something necessarily entered the ground or was completely destroyed.

A poetic witness attributed to Burj ibn Mushir al-Ṭāʾī

The Ḥamāsah anthology records a verse attributed to Burj ibn Mushir al-Ṭāʾī, a poet associated with the pre-Islamic period:

إِلَى اللَّهِ أَشْكُو مِنْ خَلِيلٍ أَوَدُّهُ
ثَلَاثَ خِلَالٍ كُلُّهَا لِيَ غَائِضُ

ilā allāhi ashkū min khalīlin awadduhu / thalātha khilālin kulluhā liya ghāʾiḍu

Translation: “To The God I complain of a friend I love: three traits of his, each of which troubles me and diminishes my strength.”

غَائِض describes traits that distress and weaken the speaker.

An early commentary explains that these traits diminish a person’s energy and strength, just as water may diminish.

There is, however, an important disagreement over the word’s reading and meaning.

Ibn Jinnī suggested that the intended word was غَائِظ with ẓāʾ: something that causes anger or distress, with ẓāʾ replaced by ḍād in pronunciation or transmission.

Ibn Sīdah instead allowed غَائِض with ḍād to be an independent form of غ ي ض, expressing a reduction of someone’s strength or rights.

The line should therefore not be presented as undisputed evidence for the root’s meaning.

If غائض is accepted, the line shows decrease applying to human strength, energy, or condition as well as water.

If the original reading was غائظ, it would belong to the separate family غ ي ظ.

Historical research must preserve this disagreement.

Burj ibn Mushir is associated with the pre-Islamic period, but this particular line’s date has not been independently established. It is valuable evidence of a usage reported in the Arabic poetic tradition, rather than proof of its precise date.

Diminishing tears in a verse attributed to Jarīr

A line recorded in the poetry attributed to the Umayyad poet Jarīr reads as follows. Its attribution is disputed: Ibn Rashīq reports it as belonging to al-Maʿlūṭ al-Saʿdī in Al-ʿUmdah.

غَيَّضْنَ مِنْ عَبَرَاتِهِنَّ وَقُلْنَ لِي
مَاذَا لَقِيتَ مِنَ الْهَوَى وَلَقِينَا

ghayyaḍna min ʿabarātihinna wa-qulna lī / mādhā laqīta mina al-hawā wa-laqīnā

Translation: “They lessened their tears and said to me: what have you suffered from love, and what have we suffered?” Lexical commentary explains the decrease here as letting tears flow until they are exhausted; the translation should not necessarily suggest holding back crying.

Here غَيَّضْنَ has been explained as depleting tears through shedding them.

The subject is tears flowing from the eye, rather than water in a pool or river.

Elsewhere, غَيَّضَ الدَّمْعَ may express restraining or reducing tears, but that general use should remain distinct from the explanation of this particular witness.

The root thus applies to liquids other than ordinary water.

Jarīr lived after the Quranic period, and the line’s attribution is itself disputed. It cannot establish pre-Quranic usage independently; its value is in showing the recorded use concerning reduced tears.

Decrease in quantity and value

Early lexicons also record غَاضَ ثَمَنُ السِّلْعَةِ: the commodity’s price fell.

This no longer concerns liquid or something entering the ground.

A price can fall below its previous level without a physical process being involved.

Arabic also records غَاضَ الْكِرَامُ وَفَاضَ اللِّئَامُ: the noble became fewer and the ignoble more numerous.

Ghayḍ and fayḍ are opposed here.

The first expresses decrease and the second abundance.

The root can therefore concern quantities of water, tears, prices, and numbers of people.

The noun and sentence structure identify what decreases; the root alone does not.

2. Semantic core and root forms

غ ي ض has a weak middle radical.

In غَاضَ, the middle yā becomes alif within the verb’s pattern; it remains visible in the verbal noun غَيْض and certain other derivatives.

The basic verbal sequence is:

غَاضَ يَغِيضُ غَيْضًا

It means to decrease or subside.

The principal recorded forms include:

غَاضَ: decreased, subsided, or fell below its previous quantity.

يَغِيضُ: decreases or subsides.

غَيْض: decrease or subsiding; in some uses, a small or diminished quantity.

غِيضَ: the perfect passive: something was diminished or made to subside.

غَائِض: something decreasing or subsiding; transitively, something or someone that diminishes another.

مَغِيض: a place where water sinks or leaves its former flow or surface level.

مَغَاض: a verbal noun or noun associated with water subsiding.

مَغِيضًا: an attested verbal-noun form for decrease and subsiding.

غَاضَهُ: diminished it or caused it to decrease.

أَغَاضَ: diminished something or made it subside.

إِغَاضَة: diminishing something or causing it to subside.

غَيَّضَ: reduced something’s amount, made it subside, or restrained its flow.

تَغْيِيض: the act of reducing or causing to subside.

انْغَاضَ: decreased or subsided.

Some lexicons record usages concerning the reduction and restraint of tears and milk.

These show that decrease may occur on its own or through another agent.

Decreasing and causing a decrease

The following constructions differ:

غَاضَ الْمَاءُ means that the water decreased.

غَاضَهُ means that a person or agent reduced its quantity.

The first reports a decrease without necessarily identifying its cause.

The second involves an agent causing the reduction.

The passive غِيضَ likewise expresses reduction without naming its agent within the verbal construction.

This matters in Hūd 11:44 because the Quranic verb is passive.

غَيْضَة: a thicket or wooded ground

Arabic sources use غَيْضَة for a thicket or a place where trees and plants grow densely together.

Its plurals are غِيَاض and أَغْيَاض.

Lexical explanations associate it with land where water collects or sinks, enabling trees to grow.

The usage is environmentally related to water and where it sinks.

It does not follow that every thicket necessarily results from water sinking or that the word’s entire history derives from this process.

غَيْضَة does not occur in the Quran.

It helps explain the wider family but should not be listed among its Quranic forms.

Forms actually found in the Quran

The root occurs only twice, in two verbal forms:

وَغِيضَ in Hūd 11:44.

تَغِيضُ in Al-Raʿd 13:8.

Both are Form I verbs.

The first is perfect passive and the second imperfect active.

Other derivatives, such as غَيْض, غَائِض, مَغِيض, غَيَّضَ, and انْغَاضَ, do not appear in the Quranic text.

The Quranic examination should therefore focus on these two occurrences.

3. وَغِيضَ الْمَاءُ: water subsiding after Noah’s flood

Hūd 11:44

وَقِيلَ يَا أَرْضُ ابْلَعِي مَاءَكِ وَيَا سَمَاءُ أَقْلِعِي وَغِيضَ الْمَاءُ وَقُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ وَاسْتَوَتْ عَلَى الْجُودِيِّ ۖ وَقِيلَ بُعْدًا لِلْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-qīla yā arḍu iblaʿī māʾaki wa-yā samāʾu aqliʿī wa-ghīḍa al-māʾu wa-quḍiya al-amru wa-istawat ʿalā al-Jūdiyyi wa-qīla buʿdan li-al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn

Hūd 11:44

Translation: “It was said: Earth, swallow your water; sky, cease. The water subsided, the matter was concluded, and it settled upon al-Jūdī. It was said: away with the wrongdoing people.”

This is the root’s first occurrence in Quranic order.

The verse appears at the conclusion of Noah’s flood narrative.

Hūd 11:40 describes the event’s beginning and the command to bring those accompanying Noah aboard.

In Hūd 11:41, Noah speaks of the vessel’s movement and resting in the name of The God.

In Hūd 11:42, the vessel moves among waves like mountains, and Noah invites his son to board.

In Hūd 11:43, the son says he will seek shelter on a mountain, but a wave separates him from his father.

Hūd 11:44 then gives the command associated with the water’s subsiding.

The construction وَغِيضَ الْمَاءُ

غِيضَ is a perfect passive verb from غ ي ض.

الْمَاءُ is the passive subject.

The sentence therefore says that the water was made to subside or reduced in quantity.

The agent is not named directly alongside the verb, while the surrounding passage presents a divine command.

The distinction between active and passive constructions must remain clear.

غَاضَ الْمَاءُ would report that the water decreased.

غِيضَ الْمَاءُ expresses the water’s being reduced in a passive construction.

The natural rendering “the water subsided” communicates the event’s result fluently, although the Arabic passive remains relevant to grammatical explanation.

Distinguishing ابْلَعِي from غِيضَ

The verse begins:

يَا أَرْضُ ابْلَعِي مَاءَكِ

yā arḍu iblaʿī māʾak

Hūd 11:44

That is: Earth, swallow your water.

ابْلَعِي comes from ب ل ع and concerns swallowing or taking something in.

غِيضَ comes from غ ي ض and concerns decrease and subsiding.

The two words describe related aspects of one event without sharing the same meaning.

The earth can absorb water, reducing the amount accumulated on the surface.

Swallowing is one action stated in the verse; ghayḍ describes the water’s reduction.

Their association does not make swallowing or infiltration the meaning of غ ي ض in every use.

When tears diminish, for example, nothing necessarily swallows them as the earth absorbs water.

Distinguishing أَقْلِعِي from غِيضَ

The sky is also commanded to cease.

أَقْلِعِي belongs to ق ل ع and concerns the cessation of rainfall here.

Two conditions thus appear together:

The earth takes in the water.

The sky ceases to rain.

The water then subsides.

This sequence relates the ending of incoming water, its absorption, and the reduction of accumulated water.

The verb غِيضَ alone should not be made to contain every natural mechanism of rain, flooding, and water flow.

The verse gives no figures for the rate or duration of the decrease, infiltration depth, or water remaining at each location.

What is clear is the ending of the flood condition and the water’s subsiding.

Does ghayḍ mean the complete destruction of water?

The root itself does not carry that meaning.

A pool may still contain water after its level subsides.

Water can also move from the surface into underground layers.

In both cases, its quantity in the former location decreases.

وَغِيضَ الْمَاءُ alone therefore does not imply that all water in the world was destroyed, or even that every location lost all its water.

It expresses the reduction and subsiding of the water discussed in the flood context.

The following وَقُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ expresses the matter’s conclusion, while وَاسْتَوَتْ عَلَى الْجُودِيِّ describes the vessel settling on al-Jūdī.

These clauses show that the flood phase has ended.

Ghayḍ still means decrease and subsiding; the event’s conclusion is understood from the verse as a whole.

4. وَمَا تَغِيضُ الْأَرْحَامُ: decrease and increase in wombs

Al-Raʿd 13:8

اللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ مَا تَحْمِلُ كُلُّ أُنْثَىٰ وَمَا تَغِيضُ الْأَرْحَامُ وَمَا تَزْدَادُ ۚ وَكُلُّ شَيْءٍ عِنْدَهُ بِمِقْدَارٍ

allāhu yaʿlamu mā taḥmilu kullu unthā wa-mā taghīḍu al-arḥāmu wa-mā tazdādu wa-kullu shayʾin ʿindahu bi-miqdār

Al-Raʿd 13:8

Translation: “The God knows what every female carries, what wombs diminish, and what they increase; everything with Him has a measure.”

This is the root’s second and final Quranic occurrence.

The verse concerns divine knowledge of what females carry and the changes occurring in wombs.

Unlike Hūd 11:44, the subject of decrease is not floodwater but gestation and what the womb contains.

The construction تَغِيضُ الْأَرْحَامُ

تَغِيضُ is an imperfect active verb from غ ي ض.

الْأَرْحَامُ is the plural of رَحِم and the verb’s subject.

An Arabic nonhuman plural may take a feminine singular verb; the feminine singular تَغِيضُ therefore agrees appropriately with الْأَرْحَامُ.

The verb’s basic meaning remains reduction or decrease.

Determining what wombs diminish requires examining the whole sentence.

The role of مَا

مَا occurs three times in the verse:

مَا تَحْمِلُ كُلُّ أُنْثَىٰ

mā taḥmilu kullu unthā

Al-Raʿd 13:8

وَمَا تَغِيضُ الْأَرْحَامُ

wa-mā taghīḍu al-arḥām

Al-Raʿd 13:8

وَمَا تَزْدَادُ

wa-mā tazdād

Al-Raʿd 13:8

Grammatical analysis allows more than one account of these instances.

On one reading, مَا is a relative pronoun: “what” or “that which.”

The God then knows what every female carries, what wombs diminish, and what they add.

On another reading, مَا introduces a verbal noun construction, referring to carrying, decreasing, and increasing themselves.

The general sense is then divine knowledge of gestation and all the decreases and increases associated with wombs.

Both readings merit consideration in Arabic grammar.

One emphasizes the things carried and changed; the other emphasizes the events and processes.

The root غ ي ض means decrease in either analysis.

What do wombs diminish?

تَغِيضُ does not itself restrict the decrease to one particular thing.

Early lexical and exegetical accounts offer several possibilities:

First, a shorter duration of gestation relative to what is considered usual.

Second, reduction or loss of a pregnancy before completion.

Third, reduction in blood or substances associated with the womb and pregnancy.

Fourth, decrease in the number, amount, or condition of what is within the womb.

وَمَا تَزْدَادُ may correspondingly concern increases in those same fields.

These possibilities can be examined in relation to the general meanings of decrease and increase.

None should become the verb’s exclusive meaning without independent evidence.

تَغِيضُ does not directly mean miscarriage in Arabic. Pregnancy loss may be considered a particular instance of decrease, but the word has a broader meaning.

Nor does it inherently mean uterine bleeding; any historical application to bleeding comes from contextual explanation.

Does the verse specify a fixed gestation period?

Some early explanations discuss decrease and increase relative to nine months.

The verse itself contains neither the number nine, the word month, nor a fixed duration for all pregnancies.

The root should therefore not be understood as meaning “pregnancy shorter than nine months.”

That is only one proposed application of decrease.

Some historical sources also transmit accounts of human pregnancies lasting several years.

Their presence in old works does not turn them into scientifically established facts.

Reliable medical evidence is relevant to actual human gestation; the lexical definition should not depend on either an old or a modern medical theory.

The linguistic meanings remain clear: decrease and increase.

They may relate to varied gestational conditions without the verse prescribing a particular duration or quantity for every pregnancy.

كُلُّ أُنْثَىٰ: the scope of divine knowledge

The verse begins:

مَا تَحْمِلُ كُلُّ أُنْثَىٰ

mā taḥmilu kullu unthā

Al-Raʿd 13:8

That is, what every female carries.

The wording is not limited to human women.

أُنْثَى can refer to females of other living creatures, and كُلُّ expresses breadth.

Divine knowledge as presented here therefore has a scope beyond human pregnancy.

The following الْأَرْحَامُ refers to wombs. That term should not be extended without examination to every mode of reproduction, including creatures without wombs.

The sentence first mentions what every female carries, then decrease and increase in wombs.

The expressions are connected but do not have exactly the same lexical scope.

وَكُلُّ شَيْءٍ عِنْدَهُ بِمِقْدَارٍ

The verse’s ending is especially significant:

وَكُلُّ شَيْءٍ عِنْدَهُ بِمِقْدَارٍ

wa-kullu shayʾin ʿindahu bi-miqdār

Al-Raʿd 13:8

Everything with The God has a measure.

This places divine knowledge of decrease and increase within the measured order of creation.

Quantity, timing, and growth conditions are not uniform across living beings.

One pregnancy may have a favorable outcome while another encounters difficulty or interrupted development.

The number of fetuses, their growth rate, and physical condition may also vary.

The verse expresses divine knowledge of such differences and changes.

بِمِقْدَارٍ does not imply that all pregnancies must have identical duration, size, and outcomes.

The verse itself speaks of decrease and increase.

Measure therefore does not mean the absence of difference or change.

Does the verse offer a medical theory of the womb?

It concerns divine knowledge of gestation and changes in wombs.

It does not explain uterine anatomy, cellular stages of fetal growth, hormones, or the medical mechanism of every increase or decrease.

These subjects can be investigated empirically.

Medicine can use appropriate instruments to examine fetal growth, fetal number, and some changes during pregnancy.

Such limited human understanding differs from the encompassing divine knowledge described by the verse.

Medical advances also do not require replacing the meaning of تَغِيضُ with a particular scientific discovery.

The root still expresses decrease; context and other evidence identify what decreases.

Decrease and increase do not establish sin or virtue

The verse discusses gestational changes without judging the mother’s or fetus’s moral worth.

A pregnancy ending early or a fetus developing with different physical conditions does not, by itself, show that the mother or family has sinned.

Favorable fetal development likewise does not establish the parents’ moral superiority.

The verse presents divine knowledge of creation, not a fixed relationship between pregnancy outcomes and religious worth.

Responsibility based on a person’s capacity and actions should be distinguished from physical and natural events.

5. Comparison with neighboring roots

غ ي ض (ghayḍ) and ن ق ص (naqṣ)

Naqṣ concerns decrease, diminished quantity, and deficiency in Arabic.

Ghayḍ approaches this meaning in many usages.

Prominent uses of غ ي ض concern subsiding liquid or something falling below its previous level or quantity. Slowness is not a necessary lexical feature.

Naqṣ has a broader range, applying to quantity, number, rights, value, and other matters.

Al-Baqarah 2:155 mentions losses of wealth, lives, and produce.

Al-Raʿd 13:8 uses تَغِيضُ for decrease associated with wombs.

This difference does not establish an absolute rule that ghayḍ always concerns liquids and naqṣ always solids.

Linguistic evidence also applies ghayḍ to prices and numbers of people.

The distinction is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary.

غ ي ض (ghayḍ) and ز ي د (ziyādah)

Al-Raʿd 13:8 places تَغِيضُ and تَزْدَادُ together.

تَزْدَادُ belongs to ز ي د and concerns increase.

Their pairing creates a clear opposition between decrease and increase.

A quantity may become smaller or greater than before.

Neither decrease nor increase by itself determines whether the outcome is good or bad.

A reduction in illness can be welcome, while reduced essential food for a child may be harmful.

Increased crop production may benefit people, while more of a harmful substance may pose a danger.

The subject and circumstances determine the outcome’s value, rather than increase or decrease alone.

غ ي ض (ghayḍ) and ف ي ض (fayḍ)

Fayḍ concerns abundance, overflowing, and flow beyond capacity.

The expression غاض الكرام وفاض اللئام draws on this semantic contrast.

غاض expresses decrease.

فاض expresses abundance and overflow.

Al-Māʾidah 5:83 describes eyes overflowing with tears upon recognizing truth.

This belongs to the sense involving abundant, flowing tears.

غَيَّضَ الدَّمْعَ, in contrast, is recorded for reducing or restraining tears.

The two families can thus express opposite conditions of the same liquid: abundance and subsiding.

Resemblance between their letters does not establish a historically common root.

غ ي ض (ghayḍ) and غ و ر (ghawr)

Applied to water, ghawr concerns sinking into the earth and entering its depths.

Al-Kahf 18:41 and Al-Mulk 67:30 use غَوْرًا for water that may sink into the earth and become inaccessible.

Ghayḍ can likewise concern diminishing water.

The events may be connected without being identical.

A spring’s level may fall if its water sinks underground.

Not every decrease in water results from sinking into the ground.

Water in a vessel, for instance, may decrease through consumption.

It may also be transferred from one place to another.

Ghawr therefore emphasizes entering the depths, while ghayḍ emphasizes diminished quantity or subsiding.

غ ي ض (ghayḍ) and غ ض ض (ghaḍḍ)

غ ض ض concerns reducing, lowering, and restraining something.

Al-Nūr 24:30 and Al-Nūr 24:31 use it for lowering the gaze.

Luqmān 31:19 and Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3 also use it for lowering the voice.

Ghayḍ is close to this family in the concept of reduction.

The roots nevertheless remain distinct.

Quranic uses of غ ض ض for gaze and voice concern human conduct and choice.

غ ي ض concerns subsiding water or decrease in wombs, events not necessarily dependent on conscious human decisions.

This tendency does not make every instance of ghaḍḍ intentional or every instance of ghayḍ unintentional.

Construction and context determine the precise meaning and agency.

غ ي ض (ghayḍ) and غ ي ظ (ghayẓ)

غ ي ظ is a separate root concerning intense anger and its inner pressure.

Āl ʿImrān 3:119 describes people biting their fingers in rage.

غ ي ض, with ḍād, concerns decrease and subsiding.

Their similar appearance should not lead to identifying them as one family.

غَائِض with ḍād can describe something decreasing or causing diminution.

غَائِظ with ẓāʾ describes something or someone causing anger.

The disputed reading in the verse attributed to Burj ibn Mushir also concerns this formal resemblance.

The actual consonant and root must therefore be examined before determining the word’s meaning.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the root’s primary sense, suitable equivalents include “decrease,” “diminish,” “subside,” and, in some contexts, “recede.”

The subject of the verb determines which is appropriate.

Hūd 11:44

وَغِيضَ الْمَاءُ

wa-ghīḍa al-māʾ

Hūd 11:44

Suggested translation:

“And the water subsided.”

This communicates both the reduction of water and its recession in the flood context.

“The water decreased” also preserves the basic meaning, but conveys the flood’s ending less vividly than “subsided.”

“The water was completely destroyed” is inaccurate: absolute destruction does not follow from the verb itself.

“All the water sank into the earth” likewise exceeds the independent meaning of غِيضَ. The preceding clause describes the earth swallowing water; each verb’s contribution should remain distinct.

Al-Raʿd 13:8

وَمَا تَغِيضُ الْأَرْحَامُ

wa-mā taghīḍu al-arḥām

Al-Raʿd 13:8

Suggested translation:

“And that which the wombs diminish.”

Or, more fluently within the verse:

“And what wombs diminish.”

The rendering proposed here, “what wombs diminish and what they increase,” preserves both verbs’ primary meanings.

It suits a base translation because it does not restrict the decrease without evidence to gestational duration, bleeding, or pregnancy loss.

Commentary may examine possible applications of decrease and increase during gestation, but these should not be recorded as the root’s own meaning.

Brief root meanings for Forqan word boxes

For وَغِيضَ in Hūd 11:44:

Brief root meaning: “Decreasing, diminishing, and subsiding in quantity.”

Meaning in this verse: “The water’s being made to subside after the flood, reducing the accumulated water.”

For تَغِيضُ in Al-Raʿd 13:8:

Brief root meaning: “Decreasing or reducing the quantity of something.”

Meaning in this verse: “What wombs diminish during gestation, contrasted with increases in the same field.”

The active and passive distinction should also remain clear in these two contextual explanations.

غِيضَ is perfect passive.

تَغِيضُ is imperfect active.

Their contextual fields should therefore express their different sentence roles; a shared general root summary does not replace this distinction. These are editorial proposals and do not authorize rewriting existing verse boxes when the study is installed.

7. Conclusion

In classical Arabic, غ ي ض concerns decreasing, diminished quantity, and subsiding.

Its familiar use concerns water whose amount decreases, level falls, or presence disappears from a former location.

Arabic sources also record the family for tears, milk, prices, and numbers of people.

This range shows that ghayḍ is not confined to water sinking underground.

Poetic witnesses also illustrate reduced strength and tears, although some readings, attributions, and precise composition dates are disputed or uncertain.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran.

In Hūd 11:44, غِيضَ الْمَاءُ describes water subsiding after Noah’s flood.

Al-Raʿd 13:8 uses تَغِيضُ الْأَرْحَامُ for decrease associated with gestation and wombs, opposite تَزْدَادُ.

In the first passage, decreasing water accompanies commands for the earth to swallow water and the sky to cease raining.

غِيضَ itself does not mean swallowing: the clause expresses the resulting subsidence.

In the second, decrease and increase concern the creation and growth of living beings.

The root itself does not mean miscarriage, bleeding, shortened gestation, or a particular medical event.

These may be examined as possible applications without restricting the lexical meaning to any one of them.

Al-Raʿd 13:8 also speaks of divine knowledge of what every female carries and of the decreases and increases associated with wombs.

Its ending describes everything as having a measure with The God.

This does not make all conditions fixed and identical: the verse itself speaks of variation, decrease, and increase.

The final root definition is:

غ ي ض: reduction in quantity or subsiding relative to a previous condition, with diminished amount or volume. Applied to water, it may accompany a falling level, recession, or disappearance from a former location.

This definition accommodates both the water’s subsiding in Noah’s story and decrease in wombs without confining every use to one physical mechanism.

The two passages show that a phenomenon’s decrease is not identical to its absolute destruction; its subject and context determine the kind of decrease.

Water can subside without disappearing entirely from the world. Decrease and increase during gestation may concern different conditions without the word itself specifying every change’s kind or result.

The meaning should therefore remain within its clear, defensible core: decreasing and subsiding, without necessarily implying destruction or complete disappearance.
