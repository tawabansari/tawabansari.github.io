A span of time, the appointed Hour, the passage of time, and Suwāʿ as a deity’s name

The Arabic family S W ʿ has several semantic branches. Its principal forms include sāʿah, a portion of time; al-sāʿah, the appointed time or event of resurrection; and Suwāʿ, the name of a deity mentioned in the account of Noah’s people. Lexicons also record the passing of time, livestock wandering away, and something being lost under this family.

The Quranic index places 49 occurrences under these letters: 48 of sāʿah and one of the proper name Suwāʿ.

In ordinary usage, sāʿah denotes a portion of time, whether a short interval or part of a day or night. In resurrection contexts, however, the definite al-sāʿah refers to a particular promised event associated with the end of the present condition of life, the raising of people, and their reckoning.

This distinction matters. Sāʿah does not always mean resurrection in the Quran; nor is al-sāʿah in resurrection passages an ordinary hour of the day.

In Sūrat Nūḥ, Suwāʿ is the proper name of one of the deities whose worship Noah’s people were urged to maintain. Morphological indexes place this name under S W ʿ, but its shared letters with sāʿah do not by themselves establish that both developed from one historical meaning.

Understanding this family requires distinguishing time as duration, the Hour as a promised event, and the proper name Suwāʿ.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Sāʿah: a portion of time

Early Arabic uses sāʿah for a portion of time. This may be an interval during the day or night, without necessarily restricting it to a specified number of minutes.

Lexicons explain sāʿah as a portion of the day or night, the present time, or a short period.

An expression such as jalastu ʿindaka sāʿatan can mean “I sat with you for a while.”

Likewise, jāʾa baʿda sāʿatin means that someone came after some time had passed.

In these examples, the word expresses an amount of time whose precise duration follows from context or a convention shared by speaker and listener.

For example, a modern listener might understand “I waited an hour” as sixty minutes. In early usage, however, an equivalent expression did not necessarily specify exactly sixty minutes.

The current meaning of an hour in modern timekeeping should therefore not be imposed on every use in older Arabic.

A poetic witness attributed to the pre-Islamic period: a while within time

In a poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays, we read:

خَلِيلَيَّ مُرَّا بِي عَلَى أُمِّ جُنْدَبِ
نُقَضِّ لُبَانَاتِ الْفُؤَادِ الْمُعَذَّبِ

فَإِنَّكُمَا إِنْ تَنْظُرَانِيَ سَاعَةً
مِنَ الدَّهْرِ تَنْفَعْنِي لَدَى أُمِّ جُنْدَبِ

khalīlayya murrā bī ʿalā Ummi Jundabi / nuqaḍḍi lubānāti al-fuʾādi al-muʿadhdhabi / fa-innakumā in tanẓurāniya sāʿatan / mina al-dahri tanfaʿnī ladā Ummi Jundabi

“My two companions, take me past Umm Jundab, that we may satisfy the desires of this tormented heart. If you wait for me a while, you will help me in my encounter with Umm Jundab.”

Here, sāʿatan mina al-dahr denotes a portion of time or a brief opportunity.

The poet asks his companions to wait a little, allowing him time with his beloved.

Sāʿah here means neither resurrection nor necessarily a sixty-minute unit. It is a limited interval providing an opportunity to do something.

The passage illustrates the ordinary temporal meaning in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The poem is transmitted in collections of early poetry and the collected verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays. Some wording varies, but sāʿatan mina al-dahr and the sense of a short interval are clear.

Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not independently establish the exact date of these lines outside their transmission history. They are useful evidence of early usage, but do not warrant stronger historical conclusions than that evidence supports.

Sāʿah as a measurable division of day and night

Arabic sources also divide the combined day and night into twenty-four hours. Lexical explanations state that when day and night are equal, each has twelve hours.

This shows that, alongside its general meaning of a portion of time, sāʿah could function within a system for counting and dividing day and night.

Three issues should nevertheless be distinguished: recognizing a portion of time, conventionally dividing the day and night into specified parts, and using mechanical clocks or precise modern instruments.

Knowing a division of time does not mean that everyone in every period used sāʿah exclusively for a fixed sixty-minute interval.

Nor should modern timekeeping devices be treated as the word’s earliest meaning.

A mechanical or digital clock is an instrument that displays time. Its Arabic name derives from time and its unit of measurement; the older meaning of sāʿah did not originate in the modern device.

Likewise, when the Quran uses sāʿah for a respite, a stay, or part of a day, there is no basis for restricting all these occurrences to exactly sixty minutes.

Poetry of the Islamic period: a short interval

The Umayyad poet al-Quṭāmī uses the image of fire:

وَكُنَّا كَالْحَرِيقِ أَصَابَ غَابًا
فَيَخْبُو سَاعَةً وَيَهُبُّ سَاعَا

wa-kunnā ka-al-ḥarīqi aṣāba ghāban / fa-yakhbū sāʿatan wa-yahubbu sāʿā

“We were like a fire that had struck a thicket: it subsides for a while and flares up at other times.”

Sāʿatan and sāʿā here refer to different intervals or periods of time.

The fire dies down for a while, then blazes again.

This illustrates how sāʿah can contrast one interval with another without precisely measuring the duration of each phase.

Lexical commentaries identify sāʿ in this line as a plural of sāʿah.

Al-Quṭāmī lived in the Islamic period, so this cannot be called pre-Quranic poetry. It demonstrates the continuing temporal sense in early Islamic Arabic.

Sawʿ and suwāʿ: part of the night

Lexicons record baʿda sawʿin mina al-layl and baʿda suwāʿin.

These expressions describe coming after part of the night has passed, or after the night has settled and advanced.

Here, sawʿ and suwāʿ concern time and its passage.

This temporal suwāʿ should not, without historical investigation, be identified with Suwāʿ as the Quranic deity’s name.

Identically formed words can have different meanings. A shared form alone establishes neither the reason for a name nor historical kinship.

These temporal usages are recorded in old lexicons, but independently dating each to the pre-Islamic period requires further evidence.

2. Semantic centers and forms of the family

One early lexical analysis explains S W ʿ through something continuing and passing on.

On that account, an hour is a portion of time that passes and elapses.

The same field also includes recorded usages concerning livestock moving about and things being lost.

Such an analysis may relate some branches, but should not reduce them all to a single certain historical meaning without evidence.

Important lexical forms

Sāʿah: a portion of time, an opportunity, or an interval; in definite Quranic resurrection contexts, the appointed Hour.

Sāʿāt: a plural of sāʿah.

Sāʿ: another recorded plural of sāʿah.

Suwayʿah: the diminutive of sāʿah, meaning a little time or a short period.

Sawʿ: part of the night or an interval of its passage.

Sāʿa al-shayʾu: in one lexical usage, something was lost or wasted.

Asāʿa al-shayʾa: he wasted or neglected it.

Sāʿati al-ibilu: the camels dispersed and wandered freely in the pasture.

Asāʿa al-ibila: he let the camels roam in the pasture.

Misyāʿ: used of a she-camel roaming in the pasture, and of a person who wastes or squanders wealth.

Musāwaʿah: hiring someone or dealing with someone by the hour.

Sāʿatun sawʿāʾu: a severe or difficult hour.

Suwāʿ: the name of a deity in the account of Noah’s people.

These forms do not all occur in the Quran. Only sāʿah and the proper name Suwāʿ are indexed there under these letters.

The meanings of being lost and being let loose also need to remain distinct. Wasting wealth or letting camels roam does not itself mean that an hour has passed.

An explanatory connection can be proposed between continued movement, loss, and time passing. Such a picture does not establish the historical derivation of every form.

3. Quranic forms and two temporal uses

The Quranic index records 49 occurrences under S W ʿ.

Of these, 48 belong to sāʿah and one is the proper name Suwāʿ.

Among the 48 temporal occurrences, al-sāʿah denotes the promised resurrection event forty times; eight uses concern a portion of time, an opportunity, or an interval.

Although both uses involve the same word, their translation and context must be distinguished.

Sāʿah as duration and opportunity

The eight ordinary temporal uses occur in these verses:

Al-Aʿrāf 7:34; Al-Tawbah 9:117; Yūnus 10:45; Yūnus 10:49; Al-Naḥl 16:61; Al-Rūm 30:55; Sabaʾ 34:30; and Al-Aḥqāf 46:35.

Here, sāʿah denotes a period, a brief interval, the length of a stay, or a difficult time.

In the other occurrences, al-sāʿah names a particular event whose coming, approach, establishment, and associated reckoning the Quran describes.

Grammatical forms

In many temporal uses, sāʿatan is an indefinite accusative noun. Sometimes it functions adverbially, expressing an amount or duration of time.

Sāʿatin is the genitive form of the same noun.

Sāʿati al-ʿusrah is a construct phrase referring to a time or period of hardship.

Al-sāʿatu, al-sāʿata, and al-sāʿati are the case forms of the definite noun. Nominative, accusative, and genitive endings follow syntactic function; they do not create new roots or derivational forms.

Grammatically, sāʿah is feminine, so an associated verb or pronoun may be feminine, as in jāʾati al-sāʿah and taqūmu al-sāʿah.

Grammatical femininity does not by itself give time an allegorical meaning or an actual gender.

4. Sāʿah: a short period, an opportunity, and the end of a respite

An hour of the day

Yūnus 10:45 states:

وَيَوْمَ يَحْشُرُهُمْ كَأَنْ لَمْ يَلْبَثُوا إِلَّا سَاعَةً مِنَ النَّهَارِ يَتَعَارَفُونَ بَيْنَهُمْ

wa-yawma yaḥshuruhum ka-an lam yalbathū illā sāʿatan mina al-nahāri yataʿārafūna baynahum

Yūnus 10:45

“And on the day He gathers them, as though they had stayed only an hour of the day, recognizing one another.”

Sāʿatan mina al-nahār here denotes a short part of the day.

Kaʾan lam yalbathū is also significant: the Quran describes their condition as though their stay had been very short.

This is therefore more than time measured in a precise unit. It compares the duration of their stay with their perception when gathered.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:35 also states:

كَأَنَّهُمْ يَوْمَ يَرَوْنَ مَا يُوعَدُونَ لَمْ يَلْبَثُوا إِلَّا سَاعَةً مِنْ نَهَارٍ

ka-annahum yawma yarawna mā yūʿadūna lam yalbathū illā sāʿatan min nahār

Al-Aḥqāf 46:35

“As though, on the day they see what they are promised, they had stayed only an hour of a day.”

Here too, sāʿah denotes a short part of a day.

Neither passage establishes that an entire lifetime, or the interval between death and resurrection, literally becomes sixty minutes on every scale.

The expression kaʾanna itself indicates a comparison.

Thus, sāʿah denotes a short period in these passages, while the experience or perception of time emerges from the complete sentence.

Two meanings of sāʿah in one verse

Al-Rūm 30:55 offers particularly clear evidence of the distinction between sāʿah and al-sāʿah:

وَيَوْمَ تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ يُقْسِمُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ مَا لَبِثُوا غَيْرَ سَاعَةٍ كَذَٰلِكَ كَانُوا يُؤْفَكُونَ

wa-yawma taqūmu al-sāʿatu yuqsimu al-mujrimūna mā labithū ghayra sāʿatin kadhālika kānū yuʾfakūn

Al-Rūm 30:55

“And on the day the Hour arises, the guilty swear that they stayed no more than an hour. Thus they used to be turned away from the truth.”

Sāʿah occurs twice in this verse.

The first occurrence, al-sāʿatu, refers to the resurrection event.

The second, sāʿatin, denotes the short period the guilty suppose they have stayed.

Rendering both as “resurrection” would make the sentence incoherent.

Conversely, treating both as an ordinary hour of the day would erase the resurrection sense of the first.

The verse shows that the Quran itself distinguishes ordinary duration from the recognized meaning of the appointed Hour.

In Al-Rūm 30:56, those given knowledge and faith correct the guilty people’s account of their stay.

Their oath in Al-Rūm 30:55 should therefore not be accepted as a definitive measurement endorsed by the Quran. Their words are reported and then set against the other group’s reply.

Distinguishing what people say from the Quran’s own judgment is essential here.

Sāʿati al-ʿusrah: a time of hardship

Al-Tawbah 9:117 states:

الَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوهُ فِي سَاعَةِ الْعُسْرَةِ

alladhīna ittabaʿūhu fī sāʿati al-ʿusrah

Al-Tawbah 9:117

“Those who followed him in the time of hardship.”

Here, sāʿah is joined to al-ʿusrah.

ʿUsrah belongs to ʿ S R and means hardship or difficulty.

Sāʿati al-ʿusrah therefore refers to a difficult period or situation.

It need not mean an exact sixty-minute interval. The context concerns difficult conditions in which following and supporting mattered.

Lexically, sāʿah expresses a period of time; hardship comes from the separate word al-ʿusrah.

Sāʿah does not itself name difficulty, suffering, or crisis.

Arabic also records sāʿatun sawʿāʾu for a severe or difficult time. An interval can be described through the events occurring within it without time itself becoming synonymous with hardship.

Lā yastaʾkhirūna sāʿatan: the end of a respite

Al-Aʿrāf 7:34 states:

فَإِذَا جَاءَ أَجَلُهُمْ لَا يَسْتَأْخِرُونَ سَاعَةً وَلَا يَسْتَقْدِمُونَ

fa-idhā jāʾa ajaluhum lā yastaʾkhirūna sāʿatan wa-lā yastaqdimūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:34

“Then, when their term arrives, they can neither delay an hour nor advance it.”

The same construction also occurs in Yūnus 10:49 and Al-Naḥl 16:61.

A related expression appears in Sabaʾ 34:30:

لَا تَسْتَأْخِرُونَ عَنْهُ سَاعَةً وَلَا تَسْتَقْدِمُونَ

lā tastaʾkhirūna ʿanhu sāʿatan wa-lā tastaqdimūn

Sabaʾ 34:30

“You can neither delay it by an hour nor advance it.”

Here, sāʿah occurs in a construction denying delay and advancement.

The point is that when the appointed end arrives, an individual or community cannot choose to move it backward or forward.

This should not be restricted to forbidding a sixty-minute delay while allowing shorter or longer delays.

In such Arabic constructions, negating sāʿah can emphasize the absence of any opportunity for delay.

The sentence therefore expresses the certainty of the term and the inability to change its appointed time.

Sāʿah denotes the interval by which delay is denied; the appointed term itself is expressed by ajal or mīʿād elsewhere in the sentence.

5. Al-sāʿah: the promised resurrection event

In forty Quranic occurrences, al-sāʿah denotes a particular event associated with resurrection, reckoning, and the end of humanity’s present stage of life.

The word remains temporal, but no longer simply names any short part of a day or night.

Al-sāʿah is the recognized designation of the time when a major, decisive event occurs.

The appointed Hour and resurrection

Al-Ḥajj 22:7 states:

وَأَنَّ السَّاعَةَ آتِيَةٌ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهَا وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ يَبْعَثُ مَنْ فِي الْقُبُورِ

wa-anna al-sāʿata ātiyatun lā rayba fīhā wa-anna Allāha yabʿathu man fī al-qubūr

Al-Ḥajj 22:7

“And that the Hour is coming, with no doubt in it, and that The God raises those in the graves.”

Here, al-sāʿah appears alongside the raising of the dead.

This connection shows that it does not mean an ordinary interval within a day.

Al-Kahf 18:21 likewise speaks of the truth of God’s promise and the certainty of the Hour.

In Ṭā Hā 20:15, the Hour’s coming is connected with every soul being recompensed for its striving.

In these usages, the Hour is therefore tied to resurrection and human accountability.

Yet sāʿah alone does not express every detail of raising, gathering, judgment, reward, and punishment.

Those stages and subjects are described through other words and verses.

The lexical definition of sāʿah should therefore not absorb every concept of the hereafter.

Taqūmu al-sāʿah: the Hour arises

Taqūmu al-sāʿah occurs in several verses, including Al-Rūm 30:12, Al-Rūm 30:14, Al-Rūm 30:55, Ghāfir 40:46, and Al-Jāthiyah 45:27.

Taqūmu belongs to Q W M and here expresses the event’s arising or taking place.

For example, Al-Jāthiyah 45:27 states:

وَيَوْمَ تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ يَوْمَئِذٍ يَخْسَرُ الْمُبْطِلُونَ

wa-yawma taqūmu al-sāʿatu yawmaʾidhin yakhsaru al-mubṭilūn

Al-Jāthiyah 45:27

“And on the day the Hour arises, on that day those devoted to falsehood will lose.”

Al-Jāthiyah 45:28 describes communities being summoned to their records.

The Hour’s arising is thus connected with the disclosure of the reckoning and the outcomes of human actions.

But taqūmu is not part of the meaning of S W ʿ. Whereas sāʿah in ordinary use denotes a duration, taqūmu al-sāʿah speaks of the promised event taking place.

Why is resurrection called the Hour?

Early lexical explanations offer several observations about this designation.

First, an hour is a portion of time, and resurrection will occur at an appointed time.

Second, the event can arrive suddenly and within a very brief interval.

Third, the arising of the Hour brings a great transformation in the condition of humanity and the world.

These explanations relate to Quranic usage, but naming and the manner of the event should remain distinct.

The Hour names a time or promised event. The name itself does not prove that every stage of the hereafter lasts only sixty minutes.

Nor does it establish that all events following resurrection must occur within an equally short interval.

In the Quran, the Hour can designate the point or event opening a major phase, while yawm al-qiyāmah and yawm al-dīn highlight other aspects of the same ultimate outcome and reckoning.

These titles concern related subjects, but their component words are not entirely synonymous.

The Hour’s sudden arrival

Al-Anʿām 6:31 states:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَتْهُمُ السَّاعَةُ بَغْتَةً قَالُوا يَا حَسْرَتَنَا عَلَىٰ مَا فَرَّطْنَا فِيهَا

ḥattā idhā jāʾathumu al-sāʿatu baghtatan qālū yā ḥasratanā ʿalā mā farraṭnā fīhā

Al-Anʿām 6:31

“Until, when the Hour comes upon them suddenly, they say: Alas for our neglect concerning it!”

Yūsuf 12:107, Al-Zukhruf 43:66, and other passages also speak of the Hour’s sudden arrival.

Baghtatan concerns suddenness and belongs to B Gh T.

In these verses, suddenness is therefore expressed by baghtatan and the sentence’s construction.

Sāʿah should not itself be defined as a sudden event in every Arabic usage.

Someone may wait or work for an hour without suddenness being part of that duration’s meaning.

In resurrection contexts, suddenness is a feature of the event, not an invariable meaning of every form in this family.

The Hour draws near

Al-Qamar 54:1 states:

اقْتَرَبَتِ السَّاعَةُ وَانْشَقَّ الْقَمَرُ

iqtarabati al-sāʿatu wa-inshaqqa al-qamar

Al-Qamar 54:1

“The Hour drew near, and the moon split.”

Iqtarabat belongs to Q R B and denotes drawing near.

Al-sāʿah is the promised event.

The verse speaks of its approach without specifying a numerical date.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:1 and other passages likewise warn that the reckoning or promised event is approaching.

Such language communicates the seriousness and proximity of the outcome in the warning. It does not justify calculating an exact date for resurrection or the remaining years without independent evidence.

The Quran calls people to readiness and accountability, not to inventing a speculative calendar for an unknown event.

Knowledge of the Hour belongs to God

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 states:

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ السَّاعَةِ أَيَّانَ مُرْسَاهَا قُلْ إِنَّمَا عِلْمُهَا عِنْدَ رَبِّي

yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-sāʿati ayyāna mursāhā qul innamā ʿilmuhā ʿinda rabbī

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187

“They ask you about the Hour: when will it arrive? Say: Knowledge of it is only with my Lord.”

Luqmān 31:34 also states:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ عِنْدَهُ عِلْمُ السَّاعَةِ

inna Allāha ʿindahu ʿilmu al-sāʿah

Luqmān 31:34

“Indeed, with The God is knowledge of the Hour.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:63 and Fuṣṣilat 41:47 address the same subject.

These verses distinguish the certainty of the Hour’s arrival from knowledge of its exact time.

The Quran announces the Hour in connection with resurrection and reckoning, but does not entrust people with knowledge of its precise date.

No one may therefore attribute a definite date to God merely through speculation, numerology, or calculation without evidence.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:42, the Prophet is also asked when the Hour will arrive. Al-Nāziʿāt 79:43, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:44, and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:45 direct attention to divine knowledge and the task of warning.

The Quranic al-sāʿah thus concerns the promised event; its exact date is a separate issue from the word’s lexical meaning.

A broader study of death, resurrection, and the life that follows should examine their relevant verses separately; the meaning of sāʿah alone cannot settle every question about them.

6. Suwāʿ: a deity’s name in the account of Noah’s people

The only nontemporal Quranic item indexed under these letters is Suwāʿ in Nūḥ 71:23.

The verse states:

وَقَالُوا لَا تَذَرُنَّ آلِهَتَكُمْ وَلَا تَذَرُنَّ وَدًّا وَلَا سُوَاعًا وَلَا يَغُوثَ وَيَعُوقَ وَنَسْرًا

wa-qālū lā tadharunna ālihatakum wa-lā tadharunna Waddan wa-lā Suwāʿan wa-lā Yaghūtha wa-Yaʿūqa wa-Nasrā

Nūḥ 71:23

“And they said: Do not abandon your deities; and do not abandon Wadd or Suwāʿ or Yaghūth and Yaʿūq and Nasr.”

Suwāʿan is the proper name of one of the deities whose worship Noah’s people were urged to retain.

It appears beside four other names.

Suwāʿ is a proper name

An Arabic proper name primarily identifies a particular person, place, or entity.

A name might originally derive from a word with a lexical meaning, but that possibility alone does not authorize replacing the name with the word’s ordinary meaning in translation.

In Nūḥ 71:23, Suwāʿ names a deity.

It should therefore not be translated as “hour,” “short time,” “opportunity,” or “something wasted.”

Suwāʿan is accusative and has nunation. This is part of the name’s grammatical form and does not turn it into an indefinite noun meaning “an hour.”

Wadd in the same verse is also a proper name, even though wadd can mean affection in Arabic.

The lexical meanings of some names do not by themselves establish why all the listed deities received their names.

A literary witness concerning Suwāʿ’s worship

Old works on Arabian names and places of worship transmit a line by an unidentified poet:

تَرَاهُمْ حَوْلَ قَيْلِهِمْ عُكُوفًا
كَمَا عَكَفَتْ هُذَيْلٌ عَلَى سُوَاعِ

tarāhum ḥawla qaylihim ʿukūfan / kamā ʿakafat Hudhaylun ʿalā Suwāʿi

“You see them gathered devotedly around their chief, as Hudhayl devoted themselves to Suwāʿ.”

Suwāʿ here names a deity associated in the poet’s account with Hudhayl.

ʿUkūf denotes remaining, attending closely, and devotion to something.

This witness matters for the name’s history: it shows that Arabian literary and historical tradition recognized Suwāʿ as a deity’s name.

The poet has not been independently identified, and the date of composition remains unclear.

The subject matter alone cannot make it certain pre-Quranic evidence.

Nor does the reported worship of Suwāʿ among Hudhayl prove that the same statue or physical object survived unchanged from Noah’s time into pre-Islamic Arabia.

That claim would require independent historical evidence.

Suwāʿ in the Quran and later historical reports

Some historical and transmitted accounts state that Suwāʿ was worshipped by Noah’s people and later by groups in Arabia.

Other accounts connect the names in Sūrat Nūḥ with righteous people whose images were made after their deaths and eventually worshipped by later generations.

The Quran itself does not narrate that history in Nūḥ 71:23.

It reports the names and, in the surrounding passage, describes the people’s disobedience, their following those with wealth and children, scheming, and the misleading of many.

A direct reading of the verse therefore does not require accepting as established fact the story of righteous people becoming idols or the statues being transferred to Arabia.

The Quran’s own statement is clear: people were urged to keep their deities, and Suwāʿ was one of the names in that appeal.

The relationship between Suwāʿ and sāʿah

By their letters, Suwāʿ and sāʿah are classified under S W ʿ.

Lexicons also record suwāʿ in another usage for part of the night.

Some lexical reports additionally give sāʿa al-shayʾu the sense “something was lost.”

These similarities do not establish that the deity was named after time, passing hours, loss, or any such specific feature.

Establishing that relationship would require evidence about the deity’s naming.

Without such evidence, the proper name should remain as it appears in the Quran.

The contextual connection with following leaders

Nūḥ 71:21 says that Noah’s people followed those whose wealth and children only increased their loss.

Nūḥ 71:22 describes a great scheme.

Then Nūḥ 71:23 reports the words of those urging people not to abandon their deities.

The sequence involves more than the names of idols. Social influence, following others, and efforts to keep people within previous beliefs are also present.

Nūḥ 71:24 also speaks of many being led astray.

Suwāʿ’s name therefore occurs amid following, scheming, and worship of deities other than God.

That conclusion follows from the narrative context, not from the lexical meaning of S W ʿ.

The name itself means neither misguidance nor scheming.

The root study and the verse’s translation must preserve this distinction.

7. Comparison with related roots

S W ʿ (sāʿah) and W Q T (waqt): duration and appointed time

Waqt concerns a specified time and assigning a temporal place to an action or event.

Sāʿah can be a portion of time or, in definite Quranic resurrection contexts, the promised event.

For example, someone may appoint a time to begin a task, while the task continues for an hour or longer.

Waqt and sāʿah are related, but do not express the same concept.

Mīqāt in the Quran likewise concerns an appointment or a specified time.

In resurrection passages, al-sāʿah names the appointed time or event without telling its audience the exact date.

S W ʿ (sāʿah) and Ḥ Y N (ḥīn): a portion of time and a time

Ḥīn denotes a time, occasion, or period whose limits are understood from context.

Sāʿah can likewise denote a portion of time.

In ordinary usage, the two can be very close.

But ḥīn does not always mean a short period, while sāʿah has the special Quranic use for the particular event of resurrection.

For example, ilā ḥīn may point to a future time without specifying the interval.

By contrast, al-sāʿah in resurrection verses is a recognized title, not simply an unspecified “time.”

S W ʿ (sāʿah) and ʾ J L (ajal): an interval and its term

Ajal concerns an appointed duration or its endpoint.

Sāʿah can be a unit or portion of time.

The two appear in one construction in Al-Aʿrāf 7:34, Yūnus 10:49, and Al-Naḥl 16:61.

Ajal specifies the term; sāʿah, in the denial of delay, expresses the interval by which moving that appointment is ruled out.

Sāʿah therefore does not itself name the whole concept of ajal or a fixed destiny.

S W ʿ (sāʿah) and Y W M (yawm): an hour and a day

Yawm ordinarily means day and can also denote a significant day or phase in the Quran.

In ordinary usage, sāʿah is a portion of time that may fall within a day.

In Yūnus 10:45 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:35, sāʿah min al-nahār denotes a short portion of a day.

But in yawm taqūmu al-sāʿah, day and Hour have different roles.

Yawm expresses the event’s temporal setting or phase, and al-sāʿah names the promised event itself.

Their combination does not mean that the entire Day of Resurrection lasts exactly one ordinary hour.

S W ʿ (sāʿah) and Q W M (qiyāmah): the appointed Hour and rising

Qiyāmah belongs to Q W M and is associated with rising and standing.

Al-sāʿah names the promised event linked in the Quran with resurrection and reckoning.

They are closely related in afterlife contexts, but are not lexically identical.

Sāʿah basically concerns time or a portion of it; qiyāmah concerns rising and standing.

Taqūmu al-sāʿah brings both fields together: the promised event arises.

S W ʿ (sāʿah) and D H R (dahr): a portion of time and time’s extent

Dahr concerns the extension of time and the ages.

Sāʿah can be a limited portion of it.

Sāʿah min al-dahr in the poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays expresses precisely this relationship: a brief opportunity within time.

Dahr is therefore not necessarily a unit of fixed length, nor does sāʿah always mean all of time.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:24 speaks of people who attribute their destruction only to time. Their claim must be examined separately from the definitions of time and hour: their judgment about the cause of death does not alter either word’s lexical meaning.

S W ʿ (sāʿah) and S ʿ Y (saʿy): time and striving

Saʿy concerns effort, purposeful movement, and striving.

Sāʿah concerns a portion of time or the promised event.

Some similar letters do not make these families identical.

For example, Ṭā Hā 20:15 connects the Hour with each soul being recompensed for what it strives to do, tasʿā.

Al-sāʿah belongs to S W ʿ; tasʿā belongs to S ʿ Y.

One names the time and event of reckoning, the other human effort and action.

Translation and root annotation must preserve that distinction.

S W ʿ (Suwāʿ) and W D D (Wadd): deities’ names and lexical meaning

Wadd and Suwāʿ appear together in Nūḥ 71:23.

Wadd concerns affection in Arabic, but here is the proper name of a deity.

Suwāʿ names another deity, yet the verse does not reveal the historical reason for its name.

Even if a defensible lexical sense can be found for one name, the same analysis should not be imposed without evidence on every neighboring name.

Listing several deities together does not itself establish that their forms, origins, or attributed characteristics were identical.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Sāʿah should be translated according to its Quranic context.

Sāʿah in ordinary temporal usage

In Yūnus 10:45 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:35:

“An hour of the day.”

The proposed Persian wording is natural and retains the sense of a short interval; “a while during the day” also makes that sense clear in English.

In Al-Rūm 30:55:

“They stayed no more than an hour.”

The second sāʿah in this verse must not be turned into “resurrection.”

In Al-Tawbah 9:117:

“The time of hardship” or “the difficult period.”

“The hour of hardship” is also close to the Arabic, but a reader might mistakenly restrict it to one exact hour.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:34, Yūnus 10:49, and Al-Naḥl 16:61; with second-person wording in Sabaʾ 34:30:

“They can neither delay an hour nor advance it”; “you can neither delay it by an hour nor advance it.”

These renderings preserve the temporal image, the negative construction, and the grammatical person of each passage.

A lexical explanation can clarify that the issue is inability to alter the appointed term, not a specific sixty-minute measurement.

Al-sāʿah in resurrection contexts

Two renderings of al-sāʿah deserve consideration in resurrection passages.

“That Hour” closely follows the proposed Persian rendering and emphasizes a particular event; “the Hour” is natural in English.

“The appointed Hour” can be clearer, but “appointed” is explanatory wording drawn from the wider Quranic context.

“Resurrection” also communicates the intended concept in many verses, but is not the direct lexical equivalent of S W ʿ.

For Forqan, retaining the Hour and explaining it briefly in the word box is therefore an appropriate approach.

For example, in Al-Ḥajj 22:7:

“And that the Hour is coming; there is no doubt in it.”

In Al-Qamar 54:1:

“The Hour drew near, and the moon split.”

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:42:

“They ask you about the Hour: when will it arrive?”

These renderings retain the event’s name without limiting it to sixty minutes.

Suwāʿ — Nūḥ 71:23

An appropriate rendering is:

“Suwāʿ.”

This proper name should not become “hour,” “time,” “opportunity,” or “wasted.”

In the phrase:

وَلَا تَذَرُنَّ وَدًّا وَلَا سُوَاعًا

wa-lā tadharunna Waddan wa-lā Suwāʿā

Nūḥ 71:23

“And do not abandon Wadd or Suwāʿ.”

The word box can explain that Suwāʿ is one of the deities named in the account of Noah’s people.

Brief root meaning and meaning in the verse

For sāʿah in temporal use:

Brief root meaning: a portion of time, duration, or opportunity; elsewhere in the family, continuation and passing on.

Meaning in this verse: an hour or short period, depending on the stay, opportunity, or appointment discussed in the sentence.

For al-sāʿah in resurrection use:

Brief root meaning: a portion of time and the time of an event.

Meaning in this verse: the appointed Hour, associated with the raising of people and their reckoning.

For Suwāʿan in Nūḥ 71:23:

Root or word status: a proper name indexed under S W ʿ, but without a certain derivational origin.

Brief word explanation: a deity’s proper name; its historical meaning is unclear. This does not assign a derivational root to the name.

Meaning in this verse: Suwāʿ, one of the deities whose worship Noah’s people were urged not to abandon.

Conclusion

The Quranic index groups two principal uses under S W ʿ: sāʿah in the temporal field and Suwāʿ as a proper name.

In early Arabic, sāʿah denotes a portion of time. It may be a short interval during day or night and can also serve as a unit dividing the full day.

The poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays uses sāʿah min al-dahr for a brief opportunity. Al-Quṭāmī’s poem uses sāʿah for intervals during which fire subsides and flares up.

Sāʿah occurs 48 times in the Quran.

Eight occurrences concern duration, opportunity, a stay, or a temporal interval. These show the word’s ordinary meaning remains active; not every occurrence names resurrection.

The other forty occurrences, with definite al-sāʿah, concern the promised resurrection event.

In these passages, the Quran describes the Hour’s coming, approach, arising, and suddenness, connecting it with resurrection and reckoning.

Yet the name alone does not specify the precise duration of all resurrection events.

Both uses occur in Al-Rūm 30:55: first al-sāʿah for the resurrection event, then sāʿah for the short stay the guilty suppose they have experienced. This is particularly clear evidence of the distinction.

Suwāʿ occurs only once, in Nūḥ 71:23, as a deity’s name among Noah’s people.

Arabian historical sources also connect the name with pre-Islamic places of worship. Such reports do not conclusively establish the name’s origin or how deities might have been transmitted from Noah’s time.

Shared letters therefore do not justify translating Suwāʿ as hour, time, or loss.

Lexicons also record passing on, continued movement, wandering livestock, and loss under S W ʿ. Some branches admit explanatory connections, but these do not prove that every branch arose from one specific historical meaning.

The family can be defined as follows:

In its principal Quranic branch, S W ʿ concerns sāʿah, a portion of time or an appointed time. In resurrection contexts, al-sāʿah names the promised event marking the end of the present stage of life and the opening of the reckoning. Suwāʿ is also a deity’s proper name in Noah’s story; its historical derivational relationship to time has not been established.

This definition preserves three essential distinctions.

First, a duration is not identical with the event occurring within it. Sāʿah can be part of a day, whereas al-sāʿah in resurrection contexts names a particular event.

Second, knowledge that an event will occur differs from knowing exactly when. The Quran announces the Hour while assigning knowledge of its time to God.

Third, shared letters do not establish a shared historical meaning. Suwāʿ is a proper name; its classification under the same letters does not authorize imposing the meanings of sāʿah or other lexical branches upon it.

Preserving these distinctions clarifies the temporal and religious uses of S W ʿ without conflating lexical meaning with later terminology, speculative histories of deities’ names, or unsupported calculations of the resurrection’s date.
