The Qur’anic Root B T R (ب ت ر)

Cutting off and severing continuation: from physical loss to the interruption of continuity, descendants, and lasting effects

Qur’anic and lexical forms

الْأَبْتَرُ
(al-abtaru)
Sole Qur’anic form; Al-Kawthar 108:3

أَبْتَر / بَتْرَاء
(abtar / batrāʾ)
Masculine and feminine descriptions: cut off or without continuation

بَتَرَ / يَبْتُرُ / بَتْر
(batara / yabturu / batr)
Verb and verbal noun concerning cutting; not Qur’anic

بَاتِر
(bātir)
A description of a cutting instrument; not Qur’anic

بَتَّار / بَتُور
(battār / batūr)
Intensive forms for a powerful cutting instrument; not Qur’anic

اِنْبَتَرَ
(inbatara)
Was cut off or interrupted; not Qur’anic

تَبَتَّرَ
(tabattara)
A lexical verb in the family of severance; not Qur’anic

أُبَاتِر
(ubātir)
In the poetic witness: a severer of kinship ties; not Qur’anic

In Arabic, B T R concerns cutting and separating part of something, especially a cutting that severs its tail or continuation and leaves it incomplete or without extension. Batara (بَتَرَ) describes cutting; abtar (أَبْتَر) describes something or someone whose tail has been cut off or whose continuation has ceased.

Physically, an animal with a severed tail is called abtar. Applied to people, the same description can concern someone with no surviving offspring or successor. More broadly, it can describe a person or undertaking whose effects, connection, or continuation have been cut off.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs only at the end of Al-Kawthar, where The God tells Muḥammad that the one who hates him is himself al-abtar (الْأَبْتَرُ).

This expression occurs in a surah beginning with the gift of al-Kawthar. One end of the surah speaks of abundant giving, while the other attributes severance and lack of continuation to the Prophet’s enemy. Abtar must therefore be examined through this contrast and the verse’s construction, rather than being restricted from the outset to having no son, physical destruction, or the disappearance of someone’s name.

1. Linguistic forms and earlier Arabic usage

The Qur’an contains only al-abtar. It is a description on the afʿal pattern, with the Arabic feminine batrāʾ (بَتْرَاء). In this use, the construction describes a condition of severance or deficiency, rather than necessarily being an elative meaning “more cut off.”

Arabic records batara (بَتَرَ), yabturu (يَبْتُرُ), batr (بَتْر), bātir (بَاتِر), battār (بَتَّار), batūr (بَتُور), inbatara (اِنْبَتَرَ), tabattara (تَبَتَّرَ), abtar (أَبْتَر), and batrāʾ (بَتْرَاء). Ubātir (أُبَاتِر) also occurs concerning the severing of kinship ties or someone short or deficient.

These forms do not all share one meaning or grammatical role. Some concern cutting, some describe a cutting instrument, and others express the resulting condition of severance or interrupted continuity.

Cutting before completion

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the root’s basic meaning as cutting something before completing it.

This explanation concerns more than separating a part: something capable of continuing is cut short and prevented from its natural extension.

For example, when an animal’s tail is cut off, the animal remains alive and present, but the part forming its bodily continuation has been lost.

The same image is intelligible for an undertaking abandoned unfinished. It may have begun, but its continuation is interrupted before completion.

Some lexical sources also record batr in the broader sense of cutting. “Before completion” is therefore a prominent feature in explaining the root’s core, rather than an exceptionless condition governing every Arabic use.

Batara al-dhanaba: the root’s physical image

One clear use of this family is cutting off an animal’s tail.

Al-abtar can describe an animal whose tail has been severed or which is naturally tailless or short-tailed.

This image matters because it shows the semantic relationship between cutting and losing a tail or continuation.

An animal with its tail cut off has not necessarily perished. The point is the loss of the part that formed its bodily continuation.

This helps explain the use of abtar for a person lacking continuation or a successor. A physical tail in one instance may correspond, in another, to the continuation of someone’s descendants, relationships, or effects.

Semantic proximity does not itself establish the historical sequence of these developments. The evidence attests physical and human uses; dating the emergence of each requires independent historical documentation.

A cutting sword and a severed relationship

Arabic uses sayfun bātirun (سَيْفٌ بَاتِرٌ) for a sword that cuts. Battār and batūr are also recorded for a very sharp or powerful cutting sword.

Here the root concerns cutting itself and an instrument’s ability to perform it. Abtar, by comparison, more often describes the condition resulting from severance or the absence of a tail or continuation.

The family also includes batara raḥimahu (بَتَرَ رَحِمَهُ): he severed his kinship ties.

This use shows that severance need not be physical. A relationship between people may be broken by their conduct.

Here no animal’s tail is cut off and no object severed with a sword. What has disappeared is the connection and continuation of a relationship.

A witness from earlier Arabic poetry

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits a line attributed to ʿUbādah ibn Ṭahfah al-Māzinī, under the kunyah Abū al-Raʾīs al-Māzinī. Asās al-Balāghah gives the same witness under Abū al-Rubays. This variation in the transmitted name should be kept in view when attributing the example:

شَدِيدُ وِكَاءِ الْبَطْنِ ضَبُّ ضَغِينَةٍ
عَلَى قَطْعِ ذِي الْقُرْبَى أَحَذُّ أُبَاتِرُ

shadīdu wikāʾi al-baṭni ḍabbu ḍaghīnatin / ʿalā qaṭʿi dhī al-qurbā aḥadhdhu ubātiru

The line means: “His belly-string is tightly tied [an image of miserliness], a lizard of rancour; swift and severing when it comes to cutting off a kinsman.” The poet censures a miserly, resentful man quick to break relations with relatives.

Ubātir here describes someone who cuts off kinship ties.

The example matters because severance has a social meaning here. What is cut off is neither a body part nor a material object, but a relationship that could have remained and continued.

A variant first hemistich beginning laʾīmun (لَئِيمٌ) is also transmitted. Ibn Barrī reports it from Al-Jawharī and calls the shadīdu wikāʾi al-baṭni reading better known. The exact composition date and a pre-Islamic attribution are uncertain; the line therefore attests the poetry transmitted in lexicons and must not be presented without evidence as indisputably pre-Islamic.

Batrāʾ: something missing a part

Batrāʾ is the feminine of abtar. Arabic uses it for something with a part cut off or something incomplete.

In a later use, a sermon lacking its recognized opening is called khuṭbatun batrāʾ (خُطْبَةٌ بَتْرَاء). This expression is particularly transmitted for a sermon delivered without its opening praise of The God.

This use shows that batr can describe a deficiency or omission within the structure of a discourse.

The oratorical term belongs to periods after the beginning of Islam and must not be offered as definitive evidence of pre-Islamic usage.

Batrāʾ in this use also does not mean false or worthless discourse. The deficiency concerns its structure and the omission of a part. Whether its content is true or false is a separate question.

2. Al-abtar in Al-Kawthar

Al-Kawthar contains three verses:

إِنَّا أَعْطَيْنَاكَ الْكَوْثَرَ

innā aʿṭaynāka al-kawthara

“Indeed, We have given you al-Kawthar, that abundant gift.” (Al-Kawthar 108:1)

فَصَلِّ لِرَبِّكَ وَانْحَرْ

fa-ṣalli li-rabbika wa-nḥar

“So pray to your Lord and offer sacrifice.” (Al-Kawthar 108:2)

إِنَّ شَانِئَكَ هُوَ الْأَبْتَرُ

inna shāniʾaka huwa al-abtaru

“Indeed, the one who hates you is himself cut off, without continuation.” (Al-Kawthar 108:3)

In the third verse, al-abtar describes the person introduced by shāniʾaka (شَانِئَكَ).

Shāniʾ comes from SH N A and means someone who harbours hatred or hostility. The suffix -ka refers to Muḥammad. The verse thus concerns someone who hates Muḥammad.

Here the Qur’an attributes abtar to his enemy, rather than to Muḥammad.

This attribution matters in relation to the surah’s opening. The person targeted by hostility and possibly contempt is presented as receiving al-Kawthar; the one whose hostility seeks to portray him as without continuation or a future is described as abtar.

Abtar: cut off, rather than necessarily destroyed

Physically, something cut off has not necessarily disappeared entirely.

A sword severing part of an object may leave the rest in place. A short-tailed animal also remains present while lacking its natural bodily continuation.

The same distinction matters in human usage.

Calling someone abtar need not imply physical destruction or immediate death. The intended meaning may concern interrupted continuation, effects, connection, or outcome.

“Perished,” “dead,” and “destroyed” are therefore not direct equivalents of abtar in every context.

Al-Kawthar likewise makes no direct announcement of the death or physical destruction of Muḥammad’s enemy. Al-abtar emphasizes his being cut off and lacking continuation.

In context, this severance may concern the fruitlessness of hostility, the interruption of good effects, or failure to leave a worthwhile continuation.

The word alone, however, does not determine when or through which historical event this outcome becomes apparent.

The role of huwa in the sentence

The verse’s construction is: inna shāniʾaka huwa al-abtaru (إِنَّ شَانِئَكَ هُوَ الْأَبْتَرُ).

Inna begins the sentence emphatically. Shāniʾaka identifies the person being described, and al-abtar is what is predicated of him.

Huwa (هُوَ) stands between them.

Grammatically, huwa can be analysed as a separating pronoun, adding emphasis and focusing the predicate on the person in question. Alternatively, huwa is the subject of a nominal sentence, al-abtar its predicate, and the whole nominal sentence serves as the predicate of inna.

Both analyses suit the emphasis on attributing al-abtar to shāniʾaka.

Accordingly, “himself” is appropriate in English: “The one who hates you is himself cut off.”

Huwa does not establish that nobody else can ever be described as abtar in any other situation. The emphasis and contrast belong to this context: in response to hostility and contempt, severance is attributed to the enemy himself.

Does shāniʾaka identify only one historical person?

Shāniʾaka is a singular active participle. In a particular situation it can refer to a specific individual, but the verse itself does not name him.

Exegetical reports identify different people or occasions, including Al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, ʿUqbah ibn Abī Muʿayṭ, and a group from Quraysh.

Some reports say that after Muḥammad’s son or sons died, opponents called him abtar, supposing that his name and call would not continue after his death.

Another report concerns separation from his people and community: opponents called Muḥammad unsupported and cut off because he had distanced himself from earlier tribal beliefs and affiliations.

These reports help reconstruct the surah’s possible linguistic and social setting. They differ, however, and their details must not all be treated as facts expressly stated in the Qur’an.

The verse names nobody. Structurally, shāniʾaka can apply to anyone who exemplifies the hostility in question.

Understanding al-abtar therefore does not depend on proving the identity of one particular historical person.

3. Interrupted descendants, or interrupted effects and outcome?

One familiar Arabic use of abtar describes a man without children or a successor.

Lexicons apply al-abtar to a man with no surviving posterity. In some earlier social uses, the description especially concerned a man whose sons had died.

A word’s social use must, however, be distinguished from moral judgments about human worth.

Having no son is not the invariable meaning of abtar

In a society where a family’s name and standing were understood chiefly through sons, having no son could become a pretext for humiliating a man.

This historical setting explains how abtar could be used as a taunt against someone without a living son.

Such usage does not mean that the Qur’an presents childlessness or having no son as a moral defect.

Al-Shūrā 42:49 and Al-Shūrā 42:50 present daughters and sons as gifts of The God and also speak of some people being childless.

Having or not having children must therefore not be equated with a person’s moral worth.

If Muḥammad’s enemies considered a child’s death or the absence of a son evidence of his worthlessness, that taunt may be the subject of the surah’s response. The response need not accept the same discriminatory criterion and merely transfer it to someone else.

The verse does not say that everyone without a son is worthless. Its subject is hostility to Muḥammad and the outcome of that hostility.

Did Muḥammad’s enemy actually remain without descendants?

If al-abtar in Al-Kawthar 108:3 means exclusively a man with no surviving children or descendants, its historical identification encounters difficulties.

For example, reports identifying Al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil as the verse’s referent also identify him as the father of ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ and Hishām ibn al-ʿĀṣ.

Merely attributing the verse to him therefore cannot establish that he necessarily had no son or that his biological line had already ended at that time.

This is an important consideration in examining the broader meaning of al-abtar.

Arabic can apply abtar to someone whose good effects or continuation have been cut off. The verse may accordingly concern failure, fruitless hostility, or being cut off from good and a favourable outcome.

This meaning must not become an unsupported claim that every enemy of Muḥammad has disappeared from historical memory. Some opponents remain known in historical accounts.

A name remaining in history books differs from leaving a good and worthwhile effect.

“Being cut off from good effects” or “hostility coming to nothing” thus fits the word’s range and the surah’s context better than treating the verse as a promise that every opponent’s biological descendants will perish.

Al-abtar and the interruption of good effects

Maqāyīs al-Lughah also records abtar for someone whose effects have been cut off from good. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī likewise emphasizes interrupted remembrance and effects when explaining the Qur’anic word.

In Arabic, a person’s continuation is therefore not confined to biological children. The effects of conduct, good remembrance, social relationships, and the results of work can also constitute continuation.

Someone may have many children yet leave no beneficial effect through their conduct. Someone else may have no children, while their knowledge, justice, or service continues to benefit others after death.

This illustrates the semantic field of severance and continuing effects; it does not mean that abtar directly denotes a lack of scholarly or social achievements in every sentence.

In Al-Kawthar 108:3, hostility towards Muḥammad is the sentence’s subject matter. The severance in question may therefore concern that hostility’s fruitlessness and failure to extinguish the effects of his call.

This reading preserves an established meaning of abtar while avoiding restricting the verse to judgments about fertility or the number of someone’s children.

4. The contrast between al-Kawthar and al-abtar

At the surah’s opening, The God speaks of giving al-Kawthar to Muḥammad.

Kawthar belongs to K TH R and concerns abundance and much good. Abtar belongs to B T R and concerns severance and interrupted continuation.

Study of K TH R

Lexically, these words are neither synonyms nor direct antonyms. One concerns abundance; the other severance.

Their placement at the surah’s beginning and end nevertheless creates a clear contrast: abundant giving on one side, severance and lack of continuation on the other.

Abundance contrasted with lack of continuation

In everyday use, having much of something does not necessarily mean that it continues. Someone may have great wealth today and lose it tomorrow.

Severance likewise does not always mean a small quantity. Something once very large or extensive may have its continuation cut off.

Kawthar and abtar therefore do not merely present a numerical contrast between much and little.

In the surah, the abundant divine gift to Muḥammad stands opposite the description of his enemy as cut off and without continuation.

This relationship may concern the continuation of effects and the enemies’ failed attempt to end Muḥammad’s call.

“Continuity” should not, however, be made an independent lexical meaning of kawthar without explanation. Abundance is its primary meaning; the contrast with abtar brings continuation and outcome into the understanding of the surah.

Wa-rafaʿnā laka dhikraka: a person’s remembrance and effects

Al-Sharḥ states:

وَرَفَعْنَا لَكَ ذِكْرَكَ

wa-rafaʿnā laka dhikraka

“And We raised your remembrance for you.” (Al-Sharḥ 94:4)

This verse matters for understanding the continuation of Muḥammad’s remembrance and effects.

Al-Kawthar describes his enemy as abtar, while Al-Sharḥ speaks of Muḥammad’s remembrance being raised.

The two statements are compatible and can be examined together among verses concerning his call and standing.

Dhikr in Al-Sharḥ and batr in Al-Kawthar are different roots. Al-abtar must not be lexically restricted to “someone whose name is forgotten”: that conclusion depends on combining indications and interpreting context.

Study of DH K R

“Remaining without continuation or effect” is therefore defensible as explanation, but the base translation should preserve the image of being cut off.

The measure of worth: descendants or effects?

Some cultures have associated the continuation of a person’s name with having children, especially sons.

Human worth cannot, however, be measured solely by the continuation of a bloodline.

Someone with many children may leave oppression and corruption that harm society. Someone with no children may leave a good and lasting effect through conduct, knowledge, and service.

This distinction suits Al-Kawthar’s contrast. Enemies may have imagined Muḥammad without continuation on the basis of kinship and social power, while the Qur’an presents his future and outcome through divine giving, rather than that same humiliating criterion.

What becomes significant in context is therefore the continuation of good and the worth of the call, rather than simply the number of children or members of a family.

This conclusion is reflection arising from the verses’ relationship; it must not be presented as the independent lexical meaning of every B T R derivative.

5. Distinctions from nearby roots

B T R (batara) compared with Q Ṭ ʿ (qaṭaʿa)

Study of Q Ṭ ʿ

Qaṭaʿa is a widely used Arabic verb for cutting and separating.

Such cutting can concern physical objects, a road, a relationship, a route, time, or other connections.

Batr also concerns cutting, but familiar uses highlight loss of a tail or continuation, incompleteness, or being prevented from continuing.

For example, cutting a rope can be expressed through qaṭʿ. When the focus is on severing a part and leaving something without its continuation, the field of batr becomes more appropriate.

This distinction must not be absolute. Earlier Arabic also records batr for cutting broadly, and the two families overlap in some uses.

B T R (batara) compared with B T K (bataka)

Study of B T K

In Arabic, batk concerns cutting and separating, especially cutting or splitting off part of something.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:119 uses this family concerning cutting livestock’s ears.

In their physical image, batk and batr are close: both can concern cutting part of a body or object.

The Qur’anic B T R occurrence, however, describes a person in connection with severed continuation or effects, whereas B T K in Al-Nisāʾ 4:119 describes the act of cutting ears.

Semantic proximity must not merge the two roots.

B T R (batara) compared with B T L (batala)

Study of B T L

Batl also concerns separation and cutting in Arabic, but its Qur’anic use develops a different field.

In Al-Muzzammil 73:8, tabattal (تَبَتَّلْ) concerns turning to and devoting oneself to The God. Batūl (بَتُول) is familiar in religious language, but does not itself occur in the Qur’an. Baghiyyan (بَغِيًّا) in Maryam 19:20 belongs to B GH Y and must not be assigned to B T L.

Study of B GH Y

In the batl family, separation can concern setting attachments aside to devote oneself to a particular aim.

Abtar, however, concerns severance and the absence of continuation, rather than devotion to The God.

Although both families share an image of separation, their direction and usage differ.

B T R (batara) compared with T B R (tabbara)

Study of T B R

T B R concerns ruin and destruction in the Qur’an. Al-Furqān 25:39 contains its verb for destroying; Nūḥ 71:28 contains tabāran (تَبَارًا).

Batr does not necessarily express complete destruction. Something may lose a part or continuation while its main body remains.

Rearranging two roots’ letters is not by itself evidence of shared derivation or historical identity.

B T R (batara) compared with SH N A (shaniʾa)

Study of SH N A

SH N A occurs in the same verse of Al-Kawthar as shāniʾaka (شَانِئَكَ).

This family concerns hatred and hostility; B T R concerns severance and interrupted continuity.

In inna shāniʾaka huwa al-abtaru, the person is first identified through hostility, then severance is attributed to him.

The words have different semantic roles. Shāniʾ identifies someone through conduct and stance; abtar describes that person’s condition or outcome.

“Being hostile” must not be inserted into the definition of abtar, just as shāniʾ cannot be made to mean “without continuation.”

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Batara can be rendered “cut,” “cut off,” or, where appropriate, “severed something’s continuation.”

Inbatara can mean “was cut off,” “was severed,” or “lost its continuation.”

Bātir describes a “cutting” or “sharp” instrument; battār and batūr describe one that cuts very powerfully. Depending on what it describes, batrāʾ can be rendered “cut off,” “without continuation,” or “incomplete and unfinished.”

For physical abtar, “with its tail cut off” or “tailless” is suitable. In uses concerning descendants, “childless,” “without a successor,” or “without continuation” can convey the meaning, but these require contextual support.

In Al-Kawthar 108:3, the principal translation should preserve the image of severance while avoiding restriction to infertility or having no son.

The proposed base translation is:

“Indeed, the one who hates you is himself cut off.”

This also agrees with Forqan’s current text and preserves the basic meaning without adding a historical interpretation.

The explanation can clarify that al-abtar here concerns someone cut off and without continuation: one deprived of continuing good effects, contrary to his own hostile expectations.

“Without descendants” or “without a son” is too restrictive for the base text, substituting one historical use for the word’s full meaning.

“Forgotten” alone is also insufficient: someone’s name may remain in history while their remembrance and effects lack good value because of wrongdoing.

“Destroyed” and “perished” likewise add a stronger meaning than abtar itself and should not replace it without contextual grounds.

For Forqan, “cut off” should therefore remain in the translation; lack of continuation, interrupted effects, and the contrast with al-Kawthar can be explained in the root study and reflection on the verse.

Conclusion

In Arabic, B T R concerns cutting and severance, especially when something loses its tail or continuation and remains incomplete or without extension.

This is clear in physical uses, such as an animal with a severed tail. Human and social uses can concern broken relationships, lack of a successor, interrupted descendants, or the loss of an undertaking’s continuing effects.

The Qur’an contains only al-abtar. At the end of Al-Kawthar, it describes the person who hates Muḥammad.

Historical reports about the taunt of childlessness and a severed line illuminate one possible background. The Qur’an itself, however, names no particular person here and does not restrict abtar to having no son.

The surah places the gift of al-Kawthar opposite the enemy’s severance and lack of continuation. This contrast suggests that human worth and continuing effects should not be measured solely by descent, tribal criteria, or the number of children.

The semantic core of B T R is cutting and severing something’s tail or continuation. Abtar describes someone or something cut off from its continuation. In Al-Kawthar 108:3, the description applies to Muḥammad’s enemy and contrasts with the abundant giving that The God announces at the surah’s beginning.
