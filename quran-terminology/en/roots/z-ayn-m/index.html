Core meaning: in speech, making a claim or advancing a statement whose truth must be assessed; in the branch of undertaking, accepting responsibility, guaranteeing, or taking charge of something.

1. The root in early Arabic and pre-Islamic poetry

The Arabic root Z ʿ M has two prominent semantic branches. The first concerns saying, claiming, supposing, and attributing a statement to oneself or another. The second concerns accepting responsibility, guaranteeing something, and undertaking another person’s affairs.

In speech, زَعَمَ is used when someone advances a statement or claims that something is true. In many uses it conveys doubt about the statement, particularly when adequate evidence has not been offered.

The word does not, however, mean lying in every use. A claim can be true or false. Its truth depends on its content and the relevant evidence.

In the other branch, زَعِيم denotes someone who undertakes responsibility for a matter or guarantees its performance. The related زعامة is also used for leadership and supervision.

One early lexical analysis distinguishes two branches:

أَحَدُهُمَا الْقَوْلُ مِنْ غَيْرِ صِحَّةٍ وَلَا يَقِينٍ، وَالْآخَرُ التَّكَفُّلُ بِالشَّيْءِ

aḥaduhumā al-qawlu min ghayri ṣiḥḥatin wa-lā yaqīnin wa-al-ākharu al-takaffulu bi-al-shayʾ

That is: “One is speech without truth or certainty; the other is undertaking responsibility for something.” This formulation emphasizes the doubtful use of the word; the wider evidence also records its use for true statements.

This division identifies two important branches, but actual usage must also be examined when studying the word before the Qur’an.

First example: claiming in the poetry of al-Aʿshā

The pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿshā says in a poem addressing tribal groups:

زَعَمَتْ حَنِيفَةُ لَا تُجِيرُ عَلَيْهِمُ
بِدِمَائِهِمْ وَأَظُنُّهَا سَتُجِيرُ

zaʿamat Ḥanīfatu lā tujīru ʿalayhimu / bi-dimāʾihim wa-aẓunnuhā satujīru

Translation:

“Ḥanīfah claims that it will not grant protection concerning their blood; yet I think it will.”

Here زَعَمَتْ introduces a statement attributed to the tribe of Ḥanīfah.

The poet reports the statement but adds وَأَظُنُّهَا سَتُجِيرُ, indicating that he expects their actual conduct to contradict their claim.

The following line also rejects their claim.

This example shows that pre-Islamic Arabic used زعم to express a claim or attribute a statement to a speaker, a statement the hearer could accept or reject.

In this use, زعم denotes making a claim; the judgment that the claim is false comes from evidence and the poet’s response.

Second example: desire and hope in ʿAntarah’s poetry

ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād says in his Muʿallaqah:

عُلِّقْتُهَا عَرَضًا وَأَقْتُلُ قَوْمَهَا
زَعْمًا لَعَمْرُ أَبِيكِ لَيْسَ بِمَزْعَمِ

ʿulliqtuhā ʿaraḍan wa-aqtulu qawmahā / zaʿman la-ʿamru abīki laysa bi-mazʿami

Translation:

“I fell in love with her unexpectedly, while killing her people: a hope, by your father’s life, that offers no ground for hope.”

Here زَعْمًا is explained as desire or hope for something that seems difficult or impossible to attain.

مَزْعَم likewise refers to a ground or occasion for hope and desire.

The poet speaks of loving a woman despite hostility between her people and his own. He therefore sees his hope of reaching her as misplaced.

Early explanations take two approaches: desire and hope, or a claim or conception lacking a realistic basis. Both emphasize the gap between what a person entertains and what can actually be achieved.

The example shows that the pre-Islamic semantic field was not confined to spoken claims: some constructions also expressed expectation, desire, and hope.

Guarantee and responsibility in early Arabic

Arabic used زَعِيم for someone who guaranteed something or accepted responsibility for it.

A group’s chief or supervisor was also called زعيم, in connection with undertaking its affairs and speaking on its behalf.

A transmitted line attributed to ʿAmr ibn Shaʾs reads as follows; another attribution names Muḍarris:

تَقُولُ هَلَكْنَا إِنْ هَلَكْتَ وَإِنَّمَا
عَلَى اللَّهِ أَرْزَاقُ الْعِبَادِ كَمَا زَعَمْ

taqūlu halaknā in halakta wa-innamā / ʿalā Allāhi arzāqu al-ʿibādi kamā zaʿam

Sense of the line:

“She says, ‘If you perish, we perish!’ Yet the servants’ provision rests with The God, as He has said and promised.”

In this example, زعم has been explained as saying or promising. It is related to undertaking provision, but should not be presented as unambiguous evidence for the precise sense “guaranteeing.”

ʿAmr ibn Shaʾs lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The date of this particular line is uncertain, and its attribution also varies; it is therefore not securely dated pre-Islamic evidence.

The verse supports the saying-and-promising usage. Guarantee and undertaking are independently attested in lexical expressions such as زَعَمَ بِالْأَمْرِ and in زَعِيم.

What the early evidence supports

The evidence supports two conclusions.

First, pre-Islamic Arabic used زعم for claiming or advancing a statement that could be accepted, doubted, or rejected. Some uses also expressed hope and desire.

Second, the early Arabic family of زعيم concerned undertaking, guarantee, and responsibility, although the precise history of every derivative cannot be established from these examples alone.

The entire root cannot therefore be reduced to “lying” or “supposing without evidence.”

A conceptual relationship between claiming and guaranteeing does not establish a historical derivation, nor does it mean that every claimant guarantees the claim. The two senses have distinct uses in Arabic.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَعَمَ يَزْعُمُ

This is a basic triliteral verb, commonly meaning “claimed,” “said,” or “supposed.”

زَعَمَ أَنَّ الْأَمْرَ كَذَا

zaʿama anna al-amra kadhā

That is: “He claimed or supposed that the matter was such-and-such.”

The construction attributes a statement to someone, but the presence of زعم alone does not prove the statement false.

In many uses, choosing this verb indicates that the person reporting the claim does not regard it as established or reliable.

زَعْم and زُعْم

These are recorded verbal nouns denoting a claim, statement, supposition, or assertion attributed to a person.

هَذَا زَعْمُ فُلَانٍ

hādhā zaʿmu fulānin

That is: “This is so-and-so’s claim.”

The expression does not necessarily endorse the claim.

زعم can also denote an unfounded claim or false statement. That meaning belongs to particular uses and contexts; not every statement called زعم is automatically false.

زَعَمَ بِالشَّيْءِ

In the undertaking branch, this is recorded as meaning to accept responsibility for something or guarantee it.

This use is also attested with the preposition باء.

زَعَمَ بِالْأَمْرِ

zaʿama bi-al-amri

That is: “He undertook that matter.”

This construction differs from زعم أن in the sense of claiming.

زَعِمَ يَزْعَمُ

One lexical use, with a kasrah on the middle root consonant in the past tense, denotes desiring or hoping for something.

This is the branch represented by زَعْمًا and مَزْعَم in the explanation of ʿAntarah’s verse.

Its existence does not mean that every Qur’anic form of زعم means hope or desire.

أَزْعَمَ

In the desire branch, this is recorded as giving someone hope or arousing desire.

أَزْعَمَهُ

azʿamahu

That is: “He gave him hope or aroused his desire.”

This use does not occur in the Qur’an.

زَاعِم

The active participle of زعم denotes a claimant or someone advancing a statement.

The designation alone does not establish that the person is lying.

The truth or falsity of the claim must be examined separately.

زَعِيم

This denotes someone who guarantees something or undertakes responsibility for it.

زَعِيمُ الْقَوْمِ also denotes the chief or supervisor of a group.

That sense is related to undertaking others’ affairs and speaking on their behalf.

In the Qur’an, زعيم means a guarantor or one responsible for an undertaking.

زُعَمَاء

This is the plural of زعيم.

In the leadership sense, it denotes the chiefs or supervisors of groups.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

زَعَامَة

This denotes guarantee, undertaking, and supervision.

In social usage it also means leadership or authority.

Lexical accounts also record زعامة for the choicest share of property or inheritance, weapons or armor, and leadership. These senses must be distinguished in context.

Some of these uses relate to social organization and the position of a chief, but none should be treated as the invariable meaning of the whole root.

مَزْعَم

This can denote a claim or disputed statement, or, in the desire branch, a ground for hope or expectation.

زَعَمَ فِي غَيْرِ مَزْعَمٍ

zaʿama fī ghayri mazʿamin

That is: “He hoped for something that offered no ground for hope.”

Context determines the sense.

مَزَاعِم

The plural of مزعم is used for claims, disputed statements, or assertions whose truth is uncertain.

Doubt can be prominent here, but a مزعم is not necessarily a deliberate lie.

تَزَعَّمَ

Arabic uses this for making claims, with some early accounts specifically associating it with false assertion; it is also used for assuming leadership or supervision.

The two uses must be distinguished through sentence structure and subject matter.

زَعُوم

This is recorded for an animal whose fatness is uncertain and which is felt by hand to assess its condition.

The use relates to doubt and lack of certainty, but does not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Qur’anic uses: claims, suppositions, and unsupported statements

The root Z ʿ M occurs seventeen times in the Qur’an: thirteen verbal occurrences, two instances of the noun زعم, and two of زعيم.

The verbal and nominal uses of زعم mostly concern claims whose truth the Qur’an challenges. زعيم, by contrast, concerns guarantee and responsibility.

Claiming faith: Al-Nisāʾ 4:60

Al-Nisāʾ 4:60 describes people who claim to believe in what was revealed to the Messenger and before him, yet wish to seek judgment from ṭāghūt despite being commanded to reject it.

يَزْعُمُونَ introduces their claim.

The important contrast is between their profession of faith and their conduct.

The Qur’an does not stop at reporting their words; it sets their actions beside them.

In this context, زعم introduces a claim whose sincerity and credibility are tested against behavior.

The verb يزعمون alone does not establish that everyone making a false claim knowingly lies. Intent and awareness are distinct questions from the inconsistency of words and actions.

Claims about partners of The God: Al-Anʿām 6:22 and Al-Anʿām 6:94

Al-Anʿām 6:22 asks where the partners they claimed are.

Al-Anʿām 6:94 uses the root twice.

First, it mentions intercessors whom they claimed to be partners concerning them.

It then states that what they once claimed has vanished from them and their bonds have been severed.

Here زعم attributes a status or authority to alleged deities or intercessors, and the Qur’an rejects those attributions.

These are not merely casual statements: they have shaped religious belief and conduct.

The rejection of such partnership and independent intercession comes from the surrounding verses, not from a lexical meaning of “associating partners” inherent in زعم.

Alleged partners and independent deities: Al-Isrāʾ, Al-Kahf, Al-Qaṣaṣ, and Sabaʾ

The same theme recurs in other verses.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:56 tells the addressees to call upon those whom they supposed to have authority apart from The God, then states that these cannot remove or redirect harm.

Al-Kahf 18:52 mentions the partners people claimed, who do not respond when called.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:62 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:74 repeat the question about the partners they claimed.

Sabaʾ 34:22 invites people to call upon those to whom they attributed power apart from The God. It then denies that they possess even an atom’s weight in the heavens or earth, share in them, or provide Him assistance.

These verses show that repeating a religious claim does not establish its truth.

Claims about a partner, intercessor, or deity possessing authority must be assessed against reality and evidence.

زعم introduces the claim that the Qur’anic text puts to the test.

A claim attributed to the Messenger: Al-Isrāʾ 17:92

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:92, opponents demand that the Messenger cause the sky to fall upon them in pieces, as they attribute to his assertion, or bring The God and the angels before them.

كَمَا زَعَمْتَ occurs in the opponents’ address to the Messenger.

Their wording does not establish that he had made precisely their demand in precisely those words.

What the Qur’an reports from the Messenger must be distinguished from what his opponents say he asserted.

Here زعم is used by opponents in attributing a claim to another; reporting their speech does not independently endorse their formulation.

Denying resurrection: Al-Kahf 18:48 and Al-Taghābun 64:7

In Al-Kahf 18:48, those assembled before The God are reminded that they had claimed no appointed meeting would be set for them.

Al-Taghābun 64:7 states:

زَعَمَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَنْ لَنْ يُبْعَثُوا قُلْ بَلَىٰ وَرَبِّي لَتُبْعَثُنَّ ثُمَّ لَتُنَبَّؤُنَّ بِمَا عَمِلْتُمْ وَذَٰلِكَ عَلَى اللَّهِ يَسِيرٌ

zaʿama alladhīna kafarū an lan yubʿathū qul balā wa-rabbī la-tubʿathunna thumma la-tunabbaʾunna bi-mā ʿamiltum wa-dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīr

Al-Taghābun 64:7

Translation:

“Those who disbelieved claimed that they would never be raised. Say: Yes indeed, by my Lord, you will certainly be raised; then you will certainly be informed of what you did. That is easy for The God.”

Here زعم introduces the claim of those who deny resurrection.

The reply قُلْ بَلَىٰ directly refutes it.

The claim’s falsity is therefore not inferred from زعم alone; the Qur’an’s explicit answer establishes it.

Translating زعم invariably as “lying” would erase the distinction between advancing a claim and demonstrating its falsity.

“Claimed” suits this verse: it identifies their assertion and leaves the judgment to the complete statement.

Claiming exclusive favor: Al-Jumuʿah 62:6

Al-Jumuʿah 62:6 addresses those who are Jews: if they claim to be The God’s favored allies to the exclusion of other people, they are to wish for death, if they are truthful.

زَعَمْتُمْ follows the conditional إِنْ.

إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ then explicitly raises the truthfulness and credibility of the claim.

The sentence thus distinguishes advancing a claim from establishing its truth.

The issue is a claim to exclusive favor with The God, not simply possessing a religious identity.

This relates to Al-Baqarah 2:111, where an exclusive claim to entry into the Garden is met with a demand for proof.

4. بِزَعْمِهِمْ: religious claims and unsupported legislation

Al-Anʿām 6:136

Al-Anʿām 6:136 describes people assigning a share of the crops and livestock The God created to Him and a share to their alleged partners.

They say that part belongs to The God and part to their partners.

The Qur’an marks this allocation with بِزَعْمِهِمْ: “according to their own claim.”

The expression distinguishes human attribution to The God from an actual divine ruling.

They claim religious authority for the division, but their assertion does not make it a divine command.

The rest of the verse condemns their unjust practice and bad judgment.

Here زعم is more than a private supposition: the claim shapes economic and religious behavior.

Al-Anʿām 6:138

In Al-Anʿām 6:138, people announce rules about livestock and crops, including that some are restricted and may be eaten only by those they choose.

The Qur’an again describes this with بِزَعْمِهِمْ.

The same verse explicitly mentions fabrication against The God.

The invalidity of those rules is established by the Qur’an’s express judgment, not by a universal lexical meaning of “fabricating legislation” in زعم.

Together, the two verses make a clear point:

A religious claim does not establish its divine origin merely by invoking The God or taking the form of a rule or ritual.

Attributing something to The God requires grounds for that attribution.

This accords with Al-Aʿrāf 7:33, which condemns saying what one does not know about The God.

Al-Naḥl 16:116 likewise prohibits falsely declaring things lawful or unlawful and attributing that to The God.

In these passages, بزعمهم is not merely neutral reporting: it exposes the distance between human assertion and actual divine authority.

5. زَعِيم: guarantee and responsibility

Alongside زعم in the claim sense, the Qur’an uses زعيم twice.

Both occurrences emphasize guarantee and undertaking responsibility.

Yūsuf 12:72

قَالُوا نَفْقِدُ صُوَاعَ الْمَلِكِ وَلِمَنْ جَاءَ بِهِ حِمْلُ بَعِيرٍ وَأَنَا بِهِ زَعِيمٌ

qālū nafqidu ṣuwāʿa al-maliki wa-li-man jāʾa bihi ḥimlu baʿīrin wa-anā bihi zaʿīm

Yūsuf 12:72

Translation:

“They said: We are missing the king’s measuring cup. Whoever brings it will receive a camel-load, and I guarantee it.”

Here زَعِيمٌ denotes the person guaranteeing payment of the announced reward.

بِهِ refers to the undertaking concerning that reward.

The speaker does not merely assert that a reward exists, but accepts responsibility for fulfilling it.

This is an important distinction between زعم as claiming and زعيم as guaranteeing.

In the first use, someone advances a statement.

In the second, someone undertakes responsibility for carrying something out.

The senses can be related in social life without being identical.

Al-Qalam 68:40

سَلْهُمْ أَيُّهُم بِذَٰلِكَ زَعِيمٌ

salhum ayyuhum bi-dhālika zaʿīm

Al-Qalam 68:40

Translation:

“Ask them which of them guarantees that.”

The preceding verses concern people who imagine a special standing and favorable outcome for themselves with The God.

Al-Qalam 68:35 asks whether those who submit are to be treated like wrongdoers.

Al-Qalam 68:37 asks whether they have a book containing the ruling they claim.

Al-Qalam 68:39 raises the possibility of binding pledges from The God.

Al-Qalam 68:40 then asks which of them guarantees the claim.

زعيم here denotes someone who would accept responsibility for the assertion and stand surety for it.

The question exposes the lack of valid support for their claim.

Both verses concern undertaking, but Yūsuf 12:72 guarantees a specific reward, whereas Al-Qalam 68:40 challenges an unsupported claim whose guarantee cannot be established.

The relationship to leadership

Arabic also uses زعيم for a chief or supervisor.

This use relates to responsibility for a group’s affairs.

A chief may represent the group, speak about its affairs, or undertake commitments.

Neither Qur’anic occurrence of زعيم denotes a political leader or religious guide.

Yūsuf 12:72 concerns the guarantor of a reward.

Al-Qalam 68:40 questions who guarantees a claim.

“Leader” is therefore inaccurate in these two verses, although that meaning exists elsewhere in Arabic.

6. Differences from related words

Z ʿ M (زَعْم) and Q W L (قَوْل)

قول denotes saying or expressing speech and has a wide range of uses.

Speech may be a report, question, command, promise, or opinion.

In its speech branch, زعم denotes an assertion someone advances, often with the reporter withholding definite endorsement.

Claiming can thus be a kind of saying, but not every instance of saying is a claim.

“So-and-so claims this,” for example, may do more than report speech: it can suggest that its truth remains unestablished.

Z ʿ M (زَعْم) and Ẓ N N (ظَنّ)

ظن concerns a person’s supposition, interpretation, or conception, with varying degrees of confidence.

زعم often emphasizes a claim advanced by or attributed to someone.

ظن may therefore describe an internal judgment, whereas زعم often appears as a stated or assessable claim.

This is not an absolute distinction. زعم can also approach supposing or believing, and ظن can be expressed in speech.

Choosing “claimed” or “supposed” in translation depends on the construction and context of the verse.

Z ʿ M (زَعْم) and K Dh B (كَذِب)

كذب concerns falsehood and speech contrary to reality.

The speech sense of زعم concerns making a claim.

A claim may be false, but falsehood is not inherent in every use of زعم.

In Al-Taghābun 64:7, the Qur’an’s response rejects the claim of those denying resurrection.

Al-Anʿām 6:138 explicitly mentions fabrication against The God.

Those judgments come from the verses’ content.

زعم should therefore not invariably be translated as “lying.”

Z ʿ M (زَعْم) and D ʿ W (دَعْوَى)

دعوى also concerns claiming or making a demand.

In some situations, زعم and دعوى can refer to the same assertion.

دعوى may be a request, demand, or assertion of a right; many Qur’anic uses of زعم attribute a proposition or belief to someone.

The words are not interchangeable in every construction.

Z ʿ M (زَعِيم) and K F L (كَفَالَة)

كفل concerns guarantee, undertaking, and responsibility.

In Qur’anic usage, زعيم likewise denotes a guarantor or one responsible for an undertaking.

In Yūsuf 12:72, زعيم is clearly related in meaning to كفيل.

The root K F L has other Qur’anic uses, however, including caring for people and taking charge of their affairs.

The overlap between زعيم and كفيل in guarantee does not make every derivative of the two roots identical.

Z ʿ M (زَعِيم) and Ḍ M N (ضَمَان)

ضمان concerns accepting responsibility for fulfilling or making good an undertaking.

Both Qur’anic uses of زعيم are close to “guarantor.”

In Arabic more broadly, زعيم is not limited to financial guarantees: it also denotes supervision and leadership.

Yūsuf 12:72 concerns a specific reward, whereas Al-Qalam 68:40 questions responsibility and surety for a claim.

The object of the guarantee is therefore different in the two contexts.

7. Assessing claims and truth

Qur’anic usage distinguishes advancing a statement, claiming its truth, and establishing or refuting it with evidence.

A person may assert something, but the assertion alone does not establish its truth.

In Al-Anʿām 6:136 and Al-Anʿām 6:138, people attribute rules and allocations to The God, and the Qur’an challenges those attributions.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:60 sets a profession of faith against actual conduct.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:6 confronts those claiming exclusive favor with a demand that tests their claim.

Al-Qalam 68:40 asks who guarantees the assertion.

These cases share a principle: a claim does not replace proof, even when confidently stated or advanced in The God’s name.

That does not mean that every unproven assertion is a deliberate lie.

A speaker may be mistaken through ignorance, imitation, misinterpretation, or unwarranted confidence.

Evidence can establish that a claim is false; proving conscious concealment of truth requires further evidence about the person’s knowledge and conduct.

Accordingly, assessing a claim’s content must remain distinct from judging the speaker’s intention. The discussed verses challenge claims through questions, conduct, or explicit answers; the root alone does not disclose every speaker’s inner state.

The زعيم branch also emphasizes responsibility. A guarantor goes beyond stating a claim and presents themselves as answerable for its fulfillment.

The two branches thus concern the credibility of a claim and responsibility for an undertaking.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

The following equivalents suit the various forms. They are study suggestions and do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

زَعَمَ: claimed, said, or supposed, according to context.

يَزْعُمُ: claims or supposes.

زَعَمُوا: they claimed or supposed.

زَعْم: a claim, supposition, or statement whose truth is under examination.

بِزَعْمِهِمْ: according to their own claim.

زَاعِم: a claimant or someone advancing a statement.

زَعِمَ: in the desire sense, hoped or desired.

مَزْعَم: a claim; in the desire sense, a ground for hope or expectation.

مَزَاعِم: claims or doubtful assertions.

زَعِيم: a guarantor or one responsible for an undertaking; socially, a chief or supervisor.

زَعَامَة: guarantee, undertaking, or supervision.

تَزَعَّمَ: made a claim, sometimes a false assertion; in the leadership sense, assumed charge.

For Qur’anic occurrences, the following choices are suitable:

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:60, “claim” suits يَزْعُمُونَ because a profession of faith is tested against behavior.

In Al-Anʿām 6:136 and Al-Anʿām 6:138, بِزَعْمِهِمْ is aptly rendered “according to their own claim.”

In passages about alleged partners of The God, زعمتم and تزعمون may be rendered “you claimed” or “you supposed.” Where authority and status are attributed to deities, “claimed” makes the assessable attribution especially clear.

In Al-Taghābun 64:7, “claimed” suits زَعَمَ because the Qur’an refutes the assertion.

In Yūsuf 12:72, زَعِيمٌ means “guarantor.”

In Al-Qalam 68:40, it likewise means a guarantor or someone taking responsibility for the claim.

A suitable brief root-box definition is:

“Making a claim or advancing a statement; in another branch, guaranteeing or undertaking responsibility.”

Each verse’s particular meaning must be stated separately: claiming and guaranteeing are distinct senses.

9. Conclusion

The root Z ʿ M has two important Arabic branches: claiming and supposing, and guaranteeing and undertaking.

Transmitted pre-Islamic poetry shows its use for advancing claims and, in some constructions, expressing desire and hope.

زعيم denotes someone responsible for or guaranteeing something. Its use for a group’s chief or supervisor belongs to the same broad field of responsibility.

Qur’anic forms of زعم mostly appear in contexts that question, test, or reject claims.

Those claims concern faith, resurrection, alleged partners of The God, intercession, exclusive religious standing, and rules attributed to The God without evidence.

Lexically, زعم does not necessarily mean deliberate lying. A claim may be true or false; its truth and the speaker’s knowledge and responsibility require separate assessment.

Both Qur’anic instances of زعيم concern guarantee and responsibility. In Yūsuf 12:72, a speaker guarantees a reward; in Al-Qalam 68:40, claimants are asked who guarantees their assertion.

A single restricted equivalent such as “lying,” “supposition,” or “leadership” cannot cover every derivative.

In the speech branch, زعم introduces a claim whose credibility is assessed; in the undertaking branch, زعيم denotes someone accepting responsibility and guarantee.

The Qur’an uses the first branch to challenge unfounded claims and the second to express responsibility and guarantee. Preserving that distinction is essential to understanding the root in its verses.
