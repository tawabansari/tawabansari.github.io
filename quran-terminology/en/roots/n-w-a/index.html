Rising under a heavy load, a load weighing on its bearer, and nawʾ in traditional star lore

In Arabic, N W ʾ concerns rising or moving with heaviness, pressure, or difficulty. Someone lifting a heavy load and struggling to rise under it can be described by نَاءَ يَنُوءُ (nāʾa yanūʾu). In another construction, the load itself weighs upon the bearer, bends them, or makes movement difficult.

Early Arabic also uses نَوْء (nawʾ) in knowledge of stars and the year’s seasons, for the rising and setting of particular stars and associated periods. Rain, wind, and changes in weather were linked with these periods in the traditional anwāʾ system.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as لَتَنُوءُ (la-tanūʾu) in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76. It describes the heaviness of the mafātiḥ associated with Qārūn’s treasures.

1. Word family and semantic range

The basic verb is نَاءَ يَنُوءُ (nāʾa yanūʾu), a simple triliteral with final hamzah and medial wāw. The alif in the past form nāʾa results from a change to wāw, while wāw appears in yanūʾu. The surface form should therefore not lead to assigning another root.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • نَاءَ يَنُوءُ (nāʾa yanūʾu): to rise with difficulty, move under a heavy load, or, in another construction, for something’s weight to press upon someone.
  • نَوْء (nawʾ) and تَنْوَاء (tanwāʾ): recorded verbal nouns; nawʾ also names a phenomenon in traditional star lore.
  • أَنَاءَ (anāʾa): as in أَنَاءَهُ الْحِمْلُ (anāʾahu al-ḥimlu), the load weighed upon or bent him under its pressure.
  • نَاءٍ (nāʾin): the active participle of nāʾa after morphological changes; a person or thing involved in rising or moving with difficulty, as context determines.
  • أَنْوَاء (anwāʾ) and نُوآن (nūʾān): recorded plurals of nawʾ, especially in relation to stellar stations and traditional weather periods.
  • اسْتَنَاءَ (istanāʾa) and related forms: recorded in uses concerning stellar phenomena, awaiting rain, and associated matters.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. The Qur’anic root is confined to one imperfect verb. Other Arabic uses, particularly the difference between lifting a load and a load weighing upon its bearer, nevertheless help explain that verb.

2. Heaviness, rising, and the pressure of a load

Early Arabic records this definition for the use:

نَهَضَ بِجَهْدٍ وَمَشَقَّةٍ

nahaḍa bi-jahdin wa-mashaqqatin

That is, “He rose with effort and difficulty.”

The concrete image is someone who struggles to lift a heavy load or rise beneath it. More than rising is involved: heaviness and difficult movement are significant features.

Lexical accounts place two closely related constructions alongside one another:

نَاءَ بِالْحِمْلِ (nāʾa bi-al-ḥimli): he rose with a load, struggling under its weight.

نَاءَ بِهِ الْحِمْلُ (nāʾa bihi al-ḥimlu): the load weighed upon him and subjected him to pressure.

The bearer is the subject in the first construction; the load is the subject in the second. This matters because the semantic relationship remains, while the sentence presents it from different directions.

Another Arabic example shows the same difference. المرأة تنوء بعجيزتها (al-marʾatu tanūʾu bi-ʿajīzatihā) describes a woman moving with the weight of her hindquarters. تنوء بها عجيزتها (tanūʾu bihā ʿajīzatuhā) presents that weight as bearing upon her.

Some records even use nāʾa for giving way or falling beneath a load. Lexical accounts have therefore sometimes included it among words with opposing senses.

Rising with difficulty and giving way under pressure share an experiential field: a heavy load straining its bearer’s strength. This connection is intelligible, but it does not mean both outcomes, rising and falling, occur simultaneously in every use.

3. Early Arabic and pre-Islamic poetry

A witness from ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s Muʿallaqah

An important pre-Islamic witness occurs in the Muʿallaqah of ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm al-Taghlibī:

وَمَتْنَيْ لَدْنَةٍ طَالَتْ وَلَانَتْ
رَوَادِفُهَا تَنُوءُ بِمَا يَلِينَا

wa-matnay ladnatin ṭālat wa-lānat / rawādifuhā tanūʾu bi-mā yalīnā

A rendering close to the sense is: “And the two sides of the back of a supple, tall woman, whose hindquarters weigh upon the adjoining parts.”

Tanūʾu here conveys heaviness and difficult movement. The bodily description uses the same relationship found in load-and-bearer constructions.

The subject of tanūʾu is the heavy body part itself, rather than necessarily the person bearing the weight. The construction seen in the Qārūn passage therefore has a precedent in early Arabic.

Versions vary, including سَمَقَتْ وَطَالَتْ (samaqat wa-ṭālat) in place of طَالَتْ وَلَانَتْ (ṭālat wa-lānat), and بِمَا وَلِينَا (bi-mā walīnā) in place of بِمَا يَلِينَا (bi-mā yalīnā). These variants do not fundamentally alter tanūʾu or its connection with the heaviness described.

A witness concerning a heavy blow

Early lexical witnesses transmit the following verse:

إِلَّا عَصَا أَرْزَنٍ طَارَتْ بُرَايَتُهَا
تَنُوءُ ضَرْبَتُهَا بِالْكَفِّ وَالْعَضُدِ

illā ʿaṣā arzanin ṭārat burāyatuhā / tanūʾu ḍarbatuhā bi-al-kaffi wa-al-ʿaḍudi

“Except a staff of arzan wood, its shavings scattered, whose blow bears heavily upon hand and upper arm.”

The subject of tanūʾu here is the blow, while the effect of its weight falls on the hand and arm.

The example helps explain nāʾa bihi al-ḥimlu. A load or applied force can be the sentence’s subject, with the person or body part bearing the pressure following bi-.

The attribution and date are less clear in the examined transmissions than for ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s famous verse. It should therefore be treated as an early lexical witness, without confidently dating it to the pre-Islamic period.

Rising and leaning the body

An early account transmits this verse from an Arabic speaker whose name is not specified:

حَتَّى إِذَا مَا الْتَأَمَتْ مَوَاصِلُهْ
وَنَاءَ فِي شِقِّ الشِّمَالِ كَاهِلُهْ

ḥattā idhā mā iltaʾamat mawāṣiluh / wa-nāʾa fī shiqqi al-shimāli kāhiluh

“Until his limbs were aligned and his upper back leaned toward the left.”

The image concerns an archer whose shoulder and upper back lean to one side while drawing a bow.

Here nāʾa concerns movement under pressure and the body leaning. Neither treasure nor a portable load is the subject; the pressure of a physical action is described.

The witness shows that the verb extends beyond lifting loads to difficult movement or leaning under force.

However, the poet’s name and date are not identified in the account, and the composition date should not be guessed.

4. Nawʾ and anwāʾ in star lore and rainfall

Another important branch uses nawʾ in knowledge of stars, seasons, and changes in weather.

In the traditional Arabic system, the apparent movement of certain stars relative to the horizon, and their rising and setting times, helped identify seasons and periods of the year. Nawʾ was an established term, with the plural anwāʾ.

One lexical explanation defines nawʾ as a star of the stellar stations setting in the west at dawn while its opposite rises in the east.

However, lexical accounts did not all agree about which side of this phenomenon the name designated precisely.

One explanation connects nawʾ with the rising star opposite the setting star. Another applies the same name to the star setting in the west.

The disagreement cannot be resolved simply by defining nawʾ as a star’s rising. Historically, the setting of one star and rising of its opposite were linked parts of a system for identifying time.

Some lexicons also classify nawʾ among words with opposing meanings because it is associated both with rising and with sinking or falling. Caution is needed about the name’s origin and which sense came first.

Nawʾ and rain

In Arabic language and culture, anwāʾ also became connected with rainfall.

Periods associated with stellar stations were linked to rain, wind, cold, and heat. مُطِرْنَا بِنَوْءِ كَذَا (muṭirnā bi-nawʾi kadhā) belongs to this setting: rainfall was expressed in connection with a particular star’s nawʾ.

Two matters should be distinguished: using stellar positions to identify a rainy period, and believing that a star independently causes rain.

The claims are different. The lexical meaning of nawʾ alone establishes neither theory.

A verse attributed to Ḥassān ibn Thābit reads:

وَيَثْرِبُ تَعْلَمُ أَنَّا بِهَا
إِذَا قَحَطَ الْغَيْثُ نُوآنُهَا

wa-yathribu taʿlamu annā bihā / idhā qaḥaṭa al-ghaythu nūʾānuhā

“Yathrib knows that, when rain grows scarce, we are its rain-bringing stars.” Nūʾān here is a plural of nawʾ, rather than its dual. The poet compares himself and his people to bringers of rain in drought.

The witness draws upon the association between nawʾ and expected rain to depict assistance and generosity in scarcity. However, Ḥassān lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Attribution alone does not establish that this verse predates Islam.

The stellar sense and the root’s core

A connection can be seen between a star rising on the horizon and nāʾa meaning rising, as lexical explanations themselves propose.

That does not establish that a rising star actually bears a heavy load, or that the astronomical name necessarily originated in the image of a person carrying a burden.

Such imagery would impose more on the root than the evidence establishes.

More securely, nawʾ has two established Arabic fields: rising or moving under pressure, and the traditional identification of time through stars. Their proposed lexical relationship can be examined without treating it as a proven history of the term’s origin.

5. Qur’anic use in the account of Qārūn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76

إِنَّ قَارُونَ كَانَ مِنْ قَوْمِ مُوسَىٰ فَبَغَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ ۖ وَآتَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْكُنُوزِ مَا إِنَّ مَفَاتِحَهُ لَتَنُوءُ بِالْعُصْبَةِ أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ إِذْ قَالَ لَهُ قَوْمُهُ لَا تَفْرَحْ ۖ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْفَرِحِينَ

inna qārūna kāna min qawmi mūsā fa-baghā ʿalayhim wa-ātaynāhu mina al-kunūzi mā inna mafātiḥahu la-tanūʾu bi-al-ʿuṣbati ulī al-quwwati idh qāla lahu qawmuhu lā tafraḥ inna allāha lā yuḥibbu al-fariḥīna

“Qarun was from the people of Moses, but he oppressed them. We had given him treasures whose keys weighed heavily upon a band of strong men. When his people said to him: Do not exult; The God does not love the exultant.”

The rendering of la-tanūʾu here conveys the same sense recorded in Forqan’s current translation.

The morphology and syntax of لَتَنُوءُ (la-tanūʾu)

La-tanūʾu comprises emphatic la-, called the displaced lām in this construction, and tanūʾu, a simple triliteral imperfect verb, third-person feminine singular, in the indicative.

مَفَاتِحَهُ (mafātiḥahu) is the accusative noun governed by إِنَّ (inna). The subject of tanūʾu is an implicit هِيَ (hiya), “it,” referring to the mafātiḥ; the verbal sentence forms inna’s predicate. Although mafātiḥ is plural in meaning, an Arabic nonhuman plural can take a feminine singular verb.

Bi- in بِالْعُصْبَةِ (bi-al-ʿuṣbah) also helps identify the relationship between load and bearers. The sentence describes the mafātiḥ weighing upon a strong company.

Semantic attribution must be distinguished from grammatical position. Heaviness is attributed to the mafātiḥ, but mafātiḥahu itself is the noun governed by inna, with the verb’s implicit subject referring back to it.

A significant lexical disagreement: who bears the weight?

لَتَنُوءُ بِالْعُصْبَةِ (la-tanūʾu bi-al-ʿuṣbah) has long been discussed in lexical explanations.

First reading: the mafātiḥ weigh upon the bearers

Heaviness is attributed to the mafātiḥ themselves: they are so heavy that they weigh upon a strong company. In the verse’s syntax, tanūʾu’s implicit subject refers back to mafātiḥahu, the noun governed by inna.

This fits nāʾa bihi al-ḥimlu, the construction in which the load is the subject and the bearer receives the pressure.

There is no need to reverse the verse’s elements. Its existing construction gives an intelligible sense: the mafātiḥ weigh upon the strong company.

Second reading: the strong company rise under the mafātiḥ

Some early lexical explanations treat the expression as reversed attribution, or qalb.

The resulting sense is that the company carry the mafātiḥ with difficulty because of their weight, as though the sentence read:

إِنَّ الْعُصْبَةَ لَتَنُوءُ بِمَفَاتِحِهِ

inna al-ʿuṣbata la-tanūʾu bi-mafātiḥihi

“The strong company struggle under the weight of his mafātiḥ.”

The reading returns to the same image of substantial heaviness but explains the construction by proposing a reversal between load and bearers.

Early explanations transmit both analyses. One accounts for the mafātiḥ weighing upon the company through the verb’s own usage and the preposition bi-.

Assessing the two readings

The lexical evidence, especially nāʾa bihi al-ḥimlu and poetic uses attributing heaviness to the source of pressure, favors the first reading.

It requires no reversal of load and bearer. The verse’s existing structure fits an established Arabic usage.

Nevertheless, the second analysis belongs in the historical record: it is an actual early explanation, rather than a modern invention.

Both readings concern a heavy weight’s effect upon strong people. The main difference is the analysis of the construction, rather than the underlying weight and difficulty of carrying it.

Mafātiḥ: keys or storehouses?

مَفَاتِح (mafātiḥ) belongs to another root, F T Ḥ.

Two significant lexical and interpretive readings are recorded in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76: keys to the treasures, or the storehouses and places where wealth was kept.

Some accounts give details about the number or material of the keys and the animals carrying them. The Qur’an does not give these numbers or specifications.

This disagreement should not be merged with tanūʾu’s meaning. Whether keys or storehouses are intended, the verb describes pressure and heaviness.

The full range of mafātiḥ belongs in an F T Ḥ study. For the present root, the point is that something associated with Qārūn’s treasures is described as heavy.

Study of F T Ḥ

ʿUṣbah and the role of strength

The verse says:

بِالْعُصْبَةِ أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ

bi-al-ʿuṣbati ulī al-quwwati

“Upon a company possessing strength.”

عُصْبَة (ʿuṣbah) comes from ʿ Ṣ B. أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ (ulī al-quwwah) emphasizes the members’ strength.

The phrase itself gives no particular number of men, because the verse contains no number.

Mentioning their strength highlights how heavy the mafātiḥ are even for strong people. That feature comes from the complete expression, rather than the root N W ʾ alone.

The emphatic la- at the beginning of the verb also highlights the description.

The sentence within Qārūn’s story

The verse first says that Qārūn belonged to Moses’ people and oppressed them. It then describes his abundant wealth before reporting his people’s words.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:77, he is urged to seek the home of the Hereafter through what The God has given him, remember his share of this world, do good, and not seek corruption on earth.

Qārūn replies:

إِنَّمَا أُوتِيتُهُ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ عِنْدِي

innamā ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilmin ʿindī

“I was given it only on the basis of knowledge I possess.” (Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:78)

La-tanūʾu bi-al-ʿuṣbah thus describes the abundance and weight of his possessions. Tanūʾu itself does not establish oppression, arrogance, corruption, or ingratitude.

Those ethical features come from other parts of the account. The verb retains its own lexical meaning of heaviness and pressure.

Any reflection on wealth, economic power, responsibility, and pride must therefore rest on the passages together, rather than assigning every idea to N W ʾ itself.

6. Distinctions from nearby words

N W ʾ (nawʾ) and N ʾ Y (naʾy)

Despite their visible resemblance, these are distinct roots.

Qur’anic N W ʾ concerns heaviness and pressure, whereas N ʾ Y concerns distance and keeping away.

For example, Al-Isrāʾ 17:83 says:

أَعْرَضَ وَنَأَىٰ بِجَانِبِهِ

aʿraḍa wa-naʾā bi-jānibihi

“He turned away and withdrew to one side.”

The same expression occurs in Fuṣṣilat 41:51. In Al-Anʿām 6:26, يَنْأَوْنَ (yanʾawna) likewise denotes keeping away.

The surface similarity of نَأَىٰ (naʾā) and تَنُوءُ (tanūʾu) does not make them forms of one root.

The distinction is especially useful in unvocalized writing, where forms of naʾā and nāʾa can appear similar to a reader.

Study of N ʾ Y

N W ʾ (nawʾ) and N W Y (nawā)

N W Y is also independent.

The Qur’an uses النَّوَىٰ (al-nawā) in Al-Anʿām 6:95:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ

inna allāha fāliqu al-ḥabbi wa-al-nawā

“The God is indeed the splitter of grain and fruit stones.”

Al-nawā here denotes fruit stones and is not morphologically part of N W ʾ. Arabic senses of intention and purpose also belong to N W Y.

Intention, purpose, and fruit stones should therefore not be imported into N W ʾ.

N W ʾ (nawʾ) and TH Q L (thiqal)

This comparison is more directly relevant semantically.

TH Q L concerns heaviness and weight in Arabic and the Qur’an, both for physical loads and other kinds of heaviness.

Nāʾa, particularly in the use discussed here, describes heaviness in relation to rising, moving, leaning, or bearing pressure.

تثقل (tathqulu), “weighs heavily,” is therefore helpful in explaining tanūʾu, without making the two roots complete synonyms.

“Weigh heavily” fits the verb’s construction and context in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76. It does not cover the whole Arabic range of N W ʾ.

7. Proposed translation for Forqan

For la-tanūʾu in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76, “indeed weighs heavily,” or “indeed weighed heavily” to fit the surrounding tense and construction, is the best equivalent in the existing sentence.

Forqan’s current translation reads:

“…whose keys weighed heavily upon a band of strong men.”

The rendering is natural, clear, and lexically defensible. It preserves the attribution of weight to the keys and does not require rewriting the verse by reversing load and bearer.

A lexical note can explain that the load presses upon its bearers, making rising or moving difficult.

However, adding “made the strong company helpless,” “broke their backs,” or “brought them down” exceeds the direct wording. Such expressions may explain the image’s intensity, but tanūʾu alone does not require them.

La-tanūʾu here does not mean a star, rainfall, a setting star, or the arrival of a particular season. That documented lexical branch is absent from the Qārūn context.

8. Conclusion

Early Arabic N W ʾ concerns rising or moving with difficulty and a load weighing upon its bearer. Some uses describe a bearer struggling to rise; others describe the load as the source of weight and pressure.

In another branch, nawʾ and its plural anwāʾ belong to traditional knowledge of stars and seasons. Different lexical accounts connect it with stars rising or setting, and the term became associated with rainy periods and other weather changes.

Only la-tanūʾu occurs in the Qur’an, in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76. Its implicit subject refers to mafātiḥahu, the accusative noun governed by inna. The verb describes the mafātiḥ associated with Qārūn’s treasures weighing upon a strong company.

There are two early analyses: one reads the load weighing upon its bearers directly, while the other proposes reversed attribution. Documented Arabic usage favors the direct reading.

A concise practical definition is therefore:

N W ʾ: rising or moving with difficulty under a heavy load; also a load weighing upon its bearer and subjecting them to pressure. In Arabic star lore, nawʾ also names a phenomenon and period associated with stars rising and setting. La-tanūʾu in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76 means “weighs heavily.”
