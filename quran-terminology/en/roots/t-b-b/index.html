Short Introduction to the Root

The root moves in the field of loss, failure, ruin, and the loss of a desired outcome. It is used of a person, act, plan, or support that does not reach benefit, rescue, or success, and whose result ends in loss and ruin.

In its Qur’anic uses, this root is not limited to bodily destruction or the ending of life. A person’s two hands, the person himself, the plot of a ruler, and objects of worship taken as supports can all stand within the field of . The root therefore extends beyond death or physical destruction and includes the failure of action, plan, power, and support.

Important Forms and Derivatives

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an in three morphological forms.

تَبَّ (tabba)

is a form I past verb. In Arabic, it can indicate suffering loss, failing, perishing, or reaching a ruined outcome.

In the Qur’an, the verb occurs twice in one ayah:

تَبَّتْ يَدَا أَبِي لَهَبٍ وَتَبَّ

“The two hands of Abu Lahab suffered loss, and he himself also suffered loss.”

Al-Masad 111:1

The form is the same verb marked as feminine because of its subject, , “two hands.” The second form, , returns to Abu Lahab himself.

تَبَاب (tabab)

is a noun or nominal verbal form indicating loss, ruin, and failure:

وَمَا كَيْدُ فِرْعَوْنَ إِلَّا فِي تَبَابٍ

“And Pharaoh’s plot was only in loss and ruin.”

Ghafir 40:37

تَتْبِيب (tatbib)

is the form II verbal noun of . In this structure, a causative or intensive meaning becomes prominent: drawing into ruin, adding to loss, or intensifying loss.

وَمَا زَادُوهُمْ غَيْرَ تَتْبِيبٍ

“And they increased them in nothing except ruin.”

Hud 11:101

The form is also known in Arabic. This accusative verbal noun is used in expressions such as to announce loss or destruction, or to call for failure. This independent form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Forms such as or may also appear in lexical sources or limited Arabic usage, but they are not central to studying the Qur’anic use of this root and should not be confused with the forms found in the Qur’an.

Semantic Core of the Root

The core of should be sought in loss and failure. Something that reaches is kept from a beneficial outcome, and its result ends in loss.

This meaning may apply to the person himself, as with Abu Lahab; to his instruments and actions, as when his two hands become the subject of the verb; to a political scheme and the plot of power, as when Pharaoh’s plot is described as being in tabab; or to supports that, instead of rescuing their followers, increase their loss.

The root therefore does not necessarily mean that the subject has immediately and outwardly been destroyed. A person may still possess wealth, power, or position; a plan may still be operating; and a system of worship may still have followers. Yet the Qur’an can place it in , because its direction and outcome do not reach true success.

The expression “the collapse of expected benefit” can help explain the connection among some Qur’anic uses, but the root’s lexical meaning should not be restricted to economic language, profit, or capital. Its field is loss and ruin, and according to context this loss may appear in action, result, life, power, or support.

Material Image of the Root

No single and certain material image is clearly available for that can be treated as the origin of all its uses. Images such as turning to ash, cutting off a hand, or the collapse of a building should therefore not be presented as the root’s certain point of origin.

Its linguistic and Qur’anic uses nevertheless provide two tangible images that help explain its field.

The first is . In early Arabic usage, an expression about the hands of a trader suffering loss could be said of a transaction that produced no benefit. In this image, the hands have acted, taken, or given something, but the result of the transaction is loss.

The second is . The hand is an instrument of taking, building, defending, acquiring, and carrying out. When the Qur’an says:

تَبَّتْ يَدَا أَبِي لَهَبٍ

the subject is not merely two bodily organs. The two hands can represent the whole field of a person’s action, effort, executive power, and achievement. The following ayah makes this image clearer:

مَا أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُ مَالُهُ وَمَا كَسَبَ

“His wealth and what he had acquired did not ward anything off from him or make him free of need.”

Al-Masad 111:2

Hand, gain, and wealth stand in one field: the instrument of action, the result of action, and the support in which the person places trust. Tabab becomes evident when this whole set cannot save its owner from a harmful outcome.

Use of the Root in Early Arabic

Arabic lexical sources explain with loss and with loss and destruction. The expression was also used to announce or call for a person’s loss and destruction.

The expression was known in Arabic for the two hands suffering loss or for the failure of the person who acts. In this structure, the hands can stand for a person’s work, transaction, or effort. This usage indicates that, for an Arabic hearer, the Qur’anic wording in Surat Al-Masad was not merely an image of bodily amputation.

Some sources also report “to cut” as a meaning of . This lexical branch should be recorded, but it should not be imposed mechanically on the ayah in Surat Al-Masad. The structure of the ayah, the known use of , and the continuation of the surah concerning wealth, gain, and the person’s outcome all strengthen the field of loss and failure.

Within the same lexical family, the form is also reported for a road becoming visible and even, or for a matter becoming established. This branch has historically been recorded under the same lexical material, but its exact relation to the field of loss and destruction is not fully clear. It should therefore not be brought into the Qur’anic core of the root without an independent examination.

History of Understanding, Misuse, or Semantic Distortion

One well-known translation of is “perish the two hands of Abu Lahab,” or a similar wording suggesting that the hands be cut off. Such a translation may convey the curse-like force at the beginning of the surah, but it creates two problems.

First, “being cut off” may give a present-day reader an image of bodily amputation, while the prominent meaning in this context is loss and failure.

Second, the past form is rendered only as a prayer or curse, and its declarative and report-like dimension becomes less visible. The expression can carry the resonance of a curse, judgment, or declaration of outcome, but morphologically it is a past verb: his two hands suffered loss or failed.

At the other extreme, translating the phrase entirely interpretively as “Abu Lahab’s deeds were destroyed” also removes part of the ayah’s image. “Deeds” is one possible implication of the hands, but the image of should remain in the translation as far as possible.

Methodological Note

Three levels must be distinguished when examining this root.

The first is : loss, destruction, decrease, and ruin.

The second is : hands as instruments of action, wealth and gain as supports, plot as a scheme of power, and objects of worship as claimed refuges.

The third is : the thought that any instrument, power, or plan can stand within tabab if it does not reach the right aim.

The third level may be inspired by the ayat, but it should not be presented as the root’s dictionary meaning. does not directly mean “the failure of technology,” “the collapse of a political project,” or “capital becoming ineffective.” These are contextual and reflective expressions of loss and failure in particular settings.

Likewise, the reported meaning “cutting” should not be turned into a general theory that the root’s core is “the cutting off of good.” That expression may serve as an interpretive explanation, but the clearer lexical evidence places loss and destruction at the center.

Qur’anic Uses of the Root

The Failure of the Hands and the Instruments of Action

تَبَّتْ يَدَا أَبِي لَهَبٍ وَتَبَّ

“The two hands of Abu Lahab suffered loss, and he himself also suffered loss.”

Al-Masad 111:1

The ayah first makes the subject of loss. Hands are the human being’s nearest instruments for action, taking possession, building, gathering, and defending. Attributing ruin to the hands can include a person’s action and effort, but the image of the hands should not be completely removed from translation.

The ayah then returns the loss to Abu Lahab himself through . This structure shows the link between the person and his actions: his instruments and efforts are not separate from him, and the outcome of action returns to the one who acts.

Yet the ayah does not necessarily create a two-stage timeline in which the hands are first destroyed and the person only afterward. The two parts can be two sides of one judgment: his action is loss-stricken, and he himself stands in the same loss.

Wealth and Gain That Do Not Provide Ighna

مَا أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُ مَالُهُ وَمَا كَسَبَ

“His wealth and what he had acquired did not ward anything off from him.”

Al-Masad 111:2

The root is not repeated in this ayah, but the second ayah explains the field of loss in the first. The issue is not that Abu Lahab possessed no wealth or gain. The ayah assumes the presence of wealth and acquisition but denies their power of and sufficiency.

Tabab, then, does not arise only from lacking instruments. A person may possess instruments, wealth, and achievements, yet they may have no rescuing function against the outcome of his action.

This should not be taken to mean that wealth or gain is condemned in itself. The context concerns their inability to ward off the result of a particular direction and action.

Pharaoh’s Plot Within Tabab

وَمَا كَيْدُ فِرْعَوْنَ إِلَّا فِي تَبَابٍ

“And Pharaoh’s plot was only in loss and ruin.”

Ghafir 40:37

In this ayah, is placed within . The preposition is important: the ayah does not merely say that his plan failed at the end, but describes it as being in the space of loss and ruin.

This plot appears in the context of Pharaoh’s command to Haman to build a lofty structure:

يَا هَامَانُ ابْنِ لِي صَرْحًا لَعَلِّي أَبْلُغُ الْأَسْبَابَ

“O Haman, build for me a lofty structure, so perhaps I may reach the ways and means.”

Ghafir 40:36

The point of the ayah is not opposition to constructing a building or using instruments. What is described as being in tabab is : a scheme placed in the service of self-exaltation, denial, and the consolidation of his claim.

Outwardly, such a plan may possess human power, instruments, resources, and a display of authority. Yet its outward greatness does not remove it from tabab. The ayah judges the direction and outcome of the plot, not the material size of the project.

Supports That Bring No Benefit

فَمَا أَغْنَتْ عَنْهُمْ آلِهَتُهُمُ الَّتِي يَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ

“The objects of worship they called upon apart from The God did not ward anything off from them.”

Hud 11:101

Here, as in Surat Al-Masad, the structure of is present. Those people expected support, sufficiency, or rescue from their objects of worship, but when the outcome arrived those supports brought them no benefit.

From Inability to Rescue to Increased Ruin

وَمَا زَادُوهُمْ غَيْرَ تَتْبِيبٍ

“And they increased them in nothing except ruin.”

Hud 11:101

The ayah does not stop at the inability of those objects of worship to rescue. They added nothing to their followers except .

Form II can carry a causative or intensive meaning here. Reliance on these objects of worship was not merely useless; dependence upon them increased loss. This increase can appear in continuing the wrong path, trusting a powerless refuge, and moving farther from the true point of rescue.

Yet definite psychological or social details should not be constructed beyond the ayah. What the text clearly says is that these objects of worship not only failed to provide ighna but also increased tatbib.

Important Qur’anic Derivatives

تَبَّتْ and تَبَّ (tabbat and tabba)

The two verbal forms in Surat Al-Masad belong to the same root and form, but their subjects differ.

In , the subject is . The verb is feminine singular because, when a verb precedes a dual subject, it normally appears in the singular, and is feminine.

In , the subject is an implicit pronoun returning to Abu Lahab. Repetition of the root carries loss from the hands to the person himself.

This distinction should be preserved in translation:

Translating it as “may Abu Lahab perish” removes the repetition between the hands and the person. Translating it as “Abu Lahab’s deeds were destroyed” also removes the image of the hands.

تَبَاب (tabab)

in Ghafir 40:37 follows the preposition and appears as an indefinite noun:

فِي تَبَابٍ

This structure can express a condition or environment of loss: Pharaoh’s plot stands in loss, rather than merely having one separate defeat added to it at the end.

Translations such as “in ruin,” “in loss,” “on a path of failure,” or “in a condition moving toward ruin” can convey part of the construction. “Condemned to failure” is more interpretive and adds a stronger certainty to the phrase; “in loss and ruin” is therefore more cautious for direct translation.

تَتْبِيب (tatbib)

is a form II verbal noun. It occurs in the following structure:

وَمَا زَادُوهُمْ غَيْرَ تَتْبِيبٍ

identifies what was added to them: nothing except tatbib.

“They increased them in nothing except ruin” conveys the structure naturally. “Loss-increasing” may be conceptually precise, but in a Qur’an translation it may sound overly technical or artificial.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

A Singular Verb With a Dual Subject

In , the verb precedes a dual subject. In this structure, the verb appears in the singular; therefore does not mean that only one hand is the subject of the ayah.

The feminine marker appears because the singular is feminine in Arabic.

The Dual Form in يَدَا (yada)

is the dual form of in the nominative case. It is the first term of a possessive construction, and is its genitive complement.

Preserving the dual in translation matters because the ayah explicitly speaks of “two hands,” not merely “a hand” or “action.”

Repetition of the Verb

The repetition in is not merely verbal repetition. The subject of the first verb is the two hands, while the subject of the second is the person himself. This change of subject expands the field of loss.

The Restrictive Construction

In the expression:

وَمَا كَيْدُ فِرْعَوْنَ إِلَّا فِي تَبَابٍ

the construction creates restriction. The ayah does not place Pharaoh’s plot outside tabab; its judgment concerning this plot is confined to loss and ruin.

The Role of فِي (fi)

The preposition places the plot inside tabab. For this reason, “Pharaoh’s plan failed” does not convey the full capacity of the structure. The Qur’anic expression shows a condition and direction broader than a single event at the end.

Form II

In , form II can indicate causing or intensifying. In the context of the ayah, the objects of worship increased the ruin of their followers; the sense of intensification and increased loss is therefore prominent.

Comparison With Nearby Roots

T B B compared with KH S R: tabab and khasr

The root has a broad field of loss, decrease of capital, losing, and the loss of benefit or value. Khasr can occur in trade, lifetime, action, or a person’s outcome.

closely overlaps with khasr, and lexical sources sometimes explain tabab directly through khasr. The difference between the two should not be enlarged beyond the linguistic evidence.

In the limited Qur’anic uses of , however, there is a particular emphasis on the failure of a person, hands, plot, and supports that do not reach a beneficial result. This pattern may be described as a feature of Qur’anic usage, not necessarily as an absolute boundary between the two roots throughout Arabic.

T B B compared with H L K: tabab and halak

The root indicates passing away, destruction, and coming to an end, and can be used of a person, people, wealth, or power.

Lexical sources also explain through destruction, but its Qur’anic use is not confined to bodily destruction. A plot, hands, and claimed supports can also enter its field.

“Destruction” is therefore one nearby meaning, but it is not the best translation in every context.

T B B compared with H B T: tabab and habat

In the Qur’an, is often used of an action whose effect, value, or fruit is lost. Its focus is the loss of the effect of an action.

T B B compared with B T L: tabab and butlan

The written and phonetic similarity between and should not cause them to be treated as one root.

T B B compared with T ʿ S: tabab and taʿs

The two can draw close in result, but their linguistic images are not the same.

T B B compared with D L L: tabab and dalal

One may say by way of explanation that dalal concerns wrong direction, while tabab concerns the loss and failure of that direction. This helps clarify their relation, but it should not be turned into an absolute dictionary boundary.

T B B compared with F SH L: tabab and fashal

Tabab does not necessarily arise from weakness. Pharaoh’s plot may have extensive executive power yet still stand in tabab.

Fashal therefore concerns weakness in capacity or will, while tabab concerns loss and a failed outcome.

T B B compared with R SH D: tabab and rashad

In the context of Surat Ghafir, the believing man from Pharaoh’s people speaks of , while Pharaoh’s plot stands in .

Rashad and tabab are not complete and direct lexical opposites, but in the structure of this dialogue they show two different directions: a path leading to right direction and a plan standing in loss.

After the name or title , Surat Al-Masad says:

“He will soon enter a blazing fire.”

Al-Masad 111:3

There is clear verbal harmony between and . The surah places the person’s known name alongside an image of blazing fire.

This harmony, however, should not be mixed with the root . belongs to the root and concerns flame and the blazing tongue of fire. belongs to and stands in the field of loss and ruin.

It therefore cannot be said that the core of is burning, turning to ash, or the extinguishing of fire. The surah builds a verbal connection between Abu Lahab’s name and his fiery outcome, but this connection does not change the etymology of .

Summary

The root moves in the Qur’an within the field of loss, failure, ruin, and a harmful outcome. It occurs four times: twice as the verb , once as , and once as .

In Surat Al-Masad, loss is first attributed to Abu Lahab’s two hands and then to the person himself. The hands can represent a human being’s instruments of action, gain, and executive power, but their explicit image should not be removed from translation. The following ayah shows that his wealth and gain had no power of ighna or rescue.

In Surat Ghafir, Pharaoh’s plot stands within tabab. This expression shows that outward power, extensive instruments, and the execution of a plan do not necessarily remove it from loss. Yet the ayah does not judge technology or construction itself negatively; its subject is Pharaoh’s plot and its direction.

In Surat Hud, the claimed objects of worship not only fail to rescue their followers but add nothing except tatbib. Form II here highlights intensification or the causing of loss.
