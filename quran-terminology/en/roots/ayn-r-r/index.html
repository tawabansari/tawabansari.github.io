Afflicting harm and contamination; approaching another person to seek help or a share of their provision

1. Introduction and meanings in early Arabic

The triliteral root ع ر ر is a doubled Arabic root: its second and third consonants are identical. Its recorded vocabulary has several semantic branches. The most relevant to the Quran are contamination or harm that afflicts something, and approaching another person to seek help or benefit.

In early Arabic, عَرّ and عُرّ were used for skin diseases, mange, and sores particularly associated with camels. These conditions damaged the animal's skin and could leave it unclean. عُرَّة is also recorded for filth and impurity.

The same semantic field includes expressions for a person being affected by evil, disgrace, or an adverse consequence. These uses extend beyond physical injury to disrepute, hardship, and other unwelcome effects that reach a person.

Another branch concerns coming to someone and approaching them. Arabic records عَرَّهُ and اعْتَرَّهُ for visiting a person to seek kindness or benefit. A مُعْتَرّ is someone who comes to another hoping for a gift or assistance.

These meanings should not be reduced to an artificial single definition. Contamination and harm are clear in one branch, and coming or approaching in another. Possible connections can be imagined, but they do not establish a single historical origin for every use.

2. Early Arabic evidence and the dating of the examples

An important witness to the early meaning of عُرّ is a verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nabighah al-Dhubyani:

لَكَلَّفْتَنِي ذَنْبَ امْرِئٍ وَتَرَكْتَهُ
كَذِي الْعُرِّ يُكْوَى غَيْرُهُ وَهُوَ رَاتِعُ

la-kallaftanī dhanba imriʾin wa-taraktahu / ka-dhī al-ʿurri yukwā ghayruhu wa-huwa rātiʿu

“You burdened me with another man's offence and left him alone, like the diseased animal for which another is cauterized while it grazes at ease.”

Here عُرّ denotes an animal disease. The image refers to a reported ancient belief or practice of cauterizing another animal on behalf of the sick one; it does not establish the treatment's effectiveness. The poet uses it to depict injustice: one party has the fault or disease, while another bears its consequences.

This verse attests the use of عُرّ for disease and injury in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. By itself, however, it does not prove that all later social and moral meanings of the root developed from that disease.

Early evidence also records عِرَار for the cry of a male ostrich, including a verse attributed to Labid ibn Rabi'ah:

تَحَمَّلَ أَهْلُهَا إِلَّا عِرَارًا
وَعَزْفًا بَعْدَ أَحْيَاءٍ حِلَالِ

taḥammala ahluhā illā ʿirāran / wa-ʿazfan baʿda aḥyāʾin ḥilāli

“Its people have departed; after the communities that once dwelt there, only the ostrich's cry and mysterious sounds of the wilderness remain.”

Labid lived both before and after the advent of Islam. The composition date of this particular verse has not been independently established in this study. It is useful evidence for the early use of عِرَار, but does not by itself prove that this verse predates the revelation of the Quran.

This sound-related branch also shows why the lexical family ع ر ر should not be restricted to disease and contamination.

Arabic dictionaries clearly record عَرَّ and اعْتَرَّ for coming to someone and seeking kindness. This study has not independently established datable pre-Quranic evidence for that branch. Its meaning can therefore be explained in Classical Arabic and Quranic usage without claiming that every detail was demonstrably used in the same way before Islam.

3. Quranic forms and their meanings

The Quran uses two forms from ع ر ر:

  • الْمُعْتَرّ: someone who comes to another seeking assistance or a share; Al-Hajj 22:36.
  • مَعَرَّة: harm, hardship, or an adverse consequence that befalls a person; Al-Fath 48:25.

The two forms belong to one lexical family, but do not have identical meanings.

مُعْتَرّ is the active participle of the Form VIII verb اعْتَرَّ. It denotes someone who approaches another or presents themselves in the hope of receiving assistance.

مَعَرَّة is a noun. In its Quranic use it denotes an unwelcome consequence that reaches a person. Its semantic field includes injury, hardship, disrepute, and the adverse effects of an action.

4. الْمُعْتَرّ: someone approaching to receive assistance

In Al-Hajj, after describing the place of camels among the divine rites and the remembrance of The God's name over them, the verse addresses the use of their meat:

وَالْبُدْنَ جَعَلْنَاهَا لَكُمْ مِنْ شَعَائِرِ اللَّهِ لَكُمْ فِيهَا خَيْرٌ ۖ فَاذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهَا صَوَافَّ ۖ فَإِذَا وَجَبَتْ جُنُوبُهَا فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا وَأَطْعِمُوا الْقَانِعَ وَالْمُعْتَرَّ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ سَخَّرْنَاهَا لَكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-l-budna jaʿalnāhā lakum min shaʿāʾiri allāhi lakum fīhā khayrun fa-dhkurū isma allāhi ʿalayhā ṣawāffa fa-idhā wajabat junūbuhā fa-kulū minhā wa-aṭʿimū al-qāniʿa wa-l-muʿtarra kadhālika sakhkharnāhā lakum laʿallakum tashkurūn

Al-Ḥajj 22:36

Al-Hajj 22:36

“We have made the camels part of The God's rites for you; in them is good for you. So mention The God's name over them as they stand in rows. Then, when their sides fall, eat from them and feed the qāniʿ and those who come to you seeking assistance. Thus We have subjected them to you, so that you may give thanks.”

Here الْمُعْتَرّ appears alongside الْقَانِع. Both are among those whom the verse instructs its audience to feed from the animals' meat.

The lexical meaning of مُعْتَرّ is not restricted to an explicit spoken request. Lexical accounts also use it for someone who comes to another hoping to receive something, whether they voice their request or express their need by presenting themselves.

For example, imagine a person in need approaching a food distribution but asking for nothing out of embarrassment or restraint. Such a person can fall within the meaning of مُعْتَرّ, because approaching the person who has provision is central to this use.

Nor should الْقَانِع and الْمُعْتَرّ be made into two absolutely opposed groups, “someone who never asks” and “someone who necessarily asks,” without separate examination. Arabic records different uses and explanations of qāniʿ, retained here as a term in the translation. Their distinction should be examined through each word's lexical family and context, rather than an absolute opposition.

In the same passage, Al-Hajj 22:28 also speaks of feeding الْبَائِسَ الْفَقِيرَ, the distressed poor. This connection shows that the subject is not simply the performance of a rite: sharing provision with people in need also belongs to it.

In this verse, الْمُعْتَرّ is someone who approaches another to receive assistance or a share. The context includes provision for people in need, but neither the word nor the instruction to feed them establishes that every such visitor is poor. The word should not simply be equated with “a poor person” or “a beggar.”

5. مَعَرَّة: an unwelcome consequence of causing harm

The root's second Quranic occurrence is in Al-Fath, concerning believing men and women living among the opposing group whose identity was unknown to the other believers.

هُمُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَصَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَالْهَدْيَ مَعْكُوفًا أَنْ يَبْلُغَ مَحِلَّهُ ۚ وَلَوْلَا رِجَالٌ مُؤْمِنُونَ وَنِسَاءٌ مُؤْمِنَاتٌ لَمْ تَعْلَمُوهُمْ أَنْ تَطَئُوهُمْ فَتُصِيبَكُمْ مِنْهُمْ مَعَرَّةٌ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ ۖ لِيُدْخِلَ اللَّهُ فِي رَحْمَتِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۚ لَوْ تَزَيَّلُوا لَعَذَّبْنَا الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْهُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

humu alladhīna kafarū wa-ṣaddūkum ʿani al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi wa-l-hadya maʿkūfan an yablugha maḥillahu wa-lawlā rijālun muʾminūna wa-nisāʾun muʾminātun lam taʿlamūhum an taṭaʾūhum fa-tuṣībakum minhum maʿarratun bi-ghayri ʿilmin li-yudkhila allāhu fī raḥmatihi man yashāʾu law tazayyalū la-ʿadhdhabnā alladhīna kafarū minhum ʿadhāban alīman

Al-Fatḥ 48:25

Al-Fath 48:25

“They are the ones who disbelieved and barred you from the Sacred Mosque, and held back the sacrificial animals from reaching their place. Had there not been believing men and believing women whom you did not know, whom you might unknowingly trample, so that an adverse consequence would befall you on their account without your knowledge, [matters would have been otherwise]; so that The God might admit into His mercy whomever He wills. Had they been separated, We would have inflicted a painful punishment on those among them who disbelieved.”

Several points matter for understanding the phrase فَتُصِيبَكُمْ مِنْهُمْ مَعَرَّةٌ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ.

First, مَعَرَّة is something that reaches the verse's addressees: تُصِيبَكُمْ means “befalls you” or “strikes you.”

Second, this consequence concerns unknowingly harming believing men and women. لَمْ تَعْلَمُوهُمْ indicates that their identity was unknown to the addressees, while بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ emphasizes the lack of knowledge.

Third, مَعَرَّة here is not merely the name of the injury those men and women would suffer. It denotes an unwelcome consequence that would reach the addressees themselves as a result.

Lexical and exegetical explanations identify several possibilities, including hardship, blame and disrepute, sin, and obligations associated with the harm caused. Compensation and expiation also appear in the interpretive discussion. These are explanations of the possible consequence in context, not a legal ruling derived from the word alone.

No single example should become the word's exclusive meaning without supporting evidence. Restricting مَعَرَّة to blood money does not convey its full semantic range, just as translating it only as “sin” can narrow the verse's expression.

The essential point is that an unknowing action can have real harmful consequences. Lack of awareness does not erase the injury, although it matters when assessing intention and responsibility.

The phrase لَوْ تَزَيَّلُوا is also important: had the groups been separated, the situation would have been different. The verse thus distinguishes opponents from believers living among them; sharing a location does not make the two groups identical.

Read in this way, the verse shows the importance of identifying people and avoiding harm to the innocent in a conflict. That conclusion comes from the whole context, not from the lexical meaning of مَعَرَّة alone.

6. Distinctions from related roots

ع ر ر compared with ض ر ر (ḍarar)

The root ض ر ر concerns loss, injury, and harmful conditions. Ḍarar may directly affect a person's health, property, capacity, or interests.

In its Quranic context, مَعَرَّة emphasizes an unwelcome consequence that reaches those responsible for harming other people.

This distinction is not an absolute rule for every Arabic use. The semantic fields of مَعَرَّة and ضَرَر can overlap.

ع ر ر compared with أ ذ ي (adhā)

أَذَى denotes harm or suffering that reaches a person or another being. It may be physical, psychological, or social.

مَعَرَّة can also include injury and distress, but Al-Fath 48:25 focuses on the adverse consequence arising from unknowingly causing harm.

“Harm” is therefore a close meaning, not necessarily a complete equivalent in every use.

ع ر ر compared with ع ر و (ʿarā)

In Arabic, ع ر و includes coming, approaching, and affecting something. It is thus semantically close to the approaching branch of ع ر ر.

Similarity in meaning does not make the roots identical. مُعْتَرّ in Al-Hajj 22:36 belongs to ع ر ر; resemblance alone does not justify assigning it to ع ر و without morphological examination.

مُعْتَرّ is also distinct from مُضْطَرّ. The latter belongs to ض ر ر and concerns someone under necessity or constraint. مُعْتَرّ in Al-Hajj denotes someone approaching another for assistance or a share. Such a person may be in poverty or distress, but compulsion is not an invariable part of the word's meaning.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

To preserve the distinction between the two Quranic forms, the following are proposed:

الْمُعْتَرّ — Al-Hajj 22:36

“Someone who comes to another seeking assistance”

“One seeking assistance” may also work in a fluent translation, but need not imply that the person voices their need. Where further explanation is needed, the word study can clarify approaching or presenting oneself in the hope of assistance.

مَعَرَّة — Al-Fath 48:25

“An adverse consequence” or “harm and hardship”

In the verse's phrase:

فَتُصِيبَكُمْ مِنْهُمْ مَعَرَّةٌ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ

fa-tuṣībakum minhum maʿarratun bi-ghayri ʿilm

Al-Fatḥ 48:25

Suggested translation:

“So that an adverse consequence would befall you on their account, without your knowledge.”

This translation does not restrict the word to sin, blood money, or disrepute. It leaves room to examine the different proposed instances in their context.

Conclusion

The Arabic root ع ر ر has several semantic branches. One early branch includes skin disease, contamination, and blemish, extending to harm and adverse consequences. Another concerns approaching a person to seek assistance or benefit.

The Quran uses two distinct forms from this family.

In Al-Hajj 22:36, الْمُعْتَرّ denotes someone who approaches hoping to receive assistance or a share. The instruction to feed them includes sharing provision with those in need, without making poverty a necessary condition for every person described by the word.

In Al-Fath 48:25, مَعَرَّة is the unwelcome consequence that could arise from unknowingly harming believing men and women. The verse shows that ignorance of people's identity does not prevent harm to them from having real consequences.

Each derivative's specific meaning should therefore be preserved in translation and explanation. مُعْتَرّ should not simply be equated with a poor person or beggar, and مَعَرَّة should not be confined to a single kind of loss or responsibility.
