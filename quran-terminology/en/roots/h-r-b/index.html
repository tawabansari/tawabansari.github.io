The Arabic root Ḥ R B has several important semantic branches. حَرْب denotes war and hostile conflict, opposed to سِلْم, peace. حَرَبَهُ مَالَهُ concerns taking or plundering someone’s property; حَرِيب is someone who has been deprived of property; and حَارَبَ in its established use means to wage war or engage in hostile confrontation with another.

The same lexical family includes مِحْرَاب, a word used in early Arabic for an elevated, distinguished place in a house, the foremost place in an assembly, a chamber, or a prominent part of a building. In the Qur’an, miḥrāb appears in the accounts of Zechariah, Mary, David, and the structures made for Solomon.

This distinction matters: Qur’anic miḥrāb cannot simply be equated with the recessed niche that marks the prayer direction and the imam’s position in later mosque architecture. That architectural use is established, but it does not encompass the word’s entire early semantic range.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies three separate lexical branches for Ḥ R B: dispossession and taking property, the name of a small animal, and a kind of place or assembly. He associates war with dispossession, but this explanation should not become a claim of proven historical origin for all the derivatives.

The root occurs 11 times in 11 Qur’anic verses: four occurrences of حَرْب, two Form III verbs, four singular occurrences of مِحْرَاب, and one plural مَحَارِيب.

1. Forms and derivatives

Ḥ R B consists of the letters ḥāʾ, rāʾ, and bāʾ and is a sound, non-geminate Arabic root.

The principal forms in this family include:

  • حَرْب: War and hostile conflict, opposed to peace and reconciliation.
  • حُرُوب: Plural of حَرْب: wars.
  • حَارَبَ ـ يُحَارِبُ: Waged war, went to war against another, or rose in hostile opposition.
  • مُحَارَبَة: Waging war and hostile confrontation.
  • حِرَاب: Another verbal noun for warring; independently, the plural of حَرْبَة, a short spear.
  • مُحَارِب: One who wages war or stands in a state of war and hostility.
  • حَرَبَ ـ يَحْرُبُ: Took someone’s property and deprived the person of it.
  • حَرَب: In the property-related use, dispossession and plunder; elsewhere, intense anger and distress.
  • حَرِبَ: Was deprived of property or became intensely angry.
  • حَرِيب / مَحْرُوب: A person whose property or possessions have been plundered.
  • حَرِيبَة: A person’s property and means of living, particularly when taken away.
  • حَارِب: In the property-related use, a plunderer or despoiler.
  • حَرْبَة: A pointed weapon, usually shorter than a long spear.
  • حَرَّبَ: Provoked war or incited someone against another.
  • تَحَارَبَ: Two parties fought one another.
  • اِحْتَرَبَ: Entered warfare or conflict; in another use, plundered someone’s property.
  • مِحْرَاب: A distinguished, elevated place in a house or building, the foremost place in an assembly, a particular chamber, or a space of worship.
  • مَحَارِيب: Plural of مِحْرَاب.
  • حِرَابَة: In general usage, war and hostile confrontation; in later jurisprudential terminology, a category of armed conduct and disruption of security.
  • حِرْبَاء: A small reptile: the chameleon.

Only four lexical groups occur in the Qur’an: حَرْب, حَارَبَ, مِحْرَاب, and مَحَارِيب.

The forms حَرِيب, مَحْرُوب, حَرِيبَة, حَرْبَة, حِرَابَة, and حِرْبَاء do not occur in the Qur’an.

By frequency, حَرْب occurs four times, حَارَبَ twice, مِحْرَاب four times, and مَحَارِيب once.

Differences between derivatives must be distinguished from changes in grammatical case or attached prepositions. For example, بِحَرْبٍ, لِلْحَرْبِ, الْحَرْبِ, and الْحَرْبُ are four occurrences of the noun حَرْب, rather than four independent derivatives.

2. Semantic branches and early Arabic usage

Ḥarb: war as opposed to peace

Lisān al-ʿArab explicitly places حَرْب in opposition to سِلْم.

This opposition shows that in its established use, ḥarb expresses a state of war, hostility, and conflict between parties.

In Arabic, وَقَعَتْ بَيْنَهُمْ حَرْبٌ means that war broke out between them.

حَارَبَهُ likewise refers to fighting another person or group.

Lexical sources usually treat حَرْب as feminine, although masculine use is also recorded.

This matters in some Qur’anic expressions, such as حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ الْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا in Muḥammad 47:4, where the verb and pronoun referring to al-ḥarb are feminine.

The word’s grammatical gender does not mean that war is imagined as a female being in Arabic. This is a feature of grammar.

A pre-Islamic witness: war in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s poetry

In his Muʿallaqah, after referring to prolonged wars and efforts to establish peace between ʿAbs and Dhubyān, Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

وَمَا الْحَرْبُ إِلَّا مَا عَلِمْتُمْ وَذُقْتُمُ
وَمَا هُوَ عَنْهَا بِالْحَدِيثِ الْمُرَجَّمِ

wa-mā al-ḥarbu illā mā ʿalimtum wa-dhuqtumu / wa-mā huwa ʿanhā bi-al-ḥadīthi al-murajjami

Meaning: “War is only what you have known and tasted; my account of it is no report founded on conjecture.”

He then says:

مَتَىٰ تَبْعَثُوهَا تَبْعَثُوهَا ذَمِيمَةً
وَتَضْرَ إِذَا ضَرَّيْتُمُوهَا فَتَضْرَمِ

matā tabʿathūhā tabʿathūhā dhamīmatan / wa-taḍra idhā ḍarraytumūhā fa-taḍrami

Meaning: “Whenever you rouse war, you rouse something blameworthy; when you spur it to ferocity, it intensifies and bursts into flame.”

In these verses, ḥarb is actual war between groups, bringing death, hostility, loss of property, and social harm.

The poet sets the experience of war against untested claims. He speaks of war his audience has experienced, rather than verbal opposition or disagreement in thought.

This witness matters because it attests ḥarb as warfare between human groups in pre-Islamic poetry, independently of later legal and exegetical terminology.

It does not establish, however, that every Qur’anic occurrence of ḥarb necessarily refers to ongoing armed conflict. Each instance also requires examination of its construction and context.

Al-Zawzanī, Sharḥ al-Muʿallaqāt al-Sabʿ: Zuhayr’s verses on war

Taking property and being deprived of possessions

Another branch of the family concerns taking someone’s property.

In early Arabic, حَرَبَهُ مَالَهُ means that someone took another’s property.

حَرِيب and مَحْرُوب describe someone who has lost property through dispossession.

Lexical sources also explain حَرِيبَةُ الرَّجُلِ as the property on which a person’s livelihood depends.

Ibn Fāris treats this branch as an important basis of the root and connects war with dispossession and taking property.

This connection is semantically understandable: war and plunder have accompanied one another in many historical settings.

War may deprive someone of property, land, security, or the means of living.

The association of two phenomena does not prove that every war originally concerned plunder, or that the word for war necessarily developed historically from a name for plundering.

Nor is every theft of property a war between two groups.

Ḥarb meaning war and ḥarab in the sense of dispossession are therefore related, but they cannot replace one another in every sentence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: war, dispossession, and distinct branches

Ḥariba and intense anger

Arabic sources also record حَرِبَ الرَّجُلُ for a person becoming intensely angry.

This usage concerns a hostile, agitated state.

Intense anger may make a person ready to fight, but anger itself does not mean that actual war has begun.

This distinction matters in studying Qur’anic usage: every state of opposition or anger should not immediately be turned into armed warfare.

Conversely, when context mentions an army, captives, weapons, and combat, actual war should not be reduced to anger or internal disagreement alone.

Ḥarbah: a weapon

حَرْبَة is the Arabic name of a pointed weapon, usually described in lexical sources as smaller than a long spear.

Its plural is حِرَاب.

In this physical use, the word names an implement.

In Arabic, حِرَاب can also be a verbal noun of حَارَبَ. Identical written forms therefore do not always represent the same kind of word.

Ḥarbah does not occur in the Qur’an, and the presence of a particular weapon should not be made part of ḥarb’s meaning in translation.

Ḥirbāʾ: a reptile

Lexical sources use حِرْبَاء for a familiar animal associated with sunlight and changes of body colour: the chameleon.

Ibn Fāris identifies it as an independent branch under Ḥ R B.

Some lexical explanations connect the animal’s name with facing the sun, as though confronting it.

This explanation is not a proven historical origin of the name.

The shared letters in ḥirbāʾ and ḥarb do not establish that the animal was originally named “one who fights the sun.”

This branch also has no role in the meanings of Qur’anic ḥarb and miḥrāb.

Miḥrāb: a distinguished place in a house or assembly

Lisān al-ʿArab describes miḥrāb as the foremost and most honoured part of a house, a chamber, and an elevated place.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ likewise records miḥrāb for the foremost place in an assembly and for a chamber.

Abū ʿUbaydah associates it with the foremost, distinguished place in an assembly, while Al-Aṣmaʿī reports its use for a palace.

These explanations show that miḥrāb in early Arabic was not exclusively the name of a place for prayer.

A distinguished part of a house, a high chamber, a notable person’s private place, or a prominent part of a large building may be called miḥrāb.

In some uses, such a place is also associated with worship and seclusion.

This association does not mean that miḥrāb itself means praying in every occurrence.

A witness from poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

Lexical and literary sources attribute the following verse to Imruʾ al-Qays:

وَمَاذَا عَلَيْهِ أَنْ ذَكَرْتُ أَوَانِسًا
كَغِزْلَانِ رَمْلٍ فِي مَحَارِيبِ أَقْيَالِ

wa-mādhā ʿalayhi an dhakartu awānisan / ka-ghizlāni ramlin fī maḥārībi aqyāli

Meaning: “What fault falls on him if I have mentioned companionable women, like gazelles of the sands, in the halls and distinguished places of rulers?”

Here, مَحَارِيب concerns distinguished places in buildings and the assemblies of rulers.

The word does not name a mosque’s prayer-direction niche here.

This witness attests maḥārīb in the context of buildings and honoured places.

Recording a verse in sources must nevertheless be distinguished from certainty about its attribution and date. The usage has independent lexical support, but the precise date of every verse attributed to a pre-Islamic poet is not always certain.

Al-Qurṭubī on Sabaʾ 34:13: the attributed poetic witness and rulers’ maḥārīb

The origin of the name miḥrāb

Lexicographers offer different explanations of miḥrāb’s connection with other branches of Ḥ R B.

One explanation treats it as an elevated, distinguished place separated from other parts of an assembly.

Another describes a place where a person withdraws from other occupations.

Some later works also say that miḥrāb is so named because a person struggles there against Satan or wrongful desires.

This last explanation may be an ethical reflection on worship, but it is not sufficient evidence for the name’s historical origin.

Miḥrāb’s uses for a hall, palace, and distinguished place were not confined to worship.

Its original meaning therefore cannot simply be presented as “a place for fighting Satan.”

The lexical evidence more clearly supports a distinguished place, chamber, prominent part, or dedicated space within a building.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr: miḥrāb and derivational explanations

3. Qur’anic uses of ḥarb

Al-Baqarah 2:279: a warning of war over usury

Verse text:

فَإِنْ لَمْ تَفْعَلُوا فَأْذَنُوا بِحَرْبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَإِنْ تُبْتُمْ فَلَكُمْ رُءُوسُ أَمْوَالِكُمْ لَا تَظْلِمُونَ وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ

fa-in lam tafʿalū faʾdhanū bi-ḥarbin mina Allāhi wa-rasūlihi wa-in tubtum fa-lakum ruʾūsu amwālikum lā taẓlimūna wa-lā tuẓlamūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“If you do not do so, be aware of a war from The God and His messenger. If you turn back, your principal sums are yours: neither wronging nor being wronged.”

In this verse, بِحَرْبٍ is an indefinite noun in the genitive.

The preposition بِـ follows the command فَأْذَنُوا.

The object of awareness and warning in this construction is a war attributed to God and His messenger.

The verse belongs, however, to the context of Al-Baqarah 2:275, Al-Baqarah 2:276, Al-Baqarah 2:277, Al-Baqarah 2:278, Al-Baqarah 2:279, and Al-Baqarah 2:280, concerning usury, property, financial claims, and the debtor’s situation.

The preceding verse, Al-Baqarah 2:278, commands the relinquishment of what remains due in usury.

In Al-Baqarah 2:279, disobedience to this command receives a severe warning.

The subject is thus not merely a difference of belief or a scholarly question, but persistence in a financial demand the Qur’an identifies as wrongful.

The relationship between ḥarb and financial wrongdoing

The end of the verse matters:

“لَا تَظْلِمُونَ وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ,” “neither wronging nor being wronged.”

This expression connects the warning of war with justice and financial wrongdoing.

The creditor is entitled to receive the principal, but not to demand the usurious addition.

The debtor, in turn, should not violate the creditor’s original entitlement.

The verse thus distinguishes preserving property rights from preventing wrongdoing.

In this context, ḥarb expresses the severity of divine confrontation with continued usurious conduct.

The word alone does not establish a precise enforcement mechanism, a specific punishment, or individuals’ authority to launch armed war against anyone accused of usury.

Such claims require separate evidence and examination.

Nor should ḥarb here be translated merely as “mild displeasure,” since the Qur’an itself chooses a much stronger expression.

“War” or “a warlike confrontation” preserves the force of the expression, while its manner of realization requires consideration of context and other evidence.

Al-Māʾidah 5:64: kindling the fire of war

Verse text:

وَقَالَتِ الْيَهُودُ يَدُ اللَّهِ مَغْلُولَةٌ غُلَّتْ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَلُعِنُوا بِمَا قَالُوا بَلْ يَدَاهُ مَبْسُوطَتَانِ يُنْفِقُ كَيْفَ يَشَاءُ وَلَيَزِيدَنَّ كَثِيرًا مِنْهُمْ مَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكَ مِنْ رَبِّكَ طُغْيَانًا وَكُفْرًا وَأَلْقَيْنَا بَيْنَهُمُ الْعَدَاوَةَ وَالْبَغْضَاءَ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ كُلَّمَا أَوْقَدُوا نَارًا لِلْحَرْبِ أَطْفَأَهَا اللَّهُ وَيَسْعَوْنَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَسَادًا وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُفْسِدِينَ

wa-qālati al-yahūdu yadu Allāhi maghlūlatun ghullat aydīhim wa-luʿinū bi-mā qālū bal yadāhu mabsūṭatāni yunfiqu kayfa yashāʾu wa-la-yazīdanna kathīran minhum mā unzila ilayka min rabbika ṭughyānan wa-kufran wa-alqaynā baynahumu al-ʿadāwata wa-al-baghḍāʾa ilā yawmi al-qiyāmati kullamā awqadū nāran li-al-ḥarbi aṭfaʾahā Allāhu wa-yasʿawna fī al-arḍi fasādan wa-Allāhu lā yuḥibbu al-mufsidīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The Jews said, ‘The God’s hand is bound.’ Bound be their hands, and they were distanced from mercy for what they said. Rather, both His hands are outspread; He gives as He wills. What has been sent down to you from your Lord will surely increase many of them in rebellion and rejection. We cast hostility and hatred among them until the Day of Resurrection. Whenever they kindled a fire for war, The God extinguished it. They strive for corruption in the earth, and The God does not love those who spread corruption.”

In this verse, الْحَرْب denotes war and hostile conflict.

The full expression أَوْقَدُوا نَارًا لِلْحَرْبِ, however, is a verbal image.

أَوْقَدُوا concerns kindling fire, and نَارًا identifies the fire.

Kindling a fire for war here pictures initiating and provoking conflict.

It is followed by أَطْفَأَهَا اللَّهُ: God extinguishes that fire.

This contrast presents the beginning and suppression of war through the image of fire.

Pre-Islamic poetry also compares war to fire that intensifies when stirred, as seen in Zuhayr’s poetry.

The image therefore has an established background in Arabic.

The scope of the audience and the conduct criticized

The verse includes كَثِيرًا مِنْهُمْ, “many of them.”

This qualification must be preserved in the analysis.

Later passages in the same surah also discuss different groups among the People of the Book and their differing situations.

The occurrence of ḥarb here therefore does not establish that every Jewish person, at every time, is characterized as a warmonger merely through religious identity.

The passage concerns specific conduct, including rejection, hostility, provoking war, and spreading corruption in the earth.

Ḥarb’s lexical meaning is war; judgment about people must be assessed through the qualities and actions stated in the verses.

Al-Anfāl 8:57: in a state of war

Verse text:

فَإِمَّا تَثْقَفَنَّهُمْ فِي الْحَرْبِ فَشَرِّدْ بِهِمْ مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَذَّكَّرُونَ

fa-immā tathqafannahum fī al-ḥarbi fa-sharrid bihim man khalfahum laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“If you gain mastery over them in war, deal with them so that those behind them are scattered and deterred, that they may take heed.”

In this verse, فِي الْحَرْبِ refers to a state of war.

The preceding context concerns people who enter into covenants and then break them.

The verse thus concerns confrontation during war, rather than every opponent in belief during peace.

فِي الْحَرْبِ identifies the setting of the event itself.

Ḥarb should therefore not be translated here merely as “intellectual disagreement” or “verbal opposition.”

The meaning of war alone does not, however, determine every limit on dealing with an enemy.

Subsequent verses also discuss breach of covenant and the enemy’s inclination to peace, including Al-Anfāl 8:58 and Al-Anfāl 8:61.

Ḥarb’s use here must therefore be examined within that legal and military context.

Muḥammad 47:4: the end of war and the laying down of its burdens

Verse text:

فَإِذَا لَقِيتُمُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فَضَرْبَ الرِّقَابِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَثْخَنْتُمُوهُمْ فَشُدُّوا الْوَثَاقَ فَإِمَّا مَنًّا بَعْدُ وَإِمَّا فِدَاءً حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ الْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا ذَٰلِكَ وَلَوْ يَشَاءُ اللَّهُ لَانْتَصَرَ مِنْهُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَبْلُوَ بَعْضَكُمْ بِبَعْضٍ وَالَّذِينَ قُتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ فَلَنْ يُضِلَّ أَعْمَالَهُمْ

fa-idhā laqītumu alladhīna kafarū fa-ḍarba al-riqābi ḥattā idhā athkhantumūhum fa-shuddū al-wathāqa fa-immā mannan baʿdu wa-immā fidāʾan ḥattā taḍaʿa al-ḥarbu awzārahā dhālika wa-law yashāʾu Allāhu la-intaṣara minhum wa-lākin li-yabluwa baʿḍakum bi-baʿḍin wa-alladhīna qutilū fī sabīli Allāhi fa-lan yuḍilla aʿmālahum

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“When you encounter those who reject in battle, strike the necks until, once you have thoroughly overcome them, you secure the bonds. Then either release by grace or release for ransom, until war lays down its burdens. Thus it is; had The God willed, He would have exacted retribution from them Himself, but He wills to test some of you through others. Those killed in The God’s path—He will not let their deeds come to nothing.”

In this verse, الْحَرْبُ refers to actual war.

The expression تَضَعَ الْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا, however, is a literary image.

أَوْزَار is the plural of وِزْر and can refer to loads and burdens.

Some lexical explanations also associate أَوْزَارُ الْحَرْبِ with the equipment and burdens of war.

The image is thus of war putting its burdens down and coming to an end.

A natural translation can say, “until war ends.”

In a root study, preserving the image of laying down war’s burdens is also useful.

Ḥarb and the question of captives

The verse concerns battle and conduct after overcoming the enemy.

After the fighting stage, securing captives is mentioned, followed by two courses: release by grace or release for ransom.

This structure shows that the verse does not concern only killing an enemy; it also concerns the situation after battle and the captives’ outcome.

Ḥarb itself, however, does not mean captivity, ransom, or release.

Those concepts come from other words in the sentence.

حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ الْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا also emphasizes war coming to an end, rather than presenting war as a desirable, permanent condition.

Al-Ṭabarī: battle, captives, and interpretations of war’s ending

4. Ḥāraba: waging war and hostile confrontation

Al-Māʾidah 5:33: warring against God and His messenger and spreading corruption in the earth

Verse text:

إِنَّمَا جَزَاءُ الَّذِينَ يُحَارِبُونَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَيَسْعَوْنَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَسَادًا أَنْ يُقَتَّلُوا أَوْ يُصَلَّبُوا أَوْ تُقَطَّعَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَأَرْجُلُهُمْ مِنْ خِلَافٍ أَوْ يُنْفَوْا مِنَ الْأَرْضِ ذَٰلِكَ لَهُمْ خِزْيٌ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَلَهُمْ فِي الْآخِرَةِ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

innamā jazāʾu alladhīna yuḥāribūna Allāha wa-rasūlahu wa-yasʿawna fī al-arḍi fasādan an yuqattalū aw yuṣallabū aw tuqaṭṭaʿa aydīhim wa-arjuluhum min khilāfin aw yunfaw mina al-arḍi dhālika lahum khizyun fī al-dunyā wa-lahum fī al-ākhirati ʿadhābun ʿaẓīmun

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The recompense of those who wage war and engage in hostile confrontation against The God and His messenger, and strive to spread corruption in the earth, is only that they be killed, crucified, have their hands and feet cut off on opposite sides, or be expelled from the land. That is disgrace for them in this world, and in the Hereafter they have a great punishment.”

يُحَارِبُونَ in this verse is a Form III imperfect verb.

In its established use, this verb denotes waging war and hostile confrontation.

Here, however, its object is اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ, God and His messenger.

The expression should therefore not be understood as physical combat with God in a material location.

In religious language, confrontation with God may refer to severe, practical opposition to His command and right.

Yet يُحَارِبُونَ here should not be extended to every act of disobedience or intellectual disagreement, since the rest of the sentence explicitly adds another quality:

“وَيَسْعَوْنَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَسَادًا,” “and strive to spread corruption in the earth.”

The verse thus concerns hostile confrontation and striving to spread corruption in the earth.

The relationship between warring and corruption

In this sentence, يُحَارِبُونَ and يَسْعَوْنَ are two verbs joined by the conjunction وَ.

This combination matters.

The entire scope of the ruling cannot be determined from the word “warring” alone without examining the description of corruption in the earth.

Conversely, not every corrupt act necessarily becomes the same kind of warring described in the verse.

The nearby context—Al-Māʾidah 5:27, Al-Māʾidah 5:28, Al-Māʾidah 5:29, Al-Māʾidah 5:30, Al-Māʾidah 5:31, and Al-Māʾidah 5:32—concerns killing, aggression, and the inviolability of human life.

Al-Māʾidah 5:34 also discusses repentance before apprehension.

These indications point to serious conduct with social and security consequences, rather than mere disagreement in thought or rejection of a belief.

In the history of jurisprudence, the term ḥirābah has been used for this verse and often associated with brigandage, armed violence, and disruption of public security.

This legal usage relates to some contextual indications, but all the jurisprudential definitions and conditions formulated in later centuries should not be presented as the certain lexical meaning of يُحَارِبُونَ.

The kind of offence, degree of responsibility, standards of proof, and relationship between punishments and different conduct require independent legal study.

Al-Ṭabarī: exegetical and legal views on warring and corruption

Is disagreement in belief itself warring?

The verse itself does not establish that conclusion.

The Qur’an makes real distinctions between rejection, disagreement, invitation, verbal opposition, wrongdoing, fighting, and corruption.

Not everyone who declines the message is necessarily waging war or undermining others’ security.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 denies compulsion in religion. Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8 and Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:9 also distinguish those who have fought the believers and expelled them from those who have not.

The severe ruling in Al-Māʾidah 5:33 therefore cannot be applied to people solely for declining a belief, criticizing a scholar, or opposing a ruler.

Conversely, the force of “warring” and its connection with corruption should not be reduced to a harmless verbal disagreement.

The verse concerns serious confrontation and conduct that spreads corruption in the earth.

Al-Tawbah 9:107: a mosque for harm and support of opponents

Verse text:

وَالَّذِينَ اتَّخَذُوا مَسْجِدًا ضِرَارًا وَكُفْرًا وَتَفْرِيقًا بَيْنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَإِرْصَادًا لِمَنْ حَارَبَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَلَيَحْلِفُنَّ إِنْ أَرَدْنَا إِلَّا الْحُسْنَىٰ وَاللَّهُ يَشْهَدُ إِنَّهُمْ لَكَاذِبُونَ

wa-alladhīna ittakhadhū masjidan ḍirāran wa-kufran wa-tafrīqan bayna al-muʾminīna wa-irṣādan li-man ḥāraba Allāha wa-rasūlahu min qablu wa-la-yaḥlifunna in aradnā illā al-ḥusnā wa-Allāhu yashhadu innahum la-kādhibūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Those who established a mosque for harm, rejection, division among the believers, and as a prepared base for one who had previously warred against The God and His messenger will surely swear, ‘We intended only good.’ The God bears witness that they are liars.”

حَارَبَ in this verse is a Form III perfect verb.

Unlike Al-Māʾidah 5:33, which uses the imperfect يُحَارِبُونَ, this verse refers to someone who previously engaged in hostile confrontation against God and His messenger.

مِنْ قَبْلُ expresses this temporal priority.

The verse does not mention ḥāraba alone, however.

Four qualities are stated for the mosque in question: causing harm, rejection, creating division, and preparing a place to support prior hostility.

The subject is therefore not merely constructing a building called a mosque, but the building’s purpose and function.

This usage shows that a place’s religious name and appearance do not by themselves prove the soundness of its purpose in the Qur’an.

Yet ḥāraba’s meaning does not establish that every mosque containing disagreement, or every group that differs from another view, falls under this verse.

The case in question is identified through specific conduct and purposes.

Al-Ṭabarī: the harmful mosque and preparation for prior hostility

5. Miḥrāb and maḥārīb in the Qur’an

Āl ʿImrān 3:37: Mary’s miḥrāb

Verse text:

فَتَقَبَّلَهَا رَبُّهَا بِقَبُولٍ حَسَنٍ وَأَنْبَتَهَا نَبَاتًا حَسَنًا وَكَفَّلَهَا زَكَرِيَّا كُلَّمَا دَخَلَ عَلَيْهَا زَكَرِيَّا الْمِحْرَابَ وَجَدَ عِنْدَهَا رِزْقًا قَالَ يَا مَرْيَمُ أَنَّىٰ لَكِ هَٰذَا قَالَتْ هُوَ مِنْ عِنْدِ اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَرْزُقُ مَنْ يَشَاءُ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

fa-taqabbalahā rabbuhā bi-qabūlin ḥasanin wa-anbatahā nabātan ḥasanan wa-kaffalahā zakariyyā kullamā dakhala ʿalayhā zakariyyā al-miḥrāba wajada ʿindahā rizqan qāla yā maryamu annā laki hādhā qālat huwa min ʿindi Allāhi inna Allāha yarzuqu man yashāʾu bi-ghayri ḥisābin

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Her Lord accepted her graciously, nurtured her well, and entrusted her care to Zechariah. Whenever Zechariah entered upon Mary in the miḥrāb, he found provision with her. He said, ‘Mary, where is this from?’ She said, ‘It is from The God; The God provides without reckoning for whomever He wills.’”

الْمِحْرَابَ in this verse is the object associated with Zechariah’s entering.

دَخَلَ عَلَيْهَا زَكَرِيَّا الْمِحْرَابَ shows that the miḥrāb is a place where Mary is present and which Zechariah can enter.

Context connects this place with Mary’s upbringing and guardianship.

The miḥrāb here is therefore a defined space within a building.

The word itself does not determine the space’s size, how many steps it had, or exactly where it lay within the building.

Early lexical usage allows the suitable meaning of a distinguished place or particular chamber.

Mary’s miḥrāb should therefore not necessarily be turned into the small prayer-direction niche of modern mosques.

Al-Ṭabarī: Zechariah’s entry and Mary’s place of worship

Āl ʿImrān 3:39: Zechariah performs ṣalāh in the miḥrāb

Verse text:

فَنَادَتْهُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَهُوَ قَائِمٌ يُصَلِّي فِي الْمِحْرَابِ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يُبَشِّرُكَ بِيَحْيَىٰ مُصَدِّقًا بِكَلِمَةٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَسَيِّدًا وَحَصُورًا وَنَبِيًّا مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

fa-nādathu al-malāʾikatu wa-huwa qāʾimun yuṣallī fī al-miḥrābi anna Allāha yubashshiruka bi-yaḥyā muṣaddiqan bi-kalimatin mina Allāhi wa-sayyidan wa-ḥaṣūran wa-nabiyyan mina al-ṣāliḥīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The angels called to him while he stood performing ṣalāh in the miḥrāb: The God gives you glad tidings of John, confirming a word from The God, a leader, restrained, and a prophet among the righteous.”

الْمِحْرَابِ in this verse follows the preposition فِي.

This linguistic construction expresses Zechariah’s location.

يُصَلِّي identifies his action.

Miḥrāb itself therefore does not mean ṣalāh; it is the place in which Zechariah performs ṣalāh.

This distinction must be preserved.

If someone supplicates in a room, for example, the room does not mean supplication. The place may be known for worship, but the name of the place and the action performed there are different concepts.

Miḥrāb is clearly associated with ṣalāh in this verse. That association does not mean, however, that every Qur’anic miḥrāb necessarily denotes a place for ṣalāh.

Maryam 19:11: Zechariah comes out from the miḥrāb

Verse text:

فَخَرَجَ عَلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ مِنَ الْمِحْرَابِ فَأَوْحَىٰ إِلَيْهِمْ أَنْ سَبِّحُوا بُكْرَةً وَعَشِيًّا

fa-kharaja ʿalā qawmihi mina al-miḥrābi fa-awḥā ilayhim an sabbiḥū bukratan wa-ʿashiyyan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“He came out from the miḥrāb to his people and signalled to them to glorify morning and evening.”

مِنَ الْمِحْرَابِ expresses the starting point of his exit.

The expression shows that Zechariah was in a place and then came out of it.

Here too, the miḥrāb is therefore an actual, defined place.

Taken together, the verses concerning Zechariah indicate that the place was connected with his seclusion, worship, and religious communication.

The word alone does not establish that its entire structure at that time resembled the miḥrāb of present-day mosques.

Nor does the verse explain the exit route, the structure’s height, or the shape of the doorway.

The appropriate meaning is coming out of a dedicated place or space for worship.

Ṣād 38:21: climbing into David’s miḥrāb

Verse text:

وَهَلْ أَتَاكَ نَبَأُ الْخَصْمِ إِذْ تَسَوَّرُوا الْمِحْرَابَ

wa-hal atāka nabaʾu al-khaṣmi idh tasawwarū al-miḥrāba

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Has the account of the disputants reached you, when they climbed over the wall into the miḥrāb?”

تَسَوَّرُوا is important in this verse.

It belongs to the S W R family and concerns climbing or crossing an enclosing wall.

Study of S W R

الْمِحْرَابَ is therefore a place whose wall or elevated part can be climbed in this context.

This image agrees with the early meaning of miḥrāb as a chamber, elevated place, or distinguished part of a building.

It is less readily compatible with imagining miḥrāb as nothing more than a shallow niche in a mosque wall.

The verse’s context thus illustrates the breadth of miḥrāb’s architectural meaning.

The following verse, Ṣād 38:22, describes the disputants’ sudden entry before David and his alarm.

This continuity shows that the miḥrāb concerns the space occupied by David.

Miḥrāb itself does not, however, mean a court or place for judicial proceedings.

Judgment and litigation appear later in the account; the lexical meaning of miḥrāb still concerns a place or space in a building.

Sabaʾ 34:13: maḥārīb made for Solomon

Verse text:

يَعْمَلُونَ لَهُ مَا يَشَاءُ مِنْ مَحَارِيبَ وَتَمَاثِيلَ وَجِفَانٍ كَالْجَوَابِ وَقُدُورٍ رَاسِيَاتٍ اعْمَلُوا آلَ دَاوُودَ شُكْرًا وَقَلِيلٌ مِنْ عِبَادِيَ الشَّكُورُ

yaʿmalūna lahu mā yashāʾu min maḥārība wa-tamāthīla wa-jifānin ka-al-jawābi wa-qudūrin rāsiyātin iʿmalū āla dāwūda shukran wa-qalīlun min ʿibādiya al-shakūru

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They made for him whatever he wished: maḥārīb, figures, large vessels like reservoirs, and firmly set cauldrons. Family of David, act in gratitude; few of My servants are thankful.”

مَحَارِيبَ here is the plural of مِحْرَاب.

It appears alongside تَمَاثِيل, large vessels, and firmly set cauldrons.

The subject is thus things made for Solomon.

Maḥārīb here may refer to buildings, halls, chambers, or distinguished places.

Early exegetical sources likewise offer different explanations: some identify large buildings, others places of worship, and others parts of palaces.

Maḥārīb alone does not establish that all these structures were merely small rooms for ṣalāh.

Its pairing with tamāthīl also does not mean that those figures were necessarily installed in a worship miḥrāb and worshipped.

The verse names several kinds of manufactured things, but does not explain their precise arrangement within buildings.

“Halls and distinguished places” or “prominent structures and spaces” are therefore defensible lexical explanations, while the precise type of each building requires further evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: maḥārīb explained as places of worship and palaces

6. Challenges of interpretation and semantic development

Does miḥrāb originally mean a place for fighting Satan?

Some later explanations connect miḥrāb with a person struggling against Satan or wrongful desires.

This may carry an ethical meaning concerning striving and concentration in worship.

It is insufficient, however, as historical etymology.

Arabic sources attest miḥrāb for a palace, the foremost place in an assembly, a chamber, and a distinguished place.

In the Qur’an, David occupies a miḥrāb whose wall people climb, and maḥārīb are built for Solomon.

Defining miḥrāb as “a place for fighting Satan” therefore cannot explain all its uses.

It is better to retain the attested meaning of a distinguished place and dedicated space, without substituting ethical explanations for lexical meaning.

Is a miḥrāb always a place for prayer?

No.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:39, Zechariah performs ṣalāh in the miḥrāb. This use is clear.

Ṣād 38:21, however, mentions climbing over its wall.

Sabaʾ 34:13 describes maḥārīb being made for Solomon.

A miḥrāb may therefore be a place of worship, but its name alone does not denote a particular act of worship.

The present-day recessed miḥrāb is likewise one architectural form later known by this name, rather than the exclusive definition of all earlier occurrences.

Does war against God mean physical combat with God?

In Al-Baqarah 2:279, Al-Māʾidah 5:33, and Al-Tawbah 9:107, war or warring is attributed to God and His messenger.

God is not presented in these verses as a bodily party on a battlefield.

The expressions concern severe confrontation with His command, right, and message.

This does not mean, however, that every occurrence is merely a metaphor for intellectual disagreement.

Al-Māʾidah 5:33 mentions corruption in the earth and severe punishments.

In Al-Tawbah 9:107, supporting someone who previously warred against the messenger is presented as part of organized, harmful conduct.

Each verse must therefore be examined with regard to its own stated conduct and context.

Are ḥarb and qitāl identical?

قِتَال belongs to Q T L and concerns fighting and conflict connected with killing.

Ḥarb also expresses war, but can refer in Arabic to a broader state of hostility and war between parties.

In some verses, ḥarb may therefore be the setting in which qitāl occurs.

Al-Anfāl 8:57 speaks of being in a state of war.

Muḥammad 47:4 likewise concerns battle, captives, and the end of war.

This distinction should not become an absolute rule whereby every ḥarb lacks combat and every qitāl means only actual killing.

The two families overlap in some referents; context determines their precise application.

Does ḥirābah mean every kind of opposition to a government?

In later jurisprudence, ḥirābah became a legal category.

The word ḥirābah itself does not occur in the Qur’an, however.

Al-Māʾidah 5:33 uses the verb يُحَارِبُونَ together with the description يَسْعَوْنَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَسَادًا.

Not every political disagreement, peaceful protest, or critical question can therefore be placed under warring without establishing the required conduct.

Nor does the root itself entitle any human government automatically to characterize opposition to itself as war against God.

That claim needs independent evidence and must be assessed against Qur’anic criteria of justice, the prohibition of wrongdoing, responsibility, and freedom of belief.

Does war always mean plunder?

Ibn Fāris’s explanation connects war with dispossession.

This connection does not mean that every war is identical with plunder.

War may begin to seize property and land; a battle may also occur in response to aggression and in defence.

Linguistically, ḥarb expresses the state of war itself.

The root alone does not determine war’s purpose, legitimacy, or outcome.

Knowing ḥarb’s meaning therefore does not replace examining the verses concerning defence, aggression, covenant, and peace.

7. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ R B (ḥarb) and Q T L (qitāl): war and combat

Study of Q T L

Ḥarb expresses a state of war and hostile confrontation.

Qitāl concerns more directly the act of fighting and engaging in conflict.

In some contexts, qitāl is part of ḥarb; this relationship should not become a fixed, universal definition of every use.

Ḥ R B (ḥarb) and J H D (jihād): war and exertion

Study of J H D

Jihād belongs to J H D and concerns serious effort and exerting one’s ability.

In some verses, this effort occurs in a setting of battle and defence.

In Al-Furqān 25:52, however, the Qur’an speaks of striving by means of the divine message; in Luqmān 31:15, it describes parents striving to make their child associate partners with God.

Jihād is therefore not intrinsically an exclusive name for war.

Ḥarb, in contrast, has a more direct connection with war and hostility in its principal use.

Ḥ R B (ḥarb) and ʿ D W (ʿadāwah): war and enmity

Study of ʿ D W

ʿAdāwah concerns enmity and hostility.

Two people may be enemies without a war having begun.

In its direct use, ḥarb expresses a state of wartime confrontation.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:64, hostility and hatred appear alongside kindling the fire of war.

This pairing shows that hostility and war may be related, but are not identical.

Ḥ R B (ḥarb) and S L M (silm): war and peace

Study of S L M

Silm in Arabic stands in opposition to war.

In Al-Anfāl 8:61, the Qur’an mentions the other party’s inclination to peace.

War and peace are therefore different states.

Yet the justification for entering war, the conditions of its ending, and the parties’ rights cannot be established solely from the meanings of these two words.

These matters concern the wider set of verses on fighting and peace.

Ḥ R B (ḥāraba) and Ḥ D D (ḥādda): warring and opposition

Study of Ḥ D D

Ḥādda, from Ḥ D D, denotes opposition and confrontation.

Ḥāraba in its established use concerns waging war and hostile antagonism.

In some Qur’anic contexts, both concern opposition to God and His messenger.

This overlap does not establish that every muḥāddah is necessarily armed warfare.

Ḥ R B (ḥaraba) and S L B (salaba): plunder and taking property

Salaba concerns taking and stripping something away.

حَرَبَهُ مَالَهُ likewise concerns taking someone’s property in early Arabic.

The families are close in this use.

Ḥ R B also includes the war and miḥrāb branches, however, while S L B retains its own semantic range.

Ḥ R B (miḥrāb) and GH R F (ghurfah): distinguished place and chamber

Ghurfah may refer to a chamber or the upper part of a building.

Early sources likewise record miḥrāb for a chamber and an elevated or distinguished place.

In Ṣād 38:21, climbing over the miḥrāb’s wall agrees with a defined, elevated space.

Not every chamber is a miḥrāb, however, and not every miḥrāb is necessarily an upper-storey room.

Ḥ R B (miḥrāb) and S J D (masjid): distinguished place and place of prostration

Study of S J D

Masjid belongs to S J D and in its established use denotes a place of prostration and worship.

Miḥrāb in early Arabic may denote a distinguished place in a house, a palace, or a place of worship.

These words are therefore not completely synonymous.

Later religious architecture treats the miḥrāb as part of a mosque, but this architectural relationship should not be imposed as the original meaning of every Qur’anic occurrence.

Ḥ R B (miḥrāb) and KH L W (khalwah): dedicated place and seclusion

Study of KH L W

In the accounts of Zechariah and Mary, the miḥrāb is associated with a dedicated space and religious activity.

Khalwah concerns being alone or withdrawing from others.

A miḥrāb may also be used for seclusion, but miḥrāb itself is not synonymous with solitude.

Sabaʾ 34:13 concerns maḥārīb being built, rather than people’s inner states.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For direct Qur’anic uses of حَرْب, “war” is a suitable basic equivalent.

In Al-Baqarah 2:279, بِحَرْبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ can be rendered “a war from The God and His messenger.” Reflection on the verse should clarify that this is a severe warning concerning demands for usury and financial wrongdoing.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:64, أَوْقَدُوا نَارًا لِلْحَرْبِ can be rendered “they kindled the fire of war.”

In Al-Anfāl 8:57, فِي الْحَرْبِ means “in war.”

In Muḥammad 47:4, حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ الْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا can be rendered “until war lays down its burdens.” “Until war ends” also expresses its contextual meaning naturally.

For يُحَارِبُونَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ in Al-Māʾidah 5:33, “wage war and engage in hostile confrontation against The God and His messenger” is suitable.

For حَارَبَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ in Al-Tawbah 9:107, “had warred against The God and His messenger” preserves the perfect verb’s meaning.

For مِحْرَاب in the verses concerning Zechariah and Mary, retaining “miḥrāb” is natural in Persian, but the lexical explanation should clarify that it denotes a particular place or space in a building.

For David’s miḥrāb in Ṣād 38:21, “an elevated, dedicated place” fits the context, although the basic translation may retain “miḥrāb.”

For مَحَارِيب in Sabaʾ 34:13, “maḥārīb” or “halls and distinguished places” is suitable. “Prayer niches” is overly restrictive and inconsistent with the word’s full semantic range.

Forqan’s word boxes should preserve two independent components.

Brief root meaning: “War and hostile confrontation; dispossession; in the miḥrāb branch, an elevated, distinguished place.”

Meaning in verses using ḥarb: “War, a state of war, or severe confrontation.”

Meaning in verses using ḥāraba: “Waging war and engaging in hostile confrontation, with attention to the conduct described in each verse.”

Meaning in verses using miḥrāb: “A dedicated space, an elevated or distinguished place in a building; sometimes a place of worship.”

Meaning in Sabaʾ 34:13: “Buildings, halls, or distinguished places made for Solomon.”

These distinctions are essential: ḥarb and miḥrāb cannot be translated with one equivalent in every verse.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ḥ R B has several semantic branches.

Ḥarb denotes war and hostile confrontation; ḥāraba concerns waging war and assuming a position of active hostility; in early Arabic, حَرَبَهُ مَالَهُ expresses taking someone’s property and depriving that person of it.

The same family also records miḥrāb for an elevated place, the foremost place in an assembly, a dedicated chamber, a hall, or a distinguished part of a building.

Ibn Fāris does not reduce all these branches entirely to one basis. An unsupported historical image should therefore not be invented to explain them all.

The four Qur’anic occurrences of ḥarb concern war, a state of war, a severe warning, and war’s end.

The two occurrences of ḥāraba concern hostile confrontation against God and His messenger. These require examination of stated conduct, such as corruption in the earth or support for prior hostility; mere disagreement in belief or critical questioning should not be turned into war against God.

The four singular miḥrāb occurrences in the accounts of Mary, Zechariah, and David concern an actual, defined place in a building. Some verses associate it with worship, but its name alone does not mean ṣalāh.

Maḥārīb in Sabaʾ 34:13 also appears among things made for Solomon and concerns prominent structures and places.

Thus, war and dispossession in one branch, and the distinguished place in the miḥrāb branch, must be examined while preserving their differences.

In Forqan’s translation, “war” for ḥarb, “wage war and engage in hostile confrontation” for ḥāraba, and “miḥrāb” or “a dedicated, distinguished place” for miḥrāb are suitable.

The final meaning of each occurrence is clarified not simply by three shared root letters, but by morphological structure, the sentence’s subject matter, accompanying particles, and the verse’s context.

Understanding these distinctions prevents two major errors: restricting every use of Ḥ R B to war, and imposing the architectural meaning of the modern miḥrāb on every place the Qur’an names with this word.
