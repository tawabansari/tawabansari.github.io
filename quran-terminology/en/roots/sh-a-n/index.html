An affair, condition, matter, or concern belonging to someone or something: shaʾn in early Arabic and the Quran

In early Arabic usage concerning human life and relationships, the root SH A N denotes an affair, matter, condition, or concern connected with a person or thing. Shaʾn may be something someone is engaged in, a situation they occupy, a subject concerning them, or an event affecting them. The word is neither positive nor negative in itself; context determines its value, importance, and character.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets attests shaʾn and its plural shuʾūn in the sense of affairs and occurrences. Alongside this usage, another branch concerns channels of tears and the joints of the skull. Both have early poetic witnesses, but historical evidence is needed before all their meanings can be derived from one physical image. The dating and transmission of each witness must be assessed separately.

The root occurs four times in the Quran, always as the noun shaʾn: Yunus 10:61, An-Nur 24:62, Ar-Rahman 55:29, and Abasa 80:37. These uses range from human activity and circumstances to divine affairs and a concern that occupies a person’s entire attention at the resurrection.

1. The root in early Arabic

Shaʾn as an affair, matter, or occurrence

An important poetic witness is a verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani:

وَحَلَّتْ فِي بَنِي الْقَيْنِ بْنِ جَسْرٍ
فَقَدْ نَبَغَتْ لَنَا مِنْهُمْ شُؤُونُ

wa-ḥallat fī banī al-qayni bni jasrin / fa-qad nabaghat lanā minhum shuʾūn

“She settled among Banu al-Qayn ibn Jasr; then affairs arose for us from their side.”

Here shuʾūn means matters and developments arising from a social and emotional situation. The poet speaks of affairs affecting the course of his life and relationship.

Early sources attribute the verse to al-Nabigha, although wording and accounts of its attribution vary. Its use of shuʾūn for affairs and occurrences is nevertheless clear.

Shuʾūn here need not mean administrative duties, official responsibilities, or routine tasks. Its scope is broader, encompassing situations, developments, and matters a person encounters.

Other recorded expressions include shaʾantu shaʾnahu, “I followed his course or purpose.” Ishʾan shaʾnaka means attending to one’s own business or doing what one knows how to do.

These expressions show that shaʾn need not be an external event: it may concern a person’s course, purpose, activity, or focus. Their inclusion in later dictionaries does not by itself establish when each expression circulated in pre-Islamic times.

Shaʾn as a channel or joint

A poem by the pre-Islamic poet Ubayd ibn al-Abras says:

عَيْنَاكَ دَمْعُهُمَا سَرُوبُ
كَأَنَّ شَأْنَيْهِمَا شَعِيبُ

ʿaynāki damʿuhumā sarūbu / ka-anna shaʾnayhimā shaʿīb

“The tears of your two eyes flow; their two channels are like a split waterskin.” Shaʿīb here means a split waterskin, not a watercourse.

Shaʾnayhimā is the dual form of shaʾn with an attached pronoun, referring to the channels of tears.

Early Arabic descriptions also use shuʾūn al-raʾs for the joints and sutures between parts of the skull. Some lexical explanations connect these joints with the passage of tears. This reflects the anatomical language and understanding of that tradition; it should not be equated without examination with modern knowledge of the eye and skull.

The pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Hajar also says:

لَا تَحْزُنِينِي بِالْفِرَاقِ فَإِنَّنِي
لَا تَسْتَهِلُّ مِنَ الْفِرَاقِ شُؤُونِي

lā taḥzunīnī bi-al-firāqi fa-innanī / lā tastahillu mina al-firāqi shuʾūnī

“Do not grieve me with separation, for my tear channels do not flow because of parting.”

Shuʾūnī here denotes the pathways or sources associated in the poet’s imagery with flowing tears. By denying that his tears flow, he describes his response to separation.

Dictionaries also record a related usage for natural fissures and channels in mountains. Shuʾūn al-jabal may refer to cracks or tracks in which water or earth passes or gathers, although the antiquity of these uses is not equally documented.

Did the two semantic branches develop from one origin?

Images can be proposed to connect “affair or matter” with “channel or fissure”: something following a particular course, for example, or a path through which a current passes.

One early lexical analysis explains this family through seeking and pursuit. This fits expressions such as shaʾantu shaʾnahu and may account for part of the relationship among its forms.

It does not follow that every pre-Islamic speaker using shaʾn had a stream, tear channel, or act of seeking in mind.

Nor is there evidence that any of the four Quranic occurrences means a fissure, channel, or passage. Quranic analysis should begin with the sense of affair, matter, and condition, then examine each verse’s context.

At least two historical semantic branches can therefore be identified: affairs and conditions, and channels or joints in bodily and natural descriptions. Their ultimate connection and the direction of historical development cannot be treated as certain.

2. Important forms and derivatives

The principal recorded Arabic forms include:

  • شَأْن — shaʾn: an affair, matter, condition, or concern connected with someone or something.
  • شُؤُون — shuʾūn: the plural of shaʾn; affairs, activities, matters, and circumstances. In bodily descriptions it also denotes channels or joints of the head.
  • شَأْنَان — shaʾnān: the dual, which may denote two affairs or two channels depending on context.
  • شَأَنَ شَأْنَهُ — shaʾana shaʾnahu: following another person’s course or purpose, or intervening in their affairs; syntax and context determine the sense.
  • اِشْأَنْ شَأْنَكَ — ishʾan shaʾnaka: attend to your own business, or do what you know how to do.
  • اِشْتَأَنَ شَأْنَهُ — ishtaʾana shaʾnahu: following another person’s course or purpose in recorded lexical usage.

Only the noun occurs in the Quran: twice as shaʾnin, once as shaʾnun, and once with a plural possessive pronoun as shaʾnihim.

Neither the plural shuʾūn nor the family’s verbs occur in the Quran. This does not mean they were foreign to Arabic at the time of revelation; it specifies the root’s actual Quranic distribution.

3. The semantic core and limits of shaʾn

Several things must be distinguished: the matter itself, a person’s situation in relation to it, the activity taking place within it, and the importance it acquires for them.

Persian expressions comparable to “This matter concerns me,” “What are you engaged in?”, “This is one of his affairs,” and “What situation is he in?” are not identical in meaning, but belong to a shared semantic field.

Arabic shaʾn can relate to each of these aspects.

An activity someone is engaged in may be their shaʾn. An event affecting their life may count among their shuʾūn. A matter requiring their responsibility or attention may likewise be their shaʾn.

Uses concerning a person’s importance and standing also belong to this field, as when someone is called a person of shaʾn. There the word concerns status and significance, but this sense should not be imposed on every occurrence.

In the Quran, shaʾn does not invariably mean high rank, social standing, or a great matter. Nor does it always mean a difficulty, duty, or executive activity.

A useful general definition for the Quranic branch is:

Shaʾn is an affair or condition connected with a person or subject, which may be a focus of activity, attention, responsibility, or involvement.

This definition preserves the word’s breadth without restricting it to will, command, crisis, or social rank. Each verse specifies the particular affair involved.

4. Quranic uses

Yunus 10:61: no affair lies beyond divine awareness

وَمَا تَكُونُ فِي شَأْنٍ وَمَا تَتْلُو مِنْهُ مِنْ قُرْآنٍ وَلَا تَعْمَلُونَ مِنْ عَمَلٍ إِلَّا كُنَّا عَلَيْكُمْ شُهُودًا إِذْ تُفِيضُونَ فِيهِ

wa-mā takūnu fī shaʾnin wa-mā tatlū minhu min qurʾānin wa-lā taʿmalūna min ʿamalin illā kunnā ʿalaykum shuhūdan idh tufīḍūna fīh

Yūnus 10:61

“You are engaged in no affair, nor recite from it any Quran, nor do you perform any deed, without Our being witnesses over you when you engage in it.”

The verse continues by saying that nothing, even the weight of a particle on earth or in heaven, is hidden from the Lord.

Its opening addresses the Prophet with singular takūnu and tatlū; taʿmalūna and tufīḍūna then extend the address to a plurality.

Shaʾn here denotes an affair or situation in which someone is engaged. Fī shaʾnin indicates that it can be a matter occupying or involving a person.

This should not be restricted to the Prophet’s religious activity. The negative construction wa-mā takūnu fī shaʾnin, with an indefinite noun, has a broad scope. Mention of recitation and action then presents divine presence and witness throughout these circumstances.

Shaʾn and ʿamal occur together in this verse. Their coexistence cautions against treating them without examination as entirely synonymous.

ʿAmal concerns doing something or performing an action, whereas shaʾn may be the matter, situation, or sphere in which that action takes place.

For example, dealing with a dispute between two people can be a shaʾn; speaking, judging, or taking a particular step within it is an ʿamal. This distinction helps explain the words, although ordinary Persian may translate both as “work.”

Shaʾn here is neither a title of rank nor simply a problem or crisis. It is a matter in which someone is involved, no part of which escapes divine knowledge and witness.

An-Nur 24:62: shared work and individual affairs

An-Nur says:

إِنَّمَا الْمُؤْمِنُونَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَإِذَا كَانُوا مَعَهُ عَلَىٰ أَمْرٍ جَامِعٍ لَمْ يَذْهَبُوا حَتَّىٰ يَسْتَأْذِنُوهُ

innamā al-muʾminūna alladhīna āmanū bi-allāhi wa-rasūlihi wa-idhā kānū maʿahu ʿalā amrin jāmiʿin lam yadhhabū ḥattā yastaʾdhinūh

Al-Nūr 24:62

“The believers are those who believe in God and His Messenger and, when they are with him on a collective matter, do not leave until they ask his permission.”

It then says:

فَإِذَا اسْتَأْذَنُوكَ لِبَعْضِ شَأْنِهِمْ فَأْذَنْ لِمَنْ شِئْتَ مِنْهُمْ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمُ اللَّهَ

fa-idhā istaʾdhanūka li-baʿḍi shaʾnihim faʾdhan li-man shiʾta minhum wa-istaghfir lahumu allāh

Al-Nūr 24:62

“When they ask your permission concerning some of their affairs, grant permission to whichever of them you wish, and ask God to forgive them.”

This is a particularly clear example of shaʾn in social relationships.

The opening mentions amrin jāmiʿin: a matter bringing people together and requiring their presence. Li-baʿḍi shaʾnihim follows, referring to some affairs concerning those individuals themselves.

The contrast is natural: a shared activity calls for people’s presence and cooperation, while each individual may also have matters and needs of their own.

Shaʾnihim does not mean their rank or social standing here, nor necessarily a major predicament. A family matter, personal concern, or other need may require someone’s presence elsewhere.

Li-baʿḍi is also significant: the verse speaks of some of their affairs in the context of leaving a collective activity. This expression alone does not establish that every aspect of an individual’s life is placed under the authority of the person directing that activity.

Grammatically, shaʾn is a singular noun with the plural pronoun hum attached. Shaʾnihim therefore means “their affair”; the plurality of its possessors does not turn the noun itself into a plural.

The verse shows that shaʾn can denote actual, ordinary matters of human life without being confined to rank, ceremony, or official responsibility.

Ar-Rahman 55:29: divine affairs and continuing governance

يَسْأَلُهُ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ كُلَّ يَوْمٍ هُوَ فِي شَأْنٍ

yasʾaluhu man fī al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi kulla yawmin huwa fī shaʾn

Al-Raḥmān 55:29

“Whoever is in the heavens and the earth asks of Him; every day He is engaged in an affair.”

Here shaʾn is attributed to God.

Its connection with the preceding verses matters. Ar-Rahman 55:26 speaks of the passing away of those upon the earth, and Ar-Rahman 55:27 of the Lord’s abiding presence. Ar-Rahman 55:29 then expresses the creatures’ need and the continuity of divine affairs.

Fī shaʾnin does not mean that God occupies a bodily condition or is busy in the human manner. Divine affairs can be illustrated through creation, provision, governance, response, changes in circumstances, and other actions the Quran attributes to God.

For example, Yunus 10:3 says:

يُدَبِّرُ الْأَمْرَ

yudabbiru al-amr

Yūnus 10:3

“He governs the affair.”

An-Naml 27:62 presents God as the One who responds to the distressed person’s appeal and removes affliction.

These verses help identify examples of divine affairs, but shaʾn itself does not mean creation, provision, forgiveness, or changing destiny. Those may exemplify divine actions; they are not the noun’s lexical definition.

Kulla yawmin also matters. It expresses continuing divine activity against a conception of God ceasing or withdrawing from the governance of creation. The world has not become independent of God after coming into being.

This should not be taken to mean that God follows a human daily schedule or learns about a new subject each day.

The Quran attributes encompassing knowledge to God and does not portray Him as subject to fatigue or incapacity. His affairs therefore cannot be equated with limitations of human work, such as attention to one matter preventing attention to another.

Later in the same surah, Ar-Rahman 55:31 says sa-nafrughu lakum ayyuhā al-thaqalān, addressing the two weighty communities. This should not imply that God is currently too busy with other tasks to attend to humans and jinn and must finish those tasks first.

Shaʾn in Ar-Rahman 55:29 thus remains within the semantic field of affairs and governance, but its attribution to God must be understood appropriately, without importing human limitations.

Abasa 80:37: an affair occupying a person’s entire attention

لِكُلِّ امْرِئٍ مِنْهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ شَأْنٌ يُغْنِيهِ

li-kulli imriʾin minhum yawmaʾidhin shaʾnun yughnīh

ʿAbasa 80:37

“Each of them that day has an affair that occupies him.”

This verse describes the resurrection. The preceding verses say that a person will flee from brother, mother, father, spouse, and children that day.

Abasa 80:37 explains this situation concisely: each has an affair sufficient to occupy their attention.

Shaʾn denotes a matter or circumstance concerning the individual, while its weight and intensity arise from the resurrection context.

Yughnīhi belongs to GH N Y. Here it concerns sufficiency and having no need to attend to other matters, rather than financial enrichment.

The affair does not provide wealth or resources. Rather, it absorbs a person so completely that no opportunity to attend to others remains.

The situation’s intensity and terror emerge from the passage as a whole. Shaʾn itself does not mean fear, anxiety, trouble, or reckoning.

This distinction matters: defining the root as “a great predicament” would needlessly restrict An-Nur 24:62 and Yunus 10:61. In Abasa, the resurrection setting makes the affair grave and all-consuming; the same word can refer elsewhere to an ordinary concern.

Nor does the verse imply that family affection or moral responsibility is inherently worthless. It describes a particular resurrection scene in which each person faces a matter concerning themselves.

5. Distinguishing shaʾn from related roots

SH A N (shaʾn) and A M R (amr)

Amr belongs to A M R and can mean a command, affair, matter, or something about which a decision is made.

Shaʾn overlaps with its senses of affair and matter, but often gives greater prominence to a matter’s relation to a person or their circumstances.

An-Nur 24:62 illustrates this: amrin jāmiʿin is a shared matter that brings people together, whereas shaʾnihim concerns their own affairs.

This is not a universal rule. Amr is not always public and shaʾn always private: Ar-Rahman 55:29 attributes shaʾn to God, while Yunus 10:61 also gives it a broad scope.

The precise distinction depends on syntax, what the word concerns, and context.

SH A N (shaʾn) and AYN M L (ʿamal)

ʿAmal denotes performing an action or task, whereas shaʾn may be the matter or situation within which it occurs.

Yunus 10:61 allows this distinction to be observed, since both words appear together.

For instance, attending to a sick person may be someone’s shaʾn, while examination, care, and particular interventions are their actions.

Persian may call both “work,” but lexical analysis benefits from preserving the distinction between the matter and the action.

SH A N (shaʾn) and KH T B (khaṭb)

In some Arabic uses, khaṭb denotes an important, difficult, or noteworthy matter, as in mā khaṭbukumā in Al-Qasas 28:23.

Shaʾn may also be an important matter, but importance and difficulty are not constant components of its meaning.

In Abasa 80:37 it is grave because the context describes the resurrection. In An-Nur 24:62 it denotes individual affairs alongside a shared activity, without the word itself proving that those affairs are great or difficult.

Shaʾn therefore cannot always be translated as “a momentous affair” or “a dangerous event.”

SH A N (shaʾn) and H W L (ḥāl)

Ḥāl concerns the condition or situation of someone or something. Shaʾn can encompass condition, but is not restricted to it: it also includes an activity, matter, or concern related to a person.

Illness may describe someone’s condition; treatment, associated responsibilities, and resulting issues belong to the broader field of their affairs.

This helps clarify usage without imposing an absolute boundary between the words in every sentence.

Distinguishing shaʾn from shāʾa, shayʾ, and shanaʾān

Superficial resemblance must not lead to the merging of separate roots.

Shaʾn belongs to SH A N, whereas shāʾa and shayʾ are studied under SH Y A. The former concerns affairs and conditions; the latter family concerns willing and a thing or subject referred to.

An-Nur 24:62 makes this especially clear, placing shaʾnihim and shiʾta in the same sentence:

فَأْذَنْ لِمَنْ شِئْتَ مِنْهُمْ

faʾdhan li-man shiʾta minhum

Al-Nūr 24:62

“Then grant permission to whichever of them you wish.”

Shiʾta belongs to the family of willing; its resemblance in writing does not make it a derivative of shaʾn.

Shanaʾān belongs to SH N A and means enmity or hatred, as in Al-Ma'idah 5:8. It is distinct from shaʾn as an affair or condition.

Likewise, mashʾama belongs to SH A M and concerns the left side and an unhappy outcome. It must not be incorporated into SH A N.

6. Syntax and translation suggestions for Forqan

The four Quranic occurrences occupy different syntactic positions.

In Yunus 10:61 and Ar-Rahman 55:29, an indefinite noun follows the preposition fī. Linguistically, fī shaʾnin denotes being engaged in an affair, but the subjects and contexts differ.

In An-Nur 24:62, shaʾnihim is annexed to a plural pronoun and follows baʿḍ. It refers to some matters concerning those people.

In Abasa 80:37, shaʾnun is an indefinite, postponed subject in li-kulli imriʾin … shaʾnun, modified by the clause yughnīhi. The person’s encompassing preoccupation comes from shaʾnun yughnīhi together, not from shaʾn alone.

The following renderings are suitable for the base translation. These suggestions do not alter the installed verse translations.

Yunus 10:61

“You are engaged in no affair …”

“Affair” is natural and clear here. A lexical explanation can also describe its scope as the situation or matter in which the person is involved.

An-Nur 24:62

“When they ask your permission concerning some of their affairs …”

“Their affairs” is natural and preserves the connection with those people. Adding “personal” may help in contextual explanation but is not necessary in the base translation.

Ar-Rahman 55:29

“Every day He is engaged in an affair.”

This preserves the direct Arabic construction. Continuing divine governance and action belong in the explanation; all these concepts need not replace one word in the translation.

Abasa 80:37

“That day each of them has an affair that occupies him.”

“Affair” preserves the breadth of the matter; the Persian choice of amr also avoids awkward repetition of “work.” Preoccupation is conveyed by shaʾn in combination with yughnīhi.

For non-Quranic forms, general shuʾūn can be rendered “affairs” or “activities,” while bodily uses call for “channels,” “sutures,” or “joints,” according to the witness.

“Rank” and “standing” suit certain Arabic and Persian expressions about social status, but should not replace the word’s main meaning in its four Quranic occurrences.

7. Conclusion

In the branch concerning human life, early Arabic SH A N denotes an activity, matter, condition, or affair connected with someone or something. Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets witnesses shuʾūn for affairs and developments. Another bodily branch concerns tear channels and skull joints, likewise attested in early witnesses.

The ultimate historical relationship between these branches is unclear. Their shared form does not justify inventing a single underlying image as the root’s original meaning.

The Quran uses shaʾn four times, always as a noun: Yunus 10:61 concerns a person’s activity or situation; An-Nur 24:62 individual affairs; Ar-Rahman 55:29 divine affairs and continuing governance; and Abasa 80:37 a concern that absorbs a person’s entire attention at the resurrection.

None requires shaʾn always to mean rank, duty, command, crisis, predicament, or bodily activity. Such ideas may be close to it in particular contexts, but do not define the root generally.

A defensible semantic core for Quranic shaʾn is “an affair or condition connected with a person or subject.” This connection may appear through activity and responsibility, circumstances and occurrences, governance, or a matter occupying someone’s attention.

For Forqan, “affair,” “matter,” and, where appropriate, “condition” are primary equivalents. The choice should follow each verse’s syntax and context, rather than imposing a single fixed equivalent on every occurrence.
