The Qur’anic root B Y T

House and shelter: from dwelling and gathering to spending the night, concealed planning, and a House for worshipping The God

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(bayt)
House and shelter; Qur’anic noun

بُيُوت
(buyūt)
Qur’anic plural of house; diverse dwellings

(abyāt)
Lexical plural, commonly poetic verses; not Qur’anic

(bāta / yabītu)

Arabic B Y T concerns houses, shelter, gathering, and spending the night. People return to a house, dwell there, and conduct private and family life. Night is also when people commonly return from outside activity and stay in a house or shelter.

The family includes bāta, spending the night; bayt, a house; bayāt, nighttime or a nocturnal occurrence; and bayyata, doing, planning, or preparing something by night.

These uses are related without being identical. A house can serve life throughout the day, and spending the night does not necessarily mean sleeping: it may involve worship, guarding, thought, or other activity.

Tabyīt can concern something planned or carried out at night. Such action may be concealed, but concealment and scheming are not necessary components of every root use.

The root occurs 73 times in 54 Qur’anic verses through four main forms: 65 bayt nouns in singular and plural forms, one yabītūna, four Form II bayyata verbs, and three bayātan occurrences.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 73 occurrences in 54 verses

Bayt ranges from human homes to bee and spider shelters, houses carved into mountains, and the House associated with Abraham and pilgrimage. Night-related forms concern worship, the timing of an event, concealed planning, or a night attack.

The lexical meaning, the subject described, and the value assigned by the verse should remain distinct throughout.

1. The semantic core and early Arabic uses

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«الْبَاءُ وَالْيَاءُ وَالتَّاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ الْمَأْوَى وَالْمَآبُ وَمَجْمَعُ الشَّمْلِ»

al-bāʾu wa-al-yāʾu wa-al-tāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa al-maʾwā wa-al-maʾābu wa-majmaʿu al-shamli

The family has one principle: shelter, a place of return, and a gathering place.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: shelter,return,and gathering

This definition brings several features of a house together.

It shelters from outside conditions and receives people returning from work and travel. Family members or other people living together commonly gather there.

These features should not become mandatory naming conditions.

A temporarily empty house remains a house, as does a dwelling occupied by one person.

Family gathering is a familiar function, rather than a necessary condition for the name.

Al-Rāghib explains:

«أَصْلُ الْبَيْتِ مَأْوَى الْإِنْسَانِ بِاللَّيْلِ»

aṣlu al-bayti maʾwā al-insāni bi-al-layli

Bayt originally denotes a person’s shelter by night.

He adds that established usage calls a dwelling bayt even without night being intended.

This clarifies the house–night connection. Night lodging may be closely associated with the word’s origin, while established Arabic extends it to dwelling and everyday life more generally.

The semantic relation alone cannot reconstruct a certain history. Deriving the house name from spending the night is intelligible lexical analysis, but historical direction needs independent evidence.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: the house and its relation to night

A house is not only a stone building

Early Arabic uses bayt both for buildings of lasting materials and for tents and shelters of hair, wool, or animal skins.

Lisān al-ʿArab discusses hair and wool dwellings and differences of size and construction.

Khibāʾ commonly describes a smaller tent, while bayt may denote a larger shelter or differently constructed dwelling.

Bayt is therefore not restricted to stone walls, bricks, timber roofs, or one architectural form.

What matters is a place provided for staying, shelter, and life.

The Qur’an accordingly mentions portable dwellings alongside fixed houses.

Bayt and family members

Some Arabic uses concern the household rather than the building alone.

Baytu al-rajuli can denote a man’s family and dependants.

Ahlu al-bayti likewise denotes those belonging to a house or family.

The place’s name extends to people related to it through dwelling and shared life.

Not every Qur’anic bayt therefore means family. The building, a ritual place, or its people may be intended in different settings.

2. Bayt in poetry and extended usage

Baytu al-shiʿri is the familiar term for a verse of poetry.

It names a unit of metrical speech.

Ibn Fāris explains the analogy: as a house gathers people together, a poetic verse gathers words, letters, and meanings into an ordered, metrical construction.

Lexicons quote this line to illustrate it:

وَبَيْتٍ عَلَى ظَهْرِ الْمَطِيِّ بَنَيْتُهُ
بِأَسْمَرَ مَشْقُوقِ الْخَيَاشِيمِ يَرْعُفُ

wa-baytin ʿalā ẓahri al-maṭiyyi banaytuhu / bi-asmara mashqūqi al-khayāshīmi yarʿufu

Translation: “A verse I built upon the back of my mount, with a dark-coloured pen whose split tip drips ink.”

Mukhtār al-Ṣiḥāḥ: the poetic verse and pen witness

The speaker depicts composing a verse and the pen as its building tool.

Bayt here is a poetic unit rather than a human dwelling.

Arabic can thus apply a house image to speech whose parts form an ordered whole.

That does not make “an ordered structure of words” the basic meaning of every bayt occurrence.

The poetic term is a particular extension using the house name to describe verbal construction.

The line appears in lexicons, but the evidence examined does not securely establish its poet or date. It should not be called pre-Islamic without documentation.

Bayyūt: an affair occupying someone’s thoughts at night

Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh al-Hudhalī says:

وَأَجْعَلُ فُقْرَتَهَا عُدَّةً
إِذَا خِفْتُ بَيُّوتَ أَمْرٍ عُضَالِ

wa-ajʿalu fuqratahā ʿuddatan / idhā khiftu bayyūta amrin ʿuḍāli

Translation: “I make the mount’s strong back a resource of readiness when I fear a difficult affair that occupies my thoughts by night.”

Lisān al-ʿArab: fuqrah,bayyūt,and the Umayyah witness

Bayyūt concerns an anticipated difficulty for which the speaker prepares.

Lexicons relate it to an affair over which someone spends the night thinking and worrying.

The root’s relation to night therefore extends beyond sleeping or staying at home.

Night can also be a time of thought, concern, and preparing a plan.

Al-Aghānī describes Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh as an Islamic-period Umayyad poet praising the Marwānids; some later accounts also call him mukhaḍram. The line’s precise date is unclear. It attests Arabic usage but should not be declared certainly pre-Qur’anic without independent evidence.

Al-Aghānī: Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh and the Umayyad period

Buyūt and abyāt

Both buyūt and abyāt are attested plurals of bayt.

Familiar usage favours buyūt for houses and dwellings, abyāt for poetic verses.

Al-Rāghib also notes this tendency.

It is not an absolute rule across all Arabic: lexicons record both within bayt’s plural range.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: buyūt,abyāt,and nighttime uses

Qur’anic buyūt occurs, while abyāt meaning poetic verses does not.

The literary term should therefore not be transferred without evidence to Qur’anic dwelling-related uses.

3. Human houses: dwelling, shelter, and movement

Al-Naḥl 16:80

Verse text:

وَاللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ بُيُوتِكُمْ سَكَنًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ جُلُودِ الْأَنْعَامِ بُيُوتًا تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا يَوْمَ ظَعْنِكُمْ وَيَوْمَ إِقَامَتِكُمْ ۙ وَمِنْ أَصْوَافِهَا وَأَوْبَارِهَا وَأَشْعَارِهَا أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

wa-Allāhu jaʿala lakum min buyūtikum sakanan wa-jaʿala lakum min julūdi al-anʿāmi buyūtan tastakhiffūnahā yawma ẓaʿnikum wa-yawma iqāmatikum wa-min aṣwāfihā wa-awbārihā wa-ashʿārihā athāthan wa-matāʿan ilā ḥīnin

Translation:

“The God made your houses a place of dwelling and rest, and made for you houses of livestock skins, light to move and erect during travel and settlement; and from their wool, fur, and hair He provided furnishings and enjoyment for a time.”

This is a central witness to bayt’s meaning.

One sentence presents two kinds of dwelling.

First are houses where people live and find rest and stability.

Second are livestock-skin dwellings easy to move and use when travelling or settling.

Bayt therefore need not be fixed or immovable.

A tent erected here today and moved elsewhere tomorrow can be bayt.

The word’s function as a place to stay and live takes priority over particular materials or permanent construction.

Sakanan and buyūtikum

Buyūtikum and sakanan occur together at the opening.

They belong to different roots.

Bayt names the place.

Sakan concerns stillness, stability, and rest.

A house can provide rest without bayt itself guaranteeing actual tranquillity in every home.

A suitable building may house people lacking peace because of violence, fear, or severe disagreement.

That does not change bayt’s lexical meaning. The verse presents dwelling as a benefit for human rest, rather than endorsing every situation that occurs inside a home.

Stone houses and houses carved into mountains

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74, Al-Ḥijr 15:82, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 mention houses carved into mountains.

A bayt may thus be made within rock rather than erected from separate materials.

Construction differs while the house name remains.

Brick or timber walls are therefore not necessary to bayt’s definition.

Houses and destruction

Al-Naml 27:52 describes the houses of people destroyed through wrongdoing as empty and abandoned.

Al-Ḥashr 59:2 also mentions houses destroyed by their owners and others.

Buyūt still names houses, whose role now appears amid destruction and loss of habitation.

The word does not guarantee enduring safety or occupation.

4. Home, privacy, and family relations

Qur’anic houses concern more than sleep and rest: family relations, possession, permission to enter, and personal privacy also arise.

Al-Nūr 24:27

Verse text:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَدْخُلُوا بُيُوتًا غَيْرَ بُيُوتِكُمْ حَتَّىٰ تَسْتَأْنِسُوا وَتُسَلِّمُوا عَلَىٰ أَهْلِهَا ۚ ذَٰلِكُمْ خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَذَكَّرُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tadkhulū buyūtan ghayra buyūtikum ḥattā tastaʾnisū wa-tusallimū ʿalā ahlihā dhālikum khayrun lakum laʿallakum tadhakkarūna

Translation:

“You who believe, do not enter houses other than your own until you establish welcome and acceptance for entry and greet their people. That is better for you, so that you may remember.”

Al-Nūr 24:27

The house is related to its inhabitants’ rights.

The Qur’an distinguishes one’s own dwelling from others’ and places a condition upon entry to another’s house.

Tastaʾnisū belongs to ʾ N S and here concerns establishing familiarity, assurance, and acceptance before entry.

Early explanations interpret this as seeking permission to enter.

Al-Ṭabarī: tastaʾnisū and permission to enter

Permission should not become bayt’s own lexical meaning.

The house remains the dwelling; the verse regulates entry to other people’s homes.

Al-Nūr 24:28 also forbids entry when no one is present or permission is not given.

A home therefore has a sphere of privacy in which others’ conduct should respect its inhabitants’ rights.

Bayt itself does not mean privacy: privacy and respect follow from the house-related rulings and social relations.

Uninhabited buildings

Al-Nūr 24:29 mentions uninhabited houses or places in which people have a benefit or purpose.

Buyūt is therefore not always one family’s private residence.

Some buildings serve storage, activity, or public use.

Human habitation is a central function, but every occurrence need not involve a permanently resident family.

Relatives’ houses

Al-Nūr 24:61 names fathers’, mothers’, brothers’, sisters’, paternal uncles’, paternal aunts’, maternal uncles’, and maternal aunts’ houses in connection with eating and family relations.

Buyūt belongs here to a network of kinship.

Homes can provide shared settings for family visits, company, and benefit.

Naming relatives’ homes does not create an unconditional right of entry or disposal of others’ property. Entry, permission, and household rights must be examined together.

Houses during marital separation

Al-Ṭalāq 65:1 concerns not expelling women from their homes or leaving them during the period in question.

Buyūtihinna attributes the homes to the women.

The possessive pronoun does not, without legal examination, establish that every title deed necessarily stood in the woman’s name.

Attribution can concern residence, belonging, or a right related to the place.

Bayt connects with family rights and responsibilities, while each ruling depends on the complete verse.

5. Ahl al-bayt: from inhabitants to family

The expression relates the house to those belonging to it.

Its Qur’anic settings vary.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:12 mentions ahli baytin capable of caring for and raising Moses.

Hūd 11:73 addresses ahla al-bayti in the setting of Abraham’s family.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:33 uses it following an address to the Prophet’s wives.

The basic relationship is to a family or those belonging to a home.

Identifying its extent in each verse remains contextual.

Ahl al-bayt in Abraham’s story

In Hūd 11:73, Abraham’s wife is part of the conversation before divine mercy and blessing upon the household are mentioned.

A real, known family is intended.

The concern is its people rather than walls and roof.

Ahl al-bayt in Al-Aḥzāb

Al-Aḥzāb 33:33 first continues addressing the wives through feminine plural pronouns:

«وَقَرْنَ فِي بُيُوتِكُنَّ»

wa-qarna fī buyūtikunna

“And remain in your homes.”

Ahla al-bayti then accompanies masculine plural ʿankumu and yuṭahhirakum.

That pronoun change matters when examining the address’s scope.

Bayt alone cannot establish that the household here either consists exclusively of the wives or necessarily excludes them.

Immediate context, address structure, and related verses require examination.

Doctrinal or legal meanings such as permanent infallibility, hereditary office, or legislative authority also exceed bayt’s lexical capacity.

Ahl al-bayt names a relationship with a house and family; what the verse says about that family requires separate examination of sentence and context.

6. The House associated with Abraham and worshipping The God

Other verses concern a particular House connected with Abraham, Mecca, and pilgrimage.

Al-Baqarah 2:125

Verse text:

وَإِذْ جَعَلْنَا الْبَيْتَ مَثَابَةً لِلنَّاسِ وَأَمْنًا وَاتَّخِذُوا مِنْ مَقَامِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ مُصَلًّى ۖ وَعَهِدْنَا إِلَىٰ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْمَاعِيلَ أَنْ طَهِّرَا بَيْتِيَ لِلطَّائِفِينَ وَالْعَاكِفِينَ وَالرُّكَّعِ السُّجُودِ

wa-idh jaʿalnā al-bayta mathābatan li-al-nāsi wa-amnan wa-ittakhidhū min maqāmi Ibrāhīma muṣallan wa-ʿahidnā ilā Ibrāhīma wa-Ismāʿīla an ṭahhirā baytiya li-al-ṭāʾifīna wa-al-ʿākifīna wa-al-rukkaʿi al-sujūdi

Translation:

“When We made the House a place of return and safety for people: take a place of ṣalāh from Abraham’s station. We charged Abraham and Ishmael: purify My House for those circling it, remaining there, bowing, and prostrating.”

Al-Baqarah 2:125

Al-bayta identifies the particular House introduced in the Abraham and worship setting.

It is made a place of return and safety for people.

Bayt’s own lexical meaning does not become safety or circumambulation.

Amnan expresses safety; mathābatan expresses return. Circling, remaining, and other actions likewise have independent words.

A real place receives particular functions and standing in the verse.

Baytiya: My House

Al-Baqarah 2:125 and Al-Ḥajj 22:26 attribute the House to The God through a first-person pronoun.

That does not imply The God bodily inhabits a building.

Arabic attribution of a place or thing may indicate a special relation, dedication, belonging, or status.

These passages relate the House to worship, purification, circumambulation, and other acts.

My House therefore expresses the place’s particular relation to The God rather than human-like residence in a room or building.

Avoiding bodily attribution likewise does not justify denying the House’s physical reality.

In Al-Baqarah 2:127, Abraham and Ishmael raise its foundations.

Al-Ḥajj 22:26 mentions its site.

Al-Ḥajj 22:29 describes circling al-bayti al-ʿatīqi.

The House is a real, accessible place in this setting.

Bayt, the Kaʿbah, and Al-Masjid al-Ḥarām

Al-Māʾidah 5:97 identifies the Kaʿbah as al-bayta al-ḥarāma.

Āl ʿImrān 3:96 connects the first House established for people with Bakkah, blessing, and guidance.

Āl ʿImrān 3:97 prescribes pilgrimage for those able to reach it.

Al-Ḥajj 22:29 and Al-Ḥajj 22:33 use al-bayti al-ʿatīqi.

These titles concern a particular place, while each qualifier has its own meaning.

Bayt names the House.

Ḥarām concerns its inviolability and limits on encroachment.

ʿAtīq belongs to ʿ T Q and requires that root’s own evidence.

Bayt itself should therefore not become old, free, sacred, or a place of circumambulation; qualifiers and context supply those characteristics.

Lexically a House, Abraham’s House has a Qur’anic role concerning divine unity, safety, people’s return, and rites of worship.

This neither makes the real place insignificant nor confines all value of worship to bodily presence in a building.

Al-bayti al-maʿmūri: Al-Ṭūr 52:4

Verse text:

وَالْبَيْتِ الْمَعْمُورِ

wa-al-bayti al-maʿmūri

Translation:

“By the inhabited House.”

Bayt accompanies maʿmūr here.

Maʿmūr belongs to ʿ M R and concerns being inhabited or maintained.

Historical and exegetical explanations differ over the House’s referent.

Some identify a heavenly House visited by angels.

Others relate it to the Kaʿbah inhabited by those circling it.

The short phrase itself does not explicitly specify an earthly or heavenly location.

A historical or transmitted account should not become al-bayt’s lexical meaning without independent examination.

Bayt names the House and maʿmūr qualifies it; its full referent requires a separate contextual study.

7. Other houses in creation and Qur’anic analogy

Bees’ houses: Al-Naḥl 16:68

Bees are inspired to take houses in mountains, trees, and what humans erect.

Buyūtan denotes their shelters and living places.

Bayt is therefore not exclusively human.

Animals too can provide places for living, rearing young, or storage.

Their dwellings need not resemble human houses in rooms, doors, or a particular architecture.

The shared meaning is a place provided for life and shelter.

The spider’s house: Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41

Verse text:

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ اتَّخَذُوا مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ أَوْلِيَاءَ كَمَثَلِ الْعَنْكَبُوتِ اتَّخَذَتْ بَيْتًا ۖ وَإِنَّ أَوْهَنَ الْبُيُوتِ لَبَيْتُ الْعَنْكَبُوتِ ۖ لَوْ كَانُوا يَعْلَمُونَ

mathalu alladhīna ittakhadhū min dūni Allāhi awliyāʾa ka-mathali al-ʿankabūti ittakhadhat baytan wa-inna awhana al-buyūti la-baytu al-ʿankabūti law kānū yaʿlamūna

Translation:

“Those taking protectors and supports besides The God are like the spider taking a house. The frailest of houses is the spider’s house, if they knew.”

The spider’s house explains a relationship of belief and practice.

The spider provides a structure for its residence and activity; compared with a human shelter, however, its protection against many external dangers is limited and frail.

The analogy concerns reliance upon a shelter unable to meet the expected need.

Its material must be distinguished from the function of the whole structure.

The verse does not establish spider silk as the weakest material in every physical property.

A strand’s tensile strength differs from a web’s protection against wind, rain, impact, or other dangers.

Nor should its weakness become spider family relations or male–female behaviour without evidence. The verse concerns shelter and reliance.

Bayt retains its meaning as a place provided for residence, while the analogy illustrates the inadequacy of supports adopted besides The God.

8. Spending the night versus sleeping

Al-Furqān 25:64

Verse text:

وَالَّذِينَ يَبِيتُونَ لِرَبِّهِمْ سُجَّدًا وَقِيَامًا

wa-alladhīna yabītūna li-rabbihim sujjadan wa-qiyāman

Translation:

“Those who spend the night for their Lord, prostrating and standing.”

Yabītūna is the imperfect of bāta.

It means spending the night.

Bāta does not itself mean sleeping.

Al-ʿAyn explains bittu urāqibu al-nujūma as spending the night watching the stars.

Al-ʿAyn: spending the night awake

The person may have remained awake throughout.

Sleeping is therefore not a sufficient equivalent for every bāta occurrence.

The verse likewise describes spending the night prostrating and standing.

Sujjadan and qiyāman identify the condition or manner of their night vigil.

Yabītūna does not name prostration or standing itself: the verb concerns spending nighttime and the companion words specify the activity.

The verse alone also does not require unbroken bodily posture without rest throughout every hour.

Its basic meaning is dedicating nighttime to worship with prostration and standing; duration and sequence require independent evidence.

9. Bayātan: a nighttime occurrence

Bayātan occurs three times: Al-Aʿrāf 7:4, Al-Aʿrāf 7:97, and Yūnus 10:50.

All three concern nighttime.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:97

Verse text:

أَفَأَمِنَ أَهْلُ الْقُرَىٰ أَنْ يَأْتِيَهُمْ بَأْسُنَا بَيَاتًا وَهُمْ نَائِمُونَ

a-fa-amina ahlu al-qurā an yaʾtiyahum baʾsunā bayātan wa-hum nāʾimūna

Translation:

“Do the people of the towns feel safe that Our might will not come upon them by night while they sleep?”

Bayātan specifies the nighttime arrival of the event.

Wa-hum nāʾimūna separately identifies their condition: asleep.

The expressions should be distinguished.

Bayātan concerns night; nāʾimūna expresses sleeping.

Equating bayātan with sleep would obscure the distinction.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:4 contrasts bayātan with wa-hum qāʾilūna, arrival by night versus midday rest.

Yūnus 10:50 contrasts night and day through bayātan aw nahāran.

The form’s basic Qur’anic meaning therefore concerns an event’s nighttime occurrence.

Does bayāt necessarily mean a surprise attack?

Some Arabic uses do denote a night attack.

Night may find people less ready to confront it and make an attack unexpected.

Every bayātan should nevertheless not become a surprise attack.

Al-Aʿrāf and Yūnus concern the arrival of divine might or punishment.

Suddenness or unawareness may follow from context; bayāt itself emphasises nighttime here.

A nighttime event also does not imply absence of prior warning.

The basic translation should retain “by night,” leaving surprise and readiness for separate examination.

10. Bayyata: nighttime preparation and concealed planning

The Qur’an has four Form II bayyata occurrences.

Two are in Al-Nisāʾ 4:81, one in Al-Nisāʾ 4:108, and one in Al-Naml 27:49.

The Arabic verb can concern doing or planning something at night.

It can also describe a decision or plan prepared secretly.

Form II alone does not make every tabyīt deceitful or violent.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:81: words contradicting public speech

Verse text:

وَيَقُولُونَ طَاعَةٌ فَإِذَا بَرَزُوا مِنْ عِنْدِكَ بَيَّتَ طَائِفَةٌ مِنْهُمْ غَيْرَ الَّذِي تَقُولُ ۖ وَاللَّهُ يَكْتُبُ مَا يُبَيِّتُونَ ۖ فَأَعْرِضْ عَنْهُمْ وَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ ۚ وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ وَكِيلًا

wa-yaqūlūna ṭāʿatun fa-idhā barazū min ʿindika bayyata ṭāʾifatun minhum ghayra alladhī taqūlu wa-Allāhu yaktubu mā yubayyitūna fa-aʿriḍ ʿanhum wa-tawakkal ʿalā Allāhi wa-kafā bi-Allāhi wakīlan

Translation:

“They say: Obedience. But when they leave you, a group plans by night and in secret something contrary to what you say. The God records what they plan. Turn away from them and trust The God; sufficient is The God as trustee.”

The verse distinguishes public words from a group’s concealed action.

They declare obedience before the Prophet, then some prepare something different upon leaving.

Bayyata concerns nighttime planning and decision here.

Disagreement with the Prophet comes from ghayra alladhī taqūlu rather than the verb itself.

The root should therefore not always mean opposition or hypocrisy.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:108: hiding from people, rather than The God

People are condemned for hiding from others while not being hidden from The God as they prepare speech displeasing to Him.

Yubayyitūna again concerns preparing or discussing concealed speech and plans.

A separate expression in the same sentence identifies the speech as unacceptable.

Its wrongfulness follows from its subject and description, rather than B Y T always being negative.

Al-Naml 27:49: a night attack on Ṣāliḥ and his family

Verse text:

قَالُوا تَقَاسَمُوا بِاللَّهِ لَنُبَيِّتَنَّهُ وَأَهْلَهُ ثُمَّ لَنَقُولَنَّ لِوَلِيِّهِ مَا شَهِدْنَا مَهْلِكَ أَهْلِهِ وَإِنَّا لَصَادِقُونَ

qālū taqāsamū bi-Allāhi la-nubayyitannahu wa-ahlahu thumma la-naqūlanna li-waliyyihi mā shahidnā mahlika ahlihi wa-innā la-ṣādiqūna

Translation:

“They said: Swear by The God that we shall attack him and his family by night, then tell his guardian: We did not witness his family’s destruction, and we speak the truth.”

La-nubayyitannahu has a human object.

It therefore differs from Al-Nisāʾ’s uses.

There, concealed speech and planning are intended.

Here, an attack on Ṣāliḥ and his family is intended.

Arabic tabyīt of an enemy can concern coming upon and attacking him at night.

The context describes a plan to destroy them and deny witnessing the event.

“Attack him by night” is consequently more precise than “plan about him by night.”

Different constructions can make the verb concern preparing a plan or executing a night attack.

Al-Ṭabarī: the night attack on Ṣāliḥ and his family

Attack and killing should not be transferred to every Qur’anic bayyata.

11. Related roots and expressions

B Y T (bayt) and S K N (sakan)

Study of S K N

Bayt names a dwelling and shelter.

Sakan concerns stillness, stability, and rest.

They occur together in Al-Naḥl 16:80.

A house can provide rest without bayt itself meaning psychological peace.

Rest may also concern situations beyond a building.

B Y T (bayt) and D W R (dār)

Study of D W R

Dār can denote a house, abode, or complex of living spaces.

Bayt sometimes identifies a particular place or part within it.

This is not absolute: either word can denote a dwelling.

Both may become “house,” but distinctions involving building, courtyard, and surrounding living space require attention to each sentence.

B Y T (bayt) and B W ʾ (tabawwuʾ)

Study of B W ʾ

These remain independent roots despite proximity of meaning.

B W ʾ concerns lodging, settling, and providing a place.

Yūnus 10:87 combines tabawwaʾā and buyūtan.

Settling and the house itself are distinct concepts.

Tabawwaʾā expresses providing or adopting a place; buyūt names the places.

B Y T (bayāt) and N W M (nawm)

Study of N W M

Bayāt concerns nighttime.

Nawm concerns sleeping.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:97 uses both.

Night and sleep may coincide without being synonymous.

The same distinction matters in Al-Furqān 25:64, where spending the night involves prostration and standing.

B Y T (tabyīt) and M K R (makr)

Study of M K R

Qur’anic makr concerns planning or strategy, potentially involving scheming and concealment according to context.

Planning-related tabyīt emphasises nighttime or concealed preparation.

Makr may be planned at night, but not every makr must occur then.

Nor is every nighttime plan wrongful makr.

Both roots should therefore not become conspiracy in every verse.

B Y T (bayt) and K ʿ B (kaʿbah)

Study of K ʿ B

Bayt names a house.

Kaʿbah is a particular Qur’anic designation for the recognised House in Mecca.

Al-Māʾidah 5:97 brings the two together.

The Kaʿbah is that particular bayt, while not every house is a Kaʿbah.

Family and animal-related bayt does not carry the Kaʿbah’s particular meaning.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

“House” is the basic general equivalent for bayt.

A tent or portable dwelling may be more precise in contexts of movable shelters.

“Houses” is the basic plural equivalent for buyūt.

Ahl al-bayt can be household or family, while context determines the people included.

Baytiya attributed to The God should remain “My House.” Its particular relationship should be explained separately rather than as bodily divine residence.

Al-bayti al-ḥarāmi can be “the inviolable House,” or the recognised Sacred House according to context.

For al-bayti al-ʿatīqi, the independently established ʿ T Q sense should guide translation rather than habitually choosing old or free.

Al-bayti al-maʿmūri can be “the inhabited House”; identifying it requires contextual study.

For bāta yabītu, use “spent the night.” Sleep describes the situation only when independently established by the sentence.

For bayātan, use “by night.”

For planning-related bayyata, use “planned by night” or “planned secretly,” according to context.

For an attack, “attacked by night” is more precise.

Proposed equivalents for important uses

Al-Naḥl 16:80

Buyūtikum: “your houses.”

Buyūtan: “houses,” or “portable dwellings” in view of their description.

Al-Baqarah 2:125

Al-bayta: “the House.”

Baytiya: “My House.”

Al-Nūr 24:27

Buyūtan ghayra buyūtikum: “houses other than your own.”

Al-Furqān 25:64

Yabītūna li-rabbihim: “spend the night for their Lord.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:97

Bayātan: “by night.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:81

Bayyata ṭāʾifatun minhum: “a group of them planned by night and in secret.”

Al-Naml 27:49

La-nubayyitannahu wa-ahlahu: “we shall attack him and his family by night.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41

Bayta al-ʿankabūti: “the spider’s house.”

House should not automatically become family, temple, Kaʿbah, privacy, or tranquillity.

Nor should bāta always become slept, or bayyata always conspired.

Context may connect such meanings, but each word requires its own sentence and subject.

13. Conclusion

B Y T concerns houses, shelter, return, and spending the night.

Ibn Fāris foregrounds shelter and gathering; al-Rāghib highlights nighttime lodging.

Houses nevertheless extend beyond night, and spending the night need not mean sleeping.

Qur’anic bayt covers human homes, portable shelters, mountain dwellings, bee and spider houses, families, and the House linked to Abraham and pilgrimage.

These houses do not share identical architecture, functions, or standing.

A human home concerns residence and family relations.

Bees’ and spiders’ houses provide places for life and activity.

Abraham’s House is a real place connected with divine unity, safety, circumambulation, and worship.

Ahl al-bayt extends the place’s name to its family, but context and address structure determine the household’s scope.

Night-related yabītūna expresses spending the night; bayātan denotes nocturnal occurrence; bayyata can describe nighttime preparation or action.

Positive and negative values arise from context.

Al-Furqān 25:64 combines spending the night with prostration and standing.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:81 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:108 connect concealed planning with wrongful words and conduct.

Al-Naml 27:49 concerns a planned night attack on Ṣāliḥ and his family.

Nighttime itself is neither virtue nor sin: subject, intention, and actual conduct determine value.

Final root definition: In its spatial branch, B Y T denotes a house or place provided for dwelling, shelter, and return; its temporal branch concerns spending the night. Related uses concern doing, planning, or preparing something at night.

The family distinctively relates house and night to residence, shelter, and nighttime activity, without restricting every use to sleeping, concealment, or one architectural form.

Qur’anic bayt means house; bāta, spending the night; bayāt, by night; and tabyīt, planning or action by night according to context.

Qualifiers such as inviolable, ʿatīq, inhabited, or belonging to a particular family require their own words and context. Bayt retains house and shelter, while the verses establish each house’s religious, social, and moral role.
