Expressing suffering, complaint and seeking redress; their relationship to patience, with a separate study of mishkāh

In one of its principal early Arabic branches, SH K W concerns pain, suffering or dissatisfaction and making it known to another. Shakā is used when someone speaks of illness, grief, another person’s harmful conduct or difficult circumstances and expresses what troubles them.

Complaint in this sense need not accuse or object to a person. Someone may complain of illness, share sorrow, seek redress for another’s wrongdoing or recount suffering to God.

These uses share a feature: suffering, dissatisfaction or harm confronting a person is made known and expressed.

The Qur’an presents this meaning in two different situations: Jacob complains of his distress and sorrow to God, and a woman discusses her marital situation with the Prophet while complaining to God.

Another word classified under this root in Qur’anic morphological indexes is mishkāh in Al-Nūr 24:35. It concerns a place for a lamp and differs in meaning from complaint. Its historical relationship to shakā is uncertain and requires separate examination.

1. The root in early Arabic

Making suffering and dissatisfaction known

In established Arabic usage, shakā denotes expressing pain, suffering, dissatisfaction or harmful conduct from which someone has suffered.

Shakā maraḍahu, shakā hammahu and shakā fulānan can respectively concern expressing the suffering of illness, recounting sorrow and complaining of another person’s conduct.

The subject of complaint is therefore not always the same: it may be a physical or mental condition, an unwelcome event or someone’s harmful behavior.

Classical Arabic records shakw, shakwā, shakāh, shakāwah and shikāyah as nominal forms in this family.

Shakāh and shakw can also name illness or suffering itself. This relates to expressing pain, but the painful condition should be distinguished from the act of complaining.

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

A clear early witness occurs in the Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād. Describing his wounded horse on the battlefield, he says:

فَازْوَرَّ مِنْ وَقْعِ الْقَنَا بِلَبَانِهِ

fa-izwarra min waqʿi al-qanā bi-labānihi

وَشَكَا إِلَيَّ بِعَبْرَةٍ وَتَحَمْحُمِ

wa-shakā ilayya bi-ʿabratin wa-taḥamḥum

لَوْ كَانَ يَدْرِي مَا الْمُحَاوَرَةُ اشْتَكَى

law kāna yadrī mā al-muḥāwaratu ishtakā

وَلَكَانَ لَوْ عَلِمَ الْكَلَامَ مُكَلِّمِي

wa-la-kāna law ʿalima al-kalāma mukallimī

Translation:

“My horse swerved from the spears striking its chest and complained to me with tears and a low neigh. Had it known how to converse, it would have complained; had it known speech, it would have spoken to me.”

The horse suffers from wounds and pain. The poet presents its bodily reaction, tears and voice as a complaint.

Shakā ilayya in the first verse conveys making suffering known to another. The horse does not speak human language, but the poet describes its response as though it expressed its pain to its rider.

The next verse confirms that this complaint is a literary portrayal of the animal’s condition: if the horse could converse, it would explain its suffering in words.

This witness shows that complaint in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period was not confined to accusation or litigation. It also concerned expressing suffering and drawing attention to it.

The attribution to ʿAntarah is established in the commentary tradition of the Muʿallaqāt, although this study has not independently established the date of these particular verses. They illuminate early usage without determining when the word originated.

Suffering, expression and the addressee

Three elements may be distinguished in a complaint: its subject, the person experiencing or presenting it, and the addressee to whom it is directed.

Not all three need to be explicitly mentioned in every sentence.

Shakā fulānan may mean complaining of that person’s conduct. Shakā ilā fulānin identifies the recipient of the complaint. A person may also make sorrow or illness the verb’s direct object.

Ilā is therefore important in this construction: it marks the direction of the complaint.

When someone complains of suffering to God, God is the addressee. The construction alone does not make God the object of the grievance.

This distinction is particularly important in Yūsuf 12:86.

2. Linguistic forms and Qur’anic occurrences

The Qur’an uses two verbs in the semantic family of complaint:

Ashkū in Yūsuf 12:86 and tashtakī in Al-Mujādilah 58:1.

Ashkū is the first-person singular imperfect of the basic verb shakā, yashkū. Its initial hamzah marks the first person, not Form IV.

In this verse, tashtakī is the third-person feminine singular imperfect of ishtakā, Form VIII. This verb also expresses complaint and suffering in Arabic.

Form VIII can create semantic differences in some words, but shakā and ishtakā overlap in the sense of complaint. Their different forms alone do not establish that the woman’s complaint differs from Jacob’s in intensity, duration or moral value.

Mishkāh also occurs in Al-Nūr 24:35 and is classified under SH K W in morphological indexes. This accords with some lexicographers’ analysis of its Arabic structure, but does not prove a single historical semantic origin for mishkāh and complaint.

The Qur’anic occurrences should therefore be treated in two distinct branches: verbs for expressing suffering and the noun mishkāh, a place for a lamp.

3. Jacob’s complaint: grief alongside patience

Yūsuf 12:86 states:

قَالَ إِنَّمَا أَشْكُو بَثِّي وَحُزْنِي إِلَى اللَّهِ وَأَعْلَمُ مِنَ اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

qāla innamā ashkū baththī wa-ḥuznī ilā allāhi wa-aʿlamu mina allāhi mā lā taʿlamūn

Yūsuf 12:86

Translation:

“He said: I complain of my distress and sorrow only to God, and I know from God what you do not know.”

Jacob speaks as a father who has been separated from his son for years and has borne profound sorrow.

Yūsuf 12:84 describes his intense grief and the whitening of his eyes. In Yūsuf 12:85, his sons then say that he will keep remembering Joseph until he is worn down or perishes.

His response in the following verse is innamā ashkū baththī wa-ḥuznī ilā allāh.

Complaint, distress and sorrow

Ashkū expresses the act of making suffering known; baththī and ḥuznī are the subjects of the complaint.

Bathth belongs to B TH TH, associated with scattering and spreading. Here it means deep distress and grief; lexical explanations connect it with sorrow a person reveals and pours out. Sorrow spreading through the body is not the verse’s literal wording.

Ḥuzn concerns sadness and grief.

Complaint is thus not sorrow itself, but its expression. This is why the whole sentence cannot be reduced to “I am sad.”

Jacob not only grieves: he expresses his grief to God.

Innamā in Jacob’s complaint

Innamā conveys restriction or exclusivity. Responding to his sons, Jacob makes clear that he directs his complaint of distress and sorrow to God.

This restriction in the particular situation must be distinguished from making a universal rule for everyone.

Jacob does not say that no one may ever share suffering with another human being. He speaks about his own conduct and the direction of his complaint.

He also makes this statement in his sons’ presence. The verse therefore cannot establish that all conversation with others about grief and suffering is forbidden.

Is complaint incompatible with patience?

In Yūsuf 12:18 and Yūsuf 12:83, Jacob speaks of fa-ṣabrun jamīl. In Yūsuf 12:86, he complains of his sorrow to God.

Both occur in the same person’s conduct.

Patience therefore cannot be defined as necessarily excluding grief, suppressing emotions or absolutely refraining from complaint.

Jacob is sorrowful and expresses his suffering while retaining hope and continuing his efforts.

In Yūsuf 12:87, he tells his sons to search for Joseph and his brother and not despair of God’s relief and mercy.

The sequence presents Jacob’s complaint alongside hope and action, not abandonment of responsibility or passive surrender to circumstances.

For a broader discussion, see Forqan’s article on patience, steadfastness and the criteria of their value.

4. The woman’s complaint in Al-Mujādilah: suffering and seeking redress

The other verb occurs in Al-Mujādilah 58:1:

قَدْ سَمِعَ اللَّهُ قَوْلَ الَّتِي تُجَادِلُكَ فِي زَوْجِهَا وَتَشْتَكِي إِلَى اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ يَسْمَعُ تَحَاوُرَكُمَا إِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ بَصِيرٌ

qad samiʿa allāhu qawla allatī tujādiluka fī zawjihā wa-tashtakī ilā allāhi wa-allāhu yasmaʿu taḥāwurakumā inna allāha samīʿun baṣīr

Al-Mujādilah 58:1

Translation:

“God has heard the words of the woman who disputes with you about her husband and complains to God. God hears your exchange. God is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.”

A woman discusses and disputes her marital situation with Muḥammad while directing her complaint to God.

Tujādiluka, from J D L, concerns an argumentative exchange and pursuing a case through discussion. Tashtakī, from SH K W, expresses her suffering or complaint.

The verbs are not identical in meaning. She argues a matter with the Prophet while also bringing her complaint to God.

Complaint alongside discussion and seeking redress

The verse opens with qad samiʿa allāhu: God has heard her words.

Wa-allāhu yasmaʿu taḥāwurakumā then emphasizes that God hears both sides of the exchange.

Taḥāwurakumā refers to the reciprocal conversation between the woman and the Prophet. She is not merely grieving inwardly or delivering a one-sided statement; she has raised the issue and engaged in discussion.

The following verses address ẓihār and its rulings. Al-Mujādilah 58:2 condemns the speech likening a wife to the speaker’s own mother and denies that it actually makes her his mother. Al-Mujādilah 58:3 and Al-Mujādilah 58:4 then set out the associated obligations.

The sequence shows that the complaint concerns a real matter affecting her rights and family life.

Does complaining of injustice contradict trust in God?

The verse challenges an interpretation that demands silence and endless endurance from an oppressed person and treats protest or seeking redress as weak faith.

The woman both complains to God and discusses her problem with the Prophet.

The verse does not oppose these actions. Turning to God has not prevented her from using human avenues to resolve the problem.

The Qur’an neither dismisses her speech nor asks her to conceal her suffering and protest to preserve appearances within the family.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:148 also expressly makes an exception for someone who has been wronged regarding the public utterance of adverse speech.

Complaining of wrongful conduct and seeking one’s rights therefore cannot be condemned merely because it is a complaint.

This does not authorize slander, false statements or violations of others’ rights. A complaint’s validity and moral value depend on its facts, manner and the bounds of legitimate rights.

The two Qur’anic complaints compared

In Yūsuf 12:86, Jacob complains of his distress and sorrow to God.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:1, a woman discusses a family matter and complains to God.

Both complaints are directed to God, but their subjects and circumstances differ.

The first emphasizes sorrow from separation and loss. The second concerns a problem within family relations and efforts to resolve it.

SH K W is therefore not confined to one kind of suffering. Complaint can express personal grief or form part of seeking redress for harmful conduct.

5. Mishkāh: a distinct lexical branch

Al-Nūr 24:35 states:

اللَّهُ نُورُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ مَثَلُ نُورِهِ كَمِشْكَاةٍ فِيهَا مِصْبَاحٌ

allāhu nūru al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi mathalu nūrihi ka-mishkātin fīhā miṣbāḥ

Al-Nūr 24:35

Translation:

“God is the light of the heavens and the earth. The likeness of His light is as a niche containing a lamp.”

In its familiar lexical explanation, mishkāh is a recess or niche in a wall that does not open through to the other side and in which a lamp is placed.

Other lexical explanations relate it to a place where a lamp is positioned or suspended. These explanations share the idea of a location or support for the lamp.

Mishkāh and miṣbāḥ are distinct in the verse. The lamp is inside the niche, after which the text elaborates on the lamp and its glass.

Translating mishkāh as the lamp itself erases the distinction between the nouns.

Mishkāh and shakwah

Shakwah names a small leather vessel used for water or milk.

Some lexical accounts place shakwah alongside mishkāh when discussing the word’s Arabic structure. Both can be pictured as places holding something, but this explanatory resemblance should not be treated as an established account of derivation.

Semantic resemblance alone does not prove a definite historical relationship.

Early accounts also disagree about mishkāh’s origin: some regard it as Arabic, while others attribute its meaning to an Ethiopic language.

Recording these attributions is not enough to establish historical origin. The actual form in the proposed language, its date of use and possible linguistic contact require independent investigation.

It is therefore not certain that mishkāh was formed from shakwah or arose from the same central meaning as complaint.

Placing mishkāh under SH K W in a morphological index is no substitute for that historical inquiry.

Mishkāh in the context of light

In Al-Nūr 24:35, the niche forms part of the image illustrating light.

A lamp is situated in a niche, and the lamp is in glass. The image relates light, the lamp, its location and the characteristics of illumination.

Mishkāh itself denotes the lamp’s location. A full interpretation of divine light requires the whole verse and related passages; the sense of complaint should not be imposed on this word.

Mishkāh belongs in the study of this indexed family, while remaining semantically distinct from the two verbs of complaint.

6. Related roots and concepts

SH K W (shakw) and B TH TH (bathth)

In its complaint branch, shakw means expressing suffering or dissatisfaction.

Bathth concerns scattering and spreading; in Yūsuf 12:86 it denotes deep distress and sorrow that may be expressed or poured out.

Jacob says ashkū baththī: he complains of his distress. Bathth is the object of complaint, not a synonym for the verb.

SH K W (shakw) and Ḥ Z N (ḥuzn)

Ḥuzn is a state of sorrow. Shakw can be the act of expressing that sorrow.

Someone may grieve without speaking about it, share it with another person or complain of it to God.

The presence of sorrow alone therefore does not establish that a complaint has occurred.

SH K W (shakw) and D ʿ W (duʿāʾ)

Duʿāʾ concerns calling or summoning. Addressed to God, it can encompass petition, prayer and seeking help.

Complaint focuses on expressing suffering or a problem.

A complaint can take the form of prayer: a person may recount suffering to God and ask for relief. But not every prayer is a complaint, and not every complaint explicitly requests a particular thing.

In Yūsuf 12:86, Jacob calls his expression of sorrow a complaint. The expression does not itself state a specific request, though it occurs in a context of hope and seeking help.

SH K W (shakw) and J Z ʿ (jazaʿ)

Jazaʿ concerns distress and loss of composure or endurance under adverse circumstances.

Complaint alone does not establish such a condition.

Someone may express pain while retaining resolve and responsibility. Another person may become overwhelmed by the same circumstances.

Not every complaint can therefore be called jazaʿ.

SH K W (shakw) and Ṣ B R (ṣabr)

Qur’anic ṣabr concerns steadfastness and holding oneself firm against pressures and impulses. Its value depends on what one persists in and toward what end.

Complaint expresses suffering.

The concepts are not necessarily lexical opposites. In the same story, Jacob speaks of beautiful patience and complains of his sorrow to God.

Patience cannot therefore be equated with absolute silence, nor complaint labeled impatience without examining context.

SH K W (shakw) and SH K R (shukr)

Shukr concerns recognizing and appreciating goodness and blessings, expressed in speech or conduct.

Complaint concerns suffering, harm or dissatisfaction.

Someone can be grateful for blessings while also complaining of illness, injustice or another problem.

Complaint alone therefore does not prove ingratitude. Judgment depends on the subject of the speech and the person’s conduct.

SH K W (shakw) and SH K K (shakk)

These roots are close in writing and sound, but differ in meaning.

Shakk, from SH K K, concerns doubt and lack of certainty.

Shakw, from SH K W, concerns expressing suffering and dissatisfaction.

Someone may doubt a report without complaining about it, or complain of illness or harmful conduct without doubting its reality.

The roots should not be confused.

Some lexicographers also regard shākī al-silāḥ, describing someone equipped with arms, as a rearranged form of shāʾik from SH W K. That use must not be treated as evidence for complaint or its origin without separate examination.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For Qur’anic shakā, “complain” and “bring a complaint” are natural, accurate equivalents.

In Yūsuf 12:86, ashkū baththī wa-ḥuznī ilā allāh may be rendered:

“I complain of my distress and sorrow to God.”

This preserves both the complaint’s subject and its direction.

Ashkū here should not be translated “I complain about God”: ilā allāh identifies God as the addressee, not the object of protest.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:1, wa-tashtakī ilā allāh may be rendered “and she complains to God.”

“Seeking redress” can be appropriate in contextual explanation, since the woman discusses and pursues a harmful family situation. Tashtakī itself should not be confined in every setting to formal complaints or legal petitions.

For mishkāh in Al-Nūr 24:35, “lamp niche” or “niche” is suitable. Persian cherāghgāh is concise; an explanation can clarify that it means a recess or place where a lamp is positioned.

Persian cherāghdān may also be used broadly for a lamp’s location or holder, but it can name the supporting implement itself. Cherāghgāh more clearly preserves the distinction between mishkāh and miṣbāḥ. These proposals do not change the site’s installed verse translations.

Conclusion

The complaint branch of SH K W concerns pain, suffering, dissatisfaction and their expression. The witness attributed to ʿAntarah shows that early poetry could describe a wounded horse’s reaction as a complaint, since its behavior made suffering apparent to its rider.

This meaning appears in two Qur’anic verbs.

In Yūsuf 12:86, Jacob complains of distress and sorrow to God, alongside beautiful patience, hope and a continuing search.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:1, a woman discusses a family problem with the Prophet and complains to God. Turning to God is compatible here with raising a problem before people and pursuing its resolution.

Mishkāh in Al-Nūr 24:35 is also indexed under this root, but denotes a place for a lamp. Its historical relationship to complaint is uncertain and it should be treated as a distinct semantic branch within the indexed family.

The crucial distinction is that expressing suffering is not identical with losing composure, ingratitude, protesting against God or abandoning patience. A complaint’s moral value depends on its subject, truthfulness, intention and the person’s response to the problem.

The principal branch may therefore be defined as follows:

Shakw expresses pain, sorrow, dissatisfaction or harm confronting someone, whether to describe their condition, draw attention, request help or seek redress for another person’s harmful conduct.

This meaning is preserved in both Qur’anic verbs. Silence, loss of composure, protest or formal litigation is not a necessary component of the word itself.
