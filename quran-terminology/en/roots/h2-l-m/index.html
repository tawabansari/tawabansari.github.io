Entry H L M — Halumma, summoning and bringing forward

Qur’anic and lexical forms

هَلُمَّ
(halumma)
Come or bring forward; summons

From inviting someone to approach to requesting that a person or thing be brought forward

هَلُمَّ is an Arabic summons: the speaker asks the addressee to come closer, approach, or bring someone or something forward.

It occurs twice in the Qur’an: once requesting witnesses, and once as people summon others to join them.

Its grammar and etymology differ from ordinary triliteral verbs. Many grammarians classify it as an imperative verb-name: an expression with imperative force that, in one major usage, does not conjugate like an ordinary verb.

Early grammarians differed over whether it was originally independent or compounded from two elements. H L M is a useful catalog heading, not proof of an independently established triliteral root.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Early Arabic used هَلُمَّ to summon someone closer or request that something be brought forward.

Its two main uses are:

First, inviting people to come closer, as when calling companions to oneself.

Second, requesting a person or thing, such as witnesses, companions, or evidence.

Both involve summoning or bringing near; sometimes the addressee approaches, sometimes they bring someone or something else.

A witness from early Arabic poetry

A poem attributed to Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr reads:

«هَلُمَّ إِلَيْنَا آلَ بُهْثَةَ إِنَّمَا
هِيَ الدَّارُ لَا نَعْتَافُهَا وَنُهِينُهَا»

Halumma ilaynā āla Buhthata innamā / hiya d-dāru lā naʿtāfuhā wa-nuhīnuhā.

“Come to us, clan of Buhthah; this is the homeland—we neither disdain it nor treat it with contempt.”

The next line reads:

«هَلُمَّ إِلَى ذُبْيَانَ إِنَّ بِلَادَهَا
حُصُونٌ وَإِنَّ السَّمْهَرِيَّ قُرُونُهَا»

Halumma ilā Dhubyāna inna bilādahā / ḥuṣūnun wa-inna s-samhariyya qurūnuhā.

“Come to Dhubyān; its lands are fortresses, and its spears are their defensive horns.”

Both lines invite a group to come and join a particular community or territory.

The poet uses the same unchanged form although addressing multiple people.

This resembles هَلُمَّ إِلَيْنَا in al-Aḥzāb 33:18: others are summoned toward the speaker or their group.

Kaʿb lived across both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The poem is preserved in his attributed collection, but its exact date is uncertain. His pre-Islamic lifetime alone does not date this line before revelation.

The poem illustrates early traditional usage; the Qur’an itself provides clearer datable evidence from the revelation period.

Does halumma always mean physical arrival?

Its ordinary direct usage can request actual movement to a place.

But when someone is asked to produce witnesses, the request is not merely for that person to come: it concerns bringing others to testify.

Classical Arabic accordingly distinguishes هَلُمَّ إِلَيْنَا, “come to us,” from هَلُمَّ شُهَدَاءَكُمُ, “bring your witnesses.”

Both occur in the Qur’an and are more decisive for interpretation than an etymological hypothesis.

2. Grammar and the question of origin

An imperative verb-name

An Arabic ism fiʿl conveys a verb’s meaning without possessing all the structural and usage features of an ordinary verb.

In Hijazi usage, هَلُمَّ is classified as an imperative verb-name.

Depending on the sentence, it means “come” or “bring,” addressed to one or more people.

Its notable Hijazi feature is one fixed form for singular, dual, plural, masculine, and feminine addressees.

Thus one can address a man, a woman, or a group with:

هَلُمَّ

Number and gender are understood from the situation and surrounding sentence rather than the word’s form.

In Tamimi and some other Arabic usage, however, it inflects like an imperative:

  • هَلُمَّ: to one man.
  • هَلُمِّي: to one woman.
  • هَلُمَّا: to two people.
  • هَلُمُّوا: to a masculine plural group.
  • هَلْمُمْنَ: to a feminine plural group.

These forms are recorded in grammatical accounts; the Qur’an uses only unchanged هَلُمَّ.

Number of addressees and the word’s form

Al-Anʿām 6:150 addresses a group of claimants, asking for their witnesses.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18 also describes people addressing their brothers.

Both nevertheless use هَلُمَّ without a plural ending.

This matches Hijazi usage, with one form for all addressees.

Its appearance therefore does not make either verse a singular address.

Translation should follow the actual addressees; Persian accordingly uses plural forms for “come” or “bring.”

Proposed derivations

Arabic grammatical tradition offers several analyses of halumma’s formation.

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad and some Basran grammarians relate it to attention-getting هَا plus لُمَّ from ل م م.

ل م م concerns collecting, gathering, and approaching.

On that analysis, the initial sense resembled “gather yourself toward us” or “come near,” with frequent use fusing the components.

Another analysis attributed to al-Farrāʾ combines هَلْ, understood as an urging expression, with a form of أَمَّ, meaning to intend or head toward something.

Other grammarians considered it an originally independent word, without requiring a compound precursor.

The disagreement leaves its historical origin unsettled.

Both compound analyses fit summoning and approach semantically; semantic plausibility alone does not prove historical composition.

Forqan therefore distinguishes the attested form هَلُمَّ, its grammatical function, and hypotheses about its origin.

Cataloging it under H L M likewise does not prove an independent triliteral root. Some Qur’anic morphological indexes associate it with ل م م, following a compound analysis.

That hypothesis does not transfer every meaning of ل م م into halumma.

3. Qur’anic forms and uses

هَلُمَّ occurs twice:

Al-Anʿām 6:150: هَلُمَّ شُهَدَاءَكُمُ requests witnesses.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18: هَلُمَّ إِلَيْنَا invites others toward the speakers.

Both express a summons or request, with different constructions.

In al-Anʿām, شُهَدَاءَكُمُ is a direct object: “bring your witnesses.”

In al-Aḥzāb, إِلَى plus نَا specifies the destination: “come to us.”

The two constructions clearly display the word’s established uses.

4. Halumma shuhadāʾakum — Calling for witnesses

Al-Anʿām 6:150

«قُلْ هَلُمَّ شُهَدَاءَكُمُ الَّذِينَ يَشْهَدُونَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ حَرَّمَ هَٰذَا فَإِنْ شَهِدُوا فَلَا تَشْهَدْ مَعَهُمْ وَلَا تَتَّبِعْ أَهْوَاءَ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا وَالَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْآخِرَةِ وَهُمْ بِرَبِّهِمْ يَعْدِلُونَ»

Qul halumma shuhadāʾakumu lladhīna yashhadūna anna llāha ḥarrama hādhā fa-in shahidū fa-lā tashhad maʿahum wa-lā tattabiʿ ahwāʾa lladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-lladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-l-ākhirati wa-hum bi-rabbihim yaʿdilūn.

“Say: Bring your witnesses who testify that The God has forbidden this. If they testify, do not testify with them; and do not follow the desires of those who denied Our signs, nor those who do not believe in the Hereafter and set equals alongside their Lord.”

هَلُمَّ here means “bring” or “produce.”

The request is not merely for the addressees to arrive, but for them to bring people who can testify to their claim.

شُهَدَاءَكُمُ is the object of that request.

What is being requested?

The preceding context concerns people attributing prohibitions on livestock and food to The God.

Al-Anʿām 6:143 and al-Anʿām 6:144 demand knowledge and evidence for such prohibitions.

Al-Anʿām 6:148 describes appeals to divine will to justify conduct and prohibitions, then asks whether they possess knowledge they can present.

Al-Anʿām 6:149 emphasizes the decisive divine argument.

Al-Anʿām 6:150 then commands هَلُمَّ شُهَدَاءَكُمُ.

Witnesses are requested where claimants must substantiate attributing a ruling to The God.

Lexically, halumma does not mean “prove.” It requests witnesses; the rest of the verse separately addresses whether their testimony is acceptable.

Does producing witnesses establish a claim?

After requesting witnesses, the verse tells Muhammad not to testify with them if they testify.

Their presence and assertions alone therefore do not establish a ruling’s divine origin.

Several people can repeat one claim while it still lacks valid evidence.

Halumma serves a demand for evidence here, but specifically means “bring your witnesses,” not the generic “produce proof.”

If someone alleges that The God prohibited a food for everyone, the basis of that attribution can be questioned. Several people repeating it do not by their number alone establish its divine source.

This follows from the al-Anʿām passage, not from halumma alone.

Summoning versus accepting

A linguistic distinction is also important.

هَلُمَّ requests presentation; it does not express acceptance of the speakers’ claims.

The Qur’an first calls for witnesses, then warns against joining their testimony.

Requesting a witness does not make all subsequent testimony necessarily valid.

Distinguishing a request to present a claim from accepting it is essential here.

5. Halumma ilaynā — Inviting people to join and withdraw from confrontation

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18

«قَدْ يَعْلَمُ اللَّهُ الْمُعَوِّقِينَ مِنْكُمْ وَالْقَائِلِينَ لِإِخْوَانِهِمْ هَلُمَّ إِلَيْنَا وَلَا يَأْتُونَ الْبَأْسَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا»

Qad yaʿlamu llāhu l-muʿawwiqīna minkum wa-l-qāʾilīna li-ikhwānihim halumma ilaynā wa-lā yaʾtūna l-baʾsa illā qalīlā.

“The God certainly knows those among you who hinder, and those who say to their brothers, ‘Come to us’; and they engage in battle only a little.”

Here هَلُمَّ means “come” or “approach us.”

إِلَيْنَا supplies the destination.

Unlike al-Anʿām 6:150, there is no direct object to bring; the addressees themselves are summoned.

Who are the speakers?

الْمُعَوِّقِينَ identifies people who obstruct or deter others’ participation.

The verse then describes people telling their brothers:

«هَلُمَّ إِلَيْنَا»

Halumma ilaynā.

That is, “come to us.”

The invitation occurs amid confrontation and danger.

In al-Aḥzāb 33:13, some urge others to return and some offer excuses to leave their position.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:16 addresses the futility of flight from death or killing.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18 describes those deterring others and participating little themselves.

This is thus more than an ordinary friendly invitation: amid danger, a group calls others toward itself, drawing them away from participation.

Does halumma mean fleeing?

No. Halumma itself does not mean fleeing.

In peaceful circumstances, هَلُمَّ إِلَيْنَا simply invites a friend to come.

In al-Aḥzāb 33:18 it is spoken by people avoiding confrontation while drawing others toward themselves.

Withdrawal from battle follows from their circumstances and behavior, not the word alone.

Translating it directly as “flee the war” substitutes a contextual conclusion for the expression’s meaning.

“Come to us” preserves the direct meaning; the invitation’s purpose belongs in the analysis.

Is summoning always positive?

Al-Anʿām 6:150 uses halumma to request witnesses.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18 places it in the speech of people deterring participation.

The word therefore has no fixed moral value.

A summons can serve hearing truth, presenting evidence, helping, or good action; it can also deter responsibility or recruit people into wrongdoing.

Its ethical value depends on purpose, speaker, circumstances, and consequences.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 further describes their agitation in fear and sharp speech after danger passes.

The issue extends beyond a single invitation to the conduct of those discouraging others in difficulty and responsibility.

That larger lesson should not be imposed on halumma’s lexical definition.

6. Comparison with nearby words and roots

ه ل م and أ ت ي — Calling someone to come and coming itself

أَتَى denotes coming or arriving.

Halumma requests coming or bringing something forward.

“He came” reports an action; “come” requests it.

Halumma resembles an imperative semantically but differs grammatically from an ordinary verb.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18 places it beside a verb from أ ت ي.

Some tell others to come to them while themselves coming to battle only a little.

Requesting others’ arrival differs from the speakers’ own conduct.

ه ل م and ع ل و — Invitations to come

تَعَالَ and plural تَعَالَوْا invite people to come.

After the witness request, al-Anʿām 6:151 invites people to come and hear what their Lord has prohibited.

That invitation concerns listening and learning.

In al-Anʿām 6:150, however, halumma takes شُهَدَاءَكُمُ directly and requests witnesses.

Halumma and taʿālaw overlap in invitation but are not always interchangeable in construction.

“Bring your witnesses” differs from calling the addressees themselves to come and listen.

ه ل م and ه ا ت — Producing and bringing

هَاتِ requests bringing or presenting something.

Al-Baqarah 2:111 and al-Naml 27:64 call on claimants to produce their proof.

This resembles هَلُمَّ شُهَدَاءَكُمُ in al-Anʿām 6:150.

Both demand something of the addressees: witnesses in one instance, proof in the other.

Similarity does not establish identical structure or origin.

Halumma can take a direct object or invite approach; hāti ordinarily requests bringing or presenting something.

ه ل م and د ع و — A summons and the act of calling

One branch of د ع و concerns calling a person.

Someone can invite another toward themselves, to an action, or to accept a statement.

Halumma is semantically related to such invitation.

It directly enacts a summons: the speaker asks someone to come or bring something.

دَعَا can instead report the act of calling in various grammatical constructions.

“He invited people” reports an invitation; “come” performs one.

This distinction clarifies halumma’s function.

ه ل م and ل م م — A possible connection with gathering

ل م م concerns gathering, approaching, and joining.

Some early grammarians analyze halumma as an attention particle plus imperative لُمَّ.

A connection between gathering and summoning is semantically conceivable.

It remains a historical hypothesis, not conclusive proof that the two lexical families are identical.

لَمًّا in al-Fajr 89:19 concerns gathering and consuming inheritance, unlike a request to come.

اللَّمَمَ in al-Najm 53:32 concerns sins and conduct.

Even accepting a derivation from ل م م would not import every derivative’s meaning into halumma.

An expression established as an independent summons must first be understood through its actual use.

7. Interpretive challenges and suggested translations

The central challenge is distinguishing the two Qur’anic constructions.

“Come” alone is insufficient in al-Anʿām 6:150, which requests witnesses.

“Bring your witnesses” or another object-based rendering is wrong for al-Aḥzāb 33:18, where people themselves are invited.

Al-Anʿām 6:150

For هَلُمَّ شُهَدَاءَكُمُ, suitable wording is:

“Bring your witnesses.”

Or:

“Produce your witnesses.”

“Bring” is natural and sufficient for direct translation.

“Proof” need not replace “witnesses”: the verse specifically requests people who testify.

The testimony’s validity belongs in contextual discussion.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18

For هَلُمَّ إِلَيْنَا, suitable wording is:

“Come toward us.”

Or, more naturally:

“Come to us.”

Both preserve the summons itself.

An explanation can say that the speakers discouraged participation in confrontation, without importing that conclusion into the short translation.

A grammatical point for translation

Both verses address multiple people; Persian therefore requires plural “come” and “bring.” English imperatives naturally serve either number.

The Arabic form remains unchanged because Hijazi usage employs one form for differing addressees.

Formal invariance should not erase differences in addressees or construction in translation.

8. Conclusion

هَلُمَّ summons, invites approach, or requests that a person or thing be brought forward.

Hijazi Arabic treats it as an imperative verb-name with one form across gender and number; other dialects inflect it like an imperative.

Its derivation is disputed: an attention particle plus a gathering verb, or a connection with heading toward something, are proposed. Neither analysis alone proves its history.

The Qur’an contains two clear uses.

Al-Anʿām 6:150 means “bring your witnesses,” requesting people who can testify to a claim.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18 means “come to us,” as a group summons others.

Forqan definition: هَلُمَّ is an imperative expression meaning “come” or “bring,” according to construction. With إِلَى it normally invites approach toward a destination; with a direct object it can request a person or thing.

It does not itself establish whether the invitation is right or wrong: one verse demands witnesses; the other quotes people deterring participation.

Lexical meaning must remain distinct from purpose, validity, and outcome. Whether the summons serves truth or obstructs responsibility follows from speaker, addressee, and context.
