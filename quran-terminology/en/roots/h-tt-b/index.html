The Qur’anic Root Ḥ Ṭ B

From firewood and kindling fire to Hell’s fuel and stirring hostility

The Arabic root ح ط ب concerns firewood and providing wood and branches for a fire. حَطَب denotes wood and branches gathered and prepared for burning. Within the same family, حَطَبَ means collecting firewood, as does احْتَطَبَ.

In everyday Arab life, the word concerned a familiar task: gathering branches and wood suitable for fuel, carrying them, and using them to light or maintain a fire. Figurative usages also developed from this image. Someone who carries provocative words between people and increases hostility is compared to someone supplying wood to kindle a fire.

The root ح ط ب occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in two verses. Al-Jinn 72:15 uses حَطَبًا for those described as firewood for Hell. Al-Masad 111:4 uses حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ of Abū Lahab’s wife, an expression with a direct meaning of carrying firewood that may also carry figurative force in Arabic.

Qur’anic forms: حَطَبًا in Al-Jinn 72:15 and الْحَطَبِ in Al-Masad 111:4.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the two nominal occurrences of ḥaṭab

Other forms, including حَطَبَ, يَحْطِبُ, احْتَطَبَ, حَاطِب, حَطَّاب, حَطِيب, and احْتِطَاب, are known in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

1. The root’s semantic core and material usages

Ḥaṭab: wood provided as fuel

Al-ʿAyn presents حَطَب as a familiar word and distinguishes حَطْب as a verbal noun from حَطَب as a noun.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: the distinction between ḥaṭb and ḥaṭab

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise defines ḥaṭab as wood or tree material prepared for kindling a fire.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for this family: الْوَقُود, material used to kindle and burn in a fire. He explains further usages in relation to this meaning.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: fuel and figurative extensions

The specific meaning of ḥaṭab must nevertheless be distinguished from the broader concept of waqūd. In direct usage, ḥaṭab concerns firewood and woody material suited to a fire, whereas waqūd may encompass any material used as fuel.

Dry branches, cut wood, and dry brush can be firewood. Not everything that burns is necessarily called ḥaṭab in ordinary Arabic.

The word’s important feature is material provided for a fire. The wood may not yet have been lit. Ḥaṭab therefore does not mean flame, heat, or the act of burning, but material capable of burning and gathered for that purpose.

Ḥaṭaba and iḥtaṭaba: gathering firewood

حَطَبَ يَحْطِبُ حَطْبًا means collecting firewood. احْتَطَبَ is likewise used for gathering it.

حَطَبْتُ لِفُلَانٍ means “I gathered firewood for someone.” حَطَبَنِي فُلَانٌ is also recorded for someone bringing a person firewood.

In these usages, ḥaṭab does not refer only to cutting down trees. Firewood can be gathered from fallen branches, dry brush, or wood already made available.

Iḥtiṭāb should therefore not always be restricted to felling trees. The main action is gathering firewood, although cutting branches or wood may be part of it.

Ḥāṭib and ḥaṭṭāb: someone who gathers firewood

حَاطِب is an active participle meaning a firewood gatherer. حَطَّاب is used for someone who does this frequently or makes a living gathering and selling firewood.

The work is not inherently disapproved of in Arabic. Gathering firewood was an ordinary task of life.

This matters for حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ: carrying wood alone is not a sin or blameworthy act. The reproach in Al-Masad must be understood from context and the expression’s possible usage, not from an assumption that gathering firewood is inherently disgraceful.

Ḥaṭīb and usages concerning plants

مَكَانٌ حَطِيبٌ describes a place with abundant firewood. أَرْضٌ حَطِيبَةٌ is likewise recorded for land rich in wood and branches suitable for fuel.

In descriptions of livestock, نَاقَةٌ مُحَاطِبَةٌ is used for a she-camel that eats dry branches and thorns. This concerns the same plant material that can be gathered as firewood.

Horticultural usages of the root are also recorded for cutting woody parts of grapevines: branches removed following annual growth.

These examples show connections with wood, branches, gathering, and cutting in everyday life. Not all these derivatives occur in the Qur’an.

Lisān al-ʿArab: wood gathering, thorn-eating camels, and pruning vines

2. Early linguistic witnesses and figurative usages

Gathering firewood in Arabic poetry

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

إِذَا مَا رَكِبْنَا قَالَ وِلْدَانُ أَهْلِنَا
تَعَالَوْا إِلَى أَنْ يَأْتِيَ الصَّيْدُ نَحْطِبِ

idhā mā rakibnā qāla wildānu ahlinā / taʿālaw ilā an yaʾtiya al-ṣaydu naḥṭibi

Meaning: Whenever we mounted, the children of our households said: Come, until the game arrives, let us gather firewood.

نَحْطِب here directly means gathering firewood. While others are away hunting, the children attend to providing wood for a fire.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah attributes this witness to Imruʾ al-Qays, and literary sources also transmit it. Its wording varies between editions, however: إِذَا مَا غَدَوْنَا and differences at the second hemistich’s end are reported. No transmitted wording should therefore be presented as the poet’s certain, exclusive text.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and variant wording

The witness matters because it presents firewood gathering as an ordinary activity of Arab life, serving cooking, warmth, and lighting a fire.

Ḥāṭib layl: indiscriminate gathering

A familiar Arabic expression is حَاطِبُ لَيْلٍ: someone gathering wood at night.

Its explanation is that a wood gatherer in darkness cannot properly see what he picks up. He may collect suitable and unsuitable wood, thorns, or even a dangerous creature with the firewood.

From this image, ḥāṭib layl is used for someone who mixes sound and unsound material in speech or judgment without examination.

The figurative usage rests on careless selection. Just as the nighttime gatherer picks things up in darkness, a heedless person may transmit helpful and harmful statements without weighing them.

The proverb does not mean that everyone gathering wood at night is careless. It is a literary image drawing on the difficulty of distinguishing objects in darkness.

Carrying firewood and stirring hostility

Another figurative usage concerns tale-bearing and creating hostility between people.

حَطَبَ فُلَانٌ بِفُلَانٍ is recorded for reporting against someone or engaging in provocative malicious talk about him.

Lexical sources also discuss an expression comparing the transmission of inflammatory words to carrying firewood. Someone who carries one person’s words to another to provoke hostility is pictured as supplying wood for the fire of discord.

A verse of uncertain attribution reads:

مِنَ الْبِيضِ لَمْ تُصْطَدْ عَلَى حَبْلِ لَأْمَةٍ
وَلَمْ تَمْشِ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ بِالْحَطَبِ الرَّطْبِ

mina al-bīḍi lam tuṣṭad ʿalā ḥabli laʾmatin / wa-lam tamshi bayna al-nāsi bi-l-ḥaṭabi al-raṭbi

Its sense is: Among fair-faced women, she has not fallen into the snare of baseness, nor carried wet firewood among people; that is, she has not engaged in tale-bearing to stir hostility.

حَطَبِ رَطْب literally means wet firewood, but in the poetic context it figuratively describes inflammatory words.

The witness is transmitted in Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab, but its attribution and precise date are uncertain. It can illustrate recorded literary usage, without being presented as certain pre-Islamic evidence. Lisān al-ʿArab also transmits عَلَى ظَهْرِ لَأْمَةٍ and بَيْنَ الْحَيِّ; the wording quoted here follows Maqāyīs al-Lughah.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the variant wet-firewood witness and tale-bearing

Ḥaṭab has not lost its basic meaning and become a direct word for speech. Rather, the image of wood feeding a fire is used for human conduct that intensifies hostility.

This distinction matters: a figurative expression is formed from a word, but does not necessarily replace its basic meaning in every usage.

3. Li-jahannama ḥaṭaban: Al-Jinn 72:15

وَأَمَّا الْقَاسِطُونَ فَكَانُوا لِجَهَنَّمَ حَطَبًا

wa-ammā al-qāsiṭūna fa-kānū li-jahannama ḥaṭaban

Translation: And as for the unjust, they will be firewood for Hell.

Al-Jinn 72:15

This is one of the root ح ط ب’s two Qur’anic occurrences.

حَطَبًا is an indefinite accusative noun, the predicate of كَانُوا. Those called الْقَاسِطُونَ are likened to, or presented as, firewood for Hell. The statement continues the speech of the jinn, so the group in this immediate context is the unjust among that community.

The preceding speech matters. In Al-Jinn 72:14, the speakers distinguish two groups: those who have submitted and those who have departed from justice.

Al-Jinn 72:15 states the second group’s outcome.

الْقَاسِطُونَ is from ق س ط and here refers to those who depart from justice and act unjustly. Its meaning must not be transferred to ḥaṭab: injustice characterizes the group, while being firewood depicts their outcome.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Jinn 72:15: the unjust in the jinn’s speech

Being firewood versus merely being present in fire

The verse uses فَكَانُوا لِجَهَنَّمَ حَطَبًا. They are described as more than those entering Hell: they are called firewood for it.

The distinction matters to the image. Firewood is not simply placed beside a fire; it burns in it and contributes to its kindling.

The verse thus offers a stronger image than being beside fire.

This does not establish that these beings physically turn into wood in the Hereafter. The verse uses familiar language of fire and firewood to state their outcome, without explaining its material nature.

The lexical meaning remains firewood. Applying it to these persons is understood through the image, not by redefining the word as “a sinful human” or “a being of Hell.”

Connections with waqūd and ḥaṣab of Hell

Al-Baqarah 2:24 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:6 describe a fire whose fuel is people and stones.

Both use waqūd, concerning the material through which a fire is kindled and burns.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98 likewise uses حَصَبُ جَهَنَّمَ for a group of people and their objects of worship.

These verses approach one another in their imagery of fire and what is cast into or burned in it, but use different words.

Ḥaṭab directly means firewood. Waqūd is broader, denoting combustible fuel. Ḥaṣab concerns the lexical field of casting and throwing; in a fire context, it can describe what is thrown into the fire or serves as its fuel.

Translating all these words as “fuel” may convey the general sense in some contexts, but reduces the differences between their lexical images.

“Firewood” better preserves the specific image of ḥaṭab in Al-Jinn 72:15.

4. Ḥammālata al-ḥaṭab: Al-Masad 111:4

وَامْرَأَتُهُ حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ

wa-mraʾatuhu ḥammālata al-ḥaṭabi

Translation: And his wife, the firewood carrier.

Al-Masad 111:4

This is the root’s second and final Qur’anic occurrence.

الْحَطَبِ is a definite noun in the genitive as the second term of the possessive construction with حَمَّالَةَ. حَمَّالَةَ itself is from ح م ل, carrying, not from ح ط ب.

The distinction matters: carrying and firewood are separate concepts combined in one expression.

حَمَّالَة has an intensive formation and can describe someone who carries something a great deal or repeatedly.

In the surah’s context, the expression reproaches Abū Lahab’s wife. To understand what conduct is reproached, however, the direct meaning and possible figurative readings must be examined separately.

First reading: physically carrying wood and thorns

On a direct reading, the verse describes a woman carrying firewood.

Early interpretive reports state that Abū Lahab’s wife gathered dry branches and thorns and put them in the Prophet’s path to injure him.

These reports seek to explain why carrying firewood becomes a reproach in this context.

If such conduct occurred, the reproach concerns not gathering wood itself, but deliberately using thorns and branches to harm others.

Al-Masad 111:4 does not explicitly mention thorns, the Prophet’s path, or a particular method of harm. These details come from reports outside the verse.

Carrying actual wood and thorns can therefore be presented as one historical explanation, without adding every detail to the lexical meaning of ḥaṭab.

Nor does ḥaṭab itself mean thorns. Dry thorns may be among materials gathered for fire, but the direct equivalent remains firewood.

Second reading: tale-bearing and inflaming hostility

As the lexical witnesses show, carrying firewood in Arabic can figuratively describe transmitting inflammatory speech and creating conditions for hostility.

On that basis, ḥammālat al-ḥaṭab may describe a woman who fuels hostility and harm through tale-bearing and provoking others.

In this reading, firewood depicts words and conduct that feed the fire of discord.

This interpretation does not rest solely on a historical narrative: the figurative connection between carrying firewood and malicious reporting is also recorded in Arabic dictionaries.

Its existence does not, however, establish that ḥaṭab in the Qur’an directly means tale-bearing.

Translating the verse as “and his wife, the tale-bearer” replaces the word firewood with the figurative meaning. The reader no longer sees the Qur’an’s original image.

The literal meaning is therefore better preserved in the translation, with the figurative reading explained separately.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Masad 111:4: thorn-carrying and tale-bearing reports

Third reading: firewood in the scene of punishment

Al-Masad 111:3 describes a blazing fire in which Abū Lahab will burn. Al-Masad 111:4 introduces his wife, and Al-Masad 111:5 speaks of a rope around her neck.

This continuity permits another reading: ḥammālat al-ḥaṭab may be part of the punishment scene, a woman bearing firewood with a rope around her neck in that scene of fire.

The expression would then concern her fate in the Hereafter, rather than only reporting a worldly activity.

This possibility can be examined particularly in light of Al-Jinn 72:15, where the unjust referred to in the jinn’s speech are also presented as firewood for Hell.

The similarity must not make the two images identical. In Al-Jinn 72:15, the persons themselves are called ḥaṭab. In Al-Masad 111:4, the woman is called ḥammālat al-ḥaṭab: someone carrying firewood.

The verse also does not specify precisely what the wood being carried is, or that the image belongs exclusively to the Hereafter.

Connecting it with the punishment scene is therefore a reading to examine, whose details should not be added to the text without clear evidence.

Ibn Kathīr: carrying firewood in the scene of punishment

The role of ḥammālata and variant readings

In the reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, حَمَّالَةَ is accusative.

One important grammatical explanation is an accusative of disparagement: a description introduced to reproach someone.

Other readings have the nominative حَمَّالَةُ, which may describe امْرَأَتُهُ or serve as the sentence’s predicate.

The grammatical variation gives the expression different positions within the sentence, without changing the basic meaning of ḥaṭab.

In either case, the firewood remains firewood and ḥammālah concerns carrying. The main difference is how the person is introduced and reproached within the sentence.

The case ending alone therefore cannot decide between actual wood carrying, a figure for tale-bearing, or an image of the Hereafter. These readings must be assessed through the full context and linguistic evidence.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn: readings and grammatical roles of ḥammālah

The image and personal responsibility

Al-Masad speaks of Abū Lahab and his wife, but a family relationship alone cannot establish someone’s ethical responsibility.

The independent description ḥammālat al-ḥaṭab in Al-Masad 111:4 identifies the woman through a distinct characteristic or conduct. Her outcome should therefore not be attributed merely to being Abū Lahab’s wife.

The verse does not by itself give a complete account of her conduct, however. Historical and figurative readings seek to clarify the description, but none should be imposed as certain and exclusive without examination.

5. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ Ṭ B (ḥaṭab) and W Q D (waqada)

Ḥaṭab directly denotes firewood: wood and branches prepared for a fire.

Waqūd denotes material through which fire is kindled and fed. Within the same family, أَوْقَدَ means kindling a fire.

Firewood used to light a fire can thus be an instance of waqūd, but waqūd is not restricted to firewood.

This matters in verses about Hell. لِجَهَنَّمَ حَطَبًا in Al-Jinn 72:15 and وَقُودُهَا النَّاسُ وَالْحِجَارَةُ in Al-Baqarah 2:24 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:6 share the field of fire and fuel, but belong to different roots.

Study of W Q D

Ḥ Ṭ B (ḥaṭab) and Ḥ Ṣ B (ḥaṣab)

ح ص ب concerns pebbles, throwing, and in some usages casting something into a fire.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98, حَصَبُ جَهَنَّمَ describes those cast into Hell and placed in the position of its fuel.

Ḥaṭab, by contrast, belongs to the family of firewood and providing material for fire.

The two can approach one another in an afterlife context, but should not be conflated simply because ḥaṣab and ḥaṭab look similar. Their different middle letters and familiar usages must remain distinct.

Study of Ḥ Ṣ B

Ḥ Ṭ B (ḥaṭab) and Ḥ Ṭ M (ḥaṭama)

Ḥaṭm concerns breaking and crushing. The derivative حُطَام denotes dry, broken plant debris.

In Al-Zumar 39:21, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:65, and Al-Ḥadīd 57:20, ḥuṭām concerns plants after withering or destruction.

Such dry fragments may be combustible, but that does not make ḥuṭām and ḥaṭab synonyms.

Ḥuṭām emphasizes fragmentation and disintegration; ḥaṭab emphasizes woody material provided for a fire.

الْحُطَمَة in Al-Humazah 104:4 and Al-Humazah 104:5 is likewise from ح ط م, not ح ط ب.

Although ḥuṭamah occurs in the context of afterlife fire, it therefore cannot be translated as firewood.

Study of Ḥ Ṭ M

Ḥ Ṭ B (ḥaṭab) and Ḥ M L (ḥamala)

In Al-Masad 111:4, حَمَّالَةَ and الْحَطَبِ are two words with independent roots.

Ḥammālah is from ح م ل and means carrying frequently or in abundance. Ḥaṭab is from ح ط ب and means firewood.

Carrying a load therefore does not belong to the semantic core of ح ط ب itself, even though gathering firewood usually involves carrying it in practice.

This prevents characteristics of a Qur’anic phrase from being incorrectly attributed to one root’s semantic core.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

حَطَبًا in Al-Jinn 72:15 can be translated as firewood:

“And as for the unjust, they will be firewood for Hell.”

This preserves the verse’s original image. “Fuel for Hell” conveys the general sense, but shows less clearly the difference between firewood and the broader concept of waqūd.

For Al-Masad 111:4, the base translation should preserve the phrase’s direct meaning:

“And his wife, the firewood carrier.”

Ḥammālah and ḥaṭab are rendered through their two concepts, a carrier and firewood.

The possibility that she carried thorns to harm others, or inflamed hostility through tale-bearing, should be discussed in a separate explanation.

Connecting the image to the fire of punishment likewise concerns the surah’s context and should not replace the word’s direct meaning.

Among non-Qur’anic forms, حَاطِب and حَطَّاب mean “firewood gatherer” or “wood carrier,” احْتَطَبَ means “gathered firewood,” and مَكَانٌ حَطِيبٌ means “a place rich in firewood.”

The figurative ḥāṭib layl can be explained as someone who speaks indiscriminately or mixes things without judgment. Retaining the wood-gathering image in the explanation helps clarify the expression’s basis.

Conclusion

The Arabic root ح ط ب concerns firewood and gathering wood and branches for a fire. Ḥaṭab names material prepared for burning; the verb ḥaṭaba and iḥtaṭaba express gathering it, while ḥāṭib and ḥaṭṭāb describe people who do that work.

Literary figurative usages developed from the same image. Ḥāṭib layl describes someone who indiscriminately mixes sound and unsound material. Carrying firewood can also depict tale-bearing and fueling hostility. These figurative meanings should not alter the root’s basic definition.

Ḥaṭab occurs only twice in the Qur’an. Al-Jinn 72:15 presents unjust members of the jinn as firewood for Hell. Al-Masad 111:4 uses ḥammālat al-ḥaṭab of Abū Lahab’s wife. Its direct meaning is carrying firewood; figurative and afterlife readings may also be examined through linguistic evidence and the surah’s context.

Brief root meaning: The root’s semantic core is firewood and providing woody material to kindle a fire. From this tangible usage arise the image of persons as firewood and the comparison of inflammatory conduct to providing fuel for hostility’s fire.

Tale-bearing is not the fixed meaning of ḥaṭab, nor is a sinner the lexical meaning of firewood. These concepts are understood in specific usages through context.

Preserving firewood as the base meaning in Qur’anic translation lets the reader see the verse’s original image. The root explanation can then distinguish direct usage, literary figure, and interpretive possibility, without allowing any to replace another without evidence.
