Postponing and moving away: from granting time and shifting the sacred months to a staff used for driving onward

1. Introducing the root and its semantic core

Arabic N S ʾ concerns postponing, putting back, granting time, and removing something from its present time or position.

Nasaʾa is used when an action, a due date, or something’s placement is deferred to another time or position. Ansaʾa likewise means postponing in many uses.

For example, nasaʾa and nasīʾah can concern postponing repayment of a debt. Intasaʾa can describe moving away from someone. Nasaʾa also has an established use for driving an animal onward with a staff.

Two related directions occur in this family: putting something back in time and moving it away or driving it forward in space.

Not every use necessarily includes both. Deferring a debt does not require moving an object in space; driving an animal with a staff does not necessarily postpone an appointment.

Other usages include adding water to milk, an animal beginning to fatten, and delayed menstruation at the start of pregnancy. Some have understandable connections with delay or increase, but historical evidence is needed before deriving them all from a single certain meaning.

Two principal forms occur in the Qur’an: «النَّسِيء» concerning changes to the sacred months, and «مِنْسَأَتَهُ» in Solomon’s story.

Another recognized reading of Al-Baqarah 2:106 also has the verb «نَنْسَأْهَا» from this root. It must be studied separately from Ḥafṣ’s «نُنْسِهَا», since they belong to different roots.

Qur’anic forms

In Ḥafṣ’s reading, N S ʾ occurs twice, both as nouns:

  • النَّسِيءُ: postponing or shifting sacred-month observance; Al-Tawbah 9:37.
  • مِنْسَأَتَهُ: his staff; Sabaʾ 34:14.

Ibn Kathīr and Abū ʿAmr have another form in Al-Baqarah 2:106:

  • نَنْسَأْهَا: We postpone it.

The count therefore depends on the reading examined. A Ḥafṣ index records two instances; readings with «نَنْسَأْهَا» add a verbal occurrence to this family.

Other established forms, such as «نَسْء», «نُسْأَة», «نَسِيئَة», «نَاسِئ», «أَنْسَأَ», «نَسَّأَ», «اِنْتَسَأَ», and «اِسْتَنْسَأَ», help explain Arabic usage but do not occur as independent derivatives of this root in Ḥafṣ’s Qur’anic reading.

2. Early Arabic usage and semantic branches

Postponing and granting time

One clear use of N S ʾ is putting back the time for an action.

Arabic says:

نَسَأْتُ الشَّيْءَ

nasaʾtu al-shayʾa

Meaning: “I postponed that thing.”

Also:

أَنْسَأْتُهُ

ansaʾtuhu

Meaning: “I deferred it or granted it more time.”

The two verbs nasaʾa and ansaʾa overlap in this sense in Arabic lexical usage.

A different verbal form therefore does not always mean a definite difference in the kind or intensity of delay.

What matters is moving a due date or putting back the realization of something.

For example, someone may postpone repayment by one month. The debt remains; its payment date changes.

Deferral is therefore different from cancelling or forgiving a debt. The debt may remain in force with only its due date altered.

This distinction also matters for Qur’anic nasīʾ, which concerns postponing and shifting sacred-month observance rather than simply abolishing every sacred month.

Nasīʾah: debt and deferred transactions

Arabic nasīʾah concerns a transaction or payment deferred to a later date.

Bayʿ al-nasīʾah describes a sale whose price or another obligation is payable later.

Also:

اِسْتَنْسَأَهُ الدَّيْنَ

istansaʾahu al-dayna

Meaning: “He asked him for more time to repay the debt.”

The basic meaning here is deferral itself.

Nasīʾah alone does not establish an increased debt, stipulated interest, or an unlawful transaction.

A contract may allow later payment without imposing an additional sum because of the delay.

Later jurisprudence uses “ribā al-nasīʾah” for certain forms of deferred transactions and debts. That technical term should not determine the general definition of N S ʾ.

Lexically, nasīʾah concerns delay and a due date. Whether a transaction is lawful is a separate question requiring the rules and evidence applicable to it.

Delay and moving away

Arabic records intasaʾa for moving back or away.

«اِنْتَسَأَ عَنْهُ» means “he moved away from him.”

«أَنْسَأَهُ عَنْهُ» can likewise concern moving someone or something away from another.

This clarifies the root’s spatial direction.

In temporal delay, a due date moves farther from the present. In spatial distancing, a person or object moves away from a location or another person.

These uses can be connected semantically, without proving one the historical origin of the other.

Nor does Arabic express every movement away through N S ʾ. N ʾ Y and B ʿ D also concern distance.

Delay and removal are established in N S ʾ, but are not equivalents for every expression of moving away.

Driving an animal with a staff

Arabic records «نَسَأَ الْبَعِيرَ» for driving a camel onward.

«نَسَأَ الدَّابَّةَ» likewise concerns moving or driving an animal.

A staff may be used to urge the animal forward.

The name minsaʾah for a staff is connected with this field, especially a shepherd’s or another person’s staff for driving animals.

The animal’s movement can involve leaving its previous position.

The established noun minsaʾah, however, does not mean temporal delay: it names a real implement.

In Sabaʾ 34:14 it therefore cannot be translated as an “instrument of delay” or a “device for postponing time.”

Its appropriate meaning in that verse is simply a staff.

A poetic witness from al-Aʿshā: driving and moving

A verse by the pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿshā says:

وَمَا أُمُّ خِشْفٍ بِالْعُلَايَةِ شَادِنٍ
تُنْسِئُ فِي بَرْدِ الظِّلَالِ غَزَالَهَا

wa-mā ummu khishfin bi-al-ʿulāyati shādinin / tunsiʾu fī bardi al-ẓilāli ghazālahā

A rendering of the sense:

“Nor a doe upon that height, urging her fawn onward in the cool shade…”

«تُنْسِئُ» here concerns moving or driving forward.

This witness matters because it shows the root’s verbal use for animal behavior and moving another onward.

Unstated details should not be invented. The verse alone does not establish that the doe strikes her fawn or uses a physical implement.

Nor should this usage become delayed menstruation, debt, or sacred months. The object and context determine the appropriate sense.

Minsaʾah: a staff in everyday life

Arabic records minsaʾah for a staff, particularly a fairly stout one used to drive animals or assist movement.

Arabic poetic tradition transmits the following verse without a secure attribution to a specific poet:

إِذَا دَبَبْتَ عَلَى الْمِنْسَاةِ مِنْ هَرَمٍ
فَقَدْ تَبَاعَدَ عَنْكَ اللَّهْوُ وَالْغَزَلُ

idhā dababta ʿalā al-minsāti min haramin / fa-qad tabāʿada ʿanka al-lahwu wa-al-ghazalu

Translation:

“When in old age you walk slowly, leaning on a staff, youthful amusement and courtship have grown distant from you.”

The minsaʾah here is a staff supporting an elderly person.

Its use is therefore not confined to driving camels and sheep.

The poem has «الْمِنْسَاة» without hamzah. Arabic records both this form and «الْمِنْسَأَة» with hamzah.

The date of composition is unclear; it should not be presented unqualifiedly as certain pre-Islamic poetry.

The witness illuminates the staff’s literary and linguistic use, rather than establishing the certain historical origin of its name.

Other branches: increase, initial fattening, and pregnancy

Arabic lexical usage also records N S ʾ for adding water to milk.

«نَسَأَ اللَّبَنَ» can mean adding water to milk.

This increases the volume of liquid.

Yet increasing volume should not be made the core meaning of every derivative. It does not adequately explain due dates, distance, or a staff.

«نُسِئَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ» describes a woman whose menstruation is later than usual and for whom pregnancy is suspected.

This is clearly connected with a delayed expected time.

Nasʾ is also recorded for animals beginning to fatten and part of their coat growing back.

Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī says:

بِهَا أَبَلَتْ شَهْرَيْ رَبِيعٍ كِلَيْهِمَا
فَقَدْ مَارَ فِيهَا نَسْؤُهَا وَاقْتِرَارُهَا

bihā abalat shahray rabīʿin kilayhimā / fa-qad māra fīhā nasʾuhā wa-iqtirāruhā

A rendering of the sense:

“There she grazed through both months of spring, and signs of the beginning and fullness of fattening appeared in her.”

This shows nasʾ describing an animal’s physical condition.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods; the date of this verse is uncertain.

This usage does not establish weight gain or pregnancy as the original meaning of every form in the family.

It is better to treat delay and moving away as the family’s principal axis and explain increase and bodily condition cautiously as separate uses.

3. Qur’anic nasīʾ: shifting sacred-month observance

Al-Tawbah 9:37

إِنَّمَا النَّسِيءُ زِيَادَةٌ فِي الْكُفْرِ يُضَلُّ بِهِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا يُحِلُّونَهُ عَامًا وَيُحَرِّمُونَهُ عَامًا لِيُوَاطِئُوا عِدَّةَ مَا حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ فَيُحِلُّوا مَا حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ زُيِّنَ لَهُمْ سُوءُ أَعْمَالِهِمْ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْكَافِرِينَ

innamā al-nasīʾu ziyādatun fī al-kufri yuḍallu bihi alladhīna kafarū yuḥillūnahu ʿāman wa-yuḥarrimūnahu ʿāman li-yuwāṭiʾū ʿiddata mā ḥarrama allāhu fa-yuḥillū mā ḥarrama allāhu zuyyina lahum sūʾu aʿmālihim wa-allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-kāfirīna

Translation:

“Nasīʾ is only an increase in disbelief; those who disbelieve are led astray by it. They permit it one year and forbid it another to match the number of what The God has made sacred, thus permitting what The God has forbidden. Their evil deeds have been made attractive to them, and The God does not guide the disbelieving people.”

Al-Tawbah 9:37

This is the principal Qur’anic occurrence of N S ʾ.

To understand nasīʾ, first consider the preceding verse.

A. Al-Tawbah 9:36: twelve months, four sacred

إِنَّ عِدَّةَ الشُّهُورِ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ اثْنَا عَشَرَ شَهْرًا فِي كِتَابِ اللَّهِ يَوْمَ خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ مِنْهَا أَرْبَعَةٌ حُرُمٌ ذَٰلِكَ الدِّينُ الْقَيِّمُ فَلَا تَظْلِمُوا فِيهِنَّ أَنْفُسَكُمْ

inna ʿiddata al-shuhūri ʿinda allāhi ithnā ʿashara shahran fī kitābi allāhi yawma khalaqa al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa minhā arbaʿatun ḥurumun dhālika al-dīnu al-qayyimu fa-lā taẓlimū fīhinna anfusakum

Translation:

“The number of months with The God is twelve, in the ordinance of The God from the day He created the heavens and earth; four of them are sacred. That is the upright religion, so do not wrong yourselves in them.”

Al-Tawbah 9:36, opening part of the verse

This identifies twelve months, four of them sacred.

The next verse describes nasīʾ as an increase in disbelief.

The connection shows that nasīʾ concerns arranging and altering the status of the sacred months.

Verse 37 does not speak only of changing the number of months; it explicitly mentions making them permitted or forbidden.

Nasīʾ should therefore not be treated merely as calendar arithmetic, ignoring the shift in sacred-month observance.

B. The lexical meaning of «النَّسِيء»

Arabic nasīʾ can denote postponing or something postponed.

Two familiar morphological analyses exist.

One treats it as a noun or verbal noun for the act of delay itself.

The other treats nasīʾ on the faʿīl pattern with passive meaning: something postponed.

It can then refer to a month whose sacred status or position has been deferred.

Both analyses are linguistically defensible.

In this construction, however, the act of postponing and shifting sacred observance seems broader and more fitting: the verse condemns a practice of permitting a month one year and forbidding it another, rather than merely naming a particular month.

“Postponing and shifting sacred-month observance” therefore suits an explanation of nasīʾ.

The base translation may retain nasīʾ and explain it separately: a single equivalent such as “delay” does not convey all the historical and normative context.

C. Nasīʾ in pre-Islamic Arab practice

Historical and lexical accounts describe groups moving the sacred observance of some months from their recognized time to other months.

One familiar form declared Muḥarram, a sacred month, permitted for a year and transferred its sacred status to Ṣafar.

Another year might again treat Muḥarram as sacred and Ṣafar as permitted.

This allowed warfare or raiding in a previously sacred month while apparently preserving four sacred months.

Early accounts describe people from Kinānah announcing the change.

They mention al-Qalammas and people authorized to announce months’ permitted or sacred status at annual gatherings.

The first individual’s identity, the succession, and the calendrical details are disputed.

The historical accounts should not all be collapsed into one certain, undisputed narrative.

What the verse itself clearly states is that people changed the months’ status to permit what The God had forbidden.

D. A poetic witness concerning shifts in the months

A verse attributed to ʿUmayr ibn Qays ibn Jidhl al-Ṭiʿān of Kinānah says:

أَلَسْنَا النَّاسِئِينَ عَلَى مَعَدٍّ
شُهُورَ الْحِلِّ نَجْعَلُهَا حَرَامَا

alasnā al-nāsiʾīna ʿalā maʿaddin / shuhūra al-ḥilli najʿaluhā ḥarāmā

Translation:

“Are we not those who impose nasīʾ upon Maʿadd’s tribes, making permitted months sacred?”

The poet boasts of his group’s authority to determine or change the months’ status.

«النَّاسِئِينَ» refers to people who carried out nasʾ or nasīʾ.

«شُهُورَ الْحِلِّ» concerns months ordinarily treated as permitted whose status was then changed.

This witness matters historically because it relates nasīʾ to actually shifting the months’ status.

Sources transmit slight differences at the verse’s beginning, including alsanā and wa-naḥnu.

Some verses about nasīʾ are also attributed to other poets.

The poem should therefore be presented with its familiar attribution and appropriate confidence, rather than as undisputed evidence for every detail of the Arab calendar.

E. «يُحِلُّونَهُ عَامًا وَيُحَرِّمُونَهُ عَامًا»

This phrase is decisive:

“They permit it one year and forbid it another.”

The verbs yuḥillūnahu and yuḥarrimūnahu belong to different root families and concern permitting and forbidding.

These acts are not the lexical meaning of nasīʾ itself; they show what postponing did in this setting.

By shifting sacred observance, people changed a month’s status between years.

The Qur’an consequently calls nasīʾ an increase in disbelief.

The essential issue is using a shift in time to alter what is permitted and forbidden.

This is more than a difference in month names or ways of counting days.

F. «لِيُوَاطِئُوا عِدَّةَ مَا حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ»

The verse gives the purpose as:

“To match the number of what The God has made sacred.”

ʿIddah here means number.

Li-yuwāṭiʾū belongs to W Ṭ ʾ and means making correspond or match in this construction.

They sought to retain the ordained number of sacred months.

For example, removing one month’s sacred status could be compensated by assigning it to another, leaving four sacred months in appearance.

Preserving the number, however, does not necessarily preserve the divine ruling.

If The God has made four particular months sacred, transferring that status among months while retaining the number four does not preserve the same ruling.

This distinction is central to the verse’s reasoning.

They could apparently claim that the number had not changed while actually permitting what was forbidden.

Preserving a ruling’s outward form while changing its substance does not necessarily constitute fidelity to that ruling.

G. Does nasīʾ mean adding a thirteenth month?

Some historical accounts connect nasīʾ with inserting a month or shifting calendrical cycles.

Such explanations describe adjustments that could shift ritual timing or month names between years.

Those reports require historical examination. Al-Tawbah 9:37 does not directly say that a thirteenth month was inserted every year.

The explicit text concerns alternating permitted and forbidden status while attempting to preserve the number of sacred months.

Nasīʾ should therefore not simply become “the thirteenth month” in the base translation.

Nor can this verse alone establish every detail of Arab calendar history.

Shifting sacred-month observance and adjusting a calendar may be historically related, but they are conceptually distinct.

Qur’anic study should first preserve the explicit statement about changing sacred status, then examine historical possibilities for how it was done.

H. «زِيَادَةٌ فِي الْكُفْرِ»: an added month or increased disbelief?

The verse begins:

إِنَّمَا النَّسِيءُ زِيَادَةٌ فِي الْكُفْرِ

innamā al-nasīʾu ziyādatun fī al-kufri

Meaning:

“Nasīʾ is only an increase in disbelief.”

Ziyādah belongs to Z Y D.

The increase in the sentence is thus connected with disbelief.

It should not silently be turned into adding a month to the year.

Historical accounts may discuss increasing the number of calendar months, but this sentence speaks of increased disbelief.

This grammatical and semantic distinction must be preserved.

The verse explains the judgment: shifting sacred status permitted what The God had forbidden.

The increase in disbelief thus concerns deliberately altering divine limits, rather than merely performing a calculation of time.

4. Qur’anic minsaʾah: Solomon’s staff

Sabaʾ 34:14

فَلَمَّا قَضَيْنَا عَلَيْهِ الْمَوْتَ مَا دَلَّهُمْ عَلَىٰ مَوْتِهِ إِلَّا دَابَّةُ الْأَرْضِ تَأْكُلُ مِنْسَأَتَهُ فَلَمَّا خَرَّ تَبَيَّنَتِ الْجِنُّ أَنْ لَوْ كَانُوا يَعْلَمُونَ الْغَيْبَ مَا لَبِثُوا فِي الْعَذَابِ الْمُهِينِ

fa-lammā qaḍaynā ʿalayhi al-mawta mā dallahum ʿalā mawtihi illā dābbatu al-arḍi taʾkulu minsaʾatahu fa-lammā kharra tabayyanati al-jinnu an law kānū yaʿlamūna al-ghayba mā labithū fī al-ʿadhābi al-muhīni

Translation:

“When We decreed death for him, nothing informed them of his death except a creature of the earth eating his staff. When he fell, it became clear that, had the jinn known the unseen, they would not have remained in the humiliating suffering.”

Sabaʾ 34:14

This is the root’s second principal Qur’anic occurrence.

Unlike Al-Tawbah, this concerns neither temporal delay nor sacred months, but a physical object called minsaʾah.

A. The lexical meaning of minsaʾah

Minsaʾah means a staff in Arabic.

It follows the mifʿalah pattern, often used for instruments.

Its familiar Arabic use concerns a staff for driving animals onward.

Its semantic relationship to «نَسَأَ الدَّابَّةَ» is clear.

That does not mean every minsaʾah was made solely for driving animals or performs that action in every use.

Arabic poetry also uses it for a staff supporting an elderly person.

The verse does not say Solomon was driving an animal with it.

It says a creature of the earth was eating his staff and Solomon then fell.

“Staff” is therefore a sufficient, natural translation.

B. The connection with Solomon’s story

The verses before Sabaʾ 34:14 describe powers and resources placed at Solomon’s disposal.

Sabaʾ 34:12 describes the obedient wind and jinn working by their Lord’s command.

Sabaʾ 34:13 describes their activities in making various structures and objects.

Verse 14 then reports Solomon’s death and its discovery.

This continuity matters: the subject extends beyond a staff breaking.

After describing his rule’s powers and resources, the verse relates an event revealing the limits of the jinn’s knowledge.

Even the beings working in Solomon’s service were unaware of his death.

Yet minsaʾah does not mean power, rule, or knowledge of the unseen.

The staff plays a part in the event. The message about the jinn’s ignorance follows from the rest of the verse.

C. «دَابَّةُ الْأَرْضِ تَأْكُلُ مِنْسَأَتَهُ»

In the full sentence, «دَابَّةُ الْأَرْضِ» is the subject of dalla following the exception. The subject of taʾkulu is an implicit pronoun referring to that creature, while «مِنْسَأَتَهُ» is its object.

Lexical and exegetical explanations commonly identify the creature as araḍah, an insect that eats and damages wood.

“Termite” is a familiar equivalent.

The verse itself, however, uses the general expression dābbat al-arḍ rather than araḍah.

“Termite” can explain a possible referent, but the base translation should preferably preserve “a creature of the earth.”

The verse does not state the staff’s wood, size, or how long the creature ate it.

What is clear is that eating the staff helped reveal Solomon’s death.

D. «فَلَمَّا خَرَّ»: Solomon falling

The verse says:

فَلَمَّا خَرَّ

fa-lammā kharra

Meaning:

“When he fell.”

Kharra belongs to KH R R and means falling down.

Context identifies Solomon as its referent.

This suggests the staff helped support him, and damage to it resulted in his fall.

But the verse does not explicitly state how long he had been leaning on it or his exact posture.

Some exegetical accounts give one year for his death remaining concealed.

Those details are not stated in the Qur’anic text and should not become the meaning of minsaʾah or part of the verse translation.

Nor does kharra mean that the staff broke. Breaking or loss of strength can be inferred from the events’ connection, but the verb concerns falling.

E. The limits of the jinn’s knowledge

The verse ends:

أَنْ لَوْ كَانُوا يَعْلَمُونَ الْغَيْبَ مَا لَبِثُوا فِي الْعَذَابِ الْمُهِينِ

an law kānū yaʿlamūna al-ghayba mā labithū fī al-ʿadhābi al-muhīni

Meaning:

“Had they known the unseen, they would not have remained in the humiliating suffering.”

This concerns what the jinn knew and did not know.

They remained unaware of Solomon’s death, and that ignorance affected the continuation of their situation.

Their special powers should therefore not be equated with unlimited knowledge of the unseen.

Nor does possessing abilities different from humans necessarily mean that a being knows every hidden matter.

This message follows from the full narrative, rather than the lexical meaning of minsaʾah.

Minsaʾah remains the name of a staff; the revelation of limited jinn knowledge results from the event narrated.

F. Does the staff’s name mean delaying death?

Because minsaʾah belongs to N S ʾ, someone might connect Solomon’s staff with postponing death or delaying knowledge of death.

This does not have sufficient lexical support.

Arabic already knew minsaʾah as a staff, connected with driving animals onward.

The staff helps reveal Solomon’s death in the verse, but that narrative role does not establish the historical meaning of its name.

It cannot be said that the staff was called minsaʾah because it postponed Solomon’s death.

Death is decreed and has occurred at the verse’s opening. What others had not discovered was that he had died.

Death, others’ awareness of it, and the staff’s lexical name must therefore be distinguished.

5. Variant readings of minsaʾah and nansaʾhā

«مِنْسَأَتَهُ» and «مِنْسَاتَهُ»

Recognized readings of Sabaʾ 34:14 have forms with and without hamzah.

Ḥafṣ’s reading has hamzah:

مِنْسَأَتَهُ

minsaʾatahu

Other readings also have the form without hamzah:

مِنْسَاتَهُ

minsātahu

Weakening or omitting hamzah is a familiar pronunciation difference in some Arabic words.

The two forms can therefore refer to the same object.

Both mean a staff.

Omitting hamzah here does not introduce a different object or change Solomon’s story.

Nor does the form without hamzah immediately establish a derivation from N S Y, forgetting.

Lexical explanations recognize it as another form of minsaʾah.

Reports connecting minsaʾah with Ethiopic

Some exegetical accounts also identify minsaʾah as a word for a staff in Ethiopic.

Such a report alone does not establish borrowing from that language into Arabic.

Historical borrowing requires examining the recorded form, its date, and the route of transmission.

Arabic also has the N S ʾ family and its verb for driving with a staff.

One exegetical report therefore cannot settle an Arabic or non-Arabic origin.

The established Arabic meaning “staff” is sufficient for understanding the verse here.

Al-Baqarah 2:106: «نُنْسِهَا» or «نَنْسَأْهَا»?

Ḥafṣ’s reading says:

مَا نَنْسَخْ مِنْ آيَةٍ أَوْ نُنْسِهَا نَأْتِ بِخَيْرٍ مِنْهَا أَوْ مِثْلِهَا

mā nansakh min āyatin aw nunsihā naʾti bi-khayrin minhā aw mithlihā

A rendering close to the wording:

“Whatever sign We abrogate or cause to be forgotten, We bring one better than it or like it.”

Al-Baqarah 2:106, opening part of the verse

Ibn Kathīr and Abū ʿAmr instead read:

أَوْ نَنْسَأْهَا

aw nansaʾhā

Meaning:

“Or We postpone it.”

This difference matters.

Nunsihā belongs to N S Y and Form IV. On the forgetting interpretation, it means causing the sign to be forgotten. Leaving it aside is another interpretation discussed.

Nansaʾhā, however, belongs to N S ʾ and means postponing.

They should not be presented as one derivative from one root.

The hamzah reading concerns postponing the sign or something ordained concerning it.

Whether this concerns its revelation, replacement, or application of a ruling is an interpretive question not resolved by the verb alone.

Lexically, nansaʾhā means “We postpone it.”

Nunsihā, by contrast, belongs to the forgetting family.

Nansakh belongs to N S KH and should not be merged with N S ʾ because they occur in the same verse.

This shows why unvocalized spelling alone is insufficient for root study. Precise form, hamzah, vowels, and the reading tradition must be considered together.

6. Comparing related roots

N S ʾ (نَسَأَ) and ʾ KH R (أَخَّرَ): postponing

The closest comparison for N S ʾ’s principal branch is ʾ KH R.

Both can concern putting something back and deferring it to a later time.

The Qur’an uses ʾ KH R for delaying appointments, granting time, and other events.

N S ʾ in Al-Tawbah, however, concerns a particular practice of shifting sacred-month observance.

For minsaʾah, delay alone is insufficient as a translation because the word names a staff.

Closeness in the postponing sense does not make every derivative overlap.

Study of ʾ KH R

N S ʾ (نَسَأَ) and N S Y (نَسِيَ): delay and forgetting

These roots are close in spelling but importantly different in meaning.

N S ʾ ends in hamzah and principally concerns postponing and moving away.

N S Y ends in yāʾ and concerns forgetting.

This distinction matters especially in Al-Baqarah 2:106, where familiar readings belong to different roots.

Some Arabic forms without hamzah may result from its weakening. This does not justify merging every similar form in the two families.

Nasīʾ in Al-Tawbah 9:37 therefore does not mean forgetting the months.

Nor does minsaʾah in Sabaʾ 34:14 mean something forgotten: it names a staff.

Study of N S Y

N S ʾ (نَسَأَ) and N ʾ Y (نَأَىٰ): moving away

N ʾ Y concerns moving away from a person or thing.

Arabic intasaʾa can likewise concern moving back or away.

The roots overlap in some uses in this respect.

Qur’anic N ʾ Y more often concerns distancing oneself from something or withdrawing in conduct.

N S ʾ, by contrast, can principally put back an action’s timing or alter a due date.

Sharing a notion of distance does not make the families fully synonymous.

Study of N ʾ Y

N S ʾ (نَسَأَ) and ʾ J L (أَجَل): deferral and a due date

Ajal names an appointed time or the end of a specified period.

N S ʾ concerns the act of putting a due date back.

For example, ajal is a debt’s due date; if that date is moved back, nasaʾa can express the deferral.

This is clear in expressions such as «نَسَأَ اللَّهُ فِي أَجَلِهِ».

The expression does not mean nasaʾa itself denotes lifespan or an appointed term.

Ajal is what may be subject to postponement; nasaʾa expresses the postponing.

Study of ʾ J L

N S ʾ (نَسَأَ) and N S KH (نَسَخَ): postponing and replacement

This comparison matters because of the variant reading of Al-Baqarah 2:106.

Arabic N S KH concerns shifting and transferring and, in some uses, removing and replacing.

N S ʾ concerns postponing.

The reading «مَا نَنْسَخْ مِنْ آيَةٍ أَوْ نَنْسَأْهَا» places two verbs together.

Their combination does not make every deferral abrogation or every abrogation necessarily involve delay.

The verbs’ semantic difference should be preserved.

The precise ruling concerning abrogation is an independent research question, not something derived solely from N S ʾ.

Study of N S KH

7. Translation challenges and Forqan proposals

The principal challenge is that the two main Qur’anic uses concern different settings.

Al-Tawbah 9:37 concerns a practice involving the months’ sacred status.

Sabaʾ 34:14 concerns a real staff.

One equivalent such as “delay” cannot serve both.

Translating nasīʾ in Al-Tawbah

Several renderings are possible:

RenderingAssessment
DelayConveys the lexical sense but leaves sacred months unspecified
Shifting sacred-month observanceFits the context and explains more
A postponed monthFits the passive analysis but does not explain the whole practice
Adding a thirteenth monthNot established with certainty by the verse itself
NasīʾRetains the Qur’anic form and needs a brief explanation

Forqan should preferably preserve nasīʾ in the base translation and explain:

“Nasīʾ: postponing and shifting the sacred observance of the sacred months.”

This prevents an unproven historical interpretation, such as adding a thirteenth month or shifting all rituals, from replacing the word itself.

The reflection should also clarify that the practice was used to permit what The God had forbidden.

Translating «مِنْسَأَتَهُ» in Sabaʾ

The clear, natural equivalent is “his staff.”

“His walking stick” is also suitable.

Expressions such as “instrument of delay,” “device for driving time,” or “staff of sovereignty” should not replace the word without evidence.

The staff may have a particular narrative role, but its lexical meaning should not absorb every message of the story.

Translating nansaʾhā in Al-Baqarah

Where this reading occurs, the appropriate equivalent is:

“We postpone it.”

This should remain distinct from nunsihā, causing to forget.

An explanation may examine recognized interpretations involving delayed revelation or application of a ruling. None should be declared certain from the root alone.

Other useful equivalents

In general Arabic, «نَسَأَ الدَّيْنَ» means postponing repayment of a debt.

«بَيْعٌ بِنَسِيئَةٍ» can be translated as a deferred-payment transaction or sale.

«اِسْتَنْسَأَهُ» means asking someone for more time.

«اِنْتَسَأَ عَنْهُ» means moving away from someone.

«نَسَأَ الدَّابَّةَ» means driving an animal onward.

These equivalents must follow the sentence’s subject matter; they are not interchangeable in every construction or context.

8. Conclusion

Arabic N S ʾ concerns postponing, putting back, granting time and, in some uses, moving something away or driving it forward.

In financial language, this appears in nasīʾah and deferred transactions.

Everyday uses include putting back a due date, moving away from a person, and driving an animal.

Physically, minsaʾah names a staff used to drive animals or lean upon.

Other branches include adding water to milk, delayed menstruation at the beginning of pregnancy, and initial animal fattening. They should not all be reduced to a single historical image without independent evidence.

The Qur’an has two principal uses of this family.

In Al-Tawbah 9:37, nasīʾ concerns shifting the sacred months’ observance. By altering months’ status, people permitted what The God had forbidden while attempting to preserve the number of sacred months in appearance.

In Sabaʾ 34:14, minsaʾatahu concerns Solomon’s staff. A creature of the earth eats it, revealing his death. The message about the jinn’s ignorance of the unseen follows from the whole narrative, rather than the staff’s name.

Another reading of Al-Baqarah 2:106 has nansaʾhā, postponing, which must remain distinct from nunsihā of N S Y.

Early Arabic witnesses show delay and removal as important semantic fields. Yet the two principal Qur’anic forms differ: one concerns time and sacred status, the other a physical implement.

Root study should illuminate a word’s meaning rather than replace each derivative’s established sense with a general image.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N S ʾ principally means postponing, putting back, granting time, and removing something from its present time or position. Uses include deferring debt, putting back a due date, moving away, and driving an animal onward.

Qur’anic nasīʾ in Al-Tawbah 9:37 concerns postponing and shifting sacred-month observance, a practice used to permit what The God had forbidden.

Minsaʾah in Sabaʾ 34:14 names a staff. Its established meaning should not be replaced with “delay” or another narrative concept.

The nansaʾhā reading of Al-Baqarah 2:106 clearly retains the principal meaning of postponement.

Thus, “postponing and moving away” is an important semantic axis of N S ʾ; translation should understand nasīʾ in the context of changing sacred-month observance and minsaʾah as a staff. Their lexical connection does not authorize merging their senses or adding unsupported interpretations to the verses.
