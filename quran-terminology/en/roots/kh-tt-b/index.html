Address, conversation, raising a matter, and making a request: from speaking to another to proposing marriage and attending to affairs

The Arabic root خ ط ب has several related semantic branches. The most important concern speaking to an addressee, raising and pursuing a subject, expressing a request, and attending to a matter that calls for attention. The same family includes khiṭāb, khuṭbah, khaṭb, and khiṭbah, which share a root but differ in meaning and use.

خِطَاب denotes speech directed to an addressee; خَطْب an affair, issue, or occurrence about which a question or discussion arises; and خِطْبَة, in the marriage context, a request to enter a marital union. خُطْبَة denotes an address or speech delivered to listeners.

In the Qur’an, this family appears in various settings: addressing The God, dealing with the ignorant, resolving disputes and judging, asking about messengers’ mission, calling people to account, and proposing marriage.

Examining these uses shows that speech and communication between parties are central to much of this family. Nevertheless, each derivative has its own meaning. All uses cannot be reduced either to “public speaking” or to “a great and important affair.”

1. Important forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • خَطَبَ ـ يَخْطُبُ (khaṭaba–yakhṭubu): delivered an address; in a marriage construction, proposed marriage.
  • خَاطَبَ ـ يُخَاطِبُ (khāṭaba–yukhāṭibu): spoke to someone or directed speech toward them.
  • تَخَاطَبَ ـ يَتَخَاطَبُ (takhāṭaba–yatakhāṭabu): conversed and exchanged speech with one another.
  • خِطَاب (khiṭāb): speech directed toward an addressee; address or communication.
  • خُطْبَة (khuṭbah): an address or speech delivered before listeners.
  • خَطِيب (khaṭīb): an orator or someone delivering an address; in certain uses, a suitor.
  • خَطْب (khaṭb): an affair, concern, subject, or occurrence, particularly one calling for attention, inquiry, or consideration.
  • خُطُوب (khuṭūb): plural of khaṭb; affairs and occurrences, especially difficult events.
  • خِطْبَة (khiṭbah): a marriage proposal or request for marriage.
  • خَاطِب (khāṭib): someone proposing marriage.
  • اِخْتَطَبَ (ikhtaṭaba): in lexical uses, relates to delivering an address or seeking marriage; its precise meaning depends on construction and context.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The root occurs twelve times, in twelve verses across eleven surahs, in four derivative categories:

خَاطَبَ, خِطَاب, خَطْب, and خِطْبَة.

The verb خَاطَبَ occurs three times; the noun خِطَاب three times; خَطْب five times; and خِطْبَة once. خُطْبَة with ḍammah on the khāʾ, meaning a public address, does not occur in the Qur’an.

Forms such as خَطْبُكُمْ, خَطْبُكَ, and خَطْبُكُمَا differ through attached pronouns; they are not three independent derivatives. Likewise, تُخَاطِبْنِي and خَاطَبَهُمُ are inflected forms of the Form III verb.

2. The semantic field in early Arabic

Speaking to an addressee

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two lexical principles for خ ط ب. The first is speech between two parties.

He places خَاطَبَهُ يُخَاطِبُهُ خِطَابًا within this field and connects the words for a public address and a marriage proposal with it.

Lisān al-ʿArab also explains خِطَاب and مُخَاطَبَة as مُرَاجَعَةُ الْكَلَامِ: speaking and the return or exchange of speech between parties.

Two points follow from this definition.

First, address is more than the mere existence of speech: it has an addressee toward whom it is directed. Speaking to someone is addressing that person.

Second, this communication may lead to conversation and an exchange, but every address need not be a complete two-way conversation. One person may speak while the other answers, remains silent, or is even denied a reply.

Thus خَاطَبَ can often be translated as “addressed” or “spoke to someone,” without necessarily implying a long conversation or equal standing between the parties.

خَطْب: an affair, concern, or issue being raised

Another important branch in Qur’anic usage is خَطْب, meaning an affair, subject, or occurrence.

Arabic uses the expression:

مَا خَطْبُكَ؟

mā khaṭbuka

That is, “What is your business?”, “What is the matter with you?”, or “What has happened?”

Lisān al-ʿArab explicitly states that khaṭb may be a small or great affair:

الْخَطْبُ: الشَّأْنُ أَوِ الْأَمْرُ، صَغُرَ أَوْ عَظُمَ.

al-khaṭbu al-shaʾnu awi al-amru ṣaghura aw ʿaẓuma

Hence Arabic records both خَطْبٌ جَلِيلٌ for a great or weighty affair and خَطْبٌ يَسِيرٌ for a minor or easy matter.

The greatness of an event is therefore not an essential part of khaṭb itself. The word can refer to a subject whose importance is established by context.

In many uses, however, khaṭb describes an event or situation that is not ordinary or insignificant, making “an important occurrence” a natural Persian equivalent in some contexts. That frequent use should not become an absolute rule.

Ibn Fāris connects khaṭb with speaking, explaining its naming through the discussion and exchange that take place about such an affair.

This relationship is intelligible: something arises, people speak about it, a question is asked, and an answer or intervention follows.

Nevertheless, this lexical explanation does not mean that speech or conversation must be added whenever khaṭb is translated. In مَا خَطْبُكَ؟, خَطْبُكَ denotes the person’s affair or business itself, not necessarily that person’s words.

Lisān al-ʿArab: a small or great affair, and the expressions jalīl and yasīr

خُطْبَة: an address and public speaking

In Arabic, خُطْبَة, with ḍammah on the khāʾ, denotes a speech delivered by an orator.

Such speech may be delivered to a group, at a public gathering, when announcing something, or in another social setting.

Lexical sources sometimes explain khuṭbah as speech with a definite beginning and end. Eloquence, organized expression, and orderly presentation have also become important in uses connected with oratory.

The modern concept of oratory, with all its religious, political, or ceremonial characteristics, should not be imposed on the root’s earlier meaning.

Khuṭbah names a kind of speech; whether its subject is religious, social, political, legal, or ceremonial depends on the occasion.

Friday sermons, marriage addresses, and other formal speeches are familiar uses of this family across different periods. They should not all be extracted directly from the word’s Qur’anic use.

خِطْبَة: proposing and requesting marriage

An important distinction within this root is that between خُطْبَة and خِطْبَة.

خُطْبَة (khuṭbah), with ḍammah on the khāʾ, usually denotes a public address. خِطْبَة (khiṭbah), with kasrah, in its familiar use means a request for marriage.

خَطَبَ الْمَرْأَةَ means that someone proposed marriage to a woman or raised the request to marry her.

This use relates to speech and requesting, since proposing marriage entails expressing a wish and raising the subject of a marital union.

Lisān al-ʿArab records خَاطِب, خَطِيب, and even خِطْب in certain constructions for a suitor. اِخْتَطَبَ الْقَوْمُ فُلَانًا also describes a woman’s family inviting a man to propose marriage.

These uses show that proposing marriage is not merely a later ceremonial label in Arabic; it belongs to the established field of requesting and interpersonal communication.

Nevertheless, a marriage proposal is not the marriage contract itself. Requesting a union differs from establishing and concluding its covenant, a distinction visible in Al-Baqarah 2:235.

Another branch: contrasting colors

Besides speech and address, Ibn Fāris records another principle for خ ط ب: the difference between two colors.

In this family, أَخْطَب and خَطْبَاء describe certain animals and plants displaying contrasting colors or colored stripes.

Examples include حِمَارٌ أَخْطَبُ and أَتَانٌ خَطْبَاءُ for certain wild asses. حَنْظَلَةٌ خَطْبَاءُ describes a colocynth fruit showing different colors or green and yellow stripes.

Some explanations of أَخْطَب also refer to greenness mixed with darkness.

This lexical branch differs from address and conversation. Shared root consonants do not justify claiming that contrasting colors developed from speaking, or the reverse.

None of the Qur’anic uses of خ ط ب has this color meaning, but it matters for a full account of the lexical family.

3. Early Arabic witnesses and their social setting

Proposing marriage in poetry attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd al-ʿIbādī, concerning the story of Jadhīmah al-Abrash and Al-Zabbāʾ, contains:

لِخِطِّيبَى الَّتِي غَدَرَتْ وَخَانَتْ
وَهُنَّ ذَوَاتُ غَائِلَةٍ لُحِينَا

li-khiṭṭībā allatī ghadarat wa-khānat / wa-hunna dhawātu ghāʾilatin luḥīnā

Approximate translation within the verse’s lexical explanation:

“Because of proposing to the woman who betrayed and broke faith; and women may at times bring harm and peril.”

Lexical sources differ over خِطِّيبَى. Some take it as a name, while Abū ʿUbayd and Al-Azharī explain it as a verbal noun meaning khiṭbah, a marriage proposal.

In this reading, the poet refers to the proposal to Al-Zabbāʾ, which the associated historical narrative connects with betrayal and Jadhīmah’s death.

The witness records another form in the family of proposing marriage in early poetry. It reaches us through lexical sources, so its attribution should be presented at that level, without treating every historical detail of the story as established. Here لِخِطِّيبَى has kasrah on its initial lām, following Jamharat al-Lughah and Kitāb al-ʿAyn. Transmissions differ in the second hemistich, whose translation remains approximate.

Jamharat al-Lughah: khiṭṭībā and its poetic witness

The expressions خَطْبٌ جَلِيلٌ and خَطْبٌ يَسِيرٌ

Classical Arabic records both expressions:

خَطْبٌ جَلِيلٌ: a great affair or weighty event.

خَطْبٌ يَسِيرٌ: a minor affair or easy matter.

These combinations illuminate khaṭb’s range. If the word always meant “a very great event” by itself, describing it as yasīr, minor or easy, would require a different explanation.

Their existence shows that khaṭb itself denotes an affair or occurrence, while an adjective or the context may establish its size or importance.

These are examples recorded in Arabic dictionaries. Without independent evidence, the precise origin of each expression should not be assigned to pre-Islamic times.

Speech and proposals in social relationships

Lexical accounts of some Arab customs include expressing a marriage request through a brief, clear declaration. In an example transmitted in Lisān al-ʿArab, the suitor uses خِطْب to state his request and the response concerning marriage uses نِكْح.

This account helps identify the social use of two lexical families: requesting a union and establishing a marriage.

A dictionary’s record of such a custom does not establish its universal practice among all Arab tribes or turn it into a religious ruling. Its relevance is the linguistic distinction between requesting marriage and the marital union itself.

4. Qur’anic uses: from an affair to addressing someone

خَطْب: asking about an affair, concern, or occurrence

خَطْب occurs five times in the Qur’an in the interrogative construction مَا خَطْبُ....

Each occurrence asks about an affair or situation requiring explanation, but the reason for the question and the subject’s importance differ between stories.

Asking the women in Joseph’s story — Yūsuf 12:51

قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُنَّ إِذْ رَاوَدْتُنَّ يُوسُفَ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ قُلْنَ حَاشَ لِلَّهِ مَا عَلِمْنَا عَلَيْهِ مِنْ سُوءٍ قَالَتِ امْرَأَتُ الْعَزِيزِ الْآنَ حَصْحَصَ الْحَقُّ أَنَا رَاوَدْتُهُ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ وَإِنَّهُ لَمِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ

qāla mā khaṭbukunna idh rāwadtunna yūsufa ʿan nafsihi qulna ḥāsha li-llāhi mā ʿalimnā ʿalayhi min sūʾin qālati imraʾatu al-ʿazīzi al-āna ḥaṣḥaṣa al-ḥaqqu anā rāwadtuhu ʿan nafsihi wa-innahu la-mina al-ṣādiqīna

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“He said, ‘What was your affair when you sought to entice Joseph?’ They said, ‘The God is beyond such a thing; we knew no evil of him.’ The governor’s wife said, ‘Now the truth has become clear. I sought to entice him, and he is surely among the truthful.’”

Yūsuf 12:51

مَا خَطْبُكُنَّ asks about the women’s past conduct. It is not simply an inquiry about their everyday circumstances, but concerns establishing the truth of an incident connected with Joseph’s accusation and imprisonment.

خَطْب refers to the affair and incident being questioned. The interrogative construction creates the setting for an answer and clarification of the truth.

Judicial investigation, establishing innocence, and confession follow from the narrative context and subsequent speech, rather than khaṭb’s lexical meaning alone.

Abraham asks the messengers — Al-Ḥijr 15:57 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:31

An identical expression appears in two surahs:

قَالَ فَمَا خَطْبُكُمْ أَيُّهَا الْمُرْسَلُونَ

qāla fa-mā khaṭbukum ayyuhā al-mursalūna

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“He said, ‘Then what is your business, O messengers?’”

Al-Ḥijr 15:57

The same expression also occurs in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:31.

In these two settings, Abraham questions messengers who have come on a mission.

In Al-Ḥijr, following his question, the messengers describe their mission concerning a criminal people. In Al-Dhāriyāt, the answer likewise concerns their being sent to a criminal people.

خَطْبُكُمْ refers here to their business, purpose, or mission.

Khaṭb does not inherently mean “a divine mission.” That particular meaning comes from the addressees’ identity and their subsequent answer.

These verses show that mā khaṭbukum can ask about the purpose of people’s presence and what they have come to do, rather than necessarily asking about an unfortunate event.

Calling Al-Sāmirī to account — Ṭā Hā 20:95

قَالَ فَمَا خَطْبُكَ يَا سَامِرِيُّ

qāla fa-mā khaṭbuka yā sāmiriyyu

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“He said, ‘Then what is your affair, O Sāmirī?’”

Ṭā Hā 20:95

This question occurs in the context of the calf and Al-Sāmirī’s conduct.

Moses has encountered his people in a state of deviation. He now asks Al-Sāmirī what his conduct has been.

خَطْبُكَ refers to Al-Sāmirī’s affair and behavior, something requiring explanation and an answer.

The story’s continuation makes clear that this is a question of accountability. The same construction, however, does not necessarily accuse or condemn its addressee in every situation.

Comparing this use with Abraham’s question to the messengers shows that context determines the question’s force and direction.

Moses asks the two women — Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23

وَلَمَّا وَرَدَ مَاءَ مَدْيَنَ وَجَدَ عَلَيْهِ أُمَّةً مِنَ النَّاسِ يَسْقُونَ وَوَجَدَ مِنْ دُونِهِمُ امْرَأَتَيْنِ تَذُودَانِ قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُمَا قَالَتَا لَا نَسْقِي حَتَّى يُصْدِرَ الرِّعَاءُ وَأَبُونَا شَيْخٌ كَبِيرٌ

wa-lammā warada māʾa madyana wajada ʿalayhi ummatan mina al-nāsi yasqūna wa-wajada min dūnihimu imraʾatayni tadhūdāni qāla mā khaṭbukumā qālatā lā nasqī ḥattā yuṣdira al-riʿāʾu wa-abūnā shaykhun kabīrun

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“When he came to the water of Midian, he found a group of people watering there, and apart from them two women holding back. He said, ‘What is your business?’ They said, ‘We do not water until the shepherds take [their flocks] away, and our father is an elderly man.’”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23

This is especially clear evidence against restricting khaṭb to a very great event or grave calamity.

Moses sees two women who, unlike the group of people, are standing back and restraining their animals. He asks about their situation.

مَا خَطْبُكُمَا can naturally be rendered “What is your business?” or “What is the matter with you two?”

The women’s answer explains their waiting through the watering conditions and their elderly father’s situation. In Ḥafṣ’s reading, يُصْدِرَ is a transitive Form IV verb; the animals are its contextually understood object. Accordingly, the proposed “take their flocks away” makes this construction clearer than simply “return.”

Al-Ṭabarī: the yuṣdira reading and taking the animals away from the water

The question concerns a situation that is unusual or calls for explanation, rather than a catastrophe or immense event.

It shows clearly that khaṭb can refer to a specific practical issue in everyday life.

خَاطَبَ: speaking to an addressee

The verb خَاطَبَ occurs three times in the Qur’an. Twice, The God forbids Noah to address Him concerning the wrongdoers; once, the ignorant address the servants of the Entirely Merciful.

Addressing The God in Noah’s story — Hūd 11:37 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:27

وَاصْنَعِ الْفُلْكَ بِأَعْيُنِنَا وَوَحْيِنَا وَلَا تُخَاطِبْنِي فِي الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا إِنَّهُمْ مُغْرَقُونَ

wa-iṣnaʿi al-fulka bi-aʿyuninā wa-waḥyinā wa-lā tukhāṭibnī fī alladhīna ẓalamū innahum mughraqūna

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Build the ship under Our eyes and through Our revelation, and do not address Me concerning those who have wronged; surely they are to be drowned.”

Hūd 11:37

وَلَا تُخَاطِبْنِي فِي الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا also occurs in Al-Muʾminūn 23:27.

تُخَاطِبْنِي is a Form III imperfect verb. The God is the addressee, while فِي الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا specifies the subject of the speech. In both verses, the verb is jussive after prohibitive lā, marked by sukūn on the bāʾ. The nūn before the yāʾ is nūn al-wiqāyah, and the yāʾ is the first-person object pronoun.

Lexically, the verb denotes addressing. فِي الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا and the following إِنَّهُمْ مُغْرَقُونَ show that discussing those people’s fate is prohibited in this particular situation.

This Qur’anic use should therefore not be confined to “chatting” in the ordinary everyday sense. Here, addressing can include making a request, conversing, or raising the matter of that group’s fate.

Nevertheless, “intercession” or “requesting rescue” is not the lexical meaning of تُخَاطِبْنِي itself. If proposed, that understanding must be argued from the story’s context and related verses, rather than replacing the word’s direct meaning.

The prohibition also concerns the specified الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا in that situation. It does not establish a general rule denying people the right to request, pray, or speak to The God in every other situation.

The ignorant address the servants of the Entirely Merciful — Al-Furqān 25:63

وَعِبَادُ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الَّذِينَ يَمْشُونَ عَلَى الْأَرْضِ هَوْنًا وَإِذَا خَاطَبَهُمُ الْجَاهِلُونَ قَالُوا سَلَامًا

wa-ʿibādu al-raḥmāni alladhīna yamshūna ʿalā al-arḍi hawnan wa-idhā khāṭabahumu al-jāhilūna qālū salāman

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The servants of the Entirely Merciful are those who walk upon the earth humbly, and when the ignorant address them, they say, ‘Peace.’”

Al-Furqān 25:63

خَاطَبَهُمُ is a perfect verb; “the ignorant” is its subject and the attached hum its object.

Here, addressing means speaking to people. The verb does not itself specify that the ignorant people’s speech was necessarily abuse, threat, or insult.

The contrast between الْجَاهِلُونَ and the servants of the Entirely Merciful, together with قَالُوا سَلَامًا, places the verse in a discussion of a fitting response to an address from the ignorant.

The verb’s direct meaning must be distinguished from the speakers’ characteristics. خَاطَبَهُمُ means that they addressed them; the ignorant people’s manner is understood from the verb’s combination with its subject and context.

The servants’ response also shows that being addressed does not force someone to reply with the same tone and method.

Address expresses verbal communication between parties, while its moral quality is established through other words and people’s conduct.

خِطَاب: speech, judgment, and expressive power

خِطَاب occurs three times in the Qur’an. These uses help distinguish the ability to speak, prevailing in argument, and the right to address.

فَصْلَ الْخِطَابِ — Ṣād 38:20

وَشَدَدْنَا مُلْكَهُ وَآتَيْنَاهُ الْحِكْمَةَ وَفَصْلَ الْخِطَابِ

wa-shadadnā mulkahu wa-ātaynāhu al-ḥikmata wa-faṣla al-khiṭābi

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“We strengthened his kingdom and gave him wisdom and decisive address.”

Ṣād 38:20

The verse concerns David. الْخِطَابِ occurs in the possessive construction فَصْلَ الْخِطَابِ.

Understanding this expression requires distinguishing ف ص ل from خ ط ب.

Faṣl concerns separating, distinguishing, and clarifying boundaries. Khiṭāb denotes speech directed to an addressee.

فَصْلَ الْخِطَابِ may refer to clear, discriminating expression or speech that, in considering affairs, establishes the distinction between truth and falsehood or disputed matters.

The following verses introduce disputing parties who come before David. This connection strengthens the phrase’s relationship with attending to disputes and judging.

Faṣl should not be dropped from the expression and the whole reduced to “eloquence and oratory.” Beautiful or lengthy speech is not necessarily speech that distinguishes and clarifies truth.

Nor should this expression be made, without evidence, into a legal term containing every procedural detail of later litigation.

A defensible contextual meaning is the ability to clarify and settle affairs through speech and judgment.

Al-Ṭabarī: interpretations of faṣl al-khiṭāb and judgment

Study of F Ṣ L

وَعَزَّنِي فِي الْخِطَابِ — Ṣād 38:23

إِنَّ هَذَا أَخِي لَهُ تِسْعٌ وَتِسْعُونَ نَعْجَةً وَلِيَ نَعْجَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ فَقَالَ أَكْفِلْنِيهَا وَعَزَّنِي فِي الْخِطَابِ

inna hādhā akhī lahu tisʿun wa-tisʿūna naʿjatan wa-liya naʿjatun wāḥidatun fa-qāla akfilnīhā wa-ʿazzanī fī al-khiṭābi

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“This is my brother; he has ninety-nine ewes, while I have one ewe. He said, ‘Entrust it to me,’ and overpowered me in speech.”

Ṣād 38:23

الْخِطَابِ occurs here in a dispute between two parties.

One says that the other prevailed over him in conversation or in pressing his request.

Khiṭāb denotes speech exchanged between the disputants. The sense of overpowering comes from عَزَّنِي, rather than khiṭāb itself.

Address does not inherently mean debate, hostility, or bullying. Here, its combination with ʿazzanī yields the sense of prevailing in speech and argument.

This distinction matters: power in speech does not necessarily equal being right.

One party complains about the other’s dominance in address. Someone may be more forceful in expressing a demand or advancing a position while the underlying matter still requires fair judgment.

This follows from the narrative and ʿazzanī, not from a lexical meaning of khiṭāb as “using power to violate a right.”

Alongside Ṣād 38:20, the verse presents related but distinct situations: David receives faṣl al-khiṭāb, while a person complains of another’s dominance in khiṭāb.

These uses show that speaking and verbal dominance differ from the ability to judge clearly and discriminatingly.

لَا يَمْلِكُونَ مِنْهُ خِطَابًا — Al-Nabaʾ 78:37

رَبِّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا الرَّحْمَٰنِ لَا يَمْلِكُونَ مِنْهُ خِطَابًا

rabbi al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-mā baynahumā al-raḥmāni lā yamlikūna minhu khiṭāban

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Lord of the heavens and the earth and what is between them, the Entirely Merciful; they possess no authority to address Him.”

Al-Nabaʾ 78:37

خِطَابًا is indefinite and the object of يَمْلِكُونَ.

لَا يَمْلِكُونَ shows that this is more than the ability to produce sound or speak. It concerns lacking independent authority and entitlement to address.

The next verse clarifies this:

يَوْمَ يَقُومُ الرُّوحُ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ صَفًّا لَا يَتَكَلَّمُونَ إِلَّا مَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَقَالَ صَوَابًا

yawma yaqūmu al-rūḥu wa-al-malāʾikatu ṣaffan lā yatakallamūna illā man adhina lahu al-raḥmānu wa-qāla ṣawāban

Al-Nabaʾ 78:38

Proposed translation of Al-Nabaʾ 78:38: “On the day when the Spirit and the angels stand in ranks, they will not speak, except one whom the Entirely Merciful permits and who speaks rightly.” Speaking is conditioned here upon the Entirely Merciful’s permission and right speech.

Reading these verses together shows that لَا يَمْلِكُونَ مِنْهُ خِطَابًا denies independent authority to address The God in that situation, rather than absolutely excluding speech with His permission.

This also shows that khiṭāb is not lexically identical with “intercession” or “bargaining.” Requests or intercession, if raised in a broader study of the verses, require separate treatment. خِطَابًا itself denotes speech and address.

This depiction of the Resurrection should likewise not be extended without evidence to people’s prayer and requests to The God in worldly life.

Al-Ṭabarī: authority to address and permission to speak

خِطْبَة: requesting marriage — Al-Baqarah 2:235

وَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِيمَا عَرَّضْتُمْ بِهِ مِنْ خِطْبَةِ النِّسَاءِ أَوْ أَكْنَنْتُمْ فِي أَنْفُسِكُمْ عَلِمَ اللَّهُ أَنَّكُمْ سَتَذْكُرُونَهُنَّ وَلَكِنْ لَا تُوَاعِدُوهُنَّ سِرًّا إِلَّا أَنْ تَقُولُوا قَوْلًا مَعْرُوفًا وَلَا تَعْزِمُوا عُقْدَةَ النِّكَاحِ حَتَّى يَبْلُغَ الْكِتَابُ أَجَلَهُ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ يَعْلَمُ مَا فِي أَنْفُسِكُمْ فَاحْذَرُوهُ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ

wa-lā junāḥa ʿalaykum fīmā ʿarraḍtum bihi min khiṭbati al-nisāʾi aw aknantum fī anfusikum ʿalima allāhu annakum satadhkurūnahunna wa-lākin lā tuwāʿidūhunna sirran illā an taqūlū qawlan maʿrūfan wa-lā taʿzimū ʿuqdata al-nikāḥi ḥattā yablugha al-kitābu ajalahu wa-iʿlamū anna allāha yaʿlamu mā fī anfusikum fa-iḥdharūhu wa-iʿlamū anna allāha ghafūrun ḥalīmun

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“There is no blame upon you for indirectly expressing a proposal to women or concealing it within yourselves. The God knows that you will mention them, but do not make secret promises to them, except to speak fitting words. Do not resolve upon the marriage bond until the prescribed term reaches its end. Know that The God knows what is within yourselves, so be mindful of Him; and know that The God is forgiving and forbearing.”

Al-Baqarah 2:235

خِطْبَةِ النِّسَاءِ is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of this derivative.

The verse relates to the waiting period following a husband’s death. Al-Baqarah 2:234 describes women whose husbands have died and who are to wait for a specified period.

Al-Baqarah 2:235 distinguishes several situations: hinting at a marriage proposal, concealing the intention to marry, making secret promises, speaking fitting words, and concluding the marriage bond.

Lexically, khiṭbah means requesting marriage. عَرَّضْتُمْ shows that this request may be expressed indirectly or through an allusion.

Khiṭbah is therefore not merely a formal speech or social ceremony. It essentially raises a request for a marital union, even when expressed indirectly in this verse’s particular situation.

عُقْدَةَ النِّكَاحِ, by contrast, concerns concluding the marital covenant. The Qur’an itself thus displays the distinction between a proposal and the marriage contract.

This distinction matters because some cultures and customs bring betrothal, proposing, contracting, and marrying close together in practice or merge them in everyday speech. All these stages should not be placed within khiṭbah’s meaning.

Nor should the verse become a general discussion of every type of relationship and marriage without examining its context. The word’s use is clear: requesting marriage where the waiting period and concluding the contract are at issue.

5. Distinctions from related roots

خ ط ب (khiṭāb) compared with ق و ل (qawl)

Qawl has a broad field encompassing saying, expressing, and the content of what is said.

Khiṭāb emphasizes speech directed toward an addressee.

For example, Al-Furqān 25:63 says خَاطَبَهُمُ الْجَاهِلُونَ, then presents the servants’ response through قَالُوا سَلَامًا.

The first verb emphasizes addressing people; the second states what is said in reply.

Every linguistic address may involve qawl, but not every use of qawl foregrounds the same structure of addressing.

Study of Q W L

خ ط ب (khiṭāb) compared with ك ل م (kalām)

The Qur’an uses ك ل م for speaking, speech, and communication.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:164 describes The God speaking to Moses. Al-Nabaʾ 78:38 uses يَتَكَلَّمُونَ concerning speaking with His permission.

Kalām can foreground speech itself or the act of speaking; khiṭāb principally concerns speech’s direction toward an addressee.

These families overlap, however. Their distinction does not establish that kalām is always one-way and khiṭāb always two-way.

لَا يَمْلِكُونَ مِنْهُ خِطَابًا in Al-Nabaʾ 78:37 and لَا يَتَكَلَّمُونَ in Al-Nabaʾ 78:38 show the families operating in closely related settings without becoming wholly synonymous.

Study of K L M

خ ط ب (khiṭāb) compared with ج د ل (jadal)

Jadal concerns discussion and argumentative contest, sometimes emphasizing dispute and the parties’ insistence against one another.

Address is not necessarily disputation.

In Al-Furqān 25:63, for instance, the ignorant address the servants of the Entirely Merciful. خَاطَبَهُمُ itself does not mean debate or argument.

In Ṣād 38:23, address appears amid disagreement and one party’s dominance. That feature comes from the story and ʿazzanī.

Every verbal dispute may consist of address and conversation, but not every address is a dispute.

Study of J D L

خ ط ب (khaṭb) compared with أ م ر (amr)

Amr has wide Qur’anic uses, from commands and orders to an affair, subject, situation, or the realization of a matter.

Khaṭb in its interrogative uses can be synonymous or very close to amr, as in مَا خَطْبُكَ؟ explained through مَا أَمْرُكَ؟.

Amr may denote a divine command or someone’s instruction. In its five Qur’anic uses, khaṭb denotes an affair or subject about which a question is raised.

Amr therefore cannot always be rendered as khaṭb, nor can a meaning of commanding be extracted from khaṭb.

Study of ʾ M R

خ ط ب (khiṭāb) compared with خ ص م (khiṣām)

Khiṣām concerns disagreement, hostility, or contention between parties.

Ṣād 38:21 introduces the disputants as الْخَصْمِ; in Ṣād 38:23, one says the other overpowered him in الْخِطَابِ.

Dispute and address can therefore occur in the same incident without having the same meaning.

Dispute describes the parties’ condition of disagreement; address describes their speech and verbal communication.

Thus ʿazzanī fī al-khiṭāb means prevailing in speech and putting forward a request, rather than khiṭāb itself meaning hostility.

Study of KH Ṣ M

خ ط ب (khaṭb) compared with خ ط أ (khaṭaʾ)

The root خ ط أ concerns error, mistake, or doing something contrary to correctness or intention.

Despite sharing their first two letters, خ ط ب and خ ط أ are independent roots.

مَا خَطْبُكَ؟ asks about someone’s affair or situation; it does not by itself call the person a wrongdoer.

In some stories, such as Al-Sāmirī’s, the question concerns error and responsibility. This relationship follows from the narrative context, rather than a shared root with خ ط أ.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The Qur’anic derivatives fall into several distinct semantic categories. Each should be translated with its own appropriate equivalent.

خَطْب: an affair, issue, concern, or occurrence

In Yūsuf 12:51, مَا خَطْبُكُنَّ can be rendered “What was your affair?”

In Al-Ḥijr 15:57 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:31, مَا خَطْبُكُمْ concerns the messengers’ business or purpose.

In Ṭā Hā 20:95, مَا خَطْبُكَ concerns Al-Sāmirī’s affair and conduct.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23, مَا خَطْبُكُمَا concerns the two women’s situation and difficulty.

“Affair” or “subject” can serve as a basic equivalent throughout, while “What has happened?” or “What is the matter?” may sometimes be more natural in Persian.

خَاطَبَ: addressing or speaking to someone

In Hūd 11:37 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:27, تُخَاطِبْنِي concerns addressing The God about a specified group.

In Al-Furqān 25:63, خَاطَبَهُمُ means that they addressed or spoke to them.

Direct translation need not add dispute, requesting forgiveness, or insult to the verb itself unless the verse’s construction and context call for those meanings.

خِطَاب: speech and address

In Ṣād 38:20, فَصْلَ الْخِطَابِ is a meaningful combination whose two components should both be considered, for example “the ability to settle matters through speech and judgment.”

In Ṣād 38:23, فِي الْخِطَابِ is close to “in conversation” or “in speech.”

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:37, خِطَابًا concerns addressing, while لَا يَمْلِكُونَ shows the absence of independent authority to do so.

خِطْبَة: proposing and requesting marriage

In Al-Baqarah 2:235, خِطْبَةِ النِّسَاءِ can be translated as “proposing marriage to women” or “requesting marriage with women.”

Khwāstgārī, proposing marriage, is the natural Persian and Dari equivalent. The lexical explanation should clarify that it denotes requesting a union and should not be equated with concluding the marriage contract.

Brief root meaning

Speaking to an addressee and raising or pursuing a matter; in other uses, an affair or occurrence, and requesting marriage.

Word boxes should give each derivative’s particular meaning in its verse. For khaṭb, “an important affair” should not replace “an affair or issue” without evidence; for khiṭāb, judgment and dispute should not become fixed parts of the root’s meaning.

Conclusion

In Arabic, خ ط ب comprises a broad family concerning speech, address, raising a subject, and requesting. Within this field, khiṭāb denotes speech directed to an addressee, khuṭbah a public address, khaṭb an affair or occurrence, and khiṭbah a request for marriage.

Lexical evidence shows that khaṭb does not always mean a very great event, just as khiṭāb is not always debate or hostility. The root also has an independent branch concerning contrasting colors that is absent from its Qur’anic uses.

The five Qur’anic uses of khaṭb ask about people’s affairs. Three occurrences of the verb khāṭaba express speaking to an addressee. Three uses of khiṭāb concern judgment, prevailing in speech, and authority to address; the sole khiṭbah occurrence concerns requesting marriage.

No single Persian equivalent can therefore serve accurately everywhere without regard to construction and context. The principal Qur’anic field is verbal communication and raising a matter between parties, but khaṭb, khiṭāb, and khiṭbah each have their own established meaning.

Preserving this distinction, especially in فَصْلَ الْخِطَابِ, وَعَزَّنِي فِي الْخِطَابِ, and خِطْبَةِ النِّسَاءِ, prevents lexical meaning from being confused with a contextual consequence, legal terminology, or a social custom. Brief root meaning: Speaking to an addressee and raising or pursuing a matter; in other uses, an affair or occurrence, and requesting marriage.
