The Qur’anic Root A W D

Burden and difficulty, bending and curvature: from the pressure of a load to preserving the heavens and earth without strain

Qur’anic and lexical forms

يَـُٔودُهُ
(yaʾūduhu)
Sole Qur’anic form: burdens him; Al-Baqarah 2:255

آدَ / يَؤُودُ
(āda / yaʾūdu)
Verb citation forms: burdening and hardship

أَوْد / أَوَد
(awd / awad)
Lexical verbal nouns: distinct vowels and meanings; not Qur’anic

أَوِدَ / آوَد
(awida / āwad)
Verb and adjective concerning crookedness; not Qur’anic

تَأَوَّدَ / أَوَّدَ
(taʾawwada / awwada)
Bending and causing to bend; non-Qur’anic forms

آدَ الْعَشِيُّ
(āda al-ʿashiyyu)
Poetic expression: the evening inclined; not Qur’anic

يَتَآدَاهُ
(yataʾādāhu)
Form transmitted in Ibn Al-Sikkīt’s example; not Qur’anic

The Arabic root A W D has two closely related semantic fields: bending or becoming crooked, and weight or pressure bearing upon someone or something. Earlier usage includes both a branch that bends and a task that weighs upon a person and causes hardship.

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is Al-Baqarah 2:255, where The God says of preserving the heavens and earth: وَلَا يَـُٔودُهُ حِفْظُهُمَا (wa-lā yaʾūduhu ḥifẓuhumā). Preserving them imposes no burden or difficulty upon Him.

Understanding this expression requires distinguishing a thing’s weight, the difficulty of bearing or performing it, and fatigue resulting from effort. These meanings are related, but not identical.

1. Linguistic forms and earlier Arabic usage

The Qur’anic form يَـُٔودُهُ (yaʾūduhu) derives from آدَ يَؤُودُ (āda / yaʾūdu). Other recorded family members include أَوَد (awad), أَوْد (awd), أَوِدَ (awida), آوَد (āwad), تَأَوَّدَ (taʾawwada), and أَوَّدَ (awwada). Their meanings differ, making vowels and morphological structure important. Āwad here is an adjective meaning bent or crooked, rather than the Qur’anic verb itself; awad, the noun for crookedness, should also be distinguished from awd in the burdening branch.

In آدَهُ الْحِمْلُ (ādahu al-ḥimlu), the load weighed upon someone or became difficult to bear. By contrast, أَوِدَ الْعُودُ (awida al-ʿūdu) describes wood or a branch becoming crooked or bent.

Taʾawwada likewise denotes bending, twisting, or curving. In أَقَامَ أَوَدَهُ (aqāma awadahu), the meaning is to straighten something crooked or remedy its crookedness.

Earlier usage also includes inclination and returning movement: the day inclining toward its end, or shadows turning back in the afternoon. These concern bending, inclination, or a change of direction, but they do not turn the particular Qur’anic verb’s meaning into “returning.”

Material imagery: bending under pressure

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris presents bending and curvature as the root’s underlying principle. He also explains آدَنِي الشَّيْءُ يَؤُودُنِي (ādanī al-shayʾu yaʾūdunī) through that image: something weighs so heavily upon someone that it is as though it bends them.

The semantic connection is clear. A person carrying a heavy load may bend under its pressure. This image helps explain the relationship between bending and difficulty in bearing a load.

Explaining a connection between meanings nevertheless differs from establishing their historical development. Bending under a load may illustrate burdening well without proving that every period’s sense of causing hardship developed directly and exclusively from that image.

What the lexical evidence clearly supports is the presence of both branches within A W D.

Examples from earlier Arabic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Muraqqish contains this line:

وَالْعَدْوَ بَيْنَ الْمَجْلِسَيْنِ إِذَا
آدَ الْعَشِيُّ وَتَنَادَى الْعَمُّ

wa-al-ʿadwa bayna al-majlisayni idhā / āda al-ʿashiyyu wa-tanādā al-ʿammu

The line means: “And running between the two gathering places, when the evening inclined and the people called to one another.” In connection with the preceding line’s supplicatory verb, al-ʿadwa is accusative; some isolated citations also give the nominative. آدَ الْعَشِيُّ (āda al-ʿashiyyu) refers to the evening’s inclination as the day approaches its end. Time’s movement is expressed through the image of inclination and turning.

This usage shows that the family was not confined to heavy loads in earlier Arabic: it could also express inclination and curvature. Attribution to Al-Muraqqish alone does not establish the precise date of each line or the word’s first use.

Another line transmitted by Ibn Al-Sikkīt reads:

إِلَى مَاجِدٍ لَا يَنْبَحُ الْكَلْبُ ضَيْفَهُ
وَلَا يَتَآدَاهُ احْتِمَالُ الْمَغَارِمِ

ilā mājidin lā yanbaḥu al-kalbu ḍayfahu / wa-lā yataʾādāhu iḥtimālu al-maghārimi

The line means: “Toward a nobleman whose dog does not bark at his guest, and whom bearing heavy financial obligations does not burden.” The first half belongs to praise of hospitality; the second describes someone who is not weighed down by bearing those financial burdens.

Lā yataʾādāhu means that bearing these burdens does not become heavy or difficult for him. Its sense approaches the Qur’anic yaʾūduhu, although the forms differ morphologically. Ibn Al-Sikkīt, as quoted in Lisān al-ʿArab, explains yataʾādāhu through a transposition relative to yataʾawwad. This concerns that transmitted form, rather than authorizing arbitrary rearrangement of letters across roots. The citation examined does not securely identify the poet, so the line should not be assigned to the pre-Islamic period without evidence.

The two examples illustrate different directions within the family: inclination and curvature, and weight and difficulty in bearing a burden. The Qur’anic occurrence emphasizes the latter.

2. Wa-lā yaʾūduhu ḥifẓuhumā: the Qur’anic occurrence

Surah Al-Baqarah states:

اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يَشْفَعُ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِشَيْءٍ مِنْ عِلْمِهِ إِلَّا بِمَا شَاءَ وَسِعَ كُرْسِيُّهُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَلَا يَـُٔودُهُ حِفْظُهُمَا وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْعَظِيمُ

allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa al-ḥayyu al-qayyūmu lā taʾkhudhuhu sinatun wa-lā nawmun lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍi man dhā alladhī yashfaʿu ʿindahu illā bi-idhnihi yaʿlamu mā bayna aydīhim wa-mā khalfahum wa-lā yuḥīṭūna bi-shayʾin min ʿilmihi illā bi-mā shāʾa wasiʿa kursiyyuhu al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa wa-lā yaʾūduhu ḥifẓuhumā wa-huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-ʿaẓīmu

“The God: there is no deity except Him, the Living, the Everlasting Sustainer. Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on earth. Who can intercede with Him except by His permission? He knows what is before them and what is behind them, and they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills. His kursī encompasses the heavens and the earth, and preserving them does not burden Him. He is the Most High, the Great.” (Al-Baqarah 2:255)

Morphology and syntax

Yaʾūduhu is an imperfect verb from āda / yaʾūdu. It is transitive in this construction: it describes something weighing upon another being and causing hardship.

In wa-lā yaʾūduhu ḥifẓuhumā, lā negates the verb. The suffix -hu is its object, while ḥifẓuhumā is the grammatical subject. Preserving the two is itself what is said not to impose weight or difficulty.

The dual suffix -humā in ḥifẓuhumā refers to the heavens and the earth mentioned in the preceding clause.

On the principal reading, which better fits the verse as a whole, -hu in yaʾūduhu refers to The God. Some commentaries also suggest reference to the kursī. Yet the verse throughout describes The God, His knowledge, dominion, and power; its ending, وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْعَظِيمُ (wa-huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-ʿaẓīm), continues that focus. Reference to The God therefore better fits the sequence of clauses.

A rendering close to the construction is: “Preserving the heavens and earth does not weigh upon Him or cause Him hardship.”

This differs completely from “The God does not preserve the heavens and earth” or “preserving them is unnecessary.” The verse speaks of preservation itself while denying only that it burdens Him or causes Him difficulty.

Weight, hardship, and fatigue

Arabic آدَهُ الأَمْرُ (ādahu al-amru) may describe an undertaking becoming difficult to perform or bear, exerting pressure upon a person, or demanding great effort.

Yaʾūdu thus relates to weight, hardship, and weariness. Its meaning should nevertheless not be confined to feeling tired after completing work.

A person may feel a task’s weight and difficulty before becoming completely tired. A heavy load exerts pressure from the moment it is carried, even while the person can still continue.

“Preserving them does not tire Him” therefore conveys part of the sense, while “preserving them does not burden Him” is closer to the lexical construction and also denies difficulty.

The Qur’an does more here than affirm The God’s ability to preserve the heavens and earth: it explicitly states that this preservation causes Him no burden or hardship.

3. The verse’s context: sustaining and preserving creation

Al-Baqarah 2:255 begins by describing The God as الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ (al-ḥayyu al-qayyūm), the Living, the Sustainer. It then says that neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him.

These two statements provide important context for understanding wa-lā yaʾūduhu ḥifẓuhumā.

People need strength and attention to carry out their tasks. Continued activity may tire them and create a need for rest. Even maintaining property, caring for a family, or overseeing a large organization may require sustained effort and attention.

The verse does not describe The God with such limitations. He is Living and Sustainer; drowsiness and sleep do not overtake Him, and preserving the heavens and earth does not burden Him.

Sinah, nawm, and awd are not synonyms. Drowsiness and sleep concern wakefulness and rest, whereas yaʾūdu concerns a task’s weight and difficulty. The verse denies these different limitations together.

Preserving the heavens and earth

Ḥifẓuhumā combines the verbal noun ḥifẓ, preservation, with a dual pronoun. It makes preserving the heavens and earth the subject of the clause.

Fāṭir 35:41 likewise speaks of The God holding the heavens and earth so that they do not pass away from their places. This verse helps explain the range of preservation and holding in the order of creation.

In Qāf 50:38, after mentioning the creation of the heavens and earth, the Qur’an states وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِنْ لُغُوبٍ (wa-mā massanā min lughūb): no fatigue or weariness touched Him.

These verses accord with Al-Baqarah 2:255, but use different words. Lughūb in Qāf 50:38 concerns fatigue and weariness; yaʾūdu in Al-Baqarah 2:255 denies burden and hardship.

Their semantic proximity should not reduce the two roots to a single lexical definition.

Nor should preserving the heavens and earth be made, without evidence, into a particular physical explanation of celestial motion, natural forces, or the universe’s structure. The verse relates preserving creation to The God’s power; every natural process’s mechanism is not the direct subject of yaʾūdu.

Denying difficulty does not deny the task’s magnitude

Another distinction matters: saying that preserving the heavens and earth is not burdensome for The God does not make them intrinsically small, insignificant, or lacking in grandeur.

The clause concerns this preservation in relation to His power, rather than determining the universe’s size or worth.

Something may be enormous and beyond human ability yet not difficult relative to another power. This comparison only helps explain the denial of hardship; it does not equate The God’s power with created beings’ limited abilities.

The verse should therefore not become “the heavens and earth have no weight.” It says that preserving them does not weigh upon The God.

4. Distinguishing A W D from related roots

A W D (awd) compared with TH Q L (thiql)

Study of TH Q L

TH Q L concerns weight and may describe a physical load, responsibility, or difficult undertaking.

In ādahu al-ḥimlu, A W D emphasizes the load’s effect upon its bearer: it presses upon them and makes carrying or undertaking it difficult.

The families are semantically close, but TH Q L may describe weight itself, whereas yaʾūduhu in Al-Baqarah 2:255 concerns preservation becoming burdensome for someone.

A W D (awd) compared with L GH B (lughūb)

Study of L GH B

Lughūb concerns fatigue and weariness. Qāf 50:38 uses it in the context of creating the heavens and earth.

A W D in the verse under examination instead denies burden and hardship. A person may find a task difficult before reaching exhaustion.

Denying lughūb and denying awd therefore reinforce one another semantically, but are not precisely the same lexical statement.

A W D (awd) compared with AYN W J (ʿiwaj)

Study of AYN W J (ʿ W J)

AYN W J concerns crookedness and departure from a straight course. One branch of A W D likewise records bending and curvature.

The connection is clear, but not all A W D uses are confined to crookedness. Yaʾūduhu does not mean that preserving the heavens and earth fails to divert The God from a straight course: it denies burden and difficulty.

The image of bending therefore helps explain the semantic relationship between the root’s branches, but should not be imposed on the verse’s direct translation.

A W D (awd) compared with W A D (waʾd)

Study of W A D

The roots contain the same three letters in a different order.

W A D is associated in the Qur’an with الْمَوْءُودَةُ (al-mawʾūdah) in Al-Takwīr 81:8, referring to a girl buried alive.

A W D in Al-Baqarah 2:255 concerns burden and hardship.

Similar letters or the existence of some transpositions in Arabic do not justify conflating the two families. Each requires its own forms and independent evidence.

A W D (awd) compared with A Y D (ayd)

Study of A Y D

A Y D concerns strength, power, and support. In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:47, بِأَيْدٍ (bi-aydin) appears in the context of creating the heaven.

Power and ability may relate semantically to bearing a load and the difficulty of a task, but the roots are distinct. A W D in Al-Baqarah 2:255 denies burden and hardship; the word itself does not mean strength or ability.

“Giving strength” or “reinforcing” should therefore not replace yaʾūduhu’s actual meaning.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

For Qur’anic yaʾūduhu, “burdens him,” “weighs upon him,” and “is difficult for him” fit the verb’s lexical field.

Within Al-Baqarah 2:255’s negative construction, translation should be fluent and precise. The principal proposal is:

“And preserving them does not burden Him.”

This preserves the subject—preserving the heavens and earth—places the pronoun referring to The God correctly, and conveys weight and difficulty without adding a philosophical explanation.

“And preserving them causes Him no hardship” is also accurate, though somewhat more formal.

“And preserving them does not tire Him” is conceptually defensible but focuses the verb more narrowly on the outcome of fatigue. If chosen, the root explanation should clarify that the word also encompasses weight and difficulty in bearing something.

“And preserving them is not impossible for Him” is unsuitable too: denying inability does not convey the full denial of hardship. A task’s being possible differs from its being easy and free of strain.

Equivalents for non-Qur’anic forms likewise depend on their constructions. Awida al-ʿūdu means a branch becoming bent or crooked; taʾawwada may mean twisting or curving; and ādahu al-ḥimlu means a load weighing upon someone and becoming difficult to bear.

Conclusion

The Arabic root A W D relates to bending, curvature, weight, and hardship. In material uses, wood or a branch may bend, while a heavy load may press upon someone. The connection is intelligible, but without further historical evidence neither meaning can be established as the certain source of every other use.

Its sole Qur’anic occurrence is Al-Baqarah 2:255. Yaʾūduhu here means a task becoming heavy and difficult for someone; wa-lā yaʾūduhu ḥifẓuhumā denies any burden or hardship for The God in preserving the heavens and earth.

This accords with describing Him as al-ḥayyu al-qayyūm and denying drowsiness and sleep, although each expression retains its distinct meaning.

This family’s semantic core is bending and curvature on one side, and weight and difficulty in bearing something on the other. The Qur’anic occurrence concerns the latter: preserving the heavens and earth does not burden The God or cause Him hardship.
