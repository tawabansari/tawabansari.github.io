The Qur’anic Root B GH Y

Seeking, pursuing, and going beyond a limit

The root ب غ ي has two strong and ancient branches in the Qur’an: seeking, searching, and pursuing something, and transgression, the pursuit of superiority, and going beyond a limit. Some lexical explanations have attempted to trace both to a single original meaning. Arabic and Qur’anic usage, however, does not allow us to insert “transgression” into every kind of seeking, or, conversely, to treat every act of aggression as simply a form of searching.

The Qur’an itself provides the clearest evidence of this duality. Moses says “ذَٰلِكَ مَا كُنَّا نَبْغِ” (dhālika mā kunnā nabghi): this is what we were seeking. Of Qārūn, by contrast, it says “فَبَغَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ” (fa-baghā ʿalayhim): he transgressed against his people. Just one verse later, he is told “وَلَا تَبْغِ الْفَسَادَ فِي الْأَرْضِ” (wa-lā tabghi l-fasāda fī l-arḍ): do not seek corruption in the land. Within a single account, one root expresses both transgressing against others and seeking something.

This accords with the Arabic linguistic tradition. Early explanations record both “seeking something” and “a kind of corruption and transgression” for B GH Y. Precisely how the two branches became connected in the language’s history is uncertain. We can see a conceptual relationship between seeking and moving toward something, on the one hand, and exceeding a measure or limit, on the other. Turning this proximity into a certain historical derivation, however, would go beyond the available evidence.

Qur’anic forms

The root B GH Y occurs 96 times in the Qur’an. Its principal forms are the basic verb baghā (بغى), yanbaghī (ينبغي), Form VIII ibtaghā (ابتغى), the noun baghy (بغي), the form baghiyy (بغيّ), the verbal noun bighāʾ (بغاء), the active participle bāghin (باغ), and the verbal noun ibtighāʾ (ابتغاء). This range itself shows that the family cannot be translated with one fixed English equivalent.

بغى
Baghā: seeking and transgression

ابتغى / ابتغاء
Ibtaghā / ibtighāʾ: pursuing an objective

ينبغي
Yanbaghī: being fitting or possible

بغي
Baghy: transgression and exceeding limits

باغ
Bāghin: the active participle in necessity

بغيّ / بغاء

From early Arabic to seeking and ibtighāʾ in the Qur’an

The branch of seeking was well established in Arabic before the Qur’an. Baghaytu sh-shayʾ means “I sought something,” and bughyah is something a person seeks. In one of its usages in pre-Islamic poetry, al-baghāyā also refers to people who go ahead in search of news or a route. This field shows that seeking and pursuing are not secondary, late usages, but an original part of the word’s history.

This meaning remains very clear in the Qur’an. When Moses says “ذَٰلِكَ مَا كُنَّا نَبْغِ” (dhālika mā kunnā nabghi) in Al-Kahf 18:64, the verb contains no sense of transgression: it concerns finding what they had been seeking. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:140, “أَغَيْرَ اللَّهِ أَبْغِيكُمْ إِلَٰهًا” (a-ghayra llāhi abghīkum ilāhā) likewise means: should I seek for you an object of worship other than The God? Of the people of the Garden, it also says “لَا يَبْغُونَ عَنْهَا حِوَلًا” (lā yabghūna ʿanhā ḥiwalā): they seek no change away from it.

Ibtaghā / ibtighāʾ continues this branch even more clearly. The Qur’an speaks of “أَنْ تَبْتَغُوا فَضْلًا مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ” (an tabtaghū faḍlan min rabbikum), seeking bounty from your Lord. A person may also act “ابْتِغَاءَ مَرْضَاتِ اللَّهِ” (ibtighāʾa marḍāti llāh), seeking the pleasure of The God, and spending may be “ابْتِغَاءَ وَجْهِ اللَّهِ” (ibtighāʾa wajhi llāh), seeking the face of The God. In none of these cases is seeking itself blameworthy.

The same form can have a negative objective: “ابْتِغَاءَ الْفِتْنَةِ” (ibtighāʾa l-fitnah), seeking fitnah, also occurs in the Qur’an. Ibtighāʾ therefore expresses only pursuing and following an objective; what the person seeks determines its ethical value.

Yanbaghī also belongs to this family, but in the language it has developed the sense of “being fitting, possible, appropriate, or attainable.” Translating it invariably as “should” is therefore imprecise. “وَمَا يَنْبَغِي لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ أَنْ يَتَّخِذَ وَلَدًا” (wa-mā yanbaghī li-r-raḥmāni an yattakhidha waladā) is not an ethical recommendation: such an attribution is incompatible with, and unfitting for, the standing of the All-Merciful. In “لَا الشَّمْسُ يَنْبَغِي لَهَا أَنْ تُدْرِكَ الْقَمَرَ” (lā sh-shamsu yanbaghī lahā an tudrika l-qamar), the sun is likewise not being addressed with an ethical command; within the order being described, such a thing is neither possible nor appointed for it. Concerning poetry, we also find “وَمَا يَنْبَغِي لَهُ” (wa-mā yanbaghī lah), and concerning Solomon’s dominion, “لَا يَنْبَغِي لِأَحَدٍ مِنْ بَعْدِي” (lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī).

These branches show that in the field of seeking, B GH Y extends far beyond “wanting something out of greed.” It can concern seeking bounty, approval, truth, fitnah, or any other objective.

Baghy as transgression and going beyond a limit

The second branch becomes particularly clear in the construction baghā ʿalā. Of Qārūn, the Qur’an says “فَبَغَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ” (fa-baghā ʿalayhim). In Ṣād, the two parties to a dispute say “بَغَىٰ بَعْضُنَا عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ” (baghā baʿḍunā ʿalā baʿḍ), and later the account says of partners “لَيَبْغِي بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ” (la-yabghī baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍ). In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9, too, if one of two groups commits baghy against the other, the transgressing group is to be confronted until it returns to the command of The God.

In these instances, baghy approaches exceeding one’s rights, imposing upon another, and aggressively pursuing superiority.

Ash-Shūrā 42:27 says that if provision were fully extended to the servants, “لَبَغَوْا فِي الْأَرْضِ” (la-baghaw fī l-arḍ): they would commit baghy in the land. The same surah condemns those who “يَبْغُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ” (yabghūna fī l-arḍi bi-ghayri l-ḥaqq). Here, too, baghy is not simply a wish: it is conduct that takes a person beyond their proper measure and rights.

Ar-Raḥmān 55:20 provides important evidence for this branch’s core: “بَيْنَهُمَا بَرْزَخٌ لَا يَبْغِيَانِ” (baynahumā barzakhun lā yabghiyān). Between the two waters is a boundary that neither crosses. Waters have no envy, arrogance, or ethical motivation, so baghy in this usage cannot inherently mean “envy” or “arrogance.” The more fundamental meaning is overstepping an appointed limit.

This matters for the recurring expression “بَغْيًا بَيْنَهُمْ” (baghyan baynahum). Al-Baqarah 2:213, Āl ʿImrān 3:19, Ash-Shūrā 42:14, and Al-Jāthiyah 45:17 all follow a similar pattern: knowledge or clear proofs have arrived, but disagreement subsequently arises, and “baghy among them” enters the account. The problem in these verses is not lack of information; the text expressly states that knowledge had arrived.

“Out of envy” may explain some motives, but it restricts the root’s meaning. Baghyan baynahum covers a broader field of the pursuit of superiority, aggressive rivalry, and transgression among themselves. Envy may cause baghy, but baghy itself is broader than envy.

Al-Baqarah 2:90 presents the same issue in a more specific setting: “أَنْ يَكْفُرُوا بِمَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ بَغْيًا أَنْ يُنَزِّلَ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ” (an yakfurū bi-mā anzala llāhu baghyan an yunazzila llāhu min faḍlihi ʿalā man yashāʾu min ʿibādih). Their reaction is to the fact that the bounty of The God descends upon whichever of His servants He wills. Envy is entirely possible here, but baghyan also expresses a claim to superiority in objecting to something over which they have no authority.

The Qur’an sometimes places baghy alongside another nearby term. In Yūnus 10:90, Pharaoh and his forces pursue the Children of Israel “بَغْيًا وَعَدْوًا” (baghyan wa-ʿadwā). Baghy and ʿudwān belong to a similar field but are not the same word. ʿUdwān directly emphasizes aggression against a boundary and another party; baghy, alongside transgression, also carries the coloring of pursuing superiority and exceeding one’s position. Rendering both simply as “wrongdoing” erases the distinction in the Qur’an’s choice of words.

Nor is baghy identical with fasād. The expression “وَلَا تَبْغِ الْفَسَادَ” (wa-lā tabghi l-fasād) itself shows that corruption can be something a person seeks. In this sentence, tabghi belongs to the seeking branch, while al-fasād is the object of that seeking.

Bāghin, baghiyy, and bighāʾ: branches in which context is decisive

The active participle bāghin occurs three times in the Qur’an, all in verses concerning dietary necessity: “فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ” (fa-mani ḍṭurra ghayra bāghin wa-lā ʿād). Since the root itself can express both seeking and transgressing, this construction must be understood from the context of necessity.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 describes a similar situation: “فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ فِي مَخْمَصَةٍ غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ” (fa-mani ḍṭurra fī makhmaṣatin ghayra mutajānifin li-ithm): someone compelled by hunger, without deliberately inclining toward sin.

From this network, ghayra bāghin wa-lā ʿād can be read within the bounds of that necessity: the person must not seek the prohibited thing as something independently desired, and must not exceed the extent of necessity. Precisely how the boundary is divided between the two descriptions cannot be extracted from the root alone, but both prevent necessity from turning into unrestricted, freely chosen conduct.

Another branch of the root has become established in the sexual sphere. The Qur’an has Mary say “وَلَمْ أَكُ بَغِيًّا” (wa-lam aku baghiyyā), and says of her mother “وَمَا كَانَتْ أُمُّكِ بَغِيًّا” (wa-mā kānat ummuki baghiyyā). Baghiyy in this construction designates a woman who engages in illicit sexual relations. “Prostitute” may say more than the text in these two verses, since payment or an occupation is not at issue. “A woman who engages in illicit sexual relations,” or more fluently “an unchaste woman,” is closer to the context.

This branch was also known before the Qur’an, and baghāyā occurs in early poetry for women in this sphere. A single poetic witness, however, should not lead us to conclude that all baghāyā were necessarily women forced into this activity for economic gain.

An-Nūr 24:33 itself clearly supplies such a setting: “وَلَا تُكْرِهُوا فَتَيَاتِكُمْ عَلَى الْبِغَاءِ إِنْ أَرَدْنَ تَحَصُّنًا لِتَبْتَغُوا عَرَضَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا” (wa-lā tukrihū fatayātikum ʿalā l-bighāʾi in aradna taḥaṣṣunan li-tabtaghū ʿaraḍa l-ḥayāti d-dunyā). The verse speaks of compulsion into bighāʾ and immediately identifies the aim of those imposing it as seeking the goods of worldly life.

This verse is particularly interesting for the study of the root because it places two branches of one family in a single sentence: al-bighāʾ has the established sexual meaning, while li-tabtaghū expresses ordinary seeking and pursuit.

The precise historical connection between bighāʾ and the root’s two main branches should not be made more certain than the evidence allows. The language has established it in the field of illicit sexual relations; this is sufficient for understanding the Qur’an.

Semitic evidence and the limits of translation

The seeking and searching branch of B GH Y has considerable support in nearby Semitic languages. In Aramaic, the form בעי is used for wanting, requesting, and seeking. Hebrew also has a related, uncommon form in the field of asking and seeking.

The difference between Arabic ghayn and Aramaic and Hebrew ʿayin does not prevent this comparison. In an early stage of the Semitic languages, ghayn and ʿayin were separate consonants. Arabic preserved this distinction, whereas in Hebrew and Aramaic the earlier ghayn merged with ʿayin. An Arabic form containing غ can therefore be historically related to a Hebrew or Aramaic form containing ʿayin.

This comparison shows that the branch of wanting and seeking is very old and cannot be treated as a late meaning.

Early Arabic also has independent evidence for the transgression branch. Baghā l-jarḥ is recorded for the spreading corruption of a wound, alongside usages concerning intensity and exceeding a measure. These show that going beyond a limit is also a deeply rooted branch within Arabic itself.

This requires us to avoid relying on a single fixed English equivalent. Baghā sometimes means “sought” and sometimes “transgressed.” Ibtighāʾ usually expresses pursuing an objective. Yanbaghī expresses being fitting, possible, or compatible, according to context. Al-baghy in social passages is generally closer to transgression and the pursuit of superiority. Baghiyy in Sūrat Maryam designates a woman who engages in illicit sexual relations, while al-bighāʾ in An-Nūr, in a context of coercion and economic gain, concerns prostitution and sexual exploitation.

This variety shows why mechanically equating baghy with either “wrongdoing” or “wanting” is inadequate. Each derivative must be understood through the syntax and context of its particular verse.

Summary

B GH Y is a family whose two ancient branches remain clearly alive in the Qur’an: seeking and pursuing, on the one hand, and transgression and going beyond a limit, on the other.

The seeking branch appears in “ذلك ما كنا نبغ” (dhālika mā kunnā nabghi), “ابتغاء وجه الله” (ibtighāʾa wajhi llāh), “ابتغاء مرضات الله” (ibtighāʾa marḍāti llāh), and “ابتغاء الفتنة” (ibtighāʾa l-fitnah). Seeking itself is neither good nor bad; the objective a person pursues determines its ethical direction.

The transgression branch appears in “فبغى عليهم” (fa-baghā ʿalayhim), “يبغون في الأرض” (yabghūna fī l-arḍ), “بغت إحداهما على الأخرى” (baghat iḥdāhumā ʿalā l-ukhrā), and “لا يبغيان” (lā yabghiyān). The last example is especially important because the two waters have no ethical motive: this branch’s fundamental meaning is, above all, crossing an appointed limit. In human beings, the same crossing can become aggression, the pursuit of superiority, and social oppression.

Baghyan baynahum is among this branch’s most important Qur’anic expressions. The Qur’an places it after the arrival of knowledge and clear proofs, so the issue is not mere ignorance. Translating it only as “envy” loses part of the word’s range, since baghy can also encompass rivalry, the pursuit of superiority, and mutual transgression.

The sexual branch, too, has its established usage. Baghiyy in Sūrat Maryam designates a woman who engages in illicit sexual relations, while al-bighāʾ in An-Nūr 24:33 refers to an activity linked in that very verse with coercion and the pursuit of financial gain. Through li-tabtaghū, the same verse also preserves the primary branch of seeking alongside it.

Aramaic and Hebrew evidence supports the antiquity of “wanting and seeking,” and the recognized development of earlier ghayn into ʿayin in these languages explains the phonetic relationship between these forms. Meanwhile, the transgression branch is sufficiently independent and established in early Arabic that there is no need to explain it through a hypothetical story of derivation from seeking.

A cautious conclusion for this root is:

B GH Y: a family with two ancient and established branches: seeking, searching, and pursuing; and going beyond a limit, transgressing, and pursuing superiority. A semantic proximity can be seen between the two, but the precise historical path connecting them remains unclear. In the Qur’an, sentence structure and context determine the branch to which each derivative belongs.
