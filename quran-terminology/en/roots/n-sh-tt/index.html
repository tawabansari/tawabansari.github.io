Movement and briskness: drawing out, moving between places, easily released knots, and al-nāshiṭāti nashṭan in the Qur’an

1. Introducing the root and its semantic centers

Arabic N SH Ṭ concerns movement, liveliness, briskness, pulling, and drawing something out. This family also describes tying and releasing a particular kind of knot, moving between territories, and a person’s readiness and willingness to act.

In a familiar use, nashiṭa describes someone ready and willing to undertake a task and pursue it briskly. In this branch, nashāṭ contrasts with sluggishness and lethargy.

Another use of nashaṭa concerns pulling something out, as when a bucket is drawn from a well by its rope.

Nāshiṭ also describes an animal moving from one territory to another, while ṭarīqun nāshiṭ may name a road branching from the main route.

Another branch concerns unshūṭah, a knot tied so that pulling a particular end of the rope readily releases it.

These uses initially look different, but movement, pulling, and changes of position occur in many of them.

They should not all be reduced to one image without examination. Tying and untying are opposite actions; drawing a bucket from a well also differs from an animal moving freely through the desert.

A lexical explanation identifies movement as the family’s general axis:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى اهْتِزَازٍ وَحَرَكَةٍ

aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā ihtizāzin wa-ḥarakatin

Meaning:

“A root denoting movement and motion.”

That explanation can encompass many uses of N SH Ṭ. Its breadth does not, however, make the word “movement” sufficient for translating every verse.

Movement is an important general axis, but pulling, drawing out, briskness, tying, releasing, and changing places are particular applications that require separate recognition.

The Qur’an uses this family only at the beginning of Al-Nāziʿāt; understanding its precise sense requires examining those uses and the verses’ context.

Qur’anic forms

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:2:

وَالنَّاشِطَاتِ نَشْطًا

wa-al-nāshiṭāti nashṭan

The two forms are:

  • Al-nāshiṭāti: the feminine plural active participle of the Form I verb, describing those performing nashṭ.
  • Nashṭan: the verbal noun, expressing the action itself.

There are therefore two lexical occurrences, but only one verse containing the root.

Arabic also has nashāṭ, nashīṭ, anshaṭa, unshūṭah, minshaṭ, munshiṭ, nashīṭah, nashūṭ, and intashaṭa. These do not occur in the Qur’an.

The familiar modern meaning of nashāṭ in Persian and Arabic should therefore not immediately be substituted for Qur’anic al-nāshiṭāt.

2. Early Arabic uses: liveliness, pulling, and movement

Nashiṭa: readiness, willingness, and briskness

Nashiṭa al-rajulu describes a person who is lively and ready to act.

Nashiṭa ilā al-ʿamali can mean turning willingly and briskly to a task.

Nashīṭ likewise describes a person or animal possessing liveliness and readiness.

An early explanation of this use states:

النَّشَاطُ ضِدُّ الْكَسَلِ

al-nashāṭu ḍiddu al-kasali

Meaning:

“Liveliness is the opposite of sluggishness.”

This sense is familiar in Persian too: calling someone active and lively describes greater energy, willingness, or readiness for a task.

Liveliness nevertheless differs from happiness.

A person can be happy but physically unready for a particular task. Someone may also work seriously and briskly without a mood of happiness or amusement.

Nashāṭ in this use should therefore not be restricted to joy or gladness.

Nor does that meaning alone establish that the Qur’anic nāshiṭāt must be happy beings.

Nashaṭa: pulling and drawing out

Another branch uses nashaṭa al-shayʾa for pulling, drawing out, or separating something.

For example:

نَشَطَ الدَّلْوَ مِنَ الْبِئْرِ

nashaṭa al-dalwa mina al-biʾri

Meaning:

“He drew the bucket out of the well.”

Lexical explanations also relate this action to drawing a bucket without a water-lifting pulley.

Some accounts call a well from which the bucket emerges with one pull biʾrun anshāṭun.

By contrast, biʾrun nashūṭun may describe a well requiring repeated pulls to bring the bucket up.

The action of nashṭ is therefore not necessarily always easy or performed in a single movement.

A shallow well may allow the bucket to emerge with one pull. A deeper well requires greater effort for the same action.

Thus, ease or difficulty is not an invariant part of the root’s meaning; it depends on the situation and the object involved.

This matters for Al-Nāziʿāt 79:2, since some translations directly render nashṭan as “gently and easily,” whereas that quality is one contextual interpretation.

Nashaṭa: drawing out and removing a covering

Another use is recorded in early Arabic:

نَشَطْتُ الشَّيْءَ

nashaṭtu al-shayʾa

With the sense “I removed its skin or covering.”

This relates to bringing something out of its covering or position.

Not every removal of a covering should be called nashṭ; Arabic has other roots for peeling and separating.

Here too, movement and separation are prominent, rather than necessarily haste, violence, or destruction of the thing uncovered.

Nashiṭa and nashaṭa: related forms with different uses

Different vowels on shīn can accompany different applications in Arabic.

Nashiṭa with kasrah on shīn commonly concerns a person’s liveliness and readiness.

Nashaṭa with fatḥah on shīn is used for drawing a bucket out and tying a particular kind of knot.

That distinction is not an absolutely fixed boundary without overlap across every dialect and recorded form.

Some dictionaries, for example, transmit certain uses with more than one vocalization.

Analyzing a Qur’anic use thus requires examining its construction and relevant evidence as well as its vowels.

3. Unshūṭah: a knot that readily comes undone

Unshūṭah is one of this family’s important applications.

It names a knot that readily opens when one end of the rope is pulled, like certain knots in clothing cords.

Tying such a knot must be distinguished from undoing it.

Nashaṭa and anshaṭa: tying and releasing

One early account of Arabic usage distinguishes tying from releasing.

Nashaṭa al-ḥabla can mean tying the rope with an unshūṭah knot.

However:

أَنْشَطَ الْعِقَالَ

anshaṭa al-ʿiqāla

Means undoing the cord’s knot by pulling its end until the knot opens.

A lexical explanation also states:

نَشَطَ الْأُنْشُوطَةَ ... عَقَدَهَا وَشَدَّهَا، وَأَنْشَطَهَا حَلَّهَا

nashaṭa al-unshūṭata … ʿaqadahā wa-shaddahā wa-anshaṭahā ḥallahā

Meaning:

“He tied and tightened the slipknot; with anshaṭa, he released it.”

This matters because some explanations of al-nāshiṭāt use the image of undoing a knot.

Qur’anic al-nāshiṭāt belongs to Form I, however, and should not be equated without examination with the Form IV active participle munshiṭāt.

Recorded Arabic does contain overlap between these constructions, and some lexicographers also explain nashaṭa through releasing and loosening.

The morphological difference therefore does not make the knot-release image impossible, but prevents declaring it the verse’s sole certain direct meaning.

Is undoing a knot the historical core of every meaning?

Quickly releasing a knot and freeing the rope can be connected imaginatively with movement and briskness.

Something emerging from a bond or restriction likewise fits movement toward a new position.

Such figurative resemblance does not establish the historical origin of every sense.

Human liveliness, animal movement, drawing buckets, and the name unshūṭah cannot all certainly be derived from one particular use concerning knots.

The evidence establishes that these applications belong to one linguistic family.

Their historical sequence requires earlier, independent evidence.

4. Nāshiṭ: movement between places

Nashaṭa mina al-makāni is recorded for leaving a place.

Nashaṭa min baladin ilā baladin also describes moving from one territory to another.

This relates to briskness and movement, but is not restricted to pulling ropes or knots.

The wandering wild bull

Early Arabic uses al-thawru al-nāshiṭu for a wild bull moving from one region to another.

The same description is also recorded for some other desert animals.

A poetic example from Dhū al-Rummah

Dhū al-Rummah, an Umayyad poet, describes a wild bull:

أَذَاكَ أَمْ نَمِشٌ بِالْوَشْيِ أَكْرُعُهُ
مُسَفَّعُ الْخَدِّ هَادٍ نَاشِطٌ شَبَبُ

a-dhāka am namishun bi-al-washyi akruʿuhu / musaffaʿu al-khaddi hādin nāshiṭun shababu

A translation close to the sense:

“Is that [mount] like this, or a wild bull with patterned lower legs and darkened cheek—a leading, wandering animal in its prime?”

Nāshiṭ here describes an animal characterized by movement between places.

It demonstrates that nāshiṭ need not mean someone who releases a knot or draws out a bucket.

Dhū al-Rummah lived after the Qur’an’s revelation. His poetry is evidence for classical Arabic, rather than direct pre-Islamic evidence.

The verse does not establish that the Qur’anic nāshiṭāt must be wild cattle.

Poetry illustrates the word’s linguistic possibilities; identifying its Qur’anic referent requires independent examination.

Branching roads and watercourses

Ṭarīqun nāshiṭ describes a road branching to the right or left of the main route.

Al-nawāshiṭ is likewise used for subsidiary paths and watercourses departing from the main course.

Here the word concerns movement and a new direction.

A road does not move by its own intention like a person or animal.

Nashṭ can therefore sometimes describe a route’s course and departure from the main path.

An Arabic active participle consequently need not always describe an agent with personal intention and will.

A poetic example from al-Ṭirimmāḥ

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ ibn Ḥakīm, an Umayyad poet, says:

وَهَلْ بِحَلِيفِ الْخَيْلِ مِمَّنْ عَهِدْتُهُ
بِهِ غَيْرُ أُحْدَانَ النَّوَاشِطِ رُوعُ

wa-hal bi-ḥalīfi al-khayli mimman ʿahidtuhu / bihi ghayru uḥdāni al-nawāshiṭi rūʿu

“Of those I once knew there, is anything left to stir the mind except solitary wandering wild cattle?”

This verse is transmitted to explain the Arabic use of al-nawāshiṭ.

Its explanation identifies al-nawāshiṭ as wild cattle moving from one territory to another.

Here, then, al-nawāshiṭ describes moving, wandering animals.

Like Dhū al-Rummah’s example, this verse belongs to the period after the Qur’an’s revelation.

Its value lies in showing an established linguistic use, rather than proving that the nāshiṭāt in Al-Nāziʿāt are those animals.

5. Pulling, biting, and swift seizure

Other Arabic uses of N SH Ṭ concern pulling, separation, and swift movement.

Biting and a sudden thrust

Nashaṭahu fī janbihi is recorded in early Arabic for striking or thrusting something into someone’s side.

Nashaṭat-hu al-ḥayyatu also means that a snake bit him.

These applications involve sudden movement and harmful contact.

They do not establish that nashṭ inherently means killing, punishment, or violence.

Many other uses concern ordinary movement, readiness for work, or an easily released knot.

Biting and thrusting are therefore particular branches, rather than definitions of the entire family.

Nashīṭah: something acquired on the way

Early Arabic also uses al-nashīṭah for property acquired by a group while traveling, before reaching its intended destination.

The use is known from accounts of war booty and its division in pre-Islamic Arab society.

A poetic example from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAnamah al-Ḍabbī

A verse attributed to ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAnamah al-Ḍabbī reads:

لَكَ الْمِرْبَاعُ مِنْهَا وَالصَّفَايَا
وَحُكْمُكَ وَالنَّشِيطَةُ وَالْفُضُولُ

laka al-mirbāʿu minhā wa-al-ṣafāyā / wa-ḥukmuka wa-al-nashīṭatu wa-al-fuḍūlu

A translation close to the sense:

“Yours are its quarter-share, the choice items, your decision, the gains taken on the way, and the remainder after division.”

Al-nashīṭah here names a share or property obtained while the group was moving.

ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAnamah was a poet who lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This verse is transmitted in the literary tradition of praising or recalling Bisṭām ibn Qays.

Bisṭām’s place in pre-Islamic history makes the example socially significant. The precise date of composition and details of its transmission should nevertheless remain distinct from the poet’s general attribution.

Nashīṭah thus had a particular technical meaning in that setting.

That use should not be imposed on Al-Nāziʿāt. Qur’anic al-nāshiṭāt does not mean booty or a group leader’s share.

Recording a historical term also does not endorse every practice it described morally or legally.

Worries that pull a person in different directions

Arabic poetry also uses N SH Ṭ for worries and thoughts that disturb a person’s rest.

A verse attributed to Himyān ibn Quḥāfah reads:

أَمْسَتْ هُمُومِي تَنْشَطُ الْمَنَاشِطَا
الشَّأْمَ بِي طَوْرًا وَطَوْرًا وَاسِطَا

amsat humūmī tanshaṭu al-manāshiṭā / al-shaʾma bī ṭawran wa-ṭawran wāsiṭā

A translation close to the sense:

“My worries pull me in different directions, sometimes toward Syria, sometimes toward Wāsiṭ.”

The poet likens worries to something drawing a person from place to place.

This connects with pulling and causing movement.

Worry and sorrow are not fixed meanings of N SH Ṭ. The poet uses movement and pulling to portray his inner condition.

This example also belongs to Arabic of the Islamic period and should not be presented as certainly pre-Qur’anic.

6. Qur’anic use: Al-Nāziʿāt 79:2

وَالنَّاشِطَاتِ نَشْطًا

wa-al-nāshiṭāti nashṭan

A translation following one of the root’s general senses:

“And by those that move briskly.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:2

This is the only Qur’anic verse containing N SH Ṭ.

It places two forms of one family together: a plural active participle and the same verb’s verbal noun.

The phrase itself does not name the beings or things described.

A. Morphology and syntax

The opening wa is an oath particle.

Al-nāshiṭāti is a sound feminine plural active participle, genitive after the oath particle.

Nashṭan is an accusative verbal noun functioning as a cognate accusative in this construction.

A rendering close to the Arabic construction is therefore:

“By those performing nashṭ—a performing of nashṭ.” This structural rendering shows the repeated verbal noun without determining the action’s final sense.

Nashṭan emphasizes the action itself.

The verbal noun alone does not specify that the action must be gentle, easy, painful, or severe.

For example, one can say “a pulling they pulled,” but repeating the verbal noun does not determine the force or quality of the pulling.

The cognate accusative likewise foregrounds nashṭ here, rather than necessarily adding “gently” to its meaning.

B. Does the feminine form indicate sex?

Al-nāshiṭāt is grammatically feminine plural.

That feature alone does not establish that its referents are biologically female beings.

Arabic feminine plurals can describe groups whose names or descriptions are treated as grammatically feminine.

Some descriptive constructions also omit the noun, leaving only its qualifying description.

The form therefore does not establish that the verse must concern women or female angels.

Identifying the referent requires context and independent evidence.

7. The opening verses and al-nāshiṭāt

Understanding Qur’anic N SH Ṭ requires reading the first five verses together:

وَالنَّازِعَاتِ غَرْقًا

wa-al-nāziʿāti gharqan

“And by those that draw out, drawing to the utmost.”

وَالنَّاشِطَاتِ نَشْطًا

wa-al-nāshiṭāti nashṭan

“And by those that move briskly.”

وَالسَّابِحَاتِ سَبْحًا

wa-al-sābiḥāti sabḥan

“And by those that swim, a swimming.”

فَالسَّابِقَاتِ سَبْقًا

fa-al-sābiqāti sabqan

“Then those that go ahead, a going ahead.”

فَالْمُدَبِّرَاتِ أَمْرًا

fa-al-mudabbirāti amran

“Then those that direct an affair.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:1; Al-Nāziʿāt 79:2; Al-Nāziʿāt 79:3; Al-Nāziʿāt 79:4; Al-Nāziʿāt 79:5

The sequence presents five descriptions or actions: drawing out, nashṭ, swimming, preceding, and directing affairs.

The first four expressions contain accusative verbal nouns, but their analysis is not identical. Nashṭan, sabḥan, and sabqan are cognate with the preceding descriptions. Gharqan belongs to another root and has been analyzed as a verbal noun expressing thoroughness in drawing out, or as a circumstantial expression.

In the fifth verse, amran is the object of the active participle al-mudabbirāt.

The regular construction foregrounds a sequence of movements or activities.

The construction alone does not conclusively determine whether all the descriptions concern one group or several different groups.

A. Nashṭ and nazʿ

The first verse says:

وَالنَّازِعَاتِ غَرْقًا

wa-al-nāziʿāti gharqan

“And by those that draw out, drawing to the utmost.”

The second says:

وَالنَّاشِطَاتِ نَشْطًا

wa-al-nāshiṭāti nashṭan

“And by those that move briskly.”

N Z ʿ concerns drawing out and separating.

Some uses of N SH Ṭ also concern pulling and drawing out.

The roots can therefore come close within the semantic field of pulling and movement.

That proximity does not make them synonymous.

Nazʿ can emphasize pulling something out with effort and force.

Nashṭ can concern pulling, brisk movement, release from a bond, or moving between places.

Some exegetical explanations relate the two verses to two ways of taking souls: extracting unbelievers’ souls with difficulty and drawing believers’ souls out easily.

That reading is intelligible as an image, but these two expressions do not themselves mention unbelievers, believers, souls, or angels.

The distinction between gharqan and nashṭan alone also does not establish that one necessarily means torment and the other peace.

Such specifications require independent exegetical argument.

B. The third, fourth, and fifth verses

Al-sābiḥāt belongs to S B Ḥ and may concern swimming or fluid movement.

Al-sābiqāt belongs to S B Q and concerns going ahead.

Al-mudabbirāt belongs to D B R and concerns directing and arranging affairs.

Nashṭ, sabḥ, and sabq can be connected through movement and advancement.

Directing affairs, however, is not identical with bodily motion.

Shared imagery alone therefore cannot establish that all these words describe one kind of physical movement.

Fa at the beginning of the fourth and fifth verses may indicate a closer connection or sequence between the subsequent descriptions. Without examining syntax and context, however, it cannot establish a definite sequence of angelic missions or celestial movements.

C. The connection with resurrection

After these oaths comes:

يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ الرَّاجِفَةُ

yawma tarjufu al-rājifatu

“On the day when the quaking one quakes.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:6

Then:

تَتْبَعُهَا الرَّادِفَةُ

tatbaʿuhā al-rādifatu

“The following one follows it.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:7

The following verses also concern frightened hearts, questions about returning after death, and decayed bones.

The opening of the surah therefore belongs to a resurrection context.

Placing the oaths before resurrection verses does not, however, establish that al-nāshiṭāt must be angels of death.

The oath’s subject can relate to the surah’s message without its referent being expressly named.

Each proposed reading should therefore be assessed for how well it explains the whole construction and context.

8. Main readings in the exegetical tradition

First reading: angels drawing out souls

One familiar interpretation identifies al-nāshiṭāt as angels drawing people’s souls out at death.

Some accounts describe this as easy and gentle for believers’ souls.

The explanation is connected with the image of a knot opening or a bucket being drawn out.

In this reading, nāshiṭāt describes agents performing the extraction.

Other Qur’anic verses do connect angels with taking people’s souls.

Al-Naḥl 16:32 includes:

الَّذِينَ تَتَوَفَّاهُمُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ طَيِّبِينَ

alladhīna tatawaffāhumu al-malāʾikatu ṭayyibīna

“Those whom the angels take in death while they are good and pure.”

Al-Sajdah 32:11 states:

قُلْ يَتَوَفَّاكُمْ مَلَكُ الْمَوْتِ الَّذِي وُكِّلَ بِكُمْ

qul yatawaffākum malaku al-mawti alladhī wukkila bikum

“Say: the angel of death entrusted with you takes you in death.”

The basic attribution of taking souls to angels thus has Qur’anic support.

Those verses do not explicitly identify those angels as al-nāshiṭāt in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:2.

This remains a possible reading for examination, rather than the only proven literal meaning.

Second reading: the souls themselves, or death

Some transmitted explanations relate nāshiṭāt to souls leaving the body at death.

Another identifies death as the agent drawing out the soul.

These readings differ grammatically and semantically.

If the nāshiṭāt are souls, the action may be their departure from the body.

If the description concerns the agent taking souls, extraction becomes more prominent.

The Qur’anic expression itself, however, mentions neither arwāḥ, “souls,” nor mawt, “death.”

Choosing between them requires independent evidence.

Third reading: stars

An early exegetical reading identifies al-nāshiṭāt as stars.

On this reading, stars move between horizons or appear and disappear along recognized celestial paths.

This connects with nashaṭa min baladin ilā baladin, moving between territories.

The Qur’an also describes the movement of celestial bodies through words such as sabḥ.

Yā Sīn 36:40 includes:

وَكُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ

wa-kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūna

“Each swims in an orbit.”

The stars reading can therefore be examined through the sense of movement and comparison with al-sābiḥāt.

That resemblance alone does not establish that nāshiṭāt must be stars.

The verse itself does not name stars or celestial orbits.

Fourth reading: ropes and hunting loops

Another early explanation identifies al-nāshiṭāt with al-awhāq.

Wahaq names a rope with a loop or knot for catching an animal.

This relates to pulling and easily released knots.

Hunting ropes and loops nevertheless represent only one possible application of N SH Ṭ.

The verse contains no explicit word for a rope, loop, or hunting.

That reading should therefore not be imposed on the base translation.

Fifth reading: animals moving between places

The established use al-thawru al-nāshiṭu allows al-nāshiṭāt to be associated with animals moving between territories.

The poetic examples from Dhū al-Rummah and al-Ṭirimmāḥ support that use.

A lexical application to animals alone does not establish that the verse has those animals as its referent.

The possibility occurs in the exegetical tradition, but a conclusive identification requires further evidence.

Assessing the readings

One early assessment does not restrict the oath’s meaning to a single referent.

It takes nāshiṭāt as beings or things that move from place to place, and does not restrict the oath to one group without decisive evidence.

That judgment matters methodologically.

Two levels should nevertheless remain distinct:

First, the lexical range of nāshiṭ and nashṭ in Arabic.

Second, the particular referent of al-nāshiṭāt in the verse.

The fact that the word can be explained in relation to angels, animals, stars, or ropes does not establish that the verse necessarily intends all of them simultaneously.

The verse might have a particular referent clear to its first audience, while the available evidence does not conclusively identify it for us.

It is therefore preferable to preserve the lexical meaning and openly acknowledge disagreement about the referent.

9. Comparison with related roots

N SH Ṭ and N Z ʿ: pulling and drawing out

N Z ʿ concerns drawing out, uprooting, and separating something from its position.

N SH Ṭ approaches the same field in applications such as drawing a bucket out.

N SH Ṭ also has uses concerning liveliness, movement between territories, and easily released knots.

N Z ʿ, meanwhile, describes removing sovereignty, removing resentment, drawing out a hand, and disputes between people in the Qur’an.

The roots occur in consecutive opening verses of Al-Nāziʿāt. Their proximity alone does not establish that one is always forceful and the other always gentle.

Some interpretations may propose that contrast, but it should not become the roots’ certain general definition.

Study of N Z ʿ

N SH Ṭ and N SH ʾ: movement and origination

N SH ʾ concerns coming into existence, beginning, growing, and bringing into existence.

N SH Ṭ more particularly concerns movement, briskness, and pulling.

Beginning a task can involve liveliness, but the two roots do not mean the same thing.

Nāshiṭāt belongs to N SH Ṭ and should not be confused with nāshiʾah from N SH ʾ.

Study of N SH ʾ

N SH Ṭ and N SH R: briskness and spreading

N SH R concerns opening, spreading, dispersing, and, in some uses, rising to life after death.

Moving between places in N SH Ṭ can also involve dispersal or displacement.

Nashr emphasizes spreading and opening, whereas nashṭ can concern briskness, pulling, movement, and release from a knot.

The roots can have related outcomes without being complete synonyms.

Study of N SH R

N SH Ṭ and N SH Z: movement and rising

N SH Z concerns rising and prominence, and in another application marital discord.

N SH Ṭ also concerns movement.

The N SH Z family, however, foregrounds elevation or departure from a previous position in its terrain and rising applications.

Nashṭ more particularly concerns briskness, pulling, and active movement.

That general resemblance does not make Qur’anic al-nāshiṭāt mean “those rising from a gathering” or “the disobedient.”

Study of N SH Z

N SH Ṭ and S B Ḥ: movement and swimming

Some applications of S B Ḥ concern fluid movement in water or space.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:3 follows nāshiṭāt immediately:

وَالسَّابِحَاتِ سَبْحًا

wa-al-sābiḥāti sabḥan

“And by those that swim, a swimming.”

That sequence may indicate a thematic connection through movement and advancement.

Sabḥ and nashṭ are nevertheless not synonymous.

Some nashṭ applications foreground pulling and briskness; sabḥ foregrounds fluid movement or swimming.

Their proximity does not determine a single kind of physical motion for every opening verse.

Study of S B Ḥ

10. Translation questions and proposals for Forqan

The main difficulty is the root’s multiple lexical applications and the absence of an explicitly named referent for the active participle.

“Angels gently taking believers’ souls” adds several interpretive elements: angels, souls, believers, and gentleness.

“Stars moving from one celestial station to another” adds stars as the referent and the image of celestial stations.

“Those undoing knots” makes one particular lexical use, especially Form IV, govern the entire expression.

“The lively ones” alone may suggest happy people to a Persian reader, a sense not established by the verse’s context.

Translation should therefore remain faithful to movement and pulling while avoiding a certain identification not expressed in the phrase itself.

Proposed renderings of Al-Nāziʿāt 79:2

By stars moving between horizonsAn interpretation for examination; the referent is not explicit

For Forqan’s base translation, a preferable proposal is:

“And by those that move briskly.”

It conveys a recognized lexical field without deciding the referent beforehand.

It should nevertheless be clear that this too is one linguistic choice, which does not convey every possibility of pulling, drawing out, and releasing.

The lexical explanation can add:

“Al-nāshiṭāt: those performing nashṭ, an action associated in Arabic with brisk movement, pulling and drawing out, moving between places, and in some uses release from a bond or knot.”

This explanation preserves the semantic range and leaves identification for separate examination.

Nashṭan in this verse

Nashṭan is an accusative verbal noun functioning as a cognate accusative.

Its direct role is therefore to emphasize the action.

It should not be translated independently of the active participle as gentleness, kindness, happiness, or suffering.

If the angelic extraction reading is adopted, an easy or gentle quality may enter its explanation. That quality is not established by the verbal noun alone.

In the stars reading, it likewise emphasizes their movement or change of place, without independently specifying an orbit, astronomical speed, or time of movement.

11. Conclusion

Arabic N SH Ṭ has diverse applications involving movement, briskness, readiness, pulling and drawing out, changing places, and tying or releasing easily undone knots.

A lexical explanation treats movement as an important general axis of the family.

That connects with human liveliness, animal movement, drawing buckets, and changes in a rope’s state.

All these uses should not be confined to a single sense such as happiness, knot-release, or drawing out.

Arabic records nashiṭa for willingness and readiness to act, and nashaṭa for pulling and certain other actions.

Unshūṭah names a readily released knot. One early account describes tying it with nashaṭa and releasing it with anshaṭa.

That morphological distinction should remain intact; the constructions should not be conflated without examination.

Early nāshiṭ also describes an animal moving between territories, as the poetry of Dhū al-Rummah and al-Ṭirimmāḥ illustrates.

Other recorded applications include drawing buckets, biting, separating something, and seizing property on the way.

None of these examples alone conclusively identifies the Qur’anic al-nāshiṭāt.

N SH Ṭ occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:2:

وَالنَّاشِطَاتِ نَشْطًا

wa-al-nāshiṭāti nashṭan

Al-nāshiṭāt describes those performing nashṭ, while nashṭan emphatically expresses the action.

The phrase does not name the referent.

The exegetical tradition relates the description to angels, souls, death, stars, hunting loops, and moving animals.

Angels taking souls fits other Qur’anic verses concerning death. That compatibility does not make the identification conclusive.

The stars reading can also be examined through movement between horizons and other verses about celestial motion. The verse itself, however, does not name stars.

Lexical meaning, morphology, the oath sequence, and identification of the referent should therefore remain distinct.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

The general axis of N SH Ṭ concerns movement, briskness, and active motion. Its established Arabic branches also describe pulling and drawing something out, moving between places, and tying or releasing a particular readily undone knot.

For people, nashāṭ means willingness and readiness to act, rather than happiness alone.

Qur’anic al-nāshiṭāt describes those performing nashṭ, while nashṭan names the action itself.

The verse does not explicitly identify these agents as angels, souls, stars, or other beings.

For the root study, brisk movement and pulling or drawing out are important, defensible senses. The verse’s final referent requires independent argument from the Qur’anic evidence.

The base translation should avoid adding unnamed referents or turning lexical meaning into a conclusive exegetical theory.
