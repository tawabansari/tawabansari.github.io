The Qur’anic Root H N N

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(wa-ḥanānan)

From longing and emotional attraction to affection, compassion, and mercy

The Arabic root ح ن ن concerns longing, emotional attraction, tenderness, and compassion. حَنَّ describes someone yearning for a person or thing; حَنِين denotes an inward longing sometimes accompanied by a cry or moan; and حَنَان denotes affection, compassion, and caring attention toward another.

One established early Arabic example is a she-camel separated from her young and yearning toward it. The longing may accompany a drawn-out sound, but does not always depend on sound. People can likewise silently yearn for their homeland, family, or a distant friend.

When this emotional inclination takes the form of attention, kindness, and care for someone else, حَنَان enters the field of mercy and compassion.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as حَنَانًا in Maryam 19:13. The word describes a divine gift in John’s story and appears with زَكَاةً and تَقِيًّا.

Qur’anic form: وَحَنَانًا in Maryam 19:13; an indefinite accusative noun from ح ن ن. Al-Ṭabarī identifies حَنان as the verbal noun of حَنَّ عليه; “verbal noun” is therefore more precise here than restricting it to the technical category ism maṣdar.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole ḥanān occurrence in Maryam 19:13

Arabic also records حَنَّ, يَحِنُّ, حَنِين, حَنَان, حَنُون, حَنَّان, حَانّ, تَحَنَّنَ, تَحَنُّن, اسْتَحَنَّ, تَحَانَّ, حَنَّانَة, and حَنَانَيْكَ; these do not occur as Qur’anic derivatives in these exact forms.

1. The root’s semantic center in early Arabic

Longing and tenderness

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for this family: الْإِشْفَاقُ وَالرِّقَّةُ, compassion and tenderness.

He explains that this condition can accompany a sound expressing distress or sorrow.

In this analysis, sound is not itself the root’s central meaning, but can be the outward sign of an inward condition.

For example, a she-camel separated from her young may call with a distinctive sound. If she yearns toward it silently, the longing nevertheless remains.

Lisān al-ʿArab records both uses: yearning with sound and yearning without sound.

Defining ḥanīn solely as a moan or sound would therefore fail to explain silent human longing for one’s homeland or loved ones.

Conversely, defining it simply as love would lose the attraction, emotional response, and desire for closeness.

In the branch of longing, ḥanīn is an emotional inclination toward a person or thing important to a human or animal; it may become audible.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: compassion and tenderness, audible or silent

حَنَّ إِلَيْهِ: yearning toward another

حَنَّ إِلَىٰ فُلَانٍ means yearning toward someone.

حَنَّ إِلَىٰ وَطَنِهِ describes someone longing for their homeland.

The preposition إلى specifies the direction of the longing.

Someone may yearn for a childhood home, a distant companion, or a place associated with important memories.

The verb حنّ itself does not mean physically returning. A person may long to return without yet taking any action.

Nor does longing always show that its object is right or valuable. Someone may also feel attached to a harmful habit or an unhealthy environment.

Ḥanīn expresses emotional attraction; its value and direction require separate assessment.

حَنَّ عَلَيْهِ: compassion and kindness

In Arabic, حَنَّ عَلَىٰ فُلَانٍ means showing kindness and compassion toward another.

The preposition على here expresses caring attention toward someone.

تَحَنَّنَ عَلَيْهِ likewise means acting kindly and compassionately.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains this through تَعَطَّفَ عَلَيْهِ and رَحِمَهُ.

For example, a mother who attends to her child’s condition and seeks to meet its needs can be described through this family.

Kindness is not confined to mother and child. Someone can show compassion toward a relative, friend, person in need, or stranger.

The direction of the relationship distinguishes this usage from حنّ إليه.

حنّ إليه emphasizes longing and attraction toward someone or something loved.

حنّ عليه emphasizes attention and kindness toward another.

Both can occur within one relationship, but they do not mean precisely the same thing.

حَنَان: affection and compassion

Early dictionaries explain حَنَان as mercy, compassion, and tenderness.

In Al-ʿAyn, Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad explains الْحَنَانُ as mercy.

Ibn Fāris records the same meaning, citing Maryam 19:13.

Lisān al-ʿArab also includes love, compassion, and tenderness of heart alongside mercy.

Ḥanān is therefore more than longing to see someone; it can describe turning toward another with affection and compassion.

It should not, however, be restricted to inward feeling. Compassion can become visible in conduct, support, care, and an appropriate manner of responding.

Nor does the word itself mean giving money or performing a particular act. Giving, care, or assistance may express ḥanān while retaining their own meanings.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr, and Lisān al-ʿArab: ḥanna ilayhi and ḥanna ʿalayhi

حَنُون and حَنَّان

حَنُون denotes someone kind and compassionate.

The description is established for a mother or anyone acting with attention and compassion.

حَنَّان can likewise denote someone exceptionally kind or abundant in compassion.

The forms’ structures and usages relate to the intensity or persistence of a quality, but a pattern alone cannot measure someone’s actual kindness.

Lexical sources also record الْحَنَّان as a divine description.

The Qur’an does not, however, contain الْحَنَّان as an independent divine name. Maryam 19:13 has حَنَانًا مِنْ لَدُنَّا.

A lexical description must therefore be distinguished from a name explicitly appearing in the Qur’an.

حَنَانَكَ and حَنَانَيْكَ

Early Arabic uses حَنَانَكَ in requests for kindness and mercy.

حَنَانَيْكَ is likewise recorded for requesting repeated affection and attention.

Ibn Fāris explains it through حَنَانًا بَعْدَ حَنَانٍ: kindness after kindness.

Grammatical sources disagree about the form حنانيك.

Some understand it as the dual of حنان; others regard it as an established expression of request and emphasis.

Both explanations nevertheless convey a request for compassion and mercy.

The usage has an independent witness in pre-Islamic poetry.

Al-Ṭabarī: ḥanān as a verbal noun and the grammatical disagreement about ḥanānayka

2. Ḥanīn in the sounds and behavior of animals and objects

A she-camel’s cry and longing for her young

حَنِينُ النَّاقَةِ is a prominent early Arabic use of this family.

A report from Al-Layth in Tahdhīb al-Lughah explains a she-camel’s ḥanīn as the sound she makes when yearning for her young.

The same report explicitly states that it can also denote her inclination toward her young without an audible sound.

The distinction matters for understanding the root.

A camel’s cry may signal longing, but longing itself is not a sound.

Ruʾbah ibn Al-ʿAjjāj, an Islamic-period poet, says:

حَنَّتْ قَلُوصِي أَمْسِ بِالْأُرْدُنِّ
حِنِّي فَمَا ظُلِّمْتِ أَنْ تَحِنِّي

ḥannat qalūṣī amsi bi-l-urdunni / ḥinnī fa-mā ẓullimti an taḥinnī

“My young she-camel cried with longing yesterday in Jordan. Cry with longing: you are not at fault for yearning.” Tahdhīb al-Lughah transmits ظُلِّمْتِ with a doubled lām, meaning being deemed at fault or unjust. The line should therefore not simply be read as reporting that the camel has not suffered injustice. Some transmissions also have an undoubled reading.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: Al-Layth’s report and Ruʾbah’s witness with ẓullimti

حَنَّتْ here describes a she-camel displaying her longing.

Lisān al-ʿArab and other lexical sources transmit this witness.

Ruʾbah belongs to the Islamic period, so the verse is not a certainly pre-Islamic witness, though it illustrates established usage in early literary Arabic.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ẓ L M: ẓallamahu as deeming someone unjust

حَنُون: a sounding wind

Arabic uses رِيحٌ حَنُونٌ for wind whose sound resembles a camel’s yearning cry.

The wind need not possess feeling or longing.

The word is applied through similarity of sound.

A family can thus extend from an emotional condition and its accompanying sound to a similar sound in nature.

حَنُون describing wind does not imply human or animal compassion and affection in the wind itself.

قَوْسٌ حَنَّانَةٌ: the sound of a bow

قَوْسٌ حَنَّانَةٌ denotes a bow producing a distinctive sound when its string is drawn or released.

Ibn Fāris mentions it alongside wind and a she-camel’s yearning cry.

The audible meaning is prominent here too.

A bow has neither human homesickness nor an affectionate reason for sounding.

A resemblance in the sound’s quality allows derivatives of this family to describe it.

This illustrates an extension of the audible meaning, but it should not be transferred to حنانا in Maryam 19:13.

That verse concerns a divine gift in John’s story, rather than the sound of a bow, wind, or animal.

More restricted usages

Lexical sources record حَنَّةُ الرَّجُلِ for a man’s wife. Ibn Fāris explains it through the attraction and affection between spouses.

The written form حنة is also recorded in some lexical usages for a woman’s head covering; this restricted usage should not be transferred to Qur’anic حنان.

طَرِيقٌ حَنَّانٌ is also recorded for a clear path. Ibn Fāris treats this as a usage not readily explained through the principle of kindness and tenderness.

Not every form recorded in a dictionary should therefore be added to the Qur’anic meaning of حنان without evidence.

The personal name حَنَّة is also known from historical reports, but the Qur’an does not name Mary’s mother in Āl ʿImrān 3:35.

Her reported historical name cannot therefore be counted as another textual Qur’anic occurrence of ح ن ن.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: wife, wind, bow, and the exceptional clear path

3. A pre-Islamic witness: requesting kindness in Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd addresses ʿAmr ibn Hind:

أَبَا مُنْذِرٍ أَفْنَيْتَ فَاسْتَبْقِ بَعْضَنَا
حَنَانَيْكَ بَعْضُ الشَّرِّ أَهْوَنُ مِنْ بَعْضِ

abā mundhirin afnayta fa-stabqi baʿḍanā / ḥanānayka baʿḍu al-sharri ahwanu min baʿḍi

“Abū Mundhir, you have brought us to destruction; leave at least some of us alive. Show us mercy: some evils are lighter than others.”

The verse is recorded in Ṭarafah’s Dīwān, and Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Fāris also cite it to explain حنان.

حَنَانَيْكَ requests kindness and forbearance here.

The poet asks someone powerful to moderate his conduct rather than make everyone suffer the consequences of a decision.

The usage matters because it does not restrict ḥanān to someone’s longing to see another.

Here ḥanān means affection and compassion that may become visible in a powerful person’s changed conduct toward others.

Lexically, حنانيك accompanies a request for mercy and gentler treatment.

This does not mean that every instance of ḥanān necessarily entails foregoing punishment or abandoning justice.

The poet requests forbearance because of his circumstances. That referent is understood from the poem, rather than because حنان always means pardoning an offense.

Unlike many later verses, this witness is recorded in a pre-Islamic poet’s Dīwān and provides useful support for the pre-Qur’anic use of affection and compassion.

Al-Ṭabarī: Ṭarafah’s witness and the request for compassion

4. وَحَنَانًا مِنْ لَدُنَّا in Maryam 19:13

وَحَنَانًا مِنْ لَدُنَّا وَزَكَاةً وَكَانَ تَقِيًّا

wa-ḥanānan min ladunnā wa-zakātan wa-kāna taqiyyan

Translation: “And tenderness from Us, and purity; and he was reverently mindful.”

Maryam 19:13

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of ح ن ن.

The verse continues John’s story.

Maryam 19:12 commands John to hold firmly to the Book and then states that The God gave him حُكْم while still a child.

Maryam 19:13 introduces حَنَانًا and زَكَاةً, then describes John as تَقِيًّا.

Maryam 19:14 also describes his goodness toward his parents and denies arrogant, disobedient conduct of him.

This continuity matters for identifying the object of the tenderness.

The structure of حَنَانًا

حَنَانًا is an indefinite accusative noun. In Al-Ṭabarī’s explanation, حنان is the verbal noun of حَنَّ عليه, showing affection and compassion.

Al-Ṭabarī: ḥanān is the verbal noun of ḥanna ʿalayhi

In a common grammatical analysis, the word is coordinated with الْحُكْمَ in the preceding verse: just as The God gave John judgment, tenderness and purity are also presented as divine gifts.

مِنْ لَدُنَّا means “from Us,” identifying the gift’s divine source.

زَكَاةً is likewise joined to حَنَانًا by a conjunction.

حكم, حنان, and زكاة therefore stand close together structurally, but their meanings differ.

حكم concerns judgment and wisdom or an authoritative position that requires examination under its own root.

حنان denotes tenderness and compassion.

زكاة belongs to ز ك و and concerns purity and growth.

Study of Z K W

Reverent mindfulness is also expressed through a distinct word at the verse’s end.

These qualities should therefore not all be folded into حنان itself.

First reading: divine affection and mercy toward John

One established reading understands حَنَانًا مِنْ لَدُنَّا as The God’s kindness and favor toward John.

On this reading, The God has embraced John in particular care and mercy.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits Mujāhid’s explanation تَعَطُّفًا مِنْ رَبِّهِ عَلَيْهِ: kindness from his Lord toward him.

Another report likewise explains ḥanān as love.

This reading fits مِنْ لَدُنَّا, identifying The God as the source of the kindness.

The surrounding verses also describe particular gifts concerning John.

This should not be understood as The God yearning for John as a human might after experiencing separation or emotional need.

Human longing can involve need, absence, or lack of knowledge; such qualities do not follow from حنانا من لدنا.

The contextual meaning is divine favor, compassion, and care.

Second reading: tenderness bestowed upon John

Another reading is also recorded in early sources. Al-Qurṭubī presents both divine tenderness toward John and John’s tenderness toward people, also citing Al-Zamakhsharī on his care for parents and others.

On this reading, The God endowed John with affection and compassion so that he would treat people, particularly his parents, with care.

حنان is a quality placed within John by The God.

The explanation fits the coordination of حنانا with الحكم, since both concern a gift to John.

Maryam 19:14, immediately describing John’s goodness toward his parents, also suits this reading.

Tenderness must nevertheless be distinguished from goodness toward parents.

بَرًّا بِوَالِدَيْهِ belongs to ب ر ر and concerns treating one’s parents well.

حنان concerns affection and compassion.

Tenderness may manifest in good treatment of parents, but the words are not complete synonyms.

The second reading can therefore fit the context without restricting حنان to goodness toward parents alone.

Al-Qurṭubī: both directions of tenderness and Al-Zamakhsharī’s explanation

Is either reading certain?

The verse’s structure clearly presents ḥanān as something from The God.

Yet وَحَنَانًا مِنْ لَدُنَّا does not independently name the tenderness’s recipient as an explicit object.

Exegetical sources consequently present two directions: divine favor toward John, and tenderness bestowed upon John.

These need not contradict one another. Someone may both receive divine favor and act kindly toward others.

Their compatibility does not mean that the verse explicitly and equally states both.

The basic translation should preserve حنان and its divine source without limiting the object of the affection through an added explanation.

The commentary can then examine both readings through their evidence and relative strength.

Tenderness and purity

Maryam 19:13 places حَنَانًا alongside زَكَاةً.

Affection and compassion can relate to purity and moral growth, but they are not identical.

Someone may feel compassion yet make mistakes in how they help another person.

Nor does right conduct always begin with intense emotion: someone may act properly from knowledge of rights and justice.

The association of tenderness and purity should therefore not imply that either replaces the other.

The verse expresses them separately, then describes John’s reverent mindfulness.

It introduces related qualities, rather than one word repeated through synonyms.

Does ḥanān mean unconditional approval of others’ conduct?

In human usage, ḥanān involves tenderness and caring attention.

Kindness does not necessarily mean approving every action or overlooking every wrong.

A parent, for example, may compassionately prevent a child from acting harmfully.

When based on understanding, justice, and appropriate methods, such prevention can be compatible with affection.

Someone may likewise work to meet another person’s needs without endorsing their wrongdoing.

Ḥanān therefore does not mean that a kind person cannot judge, warn, or resist injustice.

In John’s story, tenderness also appears alongside judgment, purity, and reverent mindfulness.

That continuity does not fit a reading that opposes kindness to sound discernment and moral responsibility.

5. حُنَيْن in Al-Tawbah 9:25: written resemblance and a place-name

Al-Tawbah 9:25 contains يَوْمَ حُنَيْنٍ.

حُنَيْن is an established place-name to which a historical event is connected.

Al-Ṭabarī identifies it as a valley between Mecca and Al-Ṭāʾif.

Its consonants resemble ح ن ن, and some analyses of the name may explore a possible relationship with this family.

Two points must nevertheless remain clear.

First, حُنَيْن in this verse is a proper place-name, rather than حَنِين meaning longing or homesickness.

Second, a place-name’s historical origin, even if derived from an established root, does not itself change its direct meaning in a Qur’anic sentence.

Al-Tawbah 9:25 concerns a day at a historical location, rather than a day of longing or kindness.

The Qur’anic morphological index therefore records only حَنَانًا in Maryam 19:13 under ح ن ن; the proper place-name حُنَيْن is not included in that count.

Al-Ṭabarī: Ḥunayn as the valley’s name

Root studies must make this clear so that similar consonants do not incorrectly increase the count of semantic derivatives.

Nor are there lexical grounds for explaining the event in Al-Tawbah 9:25 through compassion or longing. The verse’s subject and message are established through its own context.

6. Distinctions from related roots

ح ن ن (حَنَان) and ر ح م (رَحْمَة)

Mercy has a broad semantic field and can concern compassion, forgiveness, assistance, blessing, and beneficial conduct.

Established usage of حنان highlights affection and compassion with tenderness and emotional care.

Early dictionaries repeatedly explain ḥanān through mercy; the words therefore occupy closely related semantic ground.

They should nevertheless not be treated as complete synonyms in every construction.

Mercy may become visible through providing sustenance or relieving hardship without the sentence mentioning longing or tenderness.

Human ḥanān, by contrast, can particularly highlight a person’s compassionate disposition and emotional attention.

In Maryam 19:13, “kindness” and “mercy” are both defensible, but “tenderness and compassion” makes this family’s character clearer.

Study of R Ḥ M

ح ن ن (حَنَان) and ش ف ق (إِشْفَاق)

إشفاق concerns compassionate concern, attention, and fear regarding another’s condition.

Ibn Fāris uses الإشفاق to explain the principle of ح ن ن.

The closeness does not mean that every ḥanān involves fear or anxiety.

A mother may worry when separated from her child, but in another situation care for the child calmly and affectionately.

Both can be related to ḥanān.

Concern is therefore one possible condition of compassion, rather than a necessary meaning of every usage.

ح ن ن (حَنَان) and ر أ ف (رَأْفَة)

رأفة concerns gentleness and compassion in conduct.

حنان is likewise close to affection and compassion.

In many situations the words can explain a kind action together.

But حنان belongs to a family in which longing and emotional attraction are also prominent.

رأفة should not be made to mean longing for one’s homeland or a camel’s cry upon separation from its young without evidence.

The words therefore converge in kindness, while their full lexical ranges differ.

Study of R ʾ F

ح ن ن (حَنَان) and و د د (وُدّ)

وُدّ concerns love and affection.

حنان can also appear in an affectionate relationship, but established usage highlights compassion and caring attention.

Someone may love another without always considering their needs or difficulties.

Compassion, by contrast, may become visible in practical care and attention to the person’s circumstances.

This is not an absolute boundary between the roots, but shows how each word may emphasize a different aspect of a relationship.

Study of W D D

ح ن ن (حَنِين) and ش و ق (شَوْق)

شوق concerns yearning and inward attraction.

حنين occupies the same field and can refer to longing for something distant.

Yet ح ن ن also has established usages for a sound accompanying longing and for kindness and compassion toward another.

When حنين means yearning in a sentence, it does not follow that every derivative denotes homesickness alone.

حنانا in Maryam 19:13 concerns mercy and compassion, rather than simply longing for someone far away.

ح ن ن (حَنَان) and ح ف و (حَفَاوَة)

حفاوة concerns particular attention, care, and honoring another.

حنان concerns affection, compassion, and tenderness.

They can accompany one another in someone’s conduct.

A person may warmly welcome a guest while compassionately attending to their needs.

Warm behavior and outward honoring do not always mean inward love.

Someone may also feel ḥanān toward a distant person without being able to host or directly care for them.

حفاوة and حنان should therefore not be equated in every usage.

Study of Ḥ F W

ح ن ن (حَنَان) and ح ل م (حِلْم)

In ethical usage, حلم concerns forbearance and avoiding a hasty reaction.

حنان concerns tenderness and compassion.

Someone can be both kind and forbearing, but these qualities differ.

A person may feel compassion yet act hastily.

A forbearing person, conversely, may respond thoughtfully despite lacking a close emotional relationship.

Analysis of moral qualities must therefore distinguish compassionate feeling from the quality of regulating conduct.

Study of Ḥ L M

ح ن ن (حَنَان) and ح ن ف (حَنِيف)

حنيف belongs to ح ن ف and concerns orientation toward Abraham’s way in the Qur’an.

حنان belongs to ح ن ن and concerns tenderness and compassion.

The roots share only their first two consonants.

Ḥanān therefore cannot be translated as being a ḥanīf or adopting Abraham’s religious way.

A ḥanīf may act kindly, but the relationship between the two qualities cannot be established through either word alone.

Study of Ḥ N F

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Maryam 19:13, وَحَنَانًا مِنْ لَدُنَّا can be rendered:

“And tenderness from Us.”

This preserves ḥanān’s principal meaning and divine source.

“And affection and compassion from Us” is also defensible and makes the word’s compassionate character clearer.

“And mercy from Us” is close to established early dictionary explanations, but may obscure the distinction between ḥanān and the broader term mercy.

For the full verse, a suitable proposal is:

“And tenderness from Us, and purity; and he was reverently mindful.”

The translation should not simply replace the word with “divine kindness toward John” or “John’s kindness toward people” without explanation.

Both readings can be examined contextually, but the Arabic phrase does not independently and explicitly name the recipient of ḥanān.

Nor does حنان here mean homesickness, a moaning sound, or longing for one’s homeland.

Outside the Qur’an, حَنَّ إِلَيْهِ can mean “he yearned for him” or “missed him”; حَنَّ عَلَيْهِ, “he showed him affection and compassion”; حَنِين, “longing and homesickness”; حَنُون, “kind and compassionate”; and تَحَنَّنَ عَلَيْهِ, “he acted kindly toward him.”

In a request, حَنَانَيْكَ is close to “show me mercy” or “show me kindness and compassion repeatedly.”

When applied to wind or a bow, the derivatives concern sound and should not be translated through human affection.

Conclusion

Brief root meaning: Longing and emotional attraction; affection, compassion, and tenderness; in audible usages, a yearning cry and similar sounds.

Ibn Fāris identifies one principle: الإشفاق والرقة, compassion and tenderness.

Some usages express an inward condition through sound: a she-camel’s cry upon separation from her young, and similar sounds in wind or a bow.

Longing is nevertheless not confined to sound. Someone can silently miss their homeland or loved ones.

In another branch, حنّ عليه and حنان concern kindness and compassionate attention toward another.

The Qur’an contains only حَنَانًا in Maryam 19:13, within qualities and gifts described concerning John.

There are two principal contextual readings: divine affection and favor toward John, and a quality of kindness bestowed upon him.

The phrase makes the tenderness’s divine source clear, but does not independently name its recipient.

Al-Tawbah 9:25 also contains حُنَيْن, but it is a place-name and should not be confused with حَنِين as longing or حَنَان as tenderness.

The family encompasses longing and emotional attraction on one side, and affection, compassion, and tenderness on another. Ḥanīn may accompany sound, while some audible usages are understood through similarity of sound. It does not follow that every sound or instance of yearning in this family simultaneously entails affection and compassion.

The Qur’anic use highlights tenderness and compassion, rather than an animal’s cry, homesickness, or a place-name.

For Forqan’s translation, “tenderness from Us” is suitable and faithful to the wording.

It preserves the root’s clear meaning while allowing the direction of the tenderness—divine favor toward John or kindness bestowed upon him—to be examined through independent evidence and context in the commentary.
