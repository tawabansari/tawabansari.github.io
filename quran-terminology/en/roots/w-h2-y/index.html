Qur’anic Root W H Y (Wahy)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

وَهَى
(wahā)
Became weak; perfect verb

وَهْي
(wahy)
Weakening and impaired firmness; verbal noun

وَاهٍ
(wāhin)
Frail; masculine active participle

وَاهِيَةٌ
(wāhiyatun)
Frail; feminine active participle in the verse

أَوْهَى
(awhā)
Weakened; Form IV

Weakening, impaired strength, and loss of cohesion

The Arabic root و ه ي denotes weakening, diminished strength, and loss of cohesion. وَهَى describes something that has lost its strength or sustained damage; وَهْي refers to weakness, a breach, or structural damage; and وَاهٍ describes something weakened or frail.

In material uses, this root occurs with a cracked wall, worn cloth, a punctured waterskin, and a rope whose fastening has loosened. More broadly, an unsupported argument, an unfounded statement, or a force that has lost its strength may also be described as wāhin.

This family therefore does more than denote weakness in general: many of its uses emphasize weakening and damage to cohesion or structural strength.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs only once, as وَاهِيَةٌ in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16. The verse describes the sky splitting apart on the day of the great event and losing its firmness.

1. The root in early Arabic and transmitted evidence

In early Arabic, وَهَى الشَّيْءُ described something that had become weak or frail. Such weakness could arise from impact, wear, cracking, or a loss of connection between parts.

وَهَى الْحَائِطُ was said of a wall that had lost strength, cracked, or approached collapse.

وَهَى الثَّوْبُ described cloth that had worn out and torn.

وَهَى السِّقَاءُ likewise described a waterskin whose leather had become damaged or punctured.

The continuity of the leather matters in a waterskin. It may retain the outward shape of a container, yet a small hole can prevent it from holding water properly.

This example distinguishes weakness from complete destruction. Something can be weakened or damaged without all its parts having disappeared.

A verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, reads:

كَنَاطِحِ صَخْرَةٍ يَوْمًا لِيَفْلِقَهَا
فَلَمْ يَضِرْهَا وَأَوْهَى قَرْنَهُ الْوَعِلُ

ka-nāṭiḥi ṣakhratin yawman li-yafliqahā / fa-lam yaḍirhā wa-awhā qarnahu al-waʿilu

Translation: “Like a wild goat that once butted a rock to split it: it did not harm the rock, but weakened and damaged its own horn.”

In this verse, the wild goat strikes a hard rock with its horn. The expected harm to the rock is reversed: the rock remains firm, while the animal’s horn is damaged.

أَوْهَى here means to weaken or damage.

The poet uses this image for a fruitless effort that ultimately harms the person making it.

Lexically, أَوْهَى قَرْنَهُ does not necessarily mean that the horn became completely detached from the animal’s head. The damage may have weakened or broken it. The verb itself does not specify the extent of the injury.

This verse is transmitted in a well-known ode by Al-Aʿshā. Some versions have لِيُوهِنَهَا instead of لِيَفْلِقَهَا: the animal strikes the rock to weaken it.

In both versions, أَوْهَى قَرْنَهُ clearly refers to damage to the horn.

Al-Aʿshā lived before Islam and also witnessed its beginnings. The verse is transmitted in a recognized ode, but its precise date of composition has not been independently established. It is important evidence from the early linguistic tradition, not proof of when the root first emerged.

A weakening string and scattered pearls

A verse attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar also reads:

فَحَطَّتْ كَمَا حَطَّتْ وَهِيَّةُ تَاجِرٍ
وَهَى نَظْمُهَا فَارْفَضَّ مِنْهَا الطَّوَائِفُ

fa-ḥaṭṭat ka-mā ḥaṭṭat wahiyyatu tājirin / wahā naẓmuhā fa-rfaḍḍa minhā al-ṭawāʾifu

Translation: “It fell as a merchant’s pearls fall when their string weakens and their beads scatter.”

وَهَى نَظْمُهَا here refers to the weakening of the string joining the pearls together.

The string may hold the beads together for a time, but once it loses its strength, it can no longer maintain their connection, and they scatter.

In this usage, وهى denotes the weakening of the string itself; the scattering of the beads is its consequence.

The distinction matters. The root does not necessarily contain every stage of breaking and scattering; it emphasizes damage to strength and connection.

Versions of the verse also record وَنِيَّة and وَئِيَّة in place of وَهِيَّة. Its derivation therefore cannot be treated as certain. The expression وَهَى نَظْمُهَا in the cited version, however, clearly conveys the weakening of the string.

The attribution to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar is known in literary sources. Nevertheless, variant readings and transmission through later sources must be considered when assessing its historical value.

These two witnesses, concerning a damaged horn and a weakened string, show the early association of و ه ي with diminished strength and increased vulnerability.

Other uses in classical Arabic

Arabic lexicons also apply وَهْي to a breach or tear in something.

For example, a tear in cloth or a waterskin can be called its وَهْي.

In أَوْهَيْتَ وَهْيًا فَارْقَعْهُ, someone is called upon to repair a breach they have caused.

وَهَى رِبَاطُ الشَّيْءِ likewise describes a fastening that has lost its former tightness and strength.

This differs from complete severance. The fastening may still exist, yet have loosened enough that it no longer holds something firmly.

The expression وَهَتْ عَزَالِي السَّمَاءِ is also recorded of clouds, referring to water pouring from them heavily.

This can be related to the image of an opened outlet for water. It does not establish that Arabic speakers literally regarded clouds as punctured leather waterskins. It is a linguistic image for heavy rainfall.

In nonmaterial usage, كَلَامٌ وَاهٍ describes speech lacking sufficient strength or support.

حُجَّةٌ وَاهِيَةٌ may likewise denote an argument with weak foundations that cannot withstand close examination.

Without independent evidence, these expressions should not be attributed to every period before Islam. Their presence in classical Arabic is clear, but their individual dates of origin are not equally ascertainable.

Taken together, the evidence identifies two main aspects: weakness and frailty, and damage to cohesion or strength.

They are related but not always identical. Something may be weak without a visible breach, or have a breach that has not yet caused total collapse.

2. Forms and derivatives

The principal forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • وَهَى and وَهِيَ: became weak or frail; in some contexts, cracked or tore.
  • يَهِي: becomes weak or loses strength.
  • وَهْي: weakness, frailty, a breach, or damage to cohesion.
  • وُهِيّ: a recorded verbal noun, also reported as a plural of وَهْي.
  • وَاهٍ: weak, frail, or damaged.
  • وَاهِيَة: the feminine of وَاهٍ.
  • أَوْهَى: weakened something or diminished its strength.
  • إِيهَاء: weakening or impairing strength.
  • مُوهٍ: one who, or something that, causes weakness.
  • مُوهًى: something weakened or damaged.
  • وُهَيَّة: a diminutive denoting a small breach; وَهِيَّة is separately recorded as a name for a pearl.
  • أَوْهِيَة: a rare plural for breaches or flaws.
  • وَاهِيَات: the feminine plural of وَاهِيَة.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The root’s only Qur’anic derivative is وَاهِيَةٌ in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16.

وَاهِيَة is the feminine active participle of the basic verb وَهَى or وَهِيَ.

Its indefinite masculine form in the nominative and genitive may be وَاهٍ. Adding the feminine ending produces وَاهِيَة.

This morphological difference matters: the retention of yāʾ in the feminine may make وَاهِيَة look different from وَاهٍ, although both belong to the same family.

In the verse, فَهِيَ is the independent pronoun with the particle فَـ; it does not belong to و ه ي. The derivative of this root is وَاهِيَةٌ.

3. Qur’anic usage: the sky weakened and split apart

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16

وَانشَقَّتِ السَّمَاءُ فَهِيَ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَاهِيَةٌ

wa-nshaqqati al-samāʾu fa-hiya yawmaʾidhin wāhiyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16

Translation: “And the sky will split apart, and on that day it will be frail.”

This verse contains the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

وَانشَقَّتِ السَّمَاءُ speaks of the sky splitting apart.

فَهِيَ refers back to that sky.

يَوْمَئِذٍ specifies the time of this condition.

وَاهِيَةٌ describes the sky on that day.

Grammatically, وَاهِيَة is the predicate of هِيَ, while يَوْمَئِذٍ specifies when the condition holds.

The construction describes an event and its accompanying state: the sky splits apart and is frail on that day.

The difference between splitting and wahy

Two distinct roots occur together in this verse.

انشقت belongs to ش ق ق and concerns splitting apart.

واهية belongs to و ه ي and denotes weakness or loss of firmness.

Treating the words as completely synonymous would erase the distinction between the event of splitting and the condition of being frail.

To clarify the difference, imagine a cracked wall.

Cracking is an event affecting the wall. Its strength may then diminish, leaving it unable to withstand the same pressure as before.

In this example, the breach and structural weakness are connected but are not one concept.

Likewise, in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16, انشقت expresses the splitting itself, while واهية describes the sky’s frailty on that day.

The comparison does not imply that the sky is built exactly like a brick wall, or that its splitting must follow familiar laws governing the fracture of building materials.

The wall example serves only to clarify the lexical distinction.

The context in Al-Ḥāqqah

The surrounding verses matter for understanding واهية.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:13 speaks of the Trumpet being blown.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:14, the earth and mountains are lifted and crushed with a single blow.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:15 announces the occurrence of the great event.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16 then describes the sky splitting and its frail condition.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:17 places the angels at its edges, and Al-Ḥāqqah 69:18 speaks of people being presented and what was hidden becoming exposed.

This sequence places واهية within the great transformation of the world at the Resurrection.

In this scene, the earth, mountains, and sky no longer remain in their familiar, ordinary condition.

The sky that people see as extensive and enduring in this life is described as split and frail on that day.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:12 speaks of seven strong structures built above people.

The contrast matters: one verse presents the heavens’ firmness, while the other describes the sky’s frailty on a specified day.

This difference accords with the transformation of the world at the Resurrection.

It does not establish a statement about the sky’s material composition, thickness, or a specified quantity of physical strength.

In this context, واهية expresses frailty and loss of firmness, rather than a scientific measurement of material resistance.

Does واهية mean complete collapse?

Arabic sources also use وَهَى الْحَائِطُ for a wall that has weakened and is on the verge of falling.

This usage shows that wahy can be associated with the risk of collapse.

But واهية does not in itself mean complete collapse.

Something may become too weak to maintain its structure, yet the adjective alone does not specify every subsequent stage.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16 explicitly states that the sky splits apart. Frailty describes that same condition.

The following verse mentions the sky’s edges. وَاهِيَة alone therefore does not establish that no edge, part, or structure remains at that moment.

The precise manner of this transformation lies beyond the root’s lexical definition.

The Qur’an also presents other images of the sky’s transformation, including:

Al-Infiṭār 82:1 speaks of the sky splitting apart.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:1 expresses the event with another verb from ش ق ق.

Al-Mursalāt 77:9 speaks of the sky being opened or split.

Al-Takwīr 81:11 describes the sky with كُشِطَتْ, presenting an image of its being stripped or drawn away.

Al-Raḥmān 55:37 also speaks of the sky splitting and its appearance changing.

Read together, these verses offer a broader picture of the world’s transformation, but each verb and adjective retains its own semantic contribution.

وَاهِيَة should not replace them all, just as all these images cannot be reduced to weakness.

4. Distinctions from related roots

و ه ي and و ه ن: impaired firmness and diminished strength

These roots are close in sound and meaning but remain independent.

و ه ن occurs in the Qur’an for bodily weakness, diminished strength, faltering endurance, and a weakened support.

For example, in Maryam 19:4, Zachariah’s bones have weakened.

Luqmān 31:14 describes the mother’s successive weakness during pregnancy.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41 calls the spider’s house أَوْهَنَ الْبُيُوتِ.

By contrast, و ه ي in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16 describes the sky’s weakened condition alongside its splitting.

In material Arabic usage, wahy often accompanies cracking, tearing, and loss of cohesion, whereas wahn can concern diminished capacity and firmness more broadly.

The distinction is not absolute, however.

Wahn also concerns structural weakness in the spider’s house, while wahy can describe weak speech and arguments in Arabic.

It is therefore incorrect to confine wahy to physical objects and wahn to people or morale.

The distinction is a tendency discerned across their uses.

Study of w-h-n

The forms أَوْهَى and أَوْهَنَ also merit attention.

أَوْهَى, from و ه ي, means to weaken.

أَوْهَنَ, from و ه ن, is also used for weakening.

In Al-Anfāl 8:18, مُوهِنُ كَيْدِ الْكَافِرِينَ belongs to و ه ن, not و ه ي.

Semantic similarity does not justify merging their derivations.

و ه ي and و ن ي: structural weakness and declining activity

و ن ي concerns slackening, fatigue, and diminished effort in continuing an activity.

In Ṭā Hā 20:42, The God commands Moses and Aaron not to slacken in remembering Him.

و ه ي also conveys weakness, but its Qur’anic occurrence concerns the frailty of the sky.

A rope that has lost its cohesion and strength is وَاهٍ; a person whose effort and persistence in a task have diminished may be described with the family و ن ي.

This does not completely separate their semantic fields. Bodily weakness may reduce activity, and slackening effort may accompany incapacity.

What must be avoided is transferring laziness or moral negligence from و ن ي to واهية as a description of the sky.

The sky in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16 is not called واهية because it has neglected a responsibility or become unwilling to act.

Study of w-n-y

و ه ي and ش ق ق: frailty and splitting apart

ش ق ق concerns splitting, separation, and the formation of a breach.

و ه ي denotes weakness or diminished firmness, sometimes accompanied by a breach or tear.

Both families occur in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16.

انشقت expresses the event of splitting; واهية describes frailty.

A body may crack while retaining some strength. Something may also weaken without a visible crack.

Splitting and weakness thus overlap without being necessarily identical.

Study of sh-q-q

و ه ي and ف ط ر: impaired firmness and opening a breach

In usages concerning the sky, ف ط ر relates to splitting and opening.

Al-Infiṭār 82:1 speaks of the sky splitting apart.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16, واهية describes the sky’s condition after its splitting has been stated.

The families may be connected through structural change, but faṭr does not itself mean weakness in general.

Conversely, something described as وَاهٍ need not have undergone a particular kind of splitting at that same moment.

و ه ي and خ ر ق: weakening and puncturing

In material usage, خرق concerns splitting, tearing, or making a hole.

Wahy can also accompany tearing and puncturing in the examples of cloth and a waterskin.

In kharq, however, the act of tearing or creating a breach is prominent.

In wahy, attention may fall on the resulting weakness and damage.

For example, a puncture may deprive a leather waterskin of its ability to hold water.

The puncturing relates to kharq; the waterskin’s weakened, damaged condition can be described with wahy.

The meanings are close but distinct.

Study of kh-r-q

و ه ي and ه و ي: weakness and falling

ه و ي is an independent root whose uses include falling as well as inclination and desire.

و ه ي concerns weakness and frailty.

A wall may collapse because it is weak, but weakening and falling are distinct events.

For this reason, واهية should not be directly translated as “falling.”

The surface similarity of وَهَى and هَوَى also does not establish historical kinship or identical semantic fields.

5. Challenges of understanding and translation

One difficulty in translating واهية is that the Persian equivalent of “weak” can be very general.

A person may be physically weak, a sound may be faint, and an argument may be weak or unsupported.

The Persian equivalent of “frail” or “unsound,” by contrast, often suits something lacking sufficient firmness or cohesion.

It is thus a natural and accurate rendering in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16.

“Disintegrated” relates to the context of the sky splitting, but using it alone for واهية may restrict weakness and diminished strength to complete rupture.

“Collapsed” is likewise unsuitable: the word itself does not necessarily express a structure’s complete disappearance or fall.

Another challenge is turning the Qur’anic image into a theory about the sky’s physical construction.

Modern knowledge of the atmosphere, surrounding space, and celestial bodies is broader. It can prompt new questions when reflecting on verses about the sky.

But و ه ي does not establish that the sky is a physical fabric woven from particular threads, or that a specified atmospheric layer will tear at the Resurrection.

Equally, the lack of a physical explanation should not lead us to dismiss the Qur’anic image as meaningless or merely poetic.

The Qur’an announces a real, great transformation at the Resurrection. Its precise manner cannot be determined solely through current knowledge of the universe’s structure.

The linguistic contribution is clear: واهية denotes frailty and loss of firmness.

Exactly what condition the sky will assume, and how this description will be realized, goes beyond lexical definition.

6. Suggested renderings for Forqan

For وَهَى in material contexts, “became frail,” “lost strength,” or, with cloth and waterskins, “wore out and tore” are suitable.

For وَهْي, “weakness,” “loss of firmness,” and, where cracks or breaches are involved, “damage” or “a breach” are natural equivalents.

For وَاهٍ, “frail,” “weak,” or “damaged” may suit the context.

For وَاهِيَة, choose “frail” or “weak” according to what it describes.

For أَوْهَى, “weakened,” “made frail,” or “diminished its strength” are suitable.

For إِيهَاء, “weakening” or “making frail” are suggested.

For مُوهٍ, use “one that weakens”; for مُوهًى, “weakened” or “made frail.”

Figuratively, حُجَّةٌ وَاهِيَةٌ can be rendered “a weak argument” or “an unsupported claim.” This sense should not be transferred to the Qur’anic description of the sky.

The suggested rendering of Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16 is:

“And the sky will split apart, and on that day it will be frail.”

The translation is brief, clear, and faithful to the Arabic construction.

Where a more precise explanatory note is needed, one may add:

“On that day the sky splits apart and becomes weak and vulnerable in its firmness.”

In this explanation, splitting comes from انشقت, while diminished firmness comes from واهية.

“The sky is completely destroyed” is unsuitable, since complete destruction is not established by these two words alone.

“The sky becomes weak from fear” likewise adds a meaning the verse does not state.

7. Conclusion

In Arabic, و ه ي concerns frailty, diminished strength, and impaired cohesion.

Material usages include a cracked wall, worn cloth, a punctured waterskin, and a rope whose fastening has loosened.

Poetic witnesses attributed to Al-Aʿshā and Aws ibn Ḥajar depict a damaged horn and a weakened string. They illuminate the root’s early semantic field, although variant readings and later transmission prevent certainty about the dates of every usage.

Classical Arabic also uses this family for weak speech and arguments, showing how impaired firmness can extend from material objects to nonmaterial matters.

The Qur’an contains only وَاهِيَةٌ in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16.

The verse first states that the sky splits apart and then describes it as واهية on that same day.

Splitting and wahy are connected but distinct concepts. Splitting denotes the event itself; frailty describes the condition of something’s strength.

This root is semantically close to و ه ن, but they are independent. Wahn also occurs in the Qur’an for weakness of the body, endurance, and a support, whereas Qur’anic wahy describes the sky’s frailty alongside its splitting.

Complete collapse, physical falling, or a specified material construction of the sky should not be inferred from this word. Such details require independent evidence.

The root و ه ي denotes becoming weak and losing strength or cohesion, a condition that may accompany cracking, tearing, wear, or diminished endurance. In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16, وَاهِيَةٌ describes the sky at the Resurrection as frail and impaired in firmness; its actual splitting is expressed by the separate verb انشقت.
