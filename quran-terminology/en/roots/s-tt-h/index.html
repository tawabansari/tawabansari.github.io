The Arabic root S Ṭ Ḥ concerns spreading something out, leveling its surface, and extending over an area. Within this family, saṭḥ denotes a surface or upper part, such as a house roof, while saṭaḥa expresses spreading or leveling.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as suṭiḥat in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20, directing attention to the earth and how it has been spread out and made level.

Two questions should remain distinct: the actual meaning of spreading and leveling contained in the root, and the geometry of the entire body whose surface is described. They are related but not necessarily identical concepts.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Lexical definition

Classical Arabic records saṭaḥa al-shayʾa as spreading something out or leveling its surface.

The verb could be used when dates were spread out to dry or something was laid out on the ground. Leveling a roof’s surface was another clear application.

Two principal elements are recognizable in this lexical family:

First, basṭ and madd: spreading and extending something.

Second, taswiyah: making its surface even and level.

These elements are not equally prominent in every derivative. Sometimes the action of spreading stands out, and sometimes its result: a spread-out or leveled surface.

Two Arabic expressions help explain the root’s central meaning:

«بَسْطُ الشَّيْءِ وَمَدُّهُ»

basṭu al-shayʾi wa-madduhu

That is, “spreading something out and extending it.”

Saṭḥ can also name the extended upper part of a thing.

The root is therefore not confined to laying an object on the ground; an object’s broad or extended surface can itself be called saṭḥ.

The status of pre-Qur’anic evidence

To identify the word’s meaning among pre-Islamic Arabs precisely, recorded dictionary usages must be distinguished from evidence that can demonstrably be dated before the Qur’an.

Spreading and leveling are clear, established meanings in classical Arabic sources. Uses also occur in early poetry and names for everyday implements.

Claiming that pre-Islamic Arabs used all these derivatives with precisely the same semantic scope, however, requires examining each witness’s date and independence.

This article does not present an independently dated pre-Qur’anic witness from which the basic meaning of saṭaḥa can be established with certainty. That limitation does not prove that no such witness exists elsewhere.

The recorded lexical meaning is clear, but details of its pre-Qur’anic history should not be stated more confidently than the available evidence permits.

2. Early Arabic evidence and concrete uses

Misṭaḥ: a tent implement

A verse attributed to Mālik ibn ʿAwf al-Naṣrī reads:

تَعَرَّضَ ضَيْطَارُو خُزَاعَةَ دُونَنَا
وَمَا خَيْرُ ضَيْطَارٍ يُقَلِّبُ مِسْطَحَا

taʿarraḍa ḍayṭāru Khuzāʿata dūnanā / wa-mā khayru ḍayṭārin yuqallibu misṭaḥā

“The bulky men of Khuzāʿah stood in our way; what good is a bulky man wielding a tent pole?”

Ḍayṭār here describes a large-bodied person disparagingly, implying that bulk is not matched by strength or effectiveness.

Misṭaḥ names a wooden pole used to erect a tent and keep its covering extended.

The word here denotes an actual everyday implement, associated with spreading out and supporting a tent covering.

The verse is attributed to Mālik ibn ʿAwf al-Naṣrī. He lived around the emergence of Islam, but the precise date of this verse is unclear. It is evidence from the early Arabic poetic tradition, not conclusive proof of pre-Islamic use.

The tent-pole meaning comes from attested usage. Sharing the root does not establish that every implement named misṭaḥ was itself broad and flat.

Saṭḥ: a roof and the upper part of a building

A familiar meaning of saṭḥ is a house roof.

In customary Arabic architecture, a roof could form an even, extended surface above the walls. The name saṭḥ has a clear connection with that visible feature.

This use brings together two characteristics: the upper part of a building and a surface extending in length and breadth.

Being above something, however, is not the meaning of every form in this family. Something spread on the ground can be masṭūḥ without occupying a high position.

The principal feature is thus extension and the condition of a surface, not height alone.

Spreading dates and food

Arabic records saṭaḥa al-tamra for spreading dates out.

The image is straightforward: dates are distributed over an area instead of remaining piled in a heap.

“سَطَحَ الثَّرِيدَ فِي الصَّحْفَةِ” likewise describes spreading food in a dish.

In both examples, the change concerns how material is arranged on a surface.

These uses show that the verb can mean spreading and distributing something, rather than only smoothing it with a tool.

Saṭīḥ and insaṭaḥa: a body stretched out

Insaṭaḥa is recorded for someone lying stretched out on their back on the ground.

Saṭīḥ can likewise describe someone whose body lies extended or prostrate.

The concrete feature is the body’s extension over the supporting surface.

Saṭīḥ is also recorded as the name of a figure famed for divination in early narratives. Lexical explanations associate the name with his reported bodily condition, but the details of his life should not be accepted as established history without investigation.

Saṭīḥah: a waterskin

Saṭīḥah names a type of leather water container or waterskin in Arabic.

One explanation connects the name with the container spreading out when laid down or when it falls.

That explanation fits the root’s semantic field. Without a clear history of the name, however, it cannot be treated as its conclusively established historical origin.

Together, these examples associate S Ṭ Ḥ in classical Arabic with spreading objects, a stretched-out body, a building’s surface, and everyday implements.

3. Forms and Qur’anic usage

S Ṭ Ḥ occurs only once in the Qur’an:

سُطِحَتْ — Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20

It is a Form I perfect passive verb, third-person feminine singular.

Its active form is saṭaḥa: he spread something out or leveled its surface.

The passive suṭiḥa means “was spread out” or “was leveled.”

Suṭiḥat carries the feminine ending because it refers back to al-arḍ, which is grammatically feminine.

The Qur’anic form thus describes an action affecting the earth without naming the agent within the verb.

Other forms in this family are known in Arabic but do not occur in these particular forms in the Qur’an. They include:

  • Saṭḥ: an extended surface or the upper part of something.
  • Saṭaḥa: spread out or leveled; the active form.
  • Saṭṭaḥa: leveled a surface; a Form II verb.
  • Tasṭīḥ: spreading out or leveling.
  • Masṭūḥ: something spread out or leveled.
  • Insaṭaḥa: became stretched out or lay extended.
  • Masṭaḥ / misṭaḥ: a place for spreading something, or certain implements associated with spreading.

These forms clarify the root’s semantic field, but should not be presented as forms actually occurring in the Qur’an.

Neither the noun saṭḥ nor the verbal noun tasṭīḥ occurs in the Qur’an. Only suṭiḥat appears, and its meaning must be examined through that form and its verse’s context.

4. “And at the earth, how it was spread out”: the earth’s expanse

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20 states:

وَإِلَى الْأَرْضِ كَيْفَ سُطِحَتْ

wa-ilā al-arḍi kayfa suṭiḥat

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20

“And at the earth, how it has been spread out and made level?”

This verse belongs to four successive invitations to observe.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:17 states:

أَفَلَا يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَى الْإِبِلِ كَيْفَ خُلِقَتْ

a-fa-lā yanẓurūna ilā al-ibili kayfa khuliqat

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:17

“Do they not look at the camels, how they were created?”

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:18 states:

وَإِلَى السَّمَاءِ كَيْفَ رُفِعَتْ

wa-ilā al-samāʾi kayfa rufiʿat

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:18

“And at the sky, how it was raised?”

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:19 states:

وَإِلَى الْجِبَالِ كَيْفَ نُصِبَتْ

wa-ilā al-jibāli kayfa nuṣibat

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:19

“And at the mountains, how they were set up?”

Attention then turns to the earth and how it has been spread out and made level.

These four verses juxtapose different phenomena: a familiar animal, the sky overhead, mountains rising from the ground, and the earth where people live and move.

Each has a verb suited to its particular feature: camels were created, the sky raised, mountains set up, and the earth spread out and made level.

The different verbs show that these phenomena are not all introduced merely by a general expression such as “being made.” The Qur’an directs attention to a particular feature of each.

The meaning of suṭiḥat here

“Being spread out and made level” is a close, defensible meaning in this construction.

People experience the earth as the surface on which they stand, walk, build homes, cultivate land, and travel between places.

Its expanse has real importance for human life.

This does not mean that every place must lack mountains, valleys, or uneven terrain.

Immediately before the earth’s expanse, the context mentions mountains being set up. The same passage thus acknowledges both prominent mountainous relief and the spreading of the earth’s surface.

Suṭiḥat cannot accordingly be taken to mean the removal of every elevation and depression.

Nor should concrete extension be replaced with a purely spiritual explanation, such as the expansion of human opportunities. The verse concerns the earth itself and a feature available to observation.

5. The earth’s surface and its overall geometry

One question about Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20 is whether suṭiḥat means that the entire earth is flat.

An answer must first preserve the lexical meaning.

Saṭaḥa concerns spreading and leveling. Renderings such as “was spread out” and “was made level” therefore belong to the word’s actual semantic field.

The next question is precisely what this description tells us about.

An extended surface and a body’s overall shape

Speaking of a surface directs attention to the outer extent of a thing.

A surface under observation may be broad and level, while the whole body has other features at a larger scale.

For example, on a vast plain people see the ground stretching ahead and can travel long distances over it. That observation is real, but it alone cannot determine the earth’s entire geometry.

Conversely, knowing from independent evidence that the earth is approximately spherical does not justify translating suṭiḥat as “was made spherical.” The root does not contain that meaning.

Two matters should therefore be distinguished:

First, the verse actually speaks of the earth’s spreading and leveling.

Second, whether that description alone determines its entire geometric form.

The word’s lexical meaning alone is insufficient for the second conclusion.

Is it only about human perception?

It is sometimes said that the verse describes the earth as merely appearing flat “to human eyes.”

This explains part of the experience of observing the ground, but should not replace the verse’s whole meaning.

Suṭiḥat is a perfect passive verb describing a condition brought about for the earth. The verse does not merely say that it looks that way.

At the same time, the passage begins with “أَفَلَا يَنْظُرُونَ,” inviting observation. The earth’s observable character therefore matters for understanding the verse.

On the contextual reading adopted here, the verse presents the real extent of the ground within human observation and life. This phrase does not by itself provide a complete geometric description of the whole earth.

This is an interpretation of the description’s scope, not an additional lexical meaning that can be extracted from saṭaḥa alone.

Leveling, mountains, and uneven terrain

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:19 describes mountains with nuṣibat, immediately followed by suṭiḥat concerning the earth in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20.

The conjunction matters because the earth’s expanse is described in a world that also contains raised mountains.

Leveling here therefore does not mean identical elevation at every point.

On the contextual reading adopted here, the ground’s observable expanse is described without requiring every point to be an uncurved geometric plane. This conclusion concerns the description’s scope; the verb itself gives no measurement of curvature.

None of this gives S Ṭ Ḥ a direct meaning of sphericity. Its precise lexical meaning remains spreading and leveling.

6. Comparison with related roots

S Ṭ Ḥ and B S Ṭ: leveling and spreading

B S Ṭ has a broad Qur’anic range, concerning opening out, spreading, and extending.

It is used for the earth, hands, clouds, provision, and human capacity.

Nūḥ 71:19 states:

وَاللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمُ الْأَرْضَ بِسَاطًا

wa-Allāhu jaʿala lakumu al-arḍa bisāṭan

Nūḥ 71:19

“The God made the earth an expanse for you.”

Bisāṭ is something spread out, or an expanse made available like a spread covering.

Here the earth is presented as a spread-out expanse for people.

Suṭiḥat in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20, however, is a verb expressing the earth’s being spread out or made level.

The roots overlap. Basṭ emphasizes opening and extension across many domains, whereas concrete uses of saṭḥ give particular attention to spreading and the condition of a surface.

This is a tendency, not a rule excluding leveling from basṭ or extension from saṭḥ.

S Ṭ Ḥ and M D D: leveling and extending

M D D concerns extension, stretching, and adding to something’s length or reach.

The Qur’an uses madda al-arḍa in Al-Raʿd 13:3 and madadnāhā in Qāf 50:7.

These expressions emphasize the earth’s extent and expansion.

Extending something does not necessarily mean leveling its surface. Extending a road or stretching something lengthwise may be expressed with madd without making its entire surface smooth.

Saṭḥ applied to the earth gives greater prominence to the breadth and leveling of its surface.

Not every madd is therefore tasṭīḥ, nor is every tasṭīḥ simply an increase in length.

S Ṭ Ḥ and Ṭ Ḥ W: an extended surface and the earth’s expanse

Al-Shams 91:6 states:

وَالْأَرْضِ وَمَا طَحَاهَا

wa-al-arḍi wa-mā ṭaḥāhā

Al-Shams 91:6

“And by the earth and the One who spread it out.”

Ṭ Ḥ W likewise concerns spreading and extending, and in some Arabic uses, driving or drawing something onward.

These uses should be examined separately under that root, distinguishing spatial extension from moving something along.

Ṭaḥw has a broader range of movement in some applications, while saṭḥ is more closely associated with a surface’s extension and leveling.

The similarity between ṭaḥāhā and suṭiḥat does not make their roots identical or all their derivatives interchangeable.

S Ṭ Ḥ and D Ḥ W: leveling and spreading the earth

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30 states:

وَالْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ دَحَاهَا

wa-al-arḍa baʿda dhālika daḥāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30

“And afterward He spread out the earth.”

Daḥā concerns spreading out in Arabic. Preparing the earth for life is supported by the surrounding context, while dictionaries also record other uses of the verb.

The following verses, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:31 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32, describe bringing out water and pasture and establishing the mountains.

The lexical meaning of daḥw must therefore be distinguished from what those subsequent verses contribute.

Daḥw and saṭḥ can both describe the earth’s expanse, but one root’s entire range should not be imported into the other without evidence.

In particular, daḥāhā should not be translated as making the earth spherical or egg-shaped solely on the basis of modern scientific theories, nor should suṭiḥat be made the name of a complete geometric model without examining its context.

S Ṭ Ḥ and M H D: leveling and providing a resting place

Al-Nabaʾ 78:6 states:

أَلَمْ نَجْعَلِ الْأَرْضَ مِهَادًا

a-lam najʿali al-arḍa mihādan

Al-Nabaʾ 78:6

“Have We not made the earth a resting place?”

Mihād concerns a prepared bed or place of rest.

Here the earth’s role in settlement and life stands out.

Suṭiḥat, by contrast, directly describes spreading and leveling.

The meanings can complement each other: an extended surface can also provide a place for life and movement.

Being a resting place is nevertheless not the lexical meaning of saṭḥ itself; it is another quality or result expressed by a separate word.

7. Grammar and translation proposals for Forqan

Suṭiḥat in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20 has several grammatical features.

First, it is a perfect verb, presenting the condition as an accomplished action.

Second, it is passive: the agent is not named in the verb.

Third, it is third-person feminine singular, referring back to al-arḍ.

Fourth, it accompanies kayfa, directing attention to how the earth was spread out and made level.

Kayfa asks about manner; it does not itself describe every physical stage or mechanism of creation.

Several Persian renderings may be considered.

“And at the earth, how it was spread out?” conveys extension, but the Persian expression for spreading may be taken too narrowly as the image of a flat object.

“And at the earth, how it was made level?” highlights leveling; understood as eliminating all unevenness, however, it could overlook the mountains in the context.

“And at the earth, how it was extended?” is natural and clear, though it does less to distinguish saṭḥ from broader verbs of extension.

For Forqan’s base translation, the following is therefore proposed:

“And at the earth, how it has been spread out and made level?”

This conveys both principal elements of the root without inserting a theory of the earth’s entire shape into the translation.

Other Arabic forms in the family can be rendered as follows:

Saṭaḥa al-shayʾa: spread something out or leveled its surface.

Saṭḥ al-bayt: the house roof.

Tasṭīḥ: spreading out or leveling a surface.

Masṭūḥ: spread out or leveled.

Insaṭaḥa: became stretched out or lay down on the ground, according to the subject.

Translation should avoid importing a consequence that the word does not itself express. Becoming habitable, being prepared for farming, and providing routes for movement may be effects of the earth’s expanse and condition, but are not direct equivalents of saṭaḥa.

Likewise, the entire earth being flat, being spherical, or having no curvature anywhere should not be inserted into the translation solely on the basis of this root.

Conclusion

S Ṭ Ḥ concerns spreading and leveling something, as well as its extended surface.

Classical Arabic uses, from roofs and dates spread out to tent implements and a recumbent body, show a concrete semantic family. These concern extension and the state of a surface without requiring an invented abstract or philosophical meaning.

The only Qur’anic occurrence is suṭiḥat in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20, a perfect passive verb describing the earth as spread out and made level.

The context of Al-Ghāshiyah 88:17, Al-Ghāshiyah 88:18, Al-Ghāshiyah 88:19, and Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20 is particularly significant. Camels, the sky, mountains, and the earth are each presented through a particular feature.

The earth’s expanse appears alongside mountains being set up. Suṭiḥat therefore cannot be restricted to equal elevation at every point.

Nor should leveling be denied or replaced with an invented translation about sphericity. The word concerns extension and the condition of a surface; determining the whole earth’s geometry is a separate matter.

The core meaning of S Ṭ Ḥ is spreading something out, with the leveling or extension of its surface.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20 applies this meaning to the earth and draws attention to how it was spread out and made level.

This reading preserves the word’s actual meaning without making it establish or refute a geometric theory that cannot be derived from the verb alone.
