Qur’anic Root A Ṣ R

Binding, obligation, and heavy burdens: from a firm covenant to the removal of onerous constraints

Qur’anic and lexical forms

إِصْرًا
(iṣran)
Qur’anic masculine singular noun, accusative object; Al-Baqarah 2:286

إِصْرِي
(iṣrī)
The same noun with “My”; accusative object with an unexpressed case ending; Āl ʿImrān 3:81

إِصْرَهُمْ
(iṣrahum)
The same noun with “their”; accusative object; Al-Aʿrāf 7:157

آصَارَهُمْ

The Arabic root A Ṣ R concerns binding, fastening, restraining, and a connection that holds something or someone under a constraint. أَصَرَ (aṣara) can mean to bind or hold; إِصْر (iṣr), in established uses, names a firm covenant, a binding commitment, or a burden placed upon someone. The same family includes آصِرَة (āṣirah), a bond of kinship or another relationship linking one person to another.

The Qur’an uses this root three times, in three ayahs. In Āl ʿImrān 3:81, إِصْرِي (iṣrī) is the covenant The God takes from the prophets. In Al-Baqarah 2:286, believers ask The God not to place upon them a heavy burden like that placed upon their predecessors. Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 describes a Messenger who removes the heavy burden and shackles that had been upon a group of people.

These uses are related but not identical. One foregrounds the covenant’s standing and binding force; the other two, weight and constraint. Iṣr should therefore not be translated everywhere as simply “sin,” “punishment,” or “an unbearable duty.”

The essential point is that iṣr is not inherently an unjust or wrongful commitment. The word used for the divine covenant in Āl ʿImrān can describe a heavy, restrictive burden in Al-Baqarah and Al-Aʿrāf. The kind of commitment, its weight, and its value are understood from context.

1. Qur’anic forms and semantic core

The root A Ṣ R occurs three times in the Qur’an, only as the noun إِصْر (iṣr):

  • إِصْرًا (iṣran) in Al-Baqarah 2:286: a heavy burden or commitment.
  • إِصْرِي (iṣrī) in Āl ʿImrān 3:81: My firm covenant, in The God’s speech.
  • إِصْرَهُمْ (iṣrahum) in Al-Aʿrāf 7:157: the heavy burden or binding constraint that had been upon them.

All three are forms of one noun. Their differences concern grammatical role and attached pronouns, rather than three separate derivatives. In all three ayahs the noun functions as an object and is accusative; the pronouns ī, “My,” and hum, “their,” are attached possessives.

Arabic records أَصَرَ يَأْصِرُ (aṣara / yaʾṣiru) for binding, bending, restraining, and putting under constraint. أَصْر (aṣr) is its verbal noun; إِصْر (iṣr) names a covenant, heavy burden, or binding constraint.

Other recorded forms include آصِرَة (āṣirah), a bond; إِصَار (iṣār), a cord or binding implement; مَأْصِر (maʾṣir), a place or means of restraint; and آصَار (āṣār), the plural of iṣr.

These forms do not occur as such in the reading transmitted by Ḥafṣ. In that reading, the Qur’an contains only iṣr and its forms with attached pronouns.

Ibn ʿĀmir’s reading has آصَارَهُمْ (āṣārahum) in Al-Aʿrāf 7:157: their heavy burdens or covenants. It uses the plural in place of singular iṣr. The difference does not alter the principal semantic field, though the plural may emphasize multiple burdens and constraints.

The semantic core may be expressed as follows:

Binding or placing something or someone under constraint; in human use, a bond or commitment obliging a person to something and potentially placing a burden or restriction upon that person.

This does not mean that every binding relationship is harmful. A bond may be kinship or a worthy commitment, just as it may become a heavy, restrictive burden.

2. Early Arabic usage: binding, restraint, and bonds

أَصَرَ الشَّيْءَ (aṣara al-shayʾa): binding and bending

Early Arabic usage records أَصَرَ الشَّيْءَ (aṣara al-shayʾa) for bending, binding, or altering something’s position so that it is held and placed under pressure.

Another use of أَصَرَهُ (aṣarahu) means to restrain someone or prevent movement. أَصَرَنِي عَنِ الشَّيْءِ (aṣaranī ʿani al-shayʾi) means that something held me back from a goal or action.

The shared element is restricted movement or freedom to act. Something bound cannot move as before; someone held back encounters an obstacle to what that person seeks to do.

This meaning should not be confined to a physical prison. Arabic also uses the family for relationships and commitments whose binding force is social or moral.

إِصَار (iṣār): a cord that holds things fast

إِصَار (iṣār) names a cord or binding with which something is fastened.

In tent-related usage, the word can name a rope or tie connecting the lower part of a tent to a peg or support.

أَيْصَر (ayṣar) is also recorded for a small, short cord used in fastening a tent.

The connection between tying and holding is clear: the cord joins two parts and prevents their separation or unrestricted movement.

Another use of iṣār concerns a covering or bundle in which plants or fodder are collected.

Lexical transmissions attribute the following line to Al-Aʿshā, an early Arabic poet. Its wording varies across sources, and its exact date of composition is unknown:

فَهَذَا يُعِدُّ لَهُنَّ الْخَلَا
وَيَجْمَعُ ذَا بَيْنَهُنَّ الْإِصَارَا

fa-hādhā yuʿiddu lahunna al-khalā / wa-yajmaʿu dhā baynahunna al-iṣārā

“This one prepares fodder for them, while the other gathers the bundle of fodder among them.”

In this transmission, iṣār refers to collected fodder or a bundle of it; the covering used to gather fodder is also recorded under this name. Binding within this family is therefore not limited to preventing human movement: collecting and joining things also belong to its field.

This witness does not establish that all A Ṣ R derivatives must historically have developed from a tent rope or fodder bundle. These uses make binding and constraint tangible, but do not themselves establish the historical order of meanings.

آصِرَة (āṣirah): kinship and human relationships

آصِرَة (āṣirah) names a bond connecting one person with another, such as kinship, marriage, a pledge, or an obligation arising from kindness and companionship.

Its plural is أَوَاصِر (awāṣir).

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah’s poetry contains the following line:

عَطَفُوا عَلَيَّ بِغَيْرِ آصِرَةٍ
فَقَدْ عَظُمَ الْأَوَاصِرُ

ʿaṭafū ʿalayya bi-ghayri āṣiratin / fa-qad ʿaẓuma al-awāṣiru

“They showed me kindness without a bond of kinship or a pledge; bonds are indeed worthy of esteem.”

Āṣirah here concerns a relationship that may give rise to attention, kindness, or a sense of obligation between people.

The poet speaks of people who treated him kindly without such a pre-existing bond.

The witness matters because it shows that bonds and obligation in this family are not necessarily negative.

Kinship may oblige someone to respect another’s rights; a covenant may bind people to fulfill a commitment. Being bound in these settings does not mean being oppressed: it describes a relationship that creates responsibility.

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods; the exact date of this line is unknown. Its use is therefore a witness to early Arabic, rather than conclusive evidence that this particular line predates the Qur’an.

إِصْر (iṣr): a covenant that binds

Arabic used iṣr for a covenant whose commitment was firm and binding.

In such a covenant, a person does more than utter an ordinary statement: the person undertakes a responsibility whose fulfillment is expected.

A covenant and a cord thus have an intelligible connection. A rope holds something in place; a covenant holds someone to a commitment.

This semantic connection does not prove that every covenant historically derives from tying a rope. The binding meaning of a covenant is recorded in the language; understanding it does not require establishing such a historical sequence.

A further point is that a covenant may be weighty without being impossible to fulfill.

A commitment may be difficult yet within a person’s ability. A covenant may also carry serious consequences if broken.

Iṣr in the sense of covenant does not therefore necessarily mean an impossible commitment.

Heavy burdens, sin, and the consequences of breaking a covenant

Some Arabic uses also apply iṣr to weight, sin, or the serious consequences of wrongful conduct.

This is related to binding and obligation. A burden on someone’s shoulders impedes movement, just as a commitment or the consequences of an action can weigh upon that person’s condition.

Recording a meaning of sin does not establish that iṣr names sin in every Qur’anic ayah.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:81, The God speaks of إِصْرِي (iṣrī), His covenant. Such a covenant is clearly not itself a sin.

In Al-Baqarah 2:286, iṣr accompanies a verb of carrying, highlighting its weight.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 describes removing iṣr and أَغْلَال (aghlāl), shackles, from people.

Sin, punishment, or difficulty may thus arise in some lexical uses or contexts, but is not the core of all of them.

3. إِصْرِي (iṣrī): the firm covenant in Āl ʿImrān

Āl ʿImrān 3:81 states:

وَإِذْ أَخَذَ اللَّهُ مِيثَاقَ النَّبِيِّينَ لَمَا آتَيْتُكُمْ مِنْ كِتَابٍ وَحِكْمَةٍ ثُمَّ جَاءَكُمْ رَسُولٌ مُصَدِّقٌ لِمَا مَعَكُمْ لَتُؤْمِنُنَّ بِهِ وَلَتَنْصُرُنَّهُ قَالَ أَأَقْرَرْتُمْ وَأَخَذْتُمْ عَلَىٰ ذَٰلِكُمْ إِصْرِي قَالُوا أَقْرَرْنَا قَالَ فَاشْهَدُوا وَأَنَا مَعَكُمْ مِنَ الشَّاهِدِينَ

wa-idh akhadha allāhu mīthāqa al-nabiyyīna la-mā ātaytukum min kitābin wa-ḥikmatin thumma jāʾakum rasūlun muṣaddiqun li-mā maʿakum la-tuʾminunna bihi wa-la-tanṣurunnahu qāla a-aqrartum wa-akhadhtum ʿalā dhālikum iṣrī qālū aqrarnā qāla fa-shhadū wa-anā maʿakum mina al-shāhidīn

“When The God took the covenant of the prophets: Whenever I have given you a Book and wisdom, then a Messenger comes to you confirming what is with you, you shall certainly believe in him and help him. He said: Do you acknowledge this and undertake My firm covenant concerning it? They said: We acknowledge it. He said: Then bear witness, and I am with you among the witnesses.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:81

Iṣrī here concerns the covenant The God takes from the prophets.

The attached ī is a first-person possessive referring to The God. Iṣrī therefore means “My covenant” here.

This use is particularly important for defining the root: iṣr can name a commitment with valid standing.

The relationship between مِيثَاق (mīthāq) and إِصْر (iṣr)

The ayah begins with مِيثَاقَ النَّبِيِّينَ (mīthāqa al-nabiyyīna), “the covenant of the prophets,” and subsequently speaks of iṣrī.

Mīthāq, from W TH Q, concerns a firm, trustworthy covenant.

Iṣr likewise concerns a binding covenant here, whose participant accepts responsibility for fulfilling it.

The two words are close within the field of covenant and commitment, but come from different roots.

Mīthāq foregrounds the covenant’s firmness and the reliability of the commitment. Iṣr also presents its binding force and the act of undertaking it.

Their juxtaposition does not establish that every mīthāq is always a first stage, and every iṣr a second or more intense stage, of a covenant. Here both concern one binding relationship; their precise distinction should be learned through their wider usage.

What does the covenant require?

The ayah does not leave the commitment’s content unstated.

Having received a Book and wisdom, the prophets bear responsibility to believe in and help a Messenger who confirms what is with them.

Iṣrī here is therefore not an undefined covenant with no subject. عَلَىٰ ذَٰلِكُمْ (ʿalā dhālikum), “concerning that,” links it with the commitment just stated.

أَأَقْرَرْتُمْ (a-aqrartum), “do you acknowledge?” and the response أَقْرَرْنَا (aqrarnā), “we acknowledge,” also give accepting and acknowledging the commitment a clear place in the ayah’s structure.

A Ṣ R itself, however, does not mean believing or helping. These are the covenant’s content, rather than its lexical definition.

The text also explicitly names النَّبِيِّينَ (al-nabiyyīna), the prophets. Extending the covenant’s responsibility to their followers requires examining the audience and related ayahs; its scope should not be determined solely through the meaning of iṣr.

Is إِصْرِي (iṣrī) an oppressive burden here?

Nothing in this ayah indicates an oppressive covenant or pointless imposition.

On the contrary, the covenant concerns believing in and helping a Messenger who confirms what the prophets possess.

“My unbearable burden” is therefore unsuitable for iṣrī here.

“My firm covenant” or “My binding commitment” better conveys the contextual meaning.

The ayah prevents us from making heaviness and harmfulness permanent features of iṣr in the final definition.

4. وَلَا تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْنَا إِصْرًا: asking for relief from a heavy burden

The closing ayah of Al-Baqarah says:

لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا لَهَا مَا كَسَبَتْ وَعَلَيْهَا مَا اكْتَسَبَتْ رَبَّنَا لَا تُؤَاخِذْنَا إِنْ نَسِينَا أَوْ أَخْطَأْنَا رَبَّنَا وَلَا تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْنَا إِصْرًا كَمَا حَمَلْتَهُ عَلَى الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِنَا رَبَّنَا وَلَا تُحَمِّلْنَا مَا لَا طَاقَةَ لَنَا بِهِ وَاعْفُ عَنَّا وَاغْفِرْ لَنَا وَارْحَمْنَا أَنْتَ مَوْلَانَا فَانْصُرْنَا عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْكَافِرِينَ

lā yukallifu allāhu nafsan illā wusʿahā lahā mā kasabat wa-ʿalayhā mā iktasabat rabbanā lā tuʾākhidhnā in nasīnā aw akhṭaʾnā rabbanā wa-lā taḥmil ʿalaynā iṣran ka-mā ḥamaltahu ʿalā alladhīna min qablinā rabbanā wa-lā tuḥammilnā mā lā ṭāqata lanā bihi wa-ʿfu ʿannā wa-ghfir lanā wa-rḥamnā anta mawlānā fa-nṣurnā ʿalā al-qawmi al-kāfirīn

“The God does not charge any soul beyond its capacity. Each person has the good they have earned, and bears the consequences of what they have committed. Our Lord, do not call us to account if we forget or make a mistake. Our Lord, do not place a heavy burden upon us as You placed it upon those before us. Our Lord, do not impose upon us what we have no strength to bear. Pardon us, forgive us and have mercy on us. You are our Protector, so help us against the denying people.”

Al-Baqarah 2:286

In this ayah, إِصْرًا (iṣran) accompanies the verb تَحْمِلْ (taḥmil), “place/carry.”

Their combination brings the image of weight and burden into focus.

Just as someone may carry a physical load, here The God is asked not to place upon the believers a heavy burden of responsibility, obligation or difficulty.

Iṣran is the indefinite object of taḥmil. The sentence does not identify a particular burden by naming a specific rule or punishment.

It should therefore not be restricted to one historical duty without evidence.

Does إِصْر (iṣr) mean only a covenant here?

Lexically, iṣr can mean a weighty pledge or covenant.

That reading is also possible here: the believers ask not to be charged with a difficult commitment like those placed upon their predecessors.

The verb taḥmil, however, shows that weight and difficulty form part of the sentence’s image.

Translating the word simply as “covenant” may therefore obscure the burden’s weight.

“Punishment” alone is also insufficient, since the word does not directly name a punishment here.

“Heavy burden” is a more natural equivalent that preserves the sentence’s meaning. An explanation can clarify that the burden may be a difficult commitment, duty or consequence.

Two successive requests distinguished

After وَلا تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْنَا إِصْرًا (wa-lā taḥmil ʿalaynā iṣran), another sentence follows:

رَبَّنَا وَلَا تُحَمِّلْنَا مَا لَا طَاقَةَ لَنَا بِهِ (rabbanā wa-lā tuḥammilnā mā lā ṭāqata lanā bihi), “Our Lord, do not impose upon us what we have no strength to bear.”

The two requests are closely related, but not identical.

The first speaks of a heavy burden like one placed upon people before the believers.

In the second, they ask The God not to impose what they lack the strength to bear.

A heavy burden is not necessarily beyond human capacity. A task may be extremely difficult while remaining within someone’s ability.

Iṣr should therefore not be defined directly as “something no one can possibly do.”

Treating the two expressions as completely identical erases their linguistic distinction.

In this context, iṣr emphasizes weight and binding obligation; mā lā ṭāqata lanā bihi explicitly states inability to bear something.

كَمَا حَمَلْتَهُ عَلَى الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِنَا (kamā ḥamaltahu ʿalā alladhīna min qablinā)

This clause refers to a burden placed upon people before the Qur’an’s audience.

The expression itself names neither a particular community nor a specific rule.

Other ayahs mention restrictions prescribed for earlier people. For example, Al-Nisāʾ 4:160 and Al-Anʿām 6:146 describe restrictions concerning wholesome things and food in connection with wrongdoing by some predecessors.

These ayahs can illuminate the background of onerous duties or deprivations.

Al-Baqarah 2:286 does not, however, establish that every rule in earlier religious laws was oppressive or unbearable without exception.

Nor does it establish that believers are exempt from every religious responsibility and commitment.

The ayah itself begins with the principle that The God charges no soul beyond its capacity. The prayer for relief from heavy burdens therefore belongs within the relationship between human responsibility and ability; it does not reject duty itself.

5. وَيَضَعُ عَنْهُمْ إِصْرَهُمْ: removing heavy burdens and restraints

Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 says:

الَّذِينَ يَتَّبِعُونَ الرَّسُولَ النَّبِيَّ الْأُمِّيَّ الَّذِي يَجِدُونَهُ مَكْتُوبًا عِنْدَهُمْ فِي التَّوْرَاةِ وَالْإِنْجِيلِ يَأْمُرُهُمْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَيَنْهَاهُمْ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ وَيُحِلُّ لَهُمُ الطَّيِّبَاتِ وَيُحَرِّمُ عَلَيْهِمُ الْخَبَائِثَ وَيَضَعُ عَنْهُمْ إِصْرَهُمْ وَالْأَغْلَالَ الَّتِي كَانَتْ عَلَيْهِمْ فَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا بِهِ وَعَزَّرُوهُ وَنَصَرُوهُ وَاتَّبَعُوا النُّورَ الَّذِي أُنْزِلَ مَعَهُ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

alladhīna yattabiʿūna al-rasūla al-nabiyya al-ummiyya alladhī yajidūnahu maktūban ʿindahum fī al-tawrāti wa-l-injīli yaʾmuruhum bi-l-maʿrūfi wa-yanhāhum ʿani al-munkari wa-yuḥillu lahumu al-ṭayyibāti wa-yuḥarrimu ʿalayhimu al-khabāʾitha wa-yaḍaʿu ʿanhum iṣrahum wa-l-aghlāla allatī kānat ʿalayhim fa-alladhīna āmanū bihi wa-ʿazzarūhu wa-naṣarūhu wa-ttabaʿū al-nūra alladhī unzila maʿahu ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn

“Those who follow the Messenger, the ummī Prophet, whom they find written with them in the Torah and the Gospel; he commands them to do what is right and forbids them what is wrong, makes wholesome things lawful for them and foul things unlawful, and removes their heavy burden and the shackles that were upon them. Those who believe in him, honor and support him, help him, and follow the light sent down with him—it is they who prosper.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:157

Here إِصْرَهُمْ (iṣrahum) concerns a burden weighing upon the group being addressed.

The verb يَضَعُ (yaḍaʿu) with عَنْهُمْ (ʿanhum) expresses removing or laying down that burden from them.

This construction has a clear connection with Al-Baqarah 2:286.

In Al-Baqarah, believers pray not to be charged with a heavy burden. In Al-Aʿrāf, removing heavy burdens and restraints is presented as a feature of the Prophet’s mission.

This closeness does not mean that both ayahs concern one identical, specific rule.

إِصْر (iṣr) and أَغْلَال (aghlāl)

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:157, iṣrahum appears alongside الْأَغْلَالَ (al-aghlāla).

Aghlāl is the plural of غُلّ (ghull), from GH L L. In physical usage, it denotes collars and restraints that bind a hand or the neck.

The Qur’an also uses this word for actual shackles and chains.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:157, however, its combination with iṣr and the description of the Prophet’s mission support a broader understanding of onerous restrictions and restraints.

Iṣr can be a binding burden or difficult commitment, while aghlāl gives a more explicit image of being bound and deprived of freedom of movement.

The words are close in meaning, but should not be assumed to be complete synonyms.

Their coordination alone does not prove that one must denote a religious restriction and the other a political or social chain. That division requires further evidence.

Here both contribute to an image of release from burdens and restrictions that had weighed upon people.

Does إِصْرَهُمْ (iṣrahum) mean only earlier onerous rules?

Context clearly supports a reading concerning weighty religious commitments and restrictions.

The ayah describes a Prophet who commands right, forbids wrong, permits wholesome things and prohibits foul things.

Rules, lawful and unlawful conduct, and the practical direction of human life are thus present in the text itself.

Within this setting, “removing iṣr” may refer to lifting onerous commitments or restrictions borne by the group in question.

The ayah does not name every such rule. Lists of earlier onerous duties compiled in later periods should therefore not be imported into the article as the meaning of iṣr itself without independent examination.

Nor does the ayah establish that every religious rule or practical restriction is intrinsically blameworthy iṣr.

Alongside removing the burden and shackles, the same ayah mentions commanding right and prohibiting foul things.

Removing heavy burdens is therefore not equivalent to abolishing every limit and responsibility.

Does release from إِصْر (iṣr) abolish all covenants?

The usage in Āl ʿImrān 3:81 makes this question important.

There, iṣrī is a valid divine covenant. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:157, iṣrahum is a burden that is removed.

If iṣr inherently meant an improper covenant, the Āl ʿImrān usage would be inexplicable. If it always meant only a commendable covenant, the removal of a heavy burden in Al-Aʿrāf and Al-Baqarah would become less clear.

The more precise approach preserves the shared element of binding obligation while identifying its kind separately in each context.

One covenant may be rightful and require fulfillment. Another commitment may become so onerous that its removal constitutes mercy and relief.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 therefore cannot be taken to abolish faithfulness to pledges, respect for others’ rights or all ethical responsibilities.

Audience and scope

The ayah speaks of those who find the ummī Prophet written with them in the Torah and the Gospel and follow him.

Study of A M M

This description provides a specific setting for removing iṣr and shackles.

Without independent evidence, we should not directly identify every restriction in another society as this iṣrahum, or assume that all followers of the Torah and Gospel in every period bore one identical burden.

Nor does the Qur’an’s account of removing onerous burdens authorize imposing a religious law upon people who are not addressed by its particular command.

The scope of each responsibility is established through the ayah itself, its audience and the relevant ayahs together.

What this usage clearly establishes is that release from onerous restrictions and opening the way to doing good have a real place in the description of this mission.

6. How the three Qur’anic uses connect

Setting the three ayahs together clarifies the relationship between their meanings.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:81, iṣr is a covenant that is undertaken.

In Al-Baqarah 2:286, iṣr is a burden placed upon a person.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:157, iṣr is a burden removed from a person.

All three involve binding responsibility: something a person is bound by or bears.

Three different verbs, however, help determine the contextual meaning.

أَخَذْتُمْ (akhadhtum) in Āl ʿImrān highlights accepting and undertaking the covenant.

تَحْمِلْ (taḥmil) in Al-Baqarah shows the weight of what is placed upon someone.

يَضَعُ عَنْهُمْ (yaḍaʿu ʿanhum) in Al-Aʿrāf expresses removing that kind of burden and obligation.

This difference shows why the noun iṣr cannot be expected to supply the whole meaning. Its accompanying verb, preposition, pronoun and context also determine what is intended.

Nor should the three ayahs be combined into an absolute historical rule in which every covenant begins as something commendable, necessarily becomes oppressive, and must eventually be removed.

The Qur’an does not prescribe such a fixed sequence for all covenants.

The evidence shows the word’s capacity to express a binding relationship and also a heavy burden arising from it.

7. A Ṣ R and neighboring roots

A Ṣ R (إِصْر, iṣr) and ʿ H D (عَهْد, ʿahd)

Study of ʿ H D

ʿAhd in the Qur’an concerns a covenant, commitment or relationship whose fulfillment matters.

Iṣr can also mean a pledge, but binding obligation and the commitment’s weight become prominent in its Qur’anic uses.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:81, iṣrī means the firm divine covenant and can be explained through ʿahd.

In Al-Baqarah 2:286, however, “covenant” alone may not convey the image of a heavy burden adequately.

Iṣr and ʿahd therefore overlap in part of their meaning without being interchangeable in every context.

A Ṣ R (إِصْر, iṣr) and W TH Q (مِيثَاق, mīthāq)

Study of W TH Q

Mīthāq concerns a firm and dependable covenant.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:81, mīthāq and iṣr occur in the same ayah.

Their combination shows that both can describe an important commitment.

Neither their morphological patterns nor their juxtaposition establishes that mīthāq is always stronger than ʿahd and iṣr stronger than both.

Here iṣr denotes a covenant accepted and undertaken; mīthāq reinforces its firmness and validity.

A Ṣ R (إِصْر, iṣr) and GH L L (أَغْلَال, aghlāl)

Study of GH L L

Arabic ghull denotes a restraint binding a hand or the neck.

Iṣr can be a burden or commitment that weighs upon someone.

The two occur together in Al-Aʿrāf 7:157.

Their combination places both within the field of restriction and constraint, while aghlāl provides a more explicit image of chains and binding.

Iṣr, by contrast, can express a covenant or onerous duty in Qur’anic usage without referring to a physical restraint.

A Ṣ R (إِصْر, iṣr) and W Z R (وِزْر, wizr)

Study of W Z R

Wizr also occurs in Qur’anic contexts involving burden, sin and responsibility arising from action.

It connects with iṣr through weight and bearing something.

In many Qur’anic uses, however, wizr concerns responsibility for wrongdoing, whereas iṣr can be a valid covenant, as in Āl ʿImrān 3:81.

Iṣrī therefore cannot be translated as “My sin” or “My burden of sin.”

An overlap in the burden image does not make all derivatives identical in moral value and usage.

A Ṣ R (إِصْر, iṣr) and Ḥ R J (حَرَج, ḥaraj)

Study of Ḥ R J

In Qur’anic usage, ḥaraj concerns constriction, difficulty and restriction.

Al-Ḥajj 22:78 speaks of not placing hardship in religion.

This is close to removing iṣr in Al-Aʿrāf 7:157: both can concern relief and the removal of onerous restrictions.

Ḥaraj is not itself a pledge or covenant, however, while iṣr can have that meaning.

Their closeness therefore covers only part of the field of restriction and difficulty.

A Ṣ R (إِصْر, iṣr) and A S R (أَسْر, asr)

Study of A S R

The Arabic root A S R concerns binding and taking captive.

Asīr in the Qur’an denotes a captive. Al-Insān 76:28 also uses أَسْرَهُمْ (asrahum) in connection with the firmness of the human constitution.

A S R and A Ṣ R are close in the concept of binding, but are separate roots.

Qur’anic iṣr concerns covenants and binding burdens and should not be equated with asr in the sense of physical captivity.

The existence of a restraint or covenant does not in itself mean that someone is imprisoned.

A Ṣ R (إِصْر, iṣr) and Ṣ R R (أَصَرَّ, aṣarra)

Study of Ṣ R R

This distinction is especially important when reading the Arabic forms.

Insistence—persisting in a demand or decision—should be distinguished from the covenant or burden noun.

Arabic أَصَرَّ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ (aṣarra ʿalā al-shayʾi) means “he persisted in that matter.”

Aṣarra belongs to Ṣ R R, not A Ṣ R.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:135, وَلَمْ يُصِرُّوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلُوا (wa-lam yuṣirrū ʿalā mā faʿalū) describes those who do not knowingly persist in their wrongdoing.

Yuṣirrū in that ayah belongs to Ṣ R R.

Iṣr in Al-Baqarah, Āl ʿImrān and Al-Aʿrāf should therefore not be translated as “insistence” or “stubbornness.”

A resemblance in written form does not establish a shared root.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

One fixed English equivalent for A Ṣ R is unsuitable across all three ayahs.

Āl ʿImrān 3:81

The principal proposal for iṣrī is:

“My firm covenant.”

“My binding commitment” is also semantically sound.

In the complete clause:

“Do you acknowledge and undertake My firm covenant concerning this?”

This preserves iṣr’s relationship with mīthāq and accepting a commitment.

“My heavy burden” is unsuitable here because readers might understand the divine covenant as oppressive or objectionable.

Al-Baqarah 2:286

For wa-lā taḥmil ʿalaynā iṣran, the proposed translation is:

“And do not place a heavy burden upon us.”

This fits taḥmil and preserves the elements of weight and binding obligation.

An explanation may clarify that a difficult commitment or duty can be intended.

“Do not make us sinful” and “do not imprison us” are not direct translations of this sentence.

“Do not charge us with something completely impossible” may also overlap excessively with the following sentence.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:157

For wa-yaḍaʿu ʿanhum iṣrahum, the proposed translation is:

“And removes their heavy burden.”

If the binding element needs emphasis, one can say:

“And lifts from them the burden of the onerous commitments that were upon them.”

The second rendering is more explanatory, however: it introduces “commitments” on the basis of one of the word’s semantic possibilities.

In the complete ayah, the combination of iṣr and aghlāl permits:

“And removes their heavy burden and the shackles that were upon them.”

Here “heavy burden” translates iṣr and “shackles” translates aghlāl.

Using only “chains” for both would erase their distinction.

Among non-Qur’anic derivatives, āṣirah means a bond or relationship; iṣār a cord or means of binding; and maʾṣir a place or means of restraint.

These meanings belong in the discussion of Arabic usage; they should not all be packed into the translation of one Qur’anic word.

9. Conclusion

Arabic A Ṣ R concerns binding, holding, constraining and establishing a bond that ties someone or something to a matter.

Materially, this appears in a cord, rope or means of binding. Socially, āṣirah can be a kinship tie or pledge. In the field of commitment, iṣr is a covenant whose fulfillment becomes someone’s responsibility.

That obligation may become heavy and difficult in some circumstances; hence Arabic also uses iṣr for a heavy burden.

The root’s three Qur’anic occurrences each illuminate an aspect of this semantic field.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:81, iṣrī is the firm and binding divine covenant. This shows that iṣr is not intrinsically negative.

In Al-Baqarah 2:286, iṣran is a heavy burden that believers ask The God not to place upon them as He placed it upon their predecessors.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:157, iṣrahum refers to a burden removed from the group concerned, together with onerous shackles.

These differences show why the root’s meaning should not be reduced to a vague general definition such as “difficulty” or “obligation.”

Iṣr is not merely difficulty, since it can be a covenant; nor is it merely a covenant, since it can express a heavy burden lifted from someone.

Removing iṣr should not be taken to abolish all rules, covenants and human responsibilities. While speaking of lifting onerous burdens, the Qur’an also emphasizes fulfilling pledges, acting rightfully and human responsibility.

A Ṣ R must also remain distinct from Ṣ R R. Iṣr concerns a binding restraint or commitment, whereas iṣrār, “insistence,” comes from a different root.

Forqan’s final definition of A Ṣ R:

“Binding, constraining and establishing an obligatory bond; in human affairs, a pledge or commitment that binds a person to something and may place a heavy burden upon them. In the Qur’an, iṣr denotes the firm divine covenant in Āl ʿImrān 3:81, a heavy burden in Al-Baqarah 2:286, and a binding restraint and burden that is removed in Al-Aʿrāf 7:157. The bond’s value and weight are not determined by the root alone, but depend on the kind of commitment, the accompanying verb and the ayah’s context.”
