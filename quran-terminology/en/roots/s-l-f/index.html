Going ahead, preceding others, passing, and doing deeds in advance

The Arabic root S L F concerns precedence: someone moving ahead of others, a group living before later generations, an action performed in the past, or something provided in advance of a future result.

The Quran has three main applications: salafa for something that happened previously and has passed; aslafa for what a person did beforehand, with its result becoming apparent later; and salaf for those who preceded others.

Their shared focus is precedence relative to another time or position. Precedence itself neither establishes excellence nor proves error. The value of predecessors, past deeds, and what people send ahead depends on those persons and actions.

1. The root in early Arabic and witnesses attributed to the pre-Quranic period

Going ahead in movement

Early Arabic poetry uses salaf for people moving ahead of others. This is a tangible sense: a group farther forward along a route.

A verse attributed to Qays ibn Al-Khaṭīm states:

لَوْ وَقَفُوا سَاعَةً نُسَائِلُهُمْ
رَيْثَ يُضَحِّي جِمَالَهُ السَّلَفُ

law waqafū sāʿatan nusāʾiluhum / raytha yuḍaḥḥī jimālahu al-salaf

“If only they would stop a while so we could question them, while the advance party lets its camels graze at midmorning.”

Al-salaf here refers to the caravan's advance party. The word is not confined in this use to ancestors, religious figures, or a specially authoritative group.

This attests spatial precedence and order of movement within the family.

Salaf: those who went before us

Another verse attributed to the early poet Ṭufayl Al-Ghanawī states:

مَضَوْا سَلَفًا قَصْدُ السَّبِيلِ عَلَيْهِمُ
وَصَرْفُ الْمَنَايَا بِالرِّجَالِ تَقَلُّبُ

maḍaw salafan qaṣdu al-sabīli ʿalayhimu / wa-ṣarfu al-manāyā bi-al-rijāli taqallubu

“They have gone before us; our road leads their way, and the turns of death continually pass among men.”

The subject is people who have died. Salafan indicates that they preceded others in leaving this world.

This connects spatial with temporal precedence. As the caravan's advance party is ahead along a route, earlier generations precede those living afterward along the course of time.

Both witnesses convey precedence clearly. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not independently establish the exact date of a particular verse. The poems support early Arabic usage but cannot date the emergence of every derivative.

Precedence in objects, food, and transactions

Arabic lexical usage records other tangible applications.

Sulfah denotes food eaten before the main meal. Its precedence is straightforward: it comes before the principal meal.

Sulāf and sulāfah denote the first juice of grapes, its choice early portion, and also wine. The order in which the juice emerges matters in some uses; purity and quality accompany others. Excellence is not a constant component of every derivative.

In transactions, salaf can denote an advance payment for something received later. A loan is also called salaf in lexical usage: the money is given first and repayment comes afterward.

These senses are recorded in Arabic dictionaries, but their later recording does not prove that every detail of later transactions already existed in exactly the same form before the Quran.

Other forms with these consonants are recorded for marriage relationships, tools, or physical conditions. Their historical connection with precedence is not equally clear, so a single derivational story should not be imposed on them all.

Precedence provides a clearer basis for understanding the Quranic uses.

2. Quranic forms and their semantic focus

S L F occurs eight times in the Quran in three principal formations.

Salafa: a basic perfect verb meaning to have occurred earlier or passed. Its five occurrences are Al-Baqarah 2:275, Al-Nisāʾ 4:22, Al-Nisāʾ 4:23, Al-Māʾidah 5:95, and Al-Anfāl 8:38.

Aslafat / aslaftum: Form IV perfect verbs, meaning to do beforehand or send ahead. They occur in Yūnus 10:30 and Al-Ḥāqqah 69:24.

Salafan: a noun denoting predecessors or a group that went before, occurring in Al-Zukhruf 43:56.

Other known forms, including sālif, aslāf, sulāf, sulfah, istislāf, and taslīf, do not occur in those forms in the Quran.

The basic verb salafa emphasizes an event or action occurring before the relevant time. In Form IV, aslafa, a subject has done or supplied something beforehand. This distinction becomes especially clear in passages about human deeds and reckoning.

3. Mā salafa: what happened before

Most Quranic occurrences appear in expressions such as mā salafa and mā qad salafa.

They refer to an action or state already realized before the time in question. Salafa itself does not determine whether it is forgiven, condemned, accepted, or legally concluded. The accompanying clauses and context establish that.

The past and a change of conduct concerning ribā

Al-Baqarah 2:275 states:

فَمَنْ جَاءَهُ مَوْعِظَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّهِ فَانْتَهَىٰ فَلَهُ مَا سَلَفَ وَأَمْرُهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ

fa-man jāʾahu mawʿiẓatun min rabbihi fa-intahā fa-lahu mā salafa wa-amruhu ilā Allāh

Al-Baqarah 2:275

“Whoever receives an admonition from his Lord and desists may retain what has passed, and his affair rests with The God.”

Mā salafa here concerns the person's earlier situation and transactions involving ribā.

The sentence sets out three stages: receiving admonition, ceasing the prohibited conduct, and addressing what occurred earlier.

Fa-lahu mā salafa distinguishes past conduct from its continuation. What occurred before the admonition and desisting is addressed as past; this does not authorize continuing ribā after admonition. Receiving admonition does not alone prove complete prior ignorance.

Wa-amruhu ilā Allāhi, “his affair rests with God,” is also part of the statement. Salafa alone cannot establish that every ethical, legal, and financial question concerning the past has been settled in one uniform way.

The root marks the action as past; its treatment follows from the whole sentence.

Family relationships and past actions

After prohibiting marriage to women whom one's fathers married, Al-Nisāʾ 4:22 states:

إِلَّا مَا قَدْ سَلَفَ

illā mā qad salaf

Al-Nisāʾ 4:22; Al-Nisāʾ 4:23

“Except what has already passed.”

The same expression occurs in Al-Nisāʾ 4:23 regarding joining two sisters in marriage.

In both, salafa concerns a situation preceding the present directive.

The expression distinguishes past conduct from the instruction now to be followed. A situation's having existed does not make its continuation permissible.

Illā mā qad salafa does not itself specify every legal, family, or financial consequence of those relationships. Lexically, it refers to what occurred previously.

Qad before the perfect verb emphasizes its realization. Mā qad salafa means what already took place and belongs to past events.

Past conduct and repeated violation

Concerning hunting while in the consecrated state, Al-Māʾidah 5:95 states:

عَفَا اللَّهُ عَمَّا سَلَفَ وَمَنْ عَادَ فَيَنْتَقِمُ اللَّهُ مِنْهُ

ʿafā Allāhu ʿammā salafa wa-man ʿāda fa-yantaqimu Allāhu minhu

Al-Māʾidah 5:95

“The God has pardoned what has passed; whoever returns to it, God will exact retribution from him.”

This clearly distinguishes lexical meaning from moral and legal judgment.

Salafa marks the conduct as past. Pardon is expressed by ʿafā, not by salafa itself.

Wa-man ʿāda concerns returning to the prohibited conduct. The verse distinguishes past conduct from recurrence; the opening of the verse separately addresses deliberate killing of game.

Pastness and pardon perform different roles in this sentence. That distinction comes from their established meanings and syntax; it need not rest on claiming that words of related meaning could never occur together.

Ceasing earlier conduct and forgiveness

Al-Anfāl 8:38 states:

قُلْ لِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا إِنْ يَنْتَهُوا يُغْفَرْ لَهُمْ مَا قَدْ سَلَفَ وَإِنْ يَعُودُوا فَقَدْ مَضَتْ سُنَّتُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

qul li-alladhīna kafarū in yantahū yughfar lahum mā qad salafa wa-in yaʿūdū fa-qad maḍat sunnatu al-awwalīn

Al-Anfāl 8:38

“Tell those who disbelieve: if they desist, what has already passed will be forgiven them; but if they return, the precedent of earlier peoples has already taken its course.”

Forgiveness is expressed by yughfar, while mā qad salafa identifies its object: earlier actions.

The condition in in yantahū also matters. Forgiveness is connected with desisting, not simply with time having elapsed.

The verse contains both salafa and maḍat.

Salafa concerns the addressees' past deeds, while maḍat concerns the precedent of earlier peoples. Both look to the past, but their respective subjects are earlier conduct and an already established precedent.

Across these five uses, salafa does not itself mean sin being erased, responsibility being removed, or past conduct being accepted. Pastness is temporal; judgment is expressed elsewhere in the sentence.

4. Aslafa: what a person did beforehand

Form IV occurs twice, both times concerning human deeds and their outcome.

The focus moves from mere past occurrence to performing an action before its outcome or reckoning.

Every soul encounters what it sent ahead

Yūnus 10:30 states:

هُنَالِكَ تَبْلُو كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَا أَسْلَفَتْ وَرُدُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ مَوْلَاهُمُ الْحَقِّ وَضَلَّ عَنْهُمْ مَا كَانُوا يَفْتَرُونَ

hunālika tablū kullu nafsin mā aslafat wa-ruddū ilā Allāhi mawlāhumu al-ḥaqqi wa-ḍalla ʿanhum mā kānū yaftarūn

Yūnus 10:30

“There every soul experiences what it did beforehand; they are returned to The God, their true Protector, and what they used to fabricate disappears from them.”

Aslafat is a Form IV perfect verb. Its grammatical subject is an implicit feminine singular pronoun referring back to nafs. Kullu nafsin is the subject of tablū. The deeds are those each person performed before reaching this stage.

This differs significantly from mā salafa in the earlier passages.

Mā salafa presents an event or behavior as past. Mā aslafat combines temporal precedence with personal agency: the soul acted earlier and now encounters its deeds.

Form IV does not always imply a conscious intention to send something into the future. Here it emphasizes prior performance; the connection to future reckoning and results comes from context.

Mā aslafat is not restricted to good deeds. Kullu nafsin is general; the value and outcome of a deed depend on the deed and its judgment.

Good deeds sent ahead

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:24 states:

كُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا هَنِيئًا بِمَا أَسْلَفْتُمْ فِي الْأَيَّامِ الْخَالِيَةِ

kulū wa-ishrabū hanīʾan bi-mā aslaftum fī al-ayyāmi al-khāliyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:24

“Eat and drink with enjoyment for what you sent ahead in days gone by.”

Those addressed enjoy a favorable outcome in the scene of reckoning.

Aslaftum concerns their deeds in their previous life. Fī al-ayyāmi al-khāliyati identifies when: days that have now passed.

People act in the past, and the effects and results become apparent later.

This resembles the financial use of islāf in supplying something in advance, but the verse concerns deeds rather than an economic transaction.

The comparison does not make the relationship with God a commercial contract with a fixed exchange rate. The verse connects past deeds with present enjoyment; the nature and extent of reward require the context of reckoning and divine mercy.

The two Form IV passages differ: Yūnus 10:30 refers generally to every soul's deeds, whereas the reward context of Al-Ḥāqqah 69:24 points to the addressees' good deeds.

Both express prior action; context establishes its value.

5. Salafan: predecessors and their authority

The noun salaf occurs only once, in Al-Zukhruf.

Following the account of Pharaoh and his people, Al-Zukhruf 43:56 states:

فَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ سَلَفًا وَمَثَلًا لِلْآخِرِينَ

fa-jaʿalnāhum salafan wa-mathalan li-l-ākhirīn

Al-Zukhruf 43:56

“We made them predecessors and an example for those to come.”

The pronoun “them” refers to Pharaoh and his company, whose rebellion and fate are described in the preceding verses.

This matters because salaf is not necessarily a title of praise in the Quran.

Pharaoh's people are salaf because they preceded later groups and their story remained for them. They are earlier in time, but their conduct and fate are condemned in context.

Wa-mathalan li-l-ākhirīna says their story became an example for later people, with a warning function in context.

The transmitted readings include salafan and sulufan. This article follows salafan, the reading of Ḥafṣ. Both concern predecessors; the vocalization difference does not remove the central sense of precedence.

Being earlier is not the same as being righteous

Someone who lived or died before another may be their salaf. This temporal relationship alone does not determine moral worth.

People may speak of righteous ancestors or ancestors involved in injustice and error. Both are predecessors in temporal terms.

Al-Zukhruf 43:56 applies that general field to a condemned group.

Expressions such as salaf ṣāliḥ contain two components: salaf denotes predecessors, while ṣāliḥ adds righteousness. The second is not intrinsic to the first.

Later technical or religious labels derived from salaf cannot determine its original Quranic meaning.

The antiquity of a person or idea does not by itself establish correctness. Predecessors are assessed through knowledge, conduct, trustworthiness, and their relationship to truth, not merely their earlier date.

6. Distinctions from related roots

S L F and S B Q

Both concern precedence, but their semantic fields are not identical.

S B Q can emphasize overtaking, going ahead, or preceding in a course of action. The relation between those ahead and those behind often matters.

S L F more often emphasizes a person, event, or deed already preceding a later situation or having passed.

Sabq may foreground the process of going ahead; salaf foregrounds what is already earlier or past.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute rule for every Arabic use.

S L F and Q D M

Q D M also concerns being ahead, approaching, and sending forward.

Quranic expressions such as mā qaddamat aydīkum concern deeds people have performed and provided in advance.

Aslafat in Yūnus 10:30 is close to that field: an earlier deed confronts its doer at reckoning.

Q D M has a broader field involving the front, approaching, and giving precedence. The Quranic uses of S L F are more closely associated with temporal priority and previous deeds.

Overlap does not make all their derivatives identical.

S L F and M Ḍ Y

Maḍā concerns passing, elapsing, or moving beyond a stage.

Salafa can likewise express pastness, while emphasizing precedence relative to a later time or group.

Al-Anfāl 8:38 offers a comparison: mā qad salafa concerns people's past deeds, while fa-qad maḍat sunnatu al-awwalīna concerns the precedent of earlier peoples.

Their closeness is clear, but each verb fits its subject. They should not be treated as interchangeable in every construction.

S L F and KH L F

These can contrast within the order of before and after.

Salaf concerns someone or something earlier; khalaf can concern what comes behind or after another.

Earlier generations are salaf relative to later ones, and later generations are khalaf relative to those before them.

Being earlier does not intrinsically confer virtue, just as being later does not intrinsically imply corruption or worthlessness. Each generation's character and conduct matter.

KH L F also includes succession, difference, and opposition. Its entire field cannot be explained solely through the contrast with salaf.

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan

The equivalent must fit the verbal form and sentence subject.

Salafa in Al-Baqarah 2:275, Al-Nisāʾ 4:22, Al-Nisāʾ 4:23, Al-Māʾidah 5:95, and Al-Anfāl 8:38 may be rendered “has passed,” “occurred earlier,” or “what happened before.”

It must not directly become “was forgiven,” “became permissible,” or “was freed from responsibility.” Where present, those meanings come from other verbs and qualifications.

Aslafat in Yūnus 10:30 can be “did beforehand” or “sent ahead.” The rendering must preserve the general scope of deeds without restricting them to good ones.

Aslaftum in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:24 can be “you sent ahead” or “you did before.” “Sent ahead” better preserves the link between past action and later outcome.

Salafan in Al-Zukhruf 43:56 means predecessors or those who went before. It should not become “righteous leaders” or “worthy ancestors”: the word itself contains no such qualification, and the context concerns a condemned group.

In transactions, salaf can mean a loan or advance payment. That specialized sense must not be imposed on Quranic passages about past deeds, reckoning, and predecessors.

Conclusion

S L F concerns precedence and earlier occurrence. Early Arabic witnesses apply it to those moving ahead and those who passed before others. Recorded food, transactional, and physical senses show further extensions, though their dates and derivational relationships are not equally certain.

In the Quran, salafa denotes what occurred before; aslafa presents prior action with human agency; and salaf denotes a group preceding others.

Two points emerge.

First, an action's being past is not the same as its being forgiven or accepted. Pardon, forgiveness, desisting, and returning are expressed through distinct verbs and constructions. Salafa marks the temporal relation.

Second, being a predecessor does not intrinsically confer virtue or rightness. The Quran calls Pharaoh's people salaf and makes them a warning example. Predecessors' authority depends on truth and conduct rather than chronology alone.

The root can therefore be defined as follows:

S L F denotes precedence relative to another person or a later time: in movement and personal order, in events passing, or in deeds performed before their outcome or reckoning.

Context can extend this to predecessors, past deeds, deeds sent ahead, and advance payments. Moral value and judgment do not come from the root alone; they must be established in context.
