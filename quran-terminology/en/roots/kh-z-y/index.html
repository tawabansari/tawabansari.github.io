The Qur’anic Root KH Z Y

Humiliation, disgrace, and a fall in standing before others

The root خ ز ي (KH Z Y) is used in the Qur’an in the field of humiliation, disgrace, and being put to shame. Sometimes a person experiences خزي (khizy); sometimes another person brings it upon them through أخزى (akhzā). Sometimes خِزي (khizy) itself is described as a condition or outcome in this world, and in one verse The God is called مُخْزِي الْكَافِرِينَ (mukhzī al-kāfirīn).

Khizy should not, however, be treated simply as a synonym of lowliness, punishment, or inward shame. In Ta-Ha 20:134, the Qur’an places ذل (dhull) and خزي (khizy) together. In the account of Lot, it uses إخزاء (ikhzāʾ) in a situation parallel to فضح (faḍḥ), and in several verses distinguishes “khizy in this world” from “punishment in the Hereafter.” These witnesses show that alongside a decline in standing, khizy prominently involves disgrace and the exposure of that fallen condition.

Qur’anic forms

The root خ ز ي (KH Z Y) occurs 26 times in 24 verses in the Qur’an. Its Qur’anic forms are:

نَخْزَىٰ (nakhzā), from the basic verb, once;

Form IV forms of أَخْزَى (akhzā), twelve times;

خِزْي (khizy) eleven times;

أَخْزَىٰ (akhzā) as a comparative form, once;

and مُخْزِي (mukhzī), the Form IV active participle, once.

نَخْزَىٰ (nakhzā), from the basic verb, once;
Form IV forms of أَخْزَى (akhzā), twelve times;
خِزْي (khizy) eleven times;
أَخْزَىٰ (akhzā) as a comparative form, once;
and مُخْزِي (mukhzī), the Form IV active participle, once.

The Qur’an’s morphological field therefore concentrates broadly on two branches: being in a state of khizy and humiliating and disgracing another.

1. Khizy in early Arabic: humiliation, disgrace, and shame

Early sources do not give this family one completely uniform definition.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn says:

الخِزْيُ من السوء (al-khizyu min al-sūʾ) — khizy belongs to the field of badness or misfortune.

It distinguishes خِزي (khizy) from خَزاية (khazāyah), explaining khazāyah through a person’s feeling ashamed of their own offensive action. Lisān al-ʿArab likewise describes khizy in terms of falling into affliction and evil, having one’s situation exposed, and then becoming abased and humiliated. The same source also explains it through humiliation, disgrace, and exposure to dishonor.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī attempts to bring these branches together through انكسار (inkisār), a breaking or collapse: one that may arise inwardly as shame, or outwardly as humiliation and diminished standing before others. This analysis helps explain the closeness of shame and disgrace, but should not be turned into a definitive history of derivation.

Ibn Fāris proposes a different route. In the weak-root family خ ز و/ي (KH Z W/Y), he records two separate origins: managing and restraining, and driving away. He places أخزاه الله (akhzāhu Allāh) in the second branch, explaining it as driving someone away and making them an object of abhorrence, and also connects shame with distancing and withdrawal. This analysis shows that even early lexicographers did not agree on a single original image.

For this root, it is therefore better not to construct a supposedly certain original physical image. What established uses show more clearly is the field of humiliation, disgrace, being put to shame, and shame that diminishes a person’s standing.

A witness from early poetry

A poem in the Mufaḍḍaliyyāt attributed to Bashāmah ibn al-Ghadīr contains:

خِزْيُ الحَيَاةِ وَحَرْبُ الصَّدِيقِ
وَكُلٌّ أَرَاهُ طَعَامًا وَبِيلَا

Khizyu l-ḥayāti wa-ḥarbu ṣ-ṣadīqi / Wa-kullun arāhu ṭaʿāman wabīlā.

“The disgrace of life and war against a friend—each I regard as grievous food.”

The poet sees his people facing two unwelcome alternatives: the disgrace of life and war against a friend and ally. The commentary on the Mufaḍḍaliyyāt connects خزي الحياة (khizy al-ḥayāh) with the dishonor of abandoning an ally. In this witness, khizy is thus more than simple weakness: it is life accompanied by dishonor and a loss of reputation.

Chronological reports about Bashāmah himself are not entirely uniform. The familiar account connects him with the pre-Islamic setting and Zuhayr’s generation, but some sources also list him among poets of the Islamic period. This verse is therefore an important witness to early poetic usage, but should not be called an unquestionably pre-Qur’anic chronological witness without qualification.

2. خَزِيَ (khaziya): becoming humiliated and disgraced

The Qur’an’s only basic-form verb occurs in Ta-Ha 20:134:

مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ نَذِلَّ وَنَخْزَىٰ

Min qabli an nadhilla wa-nakhzā.

“Before we are abased and fall into disgrace.”

This verse is especially important for understanding the root because it coordinates نذل (nadhilla) and نخزى (nakhzā). Even if one supposed them completely synonymous, their appearance together at least invites examination of a distinction. The Qur’an uses both roots independently.

Dhull has a broad field of being lowered, defeated, subordinated, or deprived of honor and strength. Khizy may include such abasement, but lexical sources and Qur’anic uses bring it closer to the disgrace and dishonor resulting from that situation.

The expression in Ta-Ha can therefore be understood approximately as:

“Before we are humiliated and disgraced.”

This translation preserves the difference between the words better than repeating “abased and abased.”

3. أَخْزَى (akhzā): bringing another into khizy

Most verbal uses of this root are in Form IV.

In Ali Imran:

رَبَّنَا إِنَّكَ مَنْ تُدْخِلِ النَّارَ فَقَدْ أَخْزَيْتَهُ

Rabbanā innaka man tudkhili n-nāra fa-qad akhzaytahu.

“Our Lord, whoever You admit into the Fire, You have indeed disgraced.”

Ali Imran 3:192

And two verses later:

وَلَا تُخْزِنَا يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ

Wa-lā tukhzinā yawma l-qiyāmati.

“And do not disgrace us on the Day of Resurrection.”

Ali Imran 3:194

Here akhzā does not mean merely causing pain or administering punishment. Entry into the Fire also brings humiliation and disgrace upon the person.

The same construction recurs concerning punishment in several other verses:

عَذَابٌ يُخْزِيهِ

ʿAdhābun yukhzīhi.

“A punishment that disgraces him.”

Hud 11:39 and Hud 11:93

And:

مَنْ يَأْتِيهِ عَذَابٌ يُخْزِيهِ

Man yaʾtīhi ʿadhābun yukhzīhi.

“Who will receive a punishment that disgraces him.”

Az-Zumar 39:40

Punishment is something that humiliates and disgraces the person. Punishment and ikhzāʾ are therefore not the same word: one is the means or event, the other the quality and condition it brings upon someone.

4. The account of Lot: the strongest witness to the connection with disgrace

In Hud 11:78, Lot says to his people:

فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَلَا تُخْزُونِ فِي ضَيْفِي

Fa-ttaqū llāha wa-lā tukhzūni fī ḍayfī.

“So be mindful of The God, and do not disgrace and humiliate me before my guests.”

In Surah Al-Hijr, within the same account and situation, he says:

إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ ضَيْفِي فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ

Inna hāʾulāʾi ḍayfī fa-lā tafḍaḥūni.

“These are my guests, so do not disgrace me.”

Al-Hijr 15:68

And immediately afterward:

وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَلَا تُخْزُونِ

Wa-ttaqū llāha wa-lā tukhzūni.

“And be mindful of The God, and do not disgrace me.”

Al-Hijr 15:69

This comparison provides one of the clearest instances of the Qur’an illuminating the field of khizy through its own language. In one telling, لا تخزون (lā tukhzūni) appears, and in the other لا تفضحون (lā tafḍaḥūni), within the same crisis concerning Lot’s guests.

The root ف ض ح (F Ḍ Ḥ) tends toward the exposure of something objectionable and a person becoming known for it. Ibn Fāris explains it through the uncovering of something, usually an offensive matter. Lisān al-ʿArab uses افتضح (iftaḍaḥa) for someone whose bad affair has become exposed and widely known.

The two roots are still not exact synonyms. Faḍḥ places more emphasis on exposing and uncovering the objectionable matter; khizy on the resulting state of disgrace, humiliation, and loss of reputation. Yet the account of Lot shows that their overlap is real and substantial.

Another point also emerges: a person subjected to khizy need not have committed the offensive action themselves. Lot asks his people not to place him in disgrace by violating his guests. “Khizy = a person’s shame over their own sin” is therefore not an adequate definition for every Qur’anic use.

5. خِزْي (khizy) in this world: disgrace is an outcome, not a name for every punishment

The noun خِزْي (khizy) occurs eleven times in the Qur’an, and a large proportion of these occurrences concern this world.

Al-Baqarah 2:85 says:

فَمَا جَزَاءُ مَنْ يَفْعَلُ ذَٰلِكَ مِنْكُمْ إِلَّا خِزْيٌ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا

Fa-mā jazāʾu man yafʿalu dhālika minkum illā khizyun fī l-ḥayāti d-dunyā.

“What, then, is the recompense of those among you who do that, except disgrace in worldly life?”

In Al-Baqarah 2:114:

لَهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا خِزْيٌ وَلَهُمْ فِي الْآخِرَةِ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

Lahum fī d-dunyā khizyun wa-lahum fī l-ākhirati ʿadhābun ʿaẓīmun.

“For them is disgrace in this world, and for them in the Hereafter is a tremendous punishment.”

The same construction appears in Al-Maidah 5:33 and Al-Maidah 5:41: disgrace in this world stands on one side of the sentence, and tremendous punishment in the Hereafter on the other.

Al-Hajj 22:9 also says:

لَهُ فِي الدُّنْيَا خِزْيٌ وَنُذِيقُهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ

Lahu fī d-dunyā khizyun wa-nudhīquhu yawma l-qiyāmati ʿadhāba l-ḥarīqi.

“For him is disgrace in this world, and on the Day of Resurrection We will make him taste the punishment of burning.”

And Az-Zumar 39:26:

فَأَذَاقَهُمُ اللَّهُ الْخِزْيَ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَلَعَذَابُ الْآخِرَةِ أَكْبَرُ

Fa-adhāqahumu llāhu l-khizya fī l-ḥayāti d-dunyā wa-la-ʿadhābu l-ākhirati akbaru.

“So The God made them taste disgrace in worldly life, and the punishment of the Hereafter is greater.”

This pattern does not allow us to define khizy simply as a synonym of punishment.

Punishment can cause khizy;
khizy can be one aspect or result of punishment;
but the two are not the same word.

In At-Tawbah 9:14, this distinction becomes clearer even within a single sentence:

يُعَذِّبْهُمُ اللَّهُ بِأَيْدِيكُمْ وَيُخْزِهِمْ وَيَنْصُرْكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ

Yuʿadhdhibhumu llāhu bi-aydīkum wa-yukhzihim wa-yanṣurkum ʿalayhim.

“The God will punish them through your hands, disgrace them, and help you against them.”

يعذبهم (yuʿadhdhibhum) and يخزهم (yukhzihim) are two separate verbs. Ikhzāʾ therefore has a result beyond pain or punishment itself: defeat accompanied by humiliation and disgrace.

6. عَذَابُ الْخِزْي (ʿadhāb al-khizy): punishment characterized by disgrace

Alongside separating khizy from punishment, the Qur’an also combines them directly on two occasions.

Yunus 10:98:

عَذَابَ الْخِزْيِ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا

ʿAdhāba l-khizyi fī l-ḥayāti d-dunyā.

“The punishment of disgrace in worldly life.”

And Fussilat 41:16:

عَذَابَ الْخِزْيِ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا

ʿAdhāba l-khizyi fī l-ḥayāti d-dunyā.

“The punishment of disgrace in worldly life.”

In this construction, khizy explains the punishment’s quality or result: punishment accompanied by humiliation and disgrace.

Fussilat then says:

وَلَعَذَابُ الْآخِرَةِ أَخْزَىٰ

Wa-la-ʿadhābu l-ākhirati akhzā.

“And the punishment of the Hereafter is more disgraceful and humiliating.”

The form أَخْزَىٰ (akhzā) shows that in Qur’anic language, khizy admits of degree: one outcome may be more humiliating and disgraceful than another.

Khizy is therefore not the name of a fixed type of punishment, but a description of a condition that may accompany punishment, defeat, exposure to disgrace, or an outcome in the Hereafter.

7. Khizy in this world and the Hereafter

Khizy is not only a social phenomenon in worldly life.

In Ali Imran, entry into the Fire is described as ikhzāʾ. Abraham prays:

وَلَا تُخْزِنِي يَوْمَ يُبْعَثُونَ

Wa-lā tukhzinī yawma yubʿathūna.

“And do not disgrace me on the day they are raised.”

Ash-Shuara 26:87

And At-Tahrim 66:8 says:

يَوْمَ لَا يُخْزِي اللَّهُ النَّبِيَّ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مَعَهُ

Yawma lā yukhzī llāhu n-nabiyya wa-lladhīna āmanū maʿahu.

“The day when The God will not disgrace the Prophet and those who have believed with him.”

The Qur’anic element of disgrace and humiliation is therefore not confined to the presence of an earthly society. Khizy can also be spoken of in the scene of Resurrection, because the issue is a person’s manifest fall in standing and their encounter with a humiliating outcome.

In At-Tawbah 9:63, after mentioning the Fire of Hell and remaining in it, the text says:

ذَٰلِكَ الْخِزْيُ الْعَظِيمُ

Dhālika l-khizyu l-ʿaẓīmu.

“That is the tremendous disgrace.”

An outcome in the Hereafter can thus itself be presented as a full instance of khizy.

8. إِنَّ الْخِزْيَ الْيَوْمَ وَالسُّوءَ (inna al-khizya al-yawma wa-l-sūʾa): khizy is not identical with sūʾ either

An-Nahl 16:27 says:

إِنَّ الْخِزْيَ الْيَوْمَ وَالسُّوءَ عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ

Inna l-khizya l-yawma wa-s-sūʾa ʿalā l-kāfirīna.

“Today disgrace and evil are upon those who commit kufr.”

The coordination of الخزي (al-khizy) and السوء (al-sūʾ) at least shows that the two designations should not be reduced to one meaning without examination. The root س و أ (S W ʾ) has a much wider field of badness, ugliness, harm, and unwelcome events; khizy is a more specific branch of humiliation and disgrace.

Khizy is therefore not merely “something bad.” Something may be sūʾ without being khizy.

9. KH Z Y compared with DH L L: abasement and disgrace

Comparison with ذ ل ل (DH L L) begins within the Qur’an itself:

مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ نَذِلَّ وَنَخْزَىٰ

Min qabli an nadhilla wa-nakhzā.

“Before we are humiliated and disgraced.”

Ta-Ha 20:134

Dhull has a broad Qur’anic family. Depending on derivative and context, it may express a fall in standing, defeat, humility, or being tractable. This breadth shows that dhull does not inherently require disgrace or the exposure of a fault.

Abū Hilāl al-ʿAskarī summarizes the distinction as khizy being abasement accompanied by exposure to disgrace. This formula accords closely with Qur’anic evidence, but is better treated as a semantic tendency than a mathematical definition without exceptions.

Thus:

Dhull primarily expresses a lowering and breaking of a position of power and honor;
khizy tends to accompany that fall with shame, disgrace, and loss of reputation.

Ta-Ha 20:134 keeps both together.

10. KH Z Y compared with F Ḍ Ḥ: disgrace and exposure

The closest Qur’anic witness to their difference is again the account of Lot:

وَلَا تُخْزُونِ فِي ضَيْفِي

Wa-lā tukhzūni fī ḍayfī.

“And do not disgrace me before my guests.”

Hud 11:78

And:

فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ

Fa-lā tafḍaḥūni.

“So do not expose me to disgrace.”

Al-Hijr 15:68

Faḍḥ emphasizes the uncovering and exposure of something objectionable. Ibn Fāris says that one of its basic senses is something becoming uncovered, usually in an offensive matter. Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains iftaḍaḥa as a person’s bad affair becoming exposed and widely known.

In khizy, the psychological and social result is more prominent: the person becomes humiliated and disgraced.

We can therefore observe this tendency:

Faḍḥ: the exposure of an objectionable matter and uncovering of a fault;
khizy: the disgrace and fall in standing that may result from such exposure.

The account of Lot nevertheless shows how close the two may become in practice.

11. KH Z Y compared with H W N: khizy and hawān

The family ه و ن (H W N) is also used in the Qur’an for humiliating and for humiliating punishment:

عَذَابَ الْهُونِ

ʿAdhāba l-hūni.

“The punishment of humiliation.”

And:

عَذَابٌ مُهِينٌ

ʿAdhābun muhīnun.

“A humiliating punishment.”

In هوان (hawān), being regarded as low and of little worth is prominent. The Qur’an even says:

وَمَنْ يُهِنِ اللَّهُ فَمَا لَهُ مِنْ مُكْرِمٍ

Wa-man yuhini llāhu fa-mā lahu min mukrimin.

“Whomever The God humiliates has no one to honor him.”

Al-Hajj 22:18

Khizy overlaps this field, and lexical sources repeatedly explain it through hawān. Yet uses of khizy, particularly the account of Lot and its comparison with faḍḥ, bring being disgraced and put to shame more prominently into view.

Hawān and khizy can therefore be considered close, but there is no reason to treat them as absolute synonyms.

12. مُخْزِي الْكَافِرِينَ (mukhzī al-kāfirīn): a divine description, not necessarily a proper name

At-Tawbah 9:2 says:

وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ مُخْزِي الْكَافِرِينَ

Wa-anna llāha mukhzī l-kāfirīna.

“And know that The God brings disgrace upon those who commit kufr.”

مُخْزِي (mukhzī) is the Form IV active participle, attributed to The God in this verse. The derivative therefore provides a description of divine action in the Qur’an: The God can humiliate and disgrace people.

The verse does not, however, say المُخْزِي (al-mukhzī) or present it as an independent definite name in a list of divine names. The verse’s construction is qualified:

مُخْزِي الْكَافِرِينَ (mukhzī al-kāfirīn)

The root establishes this action and description, but it should not be turned without evidence into an independent, unrestricted divine name.

Divine ikhzāʾ should not be imagined bodily either. In Qur’anic uses, ikhzāʾ points to an outcome of humiliation, defeat, and disgrace, not a particular bodily action.

13. Non-Qur’anic branches and their boundaries

Within the same field of weak root letters, Arabic sources also record forms such as خزا يخزوه (khazā yakhzūhu) for managing, restraining, or taming an animal. Ibn Fāris himself identifies this branch as an origin separate from إبعاد (ibʿād), driving away.

None of the Qur’an’s 26 occurrences means “managing” or “restraining.” Mere formal proximity between these branches therefore does not justify importing restraint, taming, or management into the Qur’anic meaning of khizy.

Sources also record خَزاية (khazāyah) for inward shame and modesty, but this verbal noun does not occur in the Qur’an. Qur’anic uses mainly concern khizy and ikhzāʾ: the condition of humiliation and disgrace, or placing another in that condition.

Translation guidance

One mechanical equivalent for خِزْي (khizy) will not serve every verse.

In many cases, “disgrace” is a natural and strong equivalent:

لَهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا خِزْيٌ

Lahum fī d-dunyā khizyun.

“For them is disgrace in this world.”

Where the emphasis falls more on a humiliating outcome, “humiliation” or “being put to shame” may also be precise.

خَزِيَ / نَخْزَىٰ (khaziya / nakhzā) can be rendered “become disgraced” or “be put to shame.”

أَخْزَى / يُخْزِي (akhzā / yukhzī) means, according to context, “disgraced,” “put to shame,” or “humiliated and disgraced.” Where a brief primary translation is needed, “disgrace” is often preferable to piling up several equivalents.

أَخْزَىٰ (akhzā) in Fussilat 41:16 is a comparative form and can be translated “more disgraceful” or “more humiliating.”

مُخْزِي الْكَافِرِينَ (mukhzī al-kāfirīn) is “the one who disgraces those who commit kufr,” or more naturally, “will disgrace those who commit kufr,” depending on the sentence’s translated construction.

“Make ashamed” may come close in some contexts, but should not be a universal equivalent. In contemporary English it can suggest only an inward feeling of embarrassment, whereas Qur’anic khizy often involves humiliation and a manifest fall in standing.

Conclusion

In the Qur’an, خ ز ي (KH Z Y) is a family centered on humiliation and disgrace, but narrower than general dhull and broader than inward shame.

Early sources explain khizy through badness, humiliation, abasement, and disgrace, connecting khazāyah more closely with inward shame. Ibn Fāris attempts to explain the khizy branch through distancing and expulsion, but differences between sources show that an original physical image cannot confidently be reconstructed for this root.

The Qur’an itself clarifies the semantic boundary more effectively. In Ta-Ha 20:134, نذل (nadhilla) and نخزى (nakhzā) appear together, so khizy is not identical with dhull. In the account of Lot, لا تخزون (lā tukhzūni) appears in one telling in the same situation where another says فلا تفضحون (fa-lā tafḍaḥūni). Disgrace and exposure to dishonor are therefore important parts of the word’s field.

Khizy is not identical with punishment either. The Qur’an repeatedly places “khizy in this world” opposite “punishment in the Hereafter,” and in At-Tawbah 9:14 even gives يعذبهم (yuʿadhdhibhum) and يخزهم (yukhzihim) as separate verbs. Punishment can bring someone into khizy, and عذاب الخزي (ʿadhāb al-khizy) describes punishment that humiliates and disgraces; one should nevertheless not be made the definition of the other.

Khizy need not arise from a person’s own sin. Lot asks his people not to disgrace him concerning his guests through their conduct. The word’s core is therefore not a feeling of personal guilt, but being placed in a condition that breaks one’s standing and leaves one humiliated and disgraced.

This condition may occur in this world or in the Hereafter. Defeat, punishment, the exposure of one’s outcome, and entry into the Fire may cause khizy. The Qur’an even calls the punishment of the Hereafter أخزى (akhzā): more disgraceful and humiliating.

A cautious synthesis of the root is:

KH Z Y: a family describing humiliation and disgrace that lower a person’s standing, often accompanied by public dishonor or the exposure of that fall. Khizy overlaps dhull, hawān, and faḍḥ without being identical with any of them: it carries more of the element of disgrace than dhull, comes close to faḍḥ in the exposure of a fault, and remains distinct from punishment even though punishment may cause it.
