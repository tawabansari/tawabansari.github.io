1. Root meaning and word structure

«دَمْدَمَ» (damdama) is an Arabic quadriliteral verb. Early usages record enveloping, crushing, destroying, and sometimes anger or threatening speech. It occurs only once in the Qur’an, in the account of Thamūd’s outcome.

Damdama repeats the consonant pair D M and follows the pattern faʿlala. Its familiar verbal noun is «دَمْدَمَة» (damdamah), and its imperfect is «يُدَمْدِمُ» (yudamdimu). Repeated consonants in certain Arabic verbs can foreground intensity, continuity, repetition, or an action’s sound; none of these features, however, is established for every occurrence solely by quadriliteral structure.

Dictionaries often discuss damdam alongside triliteral D M M. The connection matters because several recorded meanings of «دَمَّ» (damma) can help explain the development or extension of damdama.

In early Arabic, «دَمَّ الشَّيْءَ» (damma al-shayʾa) meant coating or covering something. For example, «دَمَّ البَيْتَ» (damma al-bayta) concerned coating a house with clay or plaster, while «دَمَّ السَّفِينَةَ» (damma al-safīnata) concerned coating a boat with pitch.

«دَمَّ اليَرْبُوعُ جُحْرَهُ» (damma al-yarbūʿu juḥrahu) describes a jerboa sealing its burrow’s opening with earth and leveling it. «دَمَّ الأَرْضَ» (damma al-arḍa) is likewise recorded for leveling the ground.

The action is not simply putting one thing on another surface: it can close a gap, level a surface, or hide something beneath a covering.

This semantic field helps explain damdamah’s connection with enveloping and eliminating a previous form. The connection is nevertheless a credible lexical analysis, rather than conclusive proof that every use arose from one original bodily image.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: covering, coating, and leveling

2. Semantic branches of damdamah in Arabic

Enveloping and leveling

One clear use of damdama is covering something and leveling its surface.

«دَمْدَمْتُ عَلَيْهِ القَبْرَ» (damdamtu ʿalayhi al-qabra) concerns closing and leveling a grave over what lies within it. «دَمْدَمْتُ الشَّيْءَ» (damdamtu al-shayʾa) is also explained as pressing something against the ground and crushing it.

This is intelligibly related to damma al-arḍa and sealing an animal’s burrow. In these examples, covering or leveling can remove a previous shape or prominence.

Enveloping itself nevertheless does not invariably mean destroying. Covering a grave, coating a wall, and sealing a gap are different actions. Destruction arises where the action involves destructive force and the loss of what is subjected to it.

Crushing and destruction

Another use applies «دَمْدَمَ القَوْمَ» (damdama al-qawma) and «دَمْدَمَ عَلَيْهِمْ» (damdama ʿalayhim) to destroying a group of people.

Dictionaries explain it through expressions such as «طَحَنَهُمْ فَأَهْلَكَهُمْ» (ṭaḥanahum fa-ahlakahum): he crushed and destroyed them.

The verb here goes beyond simple covering. Its result is shattering and the ending of a previous condition.

Some sources likewise explain damdama al-shayʾa as destroying something completely. Their term istiʾṣāl concerns uprooting or thorough elimination.

Some lexical accounts of destruction, however, directly cite Al-Shams 91:14. They therefore cannot all serve as independent pre-Qur’anic witnesses.

Anger and threatening speech

Another branch of damdamah concerns anger.

Some sources use damdama ʿalayhi for becoming angry with someone or speaking to him angrily.

Al-damdamah is also recorded for speech that unsettles or frightens a listener, approaching the harsh or threatening quality of speech.

This branch may relate to intensity and force, but becoming angry is not identical with destroying. A person can become angry and speak without destroying anything.

This usage alone therefore should not restrict Qur’anic fa-damdama to “then he became angry.”

An overwhelming sound and shaking

Some lexical and exegetical explanations connect damdamah with a forceful sound, shaking, and severe disturbance.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī reports a view treating damdamah as an imitation of a cat’s sound and connecting it with someone damdama in his speech. That report should not become, without explanation, either “an overwhelming sound” or a proven origin for all uses. Other sources also discuss shaking and violent movement when interpreting the verse.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt and Tuḥfat al-Arīb: the sound account and shaking

Three matters nevertheless require distinction: the sound produced by an event, the force causing it, and the destruction resulting from it.

A verb may emphasize sound in one usage and destructive consequences in another. That possibility does not establish that damdamah originally named one particular natural sound, such as thunder or an earthquake.

3. Linguistic witnesses and distinctions among similar forms

Arabic poetry also records «تَدَمْدَمَ» (tadamdama) with a different meaning.

Nuṣayb ibn Rabāḥ, an Umayyad-era poet, says:

وَإِنَّ هَوَاهَا فِي فُؤَادِي لَقُرْحَةٌ
دَوًى مُنْذُ كَانَتْ قَدْ أَبَتْ مَا تَدَمْدَمُ

wa-inna hawāhā fī fuʾādī la-qurḥatun / dawan mundhu kānat qad abat mā tadamdamu

That is: “Love for her is an enduring wound in my heart, which from its beginning has refused to heal and close.”

«تَدَمْدَمَ الجُرْحُ» (tadamdama al-jurḥu) here concerns a wound closing or healing.

The witness matters in several respects.

First, the damdam lexical family is not restricted to destruction or anger. Closing a wound here is not destructive; it ends the wound’s open condition.

Second, a natural semantic connection can be seen with covering, sealing, and leveling: just as an opening or gap closes, a healing wound also closes.

Third, this is an Islamic-era poet, so the verse should not be presented as conclusive pre-Islamic evidence. It records a linguistic use and illuminates semantic range, rather than establishing priority over the Qur’anic occurrence.

Collection of Nuṣayb ibn Rabāḥ’s poetry: the wound-healing witness

Dictionaries also record names such as «الدِّمْدِم» (al-dimdim) for dried herbage and «الدُّمادِم» (al-dumādim) for a reddish substance flowing from certain trees. Shared consonants alone do not establish their semantic or historical connection with Qur’anic damdama.

Dictionaries likewise place «دَمِيم» (damīm), meaning unattractive in appearance and, in some accounts, short in stature, alongside «دَمّ» (damm), coating or covering. Damīm with dāl should not be equated without explanation with dhamīm, with dhāl, in moral blame. Proposed semantic links among these forms do not establish one original historical meaning for all of them.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: tadamdama, damdam, and damīm

For damdamah, the principal evidence informing the Qur’anic use concerns enveloping, crushing, destruction, anger, and intense sound.

4. Damdamah in the Qur’an

D M D M occurs only once in the Qur’an, as «فَدَمْدَمَ» (fa-damdama) in Al-Shams 91:14.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole quadriliteral occurrence

Al-Shams 91:14

فَكَذَّبُوهُ فَعَقَرُوهَا فَدَمْدَمَ عَلَيْهِمْ رَبُّهُم بِذَنبِهِمْ فَسَوَّاهَا

fa-kadhdhabūhu fa-ʿaqarūhā fa-damdama ʿalayhim rabbuhum bi-dhanbihim fa-sawwāhā

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They denied him and hamstrung the she-camel; then their Lord crushed them because of their sin and made them all alike.”

The meaning of fa-damdama ʿalayhim

Fa-damdama is a third-person masculine singular perfect verb here; its subject is «رَبُّهُمْ» (rabbuhum), their Lord.

The initial fa- connects the event with the people’s preceding conduct: denial and hamstringing the she-camel are stated first, followed by their consequence.

«عَلَيْهِمْ» (ʿalayhim) also matters. The verb occurs with ʿalā, describing an action that befalls the group.

«بِذَنْبِهِمْ» (bi-dhanbihim) explains its cause. The verse therefore does more than mention a destructive event: it connects that event with the people’s conduct and responsibility.

In this context, becoming angry does not sufficiently convey the full sense. The verse describes an actual destructive consequence following their conduct.

Treating damdamah only as a tremendous sound would likewise leave the result incompletely expressed. The context shows that the event culminated in the group’s crushing and destruction.

Equivalents such as “crushed,” “brought to destruction,” and “destroyed utterly” are therefore more appropriate for this occurrence.

Fa-sawwāhā and the result of damdamah

«فَسَوَّاهَا» (fa-sawwāhā) follows fa-damdama ʿalayhim.

It comes from another root and should not be incorporated directly into damdama’s lexical definition. It nevertheless matters for understanding the action’s result in the verse.

Several antecedents have been proposed for the feminine pronoun hā.

One possibility refers it to Thamūd, named as a community in Al-Shams 91:11. On this reading, destruction fell equally upon the group subjected to punishment.

Another refers it to the damdamah or punishment implied by the sentence: what befell them encompassed them all.

Some accounts understand fa-sawwāhā through the earth being leveled over them. This relates to enveloping and leveling, but the verse does not explicitly name the earth, so the image should remain a contextual interpretation rather than a certain identification of the pronoun.

In these readings, fa-sawwāhā emphasizes the event’s extent or complete result. Disagreement over the pronoun should nevertheless not restrict damdama itself to flattening the ground or burying people.

Al-Basīṭ: damdamah and three analyses of fa-sawwāhā

Damdamah alongside other verses concerning Thamūd

Other Qur’anic verses also describe Thamūd’s outcome.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:78 mentions al-rajfah; Hūd 11:67 mentions al-ṣayḥah; Al-Qamar 54:31 has ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan; and Fuṣṣilat 41:17 has ṣāʿiqatu al-ʿadhābi al-hūni.

These expressions describe Thamūd’s destruction through shaking, a cry, and severe punishment.

They need not all be synonyms. Rajfah foregrounds shaking, ṣayḥah sound, and damdamah here foregrounds crushing and destruction’s comprehensive result.

Ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan, “a single cry,” in Al-Qamar 54:31 is especially relevant: inferring from damdama’s repeated consonants that the punishment necessarily occurred in several stages or successive blows would require explaining its relation to that single cry.

Repeated consonants can foreground intensity but do not by themselves establish temporal repetition.

Al-Shams 91:12 also uses the singular ashqāhā, whereas Al-Shams 91:14 uses the plural fa-ʿaqarūhā. Al-Qamar 54:29 likewise describes one person acting directly. Direct performance of the act and the group’s collective responsibility therefore require distinction.

Hūd 11:66, moreover, describes the rescue of Ṣāliḥ and the believers with him. Comprehensive damdamah therefore does not mean destruction without exception of every person connected to Thamūd by descent or location; it concerns the group upon whom punishment fell.

This distinction also matters for fa-sawwāhā. The punishment’s extent should not negate the rescue explicitly reported elsewhere in the Qur’an.

Al-Ṭabarī: attribution of the act to the group and its outcome

5. Damdamah, destruction, and shaking

Damdamah overlaps semantically with several other Qur’anic words, each foregrounding a particular aspect of an event.

هَلَكَ (halaka): perishing or ceasing to exist. It can express destruction’s result without specifying how it occurred.

Study of H L K

دَمَّرَ (dammara): devastating or destroying severely. Qur’anic usage concerns peoples’ destruction and the loss of what they built or accumulated. Dammara and damdama belong to different roots; shared destructive meaning does not make them etymologically identical.

رَجَفَ (rajafa): shaking or trembling. Rajfah can describe the character of Thamūd’s outcome, while damdamah emphasizes its intensity and destructive result.

صَيْحَة (ṣayḥah): a loud sound or cry. A cry may occur in an event also described as damdamah, but their coexistence does not make their meanings identical.

سَوَّى (sawwā): leveling, making equal, or ordering something. It does not inherently mean destruction, since Qur’anic usage also concerns creation and proportioning. In this verse, alongside fa-damdama, its meaning concerns the destructive event’s result.

Study of S W Y

In Al-Shams, damdamah combines forceful action with a destructive result. The lexical background of enveloping and crushing helps explain this, without reducing the verse to just one material image.

6. Conclusion and translation proposal

Damdama is an Arabic quadriliteral verb with a repeated structure. Lexical witnesses record enveloping, leveling, crushing, severe destruction, anger, and sometimes a tremendous sound.

Some uses have intelligible semantic connections. Under certain conditions, covering and closing can lead to compression and the loss of a previous form. This does not prove one historical origin for every meaning.

Fa-damdama occurs only in Al-Shams 91:14, concerning the outcome of a group of Thamūd who denied their messenger and hamstrung the she-camel. It describes a forceful, destructive action befalling them because of their conduct.

Other Qur’anic descriptions of the event, including rajfah, ṣayḥah, and ṣāʿiqah, help explain its severity and character; none should replace damdamah’s independent meaning without evidence.

Brief root meaning: Enveloping and crushing; in destructive usage, severe and comprehensive destruction.

Meaning of Qur’anic fa-damdama: Then he crushed and destroyed utterly; concerning the punishment befalling the group subjected to it.

Translation proposal for Al-Shams 91:14: “Then their Lord crushed them because of their sin and made them all alike.”

“Crushed” conveys the verb’s force and destructive result better than “became angry” or “shook.” “Destroyed” expresses the result correctly but loses some of the word’s imagery.
