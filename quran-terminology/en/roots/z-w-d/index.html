1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The triliteral Arabic root Z W D concerns preparing and carrying something needed during a journey or in a coming situation. In tangible use, it can be food and other travel necessities; more broadly, something a person takes along for future use.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times in one verse of Al-Baqarah: Al-Baqarah 2:197.

Important forms in the linguistic family include:

  • زَاد (zād): provisions and what is prepared for travel or future need. Qur’anic use: yes, as al-zādi in Al-Baqarah 2:197.
  • تَزَوَّدَ / يَتَزَوَّدُ (tazawwada / yatazawwadu): to obtain provisions for oneself, take supplies, and carry them for a journey. Qur’anic use: yes, as the imperative tazawwadū in Al-Baqarah 2:197.
  • زَادَ / يَزُودُ (zāda / yazūdu): to prepare provisions. Qur’anic use: no, in this verbal form.
  • زَوَّدَ / يُزَوِّدُ (zawwada / yuzawwidu): to give someone provisions or equip them with what they need. Qur’anic use: no.
  • تَزْوِيد (tazwīd): supplying or providing provisions to another. Qur’anic use: no.
  • زَوْد (zawd): preparing provisions. Qur’anic use: no, in this verbal-noun form.
  • أَزَادَ / إِزْوَاد (azāda / izwād): recorded forms for provisioning or equipping someone. Qur’anic use: no.
  • أَزْوَاد (azwād): the plural of zād, provisions. Qur’anic use: no.
  • أَزْوِدَة (azwidah): an irregular plural of zād recorded in Arabic. Qur’anic use: no.
  • مِزْوَد (mizwad): a container or bag for storing and carrying provisions. Qur’anic use: no.
  • مَزَاوِد (mazāwid): the plural of mizwad, provision containers or bags. Qur’anic use: no.
  • مُتَزَوِّد (mutazawwid): someone obtaining provisions for themself. Qur’anic use: no, in this nominal form.

The provisions themselves, supplying another person, and taking supplies for oneself must be distinguished.

Zād names what is provided. Zawwada emphasizes equipping or provisioning another. Tazawwada denotes obtaining and taking provisions for oneself.

This matters in Al-Baqarah 2:197 because tazawwadū addresses people and calls upon them to take provisions for themselves.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two occurrences of Z W D in Al-Baqarah 2:197

2. The linguistic core: preparing something for a future need

Zād: what a person needs on the road

Older Arabic particularly used zād for food prepared for travel.

Imagine someone leaving home and familiar surroundings, uncertain whether water, food, or other necessities will be readily available at each stage. Before setting out, they prepare something to use along the way.

This is called zād.

Three features matter in this image: an approaching need, preparation before that need arises, and carrying the means of meeting it.

Zād nevertheless is not restricted to one particular food, such as bread or dates.

The food or item chosen as provision can vary with personal needs and travel conditions.

Lexical sources also record zād for food during residence in one place. Across Arabic, it therefore does not exclusively mean food necessarily carried on a camel or during an actual journey.

The travel image nevertheless remains especially prominent in understanding its primary meaning.

Mizwad: the physical image of provisions

Mizwad in Arabic names the container or bag holding provisions.

A traveler may store dates, dry bread, or durable food in a container and carry it along.

The container is not the provision itself; it stores and carries the provision.

This simple distinction clarifies the root.

Zād is what has been prepared for future need; mizwad is its carrying or storage vessel.

The root’s basic meaning is therefore not simply possessing wealth.

Someone may own wealth at home but prepare nothing for a journey. Another person with limited means may carry enough suitable food for the route and truly be mutazawwid.

Taking provisions therefore concerns readiness for a particular need, rather than the amount of wealth owned.

Is zād always material?

No.

Older lexicons also record a broader use: a person can carry something from their actions or acquisitions that will affect a future situation. Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab include both good and bad deeds or acquisitions in this figurative usage; the word itself therefore is not invariably positive.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab: provisions for travel and residence, and good or bad deeds

Someone acquiring knowledge, a skill, or experience, for example, may benefit from it later.

Knowledge or experience then resembles provisions carried from one stage of life to another.

This extension relates to the original image without making every kind of knowledge or action literally travel food.

Arabic can use provisions figuratively for what someone prepares for their future.

This capacity matters in Al-Baqarah 2:197, where the Qur’an calls mindfulness the best zād.

Zād and moving from one situation to another

In Al-Muḥkam, Ibn Sīdah records figurative zād for good or bad deeds or acquisitions carried along by a person. In this extension, usefulness alone is therefore not the criterion: the kind of deed or acquisition determines its value and consequences.

Ibn Sīdah, Al-Muḥkam: figurative zād

This analysis attends to readiness for the next stage, beyond travel food alone.

Tangible use and semantic extension must nevertheless remain distinct.

Taking food for a journey is a clear physical act. Calling mindfulness the best provision transfers the image of preparation into ethics and responsibility.

Zād therefore cannot be restricted entirely to the abstract idea of good deeds, just as its Qur’anic application to mindfulness cannot be explained solely through travel food.

The defensible core is preparing and carrying something upon which a person relies for an approaching need or stage.

3. Older Arabic evidence: tangible provisions and the provisions of conduct

Zād in Abū Khirāsh al-Hudhalī’s poetry

Abū Khirāsh al-Hudhalī’s verse states:

وَقَدْ يَأْتِيكَ بِالْأَخْبَارِ مَنْ لَا
تُجَهِّزُ بِالْحِذَاءِ وَلَا تُزِيدُ

wa-qad yaʾtīka bi-al-akhbāri man lā / tujahhizu bi-al-ḥidhāʾi wa-lā tuzīdu

English translation

Someone may bring you news whom you have neither equipped with shoes nor supplied with provisions.

Provisioning appears alongside supplying footwear.

The subject is therefore equipping someone for travel and movement.

Al-Aghānī explains the transmitted verb as equivalent to supplying provisions and relates it to zād. The form tuzīdu here should therefore not be equated with increasing, from the other root, merely because of outward similarity.

The poem’s date is another point.

Abū Khirāsh lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Al-Aghānī transmits this line in the account of his separation from his son Khirāsh and his complaint before ʿUmar, placing its reported circumstances in the Islamic period.

Al-Aghānī: Abū Khirāsh’s witness in the account of separation from his son

It is thus a witness to older Arabic, but should not be presented as securely pre-Islamic poetry.

Its value lies in clarifying tangible provisioning and equipping a traveler.

Zād in Jarīr’s poetry

A line by Jarīr, the Umayyad poet, reads:

تَزَوَّدْ مِثْلَ زَادِ أَبِيكَ فِينَا
فَنِعْمَ الزَّادُ زَادُ أَبِيكَ زَادَا

tazawwad mithla zādi abīka fīnā / fa-niʿma al-zādu zādu abīka zādā

English translation

Take for yourself provisions like your father’s among us; how good were the provisions your father had gathered for himself.

Zād appears repeatedly, but the subject goes beyond travel food.

The poet describes conduct and attainment that someone may take their father as a model for in relations with others.

Zād here approaches a person’s behavioral acquisition or resource: something gathered through life and deeds.

This shows a recognized older Arabic extension from material provisions to moral or social acquisitions.

Jarīr nevertheless lived after the Qur’anic revelation. His poetry helps explain historical usage without proving that this particular line predates the Qur’an.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab: Jarīr’s witness

Linguistic evidence and historical origin

The two witnesses show Z W D both in preparing actual travel necessities and in imagery concerning acquired conduct.

They do not establish that the Qur’anic khayru al-zādi al-taqwā was taken from a particular verse of poetry.

Nor does similarity between a poem and the verse establish precisely when the figurative usage arose.

For this study, what matters is the availability of both semantic possibilities in Arabic and their clear connection within Al-Baqarah 2:197 itself.

4. Al-Baqarah 2:197: taking provisions and the best provision

Complete verse text

الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ فَمَنْ فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ يَعْلَمْهُ اللَّهُ وَتَزَوَّدُوا فَإِنَّ خَيْرَ الزَّادِ التَّقْوَىٰ وَاتَّقُونِ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ

al-ḥajju ashhurun maʿlūmātun fa-man faraḍa fīhinna al-ḥajja fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa wa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajji wa-mā tafʿalū min khayrin yaʿlamhu Allāhu wa-tazawwadū fa-inna khayra al-zādi al-taqwā wa-ittaqūni yā ulī al-albābi

English translation

Pilgrimage is in known months. Whoever undertakes pilgrimage in them, let there be no rafath, no fusūq, and no jidāl during pilgrimage. Whatever good you do, The God knows it. Take provisions, for the best provision is taqwā. Be mindful of Me, people of understanding.

Wa-tazawwadū: the command to obtain provisions

Tazawwadū is a second-person plural imperative in Form V.

It denotes taking provisions and obtaining zād for oneself.

Here Form V fits a person acting to obtain something for their own benefit or need.

The pattern alone nevertheless does not prove that the person must produce the provisions, buy them with their own money, or obtain them without any help.

The verb expresses obtaining and taking provisions.

Significantly, wa-tazawwadū states no object or specified kind of provision.

The command does not insist on bread, dates, water, or a particular amount of money.

Arabic tazawwada can occur without an expressed object because it already conveys taking provisions.

“Take provisions” therefore follows the wording faithfully.

Whether the provisions here concern material resources alone or broader material and ethical readiness requires examining the next sentence.

Does the command concern only travel food?

Qur’anic pilgrimage involves actual travel and attendance at a specified place.

Al-Ḥajj 22:27 mentions people coming from distant routes. Al-Baqarah 2:197 locates pilgrimage within known months.

Material provisioning for such a journey is therefore a natural meaning.

A traveler may need food, water, transport, and equipment to make the journey possible and safe.

These examples should nevertheless not be presented as the verse’s literal list. Tazawwadū names none of them separately.

The continuation also identifies mindfulness as the best zād.

Restricting the entire command to food while ignoring that continuation therefore misses the verse’s full semantic capacity.

An appropriate reading retains actual taking of provisions while recognizing that the next sentence identifies the most valuable kind of human readiness.

Fa-inna khayra al-zādi al-taqwā

The expression follows the command to take provisions.

Khayra is the accusative noun governed by inna and the first term of a construct phrase; al-zādi is its genitive complement. Al-taqwā is the predicate of inna. Khayr al-zād is thus a construct phrase within this sentence.

Its natural meaning is: the best provision is mindfulness.

Mindfulness is placed in the position of provision.

This does not make it a material food that can be put in a bag.

Nor does it redefine the lexical meaning of zād as mindfulness alone.

The expression connects a familiar material concept with an ethical value.

People take provisions for physical journeys; they also need mindfulness to proceed rightly in life and fulfill responsibilities.

The verse itself supports this connection without requiring an invented etymological story.

Does mindfulness being best devalue material provisions?

No.

Khayr al-zād expresses superiority, without denying the existence or usefulness of every other provision.

Calling knowledge a person’s best possession, for example, does not necessarily make food, shelter, or tools useless.

In Al-Baqarah 2:197, too, mindfulness’s superiority should not negate a traveler’s actual needs.

The verse first commands taking provisions, then identifies the best provision.

This sequence permits retaining both levels together.

Conversely, preparing travel equipment alone should not turn khayr al-zādi al-taqwā into a marginal admonition unrelated to pilgrimage.

The verse places mindfulness at the center of its evaluation.

Material readiness and mindfulness therefore do not conflict, while mindfulness is identified as superior in standing and value.

5. Travel provisions, personal responsibility, and taqwā

Preparing for travel and depending on others

Older exegetical reports describe some people setting out for pilgrimage without provisions and expecting others to meet their needs.

Some accounts connect this conduct with a claim of reliance upon The God.

These reports can provide historical background for travel and the verse’s reception, but tazawwadū itself names no particular group.

A historical report’s details should therefore not become a fixed component of the verse’s meaning.

Al-Qurṭubī on Al-Baqarah 2:197: provisions and reports about reliance

The command also does not make every needy traveler or everyone seeking help during a journey guilty of objectionable conduct.

People differ in resources, health, and travel circumstances.

Someone may begin prepared but need help after illness, an accident, or losing their resources.

Helping such a person is compatible with mindfulness and social responsibility.

If someone has adequate means but deliberately places all their travel needs upon others without agreement, the issue instead concerns those others’ rights and personal responsibility.

This distinction comes from justice and respecting rights, rather than Z W D itself meaning absolute independence from people.

Reliance upon The God and taking provisions

Qur’anic tawakkul concerns relying upon The God.

Such reliance does not necessarily mean abandoning life’s actual means.

Preparing water and food for a journey, for example, does not in itself negate reliance upon The God.

Possessing abundant resources likewise does not automatically demonstrate tawakkul.

Al-Baqarah 2:197 places taking provisions alongside taqwā.

There is therefore no basis for inventing a conflict between travel preparation and mindfulness of The God.

Tazawwadū itself nevertheless does not mean tawakkul. Their relationship requires separate examination in the verses on trusting The God and human responsibility.

How is taqwā a provision?

Direct meaning and figurative connection must be distinguished.

Material provisions meet the needs of travel.

Taqwā is likewise identified as something valuable to carry through one’s responsibilities.

In Al-Baqarah 2:197, mindfulness is not separate from conduct.

Before the command to take provisions, the verse addresses rafath, fusūq, and jidāl during pilgrimage.

It then says that whatever good people do, The God knows it.

The verse thus directly connects avoiding inappropriate conduct, doing good, and taqwā.

Mindfulness here is therefore more than an inward feeling detached from action.

A pilgrim cannot assume that money, food, and equipment alone fulfill pilgrimage’s entire purpose while disregarding others’ rights or harming them through conduct.

Equally, the verse does not call for abandoning bodily needs and actual travel resources in the name of taqwā.

The provision image connects two aspects: real material needs and care over one’s conduct and decisions.

Connecting zād, khayr, and taqwā

Before wa-tazawwadū, the verse states:

“Whatever good you do, The God knows it.”

This places every good deed within divine knowledge.

Taking provisions and the superiority of taqwā then follow.

Mindfulness here is thus connected with doing good and avoiding objectionable conduct.

An unsupported generalization must nevertheless be avoided: a good act should not automatically become zād in the lexical translation of another verse merely because it brings future benefit.

Zād provides an image for taqwā here. The connection is valid without replacing other Qur’anic words.

Wa-ittaqūni yā ulī al-albāb

The verse ends with another command:

“Be mindful of Me, people of understanding.”

This command belongs to W Q Y, not Z W D.

Contextually, however, it directly connects with khayr al-zādi al-taqwā.

Mindfulness is first identified as the best provision; the addressees are then commanded to practice it before The God.

After naming the best provision, the Qur’an therefore returns to that subject through a direct command.

Ulī al-albāb also identifies addressees expected to use their understanding and reason.

This ending shows that taking provisions in pilgrimage is more than a mechanical action unrelated to understanding responsibility.

Z W D itself nevertheless does not mean reason, mindfulness, or obedience.

Those concepts come from the verse’s other words and construction.

6. Morphology and distinctions from nearby roots

Z W D compared with Z Y D (zāda / yazīdu)

Study of Z Y D

This is among the study’s most important root distinctions.

Some forms of Z W D and Z Y D closely resemble one another in spelling and pronunciation.

In Z W D, zād names provisions, and the recorded verb zāda / yazūdu concerns preparing them.

In Z Y D, zāda / yazīdu instead denotes increasing or adding.

Qur’anic references to increased faith or knowledge, for example, use forms from Z Y D.

These families should not be merged into one entry.

Al-zādi in Al-Baqarah 2:197 belongs to Z W D, while ziyādah and verbs of increasing belong to Z Y D.

Adding food or wealth may increase one’s provisions, but increasing quantity differs from preparing provisions.

Someone may increase their wealth without preparing for travel.

Another may prepare suitable provisions from existing wealth without increasing that wealth.

Thus taking provisions is not identical to increase.

Z W D compared with J H Z (jahhaza)

Study of J H Z

Arabic tajhīz concerns preparing resources and the equipment needed.

Tazwīd can also equip someone for travel, but concerns the specific field of provisions.

Abū Khirāsh’s poem places providing footwear alongside provisions.

The pairing shows that travel equipment and zād itself can be distinct.

Every travel item therefore does not fall within the narrower lexical sense of zād, although needed equipment can form part of broader travel preparation.

Z W D compared with M T ʿ (matāʿ)

Study of M T ʿ

Matāʿ in Arabic and the Qur’an concerns benefit and what a person uses.

Zād is also prepared for use, but particularly concerns the needs of a journey or an approaching stage.

Something may be matāʿ without being travel provisions.

A house or furnishings used by someone, for example, are not necessarily supplies carried for a journey.

Matāʿ and zād overlap in benefit and use without being identical.

Z W D compared with N F Q (nafaqa)

Study of N F Q

N F Q includes spending, expenditure, and, in some constructions, giving to others.

Tazawwud denotes obtaining provisions.

A person may spend money to obtain supplies, but spending itself differs from taking provisions.

Qur’anic infāq can also help those in need or fulfill social responsibility, beyond personal travel.

Tazawwadū alone therefore cannot establish a tax ruling or a specified amount of giving.

Z W D compared with Q D M (qaddama)

Study of Q D M

Some Qur’anic uses of qaddama concern sending ahead or preparing in advance.

This relates to the image of provisions for the future.

Zād is what someone prepares for use on the road or in a coming situation; qaddama can emphasize sending or doing something ahead of a later time.

Similarity in readiness therefore does not justify identifying the roots with one another.

Z W D compared with W Q Y (waqā)

Study of W Q Y

Waqā concerns protection, caution, and guarding oneself from harm and undesirable consequences.

Taqwā belongs to this family.

Al-Baqarah 2:197 identifies taqwā as the best zād.

Their proximity does not make zād and taqwā synonyms.

Provisions are prepared for the road; taqwā here is the best thing that can serve as a person’s provision.

The relationship is identifying a valuable instance through semantic imagery, rather than etymological identity.

Z W D compared with Z B D (zabad)

Study of Z B D

Qur’anic Z B D denotes foam or scum appearing on water or molten materials.

It differs from Z W D.

Letter similarity should not place zabad and zād in one family.

Z W R, concerning visiting or, in some forms, crookedness and falsehood, is also independent.

7. Translation proposals and synthesis

Translation proposals for Forqan

For wa-tazawwadū in Al-Baqarah 2:197, the principal direct, natural rendering is:

“And take provisions.”

“Obtain provisions for yourselves” also clarifies the verb, but is longer.

The main translation should not restrict the unspecified provision to food, bread, dates, travel money, or righteous deeds.

These may be discussed in reflection and contextual explanation; the verb itself names no particular kind of provision.

The principal proposal for fa-inna khayra al-zādi al-taqwā is:

“For the best provision is taqwā.”

The Persian explanation can bring taqwā close to mindfulness of The God and observing His boundaries. The main translation, however, is better served by retaining the Qur’anic term and explaining it in a separate reflection.

For the complete sentence, the proposal is:

“And take provisions, for the best provision is taqwā; be mindful of Me, people of understanding.”

This preserves the connection between taking provisions, taqwā’s superiority, and the closing command for mindfulness of The God.

Final synthesis

Arabic Z W D concerns preparing provisions, carrying them, and readiness for travel or a coming situation.

In tangible use, zād may be food and necessities prepared for the road. More broadly, it can be an acquisition or something carried for one’s future.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times in Al-Baqarah 2:197.

Tazawwadū is an imperative calling upon the addressees to take provisions.

Al-zādi names what is prepared for that readiness.

After the command, the Qur’an identifies taqwā as the best provision.

This construction yields two clear conclusions.

First, material preparation for actual travel is neither worthless nor irrelevant to human responsibility. Taking supplies can reasonably meet approaching needs.

Second, travel resources alone do not make someone’s pilgrimage conduct complete. The Qur’an calls taqwā the best provision, locating it amid restraint from rafath, fusūq, and jidāl, doing good, and mindfulness of The God.

Material supplies should therefore not replace taqwā, nor should taqwā justify abandoning resources and actual travel preparation.

Tazawwadū likewise does not require every needy person to live without others’ help, or make seeking assistance during travel a sign of weak faith. The root itself establishes no such judgments.

Final definition: Z W D denotes preparing and carrying something upon which a person relies for an approaching need or stage. Tangibly, it is travel provision. In broader figurative usage, it can be an acquisition from good or bad deeds carried along by a person; the kind of deed determines its benefit or harm.

Al-Baqarah 2:197 connects this image with taqwā and identifies it as the best provision.

The root’s principal meaning is therefore provisions and taking provisions; context determines their value, purpose, and kind of readiness.

Provisions prepare a person for the road; in this verse, taqwā is the best thing to carry while traveling it.
