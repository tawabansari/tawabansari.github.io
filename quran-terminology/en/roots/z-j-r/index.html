Central meaning: using a rebuke, command, or deterrent force to stop someone or something from continuing an action, or to set it in motion.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

In early Arabic, zajr occurs in two familiar settings: driving animals into motion and restraining people from particular conduct.

Both involve decisive influence on behavior, but not always in the same direction. A person may be stopped from acting, whereas an animal may be commanded to move.

An early lexical account explains the core meaning through intihār, sharp rebuke or commanding. Its examples are urging a camel forward and deterring a person from an action.

Zajr therefore includes both restraint and setting something in motion. The common feature is a rebuke or intervention intended to alter behavior or direction.

A poetic example attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A verse transmitted under the name of the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

وَلَقَدْ رَحَلْتُ الْعِيسَ ثُمَّ زَجَرْتُهَا
وَهْنًا وَقُلْتُ عَلَيْكِ خَيْرَ مَعَدِّ

wa-laqad raḥaltu al-ʿīsa thumma zajartuhā / wahnan wa-qultu ʿalayki khayra maʿaddi

The principal part means:

“I prepared the camels for travel, then urged them onward with a call in the night.”

Al-ʿīs refers to camels, and wahnan places the scene at night. Zajartuhā here does not stop their movement; it urges them onward.

The verse appears in transmitted poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and early discussions of it, with variants in some words. Its attribution should be understood within that literary transmission, not as an independently dated inscription.

Taken together, early uses connect zajr with a restraining or impelling command that brings about a response or change of conduct.

The camel example supports the impelling sense; the wider definition also draws on other Arabic uses, rather than being wholly established by this one verse.

Birds and divination

In early Arab life, zajr al-ṭayr also named interpreting birds’ movements as omens concerning the outcome of undertakings.

A practitioner might set a bird flying or observe its flight, then regard its direction as favorable or unfavorable to proceeding.

Zājir could consequently designate someone who divined from birds.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

لَعَمْرُكَ مَا تَدْرِي الضَّوَارِبُ بِالْحَصَى
وَلَا زَاجِرَاتُ الطَّيْرِ مَا اللَّهُ صَانِعُ

la-ʿamruka mā tadrī al-ḍawāribu bi-al-ḥaṣā / wa-lā zājirātu al-ṭayri mā Allāhu ṣāniʿu

Translation:

“By your life, neither those who divine with pebbles nor those who read birds’ omens know what The God will do.”

Labīd lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This verse’s date is not established, so it does not independently establish a pre-Qur’anic date for the expression, although it illustrates early Arabic zājirāt al-ṭayr.

Here zajr has a specialized divinatory application. Divination is one cultural use, not the root’s core meaning.

This matters because al-zājirāt also occurs in the Qur’an; shared wording alone does not identify its referents as diviners.

2. Forms and derivatives

The root occurs six times in five Qur’anic verses, in five derivative forms. Its principal Arabic forms include the following.

Zajara yazjuru: rebuked, restrained, drove, or urged onward. Context determines direction: zajara al-baʿīra means driving a camel, while zajarahu ʿani al-sūʾi means deterring someone from wrongdoing.

Zajr: the verbal noun, denoting rebuke, driving, or restraint.

Zājir: the active participle, one who drives, restrains, or rebukes. Zājirāt is its feminine plural.

Zajrah: a noun of a single act, a restraining or impelling cry. Qur’anic usage presents a single decisive cry.

Inzajara: was restrained, desisted, or responded to a rebuke.

Izdajara: relates to restraining or being restrained. The Qur’anic passive uzdujira concerns being harshly rebuked or driven back.

Muzdajar: a deterrent or cause for taking warning. Its morphology permits discussion as a passive participle, a verbal noun with prefixed mīm, or a place expression; deterrence and taking heed are salient in the verse.

Zajūr: describes a she-camel whose response during nursing or milking involves driving away or a rebuke. Lexical accounts differ over the exact behavior.

Mazjar and mazjarah: expressions connected with restraint, its location, or a cause of deterrence. Their contextual meaning must be distinguished from a technical morphological classification as an instrument noun.

The Qur’anic forms are al-zājirāt, zajran, zajrah, muzdajar, and uzdujira.

In form VIII, the pattern’s tāʾ changes to dāl after zāy: the morphological base iztajara is represented as izdajara. This is a morphological account, not an independently demonstrated historical sequence or a new moral meaning.

3. Qur’anic occurrences

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:2: rebuke and restraint

فَالزَّاجِرَاتِ زَجْرًا

fa-al-zājirāti zajran

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:2

Translation:

“Then by those who rebuke forcefully.”

Al-zājirāt is a feminine plural active participle; zajran is the same root’s verbal noun, emphasizing the action.

The construction emphasizes zajr without naming its object. The verse alone does not explicitly identify who acts or what they drive or restrain.

Early explanations include angels driving clouds, angels restraining disobedience, or Qur’anic warnings deterring wrongdoing.

Each interpretation can connect with zajr, but the root alone does not decide among them.

The verse follows wa-al-ṣāffāti ṣaffan in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:1 and precedes fa-al-tāliyāti dhikran in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:3. These may describe one group through several activities or distinct groups; identifying them requires further evidence.

A root study should retain rebuking and restraint without making the agents’ identity part of the lexical definition.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:19: a decisive cry

فَإِنَّمَا هِيَ زَجْرَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ فَإِذَا هُمْ يَنْظُرُونَ

fa-innamā hiya zajratun wāḥidatun fa-idhā hum yanẓurūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:19

Translation:

“It is only a single cry; then suddenly they are looking.”

Zajrah is singular and modified by wāḥidah, one.

The preceding verses question resurrection after death. The answer presents their raising as following a single command or cry.

Zajrah can mean a single decisive cry, just as a commanding call may set an animal in motion.

The animal image must not be transferred bodily into this verse: it concerns human resurrection, not driving camels.

Nor does zajrah name an instrument such as a trumpet. Any connection with the blowing of the Trumpet requires other passages.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:13: the single cry again

فَإِنَّمَا هِيَ زَجْرَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ

fa-innamā hiya zajratun wāḥidah

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:13

Translation:

“It is only a single cry.”

This verse likewise uses zajratun wāḥidatun.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:14 describes the result: people suddenly stand upon an exposed expanse.

In both occurrences, the cry accompanies a decisive change in their condition.

This relates to a command that elicits response, but the root alone cannot establish the event’s physical mechanism.

Yā Sīn 36:53 similarly uses ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan when describing the gathering of people. It reinforces the connection between a single cry and resurrection without making ṣayḥah and zajrah lexically identical.

Al-Qamar 54:4: warning and deterrence

وَلَقَدْ جَاءَهُمْ مِنَ الْأَنْبَاءِ مَا فِيهِ مُزْدَجَرٌ

wa-laqad jāʾahum mina al-anbāʾi mā fīhi muzdajar

Al-Qamar 54:4

Translation:

“Reports have indeed reached them in which there is cause for restraint and taking heed.”

Muzdajar concerns reports capable of deterring continued wrongdoing.

Unlike driving an animal by sound, deterrence here works through knowledge, warning, and recognition of consequences.

Accounts of earlier peoples can reveal the results of choices and prompt reconsideration of behavior.

Zajr is therefore not limited to loud sounds or bodily confrontation: speech and reports can also deter.

Muzdajar here does not mean punishment itself. Punishment may be the subject of a warning report, but the word emphasizes deterrence.

Al-Qamar 54:9: Noah and threatening restraint

كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ فَكَذَّبُوا عَبْدَنَا وَقَالُوا مَجْنُونٌ وَازْدُجِرَ

kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥin fa-kadhdhabū ʿabdanā wa-qālū majnūnun wa-uzdujir

Al-Qamar 54:9

Translation:

“Before them Noah’s people denied; they denied Our servant and said, ‘Mad!’ And he was harshly rebuked and driven back.”

Uzdujira is a passive past-tense form VIII verb.

Noah faces denial, accusations of madness, and attempts to restrain him.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:116 provides a clear parallel: his people threaten him with stoning unless he desists.

Wa-uzdujira thus conveys harsh deterrence, threats, and pressure in this context.

Some early explanations connect it instead with the people’s claim of madness; the reading that Noah was rebuked and threatened fits the lexical usage and the parallel in Al-Shuʿarāʾ.

On this reading, zajr is performed by the prophet’s opponents. The root therefore carries no fixed positive moral value.

4. Proper warning and improper suppression

The root’s Qur’anic settings differ in moral character.

In Al-Qamar 54:4, warning reports can deter wrongdoing and serve correction.

In Al-Qamar 54:9, Noah’s people try to obstruct his message through insult and pressure.

Both involve restraint, but one serves warning and understanding, the other denial and opposition.

Zajr does not inherently mean enforcing truth, moral reform, or just punishment. Its value depends on who uses deterrent power, for what purpose, and against what conduct.

This distinction across the passages prevents translating every occurrence with the same moral judgment.

5. Related roots

Z J R (zajr) and R D ʿ (radʿ)

Both concern restraint.

Radʿ emphasizes prevention and dissuasion. Familiar uses of zajr often involve rebuke, command, or decisive intervention.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary. Zajr can operate without an audible cry, as with a warning contained in a report.

Z J R (zajr) and N H Y (nahy)

Nahy means forbidding an action and may take the form of speech, instruction, or a ruling.

Zajr often emphasizes the force and decisiveness of restraint.

A person may forbid something without shouting or acting harshly.

Yet zajr, especially with animals, can urge movement; it cannot always be reduced to prohibition.

Z J R (zajr) and Z J W (zajw)

Z J W also concerns driving and advancing.

Some uses emphasize gradual movement or progress, whereas zajr often concerns the commanding, rebuking, or deterrent force.

The distinction requires examining Z J W across its own Qur’anic uses. Similarity does not establish one root as an altered version of the other.

Z J R (zajr) and Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣayḥah)

Ṣayḥah denotes a cry or shout.

Zajrah may be a cry, but its root also includes rebuke and influence on conduct.

They approach one another in resurrection passages; a shared referent does not make their lexical meanings identical.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents suit the Qur’anic forms. They do not replace installed translations or authored word-box explanations.

Al-zājirāt: those who rebuke, restrain, or drive, depending on context.

Zajran: rebuking, driving, or restraining.

Zajratun wāḥidatun: one cry, a single rebuke, or one decisive command.

Muzdajar: cause for restraint, warning, or taking heed.

Uzdujira: was harshly restrained, driven back, rebuked, or threatened.

For Al-Ṣāffāt 37:2, “then by those who rebuke forcefully” retains the action without imposing an identification of its agents.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:19 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:13, “a single cry” is natural; its relation to commanding rebuke can be explained separately.

In Al-Qamar 54:4, “cause for restraint and taking heed” fits the context. The general root summary in a word box should remain brief and separate.

In Al-Qamar 54:9, “was harshly rebuked” conveys the direct sense; the threats of Noah’s people can be discussed in reflection.

7. Conclusion

Early Arabic uses zajr for rebuking, driving, and restraining. The verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates that it may set an animal in motion rather than prevent movement.

The Qur’an employs this field in several forms: rebuke, a single cry, deterrent warning, and being threatened or restrained.

The differences matter: Al-Qamar 54:4 concerns warning and correction, whereas Al-Qamar 54:9 depicts people trying to stop Noah’s call.

The core lies in decisive intervention to drive, restrain, or alter conduct, not one restricted application such as shouting, punishment, divination, or moral prohibition.

In each verse, construction and context determine direction, agent, recipient, and result.
