The Qur’anic Root Q W B

Measure and distance; the separately examined branch of hollowing, splitting, and detaching

Qur’anic forms

قَابَ

Qāba

Accusative noun in a construct phrase; Al-Najm 53:9

1. Fundamental meaning and linguistic background

Arabic dictionaries group usages concerning digging and hollowing under Q W B and also record qāb as a measure or extent of distance. In a specialized use concerning a bow, qāb is the distance from its grip to one of its ends. This lexicographical grouping does not, by itself, establish a shared historical origin for all the meanings.

Classical Arabic uses qābu qawsin for a distance measured by a bow. Sufficient independent, datable evidence for this particular expression before the Qur’an is not available here. Its Qur’anic use and lexical explanations of bow parts nevertheless indicate an intelligible expression in the language of revelation.

The digging and hollowing branch includes creating a depression in the ground, uprooting something, and splitting a shell. Its historical relationship to qāb meaning a measure is examined separately below.

Qur’anic form: قَابَ (qāba), an accusative noun meaning a measure or extent of distance, occurring once, in Al-Najm 53:9. It is not a verb in this verse.

In the digging and splitting branch, qawb denotes hollowing; taqwīb, making a depression or uprooting; and inqiyāb, splitting or becoming hollow. Dictionaries also place qāʾibah, a bird’s egg, and qūb, a chick, under this entry. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an. Their grouping is not conclusive evidence that Qur’anic qāb historically developed from that branch.

2. Physical imagery and early Arabic evidence

Hollowing and leaving a mark

The Arabic expression قَابَ الْأَرْضَ (qāba l-arḍa) means making a rounded pit or depression in the ground.

Derivatives of Q W B also describe marks left on the ground by the movement or extended settlement of people and livestock.

This line is transmitted from rajaz verse attributed to al-ʿAjjāj, an Umayyad-period poet:

مِنْ عَرَصَاتِ الْحَيِّ أَمْسَتْ قُوبًا

“From the tribe’s dwelling grounds, now hollowed and marked.”

Lexicographers explain qūban here as muqawwabah: ground marked and hollowed through settlement and traffic. This Islamic-period witness concerns effects on the ground; it does not establish pre-Qur’anic antiquity for the meaning “measure.”

Qawwaba is also recorded for the effects of disease on a camel’s skin and loss of hair. Qūbāʾ names a skin ailment in early Arabic; the lexical designation alone does not establish one precise medical diagnosis for every historical occurrence.

Splitting a shell and separating

Classical Arabic uses قَابَ الطَّائِرُ بَيْضَتَهُ (qāba l-ṭāʾiru bayḍatahu) for a bird splitting its egg’s shell.

It also says:

«اِنْقَابَتِ الْبَيْضَةُ» (inqābati l-bayḍah)

That is, the egg’s shell split.

One recorded Arabic proverb says:

«تَخَلَّصَتْ قَائِبَةٌ مِنْ قُوبٍ» (takhallaṣat qāʾibatun min qūbin)

“An egg has parted from its chick.”

The proverb describes two people separating and their bond ending. Its image comes from a chick parting from the shell: afterward, the chick does not return to its former situation inside the egg.

The proverb’s origin is undated, although Arabic lexicographical sources record its use.

Qāb: measure and distance

Arabic also uses qāb and qīb to mean a measure or amount.

بَيْنَهُمَا قَابُ قَوْسٍ (baynahumā qābu qawsin) means that the distance between two things measures one bow.

In specialized bow terminology, qāb is the distance from the grip to either tip. In that sense, each bow has two qābs.

Ibn Fāris foregrounds digging and uprooting under qawb. In a separate qāb entry, however, he relates the meaning “measure” to qīd, proposing a substitution of bāʾ for dāl and a change of yāʾ to alif. This is a lexicographer’s derivational proposal, not independent historical proof of that development.

Even traditional Arabic etymology therefore did not unanimously derive the measure sense from hollowing.

It is not established that measure and distance developed from digging ground, a rounded depression, or a bow’s curvature.

3. Qur’anic usage of qāb

The word occurs in Al-Najm, in the account of receiving revelation and what Muhammad saw.

The preceding verse says:

ثُمَّ دَنا فَتَدَلّىٰ

Thumma danā fa-tadallā.

“Then he drew near and descended.”

An-Najm 53:8

It continues:

فَكانَ قابَ قَوسَينِ أَو أَدنىٰ

Fa-kāna qāba qawsayni aw adnā.

“So he was at a distance of two bows’ measure, or nearer.”

An-Najm 53:9

Three words from separate root entries contribute to this description of nearness:

The root index places qāb under Q W B, meaning a measure or distance; qawsayn, from Q W S, means two bows; and adnā, from D N W, means nearer. The indexing of qāb should not be confused with proof of its historical origin.

Qāb expresses the measure, qawsayn supplies its standard, and adnā means nearer. Aw, “or,” need not imply doubt on the speaker’s part. One grammatical explanation presents the measure from an observer’s perspective: two bows’ measure or nearer.

Morphology and syntax

Qāba is an accusative noun and the first term of a construct phrase, not a past-tense verb. One analysis takes it as an adverbial of place or distance attached to an omitted predicate of kāna. Another treats qāba qawsayn itself as kāna’s predicate, with the measure or distance of approach understood in its subject. Calling qāb a direct predicate is therefore not the only possible analysis. In both, the final fatḥah marks a noun’s accusative case, distinct from the verb qāba, “hollowed” or “split.”

Qawsayni is the dual of qaws and the genitive second term of the construct. Its genitive marker is yāʾ, not the final nūn’s kasrah. Qāb is the first term and qawsayn the second.

In the visible construction, qāb is singular and qawsayn dual.

Aw adnā compares the nearness with the stated measure: the distance was that amount or less.

Two bows or two sections of one bow?

Lexical explanations offer several interpretations of the measure intended.

On qāb’s general meaning, qāba qawsayn means the measure of two bows. Arabic describes distances with familiar objects such as bows and spears, as well as spans and cubits.

On the specialized meaning of the distance from a bow’s grip to its tip, some lexicographers understand the expression as the two sections of one bow. They treat it as an inversion of qābay qawsin.

Some early explanations describe the measure as one or two cubits.

Given the general meaning and the verse’s direct construction, “two bows’ measure” is preferable: it preserves the words’ number and grammatical arrangement.

The Qur’an does not specify the bow’s type, exact length, or measurement procedure. Converting the expression into a fixed number of meters or another modern unit is therefore unsupported.

The expression principally conveys very close proximity, rather than establishing a standardized unit.

4. Two bows’ measure in the context of revelation

After describing this nearness, the Qur’an says:

فَأَوحىٰ إِلىٰ عَبدِهِ ما أَوحىٰ

Fa-awḥā ilā ʿabdihi mā awḥā.

“Then he revealed to His servant what he revealed.”

An-Najm 53:10

Then:

ما كَذَبَ الفُؤادُ ما رَأىٰ

Mā kadhaba l-fuʾādu mā raʾā.

“The heart did not deny what it saw.”

An-Najm 53:11

Approach, receiving revelation, and seeing are presented together in these verses.

Qāba qawsayn provides an intelligible image of close proximity. An audience familiar with bows and their approximate size could understand the short distance conveyed.

Qāb itself, however, does not identify who approached, resolve the pronouns’ referents, or determine the nature of this proximity.

Those questions require examination of Al-Najm’s opening passage and other revelation verses. Gabriel is not expressly named here, although Al-Baqarah 2:97 attributes the Qur’an’s descent upon Muhammad’s heart, by divine permission, to Gabriel.

If the approach concerns the presence of the messenger of revelation, qāb still expresses a measure of distance. A reading proposing a different kind of proximity requires independent Qur’anic evidence.

Qāb’s lexical meaning does not establish a body, spatial location, or particular physical distance for The God.

Qāba qawsayn in mystical terminology

In some later mystical writings, qāba qawsayn became an expression for a degree of spiritual proximity, spiritual journeying, and nearness to The God.

Some writers also interpreted the two bows symbolically and philosophically.

These usages belong to conceptual systems developed later. The lexical meaning of qāb does not name a mystical station or a particular level of being.

The dual qawsayn alone also does not establish two levels of existence, two worlds, or two paths of descent and ascent. Such interpretations need arguments beyond the word’s lexical meaning.

5. Comparison with related roots

Q W B and Q D R: distance and measure

Qadr has a broad range covering measure, amount, capacity, value, and determination of limits.

Qāb in its Qur’anic usage denotes the measure or extent of distance.

Lexicographers accordingly explain qāb through qadr. Not every use of qadr can, however, be assigned to qāb.

Qadr can express an amount of water, provision, or capacity; qāb in the Qur’anic expression concerns distance.

Q W B and Q W S: measure and its standard

Qaws names a bow; qāb expresses a measure or distance.

In qāba qawsayn, a bow supplies the standard of comparison.

Just as “two handspans’ measure” uses a handspan, the Qur’anic expression uses a bow.

Qāb therefore does not mean “bow,” and qawsayn by itself does not name a distance.

Q W B and D N W: distance and approach

D N W denotes being near and drawing near.

In Al-Najm 53:8, danā denotes approach. In Al-Najm 53:9, qāb specifies the distance, while adnā means nearer. This should not be interpreted as the speaker’s ignorance of the distance.

Dunūw expresses nearness as a relationship; qāb here specifies its measure.

Q W B and Q R B: a distance measure and nearness

Qurb occurs in the Qur’an for spatial, temporal, and nonmaterial nearness.

Qāb in the present sense concerns measuring distance.

The proximity conveyed by qāba qawsayn follows from the short distance and context. Qāb itself does not necessarily mean spiritual closeness, love, or rank before The God.

6. Translation choices for Forqan

For qāba in Al-Najm 53:9, “at a distance of … measure” is proposed.

The proposed verse translation is:

“So he was at a distance of two bows’ measure, or nearer.”

“So the distance was two bows’ measure or less” is another readable, defensible rendering.

“He drew very near” conveys the general idea but removes both the bow image and the measure.

“At a distance of two cubits” has support in some early explanations, but is not a direct translation of qāb and qawsayn. It is better presented as an explanation of approximate distance.

7. Conclusion

Arabic records usages of Q W B for digging, hollowing, uprooting, and splitting a shell. Qāb is also recorded for a measure or distance and, more specifically, the span between a bow’s grip and tip.

The historical derivational relationship between these branches is not conclusively established.

The Qur’an uses qāb to express the measure of proximity in a revelation context. A bow supplies the measure and adnā means nearer. How “or” functions in the comparison must be understood from syntax and context, without attributing doubt to the speaker.

Definition for Forqan: qāb, a measure or extent of distance; specifically, the distance from a bow’s grip to its tip. Dictionaries also record digging, hollowing, and splitting a shell under Q W B, but the historical link between that branch and the measure sense is uncertain.
