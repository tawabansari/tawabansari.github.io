Core Qur’anic meaning: sidr names a tree familiar in Arab lands, whose fruit is called nabq and which Persian also calls konār. Sidrah denotes one such tree. The Qur’an mentions it in three settings: a small quantity in the land of Sabaʾ, trees among the delights of Paradise, and Sidrat al-Muntahā in a scene of witnessing great divine signs. Arabic S D R also has branches concerning bewilderment, disturbed vision and letting hair hang loose; these should not be imposed on the tree’s name in Qur’anic verses.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

Sidr is the Arabic name of the tree whose fruit is called nabq.

Sidrah denotes one such tree.

The tree grows in warm, dry environments as well as places with suitable water and moisture. Some kinds have sharp thorns; others have fewer thorns or larger, more palatable fruit.

Arabic lexicons distinguish two principal traditional kinds: ʿubrī and ḍāll.

ʿUbrī sidr is associated with water and favorable growing conditions, and its fruit is known as food.

Ḍāll more often denotes the wild desert kind, generally with conspicuous thorns and less palatable fruit.

This reflects traditional Arabic distinctions and should not automatically be equated with modern botanical classifications.

A pre-Islamic witness: Al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas

The pre-Islamic poet Al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas says in a poem beginning Abligh Ḍubayʿata anna al-bilāda:

دَعَا شَجَرَ الْأَرْضِ دَاعِيهِمُ
لِيَنْصُرَهُ السِّدْرُ وَالْأَثْأَبُ

daʿā shajara al-arḍi dāʿīhimu / li-yanṣurahu al-sidru wa-al-athʾabu

Translation:

“Their caller summoned the trees of the earth, that sidr and athʾab might come to his aid.”

Al-sidr and al-athʾab name two kinds of trees.

Athʾab is also a tree known in the Arabian environment.

The poet is not describing trees literally taking part in war: their names form a poetic image referring to groups of people.

Arabic literary discussion treats this as figurative reference through the names of plants and trees.

Lexically, the important point is that sidr is a familiar tree name in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period: the poet could place it beside another tree without botanical explanation.

The witness supports the presence of this name in Arabic before the Qur’an.

It does not establish that sidr originally meant a warrior, helper or tribe.

Those ideas arise from the poem’s imagery and context, not the tree name’s lexical meaning.

The verse is recorded in the poem attributed to Al-Musayyab and in early literary transmission. His place among pre-Islamic poets is known, although the text, like other oral poetry of that period, reaches us through later written transmission.

Familiar characteristics of the sidr tree

Lexical and botanical descriptions mention relatively broad leaves, spreading branches, edible fruit and thorns.

Its fruit is called nabq.

In some Persian-speaking and southern regions, the fruit is known as konār.

Sidr is botanically related to jujube, but they should not always be treated as the same plant with identical characteristics.

Arabic sources also record using sidr leaves for washing.

The leaves can be crushed or ground and mixed with water for cleaning.

This use explains why Persian sometimes calls the powdered leaves sidr as well.

That applied meaning should not replace the tree’s primary name.

In the Qur’an, sidr names the tree, not a washing powder.

A historical definition

The poem attributed to Al-Musayyab and recorded Arabic uses support the following definition of this branch:

Sidr names a tree familiar in Arabia, with fruit called nabq. It may bear thorns, and its leaves, fruit and shade are used. Sidrah denotes one such tree.

This definition identifies the plant.

The tree name alone does not convey the end of a journey, the highest rank, a refuge or an afterlife blessing.

In Qur’anic verses, such concepts emerge from accompanying words and context.

2. Other branches in Arabic

Alongside the tree name, Arabic sources record other uses under S D R.

These should be recognized without inventing a single underlying meaning unsupported by evidence.

Sadira, yasdaru: bewilderment and confusion

Sadira denotes becoming bewildered or confused.

Sadira baṣaruhu means that someone’s vision became disturbed and they could not see properly.

Some uses connect the condition with dizziness or confusion caused by intense heat.

Al-sadar is also recorded for bewilderment or disturbed vision and perception.

The subject here is human or animal perception and conduct, rather than a tree.

Sādir: bewildered or heedless

Sādir is the active participle in this branch.

It can describe someone bewildered, acting without proper judgment or paying insufficient attention to consequences.

The pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says:

سَادِرًا أَحْسَبُ غَيِّي رَشَدًا
فَتَنَاهَيْتُ وَقَدْ صَابَتْ بِقُرْ

sādiran aḥsabu ghayyī rashadan / fa-tanāhaytu wa-qad ṣābat bi-qurr

Translation:

“Heedless, I thought my error was right guidance; then I desisted, when adversity had settled upon us.” The final phrase is an idiom for hardship descending or a situation becoming settled, rather than simply reaching a neutral conclusion.

Sādiran describes a person lacking adequate awareness and judgment about the rightness of their course.

Ghayyī denotes his error.

Rashadan conveys right guidance.

Thus heedlessness comes from sādiran, while error and guidance are expressed by separate words in the verse.

The verse is recorded in Ṭarafah’s collected poetry and early lexical sources.

It documents sādir as bewildered or heedless in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

It does not show that sidr, the tree, originally named a bewildered or misguided person.

Sadara al-shaʿra: letting hair hang loose

Lexicons record sadara al-shaʿra as releasing hair and letting it hang.

Shaʿrun masdūrun describes loose, hanging hair.

Insadara al-shaʿru likewise concerns hair falling loose.

This usage is related to S D L, hanging or letting something down.

Some lexicographers explain the form with rāʾ as a variant of the form with lām.

That analysis concerns hair and should not be transferred without evidence to the tree or bewilderment branches.

Sadīr

Sadīr occurs as the name of certain places and waterways.

Early sources mention it as the name of a river or district in Al-Ḥīrah.

A Persian origin is also reported for the name.

Its resemblance to S D R does not by itself prove derivation from the tree name or an Arabic verb sadara.

The lexical branches considered together

This family has several semantic fields in Arabic:

The sidr tree; bewilderment and disturbed vision; letting hair hang loose; and certain names and other limited uses.

Some branches may be historically or phonetically related, but the evidence does not justify reducing them all to one image.

The Qur’an uses only the tree name and its unit form.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Sidr

The collective name of the sidr tree.

It can refer to a group of such trees.

This form appears in Sabaʾ 34:16 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28.

Both verses concern trees, but their circumstances and descriptions differ.

Sidrah

The name of one sidr tree.

The final tāʾ marks a unit of the collective noun.

Sidr is collective; sidrah is one tree.

The unit form appears in Al-Najm 53:14 and Al-Najm 53:16.

Sidrāt and sidar

These are recorded plurals of sidrah.

Lexicons also record other plural forms.

Sidrāt is a suffixed plural, whereas sidar is a broken plural. Neither occurs in the Qur’an; its sidr is the collective form and should be distinguished from sidar.

Nabq

The fruit of the sidr tree.

This word does not derive from S D R.

Botanically and in everyday use, sidr and nabq concern the same plant but name different things.

Sidr names the tree.

Nabq names its fruit.

Sādir

The active participle from sadira in the bewilderment branch.

It describes someone bewildered, confused or heedless.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Sadar

In the confusion branch, this is a verbal noun or name of a condition.

It denotes bewilderment, disturbed vision or a state resembling dizziness.

Its vocalization and use differ from sidr, the tree.

Masdūr

In the hair-related use, it describes something released and left hanging.

This form also does not occur in the Qur’an.

Forms actually used in the Qur’an

S D R occurs four times in two forms:

  • Sidrin in Sabaʾ 34:16.
  • Sidrin in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28.
  • Sidrati in Al-Najm 53:14.
  • Al-sidrata in Al-Najm 53:16.

All four concern the sidr tree.

No form concerning bewilderment, disturbed vision or loose hair occurs in the Qur’an.

4. A little sidr in the land of Sabaʾ

فَأَعْرَضُوا فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ وَبَدَّلْنَاهُمْ بِجَنَّتَيْهِمْ جَنَّتَيْنِ ذَوَاتَيْ أُكُلٍ خَمْطٍ وَأَثْلٍ وَشَيْءٍ مِنْ سِدْرٍ قَلِيلٍ

fa-aʿraḍū fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim sayla al-ʿarimi wa-baddalnāhum bi-jannatayhim jannatayni dhawātay ukulin khamṭin wa-athlin wa-shayʾin min sidrin qalīl

Sabaʾ 34:16

Translation:

“They turned away, so We sent upon them the flood of Al-ʿArim and replaced their two gardens with two gardens bearing bitter produce, athl trees and a little sidr.”

Sabaʾ 34:16

Sidrin is a collective noun in the genitive after min.

Qalīlin describes its small quantity.

The Qur’an therefore speaks of a small amount of sidr trees here.

The transformation of Sabaʾ’s gardens

Sabaʾ 34:15 mentions two gardens to the right and left of the people’s dwelling place.

It also mentions the Lord’s provision, gratitude and a good land.

After describing their turning away, the next verse recounts the flood and the gardens’ changed condition.

Two other gardens replace the previous pair.

The new gardens’ characteristics differ from the former ones.

They contain khamṭ, athl and a little sidr.

In plant and fruit usage, khamṭ concerns bitterness and unpleasantness.

Athl is a tree name.

Sidr is the familiar tree whose fruit is nabq.

The three words should not be conflated.

Why does qalīl matter?

Qalīl qualifies the small amount of sidr.

Its presence does not imply abundance.

A tree may offer useful fruit, leaves or shade while only a few specimens remain in a garden.

Usefulness and scarcity do not conflict.

The verse specifically mentions a little sidr.

Its mention does not establish that the new gardens matched the previous ones in produce or benefits.

Is sidr useless here?

No. Sidr does not mean a useless tree.

Arabic knows it for its fruit, leaves and shade.

Wild desert kinds may nevertheless have less palatable fruit.

Sabaʾ 34:16 alone does not identify the precise botanical kind present there.

Qalīl describes quantity, not flavor or fruit quality.

It therefore cannot be translated as “bitter sidr” or “fruitless sidr.”

The root’s meaning and the story’s message

The gardens change in a context of Sabaʾ’s people turning away.

The story thus concerns gratitude, social conduct and consequences.

Sidr itself does not mean ingratitude or punishment.

The tree may appear in favorable or unfavorable circumstances.

What matters here is the gardens’ transformation and the small quantity of sidr afterward.

In Sabaʾ 34:16, sidr names an actual tree and qalīl specifies its small quantity. The assessment of the gardens comes from the whole story, not the tree name.

5. Fī sidrin makhḍūd: sidr among Paradise’s blessings

فِي سِدْرٍ مَخْضُودٍ

fī sidrin makhḍūd

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28

Translation:

“Among sidr trees stripped of thorns.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28

Sidrin is the same familiar tree name.

Makhḍūdin qualifies it.

That adjective comes from KH Ḍ D, not S D R.

The meaning of makhḍūd

Arabic khaḍada al-shajara can mean cutting off or removing a tree’s thorns.

Makhḍūd can accordingly describe a tree whose thorns have been removed.

With branches, khaḍd can also concern bending or breaking without completely severing.

Two explanations of sidr makhḍūd are consequently discussed.

The first understands thornless or de-thorned sidr.

The second emphasizes branches bent by abundant fruit.

Both connect with recorded KH Ḍ D usages, but removing thorns has more direct support in khaḍada al-shawka.

“Sidr stripped of thorns” is therefore an appropriate rendering.

Why does removing thorns matter?

Natural sidr trees can have thorns.

Thorns can make branches and fruit difficult to reach.

Removing them makes the tree easier to enjoy.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 places sidr among the blessings of the people of the right.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29 mentions ṭalḥin manḍūd.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:30 speaks of extended shade.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 mentions flowing water, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:32 abundant fruit.

These connections place sidr within an image of comfort and enjoyment.

The following verses’ features should not all be added to sidr’s lexical meaning.

Shade is conveyed by ẓillin mamdūd.

Abundant fruit comes from fākihatin kathīrah.

The absence of thorns comes from makhḍūd.

Sidr itself remains the tree name.

Sidr in Sabaʾ and Al-Wāqiʿah

Sabaʾ 34:16 describes a small quantity of sidr.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 places it among the people of the right’s blessings, qualified by makhḍūd.

The tree name is shared; the descriptions and contexts differ.

The first concerns a small number of trees in transformed gardens.

The second emphasizes their quality in a scene of heavenly enjoyment.

Sidr’s presence in Sabaʾ’s changed gardens does not make the tree undesirable in every use.

Nor does its presence in Paradise mean every earthly sidr is perfect and thornless.

Is the heavenly tree biologically identical to the earthly one?

The Qur’an uses the familiar name sidr.

For its first audience, the name supplied an intelligible image of a tree.

It cannot alone determine every biological feature, size, structure or growth pattern of trees in the hereafter.

Makhḍūd adds a description within the setting of blessings in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28.

We can retain the literal meaning and tree image without equating every aspect of afterlife plants with familiar earthly trees.

Sidr names the tree; removal of thorns comes from makhḍūd, and its place among heavenly blessings comes from context.

6. At Sidrat al-Muntahā: the tree in a scene of divine signs

عِنْدَ سِدْرَةِ الْمُنْتَهَىٰ

ʿinda sidrati al-muntahā

Al-Najm 53:14

Translation:

“Near the sidr tree of the utmost boundary.”

Al-Najm 53:14

Sidrati is the unit form of the tree name.

Al-muntahā belongs to N H Y and here denotes an endpoint or boundary reached.

Sidrat al-Muntahā thus joins the tree name to the idea of an endpoint.

The two components must nevertheless be distinguished.

Sidrah names the tree.

Al-muntahā is the second term of the genitive construction, linking the tree with a limit or endpoint; it is not grammatically an adjective agreeing with sidrah.

The endpoint meaning does not come from S D R itself.

In what context is the tree introduced?

Al-Najm 53:13 says:

وَلَقَدْ رَآهُ نَزْلَةً أُخْرَىٰ

wa-la-qad raʾāhu nazlatan ukhrā

Al-Najm 53:13

Translation:

“Indeed, he saw him at another descent.”

Al-Najm 53:14 then locates that sighting with “near Sidrat al-Muntahā.”

Al-Najm 53:15 says:

عِنْدَهَا جَنَّةُ الْمَأْوَىٰ

ʿindahā jannatu al-maʾwā

Al-Najm 53:15

Translation:

“Near it is the Garden of Refuge.”

Together these verses describe a sighting connected with Sidrat al-Muntahā and the Garden of Refuge.

The three verses do not themselves specify every detail of what was seen or the location’s structure and spatial character.

The pronoun -hu in raʾāhu in Al-Najm 53:13 is masculine.

It should not therefore be referred without explanation to the feminine sidrah.

Its referent must be examined through the preceding verses and discourse.

The meaning of al-muntahā

Muntahā denotes an endpoint or limit.

The question is what ends at that point in this construction.

Earlier explanations propose the end of an ascending route, a limit reached by certain beings or a boundary of knowledge.

These proposals may help trace the history of interpretation.

Sidrat al-Muntahā itself does not explicitly specify which route, level of awareness or domain ends there.

One explanation should not become the word’s lexical meaning without evidence.

The construction directly identifies a sidr tree associated with the designation al-Muntahā.

A real tree or simply a figure?

Linguistically, the Qur’an uses the tree name sidrah.

Without a contextual basis, it should not be emptied of that meaning and turned into an entirely abstract concept such as knowledge, consciousness or the end of human thought.

At the same time, Sidrat al-Muntahā appears in a setting different from earthly trees.

Al-Najm 53:15 connects it with the Garden of Refuge.

Al-Najm 53:16 mentions something enveloping it.

The name alone does not establish complete material and biological identity with ordinary earthly sidr trees.

The literal tree meaning remains, while its mode of existence and characteristics are not fully disclosed by the name.

This distinction warrants neither deleting what the verse names nor inventing unseen details without evidence.

Relation to the Garden of Refuge

Al-Najm 53:15 uses ʿindahā.

The feminine pronoun -hā refers to the sidrah.

The verse then names Jannat al-Maʾwā.

It establishes proximity between the tree and the Garden of Refuge.

ʿIndahā does not make the tree and garden identical.

Sidrah is a tree name.

The Garden of Refuge is another designation.

Their proximity does not merge their meanings.

Nor does ʿindahā specify coordinates, distance or physical shape.

7. What enveloped the sidrah

إِذْ يَغْشَى السِّدْرَةَ مَا يَغْشَىٰ

idh yaghshā al-sidrata mā yaghshā

Al-Najm 53:16

Translation:

“When there enveloped the sidrah whatever enveloped it.”

Al-Najm 53:16

Al-sidrata is definite and the object of the first yaghshā.

Yaghshā belongs to GH SH Y and concerns covering or enveloping.

Mā yaghshā denotes what envelops the tree without naming its nature in this sentence.

Is the tree itself covering something?

Grammatically, al-sidrata is the object.

The tree is what is enveloped.

The subject of the first yaghshā is mā, following its object. In the second yaghshā, the subject is an implicit pronoun referring back to mā.

The sentence should not be translated as though the tree were the subject covering other things.

This matters for preserving the verse’s direct meaning.

What enveloped the tree?

The verse does not explicitly identify it.

Interpretive explanations mention light, angels and various manifestations.

The verse itself does not name those things here.

A direct translation should preserve the text’s unspecified wording.

If such a reading is discussed in interpretation, its independent Qur’anic support should be made clear.

Mā yaghshā alone does not specify the color, material, number or shape of what enveloped the tree.

Seeing great divine signs

Al-Najm 53:17 says:

مَا زَاغَ الْبَصَرُ وَمَا طَغَىٰ

mā zāgha al-baṣaru wa-mā ṭaghā

Al-Najm 53:17

Translation:

“The sight did not swerve, nor did it exceed the limit.”

Al-Najm 53:18 says:

لَقَدْ رَأَىٰ مِنْ آيَاتِ رَبِّهِ الْكُبْرَىٰ

la-qad raʾā min āyāti rabbihi al-kubrā

Al-Najm 53:18

Translation:

“Indeed, he saw some of his Lord’s greatest signs.”

These verses place the scene in a setting of seeing great divine signs.

All these concepts should not be folded into sidrah’s meaning.

Sidrah names the tree.

Muntahā conveys a limit or endpoint.

Yaghshā conveys enveloping.

Verse 18 reports seeing great signs of the Lord.

Each component should retain its meaning.

8. Earthly sidr and Sidrat al-Muntahā

The Qur’an uses one lexical family in three settings.

In Sabaʾ 34:16, sidr forms part of earthly vegetation.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28, it appears among blessings for the people of the right.

In Al-Najm 53:14 and Al-Najm 53:16, it appears in a scene of witnessing great divine signs.

Different contexts do not eliminate the tree name’s meaning.

They do prevent transferring features between those settings without evidence.

Sabaʾ describes a small quantity of sidr.

Al-Wāqiʿah describes sidr stripped of thorns.

Al-Najm identifies the tree by al-Muntahā.

None of these descriptions is essential to the meaning of every sidr tree.

Knowing sidr as an earthly plant does not establish that every biological feature of Sidrat al-Muntahā is identical to terrestrial trees.

Conversely, its unseen setting does not justify converting the tree name into an entirely different philosophical concept.

Knowledge of the earthly plant clarifies the verse’s linguistic image.

The unseen reality is known to the extent the Qur’an describes it.

9. Distinctions from related roots

S D R and N B Q

Sidr names the tree.

Nabq names its fruit.

They relate to the same plant but do not mean the same thing.

Sabaʾ 34:16 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 use the tree name.

Sidr should therefore not be reduced to “konār fruit” in direct translation.

S D R and SH J R

Shajar is a general term for trees and, in related uses, woody vegetation.

Sidr names a particular kind of tree.

Every sidr is a tree, but not every tree is sidr.

The Qur’an mentions different trees.

This diversity calls for understanding each plant name in its own use.

S D R and Ṭ L Ḥ

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 mentions sidr.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29 mentions ṭalḥ.

The tree names occur in consecutive verses describing heavenly blessings.

Their proximity does not make them synonymous.

Sidr is the familiar konār tree.

Ṭalḥ is another plant name whose precise referent is debated in interpretation.

The characteristics of one should not be transferred to the other without evidence.

S D R and S K R

In its perceptual branch, sadar concerns bewilderment and disturbed vision.

Arabic sukr concerns intoxication, obscured perception and related states.

Some symptoms may resemble each other, but the roots differ.

None of the Qur’anic S D R occurrences concerns bewilderment or intoxication.

S D R and S D L

Arabic sadl concerns hanging or letting something down.

In hair-related usage, sadr and sadl sometimes alternate.

Lexicons recognize this closeness.

It does not show that the tree name originated in hanging hair.

Such a historical connection would require independent evidence.

S D R and Ṣ D R

These roots look similar but have different first letters.

S D R begins with sīn.

Ṣ D R begins with ṣād.

Qur’anic ṣadr concerns the breast; verbal forms also concern coming forth or departing.

Qur’anic sidr, by contrast, names a tree.

Visual resemblance does not establish kinship or transfer meanings between roots.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

The basic translation is straightforward: sidr names the tree and sidrah one specimen.

In Sidrat al-Muntahā, the tree’s meaning should remain distinct from al-Muntahā.

Makhḍūd also belongs to another root and should not be made part of sidr’s basic meaning.

Important meanings in the family

Sidr: the sidr or konār tree.

Sidrah: one sidr tree.

Sidrāt: sidr trees.

Nabq: its fruit; a word from a separate root.

Sadira: in another branch, became bewildered or confused.

Sādir: bewildered, confused or heedless.

Sadar: bewilderment and disturbed vision.

Sadara al-shaʿra: let the hair hang loose.

Masdūr: released and hanging loose, in hair-related usage.

Proposed renderings of Qur’anic forms

In Sabaʾ 34:16:

وَشَيْءٍ مِنْ سِدْرٍ قَلِيلٍ

wa-shayʾin min sidrin qalīl

Sabaʾ 34:16

“And a little sidr.”

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28:

فِي سِدْرٍ مَخْضُودٍ

fī sidrin makhḍūd

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28

“Among sidr trees stripped of thorns.”

In Al-Najm 53:14:

عِنْدَ سِدْرَةِ الْمُنْتَهَىٰ

ʿinda sidrati al-muntahā

Al-Najm 53:14

“Near the sidr tree of the utmost boundary.”

In Al-Najm 53:16:

إِذْ يَغْشَى السِّدْرَةَ مَا يَغْشَىٰ

idh yaghshā al-sidrata mā yaghshā

Al-Najm 53:16

“When there enveloped the sidrah whatever enveloped it.”

In the two Al-Najm verses, Sidrat al-Muntahā may be retained as the tree’s familiar designation.

Muntahā does not name a plant species; it is a designation joined to the tree in the Qur’anic construction.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meaning

For Sabaʾ 34:16:

“Sidr trees present in small quantity in Sabaʾ’s transformed gardens.”

For Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28:

“Sidr among the blessings of the people of the right, described as stripped of thorns.”

For Al-Najm 53:14:

“The sidr tree designated al-Muntahā in the scene of witnessing divine signs.”

For Al-Najm 53:16:

“The same Sidrat al-Muntahā that the verse describes as being enveloped.”

Preserve each form’s installed Arabic spelling in word boxes. These proposals do not replace existing authored explanations.

The brief root meaning should also remain distinct from each verse-specific meaning.

11. Conclusion

Arabic S D R has several semantic branches.

In the plant branch, sidr names the tree whose fruit is nabq.

The tree was known in Arabia and mentioned in pre-Islamic poetry.

Al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas places sidr beside athʾab in an image referring to groups of people.

This supports the familiarity of the name in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Lexicons record other branches as well.

In the perceptual branch, sadar concerns bewilderment and disturbed vision.

Ṭarafah uses sādir for someone mistaking his own error for right guidance.

Letting hair hang loose and other usages are recorded under the same letters.

These cannot all be derived from one meaning without sufficient historical evidence.

The Qur’an uses S D R four times, solely for the sidr tree.

Sabaʾ 34:16 describes a little sidr in the people’s transformed gardens.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 qualifies sidr with makhḍūd among blessings for the people of the right.

Al-Najm 53:14 and Al-Najm 53:16 place Sidrat al-Muntahā in a scene of seeing great divine signs.

The tree name remains in all three settings.

Their descriptions and messages differ, however.

In Sabaʾ, scarcity comes from qalīl.

In Al-Wāqiʿah, the absence of thorns comes from makhḍūd.

In Al-Najm, al-Muntahā belongs to N H Y, and enveloping the tree is conveyed by yaghshā.

None of these features should become part of sidr’s lexical definition.

Knowledge of the earthly tree alone does not determine every characteristic of afterlife trees or Sidrat al-Muntahā.

The Qur’an uses the tree name without describing all its existential and physical details in the unseen realm.

Qur’anic S D R names the sidr or konār tree, whose fruit is nabq; sidrah denotes one tree.

Other branches, including bewilderment and disturbed vision, are established Arabic uses but absent from these four Qur’anic occurrences.

For Forqan, “sidr” and “sidr tree” are appropriate renderings; Sidrat al-Muntahā should preserve both the tree name and the endpoint designation.

The essential distinction is that sidr names a tree. Scarcity, removal of thorns, association with al-Muntahā and being enveloped are features or events expressed through their own words and contexts.
