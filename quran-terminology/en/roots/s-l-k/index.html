Entering a path, following a course, and inserting something into a place or passage

S L K in Arabic concerns entering a path or passage, moving along it, and inserting something into something else. Sometimes a person or creature travels a course; sometimes an agent places someone or something in a route, location, or condition.

The Quran displays this distinction. The bee follows its Lord's paths; people travel the earth's roads; Moses inserts his hand into his garment's opening; water is channeled through the earth as springs; and people are brought to a place or outcome in relation to their deeds.

Salaka cannot always be restricted to “walking” or “inserting.” Its shared field is entering or placing something in a particular path, location, or channel, and traveling or passing along it. The construction and context determine whether the agent travels or brings something into it.

1. Early Arabic usage and evidence attributed to the pre-Islamic period

Traveling a path and entering a course

Early Arabic attests salaka al-ṭarīqa for entering and traveling a road. The expression emphasizes the traveler's relation to a route: a path exists, and someone follows or takes it.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

بَانَ الْخَلِيطُ وَلَمْ يَأْوُوا لِمَنْ تَرَكُوا
وَزَوَّدُوكَ اشْتِيَاقًا أَيَّةً سَلَكُوا

bāna al-khalīṭu wa-lam yaʾwū li-man tarakū / wa-zawwadūka ishtiyāqan ayyatan salakū

“The companions departed, showing no compassion for those they left behind; whichever way they went, they left you provisioned with longing.”

Salakū here concerns the route or direction the departing people took. It describes movement along a course, rather than obedience to a religious command or entry into a moral or spiritual stage.

Another line in the same poem says:

تَعَلَّمَنْ هَا لَعَمْرُ اللَّهِ ذَا قَسَمًا
فَاقْدِرْ بِذَرْعِكَ وَانْظُرْ أَيْنَ تَنْسَلِكُ

taʿallaman hā la-ʿamru Allāhi dhā qasaman / fa-iqdir bi-dharʿika wa-unẓur ayna tansaliku

“Know this—by the life of The God, this is an oath: measure your capacity and consider what course or undertaking you enter.”

Tansaliku derives from insalaka and here concerns entering an undertaking or situation. The poet warns the addressee to recognize his capacity before becoming involved.

Traveling a path could thus also express entering an undertaking or condition in early Arabic.

Both lines are recorded in the poetry transmitted under Zuhayr's name and in early literary works. Their attribution has an established literary basis, although their precise dates cannot be independently fixed apart from the history of transmission.

They support the early presence of traveling a route and entering a course of action. They do not establish that every later religious, mystical, or technical use already existed then.

Inserting one thing into another

Another branch appears in salaka al-shayʾa fī al-shayʾi: inserting something into something else.

A clear lexical example is salaka al-khayṭa fī al-ibrati: passing a thread through a needle's eye.

Something passes through an opening or passage and is placed along a particular course.

Salaka yadahu fī jaybihi means inserting one's hand into the breast opening of one's garment. A directly comparable use appears in the Quran.

Arabic lexicons record these examples, which support the verb's semantic range. Their inclusion in later sources alone does not prove that every phrase in exactly that form circulated in the pre-Islamic period.

Silk and maslak

Silk denotes a thread or strand used for sewing or stringing things together. Aslāk is one of its plurals.

The strand can provide the line along which other components are arranged or connected.

Maslak denotes a route, passage, or place of transit. Its connection with salaka is clear: it is where movement or passage takes place.

Figuratively, maslak can denote a course or approach adopted in life or thought.

Silk, “thread,” sulūk, “traveling a course,” and maslak, “passage,” nevertheless differ. Membership in one lexical family does not establish the precise historical derivation of one from another.

In later periods, especially mystical literature, sulūk acquired a technical sense of spiritual training and traversing spiritual stages. That belongs to the history of a term, rather than the necessary meaning of the Quranic verb.

2. Quranic forms and verbal construction

S L K occurs twelve times in the Quran, always as a form of the basic triliteral verb salaka.

The Quranic forms are:

  • Naslukuhu — Al-Ḥijr 15:12
  • Fa-uslukī — Al-Naḥl 16:69
  • Wa-salaka — Ṭā Hā 20:53
  • Fa-usluk — Al-Muʾminūn 23:27
  • Salaknāhu — Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:200
  • Usluk — Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32
  • Fa-salakahu — Al-Zumar 39:21
  • Fa-uslukūhu — Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32
  • Li-taslukū — Nūḥ 71:20
  • Yaslukhu — Al-Jinn 72:17
  • Yasluku — Al-Jinn 72:27
  • Salakakum — Al-Muddaththir 74:42

Despite differences in tense, person, attached particles, and pronouns, these forms belong to the same basic verb.

Salaka, within its basic triliteral form, can mean either following a path or bringing someone or something into a route, place, or condition.

A causative sense does not always require Form IV or Form II. In salaknāhu the agent brings something in, whereas in fa-uslukī subula rabbiki the addressee herself follows the paths.

Aslaka, sallaka, insalaka, sulūk, silk, and maslak are also known in Arabic, but do not occur in these forms in the Quran.

The range of lexical derivatives must therefore be distinguished from the forms actually attested in the Quran.

3. Traveling along an available path

The bee and its Lord's paths

Al-Naḥl 16:69 says:

ثُمَّ كُلِي مِنْ كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ فَاسْلُكِي سُبُلَ رَبِّكِ ذُلُلًا يَخْرُجُ مِنْ بُطُونِهَا شَرَابٌ مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهُ فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ لِلنَّاسِ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

thumma kulī min kulli al-thamarāti fa-uslukī subula rabbiki dhululan yakhruju min buṭūnihā sharābun mukhtalifun alwānuhu fīhi shifāʾun li-al-nāsi inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yatafakkarūn

Al-Naḥl 16:69

“Then eat from all the fruits and follow your Lord's paths, made easy. From their bellies comes a drink of varying colors, in which there is healing for people. In that is surely a sign for people who reflect.”

Fa-uslukī is an imperative addressed to the bee.

Subula rabbiki supplies its object: the paths the bee travels.

Here salaka concerns the bee's own movement along the paths, rather than its inserting something else.

Dhululan relates to being tractable or easy. Grammatically, it has been related either to the paths or to the bee's state while traveling. Both readings retain the basic sense of movement along paths available to the bee within creation.

The verse connects the bee's behavior, food, and the product emerging from its body. Fa-uslukī itself does not mean producing honey, building a hive, or consciously following a religious law. Those matters require the wider passage and relevant evidence.

The earth and routes of passage

Ṭā Hā 20:53 says:

الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ الْأَرْضَ مَهْدًا وَسَلَكَ لَكُمْ فِيهَا سُبُلًا وَأَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ أَزْوَاجًا مِنْ نَبَاتٍ شَتَّىٰ

alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-arḍa mahdan wa-salaka lakum fīhā subulan wa-anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhrajnā bihi azwājan min nabātin shattā

Ṭā Hā 20:53

“The One who made the earth a resting place for you, provided paths in it for you, and sent water down from the sky; with it We brought forth diverse kinds of plants.”

The agent of salaka here is The God, and its object is subulan, “paths.”

Unlike the bee passage, this does not describe the agent traveling those paths. He has placed or provided them in the earth for people.

The construction thus conveys providing or laying out routes of passage.

Nūḥ 71:20 says:

لِتَسْلُكُوا مِنْهَا سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا

li-taslukū minhā subulan fijājā

Nūḥ 71:20

“So that you may travel its broad paths.”

Here people themselves are the agents who travel the paths.

The two occurrences express different sides of the relationship: Ṭā Hā 20:53 describes paths provided for people; Nūḥ 71:20 describes people traveling them.

Subul belongs to S B L and fijāj to F J J, not S L K. Subul names the paths, fijāj emphasizes broad passages, and taslukū expresses traveling them.

The verb's meaning must therefore remain distinct from that of the noun serving as its object.

4. Bringing someone or something into a place or passage

Noah and bringing creatures aboard the ship

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:27, The God commands Noah:

فَاسْلُكْ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلٍّ زَوْجَيْنِ اثْنَيْنِ وَأَهْلَكَ

fa-usluk fīhā min kullin zawjayni ithnayni wa-ahlaka

Al-Muʾminūn 23:27

“Then bring into it two of each kind, a pair, and your family.”

Fīhā, “into it,” refers to the ship.

Fa-usluk here means bringing creatures aboard. Noah is commanded to place others in a particular location; the action is not his own travel along a route.

The verb alone does not determine how they entered, their arrangement aboard, the ship's construction, or how they were maintained.

Moses and inserting his hand into his garment

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32 says:

اسْلُكْ يَدَكَ فِي جَيْبِكَ تَخْرُجْ بَيْضَاءَ مِنْ غَيْرِ سُوءٍ

usluk yadaka fī jaybika takhruj bayḍāʾa min ghayri sūʾ

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32

“Insert your hand into your garment's opening; it will come out white, without harm.”

Usluk is an imperative, with yadaka, “your hand,” as its object.

Fī jaybika identifies where the hand is inserted. Jayb in early Arabic concerns the garment's opening at the neck and breast; it should not automatically be equated with a modern small pocket.

Usluk expresses inserting the hand through that opening. Its emerging white is the result stated afterward, not part of S L K's lexical meaning.

This clearly shows that Quranic sulūk is not confined to spiritual progress or a course through life.

Water and the earth's springs

Al-Zumar 39:21 says:

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَسَلَكَهُ يَنَابِيعَ فِي الْأَرْضِ ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُ بِهِ زَرْعًا مُخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهُ ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ حُطَامًا إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَذِكْرَىٰ لِأُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ

a-lam tara anna Allāha anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-salakahu yanābīʿa fī al-arḍi thumma yukhriju bihi zarʿan mukhtalifan alwānuhu thumma yahīju fa-tarāhu muṣfarran thumma yajʿaluhu ḥuṭāman inna fī dhālika la-dhikrā li-ulī al-albāb

Al-Zumar 39:21

“Have you not seen that The God sent water down from the sky and channeled it as springs in the earth? Then with it He brings forth crops of varying colors; then they wither and you see them yellowing; then He makes them broken debris. In that is surely a reminder for people of understanding.”

The pronoun -hu in fa-salakahu refers to the water.

Water is channeled and flows in the earth as springs. The verb conveys bringing something in and channeling it along natural courses.

Yanābīʿa specifies the water's form of manifestation in the construction. Its precise grammatical analysis can be discussed, but the main image is clear: water descending from the sky appears in the earth as springs.

The passage may be considered alongside present knowledge of infiltration, groundwater, and springs. Salaka itself is not a scientific term for one particular hydrological mechanism and does not specify every watercourse's physical details.

Bringing a person into a chain

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32 says:

ثُمَّ فِي سِلْسِلَةٍ ذَرْعُهَا سَبْعُونَ ذِرَاعًا فَاسْلُكُوهُ

thumma fī silsilatin dharʿuhā sabʿūna dhirāʿan fa-uslukūhu

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32

“Then put him into a chain whose length is seventy cubits.”

Fa-uslukūhu is a command whose object is the person undergoing punishment.

The verb concerns putting or passing the person into the chain. The precise configuration of this punishment cannot be established from the lexical meaning alone.

Silsilah belongs to S L S L. Its proximity to uslukūhu does not make them derivatives of one root.

Salaka is therefore not lexically confined to desirable movement or entry; the same verb can occur in a context of punishment.

5. Entry into hearts: the message and its reception

Two closely similar constructions use this root in relation to human hearts.

Al-Ḥijr 15:12 says:

كَذَٰلِكَ نَسْلُكُهُ فِي قُلُوبِ الْمُجْرِمِينَ

ka-dhālika naslukuhu fī qulūbi al-mujrimīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:12

“Thus We cause it to enter the hearts of the criminals.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:200 also says:

كَذَٰلِكَ سَلَكْنَاهُ فِي قُلُوبِ الْمُجْرِمِينَ

ka-dhālika salaknāhu fī qulūbi al-mujrimīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:200

“Thus We caused it to enter the hearts of the criminals.”

The morphological difference is naslukuhu, an imperfect verb, and salaknāhu, a perfect verb.

Both accompany fī qulūbi al-mujrimīn. The construction concerns bringing something into the hearts of a specified group.

The important question is what the pronoun -hu refers to.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ the preceding verses concern the Quran's revelation, its Arabic language, and opponents' response. Referring the pronoun to the Quran or revealed message has clear contextual support.

In Al-Ḥijr the nearby verses mention divine remembrance, earlier messengers, and ridicule of messengers. Proposed antecedents include the divine message or the rejection described in the preceding passage.

The verb alone cannot settle that difference. It expresses bringing in; identifying the pronoun's antecedent requires context.

Entering the heart is not identical with believing

In both chapters, the statement about entry into hearts is followed by the criminals' failure to believe.

On the reading that the pronoun refers to the message, this shows that a message entering someone's awareness or heart does not necessarily mean acceptance or belief.

Someone may hear words, understand them, and even experience their inward effect without accepting them.

If, in a particular reading, the object is rejection or criminal conduct, nasluku alone does not establish that The God arbitrarily compels disbelief. The object, description of the recipients, and other passages on guidance and responsibility must be examined separately.

Salaka alone defines neither faith nor error here; it expresses the object's being brought into or finding its way into the heart.

The heart is the place of reception or occurrence. The surrounding verses establish the matter's value and outcome.

6. Entry into punishment and the outcome of deeds

Being brought into difficult punishment

Al-Jinn 72:17 says:

لِنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ وَمَنْ يُعْرِضْ عَنْ ذِكْرِ رَبِّهِ يَسْلُكْهُ عَذَابًا صَعَدًا

li-naftinahum fīhi wa-man yuʿriḍ ʿan dhikri rabbihi yaslukhu ʿadhāban ṣaʿadā

Al-Jinn 72:17

“So that We may test them through it. Whoever turns away from remembrance of his Lord, He will bring him into arduous punishment.”

Yaslukhu is an imperfect verb in the jussive as the conditional consequence. The attached -hu refers to the person turning away from remembrance of his Lord.

ʿAdhāban ṣaʿadan describes severe, arduous punishment.

ʿAdhāban can be analyzed as a second object, or through an understood preposition in a construction conveying entry into punishment. Either way, the sense is that the person is brought into and faces difficult punishment.

This is more than simply walking: someone is brought into a condition the verse calls punishment.

What brought you into Saqar?

Al-Muddaththir 74:42 says:

مَا سَلَكَكُمْ فِي سَقَرَ

mā salakakum fī Saqar

Al-Muddaththir 74:42

“What brought you into Saqar?”

Salakakum is a perfect verb, with -kum as its object.

Fī Saqara identifies the place in which those addressed find themselves.

The question concerns what led to this outcome, rather than which road they traveled within Saqar.

Their answers concern their conduct: not being among those who performed ṣalāh, not feeding the needy, joining others in wrongful discourse or conduct, and denying the Day of Recompense.

In this setting of accountability, mā salakakum asks about the cause and circumstances of reaching that end.

Lexically, salaka expresses entry into a place. Moral responsibility and the reason for entry emerge from the answers and the other verses.

The verb itself means neither “they sinned” nor “they were punished,” and is not a special name for movement toward Hell. Those ideas arise in connection with this context.

Sulūk and the limits of choice

A person may follow a course in life that leads to an outcome they did not desire.

Choosing conduct, experiencing its effects, and entering the final outcome must therefore be distinguished.

Salaka alone does not establish whether entry is fully voluntary, commanded by another, follows divine patterns, or results from judgment. The agent, object, prepositions, and context determine such distinctions.

Al-Muddaththir 74:42 asks what brought people into Saqar; Al-Jinn 72:17 states the consequence of turning away from remembrance; Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32 commands bringing a person into a chain.

All concern bringing in or entering, but their causes, agents, and manner differ.

7. Stationing guards around a messenger

Al-Jinn 72:27 says:

إِلَّا مَنِ ارْتَضَىٰ مِنْ رَسُولٍ فَإِنَّهُ يَسْلُكُ مِنْ بَيْنِ يَدَيْهِ وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِ رَصَدًا

illā mani irtaḍā min rasūlin fa-innahu yasluku min bayni yadayhi wa-min khalfihi raṣadā

Al-Jinn 72:27

“Except a messenger whom He has chosen; then He stations guards before him and behind him.”

Yasluku concerns placing guards in specified positions.

Raṣadan denotes a watchful guard or guards. Min bayni yadayhi wa-min khalfihi identifies their positions relative to the messenger.

Here sulūk is neither the messenger's walking nor necessarily a description of the guards traversing a physical route; it concerns their placement or appointment at positions of watch.

This approaches the use in Ṭā Hā 20:53, where the verb describes providing paths in the earth.

In each case, the agent places something appropriately: the earth's paths in Ṭā Hā, and guards around a messenger in Al-Jinn.

Al-Jinn 72:26, Al-Jinn 72:27, and Al-Jinn 72:28 concern the unseen and messengership. This guarding relates to the messenger's mission and conveying what has been entrusted to him. Salaka alone does not define the guards' nature or the details of their watch.

8. Distinctions from related roots

S L K and S B L: traveling and the path

Sabīl denotes a path or means of reaching a destination.

Salaka expresses entering or traveling a path, or placing something in a course.

The families appear together in Al-Naḥl 16:69, Ṭā Hā 20:53, and Nūḥ 71:20. Subul names the paths, while salaka expresses the agent's relationship to them.

A path's existence differs from traveling it. A road may exist without someone following it, or be available for human use without describing anyone's present movement along it.

S L K and Ṭ R Q: sulūk and ṭarīq

Ṭarīq belongs to the field of roads and passages. Sulūk chiefly concerns entry or movement along such a road.

Arabic can say salaka al-ṭarīqa: “he traveled the road.”

Ṭarīq supplies the route, and salaka expresses traveling it.

This distinction is not absolute across every figurative or technical use, but helps explain the construction.

S L K and D KH L: sulūk and entry

Dakhala denotes entering a place, group, or condition.

Salaka can also convey entry, often with emphasis on the route, channel, passage, or location into which something is placed.

Inserting the hand into a garment's opening can be explained through entry, while usluk yadaka retains the image of passing the hand through that opening.

This is a tendency of usage, not a rule that every dukhūl lacks a route or every sulūk requires a narrow passage.

S L K and W L J: sulūk and wulūj

Walaja also concerns entry, sometimes emphasizing something's entering within another thing.

Quranic expressions use this family for night entering day, day entering night, and things entering the earth.

Salaka can likewise express entry within something, but also traveling a road, stationing guards, and providing paths in the earth.

The roots overlap without being interchangeable in every occurrence.

S L K and T B ʿ: traveling and following

Tabiʿa concerns following someone or something.

Salaka focuses on the path itself and entry or movement along it.

Someone may travel a road without following a particular person. Conversely, a person can follow a messenger, book, command, supposition, or desire without spatial movement being involved.

Following emphasizes the follower's relationship to what is followed; sulūk emphasizes the traveler's relationship to a course or the place in which something is put.

S L K and H D Y: traveling and guidance

Guidance concerns showing the way, making a path known, and directing toward what is right.

Sulūk expresses entering or traveling the course itself.

Both may occur together: someone recognizes a path and then follows it.

Knowing the path is not traveling it, and traveling any path does not establish guidance. Its value and direction require the object and context.

S L K and J R Y: passage and flow

Jary denotes movement and flow, particularly the continuing movement of water and other phenomena.

Salaka can express something's placement in a channel or course of movement.

In Al-Zumar 39:21, water's passage in the earth relates to flow and the appearance of springs. The root does not itself mean every kind of flowing.

Flow can describe how movement occurs; sulūk emphasizes finding a way in and occupying a course.

9. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Translation should follow the verb's construction and object. One fixed equivalent cannot cover every Quranic form.

Fa-uslukī subula rabbiki in Al-Naḥl 16:69 can be rendered “follow your Lord's paths.”

Li-taslukū minhā subulan fijājan in Nūḥ 71:20 suits “so that you may travel its broad paths.”

Wa-salaka lakum fīhā subulan in Ṭā Hā 20:53 is best conveyed by “provided paths in it for you” or “laid out paths,” since the agent makes routes available for people.

Fa-usluk fīhā in Al-Muʾminūn 23:27 means “then bring into it.”

Usluk yadaka fī jaybika in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32 can be “insert your hand into your garment's opening.”

Fa-salakahu yanābīʿa fī al-arḍi in Al-Zumar 39:21 can be “channeled it as springs in the earth” or “gave it passage through the earth as springs.”

Naslukuhu and salaknāhu in Al-Ḥijr 15:12 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:200 basically mean “We bring it in” and “We brought it in.” A full translation should identify the pronoun's antecedent contextually, rather than inserting disbelief, denial, or faith without examination.

Fa-uslukūhu in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32 can be “put him into it.”

Yaslukhu ʿadhāban ṣaʿadan in Al-Jinn 72:17 means “He will bring him into arduous punishment.”

Yasluku ... raṣadan in Al-Jinn 72:27 is naturally “stations guards” or “appoints guards.”

Mā salakakum fī Saqara in Al-Muddaththir 74:42 can be “what brought you into Saqar?” or “what led you into Saqar?”

Moving, inserting, giving passage, passing through, traveling, and placing can each convey part of this field where the context supports it.

Spiritual progress, religious following, forced error, or guaranteed arrival must not be inserted as the root's fixed meaning.

Conclusion

Early Arabic S L K concerns traveling a road, entering a course, and inserting one thing into another. Poetry attests movement and involvement in an undertaking; lexical accounts record passing things through channels or placing them in particular locations.

The Quran uses only forms of the basic triliteral verb, but in two directions: the agent either travels a course or brings someone or something into a route, place, or condition.

The bee's paths, human travel, bringing creatures aboard, inserting a hand, water's passage in the earth, entry into hearts, stationing guards, bringing someone into punishment, and reaching Saqar all belong to this field.

Their agents, objects, voluntariness, moral values, and outcomes differ. Traveling a path is not inherently guidance; a message's entry into the heart is not automatically faith; bringing someone into punishment is not every form's fixed meaning.

The root can be defined as follows:

S L K denotes entering or bringing something into a particular route, channel, place, or condition, and traveling or passing along it.

This field explains physical and natural uses as well as those concerning hearts, messengership, deeds, and human outcomes, while keeping lexical meaning distinct from contextual interpretation and moral evaluation.
