Contraction, recoil, and aversion: from physical imagery in early Arabic to the hearts’ response in the Quran

In early Arabic, SH M Z concerns contraction, drawing together, and recoiling from what is felt to be unpleasant, frightening, or unwelcome. Ishmaʾazza describes a person or thing reacting to an external stimulus, recoiling from it, or feeling aversion and displeasure.

The family is not confined to mental aversion. Earlier uses also describe bodily contraction, withdrawal, and resistance to pressure. In people, such a response can arise from fear, dislike, revulsion, or rejection.

The Quran uses this root only as ishmaʾazzat in Al-Zumar 39:45. The hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter recoil when The God alone is mentioned, whereas they rejoice when others besides Him are mentioned.

Understanding this usage requires distinguishing the lexical meaning, the human response, and its cause in the verse’s setting.

1. The root in early Arabic

Contraction and recoil under pressure

Arabic shamz concerns contraction and drawing together. In human usage it describes the self or body recoiling from something unwelcome.

Ishmaʾazza also expresses contraction, aversion, alarm, and turning away.

In ishmaʾazza mina al-shayʾ, someone reacts to something found unpleasant. The reaction may remain inward aversion or become visible in facial expression and posture.

Someone suddenly encountering a frightening or deeply unpleasant scene may instinctively grimace, draw inward, or move away. This illustrates the semantic field.

Physical contraction and psychological aversion should not be equated absolutely. Someone may feel aversion without visibly contracting, or recoil from physical danger without moral repugnance.

A witness from the Muʿallaqah of ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm

An important pre-Islamic poetic witness occurs in the Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm.

Addressing ʿAmr ibn Hind, the poet describes his people’s firmness through the image of a spear shaft resisting the pressure applied to straighten it:

إِذَا عَضَّ الثِّقَافُ بِهَا اشْمَأَزَّتْ

idhā ʿaḍḍa al-thiqāfu bihā ishmaʾazzat

وَوَلَّتْهُ عَشَوْزَنَةً زَبُونَا

wa-wallat-hu ʿashawzanatan zabūnā

Translation:

“When the straightening tool bites upon it, it recoils, turning toward it hard and fiercely resistant.”

The thiqāf is a tool for straightening a crooked spear shaft. The poet portrays the shaft as reacting against its pressure and refusing to bend or submit.

Ishmaʾazzat here evokes recoil and resistance.

In the preceding verse, the poet addresses the king and recalls earlier enemies’ inability to make his people yield. The shaft thus represents their firmness and resistance.

This witness matters for several reasons.

First, the language transmitted as pre-Islamic poetry uses ishmaʾazza beyond human feelings of aversion, applying it to an object under pressure.

Second, resistance and recoil are prominent. The shaft has no literal hatred or human will; personification depicts the people’s resistance.

Third, the usage warns against reducing the whole family to modern sensory disgust.

The verse belongs to the transmitted Muʿallaqah, although wording and pronouns vary between accounts. The poem’s attribution to the pre-Islamic poet is established in that tradition; this study has not independently dated this particular line.

The face and the human self

Classical Arabic uses tashammaza wajhuhu for a face changing expression and drawing into a grimace.

Displeasure or anger can become visible on the face. Facial contraction and change here connect with a person’s inward condition.

Shamazat nafsī minhu is likewise recorded for the self recoiling with aversion from something unpleasant.

Lexical explanations also define shamz as the self’s aversion to something it dislikes.

The usage extends beyond the body to an inward state: the person’s self or heart rejects something and turns away.

Lexicographical explanations also mention alarm and shuddering, but not every instance necessarily arises from fear.

This matters in the Quranic setting: aversion to a truth may arise from its conflict with someone’s beliefs or wishes, rather than physical fear.

Is contraction historically the source of every meaning?

Physical contraction, recoil, and psychological aversion have a recognizable relationship.

Each involves reacting to something unwelcome, sometimes visibly in the body and sometimes inwardly or behaviorally.

That relationship does not prove that pre-Islamic Arabic first knew only the physical meaning and subsequently developed the psychological one.

Such a historical sequence would require independent evidence from different periods.

The evidence supports a field encompassing contraction and recoil as well as inward aversion and rejection.

2. Core meaning and Quranic forms

The Quran uses this family in only one verbal form:

Ishmaʾazzat in Al-Zumar 39:45.

It is the feminine singular perfect of ishmaʾazza; its verbal noun is ishmiʾzāz.

Lisan al-Arab places the word under triliteral SH M Z and calls its internal hamzah additional. Another morphological analysis treats it through quadriliteral SH M ʾ Z. This article follows the site’s SH M Z entry without presenting the derivation as undisputed; the alternative analyses do not change the verb’s established meaning.

Forms including shamaza, tashammaza, yashmaʾizzu, ishmiʾzāz, and mushmaʾizz occur in Arabic but not in the Quran.

Ishmaʾazzat is a perfect verb whose subject is qulūbu, “hearts.”

Feminine singular agreement with qulūb, a nonhuman broken plural in grammatical terms, is natural in Arabic. It implies no difference in the actual number or nature of the hearts.

The core in the Quranic usage is:

An inward recoil with aversion or displeasure toward something the self rejects.

Al-Zumar 39:45 attributes this response to hearts. Its primary concern is inward aversion and rejection, rather than physical contraction of the heart muscle or a specific medical event.

3. Hearts recoiling from the mention of The God

The only Quranic occurrence is in Al-Zumar 39:45:

وَإِذَا ذُكِرَ اللَّهُ وَحْدَهُ اشْمَأَزَّتْ قُلُوبُ الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْآخِرَةِ وَإِذَا ذُكِرَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ دُونِهِ إِذَا هُمْ يَسْتَبْشِرُونَ

wa-idhā dhukira allāhu waḥdahu ishmaʾazzat qulūbu alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-l-ākhirati wa-idhā dhukira alladhīna min dūnihi idhā hum yastabshirūn

Al-Zumar 39:45

Translation:

“When The God alone is mentioned, the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter recoil with aversion; but when those besides Him are mentioned, suddenly they rejoice.”

The verse sets two different responses against one another.

First, The God alone is mentioned, and the group’s hearts recoil.

Second, others besides The God are mentioned, and the same group rejoices.

This contrast is crucial to understanding ishmaʾazzat.

The importance of waḥdahu

In wa-idhā dhukira Allāhu waḥdahu, the word waḥdahu, “alone,” is essential.

The verse does not merely say that they react with aversion when The God is named. It specifies His being mentioned alone.

The preceding verses help explain this qualification.

Al-Zumar 39:3 declares that sincere devotion belongs to The God and quotes those who adopt protectors besides Him, claiming to worship them only to draw nearer to Him.

Al-Zumar 39:43 questions taking intercessors besides The God; Al-Zumar 39:44 declares that all intercession belongs to Him.

Al-Zumar 39:45 therefore places His exclusive mention within a discussion of worship and intercession belonging to Him, and the rejection of the partners and intercessors adopted besides Him.

The group did not necessarily oppose every mention of The God. Mentioning Him without the standing they assigned to others provoked their aversion.

Ishmiʾzāz here is thus a response to the call to divine oneness and exclusive devotion.

Forqan’s principle on the oneness of The God discusses His oneness in worship, lordship, guardianship, sovereignty, and legislation.

The oneness of The God

Why are hearts the subject?

The Quran does not say that their faces grimaced or their bodies trembled. It says:

اشْمَأَزَّتْ قُلُوبُ

ishmaʾazzat qulūbu

Al-Zumar 39:45

The hearts recoiled from the mention of The God alone.

In many Quranic passages, the heart is associated with knowledge, belief, acceptance, denial, inclination, and decision.

Attributing recoil to hearts therefore highlights an inward condition and orientation.

Such aversion might also appear in a face or behavior, but the verse does not require a description of that bodily reaction.

Ishmaʾazzat does not mean simply not knowing, doubting, or asking questions. It describes a negative response involving aversion.

The root alone cannot disclose every intention or the definite cause of each individual’s response. Those require context and other evidence.

Not believing in the Hereafter

The verse attributes this response to alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-l-ākhirah: those who do not believe in the Hereafter.

This qualification must remain in the analysis.

The verse is not about every person, everyone with religious questions, or anyone who disputes a religious opinion. It identifies the group by their lack of belief in the Hereafter and describes their response.

In Quranic thought, belief in the Hereafter is connected with human responsibility, judgment, and accountability before The God. Its juxtaposition with aversion to His exclusive mention is therefore significant here.

This association does not make denial of the Hereafter part of the lexical meaning of SH M Z.

Nor should the verse become a psychological rule that every person without such belief must always react this way in every situation. It describes the response under discussion.

Recoil contrasted with rejoicing

The second part says:

وَإِذَا ذُكِرَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ دُونِهِ إِذَا هُمْ يَسْتَبْشِرُونَ

wa-idhā dhukira alladhīna min dūnihi idhā hum yastabshirūn

Al-Zumar 39:45

Translation:

“But when those besides Him are mentioned, suddenly they rejoice.”

Yastabshirūna comes from B SH R and concerns happiness and rejoicing.

Two states are juxtaposed: aversion when The God alone is mentioned and delight when others besides Him are mentioned.

The contrast concerns more than a passing unpleasant sensation: it reveals different orientations and objects of acceptance.

Those whose hearts recoil in the first situation rejoice in the second. Their response changes with what is mentioned.

Istibshār does not itself mean associating partners or being misguided. Joy appears positively in many Quranic passages. What makes this response blameworthy is its object and setting.

The verse does not prohibit every mention of prophets, righteous people, or other creatures. Mention of others is here contrasted with exclusive mention of The God, within the discussion of protectors and intercessors adopted besides Him.

Its meaning should not be expanded into opposition to naming anything other than The God.

4. Recoil and related Quranic responses

SH M Z and N F R

N F R concerns moving away, turning away, fleeing, or distancing oneself.

Ishmiʾzāz highlights contraction and recoil with aversion.

The roots overlap in rejection, but N F R can also describe actual movement away, whereas Al-Zumar 39:45 describes an inward response of hearts.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:46 states:

وَإِذَا ذَكَرْتَ رَبَّكَ فِي الْقُرْآنِ وَحْدَهُ وَلَّوْا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهِمْ نُفُورًا

wa-idhā dhakarta rabbaka fī al-qurʾāni waḥdahu wallaw ʿalā adbārihim nufūrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:46

Translation:

“When you mention your Lord alone in the Quran, they turn their backs in aversion.”

This closely resembles Al-Zumar 39:45 in subject: both mention the Lord alone and describe a negative audience response.

Al-Isrāʾ emphasizes turning away; Al-Zumar emphasizes the hearts’ aversion.

Together they can illuminate related aspects of a response: inward rejection and outward withdrawal.

This contextual relationship does not make the two roots complete synonyms.

SH M Z and K R H

Karāhah concerns disliking something or finding it unwelcome.

Ishmiʾzāz can arise from dislike, but recoil and aversion are more prominent in it.

Someone may dislike an action yet perform it. That dislike is not necessarily ishmiʾzāz.

Describing someone as recoiling from a subject conveys a reaction of aversion and withdrawal.

Karāhah and ishmiʾzāz overlap, without having the same intensity or kind of response in every sentence.

SH M Z and Q SH ʿ R

Iqshiʿrār commonly describes skin shuddering or bristling in fear or profound emotion.

Al-Zumar 39:23 describes those who fear their Lord: their skins shudder at His Book, then their skins and hearts soften toward His remembrance.

This helps distinguish the two expressions.

Al-Zumar 39:23 joins the strong response with reverent fear and subsequent softening. In Al-Zumar 39:45, hearts’ recoil contrasts with joy at the mention of others.

Not every bodily or psychological shudder therefore signifies aversion and rejection.

Iqshiʿrār can accompany reverence and acceptance; ishmiʾzāz in the passage examined concerns aversion and rejection.

SH M Z and W J L

Wajal concerns fear or apprehension arising in the heart.

Al-Anfāl 8:2 describes believers whose hearts feel wajal when The God is mentioned.

This differs from ishmiʾzāz.

Fear of The God can accompany recognition of His greatness, responsibility, and reverence. The group in Al-Zumar 39:45 instead recoils from His being mentioned alone.

Every change in the heart upon His mention should therefore not be called aversion.

The object, direction, and accompanying conduct help establish each expression’s meaning.

SH M Z and SH M T

Shamātah, from SH M T, means rejoicing at another’s misfortune or failure.

Ishmiʾzāz, catalogued under SH M Z here, concerns contraction and aversion.

The roots look similar but differ in meaning.

Someone may recoil from words or conduct without enjoying another’s suffering. Conversely, someone may gloat over an enemy’s failure without feeling revulsion.

Similar letters do not establish a shared derivation or meaning.

5. Understanding and contemporary usage

In modern Persian, ishmiʾzāz often suggests disgust, strong aversion, or revulsion. An unpleasant sight, an offensive smell, or cruel behavior may evoke it.

That usage connects with Arabic meanings but does not exhaust their range.

The spear-shaft image shows recoil and resistance under pressure as another literary application. Al-Zumar 39:45 instead concerns an inward response to a religious truth.

Translating the verse with a Persian term for sensory disgust may focus attention too narrowly on bodily reactions to an unpleasant sight or smell.

Conversely, merely “becoming dissatisfied” weakens the meaning too much.

The verse describes hearts recoiling in aversion, beyond disagreement or mild dissatisfaction.

Rejecting truth and feeling discomfort

A statement can challenge someone’s beliefs, interests, or cherished position. The initial response may be discomfort or resistance.

Someone long reliant on a leader or institution’s authority may, for example, dislike hearing its absolute authority questioned.

If this stems from assigning that leader an ultimate position rivaling the truth, it may invite reflection alongside Al-Zumar 39:45.

But not every discomfort with criticism of a leader, or disagreement with a viewpoint, is a definite instance of the Quranic response.

Discomfort, disagreement, scholarly doubt, prejudice, and conscious rejection of truth are different states. Judgment requires examining the issue, knowledge, evidence, and response to reasons.

Someone may also recoil from a false religious claim or misuse of The God’s name. A religious association alone does not make such a response the blameworthy recoil described here.

Al-Zumar 39:45 specifies the object: the mention of The God alone, contrasted with delight when others besides Him are mentioned.

Any contemporary application must preserve that qualification.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Possible equivalents of ishmaʾazza include “feel aversion,” “recoil with revulsion,” “draw inward in displeasure,” and, in some settings, “come to detest.”

“Contract” preserves the earlier physical image, but without explanation could be mistaken for a purely bodily response in this verse.

“Come to detest” conveys the psychological meaning, but may not fully express the quality of recoil.

The modern Persian equivalent for sensory disgust can likewise narrow the meaning to a bodily response.

For Al-Zumar 39:45, Persian “feel aversion” is a particularly natural short equivalent.

The proposed rendering is therefore:

“When The God alone is mentioned, the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter recoil with aversion.”

A lexical explanation can add that the aversion is related to inward contraction and recoil from what is unwelcome.

A proposed translation of the complete verse is:

“When The God alone is mentioned, the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter recoil with aversion; but when those besides Him are mentioned, suddenly they rejoice.”

This retains the contrast without substituting a particular interpretive example for the Arabic wording. These proposals do not change the installed verse translations.

Conclusion

In early Arabic, SH M Z concerns contraction, recoil, and a negative response to something felt to be unpleasant or unwelcome.

The witness attributed to ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm applies it to a spear shaft resisting a straightening tool. Human uses concern aversion, revulsion, alarm, and changing facial expression.

The Quran uses the family only in Al-Zumar 39:45, as ishmaʾazzat.

There, the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter recoil when The God alone is mentioned, yet rejoice when others besides Him are mentioned.

Al-Zumar 39:3, Al-Zumar 39:43, and Al-Zumar 39:44 place the matter within worship and intercession belonging to The God and the rejection of partners and intercessors adopted besides Him. The adjective “independent” is explanatory, not part of SH M Z or an express qualification in these verses.

The root itself does not mean associating partners, disbelief, denying the Hereafter, or arrogance. Such features come from the verse’s subject, construction, or other evidence.

Its meaning can finally be defined as:

SH M Z concerns contraction and recoil from something unwelcome; psychologically, it expresses aversion that leads the self or heart to turn away and reject it.

Al-Zumar 39:45 applies this meaning to hearts responding to the mention of The God alone. Persian “feel aversion” is natural in translation, while contraction and recoil can be explained in the lexical discussion.
