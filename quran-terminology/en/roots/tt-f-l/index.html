Childhood, softness, and early growth: from birth and upbringing to puberty and awareness of privacy

The Arabic root ṭ f l relates to smallness, softness, delicacy, and early growth. Its best-known word is طِفْل, a child: a human being in early life before puberty. Arabic also applies it to newborn and young animals.

Other recorded usages describe soft fingers, fresh small plants, animals having young, and the gentle light of early or late daytime. The family extends beyond an age category, but these meanings should not all be derived from one original image without historical evidence.

The root occurs four times in the Quran, always concerning human children. طِفْلًا in Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Ghāfir 40:67 describes a stage after birth. الطِّفْل in Al-Nūr 24:31 concerns children who have not acquired the awareness of women's intimate matters relevant to the passage. الأَطْفَال in Al-Nūr 24:59 concerns reaching puberty and respecting others' privacy.

These usages show that childhood is a developmental stage, but physical abilities, understanding, and responsibilities do not remain unchanged throughout it. The Quran describes both children at birth and those approaching puberty, who learn new practices and responsibilities as they develop.

1. Historical meaning and early Arabic usage

طَفْل: softness, delicacy, and freshness

Older Arabic uses طَفْل, with fatḥah, for something soft, delicate, or fresh. بَنَانٌ طَفْلٌ describes soft, delicate fingers.

طَفْلَةُ الْأَنَامِلِ likewise describes someone with soft fingers in Arabic poetry.

Here طفلة need not mean a little girl. It may describe an adult woman, referring to her fingers' delicacy rather than her age.

طَفْل as an adjective of softness should therefore be distinguished from طِفْل as the noun for a child. Their forms and contexts determine their particular meanings.

A poetic witness attributed to ʿAmr ibn Qamīʾah

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Qamīʾah states:

إِلَى كَفَلٍ مِثْلِ دِعْصِ النَّقَا
وَكَفٍّ تُقَلِّبُ بِيضًا طِفَالًا

ilā kafalin mithli diʿṣi al-naqā / wa-kaffin tuqallibu bīḍan ṭifālā

“To a rounded hip like a mound of sand, and a hand moving white, delicate fingers.”

The poet uses طِفَالًا in describing bodily beauty.

It concerns softness and delicacy here, not children.

The witness supports an adjectival usage in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

ʿAmr ibn Qamīʾah is known as a pre-Islamic poet. As with other early verse, the attribution and reading of each line should be assessed separately from the poet's lifetime. The witness supports softness but does not alone establish that the word for a child developed from it.

طِفْل: young and not yet pubescent

Classical Arabic uses طِفْل for a young child and, more broadly, a person who has not reached puberty.

Some older lexical explanations extend childhood from birth to puberty; narrower accounts also exist, so context matters.

Everyday usage may emphasize younger children and use other terms for those approaching puberty.

It should not therefore be assumed that طفل always means only a newborn or very young child.

طِفْلَة is its feminine form, and أَطْفَال its familiar plural.

الطِّفْل can also function generically for a group of children: singular in form while referring to more than one child.

This matters in Al-Nūr 24:31, where singular الطِّفْلِ is followed by plural الَّذِينَ.

Young animals

Arabic also uses طِفْل for young animals.

It can describe an ibex, gazelle, or another animal in early life.

A poem attributed to Ṣakhr al-Ghayy al-Hudhalī describes an ibex:

بِهَا كَانَ طِفْلًا ثُمَّ أَسْدَسَ وَاسْتَوَى
فَأَصْبَحَ لَهْمًا فِي لُهُومٍ قَرَاهِبِ

bihā kāna ṭiflan thumma asdasa wa-stawā / fa-aṣbaḥa lahman fī luhūmin qarāhib

“There he was once a young one; then he reached a later stage and matured, becoming an aged, powerful beast among other aged beasts.”

طِفْلًا describes the animal's early life.

The poet traces growth over time, from smallness and youth to later stages.

This illustrates a usage extending beyond humans to young animals.

Older lexical sources attribute the verse to Ṣakhr al-Ghayy, but its precise composition date is not sufficiently clear to call it certainly pre-Quranic without qualification.

أَطْفَلَت: having young offspring

أَطْفَلَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ is used for a woman who has a young child.

أَطْفَلَتِ الظَّبْيَةُ describes a gazelle that has produced young or has its young with it.

مُطْفِل describes a mother or female animal with young offspring, including camels and other animals.

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah states:

فَعَلَا فُرُوعُ الْأَيْهَقَانِ وَأَطْفَلَتْ
بِالْجَلْهَتَيْنِ ظِبَاؤُهَا وَنَعَامُهَا

fa-ʿalā furūʿu al-ayhaqāni wa-aṭfalat / bi-l-jalhatayni ẓibāʾuhā wa-naʿāmuhā

“The ayhaqān's branches rose, and on the two sides of the valley its gazelles and ostriches had young.”

أَطْفَلَتْ concerns having offspring and being accompanied by them.

Gazelles give live birth, whereas ostriches lay eggs. The poet joins them in an image of reproduction and flourishing land.

The line does not imply identical reproductive processes; one expression can encompass different ways of producing young.

It illustrates the family's association with young offspring and early animal rearing.

Labīd lived both before and after Islam. The line belongs to the Muʿallaqah attributed to him and the tradition of pre-Islamic poetry, but a precise date should not be inferred solely from his lifetime.

Other semantic branches

طَفَلُ الْعَشِيّ is recorded for late daytime as the sun approaches sunset.

طَفَلُ الْغَدَاةِ also occurs for the early light of the morning.

Forms in the family describe fresh short plants, gentle winds, and certain small or newly emerging things.

Softness, freshness, smallness, and gentle beginnings or endings can be observed in these usages.

These similarities do not establish that the child, early daylight, and fresh vegetation all derive from a single original image.

A root study should recognize the usages without inventing an unsupported derivational history to unify them.

For living beings, recognizable associations include smallness, softness, and early growth. Delicacy and freshness stand out in adjectival usage; the Quranic noun طِفْل names a child.

2. طِفْلًا: from birth toward growth and strength

Two verses place طِفْلًا after descriptions of human creation.

They present childhood as a stage after emergence from the womb.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5: a child emerging from the womb

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 states:

وَنُقِرُّ فِي الْأَرْحَامِ مَا نَشَاءُ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى ثُمَّ نُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا ثُمَّ لِتَبْلُغُوا أَشُدَّكُمْ

wa-nuqirru fī al-arḥāmi mā nashāʾu ilā ajalin musamman thumma nukhrijukum ṭiflan thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum

Al-Ḥajj 22:5

“We retain in the wombs what We will until an appointed term; then We bring you forth as children, then so that you may reach your full strength.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:5

The verse moves from stages of creation to birth and subsequent growth.

طِفْلًا here describes being a child at birth.

The pronoun كُمْ in نُخْرِجُكُمْ is plural, whereas طِفْلًا is singular.

This does not mean that a group of people emerges as one child.

It means each person is brought forth as a child at a stage of creation.

An Arabic singular can describe the condition of each member of a group in this construction.

طِفْلًا therefore applies to all those addressed despite its singular form.

Childhood and attaining full strength

After ثُمَّ نُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا comes:

ثُمَّ لِتَبْلُغُوا أَشُدَّكُمْ

thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum

Al-Ḥajj 22:5

“Then so that you may reach your full strength.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:5

أَشُدَّكُمْ belongs to ش د د and concerns attaining strength and capacity.

The sequence indicates that a newborn has not yet attained later physical strength and development.

طفل does not itself imply absence of all understanding, will, feeling, or ability.

Abilities develop through infancy and childhood. A newborn differs from a child able to walk, speak, learn, and make decisions about conduct.

Both may be called طفل while differing in development.

The designation describes a broad life stage, not a fixed level of ability shared by all children.

Ghāfir 40:67: childhood within the course of life

Ghāfir 40:67 states:

هُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ نُطْفَةٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ عَلَقَةٍ ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا ثُمَّ لِتَبْلُغُوا أَشُدَّكُمْ ثُمَّ لِتَكُونُوا شُيُوخًا

huwa alladhī khalaqakum min turābin thumma min nuṭfatin thumma min ʿalaqatin thumma yukhrijukum ṭiflan thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum thumma li-takūnū shuyūkhā

Ghāfir 40:67

“He is the One who created you from dust, then from a drop, then from something clinging; then He brings you forth as children, then so that you may reach your full strength, then so that you may become elderly.”

Ghāfir 40:67

Here too طِفْلًا follows prenatal stages of creation.

Full strength and old age follow it.

The sequence presents childhood as a life stage after birth, followed by further growth.

It does not imply that everyone necessarily reaches old age.

The remainder of the verse states that some are taken in death earlier.

The verse describes a general course of human life while acknowledging differing individual outcomes.

Does طفل mean newborn?

In these two verses it accompanies emergence from the womb, so a newborn is a clear instance.

That does not restrict the word throughout Arabic to the first days or months after birth.

The passages in Al-Nūr show usage extending to later stages before puberty.

The word's scope must be distinguished from the particular stage described by a verse.

Al-Ḥajj and Ghāfir describe the start of postnatal life, but طفل is not confined to that moment.

3. الطِّفْلِ الَّذِينَ: childhood and awareness of women's privacy

Al-Nūr 24:31 uses طفل in another context.

The verse concerns looking, covering, adornment, and the people before whom women may reveal adornment.

Its list includes:

أَوِ الطِّفْلِ الَّذِينَ لَمْ يَظْهَرُوا عَلَىٰ عَوْرَاتِ النِّسَاءِ

awi al-ṭifli alladhīna lam yaẓharū ʿalā ʿawrāti al-nisāʾ

Al-Nūr 24:31

“Or children who have not become aware of women's intimate parts.”

Al-Nūr 24:31

The phrase matters in several respects.

Why singular الطِّفْل and plural الَّذِينَ?

الطِّفْل is singular in form.

الَّذِينَ is a plural relative pronoun, and يَظْهَرُوا a plural verb.

This fits generic usage: the singular noun encompasses a group of children.

Some Arabic nouns name a kind or group without always taking a plural form.

The singular does not restrict the statement to one particular child.

Nor does singularity alone establish an exclusively male reference.

طفل can denote a girl or boy. The singular form alone does not settle the scope of the exception; the following qualification and context must also be considered.

لَمْ يَظْهَرُوا عَلَىٰ عَوْرَاتِ النِّسَاءِ

This qualification is essential to understanding the scope.

ظ ه ر can concern becoming apparent, gaining awareness or access, or prevailing, depending on construction.

Here عَلَىٰ عَوْرَاتِ النِّسَاءِ complements the verb.

Two main explanations concern children not yet aware of women's intimate and sexual matters, or those not yet showing sexual interest in women.

Both relate to development and awareness.

The phrase should not be restricted to children who have never happened to see a woman's unclothed body.

An accidental sight differs from the understanding and attention discussed here.

Nor should the phrase become a precise medical or psychological diagnosis for all children without evidence.

It describes a relevant characteristic rather than assigning one numerical age to everyone.

Differences in development and awareness

Children do not acquire every physical and cognitive ability simultaneously.

A young child may be unaware of and inattentive to many matters of others' privacy.

An older child may have greater awareness before puberty.

Thus being a child is not the entire stated criterion: الَّذِينَ لَمْ يَظْهَرُوا عَلَىٰ عَوْرَاتِ النِّسَاءِ also qualifies the group.

Children should not all be treated as identical regardless of understanding and development.

The verse does not imply that a prepubescent child has no awareness of private matters or no responsibility to respect privacy.

Later verses in the chapter discuss permission seeking before puberty.

Privacy and adult responsibility

The verse primarily concerns women's conduct in revealing adornment.

Understanding children's development also matters to interpreting that instruction.

Children learn the distinction between private and public spaces; adults should teach it in ways appropriate to their developing understanding.

Curiosity, lack of knowledge, or a developmental stage should not lead to judging a child exactly like an informed adult.

Respect for privacy should be accompanied by teaching, understanding, and proportionate responsibility.

This is a reflection on the passage alongside other instructions in Al-Nūr; طفل itself does not mean total irresponsibility or incapacity.

4. الأَطْفَال: puberty and changing privacy responsibilities

Al-Nūr 24:59 states:

وَإِذَا بَلَغَ الْأَطْفَالُ مِنْكُمُ الْحُلُمَ فَلْيَسْتَأْذِنُوا كَمَا اسْتَأْذَنَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ

wa-idhā balagha al-aṭfālu minkumu al-ḥuluma fa-l-yastaʾdhinū kamā istaʾdhana alladhīna min qablihim

Al-Nūr 24:59

“When the children among you reach puberty, let them ask permission as those before them asked permission.”

Al-Nūr 24:59

Unlike Al-Nūr 24:31, this verse uses the plural الأَطْفَال.

أطفال is the plural of طفل and refers to children.

The focus is not merely naming children but reaching a stage that brings a changed responsibility concerning others' privacy.

بَلَغَ الْأَطْفَالُ مِنْكُمُ الْحُلُمَ

بَلَغَ belongs to ب ل غ and means reaching a limit or stage.

الحُلُم here concerns reaching puberty, associated with sexual maturation and bodily development.

The phrase describes passage into that stage.

طفل itself does not define the threshold; بَلَغَ الْحُلُمَ expresses it.

The word should not be restricted to someone who has never experienced a nocturnal emission.

Such an experience is a recognized marker in discussions of puberty, but the Arabic designation of a child is broader than one bodily sign.

Puberty should not be equated with full physical strength, intellectual maturity, or ability to manage property.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Ghāfir 40:67 mention أَشُدّ; Al-Nūr 24:59 mentions حُلُم; Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 separately considers sound judgment before entrusting orphans with property.

These related terms are not identical in meaning or associated responsibilities.

Instruction and responsibility before puberty

Al-Nūr 24:59 should also be read with the preceding verse.

Al-Nūr 24:58 states:

لِيَسْتَأْذِنْكُمُ الَّذِينَ مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ وَالَّذِينَ لَمْ يَبْلُغُوا الْحُلُمَ مِنْكُمْ ثَلَاثَ مَرَّاتٍ

li-yastaʾdhinkumu alladhīna malakat aymānukum wa-alladhīna lam yablughū al-ḥuluma minkum thalātha marrāt

Al-Nūr 24:58

“Let those whom your right hands possess and those among you who have not reached puberty ask your permission at three times.”

Al-Nūr 24:58

The verse specifies before dawn ṣalāt, at midday when garments are set aside, and after night ṣalāt.

These are occasions of particular household privacy.

The Quran therefore does not treat learning and respecting privacy as unnecessary before puberty.

Children able to learn permission seeking should be introduced to these practices in keeping with their development.

The next verse aligns permission seeking after puberty with that of those who reached that stage earlier.

This indicates that privacy responsibilities can change as children develop.

Al-Nūr 24:58 does not grant unrestricted license to violate privacy at every other time. It discusses three special occasions and ordinary household circulation; other passages in Al-Nūr also address respect for privacy.

Puberty does not complete every ability

Puberty is an important stage, but does not mean every capacity is complete.

A physically mature adolescent may still lack the experience and ability to administer substantial property independently.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 states:

وَابْتَلُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغُوا النِّكَاحَ فَإِنْ آنَسْتُمْ مِنْهُمْ رُشْدًا فَادْفَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ

wa-btalū al-yatāmā ḥattā idhā balaghū al-nikāḥa fa-in ānastum minhum rushdan fa-dfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6

“Test the orphans until they reach the stage of marriage; then, if you perceive sound judgment in them, hand over their property to them.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6

The verse separately mentions reaching that stage and establishing sound judgment before transferring property.

Different responsibilities may therefore involve different abilities.

Al-Nūr 24:59 alone does not establish that every legal, financial, and social responsibility automatically becomes identical to an adult's without further consideration.

Conversely, differing responsibilities do not mean that prepubescent children lack all understanding, choice, or capacity for instruction.

These passages consider development in relation to particular capacities and responsibilities.

5. Distinguishing طفل from related terms

ط ف ل and و ل د: child and offspring

وَلَد concerns birth and the relationship of offspring to parents.

It may describe young or adult offspring, since that relationship does not end at puberty.

طفل instead concerns a stage of growth and age.

Someone may be both طفل and ولد in childhood, remaining their parents' offspring as an adult while no longer ordinarily described as طفل by age.

The terms are not complete synonyms.

ولد should not automatically be restricted to sons: Quranic usage can include daughters and sons.

ط ف ل and ص ب و: child and young child

صَبِيّ also describes a young child.

Maryam 19:12 states:

وَآتَيْنَاهُ الْحُكْمَ صَبِيًّا

wa-ātaynāhu al-ḥukma ṣabiyyā

Maryam 19:12

“We gave him judgment while still a child.”

Maryam 19:29 also uses صَبِيًّا for a child in a cradle.

These usages describe a stage before adulthood.

طفل overlaps this age field, occurring particularly in Quranic descriptions of birth, growth toward puberty, and childhood responsibilities.

This distribution should not become an absolute rule that صبي always means newborn and طفل always an older child.

Both terms may describe the same person at some ages.

ط ف ل and غ ل م: child and young boy

غلام can describe a young boy or youth and appears in different Quranic contexts.

When Joseph is brought out of the well, Yūsuf 12:19 states:

يَا بُشْرَىٰ هَٰذَا غُلَامٌ

yā bushrā hādhā ghulām

Yūsuf 12:19

“Good news! This is a boy!”

Al-Kahf 18:74 likewise uses غُلَامًا for the boy encountered by Moses and his companion.

غلام can overlap طفل in age without matching every usage.

طفل is a general term applicable to girls and boys; غلام ordinarily refers to a boy.

غلام alone establishes neither precise age, sexual capacity, intellectual maturity, nor full responsibility.

ط ف ل and و ل د: child and infant

وَلِيد belongs to و ل د and concerns birth and early childhood.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:18, Pharaoh says to Moses:

أَلَمْ نُرَبِّكَ فِينَا وَلِيدًا

a-lam nurabbika fīnā walīdā

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:18

“Did we not raise you among us as a little child?”

وليد here concerns Moses' early life, not necessarily only the first days after birth.

طفل can likewise describe an infant, but its Quranic scope also reaches later stages before puberty.

Infancy is one instance of childhood, not the entire meaning of طفل.

ط ف ل and ي ت م: childhood and family circumstances

In established Arabic usage, يتيم describes a child who has lost their father.

It concerns family circumstances and parental loss, while طفل concerns growth and age.

One child may be orphaned while another has both parents present. Both are children, with different family circumstances.

Being orphaned does not itself mean poverty, intellectual incapacity, or having no property.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 discusses testing orphans' sound judgment and handing over their property. Family situation, puberty, and ability to manage property are distinct matters.

طفل and يتيم should therefore not be treated as synonyms.

ط ف ل and ن ط ف: childhood and a prenatal stage

نُطْفَة belongs to ن ط ف and appears in descriptions of early human creation.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Ghāfir 40:67, it precedes طِفْلًا.

The sequence distinguishes a prenatal stage from emerging as a child.

The two roots are independent. Partly similar letters or occurrence in one verse do not establish historical kinship.

طفل should not be translated as “fetus” in these passages: ثُمَّ نُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا describes emergence from the womb.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The main Persian equivalent of Quranic طِفْل is “child.”

It suits girls and boys without restricting the word to newborns or a very narrow age group.

طِفْل: child; a human being in early growth before puberty.

طِفْلَة: a girl child; distinguish adjectival طَفْلَة, with fatḥah, meaning soft or delicate.

أَطْفَال: children.

طُفُولَة: childhood.

Adjectival طَفْل: soft, delicate, or fresh.

أَطْفَلَت: had young offspring, according to the human or animal context.

مُطْفِل: a mother or female animal with young offspring.

Not all these forms occur in the Quran. The recorded Quranic forms here are طِفْلًا, الطِّفْل, and الأَطْفَال.

The following renderings are proposed for the four occurrences.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5

ثُمَّ نُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا

thumma nukhrijukum ṭiflā

Al-Ḥajj 22:5

“Then We bring you forth as children.”

Ghāfir 40:67

ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا

thumma yukhrijukum ṭiflā

Ghāfir 40:67

“Then He brings you forth as children.”

The Persian equivalent “as a child” naturally preserves the singular description applying to each plural addressee.

Al-Nūr 24:31

أَوِ الطِّفْلِ الَّذِينَ لَمْ يَظْهَرُوا عَلَىٰ عَوْرَاتِ النِّسَاءِ

awi al-ṭifli alladhīna lam yaẓharū ʿalā ʿawrāti al-nisāʾ

Al-Nūr 24:31

“Or children who have not become aware of women's intimate parts.”

The generic singular is rendered by a natural plural while preserving the qualification of awareness.

The explanation can clarify the relevance of developing awareness of women's privacy.

Al-Nūr 24:59

وَإِذَا بَلَغَ الْأَطْفَالُ مِنْكُمُ الْحُلُمَ

wa-idhā balagha al-aṭfālu minkumu al-ḥulum

Al-Nūr 24:59

“When the children among you reach puberty.”

الأَطْفَال means children; بَلَغَ الْحُلُمَ means reaching puberty.

Puberty should not be built into أطفال itself, since the subsequent construction expresses reaching that stage.

The basic translation should not replace طفل with “an infallible person,” “someone without choice,” “incapable of understanding,” or “requiring permanent guardianship.” None is expressed by the word in every usage.

7. Summary

ط ف ل relates to smallness, softness, delicacy, and early growth.

The noun طِفْل denotes a human child or young animal; adjectival طَفْل expresses softness. أَطْفَلَت and مُطْفِل concern having young offspring.

Early poetic witnesses illustrate softness, young animals, and reproduction. They do not conclusively establish that the noun for a child arose from softness or that all branches developed from one image.

The four Quranic occurrences all concern human children.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Ghāfir 40:67 place طِفْلًا after emergence from the womb within a course of further growth and capacity.

Al-Nūr 24:31 combines singular الطِّفْل with plural grammar, illustrating generic reference to children. The following qualification makes awareness of women's intimate matters relevant.

Al-Nūr 24:59 uses الأَطْفَال and connects reaching puberty with changed permission-seeking responsibilities.

Al-Nūr 24:58 and Al-Nūr 24:59 indicate that instruction in privacy matters before puberty as well, with responsibilities understood in relation to development and ability.

In the Quranic branch, ط ف ل denotes a child: a human being in early postnatal growth before puberty. Broader Arabic also applies it to young animals and includes adjectival branches concerning smallness, softness, and delicacy.

This does not restrict childhood to infancy or equate it with puberty, full physical strength, intellectual maturity, or property-management capacity.

Taken together, the passages show that childhood is not uniform and unchanging. Movement, speech, understanding, learning, and decision-making develop over time, and responsibilities can change alongside them.

A child's human worth does not depend on ability or puberty. Care and education should recognize developing personhood and abilities; acquiring some abilities should not justify burdens beyond capacity.

Childhood is a stage of development, neither absence of personhood nor automatic possession of every adult capacity. Understanding the root helps distinguish children's place in creation and the rights and responsibilities considered in each passage.
