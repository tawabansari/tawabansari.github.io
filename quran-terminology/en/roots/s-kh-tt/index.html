Core meaning: displeasure, disapproval and dissatisfaction with a person, conduct or situation that is not accepted or approved. It can involve anger and objection, but does not itself mean shouting, taking revenge or imposing punishment. The Qur’an uses the root both for human displeasure over the distribution of alms and for divine anger and displeasure at wrongful conduct. Its direct contrast with riḍā and riḍwān, approval and pleasure, is central to understanding it.

1. Introduction and meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic root S KH Ṭ concerns displeasure, dissatisfaction, disapproval and anger.

Sakhiṭa al-shayʾa means disapproving of something and being displeased with it.

Sakhiṭa ʿalayhi means being angry or displeased with someone.

Al-sakhaṭ and al-sukhṭ denote displeasure and anger.

Askhatahu means making someone displeased or angry.

Established Arabic usage places sakhaṭ in contrast with riḍā.

Someone pleased with conduct approves or accepts it; someone displeased with it finds it unwelcome and disapproves.

Displeasure does not necessarily involve an overt, forceful reaction.

A person may dislike a decision without voicing an objection.

They may become angry at someone else's conduct and react.

They may also simply disapprove and decline to accept something.

These situations share a semantic field but differ in how they manifest.

Pre-Islamic evidence: sakhaṭ and riḍā in ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm's Muʿallaqah

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm says in his famous Muʿallaqah:

وَنَحْنُ التَّارِكُونَ لِمَا سَخِطْنَا
وَنَحْنُ الْآخِذُونَ لِمَا رَضِينَا

wa-naḥnu al-tārikūna li-mā sakhiṭnā / wa-naḥnu al-ākhidhūna li-mā raḍīnā

Translation:

“We leave what displeases us, and take what pleases us.”

Sakhiṭnā and raḍīnā stand in direct contrast.

Sakhiṭnā concerns what displeases the poet's people.

Raḍīnā concerns what they approve and accept.

The active participles al-tārikūn and al-ākhidhūn express the conduct arising from these two attitudes.

They leave what they dislike and take what they approve.

The verse belongs to tribal boasting. The poet emphasizes his people's standing and power, insisting that others cannot force unwanted things upon them.

But sakhaṭ's lexical meaning is not restricted to tribal power, war or disobedience.

Power and independence are the objects of his boast; displeasure and disapproval are the meaning of sakhiṭnā.

Here displeasure accompanies leaving something, without necessarily implying revenge or an attack.

Early Arabic could thus express disapproval without the word itself denoting punishment.

The verse is transmitted in ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm's Muʿallaqah and its established commentaries. The wording cited here directly contrasts sakhiṭnā and raḍīnā; that does not establish an identical wording and order throughout every transmission.

Semantic implications of the example

Three points emerge.

First, Arabic poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period contrasts sakhaṭ with riḍā.

Second, sakhaṭ can concern disapproval and refusal to accept something.

Third, resulting conduct must be distinguished from displeasure itself.

Leaving is conveyed by al-tārikūn, rather than independently by sakhiṭnā.

Sakhaṭ should therefore not be defined as originally meaning leaving.

Nor should every usage be equated with intense anger and aggressive behavior.

Recorded Arabic usages

Lexicons record several important constructions.

سَخِطَ عَطَاءَهُ

sakhiṭa ʿaṭāʾahu

Meaning: “He was displeased with his gift.”

The gift may have been judged too small or otherwise unwelcome.

تَسَخَّطَ عَطَاءَهُ

tasakhkhaṭa ʿaṭāʾahu

Meaning: “He disliked his gift and considered it insufficient.”

أَسْخَطَنِي فُلَانٌ

askhaṭanī fulānun

Meaning: “So-and-so displeased or angered me.”

The object of displeasure matters in these usages.

It may be a gift.

It may arise from someone else's conduct.

Or it may concern a situation or decision.

The root itself does not determine whether the reason is just or unjust.

Historical definition

The poetic example and attested classical usage support this definition:

S KH Ṭ denotes being displeased with and disapproving of a person, conduct or situation that is not accepted or approved; it can involve anger, objection or refusal to accept.

Its contrast with riḍā is clear semantic evidence.

Its core meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from every reaction that may follow.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Sakhiṭa yaskhaṭu

The family's Form I verb.

It means becoming displeased, disapproving or becoming angry.

It can take a direct object or a prepositional complement.

سَخِطَ الشَّيْءَ

sakhiṭa al-shayʾa

Meaning: “He disapproved of that thing.”

سَخِطَ عَلَيْهِ

sakhiṭa ʿalayhi

Meaning: “He became angry with him.”

The Qur’an uses Form I for both The God and human beings.

Sakhaṭ and sukhṭ

Two verbal-noun forms in this family.

They denote displeasure, dissatisfaction and anger.

Both vocalizations are recorded.

The vowel difference alone does not create two entirely separate kinds of displeasure.

Sakhaṭin occurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:162.

Sākhiṭ

The Form I active participle.

It describes someone displeased or angry.

رَجُلٌ سَاخِطٌ

rajulun sākhiṭun

Meaning: “A displeased or angry man.”

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Maskhūṭ

The Form I passive participle.

It describes someone or something subjected to anger or disapproval.

مَسْخُوطٌ عَلَيْهِ

maskhūṭun ʿalayhi

Meaning: “Someone against whom anger is directed.”

This differs from sākhiṭ.

A sākhiṭ is displeased.

A maskhūṭ ʿalayhi is the object of another's displeasure.

Askhaṭa yuskhiṭu

A Form IV verb.

It means causing someone's displeasure or anger.

أَسْخَطَهُ

askhaṭahu

Meaning: “He made him displeased.”

Its subject causes sakhaṭ.

The verb occurs in Muḥammad 47:28.

The verse concerns following what displeases The God.

Iskhāṭ

The Form IV verbal noun.

It denotes causing displeasure or anger.

This verbal noun does not occur in the Qur’an in this form.

Tasakhkhaṭa yatasakhkhaṭu

A Form V verb.

It concerns displaying displeasure, disapproving or becoming angry.

Applied to a gift, it can express dissatisfaction with its amount or quality.

Form V alone does not establish that the person loudly announced displeasure or behaved aggressively.

Tasakhkhuṭ

The Form V verbal noun.

It denotes displeasure and expressing dissatisfaction.

Social usages may concern someone's response to a decision, gift or conduct.

Maskhaṭah

Something that causes anger or displeasure.

The form also appears in contrast with marḍāh.

Marḍāh concerns pleasure and what brings it about.

Maskhaṭah, in contrast, denotes a cause of displeasure.

Forms actually occurring in the Qur’an

The root occurs four times in three morphological categories: a verbal noun, Form I and Form IV.

  • Bi-sakhaṭin in Āl ʿImrān 3:162: a verbal noun with bi.
  • Sakhiṭa in Al-Māʾidah 5:80: a Form I perfect verb.
  • Yaskhaṭūna in Al-Tawbah 9:58: a Form I imperfect verb.
  • Askhaṭa in Muḥammad 47:28: a Form IV perfect verb.

Three verses attribute displeasure to The God or describe what causes His displeasure.

Al-Tawbah 9:58 applies it to human beings.

This distinction matters for the root's range.

3. Idhā hum yaskhaṭūn: human displeasure over the distribution of alms

وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَلْمِزُكَ فِي الصَّدَقَاتِ فَإِنْ أُعْطُوا مِنْهَا رَضُوا وَإِنْ لَمْ يُعْطَوْا مِنْهَا إِذَا هُمْ يَسْخَطُونَ

wa-minhum man yalmizuka fī al-ṣadaqāti fa-in uʿṭū minhā raḍū wa-in lam yuʿṭaw minhā idhā hum yaskhaṭūn

Al-Tawbah 9:58

Translation:

“Among them are those who criticize you over the alms. If they are given some, they are pleased; if they are not given any, suddenly they are angry.”

Al-Tawbah 9:58

Yaskhaṭūna is a third-person plural imperfect verb.

It belongs to Form I.

The same verse also uses raḍū.

Their juxtaposition clearly illustrates the Qur’anic contrast between displeasure and approval.

Pleasure and displeasure governed by personal benefit

The verse describes people criticizing Muḥammad over alms.

It then presents their conduct in two circumstances.

Receiving a share pleases them.

Receiving nothing produces displeasure and anger.

Their response here depends on personal benefit.

As the verse describes them, their criterion is whether they receive a share.

This distinguishes displeasure itself from its cause.

Someone may dislike a decision because it is unjust.

Another may dislike the same decision solely because it fails to provide an expected benefit.

Both experience displeasure.

Their moral standing nevertheless differs.

Sakhaṭ alone does not establish whether an objection is right or wrong. Its reason and the resulting conduct need examination.

Al-Tawbah 9:58 describes a response that changes with receiving or being denied alms.

The role of idhā hum

The latter part says:

وَإِنْ لَمْ يُعْطَوْا مِنْهَا إِذَا هُمْ يَسْخَطُونَ

wa-in lam yuʿṭaw minhā idhā hum yaskhaṭūn

Al-Tawbah 9:58

If they are not given any, suddenly they are angry.

Idhā in this construction highlights the sudden or immediate appearance of the response.

“Suddenly they are angry” thus fits the sentence.

Suddenness comes from idhā and the construction, rather than an invariant meaning of S KH Ṭ.

A person can be displeased for a long time without having become so suddenly.

Is every objection to distributing wealth blameworthy displeasure?

No. The verse does not condemn every objection to a financial decision.

It concerns alms and a particular group's behavior.

Al-Tawbah 9:60 identifies the uses of alms and eligible groups.

Distribution must therefore be assessed against the relevant rights and criteria.

A reasoned objection to injustice, discrimination or betrayal in distributing public resources differs from approval dependent solely on personal gain.

The context supports that distinction.

The first verse censures self-interested judgment; the verse on recipients then helps identify legitimate entitlements.

What this usage shows

Yaskhaṭūna in Al-Tawbah 9:58 means becoming displeased or angry.

It contrasts with raḍū.

The censure concerns the judgment and conduct of those whose satisfaction depends on personal benefit, rather than the mere existence of displeasure.

4. Sakhiṭa Allāhu ʿalayhim: divine displeasure and human responsibility

The other three occurrences relate to The God.

The word's familiar human meaning must be distinguished from its attribution to The God.

Human displeasure can arise from prejudice, fear, pride, self-interest or mistaken judgment.

The Qur’an presents divine displeasure in the context of judgment and human responsibility.

The attribution should not be turned into a bodily response or uncontrolled human anger.

Nor should the Qur’anic attribution be treated as an empty expression. How divine attributes are understood is a theological question beyond a lexical definition alone.

The Qur’an explicitly connects divine displeasure with human conduct and outcomes.

Āl ʿImrān 3:162: divine pleasure and displeasure

أَفَمَنِ اتَّبَعَ رِضْوَانَ اللَّهِ كَمَنْ بَاءَ بِسَخَطٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَمَأْوَاهُ جَهَنَّمُ وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

a-fa-mani ittabaʿa riḍwāna Allāhi ka-man bāʾa bi-sakhaṭin mina Allāhi wa-maʾwāhu jahannamu wa-biʾsa al-maṣīr

Āl ʿImrān 3:162

Translation:

“Is one who follows the pleasure of The God like one who returns bearing anger from The God, whose dwelling is Hell? What an evil destination!”

Bi-sakhaṭin combines the verbal noun with bi.

Mina Allāhi identifies The God as the source of displeasure.

It contrasts with riḍwāna Allāhi.

The verse compares two different conditions.

One is following divine pleasure.

The other is incurring divine displeasure.

The interrogative construction emphasizes their inequality.

Displeasure and human conduct

Āl ʿImrān 3:161 discusses betrayal of trust and wrongful appropriation.

It also states that every self will receive full recompense for what it earned, without injustice.

Āl ʿImrān 3:162 then contrasts pleasure and displeasure.

The connection relates divine judgment to conduct and responsibility.

It does not restrict divine displeasure exclusively to financial betrayal.

The verse presents a broader contrast between following divine pleasure and incurring displeasure.

What does bāʾa bi-sakhaṭin mean?

Bāʾa here expresses returning with a result or consequence.

Bāʾa bi-sakhaṭin therefore does not merely describe the person's own dissatisfaction.

The person confronts or incurs displeasure from The God as a result of conduct.

This distinction matters.

In Al-Tawbah 9:58, people themselves become displeased.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:162, divine displeasure is directed at a person.

The direction of the relationship changes while the semantic field remains displeasure and anger.

Al-Māʾidah 5:80: divine displeasure over a specified group's conduct

تَرَىٰ كَثِيرًا مِنْهُمْ يَتَوَلَّوْنَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَبِئْسَ مَا قَدَّمَتْ لَهُمْ أَنْفُسُهُمْ أَنْ سَخِطَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَفِي الْعَذَابِ هُمْ خَالِدُونَ

tarā kathīran minhum yatawallawna alladhīna kafarū la-biʾsa mā qaddamat lahum anfusuhum an sakhiṭa Allāhu ʿalayhim wa-fī al-ʿadhābi hum khālidūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:80

Translation:

“You see many of them taking those who disbelieved as patrons. Evil indeed is what their own selves have sent ahead for them: that The God is angry with them, and they remain in punishment.”

Sakhiṭa is a Form I perfect verb.

The God is its subject.

ʿAlayhim identifies those at whom the displeasure is directed.

Who is being censured?

The verse begins with kathīran minhum.

This means “many of them.”

The preceding verses refer to Israelites who disbelieved and were censured for disobedience and transgression.

Al-Māʾidah 5:79 also describes their failure to restrain one another from wrongdoing.

Al-Māʾidah 5:80 then discusses a group's relationship with those who disbelieved.

Divine displeasure is thus presented amid specific conduct, disobedience and wrongful relationships.

This should not be generalized without evidence to every member of an ethnic community or every present-day adherent of a religion.

“Many of them” and the specified conduct must remain part of interpretation and application.

Yatawallawna here concerns patronage and allegiance. Without examining other verses, it does not establish a ban on all human contact, equitable cooperation or association with someone of different beliefs.

Displeasure and punishment

The verse ends:

وَفِي الْعَذَابِ هُمْ خَالِدُونَ

wa-fī al-ʿadhābi hum khālidūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:80

And they remain in punishment.

This states punishment and remaining in it.

The consequence occurs in the context of divine displeasure.

The lexical meaning of sakhaṭ, however, is not simply punishment itself.

It expresses divine displeasure and anger.

Punishment is a consequence stated separately.

Directly translating sakhaṭ as punishment would erase that distinction.

Muḥammad 47:28: following what displeases The God

ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمُ اتَّبَعُوا مَا أَسْخَطَ اللَّهَ وَكَرِهُوا رِضْوَانَهُ فَأَحْبَطَ أَعْمَالَهُمْ

dhālika bi-annahumu ittabaʿū mā askhaṭa Allāha wa-karihū riḍwānahu fa-aḥbaṭa aʿmālahum

Muḥammad 47:28

Translation:

“That is because they followed what displeased The God and disliked His pleasure; so He rendered their deeds fruitless.”

Askhaṭa is Form IV.

Its subject is an implicit pronoun referring back to mā, that which causes displeasure; Allāha is the object.

This does not mean that The God undergoes a human psychological change caused by an unknown, unforeseen event.

The verse concerns conduct incompatible with divine approval, which The God disapproves of.

Context and awareness of guidance

Muḥammad 47:25 describes people turning back after guidance became clear to them.

Muḥammad 47:26 describes their promising obedience in some matters to those who disliked what The God revealed.

Muḥammad 47:28 explains the outcome through ittabaʿū mā askhaṭa Allāha.

Displeasure here concerns following what opposes divine approval.

The statement that guidance had become clear in Muḥammad 47:25 is also important.

This should not automatically be equated with unwitting error, sincere questioning or interpretive disagreement when the truth is not yet clear to someone.

Askhaṭa and sakhiṭa

In Al-Māʾidah 5:80:

سَخِطَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ

sakhiṭa Allāhu ʿalayhim

Al-Māʾidah 5:80

The God became angry with them.

The God is the subject of being displeased.

In Muḥammad 47:28:

مَا أَسْخَطَ اللَّهَ

mā askhaṭa Allāha

Muḥammad 47:28

What displeased The God.

The subject under discussion is what causes divine displeasure.

One verse directly attributes displeasure to The God.

The other identifies conduct or something that causes it.

This distinction follows from verb structure and syntactic roles.

Wa-karihū riḍwānahu

The verse follows askhaṭa Allāha with wa-karihū riḍwānahu.

This presents two aspects of the issue.

They followed what displeased The God.

Conversely, they disliked and turned away from His approval.

The verse thus describes an orientation and pattern of following in opposition to divine pleasure, rather than merely an isolated lapse.

It separately expresses the nullification of deeds through aḥbaṭa.

Askhaṭa therefore does not mean rendering deeds fruitless.

That is the separately stated result.

5. Human and divine displeasure: shared meaning, different subjects

The Qur’an applies the verb to both human beings and The God.

Displeasure is present in both usages.

But not every feature of human reaction should be attributed to The God.

Human displeasure

A person may be displeased when a desire is not met.

Al-Tawbah 9:58 relates this to receiving or not receiving alms.

Self-interest, however, is not always its cause.

Someone may be displeased by injustice, betrayal or the violation of another's rights.

Such displeasure can be morally defensible.

Sakhaṭ is therefore not intrinsically a name for sin.

Divine displeasure

The Qur’an connects divine displeasure to human conduct and orientation.

Āl ʿImrān 3:162 contrasts it with riḍwān.

Al-Māʾidah 5:80 links it with a specified group's blameworthy conduct.

Muḥammad 47:28 contrasts following what displeases The God with His pleasure.

These usages place divine displeasure within judgment and the evaluation of conduct.

They do not establish uncontrolled anger, inattention, surprise or psychological need in The God.

Nor should the expression be interpreted in a way that erases its connection with the judgment and consequences stated in the verses.

The lexical study establishes that connection, while the nature of divine attributes requires a separate theological discussion.

Does an action's occurrence establish divine approval?

No. An action occurring and The God's moral approval of it are different issues.

People can do what displeases The God.

Muḥammad 47:28 illustrates this: a group follows what causes divine displeasure.

The possibility of performing an action does not make it divinely approved.

Nor are outward success, power, wealth or advantage sufficient criteria of approval.

Conduct must be assessed against clear Qur’anic criteria, rather than material or social results alone.

6. Distinctions from nearby roots

S KH Ṭ (sakhaṭ) and R Ḍ W (riḍā)

These families clearly contrast in the Qur’an.

Riḍā concerns pleasure, approval and acceptance.

Sakhaṭ concerns displeasure and disapproval.

Al-Tawbah 9:58 applies both to people's reactions to alms distribution.

Āl ʿImrān 3:162 contrasts riḍwān Allāh with sakhaṭ min Allāh.

Muḥammad 47:28 pairs following what displeases The God with disliking His pleasure.

They express contrasting directions of evaluation.

Human pleasure does not always indicate rightness, nor displeasure wrongness.

Someone can welcome an unjust decision or dislike a just one.

Divine pleasure and displeasure, by contrast, occur in the context of divine judgment about conduct and responsibility.

The study of R Ḍ W examines this contrast more fully.

Study of R Ḍ W

S KH Ṭ (sakhaṭ) and GH Ḍ B (ghaḍab)

Ghaḍab concerns anger and response to something unwelcome or disapproved.

Sakhaṭ can likewise involve anger, while its clear uses emphasize displeasure and disapproval.

Arabic lexicons sometimes explain them through one another.

No absolute boundary makes sakhaṭ always calm anger and ghaḍab always intense or explosive anger.

Al-Tawbah 9:58 contrasts sakhaṭ with approval in response to not receiving alms.

Maghḍūbi ʿalayhim in Al-Fātiḥah 1:7 denotes those subjected to anger.

The object and context of anger matter in both families.

Divine anger or displeasure should not be defined solely through human bodily feelings.

S KH Ṭ (sakhaṭ) and K R H (karāhah)

K R H concerns disliking or disapproving of something.

Sakhaṭ belongs to a related field.

Muḥammad 47:28 includes both:

مَا أَسْخَطَ اللَّهَ

mā askhaṭa Allāha

Muḥammad 47:28

What displeased The God.

وَكَرِهُوا رِضْوَانَهُ

wa-karihū riḍwānahu

Muḥammad 47:28

And they disliked His pleasure.

The first concerns what causes divine displeasure.

The second concerns people's dislike of His pleasure.

The direction of the relationship and the subjects differ.

That distinction arises from syntax, not a universal rule that karāhah is milder and sakhaṭ stronger.

S KH Ṭ (sakhaṭ) and GH Y Ẓ (ghayẓ)

Ghayẓ concerns anger and the resulting inner state in Arabic usage.

Sakhaṭ can also be displeasure accompanied by anger.

They are nevertheless not interchangeable in every usage.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 mentions restraining ghayẓ.

Al-Tawbah 9:58 mentions sakhaṭ in response to alms distribution.

A displeased person may restrain inner anger.

Their displeasure may also lead to overt objection.

These examples do not make ghayẓ invariably hidden and sakhaṭ invariably overt.

Precise distinctions depend on each construction and context.

S KH Ṭ (sakhaṭ) and N Q M (naqm)

N Q M concerns displeasure, finding fault and, in some usages, punishment or retribution.

Sakhaṭ also concerns displeasure.

Displeasure can sometimes lead to a reaction or punishment.

But sakhaṭ itself does not mean administering punishment.

Al-Māʾidah 5:80, for example, mentions both divine displeasure and punishment through separate expressions.

N Q M likewise requires distinguishing disapproval from punishment.

Their overlap does not establish that every sakhaṭ is revenge.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Choosing between “displeasure” and “anger” depends on context.

“Displeasure” makes the contrast with riḍā clearer.

“Anger” is also a natural and defensible equivalent in many usages, especially strong reactions or divine sakhaṭ.

Preserving the root's meaning does not require an unwieldy combination of synonyms in the main translation.

Important meanings in the family

Sakhiṭa: became displeased, disapproved, became angry.

Yaskhaṭu: becomes displeased or angry.

Sakhaṭ and sukhṭ: displeasure, dissatisfaction, anger.

Sākhiṭ: displeased, angry.

Maskhūṭ: subjected to anger or disapproval.

Askhaṭa: caused displeasure or anger.

Iskhāṭ: causing displeasure or anger.

Tasakhkhaṭa: became displeased, disapproved or showed dissatisfaction.

Maskhaṭah: something causing displeasure or anger.

Proposed renderings of Qur’anic forms

In Āl ʿImrān 3:162:

كَمَنْ بَاءَ بِسَخَطٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ

ka-man bāʾa bi-sakhaṭin mina Allāhi

Āl ʿImrān 3:162

“Like one who returns bearing anger from The God.”

In Al-Māʾidah 5:80:

أَنْ سَخِطَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ

an sakhiṭa Allāhu ʿalayhim

Al-Māʾidah 5:80

“That The God became angry with them.”

In Al-Tawbah 9:58:

إِذَا هُمْ يَسْخَطُونَ

idhā hum yaskhaṭūn

Al-Tawbah 9:58

“Suddenly they are angry.”

In Muḥammad 47:28:

اتَّبَعُوا مَا أَسْخَطَ اللَّهَ

ittabaʿū mā askhaṭa Allāha

Muḥammad 47:28

“They followed what displeased The God.”

“Anger” is natural and defensible in the first three examples.

“Displeased” in Muḥammad 47:28 conveys Form IV's causative relationship.

In every case, distinguish sakhaṭ from consequences such as punishment, nullification of deeds or leaving something.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meaning

For the brief root box:

8. Conclusion

S KH Ṭ concerns displeasure, dissatisfaction, disapproval and anger.

The Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm contrasts sakhiṭnā with raḍīnā.

The example attests that contrast in poetry attributed to pre-Qur’anic Arabic.

Displeasure there accompanies leaving something, but leaving is not the verb's own meaning.

Lexicons record the family for disapproving of conduct, disliking a gift, becoming angry and causing another's displeasure.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an.

Al-Tawbah 9:58 applies it to people pleased with alms distribution only when they receive a share.

This illustrates displeasure governed by self-interest.

The root itself does not classify every instance as right or wrong.

Āl ʿImrān 3:162 directly contrasts divine displeasure with His pleasure.

Al-Māʾidah 5:80 relates it to a particular group's blameworthy conduct.

Askhaṭa in Muḥammad 47:28 occurs in the description of following what displeases The God.

These usages connect divine displeasure with conduct and responsibility.

It should not be turned into bodily anger, an uncontrolled reaction or a human psychological need.

Nor is displeasure identical to punishment: the verses state consequences separately.

An action occurring does not establish divine approval. People can act in ways that displease The God.

The contrast with riḍā is fundamental to this study.

For human beings, however, pleasure does not always indicate rightness, nor displeasure wrongness.

Someone may dislike injustice and welcome justice—or dislike justice because of self-interest.

Divine displeasure and approval occur in the context of judging truth, conduct and human responsibility.

S KH Ṭ denotes displeasure and disapproval toward a person, conduct or situation not accepted or approved. It can accompany anger and objection, but objection, leaving, revenge and punishment are not its direct meaning.

“Displeasure” and “anger” are the family's two principal defensible equivalents for Forqan.

Each verse requires identifying who is displeased, the object of displeasure and its cause.

These distinctions preserve the meaning and prevent conflating human dissatisfaction, divine anger, its cause and its consequences.
