Level ground, gentleness, reduced difficulty, and making things easy

The Arabic root S H L concerns levelness, softness, and the absence of roughness or difficulty. In physical usage, sahl denotes ground contrasted with hard, rocky, difficult terrain. Related uses describe easy tasks, a gentle disposition, mild conduct, and making conditions favorable for an activity.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as suhūlihā in Al-Aʿrāf 7:74. The verse concerns Thamūd, who built palaces and buildings on the earth’s plains and carved homes from mountains.

Here suhūl is the plural of sahl, meaning plains or level ground. It does not denote moral ease, comfortable living, or an easier obligation; it identifies terrain in contrast with mountains.

The shared semantic feature is softness and levelness contrasted with hardness and roughness. It can concern terrain, a material’s quality, a task’s difficulty, or human conduct. Nevertheless, geographical plains and gentleness of character remain distinct senses that require their own contexts.

1. Early Arabic usage and poetic evidence

Sahl: level ground contrasted with rough terrain

In early Arabic, al-sahl denotes relatively level, accessible ground contrasted with al-ḥazn.

Ḥazn here means hard, uneven, difficult terrain, which may contain rocks, rugged elevations, and challenging routes.

The contrast between sahl and ḥazn thus primarily concerns terrain and movement across it.

Sahl need not be perfectly flat, devoid of elevations, stones, or vegetation. The contrast concerns relative levelness and fewer obstacles to passage compared with ḥazn.

Arabic uses arḍ sahlah for level ground and arḍ ḥaznah for rough, difficult ground.

Sahula al-mawḍiʿ means that a place became level or less difficult.

As-hala al-qawm describes people entering level ground or descending from rugged highlands to a plain.

These uses concern physical terrain and movement, rather than the ease of an intellectual or moral problem.

Poetry attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

بِأَوْدِيَةٍ أَسَافِلُهُنَّ رَوْضٌ
وَأَعْلَاهَا إِذَا خِفْنَا حُصُونُ

bi-awdiyatin asāfiluhunna rawḍun / wa-aʿlāhā idhā khifnā ḥuṣūnu

“In valleys whose lower reaches are meadows, and whose heights become our strongholds when we fear danger.”

The following line says:

نَحُلُّ سُهُولَهَا فَإِذَا فَزِعْنَا
جَرَى مِنْهُنَّ بِالْآصَالِ عُونُ

naḥullu suhūlahā fa-idhā faziʿnā / jarā minhunna bi-al-āṣāli ʿūnu

“We settle on their plains; when we respond to a call for aid, mature horses run forth from them in the evenings.”

Suhūlahā here denotes the level parts of the territory occupied by the poet’s people.

The first line distinguishes the lower valleys from heights used as refuges in danger. The next describes settling on the plains of that same region.

This is especially useful evidence because suhūlahā has the same plural noun and attached pronoun as the Quranic suhūlihā; their case endings differ with their grammatical positions.

Here suhūl means level parts of the land, rather than easy living, gentle character, or financial prosperity.

The poem appears in the transmitted collection attributed to Zuhayr and in early literary sources. Versions differ in some wording and explanations of the second line, but the geographical sense of suhūlahā is clear.

Its attribution to Zuhayr offers evidence for usage assigned to pre-Islamic Arabic, although the precise dating of an individual line cannot be detached from the collection’s transmission history.

The geographical sense of sahl and its plural suhūl was thus known in early Arabic; the Quranic usage belongs to the same field.

Sahl contrasted with difficulty in conduct and action

Another poem by Zuhayr says:

فَأَصْبَحْتُمَا مِنْهَا عَلَى خَيْرِ مَوْطِنٍ
سَبِيلُكُمَا فِيهِ وَإِنْ أَحْزَنُوا سَهْلُ

fa-aṣbaḥtumā minhā ʿalā khayri mawṭinin / sabīlukumā fīhi wa-in aḥzanū sahlu

“You two have therefore reached an excellent position in this affair; your path in it is easy, even though others take a rough one.”

The line praises two men who worked to end conflict and bloodshed between groups.

Sahl no longer simply names level terrain. It describes a course made accessible to the two men despite the difficulties surrounding others.

Aḥzanū belongs to Ḥ Z N, associated with entering rough ground or adopting a difficult course; this image is contrasted with their easy path.

The physical opposition between sahl and ḥazn is thus also used to describe human conduct and circumstances.

This illustrates an early connection between level terrain and an easy course of action.

The connection does not make every psychological or social difficulty literally rough ground. Terrain supplies an image; context identifies the actual human situation.

Gentle character and agreeable companionship

Poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays also says:

وَأَخِي إِخَاءٍ ذِي مُحَافَظَةٍ
سَهْلِ الْخَلِيقَةِ مَاجِدِ الْأَصْلِ

wa-akhi ikhāʾin dhī muḥāfaẓatin / sahli al-khalīqati mājidi al-aṣli

“Many a loyal companion, protective of friendship, gentle in disposition and noble in origin.”

Sahl al-khalīqah describes someone whose disposition and conduct are mild and agreeable.

This differs from level ground, although the semantic connection is clear.

Just as level terrain presents fewer difficulties than rugged ground, companionship with a gentle person involves fewer harsh or hurtful encounters.

Sahl here does not mean weak, incapable, or lacking resolve. Someone may be gentle while remaining firm in matters of truth, justice, and decision.

This line is transmitted among poems attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays in the commentary tradition on pre-Islamic poetry. Its attribution and date should be accepted only to the extent supported; inclusion in such a collection does not settle every historical question.

These examples show that early S H L usage encompassed both level ground and an easy course or gentle conduct.

2. Semantic core and important derivatives

Lexical accounts explain S H L through softness and reduced harshness or roughness.

This connects geographical usage with many figurative applications.

Important forms include:

Sahl: level ground; more broadly, something easy or relatively undemanding.

Suhūl: the plural of sahl, meaning plains or level tracts.

Suhūlah: levelness, softness, and ease.

Sahula: became level, soft, or easy.

Sahhala: made level or easy; reduced the difficulty of doing something.

Tashīl: facilitating and creating less difficult conditions.

As-hala: entered level ground or descended to a plain; other uses concern gentler treatment and the easing of bodily discharge.

Tasāhala: acted leniently or approached something less strictly.

Istashala: considered something easy.

Sahl al-khuluq: gentle and agreeable in character.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but not all occur in the Quran.

Only suhūlihā appears in the Quran.

Sahl as levelness and ease

Human experience makes the connection between levelness and ease understandable.

Travel along a level route generally involves fewer obstacles than travel over rough, rocky ground.

The same word can therefore describe a task involving less complexity or difficulty.

For example, amr sahl means an easy task.

Sahhala al-amr means that someone made a task easier.

The subject is now an activity with fewer impediments, rather than land or a road.

This resemblance does not establish that every moral or intellectual use of sahl arose from the terrain sense at a particular historical moment.

Early evidence demonstrates related meanings; their precise chronological development requires separate historical inquiry.

Sihlah: sediment and soil material

Al-sihlah is recorded in lexicons for a kind of soil or sand.

Some explanations describe sand-like sediment brought and deposited by water.

Others describe sand coarser than very fine sand.

The family thus includes names of an earth material as well as descriptions of level terrain.

Al-sihlah as soil or sand is not the same usage as al-sahl, a plain.

Nor does this imply that every sahl tract consists of sand and waterborne deposits.

Soil composition is distinct from the levelness of its surface.

Ease and leniency

Tasāhul concerns mild treatment, leniency, and sometimes forbearance.

It can be commendable when someone avoids needless strictness or making a legitimate activity unnecessarily difficult.

In another context, however, it can denote negligence or insufficient care.

Leniency is therefore not invariably virtuous, nor strictness invariably objectionable.

The value of conduct depends on its object.

Facilitating access to rights and justice is beneficial, whereas neglect of others’ rights, integrity, or professional duties can be harmful.

This distinction belongs to social application and should not be absorbed into the lexical definition of sahl.

3. The Quranic form suhūlihā

S H L occurs only once in the Quran:

Suhūlihā — Al-Aʿrāf 7:74

The form consists of suhūl, a broken plural, and the attached pronoun hā.

Suhūl is the plural of sahl, meaning plains or level tracts.

The pronoun hā refers back to al-arḍ, the earth, earlier in the verse.

Suhūlihā therefore means “its plains” or “its level tracts.”

It follows the preposition min and is connected with quṣūran in the sentence.

The phrase reads:

تَتَّخِذُونَ مِنْ سُهُولِهَا قُصُورًا

tattakhidhūna min suhūlihā quṣūran

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74

“You build palaces on its plains.”

Here sahl describes land used for construction, rather than easy work or gentle character.

Suhūl and suhūlah

Suhūl and suhūlah must be distinguished.

Suhūl is the plural noun and denotes level tracts in the Quran.

Suhūlah is a verbal noun expressing levelness, softness, or ease.

Their similarity in form does not make their grammatical functions identical.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74 concerns actual plains, not the abstract idea of ease.

“You build palaces from the earth’s eases” would therefore be an incorrect translation.

Suhūl itself also does not mean cultivated, fertile, or well-watered land. Some plains may have those qualities, but levelness and fertility differ.

4. Building palaces on the plains: Thamūd’s settlement

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74 says:

وَاذْكُرُوا إِذْ جَعَلَكُمْ خُلَفَاءَ مِنْ بَعْدِ عَادٍ وَبَوَّأَكُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ تَتَّخِذُونَ مِنْ سُهُولِهَا قُصُورًا وَتَنْحِتُونَ الْجِبَالَ بُيُوتًا فَاذْكُرُوا آلَاءَ اللَّهِ وَلَا تَعْثَوْا فِي الْأَرْضِ مُفْسِدِينَ

wa-udhkurū idh jaʿalakum khulafāʾa min baʿdi ʿādin wa-bawwaʾakum fī al-arḍi tattakhidhūna min suhūlihā quṣūran wa-tanḥitūna al-jibāla buyūtan fa-udhkurū ālāʾa Allāhi wa-lā taʿthaw fī al-arḍi mufsidīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74

“Remember when He made you successors after Aad and settled you on the earth. You build palaces on its plains and carve the mountains into homes. Remember the favors of The God, and do not act wickedly on the earth, spreading corruption.”

The verse is part of Ṣāliḥ’s address to Thamūd.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:73, Ṣāliḥ calls his people to worship The God and attend to the sign brought to them.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74 recalls their historical position and resources: succeeding ʿĀd, settling on the earth, building on plains, and carving homes from mountains.

Suhūlihā forms part of this description of Thamūd’s land resources and capacity for settlement.

Plains contrasted with mountains

One sentence mentions two kinds of terrain and two building methods:

Min suhūlihā quṣūran concerns constructing palaces and buildings on plains.

Wa-tanḥitūna al-jibāla buyūtan concerns carving homes from mountains.

The contrast clarifies the meaning of suhūl.

A plain is relatively level land on which buildings can be erected or whose resources support settlement.

A mountain is elevated, hard terrain where carving a home requires different work and skills.

The verse presents two uses of the natural environment: erecting buildings on level ground and carving living spaces into mountainsides.

This does not establish that every plains building was made of earth or every mountain home exclusively of stone. The verse does not specify the precise materials of the plains buildings.

Nor does the phrase establish that plains buildings were always used in one season and mountain homes in another.

Those are possibilities concerning living arrangements that require independent historical evidence.

The function of min suhūlihā

The preposition min connects the construction with the earth’s plains.

Depending on sentence structure, min can express source, part of a whole, or a starting point.

Here min suhūlihā relates level land to the construction of buildings.

Persian can naturally say “in its plains,” whereas “from its plains” follows the Arabic preposition more closely. English “on its plains” conveys the locative reading naturally.

Explaining min solely as the source of building materials would select one interpretation that requires contextual support.

The phrase alone does not establish the precise nature of those materials.

It describes Thamūd using the plains for palaces, rather than specifying a chemical composition or exact construction material.

Does quṣūr mean ordinary houses?

Quṣūr is the plural of qaṣr.

In Arabic, qaṣr can denote a large, substantial, imposing building.

The distinction between quṣūr and buyūt in this verse also deserves attention.

Palaces on the plains are contrasted with homes carved from mountains.

This alone does not establish that mountain homes were small and poor or that every plains building belonged to rulers and aristocrats.

The words and independent historical evidence must establish a building’s precise type, size, and function.

Suhūl does not itself mean palaces. It identifies the terrain associated with their construction.

5. Level ground, construction, and human responsibility

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:74, the reference to plains and mountains accompanies an ethical command:

فَاذْكُرُوا آلَاءَ اللَّهِ وَلَا تَعْثَوْا فِي الْأَرْضِ مُفْسِدِينَ

fa-udhkurū ālāʾa Allāhi wa-lā taʿthaw fī al-arḍi mufsidīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74

“Remember the favors of The God, and do not act wickedly on the earth, spreading corruption.”

This connects construction skills and access to land resources with responsibility before The God.

Thamūd used its natural surroundings for building. That demonstrated resources and skill, but did not by itself establish righteous conduct or moral superiority.

The verse recalls building palaces and homes as a capacity and benefit. Its prohibition concerns corruption on earth.

The juxtaposition does not make every palace, large building, or architectural skill inherently corrupt.

Wealth and construction skills likewise do not license oppression, humiliation, or destruction of others’ rights.

The people are called to recognize their benefits and resources without using them for corruption.

Land use and social conduct

The Quran connects settlement and the use of earthly resources with human responsibility.

In Hūd 11:61, Ṣāliḥ reminds his people that The God brought them forth from the earth and established them in it; the phrase has also been understood as calling them to cultivate it.

That passage also calls for return and reform.

It relates to Al-Aʿrāf 7:74 because both belong to Ṣāliḥ’s address in the story of Thamūd.

Nevertheless, istaʿmarakum belongs to ʿ M R, whereas suhūl belongs to S H L. The concepts should remain distinct.

Suhūl identifies terrain; istiʿmār in the settlement sense concerns inhabiting, using, and developing land.

Together, these verses support examining responsible land use and avoidance of corruption without inserting cultivation into the lexical meaning of sahl.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 also mentions carving homes from mountains.

Al-Fajr 89:9 describes Thamūd as hewing rock in the valley.

These verses illuminate the mountainous aspect of their settlement, but do not repeat suhūl.

The root’s sole Quranic occurrence remains Al-Aʿrāf 7:74. The other verses supply context for Thamūd’s settlement and building practices.

Does easier terrain always mean easier living?

A level plain may be more suitable for construction or travel than rocky terrain.

This geographical advantage does not make life on a plain invariably easier than mountain life.

Plains can face water shortages, heat, floods, drought, or other difficulties. Mountains can offer particular advantages under some conditions.

Geographical sahl therefore denotes relative levelness, rather than ease in every aspect of life.

This prevents an unsupported transfer of figurative ease into the direct Quranic usage.

6. Sahl in gentle conduct and easy tasks

Arabic extends sahl beyond terrain to tasks, speech, disposition, and behavior.

Amr sahl is a task with relatively little difficulty.

Kalām sahl can describe fluent, understandable speech.

Rajul sahl al-khuluq is a gentle, agreeable man.

Sahhala lahu al-amr means making a task easier for someone.

These uses are semantically related to levelness and softness.

A fundamental distinction nevertheless remains.

For terrain, levelness is a physical, spatial quality.

For a task, ease concerns fewer obstacles to performance.

For conduct, ease can concern gentleness, agreeable behavior, and avoiding needless strictness.

One fixed translation should therefore not be imposed on every usage.

Ease is not negligence

Modern Persian and Arabic distinguish facilitation, leniency, and negligence.

Facilitation can mean providing conditions for accomplishing a task.

Leniency may describe flexibility and mild treatment.

Negligence usually means failing to exercise the attention and seriousness a responsibility requires.

The meanings are related but distinct.

Making a service easier to access differs from neglecting necessary health and safety standards.

Gentleness likewise does not mean abandoning rights and justice.

Sahl has no fixed ethical value in its general sense. The object and effects of leniency determine its value.

Ease and human capacities

A task described as easy may be easy for one person and difficult for another.

Physical ability, knowledge, experience, tools, and circumstances can affect its difficulty.

Ease is thus not always an absolute quality identical for everyone.

This matters for studying ease in the Quran, but many such concepts are expressed through Y S R rather than S H L.

Semantic proximity does not transfer every Quranic implication of yusr to sahl.

7. Comparison with related roots

S H L (sahl) and Ḥ Z N (ḥazn): level and difficult ground

These roots provide a clear Arabic opposition concerning terrain.

Sahl denotes level, relatively accessible ground.

Physical ḥazn denotes hard, uneven ground.

The Quranic family Ḥ Z N also concerns sorrow and heaviness of heart.

Geographical and psychological usage must therefore be distinguished.

The image of rough terrain can help explain psychological difficulty, without making grief and rocky ground the same reality.

Conversely, sahl can describe ease in conduct or work without literally referring to terrain.

Examining Ḥ Z N separately helps preserve the distinction between rough terrain and grief.

S H L (sahl) and Y S R (yusr): levelness and ease

Quranic Y S R concerns ease, relief, and favorable conditions for doing something.

Arabic S H L can likewise express ease or reduced difficulty.

The physical field of sahl concerns levelness and softness, whereas Quranic yusr directly expresses ease, relief, and the absence of difficulty.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:185 says:

يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ

yurīdu Allāhu bikumu al-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu al-ʿusr

Al-Baqarah 2:185

“The God desires ease for you and does not desire hardship for you.”

Here yusr contrasts with ʿusr and concerns ease in an obligation.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:74, however, suhūl contrasts with mountains and denotes level tracts.

The roots therefore do not perform identical functions in the Quran despite their related meanings.

A broader study of ease should examine Y S R on its own evidence.

S H L (sahl) and ʿ S R (ʿusr): ease and difficulty

ʿUsr concerns hardship, constraint, and difficult tasks.

In general Arabic usage, sahl can contrast with difficult or ʿasīr.

In the Quran, however, the familiar opposition is mainly expressed through yusr and ʿusr.

The Arabic sense “easy” does not make every Quranic yusr equivalent to sahl, or every ʿusr equivalent to rough terrain.

Semantic similarities support comparison, but actual distinctions in usage must remain clear.

S H L (sahl) and S Ṭ Ḥ (saṭḥ): levelness and spreading a surface

Saṭḥ concerns spreading and leveling a surface.

Sahl can describe relatively level, accessible ground.

Their descriptions of terrain overlap without being identical.

Saṭḥ can emphasize spreading or the surface itself; sahl emphasizes relative levelness and freedom from difficult roughness.

In Al-Ghāshiyah 88:20, suṭiḥat is used of the earth.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:74, suhūl names the plains.

Calling terrain sahl thus does not entail absolute flatness or the absence of every elevation and depression.

S H L (sahl) and J B L (jabal): plains and mountains

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74 places suhūl and jibāl in one sentence.

Jabal denotes a mountain or elevated landform.

Geographical sahl identifies more level, accessible terrain.

Both can occur in one region and support human settlement.

The contrast does not make every plain low-lying or every mountain uninhabitable.

The verse itself shows Thamūd building on plains and carving homes from mountains.

The meaning of mountains should be examined independently within J B L.

S H L (sahl) and B S Ṭ (basṭ): levelness and extent

Basṭ concerns spreading and extension.

Land can be extensive and level, but extent alone does not establish levelness.

A mountainous region can also be very extensive.

Conversely, a small plot may be level without covering a large area.

The descriptions can overlap while emphasizing different features: extent and levelness.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The sole Quranic form is suhūlihā in Al-Aʿrāf 7:74.

The Arabic phrase:

تَتَّخِذُونَ مِنْ سُهُولِهَا قُصُورًا

tattakhidhūna min suhūlihā quṣūran

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74

A suitable translation:

“You build palaces on its plains.”

This conveys the Arabic construction in natural English and agrees with Forqan’s current translation.

“You build palaces on its level tracts” also makes the sense clear but is longer than “its plains.”

A term suggesting only lowlands beside a river or sea may be too restrictive. “Plains” better conveys the general geographical sense of sahl.

“You build palaces from its easy lands” is unnatural, since the description concerns level ground rather than an easy task.

“Fertile,” “green,” or “well-watered” should not be added without independent evidence.

Suggested word information for Forqan:

Word in the verse: سُهُولِهَا

Root: S H L

Brief root meaning: Levelness, softness, and reduced roughness or difficulty; for terrain, plains and level ground.

Meaning in this verse: The earth’s plains, where Thamūd erected palaces and buildings.

Important forms in this word family

Sahl: level; more broadly, easy.

Suhūl: plains and level tracts.

Sahula: became level or easy.

Suhūlah: levelness, softness, and ease.

Sahhala: made easy or level.

Tashīl: facilitating and reducing difficulty.

As-hala: descended to a plain or entered level ground.

Tasāhala: acted leniently or flexibly.

Istashala: considered something easy.

Sahl al-khuluq: gentle and agreeable in character.

Al-sihlah: a lexical term for soil or sand deposited by water.

These forms illustrate the family, but only the plural suhūlihā occurs in the Quran.

Conclusion

Arabic S H L concerns levelness, softness, and the absence of difficulty or roughness.

Physically, it describes level ground contrasted with hard, uneven terrain.

Poetry attributed to Zuhayr uses suhūl for inhabited plains and sahl in contrast with a difficult course. Poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays also uses the family for gentle character and agreeable conduct.

Early Arabic therefore attests both geographical usage and extensions to easier action and human conduct.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as suhūlihā in Al-Aʿrāf 7:74.

That verse concerns Thamūd building palaces on the earth’s plains and carving homes from mountains.

Here suhūl denotes plains and level tracts, rather than moral ease, gentleness, or a life without difficulty.

The verse distinguishes natural resources, construction skills, and human responsibility. Building on plains or carving mountain homes demonstrates use of earthly resources; ethical value depends on how those resources are used, rights respected, and corruption avoided.

The resulting definition is:

S H L denotes levelness, softness, and reduced roughness or difficulty. Physical sahl means level ground contrasted with rugged terrain; broader uses concern easy tasks, gentle character, and facilitation. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:74, suhūlihā denotes the plains and level tracts used by Thamūd to build palaces.

This preserves the physical and historical sense while explaining broader usage without conflating it with the direct Quranic meaning.

Level terrain, an easy task, and a gentle disposition belong to one word family but are not one identical concept. Word form, sentence subject, and context determine the meaning in each occurrence.
