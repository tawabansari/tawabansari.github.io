1. Introducing the Root

The three-consonant Arabic root J M Ḥ concerns rapid movement and forceful forward motion that is not easily checked. Its family includes jamaḥa, “rushed” or “surged forward”; jimāḥ and jumūḥ for this movement; and jāmiḥ and jamūḥ for a person or animal rushing forward or escaping restraint.

One particularly clear use concerns horses. A horse that surges forward powerfully and cannot easily be stopped by its rider is called farasun jamūḥun.

Early Arabic does not always consider a horse’s jumūḥ a defect, however. Lexicographers distinguish a horse disobeying its rider from one running swiftly through abundant vigor and energy.

The first can involve unruliness and difficulty of control; the second expresses speed, spirit, and strong movement.

J M Ḥ therefore does not itself name sin, moral rebellion, or objectionable conduct. Positive or negative evaluation comes from circumstances and context.

The family also describes humans. Someone rushing insistently toward an objective and proving difficult to stop can be described by jamaḥa. A person following personal desire without restraint may likewise be jāmiḥ or jamūḥ.

Another use concerns a ship departing from its sailors’ intended course and becoming difficult to control.

These share forceful movement or advance beyond ordinary speed or controllability.

They should nevertheless not become one identical event. A horse’s swift movement, a person’s flight, and a ship going off course differ in cause, choice, and consequence.

The Qur’an uses J M Ḥ only once, as yajmaḥūna in Al-Tawbah 9:57:

Quranic Arabic Corpus:the sole occurrence in Al-Tawbah9:57

لَوْ يَجِدُونَ مَلْجَأً أَوْ مَغَارَاتٍ أَوْ مُدَّخَلًا لَوَلَّوْا إِلَيْهِ وَهُمْ يَجْمَحُونَ

law yajidūna maljaʾan aw maghārātin aw muddakhalan la-wallaw ilayhi wa-hum yajmaḥūna

The verse concerns people who would rush toward a refuge, caves, or an entrance to a hiding place if they found one.

The context concerns fear and an intense desire for escape.

Yajmaḥūna itself does not mean fearing, however. It describes their movement: intense haste and forward momentum seemingly difficult for an obstacle to stop.

Understanding the root therefore requires distinguishing speed, unruliness, flight, and fear.

Speed and surging forward belong to the family’s principal meanings. Unruliness can occur in some uses. Flight is the contextual movement in Al-Tawbah 9:57, while fear comes from the preceding verse.

These four concepts are related but not complete synonyms. Brief root meaning: Forceful forward movement and haste difficult to check; in horses, either praised speed or unruliness.

2. The Semantic Core in Early Arabic

Advancing with Overpowering Force

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْجِيمُ وَالْمِيمُ وَالْحَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ مُطَّرِدٌ، وَهُوَ ذَهَابُ الشَّيْءِ قُدُمًا بِغَلَبَةٍ وَقُوَّةٍ.

al-jīmu wa-al-mīmu wa-al-ḥāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun muṭṭaridun wa-huwa dhahābu al-shayʾi quduman bi-ghalabatin wa-quwwatin

Translation: “Jīm, mīm, and ḥāʾ have one regular principle: something’s moving forward with overpowering force.”

Maqāyīs:advancing with overpowering force

This clarifies the root’s principal core.

Dhahābu al-shayʾi quduman concerns something moving forward.

Bi-ghalabatin wa-quwwatin makes the force and overpowering momentum explicit.

The being or person does more than move between places: the advance has an intensity that makes it difficult to stop.

Ghalabah here does not necessarily mean defeating an enemy.

A horse may overpower its rider’s restraint. A person may rush so intensely that ordinary obstacles do not stop them.

Both emphasize movement’s force rather than necessarily military or moral victory.

Jamaḥa should consequently not always be translated “overcame” or “was victorious.”

The Jamūḥ Horse

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَجَمَحَ الْفَرَسُ بِصَاحِبِهِ جَمْحًا وَجِمَاحًا: ذَهَبَ يَجْرِي جَرْيًا غَالِبًا وَاعْتَزَّ فَارِسَهُ وَغَلَبَهُ.

wa-jamaḥa al-farasu bi-ṣāḥibihi jamḥan wa-jimāḥan dhahaba yajrī jaryan ghāliban wa-iʿtazza fārisahu wa-ghalabahu

Translation: “The horse jamaḥa with its rider: it ran with overpowering force, escaping and overcoming the rider’s control.”

The image concerns a living horse in motion.

The rider tries to control direction or speed, but the animal advances through its own strength.

Jumūḥ therefore extends beyond high speed: the unruly-horse use also involves difficulty of restraint.

Speed and disobedience nevertheless differ.

A horse may run fast while obeying its rider.

Conversely, fear or agitation may make a horse difficult to control without extremely high speed.

Lexicographers accordingly record two close but distinct uses of farasun jamūḥun.

Blameworthy and Praiseworthy Jumūḥ

Al-Azharī explains two meanings for a jamūḥ horse in Tahdhīb al-Lughah.

First, a horse habitually escaping its rider’s control and refusing to respond.

Here the quality is a defect.

Second, a swift, spirited horse ready to run.

Here it is commendable and indicates the animal’s capacity for movement.

Al-Thaʿālibī also transmits this distinction in Fiqh al-Lughah.

Al-Thaʿālibī:praised and defective jumūḥ

This is particularly relevant to Qur’anic research.

If jumūḥ always meant blameworthy disobedience, poetry could not use it positively in praising a horse.

J M Ḥ should therefore not be restricted from the outset to transgression, sin, or rejecting truth.

The shared core is forceful movement and momentum difficult to check, while its value comes from its subject and context.

Lisān and Tahdhīb:two uses for a jamūḥ horse

Jamaḥa Ilayhi: Rushed Toward Something

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَجَمَحَ إِلَيْهِ أَيْ أَسْرَعَ.

wa-jamaḥa ilayhi ay asraʿa

Translation: “He jamaḥa toward it: he hastened.”

This construction is close to Al-Tawbah 9:57.

Ilayhi specifies the destination, while yajmaḥūna describes the movement’s manner.

The subject is forceful, rapid movement toward a possible refuge.

The verse also contains la-wallaw, concerning turning and going toward the destination.

Yajmaḥūna should not absorb the entire sentence’s meaning.

3. Important Forms and Derivatives

Jamaḥa: Rushed and Surged Forward

Jamaḥa is Form I, with imperfect yajmaḥu.

Its familiar verbal nouns are jamḥ, jimāḥ, and jumūḥ.

In movement usages it concerns hastening, surging forward, and difficulty in stopping that movement.

With a horse as subject, bolting or uncontrolled movement may be intended.

With a person, it may describe intense haste or reckless advance.

The precise meaning requires context.

Jāmiḥ: Surging Forward or Unruly

Jāmiḥ is the active participle of jamaḥa.

Applied to an animal, it can denote a horse surging forward or escaping control.

Applied to humans, it may denote someone advancing insistently and recklessly toward personal desire.

The word itself does not establish whether that desire is right or wrong.

Some human uses acquire a negative value through unrestrained pursuit of desire.

That value comes from the conduct’s object.

Jamūḥ: Swift and Difficult to Control

Jamūḥ follows the faʿūl pattern.

The description is used for horses and other animals.

Lisān al-ʿArab explicitly permits jamūḥ for both male and female horses.

Its form alone should therefore not determine that a male is intended.

In the unruly use it can describe an animal difficult to control.

In praise, it describes a swift, spirited horse.

Jamūḥ has two close contextual uses rather than always meaning only unruly.

Jimāḥ and Jumūḥ

Lexical sources record both for intense forward movement.

Al-Azharī distinguishes some uses.

His explanation allows jimāḥ for a horse’s disobedience and positive jumūḥ for its speed and spirit.

This should not become an absolute rule for all Arabic without attention to evidence.

Some sources record both as verbal nouns of jamaḥa.

Their exact meaning therefore depends on the sentence and the verb’s object or subject.

Jamḥah: One Occurrence of Bolting or Unruliness

Some lexical accounts use jamḥah for one instance of headlong movement.

The noun’s formation can identify one occurrence of the action.

It does not occur in the Qur’an.

Nor should every swift movement or run be called jamḥah without evidence.

Jummāḥ: A Small Projectile

Early Arabic jummāḥ names a kind of arrow or small projectile.

Some definitions give it a round, blunt head rather than a sharp arrowhead.

Children used it in games and archery practice, and some hunting methods also employed it.

Lisān al-ʿArab records different materials and head shapes.

Some mention clay or chewed dates; others describe a small round-headed arrow.

This use should be distinguished from jimāḥ, headlong advance.

Resemblance of wording alone cannot prove that the tool was named specifically after a bolting horse.

Jamāmīḥ and Jummāḥah

Early Arabic botanical sources use jummāḥah for parts of some plants ending in a cluster-like structure or soft head.

Jamāmīḥ is also recorded as a plural of certain related forms.

The shape may resemble the round-headed implement.

That resemblance does not establish a certain historical derivation.

None of these botanical or projectile forms occurs in the Qur’an.

Lexical sources:forms,implements,and plants

4. Human Movement, Ships, and Games

The Jamūḥ Person

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَالْجَمُوحُ مِنَ الرِّجَالِ: الَّذِي يَرْكَبُ هَوَاهُ فَلَا يُمْكِنُ رَدُّهُ.

wa-al-jamūḥu mina al-rijāli alladhī yarkabu hawāhu fa-lā yumkinu radduhu

Translation: “A jamūḥ man follows his desire so that turning him back is difficult.”

Physical movement here extends to human conduct and decisions.

Someone may pursue a desire so forcefully that advice, opposition, or warning does not deter them.

Jumūḥ then concerns insistence and unrestrained conduct.

Not all persistence or firmness in decision is consequently blameworthy jumūḥ.

People may uphold a right against threats and pressure; such steadfastness is not necessarily whimsical rebellion.

Human jumūḥ becomes blameworthy when following desire involves disregarding truth, reason, or responsibility.

The root itself does not supply a complete criterion of truth and falsehood.

Women Leaving a Husband’s Home

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

جَمَحَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ مِنْ زَوْجِهَا: خَرَجَتْ مِنْ بَيْتِهِ إِلَى أَهْلِهَا قَبْلَ أَنْ يُطَلِّقَهَا.

jamaḥati al-marʾatu min zawjihā kharajat min baytihi ilā ahlihā qabla an yuṭalliqahā

Translation: “A woman jamaḥat from her husband: she left his home for her family before he divorced her.”

Here jamaḥa describes leaving the home and going to her family.

Lexical explanations often associate it with abruptly leaving the relationship or departing contrary to expectation.

The verb itself does not establish a definitive judgment on the woman’s decision.

Someone may decide through anger or haste; another situation may involve leaving danger or oppression.

Ethical and legal judgments require the actual circumstances.

For root research, the point is jamaḥa’s application to movement and abrupt departure from a former place.

A Ship Beyond Control

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

جَمَحَتِ السَّفِينَةُ تَجْمَحُ جُمُوحًا: تَرَكَتْ قَصْدَهَا فَلَمْ يَضْبِطْهَا الْمَلَّاحُونَ.

jamaḥati al-safīnatu tajmaḥu jumūḥan tarakat qaṣdahā fa-lam yaḍbiṭhā al-mallāḥūna

Translation: “The ship jamaḥat: it left its intended course and the sailors could not control it.”

Lisān:people,women,and ships

The subject is neither a horse nor a person.

Wind, currents, or another factor may divert a ship.

Movement then escapes ordinary control.

J M Ḥ can consequently describe something nonliving.

The verb itself does not determine the precise cause.

Going off course also does not necessarily mean sinking or destruction.

Control may become difficult and then the ship return to its course.

Jumūḥ here means movement escaping control rather than an inevitable accident or destruction.

Children’s Games with Small Bones

Arabic records jamaḥū bi-kiʿābihim for playing with small bones or game pieces.

Children knock or throw one piece to move it from its place.

Ibn Fāris also records this but treats its exact connection to the root cautiously.

The caution matters.

Swift movement and displacing a piece may relate to jamaḥa’s semantic field.

The game’s details and implements should nevertheless not become the entire root’s original meaning without evidence.

Maqāyīs:game pieces and derivational caution

5. Witnesses from Early Arabic Poetry

The Swift Horse in Poetry Attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

Lisān al-ʿArab attributes a line describing a swift, spirited horse to Imruʾ al-Qays:

جَمُوحًا مَرُوحًا وَإِحْضَارُهَا
كَمَعْمَعَةِ السَّعَفِ الْمُوقَدِ

jamūḥan marūḥan wa-iḥḍāruhā / ka-maʿmaʿati al-saʿafi al-mūqadi

Translation: “Swift and spirited, its rapid running sounds like the crackling of burning palm fronds.”

Jamūḥan describes the horse.

Marūḥan concerns its spirit and agility.

Iḥḍār here means the horse’s fast running.

The poet compares the sound of its movement to burning palm fronds.

The image praises the horse’s strength and speed.

Lisān transmits the preceding line as follows:

وَأَعْدَدْتُ لِلْحَرْبِ وَثَّابَةً
جَوَادَ الْمَحَثَّةِ وَالْمُرْوَدِ

wa-aʿdadtu li-al-ḥarbi waththābatan / jawāda al-maḥaththati wa-al-murwadi

Translation: “For war I prepared a leaping, agile horse, excellent both when urged onward and when handled gently.”

Al-Farrāʾ:urging the horse and gentle handling

Jamūḥ consequently continues a description praising the horse.

It concerns the animal’s speed and strength rather than defective disobedience.

The following line has an important variant, however.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Fiqh al-Lughah give jamūḥan marūḥan.

Ibn Durayd’s Jamharat al-Lughah transmits janūḥan marūḥan.

Janūḥ comes from J N Ḥ and concerns inclination or leaning.

The sources therefore do not all transmit the same form.

The line matters for the lexical tradition’s commendatory jumūḥ use, but the variant should remain explicit.

Imruʾ al-Qays is a familiar pre-Islamic poet. Using this particular line as direct J M Ḥ evidence nevertheless requires attention to transmission differences.

Jamharat al-Lughah:the janūḥan marūḥan reading

Unrestrained Advance in an Unattributed Poem

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab transmit:

خَلَعْتُ عِذَارِي جَامِحًا مَا يَرُدُّنِي
عَنِ الْبِيضِ أَمْثَالِ الدُّمَى زَجْرُ زَاجِرِ

khalaʿtu ʿidhārī jāmiḥan mā yaruddunī / ʿani al-bīḍi amthāli al-dumā zajru zājiri

Translation: “I have thrown off my bridle and advance recklessly; no rebuke holds me back from fair women like carved figures.”

The poet uses a horse’s bridle to portray his conduct.

ʿIdhār in animal usage denotes part of the bridle or halter.

Khalaʿtu ʿidhārī here pictures casting off restraint.

Jāmiḥan describes someone advancing recklessly toward desire.

A horse’s bodily movement is thus transferred to human conduct.

This does not make J M Ḥ always mean lust or sexual relations.

Love and women come from other words in the line.

Jāmiḥan describes the advance and lack of restraint.

The examined transmissions do not establish the poet or composition date, so the line cannot be declared definitely pre-Islamic.

Maqāyīs:the unattributed khalaʿtu ʿidhārī witness

Its value is recording an Arabic use for human jumūḥ.

A Difficult Desert in Dhū al-Rummah’s Poetry

Dhū al-Rummah, an Umayyad poet, says:

وَرُبَّ مَفَازَةٍ قُذُفٍ جَمُوحٍ
تَغُولُ مُنَحِّبَ الْقَرَبِ اغْتِيَالًا

wa-rubba mafāzatin qudhufin jamūḥin / taghūlu munaḥḥiba al-qarabi ightiyālan

Contextual translation: “Many a remote, difficult desert wears down and overcomes even the traveler striving hard on the journey to water.”

Jamūḥ describes the desert.

Qudhuf concerns distance and difficulty in crossing the land.

The desert is not a living being disobeying a rider.

The poet draws on movement and difficulty of control to describe the desert’s extent and difficulty.

The route seems so extensive and difficult that the traveler cannot easily master it.

That is an interpretation of poetic imagery rather than jamūḥ’s direct meaning in every sentence. Al-qarab here concerns a journey toward water; it is not al-qurb, ordinary nearness.

Lisān:Dhū al-Rummah and al-qarab on the journey to water

Dhū al-Rummah belongs to the Umayyad period; this is post-revelation Arabic evidence rather than pre-Islamic poetry.

The Line Attributed to Muhalhil: Uncertain Attribution

Al-Ṭabarī attributes the following to Muhalhil when explaining Al-Tawbah 9:57:

لَقَدْ جَمَحْتُ جِمَاحًا فِي دِمَائِهِمُ
حَتَّىٰ رَأَيْتُ ذَوِي أَحْسَابِهِمْ خَمَدُوا

la-qad jamaḥtu jimāḥan fī dimāʾihimu / ḥattā raʾaytu dhawī aḥsābihim khamadū

Translation: “I pressed relentlessly forward in their bloodshed until I saw their men of standing fallen still.”

If correctly attributed, it would show jamaḥa in battle and forceful advance.

Its evidentiary standing nevertheless requires caution.

Maḥmūd Muḥammad Shākir, the editor of Al-Ṭabarī, notes that he did not find it in the Muhalhil poetry he examined.

Some sources also transmit a different second hemistich.

The attribution is therefore not definitively established.

It should not become certain pre-Islamic evidence.

Its value here is a reported usage in exegetical and lexical tradition, not proof of its composition date.

Al-Ṭabarī:the Muhalhil-attributed line and editor’s doubt

Findings from the Poetic Witnesses

J M Ḥ appears across several settings: a horse’s praised speed, unrestrained human advance, difficult and seemingly inescapable desert movement, and forceful advance in battle.

Each involves speed, force, or difficulty in checking movement.

Their moral or practical evaluations differ, however.

A horse’s speed can be praised.

Unrestrained desire may be condemned.

Battle advance can be evaluated positively or negatively in context.

The desert is not itself a moral agent.

The root’s meaning should therefore remain distinct from judging its subject.

6. Qur’anic Usage: Al-Tawbah 9:57

لَوْ يَجِدُونَ مَلْجَأً أَوْ مَغَارَاتٍ أَوْ مُدَّخَلًا لَوَلَّوْا إِلَيْهِ وَهُمْ يَجْمَحُونَ

law yajidūna maljaʾan aw maghārātin aw muddakhalan la-wallaw ilayhi wa-hum yajmaḥūna

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English:

“If they found a refuge, caves, or an entrance, they would turn toward it in haste.”

This contains the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Yajmaḥūna describes humans rather than horses or other animals.

Its speed and difficulty of restraint nevertheless relate to the familiar bolting-horse usage.

Law Yajidūna: A Hypothetical Situation

The verse begins with law.

It presents a hypothetical situation: if they found refuge, caves, or a place to enter and hide, they would go toward it.

It is therefore not necessarily reporting an escape occurring at that moment.

It concerns readiness and intense desire to leave their current situation if a route became available.

This distinction matters.

The verse does not establish that they actually fled the town or hid in a cave then.

It describes their possible, intensely hasty response if they found escape.

Maljaʾan: A Refuge

Maljaʾ comes from L J ʾ.

It denotes a place sought for protection from danger.

It is one option they would go toward if found.

The refuge sense comes from maljaʾ itself, not J M Ḥ.

J M Ḥ describes their movement toward it.

Maghārātin: Caves

Maghārāt is the plural of maghārah.

Here it denotes caves that might provide concealment.

Caves and refuges can overlap in function without being identical.

A refuge may be a fortress, building, or another protected place.

A cave is a natural space or hollow within land or a mountain.

Naming both presents different possibilities for escape and concealment.

Muddakhalan: An Entrance or Passage

Lexical and exegetical sources explain muddakhalan as an entrance, narrow passage, or place one can enter.

Al-Ṭabarī explains it as a passage within the ground.

Forqan uses the Persian “entrance.”

This retains the general meaning.

Contextual analysis can explain that it may lead into hiding or beyond others’ reach.

It should not be restricted without evidence to an underground tunnel, since accounts differ on the precise entry place.

La-Wallaw Ilayhi: Turning Toward the Destination

La-wallaw comes from wallā.

Here it concerns turning from a previous position and going toward a destination.

Ilayhi specifies the direction.

If one of the refuge possibilities became available, they would turn toward it.

The movement thus has a definite direction.

They are not merely running aimlessly, but seeking a destination where they can hide or leave their current situation.

This also matters for yajmaḥūna.

Jumūḥ here does not mean having no direction or destination, but intense haste toward a particular destination.

Wa-Hum Yajmaḥūna: The Manner of Movement

Yajmaḥūna is a third-person masculine plural imperfect of Form I jamaḥa.

Its root is J M Ḥ and singular form yajmaḥu.

Wa-hum yajmaḥūna is a circumstantial clause describing their state while turning toward refuge.

Wa- marks the circumstance, while hum refers to the group.

The clause does not state a separate action after going to refuge, but clarifies that movement’s manner.

They go toward the destination while rushing forcefully, as if difficult to restrain.

Al-Zajjāj explains that they hasten so intensely that nothing readily turns them back.

This relates to the bolting-horse image.

It does not establish that these people entirely lose choice or awareness.

The verse concerns intense haste and a desire for escape rather than loss of reason or consciousness.

Lisān:Al-Zajjāj on yajmaḥūna

Does Yajmaḥūna Mean Fleeing?

Contextually, yes: jumūḥ here connects to flight.

J M Ḥ and F R R nevertheless differ lexically.

Farra concerns fleeing and moving away from danger.

Jamaḥa concerns rapid, forceful movement difficult to check.

The verse brings them together: they want escape and would rush intensely if they found a route.

“Would flee headlong” can therefore render the whole image interpretively.

Yajmaḥūna itself should not become merely “flee,” since speed and forceful advance are prominent.

Al-Ṭabarī:refuge,entrance,and haste

7. Context: Fear, Claimed Belonging, and Desire for Escape

Al-Tawbah 9:56: Fear Behind the Claim of Companionship

وَيَحْلِفُونَ بِاللَّهِ إِنَّهُمْ لَمِنكُمْ وَمَا هُم مِّنكُمْ وَلَٰكِنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ يَفْرَقُونَ

wa-yaḥlifūna bi-Allāhi innahum la-minkum wa-mā hum minkum wa-lākinnahum qawmun yafraqūna

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English:

“They swear by The God that they belong to you, though they do not; rather, they are people who fear.”

This immediately precedes yajmaḥūna.

People swear that they belong to the believers’ community.

The Qur’an denies this, then identifies their fear.

Yafraqūna comes from F R Q in its fear-related use.

Fear is therefore explicit in verse 56.

The next verse pictures their rushing toward a refuge if they found one.

The two verses are clearly connected.

The first identifies fear.

The second describes their possible response to finding escape.

The verbs’ meanings should nevertheless not merge.

Yafraqūna concerns fear.

Yajmaḥūna concerns intensity of movement and haste.

Is Fear Itself a Sign of Hypocrisy?

No.

Fear is a natural human response.

People may fear danger, disease, war, or harm to others.

The Qur’an also reports fear among prophets and others in different situations.

Al-Tawbah 9:56 and Al-Tawbah 9:57 therefore do not make every fearful person or someone seeking refuge to save their life a hypocrite.

The verses concern a particular group whose claimed belonging and companionship conflict with its actual conduct and orientation.

Fear helps clarify their situation in this context.

The criticism concerns a particular relationship of claim and conduct, not fear’s instinct or seeking refuge in danger itself.

Is Headlong Flight Always Wrong?

No.

People may flee fire, flood, a collapsing building, or unjust attack.

Haste may then be necessary to preserve life.

Someone else may rush to escape responsibility, conceal a crime, or abandon a commitment.

Both can involve swift movement while differing morally.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 places jumūḥ within the setting of a group whose false claim of companionship and fear were already described.

Condemnation therefore comes from their overall conduct and orientation.

Haste alone, apart from cause and objective, is insufficient as a moral criterion.

Al-Tawbah 9:58: Conduct Driven by Self-Interest

وَمِنْهُم مَّن يَلْمِزُكَ فِي الصَّدَقَاتِ فَإِنْ أُعْطُوا مِنْهَا رَضُوا وَإِن لَّمْ يُعْطَوْا مِنْهَا إِذَا هُمْ يَسْخَطُونَ

wa-minhum man yalmizuka fī al-ṣadaqāti fa-in uʿṭū minhā raḍū wa-in lam yuʿṭaw minhā idhā hum yaskhaṭūna

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English:

“Some of them criticize you concerning charitable distributions: if given a share, they are pleased; if not, they suddenly become angry.”

This follows the jumūḥ description.

It concerns people whose satisfaction depends on receiving charitable distributions.

These nearby verses thus depict claimed companionship alongside reactions connected to fear, desire for escape, and personal advantage.

Their proximity does not make J M Ḥ originally mean greed or criticism over charitable distributions.

Those behaviors come from other expressions.

Yajmaḥūna still expresses intensity of haste and forward movement.

Outward Companionship and Readiness to Leave

In Al-Tawbah 9:56 they swear they belong to the believers.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 depicts their intense readiness to seek another refuge.

The behavioral contrast matters.

Someone may claim membership outwardly without a firm, sincere connection inwardly.

An opportunity to leave may then produce immediate departure.

This should not become a general rule that leaving a religious or social group makes anyone a hypocrite.

People may leave because of oppression, disagreement about truth, or correction of a mistaken belief.

The subject is a false claim of companionship rather than an absolute prohibition on leaving any group.

This also accords with judging through truth and evidence.

8. Distinguishing J M Ḥ from Related Roots

J M Ḥ (Jumūḥ) and S R ʿ (Surʿah)

Study of S R ʿ

Surʿah concerns rapid movement or performance.

Jumūḥ can also denote swift movement.

Forceful advance and difficulty of restraint are more prominent in jumūḥ, however.

A horse may move swiftly while remaining fully obedient.

In one familiar use, a jamūḥ horse escapes its rider’s restraint.

Speed and jumūḥ are therefore close but not always identical.

“In haste” suits Al-Tawbah 9:57, while lexical analysis should also recognize forceful movement.

J M Ḥ (Jumūḥ) and ʿ J L (ʿAjalah)

Study of ʿ J L

ʿAjalah concerns haste and acting quickly in Arabic.

Some uses involve deciding before sufficient examination.

Jumūḥ may involve haste without always concerning hasty decision-making.

A jamūḥ horse may be described solely through speed and vigor.

Someone may conversely decide hastily without moving physically fast.

The roots overlap in some situations without sharing identical semantic fields.

J M Ḥ (Jamaḥa) and F R R (Farra)

Farra concerns fleeing danger or threat.

Jamaḥa concerns forceful forward movement and haste.

They approach one another contextually in Al-Tawbah 9:57, where the group wants to flee toward refuge.

J M Ḥ alone does not make every headlong movement an escape.

A horse may surge forward in a race or battle without intending flight.

Flight consequently comes from context and haste from yajmaḥūna.

J M Ḥ (Jamaḥa) and W L Y (Wallā)

Study of W L Y

Some Qur’anic wallā usages concern turning away or changing direction.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 combines la-wallaw with ilayhi.

That verb expresses the movement’s direction.

Yajmaḥūna describes its manner.

The verbs therefore perform different roles.

One says they would go toward refuge; the other expresses that going’s intensity and haste.

J M Ḥ (Jamaḥa) and N F R (Nafara)

Nafara can denote setting out, moving, or a group departing.

Some Qur’anic uses concern setting out for battle, acquiring knowledge, or fulfilling responsibility.

Jamaḥa likewise concerns movement, but its Qur’anic use emphasizes haste and forceful advance.

Not every nafr is therefore jumūḥ, nor every jumūḥ a group departing on a particular mission.

J M Ḥ (Jamaḥa) and R K Ḍ (Rakaḍa)

Rakaḍa concerns movement of the foot, running, or urging an animal into motion.

Jamaḥa can also describe a horse or person running quickly.

Rakḍ does not necessarily mean escaping control, however.

Some jumūḥ uses involve precisely that feature.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 uses yajmaḥūna rather than a rakḍ verb.

Translation should therefore preserve the possible image of forceful, difficult-to-check movement alongside haste.

J M Ḥ (Jamaḥa) and Ṭ M Ḥ (Ṭamaḥa)

Lexical sources consider these families close.

Lisān al-ʿArab records jamaḥa and ṭamaḥa as close in haste and movement difficult to restrain.

Both also describe a woman leaving her husband’s home for her family.

Other Ṭ M Ḥ uses concern raising the gaze, desire, or looking toward something.

Jumūḥ and ṭumūḥ are therefore not complete synonyms throughout their derivatives.

Modern Persian ambition or ṭumūḥ can carry positive or negative associations, but should not be transferred to Qur’anic yajmaḥūna without evidence.

J M Ḥ (Jummāḥ) and J B Ḥ (Jubbāḥ)

Lexical sources record both names for a small round-headed projectile.

The proximity may concern the implements’ similar names.

J M Ḥ and J B Ḥ nevertheless differ in their second consonant.

A similar noun should not merge both entire families into one root.

Neither implement name occurs in the Qur’an.

J M Ḥ (Jumūḥ) and J N Ḥ (Junūḥ)

Study of J N Ḥ

Junūḥ comes from J N Ḥ and sometimes denotes inclination or leaning.

Jumūḥ comes from J M Ḥ and concerns forceful forward movement.

Poetic witnesses sometimes bring their forms close or transmit variant readings.

The line attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays has jamūḥan in one source and janūḥan in another.

A variant does not make the roots identical.

Each form must be recognized through its actual consonants.

9. Questions of Understanding and Translation

Does Yajmaḥūna Mean Only Going Quickly?

“Going quickly” conveys an important part of meaning.

Early Arabic jumūḥ also concerns forceful advance and difficulty in stopping movement.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 combines this with fear and seeking refuge.

Analysis may therefore speak of intense haste approaching agitation.

Adding “terrified” or “involuntarily” to the base translation requires caution: these are inferences from context rather than the verb alone.

Does Jumūḥ Always Mean Rebellion?

No.

Al-Azharī records two uses for a jamūḥ horse.

One treats unruliness and disobedience as a defect.

The other praises abundant speed and spirit.

Jumūḥ therefore cannot always be reduced to rebellion.

Does Jumūḥ Always Mean Sin and Disobedience to The God?

No.

A horse usage may concern speed or uncontrolled movement alone.

The ship usage concerns going off course.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 concerns people rushing toward refuge.

Moral judgment thus comes from context.

Does the Haste in Al-Tawbah 9:57 Mean Complete Irrationality?

No.

The verse describes hasty movement in a hypothetical situation.

It does not say that these people entirely lose awareness of their destination or the ability to decide.

They are, on the contrary, seeking a refuge, caves, or an entry place.

Their movement has a destination even as its intensity and haste are emphasized.

Does Yajmaḥūna Mean Running Like Horses?

Comparing human movement to a bolting horse is intelligible in Arabic.

The verse does not explicitly make them horses or equate their nature with that of animals.

The horse image helps explain the intense haste.

The base translation should express the humans’ hasty conduct itself.

Is Everyone Seeking Refuge a Hypocrite?

No.

Seeking refuge from danger can be natural and reasonable.

Al-Tawbah 9:56 and Al-Tawbah 9:57 criticize a particular group whose claim of companionship the Qur’an identifies as false.

This contextual image should not yield a general judgment against all frightened or fleeing people.

Is the Projectile Jummāḥ the Root’s Original Meaning?

Early sources record the noun.

Ibn Fāris identifies the principal meaning as advancing with overpowering force, however.

The projectile is one lexical use within the family.

Its name alone does not establish that the root originally concerned archery exclusively.

10. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Movement-related jamaḥa can mean “hastened,” “surged forward,” or, where appropriate, “rushed with force difficult to restrain.”

Jamaḥa al-farasu may mean “the horse bolted,” “escaped control,” or “rushed forward,” depending on context.

Where disobedience and the rider’s inability to control it are explicit, bolting or becoming unruly is more precise.

Where poetry praises speed and spirit, swift or spirited is more appropriate.

Jāmiḥ can mean surging forward, unruly, or reckless; the subject and context determine the choice.

Jamūḥ for a horse can mean swift, powerful, or difficult to control.

Jimāḥ and jumūḥ concern intense movement and escape from restraint.

Jamaḥat al-safīnatu means the ship went off course and escaped control.

Jummāḥ as an implement names a small arrow or round-headed projectile.

These non-Qur’anic senses should not replace one another without evidence.

Al-Tawbah 9:57

The phrase:

لَوَلَّوْا إِلَيْهِ وَهُمْ يَجْمَحُونَ

la-wallaw ilayhi wa-hum yajmaḥūna

can be translated:

“They would turn toward it in haste.”

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English:

لَوْ يَجِدُونَ مَلْجَأً أَوْ مَغَارَاتٍ أَوْ مُدَّخَلًا لَوَلَّوْا إِلَيْهِ وَهُمْ يَجْمَحُونَ

law yajidūna maljaʾan aw maghārātin aw muddakhalan la-wallaw ilayhi wa-hum yajmaḥūna

“If they found a refuge, caves, or an entrance, they would turn toward it in haste.”

This is natural and faithful to context.

La-wallaw ilayhi expresses movement toward the destination; wa-hum yajmaḥūna makes its intense haste clear.

Lexical analysis may add that the haste resembles a bolting horse’s powerful movement that is difficult to stop.

The direct translation need not add “like unruly horses” or “involuntarily and terrified.”

These may discuss the image interpretively, but are not the verse’s literal equivalents.

“Would flee” may suit contextual explanation, while the base translation should retain haste and forward movement.

11. Conclusion

Arabic J M Ḥ concerns forceful advance, haste, and movement difficult to check.

Ibn Fāris identifies its core as dhahābu al-shayʾi quduman bi-ghalabatin wa-quwwatin: advancing with overpowering force.

A familiar jamūḥ horse rushes forward with great speed and strength.

Early Arabic nevertheless gives this description two different uses.

One treats unruliness and difficulty of control as a defect.

The other praises the animal’s speed, spirit, and movement.

Human jumūḥ can likewise concern intense advance, recklessness, or unrestrained desire.

Another usage describes a ship going off course and becoming difficult to control.

They share movement’s force and difficulty of restraint while differing in value and consequence.

Sources also record the round-headed projectile jummāḥ and botanical uses. These belong to lexical history without necessarily being the family’s original meaning.

Poetry also illustrates the root’s breadth.

In the line attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, jumūḥ praises a swift horse, though jamūḥan and janūḥan are variant readings.

An unattributed poem depicts human recklessness through casting off the bridle.

Dhū al-Rummah applies the description to a difficult desert.

The Muhalhil-attributed line also appears in exegetical tradition, but its attribution remains unconfirmed.

Linguistic history must therefore distinguish secure witnesses, lexical accounts, and lines with uncertain readings or attribution.

The Qur’an uses the root only once, as yajmaḥūna in Al-Tawbah 9:57.

It describes people who would rush intensely toward a refuge, caves, or an entry to concealment if they found one.

The preceding Al-Tawbah 9:56 identifies their fear.

Yafraqūna therefore concerns fear; yajmaḥūna concerns haste and movement’s force.

They connect contextually without being synonyms.

La-wallaw ilayhi specifies destination, while wa-hum yajmaḥūna describes that movement’s manner.

Jumūḥ here consequently means intensely hasty movement toward a perceived escape and refuge, rather than aimless running or total loss of choice.

Haste or seeking refuge is not itself a moral sign of hypocrisy.

The criticism comes from the context of people claiming companionship while fear and readiness to abandon their position appear in their conduct.

Persian shetābān, “in haste,” is appropriate for Forqan’s Al-Tawbah 9:57 translation.

Lexical explanation can discuss powerful, difficult-to-check movement without adding complete terror, irrationality, or moral disobedience to the verb without evidence.

J M Ḥ’s semantic core is forceful advance and haste difficult to check. It may show a horse’s strength and spirit, unruliness elsewhere, or the fearful group’s headlong flight in Al-Tawbah 9:57.

Preserving this distinction separates lexical meaning from contextual moral judgment and explains the Qur’anic use without turning haste into sin or fear into jumūḥ’s lexical meaning.
