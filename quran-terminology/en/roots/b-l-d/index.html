The Qur’anic Root B L D

From a tract of land to a territory, settlement, and a secure place

The root ب ل د occurs nineteen times in the Qur’an, always in nominal forms. بَلَد, including its plural بِلَاد, accounts for fourteen occurrences: nine singular and five plural. The other five occurrences are of بَلْدَة. No verb from this family occurs in the Qur’an.

The places described vary considerably. بلد can refer to the territory of Mecca, to “dead” land revived by rain, or to a destination to which loads are carried. The plural بلاد can encompass different lands and regions. This range shows why “city” is not an adequate fixed translation for the whole family.

In the rain passages, بلد is land to which water and vegetation return. In Abraham’s prayer, it is a territory for whose inhabitants security and provision are requested. In both cases, the noun names a place and the sentence identifies its qualities.

An initial definition may be stated as follows:

B L D: a recognizable tract of land, region, or territory, whether settled or unsettled, inhabited or empty. Depending on context, it can refer to a settlement, city, region, or land; none of these geographical categories is its fixed meaning in every occurrence.

Qur’anic forms

بَلَد
Singular noun; 9 occurrences

بِلَاد
Plural of balad; 5 occurrences

بَلْدَة
Noun; 5 occurrences

Early Arabic: baldah in a desert description

In the Muʿallaqah of al-Aʿshā Maymūn ibn Qays, a poet of the pre-Islamic tradition close to the period of revelation, بلدة describes a desert route:

وَبَلْدَةٍ مِثْلِ ظَهْرِ التُّرْسِ مُوحِشَةٍ
لِلْجِنِّ بِاللَّيْلِ فِي حَافَاتِهَا زَجَلُ

“And a land like the back of a shield, desolate and eerie, around whose edges the jinn raise a clamour at night.”

Later in the same passage, the poet says جاوزتها, “I crossed it,” describing his journey on a camel. The comparison to the back of a shield and the description موحشة evoke an open, lonely expanse. بلدة names that expanse; its desolation and eeriness come from the description and context. Source: al-Aʿshā’s Muʿallaqah, beginning ودّع هريرة, the verse beginning وبلدة مثل ظهر الترس and the following verses. The poem belongs by attribution to the pre-Islamic tradition; an exact date for this particular verse is not established.

This witness, together with the verses about dead land, shows that balad and baldah can be used of unsettled terrain. Buildings, a large population, a market, a government, or city walls are not constant requirements of their use.

This matters for translating the Qur’an. Encountering بلد should not automatically conjure a city that is then read into the verse. The first question is what kind of place the passage describes.

The tangible image: an expanse of land

In the evidence examined here, the tangible image of the family is an expanse of land: terrain that people cross, rain reaches, plants grow in, or people inhabit. A recognizable place does not necessarily have political boundaries or a predetermined size.

This observable meaning must be distinguished from an etymological claim about the word’s earliest origin. To establish another sense, such as “chest,” as the historical source of the word for a territory would require evidence tracing that development. The Qur’anic and early poetic witnesses examined here do not establish such a path.

The basis of this account is therefore the direct use of بلد, بلدة, and بلاد for land and place. This explains their semantic range without claiming a definitive historical sequence for every meaning associated with the same consonants.

How does the Qur’an use balad?

Land that can be living or dead

One important group of verses concerns land and rain:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَقَلَّتْ سَحَابًا ثِقَالًا سُقْنَاهُ لِبَلَدٍ مَيِّتٍ فَأَنْزَلْنَا بِهِ الْمَاءَ فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ مِنْ كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ

“Until, when the winds bore heavy clouds, We drove them toward a dead land, sent water down there, and brought forth through it fruits of every kind.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:57

The following verse says:

وَالْبَلَدُ الطَّيِّبُ يَخْرُجُ نَبَاتُهُ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِ وَالَّذِي خَبُثَ لَا يَخْرُجُ إِلَّا نَكِدًا

“And the good land—its vegetation grows by its Lord’s permission; but the poor land yields only with difficulty and sparsely.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:58

In these two verses, بلد concerns land and its capacity to support vegetation. The description طيّب is associated here with plant growth; the contrasting poor land produces growth with difficulty and sparsely. “Dead city” or “good city” would shift attention from land and its vegetation to an urban setting.

The expression بَلَدٍ مَيِّتٍ occurs again in Fāṭir 35:9. The Qur’an also uses بَلْدَةً مَيْتًا three times: in Al-Furqān 25:49, Az-Zukhruf 43:11, and Qāf 50:11.

These uses provide strong internal Qur’anic evidence that the range of بلد is broader than “city.”

A travel destination and the lands

An-Naḥl says:

وَتَحْمِلُ أَثْقَالَكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَلَدٍ لَمْ تَكُونُوا بَالِغِيهِ إِلَّا بِشِقِّ الْأَنْفُسِ

“And they carry your loads to a land you could not otherwise reach except with great personal hardship.”

An-Naḥl 16:7

Here بلد names a destination. The word alone does not tell us whether it is a large city, village, market, district, or extensive territory. Those details do not come from the root itself.

The Qur’an also uses the plural:

لَا يَغُرَّنَّكَ تَقَلُّبُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فِي الْبِلَادِ

Āl ʿImrān 3:196

And:

فَنَقَّبُوا فِي الْبِلَادِ هَلْ مِنْ مَحِيصٍ

Qāf 50:36

And:

الَّذِينَ طَغَوْا فِي الْبِلَادِ

Al-Fajr 89:11

The other two occurrences of بلاد are in Ghāfir 40:4 and Al-Fajr 89:8. The former likewise concerns the disbelievers’ movement through the lands; the latter concerns the unparalleled character of what is described in connection with Iram. These five passages account for every occurrence of the plural بلاد in the Qur’an.

In such constructions, بِلَاد denotes lands or regions. Translating it as “countries” in the specific sense of modern sovereign states with defined borders can introduce a much later political concept into the verse.

A secure balad: Mecca as a particular referent

In Abraham’s prayer:

رَبِّ اجْعَلْ هَٰذَا بَلَدًا آمِنًا

“My Lord, make this a secure land.”

Al-Baqarah 2:126

And in Ibrāhīm 14:35:

رَبِّ اجْعَلْ هَٰذَا الْبَلَدَ آمِنًا

“My Lord, make this land secure.”

Al-Balad 90:1 also says:

لَا أُقْسِمُ بِهَٰذَا الْبَلَدِ

The next verse, Al-Balad 90:2, again uses بِهَٰذَا الْبَلَدِ, placing the addressee in relation to the same place.

And At-Tīn 95:3:

وَهَٰذَا الْبَلَدِ الْأَمِينِ

Read in context, these references point to Mecca and the sacred territory. The name “Mecca” does not occur in these expressions themselves, nor is it part of the meaning of بلد. Security is expressed through آمِنًا or الأَمِينِ. In An-Naml 27:91, the place’s sacred status is likewise stated separately:

رَبَّ هَٰذِهِ الْبَلْدَةِ الَّذِي حَرَّمَهَا

“The Lord of this territory, He who has made it sacred.”

Thus بلد does not itself mean “sacred territory”; sacredness and security are expressed through other verbs and descriptions.

The noun names the place; the sentence’s descriptions and verbs state its qualities. In An-Naml 27:91, الَّذِي حَرَّمَهَا refers back to the Lord: He is the one who has made the territory sacred.

Balad and baldah: is there a fixed distinction?

The form with final tāʾ, بلدة, does not by itself specify a smaller size or a different geographical rank in the Qur’an. Its relationship to بلد must be examined through the referents and uses of both forms.

Both forms appear in references to the sacred territory:

هَٰذَا الْبَلَدِ

Al-Balad 90:1

And:

هَٰذِهِ الْبَلْدَةِ الَّذِي حَرَّمَهَا

An-Naml 27:91

Read in context, both expressions refer to the same sacred territory. The difference in word form does not itself establish a difference in the size or type of settlement.

Likewise, both بَلَدٍ مَيِّتٍ and بَلْدَةً مَيْتًا describe dry, dead land.

The Qur’anic uses of بلد and بلدة therefore overlap. The final tāʾ alone cannot establish a fixed contrast such as “country versus city” or “region versus village.” This overlap does not mean that every nuance of the two forms is identical in every sentence.

This also matters in translation: assigning each form a modern geographical category in advance and forcing every verse into that scheme would misrepresent their range.

Baldatun ṭayyibah: the land and its description

The passage about Sabaʾ says:

لَقَدْ كَانَ لِسَبَإٍ فِي مَسْكَنِهِمْ آيَةٌ جَنَّتَانِ عَنْ يَمِينٍ وَشِمَالٍ كُلُوا مِنْ رِزْقِ رَبِّكُمْ وَاشْكُرُوا لَهُ بَلْدَةٌ طَيِّبَةٌ وَرَبٌّ غَفُورٌ

“There was indeed a sign for Sabaʾ in their dwelling place: two gardens, to the right and to the left. Eat from your Lord’s provision and give thanks to Him—a good land and a forgiving Lord.”

Sabaʾ 34:15

In بلدة طيبة, بلدة names the land and طيبة describes its favourable quality. The gardens and provision mentioned in the verse provide the setting for that description. Wealth, justice, and a form of government are not independent meanings of the noun بلدة.

The gardens, provision, and people’s circumstances come from the rest of the verse. Any social or economic conclusions drawn from it must be attributed to the context of Sabaʾ, rather than to the definition of B L D.

The same applies to a “secure land”: security is an attribute, not the meaning of the root itself.

B L D alongside Q R Y, M D N, and D W R

In translation, بلد, قرية, مدينة, and دار are sometimes assigned to categories such as country, city, village, and house. Qur’anic usage does not give these four terms boundaries determined solely by size and population.

بلد can denote unsettled terrain, so “city” cannot serve as its comprehensive definition.

بلدة also describes a desert expanse in al-Aʿshā’s poem.

بلاد can encompass extensive lands and regions, but it does not by itself encode the modern political concept of an independent state.

For example, in the story of Moses and his companion, the location of the wall is described through أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ in Al-Kahf 18:77, while the explanation of that same wall uses فِي الْمَدِينَةِ in Al-Kahf 18:82. These uses show that قرية and مدينة need not designate two separate classes defined by smaller and larger size. دار must likewise be assessed through its own uses; placing these terms side by side does not establish fixed population or political boundaries for all of them.

For B L D, what can be stated with greater confidence is a place or recognizable tract of land. Settlement and population may be present, but they are not constant requirements of the word.

A non-Qur’anic witness: taballud in pre-Islamic poetry

Alongside the place nouns, the verbal form تبلّد is attested in pre-Islamic poetry. Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says in his Muʿallaqah, beginning لخولة أطلال:

إِذَا الْقَوْمُ قَالُوا مَنْ فَتًى خِلْتُ أَنَّنِي
عُنِيتُ فَلَمْ أَكْسَلْ وَلَمْ أَتَبَلَّدِ

“Whenever the people said, ‘What young champion will rise to the task?’ I thought they meant me; so I neither grew slack nor hesitated helplessly.”

Beside لم أكسل, the expression لَمْ أَتَبَلَّدِ describes the poet’s refusal to be sluggish or helpless in responding to his people’s need. This branch therefore cannot be treated as exclusively late. Source: Ṭarafah’s Muʿallaqah, the verse beginning إذا القوم قالوا, transmitted, among other places, in أشعار الشعراء الستة الجاهليين. The verse shows what تبلّد means in that particular situation; it does not by itself establish the historical path connecting it to the noun for a territory.

Only the nominal forms بلد, بلدة, and بلاد occur in the Qur’an. The verb’s meaning in Ṭarafah’s poem should not be transferred into those nouns: land described as بلد does not acquire a meaning of sluggishness or helplessness merely by sharing consonants with تبلّد.

Translation proposals for Forqan

A single fixed rendering is not appropriate throughout this family’s Qur’anic uses.

In Al-Baqarah 2:126:

رَبِّ اجْعَلْ هَٰذَا بَلَدًا آمِنًا

A suitable rendering is:

“My Lord, make this a secure land.”

“A secure city” can also be a context-specific rendering here. “Land” preserves the noun’s wider range. The difference between هذا بلدًا in Al-Baqarah and هذا البلد in Ibrāhīm does not, by itself, establish a chronology for the place’s development into a settlement.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:57:

سُقْنَاهُ لِبَلَدٍ مَيِّتٍ

“We drove it toward a dead land.”

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:58:

وَالْبَلَدُ الطَّيِّبُ يَخْرُجُ نَبَاتُهُ

“And the good land—its vegetation grows …”

In An-Naḥl 16:7:

إِلَىٰ بَلَدٍ لَمْ تَكُونُوا بَالِغِيهِ

Depending on the wording of the sentence:

“To a land which …”
Or “To a destination which …”

Both may work, although “destination” is a more explanatory rendering.

For بِلَاد:

“Lands” or “regions”

These are generally more precise than “countries,” unless the particular context calls for a political unit.

For بَلْدَةٌ طَيِّبَةٌ:

“A good land”

This is a natural rendering.

For هَٰذِهِ الْبَلْدَةِ in An-Naml 27:91, “this territory” is suitable; with Mecca understood as the referent, “this city” can also convey the context. The translation should not imply that بلدة always means “city.”

Conclusion

In its Qur’anic uses, B L D names a tract of land, a place, or a territory. Its urban or political classification is determined by context.

The place may be settled or unsettled. In al-Aʿshā’s early poem, بلدة is a desert expanse, and the Qur’an itself speaks of بلد ميت and بلدة ميتا. “City” therefore does not cover the family’s full range.

The same word can refer to Mecca, the territory of Sabaʾ, a travel destination, and, in the plural بلاد, various lands and regions. Size, population, and political organization are not determined by the root alone.

There is no fixed boundary between بلد and بلدة based on geographical size. Both are used for the sacred territory and both for dead land; their formal difference cannot support a division into “country versus city.”

The evidence examined here relates the family’s nouns to place and land. Ṭarafah’s تبلّد illustrates a different verbal branch, but this witness alone does not establish the history of its connection to the word for a territory.

B L D: a recognizable tract, area, or territory of land, whether settled or unsettled, inhabited or empty. Depending on context, it may be a settlement, city, region, or land. Its size, population, political boundaries, security, development, and moral evaluation do not come from the root itself.
