Foundational Meaning

The root D M W appears in the Qur'an through the word دَم (dam) and its plural دِمَاء (dimā'). Its primary meaning is blood: the red fluid that flows in a living body and is connected with life, wound, death, sacrifice, bodily sanctity, and food ruling.

Unlike many roots, this root does not appear in the Qur'an as a verb. The Qur'an speaks of blood as a noun, not as an independent action formed from this root.

Important Forms

The main Qur'anic form of this root is دَم (dam). Its plural is دِمَاء (dimā'). In the Qur'an, this root appears in these nominal forms, not as a verb, verbal noun, active participle, or passive participle.

This matters for analysis: in Qur'anic use, dam is the substance itself, and other verbs or descriptions act upon it. For example, سَفْك (safk) names the act of shedding blood, while مَسْفُوح (masfūḥ) describes the state of blood being poured and flowing.

Linguistic Note

In classical Arabic, dam names the substance that flows in the veins of humans and animals. The word has bodily use as well as social and legal use. In Arabic, blood is not only a physical fluid; it is connected with life, lineage, revenge, responsibility, killing, blood compensation, sacrifice, and human sanctity.

Thus when Arabic speaks of blood, it may mean the bodily fluid itself, or it may point to the right of life, kinship connection, or responsibility caused by the shedding of blood.

The semantic core of D M W is naming the substance blood. In the Qur'an, this root does not perform the work of a verb; it becomes the object of verbs and descriptions. Blood may be shed, forbidden as food, mentioned as a sensory sign, or connected with life and the body. But the root itself remains in the Qur'an as a stable noun.

Material Image

Materially, while blood is inside the body, it is a sign of life and bodily warmth. When it leaves the body, it is usually connected with wound, death, sacrifice, corruption, or prohibition as food.

The main image in this root is the fluid of life within the body's protected space: a fluid whose natural place is inside the body, and whose exit takes on special meaning.

Older Arabic Usage

In Arabic before the Qur'an, blood carried strong social weight. Shed blood could lead to revenge-seeking, tribal war, payment of blood compensation, or collective responsibility. Blood also mattered in kinship, because lineage was often understood through the image of blood.

Therefore, dam in older Arabic was both a bodily fluid and a sign of connection, sanctity, and responsibility. If a precise early Arabic witness is found for dam, blood vengeance, or the connection between blood and lineage, it can be added here. Without a precise witness, the careful general explanation is enough.

Blood in the Field of Bloodshed

In the Qur'an, dam first appears in the field of human bloodshed:

وَيَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ (wa-yasfiku al-dimā')

And he sheds bloods.

Al-Baqarah 2:30

Here dimā' is the object of the verb yasfiku. The root D M W names the substance blood, and the root S F K shows the act of pouring it out.

Another use appears in the following expression:

لَا تَسْفِكُونَ دِمَاءَكُمْ (lā tasfikūna dimā'akum)

Do not shed your own bloods.

Al-Baqarah 2:84

Here too, dimā' appears with safk. In translation, dimā'akum can be rendered as your bloods, or in an explanatory translation as one another's bloods. But the pronoun kum should be noticed, because it shows the inner bond of the addressed community.

Blood in Food Ruling

The second field is food ruling:

إِنَّمَا حَرَّمَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةَ وَالدَّمَ (innamā ḥarrama 'alaykumu al-maytata wa-al-dam)

He has only forbidden you carrion and blood.

Al-Baqarah 2:173

حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةُ وَالدَّمُ (ḥurrimat 'alaykumu al-maytatu wa-al-dam)

Carrion and blood have been forbidden to you.

Al-Ma'idah 5:3

In this use, dam is not treated as a food substance to be eaten. The Qur'an mentions it separately from flesh, and this separation shows that blood is recognized as an independent substance.

Daman Masfuhan

In Surah Al-An'am, a more precise expression appears:

أَوْ دَمًا مَّسْفُوحًا (aw daman masfūḥan)

Or blood poured and flowing.

Al-An'am 6:145

Here dam comes with the description masfūḥ: blood that has been poured and has flowed from its place. This expression is important for distinguishing dam from the root S F Ḥ. Dam is the substance blood itself; masfūḥ describes the state of that blood: poured out and flowing.

Blood as a Sensory Sign

The third field is the sensory sign in the story of Pharaoh and his people:

فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمُ الطُّوفَانَ وَالْجَرَادَ وَالْقُمَّلَ وَالضَّفَادِعَ وَالدَّمَ (fa-arsalnā 'alayhimu al-ṭūfāna wa-al-jarāda wa-al-qummala wa-al-ḍafādi'a wa-al-dam)

So We sent upon them the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs, and the blood.

Al-A'raf 7:133

In this verse, dam appears alongside other tangible signs. For root analysis, it is enough to say that blood here appears as a sensory and visible phenomenon beside other signs.

Nearby Roots

D M W is not the same as S F K. Dam is the substance, while safk is the act of shedding it. When the Qur'an says يَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ (yasfiku al-dimā'), the root S F K forms the act of bloodshed, while D M W names the object of that act.

D M W also differs from S F Ḥ. Dam is blood itself; masfūḥ shows the state of blood, meaning blood that has been poured and has flowed. Safḥ describes the physical state of flowing out, not the substance blood itself.

D M W is also separate from D M '. Dam' is tears and belongs to a different root. Both are fluids that leave the body, but their semantic fields are different. Dam is connected with life, wound, and bodily sanctity; dam' is connected with the eye, weeping, emotional response, sorrow, or humility.

D M W is not the same as nafs. Nafs points to life, self, person, or the living reality of a human being. Dam is the bodily substance of life. In social language, blood may symbolize life, but in lexical analysis, dam is not life itself.

D M W should also not be confused with rūḥ. Rūḥ in the Qur'an is a reality from the command of the Lord and is connected with life, revelation, and divine strengthening. Dam is the physical and biological substance of the body. Blood can be seen, shed, and forbidden as food; rūḥ is not such a substance.

D M W also differs from laḥm. Laḥm is flesh, a bodily substance that can be food in lawful cases. Dam is blood, and in the food-ruling verses it is mentioned independently from flesh. This independence shows that blood in the Qur'an is a separate substance with a separate ruling, not merely a part of flesh.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For دَم (dam), the main rendering is blood.

For دِمَاء (dimā'), the main rendering is bloods. In some contexts it can be made smoother as people's bloods or human bloods, but if the Arabic is attached to a pronoun, the pronoun should be preserved where possible.

For دِمَاءَكُمْ (dimā'akum), the precise rendering is your bloods or your own bloods. In explanatory translation, one another's bloods can be used, but the Arabic speaks with the pronoun kum, and that should not be forgotten.

For الدَّم (al-dam) in food-ruling verses, the proper rendering is blood, not bloodshed and not life. The subject there is the substance blood itself.

For دَمًا مَّسْفُوحًا (daman masfūḥan), suitable renderings are blood poured and flowing or blood that has flowed out. In this expression, dam is the substance and masfūḥ clarifies its state.

Summary

The root D M W in the Qur'an names blood: the red and vital fluid of the body. It does not appear in the Qur'an as a verb, but as a noun; it names the substance blood itself.

At the material level, the natural place of blood is inside the body. Its exit is connected with wound, death, sacrifice, bloodshed, or food prohibition. In Qur'anic use, dam must be kept distinct from the act of safk, the state of safḥ, the tears of dam', the life or self of nafs, the rūḥ, and the flesh of laḥm.
