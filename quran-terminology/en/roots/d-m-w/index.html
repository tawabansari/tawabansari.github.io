1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

In Arabic, dam denotes blood, the fluid circulating in human and animal bodies. In the Qur’an it appears both in its direct bodily sense and in expressions concerning bloodshed, attacks on human life and social responsibility.

The Qur’anic morphological index records the root D M W ten times across seven surahs. Every occurrence belongs to the noun dam: seven are singular and three plural. No independent verb from this family occurs in the Qur’an.

The forms examined include دَم (dam), الدَّم (al-dam), دِمَاء (dimāʾ), دِمَاءَكُمْ (dimāʾakum) and دِمَاؤُهَا (dimāʾuhā). Their differences concern singular or plural number, definiteness, attached pronouns and grammatical case; they do not each have a separate root meaning.

These occurrences fall into several principal settings: blood as a bodily substance; bloodshed and the inviolability of human life; dietary rules; blood as a sign or as evidence for a false claim; and blood in relation to ritual and taqwā.

2. Word formation and the disputed third radical

Although the Qur’anic index classifies the family under D M W, Arabic lexicographers and grammarians disagreed about the word’s original third radical. Only two root consonants are visible in dam itself; the third appears in certain inflections and derivatives.

The lexical sources present two analyses.

The first takes the underlying root as D M W. Al-Jawharī reports this analysis in al-Ṣiḥāḥ, positing damaw and explaining the verb دَمِيَ (damiya) through the phonetic change of wāw to yāʾ. The uncommon dual دَمَوَان (damawān) is also cited in support.

The second takes the root as D M Y. Sībawayh and al-Mubarrad offer analyses involving yāʾ. Forms such as دَمِيَتْ يَدُهُ (damiyat yaduhu), “his hand became bloody or bled,” the dual دَمِيَان (damiyān), and the plurals دِمَاء (dimāʾ) and دُمِيّ (dumiyy) matter to this discussion. Ibn Barrī also argues for yāʾ as the third radical.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn likewise places dam under the entry D M Y and uses دَمِيَتْ يَدُهُ (damiyat yaduhu) to explain its formation.

D M W is therefore a valid heading for this Qur’anic study because the selected index classifies the family that way. It does not mean that the old disagreement over wāw or yāʾ has been settled.

That disagreement does not change the basic Qur’anic meaning: under either analysis, dam is blood.

3. Basic meaning and earlier Arabic usage

In the early lexical sources, the principal meaning is actual bodily blood. Arabic also developed derivatives and expressions from this concrete sense.

دَمِيَ (damiya) describes a body part becoming bloody or bleeding. أَدْمَى (admā) and دَمَّى (dammā) can mean to make bloody or cause bleeding. دَامِيَة (dāmiyah) describes a wound in which blood appears. The old distinction between dāmiyah and دَامِعَة (dāmiʿah) even differentiates a wound showing blood from one from which blood drips.

مُدَمَّى (mudammā) is also recorded for certain reddish colours, especially in descriptions of horses. Blood supplies the comparison; redness does not replace blood as the word’s basic meaning.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

A poem attributed to Ḥuṣayn ibn Ḥumām al-Murrī says:

فَلَسْنَا عَلَى الْأَعْقَابِ تَدْمَى كُلُومُنَا

fa-lasnā ʿalā al-aʿqābi tadmā kulūmunā

وَلَكِنْ عَلَى أَقْدَامِنَا تَقْطُرُ الدَّمَا

wa-lākin ʿalā aqdāminā taqṭuru al-damā

Translation: “We are not those whose wounds bleed onto their heels as they turn away [from the enemy]; rather, as we advance [toward the enemy], blood drips onto the fronts of our feet.”

The poet speaks of wounds received while facing the enemy, contrasting courage in confrontation with flight. Aʿqāb denotes heels; aqdām denotes feet or their foreparts. The image concerns where blood drips when retreating or facing forward, rather than necessarily locating the wounds on the feet themselves. In the verse, تَدْمَى (tadmā) describes wounds bleeding, while الدَّمَا (al-damā) denotes blood itself. The final form al-damā also figures in grammatical discussions of the word’s formation, but cannot by itself establish either the wāw or yāʾ analysis.

Literary sources transmit variations in this line, including the verb in the second hemistich. Its value here lies chiefly in witnessing natural usage of the noun and its related verb, rather than conclusively proving one morphological analysis.

Extensions in social language

In Arabic usage, blood can extend beyond its direct bodily sense into kinship, solidarity, blood vengeance and shared responsibility. It remains the familiar substance, but its association with life and human relationships allows it to stand for a social connection.

This extension matters because Qur’anic dimāʾ does not always merely describe a liquid. Nevertheless, killing, solidarity and blood vengeance should not be presented as independent definitions of the root itself. Such meanings arise from the word’s combination with a verb and the context of speech.

4. Qur’anic uses of the root

A. Bloodshed and attacks on human life

The first occurrence in Qur’anic order comes in the angels’ discussion of placing a khalīfah on earth.

Al-Baqarah 2:30

وَإِذْ قَالَ رَبُّكَ لِلْمَلَائِكَةِ إِنِّي جَاعِلٌ فِي الْأَرْضِ خَلِيفَةً ۖ قَالُوا أَتَجْعَلُ فِيهَا مَنْ يُفْسِدُ فِيهَا وَيَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ وَنَحْنُ نُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِكَ وَنُقَدِّسُ لَكَ ۖ قَالَ إِنِّي أَعْلَمُ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

wa-idh qāla rabbuka li-l-malāʾikati innī jāʿilun fī al-arḍi khalīfatan qālū a-tajʿalu fīhā man yufsidu fīhā wa-yasfiku al-dimāʾa wa-naḥnu nusabbiḥu bi-ḥamdika wa-nuqaddisu laka qāla innī aʿlamu mā lā taʿlamūna

Translation: “And when your Lord said to the angels, ‘I am placing a khalīfah on earth,’ they said, ‘Will You place in it one who causes corruption there and sheds blood, while we glorify You with praise and sanctify You?’ He said, ‘I know what you do not know.’”

Here الدِّمَاءَ (al-dimāʾa) is the plural of dam and the object of يَسْفِكُ (yasfiku). The verb safaka supplies the meaning of shedding; blood is what the action affects.

In this setting, سَفْكُ الدِّمَاءِ (safku al-dimāʾ) refers to violent bloodshed. Its association with يُفْسِدُ فِيهَا (yufsidu fīhā) also shows that the angels are not describing a merely physical event: they place bloodshed alongside corruption on earth.

Still, the word and the complete expression must be distinguished. Dam alone does not mean killing. Combined with safaka in a context of human corruption, the expression points to shedding blood and attacking others’ lives.

The statement is also reported speech from the angels, and The God’s reply emphasizes the limits of their knowledge. Their question alone therefore cannot establish that corruption and bloodshed are the unchanging nature of every human being.

B. Your own blood: from individuals to a community

Al-Baqarah 2:84

وَإِذْ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَكُمْ لَا تَسْفِكُونَ دِمَاءَكُمْ وَلَا تُخْرِجُونَ أَنْفُسَكُمْ مِنْ دِيَارِكُمْ ثُمَّ أَقْرَرْتُمْ وَأَنْتُمْ تَشْهَدُونَ

wa-idh akhadhnā mīthāqakum lā tasfikūna dimāʾakum wa-lā tukhrijūna anfusakum min diyārikum thumma aqrartum wa-antum tashhadūna

Translation: “And when We took your covenant: ‘Do not shed your own blood or expel yourselves from your homes’; then you acknowledged it, while bearing witness.”

Here دِمَاءَكُمْ (dimāʾakum) means “your blood.” On its surface, لَا تَسْفِكُونَ دِمَاءَكُمْ refers to the addressees’ own blood. The social covenant and the continuation in Al-Baqarah 2:85, however, make clear that members of one community are committing violence against one another.

A similar construction appears in وَلَا تُخْرِجُونَ أَنْفُسَكُمْ مِنْ دِيَارِكُمْ: the point is not merely that each person must avoid expelling himself. Attacking other members of the community is also presented as an attack upon the collective self.

Blood appears here alongside anfus, “selves,” and diyār, “homes.” Together these constructions connect the inviolability of life with human security and the right to a home.

This social dimension follows from the covenant and the plural pronoun, rather than from D M W independently meaning community, unity or kinship.

C. Dietary rules and the qualifier masfūḥan

In Al-Baqarah 2:173, Al-Māʾidah 5:3 and Al-Naḥl 16:115, blood appears beside carrion and pork among prohibited foods. All three use the definite form al-dam.

Al-Anʿām 6:145, however, adds a qualifier that requires separate linguistic attention.

Al-Anʿām 6:145

قُلْ لَا أَجِدُ فِي مَا أُوحِيَ إِلَيَّ مُحَرَّمًا عَلَىٰ طَاعِمٍ يَطْعَمُهُ إِلَّا أَنْ يَكُونَ مَيْتَةً أَوْ دَمًا مَسْفُوحًا أَوْ لَحْمَ خِنْزِيرٍ فَإِنَّهُ رِجْسٌ أَوْ فِسْقًا أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ بِهِ ۚ فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَإِنَّ رَبَّكَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

qul lā ajidu fī mā ūḥiya ilayya muḥarraman ʿalā ṭāʿimin yaṭʿamuhu illā an yakūna maytatan aw daman masfūḥan aw laḥma khinzīrin fa-innahu rijsun aw fisqan uhilla li-ghayri llāhi bihi fa-mani uḍṭurra ghayra bāghin wa-lā ʿādin fa-inna rabbaka ghafūrun raḥīmun

Translation: “Say: ‘In what has been revealed to me, I find nothing forbidden for an eater to eat unless it is carrion, or blood poured out, or the flesh of swine—for it is unclean—or a transgression over which a name other than The God’s has been invoked. But whoever is compelled, neither seeking transgression nor exceeding the limit—your Lord is forgiving, merciful.’”

Here دَمًا (daman) is an indefinite singular noun, and مَسْفُوحًا (masfūḥan) its accusative adjective. Masfūḥ belongs to S F Ḥ and describes the blood as poured out or flowing.

دَمًا مَسْفُوحًا (daman masfūḥan) can therefore be translated “blood poured out” or “flowing blood.”

The difference matters: the other three verses use al-dam without this adjective, whereas Al-Anʿām 6:145 states masfūḥan explicitly.

Two linguistic points must remain separate. First, “poured out” is not part of dam’s inherent meaning, since the Qur’an can speak of blood without this adjective. Second, an explicit qualifier in a dietary prohibition matters when assessing the rule’s scope and cannot be set aside in a comparison of the relevant verses.

Should al-dam in the other three verses also be understood in light of daman masfūḥan? That requires comparison of the rules and their contexts. Etymology alone cannot determine the ruling for every form of blood: neither the explicit qualifier nor the need for independent reasoning about other cases may be ignored.

Nor should blood and every tissue containing a small amount of it be treated as absolutely identical lexical categories. Such a judgment requires defining the food in question and the scope of the rule, rather than simply consulting the meaning of dam.

D. Blood within the animal body and the emergence of milk

Al-Naḥl 16:66

وَإِنَّ لَكُمْ فِي الْأَنْعَامِ لَعِبْرَةً ۖ نُسْقِيكُمْ مِمَّا فِي بُطُونِهِ مِنْ بَيْنِ فَرْثٍ وَدَمٍ لَبَنًا خَالِصًا سَائِغًا لِلشَّارِبِينَ

wa-inna lakum fī al-anʿāmi la-ʿibratan nusqīkum mimmā fī buṭūnihi min bayni farthin wa-damin labanan khāliṣan sāʾighan li-l-shāribīna

Translation: “And surely in livestock there is a lesson for you: We give you to drink, from what is within their bellies, from between digestive contents and blood, pure milk, pleasant for those who drink.”

Here دَمٍ (damin) stands beside فَرْثٍ (farthin), and both are mentioned in relation to the emergence of pure, pleasant milk.

Blood here is neither a subject of violence nor being presented as a forbidden food or a sign of punishment. The word has its direct bodily meaning in a description of a process within an animal.

مِنْ بَيْنِ فَرْثٍ وَدَمٍ (min bayni farthin wa-damin) is semantically significant. The passage draws attention to pure milk emerging from amid substances that are not themselves milk. Blood thus forms part of the bodily and biological setting of the phenomenon.

The verse does not, however, explain anatomical details, the circulation of blood or the cellular mechanisms of milk production. Its relationship to biological knowledge can be studied, but a modern scientific theory must not be substituted for the verse’s wording.

This use also shows that “blood” has no fixed negative moral value of its own. It names a bodily substance; evaluation or a ruling arises from the passage’s subject and construction.

E. False blood: blood used to deceive

Yūsuf 12:18

وَجَاءُوا عَلَىٰ قَمِيصِهِ بِدَمٍ كَذِبٍ ۚ قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنْفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا ۖ فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ ۖ وَاللَّهُ الْمُسْتَعَانُ عَلَىٰ مَا تَصِفُونَ

wa-jāʾū ʿalā qamīṣihi bi-damin kadhibin qāla bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amran fa-ṣabrun jamīlun wa-llāhu al-mustaʿānu ʿalā mā taṣifūna

Translation: “And they brought false blood upon his shirt. He said, ‘Rather, your own selves have made a matter seem fair to you. So, beautiful patience; and The God is the One whose help is sought against what you describe.’”

بِدَمٍ كَذِبٍ (bi-damin kadhibin) is a distinctive Qur’anic construction. Damin is a genitive noun, while kadhibin serves as its qualifying description.

Blood does not ordinarily speak truth or tell lies. Here falsehood is attributed to blood presented as evidence for a fabricated story.

“False blood” is therefore natural, but it does not necessarily mean that the substance on the shirt was not real blood. The substance may be real while the claim it is supposed to establish is false.

The lexical meaning of blood remains unchanged; what matters is its role in a scene of deception and false evidence.

This is a clear example of why a word’s meaning must be distinguished from its function within a sentence.

F. Blood as a sign in Pharaoh’s story

Al-Aʿrāf 7:133

فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمُ الطُّوفَانَ وَالْجَرَادَ وَالْقُمَّلَ وَالضَّفَادِعَ وَالدَّمَ آيَاتٍ مُفَصَّلَاتٍ فَاسْتَكْبَرُوا وَكَانُوا قَوْمًا مُجْرِمِينَ

fa-arsalnā ʿalayhimu al-ṭūfāna wa-l-jarāda wa-l-qummala wa-l-ḍafādiʿa wa-l-dama āyātin mufaṣṣalātin fa-stakbarū wa-kānū qawman mujrimīna

Translation: “So We sent upon them the flood, locusts, lice, frogs and blood as distinct, clear signs; yet they acted arrogantly and were a criminal people.”

Here al-dam is one of the signs sent upon Pharaoh’s people, listed alongside the flood, locusts, qummal and frogs.

The verse itself does not specify how this blood appeared, how much there was or exactly which part of their surroundings it affected.

Exegetical reports sometimes supply further details about water turning into blood, but this verse does not itself state those details.

Lexically, the word still means blood. Contextually, it functions as a divine sign for a defiant people. How the sign occurred is a separate question that etymology alone cannot resolve.

G. Animal blood and the criterion of taqwā

Al-Ḥajj 22:37

لَنْ يَنَالَ اللَّهَ لُحُومُهَا وَلَا دِمَاؤُهَا وَلَٰكِنْ يَنَالُهُ التَّقْوَىٰ مِنْكُمْ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ سَخَّرَهَا لَكُمْ لِتُكَبِّرُوا اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَاكُمْ ۗ وَبَشِّرِ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

lan yanāla llāha luḥūmuhā wa-lā dimāʾuhā wa-lākin yanāluhu al-taqwā minkum kadhālika sakhkharahā lakum li-tukabbirū llāha ʿalā mā hadākum wa-bashshiri al-muḥsinīna

Translation: “Neither their flesh nor their blood will ever reach The God; rather, your taqwā reaches Him. Thus He has subjected them to you, so that you may proclaim The God’s greatness for having guided you. And give good news to those who do good.”

Here دِمَاؤُهَا (dimāʾuhā) is the plural of dam with the attached pronoun hā. It refers to the blood of the animals discussed in the passages about ritual observances and sacrifice.

The pairing of لُحُومُهَا (luḥūmuhā) and دِمَاؤُهَا (dimāʾuhā) matters. Flesh and blood are two actual, tangible constituents of the animal, yet the verse explicitly says that neither reaches The God. What reaches Him is human taqwā.

Nor should يَنَالُهُ التَّقْوَىٰ (yanāluhu al-taqwā) be understood as taqwā physically travelling through space. The verse distinguishes an animal’s bodily constituents from the value of human devotion.

This does not deny the reality of sacrifice. The preceding verse, Al-Ḥajj 22:36, mentions sacrificial animals, remembering The God’s name, eating their flesh and feeding those in need. The same context retains the concrete act while connecting its value with taqwā.

For this root study, the point is that dimāʾ still means actual blood. Taqwā and intention are not extracted from the word blood itself; they are understood through the sentences’ explicit contrast.

5. Grammar and semantic distinctions

The ten Qur’anic uses share the stable meaning “blood,” but their different positions within sentences create different applications.

In Al-Baqarah 2:30 and Al-Baqarah 2:84, blood occurs with safaka. The verb adds the action of shedding, while the human setting identifies violence and attacks on life.

In Al-Anʿām 6:145, masfūḥ describes the blood’s condition. The qualifier must not be absorbed into the root definition, because dam can denote blood without it.

In Yūsuf 12:18, kadhib does not turn bodily blood into a different substance. It concerns the blood’s role in substantiating a false claim.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:37, animal blood contrasts with taqwā. This does not mean that blood intrinsically signifies a lack of taqwā in Arabic.

Distinctions from neighbouring words and roots

D M W (dam) and S F K (safk)

Study of S F K

Dam names blood; safk denotes shedding. Safku al-dimāʾ combines these meanings. “Bloodshed” therefore translates the expression, rather than independently defining dam.

D M W (dam) and Q T L (qatl)

Study of Q T L

Qatl concerns killing; dam concerns blood. In some settings, shedding blood refers to killing a person, but the words are not absolute synonyms. Bleeding can occur without death, and a killing can be described without any visible bloodshed.

D M W (dam) and N F S (nafs)

Study of N F S

Nafs has a broader Qur’anic range than blood, including the person or self who bears life and responsibility. Blood is one bodily constituent of a living being; nafs cannot be reduced to blood. Their relationship in discussions of the inviolability of life is contextual and conceptual, rather than lexical identity.

D M W (dam) and D M D M (damdamah)

Study of D M D M

Their superficial resemblance must not lead to identifying the roots. Al-Shams 91:14 uses damdamah, but the morphological index classifies it as a separate family. Shared opening consonants are insufficient to establish one common meaning.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal, natural English equivalent of dam is “blood.” Most Qur’anic uses do not require substituting another expression.

Al-dam is “blood.” Dimāʾ may be “blood” as a collective substance, or “bloods” when its Arabic plural is being explicitly discussed. Dimāʾakum means “your blood,” although the context can make clear that the community is shedding one another’s blood.

In daman masfūḥan, preserve the qualifier: “blood poured out” or “flowing blood.” Bi-damin kadhibin can be “false blood,” provided the explanation makes clear that falsehood may concern the accompanying claim rather than the substance itself.

Dimāʾuhā is naturally “their blood.” English normally treats blood as a mass noun even when several animals are involved; a word-by-word study should still identify the Arabic plural rather than obscure its form.

The inviolability of life, rejection of violence, avoidance of forbidden food and the value of taqwā each belong in the explanation of their respective contexts, rather than being packed into the short translation of dam.

7. Conclusion

D M W is the Qur’anic index’s classification for the family of dam, subject to the linguistic qualification that its historical third radical is disputed between wāw and yāʾ. In the Qur’an the family occurs only as a singular or plural noun.

Its basic, stable meaning is actual blood. The Qur’an uses it within the animal body, among forbidden foods, in a scene of deception, in Pharaoh’s story, in sacrificial ritual and in expressions concerning human violence.

These settings do not create unrelated root definitions. Blood remains blood: sometimes the subject of a ruling, sometimes something shed, sometimes a narrative sign and sometimes material presented to substantiate a false claim.

The central boundary is between a word’s own meaning and the meaning supplied by the entire sentence. Blood alone is neither killing nor taqwā, neither absolute impurity nor proof of truth or falsehood. Such meanings and judgments must be derived from verbs, adjectives, rules and context.

For dietary rules, masfūḥan in Al-Anʿām 6:145 deserves particular attention alongside the other blood passages. A detailed account of the limits of dietary prohibition nevertheless goes beyond etymology.

The Qur’an thus uses a clear bodily word across different settings without erasing its principal meaning. Attending to this distinction prevents both arbitrary narrowing and the substitution of ethical concepts, legal rulings or later interpretations for the Arabic word itself.

Blood, the vital fluid of human and animal bodies. Dam is singular and dimāʾ its plural; bloodshed, dietary rulings and other implications follow from construction and context. The third radical has been analysed as either D M W or D M Y.
