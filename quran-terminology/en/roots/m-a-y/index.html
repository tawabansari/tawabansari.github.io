The number one hundred: from early Arabic counting to Quranic uses involving time, law, and society

The Quranic family indexed under M ʾ Y is represented by miʾah, “one hundred.” It names a definite quantity: ten tens, or one hundred units. Unlike many Quranic roots with extensive physical, ethical, and figurative senses, this family's Quranic use is narrow and clear.

Miʾah counts years, grains, people, and strokes, and describes populations. The objects counted vary, but the number does not: a hundred years, grains, people, or strokes all express the same numerical quantity.

The word's numerical meaning should be distinguished from the message a verse conveys through it. A hundred years may illustrate elapsed time and the power to revive the dead; a hundred grains may feature in an image of multiplied giving; a hundred strokes may specify a particular ruling.

The Quran uses the number in seven verses. Including repetitions, miʾah and its dual miʾatayn occur ten times. All belong to the same numeral; no verb or independent semantic derivative of this family occurs in the Quran.

1. Historical background of miʾah before the Quran

The numeral has a pre-Quranic history. In addition to later dictionaries, inscriptions attest a cognate word for one hundred in the region's earlier languages; their language and script must be distinguished from Quranic Arabic.

Pre-Islamic inscriptions

An important witness is the tomb inscription of Raqush, daughter of Abdmanat, at Hegra in northwestern Arabia. It dates her death to year 162, corresponding to 267 CE. Its numerical expression means “one hundred and sixty-two”; presenting that meaning in modern Arabic spelling would not reproduce the original inscription's script exactly.

The inscription uses the era of the Roman province of Arabia, also known as the Bostra era. Year 162 corresponds here to 267 CE.

This witness matters because a word with the same counting sense appears in a document centuries before the Quran. The meaning “one hundred” was not created by Quranic religious terminology.

The inscription's language and script have been discussed under Nabataean, Nabataean-Arabic, and early Arabic classifications. Those classifications should not be conflated: despite disagreement about the language, the numeral one hundred is identifiable.

Safaitic inscriptions from the ancient northern Arabian region provide another witness: an expression meaning “a hundred horsemen.”

Safaitic should not simply be equated with Quranic Arabic. The witness nevertheless establishes a closely related numeral in the region's earlier linguistic material.

These inscriptions and cognates in other Semitic languages support the word's antiquity. Hebrew and Aramaic also have closely related words for one hundred.

This relationship indicates a very old numeral. It does not establish that Arabs originally assembled a hundred particular objects and then coined the number's name from that image.

The antiquity of a numeral and the original reason for naming it are separate historical questions.

Early Arabic language and poetry

In classical Arabic, miʾah was a definite counting unit, used for livestock, coins, goods, people, and other countable things.

A verse attributed to Muzarrid ibn Dirar al-Ghatafani, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, states:

وَمَا زَوَّدُونِي غَيْرَ سَحْقِ عِمَامَةٍ
وَخَمْسِمِئٍ مِنْهَا قَسِيٌّ وَزَائِفُ

wa-mā zawwadūnī ghayra saḥqi ʿimāmatin / wa-khamsimiʾin minhā qasiyyun wa-zāʾif

“They gave me no provisions except a worn turban and five hundred coins, including debased and rejected pieces.”

The combination «خَمْسِمِئٍ» expresses five hundred. The subject is a monetary count, and its numerical meaning is clear.

Muzarrid lived in both periods, but this verse has not independently been dated to pre-Islamic times. It is evidence of early Arabic usage without being an unqualified pre-Quranic witness. Its first hemistich is also transmitted in a different version; the version quoted here is attested in lexical sources.

Here the form differs from ordinary miʾah in spelling and morphology. It functions within a combination of five hundreds, yielding five hundred; it is not simply an alternative spelling of the singular noun.

The numerical semantic core

Historical evidence and Quranic usage support this definition of the numerical branch:

Miʾah names one hundred, equal to ten tens, and is a definite unit for expressing quantity.

This definition does not add a meaning beyond the word's historical scope.

Abundance, completeness, power, blessing, or intensity may become associated with a hundred in a sentence, but are not fixed meanings of the numeral itself.

2. Word structure and its different forms

Miʾah is an Arabic numeral noun and is grammatically feminine. This does not limit it to counting grammatically feminine things.

Arabic permits both «مِائَةُ رَجُلٍ» and «مِائَةُ امْرَأَةٍ»: a hundred men and a hundred women. The quantity remains the same.

The counted noun following miʾah is ordinarily singular and genitive, as in «مِائَةَ عَامٍ» and «مِائَةُ حَبَّةٍ».

Arabic also has dual forms:

«مِائَتَانِ» in the nominative, and «مِائَتَيْنِ» in the accusative and genitive.

Both mean two hundred.

The plurals «مِئَات» and «مِئُون» mean hundreds, but do not occur in the Quran.

Is the root certainly M ʾ Y?

Arabic dictionaries disagree about the word's historical structure.

Some posit an original final yāʾ and discuss forms such as «مِئْيَة». Others posit a final wāw, or leave uncertain which weak consonant belonged to the original form.

This disagreement concerns word history, not the numeral's meaning.

Quranic morphological indexes place miʾah under M ʾ Y. This is useful for indexing and study, but does not prove every stage of the word's historical development.

Classical Arabic also records verbs such as «أَمْأَى», meaning that a count reaches one hundred or is brought to one hundred.

For example, «أَمْأَتِ الدَّرَاهِمُ» means that the dirhams reached a hundred in number.

These are numeral-based uses, illustrating the formation of Arabic verbs from number names.

Such verbs do not occur in the Quran. Their existence also does not prove that the numeral originally arose from a verb meaning gathering or completing.

Miʾah should therefore not be translated as “a complete collection,” “things gathered together,” or “the perfection of counting.” These are not established lexical definitions of the number.

The spellings «مائة» and «مئة»

Arabic writes the word for one hundred in both forms.

The traditional spelling includes an alif after mīm that is not pronounced in the usual reading. It belongs to the historical orthography and does not introduce a long ā into the word.

The spelling «مئة» is also common in modern Arabic.

The Quran preserves its Uthmanic orthography, as in «مِا۟ئَةَ».

These spelling differences do not change the number.

Nor should miʾah be confused with «ماء», water, because of visual resemblance. Water belongs to a different lexical family with its own structure and history.

3. Quranic uses: time, growth, and human numbers

Al-Baqarah 2:259: a hundred years and elapsed time

Al-Baqarah 2:259 states:

فَأَمَاتَهُ اللَّهُ مِائَةَ عَامٍ ثُمَّ بَعَثَهُ

fa-amātahu allāhu miʾata ʿāmin thumma baʿathah

Al-Baqarah 2:259

“The God caused him to die for a hundred years, then raised him.”

Later in the same verse:

قَالَ بَلْ لَبِثْتَ مِائَةَ عَامٍ

qāla bal labithta miʾata ʿām

Al-Baqarah 2:259

“He said: rather, you have remained a hundred years.”

Miʾah occurs twice here, each time referring to one hundred years.

ʿĀm is the singular counted noun. Miʾata ʿāmin expresses a duration of one hundred years.

The story concerns someone passing a ruined settlement and asking about its revival after death. The God then causes him to die and raises him after a hundred years.

Asked how long he has remained, he supposes a day or part of a day. He is told that a hundred years have passed.

The important contrast is between the stated duration and the person's perception of elapsed time.

The number itself is clear. “A long time” cannot replace it, because the verse specifies a quantity.

Conversely, the mention of a hundred years does not make miʾah itself mean death, elapsed time, or revival.

Those ideas come from «أَمَاتَ», «لَبِثْتَ», and «بَعَثَ», and from the story as a whole.

The numeral specifies only the amount of time.

Al-Baqarah 2:261: a hundred grains in each ear

Al-Baqarah 2:261 states:

كَمَثَلِ حَبَّةٍ أَنْبَتَتْ سَبْعَ سَنَابِلَ فِي كُلِّ سُنْبُلَةٍ مِائَةُ حَبَّةٍ

ka-mathali ḥabbatin anbatat sabʿa sanābila fī kulli sunbulatin miʾatu ḥabbah

Al-Baqarah 2:261

“Like a grain that grows seven ears, with a hundred grains in each ear.”

Miʾatu ḥabbatin refers to a hundred grains.

The number remains one hundred; because grains are being counted, it appears within an image of growth and increase.

One grain produces seven ears containing a hundred grains each. The image yields seven hundred grains.

This appears in a comparison about spending wealth in The God's way.

The verse illustrates the growth and multiplication of giving's outcome through a tangible plant image.

Miʾah does not mean “blessing.” A hundred grains is a defined quantity within the image; blessing and multiplication follow from the comparison and the rest of the verse.

Nor should the example become a universal agricultural law requiring every seed naturally to produce seven ears of a hundred grains.

The verse illustrates the growth of giving's outcome; it does not define a constant property of every plant.

It continues by saying that The God multiplies for whom He wills. Seven hundred should therefore not be treated as an absolute ceiling on every divine reward.

Al-Anfal 8:65: one hundred and two hundred in confrontation

Al-Anfal 8:65 states:

إِنْ يَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ عِشْرُونَ صَابِرُونَ يَغْلِبُوا مِائَتَيْنِ وَإِنْ يَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ مِائَةٌ يَغْلِبُوا أَلْفًا مِنَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا

in yakun minkum ʿishrūna ṣābirūna yaghlibū miʾatayni wa-in yakun minkum miʾatun yaghlibū alfan mina alladhīna kafarū

Al-Anfāl 8:65

“If twenty steadfast people among you are present, they will overcome two hundred; and if a hundred among you are present, they will overcome a thousand of those who disbelieve.”

Miʾatayn is the dual of miʾah and means precisely two hundred.

Miʾah denotes a hundred people.

The numerical ratio is one to ten: twenty against two hundred, and a hundred against a thousand.

This ratio comes from the relationship between several specified numbers, rather than from the root M ʾ Y itself.

Miʾah remains one hundred; the numeral does not inherently contain a military, power-related, or ethical meaning.

Al-Anfal 8:66: a changed ratio in view of human weakness

The following verse states:

الْآنَ خَفَّفَ اللَّهُ عَنْكُمْ وَعَلِمَ أَنَّ فِيكُمْ ضَعْفًا فَإِنْ يَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ مِائَةٌ صَابِرَةٌ يَغْلِبُوا مِائَتَيْنِ وَإِنْ يَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ أَلْفٌ يَغْلِبُوا أَلْفَيْنِ بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

al-āna khaffafa allāhu ʿankum wa-ʿalima anna fīkum ḍaʿfan fa-in yakun minkum miʾatun ṣābiratun yaghlibū miʾatayni wa-in yakun minkum alfun yaghlibū alfayni bi-idhni allāhi wa-llāhu maʿa al-ṣābirīn

Al-Anfāl 8:66

“Now The God has lightened your burden and knows that there is weakness among you. If a hundred steadfast people among you are present, they will overcome two hundred; and if a thousand among you are present, they will overcome two thousand by The God's permission. The God is with the steadfast.”

The ratio here is one to two.

Miʾah and miʾatayn retain the meanings one hundred and two hundred. The ratio and the conditions stated in the verse have changed.

The Quran presents this change through «خَفَّفَ اللَّهُ عَنْكُمْ» and acknowledgment of the addressees' weakness.

The preceding one-to-ten ratio should therefore not be made an unchanging law for every war, when the next verse itself describes a lighter burden and another ratio.

Together the verses illustrate numbers' role in expressing human responsibility and capacity without changing their lexical meaning.

Al-Kahf 18:25: three hundred years and nine more

Al-Kahf 18:25 states:

وَلَبِثُوا فِي كَهْفِهِمْ ثَلَاثَ مِائَةٍ سِنِينَ وَازْدَادُوا تِسْعًا

wa-labithū fī kahfihim thalātha miʾatin sinīna wa-zdādū tisʿan

Al-Kahf 18:25

“They remained in their cave for three hundred years, and added nine.”

Thalātha miʾatin means three hundred.

It is followed by sinīn, the plural of year, clarifying the duration.

The counted noun after miʾah is usually singular and genitive, but sinīn is plural here. In the quoted reading, miʾatin has nunation; sinīn can be analyzed as an explanatory apposition to the stated number. The unusual construction should not be rewritten as though it were the ordinary singular construction.

This syntactic difference does not change the number: thalātha miʾah still means three hundred.

«وَازْدَادُوا تِسْعًا» adds nine years, making the stated total three hundred and nine.

Some explanations relate these amounts to solar and lunar years. That proposal can be examined separately, but neither miʾah nor the construction alone proves it.

The basic translation should retain the stated amounts: three hundred years and nine more.

Without contextual evidence, three hundred should not become “three complete periods,” nor nine a religious code.

Al-Nur 24:2: a hundred strokes in a ruling

Al-Nur 24:2 states:

الزَّانِيَةُ وَالزَّانِي فَاجْلِدُوا كُلَّ وَاحِدٍ مِنْهُمَا مِائَةَ جَلْدَةٍ

al-zāniyatu wa-l-zānī fa-jlidū kulla wāḥidin minhumā miʾata jaldah

Al-Nūr 24:2

“The woman and man who commit unlawful sexual intercourse: give each of them a hundred lashes.”

Miʾata jaldatin denotes a hundred strokes.

Miʾah specifies the quantity, while jaldah supplies the counted unit.

«كُلَّ وَاحِدٍ مِنْهُمَا» makes clear that the number applies to each person separately.

One hundred is therefore a specified quantity, rather than a figure for severe punishment or an unspecified number of strokes.

The ruling's scope and conditions require study of the relevant passages together. Lexically, however, miʾah unambiguously specifies one hundred.

Interpreting the numeral cannot turn it into an arbitrary number of strokes.

Al-Saffat 37:147: a hundred thousand or more

Al-Saffat 37:147 states:

وَأَرْسَلْنَاهُ إِلَىٰ مِائَةِ أَلْفٍ أَوْ يَزِيدُونَ

wa-arsalnāhu ilā miʾati alfin aw yazīdūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:147

“We sent him to a hundred thousand or more.”

Miʾati alfin means a hundred thousand.

Alf belongs to another root and means a thousand. Combining the two produces one hundred thousand.

«أَوْ يَزِيدُونَ» follows that quantity and adds “or more.” The expression itself should be preserved; whether aw conveys estimation from an observer's perspective or another rhetorical function requires separate discussion.

This construction does not require interpreting the speaker as ignorant of the number. Natural language can describe a population by a large figure followed by “or more” without necessarily expressing uncertainty in the speaker's knowledge.

Nor does the expression permit deleting the explicit hundred thousand and translating only “a very large population.”

The stated numeral should be retained.

Miʾah still means one hundred when combined with alf to express a larger numerical order.

4. Comparison with related numerical families

M ʾ Y and ʿ SH R: one hundred and ten

ʿ SH R is associated with ten in Arabic counting.

One hundred equals ten tens, but this mathematical relation does not make miʾah etymologically derived from ʿashr.

The two numerals have independent linguistic forms.

Al-Anfal 8:65 and al-Anfal 8:66 express tenfold and twofold ratios through specific numbers. One hundred remains unchanged; the ratio between the groups changes.

M ʾ Y and ʾ L F: one hundred and one thousand

Alf names one thousand.

Al-Anfal 8:65 juxtaposes miʾah and alf for groups of different sizes.

In al-Saffat 37:147, miʾati alfin forms one hundred thousand.

These are distinct numerical units. Their relationship is quantitative, not proof that either is etymologically derived from the other.

M ʾ Y and ʿ D D: a numeral and the act of counting

ʿ D D concerns counting, numbers, and numerical quantity.

Miʾah names a particular number, while ʿadd can name the act of counting.

A sentence about a hundred counted objects involves counting, but miʾah does not itself mean “to count.”

This distinction keeps a numeral from being confused with the act or process of counting.

M ʾ Y and Ḍ ʿ F: quantity and multiplication

Al-Baqarah 2:261 places one hundred within an image of giving's multiplied outcome.

In its increase-related uses, Ḍ ʿ F concerns multiplication of quantity; miʾah specifies a quantity.

A hundred grains in each ear forms part of the comparison's numerical structure. Multiplication of the outcome is the broader idea conveyed by the verse.

The word for one hundred should not itself be made synonymous with blessing or increase.

5. Explicit numbers and the limits of interpretation

Quranic study sometimes seeks symbolic or philosophical meanings for clear numbers. Such reflection should begin by retaining the number's direct meaning.

Miʾata ʿāmin expresses a hundred years.

Miʾatu ḥabbatin expresses a hundred grains.

Miʾata jaldatin specifies a hundred strokes.

Miʾati alfin means a hundred thousand.

Each passage invites reflection on why the number is chosen, its relation to the subject, and the message conveyed by the complete sentence.

That reflection should not erase the basic meaning.

A hundred years in al-Baqarah 2:259 can illuminate the contrast between human experience of time and divine power; miʾah itself does not mean “time beyond human perception.”

A hundred grains in al-Baqarah 2:261 contributes to the image of increased giving; the numeral itself does not mean “unlimited blessing.”

A hundred strokes in al-Nur 24:2 forms part of a specified legal statement; the root itself says nothing about evidentiary or enforcement conditions.

Three matters should therefore be distinguished: the numerical quantity, what is counted, and the conclusion conveyed by the passage as a whole.

This distinction prevents an interpretive, philosophical, or juristic meaning from replacing the number itself.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

No separate equivalent is needed for miʾah in each context: its Quranic meaning remains “one hundred.”

«مِائَةَ عَامٍ» in al-Baqarah 2:259: “a hundred years.”

«مِائَةُ حَبَّةٍ» in al-Baqarah 2:261: “a hundred grains.”

«مِائَتَيْنِ» in al-Anfal 8:65 and al-Anfal 8:66: “two hundred.”

«مِائَةٌ» in those verses: “a hundred people,” according to the context.

«ثَلَاثَ مِائَةٍ سِنِينَ» in al-Kahf 18:25: “three hundred years.”

«مِائَةَ جَلْدَةٍ» in al-Nur 24:2: “a hundred strokes” or “a hundred lashes,” in coordination with the verb and counted unit.

«مِائَةِ أَلْفٍ» in al-Saffat 37:147: “a hundred thousand.”

“Abundant,” “complete,” “very large,” and “unlimited” are not accurate replacements for one hundred in these passages.

The dual means two hundred. In Persian, both the standard Iranian form and the form meaning “two hundreds” common in Dari can convey this correctly; regional usage should not be dismissed as linguistically wrong. Forqan should apply its chosen editorial form consistently.

Whether counting years, grains, people, or other units, translation should preserve the Arabic quantity in natural language.

7. Summary

The Quranic family M ʾ Y is represented by miʾah, the numeral one hundred, or ten tens.

Pre-Islamic inscriptions, including Raqush's inscription dated to 267 CE and Safaitic witnesses, attest the numeral in the region before the Quran. Its numerical meaning was not invented by later exegesis or jurisprudence.

Arabic dictionaries record other verbal and plural forms, but the Quran uses only the numeral miʾah and its dual.

The Quran counts durations, grains, people, strokes, and populations with it. Its meaning remains stable while the counted object and the number's role in the verse vary.

Al-Baqarah 2:259 uses a hundred years in a story of death and revival. Al-Baqarah 2:261 uses a hundred grains in the comparison illustrating giving's growth. Al-Anfal 8:65 and al-Anfal 8:66 use one hundred and two hundred in ratios between groups. Al-Kahf 18:25 gives three hundred years and nine more. Al-Nur 24:2 specifies a hundred strokes in a ruling. Al-Saffat 37:147 combines a hundred with a thousand to describe a population of a hundred thousand or more.

The Quranic numerical branch means one hundred, equal to ten tens; its dual miʾatayn means two hundred.

This family illustrates how a word can have a simple, definite lexical meaning while serving a wide range of Quranic subjects.

Lexical study need not invent a hidden meaning for every simple word. Its task is to identify the stable meaning accurately and explain its use in different expressions.
