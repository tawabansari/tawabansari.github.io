Central meanings: covering, enveloping, and overpowering, with an effect on what is covered.

1. Introducing the root

The Arabic root R Y N concerns a covering that settles on something or overcomes it. This can be perceptible, like tarnish on metal or dirt on fabric, or nonmaterial, like sleep, intoxication, sorrow, or behavior that gains mastery over a person.

Its significance includes not only covering but also enveloping and overpowering. Something affected by rayn may lose its clarity, cleanliness, or normal capacity, or come under another influence.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an, as rāna in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14. The verse concerns deeds that have overcome people’s hearts.

2. Semantic core and principal forms

An early lexical analysis identifies covering and concealment as the root’s core. Other lexical explanations also record overpowering, staining, and enveloping.

Together these senses describe a covering that comes over something and may affect its condition.

رَيْن — rayn

A verbal noun of rāna, referring to covering, staining, tarnish, or a state of being overcome or enveloped.

Rayn is also used for tarnish on a sword or mirror. This concrete usage describes a layer on a surface that reduces its clarity or cleanliness.

The root is not, however, confined to metallic tarnish.

رَانَ يَرِينُ — rāna yarīnu

A Form I verb meaning to cover, envelop, or overpower.

For example:

رَانَ عَلَيْهِ النُّعَاسُ

rāna ʿalayhi al-nuʿāsu

Drowsiness overcame him.

رَانَ عَلَيْهِ الْهَمُّ

rāna ʿalayhi al-hammu

Sorrow enveloped him.

رَانَتْ عَلَيْهِ الْخَمْرُ

rānat ʿalayhi al-khamru

Wine overcame him and he came under its influence.

These uses concern overpowering and enveloping, not the formation of a physical layer on someone’s body.

رُيُون — ruyūn

Another verbal noun of rāna, recorded in lexical accounts and poetry.

رَانَ الثَّوْبُ — rāna al-thawbu

The cloth became stained or dirty.

This usage illustrates perceptible staining and loss of cleanliness.

رِينَ بِهِ — rīna bihi

A passive construction recorded for being overtaken or trapped in circumstances that are difficult to escape.

أَرَانَ القَوْمُ — arāna al-qawmu

Recorded for people whose livestock died or became emaciated. One early explanation connects this with being overwhelmed by calamity.

These forms illustrate the root’s range, but only rāna occurs in the Qur’an.

3. Arabic witnesses

Lexical usage is not confined to visible staining. Rāna is also recorded for being overcome by sleep, intoxication, sorrow, and distress.

A line by al-Ṭirimmāḥ ibn Ḥakīm, a poet of the Umayyad period, reads:

مَخَافَةَ أَنْ يَرِينَ النَّوْمُ فِيهِمْ
بِسُكْرِ سِنَاتِهِمْ كُلَّ الرُّيُونِ

makhāfata an yarīna al-nawmu fīhim / bi-sukri sinātihim kulla al-ruyūni

Its sense is:

“For fear that sleep, with the stupor of their drowsiness, might overcome them completely.”

Sleep here does not spread over a body like a cloth. The poet describes its mastery over a person, bringing wakefulness and alertness under its influence.

This witness belongs to the Umayyad period and should not be called pre-Islamic without evidence. Alongside lexical accounts, its clear verbal usage shows that overpowering and enveloping were recognized Arabic senses.

Expressions such as rāna ʿalayhi al-hammu confirm this range. Sorrow can dominate someone and affect attention and judgment without a material covering settling on the body.

Tarnish is therefore one concrete image within this family, not its complete definition.

4. Qur’anic usage

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14

كَلَّا بَلْ رَانَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ مَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ

kallā bal rāna ʿalā qulūbihim mā kānū yaksibūna

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14

Translation:

“Not so! Rather, what they used to earn has tarnished their hearts.”

Rāna is a past-tense verb from R Y N.

ʿAlā qulūbihim identifies where the covering and overpowering occur: upon their hearts.

Mā kānū yaksibūna functions as the subject of rāna: what they habitually earned has overcome their hearts.

The verse thus identifies not merely a covering but its cause: the results of their own deeds.

Kānū yaksibūna presents their behavior over a past period and is consistent with continued, repeated conduct.

Rāna itself does not mean “repeating sin.” Continuity and repetition come from the construction and the behavioral context.

Context: from conduct to a covered heart

The opening of Al-Muṭaffifīn condemns giving short measure: people demand their full measure when receiving from others, but give less when measuring or weighing for them.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:12 associates denying the Day of Recompense with transgression and sin.

In Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:13, such a person calls the divine signs “tales of the ancients” when they are recited to him.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14 then reveals the cause of this response: what they have earned has overcome their hearts.

The sequence concerns more than verbal disagreement or rejection of a statement. It describes transgressive, sinful conduct accompanied by denial and turning away from truth.

Someone who repeatedly violates others’ rights and habitually justifies wrongdoing to protect personal interests may eventually judge truth by those same interests and desires.

A person giving short measure, for example, may initially recognize the infringement of others’ rights. Repetition and rationalization may eventually make that person unwilling even to acknowledge the wrongdoing.

This is an ethical application of the verse’s theme, not a lexical definition.

Tarnish on the heart: an image or a physical substance?

The Persian expression “has formed tarnish” suitably conveys the effect of rāna through imagery.

As tarnish can settle on a mirror and reduce its clarity, wrongful deeds in this image can overcome the heart and weaken receptivity to truth.

The verse does not specify the formation of a black substance or physical layer inside the heart. Such details should not be presented as its direct meaning.

Heart here concerns understanding, acceptance, denial, and moral orientation. Its covering describes deeds affecting a person’s receptivity to truth.

The distinction between the tarnish image and overpowering should remain clear. “Tarnish” is a figurative rendering; “covering and overpowering” expresses the broader semantic field.

5. Rayn among related Qur’anic concepts

R Y N (rayn) and Kh T M (khatm)

Khatm includes sealing in the Qur’an, as in Al-Baqarah 2:7, which describes the sealing of certain people’s hearts and hearing.

Rāna in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14 directly concerns a covering produced over the heart by a person’s deeds.

Their effects are related—restricted receptivity to truth—but their linguistic images differ.

Khatm evokes sealing and closure; rayn emphasizes covering and overpowering. This does not establish that one must always be an initial stage and the other a final stage.

R Y N (rayn) and Ṭ B ʿ (ṭabʿ)

Ṭabʿ also appears in connection with the heart and failure to understand, including Al-Tawbah 9:87 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:155.

The word concerns imprinting, marking, and sealing. In some contexts its result approaches the condition described through rayn.

A shared result does not make the roots lexically identical.

R Y N (rayn) and Q S W (qaswah)

Qaswah concerns hardness and hardheartedness, as in Al-Baqarah 2:74 and Al-Zumar 39:22.

These passages use the image of the heart becoming hard.

Rayn concerns covering and overpowering, whereas qaswah emphasizes hardness.

Both conditions may accompany a person’s conduct, but neither should simply be treated as a synonym of the other.

Rayn and being encompassed by one’s deeds

Al-Baqarah 2:81 describes someone who commits evil and whose wrongdoing encompasses him.

That verse uses Ḥ W Ṭ, not R Y N. There is nevertheless an important conceptual connection: conduct may come to dominate a person’s condition rather than remain a passing event.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14 describes the effect upon the heart; Al-Baqarah 2:81 describes wrongdoing encompassing the person.

Deeds are thus not merely things people do and leave behind. Their effects may also govern outlook, judgment, and destiny.

This connection arises from comparing the verses, not from treating their roots as identical.

6. Rayn, responsibility, and the possibility of reform

A significant feature of Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14 is its attribution of the heart’s condition to a person’s own deeds.

What they earned overcame their hearts. The passage therefore does not portray people deprived of understanding without any role or choice on their part.

Their conduct contributes to the formation of this condition.

This is conceptually related to Al-Ṣaff 61:5, where deviation of hearts follows the people’s own deviation.

Nevertheless, rayn’s lexical meaning does not establish that such a covering is permanently irreversible for every person in every circumstance.

Al-Zumar 39:53 speaks of divine mercy and not despairing of it. Al-Nisāʾ 4:110 opens the way to forgiveness for one who seeks it after wrongdoing.

These verses do not trivialize the consequences of deeds. They prevent turning one linguistic image into an absolute judgment that repentance and reform are impossible.

Nor should this passage be applied to every questioning, uncertain, or uninformed person. Its context describes transgression, sin, and denial. Honest doubt in seeking truth is not identical to deliberate persistence in denial and rationalizing corruption.

The relationship between human choice, repeated conduct, sealing of the heart, and guidance or misguidance calls for a separate conceptual study. Rayn is one Qur’anic witness relevant to that inquiry.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for the principal forms include:

Rayn: covering, tarnish, staining, or overpowering, according to usage.

Rāna ʿalayhi: overcame it; enveloped it; cast a covering over it.

Rāna ʿalayhi al-nuʿāsu: drowsiness overcame him.

Rāna ʿalayhi al-hammu: sorrow enveloped him.

Rīna bihi: he was overtaken or trapped in difficult circumstances.

Two defensible renderings of rāna ʿalā qulūbihim in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14 are:

“Overcame their hearts,” making the sense of overpowering more explicit.

“Tarnished their hearts,” conveying the image of covering and staining.

The proposed rendering “what they used to earn has tarnished their hearts” fits this lexical image. A brief root explanation should also mention “covering and overpowering,” so that tarnish is not mistaken for the only possible sense. These proposals do not replace the existing verse translation or authored word-box explanations.

8. Conclusion

R Y N concerns covering, enveloping, and overpowering, both in perceptible phenomena such as tarnish and staining and in nonmaterial conditions such as sleep, sorrow, and intoxication.

Its sole Qur’anic occurrence is Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14, where human deeds cause the heart to be covered and affected.

This usage particularly emphasizes the covering’s mastery and influence. What has overcome the heart is the person’s own conduct, associated in context with transgression, sin, and denial of truth.

Tarnish on the heart is a helpful image, but should not become a physical theory about the heart or a categorical judgment of irreversibility.

The passage shows that deeds affect more than the external world: they may also dominate the person, the capacity for judgment, and readiness to accept truth.
