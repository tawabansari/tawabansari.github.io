Years, reckoning time, difficult years, and radiance

S N W comprises several Arabic semantic branches: years and reckoning time, radiance, and usages involving drawing water and elevation. Lexicons group these within related families, but their common historical origin is not conclusively established.

In the usual morphological classification, the Quran has twenty occurrences in three principal forms: sanah, “year”; sinīn, “years”; and sanā, the radiance of lightning.

The first two concern time, while sanā concerns light. They cannot all be reduced to a single meaning such as temporal cycles, passing years, or rising.

In temporal contexts, a year measures lifespan, waiting, farming, historical events, and time reckoning. Some contexts associate it with drought and difficult years, but hardship is not its fixed meaning.

In the light-related branch, sanā is the radiance appearing from fire or lightning.

Two principles matter: each branch should be understood from its own evidence, and contextual values or consequences should not replace lexical meaning.

1. Early Arabic and evidence attributed to pre-Quranic poetry

Sanah: the year as a temporal unit

Sanah denotes a year, an established unit for measuring time.

A year can measure age, residence, contractual terms, cultivation and harvest, or the dating of historical events.

Attested plurals include sanawāt, sanahāt, sinīn, and sinūn; sunūn with initial u is also recorded as a variant.

Forms containing wāw alongside forms containing hāʾ help explain early grammatical disagreement about sanah's historical structure.

Some reconstructed sanwah, others sanhah. Classification under a root does not settle the history of derivation.

Besides its general meaning, early Arabic sometimes used sanah for a harsh year of drought. Lexicons record this, and the Quran supplies a clear example.

Precisely dating each form and special sense before Islam requires independent historical evidence. Recording a meaning in a later dictionary does not itself prove a pre-Islamic date.

What is clear in Quranic Arabic is sanah as a temporal unit and sinīn for multiple years, including difficult years.

Years and difficult livelihoods

In farming and herding, a year might bring rain, produce, and abundance, or hardship through drought and depleted resources.

Al-sanah consequently has an attested use for a year of famine or drought.

This is intelligible semantically: a period may come to designate its most prominent condition.

That extension should not become the word's universal definition.

If sanah inherently meant a difficult year, its uses for productive cultivation, long life, or reckoning would require a different explanation. The Quran uses the general temporal unit in these settings.

A year as a unit must therefore be distinguished from a difficult year in a particular setting.

Sanā: radiance and light

Another branch classified under S N W is sanā, “light” or “radiance.”

Sanā al-barq denotes the radiance of lightning; sanā also describes firelight.

A familiar example occurs in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Imruʾ al-Qays describes lightning:

يُضِيءُ سَنَاهُ أَوْ مَصَابِيحُ رَاهِبٍ
أَمَالَ السَّلِيطَ بِالذُّبَالِ الْمُفَتَّلِ

yuḍīʾu sanāhu aw maṣābīḥu rāhibin / amāla al-salīṭa bi-al-dhibāli al-mufattali

“Its radiance shines, like the lamps of a monk who tilts the oil with the twisted wicks to brighten the light.”

Sanāhu here denotes the lightning's radiance.

The poet compares it with burning lamps. Al-salīṭ means lamp oil, and al-dhibāl refers to wicks.

The second hemistich has readings including amāla and ahāna. The translation above follows amāla; ahāna conveys generously consuming oil for illumination. Neither changes sanāhu's meaning, radiance.

The line survives in the ode attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and its commentary tradition. It is important evidence for poetic sanā, although independently dating this particular transmitted line has limitations.

Sanā here means neither year, duration, nor drought, but light and radiance.

Sanā and sanāʾ: radiance and eminence

Lexicons distinguish sanā, ending in alif without hamzah, from sanāʾ, ending in alif followed by hamzah. The traditional terms maqṣūr and mamdūd do not make sanā's final vowel short: it is long ā.

Sanā clearly denotes light and radiance in its familiar usage.

Sanāʾ can concern elevation, high standing, and honor.

Verbal forms are also recorded for light rising, fire shining, or a person's standing increasing.

This proximity led some linguists to examine light and elevation together.

Shared imagery does not establish that time, light, and honor all arose from one proven historical origin.

Quranic sanā occurs once, describing lightning. Light is directly supported there; the word should not be translated as honor or high rank.

Drawing water and water-lifting equipment

Arabic lexicons record sanā al-māʾa and related forms for drawing up and supplying water.

Al-sāniyah names a camel or other animal used to draw water from a well; related uses also name water-lifting equipment and a large vessel used in the process.

Drawing and delivering water are central here.

Al-sawānī can similarly refer to animals or equipment used for drawing water.

Other forms concern elevation, favorable availability, or high standing.

These attested Arabic uses should not be imported without evidence into sanah, “year,” or sanā, the radiance of lightning.

Resemblance between lifting water and rising light does not prove a derivational history.

The family contains distinct semantic fields that should be retained rather than replaced with an excessively broad definition.

2. Quranic forms and disputed root analysis

The usual Quranic morphological classification records twenty occurrences in three principal forms.

Sanah: seven occurrences, meaning a year.

Al-Baqarah 2:96, Al-Māʾidah 5:26, Al-Ḥajj 22:47, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14, Al-Sajdah 32:5, Al-Aḥqāf 46:15, and Al-Maʿārij 70:4.

Sinīn: twelve occurrences, the plural “years,” in different grammatical roles.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:130, Yūnus 10:5, Yūsuf 12:42, Yūsuf 12:47, Al-Isrāʾ 17:12, Al-Kahf 18:11, Al-Kahf 18:25, Ṭā Hā 20:40, Al-Muʾminūn 23:112, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:18, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:205, and Al-Rūm 30:4.

Sanā: one occurrence, lightning's radiance.

Al-Nūr 24:43.

All these forms are nouns. This classification includes no Quranic verb under the root.

Sanawāt, sinūn or its variant sunūn, sanāʾ, sāniyah, musānāh, and other Arabic derivatives do not occur in those exact forms in the Quran.

Disagreement over the final radical

Sanah's historical structure is disputed in morphological literature.

Some lexicographers reconstruct final wāw, citing sanawāt and sanawī.

Others reconstruct hāʾ, citing sanahāt and sunayhah.

Sanah can therefore be investigated historically in connection with both S N W and S N H.

Sanā, “radiance,” is also discussed under families ending in wāw or yāʾ.

Grouping words under one root does not itself establish a shared historical origin.

This article examines the three Quranic forms while retaining their distinct meanings, without inventing a unified sense for years and light.

3. Sanah: measuring life and time

A thousand years of life and the human outcome

Al-Baqarah 2:96 says:

يَوَدُّ أَحَدُهُمْ لَوْ يُعَمَّرُ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ

yawaddu aḥaduhum law yuʿammaru alfa sanah

Al-Baqarah 2:96

“Each of them wishes that he might be granted a thousand years of life.”

Sanah here measures lifespan.

A thousand years is a very long human lifetime.

Sanah itself does not mean long life: the number one thousand specifies the duration.

The verse continues that a long life will not remove the person from punishment.

It distinguishes the length of life from its worth and outcome.

That conclusion comes from the context, not sanah's lexical meaning.

Forty years of wandering

Al-Māʾidah 5:26 says:

قَالَ فَإِنَّهَا مُحَرَّمَةٌ عَلَيْهِمْ أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً يَتِيهُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ

qāla fa-innahā muḥarramatun ʿalayhim arbaʿīna sanatan yatīhūna fī al-arḍ

Al-Māʾidah 5:26

“He said: It is forbidden to them for forty years; they will wander in the land.”

Here sanah measures the stated period of prohibition and wandering.

The word itself means neither wandering, deprivation, nor punishment; the accompanying clauses convey those meanings.

The number forty states a duration and should not be made merely symbolic without evidence.

Reaching forty years of age

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 says:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغَ أَشُدَّهُ وَبَلَغَ أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً

ḥattā idhā balagha ashuddahu wa-balagha arbaʿīna sanah

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15

“Until, when he reaches his full strength and reaches forty years.”

Sanah again measures time, now concerning human age and life stages.

The verse places reaching forty alongside full strength and maturity.

The word alone does not mean intellectual maturity, moral perfection, or spiritual development. Those questions belong to the full construction and context.

Nor does mentioning forty imply that everyone reaches an identical level of maturity at precisely that age.

A thousand years less fifty in Noah's story

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14 says:

فَلَبِثَ فِيهِمْ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ إِلَّا خَمْسِينَ عَامًا

fa-labitha fīhim alfa sanatin illā khamsīna ʿāmā

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14

“He remained among them a thousand years less fifty years.”

The verse uses two different Arabic words for a year: sanah and ʿām.

The numerical construction expresses Noah's stay among his people.

Some explanations relate the two words to difficult versus prosperous years or to solar versus lunar reckoning.

Neither distinction follows conclusively merely from their appearing together here.

ʿĀm is also an established word for a year, and both contribute to one temporal quantity.

Translation should preserve the numerical construction without imposing a calendar or moral sense. The Arabic lexical distinction can be noted in commentary even when both words naturally become “year.”

Years, days, and temporal scales

Al-Ḥajj 22:47 says:

وَإِنَّ يَوْمًا عِنْدَ رَبِّكَ كَأَلْفِ سَنَةٍ مِمَّا تَعُدُّونَ

wa-inna yawman ʿinda rabbika ka-alfi sanatin mimmā taʿuddūn

Al-Ḥajj 22:47

“A day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years of your reckoning.”

Al-Sajdah 32:5 likewise says:

ثُمَّ يَعْرُجُ إِلَيْهِ فِي يَوْمٍ كَانَ مِقْدَارُهُ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ مِمَّا تَعُدُّونَ

thumma yaʿruju ilayhi fī yawmin kāna miqdāruhu alfa sanatin mimmā taʿuddūn

Al-Sajdah 32:5

“Then it ascends to Him in a day whose measure is a thousand years of your reckoning.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:4 says:

تَعْرُجُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَالرُّوحُ إِلَيْهِ فِي يَوْمٍ كَانَ مِقْدَارُهُ خَمْسِينَ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ

taʿruju al-malāʾikatu wa-al-rūḥu ilayhi fī yawmin kāna miqdāruhu khamsīna alfa sanah

Al-Maʿārij 70:4

“The angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a day whose measure is fifty thousand years.”

All three use sanah as an established unit of duration.

The first two mention a thousand years; the third, fifty thousand.

These quantities should be taken seriously within their constructions. Their differences require examining each subject before proposing a general equivalence between all days and years.

Al-Ḥajj concerns demands to hasten punishment and time in relation to the divine promise.

Al-Sajdah describes the ordering of the affair and its ascent.

Al-Maʿārij describes the ascent of angels and the Spirit.

Sanah's lexical meaning cannot yield a universal physical formula for time throughout existence.

Conversely, the stated quantities should not be stripped of temporal significance without evidence.

For this root study, sanah clearly denotes a year. The relationship among the temporal scales belongs to a fuller analysis of the verses.

4. Sinīn: multiple years, reckoning, and difficult years

Sinīn is an established plural of sanah.

The Quran uses it for ordinary years, life and residence, cultivation, and hardship.

Years of famine and shortage

Al-Aʿrāf 7:130 says:

وَلَقَدْ أَخَذْنَا آلَ فِرْعَوْنَ بِالسِّنِينَ وَنَقْصٍ مِنَ الثَّمَرَاتِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَذَّكَّرُونَ

wa-laqad akhadhnā āla firʿawna bi-al-sinīna wa-naqṣin mina al-thamarāti laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:130

“We certainly afflicted Pharaoh's people with harsh years and diminished fruits, so that they might take heed.”

Al-sinīn here refers to difficult years of shortage.

This sense has lexical support and is reinforced by naqṣin mina al-thamarāt, diminished fruits.

“Harsh years” is consequently appropriate here.

It does not follow that sinīn means this everywhere.

In Joseph's story, it also describes years of sustained cultivation and storing produce.

Joseph's years in prison

Yūsuf 12:42 says:

فَلَبِثَ فِي السِّجْنِ بِضْعَ سِنِينَ

fa-labitha fī al-sijni biḍʿa sinīn

Yūsuf 12:42

“He remained in prison for several years.”

Biḍʿa sinīn means several years.

Biḍʿ expresses a limited number; sinīn supplies the temporal unit.

Sinīn should not be translated here as years of famine or economic hardship.

The hardship of prison derives from the person's situation, not from the word “years.”

Seven years of cultivation and abundance

Yūsuf 12:47 says:

قَالَ تَزْرَعُونَ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ دَأَبًا

qāla tazraʿūna sabʿa sinīna daʾabā

Yūsuf 12:47

“He said: You will cultivate continuously for seven years.”

This is significant evidence for the general meaning of sinīn.

Joseph speaks of seven years of steady cultivation and storing the harvest.

The subsequent account mentions seven difficult years consuming those reserves.

Sinīn in Yūsuf 12:47 therefore refers to productive cultivation, rather than simply drought.

A rule making sanah and sinīn invariably difficult years is incompatible with this usage.

Counting years and understanding time

Yūnus 10:5 says:

وَقَدَّرَهُ مَنَازِلَ لِتَعْلَمُوا عَدَدَ السِّنِينَ وَالْحِسَابَ

wa-qaddarahu manāzila li-taʿlamū ʿadada al-sinīna wa-al-ḥisāb

Yūnus 10:5

“He determined its phases so that you might know the number of years and the reckoning.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:12 likewise contains li-taʿlamū ʿadada al-sinīna wa-al-ḥisāb.

Both concern years that people can count and use to calculate durations.

This relates to recognizing celestial order and measuring time.

ʿAdad al-sinīn means the number of years, not their hardship.

The phrase alone does not institute a single calendar with all its present-day details. Such details require separate examination.

Years of residence and remaining

Al-Kahf 18:11 says:

فَضَرَبْنَا عَلَىٰ آذَانِهِمْ فِي الْكَهْفِ سِنِينَ عَدَدًا

fa-ḍarabnā ʿalā ādhānihim fī al-kahfi sinīna ʿadadā

Al-Kahf 18:11

“We placed a covering over their hearing in the cave for a number of years.”

Al-Kahf 18:25 expresses their stay as follows:

وَلَبِثُوا فِي كَهْفِهِمْ ثَلَاثَ مِائَةٍ سِنِينَ وَازْدَادُوا تِسْعًا

wa-labithū fī kahfihim thalātha miʾatin sinīna wa-izdādū tisʿā

Al-Kahf 18:25

“They remained in their cave three hundred years and added nine.”

Sinīn measures their stay.

Some calendar explanations connect three hundred and three hundred nine years with solar and lunar reckoning. This can be examined computationally, but sinīn itself means neither solar nor lunar years.

Establishing that relationship requires independent evidence.

The grammar of thalātha miʾatin sinīn has also been discussed, including sinīn as an explanation of the number or an appositional substitute.

The grammatical dispute does not change its essential sense: years of residence.

Years of life and historical events

Ṭā Hā 20:40 describes Moses remaining among the people of Midian for years.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:18, Pharaoh reminds Moses of years spent among them.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:205 considers people being allowed enjoyment for years.

Al-Rūm 30:4 uses fī biḍʿi sinīn for several years in predicting a reversal for the Romans.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:112 asks kam labithtum fī al-arḍi ʿadada sinīn about the duration of earthly residence.

In all these passages, sinīn measures duration.

The subjects differ: Moses' residence, remembered history, worldly enjoyment, political change, and looking back on earthly life.

The word itself denotes multiple years throughout.

This range shows that sinīn names neither a moral epoch, punishment, nor a particular life stage. It is a temporal unit measuring diverse events.

5. Sanā: lightning's radiance

Al-Nūr 24:43 says:

يَكَادُ سَنَا بَرْقِهِ يَذْهَبُ بِالْأَبْصَارِ

yakādu sanā barqihi yadhhabu bi-al-abṣār

Al-Nūr 24:43

“The radiance of its lightning almost takes away sight.”

Sanā denotes the lightning's radiance.

Barqihi is the dependent genitive identifying the source of that radiance.

Sanā therefore does not name lightning itself, but its light.

Distinguishing barq and sanā

Barq is the phenomenon of lightning in the sky.

Sanā is its brightness or radiance.

A person may see the lightning's path or only the resulting illumination of the sky and surroundings.

Lexical accounts describe sanā al-barq as light appearing from lightning, including cases where its source is not directly visible.

This foregrounds the light's effect.

The verse describes such intensity that sight is nearly overcome.

Intensity and its effect upon the eyes come from the full sentence. Sanā alone does not mean blinding or destroying vision.

Sanā, ḍawʾ, and nūr

Ḍawʾ and nūr both belong to the field of light.

Sanā is particularly attested for the radiance of fire and lightning.

The Quran uses it only for lightning.

“Radiance,” “brightness,” or “shining light” can consequently render it here.

This does not establish a fixed hierarchy in which sanā is always stronger than ḍawʾ or superior to nūr.

Each word's range and distinctions must be understood from actual usage.

Ḍawʾ and ḍiyāʾ should be examined independently within the family Ḍ W A, rather than treated as fixed synonyms for sanā.

How is radiance related to a year?

This is a central question for S N W.

The Quranic index places sanā and sanah under one root.

Semantically, however, one concerns light and the other time.

Lexicons record light, elevation, drawing water, and years among forms ending in wāw or yāʾ.

Similar forms and letters alone do not establish a single original meaning for years and lightning's radiance.

Proposed connections through rising, appearing, or cyclical return remain hypotheses without independent historical evidence.

The final definition should retain the branches “year” and “radiance,” without forcing one to derive from the other.

6. Comparison with neighboring roots

S N W (sanah) and S N H (tasannuh): year and time-related alteration

These families are closely connected in the history of sanah.

Early analyses propose both sanwah and sanhah for its historical structure.

The classification used here places sanah and sinīn under S N W and yatasannah in Al-Baqarah 2:259 under S N H. Its word box retains both proposed derivations.

Lam yatasannah there means that food and drink remained unchanged despite a long interval.

This relates semantically to passing years, but sanah and yatasannah have different grammatical roles and direct meanings.

One names a temporal unit; the other expresses alteration through age.

S N W (sanah) and Ḥ W L (ḥawl): year and temporal cycle

Ḥawl can denote a year or a completed temporal cycle.

Ḥawlayni kāmilayni in Al-Baqarah 2:233 means two complete years.

Sanah likewise denotes a year.

Ḥ W L has a broader field including turning, change, surroundings, and intervening barriers.

Their overlap in “year” does not make sanah itself mean a completed revolution or rotation.

The family Ḥ W L should be studied independently to understand this distinction.

S N W (sanah) and ʿ W M (ʿām): two words for a year

ʿĀm and sanah both denote years.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14 uses both in one duration: alfa sanatin illā khamsīna ʿāman.

Sanah is sometimes said to favor difficult years and ʿām prosperous ones.

Such tendencies can be examined in particular contexts, but are not absolute rules.

The Quran uses sabʿa sinīn for Joseph's cultivation years, and ʿām in diverse temporal contexts.

Their basic sense is a year; additional qualities come from context.

S N W (sanah) and D H R (dahr): year and extended time

Dahr concerns extended time or a period within it.

Sanah is a more specific measuring unit.

Life or residence can be measured in years; dahr need not designate a fixed counting unit.

Dahr should not invariably be reduced to a number of years, nor sanah equated with the whole concept of time.

S N W (sanā) and B R Q (barq): radiance and lightning

Sanā and barq occur together in Al-Nūr 24:43.

Barq identifies the phenomenon, sanā its radiance.

They share a field without being complete synonyms.

“The radiance of lightning” therefore retains the distinction better than collapsing both into “intense lightning.”

S N W (sanah) and S N N (sunnah): year and established practice

Sanah, “year,” is studied under S N W or historically under S N H.

Sunnah, “way” or “practice,” belongs to S N N.

Vocalization and root structure distinguish them.

Sunnata Allāhi concerns God's established way, not a year belonging to God.

Alfa sanatin means a thousand years, not a thousand practices.

Similar writing should not merge the meanings.

S N W (sanah) and W S N (wasan): year and drowsiness

Sinah in Al-Baqarah 2:255 means drowsiness.

It belongs to W S N.

Sanah, “year,” belongs to another family.

Their vowels and historical structures must remain distinct; unvocalized similarity does not establish a shared root.

7. Translation recommendations for Forqan

Direct translation should select each form's meaning according to context.

Sanah

Basic equivalent:

“Year”

Alfa sanatin in Al-Baqarah 2:96 means “a thousand years.”

Arbaʿīna sanatan in Al-Māʾidah 5:26 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 means “forty years.”

Alfi sanatin in Al-Ḥajj 22:47 and alfa sanatin in Al-Sajdah 32:5 both mean “a thousand years”; their grammatical case differs.

Khamsīna alfa sanatin in Al-Maʿārij 70:4 means “fifty thousand years.”

“A thousand years less fifty years” in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14 preserves the numerical construction. It translates both Arabic words as “year”; their lexical distinction belongs in commentary.

Without contextual evidence, sanah should not become a difficult year, a lunar year, a solar year, or a symbolic epoch.

Sinīn

Basic equivalent:

“Years”

With a number, as in sabʿa sinīn, the natural translation is “seven years.”

Bi-al-sinīn in Al-Aʿrāf 7:130 can become “with harsh years,” given the reduced harvests.

Biḍʿa sinīn in Yūsuf 12:42 and biḍʿi sinīn in Al-Rūm 30:4 appropriately become “several years.”

ʿAdada al-sinīn in Yūnus 10:5 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:12 means “the number of years.”

Residence and life passages such as Ṭā Hā 20:40, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:18, and Al-Muʾminūn 23:112 directly concern elapsed years.

Al-Kahf 18:25 should retain its stated quantity without inserting an unsupported calendar type into sinīn.

Sanā

Suggested equivalents:

“Radiance” or “brightness”

In Al-Nūr 24:43:

يَكَادُ سَنَا بَرْقِهِ يَذْهَبُ بِالْأَبْصَارِ

yakādu sanā barqihi yadhhabu bi-al-abṣār

Al-Nūr 24:43

“The radiance of its lightning almost takes away sight.”

“The brightness of its lightning” is also natural.

“The height” or “honor of its lightning” is unsuitable: the word clearly concerns light here.

Other important forms in the lexical family

Sanawāt: years.

Sinūn: years, another plural of sanah; sunūn is also recorded as a vocalization variant.

Sanawī: annual or relating to a year.

Musānāh: a transaction or agreement on a yearly basis.

Sāniyah: an animal or device used to draw up water.

Sanā: light or radiance, especially from lightning or fire.

Sanāʾ: elevation, high standing, and honor.

Saniyy: high-ranking or honorable in descriptions of status.

These forms illuminate the family, but only sanah, sinīn, and sanā occur in the Quran in this classification.

Conclusion

S N W is a family with multiple branches in the Quranic index.

In the temporal branch, sanah means a year and sinīn multiple years, measuring life, residence, wandering, cultivation, historical events, reckoning, and durations in passages about ordering affairs and ascent.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:130, sinīn concerns famine and hardship, but this cannot be generalized. In Yūsuf 12:47 it describes sustained cultivation.

In the light branch, sanā means radiance. Al-Nūr 24:43 applies it to lightning, as does the ancient poetic example attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays.

Arabic also records related forms involving drawing water and high rank. Their common historical origin with years and radiance is not conclusively established.

Sanah's analysis under S N W or S N H remains disputed. Sanawāt alongside sanahāt shows why simple classification cannot settle the issue.

The final definition should retain the distinct branches:

Quranic S N W includes two principal fields: sanah and sinīn for countable years, and sanā for radiance, particularly lightning's light. Related Arabic forms also concern drawing water and elevation, but a common historical origin for all these meanings is not conclusively proven.

Translation should preserve sanah and sinīn as temporal units and sanā as radiance.

A year itself means neither hardship nor ease; radiance does not itself name lightning or every kind of light. A year's qualities come from its events; sanā denotes radiance whose source the context identifies.

These distinctions keep lexical meaning separate from contextual features, later scientific concepts, and unproven derivational connections.
