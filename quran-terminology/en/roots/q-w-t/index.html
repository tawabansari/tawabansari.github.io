Food and the means of sustaining life: from daily sustenance and meeting needs to preservation, ability, and the divine description muqīt

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

In Arabic, Q W T is associated above all with food and what is needed to continue living. قُوت (qūt) denotes food or provision that sustains the body and meets its essential needs.

Other recorded uses of this family concern preservation, meeting needs, maintaining dependants, and having power over something. Their connection can be examined through the idea of sustaining something and providing what its survival depends on.

The root Q W T occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in two different forms:

  • أَقْوَاتَهَا (aqwātahā): its sustenance or provisions; the plural of qūt with the pronoun hā, in Fuṣṣilat 41:10.
  • مُقِيتًا (muqītan): keeper, one who has power over something and knows it; a Form IV active participle in Al-Nisāʾ 4:85.

Other derivatives are also known in Arabic:

  • قُوت (qūt): food or provision necessary to sustain life.
  • قِيت / قِيتَة (qīt / qītah): a quantity of food or means of subsistence, especially enough to meet a need.
  • قَاتَ / يَقُوتُ (qāta / yaqūtu): fed someone or met their subsistence needs.
  • أَقَاتَ / يُقِيتُ (aqāta / yuqītu): supplied sustenance or preserved; in another usage, had power over something.
  • اِقْتَاتَ (iqtāta): adopted something as sustenance and fed on it.
  • تَقَوَّتَ (taqawwata): took food or nourishment from something.
  • اِسْتَقَاتَ (istaqāta): asked another for sustenance.
  • مُقِيت (muqīt): provider of sustenance and keeper; also able, watchful, or witness, according to usage.

All these forms are recorded in Arabic, but only aqwātahā and muqītan occur in the Qur’an.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Qūt: what sustains life

Lisān al-ʿArab defines qūt as the portion of provision a person needs to continue living. Its expression ما يمسك الرمق من الرزق (mā yumsiku al-ramaqa mina al-rizqi) means the amount of provision that preserves the remaining strength to live.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ likewise relates qūt to food that sustains the human body.

This definition distinguishes qūt from every kind of material enjoyment.

A person may possess abundant food, clothing, money, housing, and other resources, but qūt in its primary usage is closer to food and the essential means of nourishment and survival.

Someone who has only one night’s food, for example, can say they have their sustenance for tonight, even if they have no wealth or reserves for the following days.

The Arabic expressions قوت ليلة (qūtu laylatin) and قيت ليلة (qītu laylatin) are recorded for one night’s food or subsistence.

Qūt therefore does not necessarily mean abundant food, preferred food, or complete comfort. Its familiar usage emphasizes what is necessary to sustain life.

It should not, however, be converted into a fixed quantity of food or a specified minimum of calories. Living beings’ needs vary with age, physical condition, activity, and circumstances. The Arabic word is not itself a scientific unit of nutritional measurement.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: qūt as food sustaining life

Feeding and maintaining dependants

The Arabic verb qāta is used for feeding someone and providing their livelihood.

قَاتَهُ يَقُوتُهُ (qātahu yaqūtuhu) means that he provided the person’s food or means of subsistence.

Lisān al-ʿArab uses أنا أقوته (anā aqūtuhu) for someone who maintains another with a small amount of provision.

This usage connects qūt as the name of food with the act of supplying it.

A father providing his child’s food, or someone responsible for a needy person’s livelihood, for example, can be called a قَائِت (qāʾit), a provider of sustenance, in this language.

The responsibility to maintain someone and provide their livelihood depends on the person’s status and relationship to the other. The root Q W T alone does not determine who is legally obliged to meet another person’s expenses.

Taking nourishment and adopting something as food

The Arabic expressions اقتات بالشيء (iqtāta bi-al-shayʾi) and تقوت بالشيء (taqawwata bi-al-shayʾi) mean that someone adopted something as their food.

In poverty, for example, a person might live on a small amount of food and make it their sustenance.

This usage differs from the verb meaning to feed another.

In قات فلانا (qāta fulānan), someone supplies another’s sustenance. In اقتات بالشيء (iqtāta bi-al-shayʾi), the person themselves takes something as food and nourishment.

Istaqāta likewise denotes requesting sustenance in lexical usage.

The Q W T family thus names the substance meeting a need, the act of supplying it, and the act of using it.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī describes a she-camel worn down by its load and the hardship of travel:

وَحَمَلْتُ كُورِي خَلْفَ نَاجِيَةٍ
يَقْتَاتُ فَضْلَ سَنَامِهَا الرَّحْلُ

wa-ḥamaltu kūrī khalfa nājiyatin / yaqtātu faḍla sanāmihā al-raḥlu

Approximate translation:

“I placed my saddle on the back of a swift she-camel; the saddle gradually consumed the surplus flesh of her hump.”

Here yaqtātu is applied to the camel’s saddle.

Ibn Sīdah explains the expression through eating and taking nourishment, as though the saddle feeds on the camel’s hump.

Ibn al-Aʿrābī, however, relates it to gradual diminution and taking something away little by little.

The poet does not mean that the saddle is literally alive and eats food. Pressure and wear caused by the load are compared to gradual consumption.

The witness clarifies two points: iqtiyāt in early Arabic concerned eating and taking sustenance, and that image could also be used figuratively.

The difference between the two lexical explanations should remain visible: one emphasizes the image of eating, the other the result of gradual depletion.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Muḥkam: Ṭufayl’s witness and two explanations of iqtiyāt

Feeding a fire: figurative usage

Early Arabic also uses اقتت لنارك (iqtat li-nārika): supply something to your fire so that it remains alive and alight.

A verse attributed to Dhū al-Rummah states:

فَقُلْتُ لَهُ ارْفَعْهَا إِلَيْكَ وَأَحْيِهَا
بِرُوحِكَ وَاقْتَتْهُ لَهَا قِيتَةً قَدْرًا

fa-qultu lahu irfaʿhā ilayka wa-aḥyihā / bi-rūḥika wa-iqtat-hu lahā qītatan qadran

Approximate translation:

“I told him: lift it toward you and revive it with your breath; sustain it with a measured amount of your blowing.”

In this image, measured blowing keeps the fire alive.

Applying qūt to fire shows that supplying what is needed to sustain a condition can extend beyond food for the human body.

This figurative usage does not mean that fire has a digestive system or biological life like living beings.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad-era poet. His verse is therefore a witness to early Arabic after Islam, rather than pre-Islamic poetry.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Mufradāt, and Asās al-Balāghah: Dhū al-Rummah’s fire witness

3. Preservation and ability: another branch of the root

Ibn Fāris’s analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies a core for Q W T encompassing three ideas: holding, preserving, and having power over something.

He explains qūt on this basis too: food sustains the body and enables it to continue living.

This analysis establishes an intelligible connection between food and preservation.

What people eat to survive helps preserve their lives. Someone providing another’s sustenance contributes to their survival within the limits of their ability.

Preservation in this family is nevertheless not necessarily restricted to feeding.

In some Arabic usages, أقات على الشيء (aqāta ʿalā al-shayʾi) means having power over something.

Besides food and subsistence, the family thus became associated in early Arabic with ability and command over a matter.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Q W T: holding, preservation, and ability

An early witness concerning ability

A verse attributed to Abū Qays ibn Rifāʿah states:

وَذِي ضِغْنٍ كَفَفْتُ النَّفْسَ عَنْهُ
وَكُنْتُ عَلَى مَسَاءَتِهِ مُقِيتَا

wa-dhī ḍighnin kafaftu al-nafsa ʿanhu / wa-kuntu ʿalā masāʾatihi muqītā

Translation:

“Many a man bearing a grudge I restrained myself from harming, though I had the power to hurt him.”

Muqītan here does not mean a provider of food; it denotes someone’s ability to do something.

The speaker says that despite being able to harm the resentful person, he refrained.

Sources attribute the verse to Abū Qays ibn Rifāʿah, while other reports attribute it to Al-Zubayr ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib or others. Some of its wording is also disputed.

The use of muqīt for ability is therefore valid lexical evidence, but the poem’s attribution should not be presented as certain.

Ability here does not mean performing the action. The person could cause harm but refrained.

In such a context, muqīt therefore denotes power and ability, without necessarily exercising that power.

Al-Ṭabarī: the ability witness and its disputed attribution

Being a witness and keeper

Besides being able or supplying sustenance, muqīt is recorded in lexical sources as meaning a keeper or witness.

A poem attributed to Al-Samawʾal ibn ʿĀdiyāʾ states:

أَلِيَ الْفَضْلُ أَمْ عَلَيَّ إِذَا حُوسِبْتُ
إِنِّي عَلَى الْحِسَابِ مُقِيتُ

a-liya al-faḍlu am ʿalayya idhā ḥūsibtu / innī ʿalā al-ḥisābi muqītu

Translation following one lexical reading:

“When I am called to account, is merit in my favor or liability against me? I am aware of my account and witness to it.”

Muqīt here is connected with accounting and awareness of one’s deeds.

The poem’s wording is disputed, however.

Another transmission replaces innī with ربي على الحساب مقيت (rabbī ʿalā al-ḥisābi muqītun): my Lord is keeper over, or has power over, the account.

Abū Saʿīd al-Sīrāfī supported this reading. Ibn Barrī, by contrast, explains that if muqīt means a witness who knows, the first reading is also defensible.

The poem is useful for understanding the word’s semantic range, provided its disputed wording and interpretation are acknowledged.

The lexical point is that muqīt in early Arabic was not restricted to supplying food; it could concern ability, preservation, and awareness of affairs.

4. Aqwātahā: the earth’s sustenance in the Qur’an

Fuṣṣilat states:

وَجَعَلَ فِيهَا رَوَاسِيَ مِنْ فَوْقِهَا وَبَارَكَ فِيهَا وَقَدَّرَ فِيهَا أَقْوَاتَهَا فِي أَرْبَعَةِ أَيَّامٍ سَوَاءً لِلسَّائِلِينَ

wa-jaʿala fīhā rawāsiya min fawqihā wa-bāraka fīhā wa-qaddara fīhā aqwātahā fī arbaʿati ayyāmin sawāʾan li-al-sāʾilīna

“And He placed firm mountains upon it from above, blessed it, and apportioned its provisions in four days, equally for those who ask.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:10

The meaning of aqwātahā

أَقْوَات (aqwāt) is the plural of قُوت (qūt), and the pronoun hā refers to the earth.

In its direct construction, aqwātahā therefore denotes the earth’s sustenance or provisions.

Since the earth is where creatures live and find nourishment, early explanations also connect the expression with sustenance for the earth’s inhabitants.

Al-Ṭabarī’s commentary discusses the food and livelihood of the earth’s inhabitants, plants, rain, animals, and resources on which earthly life depends.

These explanations show that qūt here is not limited to one particular food.

The earth can provide varied foods and essential means of sustaining living beings, from plants and fruits to resources used by animals and humans.

Every substance or natural resource should nevertheless not be included in the lexical meaning of qūt without evidence.

Stone, metals, and minerals, for example, may matter to human life and livelihood. Counting them among aqwātahā in this verse requires contextual explanation, however. The familiar meaning of qūt is closest to food and what sustains life.

The role of qaddara in the verse

The verse states:

وَقَدَّرَ فِيهَا أَقْوَاتَهَا (wa-qaddara fīhā aqwātahā)

The verb qaddara belongs to Q D R and concerns measure and apportionment.

Measuring and determining amounts therefore come from qaddara, rather than aqwāt itself.

Aqwāt names what has been apportioned; qaddara expresses the act of measuring and apportioning it.

This matters because the root Q W T itself does not establish that every portion of sustenance has a fixed, equal, or previously calculable quantity.

The verse speaks of divine apportionment on earth. How each person obtains food, their actual share of resources, and the social conditions of distribution are further questions.

Does aqwātahā mean only human food?

The preceding verse concerns the earth’s creation, while verse ten mentions its mountains, blessing, and sustenance.

The earth appears here as an environment enabling creatures to live and benefit from its resources.

Provision in the Qur’an is not confined to humans.

Hūd 11:6 states:

وَمَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا عَلَى اللَّهِ رِزْقُهَا

wa-mā min dābbatin fī al-arḍi illā ʿalā allāhi rizquhā

“There is no creature on earth whose provision does not rest with The God.”

Within the Qur’an’s network of meanings, providing sustenance therefore also concerns other living beings.

Restricting aqwātahā in Fuṣṣilat 41:10 to human food would accordingly require sufficient evidence.

The verse does not, however, by itself offer a complete scientific definition of every creature’s nutrition, biological cycles, or how food resources arose.

Its central idea is the availability and apportionment of sustenance on earth.

Aqwātahā and sawāʾan li-al-sāʾilīna

The verse ends with:

سَوَاءً لِلسَّائِلِينَ

sawāʾan li-al-sāʾilīna

The construction and interpretation of this expression have been debated.

On one reading, sawāʾ concerns the complete and equal number of days mentioned in answer to those asking.

Another reading connects the expression with the needs of those requesting and with apportioning provision in accordance with their needs.

Sawāʾ alone should therefore not be taken to mean that everyone in the world actually receives exactly equal food and resources.

Equally, overlooking this expression’s role should not remove the suitability and apportionment of provision from consideration.

Determining the precise referent and role of sawāʾan li-al-sāʾilīna requires separate grammatical and contextual study.

For Q W T, the clear conclusion is that aqwātahā concerns the earth’s sustenance and provisions. Their measure and manner of apportionment come from other parts of the verse.

5. Muqītan: a divine description in Al-Nisāʾ

The root’s second occurrence is in Al-Nisāʾ:

مَنْ يَشْفَعْ شَفَاعَةً حَسَنَةً يَكُنْ لَهُ نَصِيبٌ مِنْهَا وَمَنْ يَشْفَعْ شَفَاعَةً سَيِّئَةً يَكُنْ لَهُ كِفْلٌ مِنْهَا وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُقِيتًا

man yashfaʿ shafāʿatan ḥasanatan yakun lahu naṣībun minhā wa-man yashfaʿ shafāʿatan sayyiʾatan yakun lahu kiflun minhā wa-kāna allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin muqītan

“Whoever makes a good intercession has a share in it, and whoever makes a bad intercession has a portion of it. And The God is keeper over everything.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:85

The formation of muqīt

Muqīt is a Form IV active participle, appearing in the accusative as muqītan in the verse.

In وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُقِيتًا (wa-kāna allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin muqītan), it is the predicate of kāna.

The Qur’an gives this divine description in the indefinite form, rather than the definite al-muqīt.

Al-Muqīt is also known as a name of God in the tradition of divine names. Qur’anic study should first examine the actual form and meaning in the verse, however.

Does muqīt mean a provider of sustenance?

Some lexicographers define muqīt as someone who provides creatures with sustenance.

This is clearly related to the Q W T family: أقات الشيء (aqāta al-shayʾa) can denote supplying sustenance or preserving something.

The context of Al-Nisāʾ 4:85, however, is not food and livelihood.

The verse concerns good and bad intercession and a person’s share in its consequences.

Translating muqītan here only as “a provider of food” would therefore leave its connection with the verse’s subject unclear.

Early sources also record other meanings for muqīt: keeper, witness, one who knows, and one who has power.

These meanings relate more directly here to knowing human deeds and their consequences.

Muqīt as keeper and witness

Early commentaries also explain muqīt here through ḥafīẓ and shahīd: keeper and witness.

This meaning indicates that people’s deeds and shares remain within God’s sight and knowledge.

The verse begins with someone intervening in a good or bad matter. Such intervention may affect another’s decision and conduct, but the intercessor still bears responsibility for their own share.

The closing description muqīt emphasizes that God keeps watch over everything and knows it.

“Keeper” in Forqan’s current translation is therefore defensible.

Here the word should nevertheless not be restricted to preserving bodies or supplying food; it also concerns oversight, knowledge, and power in relation to all affairs.

Muqīt as one who has power

Al-Ṭabarī prefers qadīr, “having power,” in examining this verse, citing early Arabic usage in support.

On this reading, muqīt describes someone with ability and power over something.

This meaning also fits the construction عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ (ʿalā kulli shayʾin), “over everything.”

The existence of this usage does not by itself establish that other recorded meanings, such as keeper and witness, are impossible in the verse.

Conversely, interpreting muqīt only as keeper should not remove its established association with ability from lexical study.

A distinction should therefore be made between a possible Arabic meaning and a contextually preferred translation.

Knowing and keeping watch over human conduct clearly matter to the context. Enforcing good and bad consequences likewise concerns divine power.

These aspects may complement each other in understanding the divine description. A basic translation is better served by choosing one and explaining the other in commentary.

Al-Ṭabarī: meanings of muqīt and preference for qadīr

Muqīt and responsibility for intercession

The central subject of Al-Nisāʾ 4:85 is good and bad intercession.

Shafāʿah here concerns joining or intervening in a matter. Such intervention may support goodness or contribute to wrongdoing.

The Qur’an states that whoever makes a good intercession shares in it, and whoever makes a bad intercession also bears a share.

The closing muqītan recalls that none of these deeds lies outside God’s knowledge, watch, or power.

This can also be compared with the divine description in the next verse:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ حَسِيبًا

inna allāha kāna ʿalā kulli shayʾin ḥasīban

“Indeed, The God takes account of everything.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:86

Ḥasīb belongs to Ḥ S B, whereas muqīt belongs to Q W T.

The descriptions come close in the fields of knowledge, accounting, and human responsibility, but they are not lexically identical.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:85, God’s being muqīt concerns a person’s share in good and bad conduct. In the next verse, His being ḥasīb accompanies responding to greetings.

Their proximity permits examining a conceptual relationship, but does not justify transferring one word’s entire meaning to the other without evidence.

6. Distinctions from related roots

Q W T (qūt) and R Z Q (rizq)

Rizq has a broad Qur’anic range, concerning gifts, benefits, and resources God provides for creatures.

Qūt in its familiar usage is closer to food and what continued life depends on.

A person may have wealth, clothing, a house, resources, and food. In suitable contexts these can all be instances of rizq, but they are not necessarily qūt in the specific sense of essential food.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:10, aqwātahā denotes the earth’s sustenance. In Hūd 11:6, rizquhā concerns provision for living creatures.

The expressions overlap in sustaining life, but their lexical meanings are not identical.

Both may therefore be translated as “provision” in some contexts. Root explanations should nevertheless distinguish essential sustenance from the broader range of rizq.

Study of R Z Q

Q W T (qūt) and Ṭ ʿ M (ṭaʿām)

Ṭaʿām concerns food and what is eaten, and occurs frequently in the Qur’an.

Qūt also concerns food, but its familiar usage places greater emphasis on what sustains life.

Nutritional qūt can therefore be ṭaʿām insofar as it is food, but not every food is necessarily someone’s primary or essential sustenance.

The verb aṭʿama emphasizes feeding, whereas aqāta in lexical usage can also express supplying sustenance, preserving, and having power.

This is a tendency, rather than an absolute rule for every historical usage.

Study of Ṭ ʿ M

Q W T (qūt) and K F Y (kafā)

Kifāyah concerns sufficiency and being enough.

Qūt too may denote an amount of food sufficient to sustain life.

Sufficiency can concern very different subjects, however, including knowledge, wealth, support, testimony, and ability.

Qūt in its primary meaning, by contrast, concerns supplying food and sustaining life.

Sustenance may suffice in the necessary amount, but not everything sufficient is necessarily qūt.

Study of K F Y

Q W T (muqīt) and Ḥ F Ẓ (ḥafiẓa)

Ḥifẓ concerns keeping, caring for, and preventing something from being lost or wasted.

One recorded lexical sense of muqīt likewise means keeper or preserver.

Q W T also has the field of sustenance and meeting needs, whereas Ḥ F Ẓ does not necessarily concern food or livelihood.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:85, the families come close through preservation.

Their semantic overlap does not mean that every ḥāfiẓ is a muqīt, or that every muqīt means only a keeper.

Q W T (muqīt) and Q D R (qadara)

Q D R concerns measure, ability, and apportionment.

Muqīt too means able or powerful in some Arabic usages.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:10, qaddara itself accompanies aqwātahā. This combination shows that sustenance and apportionment are distinct concepts that can concern one subject.

Ability is likewise among the meanings proposed for muqīt in Al-Nisāʾ 4:85.

This overlap should not make the roots derivationally identical.

Study of Q D R

Q W T (qūt) and Q W Y (quwwah)

These roots resemble each other in writing but are distinct.

قُوت (qūt), from Q W T, concerns food and what sustains life.

قُوَّة (quwwah), from Q W Y, denotes strength and ability.

The words sometimes approach one another in Persian too, since food can help maintain bodily strength.

This connection does not make their final root letters or all their meanings identical.

In its many Qur’anic uses, quwwah concerns strength and ability, whereas aqwāt denotes the earth’s sustenance.

Study of Q W Y

The written resemblance between قوت (qūt) and وقت (waqt) should likewise not confuse them: waqt belongs to W Q T and concerns appointed time.

Study of W Q T

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan

قُوت (qūt): essential food; nourishment and the means of sustaining life.

أَقْوَات (aqwāt): sustenance, foods, or essential provisions.

أَقْوَاتَهَا (aqwātahā): its sustenance or provisions; in Fuṣṣilat 41:10, the earth’s sustenance and provisions.

قَاتَ / يَقُوتُ (qāta / yaqūtu): fed someone or supplied their sustenance and livelihood.

أَقَاتَ / يُقِيتُ (aqāta / yuqītu): supplied sustenance and preserved; in another usage, had power over something.

اِقْتَاتَ (iqtāta): adopted something as food and fed on it.

تَقَوَّتَ (taqawwata): took nourishment or used something for food.

مُقِيت (muqīt): keeper, provider of sustenance, one who has power, or one who knows and witnesses, according to context.

For the two Qur’anic occurrences:

وَقَدَّرَ فِيهَا أَقْوَاتَهَا

wa-qaddara fīhā aqwātahā

“And He apportioned its provisions within it.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:10

وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُقِيتًا

wa-kāna allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin muqītan

“And The God is keeper over everything.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:85

In Fuṣṣilat 41:10, “its provisions” is a natural, defensible translation. The root explanation should clarify that aqwāt concerns above all food and the essential means of continued life.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:85, “keeper” is a suitable rendering. Other meanings, such as able, aware, and witness, can be examined in contextual commentary.

Although “provider of food” is related to one branch of the family, it does not by itself adequately convey the sense needed in this verse’s context.

8. Conclusion

In Arabic, Q W T concerns sustenance, food, and what maintains life. The family includes verbs for feeding, providing a livelihood, taking nourishment, and requesting food.

Other uses connect it with preservation, care, and power over something. Ibn Fāris explains these branches through holding, preserving, and ability.

In the Qur’an, aqwātahā in Fuṣṣilat 41:10 refers to the earth’s sustenance and provisions. The verse describes their apportionment, but that meaning comes from qaddara, rather than a fixed meaning of Q W T itself.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:85, muqītan describes God. Early sources explain it through keeping, ability, witnessing, and providing sustenance.

The verse concerns a person’s share in good and bad intercession. God’s watch, knowledge, and power over deeds and their consequences therefore clearly relate to its subject.

Muqīt should not be restricted to a provider of food, nor should its historical connection with sustenance and meeting needs be overlooked.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Food and what sustains continued life; in other uses, meeting needs, preservation, and power over something.

Meaning in Fuṣṣilat 41:10:

أَقْوَاتَهَا (aqwātahā): the earth’s sustenance and provisions; what is supplied there for creatures’ nourishment and continued life.

Meaning in Al-Nisāʾ 4:85:

مُقِيتًا (muqītan): keeper over everything and possessing power over it; in context, describing God, whose knowledge and power encompass deeds and people’s shares in their good and bad consequences.
