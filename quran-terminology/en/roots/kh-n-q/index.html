The Qur’anic root KH N Q: constriction, pressure, and strangulation

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(al-munkhaniqatu)

From constriction of the throat and difficulty breathing to narrow passages, neck cords, and the strangled animal in the Qur’an

In Arabic, KH N Q concerns constriction and pressure. Its clearest use involves the throat and neck: when pressure acts on the throat or airway and makes the passage of air difficult or impossible, khanq and its derivatives are used.

The root is not limited to strangling people or animals. In early Arabic, words from the same family describe a narrow mountain pass, a constricted alley, a cord around the neck, and even a vessel filled nearly to its rim.

These usages involve a narrowing of a passage or a restriction of room for movement. Pressure on the throat can interfere with airflow; a passage can restrict movement; a full vessel has little room left for more contents.

These usages do not have identical consequences. A narrow alley does not mean its travelers are suffocating, just as a cord around the neck does not by itself mean death.

KH N Q occurs only once in the Qur’an, as al-munkhaniqatu in Al-Māʾidah 5:3. It concerns an animal affected by strangulation, within a passage prohibiting certain animal foods.

Understanding it requires distinguishing three matters: the lexical meaning of strangulation, the animal’s condition, and the ruling concerning an animal that has died and the exception involving tadhkiyah, or ritual slaughter.

1. Forms and derivatives of the root

KH N Q is a sound triliteral root. Its basic verb is khanaqa–yakhnuqu.

Its main recorded forms include:

  • Khanaqa–yakhnuqu: to compress someone’s throat or neck and cause strangulation.
  • Khanq and khaniq: verbal nouns denoting strangling or pressure on the throat.
  • Khannaqa: to strangle or apply pressure to the throat; in other uses, to fill something until little space remains.
  • Takhnīq: strangling; in some uses, filling a vessel excessively.
  • Khāniq: a strangler; spatially, a narrow passage or place.
  • Khannāq: someone whose practice or habitual activity is strangling.
  • Khanīq: a strangled person or animal.
  • Makhnūq: someone or something affected by strangulation through pressure on the throat.
  • Inkhanaqa–yankhaniqu: to undergo strangulation, with the throat or airway subjected to pressure.
  • Inkhināq: becoming strangled or being in a condition of strangulation.
  • Munkhaniq: someone or something affected by strangulation.
  • Ikhtanaqa–yakhtaniqu: to undergo strangulation; in some early uses, strangulation in which the person is directly involved in causing it.
  • Khināq: a cord, rope, or implement used to compress the neck.
  • Mikhnaqah: a necklace or ornament worn around the neck.
  • Makhnaq or mukhannaq: the throat or the place where a neck cord rests; in other usages, something constricted or filled.
  • Khunāq and khunāqiyyah: names for an illness or condition affecting the throat and airway.

These early Arabic forms are not identical or synonymous. Some describe strangling, others the condition of being strangled, and others an implement, location, or result of pressure. Sources also differ over the verbal noun’s vocalization: some prefer khaniq, while others record khanq as well. Forms naming a location and those describing fullness must likewise be distinguished in their actual uses. Several recorded vocalizations do not make all their meanings identical.

Only munkhaniqah occurs in the Qur’an: the feminine active participle of inkhanaqa, Form VII.

It should not be confused with mikhnaqah, meaning a necklace. The different vowels and morphological patterns produce entirely different meanings.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: Al-Māʾidah 5:3, word twelve; Form VII active participle

2. The semantic core: constriction and narrowing a passage

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris gives the root one principle:

الْخَاءُ وَالنُّونُ وَالْقَافُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى ضِيقٍ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-nūnu wa-al-qāfu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā ḍīqin

Translation:

“Khāʾ, nūn, and qāf have one principle indicating narrowness.”

This definition is broader than strangling itself.

In its familiar use, khanq involves pressure on the throat, but constriction can also occur elsewhere.

For example, a very narrow passage between mountain walls makes movement difficult. A rope tightening around the neck can restrict breathing.

Both situations involve constriction, but its consequence depends on what is subjected to pressure or restriction.

Ibn Fāris therefore records al-khāniq for a narrow pass and al-mikhnaqah for a necklace, alongside strangling.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: constriction, passage, and necklace

Khanq in reference to the throat

Lisān al-ʿArab records khanaqahu for applying pressure to the throat.

In this usage, a person or animal may be subjected to pressure by a hand, cord, rope, or another obstruction.

The throat is involved in the passage of air and food. Severe pressure on this region can impair breathing and lead to unconsciousness or death.

Lexically, however, pressure and strangulation should be distinguished from death through strangulation.

Someone may briefly undergo compression of the throat and then be released. Strangulation has occurred, but death has not.

If the pressure continues and the person’s life ends, the person can be said to have died from strangulation.

Death is thus one possible severe consequence of khanq, rather than a necessary element in every use of the verb.

This distinction matters for al-munkhaniqatu because the verse follows its list of animal injuries and deaths with the exception illā mā dhakkaytum.

Khanq and khināq: pressure and its implement

In Arabic, al-khināq denotes a cord placed around the neck that can be used for strangling.

The word relates to the site of the action, but the cord itself does not necessarily imply death.

A rope may be tied around an animal’s neck to restrain it, for example. If it presses on the throat, the animal can undergo strangulation.

The cord is the implement of pressure, and strangulation its result.

In another use, mikhnaqah names a necklace or ornament encircling the neck.

The necklace and khināq share a location, but their functions differ.

A necklace can be decorative without applying pressure that causes strangulation.

Accordingly, something being around the neck is not identical to strangling or being strangled. Naming an implement through its location or its capacity to exert pressure does not mean strangulation always occurs.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: verbal noun, implement, and location

Khunāq: throat disease

Arabic sources record al-khunāq and al-khunāqiyyah for illnesses affecting the throat that can make breathing or swallowing difficult.

These usages concern people and certain animals, especially horses and birds.

The connection with the root’s main field is apparent: illness or inflammation can produce a sense of constriction and blockage in the throat.

A disease name in early medicine does not necessarily correspond to one specific modern diagnosis.

For example, Persian khunāq has sometimes become a name for diphtheria. It does not follow that every early use of khunāq denotes that same disease with its particular microbial cause.

Early dictionaries establish conditions involving blockage and difficulty in the throat. Identifying each case’s medical cause is a separate matter.

Moʿīn dictionary: Persian khunāq for diphtheria

3. Other uses: constriction of places, time, and vessel space

A narrow mountain pass

An important use of this family is al-khāniq for a narrow passage.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah states:

فَالْخَانِقُ: الشِّعْبُ الضَّيِّقُ

fa-al-khāniqu al-shiʿbu al-ḍayyiqu

That is: “Al-khāniq is a narrow mountain pass.”

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise uses mukhtanaq al-shiʿb for the constricted part of a mountain passage.

This concerns restricted passage space, rather than bodily suffocation.

One can picture a route between rock walls whose width diminishes as the walls approach one another.

The image directly relates to narrowness.

The same sources report that some Yemenis called a narrow alley khāniq.

This shows that the family also describes constricted space. It does not by itself establish the direction or date of a semantic development from throat to place, or the reverse.

It should not be assumed that every narrow alley or pass was necessarily called khāniq in every Arabic dialect. The sources record a particular usage.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: throat, illness, passage, and trough

Constricted time

In another usage, khanaqa al-waqta means postponing something until the time available to do it becomes narrow and restricted.

For example, someone may repeatedly delay a task and ultimately have little time left to complete it.

Time is here imagined as a space that can be broad or narrow.

The lexicons also record an expression from this family concerning delaying ṣalāh.

This usage should not change the Qur’anic meaning of munkhaniqah. Al-Māʾidah 5:3 concerns an animal affected by strangulation, rather than limited time.

Using khanq for a limited opportunity likewise does not make delay or procrastination the root’s meaning in every context.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Majmaʿ Biḥār: time narrowed through delay

Filling a vessel excessively

Early Arabic records khannaqa al-ḥawḍa for filling a watering trough until little empty space remains.

In this image, constriction concerns the remaining space for water or other contents.

When a trough is filled nearly to its rim, its free space diminishes.

This differs from constriction of the throat while relating to the general core of restricted space.

Death or strangulation should not be transferred into this usage either. A full vessel has not suffocated; it is described through a family expressing constriction and limitation.

4. Evidence from early Arabic poetry and usage

A full trough in verse attributed to Abū al-Najm

A rajaz passage attributed to Abū al-Najm al-ʿIjlī reads:

ثُمَّ طَبَاهَا ذُو حَبَابٍ مُتْرَعُ
مُخَنَّقٌ بِمَائِهِ مُدَعْدَعُ

thumma ṭabāhā dhū ḥabābin mutraʿu / mukhannaqun bi-māʾihi mudaʿdaʿu

Translation:

“Then a brimming, foamy trough, filled completely with water, drew them toward it.”

Mukhannaqun bi-māʾihi here describes a trough filled with water.

Mutraʿ denotes fullness, and mudaʿdaʿ in this witness is likewise explained through filling, as in Kitāb al-Jīm’s entry for daʿdaʿah. Movement or rolling in other uses should not necessarily make waves in the water part of this adjective’s meaning here.

Mukhannaq does not describe a trough that literally has a throat or has been strangled. It concerns filling the vessel until its empty space is restricted.

This witness shows that early Arabic could use KH N Q to describe constriction caused by an abundance of contents.

Abū al-Najm belongs to the Umayyad period. The rajaz is therefore evidence from early Arabic of the Islamic period, rather than pre-Islamic poetry.

Kitāb al-Jīm: daʿdaʿah as filling and the rajaz witness

Mirage and covered heights in Dhū al-Rummah’s poetry

A verse attributed to Dhū al-Rummah reads:

وَقَدْ خَنَّقَ الْآلُ الشِّعَافَ وَغَرَّقَتْ
جَوَارِيهِ جُذْعَانَ الْقِضَافِ النَّوَابِكِ

wa-qad khannaqa al-ālu al-shiʿāfa wa-gharraqat / jawārīhi judhʿāna al-qiḍāfi al-nawābiki

Translation:

“The mirage has risen almost to cover the mountain heights, and its flowing waves have submerged the smaller rises of the ground.”

Al-āl here is mirage, while al-shiʿāf denotes high parts and mountain summits.

Lexical commentaries explain khannaqa al-ālu al-shiʿāfa as a mirage rising in the view until it seems almost to reach and envelop the summits.

In this poetic image, khannaqa thus concerns the narrowing and covering of the space around the heights.

Different transmissions give endings including al-barātiki and al-nawābiki. This variation does not fundamentally change the meaning of khannaqa al-ālu al-shiʿāfa.

Dhū al-Rummah is an Umayyad poet. This witness also belongs to the literary Arabic of the early Islamic centuries.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: Dhū al-Rummah’s witness and the rising mirage

Khanq in animal husbandry

Tying a rope around an animal’s neck has been one way of keeping and restraining livestock.

Lexicons record inkhanaqat al-shātu for a sheep or goat undergoing strangulation.

A rope, cord, or another obstruction may exert pressure on the animal’s neck in such a situation.

Early accounts also describe an animal putting its head between two tree branches and becoming unable to free itself.

These usages show that inkhanaqa was familiar in Arabic for an animal’s condition of strangulation.

Two kinds of evidence should nevertheless be distinguished. Independent lexical dictionaries record the verb inkhanaqa, whereas some examples involving sheep and tree branches occur in explanations of the Al-Māʾidah verse itself.

Such examples can illustrate a possible case of strangulation. Without independent evidence, they should not be presented as a general, established custom of all pre-Islamic Arab tribes.

5. Qur’anic usage: al-munkhaniqatu in Al-Māʾidah 5:3

Al-Māʾidah states:

حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةُ وَالدَّمُ وَلَحْمُ الْخِنْزِيرِ وَمَا أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ بِهِ وَالْمُنْخَنِقَةُ وَالْمَوْقُوذَةُ وَالْمُتَرَدِّيَةُ وَالنَّطِيحَةُ وَمَا أَكَلَ السَّبُعُ إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ

ḥurrimat ʿalaykumu al-maytatu wa-al-damu wa-laḥmu al-khinzīri wa-mā uhilla li-ghayri allāhi bihi wa-al-munkhaniqatu wa-al-mawqūdhatu wa-al-mutaraddiyatu wa-al-naṭīḥatu wa-mā akala al-sabuʿu illā mā dhakkaytum

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, swine flesh, what has been proclaimed over for other than The God, the strangled animal, the animal struck down by a blow, the fallen animal, the gored animal, and what a predator has eaten from, except what you have subjected to tadhkiyah.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:3

This passage places several different animal conditions alongside one another.

Al-munkhaniqatu is the animal affected by strangulation.

Al-mawqūdhatu concerns an animal severely injured or killed by a blow.

Al-mutaraddiyatu concerns falling from a height.

Al-naṭīḥatu is an animal injured or killed by goring.

Mā akala al-sabuʿu refers to an animal from which a predator has eaten.

These words do not all name new animal species. They describe different conditions or causes of injury and death.

The morphology of al-munkhaniqatu

Munkhaniqah is the feminine active participle of inkhanaqa, Form VII.

Its masculine form is munkhaniq and its feminine form munkhaniqah.

The pattern can be displayed as follows:

Khanaqa: to strangle.

Inkhanaqa: to undergo strangulation.

Munkhaniq: affected by strangulation.

Munkhaniqah: the feminine form of that description.

The distinction between khanaqa and inkhanaqa matters here.

Khanaqa is normally transitive, introducing someone or something as the agent applying pressure to the throat.

Inkhanaqa primarily describes the condition of the being undergoing strangulation.

Munkhaniqah therefore identifies the animal in terms of its condition of strangulation, rather than necessarily identifying the agent within the word itself.

For example, a sheep or goat may undergo strangulation when its neck becomes trapped in a rope or between branches.

In another situation, a person may cause strangulation by binding or compressing the animal’s neck.

Both can physically result in strangulation, while involving different actions and agents.

An active participle of Form VII should therefore not be taken to mean that the animal necessarily and knowingly strangled itself.

Form VII can express a condition occurring in its grammatical subject without that subject intentionally causing it.

Munkhaniqah and makhnūqah

Makhnūqah is the feminine passive participle of khanaqa.

It can denote an animal subjected to the act of strangling.

Munkhaniqah, however, is the active participle of inkhanaqa.

In his explanation of Al-Māʾidah 5:3, Al-Ṭabarī reports an interpretation involving deliberate strangling, then prefers strangulation in a tether or a place where the neck becomes trapped. He uses the distinction between munkhaniqah and makhnūqah to support that preference.

Al-Ṭabarī’s exegetical preference draws on morphology. In this article’s linguistic analysis, however, the difference in how the action is presented is not made an absolute separation between every possible referent.

Someone may tie a rope around an animal’s neck so that it undergoes strangulation. The human action causes the strangulation, while the animal’s condition can also be described by inkhanaqa.

The main difference is thus the perspective of the verb: makhnūqah highlights the strangling done to the animal, while munkhaniqah highlights its condition of undergoing strangulation.

Morphology alone does not establish that the Qur’anic usage includes only accidental strangulation without human involvement. This is the article’s linguistic analysis, rather than a restatement of Al-Ṭabarī’s preferred conclusion reported above.

Al-Ṭabarī: his exegetical preference and the munkhaniqah–makhnūqah distinction

Must munkhaniqah mean a dead animal?

Lexically, inkhanaqa can also describe an animal undergoing strangulation while still alive.

If a rope compresses a sheep’s neck and interferes with breathing, strangulation has occurred even if the animal is rescued before death.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, however, the word belongs to a context of prohibited foods and is preceded by al-maytatu, carrion.

The context concerns an animal whose strangulation has caused death or placed it at risk of death.

The phrase illā mā dhakkaytum also follows.

The animal’s condition before death must therefore be distinguished from that of an animal that has died through strangulation.

Strangulation is a condition or process; death is a possible result.

In this Qur’anic usage, the food prohibition concerns an animal dying in that way. The tadhkiyah exception raises the matter of reaching the animal before its life has ended.

This distinction within the verse should not be lost by making death part of the fixed definition of every derivative.

Illā mā dhakkaytum and the animal that is still alive

Illā mā dhakkaytum appears at the end of this group of animal conditions.

An important early interpretation relates the exception to animals affected by strangulation, a blow, a fall, goring, or a predator’s attack that are reached and subjected to tadhkiyah before death.

In this interpretation, an animal already dead through strangulation can no longer undergo tadhkiyah as a living animal.

An animal still alive and subjected to tadhkiyah in time has a different condition from one that has already died.

Legal and exegetical sources differ over the exception’s scope and the signs sufficient to establish remaining life.

Those differences cannot be resolved through the meaning of munkhaniqah alone.

Lexically, two points are clear: inkhanaqa denotes undergoing strangulation, and that condition does not by itself determine whether the animal is already dead or still alive at that moment.

The ruling on tadhkiyah, its conditions, and the scope of the exception require examining the whole verse and other relevant food texts.

Munkhaniqah should consequently not be defined as “an animal from which no benefit is possible under any circumstances.” That would place the disputed legal result inside the word’s lexical definition in advance.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing scopes of the tadhkiyah exception and remaining life

Why mention the strangled animal separately from carrion?

Al-maytatu occurs at the verse’s beginning, followed by several specific conditions of animal death.

This raises a question: if an animal dies through strangulation, is it not carrion in its resulting condition?

As regards its life status, an animal dead before tadhkiyah falls within the general field of carrion.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, however, the Qur’an names particular causes and conditions alongside the general category.

This detail matters because people may distinguish in practice between an animal dying on its own and one dying through pressure, a blow, or an accident.

By naming these conditions, the verse brings them within its food ruling.

Early historical explanations also report that some people ate strangled animals without regarding them as carrion in the same way as animals dying from illness or natural causes.

These reports help describe the verse’s social setting. They do not establish that all Arabs everywhere deliberately killed their animals by strangulation.

The Qur’anic text makes clear that how the animal dies is not irrelevant to the food ruling.

The connection with necessity

At the end of Al-Māʾidah 5:3, after the prohibitions, the verse describes someone compelled by necessity in a state of makhmaṣah.

This belongs to KH M Ṣ, rather than KH N Q.

Study of KH M Ṣ

The strangled animal must therefore be distinguished from the human compelled by severe hunger.

The first concerns the animal’s condition and how it dies.

The second concerns the human’s condition and genuine restrictions on capacity and choice.

The verse addresses both within one legislative passage, but their lexical meanings differ.

Necessity is not part of the meaning of munkhaniqah, just as strangulation is not part of makhmaṣah.

6. Distinctions from related roots

KH N Q (khanq) and Ḍ Y Q (ḍīq): strangulation and constriction

In Arabic, Ḍ Y Q covers a broad field of narrowness and restriction of space or circumstances.

Ibn Fāris also relates KH N Q to constriction, but its familiar usage has a special connection with pressure on the throat and strangulation.

An alley can be narrow without strangling anyone.

Pressure from a rope on the throat, by contrast, can interfere with breathing.

Khanq and ḍīq overlap in restriction, but not every restriction is strangulation.

Qur’anic ḍīq also appears in psychological and social settings, whereas munkhaniqah in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 concerns an animal’s physical condition.

KH N Q (khanq) and Ḥ L Q (ḥalq): pressure and its location

Arabic ḥalq denotes the throat; its lexical family also concerns shaving.

Khanq denotes an action or condition involving pressure on the throat and strangulation.

The throat is thus the location of khanq, rather than its meaning.

The shaving sense of ḥalq should likewise not be conflated with pressure on the throat.

Sharing a bodily location does not make two roots synonymous.

Study of Ḥ L Q

KH N Q (khanq) and Q T L (qatl): strangling and killing

Qatl concerns taking life and killing.

Khanq concerns pressure and strangulation.

Strangulation can cause killing, but these are not one action with one definition.

Someone may undergo strangulation and be rescued. Khanq has occurred, but killing has not.

Killing can also occur through causes other than strangulation.

This distinction matters in Al-Māʾidah 5:3. Munkhaniqah identifies the condition or cause of strangulation; the animal’s death is understood from the context of food and dying.

Study of Q T L

KH N Q (khanq) and W Q DH (waqdh): pressure on the throat and a blow

W Q DH concerns a severe blow and being struck down through it.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 places al-mawqūdhatu immediately after al-munkhaniqatu.

Their juxtaposition shows that the verse names two different conditions.

An animal can undergo strangulation through throat pressure, or be injured and die through a severe blow.

Both causes can occur in some accidents, but they are not linguistically identical.

Munkhaniqah should therefore not be translated as “an animal struck by a blow,” just as mawqūdhah does not by itself mean strangled.

KH N Q (khanq) and N Ḥ R (naḥr): strangulation and naḥr

In reference to animals, naḥr denotes a method of killing at the throat and lower neck.

Khanq instead concerns pressure and constriction of the airway.

The proximity of their locations does not make the actions identical.

Naḥr involves a particular act of killing an animal, whereas pressure and impaired breathing are central to khanq.

Al-munkhaniqatu cannot consequently provide every detail of naḥr or tadhkiyah.

Study of N Ḥ R

KH N Q (khanq) and DH B Ḥ (dhabḥ): strangulation and cutting

Arabic dhabḥ concerns cutting at an animal’s neck.

Khanq, by contrast, is characterized by compressing and constricting the throat passage.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, distinguishing these actions matters for examining the tadhkiyah exception.

An animal may undergo pressure and strangulation before tadhkiyah. If still alive, timely tadhkiyah becomes the issue; if its life has already ended, the situation differs.

Undergoing strangulation and undergoing tadhkiyah should therefore not be merged into each other’s meanings.

The details of correct tadhkiyah likewise do not come from KH N Q itself.

Study of DH B Ḥ

KH N Q (khanq) and GH Ṣ Ṣ (ghaṣaṣ): strangulation and something lodged in the throat

Arabic ghuṣṣah concerns something lodged in the throat and difficulty swallowing it.

A person may experience this while eating.

It can cause difficulty breathing or a sense of choking, but its origin differs from external pressure on the neck.

In lexical evidence, khanq and ghuṣṣah sometimes appear together in descriptions of a severe condition.

This closeness does not make the families complete synonyms.

Ghuṣṣah primarily concerns an internal obstruction and difficulty in the throat, whereas khanq in its familiar use concerns pressure and constriction of the airway.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

One Persian equivalent is insufficient for every use of KH N Q.

For transitive khanaqa, “strangled” or “compressed the throat” is appropriate.

For inkhanaqa, “underwent strangulation” or “was strangled” conveys the sense.

Spatial al-khāniq can be rendered “a narrow passage.”

As an implement, al-khināq is a cord or rope applying pressure to the neck; mikhnaqah as an ornament is a necklace.

The sole Qur’anic use, however, concerns an animal.

Proposed translation of wa-al-munkhaniqatu in Al-Māʾidah 5:3:

“And the strangled animal.”

This is a natural rendering that conveys the contextual meaning well.

Lexical explanation should clarify that the Arabic form is an active participle of Form VII, primarily describing the animal’s condition of strangulation.

Because the verse concerns food and prohibitions, strangulation here relates to an animal dying from it, while the tadhkiyah exception addresses reaching the animal before its life ends.

Brief root meaning

Constriction and pressure, especially pressure on the throat causing difficulty breathing or cessation of breathing.

Meaning in this verse

Al-munkhaniqatu — Al-Māʾidah 5:3: an animal affected by strangulation; in the verse’s context, one that has died from it, with regard to the exception involving tadhkiyah before death.

These definitions should remain separate.

The brief root meaning identifies the general field of constriction and pressure, whereas the contextual meaning concerns the animal’s particular condition in the food discussion.

Conclusion

KH N Q concerns constriction and pressure in Arabic. In its main, familiar usage, pressure on the throat makes breathing difficult or stops it.

The same family supplies words for a neck cord, a necklace, throat illnesses, a narrow passage, and even an excessively filled vessel. All connect with constriction, but their referents and consequences differ.

The Qur’an contains only al-munkhaniqatu, in Al-Māʾidah 5:3. As an active participle of Form VII, it describes an animal undergoing strangulation, rather than necessarily naming the agent causing it.

In context, strangulation is one condition that can cause an animal’s death. Yet undergoing strangulation and dying from strangulation are not lexically identical. This distinction matters for understanding illā mā dhakkaytum.

The root’s fundamental meaning can therefore be given as “constriction and pressure, especially on the throat and airway.” The Qur’anic derivative munkhaniqah can be understood as “an animal affected by strangulation that has died from it within the food prohibition’s context.”

This distinction preserves Arabic usage while allowing the food ruling and tadhkiyah conditions to be examined through the whole verse, without placing a legal conclusion inside the lexical definition in advance. Brief root meaning: Constriction and pressure, especially pressure on the throat causing difficulty breathing or cessation of breathing.
