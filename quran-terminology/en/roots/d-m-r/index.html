Widespread destruction and ruin: from demolished structures to societies’ collapse and human responsibility

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic triliteral D M R has two important branches: perishing, destruction, and severe ruin; and entering or bursting into a house or another place.

Familiar Arabic damār means destruction and ruin. Damara al-qawmu describes people perishing, while dammarahum means destroying them.

Concerning buildings and places, tadmīr can describe a structure’s ruin or the destruction of a settlement’s remains.

Destroying people and buildings have different objects. A society’s destruction may involve deaths, lost resources, or collapsed social structures; a building may be ruined without all its inhabitants being killed.

Tadmīr should therefore not always be restricted to killing people or demolishing buildings. Object and context identify what is destroyed.

Sources also record damara as entering or bursting in. Ibn Fāris makes that the root’s principle and examines other uses through it.

The historical link between entering and destroying is nevertheless uncertain. Imagining that an incursion might cause destruction does not prove that the destruction sense developed from it.

The root occurs ten times in eight Qur’anic verses, all concerning destroying and laying waste.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:137, the objects are Pharaoh’s and his people’s constructions.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:16 concerns a settlement’s destruction following disobedience and the fulfillment of its consequence.

In Al-Furqān 25:36, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:172, Al-Naml 27:51, and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:136, tadmīr concerns groups of people.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:25 identifies wind as the destructive agent.

Muḥammad 47:10 recalls earlier peoples’ destruction and outcome.

The Qur’anic field is fairly clear, but does not establish that every destructive natural event is divine punishment for people’s sins. Such a judgment requires independent evidence.

2. Qur’anic forms and morphology

D M R occurs ten times in eight verses through two Form II formations.

Form II verb dammara: eight occurrences

  • Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 — wa-dammarnā
  • Al-Isrāʾ 17:16 — fa-dammarnāhā
  • Al-Furqān 25:36 — fa-dammarnāhum
  • Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:172 — dammarnā
  • Al-Naml 27:51 — dammarnāhum
  • Al-Ṣāffāt 37:136 — dammarnā
  • Al-Aḥqāf 46:25 — tudammiru
  • Muḥammad 47:10 — dammara

Form II verbal noun tadmīr: two occurrences

  • Al-Isrāʾ 17:16 — tadmīran
  • Al-Furqān 25:36 — tadmīran

Tadmīran follows a verb from the same family in both verses.

Grammatically, the verbal noun is a cognate accusative emphasizing the action.

The verses thus reinforce destruction, highlighting its force or certainty through the repeated verbal noun.

That repetition does not establish destruction in a particular number of episodes. The cognate accusative does not itself specify frequency.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: eight verbs and two verbal nouns

Forms known in Arabic

Lexicographers record these forms:

Damara yadmuru: perished; in another construction, entered a place or burst into it.

Damār: destruction, perishing, and ruin.

Damr and dumūr: in some uses, entering or bursting in; in others, verbal-noun formations concerning destruction.

Dammara: destroyed or laid waste.

Tadmīr: destroying or laying waste.

Dammara ʿalā al-qawmi: brought destruction upon a people.

Dāmir: perished or destroyed.

Mudammar: something ruined or destroyed.

Mudammir: a destroyer; in another lexical use, a hunter entering his hide.

Tadmurī: in some early uses, a kind of small desert animal; in another use, a relation to the city of Tadmur.

Dāmara al-layla: spent the night awake or enduring hardship.

Not all are Qur’anic. The Qur’an has forms of the verb dammara and the verbal noun tadmīr.

Form I damara should not be equated with Form II dammara in every use.

Damara al-qawmu may describe a group itself perishing; transitive dammarahum attributes its destruction to an agent.

This matters for identifying subject and grammatical agency.

3. The semantic core in early dictionaries

Perishing and destruction

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الدَّمَارُ: اسْتِئْصَالُ الْهَلَاكِ

al-damāru istiʾṣālu al-halāki

“Damār is thorough destruction and perishing.”

It continues:

دَمَرَ الْقَوْمُ يَدْمُرُونَ دَمَارًا: هَلَكُوا

damara al-qawmu yadmurūna damāran halakū

“The people damara: they perished.”

Ibn Durayd’s Jamharat al-Lughah likewise associates destruction and damār.

These definitions go beyond slight damage or a simple change of condition.

A small scratch would ordinarily not suit tadmīr.

A structure losing its integrity and function, or a collection of remains and resources being destroyed, may be described through tadmīr.

Even istiʾṣāl should not be taken in every verse to mean every component disappearing without a material trace.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:25 mentions dwellings remaining after describing destructive wind.

Severe, widespread destruction can therefore leave some remains.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: damār and destruction

Ibn Fāris’s account: entering

Ibn Fāris gives D M R this principle in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الدُّخُولِ فِي الْبَيْتِ وَغَيْرِهِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-dukhūli fī al-bayti wa-ghayrihi

“One principle indicating entering a house or something else.”

He continues:

دَمَرَ الرَّجُلُ بَيْتَهُ، إِذَا دَخَلَهُ

damara al-rajulu baytahu idhā dakhalahu

“The man damara his house: he entered it.”

Some lexical reports restrict damara to entering without permission.

Ibn Fāris says not all linguists consider that restriction part of the general meaning.

He treats entering as the principle, with entry without permission a particular case.

Defining damara only as violating others’ privacy would obscure ordinary entering and its distinction from Qur’anic tadmīr.

Did destruction develop from entering and incursion?

A connection between incursion and destruction is conceivable.

A force might enter a house or city and destroy it.

The connection does not hold in every case, however.

Entering a house need not destroy it.

Earthquake, wind, or flood can destroy something without human entry into a house.

The branches’ historical relationship therefore needs further evidence.

Preserve both documented uses: entering or bursting in, and damār/tadmīr as destruction and ruin.

The second branch is clear and predominant in Qur’anic usage.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: entering and its distinction from destruction

4. Early Arabic witnesses

A hunter entering his hide

Ibn Fāris cites the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar for entering:

فَلَاقَى عَلَيْهِ مِنْ صُبَاحَ مُدَمِّرًا
لِنَامُوسِهِ مِنَ الصَّفِيحِ سَقَائِفُ

fa-lāqā ʿalayhi min ṣubāḥa mudammiran / li-nāmūsihi mina al-ṣafīḥi saqāʾifu

“So there he encountered a hunter of Ṣubāḥ, whose hide had roofing slabs of flat stone.” Ṣubāḥ is explained as a tribal name in commentary on this witness, rather than necessarily “morning.” The Maqāyīs wording is retained.

The verse is recorded in Aws’s collection. That collection and some dictionaries transmit ʿalayhā instead of ʿalayhi; the present quotation preserves Maqāyīs’s reading.

The lexical explanation takes mudammir as the hunter entering his hide.

Nāmūs here is the hunter’s shelter or hide.

Some lexicographers explain that the hunter burns a substance inside to prevent animals smelling him.

Ibn Fāris does not treat burning as the description’s principal basis. For him it concerns the hunter’s entry; fumigation may occur afterward.

This illustrates a careful distinction in root analysis.

An accompanying action in a particular situation need not be the verb’s own meaning.

Sharḥ Abyāt Mughnī al-Labīb: Ṣubāḥ and saqāʾif in the hunter witness

The witness attributed to the pre-Islamic Aws supports the hide-entry use, but the poet’s period alone does not establish this particular verse’s precise composition date.

Nor does it make Qur’anic tadmīr mean lying in ambush or fumigating.

Dīwān of Aws ibn Ḥajar: the hunter witness and ʿalayhā reading

Damara al-qunfudhu: an animal entering its burrow

Maqāyīs al-Lughah states:

دَمَرَ الْقُنْفُذُ، إِذَا دَخَلَ جُحْرَهُ

damara al-qunfudhu idhā dakhala juḥrahu

“The hedgehog damara: it entered its burrow.”

The subject is the animal moving into its burrow.

Neither destruction nor harming another is involved.

The entering sense is therefore also recorded outside human behavior.

That behavior should not become the certain historical origin of the entire family.

The witness establishes an entering use in Arabic.

Khāsirun dāmirun: loss and perishing

Lisān al-ʿArab records rajulun khāsirun dāmirun.

Khāsir concerns loss or failure; dāmir concerns perishing or destruction.

Together they show that damār can describe a person’s disastrous outcome and demise.

The adjectives are not identical.

Someone may suffer loss without being destroyed.

Perishing may be more final than limited loss.

Dāmir should therefore not always be rendered merely “suffering loss.”

Al-Farazdaq’s witness about Thamūd

The Umayyad poet Al-Farazdaq says:

وَكَانَ لَهُمْ كَبَكْرِ ثَمُودَ لَمَّا
رَغَا ظُهْرًا فَدَمَّرَهُمْ دَمَارًا

wa-kāna lahum ka-bakri thamūda lammā / raghā ẓuhran fa-dammarahum damāran

“He was for them like Thamūd’s camel foal: when it cried out at noon, it brought their destruction.” The poem’s context refers to Jarīr; this is Al-Farazdaq’s satirical comparison, not an independent account of how Thamūd perished.

Al-Farazdaq invokes Thamūd’s destruction to compare another group’s fate.

Dammarahum damāran combines a verb and a related verbal noun to describe destruction.

Its construction resembles Qur’anic fa-dammarnāhā tadmīran and fa-dammarnāhum tadmīran.

Al-Farazdaq lived after the Qur’an’s revelation, however.

This is not independent evidence of pre-Qur’anic tadmīr.

Nor does invoking a Qur’anic narrative establish the verse’s meaning independently of the Qur’an itself.

It documents the family and an emphatic construction in classical Arabic.

Al-Ṭabarī and editorial note: Al-Farazdaq’s witness replying to Jarīr

Tadmur and associated forms

Tadmur is an ancient Syrian city also known as Palmyra.

Early Arabic poetry names it.

Lisān al-ʿArab attributes a verse about building Tadmur to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī.

Similarity between the city’s name and D M R does not make its name mean “the destroyed city.”

Place names may have independent histories and linguistic origins.

Tadmurī also names a small animal in some lexical uses, without changing Qur’anic tadmīr’s meaning.

The city and similar words should therefore be considered separately from destroying.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Tadmur and lexical forms

5. Wa-dammarnā mā kāna yaṣnaʿu firʿawnu: Al-Aʿrāf 7:137

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137

وَدَمَّرْنَا مَا كَانَ يَصْنَعُ فِرْعَوْنُ وَقَوْمُهُ وَمَا كَانُوا يَعْرِشُونَ

wa-dammarnā mā kāna yaṣnaʿu firʿawnu wa-qawmuhu wa-mā kānū yaʿrishūna

“We destroyed what Pharaoh and his people made and what they raised.”

Dammarnā is a Form II perfect verb.

Its subject is “We”; the object begins with mā kāna yaṣnaʿu.

The direct objects here are Pharaoh’s and his people’s works and constructions.

Buildings and manufactured works

Tadmīr concerns destruction of human constructions here.

Yaṣnaʿu belongs to Ṣ N ʿ and concerns making or producing through work and skill.

Yaʿrishūna belongs to ʿ R SH and concerns erecting structures in this context.

Their fields are close, but not identical.

Everything named should therefore not be reduced to one type of building.

Dammarnā itself specifies neither construction materials nor the means of destruction.

Engineering and historical details are absent from the expression itself.

Relation to the drowning of Pharaoh’s people

Al-Aʿrāf 7:136 describes Pharaoh’s group drowning in the sea.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 then describes the oppressed group inheriting and Pharaoh’s works being destroyed.

They concern one historical outcome while contributing different meanings.

Fa-aghraqnāhum in Al-Aʿrāf 7:136 concerns drowning.

Wa-dammarnā in Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 concerns destroying works and structures.

The passage describes both people’s fate and the loss of their power’s material works and resources.

It should not be generalized without evidence to destroying every Egyptian achievement or every inhabitant.

The direct object is specified: what Pharaoh and his people made and erected.

Power and structural impermanence

Pharaoh’s works and constructions formed part of his power and resources here.

Whether a structure survives does not itself establish the truth or falsehood of its maker’s beliefs.

An oppressive person’s building may be technically sound and useful.

A building made for a good purpose may perish in a natural event.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 locates tadmīr within Pharaoh’s oppression and narrative.

It does not establish that every ruined structure belonged to oppressors.

6. Fa-dammarnāhā tadmīran: Al-Isrāʾ 17:16

Al-Isrāʾ 17:16

وَإِذَا أَرَدْنَا أَنْ نُهْلِكَ قَرْيَةً أَمَرْنَا مُتْرَفِيهَا فَفَسَقُوا فِيهَا فَحَقَّ عَلَيْهَا الْقَوْلُ فَدَمَّرْنَاهَا تَدْمِيرًا

wa-idhā aradnā an nuhlika qaryatan amarnā mutrafīhā fa-fasaqū fīhā fa-ḥaqqa ʿalayhā al-qawlu fa-dammarnāhā tadmīran

“When We intend to destroy a settlement, We command its affluent people, but they transgress within it; then the word becomes due against it, and We destroy it utterly.”

Fa-dammarnāhā is a Form II perfect verb; -hā refers to the settlement.

Tadmīran is an accusative verbal noun functioning as a cognate accusative.

It emphasizes destruction’s force and certainty.

Qaryah: settlement and human community

Qur’anic qaryah can name a place of habitation and its community.

Its destruction may concern buildings, inhabitants perishing, social life collapsing, or a combination; context must decide.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:16 concerns the conduct and transgression of its affluent people.

It is therefore not merely the demolition of an uninhabited collection of buildings.

Human conduct plays a role in the settlement’s fate.

Dammarnāhā itself does not specify the precise manner of destruction.

Amarnā mutrafīhā and the command

Amarnā mutrafīhā has prompted lexical and interpretive discussion.

In the common reading, amarnā belongs to the verb commanding.

A familiar interpretation has God commanding the affluent, who disobey.

Reported readings including āmarnā and ammarnā have also been associated with increasing the affluent’s numbers or placing them in authority.

This concerns ʾ M R, not D M R.

Here the key point is that fa-fasaqū describes the affluent’s disobedient conduct.

Tadmīr itself does not establish that God commanded corruption.

That interpretation would require argument from the entire verse and the passages on command, responsibility, and divine justice.

Relation to Al-Isrāʾ 17:15

The preceding verse says:

وَلَا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ

wa-lā taziru wāziratun wizra ukhrā

“No bearer of a burden bears another’s burden.”

It also states that punishment does not occur without sending a messenger.

Those principles matter for Al-Isrāʾ 17:16.

A settlement’s destruction should not make every innocent person morally guilty solely through someone else’s conduct.

Powerful groups’ conduct can nevertheless affect many people’s lives socially.

Personal moral responsibility differs from the social consequences of collective conduct.

Bad policy or widespread corruption may damage a society’s economy, security, and environment without every victim sharing equal moral responsibility.

That distinction agrees with personal responsibility in the Qur’an.

The affluent’s role

Mutrafīhā names a particular group.

It belongs to T R F and concerns affluence and pampered ease.

The verse highlights the privileged group’s role in the settlement’s condition.

Tadmīr itself does not make every wealthy person corrupt or economic resources a sign of imminent destruction.

The subject is disobedient conduct and its consequence.

Enjoying provision should also be distinguished from abusing position and power oppressively.

Is tadmīr a process’s end or its first event?

The verse names several stages: command, disobedience, fulfillment of the word, then destruction.

Tadmīr concludes that sequence here.

It does not therefore always mean the final stage of a long process in Arabic.

Sudden ruin can also be called tadmīr.

This particular ordering comes from the sentence, rather than the root’s fixed meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: amarnā, readings, disobedience, and tadmīr

7. Fa-dammarnāhum tadmīran: Al-Furqān 25:36

Al-Furqān 25:36

فَقُلْنَا اذْهَبَا إِلَى الْقَوْمِ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا فَدَمَّرْنَاهُمْ تَدْمِيرًا

fa-qulnā idhhabā ilā al-qawmi alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā fa-dammarnāhum tadmīran

“We said: Go, both of you, to the people who denied Our signs; then We destroyed them utterly.”

The preceding verse introduces Moses and Aaron.

They are commanded to approach a people who denied the divine signs.

The group’s destruction follows.

Fa-dammarnāhum takes people as its object, rather than buildings.

Difference from Al-Aʿrāf 7:137

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 directly concerns Pharaoh’s works and constructions.

In Al-Furqān 25:36, -hum refers to the people.

Tadmīr can therefore concern people and their constructions.

It should not be defined merely as demolishing buildings.

Dammarnāhum alone also does not establish their precise manner of death or destruction.

Other Moses passages describe Pharaoh and his forces drowning.

Those may clarify the event through the narrative network, but dammara itself does not mean drowning.

Emphasis through tadmīran

The verbal noun tadmīran again follows the verb.

It reinforces severe, widespread destruction.

It does not establish two stages of destruction or every material trace vanishing forever.

The cognate accusative emphasizes the action, rather than independently reporting stages or material details.

Relation to other groups

Al-Furqān 25:37, Al-Furqān 25:38, and Al-Furqān 25:39 also name Noah’s people, ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Al-Rass, and other generations.

The deniers’ outcome recurs across the passage.

Different events or means may nevertheless have destroyed each group.

Shared destruction terminology does not establish an identical means of perishing for all.

8. Thumma dammarnā al-ākharīna: Lot’s people

This expression occurs twice:

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:172

ثُمَّ دَمَّرْنَا الْآخَرِينَ

thumma dammarnā al-ākharīna

“Then We destroyed the others.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:136

ثُمَّ دَمَّرْنَا الْآخَرِينَ

thumma dammarnā al-ākharīna

“Then We destroyed the others.”

Dammarnā is perfect and al-ākharīna its object in both.

Both belong to Lot’s narrative.

Who are “the others” contrasted with?

Preceding verses describe Lot and his companions being saved.

Al-ākharīna names those outside the saved group.

Understand “the others” in contrast to that preceding group.

It does not mean everyone in the world or everyone living elsewhere.

The subject is the society and groups introduced in context.

Deliverance and destruction

These passages place najjaynā and dammarnā alongside one another.

Najjaynā belongs to N J W, concerning rescue and deliverance.

Dammarnā belongs to D M R, concerning destruction.

They express different outcomes.

D M R itself does not mean separating the righteous from wrongdoers.

That separation comes from the narrative’s complete construction and independent verbs.

Is tadmīr identical with punitive rain?

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:173 follows thumma dammarnā al-ākharīna with:

وَأَمْطَرْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ مَطَرًا

wa-amṭarnā ʿalayhim maṭaran

“We rained a rain upon them.”

Other Lot passages describe settlements being overturned and stones raining down.

Tadmīr is thus associated with events such as punitive rain within the narrative network.

Dammara itself does not mean raining.

Nor should the order of the two clauses in Al-Shuʿarāʾ yield a precise independent chronology of every stage without examining other verses.

Lexical study preserves tadmīr’s meaning and its relationship with other narrative verbs.

9. Dammarnāhum wa-qawmahum ajmaʿīna: Al-Naml 27:51

Al-Naml 27:51

فَانْظُرْ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ مَكْرِهِمْ أَنَّا دَمَّرْنَاهُمْ وَقَوْمَهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

fa-unẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu makrihim annā dammarnāhum wa-qawmahum ajmaʿīna

“See how their plotting ended: We destroyed them and their people altogether.”

This belongs to Ṣāliḥ’s narrative and a group from Thamūd.

Al-Naml 27:48 names nine corrupt groups or persons in the city.

Al-Naml 27:49 describes plotting a night attack against Ṣāliḥ and his household, then denying responsibility.

Their plotting and its outcome follow.

Plotting and destruction

Al-Naml 27:51 follows their plotting with destruction.

Tadmīr here concerns the outcome of deliberate aggression.

D M R itself does not mean plotting or a plan’s failure.

Makr has an independent semantic family.

Tadmīr expresses the destructive outcome.

They are associated narratively without being lexical equivalents.

Wa-qawmahum ajmaʿīna

The phrase describes the corrupt group’s destruction alongside their people.

Ajmaʿīna emphasizes comprehensiveness regarding the group in question.

Shortly afterward, Al-Naml 27:53 describes saving those who believed and were mindful.

Qawmahum ajmaʿīna should therefore not be taken to deny any person from that society being saved under any circumstances.

Al-Naml 27:52 describes the remaining houses; Al-Naml 27:53 identifies the saved group.

Comprehensive expressions such as ajmaʿīna require their referent and the full context.

Remaining houses

Al-Naml 27:52 says:

فَتِلْكَ بُيُوتُهُمْ خَاوِيَةً بِمَا ظَلَمُوا

fa-tilka buyūtuhum khāwiyatan bi-mā ẓalamū

“Those are their houses, left empty because of their wrongdoing.”

This matters for tadmīr.

Al-Naml 27:51 describes their destruction; Al-Naml 27:52 points to remaining, empty houses.

Destroying a society does not necessarily erase every material trace.

Inhabitants and social life may perish while buildings or their remains survive.

This agrees with Al-Aḥqāf 46:25.

Al-Ṭabarī: believers saved after the account of the houses

10. Tudammiru kulla shayʾin: destructive wind in Al-Aḥqāf 46:25

Al-Aḥqāf 46:25

تُدَمِّرُ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ بِأَمْرِ رَبِّهَا فَأَصْبَحُوا لَا يُرَىٰ إِلَّا مَسَاكِنُهُمْ كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي الْقَوْمَ الْمُجْرِمِينَ

tudammiru kulla shayʾin bi-amri rabbihā fa-aṣbaḥū lā yurā illā masākinuhum kadhālika najzī al-qawma al-mujrimīna

“Destroying everything by its Lord’s command. They became such that only their dwellings could be seen. Thus We recompense criminal people.”

Tudammiru is a Form II imperfect verb.

Its subject in the immediate context is the wind.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:24 describes the wind, initially mistaken by ʿĀd for a rain-bearing cloud.

The Qur’an instead identifies it as carrying painful punishment.

The wind as destructive agent

Most Qur’anic uses of this family directly attribute destruction to God.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:25 attributes the verb to the wind.

The distinction matters.

Destruction can occur through an agent within the natural world in Qur’anic language.

Bi-amri rabbihā simultaneously relates it to its Lord’s command.

Tadmīr therefore does not concern only immediate action without a natural agent.

Nor does the root alone reveal the wind’s scientific character, speed, air pressure, or precise destructive mechanism.

The verse does not give those details.

Kulla shayʾin: everything within what scope?

Tudammiru kulla shayʾin initially sounds comprehensive.

It is immediately followed by only their dwellings remaining visible.

“Everything” therefore requires the scope of the destruction and what the wind was commanded to destroy.

Destroying everything in the universe would contradict the continuation.

Even that people’s dwellings remained in some form that could be seen.

The comprehensiveness concerns the event and its objects.

The limit should not be arbitrarily derived from kull alone; context and the following clause supply it.

Did the houses remain completely intact?

The verse says only their dwellings could be seen.

It does not establish that every house was structurally intact.

Some walls, buildings, or traces of habitation may have remained.

Nor should every building be declared completely flattened without evidence, since the verse mentions seeing them.

Material traces remaining differs from buildings remaining fully intact.

Widespread social destruction can leave some traces behind.

Is every destructive wind punishment for its victims’ sins?

ʿĀd’s narrative connects their conduct with their fate.

That judgment should not be applied without evidence to every contemporary storm, tornado, or climatic event.

Winds and storms may follow known natural processes and harm both good people and wrongdoers.

Qur’anic trials, suffering, and worldly harm do not always mean direct punishment for a victim’s own sin.

Assigning a particular natural event as a particular group’s moral punishment requires evidence.

D M R expresses destruction and ruin, rather than identifying every ruin’s moral cause.

Al-Ṭabarī: the scope of everything and the remaining dwellings

11. Dammara Allāhu ʿalayhim: Muḥammad 47:10

Muḥammad 47:10

أَفَلَمْ يَسِيرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ فَيَنْظُرُوا كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ دَمَّرَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلِلْكَافِرِينَ أَمْثَالُهَا

a-fa-lam yasīrū fī al-arḍi fa-yanẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu alladhīna min qablihim dammara allāhu ʿalayhim wa-li-al-kāfirīna amthāluhā

“Have they not traveled through the earth to see the outcome of those before them? God brought destruction upon them; for the disbelievers are similar outcomes.”

Dammara is perfect here.

God is its subject.

Unlike some other uses, no direct object follows; ʿalayhim appears instead.

Dammara ʿalayhim

Arabic also records dammara ʿalayhi for bringing destruction upon a person or group.

Lisān al-ʿArab places it alongside dammarahu and treats their meanings as close.

Dammara Allāhu ʿalayhim can therefore describe destruction brought upon them.

ʿAlā does not establish buildings necessarily falling onto their heads from above.

That might occur in a real event, but the expression itself does not specify it.

“Brought destruction upon them” conveys the Arabic construction well.

Earlier peoples’ remains as signs

The verse begins by inviting travel and observation of predecessors’ outcomes.

Remains, lands, and histories may thus provide understanding and lessons.

Dammara itself does not mean traveling or observing remains.

Yasīrū and fa-yanẓurū supply those meanings.

Nor should every historical ruin be assumed punishment for its inhabitants’ disbelief or oppression.

A particular city’s or civilization’s destruction requires historical, archaeological, and other reliable evidence to identify its cause.

The Qur’an gives particular examples of oppressive societies’ destruction, without authorizing guesses about every ruin worldwide.

12. Differences from related roots

D M R (dammara) and H L K (halaka): destruction and perishing

Halāk is one of tadmīr’s closest semantic families.

Dictionaries explain damār through halāk.

Qur’anic halāk ranges across death, personal demise, a society’s destruction, and something ending.

Qur’anic tadmīr foregrounds severe destruction of people or their works.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:16 has both: nuhlika qaryatan first, then fa-dammarnāhā tadmīran.

Their association shows proximity without making all Arabic uses identical.

Perishing supplies the general subject here; tadmīr emphatically describes destruction at the process’s end.

Study of H L K

D M R (dammara) and KH R B (khariba): destruction and ruin

Kharāb concerns buildings or habitation becoming ruined or unusable.

Tadmīr, too, can concern demolished buildings.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 clearly illustrates the overlap.

Tadmīr is not limited to buildings, however: Al-Furqān 25:36 and Al-Naml 27:51 concern people.

Demolishing a building is one instance, rather than a complete definition.

Nor is every minor defect widespread tadmīr.

Study of KH R B

D M R (dammara) and T B R (tabbara): destruction and shattering

Al-Furqān 25:39 says:

وَكُلًّا تَبَّرْنَا تَتْبِيرًا

wa-kullan tabbarnā tatbīran

“Each one We shattered utterly.”

Qur’anic T B R concerns breaking, ruining, and destroying.

Earlier in the same surah appears fa-dammarnāhum tadmīran.

Their families overlap in destruction.

Some lexical tatbīr uses foreground breaking and scattering.

Tadmīr denotes severe destruction and perishing.

They cannot be interchanged universally without examination.

Study of T B R

D M R (dammara) and D K K (dakka): destruction and pounding down

Qur’anic dakk concerns being pounded flat or collapsing.

Al-Fajr 89:21, for example, describes the earth being pounded.

Tadmīr encompasses destruction more broadly.

A building may be destroyed by pounding or collapse, but tadmīr does not necessarily name that particular mechanical motion.

Wind causes destruction in Al-Aḥqāf 46:25, without the verb giving its precise mechanism.

“Pounded and flattened” should therefore not replace tadmīr in every verse.

D M R (dammara) and Q Ṣ M (qaṣama): destruction and breaking apart

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11 says:

وَكَمْ قَصَمْنَا مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ كَانَتْ ظَالِمَةً

wa-kam qaṣamnā min qaryatin kānat ẓālimatan

“How many settlements that were oppressive We broke apart.”

Qaṣm concerns breaking and severe shattering in Arabic.

Tadmīr, too, can concern a settlement’s destruction.

Qaṣm foregrounds breaking, whereas tadmīr concerns perishing and destruction.

Their outcomes may be close without their direct meanings being entirely identical.

D M R (dammara) and ʿ M R (ʿamara): ruin and habitation

ʿUmrān concerns building, developing, and sustaining life in a place.

Tadmīr can describe destroying works and habitation.

They can therefore oppose one another in uses concerning cities and society.

ʿUmrān itself does not always mean justice or right conduct.

A society may have extensive buildings, economic resources, and facilities while practicing oppression and corruption.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 makes Pharaoh’s works the object of destruction.

Material or architectural advancement alone does not establish moral rightness and stability.

Nor does material ruin prove inhabitants’ moral corruption without its cause being known.

Study of ʿ M R

D M R (dammara) and F S D (fasada): destruction and corruption

Qur’anic fasād broadly encompasses corrupted conduct, social relations, land, and other conditions.

Tadmīr concerns destruction and ruin.

Corruption may prepare a society’s destruction, but the concepts differ.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:16 places the affluent’s transgression before destruction.

Al-Naml 27:48, Al-Naml 27:49, Al-Naml 27:50, Al-Naml 27:51, and Al-Naml 27:52 connect a Thamūd group’s corruption and plotting with destruction.

Every minor corruption should not be assumed immediately destructive of an entire society.

A natural event may also destroy a building without moral corruption causing it.

Examine corruption and destruction’s relationship within each context.

Study of F S D

D M R (dammara) and D M D M (damdama): destruction and overwhelming crushing

Al-Shams 91:14 says:

فَدَمْدَمَ عَلَيْهِمْ رَبُّهُمْ بِذَنْبِهِمْ

fa-damdama ʿalayhim rabbuhum bi-dhanbihim

“Their Lord overwhelmed them because of their sin.”

D M D M resembles D M R in sound but is an independent quadriliteral root.

Both can concern a group’s destructive fate in Qur’anic usage.

Their sounds do not make them the same verb.

In Al-Shams, damdama foregrounds overwhelming crushing and the encompassing consequence; tadmīr describes destruction and perishing.

Each belongs in an independent root study.

Study of D M D M

13. Translation proposals for Forqan

The Qur’an has forms of dammara and tadmīr.

Principal Persian equivalents mean “destroy,” “lay waste,” and “destruction” or “ruin.”

Choosing “destroy” or “lay waste” should follow the object.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137

وَدَمَّرْنَا مَا كَانَ يَصْنَعُ فِرْعَوْنُ وَقَوْمُهُ

“We laid waste what Pharaoh and his people made.”

“Laid waste” fits constructions and works as the direct objects.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:16

فَدَمَّرْنَاهَا تَدْمِيرًا

“We destroyed it utterly.”

This preserves the settlement as object and conveys the verbal noun’s emphasis through “utterly.”

“Utterly” should not mean every material component disappearing without a historical trace.

Al-Furqān 25:36

فَدَمَّرْنَاهُمْ تَدْمِيرًا

“Then We destroyed them utterly.”

Since the object is people, “destroyed” is preferable here to a rendering specifically suggesting buildings laid waste.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:172 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:136

ثُمَّ دَمَّرْنَا الْآخَرِينَ

“Then We destroyed the others.”

The concise rendering need not add the punishment’s type or mechanism to the verb.

Al-Naml 27:51

أَنَّا دَمَّرْنَاهُمْ وَقَوْمَهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

“We destroyed them and their people altogether.”

“Altogether” renders ajmaʿīna; it should not be added to D M R’s own meaning.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:25

تُدَمِّرُ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ بِأَمْرِ رَبِّهَا

“Destroying everything by its Lord’s command.”

This preserves the wind as contextual subject and the verb’s direct sense.

Explanation should clarify “everything” in context without arbitrarily deleting or narrowing the Arabic expression in translation.

Muḥammad 47:10

دَمَّرَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ

“God brought destruction upon them.”

This conveys ʿalā in the Persian rendering as well.

“God destroyed them” is close in meaning but shows less of the particular construction.

Non-Qur’anic forms

Damara al-qawmu: the people perished.

Dammarahum: destroyed them.

Dammara al-bināʾa: destroyed the building.

Damār: perishing, destruction, or ruin.

Damara ʿalā al-qawmi: entered among or burst in upon the people, depending on usage.

Dāmir: perished.

Mudammir: a destroyer; in a particular use, a hunter entering his hide.

Preserve the distinctions through each sentence’s construction and subject.

14. Conclusion

Arabic D M R has documented branches of entering or bursting into a place, and severe destruction and perishing.

Ibn Fāris makes entering a house or place its basic principle, whereas Lisān al-ʿArab and Jamharat al-Lughah explain damār through destruction and perishing.

Aws ibn Ḥajar’s witness describes a hunter entering his hide as mudammir.

That witness alone does not establish destruction as necessarily derived from entering or incursion.

All ten Qur’anic occurrences in eight verses concern destroying and laying waste.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 destroys Pharaoh’s and his people’s works and constructions.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:16 places a settlement’s destruction after disobedience and fulfillment of its consequence.

Al-Furqān 25:36 describes a group of deniers being destroyed.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:172 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:136 concern a group’s destruction in Lot’s narrative.

Al-Naml 27:51 connects destruction with a Thamūd group’s plotting and corruption.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:25 has wind destroying while some traces of habitation remain despite kulla shayʾin, “everything.”

Muḥammad 47:10 presents predecessors’ destruction as something to observe and learn from.

Tadmīr can therefore concern people, societies, buildings, and other things.

Severe destruction does not necessarily erase every material trace.

Physical destruction and moral responsibility should not be joined by a simple universal rule. Particular Qur’anic narratives explain peoples’ conduct and outcomes, without making every modern storm, earthquake, or ruin direct punishment for its victims’ sins.

Nor does tadmīr name a specific destructive instrument. Drowning, wind, punitive rain, or demolished structures may accompany the outcome in different narratives, without dammara being restricted to any one.

Proposed final definition: One early D M R branch denotes entering or bursting into a place; the damār/tadmīr branch denotes perishing, destruction, and severe ruin. Only the destruction branch occurs in the Qur’an and may concern people, societies, or their works. The kind, extent, and cause of destruction follow from object, agent, and context, rather than the root alone. Brief root meaning: Severe destruction and ruin; tadmīr: destroying or laying waste. Another Arabic branch uses damara for entering or bursting in.
