The Qur’anic Root Ṣ F W

Clarity and purity, choosing, refinement, and smooth stone

The Arabic root Ṣ F W concerns clarity, purity, and freedom from cloudiness or admixture. Ṣafā al-māʾu describes water that is clear and free from turbidity. Ṣafwah denotes the pure or choice part of something. From the same family, iṣṭafā means to choose: to select someone or something from among others. Alongside these uses, ṣafwān and ṣafā are also known Arabic terms for smooth, even stone.

In the Qur’an, this root most often expresses choosing. Abraham, Moses, Mary, Saul, and other individuals and groups are described using iṣṭafā. The object and purpose of selection vary across the verses. A person may be chosen to receive a message, another to rule; a religion may be chosen for people, or a claim about choosing may itself be challenged through a question.

Two other uses also matter: ṣafwān in Al-Baqarah’s example of a smooth stone covered with soil, and muṣaffā describing the honey of the Garden. These show that the family extends beyond choosing people and retains meanings of clarity and refinement.

The Quranic Arabic Corpus counts 17 occurrences of this root in 16 verses: two Form IV verbs, aṣfā; twelve Form VIII verbs, iṣṭafā and yaṣṭafī; one plural passive participle, al-muṣṭafayn; one Form II passive participle, muṣaffā; and one noun, ṣafwān. Iṣṭafāki occurs twice in Āl ʿImrān 3:42. Verb forms carrying different pronouns are not separate derivational entries. The proper name Al-Ṣafā in Al-Baqarah 2:158 is outside this count and is treated separately here. Including it, the material discussed comprises 18 occurrences in 17 verses.

The root’s semantic core and its use in early Arabic

From clear water to a choice portion

One of the clearest uses of Ṣ F W concerns water and drink. Ṣafā al-māʾu describes water becoming clear and losing its turbidity. Its opposite, kadira, describes becoming cloudy or muddy. Here ṣafāʾ and ṣafw concern purity and the absence of unwanted admixture.

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm says:

وَنَشْرَبُ إِنْ وَرَدْنَا الْمَاءَ صَفْوًا
وَيَشْرَبُ غَيْرُنَا كَدَرًا وَطِينَا

“When we reach the water, we drink it clear; others drink it turbid and muddy.”

Ṣafwan stands against kadaran wa ṭīnā. The poet uses the contrast between clear and muddy water to boast of his tribe’s standing. The social image rests on a physical quality of water; ṣafw itself does not mean tribal superiority.

ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm; Al-Tabrīzī, Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-ʿAshr, p. 245. This transmission is recorded alongside the variant “wa annā al-shāribūna al-māʾa ṣafwan.”

A similar use is known for the atmosphere. Ṣafā al-jawwu describes a sky clear of cloud or obscurity. Clarity here is a perceptible condition in which nothing darkens the view or obscures the air; it does not express moral purity. No independent pre-Qur’anic witness for this particular expression about the atmosphere was located in this study. The direct historical evidence for clarity in this section is the verse about water.

Within this semantic field, ṣafwah denotes the pure or choice portion of something. Ṣafwat al-māl, for example, denotes the choice or outstanding portion of property. Iṣṭafāhu and istaṣfāhu are also recorded in uses concerning taking the pure or choice part of something.

An important distinction arises here. Water’s clarity is a quality it has; selecting something is an action someone performs. Their connection is intelligible: from a collection of differing parts, the portion considered purer or more desirable is set apart. Selection, however, does not always mean removing an impurity from what is selected.

This distinction is especially important for iṣṭafā. When someone is chosen from a group to perform a task, the verb means selecting that person. It does not necessarily say that every flaw or error has been removed from them at that moment.

Selection in pre-Islamic Arab social life

The use of this family for selecting and reserving something was also known in pre-Islamic Arab social relations.

An elegy attributed to ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAnamah al-Ḍabbī, concerning the tribal leader Bisṭām ibn Qays, says:

لَكَ الْمِرْبَاعُ مِنْهَا وَالصَّفَايَا
وَحُكْمُكَ وَالنَّشِيطَةُ وَالْفُضُولُ

ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAnamah, Al-Aṣmaʿiyyāt, poem beginning “Li-ummi al-arḍi waylun mā ajannat,” verse 6; also Lisān al-ʿArab, entry Ṣ F W.

“Yours is its quarter share and its choice possessions; also what you choose by your judgment, the spoils taken along the way, and the remnants that cannot be divided.”

Here al-ṣafāyā denotes possessions or objects a chief or commander selected for himself from the spoils. Such selection was a recognized privilege of some tribal leaders in pre-Islamic warfare. The elegy concerns an event from that period, although the precise date of the verse’s composition cannot be established with full confidence.

This witness shows that selection within this word family could be a social and legal act: something was separated from a collection and assigned to a particular person.

In this use, ṣafāyā does not necessarily denote possessions cleansed of every physical or moral impurity. Their value and desirability matter to their selection, but the central action is setting them apart and reserving them.

This helps explain Qur’anic iṣṭafā. Selection was already part of the language before the Qur’an; it was not restricted to choosing prophets or sacred figures. Who chooses, what is chosen, and why are established by the sentence and its context.

Smooth stone in pre-Islamic poetry

Another branch appears in terms for stone. Al-ṣafwāʾ, al-ṣafwān, and al-ṣafā are recorded in early sources for smooth, even stone.

The pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays describes his horse:

كُمَيْتٍ يَزِلُّ اللِّبْدُ عَنْ حَالِ مَتْنِهِ
كَمَا زَلَّتِ الصَّفْوَاءُ بِالْمُتَنَزِّلِ

Imruʾ al-Qays, Muʿallaqah; Dīwān, ed. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Muṣṭāwī, p. 55, verse 58; also Al-Zawzanī’s Sharḥ al-Muʿallaqāt al-Sabʿ.

“A dark reddish horse, from whose back, at the rider’s seat, the saddle felt slips, as a smooth rock makes what descends upon it slide away.”

Al-ṣafwāʾ here is a smooth, slippery rock. The poet compares the even surface of the horse’s back to a stone on which something cannot easily remain in place.

Early explanations of this verse understand al-mutanazzil sometimes as falling rain and sometimes as a person or something descending upon the rock. The difference does not change the relevant meaning of al-ṣafwāʾ: in either case, its key property is a smooth, even surface. Ḥāli matnihi denotes the rider’s seat on the horse’s back. The smoothness of the back comes from the verse’s image, not from an independent meaning of ḥāl.

This witness shows that the family’s use for smooth stone was known before the Qur’an. It does not establish that stone was the root’s earliest historical meaning, or that all meanings of clarity and selection developed from that image.

Lexical sources explain a connection between this branch and clarity or freedom from admixture. Nevertheless, the salient property of stone is the smoothness of its surface, whereas for water it is transparency and freedom from turbidity. These are related qualities, but they are not identical.

Ṣafwān, Ṣafā, and muṣaffā: uses beyond selection

Ṣafwān: a stone on which soil does not remain

Al-Baqarah says:

فَمَثَلُهُ كَمَثَلِ صَفْوَانٍ عَلَيْهِ تُرَابٌ فَأَصَابَهُ وَابِلٌ فَتَرَكَهُ صَلْدًا

“His example is like a smooth stone with soil upon it: a heavy rain strikes it and leaves it bare and hard.”

Al-Baqarah 2:264

Ṣafwān denotes a smooth stone here. Soil rests on its surface, but when heavy rain falls, the soil washes away and the stone remains bare.

Wābil expresses heavy rain, while ṣald belongs to another root and concerns the hardness and bareness of the stone’s surface. Thus, ṣafwān alone does not supply every feature of the image. The stone’s smoothness, the soil on it, the heavy rain, and the bare surface left behind each have a distinct part in the comparison.

The verse tells believers not to nullify their charitable gifts by reminders of generosity and injury. It then gives the example of someone who spends to be seen by people and does not believe in The God or the Last Day. The image of the soil-covered rock and rain makes the fruitlessness of such spending visible.

The lexical meaning of ṣafwān does not thereby become “a hypocritical heart,” “rootless faith,” or “fruitless action.” These are interpretations of how the example relates to the verse’s subject. The word itself still denotes smooth stone.

Nor does the image establish a definitive theory of the stages through which faith forms or disappears. The verse uses a familiar natural image to illuminate spending.

Al-Ṣafā: the name of a particular place

Al-Baqarah says:

إِنَّ الصَّفَا وَالْمَرْوَةَ مِن شَعَائِرِ اللَّهِ

“Indeed, Al-Ṣafā and Al-Marwah are among the ritual landmarks of The God.”

Al-Baqarah 2:158

Al-Ṣafā here is the proper name of one of two known places associated with Hajj and ʿUmrah. Lexically, ṣafā is also used in Arabic for smooth stone or rock and is related to ṣafwān.

Its status as a proper name matters. Ṣafā names a specific location and should not be translated as “purity” or “clarity.” The verse concerns two places called Ṣafā and Marwah, rather than two inner human states.

The verse continues by mentioning Hajj, ʿUmrah, and an yaṭṭawwafa bihimā. Their role in worship emerges from the complete sentence. The lexical meaning of the name Ṣafā does not by itself specify how the action is performed, how many journeys are made, or all the details of the rites.

Ṣafā also occurs alongside Marwah. Their pairing does not, without evidence, establish oppositions such as purity and impurity, spirit and body, or two stages of a spiritual path.

Ṣafā is a recognized place name, and the verse itself relates it to divine ritual landmarks. The name’s etymology may clarify its lexical associations, but it cannot replace attention to context.

ʿAsalin muṣaffā: clear, pure honey

In the description of the Garden, we read:

وَأَنْهَارٌ مِنْ عَسَلٍ مُصَفًّى

“And rivers of clear, pure honey.”

Muḥammad 47:15

Muṣaffā is a Form II passive participle. It describes something made clear or refined, or free from admixture and cloudiness.

Here it qualifies honey. “Clear,” “refined,” or “pure” is therefore an appropriate rendering.

The passive participle does not, however, establish a particular physical method for refining the honey of the Garden. The verse describes its quality, not the tools or process by which it was purified.

The same verse gives water, milk, and other drinks their own descriptions. Each substance is characterized by a quality relevant to it.

Muṣaffā here does not mean selecting a person or choosing someone for a responsibility. It belongs to the branch concerning clarity and refinement. Sharing a root does not make it synonymous with muṣṭafā, “chosen.”

Iṣṭifāʾ: choosing from among others

The choice of Abraham and of his religion

Al-Baqarah says:

وَلَقَدِ اصْطَفَيْنَاهُ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَإِنَّهُ فِي الْآخِرَةِ لَمِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

“We certainly chose him in this world, and in the Hereafter he is among the righteous.”

Al-Baqarah 2:130

The object pronoun -hu in iṣṭafaynāhu refers to Abraham; -nā is the verb’s subject. The sentence says “We chose him” and then separately states his righteousness in the Hereafter.

The two statements are related but do not have the same meaning. Iṣṭafaynāhu expresses the choice of Abraham, while mina al-ṣāliḥīn describes his place among the righteous.

The following verse presents the command to Abraham to submit and his response. Al-Baqarah 2:132 then says:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ اصْطَفَىٰ لَكُمُ الدِّينَ فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ إِلَّا وَأَنتُم مُّسْلِمُونَ

“Indeed, The God has chosen this religion for you; so do not die except in submission.”

Al-Baqarah 2:132

What is chosen here is al-dīn, the religion, rather than Abraham’s sons themselves. Lakum indicates that the choice is made for those addressed.

The distinction matters. Iṣṭafā does not apply only to human beings: a way or religion can also be chosen for people.

This verse does not establish that descent from Abraham alone makes his children chosen and righteous in every age. The sentence directly concerns the religion chosen for them and the injunction to submit to The God.

Adam, Noah, and chosen families

Āl ʿImrān says:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ اصْطَفَىٰ آدَمَ وَنُوحًا وَآلَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَآلَ عِمْرَانَ عَلَى الْعَالَمِينَ

“Indeed, The God chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ʿImrān above the worlds.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:33

Both individuals and families are objects of selection here. Adam and Noah appear as personal names; the family of Abraham and the family of ʿImrān refer to households or lineages.

Although one verb introduces them all, the objects of selection differ in number and kind. That difference should remain visible.

The selection of a family does not by itself establish that every member, in every generation and regardless of conduct, possesses the same spiritual rank. Determining the scope of this choice and its relation to individual family members requires examination of other verses.

The passage continues with descendants and the connection between generations. Family and generational continuity are therefore present in the context, but iṣṭafā itself does not mean the hereditary transmission of virtue.

Moses and selection for a message

Al-Aʿrāf says:

قَالَ يَا مُوسَىٰ إِنِّي اصْطَفَيْتُكَ عَلَى النَّاسِ بِرِسَالَاتِي وَبِكَلَامِي فَخُذْ مَا آتَيْتُكَ وَكُن مِّنَ الشَّاكِرِينَ

“He said: Moses, I have chosen you above the people with My messages and My speech. Take what I have given you and be among the grateful.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:144

Iṣṭafaytuka denotes the choice of Moses, while bi-risālātī wa bi-kalāmī specifies the setting of that choice.

Here, selection concerns receiving divine messages and speech. The verb iṣṭafā does not convey that mission on its own; the following phrase identifies the distinction and responsibility involved.

ʿAlā al-nās expresses selection in relation to other people. Without examining the context and other verses, it should not be turned into an absolute hierarchy of the worth of all human beings in every age.

At the end of the verse, Moses is commanded to take what he has been given and to be grateful. These commands connect selection with receiving something and fulfilling a responsibility. The word iṣṭifāʾ itself does not contain the meaning of all those responsibilities.

Saul and selection to rule

In Al-Baqarah, after a group objects to Saul’s kingship, their prophet says:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ اصْطَفَاهُ عَلَيْكُمْ وَزَادَهُ بَسْطَةً فِي الْعِلْمِ وَالْجِسْمِ

“Indeed, The God has chosen him above you and increased him in breadth of knowledge and physique.”

Al-Baqarah 2:247

This is an important use of iṣṭafā because the selection concerns kingship, rather than receiving revelation or holding prophetic office.

In context, the group objects to Saul’s selection and considers his wealth insufficient. The response attributes his choice to The God and then mentions his increased knowledge and physical capacity.

Accordingly, iṣṭafāhu should not be translated here as “He purified him of every error.” The subject is choosing someone to rule; the relevant advantages are explained afterward.

The verse also shows that conventional social criteria for recognizing a ruler need not coincide with the reasons for his selection. That conclusion comes from the narrative as a whole, rather than the lexical meaning of iṣṭafā alone.

Choosing messengers from angels and human beings

Al-Ḥajj says:

اللَّهُ يَصْطَفِي مِنَ الْمَلَائِكَةِ رُسُلًا وَمِنَ النَّاسِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ بَصِيرٌ

“The God chooses messengers from among the angels and from among human beings. Indeed, The God is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:75

Yaṣṭafī is a Form VIII imperfect verb expressing selection. Mina al-malāʾikah and mina al-nās identify the groups from which the choice is made, while rusulan names the role of those chosen.

The selection therefore concerns messengers from among angels and human beings. Rasūl identifies a mission here; the word alone does not establish that all messengers share the same nature of being.

Selection for a message is thus not confined to one group. Iṣṭifāʾ itself does not mean messengership, however: that role is named separately in the sentence.

Al-Naml 27:59 also mentions ʿibādihi alladhīna iṣṭafā, “His servants whom He has chosen.” This phrase does not name those servants. Identifying every person included requires examination of context; an external list should not be inserted into the meaning of the word itself.

Selection, purity, and righteousness: related but distinct

Why iṣṭafāki wa ṭahharaki matters

Āl ʿImrān says:

وَإِذْ قَالَتِ الْمَلَائِكَةُ يَا مَرْيَمُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ اصْطَفَاكِ وَطَهَّرَكِ وَاصْطَفَاكِ عَلَىٰ نِسَاءِ الْعَالَمِينَ

“And when the angels said: Mary, The God has chosen you, purified you, and chosen you above the women of the worlds.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:42

This verse is especially useful for defining the scope of iṣṭifāʾ. It contains three verbs: iṣṭafāki, ṭahharaki, and iṣṭafāki again.

Iṣṭafā expresses choosing; ṭahhara belongs to Ṭ H R, concerning purification. The Qur’an therefore expresses choosing and purifying through two distinct verbs here.

The two verbs describe Mary’s selection and purification in this context. Their pairing does not prove that their semantic fields are wholly separate. Nevertheless, a translation should preserve the contribution of each verb and should not replace choosing with purifying without evidence.

The repetition of iṣṭafāki also matters. Its second occurrence adds ʿalā nisāʾi al-ʿālamīn, specifying the scope of comparison.

Various rhetorical explanations of this repetition may be proposed. The verb alone does not prove that the first choice serves one particular purpose and the second another. Such a division requires independent evidence.

Mary is presented as both chosen and purified. The two descriptions occur together here, but their conjunction does not establish that every chosen person in every verse has necessarily been purified in the same sense and to the same degree.

Being chosen and differences in conduct

Fāṭir says:

ثُمَّ أَوْرَثْنَا الْكِتَابَ الَّذِينَ اصْطَفَيْنَا مِنْ عِبَادِنَا فَمِنْهُمْ ظَالِمٌ لِّنَفْسِهِ وَمِنْهُم مُّقْتَصِدٌ وَمِنْهُمْ سَابِقٌ بِالْخَيْرَاتِ بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ الْفَضْلُ الْكَبِيرُ

“Then We gave the Book as an inheritance to those We chose from among Our servants. Among them is one who wrongs himself, one who takes a middle course, and one who precedes others in good deeds by permission of The God. That is the great bounty.”

Fāṭir 35:32

Iṣṭafaynā is associated here with inheriting the Book. The verse then speaks of three groups: those who wrong themselves, those who take a middle course, and those who lead in good deeds.

On the reading most closely following the sentence’s structure, the pronoun in minhum refers to those chosen to inherit the Book, placing all three groups within that body. Referring it instead to the broader category “Our servants” is another analysis, but the two readings should not be assigned equal weight without examining context.

This difference should remain visible. Under neither reading, however, can the root Ṣ F W alone establish that being chosen means every individual is free from error.

This verse is especially important in preventing an unsupported translation of iṣṭifāʾ as “absolute moral purity.” Selection may involve a particular responsibility and gift, but the verb directly expresses choosing.

Nor should the opposite conclusion be drawn, as though selection had no connection with righteousness, virtue, or responsibility. The lexical meaning of choosing must be distinguished from the qualities the verse attributes to those chosen.

Al-muṣṭafayn al-akhyār: chosen and good

Ṣād says:

وَإِنَّهُمْ عِندَنَا لَمِنَ الْمُصْطَفَيْنَ الْأَخْيَارِ

“Indeed, in Our sight they are among the chosen, the good.”

Ṣād 38:47

Al-muṣṭafayn is a plural Form VIII passive participle referring to those who have been chosen. Al-akhyār describes their goodness.

Two descriptions thus apply to one group. Being chosen and being good occur together, but they are not one word.

Rendering muṣṭafayn from the outset as “the pure and good” obscures the contribution of al-akhyār. “The chosen, the good” better preserves the two descriptions.

The surrounding verses speak of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Selection and goodness therefore describe particular people. This use does not establish an absolute rule that every instance of iṣṭifāʾ in the Qur’an expresses precisely the same rank and qualities.

Aṣfā and iṣṭifāʾ in questions and hypothetical statements

Reserving an advantage for a particular group

Al-Isrāʾ says:

أَفَأَصْفَاكُمْ رَبُّكُم بِالْبَنِينَ وَاتَّخَذَ مِنَ الْمَلَائِكَةِ إِنَاثًا إِنَّكُمْ لَتَقُولُونَ قَوْلًا عَظِيمًا

“Has your Lord reserved sons for you and taken females from among the angels for Himself? Indeed, you utter an enormous and wrongful claim.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:40

Aṣfākum is Form IV. In this construction it expresses assigning or reserving something for particular people.

The passage concerns people who preferred sons for themselves while attributing daughters to The God. The Qur’an’s question challenges this unequal and contradictory attribution.

Al-Zukhruf also says:

أَمِ اتَّخَذَ مِمَّا يَخْلُقُ بَنَاتٍ وَأَصْفَاكُم بِالْبَنِينَ

“Or has He taken daughters from what He creates and reserved sons for you?”

Al-Zukhruf 43:16

In both verses, aṣfā expresses reserving something desirable for a particular group. The preference for sons is the belief being criticized; the root Ṣ F W does not itself signify male superiority.

A verb of choosing can thus appear in a critical question. Its presence in the sentence does not endorse the claim under discussion.

Aṣṭafā al-banāti ʿalā al-banīn: a different grammatical form

Al-Ṣāffāt says:

أَصْطَفَى الْبَنَاتِ عَلَى الْبَنِينَ

“Has He chosen daughters above sons?”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:153

Aṣṭafā can initially be mistaken for aṣfā, but the forms are different.

Aṣfā is Form IV. In the Ḥafṣ reading of this verse, aṣṭafā is a Form VIII verb preceded by the interrogative hamzah. When that interrogative hamzah is added, the initial connecting hamzah of iṣṭafā drops out. The ṭ is the Form VIII t changed next to ṣ; it is not a root consonant.

This distinction matters when counting and classifying forms. Aṣṭafā should not be classified as Form IV aṣfā merely because it begins with hamzah.

The sentence is also a question in meaning. It does not assert that The God chose daughters over sons; it questions a claim in that form.

It therefore does not provide evidence that The God actually prefers one sex over another. Its subject is the criticism of a statement attributed to Him.

La-iṣṭafā: choosing within a rejected supposition

Al-Zumar says:

لَوْ أَرَادَ اللَّهُ أَن يَتَّخِذَ وَلَدًا لَّاصْطَفَىٰ مِمَّا يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَاءُ سُبْحَانَهُ هُوَ اللَّهُ الْوَاحِدُ الْقَهَّارُ

“If The God had willed to take a child, He would have chosen whatever He willed from what He creates. Exalted is He; He is The God, the One, the All-Subduing.”

Al-Zumar 39:4

In la-iṣṭafā, the lām introducing the result of the condition is attached to iṣṭafā. The alif after it belongs to the verb’s connecting hamzah, which is not pronounced in connected speech. The opening letters therefore do not form the negative lā, and the expression does not mean “He would not choose.”

Linguistically, iṣṭafā still means choosing. The sentence, however, concerns a supposition from which The God is declared exalted through subḥānahu and the subsequent affirmation of His oneness.

The verse does not assert that The God actually chose a child or that having a child is a reality for Him. The conditional construction serves to challenge and reject the attribution of offspring to Him.

Even when iṣṭafā is attributed to The God, its sentence structure must be examined. Choosing in a statement, a rejecting question, and a hypothetical condition retains a similar verbal meaning, while the propositions themselves have different force.

Comparison with related roots

Ṣ F W (iṣṭifāʾ) and Kh Y R (ikhtiyār)

Ikhtiyār concerns selecting one of several possibilities. Qur’anic iṣṭifāʾ also expresses choosing and can be synonymous with ikhtiyār in many settings.

Lexical explanations of iṣṭifāʾ recognize its connection with ṣafwah, the pure or choice portion of something. That connection does not establish that iṣṭifāʾ always means choosing the morally best person in every respect while ikhtiyār means merely an ordinary choice.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:68 speaks of The God creating and choosing. Verses using iṣṭifāʾ also speak of divine selection. The precise force of each use comes from what is chosen and how the sentence is constructed, rather than a fixed hierarchy between the two roots.

Ṣ F W (iṣṭifāʾ) and J B Y (ijtibāʾ)

Ijtibāʾ is also used in the Qur’an for choosing people. In some verses, this verb expresses selection for guidance, a message, or a particular position.

Examples include Al-Anʿām 6:87, Yūsuf 12:6, and Al-Naḥl 16:121.

Iṣṭifāʾ and ijtibāʾ overlap in meaning selection. Lexical explanations associate them with differing images of separating out or taking a desired portion, but those images do not establish fixed, universal stages of divine selection.

The words themselves do not establish that everyone first becomes mujtabā and then advances to muṣṭafā, or the reverse.

Ṣ F W (iṣṭifāʾ) and Ṭ H R (taṭhīr)

Taṭhīr concerns purification, whereas iṣṭifāʾ expresses choosing.

Their conjunction in Āl ʿImrān 3:42 shows that both can describe the same person. Their occurrence together does not make their meanings identical.

Consequently, when a verse says only that The God has chosen someone, a translation should not add, without further evidence, that He purified that person of every error. Purity, its nature, and its scope must be examined through the relevant wording and context.

Ṣ F W (iṣṭifāʾ) and F Ḍ L (tafḍīl)

Tafḍīl concerns giving preference or considering something superior. Iṣṭifāʾ emphasizes choosing and can involve preference in some constructions, especially when followed by ʿalā.

Selection for a particular mission does not necessarily mean absolute superiority in every respect. Saul’s selection to rule and Moses’ selection for a message, for example, belong to different contexts.

Both involve qualities or responsibilities, but the verb alone does not define the scope of superiority.

Ṣ F W (ṣafāʾ) and Kh L Ṣ (khulūṣ)

Khulūṣ and ṣafāʾ both concern freedom from admixture and impurity. In material uses, one can speak of clear water and a pure substance. In other uses, both families extend to human relationships or the orientation of worship.

Their proximity does not make them complete synonyms. Qur’anic iṣṭifāʾ concerns choosing, whereas akhlaṣa and other forms of Kh L Ṣ can concern making a relationship or action pure, or its being pure.

Choosing someone for a mission and making an action pure are related but distinct concepts.

Ṣ F W (ṣafw) and Ṣ F F (ṣaff)

Ṣ F F concerns forming ranks, being arranged in rows, and standing alongside one another. Ṣaff, ṣāffāt, and maṣfūfah belong to that family.

It is a different root from Ṣ F W. Al-ṣāffāt at the beginning of Surah Al-Ṣāffāt, for example, belongs to the family of forming ranks, rather than clarity and selection.

Ṣawāff in Al-Ḥajj 22:36, in the Ḥafṣ reading, belongs to Ṣ F F and refers in context to sacrificial animals standing. The medial wāw in this plural form is not a root consonant. Its written resemblance to ṣawāfī likewise does not establish that the two belong to one family.

This distinction is especially important when indexing Qur’anic forms: similar spelling does not establish an identical root.

Translation choices for Forqan

For most Qur’anic uses of iṣṭafā, “chose” or “selected” is natural and precise. In Āl ʿImrān 3:33, Al-Baqarah 2:130, and Al-Ḥajj 22:75, “choose” conveys the verb’s meaning without adding an interpretive explanation.

In Al-Baqarah 2:132, iṣṭafā lakumu al-dīn means “He chose this religion for you.” Lakum should remain visible in translation because the choice is expressed as being for, or for the benefit of, those addressed.

In Al-Baqarah 2:247, iṣṭafāhu ʿalaykum means “He chose him above you.” In Al-Aʿrāf 7:144, iṣṭafaytuka ʿalā al-nās means “I chose you above the people.” Each translation must preserve the pronouns and the scope of comparison. Saul’s kingship and Moses’ mission are established by the context and other wording in the verses.

For aṣfā in Al-Isrāʾ 17:40 and Al-Zukhruf 43:16, “reserved for you” or “singled out for you” is appropriate. The subject is reserving sons for a group while daughters are attributed to The God.

In the Ḥafṣ reading of Al-Ṣāffāt 37:153, aṣṭafā should be rendered “Has He chosen?” It is Form VIII within a question and should not be confused with Form IV aṣfā.

Al-muṣṭafayn in Ṣād 38:47 means “the chosen.” Al-akhyār should separately convey “the good,” so both descriptions remain in the translation.

For muṣaffā in Muḥammad 47:15, “clear,” “pure,” or “refined” is appropriate. “Chosen” would not correctly convey the sentence’s central meaning here.

Ṣafwān in Al-Baqarah 2:264 means “smooth, even stone.” Al-Ṣafā in Al-Baqarah 2:158 should remain Ṣafā because it is a proper name; its lexical meaning can be explained separately.

Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ F W concerns clarity, purity, and freedom from cloudiness or admixture. Within this field, taking a choice portion and selecting are also established uses. Alongside them, ṣafwān and ṣafā denote smooth, even stone.

Pre-Islamic evidence shows that both the use for smooth stone and the selection and reservation of a choice portion were known in early Arabic. Iṣṭifāʾ should therefore not be understood as a term coined solely for choosing prophets.

In the Qur’an, iṣṭifāʾ applies to choosing individuals, families, messengers, and even a religion. Sometimes the mission or position is named explicitly; sometimes only the choice is stated. The verb also occurs in questions and hypothetical statements, where its presence does not affirm that a choice actually took place.

The central caution is to distinguish choosing from purification and freedom from error. For Mary, the Qur’an expresses selection and purification through different verbs. In Fāṭir, selection appears alongside differences in conduct and standing. Purity, goodness, and righteousness must therefore be established from their own evidence rather than inserted without grounds into iṣṭafā.

This family includes clarity and purity, an established branch of choosing and reserving, and a material use for smooth, even stone. The semantic connections are evident, but the available witnesses alone do not establish the historical order in which every branch arose. In the uses examined, iṣṭafā means choosing, while muṣaffā means clear or refined. They are not interchangeable in translation.

These distinctions allow divine selection, the responsibilities of those chosen, purity and moral righteousness, and the physical meaning of clarity to be understood in their respective contexts, without turning one lexical image into a comprehensive theory of every use of the root.
