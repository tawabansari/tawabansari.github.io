The Root ف ت ل — Twisting, Twining, and a Thread Representing the Smallest Amount

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَتِيلًا
(fatīlan)
A minute strand; the slightest amount

فَتَلَ
(fatala)
Lexical form: twisted and twined

The three-consonant root ف ت ل in Arabic means twisting, twining, or winding something around itself or something else, as when fibres are twisted into thread or rope. This meaning underlies words for twisted strands, wicks, and things whose shape results from twisting.

The Qur’an uses this root in only one form, فَتِيلًا, in three verses: al-Nisa 4:49, al-Nisa 4:77, and al-Isra 17:71. In each, it accompanies the negation of injustice and names something minute, expressing the utmost precision in justice.

The essential point is that ف ت ل does not inherently mean smallness or insignificance. Smallness belongs to the object called فَتِيل in the familiar explanation of its Qur’anic use: a very fine thread in a date stone’s groove.

1. The Root’s Meaning in Early Arabic

In early Arabic, فَتَلَ الْحَبْلَ means twisting a rope. Separate fibres are twisted and intertwined into a coherent strand.

This action has two features: rotating and twisting the material, and forming a twisted strand or structure from it.

فَتَلَ can describe twisting thread, rope, and similar materials. مَفْتُول describes something that has been twisted.

This had tangible applications in everyday Arab life, especially making rope from plant fibres, securing loads, handling camels, and preparing equipment for travel.

Evidence from Pre-Islamic Poetry

In his Mu‘allaqah, the pre-Islamic poet Tarafah ibn al-‘Abd describes a she-camel’s limbs:

أُمِرَّتْ يَدَاهَا فَتْلَ شَزْرٍ وَأُجْنِحَتْ

Umirrat yadāhā fatla shazrin wa-ujniḥat

لَهَا عَضُدَاهَا فِي سَقِيفٍ مُسَنَّدِ

Lahā ʿaḍudāhā fī saqīfin musannadi

Translation: “Her forelegs were firmly twisted, with an outward twist; her upper limbs inclined beneath a structure like a firmly supported roof.”

The poet likens the camel’s powerful limbs to firmly twisted rope. فَتْلَ شَزْرٍ refers to a particular direction of twisting, associated in lexical explanations with twisting outward or over the back of the hand; here the image conveys the limbs’ strength and outward inclination.

This evidence matters because it shows that twisting was a recognized image in pre-Islamic poetry and could describe the shape of an animal’s limbs.

Another verse of the same Mu‘allaqah reads:

لَهَا مِرْفَقَانِ أَفْتَلَانِ كَأَنَّمَا

Lahā mirfaqāni aftalāni kaʾannamā

تَمُرُّ بِسَلْمَيْ دَالِجٍ مُتَشَدِّدِ

Tamurru bi-salmay dālijin mutashaddidi

Translation: “She has two elbows turned away from her sides, as though she moved with the two buckets of a strong water-carrier, held away from his body.”

أَفْتَلَانِ describes the particular shape of the camel’s elbows and their distance from her sides. It shows the family’s use for twisting, turning aside, and bodily shape.

Both verses are preserved in the Mu‘allaqah attributed to Tarafah ibn al-‘Abd and constitute important evidence for pre-Islamic Arabic. The surviving texts have nevertheless reached us through later transmission and compilation.

These examples establish twisting and its connection with a turned or inclined shape. They do not establish that every branch necessarily developed from one particular tool or method of rope-making.

2. Lexical Forms and the Semantic Core

Arabic records various forms in this family:

  • فَتَلَ: twisted, twined.
  • فَتْل: twisting, twining.
  • فَتِيل: something twisted; a fine twisted strand; also the thread in a date stone’s groove.
  • فَتِيلَة: a wick or strand made from twisted fibres.
  • مَفْتُول: twisted.
  • فَتَّلَ: twisted or twined, sometimes emphasizing the action.
  • اِنْفَتَلَ: turned, turned back, or turned away from a direction.
  • تَفَتَّلَ: became twisted or assumed a twisted shape.

Some Arabic expressions also record فَتَلَهُ عَنْ رَأْيِهِ for turning someone away from an opinion. Here twisting extends to changing the direction of thought or a decision.

يَفْتِلُ فِي الذِّرْوَةِ وَالْغَارِبِ is also used for covert efforts to deceive someone or alter their decision. Its imagery comes from handling and manipulating an animal, but its social meaning is not identical to physically twisting rope.

Of all these forms, the Qur’an uses only فَتِيلًا. The others are relevant to linguistic history and range, but should not be presented as Qur’anic forms.

The root’s primary semantic core is twisting or twining something so that its shape or direction changes through the twist.

A twisted strand is one result of this action. Smallness, worthlessness, and injustice are not inherent meanings of the root.

3. What Is فَتِيل, and How Did It Represent Something Minute?

Early dictionaries describe فَتِيل as a fine thread in a date stone’s groove.

A date stone has a longitudinal groove on one side. The very fine strand found there is described as fatīl because of its threadlike appearance.

Its smallness made it an Arabic expression for an exceedingly slight amount.

Two levels of meaning should be distinguished:

Concrete meaning: a fine twisted strand or threadlike object, particularly the strand in a date stone’s groove.

Figurative meaning: the smallest amount; something exceedingly slight or insignificant.

Lexical accounts also give another explanation: fatīl can be the fine strand of dirt or accumulated material formed by rubbing and rolling two fingers together.

This also relates to twisting. Nevertheless, the thread in the date stone’s groove is the most familiar explanation of the Qur’anic word.

The two accounts share one feature: an exceedingly small, threadlike object.

Fatīl is therefore not a precise unit of measurement in the Qur’an. It does not specify a weight or length; a minute object illustrates that not even the slightest right is infringed.

4. Qur’anic Uses

A. Al-Nisa 4:49 — Justice in the Face of Self-Praise and Claims to Purity

أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى الَّذِينَ يُزَكُّونَ أَنفُسَهُمْ ۚ بَلِ اللَّهُ يُزَكِّي مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ فَتِيلًا

Alam tara ilā alladhīna yuzakkūna anfusahum; bali Allāhu yuzakkī man yashāʾu wa-lā yuẓlamūna fatīlā.

Translation: “Have you not considered those who declare themselves pure? Rather, The God purifies whom He wills, and they are not wronged even by the amount of a tiny thread.”

The verse concerns people who declare themselves pure and make claims about their own standing.

Against this claim, the Qur’an states that genuine purification and recognition of purity belong to The God.

It immediately adds: وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ فَتِيلًا.

Rejecting their self-praise does not authorize injustice against them. The God’s judgment is just, and not even their smallest right is lost.

Here فَتِيلًا expresses the least imaginable amount of injustice: they will not be wronged even to the extent of a tiny thread.

The verse concerns self-praise and divine judgment, but fatīl itself does not mean self-praise, purification, or purity. Those concepts arise from other words in the sentence.

يُظْلَمُونَ is passive: “they are not wronged.” This construction emphasizes the absence of injustice in judgment.

B. Al-Nisa 4:77 — Justice in Recompense and Reckoning

أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى الَّذِينَ قِيلَ لَهُمْ كُفُّوا أَيْدِيَكُمْ وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ فَلَمَّا كُتِبَ عَلَيْهِمُ الْقِتَالُ إِذَا فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ يَخْشَوْنَ النَّاسَ كَخَشْيَةِ اللَّهِ أَوْ أَشَدَّ خَشْيَةً وَقَالُوا رَبَّنَا لِمَ كَتَبْتَ عَلَيْنَا الْقِتَالَ لَوْلَا أَخَّرْتَنَا إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ قَرِيبٍ ۗ قُلْ مَتَاعُ الدُّنْيَا قَلِيلٌ وَالْآخِرَةُ خَيْرٌ لِمَنِ اتَّقَىٰ وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ فَتِيلًا

Alam tara ilā alladhīna qīla lahum kuffū aydiyakum wa-aqīmū al-ṣalāta wa-ātū al-zakāta fa-lammā kutiba ʿalayhimu al-qitālu idhā farīqun minhum yakhshawna al-nāsa ka-khashyati Allāhi aw ashadda khashyatan wa-qālū rabbanā lima katabta ʿalaynā al-qitāla lawlā akhkhartanā ilā ajalin qarībin; qul matāʿu al-dunyā qalīlun wa-al-ākhiratu khayrun li-mani ittaqā wa-lā tuẓlamūna fatīlā.

Translation: “Have you not considered those who were told: ‘Restrain your hands, establish salat, and give zakat’? Then, when fighting was prescribed for them, a group of them feared people as they feared The God, or even more intensely, and said: ‘Our Lord, why have You prescribed fighting for us? Why did You not grant us respite until a near term?’ Say: ‘The enjoyment of this world is slight, and the hereafter is better for whoever practices taqwa; and you will not be wronged even by the amount of a tiny thread.’”

Here a group becomes afraid after fighting is prescribed and wishes that this responsibility had been postponed.

The Qur’an directs attention to the brief enjoyment of this world and the value of the hereafter for someone who practices taqwa.

The verse ends with وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ فَتِيلًا.

Unlike the third-person plural يُظْلَمُونَ in al-Nisa 4:49, تُظْلَمُونَ directly addresses a plural audience: “you are not wronged.”

The expression conveys that even a person’s slightest right is not infringed in divine reckoning and recompense.

This promise does not mean every worldly suffering or difficulty is immediately removed. The verse distinguishes brief worldly enjoyment from the good of the hereafter and speaks of divine justice within that framework.

Fatīl has no new lexical meaning here. The same image of a minute thread emphasizes the utmost precision in justice.

C. Al-Isra 17:71 — Justice in the Reckoning of Deeds

يَوْمَ نَدْعُو كُلَّ أُنَاسٍ بِإِمَامِهِمْ ۖ فَمَنْ أُوتِيَ كِتَابَهُ بِيَمِينِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ يَقْرَءُونَ كِتَابَهُمْ وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ فَتِيلًا

Yawma nadʿū kulla unāsin bi-imāmihim; fa-man ūtiya kitābahu bi-yamīnihi fa-ulāʾika yaqraʾūna kitābahum wa-lā yuẓlamūna fatīlā.

Translation: “On the day We call every group of people with their leader, those given their book in their right hand will read their book and will not be wronged even by the amount of a tiny thread.”

The verse depicts a scene of reckoning in the hereafter.

People are summoned, and those given their book in their right hand read it.

The verse concludes that they are not wronged even to the extent of a fatīl.

Beside the record of deeds, the image of the tiny fatīl becomes clear: divine reckoning is so just that not even the slightest right is lost.

This accords with other verses about recorded deeds and just reckoning, including al-Kahf 18:49 and al-Anbiya 21:47.

Fatīl itself does not mean a record of deeds, recorded information, or an amount of reward. Those concepts arise from the context. The word expresses the least imaginable amount.

5. Distinguishing فَتِيل, نَقِير, and قِطْمِير

The Qur’an uses three words whose familiar explanations concern minute features of a date stone:

فَتِيل: the very fine thread in the date stone’s groove.

نَقِير: a small hollow or mark on the stone.

قِطْمِير: the thin skin or membrane covering the stone.

Some translations render all three as “something insignificant” or “a particle.” This conveys their figurative force but erases the difference between the original images.

In al-Nisa 4:53, نَقِيرًا describes people who, if they had a share of dominion or authority, would not give others even a minute amount.

Al-Nisa 4:124 also states that believing men and women who do righteous deeds will not be wronged even to the extent of a naqīr.

In Fatir 35:13, قِطْمِيرٍ expresses the absence of even the smallest independent ownership on the part of those invoked apart from The God.

All three can convey smallness and insignificance, but they occur in different contexts.

The three Qur’anic uses of فَتِيلًا emphasize that no slight right is lost in judgment and reckoning.

With نَقِيرًا, the verse may concern withholding even the smallest thing from others or not wronging anyone in the slightest.

With قِطْمِيرٍ, the absence of even minimal ownership is prominent.

These differences arise from each word’s combination with the verb and context. Fatīl does not inherently mean justice, naqīr does not always mean stinginess, and qiṭmīr does not always mean ownership.

6. Morphology, Syntax, and Meaning

فَتِيل follows the pattern فَعِيل.

Within this family, فَتِيل can mean something twisted, close to مَفْتُول.

This does not mean every word on the fa‘īl pattern is always a passive participle. The pattern’s function must be established from each word’s actual use.

All three Qur’anic instances of فَتِيلًا are indefinite and accusative.

All three also accompany the negation of injustice:

  • Al-Nisa 4:49: وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ فَتِيلًا
  • Al-Nisa 4:77: وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ فَتِيلًا
  • Al-Isra 17:71: وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ فَتِيلًا

The verb expressing wrongdoing is passive in this construction.

Syntactically, فَتِيلًا can be understood as the amount not deducted from someone’s right, or through an understood ظُلْمًا فَتِيلًا, specifying an exceedingly small amount of injustice. These analyses do not change the sentence’s central meaning.

Its indefiniteness in a negative sentence also accords with comprehensive negation: not even one fatīl, the slightest amount envisaged, will be deducted from anyone’s right.

All three verses concern injustice in judgment or reckoning. Their shared usage shows that فَتِيلًا functions as an established figurative expression for an exceedingly slight amount.

That conclusion should not be transferred into the root’s definition. ف ت ل still means twisting and twining; smallness comes from the object called fatīl, and the complete negation of injustice from the sentence’s construction.

7. Comparison with Related Roots

ف ت ل (فَتَلَ) and ل و ي (لَوَى)

ل و ي concerns bending, twisting, and turning something away from its usual direction.

In Al ‘Imran 3:78, يَلْوُونَ أَلْسِنَتَهُمْ concerns twisting tongues in connection with reading and presenting speech.

ف ت ل also concerns twisting, but its principal concrete use highlights twisting strands and forming a twisted structure.

Lay can describe bending or turning something without intertwining several strands into rope.

The roots may nevertheless overlap in uses concerning turning a person from an opinion or direction.

ف ت ل (فَتَلَ) and ب ر م (بَرَمَ)

In classical Arabic, ب ر م also concerns twisting and strengthening rope.

The roots are close in the field of rope-making and twisting. In some uses, إِبْرَام emphasizes making the result firm and secure.

The Qur’anic أَمْرًا alongside forms of ibrām should not, however, be reduced to rope imagery. Deciding and firmly establishing an undertaking must be studied in its own context, as in al-Zukhruf 43:79.

Nor does ف ت ل mean deciding or planning in the Qur’an. Its only Qur’anic derivative, fatīl, denotes the minute thread.

ف ت ل (فَتَلَ) and غ ز ل (غَزَلَ)

Ghazl in Arabic concerns spinning thread from fibres.

Spinning and twisting can work together in producing thread and rope, but are not precisely the same action.

Ghazl focuses on turning fibres into yarn, whereas fatl emphasizes twisting or twining the strand or fibres.

Al-Nahl 16:92 describes a woman undoing her spun thread after making it strong. The word there is غَزْلَهَا, not fatīl.

The proximity of two crafts does not make their roots or Qur’anic uses identical.

ف ت ل (فَتَلَ) and ن ق ر (نَقَرَ)

ن ق ر concerns digging, striking, or making an indentation.

In its date-stone use, نَقِير names a small hollow on the stone.

فَتِيل, by contrast, names the fine thread in its groove.

Although both express something minute in the Qur’an, they derive from different concrete images and different roots.

8. Challenges in Understanding and Translation

A common mistake is treating fatīl solely as “a particle.”

“A particle” can convey a very small amount, but does not preserve fatīl’s concrete image. Fatīl is originally a fine strand, not just any small particle.

Conversely, translating it merely as “a thread” may leave readers wondering why a thread is used in speaking of justice.

The initial explanation should therefore identify the very fine thread in a date stone’s groove. Once the image is clear, smoother renderings such as “the slightest amount” or “something minute” become possible.

Another mistake is turning fatīl into a precise unit of measurement. The Qur’an specifies no fixed mass, weight, or length; the minute example emphasizes the absence of any injustice.

Nor does فَتِيلًا imply that divine justice concerns only small deeds. Quite the reverse: if even the slightest injustice is excluded, a greater right is not outside the principle.

Finally, twisting and twining should not be artificially inserted into the three verse translations. Expressions such as “they are not wronged by the amount of a twist,” although inspired by the root, are unnatural and fail to convey fatīl’s established usage accurately.

9. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For the lexical verb فَتَلَ, suitable equivalents include “twisted,” “twined,” and “intertwined.”

مَفْتُول can be rendered as “twisted,” and فَتِيلَة in its familiar use as “a wick.”

In the three Qur’anic verses, فَتِيلًا is better conveyed by one of these expressions:

“By the amount of a tiny thread.”

“Even by the amount of a thread in a date stone.”

“By the slightest amount.”

For Forqan’s base text, “they are not wronged even by the amount of a tiny thread” is suitable. It preserves the thread image and makes its smallness clear. In al-Nisa 4:77 the pronoun must follow the direct address: “you are not wronged.”

If complete fluency is the aim, “they are not wronged in the slightest” conveys the meaning accurately, but sets aside fatīl’s lexical image.

A proposed short word-box explanation would be:

Brief root meaning: twisting and twining; a twisted strand.

Meaning in this verse: a very fine thread in a date stone’s groove; figuratively, the slightest amount, emphasizing that no injustice is done.

This distinction allows a natural translation while preserving the relationship between the Qur’anic word and its historical root.

10. Conclusion

In early Arabic, ف ت ل concerns twisting and twining. Pre-Islamic poetic evidence, especially Tarafah ibn al-‘Abd’s Mu‘allaqah, shows that the meaning was recognized in descriptions of rope and bodily shape.

In this family, فَتِيل denotes something twisted or threadlike. In the familiar explanation of its Qur’anic use, it is the very fine strand in a date stone’s groove, whose smallness made it an example of the slightest amount.

The Qur’an uses فَتِيلًا three times, always beside the negation of injustice. Each use conveys that not even a person’s smallest right is infringed in divine judgment and reckoning.

The root’s semantic core is twisting and twining; fatīl names a threadlike, twisted object; in Qur’anic usage, this minute thread figuratively expresses the slightest amount.

Preserving these three levels clarifies the root’s historical meaning and conveys the Qur’anic message naturally, without importing unrelated concepts or erasing the word’s original image.
