The right hand, the right side, oaths, ability, and good fortune: linguistic relationships and Qur’anic uses

In early Arabic, Y M N encompasses words whose meanings initially appear different: yamīn for the right hand and right side, yamīn for an oath, yumn for blessing and good fortune, and maymanah for the right side or a fortunate position.

One lexical analysis places these meanings within a single family and takes the right hand as the starting point of its explanation:

الْيَاءُ وَالْمِيمُ وَالنُّونُ: كَلِمَاتٌ مِنْ قِيَاسٍ وَاحِدٍ

al-yāʾu wa-al-mīmu wa-al-nūnu: kalimātun min qiyāsin wāḥidin

That is: “Yāʾ, mīm, and nūn form words belonging to one semantic pattern.”

This analysis relates yamīn to the right hand, yumn to blessing, and yamīn to an oath. Calling an oath yamīn is explained through the custom of joining or touching right hands when concluding a pact.

This analysis is linguistically significant because Arabic usage attests relationships among the right hand, covenants, and good fortune. Yet a semantic relationship must be distinguished from proof of when each word first developed. The explanation of the oath’s origin is a lexical interpretation of that relationship, rather than a documented account of its earliest use.

1. Qur’anic forms of the root

Y M N occurs 71 times in 63 verses across 34 surahs. These occurrences fall into three word families:

Word formOccurrencesSemantic field
يَمِين and its plural أَيْمَان65Right hand, right side, oath, and figurative uses
أَيْمَن3Right side
مَيْمَنَة3Right side and fortunate position
Total71

Of the 65 occurrences of the yamīn family, 24 are singular and 41 are the plural aymān and its attached forms.

This count follows a morphological inventory of the Qur’an. Differences in attached pronouns, prepositions, and case endings are not counted as separate derivatives.

The plural aymān does not always mean oaths in the Qur’an. In Al-Baqarah 2:225 it denotes oaths, whereas in Al-Aʿrāf 7:17 it denotes right sides. In mā malakat aymānukum, it denotes right hands within an expression concerning possession and control.

Words such as maymūn, tayammun, and yumn are also known in Arabic, but these exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an. Their absence does not mean that good fortune is absent from other uses of the root. Each Qur’anic form must be understood in its own context.

2. Semantic core and word family

The right hand and the right side

In its tangible sense, yamīn is a person’s right hand. Spatially, it denotes the direction opposite the left.

As a direction, al-yamīn stands opposite al-yasār and al-shimāl.

Other Arabic words belong to this field:

Yumnā is the feminine of ayman, as in al-yad al-yumnā, “the right hand.”

Ayman denotes the right side or something situated on the right. In other uses, it can describe someone or something regarded as fortunate or blessed.

Yamnah denotes the rightward direction, as in moving to the right.

Maymanah denotes the right side. In military formations it also names the army’s right wing.

Aymān and aymun are plurals of yamīn. Ayāmin and ayāmīn are also attested in Arabic.

These words name a direction or bodily member. Figuratively, however, the right side can become associated with ability, standing, and felicity.

For example, huwa ʿindanā bi-al-yamīn is attested in early usage for someone who enjoys a favorable standing among others.

This extension must not become an absolute claim that everything on the right is necessarily good and everything on the left necessarily bad. Spatially, right and left are simply two directions.

Yumn and maymūn: blessing and good fortune

In Arabic, al-yumn means blessing and good fortune and stands opposite al-shuʾm, ill fortune.

Maymūn describes a person or thing regarded as blessed or fortunate. Its plural is mayāmīn.

Tayammana bihi can mean regarding something as auspicious or hoping for goodness and good fortune from it.

These uses must be distinguished from the physical right hand. Someone can be described as maymūn without any reference to being right-handed or situated on the right.

Lexical explanations connect the two semantic fields: in part of Arabic linguistic culture, the right side became associated with goodness and auspiciousness, and its name was also used for felicity.

This relationship does not establish that being right-handed causes happiness or that left-handed people are inherently unfortunate.

In this branch, auspiciousness is a linguistic evaluation and usage, rather than an established natural law about geographical directions or bodily members.

Yamīn: power and ability

Power is another attested meaning of yamīn.

For many people, the right hand has been the dominant hand for grasping, carrying, and working. Through this experience, the right hand can become a linguistic image of ability.

This connection does not make yamīn mean power in every sentence.

When the Qur’an speaks of Moses’ right hand, the tangible meaning must first be considered. When forceful seizure, mastery, or divine power is involved, a figurative reading becomes more relevant.

Right hand and power are therefore semantically related within this root, but choosing between them must follow the context.

Yamīn: an oath

Yamīn is also an Arabic word for an oath.

One lexical explanation relates this name to the custom of joining one’s right hand with another person’s right hand when making a pact.

This explanation connects the oath usage with the right hand and the practice of covenant making.

It fits Arabic cultural and linguistic evidence, but does not establish that every oath in Arab history necessarily involved physical contact between hands.

In Qur’anic usage, the oath itself matters even when no bodily gesture is mentioned.

For example, aymānikum in Al-Baqarah 2:225 means your oaths, rather than bodily members.

In one attested lexical use, istaymanahu means asking someone to swear an oath.

Aymunu Allāh and aymu Allāh were also Arabic oath formulas. Grammarians differ over the precise structure of aymun: whether it is a plural of yamīn or a form that became independent within the oath construction.

These exact oath formulas do not occur in the Qur’an, but their historical use helps explain aymān.

The name Yemen

Al-Yaman is the name of the familiar region in the southern Arabian Peninsula.

Some early lexical accounts explain the name through its position on the right of an orientation point, including the Kaaba in one geographical conception. Al-Shām, by contrast, is associated with the left.

This is an attested historical explanation, but right and left always depend on the observer’s position and orientation. It should therefore not be treated as the name’s certain origin without geographical and historical research.

Yamānin, yamāniyy, and yamaniyy are Arabic forms associating people or things with this region.

The geographical name Yemen does not occur in this exact form in the Qur’an.

3. Evidence from early Arabic poetry and usage

An oath in poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

فَقُلْتُ يَمِينُ اللَّهِ أَبْرَحُ قَاعِدًا
وَلَوْ قَطَعُوا رَأْسِي لَدَيْكِ وَأَوْصَالِي

fa-qultu yamīnu allāhi abraḥu qāʿidan / wa-law qaṭaʿū raʾsī ladayki wa-awṣālī

A rendering close to the intended sense:

“I said: By The God, I shall not rise from here, even if they cut off my head and limbs beside you.”

Here yamīnu Allāh is an oath formula.

A notable grammatical feature is that some transmissions have abraḥu without lā, although lā abraḥu is understood. Grammarians discuss this omission within the response to an oath.

This witness shows that yamīn as an oath was familiar in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic Arabs.

Some transmissions read wa-law ḍarabū raʾsī instead of wa-law qaṭaʿū raʾsī. The variant must be acknowledged, but yamīnu Allāh has the same meaning in both.

Above all, the verse itself says nothing about handshaking or contact between hands. It attests the oath usage, rather than the historical origin of the oath’s name.

Aymun in poetry attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

A poem attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā reads:

فَتُجْمَعُ أَيْمُنٌ مِنَّا وَمِنْكُمْ
بِمُقْسَمَةٍ تَمُورُ بِهَا الدِّمَاءُ

fa-tujmaʿu aymunun minnā wa-minkum / bi-muqsamatin tamūru bihā al-dimāʾu

The sense of the verse:

“Then oaths from us and you are brought together, at an oath-taking place where blood is stirred.”

Here aymun is a plural of yamīn used in the setting of oaths and covenants.

The verse is useful because the plural denotes the oaths exchanged between two groups, rather than their right hands.

Hands and oaths may have been culturally related in the covenant ceremony, but the verse explicitly emphasizes the two parties’ oaths.

This witness is attributed to Zuhayr, a pre-Islamic poet. Nevertheless, as with any attributed witness, the reliability of its text and wording must be assessed through sound transmissions.

Good and bad omens in poetry attributed to al-Muraqqish

A poem attributed to al-Muraqqish reads:

فَإِذَا الْأَشَائِمُ كَالْأَيَامِنِ
وَالْأَيَامِنُ كَالْأَشَائِمْ

fa-idhā al-ashāʾimu ka-al-ayāmini / wa-al-ayāminu ka-al-ashāʾim

That is:

“Then what was deemed ill-omened became like what was deemed auspicious, and what was deemed auspicious became like what was deemed ill-omened.”

The verse matters in several respects.

First, al-ayāmin stands opposite al-ashāʾim. Auspiciousness and ill fortune therefore had linguistic uses beyond direct references to right and left hands.

Second, the preceding lines mention amulets and signs to which people looked for good or ill. The poem also questions reliance on such signs.

Third, the verse alone does not prove that all Arabs shared a single belief in auspicious right and ill-omened left. The poem itself describes favorable and unfavorable signs yielding reversed outcomes.

Its attribution is disputed: some assign it to al-Muraqqish, others to Khuzaz ibn Lawdhān.

It is therefore best presented as an attributed witness from early poetry, rather than a verse certainly composed by one particular poet.

Yamīn and power in al-Shammākh’s poetry

Al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār said of ʿArābah al-Awsī:

إِذَا مَا رَايَةٌ رُفِعَتْ لِمَجْدٍ
تَلَقَّاهَا عُرَابَةُ بِالْيَمِينِ

idhā mā rāyatun rufiʿat li-majdin / talaqqāhā ʿarābatu bi-al-yamīni

That is:

“Whenever a banner is raised for glory, ʿArābah receives it with the right hand.”

Bi-al-yamīn can evoke taking the banner with the right hand, while lexical explanations also understand ability, power, and worthiness in it.

One explanation emphasizes the literal right hand; another treats the verse as evidence of figurative ability and power.

The difference shows that a poetic expression can combine a tangible image and a figurative meaning. Neither should be declared the verse’s exclusive, certain sense without examination.

Al-Shammākh lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, and ʿArābah al-Awsī was an Islamic-era figure. This verse therefore belongs after Islam’s emergence and should not be presented as pre-Islamic evidence.

What the early witnesses establish

These four witnesses illustrate three semantic fields: oaths in pre-Islamic poetry, auspiciousness and ill omens in familiar vocabulary, and the right hand as an image of ability and worthiness in early Islamic poetry.

They do not, by themselves, prove a fixed historical sequence from right hand to power, then oath, and finally blessing.

Such a sequence may offer a defensible semantic analysis, but the precise date when each use arose remains unclear.

4. Qur’anic uses of the right hand, right side, and power

Ayman: the right side in Moses’ story

All three Qur’anic occurrences of ayman concern Moses’ story.

Maryam 19:52 reads:

وَنَادَيْنَاهُ مِنْ جَانِبِ الطُّورِ الْأَيْمَنِ وَقَرَّبْنَاهُ نَجِيًّا

wa-nādaynāhu min jānibi al-ṭūri al-aymani wa-qarrabnāhu najiyyan

“We called him from the right side of the Mount and brought him near for intimate conversation.”

Ṭā Hā 20:80 also reads:

وَوَاعَدْنَاكُمْ جَانِبَ الطُّورِ الْأَيْمَنَ

wa-wāʿadnākum jāniba al-ṭūri al-aymana

“We appointed a meeting with you on the right side of the Mount.”

And Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30:

نُودِيَ مِنْ شَاطِئِ الْوَادِ الْأَيْمَنِ فِي الْبُقْعَةِ الْمُبَارَكَةِ

nūdiya min shāṭiʾi al-wādi al-aymani fī al-buqʿati al-mubārakati

“He was called from the right bank of the valley, in the blessed location.”

In these three verses, al-ayman is a spatial adjective.

In Maryam and Ṭā Hā it qualifies jānib, side; in Al-Qaṣaṣ it qualifies shāṭiʾ, the valley’s bank.

Morphologically, ayman follows the afʿal pattern and denotes the right side.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30, al-ayman appears alongside al-buqʿah al-mubārakah. Their proximity does not require ayman itself to mean blessed. Mubārakah is a separate adjective explicitly expressing blessing.

Right is also a relative direction here. Ayman alone cannot identify the mountain’s east, west, north, or south, because right depends on the observer’s position and orientation.

Yamīn: Moses’ right hand

Ṭā Hā 20:17 reads:

وَمَا تِلْكَ بِيَمِينِكَ يَا مُوسَىٰ

wa-mā tilka bi-yamīnika yā mūsā

“What is that in your right hand, Moses?”

Moses replies:

هِيَ عَصَايَ

hiya ʿaṣāya

“It is my staff.” (Ṭā Hā 20:18)

Ṭā Hā 20:69 also reads:

وَأَلْقِ مَا فِي يَمِينِكَ تَلْقَفْ مَا صَنَعُوا

wa-alqi mā fī yamīnika talqaf mā ṣanaʿū

“Throw what is in your right hand; it will swallow what they have made.”

In both uses, yamīn denotes the right hand, with an explicit physical clue: the staff Moses holds.

There is consequently no need to replace yamīn with power, legitimacy, knowledge, or another concept in the direct translation of these verses.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48 also reads:

وَلَا تَخُطُّهُ بِيَمِينِكَ

wa-lā takhuṭṭuhu bi-yamīnika

“Nor did you write it with your right hand.”

Here too, the right hand occurs in the context of writing.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:93 says of Abraham:

فَرَاغَ عَلَيْهِمْ ضَرْبًا بِالْيَمِينِ

fa-rāgha ʿalayhim ḍarban bi-al-yamīni

“He turned upon them, striking with the right hand.”

Some interpreters also explain bi-al-yamīn as power, but striking with the right hand is a clear and defensible image in this sentence.

It is therefore inappropriate to turn every occurrence of yamīn into power or blessing alone.

The right side in descriptions of place and movement

Al-Kahf 18:17 reads:

تَزَاوَرُ عَنْ كَهْفِهِمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ

tazāwaru ʿan kahfihim dhāta al-yamīni

“It inclined away from their cave toward the right.”

Al-Kahf 18:18 reads:

وَنُقَلِّبُهُمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ

wa-nuqallibuhum dhāta al-yamīni wa-dhāta al-shimāli

“We turn them to the right and to the left.”

Sabaʾ 34:15 also reads:

جَنَّتَانِ عَنْ يَمِينٍ وَشِمَالٍ

jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimālin

“Two gardens, one on the right and another on the left.”

Al-Naḥl 16:48 likewise describes shadows moving toward the right and left.

Yamīn is spatial in these examples. Shimāl, left, standing opposite it reinforces that sense.

Although the right side symbolizes good fortune in some Arabic uses, it should not become “the blessed side” in these verses. Descriptions of the cave, gardens, and shadows concern spatial or geographical direction.

The right side in scenes of the hereafter

The Qur’an mentions the right side at several points in scenes of reckoning and human destiny.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19 reads:

فَأَمَّا مَنْ أُوتِيَ كِتَابَهُ بِيَمِينِهِ فَيَقُولُ هَاؤُمُ اقْرَءُوا كِتَابِيَهْ

fa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahu bi-yamīnihi fa-yaqūlu hāʾumu iqraʾū kitābiyah

“As for the one given his record in his right hand, he says: Here, read my record!”

The same theme appears in Al-Isrāʾ 17:71 and Al-Inshiqāq 84:7.

By contrast, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:25 describes someone given his record in his left hand.

In these scenes, right is associated with a favorable outcome and left with an unfavorable one.

Yet a sign must be distinguished from the criterion of judgment.

The Qur’an does not say that being right-handed in this life saves someone or that being left-handed causes condemnation.

Right and left form part of the description of reckoning. Human accountability concerns conduct and what is recorded in one’s record.

In Al-Ḥadīd 57:12 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:8, believers’ light also proceeds before them and on their right.

Bi-aymānihim can express the right side here. Whether the light emerges precisely from their right hands or moves on their right depends on analysis of the construction; the plural aymān alone cannot settle it.

ʿAn al-yamīn in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:28: a disputed use

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:28, one group addresses those who misled them:

إِنَّكُمْ كُنْتُمْ تَأْتُونَنَا عَنِ الْيَمِينِ

innakum kuntum taʾtūnanā ʿani al-yamīni

A rendering close to the wording:

“You used to come to us from the right.”

What, however, does ʿan al-yamīn mean here?

One interpretation understands approaching from the side of religion, truth, or goodness: the misleaders approached through something outwardly right and acceptable.

Others relate yamīn to power and dominance.

The dialogue continues with the reply:

بَلْ لَمْ تَكُونُوا مُؤْمِنِينَ
وَمَا كَانَ لَنَا عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ سُلْطَانٍ

bal lam takūnū muʾminīna / wa-mā kāna lanā ʿalaykum min sulṭānin

“Rather, you were not believers, and we had no authority over you.” (Al-Ṣāffāt 37:29 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:30)

The reply concerns deception, following others, and responsibility, rather than simply standing on the right side of a place.

Context supports the reading of approaching through the outward appearance of truth and goodness in order to mislead.

Yamīn itself does not mean deception. That sense emerges from a figurative use of the right side within the full dialogue.

Yamīn in descriptions of divine power

Two further occurrences require closer examination.

Al-Zumar 39:67 reads:

وَالْأَرْضُ جَمِيعًا قَبْضَتُهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ وَالسَّمَاوَاتُ مَطْوِيَّاتٌ بِيَمِينِهِ

wa-al-arḍu jamīʿan qabḍatuhu yawma al-qiyāmati wa-al-samāwātu maṭwiyyātun bi-yamīnihi

“The earth altogether is in His grasp on the Day of Resurrection, and the heavens are rolled up in His yamīn.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:45 also reads:

لَأَخَذْنَا مِنْهُ بِالْيَمِينِ

la-akhadhnā minhu bi-al-yamīni

“We would certainly seize him by the yamīn.”

In Arabic, yamīn can denote both the right hand and power or ability.

One reading of the Al-Ḥāqqah expression understands restraint and forceful seizure. Bi-al-yamīn has been explained here as power; another reading understands seizing a person by his right hand and humiliating him.

In Al-Zumar 39:67, the combination of qabḍah, grasp, and yamīn also matters in describing divine dominion over earth and heavens.

Root analysis alone cannot resolve every question about the manner of divine attributes.

The tangible meaning of yamīn is clear in Arabic, while its use concerning The God occurs within a description of His greatness and power over creation.

The expression therefore does not, without further evidence, establish a physical limb with human characteristics for The God. Nor should the tangible Arabic meaning of the word be denied.

A full account of these expressions belongs to an independent interpretation of the network of verses about divine attributes. Root study must preserve both attested meanings and the contextual evidence.

5. Aymān: oaths, covenants, and responsibility for speech

Inadvertent oaths and binding oaths

Al-Baqarah 2:225 reads:

لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ قُلُوبُكُمْ

lā yuʾākhidhukumu allāhu bi-al-laghwi fī aymānikum wa-lākin yuʾākhidhukum bi-mā kasabat qulūbukum

“The God does not hold you accountable for idle speech in your oaths, but holds you accountable for what your hearts have earned.”

Aymānikum is the plural of yamīn and means oaths here.

Laghw and the phrase bi-mā kasabat qulūbukum show that the verse distinguishes kinds of oath speech and human responsibility.

Al-Māʾidah 5:89 also reads:

لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا عَقَّدْتُمُ الْأَيْمَانَ

lā yuʾākhidhukumu allāhu bi-al-laghwi fī aymānikum wa-lākin yuʾākhidhukum bi-mā ʿaqqadtumu al-aymāna

“The God does not hold you accountable for idle speech in your oaths, but holds you accountable for the oaths you have firmly bound.”

The same verse goes on to describe expiation for an oath and ends with the command:

وَاحْفَظُوا أَيْمَانَكُمْ

wa-iḥfaẓū aymānakum

“Safeguard your oaths.”

The verse clearly shows that yamīn as an oath can have legal and ethical consequences.

Yamīn itself does not mean expiation, obligation, or covenant breaking. Those concepts arise from other words and constructions in the verse.

The Qur’an also distinguishes an oath from what a person intends in the heart. A lexical definition of yamīn cannot therefore exhaust the study of an oath’s ruling; intention and the kind of commitment also matter.

Oaths and obstructing good action

Al-Baqarah 2:224 reads:

وَلَا تَجْعَلُوا اللَّهَ عُرْضَةً لِأَيْمَانِكُمْ أَنْ تَبَرُّوا وَتَتَّقُوا وَتُصْلِحُوا بَيْنَ النَّاسِ

wa-lā tajʿalū allāha ʿurḍatan li-aymānikum an tabarrū wa-tattaqū wa-tuṣliḥū bayna al-nāsi

“Do not make The God an obstacle through your oaths to doing good, being mindful, and reconciling people.”

The verse places oaths in relation to goodness, mindfulness, and reconciliation among people.

Its context indicates that people should not turn their oaths into a means of abandoning good action or preventing reconciliation.

This theme should be considered alongside Al-Māʾidah 5:89 and its instruction to safeguard oaths.

The precise relationship between those commands and the limits of keeping or breaking an oath requires an independent study of oath verses. For this root article, the essential point is that aymān denotes human oaths in both verses.

Oaths in covenants and social relationships

Al-Naḥl 16:91 reads:

وَأَوْفُوا بِعَهْدِ اللَّهِ إِذَا عَاهَدْتُمْ وَلَا تَنْقُضُوا الْأَيْمَانَ بَعْدَ تَوْكِيدِهَا

wa-awfū bi-ʿahdi allāhi idhā ʿāhadtum wa-lā tanquḍū al-aymāna baʿda tawkīdihā

“Fulfill the covenant of The God when you make a covenant, and do not break oaths after confirming them.”

Al-Naḥl 16:92 and Al-Naḥl 16:94 also describe using oaths to deceive and disrupt relationships.

Oaths can build trust and commitment, yet the same instrument can be used to deceive others.

Al-Tawbah 9:12 and Al-Tawbah 9:13 discuss broken oaths and covenants within hostile relations.

Āl ʿImrān 3:77 condemns selling the covenant of The God and one’s oaths for a small price.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:16 and Al-Munāfiqūn 63:2, a group makes its oaths a shield concealing its conduct:

اتَّخَذُوا أَيْمَانَهُمْ جُنَّةً

ittakhadhū aymānahum junnatan

“They took their oaths as a shield.”

Aymān clearly means oaths here, rather than right hands. Junnah belongs to another root and denotes something behind which someone conceals or protects themselves.

The expression depicts an oath used to conceal truth.

Oath references in Al-Māʾidah 5:53 and Al-Māʾidah 5:108, Al-Anʿām 6:109, Al-Naḥl 16:38, Al-Nūr 24:53, Fāṭir 35:42, and Al-Qalam 68:39 belong to the same semantic field.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:2 uses taḥillata aymānikum, concerning release from or removal of an oath’s obligation.

Taken together, these verses show that yamīn as an oath is more than ceremonial speech. In many contexts it concerns truthfulness, intention, covenants, trust, and responsibility.

The root itself guarantees neither truthfulness nor falsehood. An oath can be sincere or deceptive; the speaker’s conduct and the verse’s contextual evidence establish the difference.

Aymān in Al-Nisāʾ 4:33: right hands or covenants?

Al-Nisāʾ 4:33 reads:

وَالَّذِينَ عَقَدَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ فَآتُوهُمْ نَصِيبَهُمْ

wa-alladhīna ʿaqadat aymānukum fa-ātūhum naṣībahum

“Give their share to those with whom your oaths and covenants have bound you.”

Aymānukum is paired with the verb ʿaqadat in this expression.

Two readings, ʿaqadat and ʿāqadat, are transmitted here, and both have been explained within the setting of covenants and alliances.

Historical accounts also mention pacts of protection, cooperation, and rights among allies. The nature of the share and some associated rulings are disputed.

The lexical point is that aymān here relates to covenants and commitments, although the image of covenant makers’ right hands may also underlie the expression.

It should therefore not be translated simply as your right hands while ignoring its relationship to binding and covenants.

The expression should not be equated with mā malakat aymānukum merely because aymānukum occurs in both. One emphasizes binding and a covenant relationship; the other possession or control.

6. Mā malakat aymānukum: the right hand in an expression of control and possession

One of this root’s most frequent and debated Qur’anic expressions is:

مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ

mā malakat aymānukum

A rendering close to the wording:

“What your right hands have possessed.”

This expression and similar forms occur in numerous verses, including Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, Al-Nisāʾ 4:24, Al-Nisāʾ 4:25, Al-Nisāʾ 4:36, Al-Naḥl 16:71, Al-Muʾminūn 23:6, Al-Nūr 24:31, Al-Nūr 24:33, Al-Nūr 24:58, Al-Rūm 30:28, Al-Aḥzāb 33:50, Al-Aḥzāb 33:52, Al-Aḥzāb 33:55, and Al-Maʿārij 70:30.

Some verses use the plural aymānukum; others, such as Al-Aḥzāb 33:50, use the singular yamīnuka.

Grammatically, aymān is the subject of malakat in malakat aymānukum. The verb is feminine singular, consistent with Arabic agreement for a nonhuman plural.

The physical right hand is not an independent owner. In this familiar expression, the bodily member represents a person’s control and disposal.

One lexical explanation regards milku yamīnī as a stronger expression of ownership than saying something is in one’s hand.

Why the whole expression cannot be defined from yamīn alone

Malaka belongs to the independent root M L K. Possession, mastery, and control arise from the verb malakat and its construction with aymān.

Yamīn alone therefore does not mean slave, female slave, servant, subordinate, or ally.

Here it forms part of an expression for possession or control.

The figurative use of the right hand also does not, by itself, determine which people legally fell within that designation.

Identifying the people concerned requires reading the verses together.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:25, for example, mimmā malakat aymānukum occurs alongside fatayātikumu al-muʾmināt.

In Al-Nūr 24:33, fakātibūhum concerns members of this group who seek kitāb.

These uses concern people within a social and legal relationship familiar at the time of revelation, rather than simply everyone with whom a person shook hands or made a friendship pact.

Their full rights and rulings cannot be derived from aymān and malakat alone. A complete study requires M L K, F T Y, ʿ B D, and the associated verses.

Study of M L K

Does milk yamīn necessarily mean physical power?

No.

The right hand mainly represents control and disposal in this expression. Reducing it to physical power leaves major questions unanswered: who owns or exercises control, what is controlled, and what are its limits?

The full construction and its context clarify those matters.

Nor does the expression establish unconditional Qur’anic approval of every proprietary treatment of human beings. Rights, limits, and responsibilities require examination of all relevant commands.

At the level of root study, the more secure conclusion is:

Yamīn in mā malakat aymānukum forms part of an expression of control and possession. Its independent meaning remains the right hand, while the legal referents depend on the whole expression and context.

7. Maymanah and aṣḥāb al-yamīn: from the right side to good fortune

Maymanah: the right side

Maymanah means the right side in Arabic and stands opposite maysarah.

In military formations, for example, maymanat al-jaysh is the army’s right wing.

The word is also related to yumn, blessing and good fortune.

When maymanah describes a human group, it can therefore evoke a favorable destiny as well as a position on the right.

Three Qur’anic occurrences of maymanah

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:8 reads:

فَأَصْحَابُ الْمَيْمَنَةِ مَا أَصْحَابُ الْمَيْمَنَةِ

fa-aṣḥābu al-maymanati mā aṣḥābu al-maymanati

“Then the companions of the right: what companions of the right!”

Al-Balad 90:18 also reads:

أُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ الْمَيْمَنَةِ

ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-maymanati

“Those are the companions of the right.”

All three occurrences concern a group of people, rather than directions within an ordinary location.

In Al-Wāqiʿah, aṣḥāb al-maymanah stand opposite aṣḥāb al-mashʾamah.

Al-Balad 90:17 describes those who believe and urge one another to patience and mercy; Al-Balad 90:18 calls them aṣḥāb al-maymanah. Al-Balad 90:19 calls those who reject the signs of The God aṣḥāb al-mashʾamah.

This contrast shows that maymanah in these two surahs goes beyond a purely spatial meaning and identifies a group with a favorable outcome.

“Companions of the right side” closely preserves the verbal image; “those of good destiny” or “the fortunate” can clarify part of the contextual meaning.

Nevertheless, a direct translation should preferably retain the image of right or the name maymanah, because its contrast with mashʾamah forms part of the verses’ linguistic structure.

Aṣḥāb al-yamīn

The Qur’an also uses aṣḥāb al-yamīn.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:27 reads:

وَأَصْحَابُ الْيَمِينِ مَا أَصْحَابُ الْيَمِينِ

wa-aṣḥābu al-yamīni mā aṣḥābu al-yamīni

“And the companions of the right: what companions of the right!”

Subsequent verses describe their blessings and destiny.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:90 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:91 also read:

وَأَمَّا إِنْ كَانَ مِنْ أَصْحَابِ الْيَمِينِ
فَسَلَامٌ لَكَ مِنْ أَصْحَابِ الْيَمِينِ

wa-ammā in kāna min aṣḥābi al-yamīni / fa-salāmun laka min aṣḥābi al-yamīni

“If he is among the companions of the right, then peace to you from the companions of the right.”

The expression also occurs in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:38 and Al-Muddaththir 74:39.

Yamīn is associated with a good outcome in these uses, and aṣḥāb al-yamīn is explained as people of felicity and good fortune.

This does not make yamīn mean salvation in every Arabic sentence.

In wa-mā tilka bi-yamīnika yā mūsā, it means the right hand. In aṣḥāb al-yamīn, the setting of the hereafter relates that image to human standing and destiny.

Do right and left have intrinsic moral value in the Qur’an?

Some verses use right and left spatially; others use them to describe people’s position at reckoning and their destiny.

These uses must be distinguished.

Both gardens on the right and left in Sabaʾ 34:15 form part of a blessing. Being on the left does not make one garden ill-omened or bad.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41, however, aṣḥāb al-shimāl stand opposite aṣḥāb al-yamīn and have a different outcome.

Spatial right and left are not themselves ethical criteria. Evaluation in verses about reckoning and reward arises from the groups’ context and situation.

Maymanah and yamīn both belong to Y M N, while mashʾamah belongs to SH ʾ M and shimāl to SH M L. Semantic proximity does not make all these roots identical. Mashʾamah concerns ill fortune and, in some usages, the left side.

8. Distinctions from related vocabulary and translation proposals

Y M N and ʾ M N: yamīn and īmān

One major written distinction is between aymān and īmān.

Aymān is a plural of yamīn and, in one use, means oaths.

Īmān belongs to ʾ M N and denotes believing or faith.

Despite their visible resemblance, the forms differ in root and meaning.

For example, Al-Tawbah 9:12 reads:

إِنَّهُمْ لَا أَيْمَانَ لَهُمْ

innahum lā aymāna lahum

“They have no reliable oaths.”

In the reading aymān, within a context of covenant breaking, the phrase means lacking reliable oaths or commitments. This form must not be equated with īmān, belief.

Faith and rejection are also discussed in the same verses, but that context does not make the two words identical.

Study of ʾ M N

Y M N and Y M M

Y M M is an independent root used for tayammamū and al-yamm in the Qur’an.

Tayammum is associated with intending and turning toward a goal; yamm denotes a body of water.

Blessing and the right hand must therefore not be imported from Y M N into tayammum.

Study of Y M M

Y M N and SH M L

Shimāl belongs to the independent root SH M L and stands opposite yamīn in many verses.

The contrast can be entirely spatial, as in Al-Kahf 18:18, or acquire evaluative significance in a scene of the hereafter, as in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:27 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41.

Such evaluations must be derived from the context itself.

Y M N and Q S M

Qasam belongs to Q S M.

Yamīn overlaps with qasam in its oath sense, but Q S M has other meanings too, including dividing and apportioning shares.

Ḥalafa, from Ḥ L F, also means swearing an oath and occurs with aymān in the Qur’an.

These overlaps do not make every derivative identical.

9. Conclusion

In early Arabic, Y M N encompasses related meanings: right hand, right side, power, oath, blessing, and good fortune.

One lexical analysis places these meanings within a single semantic pattern, taking the right hand as its starting point. Poetry and early usage attest relationships among them, but the precise historical sequence remains uncertain.

The root occurs 71 times in the Qur’an in three principal word families: yamīn and its plural aymān, ayman, and maymanah.

In tangible uses, the word denotes the right hand or side. In covenant uses, yamīn names an oath. In some expressions, the right hand represents control, ability, or power. In scenes of the hereafter, the right side is associated with favorable standing and destiny.

The main safeguard is to determine each use from its form and the verse’s context. Not every yamīn means power, not every aymān means oaths, and not every mention of right means good fortune.

Aymān, oaths, must also remain distinct from īmān, faith.

Proposed final definition for Forqan:

Y M N tangibly denotes the right hand and right side. Arabic also attests related uses of power and control, oaths and covenants, blessing and good fortune. In the Qur’an, yamīn denotes the right hand, direction, power, or oath according to context; ayman describes the right side; and maymanah in the hereafter identifies the position of a group of good destiny.
