Qur’anic Root Ṣ B GH

Dyeing, taking on color, and becoming coated: from dyed cloth and food relish to ṣibghat Allāh in the Qur’an

The three-consonant Arabic root Ṣ B GH concerns dyeing, taking on color, and coating something with another substance. Dyeing gives cloth a new color, while a morsel of bread dipped in oil or a relish becomes coated with it. The Qur’an uses this word family in two different settings: for food produced by a tree at Mount Sinai, and in صِبْغَةَ اللَّهِ, amid discussion of belief, Abraham’s way, and worship of The God.

Ṣibghat Allāh is often translated “The God’s coloring.” This preserves the word’s central image, but does not by itself explain the verse’s meaning. We must first examine ṣabgh’s uses in early Arabic, then consider why the Qur’an employs this image in a discussion of Abraham’s way and religious claims.

The root occurs three times in only two Qur’anic verses. Al-Muʾminūn 23:20 has صِبْغٍ once; Al-Baqarah 2:138 has ṣibghah twice, as صِبْغَةَ and صِبْغَةً. The difference between the latter forms is grammatical, not a separate derivation. Verbs such as صَبَغَ (ṣabagha), يَصْبُغُ (yaṣbughu), and اصْطَبَغَ (iṣṭabagha), and words such as صَبَّاغ (ṣabbāgh) and مَصْبُوغ (maṣbūgh), are recognized in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

1. Basic meaning and usage in early Arabic

Dyeing and taking on color

The best-known Arabic use of Ṣ B GH is dyeing cloth, clothes, hair, and similar things. صَبَغَ الثَّوْبَ means “he dyed the cloth.” The substance used for dyeing is also called صِبْغ or صِبَاغ.

In dyeing, a coloring substance acts upon something and changes its color. White cloth may become red, yellow, or black. The new color may remain on the surface or penetrate the fabric. Its penetration and durability depend on the substance, technique, and conditions. Ṣabgh itself does not establish that a color is always deep or irreversible.

Arabic lexical sources also record this root for a natural change of color, such as dates taking on a new color as they ripen. أَصْبَغ (aṣbagh) also describes particular color markings on some horses and birds.

This word family thus extends beyond the craft of dyeing. It can express the act of coloring, the resulting colored condition, or a thing’s color marking.

Dyeing in transmitted linguistic evidence

A rajaz verse preserved in older lexical works reads:

«وَاصْبُغْ ثِيَابِي صِبَغًا تَحْقِيقًا
مِنْ جَيِّدِ الْعُصْفُرِ لَا تَشْرِيقًا»

“Dye my clothes thoroughly and properly, with fine safflower, not with a faint, light coloring.”

ʿUṣfur is safflower, a plant used in dyeing. The speaker asks for a full, visible color rather than light coloring. Lexical explanations likewise identify tashrīq as light dyeing. In the transmitted vocalization, صِبَغًا (ṣibaghan), with an open bāʾ, is a verbal noun for dyeing. It should be distinguished from صِبْغ (ṣibgh), with a vowelless bāʾ, naming a coloring substance or relish.

The witness makes a significant distinction: taking on color admits degrees. Something may be faintly colored or display a more apparent color. Intensity and durability are therefore not fixed features of every use of the root.

The attribution and date of this rajaz passage remain insufficiently clear. Older linguistic works preserve its wording, but it cannot be treated as securely pre-Islamic without independent historical evidence. This investigation’s independent search likewise found no witness for this use with secure attribution and a securely pre-Qur’anic date; that does not mean no such witness exists anywhere.

Dipping and coating

Alongside dyeing, lexical accounts record ṣabgh for dipping something in liquid. In صَبَغَ يَدَهُ فِي الْمَاءِ, someone dips his hand in water. صَبَغَتِ النَّاقَةُ مَشَافِرَهَا فِي الْمَاءِ describes a she-camel dipping her lips in water.

A change of color is not necessary in these examples. A hand dipped in water need not acquire a new color, nor are the camel’s lips being dyed. Dipping and contact with liquid are what matter.

The same meaning appears with food. Lexical sources record ṣabagha for dipping a morsel in oil or a relish. صَبَغَ اللُّقْمَةَ means coating a morsel with a food substance. The food into which the morsel was dipped was also called ṣibgh or ṣibāgh.

An anonymous rajaz passage transmitted in lexical works describes simple food with salt or ṣibāgh:

«تَزَجَّ مِنْ دُنْيَاكَ بِالْبَلَاغِ
وَبَاكِرِ الْمِعْدَةَ بِالدِّبَاغِ
بِالْمِلْحِ أَوْ مَا خَفَّ مِنْ صِبَاغِ»

“Make do in this world with enough to get by; early in the morning, tan the stomach with salt or a little relish.”

“Tanning the stomach” here provides an image of eating simple food. Ṣibāgh appears alongside salt with a food meaning. This investigation did not establish the poet’s name or the date of composition, so the passage cannot be treated as securely dated before the Qur’an.

There is a tangible similarity between dyeing cloth and dipping bread in oil: something comes into contact with a substance and receives an effect from it. This similarity helps explain the relationship between the uses, but does not prove that one historically developed from the other.

The word family thus has two clear material uses: dyeing or taking on color, and dipping or becoming coated. They may occur together, but their results are not always identical. Dyeing ordinarily changes color; coating need not do so.

2. Wa-ṣibghin lil-ākilīn: ṣibgh in food

Sūrat Al-Muʾminūn states:

«وَشَجَرَةً تَخْرُجُ مِنْ طُورِ سَيْنَاءَ تَنْبُتُ بِالدُّهْنِ وَصِبْغٍ لِلْآكِلِينَ»

“And a tree that grows from Mount Sinai, producing oil and a relish for those who eat.”

(Al-Muʾminūn 23:20)

This verse appears among reminders of natural blessings. The preceding verses concern rain, gardens, and fruit; the next turns to livestock and the benefits people derive from them.

Early explanations identify the tree as the olive. This is compatible with olive oil’s use in food, although the verse does not explicitly name the olive.

What does ṣibgh designate?

Ṣibgh here cannot simply mean visible color. The verse speaks of something useful to eaters and places it alongside al-duhn, “oil.”

In Arabic, ṣibgh can mean food or a relish into which a morsel is dipped, close to إِدَام (idām), something eaten with bread. Bread dipped in olive oil clearly illustrates this use.

Lexical explanations offer two identifications for ṣibgh here. One takes it as olive oil itself, eaten with bread. The other identifies it as olives used as food or a relish, contrasted with al-duhn as their oil.

The second distinguishes two benefits from the tree: oil and food. The first considers one substance from two perspectives: oil as a product of the tree, and oil functioning as a relish.

The conjunction in al-duhni wa-ṣibghin can highlight the two benefits, but does not by itself prove that two different substances are necessarily intended. Ṣibgh itself does not decisively settle either identification.

The clear shared meaning is a food used to coat a morsel or eaten with another food. “Relish” is therefore more appropriate here than “color.”

This verse is especially useful for understanding the root because ṣabgh does not always concern a change of color. Contact and coating can instead be central, without necessarily changing visible color.

3. Ṣibghat Allāh: The God’s coloring amid belief and Abraham’s way

Al-Baqarah 2:138 states:

«صِبْغَةَ اللَّهِ وَمَنْ أَحْسَنُ مِنَ اللَّهِ صِبْغَةً وَنَحْنُ لَهُ عَابِدُونَ»

“The God’s coloring; who is better than The God in coloring? We worship Him.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:138)

Ṣibghah here does not concern dyed cloth or food relish. It appears in a discussion of belief, guidance, Abraham’s way, and worship of The God. The surrounding verses show that coloring has a sense beyond material color.

From “Jews or Christians” to Abraham’s way

Al-Baqarah 2:135 states:

«وَقَالُوا كُونُوا هُودًا أَوْ نَصَارَىٰ تَهْتَدُوا قُلْ بَلْ مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ»

“They said: Become Jews or Christians and you will find guidance. Say: Rather, we follow Abraham’s way, Abraham being ḥanīf and not one of those who associate partners.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:135)

The opening speakers associate guidance with accepting the designation Jewish or Christian. The Qur’an responds with Abraham’s way, describing him as ḥanīf and not among those who associate partners.

Verse 136 continues with a declaration of belief in The God and what was sent down to the Prophets. It names Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, the asbāṭ, Moses, Jesus, and the other Prophets. لَا نُفَرِّقُ بَيْنَ أَحَدٍ مِنْهُمْ emphasizes making no distinction between them. The verse ends وَنَحْنُ لَهُ مُسْلِمُونَ, “to Him we submit.”

Verse 137 likewise connects guidance with this belief. Verse 138 then states ṣibghat Allāh.

This continuity matters. After answering a claim to exclusive guidance, the Qur’an describes belief belonging to The God and His messages, rather than merely a group name. It then introduces The God’s ṣibghah.

Color in this context is an expressive image of a person’s affiliation and orientation. Just as color may settle on something and give it a recognizable appearance, the belief and way a person chooses can affect how their life’s direction is understood.

Ṣibghat Allāh is therefore clearly related to the preceding discussion of religious identity. That relationship should not become a claim that every Jewish or Christian designation is inherently fabricated or meaningless. The Qur’an challenges the claim that affiliation with a designation suffices for guidance and sets forth Abraham’s way, belief, and submission.

The distinction is fundamental: the passage does not remove historical and social names from language, but does not locate the criterion of guidance in affiliation with them alone.

Color that leaves an effect

Dyeing can illuminate another aspect of the verse. Cloth no longer has its previous appearance once colored; a visible effect has arisen.

Applied to the context of Al-Baqarah, the image suggests that the belief described is more than a name identifying a person. It also relates to their worship, submission, and action.

The verses themselves support this explanation. Al-Baqarah 2:136 concerns belief and submission; verse 138 concerns The God’s ṣibghah and worship of Him; verse 139 concerns deeds and sincerity. A person’s relationship with The God is thus not confined here to a claim of identity.

The image nevertheless has limits. Some dyes penetrate the fabric, while others remain at the surface. Ṣibghah itself does not establish that belief necessarily penetrates a person’s entire being completely and irreversibly.

Nor does the verse specify ṣibghah’s extent, degree, or duration. What is clear is its attribution to The God and its association with belief and worship.

Taking on color helps illustrate the effect of religious orientation, but inward penetration, everlasting permanence, and complete transformation of personality should not be included in ṣibghah’s lexical meaning.

The ending: wa-naḥnu lahu ʿābidūn

The verse ends وَنَحْنُ لَهُ عَابِدُونَ: “We worship Him.”

This makes ṣibghah’s orientation clearer. The passage concerns more than coloring that gives someone a social mark: it connects ṣibghah with worship of The God.

The following verse states:

«قُلْ أَتُحَاجُّونَنَا فِي اللَّهِ وَهُوَ رَبُّنَا وَرَبُّكُمْ وَلَنَا أَعْمَالُنَا وَلَكُمْ أَعْمَالُكُمْ وَنَحْنُ لَهُ مُخْلِصُونَ»

“Say: Do you dispute with us concerning The God, when He is our Lord and your Lord? Our deeds belong to us and your deeds to you; we are sincere in our devotion to Him.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:139)

This brings the discussion to responsibility for deeds and sincerity. Ṣibghat Allāh stands within a sequence concerning Abraham’s way and belief in the Prophets on one side, and human worship and action on the other.

Despite these connections, ṣibghah is not lexically synonymous with worship, sincerity, or action. Each is expressed by a separate word. In this context ṣibghah employs the image of color and the mark a divine orientation gives to human life.

4. The structure and grammar of ṣibghat Allāh

The first صِبْغَةَ اللَّهِ in Al-Baqarah 2:138 is accusative. This feature matters for understanding the verse’s connection with the preceding discourse.

Ṣibghah in Arabic can refer to a coloring substance, color, or the condition or manner of coloring. Its final tāʾ alone does not establish a single instance of dyeing, a permanent state, or complete transformation. The construction and usage must be examined to determine the precise meaning.

Several grammatical analyses explain the accusative ṣibghat Allāh. Their standing and compatibility with the continuity of the sentence are not equal.

One connects ṣibghat Allāh with مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ in Al-Baqarah 2:135, treating it as an appositional substitute or explanation. The God’s ṣibghah is then understood in relation to the way the Qur’an calls people to follow. This analysis is disputed: some grammarians consider it weak because several clauses separate the expressions.

The common analysis as an emphatic verbal noun connects the expression with the declaration of belief in Al-Baqarah 2:136, understanding it as reinforcing that declaration’s content. On this reading, the belief expressed in the preceding verses is likened to The God’s ṣibghah.

Another possibility supplies an omitted verb such as الْزَمُوا or اتَّبِعُوا: “adhere to” or “follow The God’s ṣibghah.” To fit the continuation wa-naḥnu lahu ʿābidūn, this analysis also requires further explanation of the speaker and the structure of the reported speech.

Conceptually, all three connect the expression with the preceding discussion of belief and Abraham’s way, but their grammatical implications differ and they should not be presented as equally supported possibilities. The base translation can therefore preserve the central image without inserting a definite verb absent from the text.

The second form, صِبْغَةً in وَمَنْ أَحْسَنُ مِنَ اللَّهِ صِبْغَةً, is an accusative specification, or tamyīz. The question expresses the superiority of The God’s coloring. The same word appears, but with a different sentence function.

The grammatical difference should not be turned into two independent root meanings. Both forms belong to one word family and share the same image.

5. The God’s ṣibghah and baptism

Historical examination of Al-Baqarah 2:138 has raised the possibility of a relationship between ṣibghat Allāh and Christian baptism.

Some Arabic lexical sources report that Christians immersed their children in water, understanding the act as entry into the religion or purification. They also used Ṣ B GH to describe this immersion. These accounts were recorded after the period of revelation and do not by themselves constitute direct evidence of Arabic usage in that period.

This account is linguistically understandable because ṣabgh can mean dipping something in liquid. For example, the Gospel of Philip, a Christian text from the early centuries CE, places images of a dyer and being dyed alongside immersion in water. It witnesses the image in part of pre-Islamic Christian literature, but does not establish use of this particular Arabic word or the expression’s familiarity among the Qur’an’s hearers.

The possibility that ṣibghat Allāh also recalled baptism for hearers familiar with these religious images deserves investigation. That possibility, however, does not by itself determine the verse’s definitive meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:138 does not name baptism or a person’s immersion in water. The preceding discussion is also not restricted to Christians: Al-Baqarah 2:135 includes both Jews and Christians in its discussion of guidance and religious identity.

Ṣibghat Allāh therefore cannot be confined to “The God’s baptism,” nor can the verse’s purpose be restricted to rejecting one Christian ceremony. If baptismal imagery belonged to its historical background, it would stand alongside the word’s broader linguistic meaning and the shared context of the discussion.

Is The God’s ṣibghah circumcision, washing, or fiṭrah?

Some older explanations interpret ṣibghat Allāh as circumcision, washing, or fiṭrah. Each has been connected to the verse differently: circumcision through Abraham’s legacy, washing through purification rites, and fiṭrah through the relationship between human beings and divine creation.

None of these words occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:138 itself. Ṣibghah is not lexically an exclusive name for circumcision or washing, nor does it share a root with fiṭrah.

Al-Rūm 30:30, for example, speaks of فِطْرَتَ اللَّهِ in the context of human creation. Al-Baqarah 2:138 uses ṣibghat Allāh in the context of belief, Abraham’s way, and worship. The expressions may be conceptually related, but that does not make them identical words.

Identifying one of these rites or concepts as a referent of ṣibghah requires separate examination of the evidence. The word’s lexical meaning alone does not select any one of them.

6. Related roots and translation recommendations

Ṣ B GH (ṣabagha) and L W N (lawn)

Lawn concerns color and differences of color, whereas ṣabgh more often concerns dyeing, taking on color, and the substance or process of coloring.

Al-Baqarah 2:69 states concerning the cow:

«صَفْرَاءُ فَاقِعٌ لَوْنُهَا تَسُرُّ النَّاظِرِينَ»

“Bright yellow, its color delighting those who look.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:69; part of the verse)

Lawn here expresses the cow’s color. The passage does not describe someone dyeing it.

Despite their proximity, lawn and ṣabgh are thus not exact synonyms. Descriptions such as aṣbagh are also recorded for animals’ natural color markings, however, so ṣabgh should not always be confined to intentional dyeing.

Ṣ B GH (ṣabagha) and GH M S (ghamasa)

Ghams concerns dipping something in liquid. Ṣabgh has the same meaning in some uses, such as dipping bread in oil or a hand in water.

Dipping does not always involve dyeing: something can enter water without changing color. Dyeing can also occur without complete immersion.

Ṣibgh in Al-Muʾminūn 23:20 belongs to coating food; Al-Baqarah 2:138 uses the image of taking on color figuratively. They belong to one family but should not be substituted directly for one another.

Ṣ B GH (ṣabagha) and D H N (dahana)

D H N concerns oil and oily substances. Al-Muʾminūn 23:20 places al-duhn and ṣibgh together.

Oil may be a substance used in food, while ṣibgh can identify a relish or its use with a morsel. One substance sometimes serves both roles.

Their association helps explain the food usage, without making their meanings identical.

Ṣ B GH (ṣabagha) and KH Ḍ B (khaḍaba)

Khaḍb also concerns coloring, particularly hair, hands, and parts of the body. This overlaps with ṣabgh, but the roots are not entirely interchangeable in Arabic.

Ṣabgh’s recognized uses also include cloth, coloring substances, food, and figurative expressions. This tendency does not mean khaḍb is restricted to the body or ṣabgh to cloth.

Ṣ B GH (ṣabagha) and S B GH (sabagha)

S B GH concerns extent and completeness, as in سَابِغَات (sābighāt) in Sabaʾ 34:11, in the context of making armor.

The roots’ similar sounds and the possibility of imagining covering in both do not make them identical. Sabgh cannot establish complete coverage, permanent penetration, or irreversible transformation as the definite meaning of ṣibghat Allāh.

This distinction matters especially when examining literary imagery. Similar images may facilitate explanation but cannot replace lexical evidence.

Translation recommendations for Forqan

Ṣibghin lil-ākilīn in Al-Muʾminūn 23:20 is best rendered “a relish for those who eat.” “Oil and a relish” conveys the clause naturally, leaving the precise identification of ṣibgh to a separate explanation.

Preserving the color image matters in Al-Baqarah 2:138. “The God’s coloring” is an appropriate direct rendering of ṣibghat Allāh, retaining the Arabic word’s image.

Wa-man aḥsanu mina Allāhi ṣibghatan can likewise be rendered “Who is better than The God in coloring?”

The explanation should make clear that this does not mean The God’s bodily color or a coloring substance. The surrounding verses relate it to belief, Abraham’s way, worship, and sincerity.

“The God’s religion” or “The God’s fiṭrah” may convey part of an interpretation, but removes the word’s central image. Conversely, adding “complete penetration of the soul,” “permanent transformation of personality,” or “absolute purity” goes beyond its direct meaning.

The base translation should preserve the brief, clear image, with its semantic connections developed in the explanation.

7. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ B GH concerns dyeing, taking on color, and becoming coated. Its familiar use is coloring cloth and similar materials, but the same family also describes dipping bread in food or bringing something into contact with liquid. A change of color is therefore not necessary in every use, nor does the root itself establish a permanently lasting effect.

The Qur’an uses only ṣibgh and ṣibghah. Ṣibgh in Al-Muʾminūn 23:20 concerns food, a relish or something with which a morsel is eaten and coated. Ṣibghah in Al-Baqarah 2:138 employs coloring as an image in a religious context.

In Al-Baqarah, the expression follows the Qur’an’s response to religious invitations and its statement of Abraham’s way and belief in the Prophets. The surrounding sequence also concerns worship, deeds, and sincerity. Ṣibghat Allāh thus relates to the way a person chooses in belief and servitude, rather than merely a name inherited from a group.

Historical accounts of baptism can provide background for understanding this image, but the verse itself does not name a particular ceremony. Nor does dyeing establish a person’s complete, irreversible transformation.

The semantic core of Ṣ B GH is dyeing and taking on color, with a related branch emphasizing dipping and becoming coated. Al-Muʾminūn 23:20 draws on its food use; Al-Baqarah 2:138 uses the same family to express ṣibghat Allāh, coloring and a mark whose meaning emerges through its connections with belief, Abraham’s way, and worship.

The image should be preserved without confining it to bodily color, a particular rite, or a theory of permanent inward transformation.
