Core meaning: the neck, connecting head and trunk. Other branches include embracing, projection or extension, brisk movement, and a group of people.

Arabic ʿ N Q has several semantic branches, most prominently the neck of a human or animal.

ʿUnuq names the part connecting head and trunk; aʿnāq is its plural. The related ʿānaqa describes embracing, bringing two people’s necks close together.

Elsewhere, ʿanaq names a particular animal gait, and muʿniq describes someone or something moving swiftly. ʿUnuq can also denote a group of people.

These branches should not be forced into an invented single meaning. Embracing has a clear connection with the neck, but establishing exactly how a gait or group designation arose from “neck” requires independent historical evidence.

The root occurs nine times in nine Quranic verses, always as a noun: twice as singular ʿunuq and seven times as plural aʿnāq.

Most occurrences use necks in images of shackles, responsibility, submission, or bodily position. Sad 38:33 concerns horses’ necks.

1. The root in early Arabic

ʿUnuq: the connection between head and trunk

In early Arabic, ʿunuq names the neck, joining head and trunk.

It applies to humans and animals.

Lexicons define it as wuṣlatu mā bayna al-raʾsi wa-l-jasadi, the connection between head and body.

Besides supporting the head, the neck allows it to move in different directions.

Someone can raise, lower, or turn the head. Such movements can express attention, pride, humility, or defiance in social behavior and language.

These attitudes are not the meaning of ʿunuq itself. The word names the body part; pride or submission follows from its position and the surrounding words.

Both ʿunuq, with a vowel on nūn, and ʿunq, with a vowelless nūn, are recorded.

The noun can be masculine or feminine, although some accounts describe masculine usage as more common.

Its established plural is aʿnāq.

A witness from Al-Nabighah al-Dhubyani

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Nabighah al-Dhubyani says:

إِذَا اسْتَعْجَلُوهَا عَنْ سَجِيَّةِ مَشْيِهَا
تَتَلَّعُ فِي أَعْنَاقِهَا بِالْجَحَافِلِ

idhā istaʿjalūhā ʿan sajiyyati mashyihā / tatallaʿu fī aʿnāqihā bi-l-jaḥāfil

“When they urge them beyond their natural gait, they stretch their necks and raise their muzzles.”

The poet describes horses stretching their necks and changing the position of their heads and mouths as they are hurried.

Aʿnāqihā denotes the animals’ actual necks.

Tatallaʿu describes the necks lifting or extending.

The witness shows aʿnāq naming animal necks in a description of bodily movement.

The line is transmitted in the poetry attributed to Al-Nabighah. His pre-Islamic attribution does not independently establish the date of this particular line.

Its lexical implication is clear: aʿnāq is the plural of neck, applicable to humans and animals.

ʿInāq and muʿānaqah: embracing

ʿĀnaqahu means to embrace someone.

A person places the arms around another’s body or neck and draws him close.

ʿInāq and muʿānaqah both express embracing.

A father reunited with a child after a long journey may embrace the child, bringing their heads and necks close and placing his arms around the body.

This illustrates the connection between muʿānaqah and ʿunuq.

Embracing language is not confined to affection.

Wrestlers or close combatants may also grasp one another’s necks and bodies.

Lexicons record muʿānaqah and iʿtināq in both settings.

A witness from Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma

A poem attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma says:

يَطْعَنُهُمْ مَا ارْتَمَوْا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا اطَّعَنُوا
ضَارَبَ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا مَا ضَارَبُوا اعْتَنَقَا

yaṭʿanuhum mā irtamaw ḥattā idhā iṭṭaʿanū / ḍāraba ḥattā idhā mā ḍārabū iʿtanaqā

“He spears them while they shoot; when they exchange spear thrusts, he fights with the sword; when they exchange sword blows, he grapples.”

Iʿtanaqā here describes grappling at close quarters.

The poet depicts increasingly close combat, from weapons used at a distance to bodily struggle. The final alif in iʿtanaqā lengthens the rhyme; it is not a dual subject marker.

Iʿtināq here does not mean an affectionate embrace.

The opponents are close enough to engage each other’s bodies and necks.

The family thus expressed close bodily contact as well as naming the neck in early Arabic.

The line is transmitted under the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr’s name, although its precise individual date is not independently established here.

ʿAnaq: a gait and brisk movement

Another branch describes the movement of animals.

Lexicons describe ʿanaq as an extended, relatively brisk gait. Descriptions differ in speed, so it need not mean maximum speed.

Aʿnaqa expresses moving briskly; muʿniq describes a person or animal advancing swiftly.

Here the word names a manner of movement rather than a body part.

A verse by Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjaj says:

لَمَّا رَأَتْنِي عَنَقِي دَبِيبُ
وَقَدْ أُرَى وَعَنَقِي سَرْحُوبُ

lammā raʾatnī ʿanaqī dabību / wa-qad urā wa-ʿanaqī sarḥūb

“When she saw my gait had become a slow crawl, though formerly my stride was long and swift.”

ʿAnaqī here concerns the speaker’s gait.

Ruʾbah lived in the Islamic period and died in the second Islamic century. This witness is not pre-Islamic.

Lexicons also use muʿniq for an elongated elevated stretch of terrain, projecting above surrounding flat land.

The family thus has branches concerning movement and extension.

Visual resemblance alone cannot prove that all these meanings historically arose directly from an animal’s neck movements.

ʿUnuq: a group of people

ʿUnuq is also recorded for a group of people.

Jāʾa al-qawmu ʿunuqan ʿunuqan means “the people came in groups.”

The word here identifies people moving together or forming a group, rather than an individual neck.

Some lexical accounts also use aʿnāq for prominent people or leaders.

An image of the head and neck as prominent body parts may help explain this, but does not independently establish its historical origin.

The collective usage matters in Ash-Shuara 26:4, where aʿnāquhum occurs with masculine plural khāḍiʿīna, inviting more than one linguistic explanation.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

ʿUnuq and ʿunq: neck, joining head and trunk.

Aʿnāq: necks; in some lexical uses, groups or prominent people.

ʿAniqa: became long-necked or thick-necked.

Aʿnaq: as an adjective, long-necked; the verb aʿnaqa means to move briskly.

ʿAnaq: a particular animal gait; ʿunuq is also recorded for a group of people.

Muʿniq: advancing swiftly; in terrain descriptions, an elevated elongated portion.

Miʿnāq: an animal capable of brisk forward movement.

ʿĀnaqa: embraced another.

Muʿānaqah: embracing and bringing necks together.

Taʿānaqa: embraced one another.

Iʿtanaqa: embraced or engaged another in close bodily contact.

ʿInāq: embracing; ʿanāq, with a different first vowel, denotes a young female goat.

ʿAnqāʾ: in one adjectival use, long-necked; also the name of a legendary bird in Arabic literature.

Later Arabic also uses iʿtināq for adopting an idea, belief, or religion.

“Embracing a religion” means accepting it, rather than physically holding it.

This usage must not be imposed on Quranic ʿunuq.

Only ʿunuq and its plural aʿnāq occur in the Quran. Aʿnaqa, ʿānaqa, iʿtanaqa, and the other derivatives discussed here do not.

3. ʿUnuq: the neck in human responsibility and conduct

Al-Isra 17:13: what remains attached to a person’s neck

وَكُلَّ إِنْسَانٍ أَلْزَمْنَاهُ طَائِرَهُ فِي عُنُقِهِ ۖ وَنُخْرِجُ لَهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ كِتَابًا يَلْقَاهُ مَنْشُورًا

wa-kulla insānin alzamnāhu ṭāʾirahu fī ʿunuqihi wa-nukhriju lahu yawma al-qiyāmati kitāban yalqāhu manshūrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:13

“We have bound every person’s fate to his neck; on Resurrection Day We shall bring out for him a book that he will find spread open.”

Al-Isra, 17:13

ʿUnuqihi identifies each person’s neck.

The sentence extends beyond bodily description: God has made the person’s ṭāʾir inseparable from him at his neck.

Ṭāʾir belongs to Ṭ Y R and here concerns a person’s fate, deeds, and outcome.

Alzamnāhu expresses making something binding and inseparable.

The image concerns something accompanying the person whose responsibility cannot be detached from him.

Why the neck?

The neck is visible and sensitive; collars, chains, or pendants can be placed around it.

Something attached there travels with its wearer.

A pendant remains with someone, whether moving or stationary, until removed.

Persian likewise uses the expression “having responsibility on one’s neck.”

It assigns responsibility to the person rather than describing a physical object actually resting on the neck.

This relationship helps explain the image in Al-Isra 17:13.

The figurative implication must not replace the noun’s lexical meaning.

ʿUnuq remains “neck”; inseparability comes from alzamnāhu and responsibility from the whole passage.

The neck and the record of deeds

The verse continues with a book brought out for each person on Resurrection Day, found open.

Al-Isra 17:14 tells the person to read that book, sufficient to reckon against himself.

The sequence concerns individual responsibility and confronting one’s own record.

An explanatory rendering can therefore say:

“We have bound each person’s record to his neck.”

“Record” is a contextual interpretation, rather than the lexical meaning of ʿunuq or a universal equivalent for ṭāʾir.

The image does not establish that people have been compelled from birth to perform every deed.

Al-Isra 17:15 assigns the consequences of guidance and error to each person and says that no bearer carries another’s burden.

The neck thus images responsibility remaining with the person, without denying choice.

Al-Isra 17:29: a hand bound to the neck

وَلَا تَجْعَلْ يَدَكَ مَغْلُولَةً إِلَىٰ عُنُقِكَ وَلَا تَبْسُطْهَا كُلَّ الْبَسْطِ فَتَقْعُدَ مَلُومًا مَحْسُورًا

wa-lā tajʿal yadaka maghlūlatan ilā ʿunuqika wa-lā tabsuṭhā kulla al-basṭi fa-taqʿuda malūman maḥsūrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:29

“Do not keep your hand bound to your neck, nor extend it completely, lest you remain blamed and destitute.”

Al-Isra, 17:29

ʿUnuqika denotes the addressee’s neck.

The passage concerns wealth and giving.

Al-Isra 17:26 commands giving relatives, the needy, and stranded travelers their due and avoiding waste.

The present verse then contrasts two images.

First, a hand tied to the neck, unable to extend.

Second, a hand extended without limit.

Why does the bound hand depict stinginess?

A hand literally tied to the neck would be restricted in many activities.

Its owner could not easily extend it to give something.

That image can express withholding generosity and assistance.

Someone may have sufficient wealth yet refuse a needy relative essential help or what is due.

Another may give away everything without assessing resources and become unable to meet basic needs.

The verse cautions against both behaviors.

ʿUnuq itself does not mean stinginess.

Stinginess follows from the bound-hand image and the context of giving.

Basṭ, extending the hand, belongs to a different root and must not be added to the meaning of ʿunuq.

The neck participates in an image restricting the hand; the Arabic word itself does not mean miserliness.

4. Aʿnāq: necks in shackles, captivity, and submission

Ar-Rad 13:5: shackles around the necks of resurrection-deniers

After mentioning their astonishment at renewed creation, Ar-Rad 13:5 says:

وَأُولَٰئِكَ الْأَغْلَالُ فِي أَعْنَاقِهِمْ

wa-ulāʾika al-aghlālu fī aʿnāqihim

Al-Raʿd 13:5

That is, shackles are around their necks.

Aʿnāqihim is the plural “their necks.”

Aghlāl is the plural of ghull, an instrument of binding and restraint.

Aʿnāq names the body part; aghlāl identifies what restrains it.

They must not be treated as a single root family.

Here shackled necks accompany the condition of people denying their Lord and resurrection.

The image can express inability to escape and entrapment in the consequences of conduct.

Whether these shackles refer specifically to punishment in the Hereafter or also portray denial and defiance requires examination of the broader passages.

Aʿnāq itself simply means necks.

Ya Sin 36:8: shackles reaching the chins

إِنَّا جَعَلْنَا فِي أَعْنَاقِهِمْ أَغْلَالًا فَهِيَ إِلَى الْأَذْقَانِ فَهُمْ مُقْمَحُونَ

innā jaʿalnā fī aʿnāqihim aghlālan fa-hiya ilā al-adhqāni fa-hum muqmaḥūn

Yā Sīn 36:8

“We have placed shackles around their necks, reaching their chins, so their heads are held up.”

Ya Sin, 36:8

Aʿnāqihim again identifies necks.

The image is more detailed than in Ar-Rad 13:5.

The shackles reach from the necks to the adhqān.

Adhqān is the plural of dhaqan, the chin.

The verse then says:

فَهُمْ مُقْمَحُونَ

fa-hum muqmaḥūn

Yā Sīn 36:8

“So their heads are held up.”

This describes heads held upward and restricted in movement.

Neck and chin are distinct

The neck and chin are different body parts.

The neck joins head and trunk.

The chin is part of the lower face.

Both occur in this verse, with their relationship shaping the image.

A tall, rigid collar surrounding the neck and reaching beneath the chin could restrict natural head movement.

It could prevent lowering the head, holding it unnaturally.

This example helps explain the image without claiming to identify the instrument’s precise shape or mechanism.

Ya Sin 36:9 describes barriers before and behind those addressed.

Ya Sin 36:10 then mentions people for whom warning or not warning has become the same.

The bound-neck image thus appears amid rejection of guidance and inability to see and receive it.

It can evoke obstructed understanding and entrapment in defiance; its details should not automatically be declared exclusively physical or exclusively metaphorical.

Saba 34:33: shackles and responsibility

Saba 34:33 describes an exchange between people kept weak in worldly life and the arrogant.

The former accuse the latter of constant scheming and commanding disbelief and assigning equals to God.

The verse then speaks of regret upon seeing punishment and says:

وَجَعَلْنَا الْأَغْلَالَ فِي أَعْنَاقِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا

wa-jaʿalnā al-aghlāla fī aʿnāqi alladhīna kafarū

Sabaʾ 34:33

That is, We placed shackles around the necks of those who disbelieved.

It concludes by asking whether they are recompensed except for what they did.

Aʿnāq still means necks.

The context connects the shackles with human conduct and its consequences.

It addresses the responsibility of those participating in disbelief while also describing arrogant leaders and their followers.

Misleading leaders therefore do not automatically remove followers’ moral responsibility.

That conclusion comes from the passage and the relation between deeds and recompense, rather than “neck” itself.

Ghafir 40:71: necks and chains

إِذِ الْأَغْلَالُ فِي أَعْنَاقِهِمْ وَالسَّلَاسِلُ يُسْحَبُونَ

idhi al-aghlālu fī aʿnāqihim wa-l-salāsilu yusḥabūn

Ghāfir 40:71

“When shackles and chains are around their necks, they will be dragged.”

Ghafir, 40:71

Aʿnāqihim again denotes necks.

Aghlāl and salāsil are distinct words.

Ghull concerns a binding restraint; silsilah denotes a chain.

Yusḥabūna then describes their being dragged.

The image contains necks, shackles, and chains, followed by dragging.

The setting is punishment in the Hereafter; Ghafir 40:72 describes boiling water and then burning in fire.

This is therefore a punishment scene.

The root ʿ N Q itself does not mean punishment or captivity.

It names the body part bearing the restraint; captivity and punishment come from other words and context.

Ash-Shuara 26:4: necks becoming submissive

إِنْ نَشَأْ نُنَزِّلْ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ آيَةً فَظَلَّتْ أَعْنَاقُهُمْ لَهَا خَاضِعِينَ

in nashaʾ nunazzil ʿalayhim mina al-samāʾi āyatan fa-ẓallat aʿnāquhum lahā khāḍiʿīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4

“If We will, We can send down upon them a sign from heaven, so their necks remain submissive before it.”

Ash-Shuara, 26:4

This verse is significant semantically and grammatically.

Aʿnāquhum in its familiar sense means their necks.

Khāḍiʿīna, however, is a masculine sound plural.

If agreement concerned only necks as body parts, a feminine form such as khāḍiʿah would normally be expected; the masculine plural requires explanation.

Two of the explanations recorded by lexicographers and grammarians are discussed here.

One takes aʿnāq as groups of people, allowing khāḍiʿīna to agree with their human members.

Another retains literal necks while assigning submission to their owners: the body part displays submission, but masculine plural agreement refers to the people.

Both explanations have a basis in Arabic analysis.

The first draws on the lexical sense “a group of people.”

The second emphasizes neck posture expressing human submission.

Lowering the head and neck before a power may signify the whole person’s submission.

A neck has no belief or will independently of its owner.

Thus, in the neck reading, the bodily image expresses people’s submission.

Submission and divine will

The verse begins with in nashaʾ, “if We will.”

It concerns the possibility of sending a sign before which the audience would submit.

Ash-Shuara 26:3 describes Muhammad’s distress over people’s failure to believe.

Verse 4 then expresses God’s ability to send a compelling heavenly sign producing submission.

The sequence distinguishes such compelling submission from the ordinary course of invitation and choice.

Aʿnāq alone does not mean compulsion, faith, or guidance.

Submission comes from khāḍiʿīna; its relation to the sign and divine will comes from the sentence.

Outward submission before a compelling sign should not automatically be equated with conscious faith and righteous action.

Keeping “their necks” is appropriate for the chosen rendering; the alternative grammatical readings belong in the explanation.

5. Necks in battle and descriptions of horses

Al-Anfal 8:12: necks in battle

إِذْ يُوحِي رَبُّكَ إِلَى الْمَلَائِكَةِ أَنِّي مَعَكُمْ فَثَبِّتُوا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا ۚ سَأُلْقِي فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الرُّعْبَ فَاضْرِبُوا فَوْقَ الْأَعْنَاقِ وَاضْرِبُوا مِنْهُمْ كُلَّ بَنَانٍ

idh yūḥī rabbuka ilā al-malāʾikati annī maʿakum fa-thabbitū alladhīna āmanū sa-ulqī fī qulūbi alladhīna kafarū al-ruʿba fa-ḍribū fawqa al-aʿnāqi wa-ḍribū minhum kulla banān

Al-Anfāl 8:12

“When your Lord revealed to the angels: I am with you, so strengthen those who believe. I shall cast terror into the hearts of those who disbelieved; strike above the necks and strike every fingertip of theirs.”

Al-Anfal, 8:12

Al-aʿnāqi denotes necks.

Fawqa al-aʿnāqi locates the blow above the necks. Other explanations, including the heads or striking at the necks, are also recorded; the rendering here preserves fawqa as “above.”

In battle, a blow there can cause severe injury or incapacitation.

The noun’s meaning must be distinguished from the action expressed in the sentence.

Aʿnāq means necks.

Fawqa identifies the location of the blow.

Iḍribū expresses striking.

ʿUnuq alone therefore does not mean killing, beheading, or attacking.

The passage concerns a particular battle and addresses angels and believers. A general command to harm every opponent cannot be extracted from the lexical meaning “neck.”

Sad 38:33: horses’ necks

رُدُّوهَا عَلَيَّ ۖ فَطَفِقَ مَسْحًا بِالسُّوقِ وَالْأَعْنَاقِ

ruddūhā ʿalayya fa-ṭafiqa masḥan bi-l-sūqi wa-l-aʿnāq

Ṣād 38:33

“Bring them back to me; then he began stroking their legs and necks.”

Sad, 38:33

Al-aʿnāqi appears alongside al-sūqi.

Sūq is the plural of sāq and here identifies the horses’ legs.

Sad 38:31 describes horses displayed before Solomon.

Al-aʿnāq consequently refers to those horses’ necks.

Does masḥan mean killing the horses?

Some exegetical accounts interpret the phrase as cutting the horses’ legs and necks.

A familiar meaning of masḥ is stroking or drawing something over a surface.

Classical usage also records masḥ for striking with a sword or cutting; the cutting interpretation therefore cannot simply be declared linguistically baseless.

No explicit verb such as slaughter, kill, or cut appears in the verse. That absence alone does not settle the interpretation of masḥan; context and lexical evidence must also be considered.

Sad 38:30 describes Solomon as a good servant who repeatedly turns to God.

The account then discusses the horses and his attention to them.

Stroking their legs and necks is a defensible reading, adopted here; Solomon’s commendation alone does not settle the lexical dispute.

For illustration, someone caring for horses may stroke and examine their necks and legs after having them brought back. This illustrates the reading rather than adding a reported detail to the verse.

Aʿnāq remains the horses’ necks.

The disagreement concerns masḥan and context, rather than the meaning of ʿ N Q.

6. Distinctions from related roots

ʿ N Q (ʿunuq) and R Q B (raqabah)

Both ʿunuq and raqabah can denote the neck.

Raqabah also has Quranic uses concerning freeing a person from bondage.

An-Nisa 4:92, for example, mentions freeing a raqabatin muʾminatin.

The expression refers to a believing person to be freed, rather than merely a physical neck.

ʿUnuq in Al-Isra 17:13 instead participates in an image of something bound inseparably to a person.

The words can name the same body part while differing in extended uses and constructions.

ʿ N Q (ʿunuq) and J Y D (jīd)

Jīd likewise means neck, particularly in descriptions of its beauty and adornment.

Al-Masad 111:5 says:

فِي جِيدِهَا حَبْلٌ مِنْ مَسَدٍ

fī jīdihā ḥablun min masad

Al-Masad 111:5

“Around her neck is a rope of twisted fiber.”

Jīd denotes the neck bearing the rope.

ʿUnuq also names the neck, but its Quranic images include restraints, responsibility, submission, and horses.

Shared bodily reference does not transfer every rhetorical feature from one word to the other.

ʿ N Q (ʿunuq) and DH Q N (dhaqan)

Dhaqan denotes the chin.

ʿUnuq denotes the neck.

Ya Sin 36:8 clearly distinguishes them within one sentence.

Shackles around the necks reach the chins.

Aʿnāqihim and adhqān are therefore not synonyms.

The neck joins head and trunk; the chin belongs to the lower face.

Their spatial relationship shapes the verse’s image.

ʿ N Q (ʿunuq) and GH L L (ghull)

Ghull concerns an instrument of restraint.

ʿUnuq names the neck.

The families occur together in Ar-Rad 13:5, Saba 34:33, Ya Sin 36:8, and Ghafir 40:71.

The neck itself does not mean a shackle or chain.

A neck may bear a necklace or a restraint.

The body part remains the same; the distinction comes from what surrounds it and the context.

ʿUnuq must therefore not invariably be translated as captivity or bondage.

ʿ N Q (muʿānaqah) and Ḍ M M (ḍamm)

Ḍamma concerns bringing things together.

ʿĀnaqa, in embracing, also involves bringing two bodies close.

Not every act of joining is an embrace.

Placing two books together may be ḍamm, but not muʿānaqah.

Muʿānaqah involves bodily contact, generally with arms around another’s body or neck.

It therefore names a more specific physical relationship than general joining or approaching.

ʿ N Q (ʿunuq) and ʿ N N (ʿinān)

ʿInān concerns reins or an animal’s controlling equipment.

ʿUnuq identifies the animal’s neck.

Reins may lie near the head and neck, but the words belong to different roots.

Aʿnāq in Sad 38:33 denotes horses’ necks, rather than reins.

Thematic proximity does not merge their lexical families.

ʿ N Q (ʿunuq) and ʿ N K B (ʿankabūt)

Their opening letters resemble one another, but the roots are distinct.

ʿ N Q concerns the neck and the branches discussed here.

ʿ N K B is the four-consonant lexical root under which ʿankabūt, spider, is classified.

Shared initial letters do not place “spider” in the neck family.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Neck” is the basic equivalent in the Quranic readings adopted here. Further meanings follow from surrounding words and context.

ʿUnuqihi — Al-Isra 17:13

Brief root meaning: Neck, joining head and trunk and capable of bearing something attached.

Meaning in this verse: Each person’s neck in the image of inseparable fate and responsibility.

Suggested translation: “His neck.”

The explanation may clarify individual responsibility, without replacing the lexical meaning of ʿunuq with “responsibility.”

ʿUnuqika — Al-Isra 17:29

Brief root meaning: Neck, between head and trunk.

Meaning in this verse: The addressee’s neck in the bound-hand image of withholding generosity.

Suggested translation: “Your neck.”

Phrase translation:

“Do not keep your hand bound to your neck.”

Al-aʿnāqi — Al-Anfal 8:12

Brief root meaning: Neck, joining head and body; its plural is aʿnāq.

Meaning in this verse: Opponents’ necks in battle.

Suggested translation: “The necks.”

The phrase should also preserve the separate contribution of fawqa.

Aʿnāqihim — Ar-Rad 13:5

Brief root meaning: Neck, joining head and trunk.

Meaning in this verse: The necks of those bearing restraints.

Suggested translation: “Their necks.”

Aʿnāquhum — Ash-Shuara 26:4

Brief root meaning: Neck; in extended lexical usage, a group or community.

Meaning in this verse: The audience’s necks depicting submission, with an alternative reading referring to groups of people.

Suggested translation: “Their necks.”

Aʿnāqi — Saba 34:33

Brief root meaning: Neck, joining head and trunk.

Meaning in this verse: The necks of those who disbelieved, bearing restraints.

Suggested translation: “The necks of.”

Aʿnāqihim — Ya Sin 36:8

Brief root meaning: Neck, between head and trunk.

Meaning in this verse: Their necks bearing restraints that reach their chins.

Suggested translation: “Their necks.”

Al-aʿnāqi — Sad 38:33

Brief root meaning: A human or animal neck, joining head and trunk.

Meaning in this verse: The necks of horses brought back to Solomon.

Suggested translation: “The necks.”

Phrase translation:

“Then he began stroking their legs and necks.”

Aʿnāqihim — Ghafir 40:71

Brief root meaning: Neck, joining head and trunk.

Meaning in this verse: The necks of those bound in shackles and chains in the punishment scene.

Suggested translation: “Their necks.”

“Neck” remains the basic equivalent in these chosen readings. Submission, responsibility, captivity, stinginess, or striking follow from the construction and should not be inserted into the noun’s definition.

8. Conclusion

The root has several Arabic branches, most clearly the human or animal neck.

ʿUnuq connects head and trunk; aʿnāq is its plural.

Related uses include muʿānaqah, embracing and bringing necks together.

Other branches include ʿanaq for an animal gait, ʿunuq for a group of people, and muʿniq for one moving swiftly.

All Quranic forms belong to the nominal family ʿunuq and aʿnāq.

Al-Isra 17:13 uses the neck to depict inseparable responsibility and one’s record.

Al-Isra 17:29 uses the hand bound to the neck to depict withholding generosity.

Al-Anfal 8:12 mentions necks in battle.

Ar-Rad 13:5, Saba 34:33, Ya Sin 36:8, and Ghafir 40:71 depict restraint through shackles and chains around necks.

Ash-Shuara 26:4 depicts submission; the construction permits examining the linguistic readings discussed above.

Sad 38:33 mentions horses’ necks alongside their legs.

The neck participates in physical, moral, and eschatological imagery without its lexical meaning equaling every implication of those images.

Final definition of ʿ N Q:

“Principally, the human or animal neck, joining head and trunk. Related uses concern embracing and close bodily contact. Other branches express a brisk gait, extended or projecting terrain, and groups of people. In the Quranic readings adopted here, ʿunuq and aʿnāq mean neck and necks; responsibility, submission, restraint, and punishment follow from construction and context. An alternative collective reading of aʿnāq is also recorded for Ash-Shuara 26:4.”
