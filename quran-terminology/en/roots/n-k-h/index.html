Nikāḥ: marriage, the marriage contract, and its distinction from sexual intercourse

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The three-letter Arabic root N K Ḥ is associated with marriage and sexual relations. Its two principal uses concern establishing a marriage between a woman and a man, and sexual intercourse. These meanings are related but distinct. Marriage establishes a recognized human and social relationship, whereas intercourse denotes a bodily act.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs mainly in discussions of marriage, forming a marital relationship, its conditions and restrictions, divorce, and associated rights. Understanding nikāḥ therefore requires both knowledge of its lexical meanings and attention to whether a particular verse concerns marriage itself or another aspect of the relationship between women and men.

One especially important feature of Qur’anic usage is that marriage can already exist before sexual intercourse has occurred. Nikāḥ therefore cannot simply mean intercourse in every verse; equally, its sexual use cannot be erased from the history of Arabic.

2. Forms and derivatives

The root N K Ḥ occurs 23 times in the Qur’an, in 19 verses and four morphological groups:

  • نَكَحَ، يَنْكِحُ، تَنْكِحُ and other Form I conjugations: to marry or enter a marriage.
  • أَنْكَحَ، يُنْكِحُ and related forms: to marry someone to another person, or bring about a marriage.
  • يَسْتَنْكِحَ: to seek or wish to marry, in the particular context of Al-Aḥzāb 33:50.
  • نِكَاح: marriage, a marital bond, or the possibility or stage associated with it, depending on the sentence.

Qur’anic forms such as تَنْكِحُوا, يَنْكِحْنَ, فَانْكِحُوهُنَّ, أَنْكِحُوا, and نَكَحْتُمُ derive from this same root. Differences in person, conjugation, prefix, or attached conjunction do not create a new root.

Other forms, including ناكح, ناكحة, and نُكَحَة, are recorded in Arabic dictionaries but do not belong to the four groups in which this root occurs in the Qur’an.

3. The root in early Arabic

Marriage or intercourse: which meaning came first?

Early dictionaries do not agree on the original meaning of nikāḥ.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies the root’s central meaning with biḍāʿ, a term associated with sexual intercourse. Yet he explicitly states in the same entry that nikāḥ can denote the marriage contract without intercourse.

Al-Azharī and Al-Jawharī likewise treat the sexual use as a principal meaning while acknowledging the use for marriage.

By contrast, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī maintains in Al-Mufradāt that nikāḥ originally denoted the marriage contract and was subsequently extended to intercourse.

Al-Rāghib’s argument relies largely on how sexual matters are expressed in Arabic. It does not by itself establish the historical order in which the meanings arose. Nor does a dictionary’s designation of intercourse as the original meaning settle that history.

The evidence clearly establishes that both uses exist in Arabic. Determining which arose first historically requires more than comparing dictionary definitions.

Poetry and Arab social practices

A verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā, a poet who lived into the period when Islam emerged, reads:

وَلَا تَقْرَبَنَّ جَارَةً إِنَّ سِرَّهَا
عَلَيْكَ حَرَامٌ فَانْكِحَنْ أَوْ تَأَبَّدَا

wa-lā taqrabanna jāratan inna sirrahā / ʿalayka ḥarāmun fa-nkiḥan aw taʾabbadā

“Do not approach a neighboring woman when intimacy with her is forbidden to you; marry, or else keep away from women.”

Here, the command inkaḥ is contrasted with an illicit approach to a woman. Its appropriate meaning is therefore to marry, rather than simply to have intercourse.

Literary sources attribute the verse to Al-Aʿshā. Because he lived across the transition from the pre-Islamic to the Islamic period, and the precise date of this passage is uncertain, it should not be presented without qualification as securely predating the Qur’an. It nevertheless has value as evidence for early Arabic close to the period of revelation.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the witness attributed to Al-Aʿshā

Another account of Arab marriage practices describes a brief exchange between a suitor and a woman’s family. The suitor would say:

خِطْبٌ

khiṭbun

Meaning: I have come to propose marriage.

The response would be:

نِكْحٌ

nikḥun

Meaning: the marriage proposal has been accepted.

Dictionaries and collections of Arab proverbs preserve this expression, including the famous story of Umm Khārijah. In that version, the woman herself replies nikḥ; the accounts should therefore not all be described as exchanges with her family. The narrative details require independent historical examination, but the linguistic contrast between proposing and accepting marriage shows that the lexical tradition recognized nikāḥ as establishing a marital relationship.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Muḥkam, and Lisān al-ʿArab: khiṭb and nikḥ

Uses beyond human relationships

Dictionaries also record expressions such as:

نَكَحَ المَطَرُ الأَرْضَ

nakaḥa al-maṭaru al-arḍa

Meaning: the rain came down upon the earth and affected it.

And:

نَكَحَ النُّعَاسُ عَيْنَهُ

nakaḥa al-nuʿāsu ʿaynahu

Meaning: drowsiness overcame his eye.

These uses can illustrate contact, reaching, affecting, and overcoming in Arabic. They are insufficient, however, to prove that the entire N K Ḥ family originally meant only “two things adhering,” “interpenetration,” or “one thing overcoming another.”

These expressions are recorded in dictionaries. Unless their dates are independently established, they should not be presented as securely pre-Islamic evidence.

It is therefore preferable to avoid inventing a hypothetical physical image for the root, and instead examine its attested uses for marriage and intercourse through their contexts and linguistic evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: rain, earth, and drowsiness

4. Qur’anic uses

A. Nikāḥ as a marital relationship

One of the clearest Qur’anic examples appears in Al-Aḥzāb:

Al-Aḥzāb 33:49

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا نَكَحْتُمُ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ ثُمَّ طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ فَمَا لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِنَّ مِنْ عِدَّةٍ تَعْتَدُّونَهَا فَمَتِّعُوهُنَّ وَسَرِّحُوهُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā nakaḥtumu al-muʾmināti thumma ṭallaqtumūhunna min qabli an tamassūhunna fa-mā lakum ʿalayhinna min ʿiddatin taʿtaddūnahā fa-mattiʿūhunna wa-sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan jamīlan

“You who have believed, when you marry believing women and then divorce them before touching them in marital intimacy, there is no waiting period for you to count for them. So provide for them and release them graciously.”

The sequence is clear: marriage occurs first; divorce may then occur even though mass, or marital intimacy, has not yet taken place.

In this verse, nakaḥtumu cannot mean that intercourse has occurred. Nikāḥ is the marriage relationship, which can exist without consummation and can be ended by divorce.

This is an important guide to the root’s Qur’anic use. Its sexual meaning in early Arabic does not authorize transferring that meaning to every Qur’anic occurrence.

B. Nikāḥ as a bond that can be contracted

Al-Baqarah 2:235 contains the expression عُقْدَةَ النِّكَاحِ, “the marriage bond.”

ʿUqdah belongs to a different root and denotes a knot or a bound connection. Its combination with al-nikāḥ shows that the verse concerns a marriage whose establishment has a specified time and limits.

The same verse distinguishes proposing marriage or expressing interest indirectly from resolving to conclude the marriage bond. Interest, a proposal, and marriage itself are not a single stage.

Al-Baqarah 2:237 also uses عُقْدَةُ النِّكَاحِ in the context of divorce before marital intimacy and the woman’s financial rights.

Both passages associate nikāḥ with a committed relationship having legal consequences. The word alone does not specify every detail of forming that bond, its social or administrative formalities, or who has decision-making authority. These questions require examination of the relevant verses together and the precise wording of each provision.

The essential point is that ʿuqdah makes the contracted bond explicit. Its entire semantic contribution should not be assigned to N K Ḥ itself.

C. Nikāḥ and the wishes of both parties

Al-Baqarah 2:232 states:

وَإِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَبَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَلَا تَعْضُلُوهُنَّ أَنْ يَنْكِحْنَ أَزْوَاجَهُنَّ إِذَا تَرَاضَوْا بَيْنَهُمْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ذَٰلِكَ يُوعَظُ بِهِ مَنْ كَانَ مِنْكُمْ يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ ذَٰلِكُمْ أَزْكَىٰ لَكُمْ وَأَطْهَرُ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنْتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

wa-idhā ṭallaqtumu al-nisāʾa fa-balaghna ajalahunna fa-lā taʿḍulūhunna an yankiḥna azwājahunna idhā tarāḍaw baynahum bi-al-maʿrūfi dhālika yūʿaẓu bihi man kāna minkum yuʾminu bi-allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri dhālikum azkā lakum wa-aṭharu wa-allāhu yaʿlamu wa-antum lā taʿlamūna

“When you divorce women and they reach the end of their waiting period, do not prevent them from marrying their husbands when they mutually agree on acceptable terms. This admonishes whoever among you believes in The God and the Last Day. That is more wholesome and purer for you. The God knows, and you do not know.”

The verb yankiḥna has the women themselves as its subject. They are the ones marrying, and the verse prohibits others from preventing that marriage when there is mutual agreement on acceptable terms.

Mutual agreement comes from the separate expression تَرَاضَوْا بَيْنَهُمْ, rather than from the root nikāḥ. Their combination nevertheless matters: in this context, marriage is connected with the decisions and agreement of its participants.

The verse also shows that this verb is not restricted to a male subject. A woman can likewise be the subject of nakaḥa.

In Al-Baqarah 2:230, a woman is also the subject of tankaḥa: the verse concerns her marriage to another husband. The verb’s form therefore does not itself mean that only the man acts while the woman is merely the object of an action.

D. Nakaḥa and ankaḥa

Forms I and IV have distinct uses in the Qur’an.

Al-Baqarah 2:221 uses both constructions in a single verse:

وَلَا تَنْكِحُوا الْمُشْرِكَاتِ حَتَّىٰ يُؤْمِنَّ وَلَأَمَةٌ مُؤْمِنَةٌ خَيْرٌ مِنْ مُشْرِكَةٍ وَلَوْ أَعْجَبَتْكُمْ وَلَا تُنْكِحُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ حَتَّىٰ يُؤْمِنُوا وَلَعَبْدٌ مُؤْمِنٌ خَيْرٌ مِنْ مُشْرِكٍ وَلَوْ أَعْجَبَكُمْ أُولَٰئِكَ يَدْعُونَ إِلَى النَّارِ وَاللَّهُ يَدْعُو إِلَى الْجَنَّةِ وَالْمَغْفِرَةِ بِإِذْنِهِ وَيُبَيِّنُ آيَاتِهِ لِلنَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَذَكَّرُونَ

wa-lā tankiḥū al-mushrikāti ḥattā yuʾminna wa-la-amatun muʾminatun khayrun min mushrikatin wa-law aʿjabatkum wa-lā tunkiḥū al-mushrikīna ḥattā yuʾminū wa-la-ʿabdun muʾminun khayrun min mushrikin wa-law aʿjabakum ulāʾika yadʿūna ilā al-nāri wa-allāhu yadʿū ilā al-jannati wa-al-maghfirati bi-idhnihi wa-yubayyinu āyātihi li-al-nāsi laʿallahum yatadhakkarūna

“Do not marry women who associate partners until they believe. A believing enslaved woman is better than a woman who associates partners, even if she impresses you. And do not marry women to men who associate partners until they believe. A believing enslaved man is better than a man who associates partners, even if he impresses you. They call toward the Fire, while The God calls toward the Garden and forgiveness by His permission, and makes His signs clear to people so that they may take heed.”

In the first part, تَنْكِحُوا is Form I and concerns the addressees themselves marrying.

In the second, تُنْكِحُوا is Form IV and means marrying someone else to another person.

The different vowels mark a real morphological and semantic difference, even though the forms look almost identical without vowel marks.

The same causative construction occurs in Al-Nūr 24:32:

وَأَنْكِحُوا الْأَيَامَىٰ مِنْكُمْ وَالصَّالِحِينَ مِنْ عِبَادِكُمْ وَإِمَائِكُمْ إِنْ يَكُونُوا فُقَرَاءَ يُغْنِهِمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

wa-ankiḥū al-ayāmā minkum wa-al-ṣāliḥīna min ʿibādikum wa-imāʾikum in yakūnū fuqarāʾa yughnihimu allāhu min faḍlihi wa-allāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīmun

“Arrange marriages for the unmarried among you and the worthy among your enslaved men and women. If they are poor, The God will enrich them from His bounty. The God is vast in provision and knowing.”

Ankiḥū is a command assigning the addressees responsibility for bringing about other people’s marriages. It does not directly mean that the addressees themselves should marry.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:27, a man says to Moses:

قَالَ إِنِّي أُرِيدُ أَنْ أُنْكِحَكَ إِحْدَى ابْنَتَيَّ هَاتَيْنِ عَلَىٰ أَنْ تَأْجُرَنِي ثَمَانِيَ حِجَجٍ فَإِنْ أَتْمَمْتَ عَشْرًا فَمِنْ عِنْدِكَ وَمَا أُرِيدُ أَنْ أَشُقَّ عَلَيْكَ سَتَجِدُنِي إِنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

qāla innī urīdu an unkiḥaka iḥdā ibnatayya hātayni ʿalā an taʾjuranī thamāniya ḥijajin fa-in atmamta ʿashran fa-min ʿindika wa-mā urīdu an ashuqqa ʿalayka sa-tajidunī in shāʾa allāhu mina al-ṣāliḥīna

“He said: I wish to marry one of these two daughters of mine to you, on condition that you work for me for eight years. If you complete ten, that will be of your own accord. I do not wish to make things difficult for you. If The God wills, you will find me among the worthy.”

Here, unkiḥaka means that the speaker wishes to marry one of his daughters to Moses.

This illustrates another person’s role in proposing or bringing about a marriage. The verb alone does not establish that the woman’s own wishes and consent are irrelevant or that all marriages must follow this arrangement. The story reports a particular proposal; general marital rights and conditions require examination of other verses.

E. Istinkāḥ: seeking marriage

Al-Aḥzāb 33:50 contains the form يَسْتَنْكِحَهَا.

The context concerns a believing woman offering herself to the Prophet, followed by the Prophet’s wish to marry her.

Form X can express seeking or requesting, but that function is not identical in every verb or context.

Here, إِنْ أَرَادَ النَّبِيُّ explicitly introduces the Prophet’s wish and intention concerning the marriage. In this context, yastankiḥahā may be rendered “wish to marry her” or “take her as his wife.”

The Form X pattern alone does not establish a separate ceremony for requesting marriage or its legal form. The verse’s qualification that this is a special provision must also be retained; this particular use does not by itself establish a rule for every marriage.

F. Nikāḥ, maturity, and the prospect of marriage

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 states:

وَابْتَلُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغُوا النِّكَاحَ فَإِنْ آنَسْتُمْ مِنْهُمْ رُشْدًا فَادْفَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ وَلَا تَأْكُلُوهَا إِسْرَافًا وَبِدَارًا أَنْ يَكْبَرُوا وَمَنْ كَانَ غَنِيًّا فَلْيَسْتَعْفِفْ وَمَنْ كَانَ فَقِيرًا فَلْيَأْكُلْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ فَإِذَا دَفَعْتُمْ إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فَأَشْهِدُوا عَلَيْهِمْ وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ حَسِيبًا

wa-ibtalū al-yatāmā ḥattā idhā balaghū al-nikāḥa fa-in ānastum minhum rushdan fa-idfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum wa-lā taʾkulūhā isrāfan wa-bidāran an yakbarū wa-man kāna ghaniyyan fa-l-yastaʿfif wa-man kāna faqīran fa-l-yaʾkul bi-al-maʿrūfi fa-idhā dafaʿtum ilayhim amwālahum fa-ashhidū ʿalayhim wa-kafā bi-allāhi ḥasīban

“Test the orphans until they reach the stage of marriage. Then, if you perceive sound judgment in them, hand over their property to them. Do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up. Whoever is wealthy should refrain, and whoever is poor may consume on acceptable terms. When you hand over their property, have witnesses present. The God suffices as a reckoner.”

Balaghū al-nikāḥa does not mean that the orphans have already married or had intercourse. It speaks of reaching a stage at which marriage becomes relevant for them.

Physical maturity is important to understanding this stage, but nikāḥ itself should not simply be equated with puberty. Nikāḥ is not the name of a biological stage; the construction concerns reaching the threshold of marriage.

The verse then states فَإِنْ آنَسْتُمْ مِنْهُمْ رُشْدًا as a condition for handing over property. Reaching the stage of marriage and demonstrating sound judgment in managing property are therefore distinct elements in the sentence.

This distinction prevents the conditions of physical maturity, intellectual development, financial competence, and readiness for marriage from all being compressed into the lexical meaning of one word.

G. Nikāḥ, rights, justice, and social circumstances

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, fankiḥū follows concern about fairness toward orphans. The verse then discusses the number of spouses and the condition of justice.

Here, nikāḥ means marriage. Fairness, the number of wives, and fear of injustice come from the verse as a whole, rather than from the root’s lexical meaning.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:127 uses tankiḥūhunna in discussing orphaned women who are not being given their prescribed rights. Marriage can thus arise in circumstances where women’s financial and social rights need protection. The verb itself does not mean deprivation of rights; justice or injustice is expressed by other parts of the verse.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 concerns marriage involving believing women in circumstances of enslavement or another person’s control. Marriage appears alongside permission from their people, payment of financial dues, chastity, and avoidance of uncommitted sexual relations.

This distinguishes marriage from sexual relations outside commitment. Nevertheless, بِإِذْنِ أَهْلِهِنَّ qualifies the particular group discussed in the verse; extending it to all marriages requires independent evidence.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:22 prohibits marrying women whom one’s fathers married. The prohibition concerns a particular family relationship, identified by the complete sentence. The root nikāḥ itself does not mean a prohibited relationship or prohibition.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:53 prohibits marrying the Prophet’s wives after him. Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:10 discusses marrying believing emigrant women while observing the financial rights specified in that verse. Both concern marriage; their conditions, addressees, and particular provisions differ.

H. Nikāḥ and its distinction from illicit sexual relations

Al-Nūr 24:3 is an important passage whose use of nakaḥa has received differing interpretations:

الزَّانِي لَا يَنْكِحُ إِلَّا زَانِيَةً أَوْ مُشْرِكَةً وَالزَّانِيَةُ لَا يَنْكِحُهَا إِلَّا زَانٍ أَوْ مُشْرِكٌ وَحُرِّمَ ذَٰلِكَ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

al-zānī lā yankiḥu illā zāniyatan aw mushrikatan wa-al-zāniyatu lā yankiḥuhā illā zānin aw mushrikun wa-ḥurrima dhālika ʿalā al-muʾminīna

“A man who commits illicit intercourse marries only a woman who commits illicit intercourse or associates partners; and a woman who commits illicit intercourse is married only by a man who commits illicit intercourse or associates partners. That is forbidden to the believers.”

Lexical and exegetical sources record two readings: some take yankiḥu here as marriage, while others understand sexual intercourse.

The marriage reading accords with the root’s predominant Qur’anic use and a construction describing relationships with specified groups. The concluding prohibition also places the verse in an ethical and legal setting. Nevertheless, the sexual meaning in Arabic and the discussion of zinā have led to the second reading in the lexical and exegetical tradition.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing readings of nikāḥ in Al-Nūr 24:3

A final assessment requires studying the differences between nikāḥ, zinā, iḥṣān, and sifāḥ across the relevant verses. The mere lexical possibility of intercourse does not prove that this must be the meaning here.

Conversely, translating nikāḥ as marriage should not lead to the hasty conclusion that a person’s moral condition can never change after wrongdoing. Repentance and reform require separate examination. Al-Nūr 24:5 directly concerns the repentance and reform of the accusers mentioned in Al-Nūr 24:4; it does not directly state the marriage ruling for people who commit zinā. Al-Furqān 25:68, Al-Furqān 25:69, Al-Furqān 25:70, and Al-Furqān 25:71 are more directly relevant to repentance from zinā. Its legal consequences remain a separate question.

Al-Ṭabarī: repentance of accusers in Al-Nūr 24:5

I. Nikāḥ as the possibility or hope of marriage

The noun nikāḥ does not always occur in a statement reporting that a marriage has taken place.

Al-Nūr 24:33 states:

وَلْيَسْتَعْفِفِ الَّذِينَ لَا يَجِدُونَ نِكَاحًا

wa-l-yastaʿfifi alladhīna lā yajidūna nikāḥan

Those who cannot find the possibility of marriage should remain chaste.

Lā yajidūna nikāḥan does not itself mean only lacking money. The verse continues by mentioning enrichment through God’s bounty, and economic constraints may be one obstacle. More broadly, the expression concerns not finding the possibility of marriage.

Al-Nūr 24:60 describes women who لَا يَرْجُونَ نِكَاحًا: they have no expectation or hope of marriage.

Here, nikāḥ denotes neither the occurrence of intercourse nor necessarily a particular contract now being concluded. It is the prospect or hope of marriage in their circumstances.

The noun can thus concern the marital bond itself or the possibility and expectation of it. The sentence determines the meaning.

J. Remarriage after divorce

Al-Baqarah 2:230 discusses a woman’s marriage to another husband after a specified stage of divorce. تَنْكِحَ زَوْجًا غَيْرَهُ indicates entering marriage with a husband other than the previous one.

Nakaḥa and zawj occur together: the verb concerns establishing marriage, while zawj identifies the person who is the other party to that bond.

Exegetical and legal traditions discuss consummation of the second marriage, and the prevalent juristic view requires it. Alongside interpretation of the verse, an important basis is reports such as ʿĀʾishah’s narration in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, hadith 5261. This is therefore more than a late dispute about a word. Lexically, nikāḥ alone does not establish consummation as a condition: Al-Aḥzāb 33:49 explicitly recognizes a marriage in which marital intimacy has not yet occurred.

Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5261: a transmitted basis for the consummation discussion

This observation does not settle the entire legal question of Al-Baqarah 2:230. Any additional conditions require independent evidence, rather than automatically inserting intercourse into every occurrence of nikāḥ.

5. Related roots and expressions

N K Ḥ (nakaḥa) and Z W J (zawj)

These families overlap in discussions of marriage but have different semantic ranges.

In the Qur’an, N K Ḥ mainly concerns establishing marriage, its possibility, or people’s roles in bringing it about.

Zawj has a broader range, including a spouse, a mate, pairing, and corresponding or companion kinds. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:49 and Yā Sīn 36:36, for example, discuss pairing in creation rather than marriage contracts.

The predominant Qur’anic use of nikāḥ concerns forming a marital relationship, but not every use of zawj means nikāḥ.

Study of Z W J

N K Ḥ (nakaḥa) and ʿ Q D (ʿaqada)

ʿAqd denotes binding and securing a connection or commitment. It applies to different subjects.

In عُقْدَةَ النِّكَاحِ, the two semantic families meet: nikāḥ identifies marriage as the subject, and ʿuqdah highlights the bond being concluded and established.

This does not make nikāḥ and ʿaqd complete synonyms throughout Arabic.

Study of ʿ Q D

N K Ḥ (nakaḥa) and M S S (massa)

M S S denotes contact and touching. In some passages on marriage and divorce, it refers to marital intimacy.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:49 and Al-Baqarah 2:237 clearly distinguish marriage from marital intimacy. Marriage may already exist although sexual contact has not occurred.

This is among the clearest reasons not to translate nikāḥ and intercourse identically in every verse.

Study of M S S

N K Ḥ (nakaḥa), Z N Y (zanā), and S F Ḥ (safaḥa)

Zinā and sifāḥ differ semantically from nikāḥ in passages concerning sexual relations.

Zinā designates illicit sexual conduct. غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ and similar expressions in Al-Nisāʾ 4:24, Al-Nisāʾ 4:25, and Al-Māʾidah 5:5 distinguish the relevant relationship from sex without commitment and a legitimate framework.

Within this network, nikāḥ is a recognized and responsible relationship. Yet its lexical meaning should not absorb all the ethical, legal, economic, and emotional characteristics of marriage. Those emerge from the Qur’an’s provisions and descriptions together.

N K Ḥ (nakaḥa) and KH Ṭ B (khaṭaba)

Proposing marriage and marriage itself are also distinct.

Al-Baqarah 2:235 discusses expressing interest and proposing marriage alongside the prohibition on resolving to conclude the marriage bond before the specified period ends.

A proposal is the stage of offering marriage and expressing interest; nikāḥ concerns establishing the marital bond itself.

This distinction matters in translation: not every proposal or promise of marriage constitutes an accomplished marriage.

6. Translation suggestions for Forqan

In most Qur’anic uses, the most natural equivalents are “to marry” or “to take as a spouse.”

For Form I, the choice between “to marry,” “to marry someone,” and “to take as a spouse” depends on the subject, object, and context. Where a woman is the subject, the translation should preserve her role.

For Form IV, “to marry someone to another person” or “to bring about someone’s marriage” is appropriate. أُنْكِحَكَ إِحْدَى ابْنَتَيَّ, for example, means “marry one of my two daughters to you.”

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:50, yastankiḥahā may be rendered “wish to marry her” or “wish to take her as his wife,” reflecting the surrounding expression of wish and intention.

Depending on the sentence, nikāḥ can be rendered “marriage,” “the marital bond,” “the marriage contract,” “the stage of marriage,” or “the possibility of marriage.” For example:

  • عُقْدَةَ النِّكَاحِ: the marriage contract or concluded marriage bond.
  • بَلَغُوا النِّكَاحَ: they reached the stage or age of marriage.
  • لَا يَجِدُونَ نِكَاحًا: they cannot find the possibility of marriage.
  • لَا يَرْجُونَ نِكَاحًا: they have no hope or expectation of marriage.

Where the Qur’an itself speaks of sexual relations, “sexual intercourse” or “sexual intimacy” can be clear equivalents. Nikāḥ should not, however, be translated this way in every verse merely because dictionaries record its sexual meaning.

Nor should “marriage contract” be restricted without evidence to administrative formalities, a particular person or institution, or one culture’s legal format. Such matters, when relevant, belong in a separate study of marriage provisions.

7. Conclusion

N K Ḥ has two recognized Arabic uses: marriage and intercourse. The evidence examined does not conclusively establish which came first historically. Qur’anic occurrences, however, mainly concern marriage and the marital relationship.

Morphological distinctions matter too. Nakaḥa usually means marrying; ankaḥa means marrying another person to someone; and istankaḥa, in its Qur’anic context, concerns seeking marriage. The noun nikāḥ may denote marriage itself or its stage and possibility.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:49 is the principal example distinguishing marriage from intercourse, since it recognizes marriage and divorce before marital intimacy. ʿUqdat al-nikāḥ also places nikāḥ in a bond that can be contracted and has legal consequences.

“Marriage” and “the marital bond” are therefore the most appropriate starting points for translating this family in general Qur’anic usage. Where a sexual meaning is proposed, it must be assessed using independent evidence from that particular verse.

Nikāḥ should not be reduced to a bodily relationship. Equally, its lexical meaning should not absorb all the desirable qualities of marriage, such as tranquility, affection, justice, consent, fidelity, and responsibility. These come from the wider network of verses about the marital relationship.

A proposed definition for Qur’anic usage: N K Ḥ is a linguistic family concerning marriage. Its Qur’anic verbal and nominal forms denote forming a marital bond, marrying another person to someone, seeking marriage, or its possibility. Its sexual use is attested in early Arabic, but must be assessed separately from marriage when interpreting each verse. Brief root meaning: Marriage and establishing a marital bond; marrying someone to another or seeking marriage, according to form; a use for intercourse is also attested in Arabic.
