Turning, turning back, and changing direction: from directing winds and averting harm to presenting the signs in varied ways

The Arabic root Ṣ R F concerns turning something from one direction to another, turning it back, averting it, changing its course, and moving between different conditions. Something may be redirected from its course, guided toward another destination, averted from someone, or appear in different forms and states.

The family includes ṣarf, turning and turning back; taṣrīf, directing in different directions or forms; inṣirāf, returning or turning away; and maṣrif, a way or place of return and redirection.

The root occurs 30 times in 29 Qur’anic verses, in seven morphological and lexical categories: fourteen Form I verbs, ten Form II verbs, one inṣarafū, one ṣarf verbal noun, one maṣrif, one maṣrūf, and two occurrences of the Form II verbal noun taṣrīf.

Its Qur’anic uses cover directing and varying winds, presenting the signs in varied ways, averting harm and punishment, turning eyes and hearts, leaving a gathering, directing a group toward a destination, and inability to find an escape.

The root’s central feature is changing direction or course, but this does not necessarily mean departing from truth or going astray. Something may be averted from danger, diverted from the right way, guided toward a suitable destination, or expressed and presented in varied forms.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 30 occurrences in seven categories

1. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Turning something from one direction to another

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

مُعْظَمُ بَابِهِ يَدُلُّ عَلَى رَجْعِ الشَّيْءِ

muʿẓamu bābihi yadullu ʿalā rajʿi al-shayʾi

That is, most uses in this family concern turning something back.

He illustrates this with ṣaraftu al-qawma ṣarfan fa-inṣarafū: I turned the people back, and they returned.

Lisān al-ʿArab gives the basic meaning as follows:

الصَّرْفُ: رَدُّ الشَّيْءِ عَنْ وَجْهِهِ

al-ṣarfu raddu al-shayʾi ʿan wajhihi

That is, turning something away from its direction or course.

Wajh in this definition need not mean a human face: it can mean the direction, course, or destination toward which something is oriented.

For example, ṣarafahu can describe turning someone back while they are heading toward a destination. The root can likewise describe redirecting a vehicle from one route to another.

Ṣarf does not always mean returning to the starting point.

Something can be directed along a new course without returning to its original place.

The family’s broader, more precise meaning is therefore turning away from a previous direction or toward another one. Returning to the starting point is one instance, not the whole meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: turning and turning back

Transitive and intransitive verbs: turning something and turning back

In its established use ṣarafa is transitive: an agent turns something or someone.

For example:

صَرَفْتُ الرَّجُلَ عَنْ وَجْهِهِ

ṣaraftu al-rajula ʿan wajhihi

That is, I turned the person away from the course he had been following.

Inṣarafa al-rajulu, however, refers to the person himself returning or moving away.

The Qur’an also makes this distinction clear.

Ṣarafakum in Āl ʿImrān 3:152 concerns turning a group away.

Inṣarafū in Al-Tawbah 9:127 describes the group themselves turning away or returning.

Ṣarf and inṣirāf should therefore not be translated identically without considering the verb’s structure.

The role of prepositions

ʿAn in many constructions identifies what a person or thing moves away from.

Ṣarafahu ʿanhu means that he turned or diverted him away from it.

Ṣarafahu ilayhi can instead express directing him toward a destination.

This matters because Ṣ R F does not inherently mean only turning away. The sentence’s structure and preposition establish the direction.

Yūsuf 12:33 concerns averting the women’s plotting from Joseph, whereas Al-Aḥqāf 46:29 concerns directing a group of jinn toward the Prophet to hear the Qur’an.

One lexical family can thus express both turning away and directing toward a destination without contradiction.

2. Material, social, and economic uses in Arabic

Directing winds and changing their course

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad’s explanation of taṣrīfu al-riyāḥ as turning winds from one direction to another.

Winds may blow from north, south, east, or west, with changes in their course or condition.

Taṣrīf can also concern variations in the winds’ kinds and states.

In such constructions ṣarrafa does not describe simple motion alone: it includes different directions and conditions of movement.

Taṣrīf of winds thus concerns their circulation and variation, rather than merely one wind blowing in a fixed direction.

Lisān al-ʿArab: taṣrīf and changes of wind direction

Ṣurūf al-dahr: the changes of time

Arabic uses ṣurūfu al-dahri for the changes and events of time.

Ṣarfu al-dahri also sometimes refers to an event that changes someone’s circumstances.

Time is pictured as though it turns people and their conditions from one state to another.

The expression does not establish that time itself has an independent will and power to produce events.

Literary language can attribute life’s changes to time without necessarily making a philosophical judgment about their ultimate cause.

The use has a semantic connection with D H R, but ṣarf itself does not mean time: it describes change attributed to time.

The poetic witness of Ṣakhr al-Ghayy

A poem attributed to Ṣakhr al-Ghayy al-Hudhalī reads:

عَاوَدَنِي حُبُّهَا وَقَدْ شَحَطَتْ
صَرْفُ نَوَاهَا فَإِنَّنِي كَمِدُ

ʿāwadanī ḥubbuhā wa-qad shaḥaṭat / ṣarfu nawāhā fa-innanī kamidun

A close rendering of the sense: “Love for her returned to me, though the turn of her departure had taken her far away; and I am deeply grieved.”

Here ṣarf accompanies nawā, distance and separation.

The image concerns a change in the distance and direction through which the beloved has gone away from the poet.

Ṣakhr al-Ghayy is a Hudhayl poet whom sources variously identify as pre-Islamic or as having lived across the advent of Islam. Reports also differ over attributing this ode to him or to ʿAmr Dhū al-Kalb.

The line is therefore an early witness to ṣarf concerning redirection and changing distance, but its date and final attribution should not be presented more confidently than the evidence allows.

Kitāb al-Aghānī: Ṣakhr al-Ghayy’s witness and disputed attribution

Ṣarf: exchanging money and trade

One established Arabic use of this root is exchanging one kind of money for another.

For example:

صَرَفْتُ الدَّرَاهِمَ بِالدَّنَانِيرِ

ṣaraftu al-darāhima bi-al-danānīri

That is, I exchanged dirhams for dinars.

Value or property is converted from one monetary form to another.

The same family supplies ṣarrāf, ṣayraf, and ṣayrafī for someone skilled in exchanging and assessing money.

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad connects changing one currency into another with the basic meaning of turning and conversion.

That connection does not make every Qur’anic use of ṣarf a financial exchange or transaction.

The financial meaning is a specialized branch of the family.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: monetary exchange

Ṣayraf: resourcefulness and skill in affairs

Beyond their financial use, Arabic also uses ṣayraf and ṣayrafī for someone skilled in managing affairs, changing methods, and finding a suitable way forward.

A poem attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh al-Hudhalī reads:

قَدْ كُنْتُ خَرَّاجًا وَلُوجًا صَيْرَفًا
لَمْ تَلْتَحِصْنِي حَيْصَ بَيْصَ لَحَاصِ

qad kuntu kharrājan walūjan ṣayrafan / lam taltaḥiṣnī ḥayṣa bayṣa laḥāṣi

Rendering: “I have known well the ways in and out, resourceful in managing affairs; tangled troubles have not trapped me.”

Ṣayraf here describes an experienced, resourceful person.

It need not mean a money changer, but someone able to change approach when facing difficulty.

The witness is recorded in lexicons and the Hudhayl poetry collection, although its precise composition date is not clear independently of its attribution.

Taṣarruf here concerns being able to act in different circumstances, without necessarily implying deception or blameworthy trickery.

Early lexicons: Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh’s witness and ṣayraf

Ṣarīf: sounds of contact and friction

Arabic sources also record ṣarīf for sounds made by teeth, doors, well wheels, and certain other implements.

Ṣarīfu al-bābi, for example, describes a sound produced by a door’s movement and friction.

Ṣarīfu anyābi al-baʿīri is likewise recorded for the sound of a camel’s teeth.

Ibn Fāris attempts to explain this through back-and-forth movement or repeated contact.

The connection is understandable, but does not establish that all shrill or prolonged sounds historically derive from turning.

Ṣarīf is also sometimes used for freshly drawn milk. That use must be recognized separately, without turning it into the sound of teeth or a monetary transaction.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: ṣarīf as sound and fresh milk

3. Directing winds and water in the Qur’an

Al-Baqarah 2:164: circulation and variation of winds

Al-Baqarah 2:164 reads:

وَتَصْرِيفِ الرِّيَاحِ وَالسَّحَابِ الْمُسَخَّرِ بَيْنَ السَّمَاءِ وَالْأَرْضِ

wa-taṣrīfi al-riyāḥi wa-al-saḥābi al-musakhkhari bayna al-samāʾi wa-al-arḍi

Rendering: “And in the circulation and variation of winds, and the clouds subjected between heaven and earth.”

Taṣrīf here is a Form II verbal noun.

Al-riyāḥ names winds, and taṣrīfi al-riyāḥ their directing and variation.

Structurally, taṣrīf stands in a genitive construction with al-riyāḥ: the winds are the object of the directing.

Changes of direction and condition clearly fit the root’s lexical use here.

Is taṣrīf only a change in wind direction?

Changing direction is one clear lexical explanation of directing winds.

The Qur’anic use should not, however, be restricted without evidence to a change in the angle of blowing.

Winds can vary in direction, strength, duration, extent, and effects.

Some Qur’anic verses connect winds with rain and fertility; others describe a destructive wind.

This diversity fits taṣrīf.

Taṣrīf itself does not supply a fixed list of all these features. Changes of direction and condition form the basic meaning; the winds’ different effects are known through other verses.

Al-Ṭabarī discusses two explanations of directing winds: variations in their directions and differences in their effects, such as beneficial and destructive winds.

Both fit the root’s semantic field.

Taṣrīf and taṣarruf of winds

Morphologically, taṣrīf is the Form II verbal noun, whereas taṣarruf is the Form V verbal noun.

The former highlights directing something through different directions and conditions.

The latter can concern the thing’s own movement or change of condition.

Al-Baqarah 2:164 uses taṣrīf, rather than taṣarruf.

“The circulation and variation of winds” is a natural translation, but precise explanation should clarify that the verse concerns the directing of winds within creation, rather than merely the winds’ independent decisions or conduct.

Al-Ṭabarī: taṣrīf, taṣarruf, and the winds’ effects

Study of R W Ḥ

Al-Jāthiyah 45:5: directing winds alongside night and day

The verse reads:

وَاخْتِلَافِ اللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ وَمَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مِنْ رِزْقٍ فَأَحْيَا بِهِ الْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا وَتَصْرِيفِ الرِّيَاحِ آيَاتٌ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْقِلُونَ

wa-ikhtilāfi al-layli wa-al-nahāri wa-mā anzala allāhu mina al-samāʾi min rizqin fa-aḥyā bihi al-arḍa baʿda mawtihā wa-taṣrīfi al-riyāḥi āyātun li-qawmin yaʿqilūna

Rendering: “And in the alternation of night and day, and the provision The God sends down from heaven, bringing the earth to life after its death, and in the directing of winds, are signs for people who reason.”

The same verbal noun taṣrīf occurs here.

Placing winds alongside night and day, descending water, and the earth’s revival shows that actual natural phenomena are involved.

Directing winds does not itself mean the alternation of night and day: each phenomenon has its own expression.

The verse allows the directing of winds to be studied within nature’s wider order, without making the root itself a particular scientific theory of the atmosphere.

Al-Furqān 25:50: directing water among people

The verse reads:

وَلَقَدْ صَرَّفْنَاهُ بَيْنَهُمْ لِيَذَّكَّرُوا فَأَبَىٰ أَكْثَرُ النَّاسِ إِلَّا كُفُورًا

wa-laqad ṣarrafnāhu baynahum li-yadhdhakkarū fa-abā aktharu al-nāsi illā kufūran

Rendering: “And We have indeed directed it among them in varied ways so that they might take heed, yet most people refuse anything but ingratitude.”

Ṣarrafnāhu is a Form II perfect verb with the object pronoun hu.

The preceding verses concern sending winds, bringing pure water down from heaven, reviving land, and giving creatures water to drink.

The nearby, clear referent of hu is therefore that water.

On this reading ṣarrafnāhu baynahum means directing and distributing water among people and lands.

Is the amount of rainfall the same every year?

Some early exegetical reports explain that rain’s overall quantity does not change from year to year, but its places of falling differ.

This claim should not become taṣrīf’s lexical meaning or a certain conclusion of the verse itself.

The verse concerns directing water among people, rather than a fixed annual amount of rainfall across the earth.

Distribution and changes in rainfall location fit the context, but a theory of fixed global rainfall requires independent evidence.

Some commentaries suggest that hu refers to the Qur’an. The connected discussion of water, however, gives rain and its distribution closer contextual support.

In either possibility, ṣarrafa concerns directing, varying, and presenting in different directions or forms.

Al-Ṭabarī: distributing rain and reports about annual quantity

4. Varying the signs and the Qur’an’s presentation

One of the root’s broadest Qur’anic uses is taṣrīfu al-āyāt.

Here the concern is not turning a body along material paths, but presenting signs, examples, and warnings in varied forms and ways.

Al-Anʿām 6:46: nuṣarrifu al-āyāti

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِنْ أَخَذَ اللَّهُ سَمْعَكُمْ وَأَبْصَارَكُمْ وَخَتَمَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِكُمْ مَنْ إِلَٰهٌ غَيْرُ اللَّهِ يَأْتِيكُمْ بِهِ انْظُرْ كَيْفَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ ثُمَّ هُمْ يَصْدِفُونَ

qul a-raʾaytum in akhadha allāhu samʿakum wa-abṣārakum wa-khatama ʿalā qulūbikum man ilāhun ghayru allāhi yaʾtīkum bihi unẓur kayfa nuṣarrifu al-āyāti thumma hum yaṣdifūna

Translation: Say, “Have you considered: if The God took your hearing and sight and sealed your hearts, what deity other than Him would restore it to you? See how We present the signs in varied ways; yet they turn away.”

Nuṣarrifu here is Form II.

Al-āyāti is its object and refers to divine signs.

Taṣrīf here means presenting signs in different ways and forms, allowing the audience to encounter the subject from different directions.

Lisān al-ʿArab also explains taṣrīfu al-āyāt through tabyīnuhā: clarifying and explaining them.

Clarification is nevertheless a result and function of taṣrīf. The root’s particular feature is varying and turning the presentation through different forms.

Lisān al-ʿArab: varying the signs and clarification

Taṣrīf and simple ṣarf

Ṣarafa in its basic use can describe an act of turning or turning back.

Form II ṣarrafa often highlights directing through varied directions and conditions.

In nuṣarrifu al-āyāti, the structure and use concerning the signs make such variation clear.

Form II alone does not, however, specify a particular number of repetitions or forms.

Taṣrīf does not necessarily mean three, seven, or any fixed number of presentations.

Al-Anʿām 6:65 and Al-Anʿām 6:105: varied presentation for understanding

Al-Anʿām 6:65 reads:

انْظُرْ كَيْفَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَفْقَهُونَ

unẓur kayfa nuṣarrifu al-āyāti laʿallahum yafqahūna

That is, “See how We present the signs in varied ways, so that they might understand.”

Al-Anʿām 6:105 also reads:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ

wa-kadhālika nuṣarrifu al-āyāti

These uses connect taṣrīf with the audience’s understanding and response.

Varied presentation can let someone see a subject from different directions, compare examples, and reach clearer understanding.

These verses do not establish that every audience will necessarily accept the truth after encountering several presentations.

In Al-Anʿām 6:46, the account of varying the signs is immediately followed by a group’s turning away.

Making varied presentation available therefore differs from the audience’s acceptance or rejection.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:58: varying the signs for thankful people

The verse reads:

كَذَٰلِكَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَشْكُرُونَ

kadhālika nuṣarrifu al-āyāti li-qawmin yashkurūna

The preceding context describes the difference between good and unsuitable land in producing plants.

The varying of the signs is then mentioned.

The natural example and the different outcomes of growth can clarify the meaning here.

Taṣrīf itself does not mean agriculture or plant growth.

The particular example comes from context; taṣrīf concerns how it is presented.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:41: reminding through varied presentations

The verse reads:

وَلَقَدْ صَرَّفْنَا فِي هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ لِيَذَّكَّرُوا

wa-laqad ṣarrafnā fī hādhā al-qurʾāni li-yadhdhakkarū

That is, “And We have indeed presented in varied ways within this Qur’an so that they might take heed.”

The direct object of ṣarrafnā is not separately stated in this construction.

Fī hādhā al-qurʾāni identifies the setting of the varied presentation.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:89 also reads:

وَلَقَدْ صَرَّفْنَا لِلنَّاسِ فِي هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ مِنْ كُلِّ مَثَلٍ

wa-laqad ṣarrafnā li-al-nāsi fī hādhā al-qurʾāni min kulli mathalin

Al-Kahf 18:54 repeats a very similar construction.

Min kulli mathalin in these two verses clarifies the role of examples in varied presentation.

Taṣrīf can therefore work through different examples, repeating a truth in varied constructions, or presenting a subject’s different aspects.

This does not make taṣrīf mean only giving examples.

An example is one way of varying the signs, rather than the whole meaning.

Ṭā Hā 20:113: presenting warnings in varied ways

The verse reads:

وَصَرَّفْنَا فِيهِ مِنَ الْوَعِيدِ

wa-ṣarrafnā fīhi mina al-waʿīdi

That is, “And We presented warnings within it in varied ways.”

Warnings are the subject of taṣrīf here.

This difference from the preceding verses shows how the Qur’an uses taṣrīf in presenting examples, signs, and warnings.

Taṣrīf itself does not mean threatening or frightening.

The content being presented is identified by al-waʿīdi.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:27: varied signs and the possibility of return

The verse reads:

وَصَرَّفْنَا الْآيَاتِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْجِعُونَ

wa-ṣarrafnā al-āyāti laʿallahum yarjiʿūna

That is, “And We presented the signs in varied ways so that they might return.”

Taṣrīf here is connected with rujūʿ, returning.

That connection does not make taṣrīf and rujūʿ complete synonyms.

Ṣarrafnā concerns the manner of presenting the signs; yarjiʿūna concerns the audience’s hoped-for response.

Varied presentation can thus enable reconsideration and return, without the audience’s choice and response being contained in taṣrīf itself.

5. Turning people, eyes, and hearts: averting and directing

Āl ʿImrān 3:152: turning a group away from a situation

The verse reads:

ثُمَّ صَرَفَكُمْ عَنْهُمْ لِيَبْتَلِيَكُمْ

thumma ṣarafakum ʿanhum li-yabtaliyakum

That is, “Then He turned you away from them to test you.”

The expression occurs in the context of battle and a change in a group of believers’ circumstances.

Ṣarafakum is a transitive perfect verb.

Kum is its object, and ʿanhum identifies the direction of withdrawal.

The concern here is a change of situation and turning back from confrontation with the opposing group.

Li-yabtaliyakum separately expresses the purpose of testing.

Testing is not ṣarafa’s independent meaning: it comes from the separate phrase li-yabtaliyakum.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:47: eyes being turned

The verse reads:

وَإِذَا صُرِفَتْ أَبْصَارُهُمْ تِلْقَاءَ أَصْحَابِ النَّارِ

wa-idhā ṣurifat abṣāruhum tilqāʾa aṣḥābi al-nāri

That is, “And when their eyes are turned toward the people of the Fire.”

Ṣurifat here is a passive perfect verb.

Abṣāruhum is its grammatical subject, and tilqāʾa aṣḥābi al-nāri identifies the direction of looking.

This shows that ṣarf does not always mean turning away from something: here the gaze is directed toward another group.

The general equivalent “turning” is therefore more accurate here than “averting.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:29: directing a group toward hearing the Qur’an

وَإِذْ صَرَفْنَا إِلَيْكَ نَفَرًا مِنَ الْجِنِّ يَسْتَمِعُونَ الْقُرْآنَ فَلَمَّا حَضَرُوهُ قَالُوا أَنْصِتُوا فَلَمَّا قُضِيَ وَلَّوْا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِمْ مُنْذِرِينَ

wa-idh ṣarafnā ilayka nafaran mina al-jinni yastamiʿūna al-qurʾāna fa-lammā ḥaḍarūhu qālū anṣitū fa-lammā quḍiya wallaw ilā qawmihim mundhirīna

Translation: And when We directed a group of jinn toward you to hear the Qur’an: when they attended it, they said, “Be silent and listen.” When it was concluded, they returned to their people as warners.

Ṣarafnā ilayka here concerns directing a group toward the verse’s addressee.

The use is important evidence for Ṣ R F as redirection and directing toward a destination, rather than invariably averting or preventing.

Here a group have come toward the Prophet to hear the Qur’an.

They subsequently return to their people and warn them.

Ṣarafnā alone does not establish that the group were compelled to believe without choice.

The verse concerns an encounter and hearing the Qur’an; their response is described later in the account.

Al-Ṭabarī: directing jinn toward hearing the Qur’an

Yūsuf 12:33 and Yūsuf 12:34: averting a plot

Yūsuf 12:33 reads:

وَإِلَّا تَصْرِفْ عَنِّي كَيْدَهُنَّ أَصْبُ إِلَيْهِنَّ

wa-illā taṣrif ʿannī kaydahunna aṣbu ilayhinna

That is, “And unless You avert their plotting from me, I will incline toward them.”

The next verse reads:

فَاسْتَجَابَ لَهُ رَبُّهُ فَصَرَفَ عَنْهُ كَيْدَهُنَّ

fa-istajāba lahu rabbuhu fa-ṣarafa ʿanhu kaydahunna

That is, “Then his Lord answered him and averted their plotting from him.”

Ṣ R F in these two verses means averting something harmful.

The first asks for the plotting to be averted; the second describes its fulfillment.

These constructions show that ṣarf can also concern protection and preventing harm from reaching someone.

Ṣarf itself does not mean plotting, mindfulness of God, or chastity.

Kaydahunna identifies the danger being averted.

Yūsuf 12:24 likewise concerns averting evil and indecency from Joseph.

These uses confirm averting harm from a person; how it happens must be understood through the account’s structure and related verses.

Al-Nūr 24:43: averting a natural phenomenon

The verse reads:

فَيُصِيبُ بِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَصْرِفُهُ عَنْ مَنْ يَشَاءُ

fa-yuṣību bihi man yashāʾu wa-yaṣrifuhu ʿan man yashāʾu

That is, “He causes it to strike whom He wills and averts it from whom He wills.”

The context concerns moving clouds, precipitation, and hail.

Hu in yaṣrifuhu refers to something mentioned in that scene of precipitation.

Exegetical explanations consider hail, and also precipitation, as possible referents.

If it refers to hail, averting it can concern preventing damage to crops or property.

If it refers more generally to precipitation, its being averted can concern water and rain not reaching a place.

These differences do not change the verb’s basic meaning: something is averted from the person or place concerned.

Yaṣrifuhu alone does not make every averted natural phenomenon necessarily a blessing or punishment. Its value and effects depend on the phenomenon and circumstances.

Al-Ṭabarī: hail and its being averted

6. Turning away, departing, and the consequences of human choice

Al-Tawbah 9:127: thumma inṣarafū ṣarafa allāhu qulūbahum

وَإِذَا مَا أُنْزِلَتْ سُورَةٌ نَظَرَ بَعْضُهُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ هَلْ يَرَاكُمْ مِنْ أَحَدٍ ثُمَّ انْصَرَفُوا صَرَفَ اللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ

wa-idhā mā unzilat sūratun naẓara baʿḍuhum ilā baʿḍin hal yarākum min aḥadin thumma inṣarafū ṣarafa allāhu qulūbahum bi-annahum qawmun lā yafqahūna

Translation: Whenever a surah is sent down, some of them look at others: “Does anyone see you?” Then they turn away and leave. The God has turned their hearts away, because they are people who do not understand.

The verse places two different forms of the same root together.

Inṣarafū is Form VII and concerns the people themselves returning or turning away.

Ṣarafa allāhu qulūbahum uses the transitive Form I verb and describes their hearts being turned.

The combination matters: human conduct is mentioned alongside a consequence attributed to God.

Does ṣarafa allāhu qulūbahum mean an initial removal of choice?

The sequence and stated reason in the verse matter.

The group’s conduct is mentioned first: they look at one another and then turn away.

Their hearts being turned is described afterward.

The ending reads:

بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ

bi-annahum qawmun lā yafqahūna

That is, “Because they are people who do not understand.”

Ṣarafa allāhu qulūbahum cannot therefore be read apart from the conduct and stated reason in the verse.

Human turning away and the heart’s condition are connected here.

Ṣ R F itself does not mean compelling a person to disbelieve. The verb expresses turning; the agent, object, and reason are understood through the whole sentence.

This continuity can be compared with Al-Ṣaff 61:5, where the group’s own deviation precedes the deviation of their hearts being attributed to God.

The verses should not, however, become a mechanical formula explaining every stage of every person’s guidance or error without considering knowledge, choice, and circumstances.

Al-Ṭabarī: departure and the turning of hearts

Al-Aʿrāf 7:146: sa-aṣrifu ʿan āyātiya

The verse reads:

سَأَصْرِفُ عَنْ آيَاتِيَ الَّذِينَ يَتَكَبَّرُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ

sa-aṣrifu ʿan āyātiya alladhīna yatakabbarūna fī al-arḍi bi-ghayri al-ḥaqqi

That is, “I will turn away from My signs those who act arrogantly on earth without right.”

The rest describes people who do not believe when they see a sign, and do not take the way of right guidance when they see it.

Their denial of the signs and heedlessness are then given as the reason for this condition.

Being turned from the signs here thus connects with arrogance, denial, and disregard.

Sa-aṣrifu lexically expresses only turning and averting.

The conditions and responsibility surrounding such averting are understood through the remainder of the verse.

It should therefore not become a rule that God deprives some people of understanding truth without any background or human conduct.

Al-Ṭabarī: arrogance and being turned from signs

Yūnus 10:32, Al-Zumar 39:6, and Ghāfir 40:69

These three verses contain passive forms of ṣarafa.

In Yūnus 10:32:

فَأَنَّىٰ تُصْرَفُونَ

fa-annā tuṣrafūna

Al-Zumar 39:6 repeats the same expression.

Ghāfir 40:69 reads:

أَنَّىٰ يُصْرَفُونَ

annā yuṣrafūna

The verbs are passive, and the question concerns how or in what direction people are turned away from truth.

Passive voice does not mean that the actual agent of every such deviation is identified in the expression itself.

Grammatically, a passive verb focuses on the condition of the person redirected or turned.

The internal or external factors involved must be examined through the verses’ contexts.

These verses mention truth, God’s lordship, and disputing the signs. Turning away from truth thus acquires moral and doctrinal significance.

That significance comes from the object and context, rather than ṣarf inherently meaning misguidance.

7. Averting punishment and inability to escape

Al-Anʿām 6:16: man yuṣraf ʿanhu

The verse reads:

مَنْ يُصْرَفْ عَنْهُ يَوْمَئِذٍ فَقَدْ رَحِمَهُ

man yuṣraf ʿanhu yawmaʾidhin fa-qad raḥimahu

That is, “Whoever has [punishment] averted from him that day has indeed received His mercy.”

Yuṣraf here is a passive jussive imperfect verb.

What is averted is known from the preceding context: Al-Anʿām 6:15 mentions the punishment of a tremendous day.

The verb therefore means punishment being averted from a person.

Mercy is stated in the following clause as the outcome of that averting; Ṣ R F itself does not mean mercy.

Al-Furqān 25:65: asking for punishment to be averted

The verse reads:

رَبَّنَا اصْرِفْ عَنَّا عَذَابَ جَهَنَّمَ

rabbanā iṣrif ʿannā ʿadhāba jahannama

That is, “Our Lord, avert from us the punishment of Hell.”

Iṣrif is a Form I imperative used in a supplication and request.

People here ask God for punishment to be averted from them.

The use makes the root’s positive, protective application clear.

It does not make every Qur’anic use of ṣarf mean forgiveness or salvation in the Hereafter.

Hūd 11:8: laysa maṣrūfan ʿanhum

The verse reads:

أَلَا يَوْمَ يَأْتِيهِمْ لَيْسَ مَصْرُوفًا عَنْهُمْ

a-lā yawma yaʾtīhim laysa maṣrūfan ʿanhum

That is, “Indeed, the day [punishment] reaches them, it will not be averted from them.”

Maṣrūf is the passive participle of Form I ṣarafa.

The thing that cannot be averted here is punishment.

Laysa maṣrūfan ʿanhum concerns its not being turned away from the group.

The form alone does not identify the punishment’s nature or manner.

Al-Furqān 25:19: fa-mā tastaṭīʿūna ṣarfan wa-lā naṣran

Ṣarfan here is the Form I verbal noun.

The sentence expresses inability to repel or avert the consequence, and inability to obtain help.

Ṣarf and naṣr are different words.

Ṣarf can mean averting or turning something back; naṣr concerns aid and support.

Their combination therefore expresses two connected but distinct aspects.

Some lexicons explain ṣarf in such a construction as resourcefulness or a way of escape.

That explanation fits the verb’s use: resourcefulness may involve attempting to avert danger or a consequence from oneself.

Trickery should not, however, become the root’s fixed, general meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: inability to avert and help

Al-Kahf 18:53: maṣrifan

وَرَأَى الْمُجْرِمُونَ النَّارَ فَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُمْ مُوَاقِعُوهَا وَلَمْ يَجِدُوا عَنْهَا مَصْرِفًا

wa-raʾā al-mujrimūna al-nāra fa-ẓannū annahum muwāqiʿūhā wa-lam yajidū ʿanhā maṣrifan

Translation: The offenders see the Fire and realize that they will encounter it; they find no way to turn away and escape from it.

Maṣrif belongs to ṣarafa and follows the pattern mafʿil.

The form can name a place or way in which turning back or changing course is possible.

Maṣrifan here means a way of escape or of turning away from the Fire.

ʿAnhā also identifies what must be escaped.

“They find no way of escape” is therefore a natural rendering of the construction.

Maṣrif itself does not lexically name fire, punishment, or Hell.

Those concepts come from context.

Al-Ṭabarī: no escape from the Fire

8. Morphological distinctions and extended uses

Ṣarafa and ṣarf

Ṣarafa is Form I and basically concerns turning and turning back.

Ṣarf is its verbal noun.

The Qur’an uses both active and passive forms of the verb, as well as its verbal noun.

Some constructions identify the agent; others focus on being turned.

Ṣarrafa and taṣrīf

Ṣarrafa is Form II.

Its Qur’anic uses concern directing in varied directions or forms.

Taṣrīf is its verbal noun.

The Qur’an uses these concerning winds, water, and signs.

Varied wind directions, changed rainfall locations, and varied Qur’anic presentation are three contextual applications, rather than three unrelated meanings.

Directing or presenting in different forms and directions can be recognized in all three.

Inṣarafa

Inṣarafa is Form VII and concerns the person themselves returning or moving away.

Only inṣarafū in Al-Tawbah 9:127 occurs in the Qur’an.

It highlights the group’s own movement or turning away.

Maṣrif and maṣrūf

Maṣrif can express a way, place, or possibility of changing course and returning.

Maṣrūf is a passive participle describing something turned back or averted.

Both Qur’anic forms occur in contexts of averting punishment, but their lexical meanings are not confined to the Hereafter.

Taṣarruf and mutaṣarrif

Taṣarruf is the Form V verbal noun, also recorded in Arabic for moving in different directions, managing affairs, and making use of resources.

Mutaṣarrif can describe someone who acts in affairs or manages and organizes them.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

In contemporary Persian taṣarruf can also mean taking possession, administration, dealing with property, or alteration.

Those modern legal and administrative uses should not automatically be transferred to every Qur’anic form.

Ṣarf and taṣrīf as the science of morphology

In Arabic linguistics ṣarf and taṣrīf name the discipline studying word structure, changes of form, and relationships between derivatives.

The terminology relates semantically to changing a word’s form and turning it through different patterns and constructions.

Morphology as a systematically developed discipline nevertheless has its own technical name and structure.

Nuṣarrifu al-āyāti in the Qur’an therefore does not mean teaching morphology and syntax.

Varying the signs also does not necessarily mean changing Qur’anic words or converting a verse into another morphological form.

The concern is presenting signs, examples, and expressions in varied ways.

9. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ R F (ṣarafa) and Ṣ D D (ṣadda)

Arabic ṣadda concerns turning away and preventing.

Ṣarafa can likewise turn someone away from a way or direction.

The roots are close in this use.

Ṣ R F nevertheless has a broader range involving redirection and variation.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:29 describes a group directed toward the Prophet; Al-Baqarah 2:164 describes winds directed through varied directions and conditions; Al-Anʿām 6:46 describes signs presented in different ways.

These uses cannot all be explained as preventing.

Ṣadd and ṣarf therefore overlap in some constructions, but are not complete synonyms.

Study of Ṣ D D

Ṣ R F (ṣarafa) and R J ʿ (rajaʿa)

Rujūʿ concerns returning.

Ṣarf can likewise mean turning back.

Ibn Fāris uses return to explain the root’s core for this reason.

Ṣarf also highlights redirecting from a previous course, without necessarily returning to a starting point.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:29, for example, a group are directed toward the Prophet; the movement need not be their return to an original place.

Turning back is therefore one use of ṣarf, while turning is a broader equivalent for the family.

Study of R J ʿ

Ṣ R F (ṣarafa) and Ḥ W L (ḥawwala)

Ḥawwala concerns changing and transferring from one state or place to another.

Ṣarafa can also express such a change.

Ṣarf highlights redirection and turning from one course to another.

Taḥwīl can concern a change of state or transfer without the image of turning back from a direction.

The distinction is relative, and the roots approach each other in some uses.

Study of Ḥ W L

Ṣ R F (ṣarafa) and Q L B (qalaba)

Arabic qalaba concerns turning over, overturning, and changing a state.

Ṣarafa likewise means turning and changing.

Qalb can highlight overturning or completely reversing something, whereas ṣarf may merely avert it or direct it along another course.

Yaṣrifuhu in Al-Nūr 24:43 concerns averting a natural phenomenon, without necessarily reversing its shape.

Varying the signs in Al-Anʿām 6:46 likewise means varied presentation, rather than overturning their truth.

Study of Q L B

Ṣ R F (ṣarafa) and B Y N (bayyana)

Bayyana concerns clarifying and making a meaning apparent.

Ṣarrafa concerning signs can clarify a subject through different presentations.

Some lexicographers therefore explain taṣrīfu al-āyāt through tabyīnuhā.

Tabyīn and taṣrīf are nevertheless distinct concepts.

Tabyīn focuses on clarity and making meaning apparent; taṣrīf on turning the presentation through varied forms and methods.

A subject may thus be clarified with one presentation or explained to audiences through several examples and methods.

Study of B Y N

Ṣ R F (ṣarafa) and W Z ʿ (wazzaʿa)

Concerning rain and resources, taṣrīf can involve distribution among different places.

Distribution describes dividing or delivering something to different parts, whereas taṣrīf can also include changing direction or form and moving between different conditions.

The distribution of water in Al-Furqān 25:50 is therefore one application of taṣrīf, rather than the whole family’s definition.

Ṣ R F (ṣarafa) and Ṣ Y R (ṣāra)

Arabic ṣāra concerns becoming and reaching another state.

This family is close to Ṣ R F in involving changed conditions.

Ṣāra in ordinary use can focus on the result of a change, whereas ṣarf more often describes turning, turning back, or redirecting.

Arabic maṣīr belongs to Ṣ Y R, while maṣrif belongs to Ṣ R F.

Despite their phonetic and semantic proximity, these forms should not be conflated in morphological analysis.

10. Challenges of interpretation and translation

Does ṣarf always mean turning away from something?

No.

Some verses clearly involve averting, such as averting the plot from Joseph or averting punishment.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:29, however, concerns directing toward the Prophet.

Al-Baqarah 2:164 describes the circulation and redirection of winds.

Al-Anʿām 6:46 presents signs in varied ways.

Restricting the whole root to averting is therefore incorrect.

Does varying the signs mean changing their truth?

No.

Varying the signs means presenting and clarifying them in different forms and ways.

Differences in examples, warnings, and methods of expression do not by themselves change the truth or create contradictions in content.

The presence of taṣrīf alone also does not establish that every two different Qur’anic expressions necessarily have an identical meaning.

Each verse’s content and connections must be examined separately to understand their relationship.

Does ṣarf always involve compulsion?

The verb can express an agent’s action of turning or averting someone else.

That construction alone does not establish that the person’s choice, awareness, or responsibility has been removed in every instance.

Verses concerning guidance and error require examining human conduct, stated reasons, and their relationship with divine ways.

Al-Tawbah 9:127 places the group’s turning away alongside the turning of their hearts.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:146 likewise mentions arrogance, denial, and heedlessness in the context of turning away from the signs.

A verb form therefore cannot be separated from all its context to construct a conclusive theory of human compulsion from the root itself.

Is directing winds limited to changes of direction?

Changing direction is one clear use.

Taṣrīf can also involve movement through different conditions.

All modern meteorological findings should not, however, be incorporated into the word’s lexical meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:164 and Al-Jāthiyah 45:5 invite reflection on nature’s order, but taṣrīf itself does not name a scientific formula or a complete atmospheric circulation model.

Does ṣarf mean a transaction or money changing?

In Arabic economic usage, yes; but the Qur’an does not use this root in that way.

Ṣarf in Al-Furqān 25:19 concerns the ability to avert or repel, rather than currency exchange.

Maṣrif in Al-Kahf 18:53 means a way to escape or turn away from the Fire, rather than a place to spend money.

Familiar financial meanings in contemporary Persian should therefore not be imposed on these verses without evidence.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

Turning, turning back, and changing direction suit the root’s general meaning.

For harm, averting and repelling are more natural.

For signs, “presenting in varied ways” better conveys the contextual meaning.

For winds, circulation and variation are suitable.

12. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ R F basically expresses turning and changing direction. Something may be turned back from its earlier course, directed toward another destination, averted from someone, or appear in varied directions and forms.

In early Arabic the family occurs in descriptions of directing winds, changes of time, managing affairs, monetary exchange, resourcefulness, and certain sounds.

Its Qur’anic uses include directing winds and water, varying the presentation of signs, averting harm and punishment, turning eyes and hearts, human turning away, and inability to find an escape.

These uses show that ṣarf inherently means neither guidance nor misguidance. Something may be averted from danger, or a person diverted from truth. A group may be directed toward hearing the Qur’an, or signs presented in varied forms.

In every case the direction, object, agent, and outcome must be identified separately.

Short definition of the root: Ṣ R F means turning something from its previous direction or course, turning it back, or directing it toward another direction; in Form II it can highlight directing and presenting through varied directions, conditions, or forms. Brief root meaning: Turning, turning back, and changing direction; in taṣrīf, directing or presenting through varied forms and ways

Ṣarf should therefore not be equated solely with preventing, averting, or returning.

In Qur’anic use taṣrīfu al-riyāḥ concerns the circulation and variation of winds; taṣrīfu al-āyāt, varying the presentation of signs; ṣarfu al-sūʾ, averting harm; and inṣirāf, a person’s returning or turning away.

The family’s central distinction is that turning is an action whose direction and value are determined by its object and context. This feature connects the root’s material, linguistic, and behavioral uses while preserving their actual differences.
