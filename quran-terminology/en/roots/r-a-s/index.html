1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The triliteral Arabic root R ʾ S primarily concerns the head of a person or animal. From this concrete meaning extend uses for the upper or initial part of things, leadership, and the principal portion of an asset.

The root occurs 18 times in 17 verses across 15 surahs. Sixteen verses contain one occurrence each; Al-Baqarah 2:196 contains two.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: eighteen occurrences of the noun raʾs

Every Qur’anic form belongs to the noun raʾs. The principal forms in the lexical family are:

  • رَأْس: the head of a person or animal; by extension, the top, beginning, or principal part of something. Qur’anic: yes.
  • رُءُوس / رُؤُوس: heads, the plural of raʾs. Qur’anic: yes.
  • أَرْؤُس: another plural of raʾs. Qur’anic: no.
  • رَأَسَ / يَرْأَسُ: to strike or injure someone’s head; in another use, to lead. Qur’anic: no.
  • رَأَّسَ / تَرْئِيس: to appoint someone as chief or leader. Qur’anic: no.
  • تَرَأَّسَ: to assume leadership of a group. Qur’anic: no.
  • رَئِيس: a leader, supervisor, or person heading a group; some classical lexical uses also denote someone whose head has been injured. Qur’anic: no.
  • رِئَاسَة: leadership or the position of a chief. Qur’anic: no.
  • رَئِيسِيّ: principal, or pertaining to a foremost or important position. Qur’anic: no, in this adjectival form.
  • مَرْؤُوس: someone under another’s leadership; in another lexical use, someone whose head has been injured. Qur’anic: no.
  • رَأْسُ الْمَال: the principal portion of property, distinguished from additions and returns. Qur’anic: yes, in the plural phrase ruʾūsu amwālikum in Al-Baqarah 2:279.
  • رَأْسُ الْجَبَل: the summit or top of a mountain. Qur’anic: no, in this combination.
  • رَأْسُ السَّنَة: the beginning of the year. Qur’anic: no.

Two points follow. The Arabic family is broader than its Qur’anic forms. Although every Qur’anic occurrence is formed from the noun raʾs, the meaning is not identical in every verse.

In most verses, raʾs is the physical human head. Sometimes its movement or posture expresses behavior or a psychological state, such as shaking the head in denial or lowering it in shame. In one verse, ruʾūs al-amwāl denotes principal capital.

The study must therefore distinguish the physical head, images of human behavior involving it, and the economic meaning of principal capital.

2. The linguistic core and early Arabic witnesses

Raʾs: the head and the upper part

In Arabic, raʾs names the familiar part of a human or animal body.

The human head is usually at the top of the body, with organs such as the eyes, ears, and mouth in or around it.

Broader uses extend from this bodily reality. Arabic applies raʾs to the top of a mountain, tree, or building, and to the beginning of certain periods and affairs.

This extension is natural: a word for the highest part of the body can name the highest part of other things.

Yet “upper” should not be imposed on every use of the root.

Raʾs al-māl, for example, means principal capital, not money physically positioned above other money.

Two related features therefore appear in the family: prominence or precedence, and being the principal element in a whole.

These features are not equally present in every use.

Gathering and elevation: a classical lexical analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the root through two concepts: tajammuʿ, “gathering,” and irtifāʿ, “elevation.”

The former concerns coming together; the latter concerns height or prominence.

On this account, the head is a prominent, distinct part of the body. Some early uses also apply raʾs to a large body of people.

This analysis helps explain several branches without making every occurrence mean “gathering.”

When the Qur’an speaks of a person’s raʾs, the direct meaning is the head, not the assembly of bodily organs or thoughts.

Likewise, ruʾūsu amwālikum has the clear economic meaning of principal capital; it should not be translated as “an abundance of assets.”

Gathering and elevation can illuminate historical and figurative connections, but do not replace each expression’s established meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: raʾs, gathering and elevation

Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, the pre-Islamic Arabic poet, states:

فَيَوْمًا إِلَى أَهْلِي وَيَوْمًا إِلَيْكُمُ
وَيَوْمًا أَحُطُّ الْخَيْلَ مِنْ رُؤْسِ أَجْبَالِ

fa-yawman ilā ahlī wa-yawman ilaykumu / wa-yawman aḥuṭṭu al-khayla min ruʾsi ajbāli

That is: “One day toward my family, another toward you, and another I bring the horses down from mountain summits.”

Ruʾs ajbāl denotes the summits or upper parts of mountains.

This witness illustrates the extension from a body part to the top of natural objects.

Ruʾs here is a shortened form of the plural ruʾūs, also recorded in poetic usage.

Lexicons attribute the line to Imruʾ al-Qays, but attribution should be distinguished from independent proof of the date of each verse.

Lisān al-ʿArab: raʾs, the Imruʾ al-Qays witness and shortened plural

Another witness, attributed to ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm, states:

بِرَأْسٍ مِنْ بَنِي جُشَمَ بْنِ بَكْرٍ
نَدُقُّ بِهِ السُّهُولَةَ وَالْحُزُونَا

bi-raʾsin min banī Jushama bni Bakrin / naduqqu bihi al-suhūlata wa-al-ḥuzūnā

That is: “With a raʾs from Banū Jusham ibn Bakr, we traverse and pound the level and rugged ground.”

Here raʾs concerns a body of people.

Its explanation differs: some understand a large company, while others favor a chief or leader because of the phrase’s construction.

The witness thus illustrates extension into social organization and leadership, without requiring either reading to be declared certain without examination.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: the group and chief analyses of the ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm witness

Raʾs and leadership

Arabic uses raʾīs for someone leading, commanding, or supervising a group.

The bodily connection is understandable: as the head occupies a prominent position, social language can call a leader raʾīs.

This semantic transfer should not become a certain theory of the origin of all leadership structures.

Language may use the bodily head to describe a leader’s position. That does not mean leaders literally perform its biological role for their people or gain unlimited authority over them.

Raʾīs itself does not occur in the Qur’an. Political authority, imamate, and obedience must therefore be studied through their relevant Qur’anic vocabulary and verses, rather than importing leadership from a derivative absent from the Qur’an.

Raʾs as beginning and principal part

Arabic also applies raʾs to the beginning of certain periods, as in raʾs al-sanah, “the beginning of the year.”

Here it no longer names a physical head or necessarily a top, but the starting point or first part of a period.

This approaches the economic expression raʾs al-māl: a principal element against which additions are recognized.

“Beginning,” “principal part,” and “top” nevertheless remain distinct concepts; they cannot be substituted for one another everywhere.

3. The physical head: hair, purification, and bodily condition

Al-Baqarah 2:196: shaving the head and discomfort affecting it

Verse text

وَأَتِمُّوا الْحَجَّ وَالْعُمْرَةَ لِلَّهِ فَإِنْ أُحْصِرْتُمْ فَمَا اسْتَيْسَرَ مِنَ الْهَدْيِ وَلَا تَحْلِقُوا رُءُوسَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ الْهَدْيُ مَحِلَّهُ فَمَنْ كَانَ مِنْكُمْ مَرِيضًا أَوْ بِهِ أَذًى مِنْ رَأْسِهِ فَفِدْيَةٌ مِنْ صِيَامٍ أَوْ صَدَقَةٍ أَوْ نُسُكٍ فَإِذَا أَمِنْتُمْ فَمَنْ تَمَتَّعَ بِالْعُمْرَةِ إِلَى الْحَجِّ فَمَا اسْتَيْسَرَ مِنَ الْهَدْيِ فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ ثَلَاثَةِ أَيَّامٍ فِي الْحَجِّ وَسَبْعَةٍ إِذَا رَجَعْتُمْ تِلْكَ عَشَرَةٌ كَامِلَةٌ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ أَهْلُهُ حَاضِرِي الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

wa-atimmū al-ḥajja wa-al-ʿumrata li-Allāhi fa-in uḥṣirtum fa-mā istaysara mina al-hadyi wa-lā taḥliqū ruʾūsakum ḥattā yablugha al-hadyu maḥillahu fa-man kāna minkum marīḍan aw bihi adhan min raʾsihi fa-fidyatun min ṣiyāmin aw ṣadaqatin aw nusukin fa-idhā amintum fa-man tamattaʿa bi-al-ʿumrati ilā al-ḥajji fa-mā istaysara mina al-hadyi fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu thalāthati ayyāmin fī al-ḥajji wa-sabʿatin idhā rajaʿtum tilka ʿasharatun kāmilatun dhālika li-man lam yakun ahluhu ḥāḍirī al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi wa-ittaqū Allāha wa-iʿlamū anna Allāha shadīdu al-ʿiqābi

English translation

Complete the Ḥajj and ʿUmrah for The God. If you are prevented, then whatever offering is feasible; and do not shave your heads until the offering reaches its place. Whoever among you is ill or has discomfort affecting his head, then a compensation of fasting, charity, or sacrifice. When you are secure, whoever benefits from ʿUmrah until the Ḥajj, then whatever offering is feasible; whoever cannot find one shall fast three days during the Ḥajj and seven when you return: ten complete days. This is for one whose family is not among those present at the Sacred Mosque. Be mindful of The God, and know that The God is severe in requital.

Usage analysis

The root occurs twice in this verse.

First, ruʾūsakum is the plural of raʾs with the second-person plural pronoun; then raʾsihi is singular with a third-person pronoun.

Both denote the physical human head.

In wa-lā taḥliqū ruʾūsakum, taḥliqū belongs to Ḥ L Q and means shaving.

Shaving the head means shaving its hair in Arabic. Ruʾūsakum therefore does not itself mean “your hair”; the head is the object of an action performed on its hair.

Lexical study should preserve this distinction.

The second phrase, adhan min raʾsihi, concerns discomfort or irritation affecting the head.

Raʾs itself does not mean illness, pain, or discomfort. Adhā supplies that meaning; min raʾsihi identifies its location or source.

The head’s lexical meaning is therefore distinct from rulings concerning it.

The verse discusses shaving the head and discomfort affecting it. R ʾ S alone establishes no independent ruling about hair type, shaving tools, or other body parts.

Al-Fatḥ 48:27: shaving and shortening

Verse text

لَقَدْ صَدَقَ اللَّهُ رَسُولَهُ الرُّءْيَا بِالْحَقِّ لَتَدْخُلُنَّ الْمَسْجِدَ الْحَرَامَ إِنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ آمِنِينَ مُحَلِّقِينَ رُءُوسَكُمْ وَمُقَصِّرِينَ لَا تَخَافُونَ فَعَلِمَ مَا لَمْ تَعْلَمُوا فَجَعَلَ مِنْ دُونِ ذَٰلِكَ فَتْحًا قَرِيبًا

laqad ṣadaqa Allāhu rasūlahu al-ruʾyā bi-al-ḥaqqi la-tadkhulunna al-masjida al-ḥarāma in shāʾa Allāhu āminīna muḥalliqīna ruʾūsakum wa-muqaṣṣirīna lā takhāfūna fa-ʿalima mā lam taʿlamū fa-jaʿala min dūni dhālika fatḥan qarīban

English translation

The God has indeed confirmed His Messenger’s vision in truth: if The God wills, you will surely enter the Sacred Mosque in safety, having shaved your heads or shortened your hair, without fear. He knew what you did not know and provided, before that, a near opening.

Usage analysis

Ruʾūsakum again denotes physical heads.

Two accompanying descriptions matter: muḥalliqīn and muqaṣṣirīn.

The first belongs to Ḥ L Q, the second to Q Ṣ R.

In this context, one concerns shaving the hair and the other shortening it.

Raʾs should not be equated with either action. It names the body part; shaving and shortening are distinct actions involving its hair.

Nor does raʾs itself determine how much hair should be shaved or shortened.

Those details require examination of the associated practices and rulings.

Al-Māʾidah 5:6: wiping the head

Verse text

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا قُمْتُمْ إِلَى الصَّلَاةِ فَاغْسِلُوا وُجُوهَكُمْ وَأَيْدِيَكُمْ إِلَى الْمَرَافِقِ وَامْسَحُوا بِرُءُوسِكُمْ وَأَرْجُلَكُمْ إِلَى الْكَعْبَيْنِ وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ جُنُبًا فَاطَّهَّرُوا وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ مَرْضَىٰ أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ أَوْ جَاءَ أَحَدٌ مِنْكُمْ مِنَ الْغَائِطِ أَوْ لَامَسْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا فَامْسَحُوا بِوُجُوهِكُمْ وَأَيْدِيكُمْ مِنْهُ مَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ لِيَجْعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ حَرَجٍ وَلَٰكِنْ يُرِيدُ لِيُطَهِّرَكُمْ وَلِيُتِمَّ نِعْمَتَهُ عَلَيْكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā qumtum ilā al-ṣalāti fa-ighsilū wujūhakum wa-aydiyakum ilā al-marāfiqi wa-imsaḥū bi-ruʾūsikum wa-arjulakum ilā al-kaʿbayni wa-in kuntum junuban fa-iṭṭahharū wa-in kuntum marḍā aw ʿalā safarin aw jāʾa aḥadun minkum mina al-ghāʾiṭi aw lāmastumu al-nisāʾa fa-lam tajidū māʾan fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban fa-imsaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum minhu mā yurīdu Allāhu li-yajʿala ʿalaykum min ḥarajin wa-lākin yurīdu li-yuṭahhirakum wa-li-yutimma niʿmatahu ʿalaykum laʿallakum tashkurūna

English translation

You who believe, when you rise for ṣalāh, wash your faces and your hands up to the elbows, and wipe over your heads, and your feet up to the two ankle prominences. If you are in a state of janābah, purify yourselves. If you are ill or traveling, or one of you has come from relieving himself, or you have had contact with women and find no water, turn to a clean surface and wipe your faces and hands with it. The God does not intend to place you in hardship, but intends to purify you and complete His blessing upon you, so that you may be thankful.

Usage analysis

Bi-ruʾūsikum comprises the preposition bi-, the plural noun ruʾūs, and the second-person plural pronoun.

Ruʾūs here simply means heads.

Wa-imsaḥū belongs to M S Ḥ and expresses wiping.

The head is therefore the object or area of wiping; raʾs itself does not mean wiping or cleanliness.

The Qur’an uses fa-ighsilū for the face and hands, and wa-imsaḥū for the head.

This distinction comes from the verbs, not the noun raʾs.

Does bi-ruʾūsikum specify how much to wipe?

The presence of bi- has syntactic significance.

Arabic grammarians differ about its role in certain wiping constructions. Explanations include contact and connecting the action to its object; some analyses also discuss whether it indicates part of the object.

R ʾ S alone cannot establish whether wiping must cover the whole head or a particular portion.

Raʾs lexically means the head. The extent and manner of wiping require examination of the verb, preposition, complete construction, and relevant evidence.

Al-Qurṭubī on Al-Māʾidah 5:6: the role of bi- and extent of wiping

Later disagreements over the extent of wiping should not change the clear meaning of ruʾūs.

The verse speaks of the head, not the beard, neck, or another body part.

Conclusion from these three verses: In pilgrimage and purification passages, raʾs names the physical human head. The actions come from Ḥ L Q, Q Ṣ R, and M S Ḥ; none belongs to the meaning of R ʾ S itself.

4. The head in narratives: the body, aging, and human behavior

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150: grasping Aaron’s head

Verse text

وَلَمَّا رَجَعَ مُوسَىٰ إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا قَالَ بِئْسَمَا خَلَفْتُمُونِي مِنْ بَعْدِي أَعَجِلْتُمْ أَمْرَ رَبِّكُمْ وَأَلْقَى الْأَلْوَاحَ وَأَخَذَ بِرَأْسِ أَخِيهِ يَجُرُّهُ إِلَيْهِ قَالَ ابْنَ أُمَّ إِنَّ الْقَوْمَ اسْتَضْعَفُونِي وَكَادُوا يَقْتُلُونَنِي فَلَا تُشْمِتْ بِيَ الْأَعْدَاءَ وَلَا تَجْعَلْنِي مَعَ الْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-lammā rajaʿa Mūsā ilā qawmihi ghaḍbāna asifan qāla biʾsamā khalaftumūnī min baʿdī a-ʿajiltum amra rabbikum wa-alqā al-alwāḥa wa-akhadha bi-raʾsi akhīhi yajurruhu ilayhi qāla ibna umma inna al-qawma istaḍʿafūnī wa-kādū yaqtulūnanī fa-lā tushmit biya al-aʿdāʾa wa-lā tajʿalnī maʿa al-qawmi al-ẓālimīna

English translation

When Moses returned to his people angry and grieved, he said, “How badly you succeeded me after my departure! Did you hasten your Lord’s command?” He cast down the tablets and took his brother by the head, pulling him toward himself. Aaron said, “Son of my mother, the people deemed me weak and almost killed me. Do not let the enemies rejoice at my expense, and do not place me among the wrongdoing people.”

Usage analysis

Bi-raʾsi akhīhi denotes Aaron’s physical head.

Akhadha expresses taking or grasping; yajurruhu expresses pulling him toward himself.

The head is the object of a physical action.

Grasping the head does not disclose every detail of Moses’ hand movement. For example, this verse does not specify which part of the head he held.

Ṭā Hā 20:94 illuminates the same event through Aaron’s words:

قَالَ يَا ابْنَ أُمَّ لَا تَأْخُذْ بِلِحْيَتِي وَلَا بِرَأْسِي إِنِّي خَشِيتُ أَنْ تَقُولَ فَرَّقْتَ بَيْنَ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ وَلَمْ تَرْقُبْ قَوْلِي

qāla yā ibna umma lā taʾkhudh bi-liḥyatī wa-lā bi-raʾsī innī khashītu an taqūla farraqta bayna banī Isrāʾīla wa-lam tarqub qawlī

English translation

He said, “Son of my mother, do not take me by my beard or my head. I feared that you would say, ‘You divided the Children of Israel and did not heed my word.’”

Liḥyatī and raʾsī occur side by side.

Their pairing shows that beard and head are distinct linguistic designations.

This does not mean they lack a bodily connection, or that every anatomical classification places the beard entirely outside the head region.

The point here is the distinction between the two places Aaron names.

Grasping the head should not automatically become an idiom for political domination. The verse describes a specific physical action, though it occurs amid anger and disagreement.

Maryam 19:4: Zechariah’s whitening head

Verse text

قَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّي وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا وَلَمْ أَكُنْ بِدُعَائِكَ رَبِّ شَقِيًّا

qāla rabbi innī wahana al-ʿaẓmu minnī wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shayban wa-lam akun bi-duʿāʾika rabbi shaqiyyan

English translation

He said, “My Lord, my bones have grown weak, and my head has flared with white hair. In calling upon You, my Lord, I have never been disappointed or deprived.”

Usage analysis

Al-raʾs is singular and definite here.

The subject is Zechariah’s advancing age and its signs.

Wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shayban presents a striking image.

Ishtiʿāl literally concerns flaring into flame. Here it describes white hair spreading across the head.

The phrase does not describe Zechariah’s head actually catching fire.

Nor is raʾs synonymous with shayb. The head is the place across which whitening appears; shayban expresses the white hair.

The image shows how age has become visible in a person’s appearance.

The physical meaning of raʾs remains, while the accompanying verb creates an image.

Yūsuf 12:36 and Yūsuf 12:41: the head in a dream and its interpretation

Yūsuf 12:36

وَدَخَلَ مَعَهُ السِّجْنَ فَتَيَانِ قَالَ أَحَدُهُمَا إِنِّي أَرَانِي أَعْصِرُ خَمْرًا وَقَالَ الْآخَرُ إِنِّي أَرَانِي أَحْمِلُ فَوْقَ رَأْسِي خُبْزًا تَأْكُلُ الطَّيْرُ مِنْهُ نَبِّئْنَا بِتَأْوِيلِهِ إِنَّا نَرَاكَ مِنَ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

wa-dakhala maʿahu al-sijna fatayāni qāla aḥaduhumā innī arānī aʿṣiru khamran wa-qāla al-ākharu innī arānī aḥmilu fawqa raʾsī khubzan taʾkulu al-ṭayru minhu nabbiʾnā bi-taʾwīlihi innā narāka mina al-muḥsinīna

English translation

Two young men entered the prison with him. One said, “I see myself pressing wine.” The other said, “I see myself carrying bread above my head, from which birds are eating. Tell us its interpretation; we see you as one of those who do good.”

Yūsuf 12:41

يَا صَاحِبَيِ السِّجْنِ أَمَّا أَحَدُكُمَا فَيَسْقِي رَبَّهُ خَمْرًا وَأَمَّا الْآخَرُ فَيُصْلَبُ فَتَأْكُلُ الطَّيْرُ مِنْ رَأْسِهِ قُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ الَّذِي فِيهِ تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ

yā ṣāḥibayi al-sijni ammā aḥadukumā fa-yasqī rabbahu khamran wa-ammā al-ākharu fa-yuṣlabu fa-taʾkulu al-ṭayru min raʾsihi quḍiya al-amru alladhī fīhi tastaftiyāni

English translation

“My two prison companions, one of you will serve his master wine. The other will be crucified, and birds will eat from his head. The matter about which you seek an answer has been decided.”

Usage analysis

Raʾsī and raʾsihi both denote a physical human head.

The head plays different roles in the two scenes.

In the dream, the young man carries bread above his head. In Joseph’s interpretation, birds eat from the head of someone who will be crucified.

Lexically, raʾs means head in both cases; what happens in relation to it differs.

In the first verse, fawqa locates the bread relative to the head. In the second, min connects the birds’ eating with the person’s head.

Raʾs should not be changed into status, thought, or leadership in either verse.

A dream and its interpretation do not make the word raʾs lose its physical meaning at the linguistic level.

Interpreting a dream is a separate step from identifying the lexical meanings of the things seen in it.

5. Head movements and human behavior

In several verses, the head does more than name a body part: its movement or posture contributes to the portrayal of behavior.

These uses show how bodily movement can have different social or psychological meanings in different situations.

Those meanings do not arise from raʾs alone. The verb, the person’s circumstances, and the verse’s context determine them.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:51: shaking heads in response to resurrection

Verse text

أَوْ خَلْقًا مِمَّا يَكْبُرُ فِي صُدُورِكُمْ فَسَيَقُولُونَ مَنْ يُعِيدُنَا قُلِ الَّذِي فَطَرَكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ فَسَيُنْغِضُونَ إِلَيْكَ رُءُوسَهُمْ وَيَقُولُونَ مَتَىٰ هُوَ قُلْ عَسَىٰ أَنْ يَكُونَ قَرِيبًا

aw khalqan mimmā yakburu fī ṣudūrikum fa-sa-yaqūlūna man yuʿīdunā quli alladhī faṭarakum awwala marratin fa-sa-yunghiḍūna ilayka ruʾūsahum wa-yaqūlūna matā huwa qul ʿasā an yakūna qarīban

English translation

“Or a created thing that seems great within your breasts.” They will say, “Who will bring us back?” Say, “The One who originated you the first time.” They will shake their heads toward you and say, “When will it be?” Say, “Perhaps it is near.”

Usage analysis

Ruʾūsahum is the object of fa-sa-yunghiḍūna.

The verb belongs to N GH Ḍ and concerns moving the head.

In Arabic this can mean moving the head up and down or shaking it.

Here the movement occurs amid a question denying resurrection.

It can therefore indicate surprise, denial, or mockery.

That meaning is not a permanent feature of raʾs. Nor does every head movement indicate denial in all cultures and circumstances.

The dialogue and the opponents’ question clarify the behavioral meaning here.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:5: turning heads and turning away

Verse text

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ تَعَالَوْا يَسْتَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ لَوَّوْا رُءُوسَهُمْ وَرَأَيْتَهُمْ يَصُدُّونَ وَهُمْ مُسْتَكْبِرُونَ

wa-idhā qīla lahum taʿālaw yastaghfir lakum rasūlu Allāhi lawwaw ruʾūsahum wa-raʾaytahum yaṣuddūna wa-hum mustakbirūna

English translation

When they are told, “Come, so that the Messenger of The God may seek forgiveness for you,” they turn their heads, and you see them turning away in arrogance.

Usage analysis

Lawwaw ruʾūsahum describes turning or twisting their heads.

It occurs alongside yaṣuddūna and mustakbirūna.

In this context the movement indicates rejection and arrogance.

The physical action should nevertheless be distinguished from the moral description.

Lawwaw supplies the turning, yaṣuddūna the turning away, and mustakbirūna the arrogant condition.

Ruʾūs itself does not mean arrogance.

Translation should retain the heads and their movement, allowing the behavioral significance to emerge from context.

Ibrāhīm 14:43: heads raised

Verse text

مُهْطِعِينَ مُقْنِعِي رُءُوسِهِمْ لَا يَرْتَدُّ إِلَيْهِمْ طَرْفُهُمْ وَأَفْئِدَتُهُمْ هَوَاءٌ

muhṭiʿīna muqniʿī ruʾūsihim lā yartaddu ilayhim ṭarfuhum wa-afʾidatuhum hawāʾun

English translation

Hurrying, with their heads raised, their gaze not returning to them, and their hearts empty.

Usage analysis

Ruʾūsihim accompanies muqniʿī.

Linguistic explanations emphasize raising or holding the head up.

Not every raised head signifies dignity or pride.

Here the people are described as hurrying, with a fixed gaze and empty hearts.

Their raised heads belong to a terrifying situation, rather than honor or victory.

The same physical posture can thus mean different things in different circumstances.

Al-Sajdah 32:12: heads lowered

Verse text

وَلَوْ تَرَىٰ إِذِ الْمُجْرِمُونَ نَاكِسُو رُءُوسِهِمْ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ رَبَّنَا أَبْصَرْنَا وَسَمِعْنَا فَارْجِعْنَا نَعْمَلْ صَالِحًا إِنَّا مُوقِنُونَ

wa-law tarā idhi al-mujrimūna nākisū ruʾūsihim ʿinda rabbihim rabbanā abṣarnā wa-samiʿnā fa-irjiʿnā naʿmal ṣāliḥan innā mūqinūna

English translation

If only you could see when the offenders lower their heads before their Lord, saying, “Our Lord, we have now seen and heard. Send us back so that we may act rightly. We are now certain.”

Usage analysis

Nākisū ruʾūsihim describes lowering or bowing the head.

Their posture is connected to confronting a truth they now face.

A lowered head can accompany shame, helplessness, or humility; the situation and surrounding expressions establish these meanings.

Here the people speak of seeing, hearing, and certainty, and ask to return to do good.

Their head posture forms part of the scene.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65: overturned upon their heads

Verse text

ثُمَّ نُكِسُوا عَلَىٰ رُءُوسِهِمْ لَقَدْ عَلِمْتَ مَا هَٰؤُلَاءِ يَنْطِقُونَ

thumma nukisū ʿalā ruʾūsihim la-qad ʿalimta mā hāʾulāʾi yanṭiqūna

English translation

Then they were overturned upon their heads: “You certainly know that these do not speak.”

Usage analysis

This verse belongs to Abraham’s dialogue with his people about the idols.

Nukisū ʿalā ruʾūsihim presents an image of overturning or reversal.

In this context, a reversal of thought and argumentative position can also be considered.

Confronted by Abraham’s question, the people themselves admit that the idols do not speak.

A figurative reading of returning to their earlier position and reversing the argument therefore has contextual support.

This does not make raʾs itself mean thought or belief.

The image comes from combining nukisū with ruʾūs. Its contextual force may exceed bodily movement, while R ʾ S retains its own meaning.

Conclusion from these five verses: The head can contribute to expressions of behavior, denial, rejection, fear, shame, and reversal of position. Those meanings arise from the verbs and situations, rather than raʾs inherently meaning arrogance, shame, faith, or denial.

6. The head in images of the Hereafter

Al-Ḥajj 22:19: hot liquid poured from above the heads

Verse text

هَٰذَانِ خَصْمَانِ اخْتَصَمُوا فِي رَبِّهِمْ فَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا قُطِّعَتْ لَهُمْ ثِيَابٌ مِنْ نَارٍ يُصَبُّ مِنْ فَوْقِ رُءُوسِهِمُ الْحَمِيمُ

hādhāni khaṣmāni ikhtaṣamū fī rabbihim fa-alladhīna kafarū quṭṭiʿat lahum thiyābun min nārin yuṣabbu min fawqi ruʾūsihimu al-ḥamīmu

English translation

These are two opposing parties who dispute concerning their Lord. For those who disbelieve, garments of fire are cut out, and scalding liquid is poured from above their heads.

Usage analysis

Ruʾūsihimu is the plural of raʾs.

Min fawqi ruʾūsihimu specifies the location or direction from which the scalding liquid is poured.

Raʾs means head; fawqa supplies “above.”

Raʾs itself does not mean punishment or fire. Other words in the verse provide those meanings.

Al-Dukhān 44:48: punishment poured over the head

Verse text

ثُمَّ صُبُّوا فَوْقَ رَأْسِهِ مِنْ عَذَابِ الْحَمِيمِ

thumma ṣubbū fawqa raʾsihi min ʿadhābi al-ḥamīmi

English translation

Then pour over his head some of the punishment of scalding liquid.

Usage analysis

This image relates to Al-Ḥajj 22:19.

In both, the head is the reference point for pouring the scalding liquid.

Al-Ḥajj uses the plural ruʾūsihim, while Al-Dukhān uses singular raʾsihi.

This difference concerns the people addressed and the scene depicted, not a change in the root’s meaning.

Raʾs retains the meaning of head in both.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:65: heads of devils

Verse text

طَلْعُهَا كَأَنَّهُ رُءُوسُ الشَّيَاطِينِ

ṭalʿuhā ka-annahu ruʾūsu al-shayāṭīni

English translation

Its fruit cluster is as though it were the heads of devils.

Usage analysis

Ruʾūsu al-shayāṭīn occurs within a simile.

The subject is the emerging fruit of the Zaqqūm tree.

The simile should not become a certain claim about the physical shape of devils.

This verse specifies no size, color, horns, eyes, or other features of their heads.

It therefore does not provide a particular anatomical account of a devil’s head.

The simile creates a frightening and repellent image in context.

Ruʾūs still means heads. The comparison concerns devils; raʾs itself does not mean ugliness or fear.

This illustrates the distinction between a word’s lexical meaning and its pictorial effect.

7. Ruʾūsu amwālikum: principal capital and ribā additions

Al-Baqarah 2:279

Verse text

فَإِنْ لَمْ تَفْعَلُوا فَأْذَنُوا بِحَرْبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَإِنْ تُبْتُمْ فَلَكُمْ رُءُوسُ أَمْوَالِكُمْ لَا تَظْلِمُونَ وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ

fa-in lam tafʿalū fa-ʾdhanū bi-ḥarbin mina Allāhi wa-rasūlihi wa-in tubtum fa-lakum ruʾūsu amwālikum lā taẓlimūna wa-lā tuẓlamūna

English translation

If you do not do so, be informed of war from The God and His Messenger. If you turn back, your principal sums are yours: you neither wrong others nor are wronged.

Usage analysis

This is the root’s most significant economic use in the Qur’an.

Ruʾūsu amwālikum is a genitive construction: ruʾūs is the plural of raʾs, and amwālikum combines the plural of māl with the second-person plural pronoun.

Raʾs no longer denotes a body part here.

It means the principal or financial base, distinguished from additions demanded upon it.

Arabic economic language knows this as raʾs al-māl.

For example, when someone gives another a specified amount and subsequently demands an addition, the principal and addition are distinct.

Lexically, raʾs al-māl denotes the principal of that property.

This definition should not be extended without examination to every kind of profit, asset, or transaction.

Principal and addition

In Al-Baqarah 2:279, fa-lakum ruʾūsu amwālikum follows the discussion of abandoning ribā.

Context therefore supplies the distinction between principal and the ribā addition.

Al-Baqarah 2:278 commands abandoning what remains of ribā. Al-Baqarah 2:279 then preserves the principal sums for their owners.

The sequence matters.

The Qur’an rejects the demand for ribā additions in this context while preserving the principal property.

Lā taẓlimūna wa-lā tuẓlamūna clarifies the bounds of justice.

Owners may not wrong others through ribā; neither are their underlying financial rights simply disregarded.

Preserving the principal and prohibiting injustice thus appear together in the verse’s construction.

Al-Ṭabarī on Al-Baqarah 2:279: principal sums and ribā additions

Does raʾs al-māl mean every investment?

In contemporary economics, capital can include money, buildings, productive equipment, shares, financial assets, or a collection of economic resources.

These broader uses should not be imported without examination into ruʾūsu amwālikum in Al-Baqarah 2:279.

The verse concerns ribā and abandoning its demand.

Raʾs al-māl here denotes the principal being claimed; the phrase does not itself define every contemporary form of investment.

Profit from actual trade, an addition demanded on a debt, and appreciation of an asset can have different economic and legal characteristics.

R ʾ S alone does not determine which constitutes ribā.

That requires separate study of R B W, the economic relationship, contractual conditions, and verses on justice and debt.

Is raʾs al-māl always the original numerical amount?

Lexically, raʾs al-māl is the principal property.

That alone does not answer every question about changing monetary value, inflation, reduced purchasing power, or debt repayment under different economic conditions.

A debt’s nominal amount may remain fixed while its real value changes over time.

This matters in modern economics, but ruʾūs alone cannot establish that the Qur’an prescribes a particular financial formula for every method of calculating real value.

Conversely, economic changes do not authorize abandoning the clear meaning of principal property in the verse.

The lexical definition, the parties’ actual rights, and economic justice must be distinguished.

Forqan’s separate study of ribā examines these matters more fully in relation to money, debt, profit, inflation, risk, and social justice.

The Book of Riba: Beyond Interest

The lexical conclusion in this root study remains limited and clear: raʾs al-māl is the principal property, distinguished from its additions.

Does raʾs here mean “commander”?

No.

Although raʾīs and riʾāsah belong to the family, ruʾūsu amwālikum does not concern commanding or governing assets.

Raʾs denotes the principal portion or financial base.

The root’s figurative uses cannot all be transferred into one another.

Just as raʾs al-jabal does not mean “commander of the mountain,” raʾs al-māl does not mean “leader of the assets.”

Each extension should be understood within its own established expression.

8. Morphology, related roots, and translation proposals

Singular and plural of raʾs

Raʾs is singular; ruʾūs is its plural.

Arabic also records the plural arʾus, which does not occur in the Qur’an.

Qur’anic spellings vary with prepositions and attached pronouns, as in bi-raʾsi, raʾsī, ruʾūsihim, and bi-ruʾūsikum.

These differences do not create separate roots or independent derivatives.

Nor do the spellings رؤوس and رءوس change the underlying root.

R ʾ S (raʾs) compared with W J H (wajh)

Study of W J H

In bodily use, wajh denotes the face, the forward-facing portion of the head.

Raʾs names the head more broadly.

This matters in Al-Māʾidah 5:6, where the face and head appear in separate phrases.

Ruʾūsakum therefore cannot be translated as “your faces.”

The distinction does not imply that Arabic recognizes no overlap or connection between regions of the face and head.

R ʾ S (raʾs) compared with N Ṣ Y (nāṣiyah)

Study of N Ṣ Y

Nāṣiyah concerns the front of the head, especially the forehead or forelock.

Raʾs concerns the head in its broader extent.

Nāṣiyah occurs in passages such as Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:16.

Nāṣiyah and raʾs are therefore not complete synonyms.

Nor do actions described concerning the forelock automatically establish rulings about the whole head.

R ʾ S (raʾs) compared with SH ʿ R (shaʿr)

Study of SH ʿ R

Shaʿr means hair, whereas raʾs means head.

In Al-Baqarah 2:196, shaving the head means removing its hair, as understood from combining Ḥ L Q with raʾs.

Raʾs should not therefore be indexed as a derivative of the root for hair.

Head hair and the head itself are distinct concepts.

R ʾ S (raʾs) compared with R ʾ Y (raʾā)

Study of R ʾ Y

R ʾ Y concerns seeing and, in some uses, opinion or judgment.

It differs from R ʾ S.

The location of the eyes in the head creates a bodily connection between seeing and the head, but does not make the roots identical.

Raʾā, ruʾyā, and yarā belong to R ʾ Y, not R ʾ S.

Ruʾyā in Joseph’s dream or the vision of entering the Sacred Mosque should therefore not be indexed under R ʾ S.

R ʾ S (raʾs) compared with Q M M (qimmah)

Arabic uses qimmah for the summit or highest part of something.

Raʾs can likewise denote the top of a mountain or object.

Its semantic range is broader, also covering the bodily head, beginnings, leadership, and principal capital.

Overlap in describing a summit does not make these root families identical.

Translation proposals for Forqan

In bodily uses, raʾs should be translated as “head.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:196, ruʾūsakum means “your heads,” and raʾsihi means “his head.”

In Al-Māʾidah 5:6, bi-ruʾūsikum is close to “over your heads.”

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 and Ṭā Hā 20:94, raʾs is Aaron’s head. Adding “hair” or “locks” requires contextual reasoning, since raʾs itself does not mean hair.

In Maryam 19:4, the image of ishtiʿāl al-raʾs shayban should be retained where possible, as in “my head has flared with white hair.” The more natural “the hair of my head has turned completely white” conveys the intended meaning but reduces the image carried by ishtiʿāl.

“They will shake their heads” suits Al-Isrāʾ 17:51. “They turn their heads” in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:5 retains the movement accompanying rejection.

“They have lowered their heads” suits Al-Sajdah 32:12; “with their heads raised” suits Ibrāhīm 14:43.

“Heads of devils” should be retained in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:65: the phrase is a simile, and the Qur’an supplies no detailed account of their shape.

In Al-Baqarah 2:279, ruʾūsu amwālikum should be “your principal sums” or “the principal of your assets.” A literal “heads of your assets” would not convey the established economic meaning to a Persian reader.

9. Final synthesis

Arabic R ʾ S starts from the concrete meaning of head and extends to the upper, initial, or principal part of objects and affairs.

Early uses include mountain tops, leadership, and principal property. Prominence, precedence, and being principal connect these extensions, without reducing them all to one fixed meaning.

All eighteen Qur’anic occurrences belong to the noun raʾs and its plural.

Most concern the human head: shaving and wiping, illness and aging, Moses and Aaron, and the dreams of Joseph’s fellow prisoners.

In some verses, posture and movement contribute to the portrayal of behavior. Shaking, turning, raising, or lowering the head can indicate denial, rejection, fear, shame, or a reversal of position in different contexts.

These are not fixed meanings of the root. They arise from the accompanying verb and sentence construction.

Raʾs retains its meaning in images of the Hereafter: the head is sometimes the reference point for pouring scalding liquid; elsewhere the heads of devils form a simile describing Zaqqūm’s fruit.

Ruʾūsu amwālikum in Al-Baqarah 2:279 is the root’s only economic use in the Qur’an. Raʾs denotes principal property distinguished from additions demanded upon it.

A word can thus extend from a body part to an economic concept without requiring the bodily image to remain in translation.

Final definition: R ʾ S primarily denotes the head of a person or animal. In extended uses it can name the upper, initial, prominent, or principal part of something. In the Qur’an this range appears chiefly in the bodily head and once in the economic sense of principal property.

Each occurrence requires distinguishing the head itself, the action performed on it, the state expressed through its movement, and any figurative or idiomatic meaning.

Raʾs therefore does not itself mean thought, arrogance, shame, command, or financial profit. Where such concepts are relevant, particular combinations, verbs, and contexts establish them.

A defensible common core is the head and its extension to a prominent or principal part. The precise meaning still depends on the subject and construction of each phrase.
