Root H SH SH — Bringing down leaves and cheerfulness

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَهُشُّ
(ahushshu)
I knock down leaves; imperfect

From shaking tree leaves down for livestock to softness, liveliness, and a warm welcome in Arabic

The Arabic triliteral root ه ش ش has several semantic branches: loose cohesion, softness, and easy breakage; shaking or striking branches to bring down their leaves; and happiness, cheerfulness, and a willing welcome to people or good deeds.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an, in Moses’ explanation of his staff. He says that he uses it to bring down leaves for his sheep to eat.

Here hashsh does not mean driving sheep, striking animals, or merely waving a staff. It names a familiar pastoral activity: using a stick to make tree leaves available to livestock.

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic lexicons record هَشّ and هَشِيش for something soft, loose, or easily broken. هَشَّ الْخُبْزُ describes bread becoming light and crumbly; هَشَّ الْعُودُ refers to wood breaking or becoming brittle.

The shared feature is loose cohesion or easy separation. Softness and brittleness are nevertheless different: something may be soft, or dry and crisp enough to break easily. Arabic sources record both qualities within this family.

Another branch concerns human behavior. هَشَّ لَهُ and هَشِشْتُ بِفُلَانٍ express pleasure at meeting someone, willingness, and a warm welcome. هَشٌّ بَشٌّ describes a friendly, cheerful person.

Evidence from pre-Qur’anic Arabic poetry

A poem attributed to the renowned pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿshā says of Salāmah Dhū Fāʾish:

«أَصْبَحَ ذُو فَائِشٍ سَلَامَةُ ذُو التَّفْضَالِ هَشًّا فُؤَادُهُ جَذِلَا»

Aṣbaḥa dhū Fāʾishin Salāmatu dhū t-tafḍāli hashshan fuʾāduhu jadhilā.

“Salāmah Dhū Fāʾish, that generous man, has a joyful and open heart.”

هَشًّا alongside جَذِلَا expresses joy and openness of heart. The verse concerns neither breaking objects nor shaking down leaves: it praises readiness for kindness and generosity.

Preserved in the collected poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā and in lexical citations, the line fits its poem’s praise of generosity. As with many pre-Islamic poetic witnesses, however, its attribution comes through later compilations; its precise composition date is not independently established.

It shows that cheerfulness, openness, and a warm welcome have a place in the early Arabic poetic tradition; these are not merely modern senses.

Hashsh in pastoral life

In classical Arabic, هَشَّ الْوَرَقَ means striking leaves and branches with a stick to make leaves detach and fall. هَشَّ عَلَى غَنَمِهِ describes bringing down tree leaves with a staff for one’s livestock.

Not all animal fodder lies within reach on the ground. Sheep or goats may be unable to reach higher leaves. A shepherd can strike or move the branches with a staff so that leaves fall for the animals to eat.

This is distinct from cutting down a tree or breaking its trunk. The aim is to bring edible leaves down; the word alone does not specify the precise method or any damage to the branches.

Lexicons differ over whether hashsh also includes pulling a branch toward oneself. Some record that sense; others identify striking branches and bringing down leaves as the principal livestock-related usage.

The latter has clearer support for the Qur’anic verse: the staff is the implement, and providing leaves for livestock is the action’s result.

This study has not established an independently dated pre-Qur’anic poetic witness describing this exact pastoral act. Its meaning is nevertheless clear in classical Arabic and directly attested in the Qur’an; no invented witness is needed to establish an earlier date.

2. Forms and derivatives

The Qur’an uses this root only in وَأَهُشُّ.

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • هَشَّ يَهُشُّ: striking branches and leaves with a stick to bring the leaves down.
  • هَشّ: the verbal noun for shaking down leaves; adjectivally, soft, loose, or brittle.
  • هَشَّ يَهَشُّ / هَشِشْتُ: becoming pleased or cheerful, or turning willingly toward someone or something.
  • هَشَاشَة: softness or looseness in material contexts; liveliness, friendliness, or willingness in human contexts.
  • هَشِيش: soft, loose, or easily broken; of people, friendly and cheerful.
  • هَشُوش: recorded for certain soft qualities or abundant milk production in livestock.
  • اِهْتَشَّ: becoming pleased and responding with willingness or enthusiasm.

Their presence in Arabic sources does not make all these forms Qur’anic. Only وَأَهُشُّ occurs in the Qur’an.

The root is geminate: its second and third consonants are identical. In أَهُشُّ they appear as a doubled shīn.

The initial hamzah marks the first-person singular imperfect, “I.” This is a basic Form I verb, not Form IV merely because it begins with hamzah.

3. Qur’anic usage

Ṭā Hā 20:18 — Moses and his shepherd’s staff

«قَالَ هِيَ عَصَايَ أَتَوَكَّؤُا۟ عَلَيْهَا وَأَهُشُّ بِهَا عَلَىٰ غَنَمِي وَلِيَ فِيهَا مَـَٔارِبُ أُخْرَىٰ»

Qāla hiya ʿaṣāya atawakkaʾu ʿalayhā wa-ahushshu bihā ʿalā ghanamī wa-liya fīhā maʾāribu ukhrā.

“He said: This is my staff; I lean on it, and with it I bring down leaves for my sheep, and I have other uses for it.”

In Ṭā Hā 20:17, Moses is asked what he holds in his right hand. He identifies his staff and describes several uses.

First, أَتَوَكَّؤُا۟ عَلَيْهَا means that he leans on it.

Next, وَأَهُشُّ بِهَا عَلَىٰ غَنَمِي means that he uses the same staff to bring down tree leaves for his sheep.

Finally, وَلِيَ فِيهَا مَـَٔارِبُ أُخْرَىٰ says that it meets other purposes and needs as well.

His answer presents the staff as an ordinary, multipurpose object in his life.

The role of أَهُشُّ in the sentence

أَهُشُّ is a first-person singular imperfect verb, describing something Moses habitually does with his staff.

In context, it denotes shaking or striking branches to bring down leaves.

In بِهَا, the preposition bāʾ marks the instrument, while the pronoun refers to the staff.

In عَلَىٰ غَنَمِي, the sheep are not the objects being struck. The purpose is to provide leaves for them, consistent with the established expression هَشَّ عَلَى غَنَمِهِ.

Thus أَهُشُّ بِهَا عَلَىٰ غَنَمِي should not become “I beat my sheep with my staff.” That changes the action’s direction and ignores its implicit object, the tree leaves.

The verse does not expressly contain وَرَق, “leaves.” Yet the verb in this construction conventionally includes bringing leaves down. Including “leaves” in translation conveys the verb’s contextual meaning rather than adding an unwarranted explanation.

Hashsh and the staff

The staff serves both as a support and as a means of bringing down leaves.

The actions differ: in أَتَوَكَّؤُا۟ عَلَيْهَا it supports the body; in أَهُشُّ بِهَا it acts upon tree branches.

An object can have several functions without merging the meanings of the verbs describing its uses.

In Ṭā Hā 20:19 Moses is commanded to throw down the staff; in Ṭā Hā 20:20 it becomes a moving snake.

The sequence identifies an ordinary everyday implement before the extraordinary event. أَهُشُّ itself means neither a miracle nor the staff’s transformation: it describes one of its normal uses.

4. Relations among the semantic branches

The family has three main usages: structural softness or looseness, bringing down leaves by movement or blows, and human cheerfulness or liveliness.

One can imagine an association: a loosely cohesive substance changes under slight pressure; shaken leaves detach; and human openness contrasts with rigidity or reserve.

This is an explanatory analogy, not proof of an etymological sequence.

Al-Aʿshā’s poetic witness illustrates cheerfulness; lexicons also record material and pastoral usages. These facts do not establish that friendliness developed from crumbly bread, or that bringing down leaves originated all the other meanings.

Nor should cheerfulness enter the Qur’anic meaning of أَهُشُّ. In Ṭā Hā 20:18 Moses describes work done with his staff, not his own happiness.

Each branch must retain its meaning within its own construction and context.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

ه ش ش and ه ز ز — Bringing down leaves and shaking

ه ز ز denotes moving or shaking.

In Maryam 19:25, Mary is told to shake the palm trunk toward herself so that fresh dates fall upon her.

The result resembles Moses’ activity: moving part of a tree releases food from it.

The two verbs nevertheless have different meanings.

Hazz names the shaking; what falls from the tree is specified by the rest of the verse.

Pastoral hashsh names a familiar way of detaching leaves to provide animal fodder.

Shaking a branch can bring leaves down, but not every shaking is hashsh, nor does every relevant usage require one exact movement.

ه ش ش and خ ب ط — Bringing down leaves and striking

One Arabic use of khabṭ denotes striking, including striking a tree to bring down its leaves.

This overlaps with pastoral hashsh, which explains why Arabic glosses of أَهُشُّ sometimes use أَخْبِطُ.

Khabṭ has a broader range of striking contexts. Hashsh in أَهُشُّ بِهَا عَلَىٰ غَنَمِي concerns the specific activity of providing leaves for livestock.

This does not establish that every khabṭ is harsh and every hashsh gentle. Intensity and manner depend on the usage and its circumstances.

ه ش ش and ه ش م — Two different roots

These roots are close in sound and meaning but remain distinct.

ه ش ش repeats shīn, whereas the third consonant of ه ش م is mīm.

The Qur’anic هَشِيم belongs to ه ش م, appearing in al-Kahf 18:45 and al-Qamar 54:31.

Hashīm denotes dry, fragmented, or broken plant remains.

Moses uses أَهُشُّ in Ṭā Hā 20:18 for supplying fodder leaves; hashīm elsewhere depicts dry, broken vegetation.

The similar imagery neither makes hashīm a derivative of ه ش ش nor puts the destruction of vegetation into أَهُشُّ.

ه ش ش and ب ش ش — Friendliness and a warm welcome

Arabic sometimes pairs هَشّ and بَشّ in هَشٌّ بَشٌّ.

Both concern friendliness. Bashsh tends toward a bright, welcoming face; hashsh can also express inner enthusiasm, willingness, and readiness to welcome someone or do good.

This is a tendency, not a rigid distinction: their usages overlap.

The human branch helps describe Arabic’s lexical range but does not determine the Qur’anic pastoral meaning.

6. Suggested translations for Forqan

For وَأَهُشُّ بِهَا عَلَىٰ غَنَمِي in Ṭā Hā 20:18, a suitable translation is:

“And with it I bring down leaves for my sheep.”

This is concise, natural, and faithful to the verb’s use.

A fuller explanation could read:

“And with it I strike tree branches so that their leaves fall for my sheep.”

The longer version suits a root study or word-box explanation; the shorter wording suffices for the direct verse translation.

“I drive my sheep with the staff” or “I beat my sheep with the staff” is unsuitable for this reading and context, replacing leaf gathering with driving or striking animals.

Other usages require their own equivalents: هَشّ of bread can mean soft and crumbly or easily broken; of people, friendly, openhearted, or lively; pastorally, bringing down leaves with a stick.

These distinctions should remain visible rather than being merged into one translation.

7. Conclusion

Arabic records several branches of ه ش ش: structural softness and brittleness, bringing down leaves through movement or blows, and human cheerfulness.

The poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā witnesses the human sense in early tradition. Classical Arabic records the pastoral sense, directly attested in the Qur’an.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an as وَأَهُشُّ: Moses describes using his staff to bring down leaves for his sheep.

Forqan definition: In Qur’anic usage, ه ش ش means shaking or striking tree branches to bring down their leaves. The Arabic family also includes softness and easy breakage, and human liveliness and cheerfulness. The precise historical relationship among these branches is uncertain; each derivative requires contextual interpretation.

In Ṭā Hā 20:18, أَهُشُّ means neither driving livestock nor breaking a tree. It is a familiar pastoral use of a staff to make leaves available as fodder.
