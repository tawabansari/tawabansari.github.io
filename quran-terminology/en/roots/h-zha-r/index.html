The Qur’anic Root Ḥ Ẓ R

From enclosure and restraint to an unwithheld gift and a fence builder’s broken branches

The Arabic root ح ظ ر concerns restraint, restricting access, and placing a barrier between something and what it seeks to reach. حَظَرَ means preventing or prohibiting. مَحْظُور describes something whose performance or access is prohibited or withheld.

The meaning also has a tangible image in material life. A حَظِيرَة is a place enclosed with branches, wood, reeds, or thorns. Such a fence may keep livestock within a boundary and prevent harmful animals, cold, or wind from entering. In either case, the fence forms a barrier between the two sides of a boundary.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:20, مَحْظُورًا concerns the Lord’s gift: a gift not reserved for one group and withheld from the other. In Al-Qamar 54:31, الْمُحْتَظِرِ occurs in an image of dry, broken branches associated with livestock fencing.

Qur’anic forms: مَحْظُورًا in Al-Isrāʾ 17:20 and الْمُحْتَظِرِ in Al-Qamar 54:31.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the two occurrences of Ḥ Ẓ R and their morphology

Forms such as حَظَرَ, يَحْظُرُ, حَظْر, حِظَار, حَظِيرَة, حَاظِر, احْتَظَرَ, and حَظَائِر are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

1. The root’s semantic core and early Arabic usage

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for ح ظ ر: الْمَنْع, preventing or restraining.

He explains the verb حَظَرَ and the passive participle مَحْظُور in this sense, then relates حِظَار to a fence made from branches for sheep and other livestock.

Al-ʿAyn likewise defines حِظَار as the enclosure’s wall and explains that a حَظِيرَة is made from wood or reeds. It states that anything forming a barrier between someone and something else can prevent access to it.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: the fence, the enclosed place, and its builder

Preventing and constructing an enclosure are therefore not wholly unrelated ideas. Both involve a barrier between two sides. Their shared root does not, however, make all their features identical. Prevention may operate through speech, an order, or a social restriction, whereas a physical fence is made from wood and branches.

Ḥaẓara: preventing access or an action

حَظَرَ الشَّيْءَ means prohibiting something. حَظَرَ عَلَيْهِ is also used for imposing a restriction on someone.

An Arabic example is لَا حِظَارَ عَلَى الْأَسْمَاءِ: there is no restriction on naming.

The expression does not concern an actual wall or fence. It means that someone is not prevented from choosing a name.

This usage shows that ḥaẓr can concern a nonmaterial barrier: a restriction that blocks access, choice, or the performance of an action.

Lexical sources also place ḥaẓr opposite إِبَاحَة, permission. Accordingly, in certain legal and juristic contexts, مَحْظُور means prohibited or religiously unlawful.

The lexical meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from a legal technical term. Not every prohibited matter constitutes a divine prohibition. A dangerous road may be closed, entry to a place restricted, or someone prevented from using another person’s property. These restrictions are not in themselves legal rulings from God.

The root’s basic meaning is prevention or restraint. The verb alone does not establish who imposes the restriction, why, or by what authority.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: prevention, permission, and fencing usages

Ḥiẓār and ḥaẓīrah: a fence and an enclosed place

حِظَار denotes a barrier constructed around something. In Arab pastoral life, this fence was commonly made from branches, brush, wood, or reeds.

حَظِيرَة denotes a place surrounded by such a barrier. Its plural is حَظَائِر.

The place served several practical purposes. It kept livestock within a boundary so they would not scatter. It could also protect them from cold wind, predators, and external dangers.

The shared feature is a barrier and a boundary. A fence controls movement or access, but its purpose may be containment, protection, or preventing entry and exit.

Ḥaẓīrah should therefore not mean only a prison or only a shelter. An enclosure may both protect what is inside it and restrict its movement.

Some Arabic dialects also record ḥaẓīrah for an enclosed place used to store dates. This shows that the word was not exclusive to livestock pens.

Ḥāẓir and muḥtaẓir: someone who makes a barrier

حَاظِر is the active participle of حَظَرَ, describing someone who prevents something or creates a barrier.

مُحْتَظِر derives from احْتَظَرَ. In pastoral usage, the verb means constructing an enclosure, and muḥtaẓir denotes someone who provides a fence for himself or his animals.

Al-ʿAyn records a distinction between someone building an enclosure for himself and someone doing so for another. In this explanation, مُحْتَظِر particularly concerns someone providing the enclosed place for his own use.

This distinction is not an absolute rule in every Arabic usage. It does show, however, that muḥtaẓir fundamentally identifies a person or agent performing an action, rather than directly naming dry branches or pieces of fencing.

In Al-Qamar 54:31, الْمُحْتَظِر must therefore be distinguished from الْهَشِيم. In the reading of Ḥafṣ, the first refers to a fence builder; the second concerns dry, broken fragments.

2. Natural fencing and extended usages

Branches and thorns used for fencing

In pastoral areas, fences were not always built from stone or brick. Branches, thorny bushes, and tall reeds could be placed together to form a temporary or relatively durable barrier.

Fresh, flexible branches were sometimes more suitable for construction. After a time they dried and broke under pressure, impact, or the passage of time.

Ibn Fāris mentions this usage when explaining حِظَار, noting that the fresh branches eventually become dry and brittle.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: prevention and fencing material becoming dry and brittle

This image is particularly relevant to Al-Qamar 54:31. There, هَشِيم stands alongside الْمُحْتَظِر: the image of dry, broken branches is connected to someone who builds a fence.

Ḥaẓr does not itself mean drying or breaking. These features come from hashīm and the condition of the material used in the enclosure.

Extension to gardens and trees

A verse by Al-Marār ibn Munqidh al-ʿAdawī reads:

فَإِنَّ لَنَا حَظَائِرَ نَاعِمَاتٍ
عَطَاءَ اللَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَا

fa-inna lanā ḥaẓāʾira nāʿimātin / ʿaṭāʾa allāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīnā

Meaning: We have lush, flourishing enclosures, a gift from God, Lord of the worlds. In the lexical explanation, these “enclosures” are an image of the poet’s palm groves.

Explaining the verse, lexicographers state that the poet uses ḥaẓāʾir to describe date palms. The poem’s continuation also speaks of branches and the trees’ growth.

Al-Azminah wa-l-Amkinah: Al-Marār’s witness and its continuing palm-grove image

Here, the word extends from an enclosed place to a group of trees or a palm plantation.

Al-Marār ibn Munqidh was a poet of the Islamic period; his death is recorded at approximately the end of the first Islamic century. The verse is therefore a witness to early Arabic usage, but should not be presented as pre-Islamic evidence or certain proof of usage before the Qur’an’s revelation.

Al-Aʿlām: Al-Marār al-ʿAdawī, a poet of the Islamic period

Its significance lies in showing the word’s range in literary language, rather than establishing the root’s historical origin.

Ḥaẓir and figurative expressions

Arabic also records حَظِر for branches and brush used to construct fences.

The expression الْحَظِرِ الرَّطْبِ, fresh fencing brush, developed from this image in some figurative usages. Fresh branches and thorns gathered for an enclosure could cause trouble for someone caught among them.

Accordingly, lexical sources record expressions concerning becoming entangled in a difficult undertaking, as well as producing offensive speech or inflammatory lies.

جَاءَ بِالْحَظِرِ الرَّطْبِ is reported of someone producing an offensive statement or an enormous lie. أَوْقَدَ فِي الْحَظِرِ is also recorded for tale-bearing and inflaming hostility.

The usage is close to the image of fire and firewood, but should not be confused with ح ط ب. Ḥaṭab concerns firewood, whereas ḥaẓr concerns fencing and fencing material. Both may use branches and fire in a figurative image, but belong to distinct lexical families.

Tale-bearing is likewise not the fixed meaning of ḥaẓr. It is a figurative usage based on fencing material and the image of fire.

3. “Wa-mā kāna ʿaṭāʾu rabbika maḥẓūran”: Al-Isrāʾ 17:20

كُلًّا نُمِدُّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ وَهَٰؤُلَاءِ مِنْ عَطَاءِ رَبِّكَ وَمَا كَانَ عَطَاءُ رَبِّكَ مَحْظُورًا

kullan numiddu hāʾulāʾi wa-hāʾulāʾi min ʿaṭāʾi rabbika wa-mā kāna ʿaṭāʾu rabbika maḥẓūran

Translation: We sustain both groups, these and those, from your Lord’s gift; and your Lord’s gift is not withheld.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:20

مَحْظُورًا here is a passive participle from the form I verb حَظَرَ, meaning prohibited or withheld.

The adjective refers to عَطَاءُ رَبِّكَ, your Lord’s gift. By negating its being maḥẓūr, the verse emphasizes that His giving is not restricted so that only one group benefits while the other is excluded from receiving any share at all.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Isrāʾ 17:20: both groups receiving divine gifts

The two groups in context

To understand the scope of maḥẓūr, the preceding two verses must be considered.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:18 discusses someone who desires the fleeting worldly life. It explains that what such a person receives in this world depends on divine will; it does not say that every desire must be fulfilled.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:19 describes someone who desires the Hereafter, strives for it appropriately, and is a believer.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:20 then discusses both groups and states that the Lord’s gift reaches them.

The subject is therefore receiving divine gifts among people with different orientations.

Maḥẓūran does not mean religiously unlawful in this context. The verse is not classifying the Lord’s gift as legally permitted or prohibited. Rather, His giving is not withheld from one group and reserved exclusively for the other.

An unwithheld gift does not mean equal provision

Negating restriction of the Lord’s gift must not become a claim that everyone receives equal wealth, resources, health, and comfort in this world.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:21, immediately following this verse, discusses differences in people’s degrees and advantages.

The Qur’an thus distinguishes two matters: divine giving is not exclusive to one of the two groups under discussion, and the amount and kind of provision people receive are not identical.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:18 also makes clear that worldly giving is connected to divine will and that people do not necessarily obtain everything they desire.

An unwithheld gift therefore does not mean the absence of every measure, condition, difference, or limitation in human life.

What this verse negates is withholding the Lord’s gift from one of the two groups being discussed.

Worldly provision and judging human worth

The verse’s context conveys another significant point: access to worldly resources is not in itself a measure of someone’s truthfulness or moral superiority.

Someone with mistaken beliefs and conduct may possess material resources, while a good person faces hardship. Their actions’ ultimate worth cannot be inferred from this difference.

Al-Baqarah 2:126 likewise discusses worldly provision for someone who disbelieves. Al-Aʿrāf 7:32 considers access to adornment and good provisions.

These verses help explain the breadth of divine giving, but do not change maḥẓūr’s lexical meaning. It still means prohibited or withheld; the ethical and social implications emerge from the verses together and their contexts.

Nor should the verse imply a right to obtain resources through oppression, usurpation, or corruption. The Lord’s gift not being withheld is not the same as every method of acquiring wealth and resources being permissible.

This distinction particularly matters when someone uses prosperity to justify wrongful conduct. Divine giving and human responsibility for using it are separate matters.

4. “Ka-hashīmi al-muḥtaẓiri”: Al-Qamar 54:31

إِنَّا أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً فَكَانُوا كَهَشِيمِ الْمُحْتَظِرِ

innā arsalnā ʿalayhim ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan fa-kānū ka-hashīmi al-muḥtaẓiri

Translation: We sent a single overwhelming cry against them; then they became like the fence builder’s dry, broken branches.

Al-Qamar 54:31

This verse occurs in the account of Thamūd. The preceding verses discuss their rejection of warnings and treatment of the she-camel. Al-Qamar 54:29 describes hamstringing the she-camel, and Al-Qamar 54:30 mentions punishment and warnings; Al-Qamar 54:31 then describes the sending of a single cry and their condition afterward.

Two words must be understood separately here: هَشِيم and الْمُحْتَظِر.

Hashīm comes from ه ش م and concerns dry, broken, brittle vegetation.

Al-muḥtaẓir comes from ح ظ ر and, in the reading of Ḥafṣ, is a form VIII active participle: someone who builds an enclosure or provides a fenced place for himself.

The construction هَشِيمِ الْمُحْتَظِرِ connects them: dry, broken fragments associated with a fence builder and his enclosure.

The verse’s tangible image

In pastoral life, enclosures could be made from branches and thorny brush. Once dry, such branches became brittle.

Animal movement, contact with the fence, or the passage of time could break the branches into small pieces.

Some lexical reports and early explanations connect hashīm al-muḥtaẓir with dry branches gathered for building a fence. Others describe fragments falling from the enclosure or being crushed under livestock’s feet.

In both explanations, the central image is clear: branches once possessing shape and firmness have dried, broken, and become scattered fragments.

The verse compares Thamūd’s condition following the cry to this situation.

The simile portrays their collapse and loss of former strength and stability. It should not imply that everyone’s body literally became dry plant matter. The language presents a comparison.

Al-muḥtaẓir is neither a name for the punishment nor a word meaning destroyer or breaker. Its meaning comes from constructing an enclosure; the quality of fragmentation comes from hashīm.

The difference between muḥtaẓir and muḥtaẓar

In the reading of Ḥafṣ, الْمُحْتَظِرِ has a kasrah, an i vowel, on the letter ẓāʾ.

This is a form VIII active participle, referring to someone who builds an enclosure.

Reports of recitation also transmit الْمُحْتَظَرِ, with a fatḥah, an a vowel, on ẓāʾ.

In that reading, the word can refer to something enclosed, an enclosed place, or the material placed inside it. Some grammarians also offer a verbal-noun analysis of the form.

Al-Qurṭubī: the i and a readings of ẓāʾ and the verbal-noun analysis

The distinction matters: the first foregrounds the enclosure’s owner or builder; the second foregrounds the enclosed place or state.

Both nevertheless relate to an enclosure and its dry, broken material.

The variation must not lead us to assign the same morphological role to both forms without attending to their vowels.

For a translation based on the reading of Ḥafṣ, “fence builder” or “owner of the livestock enclosure” is appropriate. Commentary can explain that the image concerns dry fragments of fencing material.

Dry branches, fence debris, or burnt material?

Early exegetical reports offer several explanations of hashīm al-muḥtaẓir.

One explanation concerns dry, broken branches from a fence. Another report preserved by Al-Ṭabarī describes earth crumbling from a wall, so not every report should be reduced to wood fragments. Some reports also mention burnt bones.

These explanations do not all have the same degree of lexical support.

Hashīm has a clear connection in Arabic with vegetation becoming dry and broken. Muḥtaẓir likewise concerns fencing and constructing it. The image of dry, broken branches and fencing material therefore has more direct support in the two words themselves.

Being burnt is not a fixed feature of either hashīm or muḥtaẓir. It may occur in some interpretations and punishment accounts, but should not be added to the base translation without evidence.

Nor does the verse explain the cry’s physical mechanism or its detailed effects on bodies. It explicitly states the sending of a single cry and compares the people’s resulting condition to hashīm al-muḥtaẓir.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Qamar 54:31: fencing branches, wall earth, and the burnt-bone report

The image and other Qur’anic usages of hashīm

In Al-Kahf 18:45, hashīm concerns vegetation that becomes dry and scattered after growth and flourishing.

This helps clarify hashīm in Al-Qamar 54:31, showing that dry, broken vegetation is a distinct, familiar Qur’anic image.

In Al-Qamar 54:31, however, the addition of al-muḥtaẓir makes the image more specific: it is not merely dry vegetation, but hashīm associated with a fence and its builder.

“They became like dry vegetation” conveys the general sense, but omits part of the verse’s particular image.

Conversely, adding details such as precisely how often the animals trod on the branches or exactly how the enclosure collapsed goes beyond what the verse states.

5. Differences from related roots

Ḥ Ẓ R (ḥaẓr) and M N ʿ (manʿ)

Manʿ denotes prevention or restraint and can be used in many situations.

Ḥaẓr likewise has a direct lexical connection with prevention, but its family also includes the particular image of fencing and establishing a boundary.

Accordingly, ḥaẓīrah names an enclosed place, whereas manʿ does not itself name such a structure.

This does not mean every instance of ḥaẓr necessarily concerns a physical wall. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:20, maḥẓūr concerns divine giving and means withheld.

Study of M N ʿ

Ḥ Ẓ R (ḥaẓr) and Ḥ J R (ḥajr)

Some usages of ḥajr concern prevention and restriction, bringing its semantic range close to ḥaẓr.

The ḥajr family also records usages concerning stone, reason, sanctity, and restriction. Understanding each branch requires attention to the word’s form and context.

A shared sense of prevention does not justify treating all their derivatives as identical.

In legal usage, too, we must consider what is restricted and why. Neither ḥajr nor ḥaẓr alone establishes a prohibition’s legitimacy.

Study of Ḥ J R

Ḥ Ẓ R (ḥaẓr) and Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣr)

Ḥaṣr concerns confinement, restriction, and placing something in a constricted situation.

Ḥaẓr may likewise involve a fence or barrier. In tangible usage, however, a ḥaẓīrah is a place surrounded by a wall of particular materials.

Their overlap in restriction must not cause the sense of fencing or siege in one root to be transferred to every usage of the other.

For example, the unwithheld gift in Al-Isrāʾ 17:20 can be translated as “giving that is not held back,” without introducing the image of siege or confinement.

Study of Ḥ Ṣ R

Ḥ Ẓ R (ḥaẓr) and Ḥ R M (ḥurm)

Ḥ R M occurs in the Qur’an concerning sanctity, prohibition, and matters possessing a protected boundary or special standing.

Ḥaẓr can also denote prevention and prohibition in legal and juristic language. This does not make the roots complete Qur’anic synonyms.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:20, maḥẓūr means prohibited or withheld; the verse does not concern a particular ruling on what is religiously lawful or unlawful.

Nor does a social or legal restriction establish that God has forbidden the same thing. Attributing a prohibition to God requires evidence from revelation independent of the word itself.

Study of Ḥ R M

Ḥ Ẓ R (ḥaẓr) and Ḥ Ẓ Ẓ (ḥaẓẓ)

ح ظ ظ is a different root concerning a share, portion, or allotment.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:176, ḥaẓẓ concerns a share. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:79 likewise uses it for a portion or advantage.

Ḥaẓr, by contrast, ends in rāʾ and concerns prevention and fencing.

Maḥẓūr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:20 must therefore not be confused with maḥẓūẓ, someone favored with a portion.

They may appear together in a discussion of human provision, but are lexically distinct families.

Ḥ Ẓ R (ḥaẓr) and Ḥ Ḍ R (ḥaḍara)

Ḥaḍara concerns presence or being present, whereas ḥaẓr concerns prevention and fencing.

The different second letters, ض and ظ, identify different roots.

Particularly when reading unvowelled Arabic or typing Persian, مُحْتَظِر must not be confused with forms concerning presence.

Study of Ḥ Ḍ R

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:20, مَحْظُورًا means prohibited or withheld.

The following translation fits the verse’s wording and context:

“We sustain both groups, these and those, from your Lord’s gift; and your Lord’s gift is not withheld.”

“Is not withheld” preserves the root’s basic meaning. Commentary can add that divine giving in this context is not exclusive to one of the two groups under discussion.

Translations such as “your Lord’s gift is unlimited and endless” do not follow from maḥẓūran itself: negating withholding does not alone negate every measure or difference in giving.

In Al-Qamar 54:31, الْمُحْتَظِرِ denotes someone who builds an enclosure.

Given the reading of Ḥafṣ, “fence builder” or “owner of the livestock enclosure” is appropriate for this derivative itself.

The complete sentence may be translated as follows:

“We sent a single overwhelming cry against them; then they became like the fence builder’s dry, broken branches.”

To convey the image more naturally, commentary can explain that the reference is to dry branch fragments used in a livestock enclosure or fallen from it.

Hashīm and muḥtaẓir must retain distinct meanings here. Muḥtaẓir does not mean dry, broken, or destroyed; these features emerge from hashīm and the simile as a whole.

In non-Qur’anic usages, حَظَرَ means “he prohibited,” حِظَار means “the enclosure’s wall,” حَظِيرَة means “a livestock pen” or “an enclosed place,” احْتَظَرَ means “he built an enclosure,” and مُحْتَظِر means “a fence builder.”

Conclusion

Brief root meaning: The Arabic root ح ظ ر denotes prevention, restraint, and creating a barrier to access. This meaning appears both in nonmaterial restrictions and in constructing actual enclosures.

A حَظِيرَة is a place enclosed with wood, reeds, or branches. حِظَار denotes its wall or barrier, احْتَظَرَ means constructing such an enclosure, and مُحْتَظِر denotes someone who provides it.

In legal and social usage, حَظَرَ means prohibiting, and مَحْظُور describes something whose performance or access is prohibited. Prohibition does not in itself mean that God has declared something religiously unlawful; the source and validity of each prohibition must be understood separately.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:20, مَحْظُورًا concerns the Lord’s gift, emphasizing that it is not withheld from either group under discussion. In Al-Qamar 54:31, الْمُحْتَظِرِ evokes a fence builder whose enclosure is associated with dry, broken branches.

Although outwardly different, both usages belong to one semantic range: creating a barrier that restricts access or movement, and the state of something held back or enclosed by such a barrier.

In Al-Isrāʾ, withholding the gift is negated. In Al-Qamar, an actual enclosure and its constituent material supply the background for a simile of destruction.

Ḥaẓr can therefore be understood generally as prevention and restraint, but translating each derivative also requires its structure and object. Maḥẓūr means withheld, whereas muḥtaẓir means a fence builder. Drying and breaking in Al-Qamar are not this root’s own meaning; they belong to hashīm and the image produced by combining the two words.
