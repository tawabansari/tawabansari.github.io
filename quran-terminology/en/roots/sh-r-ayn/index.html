An open way and access to water: from physical meanings in early Arabic to prescribed ways and the authority to prescribe religion in the Qur’an

In early Arabic, SH R ʿ concerns openness, entry, direct access, and coming forward or becoming visible. Water supplies provide a clear early usage. Sharīʿah and shirʿah denote a place where people or animals can reach water: an accessible approach beside a river or pool.

Sharaʿati al-ibilu fī al-māʾ describes camels entering water and drinking. Mashraʿah names the point of access, while shāriʿ also denotes an accessible road leading somewhere.

The family also covers bringing a spear forward, opening a way, beginning an undertaking, and becoming visible. Its meanings cannot all be reduced to law or the establishment of regulations.

In religious usage, sharaʿa means prescribing and opening a way to follow in practice. Shirʿah and sharīʿah can denote that prescribed way within which people act.

The root occurs five times: shirʿatan in Al-Māʾidah 5:48, shurraʿan in Al-Aʿrāf 7:163, sharaʿa in Al-Shūrā 42:13, sharaʿū in Al-Shūrā 42:21, and sharīʿatin in Al-Jāthiyah 45:18.

These usages are instructive: the Qur’an uses this family both for prescribing religion and for fish coming visibly within reach.

Reading the relevant verses together supports a distinction between the common foundation of the prophets’ religion and prescribed practical ways that may differ among communities. This is a contextual interpretation, not an exhaustive lexical definition of either term.

1. The root in early Arabic

Sharīʿah: an approach to water

Access to water was vital in early Arab life. People and livestock needed springs, rivers, pools, and other sources, but water’s existence alone was insufficient: it had to be accessible.

Drawing deep well water requires equipment and effort. Some riverbanks and pools, however, allow people or animals to approach and drink directly.

Such a place was called sharīʿah or shirʿah.

Early lexical explanations describe an approach to water, particularly visible, accessible water that does not need to be drawn from a well.

Some definitions also require a perennial supply. That qualification belongs to a particular recorded definition and should not automatically be extended to every local or historical usage.

The clear feature here is an open, recognizable approach providing direct access to water.

Sharīʿah is thus the place or approach providing access, rather than simply the water itself.

The distinction offers an illustration: just as an approach to a spring differs from the spring, a prescribed practical way need not exhaust the concept of religion. The theological distinction must nevertheless rest on its own contextual evidence.

Early poetry: sharāʾiʿ in Rabīʿah ibn Maqrūm

Rabīʿah ibn Maqrūm al-Ḍabbī, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, describes thirsty animals approaching water:

فَأَوْرَدَهَا مَعَ ضَوْءِ الصَّبَاحِ
شَرَائِعَ تَطْحَرُ عَنْهَا الْجَمِيمَا

fa-awradahā maʿa ḍawʾi al-ṣabāḥi / sharāʾiʿa taṭḥaru ʿanhā al-jamīmā

“At daybreak he brought them to watering places that drove the gathered debris away.”

Sharāʾiʿ is the plural of sharīʿah.

The animals have crossed dry country in thirst and now reach places where they can drink.

The following verses describe abundant, clear water.

Sharāʾiʿ here denotes watering places and approaches to water, rather than laws or rulings.

An early commentary compares sharīʿah to a furḍah in a river: an opening through which water can be reached.

This is useful evidence for the physical sense. Because the poet lived across both periods, attribution alone does not securely date this particular verse before the Qur’an. It is early Arabic evidence close to the Qur’anic period.

Its usage is nevertheless clear: places or approaches for entering the water.

Entering water and watering animals

Sharaʿati al-dawābbu fī al-māʾ describes animals entering water and drinking.

Sharaʿa ibilahu or ashraʿahā describes an owner bringing camels to accessible water so they can drink.

The verb can describe either the animals’ entry or the owner’s act of bringing them to water.

The subject’s role and the presence of an object distinguish these constructions.

In “the camels entered the water,” the camels are the subject. In “the owner brought the camels into the water,” another participant causes the entry.

Tashrīʿ also has a recorded early sense of watering animals at a place with direct access.

An old proverb calls this the easiest way of watering: the animals approach the water, so it need not be laboriously drawn from a well.

This attests a physical use of tashrīʿ associated with water. It does not, by itself, establish its historical priority over every religious usage.

The image does not imply that everything called sharīʿah is necessarily easy or effortless. Ease here concerns the particular watering place, not every derivative’s fixed meaning.

An open road, a forward-pointing spear, and visibility

Shāriʿ also denotes a road people can enter and travel along.

Ṭarīqun shāriʿun is an open, apparent, accessible road.

Bābun shāriʿun ilā al-ṭarīq describes a door opening onto a public road.

Openness and access are prominent here.

Ashraʿa al-rumḥa means bringing a spear forward or directing it toward something.

A warrior who brings a held spear forward toward an opponent, for example, extends it in that direction.

The relevant sense is extension or forward movement, rather than legislation or a watering place.

Shirāʿ, a ship’s sail, also belongs to this family: cloth spread and raised to catch the wind and move the vessel.

Shirʿah and related forms are also recorded for a bowstring. This is distinct from a prescribed religious way, although both are reported under the same lexical family.

Openness, extension, visibility, and forward movement provide recognizable semantic connections among these branches.

Those connections do not establish that pre-Islamic speakers understood every use through the watering-place image, or that every derivative arose simultaneously along one historical path.

From opening a way to prescribing one

Sharaʿa fī al-amr means entering into or beginning an undertaking.

The usage is associated with entering a course of action.

Beginning a task, for example, takes a person from intention or preparation into actual performance.

In religious contexts, sharaʿa has the more specific sense of prescribing a way to follow in practice.

The open-way image is relevant, but the meaning cannot be reduced to any path someone happens to choose toward any goal.

When The God prescribes a way in the Qur’an, its legitimacy is connected with divine permission and command.

Al-Shūrā 42:13 and Al-Shūrā 42:21 make the distinction clear: one describes divine prescription, the other censures prescribing religion without divine permission.

The religious meaning is therefore prescribing a way to follow in practice. Its legitimacy depends on the prescribing authority and the way’s content.

2. Important forms and derivatives

This Arabic family is extensive, but not every form occurs in the Qur’an.

Important forms include:

  • شَرَعَ / يَشْرَعُ: sharaʿa / yashraʿu; entered, opened a way, began, or prescribed a way. The Qur’an uses sharaʿa and sharaʿū.
  • شَرْع: sharʿ; opening or prescribing a way; religiously, a prescribed way or ruling.
  • شِرْعَة: shirʿah; an approach to water; religiously, a prescribed practical way. It occurs once in the Qur’an.
  • شَرِيعَة: sharīʿah; access to water; religiously, a prescribed way to follow. It also occurs once.
  • شَرَائِع: sharāʾiʿ; plural of sharīʿah, watering places or prescribed ways according to context.
  • شَارِع: shāriʿ; an open road, or something coming forward toward a destination.
  • شُرَّع: shurraʿ; a plural of shāriʿ, describing creatures coming visibly forward or within reach. The Qur’an uses it for fish.
  • شُرُوع: shurūʿ; entry or beginning.
  • أَشْرَعَ: ashraʿa; brought in, opened, or brought forward, as with animals and water or a spear and its target.
  • شَرَّعَ: sharraʿa; opened a way or made something openly accessible; also used for prescribing rulings.
  • تَشْرِيع: tashrīʿ; bringing animals to accessible water in an early physical usage; prescribing a way or ruling in religious and legal usage.
  • مَشْرَعَة: mashraʿah; a place of entry or access to water.
  • شِرَاع: shirāʿ; a sail; also, in some early usages, a taut bowstring.

In current religious and legal language, sharʿ often denotes religious laws collectively, and tashrīʿ commonly means legislation.

These usages are understandable through the idea of prescribing a way or ruling, but their modern scope should not be imposed on every Qur’anic derivative.

The five occurrences comprise four principal lexical forms: the verb sharaʿa, the nouns shirʿah and sharīʿah, and the plural description shurraʿ.

Sharaʿū in Al-Shūrā 42:21 is also Form I, not Form II. It is a plural past-tense verb, with wāw marking its plural subject.

3. Qur’anic usages

Al-Māʾidah 5:48: prescribed ways for different communities

After discussing the Torah and Gospel, the Qur’an addresses the Prophet:

وَأَنْزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ مُصَدِّقًا لِمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ مِنَ الْكِتَابِ وَمُهَيْمِنًا عَلَيْهِ

wa-anzalnā ilayka al-kitāba bi-l-ḥaqqi muṣaddiqan li-mā bayna yadayhi mina al-kitābi wa-muhayminan ʿalayh

Al-Māʾidah 5:48

“We sent down to you the Book in truth, confirming the Scripture before it and safeguarding it.”

The command to judge by what The God has revealed follows, and then:

لِكُلٍّ جَعَلْنَا مِنْكُمْ شِرْعَةً وَمِنْهَاجًا

li-kullin jaʿalnā minkum shirʿatan wa-minhājā

Al-Māʾidah 5:48

“For each of you We appointed a prescribed way and a clear path.”

Shirʿah names a way appointed for those addressed.

The preceding context is essential.

Al-Māʾidah 5:44 describes the Torah as containing guidance and light. Al-Māʾidah 5:46 says the same of the Gospel. Al-Māʾidah 5:48 then discusses the Book revealed to Muḥammad.

In this context, the phrase concerns different prescribed ways for communities addressed by revelation.

Minhāj, from N H J, means a clear path. Its combination with shirʿah emphasizes an evident way to proceed.

A distinction between prescription and clarity may be useful, but coordination alone does not establish that shirʿah always means a list of laws and minhāj only their implementation method.

Arabic can coordinate near-synonyms for complement or emphasis. Any asserted distinction requires independent usage evidence.

The verse continues:

وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَجَعَلَكُمْ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ فِي مَا آتَاكُمْ

wa-law shāʾa allāhu la-jaʿalakum ummatan wāḥidatan wa-lākin li-yabluwakum fī mā ātākum

Al-Māʾidah 5:48

“Had The God willed, He would have made you one community, but [He gave you these ways] to test you in what He has given you.”

This does not present every difference in prescribed ways as human error or departure from The God.

The Qur’an says that The God appointed a shirʿah and minhāj for the different addressees.

Differences in divinely prescribed ways therefore do not themselves establish a different deity, a different foundation of revelation, or one group’s misguidance.

This does not validate every custom people label religious law. The verse concerns ways The God appointed, not every later body of regulations established in religion’s name.

The verse then says:

فَاسْتَبِقُوا الْخَيْرَاتِ

fa-stabiqū al-khayrāt

Al-Māʾidah 5:48

“So compete in good deeds.”

The command occurs in the same discussion of differing prescribed ways.

The audience is called to excel in good deeds rather than turn practical differences into religious hostility and claims of superiority.

The conclusion states that all return to The God, who will inform them about their disagreements.

Religious identity alone therefore does not give a group ultimate authority to judge others.

Shirʿah here is an appointed practical way that can differ among communities without turning service to The God into mutually contradictory ultimate truths.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:163: fish coming visibly forward

وَاسْأَلْهُمْ عَنِ الْقَرْيَةِ الَّتِي كَانَتْ حَاضِرَةَ الْبَحْرِ إِذْ يَعْدُونَ فِي السَّبْتِ إِذْ تَأْتِيهِمْ حِيتَانُهُمْ يَوْمَ سَبْتِهِمْ شُرَّعًا وَيَوْمَ لَا يَسْبِتُونَ لَا تَأْتِيهِمْ كَذَٰلِكَ نَبْلُوهُمْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَفْسُقُونَ

wa-sʾalhum ʿani al-qaryati allatī kānat ḥāḍirata al-baḥri idh yaʿdūna fī al-sabti idh taʾtīhim ḥītānuhum yawma sabtihim shurraʿan wa-yawma lā yasbitūna lā taʾtīhim kadhālika nablūhum bi-mā kānū yafsuqūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:163

“Ask them about the town beside the sea, when they transgressed concerning the Sabbath: their fish came openly to them on their Sabbath, but did not come on days when they did not observe it. Thus We tested them because they were disobedient.”

This is especially significant lexically because it uses the root outside religious prescription.

Shurraʿan describes the fish’s condition: coming visibly within reach or appearing at the water’s surface.

Early explanations describe fish raising their heads or coming to the surface near the shore.

Other explanations mention lowering their heads to drink. These are inherited descriptions, not a zoological claim endorsed here; no exact head movement should be made essential to the root’s meaning.

The common feature is fish coming forward visibly within reach.

The verse says they came openly on the Sabbath but did not come on days when the people did not observe it.

The setting concerns a test and transgression of the Sabbath command.

Shurraʿan does not itself mean “numerous,” although the scene may suggest conspicuous presence. It describes their visible, accessible condition.

It must not be translated here as “according to religious law” or “legally.”

The word describes fish, not religious rulings.

Fish deep offshore may be unseen or difficult to reach; fish approaching the surface near shore become more visible and accessible.

This example illustrates the contrast conveyed by shurraʿan.

The continuation describes different responses to the test. Al-Aʿrāf 7:165 reports the rescue of those who forbade wrongdoing and the punishment of the wrongdoers.

The event thus belongs to a discussion of fidelity to divine command and responses to testing.

Shurraʿan itself still means visible approach, not disobedience, testing, or punishment. Those meanings come from other words and the narrative context.

Al-Shūrā 42:13: prescription and religion’s shared foundation

شَرَعَ لَكُمْ مِنَ الدِّينِ مَا وَصَّىٰ بِهِ نُوحًا وَالَّذِي أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ وَمَا وَصَّيْنَا بِهِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَمُوسَىٰ وَعِيسَىٰ أَنْ أَقِيمُوا الدِّينَ وَلَا تَتَفَرَّقُوا فِيهِ

sharaʿa lakum mina al-dīni mā waṣṣā bihi nūḥan wa-alladhī awḥaynā ilayka wa-mā waṣṣaynā bihi ibrāhīma wa-mūsā wa-ʿīsā an aqīmū al-dīna wa-lā tatafarraqū fīh

Al-Shūrā 42:13

“He prescribed for you of religion what He enjoined upon Noah, what We revealed to you, and what We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus: uphold the religion and do not divide within it.”

Sharaʿa is past tense, with The God as its understood subject.

Min al-dīn identifies the religious domain; mā waṣṣā bihi and the coordinated phrases specify what was prescribed.

The verse names Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus and refers to Muḥammad through the direct address “what We revealed to you.”

It speaks of the foundational connection between prophetic missions.

Upholding religion and avoiding division are presented as their shared charge.

This does not contradict Al-Māʾidah 5:48.

Al-Māʾidah discusses different prescribed ways for different audiences, while Al-Shūrā describes their common religious foundation.

The passages can thus be read as distinguishing religion’s foundation from prescribed practical ways.

The common foundation concerns serving The God, receiving guidance, and human responsibility. Practical prescriptions may include rules and forms differing with their recipients and circumstances.

For example, in Āl ʿImrān 3:50 Jesus says that he has come to permit some things previously forbidden to the Children of Israel.

That change does not mean that he introduced another deity or changed the foundation of serving The God.

Al-Anʿām 6:146 also describes particular dietary restrictions imposed on those who were Jews.

These examples show that different practical rulings need not imply different foundations of religion.

Al-Shūrā 42:13 commands:

أَنْ أَقِيمُوا الدِّينَ وَلَا تَتَفَرَّقُوا فِيهِ

an aqīmū al-dīna wa-lā tatafarraqū fīh

Al-Shūrā 42:13

“Uphold the religion and do not divide within it.”

Turning that shared foundation into mutually hostile, exclusive identities obscures the command to uphold religion without division.

A historical prescribed way—and still less one later religious school within it—should therefore not be equated with every meaning of religion.

Forqan’s article “Religion, Islam, and Shariah in the Qur’an” and its principle on the unity of religion and diversity of prescribed ways discuss this distinction further.

Al-Shūrā 42:21: prescription without divine permission

أَمْ لَهُمْ شُرَكَاءُ شَرَعُوا لَهُمْ مِنَ الدِّينِ مَا لَمْ يَأْذَنْ بِهِ اللَّهُ

am lahum shurakāʾu sharaʿū lahum mina al-dīni mā lam yaʾdhan bihi allāh

Al-Shūrā 42:21

“Or do they have partners who have prescribed for them as religion what The God has not permitted?”

This is an important occurrence of the root.

The God is the prescribing agent in Al-Shūrā 42:13. Al-Shūrā 42:21 describes partners prescribing religion without His permission.

The two verbs share a root and morphological form, but the actions differ in legitimacy.

That difference comes from the subject and divine authorization, not from the verb alone.

Sharaʿa therefore does not always mean prescribing a correct ruling. People can prescribe a religious way or rule without divine warrant.

The passage raises the question of religious authority.

Who may declare something obligatory or forbidden in The God’s name?

Who may impose their desire, interpretation, or law on others as a divine command?

Attributing something to The God without valid evidence does not make it divine merely because it is called religious.

Al-Naḥl 16:116 likewise states:

وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَا تَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُكُمُ الْكَذِبَ هَٰذَا حَلَالٌ وَهَٰذَا حَرَامٌ لِتَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ

wa-lā taqūlū li-mā taṣifu alsinatukumu al-kadhiba hādhā ḥalālun wa-hādhā ḥarāmun li-taftarū ʿalā allāhi al-kadhib

Al-Naḥl 16:116

“Do not say, through the falsehood your tongues describe, ‘This is lawful and this is forbidden,’ fabricating a lie against The God.”

The issue is claiming divine authority for lawful and forbidden categories, beyond merely holding an opinion or adopting social regulations.

A community may establish rules for traffic, health, or public administration without claiming that those rules are themselves divine commands.

Presenting that same social rule as a divine obligation or prohibition without Qur’anic warrant raises a different question.

Organizing public affairs differs from claiming to prescribe religion on The God’s behalf.

Al-Shūrā 42:21 censures religious prescription without divine authorization.

The principle is not confined to stone idols or openly worshiped deities. Claims by individuals or institutions to independent religious authority must also be assessed by it.

Not every interpretive disagreement is deliberate unauthorized prescription. Misunderstanding, defensible disagreement, and knowingly attributing an unsupported claim to The God are different.

Legitimacy in religious prescription rests on divine authorization, not personal fame, a tradition’s age, or institutional power.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:18: a prescribed way to follow

ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَاكَ عَلَىٰ شَرِيعَةٍ مِنَ الْأَمْرِ فَاتَّبِعْهَا وَلَا تَتَّبِعْ أَهْوَاءَ الَّذِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

thumma jaʿalnāka ʿalā sharīʿatin mina al-amri fa-ttabiʿhā wa-lā tattabiʿ ahwāʾa alladhīna lā yaʿlamūn

Al-Jāthiyah 45:18

“Then We placed you upon a prescribed way concerning the matter; follow it, and do not follow the desires of those who do not know.”

Sharīʿah denotes the way upon which The God placed the Prophet.

ʿAlā sharīʿatin fits the image of standing upon a path.

Fa-ttabiʿhā then commands him to follow that prescribed way.

Following the desires of those without knowledge is contrasted with it and prohibited.

This is not a path invented from the Prophet’s personal wishes. He is placed upon it and commanded to follow it.

The preceding verses reinforce the distinction.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:16 mentions the Book, judgment, and prophethood given to the Children of Israel. Al-Jāthiyah 45:17 describes disagreement after knowledge had reached them.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:18 then places the Prophet upon a prescribed way and commands him to follow it.

Here sharīʿah concerns divine prescription, rather than divisions arising from human desire or transgression.

In this religious context, sharīʿah means a prescribed way. The noun alone does not specify its full content.

Its commands and boundaries must be identified through revelation and related passages.

Equating sharīʿah simply with later fatwas, legal opinions, and religious rules erases a distinction: here it is the way The God appointed for the Prophet.

It should not automatically be identified with everything later compiled under that name.

4. Religion and prescribed ways: a shared foundation and practical differences

Al-Māʾidah 5:48, Al-Shūrā 42:13, and Al-Jāthiyah 45:18 illuminate an important distinction.

Al-Shūrā 42:13 describes the religion the prophets were charged to uphold.

Al-Māʾidah 5:48 describes different prescribed ways and clear paths for the recipients of revelation.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:18 places Muḥammad upon a prescribed way and commands him to follow it.

Religion and sharīʿah are related but should not be assumed completely synonymous in every context.

In contexts of service and obedience, religion concerns authority, allegiance, and accountability. A prescribed way concerns how that obedience is enacted.

An everyday comparison helps illustrate this distinction.

Two communities may uphold justice, trustworthiness, and service to The God while differing in some worship practices or dietary and ritual rules.

Such differences alone do not establish different deities or incompatible foundations of truth and justice.

The relevant question is whether the way was divinely prescribed and accords with clear divine principles, or was added to religion without authorization.

Diversity in divine prescriptions is therefore distinct from unauthorized prescription.

Differences between prescribed ways must also be distinguished from sectarian fragmentation.

Al-Anʿām 6:159 states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ فَرَّقُوا دِينَهُمْ وَكَانُوا شِيَعًا لَسْتَ مِنْهُمْ فِي شَيْءٍ

inna alladhīna farraqū dīnahum wa-kānū shiyaʿan lasta minhum fī shayʾ

Al-Anʿām 6:159

“Those who divided their religion and became factions—you have nothing to do with them.”

The verse censures dividing religion into separate factions.

Al-Māʾidah 5:48, however, concerns different ways appointed by The God for different audiences.

Divinely prescribed differences must not be equated with human sect-making.

Nor does diversity authorize each community to attribute whatever rules or prohibitions it wishes to The God.

Al-Shūrā 42:21 challenges precisely such unauthorized religious prescription.

Both principles must be retained: The God may prescribe different practical ways for different communities, but people may not present their own wishes as divine commands without His authorization.

5. Distinctions from related roots

SH R ʿ compared with N H J

Nahj concerns a clear, evident path.

Shirʿah and minhāj occur together in Al-Māʾidah 5:48.

Shirʿah concerns an open or prescribed way, while minhāj emphasizes a clear path of movement.

This must not become an absolute formula in which one means only law and the other only its implementation.

That distinction may be useful in some explanations, but coordination alone cannot establish it.

Both words here describe the way appointed for those addressed.

SH R ʿ compared with S B L

Sabīl denotes a way along which someone or something can move.

The Qur’an uses it for both right and wrong ways.

Sharīʿah in Qur’anic religious usage, however, is a prescribed way that is to be followed.

Not every sabīl is therefore a sharīʿah.

Someone may choose a route to personal advantage. It may be their sabīl or ṭarīq without thereby becoming a divinely prescribed way.

The relationship with the prescribing authority matters.

SH R ʿ compared with Ṭ R Q

Ṭarīq means a road or path, physically or figuratively.

An early meaning of sharīʿah is a particular approach to water.

Every sharīʿah in that usage provides access to water, but not every ṭarīq is such an approach.

Religiously, sharīʿah concerns a prescribed way to follow, while ṭarīq can denote a path or method generally.

This does not make ṭarīq invariably human and sharīʿah invariably divine. Each occurrence requires its own construction and context.

SH R ʿ compared with Ḥ K M

Ḥukm concerns judgment, decision, or command.

Religious prescription can include rulings and limits, but is not identical with ḥukm itself.

Al-Māʾidah 5:48 first commands judgment by revelation and then discusses shirʿah and minhāj.

Judgment and the prescribed way are connected, but one concerns judging or deciding, while the other concerns the framework of following a way.

Sharīʿah can include rulings without being reduced to judicial decisions or penalties.

SH R ʿ compared with D Y N

Dīn belongs to a separate family with Qur’anic usages concerning service, obedience, judgment, reckoning, and accountability.

The religious branch of SH R ʿ emphasizes prescribing a way of following and acting.

Al-Shūrā 42:13 places them together:

شَرَعَ لَكُمْ مِنَ الدِّينِ

sharaʿa lakum mina al-dīn

Al-Shūrā 42:13

“He prescribed for you of religion.”

They are not one word with two pronunciations, as the construction itself makes clear.

Read with Al-Shūrā 42:13, Al-Māʾidah 5:48 supports distinguishing practical diversity from an absolute difference in religion’s foundation.

Religion and prescribed ways are thus interrelated, while illuminating different aspects of humanity’s relationship with divine guidance and command.

SH R ʿ compared with SH R K

The roots occur together in Al-Shūrā 42:21:

شُرَكَاءُ شَرَعُوا لَهُمْ

shurakāʾu sharaʿū lahum

Al-Shūrā 42:21

“Partners who prescribed for them.”

Shurakāʾ comes from SH R K, while sharaʿū comes from SH R ʿ.

Their similar consonants must not cause confusion.

The verse concerns partners prescribing religion for others, connecting associated religious authorities with the act of prescription.

SH R ʿ does not itself mean associating partners. The God is also the subject of sharaʿa in Al-Shūrā 42:13.

Prescription’s legitimacy depends on authority and content, not the verb by itself.

SH R ʿ compared with SH R Ḥ and SH R Ṭ

SH R Ḥ concerns opening, spreading, and expanding the breast.

SH R Ṭ includes signs, stipulations, and conditions.

SH R ʿ concerns open ways, access, entry, and prescription.

Similar form does not establish identical histories or usages.

Explaining a law, stipulating a contractual condition, and prescribing a rule may feature in one legal discussion, but these Arabic words belong to separate families.

6. Morphology and translation proposals for Forqan

The five occurrences differ in morphology and syntax.

Al-Māʾidah 5:48

Shirʿatan is an indefinite singular noun in the accusative in jaʿalnā … shirʿatan wa-minhājan.

It denotes a prescribed way for those addressed.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:163

Shurraʿan is an accusative plural description functioning as a circumstantial qualifier of ḥītānuhum: the fish’s condition as they came.

Its meaning concerns coming openly forward or within reach.

Al-Shūrā 42:13

Sharaʿa is a Form I, third-person masculine singular past-tense verb. Its implicit subject refers to The God; mā waṣṣā bihi and the coordinated phrases specify what He prescribed.

Al-Shūrā 42:21

Sharaʿū is a Form I, third-person masculine plural past-tense verb. Its subject is the plural wāw referring to shurakāʾ.

Prescribing religion without divine authorization is censured here.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:18

Sharīʿatin is an indefinite singular noun in the genitive after ʿalā.

It denotes the way upon which the Prophet is placed and which he must follow.

Translation proposals

Sharaʿa: “opened,” “entered,” “began,” or, religiously, “prescribed.”

Sharʿ: “prescribed way” or “prescribed ways and rulings,” depending on usage.

Shirʿah: physically, an “approach to water” or “watering place”; religiously, a “prescribed way to follow in practice.”

Sharīʿah: physically, “access to water”; religiously, a “prescribed way,” or “shariah” with an explanation.

Shurraʿ: “coming openly forward,” “visible at the surface,” or “within reach,” according to its referent.

Shurūʿ: “beginning” or “entering.”

Ashraʿa: “brought forward,” “opened,” or “brought in.”

Mashraʿah: “watering place” or “approach to water.”

Shirāʿ: “sail”; in the bow-related usage, “bowstring.”

Tashrīʿ: physically, “bringing animals to accessible water”; religiously, “prescribing a way or ruling.”

Proposed translations of the five Qur’anic usages

Al-Māʾidah 5:48

لِكُلٍّ جَعَلْنَا مِنْكُمْ شِرْعَةً وَمِنْهَاجًا

li-kullin jaʿalnā minkum shirʿatan wa-minhājā

Al-Māʾidah 5:48

“For each of you We appointed a prescribed way and a clear path.”

Explaining shirʿah and minhāj in commentary is preferable to inserting elaborate legal terminology into the translation. These proposals do not alter the installed verse translations.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:163

إِذْ تَأْتِيهِمْ حِيتَانُهُمْ يَوْمَ سَبْتِهِمْ شُرَّعًا

idh taʾtīhim ḥītānuhum yawma sabtihim shurraʿā

Al-Aʿrāf 7:163

“When their fish came openly to them on their Sabbath.”

This conveys the fish’s visibility without adding a count, an exact direction, or unstated details.

Al-Shūrā 42:13

شَرَعَ لَكُمْ مِنَ الدِّينِ مَا وَصَّىٰ بِهِ نُوحًا

sharaʿa lakum mina al-dīni mā waṣṣā bihi nūḥā

Al-Shūrā 42:13

“He prescribed for you of religion what He enjoined upon Noah.”

“Prescribed” fits better than “began” or “opened” here because the subject is a religious way and divine instruction.

Al-Shūrā 42:21

أَمْ لَهُمْ شُرَكَاءُ شَرَعُوا لَهُمْ مِنَ الدِّينِ مَا لَمْ يَأْذَنْ بِهِ اللَّهُ

am lahum shurakāʾu sharaʿū lahum mina al-dīni mā lam yaʾdhan bihi allāh

Al-Shūrā 42:21

“Or do they have partners who have prescribed for them as religion what The God has not permitted?”

The verb again means prescribing, but the action’s legitimacy is denied.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:18

ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَاكَ عَلَىٰ شَرِيعَةٍ مِنَ الْأَمْرِ فَاتَّبِعْهَا

thumma jaʿalnāka ʿalā sharīʿatin mina al-amri fa-ttabiʿhā

Al-Jāthiyah 45:18

“Then We placed you upon a prescribed way concerning the matter; follow it.”

This preserves the image of being upon a way and following it.

A specialized discussion may retain “shariah,” but should define it, since current usage can suggest only the later body of jurisprudential rules.

7. Conclusion

Early Arabic associates SH R ʿ with an open way, entry, direct access, and coming forward.

A clear physical usage is sharīʿah, an approach to water. Early poetry uses sharāʾiʿ for watering places accessible to people and animals.

The family includes open roads, bringing a spear forward, sails, beginning an undertaking, and prescribing a way. Their semantic connections do not establish a single proven historical origin.

The root occurs five times in the Qur’an.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:163, shurraʿan describes fish coming visibly within reach.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:48, shirʿah denotes a way prescribed for different recipients of revelation.

Al-Shūrā 42:13 uses sharaʿa for divine religious prescription and emphasizes the prophets’ common foundational message.

Al-Shūrā 42:21 uses the same verb for prescribing religion without divine permission.

In Al-Jāthiyah 45:18, sharīʿah denotes the way The God appointed for the Prophet and commanded him to follow.

The root therefore cannot be reduced to legislation. Even religiously, sharīʿah is a prescribed practical way, not necessarily everything later collected in jurisprudence, institutions, and social traditions.

The related passages support distinguishing religion’s shared foundation from diverse prescribed ways. Practical differences do not imply different deities or contradictory truths, just as a shared foundation does not require identical practical rules.

A defensible central description is opening or providing access and entry, and, religiously, prescribing a way to follow in practice.

Sharīʿah in its Qur’anic occurrence is thus a divinely prescribed way whose legitimacy rests on divine command and authorization.

A humanly devised path does not automatically become divine law; an inherited ruling does not become divine merely through age; and every practical difference does not signify a different foundation of religion.

These distinctions help explain shirʿah, sharīʿah, and sharaʿa and assess what is presented today in the name of religion and prescribed law.
