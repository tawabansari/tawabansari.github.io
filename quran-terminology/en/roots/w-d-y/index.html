The Arabic root و د ي has several semantic branches, two of which occur in the Quran: وَادِي, a valley or natural watercourse, and دِيَة, blood money or financial compensation connected with killing.

Arabic lexicographical classification places these words in the same root family, but their meanings differ. One concerns geography and watercourses; the other concerns payment following a killing. A shared root does not establish that both developed from a single original image.

The root occurs twelve times in eleven Quranic verses: ten occurrences concern valleys and two concern compensation. The geographical uses include actual places, such as Moses' sacred valley and the ants' valley. One verse uses the valley figuratively to describe wandering in speech and conduct.

The two principal branches should therefore be examined independently before their roles in the verses are explained.

1. The root in early Arabic

A valley as a natural watercourse

In early Arabic, وَادِي denotes a natural depression or passage through which rainwater and floods can flow. It may lie between mountains, hills, or other elevations.

Permanent water is not required. Many Arabian wadis are dry during dry seasons and carry water only after rain.

One witness in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period appears in the collected poems of Imruʾ al-Qays:

وَوَادٍ كَجَوْفِ الْعَيْرِ قَفْرٍ قَطَعْتُهُ

wa-wādin ka-jawfi al-ʿayri qafrin qaṭaʿtuhu

بِهِ الذِّئْبُ يَعْوِي كَالْخَلِيعِ الْمُعَيَّلِ

bihi al-dhiʾbu yaʿwī ka-al-khalīʿi al-muʿayyali

“Many a barren, empty valley, like the hollow of the wild ass, have I crossed, where a wolf howls like an outcast burdened with dependents.” The comparison with the wild ass is one explanation of the transmitted image.

The poet describes crossing an empty, remote valley where a wolf's howl can be heard.

Different explanations of كَجَوْفِ الْعَيْرِ have been transmitted. The meaning of وَادٍ is nevertheless clear: a natural passage in the land that the poet crossed. الْخَلِيع refers to someone cast off by his people, and some commentators understand a gambler here; “distressed” alone does not convey this feature.

The line appears in the collection attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, although the attribution of some lines in this part of the ode is disputed. It is evidence from the tradition of pre-Islamic poetry, rather than a document with a certain composition date.

The important point is that this valley is dry and empty. The word is not restricted to a river or running water.

Arabic dictionaries also record وَدَى الشَّيْءُ for something flowing. Some lexicographers connect the name وَادِي with the water and floods flowing through it.

The connection is semantically plausible because a valley is a natural route for water. Its appearance in later dictionaries does not by itself establish the verb as the historical source of the noun.

The evidence more securely shows that وَادِي named a natural landform in early Arabic, with its role as a watercourse among its recognized features.

Compensation paid in connection with killing

The second branch is دِيَة.

وَدَى الْقَتِيلَ means paying compensation for a slain person. دِيَة is property paid to those entitled to it in connection with a killing.

Traditional morphological explanations connect دِيَة with وَدَى يَدِي and describe its ending as compensating for the omitted initial wāw. This grammatical analysis should not be presented as a conclusively documented historical sequence of word formation.

Although دِيَة begins visibly with dāl, this analysis assigns it to و د ي.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā clearly depicts payments made to end bloodshed and conflict between tribes:

تُعَفَّى الْكُلُومُ بِالْمِئِينَ فَأَصْبَحَتْ

tuʿaffā al-kulūmu bi-al-miʾīna fa-aṣbaḥat

يُنَجِّمُهَا مَنْ لَيْسَ فِيهَا بِمُجْرِمِ

yunajjimuhā man laysa fīhā bi-mujrimi

“The wounds are effaced by hundreds of camels; those who committed no crime in the affair now undertake their payment in installments.”

The line concerns efforts to end warfare and bloodshed between ʿAbs and Dhubyān.

The poet describes compensation for the losses caused by bloodshed, with individuals and groups accepting a substantial financial burden to establish peace.

The word دِيَة does not occur in this line. It is therefore not direct evidence for that lexical form, but a historical witness to financial compensation for bloodshed in pre-Islamic Arab society.

This distinction matters: evidence for a social practice does not by itself date the emergence of its name.

Nevertheless, the meaning of دِيَة is clear in Arabic sources, and its Quranic usage confirms the sense of financial payment connected with killing.

Do valley and compensation share an original meaning?

Some lexical explanations propose ideas such as flowing or issuing forth to connect the family's branches.

A valley carries water, and some uses of وَدَى concern flowing.

This does not establish that دِيَة was originally named after flowing blood or that paying compensation meant making wealth flow.

A historical claim of that kind needs independent evidence.

It is therefore preferable to distinguish the geographical and water-flow branch from financial compensation for killing.

2. Forms and semantic branches

The principal Quranic forms are:

وَادٍ: a valley or natural watercourse; a singular form whose final yā does not appear in some grammatical conditions.

وَادِي: the same singular noun, whose yā appears in forms such as وَادِيًا.

أَوْدِيَة: the plural of وَادِي; valleys and natural watercourses.

دِيَة: blood money or financial payment connected with killing.

أَوْدِيَة occurs twice, singular وَادِي in its various forms eight times, and دِيَة twice.

The written variants of singular وَادِي are not necessarily separate derivatives. Case, definiteness, and the construct state can affect its appearance; Quranic orthography also omits the final yā in some forms written with the definite article.

Non-Quranic forms

Arabic records other forms under this root as well.

One use of وَدَى means flowing.

Another form, أَوْدَى, means perishing or passing away; أَوْدَى بِهِ الْمَوْتُ means that death carried him away.

وَدْي or وَدِيّ names a bodily fluid discharged in certain physical circumstances.

Another branch uses وَدِيّ for young date-palm plants.

تَوْدِيَة names an implement used to bind a she-camel's teat and prevent her young from suckling.

These uses do not occur in the Quran. Similar forms or inclusion under one dictionary entry do not suffice to establish a single definition covering them all.

Semantic core

Two separate definitions are more accurate for the Quranic usages:

Valley: a natural depression or passage that can carry rainwater and floods, whether flowing with water at the time or dry.

Compensation: property or financial compensation paid to those entitled to it in connection with a killing.

These definitions explain their respective Quranic usages without requiring an artificial common core.

3. Actual valleys in the Quran

A. Al-Raʿd 13:17 — Floodwater flowing through valleys

أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَسَالَتْ أَوْدِيَةٌ بِقَدَرِهَا فَاحْتَمَلَ السَّيْلُ زَبَدًا رَابِيًا ۚ وَمِمَّا يُوقِدُونَ عَلَيْهِ فِي النَّارِ ابْتِغَاءَ حِلْيَةٍ أَوْ مَتَاعٍ زَبَدٌ مِثْلُهُ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْحَقَّ وَالْبَاطِلَ ۚ فَأَمَّا الزَّبَدُ فَيَذْهَبُ جُفَاءً ۖ وَأَمَّا مَا يَنْفَعُ النَّاسَ فَيَمْكُثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْأَمْثَالَ

anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-sālat awdiyatun bi-qadarihā fa-iḥtamala al-saylu zabadan rābiyan wa-mimmā yūqidūna ʿalayhi fī al-nāri ibtighāʾa ḥilyatin aw matāʿin zabadun mithluhu kadhālika yaḍribu allāhu al-ḥaqqa wa-al-bāṭila fa-ammā al-zabadu fa-yadhhabu jufāʾan wa-ammā mā yanfaʿu al-nāsa fa-yamkuthu fī al-arḍi kadhālika yaḍribu allāhu al-amthāl

Al-Raʿd 13:17

“He sent down water from the sky, and valleys flowed according to their capacity. The flood carried rising foam. From what they heat in the fire, seeking ornaments or useful goods, comes similar foam. Thus The God presents truth and falsehood in a likeness. The foam passes away as discarded matter, while what benefits people remains in the earth. Thus The God sets forth likenesses.”

أَوْدِيَةٌ is the plural of وَادِي.

فَسَالَتْ أَوْدِيَةٌ بِقَدَرِهَا says that the valleys flowed according to their measure.

Physically, water flows through a valley's bed, but the verse attributes flowing to the valleys themselves. Ordinary language can name a place for what occurs within it.

Similarly, “the river overflowed” refers to the increased flow of its water.

بِقَدَرِهَا matters too: the flow in each valley relates to its size and capacity.

The word وَادِي alone does not express capacity, moral worth, or human capacity for faith. Such features come from the complete phrase and its figurative use.

The Quran then connects flowing water and its foam with truth and falsehood.

Foam disappears, while what is beneficial remains in the earth.

There are consequently two levels: the actual image of water and valleys, and its figurative use to illuminate truth and falsehood.

The latter should not replace the lexical meaning of أَوْدِيَة. A valley remains a valley and natural watercourse, which the Quran employs within a wider likeness.

B. Al-Aḥqāf 46:24 — A cloud approaching the valleys of ʿĀd

فَلَمَّا رَأَوْهُ عَارِضًا مُسْتَقْبِلَ أَوْدِيَتِهِمْ قَالُوا هَٰذَا عَارِضٌ مُمْطِرُنَا ۚ بَلْ هُوَ مَا اسْتَعْجَلْتُمْ بِهِ ۖ رِيحٌ فِيهَا عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

fa-lammā raʾawhu ʿāriḍan mustaqbila awdiyatihim qālū hādhā ʿāriḍun mumṭirunā bal huwa mā istaʿjaltum bihi rīḥun fīhā ʿadhābun alīm

Al-Aḥqāf 46:24

“When they saw it as a cloud approaching their valleys, they said, ‘This is a cloud bringing us rain.’ Rather, it is what you sought to hasten: a wind containing painful punishment.”

أَوْدِيَتِهِمْ is the plural noun with the pronoun هُمْ: their valleys.

The people see a cloud approaching their valleys and expect rain.

This expectation fits the familiar role of valleys in receiving and conveying rainwater.

What arrives, however, is a punishing wind, contrary to their expectation.

The valley retains its geographical meaning. Hope for rain and flourishing land, or the consequences of punishment, arise from the narrative context.

A valley is thus more than a travel route: it can be part of a community's living environment and the place where water and rainfall are expected.

C. Ibrāhīm 14:37 — An uncultivated valley

رَبَّنَا إِنِّي أَسْكَنْتُ مِنْ ذُرِّيَّتِي بِوَادٍ غَيْرِ ذِي زَرْعٍ عِنْدَ بَيْتِكَ الْمُحَرَّمِ رَبَّنَا لِيُقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ فَاجْعَلْ أَفْئِدَةً مِنَ النَّاسِ تَهْوِي إِلَيْهِمْ وَارْزُقْهُمْ مِنَ الثَّمَرَاتِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَشْكُرُونَ

rabbanā innī askantu min dhurriyyatī bi-wādin ghayri dhī zarʿin ʿinda baytika al-muḥarrami rabbanā li-yuqīmū al-ṣalāta fa-ijʿal afʾidatan mina al-nāsi tahwī ilayhim wa-urzuqhum mina al-thamarāti laʿallahum yashkurūn

Ibrāhīm 14:37

“Our Lord, I have settled some of my descendants in an uncultivated valley beside Your sacred House. Our Lord, so that they may establish salat. Make some people's hearts incline toward them, and provide them with fruits, so that they may give thanks.”

بِوَادٍ means “in a valley.”

غَيْرِ ذِي زَرْعٍ separately describes the absence of cultivation.

The two components should not be conflated.

وَادِي does not itself mean uncultivated land. Some valleys have fertile soil, water, and vegetation.

The absence of cultivation is expressed separately here.

Rendering وَادِي as “a dry, barren desert” is therefore imprecise: much of that meaning comes from the qualification or a contextual inference, rather than the noun itself.

Its proximity to بَيْتِكَ الْمُحَرَّمِ is also a feature of this valley in Abraham's narrative, rather than part of the general definition.

D. Ṭā Hā 20:12 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:16 — The sacred valley

In Ṭā Hā 20:12, The God tells Moses to remove his sandals because he is in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:16 recalls Moses' summons using the same expression, الْوَادِ الْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى.

الْوَادِ means valley in both verses.

الْمُقَدَّسِ qualifies the valley, while طُوًى identifies the location in this construction.

Sacredness is therefore not part of the lexical meaning of valley. The verse describes this particular valley as sacred; the noun وَادِي itself does not determine why it is sacred.

The usage does not imply that every valley is sacred or that وَادِي means a place of revelation.

E. Al-Naml 27:18 — The ants' valley

In Al-Naml 27:18, Solomon's forces reach وَادِ النَّمْلِ, the ants' valley.

An ant then warns the others to enter their dwellings so that Solomon and his forces do not crush them.

The valley is the setting of the event and the ants' habitat.

Adding النَّمْلِ associates it with ants, but the phrase alone does not determine the valley's size, the number of ants, or their social organization.

The basic meaning remains a natural location in the land.

F. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 — The valley's bank

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 mentions شَاطِئِ الْوَادِ الْأَيْمَنِ, the right side or bank of the valley.

شَاطِئ concerns an edge or bank, and الْأَيْمَنِ specifies its direction.

The construction indicates that the valley has recognizable geographical sides and boundaries.

شَاطِئ should not lead us to assume that this valley necessarily contains a permanent river.

A dry valley can also have a defined bed, banks, and slopes.

The precise orientation of “right” must be examined through sentence structure and viewpoint, rather than converted without evidence into a fixed compass direction.

G. Al-Fajr 89:9 — Cutting rock in the valley

Al-Fajr 89:9 says of Thamūd:

وَثَمُودَ الَّذِينَ جَابُوا الصَّخْرَ بِالْوَادِ

wa-thamūda alladhīna jābū al-ṣakhra bi-al-wād

Al-Fajr 89:9

“And Thamūd, who cut the rock in the valley.”

الْوَادِ is the location of the rock-cutting.

جَابُوا expresses cutting or cleaving, and الصَّخْرَ is its object.

The stoneworking comes from the verb and object, rather than the meaning of valley.

This expression alone does not identify a city, archaeological site, or particular kind of building.

The valley is the natural location where the activity took place.

H. Al-Tawbah 9:121 — Crossing a valley

Al-Tawbah 9:121 speaks of small and large expenditures and crossing valleys in the course of the undertaking.

وَلَا يَقْطَعُونَ وَادِيًا means “nor do they cross any valley.”

وَادِيًا is the object of يَقْطَعُونَ.

In Arabic, “cutting a valley” means crossing or traversing it, rather than physically cutting or destroying the valley.

The context concerns recording the efforts and deeds of those engaged in the undertaking.

The valley is actual and geographical; the value and reward of crossing it come from the context and purpose of the mission.

4. Figurative use of the valley

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225 — Wandering in every valley

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّهُمْ فِي كُلِّ وَادٍ يَهِيمُونَ

a-lam tara annahum fī kulli wādin yahīmūn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225

“Do you not see that they wander in every valley?”

This follows a statement about a group of poets.

وَادٍ remains the geographical noun, but فِي كُلِّ وَادٍ يَهِيمُونَ is figurative in this context.

يَهِيمُونَ comes from ه ي م and concerns wandering or moving without direction.

Wandering through different valleys depicts scattered and unstable speech and conduct.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:224 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:226 also matter for understanding the image.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:224 speaks of poets followed by those who stray.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:226 describes people saying what they do not do.

Wandering in every valley stands between these descriptions and concerns aimless speech and the distance between words and deeds.

وَادِي itself does not mean false speech, lying, or instability.

Those meanings arise from the complete expression and context.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:227 makes an exception for those who have faith, do righteous deeds, remember The God often, and defend themselves after suffering injustice.

The figure should not therefore be understood as condemning every poet or the whole art of poetry.

A geographical word can serve ethical and social expression while retaining its original image.

5. Compensation in Al-Nisāʾ 4:92

The root's second Quranic branch occurs in Al-Nisāʾ 4:92.

وَمَا كَانَ لِمُؤْمِنٍ أَنْ يَقْتُلَ مُؤْمِنًا إِلَّا خَطَأً ۚ وَمَنْ قَتَلَ مُؤْمِنًا خَطَأً فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ وَدِيَةٌ مُسَلَّمَةٌ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ إِلَّا أَنْ يَصَّدَّقُوا ۚ فَإِنْ كَانَ مِنْ قَوْمٍ عَدُوٍّ لَكُمْ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ ۖ وَإِنْ كَانَ مِنْ قَوْمٍ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ مِيثَاقٌ فَدِيَةٌ مُسَلَّمَةٌ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ وَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ ۖ فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ شَهْرَيْنِ مُتَتَابِعَيْنِ تَوْبَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا

wa-mā kāna li-muʾminin an yaqtula muʾminan illā khaṭaʾan wa-man qatala muʾminan khaṭaʾan fa-taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminatin wa-diyatun musallamatun ilā ahlihi illā an yaṣṣaddaqū fa-in kāna min qawmin ʿaduwwin lakum wa-huwa muʾminun fa-taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminatin wa-in kāna min qawmin baynakum wa-baynahum mīthāqun fa-diyatun musallamatun ilā ahlihi wa-taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminatin fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu shahrayni mutatābiʿayni tawbatan mina allāhi wa-kāna allāhu ʿalīman ḥakīmā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92

“It is not for a believer to kill a believer except by mistake. Whoever kills a believer by mistake must free a believing enslaved person and deliver compensation to the deceased's family, unless they waive it. If the deceased belongs to a people hostile to you and is a believer, then a believing enslaved person must be freed. If the deceased belongs to a people with whom you have a covenant, compensation must be delivered to the family and a believing enslaved person freed. Whoever cannot find the means must fast for two consecutive months, as a turning in mercy from The God. The God is knowing and wise.”

The meaning of دِيَةٌ

Here دِيَة is property paid to the deceased's family in connection with accidental killing.

مُسَلَّمَةٌ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ identifies the recipients: the compensation must be delivered to the deceased's family.

It is consequently not merely a general fine payable to any institution. The verse expressly connects it with the family.

Nor should it be understood as the “price of a human life” in the sense of that life's true worth.

Money cannot restore the deceased to life or remove every human and emotional loss caused by the death.

Here it is a financial obligation connected with accidental killing, rather than a monetary assessment of a person's intrinsic worth.

Distinguishing compensation from other consequences

The same verse also mentions freeing a believing enslaved person.

The two obligations differ in their objects and functions.

The compensation is delivered to the deceased's family.

Freeing an enslaved person is a separate act mentioned in the same ruling.

The verse's entire ruling should not be reduced to compensation, nor should the two obligations be treated as lexically identical.

The word دِيَة alone does not establish a fixed amount, currency, payer in every circumstance, or the details of an entire legal system.

Those questions require separate consideration of all relevant evidence.

إِلَّا أَنْ يَصَّدَّقُوا — The possibility of waiving the right

Following the first requirement to pay compensation, the verse says:

إِلَّا أَنْ يَصَّدَّقُوا

illā an yaṣṣaddaqū

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92

That is, unless they waive their financial right.

The family may therefore forgo receiving the compensation.

The construction concerns a financial right that its holders can relinquish.

The possibility of waiver does not mean that the family must always forgo payment.

A right to waive is different from being compelled to waive.

Why does فَدِيَةٌ here not belong to ف د ي?

This is an important morphological distinction.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 first says:

وَدِيَةٌ مُسَلَّمَةٌ

wa-diyatun musallamah

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92

Later it says:

فَدِيَةٌ مُسَلَّمَةٌ

fa-diyatun musallamah

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92

In the first phrase, wāw is a conjunction followed by the noun دِيَة.

In the second, fā is a connecting particle introducing the consequence of the condition, followed by the same noun.

The second construction is therefore:

فَـ + دِيَةٌ

Not فِدْيَةٌ from ف د ي.

Without vowel marks, they can look similar, but they differ in structure and meaning.

دِيَة belongs to و د ي and here concerns compensation for a killing.

فِدْيَة belongs to ف د ي. Elsewhere in the Quran it concerns giving something for release, compensation, or substitution, as in Al-Baqarah 2:184 and Al-Baqarah 2:196.

Both instances of دِيَة in Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 must therefore be studied under و د ي.

Root identification cannot rely on visual similarity alone: attached particles, vowels, and morphological structure also matter.

The verse's different circumstances

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 distinguishes several circumstances.

It begins with a believer being killed by mistake.

It then addresses a deceased believer belonging to a people hostile to the addressees.

Next comes someone belonging to a people bound to them by a covenant.

The stated requirements are not formulated identically in these circumstances.

One part's ruling should not be extended to every other circumstance without attending to these differences.

The differences do not change the lexical meaning of دِيَة. Wherever it appears, it concerns payment to the deceased's family; its conditions and accompanying obligations come from the complete sentence.

6. Comparison with neighboring roots

وَادِي compared with نَهْر

نَهْر concerns a water channel or the recognized course of a river.

وَادِي denotes a natural passage or depression that may carry water.

They overlap in some circumstances but are not identical.

A valley can be dry, while a river is normally identified with flowing water.

Al-Raʿd 13:17 shows valleys carrying floodwater after rain.

Ibrāhīm 14:37 describes an uncultivated valley without asserting that it has a permanent river.

Translating every instance of وَادِي as “river” is therefore inaccurate.

وَادِي compared with سَيْل

س ي ل concerns the flowing of liquid.

Al-Raʿd 13:17 places أَوْدِيَة alongside السَّيْل.

أَوْدِيَة names the natural channels.

سَيْل denotes the flow of water or floodwater.

Their pairing distinguishes the place of flow from the flow itself.

The Quran can figuratively attribute flowing water's movement to a valley, but this does not make the valley lexically identical to the water.

وَادِي compared with شِعْب

In geographical usage, شِعْب can denote a cleft, passage, or route between mountains.

In that respect it overlaps with a valley.

وَادِي is particularly associated with a depression and watercourse, while شِعْب may emphasize the cleft or route between elevations.

This does not mean that every شِعْب is dry or every وَادِي contains water.

دِيَة compared with فِدْيَة

Some derivatives of these roots resemble one another in writing.

دِيَة in Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 denotes financial payment connected with killing.

Other Quranic uses of فِدْيَة concern something given for release, compensation, or substitution.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:184 discusses fidyah in connection with fasting.

Both concepts may involve payment or compensation, but they are not identical.

Compensation for killing should not be explained merely through a general definition of fidyah, nor should every unvocalized فدية be assigned to one root without checking vowels and structure.

دِيَة compared with قَتْل

ق ت ل concerns killing and ending life.

دِيَة is not the killing itself, but a financial payment linked to it by a particular ruling.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, قَتْل expresses the event and دِيَة part of the resulting obligation.

خَطَأً also specifies how the killing occurred.

Accidentality is therefore not intrinsic to the meaning of دِيَة; it is a condition stated in this verse.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the geographical forms, “valley” is the primary English equivalent, corresponding to the ordinary Persian term for a valley. The proposals below do not replace existing verse word boxes.

“Wadi” can also be retained where useful. In Persian it is familiar, although the ordinary Persian equivalent is clearer for a general readership.

In Al-Raʿd 13:17:

“The valleys flowed according to their capacity.”

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:24:

“They saw a cloud approaching their valleys.”

In Ibrāhīm 14:37:

“I have settled some of my descendants in an uncultivated valley.”

In Ṭā Hā 20:12 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:16:

“The sacred valley of Ṭuwā.”

In Al-Naml 27:18:

“The ants' valley.”

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30:

“The right bank of the valley.”

In Al-Fajr 89:9:

“Those who cut the rock in the valley.”

In Al-Tawbah 9:121:

“Nor do they cross any valley.”

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225:

“They wander in every valley.”

Retaining “wadi” in Persian is also suitable here because wandering in every wadi is a familiar figurative expression.

For the legal branch, “diyah” is an established and precise Persian equivalent; English can use “compensation” with its context made clear.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:92:

“And compensation delivered to his family.”

Or in a more fluent sentence:

“And payment of compensation to his family.”

“Blood money” can serve as a general explanation, while the established Persian term “diyah” more directly preserves the Quranic term.

An explanation should clarify that this is financial payment connected with killing, rather than a measure of a life's true worth or an automatic substitute for every other obligation in the verse.

8. Conclusion

و د ي has two distinct semantic branches in the Quran.

The first is وَادِي and its plural أَوْدِيَة: a valley or natural passage that can carry water and floods. Permanent rivers, vegetation, dryness, sacredness, and human habitation are not fixed components of the noun; they come from descriptions and context.

The Quran mentions valleys in several settings: flowing floods, uncultivated land, Moses' sacred valley, the ants' valley, rock-cutting, and human travel.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225 uses the same geographical image figuratively for wandering in speech and conduct.

The second branch is دِيَة, occurring twice in Al-Nisāʾ 4:92. It denotes payment to the deceased's family in the stated circumstances. فَدِيَةٌ combines the particle فَـ with دِيَة and must not be confused with فِدْيَة from ف د ي.

Lexical and historical evidence does not establish one certain original meaning for every branch. In particular, water flowing through valleys does not justify an unsupported account of the origin of دِيَة.

The Quranic definition has two parts:

Valley: a natural depression or passage that can carry water and floods.

Compensation: a financial payment prescribed in connection with killing, delivered to the deceased's family in Al-Nisāʾ 4:92.

This distinction preserves the valley's historical and geographical meaning while preventing confusion between diyah and fidyah or the insertion of unproven etymologies into Quranic meaning.
