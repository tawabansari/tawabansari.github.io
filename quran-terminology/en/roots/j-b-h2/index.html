In Arabic, جَبْهَة (jabhah) denotes the forehead: the upper front part of the face, turned toward another person in a face-to-face encounter. Other uses of this lexical family concern direct confrontation, receiving unwelcome speech, leadership, and groups of people.

جَبَهَهُ (jabahahu) can mean striking someone’s forehead. With unwelcome speech or conduct, it can denote confronting someone harshly and directly. جَبْهَةُ الْقَوْمِ (jabhatu al-qawmi) is also recorded for a prominent member or leader of a group; jabhah can denote a group of people or horses.

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as جِبَاهُهُمْ (jibāhuhum). In Al-Tawbah 9:35 it stands alongside sides and backs, referring to the foreheads of those whose conduct in the preceding verse includes hoarding gold and silver and not spending it in God’s way.

The Qur’anic meaning is therefore clear: foreheads. The family’s other uses nevertheless show how Arabic connects the front of the face with direct encounter and a prominent position.

1. Forms and the semantic core

The Qur’an uses J B H only in جِبَاهُهُمْ (jibāhuhum): the plural of jabhah with the pronoun hum, “their.”

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole occurrence in Al-Tawbah 9:35

No verb or other derivative of this root occurs in the Qur’anic text.

Important early Arabic forms include جَبْهَة (jabhah), جِبَاه (jibāh), جَبَهَ (jabaha), جَبْه (jabh), أَجْبَه (ajbah), جَبْهَاء (jabhāʾ), جَابِه (jābih), جَبِيهَة (jabīhah), and تَجْبِيه (tajbīh).

These forms occupy several related semantic fields.

Jabhah names the forehead, and jibāh is its plural. Ajbah describes someone with a broad or projecting forehead; jabhāʾ is the feminine form.

The verb jabaha is recorded for striking the forehead, confronting directly, and confronting someone with unwelcome speech or conduct. In some uses, jābih denotes one who faces something or comes across another’s path.

In collective uses, jabhah can denote a group of people or horses. It is also used for a prominent member or leader of a group.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris treats the family as one lexical item extended through resemblance and transferred usage. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains jabhah as the part of the head used in prostration and then records other uses.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: one lexical item and extensions through resemblance

On this evidence, the clear physical meaning is the forehead and front of the face. Direct encounter and confrontation can explain connections between several extended uses, but should not replace the actual forehead meaning in the Qur’an. Brief root meaning: the forehead and front of the face; in extended uses: direct encounter, confrontation, and a prominent position.

2. The forehead in early Arabic

Jabhah: the front of the face

Lisān al-ʿArab describes the forehead as مُسْتَوَى مَا بَيْنَ الْحَاجِبَيْنِ إِلَى النَّاصِيَةِ (mustawā mā bayna al-ḥājibayni ilā al-nāṣiyati): the front part of the head extending from the region between the eyebrows to the hairline at the front of the head.

Another explanation calls the forehead the “place of prostration,” because this part of the head touches the ground during prostration.

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: place of prostration and other uses

“Place of prostration” is not, however, the exclusive or fundamental definition of every use of jabhah. The forehead is part of a person’s face whether they are prostrating, standing, sitting, or facing someone else.

Arabic also uses jabhah for a horse. Its forehead is the region at the front of its head, above the eyes and below the ears.

This usage shows that the word concerns a particular physical region; its definition cannot be confined to a human devotional posture.

The distinction between jabhah and jabīn

Arabic records a distinction between jabhah and jabīn.

Jabhah denotes the upper front, central part of the face. In more precise lexical usage, jabīn denotes one of the two sides of the forehead.

A person has two jabīns: one to the right and one to the left of the central forehead.

This distinction also matters in the two Qur’anic uses. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:103 uses الْجَبِينِ (al-jabīni) in the account of Abraham and his son, whereas Al-Tawbah 9:35 uses جِبَاهُهُمْ (jibāhuhum).

In modern Persian, jabīn commonly also means the forehead. This Persian usage should not obscure the distinction recorded between the Arabic words.

In precise Arabic translation, jabhah can therefore be rendered “forehead” and jabīn “the side of the forehead.”

A broad or projecting forehead

أَجْبَه (ajbah) describes someone with a broad, large, or projecting forehead. Its feminine is جَبْهَاء (jabhāʾ).

For example, رَجُلٌ أَجْبَهُ (rajulun ajbahu) describes a man with a broad, conspicuous forehead.

Lexical sources also record فَرَسٌ أَجْبَهُ (farasun ajbahu), a horse whose forehead physically projects.

These uses show that J B H also describes the forehead’s shape, size, and prominence.

Breadth or projection is not, however, essential to the meaning of jabhah. A person’s forehead can be broad or narrow; in either case it remains a jabhah.

Lisān al-ʿArab: forehead, ajbah, and jabhāʾ

A witness from early poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays describes a horse as follows:

«لَهَا جَبْهَةٌ كَسَرَاةِ الْمِجَنِّ
حَذَّقَهُ الصَّانِعُ الْمُقْتَدِرُ»

lahā jabhatun ka-sarāti al-mijanni / ḥadhdhaqahu al-ṣāniʿu al-muqtadiru

That is: “Her forehead is like the raised back of a shield, skillfully fashioned and finished by an able craftsman.”

This image compares the horse’s forehead to the raised, well-made back of a shield.

The poet describes the forehead’s shape and prominence, rather than leadership or confrontation. The witness therefore matters for the word’s physical, descriptive meaning.

The verse appears among poems attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet alone does not conclusively establish this particular verse’s date or authenticity, so it should be treated as attributed evidence. Ibn al-Sīd al-Baṭalyawsī, in Al-Iqtiḍāb, identifies a disputed attribution and, citing Al-Aṣmaʿī through Abū ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAlāʾ, prefers Rabīʿah ibn Jusham of Al-Namir ibn Qāsiṭ. His account explains حَذَّقَهُ (ḥadhdhaqahu) as skillful finishing; Asās al-Balāghah also records the variant حَذَّفَهُ (ḥadhdhafahu).

Ibn al-Sīd al-Baṭalyawsī, Al-Iqtiḍāb: disputed attribution and explanation

3. From the forehead to direct encounter and confrontation

Al-Zamakhsharī, Asās al-Balāghah, Ḥ DH F: variant ḥadhdhafahu

Striking the forehead

Arabic uses جَبَهَهُ جَبْهًا (jabahahu jabhan) for striking someone’s forehead.

Here the verb’s relation to the noun jabhah is direct and clear: the body-part name supplies a verb concerning contact with that part.

The verb’s use is not limited to physical contact.

Lexical sources record:

«جَبَهْتُ فُلَانًا بِالْمَكْرُوهِ»

jabahtu fulānan bi-al-makrūhi

That is: “I confronted so-and-so directly with something unwelcome.”

جَبَهْتُهُ بِالْكَلَامِ (jabahtuhu bi-al-kalāmi) is likewise recorded for confronting someone with harsh or unwelcome speech.

The blow need not be physical in these uses. Someone may confront another with speech, a harsh reply, or humiliating conduct.

This allows us to recognize a connection between the forehead as the front of the face and directly facing another person.

The concept of confrontation is also expressed through a different lexical family, however; not every word concerning face-to-face encounter derives from J B H.

Turning away someone seeking help

Lexical sources also use جَبَهَ الرَّجُلَ (jabaha al-rajula) for refusing someone’s request or confronting them with an unwelcome response.

In this use, someone approaches to have a need met but encounters conduct that sends them away disappointed or ashamed.

The important feature is a direct, unwelcome encounter.

Not every negative reply or refusal should, however, be called jabh without regard to context. The recorded use also involves unpleasantness and sometimes harsh conduct.

This branch includes terms in the fields of humiliation, shame, and encountering unwelcome treatment.

These are extensions of the lexical family. In Al-Tawbah 9:35, however, jibāh does not mean shame or refusing a request; it remains a body-part noun.

Reaching water without equipment

One notable early Arabic expression is:

«جَبَهْنَا الْمَاءَ»

jabahnā al-māʾa

It describes people reaching water without equipment for drawing it.

Ibn Fāris explains that they faced the water without the tools needed to make use of it.

Here jabaha does not directly mean a blow or a forehead. It concerns reaching and encountering something in circumstances that prevent practical access to it.

Lexical sources also record جَبَهَنَا الشِّتَاءُ (jabahanā al-shitāʾu): winter came upon us when we had not prepared for it.

These uses show that encounter in this family is not restricted to one person facing another. A person may face circumstances or a phenomenon for which they are unprepared.

Unpreparedness should not, however, be treated as the root’s primary meaning. It comes from the situations in which the verb is used.

Asās al-Balāghah and Lisān: encountering water and winter

4. Jabhah as a group, leadership, and prominence

The leader of a group

Arabic uses the expression:

«جَبْهَةُ الْقَوْمِ»

jabhatu al-qawmi

It can denote a prominent member or leader of a group.

Lisān al-ʿArab records “the jabhah of a people” for their chief or leader.

Its imagery is close to wajh al-qawm: someone with a prominent position within a group or known as its representative.

Just as the forehead occupies the front, visible part of the head, a group’s prominent member may figuratively be called its forehead.

This image explains a usage; it does not mean that every forehead inherently signifies leadership in Arabic.

A group of people

Jabhah is also recorded for a group of people.

For example, جَاءَتْنَا جَبْهَةٌ مِنَ النَّاسِ (jāʾatnā jabhatun mina al-nāsi) means that a group of people came to us.

Here jabhah is neither a body part nor necessarily one particular person, but a group of human beings.

A group’s leader and the group itself differ in meaning. Collective uses of jabhah therefore cannot all be reduced to leadership.

Early Arabic also applies jabhah to horses.

Sometimes it denotes a group of horses; some lexical explanations concern outstanding, select horses.

In Al-Zamakhsharī’s Asās al-Balāghah, an expression concerning جَبْهَةُ الْخَيْلِ (jabhatu al-khayli) is explained through select horses.

A poem attributed to a member of Banū Fazārah also reads:

«وَلَّيْتُ جَبْهَةَ خَيْلِي شَطْرَ خَيْلِهِمْ
وَوَاجَهُونَا بِأُسْدٍ قَابَلُوا أُسُدَا»

wallaytu jabhata khaylī shaṭra khaylihim / wa-wājahūnā bi-usdin qābalū usudā

That is: “I directed the foremost group of my horses toward theirs; they faced us with lionhearted men, brave men confronting brave men.”

In this verse, جَبْهَةَ خَيْلِي (jabhata khaylī) concerns a group of horses deployed in confrontation and battle.

The use matters for understanding jabhah in relation to a group and its leading part. Because the poet’s precise identity and date are unknown, however, the verse should not be presented as definite pre-Islamic evidence.

Al-Zamakhsharī, Asās: verse attributed to a member of Banū Fazārah

Jabhah in names for celestial features

Arabic sources also record جَبْهَةُ الْأَسَدِ (jabhatu al-asadi), “the lion’s forehead.” This name concerned a group of stars in Leo and was one of the familiar lunar mansions in traditional astronomy.

Here jabhah names the region of the constellation associated with the lion’s forehead in its traditional depiction.

An anonymous poem transmitted by Al-Aṣmaʿī also uses جَبْهَةُ الْقُمَيْرِ (jabhatu al-qumayri) for the visible face of the moon.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān: the lion’s forehead and the moon witness

These examples show that the forehead’s name can be applied to the front or prominent part of a celestial image.

Naming stars or constellations does not change jabhah’s Qur’anic meaning. Nothing in Al-Tawbah 9:35 indicates that jibāhuhum concerns stars, a celestial position, or a leading group.

5. Qur’anic use: foreheads in Al-Tawbah 9:35

يَوْمَ يُحْمَىٰ عَلَيْهَا فِي نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ فَتُكْوَىٰ بِهَا جِبَاهُهُمْ وَجُنُوبُهُمْ وَظُهُورُهُمْ ۖ هَٰذَا مَا كَنَزْتُمْ لِأَنْفُسِكُمْ فَذُوقُوا مَا كُنْتُمْ تَكْنِزُونَ

yawma yuḥmā ʿalayhā fī nāri jahannama fa-tukwā bihā jibāhuhum wa-junūbuhum wa-ẓuhūruhum hādhā mā kanaztum li-anfusikum fa-dhūqū mā kuntum taknizūna

“On the day it is heated in the fire of Hell and their foreheads, sides, and backs are branded with it: ‘This is what you hoarded for yourselves, so taste what you used to hoard.’” (Al-Tawbah 9:35)

This continues Al-Tawbah 9:34, which concerns people hoarding gold and silver and not spending it in God’s way.

Al-Tawbah 9:35 therefore describes a scene showing the consequences of that conduct.

The forehead alongside the side and back

The verse names three parts of the body: جِبَاهُهُمْ (jibāhuhum), جُنُوبُهُمْ (junūbuhum), and ظُهُورُهُمْ (ẓuhūruhum).

Jibāh is the plural of jabhah and means foreheads. Junūb is the plural of janb and means sides or flanks. Ẓuhūr is the plural of ẓahr and means backs.

Study of J N B; Study of Ẓ H R

All three nouns refer to particular physical regions.

The sequence moves from the front of the head to the sides and then the back of the body.

فَتُكْوَىٰ (fa-tukwā), from K W Y, concerns branding or burning with something hot.

Study of K W Y

Syntactically, jibāhuhum is the grammatical subject of the passive verb tukwā; junūbuhum and ẓuhūruhum are coordinated with it.

The sentence therefore describes branding these three parts of the body.

Jabhah’s meaning here is clearly physical. The family’s figurative Arabic uses do not justify replacing foreheads with leaders, groups, or social positions without evidence.

Why the forehead, side, and back?

This question may arise in interpreting the verse, but its answer must be distinguished from jabhah’s lexical meaning.

Some exegetical explanations connect the forehead with ostentation or pride, the side with turning away, and the back with turning one’s back on people in need.

Such readings can be examined as figurative interpretations, since the Qur’an elsewhere connects outward conduct with inward or ethical states.

Al-Tawbah 9:35 itself nevertheless does not provide this explanation for each body part.

The verse does not say that the forehead was chosen for pride, the side for turning away, or the back for refusing help.

These correspondences should therefore not be presented as the words’ certain meanings.

What the text clearly provides is the naming of three body parts and their branding. Connecting each one to a particular sin requires independent evidence and argument.

Al-Qurṭubī: exegetical explanations of the three body regions

Hoarding wealth and branding

Al-Tawbah 9:34 concerns hoarding gold and silver and not spending it in God’s way. Al-Tawbah 9:35 says that what was hoarded is heated and used to brand their bodies.

In فَتُكْوَىٰ بِهَا (fa-tukwā bihā), bihā refers back to what was hoarded in the preceding verse.

The verse’s closing phrase, هَٰذَا مَا كَنَزْتُمْ لِأَنْفُسِكُمْ (hādhā mā kanaztum li-anfusikum), emphasizes the same connection.

There is therefore a clear relation between conduct involving wealth and the punishment scene.

What someone hoarded for themselves becomes the instrument of their punishment in this scene.

This connection comes from the structure of Al-Tawbah 9:34 and Al-Tawbah 9:35, rather than from J B H alone.

Nor can these verses, without a fuller examination of their context and related verses, establish that every form of keeping wealth, saving, or economic reserve is forbidden. The censured conduct is presented in connection with hoarding gold and silver and not spending it in God’s way.

Determining the scope of its economic and religious ruling requires a separate study of kanz, spending, and financial obligations.

Al-Ṭabarī: hoarded gold and silver and financial obligations

Study of K N Z

Is the forehead a symbol of personality here?

In different languages, the forehead can be associated with the visible face, reputation, shame, or social standing.

Such usages can be studied linguistically. In Al-Tawbah, however, the forehead stands alongside two other body regions, and tukwā concerns branding.

This combination supports a physical reading of the word.

Any figurative reflection on reputation, standing, or the ethical consequences of hoarding wealth should be presented separately.

Even if such a reflection is accepted, jibāhuhum still means “their foreheads” at the basic lexical level.

6. Distinctions from related roots and translation proposals

J B H (jabhah) compared with J B N (jabīn)

Jabhah and jabīn are related in sense but belong to two distinct roots.

Jabhah primarily denotes the front, central upper part of the face, whereas jabīn denotes one of its two sides.

The Qur’an uses jibāhuhum in Al-Tawbah 9:35 and al-jabīn in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:103.

The distinction matters in translation: “forehead” suits jabhah, while “the side of the forehead” is more precise for jabīn.

جُبْن (jubn), “cowardice,” also belongs to J B N and should not be confused with jabhah.

Study of J B N

J B H (jabhah) compared with W J H (wajh)

وَجْه (wajh) concerns the human face and has broader uses involving direction, turning toward something, and face-to-face encounter.

Physically, jabhah is a particular region of the head and face, whereas wajh can denote the whole face.

The two families come close in some figurative uses. Wajh al-qawm and jabhatu al-qawm, for example, can both denote a prominent member of a group.

They are nevertheless not interchangeable in every construction or use.

Jibāhuhum in Al-Tawbah 9:35 does not mean “their faces”; it names the particular region of the forehead.

Study of W J H

J B H (jabhah) compared with N Ṣ W (nāṣiyah)

نَاصِيَة (nāṣiyah) concerns the front of the head and the forelock. Lexical sources examine this family under N Ṣ W and N Ṣ Y; Forqan’s installed root page is indexed under N Ṣ Y.

Lexical sources: nāṣiyah under N Ṣ W

The Qur’an uses this word in Hūd 11:56, Al-ʿAlaq 96:15, and Al-ʿAlaq 96:16.

Nāṣiyah and jabhah are physically close. Nāṣiyah can emphasize the forelock and the place by which it is grasped, whereas jabhah names the forehead region.

Not every occurrence of nāṣiyah should therefore be translated simply as “forehead,” nor every jabhah as “forelock.”

Study of N Ṣ Y

Translation proposals for Forqan

“Their foreheads” is a natural, precise rendering of Qur’anic jibāhuhum.

The existing Persian Forqan translation of Al-Tawbah 9:35 uses this equivalent; rendered here in English, it reads:

“On the day it is heated in the fire of Hell and their foreheads, sides, and backs are branded with it: ‘This is what you hoarded for yourselves, so taste what you used to hoard.’”

This translation should be preserved: it conveys the direct meanings of the three body parts without adding an exegetical explanation.

A brief word explanation may read:

“Jabhah: the forehead; the upper front part of the face.”

For its particular meaning in the verse:

“Jibāhuhum: their foreheads; one of the three bodily regions that the verse describes as being branded.”

Other uses, including direct confrontation, leadership, and groups, can be examined in the full root study. They should not be added to the verse’s basic translation.

Conclusion

In Arabic, J B H concerns the forehead and front of the face. Uses involving striking the forehead, confronting another directly, and encountering unwelcome speech or circumstances are also known in relation to this physical meaning.

Early Arabic records jabhah not only for the body part but also for a prominent person or leader, a group of people, horses, and certain astronomical names.

These uses can be related to direct encounter and prominence through the forehead’s position as the visible front of the face. This semantic connection should nevertheless not become a universal, certain etymological theory.

Only jibāhuhum occurs in the Qur’an, in Al-Tawbah 9:35. Alongside sides and backs, it denotes a real body region in a scene showing the consequences of hoarding wealth and not spending it in God’s way.

The direct, defensible meaning of Qur’anic jabhah is the forehead; the Arabic lexical family J B H also includes a broader range of direct encounter, confrontation, prominence, and leadership.

These meanings matter for understanding Arabic’s history and expressive range. None, however, justifies turning jibāhuhum in the verse’s translation into reputation, arrogance, leaders, or human groups. Such readings, if proposed, should be examined as reflection or interpretation with independent arguments, separately from the lexical meaning.
