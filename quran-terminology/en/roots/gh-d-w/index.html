Morning, the beginning of the day, morning departure, and tomorrow

The Arabic root gh d w is associated with the beginning of the day, morning, and movement during its first part. Within this family, ghudūw means morning or going out then; ghadāh and ghudwah name the early part of the day; ghadā means to set out in the morning; and ghad means the day after today. In early usage, ghadāʾ also names the meal eaten at the beginning of the day.

This word family has two related directions of meaning: the beginning of the day and activities undertaken then; and the coming day, called ghad in Arabic.

The root appears in the Quran in five main forms: the verb ghadā and the nouns ghudūw, ghadāh, ghadāʾ, and ghad. Together they occur sixteen times. Their uses encompass morning departure, remembering The God at the beginning of the day, food, the near future, and the eventual consequences of human actions.

Although these words belong to one root family, their meanings differ. Ghudūw is morning, ghad is tomorrow, and ghadāʾ is a meal whose name is associated with when it is eaten. Translation and analysis must preserve these distinctions.

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic uses ghudwah and ghadāh for the beginning of the day and the morning. Some lexical definitions restrict this period to the interval between dawn becoming light and sunrise, although broader usage can encompass the early part of the day.

In its primary use, ghadā yaghdū refers to going or setting out in the morning. It can describe someone leaving home early, setting out to hunt, going to pasture, or beginning a journey.

This use reflects a natural division of time in everyday Arab life. In a society where travel, hunting, herding, and movement depended on weather and daylight, a morning departure had practical importance.

In contrast to ghudūw, rawāḥ was used for going or moving during the later part of the day. This contrast appears in Arabic poetry and in the Quran.

Arabic also uses ghādiyah for a cloud appearing in the morning or rain falling then.

The common element is the beginning of the day, but each derivative serves a different function: one names a time, another movement at that time, and another a natural occurrence in the morning.

A witness from poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period: Imruʾ al-Qays’s morning departure

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

وَقَدْ أَغْتَدِي وَالطَّيْرُ فِي وُكُنَاتِهَا

wa-qad aghtadī wa-l-ṭayru fī wukunātihā

بِمُنْجَرِدٍ قَيْدِ الْأَوَابِدِ هَيْكَلِ

bi-munjaridin qaydi al-awābidi haykali

“Often I set out in the morning, while the birds are still in their nests, on a large, swift horse that seems to hold wild animals captive.”

Here aghtadī belongs to gh d w and refers to setting out at the beginning of the day.

The poet indicates the time through the birds’ condition: they are still in their nests and have not begun their daily movement.

The image makes the early departure clear. Aghtadī here is more than simply going: its morning timing matters.

This is a familiar witness from poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, transmitted in early versions of Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah. As with other poetry transmitted from that period, however, attribution of the poem must be distinguished from the precise dating of each verse.

Another witness: ghudwah in Labīd’s poetry

A poem attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah includes:

فَقَدَّرْتُ لِلْوِرْدِ الْمُغَلِّسِ غُدْوَةً

fa-qaddartu li-l-wirdi al-mughallisi ghudwatan

فَوَرَدْتُ قَبْلَ تَبَيُّنِ الْأَلْوَانِ

fa-waradtu qabla tabayyuni al-alwāni

“I set a time for reaching the watering place in the darkness of early morning, and arrived before the colours could be distinguished.”

Ghudwah names the early part of the day here, alongside mughallis, which refers to movement in darkness before the light is fully established.

The distinction matters: ghudwah names the morning period, whereas mughallis emphasizes its darkness.

Labīd lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating this particular verse, it cannot be treated as a definitively pre-Quranic witness. It nevertheless illustrates a familiar use of ghudwah in the early Arabic poetic tradition.

Extension of morning departure to going more generally

Lexicons also record a broader use of ghadā for going or moving, even when the precise morning time is not the main point.

Such extension is understandable: a verb originally used for going at a particular time may also come to denote the movement itself.

This broader use should not be imposed on every Quranic occurrence. In the Quran, temporal clues show that the morning sense remains present in the verb ghadā.

2. Main senses and derivatives

A. Ghudūw: morning and the beginning of daily movement

Ghudūw can refer to the beginning of the day or movement during that period.

When contrasted with ʿashiyy or āṣāl, its temporal meaning is prominent: morning as opposed to the later part of the day.

When used of a person’s departure or the wind’s movement, however, it can express morning movement.

The time itself must therefore be distinguished from an action undertaken then.

B. Ghadāh and ghudwah: the early part of the day

Ghadāh and ghudwah are closely related terms for morning.

Lexicons use both for the beginning of the day. Some grammatical differences are discussed, including the definiteness of ghudwah in certain constructions, but these do not alter the basic morning sense.

The Quran’s two occurrences written بِالْغَدَوٰةِ are pronounced bi-l-ghadāti in the familiar reading of Ḥafṣ. Ibn ʿĀmir’s reading has bi-l-ghudwati.

Both refer to the beginning of the day. This reading difference does not by itself create two religious concepts or two wholly different times.

C. Ghadāʾ: the early-day meal

In early Arabic, ghadāʾ names the meal eaten at the beginning of the day.

Its name is connected with its timing, just as ʿashāʾ is connected with the later part of the day.

Modern Arabic commonly uses ghadāʾ for the midday meal, but that present-day usage should not displace the word’s older meaning in the Quran.

The Quran uses ghadāʾanā in the story of Moses for the food he and his companion wanted to eat on their journey.

D. Ghad: tomorrow and future time

Ghad means the day after today.

Lexicons give ghadw as its underlying form and explain that the wāw has been omitted in the shortened form ghad.

Alongside the specific meaning “tomorrow,” Arabic can use ghad for a future that has not yet arrived.

For example, someone might say of a result, “It will become clear tomorrow,” without necessarily meaning twenty-four hours later: the reference may be to a future time when the outcome becomes apparent.

This extended use matters for some Quranic verses. It does not warrant detaching ghad from ordinary “tomorrow” in every verse.

3. Quranic forms of the root

The Quran contains five main categories of derivatives from gh d w:

  • Ghadā: the verb “to go in the morning,” occurring as ghadawta, ughdū, and ghadaw.
  • Ghudūw: morning or morning movement, occurring as bi-l-ghudūw, ghudūwuhā, and ghudūwan.
  • Ghadāh: the beginning of the day, in the two occurrences written بِالْغَدَوٰةِ.
  • Ghadāʾ: a meal, occurring as ghadāʾanā.
  • Ghad: tomorrow, occurring as ghadan and li-ghadin.

Ghādiyah, ightadā, ghudwah in lexical uses outside the Quranic forms just described, and other derivatives are also known in Arabic. They should not all be presented as separate forms occurring in the Quran.

Prefixes, attached pronouns, and differences in case endings do not by themselves constitute independent derivatives.

4. Ghadā: setting out at the beginning of the day

Āl ʿImrān 3:121: Muhammad’s morning departure

وَإِذْ غَدَوْتَ مِنْ أَهْلِكَ تُبَوِّئُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ مَقَاعِدَ لِلْقِتَالِ وَاللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

wa-idh ghadawta min ahlika tubawwiʾu al-muʾminīna maqāʿida li-l-qitāli wa-llāhu samīʿun ʿalīm

Āl ʿImrān 3:121

“And when you left your family in the morning, assigning the believers positions for battle; and The God is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”

Ghadawta is a past-tense form of ghadā, addressed to Muhammad.

The verse contains two distinct matters: leaving at the beginning of the day, and assigning the believers positions for battle.

Ghadawta itself does not mean preparing an army or commanding a battle. Those activities are understood from the rest of the sentence, especially “assigning the believers positions for battle.”

The contribution of gh d w here is morning movement and departure.

Al-Qalam 68:22: the garden owners’ morning departure

أَنِ اغْدُوا عَلَىٰ حَرْثِكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَارِمِينَ

ani ughdū ʿalā ḥarthikum in kuntum ṣārimīn

Al-Qalam 68:22

“Go early to your field, if you intend to harvest.”

Ughdū is the plural imperative of ghadā.

The garden owners urge one another to set out in the morning to gather their crop.

The verb’s meaning is clear: set out at the beginning of the day.

The intention to harvest comes from ḥarthikum, ṣārimīn, and the narrative context, rather than from gh d w itself.

Al-Qalam 68:25

وَغَدَوْا عَلَىٰ حَرْدٍ قَادِرِينَ

wa-ghadaw ʿalā ḥardin qādirīn

Al-Qalam 68:25

“And they set out in the morning, resolved on their purpose and considering themselves capable.”

Ghadaw here is a plural past-tense verb.

Al-Qalam 68:22 gives the instruction to set out in the morning; the departure is now reported as taking place.

This connection shows ghadā used both to command a departure and to report one that has occurred.

The preceding verses show that the garden owners intended to gather their crop and deprive the needy of it.

That wrongful intention is not part of the meaning of ghadaw. The verb simply expresses their morning departure; the moral character of their conduct emerges from their decisions, speech, and actions throughout the story.

Gh d w is therefore neither inherently commendatory nor condemnatory. The same morning departure can serve a good or a bad purpose.

5. Morning and evening in remembering The God

Several verses contrast ghudūw and ghadāh with ʿashiyy or āṣāl.

This contrast emphasizes the beginning and end of the day and, in some contexts, conveys the continuity of remembering The God in human life.

Al-Anʿām 6:52: those who call upon their Lord morning and evening

وَلَا تَطْرُدِ الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ رَبَّهُم بِالْغَدَوٰةِ وَالْعَشِيِّ يُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَهُ مَا عَلَيْكَ مِنْ حِسَابِهِمْ مِنْ شَيْءٍ وَمَا مِنْ حِسَابِكَ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنْ شَيْءٍ فَتَطْرُدَهُمْ فَتَكُونَ مِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-lā taṭrudi alladhīna yadʿūna rabbahum bi-l-ghadāti wa-l-ʿashiyyi yurīdūna wajhahu mā ʿalayka min ḥisābihim min shayʾin wa-mā min ḥisābika ʿalayhim min shayʾin fa-taṭrudahum fa-takūna mina al-ẓālimīn

Al-Anʿām 6:52

“Do not drive away those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His face. Nothing of their account rests upon you, and nothing of your account rests upon them, that you should drive them away and become one of the wrongdoers.”

Bi-l-ghadāti wa-l-ʿashiyy gives the time of calling upon the Lord.

The verse’s main concern is the prohibition against driving away those who call upon their Lord and seek His face.

Morning and evening appear alongside their continuing conduct, but these two words alone do not specify a ritual type, a number of acts of worship, or a particular form of prayer.

Ghadāh expresses the morning time; yadʿūna expresses calling and requesting; and yurīdūna wajhahu clarifies their orientation and intention.

Within this context, their value is associated with their conduct and intention, rather than merely their presence at a particular hour.

Al-Kahf 18:28 likewise describes people who call upon their Lord morning and evening. Muhammad is told to remain patiently with them and not let his eyes turn from them towards the adornment of worldly life.

Read together, the two verses indicate that material resources or social standing should not determine who is welcomed into or excluded from a gathering of guidance.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:205: remembering The God at the beginning and end of the day

وَاذْكُرْ رَبَّكَ فِي نَفْسِكَ تَضَرُّعًا وَخِيفَةً وَدُونَ الْجَهْرِ مِنَ الْقَوْلِ بِالْغُدُوِّ وَالْآصَالِ وَلَا تَكُنْ مِنَ الْغَافِلِينَ

wa-dhkur rabbaka fī nafsika taḍarruʿan wa-khīfatan wa-dūna al-jahri mina al-qawli bi-l-ghudūwi wa-l-āṣāli wa-lā takun mina al-ghāfilīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:205

“Remember your Lord within yourself, humbly and fearfully, and in speech below a loud voice, in the mornings and at the ends of the day; and do not be among the heedless.”

Bi-l-ghudūw is contrasted with al-āṣāl.

Ghudūw denotes the beginning of the day, while āṣāl refers to its later parts.

Morning and the day’s end are presented as two times for remembrance. Yet the verse ends with “do not be among the heedless”: its concern extends beyond an act at two restricted times, placing avoidance of heedlessness at the centre of the command.

This does not remove the importance of the specified times. They occur in the verse itself and must be preserved in translation.

Al-Raʿd 13:15: shadows in the morning and at the day’s end

Here bi-l-ghudūwi wa-l-āṣāl is used of shadows.

Shadows change throughout the day as the light and the sun’s position change.

Ghudūw again expresses the beginning of the day. The prostration of shadows, however, requires consideration of the whole verse and Quranic uses of prostration.

The word ghudūw alone cannot specify the physical mechanism or character of that prostration.

Al-Nūr 24:36: glorification in the morning and at the day’s end

Here bi-l-ghudūwi wa-l-āṣāl concerns glorifying The God in houses that He has permitted to be raised and in which His name is remembered.

Again, two parts of the day are mentioned, but gh d w expresses only the time. The meanings of glorification, the houses, and their elevation come from other words and the context.

The entire concept of glorification, or a particular ritual form, should not be incorporated into the meaning of ghudūw.

6. Ghudūw in the wind’s movement and a scene of punishment

Sabaʾ 34:12: the wind’s morning movement

وَلِسُلَيْمَانَ الرِّيحَ غُدُوُّهَا شَهْرٌ وَرَوَاحُهَا شَهْرٌ وَأَسَلْنَا لَهُ عَيْنَ الْقِطْرِ وَمِنَ الْجِنِّ مَنْ يَعْمَلُ بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِ وَمَنْ يَزِغْ مِنْهُمْ عَنْ أَمْرِنَا نُذِقْهُ مِنْ عَذَابِ السَّعِيرِ

wa-li-sulaymāna al-rīḥa ghudūwuhā shahrun wa-rawāḥuhā shahrun wa-asalnā lahu ʿayna al-qiṭri wa-mina al-jinni man yaʿmalu bayna yadayhi bi-idhni rabbihi wa-man yazigh minhum ʿan amrinā nudhiqhu min ʿadhābi al-saʿīr

Sabaʾ 34:12

“And for Solomon, the wind: its morning journey was a month and its later journey a month. We made a spring of molten copper flow for him; and among the jinn were those who worked before him by his Lord’s permission. Whoever among them deviated from Our command, We would make him taste the punishment of the blazing fire.”

Ghudūwuhā refers to the wind’s morning movement, contrasted with rawāḥuhā.

The contrast distinguishes two parts of its movement: the morning journey and the journey during the later part of the day.

Shahr, “a month,” is the measure given for each part. The usual reading understands each as covering the distance of a month’s travel.

The root gh d w itself does not contain the meaning of exceptionally high speed, a month’s distance, or the mechanism of the wind’s movement.

Those details arise from ghudūwuhā shahr and the context concerning Solomon.

Nor should the wind’s movement be equated without evidence with a particular vehicle or modern technology. The root’s lexical sense remains morning movement; the nature of the event is a separate question.

Ghāfir 40:46: being exposed morning and evening

النَّارُ يُعْرَضُونَ عَلَيْهَا غُدُوًّا وَعَشِيًّا وَيَوْمَ تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ أَدْخِلُوا آلَ فِرْعَوْنَ أَشَدَّ الْعَذَابِ

al-nāru yuʿraḍūna ʿalayhā ghudūwan wa-ʿashiyyan wa-yawma taqūmu al-sāʿatu adkhilū āla firʿawna ashadda al-ʿadhāb

Ghāfir 40:46

“The fire: they are exposed to it morning and evening. And on the day when the Hour arises: ‘Admit Pharaoh’s people to the severest punishment.’”

Ghudūwan here means “in the morning,” contrasted with ʿashiyyan.

The two words express the timing or recurrence of exposure in two parts of the day.

The punishment and its character are understood from other parts of the verse, not from gh d w.

Nor does ghudūw alone establish that day and night in this condition follow precisely the same physical structure as earthly life.

The verse uses the language of morning and evening, which should be preserved. Determining the nature of time in that condition requires independent evidence.

7. Ghadāʾ: the morning meal

Al-Kahf 18:62

فَلَمَّا جَاوَزَا قَالَ لِفَتَاهُ آتِنَا غَدَاءَنَا لَقَدْ لَقِينَا مِنْ سَفَرِنَا هَٰذَا نَصَبًا

fa-lammā jāwazā qāla li-fatāhu ātinā ghadāʾanā la-qad laqīnā min safarinā hādhā naṣaban

Al-Kahf 18:62

“When they had passed beyond that place, he said to his young companion: ‘Bring us our meal; we have certainly experienced weariness on this journey of ours.’”

Ghadāʾanā consists of ghadāʾ, a meal, and the attached pronoun nā, “our.”

In early Arabic, ghadāʾ named the early-day meal.

Moses asks his companion to bring their food. The next clause expresses the weariness and hardship of the journey.

This does not establish that the requested meal was eaten at a particular morning hour, since ghadāʾ was also the name of a meal.

“Our meal” or “our food” is natural in translation. A lexical explanation can add that ghadāʾ originally referred to food eaten at the beginning of the day.

The difference between ghadāʾ and ghidhāʾ

These words are similar in spelling and pronunciation but belong to different roots.

Ghadāʾ, with dāl, belongs to gh d w and refers to a meal whose name derives from its timing.

Ghidhāʾ, with dhāl, belongs to gh dh w and concerns food, nourishment, and sustaining the body.

The Persian word for food, ghazā, is known from the second family and has the general meaning “food.”

Quranic ghadāʾ must not therefore be identified with ghidhāʾ merely because they sound similar and overlap in meaning.

Nor should the entire field of food, nutrition, and bodily growth be transferred to gh d w. The relationship between ghadāʾ and the meal’s timing matters in this family.

8. Ghad: tomorrow and the future

Yūsuf 12:12: the next day

In Yūsuf 12:12, Joseph’s brothers ask their father to send him with them ghadan.

Ghad here refers to the following day.

Its meaning is straightforward: tomorrow.

Their promise to look after Joseph and their actual intentions belong to the narrative context, not to the meaning of ghad.

Al-Kahf 18:23: speaking about the future

وَلَا تَقُولَنَّ لِشَيْءٍ إِنِّي فَاعِلٌ ذَٰلِكَ غَدًا

wa-lā taqūlanna li-shayʾin innī fāʿilun dhālika ghadan

Al-Kahf 18:23

“Never say of anything, ‘I will do that tomorrow.’”

The next verse, Al-Kahf 18:24, introduces dependence on the divine will.

Ghadan in the first verse refers to tomorrow. The passage’s message, however, concerns the limits of human knowledge and control over the future.

A person may have a plan for tomorrow, but its realization does not depend on that person alone.

This point comes from the passage as a whole. In this occurrence, gh d w supplies the reference to the near future.

Luqmān 31:34: not knowing tomorrow’s gain

The verse states that no one knows what they will earn tomorrow.

Ghadan means tomorrow and, alongside the other statements in the verse, indicates the limits of human knowledge of the future.

The point is not that people can make no predictions, plans, or probable assessments about tomorrow. Certain knowledge of future events differs from prediction based on available information.

This distinction is drawn from the verse’s content, not from the independent meaning of ghad.

Al-Qamar 54:26: a future that reveals the truth

سَيَعْلَمُونَ غَدًا مَنِ الْكَذَّابُ الْأَشِرُ

sa-yaʿlamūna ghadan mani al-kadhdhābu al-ashir

Al-Qamar 54:26

“Tomorrow they will know who is the insolent liar.”

Ghadan points to a future time when the truth will become clear to those addressed.

In Arabic, “tomorrow” can refer both to the next day and to the near future or a time when an outcome becomes apparent.

Without considering the context, the outcome should therefore not be restricted to the day immediately after the statement.

Conversely, ghad should not be detached entirely from “tomorrow” and defined simply as “resurrection” or “punishment.” Such events are possible contextual referents of that future.

Al-Ḥashr 59:18: what a person sends ahead for tomorrow

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَلْتَنْظُرْ نَفْسٌ مَا قَدَّمَتْ لِغَدٍ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū ittaqū allāha wa-l-tanẓur nafsun mā qaddamat li-ghadin wa-ttaqū allāha inna allāha khabīrun bi-mā taʿmalūn

Al-Ḥashr 59:18

“You who have believed, be mindful of The God, and let every soul consider what it has sent ahead for tomorrow. Be mindful of The God; The God is fully aware of what you do.”

Li-ghadin consists of the preposition lām and the noun ghad.

Ghad refers to tomorrow; lām relates what has been sent ahead to that tomorrow.

In context, however, “tomorrow” is more than the day after today. The verse concerns human actions and the outcome people prepare for themselves.

Al-Ḥashr 59:20 subsequently contrasts the people of the Fire with the people of the Garden.

Ghad here therefore encompasses the future, particularly a person’s outcome in the hereafter.

This illustrates how an everyday word, “tomorrow,” can make a larger future feel close and tangible in a Quranic context.

For example, someone planting seeds today might say they are working for their tomorrow, although the seeds’ results may only emerge months or years later.

“Tomorrow” in that expression contrasts with today and names a future affected by today’s work.

The verse likewise invites people to consider what they have sent ahead for their future. This reflection concerns responsibility for present actions, while the basic meaning of ghad remains tomorrow and the time ahead.

9. Distinctions from related roots

Gh d w and ṣ b ḥ: morning and the appearance of morning light

The Quranic root ṣ b ḥ concerns morning, the appearance of light, and the beginning of the day.

Gh d w is also associated with morning, but its verbal use emphasizes movement at the beginning of the day.

Ghadawta in Āl ʿImrān 3:121, for example, expresses a morning departure, whereas ṣubḥ can name the time itself or its emerging light.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary: ghadāh and ghudwah are also time words, like ṣubḥ.

Gh d w and b k r: the beginning of the day and earliness

B k r concerns beginnings, precedence, and being early.

The Quran uses bukrah for the early part of the day.

It is temporally close to ghadāh and ghudwah, but the derivatives of the two roots are not all equivalent.

Bikr, for example, can be used outside the time of day, as for being first or untouched, while ghad in the family under examination means tomorrow.

A shared association with morning does not make all branches of the two families synonymous.

Gh d w and r w ḥ: morning departure and movement later in the day

Ghudūw and rawāḥ are contrasted in some Arabic uses.

This contrast also occurs in Sabaʾ 34:12.

Ghudūwuhā refers to the wind’s morning movement, and rawāḥuhā to its movement during the later part of the day.

R w ḥ nevertheless has a wider semantic field; its derivatives cannot all be restricted to evening movement.

Gh d w and ʿ sh w: the beginning and later part of the day

Al-Anʿām 6:52 and Al-Kahf 18:28 contrast ghadāh with ʿashiyy.

Ghadāh names the beginning of the day, while ʿashiyy concerns its later part.

The contrast can express a behaviour repeated at two times of day, but it does not independently determine the exact number of occurrences or a specific ritual form.

Gh d w and gh dh w: the morning meal and nourishment

These roots look similar in writing, but the distinction between dāl and dhāl is real.

Ghadāʾ belongs to gh d w and names a meal.

Ghidhāʾ belongs to gh dh w and concerns food and nutrition.

Quranic lexical analysis must not merge them because of similar pronunciation or a shared Persian equivalent.

10. Questions of interpretation and translation

Does ghudūw always mean movement?

No. Morning movement is prominent in the verb ghadā, but ghudūw can name the morning itself in temporal constructions.

Bi-l-ghudūwi wa-l-āṣāl, for example, concerns two parts of the day and need not refer to movement.

By contrast, ghudūwuhā concerning the wind in Sabaʾ 34:12 occurs in a context of movement.

Each derivative’s meaning must therefore be considered separately.

Does ghadāh mean the entire day?

No. Ghadāh and ghudwah primarily concern the early part of the day.

Some expressions may use morning and evening to convey continuing conduct, but that contextual extension does not make ghadāh independently mean the entire day.

Does ghad always mean precisely the next day?

In ordinary usage, yes: ghad means tomorrow.

In some constructions, however, it can refer to a future that has not yet arrived.

Yūsuf 12:12 means the following day; Al-Ḥashr 59:18 concerns a future beyond it.

The difference arises from usage and context, not from two independent roots.

Are ghadāʾ and ghidhāʾ the same?

No. They are etymologically separate.

Ghadāʾ refers to a meal and has a name associated with the beginning of the day. Ghidhāʾ concerns food and nourishment.

Root studies must preserve this distinction even when both are sometimes translated into Persian with the same word for food.

Is remembrance at ghudūw and āṣāl restricted to two times?

The verses name two times, which translation must preserve.

Naming them does not, without further evidence, establish that remembrance at other hours has no relevance.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:205 combines the command to remember with a prohibition against heedlessness.

The importance of morning and the day’s end is thus compatible with continuing attentiveness and remembrance of The God.

11. Translation suggestions for Forqan

The following renderings are suggested for the Quranic uses:

Ghadawta: “you set out in the morning” or “you left early.”

Ughdū: “set out in the morning” or “go early.”

Ghadaw: “they set out in the morning” or “they went early.”

Ghudūw: “morning,” “the beginning of the day,” or, in a construction about movement, “morning departure.”

Bi-l-ghudūw: “in the morning” or “at the beginning of the day.”

Ghudūwuhā: “its morning journey” or “its morning movement.”

Ghudūwan: “in the morning.”

Ghadāh: “morning” or “the beginning of the day.”

Bi-l-ghadāti: “in the morning,” following the reading ghadāh and its temporal meaning.

Ghadāʾanā: “our meal” or “our food”; the lexical explanation can specify the early-day meal.

Ghadan: “tomorrow”; where a wider future is intended, retain “tomorrow” and explain that future in the analysis.

Li-ghadin: “for tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow” should not be replaced with “the hereafter” merely because a verse points towards resurrection. Preserve the direct meaning of ghad and clarify its broader reference in the reflection.

Likewise, ghudūw in remembrance verses should not be translated as a particular form of worship: the word itself expresses time.

Conclusion

Gh d w concerns morning, the beginning of the day, and movement during its first part. The same family includes ghad, “tomorrow,” and ghadāʾ, a meal named in relation to its timing.

Early usage employs ghadā for morning movement, a meaning also found in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Classical Arabic can extend it to going more generally, but the Quranic occurrences retain a clear connection with the beginning of the day.

The Quran contains five principal forms: the verb ghadā and the nouns ghudūw, ghadāh, ghadāʾ, and ghad. They express morning departure, the morning period, a meal, and tomorrow.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:121, Al-Qalam 68:22, and Al-Qalam 68:25, ghadā expresses movement at the beginning of the day. In remembrance verses, ghudūw and ghadāh name the morning. Sabaʾ 34:12 uses ghudūw for the wind’s movement, and Ghāfir 40:46 for the time of exposure to the fire.

Ghadāʾ in Al-Kahf 18:62 names a meal and must not be confused etymologically with ghidhāʾ.

Ghad means ordinary tomorrow in some verses. In others, such as Al-Ḥashr 59:18, it concerns the future and the consequences of human actions in a broader context.

This family cannot therefore be represented by “morning” or “tomorrow” alone. A precise account distinguishes the early-day period, movement then, the meal named after that time, and the independent use of ghad for the coming day.

The root’s central field is morning and the beginning of the day, with branches for morning movement, the meal taken then, and the tomorrow that follows today.
