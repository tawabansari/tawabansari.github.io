Gathering, driving, moving away, and the expression حَاشَ لِلَّهِ (ḥāsha li-llāh)

The Arabic root ح و ش concerns gathering, driving, and directing something to one side. Early uses include driving game toward a trap, gathering livestock, and things coming together within an area. Forms such as اِنْحَاشَ (inḥāsha) also describe moving away or taking fright.

Another branch concerns حُوشِيّ (ḥūshī), used in classical Arabic for what is wild, unfamiliar, or removed from ordinary company. Its historical relationship with وَحْشِيّ (waḥshī) is disputed; some lexicographers consider one a form of the other with its consonants rearranged.

In the Qur’an, حَاشَ (ḥāsha) occurs twice in حَاشَ لِلَّهِ (ḥāsha li-llāh). Arabic uses this expression to declare freedom from an improper attribution, disavow it, and put it beyond consideration. Its derivation from ح و ش is nevertheless uncertain: some lexicographers connect it with ح ش و or ح ش ي and the meanings of setting aside and excepting.

The distinction matters: the expression’s meaning in the two Qur’anic verses is clear, but its historical formation cannot be described with the same certainty.

1. Forms and semantic core

The Qur’anic morphological index records ح و ش in one form, حَاشَ (ḥāsha), occurring twice: Yūsuf 12:31 and Yūsuf 12:51.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two occurrences of ḥāsha in Yūsuf 12:31 and Yūsuf 12:51

In both verses, it accompanies لِلَّهِ (li-llāh). Arabic lexica, however, record a wider family of forms:

  • حَاشَ ـ يَحُوشُ (ḥāsha–yaḥūshu): to gather, drive, or direct something from the surrounding area toward a point.
  • حَوْش (ḥawsh): gathering and driving, particularly game and livestock.
  • حِيَاش (ḥiyāsh): another verbal noun for driving and gathering game.
  • أَحَاشَ and أَحْوَشَ (aḥāsha and aḥwasha): to drive game toward a hunter or help gather it.
  • حَوَّشَ (ḥawwasha): to gather and bring together.
  • اِحْتَوَشَ (iḥtawasha): to gather from the surrounding sides or place someone in the middle.
  • تَحَاوَشَ (taḥāwasha): for several people to gather around someone and put them in their midst.
  • تَحَوَّشَ (taḥawwasha): to move aside or away.
  • اِنْحَاشَ (inḥāsha): to move away, take fright, or distance oneself from something.
  • حَائِش (ḥāʾish): a dense, interconnected stand of trees, especially date palms.
  • حُوشِيّ (ḥūshī): wild, unfamiliar, or removed from ordinary company; of speech, unusual and difficult to understand.

These derivatives are recorded in dictionaries but should not be counted as Qur’anic forms. The relationship of ḥūshī to waḥshī, and the derivation of ḥāsha li-llāh, are also disputed.

Semantic core

In uses concerning game and livestock, gathering and driving are clear. Hunters may approach an animal from several directions and drive it toward a trap.

Approaching from the sides, limiting opportunities to scatter, and directing the animal’s movement can occur together in this action.

In اِحْتَوَشَ القومُ فلانًا (iḥtawasha al-qawmu fulānan), people put someone in their midst. In حَائِش (ḥāʾish), trees grow close and densely enough to form a connected stand.

These witnesses establish gathering and encompassing as one recognized axis of the family ح و ش.

اِنْحَاشَ (inḥāsha), however, can mean taking fright and moving away. It cannot simply be equated with gathering: one use describes movement toward a company, while another describes movement away from something.

Driving and taking fright can be practically connected: an animal being driven may move away from one side. Proving that every form arose from precisely this image would nevertheless require further historical evidence.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris first connects حُوش (ḥūsh) with وَحْش (waḥsh), suggesting rearrangement of the consonants. He then discusses gathering game. His caution likewise shows that the historical origins of all the branches are not equally certain.

2. Early Arabic uses

Gathering game

Dictionaries record حُشْتُ الصَّيْدَ حَوْشًا (ḥushtu al-ṣayda ḥawshan) for gathering and driving game.

They explain it as approaching game from the surrounding area to drive it toward a trap.

اِحْتَوَشَ القومُ الصَّيْدَ (iḥtawasha al-qawmu al-ṣayda) likewise describes people driving game toward one another and enclosing it.

These examples describe a concrete practice. A hunter need not catch the animal by hand: movement, sound, or presence may redirect it toward the intended point.

In such a use, ḥawsh is consequently more than several things happening to collect together. Direction and driving can also be involved.

The trap, hunter, and hunting equipment are not fixed parts of the root’s meaning. They belong to the object and circumstances of this particular use.

Gathering livestock

The lexica use حُشْتُ الإِبِلَ (ḥushtu al-ibila) for gathering and driving camels.

The action resembles driving game, but its purpose may differ. Game is directed toward a trap, whereas camels may be collected to prevent them scattering or to move them elsewhere.

The verb itself therefore describes gathering and driving; the situation establishes the purpose.

An early poetic witness: حُوشَ الْفُؤَادِ (ḥūsha al-fuʾādi)

Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī, an early Arabic poet, describes an alert, keen-minded person as follows. The sources examined do not establish the precise date of this verse:

فَأَتَتْ بِهِ حُوشَ الْفُؤَادِ مُبَطَّنًا
سُهُدًا إِذَا مَا نَامَ لَيْلُ الْهَوْجَلِ

fa-atat bihi ḥūsha al-fuʾādi mubaṭṭanan / suhudan idhā mā nāma laylu al-hawjali

Translation:

“She bore him keen of heart, slender of waist, and little given to sleep, when the slow, dull man sleeps through the night.”

Here حُوشَ الْفُؤَادِ (ḥūsha al-fuʾādi) describes the condition and character of the person’s heart.

Lexicographers explain it through keenness, firmness, or a wild and unfamiliar disposition of the heart. The person described is alert and ready; unlike someone slow and inattentive, he does not easily lapse into ease and sleep.

This witness shows that ḥūsh can also describe a person, beyond driving game or gathering livestock.

Nevertheless, keenness of heart in this construction should not define every derivative. It is a particular description within a poetic construction.

Lisān al-ʿArab, S H D: suhud and sleeping little

حَائِش (ḥāʾish): a dense stand of trees

Arabic records حَائِش (ḥāʾish) for a dense stand of date palms or other trees.

Al-Akhṭal, an Umayyad poet, says:

وَكَأَنَّ ظُعْنَ الْحَيِّ حَائِشُ قَرْيَةٍ
دَانِي الْجَنَاةِ وَطَيِّبُ الْأَثْمَارِ

wa-ka-anna ẓuʿna al-ḥayyi ḥāʾishu qaryatin / dānī al-janāti wa-ṭayyibu al-athmāri

Approximate translation:

“The women’s traveling litters seem like the dense palm grove of a village, its fruit within reach and pleasant to taste.”

In this simile, ḥāʾish is a stand of interconnected trees. The main image is their density and gathering in one place.

This witness belongs to the Umayyad period and should not be presented as pre-Islamic poetry.

Alongside uses concerning hunting and livestock, ḥāʾish shows that gathering can be expressed without driving or pursuit.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: Al-Akhṭal’s witness and ḥāʾish

Moving away and taking fright

Dhū al-Rummah, an Umayyad poet, describes an ostrich egg:

وَبَيْضَاءَ لَا تَنْحَاشُ مِنَّا وَأُمُّهَا
إِذَا مَا رَأَتْنَا زِيلَ مِنْهَا زَوِيلُهَا

wa-bayḍāʾa lā tanḥāshu minnā wa-ummuhā / idhā mā raʾatnā zīla minhā zawīluhā

Approximate translation:

“And a white egg that does not flee from us, though its mother, when she sees us, is thrown into great alarm.”

In this verse, لَا تَنْحَاشُ (lā tanḥāshu) refers to the ostrich egg. The egg does not flee from its place, but its mother takes fright and moves away when she sees people.

Tanḥāshu concerns taking fright or moving away. This witness establishes a meaning different from gathering game.

The lexica also explain تَحَوَّشَ القومُ عَنِّي (taḥawwasha al-qawmu ʿannī) as people moving away from someone.

Distance is prominent in both uses. That does not make every derivative of ح و ش mean fleeing or fear.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab: Dhū al-Rummah’s witness, inḥāsha, and taḥawwasha

حُوشِيّ (ḥūshī) and unfamiliar speech

Arabic sources use رَجُلٌ حُوشِيٌّ (rajulun ḥūshiyyun) for someone who mixes little with people and is unaccustomed to ordinary company.

They also record حُوشِيُّ الْكَلَامِ (ḥūshiyyu al-kalām) for unusual, unfamiliar, and difficult speech.

Its use is close to وَحْشِيُّ الْكَلَامِ (waḥshiyyu al-kalām).

Ibn Fāris, however, suggests that ḥūsh and waḥsh may involve rearranged consonants. Their semantic similarity can therefore be recognized without treating the historical derivation of one from the other as certain.

Lexica also transmit stories about ḥūsh as a land or beings associated with the jinn. These report Arab beliefs and naming traditions; they do not historically establish the existence of that land or those animals.

The lexical point established by this branch is the use of ḥūshī for what is unfamiliar, removed from company, or unusual.

3. حَاشَ لِلَّهِ (ḥāsha li-llāh) and its disputed derivation

The principal Qur’anic form relevant to this study is حَاشَ (ḥāsha).

In both Qur’anic occurrences, it forms an expression with لِلَّهِ (li-llāh).

Arabic uses حَاشَ لِلَّهِ or حَاشَى لِلَّهِ (ḥāsha li-llāh or ḥāshā li-llāh) to declare freedom from an improper attribution, disavow it, or express astonishment and denial.

There are, however, differing historical analyses of its root and formation.

Connection with ح و ش

Some dictionaries discuss ḥāsha li-llāh under ح و ش. The Qur’anic morphological index likewise places it in that family.

Within this classification, a semantic connection can be seen between moving away, setting aside, and putting an attribution beyond consideration.

That proximity alone does not prove that ḥāsha li-llāh derives directly from the verb for driving and gathering game.

Such a claim also requires evidence of the word’s morphological formation and historical use.

Connection with ح ش و and ح ش ي

Some lexicographers regard ḥāsha as a shortened form of ḥāshā.

In this analysis, ḥāshā concerns setting aside, separating, and excepting.

Al-Farrāʾ explains it through حَاشَيْتُ (ḥāshaytu): I excepted someone from among others.

Al-Zajjāj also connects the expression with حَشَا (ḥashā), meaning a side or region. In this explanation, the central meaning is distancing someone or something from an attribution and excluding them from it.

In this family, حاشى فلانًا (ḥāshā fulānan) can mean excepting someone from a judgment or description.

For example, if a group is given a blameworthy description and someone is excepted, that description is not accepted as applying to that person.

This meaning fits the use of ḥāsha li-llāh to disavow and reject an improper attribution.

Morphologically, however, this analysis differs from simply placing ḥāsha under ح و ش.

Two questions must therefore be distinguished: the expression’s established meaning in use, and its precise historical derivation.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ḥāshā, setting aside, exception, and Al-Farrāʾ’s account

Is ḥāsha a verb, particle, or noun?

Grammarians also disagree about the grammatical status of ḥāshā.

In constructions of exception, some regard it as a preposition, while others also recognize verbal uses.

Arabic can, for example, use ḥāshā to except someone from a group. The case of the following word depends on the grammatical analysis.

The Qur’anic ḥāsha li-llāh, however, differs from an ordinary exception naming several people and then excluding one.

Here it functions as an expression of disavowal and rejection.

In some grammatical analyses of these two verses, ḥāsha is a noun expressing disavowal and standing in place of an absolute object, as if one said تنزيهًا لله (tanzīhan li-llāh). This differs from other analyses treating it as a verb or particle.

This analysis explains how the expression can convey disavowal and distancing without an independently stated verb.

Nevertheless, grammatical disagreement should not create an artificial dispute over the sentence’s clear sense. In both verses, the women use ḥāsha li-llāh to reject something as unacceptable and then state their point.

Al-Mujtabā min Mushkil Iʿrāb al-Qurʾān: ḥāsha as a substitute for the absolute object

4. Qur’anic uses

Yūsuf 12:31: astonishment on seeing Joseph

فَلَمَّا سَمِعَتْ بِمَكْرِهِنَّ أَرْسَلَتْ إِلَيْهِنَّ وَأَعْتَدَتْ لَهُنَّ مُتَّكَأً وَآتَتْ كُلَّ وَاحِدَةٍ مِنْهُنَّ سِكِّينًا وَقَالَتِ اخْرُجْ عَلَيْهِنَّ فَلَمَّا رَأَيْنَهُ أَكْبَرْنَهُ وَقَطَّعْنَ أَيْدِيَهُنَّ وَقُلْنَ حَاشَ لِلَّهِ مَا هَٰذَا بَشَرًا إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا مَلَكٌ كَرِيمٌ

fa-lammā samiʿat bi-makrihinna arsalat ilayhinna wa-aʿtadat lahunna muttakaʾan wa-ātat kulla wāḥidatin minhunna sikkīnan wa-qālati ukhruj ʿalayhinna fa-lammā raʾaynahu akbarnahu wa-qaṭṭaʿna aydiyahunna wa-qulna ḥāsha li-llāhi mā hādhā basharan in hādhā illā malakun karīmun

Proposed translation:

“When she heard of their scheming, she sent for them, prepared a reclining place for them, and gave each a knife. She said, ‘Come out before them.’ When they saw him, they regarded him as extraordinary and cut their hands, saying, ‘The God forbid! This is no mortal; this is none other than a noble angel.’”

Yūsuf 12:31

The women who had been speaking about Joseph are invited to a gathering. Seeing him, they are so affected that they cut their hands and speak in astonishment.

Their words begin with حَاشَ لِلَّهِ (ḥāsha li-llāh), followed by two sentences: مَا هَٰذَا بَشَرًا (mā hādhā basharan) and إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا مَلَكٌ كَرِيمٌ (in hādhā illā malakun karīmun).

In this setting, ḥāsha li-llāh expresses astonishment together with disavowal and distancing from their ordinary conception.

On seeing Joseph, the women say that this person is not a mortal but can only be a noble angel.

Their speech, however, reports their impression of Joseph. It does not mean that the Qur’an itself denies that Joseph is human.

Nor does ḥāsha li-llāh itself mean being an angel or being beautiful. Those meanings come from the following sentences and the situation.

The Persian “panāh bar Khodā!” can convey their astonishment and denial. The lexical explanation should nevertheless retain the connection with disavowal and distancing.

Al-Ṭabarī: maʿādha Allāh and disavowal, with different analyses of the word

Yūsuf 12:51: distancing Joseph from wrongdoing

قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُنَّ إِذْ رَاوَدْتُنَّ يُوسُفَ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ قُلْنَ حَاشَ لِلَّهِ مَا عَلِمْنَا عَلَيْهِ مِنْ سُوءٍ قَالَتِ امْرَأَتُ الْعَزِيزِ الْآنَ حَصْحَصَ الْحَقُّ أَنَا رَاوَدْتُهُ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ وَإِنَّهُ لَمِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ

qāla mā khaṭbukunna idh rāwadtunna yūsufa ʿan nafsihi qulna ḥāsha li-llāhi mā ʿalimnā ʿalayhi min sūʾin qālati imraʾatu al-ʿazīzi al-āna ḥaṣḥaṣa al-ḥaqqu anā rāwadtuhu ʿan nafsihi wa-innahu la-mina al-ṣādiqīna

Proposed translation:

“He said, ‘What was your affair when you sought to entice Joseph against his will?’ They said, ‘The God forbid! We know of no evil on his part.’ The wife of the dignitary said, ‘Now the truth has come to light. I was the one who sought to entice him, and he is certainly among the truthful.’”

Yūsuf 12:51

The women’s speech about Joseph appears in a different situation here.

The question concerns the affair in which they sought to draw Joseph into a relationship contrary to his wishes. Their response begins with the same حَاشَ لِلَّهِ (ḥāsha li-llāh), but this time the following sentence is:

مَا عَلِمْنَا عَلَيْهِ مِنْ سُوءٍ (mā ʿalimnā ʿalayhi min sūʾin)

They know of no wrongdoing on Joseph’s part.

Here ḥāsha li-llāh accompanies the denial of wrongdoing by Joseph. In the women’s speech, it declares him free of the allegation and rejects its attribution to him.

The verse then quotes the wife of the dignitary, who brings the truth to light: she sought to entice Joseph, and he was among the truthful.

The difference from Yūsuf 12:31 matters.

In the first verse, the women are astonished by seeing Joseph and describe him as surpassing their ordinary conception of a human being.

In the second, the same expression answers a question about conduct and moral responsibility.

Ḥāsha li-llāh alone therefore does not establish whether the subject is beauty, astonishment, chastity, or innocence. The following sentences identify what is denied or disavowed.

In both verses, the expression conveys rejection and distancing, but its subject differs.

مَا عَلِمْنَا (mā ʿalimnā) also refers to what those women know. It should not be turned into a claim beyond their words: they say that they know of no wrongdoing by Joseph, rather than claiming unlimited knowledge of his entire life.

5. Comparison with related roots

ح و ش (ḥawsh) and ح و ز (ḥawz): gathering and possession

The two roots are close in uses concerning gathering and driving.

In witnesses concerning hunting, ح و ش describes driving animals toward a trap and gathering them.

ح و ز can likewise describe gathering something and bringing it within one’s control.

Some derivatives of ح و ز emphasize possession or acquisition, whereas ح و ش has clear witnesses for driving game and surrounding from the sides.

The distinction is not absolute, since both roots can express gathering.

Their proximity also does not justify equating ḥāsha li-llāh with ḥāza. The Qur’anic expression has its own construction and lexical history.

Study of Ḥ W Z

ح و ش (ḥawsh) and و ح ش (waḥsh): unfamiliarity and distance from company

Waḥsh and waḥshī concern wild animals and what is removed from the human environment or a tame, familiar condition.

Arabic also uses ḥūshī for someone removed from company and for unusual, unfamiliar speech.

The overlap is evident, but Ibn Fāris’s discussion raises the possibility of rearranged consonants. Semantic similarity alone cannot establish the two families’ historical identity.

Ḥūshī speech is difficult and unusual, rather than necessarily false or worthless.

ح و ش (ḥawsh) and ح ش و / ح ش ي (ḥashw / ḥashy): gathering and setting aside

This comparison is especially relevant to حَاشَ لِلَّهِ (ḥāsha li-llāh).

In the family ح ش و and related forms, ḥashw concerns the interior or what is placed inside something, while ḥāshiyah and some related constructions concern a side or region.

From the notion of a side or region, lexical explanations of ḥāshā introduce setting aside and excepting.

In this analysis, ḥāshā means excluding someone from a description or judgment.

This field differs from ḥawsh in gathering game. In one, something is driven toward a collection; in the other, someone or some description is separated and excepted from a collection or attribution.

Because the derivation of ḥāsha li-llāh is disputed, the two paths cannot be merged without evidence.

The expression’s Qur’anic meaning can be established from its use even where its precise morphological origin remains unsettled.

ح و ش (ḥawsh) and ن ز ه (nazah): disavowal and declaring freedom from defect

In ḥāsha li-llāh, disavowal and distancing from an improper attribution are relevant.

The Arabic root ن ز ه concerns being far from something and declaring freedom from what is inappropriate.

Ḥāsha li-llāh, however, is a particular expression. The whole family ح و ش should not be assigned the meaning of declaring freedom from defect.

حُشْتُ الصَّيْدَ (ḥushtu al-ṣayda) does not mean purifying game of evil; nor does اِحْتَوَشَ القومُ فلانًا (iḥtawasha al-qawmu fulānan) mean declaring someone free of defect.

This shows that one expression’s proximity to disavowal does not redefine every derivative attributed to its root.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Depending on the construction, suitable translations for classical derivatives of ح و ش include gathering, driving, directing toward something, encompassing, moving aside, and taking fright.

For ḥāʾish in relation to trees, “a dense stand of trees” or “a dense palm grove” is appropriate.

The Qur’anic expression under examination, however, is ḥāsha li-llāh and must be understood as a whole.

In Yūsuf 12:31, the Persian “panāh bar Khodā!” is a natural opening to the women’s speech, conveying their astonishment and denial.

The same rendering can be used in Yūsuf 12:51, where the context instead emphasizes distancing Joseph from wrongdoing.

“The God is free of defect!” is also defensible in terms of the established sense of disavowal, although its Persian equivalent may sound less natural in some representations of the women’s speech.

The Persian “ḥāshā!” retains the Arabic expression, but may need explanation for readers unfamiliar with it.

For Forqan’s base Persian text, the proposal is to render ḥāsha li-llāh in both verses as “panāh bar Khodā!” and explain in the root study that Arabic uses it for disavowal, denial, and distancing an attribution. In the English proposals above, “The God forbid!” conveys that rejection idiomatically.

Translation must also be distinguished from explaining a root. In these verses, ḥāsha li-llāh should not be translated directly as gathering, driving, or surrounding: those meanings belong to other uses of the lexical family, rather than the Qur’anic expression itself.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root ح و ش concerns gathering, driving, and encompassing. Early uses include driving game toward a trap, gathering livestock, and trees collecting within one area.

Forms such as inḥāsha and taḥawwasha can concern moving aside or taking fright. Ḥūshī also describes what is unfamiliar, wild, or unusual, but its historical relationship with waḥshī is disputed.

Ḥāsha li-llāh occurs twice in Sūrat Yūsuf. In the first, the women are astonished at seeing Joseph; in the second, answering a question about him, they know of no wrongdoing by him.

In both verses, the expression conveys disavowal, denial, and distancing. Whether it historically belongs to ح و ش or developed from ح ش و / ح ش ي and the notion of setting aside and excepting remains disputed.

Final definition: In established Arabic uses, ح و ش denotes gathering, driving, and encompassing. Some derivatives also express moving away, taking fright, and unfamiliarity. The Qur’anic حَاشَ لِلَّهِ (ḥāsha li-llāh) expresses disavowal and denial, but its derivation from this root is uncertain, and the study must preserve that disagreement. Brief root meaning: Gathering, driving, and encompassing; ḥāsha li-llāh expresses disavowal and denial, and its derivation from this root is disputed.
