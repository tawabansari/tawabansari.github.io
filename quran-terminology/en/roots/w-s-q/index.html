Root w-s-q — Gathering, encompassing, and becoming whole

Qur’anic and lexical forms

وَسَقَ
(wasaqa)
He gathered; Form I perfect

اتَّسَقَ
(ittasaqa)
It became full; Form VIII perfect

وَسْق
(wasq)
Load and measure; non-Qur’anic noun

وَاسِق
(wāsiq)
Carrier; non-Qur’anic active participle

وَسِيقَة
(wasīqah)
Group of animals; non-Qur’anic noun

اتِّسَاق
(ittisāq)
Coherence; non-Qur’anic verbal noun

The Arabic root w-s-q concerns gathering, bringing together, encompassing, and carrying. Its forms can describe scattered things brought into a collection, a load assembled in one place, or parts becoming orderly and complete.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in consecutive verses of Al-Inshiqāq: وَسَقَ concerning night and اتَّسَقَ concerning the moon.

The first verse concerns night and what it encompasses or gathers. The second describes the moon when its illuminated disk appears complete and whole.

The two uses share a semantic field but are not identical. وَسَقَ concerns gathering and encompassing; اتَّسَقَ concerns the moon attaining fullness.

The root should therefore not be confined to “collecting,” nor should “becoming complete” be extended to every derivative.

1. The root in early Arabic

Gathering and carrying

In early Arabic, وَسْق designates a load or something gathered and carried.

A camel’s load is a familiar example: goods and produce are assembled and carried on an animal.

Two features can be distinguished: bringing things together and making them into a load that can be carried.

Arabic sources record وَسَقَ الشَّيْءَ for gathering or carrying something, and أَوْسَقَ الْبَعِيرَ for loading a camel.

The usage is not restricted to inanimate objects.

A pregnant she-camel is also described as وَاسِق because she carries a fetus within her.

A witness appears in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim:

أَلَظَّ بِهِنَّ يَحْدُوهُنَّ حَتَّىٰ

alaẓẓa bihinna yaḥdūhunna ḥattā

تَبَيَّنَتِ الْحِيَالُ مِنَ الْوِسَاقِ

tabayyanati al-ḥiyālu mina al-wisāqi

“He persisted in driving them until the nonpregnant camels became distinguishable from the pregnant ones.”

الْوِسَاقِ here refers to pregnant camels.

الْحِيَالُ, by contrast, refers to those that are not pregnant.

The word thus concerns carrying a fetus: a living creature bearing another within itself.

Arabic sources attribute the verse to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim, with reported variations in some wording. The lexical explanations nevertheless clearly identify al-wisāq as pregnant camels.

This connects the family in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period with carrying and containing.

Gathering animals into a group

Another use appears in وَسِيقَة.

The word designates a group of camels or other animals driven together, particularly animals taken away as a group; lexical explanations also mention stolen animals driven off together.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur says:

كَذَبْتُ عَلَيْكَ لَا تَزَالُ تَقُوفُنِي

kadhabtu ʿalayka lā tazālu taqūfunī

كَمَا قَافَ آثَارَ الْوَسِيقَةِ قَائِفُ

kamā qāfa āthāra al-wasīqati qāʾifu

Its sense is: “Keep following my trail, as a tracker follows the tracks of the animals driven away.”

الْوَسِيقَةِ here concerns animals driven together.

Keeping them together is emphasized in explaining this usage: dispersal makes the group harder to move and control.

Gathering in this branch thus extends beyond putting objects into a container; it can concern keeping living creatures together.

The attribution varies: some transmissions name Al-Quṭāmī. It cannot therefore be treated as a witness certainly attributable to Al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur.

Despite that limitation, wasīqah for a group of animals driven together is recorded in dictionaries.

Wasq as a load and a measure

Besides a load, وَسْق became the name of a measure for produce.

In established Islamic-period usage, one wasq is reckoned as sixty ṣāʿ.

The connection with a load is understandable: a quantity of produce is gathered and treated as a load or measured unit.

The older sense of a load should nevertheless be distinguished from the measure’s precise quantity.

A sixty-ṣāʿ unit in Islamic-period sources does not prove that the same measure was fixed in every detail throughout the pre-Islamic period.

The mass of an actual load can also vary with the produce. Wasq should not be converted into one fixed modern weight in every historical use.

This unit does not occur in the Qur’an. It helps explain the gathering and carrying branch behind the two Qur’anic uses.

What the historical witnesses establish

The evidence connects early Arabic w-s-q with carrying, gathering, and keeping things or creatures together.

Examples include a camel’s load, animal pregnancy, and animals driven in a group.

The earliest historical meaning underlying every branch cannot, however, be established with certainty.

Gathering and carrying a load may be semantically connected, but that alone does not prove which arose first.

The poetic witnesses discussed here also do not independently establish pre-Islamic ittasaqa concerning the moon. That sense occurs in the Qur’an itself and is explained in classical Arabic sources.

2. Forms and semantic center

Important forms in Arabic include:

وَسَقَ: he gathered, encompassed, or carried.

وَسْق: a load or gathered quantity; also an established measure for produce.

وُسُوق and أَوْسُق: plurals of وَسْق concerning loads and quantities.

وَاسِق: a carrier; in animal usage, a pregnant animal.

أَوْسَقَ: he loaded something or placed a load on an animal; also recorded concerning a date palm bearing abundantly.

وَسِيقَة: a group of animals, especially camels driven together.

اسْتَوْسَقَ: it gathered or became a connected whole; concerning animals, they gathered and followed together.

اتَّسَقَ: its parts joined, became orderly, or reached completeness and harmony.

اتِّسَاق: connectedness, coordination, order, and the arrangement of parts into a coherent whole.

مُتَّسِق: orderly, coordinated, or having connected parts.

وَسَّقَ: he made something into specified loads or measures, such as produce measured out in wasqs.

These forms are known in Arabic, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

The actual Qur’anic forms

Only two forms from this family appear in the Qur’an.

وَسَقَ: a Form I perfect verb, in Al-Inshiqāq 84:17.

اتَّسَقَ: a Form VIII perfect verb, in Al-Inshiqāq 84:18.

The nouns wasq, wasīqah, ittisāq, and mutta­siq do not occur in the Qur’an.

Nor do istawsaqa or the forms describing loading camels or measuring produce.

Semantic center

A defensible definition is:

Gathering or encompassing things in a collection, and in some constructions bringing parts together into order, harmony, or completeness.

وَسَقَ emphasizes gathering or encompassing.

اتَّسَقَ emphasizes a state in which parts or features have become coordinated and complete.

This distinction should not become an absolute rule for every use of Forms I and VIII.

In the Qur’an, construction and subject also clarify the distinction: night encompasses something, while the moon attains fullness.

3. The first Qur’anic use: night and what it encompasses

Al-Inshiqāq 84:17

وَاللَّيْلِ وَمَا وَسَقَ

wa-al-layli wa-mā wasaq

Al-Inshiqāq 84:17

“And by the night and what it gathers.”

وَسَقَ is a Form I perfect verb.

Its implied subject refers to night; مَا, in the relative-pronoun reading, denotes what night encompasses or gathers.

وَمَا وَسَقَ can therefore be explained as:

What night gathers within itself or encompasses as it arrives.

How does night gather things?

As night falls, daylight diminishes and darkness spreads across the land.

Mountains, plains, trees, houses, and animals fall within its reach.

Many daytime animals return to their nests and resting places, while nocturnal creatures become active.

Human life also changes. Some finish their day’s work and gather at home, while other activities begin in darkness.

These images illustrate how night encompasses creatures, places, and events.

Older lexical explanations likewise describe وَمَا وَسَقَ as what night gathers, carries, or covers.

The verb’s meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from illustrative examples.

The verse does not restrict the gathering to people returning home, animals alone, or stars alone.

مَا has a broad scope in this construction.

Restricting it to one group of beings or one nocturnal event without sufficient indication narrows the verse.

Does وَسَقَ mean covering?

Some Arabic explanations interpret وَمَا وَسَقَ as what night covers and encompasses.

This fits the image of night.

As darkness spreads, many things fall within it and become less visible.

Covering and gathering are nevertheless not exact synonyms.

Gathering, carrying, and encompassing have clear lexical support in w-s-q.

Objects becoming hidden from view can be a consequence of darkness arriving.

Night may hide a tree from sight, but the tree has not moved or been collected elsewhere merely because it is dark.

Night’s gathering therefore need not mean every creature physically assembling in one place.

“Encompassing” can clarify the broader sense here.

Mā wasaqa and the sense of carrying

Wasaqa is also used for carrying in Arabic.

Some lexical explanations accordingly approach the verse as “what night carries.”

In modern Persian, however, that wording may suggest a physical picture, as if night carried objects on its back like a camel or person.

The verse does not require such a picture.

Night encompasses things within its reach, with diverse events and creatures occurring within it.

For general translation, “what it gathers” or “what it encompasses” is therefore more natural than “what it carries.”

Carrying remains part of the lexical background but should not be imposed on night with all its physical attributes.

4. The second Qur’anic use: the moon when it becomes full

Al-Inshiqāq 84:18

وَالْقَمَرِ إِذَا اتَّسَقَ

wa-al-qamari idhā ittasaq

Al-Inshiqāq 84:18

“And by the moon when it becomes full.”

اتَّسَقَ is a Form VIII perfect verb.

Its subject is the implied pronoun “it,” referring to the moon. الْقَمَرِ itself is genitive after the oath particle wāw.

إِذَا specifies the relevant time: when the moon attains ittisāq.

Arabic records ittasaqa for coming together, becoming orderly, and reaching completeness or harmony.

Concerning the moon, this is when its illuminated disk appears full and round.

Ittasaqa al-qamar thus concerns the full moon, or badr.

Does the moon physically become more complete?

Throughout a lunar month, the moon appears in different phases to an observer on Earth.

Sometimes only a thin illuminated crescent is visible.

The visible illuminated portion then grows until the disk appears full.

Afterward, the visible illuminated portion decreases again.

This does not mean that the moon’s body is incomplete at the beginning of the month and acquires new parts halfway through it.

The moon remains the same celestial body through its usual phases; what changes is how much of its illuminated portion is visible from Earth.

Ittasaqa here therefore concerns the illuminated disk appearing full and whole.

That fits “reaching completeness,” but does not yield a theory about the moon changing physical size or collecting separate pieces.

Fullness and mere roundness

The moon is always approximately spherical, yet does not appear as a fully illuminated disk every night.

Ittasaqa should therefore not simply mean “became round,” as though the moon had not previously been physically round.

Arabic explanations use expressions such as becoming complete, filling out, and attaining fullness for lunar ittisāq.

These concern the fullness of the moon’s illuminated appearance.

“The moon became full” naturally conveys this meaning.

The root explanation can clarify that the illuminated disk appears complete.

Does ittasaqa always mean a full moon?

No.

In general Arabic usage, it can describe things whose parts have become ordered or coordinated.

Ittisāq can, for example, describe affairs or activities arranged in a clear order.

In Al-Inshiqāq 84:18, however, the verb concerns the moon.

Lunar fullness is therefore determined by the word’s established usage and context.

The verse does not make ittisāq exclusively the name of the full-moon phase throughout Arabic.

Likewise, the presence of “order” in the root family does not establish that the verse discusses the mathematics of the lunar orbit or every law governing celestial motion.

Those can be subjects of separate scientific study or reflection, but they are not the direct meaning of ittasaqa.

5. The connection between the verses in Al-Inshiqāq

The two Qur’anic occurrences are consecutive and should be read together.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:16 speaks of twilight.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:17 concerns night and what it encompasses.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:18 describes the moon at fullness.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:19 then concerns passing from one state to another.

The sequence presents familiar images to the reader.

Twilight recalls the transition from daylight to night.

Night encompasses a realm containing diverse creatures and events.

The moon also changes in appearance over time to an earthly observer, reaching a phase in which its illuminated disk appears full.

These images concern change, passing time, and reaching different states.

Do wasaqa and ittasaqa mean the same thing?

Gathering and a form of wholeness connect the two verses.

There is, however, an important difference.

In وَمَا وَسَقَ, night gathers or encompasses something within its reach.

In إِذَا اتَّسَقَ, the moon itself reaches a state in which it appears complete and whole.

The first emphasizes gathering or encompassing.

The second emphasizes the resulting state of fullness.

The verbs belong to one semantic family but are not interchangeable.

Translating وَسَقَ in the night verse as “became complete” loses both the relation to مَا and the sense of gathering.

Translating اتَّسَقَ concerning the moon merely as “it collected things” fails to convey the illuminated disk’s fullness.

Connection with ṭabaqan ʿan ṭabaq

Al-Inshiqāq 84:19 contains طَبَقًا عَنْ طَبَقٍ.

In the surah’s context, human beings face changing conditions and passage from one stage to another.

Its connection with twilight, night, and moon can draw attention to change and transformation.

Their juxtaposition does not establish that each natural change directly names a particular stage of human life or the Hereafter.

Such correspondences require independent evidence.

Nor does وَالْقَمَرِ إِذَا اتَّسَقَ alone say that every stage of human life must repeat like the phases of the moon.

Natural imagery and human destiny stand alongside one another, while each word’s lexical meaning must retain its place.

6. Comparison with related roots

w-s-q (wasaq) compared with j-m-ʿ (jamaʿa)

J-m-ʿ concerns gathering and collecting.

Gathering is likewise a principal branch of w-s-q.

Wasaq also has established uses involving carrying a load and encompassing things.

Ittasaqa can describe a collection becoming connected, orderly, or complete.

Jamʿ may therefore explain some uses of wasq but does not cover its entire semantic field.

“It gathers” suits Al-Inshiqāq 84:17; in Al-Inshiqāq 84:18, “becomes full” is clearer than “gathers together.”

w-s-q (wasaq) compared with n-s-q (nasaq)

These roots are close in sound and meaning but remain distinct.

Arabic nasaq concerns things arranged in sequence and order.

Ittisāq, from w-s-q, can also express order and coordination.

Both families occur in discussions of arrangement and harmony.

This overlap does not permit changing w-s-q into n-s-q or identifying their histories of derivation.

The Qur’anic expression concerning the moon means its becoming full, rather than merely arranging things in a row.

w-s-q (wasaq) compared with s-w-q (sawq)

Reversing the positions of wāw and sīn can lead to confusion between these roots.

In its verbal use, s-w-q concerns driving something onward, often from behind.

W-s-q concerns gathering, carrying, and encompassing; driving animals together is also recorded in its lexical range.

Their semantic fields can overlap in uses involving camels.

Driving a herd may require keeping the animals together and coordinated.

Driving animals and keeping them together remain distinguishable aspects of the action, despite that overlap.

Night in Al-Inshiqāq 84:17 is not thereby described as driving every creature toward one destination.

That meaning cannot be inferred merely from the resemblance of wasq and sawq.

w-s-q (wasaq) compared with w-th-q (wathaq)

W-th-q concerns firmness, reliability, and binding securely.

Wathāq and tawthīq belong to that family.

Wasq is written with sīn and is a separate root.

In modern Persian, tawthīq concerns authentication or documentation, whereas ittisāq concerns the coordination and consistency of parts.

A text may be both coherent and documented, but those are different qualities.

It may be internally consistent yet lack sufficient evidence for its claims.

Conversely, reliable information may be assembled without clear organization or coherence.

Ittisāq alone therefore does not prove a claim’s truth or validity.

w-s-q (wasaq) compared with t-m-m (tamma)

T-m-m concerns completion and becoming complete.

In Al-Inshiqāq 84:18, ittasaqa likewise concerns fullness in the context of the moon.

This overlap does not make the roots fully equivalent.

Tamma can describe a task ending or reaching completion in diverse contexts.

Ittasaqa also concerns connection, order, and parts reaching a coordinated state.

For the moon, that state is its illuminated disk appearing full.

“Became full” therefore suits this verse, but the general definition of w-s-q should not be confined to completion.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For Al-Inshiqāq 84:17, a suitable rendering is:

“And by the night and what it gathers.”

This preserves gathering and fits the Arabic construction.

“And by the night and what it encompasses” is also defensible: gathering here involves night’s encompassing creatures and places.

The basic translation need not be burdened with long wording combining both explanations.

“And by the night and what it gathers” suffices for the main text; encompassing can be clarified in the root explanation.

“And by the night and what it carries” connects with one lexical branch but gives a more physical and less natural image for a Persian reader.

“And by the night that hides everything” restricts gathering to concealment and changes the scope of mā wasaqa.

For Al-Inshiqāq 84:18, a suitable rendering is:

“And by the moon when it becomes full.”

It is short, clear, and natural.

An explanation can add:

“When the moon’s illuminated disk appears complete.”

That entire explanation need not be inserted into the basic translation.

“And by the moon when it becomes orderly” relates to the general sense of ittisāq but poorly conveys the specific lunar state here.

The Persian expression using badr is also precise, but “becomes full” is more accessible to a general reader.

Other equivalents in the family

Outside the Qur’an, وَسَقَ الشَّيْءَ can mean “he gathered it” or “carried it.”

وَسْق generally means a load, or in measurement the name of a particular unit.

أَوْسَقَ الْبَعِيرَ means “he loaded the camel.”

اسْتَوْسَقَتِ الْإِبِلُ means “the camels gathered” or “formed a coordinated group.”

اتَّسَقَ الْأَمْرُ means “the affair became orderly” or “its parts became coordinated.”

اتِّسَاق can be rendered as coordination, connectedness, or coherence, depending on whether the subject is arrangement, consistent discourse, or connections within a collection.

The family extends beyond one simple verb, and each derivative’s translation depends on construction and subject. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

8. Conclusion

Arabic w-s-q concerns gathering, encompassing, and carrying. Some derivatives also concern joining together and reaching order or completeness.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period illustrates animal pregnancy and keeping animals together, with the attribution reservations noted above.

Arabic sources record wasq for a load and, in a particular use, a measure for produce.

The historical order of these branches remains uncertain; one analogy cannot determine the origin of every meaning.

The root occurs twice in consecutive Qur’anic verses.

In Al-Inshiqāq 84:17, وَسَقَ concerns night and what it gathers or encompasses.

In Al-Inshiqāq 84:18, اتَّسَقَ concerns the moon when its illuminated disk appears complete and whole.

They belong to one semantic family without being fully synonymous.

The root can be defined as gathering or encompassing things in a collection, and in some constructions joining parts into order, coordination, and completeness.

وَسَقَ emphasizes gathering and encompassing.

اتَّسَقَ, particularly concerning the moon, emphasizes attaining the relevant fullness.

“What it gathers” for night and “when it becomes full” for the moon are precise, natural renderings.

The distinction preserves the lexical range and avoids two errors: turning night’s gathering into every creature physically relocating, and turning lunar fullness into a claim that the moon’s physical size or structure changes.

Night encompasses what falls within its reach; the moon follows its familiar cycle to a phase in which its illuminated disk appears full. The Qur’an places these familiar images beside twilight and human passage from state to state, without their lexical meanings requiring an added scientific or philosophical hypothesis.
