The Qur’anic Root R W Ḥ

Wind, breath, relief, and spirit

The root ر و ح is one of the Qur’an’s families with several branches. It includes rūḥ, rīḥ, riyāḥ, rawḥ, rayḥān, rawāḥ, and the verb turīḥūn. Although their English equivalents belong to different semantic fields, Arabic preserves an ancient connection between moving air, breath, scent, ease, and spirit.

One early lexical analysis explains this family’s core through spaciousness and relief, treating rīḥ as central to understanding the other branches. There is also an important morphological point: rīḥ belongs to the very same root, R W Ḥ. The yāʾ in rīḥ is not a separate root consonant. In this formation, the root’s medial wāw has become yāʾ because of the preceding kasrah. The resemblance between rūḥ and rīḥ is therefore not merely phonetic: they belong to one family.

This morphological relationship should not, however, be used to reconstruct the history of semantic development with certainty. To assert that “rūḥ must have been formed from rīḥ at a later stage” goes beyond the evidence itself. West Semitic languages also have very ancient forms that bring wind, breath, and spirit into one field. This distribution shows that the relationship between these concepts is very old and not confined to Islamic Arabic.

Qur’anic forms

The root R W Ḥ occurs 57 times in the Qur’an: rūḥ 21 times, rīḥ and riyāḥ 29 times, rawḥ three times, rayḥān twice, rawāḥ once, and turīḥūn once.

Rīḥ: from wind to strength and scent

The clearest physical branch of this root is rīḥ: air in motion. The Qur’an speaks of rīḥ ṭayyibah for a pleasant wind, rīḥ ʿāṣif for a stormy wind, and rīḥ ṣarṣar for a harsh, destructive wind. Taṣrīf ar-riyāḥ describes the circulation and changing of winds within the natural order.

These uses show that the familiar rule “singular rīḥ always means punishment, while plural riyāḥ always means mercy” is not absolute. The Qur’an itself says:

بِرِيحٍ طَيِّبَةٍ

Bi-rīḥin ṭayyibah.

“With a pleasant wind.”

Yūnus 10:22

Solomon’s wind also appears in the singular, ar-rīḥ. The word’s positive or negative force comes from its qualification and context, not from singular or plural number alone.

Rīḥ also extends beyond physical wind. Jacob says:

إِنِّي لَأَجِدُ رِيحَ يُوسُفَ

Innī la-ajidu rīḥa Yūsuf.

“I sense the scent of Joseph.”

Yūsuf 12:94

Here rīḥ is an effect carried by the air to the sense of smell. The same field extends in Arabic to rāʾiḥah and other words relating to scent.

Another branch appears in Al-Anfāl 8:46:

وَلَا تَنَازَعُوا فَتَفْشَلُوا وَتَذْهَبَ رِيحُكُمْ

Wa-lā tanāzaʿū fa-tafshalū wa-tadhhaba rīḥukum.

“Do not dispute, lest you lose resolve and your rīḥ depart.”

Rīḥukum here is not a group’s physical wind. Early Arabic also used rīḥ for a people’s strength, ascendancy, and flourishing position. A verse attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ reads:

كَمَا حَمَيْنَاكَ يَوْمَ النَّعْفِ مِنْ شَطَبٍ
وَالْفَضْلُ لِلْقَوْمِ مِنْ رِيحٍ وَمِنْ عَدَدِ

Kamā ḥamaynāka yawma n-naʿfi min Shaṭabin / wa-l-faḍlu li-l-qawmi min rīḥin wa-min ʿadad.

“A people’s superiority comes from their rīḥ and their numbers.”

This evidence shows that using rīḥ for strength and social ascendancy was known in early language. In Al-Anfāl, too, dispute leads first to fashal, loss of resolve, and then removes the group’s rīḥ. Renderings such as “strength,” “power,” or “driving force” explain the verse more clearly than “your wind,” although the original image remains behind the expression.

Rawḥ, rawāḥ, and rayḥān: relief and ease

The form rawḥ is not identical with rūḥ, pronounced with a ḍammah. In the account of Joseph, we read:

وَلَا تَيْأَسُوا مِنْ رَوْحِ اللَّهِ

Wa-lā tayʾasū min rawḥi llāh.

“Do not despair of relief from The God.”

Yūsuf 12:87

Here rawḥ is close to deliverance, ease, and relief that removes distress. The same verse says again that none but the disbelieving people despair of the rawḥ of The God.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:89, too:

فَرَوْحٌ وَرَيْحَانٌ وَجَنَّتُ نَعِيمٍ

Fa-rawḥun wa-rayḥānun wa-jannatu naʿīm.

“Then there are ease, rayḥān, and a garden of bliss.”

Rawḥ in this construction likewise fits the field of comfort and relief.

Rawāḥ in Sabaʾ 34:12 describes movement during the latter part of the day:

غُدُوُّهَا شَهْرٌ وَرَوَاحُهَا شَهْرٌ

Ghuduwwuhā shahrun wa-rawāḥuhā shahr.

“Its morning journey was a month, and its afternoon journey was a month.”

Turīḥūn in An-Naḥl 16:6 concerns bringing livestock back at the end of the day. These usages belong to ordinary daily life and show that R W Ḥ was not an exclusively theological family from the outset.

Rayḥān also occurs twice. In Ar-Raḥmān 55:12, it stands alongside grain and vegetation, with a sense close to fragrant plants. In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:89, its field is broader and has long been explained through provision, bounty, and fragrance. The precise rendering must be determined from the context of each verse.

Rūḥ: one word with several Qur’anic uses

The most important point about rūḥ is that the Qur’an does not use it for one single referent. Sometimes rūḥ appears in human creation; sometimes revelation itself is called rūḥ; sometimes it is a being who is sent and speaks. Elsewhere it appears in the titles Rūḥ al-Qudus or ar-Rūḥ al-Amīn, and in several verses ar-rūḥ is mentioned alongside the angels.

This diversity prevents us from immediately translating every occurrence of rūḥ as “the human soul,” “Gabriel,” or another fixed concept.

Concerning Adam’s creation, we read:

فَإِذَا سَوَّيْتُهُ وَنَفَخْتُ فِيهِ مِنْ رُوحِي

Fa-idhā sawwaytuhu wa-nafakhtu fīhi min rūḥī.

“Then, when I have fashioned him and breathed into him of My rūḥ.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:29

The same construction appears in Ṣād 38:72, while As-Sajdah 32:9 reads:

وَنَفَخَ فِيهِ مِنْ رُوحِهِ

Wa-nafakha fīhi min rūḥih.

“And He breathed into him of His rūḥ.”

Concerning Mary, too:

فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهَا مِنْ رُوحِنَا

Fa-nafakhnā fīhā min rūḥinā.

“Then We breathed into her of Our rūḥ.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:91

And concerning Jesus:

وَرُوحٌ مِنْهُ

Wa-rūḥun minh.

“And a rūḥ from Him.”

An-Nisāʾ 4:171

These relational expressions should not be understood physically. Min rūḥī or rūḥun minhu does not by itself say that part of the essence of The God separated and entered a created being. Elsewhere, the Qur’an also uses minhu for things that are from Him without being parts of His essence.

What these verses establish is a relationship between rūḥ and creation and life. The exact nature of that relationship is not explained by the root itself.

When revelation itself is called rūḥ

Ash-Shūrā 42:52 is among the clearest verses concerning this family:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ رُوحًا مِنْ أَمْرِنَا مَا كُنْتَ تَدْرِي مَا الْكِتَابُ وَلَا الْإِيمَانُ وَلَٰكِنْ جَعَلْنَاهُ نُورًا

Wa-kadhālika awḥaynā ilayka rūḥan min amrinā mā kunta tadrī mā l-kitābu wa-lā l-īmānu wa-lākin jaʿalnāhu nūrā.

“And thus We revealed to you a rūḥ from Our command. You did not know what the Book or faith was, but We made it a light…”

In this verse, rūḥ is something that is revealed, then connected with “light” through the pronoun in “We made it.” At least here, therefore, rūḥ cannot be restricted to a human soul or a spiritual being.

Ghāfir 40:15 says:

يُلْقِي الرُّوحَ مِنْ أَمْرِهِ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ لِيُنْذِرَ يَوْمَ التَّلَاقِ

Yulqī r-rūḥa min amrihi ʿalā man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi li-yundhira yawma t-talāq.

“He casts the rūḥ from His command upon whomever He wills of His servants, to warn of the Day of Meeting.”

And An-Naḥl 16:2:

يُنَزِّلُ الْمَلَائِكَةَ بِالرُّوحِ مِنْ أَمْرِهِ ... أَنْ أَنْذِرُوا

Yunazzilu l-malāʾikata bi-r-rūḥi min amrihi … an andhirū.

“He sends down the angels with the rūḥ from His command… that you should warn.”

The angels descend with a rūḥ from His command, and the mission in the verse is warning.

Together, these passages connect rūḥ directly with revelation and divine news. Revelation may be called rūḥ because, just as rūḥ is associated with life, revelation gives life and awakening. This, however, is an intelligible semantic connection, not an established history of how the name arose.

Rūḥ as a messenger

A different usage appears in Sūrat Maryam:

فَأَرْسَلْنَا إِلَيْهَا رُوحَنَا فَتَمَثَّلَ لَهَا بَشَرًا سَوِيًّا

Fa-arsalnā ilayhā rūḥanā fa-tamaththala lahā basharan sawiyyā.

“Then We sent Our rūḥ to her, and he appeared to her as a fully formed human being.”

Maryam 19:17

A few verses later, the same being says:

إِنَّمَا أَنَا رَسُولُ رَبِّكِ

Innamā anā rasūlu rabbik.

“I am only the Messenger of your Lord.”

Maryam 19:19

Here rūḥanā is neither an abstract concept nor revelation itself. It is a being who is sent, appears in human form, and speaks.

This usage alone does not establish his name. The Qur’an does not say “Gabriel” in this account, so a name that the verse does not supply here should not be inserted into the translation.

Ar-Rūḥ al-Amīn, Rūḥ al-Qudus, and Gabriel

Three passages provide very strong evidence within the Qur’an itself concerning the descent of the Qur’an.

Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:193–194 says:

نَزَلَ بِهِ الرُّوحُ الْأَمِينُ
عَلَىٰ قَلْبِكَ

Nazala bihi r-rūḥu l-amīn / ʿalā qalbik.

“The Trustworthy Spirit brought it down upon your heart.”

An-Naḥl 16:102 says:

قُلْ نَزَّلَهُ رُوحُ الْقُدُسِ مِنْ رَبِّكَ بِالْحَقِّ

Qul nazzalahu rūḥu l-qudusi min rabbika bi-l-ḥaqq.

“Say: Rūḥ al-Qudus brought it down from your Lord in truth.”

And Al-Baqarah 2:97:

فَإِنَّهُ نَزَّلَهُ عَلَىٰ قَلْبِكَ بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ

Fa-innahu nazzalahu ʿalā qalbika bi-idhni llāh.

“For he brought it down upon your heart by the permission of The God.”

Here Gabriel is explicitly identified as the agent.

In all three constructions, something is brought down. Two also explicitly name the “heart” as its destination, while Al-Baqarah names Gabriel as the agent. Comparing these passages provides very strong evidence connecting ar-Rūḥ al-Amīn and Rūḥ al-Qudus with Gabriel in the mission of bringing down the Qur’an.

This conclusion should not be extended to every use of rūḥ. Ash-Shūrā 42:52 calls revelation itself rūḥ, while Maryam 19:17 says only rūḥanā. Identifying ar-Rūḥ al-Amīn with Gabriel in the descent of the Qur’an does not define the entire root.

The expression Rūḥ al-Qudus must also be read in its own construction. Al-qudus and al-quddūs are not identical forms. Al-Quddūs is an independent description of The God; Rūḥ al-Qudus is a separate genitive construction.

Rūḥ al-Qudus is also connected with Jesus three times:

وَأَيَّدْنَاهُ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ

Wa-ayyadnāhu bi-rūḥi l-qudus.

“And We strengthened him with Rūḥ al-Qudus.”

Al-Baqarah 2:87 and 2:253

And:

إِذْ أَيَّدْتُكَ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ

Idh ayyadtuka bi-rūḥi l-qudus.

“When I strengthened you with Rūḥ al-Qudus.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:110

As seen in the study of the root ʾ Y D, the sentence’s construction says that Jesus was strengthened with Rūḥ al-Qudus. The expression does not by itself establish a theory of the nature of Rūḥ al-Qudus or its relationship to the essence of The God.

For the same reason, the developed Trinitarian conception of the “Holy Spirit” should not be the starting point for translating Qur’anic verses. We must first examine the roles in which the Qur’an itself uses this title.

Ar-rūḥ alongside the angels

In three verses, ar-rūḥ is separately mentioned alongside the angels:

تَعْرُجُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَالرُّوحُ إِلَيْهِ

Taʿruju l-malāʾikatu wa-r-rūḥu ilayh.

“The angels and ar-rūḥ ascend to Him.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:4

يَوْمَ يَقُومُ الرُّوحُ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ صَفًّا

Yawma yaqūmu r-rūḥu wa-l-malāʾikatu ṣaffā.

“On the day when ar-rūḥ and the angels stand in a row.”

An-Nabaʾ 78:38

تَنَزَّلُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَالرُّوحُ فِيهَا

Tanazzalu l-malāʾikatu wa-r-rūḥu fīhā.

“The angels and ar-rūḥ descend therein.”

Al-Qadr 97:4

This coordination shows that rūḥ has a distinct place in these scenes, but does not by itself prove that it is not an angel. Arabic may separately mention a prominent member after the group to which that member belongs.

On the other hand, these verses do not explicitly call rūḥ Gabriel either. Its precise nature should therefore remain open in the absence of stronger evidence.

“They ask you about ar-rūḥ”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:85 says:

وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الرُّوحِ قُلِ الرُّوحُ مِنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّي وَمَا أُوتِيتُمْ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

Wa-yasʾalūnaka ʿani r-rūḥi quli r-rūḥu min amri rabbī wa-mā ūtītum mina l-ʿilmi illā qalīlā.

“They ask you about ar-rūḥ. Say: Ar-rūḥ is from the command of my Lord, and you have been given only a little knowledge.”

The verse itself does not say that ar-rūḥ means the human spirit. Within the same Qur’an, this word is also used for revelation, a being who is sent, and in other constructions.

The expression:

الرُّوحُ مِنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّي

Ar-rūḥu min amri rabbī.

“Ar-rūḥ is from the command of my Lord.”

This expression also belongs to a network of similar expressions:

رُوحًا مِنْ أَمْرِنَا

Rūḥan min amrinā.

“A rūḥ from Our command.”

Ash-Shūrā 42:52

يُلْقِي الرُّوحَ مِنْ أَمْرِهِ

Yulqī r-rūḥa min amrih.

“He casts the rūḥ from His command.”

Ghāfir 40:15

يُنَزِّلُ الْمَلَائِكَةَ بِالرُّوحِ مِنْ أَمْرِهِ

Yunazzilu l-malāʾikata bi-r-rūḥi min amrih.

“He sends down the angels with the rūḥ from His command.”

An-Naḥl 16:2

The last two verses clearly concern warning and revelation, while Ash-Shūrā calls rūḥ something that has been revealed. This resemblance makes a connection between ar-rūḥ in Al-Isrāʾ and divine command and revelation a serious possibility, preventing its restriction to “the human soul” without explanation.

The immediate context in Al-Isrāʾ strengthens this possibility. Before the question about rūḥ, the Qur’an speaks of the Qur’an itself as healing and mercy:

وَنُنَزِّلُ مِنَ الْقُرْآنِ...

Wa-nunazzilu mina l-qurʾān…

“And We send down of the Qur’an…”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82

Immediately after the verse about rūḥ, we read:

وَلَئِنْ شِئْنَا لَنَذْهَبَنَّ بِالَّذِي أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ

Wa-laʾin shiʾnā la-nadhhabanna bi-lladhī awḥaynā ilayk.

“If We will, We shall take away what We have revealed to you.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:86

A few verses later, the discussion again concerns this Qur’an and the inability of human beings and jinn to produce its like.

This context makes “revelation” one of the important readings of verse 85. Yet because the Qur’an also uses rūḥ for a being who is sent, the word alone should not be treated as settling the verse’s referent.

“You have been given only a little knowledge”

The continuation of the verse must also be read carefully. The Qur’an does not say:

“You have only a little knowledge about rūḥ.”

It says:

وَمَا أُوتِيتُمْ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

Wa-mā ūtītum mina l-ʿilmi illā qalīlā.

“And you have been given only a little knowledge.”

Its position after the question about rūḥ connects this sentence with the answer. In its wording, however, the limitation is expressed generally about al-ʿilm, knowledge. Turning it into the technical statement “the nature of the human spirit is forever unknowable” adds something beyond the Qur’an’s expression.

Nor does qalīlan, “a little,” mean “nothing.” The verse states a limit to human knowledge, not a complete denial of the possibility of knowing.

The identity of the questioners

The Qur’an says only:

وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ

Wa-yasʾalūnak.

“And they ask you.”

No community is named in the verse.

External narratives have proposed specific identities for the questioners. Some reports place the question about rūḥ together with questions about the Companions of the Cave and Dhū al-Qarnayn in a single episode. This information does not come from the Qur’an itself.

Nor does the Qur’an present these three questions as one test posed by a single group. The accounts of the Companions of the Cave and Dhū al-Qarnayn occur in Sūrat al-Kahf, while the question about rūḥ occurs in Sūrat al-Isrāʾ. A historical connection between them must be established separately.

This does not establish another group’s identity either. The fact that the story of the Companions of the Cave was known in a Christian setting before the Qur’an does not by itself prove that the questioners in Al-Isrāʾ were Christians. Equally, the presence of the concept of “spirit” in Jewish tradition does not prevent a Christian from asking about it.

The conclusion permitted by the Qur’an itself is more limited and more secure:

The text does not identify the questioners.

For understanding rūḥ in this verse, the Qur’an’s own usages provide more direct evidence than reconstructing the questioners’ identity.

The root in Semitic languages and its distinction from nafs

In West Semitic languages, a family very close to R W Ḥ is known for wind, breath, and spirit. In Hebrew, this family can express wind, the breath of life, the human spirit, an inner state, and divine spirit. Aramaic and Syriac likewise have cognate forms in the field of wind and spirit.

This overlap shows that the connection between wind, breath, and spirit is very ancient and did not arise from the theology of later periods.

It should not, however, be extended incautiously to all Semitic languages. Akkadian has other common words for wind and breath, and does not show a clear R W Ḥ cognate in this field in the West Semitic pattern. The available evidence therefore supports, above all, a very ancient network within the West Semitic branches.

This comparative evidence does not determine the meaning of each Qur’anic verse either. A word covering wind and spirit in a related language establishes only the antiquity and historical semantic field.

The same care is needed in distinguishing rūḥ and nafs. The Qur’an does not treat them as one word.

It says:

كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَائِقَةُ الْمَوْتِ

Kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu l-mawt.

“Every nafs shall taste death.”

And:

اللَّهُ يَتَوَفَّى الْأَنْفُسَ حِينَ مَوْتِهَا

Allāhu yatawaffā l-anfusa ḥīna mawtihā.

“The God takes the souls at the time of their death.”

Az-Zumar 39:42

Responsibility, earning, death, and reckoning are repeatedly attributed to nafs. Rūḥ, by contrast, appears in another network involving creation, revelation, the messenger of revelation, and Rūḥ al-Qudus.

This difference does not prove that rūḥ and nafs have no relationship. It simply prevents us from treating one as the definition of the other without evidence.

Translation guide

Rīḥ / riyāḥ usually means “wind / winds.” Rīḥ Yūsuf in Yūsuf 12:94 means “the scent of Joseph,” while tadhhaba rīḥukum in Al-Anfāl 8:46 expresses the loss of a group’s strength and power.

Rawḥ in Yūsuf 12:87 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:89 is close to relief and ease, and should not be equated with rūḥ.

Rawāḥ is movement during the latter part of the day, and turīḥūn concerns bringing livestock back at that time.

Rayḥān, depending on context, relates to fragrant plants, fragrance, provision, and bounty.

There is no single equivalent for rūḥ that can replace it in every verse. In many cases, retaining rūḥ and explaining its role in context is more precise.

Ash-Shūrā 42:52 places rūḥ in the field of revelation; Maryam 19:17 presents it as a being who is sent; Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:193 and An-Naḥl 16:102 speak of the bearer of revelation. The creation verses connect it with the emergence of life, while in Al-Maʿārij, An-Nabaʾ, and Al-Qadr, ar-rūḥ appears alongside the angels.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:85, too, it is better to retain the expression:

الرُّوحُ مِنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّي

Ar-rūḥu min amri rabbī.

“Ar-rūḥ is from the command of my Lord.”

The wording should be retained in this form, with the referent of rūḥ examined in the explanation. Translating it as “the human soul” before examining the context assumes that part of the question has already been settled.

Summary

R W Ḥ is a very ancient family that connects moving air, scent, relief, and spirit in Arabic. The relationship between rūḥ and rīḥ is not merely semantic: morphologically, rīḥ belongs to this very root, its medial wāw becoming yāʾ in the word’s formation. The early lexical tradition even attempted to explain all these branches through the movement of wind and relief. Yet the actual history of semantic development cannot be established from a lexical analysis alone.

Early poetry also shows that, before the Qur’an, rīḥ extended beyond air in motion to the strength and superiority of a people. The same field is visible in wa-tadhhaba rīḥukum.

Hebrew, Aramaic, and Syriac evidence shows that the connection between wind, breath, and spirit is very old in West Semitic. Akkadian evidence, however, prevents this pattern from being extended incautiously to the entire Semitic family.

Within the Qur’an itself, the most important conclusion is that rūḥ has no single fixed referent. Sometimes it is connected with life and creation; sometimes revelation itself is called rūḥ. At other times it is a being who is sent and speaks, while titles such as ar-Rūḥ al-Amīn and Rūḥ al-Qudus appear in the mission of conveying revelation.

Comparing Al-Baqarah 2:97, An-Naḥl 16:102, and Ash-Shuʿarāʾ 26:193 provides very strong evidence that Gabriel, Rūḥ al-Qudus, and ar-Rūḥ al-Amīn refer to one messenger in the role of bringing down the Qur’an. This conclusion should not turn every occurrence of rūḥ into Gabriel.

Nor should Rūḥ al-Qudus be defined through developed Trinitarian theology. The Qur’an must first be read through its own network of vocabulary. Al-qudus is not the same form as al-quddūs, and the verses present Rūḥ al-Qudus in the descent of revelation and the strengthening of Jesus, without constructing a complete philosophical theory of his nature.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:85 must also be read within this network. Ar-rūḥu min amri rabbī has clear parallels in the expressions rūḥan min amrinā and ar-rūḥa min amrihi in verses about revelation, while the surrounding context concerns the Qur’an and revelation. This evidence makes the revelation reading a serious possibility and prevents restricting rūḥ to the human soul without explanation.

The continuation does not say “you know only a little about rūḥ.” It says that you have been given only a little knowledge. The sentence recalls the limits of human understanding; it does not present a complete theory of the unknowability of the nature of rūḥ.

Finally, the Qur’an does not identify those who asked about rūḥ. Attributing the question to Jews, Christians, or any particular group requires evidence outside the verse. It should not be inserted into the translation or definition of the root as information supplied by the Qur’an itself.

A cautious conclusion for this root is:

R W Ḥ: an ancient family in the field of wind, moving air, relief, and spirit. Rīḥ belongs morphologically to this root, whose Qur’anic branches extend from wind and scent to strength, ease, revelation, and rūḥ. Rūḥ in the Qur’an does not designate one single referent. Its precise reference is determined by the construction and context of each verse; the human soul, Gabriel, revelation, or a later theological conception should not be imposed on every occurrence.
