The root ه ي م — Wandering and extreme thirst

Qur’anic and lexical forms

يَهِيمُونَ
(yahīmūna)
They wander aimlessly

الْهِيمِ
(al-hīm)
Intensely thirsty camels

From aimless wandering to unquenchable thirst and attachment that overwhelms self-possession

The Arabic root ه ي م concerns wandering, movement without clear direction, extreme thirst, and restless attachment. Hāma yahīmu can describe someone wandering without knowing where to go. With camels, the family concerns intense thirst that causes restlessness and may remain unquenched despite abundant drinking.

It occurs twice in the Qur’an, in different forms. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225 describes poets as yahīmūna, wandering in every valley. Al-Wāqiʿa 56:55 uses al-hīm, intensely thirsty camels, to portray the drinking of the people of the Fire.

A connection is imaginable: thirst, attachment, or disturbance may make someone restless and directionless. The family’s meanings should nevertheless not all be reduced to one mental or bodily condition.

The first Qur’anic passage emphasizes wandering in movement and speech; the second, intense thirst and abundant drinking. Each requires its own contextual reading.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Classical hāma yahīmu denotes wandering aimlessly and straying from one path to another.

Hāma ʿalā wajhihi describes someone roaming without a known destination or clear route.

Hāʾim is a bewildered wanderer who has lost direction.

Fear, love, thirst, disturbance, or another cause may produce this condition. The verb alone does not specify its cause.

Poetry attributed to ʿAntara ibn Shaddād

A passage attributed to the pre-Islamic poet and warrior ʿAntara ibn Shaddād says:

«سَلُوا عَنَّا جُهَيْنَةَ كَيْفَ بَاتَتْ
تَهِيمُ مِنَ الْمَخَافَةِ فِي رُبَاهَا»

Salū ʿannā Juhayna kayfa bātat / tahīmu mina al-makhāfati fī rubāhā.

“Ask Juhayna how it spent the night, wandering in fear among its heights.”

The following verse continues:

«رَأَتْ طَعْنِي فَوَلَّتْ وَاسْتَقَلَّتْ
وَسُمْرُ الْخَطِّ تَعْمَلُ فِي قَفَاهَا»

Raʾat ṭaʿnī fa-wallat wa-istaqallat / wa-sumru al-khaṭṭi taʿmalu fī qafāhā.

“It saw my spear-thrusts, then turned and fled, while the brown spears of al-Khaṭṭ worked against its rear.”

Tahīmu here describes wandering caused by fear.

Danger and the pressure of battle deprive the group of calm and direction.

Mina al-makhāfati specifies the cause. Without this qualification, tahīmu would not necessarily mean “to fear.”

The distinction matters: the root can convey disordered, directionless movement, while surrounding expressions identify fear, love, or thirst.

The passage occurs in the poetry attributed to ʿAntara. Its attribution is qualified in literary collections, so neither its precise date nor its authorship should be treated as certain.

It helps illustrate the word’s traditional usage, but the poet’s name alone does not establish pre-Qur’anic authenticity.

Extreme thirst in Arabic

Haymān and ahyam describe a very thirsty person or animal.

Haymāʾ is the feminine of ahyam, and hīm can be its plural.

Camels were central to pastoral life; their thirst, illnesses, and movements were described through varied vocabulary.

One condition was called al-huyām, described in early lexicography as a camel illness or disorder involving severe thirst, restlessness, and failure to become satiated despite drinking.

Thirst here exceeds an ordinary need for water and becomes severe and abnormal.

Huyām is a traditional name for a condition or illness. Equating it exactly with a modern veterinary diagnosis would require independent historical and veterinary research.

A verse by Labīd ibn Rabīʿa

Labīd, who lived before and after Islam, describes a difficult journey:

«أَجَزْتُ إِلَى مَعَارِفِهَا بِشُعْثٍ
وَأَطْلَاحٍ مِنَ الْعِيدِيِّ هِيمِ»

Ajaztu ilā maʿārifihā bi-shuʿthin / wa-aṭlāḥin mina al-ʿīdiyyi hīmi.

“I crossed toward its recognizable landmarks with disheveled travelers and exhausted, intensely thirsty ʿĪdī camels.”

Hīm qualifies the camels.

The poet depicts difficult travel, heat, fatigue, and camels suffering thirst and exhaustion.

Aṭlāḥ conveys their weariness and weakness; hīm emphasizes thirst.

The descriptions differ. A long journey can exhaust a camel without extreme thirst, though severe thirst may contribute to exhaustion.

The verse is recorded in Labīd’s poetry and cited lexically for hīm. Its date is not independently known: his lifetime spanning both periods does not prove this verse predates the Qur’an.

Alongside the Qur’an itself, it illustrates the recognized sense of intensely thirsty camels.

Huyām in love and attachment

Huyām also describes an intense love or attachment that unsettles a person’s composure.

Hāma bi-fulānatin means becoming deeply enamored of a woman, with heart and thought absorbed in her.

Rajulun hāʾimun can describe a man bewildered by love.

Mustahāmu al-fuʾādi likewise describes a heart deprived of peace through intense attachment.

The Umayyad poet Kuthayyir ʿAzza says:

«وَإِنِّيَ قَدْ أَبْلَلْتُ مِنْ دَنَفٍ بِهَا
كَمَا أَدْنَفَتْ هَيْمَاءُ ثُمَّ اسْتَبَلَّتِ»

Wa-inniya qad ablaltu min danafin bihā / kamā adnafat haymāʾu thumma istaballati.

“I have recovered from the wasting pain of love for her, as a she-camel stricken with huyām becomes ill and then recovers.”

The poet compares his suffering and attachment to a camel’s illness and thirst.

The comparison illustrates a familiar imaginative connection between the haymāʾ camel and a person overwhelmed by love.

Kuthayyir lived after revelation. His poem illustrates Umayyad usage rather than supplying pre-Islamic evidence.

The simile does not make human love literally the camel’s bodily illness. Shared restlessness and suffering explain the poetic comparison.

Is thirst the origin of every meaning?

Ibn Fāris places extreme thirst at the family’s center and relates a lover’s wandering to thirsty camels’ restlessness.

The semantic explanation is understandable.

A thirsty camel may roam in search of water; fear, attachment, or turmoil may likewise deprive a person of direction.

This resemblance does not prove that wandering historically developed after the thirst sense.

Hāma yahīmu also has an independently established use for wandering.

Both principal branches should therefore be retained: extreme thirst and wandering movement.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

The root occurs twice in two forms:

  • Yahīmūna — al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225: an imperfect basic-stem verb, “they wander,” moving aimlessly from path to path.
  • Al-hīm — al-Wāqiʿa 56:55: a plural denoting intensely thirsty camels, in a comparison describing drinking.

Hāma yahīmu is a hollow verb with yāʾ as its second root consonant.

Yahīmūna is active imperfect, third-person masculine plural; its wāw marks the plural subject.

Hīm is a noun, counted in camel-related usage as the plural of ahyam and haymāʾ.

Other recorded forms include:

Hāma, yahīmu, haym, huyām, hayamān, hāʾim, haymān, haymāʾ, hayūm, mahyūm, hayyama, tahayyama, and mustahām.

Only yahīmūna and al-hīm occur in the Qur’an.

Hīm and huyām

Hīm in al-Wāqiʿa identifies intensely thirsty camels.

Huyām is recorded for extreme thirst and the traditionally named camel illness.

It also denotes infatuation and intense love.

Al-hīm should not therefore be translated “lovers” here, even though the family has love-related uses elsewhere.

Nor should the camel description be restricted to one disease: the descriptive meaning exceeds a veterinary diagnosis.

3. Yahīmūna: wandering in every valley

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225

«أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّهُمْ فِي كُلِّ وَادٍ يَهِيمُونَ»

A-lam tara annahum fī kulli wādin yahīmūn.

“Have you not seen that they wander in every valley?”

The preceding verse describes poets followed by those who stray.

The next verse describes people saying what they do not do.

Yahīmūna thus contributes to criticism of the words and conduct of a group of poets.

Fī kulli wādin: every valley and path

Wād ordinarily denotes a valley or natural passage between heights.

The verse is not describing poets literally traveling through every valley on earth.

The expression pictures movement along many different paths.

Imagine someone without a clear destination moving from valley to valley, turning right and left, continually changing course.

The picture illustrates someone likewise shifting in speech and thought without a stable criterion.

In this context, valleys can represent different fields of speech, praise, blame, and conflicting claims.

This figurative meaning arises from the whole sentence and context. The root itself does not mean composing poetry or changing subjects.

Yahīmūna: directionless movement

Hāma yahīmu may describe movement without a destination or a person losing the way through disturbance.

It differs from purposeful travel.

Someone may cross several valleys while knowing the route and destination. Crossing valleys alone does not make that orderly movement wandering.

Someone else may drift from path to path without direction.

This is closer to yahīmūna.

Fī kulli wādin accentuates that wandering.

“They wander in every valley” preserves both the Arabic picture and its directionless quality.

Its relationship to the poets’ speech

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:226 describes saying things the speakers themselves do not do.

This clarifies the criticized conduct.

Someone might praise a person for a benefit received today and condemn the same person tomorrow when that benefit is lost.

Or claim courage, generosity, and justice while behaving otherwise.

Speech then follows personal wishes and interests from path to path, rather than truth and consistent conduct.

These examples illuminate wandering in every valley.

They do not establish that every poetic change of subject or imaginative expression exemplifies the criticized yahīmūna.

A poet can discuss nature, love, justice, war, and life honestly and responsibly.

Variety and imagination alone are not this blameworthy wandering.

Does the Qur’an condemn all poets?

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:227 excepts those who believe, act rightly, remember God often, and defend themselves after suffering injustice.

The exception is decisive.

Verse 26:225 cannot make every poet misguided or aimless merely for being a poet.

Nor does it make imaginative poetry, or speech other than direct factual reporting, inherently false or contrary to the Qur’an.

The verses concern speech’s relationship to truth, action, and moral responsibility.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225 supplies the wandering image; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:226 describes discord between word and deed; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:227 excepts believing poets who do good.

The lexical meaning is wandering; its applications to the criticized poets emerge from these verses together.

4. Al-hīm: intensely thirsty camels

Al-Wāqiʿa 56:55

«فَشَارِبُونَ شُرْبَ الْهِيمِ»

Fa-shāribūna shurba al-hīm.

“Then you will drink as intensely thirsty camels drink.”

Al-hīm denotes camels suffering intense thirst.

A very thirsty camel can be ahyam, a female haymāʾ; their plural is hīm.

Lexical accounts particularly describe abnormal, severe thirst unquenched despite abundant drinking.

The picture exceeds an animal’s ordinary drinking after thirst: it emphasizes avidity and intensity.

The context of drinking boiling water

Al-Wāqiʿa 56:52 mentions eating zaqqūm.

Al-Wāqiʿa 56:53 describes filling bellies with it.

Al-Wāqiʿa 56:54 then mentions drinking boiling water over it.

Al-Wāqiʿa 56:55 compares the manner of drinking to intensely thirsty camels.

This sequence matters.

Al-ḥamīm in the preceding verse identifies the hot, boiling water.

Al-hīm here portrays the manner of drinking.

The words should not be confused.

Ḥamīm identifies what is drunk; hīm supplies the comparison clarifying the drinking’s intensity.

Shurba al-hīm: drinking like thirsty camels

Shurb is the verbal noun for drinking.

It is accusative here and specifies the kind of drinking.

Al-hīm is its genitive complement: drinking like that of these camels.

The comparison invokes animals whose extreme thirst was familiar.

Imagine a camel reaching water after severe thirst and drinking urgently and intensely.

Lexical accounts go beyond ordinary thirst: these camels may keep drinking without becoming satiated.

The simile makes intense drinking and the absence of ordinary relief imaginable.

The word alone does not establish that a person in the Fire literally has the camel’s illness.

The Qur’an compares drinking to a familiar animal condition. Human biology in the hereafter or a specific diagnosis should not be deduced from the comparison without evidence.

Can hīm also denote sand?

Arabic dictionaries record another sense.

Hiyām describes very fine, dry sand that absorbs water without retaining it.

Some early explanations accordingly understand al-hīm in 56:55 as sand into which water disappears, as though no amount could satiate it.

The picture is understandable: both parched camels and dry sand can take in abundant water.

Two distinctions remain important.

First, sand does not literally drink; it absorbs water. This interpretation requires figurative drinking.

Second, lexical evidence and Labīd’s poetry directly support thirsty camels. This makes them the clearer, more natural basis for translation.

Sand can be discussed as another lexical usage and interpretive possibility without giving it equal standing in the main translation without justification.

Camel huyām and failure to become satiated

Lexical explanations describe a camel so thirsty that abundant water does not satiate it.

This property matters in the Qur’anic comparison.

Ordinarily, drinking an appropriate amount relieves a person’s thirst.

The hīm picture instead evokes drinking that need not bring rest or satisfy the need.

The drinker’s condition therefore matters alongside the amount drunk.

This follows recorded Arabic usage, rather than a scientific theory about camel diseases.

The simile does not specify unlimited drinking time or any numerical volume.

It conveys intensity, leaving duration, quantity, and mechanism unspecified.

5. Wandering and thirst within the family

The two Qur’anic uses appear to concern different subjects.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225 foregrounds movement and wandering.

Al-Wāqiʿa 56:55 foregrounds severe thirst and abundant drinking.

Arabic nevertheless recognizes a connection between these branches.

A severely thirsty camel may roam restlessly in search of water. Intense attachment can likewise absorb a person until calm and direction are lost.

Some dictionaries therefore explain the lover’s wandering through the hīm camel’s thirst.

This connection illuminates the semantic field.

It does not make yahīmūna mean “the poets are thirsty,” or al-hīm mean “wandering people” in al-Wāqiʿa.

Both branches are established, but each Qur’anic occurrence has its particular use.

Nor can one confidently assert that pre-Islamic Arabic first formed the thirst sense and then wandering. That requires independent historical evidence for the order of development.

Haymāʾ: waterless desert

Haymāʾ is also recorded for waterless desert terrain.

It is semantically close to thirst and wandering.

Without water or clear guidance, a traveler may become thirsty and lose the way.

Here haymāʾ describes terrain rather than a person’s love or anxiety.

The usage shows the root’s connections with thirst, travel, and difficulty finding a route in pastoral life.

This resemblance alone cannot establish the precise historical origin of the geographical term.

6. Distinctions from neighboring roots

ه ي م and ت ي ه: wandering and losing the way

ت ي ه concerns wandering, losing one’s way, and difficulty finding a route.

Al-Māʾida 5:26 describes wandering in the land.

ه ي م in al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225 likewise concerns aimless movement.

The roots are close without having identical ranges.

Haym also concerns intense thirst and romantic attachment, whereas route-related tīh more directly emphasizes losing the way.

In al-Shuʿarāʾ, haym accompanies “every valley,” picturing aimlessness in speech.

“Wandering” may translate both without merging their histories and derivatives.

ه ي م and ض ل ل: wandering and misguidance

Qur’anic ḍalāl concerns losing the way, departing from the right path, and often doctrinal or behavioral misguidance.

Lexical haym concerns wandering movement and need not pronounce a specific moral or doctrinal judgment.

Fear or thirst may cause wandering without moral wrongdoing.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225 is critical because it concerns poets whose speech and conduct conflict with truth and responsibility.

Its negative force emerges from the context, rather than the root always meaning disbelief or misguidance.

ه ي م and ظ م أ: thirst and intense thirst

ظ م أ denotes thirst in the Qur’an.

Ṭā Hā 20:119 describes freedom from thirst in the garden.

Al-Tawba 9:120 includes thirst in God’s cause among believers’ hardships that do not go unrewarded.

Camel-related hīm emphasizes abnormally severe thirst and abundant drinking.

Ẓamaʾ can denote thirst itself; hīm in 56:55 supplies the particular picture of intensely thirsty camels.

This does not mean ẓamaʾ can never be severe: intensity depends on context.

ه ي م and ع ط ش: the need for water and its severe form

ʿAṭash means thirst in Arabic.

Camel-related hīm denotes very severe thirst, especially when abundant drinking may fail to relieve it.

This use is more specific than ordinary thirst.

Not every thirsty camel is necessarily hīm, and severe human thirst should not automatically be equated with the traditional camel disease.

ʿAṭash itself does not occur in the Qur’an. This comparison concerns Arabic meanings, rather than two Qur’anic derivatives of one family.

ه ي م and ه و ي: attachment and bewilderment

ه و ي concerns desire and inclination in Arabic and the Qur’an, sometimes personal wishes and impulses.

The love-related branch of ه ي م likewise concerns intense attachment.

Not every liking or desire produces bewilderment.

A person may love something while pursuing it with a clear aim and considered conduct.

Literary huyām describes attachment so intense that it unsettles composure and direction.

Hawā and huyām may be related without being exact synonyms.

ه ي م and ه ي ل: absorbent sand and cascading sand

The roots resemble one another in letters but remain distinct.

ه ي ل appears as mahīlan in al-Muzzammil 73:14, describing loose, cascading sand.

One lexical use of hīm concerns fine, dry sand that absorbs water.

Both families may describe sand from different perspectives.

Mahīl conveys grains falling and moving.

Sand-related hīm emphasizes dryness and absorption.

A shared subject does not make the roots identical.

ه ي م and ه ي م ن: separate families

ه ي م ن occurs in forms such as muhayminan.

It concerns oversight, witness, and guardianship.

Qur’anic ه ي م concerns wandering and intense thirst.

Shared initial letters do not justify deriving guardianship from thirst or wandering.

The Quran root index accordingly treats them separately.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

A single fixed equivalent is insufficient for the two different Qur’anic uses.

Yahīmūna — al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225

Suitable equivalents include “they wander,” “they roam aimlessly,” and “they stray from path to path.”

Suggested translation:

«أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّهُمْ فِي كُلِّ وَادٍ يَهِيمُونَ»

A-lam tara annahum fī kulli wādin yahīmūn.

“Have you not seen that they wander in every valley?”

This preserves the Arabic picture and directionless movement.

A lexical note can add:

“Yahīmūna: they wander without direction; here, a picture of scattered, unstable speech among a group of poets.”

“They are misguided” alone substitutes a doctrinal concept for the wandering image.

“They jump from topic to topic” can illustrate the contextual application but removes the valley picture if used as a direct translation.

Al-hīm — al-Wāqiʿa 56:55

“Intensely thirsty camels” is suitable.

Suggested translation:

«فَشَارِبُونَ شُرْبَ الْهِيمِ»

Fa-shāribūna shurba al-hīm.

“Then you will drink as intensely thirsty camels drink.”

This preserves the word’s clear, established sense.

A lexical note can add:

“Al-hīm: camels suffering extreme thirst, which in familiar Arabic usage may remain unquenched even after abundant drinking.”

To clarify the picture’s intensity further:

“Like camels that keep drinking in extreme thirst without becoming satiated.”

This longer explanation need not replace the brief verse translation.

The interpretation “you drink like dry sand,” though recorded lexically and exegetically, requires more figurative explanation than thirsty camels.

The camel reading is therefore preferred for the main translation.

Non-Qur’anic equivalents should likewise follow the subject.

Hāma ʿalā wajhihi: “He wandered aimlessly.”

Hāma bihā: “He became infatuated with her.”

Rajulun hāʾimun can mean “a wandering man,” or in a love context, “a restless, lovestruck man.”

Nāqatun haymāʾu describes an intensely thirsty she-camel.

Geographical haymāʾ denotes a waterless desert.

The root study should preserve these distinctions.

8. Synthesis

ه ي م has two major branches: directionless wandering and very severe thirst.

Of people, hāma yahīmu can denote wandering movement or inward agitation caused by fear, love, or something else.

Of camels, hīm describes extreme thirst that lexical accounts say may persist despite abundant drinking.

The poem attributed to ʿAntara illustrates fear-driven wandering; Labīd’s verse records hīm for parched camels. Their precise dates are uncertain, and claims should remain within their historical support.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225 depicts some poets wandering through different valleys, connected contextually with discord between word and deed. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:227 shows that the criticism does not include every poet.

Al-Wāqiʿa 56:55 compares the people of the Fire’s intense drinking to parched camels. Al-hīm neither names boiling water nor independently determines human bodily conditions in the hereafter.

Forqan definition: ه ي م denotes wandering without direction and, in another branch, extreme thirst. Qur’anic yahīmūna conveys aimless roaming; al-hīm denotes camels whose drinking exemplifies intensity and failure to become satiated.

Restlessness and disturbance of an ordinary state provide an imaginable connection, but do not establish the historical development of all senses.

The root alone does not determine moral value. Danger or thirst may make someone lose the way innocently; unstable speech and disregard for truth can instead exemplify the criticized wandering in every valley.

Intensity matters in both Qur’anic uses: directionless movement through many valleys, and intense drinking with thirst not readily relieved. Distinguishing them preserves each verse’s meaning without reducing the entire family to love, thirst, or misguidance.
