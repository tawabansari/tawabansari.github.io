The Qur’anic root ف ن د (fanida)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

تُفَنِّدُونِ
(tufannidūni)
You accuse me of unsound judgment; Yūsuf 12:94

فَنَد، فَنِدَ، فَنَّدَ
(fanad; fanida; fannada)
Unsound judgment; weakening in judgment; considering judgment unsound

Unsound judgment, erroneous speech, and attributing error to another’s words: from accusations of poor judgment to recognizing truth in Jacob’s story

1. The root in early Arabic

One principal branch of the Arabic root ف ن د concerns error and weakness in judgment and speech. Fanad may designate false speech, mistaken judgment, or a weakness that calls someone’s words and discernment into question.

Attested classical usages have several related meanings: error in speech or opinion, attributing falsehood to someone’s words, reproaching them for poor judgment, and impaired discernment sometimes associated with old age or illness.

فَنِدَ الرَّجُلُ describes someone whose judgment or discernment has weakened. أَفْنَدَ الرَّجُلُ also sometimes concerns the development of such weakness. فَنَّدَ رَأْيَهُ means considering someone’s opinion unsound or mistaken; tafnīd can involve reproach and questioning the soundness of a judgment.

Making a mistake, being accused of error, and losing cognitive capacity are different things. Someone may say something false without losing their ability to reason. They may also speak truthfully while others mistakenly consider their judgment unsound.

This distinction matters for the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

A witness from pre-Qur’anic poetry

A poem attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī, the renowned pre-Islamic poet, contains this line:

إِذًا فَعَاقَبَنِي رَبِّي مُعَاقَبَةً

قَرَّتْ بِهَا عَيْنُ مَنْ يَأْتِيكَ بِالْفَنَدِ

Idhan faʿāqabanī rabbī muʿāqabatan / qarrat bihā ʿaynu man yaʾtīka bi-l-fanadi.

“Then may my Lord punish me with a punishment that delights whoever brings you false speech.”

The line belongs to a passage in which the poet defends himself before al-Nuʿmān ibn al-Mundhir. Hurt by others’ statements about him, he seeks to show that their accusations are false.

Here al-fanad concerns false speech and allegations brought to someone to damage another person’s reputation.

The example shows that this semantic field was not confined to weakness associated with aging. Its focus is false speech and the harm that unfounded allegations can cause.

The line is transmitted in a well-known poem by al-Nābighah, and its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet has an established literary basis. Yet, as with other early poems preserved through later transmission and compiled manuscripts, the poet’s reputation alone cannot establish every line’s date and wording with certainty.

Together with the Qur’anic usage, this witness helps explain early fanad in the domain of error, false speech, and undermining the credibility of words.

Unsound judgment and its association with old age

Classical Arabic sometimes associates fanad with kharaf: impaired or weakened discernment that may occur in old age or illness.

شَيْخٌ مُفَنَّدٌ can describe an elderly man whose judgment and discernment have weakened.

This association does not make old age and fanad identical.

Old age is a stage of life; fanad in this branch concerns impaired discernment or unsound opinion. An elderly person can remain wise and capable of sound judgment.

Lexical accounts also distinguish physical weakness from weakness of judgment. Someone may be physically weak yet judge soundly, or physically strong yet make mistakes in judgment.

Fanad should therefore not always be translated as “old age” or “dementia.”

Other branches of the family

Arabic also records find, with an initial i vowel, for a large projecting part of a mountain or a great mountain. Its plural is afnād. Related forms also occur for separate divisions and groups.

The epithet al-Find al-Zimmānī, borne by a renowned pre-Islamic Arab warrior, is also explained in this connection.

Associating a mountain’s bulk and weight with the weight of reproach or the difficulty of weakness does not establish a shared historical origin for these senses. All branches should not be traced to one physical image without evidence.

For Qur’anic تُفَنِّدُونِ, the branch concerning error, unsound judgment, and attributing falsehood to speech has more direct support.

2. Related forms and Qur’anic usage

Important Arabic forms include فَنَد, فَنِدَ, أَفْنَدَ, فَنَّدَ, تَفْنِيد, إِفْنَاد, and مُفَنَّد.

These forms do not all perform the same function.

Fanad can name error or unsound judgment. Fanida and afnada sometimes concern the development of such weakness. Transitive fannada means considering another person’s judgment or speech unsound, mistaken, or unreliable.

In the Qur’an, however, this family occurs only once, in a single verbal form:

تُفَنِّدُونِ — Yūsuf 12:94

This is a form II verb in the construction لَوْلَا أَنْ تُفَنِّدُونِ.

Morphologically, it is a second-person plural imperfect verb in the subjunctive after an. Its subjunctive marking is the deletion of the inflectional nūn. The wāw is the plural subject pronoun, referring to those Jacob addresses.

The remaining final nūn is the protective nūn; the first-person object yāʾ is omitted. Restoring that object suffix gives تُفَنِّدُونِي.

The statement refers to Jacob himself: “you consider my judgment unsound,” or “you regard my words as arising from poor judgment.”

The verb targets the speaker himself, rather than simply an assertion detached from him. Jacob anticipates that his hearers will attribute his words to weakened discernment and judgment.

Form II alone does not establish the severity of their reproach, deliberate intent to accuse, or their inner motives. Those details must be understood from their conversation and conduct.

3. Yūsuf 12:94: was Jacob’s judgment impaired?

وَلَمَّا فَصَلَتِ الْعِيرُ قَالَ أَبُوهُمْ إِنِّي لَأَجِدُ رِيحَ يُوسُفَ لَوْلَا أَنْ تُفَنِّدُونِ

Wa-lammā faṣalati l-ʿīru qāla abūhum innī la-ajidu rīḥa Yūsufa lawlā an tufannidūni.

“When the caravan departed, their father said, ‘Indeed, I perceive Joseph’s scent—if you do not accuse me of unsound judgment.’”

Yūsuf 12:94

The verse comes near the end of Joseph’s story, after he has revealed himself to his brothers and they have prepared to return to their father.

Jacob has long been separated from his son. Yūsuf 12:84 describes his grief and the whitening of his eyes. Yet he has not lost hope; in Yūsuf 12:86 he says that he knows from The God what his hearers do not know.

As the caravan departs, Jacob says something unexpected to those present: he perceives Joseph’s scent.

إِنِّي لَأَجِدُ is emphatic. Jacob reports his perception, then alludes through لَوْلَا أَنْ تُفَنِّدُونِ to his hearers’ possible or familiar response.

Lawlā here introduces a conditional construction whose consequent is not explicitly stated. Broadly, the sense is that, without their attribution of poor judgment standing in the way, he could speak without concern over such an assessment or affirm his statement more fully.

The wording nevertheless makes clear that Jacob is aware of being accused of faulty discernment.

Does tufannidūni mean “I have grown old”?

The verse itself does not say that.

Jacob was elderly at this point, but tufannidūni directly concerns his hearers’ action: they may attribute his words to unsound judgment.

Lexical usage may associate this weakness with aging, but the verb does not mean simply “being old.”

Translating it merely as “you consider me old” misses the issue, since the hearers already knew Jacob’s age. The question is whether his words remain reliable and true.

“You consider me mad” likewise introduces a stronger meaning requiring independent justification.

“You consider me foolish” is broadly related, but “you consider my judgment unsound” or “you accuse me of unsound judgment” more precisely conveys the assessment of his words.

The hearers’ reply in the next verse

قَالُوا تَاللَّهِ إِنَّكَ لَفِي ضَلَالِكَ الْقَدِيمِ

Qālū ta-llāhi innaka la-fī ḍalālika l-qadīm.

“They said, ‘By The God, you are still in your old error.’”

Yūsuf 12:95

This response clarifies the setting of Jacob’s statement.

Rather than examining what he has just said, his hearers attribute it to something they already believed about him.

In this context, ضَلَالِكَ الْقَدِيمِ relates to their view of Jacob’s longstanding attachment to and hope for Joseph. Their quoted words should not be presented, without identifying the speakers, as the Qur’an’s own direct judgment that Jacob was religiously misguided.

They imagine that Jacob remains caught in his former mistaken belief.

The reply shows that his anticipation of tafnīd was not groundless. They do not accept his words as true, interpreting them through their prior judgment.

Tafnīd and ḍalāl are nevertheless not synonyms. Jacob speaks of being accused of unsound judgment; his hearers describe his condition with another word.

The truth becomes clear as the story continues

فَلَمَّا أَنْ جَاءَ الْبَشِيرُ أَلْقَاهُ عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِ فَارْتَدَّ بَصِيرًا قَالَ أَلَمْ أَقُلْ لَكُمْ إِنِّي أَعْلَمُ مِنَ اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

Fa-lammā an jāʾa l-bashīru alqāhu ʿalā wajhihi fa-rtadda baṣīran qāla a-lam aqul lakum innī aʿlamu mina llāhi mā lā taʿlamūn.

“Then, when the bearer of good news arrived, he cast it over his face, and he regained his sight. He said, ‘Did I not tell you that I know from The God what you do not know?’”

Yūsuf 12:96

This verse shows that the hearers’ earlier judgment of Jacob was mistaken.

They interpreted his words as poor judgment and longstanding error, but the continuing story reveals the truth of what Jacob had spoken about.

Two matters must remain distinct: the truth of Jacob’s perception and our knowledge of how that perception occurred.

The Qur’an reports that Jacob perceived Joseph’s scent and subsequently spoke of the realization of what he knew. It does not give a specific physical or biological explanation of how the scent reached him or how he perceived it.

Conversely, the truth of Jacob’s perception in this story does not justify accepting every unusual human claim without examination.

For this root study, the important distinction is between a true statement and a mistaken judgment about its speaker.

Tafnīd in this verse concerns people who suppose or assert that the speaker’s discernment is mistaken or weakened; it does not establish that he actually has such an impairment.

4. Distinctions from related roots

ف ن د (fanad) and ك ذ ب (kadhib)

ك ذ ب concerns false reports, lying, and declaring something false.

Some Arabic uses of fanad also concern false speech. Yet Qur’anic tufannidūni involves more than denying a report: it also attributes error and poor judgment to its speaker.

Someone may consider a report false without considering its speaker foolish. Conversely, someone may discredit another’s words because they imagine that person’s discernment is impaired.

This distinction matters in Yūsuf 12:94.

“You call me a liar” may express part of the effect of tafnīd, but does not convey its full contextual meaning.

For the independent uses of kadhib, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root ك ذ ب.

ف ن د (fanad) and ض ل ل (ḍalla)

ض ل ل has uses concerning losing one’s way, going astray, error, and departing from the right path.

In Yūsuf 12:95, the hearers say ضَلَالِكَ الْقَدِيمِ, describing Jacob’s condition or course of thought from their perspective.

Tufannidūni in the preceding verse instead concerns attributing error and poor judgment.

These concepts may come close within a conversation, but are not identical.

Nor does ḍalāl necessarily mean religious misguidance or conscious rejection of truth in every verse; its referent and speaker must be considered.

For further discussion, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root ض ل ل.

ف ن د (fanad) and ض ع ف (ḍaʿufa)

ض ع ف concerns weakness and inability, whether physical or manifested in circumstances, capacity, resources, or other respects.

Some lexical uses of fanad likewise concern weak judgment or discernment.

Physical weakness and cognitive weakness, however, are different.

Someone may become physically weak in old age while continuing to speak truthfully and judge accurately.

Qur’anic tufannidūni primarily concerns others’ judgment of the reliability of Jacob’s opinion and words. His physical weakness is not the verb’s direct meaning.

For the semantic range of weakness, see the study of the root ض ع ف.

ف ن د (fanad) and ش ي خ (shaykh)

Shaykh designates an elderly man; in some usages it is also a title for an elder or a person of social standing.

Old age in itself does not indicate error or unsound judgment.

Some Arabic usages associate fanad with age-related weakness, but this does not make the two terms synonymous.

Not all elderly people experience fanad, and not every mistaken judgment arises from old age.

The distinction is especially important in Jacob’s story: neither his age nor his grief suffices to discredit his words.

ف ن د (fanad) and ل و م (lawm)

Lawm concerns blame and reproach.

Arabic tafnīd can also involve reproach, but its particular focus in this branch is considering someone’s judgment or speech mistaken or unsound.

Someone may reproach another for objectionable conduct without questioning their ability to discern.

Conversely, someone may attribute another’s words to poor judgment without voicing explicit reproach.

Tafnīd and lawm therefore overlap without being identical.

5. Translation challenges and the technical use of tafnīd

In contemporary Persian, tafnīd is mainly used for refuting a claim, theory, or argument.

For example, one might say, “The researcher refuted that claim by presenting evidence.”

In this usage, refutation can be a scholarly and reasoned activity: someone examines a claim and shows that it lacks sufficient support or conflicts with the evidence.

This familiar Persian usage should not be transferred uncritically to Yūsuf 12:94.

There, the verb directly refers to Jacob, against the background of his age and the hearers’ assessment of his credibility.

The subsequent narrative also exposes the error in their judgment.

Tafnīd here therefore does not mean the successful scholarly refutation of a false claim. It concerns attributing weakness and error to someone whose words the story later confirms.

A word may acquire a broader or different technical use over time. Its Qur’anic meaning must nevertheless be understood through the language and context of the verse itself.

Conversely, the story does not imply that every disagreement with an elderly or high-ranking person is objectionable tafnīd.

Examining evidence, asking questions, and criticizing a claim differ from groundlessly attributing cognitive incapacity to its speaker.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In the judgment-and-speech branch, suitable equivalents for fanad include “error of judgment,” “unsound judgment,” “false speech,” and, in some uses, “foolishness.”

فَنَّدَ رَأْيَهُ can be rendered “considered his judgment unsound,” “deemed his opinion mistaken,” or “questioned the reliability of his judgment.”

For afnada when describing a person, “his discernment weakened” or “his ability to judge deteriorated” may fit, provided the context supports that meaning.

For the sole Qur’anic form tufannidūni, however, the principal proposal is:

“You accuse me of unsound judgment.”

Accordingly, Yūsuf 12:94 may be translated:

“When the caravan departed, their father said, ‘Indeed, I perceive Joseph’s scent—if you do not accuse me of unsound judgment.’”

A shorter alternative is:

“If you do not consider my judgment unsound.”

“If you do not consider me foolish” is defensible within the semantic field, but makes the particular connection with weakened judgment less explicit.

“If you do not consider me mad” is stronger and is not preferred as the basic translation.

“If you do not consider me old” is also imprecise: Jacob’s age is not in dispute; the issue is the assessment of his words.

“If you do not call me a liar” conveys part of the meaning but does not explicitly convey the attribution of poor judgment.

For Forqan, “if you do not accuse me of unsound judgment” is preferable because it preserves an attested sense of the root and remains clear in the verse’s context.

Conclusion

One principal branch of early Arabic ف ن د concerns error, false speech, unsound judgment, and attributing such weakness to another person.

The verse attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī illustrates fanad in false speech and disparagement. Classical Arabic also associates this family with impaired discernment in old age or illness, without making aging and fanad identical.

The Qur’an uses only tufannidūni, in Jacob’s speech in Yūsuf 12:94.

Jacob reports perceiving Joseph’s scent and anticipates that his hearers will consider his judgment unsound. Their response in Yūsuf 12:95 interprets his words as longstanding error; the continuing narrative exposes that assessment as mistaken.

This usage distinguishes three situations: a statement being false, its speaker being judged mistaken, and that speaker actually having impaired discernment. None necessarily establishes another.

Likewise, contemporary Persian tafnīd as the reasoned refutation of a claim should not replace the Qur’anic meaning without examination.

Final definition for Forqan:

ف ن د: in the judgment-and-speech branch, error, falsehood, or unsound judgment; transitive fannada concerns considering another’s judgment and speech mistaken or unsound and attributing that weakness to them. Some Arabic usages associate it with impaired discernment in old age or illness, but it is not confined to these.

In Yūsuf 12:94, tufannidūni means “you accuse me of unsound judgment.” It expresses the hearers’ assessment of Jacob rather than confirming that his judgment was actually impaired.
