Central Qur’anic sense: left unattended, abandoned, or neglected. In its Qur’anic context, sudan challenges the assumption that the human being is left without obligation or accountability after death. These contextual implications should be distinguished from the basic lexical sense. Other Arabic branches include warp threads, beneficence, dew, and certain uses concerning movement and mastery; these must not be imposed on the Qur’anic occurrence without evidence.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic family S D Y has several semantic branches, an important one concerning abandonment and leaving something unattended.

«إِبِلٌ سُدًى» describes camels left to themselves, without anyone tending or directing them.

«أَسْدَيْتُ الْإِبِلَ» means: I left the camels unattended.

In this usage, al-sudā denotes a state of abandonment or neglect.

Pastoral life distinguishes grazing livestock from leaving them without care.

Camels may move freely in a pasture while a herder remains responsible for them, watches their movement, and protects them from danger when necessary.

Leaving them without guarding or care creates a different situation.

The issue is not simply the animal’s freedom of movement, but the absence of care and supervision.

This distinction matters for understanding sudan.

In the branch concerning abandonment, sudan describes something left to itself, without the care or oversight that might otherwise be exercised over it.

Early poetry: not abandoning what one tends

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says in a poem beginning «أَرَى النَّفْسَ لَجَّتْ فِي رَجَاءٍ مُكَذِّبِ»:

فَلَمْ أُسْدِ مَا أَرْعَى وَتَبْلٍ رَدَدْتُهُ
وَأَنْجَحْتُ بَعْدَ اللَّهِ مِنْ خَيْرِ مَطْلَبِ

fa-lam usdi mā arʿā wa-tablin radadtuhu / wa-anjaḥtu baʿda Allāhi min khayri maṭlabi

Translation:

“I did not neglect what I tended; I repaid a grievance, and, with The God’s aid, succeeded in a worthy pursuit.”

Here usdi is a form of the verb asdā.

Mā arʿā refers to what the poet undertakes to tend and care for.

Tabl here concerns enmity or a grievance calling for requital; raddadtuhu conveys responding to or repaying it.

The poet speaks of maintaining his charge, responding to hostility, and attaining his purpose. These are descriptions of his conduct, not a general ethical definition of the root.

In fa-lam usdi mā arʿā, the verb appears in a negative construction.

He says he did not abandon what he was responsible for tending.

This clearly illustrates leaving something unattended.

Here asdā does not mean bestowing, weaving, or reaching a goal; it means abandoning something entrusted to one’s care.

The verse is recorded in the poetry attributed to Labīd and in early lexical records.

Labīd lived partly before Islam and partly in the Islamic period. The precise date of this poem is not independently established; his having lived before Islam does not by itself prove that this particular verse predates the Qur’an.

Nevertheless, its presence in his collected poetry and its lexical transmission support the early use of asdā for abandonment.

The expression ibil sudan

Arabic lexical records attest «إِبِلٌ سُدًى» for unattended, unguarded camels.

This expression illustrates the noun’s meaning without requiring a philosophical or theological construction.

Camels may occupy a pasture and move freely, yet be described as sudan if no one guards or tends them.

Several points should be distinguished here.

Being abandoned does not necessarily mean being destroyed.

Moving freely does not necessarily mean lacking supervision.

Nor does abandoning an animal mean that the animal itself has no needs, abilities, or instincts.

The issue is its relation to care and oversight.

This distinction also matters in the Qur’anic usage, which concerns a human being rather than an animal in a pasture.

A definition grounded in historical usage

The poetic example attributed to Labīd and recorded Arabic usages support the following account:

In the abandonment branch, sudan describes someone or something left to itself without care, guidance, or attention. Asdā in this usage means leaving unattended.

This meaning is clearly attested in classical Arabic.

Labīd’s verse also illustrates early poetic usage, although its precise pre-Qur’anic date cannot be established.

The Qur’an employs this semantic field to describe a broader assumption about the human being.

2. Important forms and semantic branches

Sudan: left unattended

Applied to humans or animals, sudan denotes being left unattended or neglected.

This noun can be used of a singular or plural referent.

For example:

إِبِلٌ سُدًى

ibilun sudan

That is: “Camels left unattended and unguarded.”

This form occurs in Al-Qiyāmah 75:36.

Some Arabic usages also attest sadan with fatḥah on the sīn for this meaning; the Qur’anic form under discussion is sudan with ḍammah.

Asdā: he left unattended

One attested meaning of asdā is to abandon or leave something to itself.

أَسْدَى إِبِلَهُ

asdā ibilahu

That is: “He left his camels without guarding them.”

The same verb has other meanings in other constructions; it should not always be translated as abandoning.

Al-sadā: warp threads

In Arabic weaving terminology, al-sadā denotes the threads stretched lengthwise through a fabric.

These stand opposite al-luḥmah.

Luḥmah here denotes the transverse threads passing between the longitudinal threads to form the weave.

Al-sadāh is also attested for a single warp thread.

Asdā al-thawba in this branch concerns preparing or stretching the warp of a fabric.

This form and meaning differ from al-sudā in the sense of something abandoned.

The former concerns arranging threads for weaving.

The latter concerns abandonment and neglect.

Similarity of form alone does not establish that Qur’anic sudan refers to unfinished fabric or unwoven threads.

If such an image is used in philosophical explanation, it should be identified as an analogy, not a lexical definition or a demonstrated historical origin.

Asdā ilayhi maʿrūfan: doing someone a kindness

In another familiar construction, asdā ilayhi maʿrūfan means doing someone good or extending kindness to them.

Here asdā conveys bestowing or conferring a benefit.

This is not the same meaning as abandoning livestock without a guard.

Some lexical explanations propose a connection between giving and releasing something from the giver’s possession, but this alone does not establish a shared historical origin for the two usages.

Tasaddā: advancing and gaining mastery

Some Arabic uses of tasaddā concern advancing, coming over something, or gaining mastery over it.

Tasaddā al-amra in the mastery branch is used for taking control of a matter.

This particular form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Sadiyat al-laylah: dew and moisture

Sadiyat al-laylah is recorded of a night with abundant dew and moisture.

Al-sadā can denote dew in this usage.

Laylah sadiyah likewise describes a damp, dewy night.

This differs from the Qur’anic sense of abandonment.

Sadā and sadāʾ: unripe dates

Some Arabic dialects attest sadā and sadāʾ for green, unripe dates.

This concerns the fruit’s condition and should not be transferred to Qur’anic sudan.

The form actually used in the Qur’an

The root S D Y occurs only once in the Qur’an:

Sudan in Al-Qiyāmah 75:36.

Syntactically, it is an accusative circumstantial expression describing the state in which the human being supposes he will be left.

The family’s other derivatives and lexical usages do not occur there in those forms.

3. Al-Qiyāmah 75:36: does the human being expect to be left without purpose?

أَيَحْسَبُ الْإِنْسَانُ أَنْ يُتْرَكَ سُدًى

a-yaḥsabu al-insānu an yutraka sudan

Al-Qiyāmah 75:36

Translation:

“Does the human being think he will be left without purpose?”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:36

Sudan describes the condition in which the human being is left.

A-yaḥsabu questions the human being’s assumption.

Al-insān is the subject of that question.

An yutraka concerns being left or abandoned.

Sudan specifies the condition of that abandonment.

Grammatically, sudan is the circumstantial accusative referring to the implicit passive subject of yutraka.

Thus the verse does more than mention being left: it asks whether the human being expects abandonment without responsibility or any reckoning concerning him.

Yutraka and sudan

Two closely related concepts occur together.

Yutraka belongs to T R K and means being left or abandoned.

Sudan describes being left unattended; freedom from obligation or accountability is a contextual interpretation here.

The two words overlap semantically but serve different grammatical roles: the verb expresses being left, while the circumstantial expression specifies the state involved.

Something may be left somewhere temporarily while remaining under care.

For example, someone may leave a child at school while designated people remain responsible for the child.

Merely leaving the location does not mean abandoning the child without supervision.

Leaving someone without attention, care, or responsibility is different.

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:36, yutraka sudan raises precisely this notion of unattended abandonment.

Does sudan mean only “without purpose”?

“Left without purpose” is a natural and defensible rendering of the verse.

But sudan is broader than lacking personal goals in life.

Someone may have many goals and strive for wealth, knowledge, family, power, or comfort.

Possessing such goals does not by itself answer the verse’s question.

The question is not whether the human being chooses personal plans and objectives.

It is whether he imagines that his creation and life involve no responsibility before his Lord, no guidance, and no final outcome.

Thus “left without obligation or accountability” is also related to the verse’s context.

This explanation should not replace the basic translation without consideration.

Forqan’s rendering “left without purpose” conveys the general sense; the implications for responsibility and accountability can be explained in the analysis.

4. Sudan within Al-Qiyāmah: responsibility and return after death

The preceding and following verses matter for understanding sudan in Al-Qiyāmah 75:36.

From its opening, the surah concerns resurrection and human accountability.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:3 questions the human assumption that his bones will not be gathered.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:4 declares The God’s power to restore even his fingertips.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:5 speaks of the human being wishing to continue wrongdoing.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:6 presents his question about the time of the Day of Resurrection.

These verses connect denial of return after death with human behavior and choice in life.

Responsibility for conduct

Al-Qiyāmah 75:13 states that on the Day of Resurrection the human being will be informed of what he sent ahead and left behind.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:14 presents the human being as a discerning witness against himself.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:15 mentions the excuses he may offer.

The surah thus presents human conduct as consequential: people will confront the truth of their actions.

This context helps explain sudan.

Humans are not left to do whatever they wish while assuming that no reckoning will follow.

Yet responsibility is not the same as the absence of choice.

The ability to choose is one condition that makes responsibility meaningful.

Where people can choose between different actions, those choices can become subjects of judgment and accountability.

Rejecting sudan therefore does not negate human will or choice; it rejects absolute freedom from responsibility.

Denial, turning away, and assuming abandonment

Al-Qiyāmah 75:31 and Al-Qiyāmah 75:32 describe someone who neither affirmed nor performed ṣalāh, but denied and turned away.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:33 describes his arrogant return to his family.

The warnings in Al-Qiyāmah 75:34 and Al-Qiyāmah 75:35 follow.

Then comes the question «أَيَحْسَبُ الْإِنْسَانُ أَنْ يُتْرَكَ سُدًى».

This sequence places the assumption of abandonment within a context of denial and evasion of responsibility.

Someone may suppose that because an action is currently possible and meets no immediate obstacle, no accounting or consequence will follow.

The Qur’an distinguishes the ability to act from the absence of responsibility.

Having opportunities for choice and action in this life does not place conduct beyond judgment.

Initial creation and the possibility of resurrection

After Al-Qiyāmah 75:36, the Qur’an draws attention to the beginning of human creation.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:37 mentions an emitted drop.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:38 addresses further stages of human formation and proportioning.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:39 mentions the making of the two sexes, male and female.

The surah closes with Al-Qiyāmah 75:40, questioning whether The God has the power to bring the dead to life.

This sequence matters.

After questioning human abandonment, the Qur’an returns to the origin of human creation and ends with the power to resurrect.

Sudan here therefore concerns more than responsibilities in earthly life.

The human outcome after death is also part of the argument.

The verse challenges both life without responsibility and the assumption that death ends everything without a return for reckoning.

Connection with Al-Muʾminūn 23:115

Al-Muʾminūn 23:115 asks a related question.

It challenges the assumption of purposeless creation and of not returning to The God.

This helps explain the connection between creation’s purpose and human return.

Nevertheless, ʿabathan and sudan belong to different roots and should not be treated as identical in every respect.

ʿAbath concerns futility or action lacking a sound, meaningful purpose.

Sudan concerns abandonment and lack of care.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:36 challenges being left without purpose or responsibility.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:115 challenges purposeless creation and the absence of return to The God.

The verses illuminate each other without erasing the distinct meanings of their roots.

Does rejecting sudan specify every detail of human life?

The verse denies that humans are abandoned without purpose or responsibility.

That statement alone does not establish that The God has issued a detailed, predetermined instruction for every daily choice.

People may choose their profession, place of residence, permissible clothing, food, and many other personal matters.

Choice in these areas does not imply exemption from ethical and divine responsibility.

People can be responsible within their freedom, for example by avoiding oppression, betrayal, or violations of others’ rights.

Conversely, the assertion that humans are not left sudan does not turn every human-made rule or prohibition into a divine command.

Each specific obligation or prohibition requires its own evidence.

The principle of responsibility before The God must therefore be distinguished from the details of practical rulings.

5. Distinctions from related roots

S D Y and H M L

H M L concerns leaving something unattended or uncared for.

Sudan is directly glossed as muhmal, neglected, in lexical records.

Ibil sudan likewise describes unguarded camels.

The two families clearly overlap in the field of abandonment.

Their entire ranges of usage should nevertheless not be equated without examination.

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:36, sudan appears in the context of human responsibility and return.

That sense is understood from the word together with the surah’s context.

S D Y and T R K

Tark means leaving or abandoning.

The verb yutraka itself appears in Al-Qiyāmah 75:36.

Sudan further specifies the condition of being left.

Leaving something may involve intention, responsibility, or care provided by someone else.

Sudan in the abandonment branch emphasizes the absence of care.

The presence of both expressions therefore has a function.

Yutraka expresses being left; sudan describes the state in which one is left.

S D Y and ʿ B TH

ʿAbath concerns futility and action lacking a proper purpose.

Sudan concerns neglect and being left to oneself.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:115 places ʿabath within creation and return.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:36 places sudan within responsibility and resurrection.

The concepts are close, but their lexical meanings are not identical.

Futile action or creation is one issue; abandonment without guidance or accountability is another.

In these verses, both relate to creation’s purpose and human responsibility.

S D Y and B Ṭ L

Qur’anic bāṭil concerns falsehood, groundlessness, or what is not true and right.

Ṣād 38:27 denies that the heavens, earth, and what lies between them were created in vain.

The verse connects with Al-Qiyāmah 75:36 through creation’s purpose.

But bāṭil does not itself mean abandoned or unguarded.

Sudan should therefore not be replaced with bāṭil in every usage.

S D Y and Ḍ Y ʿ

Ḍayāʿ concerns loss, ruin, or something going to waste.

Sudan in the abandonment branch concerns the absence of care.

Abandonment may cause something to go to waste.

That consequence is not, however, an essential part of the word’s meaning.

An unguarded camel may remain alive and healthy.

Abandonment and destruction or loss are therefore distinct concepts.

S D Y and S D D

These roots share their first two letters, but differ in their final letter and primary meanings.

S D D concerns closing passages and gaps in its physical uses, and correctness and firmness in another branch.

Qur’anic S D Y concerns being left unattended.

Letter similarity alone cannot make sudan mean a dam, barrier, or sound speech.

S D Y and S D W

Lexical records bring the forms with final wāw and yāʾ together in some usages.

Sadw is also attested for extending a hand or advancing in movement.

Applied to camels and horses, it can describe their manner of proceeding.

Movement and being left without a guard are not the same meaning.

Movement-related uses do not establish that Qur’anic sudan means traveling along an endless path.

The Qur’anic meaning is understood from the form sudan and its own verse context.

6. Rendering sudan for Forqan

Forqan currently renders Al-Qiyāmah 75:36 as follows:

“Does the human being think he will be left without purpose?”

This is a natural and defensible rendering.

A fuller understanding nevertheless requires recognizing the Arabic sense of abandonment and neglect.

Within Al-Qiyāmah, this connects with human responsibility, guidance, and return after death.

“Left without accountability” can highlight answerability, but the basic translation should not replace a word with every detail of contextual analysis.

Two distinct word-box meanings are appropriate:

Brief root meaning: “Left to oneself, unattended, or without care.”

Meaning in this verse: “Unattended; in context, left without obligation or accountability, an assumption the verse challenges.”

Preserve the precise Qur’anic form «سُدًى».

It is an accusative circumstantial expression describing the condition of being left.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic family S D Y has several semantic branches.

In one important usage, sudan describes something left to itself without guarding or care.

Ibil sudan, for unattended camels, provides a clear example.

In the poetry attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, fa-lam usdi mā arʿā likewise expresses not abandoning what one tends.

This is attested in early Arabic poetry, although the precise date of the verse is uncertain.

Other branches are also recorded.

Al-sadā denotes warp threads.

Asdā ilayhi maʿrūfan means doing someone a kindness.

Sadā in some usages concerns dew and moisture.

Tasaddā can concern advancing or gaining mastery.

Shared letters do not justify merging these senses into an artificial definition.

The root S D Y occurs only once in the Qur’an.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:36 asks whether the human being expects to be left without purpose.

The question appears in a surah addressing responsibility for conduct, denial of resurrection, awareness of one’s actions, and return after death.

The following verses recall human formation and The God’s power to revive the dead.

Sudan here therefore goes beyond lacking personal plans; the rejected assumption includes exemption from responsibility and from accountability after death.

Denying abandonment does not, however, remove human choice.

People can choose and be responsible for their choices.

Nor does this principle alone authorize religious obligations or prohibitions without independent evidence.

The basic sense of Qur’anic sudan is being left unattended or neglected. Within the surah, the rejected assumption is that humans are left without obligation or accountability; these details arise from the context alongside the word’s lexical meaning.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:36 challenges that assumption and rejects it through the reminder of human creation and the possibility of resurrection.

“Does the human being think he will be left without purpose?” preserves the verse’s general meaning.

The contextual reflection is also clear: human life is not without purpose or responsibility, and death is not presented as definitively ending accountability before The God.
