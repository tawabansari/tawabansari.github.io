1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic form

The triliteral Arabic root J S S concerns touching, inspecting, and seeking knowledge through close examination. Physical jassa means touching by hand, particularly to determine something’s condition or qualities.

The family also describes visual inspection, testing, and seeking information. Tajassus names searching for reports and hidden matters; jāsūs names someone who seeks information.

Touching, inspecting, and spying should be distinguished. Touch can be simple and open, such as feeling a pulse or examining material. Information-related tajassus more often involves investigating something unknown or concealed.

The entire family cannot be confined to modern espionage. Conversely, the tactile sense should not reduce Qur’anic tajassasū to ordinary inspection or curiosity. Brief root meaning: Touching and inspecting to learn; in information-related usage, seeking and following up reports.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an:

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12: wa-lā tajassasū, “and do not spy.”

Tajassasū is a second-person plural Form V imperfect in a prohibition: “do not spy” or “do not pry.” Its fuller form is tatajassasū, with one of the two tāʾs omitted; the prohibition makes it jussive through deletion of nūn.

Jassa, jāsūs, tajassus, and majassah do not occur in the Qur’an. Taḥassasū in Yūsuf 12:87 and jāsū in Al-Isrāʾ 17:5 belong to different roots despite phonetic and semantic proximity.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one Form V occurrence

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Learning through careful touch

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْجِيمُ وَالسِّينُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ تَعَرُّفُ الشَّيْءِ بِمَسٍّ لَطِيفٍ.

al-jīmu wa-al-sīnu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa taʿarrufu al-shayʾi bi-massin laṭīf

Translation: “Jīm and sīn have one principle: learning about something through gentle touch.”

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: learning through gentle touch

This definition combines contact and knowledge.

Touch may be simple contact, but jass, particularly in Ibn Fāris’s account, uses it to determine a condition.

Feeling a fruit to judge softness or hardness, for instance, is inspection rather than mere contact.

A physician may similarly feel part of a patient’s body to learn something not immediately evident to sight.

This illustrates a distinction from ordinary touch without making every jassa secret investigation. Sometimes physical feeling and inspection are all that is intended.

Jassa and ijtassa

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Ibn Manẓūr quotes Ibn Sīdah:

جَسَّهُ بِيَدِهِ يَجُسُّهُ جَسًّا وَاجْتَسَّهُ، أَيْ مَسَّهُ وَلَمَسَهُ.

jassahu bi-yadihi yajussuhu jassan wa-ijtassahu ay massahu wa-lamasah

Translation: “He jassa it with his hand, and ijtassa it: he touched or felt it.”

Ibn Sīdah in Lisān: jassa and ijtassa

Jassa and ijtassa are close in this tactile use.

Jassa is Form I; ijtassa is Form VIII. Both can express hand contact here.

The Form VIII pattern alone does not make the action invariably stronger, more secret, or longer. Actual usage must establish such distinctions.

Majass and majassah

Lexical sources record majass and majassah for the place where the hand rests during examination.

A physician’s point of contact can thus be called a majass.

Mijass as an examining instrument is also recorded in later sources: Dozy, citing Bocthor, describes a wound probe. This does not establish pre-Islamic use. Nor does the vowel of mīm invariably separate instrument from location: Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr also records mijassah, with kasrah, for a physician’s point of contact.

Dozy citing Bocthor: mijass as a wound probe

These derivatives establish the physical branch alongside reports and personal affairs.

Miṣbāḥ: mijassah as a physician’s point of contact

Inspection with the eyes

Ibn Durayd explains in Jamharat al-Lughah that jass can also be done with the eyes.

The observer looks closely to identify something more reliably.

The act therefore extends beyond direct hand contact to visual examination.

Ordinary seeing need not involve inspection. Concentrating on something to identify its nature or condition approaches the investigative sense.

This does not yet necessarily concern espionage or intrusion into private affairs; the point is close examination for knowledge.

From inspecting objects to seeking reports

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَجَسَّ الْخَبَرَ وَتَجَسَّسَهُ: بَحَثَ عَنْهُ وَفَحَصَ.

wa-jassa al-khabara wa-tajassasahu baḥatha ʿanhu wa-faḥaṣ

Translation: “He jassa the report or investigated it: he searched and examined it.”

Lisān: investigating reports

This establishes another branch of the family.

Reports cannot literally be handled, but people can follow clues, ask questions, and examine scattered information.

Jass here extends from physical touch to investigating reports.

The connection is intelligible without establishing its historical sequence. Both uses are attested; dating the development of information-seeking is a separate question.

3. Early Arabic life and usage

Examining the body and pulse

A familiar jass usage involves feeling a body to assess its condition.

Jassa al-ʿirqa is recorded for feeling or examining a vessel or pulse.

The hand helps establish something not plainly visible.

Feeling a pulse, for example, can provide information about heartbeat and bodily condition.

Jass is not inherently blameworthy: it can assess illness, identify a problem, or examine an object’s qualities.

The Qur’anic prohibition’s negative context should not be transferred to every root usage.

Nor should the medical example monopolize the definition. Physicians use jass; the root is not exclusively medical.

Al-Rāghib and Al-Mughrib: feeling a vessel and examining the body

Afwāhuhā majāssuhā: a proverb about camels

Arabic sources record the proverb:

أَفْوَاهُهَا مَجَاسُّهَا.

afwāhuhā majāssuhā

Translation: “Their mouths are their places of examination.”

It is explained through camels’ feeding and fatness.

Pastoral examination can involve feeling an animal’s flesh. The proverb instead treats good eating as an indication of its condition, reducing the need for direct palpation.

An outward sign thus provides knowledge of another condition.

Majāss names examination sites, directly connected with bodily assessment.

The proverb occurs in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and proverb collections. Recording it centuries later does not independently date its origin to pre-Islamic times.

Ṣiḥāḥ and Asās: the camels’ mouths proverb

It nevertheless clarifies the root’s pastoral and bodily uses.

Visual examination in poetry

Ibn Durayd quotes two verses in Jamharat al-Lughah for visual jass:

وَفِتْيَةٍ كَالذِّئَابِ الطُّلْسِ قُلْتُ لَهُمْ
إِنِّي أَرَى شَبَحًا قَدْ زَالَ أَوْ حَالَا

فَاعْصَوْصَبُوا ثُمَّ جَسُّوهُ بِأَعْيُنِهِمْ
ثُمَّ اخْتَفَوْهُ وَقَرْنُ الشَّمْسِ قَدْ زَالَا

wa-fityatin ka-al-dhiʾābi al-ṭulsi qultu lahum / innī arā shabaḥan qad zāla aw ḥālā // fa-ʿṣawṣabū thumma jassūhu bi-aʿyunihim / thumma ikhtafawhu wa-qarnu al-shamsi qad zālā

Translation: “To youths like dusky wolves I said: I see a shape that has moved or changed position. They gathered together and examined it with their eyes, then made it discernible, while the sun had inclined.” Zāla describes the sun’s shift or inclination; it does not alone establish a definite sunset time.

ʿUbāb: visual inspection and ikhtafawhu

Jassūhu bi-aʿyunihim means examining it with their eyes.

A distant, uncertain shape prompts close observation to identify it more clearly.

Jass thus concerns focused sight and recognition, rather than hand contact.

The movement is from indistinct observation toward clearer knowledge.

Ikhtafawhu is glossed here as revealing or making discernible. It should not be translated as concealment merely from modern associations with ikhtifāʾ.

The examined transmission does not clearly identify the poet, so a pre-Islamic date cannot be assigned confidently.

Lisān and Tāj: unnamed poetic witness and transmission

Despite that limit, it remains evidence for visual inspection.

Seeking information and the jāsūs

Arabic sources describe jāsūs as someone who seeks reports and acquires information.

Some definitions specify gathering reports for another person or group. In warfare or rivalry, the role can involve secretly learning the opponent’s condition.

The activity then exceeds simple inspection, involving concealed information or methods.

The word alone does not establish that reports are always true or false, that the person serves an enemy, or that the subject is necessarily a crime or misconduct.

The role and circumstances determine those features.

Early jāsūs could name an information gatherer; modern political usage more readily suggests organized, clandestine acquisition of confidential information.

Translation and analysis should preserve that difference.

Jawāss and human senses

Lexical sources transmit from Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad that jawāss can name human senses.

It concerns means of perception, such as sight, hearing, and touch.

The connection with jass is intelligible: senses provide knowledge of the surrounding world.

Arabic also has the more familiar ḥawāss, with ḥāʾ.

Their proximity does not make the roots identical or establish universal interchangeability of jīm and ḥāʾ.

Jawāss documents sensory knowledge within J S S, but is not Qur’anic.

Al-ʿAyn and Ṣiḥāḥ: jawāss alongside ḥawāss

4. Important forms and derivatives

Jassa: touching and inspecting

Jassa is Form I, with imperfect yajussu.

Its physical use concerns touching and examining; its informational use concerns investigation.

The object may be a body, someone’s condition, or a report to be understood.

Ijtassa: touching

Ijtassa is Form VIII, recorded for touching.

It can overlap with jassa; its pattern alone establishes neither secrecy nor malicious intent.

Tajassasa: seeking reports

Tajassasa is Form V, with verbal noun tajassus.

It concerns searching or following up information.

Where private personal matters are involved, it can describe investigating without the person’s knowledge or consent.

Secrecy and intrusion nevertheless do not follow from Form V alone in every lexical use. Subject, method, and context matter.

Jāsūs: an information gatherer

Jāsūs follows the fāʿūl pattern.

Ibn Fāris connects it with examining what one seeks through subtle, concealed means.

Its familiar plural is jawāsīs.

It can concern military intelligence, personal information, and clandestine activities.

Defining it as a modern state or intelligence-service agent would restrict its historical range.

Mutajassis: someone who investigates

Mutajassis is the Form V active participle.

It describes someone investigating reports or others’ affairs.

Its morphology differs from jāsūs, without proving that one is necessarily professional and the other always an ordinary person.

Context establishes the activity and role.

Majass and mijass

Majass names a place of touch or examination; mijass can name an examining instrument in some uses.

Physical examination is established in early sources; later instrumental mijass extends the family. One meaning’s antiquity should not automatically date every derivative.

Jassās

Jassās is also a personal name, notably Jassās ibn Murrah in accounts of the Basūs War.

A personal name alone cannot determine the verb’s meaning or historical origin.

Its appearance in old reports is also distinct from Qur’anic tajassasū.

5. Qur’anic usage: Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اجْتَنِبُوا كَثِيرًا مِنَ الظَّنِّ إِنَّ بَعْضَ الظَّنِّ إِثْمٌ ۖ وَلَا تَجَسَّسُوا وَلَا يَغْتَب بَّعْضُكُم بَعْضًا ۚ أَيُحِبُّ أَحَدُكُمْ أَن يَأْكُلَ لَحْمَ أَخِيهِ مَيْتًا فَكَرِهْتُمُوهُ ۚ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ تَوَّابٌ رَّحِيمٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū ijtanibū kathīran mina al-ẓanni inna baʿḍa al-ẓanni ithmun wa-lā tajassasū wa-lā yaghtab baʿḍukum baʿḍan a-yuḥibbu aḥadukum an yaʾkula laḥma akhīhi maytan fa-karihtumūhu wa-ttaqū Allāha inna Allāha tawwābun raḥīm

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “Believers, avoid much suspicion; some suspicion is sinful. Do not spy, and do not backbite one another. Would any of you like to eat his dead brother’s flesh? You would find it repugnant. Be mindful of The God: The God is indeed accepting of repentance and especially merciful.”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Tajassasū is prohibited by lā, addressing a plural audience.

The subject is human relationships: suspicion, others’ affairs, and speaking about one another.

Spying alongside suspicion and backbiting

The verse contains three closely related instructions.

First, avoid much suspicion, because some suspicion is sinful.

Second, do not spy.

Third, do not backbite one another.

These are lexically distinct but can be connected in conduct.

Someone might suspect another, investigate privately to confirm the suspicion, then circulate information or allegations.

Suspicion, spying, and backbiting then form a behavioral sequence.

The verse does not make all spying begin in suspicion or all backbiting result from spying.

Curiosity can prompt intrusion without prior suspicion; backbiting can occur without prior spying.

Despite their connection, the prohibitions remain distinct.

Is tajassus simply curiosity?

Curiosity motivates questions about nature, history, society, or science and research to answer them.

Such inquiry does not inherently constitute the prohibited tajassus of Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12.

The verse places it within interpersonal relations, suspicion, and backbiting.

The context concerns entering others’ affairs to discover private information, particularly faults, secrets, or hidden matters.

“Do not spy” is therefore appropriate, with an explanation distinguishing it from ordinary questions, learning, or scientific inquiry.

The subject, method, and circumstances of investigation make the difference.

Seeking others’ faults

Al-Ṭabarī explains the verse through pursuing others’ faults and hidden affairs.

Al-Ṭabarī: prohibition of pursuing faults and secrets

This fits the context.

The preceding Al-Ḥujurāt 49:11 discusses ridicule, fault-finding, and offensive names. Verse 12 continues with suspicion, spying, and backbiting.

The passage concerns behavior capable of damaging dignity and social standing.

Spying can obtain information used to judge, humiliate, or defame someone.

It does not follow that spying becomes objectionable only after backbiting or defamation occurs.

The verse prohibits it independently.

Is spying prohibited only when its findings are false?

Tajassus concerns investigation and acquisition methods, rather than necessarily false results.

Spying can uncover true information; truth alone does not establish the propriety of its acquisition.

A private matter might be discovered by secretly accessing someone’s possessions, writings, or communications.

Accuracy does not in itself justify the intrusion.

Conversely, simply learning of openly harmful conduct cannot automatically be called spying.

Truth, acquisition methods, and responsibility for using information should therefore be distinguished.

Spying and privacy

Respect for private living space is not confined to Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12.

Al-Nūr 24:27 and Al-Nūr 24:28 connect entering others’ homes with permission and respecting residents’ wishes.

Al-Nūr 24:58 likewise requires permission to enter private space at specified times.

These connect with the prohibition: a wish to know or an ability to gain access does not alone justify disregarding others’ boundaries.

J S S nevertheless does not lexically mean a house, ownership, or privacy.

Protecting privacy follows from Al-Ḥujurāt’s context and its relationship to other Qur’anic instructions.

Modern examples potentially include secretly accessing personal messages, examining private belongings without permission, or covertly monitoring someone’s life.

New technology alone does not determine the judgment. Use, subject matter, and people’s rights require consideration.

Spying and establishing the truth

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6 commands fa-tabayyanū when a wrongdoer brings a report.

The purpose is to avoid harming people unknowingly.

One passage therefore calls for verification, while another in the same surah forbids spying.

The two instructions should not be confused.

Tabayyun concerns clarifying truth and avoiding uninformed judgment or action. Tajassus in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 concerns pursuing others’ affairs in the setting of suspicion, fault-finding, and backbiting.

Investigating evidence of alleged harm to prevent injustice is different from unjustified searching through private lives.

That distinction does not make every information-gathering method permissible under the name of research or verification.

Nor should the prohibition prevent examination of supported allegations, restoring rights, or fairly addressing actual harm.

The limits and methods also involve Qur’anic principles of justice, testimony, trust, and responsibility.

The root’s meaning thus authorizes neither unlimited searching nor a prohibition of all inquiry.

6. Tajassus, taḥassus, and related roots

J S S (tajassus) and Ḥ S S (taḥassus)

Study of Ḥ S S

Their letters and some uses are close, but the roots are distinct.

J S S centrally concerns touch and close inspection, extending to seeking reports and information.

Ḥ S S has a broader field of sensing, perceiving, feeling, and becoming aware.

Taḥassasū occurs in Yūsuf 12:87:

يَا بَنِيَّ اذْهَبُوا فَتَحَسَّسُوا مِنْ يُوسُفَ وَأَخِيهِ وَلَا تَيْأَسُوا مِنْ رَوْحِ اللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهُ لَا يَيْأَسُ مِنْ رَوْحِ اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْقَوْمُ الْكَافِرُونَ

yā baniyya idhhabū fa-taḥassasū min Yūsufa wa-akhīhi wa-lā tayʾasū min rawḥi Allāhi innahu lā yayʾasu min rawḥi Allāhi illā al-qawmu al-kāfirūn

Proposed translation based on this study: “My sons, go and seek news of Joseph and his brother, and do not despair of The God’s relief. None despair of The God’s relief except disbelieving people.”

Jacob directs his sons to search for Joseph and his brother.

Fa-taḥassasū concerns following information and finding signs of them.

Taḥassasū and tajassasū are both Form V, but belong to different roots.

Some lexical accounts associate taḥassus more with seeking good and tajassus more with seeking evil.

Another distinguishes seeking information for someone else from seeking it for oneself.

Other lexicographers treat the two as close or synonymous in seeking reports.

The differing explanations preclude an exceptionless boundary.

Lisān and Majmaʿ Biḥār: differing accounts of taḥassus and tajassus

The Qur’anic contextual distinction is clear: taḥassus seeks missing people in Joseph’s story; tajassus in Al-Ḥujurāt occurs among prohibitions of misconduct toward others.

The search’s positive value and spying’s condemnation therefore follow from purpose and context, rather than merely jīm versus ḥāʾ.

J S S (tajassasa) and J W S (jāsa)

Study of J W S

Jāsū in Al-Isrāʾ 17:5 belongs to J W S.

Maqāyīs connects it with penetrating and moving through something.

Fa-jāsū khilāla al-diyāri concerns movement and penetration among dwellings.

Forqan’s existing Persian translation renders it as searching among the houses.

Jāsū is nevertheless not the same verb as tajassasū.

Wāw is radical in jāsū; tajassasū has two radical sīns and follows Form V.

Shared associations with searching do not erase the root distinction.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:5 concerns movement through dwellings during a social and military event; Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 concerns interpersonal behavior and forbids spying.

Maqāyīs and lexical sources: independent J W S

J S S (jass) and M S S (mass)

Study of M S S

Mass concerns contact or something reaching something else.

Physical jass also involves contact and touch.

Ibn Fāris emphasizes learning through careful touch in jass.

An accidental hand contact may be mass; examining the surface for hardness or softness can be jass.

This is not an absolute rule. Mass has broader Arabic and Qur’anic uses, including harm or being affected, and may involve awareness in some contexts.

The families overlap in physical contact, while J S S develops its own inspection and information-seeking uses.

J S S (tajassus) and B Ḥ TH (baḥth)

Study of B Ḥ TH

Baḥth concerns searching, probing, and investigation.

Information-related tajassus can likewise involve inquiry.

Not every search is tajassus.

Scientific research, examining a historical document, or seeking public information can be baḥth without entering someone’s private sphere.

Searching a person’s hidden affairs without an appropriate right or justification relates to tajassus in a context such as Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12.

Baḥth and tajassus therefore cannot replace each other everywhere.

J S S (tajassus) and F Ḥ Ṣ (faḥṣ)

Faḥṣ concerns careful examination or testing.

Lexicographers sometimes explain jassa al-khabara through faḥaṣa, reflecting overlap in investigating reports.

Faḥṣ can apply across scientific, bodily, and informational contexts rather than one restricted kind of examination.

Personal tajassus more often pursues information not openly available to everyone.

Shared investigation does not make every use identical.

J S S (tajassus) and Ẓ N N (ẓann)

Study of Ẓ N N

Ẓann concerns an opinion or judgment that may fall short of certainty.

Tajassus is searching or following up to acquire information.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 places them together.

A suspicion may prompt investigation to clarify or confirm it.

The judgment and investigation nevertheless remain distinct: suspicion can remain mental, whereas tajassus acts to obtain information.

Unjustified curiosity can also prompt spying without a prior suspicion.

Tajassasū should therefore not be rendered “have suspicions,” nor should every suspicion be called spying.

J S S (tajassus) and GH Y B (ghībah)

Study of GH Y B

Ethical ghībah concerns speaking about someone in their absence in a way they would dislike or that harms their dignity or reputation.

Tajassus concerns seeking information.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 prohibits them separately.

Spying might yield information later circulated, but acquiring it and speaking about the person are different actions.

The separate wording requires preserving that distinction.

J S S (tajassus) and B Y N (tabayyun)

Study of B Y N

Tabayyun concerns becoming clear or clarifying something.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6 commands verification to avoid uninformed harm.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 instead addresses objectionable investigation of others alongside suspicion and backbiting.

Both may acquire information, but their purpose and position differ.

Clarifying a report to prevent injustice differs from secretly pursuing faults to satisfy curiosity or defame someone.

Both require the broader Qur’anic framework of justice, rights, and trustworthiness.

7. Questions of understanding and translation

Does tajassus always use concealed devices?

Lexically, tajassus concerns seeking and examining information through potentially different methods.

Secrecy may concern the means or the information itself, rather than only hidden equipment.

Questioning others, tracking conduct, examining writings, or using communications tools can acquire information.

The concept should therefore not be restricted to covert listening, secretly entering a house, or an intelligence mission.

Conversely, not every question or use of communications technology is spying.

Subject matter, access rights, method, and context matter.

Is every search for truth forbidden?

J S S itself also names bodily examination, assessing conditions, and investigating reports.

The Qur’anic prohibition concerns interpersonal relationships and respect for dignity.

It should not cover all research, examination, testimony, or necessary efforts to establish truth.

Nor does truth-seeking permit every intrusion into private lives.

Appropriate boundaries require relevant evidence and principles, rather than treating every search alike.

Is spying sinful only if its findings are disclosed?

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 prohibits tajassus independently of backbiting.

Investigating and speaking about someone are therefore distinct behaviors.

One cannot claim that acquiring information remains outside any prohibition until publication.

Specific responsibility also depends on circumstances; categorical judgments about people require knowledge of the subject and purpose.

Is espionage the root’s only meaning?

“Spying” fits Qur’anic tajassasū in Al-Ḥujurāt, but does not exhaust J S S.

Jassa can mean touching; majass is an examination site; jawāss sometimes denotes human senses; jāsūs names an information gatherer.

Defining the entire family as covert intrusion disregards its core and non-Qur’anic uses.

Conversely, rendering tajassasū here as merely “touch” or “examine” loses the prohibition’s particular meaning.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Physical jassa can mean “touched” or “examined”; “examined by touch” can better convey its investigative purpose.

Jassa al-khabara or tajassasa al-khabara can be “followed up the report,” “sought information,” or “investigated the report,” according to context.

Persian jāsūs is appropriate for jāsūs, with historical explanation where needed: gathering reports does not necessarily make the person a modern intelligence agent.

Majass can be “place of touch” or “examination site”; instrumental mijass can be “examining tool.”

For the only Qur’anic instance, tajassasū in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12, “do not spy” is natural and appropriate.

The complete phrase is:

وَلَا تَجَسَّسُوا

wa-lā tajassasū

Forqan appropriately translates it in Persian as:

“And do not spy.”

A lexical note can explain the contextual pursuit of others’ hidden information, especially alongside suspicion, fault-finding, and backbiting.

“To discover sin” or “with the intent to defame” need not enter the base translation; those qualifications do not follow from every use of the verb alone.

Translation should remain concise and faithful, leaving limits and examples to research and reflection.

9. Conclusion

J S S concerns touching, inspecting, and seeking knowledge through close examination.

Physical jassa examines a condition by touch; the family also allows focused sight. In reports, it concerns searching, following up, and gathering information.

Tajassus and jāsūs belong to that field.

The family is not solely negative: bodily assessment, examining livestock, and close inspection are not inherently espionage or wrongful intrusion.

The Qur’an uses it once, as wa-lā tajassasū in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12.

Between suspicion and backbiting, the context concerns behavior capable of harming dignity, trust, and privacy.

Suspicion, spying, and backbiting nevertheless remain distinct: mental judgment, pursuing information, and speaking about someone in their absence.

Tajassasū also differs in root from taḥassasū in Yūsuf 12:87 and jāsū in Al-Isrāʾ 17:5. Actual language and context, rather than an absolute good-versus-bad distinction based on one consonant, explain their uses.

The family’s broad core is touching and examining for knowledge; its informational branch is seeking reports; the Qur’anic use concerns spying or prying.

“Do not spy” is the suitable contextual equivalent for Forqan.

It preserves the prohibition without classifying every question, study, investigation, or examination as spying.

The central distinction is knowledge and the means of acquiring it. Seeking truth does not itself grant access to others’ hidden affairs. Alongside instructions about wrongful suspicion, backbiting, and people’s rights, this establishes the place of tajassasū.
