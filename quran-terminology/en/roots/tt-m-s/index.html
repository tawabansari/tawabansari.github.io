Effacing and removing recognizable features: from vanished road marks to eyes, possessions, and starlight

The Arabic root ط م س concerns effacing or losing a trace, sign, form, or visible feature. طَمَسَ الأَثَرُ means a trace disappeared beyond recognition; طَمَسَ الشَّيْءَ means someone effaced a thing's traces or features.

Usages include obliterated route markers, erased writing, obscured starlight, lost sight, and altered faces. A formerly recognizable sign or quality disappears.

Effacing a trace need not destroy the entire thing. Writing can disappear while its page remains, route markers while the terrain remains, or light without the word itself determining the body's destruction.

This distinction is fundamental to the Quranic usages.

The root occurs five times in five verses: faces in Al-Nisāʾ 4:47; Pharaoh's wealth in Moses's prayer in Yūnus 10:88; possible effacement of eyes in Yā Sīn 36:66; the eyes of Lot's aggressors in Al-Qamar 54:37; and stars in Al-Mursalāt 77:8.

Form, trace, function, or light may be affected. Context determines what disappears and how far the passage specifies the event.

1. Historical meaning in early Arabic

Vanishing traces and signs

Early dictionaries explain الطَّمْس and الطُّمُوس through المَحْو and الدُّرُوس: the disappearance of traces or distinguishing signs.

Recorded examples include:

طَمَسَ الطَّرِيقُ: the road's signs disappeared.

طَمَسَ الأَثَرُ: the trace vanished.

طَمَسْتُ الْخَطَّ: I erased the writing.

انْطَمَسَ الرَّسْمُ: the mark disappeared.

They concern loss of something formerly recognizable through a trace.

Imagine a traveler crossing a previously traveled desert route.

Human or animal tracks and other marks may identify the way.

Wind-driven sand can cover them, making the route unrecognizable.

Its signs can then be described as effaced.

The ground remains, and the route need not have become impassable. What has vanished is what made it recognizable.

This supplies a concrete example of the root.

A witness from Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr

The famous Bānat Suʿād poem attributed to Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr includes:

مِنْ كُلِّ نَضَّاخَةِ الذِّفْرَى إِذَا عَرِقَتْ
عُرْضَتُهَا طَامِسُ الْأَعْلَامِ مَجْهُولُ

min kulli naḍḍākhati al-dhifrā idhā ʿariqat / ʿurḍatuhā ṭāmisu al-aʿlāmi majhūl

A camel whose sweat gushes behind its ears when it sweats, facing an unknown route with effaced markers.

The poet describes a strong camel sweating on a difficult, unfamiliar route whose markers have disappeared.

طَامِسُ الْأَعْلَامِ identifies a route without visible guiding signs.

The expression does not destroy the land or desert itself: it effaces the markers.

The witness illustrates the connection between lost signs and difficulty recognizing the way.

Kaʿb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, but Bānat Suʿād belongs to the Islamic period. This is early Arabic close to revelation, not an independent pre-Quranic witness.

Starlight disappearing

طَمَسَ النَّجْمُ is recorded for a star whose light disappears from view.

النُّجُومُ الطَّوَامِسُ describes stars whose light becomes faint or concealed.

A verse attributed to Dhū al-Rummah reads:

فَلَا تَحْسَبِي شَجِّي بِكِ الْبِيدَ كُلَّمَا
تَلَأْلَأَ بِالْغَوْرِ النُّجُومُ الطَّوَامِسُ

fa-lā taḥsabī shajjī biki al-bīda kullamā / talaʾlaʾa bi-l-ghawri al-nujūmu al-ṭawāmis

Do not suppose that my crossing the deserts with you, whenever the fading stars glitter in the late night, …

النُّجُومُ الطَّوَامِسُ concerns stars that fade or disappear from view. Its combination with glittering in the poetic line should not be read as a technical claim about permanently extinguished light.

The usage extends beyond road marks and writing to light.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad poet: the witness belongs to early Islamic Arabic, not securely pre-Quranic language.

Poetic fading or concealment does not prove destruction of the star's body.

Loss of sight

طَمَسَ بَصَرُهُ and طُمِسَتْ عَيْنُهُ describe loss of sight or effacement of the eye's visible features.

Some definitions of مَطْمُوسُ الْعَيْنِ describe an eye whose normal opening or features are no longer distinguishable.

Others explain it as loss of sight.

The senses are related but distinct.

One emphasizes outward form; the other the ability to see.

Quranic contexts must therefore be examined for what they establish.

A defensible historical definition

The recorded usages support the following core:

ط م س concerns effacing or losing a trace, sign, form, or visible feature so that what was recognizable no longer appears as before.

It encompasses road markers, writing, light, and sight.

The independently checked witnesses here do not establish a precisely dated pre-Quranic sequence for every branch's development.

Later poetry should not supply an invented chronology.

The Quran directly witnesses the root in the language of revelation, with all five occurrences involving effacement of a feature or effect.

2. Morphology and non-Quranic usages

طَمَسَ is a Form I verb used both intransitively and transitively.

Intransitively, the trace itself disappears:

طَمَسَ الأَثَرُ: the trace vanished.

Transitively, an agent removes it:

طَمَسْتُ الأَثَرَ: I effaced the trace.

انْطَمَسَ and تَطَمَّسَ also describe effacement or disappearance.

طَامِس is an active participle that can describe something whose signs have vanished.

مَطْمُوس is a passive participle for something whose features have been effaced.

طَمِيس can describe a blind person or an eye lacking its usual visible features.

Dictionaries record narrower usages for going far away or looking into the distance. They do not occur in the Quran, and their precise historical connection with effacement is not established here.

All five Quranic occurrences are Form I verbs:

  • نَطْمِسَ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:47: imperfect after أَنْ.
  • اطْمِسْ in Yūnus 10:88: imperative.
  • لَطَمَسْنَا in Yā Sīn 36:66: perfect with the lām introducing the consequence of لَوْ.
  • فَطَمَسْنَا in Al-Qamar 54:37: perfect with فَـ.
  • طُمِسَتْ in Al-Mursalāt 77:8: feminine singular passive perfect.

They differ in inflection, syntax, and assignment of agency, not in having independent lexical roots.

3. Faces effaced: Al-Nisāʾ 4:47

Al-Nisāʾ 4:47 states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ آمِنُوا بِمَا نَزَّلْنَا مُصَدِّقًا لِمَا مَعَكُمْ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ نَطْمِسَ وُجُوهًا فَنَرُدَّهَا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهَا أَوْ نَلْعَنَهُمْ كَمَا لَعَنَّا أَصْحَابَ السَّبْتِ وَكَانَ أَمْرُ اللَّهِ مَفْعُولًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna ūtū al-kitāba āminū bi-mā nazzalnā muṣaddiqan li-mā maʿakum min qabli an naṭmisa wujūhan fa-naruddahā ʿalā adbārihā aw nalʿanahum ka-mā laʿannā aṣḥāba al-sabti wa-kāna amru allāhi mafʿūlā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:47

“You who have been given the Book, believe in what We have sent down, confirming what is with you, before We efface faces and turn them toward their backs, or curse them as We cursed the companions of the Sabbath. The command of The God is accomplished.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:47

نَطْمِسَ is a first-person plural imperfect following أَنْ.

وُجُوهًا is its direct object.

Faces are therefore what is effaced.

فَنَرُدَّهَا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهَا then describes turning them toward their backs.

What does effacing faces mean?

Eyes, nose, mouth, and other features make a face recognizable.

A physical reading can concern loss of these distinguishing features and the face's familiar appearance.

That fits the lexical field of effacing signs or form.

وجه also has Arabic usages involving direction, orientation, or standing, beyond the bodily face.

An interpretation concerning loss of guidance and turning away from the right course has consequently also been proposed.

Face and back then picture orientation and reversal.

The interpretations should remain distinct.

The verb itself means effacing. Whether the passage describes bodily transformation or also conveys a warning about lost guidance belongs to interpretation of the complete verse.

The root alone cannot prove that a particular physical transformation historically occurred.

Nor should explicit face-and-back imagery be removed from translation and replaced solely by misguidance without argument.

The warning's context

The verse begins by calling for belief in what has been sent down.

Nearby verses criticize distortion of words and resistance to truth.

The warning therefore concerns invitation, recognition, and responsibility toward the message.

Addressing those given the Book does not establish an identical judgment on every member of a religious community in every age.

Quranic passages distinguish different conduct among the People of the Book.

Nor does this verse commission individuals to punish religious disagreement. It describes divine command and judgment, not a human mandate to carry out such punishment.

The lexical meaning is clear; the threat's precise realization should not be asserted beyond the evidence.

4. Pharaoh's possessions: Yūnus 10:88

Yūnus 10:88 states:

وَقَالَ مُوسَىٰ رَبَّنَا إِنَّكَ آتَيْتَ فِرْعَوْنَ وَمَلَأَهُ زِينَةً وَأَمْوَالًا فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا رَبَّنَا لِيُضِلُّوا عَنْ سَبِيلِكَ رَبَّنَا اطْمِسْ عَلَىٰ أَمْوَالِهِمْ وَاشْدُدْ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ فَلَا يُؤْمِنُوا حَتَّىٰ يَرَوُا الْعَذَابَ الْأَلِيمَ

wa-qāla mūsā rabbanā innaka ātayta firʿawna wa-malaʾahu zīnatan wa-amwālan fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā rabbanā li-yuḍillū ʿan sabīlika rabbanā iṭmis ʿalā amwālihim wa-ushdud ʿalā qulūbihim fa-lā yuʾminū ḥattā yarawū al-ʿadhāba al-alīm

Yūnus 10:88

“Moses said: Our Lord, You have given Pharaoh and his chiefs adornment and wealth in worldly life; our Lord, that they may lead others away from Your path. Our Lord, efface their wealth and harden their hearts, so that they do not believe until they see the painful punishment.”

Yūnus 10:88

اطْمِسْ is an imperative from ط م س.

Moses asks The God to efface Pharaoh's and his chiefs' wealth.

Faces are the object in Al-Nisāʾ; possessions here.

What does effacing wealth mean?

The verb can concern removal of a thing's effects, form, or functioning.

With possessions, it can concern destruction, lost value, or loss of usable benefit.

It specifies no particular mechanism.

Some exegetical reports describe the possessions turning to stone.

That is a transmitted interpretation; the verse itself does not explicitly say coins, jewelry, or other assets became stone.

Such details should not enter translation as the lexical definition of اطمس.

Efface their wealth stays close to the wording; explanation can discuss destruction or loss of usefulness.

Wealth and the power to mislead

Moses first mentions worldly adornment and wealth held by Pharaoh and his chiefs.

Their prosperity is then connected with leading people astray.

Possessions here can support influence and power as well as private enjoyment.

Wealth can meet needs, support production, education, and development; it can also finance deception, purchased loyalty, oppression, and coercion.

This context concerns wealth serving misguidance.

It does not condemn possession of wealth or adornment as such.

Nor should Moses's prayer become a general authorization to equate personal opponents with Pharaoh and demand their financial ruin.

The verse concerns a particular setting of oppression and resistance to the divine message.

وَاشْدُدْ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ belongs to another root

Moses next asks that their hearts be hardened.

That verb belongs to ش د د, not ط م س.

The two requests are not one verb with two objects.

The first concerns possessions.

The second concerns hearts.

Arabic does record طموس القلب for corruption of the heart, but that construction is absent here.

This distinction prevents importing heart-hardening into the definition of the verb actually used for possessions.

Yūnus 10:89 answers that the two men's prayer has been accepted and commands them to remain steadfast.

The connection situates the prayer within the confrontation with Pharaoh, while the exact mechanism affecting his possessions remains unspecified.

5. Eyes effaced: sight and its loss

Two further Quranic occurrences concern eyes.

One is conditional; the other reports an event involving Lot's people.

Their different constructions matter.

Yā Sīn 36:66: divine power over sight

Yā Sīn 36:66 states:

وَلَوْ نَشَاءُ لَطَمَسْنَا عَلَىٰ أَعْيُنِهِمْ فَاسْتَبَقُوا الصِّرَاطَ فَأَنَّىٰ يُبْصِرُونَ

wa-law nashāʾu la-ṭamasnā ʿalā aʿyunihim fa-stabaqū al-ṣirāṭa fa-annā yubṣirūn

Yā Sīn 36:66

“If We willed, We would efface their eyes; then they would race toward the path, but how would they see?”

Yā Sīn 36:66

لَطَمَسْنَا is a perfect verb with the lām marking the consequence of the conditional لَوْ.

The verse begins وَلَوْ نَشَاءُ: if We willed.

It describes the capacity to act, not independently an event that occurred at that moment.

Translation and reflection must preserve this distinction.

Eyes or eyesight?

أَعْيُنِهِمْ identifies what is effaced.

The verse then asks how they would see.

Loss of sight is the central consequence emphasized.

Lexical usages can describe either effaced outward eye features or lost vision.

The root does not prescribe a bodily mechanism.

The contextual result is inability to see the path.

Speed toward a destination cannot by itself solve loss of the ability to see the way.

The image can draw attention to human dependence on the order of creation.

It does not establish that every case of blindness is divine punishment or the result of personal sin.

The verse makes no such universal judgment.

طمس and مسخ in the next verse

Yā Sīn 36:67 states:

وَلَوْ نَشَاءُ لَمَسَخْنَاهُمْ عَلَىٰ مَكَانَتِهِمْ فَمَا اسْتَطَاعُوا مُضِيًّا وَلَا يَرْجِعُونَ

wa-law nashāʾu la-masakhnāhum ʿalā makānatihim fa-mā istaṭāʿū muḍiyyan wa-lā yarjiʿūn

Yā Sīn 36:67

“If We willed, We would transform them in their places, and they would be unable to proceed or return.”

Yā Sīn 36:67

This uses م س خ.

The preceding طمس concerns eyes or sight.

مسخ concerns transformation of the beings' form or condition.

Both express divine power and human limitation, but the roots are distinct.

Both are conditional: possibility must not be converted into reported occurrence without evidence.

Al-Qamar 54:37: the eyes of Lot's aggressors

Al-Qamar 54:37 states:

وَلَقَدْ رَاوَدُوهُ عَنْ ضَيْفِهِ فَطَمَسْنَا أَعْيُنَهُمْ فَذُوقُوا عَذَابِي وَنُذُرِ

wa-la-qad rāwadūhu ʿan ḍayfihi fa-ṭamasnā aʿyunahum fa-dhūqū ʿadhābī wa-nudhur

Al-Qamar 54:37

“They indeed pressed him concerning his guests; then We effaced their eyes. Taste My punishment and warnings.”

Al-Qamar 54:37

فَطَمَسْنَا is perfect and, unlike Yā Sīn 36:66, not conditional.

It reports an event in the story of Lot's people.

The preceding verse describes his warning and their disputation of it.

Their attempt to obtain access to his guests is followed by effacement of their eyes.

The context is punishment of an aggressive group.

Eye structure or sight?

Early explanations describe either disappearance of the eyes' outward form or blindness.

Both connect with parts of the verb's lexical range.

The verse does not independently describe the exact physical alteration.

It attributes the effacement to The God following their aggression.

Effaced their eyes preserves the image; explanation can discuss lost sight or altered eyes.

This does not make blindness generally a sign of punishment or moral corruption.

The Quran reports a specific event involving a particular group.

Nor should the reported event be reduced without evidence to an exclusively moral metaphor.

Unstated physiological details likewise exceed the wording.

6. طُمِسَتْ: the stars effaced

Al-Mursalāt 77:8 states:

فَإِذَا النُّجُومُ طُمِسَتْ

fa-idhā al-nujūmu ṭumisat

Al-Mursalāt 77:8

“Then, when the stars are effaced.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:8

طُمِسَتْ is feminine singular passive perfect.

النُّجُومُ is a nonhuman plural, with which feminine singular verbal agreement is possible in Arabic.

The passive foregrounds the event of effacement.

Lexical طمس النجم concerns disappearance of starlight.

Here effacement can therefore concern loss of light or visible appearance.

Lost light does not necessarily mean a destroyed body

Ordinary language can say a star disappeared when it merely passed out of an observer's view.

Clouds, daylight, or other conditions may obscure it without destroying it.

Al-Mursalāt 77:8, however, concerns the great transformations associated with the Day of Judgment.

It should not be reduced to ordinary cloud cover.

Conversely, the verb alone determines no physical mechanism for lost light or destroyed stars.

It establishes neither a particular explosion, conversion into black holes, nor any other specific scientific process.

The verse describes stars being effaced amid that day's great transformations.

Further details require independent evidence.

Comparison with Al-Takwīr 81:2

Al-Takwīr 81:2 states:

وَإِذَا النُّجُومُ انْكَدَرَتْ

wa-idhā al-nujūmu inkadarat

Al-Takwīr 81:2

“And when the stars fall scattered.”

Al-Takwīr 81:2

انْكَدَرَتْ belongs to ك د ر. Explanations include darkening and scattering or falling; the rendering above follows the latter instead of presenting both alternatives as two explicit actions.

طمست in Al-Mursalāt concerns effacement or loss of visible appearance and light.

The images are close without making the roots universally synonymous.

كدر includes turbidity and loss of clarity; طمس concerns effacement of traces and appearance.

Both passages place stellar transformation within the great events of that day.

This supports the broader picture without deriving its precise mechanics solely from roots.

7. Distinguishing related roots

ط م س and م ح و: effacing traces

م ح و concerns erasing writing, marks, or traces.

Al-Raʿd 13:39 states:

يَمْحُو اللَّهُ مَا يَشَاءُ وَيُثْبِتُ وَعِنْدَهُ أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ

yamḥū allāhu mā yashāʾu wa-yuthbitu wa-ʿindahu ummu al-kitāb

Al-Raʿd 13:39

“The God effaces what He wills and establishes; with Him is the source of the Book.”

Al-Raʿd 13:39

محو and طمس overlap in removing traces or signs.

Both can approach erasing writing or vanished route markers.

Quranic طمس concerns faces, wealth, eyes, and stars; محو also has other applications.

This does not make طمس invariably total destruction and محو invariably superficial erasure.

Their fields overlap, with context determining the particular distinction.

ط م س and ع م ي: effacement and blindness

ع م ي concerns blindness, including figurative failures to perceive truth or guidance.

طمس can describe eyes and loss of sight.

The roots nevertheless differ.

عمي may describe being blind.

Transitive طمس foregrounds the action or process effacing a trace, form, or capacity to see.

The final question in Yā Sīn 36:66 clarifies the consequence for sight.

Not every use of عمي should be reduced to effaced eyes: Quranic blindness can be bodily or cognitive.

ط م س and م س خ: effacement and transformation

م س خ concerns transformed form or condition.

The roots appear in adjacent verses, Yā Sīn 36:66 and Yā Sīn 36:67.

The first concerns eyes.

The second concerns transforming the people in their places.

Two different expressions are used.

Effacement and transformation may coincide in an event without becoming completely synonymous.

ط م س and د ر س: vanishing traces

One Arabic usage of د ر س concerns worn or vanished traces.

دَارِس can describe writing or a mark worn away over time.

طمس also concerns lost traces.

Early dictionaries consequently explain الطُّمُوس through الدُّرُوس.

Quranic درس also concerns reading and learning.

These branches should not all be collapsed into deterioration.

ط م س and خ ت م: effacing and sealing

خ ت م concerns sealing, ending, and closing.

Some Quranic passages describe sealed hearts or hearing in contexts of rejecting guidance.

Sealing differs from effacing.

Something may be sealed while retaining its shape and traces.

طمس instead concerns loss of a recognizable feature or effect.

Neither اشدد على قلوبهم in Yūnus 10:88 nor other expressions for sealed hearts should be silently replaced by طمس.

ط م س and ط س م: similar letters, different order

Some old dictionaries record طَسَمَ, reversing the final consonants, in a sense close to vanished traces.

The consonant sequences nevertheless differ.

Related meanings alone establish neither a direction of derivation nor a definitive shared history.

The Quranic forms studied here belong to ط م س and must not be reassigned by rearranging their letters.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal equivalents are effacing and becoming effaced.

Removing a trace, obscuring a sign, taking away sight, rendering ineffective, or extinguishing visible light may fit particular contexts.

طَمَسَ الأَثَرُ: the trace vanished.

طَمَسَ الطَّرِيقُ: the road's markers disappeared.

طَمَسْتُ الأَثَرَ: I effaced the trace.

انْطَمَسَ: became effaced or disappeared.

طَامِس: effaced, or having vanished signs, depending on the construction.

مَطْمُوس: something whose trace or feature has been effaced.

طُمُوس: effacement or disappearance of traces.

For the Quranic occurrences:

Al-Nisāʾ 4:47 — نَطْمِسَ وُجُوهًا

“We efface faces.”

This retains the image; the transformation's nature and interpretations involving guidance belong in the reflection.

Yūnus 10:88 — اطْمِسْ عَلَىٰ أَمْوَالِهِمْ

“Efface their wealth.”

Explanation can discuss destruction or lost usefulness, but should not impose conversion to stone on the translation.

Yā Sīn 36:66 — لَطَمَسْنَا عَلَىٰ أَعْيُنِهِمْ

“We would efface their eyes.”

The conditional force must be preserved.

Al-Qamar 54:37 — فَطَمَسْنَا أَعْيُنَهُمْ

“Then We effaced their eyes.”

This reports an event and must not be reduced to a hypothetical possibility.

Al-Mursalāt 77:8 — طُمِسَتْ

“Are effaced.”

In the full construction: “Then, when the stars are effaced.”

This preserves the root without predetermining a physical mechanism.

9. Summary

ط م س concerns effacing or losing traces, signs, form, or visible features.

Early usages describe road markers, writing, starlight, and sight.

Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr's early Islamic poem illustrates a route with vanished markers; Dhū al-Rummah's verse illustrates fading or concealed stars.

The exact pre-Islamic history of every usage is not established, and later witnesses should not be silently backdated.

The Quran contains five occurrences.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:47 places effaced faces within a warning and invitation to faith. Physical and guidance-related interpretations must be assessed from the passage, not settled by the root alone.

Yūnus 10:88 records Moses's request concerning Pharaoh's and his chiefs' wealth, used in misguidance; the mechanism of its effacement is not stated.

Yā Sīn 36:66 describes possible effacement of eyes and retains conditional force.

Al-Qamar 54:37 reports effacement of the aggressors' eyes as an event.

Al-Mursalāt 77:8 concerns stars amid the Day of Judgment's transformations.

Complete destruction is not inherent in every usage. A trace, form, function, or light may disappear.

The root concerns losing or removing a trace, sign, form, or recognizable feature so that what was visible or identifiable no longer appears as before.
