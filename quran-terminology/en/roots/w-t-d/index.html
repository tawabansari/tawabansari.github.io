Watad: a peg driven into the ground or a wall to secure, fasten, or hold something in place

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root w-t-d concerns pegs, driving them in, and securing things. Watad names a wooden or metal peg or nail driven into the ground or a wall so that something can be tied to it, held, or fixed in place.

A familiar use in early Arab life was securing tents. Tent ropes were tied to pegs driven into the ground, preventing the tent from moving easily when its fabric was pulled or the wind blew.

A tent supported only by poles may lose its balance in the wind. Fastening its ropes to pegs embedded in the ground makes the structure more secure.

The peg has two relevant features: part enters the ground, while another part provides a fastening or holding point.

These features allowed watad to extend beyond its physical meaning to descriptions of the stability of people, governments, and social structures.

A poetic witness attributed to al-Mutalammis

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Mutalammis states:

وَلَا يُقِيمُ بِدَارِ الذُّلِّ يَعْرِفُهَا

wa-lā yuqīmu bi-dāri al-dhulli yaʿrifuhā

إِلَّا الْأَذَلَّانِ عَيْرُ الْأَهْلِ وَالْوَتِدُ

illā al-adhallāni ʿayru al-ahli wa-al-watidu

“Only the two most abased remain in a dwelling whose humiliation they know: the household donkey and the peg.”

The poet describes a person who should not accept remaining in a condition of humiliation and injustice once he recognizes it.

He illustrates this through the tethered donkey and the peg driven into the ground.

A donkey may be tied with a rope and unable to leave. The peg is driven into the ground and stays there.

Here al-watad names an actual object, not a moral quality.

Its familiar property of being embedded and remaining in place supplies the literary image.

The verse appears in the collection attributed to al-Mutalammis and in early collections of Arabic proverbs. Some versions have a different expression concerning the household donkey in place of al-adhallān. The exact date of composition is not independently established.

Nevertheless, its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet and transmission in the early poetic tradition provide evidence for the old usage of watad.

A poetic witness attributed to al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur al-Nahshalī states:

وَلَقَدْ غَنُوا فِيهَا بِأَنْعَمِ عِيشَةٍ

wa-la-qad ghanaw fīhā bi-anʿami ʿīshatin

فِي ظِلِّ مُلْكٍ ثَابِتِ الْأَوْتَادِ

fī ẓilli mulkin thābiti al-awtādi

“They once lived there in the greatest ease, in the shade of a kingdom whose pegs stood firm.”

The poet describes people who once enjoyed ease and power, but whose life and kingdom eventually passed away.

Thābit al-awtād does not simply mean possessing a few ordinary pegs. The kingdom is pictured as a tent with pegs firmly embedded and a secure structure.

The peg thus becomes an image of the government's strength and stability.

This witness matters because it shows awtād describing the strength of rule in the poetic tradition attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic.

It does not establish that every Quranic occurrence means government or political power. The basic meaning remains “peg,” and figurative usage must be assessed in each context.

What the historical witnesses establish

The two witnesses illustrate two levels of meaning.

First, watad names a real peg driven into the ground to hold something in place.

Second, literary Arabic could use this object as an image of stability and endurance.

A defensible definition for early Arabic is therefore:

A peg or implement inserted into the ground or another surface to fix, hold, or fasten something securely.

Stability and endurance relate to its function; the word itself primarily names a particular object.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded forms include:

  • وَتَد، وَتِد، وَتْد: watad, watid, watd; a peg or nail driven into the ground or a wall.
  • أَوْتَاد: awtād; plural, pegs.
  • وَتَدَ: watada; he drove in a peg or secured something.
  • يَتِدُ: yatidu; he drives in a peg or fixes something.
  • وَتْد: watd; driving in pegs and securing.
  • وَتَّدَ: wattada; he secured or fixed with pegs.
  • تَوْتِيد: tawtīd; securing and making firm.
  • أَوْتَدَ: awtada; he drove in pegs or secured something with them.
  • مُوَتَّد: muwattad; secured with pegs, firm.
  • وَاتِد: wātid; fixed and stable, in descriptive usage.
  • مِيتَدَة: mītadah; an implement for driving in pegs, such as a hammer or heavy mallet.

Early dialects had different pronunciations of watad. Lexicographers even record wadd as a phonetic variant of the same word.

This variation must not be confused with the independent root w-d-d relating to affection.

Only the plural awtād occurs in the Quran; no conjugated verb from w-t-d appears.

The semantic core

Three elements can be distinguished in the primary use of watad.

First, an object, usually elongated, can be driven into the ground or another surface.

Second, it is secured in place.

Third, once positioned, it can hold something or help it remain fixed.

A peg driven into the ground with a tent rope attached helps hold the tent.

The peg alone is not the entire tent structure. Poles, fabric, ropes, and other components also contribute.

We should distinguish the object itself, its penetration into the ground, and its contribution to structural stability.

These features are related, but not everything stable is lexically called a watad.

Extended uses

Arabic sources also record awtād al-arḍ for mountains and awtād al-bilād for the leading figures of a land.

Mountains resemble pegs in their settled firmness.

Leaders are those on whom the social or political structure depends.

Awtād al-fam is also recorded for teeth, particularly in figurative language.

These names show how the peg's shape or function can motivate semantic extension.

Such comparisons do not erase the basic meaning. A tooth, mountain, and ruler are different things, although one aspect of each may be compared to a peg.

Watad in prosody

In Arabic prosody, watad names a component of poetic metre.

The terminology distinguishes watad maqrūn, also called majmūʿ, from watad mafrūq.

This naming concerns metrical structure and has a figurative affinity with the peg's supporting role.

The technical prosodic meaning should not be imposed on Quranic usage or the Arabic of the revelation period.

Quranic awtād does not mean units of poetic metre.

3. Quranic occurrences

The root w-t-d occurs three times in the Quran.

All three use the plural awtād: once concerning mountains and twice concerning Pharaoh.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:7: mountains as pegs

وَالْجِبَالَ أَوْتَادًا

wa-al-jibāla awtādā

Al-Nabaʾ 78:7

“And the mountains as pegs?”

This continues Al-Nabaʾ 78:6, which speaks of making the earth a resting place.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:6

أَلَمْ نَجْعَلِ الْأَرْضَ مِهَادًا

a-lam najʿali al-arḍa mihādā

Al-Nabaʾ 78:6

“Have We not made the earth a resting place,”

Together the verses compare the earth to a resting place for life and mountains to pegs.

Grammatically, al-jibāl is coordinated with al-arḍ in the preceding verse; awtādan occupies the second-object position of najʿal.

The statement thus conveys that God made the mountains as pegs.

It draws on the familiar character of a peg: firmly positioned and associated with securing and holding something.

Why are mountains compared to pegs?

Tent pegs are familiar tools for securing shelter.

When a peg enters the ground, the buried part helps it resist displacement and remain in place.

A mountain likewise appears as a great, solid mass rising from and connected to the earth.

These qualities provide a basis for the comparison.

The verse does not specify how far each mountain extends underground, what proportion lies beneath the surface, or its precise physical mechanism.

The Quranic image must therefore be distinguished from scientific models of mountain structure.

Modern geology recognizes deep crustal structures beneath many mountain ranges; crustal roots are discussed in connection with isostatic balance.

This knowledge can make an aspect of the peg comparison more tangible to a modern reader.

The word awtād itself does not establish that the Quran states a particular ratio between mountain height and root depth.

Nor do mountains mean the absence of earthquakes or tectonic activity. Many mountainous regions are geologically active.

The image must not become an absolute scientific claim that mountains prevent all ground shaking.

Relationship to the verses about rawāsī

Al-Naḥl 16:15 speaks of placing firm mountains in the earth so that it does not sway with people.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:31 and Luqmān 31:10 also connect firm mountains with the earth.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32, arsāhā concerns mountains and conveys establishing them firmly.

These passages matter for the image in Al-Nabaʾ 78:7.

Awtād evokes pegs and their securing function.

Rawāsī belongs to r-s-w and emphasizes settled firmness.

The families overlap in stability but are not complete synonyms.

Watad names an implement used for fixing; rusūw and rawāsī directly concern being settled and firm.

Calling mountains awtād employs the peg image; calling them rawāsī emphasizes their firm establishment.

This internal Quranic connection illuminates the image without specifying geological mechanisms.

Does mountain stability mean immutability?

No. Other Quranic passages describe mountains changing and breaking apart.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6, they are crushed and become scattered dust.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:20 describes mountains being set in motion and becoming a mirage.

Describing mountains as pegs in the familiar condition of the world does not make them unchanging or indestructible.

Stability in one condition is compatible with transformation in another.

4. Pharaoh, possessor of the pegs

Ṣād 38:12

كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ وَعَادٌ وَفِرْعَوْنُ ذُو الْأَوْتَادِ

kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu nūḥin wa-ʿādun wa-firʿawnu dhū al-awtād

Ṣād 38:12

“Before them, the people of Noah, ʿĀd, and Pharaoh, possessor of the pegs, denied.”

Dhū al-awtād describes Pharaoh.

Dhū means possessor or holder; al-awtād is the definite plural of watad.

The direct meaning is therefore “possessor of the pegs” or “holder of the pegs.”

Why is Pharaoh described this way?

We must distinguish the clear lexical meaning from identification of its historical referent.

First possibility: actual stakes and instruments of torment

One early explanation holds that Pharaoh used stakes to bind and torture people.

Some reports describe stakes to which a victim's hands and feet were tied.

This is lexically possible: a watad can secure a thing or a person.

The Quran also portrays Pharaoh as an oppressive ruler who persecuted the Children of Israel and killed their children.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:4, for example, describes his self-exaltation in the land and oppression of a group of people.

Instruments of torment are therefore compatible with this portrayal of his oppression.

But dhū al-awtād does not expressly identify the pegs as torture instruments.

Their number, how victims were bound, or the kind of punishment cannot be derived from this expression alone.

The possibility merits contextual consideration but should not replace the direct translation as a certain meaning.

Second possibility: political power and secure rule

Al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur's poetic use shows that awtād can picture the stability of a kingdom.

Mulk thābit al-awtād means rule or power whose foundations are secure.

Accordingly, “Pharaoh, possessor of the pegs” may picture a ruler whose power is secured by supporting instruments and forces.

Troops, officials, and other supporters can be compared to pegs holding up the tent of power.

Arabic literary usage supports this possibility.

Ṣād 38:11 mentions a defeated army and allied groups.

Ṣād 38:12 and Ṣād 38:13 then name earlier groups that denied the divine Messengers.

Pharaoh's description here may therefore relate to his political and military power.

Yet awtād does not directly mean “soldiers.”

On this reading, troops exemplify the image of stable rule; they are not the lexical definition of pegs.

Third possibility: substantial buildings and structures

Another explanation connects the title with buildings, large tents, or structures erected with pegs and ropes.

The Quran associates Pharaoh with political power and major construction.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38 mentions his order to Hāmān to build a lofty structure.

Ghāfir 40:36 and Ghāfir 40:37 likewise present his request for a high building.

A connection with structures displaying his building power is therefore conceivable.

These verses do not, however, call those buildings awtād.

Nor does this expression mention the Egyptian pyramids.

Equating the title with “possessor of the pyramids,” or a specific form of ancient Egyptian architecture, requires independent historical and linguistic evidence.

Which reading is preferable?

All three possibilities share the peg's familiar function of securing or firmly fastening something.

In the first, it is an actual instrument for binding and tormenting people.

In the second, it pictures the forces and supports securing rule.

In the third, it relates to structures and buildings associated with Pharaoh's power.

None is lexically impossible, but the Quranic expression itself provides insufficient evidence to establish one conclusively.

The base translation should therefore retain “possessor of the pegs,” with credible possibilities discussed in explanation.

5. The third occurrence: Pharaoh in Al-Fajr

Al-Fajr 89:10

وَفِرْعَوْنَ ذِي الْأَوْتَادِ

wa-firʿawna dhī al-awtād

Al-Fajr 89:10

“And with Pharaoh, possessor of the pegs,”

This has the same descriptive meaning as Ṣād 38:12 but a different grammatical construction.

In Ṣād 38:12, dhū is nominative.

In Al-Fajr 89:10, dhī is the genitive form of the same word.

Firʿawna is also syntactically genitive here, but as a diptote proper name it takes fatḥah as its genitive marker.

Dhū and dhī thus reflect grammatical position, not different titles or types of pegs.

The context of Al-Fajr

Al-Fajr 89:6 introduces the fate of ʿĀd.

Al-Fajr 89:7 contains Iram dhāt al-ʿimād.

Al-Fajr 89:9 mentions Thamūd, who cut rock in the valley.

Al-Fajr 89:10 then introduces Pharaoh as dhī al-awtād.

The following verses say these groups transgressed in the lands and increased corruption.

This continuity is significant.

The passages concern groups and powers with strength and resources that they used for transgression and corruption.

Pharaoh's title therefore occurs in a context of power and oppression.

The juxtaposition of dhāt al-ʿimād, rock cutting, and dhī al-awtād may also draw attention to structures and signs of material power.

That connection alone does not prove that awtād specifically names Pharaoh's buildings or pillars.

We should recognize the contextual clue of power and stability while retaining uncertainty about the historical referent.

Secure power does not establish legitimacy

In early Arabic poetry, a kingdom's firmly placed pegs may picture its strength and endurance.

Al-Fajr nevertheless shows that power and apparent strength do not by themselves establish a ruler's legitimacy.

Pharaoh had resources and power, yet is listed among those who transgressed and spread corruption.

There is a distinction between the ability to sustain a government and the rightness of its manner of ruling.

A ruler may possess troops, wealth, buildings, and many instruments. Using them for oppression means governmental strength does not amount to justice.

This follows from the context of Al-Fajr, not from watad alone.

6. Comparison with related roots

w-t-d and r-s-w: peg and settled firmness

Watad names an implement used to fix something.

Rusūw concerns being settled and firm.

Quranic rawāsī describes mountains.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:7 compares mountains to pegs; Al-Naḥl 16:15 describes them as rawāsī.

The expressions overlap in stability but have different semantic structures.

Watad supplies the image of a securing implement; rusūw emphasizes establishment itself.

w-t-d and ʿ-m-d: peg and pillar

ʿAmad, from ʿ-m-d, commonly concerns pillars and supporting structures.

Al-Fajr 89:7 contains dhāt al-ʿimād.

Both pillars and pegs can stabilize a building or tent, but their usual functions differ.

A pillar generally supports the structure from below or bears its weight.

A peg enters the ground and may hold ropes or other parts in position.

In a tent, the pole holds up the roof while the pegs secure the ropes.

This distinction is not an absolute rule governing every use of ʿamad and watad.

w-t-d and th-b-t: securing implement and stability itself

Thabata concerns remaining fixed, established, and firm.

Al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur's verse contains thābit al-awtād.

Thābit describes firmness, while awtād names the pegs.

The words do not have identical roles.

Thabāt is a state or quality; watad primarily names an object that can help create or preserve stability.

Figurative usage may retain this relationship.

w-t-d and r-b-ṭ: peg and tying

Rabaṭa concerns binding and connecting things.

When a rope is tied to a peg, the rope performs the binding while the peg provides an anchoring point.

Rabṭ and watad are connected here but are not synonyms.

The Quran also uses r-b-ṭ nonphysically, as in strengthening hearts.

This does not imply that the human heart has physical pegs and ropes.

w-t-d and w-th-q: peg and firm binding

W-th-q concerns firmness, trust, and secure binding.

Muḥammad 47:4 contains fa-shuddū al-wathāq in a context of warfare and taking captives.

The expression refers to firm binding, without itself naming a particular implement such as a peg.

A watad, by contrast, may be an implement used to hold or secure something.

Wathāq concerns the bond or firm fastening; watad names an object that may be used in that action.

w-t-d and d-q-q: peg and striking

Daqqa concerns pounding, striking, and crushing.

A peg may be driven into the ground with a hammer or stone.

Pounding is the action, while watad is the object placed in the ground.

Its meaning should not be reduced to striking itself.

Not every object struck is a watad.

w-t-d and w-t-r: peg and being odd

W-t-r concerns being odd or single and, in other branches, deprivation or blood vengeance.

W-t-d concerns pegs and their firm placement.

The roots differ only in their final consonant; similarity of sound or spelling must not merge them.

Al-watr in Al-Fajr 89:3 belongs to w-t-r, whereas al-awtād in Al-Fajr 89:10 belongs to w-t-d.

Their proximity in one surah does not establish a shared root or identical meaning.

7. Interpretive and translation challenges

Is a watad necessarily an iron nail?

No. Early Arabic watad could be made of wood or other materials.

Wooden pegs were familiar in tent life.

Awtād should therefore not invariably be translated as iron nails.

The material requires context or independent evidence.

Does watad mean a mountain root?

Al-Nabaʾ 78:7 compares mountains to pegs.

Because a peg enters the ground to become secure, comparison with the hidden, deep structures of some mountains can be explored.

But watad itself does not mean “geological mountain root.”

The base translation preserves “peg”; its relationship to mountains belongs in analysis.

The word likewise yields neither an exact above-ground to below-ground ratio nor a definite theory of plate movement.

Do mountains prevent all earthquakes?

Awtād itself does not support that conclusion.

Other Quranic verses discuss firm mountains and their role in the earth's condition, but these statements should not become an unexamined absolute claim that mountains stop every earthquake.

Conversely, earthquakes in mountainous regions do not by themselves invalidate the linguistic comparison with pegs.

The Quranic image and known scientific mechanisms should be distinguished.

Does dhū al-awtād certainly refer to Pharaoh's torture sites?

No. Torture stakes are one proposed reading; these two verses do not detail such a practice.

The reading concerning stable rule, and possible connections with structures, also have linguistic or contextual support.

None should replace the direct wording without decisive evidence.

Does the title mean possessor of the pyramids?

The expression does not state that.

The pyramids are known historical structures, but their existence alone does not establish that dhū al-awtād refers to them.

Such identification requires independent historical or linguistic evidence.

“Possessor of the pegs” is preferable in the base translation.

Is anything that provides stability a watad?

No. Securing is part of its familiar function, but the basic word names a particular implement.

A pillar, wall, stone base, or human force can also provide stability; they cannot all be called watad without a contextual basis.

Images such as the “pegs of rule” arise through comparison and semantic transfer, not the direct definition.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents suit recognized forms of this family.

وَتَد، وَتِد، وَتْد: watad, watid, watd; peg or nail driven into the ground or a wall.

أَوْتَاد: awtād; pegs.

وَتَدَ: watada; he drove in a peg, fixed something.

يَتِدُ: yatidu; he drives in pegs, fixes.

وَتَّدَ: wattada; he secured, fixed with pegs.

تَوْتِيد: tawtīd; securing, fixing with pegs.

مُوَتَّد: muwattad; secured with pegs, firm.

وَاتِد: wātid; fixed and stable, descriptively.

مِيتَدَة: mītadah; peg-driving implement.

Translating the three Quranic occurrences

Al-Nabaʾ 78:7 — أَوْتَادًا

“Pegs.”

Proposed Forqan translation:

“And the mountains as pegs?”

This retains the original image.

Ṣād 38:12 — ذُو الْأَوْتَادِ

“Possessor of the pegs.”

Proposed Forqan translation:

“Before them, the people of Noah, ʿĀd, and Pharaoh, possessor of the pegs, denied.”

Al-Fajr 89:10 — ذِي الْأَوْتَادِ

“Possessor of the pegs.”

Proposed Forqan translation:

“And with Pharaoh, possessor of the pegs,”

Both Pharaoh passages should retain the direct wording because the Quran does not identify the precise referent of the pegs.

“Possessor of mighty armies,” “possessor of torture sites,” and “possessor of pyramids” express interpretations and should not replace the original wording without conclusive evidence.

9. Conclusion

Early Arabic w-t-d concerns pegs and securing something with them.

Watad primarily names an implement driven into the ground or a wall to fix, hold, or firmly fasten something.

Early poetic witnesses attest both physical pegs and images of stable rule.

Only the plural awtād occurs in the Quran.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:7 compares mountains to pegs. The image concerns establishment and stability but does not independently specify the physics of mountain structure.

Ṣād 38:12 and Al-Fajr 89:10 describe Pharaoh as possessor of the pegs.

The title may concern actual stakes, instruments of torment, structures, or stable political power. The available evidence does not conclusively identify one referent.

All three uses retain the basic peg meaning; the difference lies in what is compared to pegs or described as possessing them.

Final Forqan definition:

In its primary branch, w-t-d concerns a peg inserted into the ground or another surface to fix, fasten, and hold something. Its verbs concern driving in pegs and securing. Figuratively, firmness and stability may be attributed to a mountain, government, or other structure.

Quranic awtād means “pegs”: once in the image of mountains and twice describing Pharaoh. The direct meaning is clear, but the historical referent of Pharaoh's title and scientific details of the mountain comparison require evidence beyond the root itself.
