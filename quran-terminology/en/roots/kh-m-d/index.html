Subsiding, extinction, and the end of living movement

The Arabic root خ م د concerns subsiding, becoming still, and the cessation of previously existing activity or movement. Its clearest physical application concerns fire: a flame that was rising dies down and ceases to blaze.

The root is not restricted to fire. Arabic also uses it for a fever subsiding, a person becoming quiet and motionless, fainting, and even death. These uses share the cessation or subsiding of a previously active, vigorous condition.

In the Qur’an, خ م د occurs twice, both times describing people with the participle khāmid. In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15, an unjust people become حَصِيدًا خَامِدِينَ after punishment overtakes them; in Yā Sīn 36:29, a people become خَامِدُونَ after a single cry.

In both verses, extinction extends beyond physical fire to the end of human life and movement. Nevertheless, the general meaning of subsiding must be distinguished from the specific meaning of death in these two contexts.

1. Forms and Derivatives

خ م د is a sound triliteral root whose basic verb is خَمَدَ ـ يَخْمُدُ (khamada–yakhmudu). Some sources also record خَمِدَ (khamida) in this family; this vocalization difference does not alter the Qur’anic forms خَامِدِينَ and خَامِدُونَ.

The principal recorded forms include:

  • خَمَدَ ـ يَخْمُدُ (khamada–yakhmudu): to subside, become still, or die down; of a person, in some uses, to faint or die.
  • خَمْد and خُمُود (khamd and khumūd): subsiding, stillness, and extinction.
  • خَامِد (khāmid): something or someone whose activity, flame, or movement has subsided.
  • خَامِدَة (khāmidah): the feminine of khāmid, including a description of an extinguished or subsiding fire.
  • أَخْمَدَ ـ يُخْمِدُ (akhmada–yukhmidu): to cause something to become extinguished or subside, as in extinguishing fire.
  • إِخْمَاد (ikhmād): the act of extinguishing or subduing.
  • مُخْمِد (mukhmid): in recorded early uses, someone quiet, silent, or still; in a transitive construction, someone causing something to become extinguished.
  • الخَمُّود (al-khammūd): a place in which fire is covered or buried so that it dies down.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an contains only the active participle خَامِد (khāmid), in two inflected forms:

خَامِدِينَ (khāmidīna) — Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15, sound masculine plural in the accusative.

خَامِدُونَ (khāmidūna) — Yā Sīn 36:29, sound masculine plural in the nominative.

These forms do not have two independent lexical meanings. Their different endings result from their grammatical roles in the sentences.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two active-participle forms in two verses

The semantic core in the lexicons

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one underlying principle for this root:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، يَدُلُّ عَلَى سُكُونِ الْحَرَكَةِ وَالسُّقُوطِ.

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā sukūni al-ḥarakati wa-al-suqūṭi

Meaning:

“It has one underlying principle, indicating the stilling of movement and falling.”

He then lists the dying down of a fire’s flame, the subsiding of a fever’s heat, and a person fainting among the root’s applications.

This account shows that khumūd is not limited to simple stillness without a preceding condition. It often describes something previously active, hot, or moving that subsequently subsides.

A fire’s flame subsides; a fever’s intense heat abates; a person’s visible movement and activity disappear.

The semantic core may therefore be described as the subsiding and stilling of existing activity or movement.

This definition does not mean that every use necessarily involves death or total destruction. The extent and outcome of extinction must be determined from its subject and context.

2. The Physical Image: A Fire Dying Down

The flame subsides while embers remain

One familiar early Arabic expression is:

خَمَدَتِ النَّارُ

khamadati al-nāru

Meaning: “The fire’s flame subsided.”

Al-Aṣmaʿī, as transmitted by Abū ʿUbayd in Tahdhīb al-Lughah, distinguishes two conditions.

When a fire’s flame has subsided but its embers remain, the expression خَمَدَتِ النَّارُ (khamadati al-nāru) is used.

When its embers too have become completely extinguished, هَمَدَتْ (hamadat) is used.

This distinction is also recorded in Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and in Lisān al-ʿArab.

To picture it, consider a wood fire. Initially, flames rise and heat is evident. As the fuel diminishes, the flames die down, but embers remain among the ashes.

Under this distinction, the fire is khāmid even though its heat has not entirely disappeared.

If the embers also become cold and no trace of burning remains, extinction is complete.

This lexical distinction, however, should not become an absolute rule.

Other dictionaries, including Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ, record both a flame subsiding while embers remain and a fire becoming completely extinguished for خَمَدَتِ النَّارُ.

Thus, embers may remain in some uses of khumūd, but this is not a necessary component of every application of the root.

Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ: subsiding with embers remaining and complete extinction

This distinction matters for understanding the Qur’anic verses. The possible survival of embers in an actual fire cannot establish that people described as khāmidīn retain some of their bodily life.

The verse concerns people; its final meaning must be determined by the context of their destruction.

Fever and intense heat subsiding

Arabic uses خَمَدَتِ الْحُمَّى (khamadati al-ḥummā) for a fever subsiding.

In this application, fever is compared to fire because of its intense heat.

When the intensity of a fever diminishes, it can be said to have subsided in this sense.

A fever subsiding does not necessarily mean that the illness has disappeared completely.

Body temperature may have fallen while the underlying illness remains.

This use shows that khumūd can express the reduction or cessation of an intense condition without making a judgment about every other aspect of its subject.

Stillness, motionlessness, and fainting

The lexicons record خَمَدَ الْمَرِيضُ (khamada al-marīḍu) for a sick person who has fainted or died.

In the material called Nawādir al-Aʿrāb, مُخْمِد (mukhmid) also describes someone silent and motionless.

These applications relate to the image of activity subsiding.

A living person normally moves, breathes, speaks, and responds. When movement and responsiveness decrease drastically or cease, language can describe the person as becoming extinguished or still.

Fainting and death must nevertheless be distinguished.

An unconscious person may remain alive, whereas death means the end of bodily life.

The conditions resemble one another in some outward signs, such as immobility and lack of response, but differ in their reality and outcome.

Recording both meanings for خَمَدَ الْمَرِيضُ therefore shows that the expression alone does not always determine the precise bodily condition without context.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: fire, fever, fainting, and death

3. Early Arabic Usage and Poetry

The fire of hospitality in Arab life

In pre-Islamic Arab life, keeping a fire burning at night could serve to guide travelers and receive guests.

A traveler moving through darkness might see a flame from a distance and head toward it.

In some Arabic poetry, fire is consequently more than a means of heating or cooking: it can signal hospitality, human presence, and readiness to receive guests.

Such a fire dying down might prevent a traveler or guest from finding the place.

This social setting helps explain khumūd in relation to fire. The flame subsiding removes not only heat but also light and a visible sign of people’s presence.

A witness attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes the following verse from Labīd ibn Rabīʿah:

وَجَدْتُ أَبِي رَبِيعًا لِلْيَتَامَى
وَلِلضِّيفَانِ إِذْ خَمَدَ الْفَئِيدُ

wajadtu abī rabīʿan li-al-yatāmā / wa-li-al-ḍīfāni idh khamada al-faʾīdu

Translation:

“I found my father a springtime for orphans and guests, when the fire had died down.”

Al-faʾīd in this verse means fire.

The lexical commentary explains خَمَدَ الْفَئِيدُ as the flame subsiding at night, potentially preventing a guest or nighttime traveler from seeing the fire.

The poet presents his father as a source of benefit for orphans and guests, placing this description alongside the dying down of fire.

The wording of the verse, however, differs between sources.

One edition of Labīd’s dīwān gives the second hemistich as وَلِلْأَضْيَافِ إِذْ حُبَّ الْفَئِيدُ: it records ḥubba in place of khamada.

Since the variant affects the very word under study, the verse cannot be treated as an undisputed, definitive witness to khamada in Labīd’s poetry.

Nevertheless, the wording خَمَدَ الْفَئِيدُ is recorded in early lexicons, and its meaning agrees with the root’s familiar application to fire.

Labīd was a mukhaḍram poet who lived both before and after the advent of Islam. Even if the attribution is accepted, the date of this particular verse cannot be established conclusively from its text.

Labīd’s dīwān: the different wording ḥubba al-faʾīd

Mukhmid in another witness from Labīd

Lisān al-ʿArab also quotes another hemistich attributed to Labīd:

مِثْلُ الَّذِي بِالْغِيلِ يَقْرُو مُخْمِدًا

mithlu alladhī bi-al-ghīli yaqrū mukhmidan

Its lexical explanation glosses mukhmid as quiet and silent: someone or some creature that has prepared or steadied itself to face a matter. “The one in the thicket” should not be restricted to a human being without the full context.

Here khumūd is not limited to fire dying down; it describes the composed, steady condition of the creature being portrayed.

The witness quoted under khamada is a hemistich. Under dūna, Lisān al-ʿArab gives a fuller version with يَغْزُو (yaghzū) in place of يَقْرُو (yaqrū), explaining it as advancing despite threats. The quotation above is therefore retained according to the khamada entry, without deriving absolute immobility or definite psychological details from it.

The lexicon explains mukhmid through stillness and composure; in the witness itself, such composure and steadiness can coexist with movement and advance.

Lisān al-ʿArab, dūna: a fuller quotation with yaghzū

Together, the early witnesses show applications to fire, stillness, and human conditions. They do not provide sufficient evidence to establish the historical order in which these meanings developed.

Lisān al-ʿArab, khamada: Labīd’s witnesses and the explanation of mukhmid

4. Qur’anic Applications

A. Ḥaṣīdan khāmidīna — Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15

فَمَا زَالَتْ تِلْكَ دَعْوَاهُمْ حَتَّىٰ جَعَلْنَاهُمْ حَصِيدًا خَامِدِينَ

fa-mā zālat tilka daʿwāhum ḥattā jaʿalnāhum ḥaṣīdan khāmidīna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“That remained their cry until We made them like a reaped crop, extinguished and lifeless.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15

The verse occurs in the context of the destruction of unjust settlements.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11 speaks of unjust settlements that were shattered. The following verses describe their inhabitants’ response upon perceiving the punishment.

They first try to flee; they are then told to return to the comforts they had enjoyed.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:14 they confess:

يَا وَيْلَنَا إِنَّا كُنَّا ظَالِمِينَ

yā waylanā innā kunnā ẓālimīna

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “Woe to us! We were wrongdoers.”

Verse fifteen then describes their end:

حَصِيدًا خَامِدِينَ

ḥaṣīdan khāmidīna

Two images appear together in this expression.

First, ḥaṣīdan, which relates to reaping vegetation.

While vegetation stands and grows, it forms part of the earth’s living landscape. Once reaped, it leaves that previous condition and falls to the ground.

Second, khāmidīna, from khumūd, expressing subsiding and extinction.

This participle describes people overtaken by destruction.

Together, the expressions portray the end of the group’s activity and life: laid low like a reaped crop, they have ceased to move and live like an extinguished fire.

Why is ḥaṣīdan singular while khāmidīna is plural?

In the verse, جَعَلْنَاهُمْ (jaʿalnāhum) contains the plural object pronoun hum, “them.”

Ḥaṣīdan is singular accusative and, in a familiar grammatical analysis, is the second object of jaʿala.

The word describes a reaped collection and can therefore remain singular while referring to multiple individuals.

Khāmidīna, however, is a sound masculine plural in the accusative, describing the people’s condition.

Several grammatical analyses are possible: khāmidīna may be a circumstantial expression referring to hum, or it may join ḥaṣīdan in the descriptions expressing the resulting condition produced by jaʿalnāhum.

In either analysis, the principal meaning is clear: the verse describes a group reduced to a reaped and extinguished condition.

The grammatical alternatives do not change the meaning of خ م د.

Rūḥ al-Maʿānī: grammatical analyses of ḥaṣīdan khāmidīna

Does khāmidīna mean merely becoming silent?

Arabic can use khāmid for someone silent and still. Here, however, the subject is not the end of an ordinary conversation or the subsiding of an everyday dispute.

The preceding verses speak of punishment arriving and unjust settlements being shattered.

In this context, khāmidīna therefore means the group’s destruction and the end of its life, rather than temporary silence.

“Extinguished and lifeless” consequently conveys the contextual meaning better than “calm” or “silent” alone.

Nevertheless, death should not be presented as a necessary element of every application of the root. Arabic uses the same root for a fever abating or a flame subsiding.

Al-Ṭabarī: khāmidīna in the context of destruction and movement ceasing

B. Fa-idhā hum khāmidūna — Yā Sīn 36:29

إِنْ كَانَتْ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً فَإِذَا هُمْ خَامِدُونَ

in kānat illā ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan fa-idhā hum khāmidūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“It was only a single cry; then suddenly they were extinguished and lifeless.”

Yā Sīn 36:29

This verse belongs to the account of a people who rejected the messengers as false.

In the preceding verses, a man comes from the farthest part of the city and urges his people to follow the messengers. He emphasizes following those who ask for no payment and are rightly guided.

He then declares his faith. The account goes on to describe his admission to the Garden and his wish that his people knew what The God had granted him.

Yā Sīn 36:28 states that no army was sent down from heaven against his people afterward.

The next verse states the outcome in a short, forceful expression:

إِنْ كَانَتْ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً

in kānat illā ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “It was only a single cry.”

Then comes:

فَإِذَا هُمْ خَامِدُونَ

fa-idhā hum khāmidūna

Here khāmidūna is the predicate of the subject hum, appearing as a nominative sound masculine plural.

In this construction, idhā expresses sudden occurrence or surprise.

The sentence thus portrays a rapid change of condition.

A people who had been active, speaking, opposing, and taking a position against the messengers become extinguished and lifeless through a single cry.

The contrast between the cry and extinction

The contrast between ṣayḥah and khāmidūna is significant.

Ṣayḥah denotes a cry or powerful sound, whereas khumūd, when applied to people here, portrays the end of movement and activity.

The verse thus moves from a cry to the people’s complete extinction.

The sound and its outcome must nevertheless be distinguished. Khumūd itself does not mean hearing a sound or experiencing the physical effect of a sound wave.

What the Qur’an explicitly describes is a single cry and the people’s sudden destruction. The details of its physical mechanism cannot be derived from khāmidūna itself.

Nor does the verse identify a particular weapon, explosion, or natural phenomenon.

“A single cry” suffices to describe the event; khāmidūna describes the people’s resulting condition.

Does khāmidūna mean death?

In this context, yes: death and destruction are understood from the verse and narrative together.

In the explanation attributed to Al-Zajjāj in Lisān al-ʿArab, khāmidūna describes those who have become silent and died, like the ashes of an extinguished fire.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains فَإِذَا هُمْ خَامِدُونَ as their destruction.

This meaning fits the verse’s construction: a single cry is presented as the destructive event, followed immediately by the people’s extinction as its outcome.

Khāmidūna here therefore means more than reduced activity, subdued protest, or silence after fear; it describes the end of their lives.

Al-Ṭabarī: one cry and the people’s destruction

Comparing the two Qur’anic applications

Both verses describe people with the participle khāmid, and both concern destruction.

Their imagery, however, differs.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15, khāmidīna accompanies ḥaṣīdan. The images of reaping and extinction jointly describe a group’s end.

In Yā Sīn 36:29, khāmidūna follows “a single cry” and emphasizes the people’s sudden change of condition.

The first foregrounds being laid low and extinguished; the second foregrounds the speed and suddenness of the outcome.

Nevertheless, the root’s contextual meaning points to the same outcome in both: the cessation of living movement and activity, reaching death and destruction.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

KH M D (khumūd) and H M D (humūd)

These roots are close in Arabic, particularly when describing fire.

Some early explanations assign khumūd to a flame subsiding while embers remain, and humūd to complete extinction.

This distinction, however, does not establish an absolute boundary in every Arabic application.

ه م د also applies to barren land, a dry tree, a voice becoming quiet, and the end of activity.

An excerpt from Al-Ḥajj 22:5 reads:

وَتَرَى الْأَرْضَ هَامِدَةً فَإِذَا أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْهَا الْمَاءَ اهْتَزَّتْ وَرَبَتْ

wa-tarā al-arḍa hāmidatan fa-idhā anzalnā ʿalayhā al-māʾa ihtazzat wa-rabat

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt:

“You see the earth dormant and still; then, when We send water down upon it, it stirs and swells.”

In this verse, hāmidah describes the earth.

Humūd can thus describe land without visible signs of growth or movement that changes when water arrives.

By contrast, khāmidīna and khāmidūna in the two verses under study concern people who have perished.

Accordingly, not every instance of humūd means certain death. Equally, the possibility of embers remaining in a fire cannot reduce the description of people as khāmid in these destruction passages to temporary unconsciousness.

KH M D (khumūd) and Ṭ F ʾ (ṭufūʾ)

ط ف أ concerns fire or light becoming extinguished and the act of extinguishing it.

Al-Tawbah 9:32 reads يُرِيدُونَ أَنْ يُطْفِئُوا نُورَ اللَّهِ (yurīdūna an yuṭfiʾū nūra allāhi), whereas Al-Ṣaff 61:8 has a different construction: يُرِيدُونَ لِيُطْفِئُوا نُورَ اللَّهِ (yurīdūna li-yuṭfiʾū nūra allāhi). Both concern the wish to extinguish the light, but their Arabic wording should not be quoted as identical.

Iṭfāʾ here concerns the act of extinguishing light.

Khumūd, in its principal field, can express the subsiding and extinction of an active condition.

The roots overlap in reference to fire but are not identical in grammatical construction or semantic range.

In particular, Arabic also uses khamada for fever, stillness, and death.

Their difference does not establish that iṭfāʾ always means complete extinction and khumūd always incomplete extinction. Linguistic evidence does not support that absolute division.

KH M D (khumūd) and S K N (sukūn)

س ك ن encompasses a broad field of becoming calm, stillness, settling, and dwelling.

Someone may become calm and settle in a place without their activity or life ending.

Familiar uses of khumūd, by contrast, often concern the subsiding of something previously active or intense.

For example, a blazing fire dies down, or an intense fever abates.

Khumūd can therefore involve a kind of stillness, but not every instance of stillness is khumūd.

In the two Qur’anic verses, khāmid does not mean ordinary calm or residence; it describes the end of the people’s life and movement.

Study of S K N

KH M D (khumūd) and M W T (mawt)

The Qur’an explicitly uses mawt for death, opposed to life.

خ م د, however, primarily concerns extinction and subsiding and acquires the meaning of death only in some applications.

Māta and khamada are consequently not complete synonyms.

خَمَدَتِ النَّارُ does not describe a living creature’s death. خَمَدَتِ الْحُمَّى does not mean that the patient has died.

When the Qur’an describes a group under punishment as khāmidīn, however, the context shows that their extinction has reached death and destruction.

This is the distinction between the root’s meaning and its specific meaning in a verse.

Khāmidīn in this context also describes the end of their lives. The word alone does not establish the absolute annihilation of human existence or the nature of life after death.

The Qur’an addresses those questions through other verses and concepts. Details about the nature of death or human return should not be extracted from the image of a fire dying down.

Study of M W T

KH M D (khumūd) and Ḥ Ṣ D (ḥaṣd)

In its physical application, ح ص د concerns reaping vegetation and crops.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15 places ḥaṣīdan and khāmidīna together.

The two words do not have the same meaning.

Ḥaṣīd introduces the image of reaped vegetation, whereas khāmid expresses subsiding and extinction.

Together, the images point to one outcome: the end of the people’s previous condition.

Khāmidīna should therefore not be translated as “reaped,” just as ḥaṣīdan itself does not mean a fire becoming extinguished.

Study of Ḥ Ṣ D

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish the root’s general Arabic uses from its Qur’anic applications.

In Arabic, خَمَدَتِ النَّارُ can mean “the fire’s flame subsided” or, where appropriate, “the fire went out.”

خَمَدَتِ الْحُمَّى means “the fever subsided.”

In the two Qur’anic verses, however, because the participle describes destroyed groups, “extinguished and lifeless” or “lifeless and motionless” conveys the meaning more precisely.

“Silent” alone is insufficient in these verses: both concern the end of life rather than a temporary cessation of speech.

Conversely, “dead” alone conveys the contextual outcome while weakening the lexical image of extinction and subsiding.

Brief root meaning

The subsiding and extinction of flame, heat, or activity; of people, becoming quiet and still, or the end of life.

Meanings in the verses

Khāmidīna — Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15: extinguished and lifeless; people whose life and movement ended under punishment.

Khāmidūna — Yā Sīn 36:29: extinguished and lifeless; a people suddenly destroyed after a single cry.

The brief root meaning introduces the general semantic range, whereas the contextual meanings describe its specific outcome in the destruction passages.

Conclusion

Arabic خ م د concerns the subsiding and stilling of activity and movement. A fire whose flames die down is a clear example. Early usage also extends this meaning to a fever abating, human stillness, fainting, and death.

Some lexical accounts distinguish khumūd from humūd in reference to fire: the former involves a flame subsiding while embers remain, the latter complete extinction. Other evidence shows that the distinction is not conclusive in every application.

The Qur’an contains only the derivative khāmid, occurring twice, both times concerning people overtaken by destruction.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15, they become extinguished and lifeless like a reaped crop and a fire that has died down. In Yā Sīn 36:29, one cry suffices for the people to cease their activity and life.

Thus, the basic meaning of khumūd is movement or activity subsiding and becoming extinguished; in its two Qur’anic applications, this extinction means human death and destruction.

This distinction preserves the root’s principal image while preventing features of fire, such as the possible survival of embers, from being transferred without evidence to the reality of human death. Brief root meaning: The subsiding and extinction of flame, heat, or activity; of people, becoming quiet and still, or the end of life.
