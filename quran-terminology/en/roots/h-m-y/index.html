The Qur’anic Root H M Y

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(ḥāmin)
Al-Māʾidah 5:103; defective active participle, a customary male-camel designation

يُحْمَىٰ
(yuḥmā)

الْحَمِيَّةَ
(al-ḥamiyyata)

حَمِيَّةَ الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ

From heat and protection to protective zeal, partisanship, and invented restrictions

The Arabic root ح م ي encompasses several semantic fields. حَمِيَ describes something becoming warm or hot, such as fire, iron, or water. حَمَى means to protect, ward off harm, or prevent others from gaining access. حِمَى denotes a place or area from which others are excluded or whose use is restricted. In human conduct, حَمِيَّة refers to protective zeal, emotional arousal, and a response in defense of oneself, one’s group, or something to which one is attached.

These meanings are not identical. Heating iron is a physical event; protecting someone is an action; zeal or partisanship is a human response that may lead either to defending a right or to insisting on falsehood. The derivatives of this family therefore cannot all be translated simply as “heat,” “protection,” or “zeal.”

In the Qur’an, the root ح م ي occurs in three settings: heated metal and the intense heat of fire; حام, a customary designation for a male camel that the Qur’an does not recognize as divinely instituted; and حمية الجاهلية, referring to partisanship and resistance to accepting what is right.

Qur’anic forms: This root occurs six times in five verses across five surahs: حَامٍ in Al-Māʾidah 5:103; يُحْمَىٰ in Al-Tawbah 9:35; الْحَمِيَّةَ and حَمِيَّةَ in Al-Fatḥ 48:26; حَامِيَةً in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:4; and حَامِيَةٌ in Al-Qāriʿah 101:11.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: six occurrences in five verses

Other forms recorded in Arabic include حَمَى, حَمِيَ, حِمَى, حِمَايَة, حَامِي, حُمَاة, أَحْمَى, إِحْمَاء, حَامَى, مُحَامَاة, احْتَمَى, حِمْيَة, and مَحْمِيّ. These exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. Semantic centers and early Arabic usage

حَمِيَ: increasing heat

In Al-ʿAyn, حَمِيَ الشَّيْءُ describes something that has become hot. The verb is used of iron, fire, sunlight, and even an animal’s body.

حَمِيَتِ النَّارُ means that the fire became hot and burning. حَمِيَ النَّهَارُ describes a day whose heat has intensified.

In the same family, أَحْمَى الْحَدِيدَ means to heat iron.

Two situations must be distinguished: something may become hot, or an agent may heat it. حَمِيَ الْحَدِيدُ describes the iron becoming hot; أَحْمَيْتُ الْحَدِيدَ describes someone heating it.

Lexical sources also record الحَمْي and الحُمُوّ for intense heat.

Al-ʿAyn, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: heat and verbal nouns

This branch does not mean every kind of change in a substance. Iron can bend, break, cool, or become hot; the established thermal meaning of حمي applies to the last of these situations.

In the Qur’an, this branch appears in Al-Tawbah 9:35, Al-Ghāshiyah 88:4, and Al-Qāriʿah 101:11.

حَمَى: protecting and keeping harm away

حَمَى الشَّيْءَ يَحْمِيهِ means to protect something, defend it, or prevent encroachment upon it.

Al-ʿAyn records the verb for looking after people and preventing harm from reaching them.

In Lisān al-ʿArab, حَمَى الْقَوْمَ means to defend a people and keep their enemy away.

Three participants can be identified in this usage: the object protected, the person or force providing protection, and whatever is prevented from reaching the protected object.

For example, someone may protect family members or companions by standing against an attacker.

Protection does not always involve fighting. It can be achieved through care, prevention of danger, restrictions on access, or appropriate precautions.

Conversely, not every restriction is ethical or legitimate. A person may protect someone else’s right or wrongfully monopolize a resource that belongs to everyone.

The meaning “protection” therefore does not by itself establish the legitimacy of what is protected or of the means used.

حِمَى: a protected place or restricted area

حِمَى denotes land reserved by a person or group for themselves or their livestock, excluding others from entering it or grazing there.

In early Arabic, the word was associated with pastures and land whose access was restricted through power or agreement.

حَمَيْتُ الْمَكَانَ means that I protected a place from others’ access or barred them from it. أَحْمَيْتُ الْمَكَانَ is also recorded for declaring a place a protected reserve.

Some lexical reports distinguish between announcing a prohibition and its actual acceptance among people.

Thus حِمَى can in one situation be a place of safekeeping, and in another an area from whose use others are wrongfully excluded.

This distinction matters when reading Qur’anic verses about what is permitted or forbidden and the attribution of rulings to The God.

Yet حِمَى itself does not mean a divine prohibition. Who may restrict access to land or resources, and how valid that restriction is, require independent grounds and consideration of the circumstances.

حَامِي: a protector or defender

حَامِي is the active participle of حَمَى and denotes someone who protects a person, group, or thing.

Its plural is حُمَاة.

In Arabic, حَامِيَةُ الْقَوْمِ can denote a group who defend their people against an enemy.

Al-ʿAyn records حامية for both an individual defender and a group of protectors.

In some usages, the word denotes those who remain at the rear during a retreat to protect others.

But حامي in Al-Māʾidah 5:103 does not have the general meaning of a military protector. There it is a particular designation used in customs concerning camels.

This use must be distinguished from the general meaning of the verb “to protect.”

حِمْيَة: abstention to prevent harm

حِمْيَة, with kasrah on the first consonant and sukūn on the second, conventionally means abstaining from something harmful.

حَمَى الْمَرِيضَ مَا يَضُرُّهُ means that someone kept a patient from what would harm them.

احْتَمَى الْمَرِيضُ is also used of a patient who avoids harmful food or conduct.

Here, abstention is a form of protection: by refraining from something, a person avoids its potential danger.

This meaning must not be conflated with حَمِيَّة in Al-Fatḥ 48:26.

حِمْيَة, “abstention,” and حَمِيَّة, “protective zeal, arousal, or partisanship,” are distinct usages whose Arabic forms and vowels must be preserved.

Nor is every dietary abstention a religious ruling. A person may avoid a food for health reasons without being entitled to declare that food divinely forbidden for everyone.

Lisān al-ʿArab: protection, reserves, and abstention

حَمِيَّة: protective zeal and emotional arousal

In Arabic, حَمِيَّة denotes a response involving a strong sense of defense, zeal, or refusal.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains this meaning through أَنَفَة and غَيْرَة.

Here أَنَفَة denotes a disposition in which a person regards doing or accepting something as disgraceful and refuses it.

Arabic also uses حَمِيَ أَنْفُهُ of someone whose zeal has been stirred or who feels that their standing is being challenged.

This response may lead to defending what is right, but it may also draw a person into insisting upon a false claim.

For example, someone who resists humiliation or the violation of others’ rights may direct their zeal toward justice.

But when someone refuses to accept the truth merely to preserve reputation, power, or their group’s superiority, that same arousal becomes harmful partisanship.

Consequently, حمية in Arabic is not inherently equivalent to disbelief, injustice, or wrongdoing.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:26, the word is qualified by الجاهلية. This qualifier and the conduct of the group being discussed identify the censured kind of zeal.

Do heat and zeal have a common origin?

An understandable resemblance exists between intense heat and intense anger or zeal.

Everyday language can likewise describe an angry person as becoming heated or boiling over.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī proposes a semantic connection between intense heat and the arousal of anger.

This resemblance does not establish with certainty that the meanings of protection and zeal historically developed from the heat of fire in Arabic.

The lexicons clearly record usages involving both heat and protection. Their relationship can be investigated, but a possible image must not replace historical evidence.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: the proposed connection between heat and arousal

Moreover, defending a right does not always involve anger or intense emotion. Someone can protect another’s rights calmly, deliberately, and thoughtfully.

Protection must therefore not be reduced to an intense emotional response.

2. Early witnesses: protection, zeal, and defense

Defending honor in Al-Mutalammis’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Al-Mutalammis al-Ḍubaʿī says of his bond with his people:

أَلَا إِنَّنِي مِنْهُمْ وَعِرْضِي عِرْضُهُمْ
كَذَا الرَّأْسُ يَحْمِي أَنْفَهُ أَنْ يُكْشَمَا

a-lā innanī minhum wa-ʿirḍī ʿirḍuhum / kadhā al-raʾsu yaḥmī anfahu an yukshamā

“Know that I am one of them, and my honor is their honor, just as the head protects its nose from injury and cutting.”

In this verse, يَحْمِي means “protects.”

The poet compares his relationship with his people to that of the head and nose: injury to one part also concerns the other parts.

The image shows that protection in Arabic could extend from physical safeguarding to the defense of honor and social bonds.

The verse is transmitted in early literary and lexical sources, including commentaries on Al-Fatḥ 48:26.

The second hemistich and its ending have variant transmissions, including يُكْشَمَا and يُهْشَمَا and differing versions of the hemistich’s opening. The version reproduced here appears in Al-Ṭabarī’s transmission. These differences do not change the basic meaning of يَحْمِي: protecting.

Al-Ṭabarī: Al-Mutalammis’s witness and the zeal of ignorance

The witness matters because it illustrates defending honor in Arabic. Yet defending honor is not always a virtue irrespective of truth and justice.

حامية in Labīd’s poetry

In one of his odes, Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

وَمَعِي حَامِيَةٌ مِنْ جَعْفَرٍ
كُلَّ يَوْمٍ تَبْتَلِي مَا فِي الْخِلَلِ

wa-maʿī ḥāmiyatun min jaʿfarin / kulla yawmin tabtalī mā fī al-khilali

“With me is a band of defenders from [Banū] Jaʿfar, who each day test what lies in the scabbards.” In this reading, تَبْتَلِي is active and means “tests”; خِلَل is the plural of خِلَّة, a sword’s scabbard. The image concerns testing swords in action, rather than the group itself being tested. Some lexical transmissions have نَبْتَلِي; تَبْتَلِي is recorded in the Dīwān and the transmission in Kitāb al-Afʿāl.

Kitāb al-Afʿāl: Labīd’s witness with tabtalī and transmission variants

حَامِيَة here denotes a group of warriors and defenders.

This usage shows that the word could denote a group performing the task of defense and protection.

Labīd was a mukhaḍram poet who lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating this particular ode, it should not be presented as a certainly pre-Islamic witness.

Its usage in his poetry and the early lexicons is nevertheless clear.

Lisān al-ʿArab, KH L L: khilal as sword scabbards

Heat and a change in a substance’s condition

In everyday Arabic, حَمِيَ الْحَدِيدُ described iron becoming hot in a fire.

The material remains iron, but its temperature increases and it can affect something else.

This usage matters for Al-Tawbah 9:35, which describes heating something in fire and then branding bodies with it.

Here heat is a physical property. Moral meanings such as anger, zeal, or partisanship must not be transferred into this usage.

3. حَامٍ: a customary designation for a male camel in Al-Māʾidah 5:103

مَا جَعَلَ اللَّهُ مِنْ بَحِيرَةٍ وَلَا سَائِبَةٍ وَلَا وَصِيلَةٍ وَلَا حَامٍ وَلَٰكِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا يَفْتَرُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ وَأَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

mā jaʿala allāhu min baḥīratin wa-lā sāʾibatin wa-lā waṣīlatin wa-lā ḥāmin wa-lākinna alladhīna kafarū yaftarūna ʿalā allāhi al-kadhiba wa-aktharuhum lā yaʿqilūna

Translation: “The God has not instituted any baḥīrah, sāʾibah, waṣīlah, or ḥām. Rather, those who disbelieve fabricate lies against The God, and most of them do not reason.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:103

In this verse, حَامٍ is an active participle of حَمَى. In context, however, this form was an established customary designation for a certain male camel among some Arabs.

Lexical and historical reports state that when a male camel reached a particular number or stage in breeding, people said that it had protected its back.

In some customs, they would then no longer load or ride it, or would restrict access to some of its uses.

The meaning of حامي in this usage

Al-Farrāʾ and other lexicographers explain the term as a male camel withdrawn from ordinary use after breeding on a number of occasions.

Some reports mention ten occasions of mating or reproduction. Another makes the camel’s descendants reaching a particular stage the criterion for the designation.

These differences show that the details of the custom concerning حام are not identical in every transmission.

The common feature is clear: the camel was placed in a condition that prevented others from making ordinary use of it.

In this context, حامي is consequently a particular name for a customary status, rather than simply “guard” or “defender.”

Al-Ṭabarī: differing reports about ḥām and other customary designations

Does The God reject protecting animals as such?

Several customary designations are listed together in this verse, and their attribution to The God is then rejected.

The important point is that the Qur’an denies that these designations were divinely instituted, and then speaks of fabricating lies against The God.

The issue is therefore the attribution of humanly invented regulations and restrictions to The God.

This does not mean that resting an animal, protecting it from harm, or limiting strenuous work for an exhausted animal is inherently wrong.

Such actions may be justified by an animal’s actual needs and human responsibility toward it.

Turning a custom concerning camels into a religious ruling and attributing it to The God is a different matter.

Practical care for animals must thus be distinguished from creating an unsupported religious designation.

Connection with inventing permissions and prohibitions

Al-Māʾidah 5:103 belongs to a group of verses addressing provision, what is permitted or forbidden, and the attribution of rulings to The God.

Al-Anʿām 6:138 and Al-Anʿām 6:139 also describe regulations that a group devised about livestock and crops and attributed to The God.

These verses show that claims of religious legislation require evidence.

Yet حام itself does not mean “forbidden.” Its customary designation involves a restriction, but the claim that such a condition is divinely prohibited comes from the people making that claim.

It should therefore not be translated directly as “a camel forbidden by The God,” since the verse rejects precisely that divine attribution.

In the basic translation, retaining ḥām as a particular designation, with a brief lexical explanation, is preferable.

4. Heated metal and intense fire

Al-Tawbah 9:35: يُحْمَىٰ عَلَيْهَا

يَوْمَ يُحْمَىٰ عَلَيْهَا فِي نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ فَتُكْوَىٰ بِهَا جِبَاهُهُمْ وَجُنُوبُهُمْ وَظُهُورُهُمْ هَٰذَا مَا كَنَزْتُمْ لِأَنْفُسِكُمْ فَذُوقُوا مَا كُنْتُمْ تَكْنِزُونَ

yawma yuḥmā ʿalayhā fī nāri jahannama fa-tukwā bihā jibāhuhum wa-junūbuhum wa-ẓuhūruhum hādhā mā kanaztum li-anfusikum fa-dhūqū mā kuntum taknizūna

Translation: “On the day when it is heated in the fire of Hell, and their foreheads, sides, and backs are branded with it: ‘This is what you hoarded for yourselves, so taste what you used to hoard.’”

Here يُحْمَىٰ is a passive imperfect verb. The Quranic Arabic Corpus records it as Form I; Al-Durr al-Maṣūn allows analyses from both حَمَيْتُ and أَحْمَيْتُ, corresponding to Form I and Form IV. The passive form is identical in both analyses, and the meaning of heating something or kindling fire upon it remains clear.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn: possible Form I and Form IV analyses of yuḥmā

The pronoun in عَلَيْهَا refers back to the hoarding of gold and silver described in the preceding verse.

What is heated is thus hoarded wealth, brought into contact with the heat of fire in this scene of punishment.

فَتُكْوَىٰ belongs to the root ك و ي and concerns branding the body with a hot substance.

Two actions must be distinguished: first the substance is heated, and then the body is branded with it.

يحمى does not by itself mean punishment or the branding of foreheads and sides. Those consequences are conveyed by تكوى and the complete verse.

Al-Basīṭ: heating the hoarded wealth and the pronoun’s referent

Connection with hoarding wealth

Al-Tawbah 9:34 describes people who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in the way of The God.

Then, in Al-Tawbah 9:35, those same stores appear in the scene of punishment.

This connection places the image of heating metal in a context criticizing human financial conduct.

The root ح م ي itself does not mean hoarding wealth, stinginess, or withholding others’ rights.

Those concepts are understood from the preceding verse and the conduct of the group being discussed.

Nor does this usage mean that heating metal or keeping gold and silver is reprehensible in every situation.

What is censured is identified through human conduct and the conditions stated in the verse, rather than through the heating of a substance.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:4: an intensely hot fire

تَصْلَىٰ نَارًا حَامِيَةً

taṣlā nāran ḥāmiyatan

Translation: “They enter an intensely hot, burning fire.”

In this verse, حَامِيَةً is an adjective modifying نَارًا.

Here حامية denotes fire of intense heat.

تَصْلَىٰ belongs to another root and concerns entering or suffering in fire.

حامية therefore does not itself mean entering the fire; it describes the fire’s quality.

Nor does حامية here mean “protector” or “defender,” although the same written form can have a protective use elsewhere in Arabic, depending on its precise vowels and context.

In this sentence, نارا clearly indicates that the thermal branch is intended.

Al-Qāriʿah 101:11: نَارٌ حَامِيَةٌ

نَارٌ حَامِيَةٌ

nārun ḥāmiyatun

Translation: “An intensely hot, burning fire.”

This is the second occurrence of حامية describing fire.

Al-Qāriʿah 101:9 mentions هَاوِيَةٌ. Al-Qāriʿah 101:10 then asks about it, and Al-Qāriʿah 101:11 explains it through نار حامية.

Here حامية emphasizes the fire’s intense heat.

The adjective alone does not determine the shape, size, fuel, or physical workings of the fire of the hereafter.

The two usages in Al-Ghāshiyah and Al-Qāriʿah share a clear meaning: fire with intense heat.

Nevertheless, نار حامية does not in itself mean an endless fire or one at a numerically specified temperature.

Those matters require independent evidence.

Relationship with حَمِيم

The Qur’an also uses حَمِيم, from the root ح م م, for very hot water.

حامية in these two verses belongs to ح م ي and describes fire in terms of heat.

They are close in the thermal field, but differ in form and range of usage.

In Muḥammad 47:15, مَاءً حَمِيمًا denotes very hot water.

In Al-Ghāshiyah 88:4, نَارًا حَامِيَةً describes very hot fire.

Translation should therefore preserve both the different substances being discussed and the different roots.

5. حَمِيَّةَ الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ in Al-Fatḥ 48:26

إِذْ جَعَلَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الْحَمِيَّةَ حَمِيَّةَ الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ فَأَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ سَكِينَتَهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِ وَعَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَأَلْزَمَهُمْ كَلِمَةَ التَّقْوَىٰ وَكَانُوا أَحَقَّ بِهَا وَأَهْلَهَا وَكَانَ اللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمًا

idh jaʿala alladhīna kafarū fī qulūbihimu al-ḥamiyyata ḥamiyyata al-jāhiliyyati fa-anzala allāhu sakīnatahu ʿalā rasūlihi wa-ʿalā al-muʾminīna wa-alzamahum kalimata al-taqwā wa-kānū aḥaqqa bihā wa-ahlahā wa-kāna allāhu bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīman

Translation: “When those who disbelieved put zeal and partisanship in their hearts—the partisanship of ignorance—The God sent down His tranquility upon His messenger and upon the believers, and bound them to the word of reverent mindfulness. They were more entitled to it and worthy of it. And The God knows all things.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:26 is the only verse containing حَمِيَّة in the sense of zeal or partisanship; the word occurs twice within it.

The first occurrence has the definite article, الْحَمِيَّةَ; the second is in a construct phrase, حَمِيَّةَ الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ.

The repetition specifies the kind of zeal intended.

Lexically, حمية concerns emotional arousal, protective zeal, and strong refusal.

The verse qualifies it through الجاهلية. Consequently, what is censured is not every kind of zeal or steadfastness, but a form of partisanship and response that, in the conduct of this group, stands against truth and reverent mindfulness.

Ḥamiyyah contrasted with tranquility

In this same verse, حمية الجاهلية stands in contrast to سكينة.

On one side is a group who have placed the partisanship of ignorance in their hearts.

On the other are the messenger and believers, upon whom divine tranquility has descended and who have been bound to the word of reverent mindfulness.

The contrast shows how intense reactions and partisanship can distance a person from considered judgment.

It does not mean that believers should lack all feeling, protective zeal, or ability to defend what is right.

Tranquility and reverent mindfulness are compatible with resisting injustice and safeguarding people’s rights.

The central difference is whether a response rests on truth and understanding or on pride, partisanship, and refusal to accept the truth.

The conduct criticized in context

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 describes people who disbelieved and kept the believers from the Sacred Mosque.

Al-Fatḥ 48:26 then describes the zeal placed in that same group’s hearts.

The kind of zeal criticized is connected to their conduct. It cannot be detached from this context and generalized to everyone or every form of defending one’s group.

Historical and exegetical reports connect the verse with Al-Ḥudaybiyyah and the refusal of certain Quraysh representatives to accept expressions acknowledging Muḥammad’s messengership in the agreement.

These reports can help explain the historical setting, but the verse itself does not state every detail of the agreement’s wording or name the individuals involved.

For lexical study, the defensible conclusion is that the group’s zeal appears in a context of obstructing access and insisting upon a position the Qur’an calls ignorant.

Al-Ṭabarī: zeal and the Al-Ḥudaybiyyah setting

Is all zeal the zeal of ignorance?

In Arabic, zeal and the impulse to defend one’s honor or group do not have an inherently negative meaning.

Someone may defend an oppressed person’s rights, protect family members from aggression, or resist social corruption.

Such conduct does not become حمية الجاهلية merely because it involves strong resolve or defense.

But when ethnic, family, religious, or social attachment makes someone unwilling to acknowledge their own or their group’s wrongdoing, zeal can turn into partisanship that opposes what is right.

For example, a person may know that a relative has wronged someone, yet defend the wrongdoer for fear of losing reputation or social standing.

What is criticized in that situation is not simply affection for one’s family, but preferring group loyalty over truth and justice.

This example illustrates a contemporary conceptual application. It does not justify attributing every family or social disagreement to the zeal of ignorance without examining people’s knowledge and intentions.

Lexically, حمية remains zeal and an intense response; “ignorance” specifies its censured kind and direction in the verse.

Relationship with أَخَذَتْهُ الْعِزَّةُ بِالْإِثْمِ

Al-Baqarah 2:206 describes a person who, when called to reverent mindfulness, is seized by pride in sin.

This expression belongs to another root, but is conceptually related to the zeal of ignorance.

Both concern a person’s response to truth and moral admonition.

If someone insists upon wrongdoing merely to preserve outward dignity, social standing, or group superiority, pride and partisanship can prevent reform.

Yet عزة and حمية are not complete synonyms. Each has its own root and wider usages.

Al-Fatḥ 48:26 directly concerns the zeal of ignorance and its contrast with tranquility; Al-Baqarah 2:206 emphasizes someone’s response to a call for reverent mindfulness.

6. Distinctions from related roots and translation proposals

ح م ي (حَمِيَ) and ح م م (حَمِيم)

In its thermal branch, ح م م concerns very hot water; in other usages it concerns an intimate friend and black smoke.

ح م ي also has a branch concerning heat, but its Qur’anic uses additionally include the customary designation حام from the protective branch, and حمية as zeal.

In Muḥammad 47:15, حَمِيمًا describes very hot water.

In Al-Ghāshiyah 88:4, حَامِيَةً describes very hot fire.

These are close in meaning, but their different roots and forms must be preserved.

Study of Ḥ M M

ح م ي (حَمِيَ) and ح م أ (حَمَإ)

ح م أ concerns dark mud.

In Al-Kahf 18:86, the reading حَمِئَةٍ denotes a muddy spring.

Another reading of the same verse has حَامِيَةٍ, which belongs to the thermal family and means a hot spring.

The two readings do not have the same word or root.

The Ḥafṣ reading has حمئة with hamzah; it is not counted among the six occurrences of ح م ي.

The reading حامية is also established in the transmitted readings and means “warm” or “hot.”

A study of this root should therefore acknowledge the different readings while keeping its occurrence count explicitly tied to the Qur’anic text being examined.

Study of Ḥ M ʾ

ح م ي (حِمَى) and ح ظ ر (حَظْر)

حظر concerns prohibiting and creating a barrier to access.

حِمَى likewise denotes a place to which others’ access is restricted or prohibited.

The two are close in the field of prevention and restriction, but their usages differ.

حظر can denote a general prohibition or a nonphysical restriction; حِمَى in its established usage denotes a protected place or area.

This closeness does not mean that every protected area is forbidden by divine decree.

Study of Ḥ Ẓ R

ح م ي (حَمَى) and ح ف ظ (حَفِظَ)

حفظ concerns keeping, looking after, and preventing something from being lost or wasted.

In its established usage, protection emphasizes warding off harm or encroachment and defending something.

Someone may preserve the property in a place by protecting the place itself, but these roots are not identical in every usage.

In Arabic, حفظ can also concern knowledge and memory, whereas protection in its concrete usage concerns defense and preventing harmful access.

Study of Ḥ F Ẓ

ح م ي (حَمِيَّة) and ع ص ب (عَصَبِيَّة)

عصبية concerns group attachment and partisanship.

In human conduct, حمية refers to protective zeal and intense arousal.

The two may combine in one action, but they are not identical.

Someone may belong to a group yet judge a dispute fairly. Conversely, someone with no tribal affiliation may display ḥamiyyah out of personal pride.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:26, the zeal of ignorance occurs within group conduct, but the root itself is not confined to an ethnic group or tribe.

ح م ي (حَمِيَّة) and غ ي ر (غَيْرَة)

In Arabic usage, غيرة concerns a person’s response to encroachment upon or a threat to something important to them.

حمية can also express such a response, especially when a person defends their dignity, honor, or standing.

This closeness does not mean that every instance of protective zeal is ignorant partisanship.

The conduct is evaluated through what is defended, the person’s awareness, their methods, and the action’s relationship to justice.

Study of GH Y R

ح م ي (حَمِيَّة) and س ك ن (سَكِينَة)

سكينة belongs to the family س ك ن and stands in contrast to the zeal of ignorance in Al-Fatḥ 48:26.

This contrast highlights tranquility and stability against a partisan response.

Tranquility does not in itself mean abandoning action or becoming passive.

A person can defend what is right calmly and firmly, just as someone intensely agitated can act harmfully without reflection.

The concepts therefore differ in more than the amount of movement or loudness of speech; the quality of judgment and response matters too.

Study of S K N

Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Māʾidah 5:103, حَامٍ is best retained as a customary designation:

“The God has not instituted any baḥīrah, sāʾibah, waṣīlah, or ḥām.”

A brief explanation can add that ḥām designated a male camel withdrawn from ordinary use under certain customs after reaching a stage in breeding.

In Al-Tawbah 9:35, يُحْمَىٰ can be rendered “is heated,” or by an indefinite active expression meaning “they heat it.” The passive is closer to the Arabic form; in a complete Persian sentence, a natural construction may be chosen for fluency.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:26, الْحَمِيَّةَ حَمِيَّةَ الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ may be rendered “zeal and partisanship—the partisanship of ignorance.”

If a short equivalent is chosen, “ignorant partisanship” conveys the contextual meaning more clearly. The lexical article should nevertheless explain that حمية has a wider range in Arabic than censured partisanship alone.

In Al-Ghāshiyah 88:4 and Al-Qāriʿah 101:11, حَامِيَة should be translated as “intensely hot” or “fiercely burning.”

In both verses, حامية describes fire and should not be translated as “protector” or “defender.”

For the reading حَامِيَةٍ in Al-Kahf 18:86, “a hot spring” is appropriate, with the explanation that حَمِئَةٍ belongs to another root and denotes a muddy spring.

Outside the Qur’an, حِمَى can be translated as “a protected reserve”; حِمَايَة as “protection and defense”; حِمْيَة as “abstention to prevent harm”; حَامِي as “protector and defender”; and حَمِيَّة, according to context, as “protective zeal,” “arousal,” or “partisanship.”

Conclusion

Brief root meaning: Heat and becoming hot; protection and preventing harm or access; ḥamiyyah: protective zeal, arousal, and partisanship.

In the first branch, the subject is heat and becoming hot. حَمِيَ describes fire, iron, and other things whose temperature has risen. The Qur’anic forms يُحْمَىٰ in Al-Tawbah 9:35 and حَامِيَة in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:4 and Al-Qāriʿah 101:11 belong here.

In the second branch, حَمَى concerns protection and preventing harm or access. حِمَى is a protected area, and حَامِي is a person or thing serving as a protector.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:103, حام is a customary designation for a male camel withdrawn from ordinary use under humanly invented regulations. The Qur’an does not accept these designations as divine legislation.

In the third branch, حَمِيَّة denotes protective zeal, arousal, and a response in defense of oneself, one’s group, or one’s standing.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:26, the word is qualified by الجاهلية and placed in contrast to divine tranquility.

This usage shows that zeal and an intense response can become harmful partisanship when separated from truth and justice.

It does not mean that every defense of a right or every feeling of protective zeal is ignorant or reprehensible.

The conclusion is that ح م ي has three distinct Qur’anic fields of usage: intense heat; the customary designation حام, connected with protection and restricted use; and human zeal and arousal. These meanings have points of closeness, but their entire historical relationship cannot be explained through one supposedly certain image.

For translation, “becoming hot” and “fiercely burning” suit the thermal branch; ḥām in Al-Māʾidah is a particular designation; and “ignorant partisanship” in Al-Fatḥ conveys the contextual meaning of the censured kind of zeal.

The central distinction is that heat is a physical property, protection an action, and ḥamiyyah a human response. None of these alone defines the others.

Nor does protection or zeal in itself establish the rightness of conduct. A person may defend truth and justice or, to preserve their own standing, prevent others from receiving their rights.

In Forqan’s research and translation, each derivative’s meaning should first be identified. Its context should then determine whether the subject is heat, legitimate protection, invented restrictions, or partisanship opposing what is right.
