Morning brightness and exposure to sunlight: from the opening of day and solar heat to outlying areas, morning food, and sacrifice

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Ḍ Ḥ W concerns daylight, the sun rising, and something becoming exposed to sunlight. Its best-known word is ḍuḥā, the bright part of the morning after sunrise as the sun ascends.

Other uses associate ḍaḥā with coming out into the sun and ḍaḥiya with being exposed to sunlight and its heat.

The family also includes names for an exposed, sunny part of a town, food eaten during daylight, and a sacrifice performed at a particular time of day.

The root occurs seven times in seven Qur’anic verses:

  • ضُحًى: morning daylight; Al-Aʿrāf 7:98 and Ṭā Hā 20:59.
  • تَضْحَىٰ: you are exposed to the sun and its heat; Ṭā Hā 20:119.
  • ضُحَاهَا: its brightness or morning; Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:46, and Al-Shams 91:1.
  • الضُّحَىٰ: morning brightness; Al-Ḍuḥā 93:1.

Six occurrences are nominal ḍuḥā, and one is a Form I verb. Taḍḥā belongs to the branch of exposure to the sun, while the nominal forms concern daylight or the time when it becomes apparent.

Other important Arabic forms include:

  • ضَحَا / يَضْحُو: appeared in the sun or became exposed to it.
  • ضَحِيَ / يَضْحَى: was exposed to sunlight and the sun’s heat.
  • ضُحَى: morning brightness and the time of the sun’s ascent.
  • ضَحْو and ضَحْوَة: the early part of the day after sunrise.
  • ضَحَاء: more extensive daylight; elsewhere, morning food.
  • أَضْحَى: reached the time of ḍuḥā; more broadly, entered a state or became.
  • ضَاحٍ: exposed, uncovered, or open to sunlight.
  • ضَاحِيَة: an exposed outer part of a town or territory; in modern use, a suburb.
  • مَضْحَاة: ground or a place exposed to sunlight.
  • تَضَحَّى: ate the morning meal.
  • ضَحَّى: fed someone or performed a sacrifice at ḍuḥā; other related meanings also occur.
  • أُضْحِيَّة, ضَحِيَّة, and أَضْحَاة: the recognized sacrifice whose name is associated with the time of its performance.

These forms do not all occur in the Qur’an. Only ḍuḥā and taḍḥā occur there from this family.

2. The semantic core: appearing in daylight

Ibn Fāris’s analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies the root’s principle as follows:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى بُرُوزِ الشَّيْءِ

aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā burūzi al-shayʾi

That is, “One sound semantic principle indicating something becoming apparent.”

He then relates ḍaḥāʾ to the extension of the day and the time when light becomes manifest and widespread.

This analysis fits the root’s principal uses.

At night, many objects are in darkness and difficult to see. As the sun rises and light spreads, the ground, roads, buildings, and other surroundings become more visible.

Ḍuḥā identifies a time when the sun has risen and its brightness has spread.

In another use, someone who leaves the shade and stands in the sun is called ḍāḥin.

Both involve appearing and being in the light.

This connection does not mean every derivative has precisely the same meaning. A time of daylight, exposure to heat, and a town’s outskirts are related but distinct uses.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: appearing, ḍaḥāʾ, outskirts, and the sacrifice name

Which part of the day is ḍuḥā?

Early Arabic sources use ḍuḥā for a part of the day when the sun has risen above the horizon and its light is visible and widespread.

Lisān al-ʿArab records several related explanations.

One identifies ḍaḥwah as the early period after sunrise and ḍuḥā as slightly later.

Another extends ḍuḥā from sunrise until the day advances and the sunlight is fully manifest.

Ḍaḥāʾ then concerns the more extended daytime approaching noon.

Lexicographers have not all drawn these boundaries identically.

Some uses extend ḍuḥā more broadly to daylight or much of the day.

Ḍuḥā should therefore not be restricted in every text to a fixed hour or a precise number of minutes.

Its principal meaning relates to the sun’s ascent and spreading daylight. The particular use and context determine its precise temporal extent.

Ḍaḥwah, ḍuḥā, and ḍaḥāʾ

Three related forms appear in lexical sources:

ضَحْوَة: early daylight after the sun has risen.

ضُحَى: the bright part of the morning when the sun is higher.

ضَحَاء: in some early classifications, more extensive daylight nearer noon.

Some sources explain these distinctions as successive stages of the day.

Their uses also genuinely overlap, however. They should not be turned into a rigid timetable universally agreed upon by Arabic speakers.

“Morning brightness” or “in daylight” suits many Qur’anic contexts.

Where the verse concerns the sunlight itself, however, “morning” alone may fail to convey part of the meaning.

3. Light, sunlight, and exposure

Ḍaḥā: coming out into sunlight

Arabic uses the expression:

ضَحَا الرَّجُلُ لِلشَّمْسِ

ḍaḥā al-rajulu li-al-shamsi

That is, the person appeared in the sun or became exposed to it.

The issue is more than the surroundings becoming brighter: someone leaves cover or shade, and sunlight falls directly on them.

This condition may be pleasant or unpleasant.

In cold weather, a person may benefit from the sun’s warmth; in very hot weather, direct exposure may also cause harm.

Ḍaḥā does not therefore necessarily mean suffering injury.

Exposure to sunlight and appearing in the sun are the basic meanings. The circumstances determine benefit or harm.

Ḍaḥiya: exposure to the sun’s heat

Lexical sources use ضَحِيَ يَضْحَى for someone upon whom sunlight falls and to whom its heat reaches.

Al-Layth explains this use through the sun’s heat reaching someone.

Al-Farrāʾ likewise emphasizes potentially harmful sunshine in discussing Ṭā Hā 20:119.

Another reported use associates ḍaḥiya with sweating.

Sweating is not, however, the fixed meaning of every use of this verb.

Someone may be exposed to sunlight and sweat, but exposure and perspiration are different events.

Exposure to sunlight is the clearer lexical and contextual meaning in Ṭā Hā 20:119.

An early poetic witness concerning sun exposure

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes:

رَأَتْ رَجُلًا أَمَّا إِذَا الشَّمْسُ عَارَضَتْ
فَيَضْحَى وَأَمَّا بِالْعَشِيِّ فَيَخْصَرُ

raʾat rajulan ammā idhā al-shamsu ʿāraḍat / fa-yaḍḥā wa-ammā bi-al-ʿashiyyi fa-yakhṣaru

Translation:

“She saw a man who, when the sun shone upon him, was exposed to its heat, and in the evening felt the cold.”

Yaḍḥā stands alongside yakhṣaru in this verse.

The latter concerns suffering from the cold here.

The poet thus describes two environmental conditions: exposure to the sun and being afflicted by cold.

This witness shows that verbal ḍaḥā concerned the body’s actual condition in sunlight.

Lisān al-ʿArab does not name the poet in this quotation. Al-Ṭabarī, however, attributes it to Ibn Abī Rabīʿah, identified in the edition’s note as ʿUmar ibn Abī Rabīʿah. On that attribution, it is an Islamic-period witness rather than a securely pre-Islamic poem.

Al-Ṭabarī: the witness attributed to ʿUmar ibn Abī Rabīʿah and solar heat

Ḍāḥiyah: an exposed outer area

Arabic uses ḍāḥiyah for the exposed or outer part of a town or territory.

Early sources identify ḍawāḥī al-balad as a town’s outer districts.

This relates to exposure and being in open space.

Modern Arabic also uses ḍāḥiyah for a suburb.

That modern use should not be treated as the word’s entire historical meaning.

Ḍāḥiyah can identify the visible outer part of a place, distinct from its more enclosed interior.

Maḍḥāh likewise names a place exposed to sunlight with little sheltering shade.

Such uses emphasize exposure to sunlight.

A road becoming visible

Early Arabic uses ضَحَا الطَّرِيقُ for a road becoming apparent and visible.

This relates to Ibn Fāris’s analysis of appearing and becoming exposed.

A visible road is not necessarily a morally right road, however.

A “clear road” may identify a visible route, whereas the path of truth or guidance is a broader matter.

Religious guidance or recognition of truth should therefore not be derived from Ḍ Ḥ W alone.

Asās al-Balāghah and Tāj al-ʿArūs: outskirts, exposed ground, and a visible road

4. Early witnesses concerning the time of ḍuḥā

A witness attributed to Ḥassān ibn Thābit

A poem attributed to Ḥassān ibn Thābit reads:

لَنَا الْجَفَنَاتُ الْغُرُّ يَلْمَعْنَ بِالضُّحَى
وَأَسْيَافُنَا يَقْطُرْنَ مِنْ نَجْدَةٍ دَمَا

lanā al-jafanātu al-ghurru yalmaʿna bi-al-ḍuḥā / wa-asyāfunā yaqṭurna min najdatin damā

Translation:

“Ours are the bright great bowls shining in morning daylight, and our swords drip blood from valor.”

Bi-al-ḍuḥā identifies the time when sunlight reveals the vessels’ brightness.

Literary criticism associates this verse with an account of Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī judging Ḥassān’s poetry.

In that account, the poet is told that invoking nighttime darkness instead of morning light would have made the vessels’ brilliance more striking.

Whatever the reliability of every detail in the exchange, al-ḍuḥā’s use is clear: a time when sunlight falls on objects and reveals them.

Ḥassān ibn Thābit was a mukhaḍram poet who composed before and after Islam. The verse is associated with accounts of pre-Islamic poetic adjudication, but its composition date is not independently certain.

Khizānat al-Adab: Ḥassān’s witness and the reported criticism

Morning food: a witness from Dhū al-Rummah

Early Arabic also used ḍaḥāʾ for food eaten at the time of ḍuḥā.

Describing an animal, Dhū al-Rummah says:

تَرَى الثَّوْرَ يَمْشِي رَاجِعًا مِنْ ضَحَائِهِ
بِهَا مِثْلَ مَشْيِ الْهِبْرِزِيِّ الْمُسَرْوَلِ

tarā al-thawra yamshī rājiʿan min ḍaḥāʾihi / bihā mithla mashyi al-hibriziyyi al-musarwali

An approximate rendering of the sense:

“You see the wild bull returning from its morning feeding, moving there with a firm, measured gait.”

Ḍaḥāʾihi concerns food the animal obtained from grazing during daylight.

The connection with the time of ḍuḥā is clear.

Just as morning and evening meals may be named by the time of eating, Arabic uses ḍaḥāʾ for the food of ḍuḥā.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad poet. This witness therefore belongs to early Islamic Arabic, rather than the pre-Islamic period.

Lisān al-ʿArab: morning food and Dhū al-Rummah’s witness

Eating and grazing at ḍuḥā

The family also records taḍaḥḥā for eating morning food.

In pastoral use, taḍaḥḥat al-ibil describes camels grazing at ḍuḥā.

Ḍaḥḥaytu al-ibil can likewise refer to feeding camels or allowing them to graze then.

Lexical sources also record the saying ضَحِّ رُوَيْدًا.

It is explained through travelers slowing down so their animals have enough time to graze and feed.

The name of a time thus extends to an activity performed then.

Eating and grazing are not, however, the fixed meanings of Qur’anic ḍuḥā.

In Qur’anic verses, ḍuḥā identifies brightness or a time of day, rather than food itself.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: food, grazing, and ḍaḥḥi ruwaydan

5. Qur’anic ḍuḥā: brightness and time of day

Al-Aʿrāf 7:98: an event in daylight

Al-Aʿrāf reads:

أَوَأَمِنَ أَهْلُ الْقُرَىٰ أَنْ يَأْتِيَهُمْ بَأْسُنَا ضُحًى وَهُمْ يَلْعَبُونَ

a-wa-amina ahlu al-qurā an yaʾtiyahum baʾsunā ḍuḥan wa-hum yalʿabūna

“Do the people of the settlements feel secure that Our adversity will not come upon them in daylight while they play?”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:98

Ḍuḥan is a temporal adverb here.

The preceding verse concerns divine adversity arriving at night while people sleep.

The present verse identifies daylight, when people are awake and occupied with work or play.

Two temporal situations therefore contrast: nighttime sleep and daytime occupation or amusement.

Ḍuḥā concerns daylight in this context, rather than a particular act of worship or a named event.

Nor does the verse state that danger comes only in the first morning hours.

The point is to remove a false sense of security at different times, whether resting at night or active by day.

“In daylight” therefore conveys the contextual meaning well.

Ṭā Hā 20:59: people gathering in morning daylight

The account of Moses and Pharaoh reads:

قَالَ مَوْعِدُكُمْ يَوْمُ الزِّينَةِ وَأَنْ يُحْشَرَ النَّاسُ ضُحًى

qāla mawʿidukum yawmu al-zīnati wa-an yuḥshara al-nāsu ḍuḥan

“He said: Your appointed time is the day of adornment, and that the people be gathered in morning daylight.”

Ṭā Hā 20:59

Ḍuḥā specifies the gathering’s time.

Yuḥshara belongs to Ḥ SH R and concerns gathering people.

Ḍuḥā therefore does not name the gathering itself; it identifies when it takes place.

In morning daylight, people can see one another and the events before them.

This fits the public confrontation between Moses and the magicians.

The word alone does not establish a particular location within the town, a set number of participants, or an exact hour.

Those details are not contained in the word.

Its direct meaning is the bright morning period.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29: daylight contrasted with night’s darkness

Al-Nāziʿāt reads:

وَأَغْطَشَ لَيْلَهَا وَأَخْرَجَ ضُحَاهَا

wa-aghṭasha laylahā wa-akhraja ḍuḥāhā

“He darkened its night and brought forth its daylight.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29

Ḍuḥāhā stands in contrast with laylahā here.

The pronoun -hā in this passage refers to the heaven whose creation and ordering the preceding verses describe.

Ḍuḥāhā does not merely name a fixed hour after sunrise in this context.

Daylight stands opposite the darkening of night.

“Its daylight” therefore conveys the context better than restricting the word to “early morning.”

This shows that Qur’anic ḍuḥā can emphasize the manifest brightness of day itself.

Akhraja likewise expresses bringing that brightness forth.

No physical mechanism for light’s appearance or the movement of celestial bodies should be derived from this expression without independent evidence. The verse speaks of daylight in contrast with nighttime darkness.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:46: ʿashiyyatan aw ḍuḥāhā

The end of Al-Nāziʿāt reads:

كَأَنَّهُمْ يَوْمَ يَرَوْنَهَا لَمْ يَلْبَثُوا إِلَّا عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَاهَا

ka-annahum yawma yarawnahā lam yalbathū illā ʿashiyyatan aw ḍuḥāhā

“On the day they see it, it will be as though they had remained only an evening or its morning.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:46

ʿAshiyyah and ḍuḥā stand in contrast here.

ʿAshiyyah concerns the latter part of the day, while ḍuḥā identifies its early, bright part.

Their contrast portrays the brevity of the period.

On seeing the final event, people find their earlier stay so short that it seems only part of a day.

The pronoun in ḍuḥāhā

In aw ḍuḥāhā, the pronoun -hā refers back to ʿashiyyah.

This does not mean an evening has a separate morning of its own.

In this Arabic construction, ʿashiyyah can identify the latter part of a day, with ḍuḥāhā identifying the early part of that same day.

Al-Ṭabarī explains it through ordinary Arabic constructions such as “a day’s morning and its evening.”

The reference is therefore to two parts of one day: its bright opening and its latter part.

This matters in translation: moving ḍuḥāhā to another day’s morning would depart from the image of a short duration.

Nor does the verse identify the Resurrection’s exact hour. It concerns the apparent brevity of the preceding stay when confronted with the great event ahead.

Al-Ṭabarī: evening and the morning of the same day

Al-Shams 91:1: the sun’s brightness

Al-Shams reads:

وَالشَّمْسِ وَضُحَاهَا

wa-al-shamsi wa-ḍuḥāhā

“By the sun and its morning brightness.”

Al-Shams 91:1

Ḍuḥāhā refers to the sun here.

Early exegetical sources relate Al-Farrāʾ’s explanation to the day and Al-Zajjāj’s to sunlight.

The interpretations are close in meaning because ḍuḥā is the time when sunlight becomes apparent.

The sun itself, its emitted light, and the time when that light appears in the surroundings are nevertheless distinct concepts.

The verse places the sun and its ḍuḥā together.

Ḍuḥāhā should therefore not simply be translated as “the sun,” erasing the distinction between the expressions.

Al-Shams 91:3 also reads:

وَالنَّهَارِ إِذَا جَلَّاهَا

wa-al-nahāri idhā jallāhā

“By the day when it reveals it.”

The passage places the sun, brightness, day, and night within a network of related phenomena.

The root itself does not disclose every aspect of the earth’s illumination or the scientific mechanism of solar and terrestrial movement.

The word’s clear meaning here is the sun’s manifest brightness, spreading in the morning.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Farrāʾ and Al-Zajjāj on ḍuḥāhā

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:1: an oath by morning brightness

Al-Ḍuḥā opens:

وَالضُّحَىٰ
وَاللَّيْلِ إِذَا سَجَىٰ

wa-al-ḍuḥā / wa-al-layli idhā sajā

“By morning brightness, and by the night when it grows still.”

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:1 and Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2

Morning brightness and the stillness of night occur together here.

Al-ḍuḥā identifies the bright daytime period.

Al-layli idhā sajā concerns night as it becomes still or stillness spreads through it.

Their contrast can present two natural conditions: light appearing and quiet nighttime darkness arriving.

The next verse reads:

مَا وَدَّعَكَ رَبُّكَ وَمَا قَلَىٰ

mā waddaʿaka rabbuka wa-mā qalā

“Your Lord has neither abandoned you nor hated you.”

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:3

The oath by ḍuḥā and night thus introduces reassurance that Muhammad has not been abandoned by his Lord.

Ḍuḥā itself does not mean revelation, guidance, or the end of sorrow.

Those concepts emerge from the surah’s context and the relationship between its verses.

“Morning brightness” preserves the lexical meaning well in the base translation.

6. Wa-lā taḍḥā: exposure to the sun’s heat

Adam’s story reads:

إِنَّ لَكَ أَلَّا تَجُوعَ فِيهَا وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ
وَأَنَّكَ لَا تَظْمَأُ فِيهَا وَلَا تَضْحَىٰ

inna laka allā tajūʿa fīhā wa-lā taʿrā / wa-annaka lā taẓmaʾu fīhā wa-lā taḍḥā

“There, it is yours neither to hunger nor be naked, and neither to thirst nor suffer the sun’s heat.”

Ṭā Hā 20:118 and Ṭā Hā 20:119

The meaning of taḍḥā

Taḍḥā is a second-person singular Form I imperfect verb.

This form occurs only once in the Qur’an.

Established Arabic usage concerns exposure to the sun and its heat reaching someone.

The negative lā taḍḥā states here that Adam will not be afflicted by exposure to direct sunshine in that place.

Three other needs or difficulties stand alongside it: hunger, nakedness, and thirst.

Together, the four expressions portray comfort and the provision of basic human needs.

Each word nevertheless retains its independent meaning.

Tajūʿa concerns hunger, taʿrā nakedness, taẓmaʾu thirst, and taḍḥā exposure to the sun.

Taḍḥā should therefore not simply be translated as “you become thirsty,” since another verb already expresses thirst.

Does taḍḥā mean sweating?

Some exegetical sources also explain lā taḍḥā as not sweating.

Al-Farrāʾ instead associates it with not being reached by harmful sunshine, and Al-Azharī considers that explanation more appropriate.

Sweating may result from intense heat, but it is not the verb’s fixed meaning.

“You will not suffer the sun’s heat” is therefore more precise than “you will not sweat.”

Does the verse say there was no sun there?

No. The expression itself does not state that.

Sunlight being present, direct exposure to it, and suffering from its heat are distinct matters.

A place may be bright while someone remains shaded and protected from harmful heat.

A person may likewise benefit from sunlight without experiencing damaging conditions.

Lā taḍḥā alone therefore cannot establish whether the sun existed there, the place’s geographical location, or the environment’s physical properties.

Its lexical and contextual meaning concerns protection from exposure to the sun and the difficulty of direct sunshine.

Al-Ṭabarī and the cited Lisān passage: sun exposure distinguished from sweating

7. Ḍuḥā in food, sacrifice, and later terminology

Morning food

Besides extensive daylight, Arabic uses al-ḍaḥāʾ for food eaten at that time.

Taḍaḥḥā identifies eating that meal.

This resembles ghadāʾ and ʿashāʾ: eating may be named by the time when it takes place.

Ḍuḥā does not refer to food in Qur’anic verses, however.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:98 and Ṭā Hā 20:59 concern the time of day, while Al-Shams and Al-Ḍuḥā concern its brightness.

Morning food should therefore not be imported into these verses.

Uḍḥiyyah and ḍaḥiyyah

Arabic records uḍḥiyyah, ḍaḥiyyah, and aḍḥāh for the recognized sacrifice associated with the time of ḍuḥā.

Ibn Fāris transmits the name’s variant forms from Al-Aṣmaʿī.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains its original association with performance in daylight after the sun had risen.

The recognized day of sacrifice is consequently also called yawm al-aḍḥā.

The history of a word should nevertheless be distinguished from a Qur’anic ruling.

Uḍḥiyyah in that form does not occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an uses terms such as hady, naḥr, nusuk, and bahīmat al-anʿām for sacrifice and related rites. Each requires separate examination.

The Arabic name uḍḥiyyah and its relationship with ḍuḥā cannot by themselves establish that all human beings must perform a particular ceremony, a set number of sacrifices, or specific legal details.

Modern uses of taḍḥiyah for self-sacrifice or sacrificing oneself are likewise linguistic extensions, rather than the direct meaning of ḍuḥā in Qur’anic verses.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: uḍḥiyyah forms and the name’s temporal association

Ṣalāt al-ḍuḥā

Post-Qur’anic devotional traditions discuss a prayer called ṣalāt al-ḍuḥā.

The name identifies its time: the bright morning period after sunrise.

Al-ḍuḥā itself in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:1 does not name a prayer.

It identifies a natural phenomenon and bright time of day invoked in an oath.

Naming an act of worship by the time of ḍuḥā does not therefore mean that the surah itself commands that act.

This is a separate question from the word’s root study.

8. Comparison with neighboring roots

Ḍ Ḥ W (ḍuḥā) and Ṣ B Ḥ (ṣubḥ)

Ṣubḥ concerns the opening of day and the ending of night.

Ḍuḥā identifies light after the sun’s ascent and its spreading.

The concepts are close without being identical.

Ṣubḥ may also concern the first light before sunrise.

Ḍuḥā is more particularly associated with the sun rising higher and its light becoming apparent.

Translating both merely as “morning” may therefore erase a distinction in some verses.

Study of Ṣ B Ḥ

Ḍ Ḥ W (ḍuḥā) and F J R (fajr)

Fajr concerns darkness splitting and the first daylight appearing.

Ḍuḥā identifies a stage of light when the sun has risen and the day is more apparent.

They belong to one natural cycle but are different designations.

The Qur’an uses both independently.

For example, Al-Fajr 89:1 mentions al-fajr, while Al-Ḍuḥā 93:1 mentions al-ḍuḥā.

They should not be treated as wholly synonymous without considering their respective times and imagery.

Study of F J R

Ḍ Ḥ W (ḍuḥā) and N H R (nahār)

Nahār concerns the daytime period in contrast with night.

Ḍuḥā usually identifies morning brightness or part of the day.

In some Qur’anic uses, such as Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29, it may identify more general daylight contrasted with night.

The families therefore overlap in some contexts without having identical meanings.

Study of N H R

Ḍ Ḥ W (ḍuḥā) and Ḍ W ʾ (ḍawʾ)

Ḍawʾ concerns light and brightness.

Ḍuḥā also concerns light, while retaining in its principal use a temporal and natural connection with the sun’s ascent.

Ḍuḥāhā in Al-Shams 91:1 concerns sunlight.

This overlap does not imply that every light is ḍuḥā or that all light must come from the sun.

Ḍawʾ has a more general field of brightness; familiar uses of ḍuḥā relate to the bright part of the day.

Study of Ḍ W ʾ

Ḍ Ḥ W (ḍaḥā) and W Ḍ Ḥ (waḍaḥa)

Waḍaḥa concerns being clear and apparent.

One use of ḍaḥā likewise concerns appearing.

Their meanings are close, but their root letters and ordering differ.

A shared concept of appearing does not itself prove that both developed from one historical root.

Ḍ Ḥ W (ḍuḥā) and Ḍ Ḥ K (ḍaḥika)

Ḍaḥika concerns laughing.

Ḍuḥā concerns morning brightness and exposure to sunlight.

Some figurative uses of ḍaḥika concern blossoms opening or light appearing.

This shared imagery does not make the roots identical.

Wa-al-ḍuḥā should therefore not be translated as “the day’s laughter,” nor should laughing be transferred into Ḍ Ḥ W.

Study of Ḍ Ḥ K

Ḍ Ḥ W (ḍuḥā) and D Ḥ W (daḥā)

The letters and sound of these roots are close, but they differ.

Qur’anic daḥw concerns spreading or extending.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:30 reads:

وَالْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ دَحَاهَا

wa-al-arḍa baʿda dhālika daḥāhā

“After that, He spread out the earth.”

The preceding verse has ḍuḥāhā.

The words differ by only one letter, but one concerns daylight and the other spreading the earth.

Their similar form therefore does not establish a shared root or an identical historical meaning.

Study of D Ḥ W

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

ضُحَى: morning brightness; the bright part of the day after sunrise.

ضَحْوَة: early daylight.

ضَحَاء: extensive daylight; elsewhere, morning food.

ضَحَا: appeared exposed to sunlight.

ضَحِيَ / يَضْحَى: became exposed to sunlight and its heat.

تَضْحَىٰ: you are exposed to the sun’s heat; in Ṭā Hā 20:119.

ضَاحٍ: exposed, uncovered, or open to sunlight.

ضَاحِيَة: an exposed outer part of a town; a suburb.

مَضْحَاة: sun-exposed ground.

أَضْحَى: reached the time of ḍuḥā; more broadly, became or entered a state.

تَضَحَّى: ate the morning meal.

أُضْحِيَّة: the recognized sacrifice with a name associated with ḍuḥā; a term not occurring in that form in the Qur’an.

For the seven Qur’anic uses:

Al-Aʿrāf 7:98

أَنْ يَأْتِيَهُمْ بَأْسُنَا ضُحًى

an yaʾtiyahum baʾsunā ḍuḥan

“That Our adversity come upon them in daylight.”

Ṭā Hā 20:59

وَأَنْ يُحْشَرَ النَّاسُ ضُحًى

wa-an yuḥshara al-nāsu ḍuḥan

“And that the people be gathered in morning daylight.”

Ṭā Hā 20:119

وَلَا تَضْحَىٰ

wa-lā taḍḥā

“Nor suffer the sun’s heat.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29

وَأَخْرَجَ ضُحَاهَا

wa-akhraja ḍuḥāhā

“And brought forth its daylight.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:46

إِلَّا عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَاهَا

illā ʿashiyyatan aw ḍuḥāhā

“Only an evening or its morning.”

Al-Shams 91:1

وَالشَّمْسِ وَضُحَاهَا

wa-al-shamsi wa-ḍuḥāhā

“By the sun and its morning brightness.”

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:1

وَالضُّحَىٰ

wa-al-ḍuḥā

“By morning brightness.”

“Morning brightness” is a principal, natural equivalent in these uses.

“Daylight” is more suitable in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29 because it contrasts with nighttime darkness.

Ṭā Hā 20:119 instead requires preserving the verbal meaning of exposure to sunlight.

A uniform “morning” for every use would therefore erase real distinctions within the family.

10. Conclusion

Ḍ Ḥ W concerns appearing in daylight, the sun rising, and exposure to sunlight.

Its best-known word, ḍuḥā, identifies morning brightness and a time after sunrise.

Other uses associate ḍaḥā and ḍaḥiya with sun exposure. Ḍāḥiyah identifies a town’s exposed outskirts, while one use of ḍaḥāʾ names food eaten during daylight.

Uḍḥiyyah and certain devotional or social terms are likewise associated with the family, but these later uses should not be imposed upon the direct meaning of Qur’anic verses.

The Qur’an contains six uses of nominal ḍuḥā and one of the verb taḍḥā.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:98 and Ṭā Hā 20:59, ḍuḥā expresses the bright daytime period.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29, it emphasizes daylight contrasted with night’s darkness.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:46, a part of the day contrasts with ʿashiyyah and portrays the period’s brevity.

In Al-Shams 91:1, ḍuḥā concerns sunlight.

In Al-Ḍuḥā 93:1, morning brightness is invoked in an oath.

In Ṭā Hā 20:119, taḍḥā concerns exposure to sunlight and its heat; Adam’s comfort is described through freedom from the sun’s affliction and the resulting hardship.

Thus the time of brightness, brightness itself, and exposure to sunlight are three related but distinguishable uses.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Appearing in daylight and exposure to sunlight; nominally, morning brightness and the time of the sun’s ascent.

The word’s formation and its particular verse context determine each derivative’s precise meaning. Some verses emphasize morning time, others sunlight; the verbal use concerns exposure and the effect of solar heat.
