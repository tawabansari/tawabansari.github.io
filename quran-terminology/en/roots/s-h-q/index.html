Core meaning: Arabic has two important branches in this family: becoming very distant, and pounding, crushing or wearing something out. In the first, suḥq denotes great remoteness and saḥīq describes something very far away. In the second, saḥq denotes pulverizing or wearing something down. Only the distance branch occurs in the Qur’an: once describing a remote place and once in fa-suḥqan, an expression of rejection whose implications for a person's outcome follow from context.

1. Introduction and meaning in early Arabic

The root S Ḥ Q has two recognized semantic fields that should not be conflated without evidence.

Saḥuqa al-shayʾu means that something became very distant.

Makānun saḥīqun denotes a place far away.

In another usage, saḥaqa al-shayʾa means to pound and crush something.

Saḥaqa al-ḥabba means to pound and crush grain.

Saḥaqa al-thawba also denotes wearing a garment out.

The first usage concerns distance and remoteness.

The second concerns a change in, or loss of, structure or quality through pounding, rubbing and wear.

These meanings may be associated in an imagined picture, but lexical evidence does not establish either as the other's historical source.

Early Arabic linguistic analyses also distinguish the two fields.

Pre-Islamic evidence: tall palms in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā's poetry

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, a pre-Islamic poet, says in an ode beginning “إِنَّ الْخَلِيطَ أَجَدَّ الْبَيْنَ فَانْفَرَقَا”:

كَأَنَّ عَيْنَيَّ فِي غَرْبَيْ مُقَتَّلَةٍ
مِنَ النَّوَاضِحِ تَسْقِي جَنَّةً سُحُقًا

kaʾanna ʿaynayya fī gharbay muqattalatin / mina al-nawāḍiḥi tasqī jannatan suḥuqā

Translation:

“It is as though my eyes were in two great buckets belonging to a work-trained watering camel, irrigating a grove of towering palms.”

Gharbay refers to two large buckets.

Muqattalah describes a she-camel made tractable by extensive work.

Al-nawāḍiḥ denotes camels used to draw water for irrigation.

Suḥuqan is the plural of saḥūq in this reading.

Applied to palms, saḥūq describes a very tall tree.

The poet compares his tear-filled eyes to great water buckets used to irrigate a grove of tall palms.

Here, the root does not mean pounding or destroying the palms.

The trees are tall, their upper parts far from the ground.

Saḥūq in this verse thus relates to distance and considerable extension.

The example shows that early Arabic used this family to express great distance and height.

Why is a tall palm called saḥūq?

Nakhlatun saḥūqun denotes a tall palm in lexical usage.

Its height places its upper parts and fruit far from the ground.

The connection between a tall palm and distance is therefore clear.

This does not make height the meaning of the root in every usage.

A place can be very distant without being high up.

Something can also lie a great distance below a person.

Great distance is central to this branch; the palm's height is one instance.

Historical standing of the example

The verse appears in the collected poems attributed to Zuhayr and in established literary transmissions.

Lexical explanations generally take suḥuqan as the plural of saḥūq, describing tall palms.

Some explanations also mention the garden's widely separated edges.

The tall-palm reading nevertheless has clear support in the attested expression nakhlatun saḥūqun.

Zuhayr's pre-Islamic standing and the verse's place in the transmission of his poetry make it relevant to the root's early meaning.

As with other pre-Islamic poetry, however, the text survives through later transmission; the precise date of this verse is not independently known.

Crushing and wearing out

Arabic lexicons record saḥaqa al-shayʾa for forcefully pounding and crushing something.

For example:

سَحَقَ الْحَبَّ

saḥaqa al-ḥabba

Meaning: “He pounded and crushed the grain.”

A previously relatively coherent body is reduced to smaller parts by impact or grinding.

Saḥaqati al-rīḥu al-arḍa also describes a strong wind scouring the ground and effacing its traces.

For clothing, thawbun saḥqun denotes an old, worn garment.

A verse attributed to Muzarrid ibn Ḍirār, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says:

وَمَا زَوَّدُونِي غَيْرَ سَحْقِ عِمَامَةٍ
وَخَمْسِ مِئٍ مِنْهَا قَسِيٌّ وَزَائِفُ

wa-mā zawwadūnī ghayra saḥqi ʿimāmatin / wa-khamsi miʾin minhā qasiyyun wa-zāʾifu

Translation:

“They provisioned me with nothing but a worn turban and five hundred coins, including debased and rejected ones.”

Saḥqi ʿimāmatin refers to a worn-out turban.

Some transmissions have ʿabāʾah, a cloak, instead of ʿimāmah, a turban.

The variant changes the garment, but not the meaning of wear conveyed by saḥq.

Since Muzarrid lived across both periods, this verse cannot securely be dated before Islam without evidence for its composition.

It nevertheless shows the early use of saḥq for something whose original quality has deteriorated through use and wear.

Historical definition

The poetic examples and attested usages distinguish two branches:

First, great remoteness: being or becoming very distant from a person, place or relevant condition.

Second, pounding, crushing and wearing out: changing or destroying structure or quality through impact, rubbing or erosion.

The tall-palm usage highlights the distance of the tree's upper parts from the ground.

Crushed grain and worn clothing illustrate changes in earlier structure or quality.

Both branches are established in Arabic, but their usages cannot all be traced to a single historical origin without evidence.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Saḥuqa and saḥiqa

Both patterns are recorded for becoming distant.

سَحُقَ الشَّيْءُ

saḥuqa al-shayʾu

Meaning: “The thing became very distant.”

سَحِقَ الشَّيْءُ

saḥiqa al-shayʾu

The thing became very distant.

This also belongs to the same semantic field.

The verbal nouns in this usage are suḥq and suḥuq.

These forms express great remoteness.

Suḥq

A verbal noun denoting becoming distant or great remoteness.

Arabic uses suḥqan lahu as an expression of rejection: “Away with him.”

Distance here can extend beyond physical separation.

Someone may, for example, be described as removed from a desirable standing, mercy or a good outcome.

What the person is removed from must come from context; the word alone does not specify it.

This form occurs in Al-Mulk 67:11.

Saḥīq

An adjective meaning very distant.

مَكَانٌ سَحِيقٌ

makānun saḥīqun

Meaning: “A very distant place.”

The adjective occurs in Al-Ḥajj 22:31.

Saḥīq follows the faʿīl pattern and expresses great distance in its Qur’anic usage.

The pattern alone does not specify a measurable distance or everlasting duration.

Saḥūq

In the palm-tree usage, it describes a very tall tree.

نَخْلَةٌ سَحُوقٌ

nakhlatun saḥūqun

Meaning: “A very tall palm.”

The description relates to the distance of its upper parts from the ground.

Its plural appears in Zuhayr's verse.

Suḥuq

The plural of saḥūq when referring to tall palms.

This is its reading in the description of the grove in Zuhayr's verse.

However, suḥuq is also recorded as a verbal noun of distance, alongside suḥq. Vocalization alone therefore cannot determine whether it is a plural or a verbal noun; context and syntax must decide.

Identical consonants do not imply identical grammatical functions.

Saḥaqa yasḥaqu

In the other branch, this is a transitive Form I verb meaning to pound, crush or wear something out.

سَحَقَ الدَّوَاءَ

saḥaqa al-dawāʾa

Meaning: “He pounded the medicine into powder.”

سَحَقَ الثَّوْبَ

saḥaqa al-thawba

Meaning: “He wore the garment out.”

In this usage, the object undergoes change.

The verb does not occur with this meaning in the Qur’an.

Saḥq

The verbal noun of pounding and crushing.

As a noun, it can also denote worn-out clothing.

The different vowels in saḥq and suḥq matter in distinguishing the two branches.

Saḥq in the pounding usage concerns crushing and wearing down.

Suḥq in the distance usage expresses great remoteness.

Yet vowel differences alone do not impose absolute semantic boundaries throughout historical usage; syntax and attested use also matter.

Masḥūq

The passive participle of saḥaqa.

It describes something pounded or crushed.

Ḥabbun masḥūqun, for example, means crushed grain.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Insaḥaqa

A Form VII verb.

In the crushing branch, it denotes being crushed or worn down.

Other usages record the same pattern for becoming distant.

Its meaning must therefore be determined from its subject and context.

Asḥaqa

A Form IV verb.

In distance usages, it denotes becoming distant or making distant.

Other usages describe becoming worn or dry.

It does not occur in the Qur’an as a verb derived from S Ḥ Q.

Does the name Isḥāq belong to this root?

The prophet's name Isḥāq resembles this family in spelling.

However, Isḥāq is a proper name of non-Arabic origin.

Resemblance alone does not make it a derivative of the Arabic verb saḥaqa.

The prophet's name in the Qur’an does not mean “crushed,” “distanced” or “one who distances.”

Arabic root indexes of the Qur’an distinguish the name from the two actual derivatives of S Ḥ Q.

Forms actually occurring in the Qur’an

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an:

  • Saḥīqin in Al-Ḥajj 22:31: an adjective meaning very distant.
  • Fa-suḥqan in Al-Mulk 67:11: an accusative verbal noun in an expression of rejection and distancing.

Both belong to the distance branch.

Forms meaning pounding and crushing do not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Fī makānin saḥīqin: a very distant place in Al-Ḥajj 22:31

حُنَفَاءَ لِلَّهِ غَيْرَ مُشْرِكِينَ بِهِ وَمَنْ يُشْرِكْ بِاللَّهِ فَكَأَنَّمَا خَرَّ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَتَخْطَفُهُ الطَّيْرُ أَوْ تَهْوِي بِهِ الرِّيحُ فِي مَكَانٍ سَحِيقٍ

ḥunafāʾa li-Allāhi ghayra mushrikīna bihi wa-man yushrik bi-Allāhi fa-kaʾannamā kharra mina al-samāʾi fa-takhṭafuhu al-ṭayru aw tahwī bihi al-rīḥu fī makānin saḥīq

Al-Ḥajj 22:31

Translation:

“Turning toward The God, without associating partners with Him. Whoever associates partners with The God is as though he had fallen from the sky and birds snatched him away, or the wind cast him into a distant place.”

Saḥīqin qualifies makānin in this verse.

Both are indefinite and genitive.

Makān denotes a place or location.

Saḥīq expresses its great remoteness.

The direct meaning of fī makānin saḥīqin is therefore “in a very distant place.”

The image of falling from the sky

The verse begins with the call to turn sincerely toward The God without associating partners with Him.

It then depicts the condition of someone who associates partners through a simile.

A person falls from the sky.

One of two outcomes follows in the image: birds snatch him away, or the wind casts him into a very distant place.

This makes the loss of one's position and exposure to danger and remoteness tangible.

Each component is conveyed by its own wording.

Kharra expresses falling.

Fa-takhṭafuhu expresses rapid snatching.

Tahwī bihi al-rīḥu describes the wind carrying or casting him down.

Saḥīqin describes the remoteness of the place into which the wind casts him.

Saḥīq itself therefore does not mean falling, being snatched away or destruction.

Those elements emerge from the complete image.

Great distance, not necessarily great depth

In Persian, makān saḥīq is sometimes explained as a very deep valley or very low place.

Such expressions can relate to the falling image.

Lexically, however, saḥīq means very distant.

A location at the bottom of a valley may be remote because of its depth.

But a distant place need not be a valley or deep pit.

Al-Ḥajj 22:31 names no particular valley, well or precipice.

“A very distant place” is therefore more accurate as a direct translation than adding a named geographical feature.

The wind and displacement

The wind carries the person to a very distant place in this image.

This can illustrate a condition in which the person no longer controls the route or destination.

In ordinary experience, someone falling from a height and then driven by strong wind lacks control over the course of movement.

The image explains the consequences of abandoning the right orientation toward The God.

The simile should not, however, be turned into a prediction of a physical event for everyone who associates partners.

The verse says fa-kaʾannamā: “it is as though” the person had fallen from the sky.

Its structure is comparative; the image must be distinguished from the subject it illustrates.

Two outcomes in one image

The conjunction aw, “or,” separates being snatched by birds from being cast away by wind.

It presents two possibilities within the image.

Both events need not happen consecutively to one person.

In one branch, birds snatch the person away.

In the other, wind casts him into a distant place.

Their common feature is danger beyond the person's control.

Saḥīq, however, occurs only in the second branch.

Great remoteness should therefore not be inserted into the lexical meaning of the birds' snatching action.

Does saḥīq mean distance from guidance?

The verse contrasts associating partners with sincere devotion to The God.

Falling and being cast far away can illustrate the consequences of losing that orientation.

Spatial distance in the image thus helps convey distance from the right path.

This contextual implication differs from the word's lexical meaning.

Saḥīq by itself does not mean associating partners, misguidance or loss of guidance.

These ideas follow from the verse's subject.

Nor does the image, without examining other verses, settle any particular person's ultimate fate on the basis of interpretive disagreement or religious affiliation.

The meaning of associating partners, conditions of responsibility and a person's relationship to truth require examination of the relevant verses.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:31, saḥīq conveys great distance within a simile. The verse uses the image to explain the consequences of associating partners, while the word's lexical meaning remains “very distant.”

4. Fa-suḥqan li-aṣḥābi al-saʿīr: away with the inhabitants of the blazing Fire

فَاعْتَرَفُوا بِذَنْبِهِمْ فَسُحْقًا لِأَصْحَابِ السَّعِيرِ

fa-iʿtarafū bi-dhanbihim fa-suḥqan li-aṣḥābi al-saʿīr

Al-Mulk 67:11

Translation:

“Then they admit their sin; so away with the inhabitants of the blazing Fire!”

Al-Mulk 67:11

Suḥqan is an accusative verbal noun here.

The conjunction fa is attached to it.

Li-aṣḥābi al-saʿīr identifies the group to whom the rejection applies.

Unlike Al-Ḥajj 22:31, this does not describe a remote location.

Suḥqan is part of an established Arabic expression of rejection and distancing.

Suḥqan in Arabic

Suḥqan lahu means “Away with him.”

It is semantically related to buʿdan lahu.

Both use an accusative verbal noun to express distancing or rejection.

Grammatically, such an expression can be understood with an omitted verb, as though saying that the person should be removed far away.

A natural Persian translation need not reconstruct that verb in a lengthy phrase.

Dūr bād, “away with,” conveys the expression clearly and briefly.

Spatial distance and rejection

Saḥīq in Al-Ḥajj 22:31 qualifies a place.

Suḥqan in Al-Mulk 67:11 expresses rejection and distancing.

They belong to the same semantic branch but differ in structure and function.

The first concerns great distance between locations.

The second pronounces distancing against a specified group.

Such language need not describe bodily movement from one point to another.

It may concern removal from mercy, good or a desirable standing.

However, Al-Mulk 67:11 does not explicitly state in a separate phrase what they are removed from.

Explaining it as distance from divine mercy is therefore contextual interpretation, rather than the complete lexical meaning of suḥqan itself.

The context of admitting sin

Al-Mulk 67:8 describes people cast into the Fire, whose keepers ask whether a warner had come to them.

In Al-Mulk 67:9, they acknowledge that a warner came but that they rejected him as false.

In Al-Mulk 67:10, they say that had they listened or reasoned, they would not have been among the inhabitants of the blazing Fire.

Al-Mulk 67:11 then states their admission of sin and adds fa-suḥqan.

This sequence matters.

The people acknowledge their failure while facing accountability and the consequences of their deeds.

Fa-suḥqan thus concerns those who previously rejected the warning and now admit their sin.

Is admitting sin the same as repentance?

The verse says fa-iʿtarafū bi-dhanbihim.

Iʿtirāf belongs to ʿ R F and here concerns acknowledging and admitting sin.

Admission alone does not necessarily encompass repentance, reform and practical return.

A person may admit a mistake after its consequences become apparent.

Such admission concerns recognizing and accepting the error; the conditions and possibility of reform must be considered separately.

Al-Mulk 67:11 places the admission in a scene where the group confronts the outcome of its deeds.

The verse should not be detached from its timing and circumstances to deny the value of all admission and repentance during earthly life.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an presents repentance and reform as a way of return.

The specific subject here is admission after confronting the outcome of one's deeds.

Does fa-suḥqan mean being crushed and destroyed?

Arabic also has a crushing and pounding branch of S Ḥ Q.

But suḥqan in this construction concerns distance and rejection.

Rendering it simply as “may they be crushed” substitutes another branch for the expression's established meaning.

Rejection in a context of punishment may accompany destruction or a harsh outcome, but that implication follows from the broader passage.

The direct meaning of fa-suḥqan is “so away with.”

Is this a direct utterance by the inhabitants of the Fire?

The verb fa-iʿtarafū attributes the admission to the inhabitants of the Fire.

It is followed by fa-suḥqan li-aṣḥābi al-saʿīr.

This should not automatically be treated as part of their own confession.

Its structure expresses rejection and censure of that group.

Their admission of sin is distinct from the following pronouncement: “Away with the inhabitants of the blazing Fire.”

In Al-Mulk 67:11, suḥqan expresses great remoteness through rejection and censure, rather than physical pounding or crushing.

5. The relationship between the two branches

An Arabic word family may contain several closely or distantly related meanings.

Shared consonants alone do not establish that every meaning developed from one original image.

Both distance and crushing are attested for S Ḥ Q.

Does crushing produce distance?

One could imagine that when a body is pulverized, its parts move away from their earlier coherent state.

That image is insufficient historical evidence that distance developed from crushing.

Conversely, great distance does not naturally establish pounding and pulverizing without evidence.

Both branches are best understood through their actual usages.

Is wear related to distance?

Applied to old clothing, saḥq can describe the loss of its original quality.

One might explain this as the garment moving away from its freshness and initial condition.

That is primarily an imagined connection between the meanings.

It does not establish that pre-Islamic speakers necessarily understood every usage through “departure from an initial state.”

Nor do two semantic branches necessarily establish two entirely unrelated historical roots.

Without sufficient evidence for the developmental path, describe the branches independently and avoid certainty about a common or separate origin.

6. Distinctions from nearby roots

S Ḥ Q (suḥq) and B ʿ D (buʿd)

Buʿd denotes distance and remoteness.

The Qur’anic branch of S Ḥ Q belongs to the same field.

Lexicons explain suḥq as great remoteness.

Saḥīq can accordingly be an emphatic description of distance.

But no fixed numerical or spatial boundary separates the roots.

Baʿīd is not restricted to distances below a particular threshold and saḥīq to those above it.

The distinction concerns emphasis and usage, not a measurement encoded in the word.

Suḥqan in Al-Mulk 67:11 is close to buʿdan in expressions of rejection.

S Ḥ Q (saḥīq) and N ʾ Y (naʾy)

Naʾy concerns becoming distant or moving away.

The Qur’an also uses this family for turning away and withdrawing.

Saḥīq in Al-Ḥajj 22:31 qualifies a very distant place.

The verb naʾā can express the act of moving or turning away.

The families overlap in distance but differ in construction and function.

The use of naʾy for withdrawal does not make every saḥīq an expression of indifference or deliberate rejection.

S Ḥ Q (saḥīq) and Q Ṣ W (qaṣw)

Q Ṣ W concerns remoteness and the farthest part of something.

The Qur’an uses al-quṣwā in a reference to the farther bank.

This family is close to S Ḥ Q in the field of distance.

However, quṣwā can operate comparatively or in relation to a nearer bank.

Saḥīq in Al-Ḥajj 22:31 describes a place's great remoteness.

The word does not identify that place as the farthest possible point in the universe.

“Very distant” is therefore preferable to “the farthest place in existence.”

S Ḥ Q (saḥq) and D Q Q (daqq)

Daqq concerns pounding and reducing to small pieces.

The crushing branch of saḥq is close to this field.

Arabic sources explain saḥq as forceful pounding and pulverizing.

The roots can overlap in crushing grain or pounding hard material.

This branch, however, does not occur in the Qur’an.

Crushing should therefore not be imported into saḥīq in Al-Ḥajj 22:31 or suḥqan in Al-Mulk 67:11.

S Ḥ Q (suḥq) and Ṭ R D (ṭard)

Ṭard denotes driving away or expelling.

Suḥq in suḥqan lahu can convey rejection and distancing.

Yet not all distance results from someone deliberately driving another away.

A place, for example, can be very remote without having been expelled.

Rejection in Al-Mulk 67:11 follows from the imprecatory construction and context of censure.

Saḥīq in Al-Ḥajj 22:31 describes great distance and does not itself mean expelling.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Distinguishing saḥq, crushing, from suḥq, remoteness, matters because conflating them can change the verses' translation.

Important meanings in the family

Saḥuqa and saḥiqa: became very distant.

Suḥq: great remoteness; in an imprecatory expression, away with.

Saḥīq: very distant.

Saḥūq: very tall, when describing a palm.

Suḥuq: very tall palms, the plural of saḥūq in this usage; also a verbal noun of remoteness in other constructions.

Saḥaqa: pounded, crushed, wore out.

Saḥq: pounding and crushing; as a noun, worn clothing.

Masḥūq: pounded or crushed.

Insaḥaqa: became crushed or worn; in some usages, became distant.

Asḥaqa: became distant or made distant; in other usages, became worn or dry.

Proposed translations of the Qur’anic forms

Saḥīqin in Al-Ḥajj 22:31 means “very distant.”

Fa-suḥqan in Al-Mulk 67:11 means “so away with” in an expression of rejection.

These are proposals from the root study; they do not replace the installed verse translations.

Neither verse calls for “crushed” or “pounded,” since those meanings belong to another branch.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meaning

For the brief root box:

“Great distance and remoteness; in another branch, pounding and crushing.”

For Al-Ḥajj 22:31:

“The great remoteness of the place into which the wind casts the person in the verse's simile.”

For Al-Mulk 67:11:

“Rejection and distancing in the censure of the inhabitants of the blazing Fire: away with.”

Preserve the installed Arabic spelling in the Al-Ḥajj 22:31 box and distinguish the adjective saḥīq.

Preserve the attached fa in the Al-Mulk 67:11 box. These proposals do not replace existing authored verse explanations.

The adjective saḥīq must also be distinguished from the accusative verbal noun suḥqan.

8. Conclusion

Arabic has two important branches of S Ḥ Q: great remoteness, and pounding or wearing out.

In the verse attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, suḥuq describes very tall palms.

This illustrates the established early Arabic meaning of considerable distance.

The other branch is recorded for crushing grain, wearing clothing out and scouring a surface.

Muzarrid ibn Ḍirār's saḥq ʿimāmah likewise refers to a worn turban.

The branches differ semantically; an imagined picture alone cannot establish their historical relationship.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

Saḥīq in Al-Ḥajj 22:31 qualifies a very distant place.

It occurs in the image of a person falling from the sky and being cast away by wind.

Spatial distance is its direct meaning; the lesson about associating partners and losing the right orientation follows from the entire simile.

Fa-suḥqan in Al-Mulk 67:11 follows the inhabitants of the Fire admitting their sin.

It expresses rejection and distancing, naturally rendered in Persian as pas dūr bād, “so away with.”

Distance here is more than a geographical interval; it forms part of the censure and outcome pronounced upon the group.

Suḥqan itself nevertheless does not encode every detail of punishment or the nature of removal from divine mercy.

Neither Qur’anic occurrence invokes pounding or pulverizing.

Keep the two branches distinct: becoming very distant, and pounding, crushing or wearing something out. Both Qur’anic forms, saḥīq and suḥqan, belong to the remoteness branch.

For Forqan, “very distant” for saḥīq and “away with” for suḥqan are appropriate renderings.

The central difference is that saḥīq describes distance, whereas suḥqan operates in an imprecatory expression of censure. Crushing, although an attested Arabic meaning, should not be imposed on these Qur’anic uses.
