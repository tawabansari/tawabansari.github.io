The Root Ḥ B Ṭ

The loss of a deed’s fruit, effort coming to nothing, and the forfeiture of a work’s value and outcome

The Arabic root ح ب ط concerns spoiling, loss of benefit, and fruitlessness. Early uses include illness and abdominal swelling in livestock, a well losing its water, blood going unrequited, and work coming to nothing. No single identical physical image fits every use, but loss of soundness, benefit, or outcome is an important semantic connection.

The Qur’an uses this root only concerning human deeds. حَبِطَ عَمَلُهُ (ḥabiṭa ʿamaluhu) means that someone’s work loses its value and expected fruit; أَحْبَطَ أَعْمَالَهُمْ (aḥbaṭa aʿmālahum) concerns rendering their deeds fruitless or ineffective.

The Qur’anic settings vary: associating partners with God, rejection of faith, denial of the signs, injustice and knowing opposition to truth, hypocritical conduct, and even a warning to believers about their manner toward the Prophet.

Ḥabṭ cannot therefore be restricted to the visible destruction of work or a material event. An action may have occurred and left external effects, yet fail to secure the value, reward, or outcome its agent expects in divine judgment.

This distinction matters: the Qur’an speaks of deeds coming to nothing, rather than time reversing and past events being erased from history.

1. Forms and Derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • حَبِطَ ـ يَحْبَطُ (ḥabiṭa–yaḥbaṭu): became fruitless, losing benefit or value.
  • حَبْط (ḥabṭ): becoming fruitless; loss of a deed’s value or outcome.
  • حُبُوط (ḥubūṭ): another verbal noun for becoming fruitless or spoiled.
  • حَبِطَتِ الدَّابَّةُ (ḥabiṭat al-dābbah): the animal developed abdominal swelling from harmful food or overeating.
  • حَبَط (ḥabaṭ): livestock illness and abdominal swelling; elsewhere, an injury’s marks or effects.
  • حُبَاط (ḥubāṭ): the name of an illness involving abdominal swelling and disorder.
  • أَحْبَطَ ـ يُحْبِطُ (aḥbaṭa–yuḥbiṭu): rendered something fruitless or ineffective, depriving it of value or outcome.
  • إِحْبَاط (iḥbāṭ): rendering fruitless or ineffective; removing value or fruit.
  • مُحْبِط (muḥbiṭ): an agent or cause of fruitlessness.
  • مُحْبَط (muḥbaṭ): something rendered fruitless or ineffective.

The root occurs sixteen times in the Qur’an: twelve as the Form I verb ḥabiṭa and four as the Form IV verb aḥbaṭa.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: twelve Form I and four Form IV verbs

The sixteen occurrences span twelve surahs: Al-Baqarah 2:217; Āl ʿImrān 3:22; Al-Māʾidah 5:5 and Al-Māʾidah 5:53; Al-Anʿām 6:88; Al-Aʿrāf 7:147; Al-Tawbah 9:17 and Al-Tawbah 9:69; Hūd 11:16; Al-Kahf 18:105; Al-Aḥzāb 33:19; Al-Zumar 39:65; Muḥammad 47:9, Muḥammad 47:28, and Muḥammad 47:32; and Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2.

Every Qur’anic occurrence is a verb. The verbal nouns ḥabṭ, ḥubūṭ, and iḥbāṭ, and the forms muḥbiṭ and muḥbaṭ, do not occur in these forms in the Qur’anic text.

2. Semantic Core and Early Arabic Uses

Spoiling and loss of benefit

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris connects Ḥ B Ṭ with nullity or pain and injury. He explains أَحْبَطَ اللَّهُ عَمَلَ الْكَافِرِ (aḥbaṭa Allāhu ʿamala al-kāfir) as rendering the deed void, then discusses livestock illness and wound marks.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: nullity or pain

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī explains ḥabiṭa ʿamaluhu through the reward and outcome of work becoming void.

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits Al-Azharī’s explanation that when someone performs work and then spoils it, the work is said to have ḥabiṭa.

The accounts share one point: something that existed no longer has its expected benefit or fruit.

Someone might invest considerable effort and complete much of a task, then act in a way that loses its earlier fruit. The effort remains a historical fact, while its intended outcome fails to materialize.

This does not mean that ḥabṭ always follows completion of a task. Work may lack from the outset the conditions needed for its claimed value, or later conduct may spoil an earlier outcome.

The root itself establishes no single temporal sequence for every case.

Ḥabaṭ: abdominal swelling in livestock

One clear physical use is an illness recorded for livestock, particularly camels and sheep.

Lisān al-ʿArab describes animals eating harmful herbage or overeating, causing abdominal swelling and sometimes death.

The condition is called حَبَط (ḥabaṭ).

The abdomen appears larger, but the increase does not indicate greater health or strength. Visible enlargement can result from illness and injury.

This image has also been used to explain fruitless work: something may appear great and impressive yet produce no benefit or lead to ruin.

Al-Azharī compares the loss of a deed’s fruit with abdominal illness: just as the illness can destroy an animal, corruption can deprive work of its value and outcome.

Lisān and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: fruitless work and abdominal illness

This lexical explanation is not a secure history of the word’s origin. Similar imagery and results do not prove that the meaning relating to deeds necessarily developed from livestock illness throughout its history.

None of the sixteen Qur’anic occurrences concerns livestock illness. The image informs the language’s background; it does not justify adding swelling, abdomen, or disease to the verse translations.

Ḥabiṭat al-biʾr: a well losing its water

Lexical sources also record حَبِطَتِ الْبِئْرُ (ḥabiṭat al-biʾr): the well lost its water.

An account transmitted from Abū ʿAmr explains iḥbāṭ here as removing a well’s water so that it does not return as before.

Lisān: a well losing water and the report from Abū ʿAmr

This differs from abdominal illness. In one case something becomes swollen and ill; in the other, a source expected to provide water loses it.

The shared feature is loss of function and benefit.

The physical well may remain, but without water it no longer provides its expected benefit.

The image can clarify fruitless work without becoming a proven historical origin for the Qur’anic meaning.

Ḥabiṭa damu al-qatīl: blood going unrequited

Lexical sources record حَبِطَ دَمُ الْقَتِيلِ (ḥabiṭa damu al-qatīl).

The slain person’s blood goes unrequited and the associated claim is lost.

This does not mean that the material blood disappears. It concerns a claim losing its recognition and outcome.

An event may have occurred or something existed materially, while its legal effect or expected result is lost.

This lexical use cannot establish every rule of killing, retaliation, or judicial responsibility. Those questions require independent study of the verses on justice and retaliation.

Ḥabiṭa al-jurḥ: a wound’s lingering effects

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab record حَبِطَ الْجُرْحُ (ḥabiṭa al-jurḥ) for a wound retaining adverse effects or developing problems again after healing.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and other sources: wound complications and remaining marks

The use concerns an injured bodily condition.

The root does not necessarily mean complete destruction here; it can concern deterioration, complications, or remaining injury marks.

These physical uses therefore do not support a narrowly absolute definition such as “the total, irreversible destruction of everything.”

For Qur’anic language, loss of a deed’s value or fruit is closer to the intended meaning. Its extent and kind must be understood contextually.

Historical reports and the name Al-Ḥabaṭ

Genealogical and lexical sources identify Al-Ḥabaṭ as a byname of Al-Ḥārith ibn Māzin of Banū Tamīm.

Reports connect the byname with abdominal swelling attributed to him, and call his descendants or associated group Al-Ḥabaṭāt.

The word thus appears in Arabic tradition in names and tribal affiliations as well as ordinary usage.

Sources differ in details of descent and the reason for the byname. The illness story should not be treated as certain history or decisive evidence of the root’s origin.

Lexical sources: the byname Al-Ḥabaṭ and genealogical variants

Clear lexical evidence records livestock illness, unrequited blood, and wells losing water. This study, however, has not established an independent pre-Islamic poetic witness for the particular meaning with sufficiently secure wording, attribution, and dating.

An unsupported verse or reconstructed phrase should not be attributed to a pre-Islamic poet merely to supply an earlier witness.

3. Morphology: Ḥabiṭa and Aḥbaṭa

Ḥabiṭa: work becoming fruitless

Qur’anic ḥabiṭa is an intransitive Form I verb.

In حَبِطَتْ أَعْمَالُهُمْ (ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum), aʿmāluhum, “their deeds,” is the grammatical subject.

Their deeds become fruitless and lose their value or outcome.

The construction does not necessarily name an external agent rendering them fruitless. It emphasizes the work’s condition and result.

This does not mean that the outcome is independent of God’s judgment within the verse’s worldview. The distinction here is morphological and syntactic: the sentence describes the work as becoming fruitless.

Aḥbaṭa: rendering work fruitless

Aḥbaṭa is a transitive Form IV verb.

In فَأَحْبَطَ اللَّهُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ (fa-aḥbaṭa Allāhu aʿmālahum), God is the subject and their deeds the object.

The sentence therefore speaks not only of the deeds becoming fruitless, but of God rendering them so through judgment and action.

Translation should preserve this difference.

Ḥabiṭa ʿamaluhu can be rendered “his work came to nothing”; aḥbaṭa Allāhu aʿmālahum means “God rendered their deeds fruitless and ineffective.”

The forms should not be turned into two separate, opposing kinds of ruin. Both concern loss of value or outcome, expressed from different angles.

Emphasis, condition, and tense

The Qur’an does not always report this root as a definite, completed event.

Sometimes it appears conditionally, as in Al-Anʿām 6:88 and Al-Zumar 39:65.

Sometimes it warns against a consequence, as in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2.

Muḥammad 47:32 marks the future through سَيُحْبِطُ (sa-yuḥbiṭu).

The lexical meaning concerns becoming or rendering fruitless. Whether it has occurred, is conditional, is warned against, or is future comes from the complete sentence.

4. Qur’anic Uses

Al-Baqarah 2:217: abandoning religion and dying in unbelief

Arabic verse:

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الشَّهْرِ الْحَرَامِ قِتَالٍ فِيهِ قُلْ قِتَالٌ فِيهِ كَبِيرٌ وَصَدٌّ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَكُفْرٌ بِهِ وَالْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَإِخْرَاجُ أَهْلِهِ مِنْهُ أَكْبَرُ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَكْبَرُ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ وَلَا يَزَالُونَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَرُدُّوكُمْ عَنْ دِينِكُمْ إِنِ اسْتَطَاعُوا وَمَنْ يَرْتَدِدْ مِنْكُمْ عَنْ دِينِهِ فَيَمُتْ وَهُوَ كَافِرٌ فَأُولَٰئِكَ حَبِطَتْ أَعْمَالُهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ وَأُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-shahri al-ḥarāmi qitālin fīhi qul qitālun fīhi kabīrun wa-ṣaddun ʿan sabīli Allāhi wa-kufrun bihi wa-al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi wa-ikhrāju ahlihi minhu akbaru ʿinda Allāhi wa-al-fitnatu akbaru mina al-qatli wa-lā yazālūna yuqātilūnakum ḥattā yaruddūkum ʿan dīnikum ini istaṭāʿū wa-man yartadid minkum ʿan dīnihi fa-yamut wa-huwa kāfirun fa-ulāʾika ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati wa-ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They ask you about fighting in the sacred month. Say: Fighting in it is grave; but hindering the way of The God, rejecting Him and the Sacred Mosque, and expelling its people are graver before The God, and fitnah is graver than killing. They will keep fighting you to turn you back from your religion if they can. Whoever among you turns back from their religion and dies an unbeliever—their deeds have come to nothing in this world and the Hereafter; they are the people of the Fire, remaining there.”

Ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum follows a condition.

The condition has two parts: turning back from religion and dying in unbelief.

فَيَمُتْ وَهُوَ كَافِرٌ (fa-yamut wa-huwa kāfir) belongs to the same condition and cannot be omitted when examining the deeds’ outcome.

The verse does not simply say that anyone who turns back at one moment immediately and permanently loses every deed. It describes someone who turns back and dies in that condition.

فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ (fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirah) states the scope of the outcome in this case.

This should not mean that every material and social effect of someone’s work disappears. Others may have genuinely benefited from an action. The verse judges the work’s value and outcome in the setting of unbelief and turning back, rather than erasing all its effects from history.

Nor can this verse alone establish a worldly punishment for changing belief or authorize people to kill someone. The expression under examination principally concerns deeds becoming fruitless and the stated conditions of the final outcome.

Al-Anʿām 6:88 and Al-Zumar 39:65: association and the loss of a deed’s fruit

Al-Anʿām 6:88

ذَٰلِكَ هُدَى اللَّهِ يَهْدِي بِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ وَلَوْ أَشْرَكُوا لَحَبِطَ عَنْهُمْ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

dhālika hudā Allāhi yahdī bihi man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi wa-law ashrakū la-ḥabiṭa ʿanhum mā kānū yaʿmalūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“That is the guidance of The God, by which He guides whomever He wills among His servants. Had they associated partners with Him, what they used to do would have come to nothing for them.”

This follows the presentation of prophets and divine guidance.

وَلَوْ أَشْرَكُوا (wa-law ashrakū) is conditional: had they associated partners, their deeds would have come to nothing.

It should not be presented as reporting actual association by all the prophets mentioned.

ʿAn in لَحَبِطَ عَنْهُمْ (la-ḥabiṭa ʿanhum) emphasizes the relationship between loss of the work’s fruit and those who performed it.

Al-Zumar addresses the same warning directly to Muhammad:

Al-Zumar 39:65

وَلَقَدْ أُوحِيَ إِلَيْكَ وَإِلَى الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِكَ لَئِنْ أَشْرَكْتَ لَيَحْبَطَنَّ عَمَلُكَ وَلَتَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

wa-la-qad ūḥiya ilayka wa-ilā alladhīna min qablika la-in ashrakta la-yaḥbaṭanna ʿamaluka wa-la-takūnanna mina al-khāsirīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“It has certainly been revealed to you and to those before you: If you associate partners, your work will surely come to nothing, and you will certainly be among the losers.”

لَيَحْبَطَنَّ (la-yaḥbaṭanna) is an emphatic imperfect with lām and the emphatic nūn.

Muhammad is addressed directly, with association placed before him as a condition.

A theological assumption should not rewrite the warning to remove its actual addressee. The verse places Muhammad and earlier prophets before the same principle of divine oneness and responsibility for action.

A conditional construction, however, does not establish that its condition occurred.

Both verses show that even prophetic standing does not change the meaning of association. Identifying association itself requires study of divine oneness and authority, rather than an arbitrary expansion of ḥabṭ.

Al-Māʾidah 5:5: rejection of faith

Arabic verse:

الْيَوْمَ أُحِلَّ لَكُمُ الطَّيِّبَاتُ وَطَعَامُ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ حِلٌّ لَكُمْ وَطَعَامُكُمْ حِلٌّ لَهُمْ وَالْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ وَالْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ إِذَا آتَيْتُمُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ وَلَا مُتَّخِذِي أَخْدَانٍ وَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ بِالْإِيمَانِ فَقَدْ حَبِطَ عَمَلُهُ وَهُوَ فِي الْآخِرَةِ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

al-yawma uḥilla lakumu al-ṭayyibātu wa-ṭaʿāmu alladhīna ūtū al-kitāba ḥillun lakum wa-ṭaʿāmukum ḥillun lahum wa-al-muḥṣanātu mina al-muʾmināti wa-al-muḥṣanātu mina alladhīna ūtū al-kitāba min qablikum idhā ātaytumūhunna ujūrahunna muḥṣinīna ghayra musāfiḥīna wa-lā muttakhidhī akhdānin wa-man yakfur bi-al-īmāni fa-qad ḥabiṭa ʿamaluhu wa-huwa fī al-ākhirati mina al-khāsirīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Today good things have been made lawful for you. The food of those given the Book is lawful for you, and yours is lawful for them; and chaste women among the believers and among those given the Book before you, when you give them their dues, remaining chaste, neither committing fornication nor taking secret lovers. Whoever rejects faith—their work has come to nothing, and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers.”

Ḥabiṭa ʿamaluhu is connected with يَكْفُرْ بِالْإِيمَانِ (yakfur bi-al-īmān).

The stated issue is rejection of faith.

Its placement in a verse on food and marriage does not mean that marriage to the People of the Book or eating their food itself nullifies deeds: the verse declares those things lawful within its stated limits.

This clearly illustrates the need to attend to sentence structure and the subject of a statement.

Ḥabiṭa ʿamaluhu lexically expresses work becoming fruitless; the cause stated here is rejection of faith.

Āl ʿImrān 3:22: fruitless deeds in the context of injustice

Arabic verse:

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ حَبِطَتْ أَعْمَالُهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ وَمَا لَهُمْ مِنْ نَاصِرِينَ

ulāʾika alladhīna ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati wa-mā lahum min nāṣirīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They are those whose deeds have come to nothing in this world and the Hereafter, and they have no helpers.”

This continues Āl ʿImrān 3:21, which concerns rejection of God’s signs, wrongful killing of prophets, and killing those who command justice.

Ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum is therefore attributed to people described through particular actions.

The context is more than an abstract belief: opposition to truth and grave injustice are also present.

The verse should not be separated from those actions and reduced to the name of a people or membership in a historical group.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:147: denial of the signs and the meeting of the Hereafter

Arabic verse:

وَالَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا وَلِقَاءِ الْآخِرَةِ حَبِطَتْ أَعْمَالُهُمْ هَلْ يُجْزَوْنَ إِلَّا مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

wa-alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-liqāʾi al-ākhirati ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum hal yujzawna illā mā kānū yaʿmalūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Those who denied Our signs and the meeting of the Hereafter—their deeds have come to nothing. Are they recompensed for anything except what they used to do?”

The loss of the deeds’ fruit is connected with denying the signs and the meeting of the Hereafter.

The concluding question also links recompense to people’s own actions.

Ḥabṭ is thus inseparable here from judgment of work and its outcome.

Al-Kahf 18:105 likewise joins rejection of the Lord’s signs and meeting Him with deeds becoming fruitless.

The association connects the value and outcome of conduct with a person’s relationship to truth and accountability. The root alone does not specify every detail of that relationship.

Al-Māʾidah 5:53 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:19: deeds without the support of faith

Al-Māʾidah 5:53

وَيَقُولُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَهَٰؤُلَاءِ الَّذِينَ أَقْسَمُوا بِاللَّهِ جَهْدَ أَيْمَانِهِمْ إِنَّهُمْ لَمَعَكُمْ حَبِطَتْ أَعْمَالُهُمْ فَأَصْبَحُوا خَاسِرِينَ

wa-yaqūlu alladhīna āmanū a-hāʾulāʾi alladhīna aqsamū bi-Allāhi jahda aymānihim innahum la-maʿakum ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum fa-aṣbaḥū khāsirīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Those who believe say: Are these the ones who swore their strongest oaths by The God that they were certainly with you? Their deeds came to nothing, and they became losers.”

The context concerns people presenting themselves as companions of the believers through emphatic oaths.

Strong oaths did not guarantee truthful claims or firm faith.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 describes people afraid during danger, sharp-tongued once it passes, and stingy concerning good; it then says that they have not believed and God has rendered their deeds fruitless.

It uses فَأَحْبَطَ اللَّهُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ (fa-aḥbaṭa Allāhu aʿmālahum).

Al-Māʾidah reports the deeds becoming fruitless, while Al-Aḥzāb identifies God as the agent rendering them so.

Both distinguish claimed companionship or outward conduct from the reality of faith.

Neither establishes that every lapse, fear, or shortcoming of a believer itself proves hypocrisy or destroys all their deeds. The verses judge groups described by a combination of particular traits and actions.

Al-Tawbah 9:17 and Al-Tawbah 9:69: outward activity and loss of its fruit

Al-Tawbah 9:17 says that associators are not entitled to maintain God’s places of worship while bearing witness against themselves to unbelief. It then reports their deeds coming to nothing.

Maintaining a building can be a real activity with material effects. A constructed building may remain; the work itself does not establish acceptance of its agent’s religious or spiritual standing.

Ḥabṭ should not therefore be equated with the disappearance of bricks, walls, or the building itself.

Al-Tawbah 9:69 compares people with earlier generations possessing strength, wealth, and children who indulged in their worldly share, then speaks of their deeds becoming fruitless in this world and the Hereafter.

Abundant wealth and worldly power do not establish conduct’s ultimate value.

The two verses distinguish an activity’s outward greatness from its value in divine judgment.

Hūd 11:16: worldly results without a fruit in the Hereafter

Arabic verse:

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ لَيْسَ لَهُمْ فِي الْآخِرَةِ إِلَّا النَّارُ وَحَبِطَ مَا صَنَعُوا فِيهَا وَبَاطِلٌ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

ulāʾika alladhīna laysa lahum fī al-ākhirati illā al-nāru wa-ḥabiṭa mā ṣanaʿū fīhā wa-bāṭilun mā kānū yaʿmalūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They are those who have nothing in the Hereafter except the Fire. What they made and did in the world has come to nothing, and what they used to do is vain.”

This follows Hūd 11:15, concerning those who desire worldly life and its adornment and receive the fruit of their deeds in this world.

The issue is therefore not a complete absence of worldly results.

They may have gained worldly benefits, but those benefits do not secure the expected final outcome.

Ḥabiṭa occurs here alongside بَاطِلٌ (bāṭilun).

The juxtaposition shows the proximity of Ḥ B Ṭ and B Ṭ L in the field of fruitless or valueless work.

Their occurrence together does not establish two fixed, separate stages of ḥabṭ and buṭlān.

Nor does the verse deny every real effect of work in the world. Contextually, it concerns the lack of a final fruit in the Hereafter for those who performed it.

Al-Ṭabarī: worldly results and the loss of final reward

Al-Kahf 18:105: great effort without weight in the final judgment

Arabic verse:

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِآيَاتِ رَبِّهِمْ وَلِقَائِهِ فَحَبِطَتْ أَعْمَالُهُمْ فَلَا نُقِيمُ لَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ وَزْنًا

ulāʾika alladhīna kafarū bi-āyāti rabbihim wa-liqāʾihi fa-ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum fa-lā nuqīmu lahum yawma al-qiyāmati waznan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They are those who rejected their Lord’s signs and meeting Him. Their deeds have come to nothing, and on the Day of Resurrection We assign them no weight.”

This follows Al-Kahf 18:103 and Al-Kahf 18:104, concerning those who lose most in their deeds: their effort goes astray in worldly life while they imagine they are doing good.

This context adds an important point to understanding ḥabṭ.

People may work very hard and believe their work right and valuable. Neither great effort nor personal conviction guarantees a sound course or ultimate value.

Al-Kahf 18:105 identifies the issue through rejection of the Lord’s signs and meeting Him.

This does not mean that everyone making a theoretical or practical mistake loses the value of every effort in the Hereafter.

The statement فَلَا نُقِيمُ لَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ وَزْنًا (fa-lā nuqīmu lahum yawma al-qiyāmati waznan) concerns final judgment. It should not deny people’s human worth across worldly relationships and rights.

Ḥabṭ here is linked with loss of the deeds’ weight and standing in final assessment.

Muḥammad 47:9, Muḥammad 47:28, and Muḥammad 47:32: deeds rendered fruitless through opposition to guidance

Surah Muḥammad uses this root’s Form IV three times.

Muḥammad 47:9 identifies dislike of what God sent down as the cause.

Muḥammad 47:28 concerns following what angers God and disliking His approval.

Muḥammad 47:32 connects the issue with opposition and hindering God’s way after guidance became clear.

Muḥammad 47:32

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَصَدُّوا عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَشَاقُّوا الرَّسُولَ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الْهُدَىٰ لَنْ يَضُرُّوا اللَّهَ شَيْئًا وَسَيُحْبِطُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ

inna alladhīna kafarū wa-ṣaddū ʿan sabīli Allāhi wa-shāqqū al-rasūla min baʿdi mā tabayyana lahumu al-hudā lan yaḍurrū Allāha shayʾan wa-sa-yuḥbiṭu aʿmālahum

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Those who reject, hinder the way of The God, and oppose the Messenger after guidance has become clear to them will not harm The God at all, and He will render their deeds fruitless.”

Sa-yuḥbiṭu expresses the future.

مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الْهُدَىٰ (min baʿdi mā tabayyana lahumu al-hudā) matters: the opposition is described as following clarity of guidance.

This is not simply about someone who has not yet recognized a truth or asks questions to understand it.

Sa-yuḥbiṭu aʿmālahum concerns rendering their deeds fruitless; the preceding descriptions identify who faces that outcome and why.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2: a warning to believers

Arabic verse:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَرْفَعُوا أَصْوَاتَكُمْ فَوْقَ صَوْتِ النَّبِيِّ وَلَا تَجْهَرُوا لَهُ بِالْقَوْلِ كَجَهْرِ بَعْضِكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ أَنْ تَحْبَطَ أَعْمَالُكُمْ وَأَنْتُمْ لَا تَشْعُرُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tarfaʿū aṣwātakum fawqa ṣawti al-nabiyyi wa-lā tajharū lahu bi-al-qawli ka-jahri baʿḍikum li-baʿḍin an taḥbaṭa aʿmālukum wa-antum lā tashʿurūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“You who believe, do not raise your voices above the Prophet’s voice or speak loudly to him as you do to one another, lest your deeds come to nothing without your awareness.”

The verse matters in several respects.

First, it addresses those who believe. Warnings of fruitless deeds are therefore not restricted to people elsewhere called unbelievers or associators.

Second, أَنْ تَحْبَطَ أَعْمَالُكُمْ (an taḥbaṭa aʿmālukum) occurs in a warning. The verse does not say that every addressee’s deeds have already become fruitless; it prohibits conduct that may have that consequence.

Third, وَأَنْتُمْ لَا تَشْعُرُونَ (wa-antum lā tashʿurūn) emphasizes possible unawareness of the consequence.

The prohibited conduct concerns speaking to the Prophet and respecting him.

It does not establish that every raised voice by a believer in every social situation nullifies every deed. The verse specifies the subject, addressees, relationship, and conduct.

The warning should not, however, be emptied of force. Believers are reminded that even their manner of speech and behavior can have serious consequences.

Ḥabṭ thus appears not only in descriptions of unbelievers’ outcome, but also in the education and warning of believers.

Al-Ṭabarī: warning of fruitless deeds without awareness

5. Interpretive Challenges and Semantic Development

Does ḥabṭ al-aʿmāl mean erasing deeds from history?

No. That meaning does not follow from the root.

A past action remains a historical fact. Building something, giving to someone in need, or helping another person cannot be turned into an event that never occurred merely by a word.

The same deed may nevertheless lack the value and reward its agent claims under another assessment.

Loss of a deed’s fruit or standing differs from erasing all its material and social effects.

That distinction does not make ḥabṭ a merely subjective evaluation without real consequences. Many of the verses speak of loss in the Hereafter, the Fire, and lack of value in final judgment.

It is a real judgment of conduct’s fruit and outcome, without necessarily destroying past events materially.

Does every sin eliminate all good deeds?

The root Ḥ B Ṭ itself states no such general rule.

The verses identify specific causes: association, rejection of faith, denial of the signs, opposition after clarity of guidance, and other contextual conduct.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2 warns believers about a particular manner toward the Prophet.

The mere presence of ḥabṭ does not mean that every minor or major sin permanently destroys all good deeds regardless of repentance, reform, redress, and other Qur’anic principles.

This caution does not mean that someone’s behavior never affects the value of other deeds. The verses warn precisely of such consequences under specified conditions.

The task is to identify those conditions from the Qur’an, rather than formulate a broader rule than the verses state.

Ḥabṭ, repentance, and reform

Al-Baqarah 2:217 explicitly qualifies the case by dying in unbelief.

The person’s final condition therefore matters for the statement made.

Other verses speak of repentance, reform, and acceptance of return, including Al-Furqān 25:70 and Al-Zumar 39:53.

Ḥabṭ should not be detached from this Qur’anic network to portray every path of return and reform as absolutely closed, against clear verses.

The root alone, however, cannot establish how rewards for earlier deeds are restored, calculated, or assessed in the Hereafter.

Such questions require independent study of repentance, righteous action, recompense, and accounting.

Do good and bad deeds form a numerical accounting system?

Islamic theology developed extensive debates about إِحْبَاط (iḥbāṭ) and تَكْفِير (takfīr).

One question was whether bad deeds eliminate the reward of good deeds, whether good deeds remove punishment for wrongdoing, and how such a relationship is calculated.

Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī reports differing theological positions on ḥabṭ when discussing Al-Baqarah 2:217.

Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī: differing accounts of deeds becoming fruitless

The Qur’anic word itself supplies no numerical formula for adding, subtracting, or canceling rewards and punishments.

The Qur’an speaks of weighing deeds, justice, reward, punishment, mercy, forgiveness, and repentance. Their relationship requires considering the verses together.

The later theological expression “iḥbāṭ and takfīr” merits independent study; its theories of balancing deeds should not all be imposed on ḥabiṭa as lexical meaning.

Is ḥabṭ always caused by a wrong intention?

Intention and orientation matter in the Qur’an, but the sixteen occurrences are not restricted to one cause.

Hūd 11:15 and Hūd 11:16 emphasize desire for worldly life and its adornment.

Al-Anʿām 6:88 and Al-Zumar 39:65 state association as a condition.

Muḥammad 47:32 emphasizes opposition after guidance has become clear.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2 warns about conduct and speech toward the Prophet.

Reducing every instance to “bad intention” or “showing off” is therefore inaccurate.

Intention may be important in some cases, while others explicitly name conduct, belief, or a person’s relationship to truth.

People may also think their conduct right, as shown by Al-Kahf 18:103, Al-Kahf 18:104, and Al-Kahf 18:105. Personal conviction alone does not establish right action.

Iḥbāṭ and frustration in modern language

Modern Persian sometimes uses expressions of ḥabṭ, failure, or frustration for the psychological condition of someone whose efforts fail to achieve their aim. Modern Arabic iḥbāṭ can likewise mean discouraging someone, frustrating them, or thwarting their effort.

These meanings relate to fruitless effort, but the modern psychological sense should not be transferred into the Qur’an.

The verses do not concern merely the agent’s feeling of despair.

A person might not even recognize the loss, as Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2 warns.

Ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum therefore does not mean “they felt frustrated.” It concerns the value and fruit of the deeds themselves.

6. Differences from Related Roots

Ḥ B Ṭ (ḥabiṭa) and B Ṭ L (baṭala): loss of fruit and nullity

Study of B Ṭ L

Baṭala concerns becoming void, invalid, or ineffective.

Qur’anic ḥabiṭa also concerns work becoming fruitless or valueless.

The families occur together in Hūd 11:16, illustrating semantic proximity.

Ḥabṭ often emphasizes a deed’s outcome and loss of benefit; buṭlān can concern incorrectness, invalidity, or absence of value.

This tendency is not an absolute rule in which ḥabṭ always follows initially valid work and buṭlān always exists from the outset.

Both can concern fruitless work; context determines the precise difference.

Ḥ B Ṭ (ḥabiṭa) and Ḍ L L (ḍalla): loss of fruit and losing the way

Study of Ḍ L L

Some uses of ḍalla concern becoming lost or leaving the right path.

Muḥammad 47:1 and Muḥammad 47:8 use Ḍ L L concerning deeds rendered fruitless, while the same surah also uses aḥbaṭa.

The roots can therefore overlap in describing fruitless effort.

The lexical image of ḍalāl can emphasize losing the way and failing to reach a destination, while ḥabṭ concerns loss of the fruit.

Their Qur’anic juxtaposition establishes no absolute technical boundary between every use.

Ḥ B Ṭ (ḥabiṭa) and KH S R (khasira): fruitless work and its agent’s loss

Study of KH S R

Khasira concerns loss and forfeiting profit or capital.

Several verses pair deeds becoming fruitless with their agent’s loss, including Al-Māʾidah 5:5, Al-Māʾidah 5:53, and Al-Zumar 39:65.

The connection is understandable: loss of a deed’s fruit and value can entail loss for its agent.

Khusrān and ḥabṭ, however, are not complete synonyms.

Ḥabṭ describes the work’s condition and outcome; khusrān describes the agent’s loss.

People can suffer loss in many ways without Ḥ B Ṭ occurring in every case.

Ḥ B Ṭ (ḥabiṭa) and F S D (fasada): corruption and spoiling

Study of F S D

Fasada concerns becoming spoiled or departing from a sound, proper state.

Ḥabiṭa can also concern spoiling and loss of benefit.

Lexical sources explain ḥabṭ of work through its corruption.

Qur’anic F S D has a much broader range, including land, society, conduct, and human relationships.

Qur’anic ḥabṭ is restricted to deeds and their fruit.

Not every corruption is therefore ḥabṭ al-aʿmāl, and ḥabṭ should not automatically be rendered as social or bodily corruption without contextual examination.

Ḥ B Ṭ (ḥabiṭa) and M Ḥ W (maḥā): loss of value and erasure

Maḥā concerns removing or effacing a trace in Arabic.

Qur’anic M Ḥ W likewise concerns erasure or removal.

Ḥabṭ can concern loss of a deed’s fruit or standing, without necessarily erasing every historical effect.

“Complete erasure of every trace of deeds from the world” is therefore inaccurate.

Both families can express disappearance in certain settings, but their imagery and uses differ.

Ḥ B Ṭ (ḥabiṭa) and H B Ṭ (habaṭa): becoming fruitless and descending

Study of H B Ṭ

Ḥabiṭa can be confused with هَبَطَ (habaṭa).

Ḥ B Ṭ begins with ḥāʾ and concerns deeds becoming fruitless in the Qur’an.

H B Ṭ begins with hāʾ and concerns descending or going down.

Ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum does not mean “their deeds descended.”

Similarity in sound or writing proves neither shared derivation nor identical meaning.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For ḥabiṭa ʿamaluhu, the Persian ʿamalash tabāh shod, “his work came to nothing,” is clear, brief, and faithful.

“Their deeds came to nothing” suits ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum. “Their deeds became fruitless” also clarifies the root, though some contexts might restrict a reader’s understanding to a worldly goal not being achieved.

“God rendered their deeds fruitless and ineffective” preserves the distinction between Form IV aḥbaṭa Allāhu aʿmālahum and the intransitive verb.

“He will render their deeds fruitless” suits sa-yuḥbiṭu aʿmālahum in Muḥammad 47:32 and preserves the future.

“Your work will surely come to nothing” naturally renders la-yaḥbaṭanna ʿamaluka in Al-Zumar 39:65 while preserving emphasis.

“Lest your deeds come to nothing” suits an taḥbaṭa aʿmālukum in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2 better than a categorical statement, because the construction warns of a possible consequence.

“What they did in the world has come to nothing” suits ḥabiṭa mā ṣanaʿū fīhā in Hūd 11:16. Reflection can explain the lack of expected value and fruit in final judgment, rather than deny that the work occurred historically.

For Forqan’s base text, tabāh shodan-e ʿamal, “work coming to nothing,” is best retained as a basic rendering, with loss of effect, fruit, and value explained lexically.

“Becoming void” is usable in some cases. Since the Qur’an also has B Ṭ L, preserving the distinction between the roots helps translation and analysis.

“Being destroyed” is too absolute if understood as erasing the work’s material or historical existence. The Persian “becoming spoiled/fruitless” is less restrictive and allows precise contextual explanation.

8. Conclusion

Ḥ B Ṭ concerns spoiling, loss of benefit, and fruitlessness. Early uses for livestock illness, a well drying up, blood going unrequited, and work coming to nothing show more than one kind of material deterioration. Brief root meaning: A deed losing its fruit and value; in Form IV, rendering it fruitless and ineffective.

All sixteen Qur’anic occurrences concern human deeds.

Ḥabiṭa expresses work becoming fruitless; aḥbaṭa expresses rendering it fruitless and ineffective.

The contexts give different causes: association, rejection of faith, denial of the signs, opposition after clarity of guidance, hypocritical behavior, and failure to respect the Prophet.

Ḥabṭ should not become an unlimited rule in which every mistake permanently destroys every deed regardless of its nature, repentance, reform, and the Qur’an’s stated conditions.

Nor should the warning be reduced to mere words. It concerns a real loss of conduct’s value and fruit in divine judgment.

An action occurring and its outcome being valuable are distinct. People may strive, build, give, speak, or work hard toward a goal; effort and outward magnitude alone do not determine the action’s value and outcome.

Its orientation, relationship to truth, the agent’s conduct, and each verse’s stated conditions affect the outcome.

Ḥabṭ al-aʿmāl thus concerns deeds losing their fruit and value, rather than necessarily erasing the action and every material effect from history.

This distinction preserves the lexical meaning and prevents an unsupported categorical theory of calculating all human good and evil deeds from being imposed on it.
