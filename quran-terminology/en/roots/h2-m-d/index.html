Root H M D — Extinction and the subsiding of visible life

Qur’anic and lexical forms

هَامِدَةً
(hāmidatan)
Dry and vegetationless; feminine active participle

From extinguished fire and lost movement to dry land that grows again after rain

The Arabic triliteral root ه م د concerns extinction, subsiding activity, lost heat, and deterioration. It describes extinguished fire, quieted sound, dried vegetation, and worn things that have lost their earlier strength or condition.

The Qur’an uses it once as هَامِدَةً: land dry and without vegetation, then stirred into growth when water descends.

Here hāmidah is more than merely motionless land. Visible green growth has ceased; water then changes that condition.

Humūd does not invariably mean absolute destruction. Extinguished fire may have lost its heat, but hāmidah land can grow plants again under suitable conditions. The object and context determine the precise sense.

1. The root in early Arabic

Classical Arabic records هَمَدَ يَهْمُدُ هُمُودًا for becoming extinguished or inactive.

One familiar expression concerns fire:

هَمَدَتِ النَّارُ

“The fire went out.”

Lexical explanations sometimes distinguish subsiding flames from complete extinction. Embers may remain after flames subside; in one precise usage, humūd describes complete extinction and loss of heat.

رَمَادٌ هَامِدٌ likewise describes cold ash, possibly compacted by time and moisture.

This branch therefore extends beyond reduced flames to the cessation of fire’s activity and heat.

A pre-Qur’anic poetic witness

The renowned pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes abandoned dwelling places:

«وَغَيْرُ ثَلَاثٍ كَالْحَمَامِ خَوَالِدٍ
وَهَابٍ مُحِيلٍ هَامِدٍ مُتَلَبِّدِ»

Wa-ghayru thalāthin ka-l-ḥamāmi khawālidin / wa-hābin muḥīlin hāmidin mutalabbidi.

“And nothing remained but three hearthstones, enduring like perched doves, and year-old ash, cold and compacted.”

The site once housed people; hearthstones and cold ash remain as traces of its former life.

هَاب denotes ash. مُحِيل describes something over which a year has passed; هَامِد its extinguished, heatless state; مُتَلَبِّد its compacted condition.

Together they depict a former fire reduced to old, cold remains.

Hāmid here is not arbitrary stillness: applied to ash, it concerns the end of the fire’s activity and heat.

The line survives in Zuhayr’s attributed collection and older commentaries. Its place in pre-Islamic poetic tradition is established, though its exact composition date is not independently known.

It supports the extinction sense in early Arabic without proving that every branch originated in fire imagery.

Sound and movement

هَمَدَتْ أَصْوَاتُهُمْ describes voices falling silent.

Nothing burns here: the sound ceases and silence returns.

هَمْدَة is also recorded for silence or a pause in sound.

The family can thus describe the subsiding of formerly perceptible activity.

Death and deterioration

هَمَدَ الرَّجُلُ is also recorded for a person dying.

The family can describe a destroyed community, whose life or activity has ended.

Death should not be imposed on every derivative.

هَمَدَ الثَّوْبُ describes cloth so worn that it falls apart when touched.

Cloth does not literally die; its former strength and usability are lost.

نَبَاتٌ هَامِدٌ describes dry, deteriorated vegetation. Related expressions also describe blackened, spoiled fruit.

The root can therefore concern lost life, activity, growth, or strength, with its object specifying the condition.

Some sources record أَهْمَدَ both for staying in a place and for moving quickly. Their historical connection with extinction is uncertain; no invented link is needed to force all branches into one meaning.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

The root occurs once in one form:

هَامِدَةً — al-Ḥajj 22:5

هَامِدَة is the feminine active participle of basic هَمَدَ, used here in the accusative.

In وَتَرَى الْأَرْضَ هَامِدَةً, it describes the observed land. الْأَرْض is grammatically feminine, hence the feminine description.

Arabic also records هَمَدَ, يَهْمُدُ, هُمُود, هَمْد, هَامِد, هَمِيد, هَمْدَة, and أَهْمَدَ.

These exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Since هَامِدَةً describes land in al-Ḥajj 22:5, its dry, ungrowing sense takes priority over human death or residence in a place.

3. Hāmidah — Dry, inactive land

Al-Ḥajj 22:5

«يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ إِن كُنتُمْ فِى رَيْبٍ مِّنَ ٱلْبَعْثِ فَإِنَّا خَلَقْنَـٰكُم مِّن تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ عَلَقَةٍ ثُمَّ مِن مُّضْغَةٍ مُّخَلَّقَةٍ وَغَيْرِ مُخَلَّقَةٍ لِّنُبَيِّنَ لَكُمْ ۚ وَنُقِرُّ فِى ٱلْأَرْحَامِ مَا نَشَآءُ إِلَىٰٓ أَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى ثُمَّ نُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا ثُمَّ لِتَبْلُغُوٓا أَشُدَّكُمْ ۖ وَمِنكُم مَّن يُتَوَفَّىٰ وَمِنكُم مَّن يُرَدُّ إِلَىٰٓ أَرْذَلِ ٱلْعُمُرِ لِكَيْلَا يَعْلَمَ مِنۢ بَعْدِ عِلْمٍ شَيْـًٔا ۚ وَتَرَى ٱلْأَرْضَ هَامِدَةً فَإِذَآ أَنزَلْنَا عَلَيْهَا ٱلْمَآءَ ٱهْتَزَّتْ وَرَبَتْ وَأَنۢبَتَتْ مِن كُلِّ زَوْجٍ بَهِيجٍ»

Yā ayyuhā n-nāsu in kuntum fī raybin mina l-baʿthi fa-innā khalaqnākum min turābin thumma min nuṭfatin thumma min ʿalaqatin thumma min muḍghatin mukhallaqatin wa-ghayri mukhallaqatin li-nubayyina lakum wa-nuqirru fī l-arḥāmi mā nashāʾu ilā ajalin musamman thumma nukhrijukum ṭiflan thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum wa-minkum man yutawaffā wa-minkum man yuraddu ilā ardhali l-ʿumuri li-kaylā yaʿlama min baʿdi ʿilmin shayʾan wa-tarā l-arḍa hāmidatan fa-idhā anzalnā ʿalayhā l-māʾa htazzat wa-rabat wa-anbatat min kulli zawjin bahīj.

“People, if you doubt the resurrection, We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging thing, then from a lump of flesh, formed and unformed, to make matters clear to you. We retain in wombs what We will until an appointed term, then bring you out as infants, then so that you reach full strength. Some of you are taken in death, and some are returned to the most diminished stage of life, knowing nothing after having known. You see the land dry and without vegetation; when We send water down upon it, it stirs, swells, and produces every delightful kind of plant.”

The verse begins with resurrection after death. It recalls human formation and life, then presents an observable natural example.

Land can be dry and inactive, without visible green growth; rain then brings movement and vegetation to that same land.

وَتَرَى الْأَرْضَ هَامِدَةً

Hāmidah describes the land’s condition.

Lexicons explain أَرْضٌ هَامِدَةٌ as dry land without visible growth, or land bearing only dry, broken plant remains.

The condition is associated with water shortage and lost plant cover.

Imagine a plain after months without rain: its vegetation has dried, former greenery has disappeared, and it looks still and ungrowing.

That approximates the image of hāmidah.

Does it prove that every part of the soil lacks all life or capacity for growth?

No. It describes dryness and absent visible growth, not a scientific finding that all soil life is absent.

Nor does it mean intrinsically and permanently infertile land: the verse immediately describes its change after water arrives.

The land is hāmidah in one condition, then visibly grows when watered.

اهْتَزَّتْ — Movement after inactivity

The description is followed by اهْتَزَّتْ.

This belongs to ه ز ز, concerning shaking or stirring.

Here the stirring follows water and accompanies plant growth.

Nature offers an observable image: previously dry, still-looking land changes after rain as shoots emerge, leaves open, and plants grow.

Its appearance shifts from stillness to active growth.

اهْتَزَّتْ belongs to a different root; its movement should not all be incorporated into hāmidah’s definition.

The phrase alone does not establish an earthquake or a specific geological movement. Its context concerns growth after water.

رَبَتْ — Rising and increasing

رَبَتْ belongs to ر ب و, concerning increase, swelling, and growth here.

It relates to اهْتَزَّتْ without being identical.

One describes stirring and change; the other rising and increase.

Water therefore brings more than an end to stillness: growth and expansion become visible.

Plant growth, water uptake, soil expansion, and biological processes can be studied separately; they are not all direct lexical meanings of these words.

أَنْبَتَتْ — Visible plant growth

أَنْبَتَتْ completes the scene: the land produces vegetation.

It identifies the visible result of the preceding change.

Dry land becomes a place where different plants grow after water arrives.

مِن كُلِّ زَوْجٍ بَهِيجٍ conveys the diversity and beauty of the growth.

Each word has its contribution: هَامِدَةً the initial condition, اهْتَزَّتْ stirring, رَبَتْ rising and increase, and أَنْبَتَتْ plant production.

The sequence depicts change without requiring every stage to occur simultaneously or through one physical mechanism.

4. Hāmidah land and resurrection

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 places this description within creation, death, and resurrection.

The verse recalls human formation, infancy, maturity, death for some, and the weakness of old age for others.

It then depicts dry land and renewed growth.

Al-Ḥajj 22:6 mentions reviving the dead; al-Ḥajj 22:7 affirms the coming Hour and raising those in graves.

The land illustrates transformation and renewed life within the argument about resurrection.

Does hāmidah mean the land’s absolute death?

Humūd can describe human death or completely extinguished fire.

Those usages do not automatically transfer every feature to land.

In al-Ḥajj 22:5, the land is dry and lacks visible growth; water brings plants and renewed growth.

The wording does not say the land’s entire existence vanished and then emerged from absolute nothingness.

Conversely, knowledge that seeds or living structures can survive in soil does not negate the passage’s connection with reviving the dead.

The two questions should remain distinct.

The word directly describes the land; the verse uses its transformation to recall divine creative and resurrecting power.

The natural example supports the possibility of resurrection in context without explaining its physical mechanism.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

ه م د and خ م د — Extinction and subsiding flames

The roots resemble one another in sound and meaning but remain independent.

Classical Arabic sometimes distinguishes them.

خَمَدَتِ النَّارُ can mean that flames subsided while embers remained.

هَمَدَتِ النَّارُ, in one precise usage, means complete extinction and loss of heat.

Lexical accounts attribute this distinction to al-Aṣmaʿī as well.

It is not an absolute rule: usages overlap, and both can concern the end of life or activity.

خ م د describes destroyed communities in al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15 and Yā Sīn 36:29.

ه م د describes dry, ungrowing land in al-Ḥajj 22:5.

The Qur’anic uses differ, but khumūd is not invariably temporary and humūd invariably irreversible. Hāmidah land grows again after rain.

ه م د and خ ش ع — Inactive and subdued land

Fuṣṣilat 41:39 closely resembles al-Ḥajj 22:5.

Al-Ḥajj uses هَامِدَةً; Fuṣṣilat uses خَاشِعَةً.

Both follow water’s arrival with اهْتَزَّتْ and رَبَتْ.

The parallel construction invites comparison.

Hāmidah emphasizes dryness, inactivity, and absent visible growth.

Khāshiʿah belongs to خ ش ع, associated with lowering, humility, and a subdued condition.

Both can describe land before rain from different perspectives.

Hāmidah stresses inactive growth; khāshiʿah emphasizes its lowered or subdued appearance.

The latter does not by itself attribute human worshipful awareness or will to the land.

Sharing a natural scene does not make the words perfect synonyms.

ه م د and م و ت — Inactive land and death

Mawt describes the end of living creatures’ lives and also occurs in expressions about land.

Al-Rūm 30:50 speaks of reviving land after its death; Fāṭir 35:9 describes reviving dead land through water.

These natural images resemble al-Ḥajj 22:5.

Mawt and humūd nevertheless belong to different roots.

Mawt principally concerns death; humūd also applies to extinguished fire, silenced voices, worn cloth, and dry land.

Both can describe land lacking visible plant life in growth contexts.

Mawt’s range does not justify translating humūd as death everywhere.

“Dry and without vegetation” or “with growth inactive” is clearer for hāmidah land; its connection with revival belongs in contextual explanation.

ه م د and ي ب س — Dryness and inactive growth

ي ب س concerns drying and lack of moisture.

A plant can dry while retaining its previous shape.

Hāmidah land additionally presents visible growth as inactive.

This distinction is not absolute: hāmid can describe dry, deteriorated plants, overlapping with yābis.

Still, dryness and inactive growth are not identical. Dry land can retain scattered plants; al-Ḥajj 22:5 depicts land whose growth changes with rain.

ه م د and ه ش م — Inactive growth and fragmented plants

ه ش م appears in hashīm, dry and fragmented vegetation.

It can naturally accompany hāmidah land, where broken plant remains may survive prolonged dryness.

Hashīm chiefly describes the plant remains themselves; hāmidah in al-Ḥajj 22:5 describes the land.

Hāmidah land need not be covered with fragments: its plants may be gone or leave only a few traces.

Fragmentation is not a necessary part of hāmidah.

The distinction supports separate contextual translations.

6. Interpretive challenges and suggested translations

The challenge is to convey inactive visible growth without importing unsupported meanings.

“Still” alone is insufficient: green land can also look motionless from a distance.

“Dry” is closer but may leave the cessation of visible growth unclear.

“Dead” connects with other revival passages but may misleadingly suggest that all life and growth potential are absent.

“Infertile” can wrongly imply an inherent, permanent inability to grow, whereas the verse describes change after water.

For al-Ḥajj 22:5, the suggested passage is:

“You see the land dry and without vegetation; when We send water down upon it, it stirs, swells, and produces every delightful kind of plant.”

This preserves hāmidah’s land-related meaning and the distinct contributions of the following verbs.

A fuller lexical explanation can read:

“Hāmidah describes land whose visible growth and greenery have subsided through dryness.”

Other usages require equivalents appropriate to their objects.

هَمَدَتِ النَّارُ means “the fire went out”; هَمَدَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ, “the voices fell silent”; نَبَاتٌ هَامِدٌ, “dry, deteriorated vegetation”; ثَوْبٌ هَامِدٌ, “old, decayed cloth.”

One lexical family does not require one fixed translation.

7. Conclusion

ه م د concerns extinction, subsiding activity, lost heat, and deterioration.

The line attributed to Zuhayr illustrates hāmid applied to cold, extinguished ash in early poetry.

Lexicons record extinguished fire, quieted voices, dried plants, worn cloth, and human death. These concern lost or subsiding former activity, without collapsing into one physical or biological meaning.

The sole Qur’anic occurrence is هَامِدَةً in al-Ḥajj 22:5: dry land without visible growth becomes stirred, swollen, and productive after water arrives.

Forqan definition: The principal branch of ه م د concerns extinction or the subsiding of an earlier active condition. Qur’anic هَامِدَةً describes dry land without visible vegetation, not absolute destruction of all life or permanent inability to grow.

Its distinctions from خ م د, خ ش ع, م و ت, and ه ش م remain important despite overlapping notions of silence, stillness, death, or deterioration.

In al-Ḥajj 22:5, hāmidah land contributes to the argument for resurrection. Its renewed growth recalls creative power and raising after death without restricting the next world’s physical nature to plant growth.
