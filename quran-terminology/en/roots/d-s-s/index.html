Inserting beneath something and concealing: from burial in earth to obscuring the soul

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic D S S concerns putting something beneath or among other things so it remains concealed. Dassa al-shayʾa describes inserting and hiding an object under earth, clothing, another covering, or among other objects.

Two features stand out: insertion beneath or within a covering, and remaining hidden from sight or attention.

Extended uses include entering a group unnoticed, sending someone to obtain information without disclosing the mission, and concealing an intention or a behavior’s reality.

These do not necessarily have the same moral value. Concealment may protect something, while elsewhere it may facilitate deception or wrongdoing.

The root itself therefore does not mean lying, hypocrisy, crime, or deception. Those follow from the object, intention, and context.

Its unambiguous Qur’anic form is yadussuhu in Al-Naḥl 16:59. Concealing a child in earth appears within criticism of people who resented a daughter’s birth.

Al-Shams 91:10 has dassāhā, connected with this family by many early lexicographers. Its object is no longer bodily burial but obscuring or corrupting the soul, opposed to purification.

This relationship matters, while disagreement about dassāhā’s morphological root must remain visible.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivation

Yadussuhu: the securely assigned occurrence

The Quranic Arabic Corpus indexes D S S once, in Al-Naḥl 16:59:

Yadussuhu: a Form I present verb from dassa–yadussu, with the object pronoun -hu.

Dassa is a doubled root: its second and third consonants are both sīn, normally expressed through gemination.

Yadussuhu describes concealing something beneath or within something else.

Fī al-turābi specifies the location in Al-Naḥl 16:59.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: yadussuhu at Al-Naḥl 16:59

Dassāhā: a lexical connection with disputed root analysis

Al-Shams 91:10 has:

Dassāhā: obscured it or reduced it to abasement and corruption, depending on lexical analysis and context.

Many early dictionaries connect this with D S S.

On that analysis, the underlying form is dassasahā: one sīn becomes yāʾ to avoid repetition, and that yāʾ becomes alif in this construction. Distinguish this derivation from separate indexing under D S W.

Other sources separately record dasā and dasā with final alif maqṣūrah for becoming low or opposing zakā.

The Corpus indexes dassāhā under D S W rather than D S S.

Both cannot therefore be counted without qualification as secure occurrences of one root across all morphological systems.

The difference does not prevent lexical comparison: reputable lexicographers explicitly connect dassāhā with dassa and concealment.

Here yadussuhu is the secure D S S occurrence, while dassāhā is a related, disputed form. A separate D S W study can examine its derivation and evidence further.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: dassāhā indexed under D S W

Recorded Arabic forms

Other recorded forms include:

Dassa: concealed something beneath or within something else.

Dass: the verbal noun for inserting and concealing.

Dassasa: concealed or inserted something, within this doubled-root family.

Indassa: entered secretly or placed oneself beneath something.

Tadassasa: entered or advanced quietly and unnoticed.

Dasīs and dasīsah: something or someone introduced secretly; sometimes an agent gathering information or carrying out a covert task.

Dassās: something penetrating unseen; also the name of certain burrowing animals.

Madsūs: something inserted or concealed somewhere.

Tadsīs: insertion, concealment, or introducing something covertly.

These are Arabic forms, but only yadussuhu appears as an independent securely indexed D S S form in the Qur’an.

Similar-looking forms of other roots should not automatically enter this family.

3. The semantic core in early Arabic

Inserting something beneath a covering

Ibn Fāris describes entry into concealment in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى دُخُولِ الشَّيْءِ تَحْتَ خَفَاءٍ وَسِرٍّ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā dukhūli al-shayʾi taḥta khafāʾin wa-sirrin

“One principle indicating something entering into concealment and secrecy.”

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

الدَّسُّ إِدْخَالُ الشَّيْءِ مِنْ تَحْتِهِ

al-dassu idkhālu al-shayʾi min taḥtihi

“Dass is inserting something from beneath.”

Insertion matters alongside the resulting hidden condition.

A stone already underground is not necessarily something someone has performed dass upon merely because it is hidden.

If someone puts it beneath earth and covers it, dassa can describe the action.

Earth or another material may provide the covering. Earth is not inherent in every use; the sentence specifies the location.

Fī al-turābi supplies it in Al-Naḥl 16:59.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: entering concealment

Concealing through pressure or force

Some dictionaries explain dassa through forceful insertion.

Someone might press an object among other materials to hide it.

Not every dass must therefore be violently forceful.

Nor should the root be restricted to slow, gentle motion.

An object may be pressed into concealment, while a person may quietly enter a group unseen.

Entry into concealment or unnoticed presence is shared; force and manner depend on context.

Entering a group unnoticed

Indassa fulānun can describe someone entering a place or group secretly.

No burial of a physical object is involved.

The entrant wishes to conceal presence, identity, or intention.

Dasīs can therefore name someone moving covertly among people to obtain or convey information.

Lisān al-ʿArab sometimes uses it for someone resembling a spy or informer.

Secret movement nevertheless differs from an intelligence mission.

Someone entering secretly need not be a spy. The mission and conduct determine that, beyond the general root.

Modern Persian dasīseh, usually a harmful secret plot, represents only an extension of this family.

That later sense of conspiracy should not enter Qur’anic yadussuhu.

Lisān al-ʿArab: dass, dasīs, and covert entry

4. Tangible witnesses and historical uses

An animal hiding in sand

Dictionaries call a burrowing reptile al-dassāsah.

It hides its body in sand and disappears from sight.

The animal enters a natural covering and becomes concealed, closely matching the semantic core.

Its name does not prove that reptile movement historically generated every root meaning.

Dassās and dassāsah also have varied animal descriptions in early sources.

They should not be equated with one modern biological species without precise evidence.

Applying a preparation to part of a camel’s body

Arab pastoral life used substances to treat certain camel skin conditions.

Dictionaries record dass for limited application of a treatment to parts of the body.

A small affected area treated locally could be described this way.

This is localized coating rather than ethical secrecy.

A proverb consequently describes doing only a little where fuller attention is needed.

Limited coverage and amount are thus present in some uses.

Small quantity is not the root’s universal meaning; it belongs to this particular treatment.

A witness from Dhū al-Rummah

Dhū al-Rummah says:

تَبَيَّنَ بَرَّاقَ السَّرَاةِ كَأَنَّهُ
فَنِيقُ هِجَانٍ دُسَّ مِنْهُ الْمَسَاعِرُ

tabayyan barrāqa al-sarāti ka-annahu / fanīqu hijānin dussa minhu al-masāʿiru

“Look at that bright-backed animal, as though a prized breeding camel lightly treated at its armpits and inner thighs.” The first hemistich invites observation, as indicated by its accusative object barrāqa, rather than merely stating that the animal becomes visible.

Dussa describes localized application of a treatment to the animal.

Al-Aṣmaʿī cites this in explaining limited treatment of a camel’s skin disease.

Some transmissions have qarīʿu hijānin instead of fanīqu hijānin; Ibn Barrī prefers the latter for this verse.

The variation does not materially alter D S S, but should remain visible for faithful quotation.

Dhū al-Rummah is an Umayyad poet, so this is classical rather than pre-Islamic evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: camel treatment and the Dhū al-Rummah witness

Concealing intentions and behavior

Dictionaries also record al-dasīs for concealing a stratagem.

They describe people entering groups of the virtuous or learned without genuinely belonging to them.

Identity or actual standing is concealed, beyond hiding an object.

Deception and pretence follow from the behavior.

Someone hiding identity or residence from an oppressor cannot simply be called hypocritical for concealment itself.

Ethical uses require the intention, object, and consequences to be examined.

The limits of pre-Qur’anic evidence

Early dictionaries clearly document insertion and concealment.

For some particular forms, this study did not locate poetry securely datable before the Qur’an.

Dictionary examples and Umayyad poetry should not become certainly pre-Islamic witnesses.

Absence from this examined material does not prove absence from earlier speech; it marks the evidence’s limits.

Reliable lexical definitions and the clear use in Al-Naḥl 16:59 remain the principal supports.

5. Qur’anic usage: Al-Naḥl 16:59

Al-Naḥl 16:59

يَتَوَارَىٰ مِنَ الْقَوْمِ مِنْ سُوءِ مَا بُشِّرَ بِهِ أَيُمْسِكُهُ عَلَىٰ هُونٍ أَمْ يَدُسُّهُ فِي التُّرَابِ أَلَا سَاءَ مَا يَحْكُمُونَ

yatawārā mina al-qawmi min sūʾi mā bushshira bihi a-yumsikuhu ʿalā hūnin am yadussuhu fī al-turābi alā sāʾa mā yaḥkumūna

“He hides from the people because of the ill he sees in the news given him: should he keep it in humiliation or hide it in the earth? How badly they judge!”

Yadussuhu is a Form I present verb.

The object pronoun -hu refers to what was announced in the preceding statement.

Al-Naḥl 16:58 describes his face darkening and his suppressed anger or distress upon news of a daughter.

Al-Naḥl 16:59 therefore concerns his response to a daughter’s birth.

Yatawārā and yadussu: two kinds of hiding

Yatawārā opens the verse.

It belongs to W R Y and here concerns the man hiding himself from people.

Yadussuhu instead concerns concealing something in earth.

The difference matters.

In the first action he hides himself, ashamed or distressed by the news.

In the second, he considers an action upon the child: concealing her in earth.

Hiding oneself and hiding the child are different acts.

They share a social context without being lexically identical.

Why the pronoun -hu?

The verse uses masculine -hu rather than feminine -hā.

This should not turn the child into a son.

The nearby expression is mā bushshira bihi, what he was given news of.

The pronoun can refer to mā, grammatically accompanied by masculine singular agreement.

Context clearly identifies the news as a daughter’s birth.

Pronoun reference follows construction and context, not apparent grammatical gender alone.

Fī al-turābi: concealment in earth

The preposition fī and noun al-turāb specify the location.

Dassa need not always mean burial, but the combined expression clearly describes insertion and concealment in earth.

Burying is therefore natural and defensible here.

The root alone nevertheless does not mean killing.

Al-Takwīr 81:8 and Al-Takwīr 81:9 concern the buried-alive girl and the offense for which she was killed.

Their common thematic setting shows that concealing a daughter in earth could involve killing her.

Lexically distinguish insertion and concealment from the criminal killing that occurred in that setting.

Not every dass means killing or burial alive.

Al-Qurṭubī: pronoun reference and burial in earth

Does the verse judge every family?

It criticizes people who resented a daughter’s birth and showed shame or anger.

It does not establish that all Arabs or all contemporary families buried daughters.

Nor should any parent who once wished for a child of a particular sex be equated with this crime.

The issue is judgment and behavior devaluing a daughter and contemplating her concealment in earth.

The ending explicitly condemns their judgment.

The root’s contribution

Yadussuhu describes concealing.

Fī al-turābi supplies the location.

A-yumsikuhu ʿalā hūnin gives the alternative of keeping the child with humiliation.

Alā sāʾa mā yaḥkumūna states the Qur’an’s moral judgment.

These components should be distinguished.

Dassa should not itself become shame over daughters, devaluing women, or bad judgment.

Those are contextual meanings expressed through other clauses.

6. Dassāhā: obscuring the soul in Al-Shams 91:10

Al-Shams 91:9

قَدْ أَفْلَحَ مَنْ زَكَّاهَا

qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā

“Successful indeed is the one who purifies it.”

Al-Shams 91:10

وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنْ دَسَّاهَا

wa-qad khāba man dassāhā

“And unsuccessful is the one who obscures it.”

These adjacent verses establish a clear contrast.

The first concerns purification of the soul and success.

The second concerns dassāhā and failure.

Al-Shams 91:7 and Al-Shams 91:8 discuss the soul, its formation, and inspiration of its wickedness and mindfulness.

The section concerns the soul’s condition and orientation.

Dassāhā and concealment

Some early lexicographers derive it from dassa.

As something may be hidden beneath earth or another covering, a person may obscure the soul’s clarity, growth, or good capacities.

Concealment extends into behavior and the soul’s condition, without physical soil.

It does not mean the soul is bodily buried or its existence entirely destroyed.

Ethical orientation and outcome are at issue.

Lisān al-ʿArab: dasā and its relation to D S S

What does obscuring the soul mean?

Dictionaries offer several explanations.

One is concealing and obscuring it.

Another is lowering its standing through wrongdoing.

Another concerns corrupting or misleading it.

These outcomes are close but not identical.

Derivation from dassa emphasizes concealment.

Analysis through dasā opposed to zakā emphasizes debasement and corruption.

Both connect contextually with purification versus failure.

A base translation should not merge every explanation into one undifferentiated phrase.

Is the verse only about concealing sins?

Secrecy can be one kind of conduct within the lexical field.

Dassāhā in Al-Shams 91:10 should not be restricted to hiding sins from others.

Someone may conceal wrongdoing, but the verse extends beyond whether an act is public or hidden.

Its contrast with purification concerns removing the soul from growth and purity or obscuring its good capacities.

Neither every hidden sin exhausts its meaning nor every public sin falls outside it.

Conduct, orientation, and effects upon the soul require the wider verses about wickedness and mindfulness.

Nor does it mean hiding the soul or actions from divine knowledge.

Being unseen by people differs from obscuring or corrupting moral condition.

Purification and obscuring

Zakkāhā belongs to Z K W / Z K Y, concerning purity, growth, and flourishing.

Dassāhā is placed opposite it.

The contrast presents purification and growth versus obscuring and corruption.

Purification alone should not define D S S.

If purification concerns growth, not every Arabic dass must mean preventing growth.

Al-Naḥl 16:59 concerns concealment in earth without a direct purification contrast.

Al-Shams helps explain dassāhā without establishing a universal rule for the entire family.

Human agency

Both verses contain man, raising the person or soul whose success or failure is stated. Identifying the agent of purification or obscuring requires analysis of the sentence and pronoun references.

Commentaries record different views of the ultimate agent.

The nearby wickedness and mindfulness, followed by Thamūd’s rebellion, nevertheless highlight human responsibility and orientation.

A direct contextual reading can therefore describe someone purifying or corrupting their own soul.

D S S itself does not mean free choice or moral responsibility. Those follow from agency, object, construction, and related verses.

Al-Ṭabarī: dassāhā and disagreement about agency

Thamūd in the following verses

Al-Shams 91:11, Al-Shams 91:12, Al-Shams 91:13, and Al-Shams 91:14 concern Thamūd and its rebellion.

They reject God’s messenger and transgress against the she-camel and its drinking right.

This follows the soul’s purification and obscuring.

Rebellion and its consequences thus continue the ethical discussion.

Dassāhā itself does not mean rejecting a messenger, hamstringing a camel, or destroying a people.

Those acts belong to Thamūd’s narrative and can illustrate a wrongful orientation’s consequences.

Al-Qurṭubī: derivation and interpretation of dassāhā

7. D S S and neighboring roots

D S S and KH F Y: concealing and being hidden

Khafiya concerns hiddenness and lack of visibility.

Dassa also includes inserting something beneath or among other things.

Something may be hidden without anyone placing it somewhere.

Dassa emphasizes insertion and concealment.

Al-Naḥl 16:59 illustrates this through insertion in earth.

If dassāhā is connected derivationally, concealment can likewise help explain the soul’s condition in Al-Shams 91:10.

That image should not become literal bodily motion.

Study of KH F Y

D S S and K T M: concealing and withholding truth

Qur’anic kitmān concerns concealing, especially truth or knowledge that should be disclosed.

Dass can also describe concealment.

There is nevertheless an important distinction.

Dassa principally places something beneath or inside a covering.

Kitmān may emphasize withholding truth or information.

Both can coincide when someone hides a document to prevent truth becoming known.

Hiding the document and knowingly withholding truth remain distinguishable actions.

Deliberate concealment of truth should not be equated with every kind of personal privacy.

Study of K T M

D S S and W ʾ D: burial and burial alive

W ʾ D concerns the girl buried alive in the Qur’an.

Al-Takwīr 81:8 and Al-Takwīr 81:9 question her and the killing inflicted upon her.

Dass principally describes insertion and concealment.

Al-Naḥl 16:59 connects it contextually with condemned conduct toward a daughter.

Burial there can therefore be studied alongside burial alive.

Not every dass means burying alive.

An object can be hidden in earth without killing or harming a living being.

D S S and GH SH Y: insertion beneath a covering and enveloping

Qur’anic ghashiya concerns covering and enveloping.

Night, for example, can cover something.

Dass emphasizes putting something beneath or among a covering.

With ghashiya a covering may spread over an object from outside.

The resulting concealment is close, but the direction and action need not match.

Not every dass should become “enveloped,” nor every ghashiya insertion into earth.

D S S and D S W: disputed derivation and related meaning

Some sources connect these families.

Some lexicographers treat dassāhā as a modified D S S form.

Another analysis records dasā under a family ending in wāw or yāʾ.

With the soul, debasement or corruption contrasts with purification.

The semantic relationship is clear, while historical and morphological identity requires qualification.

This matters to Forqan’s index, which has distinct D S S and D S W entries.

Both articles can explain the relationship without deleting one or transferring all its occurrences without adequate research and evidence.

Study of D S W

D S S and D L S: concealment and hiding defects

Arabic tadlīs and later legal uses concern hiding a defect or truth to deceive another.

Dass more generally concerns insertion and concealment.

Concealment may serve tadlīs without making the roots identical.

Technical tadlīs in transactions or transmitting reports should not define Qur’anic yadussuhu.

Al-Naḥl 16:59 concerns concealment in earth, rather than fabricating reports or hiding merchandise defects.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Al-Naḥl 16:59: yadussuhu

Suitable Persian equivalents convey:

“Conceal it” or “put it in earth and conceal it.”

With fī al-turābi already present, “hide it in the earth” is concise, natural, and accurate.

“Bury it in earth” also fits.

“Kill it” alone omits the act of hiding in earth and is incomplete.

“Bury alive” belongs in historical explanation alongside related verses; a close translation can preserve the verse’s immediate image.

The current Persian Forqan rendering is therefore suitable:

“Should he keep it in humiliation or hide it in the earth?”

Al-Shams 91:10: dassāhā

Retain the contrast with zakkāhā.

“Obscured it” suits derivation from dassa.

“Corrupted it” conveys some contextual meaning but weakens concealment’s image.

“Debased it” fits some lexical explanations of the purification contrast but can emphasize moral outcome more than linguistic imagery.

The current Persian Forqan translation, rendered in English, reads:

“And unsuccessful is the one who obscures it.”

This follows a reputable lexical analysis without adding specific interpretive instances.

The explanation can clarify obscuring or corrupting the soul in contrast with purifying and nurturing it.

The two interpretations should not become an undifferentiated mixed translation.

Uses outside the Qur’an

Dassa: inserted beneath something and concealed.

Indassa: entered secretly.

Dasīs: a person or thing entering covertly; sometimes a covert information agent.

Dasīsah: secrecy; in later Persian, a secret plot.

Madsūs: concealed or covertly inserted.

Translate according to context, without making every form imply conspiracy, espionage, or hypocrisy.

9. Conclusion

D S S concerns inserting something beneath or among other things while concealing it.

Tangible uses involve earth or another covering.

Extensions describe entering a group unnoticed, hiding identity, covert missions, and other concealment.

Deception, espionage, hypocrisy, and killing are not necessary meanings of the root itself.

The secure Qur’anic D S S occurrence is Al-Naḥl 16:59, concerning concealing a daughter in earth within condemnation of hostility toward her birth.

Fī al-turābi gives the location; the ending gives the moral judgment.

Dassāhā in Al-Shams 91:10 is related to dassa by many lexicographers.

Concealment’s image extends to the soul’s condition, opposed to purification.

The derivation remains disputed: some sources and morphological indexes treat it under D S W or related forms.

Forqan should distinguish the secure yadussuhu assignment from the related but disputed dassāhā.

Concealment should not acquire one fixed moral outcome. Al-Naḥl condemns wrongful judgment and burial; Al-Shams contrasts obscuring with purification and ethical growth.

Proposed final root definition: D S S principally concerns inserting something beneath or within a covering so it remains concealed. Extensions can involve covert entry, hidden identity, or obscuring a person’s actual condition. Al-Naḥl 16:59 directly concerns hiding in earth. In Al-Shams 91:10, the lexical analysis connecting this root understands obscuring or corrupting the soul in opposition to purification, while preserving the disputed morphological derivation. Brief root meaning: Inserting something beneath or among a covering so that it remains concealed.
