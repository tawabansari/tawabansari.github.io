Man, foot and leg, walking on foot, and distinct branches of meaning

The Arabic family R J L contains words that share three root letters yet belong to several distinct branches of meaning. Its best-known members are rajul, “man,” rijl, “foot” or “leg,” and rājil, someone travelling on foot.

This family matters because similar written forms do not always have the same meaning. Rijāl means “men” in many verses, but in some it means “people on foot.” Nor does rajul itself describe courage, justice, wisdom, or moral superiority. When present, such qualities come from accompanying descriptions and the context.

The Qur’an contains 73 occurrences of this root in 66 verses across 32 surahs. All are nominal, involving rajul, rijāl, rijl, and rajil, including duals, plurals, and forms with attached pronouns or prepositions.

Qur’anic forms examined: rajul, rajulan, rajulin, rajulāni, rajulayni, rijāl, rijālan, al-rijāl, rijālikum, rijl, rijlika, rijlayni, arjul, arjulakum, arjulihim, arjulihinna, and rajilika.

Other recorded Arabic derivatives include rājil, rajjālah, rajīl, rajīlah, tarajjul, irtijāl, rujūlah, and tarjīl. They help establish the family’s semantic range, but these exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. Semantic centers and early Arabic usage

Foot and leg: limbs used for movement and standing

Rijl denotes the foot or leg of a human or another creature with legs. It contrasts with yad, “hand,” and appears in descriptions of bodily parts, movement, standing, and walking.

Lexical accounts describe its precise anatomical extent in different ways. Some encompass the lower limb from its upper region down to the foot, while other usages focus on the lower part or the area touching the ground.

Rijl should therefore not be restricted to one extremely precise anatomical boundary in every sentence. Persian naturally uses pā; English may require “foot” or “leg.” When a verse concerns washing, moving, cutting, or striking with the limb, its construction and context help clarify the intended extent.

The word extends beyond humans to animals. Figurative and technical Arabic usages also apply rijl to lower or supporting parts of objects, including parts of a bow.

Such usages show that a bodily term can extend to parts of objects. They do not establish “support,” “steadfastness,” “rule,” or “power” as the basic meaning of rijl in every occurrence.

Walking on foot: movement without riding

Rājil describes someone travelling on foot, in contrast to rākib, someone riding.

The expression tarajjala al-rajulu can mean that a man dismounted or proceeded on foot. Rajīl is also recorded for someone strong and enduring at walking.

This sense has a clear connection with rijl: someone without a mount covers the route on their own feet.

Walking is not confined to one sex. Women, men, children, or anyone travelling on foot can be contrasted with people riding when their manner of travel is described.

Consequently, certain plurals, including rijāl, serve both as plurals of rajul and as plurals of rājil. Context distinguishes these usages.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah includes:

أَنَّى اهْتَدَيْتِ وَكُنْتِ غَيْرَ رَجِيلَةٍ
وَالْقَوْمُ قَدْ قَطَعُوا مِتَانَ السَّجْسَجِ

annā ihtadayti wa-kunti ghayra rajīlatin / wa-al-qawmu qad qaṭaʿū mitāna al-sajsaji

“How did you find the way, though you were not strong at walking, when the company had crossed the hard, level stretches of land?”

Rajīlah is a feminine adjective here describing ability to walk.

The witness matters because an adjective of walking from this family is applied to a woman. Words belonging to the walking branch should therefore not be restricted to maleness.

Some words in this verse have variant transmissions, but rajīlah and its sense of ability to walk are established. In the poem’s setting, the beloved’s apparition visits at night, and the feminine address turns to the beloved herself. This is evidence for the adjective’s linguistic usage, rather than a report of that woman’s actual journey.

Another transmitted lexical witness contains “nisāʾī ilaykum rijālan,” meaning “my women, towards you on foot.” This too shows that rijālan in a description of travel can refer to women and need not mean “men.”

Man: a human and social designation

Another branch is rajul, ordinarily meaning a man in Arabic. Its familiar plural is rijāl.

Rajul generally identifies a male who has passed childhood, although neither its usage nor the age boundary is fixed by a single numerical criterion in every sentence.

Unlike rājil, rajul does not primarily describe being on foot. Someone can be walking or riding, strong or weak, courageous or fearful, upright or unjust, and still be called a rajul.

Lexical explanations connect much of this family with rijl, the bodily limb, and with walking. Rajul, “man,” however, cannot readily be derived from that same center. This distinction prevents a conceptual resemblance from being treated as an established history of derivation.

The distinction matters. Shared letters, or the human ability to stand and walk, do not establish that “man” originally meant “someone standing on his own feet,” “an independent person,” “a support of society,” or “a responsible, powerful individual.”

Historical or cultural connections between these meanings may exist, but they require independent evidence. The established Qur’anic meaning of rajul is “man”; other qualities require separate examination.

Other non-Qur’anic usages

Tarjīl al-shaʿr means combing and arranging hair. Shaʿrun rajilun also describes hair between completely straight and tightly curled.

Irtijāl is used for speaking or composing poetry without preparation.

These usages belong to the family’s lexical history. They should not be forced into a single definition of foot, man, and walking, or all derived from an unproven material image.

2. Rajul and rijāl in the Qur’an: man and men without inherent virtue

Men contrasted with women

One of the clearest uses of rijāl identifies men in contrast to women.

Part of Al-Nisāʾ 4:1 reads:

وَبَثَّ مِنْهُمَا رِجَالًا كَثِيرًا وَنِسَاءً

wa-baththa minhumā rijālan kathīran wa-nisāʾā

“And from those two He spread many men and women.”

Rijālan refers to men and nisāʾan to women. The subject is the propagation of human generations and the formation of the human population.

The contrast concerns human sex here, rather than power, bravery, or management.

Part of Al-Nisāʾ 4:7 reads:

لِلرِّجَالِ نَصِيبٌ مِمَّا تَرَكَ الْوَالِدَانِ وَالْأَقْرَبُونَ وَلِلنِّسَاءِ نَصِيبٌ

li-al-rijāli naṣībun mimmā taraka al-wālidāni wa-al-aqrabūna wa-li-al-nisāʾi naṣīb

“For men there is a share of what parents and close relatives leave behind, and for women there is a share.”

Here too, rijāl and nisāʾ designate two human groups whose entitlement to inheritance is discussed.

The same contrast appears in Al-Nisāʾ 4:32, concerning what men and women acquire:

لِلرِّجَالِ نَصِيبٌ مِمَّا اكْتَسَبُوا وَلِلنِّسَاءِ نَصِيبٌ مِمَّا اكْتَسَبْنَ

li-al-rijāli naṣībun mimmā iktasabū wa-li-al-nisāʾi naṣībun mimmā iktasabna

“For men there is a portion of what they have earned, and for women there is a portion of what they have earned.”

Being male or female does not take the place of action in this verse. Both groups are described in relation to what they have acquired.

Rijāl itself therefore neither creates financial entitlement nor specifies a kind of social responsibility. These come from the verse’s complete statement and provision.

Al-rijālu qawwāmūna ʿalā al-nisāʾ

A familiar occurrence appears in Al-Nisāʾ 4:34:

الرِّجَالُ قَوَّامُونَ عَلَى النِّسَاءِ

al-rijālu qawwāmūna ʿalā al-nisāʾ

“Men are maintainers of women.”

Al-rijāl still means “men.” Qawwāmūn belongs to a different root, Q W M, and requires separate study.

Study of Q W M

The meaning of qawwām, responsibility, management, protection, or any other legal interpretation cannot be inserted into the definition of rajul itself.

Understanding the responsibilities and limits of the relationship described requires examining the entire statement, its subsequent reasons, and the related verses on the rights and duties of women and men. Studying R J L alone does not settle this verse’s complete provision.

Nor does the verse establish that rajul means “guardian,” “commander,” or “person in authority” throughout the Qur’an.

Manhood and maturity

In the conversation between the garden owner and his companion, part of Al-Kahf 18:37 reads:

ثُمَّ مِنْ نُطْفَةٍ ثُمَّ سَوَّاكَ رَجُلًا

thumma min nuṭfatin thumma sawwāka rajulā

“Then from a drop, then He formed you into a man.”

Rajulan is connected here with human formation and development. The verse mentions the individual’s beginnings and then describes him as a man.

This shows that rajul can contrast with earlier stages of development.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:40 also states:

مَا كَانَ مُحَمَّدٌ أَبَا أَحَدٍ مِنْ رِجَالِكُمْ

mā kāna muḥammadun abā aḥadin min rijālikum

“Muḥammad has not been the father of any of your men.”

Rijālikum refers to men known within the addressed community. Identifying the historical individuals and the relationship to family provisions requires study of the verse’s context; it is not part of rajul’s lexical definition.

These examples accord with the ordinary use of rajul for an adult man, but do not independently establish a numerical age or a complete legal definition of maturity.

A man identified without a moral judgment

The Qur’an uses rajul to introduce different people.

In the account of Moses, part of Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:20 reads:

وَجَاءَ رَجُلٌ مِنْ أَقْصَى الْمَدِينَةِ يَسْعَىٰ

wa-jāʾa rajulun min aqṣā al-madīnati yasʿā

“And a man came hurrying from the farthest part of the city.”

In the account of the messengers, part of Yā Sīn 36:20 reads:

وَجَاءَ مِنْ أَقْصَى الْمَدِينَةِ رَجُلٌ يَسْعَىٰ

wa-jāʾa min aqṣā al-madīnati rajulun yasʿā

“And from the farthest part of the city a man came hurrying.”

In both, rajul identifies the person as male. His haste, goodwill, warning, or invitation is conveyed by the verbs and the words that follow.

Ghāfir 40:28 states:

وَقَالَ رَجُلٌ مُؤْمِنٌ مِنْ آلِ فِرْعَوْنَ يَكْتُمُ إِيمَانَهُ

wa-qāla rajulun muʾminun min āli firʿawna yaktumu īmānahu

“And a believing man from Pharaoh’s household, who concealed his faith, said.”

The person’s belief comes from the adjective muʾmin, rather than rajul.

By contrast, part of Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15 mentions two men fighting:

فَوَجَدَ فِيهَا رَجُلَيْنِ يَقْتَتِلَانِ

fa-wajada fīhā rajulayni yaqtatilān

“Then he found two men there fighting one another.”

Their being men does not guarantee that their conduct is right. Their fighting is conveyed by yaqtatilān.

The account of Lot also mentions al-rijāl in a context of condemned conduct: Al-Aʿrāf 7:81, Al-Naml 27:55, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29.

Moral virtue therefore cannot be made a constant component of rajul’s meaning.

Rijāl and worthy conduct

Part of Al-Nūr 24:37 introduces men with commendatory descriptions:

رِجَالٌ لَا تُلْهِيهِمْ تِجَارَةٌ وَلَا بَيْعٌ عَنْ ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ

rijālun lā tulhīhim tijāratun wa-lā bayʿun ʿan dhikri allāh

“Men whom neither trade nor sale distracts from remembering The God.”

Part of Al-Aḥzāb 33:23 also states:

مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ رِجَالٌ صَدَقُوا مَا عَاهَدُوا اللَّهَ عَلَيْهِ

mina al-muʾminīna rijālun ṣadaqū mā ʿāhadū allāha ʿalayhi

“Among the believers are men who have been true to the covenant they made with The God.”

In the first verse, their merit lies in not being distracted from remembering The God; in the second, in their fidelity to their covenant.

In neither does rijāl itself mean “those faithful to their covenant,” “the courageous,” or “perfect human beings.”

Nor do these verses make remembrance, loyalty, or truthfulness exclusive to men. Al-Aḥzāb 33:35 places men and women alongside each other in belief, truthfulness, patience, obedience, and remembrance of The God.

Praising a group of men should therefore not become an exclusive definition of virtue based on sex.

Rijāl in relation to the jinn

Al-Jinn 72:6 states:

وَأَنَّهُ كَانَ رِجَالٌ مِنَ الْإِنْسِ يَعُوذُونَ بِرِجَالٍ مِنَ الْجِنِّ فَزَادُوهُمْ رَهَقًا

wa-annahu kāna rijālun mina al-insi yaʿūdhūna bi-rijālin mina al-jinni fa-zādūhum rahaqā

“And there were men among humankind who sought refuge with men among the jinn, and they increased them in burden.”

This usage is particularly important for establishing the word’s range.

In ordinary usage, rajul generally means a human man. This verse, however, also uses rijālin mina al-jinn, “men among the jinn.”

Consequently, rijāl cannot be described as exclusively naming human men in every Qur’anic occurrence.

The verse shows that an appropriate qualification allows the word to apply to a group of jinn. This usage alone, however, does not establish that their sex and bodily structure are exactly like those of humans.

The expression clearly identifies persons or beings designated rijāl in two different groups. The nature of the jinn’s existence does not follow from this root itself.

3. Rijl and arjul in the Qur’an: the bodily limb

Another Qur’anic branch is rijl, “foot” or “leg,” and its plural arjul.

The limb used for walking

Part of Al-Nūr 24:45 reads:

وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَمْشِي عَلَىٰ رِجْلَيْنِ

wa-minhum man yamshī ʿalā rijlayn

“And among them are those that walk on two legs.”

Rijlayn is the dual of rijl and denotes two legs.

The context concerns living creatures and their various modes of movement. The same verse also mentions movement on the belly and on four limbs.

This is clear evidence that rijl denotes a bodily limb and need not refer specifically to a human or a man.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:195 also asks:

أَلَهُمْ أَرْجُلٌ يَمْشُونَ بِهَا

a-lahum arjulun yamshūna bihā

“Do they have feet with which they walk?”

The ability to walk on feet is part of an argument against powerless objects of worship.

Arjul here is the plural of rijl, rather than rajul.

Moreover, the ability to move comes from yamshūn, “they walk.” Rijl names the limb, rather than the act of walking.

Feet in bodily washing and related provisions

Al-Māʾidah 5:6 contains:

وَأَرْجُلَكُمْ إِلَى الْكَعْبَيْنِ

wa-arjulakum ilā al-kaʿbayn

“And your feet up to the two ankles.”

Arjulakum refers to the addressees’ feet, while ilā al-kaʿbayn specifies an extent.

There are established discussions concerning this phrase’s syntactic connection with the preceding commands and its different case readings. These differences do not change arjul’s basic meaning.

The word still denotes feet. Determining the action required in the complete verse needs examination of syntax, recitation, and context, rather than changing the root’s meaning to “washing” or “wiping.”

Moving or striking with the foot

Part of Ṣād 38:42 reads:

ارْكُضْ بِرِجْلِكَ

urkuḍ bi-rijlika

“Strike with your foot.”

Rijlika refers to the addressee’s foot. The verb urkuḍ expresses the requested action.

The limb’s meaning must be distinguished from the verb’s. Rijl does not itself mean striking, running, or bringing forth water; these details come from the verb and the continuation of the verse.

Al-Nūr 24:31 states:

وَلَا يَضْرِبْنَ بِأَرْجُلِهِنَّ لِيُعْلَمَ مَا يُخْفِينَ مِنْ زِينَتِهِنَّ

wa-lā yaḍribna bi-arjulihinna li-yuʿlama mā yukhfīna min zīnatihinna

“And let them not stamp their feet so that what they conceal of their adornment becomes known.”

The striking or stamping comes from yaḍribna, and its purpose is expressed by li-yuʿlama, “so that it becomes known.”

Lexical and historical explanations connect this conduct with moving the feet to make ornaments such as anklets audible. This fits the verse’s construction, but arjulihinna itself refers only to the women’s feet.

Neither adornment nor attracting attention should therefore be inserted into rijl’s definition.

Feet in threats and punishment

Several verses place arjul alongside aydī, “hands.”

For example, part of Al-Aʿrāf 7:124 reads:

لَأُقَطِّعَنَّ أَيْدِيَكُمْ وَأَرْجُلَكُمْ مِنْ خِلَافٍ

la-uqaṭṭiʿanna aydiyakum wa-arjulakum min khilāf

“I will certainly cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides.”

The same construction appears in Pharaoh’s threats in Ṭā Hā 20:71 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:49. Al-Māʾidah 5:33 also contains aydīhim wa-arjuluhum min khilāf.

Arjul refers to the bodily limb in these verses, with the same basic lexical meaning as other uses of feet.

The threat, violence, or legal provision comes from the verbs and the verses’ contexts, rather than rijl itself.

Feet testifying about bodily actions

Part of Yā Sīn 36:65 reads:

وَتَشْهَدُ أَرْجُلُهُمْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ

wa-tashhadu arjuluhum bi-mā kānū yaksibūn

“And their feet testify to what they used to earn.”

Al-Nūr 24:24 likewise mentions tongues, hands, and feet testifying concerning human actions.

These verses describe bodily members’ role in judgment. They do not establish “recorder of deeds” or “moral witness” as rijl’s lexical meaning.

How this testimony occurs is a question concerning the Qur’anic description of the Hereafter; the root R J L alone does not explain it.

Spatial and idiomatic uses of feet

Al-Māʾidah 5:66 states:

لَأَكَلُوا مِنْ فَوْقِهِمْ وَمِنْ تَحْتِ أَرْجُلِهِمْ

la-akalū min fawqihim wa-min taḥti arjulihim

“They would certainly have eaten from above them and from beneath their feet.”

“Beneath their feet” denotes a spatial direction, connected in context with abundant enjoyment and provision.

Al-Anʿām 6:65 mentions a punishment that may come min taḥti arjulikum, “from beneath your feet.”

Both verses use feet in a spatial construction, but the outcomes differ: provision and enjoyment in one, danger and punishment in the other.

This shows that neither abundant provision nor punishment belongs to rijl’s meaning.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12 also uses bayna aydīhinna wa-arjulihinna in connection with a fabricated accusation. Arjul still basically means “feet,” while understanding the complete expression requires examination of the sentence and its linguistic usages. A possible interpretive application should not replace the literal meaning of the bodily limb.

4. Rijālan: people on foot

One of this root’s most important Qur’anic distinctions is the use of rijālan to mean “on foot.”

On foot or mounted in a state of fear

Al-Baqarah 2:239 states:

فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ فَرِجَالًا أَوْ رُكْبَانًا

fa-in khiftum fa-rijālan aw rukbānā

“Then if you are afraid, on foot or mounted.”

Rijālan stands in contrast to rukbānan.

Rukbān refers to riders, so rijālan here denotes those who are on foot.

The meaning is not “if you are afraid, then men or riders.” That rendering destroys the contrast and turns a distinction in manner of travel into one of sex.

This matters to understanding the verse: those addressed by the instruction concerning fear are described in two conditions, on foot and mounted, rather than divided into men and some other group.

Consequently, rijālan here should not be confined to men merely because its form resembles the plural of rajul.

Coming on foot for pilgrimage

Part of Al-Ḥajj 22:27 reads:

وَأَذِّنْ فِي النَّاسِ بِالْحَجِّ يَأْتُوكَ رِجَالًا وَعَلَىٰ كُلِّ ضَامِرٍ

wa-adhdhin fī al-nāsi bi-al-ḥajji yaʾtūka rijālan wa-ʿalā kulli ḍāmir

“And proclaim the pilgrimage among the people; they will come to you on foot and on every lean mount.”

Rijālan likewise means people on foot here.

The clear contextual indicator is wa-ʿalā kulli ḍāmir, which refers to coming on a mount.

The verse puts two ways of arriving alongside each other: coming on one’s own feet and coming on a mount.

Rendering rijālan as “men” here may mislead the reader into thinking the contrast concerns men and women, while the subject is the pilgrims’ manner of arrival.

The opening expression is also fī al-nās: an invitation among the people.

This usage therefore provides no lexical basis for restricting pilgrimage to men.

Rajilika contrasted with khaylika

Part of Al-Isrāʾ 17:64 reads:

وَأَجْلِبْ عَلَيْهِمْ بِخَيْلِكَ وَرَجِلِكَ

wa-ajlib ʿalayhim bi-khaylika wa-rajilika

“And mobilize and charge against them with your riders and foot soldiers.”

Khayl concerns horses and mounted forces; rajil, in contrast, refers to forces on foot.

Rajilika should not be confused with rijlika, “your foot.” Different vowels and word structures produce an important difference in meaning.

The context concerns foot soldiers contrasted with mounted forces, rather than a bodily limb.

The verse concerns Satan and his efforts to mislead and mobilize his forces. How far its mounted and walking imagery is figurative or has external referents is a question of contextual interpretation.

Rajil lexically means people or forces on foot here. The word alone does not establish an army with a particular physical organization or unseen military details.

5. Distinguishing R J L from nearby words

R J L (rajul) and DH K R (dhakar)

Dhakar identifies the male sex in the Qur’an and can apply to humans or animals.

Al-Anʿām 6:143 uses al-dhakarayn and al-unthayayn in discussing livestock.

Rajul, however, ordinarily designates a human man, generally in connection with his stage of development and social setting.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 uses min dhakarin wa-unthā. The phrase concerns sex, rather than identifying the groups of adult men and women within a society.

Dhakar and rajul are therefore not interchangeable in every usage.

Conversely, rijālin mina al-jinn in Al-Jinn 72:6 shows that rijāl cannot always be limited to human men without regard to its qualification and context.

Study of DH K R

R J L (rajul) and M R ʾ (imraʾah)

In ordinary Arabic, rajul and imraʾah are familiar designations for a human man and woman.

Some verses place them directly alongside each other. Al-Baqarah 2:282, for example, states:

فَإِنْ لَمْ يَكُونَا رَجُلَيْنِ فَرَجُلٌ وَامْرَأَتَانِ

fa-in lam yakūnā rajulayni fa-rajulun wa-imraʾatān

“Then if they are not two men, one man and two women.”

Rajulayni and rajulun concern a man or men, while imraʾatāni is the dual form for women.

The numerical relationship is part of that verse’s provision. Neither a number nor qualification to testify is rajul’s lexical meaning.

Nor does the contrast establish that a man basically means “a complete, responsible individual” and a woman “a dependent individual.” These meanings do not reside in the words themselves.

Study of M R ʾ

R J L (rijl) and Q D M (qadam)

Rijl and qadam overlap in the field of the lower limb, but their ranges of usage are not identical.

Qadam can denote the foot, the lower part of the limb, and in some constructions precedence, prior service, or an earlier standing.

Part of Al-Raḥmān 55:41 reads:

فَيُؤْخَذُ بِالنَّوَاصِي وَالْأَقْدَامِ

fa-yuʾkhadhu bi-al-nawāṣī wa-al-aqdām

“Then they are seized by the forelocks and the feet.”

Aqdām means feet here.

By contrast, rijl in expressions such as ʿalā rijlayn in Al-Nūr 24:45 emphasizes the legs on which a creature moves.

This distinction should not be made absolute. The terms can overlap in ordinary Arabic, sometimes both referring to what Persian calls pā.

Study of Q D M

R J L (rijl) and Y D Y (yad)

The Qur’an repeatedly places arjul and aydī alongside each other.

This contrast occurs in bodily descriptions, threats to cut off limbs, and the testimony of bodily members.

Yad denotes the hand and rijl the foot or leg. Their functions should not become an absolute theory in which the hand always symbolizes choice, and the foot always obedience, dependence, or social movement.

A figurative usage may occur in a particular context, but requires support from the sentence and independent evidence.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

A single fixed equivalent does not suit every form in this family.

Rajul: a man; ordinarily a human male who has passed childhood.

Rajulāni / rajulayni: two men.

Rijāl: men, when it is the plural of rajul.

Rijl: foot or leg, a bodily limb.

Rijlayni: two feet or legs.

Arjul: feet or legs.

Rājil: someone on foot, travelling on their own feet.

Rijālan: on foot, people on foot, in constructions contrasting them with riders.

Rajilika: your foot soldiers, in Al-Isrāʾ 17:64.

Rajīl: strong and enduring at walking. The form is recorded in Arabic but does not occur in the Qur’an.

Tarajjala: dismounted or proceeded on foot. This form likewise does not occur in the Qur’an.

Specific usages should be translated in accordance with their context:

  • وَبَثَّ مِنْهُمَا رِجَالًا كَثِيرًا وَنِسَاءً (Al-Nisāʾ 4:1): “And from those two He spread many men and women.”
  • فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ فَرِجَالًا أَوْ رُكْبَانًا (Al-Baqarah 2:239): “Then if you are afraid, on foot or mounted.”
  • يَأْتُوكَ رِجَالًا (Al-Ḥajj 22:27): “They will come to you on foot.”
  • وَأَجْلِبْ عَلَيْهِمْ بِخَيْلِكَ وَرَجِلِكَ (Al-Isrāʾ 17:64): “And mobilize and charge against them with your riders and foot soldiers.”
  • وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَمْشِي عَلَىٰ رِجْلَيْنِ (Al-Nūr 24:45): “And among them are those that walk on two legs.”
  • ارْكُضْ بِرِجْلِكَ (Ṣād 38:42): “Strike with your foot.”

When translating rijāl, particular care is needed to distinguish “men” from “people on foot.”

Nor should “real men,” “steadfast people,” “those with responsibility,” “leaders,” or “supports of society” become basic equivalents of rajul. Such descriptions are appropriate only where the verse or other evidence expresses them.

7. Conclusion

The Qur’anic family R J L has several clear branches of meaning that should be recognized separately.

First, rijl means foot or leg: a bodily limb used by humans and other creatures for walking, movement, and standing. The Qur’an uses it in descriptions of creatures’ movement, bodily washing, striking, threatened amputation, and the testimony of bodily members.

Second, rājil and related forms mean someone on foot, moving on their own feet in contrast to someone riding. Rijālan in Al-Baqarah 2:239 and Al-Ḥajj 22:27 has this sense. Rajilika in Al-Isrāʾ 17:64 likewise refers to foot soldiers contrasted with mounted forces.

Third, rajul means man, with the familiar plural rijāl. The Qur’an uses it to identify men in family, social, legal, and historical settings, and in accounts of the prophets. Applying rijāl to the jinn shows that its referents are not always confined to humans.

Two errors should be avoided in studying this root.

The first is treating every form as equivalent: translating rijālan as “men” in passages contrasting walking and riding, or confusing rajilika in Al-Isrāʾ with rijlika, “your foot.”

The second is adding moral and social values to rajul’s basic meaning. Being a man does not itself mean courage, justice, wisdom, command, independence, or superiority. The Qur’an describes men whose conduct is commendable and others whose conduct is blameworthy. Their worth is understood from their belief, deeds, and qualities.

Final definition: In Qur’anic usage, R J L has three principal branches: rijl, “foot” or “leg”; forms associated with rājil, “on foot” or “people on foot”; and rajul, “man.” The connection between feet and walking is clear. Without sufficient historical evidence, however, manhood should not be derived from walking, standing, or abstract ideas of power and responsibility. Each form’s precise meaning must be established from its structure and the verse’s context.
