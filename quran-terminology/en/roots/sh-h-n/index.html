Filling, loading, and packing: from a laden ship to driving away and enmity in early Arabic

Early Arabic SH Ḥ N has two recognized branches. The first denotes filling a container, place, or vehicle. Shaḥana al-safīnah means loading or filling a ship; mashḥūn describes something filled or laden.

This use emphasizes a place being filled with people, goods, equipment, or other contents, rather than simply placing something there. Loading a ship may prepare it for a voyage, but readiness to sail is not itself established by the word.

The second branch concerns driving away, distancing, and separation. Shaḥana can describe driving people or animals away; shaḥnāʾ names enmity or rancor that separates people.

Both branches are recorded in early dictionaries, but should not be derived from one image without historical evidence. A heart filled with rancor may illustrate shaḥnāʾ, without proving that enmity historically developed from filling.

The Quran uses only al-mashḥūn, three times: Ash-Shuara 26:119, Ya Sin 36:41, and As-Saffat 37:140. Each describes al-fulk, a full or laden ship.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Filling and loading

Shaḥana al-safīnah means filling or loading a ship. Preparing supplies or completing equipment may accompany this, but neither every item of equipment nor readiness for departure follows from the verb alone.

Shaḥn names the act of filling or loading; shiḥnah can name what has been loaded.

A ship may be filled with people, food, equipment, goods, or other supplies. Mashḥūn describes its loaded condition and contents.

Shaḥana al-balad bi-al-khayl is also attested: filling a town or region with horses or mounted troops.

The sense is not restricted to ships: a house, town, or vessel may be filled with people or things.

Filling here means occupying the space with contents, not necessarily pouring a liquid or solid into a container.

Shiḥnah is recorded for troops stationed in a town or region, associated with forces occupying a particular location.

It also names fodder supplied for an animal for a day and night. This relates to provision and contents, but the quantity or duration is not a fixed part of the root's meaning.

The first branch therefore concerns filling a place or vehicle with contents and, for transport, loading it. Readiness to depart may be a practical result, but is not guaranteed by the word.

Historical witnesses and their limits

Arabic poetry includes descriptions of ships and their cargo. A line transmitted in lexical sources reads:

تَأَطَّرْنَ بِالْمِينَاءِ ثُمَّ تَرَكْنَهُ
وَقَدْ لَجَّ مِنْ أَحْمَالِهِنَّ شُحُونُ

taʾaṭṭarna bi-al-mīnāʾi thumma taraknahu / wa-qad lajja min aḥmālihinna shuḥūnu

In this version, shuḥūn is connected with the ships' loads.

The wording varies: other transmissions have shujūn rather than shuḥūn. Attribution to poets after Islam has also been reported.

It cannot therefore serve as certain evidence for this root in pre-Islamic poetry.

The Quranic al-mashḥūn provides a clear witness to the filling or loading sense in the language of revelation. Early dictionaries also record filling a ship, house, or town.

These attestations establish the familiar early meaning without proving that every recorded derivative predates Islam.

Driving away and distancing

Shaḥana al-qawm is recorded for driving a group of people away.

Here the act concerns moving people away from a place, rather than filling something.

Shawāḥin describes hunting dogs that pursue or drive quarry far away without catching it.

A poem by the Umayyad poet al-Tirimmah attests this use. It is evidence for early Arabic usage, but not an independent pre-Islamic witness.

Other uses concern vigorous running or distancing during pursuit.

This branch emphasizes driving away and distance, rather than filling.

Shaḥnāʾ: rancor and enmity

Shaḥnāʾ denotes hostility or rancor between people.

A passage attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Tufayl al-Ghanawi states:

وَأَصْبَحَ لَا يَدْرِي أَيَقْعُدُ فِيكُمُ
عَلَى حَسَكِ الشَّحْنَاءِ أَمْ أَيْنَ يَذْهَبُ

wa-aṣbaḥa lā yadrī a-yaqʿudu fīkumu / ʿalā ḥasaki al-shaḥnāʾi am ayna yadhhabu

“He no longer knows whether to remain among you upon the thorns of enmity, or where to go.”

Ḥasak denotes sharp, painful thorns. The expression portrays living amid hostility as sitting on thorns.

Rancor makes remaining among others painful and difficult.

An early poetry collection attributes the passage to Tufayl, though attribution of some lines is disputed. It is a useful witness for early shaḥnāʾ, but the entire passage should not be assigned definitively to the pre-Islamic period without scrutiny.

Shaḥnāʾ can describe sustained hostility, rather than merely a passing unpleasant feeling. A particular duration, however, is not a necessary part of its definition.

Some lexical explanations associate it with distance, since enmity separates people.

Others explain it through a heart becoming filled with rancor.

Both images can help understanding without proving a definite historical origin.

Results of the early evidence

The family should not be reduced to an invented formula such as “becoming filled enough to drive others away.”

Early Arabic has two distinct attested branches:

First, filling, packing, and loading.

Second, driving away and distancing, with related hostility.

Only the first occurs in the Quran. Al-mashḥūn must therefore be understood through a ship's loaded condition, not through enmity or expulsion.

2. Important forms and derivatives

The following forms are attested in Arabic.

Shaḥana / yashḥanu: to fill or load; in the other branch, to drive away.

Shaḥn: filling or loading; elsewhere, driving away or distancing.

Shiḥnah: a load or supplied contents; also stationed troops or a specified provision of animal fodder.

Shāḥin: one who fills or loads. Markab shāḥin is also recorded for a laden vessel, using an active-participle form in a sense close to a passive description.

Mashḥūn: filled or laden; something supplied with contents or a load.

Ashḥana: recorded for filling or supplying a place with forces or contents. Other uses depend on context, including preparing to weep or being on the verge of tears.

Shaḥnāʾ: rancor, enmity, or hostility.

Mushāḥin: someone hostile toward another.

Mushāḥanah: enmity and hostile contention.

Tashāḥun: mutual hostility among people or groups.

Shawāḥin: a plural of shāḥin in the use concerning dogs that drive quarry far away.

These are Arabic forms, but only mashḥūn occurs in the Quran.

All three Quranic occurrences are al-mashḥūni: definite and genitive because it modifies al-fulki.

Mashḥūn is the passive participle of Form I shaḥana, on the mafʿūl pattern. It describes something subjected to filling or loading.

It therefore means something laden, not someone performing the loading.

3. Quranic uses

Ash-Shuara 26:119: the laden ship in Noah's story

فَأَنْجَيْنَاهُ وَمَنْ مَعَهُ فِي الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ

fa-anjaynāhu wa-man maʿahu fī al-fulki al-mashḥūn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:119

“We saved him and those with him in the laden ship.”

This occurs in Noah's story.

In the preceding passage, Noah asks The God to judge between him and his people and save him and the believers with him.

The Quran then describes their rescue in al-fulk al-mashḥūn.

Al-fulk names the ship; al-mashḥūn describes its filled or loaded condition.

Hud 11:40 provides further information about its contents:

قُلْنَا احْمِلْ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلٍّ زَوْجَيْنِ اثْنَيْنِ وَأَهْلَكَ إِلَّا مَنْ سَبَقَ عَلَيْهِ الْقَوْلُ وَمَنْ آمَنَ

qulnā iḥmil fīhā min kullin zawjayni ithnayni wa-ahlaka illā man sabaqa ʿalayhi al-qawlu wa-man āman

Hūd 11:40

“We said: Carry aboard it two of each kind, a pair, and your household, except those concerning whom the word has already gone forth, and whoever has believed.”

The verse describes carrying companions and other beings aboard.

Mashḥūn fits a vessel carrying more than a single person: people and other beings were aboard.

The word itself specifies neither passenger numbers, vessel size, precise capacity, nor every kind of being carried.

A laden condition does not necessarily mean loading beyond safe capacity.

An “overloaded ship” in modern language may be endangered by excessive weight, but mashḥūn alone does not express that.

Here the ship carries Noah and his companions to safety. Its description as mashḥūn does not by itself establish danger from excessive loading.

It describes contents and loading, rather than every detail of construction or propulsion.

Ya Sin 36:41: the laden ship as a sign

وَآيَةٌ لَهُمْ أَنَّا حَمَلْنَا ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ فِي الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ

wa-āyatun lahum annā ḥamalnā dhurriyyatahum fī al-fulki al-mashḥūn

Yā Sīn 36:41

“A sign for them is that We carried their descendants in the laden ship.”

Al-mashḥūn again modifies al-fulk.

Unlike Ash-Shuara 26:119, this verse itself does not name Noah.

It describes carrying dhurriyyatahum in the laden ship and presents this as a sign.

Interpretations differ over the precise referent of dhurriyyatahum and the relationship to Noah's ship.

One reading connects it to Noah's ship and the preservation of human generations through that event, recalling the continuity of human life through rescue.

Another focuses on carrying people's children and descendants in ships, within the signs of creation and provisions for human life and travel.

Evaluating these readings requires attention to pronouns, dhurriyyah, and adjacent verses. Al-fulk al-mashḥūn alone does not identify one particular historical vessel.

Ya Sin 36:42 continues:

وَخَلَقْنَا لَهُمْ مِنْ مِثْلِهِ مَا يَرْكَبُونَ

wa-khalaqnā lahum min mithlihi mā yarkabūn

Yā Sīn 36:42

“We created for them things like it on which they ride.”

Human transport and the means people ride are central to this passage.

Ya Sin 36:43 mentions possible drowning and the absence of a rescuer. Water travel and deliverance from danger thus belong to the context.

Lexically, mashḥūn still means laden or filled.

The verse does not say that the ship carried only merchandise or was built solely for passengers. The description specifies neither cargo type nor quantity.

Another linguistic distinction appears nearby. Ya Sin 36:40 uses falak for the course of celestial bodies:

وَكُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ

wa-kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn

Yā Sīn 36:40

“Each swims in an orbit.”

In Ya Sin 36:41, fulk means a ship.

Their unvocalized spelling is similar, but pronunciation and use differ. Their proximity and shared imagery of movement may invite reflection, without making al-fulk al-mashḥūn a celestial body or spacecraft.

The direct contextual meaning is a laden ship. Broader figurative readings require independent evidence and must not replace the lexical meaning.

As-Saffat 37:140: the laden ship in Jonah's story

After identifying Jonah as a messenger, the Quran states:

إِذْ أَبَقَ إِلَى الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ

idh abaqa ilā al-fulki al-mashḥūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140

“When he fled to the laden ship.”

This is the root's third and final Quranic occurrence.

Mashḥūn describes the ship toward which Jonah goes.

As-Saffat 37:141 continues:

فَسَاهَمَ فَكَانَ مِنَ الْمُدْحَضِينَ

fa-sāhama fa-kāna mina al-mudḥaḍīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141

“He drew lots and was among those who lost.”

Then As-Saffat 37:142 states:

فَالْتَقَمَهُ الْحُوتُ وَهُوَ مُلِيمٌ

fa-iltaqamahu al-ḥūtu wa-huwa mulīm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142

“The fish swallowed him while he was blameworthy.”

The laden description is compatible with a vessel already carrying other passengers or cargo.

Al-mashḥūn does not itself say that weight endangered the vessel or that lots were drawn to lighten it.

As-Saffat 37:141 reports drawing lots without independently specifying the precise reason.

Some narratives and historical explanations connect the lots with danger of sinking and a decision to lighten the vessel. That is a possible narrative explanation, but not part of mashḥūn's lexical meaning.

“Loaded beyond capacity” would add something to the direct meaning.

Mashḥūn establishes a laden condition. The reason for drawing lots and the vessel's exact circumstances are separate questions.

Comparison with Noah's story clarifies this: both ships are mashḥūn, but one conveys Noah and his companions to safety, while Jonah flees to the other before the drawing of lots.

Neither rescue nor danger is a fixed element of the adjective. It describes loading; context supplies the outcome.

4. Distinctions from related roots

SH Ḥ N and M L ʾ: filling

M L ʾ broadly concerns filling or fullness, applicable to a vessel, space, eye, heart, or gathering of people.

Shaḥn also concerns filling, but in shipping emphasizes loading and supplying a vessel's contents.

A container filled with water naturally illustrates M L ʾ. Loading a ship with people, food, equipment, or goods illustrates shaḥn.

Shaḥn is not exclusive to transport: filling houses or towns is also attested.

M L ʾ offers a broader field of fullness, while transport uses of shaḥn emphasize loads and contents.

SH Ḥ N and Ḥ M L: carrying

Ḥaml concerns lifting, bearing, placing a load upon something, or transporting it.

It does not necessarily express a vehicle becoming full.

Someone bearing a package carries it; that fact alone does not justify calling the person mashḥūn.

Ya Sin 36:41 brings both fields together:

حَمَلْنَا ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ فِي الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ

ḥamalnā dhurriyyatahum fī al-fulki al-mashḥūn

Yā Sīn 36:41

“We carried their descendants in the laden ship.”

Ḥamalnā names the act of carrying; al-mashḥūn describes the ship's loaded condition.

One concerns the action; the other the vehicle's state.

This is why mashḥūn should not simply be translated “carrier.”

SH Ḥ N and TH Q L: weight

TH Q L concerns heaviness and weight.

Something can be heavy while occupying little space, or occupy much space while weighing little.

A small metal load may be heavy, whereas a large volume of lightweight material may fill much space.

Mashḥūn emphasizes being filled or loaded, without a specified weight.

It consequently does not establish that the load exceeded the ship's capacity.

Any claim about weight or strain must come from another word or contextual evidence.

SH Ḥ N and F L K: ship and loaded condition

Fulk in maritime usage names a ship or vessel.

Mashḥūn describes that vessel's loading.

A vessel may be empty, lightly laden, or full of passengers and goods. It remains fulk in each case, while mashḥūn describes its being filled or laden.

Fulk appears without mashḥūn elsewhere, including Al-Baqarah 2:164 and Al-Ankabut 29:65.

Loading is therefore not inherent in fulk. The added adjective expresses a particular characteristic.

SH Ḥ N compared with Ḥ Q D and SH N ʾ

In the second branch, shaḥnāʾ concerns rancor and enmity.

Ḥiqd concerns inwardly retained rancor; shanaʾān concerns hatred and enmity.

Shaḥnāʾ occupies this field too, with lexical explanations emphasizing hostility and distance between people.

Longstanding hostility may cause two people to avoid each other or act and speak antagonistically. Shaḥnāʾ can describe that situation.

Shared semantic territory does not make the words completely synonymous or establish fixed stages from one to another.

The Quran uses other roots for enmity, but does not use shaḥnāʾ in that form.

Enmity must therefore not enter the translation of al-mashḥūn in the three ship passages.

5. Syntax and translation proposals for Forqan

In each Quranic occurrence, al-mashḥūn is a definite passive participle modifying al-fulk.

Its structure is:

  • Root: SH Ḥ N
  • Base verb: shaḥana
  • Passive participle: mashḥūn
  • Quranic form: al-mashḥūni

The final kasrah marks agreement with genitive al-fulki.

Al-fulki al-mashḥūni is a noun with its adjective: a filled or laden ship.

The passive participle does not itself name the loader. In these verses, it specifies neither who loaded the vessel nor its exact cargo or percentage of capacity used.

Morphologically al-mashḥūn differs from al-shāḥin. The latter can denote an agent, whereas mashḥūn describes what receives the action. The attested markab shāḥin mentioned earlier shows that context remains important for lexicalized descriptions.

Suitable equivalents include:

Shaḥana: “filled,” “loaded,” or “packed,” according to its object.

Shaḥn: “filling,” “loading,” or “packing”; in the other branch, “driving away.”

Shiḥnah: “load” or “cargo”; specific historical uses include stationed forces or supplied fodder.

Shāḥin: “loader” or “one who fills,” according to the construction.

Mashḥūn: “filled,” “laden,” or “packed.”

Shaḥnāʾ: “rancor,” “enmity,” or “hostility.”

Mushāḥin: “hostile” or “bearing rancor.”

Mushāḥanah and tashāḥun: “hostility,” “mutual enmity,” or “hostile contention.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140

“When he fled to the laden ship.”

The Persian “full ship” is brief and direct across all three; “laden ship” is a natural English equivalent.

“Loaded ship” is also lexically sound, though it can suggest commercial freight. The contents may instead include passengers, animals, food, and equipment.

“Packed ship” is possible, but may sound less natural or suggest overcrowding.

Forqan's proposed basic Persian equivalent is “full ship,” with English “laden ship.” A lexical note can explain filling with a load or contents. These proposals do not change installed verse translations.

6. Conclusion

SH Ḥ N has two recognized Arabic branches: filling and loading; and driving away or distancing, alongside uses concerning enmity.

In the first, people, goods, equipment, or other contents fill something. In the second, someone or something is driven away; shaḥnāʾ concerns hostility between people.

The branches should not be merged into a single historical image without sufficient evidence. A heart full of rancor is intelligible imagery, but not proof of historical derivation.

Only al-mashḥūn occurs in the Quran, always describing al-fulk.

In Ash-Shuara 26:119, the laden ship saves Noah and his companions. In Ya Sin 36:41, it appears among signs concerning the carrying of human generations. In As-Saffat 37:140, Jonah flees to it before the drawing of lots.

Mashḥūn thus does not inherently mean endangered, commercial, or overloaded beyond capacity. Such details require independent evidence.

The Quranic branch's semantic core is filling or loading a place or vehicle with contents. Mashḥūn accordingly describes something filled or laden.

“Full,” “laden,” and, where appropriate, “packed” are the main equivalents. “Full ship” in Persian and “laden ship” in English preserve the direct sense without inserting historical or interpretive details.
