Root S R Q: taking secretly, theft, and covert listening

Arabic S R Q concerns taking something secretly, without the awareness of its owner or those overseeing it. Its best-known use is theft: taking another’s property without entitlement and concealing the act from its owner or others.

The family is not limited to material possessions. Covert listening and looking can also be expressed through its derivatives. Alongside property theft, the Quran uses istaraqa al-samʿa.

Three matters should be distinguished: the word’s meaning, whether theft actually occurred, and establishing that a particular person committed it. This matters especially in Joseph’s story, where several uses occur in accusations whose utterance alone does not establish the crime.

1. The root in early Arabic

Saraqa primarily concerns taking something secretly and under concealment. The expression akhdhu shayʾin fī khafāʾin wa-sitrin explains this as taking something in secrecy and concealment.

The definition has two elements: taking or acquiring something, and concealing how it is done.

When the object is another’s property and there is no right to take it, the act is theft. Broader uses retain covert taking or receiving while applying it to hearing words or looking.

A familiar distinction separates someone secretly taking property from its place of keeping from someone openly snatching or forcibly appropriating it. This shows the importance of concealment in the meaning of theft.

Lexical meaning should nevertheless be separated from legal conditions. A particular place of safekeeping, financial value, age, or legal capacity may matter in jurisprudence and adjudication, but they are not all part of the basic lexical meaning of saraqa.

Pre-Qur’anic background

Covert taking is clearly established in early lexical records and fits Qur’anic usage. Determining its precise pre-Islamic range, however, requires independent evidence from that period.

This study does not present a securely dated pre-Qur’anic verse or expression capable of establishing every detail of the root’s meaning. Later juristic conditions therefore cannot be presented as the definitive pre-Islamic definition.

There are reports of pre-Islamic poets being accused of taking each other’s poetry. Such reports concern the early question of borrowing and appropriating others’ speech. Without examining their dates and exact wording, however, they cannot establish that the later technical concept of literary theft was already fixed then.

A defensible starting definition is that recorded early Arabic saraqa concerns covert taking; in property contexts, theft is secretly taking another’s assets without right.

Other lexical branches

Related expressions include sāraqa al-naẓara, looking when someone is unaware, and saraqatnī ʿaynī, used when sleep overtakes someone.

Other uses concern concealment, weakness, and hoarseness of voice. These should not become the core of every derivative without independent study.

Saraq also names a type of fabric or pieces of silk. Similarity of form does not prove that this name derives from theft. It has no direct role in explaining Qur’anic stealing and should not be given an invented common image.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

S R Q occurs nine times in seven verses, divided here into four morphological groups:

Saraqa and its verbal forms: the basic triliteral verb, to steal. Saraqa, yasriq, and yasriqna occur in the Quran.

Sāriq: the masculine active participle, a thief or one who steals. Qur’anic forms include al-sāriqu, la-sāriqūna, and sāriqīna.

Sāriqa: the feminine active participle, a female thief. Al-sāriqatu occurs in the Quran.

Istaraqa: a Form VIII verb, covertly receiving or taking something; its Qur’anic use concerns covert listening.

The verbal nouns sariqa and saraq, and the passive participle masrūq, are known in Arabic but do not occur in those forms in the Quran.

Three main settings are identifiable: theft and accusations in Joseph’s story, theft as prohibited conduct and a subject of judgment, and covert listening.

3. Theft and accusations in Joseph’s story

Most occurrences are in Sūrat Yūsuf. The story brings together accusations, the accused persons’ replies, discovery of an object, and assessment of what actually happened.

The caller’s accusation

Yūsuf 12:70 states:

فَلَمَّا جَهَّزَهُمْ بِجَهَازِهِمْ جَعَلَ السِّقَايَةَ فِي رَحْلِ أَخِيهِ ثُمَّ أَذَّنَ مُؤَذِّنٌ أَيَّتُهَا الْعِيرُ إِنَّكُمْ لَسَارِقُونَ

fa-lammā jahhazahum bi-jahāzihim jaʿala al-siqāyata fī raḥli akhīhi thumma adhdhana muʾadhdhinun ayyatuhā al-ʿīru innakum la-sāriqūna

Yūsuf 12:70

“When he had furnished them with their provisions, he placed the cup in his brother’s baggage. Then a caller called: Caravan, you are certainly thieves.”

La-sāriqūna is a plural active participle with an emphatic lām. The caller emphatically accuses the caravan; emphasis alone does not reveal the speaker’s knowledge or intention.

Before that accusation, however, the verse gives the reader important information: Joseph had placed the cup in his brother’s baggage.

Innakum la-sāriqūna is the caller’s statement, not proof that the caravan actually stole the cup.

The distinction is essential. The Quran can report a person’s words without thereby endorsing an accusation’s truth.

The accused persons’ response

Yūsuf 12:73 states:

قَالُوا تَاللَّهِ لَقَدْ عَلِمْتُمْ مَا جِئْنَا لِنُفْسِدَ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَمَا كُنَّا سَارِقِينَ

qālū ta-Allāhi la-qad ʿalimtum mā jiʾnā li-nufsida fī al-arḍi wa-mā kunnā sāriqīna

Yūsuf 12:73

“They said: By The God, you know that we did not come to spread corruption on earth, and we have not been thieves.”

Sāriqīna is the predicate of kunnā; the speakers deny being thieves.

They defend themselves by appealing to their history and purpose in coming, then rejecting the accusation.

Corruption on earth and theft occur together in their speech without being synonyms. Corruption is broader, while theft can produce corruption and violation of rights in a particular setting.

An accusation against Joseph

Yūsuf 12:77 states:

قَالُوا إِنْ يَسْرِقْ فَقَدْ سَرَقَ أَخٌ لَهُ مِنْ قَبْلُ فَأَسَرَّهَا يُوسُفُ فِي نَفْسِهِ وَلَمْ يُبْدِهَا لَهُمْ قَالَ أَنْتُمْ شَرٌّ مَكَانًا وَاللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا تَصِفُونَ

qālū in yasriq fa-qad saraqa akhun lahu min qablu fa-asarrahā Yūsufu fī nafsihi wa-lam yubdihā lahum qāla antum sharrun makānan wa-Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā taṣifūna

Yūsuf 12:77

“They said: If he steals, a brother of his had stolen before. Joseph concealed it within himself and did not disclose it to them. He said: You are worse in position, and The God knows best what you describe.”

Yasriq is an imperfect verb in the conditional in yasriq. The conditional alone does not establish theft; it means “if he steals.”

Saraqa is a perfect verb: the brothers attribute an earlier theft to his other brother.

The first verb is conditional, while the second presents a claim about the past.

The verse then says Joseph concealed the matter within himself and did not reveal it to them, followed by wa-Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā taṣifūna.

It should not be concluded that the Quran confirms Joseph’s earlier theft. The verse reports the brothers’ claim without independently proving its historical truth.

Testimony to what was known or proof of a crime?

In Yūsuf 12:81, the eldest brother tells the others:

ارْجِعُوا إِلَىٰ أَبِيكُمْ فَقُولُوا يَا أَبَانَا إِنَّ ابْنَكَ سَرَقَ وَمَا شَهِدْنَا إِلَّا بِمَا عَلِمْنَا وَمَا كُنَّا لِلْغَيْبِ حَافِظِينَ

irjiʿū ilā abīkum fa-qūlū yā abānā inna ibnaka saraqa wa-mā shahidnā illā bi-mā ʿalimnā wa-mā kunnā li-al-ghaybi ḥāfiẓīna

Yūsuf 12:81

“Return to your father and say: Our father, your son stole. We testified only to what we knew, and we were not guardians of the unseen.”

Saraqa is again a perfect verb. The speakers attribute theft to their brother.

The continuation exposes the limits of their knowledge: wa-mā shahidnā illā bi-mā ʿalimnā.

They speak from what they knew or observed without knowing the whole situation.

From Yūsuf 12:70 and Yūsuf 12:76, the reader knows how the cup was placed in the baggage and then retrieved. Finding it alone does not prove its possessor stole it.

Being accused of theft, possessing an object, and being shown to have stolen it are three different matters.

This does not redefine S R Q. It shows why the word’s meaning must be distinguished from the credibility of a speaker’s claim.

4. Theft as prohibited conduct and a subject of judgment

The male thief and the female thief

Al-Māʾidah 5:38 states:

وَالسَّارِقُ وَالسَّارِقَةُ فَاقْطَعُوا أَيْدِيَهُمَا جَزَاءً بِمَا كَسَبَا نَكَالًا مِنَ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-al-sāriqu wa-al-sāriqatu fa-iqṭaʿū aydiyahumā jazāʾan bi-mā kasabā nakālan mina Allāhi wa-Allāhu ʿazīzun ḥakīmun

Al-Māʾidah 5:38

“The male thief and the female thief—cut off their hands as recompense for what they have earned, an exemplary punishment from The God. The God is mighty, wise.”

Al-sāriqu and al-sāriqatu are definite active participles referring to people who commit theft and are subjects of the ruling. An accusation alone should not be equated with established conduct.

Naming man and woman separately shows that the ruling is not restricted to one sex.

Bi-mā kasabā links recompense to what both have done and earned. The subject is conduct and criminal acquisition, not merely social status or membership of a group.

Sāriq does not lexically specify a fixed value, age, place of safekeeping, or method of proving the crime. These require separate examination of the ruling.

The meaning and scope of fa-iqṭaʿū aydiyahumā also involve Q Ṭ ʿ and Y D Y, together with the sentence and its context. Defining S R Q alone cannot settle the precise action or penalty expressed by cutting the hands.

Examination of that ruling belongs in a separate study of the verse and its relevant roots. This article concentrates on sāriq and its distinction from accusation and other forms of taking property, without presupposing that a separate article has already been published.

The following verse, Al-Māʾidah 5:39, speaks of repentance and reform after wrongdoing. The immediate context therefore does not end with crime and recompense; it includes return and correction.

Theft in the believing women’s pledge

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12 states:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ إِذَا جَاءَكَ الْمُؤْمِنَاتُ يُبَايِعْنَكَ عَلَىٰ أَنْ لَا يُشْرِكْنَ بِاللَّهِ شَيْئًا وَلَا يَسْرِقْنَ وَلَا يَزْنِينَ وَلَا يَقْتُلْنَ أَوْلَادَهُنَّ وَلَا يَأْتِينَ بِبُهْتَانٍ يَفْتَرِينَهُ بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِنَّ وَأَرْجُلِهِنَّ وَلَا يَعْصِينَكَ فِي مَعْرُوفٍ فَبَايِعْهُنَّ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُنَّ اللَّهَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu idhā jāʾaka al-muʾminātu yubāyiʿnaka ʿalā an lā yushrikna bi-Allāhi shayʾan wa-lā yasriqna wa-lā yaznīna wa-lā yaqtulna awlādahunna wa-lā yaʾtīna bi-buhtānin yaftarīnahu bayna aydīhinna wa-arjulihinna wa-lā yaʿṣīnaka fī maʿrūfin fa-bāyiʿhunna wa-istaghfir lahunna Allāha inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12

“Prophet, when believing women come to pledge to you that they will associate nothing with The God, will not steal, commit adultery, kill their children, bring a slander they fabricate between their hands and feet, or disobey you in what is right, accept their pledge and seek The God’s forgiveness for them. The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Yasriqna is a feminine plural imperfect verb, fixed on sukūn because of the feminine plural nūn. Here lā is negative; the verb is coordinated within the construction governed by an and occupies the subjunctive position. The pledge means “that they will not steal,” rather than a separate direct prohibition.

Refraining from theft stands alongside several other prohibited behaviors, each retaining its own meaning. Theft is not identical to adultery, killing a child, or slander.

The verb is feminine plural because believing women are the immediate participants. This does not make theft or its prohibition exclusive to women: Al-Māʾidah 5:38 names both male and female thieves.

Unlike Joseph’s story, this is not one person accusing another. Refraining from theft is an explicit undertaking of the pledge.

5. Istirāq al-samʿ: covert reception without taking property

Al-Ḥijr 15:18 states:

إِلَّا مَنِ اسْتَرَقَ السَّمْعَ فَأَتْبَعَهُ شِهَابٌ مُبِينٌ

illā mani istaraqa al-samʿa fa-atbaʿahu shihābun mubīnun

Al-Ḥijr 15:18

“Except one who listens covertly, whereupon a clear flame pursues him.”

Istaraqa is Form VIII, used with al-samʿa.

The object is covert hearing, not money or a material possession. The expression demonstrates that covert reception extends beyond property to hearing.

Modern Persian still uses istirāq-i samʿ for secretly listening to a conversation not addressed to the listener.

Istirāq here does not mean removing an ear or stealing the faculty of hearing. Al-samʿ concerns hearing and what is covertly heard.

Al-Ḥijr 15:16 and Al-Ḥijr 15:17 concern the sky and its protection. The scene is therefore not ordinary human eavesdropping, although it uses the same linguistic capacity to express covert reception.

Form VIII alone does not establish that istirāq is necessarily more intense, repeated, or effortful than ordinary theft. Its meaning should follow its actual use in istaraqa al-samʿa.

The lexical point is that S R Q is not limited to physically moving property; covert reception also belongs to its range.

6. Distinctions from related words

S R Q and ʾ Kh Dh: taking and stealing

Akhadha broadly means taking. It does not itself establish secrecy, wrongfulness, or criminality.

Someone may take their entitlement, a gift, or something with its owner’s permission. All can be described by akhadha without being theft.

Saraqa, when applied to another’s property, denotes covert and wrongful taking.

Every theft involves taking, but not every taking is theft.

S R Q and Gh Ṣ B: covert taking and forcible appropriation

Arabic ghaṣb concerns wrongful appropriation of another’s property or rights, especially through force and domination.

The familiar distinction emphasizes concealment in theft, while open domination and wrongful appropriation may be central to ghaṣb.

Someone secretly removing another’s property from safekeeping clearly exemplifies theft. Someone openly taking it by force may fall under ghaṣb.

This lexical distinction does not imply less injustice in either act. Ghaṣban does occur in the Quran: Al-Kahf 18:79 describes a king taking every ship by force. Gh Ṣ B is therefore a Qur’anic root distinct from S R Q.

S R Q and Kh W N: theft and breach of trust

Kh W N concerns betrayal and violation of trust.

Someone may receive property in trust and then use it contrary to the owner’s rights. Breach of trust matters because initial access was granted through permission or confidence.

In theft, covertly taking another’s property is prominent.

The categories are not always wholly separate in practice. Someone breaching trust may also secretly take property or part of it. Precise legal classification depends on the circumstances.

Possible overlap between acts should not make khāʾin and sāriq lexically identical.

S R Q and Kh L S: covert taking and sudden snatching

Classical Arabic ikhtilās concerns seizing something in a brief, unexpected opportunity. Someone may quickly snatch it in the owner’s presence or while the owner is inattentive.

Secrecy is prominent in theft, while opportunistic, sudden seizure is more prominent in ikhtilās.

Modern Persian often uses ikhtilās for unlawful appropriation of institutional, public, or entrusted funds. That modern legal usage should not be projected onto every historical Arabic use.

Nor should its modern sense automatically be equated with Qur’anic theft without examining the actual conduct.

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan

“To steal” is the natural base rendering of saraqa in property contexts. Where lexical explanation is needed, “secretly and wrongfully taking another’s property” is suitable.

The important Qur’anic forms may be rendered as follows:

  • Al-sāriqu: the male thief.
  • Al-sāriqatu: the female thief.
  • La-sāriqūna: certainly thieves; as the caller’s accusation in Yūsuf 12:70.
  • Sāriqīna: thieves; in Yūsuf 12:73, “we have not been thieves.”
  • In yasriq: if he steals, retaining the conditional.
  • Fa-qad saraqa: indeed he stole, or had stolen earlier, according to time and context.
  • Inna ibnaka saraqa: your son stole; the brothers’ reported words in Yūsuf 12:81.
  • Istaraqa al-samʿa: listened covertly or eavesdropped.
  • Wa-lā yasriqna: and that they will not steal.

Knowledge that the accusation is unfounded should not turn saraqa into “was accused” in translating Joseph’s story. In the brothers’ speech, it means “stole.” The truth of the claim belongs to narrative analysis, not alteration of the verb’s meaning.

Without contextual grounds, sāriq should not become a professional, habitual, or repeat thief. The active participle alone does not establish a count or history of offenses.

“Listening covertly” is more natural and precise than the awkward literal rendering “stealing hearing.”

Conclusion

The defensible Qur’anic core of S R Q is secretly taking or receiving something. When it concerns another’s property without entitlement, it is theft. In istaraqa al-samʿa, concealment operates in the sphere of hearing.

Lexical definition should not be equated with juristic and judicial conditions. Value, proof, responsibility, and punishment are separate matters requiring examination of the ruling and relevant legal principles.

Joseph’s story shows that characters’ use of theft language is not necessarily Qur’anic confirmation that theft occurred. Accusation, observation, and proof should remain distinct. Finding something in a person’s baggage or possession does not alone establish that they secretly and wrongfully took it.

The Quran also identifies theft as prohibited conduct for which men and women alike are responsible. Recompense, deterrence, repentance, and reform accompany its treatment.

The resulting definition is:

S R Q primarily concerns covert taking: secretly taking another’s property without right in financial contexts, and covertly receiving something such as speech or sound in broader usage.

Its distinguishing feature is concealment in taking or receiving. Moral and legal responsibility, especially attribution of a crime to someone, must rest on actual conduct and valid evidence rather than accusation or appearances alone.
