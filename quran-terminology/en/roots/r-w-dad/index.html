Green meadows, openness and spaciousness, and softening and training

Brief meaning: Open, green land where water and moisture support vegetation; a meadow, grassy expanse, or flourishing garden. A separate branch concerns softening, taming, and preparing a person or animal through practice. In the Qur’an, only the meadow branch occurs.

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The three-consonant Arabic root R W Ḍ has two important semantic branches.

The first concerns openness, spaciousness, and green land. Rawḍah denotes land where water collects or sufficient moisture allows various plants and grasses to grow.

Such land may be a natural meadow green after rainfall. In broader usage, a beautiful, flourishing garden is also called rawḍah.

The second branch concerns softening and preparation. Rāḍa al-dābbah means training and taming an animal to accustom it to movement, commands, or a particular task.

This branch includes riyāḍah, associated in early Arabic with training and taming and used today for sport and physical exercise.

Early lexicons record both branches, but the meaning of one should not be transferred to the other without evidence.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs only in connection with green places.

Al-Rūm 30:15 mentions a rawḍah in which believing people who do righteous deeds are made joyful.

Al-Shūrā 42:22 has rawḍāt al-jannāt: the meadows of the gardens where believing people who do righteous deeds dwell.

The Qur’anic usage concerns a green, beautiful environment rather than sport, physical training, or taming animals.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

The root occurs in the Qur’an twice, in two verses and two nominal forms:

  • رَوْضَةٍ — Al-Rūm 30:15: A meadow or green place; a singular, indefinite noun in the genitive.
  • رَوْضَاتِ — Al-Shūrā 42:22: Meadows or green places; a sound feminine plural in the genitive.

Other derivatives and usages are known in Arabic:

  • رَوْضَة: A meadow, grassy expanse, or flourishing garden.
  • رَوْض: A collective form for meadows, also listed among the plurals of rawḍah.
  • رِيَاض: A plural of rawḍah: meadows.
  • رَوْضَات: Another plural of rawḍah.
  • أَرَاضَ المكانُ: Water collected in a place, or the place became abundant in meadows.
  • أَرْوَضَتِ الأرضُ: The land became clothed in vegetation.
  • اسْتَرَاضَ المكانُ: A place became spacious; in another usage, water collected there.
  • رَوَّضَ الأرضَ: Turned the land into a meadow.
  • رَاضَ الدابَّةَ: Trained and tamed an animal.
  • رِيَاضَة: Training, softening, and preparation for a task.
  • رَائِض: An animal trainer.
  • مَرُوض and مُرَوَّض: Trained or tamed.
  • ارْتَاضَ: Became accustomed to training or became tractable.
  • مُرَاوَضَة: Gentle persuasion, discussion, or an attempt to bring someone around to a matter.

These forms occur in lexical records, but only rawḍah and rawḍāt occur in the Qur’an.

2. Semantic cores in early Arabic

Two principles in an early lexical analysis

An early lexical analysis distinguishes two closely related principles:

أَحَدُهُمَا يَدُلُّ عَلَى اتِّسَاعٍ، وَالْآخَرُ عَلَى تَلْيِينٍ وَتَسْهِيلٍ

aḥaduhumā yadullu ʿalā ittisāʿin wa-al-ākharu ʿalā talyīnin wa-tashīlin

Translation: “One indicates breadth, and the other softening and making easy.”

This distinction matters because rawḍah and riyāḍah share a root but belong to different fields of usage.

The first branch emphasizes a place: open land, the availability of water, and vegetation.

The second concerns a change in a living being or activity: becoming tractable, accustomed, or more capable of movement or behavior through practice.

A figurative connection can be imagined: land becoming suitable for growth and an animal becoming ready for movement both move from a less favorable condition to a more favorable one.

That resemblance alone does not prove that the name for a meadow derives from taming an animal, or that riyāḍah originally meant a meadow.

The two branches should therefore be examined separately.

Rawḍah: land with greenery and growth

An early lexical explanation states:

الرَّوْضَةُ: الْأَرْضُ ذَاتُ الْخُضْرَةِ

al-rawḍatu al-arḍu dhātu al-khuḍrati

Translation: “Rawḍah is land possessing greenery.”

Another explanation describes rawḍah as a beautiful, flourishing garden.

A further definition describes a place where water collects and vegetation grows abundantly.

These definitions have several important features.

First, they concern a place.

Second, greenery and plant growth help characterize it.

Third, water or moisture underlies this greenery in many early usages.

Rawḍah is thus more than an open piece of ground.

Land may be broad and open yet dry and bare. Openness alone does not make it a meadow.

Where diverse plants cover the ground and create a flourishing place, the use of rawḍah is clearer.

Rawḍah and water

Early Arabic usage emphasizes the relationship between meadows and collected water.

Arāḍa al-wādī is used of a valley in which water collects.

Istarāḍa is also used of water spreading across the bottom of a basin or hollow.

Some lexical explanations define rawḍah as a place where water collects and vegetation consequently grows abundantly.

This usage relates to the natural conditions of arid and semi-arid regions.

Rainfall there can produce more growth in depressions or ground that retains water well.

Previously dry land may consequently become green meadowland after rain.

This does not mean that every meadow must retain a visible pool on its surface.

Soil moisture may sustain vegetation even when no standing water is visible.

In the Qur’anic sense of rawḍah, the greenery and flourishing character of the place matter; water is its familiar natural background in early Arabic.

Must a rawḍah always be treeless?

One early lexical explanation excludes tree-covered ground from the designation rawḍah.

In that usage, the word refers primarily to grass and low vegetation.

Another explanation, however, allows the meaning of a beautiful, flourishing garden.

Some natural meadows may also contain scattered trees.

Absolute absence of trees cannot therefore be treated as an exceptionless condition of the word’s meaning.

Greenery, growth, flourishing vegetation, and openness are prominent in familiar usage.

For this reason, “meadow” is an appropriate equivalent.

“Garden” also fits some contexts, but may suggest a deliberately cultivated place with trees and defined boundaries.

Early Arabic rawḍah also often evokes natural green meadowland.

Riyāḍ and rawḍāt: plural forms

Rawḍah has several plural or collective forms in Arabic.

Riyāḍ and rawḍāt are familiar plurals; rawḍ is a collective form also listed among its plurals.

Riyāḍ is a broken plural.

Rawḍāt is a sound feminine plural.

In riyāḍ, the root’s wāw changes to yāʾ in the plural pattern, a morphological change with parallels in Arabic.

Both plurals can refer to several meadows or a collection of green places.

Their different forms do not mean that riyāḍ necessarily denotes large meadows and rawḍāt small ones.

The plural patterns do not establish such a difference in size.

Al-Shūrā 42:22 uses rawḍāt.

This fits the sentence, but does not imply “gardens more special than riyāḍ.”

Arwaḍat al-arḍ: land becoming green

Arabic uses the expression:

أَرْوَضَتِ الْأَرْضُ

arwaḍati al-arḍu

Meaning: “The land became clothed in vegetation.”

Rawwaḍa al-saylu al-arḍa is also used of a flood that makes land into a meadow.

These usages clarify the relationship between water, land, and plant growth.

Rawḍah itself does not mean rain or flood.

Rain may cause growth; land is where growth occurs; the meadow is the green place with abundant vegetation.

These concepts are related but not lexically identical.

Rawḍah should therefore not be translated as “rain,” “abundant water,” or “rain-soaked land” in the Qur’an.

Those may describe associated natural features; the place itself is a meadow.

Istarāḍa: openness and spaciousness

Istarāḍa al-makān means that a place became open and spacious.

The expression “ما دام النَّفَسُ مُسْتَرِيضًا” concerns breathing that remains easy and unconfined; available opportunity is an explanation of its use, not a direct replacement for “breath.”

This shows that the root’s scope extends beyond plants and water to spaciousness and ease.

For example, saying that there is still ample opportunity means that sufficient scope for action remains.

This expression relates to spatial breadth without referring to an actual meadow or garden.

It does not mean that rawḍah in the Qur’an directly signifies opportunity, freedom, or relief in affairs.

In the verses examined, the word names a green place.

3. Training and taming in the second branch

Rāḍa al-dābbah: training an animal

In the second branch, rāḍa al-dābbah refers to training an animal.

Lexical explanations describe preparing it for movement and making its behavior tractable.

A young horse unfamiliar with a rider and commands may need training.

As it gradually becomes accustomed to the required movements and commands, it undergoes riyāḍah.

Training and taming are not always identical in their methods or ethical implications.

Training may use gentle methods or involve harsh treatment.

The root alone does not determine whether a method is right or wrong.

The lexical concern is the animal’s transition from unfamiliarity or resistance toward readiness and responsiveness.

Riyāḍah: practice and preparation

Riyāḍah is a verbal noun associated with training and taming.

Early usage is particularly familiar in connection with animals, especially horses and camels.

The concept can also extend to human practice.

A person may, for example, learn a skill through repetition and practice.

Riyāḍat al-nafs is used in Arabic for training oneself to adopt a particular behavior or disposition.

In modern Arabic, riyāḍah is the common word for sport.

This involves physical exercise to develop strength, skill, and bodily readiness.

The modern meaning relates to the earlier branch through practice and preparation.

Riyāḍah does not, however, occur in the Qur’an.

The occurrence of rawḍah and rawḍāt therefore does not make these verses direct statements about sport or self-discipline.

Rāʾiḍ and murawwaḍ

Rāʾiḍ denotes someone who trains an animal.

Marūḍ and murawwaḍ can describe a trained animal.

Some lexical records use rayyiḍ for an animal whose training has begun but which is not yet fully tractable.

This distinction illustrates that training is a process.

An animal may initially be unfamiliar and resistant, then gradually learn until the task becomes easier.

This branch should not be merged with the green place denoted by rawḍah.

One concerns a place and plant growth.

The other concerns training and a change in a living being’s condition.

Murāwaḍah: gentle persuasion and discussion

Yurāwiḍu fulānan ʿalā amrin concerns an attempt to bring someone around to a matter through accommodation or discussion.

Murāwaḍah is also used in some contexts for negotiation and bargaining.

Each party then seeks to move the other toward accepting certain terms.

This can relate to softening a position and gaining cooperation.

It should not be confused with murāwadah from R W D.

In Joseph’s story, rāwadat belongs to R W D.

The similar sound of murāwaḍah and murāwadah does not establish an identical root or linguistic history.

Accommodation and discussion may be involved in murāwaḍah, but the word does not invariably mean deception or wrongful coercion.

The circumstances and intentions determine the conduct’s character.

4. Evidence from pre-Islamic and early Arabic poetry

Green meadowland in al-Aʿshā’s poetry

Al-Aʿshā, a pre-Islamic poet, describes a green meadow in a celebrated ode:

مَا رَوْضَةٌ مِنْ رِيَاضِ الْحَزْنِ مُعْشِبَةٌ
خَضْرَاءُ جَادَ عَلَيْهَا مُسْبِلٌ هَطِلُ

mā rawḍatun min riyāḍi al-ḥazni muʿshibatun / khaḍrāʾu jāda ʿalayhā musbilun haṭilu

Translation: “No grassy meadow among the meadows of rugged upland, green and generously watered by a pouring rain-cloud…”

The following lines describe flowers shining in sunlight and the meadow’s fragrance.

The poet compares these with his beloved’s beauty and scent.

The image includes land, plants, rain, greenery, flowers, and fragrance.

This is useful lexical evidence because rawḍah clearly denotes natural meadowland.

Riyāḍ al-ḥazn should not be translated as “meadows of sorrow.”

Ḥazn here concerns high or rugged ground, not emotional grief.

Similar-looking Arabic words must be interpreted in context.

Must a meadow lie in a depression?

Some lexical definitions describe a meadow as a place where rainwater collects in low ground.

Al-Aʿshā’s riyāḍ al-ḥazn, however, refers to meadows in upland or rugged terrain.

The two descriptions should not immediately be treated as contradictory.

Higher terrain can also contain hollows that retain water.

Soil moisture and rainfall may also support vegetation without the entire meadow lying in a deep hollow.

Depressed ground characterizes some meadows; it is not an absolute requirement for all of them.

The clear shared feature is conditions supporting growth and greenery.

Rawḍah in an early explanation of the verse

An early explanation of Al-Rūm 30:15 uses this same poem by al-Aʿshā to illustrate rawḍah.

It describes the meadow through its plants, flowers, and pleasant scents.

The value of this citation for research lies in its linguistic evidence.

Al-Aʿshā’s poetry shows rawḍah naming a green, attractive place in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Not every detail of the explanation or poem belongs to the word’s lexical meaning.

Particular flowers or scents, for example, belong to the scene being described.

Rawḍah itself does not name a specific flower, fragrance, or time of day.

Rawḍat al-ḥawḍ: a usage concerning water

Lexical records attribute the following lines to Himyān al-Saʿdī:

وَرَوْضَةٍ فِي الْحَوْضِ قَدْ سَقَيْتُهَا
نِضْوِي وَأَرْضٍ قَدْ أَبَتْ طَوَيْتُهَا

wa-rawḍatin fī al-ḥawḍi qad saqaytuhā / niḍwī wa-arḍin qad abat ṭawaytuhā

Approximate translation: “Water spread in the basin: I gave it to my worn mount to drink; and land that resisted passage: I crossed it.” The second clause does not explicitly say that the ground yielded no water.

Here rawḍah refers to water spread across the basin’s bottom.

This differs from the meadow sense.

In a meadow, water supports vegetation.

In rawḍat al-ḥawḍ, the term names the water covering the basin’s bottom.

This usage shows a lexical connection with water spreading out; by itself it does not establish the historical direction of derivation.

Himyān al-Saʿdī belongs to the early Islamic period, so this should not be presented as pre-Islamic poetry.

Taming in poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays says:

وَصِرْنَا إِلَى الْحُسْنَى وَرَقَّ كَلَامُنَا
وَرُضْتُ فَذَلَّتْ صَعْبَةً أَيَّ إِذْلَالِ

wa-ṣirnā ilā al-ḥusnā wa-raqqa kalāmunā / wa-ruḍtu fa-dhallat ṣaʿbatan ayya idhlāli

Approximate translation: “We came to accord and our speech softened; I tamed her, and the resistant one yielded completely.” This is the poet’s portrayal of a woman, not an abstract “difficulty.”

Ruḍtu here belongs to the training and taming branch.

The poet employs the verb in a human, amorous image.

The usage illustrates an extension of taming language from animals to human relationships and behavior.

Poetic usage does not provide ethical endorsement for domination or coercion of people.

Poetry provides linguistic evidence, not a final standard of right conduct.

Nor does this line establish that rawḍah means a tamed person.

The two branches must remain distinct in morphology and usage.

What the poetic evidence shows

In al-Aʿshā’s poem, rawḍah denotes green meadowland.

In the lines attributed to Himyān, it denotes water spread in a basin.

In Imruʾ al-Qays’s poem, ruḍtu concerns softening and taming.

These examples fit the earlier lexical division: one branch concerns openness, water, and meadows, while the other concerns softening and preparation.

They do not by themselves establish the precise historical origin of the relationship between the branches.

5. Rawḍah: the meadow of joy in Al-Rūm 30:15

The verse

Al-Rūm 30:15

فَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ فَهُمْ فِي رَوْضَةٍ يُحْبَرُونَ

fa-ammā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti fa-hum fī rawḍatin yuḥbarūna

Al-Rūm 30:15

Translation:

“As for those who believed and did righteous deeds, they will be made joyful in a meadow.”

The meaning of rawḍah in the verse

Rawḍatin is a singular, indefinite noun in the genitive.

The preceding preposition fī indicates where the people are.

Fī rawḍatin therefore means “in a meadow.”

Lexically, it names a green, flourishing place with vegetation.

Here the meadow forms part of the portrayal of the believers’ good end.

Rawḍah itself does not mean joy.

Joy is expressed by the separate verb yuḥbarūn.

The ideas are connected: a flourishing green place accompanies the joy of those within it.

The name of the place and the condition of its inhabitants should nevertheless be distinguished.

Yuḥbarūn: joy and enjoyment

Yuḥbarūn comes from Ḥ B R.

The verb is passive here and concerns people being made joyful and granted enjoyment.

Early usage connects ḥubūr with happiness, delight, blessing, and their pleasing effect on a person.

Some explanations of the verse also mention enjoying blessings and being honored.

Yuḥbarūn itself is not from R W Ḍ.

Joy should therefore not be presented as rawḍah’s lexical meaning.

A beautiful meadow may occasion joy, but the verse uses separate words for the place and its inhabitants’ condition.

The proposed wording “made joyful in a meadow” preserves this distinction.

The context of Al-Rūm 30:14, Al-Rūm 30:15, and Al-Rūm 30:16

Al-Rūm 30:14 describes people separating on the day the Hour arises.

Verse 15 then presents the outcome for believing people who did righteous deeds.

Al-Rūm 30:16 describes those who disbelieved and denied the signs and the meeting of the Hereafter being brought into punishment.

Rawḍah in verse 15 thus contributes to the contrast between the two groups’ outcomes.

On one side are belief, righteous action, and joy in a meadow.

On the other are disbelief, denial, and presence in punishment.

This contrast comes from the passage, not from the root R W Ḍ alone.

Rawḍah alone means neither faith nor reward; it names the place assigned here to believing people who do righteous deeds.

Is rawḍah here the same as jannah?

Al-Rūm 30:15 does not explicitly use jannah.

It speaks of a rawḍah.

Its context is people’s outcome after the Hour, and this meadow is associated with the believers’ joy.

Al-Shūrā 42:22 also uses the same word family in rawḍāt al-jannāt.

The Qur’an thus supports the connection between the meadow and the blessings of the gardens of the Hereafter.

Nevertheless, the more general “Paradise” should not replace rawḍah without explanation in a direct translation of Al-Rūm 30:15.

“Paradise” can explain the otherworldly setting contextually.

“Meadow” more precisely renders the word itself.

This distinction preserves the particular image used in the Qur’an.

Does the singular mean one meadow for all believers?

Rawḍatin is singular in this verse.

Its grammatical number does not necessarily settle the total number of places in the Hereafter.

An indefinite singular noun can introduce a type of place or a scene.

Al-Shūrā 42:22, by contrast, uses the plural rawḍāt.

The singular in Al-Rūm does not establish that only one meadow exists in the Hereafter.

The plural likewise does not specify an exact number.

What is clear in Al-Rūm 30:15 is the believers’ presence in a flourishing place associated with joy.

Is rawḍah merely a symbol of happiness?

Someone might argue that, because the verse concerns the Hereafter, the meadow is only a mental symbol of happiness and peace.

Yet rawḍah in Arabic names a familiar kind of place.

The Qur’an uses that name to describe blessing in the Hereafter.

Its spatial and natural sense should therefore not be erased.

A shared name does not establish that every physical characteristic matches earthly meadows exactly.

The verse gives no details of its size, plants, flower colors, or irrigation.

The lexical sense is preserved without supplying unmentioned details.

6. Rawḍāt al-jannāt: the meadows of the gardens in Al-Shūrā 42:22

The verse

Al-Shūrā 42:22

تَرَى الظَّالِمِينَ مُشْفِقِينَ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا وَهُوَ وَاقِعٌ بِهِمْ ۗ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ فِي رَوْضَاتِ الْجَنَّاتِ ۖ لَهُمْ مَا يَشَاءُونَ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ الْفَضْلُ الْكَبِيرُ

tarā al-ẓālimīna mushfiqīna mimmā kasabū wa-huwa wāqiʿun bihim wa-alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti fī rawḍāti al-jannāti lahum mā yashāʾūna ʿinda rabbihim dhālika huwa al-faḍlu al-kabīru

Al-Shūrā 42:22

Translation:

“You see the wrongdoers fearful of what they have earned, and it will fall upon them; while those who believed and did righteous deeds are in the meadows of the gardens. They have whatever they wish with their Lord. That is the great bounty.”

The construction rawḍāt al-jannāt

Rawḍāt is the sound feminine plural of rawḍah.

It follows fī and is in the genitive.

Al-jannāt is its genitive complement.

The construction therefore means “the meadows of the gardens.”

It combines two place nouns without making them lexically identical.

Rawḍāt denotes meadows and green places.

Jannāt denotes gardens, which in Qur’anic usage can also describe blessings in the Hereafter.

Rawḍāt can thus be understood as flourishing green places within the gardens.

The genitive construction does not establish their exact number, size, or shape.

Rawḍah and jannah

Jannah belongs to J N N.

That family concerns covering and concealment.

In its spatial use, jannah denotes a garden covered by trees and vegetation.

Rawḍah, in familiar Arabic usage, denotes green meadowland covered with grasses and plants.

A difference in imagery can therefore be observed:

Jannah emphasizes the garden and its vegetation cover.

Rawḍah emphasizes green meadowland, abundant growth, and its relationship with water and moisture.

This must not become an absolute rule that every jannah has walls or every rawḍah lacks trees.

Their ranges overlap in lexical usage.

Jannah can also denote either an earthly garden or a garden of the Hereafter in the Qur’an; context determines which.

Why rawḍāt al-jannāt?

The combination allows a more specific picture of the gardens’ green places; their co-occurrence alone does not prove the impossibility of synonymous usage.

The difference in their lexical ranges helps clarify the construction.

Jannāt introduces the gardens collectively.

Rawḍāt highlights their flourishing, open green places.

The verse thus gives more than the general statement that believers are in gardens: it portrays their meadows.

This fits rawḍah’s familiar natural meaning.

It does not establish that the gardens of the Hereafter have precisely the same separate geographic divisions as earthly gardens.

The verse supplies no such detail.

Wrongdoers and believing people who do righteous deeds

The verse begins:

“تَرَى الظَّالِمِينَ مُشْفِقِينَ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا”

The wrongdoers are fearful of what they have earned.

It then states that what they fear will fall upon them.

Next comes the believers’ condition:

“وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ فِي رَوْضَاتِ الْجَنَّاتِ”

This contrast matters.

One side presents fear and the consequences of wrongdoing.

The other presents belief, righteous deeds, and presence in the gardens’ meadows.

Rawḍāt itself does not mean righteous conduct.

The word appears within a separate clause identifying the second group’s final dwelling.

The contrast does not imply that any person who commits the slightest error belongs to the particular wrongdoers described.

Judgment concerning conduct, intention, and reform must be examined with the relevant verses.

This root study focuses on the green place and its role in the verse’s imagery.

Whatever they wish with their Lord

The verse continues:

“لَهُمْ مَا يَشَاءُونَ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ”

They have whatever they wish with their Lord.

This extends the portrayal of blessing beyond the garden environment.

The verse describes both natural beauty and the believers’ enjoyment of what they wish.

Rawḍah itself does not mean wishes being fulfilled.

A separate clause expresses that idea.

The manner in which wishes are fulfilled in the Hereafter is also not defined by R W Ḍ.

The meadow’s name must be distinguished from the other blessings the verse describes.

That is the great bounty

The verse ends with al-faḍl al-kabīr.

This identifies the character of the blessings described.

Faḍl belongs to F Ḍ L and has its own meaning.

Rawḍāt names the meadows.

Rawḍah should therefore not be translated as “great bounty” or “divine reward.”

Reward and bounty belong to the context; the meadow is the specific spatial image chosen to convey part of that outcome.

The relationship between the two Qur’anic uses

Al-Rūm 30:15 says:

“فَهُمْ فِي رَوْضَةٍ يُحْبَرُونَ”

Al-Shūrā 42:22 says:

“فِي رَوْضَاتِ الْجَنَّاتِ”

Both describe people who believed and did righteous deeds.

Both identify a green place for them.

The accompanying features differ.

Al-Rūm expresses joy through yuḥbarūn.

Al-Shūrā mentions enjoyment of wishes and great bounty.

Together, they show rawḍah in Qur’anic usage as a place connected with blessing and a good outcome.

Joy, fulfilled wishes, and divine bounty are nevertheless other elements of the verses’ full picture, not rawḍah’s lexical meanings.

7. Related roots and translation questions

R W Ḍ (rawḍah) and J N N (jannah)

Jannah concerns a garden and its vegetation cover.

Rawḍah denotes flourishing green meadowland.

Al-Shūrā 42:22 combines both words.

They can therefore contribute to one spatial image without having completely identical meanings.

In the Qur’anic usage, rawḍah can be rendered “meadow” and jannah “garden.”

This distinction preserves more of the verse’s imagery.

R W Ḍ (rawḍah) and Ḥ D Q (ḥadīqah)

Ḥadīqah denotes a garden where trees or plants grow.

Al-Naml 27:60 uses “حَدَائِقَ ذَاتَ بَهْجَةٍ”.

ʿAbasa 80:30 also has “حَدَائِقَ غُلْبًا”.

These usages concern gardens and their vegetation.

Rawḍah is also a green place, but its familiar usage can emphasize natural meadowland with grasses and plants.

The families overlap, but every characteristic of one should not be transferred to the other.

Nor does ḥadīqah invariably establish a particular physical wall or fence in every usage.

R W Ḍ (rawḍah) and N B T (nabāt)

N B T concerns growing and vegetation.

Rawḍah names a place where plants grow abundantly.

Plant growth is a familiar feature of a meadow.

Rawḍah itself, however, is not the verb “to grow.”

The Qur’an expresses growth and the emergence of plants through separate words and verbs.

This distinguishes the process of growth from the resulting green place.

R W Ḍ (rawḍah) and Kh Ḍ R (khuḍrah)

Khuḍrah concerns greenness.

A rawḍah is ordinarily green and flourishing.

Greenness is a visible property; meadow is the name of a place.

A tree leaf may be green without itself being called rawḍah.

A meadow, by contrast, refers to land together with its vegetation.

Rawḍah should not be translated merely as “the color green.”

A green place or environment is central to its Qur’anic meaning.

R W Ḍ (rawḍah) and R W Y (rayy)

Rayy concerns becoming sufficiently watered or having one’s thirst quenched.

A natural rawḍah also relates to water and moisture.

The roots and their direct meanings nevertheless differ.

Rayy names the condition of being sufficiently watered.

Rawḍah names a place with abundant vegetation.

Water’s presence in both concepts does not make them one root or one meaning.

R W Ḍ (riyāḍah) and R W D (murāwadah)

The families share their first two consonants but differ in the third.

The training branch of R W Ḍ concerns softening and habituation.

Some uses of R W D concern seeking, requesting, and attempting to gain another’s cooperation.

Murāwaḍah and murāwadah may thus describe related behavior in some situations, but their roots differ.

Rāwadat in Joseph’s story comes from R W D, not R W Ḍ.

Forqan’s root index must preserve this distinction.

Does rawḍah mean self-discipline in the Qur’an?

No.

Only the two meadow nouns from this family occur in the Qur’an.

Riyāḍah and the verb rāḍa do not occur there.

Training, moral cultivation, and self-discipline may be discussed in a lexical study of the family, but they are not the direct meaning of rawḍah and rawḍāt in these verses.

Nor does the meadow’s presence in descriptions of otherworldly blessing prescribe a particular physical or psychological discipline for reaching it.

Those questions belong to the verses about righteous conduct, mindfulness, and human responsibility.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan and conclusions

Rawḍatin — Al-Rūm 30:15

Suitable equivalents: “A meadow,” “grassland,” or “a green place.”

A natural, direct translation is:

“As for those who believed and did righteous deeds, they will be made joyful in a meadow.”

This proposed translation is suitable for the lexical meaning.

“Meadow” is preferable to the general “Paradise” here because it preserves the particular green setting named.

“Made joyful” renders yuḥbarūn and should not be merged into rawḍah’s meaning.

Rawḍāti — Al-Shūrā 42:22

Suitable equivalents: “Meadows,” “grasslands,” or “green places.”

In the full phrase:

“فِي رَوْضَاتِ الْجَنَّاتِ”

A suitable translation is:

“In the meadows of the gardens.”

This wording is proposed for the phrase.

It preserves the distinction between rawḍāt and jannāt rather than reducing both to one general designation.

Proposed word-box explanations for Forqan

Root: R W Ḍ

Brief root meaning: Meadows and green places; openness and spaciousness. A separate branch concerns softening and preparing through practice.

Word: رَوْضَةٍ — Al-Rūm 30:15

Meaning here: A flourishing green meadow where believing people who do righteous deeds are made joyful.

Word: رَوْضَاتِ — Al-Shūrā 42:22

Meaning here: The flourishing green meadows of the gardens of the Hereafter where believing people who do righteous deeds dwell.

These proposals distinguish the brief root meaning from the word’s meaning in each verse.

Training, taming, collected water, and differing spatial definitions belong in the full article rather than lengthy word boxes. These proposals do not replace the existing authored verse explanations.

Conclusion and final definition

R W Ḍ has two important semantic branches in Arabic.

The first concerns openness, spaciousness, collected water, and the emergence of green places.

Rawḍah in this branch names meadowland with abundant vegetation.

Riyāḍ and rawḍāt are plurals; rawḍ is a collective form also listed among the plurals.

In extended usage, istarāḍa can concern spaciousness or room to act, while arwaḍat al-arḍ concerns land becoming green.

The second branch concerns softening and making easy.

Rāḍa al-dābbah here means training and taming an animal.

Riyāḍah concerns practice and preparation and is widely used for physical sport today.

An early lexical analysis distinguishes the two branches while treating them as semantically close.

That closeness does not establish one historical origin for all the derivatives.

Pre-Islamic and early Arabic poetic evidence supports both branches.

Al-Aʿshā’s rawḍah is green meadowland watered by rain and covered in plants and flowers.

In Imruʾ al-Qays’s poem, ruḍtu concerns softening and taming.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

In Al-Rūm 30:15, rawḍah names the place of joy for believing people who do righteous deeds.

In Al-Shūrā 42:22, rawḍāt al-jannāt denotes the meadows of the gardens of the Hereafter.

Both Qur’anic uses therefore belong to the first branch.

The meadow accompanies joy, enjoyment, and divine bounty in these verses; rawḍah itself does not mean happiness or reward.

Those ideas come from the clauses and their context.

Final definition of R W Ḍ:

This word family has two main fields:

1. Openness and green places: Flourishing, spacious land where water and moisture support abundant vegetation; meadowland.

2. Softening and preparation: Training and taming an animal or preparing for a task; by extension, practice and skill development.

Only the first field occurs in the Qur’an.

Rawḍah means a meadow or flourishing green place; rawḍāt is its plural.

Their Qur’anic meaning should not be shifted to sport, ascetic discipline, or taming.

Associated features such as water, vegetation, beauty, happiness, and enjoyment should likewise not be merged indiscriminately into one lexical definition.

Water can support growth; vegetation makes the land green; meadow names the place; the believers’ joy is expressed separately in the verses.

The most suitable equivalents for Forqan’s study and translation are:

رَوْضَةٍ: “A meadow”

رَوْضَاتِ: “Meadows”

These preserve the familiar natural Arabic sense while allowing the verses’ spiritual and otherworldly features to emerge from context.

Accurate study requires three distinctions: the lexical meaning of meadow, the separate training and taming branch, and the meadow’s place in Qur’anic descriptions of otherworldly blessing.

Keeping these distinctions preserves the natural sense without inventing unsupported interpretation or etymology from shared letters and literary images.
