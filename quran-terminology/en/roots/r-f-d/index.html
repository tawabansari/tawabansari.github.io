Helping, supporting, and supplying something to strengthen or benefit another

The Arabic root R F D concerns helping, supporting, and providing something to assist another. This may involve giving money or food, speaking on someone’s behalf, supplying strength, or reinforcing something.

Within this family, rifd denotes assistance or a gift received; rāfid denotes a helper or supporting thing; tarāfadū concerns mutual assistance; and rifādah historically named collective contributions for feeding pilgrims.

The family is not confined to financial assistance. Speech, testimony, social support, or equipment may help another person. In material uses, a timber supporting a wall or roof can also bear a name from this family.

Rafd and mirfad are also recorded for a large vessel, particularly one used for milking or serving guests food and drink.

These uses relate to giving and support, but should not all be reduced to one physical image without historical investigation.

The root occurs in only one Qur’anic verse, in two forms: الرِّفْدُ and الْمَرْفُودُ.

The verse concerns Pharaoh and those who followed his command. After describing their fate on Resurrection Day, it speaks of a curse following them in this life and at resurrection, then says:

بِئْسَ الرِّفْدُ الْمَرْفُودُ

biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu

Hūd 11:99

“How evil is the gift bestowed!”

Here a word ordinarily associated with assistance and giving occurs in a denunciation. A word’s lexical meaning and its rhetorical purpose in a sentence are not always identical.

Qur’anic forms: الرِّفْدُ and الْمَرْفُودُ, both in Hūd 11:99.

Arabic also records رَفَدَ, يَرْفِدُ, رَفْد, رِفْد, رِفَادَة, رَافِد, رَافِدَة, رَوَافِد, رَفُود, مَرْفَد, مِرْفَد, مُرْفِد, مَرْفُود, أَرْفَدَ, إِرْفَاد, رَافَدَ, مُرَافَدَة, تَرَافَدَ, تَرَافُد, اِسْتَرْفَدَ, اِسْتِرْفَاد, اِرْتَفَدَ, and اِرْتِفَاد.

Each form requires its own morphological and contextual analysis. Not all occur in the Qur’an in these forms.

1. The semantic core and early Arabic uses

Rafadahu: helping and supporting

The family’s semantic core has been described in early Arabic as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ مُطَّرِدٌ مُنْقَاسٌ، وَهُوَ الْمُعَاوَنَةُ وَالْمُظَاهَرَةُ بِالْعَطَاءِ وَغَيْرِهِ

aṣlun wāḥidun muṭṭaridun munqāsun wa-huwa al-muʿāwanatu wa-al-muẓāharatu bi-al-ʿaṭāʾi wa-ghayrihi

“One consistent, regular principle: assistance and support through giving and other means.”

This definition highlights two points.

First, R F D concerns assistance and support.

Second, this support need not be financial.

The phrase بِالْعَطَاءِ وَغَيْرِهِ makes giving one means of assistance, rather than the root’s entire meaning.

Rafada fulānan can mean helping someone or supplying something that supports their needs or work.

Providing money for another person may be described as rafd.

Helping through speech or social support may use the same family.

Installing a support to sustain a structure is also described with rafada in some Arabic uses.

The intention to help and the actual result must nevertheless be distinguished.

Someone may give something intending to help, although it proves unhelpful.

Assistance may also reach someone who uses it for a wrongful purpose.

R F D alone therefore establishes neither a rightful purpose, a beneficial result, nor the moral character of giver or recipient.

Rafd: the verbal noun of helping

Rafd, with fatḥah on rāʾ, is recorded as the verbal noun of rafada.

For example:

رَفَدَهُ يَرْفِدُهُ رَفْدًا

rafadahu yarfiduhu rafdan

“He helped him; he supplied assistance to him.”

Here rafdan names the act of helping.

The act of helping differs from the thing provided as assistance.

A person may perform the act of helping through money, food, equipment, or speech.

The verbal noun names the action, whereas rifd can name the assistance or gift received.

This distinction matters for the Qur’anic expression.

Rifd: a gift or assistance received

The lexical explanation of rifd states:

الرِّفْدُ بِالْكَسْرِ: الْعَطَاءُ وَالصِّلَةُ

al-rifdu bi-al-kasri al-ʿaṭāʾu wa-al-ṣilatu

“Rifd, with kasrah on rāʾ: a gift, bounty, or contribution.”

Ṣilah here concerns a gift or something given to maintain a connection and assist another.

Rifd can thus name something a person receives.

Yet not everything transferred between people should be called rifd without regard to actual usage.

Giving and assistance are prominent in this family’s established sense.

Delivering food to someone in need or providing money to help another can exemplify rifd.

In Hūd 11:99, however, rifd occurs in a denunciation. It can be used ironically for something that is not genuinely a beneficial gift.

Ordinary meaning and rhetorical use must therefore be distinguished.

2. Assistance through money, speech, and social support

Rafadtuhu bi-kadhā

The range of assistance in this family is described as follows:

الرِّفْدُ: الْمَعُونَةُ بِالْعَطَاءِ، وَسَقْيِ اللَّبَنِ، وَالْقَوْلِ، وَكُلِّ شَيْءٍ

al-rifdu al-maʿūnatu bi-al-ʿaṭāʾi wa-saqyi al-labani wa-al-qawli wa-kulli shayʾin

“Rifd: assistance through giving, providing milk to drink, speech, and anything else.”

This definition illustrates the root’s breadth.

Assistance here is more than a transfer of money.

Providing food can also help.

Speaking in someone’s support can likewise exemplify it.

Verbal assistance is also explained as follows:

رَفَدَنِي: أَعَانَنِي بِلِسَانِهِ

rafadanī aʿānanī bi-lisānihi

“He rafada me: he helped me with his tongue.”

In this use, someone supports another through speech.

The word itself does not determine whether that speech is true or false.

One person may speak to defend another’s rights.

Another may support an oppressor through false speech or testimony.

Both can be verbal support as a type of action, although their moral value differs.

Lexical assistance and morally rightful assistance are distinct questions.

This distinction matters when examining the root’s Qur’anic use.

Tarāfadū: cooperation and mutual assistance

Tarāfadū belongs to Form VI.

Arabic uses this form for people who help one another.

The following expression is recorded for verbal cooperation:

تَرَافَدُوا عَلَى فُلَانٍ بِأَلْسِنَتِهِمْ إِذَا تَنَاصَرُوا

tarāfadū ʿalā fulānin bi-alsinatihim idhā tanāṣarū

“They are said to tarāfadū when they support one another with their tongues against someone.”

This usage expresses collective support.

People may pool money or resources for a shared undertaking.

They may also support a person or group in a dispute.

Tarāfud does not by itself mean cooperation in something right.

The Qur’an states a separate criterion for cooperation:

وَتَعَاوَنُوا عَلَى الْبِرِّ وَالتَّقْوَىٰ وَلَا تَعَاوَنُوا عَلَى الْإِثْمِ وَالْعُدْوَانِ

wa-taʿāwanū ʿalā al-birri wa-al-taqwā wa-lā taʿāwanū ʿalā al-ithmi wa-al-ʿudwāni

Al-Māʾidah 5:2

“Cooperate in goodness and mindfulness, and do not cooperate in sin and aggression.”

This verse uses the root ʿ W N, not R F D.

Conceptually, however, it clarifies the difference between commendable and reprehensible cooperation.

Thus, although tarāfadū means mutual support, cooperation alone does not establish positive moral value.

Its purpose and manner require separate assessment.

Istarfada: requesting assistance

Istarfadahu is recorded for requesting help or a gift from another.

This is Form X.

Someone with a need or request seeks another’s assistance.

Requesting help and actually receiving it are different.

Someone may ask for assistance yet receive nothing.

Conversely, someone may receive help without asking beforehand.

Istirfād emphasizes seeking or requesting, whereas rafd can concern giving assistance or the gift supplied.

Irtafada: receiving or acquiring

Irtafada min fulānin is recorded for benefiting from another person’s wealth or assistance.

Irtafada mālan is also recorded for acquiring or earning wealth.

The R F D family can thus be considered from both the helper’s and the recipient’s perspective.

Its forms should not all be confined to requesting assistance.

Verb structure and context determine whether the issue is giving, requesting, receiving, or mutual cooperation.

3. Rifādah: collective assistance and hospitality for pilgrims

Rifādah is an established historical use of this family.

It names contributions Quraysh provided before Islam to host and feed pilgrims.

Accounts describe individuals contributing according to their means, with the collected funds supplying food and other provisions for pilgrims.

This use clearly relates to cooperation and financial support.

Individuals supplied separate shares that served a shared undertaking.

Historical accounts and the root’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished.

Rifādah here named a social service and collective hospitality.

This does not make every form of R F D exclusively about pilgrimage or hosting pilgrims.

Nor does the existence of a pre-Islamic custom establish a religious obligation for all its details.

Religious rulings concerning pilgrimage and related services require Qur’anic and independent evidence.

Rifādah, charity, and zakāh

Historical rifādah concerns gathering contributions for hospitality.

Ṣadaqah and zakāh have their own Qur’anic uses and rulings.

Financial assistance to someone in need may overlap in outcome with charity or other spending.

Such overlap does not make the three terms lexically or legally identical.

People might, for example, collect contributions to feed guests.

This can be tarāfud and rifādah, but the name alone does not make every condition and ruling of zakāh applicable.

A root study should preserve assistance and financial participation without importing the independent rulings of other concepts.

4. Physical support: walls, roofs, and supporting equipment

Rafada al-ḥāʾiṭa

Rafada al-ḥāʾiṭa means placing a support against a wall.

Supporting a wall is described as follows:

رَفَدَ فُلَانٌ حَائِطَهُ

rafada fulānun ḥāʾiṭahu

That is, someone placed a support against his wall to prevent its collapse.

Here assistance extends from human relationships to objects.

Timber or another device holds the wall up.

Rafd here is physical support, rather than financial assistance to a person.

This relates to the semantic core of assistance.

It does not make every rifd a wooden prop or building tool.

Rawāfid: supporting roof timbers

The building-related use states:

وَالرَّوَافِدُ خَشَبُ السَّقْفِ

wa-al-rawāfidu khashabu al-saqfi

“Rawāfid are the timbers of a roof.”

Rawāfid is the plural of rāfidah.

Timbers and beams carrying a roof’s weight function as its supports.

Their naming within a family of assistance and support is therefore understandable.

This image does not prove that R F D originally arose from building construction.

Assistance and support aptly explain the family; roof beams are one application.

Rifādat al-sarj

Rifādat al-sarj names something placed under a saddle or pack-saddle to support it or reduce direct pressure on the animal’s body.

Here rifādah supports and adjusts the saddle’s position.

This sense should not be equated with hosting pilgrims.

Both belong to R F D, but their referents differ.

In one, people assist others with money and food.

In the other, an object beneath a saddle supports it.

Support is shared; the material and service are different.

5. Rafd and mirfad: large vessels for milk and hospitality

Rafd, rifd, and mirfad are also recorded for a large vessel.

This use relates to giving and hospitality but requires separate examination.

Al-rafd: a large vessel

Rafd can name a large bowl or vessel into which milk is drawn or in which food and drink are provided.

ʿUss also names a kind of large vessel.

Some accounts describe a rafd as larger than an ʿuss.

Others use it more generally for a large vessel.

These variations prevent deriving exact dimensions from the word alone.

The clear meaning is a large vessel for holding or supplying drink and food.

Rafd and rifd

Older explanations sometimes distinguish the forms:

Rafd, with fatḥah on rāʾ, for a large vessel.

Rifd, with kasrah on rāʾ, for a gift or assistance.

This distinction is not absolute across all lexical accounts.

Some record both vocalizations for a vessel.

Vocalization also varies in accounts of pre-Islamic poetry.

A predominant meaning must therefore be distinguished from an absolute boundary.

Hūd 11:99 has al-rifdu, with kasrah on rāʾ.

Assistance or gift best fits that context, although some interpretations also invoke vessels and drink to explain the rhetorical image.

Rafūd: an animal yielding abundant milk

Nāqatun rafūdun describes a she-camel yielding enough milk to fill a large vessel in one milking.

Another explanation concerns an animal whose milk continues across periods.

This use relates to vessels and providing milk.

It does not mean every milk-producing animal.

Abundance or continuity of milk is prominent.

This use does not make Qur’anic rifd refer to nursing or providing milk.

Hūd 11:99 concerns the curse and fate of Pharaoh’s followers.

The husbandry use must remain distinct.

6. Rāfid and rāfidān: helpers and sustaining rivers

Rāfid: a helper

Rāfid is the active participle of rafada.

Its primary use names someone who supplies assistance or a gift.

The description can also apply to things that contribute to something larger.

A timber sustaining part of a building functions as its support.

In modern Arabic, a smaller river supplying a larger river is also called a rāfid.

This relates to contribution and support.

It does not make the root itself mean river or flowing water.

Water flow is one instance of contributing to and sustaining something else.

Al-rāfidān: the Tigris and Euphrates

Al-rāfidān is also recorded for the Tigris and Euphrates.

This expression was known in Arabic poetry and later became common geographically.

Here the two rivers matter as sources of water sustaining their region.

The name does not make them merely two small tributaries of a larger river throughout their courses.

Geographical naming and the precise structure of a river network are separate issues.

Nor does this use make Qur’anic al-rifd mean the Tigris or Euphrates.

Rāfid: a ruler’s deputy or supporter

Rāfid is also recorded for someone serving beside a ruler and assuming some responsibilities during the ruler’s absence.

This concerns assistance in administering affairs.

It does not make R F D lexically mean rulership or political power.

Supporting a ruler is one social application of the root.

Such support may serve right or wrong.

The title rāfid alone establishes neither the ruler’s nor the supporter’s legitimacy.

7. Pre-Islamic poetic witnesses: vessels, gifts, and assistance

Rubba rafdin haraqtahu in al-Aʿshā’s poetry

An established witness is this line by al-Aʿshā, the well-known pre-Islamic poet:

رُبَّ رَفْدٍ هَرَقْتَهُ ذَلِكَ الْيَوْمَ
وَأَسْرَى مِنْ مَعْشَرٍ أَقْتَالِ

rubba rafdin haraqtahu dhālika al-yawma / wa-asrā min maʿsharin aqtāli

“Many a milk vessel you ruined that day, and captives you took from worthy adversaries.”

In an early reading, rafd here refers to a large milk vessel.

The poet describes warfare involving the loss of livestock and property and the taking of captives.

The destruction of a milk vessel or its contents evokes economic loss and lost means of livelihood.

This line illustrates rafd meaning a large vessel.

Lexical accounts differ over the vowel on rāʾ.

Some read rafd with fatḥah for the vessel; rifd with kasrah is also reported.

Some versions have dhāka al-yawma, others dhālika al-yawma.

These variations matter for the poem’s transmission, but the vessel or its contents remain the lexical explanation.

Does this witness determine the Qur’anic meaning?

The line shows that early Arabic could use rafd for a large vessel or what it supplied.

Hūd 11:99, however, concerns the curse and fate of Pharaoh’s followers.

Al-Aʿshā’s poem alone therefore does not justify translating the verse as “How evil is the vessel given!”

Some interpretations invoke vessels and drink, but assistance and gift fit the Qur’anic vocalization and context more directly.

Al-Aʿshā himself is discussing warfare, captivity, and lost resources.

These events form the poetic context; they are not independent meanings of R F D.

Wa-law taʾaththafaka al-aʿdāʾu bi-al-rifadi in al-Nābighah

A poem attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

لَا تَقْذِفَنِّي بِرُكْنٍ لَا كِفَاءَ لَهُ
وَلَوْ تَأَثَّفَكَ الْأَعْدَاءُ بِالرِّفَدِ

lā taqdhifannī bi-ruknin lā kifāʾa lahu / wa-law taʾaththafaka al-aʿdāʾu bi-al-rifadi

“Do not strike me with a force I cannot withstand, even if enemies gather around you in groups.”

Al-rifad here is the plural of rifdah, a group or party. Cooperation and mutual support emerge from the poetic context.

The subject is not a vessel of milk or food.

The poet describes hostile groups gathering around the addressee and supporting one another against him—the poet.

This illustrates rifdah and its plural for human groups; collective support is understood from context.

The line’s wording nevertheless requires care.

Other versions read wa-in taʾaththafaka; the opening of the second hemistich is not uniform.

Taʾaththafaka evokes gathering around the addressee like stones positioned around a cooking pot.

Al-rifad, with fatḥah on fāʾ, is a plural meaning groups. It should not be confused with Qur’anic al-rifd, with sukūn on fāʾ.

Does rifd always support what is right?

The line has an important semantic implication.

Collective support can serve confrontation and even hostility.

Rifd in Arabic is therefore not exclusively charitable assistance.

People may support one another for a wrongful person or purpose.

This possibility matters for the Qur’an’s denunciatory use.

If rifd lexically meant only a good and commendable gift, its application to a curse and evil outcome would require still closer attention to rhetoric.

Lexical uses concern support and provision as such; their value depends on the kind and purpose of assistance.

Hūd 11:99 uses this family in a denunciation.

8. Biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu: the only Qur’anic verse

The verse reads:

وَأُتْبِعُوا فِي هَٰذِهِ لَعْنَةً وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ بِئْسَ الرِّفْدُ الْمَرْفُودُ

wa-utbiʿū fī hādhihi laʿnatan wa-yawma al-qiyāmati biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu

Hūd 11:99

“And a curse was made to follow them in this [life] and on Resurrection Day. How evil is the gift bestowed!”

Both forms of R F D occur in one expression.

Understanding it first requires identifying the people discussed.

Pharaoh and those following his command

Hūd 11:96 says:

وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا مُوسَىٰ بِآيَاتِنَا وَسُلْطَانٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-laqad arsalnā Mūsā bi-āyātinā wa-sulṭānin mubīnin

Hūd 11:96

“And We certainly sent Moses with Our signs and clear authority.”

The following verse says:

إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ وَمَلَإِيهِ فَاتَّبَعُوا أَمْرَ فِرْعَوْنَ وَمَا أَمْرُ فِرْعَوْنَ بِرَشِيدٍ

ilā Firʿawna wa-malaʾihi fa-ittabaʿū amra Firʿawna wa-mā amru Firʿawna bi-rashīdin

Hūd 11:97

“To Pharaoh and his chiefs; they followed Pharaoh’s command, although Pharaoh’s command was not rightly guided.”

The passage concerns Moses, Pharaoh, his chiefs, and following Pharaoh’s command.

That command is described as not rightly guided.

The description belongs to R SH D, not R F D.

It nevertheless matters for understanding his followers’ fate.

They followed a command the Qur’an identifies as lacking right guidance.

The outcome of that following is then described.

Yaqdumu qawmahu yawma al-qiyāmati

Hūd 11:98 says:

يَقْدُمُ قَوْمَهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فَأَوْرَدَهُمُ النَّارَ وَبِئْسَ الْوِرْدُ الْمَوْرُودُ

yaqdumu qawmahu yawma al-qiyāmati fa-awradahumu al-nāra wa-biʾsa al-wirdu al-mawrūdu

Hūd 11:98

“He goes before his people on Resurrection Day and leads them into the Fire. How evil is the watering place entered!”

Following Pharaoh is thus connected with an evil outcome in the next life as well.

In worldly life they followed his command.

On Resurrection Day he goes before them and leads them into the Fire.

The relationship between leader and followers, and the consequence of following a wrongful command, are prominent.

This does not make everyone called a leader or guide necessarily like Pharaoh.

The subject is a particular command and conduct identified as wrong.

Nor does the title of leader or ruler itself establish the rightness of a command.

This connects conceptually with R F D: support for a person or group can be judged only by considering its purpose and outcome.

9. Wa-utbiʿū fī hādhihi laʿnatan: what followed them

Hūd 11:99 begins:

وَأُتْبِعُوا فِي هَٰذِهِ لَعْنَةً وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ

wa-utbiʿū fī hādhihi laʿnatan wa-yawma al-qiyāmati

Hūd 11:99

“And a curse was made to follow them in this life and on Resurrection Day.”

The expression concerns a curse being made to follow and accompany the group.

Utbiʿū belongs to a different root

Utbiʿū belongs to T B ʿ.

The verb is passive.

Here a curse is made to follow or is attached to the people described.

Utbiʿū must not be assigned to R F D.

Following and rafd belong to separate word families.

The first concerns following or being followed.

The second concerns assistance and giving.

They are connected in this verse but are not lexically identical.

Fī hādhihi: in this life

Fī hādhihi includes the demonstrative hādhihi, “this.”

The context identifies worldly life as its referent.

Wa-yawma al-qiyāmati then names another time.

Two settings appear together: worldly life and resurrection.

The word al-dunyā is not explicitly present in this expression.

A literal translation can retain “in this,” with the contextual explanation identifying this life.

A curse in this life and at resurrection

Early explanations connect wa-utbiʿū … laʿnatan with a curse in this life and another at resurrection.

On this reading, the people face two stages of condemnation and evil consequence.

Some interpretations associate the worldly curse with denunciation and rejection.

Others connect it with destruction and drowning.

The resurrection curse has likewise been explained through the final outcome and exclusion from divine mercy.

The verse’s words and interpretive referents must be distinguished.

The verse itself speaks of a curse in this life and on Resurrection Day.

Its precise forms require examination alongside related verses and evidence.

The meaning of R F D cannot supply every detail of their punishment, exclusion, or fate.

10. Biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu: a condemned gift

We can now consider the central expression:

بِئْسَ الرِّفْدُ الْمَرْفُودُ

biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu

Hūd 11:99

“How evil is the gift bestowed!”

Rifd occurs in a construction beginning with condemnation.

Biʾsa: a verb of condemnation

Arabic uses biʾsa to condemn or pronounce something bad.

The Qur’an uses this construction repeatedly.

For example:

بِئْسَ الِاسْمُ الْفُسُوقُ بَعْدَ الْإِيمَانِ

biʾsa al-ismu al-fusūqu baʿda al-īmāni

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:11

“How evil is the name of disobedience after faith!”

In Hūd 11:99, biʾsa precedes al-rifdu.

The sentence is thus denunciatory from its opening.

Al-rifd here should not be presented as a good, praised gift.

The sentence itself condemns it.

Al-rifdu: a gift or assistance

Al-rifdu here has kasrah on rāʾ.

Its established use concerns gifts and assistance.

Grammatically, al-rifdu is the subject of the fixed verb of condemnation biʾsa.

The construction therefore means:

“How evil a rifd …”

The central question is why something ordinarily associated with assistance and giving is condemned here.

The context provides the answer.

The verse speaks of a curse in this life and at resurrection, then describes it through the language of gifts.

The word is used ironically, with an inversion of expectation.

An evil outcome is named through the language of giving and assistance, and its badness is immediately declared.

Arabic rhetoric can describe this as an ironic metaphor or expression.

That description does not make the verse merely a joke or a statement without serious purpose.

It emphasizes condemnation and the reversal embodied in their outcome.

Al-marfūdu: a passive participle

Al-marfūdu is the passive participle of the basic triliteral verb rafada.

It follows the mafʿūl pattern.

Ordinarily, marfūd describes someone or something receiving assistance or a gift.

Rafadahu means “he helped him”; marfūd can describe the person helped.

One grammatical analysis makes al-marfūdu an adjective modifying al-rifdu, with the particular object of condemnation omitted. Another treats it as that particular object of condemnation.

On the adjectival analysis, the gift or assistance is itself described as receiving an addition or reinforcement. This rhetorical image should be distinguished from determining the word’s grammatical role.

Early explanations offer two related readings:

“A gift that has been given.”

“Assistance accompanied by further assistance or an addition.”

These share a semantic field but are not morphologically identical in precision.

The first understands the passive participle as describing something given or received.

The second emphasizes reinforcement and something being added to something else.

Both contextual explanations connect rifd with the curses of this life and resurrection.

The word’s structure alone does not determine the addition’s exact quantity or form.

11. Why is the gift condemned here?

Giving and assistance can ordinarily be beneficial.

Here, however, the subject is the fate of Pharaoh’s followers.

They are described as accompanied by a curse in this life and on Resurrection Day.

That fate is then characterized by the denunciation biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu.

A gift that is not a good reward

Rifd here does not denote a genuinely beneficial gift.

The construction ironically calls the consequence of their conduct a gift.

Ordinary beneficial assistance might be described as:

“What a fine gift he received.”

But when wrongful conduct brings a severe consequence, the same term may be used in condemnation:

“What an evil gift has fallen to him.”

The ordinary meaning is not erased; its inverted application strengthens the condemnation.

In Hūd 11:99, biʾsa makes this explicit.

A curse accompanied by another curse

One explanation of the expression is that another curse is added to the earlier one.

This connects the curse in this life with the curse at resurrection.

One accompanies the other, forming a condemned whole.

This fits rifd as assistance and added reinforcement.

What is added here, however, is an evil consequence, not beneficial help.

The expression is therefore not genuinely benevolent assistance.

Does the verse say The God gave them a present?

A literal rendering can translate rifd as a gift or bounty.

That word does not make the curse morally a good present.

Biʾsa explicitly condemns it from the opening.

Gift belongs to the rhetorical image here.

Nor should Persian bakhshish be understood as forgiveness in this passage.

That Persian word can mean either giving something to another or forgiving a fault.

Al-rifdu al-marfūdu concerns the former—giving or bestowing something—not forgiving sin.

This distinction matters for Persian translation.

12. The connection with wa-biʾsa al-wirdu al-mawrūdu

The preceding verse says:

وَبِئْسَ الْوِرْدُ الْمَوْرُودُ

wa-biʾsa al-wirdu al-mawrūdu

Hūd 11:98

“How evil is the watering place entered!”

Immediately afterward, the next verse says:

بِئْسَ الرِّفْدُ الْمَرْفُودُ

biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu

Hūd 11:99

“How evil is the gift bestowed!”

The two expressions have similar structures.

Both use biʾsa.

Both pair a noun with another form from the same word family.

Their roots nevertheless differ.

Wird: approaching a watering place

Wird belongs to W R D.

Established Arabic uses concern going to a watering place or entering a place.

In Hūd 11:98, Pharaoh leads his people into the Fire.

The Fire is then called a wird in a denunciatory expression.

This also reverses an expectation: a watering place ordinarily offers water and relief from thirst, but their destination here is fire.

Rifd: a gift or assistance

The following verse uses rifd.

This concerns a gift or help.

Here Pharaoh’s followers face a curse and condemnation.

Two consecutive verses therefore invert two familiar images of human life:

A watering place that is actually fire, and a gift that is actually an evil outcome.

Their juxtaposition strengthens the rhetorical effect.

The similarity of sound between wird and rifd does not make them one root.

Wird belongs to W R D.

Rifd belongs to R F D.

Similar sound differs from a shared root.

Do the expressions mean the same thing?

No.

Al-wirdu al-mawrūdu occurs in the context of entering the Fire.

Al-rifdu al-marfūdu occurs in the context of curses in this life and at resurrection.

They concern the same group’s fate but use different images and lexical meanings.

If preserving the Qur’an’s lexical distinctions is the aim, both should not be reduced to “How evil a punishment!”

That rendering may convey the contextual result while losing each root’s particular image.

13. ʿAṭāʾan ghayra majdhūd: genuine giving and a condemned gift

Later in Hūd, after different outcomes at resurrection are described, the fortunate are addressed:

وَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ سُعِدُوا فَفِي الْجَنَّةِ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا مَا دَامَتِ السَّمَاوَاتُ وَالْأَرْضُ إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ رَبُّكَ عَطَاءً غَيْرَ مَجْذُوذٍ

wa-ammā alladhīna suʿidū fa-fī al-jannati khālidīna fīhā mā dāmati al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍu illā mā shāʾa rabbuka ʿaṭāʾan ghayra majdhūdhin

Hūd 11:108

“As for those made fortunate, they are in the Garden, remaining there as long as the heavens and earth endure, except as your Lord wills: an unbroken gift.”

ʿAṭāʾan belongs to another word family and concerns giving.

Its semantic field is close to rifd.

The contextual difference is crucial.

In Hūd 11:99, rifd occurs in an ironic denunciation of Pharaoh’s followers’ fate.

In Hūd 11:108, ʿaṭāʾ describes what the fortunate enjoy.

Nearby passages thus distinguish a condemned “gift” in ironic language from a praised gift.

This does not make rifd always negative or ʿaṭāʾ always positive.

Arabic ordinarily uses rifd for help and assistance, which can be positive.

A gift’s value also requires considering its source, purpose, and result.

The verses differ in context and rhetorical use, not through inherent opposition between the roots.

14. Was following Pharaoh a form of support?

Hūd 11:97 says:

فَاتَّبَعُوا أَمْرَ فِرْعَوْنَ

fa-ittabaʿū amra Firʿawna

Hūd 11:97

“They followed Pharaoh’s command.”

Following a ruler’s command can, in practice, strengthen his position.

A ruler may also rely on cooperation, obedience, and support from various groups to retain power.

The verse expresses this following with ittabaʿū, not rafadū.

Biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu should therefore not be confined without evidence to “How badly they supported Pharaoh!”

In verse 99, the clearer explanation concerns the curse and evil outcome of that group.

Conceptually, the passage nevertheless shows that following a wrongful command can lead to an undesirable outcome.

This follows from the relationship between Hūd 11:97, Hūd 11:98, and Hūd 11:99, not from the independent meaning of R F D.

Responsibility of leader and followers

The passage discusses both Pharaoh’s command and those who followed him.

Hūd 11:97 describes his command as lacking right guidance.

Hūd 11:98 places him before his people and describes their fate.

Hūd 11:99 speaks of a curse in this life and at resurrection.

Leadership titles, political power, and collective following are not sufficient criteria for a sound path.

This does not make all followers equally responsible in every situation regardless of their knowledge, freedom, and participation.

Assessing individual responsibility requires other verses concerning knowledge, choice, coercion, misleading others, and conduct.

For this root study, that is contextual background, not part of rifd’s lexical definition.

15. R F D and related roots

R F D (rifd) and ʿ W N (ʿawn)

Study of ʿ W N

ʿAwn concerns help and assistance.

Rifd likewise concerns assistance and support in its primary use.

Lexical explanations use maʿūnah to explain rifd.

The roots are nevertheless not identical.

Rifd sometimes emphasizes the thing supplied as help or a gift.

ʿAwn can apply more broadly to helping and cooperation themselves.

There is no absolute boundary making ʿawn always nonfinancial and rifd always financial.

Arabic also allows rafd through speech and nonfinancial support.

The distinction must follow actual usage.

R F D (rifd) and M D D (madad)

Study of M D D

Madad concerns addition, extension, and supplying further strength.

The Qur’an says:

وَأَمْدَدْنَاهُمْ بِفَاكِهَةٍ وَلَحْمٍ مِمَّا يَشْتَهُونَ

wa-amdadnāhum bi-fākihatin wa-laḥmin mimmā yashtahūna

Al-Ṭūr 52:22

“And We supplied them with fruit and meat of whatever they desire.”

This concerns providing something for enjoyment and sustenance.

Rifd too can mean a gift or assistance.

Madad can emphasize increased strength or extension.

Rifd highlights the assistance or gift supplied in its established use.

They overlap in some situations but are not identical.

R F D (rifd) and ʿ Ṭ W / ʿ Ṭ Y (ʿaṭāʾ)

ʿAṭāʾ concerns giving and bestowing.

Rifd can likewise concern a gift or what is given to another.

Assistance and support are particularly important in rifd.

Financial rifd can thus be a kind of gift, while not every gift is necessarily understood through assistance and support.

The root structures also differ.

In Hūd 11:99, rifd occurs in a denunciatory construction.

In Hūd 11:108, ʿaṭāʾ concerns the fortunate receiving bounty.

Context determines this difference; neither root is inherently positive or negative.

R F D (rifd) and N Ṣ R (naṣr)

Study of N Ṣ R

Qur’anic naṣr frequently concerns assistance against an enemy or obstacle.

Rifd can also be assistance.

Not every rafd concerns warfare or defeating an enemy.

Hosting guests, financially helping someone in need, and supporting a roof with timber also belong to this Arabic family.

Rafd should therefore not always be translated as victory or military assistance.

Nor does the word alone establish that assistance achieved victory.

R F D (rifd) and S N D (sanad)

Sanad concerns a support or backing.

Rafada al-ḥāʾiṭa is explained through asnadahu, propping it up.

The overlap is clear in building-related usage.

Other R F D uses include gifts and financial or verbal assistance.

Sanad has its own independent range of uses.

Not every R F D form can be explained solely through a pillar or physical prop.

R F D (rifd) and R D F (radf)

Study of R D F

The roots resemble one another in writing and sound.

Their second and third consonants occur in different orders.

R F D comprises rāʾ, fāʾ, and dāl.

R D F comprises rāʾ, dāl, and fāʾ.

Radf concerns coming afterward or following.

The Qur’an says:

تَتْبَعُهَا الرَّادِفَةُ

tatbaʿuhā al-rādifatu

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:7

“The following event comes after it.”

Rifd, however, concerns assistance and giving.

In Hūd 11:99, a curse follows a group and is then described through rifd.

This juxtaposition must not merge the roots.

The curse’s following is expressed by utbiʿū.

Giving and assistance belong to al-rifdu.

R F D (rifd) and W R D (wird)

Hūd 11:98 and Hūd 11:99 place these in similar expressions.

Wird concerns approaching a watering place or entering.

Rifd concerns assistance and giving.

Both are condemned with biʾsa here but retain their distinct meanings.

Al-rifdu al-marfūdu should not simply repeat the meaning of al-wirdu al-mawrūdu.

16. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish helping, what is given as assistance, its recipient, and material applications.

رَفَدَ — rafada: helped; supported; supplied someone with assistance or a gift.

يَرْفِدُ — yarfidu: helps; supplies assistance or a gift.

رَفْد — rafd: the act of helping; assistance; in another use, a large vessel.

رِفْد — rifd: a gift, bounty, or something supplied to help another.

رِفَادَة — rifādah: assistance and support; historically, collective provision for pilgrims; physically, saddle-supporting padding.

رَافِد — rāfid: helper or support; for rivers, a watercourse supplying a larger system.

رَوَافِد — rawāfid: supporters; in construction, roof timbers; for water, tributaries.

رَفُود — rafūd: in husbandry, an animal yielding abundant or continuous milk.

أَرْفَدَ — arfada: supplied help or a gift.

إِرْفَاد — irfād: assisting, giving, or supplying help.

رَافَدَ — rāfada: cooperated with and supported another, in the relevant uses.

مُرَافَدَة — murāfadah: mutual assistance and support.

تَرَافَدَ — tarāfada: cooperated with others in mutual assistance.

تَرَافُد — tarāfud: cooperation and participation in supplying help.

اِسْتَرْفَدَ — istarfada: requested help or a gift from another.

اِسْتِرْفَاد — istirfād: requesting assistance and support.

اِرْتَفَدَ — irtafada: received assistance or benefit; in some uses, acquired wealth.

مِرْفَد — mirfad: a large milk or food vessel; in other uses, assistance or a supporting means.

مَرْفَد — marfad: help and assistance; in some vocalizations, a large vessel.

مَرْفُود — marfūd: someone or something receiving help or a gift; in Hūd 11:99 it occurs in a denunciatory expression concerning rifd.

Hūd 11:99

بِئْسَ الرِّفْدُ الْمَرْفُودُ

biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu

Hūd 11:99

A proposed Forqan rendering:

“How evil is the gift bestowed!”

This preserves giving and the condemnation expressed by biʾsa.

The lexical explanation should clarify that marfūd is a passive participle and can ordinarily name a recipient of help or a gift.

Here that participle accompanies rifd in the context of curses in this life and at resurrection.

A contextual explanation might say:

“How evil is the gift and addition that has fallen to them!”

Or:

“How evil is the assistance and gift accompanied by another like it!”

These are contextual interpretations and should not replace the direct translation without attention to the Arabic construction.

“How evil a curse!” conveys a contextual result but loses the wording and distinctive image of al-rifdu al-marfūdu.

Retaining gift or bounty in the base translation is defensible, provided the explanation clarifies its irony.

17. Conclusion

R F D concerns helping, supporting, and supplying something to assist another.

Assistance may take the form of money, food, speech, or a physical device.

Support through giving and other means aptly explains the family’s uses.

Arabic records rafada for helping, rifd for a gift or assistance, rāfid for a helper, and tarāfadū for mutual cooperation.

Historical rifādah named collective contributions for hosting pilgrims.

Physical uses include wall props, roof timbers, and saddle-supporting padding.

Rafd and mirfad also name large vessels for milk or hospitality.

These uses relate to assistance and support, but should not all be derived from one physical image without sufficient historical evidence.

Al-Aʿshā’s poetry illustrates rafd in the setting of milk vessels and means of livelihood.

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī uses al-rifad for groups in a context of enemies cooperating.

These witnesses demonstrate both material and social uses in early Arabic.

The Qur’an uses the root only in Hūd 11:99, in the two forms al-rifdu and al-marfūdu.

This follows the account of obeying Pharaoh’s command and of his and his people’s fate on Resurrection Day.

Hūd 11:97 describes Pharaoh’s command as lacking right guidance.

Hūd 11:98 has him lead his people into the Fire and says wa-biʾsa al-wirdu al-mawrūdu.

Hūd 11:99 describes curses in this life and at resurrection, then says biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu.

The expressions resemble each other in sound and rhetorical structure but use different roots.

Wird invokes a watering place and entering.

Rifd invokes assistance and giving.

Both use words ordinarily capable of suggesting benefit to describe an evil outcome.

In Hūd 11:99, rifd is not a genuinely good gift: biʾsa condemns it, and the subject is the curse and fate of Pharaoh’s followers.

Its application here is ironic.

Marfūd is a passive participle; its combination with rifd can contextually suggest a bestowed gift or assistance accompanied by a further addition.

Early explanations connect the expression with curses in this life and at resurrection.

The root’s meaning does not supply every detail of curse or punishment.

Nor should rifd become negative in every Arabic use.

Assistance and gifts may ordinarily be commendable, while the moral value of a particular contribution depends on its purpose, manner, and result.

Distinctions from nearby roots must also be preserved.

ʿAwn concerns help; madad, added strength and extension; ʿaṭāʾ, giving; naṣr, assistance; radf, following; and wird, entering or reaching a watering place.

These concepts can overlap with rifd without being identical to it.

Final definition: R F D concerns helping, supporting, and supplying something to strengthen or benefit another. Rafd names the act of helping; rifd, a gift or assistance supplied; rāfid, a helper; and marfūd, someone or something receiving assistance. The Qur’anic expression biʾsa al-rifdu al-marfūdu concerns Pharaoh’s followers in the context of curses in this life and at resurrection. It uses the language of giving and assistance ironically and condemnatorily to express an evil outcome. The gift’s nature, moral value, and the expression’s particular meaning must be determined from the sentence and its context, not from an invariably positive or negative definition imposed on every derivative.
