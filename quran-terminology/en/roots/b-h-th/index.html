The Qur’anic Root B Ḥ TH (baḥth)

Digging in the ground, turning soil and searching: from the raven in the story of two brothers to inquiry and research in Arabic

Qur’anic and lexical forms

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic form

Arabic B Ḥ TH concerns digging, turning something over and searching. In physical usage it describes digging and moving soil aside; other uses concern looking for something, asking questions and trying to obtain news or understand a matter.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as yabḥathu in Al-Māʾidah 5:31. It describes a raven digging in the ground. Seeing its behaviour leads the man who killed his brother to understand how to cover his brother’s body.

Yabḥathu is an active form I imperfect verb, third-person masculine singular. Its perfect is baḥatha and its verbal noun baḥth. Its grammatical subject here is an implicit huwa, “it,” referring to the raven. Ghurāban itself is the accusative object of baʿatha, “sent”; the clause yabḥathu fī al-arḍ describes that raven.

Other recorded Arabic forms include baḥth, bāḥith, baḥūth, ibtaḥatha, istabḥatha and tabaḥḥatha, but these do not occur in the Qur’an. The technical meaning of “research” in modern academic language must therefore not automatically be imposed on the sole Qur’anic occurrence.

2. The semantic core in earlier Arabic

Stirring something up and turning it over

In the baḥth entry of Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris introduces the root’s core as:

«أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، يَدُلُّ عَلَى إِثَارَةِ الشَّيْءِ»

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā ithārati al-shayʾi

That is, the root concerns stirring something up and turning it over.

He then quotes al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad:

«الْبَحْثُ طَلَبُكَ شَيْئًا فِي التُّرَابِ»

al-baḥthu ṭalabuka shayʾan fī al-turābi

Baḥth thus means looking for something in soil.

In this use, a person or animal moves soil aside, scratches it or digs into it. The purpose may be to find something hidden beneath it. The digging movement itself can nevertheless be described by this verb even if the sentence does not name an object being sought.

For example, Arabic uses baḥth for an animal pushing soil backwards with its limbs.

This meaning is not absolutely narrower or broader than ḥafr, digging a hole; the two overlap. Baḥth foregrounds the movement of probing and shifting material, while ḥafr can focus on making a hole or an opening in the ground.

From digging soil to inquiring about a matter

The same earlier sources do not restrict baḥth to hand movements or digging in the ground.

Alongside searching in soil, al-Khalīl includes asking questions and seeking information within this family’s uses. Ibn Fāris also records baḥatha ʿani al-khabar: seeking knowledge of some news.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records istabḥathtu, ibtaḥathtu and tabaḥḥathtu ʿani al-shayʾ for searching and inquiring about something.

The meanings can be related as follows: someone moves soil aside to find something; an inquirer similarly asks questions, examines evidence and gathers information to reach what they do not yet know.

This explains a semantic relationship, without proving that inquiry historically developed after the physical sense of digging. Both uses occur in earlier lexical sources; establishing their chronological order requires independent evidence.

The family can therefore be understood through two related uses: physical probing and seeking knowledge. The verb’s object and sentence construction distinguish them.

3. Uses recorded in earlier Arabic

Digging soil with a hand or an animal’s limbs

Kitāb al-ʿAyn describes baḥūth camels as throwing soil backwards with their forelegs while moving. No questioning, or necessarily searching for a hidden object, is involved: the soil-moving action itself is in view.

This matters because yabḥathu in Al-Māʾidah 5:31 also concerns an animal. It can be understood in the same physical sense of digging and moving soil aside, without first assigning abstract inquiry or scientific research to it.

The lexicons record derivatives for camels and some burrowing animals. These uses show that Arabic could use the family to describe the perceptible movement of an animal’s limbs against soil.

A witness from Arabic poetry

A rajaz expression transmitted in literary and lexical sources says:

يَبْحَثْنَ بَحْثًا كَمُضِلَّاتِ الْخَدَمِ

yabḥathna baḥthan ka-muḍillāti al-khadami

Translation: “They dig the soil, like women who have lost their anklets.”

The expression describes camels digging into the ground with their feet as they move. The poet compares their movements to those of people turning soil over in search of lost anklets.

Khadam here is the plural of khadamah, an anklet worn around the ankle. The comparison concerns the movement of searching for something in soil.

Maqāyīs quotes this wording without naming a poet. A related version beginning fa-hunna baḥthan, within the same image of lost anklets, appears in Jarīr’s Dīwān with Muḥammad ibn Ḥabīb’s commentary and in Simṭ al-Laʾālī within rajaz attributed to Jarīr. The lexical quotation’s wording must be distinguished from the poetic version. This attribution does not justify presenting the witness as pre-Islamic poetry. Its value is evidence of earlier Arabic usage, rather than proof of a specific date before the Qur’an.

A search that harms the searcher

Arabic proverbs record the expression كَالْبَاحِثَةِ عَنْ حَتْفِهَا بِظِلْفِهَا.

It means: “Like an animal searching for its own death with its hoof.”

The proverb is explained through an animal uncovering a blade or knife while digging soil; that same implement then brings about its death.

It applies to someone whose behaviour supplies the means of their own harm or destruction. Sources transmit different versions and explanations of the story.

The root still concerns searching and turning something over. Death or destruction comes from the proverb’s story, not from baḥth itself.

This distinction particularly matters for Qur’anic study: the root’s presence in a murder narrative does not make killing, death or burying the dead its inherent meaning.

Names and other terms

Maqāyīs uses al-bāḥithāʾ for soil gathered by a jerboa, the desert rodent called yarbūʿ. Lisān al-ʿArab also explains it in connection with the openings and soil of that animal’s burrow; it is not a general category of soil. Baḥūth likewise describes camels that turn soil over.

The latter source also records baḥatha ʿani al-khabar and tabaḥḥatha ʿani al-shayʾ for inquiring and searching.

These forms show that the family did not remain restricted to a single movement. Differences between derivatives still matter: a place name, an animal’s description and a verb of inquiry cannot replace one another in every sentence.

4. The Qur’anic use: a raven digging in the ground

Al-Māʾidah 5:31

فَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ غُرَابًا يَبْحَثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ لِيُرِيَهُ كَيْفَ يُوَارِي سَوْءَةَ أَخِيهِ ۚ قَالَ يَا وَيْلَتَا أَعَجَزْتُ أَنْ أَكُونَ مِثْلَ هَٰذَا الْغُرَابِ فَأُوَارِيَ سَوْءَةَ أَخِي ۖ فَأَصْبَحَ مِنَ النَّادِمِينَ

fa-baʿatha llāhu ghurāban yabḥathu fī al-arḍi li-yuriyahu kayfa yuwārī sawʾata akhīhi qāla yā waylatā a-ʿajaztu an akūna mithla hādhā al-ghurābi fa-uwāriya sawʾata akhī fa-aṣbaḥa mina al-nādimīna

Translation: “Then The God sent a raven digging in the ground, to show him how to cover his brother’s body. He said: ‘Woe is me! Was I unable to be like this raven and cover my brother’s body?’ Then he became one of the regretful.”

The meaning of yabḥathu fī al-arḍ

The verb here describes the raven’s physical behaviour: it digs in the ground and moves soil aside.

Fī al-arḍ identifies the action’s setting and location. Fī here is distinct from ʿan in baḥatha ʿani al-khabar, seeking information about news.

The construction does not state what the raven was seeking. It does not even specify that its immediate purpose was to find something beneath the soil. It describes its digging behaviour.

“Was digging in the ground” is therefore more precise than “was looking for something”: the latter assigns the action an object the verse does not name.

“Was scratching the ground” can convey part of the image. If scratching is understood as merely touching the surface without shifting soil, however, it moves away from the intended sense. Digging conveys the action and allows for soil being displaced.

Baḥth and covering the brother’s body

The next part of the sentence is:

«لِيُرِيَهُ كَيْفَ يُوَارِي سَوْءَةَ أَخِيهِ»

li-yuriyahu kayfa yuwārī sawʾata akhīhi

This expresses the purpose of showing the raven’s behaviour: the man is to learn how to cover his brother’s body.

Yuwārī belongs to W R Y, not B Ḥ TH. Covering and concealing come from that verb.

The distinction matters: baḥth concerns digging and moving soil aside; muwārāh concerns covering and hiding from sight.

The actions may combine in burial: earth is first dug and the body then covered with soil. This possible sequence must not become a claim that baḥth lexically means burying.

Within the verse, the raven digs in the ground and the man learns how to cover his brother’s body by observing it. The connection between digging and covering comes from the narrative as a whole.

Does the Qur’an mention two ravens?

Some exegetical reports describe two ravens fighting, one being killed and the other digging and burying the dead bird. Other reports concern how long the brother’s body remained or further details of the raven’s behaviour.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits differing versions in his commentary on Al-Māʾidah 5:31.

The verse itself, however, mentions only a single raven digging in the ground.

It names no second raven, fight, death of one bird or length of time that the brother’s body remained on the ground.

This does not make such details logically impossible; it means that the verse’s wording does not establish them.

Understanding B Ḥ TH does not require adding one of these reports to the word’s meaning. Lexical evidence and the Qur’anic context make the verb intelligible.

Does baḥth mean learning?

Observing the raven’s behaviour leads to learning an action. That result does not supply a new lexical meaning for baḥth.

The raven itself digs in the ground in yabḥathu fī al-arḍ. Learning results from observing its behaviour.

This distinction rules out translating the verb as “a raven teaching.” Liyuriyahu already expresses the purpose of showing; that meaning need not be inserted into baḥth as well.

The verse also mentions the man’s regret after he sees the raven and speaks. B Ḥ TH itself does not supply regret, repentance or moral reform.

A simple bodily action thus serves an important outcome in the story without the verb’s own meaning extending beyond digging in the ground.

5. Distinctions from neighbouring roots

B Ḥ TH (baḥth) and B ʿ TH (baʿth)

These roots occur side by side in Al-Māʾidah 5:31.

Fa-baʿatha belongs to B ʿ TH and concerns sending or rousing. Yabḥathu belongs to B Ḥ TH and concerns digging.

The sentence describes two different acts: The God sends a raven, and the raven digs in the ground.

The verbs’ visual and phonetic resemblance does not establish a shared root. ʿAyn and ḥāʾ are different letters, and the roots have distinct meanings.

Study of B ʿ TH

B Ḥ TH (baḥth) and Ḥ F R (ḥafr)

Ḥafr concerns digging and making a hole or opening. Baḥth can accompany it in digging the ground, while also emphasizing turning over, shifting aside and searching.

The two can therefore be close in some contexts, without being complete synonyms in every use.

Study of Ḥ F R

B Ḥ TH (baḥth) and F Ḥ Ṣ (faḥṣ)

Maqāyīs al-Lughah records an Arabic distinction associating baḥth with the hand and faḥṣ with the foot.

This indicates that the instrument of digging could influence the choice of a verb describing physical movement.

It must not become an absolute rule: the same sources record forms of baḥth for camels moving soil aside with their limbs.

The Qur’an itself uses baḥth in the raven story. That lexical distinction therefore does not make its application to a bird inappropriate.

B Ḥ TH (baḥth) and W R Y (muwārāh)

Their distinction is especially clear in the principal verse.

Digging in the ground does not by itself mean covering something. Conversely, covering or hiding from sight does not necessarily require digging.

They can nevertheless be connected actions in burial. Seeing soil being dug shows the man how to cover his brother’s body.

Accurate translation must therefore preserve each verb’s independent meaning.

Study of W R Y

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

A suitable brief definition is: “Digging and turning over, especially soil; also searching and inquiring to find something or learn about a matter.”

In physical contexts, consider “digging,” “turning soil over” or, as appropriate, “moving soil aside.”

For knowledge or news, “searching,” “inquiring,” “examining” or “researching” may fit. The verb’s object and construction should determine the choice.

For yabḥathu fī al-arḍ in Al-Māʾidah 5:31, the principal proposal is:

“Was digging in the ground.”

This is concise and conveys the physical action without assigning an unknown purpose to the raven’s behaviour.

English “digging in the ground” naturally preserves the locative fī al-arḍ. Persian can more naturally use “digging the ground”; grammatical idiom in each target language should guide the construction without changing the event.

Translations such as “was searching the ground for food” or “was digging a grave to bury another raven” introduce information not stated by the verb and Qur’anic phrase themselves.

“Was researching” is likewise inappropriate here despite its lexical relationship with the family: it transfers a modern abstract sense to the raven’s physical behaviour.

7. Conclusion

B Ḥ TH occurs only once in the Qur’an, as yabḥathu in Al-Māʾidah 5:31.

Earlier Arabic sources record two prominent uses: digging and turning soil over, and searching or inquiring about something. Their semantic fields are related, but their historical order should not be asserted without adequate evidence.

The Qur’anic physical sense is clear: the raven digs in the ground, and observing its behaviour shows the man how to cover his brother’s body.

Burial and covering are understood through yuwārī and the full narrative, not yabḥathu alone. Details such as a second raven, a fight or how long the body remained on the ground are not stated in the verse itself.

An appropriate final definition for Forqan:

Arabic B Ḥ TH concerns digging and turning something over, especially soil, and more broadly searching and inquiring. Its sole Qur’anic occurrence is yabḥathu in Al-Māʾidah 5:31, describing a raven digging in the ground. Covering the brother’s body is an outcome expressed through the verse’s complete construction and must not be made part of baḥth’s own lexical meaning.
