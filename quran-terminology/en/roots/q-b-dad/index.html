The Qur’anic Root Q B Ḍ

Taking, gathering, bringing into one’s possession, and restricting

Qur’anic forms

In early Arabic, Q B Ḍ expresses taking something, gathering its parts, bringing it under one’s control, and restricting its extension. A clear material image is closing the fingers around something and grasping it.

Arabic lexicons record receiving property, folding a bird’s wings, the contraction of skin under heat, and holding something within this word family. These attest its range in classical Arabic, although lexical documentation alone does not securely date all these uses to pre-Islamic times. The Qur’an itself clearly attests the root’s broad usage in the language of revelation.

Maqbiḍ names the handle of a sword, bow, or other implement grasped with the fingers. Related expressions include qabḍ al-māl, receiving property; qabḍ wa-basṭ, contraction and expansion; and qabḍ al-rūḥ, taking the spirit. Inqibāḍ denotes contraction, including muscular contraction or reduced extension.

Forms examined: qabaḍa (قَبَضَ), yaqbiḍu (يَقْبِضُ), qabḍ (قَبْض), qabḍah (قَبْضَة), qābiḍ (قَابِض), maqbūḍ (مَقْبُوض), maqbiḍ (مَقْبِض), inqabaḍa (اِنْقَبَضَ), inqibāḍ (اِنْقِبَاض), and taqābuḍ (تَقَابُض).

Qur’anic forms: the verb qabaḍa, verbal noun qabḍ, noun qabḍah, and passive participle maqbūḍah. The root occurs nine times across seven verses.

1. Core meaning and its material image

Opening the hand separates the fingers and exposes the palm. Taking something and closing the fingers around it exemplifies qabḍ.

This image helps explain several related uses: taking and gaining possession of something, drawing together what is spread out, limiting its extent, and preventing its release or availability to another.

Qabḍ names the act of taking or gathering. Qabḍah can denote a handful or a state of holding something under one’s control. Maqbūḍ is something taken, while inqibāḍ denotes contraction or reduced extension.

Qabḍ carries no inherently positive or negative moral value. Receiving collateral to protect a right, folding a bird’s wings, and withholding generosity from the needy can all involve this root, but differ in value and outcome.

2. Material uses in the Qur’an

Taking a handful: the Sāmirī’s account

قَالَ بَصُرْتُ بِمَا لَمْ يَبْصُرُوا بِهِ فَقَبَضْتُ قَبْضَةً مِنْ أَثَرِ الرَّسُولِ فَنَبَذْتُهَا وَكَذَٰلِكَ سَوَّلَتْ لِي نَفْسِي

Qāla baṣurtu bi-mā lam yabṣurū bihi fa-qabaḍtu qabḍatan min athari l-rasūli fa-nabadhtuhā wa-kadhālika sawwalat lī nafsī.

He said, “I perceived what they did not perceive, so I took a handful from the trace of the Messenger and cast it away. Thus my self made it seem appealing to me.”

Ta-Ha 20:96

Qabaḍtu expresses the act of taking, while qabḍatan denotes the amount acquired in a grasp.

The preposition min also indicates that the Sāmirī took something from the Messenger’s trace. The feminine pronoun in fa-nabadhtuhā refers back to that handful.

This verse does not explicitly identify the precise substance taken or the Messenger in question. Adding soil, a horse’s hoofprint, or Gabriel’s name to the translation introduces details absent from its wording.

It also reports the Sāmirī’s own explanation of his actions. The lexical meaning of qabḍah should not depend on whether his claim is true or false.

Folding birds’ wings

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَى الطَّيْرِ فَوْقَهُمْ صَافَّاتٍ وَيَقْبِضْنَ ۚ مَا يُمْسِكُهُنَّ إِلَّا الرَّحْمَٰنُ ۚ إِنَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ بَصِيرٌ

A-wa-lam yaraw ilā l-ṭayri fawqahum ṣāffātin wa-yaqbiḍna. Mā yumsikuhunna illā l-raḥmānu innahu bi-kulli shayʾin baṣīr.

Have they not looked at the birds above them, spreading and folding their wings? None holds them but the Entirely Merciful. He sees all things.

Al-Mulk 67:19

Here, qabḍ contrasts with the spreading of the birds’ wings.

Ṣāffāt describes outspread wings, while yaqbiḍna expresses folding them. The verb’s object is unstated but understood from the context of flight and outspread wings.

The two different roots in this verse are also significant: the birds fold their wings, yaqbiḍna, and the Entirely Merciful holds them, yumsikuhunna.

Qabḍ concerns folding the wings; imsāk concerns holding the birds in the air.

Hands, fingers, or taking something from another person are therefore not necessary for this root’s meaning. Drawing together something previously spread out also belongs within its range.

Drawing in the shadow

أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ كَيْفَ مَدَّ الظِّلَّ وَلَوْ شَاءَ لَجَعَلَهُ سَاكِنًا ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَا الشَّمْسَ عَلَيْهِ دَلِيلًا

A-lam tara ilā rabbika kayfa madda l-ẓilla wa-law shāʾa la-jaʿalahu sākinan thumma jaʿalnā l-shamsa ʿalayhi dalīlā.

Have you not considered your Lord, how He extended the shadow? Had He willed, He could have made it still. Then We made the sun an indicator of it.

Al-Furqan 25:45

ثُمَّ قَبَضْنَاهُ إِلَيْنَا قَبْضًا يَسِيرًا

Thumma qabaḍnāhu ilaynā qabḍan yasīrā.

Then We drew it in toward Us with an easy drawing-in.

Al-Furqan 25:46

The first verse uses madda for extending the shadow; the second uses qabaḍnāhu for drawing in that same shadow.

The attached pronoun hu refers to the shadow in the preceding verse. Qabḍan is a cognate verbal noun functioning as an absolute object; yasīran describes how the action is performed.

As the sun’s position relative to the earth’s surface and objects changes, shadows change length and direction. A shadow extended during one part of the day becomes shorter during another.

Ilaynā, “toward Us,” attributes the drawing-in of the shadow to The God. It need not signify the shadow’s relocation to a bodily place occupied by Him.

Here, qabḍ is neither grasping with a hand nor acquiring property. It is reducing the extent of something previously spread out.

3. Financial relations and social behavior

Receiving collateral: from a promise to actual possession

وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ وَلَمْ تَجِدُوا كَاتِبًا فَرِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ ۖ فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُمْ بَعْضًا فَلْيُؤَدِّ الَّذِي اؤْتُمِنَ أَمَانَتَهُ وَلْيَتَّقِ اللَّهَ رَبَّهُ ۗ وَلَا تَكْتُمُوا الشَّهَادَةَ ۚ وَمَنْ يَكْتُمْهَا فَإِنَّهُ آثِمٌ قَلْبُهُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ عَلِيمٌ

Wa-in kuntum ʿalā safarin wa-lam tajidū kātiban fa-rihānun maqbūḍatun. Fa-in amina baʿḍukum baʿḍan fa-l-yuʾaddi lladhī uʾtumina amānatahu wa-l-yattaqi llāha rabbahu. Wa-lā taktumū l-shahādata wa-man yaktumhā fa-innahu āthimun qalbuhu. Wa-llāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna ʿalīm.

If you are traveling and find no scribe, then pledges taken into possession. If one of you trusts another, let the one entrusted discharge his trust and be mindful of The God, his Lord. Do not conceal testimony; whoever conceals it has a sinful heart. The God knows what you do.

Al-Baqarah 2:283

Maqbūḍah is a feminine singular passive participle qualifying rihān. An Arabic nonhuman plural may take a feminine singular adjective.

Pledging property and receiving the pledge are distinct. Rahn concerns property held as security for an obligation; qabḍ concerns receiving that property and taking possession of it.

A debtor may promise to pledge property without yet delivering it. Maqbūḍah expresses actual receipt and possession of the collateral.

Possession does not necessarily transfer ownership. Someone can hold property owned by another person.

Receiving money, taking delivery of goods, and receiving collateral can also be described through qabḍ in contemporary financial language, without necessarily involving physically grasping them with the hand.

Closing the hand: withholding generosity

الْمُنَافِقُونَ وَالْمُنَافِقَاتُ بَعْضُهُمْ مِنْ بَعْضٍ ۚ يَأْمُرُونَ بِالْمُنْكَرِ وَيَنْهَوْنَ عَنِ الْمَعْرُوفِ وَيَقْبِضُونَ أَيْدِيَهُمْ ۚ نَسُوا اللَّهَ فَنَسِيَهُمْ ۗ إِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ هُمُ الْفَاسِقُونَ

Al-munāfiqūna wa-l-munāfiqātu baʿḍuhum min baʿḍin. Yaʾmurūna bi-l-munkari wa-yanhawna ʿani l-maʿrūfi wa-yaqbiḍūna aydiyahum. Nasū llāha fa-nasiyahum. Inna l-munāfiqīna humu l-fāsiqūn.

The hypocritical men and women are of one another. They command what is wrong, forbid what is right, and keep their hands closed. They forgot The God, so He forgot them. The hypocrites are the defiantly disobedient.

At-Tawbah 9:67

Yaqbiḍūna aydiyahum, “they keep their hands closed,” figuratively expresses withholding generosity and assistance.

An open hand can give to another; a hand kept closed retains what it holds for itself.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:29 also uses the hand to express financial behavior:

وَلَا تَجْعَلْ يَدَكَ مَغْلُولَةً إِلَىٰ عُنُقِكَ وَلَا تَبْسُطْهَا كُلَّ الْبَسْطِ فَتَقْعُدَ مَلُومًا مَحْسُورًا

Wa-lā tajʿal yadaka maghlūlatan ilā ʿunuqika wa-lā tabsuṭhā kulla l-basṭi fa-taqʿuda malūman maḥsūrā.

Do not keep your hand bound to your neck, nor extend it to its fullest extent, lest you sit reproached and exhausted.

Al-Isra 17:29

Just as the Qur’an condemns stinginess and withholding expenditure, it does not recommend indiscriminate giving that exhausts all one’s resources.

Not every retention of property is stinginess. Al-Tawbah 9:67 condemns those who withhold their hands from good and from helping others.

4. Qabḍ and basṭ in divine actions and descriptions

The God restricts and grants expansion

مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يُقْرِضُ اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا فَيُضَاعِفَهُ لَهُ أَضْعَافًا كَثِيرَةً ۚ وَاللَّهُ يَقْبِضُ وَيَبْسُطُ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

Man dhā lladhī yuqriḍu llāha qarḍan ḥasanan fa-yuḍāʿifahu lahu aḍʿāfan kathīratan. Wa-llāhu yaqbiḍu wa-yabsuṭu wa-ilayhi turjaʿūn.

Who will lend The God a good loan, so He may multiply it for him many times over? The God restricts and grants expansion, and to Him you will be returned.

Al-Baqarah 2:245

Qabḍ and basṭ are explicitly contrasted here.

Qabḍ denotes taking and restricting, while basṭ concerns opening and expanding.

Neither verb has an explicit object. The context of a good loan and giving property connects them with provision and human resources, although the sentence’s semantic range need not be confined to money and wealth.

Health, ability, opportunity, resources, and other forms of well-being can also undergo restriction and expansion. This is a broader reflection, not a list supplied by the verse itself.

People are not independent, permanent owners of everything at their disposal. Today’s resources may be lost tomorrow; opportunities for doing good should not be postponed in hope of greater abundance.

The earth in the grasp of The God

وَمَا قَدَرُوا اللَّهَ حَقَّ قَدْرِهِ وَالْأَرْضُ جَمِيعًا قَبْضَتُهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ وَالسَّمَاوَاتُ مَطْوِيَّاتٌ بِيَمِينِهِ ۚ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَىٰ عَمَّا يُشْرِكُونَ

Wa-mā qadarū llāha ḥaqqa qadrihi wa-l-arḍu jamīʿan qabḍatuhu yawma l-qiyāmati wa-l-samāwātu maṭwiyyātun bi-yamīnihi. Subḥānahu wa-taʿālā ʿammā yushrikūn.

They did not esteem The God as He deserves to be esteemed. The entire earth will be in His grasp on the day of resurrection, and the heavens folded in His right hand. Glory be to Him, exalted above what they associate with Him.

Az-Zumar 39:67

Qabḍatuhu expresses the encompassing power and authority of The God over the whole earth.

In Arabic, saying that something is in a person’s grasp need not mean that it physically fits in a fist. The expression can convey control and dominion.

The image of the earth in His grasp and the heavens folded up sets the greatness of divine power against human perceptions of the universe’s magnitude.

The verse begins with the due estimation of The God and ends by declaring Him beyond partners. Its principal subject is His incomparable power and sovereignty. Interpreting the image as dominion does not require attributing bodily organs to Him.

Al-Qābiḍ and al-Bāsiṭ

Common lists of the beautiful names attribute al-Qābiḍ and al-Bāsiṭ to The God.

Their meaning has Qur’anic support, including Al-Baqarah 2:245. Yet these two nominal forms do not occur in the Qur’an as divine names. The verse contains the verbs yaqbiḍu and yabsuṭu.

A description derived from a divine action should therefore be distinguished from a name explicitly occurring in that form in the Qur’an.

5. “Taking the spirit”: common expression and Qur’anic wording

Arabic and Persian commonly use qabḍ al-rūḥ for taking a person’s life at death. Arabic qubiḍa fulān can also mean that someone died.

None of the Qur’anic occurrences of Q B Ḍ, however, makes rūḥ or nafs the object of this verb.

The Qur’an uses another expression for human death and sleep:

اللَّهُ يَتَوَفَّى الْأَنْفُسَ حِينَ مَوْتِهَا وَالَّتِي لَمْ تَمُتْ فِي مَنَامِهَا ۖ فَيُمْسِكُ الَّتِي قَضَىٰ عَلَيْهَا الْمَوْتَ وَيُرْسِلُ الْأُخْرَىٰ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

Allāhu yatawaffā l-anfusa ḥīna mawtihā wa-llatī lam tamut fī manāmihā. Fa-yumsiku l-latī qaḍā ʿalayhā l-mawta wa-yursilu l-ukhrā ilā ajalin musamman. Inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yatafakkarūn.

The God takes the selves in full at their death, and those that have not died during their sleep. He retains those for which He has decreed death and sends the others back until an appointed term. In this are signs for people who reflect.

Az-Zumar 39:42

Three distinct verbs occur here: tawaffā, amsaka, and arsala.

Tawaffī concerns taking the self in full; imsāk, retaining the self for which death has been decreed; and irsāl, sending the other until an appointed term.

The currency of qabḍ al-rūḥ in Arabic does not make it the Qur’an’s own expression. Research into death and human destiny must distinguish Qur’anic wording from conventional expressions attested in later usage; this alone does not date their first appearance.

6. Comparison with related roots

Q B Ḍ (qabḍ) and B S Ṭ (basṭ)

Qabḍ and basṭ are opposites in many contexts. Qabḍ concerns taking, gathering, and restricting; basṭ concerns opening, spreading, and increasing extent.

This contrast appears materially, as in opening and closing the hand, and nonmaterially, as in restriction and expansion of resources.

Q B Ḍ (qabḍ) and ʾ KH DH (akhdh)

Akhdh has a broader range: taking, receiving, accepting, or obtaining something.

In contexts of taking, qabḍ places more emphasis on gathering and gaining possession.

Someone may receive and accept a statement, covenant, or recommendation without these uses necessarily being expressed through qabḍ.

Q B Ḍ (qabḍ) and M S K (imsāk)

Qabḍ emphasizes taking or gathering; imsāk emphasizes retaining, maintaining a state, or preventing release.

Al-Mulk 67:19 clearly shows the distinction: the birds fold their wings, while The God holds them in the air.

The two word families nevertheless overlap in some Arabic uses involving taking and retaining something.

Q B Ḍ (qabḍ) and Q D R (qadr)

In provision contexts, qadr may express setting a measure and restricting the extent of resources.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:30 contrasts yaqdiru with expanding provision, just as Al-Baqarah 2:245 contrasts yaqbiḍu with yabsuṭu.

Here, qabḍ emphasizes restriction, while qadr emphasizes measuring out and limiting the amount. Both can express reduced resources in a provision context.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Choose the equivalent of Q B Ḍ according to the verb’s object and context:

  • Yaqbiḍu, Al-Baqarah 2:245: restricts.
  • Maqbūḍah, Al-Baqarah 2:283: taken into possession.
  • Yaqbiḍūna aydiyahum, Al-Tawbah 9:67: keep their hands closed.
  • Qabaḍtu qabḍatan, Ṭā Hā 20:96: I took a handful.
  • Qabaḍnāhu, Al-Furqān 25:46: We drew it in.
  • Qabḍatuhu, Al-Zumar 39:67: in His grasp.
  • Yaqbiḍna, Al-Mulk 67:19: fold their wings.

Brief root meaning: Taking and gaining possession, gathering what is spread out, and restricting or preventing expansion and release.

Conclusion

Q B Ḍ comprises words for taking, gathering, gaining possession, and restricting. Closing the fingers around something is a concrete example of this semantic field.

The Qur’an uses it for taking a handful, folding birds’ wings, drawing in a shadow, receiving collateral, withholding generosity, restricting resources, and divine power encompassing the earth.

Qabḍ contrasts with basṭ, but does not inherently mean stinginess, deprivation, ownership, or death. These meanings may be associated with it in an appropriate context.

Taking and gathering lie at the root’s semantic core; the object, purpose, and outcome are understood from the sentence and its context.
