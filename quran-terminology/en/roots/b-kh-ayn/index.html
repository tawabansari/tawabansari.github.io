The Qur’anic Root B KH ʿ (bakhʿ)

From killing carried to its limit and consuming the self to intense grief, exertion and submission: bākhiʿun nafsaka in the address to the Prophet

Qur’anic and lexical forms

بَاخِعٌ
(bākhiʿun)
Qur’anic form I active participle; Al-Kahf 18:6 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:3

بَخَعَ
(bakhaʿa)
Lexical base perfect; this form itself does not occur in the Qur’an

بَخْع
(bakhʿ)
Lexical verbal noun; not itself Qur’anic

بُخُوع
(bukhūʿ)
Lexical verbal noun, including acknowledgment and submission uses; not Qur’anic

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

Arabic B KH ʿ concerns killing, bringing down, exhausting and carrying an action to its limit. One familiar use portrays grief or anger so intense that someone seems to destroy their own life. Other recorded uses concern strenuous exertion, committing one’s full capacity, humility and submission.

The root occurs twice in two verses across two surahs: Al-Kahf 18:6 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:3. Both use bākhiʿun and address the Prophet.

Bākhiʿun is the active participle of form I bakhaʿa. Bakhʿ and bukhūʿ are recorded verbal nouns. No other form, such as a perfect verb, imperfect verb or verbal noun, occurs in the Qur’an.

Both occurrences share laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka. Bākhiʿun describes someone seemingly bringing their own life to destruction, while nafsaka identifies what the action affects.

The contexts concern the Prophet’s response to people’s unbelief, rather than reporting a killing or a physical suicide attempt. Lexical intensity, rhetorical imagery and what the verses actually describe must therefore be distinguished.

2. The semantic core in earlier Arabic

Killing and bringing down

In Kitāb al-ʿAyn, al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad defines this use as:

«بَخَعَ نَفْسَهُ: قَتَلَهَا غَيْظًا مِنْ شِدَّةِ الْوَجْدِ»

bakhaʿa nafsahu: qatalahā ghayẓan min shiddati al-wajdi

That is: “He destroyed his own life in rage arising from overwhelming emotion.” Ghayẓan explicitly states anger in this quotation; grief is also attested elsewhere in the family’s uses.

The definition connects killing with an intense inner state. Wajd here denotes powerful emotion, which may involve sorrow, longing or anger.

Ibn Fāris likewise proposes a semantic centre in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«الْقَتْلُ وَمَا دَانَاهُ مِنْ إِذْلَالٍ وَقَهْرٍ»

al-qatlu wa-mā dānāhu min idhlālin wa-qahrin

That is: “Killing and what approaches it in humiliation and subjugation.”

This extends beyond killing itself. Something can be subjected to pressure and depletion to the point of extreme weakness without bodily death necessarily occurring.

Intensity and carrying an action to its limit thus matter in the family’s uses. This must not become an unrestricted definition of any strenuous effort: Qur’anic bākhiʿun nafsaka carries a stronger image than ordinary exertion.

Did the root originally mean cutting the neck?

Some lexicons use bakhaʿa al-dhabīḥah for slaughter reaching the far part of the neck, with cutting exceeding the usual extent.

Ibn Fāris transmits a lexicographer called al-Ḍabbī distinguishing bakhʿ from nakhʿ in cutting an animal’s neck. That report describes nukhāʿ as a white strand along the neck and vertebral column, and bikhāʿ as a vessel in the back.

This establishes an intensive-slaughter use within the lexical tradition. It does not establish that every sense of the family originally arose from cutting one specific body part. The anatomical term bikhāʿ itself was disputed: Lisān al-ʿArab records Ibn al-Athīr’s objection that he had not found this name in his searches through lexical and anatomical sources. That disagreement reinforces caution about identifying one body part as the root’s origin.

A historical origin requires more than semantic resemblance and a few lexical explanations: relative chronology and the evidence’s reliability must also be examined. Al-ʿAyn itself defines bakhaʿa nafsahu directly through killing from overwhelming emotion and anger, without making it dependent on neck anatomy.

Intensive slaughter may therefore be an important use, but Qur’anic bākhiʿ must not be restricted to cutting one’s own neck.

3. Other uses and earlier Arabic witnesses

Being overcome by grief

A particularly clear literary witness is a line by Dhū al-Rummah, an Umayyad poet, transmitted in his Dīwān and major lexicons:

أَلَا أَيُّهَذَا الْبَاخِعُ الْوَجْدُ نَفْسَهُ

alā ayyuhādhā al-bākhiʿu al-wajdu nafsahu

بِشَيْءٍ نَحَتْهُ عَنْ يَدَيْهِ الْمَقَادِرُ

bi-shayʾin naḥat-hu ʿan yadayhi al-maqādiru

Translation: “Ah, you whose life grief is destroying over something the decrees of fate have removed from his reach!”

Versions differ, including bi-shayʾ and li-shayʾ, and “from his hands” versus “from your hands.” Al-wajd is transmitted as nominative, functioning as subject, or accusative, expressing cause. A genitive vocalization appears in some dictionary editions, but must not be treated without explanation as equivalent to the subject analysis: where grief is the subject of al-bākhiʿ, al-wajd is nominative.

On the nominative reading, al-wajd is the active participle’s subject: grief itself destroys the person’s life. The expression therefore need not describe a deliberate decision to kill oneself; it can vividly portray grief’s effect on a person.

The witness is useful for understanding the Qur’an because bākhiʿ likewise occurs in a setting of intense grief.

Dhū al-Rummah’s death is reported in 117 AH, and he was an Umayyad poet. The line must therefore not be presented as pre-Islamic or certainly preceding the Qur’an. Its transmission in his Dīwān and citation by lexicographers such as al-Khalīl and Ibn Fāris nevertheless give it value for understanding Arabic reasonably close to revelation’s period.

This investigation did not identify a securely attributed pre-Islamic poetic witness that could replace this line.

Committing one’s entire capacity

Bakhaʿtu laka nafsī wa-nuṣḥī is also recorded: committing all one’s capacity and sincere counsel to someone.

No actual killing is involved. The expression foregrounds intense action and exertion to one’s limit.

In Asās al-Balāghah, al-Zamakhsharī records uses for strenuous effort, sincere counsel and exhausting one’s capacity in a task. Bakhaʿa arḍahu bi-l-zirāʿah describes land worked so continuously that its productive strength is exhausted.

The family can thus describe pressure exerted to the limit of capacity. The land has not been killed; intensive exploitation has depleted its productive strength.

Exhaustion must nevertheless not replace every other sense. Bakhaʿa al-dhabīḥah concerns bodily death, the land use concerns depleted capacity, and bakhaʿa nafsahu from grief invokes destroying one’s life.

Humility, acknowledgment and submission

Al-ʿAyn records bakhaʿa bi-l-ṭāʿah for yielding and submitting, and bakhaʿtu bihi bukhūʿan for acknowledging or accepting something.

These uses concern neither killing nor grief. A person gives up resistance and yields to a due, command or another person.

Ibn Fāris places this branch beside killing, subjugation and humiliation. Complete submission and resistance being overcome can connect them semantically, without proving that all historically developed from killing.

Nor must every submission be reduced to humiliation. Someone may humbly accept a truth without that acceptance being degrading. The object and context establish the act’s ethical character.

This branch is not the sense of Qur’anic bākhiʿ. “You submit yourself” or “you act humbly” therefore does not accurately convey bākhiʿun nafsaka in these verses.

4. The first Qur’anic use: Al-Kahf 18:6

فَلَعَلَّكَ بَاخِعٌ نَفْسَكَ عَلَىٰ آثَارِهِمْ إِنْ لَمْ يُؤْمِنُوا بِهَٰذَا الْحَدِيثِ أَسَفًا

fa-laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka ʿalā āthārihim in lam yuʾminū bi-hādhā al-ḥadīthi asafan

Translation: “Perhaps, then, you would destroy yourself with grief in their wake, if they do not believe in this discourse.”

The Prophet is addressed. Al-Kahf’s opening concerns the Book’s revelation, warning and people claiming that The God has a child. Verse six then portrays the Prophet’s intense grief over their unbelief.

The construction bākhiʿun nafsaka

Laʿalla introduces a nominal clause. The pronoun -ka is its accusative nominal element; bākhiʿun is its nominative predicate.

Nafsaka is the active participle’s object. Grammatically, bākhiʿ identifies the one to whom destroying is attributed, and nafs the self affected by it.

Here the participle portrays a state and the danger of approaching destruction rather than reporting a completed action.

Laʿalla also matters. It must not be treated merely as a prediction of an event’s probability or as ignorance of the future on the part of The God. The expression conveys warning and concern, as though the addressee were cautioned against allowing grief to destroy him.

This accords with passages such as Fāṭir 35:8 and Al-Naḥl 16:127, which restrain the Prophet from consuming himself in grief and regret over others’ behaviour.

The meaning of ʿalā āthārihim

Āthār, plural of athar, can denote a mark or trace left by someone or something.

Here ʿalā āthārihim occurs amid people turning away and refusing belief. “In their wake” can express grief following their departure without requiring bodily pursuit.

The image thus places the Prophet’s grief after their turning away.

It does not establish that the Prophet physically walked after them. “Upon their traces” can be a rhetorical image of grief after their departure and rejection.

This phrase does not supply bākhiʿ’s basic meaning; āthārihim identifies the grief’s setting and direction.

The role of asafan

Asafan concludes the verse and can be analysed as an accusative of cause: intense grief is presented as the reason for destroying oneself.

Exegetical sources record sorrow, regret, distress and sometimes anger for asaf here. Al-Ṭabarī foregrounds grief over people turning away.

The distinction matters: asaf names the inner state and overwhelming emotion; bakhʿ portrays the terrible endpoint toward which such grief could carry someone.

They are therefore not complete synonyms.

Translating bākhiʿ merely as “sad” loses the image’s force. Restricting it without explanation to suicide, conversely, imposes a direct intention to kill oneself that the context does not establish.

What does bi-hādhā al-ḥadīth refer to?

The surah opens with the Book revealed to the Prophet. “This discourse” in verse six therefore refers to that revealed speech.

Ḥadīth here is not the later technical category of compiled collections of prophetic reports. Its direct meaning is speech or discourse in which the audience is being called to believe.

“This discourse” is a natural translation, with commentary explaining its Qur’anic referent in context.

This prevents a later technical sense from replacing the Qur’anic word.

5. The second Qur’anic use: Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:3

لَعَلَّكَ بَاخِعٌ نَفْسَكَ أَلَّا يَكُونُوا مُؤْمِنِينَ

laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka allā yakūnū muʾminīna

Translation: “Perhaps you would destroy yourself because they do not become believers.”

This is shorter than Al-Kahf 18:6, but retains the same principal construction: laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka.

The Prophet is again addressed, and people’s unbelief is again the concern.

The meaning of allā yakūnū muʾminīn

Allā consists of the nominalizing particle an and negative lā. The phrase means their not being believers or not coming to believe.

It expresses the reason for distress. The verbal noun understood from an and its verb can be analysed causally: because they are not believers.

Al-Qurṭubī likewise transmits al-Zajjāj’s analysis treating the expression semantically as an accusative of cause.

Unlike Al-Kahf 18:6, this verse has no separate asafan. Its absence does not imply an absence of grief: bākhiʿun nafsaka amid people’s unbelief portrays the Prophet’s intense distress.

Bākhiʿ must nevertheless not itself be defined as grief. It portrays bringing down or destroying oneself, whose cause the sentence identifies.

The connection with Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4

The following verse says that, if The God wills, He can send them a sign from heaven before which their necks become bowed in submission.

This continuation matters. The address concerns both the Prophet’s intense feeling and the limits of his power over others’ choices and responses.

The Prophet is responsible for conveying the message, but exhausting his own life cannot guarantee that people believe.

This relates to Yūnus 10:99 and Al-Baqarah 2:256, concerning faith, human choice and the rejection of compulsion.

Freedom of belief or rejection of coercion must nevertheless not be derived from B KH ʿ itself. These concerns follow from the passages together and their relationships.

Comparing the two Qur’anic uses

The verses share a semantic core, while expressing it differently.

Al-Kahf 18:6 develops the image: in the wake of people turning away, the Prophet is portrayed as nearly destroying himself through grief if they do not believe in this discourse.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:3 is more compressed, directly identifying their unbelief as the cause of distress.

Āthārihim and asafan make the grief image more concrete in Al-Kahf. Al-Shuʿarāʾ’s following verse emphasizes that others’ belief cannot be made certain through the Prophet’s effort or suffering alone.

These are not independent definitions of bākhiʿ. Each verse applies one shared meaning within its own context.

6. Distinctions from neighbouring roots

B KH ʿ (bakhʿ) and Q T L (qatl)

Qatl directly concerns killing or ending life.

Bakhʿ can also mean killing, but the witnesses examined foreground intensity, carrying an act to its limit and bringing down.

In a grief context, bākhiʿun nafsaka therefore becomes a powerful image of destroying one’s own life.

This does not mean bakhʿ is always a degree more intense than qatl. The evidence establishes no fixed hierarchy across all their uses.

Study of Q T L

B KH ʿ (bakhʿ) and H L K (halāk)

Halāk concerns destruction or perishing, and applies in the Qur’an to people, communities and other subjects.

“Destroying yourself” can render bākhiʿun nafsaka in these verses, retaining the Arabic image’s intensity.

This equivalent must not restrict every bakhʿ use to halāk. Strenuous exertion, depleted capacity and submission are also recorded in the language’s history.

Study of H L K

B KH ʿ (bakhʿ) and A S F (asaf)

They occur together in Al-Kahf 18:6.

Asaf is intense inner emotion, sorrow or anger according to context. Bakhʿ here portrays life being destroyed under that emotional pressure.

Translation should preserve both contributions: the cause and the terrible outcome being pictured.

Study of A S F

B KH ʿ (bakhʿ) and Ḥ S R (ḥasrah)

Ḥasrah concerns regret, sorrow and grief over what has been lost or not attained.

Fa-lā tadhhab nafsuka ʿalayhim ḥasarātin in Fāṭir 35:8 is thematically close to both bākhiʿun nafsaka verses.

Ḥasarāt foregrounds the sorrow and regret themselves; bakhʿ concerns the image of the self being exhausted or brought down.

They may accompany one another in experience, but are lexically distinct.

Study of Ḥ S R

B KH ʿ (bakhʿ) and KH Ḍ ʿ (khuḍūʿ)

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4 immediately follows bākhiʿun nafsaka with khāḍiʿīn.

Khuḍūʿ concerns humility and yielding; bakhʿ in the preceding verse portrays destroying oneself.

Although submission and humility are also recorded for bakhʿ, this overlap does not make the roots equivalent in these two verses.

Verse three concerns the Prophet’s intense grief; verse four concerns people’s necks bowing before a sign.

Study of KH Ḍ ʿ

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The image’s intensity should remain, without making the verse sound like a report of the Prophet’s deliberate decision to end his life.

In a grief context, bakhaʿa nafsahu may be rendered “destroyed himself,” “died of overwhelming grief” or “brought himself to destruction.”

“Exhausted himself” conveys part of the meaning but softens bākhiʿ and weakens the final image of killing or destruction.

“Committed suicide” in contemporary English normally implies a deliberate act of self-killing. That is not the preferred contextual interpretation of these verses, so it is better avoided as the basic translation.

For Al-Kahf 18:6, the proposal is:

“Perhaps, then, you would destroy yourself with grief in their wake, if they do not believe in this discourse.”

For Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:3:

“Perhaps you would destroy yourself because they do not become believers.”

“Destroy yourself” preserves bakhʿ’s intensity, while other sentence elements establish the cause of distress.

“Perhaps” follows laʿalla’s surface construction, but commentary should explain the warning and concern rather than necessarily uncertainty about the future.

Outside the Qur’an, the subject changes the equivalent. Bakhaʿa al-arḍa bi-l-zirāʿah can be “exhausted the land through continuous cultivation”; bakhaʿa lahu nuṣḥahu, “devoted his full capacity to sincere counsel for him.” Acknowledgment and submission instead suit “yielded” or “humbly accepted.”

These differences reflect real variation in use, rather than permission to choose one equivalent arbitrarily for every derivative.

8. Conclusion

B KH ʿ occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both as the active participle bākhiʿun and in the same construction, laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka.

Earlier Arabic uses concern killing, bringing down and carrying an action to its limit. Extended uses include exhausting capacity, strenuous effort, humility and yielding. They are semantically related, but cannot all be derived from a single physical image without adequate historical evidence.

Dhū al-Rummah’s poem shows bākhiʿ applied to grief’s overwhelming effect on someone’s life. This supplies a linguistic witness, separate from later exegetical explanations, supporting that grief-related sense in both Qur’anic verses.

Al-Kahf 18:6 portrays the Prophet so distressed over people turning away and refusing revealed discourse that he seems to destroy himself. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:3 uses the same image more concisely for their unbelief.

The image conveys the Prophet’s intense concern and recalls a limit to the suffering he imposes on himself over others’ choices. Neither verse reports an actual killing or a direct intention to commit suicide.

An appropriate final definition for Forqan:

Arabic B KH ʿ concerns killing, bringing down and carrying an action to its limit; different uses can concern exhausting capacity, intense effort or complete submission. The Qur’an uses it only in bākhiʿun nafsaka, portraying the destruction of one’s own life through overwhelming grief over people’s unbelief. In both contexts this conveys warning and concern for the Prophet’s suffering, rather than reporting suicide.
