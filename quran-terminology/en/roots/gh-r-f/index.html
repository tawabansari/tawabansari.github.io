Scooping by hand, the amount scooped, and elevated chambers: from the test of Saul’s troops to the chambers of Paradise in the Qur’an

The root GH R F has several important uses in early Arabic, two of which occur in the Qur’an: taking up water or another substance with the hand or an implement such as a ladle; and ghurfah, an upper room or elevated chamber in a building.

In the first use, gharafa describes taking a quantity of water, liquid food, or another substance from its container or location. Ightarafa likewise means to scoop something up, particularly with the hand or a specified implement. In this field, ghurfah can denote the amount of water taken in one scoop.

In the second use, ghurfah names a room in the upper part of a building, elevated above the ground or other parts of the structure. Its plurals include ghuraf and ghurufāt.

A connection through raising or elevation is conceivable: water is scooped out and lifted, while an upper room stands at a height. This resemblance alone, however, does not establish that the name of the room developed from scooping water. Such a development would require independent historical evidence.

In the Qur’an, ightarafa and ghurfah occur in Saul’s story for taking a limited amount of water by hand. In four other verses, ghurfah, ghuraf, and ghurufāt describe elevated chambers and the dwellings of the people of Paradise.

A study of this root must distinguish the act of scooping, the amount scooped, and an elevated chamber. Nor should the height of the chambers of Paradise be turned, without evidence, into an inherent moral meaning of the word, a specified spiritual rank, or an architectural structure with unestablished details.

1. Early Arabic usage and lexical evidence

Gharf: scooping water or another substance

In established Arabic usage, gharafa al-māʾa bi-yadihi means that someone scooped up some water with their hand.

This usually involves dipping a hand or implement into water and then taking some of it up.

Gharafa al-maraqa is also used for ladling broth or liquid from a cooking pot.

Here, a portion is separated from a larger whole and taken in the amount the hand or implement can hold.

Someone sitting beside a stream, for example, can cup their hand to take up water and drink. The quantity depends on the capacity of their hand; it is not the whole stream.

Similarly, a ladle may be used to take broth from a pot. This too is gharf, although the implement is a ladle rather than the hand.

The verb is therefore not restricted to a single implement. Both a hand and a ladle can perform the action.

Mighrafah, from this family, names an implement used to scoop water or another liquid.

In everyday Arabic life, especially when taking water from a vessel, well, or storage place, this was a clear and tangible meaning.

Gharfah and ghurfah: the action and its yield

Arabic records a distinction between gharfah, with a fatḥah on ghayn, and ghurfah, with a ḍammah.

Gharfah can indicate a single performance of gharf: scooping water once.

Ghurfah can name the quantity taken by that action, such as the water held in the palm after one scoop.

The distinction can be illustrated by “one act of scooping water” and “one handful of scooped water.”

The first focuses on the action.

The second concerns the quantity of water obtained.

This distinction appears in early lexical explanations, but it should not be made an exceptionless rule for every Arabic dialect and usage. Both forms are known in closely related senses of scooping water, and both are reported in recognized readings of Al-Baqarah 2:249.

In ordinary usage, ghurfah can refer to a palmful of water. Its exact volume is not identical for everyone, since hands differ in capacity.

Thus “a handful of water” conveys a limited amount without imposing a numerical measurement on the verse.

Ghurfah: an upper room

Another Arabic meaning of ghurfah is a room or part of a building situated at a height.

Ghurfah is explained as ʿulliyyah: an upper room or upper chamber.

Such a room may be on an upper storey or above another part of the building.

Imagine a house whose main rooms are at ground level, with another room built above them. That upper room can be called a ghurfah in Arabic.

Here, the room’s elevation and position matter.

The word does not itself specify whether it is built of stone, mudbrick, wood, or another material, how many windows it has, its size, or who lives there.

Nor does its upper position establish that it houses a king, a wealthy person, or someone of religious standing.

An upper room may belong to an ordinary house. The shared feature is its elevated position in the building.

Lexical explanations sometimes also use ghurfah for a very high place. Without contextual evidence, these uses should not become the exclusive meaning of every Qur’anic occurrence.

Do scooping water and an upper room share an origin?

Some lexical explanations connect the two branches through rafʿ, raising or lifting.

In gharf, water is scooped from its location and lifted.

In the architectural use, the chamber is elevated.

This connection helps explain a possible relationship between the senses. Nevertheless, raising cannot be declared the certain original meaning of every branch without examining the full range of uses.

Early Arabic records uses that do not readily fit this image, including cutting hair or part of something, bending or breaking, and names for certain plants and thickets.

It is therefore preferable to define the two Qur’anic senses separately and explain their possible relationship without assuming their historical unity as certain.

A poetic witness attributed to Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm contains these lines:

تَنَامُ عَنْ كُبْرِ شَأْنِهَا فَإِذَا

tanāmu ʿan kubri shaʾnihā fa-idhā

قَامَتْ رُوَيْدًا تَكَادُ تَنْغَرِفُ

qāmat ruwaydan takādu tangharifu

Translation:

“She sleeps untroubled by the great matter concerning her; when she rises slowly, she almost bends or breaks.” The description of a slender waist belongs to the explanation of the second half-line.

In this verse, tangharifu describes bending or almost breaking.

Lexical explanations sometimes gloss it as tatathannā, bending or folding, and sometimes as tanqaṣifu, breaking or being on the verge of breaking.

The image emphasizes the slenderness and suppleness of the woman’s figure.

This witness does not directly establish the meanings of scooping water or an upper room. It instead shows that a sense of bending or breaking occurs within the GH R F family in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The line is transmitted under Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm’s name. The line alone, however, cannot establish the chronological order in which the root’s different senses developed.

Gharīf and gharaf: plant-related uses

In early Arabic, gharīf can also denote a dense growth of trees and vegetation. Some explanations mention reeds, rushes, and similar plants; the word is not restricted to wet environments.

The word occurs in poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā and is explained as a thicket or dense mass of vegetation.

Plant names gharaf and gharf are also recorded in Arabic. Some explanations connect them with vegetation used in tanning hides; other accounts distinguish the tanning plant from a kind of thumām called gharaf.

The descriptions of the plant, its vocalization, and its use in tanning are disputed. They should not be presented as a single, undisputed botanical identification.

Gharafa al-nāṣiyata is also recorded for cutting or trimming the forelock of a person or animal.

These uses show that the GH R F family extends beyond its two Qur’anic senses.

The plant name, hair-cutting, and an upper room cannot be assigned a single certain pictorial origin merely because they share three consonants.

Early Arabic evidence and the Qur’an clarify the meanings of scooping water and an upper room. Establishing precisely when and in what order they arose would require more datable evidence.

2. Qur’anic forms and the root’s central senses

The GH R F family occurs seven times in five Qur’anic verses and can be presented in three morphological groups.

First is the Form VIII verb ightarafa in Al-Baqarah 2:249.

Second are the noun ghurfah and its plural ghuraf, occurring in Al-Baqarah 2:249, Al-Furqān 25:75, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:58, and Al-Zumar 39:20.

Third is the plural ghurufāt in Sabaʾ 34:37.

Ghuraf is a broken plural of ghurfah. Ghurufāt is also a plural of ghurfah with the feminine plural ending; the vowel of the second consonant appears in this plural pattern. Its form alone does not require treating it as a plural of a plural.

This difference in plural formation does not itself determine the number of storeys, the rooms’ size, or the spiritual value of the dwellings.

Other forms, including gharafa, yaghrifu, mighrafah, gharrāf, gharīf, and maghrūf, are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an.

Two distinct central senses appear in Qur’anic usage:

Gharf and ightirāf: taking up a quantity of water or another substance, particularly with a hand or implement that holds it.

Ghurfah in its architectural sense: a room or chamber in an elevated, upper part of a building.

These senses must remain distinct in translation. In Saul’s story, ghurfah concerns an amount of water; in the Paradise passages, it denotes a dwelling.

3. Ightirāf in Saul’s story: testing the troops beside a river

Al-Baqarah 2:249 states:

فَلَمَّا فَصَلَ طَالُوتُ بِالْجُنُودِ قَالَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ مُبْتَلِيكُمْ بِنَهَرٍ فَمَنْ شَرِبَ مِنْهُ فَلَيْسَ مِنِّي وَمَنْ لَمْ يَطْعَمْهُ فَإِنَّهُ مِنِّي إِلَّا مَنِ اغْتَرَفَ غُرْفَةً بِيَدِهِ فَشَرِبُوا مِنْهُ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِنْهُمْ

fa-lammā faṣala ṭālūtu bi-l-junūdi qāla inna allāha mubtalīkum bi-naharin fa-man shariba minhu fa-laysa minnī wa-man lam yaṭʿamhu fa-innahu minnī illā mani ightarafa ghurfatan bi-yadihi fa-sharibū minhu illā qalīlan minhum

Al-Baqarah 2:249

Translation:

“When Saul set out with the troops, he said, ‘The God will certainly test you with a river. Whoever drinks from it is not of me, and whoever does not taste it is of me, except someone who scoops a handful with his own hand.’ Then they drank from it, except a few of them.”

This verse continues the account of Saul’s appointment as king and commander of the Children of Israel.

In Al-Baqarah 2:246, a group of Israelite leaders had requested the appointment of a king so that they could fight.

In Al-Baqarah 2:247, their Prophet announces Saul’s appointment, but some object to his eligibility.

After the sign described in Al-Baqarah 2:248, Saul sets out with the troops in Al-Baqarah 2:249, and they face a test beside the river.

Here, ightarafa and ghurfah concern taking a limited amount of water by hand.

The meaning of ightarafa ghurfatan

Ightarafa is a Form VIII verb meaning to scoop up water or something else.

Ghurfatan expresses the amount taken by this action.

Bi-yadihi specifies the means: the person’s own hand.

Together, these three elements convey a clear meaning.

The person is permitted to take some water with their hand.

Ghurfah cannot therefore mean a room or elevated chamber here. The river, drinking, and the hand establish the sense of scooping water.

Nor does the verse mention a large vessel for taking water. Bi-yadihi indicates that the permitted quantity is taken with the person’s hand.

Thus “a handful of water” is a natural rendering close to the phrase’s meaning.

The two readings gharfah and ghurfah

Recognized readings of this verse include gharfatan, with a fatḥah on ghayn, and ghurfatan, with a ḍammah. The Ḥafṣ reading used for the text here has ghurfatan.

In the familiar lexical explanation, the first form emphasizes one act of scooping, while the second emphasizes the quantity scooped.

The distinction can be expressed as follows:

Gharfah, viewed as an action: one scoop of water.

Ghurfah, viewed as its yield: the amount of water scooped.

Actual Arabic usage nevertheless includes overlap, and the distinction should not be turned into two entirely separate rulings.

In both readings, bi-yadihi connects the permitted quantity with scooping by hand.

The central meaning of the test therefore remains unchanged.

Scooping versus drinking

The verse uses two different verbs: shariba and ightarafa.

Shariba concerns drinking.

Ightarafa concerns taking up water.

A person may scoop water and then drink it, but scooping and drinking are not lexically identical actions.

Here, the test’s rule begins with drinking from the river, while its exception is expressed through taking a limited quantity by hand.

Ightarafa should therefore not be translated merely as “drank,” erasing the distinction between the verbs.

The verb ightarafa alone also does not establish that the person drank all the water taken. Permission to drink the specified amount is understood through the exception’s relationship to the drinking rule and the context of the test.

Was taking a handful of water disobedience?

Saul first says that whoever drinks from the river is not of him, but then states the exception: illā mani ightarafa ghurfatan bi-yadihi.

Taking the permitted amount therefore falls within the command’s exception.

This exception should not be equated with defying the test itself.

Someone who drinks beyond the stated limit differs from someone who keeps to the excepted quantity.

The verse thus distinguishes observing a limit from exceeding it.

The exception does not establish that everyone was equally thirsty or in identical physical condition. Such details are not stated.

Saul’s test and the troops’ responsibility

The verse continues:

فَشَرِبُوا مِنْهُ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِنْهُمْ

fa-sharibū minhu illā qalīlan minhum

Al-Baqarah 2:249

“Then they drank from it, except a few of them.”

This indicates that most of the troops failed to keep to what Saul had prescribed.

The Qur’an nevertheless does not separately explain each individual’s inner reason for acting as they did.

Thirst, fear, lack of self-restraint, or other factors may affect people’s behavior, but determining each person’s intention requires evidence.

Later in Al-Baqarah 2:249, another issue arises: after Saul and the believers with him cross the river, some speak of their inability to confront Goliath and his troops.

By contrast, those who believe they will meet The God recall how often a small group has defeated a larger one by The God’s permission.

This continuity places the river test within the assessment of the troops before they confront the enemy.

The lexical meaning of gharf is not obedience, faith, endurance, or courage itself. Those concepts emerge from the story and the people’s responses.

4. Ghurfah: an elevated dwelling as a reward in Paradise

Al-Furqān 25:75 states:

أُولَٰئِكَ يُجْزَوْنَ الْغُرْفَةَ بِمَا صَبَرُوا وَيُلَقَّوْنَ فِيهَا تَحِيَّةً وَسَلَامًا

ulāʾika yujzawna al-ghurfata bi-mā ṣabarū wa-yulaqqawna fīhā taḥiyyatan wa-salāmā

Al-Furqān 25:75

Translation:

“Those will be rewarded with the chamber for their steadfastness, and will be received there with greetings and peace.”

Here, al-ghurfah does not mean a quantity of scooped water. It denotes an elevated, honored dwelling in Paradise.

The context of reward, residence, and the following verse makes this clear.

Al-Furqān 25:76 states:

خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا حَسُنَتْ مُسْتَقَرًّا وَمُقَامًا

khālidīna fīhā ḥasunat mustaqarran wa-muqāmā

Al-Furqān 25:76

Translation:

“Remaining there: how excellent a place of settlement and residence!”

The chamber is thus a place to dwell.

Does al-ghurfah mean only one room?

Al-ghurfah in Al-Furqān 25:75 is definite and singular.

Its singular form does not establish that all the people of Paradise live in just one particular room.

In Arabic, a definite singular noun can also express a kind or class.

Here, al-ghurfah can name the elevated and valued dwelling of reward without specifying the actual number of chambers or their distribution among individuals.

Other Qur’anic verses use the plurals ghuraf and ghurufāt.

The singular in Al-Furqān therefore does not conflict with the multiple dwellings described elsewhere.

The chamber and steadfastness

The verse states:

بِمَا صَبَرُوا

bi-mā ṣabarū

Al-Furqān 25:75

“For their steadfastness.”

The reward of the chamber is connected with this group’s patience and perseverance.

Qur’anic ṣabr is not restricted to silently enduring suffering and deprivation.

The preceding verses of Al-Furqān describe people who respond thoughtfully to ignorance and abuse, avoid both extravagance and miserliness in spending, observe divine rights and limits, avoid false testimony and idle conduct, and attend consciously to their Lord’s signs.

Their steadfastness belongs within this range of ethical conduct and practical action.

Bi-mā ṣabarū should therefore not become a rule that anyone who endures more physical suffering or poverty in this world necessarily receives a higher chamber in the Hereafter.

The verse rewards the perseverance of the people whose conduct the preceding verses describe.

The chamber, greetings, and peace

The passage continues:

وَيُلَقَّوْنَ فِيهَا تَحِيَّةً وَسَلَامًا

wa-yulaqqawna fīhā taḥiyyatan wa-salāmā

Al-Furqān 25:75

“And will be received there with greetings and peace.”

This shows that the chamber is not merely a building name; in this setting of reward, it is a dwelling accompanied by welcome and peace.

Yet taḥiyyah and salām do not derive from the root GH R F itself.

An elevated dwelling is one meaning; how its inhabitants are welcomed, and their security and comfort, are conveyed by other words.

Ghurfah should therefore not be lexically translated as greetings, security, or tranquility.

5. The chambers of Paradise: residence, security, and chambers above chambers

Chambers for believers who do good

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:58 states:

وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ لَنُبَوِّئَنَّهُمْ مِنَ الْجَنَّةِ غُرَفًا تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا نِعْمَ أَجْرُ الْعَامِلِينَ

wa-alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti la-nubawwiʾannahum mina al-jannati ghurafan tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru khālidīna fīhā niʿma ajru al-ʿāmilīn

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:58

Translation:

“Those who believe and do righteous deeds, We will surely settle in chambers of Paradise beneath which rivers flow, remaining there. How excellent the reward of those who work!”

Here, ghurafan is the plural of ghurfah.

La-nubawwiʾannahum concerns settling people and providing them a place of residence.

The chambers here are therefore dwellings, not quantities of water or acts of scooping.

The verse connects these chambers with faith and righteous action.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:59 then speaks of those who persevere and trust in their Lord.

This connects with Al-Furqān 25:75, since both passages associate a dwelling in Paradise with human perseverance and conduct.

That relationship does not mean that ghurfah itself includes the meanings of faith, righteous action, or patience.

The chamber names a place; faith, righteous action, and patience concern the criteria for receiving it.

Ghurufāt and security

Sabaʾ 34:37 states:

وَمَا أَمْوَالُكُمْ وَلَا أَوْلَادُكُمْ بِالَّتِي تُقَرِّبُكُمْ عِنْدَنَا زُلْفَىٰ إِلَّا مَنْ آمَنَ وَعَمِلَ صَالِحًا فَأُولَٰئِكَ لَهُمْ جَزَاءُ الضِّعْفِ بِمَا عَمِلُوا وَهُمْ فِي الْغُرُفَاتِ آمِنُونَ

wa-mā amwālukum wa-lā awlādukum bi-llatī tuqarribukum ʿindanā zulfā illā man āmana wa-ʿamila ṣāliḥan fa-ulāʾika lahum jazāʾu al-ḍiʿfi bi-mā ʿamilū wa-hum fī al-ghurufāti āminūn

Sabaʾ 34:37

Translation:

“It is not your wealth or your children that bring you near to Us, except for whoever believes and does righteous deeds. Those will receive a multiplied reward for what they have done, and they will be secure in the chambers.”

Al-ghurufāt here is a plural referring to elevated chambers.

Āminūn expresses the condition of those dwelling there.

Security therefore comes from āminūn, not from the lexical meaning of ghurufāt.

An upstairs room in this world may be unsafe. Height and security are thus distinct concepts.

Here the chambers are accompanied by security, and their combination forms part of the description of blessings in the Hereafter.

Wealth and children are not the measure of nearness

The context of Sabaʾ 34:37 matters for understanding the value of these dwellings.

In the preceding verses, some people rely on their abundance of wealth and children to suppose that they are safe from punishment.

The Qur’an responds that wealth and children are not, in themselves, the measure of nearness to The God.

It then connects faith and righteous action with reward.

The chambers here signify the blessings in the Hereafter received by those meeting the stated criteria of faith and conduct.

Their elevation does not establish worldly wealth, social status, or grand architecture as certain signs of a person’s superiority before The God.

On the contrary, the context challenges such a measure of worth.

Chambers above other chambers

Al-Zumar 39:20 states:

لَٰكِنِ الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا رَبَّهُمْ لَهُمْ غُرَفٌ مِنْ فَوْقِهَا غُرَفٌ مَبْنِيَّةٌ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ وَعْدَ اللَّهِ لَا يُخْلِفُ اللَّهُ الْمِيعَادَ

lākini alladhīna ittaqaw rabbahum lahum ghurafun min fawqihā ghurafun mabniyyatun tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru waʿda allāhi lā yukhlifu allāhu al-mīʿād

Al-Zumar 39:20

Translation:

“But those who are mindful of their Lord will have chambers above which are chambers built, beneath which rivers flow. The God’s promise; The God does not fail the appointed promise.”

This is the clearest Qur’anic use of chambers to convey elevated dwellings arranged above one another.

The first ghuraf refers to elevated chambers.

Min fawqihā ghuraf describes other chambers above them.

Mabniyyah also describes these places as built structures.

Height and chambers positioned above one another are therefore explicit here.

This complete picture should not be extracted from ghurfah alone, since min fawqihā and mabniyyah also contribute to it.

Does the Qur’an specify the number of storeys in Paradise?

Al-Zumar 39:20 speaks of chambers above which other chambers are built.

It does not specify their number of storeys, room dimensions, construction methods, materials, or distance from one another.

Nor does this picture alone establish that each storey necessarily corresponds to a specific spiritual degree.

Their height and arrangement may contribute to an appreciation of the reward’s greatness and value. Such reflection should not become a definitive architectural plan of Paradise.

Conversely, uncertainty about the nature of the Hereafter does not justify discarding the verse’s clear image.

The Qur’an speaks of chambers above other chambers. Translation should preserve that image without filling its unknown details with inventions.

The chambers and flowing rivers

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:58 and Al-Zumar 39:20, the chambers are associated with rivers flowing beneath them.

This description connects the dwellings with the landscape and blessing of water.

The rivers’ presence, however, does not derive from the root GH R F itself.

In Al-Baqarah 2:249, this family concerns scooping water from a river. In the Paradise passages, the chambers are dwellings and water is introduced through al-anhār, the rivers.

This association does not establish that the upper-room sense originally developed from taking water from a river.

The presence of water in both settings is insufficient to prove the historical origin of the name ghurfah.

6. Comparison with neighboring roots

GH R F and ʾ KH DH: scooping and taking

Akhdh concerns taking or obtaining something and has a broad semantic range.

A person may take an object, receive property, or gain possession of something.

In its water-related use, gharf is a more specific kind of taking: scooping a quantity of a substance such as water or liquid food.

Picking up a water vessel, for example, is not necessarily gharf.

By contrast, taking some water from the vessel with a hand or ladle is appropriately described as gharf.

Thus, in this sense, scooping involves taking, but not every act of taking is scooping.

GH R F and N Z ʿ: scooping and pulling out

In many uses, nazʿ concerns pulling something out and separating it from its location.

Gharf can also separate a quantity of water or another substance, but it emphasizes scooping by hand or a suitable implement.

Pulling an object from where it is firmly fixed differs from scooping a handful of river water.

The first emphasizes extraction and separation; the second, taking up a portion of a substance.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute rule governing every use of either root.

GH R F and R F ʿ: scooping and raising

Rafʿ concerns lifting or raising something.

Scooping water can involve lifting it, while architectural ghurfah is an elevated chamber.

An understandable conceptual relationship therefore exists between the families.

Rafʿ nevertheless has a broader range, including raising objects, positions, and ranks.

Qur’anic GH R F, by contrast, concerns either taking a limited amount of water or naming an elevated dwelling.

Ghurfah cannot therefore be translated in every occurrence as “something raised.”

A shared idea of height also does not establish a historical relationship between the two roots.

GH R F and ʿ L W: chambers and elevation

ʿUluww concerns being higher, whether spatially or in rank.

Architectural ghurfah names a place in the upper part of a building.

ʿUluww denotes the quality of being high, whereas ghurfah names a particular room or chamber.

In Al-Zumar 39:20, min fawqihā makes the chambers’ relative height more explicit.

Ghurfah and ʿuluww are therefore conceptually related through height but do not mean the same thing.

GH R F and B Y T: chambers and houses

In residential uses, bayt denotes a house or a place where someone stays.

Ghurfah can likewise be part of a dwelling, but its familiar architectural sense emphasizes an upper, elevated position.

A house may have an upper chamber; not every house necessarily does.

Nor is every chamber an independent house with all the facilities of a home.

The Paradise passages present the chambers as dwellings, but the word alone does not transfer every feature of earthly houses to them.

GH R F and GH R Q: scooping and submersion

Gharf and gharq resemble one another in their consonants and possible association with water, but they are distinct roots.

In Al-Baqarah 2:249, gharf concerns taking some water by hand.

Gharq concerns sinking or being submerged in water or something else, particularly when a person or object goes under.

In scooping, water is taken from a river or vessel.

In drowning or submersion, a person or something else sinks into the water.

The difference shows why two shared consonants and the presence of water do not justify identifying the roots with each other.

7. Translation recommendations for Forqan

For ightarafa in Al-Baqarah 2:249, the Persian equivalents for “scoop up” or “take by hand” are suitable.

For ghurfatan bi-yadihi, “a handful with his own hand” or “a handful of water with his own hand” is natural.

Since the verse concerns a river, the full phrase can be rendered:

“Except someone who scoops a handful of water with his own hand.”

This preserves both the limited quantity and the means of taking it.

“One sip” may suggest a little water, but a sip primarily concerns drinking, while the Arabic phrase concerns taking water by hand.

“A handful of water” is therefore closer to the phrase’s semantic structure.

For architectural ghurfah, suitable equivalents include “upper room,” “upper chamber,” and “elevated dwelling,” or the Persian borrowing ghurfeh.

Ghurfeh is familiar in Persian, but today it may suggest an exhibition booth or small sales stall. Its first introduction in the Qur’anic context should therefore clarify that an upper room or elevated chamber is meant.

Al-Furqān 25:75 can be rendered:

“Those will be rewarded with the elevated dwelling for their steadfastness, and will be received there with greetings and peace.”

Or, retaining the chamber terminology:

“Those will be rewarded with the chamber for their steadfastness, and will be received there with greetings and peace.”

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:58:

“Those who believe and do righteous deeds, We will surely settle in chambers of Paradise beneath which rivers flow.”

In Sabaʾ 34:37:

“And they will be secure in the chambers.”

In Al-Zumar 39:20:

“But those who are mindful of their Lord will have chambers above which are chambers built.”

These renderings should preserve the upper-room or elevated-dwelling sense. Without contextual support, “palace,” “spiritual degree,” or “special rank” should not replace it.

Palaces may be large and magnificent buildings, but ghurfah itself does not denote the dimensions, splendor, or amenities of a palace.

Nor should ghurfah in the reward passages become merely “an exalted station,” eliminating the image of a dwelling.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic GH R F family has several branches. Two important senses appear in the Qur’an: taking up a quantity of water or another substance, and a room or chamber in the upper part of a building.

In the first use, gharf and ightirāf concern taking a quantity of water or another substance. Gharfah or ghurfah may name one act of scooping or the amount obtained, with the lexical distinction and overlap explained above.

In the second, ghurfah names an upper, elevated dwelling; ghuraf and ghurufāt name upper rooms or chambers in the plural.

Early Arabic evidence also includes cutting hair, bending or breaking, and names of plants and thickets. This range cautions against reducing every meaning to one image without sufficient historical evidence.

In Al-Baqarah 2:249, ightarafa and ghurfah concern the testing of Saul’s troops. Drinking from the river is distinguished from taking a limited amount by hand. “A handful of water” appropriately conveys the permitted amount.

In Al-Furqān 25:75, al-ghurfah is the rewarded dwelling of those who persevere.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:58, ghurafan denotes the dwellings of believers who do good in Paradise.

In Sabaʾ 34:37, al-ghurufāt are the dwellings of those who receive a multiplied reward through faith and righteous action and are secure there.

Al-Zumar 39:20 describes chambers above other chambers, with rivers flowing beneath them.

These verses present the chamber in the Hereafter as an elevated and valued dwelling. Its value and security emerge from the context of reward, faith, righteous action, mindfulness, and accompanying descriptions, rather than an inherent meaning of virtue or nearness to The God in the root itself.

The root’s Qur’anic definition therefore has two branches:

In its scooping branch, GH R F denotes taking a quantity of water or another substance by hand or a specified implement. Ghurfah can name the amount taken. In the architectural branch, ghurfah is an upper room or elevated chamber, with ghuraf and ghurufāt as plurals. A possible relationship through lifting or elevation is intelligible, but a single historical origin is not certain.

For Persian Forqan, equivalents of “scoop up” and “a handful of water” suit Saul’s story, while “chamber,” “upper room,” or “elevated dwelling” suit the Paradise passages.
