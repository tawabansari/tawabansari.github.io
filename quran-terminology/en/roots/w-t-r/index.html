Watr and witr: being odd or single, diminishing someone's due, and things arriving in succession

1. The root in early Arabic

Early Arabic w-t-r has several branches: being odd, diminishing something, causing loss, and successive arrival. Watar also names the bowstring stretched between the ends of a bow.

Numerical witr and watr describe something single or an odd number that cannot be divided into complete pairs.

Two is even, while three is odd: removing a pair leaves one unit.

This is not restricted to one. One, three, five, and other odd numbers can all be called witr.

Another branch uses watarahu for diminishing someone's rights or property and depriving them of something.

The family also described the loss and blood vengeance arising when someone killed another person's relative.

Tirah and witr in such uses concern injury, violated rights, and blood vengeance.

Another branch concerns things following one another. Muwātarah and tawātur describe successive arrivals or actions.

Intervals may occur between successive events; muwātarah need not mean events occurring without the slightest gap.

A poetic witness attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

A poem attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā states:

نَجَاءٌ مُجِدٌّ لَيْسَ فِيهِ وَتِيرَةٌ

najāʾun mujiddun laysa fīhi watīratun

وَتَذْبِيبُهَا عَنْهَا بِأَسْحَمَ مِذْوَدِ

wa-tadhbībuhā ʿanhā bi-asḥama midhwadi

“Swift, persistent flight with no slackening, and her warding attackers away with a dark, defensive horn.”

The poet describes an animal moving swiftly and defending itself with its horn.

Watīrah here concerns a pause, slowing, or interruption in movement.

Laysa fīhi watīrah emphasizes movement without slackening or stopping.

The witness attests forms in this family concerning intervals, interruptions, and the manner in which movement continues.

The verse is recorded in the collection attributed to Zuhayr and early Arabic linguistic sources. Versions differ in the closing expression, but watīrah and its meaning are clear.

Zuhayr was pre-Islamic, although this verse is not independently dated. It illustrates old usage without establishing the exact date of its origin.

A poetic witness attributed to al-Aʿshā

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, states:

عَلْقَمُ مَا أَنْتَ إِلَى عَامِرِ

ʿalqamu mā anta ilā ʿāmiri

النَّاقِضِ الْأَوْتَارِ وَالْوَاتِرِ

al-nāqiḍi al-awtāri wa-al-wātiri

“ʿAlqamah, where do you stand beside ʿĀmir, who settles blood claims and himself inflicts loss?”

Al-awtār here concerns bloodshed, injuries, and claims arising from harm.

Al-wātir is someone who causes another such loss or injury.

This differs from numerical oddness. The poet discusses hostile relations, harm, and blood vengeance in tribal society, not odd and even numbers.

The witnesses checked read al-nāqiḍ rather than al-nāqim; al-nāqiḍ al-awtār concerns settling or undoing blood claims through vengeance. The poem's exact date is unclear, since accounts of praising ʿĀmir and criticizing ʿAlqamah also concern the period close to Islam's emergence.

It is evidence of early poetic language and social usage, not an independently dated pre-Quranic document.

Historical findings

Early witnesses and Arabic linguistic sources attest at least four uses:

Being odd or single; diminishing and depriving; loss and blood vengeance; and successive arrival with intervals.

Watar, the bowstring, was also a familiar physical usage.

Some branches can be conceptually connected: deprivation entails losing something, and removing an item from a set may leave it unpaired.

This resemblance does not prove that every meaning historically developed from one original image.

A definition should preserve the attested branches rather than explain everything through an indistinct generality.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded forms include:

  • وَتْر، وِتْر: watr, witr; odd, single; elsewhere, violated rights or blood vengeance.
  • وَتَر: watar; bowstring stretched between a bow's ends.
  • أَوْتَار: awtār; bowstrings or blood claims, according to context.
  • وَتَرَ: watara; he diminished someone's rights or property; elsewhere, made someone single or unpaired.
  • يَتِرُ: yatiru; he diminishes, deprives.
  • أَوْتَرَ: awtara; he made a number odd; elsewhere, strung a bow.
  • وَتَّرَ: wattara; he tightened or stretched a bowstring.
  • تَوْتِير: tawtīr; stretching and tightening a bowstring.
  • وَاتَرَ: wātara; he did something successively, in turns.
  • مُوَاتَرَة: muwātarah; successive delivery or action, potentially with intervals.
  • تَوَاتُر: tawātur; successive, repeated occurrence.
  • تَتْرَى: tatrā; one after another, in successive turns.
  • مَوْتُور: mawtūr; someone deprived of a right or loved one, suffering loss or bearing a blood claim.
  • تِرَة: tirah; violated right, injury, or blood claim.
  • وَتِيرَة: watīrah; continuing manner or course; elsewhere, interruption or slackening.
  • وَتَرَة: watarah; the membrane or narrow partition between the nostrils and analogous bodily structures.

Not all these forms occur in the Quran.

The Quran contains three occurrences of the family:

1. Tatrā in Al-Muʾminūn 23:44.

2. Yatirakum in Muḥammad 47:35.

3. Al-watr in Al-Fajr 89:3.

They differ in formation and contextual meaning.

Did all the meanings develop from the bowstring?

Watar, bowstring, is an established physical use.

The string stretches between a bow's ends; its tension helps propel an arrow when released.

This image can illuminate uses involving tension and firmness.

Its existence does not establish that oddness, blood vengeance, and succession all historically derive from this implement.

Such a relationship requires datable evidence showing which use came first and how another developed.

Without it, the main branches should be explained separately.

3. Quranic usage: Al-Muʾminūn 23:44

Messengers one after another

ثُمَّ أَرْسَلْنَا رُسُلَنَا تَتْرَىٰ كُلَّ مَا جَاءَ أُمَّةً رَسُولُهَا كَذَّبُوهُ فَأَتْبَعْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ بَعْضًا وَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ أَحَادِيثَ فَبُعْدًا لِقَوْمٍ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

thumma arsalnā rusulanā tatrā kulla mā jāʾa ummatan rasūluhā kadhdhabūhu fa-atbaʿnā baʿḍahum baʿḍan wa-jaʿalnāhum aḥādītha fa-buʿdan li-qawmin lā yuʾminūn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:44

“Then We sent Our Messengers in succession. Whenever a community's Messenger came to it, they denied him; so We made some follow others and made them tales. Away, then, with a people who do not believe!”

Tatrā describes how the Messengers were sent.

The Quran discusses Messengers sent in different periods and among different communities.

Tatrā means one after another or in successive turns.

Arabic connects it with muwātarah: things arriving or occurring in succession.

The formation of tatrā

The established Arabic analysis derives it from watrā.

On that analysis, initial wāw became tāʾ, producing tatrā.

Comparable substitutions occur in Arabic, but this must not become an unchecked general rule for all words beginning with wāw.

There are also analyses of its grammatical category.

One treats it as a noun or verbal noun functioning as a circumstantial expression qualifying rusulanā.

The sense is: We sent Our Messengers as they came one after another.

Another analysis emphasizes its collective and sequential character.

Either way, tatrā is not an independent verb meaning “We sent.” Arsalnā expresses sending; tatrā describes its order and manner.

Does tatrā mean arrival without intervals?

No. Early muwātarah can describe succession with gaps between occurrences.

Someone may send letters at different times: one arrives, time passes, and another is sent.

The letters come successively, neither simultaneously nor necessarily without interruption.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:44 concerns Messengers throughout communities' history.

Tatrā does not say all were sent without any temporal gap.

Nor does it specify the interval between Messengers.

It may be short or long; the word conveys order and succession.

Was only one Messenger ever sent at a time?

Succession does not imply that God never sent two Messengers simultaneously.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:45 describes Moses and his brother Aaron being sent on one mission.

Historical succession of missions is compatible with more than one Messenger being present together.

Tatrā describes successive sending, not a limit of one Messenger per historical period.

Tatrā and the communities' fate

Al-Muʾminūn 23:42 describes other generations arising after earlier peoples.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:43 states that no community advances or delays its appointed term.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:44 then describes successive Messengers and their rejection by their communities.

The passage depicts continuing guidance and different communities' responses.

Tatrā itself does not mean denying or destroying.

Kadhdhabūhu describes the communities' conduct.

Fa-atbaʿnā baʿḍahum baʿḍan describes their following one another in the fate under discussion.

There are two sequences: the sending of Messengers and the successive fate of the rejecting communities.

Tatrā concerns the first.

4. Quranic usage: Muḥammad 47:35

No diminution of deeds

فَلَا تَهِنُوا وَتَدْعُوا إِلَى السَّلْمِ وَأَنْتُمُ الْأَعْلَوْنَ وَاللَّهُ مَعَكُمْ وَلَنْ يَتِرَكُمْ أَعْمَالَكُمْ

fa-lā tahinū wa-tadʿū ilā al-salmi wa-antumu al-aʿlawna wa-allāhu maʿakum wa-lan yatirakum aʿmālakum

Muḥammad 47:35

“So do not weaken and call for peace while you have the upper hand; The God is with you and will not diminish your deeds.”

Yatirakum belongs to watara, yatiru.

In the branch concerning rights and property, the verb means diminishing, depriving, or taking away something belonging to someone.

Here the Quran states that God will not diminish the addressees' deeds.

The formation of yatirakum

Yatira is a third-person masculine singular imperfect verb of the basic form.

Preceding lan makes it subjunctive and negates future occurrence.

Its subject is an implicit pronoun referring to God.

Attached kum is the plural pronoun and first object.

Aʿmālakum is the second object, identifying what is not diminished.

The construction thus has two sides: the people who might be deprived and the thing subject to diminution.

“He will not diminish your deeds” conveys the sentence naturally.

Are the deeds themselves or their reward diminished?

Once a person has acted, the occurrence belongs to the past.

Its result, value, and reward can nevertheless be assessed and judged.

Muḥammad 47:35 promises no diminution of the addressees' deeds.

The following verse, Muḥammad 47:36, mentions rewards and says they will receive them if they believe and remain mindful.

No diminution of the fruits and reward of deeds therefore fits the immediate context.

Direct translation and contextual explanation should remain distinct.

Aʿmālakum means “your deeds”; the Arabic does not instead say “the reward of your deeds.”

The base translation can preserve “your deeds,” with the connection to reward explained separately.

Yatirakum and tubṭilū

Muḥammad 47:33 commands believers not to invalidate their deeds.

That expression belongs to b-ṭ-l and concerns invalidating or rendering ineffective.

Muḥammad 47:35 instead uses yatirakum concerning diminution of deeds.

The meanings approach one another but are not complete synonyms.

Invalidation emphasizes loss of validity or result.

Diminution concerns reduction or deprivation.

Paying a worker less than the agreed wage diminishes part of their due.

Declaring all their work worthless and recognizing no entitlement goes beyond a partial reduction.

This illustrates a semantic distinction, not an exact equivalence between divine reward and human financial contracts.

The command and promise in context

Muḥammad 47:35 begins by prohibiting weakening and calling for peace when the addressees have the upper hand.

The setting is confrontation and warfare.

Muḥammad 47:4 also discusses conduct in warfare and its ending.

Al-Anfāl 8:61, meanwhile, commands acceptance of peace when the other side inclines to it.

Muḥammad 47:35 must therefore not become an absolute prohibition of every peace agreement.

In its particular context, the promise addresses anxiety that effort and perseverance will yield nothing.

Watara here does not mean fighting, victory, or peace.

It means diminishing or depriving.

5. Quranic usage: Al-Fajr 89:3

Odd in contrast to even

وَالشَّفْعِ وَالْوَتْرِ

wa-al-shafʿi wa-al-watr

Al-Fajr 89:3

“And by the even and the odd,”

Al-watr is paired in contrast with al-shafʿ.

Shafʿ belongs to sh-f-ʿ and numerically concerns being paired or even.

Watr concerns being odd or single.

Two is even because it forms one pair.

Three is odd because forming a pair leaves one unit.

Here watr does not mean loneliness or abandonment; it describes a numerical or qualitative feature.

Does watr mean only one?

No. Arabic can apply it to any odd number.

One, three, five, and other odd numbers belong to this semantic field.

Translating al-watr as “one” unnecessarily restricts the meaning.

“Odd” or “single” is more suitable.

What is the oath by?

Al-Fajr 89:1 swears by dawn.

Al-Fajr 89:2 mentions ten nights.

Al-Fajr 89:3 swears by the even and odd.

Al-Fajr 89:4 mentions the passing night.

Time, night, and the order of events feature in this sequence.

Different referents have been proposed for shafʿ and watr.

Some relate them to particular days of annual rites.

Others connect shafʿ with paired creatures and watr with God's oneness.

Others understand the broader meanings of even and odd.

The verse itself names no particular day, rite, or class of beings.

These proposals should not replace the direct words without independent evidence.

Watr and God's oneness

God's oneness is explicit in the Quran.

Al-Ikhlāṣ 112:1, for example, describes God as aḥad.

An interpretive reading may therefore connect watr with divine oneness and shafʿ with pairs in creation.

This conceptual connection can be discussed.

Watr is not, however, an exclusive name for God in Arabic.

Three is also odd, although it is not God's exclusive name or description.

General oddness must be distinguished from its application to divine oneness.

Likewise, shafʿ here does not establish that every created being must have one specific numerical and physical counterpart.

Does watr name a particular prayer here?

Islamic devotional usage calls a prayer with an odd number of units witr.

Al-Fajr 89:3 itself does not mention prayer, units, or a method of worship.

The technical prayer meaning cannot simply replace the verse's lexical meaning.

Connecting the oath with a particular act of worship requires separate interpretive discussion.

“By the even and the odd” is clearer and more faithful as a base translation.

6. Connections among the three Quranic uses

The three occurrences concern apparently different subjects.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:44 describes Messengers sent successively.

Muḥammad 47:35 concerns diminishing or depriving the addressees of their deeds' fruits.

Al-Fajr 89:3 places odd against even.

Separation or remaining unpaired can help explain their conceptual relationship.

In the numerical branch, pairing leaves one unit over.

In diminution, something is taken from someone's due or property, leaving them deprived.

In muwātarah, events occur one after another in distinct turns.

This can make their semantic proximity tangible.

It is not a proven historical origin for every meaning.

The bowstring also belongs to the family; connecting it historically with oddness or deprivation requires independent evidence.

The final definition should not reduce everything to a vague phrase such as “something separating.”

Each branch has its own established meaning.

7. Comparison with related roots

w-t-r and sh-f-ʿ: odd and even

Both words occur together in Al-Fajr 89:3.

Numerical shafʿ concerns being paired.

Watr expresses the contrasting oddness.

Quranic sh-f-ʿ also has other uses, including intercession.

That broader usage cannot be restricted to numerical pairing.

Nor do all meanings of w-t-r arise from contrast with shafʿ.

Their clear contrast in Al-Fajr 89:3 concerns even and odd.

w-t-r and n-q-ṣ: diminishing and decreasing

Quranic naqṣ concerns decrease and reduction.

Watara in Muḥammad 47:35 concerns diminishing someone's due or the fruits of their action.

The meanings overlap.

Arabic watara can particularly emphasize deprivation of something belonging to a person.

If an accident reduces someone's property, a decrease occurs.

If someone wrongfully takes part of their due, deprivation of a right occurs as well.

This distinction is useful for rights-related contexts, but naqṣ need not lack an agent, nor must every watara involve deliberate injustice.

Muḥammad 47:35 itself promises no diminution of deeds.

w-t-r and b-kh-s: deprivation and shortchanging

Quranic bakhs concerns giving less than due or disregarding a right or value.

Hūd 11:85 prohibits diminishing people's things.

This approaches watara's rights-related branch.

Quranic bakhs commonly concerns shortchanging, undervaluing, or disregarding others' rights.

W-t-r has a wider field including oddness and succession.

The roots cannot replace one another under a single definition.

w-t-r and t-b-ʿ: succession and following

Tabiʿa concerns going after or following something.

Tatrā in Al-Muʾminūn 23:44 also concerns succession.

The concepts are not identical.

Caravans may pass along a road successively without the second obeying the first caravan's commands or beliefs.

Conversely, a person may follow another without having lived after them.

Tatrā describes the order of sending Messengers, not their unthinking imitation of one another.

w-t-r and w-l-y: tawālī and muwātarah

Tawālī, from w-l-y, concerns following in succession and continuing.

Muwātarah also involves succession, but lexicographers identify intervals as a familiar feature.

Several letters may be sent days apart.

They arrive successively without being dispatched simultaneously or without gaps.

Tatrā in Al-Muʾminūn 23:44 does not mean all Messengers were sent simultaneously or revelation continued without any historical interval.

w-t-r and ʾ-l-t: no diminution of deeds

Al-Ṭūr 52:21 uses alatnāhum concerning no diminution of deeds.

Yalitkum in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:14 occupies the same semantic field.

These are close to yatirakum in Muḥammad 47:35.

The root w-t-r remains distinct from ʾ-l-t and associated forms.

All three passages concern no diminution of deeds, but precise distinctions require separate analysis of their verbs and objects.

A shared translation, “will not diminish,” does not establish identical roots or usage.

w-t-r and w-t-d: oddness and pegs

The roots share their first two consonants.

Quranic w-t-d appears as awtād, pegs.

W-t-r in Al-Fajr 89:3 means odd or single.

Watar can also name a bowstring, which is not a tent peg.

A tent peg secures a rope; a bowstring stretches between the bow's ends.

Both may involve tension and firmness, but that does not merge their roots.

w-t-r and w-ṭ-r: oddness and a need

Waṭar belongs to w-ṭ-r and occurs in Al-Aḥzāb 33:37.

There it concerns a need, desire, or matter that a person has fulfilled.

Watr belongs to w-t-r and expresses oddness in Al-Fajr 89:3.

The different consonant must not be overlooked.

Similar spelling or sound does not transfer “need” or “desire” to watr.

8. Interpretive and translation challenges

Is the entire root about loneliness?

Being single is an established branch but does not explain every use.

Numerical watr expresses oddness.

Yatirakum concerns diminishing and depriving.

Tatrā expresses succession.

Watar also names the bowstring.

“Loneliness” is therefore too restrictive for the whole family.

Preserve the branches and explain possible connections only as far as the evidence allows.

Does tawātur mean conclusive proof of a report?

In hadith studies and some historical discussions, a mutawātir report is one transmitted through multiple channels.

That is a technical usage defined in later disciplines.

Lexical tawātur concerns successive, repeated arrival.

Tatrā in Al-Muʾminūn 23:44 concerns Messengers, not a report's number of transmitters or historical reliability criteria.

The technical report meaning must not replace Quranic tatrā.

Is watr in Al-Fajr 89:3 certainly God's name?

God's oneness is clear in the Quran and can be discussed in relation to singleness.

Watr is not exclusively a name for God.

Arabic also uses it for odd numbers and other single things.

Identifying its referent conclusively as God goes beyond the direct meaning and requires independent contextual or Quranic evidence.

Does no diminution of deeds mean equal reward for everyone?

No. Muḥammad 47:35 concerns the addressees' deeds not being diminished.

Elsewhere the Quran connects deeds' value with faith, mindfulness, intention, justice, and responsibility.

The promise does not give everyone identical results regardless of their actions and circumstances.

The same surah distinguishes worthy deeds from actions that become fruitless.

Just reckoning is different from identical outcomes for different actions.

Is every reduction unjust?

No. Reduction can result naturally from events or occur on legitimate grounds.

One branch of watara concerns depriving someone of a right or possession.

When such deprivation is wrongful, injustice also occurs.

Its moral character depends on the object, agent, and circumstances; not every quantitative reduction is automatically injustice.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents suit established forms.

وَتْر، وِتْر: watr, witr; odd, single; elsewhere, violated right or blood claim.

وَتَر: watar; bowstring, in archery contexts.

أَوْتَار: awtār; bowstrings; elsewhere, blood claims or violated rights.

وَتَرَ: watara; diminished someone's rights or property, deprived them.

يَتِرُ: yatiru; diminishes, deprives.

أَوْتَرَ: awtara; made odd; elsewhere, strung a bow.

وَتَّرَ: wattara; stretched or tightened a bowstring.

تَوْتِير: tawtīr; stretching and tightening the string.

وَاتَرَ: wātara; did or sent successively.

مُوَاتَرَة: muwātarah; successive arrival or action, potentially with intervals.

تَوَاتُر: tawātur; successive, repeated occurrence.

تَتْرَى: tatrā; one after another, successively.

مَوْتُور: mawtūr; deprived of a right or loved one; specifically, bearing a blood claim.

تِرَة: tirah; violated right, injury, or blood claim.

وَتِيرَة: watīrah; continuing course or manner; elsewhere, interruption or slackening.

Translating the three Quranic occurrences

Al-Muʾminūn 23:44 — تَتْرَى

“In succession.”

Proposed Forqan translation:

“Then We sent Our Messengers in succession.”

This is natural and appropriate. Explanation can clarify that succession need not exclude intervals between missions.

Muḥammad 47:35 — يَتِرَكُمْ

“He deprives you”; with lan in the complete sentence, “He will not diminish your deeds.”

Proposed Forqan translation:

“So do not weaken and call for peace while you have the upper hand; The God is with you and will not diminish your deeds.”

This preserves the verb's relationship to the addressees and their deeds.

Al-Fajr 89:3 — الْوَتْرِ

“The odd” or “the single.”

Proposed Forqan translation:

“And by the even and the odd,”

This clearly conveys the numerical and descriptive contrast without imposing an unsupported referent.

10. Conclusion

Arabic w-t-r is a family with several branches.

First, witr and watr concern being odd or single: one unit or an odd number such as three or five.

Second, watara can express diminishing a right, deprivation, or causing loss, connected in social usage with injury and blood vengeance.

Third, muwātarah and tawātur concern succession in separate turns, potentially with intervals.

Watar, bowstring, is also a physical usage, but its historical relationship to all other branches is not conclusively established.

The Quran contains three distinct occurrences.

Tatrā in Al-Muʾminūn 23:44 expresses the successive sending of Messengers.

Yatirakum in Muḥammad 47:35 occurs in the promise not to diminish the addressees' deeds.

Al-watr in Al-Fajr 89:3 means odd or single in contrast to al-shafʿ.

A shared root does not make its words identical in meaning in every sentence.

Final Forqan definition:

Numerical w-t-r concerns oddness or singleness; its deprivation branch concerns diminishing a right or depriving someone of something; its sequential branch concerns things arriving or occurring one after another, potentially with intervals.

The bowstring and usages concerning loss and blood vengeance also belong to the family, but insufficient historical evidence prevents reducing all branches to one certain origin.

For Quranic translation, “in succession,” “diminish,” and “odd” convey the respective derivatives in their specific contexts.
