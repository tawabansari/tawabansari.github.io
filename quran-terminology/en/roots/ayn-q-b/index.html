Qur’anic root ع ق ب

Heel, succession, replacement and outcome: from consequence and punishment to following up and crossing the difficult pass

The three-letter root ع ق ب encompasses a broad Qur’anic family of words, most of which concern coming after, following, and what happens after something else. The heel lies at the back of the foot; a child comes after a parent; an outcome becomes apparent after an action; and punishment can be a response that follows someone’s conduct. Following up also involves pursuing and continuing after something. Within this same family, however, عَقَبَة denotes a difficult mountain pass or arduous mountain path. This use should not be reduced to succession without evidence.

In the Qur’an, this root expresses turning back from a course someone has taken, a statement enduring among later generations, a good or bad outcome, punishment for conduct, a proportionate response to harm, coming in succession, and crossing a difficult path. This breadth means that translating the root simply as “outcome” or “punishment” cannot explain it. Each derivative needs to be considered within its own form and context.

This root occurs 80 times in the Qur’an; the proper name يَعْقُوب (Jacob) is not included in this count of the root’s Arabic uses. The principal Qur’anic forms in this family are عَقِب, أَعْقَاب, عَاقِبَة, عُقْب, عُقْبَى, عِقَاب, عَاقَبَ, أَعْقَبَ, يُعَقِّبْ, مُعَقِّب, مُعَقِّبَات and عَقَبَة. Differences such as singular and plural, active and passive voice, an attached pronoun, or a case ending do not create a new independent derivative.

Arabic also has words such as عُقُوبَة, تَعَاقُب, تَعْقِيب, عَقِيب and عُقْبَة meaning a turn, but not all of these occur in the Qur’an in these forms.

1. The semantic core and the root in early Arabic

One thing coming or standing after another

One clear meaning of ع ق ب in early Arabic is one thing coming after another. In عَقَبَ هَذَا ذَاكَ, something follows or takes the place of something else. عَقِيب designates someone or something that comes after another, while تَعَاقُب refers to coming in succession or taking one another’s place.

Night and day provide a concrete example. Night follows day and day follows night; each replaces the other. Arabic expresses this succession with verbs from the ع ق ب family.

The same meaning appears in everyday life. Two travellers may take turns riding one animal: one dismounts and the other takes their place. Each person’s turn riding has been called an عُقْبَة. Here the subject is a turn that follows another turn, rather than a difficult mountain pass.

Lexical accounts of livestock keeping also describe camels that approach a watering place in turns or change their grazing grounds. One group drinks and moves aside, and another takes its place. These examples show concrete applications of succession to movement, travel, livestock keeping and the division of turns in Arabic lexical records.

عَقِب also denotes the back of the foot or heel. Expressions for walking after someone and turning backwards are formed with this word. The connection between the heel and coming behind is intelligible, but this does not establish that every meaning of the root historically originated in the name for the heel.

Usage in pre-Islamic poetry

In one established reading of Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah, the description of a horse includes:

«عَلَى الْعَقْبِ جَيَّاشٌ كَأَنَّ اهْتِزَامَهُ
إِذَا جَاشَ فِيهِ حَمْيُهُ غَلْيُ مِرْجَلِ»

“Through repeated runs, the horse remains vigorous; its roaring, when its strength surges, is like the boiling of a cauldron.”

On this reading, الْعَقْب refers to one run following another. As the horse continues moving, it does not lose its strength and continues running vigorously.

The verse nevertheless has variant readings. Some transmissions of the Muʿallaqah give عَلَى الذَّبْلِ or عَلَى الضَّمْرِ in place of عَلَى الْعَقْبِ. Even explanations of عَلَى الْعَقْبِ include the possibility of urging the horse with the heel. This is useful evidence for early usage, but its textual and interpretive differences should remain visible.

A poem by the pre-Islamic poet Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī contains:

«فَلَنْ يَجِدَ الْأَقْوَامُ فِينَا مَسَبَّةً
إِذَا اسْتُدْبِرَتْ أَيَّامُنَا بِالتَّعَقُّبِ»

“People will find no fault in us, even when our past days are examined through pursuit and review.”

Here التَّعَقُّب concerns returning to past deeds and examining them again. The speaker says that even if others follow up their previous conduct and reassess it, they will find nothing deserving reproach.

This use extends following from bodily movement to investigating and pursuing a matter. A person can follow another’s footsteps, but can also pursue a report, an action or a judgement and question it again.

عَقَبَة and the branch concerning difficulty

Alongside succession, lexical sources record another field under ع ق ب: elevation, hardness and difficulty. A clear example is عَقَبَة, a mountain pass that is difficult to ascend and cross.

An عَقَبَة can be a narrow, difficult path in the mountains that requires effort to traverse. In this use, difficulty belongs to the path itself, rather than to the temporal order of two events.

Some lexicographers group these two fields under one entry while distinguishing succession from hardness. That distinction matters: there is no need to explain every use of a root through a single image. This classification also appears in dictionaries compiled after the period of revelation; the lexical classification itself does not establish when the branches arose or whether they have independent etymological origins.

Arabic also uses العَقَب, with fatḥah on both ʿayn and qāf, for tough sinew or tendon used to make bowstrings or to bind and secure equipment. Semantically this use is close to hardness and strength, rather than necessarily to outcome or replacement.

The root therefore needs to be considered in two fields: the broad family of succession, pursuit and result; and the family concerning difficulty, hardness and elevation. Shared letters and inclusion under one lexical entry do not alone prove a common historical origin for all these uses.

2. عَقِب and أَعْقَاب: heel, turning back and later generations

Turning back on one’s heels

An important Qur’anic use of this root is انْقَلَبَ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ and related expressions.

Al-Baqarah 2:143, speaking of the qiblah, says:

«وَمَا جَعَلْنَا الْقِبْلَةَ الَّتِي كُنْتَ عَلَيْهَا إِلَّا لِنَعْلَمَ مَنْ يَتَّبِعُ الرَّسُولَ مِمَّنْ يَنْقَلِبُ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ»

“We appointed the qiblah that you previously faced only so that We might know who follows the Messenger and who turns back on his heels.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:143; part of the verse)

عَقِبَيْهِ is the dual of عَقِب with an attached pronoun: his two heels. Yet يَنْقَلِبُ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ is an established expression for turning back from a course someone has taken.

The image is clear: someone has turned towards a path, then turns back and abandons the course they had taken.

The verse concerns following the Messenger and the response to the qiblah. Turning back on one’s heels here therefore concerns withdrawing from the course of following, beyond the physical movement of the feet.

The figurative nature of this expression does not mean that the qiblah itself has no spatial reality. The verse speaks about the qiblah while also using the image of the heel for a change or reversal in religious orientation. These two levels need to be distinguished.

Āl ʿImrān 3:144 says:

«وَمَا مُحَمَّدٌ إِلَّا رَسُولٌ قَدْ خَلَتْ مِنْ قَبْلِهِ الرُّسُلُ أَفَإِنْ مَاتَ أَوْ قُتِلَ انْقَلَبْتُمْ عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِكُمْ وَمَنْ يَنْقَلِبْ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ فَلَنْ يَضُرَّ اللَّهَ شَيْئًا وَسَيَجْزِي اللَّهُ الشَّاكِرِينَ»

“Muḥammad is only a Messenger; Messengers have passed away before him. If he dies or is killed, will you turn back on your heels? Whoever turns back on his heels will not harm The God at all, and The God will reward the grateful.”

(Āl ʿImrān 3:144)

This verse contains both أَعْقَابِكُمْ and عَقِبَيْهِ. The first is plural and the second dual, but both belong to the same expression for turning back.

This use clearly shows that turning back on one’s heels is a verbal image for reversing course. The verse questions constancy or inconstancy in following, rather than describing bodily movement towards the rear.

The same image appears in Āl ʿImrān 3:149, Al-Anʿām 6:71, Al-Anfāl 8:48 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:66.

In Al-Anfāl 8:48, نَكَصَ appears with عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ and expresses retreat. In Al-Muʾminūn 23:66, عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِكُمْ تَنْكِصُونَ occurs in the context of turning away from the signs.

عَقِب itself therefore means heel, while its combination with verbs such as انقلب, رد and نكص conveys turning back or retreating. This meaning of the whole expression should not be extended to every independent use of عقب.

فِي عَقِبِهِ: generations that come after a person

Al-Zukhruf 43:28 says:

«وَجَعَلَهَا كَلِمَةً بَاقِيَةً فِي عَقِبِهِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْجِعُونَ»

“He made it an enduring word among the generations after him, so that they might return.”

(Al-Zukhruf 43:28)

This follows Abraham’s statement rejecting his people’s objects of worship and turning towards the Creator.

عَقِبِهِ here concerns those who descend from Abraham after him, rather than the back of his foot. This use is also established in early Arabic: عَقِب can encompass a person’s children and later descendants.

Its connection to succession is clear: children and later generations come after the earlier generation.

An utterance enduring within a lineage must nevertheless be distinguished from every member of that lineage accepting it. The verse says that the word endured in Abraham’s descendants and expresses the prospect of their returning through لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْجِعُونَ. The word عقب itself therefore cannot establish universal faith or guaranteed guidance for all Abraham’s descendants.

Lexical sources sometimes restrict عقب to the surviving line through sons; other uses refer more broadly to children and later generations. Without a contextual indication, this lexical difference should not restrict the verse to a particular family branch.

Nor does عَقِب here name a religious office or hereditary entitlement; it expresses descent. Faith, imamate and the divine covenant are separate matters, understood through their own verses and expressions.

3. عَاقِبَة, عُقْب and عُقْبَى: good and bad outcomes

ʿĀqibah: what becomes apparent at the end

عَاقِبَة is among the most frequent Qur’anic derivatives of this root. It concerns the end and result of a matter: what becomes apparent after an event or process has run its course.

Āl ʿImrān 3:137 says:

«قَدْ خَلَتْ مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ سُنَنٌ فَسِيرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ فَانْظُرُوا كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الْمُكَذِّبِينَ»

“Ways and precedents have passed before you. Travel through the earth and see what the outcome of those who denied was.”

(Āl ʿImrān 3:137)

Here عاقبت concerns the result and end of the history of people who lived previously. The command to travel through the earth and inspect their outcome calls people to examine what actually happened to them.

Related constructions recur in Al-Anʿām 6:11, Yūnus 10:39 and 10:73, Al-Naḥl 16:36, Al-Naml 27:69, Al-Rūm 30:9 and 30:42, Fāṭir 35:44 and other verses. Some concern the outcome of those who denied, others that of wrongdoers or those who caused corruption.

These uses show that an outcome can become apparent in human history and collective life. عاقبة in the Qur’an is therefore not an exclusive name for the Day of Resurrection or reward in the hereafter.

Each verse must nevertheless be examined for the outcome in question. The word alone does not identify the event, the cause of destruction or how punishment occurs.

Is ʿāqibah always good?

Some Qur’anic uses associate العاقبة with a good result.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:128 says:

«إِنَّ الْأَرْضَ لِلَّهِ يُورِثُهَا مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ وَالْعَاقِبَةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ»

“The earth belongs to The God. He gives it as an inheritance to whichever of His servants He wills, and the good outcome belongs to those who remain mindful.”

(Al-Aʿrāf 7:128; part of the verse)

Hūd 11:49 also says فَاصْبِرْ إِنَّ الْعَاقِبَةَ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ: “Be patient; the good outcome indeed belongs to those who remain mindful.”

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:83, the same expression follows a description of those who seek neither supremacy on earth nor corruption.

In these cases, context indicates a desirable, good outcome. This positive use should not become an inherent and universal definition of the word.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:9 says of a community that defied its Lord’s command:

«فَذَاقَتْ وَبَالَ أَمْرِهَا وَكَانَ عَاقِبَةُ أَمْرِهَا خُسْرًا»

“It tasted the grievous consequence of its conduct, and the outcome of its conduct was loss.”

(Al-Ṭalāq 65:9)

Al-Ḥashr 59:17 also says:

«فَكَانَ عَاقِبَتَهُمَا أَنَّهُمَا فِي النَّارِ خَالِدَيْنِ فِيهَا وَذَٰلِكَ جَزَاءُ الظَّالِمِينَ»

“The outcome for them both was that they would remain in the Fire, and that is the recompense of wrongdoers.”

(Al-Ḥashr 59:17)

In these verses, عاقبت clearly denotes an unpleasant result. Its negative character comes from خُسْرًا and from the description of what happened to the people concerned.

عاقبة can therefore have a good or bad referent. Its association with a good outcome in some established Qur’anic constructions should not be extended to every use of the root.

عُقْبَى: an outcome that can be the Garden or the Fire

Al-Raʿd 13:35 says:

«مَثَلُ الْجَنَّةِ الَّتِي وُعِدَ الْمُتَّقُونَ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ أُكُلُهَا دَائِمٌ وَظِلُّهَا تِلْكَ عُقْبَى الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا وَعُقْبَى الْكَافِرِينَ النَّارُ»

“The description of the Garden promised to those who remain mindful is this: rivers flow beneath it, and its fruit and shade endure. That is the outcome for those who were mindful, while the outcome for those who deny is the Fire.”

(Al-Raʿd 13:35)

This verse is decisive for defining عُقْبَى. The word occurs once for those who remain mindful and again for those who deny. In its first use the outcome is the Garden; in its second, the Fire.

عُقْبَى therefore does not itself mean a good reward or the Garden. It denotes outcome, whose content the other expressions in the verse determine.

In Al-Raʿd 13:22 and 13:24, عُقْبَى الدَّارِ concerns a good outcome. The expression دار and the context help identify the destination; عقبى does not invariably mean a good dwelling in the hereafter in every sentence.

Al-Kahf 18:44 also contains the form عُقْب:

«هُنَالِكَ الْوَلَايَةُ لِلَّهِ الْحَقِّ هُوَ خَيْرٌ ثَوَابًا وَخَيْرٌ عُقْبًا»

“There, protective authority belongs to The God, the True. He is better in reward and better in outcome.”

(Al-Kahf 18:44)

ثَوَابًا and عُقْبًا stand together here. ثواب concerns recompense and reward, while عقب concerns result and outcome. They can be related without being exact synonyms.

وَلَا يَخَافُ عُقْبَاهَا: the outcome or consequence of an act

The end of Al-Shams says:

«وَلَا يَخَافُ عُقْبَاهَا»

“And he does not fear its consequence.”

(Al-Shams 91:15)

This verse follows the account of Thamūd, the killing of the she-camel and their punishment. عُقْبَاهَا refers to the consequence of an act or event, but the subject of يَخَافُ is disputed.

On the more usual reading, the subject is The God: He does not fear the consequence of punishing Thamūd. On another, the expression refers back to the person who killed the she-camel: when doing so, he did not fear its consequence.

There is also a recitation difference. Ḥafṣ reads وَلَا يَخَافُ with wāw, whereas Nāfiʿ, Ibn ʿĀmir and Abū Jaʿfar read فَلَا يَخَافُ with fāʾ.

This difference affects the sentence’s connection to the preceding statement, while عُقْبَى retains the sense of consequence or outcome in both readings. Disagreement over the subject should therefore be distinguished from disagreement over the root’s own meaning.

4. عِقَاب, عَاقَبَ and أَعْقَبَ: from the consequence of conduct to punishment and a proportionate response

ʿIqāb: a response that follows conduct

The Qur’an uses عِقَاب for punishment. In Arabic, عَاقَبَهُ بِذَنْبِهِ means punishing someone for a wrong they committed. In this use, punishment is an unpleasant response that follows earlier conduct.

The pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

«وَمَنْ عَصَاكَ فَعَاقِبْهُ مُعَاقَبَةً
تَنْهَى الظَّلُومَ وَلَا تَقْعُدْ عَلَى ضَمَدِ»

“Whoever disobeys you, punish him with a punishment that restrains the wrongdoer; and do not remain nursing resentment and pent-up anger.”

This verse belongs to the poet’s discussion of a ruler’s treatment of the obedient and disobedient. عَاقَبَ and مُعَاقَبَة clearly express responding to disobedience with a punishment that deters.

This evidence does not define the entire root. Punishment is established in this branch, while عاقبة, عقبى, عقب and عقبة have other uses.

شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ and سَرِيعُ الْعِقَابِ

The Qur’an repeatedly describes The God as شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ.

Āl ʿImrān 3:11, after recalling earlier groups’ denial of the signs and their being seized for their sins, says:

«فَأَخَذَهُمُ اللَّهُ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ وَاللَّهُ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ»

“The God seized them for their sins, and The God is severe in punishment.”

(Āl ʿImrān 3:11; part of the verse)

Here punishment is connected to the people’s previous conduct, and شديد expresses its severity.

Al-Anʿām 6:165 says:

«إِنَّ رَبَّكَ سَرِيعُ الْعِقَابِ وَإِنَّهُ لَغَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“Your Lord is indeed swift in punishment, and He is forgiving, merciful.”

(Al-Anʿām 6:165; the end of the verse)

This expression describes punishment as swift, while forgiveness and mercy appear in the same sentence.

سريع العقاب should not be taken to mean that every sin necessarily receives an immediate, visible punishment at the very moment it occurs. Other Qur’anic verses speak of respite, delay and an opportunity to return. The adjective “swift” needs its own examination, while عقاب continues to denote punishment.

Ghāfir 40:3 also places غَافِرِ الذَّنْبِ, قَابِلِ التَّوْبِ and شَدِيدِ الْعِقَابِ together. Their association does not make punishment The God’s only description or make severe punishment negate the possibility of forgiveness and repentance.

Qur’anic punishment appears both in accounts of historical events and in divine warnings. It should therefore not be treated solely as a name for torment in the hereafter.

وَإِنْ عَاقَبْتُمْ: a proportionate response to harm

Al-Naḥl 16:126 says:

«وَإِنْ عَاقَبْتُمْ فَعَاقِبُوا بِمِثْلِ مَا عُوقِبْتُمْ بِهِ وَلَئِنْ صَبَرْتُمْ لَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لِلصَّابِرِينَ»

“If you retaliate, respond in proportion to the harm inflicted on you; and if you are patient, that is better for those who are patient.”

(Al-Naḥl 16:126)

This verse matters for both language and the regulation of conduct. عَاقَبْتُمْ and فَعَاقِبُوا are active verbs, while عُوقِبْتُمْ is passive.

A mechanically literal English rendering might say: “If you punish, punish to the extent that you were punished.” This can suggest that the people addressed had themselves committed an offence and deserved punishment.

The context does not require that conclusion. The verse concerns responding to harm inflicted on those addressed. “The harm inflicted on you” therefore conveys the intended meaning more clearly in a basic translation.

بِمِثْلِ also matters. The permitted extent of the response is measured against what happened previously. Form III alone does not establish equality between two sides; the verse’s explicit qualification prevents a response exceeding the measure.

The ending presents patience as better. The verse thus distinguishes the possibility of a proportionate response from the superiority of patience.

The same use appears in Al-Ḥajj 22:60:

«ذَٰلِكَ وَمَنْ عَاقَبَ بِمِثْلِ مَا عُوقِبَ بِهِ ثُمَّ بُغِيَ عَلَيْهِ لَيَنْصُرَنَّهُ اللَّهُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَعَفُوٌّ غَفُورٌ»

“That is so. Whoever responds in proportion to the harm inflicted on him and is then wronged again, The God will certainly help him. The God is indeed pardoning, forgiving.”

(Al-Ḥajj 22:60)

ثُمَّ بُغِيَ عَلَيْهِ makes clear that the person concerned is subjected to injustice. In such a context, عُوقِبَ should therefore not be translated as implying that this person deserved punishment.

These two verses clarify an important use: in the language of retaliation and reciprocal response, عاقب can describe responding to aggression. In this construction, عقاب does not necessarily judge the person first harmed to have been guilty.

Punishment for wrongdoing therefore needs to be distinguished from a proportionate response to harm inflicted.

فَعَاقَبْتُمْ: a turn for compensation in a financial matter

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:11 says:

«وَإِنْ فَاتَكُمْ شَيْءٌ مِنْ أَزْوَاجِكُمْ إِلَى الْكُفَّارِ فَعَاقَبْتُمْ فَآتُوا الَّذِينَ ذَهَبَتْ أَزْوَاجُهُمْ مِثْلَ مَا أَنْفَقُوا وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ الَّذِي أَنْتُمْ بِهِ مُؤْمِنُونَ»

“If any financial entitlement concerning wives who have gone to those who deny escapes you, and your turn for compensation then comes, give those whose wives have gone the equivalent of what they spent; and remain mindful of The God in whom you believe.”

(Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:11)

فَعَاقَبْتُمْ has the same Form III structure here, but its subject matter differs from Al-Naḥl 16:126 and Al-Ḥajj 22:60.

These verses concern women going from one community to another, financial entitlements and what was spent on marriage. فَعَاقَبْتُمْ is therefore discussed in connection with a turn for compensation or an opportunity to repay an entitlement.

Linguistic and historical explanations sometimes connect it to gaining spoils or property from the other party; others emphasise taking a turn and reciprocal action.

The root without its context does not specify the kind of property from which compensation must be made. The next clause commands giving those whose wives have left the equivalent of what they spent.

This use should not be limited to “you punished them.” The verse concerns restoring or compensating a financial entitlement in a reciprocal relationship. Such a use is also close to the old meaning of عُقْبَة as a turn, and to one action following another.

فَأَعْقَبَهُمْ نِفَاقًا: a consequence following conduct

Al-Tawbah 9:77 says:

«فَأَعْقَبَهُمْ نِفَاقًا فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ يَلْقَوْنَهُ بِمَا أَخْلَفُوا اللَّهَ مَا وَعَدُوهُ وَبِمَا كَانُوا يَكْذِبُونَ»

“He brought hypocrisy into their hearts as a consequence, lasting until the day they meet Him, because they failed to keep what they had promised The God and because they used to lie.”

(Al-Tawbah 9:77)

The preceding verses describe people who covenanted with The God that, if He gave them of His bounty, they would give charity and be among the righteous. After receiving it, however, they became stingy and turned away.

أَعْقَبَ here concerns something arising after earlier conduct. Hypocrisy in their hearts is presented as a consequence associated with breaking a covenant and lying.

Two analyses of the verb’s subject have been proposed. In one, The God is the subject and places hypocrisy in their hearts following their conduct. In the other, actions such as stinginess and turning away are understood to have brought about this consequence.

This difference does not change the semantic core of أَعْقَبَ. In either analysis, the verb denotes leaving a consequence after previous conduct.

This use should not suggest that Form IV أَعْقَبَ always produces a bad consequence. Arabic also records أَعْقَبَهُ خَيْرًا for something good replacing or being left after something else. The negative result in Al-Tawbah 9:77 comes from نِفَاقًا and the context of covenant breaking.

5. يُعَقِّبْ, مُعَقِّب and مُعَقِّبَات: following, returning and coming in succession

وَلَمْ يُعَقِّبْ: Moses did not return

Al-Naml 27:10 says:

«وَلَّىٰ مُدْبِرًا وَلَمْ يُعَقِّبْ»

“He turned away, retreating, and did not return.”

(Al-Naml 27:10; part of the verse)

This occurs in the account of Moses, when he sees his staff moving in a frightening way.

The same expression recurs in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31.

يُعَقِّبْ is a Form II verb. This construction concerns turning back, returning to a previous situation or following up what has been left behind.

Some lexical explanations render لَمْ يُعَقِّبْ as “did not look behind,” others as “did not return” or “did not pause.” These meanings relate to a similar situation: Moses turned away from the scene and did not stop to turn back or stand still.

Looking behind, returning and pausing nevertheless do not denote exactly the same action. “Did not return” naturally conveys the sentence’s central meaning in English; his fear and hurried movement are supplied by the context.

The verb alone makes no precise statement about how afraid Moses was, how fast he ran or how his body moved.

مُعَقِّبَات: those who come in succession

Al-Raʿd 13:11 says:

«لَهُ مُعَقِّبَاتٌ مِنْ بَيْنِ يَدَيْهِ وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِ يَحْفَظُونَهُ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ»

“For him there are successive guards before him and behind him, who protect him from the command of The God.”

(Al-Raʿd 13:11; part of the verse)

مُعَقِّبَات is a plural from the عَقَّبَ family and refers to people or things that come after or replace one another.

Arabic lexical accounts record words from this family for groups taking turns at a task. For example, guards or groups of camels arriving at a watering place in turns could be described with expressions concerning succession.

Protection is also mentioned in Al-Raʿd, but its meaning comes from يَحْفَظُونَهُ, rather than from مُعَقِّبَات itself.

Must the muʿaqqibāt be angels?

There is an established interpretive disagreement over this verse. In the usual reading, مُعَقِّبَات are angels who take turns watching over a person. This can be compared with Qur’anic expressions for angels who guard and record deeds.

In another reading, معقبات refers to human guards and powerful attendants protecting someone from in front and behind. This possibility has been considered alongside the continuation of this same verse, which speaks of human inability to withstand the will of The God.

Lexically, مُعَقِّبَات is not specific to angels. It denotes coming in succession or following, while the identity of those who do so needs to be determined from context.

يَحْفَظُونَهُ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ also matters in identifying the referent. In one analysis, protection takes place by the command of The God; in another, it concerns protection against what may befall the person. The relation of مِنْ to the protection is disputed here.

مُعَقِّبَات should therefore not be lexically defined as “guardian angels” without examining the context and Qur’anic evidence. That interpretation of the verse may be accepted, but the derivative itself does not require that referent.

Nor does the feminine plural form مُعَقِّبَات mean that the guards are female. A form’s grammatical gender alone does not determine the actual gender of its referents.

لَا مُعَقِّبَ لِحُكْمِهِ: no one overturns the judgement of The God

Al-Raʿd 13:41 says:

«أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّا نَأْتِي الْأَرْضَ نَنْقُصُهَا مِنْ أَطْرَافِهَا وَاللَّهُ يَحْكُمُ لَا مُعَقِّبَ لِحُكْمِهِ وَهُوَ سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ»

“Do they not see that We come to the land, reducing it at its edges? The God judges; no one can overturn or annul His judgement, and He is swift in reckoning.”

(Al-Raʿd 13:41)

مُعَقِّب is a Form II active participle. Lexically it can denote someone who comes after another, follows up their work or acts to change and reverse its result.

لَا مُعَقِّبَ لِحُكْمِهِ here is connected to وَاللَّهُ يَحْكُمُ: after The God’s judgement, no one can reverse or invalidate it.

This use belongs to the same family as مُعَقِّبَات in the same surah, but has a different application. In verse 11, succession is connected to coming in turns and protection; in verse 41, the point is the absence of anyone able to change The God’s judgement.

This expression should not be taken to prohibit questioning, thinking about or examining the meaning of The God’s rulings. The verse concerns the ability to annul or overturn His judgement. Understanding a judgement and changing the judgement itself are separate matters.

6. الْعَقَبَة: the difficult pass and human action

Al-Balad uses another expression from this root:

«فَلَا اقْتَحَمَ الْعَقَبَةَ ۝ وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا الْعَقَبَةُ»

“Yet he did not brave the difficult pass. And what will make you know what the pass is?”

(Al-Balad 90:11–12)

In Arabic, عَقَبَة denotes a difficult mountain path that takes effort to cross. This use concerns elevation, hardness and difficulty.

These two verses use the image of that difficult pass, but the subsequent verses explain what crossing it means:

«فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ ۝ أَوْ إِطْعَامٌ فِي يَوْمٍ ذِي مَسْغَبَةٍ ۝ يَتِيمًا ذَا مَقْرَبَةٍ ۝ أَوْ مِسْكِينًا ذَا مَتْرَبَةٍ ۝ ثُمَّ كَانَ مِنَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالصَّبْرِ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالْمَرْحَمَةِ»

“Freeing a person in bondage; or, on a day of severe hunger, feeding an orphan who is a relative or a destitute person; then being among those who believe and counsel one another to patience and counsel one another to mercy.”

(Al-Balad 90:13–17)

These verses connect the image of the pass to real actions: freeing a person in bondage, feeding someone in need during hunger, and belonging to a community whose members counsel one another to patience and mercy.

The basic translation of عقبة should therefore not turn it into a “psychological problem,” “stages of spiritual development” or an invisible obstacle’s name. The original image is a difficult path, and the verses themselves explain examples of crossing it.

Difficulties do not all have equal moral value in themselves. A person can follow a hard path towards a wrongful aim. In Al-Balad, the value of crossing the pass is understood from the actions subsequently named.

Nor should عقبة be restricted to personal difficulty or an inward feeling. Freeing a person in bondage and feeding someone in need are concrete social actions. The Qur’an places these actions within the image of crossing a difficult pass.

The difference between عَقَبَة and عُقْبَة

Arabic عَقَبَة, with fatḥah on ʿayn and qāf, denotes a difficult pass, whereas عُقْبَة in another use denotes a turn that follows another turn.

Without vowel marks, these two words look identical in writing, but they differ in meaning.

الْعَقَبَة in Al-Balad is an arduous passage. By contrast, عُقْبَة in expressions for taking turns riding or dividing work concerns a turn and succession.

Their shared written appearance should neither lead us to translate the difficult pass as a “turn” nor derive mandatory, consecutive stages for the actions in Al-Balad from the idea of succession.

The Qur’an itself names examples of crossing the pass in this surah. The word’s form alone cannot establish a definite temporal order between freeing someone, feeding, faith and mutual counsel. The relation between these actions needs to be understood through the verses’ full construction.

7. Distinguishing roots and translation choices for Forqan

ع ق ب (عَقَبَ) and أ خ ر (أَخَّرَ)

أخر concerns being later, coming late and delaying. Many uses of عقب likewise concern something coming after something else.

In its constructions, however, عقب can highlight replacement, following, turning back or a consequence arising after an action. آخر can simply express a temporal position or order.

عاقبت is therefore close to “the end,” but need not denote only an event’s final moment; it can be the result or outcome reached after a series of actions.

ع ق ب (عَقَبَ) and خ ل ف (خَلَفَ)

خلف concerns being behind and taking another’s place. In this respect it is close to succession and replacement in عقب.

In Arabic, someone who comes after another can be called their خَلَف or عَقِيب. These two roots nevertheless do not cover each other’s entire semantic field. Qur’anic عقب is also associated with outcome, punishment and the difficult pass, each of which needs separate examination.

Succession itself also establishes neither the successor’s superiority nor their correctness.

ع ق ب (عَاقَبَ) and ج ز ي (جَزَىٰ)

جزاء concerns a response or result given in return for an action. That response can be good or bad. عقاب in its established use more specifically concerns punishment and an unpleasant response.

Al-Ḥashr 59:17 places عَاقِبَتَهُمَا alongside جَزَاءُ الظَّالِمِينَ. The former describes the outcome for the two of them; the latter clarifies the relation of that outcome to wrongful conduct.

Outcome and recompense therefore overlap without being exact synonyms. Every result is not necessarily the moral recompense for an action, and every occurrence of عاقبت does not mean punishment.

ع ق ب (عِقَاب) and ث و ب (ثَوْب)

In Qur’anic use, ثواب concerns recompense and reward, and is opposed to punishment in some contexts. This opposition should not imply that all derivatives of ع ق ب are negative.

Al-Raʿd 13:35 uses عُقْبَى for both those who remain mindful and those who deny. Al-Kahf 18:44 also places ثَوَابًا and عُقْبًا together.

The distinction therefore needs to be made between specific derivatives: Qur’anic عِقَاب concerns punishment, while عاقبة and عقبى can denote a good or bad outcome.

The difference between عِقَاب and عُقَاب

Arabic عُقَاب, with ḍammah on ʿayn, is also the name of a bird of prey. Lexical sources record it, but the Qur’an does not use it as that bird’s name.

By contrast, Qur’anic عِقَاب, with kasrah on ʿayn, means punishment.

This difference shows how vowel marks can matter in distinguishing lexical forms. The shared letters ع ق ب do not justify translating the bird’s name as punishment, or the reverse.

Similar caution is needed with يَعْقُوب (Jacob). This is a proper name in the Qur’an. Its letters’ resemblance to ع ق ب does not make it a descriptive Arabic derivative. The name’s history and meaning in Hebrew form a separate discussion, which cannot replace examining this root’s Arabic uses.

Translation choices for Forqan

For bodily عَقِب, “heel” is appropriate. In عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ, the original image should also be retained as far as possible: “turned back on his heels.” An explanation can clarify that the person is reversing the course they had taken.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:28, فِي عَقِبِهِ can be rendered “among the generations after him” or “among his descendants.” “At the back of his foot” is incorrect in this context.

For عَاقِبَة, “outcome” or “end result” is appropriate. When a verse speaks of destruction, loss or fire, its negative value should be understood from that explanation. For وَالْعَاقِبَةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ, “the good outcome belongs to those who remain mindful” is natural.

عُقْبَى can also usually be rendered “outcome.” In Al-Raʿd 13:35, this works for both those who remain mindful and those who deny. In Al-Shams 91:15, “consequence” is more appropriate because the subject is the result of an action or event.

For عُقْبًا in Al-Kahf 18:44, “outcome” or “end result” is appropriate.

عِقَاب, in uses concerning an unpleasant response, should be rendered “punishment.” For شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ, “severe in punishment” is clear and avoids cumbersome invented expressions.

عَاقَبَ requires individual attention to context. In Al-Naḥl 16:126 and Al-Ḥajj 22:60, “respond proportionately” or “respond to the extent of the harm” better conveys the sentence. “Punish severely” or “punish the guilty person” can introduce a meaning beyond the text.

In Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:11, فَعَاقَبْتُمْ is better translated with an explanation of the turn for compensation and financial entitlements, rather than simply “you punished.”

فَأَعْقَبَهُمْ in Al-Tawbah 9:77 is close to “left for them as a consequence” or “brought about for them as a consequence.” نِفَاقًا identifies the kind of consequence.

وَلَمْ يُعَقِّبْ in Al-Naml and Al-Qaṣaṣ can be rendered “did not return.” مُعَقِّبَات in Al-Raʿd 13:11 is close to “those who come in succession” or “guards taking turns”; identifying them as angels in the basic translation requires a separate interpretive preference.

لَا مُعَقِّبَ لِحُكْمِهِ can naturally be translated “no one can overturn or annul His judgement.”

For الْعَقَبَة in Al-Balad 90:11–12, “the difficult pass” is appropriate. The subsequent verses give the image’s social and moral explanation, which need not all be placed inside the translation of one word.

8. Conclusion

Qur’anic ع ق ب has several semantic branches. Much of the family concerns coming after, following, replacement and outcome. عَقِب can denote the heel or a person’s later descendants; عاقبة and عقبى concern an end or consequence; تعقيب concerns following and returning; and عقاب in punitive use is a response following conduct.

The derivatives nevertheless differ in their value and result. عاقبة and عقبى can be good or bad, whereas Qur’anic عقاب more specifically concerns punishment. Some verses also use عَاقَبَ for a proportionate response to harm someone has suffered, so its uses should not all be confined to punishing a guilty person.

مُعَقِّبَات and مُعَقِّب are two further uses of the family. The first concerns coming in succession or following; in Al-Raʿd 13:41, the second concerns someone able to follow up an action and alter its result, which is impossible against The God’s judgement. The identity of the معقبات in Al-Raʿd 13:11 must be determined from context and other evidence, rather than from the root’s lexical meaning alone.

The other branch includes عَقَبَة, the difficult pass. Al-Balad uses it as an image for actions whose difficulty is explained through freeing a person in bondage, feeding someone in need, faith and mutual counsel to patience and mercy. This branch cannot be reduced to succession without adequate evidence.

The semantic core of the main part of ع ق ب can be expressed as one thing coming or standing after another, following it, or a result arising after an action or event. This brings together the heel and the rear, successive generations, alternating turns, outcome and punitive response.

Alongside this field, عَقَبَة has an established branch concerning difficulty, elevation and an arduous path. Its meaning need not be forcibly derived from the heel or replacement to preserve an apparent unity of the root.

This is the crucial distinction in understanding the verses: what follows something is not necessarily punishment, and what is called an outcome is not necessarily good or bad. The action, description and context specify the kind of consequence. Understanding this difference keeps Qur’anic translations from becoming too narrow and prevents a lexical image from replacing the verse’s own clear meaning.
