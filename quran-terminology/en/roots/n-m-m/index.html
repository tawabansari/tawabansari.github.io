Disclosure, tale-bearing, and the boundary between conveying news and damaging human relationships

In Arabic, the root N M M concerns something becoming apparent or being disclosed, particularly when speech, a secret, a sound, or a sign emerges from concealment and becomes known to others. An important use is namm or namīmah: carrying other people’s words in a way that can create suspicion, hostility, and discord.

The root occurs in the Qur’an only as بِنَمِيمٍ (bi-namīm), in Al-Qalam 68:11. Understanding it requires distinguishing the root’s broader Arabic range from the particular meaning of namīm in the verse.

1. Semantic core: bringing something concealed into the open

One lexical analysis explains the branch of disclosure in this family as follows:

إِظْهَارُ شَيْءٍ وَإِبْرَازُهُ

iẓhāru shayʾin wa-ibrāzuhu

That is, making something apparent and bringing it into view.

This meaning appears in different uses of the root. نَمَّ الْحَدِيثُ (namma al-ḥadīthu) can mean that speech became known, whereas نَمَّ الْحَدِيثَ (namma al-ḥadītha) means conveying or disclosing it. The difference between a report becoming apparent and someone making it apparent is thus reflected in the verb’s construction.

Early Arabic records uses involving the conveyance of speech, disclosure of secrets, faint sounds, and revealing signs.

Becoming apparent is not inherently objectionable. The negative force in tale-bearing arises from how speech is conveyed, the purpose, and its effects on relationships. Not every disclosure of truth or conveyance of news is namīmah.

Important forms and derivatives

The family has several forms in Arabic.

نَمَّ (namma) is a verb that can concern speech becoming apparent or someone conveying it.

نَمّ (namm) is a verbal noun. In speech-related usage, it denotes disclosing other people’s words and tale-bearing.

نَمِيم (namīm) and نَمِيمَة (namīmah) name this conduct or speech conveyed in this way. Both are recorded in the same semantic field, although their morphological relationship has been analyzed differently.

نَمَّام (nammām) describes someone who habitually carries others’ words and, in its objectionable use, sows discord through tale-bearing. The pattern فَعَّال (faʿʿāl) can express repetition or habitual conduct; its extent and character depend on context.

نَمُوم (namūm) and some other forms also describe a tale-bearer.

Of these forms, only نَمِيم (namīm) occurs in the Qur’an. Although familiar in Arabic, نَمَّام (nammām) and نَمِيمَة (namīmah) do not themselves occur in the Qur’anic text.

2. Uses in early Arabic

The root’s range extends beyond tale-bearing. Some uses concern something becoming apparent through subtle signs.

Faint sound and movement

Arabic also records نَمِيمَة (namīmah) for a faint sound, murmur, or movement that reveals someone’s or something’s presence.

For example, Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī describes animals sensing the sound of a concealed hunter:

وَنَمِيمَةً مِنْ قَانِصٍ مُتَلَبِّبٍ
فِي كَفِّهِ جَشْءٌ أَجَشُّ وَأَقْطُعُ

wa-namīmatan min qāniṣin mutalabbibin / fī kaffihi jashʾun ajashshu wa-aqṭuʿu

“And a faint sound from a hunter with his garment secured about him; in his hand were a resonant bow and arrowheads.”

Here namīmah denotes a faint sound or audible sign of the hunter’s presence, rather than tale-bearing between people.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The verse belongs to his famous elegy; without establishing when it was composed, it should not be presented as securely predating the Qur’an.

A revealing scent

Lexical usage also records the following:

نَمَّ الشَّيْءُ (namma al-shayʾu) is also said when something’s scent becomes noticeable and spreads.

نَمَّام (nammām) is also recorded as the name of a fragrant plant. The name has been connected semantically with disclosure, since the plant’s scent can reveal its presence.

This explanation shows that some uses concern a presence revealed through a sign, even when the sign is not human speech.

However, linking the plant’s scent with disclosure is a lexical analysis. It does not, by itself, establish the history of the plant’s name.

Disclosing speech and secrets

Expressions such as التَّوْرِيش (al-tawrīsh) and الإِغْرَاء (al-ighrāʾ) are recorded for namma and namīmah: inciting people against one another and stirring discord.

One early explanation describes a tale-bearer as someone who passes on words that should have been kept. Such a person is compared to a skin that cannot hold water. The comparison explains the disclosure of speech; it does not establish a single historical origin for every sense of the root.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Qays ibn al-Ḥaddādiyyah reads:

بَكَتْ مِنْ حَدِيثٍ نَمَّهُ وَأَشَاعَهُ
وَلَفَقَهُ وَاشٍ مِنَ الْقَوْمِ وَاضِعُ

bakat min ḥadīthin nammahu wa-ashāʿahu / wa-lafaqahu wāshin mina al-qawmi wāḍiʿu

“She wept over a report that a malicious tale-bearer among the people had conveyed, spread, and pieced together.”

Here نَمَّهُ (nammahu) accompanies أَشَاعَهُ (ashāʿahu), linking the conveyance of speech with its circulation. Other transmissions vary, including بَثَّهُ (baththahu) in place of nammahu and different verbs for piecing the report together. The verse is useful evidence of early usage, but its textual variants must remain part of the assessment.

Truth and falsehood

Does namīmah necessarily mean conveying a false report?

No. Early lexical explanations include fabricating or embellishing speech with lies as one form of namīmah, but they do not all restrict it to falsehood.

Someone may repeat words that were genuinely spoken in order to anger another person, damage a relationship, or create hostility. The truth of the original words does not, by itself, prevent their transmission from being tale-bearing.

For example, a person privately complains about a friend. A third party carries that complaint to the friend to provoke hostility. The report may be entirely true, while the manner of conveying it remains tale-bearing.

Conversely, truthfully reporting an injustice, giving evidence to a body hearing a case, or warning someone of a real danger is not namīmah merely because it conveys someone else’s words. Purpose, necessity, accuracy, and manner of communication must be considered separately.

3. Qur’anic use: مَشَّاءٍ بِنَمِيمٍ (mashshāʾin bi-namīm)

Al-Qalam 68:11

هَمَّازٍ مَشَّاءٍ بِنَمِيمٍ

hammāzin mashshāʾin bi-namīmin

“A faultfinder who constantly goes about carrying tales.”

In this verse, بِنَمِيمٍ (bi-namīm) consists of the preposition بِـ (bi-) and the noun نَمِيم (namīm). The noun is indefinite and in the genitive case.

مَشَّاء (mashshāʾ) comes from M SH Y on the pattern فَعَّال (faʿʿāl). Especially in this context, the pattern suggests frequent or repeated going about. The expression highlights tale-bearing as recognizable, repeated conduct; the pattern alone does not determine an exact number of occasions or settle the assessment of every isolated case.

هَمَّاز (hammāz) also describes a faultfinder and taunter. Its combination with mashshāʾin bi-namīm brings together two related forms of conduct: injuring others through faultfinding and taunts, and carrying speech to stir discord.

The verse’s context

Al-Qalam 68:11 forms part of a continuous description in the surah:

وَلَا تُطِعْ كُلَّ حَلَّافٍ مَهِينٍ

wa-lā tuṭiʿ kulla ḥallāfin mahīnin

Al-Qalam 68:10 — “And do not obey any contemptible, habitual swearer.”

هَمَّازٍ مَشَّاءٍ بِنَمِيمٍ

hammāzin mashshāʾin bi-namīmin

Al-Qalam 68:11 — “A faultfinder who constantly goes about carrying tales.”

مَنَّاعٍ لِلْخَيْرِ مُعْتَدٍ أَثِيمٍ

mannāʿin li-al-khayri muʿtadin athīmin

Al-Qalam 68:12 — “An obstructer of good, a transgressor, a sinner.”

Together, these verses describe someone who swears excessively, finds fault and carries tales, obstructs good, and oversteps bounds.

The verse does more than condemn a particular utterance. It places tale-bearing alongside other traits of someone who should not be obeyed or followed.

In this context, namīm is more than conveying news or repeating words. It is speech turned into an instrument for damaging relationships by a faultfinding and injurious person.

Different historical individuals have been proposed as the referent. However, Al-Qalam 68:10 uses the general expression كُلَّ حَلَّافٍ مَهِينٍ (kulla ḥallāfin mahīn). The lexical and ethical significance should therefore not be restricted to one historical person.

Is نَمِيم (namīm) a plural of نَمِيمَة (namīmah)?

Two lexical analyses are recorded.

In one, namīm is itself the name of the conduct, denoting tale-bearing like namīmah. In the other, it can be a plural of namīmah.

This difference does not fundamentally change the translation. In this construction, bi-namīm denotes tale-bearing conduct or utterances. The word alone does not specify how many statements have been conveyed.

In Persian, “with tale-bearing” is a natural and sufficient equivalent. Repeated conduct is conveyed by mashshāʾ and the context, rather than by imposing a definite numerical meaning on namīm.

4. Namīm within related Qur’anic concepts

Although N M M occurs only once in the Qur’an, the conduct is connected with several other Qur’anic concepts. These passages help clarify its ethical boundaries without making distinct words complete synonyms.

Backbiting and faultfinding

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 says:

وَلَا يَغْتَبْ بَعْضُكُمْ بَعْضًا

wa-lā yaghtab baʿḍukum baʿḍan

“And do not backbite one another.”

Backbiting concerns recounting someone’s faults or something objectionable about them in their absence; merely speaking about an absent person is not backbiting. In namīmah, the conveyance of speech between people and its effect on their relationship are prominent.

The two can occur together but are not identical. Someone can discuss an absent person’s faults without carrying words between two parties; someone can also carry words from one party to another and thereby create hostility.

The pairing of hammāz and mashshāʾin bi-namīm in Al-Qalam is consistent with this distinction, although it does not prove that the two kinds of conduct can never overlap.

Conveying reports without knowledge

Al-Nūr 24:15 says:

إِذْ تَلَقَّوْنَهُ بِأَلْسِنَتِكُمْ وَتَقُولُونَ بِأَفْوَاهِكُمْ مَا لَيْسَ لَكُمْ بِهِ عِلْمٌ

idh talaqqawnahu bi-alsinatikum wa-taqūlūna bi-afwāhikum mā laysa lakum bihi ʿilmun

“When you received it with your tongues and said with your mouths what you had no knowledge of.”

This verse identifies a major danger in circulating speech: hearing a report and passing it on without knowing whether it is true.

Such conduct can spread falsehood and accusations even when the person passing it on did not invent the report.

Here the problem also concerns the absence of knowledge. In mashshāʾin bi-namīm, the emphasis is on tale-bearing and harmful transmission. The two fields can intersect without having the same definition.

The need to examine news

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6, the Qur’an commands:

إِن جَاءَكُمْ فَاسِقٌ بِنَبَإٍ فَتَبَيَّنُوا

in jāʾakum fāsiqun bi-nabaʾin fa-tabayyanū

“If a transgressor brings you a report, investigate.”

The verse matters from the recipient’s perspective. A person may convey words in the guise of concern or information, but the hearer should not use them as grounds for judgment and action against others without examination.

The harm of namīmah is therefore not confined to the messenger’s behavior. Hasty acceptance and an uninformed response can also spread discord.

However, نَبَأ (nabaʾ), “report,” in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6 does not mean namīmah. Not every report is tale-bearing. The verse supplies a complementary principle for assessing news and preventing harm from it.

Distinguishing tale-bearing from exposing injustice and truthful testimony

An essential boundary is the difference between tale-bearing and disclosing truth in order to uphold a right.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:148 says:

لَا يُحِبُّ اللَّهُ الْجَهْرَ بِالسُّوءِ مِنَ الْقَوْلِ إِلَّا مَن ظُلِمَ

lā yuḥibbu allāhu al-jahra bi-al-sūʾi mina al-qawli illā man ẓulima

“The God does not love the public utterance of evil speech, except by someone who has been wronged.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:135 also calls believers to uphold justice and testify for The God, even against themselves or their close relatives.

If disclosing other people’s words were inherently objectionable in every circumstance, the command to give truthful testimony and the allowance for speaking of injustice suffered would be incompatible with such an absolute prohibition.

A distinction is needed between circulating words to sow discord and giving a substantiated report to prevent injustice. Truth alone does not rule out namīmah, but disclosing truth is not inherently namīmah either.

Assessment in such cases involves the report’s accuracy, the purpose of conveying it, the need to speak, the manner of circulation, and the rights of the people concerned. These criteria emerge from reading the passages together, rather than from the lexical meaning of namīm alone.

5. Distinctions from related roots

N M M (namm) and N M L (naml)

In its familiar sense, نَمْل (naml) names ants, whereas نَمّ (namm) concerns disclosure and the conveyance of speech.

Some lexical records also include tale-bearing under N M L. In some dialects, نِمَّة (nimmah) is recorded as a name for an ant.

These limited overlaps do not make the two roots identical or turn Qur’anic naml into “tale-bearer.” Each word must be understood through its structure, usage, and context.

Study of N M L

N M M (namm) and N M Y (namā)

نَمَى (namā) concerns increase, rising, and growth. Uses involving conveying or raising someone’s words to another person are also recorded for this root.

Some lexical explanations distinguish نَمَيْتُ الْحَدِيثَ (namaytu al-ḥadītha) from نَمَّيْتُ الْحَدِيثَ (nammaytu al-ḥadītha): the former may concern conveying words for reconciliation, the latter for discord. However, differences in lexical accounts mean that this distinction should not be made absolute.

In particular, نَمَّ (namma), from the doubled root N M M, is not morphologically identical with نَمَّى (nammā), the intensified form from N M Y.

An overlap in some speech-related uses does not justify merging the full families of these two roots.

N M N M (namnama) and fine patterns

نَمْنَمَ (namnama) is recorded for marking or decorating with small, closely spaced patterns; مُنَمْنَم (munamnam) means patterned or ornamented.

One lexical analysis treats the branch of patterning and ornamentation separately from disclosure. Namnama has a four-consonant structure with repeated letters. Its placement alongside N M M in a lexicon does not justify deriving all its meanings directly from tale-bearing.

This word family does not occur in the Qur’an. It reminds us that words grouped under one dictionary entry do not necessarily share a proven original image.

6. Proposed translation for Forqan

For Qur’anic نَمِيم (namīm), the principal natural equivalent is “tale-bearing.”

For the whole phrase هَمَّازٍ مَشَّاءٍ بِنَمِيمٍ (hammāzin mashshāʾin bi-namīm) in Al-Qalam 68:11, a suitable rendering is:

“A faultfinder who constantly goes about carrying tales.”

“Tale-bearing” preserves the word’s established negative force without necessarily adding fabrication or slander. “Goes about” also retains the movement conveyed by mashshāʾ.

A word note can explain that the conduct consists of carrying speech between people in a divisive and harmful way. The direct verse translation need not incorporate every detail.

7. Conclusion

N M M has a clear Arabic branch involving becoming apparent or disclosing something. Speech, sound, movement, and even scent can reveal what was previously concealed. In human relationships, this range extends to conveying and disclosing speech.

In the Qur’anic phrase hammāzin mashshāʾin bi-namīm, namīm denotes tale-bearing: conduct that makes other people’s words an instrument of faultfinding, discord, or damage to relationships.

The concept is neither necessarily identical with falsehood nor inclusive of every repetition of someone’s words. A true report can be tale-bearing, while truthfully reporting injustice or giving evidence can be necessary and just. The distinction is understood through usage and the related Qur’anic passages.

The contextual meaning of namīm in Al-Qalam 68:11 is therefore tale-bearing. The full root study shows a broader Arabic range of becoming apparent, disclosing, and revealing signs.
