Rubbing and moving over a surface; the sun’s decline and the beginning of a time interval

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic triliteral D L K concerns rubbing, friction, and moving something over another surface. Familiar uses include rubbing the body, scrubbing clothes during washing, applying fragrance to skin, and removing grain husks by rubbing.

Movement and contact matter in these uses. A hand or tool moves over something and may change its surface through that movement.

The family is not restricted to physical rubbing. Early Arabic also records it for the sun’s movement and decline, the wearing effects of travel and experience on people and animals, and even delaying payment of a right.

One important expression is dulūku al-shamsi.

It does not describe rubbing the sun, but its movement, inclination, or departure from a previous position.

Early dictionaries give three explanations: passing the middle of the sky, inclining toward sunset, and sunset itself.

These explanations are related but do not identify the same time.

After reaching its highest daily position, the sun appears from the earth to descend toward the western horizon, continuing until sunset.

Inclination and departure may therefore unite these uses. Whether dulūk in a particular sentence names the beginning, duration, or end of that movement requires context and linguistic evidence.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78:

Li-dulūki al-shamsi.

The verse places the sun’s dulūk alongside the night’s ghasaq and the dawn recitation.

The principal question is therefore more than finding a lexical equivalent: which stage of the sun’s apparent movement does dulūk identify, and how does it relate to the verse’s time interval?

The root alone should not yield a specified number of prayers, prayer units, or a complete form of worship. These require an independent study of the verses on ṣalāt.

2. Qur’anic forms and Arabic derivatives

Qur’anic occurrence

D L K occurs only once in the Qur’an:

Dulūk — Al-Isrāʾ 17:78

The verse has li-dulūki.

Li- is a preposition.

Dulūki is a genitive verbal noun from dalaka, forming a construct phrase with al-shamsi.

Dulūk al-shams therefore names an event or state concerning the sun’s inclination and decline.

It is neither an active participle nor a perfect or imperfect verb, but a verbal noun.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole occurrence in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78

Other forms recorded in Arabic include:

Dalaka yadluku: rubbed, abraded, or applied pressure while moving a hand over a surface.

Dalk: the verbal noun for rubbing and friction.

Dulūk: a verbal noun associated with rubbing and, in solar usage, inclination and decline.

Dalakat al-shamsu: the sun inclined, passed its midday position, or set, depending on usage.

Dālik: an active participle, one who rubs; in solar description, a sun that has inclined or declined.

Madlūk: something rubbed or abraded; in some uses, something worn or seasoned by experience and use.

Tadallaka: rubbed oneself or applied a substance to one’s body.

Dalūk: in another use, a substance such as fragrance or a cleanser used for rubbing.

Dalīk: in some uses, a seasoned, experienced person or animal; in other branches, a particular food or plant.

Mudālakah: in some uses, procrastination or persistent contention over claiming a right.

These forms are known in Arabic, but only the verbal noun dulūk appears in the Qur’an.

Rubbing, food, experience, and procrastination therefore explain the family’s linguistic range; they are not all components of Al-Isrāʾ 17:78.

3. The semantic core in early sources

Ibn Fāris’s definition

Ibn Fāris gives the root a single principle in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى زَوَالِ شَيْءٍ عَنْ شَيْءٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā zawāli shayʾin ʿan shayʾin

“One principle indicating the departure of something from something else.”

He adds that this movement is accompanied by gentleness in the principal examples.

This matters because he attempts to explain bodily rubbing and solar dulūk through a broader concept of movement and departure.

As a hand rubs a surface, it continually moves from one point to another.

The solar use also concerns apparent movement and departure from a position.

Ibn Fāris’s analytical definition should nevertheless be distinguished from all actual uses of the language.

Not every gentle movement is called dalk in Arabic.

Nor is all rubbing necessarily gentle: considerable pressure may be applied.

Movement involving contact or a change of position may provide a shared feature, but its precise form differs between uses.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: departure, movement, and Ibn Fāris’s analysis

Rubbing and friction

Lisān al-ʿArab explains dalaka al-shayʾa through rubbing and friction.

For example:

Dalaktu al-shayʾa bi-yadī.

That is: I rubbed the thing with my hand.

A hand moves over an object’s surface and may warm, smooth, clean, or wear it.

None of those results is an essential meaning of the verb itself.

Someone may rub something without cleaning it.

Rubbing may also aim at cleaning, perfuming, or softening something.

Purpose and outcome depend on context.

Removing grain husks

Dictionaries record that rubbing an ear of grain or grain itself can separate its husk.

This illustrates the relation between rubbing and a physical change.

In this example, dalaka al-sunbula is also explained as husking. Rubbing the ear is the prominent action, with husk separation its result in that use.

One particular outcome should not become the definition of every form of the root.

Washing clothes

Dalaka al-thawba is also recorded for rubbing or scrubbing a garment during washing.

The friction may help remove dirt.

Dalk and ghasl are not complete synonyms, however.

Washing may involve water without vigorous scrubbing; someone may also rub something without washing it.

The distinction matters: rubbing is a particular action, while cleanliness is one possible result.

4. Applying fragrance and the experience of life

Tadallaka: applying a substance to the body

Arabic sources use tadallaka for rubbing one’s body or applying fragrance to skin.

Dalūk, too, can name something used for rubbing in a non-Qur’anic use.

Cleansers or fragrances may, for example, be used in rubbing.

This differs from dulūki al-shamsi.

Similar written forms within one root family do not establish identical vocalization or meaning: dalūk is a rubbing substance, while Qur’anic dulūk concerns the sun’s decline. Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 is not about perfume or washing.

The sentence concerns the sun, which identifies the appropriate semantic field.

Experience and wear

Lisān al-ʿArab uses dalakahu al-dahru for someone seasoned and taught by passing time.

Baʿīrun madlūkun likewise describes a camel accustomed to repeated journeys and affected by their hardship.

Neither the person nor the animal is physically rubbed by hand in this expression.

Repeated experience and hardship exert their effects.

Rubbing a material and seasoning a person can be related figuratively.

Just as repeated use and contact can change an object, successive experiences may affect a person’s abilities, conduct, or condition.

This image should not become a certain account of historical derivation.

Madlūk here does not inherently mean wise, just, or worthy: experience can have different outcomes.

Delaying payment of a right

Dictionaries record dālaka al-rajula for procrastinating and delaying fulfillment of a right.

The use may relate to drawing out a matter, postponing it repeatedly, and moving performance from one time to another.

This connection is only a possible semantic explanation.

It does not establish that dalk originally always meant delay or deception.

Nor does dulūk in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 mean delaying prayer or postponing worship.

It concerns the sun’s position.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Asās al-Balāghah: rubbing, experience, dalūk, and procrastination

5. Dulūk al-shams in Arabic

The family’s solar usage presents its principal question here.

Early sources give three main explanations:

1. The sun’s decline from its midday position.

2. Its inclination from that decline until sunset.

3. Sunset and disappearance.

They concern one natural phenomenon but identify different times.

Decline from the midday position

In this reading, the sun inclines after reaching its highest daily position.

Arabic calls this moment zawāl al-shams.

Dalakat al-shams can refer to this inclination after midday.

Al-Zajjāj includes that meaning in his explanation of dulūk.

Dulūk here begins the sun’s movement away from its midday position toward the west.

It is distinct from sunrise: sunrise reveals the sun above the horizon, while midday decline follows its highest daily position.

That decline does not necessarily coincide with twelve o’clock on the civil clock in every location and season.

Its clock time depends on solar position, longitude, season, and local clock settings.

On the midday interpretation, dulūk is consequently a solar marker, rather than a fixed clock number for every place on earth.

Inclination toward sunset

In the second reading, dulūk encompasses the sun’s apparent movement after midday toward sunset, rather than just one point in time.

Lexical reports from Al-Akhfash and Al-Mubarrad describe it as extending from midday decline to sunset.

This suits the broader sense of inclination and movement.

After midday the sun gradually descends toward the horizon.

The movement’s beginning should nevertheless be distinguished from its entire duration.

Describing dulūk as extending from midday decline to sunset may refer to the process of inclination throughout that interval.

If a sentence uses dulūk to mark the beginning of an obligation, its construction must identify the starting point.

This matters in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 because li-dulūki appears together with ilā ghasaqi al-layli.

Sunset

Early Arabic sources also record dalakat al-shamsu for the sun setting.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Muḥkam state this use explicitly.

The sun has reached the stage of sinking below the horizon or disappearing from sight.

Sunset can thus be the final stage of the sun’s afternoon inclination.

That relationship does not make midday decline and sunset the same time.

Several hours may separate them.

Their difference in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 is therefore a real difference in the interval’s starting point.

Majmaʿ al-Bayān: Al-Mubarrad on midday decline through sunset

6. Poetic witnesses to the sun’s dulūk

A witness for midday decline

Dictionaries transmit the following verse:

مَا تَدْلُكُ الشَّمْسُ إِلَّا حَذْوَ مَنْكِبِهِ
فِي حَوْمَةٍ دُونَهَا الْهَامَاتُ وَالْقَصَرُ

mā tadluku al-shamsu illā ḥadhwa mankibihi / fī ḥawmatin dūnahā al-hāmātu wa-al-qaṣaru

“The sun declines only level with his shoulder, in an expanse below which are heads and necks.” This follows the wording transmitted here.

Its explanation associates tadluku al-shamsu with the sun declining from its high position.

It documents the use of dulūk around midday in the Arabic literary tradition.

The examined sources do not establish a certain attribution or composition date for this verse.

The second hemistich also has variant readings.

It is useful lexical evidence, but should not be called certainly pre-Islamic without proof.

Lisān al-ʿArab: midday witness and textual variants

Hādhā maqāmu qadamay Rabāḥi

Al-Farrāʾ, an early linguist, transmits this rajaz:

هَذَا مَقَامُ قَدَمَيْ رَبَاحِ
ذَبَّبَ حَتَّى دَلَكَتْ بِرَاحِ

hādhā maqāmu qadamay rabāḥi / dhabbaba ḥattā dalakat bi-rāḥi

“Here is where Rabāḥ’s two feet stood; flies became numerous around him until the sun set, [while an observer shaded the eyes with] a palm.” This follows Majmaʿ al-Bayān’s explanation of dhabbaba as numerous flies and takes bi-rāḥ as the palm. The Barāḥ and ghudwatan variants produce different renderings.

The explanation describes a man named Rabāḥ remaining at his station and continuing his work until the sun’s dulūk.

Al-Farrāʾ cites it in discussing dulūk as sunset.

Bi-rāḥ and Barāḥ are themselves disputed, however.

One reading takes Barāḥ as a name for the sun.

Another connects bi-rāḥ with the palm: the observer places a hand before the eyes to reduce glare while checking the sun.

An unvocalized reading should therefore not yield a wholly certain historical meaning.

Majmaʿ al-Bayān: dhabbaba and the bi-rāḥ/Barāḥ variation

Al-Farrāʾ’s report establishes a use of dulūk concerning the approach of sunset or sunset itself.

Al-Basīṭ: the Rabāḥ witness and dhabbaba, ghudwatan, and li-al-shams variants

Dhū al-Rummah’s witness concerning celestial bodies

Dhū al-Rummah, an Umayyad poet, uses al-āfilāt al-dawālik in a witness drawing on the image of lamps and stars:

مَصَابِيحُ لَيْسَتْ بِاللَّوَاتِي يَقُودُهَا
نُجُومٌ وَلَا بِالْآفِلَاتِ الدَّوَالِكِ

maṣābīḥu laysat bi-allawātī yaqūduhā / nujūmun wa-lā bi-al-āfilāti al-dawāliki

“Lamps that are neither those guided by stars nor those that set and disappear.” This preserves the verse’s negative construction.

Dawālik appears alongside āfilāt.

Ufūl concerns a celestial body setting and disappearing from sight.

Its association with dawālik supports the reading involving setting and disappearance of celestial bodies.

The witness should nevertheless not be overgeneralized.

First, dawālik here occurs in a setting of celestial bodies disappearing, rather than necessarily the midday sun. The verse’s negative construction must also be preserved in translation.

Second, the poet uses dawālik in descriptive literary language.

Third, Dhū al-Rummah lived after the Qur’an’s revelation. This is evidence of classical Arabic, not independent and certain pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Despite these limits, the witness documents dulūk and its derivatives in the field of inclination, setting, and disappearance of celestial bodies.

Ibn Qutaybah’s Gharīb al-Qurʾān: Dhū al-Rummah’s setting witness

7. Does solar dulūk derive from rubbing the eyes while observing the sun?

Some early sources connect dulūk al-shams with rubbing the eyes.

In that explanation, an observer may rub the eyes or place a palm before them to reduce the light’s intensity.

Some linguists situate the behavior around midday, when sunlight is intense.

Others connect it with sunset, when someone looks toward the horizon to check the sun’s position or disappearance.

This may provide a figurative link between rubbing and solar dulūk.

Distinguish the recording of that explanation in dictionaries from its being the certain historical origin of the expression dulūk al-shams.

A figurative connection alone does not establish historical derivation.

Ibn Fāris explains solar dulūk through inclination and departure; other sources connect it with rubbing the eyes.

One explanation should not become the root’s sole original meaning without examination.

Qur’anic dulūk clearly concerns the sun’s state, rather than commanding eye rubbing or direct observation of the sun.

Nor is this lexical discussion a recommendation to look at the sun. Looking directly at it can damage the eyes and is unnecessary for understanding the word.

American Academy of Ophthalmology: harm from direct solar viewing

8. Qur’anic use: Al-Isrāʾ 17:78

Al-Isrāʾ 17:78

أَقِمِ الصَّلَاةَ لِدُلُوكِ الشَّمْسِ إِلَىٰ غَسَقِ اللَّيْلِ وَقُرْآنَ الْفَجْرِ إِنَّ قُرْآنَ الْفَجْرِ كَانَ مَشْهُودًا

aqimi al-ṣalāta li-dulūki al-shamsi ilā ghasaqi al-layli wa-qurʾāna al-fajri inna qurʾāna al-fajri kāna mashhūdan

“Establish prayer from the sun’s decline until the darkness of night, and the dawn recitation; indeed, the dawn recitation is witnessed.”

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of dulūk.

The command aqimi al-ṣalāta is followed by a time interval expressed through li-dulūki al-shamsi and ilā ghasaqi al-layli.

Wa-qurʾāna al-fajri also appears separately.

Understanding dulūk therefore requires attention to three parts: establishing prayer, the interval’s beginning, and its end at the night’s ghasaq.

Li-dulūki al-shamsi: the role of li-

Grammatical sources offer different explanations of li- here.

One takes it as indicating the beginning of a time or a sense close to “after”: from the time of the sun’s dulūk or after it.

Another takes li- as linking the command specifically to the time of dulūk.

Both can lead to dulūk serving as a starting marker or a time associated with establishing prayer.

The preposition li- should not be treated as part of D L K.

Dulūk identifies the solar event; the preposition relates it to the command to establish prayer.

Persian “from” is natural alongside ilā, “until,” but li- does not mean “from” in every Arabic use.

Beginning the interval at midday decline

If dulūk al-shams means the sun’s midday decline, the stated interval begins after midday.

This agrees with Al-Zajjāj and several early lexicographers.

It also relates clearly to dulūk as the beginning of the sun’s westward inclination.

The verse then supplies a natural marker for the interval’s start.

That marker alone cannot yield a specified number of prayer occasions or units for each occasion.

The verse gives a time interval. A complete account of worship and its relation to other prayer passages is a separate question.

Beginning the interval at sunset

If dulūk al-shams means sunset, the interval begins later.

The span between dulūk and the night’s ghasaq becomes shorter.

This interpretation has lexical support: early sources record dulūk for sunset.

Preferring it in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 requires examining how sunset relates to ghasaq al-layl.

If ghasaq is the beginning of darkness, a shorter interval is involved.

If it means the spread of darkness, the interval runs from sunset until widespread night darkness.

Either way, choosing sunset affects the verse’s time span.

The lexical disagreement is therefore more than a verbal difference.

Ilā ghasaqi al-layli

Ghasaq belongs to GH S Q and concerns night darkness and its spread.

Ghasaq al-layl identifies the end of an interval beginning with solar dulūk.

Its precise meaning belongs in its own root study.

It should not be equated without examination with a fixed clock time or a particular prayer’s name.

Dulūk itself does not mean night darkness; ghasaq supplies that meaning.

Preserve their distinction in translation and explanation.

Study of GH S Q

Wa-qurʾāna al-fajri

The verse continues with qurʾān al-fajr.

The phrase comes from other roots and concerns reading or recitation at dawn.

Dawn is named separately in the verse’s construction.

This separation may matter in studying prayer times.

Dulūk itself does not mean dawn, however, and all the times of day and night should not be included in its definition.

Nor can the number of prayers be determined merely by counting this single verse’s temporal markers.

Study of Q R ʾ

Relation to other prayer passages

Al-Nisāʾ 4:103 describes prayer as appointed to times.

Hūd 11:114 names the two ends of the day and portions of the night.

Al-Nūr 24:58 names dawn prayer and night prayer.

These matter in a comprehensive study of prayer times.

Study of Ṣ L W

A study of D L K, however, principally clarifies dulūk’s meaning and range.

Disagreement over the number of customary prayers should not decide the preferred lexical meaning in advance.

Arguing that dulūk must mean midday decline solely because that allows a particular number of customary prayers to be placed in the verse begins from a desired outcome.

The reverse has the same problem: choosing sunset solely because it better suits a preferred worship schedule does not establish the verse’s lexical meaning.

Examine Arabic evidence and Qur’anic context first, then use the findings in the study of prayer.

9. Assessing the three readings of dulūk

Early lexical evidence places solar dulūk in the field of inclination and departure.

Its precise temporal boundary has nevertheless been disputed.

The midday reading identifies the beginning of the sun’s apparent westward decline.

The reading extending from midday decline to sunset encompasses the whole afternoon descent.

The sunset reading highlights that movement’s final stage.

These need not be three unrelated meanings; they may be related uses along a single process.

Semantic relationship alone does not establish a particular time.

The interval construction in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 requires identifying a starting point.

Midday decline may be preferred for the base translation through evidence concerning the beginning of post-noon inclination and the preference of commentators including Al-Ṭabarī. The interval construction alone does not settle midday versus sunset, since either could begin an interval.

This choice agrees with part of the early lexical evidence and the use of zawāl.

Sunset, too, is recorded by reliable Arabic sources and should not be dismissed as invented or unsupported.

Preferring midday decline should therefore be a reasoned preference, rather than denying sunset usage.

Some lexical explanations may concern the sun’s broader afternoon movement.

That helps establish the word’s range, but a phrase identifying an interval’s beginning still requires a starting point.

For the base translation of Al-Isrāʾ 17:78, “the sun’s decline” is a defensible preferred choice, while preserving the established disagreement concerning sunset and the duration of inclination.

Al-Ṭabarī: both readings and his preference for midday decline

10. Differences from related words

D L K (dalaka) and Z W L (zāla): solar decline and departure

Zawāl in Arabic concerns departure from a previous position or state.

Dictionaries also explain solar dulūk through zawāl.

Zawāl has a broader family, encompassing disappearance or changes of state in various matters.

Qur’anic dulūk, by contrast, concerns only the sun.

Zawāl al-shams suitably conveys the Qur’anic use, but the two roots are not identical throughout their derivatives.

D L K (dalaka) and M Y L (māla): moving in another direction

Mayl concerns inclination and movement from one direction or state toward another.

Early lexicographers explain solar dulūk through mayl.

The semantic fields are close in this use.

Mayl can also concern human inclinations, preference, and deviation, whereas dulūk in the verse concerns the sun’s position.

Dulūk should therefore not be translated as psychological preference or moral inclination.

D L K (dalaka) and GH R B (gharaba): inclination and sunset

In solar use, GH R B concerns setting and disappearing below the horizon.

Dulūk has this meaning in some early uses as well.

Other sources begin dulūk at midday decline.

Sunset is one recorded meaning, but not the entire range of solar dulūk.

If dulūk means midday decline, that moment differs in time from sunset itself.

Translation should preserve the distinction.

Study of GH R B

D L K (dalaka) and ʾ F L (afala): decline and disappearance

Ufūl describes celestial bodies disappearing in the Qur’an, including Al-Anʿām 6:76, Al-Anʿām 6:77, and Al-Anʿām 6:78.

Dhū al-Rummah also places āfilāt and dawālik together.

Their association shows that dulūk can concern celestial bodies setting and disappearing.

Not all sources restrict dulūk to ufūl, however.

Midday decline is another established use, although the sun remains visible then.

These families are therefore not complete synonyms.

Study of ʾ F L

D L K (dalaka) and M R S (marasa): rubbing and friction

Maras in Arabic concerns rubbing, friction, and kneading.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains dalk through maras as well.

Both may describe a hand moving over a surface in physical uses.

This proximity does not make solar dulūk directly concern kneading an object.

The Qur’anic expression concerns time and the sun’s apparent movement.

D L K (dalaka) and F R K (faraka): rubbing and separation by friction

Fark can concern rubbing and friction, especially to separate components.

Removing grain husks by rubbing belongs to this field.

Dalk does not necessarily mean separation, however.

Someone may rub something without detaching a piece.

The roots are close in the action of rubbing, while its outcome may differ.

D L K (dalaka) and ʿ R K (ʿaraka): rubbing and experience

ʿArk concerns rubbing and friction, and in some uses experience and contention.

Lisān al-ʿArab relates dalk and ʿark in explaining physical action.

Dalakahu al-dahru and expressions for becoming seasoned may also be compared figuratively.

Their proximity does not establish a single historical origin for both roots.

Each has an independent linguistic family.

D L K (dalaka) and D L J (dalaja): solar decline and night travel

These roots look similar, but their third letters differ.

Qur’anic dulūk concerns the sun’s inclination or decline.

Dalaj in Arabic concerns travel at night or during parts of the night.

Similar sounds do not establish that dulūk means traveling in night darkness.

Night appears independently in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 through ghasaq al-layl.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

Only dulūk from this family occurs in the Qur’an.

The Persian equivalent should therefore be chosen first for that particular use.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:78

لِدُلُوكِ الشَّمْسِ

Preferred rendering:

“From the sun’s decline.”

This agrees with dulūk as the sun’s westward inclination from its midday position.

“From the sun’s inclination” is also close to the root’s field, but may leave unclear whether it begins after midday or near sunset.

“From sunset” is defensible through recorded early Arabic usage, but changes the verse’s time interval.

“The sun’s decline” is consequently suitable for Forqan’s base translation.

Existing translation:

“Establish prayer from the sun’s decline until the darkness of night, and the dawn recitation; indeed, the dawn recitation is witnessed.”

D L K’s meaning provides no necessity to change this translation.

Explanation should clarify that, in this reading, the decline follows the sun’s highest daily position and begins its westward descent.

The established sunset interpretation should also remain visible.

Non-Qur’anic equivalents

Dalaka al-shayʾa: rubbed or abraded something.

Dalaka al-thawba: scrubbed a garment or rubbed it during washing.

Tadallaka: rubbed oneself.

Dalūk, with fatḥah on dāl: a rubbing or perfuming substance. By contrast, dulūk with ḍammah on dāl concerns the sun’s inclination or decline in solar usage.

Madlūk: rubbed; in uses concerning experience, seasoned or worn.

Dalakat al-shamsu: the sun inclined, declined from its midday position, or set, depending on context.

Preserve these meanings independently.

Physical rubbing should not enter the translation of Al-Isrāʾ 17:78, nor should solar decline be generalized to every derivative of the root.

12. Conclusion

Arabic D L K concerns rubbing, friction, and moving something over a surface.

Sources also record experience and wear, rubbing substances, procrastination, and solar movement in this family.

Ibn Fāris attempts to explain these uses through departure and movement from a position.

His analysis may illuminate semantic connections without establishing the certain historical origin of every use.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as the verbal noun dulūk in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78.

Early sources give three principal solar readings: midday decline, inclination from midday to sunset, and sunset itself.

All concern the sun’s apparent movement, but identify different times.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 combines dulūk with ghasaq al-layl to identify an interval and names qurʾān al-fajr separately.

Choosing dulūk’s meaning therefore affects the interval’s starting point.

Midday decline agrees with reliable lexical evidence and can serve the construction marking an interval’s beginning. Sunset is also recorded in early Arabic and should not be treated as unsupported.

Neither meaning alone establishes a specified number of prayers, prayer units, or every detail of establishing ṣalāt.

These matters require independent study of prayer and the relevant verses on worship’s times and manner.

Proposed final definition: In tangible uses, D L K denotes rubbing and friction through movement and contact. For the sun it describes inclination and departure from a previous position, with recorded senses of midday decline, movement toward sunset, and sunset itself. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:78, “the sun’s decline” is preferred for the base translation while preserving established lexical disagreement over its temporal boundary. Brief root meaning: Rubbing and friction; solar dulūk is inclination and decline, with recorded uses from midday decline to sunset and disagreement over the precise time.
