Harm, destruction, and loss of strength: from hidden dangers to the harmless drink of Paradise

In early Arabic, the root gh-w-l concerns harming, destroying, or exposing someone to injury that may arrive unexpectedly or from an unknown direction. The verb ghāla is used when something harms a person, deprives them of strength, or causes their destruction.

Ghawl denotes harm or a damaging consequence arising from such an event. Depending on usage, this harm may affect a person's body, mind, capabilities, or life.

In the same family, ightāla can mean harming or killing someone secretly or by surprise. Ghāʾilah also denotes a danger, injury, or damaging consequence that may be concealed in something and unknown to the person affected.

Alongside these meanings, ghūl, with an initial u-vowel, names a legendary creature in early Arabic beliefs and stories. It was said to appear in deserts, assume different shapes, and frighten or destroy travelers.

However, ghawl meaning harm and ghūl in its legendary sense must be distinguished. They belong to the same lexical family but refer to different things. This distinction is contextual: ghūl is not restricted to a legendary creature in every lexical usage.

The root gh-w-l occurs only once in the Quran, as غَوْلٌ in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47.

The verse speaks of the drink of the people of Paradise and states that it contains no ghawl. The root's meaning and the context indicate the absence of harm and damaging consequences for those who drink it.

Understanding the word therefore requires distinguishing the general meaning of harm, particular examples such as headache or impaired reason, and the different use of ghūl in legendary stories.

1. The root in early Arabic

Early Arabic sources explain ghālahu al-shayʾ as something harming, destroying, or unexpectedly injuring a person.

A notable feature of this family is harm that may be sudden, concealed, or arrive from an unknown direction.

For example, something may initially seem harmless, yet using it may diminish a person's strength or damage their health.

Someone may also be unaware of a danger and suddenly encounter an event that deprives them of their strength or life.

Actual harm matters in these uses. Secrecy and surprise, however, are not necessary components of every derivative: some uses concern only a harmful or destructive effect.

A poetic witness attributed to Bashāmah ibn ʿAmr

Bashāmah ibn ʿAmr, a pre-Islamic poet, says in a poem urging his people to stand firm against an enemy:

وَلَا تَقْعُدُوا وَبِكُمْ مِنَّةٌ
كَفَى بِالْحَوَادِثِ لِلْمَرْءِ غُولًا

wa-lā taqʿudū wa-bikum minnatun / kafā bi-al-ḥawādithi li-al-marʾi ghūlā

Translation: “Do not remain inactive while you still have strength; life's calamities are enough to harm and bring a person down.”

Here, ghūlan concerns the danger and harm caused by life's events. In this transmitted wording, the initial u-vowel does not by itself require the legendary sense.

The poet says that a person is not safe from life's dangers and death even without taking action against an enemy.

He is describing the destructive power of events, rather than a legendary creature.

Minnah here means a person's strength or ability, not reminding another person of a favor.

This witness shows that the family gh-w-l could describe the real harm of events in early usage without being restricted to a single illness or danger.

The verse is attributed to Bashāmah ibn ʿAmr in collections of early poetry, including Al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt. He is known as a pre-Islamic poet, but the surviving poem comes through transmission compiled later. It is therefore important evidence for early usage, although an original record contemporary with its composition has not been established here.

A poetic witness attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

A poem attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes a bird of prey:

مِنْ مَرْقَبٍ فِي ذُرَى خَلْقَاءَ رَاسِيَةٍ
حُجْنُ الْمَخَالِبِ لَا يَغْتَالُهُ الشِّبَعُ

min marqabin fī dhurā khalqāʾa rāsiyatin / ḥujnu al-makhālibi lā yaghtāluhu al-shibaʿu

Translation: “From a lookout on the heights of a firm, smooth rock: a bird with hooked talons whose strength is not taken away by satiety.”

The significant expression is lā yaghtāluhu al-shibaʿ: “satiety does not deprive it of its strength.”

The poet says that eating its fill does not make the bird so heavy or weak that it loses its power and agility.

The issue here is loss of strength and capability, rather than being killed.

This use shows that ightāla does not always mean killing someone. Something can impair a human or animal's abilities and functioning without destroying its existence.

For example, consuming something may reduce awareness or weaken a person so much that they cannot function as before.

This contemporary example illustrates the root's semantic field; it does not mean that every such consequence is independently encoded in ightāla.

Zuhayr's poetry has likewise been preserved through his collected poems and early literary sources. The date of this particular verse's composition is not independently established here.

What the early witnesses show

These two witnesses illuminate two important aspects.

In Bashāmah's poem, ghūl concerns the harm and destruction caused by events.

In Zuhayr's poem, ightiyāl concerns the loss of strength and effectiveness.

The family should therefore not be defined solely in terms of death or complete destruction.

Harm that impairs ability, health, or functioning can also belong to this root's semantic field.

This distinction is essential to understanding Quranic ghawl.

2. Core meaning and root forms

Gh-w-l is a root with a weak middle radical.

In ghāla, the middle wāw appears as alif, whereas the verbal noun ghawl retains the wāw.

The basic verbal pattern is:

غَالَ يَغُولُ غَوْلًا

That is, to harm, take away something's strength, or cause its destruction.

Important recorded derivatives and uses include:

غَالَ: harmed, destroyed, or exposed someone to a damaging event.

يَغُولُ: harms or causes loss of strength or an existing condition.

غَوْل: harm and a damaging effect; in some uses, destruction or severe adversity.

اغْتَالَ: harmed or killed someone secretly or by surprise; in some uses, impaired strength without killing.

اغْتِيَال: the act of secretly or unexpectedly harming or killing; its precise meaning depends on context.

غَائِلَة: danger, harm, or a damaging consequence that may be hidden in something.

غَوَائِل: plural of ghāʾilah; dangers, injuries, and harmful adversities.

غُول: in its legendary use, a creature believed in early Arabic tradition to frighten, mislead, or destroy travelers; the word also has other lexical uses.

أَغْوَال and غِيلَان: plural forms of ghūl, including its legendary use.

تَغَوَّلَ: in some uses, changed shape or appearance; in legendary descriptions, assumed various forms.

مِغْوَل: a type of narrow sword or weapon mentioned in early dictionaries.

Not all these forms occur in the Quran.

Only the noun غَوْلٌ occurs there. The verbs ghāla and ightāla, and the legendary name ghūl, do not occur in the Quranic text.

Concealed harm and surprise

Secrecy and surprise are prominent in the covert-attack use of ightiyāl, though they do not define every use of the verb.

For example, deceiving someone or drawing them into an ambush and then suddenly harming them fits a recognized use of ightiyāl.

In modern Persian and Arabic, ightiyāl is particularly associated with the covert, planned killing of a person.

This modern specialized sense should not be imposed on every derivative of the root.

Ghawl in the Quran does not mean assassination or covert killing.

The word appears in a drinking context and concerns a possible harmful effect.

The two uses share the idea of causing harm, but the kind of harm and how it occurs differ.

Ghāʾilah and a damaging consequence

Arabic sources use ghāʾilah for danger or harm that can arise from something.

For example, someone may enjoy food or drink but later suffer a damaging consequence.

This situation relates to ghāʾilah because the undesirable effect may not be apparent at the time of consumption.

Nevertheless, not every delayed effect or unknown consequence should be treated as the word's definite meaning without contextual evidence.

Harm and destruction form the semantic core. When the harm becomes apparent or whether its cause is concealed are features emphasized in particular uses.

Ghūl in its legendary sense

In early Arabic stories and beliefs, a ghūl was said to appear in deserts, change shape, and mislead or harm travelers.

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays says:

أَيَقْتُلُنِي وَالْمَشْرَفِيُّ مُضَاجِعِي
وَمَسْنُونَةٌ زُرْقٌ كَأَنْيَابِ أَغْوَالِ

a-yaqtulunī wa-al-mashrafiyyu muḍājiʿī / wa-masnūnatun zurqun ka-anyābi aghwāli

Translation: “Will he kill me while a Mashrafī sword lies beside me, and I have sharpened, blue-hued spears like the fangs of aghwāl?”

Aghwāl is a plural of ghūl. Early explanations of this verse identify the referent variously as legendary beings or as snakes.

The poet compares the sharpness and threatening appearance of his weapons to fangs. On the legendary interpretation, the image draws on familiar beliefs and stories; on the snake interpretation, it evokes an animal's dangerous teeth.

This simile illustrates a terrifying image in Arabic poetry, but the verse alone does not settle which interpretation of aghwāl is intended.

If understood as a legendary image, its presence in poetry does not prove that such beings exist outside the story.

Just as a poet may compare something to a dragon or imaginary creature, the legendary sense of ghūl can magnify fear and danger.

This distinction matters in Quranic study: ghawl in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 does not name a legendary being.

3. “Lā fīhā ghawlun”: no harm in the drink of Paradise

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47

لَا فِيهَا غَوْلٌ وَلَا هُمْ عَنْهَا يُنْزَفُونَ

lā fīhā ghawlun wa-lā hum ʿanhā yunzafūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47

Translation: “There is no harm in it, nor are they intoxicated by it.”

This is the root gh-w-l's only Quranic occurrence.

The preceding verses establish the subject.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:41 speaks of the provision of the people of Paradise.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:42 mentions fruit and the honor shown to them.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:45 describes a cup from a flowing drink being passed around among them.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:46 describes the drink as white and delightful to those who drink it.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 adds two features: it contains no ghawl, and those who drink it are not intoxicated.

This sequence shows that ghawl here concerns harm and the damaging consequences of a drink.

The construction lā fīhā ghawlun

Lā is a particle of negation.

Fīhā combines the preposition fī with the feminine pronoun hā, referring to the drink under discussion.

Ghawlun is an indefinite noun in the nominative.

In this construction, fīhā is the fronted predicate and ghawlun the delayed subject.

The sentence therefore denies the presence of ghawl in that drink.

The indefinite noun in a negative context does not restrict the statement to one particular injury.

“There is no harm in it” thus preserves the sentence's broad and natural meaning.

By contrast, translating ghawl only as “headache” or “stomachache” substitutes one possible example for the word's wider meaning.

Does ghawl mean headache?

Some early lexical and exegetical explanations interpret ghawl in this verse as headache.

Others mention stomachache, illness, intoxication, or loss of reason.

These explanations emphasize different kinds of harm associated with intoxicating drink. The general sense of harm can encompass them, but their precise relation must be considered in each explanation.

The point here is lexical: headache and impairment of reason are among the examples given in early explanations of this word.

Such effects can be instances of harm.

The word ghawl itself is not restricted to the name of one of these effects.

The distinction can be expressed as follows:

“Headache” names a particular condition.

“Impairment of reason” concerns diminished or lost capacity to understand and judge.

Ghawl in this verse expresses the wider idea of harm or a damaging effect.

Therefore, headache and impaired reason may be examples of ghawl, but ghawl should not be restricted to either one alone.

The relationship between ghawl and yunzafūn

The second part of the verse reads:

وَلَا هُمْ عَنْهَا يُنْزَفُونَ

wa-lā hum ʿanhā yunzafūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47

In the reading represented by the installed Quranic text, this expression concerns the drinkers' not becoming intoxicated or losing their reason.

Yunzafūn belongs to n-z-f, not gh-w-l.

In Arabic, that family concerns depletion and loss. Within this semantic field, the loss of awareness or reason through drinking is also recognized.

Some readings and linguistic explanations also discuss the exhaustion of the drink. In the current Forqan translation of Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47, however, intoxication is negated. The installed verse is marked provisional, so this wording should not be presented as a final approved translation.

The two parts of the verse should therefore not be identified without justification.

Lā fīhā ghawlun expresses the absence of harm and damaging consequences.

Wa-lā hum ʿanhā yunzafūn concerns a condition that does not befall the drinkers as a result of drinking it.

The meanings are close, but each contributes independently to the description.

Connection with Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19 also describes the drink of the people of Paradise.

That verse speaks of the drinkers not suffering headache or losing their awareness.

The connection shows that Quranic descriptions distinguish the pleasure of drinking from its harmful consequences.

In worldly life, something may be enjoyable when consumed yet subsequently harm the body or mind.

The Quranic description of the heavenly drink combines pleasure with the absence of harm.

This image should not be made into a definitive theory about the drink's chemical composition or the biological structure of human bodies in the afterlife.

The verse describes the drink's qualities for its audience; it does not specify its manufacture or physical ingredients.

Is the heavenly drink identical to worldly khamr?

Muḥammad 47:15 speaks of rivers of khamr that are delightful to those who drink.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19 describe qualities that distinguish the drinks of Paradise from the known harms of worldly intoxicating drinks.

A shared name or similar imagery does not establish identical composition, bodily effects, or qualities in worldly and heavenly drinks.

Nor does the existence of a delightful drink in the afterlife by itself determine the ruling on consuming khamr in this life.

Regarding worldly khamr, Al-Baqarah 2:219 speaks of sin and benefits; Al-Māʾidah 5:90 and Al-Māʾidah 5:91 address avoidance and damaging consequences.

Those rulings must be considered in their own contexts.

For the study of gh-w-l, the central point is that the drink described in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 is declared free of a harmful effect.

4. Ghawl: real harm or a legendary being?

One possible mistake is to identify Quranic ghawl with the ghūl of legends.

In present-day Persian, ghūl usually evokes a large, frightening, or imaginary being.

Early Arabic stories likewise describe a creature that frightened, misled, or destroyed desert travelers.

That use differs from the meaning in Al-Ṣāffāt.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 concerns a drink and its effects on those who consume it.

The verse says that the drink contains no harm; it does not discuss the absence of a legendary creature inside it.

For example, “There is no harm in this food” means that it has no damaging consequence, rather than that it contains no animal or legendary being.

The meaning of harm likewise fits the verse's context.

Conversely, the mention of ghūl in early poetry or dictionaries does not establish the external reality of legendary creatures.

The name may reflect people's beliefs and literary imagery.

The verse should therefore not be used either to prove the existence of legendary ghouls or categorically to deny every being described under that name in old stories.

Its subject is the absence of harm in the drink of the people of Paradise.

5. Comparison with related roots

Gh-w-l (ghawl) and ḍ-r-r (ḍarar)

Ḍarar concerns injury and harm and has a broad range of Quranic uses.

Ghawl can also refer to harm, but certain early Arabic uses emphasize injury from an unknown source or the loss of a person's strength or condition.

For example, injury to someone's body can be called ḍarar.

If food or drink unexpectedly impairs awareness or strength after consumption, ghawl is also understandable as a description of that effect.

Nevertheless, ghawl is not necessarily always concealed, nor ḍarar always apparent.

The families overlap in the field of harm; their differences must be assessed in actual constructions and contexts.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47, ghawl concerns the absence of a drink's damaging consequences.

Gh-w-l (ghawl) and n-z-f (nazf)

Nazf in Arabic concerns depletion or loss.

In the context of intoxicating drink, this family expresses loss of reason or awareness.

Ghawl and yunzafūn occur together in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47.

Both contribute to describing the drink's effects, but their meanings are not completely identical.

Ghawl denotes harm and a damaging consequence.

Nazf in this context concerns loss of the normal state of awareness.

Translating both simply as “intoxication” without explanation weakens this distinction.

Gh-w-l (ghawl) and s-k-r (sukr)

Sukr in the Quran concerns intoxication and disturbance of ordinary awareness.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 prohibits approaching prayer while intoxicated until one knows what one is saying.

Intoxication here relates to diminished understanding and awareness of one's speech.

Ghawl, however, is not itself the name of the state of intoxication.

Intoxication can be a damaging consequence of a drink, but harm is not limited to headache, stomachache, or intoxication.

The presence of both ghawl and yunzafūn in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 makes this distinction clearer.

Gh-w-l (ghawl) and ṣ-d-ʿ (ṣudāʿ)

Ṣudāʿ means headache in Arabic.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19 describes a drink that does not cause its drinkers headache.

Headache can be an instance of harm, but it does not exhaust the meaning of ghawl.

Lā fīhā ghawlun should therefore not be restricted to “It does not cause headache.”

Headache may be offered as an explanatory example, while the basic translation should preserve the wider meaning of harm.

Gh-w-l (ghawl) and gh-l-w (ghuluww)

Gh-l-w is a different root concerning exceeding a limit and going to excess.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:171 and Al-Māʾidah 5:77 prohibit excess in religion.

Ghawl, from gh-w-l, concerns harm and destruction.

Excess in a particular behavior may cause harm, but that possible relationship does not make the roots identical.

Ghuluww is the act of exceeding a limit; ghawl in the use under study denotes a damaging effect.

Gh-w-l (ghawl) and gh-l-y (ghalayān)

Gh-l-y concerns boiling and the agitation of liquid under heat.

The Quran uses this family in connection with boiling liquids or punishment.

Ghawl does not mean boiling.

A very hot drink may harm the body, but heat and harmfulness are different properties.

Ghawl in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 should therefore not be translated as boiling, heat, or burning.

Gh-w-l (ghawl) and gh-l-l (ghill)

Gh-l-l is a separate family associated in the Quran with inner resentment, fetters and chains, and certain uses concerning betrayal.

These meanings are not identical to those of gh-w-l.

Resentment and hostility may lead to harmful action, but ghawl itself does not imply resentment or betrayal.

Nor does similarity in the spelling of two roots establish a historical relationship between them.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ghawl in its main use, equivalents worth considering include “harm,” “injury,” “destruction,” and “damaging effect.”

“Harm” is clear and natural and preserves the word's broad scope.

“Damaging effect” is also precise, particularly when food or drink is involved.

“Destruction” suits some uses but may imply a degree of severity beyond what the verse requires.

Headache and impaired reason are examples that can be explained, but neither alone fully translates ghawl.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47

لَا فِيهَا غَوْلٌ

lā fīhā ghawlun

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47

A suitable translation is:

“There is no harm in it.”

The current Forqan translation preserves this meaning and is lexically defensible; the installed verse remains marked provisional.

In the complete verse:

“There is no harm in it, nor are they intoxicated by it.”

This translation keeps the two clauses distinct.

“There is no ghoul or monster in it” would be incorrect, substituting the legendary sense for the contextual meaning.

“There is no alcohol in it” is likewise not a direct translation, since the verse does not specify the drink's chemical composition.

What is stated is the absence of harm and intoxication for those who drink it.

Brief root meaning in a Forqan word box

For غَوْلٌ in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47:

Brief root meaning: “Causing harm, taking away strength, or exposing someone to injury that may be sudden or concealed.”

Meaning in this verse: “Harm or a damaging consequence from which the drink of the people of Paradise is free.”

The word box need not explain the entire history of the legendary ghoul or all the forms of ightiyāl.

Those subjects belong in the root article. These are editorial proposals; installing the study does not rewrite existing verse boxes.

For other Arabic derivatives, ghālahu can be translated “harmed him” or, in a more severe context, “destroyed him.”

Ightālahu, in the covert-killing use, means “killed him secretly or by surprise.”

Ghāʾilah can be translated “danger” or “damaging consequence.”

Ghūl in its legendary use corresponds to the Persian ghūl and English “ghoul,” but that meaning must not be transferred to Quranic غَوْلٌ.

7. Conclusion

In early Arabic, gh-w-l concerns harm, destruction, or loss of strength or condition. Some uses also emphasize suddenness or concealment.

Early poetic witnesses show uses concerning the harm of events, loss of a living being's strength, and destructive dangers.

Its meaning is therefore not limited to death or complete destruction.

Forms such as ghāla, ightāla, ghāʾilah, and ghawl belong to this semantic field.

Alongside them, ghūl names a creature in Arabic beliefs and legendary imagery said to harm or mislead travelers.

The legendary use is not the direct meaning of ghawl in the Quran.

The root occurs only once there, as غَوْلٌ in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47.

Continuing the description of the drink of Paradise, the verse states that it contains no harm and that its drinkers are not intoxicated by it.

Early sources explain the ghawl of such a drink through examples including headache, stomachache, and impaired reason. None alone encompasses the root's full meaning.

The broader and more precise meaning is the absence of a damaging effect.

The concluding definition is:

Gh-w-l: causing harm, destroying or diminishing strength, health, or a condition, sometimes through a concealed, sudden, or unknown means. As a noun, ghawl denotes harm and the damaging consequence of such an effect.

In the legendary branch, ghūl names an imaginary being associated in early beliefs with harming and misleading travelers.

The uses are semantically related, but must not be conflated in translation or analysis.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47, ghawl describes a quality of the drink, not a legendary creature.

Comparison with Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19 shows that the drink of Paradise is described as delightful and without harmful consequences.

This description highlights a distinction between pleasure and harm: something's being enjoyable does not necessarily make it harmless. A person may enjoy something that nonetheless damages health or the capacity to understand and make decisions.

Such a damaging consequence is negated for the drink the Quran describes for the people of Paradise.

Ghawl in this verse therefore means harm, and “There is no harm in it” remains a brief, natural translation faithful to the word's semantic field.
