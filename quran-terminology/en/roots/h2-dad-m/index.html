Root H Ḍ M — Diminution and compression

Qur’anic and lexical forms

هَضْمًا
(haḍman)
Diminution of a due; verbal noun

هَضِيمٌ
(haḍīmun)
Soft or closely packed; adjective

From reducing a person’s due or a quantity to the tenderness and close packing of palm blossoms

The Arabic triliteral root ه ض م concerns diminishing, compressing, breaking, and reducing volume or quantity. Its applications may be physical, as in compression or a slender waist; concern rights, as in reducing someone’s share; or describe something tender and closely packed.

The Qur’an uses it in two different contexts: هَضْمًا in Ṭā Hā concerns diminution of a person’s due and reward, while هَضِيمٌ in al-Shuʿarāʾ describes palm spathes or emerging clusters, with senses of softness, tenderness, and close packing.

These usages require different translations. Each verse determines what is diminished or described as tender or compressed, and what belongs to the word itself rather than its context.

1. The root in early Arabic

Classical Arabic records هَضَمَ الشَّيْءَ for breaking, compressing, or diminishing something. The family can describe reduced quantity or volume, or a structure pressed inward.

هَضَمَ حَقَّهُ means diminishing someone’s right. By contrast, هَضَمْتُ لَهُ مِنْ حَقِّي can mean voluntarily relinquishing some of one’s own due for another person.

The root is therefore not invariably negative. Forcibly reducing another’s right is injustice; willingly giving up part of one’s own share still involves reduction without constituting the same wrongdoing.

The family also describes the body. أَهْضَمُ الْكَشْحَيْنِ denotes a person with slender flanks or waist; هَضِيمُ الْكَشْحِ likewise describes a fine, slender waist or flank.

This describes bodily shape and proportion, not actual pressure on the body, using vocabulary associated with reduced volume and compactness.

A pre-Qur’anic poetic witness

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd includes:

«وَلَا خَيْرَ فِيهِ غَيْرَ أَنَّ لَهُ غِنًى
وَأَنَّ لَهُ كَشْحًا إِذَا قَامَ أَهْضَمَا»

Wa-lā khayra fīhi ghayra anna lahu ghinan / wa-anna lahu kashḥan idhā qāma ahḍamā.

“There is no good in him except that he has wealth, and that, when he stands, he has a slender waist.”

The line satirizes a wealthy man: the poet recognizes no virtue beyond his possessions and slender waist.

أَهْضَمَا describes bodily shape, not deprivation of rights or injustice: the waist is slender and the flanks drawn in.

The line survives in versions of Ṭarafah’s collected poems and accounts of his satire of ʿAbd ʿAmr ibn Bishr. Its precise composition date is not independently established beyond later transmission, but it remains an examinable witness to this semantic branch in the pre-Islamic poetic tradition.

Reducing one’s possessions through generosity

Another branch describes generosity.

Poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā includes:

«فَأَمَّا إِذَا قَعَدُوا فِي النَّدِيِّ
فَأَحْلَامُ عَادٍ وَأَيْدٍ هُضُمْ»

Fa-ammā idhā qaʿadū fī n-nadiyyi / fa-aḥlāmu ʿĀdin wa-aydin huḍum.

“When they sit in assembly, theirs are the judgment of ʿĀd and generous hands.”

هُضُمْ is understood as the plural of هَضُوم, describing hands that diminish their owners’ wealth by giving it away.

Diminution need not deprive someone else: a person may reduce their own possessions to benefit another.

Generosity here emerges from the description of hands and the praise context. It does not define every use of ه ض م.

Physical uses and other branches

Arabic sources also record هَضْم for reducing volume or quantity, مُنْهَضِم for something compressed or broken, and هَضِيم for something tender or soft.

هِضْم can denote a depression in the ground or lower parts of a valley: land lying below its surroundings.

Classical Arabic also recognizes هَضْمُ الطَّعَامِ for digestion. Food changes from its initial state, but modern biological explanations of digestion are not proof of the word’s historical origin.

This study has not established independently datable pre-Qur’anic evidence for every branch. Early poetry illustrates bodily shape and generosity; classical lexicons record a wider range.

Reduction, compression, and softness help explain the family, but do not establish the historical order in which all its senses developed.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an, in two forms:

  • هَضْمًا — Ṭā Hā 20:112: a verbal noun denoting diminution of a right or rightful share.
  • هَضِيمٌ — al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148: an adjective on the faʿīl pattern describing palm clusters.

Arabic also records هَضَمَ, يَهْضِمُ, مَهْضُوم, هَضِيمَة, اِهْتَضَمَ, اِنْهَضَمَ, أَهْضَم, and هَاضِم, but these exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

The two Qur’anic forms differ in structure and meaning.

هَضْمًا is a noun, expressing diminution of a right in Ṭā Hā. هَضِيمٌ is an adjective describing a quality of palm clusters in al-Shuʿarāʾ.

The faʿīl pattern alone does not determine whether something is broken, soft, or compressed. Usage and the object described determine the meaning.

3. Haḍm — No diminution of a person’s due

Ṭā Hā 20:112

«وَمَن يَعْمَلْ مِنَ الصَّالِحَاتِ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَلَا يَخَافُ ظُلْمًا وَلَا هَضْمًا»

Wa-man yaʿmal mina ṣ-ṣāliḥāti wa-huwa muʾminun fa-lā yakhāfu ẓulman wa-lā haḍmā.

“Whoever does righteous deeds while being a believer fears neither injustice nor diminution of their due.”

هَضْمًا stands beside ظُلْمًا, both within reassurance to someone who believes and acts rightly.

The context concerns the Day of Account and human outcomes. Ṭā Hā 20:111 describes the failure of one burdened with injustice; Ṭā Hā 20:112 then describes the believing person who acts rightly.

The distinction between ẓulm and haḍm

Ẓulm is broad: in judgment and accounting it concerns unjust treatment and displacement from what is rightfully due.

Haḍm here specifically concerns reducing the right or share a person should receive.

Two situations illustrate the distinction:

Convicting someone of a crime they did not commit is ẓulm. Removing part of the reward due for their good deed diminishes, or subjects to haḍm, their entitlement.

Both involve injustice, but in different forms.

Accordingly, explanations of the verse distinguish unjust additions to a person’s sins from reductions in the reward for their good deeds.

This fits the context but is no universal definition of either root. Ẓulm is broader than adding sins, and haḍm here identifies a particular deprivation of a person’s due.

Some lexical analyses distinguish complete deprivation from partial reduction. This helps explain the pairing, but the phrase does not establish that every ẓulm is total deprivation or every haḍm necessarily partial.

Justice in the accounting of deeds

In Ṭā Hā 20:112, haḍm concerns what a person is due for righteous action.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:40 likewise states that The God does not wrong anyone by even an atom’s weight, and multiplies a good deed.

Justice in accounting is compatible with generosity and increased reward. What is denied is injustice and wrongful reduction of a person’s due.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 also describes just balances on the Day of Resurrection and no person being wronged.

Together with Ṭā Hā 20:112, these verses present divine accounting as just: human deeds are not disregarded arbitrarily.

Haḍm alone does not disclose every detail of reward, forgiveness, or accounting. It expresses diminution; the scope of the promise follows from the complete verse and other verses.

Is every reduction wrongful haḍm?

Arabic can describe both voluntary and imposed reduction.

Voluntarily relinquishing part of one’s property or share need not be unjust. Reducing another’s right without consent or just authorization raises the issue of deprivation.

In Ṭā Hā 20:112, divine judgment and reassurance to the believer make haḍm a deprivation of their due and reward.

Its negative value here follows from rights, reduction, and justice in context, not from every reduction being sinful throughout Arabic.

4. Haḍīm — Tenderness and close packing of palm clusters

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148

«وَزُرُوعٍ وَنَخْلٍ طَلْعُهَا هَضِيمٌ»

Wa-zurūʿin wa-nakhlin ṭalʿuhā haḍīm.

“And cultivated fields and palm trees whose emerging clusters are tender.”

هَضِيمٌ describes the palm’s طَلْع.

Ṭalʿ denotes the newly emerging, blossom-like part of a palm, initially enclosed within its covering and then exposed.

It concerns the emerging inflorescence and subsequent fruit formation. It should not invariably be translated as fully ripe, harvest-ready dates without attention to growth stage.

What quality does haḍīm describe?

Lexical sources record several nearby meanings for haḍīm applied to palm clusters.

One is softness and tenderness: the new growth has a soft, yielding texture.

Another is close packing: its parts lie densely together within their covering or alongside one another.

A further explanation associates it with ripening and softening fruit, which yields more readily to pressure.

These explanations belong to a related semantic range, but should not all be combined into one indisputable definition.

Softness is not identical to density. Something can be soft without being closely packed, or dense without being fully ripe and softened.

Both tenderness and close packing have lexical support for طَلْعُهَا هَضِيمٌ. The verse alone does not conclusively identify one particular stage of date ripening.

“Tender emerging clusters” is suitable for a basic translation; close packing can be explained in the lexical discussion.

Haḍīm, diminution, and compression

At first, reducing someone’s due and describing palm clusters may seem unrelated.

Physical usages include reduced volume, compactness, and deformation under pressure. Palm clusters can likewise be tender and closely packed.

One can therefore envisage a semantic association among diminishing quantity, compact structure, and yielding softness.

That image is not a proven historical derivation. The evidence does not establish that the palm sense developed from depriving people of rights, or vice versa.

The Qur’anic usages remain distinct: one concerns justice in accounting, the other a plant’s qualities.

The verse in Ṣāliḥ’s story

In al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:146, Ṣāliḥ asks whether his people expect to be left secure amid their present blessings.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:147 mentions gardens and springs; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148 adds cultivated fields and palms with tender clusters.

The passage depicts cultivation, flourishing settlement, and natural blessings.

The tender palm growth fits this favorable condition and enjoyment of blessings.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 mentions houses carved into mountains. Together, the verses present both natural resources and human abilities.

Haḍīm itself does not mean wealth, prosperity, security, or abundance. Those features arise from the broader context.

Nor should Ṣāliḥ’s warning about responsibility and consequences be turned into a botanical definition. The word describes the palm cluster; the larger message comes from its place in the narrative.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

ه ض م and ن ق ص — Diminution and reduction

Naqṣ denotes reduction in quantity, size, ability, or share.

In its rights-related usage, haḍm concerns a person’s entitlement or share: something has been taken from what is rightfully theirs.

Naqṣ thus expresses reduction more generally; expressions such as haḍm of a right focus on a person’s rightful share.

This does not make haḍm exclusively juridical or naqṣ exclusively physical. Both have wider ranges.

ه ض م and ب خ س — Diminishing a due and giving less

Qur’anic bakhs concerns shortchanging, reducing a right or value, and depriving someone of their proper share.

Hūd 11:85 forbids diminishing people’s things, close to the idea of depriving them of their due.

For example, failure to pay the rightful value or amount for goods raises the issue of bakhs. Reducing a person’s rightful reward in the accounting of deeds resembles haḍm in Ṭā Hā 20:112.

They are not perfect synonyms. Qur’anic bakhs clearly concerns unjust diminution; haḍm also has physical and descriptive branches in Arabic.

ه ض م and ظ ل م — Diminution of rights and injustice

The words stand together in Ṭā Hā 20:112.

Ẓulm broadly includes different forms of injustice, violation of rights, and harm.

Haḍm here emphasizes reduction of a person’s due.

Every unjust haḍm is a form of ẓulm, but not every ẓulm takes the form of diminishing a quantity or share.

The distinction avoids flattening both words into “injustice and injustice.”

ه ض م and ه ش م — Compression and fragmentation

The roots sound similar but are independent.

ه ش م appears in the Qur’an as hashīm, dry and fragmented vegetation.

ه ض م in haḍīm concerns the tenderness or close packing of palm clusters.

Both families can concern structural change. Plant-related hashm emphasizes breakage and fragmentation; haḍīm can emphasize tenderness and close arrangement.

Haḍīm should therefore not be translated as hashīm.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148 describes palms amid cultivation and blessings; al-Kahf 18:45 depicts dry, fragmented vegetation scattered by winds.

The contexts further clarify the words’ independence.

6. Suggested translations for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for هَضْمًا in Ṭā Hā 20:112 include “diminution of one’s due,” “loss of one’s rightful share,” or “reduction of the reward deserved.”

A suggested translation of:

«فَلَا يَخَافُ ظُلْمًا وَلَا هَضْمًا»

Fa-lā yakhāfu ẓulman wa-lā haḍmā.

is:

“So they fear neither injustice nor diminution of their due.”

This preserves the distinction without inserting the entire explanation of accounting into the short sentence.

For هَضِيمٌ in al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148, “tender” or “soft,” and in fuller explanation “closely packed,” are defensible.

A suggested translation of:

«وَزُرُوعٍ وَنَخْلٍ طَلْعُهَا هَضِيمٌ»

Wa-zurūʿin wa-nakhlin ṭalʿuhā haḍīm.

is:

“And cultivated fields and palm trees whose emerging clusters are tender.”

A lexical explanation can add that the description concerns both tenderness and the closely arranged parts of palm clusters.

Other usages require suitable equivalents: haḍm of rights means reducing a due; haḍm of food means digestion; أَهْضَمُ الْكَشْحَيْنِ means slender-waisted; هَضَمْتُ لَهُ مِنْ حَقِّي means relinquishing part of one’s own due.

One linguistic family does not require one translation for every sense.

7. Conclusion

ه ض م has branches involving reduction, reduced volume, compression, and softness. It can concern deprivation of rights, physical indentation or deformation, and tenderness or close packing in palm clusters.

Early poetic witnesses include a slender waist and generosity through diminishing one’s own possessions. They prevent reducing the entire family to injustice or digestion.

The Qur’an uses the family in two contexts.

In Ṭā Hā 20:112, هَضْمًا concerns deprivation of one’s due in accounting. The righteous believer is assured of neither injustice nor loss of a rightful share.

In al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148, هَضِيمٌ describes palm clusters, with senses of tenderness and close packing, amid the natural blessings enjoyed by Ṣāliḥ’s people.

Forqan definition: ه ض م includes diminution, reduced quantity or volume, and compression. Qur’anic هَضْمًا concerns reduction of a person’s rightful due; هَضِيمٌ describes tender palm clusters, with lexical associations of close packing.

These uses should not be forced into one translation. One concerns justice and rights; the other nature and palm produce. Their semantic associations aid understanding, but each verse’s construction and context determine its final meaning.
