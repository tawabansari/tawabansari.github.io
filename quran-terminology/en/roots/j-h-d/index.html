1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic triliteral root J Ḥ D has two established semantic branches. One concerns denying or refusing something encountered, or a right that should be acknowledged; the other concerns scarcity, hardship, diminished good, and deprivation. Early Arabic dictionaries record both branches, but all Qur’anic occurrences belong to the first: denial or juḥūd.

Arabic juḥūd means denial, with the significant qualification that many lexicographers define it as denial accompanied by knowledge. A person recognizes a truth or knows it to be correct, yet withholds acknowledgment and acceptance.

This distinction is especially explicit in Al-Naml: people denied divine signs while their own souls were certain of those signs.

The root’s entire range should nevertheless not be reduced to one moral definition. Jaḥada can describe denying another person’s right, whereas jaḥida, in another branch of the same family, concerns hardship and scarcity. Each usage requires separate consideration.

J Ḥ D occurs twelve times in the Qur’an, always as a Form I verb. Its Qur’anic forms are:

  • «جَحَدُوا» (jaḥadū): “they denied,” in Hūd 11:59 and Al-Naml 27:14.
  • «يَجْحَدُ» (yajḥadu): “he denies,” in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49, and Luqmān 31:32.
  • «يَجْحَدُونَ» (yajḥadūna): “they deny,” in Al-Anʿām 6:33, Al-Aʿrāf 7:51, Al-Naḥl 16:71, Ghāfir 40:63, Fuṣṣilat 41:15, Fuṣṣilat 41:28, and Al-Aḥqāf 46:26.

The active participle jāḥid, the verbal noun juḥūd, and the descriptive forms concerning straitened livelihood do not occur in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: twelve Form I verbs

The verb takes the preposition bi- in all twelve Qur’anic occurrences. Eleven concern divine signs, either named explicitly or referred to by the pronoun in bi-hā in Al-Naml 27:14. In Al-Naḥl 16:71, the object is niʿmat Allāh, “the favor of The God.” This difference helps define the Qur’anic field of use.

2. The root’s semantic core in early Arabic

Juḥūd as the opposite of acknowledgment

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْجَحْدُ وَالْجُحُودُ نَقِيضُ الْإِقْرَارِ

al-jaḥdu wa-al-juḥūdu naqīḍu al-iqrār

Translation: “Jaḥd and juḥūd are the opposite of acknowledgment.”

Ibn Manẓūr, Lisān: the opposite of acknowledgment

This identifies juḥūd’s fundamental relationship with iqrār. Acknowledgment means accepting something or admitting a right; juḥūd means denying it or withholding recognition. Brief root meaning: Denying or refusing to accept something that calls for acknowledgment; in its prominent sense, denial despite recognizing the truth.

For example, jaḥadahu ḥaqqahu describes denying another person’s right. The expression matters in human and legal relationships: someone may refuse to acknowledge an obligation owed to another.

Here juḥūd is more than expressing a different opinion. It concerns a right or claim that the person is expected to recognize or acknowledge.

A simple example clarifies the distinction. If someone genuinely does not know that property belongs to another, ignorance alone does not establish knowing denial. If the person knows it belongs to another yet denies that ownership, the conduct is a clear instance of juḥūd.

Knowledge in the definition of juḥūd

Al-Jawharī writes in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ:

الْجُحُودُ الْإِنْكَارُ مَعَ الْعِلْمِ

al-juḥūdu al-inkāru maʿa al-ʿilm

Translation: “Juḥūd is denial accompanied by knowledge.”

Al-Jawharī, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: denial with knowledge

This distinguishes juḥūd from denial arising from ignorance.

A person may deny something through ignorance, lack of evidence, or error. In Al-Jawharī’s precise sense, juḥūd occurs when someone refuses acknowledgment despite recognizing the truth.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī likewise explains in Al-Mufradāt:

الْجُحُودُ نَفْيُ مَا فِي الْقَلْبِ إِثْبَاتُهُ، وَإِثْبَاتُ مَا فِي الْقَلْبِ نَفْيُهُ

al-juḥūdu nafyu mā fī al-qalbi ithbātuhu wa-ithbātu mā fī al-qalbi nafyuhu

Translation: “Juḥūd is to deny what the heart affirms, and to affirm what the heart denies.”

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: inward conviction and outward assertion

This definition emphasizes the discrepancy between inward recognition and the outward position. A person accepts a truth inwardly but takes a contrary position in speech or conduct.

It does not follow that everyone who rejects a claim secretly knows it to be true. Establishing another person’s inward awareness is a separate question requiring evidence.

The other branch: scarcity and hardship

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris relates this root’s principle to a shortage of good:

الْجِيمُ وَالْحَاءُ وَالدَّالُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى قِلَّةِ الْخَيْرِ

al-jīmu wa-al-ḥāʾu wa-al-dālu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā qillati al-khayr

Translation: “Jīm, ḥāʾ, and dāl form a root indicating a scarcity of good.”

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: scarcity of good

He then gives examples from early Arabic.

ʿĀmun jaḥidun denotes a year of little rainfall.

Rajulun jaḥidun can describe someone poor, impoverished, or possessing little.

Arḍun jaḥdatun denotes dry land with little growth or yield.

Jaḥida al-nabtu is also recorded for vegetation growing sparsely or remaining short.

These usages do not concern denying truth. They describe reduction or shortage in something normally expected: rain, plants, wealth, or good.

Ibn Fāris also places denial within this family. The presence of both branches does not by itself prove that denying truth historically developed from the image of drought or poverty.

Withholding another person’s right may have a conceptual resemblance to deprivation or scarcity. Turning that resemblance into a definite account of the word’s origin, however, requires independent historical evidence.

It is therefore more precise to recognize the two principal branches: denial opposed to acknowledgment, and scarcity or diminished good. Only the first appears in the Qur’an.

3. Important Arabic forms and derivatives

Jaḥada is a Form I verb meaning “to deny.” Its imperfect is yajḥadu, and its two established verbal nouns are jaḥd and juḥūd.

Jāḥid is the active participle, “one who denies.” Its plural can be jāḥidūna or jāḥidīna. These are established Arabic forms, but they do not occur in the Qur’an.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib explains tajaḥḥada with takhaṣṣaṣa bi-fiʿli dhālika: becoming characterized by this conduct or engaging in juḥūd. This form is not Qur’anic.

Al-Rāghib: tajaḥḥada

In the scarcity branch, jaḥida is recorded for diminished good, difficult living conditions, or poverty.

Jaḥid and ajḥad can describe someone poor or possessing little. Mujḥid likewise describes someone whose wealth or resources have declined.

In descriptions of nature, jaḥdah denotes dry, unproductive land, and jaḥid describes a year of little rainfall.

Some sources also record farasun jaḥdun for a thickset, short horse. Other uncommon words in the family have different meanings; shared consonants alone do not establish a definite semantic history for all of them. Lisān al-ʿArab gives the horse adjective with a silent ḥāʾ; Al-Qāmūs and Tāj also record jaḥidun with kasrah on ḥāʾ, on the pattern katif.

Lisān, Qāmūs, and Tāj: the horse adjective

These forms establish the root’s linguistic range, while the Qur’anic study remains centered on jaḥada meaning “to deny.”

4. Pre-Islamic Arabic and usage near the time of revelation

Scarcity in poetry attributed to ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah says:

دَافَعْتُ عَنْ شَاسٍ بِشِعْرِيَ إِذْ
كَانَ لِقَوْمِي فِي الْفِدَاءِ جَحَدْ

dāfaʿtu ʿan shāsin bi-shiʿriya idh / kāna li-qawmī fī al-fidāʾi jaḥad

Translation: “I defended Shās through my poetry when my people lacked the means to pay his ransom.”

Jaḥad here concerns scarce resources. The context is freeing a captive and paying ransom, and the poet describes his poetry’s role in defending Shās.

The witness appears in the collection of poetry attributed to ʿAlqamah and in Ibn Durayd’s account, with variant wording. Jamharat al-Lughah gives dāfaʿtu ʿanhu bi-shiʿriya idh / kāna fī al-māli jaḥad, while the text quoted here has dāfaʿtu ʿan Shās and kāna li-qawmī fī al-fidāʾi jaḥad. An editorial note in the printed commentary on the six pre-Islamic poets reports a metrical difficulty in the dāfaʿtu ʿanhu version and records William Ahlwardt’s proposed correction, dāfaʿtuhu ʿanhu. That editorial proposal should not be presented without explanation as the poet’s certain original wording.

Commentary on ʿAlqamah: variant readings and a proposed metrical correction

The variant readings should therefore remain explicit. Both use jaḥad for scarcity or shortage.

Ibn Durayd, Jamharah: the ʿanhu and fī al-māl reading

This matters because the scarcity branch is supported by a witness attributed to a pre-Islamic poet, rather than only by later dictionary definitions.

Scarcity in livelihood

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits Al-Farrāʾ’s account of jaḥd and juḥd for straitened livelihood. His example jaḥida ʿayshuhum describes hard and restricted living conditions.

It also quotes a verse whose poet is not identified in the account:

لَئِنْ بَعَثَتْ أُمُّ الْحُمَيْدَيْنِ مَائِرًا
لَقَدْ غَنِيَتْ فِي غَيْرِ بُؤْسٍ وَلَا جَحْدِ

la-in baʿathat ummu al-ḥumaydayni māʾiran / la-qad ghaniyat fī ghayri buʾsin wa-lā jaḥd

Translation: “If Umm al-Ḥumaydayn has sent someone to obtain provisions, she previously lived free from hardship and want.”

Al-Farrāʾ in Lisān and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: the livelihood witness

Jaḥd occurs alongside buʾs here and concerns poverty and difficulty.

Since the transmitted account does not identify the poet or date, this cannot be called a certain pre-Islamic witness without further evidence. Its value is that lexicographers preserve the usage.

Losing wealth

A verse by the Umayyad poet Al-Farazdaq includes ḥumūlata mujḥid. Mujḥid here refers to someone with little property or resources.

Maqāyīs and Lisān: mujḥid in Al-Farazdaq

This witness postdates Qur’anic revelation and should not be equated with pre-Islamic poetry.

The scarcity branch therefore has literary witnesses, but the available examples differ in date and evidential certainty.

Denying and refusing a right

The expression jaḥadahu ḥaqqahu in early sources also establishes the root’s use in human and legal relationships.

The person refuses to recognize or acknowledge a right belonging to another.

This closely resembles Qur’anic juḥūd concerning divine signs and favors: both concern refusing something before which a person bears responsibility.

The settings nevertheless differ. Denial of a financial right, a divine sign, and a favor can have different motives and consequences.

In lexical and Qur’anic witnesses, jaḥd names the act of denial. Knowledge, intention, injustice, or ingratitude accompanying it are established through a precise lexical definition or contextual evidence.

5. Qur’anic usage: denial despite certainty

Al-Naml 27:14: the clearest witness

وَجَحَدُوا بِهَا وَاسْتَيْقَنَتْهَا أَنْفُسُهُمْ ظُلْمًا وَعُلُوًّا ۚ فَانظُرْ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الْمُفْسِدِينَ

wa-jaḥadū bi-hā wa-stayqanat-hā anfusuhum ẓulman wa-ʿuluwwan fa-nẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mufsidīn

Translation: “And they rejected them in wrongdoing and arrogance, although their souls were certain of them. So see how the corrupters ended.”

This verse belongs to the account of Moses confronting Pharaoh and his associates with divine signs.

Jaḥadū expresses their denial, while wa-stayqanat-hā anfusuhum establishes that these same people were inwardly certain of the signs’ truth.

The clauses stand together, revealing the distinction between their denying stance and inward recognition.

Ẓulman wa-ʿuluwwan then introduces wrongdoing and arrogance. The verse itself identifies the conduct’s motive or direction.

Their juḥūd is not simply the result of lacking evidence or being unable to recognize the truth. They received the signs and were certain of them, yet withheld acceptance.

This is the Qur’an’s strongest explicit witness for knowing denial.

Al-Ṭabarī: denial despite certainty

The verse’s specific assertion should nevertheless be distinguished from a universal generalization. It does not authorize proving hidden certainty whenever someone denies a statement, without examining evidence.

The Qur’an reports inward certainty concerning this group and event. Attributing the same condition to someone else requires independent evidence.

Al-Anʿām 6:33: distinguishing takdhīb from juḥūd

قَدْ نَعْلَمُ إِنَّهُ لَيَحْزُنُكَ الَّذِي يَقُولُونَ ۖ فَإِنَّهُمْ لَا يُكَذِّبُونَكَ وَلَٰكِنَّ الظَّالِمِينَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ يَجْحَدُونَ

qad naʿlamu innahu la-yaḥzunuka alladhī yaqūlūna fa-innahum lā yukadhdhibūnaka wa-lākinna al-ẓālimīna bi-āyāti Allāhi yajḥadūn

Translation: “We know that what they say grieves you. It is not you they deny; rather, the unjust reject the signs of The God.”

The verse places yukadhdhibūnaka and yajḥadūna side by side.

The first belongs to K DH B and concerns calling someone false or attributing untruth. The second belongs to J Ḥ D and concerns denying divine signs.

The verse distinguishes rejecting the Prophet personally as false from denying the signs of The God. Muhammad is addressed, and the subject is his grief at his opponents’ statements.

Rejecting a message does not always mean considering its bearer a liar in every matter. A person may outwardly oppose a message while holding a different judgment concerning the bearer’s honesty.

The verse does not establish that every opponent of Muhammad at every time was certain of his honesty. Its statement concerns a particular context and should not be extended to everyone.

Together with Al-Naml 27:14, it shows that Qur’anic juḥūd can involve a discrepancy between recognition and the position a person takes.

Juḥūd and clear signs

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47 and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49 use yajḥadu concerning divine signs.

The first concerns the book’s revelation and people among earlier scripture communities and others believing in it. It then attributes denial of the signs to the disbelievers.

The second speaks of clear signs and people given knowledge, and attributes denial to the unjust.

Both place juḥūd opposite signs and evidence. The descriptions of the groups arise from each verse’s own context.

One uses al-kāfirūn, and the other al-ẓālimūn. This difference should neither be removed nor turned into a claim that the words are exact synonyms throughout the Qur’an.

Juḥūd is the denying act in these verses; disbelief and injustice describe those to whom it is attributed.

6. Juḥūd concerning favor and responsibility

Al-Naḥl 16:71: denying a favor

وَاللَّهُ فَضَّلَ بَعْضَكُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فِي الرِّزْقِ ۚ فَمَا الَّذِينَ فُضِّلُوا بِرَادِّي رِزْقِهِمْ عَلَىٰ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُهُمْ فَهُمْ فِيهِ سَوَاءٌ ۚ أَفَبِنِعْمَةِ اللَّهِ يَجْحَدُونَ

wa-Allāhu faḍḍala baʿḍakum ʿalā baʿḍin fī al-rizqi fa-mā alladhīna fuḍḍilū bi-rāddī rizqihim ʿalā mā malakat aymānuhum fa-hum fīhi sawāʾun a-fa-bi-niʿmati Allāhi yajḥadūn

Translation: “The God has favored some of you over others in provision. Those favored do not hand their provision over to those whom their right hands possess, so that they would be equal in it. Do they then deny the favor of The God?”

The object of juḥūd here is niʿmat Allāh, rather than the signs of The God.

This matters: juḥūd can concern either the truth of a sign or a favor someone has received.

In ordinary language, a person may receive something yet refuse to acknowledge it or its giver.

The context concerns differences in provision and people’s relationship with their resources, followed by a question about denying divine favor.

Denying a favor does not necessarily mean denying the physical existence of wealth or provision. Someone may possess the benefit while refusing its source, reality, or associated responsibility. Nevertheless, every failure in using a benefit or fulfilling its responsibilities does not by itself establish juḥūd; evidence of denial is required.

“Do they deny the favor of The God?” preserves the sentence’s principal meaning. The context should explain the form that denial takes.

Luqmān 31:32: denial after deliverance

وَإِذَا غَشِيَهُم مَّوْجٌ كَالظُّلَلِ دَعَوُا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ فَلَمَّا نَجَّاهُمْ إِلَى الْبَرِّ فَمِنْهُم مُّقْتَصِدٌ ۚ وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا إِلَّا كُلُّ خَتَّارٍ كَفُورٍ

wa-idhā ghashiyahum mawjun ka-al-ẓulali daʿawū Allāha mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīna fa-lammā najjāhum ilā al-barri fa-minhum muqtaṣidun wa-mā yajḥadu bi-āyātinā illā kullu khattārin kafūr

Translation: “And when a wave like canopies covers them, they call upon The God, making religion sincere for Him. Then when He delivers them to land, some of them are moderate. And none rejects Our signs except everyone treacherous and ungrateful.”

People face danger and call sincerely upon The God. After deliverance, their conduct differs.

The end attributes juḥūd to someone characterized by khattār and kafūr.

Khattār concerns treachery or betrayal, while kafūr belongs to K F R and here relates to ingratitude.

These descriptions do not constitute the meaning of yajḥadu itself. They characterize the people who deny the signs of The God.

The verse shows how juḥūd can follow a vivid experience of danger and rescue, when a person’s response changes after deliverance.

Experience, recognition, conduct, and moral judgment should remain distinct. The verse does not portray all those rescued alike; it differentiates their responses.

7. Juḥūd, arrogance, and following power

Hūd 11:59: denial alongside disobedience

وَتِلْكَ عَادٌ ۖ جَحَدُوا بِآيَاتِ رَبِّهِمْ وَعَصَوْا رُسُلَهُ وَاتَّبَعُوا أَمْرَ كُلِّ جَبَّارٍ عَنِيدٍ

wa-tilka ʿādun jaḥadū bi-āyāti rabbihim wa-ʿaṣaw rusulahu wa-ittabaʿū amra kulli jabbārin ʿanīd

Translation: “That was Aad. They rejected the signs of their Lord, disobeyed His messengers, and followed the command of every stubborn tyrant.”

The verse names three actions by ʿĀd: denying the signs, disobeying the messengers, and following stubborn tyrants.

These expressions are related without having identical meanings.

Juḥūd is denial of the signs. ʿIṣyān is disobedience to a command. Following a jabbār ʿanīd concerns choosing the authority and command one follows in practice.

This sequence places juḥūd within a broader network of social and political conduct. A person may deny a truth while following a power opposed to it.

J Ḥ D itself does not primarily mean following tyrants. That meaning comes from ittabaʿū and the description jabbār ʿanīd.

Fuṣṣilat 41:15: power and arrogance

Fuṣṣilat 41:15 describes ʿĀd’s arrogance in the land and their claim to superior power. They ask who is stronger than they are, while the verse recalls their Creator’s power.

Its ending attributes denial of the signs to them.

Juḥūd here occurs amid pride in power and self-exaltation.

Istikbār and juḥūd are not synonymous. The former concerns arrogance and seeking superiority; the latter concerns denying the truth or signs.

The account of ʿĀd joins these two behaviors.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:26: faculties of perception alongside denial

Al-Aḥqāf 46:26 recalls resources and abilities given to a people: hearing, sight, and hearts. These faculties did not benefit them because they were denying the signs of The God.

Juḥūd here appears alongside possession of perceptual faculties.

Having ears, eyes, and a heart does not alone prove that each individual knows every truth. The point is that perceptual abilities and resources do not suffice to save people when accompanied by denial of the signs.

The verse thus highlights the difference between having the means to know and accepting the truth.

8. Other Qur’anic occurrences and their shared pattern

The remaining occurrences mostly use constructions such as jaḥada bi-āyāti Allāh, “denied the signs of The God.”

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:51, denial accompanies forgetting the encounter with the Last Day and the conduct of people who made their religion play and amusement.

In Ghāfir 40:63, denial is linked with the passive yuʾfaku: people being turned away from the truth. The context supplies the explanation of that deviation.

Al-Ṭabarī: deviation from truth and passive yuʾfaku

In Fuṣṣilat 41:28, denying the signs is given among the grounds for the opponents’ ruinous end.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47 and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49, juḥūd concerns divine signs and scripture, alongside descriptions of disbelief and injustice.

Several features emerge from these occurrences.

First, the Qur’an uses this root for denying divine signs, with divine favor explicitly named as the object in only one occurrence.

Second, denial repeatedly accompanies injustice, arrogance, disobedience, ingratitude, and following rebellious power. These associations matter to context without all belonging inside the root’s lexical definition.

Third, juḥūd is condemned in these Qur’anic settings because its objects are divine signs and favors. The judgment arises from the act’s relationship with its object and context.

Fourth, Al-Naml 27:14 explicitly shows denial accompanying inward certainty. This familiar lexical sense also matters elsewhere, while the degree of each person’s awareness requires contextual evidence.

9. Juḥūd, kufr, takdhīb, inkār, and kitmān

J Ḥ D (jaḥd) and K F R (kufr)

Study of K F R

Kufr has a wider Arabic semantic field, with covering among its principal meanings. The Qur’an uses this family in relation to faith, favor, ingratitude, and rejecting the truth.

Jaḥd concerns denial and withholding acknowledgment, especially when a person recognizes the truth or right at issue yet refuses acceptance.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47 attributes denial to the disbelievers; Luqmān 31:32 places it alongside kafūr.

These associations show that juḥūd can be a form of kufr toward truth or favor. The two roots nevertheless do not mean exactly the same thing.

Kufr has wider Qur’anic applications not all expressed through jaḥada. Nor does every Arabic use of jaḥada necessarily denote religious disbelief: it can describe denying another person’s financial right.

The two words therefore cannot be reduced to one Persian equivalent everywhere without examining context.

J Ḥ D (jaḥd) and K DH B (kadhib)

Study of K DH B

Kadhib concerns false speech or statements contrary to fact; takdhīb concerns judging a report or its bearer false.

Jaḥd concerns denying or refusing recognition to truth or a right.

Al-Anʿām 6:33 places lā yukadhdhibūnaka alongside bi-āyāti Allāhi yajḥadūna.

This contrast distinguishes treating the Prophet as false from denying the signs in that context.

It should not become an absolute rule that every takdhīb arises from ignorance while every juḥūd involves certainty. Takdhīb too can be knowing and deliberate. Each word’s precise force depends on its lexical meaning, object, and sentence structure.

J Ḥ D (jaḥd) and N K R (nukr and inkār)

Study of N K R

Arabic inkār can concern not recognizing, refusing, finding unfamiliar, or rejecting something.

This range is broader than knowing denial. Someone may reject something through unfamiliarity or lack of knowledge.

Juḥūd, in many lexicographers’ precise definition, specifically concerns denial accompanied by knowledge.

Inkār itself can nevertheless denote knowing rejection. Their difference is therefore not always a complete opposition between ignorance and knowledge.

Persian “to deny” can suit both. Where knowing juḥūd is at issue, the explanation “denial despite recognition” supplies greater precision.

J Ḥ D (jaḥd) and K T M (katm and kitmān)

Study of K T M

Kitmān concerns concealing something or withholding its disclosure.

A person may know a truth yet remain silent or prevent it becoming known. Juḥūd, especially in the explicit Al-Naml 27:14 example, takes a position of denial toward that truth.

Concealing truth and denying it are therefore distinct.

Someone may know another person’s right but remain silent: this can concern kitmān. If the same person explicitly denies that the right exists, the conduct can also constitute juḥūd.

In either case, moral responsibility depends on knowledge, freedom of choice, intention, and consequences. The two roots nevertheless retain different lexical meanings.

J Ḥ D (jaḥd) and ʿ N D (ʿinād)

Study of ʿ N D

ʿInād concerns stubbornness and obstinate opposition.

Juḥūd may arise from obstinacy, but J Ḥ D does not directly mean obstinacy itself.

In Hūd 11:59, ʿĀd deny the signs and follow every jabbār ʿanīd. The expressions bring denial, rebellion, and following power into one picture.

Juḥūd and ʿinād can thus be associated without being exact synonyms.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal Persian equivalent for jaḥada and its Qur’anic forms is inkār kardan, “to deny.”

Where the Qur’an mentions inward certainty or prior recognition, analysis can explain “denial despite recognition” or “knowing denial.” The addition need not be inserted into every translation irrespective of context.

Jaḥadū is appropriately rendered “they denied.”

Yajḥadu is “he denies,” and yajḥadūna is “they deny.”

In Al-Naml 27:14, wa-jaḥadū bi-hā wa-stayqanat-hā anfusuhum can be translated:

“And they denied them, although their souls were certain of them.”

This keeps denial distinct from inward certainty instead of absorbing wa-stayqanat-hā into jaḥadū itself.

In Al-Naḥl 16:71, a-fa-bi-niʿmati Allāhi yajḥadūna naturally means “Do they then deny the favor of The God?”

Where divine signs are at issue, “they deny Our signs” or “they deny the signs of The God” is sufficient.

Ingratitude, obstinacy, calling false, concealment, and disobedience may accompany juḥūd in particular contexts. None should consistently replace its principal meaning.

For non-Qur’anic usages such as ʿāmun jaḥidun and arḍun jaḥdatun, equivalents such as “a year of little rainfall” and “dry, unproductive land” are appropriate. These senses should not be transferred to the Qur’anic verb forms.

11. Conclusion

J Ḥ D has two established Arabic branches: denial opposed to acknowledgment, and scarcity or restriction of good and resources.

Lexical evidence and early poetry document the second branch for rain, plants, wealth, and livelihood. All Qur’anic occurrences belong to the denial branch.

Many lexicographers precisely define juḥūd as denial accompanied by knowledge. Al-Naml 27:14 provides an explicit example: people denied divine signs while remaining inwardly certain of them.

Other Qur’anic usages also concern the signs and favors of The God, in contexts involving injustice, arrogance, ingratitude, disobedience, and following tyrants. These behaviors clarify juḥūd’s moral setting without defining every derivative of the root.

Jaḥd relates semantically to kufr, takdhīb, inkār, kitmān, and ʿinād without being identical to any of them throughout their ranges. It emphasizes refusing and denying what calls for recognition or acknowledgment; the others express different aspects of covering, calling false, concealing, or stubborn opposition.

For Forqan, “to deny” is the principal natural equivalent for the Qur’anic forms. Where clear evidence establishes inward knowledge and certainty, “denial despite recognition” gives a more precise explanation.

The distinction matters because someone who has not yet recognized a truth differs from someone who recognizes it yet withholds acknowledgment. In its explicit instances of juḥūd, the Qur’an emphasizes human responsibility toward an encountered truth.
