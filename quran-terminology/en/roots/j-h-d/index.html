The Qur’anic Root J Ḥ D

Juḥūd: denying truth and refusing acknowledgment, even of what one knows

Arabic «جَحَدَ» (jaḥada) means denying something, refusing to accept it, or withholding acknowledgment. It is particularly significant where a truth, right, or sign is presented to someone who refuses to accept or acknowledge it.

«جَحَدَ حَقَّهُ» (jaḥada ḥaqqahu) is used for denying another person’s right. Such denial can coexist with knowledge that the right exists. Accordingly, several lexicographers define juḥūd as denial despite knowing the truth.

The clearest Qur’anic example concerns Moses and Pharaoh. Pharaoh and his companions denied the signs although inwardly certain of them. The Qur’an also uses the root elsewhere for denying divine signs or favor, turning from truth, and denial associated with wrongdoing, arrogance, and kufr.

An accurate study should distinguish the denial itself, the person’s knowledge of what they deny, and their motive. All three can occur together, but their details should not be packed into one word without examining the context.

1. Forms and semantic core

Arabic records forms including «جَحَدَ» (jaḥada), «يَجْحَدُ» (yajḥadu), «جَحْد» (jaḥd), «جُحُود» (juḥūd), «جَاحِد» (jāḥid), «جَحِد» (jaḥid), «أَجْحَدَ» (ajḥada), «تَجَحَّدَ» (tajaḥḥada), «جَحْدَة» (jaḥdah), and «مُجْحِد» (mujḥid). The verb jaḥada in the denial branch should be distinguished from jaḥida in the branch of scarcity and straitness: the middle radical’s vowel matters here.

These forms do not all share one meaning. Some concern denial and refusal to accept a right; others concern scarcity, poverty, drought, or diminished goodness and yield.

The root occurs twelve times in the Qur’an, always as a Form I verb. Two occurrences are perfect forms, «جَحَدُوا» (jaḥadū) and «وَجَحَدُوا» (wa-jaḥadū). Ten are imperfect «يَجْحَدُ» (yajḥadu) or «يَجْحَدُونَ» (yajḥadūna), with or without attached particles.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: twelve Form I verbs

The occurrences are Al-Anʿām 6:33, Al-Aʿrāf 7:51, Hūd 11:59, Al-Naḥl 16:71, Al-Naml 27:14, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49, Luqmān 31:32, Ghāfir 40:63, Fuṣṣilat 41:15, Fuṣṣilat 41:28, and Al-Aḥqāf 46:26.

No active participle, verbal noun, or independent adjective from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

Juḥūd in contrast to acknowledgment

Al-Khalīl’s Kitāb al-ʿAyn places juḥūd opposite acknowledgment. In this account, a person rejects something that calls for acceptance or acknowledgment.

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ offers a more specific definition:

«الْجُحُودُ الْإِنْكَارُ مَعَ الْعِلْمِ»

al-juḥūdu al-inkāru maʿa al-ʿilmi

Meaning: “Juḥūd is denial despite knowledge.”

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: denial despite knowledge

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī likewise explains it through a discrepancy between inward belief and outward expression:

«الْجُحُودُ نَفْيُ مَا فِي الْقَلْبِ إِثْبَاتُهُ، وَإِثْبَاتُ مَا فِي الْقَلْبِ نَفْيُهُ»

al-juḥūdu nafyu mā fī al-qalbi ithbātuhu wa-ithbātu mā fī al-qalbi nafyuhu

Meaning: “Juḥūd is denying what one inwardly affirms, or affirming what one inwardly denies.”

This definition goes beyond simple, uninformed rejection. A person creates a distance between what they know or believe inwardly and what they express.

There is nevertheless an important difference between a lexical definition and knowledge of any actual person’s condition.

If someone rejects a statement because they consider it false, that rejection alone does not establish knowing denial of truth. Even if their statement is objectively wrong, its wrongness does not independently prove that they inwardly recognized the truth.

Qur’anic research should therefore examine denial separately from the denier’s knowledge and certainty. Some verses explicitly state inward awareness; elsewhere, behavior and context indicate resistance to truth. Tāj al-ʿArūs also records a broader use for denial alongside the definition involving knowledge. Brief root meaning: Denying or refusing to accept something that calls for acknowledgment; in its prominent sense, denial despite recognizing the truth.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Mufradāt, and Tāj: acknowledgment, knowledge, and denial

2. Scarcity, poverty, and dry land in early Arabic

Besides denial, the Arabic family has a branch associated with scarcity and straitness.

Ibn Fāris’s Maqāyīs al-Lughah connects its root principle with «قِلَّةُ الْخَيْرِ» (qillatu al-khayr), a scarcity of goodness or benefit.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: scarcity of good and denial

Lexical sources use «عَامٌ جَحْدٌ» (ʿāmun jaḥdun) for a year with little rain, «أَرْضٌ جَحْدَةٌ» (arḍun jaḥdatun) for dry land with scant vegetation, and «جَحِدَ النَّبْتُ» (jaḥida al-nabtu) for plants that grew little and remained short.

These uses describe deficiency in quantity, benefit, or yield.

«رَجُلٌ جَحِدٌ» (rajulun jaḥidun) describes a person of little wealth or benefit. In some uses, it can also mean miserly or ungenerous.

The conditions should be distinguished: someone might genuinely lack anything to give, or have wealth but withhold it.

Scarcity of good in this branch can thus involve actual deprivation or a person’s ungenerosity. Without context, the word does not settle every financial or moral detail.

A witness concerning everyday life

Lexical sources transmit the following verse without a securely identified poet here:

«لَئِنْ بَعَثَتْ أُمُّ الْحُمَيْدَيْنِ مَائِرًا
لَقَدْ غَنِيَتْ فِي غَيْرِ بُؤْسٍ وَلَا جَحْدِ»

la-in baʿathat ummu al-ḥumaydayni māʾiran / la-qad ghaniyat fī ghayri buʾsin wa-lā jaḥdi

Meaning: “If Umm al-Ḥumaydayn has sent someone to obtain provisions, she previously lived free of hardship and want.” Here, ghaniyat is rendered as lived; freedom from hardship and want is expressed by «فِي غَيْرِ بُؤْسٍ وَلَا جَحْدِ» (fī ghayri buʾsin wa-lā jaḥdi).

«مَائِر» (māʾir) concerns someone who goes to obtain food and provisions. Buʾs and jaḥd occur together to describe hardship and straitened livelihood.

Here, jaḥd does not mean denying a truth. The subject is deprivation and difficult living conditions.

The poet and precise date are not established here. Its preservation in early dictionaries does not independently make it a securely dated pre-Islamic witness.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the provisions witness

A witness from Al-Farazdaq

Al-Farazdaq, a poet of the Islamic period, says:

«لِبَيْضَاءَ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْمَدِينَةِ لَمْ تَذُقْ
بَئِيسًا وَلَمْ تَتْبَعْ حَمُولَةَ مُجْحِدِ»

li-bayḍāʾa min ahli al-madīnati lam tadhuq / baʾīsan wa-lam tatbaʿ ḥamūlata mujḥidi

Meaning: “For a fair woman of Medina who has never tasted hardship or followed the caravan of an impoverished man.”

Mujḥid in this witness concerns insufficient wealth and resources.

It has no reference here to doctrinal denial or refusal of truth; it describes material circumstances and deprivation.

Lexical transmissions differ at the line’s opening, and Ibn Barrī prefers «لِبَيْضَاءَ» (li-bayḍāʾa). Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Maqāyīs also transmit «بَئِيسًا» (baʾīsan), while Lisān and Asās al-Balāghah have «يَبِيسًا» (yabīsan). The supplied reading is retained here; in the second reading, the image of dry food also helps explain a life unfamiliar with deprivation.

Lisān, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Asās: variants of Al-Farazdaq’s witness

Since Al-Farazdaq belongs to the Islamic period, the line witnesses older Arabic after the Qur’an’s revelation, rather than independently documenting pre-Islamic usage.

Do scarcity of good and denial of truth have one meaning?

Ibn Fāris also discusses denial under the branch of scarcity of good.

A conceptual relationship can be imagined: denying a right withholds acknowledgment, while withholding good prevents something beneficial from reaching someone else.

That connection alone does not establish that denial historically developed from poverty or dry land.

In actual usage, «جَحَدَ حَقَّهُ» (jaḥada ḥaqqahu) concerns denying a right; «عَامٌ جَحْدٌ» (ʿāmun jaḥdun) describes a year of little rain.

The usages should be recognized in their respective branches. The Qur’anic branch concerns denial and refusal to accept, rather than insufficient rain, wealth, or agricultural yield.

3. Denial despite certainty: the clearest Qur’anic example

Al-Naml 27:14

وَجَحَدُوا بِهَا وَاسْتَيْقَنَتْهَا أَنْفُسُهُمْ ظُلْمًا وَعُلُوًّا فَانْظُرْ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الْمُفْسِدِينَ

wa-jaḥadū bihā wa-istayqanat-hā anfusuhum ẓulman wa-ʿuluwwan fa-unẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mufsidīna

“And they rejected them in wrongdoing and arrogance, although their souls were certain of them. So see how the corrupters ended.”

This verse concerns Pharaoh and his companions’ response to the signs Moses brought.

The preceding Al-Naml 27:13 says that illuminating signs reached them and they called them evident magic.

Al-Naml 27:14 then describes their denial.

Three parts of the expression deserve particular attention.

First, «وَجَحَدُوا بِهَا» (wa-jaḥadū bihā) expresses the denial.

Second, «وَاسْتَيْقَنَتْهَا أَنْفُسُهُمْ» (wa-istayqanat-hā anfusuhum) states their inward certainty.

Third, «ظُلْمًا وَعُلُوًّا» (ẓulman wa-ʿuluwwan) identifies wrongdoing and self-exaltation in connection with their conduct.

The three are related, but each adds information of its own.

Outward denial and inward certainty

«وَاسْتَيْقَنَتْهَا أَنْفُسُهُمْ» (wa-istayqanat-hā anfusuhum) shows that simple ignorance or lack of evidence was not their only problem.

They had encountered the signs, and the Qur’an reports their inward certainty.

They nevertheless denied those very signs.

This is a particularly clear example of a gap between recognizing truth and accepting it in practice.

A person may know something, or inwardly be confident of its truth, yet refuse acknowledgment for other motives.

The main problem then is more than insufficient information: it concerns the person’s response to knowledge already acquired.

«وَجَحَدُوا» (wa-jaḥadū) and «وَاسْتَيْقَنَتْهَا» (wa-istayqanat-hā) are nevertheless separate verbs: the first expresses denial, the second certainty. Istīqān and juḥūd themselves are verbal nouns naming the two acts.

Even where lexicographers include awareness in juḥūd, the Qur’an independently emphasizes the strength and definiteness of that awareness here.

Al-Ṭabarī: inward certainty and denial

Wrongdoing and self-exaltation

The verse places «ظُلْمًا وَعُلُوًّا» (ẓulman wa-ʿuluwwan) after the report of denial and certainty.

The words show that their denial accompanied wrongdoing and self-exaltation.

Their response cannot therefore be reduced to a neutral intellectual disagreement. The Qur’an itself identifies its moral character or motive.

Wrongdoing and self-exaltation should not, however, be made permanent components of the verb jaḥada’s morphology or meaning.

Elsewhere, denial can accompany kufr, ingratitude, or rebellion. Which feature applies in a particular setting must be learned from that verse and its context.

Here, knowing denial, wrongdoing, and self-exaltation together present resistance to truth clearly.

The Qur’an reports Pharaoh and his companions’ inward condition, but other humans do not have comparable direct access to people’s inner states.

The verse therefore does not authorize assuming, without evidence, that every intellectual opponent inwardly knows the truth and deliberately denies it.

4. Denying the signs and charging the messenger with falsehood

Al-Anʿām 6:33

قَدْ نَعْلَمُ إِنَّهُ لَيَحْزُنُكَ الَّذِي يَقُولُونَ فَإِنَّهُمْ لَا يُكَذِّبُونَكَ وَلَٰكِنَّ الظَّالِمِينَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ يَجْحَدُونَ

qad naʿlamu innahu la-yaḥzunuka alladhī yaqūlūna fa-innahum lā yukadhdhibūnaka wa-lākinna al-ẓālimīna bi-āyāti Allāhi yajḥadūna

“We know that what they say grieves you. It is not you they deny; rather, the unjust reject the signs of The God.”

The verse distinguishes charging the messenger with falsehood from denying divine signs.

Its opening mentions words that grieved the messenger, followed by «لَا يُكَذِّبُونَكَ» (lā yukadhdhibūnaka).

The continuation «وَلَٰكِنَّ الظَّالِمِينَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ يَجْحَدُونَ» (wa-lākinna al-ẓālimīna bi-āyāti Allāhi yajḥadūna) directs attention to denial of divine signs.

Someone may recognize a messenger’s personal honesty yet resist the message or truth he brings.

This does not establish that every opponent accepted his honesty in every situation. Other Qur’anic passages describe messengers being accused of falsehood.

Here, the verse emphasizes the group’s denial of the signs. Al-Ṭabarī records both recognizing truthfulness while verbally denying it out of obstinacy, and distinguishing the messenger from what he brought. The distinction therefore does not exclude every spoken accusation against him.

Distinguishing yukadhdhibūnaka from yajḥadūna

«يُكَذِّبُونَكَ» (yukadhdhibūnaka) is Form II of K DH B and concerns considering the addressee a liar. The lighter reading «لَا يُكْذِبُونَكَ» (lā yukdhibūnaka) is also recorded. The quotation here retains the doubled form of the installed text.

Al-Ṭabarī: takdhīb, juḥūd, and two readings

«يَجْحَدُونَ» (yajḥadūna) is Form I of J Ḥ D, here concerning denial of divine signs.

The difference in object and sentence construction also matters.

The first clause’s suffix ka refers to the messenger; in the second, bi-āyāti Allāhi identifies what is denied.

The verse thus does more than repeat neighboring words. It distinguishes the messenger’s position from that of the divine signs in the opponents’ response.

Juḥūd can therefore concern rejecting something’s truth even where the issue is more than calling its bearer a liar.

Denial and disobedience in the story of ʿĀd

وَتِلْكَ عَادٌ جَحَدُوا بِآيَاتِ رَبِّهِمْ وَعَصَوْا رُسُلَهُ وَاتَّبَعُوا أَمْرَ كُلِّ جَبَّارٍ عَنِيدٍ

wa-tilka ʿādun jaḥadū bi-āyāti rabbihim wa-ʿaṣaw rusulahu wa-ittabaʿū amra kulli jabbārin ʿanīdin

“That was Aad. They rejected the signs of their Lord, disobeyed His messengers, and followed the command of every stubborn tyrant.” (Hūd 11:59)

Three behaviors occur together: denying signs, disobeying messengers, and following obstinate tyrants’ commands.

Jaḥadū expresses denial, ʿaṣaw disobedience, and ittabaʿū following.

The acts are related but not synonymous.

A person may outwardly deny a statement, disobey an order, or follow another authority. The verse attributes all three to ʿĀd.

Fuṣṣilat 41:15 also concerns ʿĀd. They acted arrogantly on earth without right and asked who was stronger than themselves, before the verse describes their denial of divine signs.

Their denial is thus connected contextually with a sense of power and superiority.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:26 likewise mentions their abilities, hearing, sight, and hearts, which did not protect them from denial’s consequences.

Across these passages, juḥūd is more than a brief spoken answer. It relates to a way of life, chosen authority, and response to divine warnings.

Each behavior should nevertheless retain its own meaning. Juḥūd alone does not mean following a tyrant or being arrogant; these features emerge from the story’s context.

5. Denying favor and ingratitude

Al-Naḥl 16:71

وَاللَّهُ فَضَّلَ بَعْضَكُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فِي الرِّزْقِ فَمَا الَّذِينَ فُضِّلُوا بِرَادِّي رِزْقِهِمْ عَلَىٰ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُهُمْ فَهُمْ فِيهِ سَوَاءٌ أَفَبِنِعْمَةِ اللَّهِ يَجْحَدُونَ

wa-Allāhu faḍḍala baʿḍakum ʿalā baʿḍin fī al-rizqi fa-mā alladhīna fuḍḍilū bi-rāddī rizqihim ʿalā mā malakat aymānuhum fa-hum fīhi sawāʾun a-fa-bi-niʿmati Allāhi yajḥadūna

“The God has favored some of you over others in provision. Those favored do not hand their provision over to those whom their right hands possess, so that they would be equal in it. Do they then deny the favor of The God?”

Here, juḥūd concerns favor rather than the signs.

«أَفَبِنِعْمَةِ اللَّهِ يَجْحَدُونَ» (a-fa-bi-niʿmati Allāhi yajḥadūna) identifies divine favor as the object of denial.

Juḥūd should consequently not be confined to rejecting a doctrinal proposition or report concerning messengers.

Someone may receive and enjoy a favor yet fail to acknowledge its due or source in their conduct and judgment. Al-Ṭabarī also connects this passage with rejecting the assignment of partners to The God; that interpretation should be distinguished from the verb’s lexical meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: favor and rejecting partners

The next verse, Al-Naḥl 16:72, has «وَبِنِعْمَتِ اللَّهِ هُمْ يَكْفُرُونَ» (wa-bi-niʿmati Allāhi hum yakfurūna).

Juḥūd and kufr in successive verses show their proximity in the context of favor.

That proximity does not make the roots entirely identical.

Jaḥd emphasizes denial and nonacceptance; K F R has a broader range involving covering truth, ingratitude, and rejecting truth.

Ingratitude can be a consequence of denying favor in this setting.

Is denying favor confined to words?

Denying favor does not always take the form of saying that one has no favor at all.

Someone may acknowledge its existence yet attribute it to themselves or another power in a way that disregards its giver or the responsibility arising from it.

Care is needed in distinguishing practical ingratitude from juḥūd in its precise lexical sense.

Not every misuse of a favor necessarily constitutes juḥūd. A person may make a mistake, waste resources, or fail to fulfill someone’s right without denying the favor itself.

Al-Naḥl explicitly connects denial with favor; applying that description to a specific present-day action requires separate grounds.

Luqmān 31:32: turning in danger and denying after deliverance

وَإِذَا غَشِيَهُمْ مَوْجٌ كَالظُّلَلِ دَعَوُا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ فَلَمَّا نَجَّاهُمْ إِلَى الْبَرِّ فَمِنْهُمْ مُقْتَصِدٌ وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا إِلَّا كُلُّ خَتَّارٍ كَفُورٍ

wa-idhā ghashiyahum mawjun ka-al-ẓulali daʿaw Allāha mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīna fa-lammā najjāhum ilā al-barri fa-minhum muqtaṣidun wa-mā yajḥadu bi-āyātinā illā kullu khattārin kafūrin

“And when a wave like canopies covers them, they call upon The God, making religion sincere for Him. Then when He delivers them to land, some of them are moderate. And none rejects Our signs except everyone treacherous and ungrateful.”

The verse compares human response during danger with behavior after rescue.

When great waves cover them, they call upon The God sincerely.

After reaching land, the verse says fa-minhum muqtaṣidun. Forqan currently translates this as some being moderate. Early commentary interprets the description differently: Al-Ṭabarī transmits both moderation in speech and acknowledgment alongside inward disbelief, and a state of uprightness. The word alone therefore should not establish complete faith for everyone in this group.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing interpretations of muqtaṣid

The verse then attributes denial of the signs to someone described as khattārin kafūrin.

Khattār concerns treachery and betrayal; kafūr concerns ingratitude and kufrān.

They accompany juḥūd but retain their own meanings.

Turning to The God during danger does not by itself guarantee a person’s subsequent conduct.

Fa-minhum concerns some of those rescued. A uniform judgment about them all should not be made without examining the context and differing interpretations of muqtaṣid.

This differs from Al-Naml 27:14. Pharaoh’s story explicitly states inward certainty, whereas Luqmān associates denial with treachery and ingratitude.

The passages allow different motives and accompanying features to be recognized without requiring them all in every use. The Luqmān 31:32 translation quoted here matches the current site; the installed verse itself remains unchanged.

6. Juḥūd, kufr, and wrongdoing across the verses

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47

وَكَذَٰلِكَ أَنْزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ فَالَّذِينَ آتَيْنَاهُمُ الْكِتَابَ يُؤْمِنُونَ بِهِ وَمِنْ هَٰؤُلَاءِ مَنْ يُؤْمِنُ بِهِ وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا إِلَّا الْكَافِرُونَ

wa-kadhālika anzalnā ilayka al-kitāba fa-alladhīna ātaynāhumu al-kitāba yuʾminūna bihi wa-min hāʾulāʾi man yuʾminu bihi wa-mā yajḥadu bi-āyātinā illā al-kāfirūna

“Thus We sent down the Book to you. Those to whom We have given the Book believe in it, and among these are some who believe in it. None reject Our signs except the disbelievers.”

The verse mentions two groups believing in the revealed Book, then connects denying signs with the disbelievers.

Wa-mā yajḥadu bi-āyātinā illā al-kāfirūna expresses restriction: denying these signs is attributed to the disbelievers.

This does not make juḥūd and kufr exactly synonymous throughout the language.

The verse clarifies their relationship within this particular setting.

By identifying believers among the People of the Book, it also shows that membership of a religious community alone does not determine every member’s response.

The preceding Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:46 also matters: it commands the best manner of discussion with the People of the Book, except those among them who do wrong.

The context itself preserves distinctions between people and behaviors.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49

بَلْ هُوَ آيَاتٌ بَيِّنَاتٌ فِي صُدُورِ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا إِلَّا الظَّالِمُونَ

bal huwa āyātun bayyinātun fī ṣudūri alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma wa-mā yajḥadu bi-āyātinā illā al-ẓālimūna

“Rather, it is clear signs in the breasts of those given knowledge. None reject Our signs except the wrongdoers.”

The same restrictive construction occurs, with al-ẓālimūna replacing al-kāfirūna.

That difference matters.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47 connects denial of signs with kufr; Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49 connects it with wrongdoing.

In these two settings, denying divine signs is censured conduct associated with kufr and wrongdoing.

It does not follow that every Qur’anic use of kufr or wrongdoing is limited to juḥūd.

All three roots have broader ranges of meaning.

Other occurrences concerning denial of signs

Al-Aʿrāf 7:51 describes people who made their religion play and diversion, were deceived by worldly life, and denied the divine signs.

Ghāfir 40:63 connects denial of signs with being turned away from truth, describing people who denied the signs and were drawn into falsehood.

Fuṣṣilat 41:28 presents denial of the signs as grounds for the recompense of a group of divine enemies.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:26 describes people with strength and faculties of knowledge whose hearing, sight, and hearts did not save them from denial’s consequences.

These uses predominantly set juḥūd against divine signs and proofs.

In these passages, signs and proofs are more than information to hear and remember: the response to them is connected with human responsibility, conduct, and choice.

Possessing faculties of knowledge does not independently prove that every person has comprehended all truth. Qur’anic judgments about deniers rest on The God’s complete knowledge of their condition.

That distinction should be preserved when assessing other people.

7. Distinguishing J Ḥ D from neighboring roots

J Ḥ D (jaḥd) and K F R (kufr)

Arabic K F R concerns covering and has a broad Qur’anic range involving ingratitude, covering truth, and rejecting truth.

Qur’anic J Ḥ D emphasizes denying and refusing what calls for acknowledgment and acceptance.

Juḥūd can therefore be one form of kufr, particularly when someone rejects a clear sign.

They are not entirely synonymous: not every use of kufr is expressed through juḥūd or an explicit construction of denial.

J Ḥ D (jaḥd) and K DH B (kadhib)

Kadhib concerns lying and speaking contrary to fact. Qur’anic takdhīb can mean treating a report, message, or person as false.

Jaḥd likewise involves rejection and denial, but its construction need not mean calling the speaker a liar.

Al-Anʿām 6:33 illustrates this by distinguishing lā yukadhdhibūnaka from bi-āyāti Allāhi yajḥadūna.

Juḥūd and takdhīb can nevertheless coincide: someone might deny a truth and accuse its bearer of lying.

Overlap in a particular act does not make the roots identical.

Study of K DH B

J Ḥ D (jaḥd) and N K R (inkār)

Arabic N K R concerns not recognizing, not accepting, and denying.

Its Qur’anic derivatives occur in different settings. Yūsuf 12:58 describes Joseph recognizing his brothers while they do not recognize him; munkirūna there concerns failure to recognize a person.

The family can thus occur without an intention knowingly to deny truth.

Qur’anic juḥūd concerns rejecting divine signs or favor, while lexical sources connect it with refusing something known to the person.

Not every act of rejection is therefore knowing juḥūd, particularly where someone genuinely does not recognize something.

The distinction should not be absolute, since inkār can itself express knowing rejection in some contexts.

Study of N K R

J Ḥ D (jaḥd) and K T M (kitmān)

Kitmān concerns concealing something.

Al-Baqarah 2:146 describes a group who know the truth but conceal it.

Kitmān there concerns keeping truth hidden, whereas juḥūd can concern rejecting and refusing it.

Someone might know a truth and remain silent, or openly state that they reject it.

The consequences may be close, but the acts are not identical.

Al-Naml 27:14 describes denial despite certainty. Concealment without expressed denial would raise the concept of kitmān, but the verse’s chosen verb is jaḥadū.

Study of K T M

J Ḥ D (jaḥd) and J H D (jahd)

J Ḥ D and J H D are distinct roots.

J H D concerns effort, exerting capacity, and enduring difficulty in a task; jihād and mujāhadah belong to that family.

Qur’anic J Ḥ D concerns denial and refusal to accept.

Their written resemblance and the similarity of some unvowelled forms should not lead to conflating them.

Qur’anic yajḥadūna belongs to J Ḥ D, not J H D.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For Qur’anic J Ḥ D, “deny” is a natural, clear, defensible equivalent.

Wa-jaḥadū bihā in Al-Naml 27:14 can be rendered “they denied them.” Inward certainty has its own expression, wa-istayqanat-hā anfusuhum, and wrongdoing and self-exaltation are expressed by ẓulman wa-ʿuluwwan.

All those components need not be repeated within the translation of jaḥadū itself.

Yajḥadūna in Al-Anʿām 6:33 likewise means denying, while its difference from yukadhdhibūnaka should be explained.

In Al-Naḥl 16:71, the object is favor. “They deny the favor of The God” fits the construction; “they are ungrateful” can convey an implication while weakening the direct sense of denial.

Luqmān 31:32 places juḥūd alongside khattārin kafūrin. A suitable translation distinguishes denial, treachery, and ingratitude.

Where signs are concerned, as in Al-Aʿrāf 7:51, Hūd 11:59, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49, Ghāfir 40:63, Fuṣṣilat 41:15, Fuṣṣilat 41:28, and Al-Aḥqāf 46:26, “denying the signs” is appropriate.

A brief root explanation can read:

“J Ḥ D: denying or refusing to accept something that calls for acknowledgment; in its prominent sense, denial despite recognizing the truth.”

For the meaning in the Qur’anic verses:

“Jaḥd: rejecting and denying divine signs or favor; the denier’s awareness and motive are learned from each verse’s context.”

This retains the important feature of knowing denial without enabling unsupported judgments about everyone’s inner state.

Conclusion

Arabic J Ḥ D has two important branches: denial and nonacceptance, and scarcity or straitness in wealth, goodness, or agricultural yield.

The Qur’anic uses concern the first branch. Denial of divine signs and favor appears in contexts associating it with wrongdoing, kufr, arrogance, rebellion, and ingratitude.

Al-Naml 27:14 is a particularly clear example of knowing denial: Pharaoh and his companions deny the signs despite their stated inward certainty.

Recognizing and accepting truth are different. A person may recognize it yet withhold acknowledgment through wrongdoing, self-interest, or self-exaltation.

Refusing a statement does not independently prove knowledge of its truth. Denial, awareness, and motive should be distinguished.

The central, defensible Qur’anic meaning of juḥūd is rejecting divine signs or favor presented to a person. In its prominent, familiar sense, it is denial despite knowledge of the truth; determining any person’s degree of knowledge and motive nevertheless requires independent evidence.

Juḥūd should therefore not be equated with simple ignorance, sincere doubt, or refusal of an unsupported claim. Nor should every opponent be accused of knowing denial without understanding their actual situation.
