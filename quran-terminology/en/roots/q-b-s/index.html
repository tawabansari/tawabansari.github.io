The Qur’anic Root Q B S

Taking fire from another fire: from a flame to acquiring light and knowledge

Qur’anic forms

قَبَس

qabas

A firebrand
نَقْتَبِسْ

naqtabis

That we may take light

In early Arabic, Q B S concerns taking fire from an already burning source. Qabas is a flame or piece of fire taken from another fire or carried elsewhere for use.

This sense is clearly recorded in the Arabic lexical tradition and confirmed by Qur’anic usage. Lexical documentation alone, however, does not establish the precise pre-Islamic date of every usage; no securely dated pre-Islamic poetic witness is presented here.

In everyday life, someone could light the end of a stick by bringing it close to a burning fire, then carry it elsewhere for light, warmth, or kindling another fire.

The same material image extends to nonmaterial uses. Just as someone takes a flame from another’s fire, they can benefit from another’s knowledge.

Forms examined: qabasa (قَبَسَ), qabas (قَبَس), aqbās (أَقْبَاس), qābis (قَابِس), aqbasa (أَقْبَسَ), iqtabasa (اِقْتَبَسَ), iqtibās (اِقْتِبَاس), miqbās (مِقْبَاس), and miqbas (مِقْبَس).

Qur’anic forms: qabas and the Form VIII verb iqtabasa in the form naqtabis. There are three occurrences: two nouns and one verb.

1. Core meaning and distinctions among derivatives

This word family distinguishes fire taken from a source, taking fire, and giving fire to another person.

Qabas names a flame or piece of fire taken from a burning source.

Qabasa and iqtabasa express taking fire and, more broadly, learning from another person. Aqbasa can mean giving someone fire or imparting knowledge to them.

A distinctive feature is the relationship between what is acquired and its source. Taking fire can light another fire without extinguishing the first. This also provides an apt image for acquiring knowledge: teaching someone does not diminish the teacher’s knowledge.

2. Qabas in the story of Moses

إِذْ رَأَىٰ نَارًا فَقَالَ لِأَهْلِهِ امْكُثُوا إِنِّي آنَسْتُ نَارًا لَعَلِّي آتِيكُمْ مِنْهَا بِقَبَسٍ أَوْ أَجِدُ عَلَى النَّارِ هُدًى

Idh raʾā nāran fa-qāla li-ahlihi umkuthū innī ānastu nāran laʿallī ātīkum minhā bi-qabasin aw ajidu ʿalā l-nāri hudā.

When he saw a fire and said to his family, “Stay here. I have perceived a fire; perhaps I will bring you a firebrand from it, or find guidance at the fire.”

Ta-Ha 20:10

Moses sees a fire at a distance and hopes to bring some of it to his family or find guidance there.

Minhā bi-qabasin explicitly connects the firebrand to its source. Moses does not intend to bring the whole fire, but to take a portion of it.

Sūrat al-Naml recounts the same event with a different expression:

إِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِأَهْلِهِ إِنِّي آنَسْتُ نَارًا سَآتِيكُمْ مِنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ آتِيكُمْ بِشِهَابٍ قَبَسٍ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ

Idh qāla Mūsā li-ahlihi innī ānastu nāran sa-ātīkum minhā bi-khabarin aw ātīkum bi-shihābin qabasin laʿallakum taṣṭalūn.

When Moses said to his family, “I have perceived a fire. I will bring you news from it, or bring you a burning brand, so that you may warm yourselves.”

An-Naml 27:7

In shihābin qabasin, shihāb emphasizes the fire’s brilliance and flame, while qabas emphasizes its being taken from a source.

The transmitted readings include both shihābin qabasin, with nunation, and shihābi qabasin, a genitive construction. In the first, qabas can qualify shihāb or stand in apposition to it; in the second, the genitive construction specifies the kind of flame. Both concern a flame taken from a fire.

In Ṭā Hā, Moses seeks fire or guidance; in al-Naml, news or fire to warm his family. What he encounters, however, begins his reception of divine guidance and commission.

The connection between seeking material fire and receiving guidance emerges from the narrative. The lexical meaning of qabas remains a piece of fire.

3. Acquiring light on the day of resurrection

In Sūrat al-Ḥadīd, Q B S moves from the setting of material fire to a resurrection scene.

يَوْمَ يَقُولُ الْمُنَافِقُونَ وَالْمُنَافِقَاتُ لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا انْظُرُونَا نَقْتَبِسْ مِنْ نُورِكُمْ قِيلَ ارْجِعُوا وَرَاءَكُمْ فَالْتَمِسُوا نُورًا فَضُرِبَ بَيْنَهُمْ بِسُورٍ لَهُ بَابٌ بَاطِنُهُ فِيهِ الرَّحْمَةُ وَظَاهِرُهُ مِنْ قِبَلِهِ الْعَذَابُ

Yawma yaqūlu l-munāfiqūna wa-l-munāfiqātu li-lladhīna āmanū unẓurūnā naqtabis min nūrikum. Qīla irjiʿū warāʾakum fa-ltamisū nūran fa-ḍuriba baynahum bi-sūrin lahu bābun bāṭinuhu fīhi l-raḥmatu wa-ẓāhiruhu min qibalihi l-ʿadhāb.

On the day when the hypocritical men and women say to those who have believed, “Wait for us so that we may take some of your light,” it will be said, “Go back behind you and seek light.” Then a wall will be set between them, with a gate: within it is mercy, and outside it, on that side, is punishment.

Al-Hadid 57:13

Naqtabis is a first-person plural imperfect verb in Form VIII. Its final consonant is vowelless because it is jussive in response to the request unẓurūnā: “Wait for us so that we may take some of your light.”

The pattern of taking something from another source remains, but what is requested here is light.

The preceding verse, Al-Ḥadīd 57:12, describes believing men and women whose light moves before them and on their right.

The hypocrites hope to benefit from the believers’ light, just as in worldly life one can take a flame from another’s fire. Their request, however, is unsuccessful.

The next verse explains this deprivation:

يُنَادُونَهُمْ أَلَمْ نَكُنْ مَعَكُمْ قَالُوا بَلَىٰ وَلَٰكِنَّكُمْ فَتَنْتُمْ أَنْفُسَكُمْ وَتَرَبَّصْتُمْ وَارْتَبْتُمْ وَغَرَّتْكُمُ الْأَمَانِيُّ حَتَّىٰ جَاءَ أَمْرُ اللَّهِ وَغَرَّكُمْ بِاللَّهِ الْغَرُورُ

Yunādūnahum a-lam nakun maʿakum. Qālū balā wa-lākinnakum fatantum anfusakum wa-tarabbaṣtum wa-rtabtum wa-gharratkumu l-amāniyyu ḥattā jāʾa amru llāhi wa-gharrakum bi-llāhi l-gharūr.

They call to them, “Were we not with you?” They say, “Yes, but you exposed yourselves to temptation, waited, doubted, and were deceived by wishes until the command of The God came; and the deceiver deceived you concerning The God.”

Al-Hadid 57:14

Outward association with believers does not replace faith and personal responsibility. In this world, people can benefit from others’ knowledge, guidance, and experience, but mere association does not entitle them to the fruits of those others’ faith on the day of reckoning.

The principle of individual responsibility also appears in Al-Anʿām 6:164 and Al-Najm 53:39.

4. Acquiring knowledge and the development of usage

The Arabic expression iqtabasa minhu ʿilman means acquiring knowledge from someone else.

In modern Arabic and Persian, iqtibās also denotes quoting a passage, drawing on another’s ideas, or adapting an earlier work.

In rhetoric, it acquired a specialized sense: incorporating a Qur’anic or hadith expression into speech or writing.

These literary uses draw on taking something from another source. The later technical meaning should not, however, be imposed on Qur’anic usage.

5. Comparison with related roots

Q B S (qabas) and J DH W (jadhwah)

In al-Qaṣaṣ, the Qur’an uses jadhwah in recounting the same event in Moses’ life:

فَلَمَّا قَضَىٰ مُوسَى الْأَجَلَ وَسَارَ بِأَهْلِهِ آنَسَ مِنْ جَانِبِ الطُّورِ نَارًا قَالَ لِأَهْلِهِ امْكُثُوا إِنِّي آنَسْتُ نَارًا لَعَلِّي آتِيكُمْ مِنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ جَذْوَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ

Fa-lammā qaḍā Mūsā l-ajala wa-sāra bi-ahlihi ānasa min jānibi l-ṭūri nāran. Qāla li-ahlihi umkuthū innī ānastu nāran laʿallī ātīkum minhā bi-khabarin aw jadhwatin mina l-nāri laʿallakum taṣṭalūn.

When Moses had completed the term and set out with his family, he perceived a fire on the side of the Mount. He said to his family, “Stay here. I have perceived a fire; perhaps I will bring you news from it, or a firebrand, so that you may warm yourselves.”

Al-Qasas 28:29

Jadhwah chiefly denotes a burning piece, ember, or wood carrying fire, whereas qabas emphasizes fire taken from another source.

A burning piece of wood may be both a jadhwah and a qabas. Each word highlights a different aspect of the same object.

Q B S (qabas) and SH H B (shihāb)

Shihāb concerns brilliance and flame. The Qur’an uses it for both a flame of fire and luminous phenomena in the sky.

Qabas denotes fire taken from another source. Not every shihāb is therefore necessarily a qabas.

Their combination in Al-Naml 27:7 describes a bright flame taken from a fire.

Q B S (qabas) and W Q D (wuqūd)

W Q D concerns kindling, burning, and fuel.

A qabas can be used to kindle a fire, but kindling does not necessarily mean taking fire from another fire.

Iqtibās involves a source from which fire is taken; īqād focuses on the act of kindling itself.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In a fire context, render qabas as “a firebrand,” “a piece of fire,” or “a flame taken from a fire.”

Render shihābin qabasin as “a burning brand” or “a flame taken from a fire,” and naqtabis min nūrikum as “so that we may take some of your light.”

“Benefit from the light” conveys the general idea, but “take some of the light” better preserves the verb’s connection with the root’s basic imagery.

Brief root meaning: Taking a flame or piece of fire from a burning source; more broadly, acquiring light, knowledge, or benefit from another source.

Conclusion

Q B S concerns taking fire from another fire and drawing on an existing source. Qabas names the acquired fire; iqtibās expresses taking and benefiting from it.

The Qur’an uses this root for bringing fire in Moses’ story and requesting a share of the believers’ light in al-Ḥadīd.

Both settings preserve the relationship between recipient and source. Al-Ḥadīd also reveals another truth: one may take fire and learn from another, but faith and responsibility before the Lord cannot be delegated to someone else.
