From striking and irregular movement to disturbed behavior and loss of balance

The Arabic root خ ب ط concerns striking, beating, and colliding. In early usage, it described a camel pounding the ground with its forefeet, someone striking tree branches with a stick to bring down their leaves, or a person moving this way and that without knowing the route or attending to obstacles.

An important feature of this family is the connection between blows or movement and disorder. Someone proceeding through darkness without knowing the way may strike obstacles and move erratically. Arabic also expresses this situation through derivatives of خ ب ط.

Nevertheless, striking with this root is not necessarily aimless. Someone beating tree branches to obtain fodder has a definite purpose. By contrast, a camel stepping forward without distinguishing obstacles because of poor eyesight exemplifies irregular movement and collision.

From this semantic field, the early Arabic expression خَبْطَ عَشْوَاءَ came to describe acting without insight or considered judgment.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Al-Baqarah 2:275, in يَتَخَبَّطُهُ الشَّيْطَانُ مِنَ الْمَسِّ. The verse compares the rising of those who consume ribā to that of someone whom Satan throws into disorder through mass.

This usage concerns irregular movement and loss of balance. Understanding it requires distinguishing takhabbuṭ itself, Satan’s role, the meaning of mass, and the subject of the comparison. These are connected elements of a sentence; they are not all contained within the lexical definition of خ ب ط.

1. Root forms and semantic core

The root خ ب ط occurs only once in the Qur’an, as يَتَخَبَّطُهُ in Al-Baqarah 2:275.

This is an imperfect verb in Form V, tafaʿʿul. Its final pronoun هُ refers to the person affected by takhabbuṭ.

Important Arabic derivatives of this root include:

  • خَبَطَ ـ يَخْبِطُ: He struck, beat, or trampled something.
  • خَبْط: Beating and striking; in some uses, irregular movement or action.
  • خَبَط: Leaves beaten down from a tree and used as animal fodder.
  • خَبْطَة: A single blow or collision; in other uses, a small amount of something.
  • خَابِط: Someone striking or proceeding without knowing the route.
  • خَبُوط and خَبِيط: Descriptions of an animal pounding the ground with its feet; khabūṭ is also recorded for an oppressive ruler.
  • مِخْبَط and مِخْبَطَة: A stick or implement used for striking, especially tree branches.
  • اِخْتَبَطَ: He struck or beat something; in social usage, he sought someone’s assistance or generosity.
  • تَخَبَّطَ: He struck or collided through irregular blows or movements; in uses concerning people, he caused disorder and disrupted orderly movement.
  • خُبَاط: In lexicons, the name of a condition resembling epilepsy or a severe disturbance of movement and behavior.
  • خِبْط: In another use, a small amount of water remaining in a vessel or pool.

These forms are recorded in lexicons, but apart from يَتَخَبَّطُهُ, none of the other derivatives occurs in the Qur’an.

The root’s central meaning

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies a single basic principle for this entry:

الْخَاءُ وَالْبَاءُ وَالطَّاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى وَطْءٍ وَضَرْبٍ

al-khāʾu wa-al-bāʾu wa-al-ṭāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā waṭʾin wa-ḍarbin

That is, the root signifies trampling and striking.

He illustrates this meaning with a camel pounding the ground with its forefoot and the beating of leaves from a tree.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī explains خَبْط as follows:

الْخَبْطُ: الضَّرْبُ عَلَى غَيْرِ اسْتِوَاءٍ

al-khabṭu: al-ḍarbu ʿalā ghayri istiwāʾin

That is, striking in an irregular manner.

These definitions occupy the same semantic field, but their emphasis differs.

Ibn Fāris centers striking and trampling; Al-Rāghib emphasizes the irregularity of the blows.

The root’s uses clarify this difference.

Someone striking a tree with a stick to bring down its leaves may know exactly what they are doing. Aimlessness and ignorance therefore cannot be necessary components of every use of the root.

In خَبْطَ عَشْوَاءَ, however, poor perception makes the movement and collisions irregular.

A defensible semantic core is thus striking, beating, and colliding. Extended uses concern movement or behavior without clear order or direction, leading to irregular collisions.

In the Qur’anic form takhabbuṭ, this aspect of disturbance and disrupted movement comes to the foreground.

2. Early uses: from pounding the ground to movement without discernment

A camel pounding the ground

In early Arabic, خَبَطَ الْبَعِيرُ الْأَرْضَ بِيَدِهِ described a camel striking the ground with its forefoot.

Arabic also calls a camel’s forelegs أيدي. In this example, بيده therefore refers to the camel’s forefoot, not a human hand.

As a camel moves, it presses against the ground with its feet and may deliver heavy blows.

Here khabṭ describes a visible physical action.

The verb itself does not explain why the camel moves this way. Its movement may be ordinary, hurried, or irregular.

Accompanying descriptions identify the movement’s precise cause and manner.

Khabṭ al-shajar: beating a tree to bring down its leaves

An important use of this root in Arab life concerned gathering tree leaves as fodder.

Trees such as ṭalḥ and other desert trees had leaves that could feed camels and livestock.

To obtain these leaves, someone struck the branches with a stick so that they detached and fell to the ground.

This action was called خَبْطُ الشَّجَرِ.

Some sources also call the fallen leaves themselves خَبَط.

This illustrates how a word can name both an action and something obtained through it.

Leaves are not, however, the fundamental meaning of the entire خ ب ط family; they are the product of one specific use of the verb.

This striking can also be purposeful. The livestock keeper beats the tree to provide animal feed; lack of purpose or ignorance of the result is therefore not a constant feature of khabṭ.

This distinction matters for the Qur’anic usage: irregular movement in one branch must not be imposed on every derivative.

Khabṭ al-layl: moving through darkness without knowing the way

Lexicons record خَبَطَ اللَّيْلَ for traveling at night without clearly knowing the route.

Someone moving through darkness without seeing the path may repeatedly change direction or collide with obstacles.

Here striking and movement are associated with insufficient guidance or perception.

Darkness is not the root’s own meaning. Night and lack of light are circumstances explaining a particular kind of khabṭ.

Nor does khabṭ in darkness necessarily mean a lack of reason. A person may be mentally well yet move irregularly because there is no light or because the route is unfamiliar.

Expressions for acting without insight can be understood through this usage.

A pre-Islamic witness: khabṭ ʿashwāʾ in Zuhayr’s Muʿallaqah

In his celebrated Muʿallaqah, Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

رَأَيْتُ الْمَنَايَا خَبْطَ عَشْوَاءَ مَنْ تُصِبْ
تُمِتْهُ وَمَنْ تُخْطِئْ يُعَمَّرْ فَيَهْرَمِ

raʾaytu al-manāyā khabṭa ʿashwāʾa man tuṣib / tumithu wa-man tukhṭiʾ yuʿammar fa-yahrami

Translation:

“I have seen deaths strike like a poorly sighted she-camel: whoever they hit, they kill; whoever they miss lives on until old age.”

Here عَشْوَاء describes a she-camel whose eyesight is weak and which does not properly distinguish obstacles as it moves.

Such a camel may strike things with its feet without knowing in advance what lies in its path.

Zuhayr compares death to this movement: from a human perspective, death may take one person in youth while leaving another until old age.

This image is important for understanding khabṭ.

The poet speaks of striking, while the construction highlights lack of discernment and irregular collision.

Nevertheless, this verse should not be treated as a scientific or theological assertion that death has no wisdom behind it. The poet expresses human experience and observation through a comparison.

The adjective ʿashwāʾ also contributes to the expression. Khabṭ alone does not establish that all blows are always blind or unintentional.

This is a recognized witness of pre-Islamic poetry, preserved in Zuhayr’s Muʿallaqah and its early commentaries, including Al-Zawzanī’s.

Zuhayr’s poetic witness in the lexicons: khabṭ ʿashwāʾ

From irregular movement to action without judgment

Arabic also uses يَخْبِطُ خَبْطَ عَشْوَاءَ proverbially for acting without understanding or attention to consequences.

Someone facing an important matter may, for example, make successive, inconsistent decisions without examining the circumstances. They turn first one way and then another, unable to act on clear understanding.

Such behavior can be compared to a camel moving without seeing the obstacles.

This extends the physical meaning of striking and movement into human decisions and behavior.

The comparison does not make every mistaken decision or error an instance of khabṭ.

The defining feature here is disorder and insufficient order or discernment, rather than merely an outcome contrary to the person’s expectations.

Someone may make a carefully considered decision that fails because of unforeseen circumstances. The outcome alone does not justify calling this khabṭ.

Ikhtibāṭ al-maʿrūf: seeking help without a previous connection

Lexicons use اِخْتَبَطَ فُلَانًا for seeking another person’s help or generosity without prior acquaintance, kinship, or an intermediary.

This usage concerns approaching someone and asking.

Ibn Fāris connects it to traveling and treading the ground while seeking assistance. Al-Rāghib suggests an analogy with beating a tree to obtain leaves.

These explain possible paths of semantic development. Without independent historical evidence, neither should be treated as the certain origin.

Seeking assistance from a stranger is not necessarily wrong or thoughtless.

This social use of ikhtibāṭ therefore shows that the root family extends beyond mental disturbance.

3. Qur’anic usage: يَتَخَبَّطُهُ الشَّيْطَانُ

الَّذِينَ يَأْكُلُونَ الرِّبَا لَا يَقُومُونَ إِلَّا كَمَا يَقُومُ الَّذِي يَتَخَبَّطُهُ الشَّيْطَانُ مِنَ الْمَسِّ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا إِنَّمَا الْبَيْعُ مِثْلُ الرِّبَا وَأَحَلَّ اللَّهُ الْبَيْعَ وَحَرَّمَ الرِّبَا فَمَنْ جَاءَهُ مَوْعِظَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّهِ فَانْتَهَىٰ فَلَهُ مَا سَلَفَ وَأَمْرُهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَمَنْ عَادَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

alladhīna yaʾkulūna al-ribā lā yaqūmūna illā kamā yaqūmu alladhī yatakhabbaṭuhu al-shayṭānu mina al-massi dhālika bi-annahum qālū innamā al-bayʿu mithlu al-ribā wa-aḥalla allāhu al-bayʿa wa-ḥarrama al-ribā fa-man jāʾahu mawʿiẓatun min rabbihi fa-intahā fa-lahu mā salafa wa-amruhu ilā allāhi wa-man ʿāda fa-ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūna

Proposed translation for this root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Those who consume ribā do not rise except as someone rises whom Satan throws into disorder through mass. That is because they said, ‘Trade is just like ribā,’ whereas The God has permitted trade and forbidden ribā. Whoever receives an admonition from his Lord and desists may retain what has passed, and his affair is entrusted to The God. Whoever returns—those are the people of the Fire; they will remain in it.”

Al-Baqarah 2:275

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of خ ب ط.

The verse begins by describing those who consume ribā. It then compares their rising to that of someone whom Satan throws into takhabbuṭ through mass.

It next connects the reason for this situation to their statement equating trade and ribā.

The verse then distinguishes someone who desists after receiving admonition from someone who returns to the previous behavior.

The opening comparison should therefore remain connected to ribā, the statement used to justify it, and the possibility of desisting after admonition.

يَتَخَبَّطُهُ: who is affected by the disturbance?

The verb يَتَخَبَّطُهُ is in Form V, tafaʿʿul.

In this sentence, الشَّيْطَانُ is the subject, while the pronoun هُ refers to the person upon whom takhabbuṭ acts.

The sentence consequently concerns a person whom Satan causes to experience takhabbuṭ.

This grammatical point matters: yatakhabbaṭuhu cannot simply be understood as “he moves irregularly himself” while ignoring the sentence’s subject.

Some Arabic Form V verbs can be transitive. An object pronoun with this verb is therefore compatible with Arabic grammar.

In this use, the person is in a state in which movement and rising are disturbed.

The verb alone does not determine details such as the movement of the hands or feet or how the person falls.

مِنَ الْمَسِّ: distinguishing mass from takhabbuṭ

Two different roots occur in يَتَخَبَّطُهُ الشَّيْطَانُ مِنَ الْمَسِّ.

Yatakhabbaṭuhu comes from خ ب ط and concerns disturbance associated with blows or irregular movement.

Al-mass comes from م س س; its basic meaning concerns contact and one thing reaching another.

In different Arabic constructions, mass can describe physical contact or an effect reaching a person. Some early usages also called conditions associated with madness or severe behavioral disturbance mass.

This breadth does not make the two roots identical.

Khabṭ concerns striking, collision, or disturbance; mass expresses contact or the reaching of an effect.

In a common reading, مِنْ expresses a causal relationship here: takhabbuṭ occurs through mass. Grammatical and exegetical sources also record different explanations of which part of the sentence the phrase modifies.

Thus مِنَ الْمَسِّ should neither disappear from translation nor be treated as the independent meaning of يَتَخَبَّطُهُ.

كَمَا يَقُومُ: the comparison’s structure

The verse says:

“لَا يَقُومُونَ إِلَّا كَمَا يَقُومُ”

This construction compares the rising of a group to that of another person.

The kāf in kamā marks a comparison.

This matters because the verse directly describes the state of those who consume ribā, using the image of someone experiencing takhabbuṭ to make it clear.

In this context, the principal resemblance is disturbed, unsteady rising.

The comparison itself does not establish exactly when this state occurs or its precise character.

The predominant reading in early commentaries concerns this group’s rising on the Day of Resurrection. On this reading, the manner of their rising signifies the outcome of their actions.

Another reading emphasizes the disordered condition of people whose greed and justification of ribā unbalance their conduct and judgment in worldly life.

Both readings should be assessed against the complete context and other verses concerning ribā and resurrection.

Lexically, yatakhabbaṭuhu does not itself specify a time. Nor, without a separate indication, does yaqūmūna mean more than rising: whether rising from graves or standing in worldly life is intended must be examined through context and other evidence.

A root study should not turn one of these interpretations into the word’s definition without sufficient demonstration.

4. Takhabbuṭ, Satan’s mass, and illness

Early Arabic also used derivatives of خ ب ط for unusual conditions of movement and behavior.

Some sources apply خُبَاط to a condition resembling madness or epilepsy.

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Al-Rāzī’s Mukhtār al-Ṣiḥāḥ describe it as a condition resembling madness, without necessarily equating the two.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records خبطه الشيطان and تخبطه الشيطان for a condition in which someone becomes disturbed and falls.

These records show that early Arabic used takhabbuṭ to describe abnormal movement and loss of balance.

They do not establish خ ب ط as the name of a particular illness defined by modern medicine.

Historically, different conditions involving sudden collapse, convulsions, altered consciousness, or severe behavioral disturbance were sometimes explained through terms associated with madness, epilepsy, and mass.

These historical terms are not identical to present medical classifications.

Does the verse determine epilepsy’s medical cause?

Al-Baqarah 2:275 uses the image of a person whose condition is associated with Satan and mass.

Early commentaries record different accounts of this relationship.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the image through someone disturbed and thrown down through mass; in his reading, the rising of those who consume ribā takes place on the Day of Resurrection.

Al-Ṭabarī: rising at resurrection and the explanation of takhabbuṭ

Al-Qurṭubī also uses this phrase to defend the possibility of bodily harm caused by jinn.

Al-Qurṭubī: his position on collapse and jinn

In Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb, however, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī explains takhabbuṭ while also reporting opposing views. These include Al-Jubbāʾī’s argument rejecting Satan’s bodily power to throw people down.

Al-Rāzī also reports Al-Qaffāl’s explanation that people of that time attributed certain unusual conditions to Satan and jinn; the image could therefore relate to the audience’s familiar language and conceptions.

These views show that even early exegetical tradition offered more than one explanation of Satan’s relationship to this condition.

Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī: the views of Al-Jubbāʾī and Al-Qaffāl and their discussion

Identifying the cause of a neurological illness is, however, a different question from establishing a word’s meaning or examining a Qur’anic image.

The verse does not establish that Satan controls every person with epilepsy, convulsions, impaired balance, or a mental illness.

Nor are such illnesses evidence of a person’s sin, consumption of ribā, or weak faith.

Here the person experiencing takhabbuṭ supplies the image of comparison; that person is not condemned for being ill.

The verse’s moral condemnation concerns the conduct of those who consume ribā and their justification for equating it with trade.

Conversely, the presence of a comparison alone does not conclusively prove that the Qur’an refers to no nonmaterial reality. The matter should be examined alongside verses concerning Satan, mass, and the limits of his power.

Knowledge of خ ب ط alone thus neither proves nor disproves every possible kind of Satanic influence.

What this article establishes lexically is the image of disturbance and disrupted movement.

Comparison with other verses concerning Satan

Al-Aʿrāf 7:201 describes people who, when a visitation from Satan touches them, remember and regain sight.

That verse matters for examining Satan’s influence in relation to human remembrance and awareness, but its verb mass is not identical to takhabbuṭ.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, Satan speaks of the limits of his authority and of his invitation, which people accepted.

Al-Naḥl 16:99 and Al-Naḥl 16:100 distinguish believers who trust in their Lord from those who take Satan as their ally or patron.

These verses matter for a separate examination of the limits of Satan’s influence.

Nevertheless, invitation, allegiance, whispering, and mass should not be made complete synonyms merely because they concern Satan.

Nor, without a separate argument, should a limit on Satan’s authority in one domain become a definitive ruling about every possible kind of influence.

In a study of خ ب ط, the principal issue remains the disturbance expressed by yatakhabbaṭuhu.

5. Takhabbuṭ, ribā, and the statement used to justify it

After the opening comparison, the verse says:

“ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا إِنَّمَا الْبَيْعُ مِثْلُ الرِّبَا”

The described condition is thus connected to the statement of those who regarded trade as being like ribā.

The Qur’an then distinguishes the two, stating that trade is permitted and ribā forbidden.

This passage helps identify the comparison’s purpose.

Their statement disregards the difference between permitted exchange and ribā. By presenting two different practices as equivalent, they justify their desired ruling.

A conceptual connection can therefore be considered between the opening image’s disorder and their mistaken judgment.

This connection must, however, remain a contextual reflection.

Khabṭ itself does not mean consuming ribā, economic profiteering, or inability to distinguish what is permitted from what is forbidden.

Those concepts arise from the verse’s subject and its other clauses.

Nor does the verse merely describe those who consume ribā: it distinguishes someone who desists after admonition from someone who returns to the same practice.

This indicates that a person’s response to admonition and awareness matters in the verse’s ethical analysis.

Yatakhabbaṭuhu should not, however, be translated as “persists in sin”: persistence and returning to previous conduct are considered elsewhere in the verse.

Similarly, ribā and its varieties require a separate study of the verses concerning ribā and trade. Defining خ ب ط should not become a comprehensive treatment of all financial transactions.

6. Distinctions from related roots

خ ب ط (khabṭ) and ض ر ب (ḍarb): striking and irregular collision

Arabic ḍarb has a broad range of meanings, including striking and various other uses.

Khabṭ also concerns striking, but many of its uses highlight pounding the ground, heavy collisions, or disorderly movement.

Striking a particular point, for example, may be entirely purposeful.

Khabṭ ʿashwāʾ, by contrast, depicts collisions made without properly discerning obstacles.

This does not mean that ḍarb is always orderly or that khabṭ is always aimless.

In khabṭ al-shajar, striking can be purposeful. The precise distinction depends on the verb’s object and the sentence’s construction.

Study of Ḍ R B

خ ب ط (khabṭ) and خ ب ل (khabal): disturbed movement and impairment

Arabic khabal concerns damage, impairment, and disturbance in a living being’s condition or capacities.

Early commentaries sometimes explain takhabbuṭ through takhabbul.

This proximity is understandable: someone experiencing impairment may move or behave unevenly.

The two roots are nevertheless distinct.

Khabṭ fundamentally concerns blows and collisions, whereas khabal concerns damage or impairment in a person or thing.

Khabal may consequently produce disordered movement, but khabṭ itself does not identify the impairment or its cause.

خ ب ط (khabṭ) and خ ل ط (khalṭ): disorder and mixture

Khalṭ concerns mixing things together.

Persian āshuftagī, “disorder” or “confusion,” sometimes describes both irregular movement and the mingling of things.

Khabṭ and khalṭ, however, have different basic meanings.

Someone mixing two substances is acting within khalṭ’s semantic field.

Someone colliding with obstacles and wandering this way and that without knowing the route moves closer to khabṭ’s field.

Thus yatakhabbaṭuhu in Al-Baqarah 2:275 should not merely be translated as “mixes up his thoughts.”

That rendering weakens the image of disturbed movement and rising.

خ ب ط (khabṭ) and م س س (mass): disturbance and contact

These roots stand together in Al-Baqarah 2:275.

The basic meaning of mass concerns contact or the reaching of an effect.

Khabṭ concerns striking and collision and, by extension, disturbed movement or behavior.

Contact is therefore not takhabbuṭ’s own meaning, nor does takhabbuṭ alone mean contact.

The Qur’anic sentence connects them through its grammatical structure.

Understanding “Satan’s mass” precisely requires a separate examination of م س س and other relevant verses.

Study of M S S

خ ب ط (khabṭ) and ح ب ط (ḥabaṭ): collision and the loss of an action’s result

خ ب ط and ح ب ط resemble one another in writing but are distinct roots.

Qur’anic ḥabaṭ concerns an action’s result coming to nothing or losing its effect.

Khabṭ, by contrast, concerns striking and disturbance.

For example, حَبِطَ عَمَلُهُ concerns an action losing its effect, whereas Qur’anic تَخَبَّطَ concerns a disturbed condition.

The resemblance does not justify turning disturbance into the nullification of deeds, or vice versa.

7. Translation proposal for Forqan

In Al-Baqarah 2:275, the principal phrase is:

“الَّذِي يَتَخَبَّطُهُ الشَّيْطَانُ مِنَ الْمَسِّ”

Possible renderings of yatakhabbaṭuhu include “throws him into disorder,” “disturbs his movement,” and, as a contextual explanation, “throws him off balance.”

These renderings are not equally precise.

“Throws him to the ground” may describe a consequence of takhabbuṭ, but the verb does not necessarily mean a complete fall.

“Makes him mad” is also unsuitable as a direct translation: it adds a broad diagnostic meaning and confines the image of disturbed movement to a particular mental condition.

“Gives him a seizure” is likewise imprecise. Seizure has a technical meaning in present medical language, whereas خ ب ط is not itself the name of that medical phenomenon.

“Confuses his thoughts” may offer a contextual interpretation, but it confines both the word and the comparison of rising to mental disturbance.

A suitable proposal for Forqan’s base translation is therefore:

“Someone whom Satan throws into disorder through mass.”

The entire opening sentence could be rendered:

“Those who consume ribā do not rise except as someone rises whom Satan throws into disorder through mass.”

The word explanation can clarify that this disturbance, within the verse’s image, concerns unsteady rising and movement.

This approach preserves the Arabic verb’s subject and object while avoiding added medical details or a definitive theory about Satan’s mass.

Brief root meaning and meaning in the verse

يَتَخَبَّطُهُ — Al-Baqarah 2:275

Root: خ ب ط

Grammar: Active imperfect verb, third-person masculine singular, Form V. The pronoun هُ is its object, and الشَّيْطَانُ is its subject.

Brief root meaning: Striking, beating, and colliding; in extended uses, causing disturbed and irregular movement or behavior.

Meaning in this verse: Satan places someone in a disturbed condition in which their rising and movement are unsteady; this condition supplies the comparison for the rising of those who consume ribā.

This separation makes the lexical meaning, grammatical structure, and Qur’anic image distinguishable without turning every element of the verse into the root’s definition.

8. Conclusion

In early Arabic, خ ب ط concerns striking, beating, and colliding. This appears clearly in a camel pounding the ground with its forefoot and in beating tree branches to bring down their leaves.

Other uses connect khabṭ with irregular movement and action without sufficient understanding. The pre-Islamic expression khabṭ ʿashwāʾ is a recognized example of this semantic extension.

Lack of order or discernment is not constant across the root’s forms: some uses, such as gathering tree leaves, describe purposeful actions.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Al-Baqarah 2:275. The verse compares the rising of those who consume ribā to that of someone whom Satan throws into takhabbuṭ through mass.

In this context, yatakhabbaṭuhu principally conveys disturbance and the disruption of orderly movement.

The word alone does not determine when this condition occurs, its medical cause, or the complete nature of Satan’s influence. These questions require examination of the sentence, its context, and other relevant verses.

The comparison should not become a judgment that people with neurological or mental illnesses are sinful. The verse condemns the behavior and justification of those who consume ribā, not the illness of the person appearing in the comparison.

Final definition: خ ب ط signifies striking, beating, and colliding, extending in some uses to disturbed and irregular movement or behavior. يَتَخَبَّطُهُ in Al-Baqarah 2:275 describes a condition involving disturbance and unsteady movement. Satan’s role, the relationship to mass, and the comparison’s meaning for those who consume ribā arise from the verse’s construction and context, rather than the root’s independent meaning. Brief root meaning: Striking, beating, and colliding; in some uses, disturbed or irregular movement and behavior.
