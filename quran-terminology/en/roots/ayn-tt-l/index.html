Core meaning: being empty of, or lacking, something normally accompanying, maintaining, or enabling the use of a thing; other uses concern abandonment, lack of care, and cessation of work or use, without necessarily implying destruction of the thing itself.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

The three-consonant Arabic root ʿ ṭ l concerns emptiness, abandonment, and lacking what normally accompanies or is needed by a person or thing.

In early Arabic, ʿaṭilat al-marʾah was said of a woman without jewelry or a necklace. Imraʾah ʿāṭil describes a woman unadorned with jewelry, especially one whose neck has no necklace.

This does not mean that she lacks clothing or bodily covering. What is absent is jewelry used for adornment.

Qaws ʿuṭul denotes a bow without a string. Its wooden structure may be sound, but without the string it cannot be used in the usual way.

Faras ʿuṭul likewise describes a horse without a halter or a particular tether.

These examples show that ʿaṭal need not mean destruction or damage. Something may remain intact yet lack equipment, a covering, or another customary accompaniment.

In another branch, ʿaṭṭala al-dār means leaving a house empty, while dār muʿaṭṭalah describes a house left abandoned and unused.

Ibil muʿaṭṭalah denotes camels left without a keeper or herder.

Biʾr muʿaṭṭalah denotes a well from which water is no longer drawn, or to which no one comes for use and upkeep.

Sometimes a specific item is absent, such as a necklace or bowstring. Elsewhere the object remains, but its usual use, care, or activity has ceased.

The two situations are related but not identical.

The central field may be described as “lacking a customary accompaniment, care, or function,” a condition that can lead to abandonment and disuse.

Not all uses should be reduced to being out of operation. A woman without jewelry has not necessarily stopped an activity, just as an unstrung bow need not be broken. Lexicons also record a separate branch concerning bodily stature, fullness, and length; that branch is not the meaning of the two Quranic forms discussed here.

2. Early poetic evidence and its historical setting

An early example occurs in a verse attributed to Labid ibn Rabiʿah:

يَرُضْنَ صِعَابَ الدُّرِّ فِي كُلِّ حِجَّةٍ
وَلَوْ لَمْ تَكُنْ أَعْنَاقُهُنَّ عَوَاطِلَا

yaruḍna ṣiʿāba al-durri fī kulli ḥijjatin / wa-law lam takun aʿnāquhunna ʿawāṭilā

“They pierce hard pearls for each earlobe, even if their necks are not bare of ornaments.”

ʿAwāṭil is the plural of ʿāṭil and describes necks without jewelry.

The verse's lexical explanation takes yaruḍna as piercing pearls and ḥijjah as the earlobe. The image concerns preparing adornments for the ears, alongside mention of necklaces.

It illustrates a tangible, everyday meaning: absence of an ornament customarily worn on part of the body.

ʿAwāṭil here does not mean unemployed women, abandoned houses, or broken objects; it concerns necks without ornaments.

Labid lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This verse is transmitted in lexical works under his name, but its precise date has not been independently established in this study. It illustrates early usage without conclusively dating this particular verse to before Islam.

Another example is found in a verse by al-Aʿsha:

وَتُسْمَعُ فِيهَا هَبِي وَاقْدَمِي
وَمَرْسُونُ خَيْلٍ وَأَعْطَالُهَا

wa-tusmaʿu fīhā habī wa-iqdamī / wa-marsūnu khaylin wa-aʿṭāluhā

“There the cries ‘Rise!’ and ‘Advance!’ are heard, with haltered horses and those without halters.”

Marsūn refers to horses fitted with a rasan, a halter or tether.

Aʿṭāluhā, in contrast, refers to horses without such equipment.

The contrast clarifies the meaning.

A horse described as ʿuṭul is not necessarily sick, weak, or worthless. It lacks something that another horse has.

Here the absence is that of a halter, not the ability to move or the animal's natural capacities.

The verse is attributed to al-Aʿsha, the well-known poet of the late pre-Islamic and early Islamic period. The precise date of this particular poem has not been independently established in this study.

Together, these examples attest early uses concerning the absence of jewelry, a halter, or customary equipment.

Lexicons also record the family for an uninhabited house, an unused well, and livestock without a keeper.

Their recording in later dictionaries does not prove that every detail had exactly the same range in pre-Islamic Arabic.

The evidence establishes a field concerning the absence of an accompaniment or keeper, and being left abandoned or unused.

3. Lexical and Quranic forms

Important forms include ʿaṭila, yaʿṭalu, ʿaṭal, ʿuṭūl, ʿāṭil, ʿawāṭil, ʿuṭul, ʿaṭṭala, taʿṭīl, muʿaṭṭal, taʿaṭṭala, and mutaʿaṭṭil.

They share a root but differ in function and meaning.

ʿAṭila: in uses such as a woman lacking jewelry, it expresses the absence of a customary accompaniment.

ʿĀṭil: describes someone or something without a particular ornament, implement, or accompaniment. Modern usage also applies it to an unemployed person.

ʿUṭul: recorded for an unstrung bow, an animal without a halter, or a person lacking particular equipment.

ʿAṭṭala: the Form II verb concerns leaving empty, abandoning, or stopping customary use and activity.

Taʿṭīl: leaving unattended, putting out of use, or stopping an activity.

Muʿaṭṭal: something left abandoned or unused, or whose customary activity has been stopped.

Taʿaṭṭala: to cease working or being used, or to lack a customary condition or implement.

Classical Arabic also records ʿuṭṭilat al-mazāriʿ for farmland left uncultivated and untended.

A field or natural resource may thus fall out of use without the land itself disappearing.

Modern Persian taʿṭīl

Modern Persian taʿṭīl commonly concerns a closed workplace, suspended administrative activity, school closure, or time off work.

This relates to the field of abandonment or suspended activity.

The modern sense of a holiday should not be projected onto every early Arabic use.

For example, Quranic biʾr muʿaṭṭalah does not mean a well closed for a holiday and scheduled to reopen tomorrow.

Al-ʿishāru ʿuṭṭilat likewise concerns abandoned camels, not a planned temporary suspension of their economic activity.

A scheduled closure in modern usage should therefore be distinguished from abandonment and lack of care in these Quranic uses.

Quranic forms

The root occurs in two Quranic verses in two forms:

Muʿaṭṭalatin — Al-Hajj 22:45

A feminine Form II passive participle in the genitive, modifying biʾrin.

ʿUṭṭilat — Al-Takwir 81:4

A third-person feminine singular Form II passive perfect verb, concerning al-ʿishār.

Both concern something left abandoned or unused.

One concerns a well and its use; the other, valuable camels whose care is set aside.

The forms ʿāṭil, ʿuṭul, taʿaṭṭala, and the verbal noun taʿṭīl do not occur in the Quran.

A Quranic study should center on the two actual forms, using other Arabic uses to illuminate their semantic field.

4. Biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin: a well no longer used

The first occurrence in Quranic order is in Al-Hajj:

فَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ أَهْلَكْنَاهَا وَهِيَ ظَالِمَةٌ فَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا وَبِئْرٍ مُعَطَّلَةٍ وَقَصْرٍ مَشِيدٍ

fa-ka-ayyin min qaryatin ahlaknāhā wa-hiya ẓālimatun fa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā wa-biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin wa-qaṣrin mashīd

Al-Ḥajj 22:45

Al-Hajj 22:45

“How many a settlement We destroyed while it was wrongdoing, now fallen upon its roofs; and how many an abandoned well and lofty palace!”

Muʿaṭṭalatin modifies biʾrin.

Biʾr is a well dug to reach water.

A well may contain water used for drinking, watering livestock, and other needs.

If the local people perish or leave their settlement, the well may remain although no one draws water from it.

That condition fits biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin.

Must an abandoned well have dried up?

An important distinction is that between a well drying up and a well being abandoned.

If a well dries up, drawing water from it may no longer be possible.

If it is abandoned, it may still contain water and remain structurally usable, although no one comes to use it.

Muʿaṭṭalah does not itself say that the water has dried up.

Nor does it establish that the well's wall has collapsed, its opening is blocked, or its drawing equipment is broken.

These are possible circumstances, not fixed components of the word's meaning.

“A dried-up well” or “a ruined well” is therefore not a precise equivalent of biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin.

In this context, the primary meaning is a well left abandoned and no longer used.

The well and the ruined settlement

The verse opens with settlements destroyed while engaged in wrongdoing.

It then describes their ruined condition.

An abandoned well and a lofty palace follow.

Together they present traces of former human life.

The well provides water and sustains life.

A palace may display the ability, wealth, or power of its builders and inhabitants.

When a community perishes, these structures may remain although those who used them are gone.

Imagine a city once inhabited by thousands. Its wells supplied people's water, and its large buildings served as dwellings or places of activity.

If its inhabitants perish, some buildings may remain standing and its wells usable, yet no one benefits from them.

Their former role in people's lives has ended, although the structures themselves need not have been entirely destroyed.

Muʿaṭṭalatin and mashīdin

Biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin appears beside qaṣrin mashīdin.

Mashīd belongs to sh y d. It has been explained as lofty or firmly built, and also as plastered. The selected translation here follows the lofty-building interpretation.

The two expressions identify different features.

Muʿaṭṭalah describes the well's abandonment and disuse.

Mashīd describes the palace's construction.

Mashīd does not prove that the palace is inhabited, just as muʿaṭṭalah does not prove the well physically ruined.

Their common implication arises from their juxtaposition and the setting of destroyed settlements: traces of human construction and capability may remain after their users have gone.

The root itself means neither the collapse of civilization nor divine punishment.

Those ideas come from the verse and its surrounding context.

Connection with the next verse

Al-Hajj 22:46 asks whether people have traveled through the land so that they might have hearts with which to reason and ears with which to hear.

It then states that the blindness at issue is not blindness of the eyes but of the hearts within the breasts.

The connection presents the remains of destroyed settlements as occasions for reflection and learning.

An abandoned well is more than an old object: it can bear witness to life once sustained there and now gone.

This reflection arises from the connection between verses; muʿaṭṭalah does not lexically mean a lesson or awakening.

5. Wa-idhā al-ʿishāru ʿuṭṭilat: valuable camels left unattended

The second Quranic occurrence is in Al-Takwir:

وَإِذَا الْعِشَارُ عُطِّلَتْ

wa-idhā al-ʿishāru ʿuṭṭilat

Al-Takwīr 81:4

Al-Takwir 81:4

“And when the ten-month-pregnant she-camels are left unattended.”

ʿUṭṭilat is a Form II passive perfect verb.

Al-ʿishār is the plural of ʿusharāʾ, used for she-camels at an advanced stage of pregnancy, especially those ten months pregnant.

These animals were highly valuable in Arab pastoral life.

Beyond her own value, a pregnant she-camel could benefit her owner through milk and offspring after giving birth.

Her care therefore mattered greatly, and leaving such an animal unattended was no ordinary, insignificant act.

The meaning of ʿuṭṭilat

Here ʿuṭṭilat concerns camels being abandoned and left without care.

The verb itself does not say that they have died, been destroyed, or lost the ability to move.

Nor does it mean that their pregnancy has ceased.

They may remain alive and pregnant while their owners no longer tend them.

Imagine a herder whose most valuable possessions are a few pregnant camels. Ordinarily he works to supply their water, food, safety, and care.

A tremendous event threatening his life and fate may absorb him so completely that he sets aside even the care of his most valuable property.

The camels need not have suffered physical injury; what has ceased is their owners' attention and care.

That image fits ʿuṭṭilat.

Why mention al-ʿishār?

The opening of Al-Takwir describes a series of immense changes.

Al-Takwir 81:1 speaks of the sun being folded up.

In Al-Takwir 81:2, the stars undergo change.

In Al-Takwir 81:3, the mountains are set in motion.

Then, in Al-Takwir 81:4, ten-month-pregnant camels are left unattended.

Al-Takwir 81:5 describes wild animals being gathered.

The sequence includes a condition affecting people's lives and property alongside great changes in the world.

For a society in which camels had economic and subsistence value, unattended pregnant camels provided an intelligible image of how radically circumstances had changed.

People who ordinarily strive to preserve valuable possessions face an event that takes their attention away from them.

The image invites reflection on changing human priorities as well as wealth.

Someone may spend hours each day protecting property, a home, or a business. When an overwhelming danger arises, attention may shift entirely to saving oneself and one's family.

What previously seemed the greatest concern suddenly becomes secondary.

This example illustrates the image's reflective potential; it is not the lexical meaning of ʿuṭṭilat.

The verb means being left unattended. The event's severity and the change of priorities arise from the surah's context.

A real event or a figurative image?

Al-Takwir presents several events with the construction wa-idhā, “and when.”

Like the other verbs in this sequence, ʿuṭṭilat describes an event within the scene of immense changes.

The verb alone cannot specify precisely when this abandonment occurs, or whether all camels everywhere are abandoned identically and simultaneously.

The image's origin in Arab life does not, by itself, warrant denying the possibility of a real event.

The Quran speaks of something familiar to its audience without specifying its timing and material details here.

The basic translation should therefore preserve the clear sense: pregnant camels are left unattended.

How widely this image embraces changes in the world and human life belongs to contextual reflection.

The passive construction

ʿUṭṭilat is passive.

The agent is not explicitly identified.

The verse does not state who abandoned the camels; it foregrounds their condition of abandonment.

The verb's structure alone cannot establish that all their owners have died, fled, or deliberately decided to abandon them.

Their owners' preoccupation with a tremendous event may explain the abandonment, but the verb cannot determine those details.

The stated outcome is that camels ordinarily receiving care are left unattended.

6. Comparing the two Quranic uses

Al-Hajj 22:45 and Al-Takwir 81:4 both concern the abandonment of something ordinarily important to people.

Al-Hajj concerns a well.

Al-Takwir concerns pregnant camels.

The well provides access to water and supports human life.

Pregnant camels were valuable possessions in pastoral life.

In both cases, something customarily used or tended no longer receives its usual use or care.

The causes and contexts nevertheless differ.

In Al-Hajj, the abandoned well stands alongside destroyed settlements and lofty palaces, depicting traces of departed communities.

In Al-Takwir, unattended camels appear among the world's immense transformations.

The meanings are close, but one describes the aftermath of past communities' destruction, the other the scene of great future events.

This does not turn the root's meaning into two entirely separate definitions.

Muʿaṭṭalah and ʿuṭṭilat both concern abandonment and the loss of customary use or care.

What was abandoned, why, and with what consequences depend on each verse's subject and context.

7. Comparison with related semantic fields

ʿ ṭ l and t r k

The root t r k concerns leaving, setting aside, or leaving something behind.

ʿ Ṭ l can also concern abandonment, with particular emphasis on lacking a customary accompaniment or care, or falling out of use.

Someone may leave a house while others continue living in it.

The first person has left it, but the house is not necessarily muʿaṭṭalah.

If it becomes empty of inhabitants and is no longer used, the latter description becomes clearer.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary: tark can also express complete abandonment in some constructions.

ʿ ṭ l and h m l

Ihmāl concerns leaving unattended, inattention, and failure to provide care.

For the camels, ʿuṭṭilat and uhmilat are close in meaning.

The family ʿ ṭ l is not confined to human inattention.

A bow without a string is ʿuṭul, although this condition need not result from someone's negligence.

A woman without jewelry is ʿāṭil without the word necessarily conveying blame or neglect.

Ihmāl can thus overlap with abandonment in this family without exhausting its range.

ʿ ṭ l and kh l w

The root kh l w concerns emptiness and becoming empty.

This closely relates to one major branch of ʿ ṭ l.

A house emptied of inhabitants can be described as khāliyah.

Muʿaṭṭalah can emphasize disuse or the absence of customary use in addition to emptiness or abandonment.

A well may lack users while still containing water.

Emptiness should therefore not always be understood as absence of all the object's contents.

ʿ ṭ l and kh r b

Kharāb concerns the ruin or damage of a building or place.

Taʿṭīl does not necessarily mean damage.

A well may be intact but unused.

Conversely, a well may be damaged while people still try to draw water from it.

Physical ruin and cessation of use are distinguishable.

This matters in Al-Hajj 22:45, which mentions ruined settlements and an abandoned well without describing them all with one word.

ʿ ṭ l and w q f

In movement contexts, waqafa concerns standing and stopping.

ʿAṭṭala, by contrast, can mean stopping the use, activity, or care of something.

An implement may momentarily stop moving without having been put out of use.

A workshop may likewise stand idle for a time while its building and tools remain sound.

A halt in movement and suspension of function can be related without being identical.

ʿ ṭ l and ʿ ṭ w

The root ʿ ṭ w concerns extending, reaching, taking, and turning toward something.

In Al-Qamar 54:29, fa-taʿāṭā belongs to this family, followed by fa-ʿaqara from ʿ q r.

ʿ Ṭ l, however, concerns being left without customary use or care.

Sharing the first two consonants does not make the roots identical.

ʿ ṭ l and ʿ ṭ f

The root ʿ ṭ f concerns bending, turning, and changing direction.

ʿIṭfihi in Al-Hajj 22:9 belongs to that family.

ʿ Ṭ l in Al-Hajj 22:45 concerns an abandoned well.

Only one consonant differs, but the roots' central meanings and Quranic uses are independent.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for the two Quranic forms are “abandoned” and “are left unattended.”

They fit Arabic usage while preserving the distinction between abandonment and physical destruction.

Muʿaṭṭalatin — Al-Hajj 22:45

Word: muʿaṭṭalatin.

Root: ʿ ṭ l.

Word type: a feminine Form II passive participle, genitive, modifying biʾrin.

Brief root meaning: lacking a customary accompaniment, care, or function; leaving abandoned or unused.

Meaning in this verse: a well left abandoned and no longer used.

Suggested translation:

وَبِئْرٍ مُعَطَّلَةٍ

wa-biʾrin muʿaṭṭalah

Al-Ḥajj 22:45

“And an abandoned well.”

This agrees with Forqan's existing Persian rendering.

“A dried-up well” is unsuitable because muʿaṭṭalah does not itself establish loss of water.

“A ruined well” likewise describes only one possible condition and should not replace the word's basic meaning.

ʿUṭṭilat — Al-Takwir 81:4

Word: ʿuṭṭilat.

Root: ʿ ṭ l.

Word type: a third-person feminine singular Form II passive perfect verb.

Brief root meaning: abandoning, leaving unattended, and stopping customary use or care.

Meaning in this verse: they are left unattended and no longer cared for; the perfect form occurs within a scene referring to the future.

Suggested translation:

وَإِذَا الْعِشَارُ عُطِّلَتْ

wa-idhā al-ʿishāru ʿuṭṭilat

Al-Takwīr 81:4

“And when the ten-month-pregnant she-camels are left unattended.”

This also agrees with Forqan's existing Persian text.

ʿUṭṭilat here should not be rendered “were destroyed,” “died,” or “ceased to be pregnant.”

Nor does the verb itself mean “became worthless.”

The camels may remain valuable while circumstances prevent anyone from tending them.

Conclusion

The Arabic root ʿ ṭ l concerns emptiness, abandonment, and lacking customary accompaniment, care, or use.

Early uses describe a woman without a necklace, an unstrung bow, and a horse without a halter.

The absence of something particular matters; damage or destruction need not be involved.

In other uses, a house, well, field, or livestock may stand abandoned and unused while remaining in existence.

The root occurs twice in the Quran.

In Al-Hajj 22:45, muʿaṭṭalatin describes a well no longer used. It appears alongside destroyed settlements and lofty palaces, presenting traces of past communities' lives.

In Al-Takwir 81:4, ʿuṭṭilat concerns pregnant camels left unattended. Amid immense transformations, even care for valuable possessions is set aside.

Both uses concern abandonment and the cessation of customary care or use, but their subjects and contexts differ.

The principal field discussed here is “lacking a customary accompaniment, care, or function”; the two Quranic derivatives concern being left abandoned or unused.

Forqan's translation should preserve the differences between abandonment, damage, destruction, dryness, and temporary suspension. An abandoned object may remain sound and usable; what has changed is its relationship with users, keepers, or customary function.
