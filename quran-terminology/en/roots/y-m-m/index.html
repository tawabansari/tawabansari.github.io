Intending and turning toward a destination; bodies of water and Qur’anic tayammum

Qur’anic Y M M includes two important word families: tayammama, intending, approaching, or targeting something; and yamm, a large body of water that can be a sea or a great river.

The Qur’anic root index groups both under Y M M, but similar forms do not establish that sea derives from intending, or intending from sea. One lexical analysis, after discussing intention, explicitly states:

فَأَمَّا الْبَحْرُ فَلَيْسَ مِنْ هَذَا الْقِيَاسِ

fa-ammā al-baḥru fa-laysa min hādhā al-qiyāsi

Meaning: “As for the sea, it does not follow this semantic analogy.”

The family occurs eleven times in ten Qur’anic verses: three verbal tayammamū forms and eight instances of al-yamm. The verbs occur in Al-Baqarah 2:267, Al-Nisāʾ 4:43, and Al-Māʾidah 5:6. Al-yamm occurs in accounts of Moses, Pharaoh, and the Samaritan.

The verb itself appears in two different settings: deliberately selecting something poor for giving, and turning toward ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban when water is not found.

Understanding should begin with the general sense of intending, then examine its use in a particular purification instruction.

1. Two semantic branches and important early Arabic forms

Yammama and tayammama: intending and choosing a destination

A lexical definition of the intention branch says:

الْيَاءُ وَالْمِيمُ: كَلِمَةٌ تَدُلُّ عَلَى قَصْدِ الشَّيْءِ وَتَعَمُّدِهِ

al-yāʾu wa-al-mīmu kalimatun tadullu ʿalā qaṣdi al-shayʾi wa-taʿammudihi

Meaning: “Yāʾ and mīm indicate intending something and deliberately directing oneself toward it.”

Yammamtuhu denotes intending a person or thing; tayammamtuhu means turning toward it and making it one’s objective.

Someone approaching a particular person to meet them can say تَيَمَّمْتُ فُلَانًا. The verb also applies to a particular place as destination.

This is not any accidental movement: a destination has been identified and approached.

Arrow and spear uses make this clearer: a warrior may target a particular person with a spear rather than anyone in front of him.

Dictionaries also relate tayammama and taʾammama. Both can express intending and approaching an objective.

The relationship with ʾ M M

A central question is the relationship between Y M M and ʾ M M.

Lexical transmissions group ammahu, taʾammamahu, yammahu, and tayammamahu under intending.

Another explanation treats yāʾ in some of these forms as substituted for hamzah.

Other definitions examine yammama separately and record intending for it.

Two questions should therefore remain distinct: the usual morphology places tayammum under Y M M, while reports of sound substitution concern its historical relationship with ʾ M M.

Shared meaning does not merge all derivatives. Nor does the disagreement remove Qur’anic tayammamū from the root under examination.

In particular, umm, ummah, imām, and āmmīn fall under ʾ M M in the Qur’anic morphological index. Sharing a concept of destination or intention does not add them to the Y M M count.

Study of ʾ M M

Other derivatives

Yammama is Form II: intending, directing toward a target, or orienting something toward a destination.

Tayammama is Form V: intending and turning toward a destination.

Tayammum is the Form V verbal noun. Generally it denotes intending; religious usage names a particular action when water is not found.

Taymīm is the verbal noun of yammama.

Muyammam can describe a person sought by others. Some descriptions also apply it to someone successful in obtaining what they seek.

Yamāmah is recorded in one use for intention or approaching. It should not invariably be equated with yamāmah as a bird name.

Al-yamm names a body of water. Related uses include yumma al-rajulu, a man being cast into the water, and yumma al-sāḥilu, the shore being covered by water.

Maymūm in the water branch describes someone cast into yamm or drowned.

These forms concern two different fields: intending and targeting, and abundant water and events associated with it.

Yamām also names a bird related to the pigeon, and al-Yamāmah names a historical region. Lexicographers differ over the boundaries of yamām and ḥamām. Similarity does not prove that either the bird or place name derives from intending or water.

2. Early Arabic witnesses: journeys and encounters

Approaching a destination in poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā

A verse attributed to al-Aʿshā says:

تَيَمَّمْتُ قَيْسًا وَكَمْ دُونَهُ
مِنَ الْأَرْضِ مِنْ مَهْمَهٍ ذِي شَزَنْ

tayammamtu qaysan wa-kam dūnahu / mina al-arḍi min mahmahin dhī shazan

Meaning: “I set out toward Qays, with so many difficult, rugged deserts between us.”

Tayammamtu means intending and moving toward a particular destination.

Qays is a person reached by crossing difficult terrain. The verb itself denotes intention and direction, rather than desert or hardship.

Other words in the verse supply the desert and difficulty.

Some transmissions give tayammamu Qaysan, explained as a shortened feminine imperfect tatayammamu whose subject is the poet’s she-camel. Attribution also varies between simply al-Aʿshā and specifically al-Aʿshā of Bāhilah.

The variation should remain, while both readings express approaching a destination.

Aiming a spear in poetry attributed to ʿĀmir ibn Mālik

Another witness is attributed to ʿĀmir ibn Mālik, known as Mulāʿib al-Asinnah:

لَمَّا رَأَيْتُ ضِرَارًا فِي مُلَمْلَمَةٍ
كَأَنَّمَا حَافَتَاهَا حَافَتَا نِيقِ

يَمَّمْتُهُ الرُّمْحَ شَزْرًا ثُمَّ قُلْتُ لَهُ
هَذِي الْمُرُوءَةُ لَا لَعِبُ الزَّحَالِيقِ

lammā raʾaytu ḍirāran fī mulamlamatin / ka-annamā ḥāfatāhā ḥāfatā nīqi / yammamtuhu al-rumḥa shazran thumma qultu lahu / hādhī al-murūʾatu lā laʿibu al-zaḥālīqi

Its sense: “When I saw Ḍirār amid a dense company whose two edges were like the sides of a high mountain, I aimed the spear at him from the side and said: this is manliness, not playing on slippery ground.”

Yammamtuhu al-rumḥa means targeting a particular person with a spear.

A transmitted explanation accompanying the second couplet prefers yammamtuhu to ammamtuhu because shazran concerns aiming from the side rather than necessarily straight ahead. That transmission also reads hādhī al-basālatu in place of hādhī al-murūʾatu.

This is one reported witness for distinctions in the usage of Y M M and ʾ M M.

ʿĀmir ibn Mālik is known as a pre-Islamic figure, and lexical transmissions attribute the poem to him.

What the witnesses establish

Both concern intending, but the settings differ.

In the al-Aʿshā witness, someone approaches a distant destination. In ʿĀmir’s, a warrior targets a particular person.

Neither tayammama nor yammama is therefore necessarily restricted to moving one’s own body toward a destination. An implement such as a spear can be directed toward a target.

Identifying an objective and directing toward it is common to these uses.

This attested meaning grounds the understanding of Qur’anic tayammamū.

3. Wa-lā tayammamū al-khabītha: selecting something for giving

Al-Baqarah 2:267

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَنْفِقُوا مِنْ طَيِّبَاتِ مَا كَسَبْتُمْ وَمِمَّا أَخْرَجْنَا لَكُمْ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ وَلَا تَيَمَّمُوا الْخَبِيثَ مِنْهُ تُنْفِقُونَ وَلَسْتُمْ بِآخِذِيهِ إِلَّا أَنْ تُغْمِضُوا فِيهِ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ غَنِيٌّ حَمِيدٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū anfiqū min ṭayyibāti mā kasabtum wa-mimmā akhrajnā lakum mina al-arḍi wa-lā tayammamū al-khabītha minhu tunfiqūna wa-lastum bi-ākhidhīhi illā an tughmiḍū fīhi wa-iʿlamū anna allāha ghaniyyun ḥamīdun

A close rendering:

“You who believe, give from the good things you have earned and what We have brought forth for you from the earth. Do not deliberately select what is bad for giving, when you yourselves would not accept it unless you overlooked its defects. Know that The God is self-sufficient and praiseworthy.”

Tayammamū occurs in wa-lā tayammamū al-khabītha.

It is a Form V imperfect made jussive by prohibitive lā. Before the omission of one tāʾ, this same jussive form would be tatayammamū.

Al-khabītha is its object: what the audience deliberately chooses for giving.

Does it mean ritual tayammum here?

No.

The subject is giving, rather than lack of water, washing, or wiping.

Tayammamū retains its general lexical sense of intending, selecting, or specifically targeting something.

The verse prohibits deliberately choosing something bad to give.

Tayammum itself does not mean giving: tunfiqūna expresses that action, while tayammamū expresses selecting or intending something for it.

Treating tayammum only as a ritual name from the outset would leave this clear occurrence unexplained.

Al-khabīth in context

Ṭayyibāt and al-khabīth stand in contrast.

The continuation says the givers would not themselves accept what they selected without overlooking its defects.

At least one prominent contextual instance is therefore something poor or undesirable that a person would not want for themselves but chooses for others.

The concern is not merely transferring something: quality of selection and regard for the recipient matter too.

These points come from the contrast and complete sentence, rather than Y M M alone.

4. Fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban: intending within a purification instruction

The other two verbal occurrences concern purification.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43

فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا فَامْسَحُوا بِوُجُوهِكُمْ وَأَيْدِيكُمْ

fa-lam tajidū māʾan fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban fa-imsaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum

“If you find no water, turn toward clean earth and wipe your faces and hands.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:6

فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا فَامْسَحُوا بِوُجُوهِكُمْ وَأَيْدِيكُمْ مِنْهُ

fa-lam tajidū māʾan fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban fa-imsaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum minhu

“If you find no water, turn toward clean earth and wipe your faces and hands from it.”

Fa-tayammamū is a second-person plural imperative of Form V.

Ṣaʿīdan is the object, modified by ṭayyiban.

The wording retains the familiar sense of intending: turn toward ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban. A separate verb, fa-imsaḥū, then expresses wiping.

The difference between tayammamū and imsaḥū

This is central to the study.

Two different verbs occur in one instruction:

Fa-tayammamū: turn toward something specific and intend it.

Fa-imsaḥū: wipe.

Lexically, tayammum alone does not mean wiping.

One lexical explanation gives uqṣudū, “intend,” as the original meaning, while frequent use in purification made tayammum the familiar name of the action.

Lexical transmissions record this explanation.

The lexical verb and the action’s technical name should therefore be distinguished.

This does not deny the purification action: after turning toward ṣaʿīd, the Qur’an explicitly commands wiping faces and hands.

Tayammamū should neither invariably be translated “wipe,” nor should the lexical sense be used to disregard fa-imsaḥū.

What does ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban denote?

Ṣaʿīd belongs to the separate root Ṣ ʿ D.

Dictionaries record the earth’s surface and what confronts a person from it. Some explanations emphasize soil; others give a broader scope.

Ṭayyib belongs to Ṭ Y B and describes cleanness and suitability in purification contexts.

Whether only fine soil or other kinds of earthly surface are intended requires the separate Ṣ ʿ D study and examination of purification verses.

For the present root, the clear point is intending a particular thing, identified in both verses as ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban.

Ṣaʿīd’s meaning should not become part of Y M M itself.

Study of Ṣ ʿ D

Similar verses with an important difference

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 and Al-Māʾidah 5:6 are close in this passage but not identical.

Al-Māʾidah 5:6 adds minhu after fa-imsaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 has no minhu in that position.

Minhu makes the relation between wiping and ṣaʿīd more explicit. Any study of how the action is performed should consider both verses together.

The root alone does not determine the number of hand strikes on the earth, detailed sequence, or other disputed procedural rules.

Ease in the instruction’s context

After the purification instructions, Al-Māʾidah 5:6 says:

مَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ لِيَجْعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ حَرَجٍ وَلَٰكِنْ يُرِيدُ لِيُطَهِّرَكُمْ

mā yurīdu allāhu li-yajʿala ʿalaykum min ḥarajin wa-lākin yurīdu li-yuṭahhirakum

“The God does not intend to impose hardship on you; He intends to purify you.”

This matters to tayammum’s place in the verse.

An alternative purification instruction is given when water is not found, and the verse’s ending emphasizes that creating hardship is not the purpose.

This agrees with Al-Nisāʾ 4:43, ending with إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَفُوًّا غَفُورًا: “The God is ever pardoning and forgiving.”

Ease and removal of hardship nevertheless belong to the purpose and context, rather than tayammum’s lexical meaning.

Root study should clarify the relation without replacing the definition with the general principle of removing hardship.

5. Al-yamm: sea, great river, and expanse of water

The other branch is al-yamm.

One lexical definition says:

الْيَمُّ: الْبَحْرُ الَّذِي لَا يُدْرَكُ قَعْرُهُ وَلَا شَطَّاهُ

al-yammu al-baḥru alladhī lā yudraku qaʿruhu wa-lā shaṭṭāhu

Meaning: “Yamm is a sea whose bottom and two shores cannot be reached.”

Other recorded explanations do not restrict the meaning so narrowly.

An account of its scope applies it to the sea and includes both salt water and a great freshwater river.

In Moses’ infancy story, it denotes a great body of water historically identified with the Nile.

Restricting yamm to salt water or waters whose shores cannot be seen therefore does not explain all its uses.

Yamm and baḥr

Qur’anic baḥr also denotes a body of water.

The account of Israel’s rescue and Pharaoh’s drowning uses both words.

Al-Baqarah 2:50 says:

وَإِذْ فَرَقْنَا بِكُمُ الْبَحْرَ فَأَنْجَيْنَاكُمْ وَأَغْرَقْنَا آلَ فِرْعَوْنَ

wa-idh faraqnā bikumu al-baḥra fa-anjaynākum wa-aghraqnā āla firʿawna

“When We parted the sea for you, We saved you and drowned Pharaoh’s people.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:136 says:

فَأَغْرَقْنَاهُمْ فِي الْيَمِّ

fa-aghraqnāhum fī al-yammi

“We drowned them in the yamm.”

These concern the same event, showing that baḥr and yamm can refer to the same waters within one story.

Shared reference does not establish the same root or identical semantic fields.

Baḥr has the separate root B Ḥ R; yamm is also an independent name.

The infancy passages allow yamm to be understood as a great river, while the drowning account describes waters elsewhere called baḥr.

Depending on context, “sea” or “great river” can thus be suitable.

Is yamm borrowed from another Semitic language?

Lexical reports attribute to some writers the view that yamm entered Arabic from Syriac.

Syriac yammā and Hebrew yām denote sea or a body of water, and a related form is also recorded in Ugaritic.

These similarities show the name’s broad Semitic history, but its presence in several languages does not alone prove direct borrowing into Arabic from Syriac.

A shared Semitic word may be involved; possible transfer between languages requires separate historical investigation.

Nor should shared Semitic forms import every associated meaning, such as a mythological sea being, into the Qur’anic word.

In the passages examined, al-yamm names waters rather than a person.

6. All Qur’anic al-yamm occurrences

The eight occurrences appear in seven verses, because Ṭā Hā 20:39 uses the noun twice. They concern three parts of Moses’ story: his infancy, Pharaoh’s drowning, and the Samaritan’s calf.

The infant Moses and the water

Ṭā Hā 20:39

أَنِ اقْذِفِيهِ فِي التَّابُوتِ فَاقْذِفِيهِ فِي الْيَمِّ فَلْيُلْقِهِ الْيَمُّ بِالسَّاحِلِ يَأْخُذْهُ عَدُوٌّ لِي وَعَدُوٌّ لَهُ

ani iqdhifīhi fī al-tābūti fa-iqdhifīhi fī al-yammi fa-l-yulqihi al-yammu bi-al-sāḥili yaʾkhudhhu ʿaduwwun lī wa-ʿaduwwun lahu

“Put him in the chest, then cast it into the yamm; let the yamm cast him onto the shore, where an enemy of Mine and his will take him.”

Al-yamm occurs twice here.

The first follows fī and is genitive. The second is the subject of fa-l-yulqihi and nominative.

The change of grammatical role does not change the noun’s meaning.

Both denote the particular waters into which the chest is cast before reaching the shore.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:7 also says:

فَإِذَا خِفْتِ عَلَيْهِ فَأَلْقِيهِ فِي الْيَمِّ وَلَا تَخَافِي وَلَا تَحْزَنِي

fa-idhā khifti ʿalayhi fa-alqīhi fī al-yammi wa-lā takhāfī wa-lā taḥzanī

“When you fear for him, cast him into the yamm, and neither fear nor grieve.”

The verses illuminate each other.

Sāḥil, “shore,” in Ṭā Hā 20:39 establishes that these waters have a shore. The narrow definition denying accessible shores should not become a necessary condition of every use.

Historical accounts identify the waters with the Nile, but neither quoted phrase contains that geographical name.

Pharaoh’s drowning

Al-Aʿrāf 7:136 says:

فَانْتَقَمْنَا مِنْهُمْ فَأَغْرَقْنَاهُمْ فِي الْيَمِّ بِأَنَّهُمْ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا

fa-intaqamnā minhum fa-aghraqnāhum fī al-yammi bi-annahum kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā

“We called them to account and drowned them in the yamm because they denied Our signs.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:40 also says:

فَأَخَذْنَاهُ وَجُنُودَهُ فَنَبَذْنَاهُمْ فِي الْيَمِّ

fa-akhadhnāhu wa-junūdahu fa-nabadhnāhum fī al-yammi

“We seized him and his soldiers and cast them into the yamm.”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:40 repeats the same expression with a different continuation.

Ṭā Hā 20:78 says:

فَأَتْبَعَهُمْ فِرْعَوْنُ بِجُنُودِهِ فَغَشِيَهُمْ مِنَ الْيَمِّ مَا غَشِيَهُمْ

fa-atbaʿahum firʿawnu bi-junūdihi fa-ghashiyahum mina al-yammi mā ghashiyahum

“Pharaoh pursued them with his soldiers, and what overwhelmed them from the yamm overwhelmed them.”

Yamm denotes the waters involved in Pharaoh’s army being drowned.

The noun itself does not mean drowning, perishing, or punishment.

Verbs such as aghraqnā, nabadhnāhum, and ghashiyahum, together with context, supply those meanings.

Replacing yamm everywhere with punishment or destruction would discard its literal meaning.

The Samaritan and the calf

Ṭā Hā 20:97

لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ ثُمَّ لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ فِي الْيَمِّ نَسْفًا

la-nuḥarriqannahu thumma la-nansifannahu fī al-yammi nasfan

“We will surely burn it, then scatter it thoroughly in the yamm.”

The subject is the fate of the calf made by the Samaritan.

Yamm names the location where the object of la-nansifannahu is scattered.

The wording identifies neither necessarily salt water nor precisely the waters of Pharaoh’s drowning. It does not establish a geographical location.

Nasf and taḥrīq belong to separate roots and should not generate a new meaning for Y M M.

What the water occurrences show

In all eight, al-yamm denotes a body of water.

The infancy passages show waters with an accessible shore.

The drowning passages show that yamm and baḥr can name the same waters in one event.

The Samaritan passage makes the waters the place of casting and scattering something.

None of these occurrences means intending.

7. Distinctions from related roots

Y M M and ʾ M M

Both families concern intending and approaching a destination.

Umm, ummah, and imām nevertheless have their own uses and structures. Shared intention does not make them Y M M derivatives.

The historical disagreement over yāʾ replacing hamzah or forming a separate family should also remain visible.

Y M M and Q Ṣ D

Qaṣada likewise concerns intending and approaching, and early dictionaries use it to explain tayammama.

Q Ṣ D is nevertheless separate and has Qur’anic uses concerning moderation and a straight course.

The verbs may explain each other in some sentences without their entire semantic families being identical.

Y M M and B Ḥ R

Baḥr and yamm can both name waters.

The Pharaoh account uses both, while baḥr has a wider lexical family requiring separate study.

Shared reference does not identify roots.

Study of B Ḥ R

Y M M and N H R

Arabic nahr concerns a river and flowing water.

Moses’ infancy story associates yamm with a great river, but the quoted expressions do not themselves use nahr.

Context may explain it as a great river without limiting the general definition to rivers.

Study of N H R

Y M M and Y W M

Yawm belongs to Y W M and denotes a day or unit of time.

Resemblance between yamm and yawm in some unvocalized writing does not remove their structural and semantic differences.

Qur’anic al-yawm and other yawm forms do not belong in the Y M M count.

Study of Y W M

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

One fixed equivalent cannot cover both Qur’anic semantic families.

Selecting or choosing suits Al-Baqarah 2:267: something is deliberately targeted for giving.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 and Al-Māʾidah 5:6, turning toward preserves the verb’s lexical sense and lets the subsequent wiping instruction be read independently.

Sea is a familiar general equivalent for al-yamm; great river may better suit the infancy passages.

Substituting the Nile or a particular sea in a direct translation supplies an interpretive geographical identification. It needs its own supporting explanation.

9. Conclusion

The Qur’anic index groups two distinct word families under Y M M.

Yammama and tayammama concern intending, identifying an objective, and directing toward it. Early Arabic witnesses attest travel, visiting people, and aiming a spear.

Lexical accounts discuss a historical connection with ʾ M M and sometimes treat yāʾ as substituted for hamzah. Complete historical independence should therefore not be assumed without evidence.

Tayammamū occurs three times: once in giving and twice in purification when water is not found. Intending is recognizable throughout; purification passages add a separate wiping command, and technical tayammum names the action.

Al-yamm names a great body of water. Its eight Qur’anic occurrences allow sea or great river according to context. The cited lexical analysis treats water separately from the semantic analogy of intending.

Other Semitic languages have related sea words, while their precise historical relationship with Arabic requires independent study.

A proposed final definition for Forqan:

The verbal branch of Y M M concerns intending, targeting, or turning toward something specific. Tayammama generally has that meaning; purification verses direct people toward clean ṣaʿīd within an instruction to wipe. In the nominal branch, yamm denotes a great body of water, including a sea or great river. The Qur’anic morphological index groups both families together, but a historical link between water and intention is unestablished.
