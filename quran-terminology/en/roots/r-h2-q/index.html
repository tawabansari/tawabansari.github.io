Overtaking, approaching, imposing pressure, and burdening with hardship

Brief root meaning: Something reaching or overtaking a person or thing; approaching the point of arrival or prevailing over something; and, in extended uses, imposing pressure, difficulty, injustice, or an unwelcome condition. Other branches describe haste, transgression, and approaching a stage of life.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The triliteral root R H Q comprises meanings associated with approaching, reaching, overtaking, and imposing pressure.

In رَهِقَهُ الأَمْرُ, an affair reaches or overtakes someone. It may be a difficulty, debt, or condition affecting their life and capacity.

Rahiqahu can also describe approaching and catching up with someone, as when a pursuer draws near.

Form IV arhaqa can mean burdening another with a difficult condition, imposing pressure, or making someone endure something heavy.

The root is not restricted to difficulty: the Qur’an also uses it for darkness or humiliation overtaking faces.

Early Arabic uses رَاهَقَ الغُلَامُ الحُلُمَ for a boy approaching puberty. Nearness is central here, without necessarily implying pressure or oppression.

The entire family should therefore not be reduced to modern senses such as “exhausting” or “causing psychological stress.”

Three main Qur’anic fields emerge: overtaking faces or people; burdening someone with difficulty; and rahaq as injustice, transgression, or distress.

Forms examined and Qur’anic occurrences

R H Q occurs ten times in ten Qur’anic verses, in three morphological groups:

  • Form I verbs: يَرْهَقُ in Yūnus 10:26; وَتَرْهَقُهُمْ in Yūnus 10:27; تَرْهَقُهُمْ in Al-Qalam 68:43 and Al-Maʿārij 70:44; and تَرْهَقُهَا in ʿAbasa 80:41.
  • Form IV verbs: تُرْهِقْنِي in Al-Kahf 18:73; يُرْهِقَهُمَا in Al-Kahf 18:80; and سَأُرْهِقُهُ in Al-Muddaththir 74:17.
  • The noun rahaq: رَهَقًا in Al-Jinn 72:6 and Al-Jinn 72:13.

Other known Arabic forms include رَهِقَ, رَهَق, رَهْقَة, أَرْهَقَ, إِرْهَاق, مُرْهَق, رَاهَقَ, مُرَاهَقَة, مُرَاهِق, رَهُوق, and رَهْقَى.

This list represents the broader lexical family: some forms or their inflections occur in the Qur’an, while others, such as murāhiq and murāhaqah, do not.

2. Semantic cores and early Arabic usage

Overtaking and approaching: An early lexical analysis

An early analysis identifies two closely related principles for R H Q:

الرَّاءُ وَالْهَاءُ وَالْقَافُ أَصْلَانِ مُتَقَارِبَانِ: فَأَحَدُهُمَا غِشْيَانُ الشَّيْءِ الشَّيْءَ، وَالْآخَرُ الْعَجَلَةُ وَالتَّأْخِيرُ.

al-rāʾu wa-al-hāʾu wa-al-qāfu aṣlāni mutaqāribāni fa-aḥaduhumā ghishyānu al-shayʾi al-shayʾa wa-al-ākharu al-ʿajalatu wa-al-taʾkhīru

Translation: “Rāʾ, hāʾ, and qāf have two related principles: one thing overtaking another, and haste and delay.”

The classification matters because it recognizes distinctions within the family rather than imposing one undifferentiated meaning.

In the first branch, something reaches, comes upon, or overtakes another.

The second involves haste, approaching a particular time or situation, and delaying until the next stage is near.

For example, the family can describe delaying an activity until the next one is approaching.

It can also describe hurrying after someone and drawing near.

An association between approach, arrival, and overtaking is possible, but it is not a demonstrated historical derivation of every form.

Rahiqahu al-amr: An affair overtaking someone

Early lexical sources record:

رَهِقَهُ الأَمْرُ: غَشِيَهُ.

rahiqahu al-amru ghashiyahu

Translation: “An affair overtook him.”

They also record:

رَهِقَهُ مَا يَكْرَهُ.

rahiqahu mā yakrahu

Meaning: “Something unwelcome reached or overtook him.”

The unwelcome affair may become a burden affecting the person’s circumstances.

The verb itself does not mean that the condition necessarily lasts forever.

A difficulty can overtake someone and later pass.

Overtaking and permanent duration are distinct concepts.

For someone burdened by debt, Arabic also says:

رَهِقَهُ دَيْنٌ.

rahiqahu daynun

Meaning: “Debt weighed upon him or burdened him.”

Debt is not a material covering on the body; its obligation and pressure affect the person’s situation.

Overtaking can thus be applied in physical, social, and legal settings.

Approaching and reaching

An early lexical explanation states:

رَهِقَ فُلَانٌ فُلَانًا: تَبِعَهُ فَقَارَبَ أَنْ يَلْحَقَهُ.

rahiqa fulānun fulānan tabiʿahu fa-qāraba an yalḥaqahu

Translation: “One person followed another and came close to catching up with him.”

This differs importantly from pressure or hardship.

A pursuer can approach without yet reaching the other person.

Lexical explanations also use rahiqtuhu for drawing near, whether or not the pursuer actually catches the other person.

Actual arrival or capture is therefore not necessary to every use.

Al-rahqā is likewise recorded for a kind of hurried running. Descriptions vary as to whether someone is pursuing or fleeing and making pursuit difficult; it should not be restricted to one running scenario.

Haste, pursuit, and nearness are associated in this field.

Rapid running is not itself injustice or imposed hardship. Context establishes the movement’s character and result.

Rahūq: A swift-striding camel

Early Arabic uses rahūq for a camel taking long, swift strides.

When led by a rein, the camel’s long strides bring it so close that it seems about to reach its leader with its feet.

This is readily associated with approaching and reaching.

It does not make high speed the meaning of every occurrence of R H Q.

Yūnus 10:26, for example, concerns faces being overtaken, not moving rapidly.

Speed belongs to the camel’s movement and this particular derivative.

Arhaqahu ʿusran: Imposing hardship

Form IV has an established construction:

أَرْهَقَهُ عُسْرًا.

arhaqahu ʿusran

Meaning: “He imposed hardship on him.”

Another expression is:

لَا تُرْهِقْنِي، لَا أَرْهَقَكَ اللَّهُ.

lā turhiqnī lā arhaqaka Allāhu

Translation: “Do not make things difficult for me; may The God not make things difficult for you.”

Arhaqa here goes beyond simply overtaking to burdening someone with difficulty.

Al-Kahf 18:73 clearly illustrates this use with ʿusran.

Form IV alone does not establish that every arhaqa imposes something absolutely beyond human capacity.

A demand may be heavy to endure without being physically or psychologically impossible.

Context establishes the pressure’s severity and the person’s capacity.

Murāhiq: Approaching puberty

رَاهَقَ الغُلَامُ الحُلُمَ describes a boy approaching puberty.

Murāhiq identifies someone nearing that stage.

Modern Arabic also uses murāhiq and murāhaqah in discussions of adolescence.

The older meaning and the modern term nevertheless differ in scope.

The early sense emphasizes approaching puberty.

Modern usage may encompass a wider period of physical, psychological, and social development.

Not every medical, psychological, or social feature of adolescence can be extracted from R H Q.

Nor does the word itself mean adolescent distress or psychological pressure.

Its connection here is approaching a particular stage of life.

Rahaq: Haste, rashness, and transgression

Arabic sources record rahaq for haste, rashness, ignorance, transgression, and reprehensible conduct.

An established expression is:

فِي فُلَانٍ رَهَقٌ.

fī fulānin rahaqun

Meaning: The person displays rashness, haste, or a tendency to overstep limits.

Rahaq is also explained in some sources through lying, fault, injustice, and sin.

This branch can carry a negative moral sense.

That does not make every derivative mean sin or corruption.

Murāhiq, someone nearing puberty, is not a moral label for a sinner.

Likewise, yarhaqu wujūhahum in Yūnus 10:26 concerns faces being overtaken; the verb cannot simply be replaced with “committing sin.”

Moral value belongs to a particular usage and its context, not indiscriminately to the entire root family.

3. Pre-Islamic poetry and early Arabic evidence

Rahaq in al-Aʿshā’s poetry

A verse attributed to al-Aʿshā, the pre-Islamic poet, reads:

لَا شَيْءَ يَنْفَعُنِي مِنْ دُونِ رُؤْيَتِهَا
هَلْ يَشْتَفِي وَامِقٌ مَا لَمْ يُصِبْ رَهَقَا

lā shayʾa yanfaʿunī min dūni ruʾyatihā / hal yashtafī wāmiqun mā lam yuṣib rahaqā

A close rendering under the transgression reading: “Nothing benefits me without seeing her; can a lover find relief without encountering transgression?” This is an interpretive rendering, not proof of a particular act.

Rahaq occurs here in the setting of love and seeking the beloved.

An early explanation connects it with approaching forbidden or reprehensible acts; “distress” alone does not adequately convey that reading.

The verse alone does not identify the exact act intended.

A particular sexual act, sin, or assault should therefore not be supplied without evidence.

The witness records rahaq in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, understood in an early explanation as transgression.

This relates to the ethical branch, without making every use morally negative.

Being frequented as a positive image in Zuhayr’s poetry

A verse attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, the pre-Islamic poet, reads:

وَمُرَهَّقِ النِّيرَانِ يُحْمَدُ فِي الْـ
لَأْوَاءِ غَيْرِ مُلَعَّنِ الْقِدْرِ

wa-murahhaqi al-nīrāni yuḥmadu fī al-laʾwāʾi ghayri mulaʿʿani al-qidri

Translation: “One whose fires are much frequented is praised in hardship, his cooking pot not reviled.”

Murahhaq al-nīrān depicts a generous man whose fire and food attract guests and those in need.

Early commentary explains the expression as guests coming upon or frequenting his fires.

The witness is significant because coming upon something in this family need not be negative.

Many visitors here signal generosity and hospitality.

The positive value comes from the man’s conduct, not from an inherent meaning of generosity in R H Q.

That distinction is essential to lexical study.

Arhaqahu: Bringing harm or danger upon someone

Abū Khirāsh al-Hudhalī says:

وَلَوْلَا نَحْنُ أَرْهَقَهُ صُهَيْبٌ
حُسَامَ الْحَدِّ مَذْرُوبًا خَشِيبَا

wa-lawlā naḥnu arhaqahu ṣuhaybun / ḥusāma al-ḥaddi madhrūban khashībā

A close rendering: “Had it not been for us, Ṣuhayb would have brought a sharp-edged sword upon him.”

Arhaqahu occurs with ḥusām, a sword.

In this construction, it means bringing or directing the sword upon someone.

Irhāq can therefore involve bringing something near or upon another.

The sword and martial injury come from ḥusām and the context, not because arhaqa always means attacking with a sword.

Early transmissions vary in the sword’s adjective, including madhrūban and maṭrūran.

Abū Khirāsh lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without an independently established date, this verse should not be called certainly pre-Islamic.

Haste and rashness in al-Akhṭal’s poetry

Al-Akhṭal, the Umayyad poet, says:

صُلْبُ الْحَيَازِيمِ لَا هَذْرُ الْكَلَامِ إِذَا
هَزَّ الْقَنَاةَ وَلَا مُسْتَعْجِلٌ رَهِقُ

ṣulbu al-ḥayāzīmi lā hadhru al-kalāmi idhā / hazza al-qanāta wa-lā mustaʿjilun rahiqu

A close rendering: “Firm and strong, no babbler when he shakes the spear, nor a hasty, reckless man.”

Rahiq here concerns haste and rashness.

The poet praises someone who does not behave impulsively in conflict or difficulty.

This illustrates the behavioral branch.

Rahiq here does not mean physical pressure or a face being covered.

Early poetry thus attests at least three fields: approach and overtaking; burdening or bringing something upon another; and haste or transgression.

They are related without becoming one exceptionless meaning.

4. Yarhaqu: Overtaking faces and people in the Qur’an

Yūnus 10:26: Faces not covered by darkness or humiliation

Verse text:

لِلَّذِينَ أَحْسَنُوا الْحُسْنَىٰ وَزِيَادَةٌ ۖ وَلَا يَرْهَقُ وُجُوهَهُمْ قَتَرٌ وَلَا ذِلَّةٌ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ الْجَنَّةِ ۖ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

li-alladhīna aḥsanū al-ḥusnā wa-ziyādatun wa-lā yarhaqu wujūhahum qatarun wa-lā dhillatun ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-jannati hum fīhā khālidūna

Yūnus 10:26

Translation:

“For those who do good is the best and an increase. Neither darkness nor humiliation covers their faces. They are the people of the garden, remaining there.”

Yarhaqu is a Form I imperfect verb.

Qatarun is its subject, with dhillatun coordinated to it.

Wujūhahum is the object.

The construction concerns faces being overtaken but negates that occurrence.

Lā yarhaqu wujūhahum means that darkness and humiliation do not overtake their faces.

Covering or overtaking is clear here.

R H Q itself does not mean darkness or humiliation; qatar and dhillah supply those meanings.

Nor does the verb alone establish a material substance covering the faces.

The verse describes a condition in which darkness and humiliation become visible upon or overtake the face.

“Does not cover” or “does not overtake” therefore suits this context.

Yūnus 10:27: Humiliation overtaking people

Verse text:

وَالَّذِينَ كَسَبُوا السَّيِّئَاتِ جَزَاءُ سَيِّئَةٍ بِمِثْلِهَا وَتَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّةٌ ۖ مَا لَهُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ مِنْ عَاصِمٍ ۖ كَأَنَّمَا أُغْشِيَتْ وُجُوهُهُمْ قِطَعًا مِنَ اللَّيْلِ مُظْلِمًا ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ ۖ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

wa-alladhīna kasabū al-sayyiʾāti jazāʾu sayyiʾatin bi-mithlihā wa-tarhaquhum dhillatun mā lahum mina Allāhi min ʿāṣimin ka-annamā ughshiyat wujūhuhum qiṭaʿan mina al-layli muẓliman ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūna

Yūnus 10:27

Translation:

“Those who acquire evil deeds receive the recompense of an evil deed in its like, and humiliation covers them. They have no protector against The God. It is as though their faces were covered with portions of a dark night. They are the people of the fire, remaining there.”

Wa-tarhaquhum consists of a conjunction, an imperfect verb, and the object suffix -hum.

Dhillatun is the subject.

The previous verse negated darkness and humiliation overtaking faces.

This verse affirms humiliation overtaking another group.

The contrast matters.

Both verbs preserve the shared meaning of overtaking; the people and their condition differ.

Verse 27 then compares their faces to being covered with portions of a dark night.

This reinforces the image of covering.

It does not make a darkening face the meaning of every occurrence of R H Q.

Facial darkness belongs to this context; the root has a wider range.

Al-Qalam 68:43: Humiliation overtaking them

Verse text:

خَاشِعَةً أَبْصَارُهُمْ تَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّةٌ وَقَدْ كَانُوا يُدْعَوْنَ إِلَى السُّجُودِ وَهُمْ سَالِمُونَ

khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum tarhaquhum dhillatun wa-qad kānū yudʿawna ilā al-sujūdi wa-hum sālimūna

Al-Qalam 68:43

Translation:

“Their eyes downcast, humiliation overtakes them; they had previously been called to prostrate while they were sound.”

Tarhaquhum has the same verbal structure as in Yūnus 10:27.

Its subject is dhillah.

The opening khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum describes the eyes.

Tarhaquhum dhillah then describes humiliation overtaking them.

The two expressions are related but not synonymous.

Downcast eyes describe their gaze; encompassing humiliation describes the people’s condition.

The verse recalls their earlier invitation to prostrate while they were healthy and able.

The humiliation therefore belongs to the outcome of their conduct and situation.

Tarhaquhum itself does not mean inability to prostrate; the preceding verse and context supply that detail.

Al-Maʿārij 70:44: The same construction on the promised day

Verse text:

خَاشِعَةً أَبْصَارُهُمْ تَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّةٌ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ الْيَوْمُ الَّذِي كَانُوا يُوعَدُونَ

khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum tarhaquhum dhillatun dhālika al-yawmu alladhī kānū yūʿadūna

Al-Maʿārij 70:44

Translation:

“Their eyes downcast, humiliation overtakes them. This is the day they were promised.”

Tarhaquhum dhillah occurs here as well.

Its construction matches Al-Qalam 68:43.

Both verses present humiliation as overtaking people.

Their continuations differ, however.

Al-Qalam recalls the earlier call to prostration.

Al-Maʿārij emphasizes the previously promised day.

R H Q preserves its semantic field in both, while context supplies each verse’s fuller message.

ʿAbasa 80:41: Darkness overtaking faces

Verse text:

تَرْهَقُهَا قَتَرَةٌ

tarhaquhā qataratun

ʿAbasa 80:41

Translation:

“Darkness overtakes them.”

The preceding verse says:

وَوُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ عَلَيْهَا غَبَرَةٌ

wa-wujūhun yawmaʾidhin ʿalayhā ghabaratun

ʿAbasa 80:40

“And faces that day will have dust upon them.”

The suffix -hā in tarhaquhā refers to those faces.

Qataratun is the subject.

The verb again expresses overtaking or covering.

ʿAbasa 80:42 identifies those people as الْكَفَرَةُ الْفَجَرَةُ, the disbelieving and wicked.

The sequence places the faces’ darkness in the scene of their final outcome.

R H Q itself does not mean disbelief or wickedness here.

The next verse states those qualities with separate words.

Findings from the five Form I occurrences

Overtaking is clear in all five Qur’anic Form I occurrences.

Sometimes darkness or humiliation overtakes faces.

Sometimes humiliation encompasses the people themselves.

The verb concerns a condition becoming apparent upon or prevailing over someone.

Accompanying words identify that condition and its character.

In these constructions, R H Q is not darkness, humiliation, or punishment itself: it expresses that condition overtaking a person or face.

5. Arhaqa: Burdening with hardship and pressure

Al-Kahf 18:73: Wa-lā turhiqnī min amrī ʿusran

Verse text:

قَالَ لَا تُؤَاخِذْنِي بِمَا نَسِيتُ وَلَا تُرْهِقْنِي مِنْ أَمْرِي عُسْرًا

qāla lā tuʾākhidhnī bi-mā nasītu wa-lā turhiqnī min amrī ʿusran

Al-Kahf 18:73

Translation:

“He said: Do not hold me to account for what I forgot, and do not make my affair difficult for me.”

Turhiqnī is a Form IV verb.

The prohibitive lā puts the verb in the jussive; -nī is its object suffix.

ʿUsran identifies the hardship that should not be imposed.

After questioning and objecting, Moses asks his companion not to be harsh with him over his forgetfulness.

The two clauses have distinct functions.

Lā tuʾākhidhnī bi-mā nasītu concerns holding him to account for forgetting.

Wa-lā turhiqnī min amrī ʿusran concerns imposing difficulty upon his affair.

Irhāq is not merely verbal reproach here; it concerns making someone’s situation burdensome.

Arhaqa itself does not mean forgetting.

Nasītu expresses forgetting, and ʿusran expresses difficulty.

“Do not make my affair difficult for me” is therefore a natural rendering.

Is irhāq always beyond human capacity?

Some lexical explanations describe irhāq as imposing something a person cannot bear.

That use helps explain intense pressure.

It does not establish that every occurrence means an absolutely impossible demand.

In Al-Kahf 18:73, Moses asks his companion not to make his situation difficult.

ʿUsran and their relationship’s context clarify the difficulty.

The statement also does not condemn every difficulty involved in learning, companionship, or responsibility.

It is Moses’ request in the circumstances of this journey.

Al-Kahf 18:80: Yurhiqahumā ṭughyānan wa-kufran

Verse text:

وَأَمَّا الْغُلَامُ فَكَانَ أَبَوَاهُ مُؤْمِنَيْنِ فَخَشِينَا أَنْ يُرْهِقَهُمَا طُغْيَانًا وَكُفْرًا

wa-ammā al-ghulāmu fa-kāna abawāhu muʾminayni fa-khashīnā an yurhiqahumā ṭughyānan wa-kufran

Al-Kahf 18:80

Translation:

“As for the boy, his parents were believers, and we feared that he would overwhelm them with transgression and disbelief.”

Yurhiqahumā is a subjunctive Form IV imperfect verb.

The dual suffix -humā refers to the parents.

Ṭughyānan wa-kufran specifies what they would be overwhelmed with; the relation of that transgression and disbelief to the boy or parents requires interpretation.

The grammar identifies the parents as those affected, but does not by itself prove they would become disbelievers. Early readings include suffering under the boy’s transgression and being drawn by love for him into following his disbelief.

The dual object must therefore be retained without conflating the parents’ being affected with their necessarily adopting the boy’s beliefs.

R H Q itself means neither disbelief nor transgression.

Those are separately named in the sentence.

Irhāq here expresses overwhelming or burdening another with an adverse condition.

The event’s rationale and the story’s full interpretation require the surrounding verses.

The verb’s lexical meaning does not establish a general permission to harm children or claim certain knowledge of people’s future conduct.

Al-Muddaththir 74:17: Sa-urhiquhu ṣaʿūdan

Verse text:

سَأُرْهِقُهُ صَعُودًا

sa-urhiquhu ṣaʿūdan

Al-Muddaththir 74:17

Translation:

“I shall burden him with an arduous ascent.”

Sa-urhiquhu consists of the future marker sa-, a Form IV imperfect verb, and the object suffix -hu.

The God is the speaker and subject.

Ṣaʿūdan specifies what the person will be burdened with or made to endure.

It belongs to Ṣ ʿ D, a family associated with ascending.

Ṣaʿūd can denote a steep ascent or arduous climb and, in explanatory usage, grievous punishment. It should not be treated as though it were only the ordinary verbal noun ṣuʿūd.

Some exegetical reports identify it as a mountain in hell.

The verse itself does not explicitly call it a mountain or state its dimensions or manner of ascent.

“An arduous ascent” is therefore a defensible basic rendering.

Irhāq here means burdening someone with a severe condition.

R H Q itself does not mean ascending.

The ascent comes from ṣaʿūdan; sa-urhiquhu supplies the imposition.

Findings from the three Form IV occurrences

All three place a person under difficulty, pressure, or a heavy condition.

In Al-Kahf 18:73, Moses asks not to be burdened with hardship.

Al-Kahf 18:80 concerns the parents being overwhelmed with transgression and disbelief; the interpretive alternatives explained above remain relevant.

Al-Muddaththir 74:17 concerns an arduous ascent imposed upon someone.

Irhāq clearly involves pressure or imposition in these contexts.

The kind of burden varies with the complements and context.

6. Rahaqan: Distress, injustice, and transgression in Al-Jinn

Al-Jinn 72:6

Verse text:

وَأَنَّهُ كَانَ رِجَالٌ مِنَ الْإِنْسِ يَعُوذُونَ بِرِجَالٍ مِنَ الْجِنِّ فَزَادُوهُمْ رَهَقًا

wa-annahu kāna rijālun mina al-insi yaʿūdhūna bi-rijālin mina al-jinni fa-zādūhum rahaqan

Al-Jinn 72:6

Translation:

“Men among humans used to seek refuge with men among the jinn, and they increased them in distress.”

The meaning of rahaqan

Rahaqan is an indefinite accusative noun here.

It follows fa-zādūhum.

The verse describes an increase in the condition named rahaq.

Early lexical and exegetical explanations include injustice, fear, humiliation, rashness, transgression, and sin.

The variation need not mean there is no semantic relationship at all.

Many of these involve pressure, prevailing over someone, overstepping limits, or an adverse condition.

Which aspect is emphasized depends partly on context and the pronouns’ antecedents.

Fa-zādūhum: Who increased whose rahaq?

The verse introduces two groups:

رِجَالٌ مِنَ الْإِنْسِ: men among humans.

And

رِجَالٍ مِنَ الْجِنِّ: men among the jinn.

It then says fa-zādūhum.

Different analyses assign the subject and object pronouns to these groups differently.

In one reading, the jinn increase the fear and distress of the humans seeking their protection.

In another, human resort to the jinn increases the jinn’s arrogance or transgression.

Both readings occur in early exegesis.

Neither should be made the root’s sole certain meaning merely because one report is well known.

Lexically, rahaq can concern distress or oppression as well as transgression.

Identifying fa-zādūhum’s subject and object is a syntactic and contextual question distinct from defining the root.

Seeking refuge and dependence

In Al-Jinn 72:6, humans seek refuge with jinn.

Yaʿūdhūna belongs to ʿ W Dh and concerns seeking protection.

The verse thus concerns reliance on another power for support.

Some historical accounts describe travelers entering unfamiliar valleys and appealing to an imagined local chief or powerful being for protection.

These reports may illuminate early practices of seeking refuge.

The verse itself does not name a particular valley, individual, or ritual.

It clearly associates this refuge-seeking with increased rahaq.

Instead of the expected relief, an adverse result follows: on one reading, human fear and distress grow; on another, the supposed protectors’ transgression increases.

These are possible explanations of the relationship, not a reason to confine rahaq to just one consequence.

Al-Jinn 72:13

Verse text:

وَأَنَّا لَمَّا سَمِعْنَا الْهُدَىٰ آمَنَّا بِهِ ۖ فَمَنْ يُؤْمِنْ بِرَبِّهِ فَلَا يَخَافُ بَخْسًا وَلَا رَهَقًا

wa-annā lammā samiʿnā al-hudā āmannā bihi fa-man yuʾmin bi-rabbihi fa-lā yakhāfu bakhsan wa-lā rahaqan

Al-Jinn 72:13

Translation:

“When we heard the guidance, we believed in it. Whoever believes in his Lord need fear neither loss of right nor injustice and wrongful pressure.”

Bakhs and rahaq

Rahaqan stands alongside bakhsan here.

Bakhs, from B Kh S, concerns withholding or diminishing someone’s due.

Rahaq in this context can denote oppression or an unjust burden imposed upon someone.

Early explanations sometimes distinguish them as neither subtracting from a person’s good deeds nor adding an undeserved sin or burden.

This fits the context of just reckoning.

Two directions can therefore be distinguished:

Being deprived of what is due.

Being saddled with an unjust burden that should not be imposed.

The contrast does not make bakhs always mean reducing reward or rahaq always mean adding sin throughout Arabic.

It explains their relationship in this particular context.

Does a believer never experience difficulty?

Al-Jinn 72:13 says the believer does not fear bakhs or rahaq.

It does not follow that believers never experience illness, poverty, social pressure, or hardship in earthly life.

Other Qur’anic passages discuss trials, losses, afflictions, and responsibility amid difficulty.

Here the statement follows belief in guidance and confidence in the Lord’s justice.

Freedom from unjust treatment in divine judgment differs from freedom from every worldly difficulty.

Rahaq here does not mean every strenuous effort or heavy responsibility.

It concerns a condition or burden wrongfully imposed upon a person.

The two uses of rahaq in Al-Jinn

Al-Jinn 72:6 places rahaq in the outcome of humans seeking refuge with jinn.

Al-Jinn 72:13 pairs it with bakhs in an assurance to believers.

The first involves increased distress, pressure, or transgression.

The second negates fear of oppression or an unjust burden.

Rahaq has a broad field involving pressure and overstepping limits, while its particular referent varies.

Neither verse should be confined to one narrow interpretation applied to every occurrence, such as only sin, only fear, or only humiliation.

7. Comparison with related roots

R H Q (rahaq) and Gh Sh Y (ghishyān)

These families overlap in overtaking or covering.

Gh Sh Y concerns covering or coming upon something.

R H Q approaches that field in Yūnus, Al-Qalam, Al-Maʿārij, and ʿAbasa.

Other R H Q branches concern approaching, exerting pressure, and burdening with difficulty.

Gh Sh Y cannot therefore replace it in every context.

Yūnus 10:27 uses both fields: humiliation overtakes people, while their faces are described through an image of being covered in darkness.

Their coexistence does not establish a common historical root.

R H Q (rahaq) and L Ḥ Q (luḥūq)

L Ḥ Q concerns reaching, following, and joining.

Some early R H Q usages likewise concern approaching and reaching.

R H Q can also express an encompassing burden or pressure absent from some uses of L Ḥ Q.

Conversely, R H Q sometimes describes approaching someone without yet fully reaching them.

Approaching, arriving, and burdening should thus be distinguished.

R H Q (irhāq) and ʿ S R (ʿusr)

ʿUsr means difficulty.

Irhāq can express imposing difficulty on another.

Al-Kahf 18:73 places both in one sentence.

ʿUsran names the difficulty; turhiqnī concerns burdening the person with it.

Arhaqa itself therefore does not name every kind of difficulty.

Difficulty may exist without someone imposing it on another.

Irhāq in constructions such as arhaqahu ʿusran expresses a relationship between the agent and the burdened person.

R H Q (rahaq) and Ẓ L M (ẓulm)

Ẓulm concerns injustice and placing matters wrongly in the Qur’an.

Some lexical uses of rahaq likewise involve oppression and transgression.

In Al-Jinn 72:13, injustice or wrongful pressure fits the context.

Not every R H Q occurrence, however, means injustice.

Approaching puberty or drawing near to someone is not inherently oppressive.

A negative value must be supported by the particular use and context.

R H Q (rahaq) and Dh L L (dhillah)

Dhillah concerns humiliation and lowered standing.

Humiliation overtakes people in Yūnus 10:27, Al-Qalam 68:43, and Al-Maʿārij 70:44.

Dhillah names the condition; R H Q expresses its overtaking the person.

Translation should not erase their distinction.

Rendering tarhaquhum dhillah as “humiliation humiliates them” obscures the difference between verb and subject.

“Humiliation overtakes them” is more accurate.

R H Q (rahaq) and R H B (rahbah)

The roots differ only in their final consonant but have distinct principal meanings.

Rahbah concerns fear and apprehension.

Rahaq may accompany fear and pressure without being restricted to fear.

One reading of rahaqan in Al-Jinn 72:6 involves increased human fear.

Other occurrences concern humiliation, hardship, or overtaking.

Sound similarity and overlapping contexts of fear do not justify conflating R H B and R H Q.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Equivalents for the Qur’anic occurrences

يَرْهَقُ — Yūnus 10:26

Suitable equivalents: “overtakes” or “covers.”

In the negative clause: “Darkness and humiliation do not cover their faces.”

وَتَرْهَقُهُمْ — Yūnus 10:27

Suitable equivalents: “and overtakes them” or “and covers them.”

In the full construction: “And humiliation overtakes them.”

تُرْهِقْنِي — Al-Kahf 18:73

Within the prohibition: “Do not be harsh with me” or “do not impose hardship on me.”

In the verse: “Do not make my affair difficult for me.”

يُرْهِقَهُمَا — Al-Kahf 18:80

Suitable equivalents: “overwhelm them both” or “burden them both.”

In the sentence: “We feared that he would overwhelm them with transgression and disbelief.” The interpretation should remain distinct from the verb’s basic meaning.

تَرْهَقُهُمْ — Al-Qalam 68:43

Suitable equivalent: “overtakes them.”

In the sentence: “Humiliation overtakes them.”

تَرْهَقُهُمْ — Al-Maʿārij 70:44

The same rendering applies: “Humiliation overtakes them.”

رَهَقًا — Al-Jinn 72:6

Possible renderings to examine include “distress,” “oppression,” “transgression,” or “overstepping limits.”

“They increased them in distress” preserves a general sense; identifying the subject, object, and precise kind of rahaq requires separate syntactic and contextual analysis.

رَهَقًا — Al-Jinn 72:13

Suitable equivalents: “injustice,” “wrongful pressure,” or “an undeserved burden.”

With bakhsan: “He does not fear loss of right or unjust oppression.”

سَأُرْهِقُهُ — Al-Muddaththir 74:17

Suitable equivalents: “I shall burden him” or “I shall compel him.”

In full: “I shall burden him with an arduous ascent.”

تَرْهَقُهَا — ʿAbasa 80:41

Suitable equivalents: “overtakes them” or “covers them.”

In the sentence: “Darkness overtakes them.”

9. Conclusions and final definition

R H Q encompasses approaching, reaching, overtaking, and imposing pressure.

In one principal branch, something reaches or overtakes a person.

In another, a person or thing draws near, sometimes almost reaching another.

Form IV arhaqa can burden someone with difficulty or pressure.

Early Arabic also records rahaq for haste, rashness, transgression, sin, and injustice.

Murāhiq in early usage identifies someone nearing puberty, showing that approach to a life stage can occur in this family without oppression or pressure.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period likewise illustrates the root’s breadth.

An early explanation reads rahaq in al-Aʿshā’s verse as transgression or approaching forbidden acts.

Zuhayr’s murahhaq al-nīrān depicts a generous man whose fire and food attract guests.

Abū Khirāsh’s arhaqahu concerns bringing a sword upon someone.

Positive or negative value is therefore not fixed across every derivative.

The root occurs ten times in the Qur’an.

Five Form I verbs describe overtaking faces or people.

Three Form IV verbs describe imposing hardship, overwhelming with transgression and disbelief, or burdening with an arduous ascent.

The noun rahaq occurs twice in Al-Jinn: once with refuge-seeking and increased distress or transgression, and once with assurance against injustice or wrongful pressure for believers.

The occurrences share a field of reaching and overtaking but cannot all be translated with one fixed Persian or English word.

Final definition of R H Q:

In its principal field, the family denotes approaching, reaching, or overtaking someone or something.

Extended uses can concern a condition prevailing over someone, imposed hardship, unjust burdens, and rash transgression.

Its three main Qur’anic branches are:

1. Overtaking: Darkness or humiliation covering faces or people.

2. Burdening or imposing: Hardship for Moses, overwhelming the parents with transgression and disbelief, or an arduous ascent.

3. Rahaq as an adverse condition: Distress, pressure, transgression, or injustice in Al-Jinn’s two verses.

Not every form means humiliation, sin, disbelief, fear, or difficulty. The verbs of overtaking draw the specific condition from their complements; the noun rahaq itself, however, has attested senses such as sin, transgression, and injustice. Those senses should neither be denied nor transferred to every derivative.

Approaching need not imply actual arrival, and pressure should not always be reduced to complete incapacity or psychological distress.

A suitable semantic core for Forqan’s study and translation is:

“Approaching and overtaking; in extended usage, burdening with pressure, difficulty, or an unjust condition.”

This preserves the connection between Qur’anic derivatives without forcing their differing meanings into one image.

Each use requires attention to what or who overtakes, who is affected, and what condition or pressure is involved.

This keeps distinct facial darkness, imposed hardship, overwhelming transgression and disbelief, and rahaq as injustice or wrongful pressure, while recognizing their shared lexical family.
