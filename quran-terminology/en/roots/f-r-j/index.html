The root ف ر ج — Gaps, openings, sexual privacy, and relief from distress

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فُرُوج
(furūj)
Gaps or private parts, according to context

(farjahā)
Singular usage concerning Mary

فُرِجَتْ
(furijat)
Was opened; passive verb

In early Arabic, the triliteral root ف ر ج concerns a gap, interval, or opening between things. In physical usage, فَرْج denotes a space or cleft between two parts, such as the space between the legs, a crack in a wall, or an opening in a structure.

The same family includes فَرْج as a word for human or animal genitals. فَرَج also denotes relief from hardship and the removal of sorrow, as though the constriction pressing upon a person has opened.

The root occurs nine times in the Qur’an in two principal forms: the noun فَرْج, including its plural فُرُوج, and the passive verb فُرِجَتْ. Its uses concern two semantic fields: gaps and the opening of the sky, and guarding human genitals and sexual privacy.

Farj is not exclusively sexual in Arabic. Conversely, its general sense of openness should not erase its clear reference to sexual organs and chastity in ethical passages. The sentence’s subject and accompanying words determine the intended meaning.

1. Early Arabic meanings and the historical standing of the evidence

A gap or interval between things

In Arabic, فَرْج denotes a space or opening between two parts.

A gap between two stones, for example, can be called farj. An opening in a wall belongs to the same semantic field.

In geographical expressions, فَرْجُ الْوَادِي denotes the open space between a valley’s sides, while فَرْجُ الْجَبَلِ denotes a cleft or passage between parts of a mountain.

Some early uses apply فُرُوج to vulnerable openings between defensive positions: places needing protection against enemy penetration.

The physical sense therefore concerns both making an opening and an existing gap or interval.

A poetic example attributed to the pre-Islamic period

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays describes a horse:

لَهَا ذَنَبٌ مِثْلُ ذَيْلِ الْعَرُوسِ
تَسُدُّ بِهِ فَرْجَهَا مِنْ دُبُرْ

Lahā dhanabun mithlu dhayli al-ʿarūs; tasuddu bihi farjahā min dubur.

Translation: “She has a tail like the train of a bride’s garment, with which she covers the space between her legs from behind.”

The poet describes the tail’s length and fullness.

Here فَرْجَهَا refers to the space between the horse’s hind legs, which its long, thick tail can cover from behind.

Farj here need not name the reproductive organ; it can carry the broader meaning of a space between bodily parts.

The line is transmitted in the collection attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and in early literary writings, although some accounts attribute it to another poet. It is valuable evidence of early usage, but its attribution to Imruʾ al-Qays and precise date should not be treated as established beyond dispute.

The relevant sense is clear: farj could describe a space between limbs without necessarily referring to sexual activity or ethical conduct.

A name for the sexual organ

In established Arabic usage, فَرْج also names the genital organ or private parts of a human or animal.

This use relates to the idea of an interval or opening, but has become an established name for a body part.

Once a word has acquired such a settled use, it cannot simply be translated as “gap” in every sentence.

For example, حفظ فرجه concerns guarding one’s private parts and sexual boundaries, rather than maintaining a geometrical space.

In this usage, furūj is the plural for private parts; in فروج السماء, however, it refers to gaps or openings in the sky.

The root’s general meaning must therefore remain distinct from a noun’s established contextual meaning.

Relief from sorrow and hardship

Another branch appears in expressions such as فَرَجَ اللَّهُ الْهَمَّ and فَرَّجَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ.

These concern the removal of sorrow, the appearance of a way out, and relief after hardship.

A verse attributed to Umayya ibn Abī al-Ṣalt says:

رُبَّمَا تَكْرَهُ النُّفُوسُ مِنَ الْأَمْرِ
لَهُ فُرْجَةٌ كَحَلِّ الْعِقَالِ

Rubbamā takrahu al-nufūsu mina al-amri; lahu furjatun ka-ḥalli al-ʿiqāl.

Translation: “Sometimes people dislike a circumstance, yet it has an opening like the untying of a tether.”

The image حَلِّ الْعِقَالِ concerns undoing a bond that restrains something.

Physical opening is extended here to a person’s situation in distress.

Umayya ibn Abī al-Ṣalt lived near the advent of Islam, and poems attributed to him survive through later transmission. The wording and authenticity of some are disputed; attribution alone cannot establish when the sense “relief from sorrow” first developed.

Nevertheless, early dictionaries clearly record this meaning.

Its imagery is understandable: removing an obstacle opens a path; removing hardship and sorrow releases a person from pressure and constriction.

This explanation does not establish a definitive historical sequence for every branch of the root.

2. Linguistic forms and the central meaning

Arabic recognizes various forms in this family:

  • فَرْج: a gap or interval between things; also the genital organ.
  • فُرُوج: plural of farj; gaps or private parts, depending on context.
  • فُرْجَة and فَرْجَة: an interval, opening, or open space between parts.
  • فَرَج: relief from sorrow, hardship, or distress.
  • فَرَجَ: opened or made a gap or interval.
  • فَرَّجَ: opened, widened, or relieved hardship.
  • أَفْرَجَ: opened, released, or removed an obstacle.
  • اِنْفَرَجَ: opened, moved apart, or emerged from constriction.
  • تَفَرَّجَ: opened or obtained relief from grief and trouble.
  • مُنْفَرِج: open, spread apart, or having an interval.
  • فَارِج: an opener or remover of obstacles and hardship.

Modern Persian also uses borrowings such as faraj, infirāj, and tafarruj. The current sense of tafarruj as strolling or sightseeing should not, however, be presented as this root’s direct Qur’anic meaning.

Within this family, the Qur’an uses only the noun farj and its plural furūj, and the passive verb فُرِجَتْ.

The central meaning can be expressed as follows:

An existing or emerging gap or opening between things; by extension, the opening of a constricted situation and removal of an obstacle.

The genital designation is an established use of this family. Relief from sorrow is also a recognized Arabic branch, although the Qur’an does not directly express that sense through this root.

3. Furūj concerning the sky: gaps and openings

Two Qur’anic occurrences concern the sky.

A. Qāf 50:6: a sky without gaps

أَفَلَمْ يَنْظُرُوا إِلَى السَّمَاءِ فَوْقَهُمْ كَيْفَ بَنَيْنَاهَا وَزَيَّنَّاهَا وَمَا لَهَا مِنْ فُرُوجٍ

A-fa-lam yanẓurū ilā al-samāʾi fawqahum kayfa banaynāhā wa-zayyannāhā wa-mā lahā min furūj.

Translation: “Have they not looked at the sky above them, how We built it and adorned it, and it has no gaps?”

Here فُرُوج denotes gaps, ruptures, or openings that could disrupt a structure’s continuity.

وَمَا لَهَا مِنْ فُرُوجٍ concerns the sky’s integrity and wholeness in the Qur’anic image.

Farj here has no sexual reference.

Nor does the word pronounce a scientific judgment on every empty space in the universe. Distances between stars are not, in themselves, gaps or defects in the sky’s structure.

The Qur’an invites the audience to observe the sky, its construction, and its adornment.

In this context, furūj concerns ruptures or gaps in the sky’s structure. The word alone does not establish that the sky is a solid material roof or that the universe contains no empty space.

Al-Mulk 67:3 likewise uses فُطُور in the context of observing the sky and seeking a gap or flaw in its structure.

The expressions belong to different roots but overlap here in the semantic field of clefts and ruptures.

B. Al-Mursalāt 77:9: the sky opened

وَإِذَا السَّمَاءُ فُرِجَتْ

Wa-idhā al-samāʾu furijat.

Translation: “And when the sky is split open.”

فُرِجَتْ is a third-person feminine singular passive perfect verb of Form I.

Its semantic agent is not stated; the sky is presented as undergoing the action.

The subject is no longer an absence of gaps, but an event in which the sky opens or splits.

The surrounding verses also describe great transformations.

Al-Mursalāt 77:8 speaks of the stars being extinguished or effaced; al-Mursalāt 77:9 of the sky being opened; and al-Mursalāt 77:10 of the mountains being scattered.

فُرِجَتْ thus denotes an event changing the sky’s previous condition.

Do Qāf 50:6 and al-Mursalāt 77:9 conflict?

Qāf 50:6 describes the sky as observed by people, without gaps.

Al-Mursalāt 77:9 speaks of its future opening amid great transformations.

Time and context distinguish the descriptions.

A structure’s present integrity does not mean that it can never change or open.

Conversely, the verse in al-Mursalāt does not specify the physical mechanism. The word furijat alone cannot establish that the event means splitting a solid shell, opening a spatial portal, or any particular scientific phenomenon.

Its clear meaning is the sky’s opening or splitting; the nature of that transformation is a separate question.

4. Guarding the furūj: protecting private parts and sexual boundaries

Most Qur’anic occurrences concern human sexual organs.

Farj in these passages names the private body part, while ḥifẓ expresses guarding it.

These should be distinguished:

Farj denotes the private organ.

Ḥifẓ al-farj describes a person’s conduct in protecting that organ and sexual boundaries.

Chastity is therefore not the independent lexical meaning of farj. It arises from its combination with guarding or protecting and from the verse’s context.

A. Al-Muʾminūn 23:5: guarding sexual boundaries

وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ لِفُرُوجِهِمْ حَافِظُونَ

Wa-alladhīna hum li-furūjihim ḥāfiẓūn.

Translation: “And those who guard their private parts and sexual boundaries.”

The verse describes believers’ qualities.

لِفُرُوجِهِمْ denotes their private parts, and حَافِظُونَ expresses guarding and preservation.

The subject is human sexual conduct and observance of its limits.

The following verse, al-Muʾminūn 23:6, mentions spouses and people held in ownership within that period’s social structure.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:7 then addresses those seeking beyond the stated limits.

The context therefore shows that guarding the furūj includes sexual boundaries, not merely concealing the organs from sight.

Nevertheless, the word farj alone cannot yield every rule or condition governing sexual relations. Those questions involve the following sentences and other verses.

B. Al-Maʿārij 70:29: the same expression in another setting

وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ لِفُرُوجِهِمْ حَافِظُونَ

Wa-alladhīna hum li-furūjihim ḥāfiẓūn.

Translation: “And those who guard their private parts and sexual boundaries.”

This verse repeats the wording of al-Muʾminūn 23:5.

The preceding passage in al-Maʿārij describes people who remain constant in their salat, recognize a right for the needy in their wealth, affirm the Day of Judgment, and fear their Lord’s punishment.

Guarding sexual boundaries thus appears alongside responsibilities in worship, finances, and ethics.

Al-Maʿārij 70:30 and al-Maʿārij 70:31 explain sexual boundaries in wording close to the al-Muʾminūn passage.

The repetition identifies guarding the furūj as a quality of believers’ conduct, rather than a designation confined to one sex.

C. Al-Nūr 24:30: men’s gaze and sexual boundaries

قُلْ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ يَغُضُّوا مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِمْ وَيَحْفَظُوا فُرُوجَهُمْ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ أَزْكَىٰ لَهُمْ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا يَصْنَعُونَ

Qul li-l-muʾminīna yaghuḍḍū min abṣārihim wa-yaḥfaẓū furūjahum; dhālika azkā lahum; inna Allāha khabīrun bi-mā yaṣnaʿūn.

Translation: “Tell believing men to lower their gazes and guard their private parts and sexual boundaries. That is purer for them. Indeed, The God is fully aware of what they do.”

Two commands appear together:

Lowering the gaze and guarding the furūj.

يَغُضُّوا belongs to غ ض ض and denotes lowering or restraining the gaze.

يَحْفَظُوا belongs to ح ف ظ and expresses guarding.

فُرُوجَهُمْ is the object of that guarding.

The commands’ proximity connects looking and guarding sexual boundaries within this context.

They remain two distinct actions.

Lowering the gaze concerns the use of the eyes; guarding the furūj concerns bodily privacy and sexual conduct.

Farj cannot therefore be translated as sight, an eye, or sexual desire.

Guarding the furūj here should neither be reduced to clothing alone nor stripped of its concern for private parts and avoiding improper exposure.

D. Al-Nūr 24:31: women’s sexual boundaries

The next verse, al-Nūr 24:31, likewise directs believing women to lower their gazes and guard their furūj.

It says:

«وَيَحْفَظْنَ فُرُوجَهُنَّ»

Wa-yaḥfaẓna furūjahunna.

This expresses the preceding verse’s semantic structure for women.

The grammatical differences reflect the sex and number of those addressed:

In al-Nūr 24:30, فُرُوجَهُمْ concerns men.

In al-Nūr 24:31, فُرُوجَهُنَّ concerns women.

The root meaning remains the same: guarding private parts and sexual boundaries.

The women’s verse then discusses adornment, covering, and limits on displaying adornment. These concern bodily privacy, but farj itself does not mean all adornment, clothing, or covering.

The parallel commands to men and women in consecutive verses show that responsibility for sexual conduct and boundaries is not placed upon one side alone.

E. Al-Aḥzāb 33:35: men’s and women’s shared responsibility

Al-Aḥzāb 33:35 presents a series of believers’ qualities in paired masculine and feminine forms.

Among them is:

«وَالْحَافِظِينَ فُرُوجَهُمْ وَالْحَافِظَاتِ»

Wa-al-ḥāfiẓīna furūjahum wa-al-ḥāfiẓāt.

That is: “And men who guard their private parts and sexual boundaries, and women who guard theirs.”

Furūjahum is stated after al-ḥāfiẓīn, while the object of al-ḥāfiẓāt is understood from the first part.

This structure makes guarding sexual boundaries a shared ethical quality of men and women.

The same verse also names truthfulness, patience, humility, charity, fasting, and remembrance of The God for both groups.

Guarding sexual boundaries thus belongs within a wider range of responsible conduct. It should not be presented as the sole measure of faith or a person’s moral worth.

5. أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا: Mary’s preservation of chastity

Two further occurrences concern Mary, daughter of ʿImrān.

Both pair farj with the verb aḥṣanat.

Aḥṣanat belongs to ح ص ن and relates to protecting, strengthening, or placing something within a guarded boundary.

A. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:91

وَالَّتِي أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهَا مِنْ رُوحِنَا وَجَعَلْنَاهَا وَابْنَهَا آيَةً لِلْعَالَمِينَ

Wa-allatī aḥṣanat farjahā fa-nafakhnā fīhā min rūḥinā wa-jaʿalnāhā wa-ibnahā āyatan li-l-ʿālamīn.

Translation: “And the woman who guarded her chastity: We breathed into her of Our spirit, and made her and her son a sign for all peoples.”

أحصنت فرجها here concerns Mary’s chastity.

The narrative context presents Mary as becoming pregnant without sexual relations with a man.

In Maryam 19:20, responding to the announcement of a child, she says that no human has touched her sexually and that she has not been unchaste.

Chastity is therefore an actual description of Mary’s conduct, not merely a metaphor for purity of thought or faith.

Farj should not, however, be replaced with “womb” in translation. They are not linguistically or anatomically identical.

Farjahā concerns her sexual privacy; the manner of pregnancy and the child’s coming into being is a further subject addressed by the continuation.

B. Al-Taḥrīm 66:12

وَمَرْيَمَ ابْنَتَ عِمْرَانَ الَّتِي أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهِ مِنْ رُوحِنَا وَصَدَّقَتْ بِكَلِمَاتِ رَبِّهَا وَكُتُبِهِ وَكَانَتْ مِنَ الْقَانِتِينَ

Wa-Maryama ibnata ʿImrāna allatī aḥṣanat farjahā fa-nafakhnā fīhi min rūḥinā wa-ṣaddaqat bi-kalimāti rabbihā wa-kutubihi wa-kānat mina al-qānitīn.

Translation: “And Mary, daughter of ʿImrān, who guarded her chastity: We breathed into it of Our spirit; she affirmed her Lord’s words and His books, and was among the obedient.”

The verse repeats aḥṣanat farjahā, but its following pronoun differs from al-Anbiyāʾ 21:91.

Al-Anbiyāʾ says:

«فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهَا»

Fa-nafakhnā fīhā.

That is: “Then We breathed into her.”

Al-Taḥrīm instead says:

«فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهِ»

Fa-nafakhnā fīhi.

That is: “Then We breathed into it.”

The masculine pronoun in fīhi can grammatically refer to farj, a masculine noun, whereas fīhā in al-Anbiyāʾ refers to Mary.

The distinction is real and should not be overlooked in linguistic analysis.

A pronoun’s referent alone, however, cannot disclose the physical or biological mechanism of Jesus’s coming into being.

The verses speak of divine action and the creation’s significance as a sign, without detailing its physical workings.

Nor does “We breathed” establish that The God has a mouth, breath, or bodily organs like physical beings.

Direct meaning, grammatical structure, and divine creation should remain distinct from imagined corporeal descriptions.

Guarding and protecting: ḥifẓ and iḥṣān

The passages in al-Muʾminūn, al-Nūr, al-Aḥzāb, and al-Maʿārij principally use the expression “guarding the furūj.”

Mary’s description instead uses aḥṣanat farjahā.

Ḥifẓ concerns guarding and preservation.

Iḥṣān here describes protecting sexual privacy and chastity.

Their meanings are close, but their roots and ranges of application differ.

Different verbs do not establish that Mary’s chastity was wholly unlike other people’s. Her story’s exceptional character arises from the combined passages about her pregnancy and Jesus’s creation, not simply from the verb aḥṣanat.

6. Faraj as relief from distress

In modern Persian, faraj is particularly familiar in expressions meaning “the arrival of relief,” “relief after hardship,” or “awaiting relief.”

Arabic likewise has this established sense, expressed through verbs such as farraja, infaraja, and afraja.

For example, فَرَّجَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ الْكَرْبَ means that The God removed a person’s distress and hardship.

Here physical openness is extended to a person’s circumstances.

Someone in distress seems to have closed paths ahead; the removal of hardship opens a way.

This imagery should not be turned into a definitive historical account of every use of the root.

Also, فَرَج meaning relief from sorrow does not occur in that form in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an expresses relief, the removal of hardship, deliverance, and the ending of sorrow through other words and constructions. For example:

Al-Sharḥ 94:5 and al-Sharḥ 94:6 speak of ease accompanying hardship.

Al-Naml 27:62 speaks of answering the distressed and removing adversity.

Al-Anʿām 6:17 likewise mentions the removal of harm and suffering by The God’s will.

These ideas relate to relief from distress, but are not derivatives of ف ر ج.

A root study should therefore distinguish recognized Arabic meanings from the forms actually used in the Qur’an.

7. Comparison with nearby roots

ف ر ج (فَرَجَ) and ف ج ج (فَجّ)

Both roots concern openness and intervals.

ف ج ج in the Qur’an concerns open routes and passages through the land.

ف ر ج has a wider range: gaps between things, private parts, the sky’s splitting, and relief from hardship.

A mountain passage may involve an opening, but not every gap or interval is a passage.

ف ر ج (فَرَجَ) and ف ج و (فَجْوَة)

ف ج و occurs as fajwa in al-Kahf 18:17, denoting a spacious, open part of the cave.

Farj can likewise denote an interval or opening, with the gap between parts prominent in its general usage.

A fajwa may be a broad open area, whereas a farj may be a narrow cleft or limited interval.

This difference can be observed in usage, but semantic resemblance does not establish a shared derivational history.

ف ر ج (فَرَجَ) and ف ج ر (فَجَرَ)

These roots have closely related letters, but their second and third letters occur in opposite order.

ف ج ر concerns splitting and opening in uses such as dawn and water bursting forth.

For example, فَانْفَجَرَتْ in al-Baqarah 2:60 describes water bursting out of stone.

ف ر ج likewise concerns the sky’s opening in al-Mursalāt 77:9.

The roots can converge in imagery of opening and splitting, while remaining morphologically and Qur’anically distinct.

The ethical sense of fujūr must not be transferred to ف ر ج.

ف ر ج (فَرَجَ) and ف ط ر (فَطَرَ)

فُطُور in al-Mulk 67:3 concerns gaps or ruptures in the sky’s structure.

فُرُوج occurs in a similar setting in Qāf 50:6.

These words can overlap in descriptions of clefts and ruptures.

ف ط ر, however, also has uses concerning initiation and bringing into being.

All meanings of faṭr should not be transferred to farj, nor should the roots be treated as absolute synonyms.

ف ر ج (فَرَجَ) and ع و ر (عَوِرَ)

ʿAwra in Qur’anic language concerns private bodily areas as well as exposure and vulnerability.

عَوْرَاتِ النِّسَاءِ in al-Nūr 24:31 concerns women’s private parts.

إِنَّ بُيُوتَنَا عَوْرَةٌ in al-Aḥzāb 33:13 concerns houses the speakers claimed were exposed and unprotected.

These uses show that ʿawra and farj belong to different roots and semantic fields despite proximity in some subjects.

In bodily usage, farj names the private organ or area; ʿawra can emphasize a private part or a condition of exposure and lack of protection.

All rulings and meanings associated with ʿawra cannot therefore be transferred to farj.

ف ر ج (فَرَجَ) and ح ف ظ (حَفِظَ)

These roots appear together in most of the passages concerning sexual conduct.

Farj is the object being guarded.

Ḥifẓ expresses the act of guarding.

Chastity in “guarding the furūj” arises from their combination and context, not because farj itself means chastity.

The same distinction applies to aḥṣanat farjahā in Mary’s story.

8. Challenges in understanding and translation

One major error is equating every use of farj with the sexual organ.

In Qāf 50:6 and al-Mursalāt 77:9, the root concerns gaps and the opening of the sky. Nothing supports importing a sexual meaning into these passages.

The opposite error removes the sexual meaning from ethical passages by appealing to general openness.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:5, al-Maʿārij 70:29, and the passages in al-Nūr clearly concern sexual privacy and its protection. Turning furūj into “existential gaps,” “psychological vulnerabilities,” or “every human weakness” erases their specific meaning.

Protecting personal privacy may relate to broader aspects of life, but such extensions should not replace the word’s direct meaning.

A third challenge confuses the name of an organ with ethical conduct.

Farj does not itself mean chastity. Chastity is understood through its association with ḥifẓ or iḥṣān and the verses’ context.

A fourth challenge confines guarding the furūj to one sex.

Al-Nūr 24:30 and al-Nūr 24:31 separately address men and women, while al-Aḥzāb 33:35 places their responsibilities together.

Neither the root meaning nor the basic ethical responsibility belongs exclusively to men or women.

A fifth challenge uses the image of the sky opening to construct a definitive scientific theory.

Al-Mursalāt 77:9 states that the sky opens, but does not specify the event’s form, instruments, duration, or physical mechanism.

Likewise, the absence of furūj in Qāf 50:6 does not itself establish that the sky is solid or that the universe contains no empty space.

A sixth challenge transfers the familiar sense of relief from sorrow to every Qur’anic occurrence.

That Arabic meaning is established, but does not occur as a distinct derivative here in the Qur’an. Neither guarding the furūj nor furijat should therefore be translated as awaiting relief or deliverance from trouble.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

An equivalent should be chosen according to the derivative and context.

In physical uses, farj may mean a “gap,” “interval,” or “opening.”

For وَمَا لَهَا مِنْ فُرُوجٍ in Qāf 50:6, an appropriate rendering is:

“And it has no gaps.”

For وَإِذَا السَّمَاءُ فُرِجَتْ in al-Mursalāt 77:9:

“And when the sky is split open.”

In passages on sexual conduct, farj and furūj denote private parts. “Genitals” or “private parts” can convey the anatomical meaning directly.

In “guarding the furūj,” however, a natural translation should also clarify the conduct intended.

For al-Muʾminūn 23:5 and al-Maʿārij 70:29:

“And those who guard their private parts and sexual boundaries.”

Al-Nūr 24:30 and al-Nūr 24:31 should preserve this sense while reflecting the sex of those addressed.

For أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا in al-Anbiyāʾ 21:91 and al-Taḥrīm 66:12, a natural rendering is:

“She guarded her chastity.”

Chastity here conveys the phrase aḥṣanat farjahā as a whole, not the independent meaning of the noun farj.

Word-box explanations can retain the same distinction.

For فُرُوجٍ in Qāf 50:6:

Brief root meaning: a gap or opening between things.

Meaning in this verse: gaps or ruptures in the sky’s structure.

For فُرُوجَهُمْ in al-Nūr 24:30:

Brief root meaning: a gap or opening; in bodily usage, a private organ.

Meaning in this verse: believing men’s private parts and sexual boundaries, which they are commanded to guard.

For فَرْجَهَا in al-Anbiyāʾ 21:91:

Brief root meaning: a gap or opening; in bodily usage, a private organ.

Meaning in this verse: Mary’s sexual privacy, which she protected; an expression of her chastity.

For فُرِجَتْ in al-Mursalāt 77:9:

Brief root meaning: opening and splitting.

Meaning in this verse: the sky was split open; a passive perfect describing a future event.

This method separates the root’s meaning from the derivative’s particular contextual meaning without making the translation cumbersome or unnatural.

10. Conclusion

Early Arabic ف ر ج concerns gaps, intervals, and openings. Poetic evidence and lexical reports record these senses for land, walls, spaces between limbs, and other physical openings.

Farj is also an established name for human and animal sexual organs. Ethical conduct is conveyed by accompanying verbs, especially guarding and protecting.

Another branch is faraj, relief from grief and hardship. Though recognized in Arabic, this should not be presented as a direct Qur’anic use.

The Qur’an uses ف ر ج in two main semantic fields.

Qāf 50:6 describes the absence of gaps in the sky; al-Mursalāt 77:9 describes its opening amid great transformations.

In al-Muʾminūn, al-Nūr, al-Aḥzāb, and al-Maʿārij, furūj concerns private parts and sexual boundaries, whose protection is an ethical responsibility.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:91 and al-Taḥrīm 66:12 express Mary’s chastity through aḥṣanat farjahā.

The central meaning is a gap or opening between things; established uses also name the private organ or relief from distress.

Accurate translation should not reduce every use to one equivalent. The derivative, accompanying verb, grammatical structure, and context determine whether a physical gap, sexual privacy, or relief from hardship is intended.

This distinction preserves the root’s historical meanings and prevents sexual, ethical, natural, or philosophical concepts from being indiscriminately transferred between uses.
