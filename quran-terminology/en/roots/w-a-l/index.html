Waʾl: seeking refuge, turning toward safety, and finding escape

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Wāw–hamzah–lām concerns seeking refuge, turning toward safety, and escaping danger. Waʾala, yaʾilu, waʾlan describes turning to someone or somewhere for protection from danger or difficulty.

Waʾala ilayhi means seeking refuge with someone or something. Mawʾil denotes a place or means to which someone can turn for escape and protection.

Imagine someone caught in a severe desert storm moving toward a cave for protection from wind and sand. The cave can be their mawʾil, the place sought as refuge from danger.

Someone fleeing an enemy may similarly turn to a safe house or fortress to escape pursuit.

These examples connect movement away from danger with reaching a refuge offering safety or possible deliverance.

Seeking refuge and succeeding must nevertheless be distinguished. Someone may seek shelter in a place unable to protect them. The act alone does not guarantee safety.

Early dictionaries record waʾl and mawʾil as refuge. Besides seeking shelter, waʾala can also mean attaining safety or escaping in some uses.

The family therefore extends beyond naming a place to the relationship among a fugitive, danger, and a refuge sought for escape.

A witness from al-Aʿshā

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, contains:

وَقَدْ أُخَالِسُ رَبَّ الْبَيْتِ غَفْلَتَهُ

wa-qad ukhālisu rabba al-bayti ghaflatahu

وَقَدْ يُحَاذِرُ مِنِّي ثُمَّ مَا يَئِلُ

wa-qad yuḥādhiru minnī thumma mā yaʾilu

“I may catch the master of the house off guard; though he watches against me, he still finds no escape.”

Yaʾilu belongs to wāw–hamzah–lām.

The poet says that the other person cannot escape him despite caution and vigilance.

The meaning directly concerns escape and deliverance, rather than necessarily entering a particular building for shelter.

The witness shows that the family is not confined to naming a refuge: it can express escape itself.

The line appears in al-Aʿshā’s well-known poem and early commentaries. Some readings have urāqibu instead of ukhālisu, but yaʾilu and its meaning remain clear.

Al-Aʿshā belongs to the late pre-Islamic period, although this line’s precise composition date is not independently established. It is evidence from the early poetic tradition, not proof of the root’s first appearance or an exact date for a particular use.

Other Arabic evidence

Dictionaries also record lā waʾalat nafsuka: “May your life find no escape,” or “May you not be delivered.”

Here the word denotes attaining safety itself.

Wāʾala ilā al-makān describes hastening and turning toward a place, especially one offering shelter or safety.

Three closely related notions emerge: seeking refuge, turning toward safety, and attaining deliverance.

They are not interchangeable in every derivative. One form may emphasize seeking shelter, another the place, and another the outcome.

A historically defensible definition: In early Arabic, wāw–hamzah–lām concerns turning toward a place or means of escape from danger, and in some uses attaining safety itself.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded forms include:

  • وَأَلَ: sought refuge, turned toward shelter, or escaped.
  • يَئِلُ: seeks refuge or finds escape.
  • وَأْل: seeking refuge; as a noun, refuge.
  • وُؤُول: seeking refuge and turning toward shelter.
  • وَئِيل: a recorded verbal noun of seeking refuge.
  • وَائِل: someone seeking shelter or turning toward safety.
  • وَاءَلَ: sought deliverance or hurried toward refuge.
  • مُوَاءَلَة: hastening and seeking refuge.
  • وِئَال: a verbal noun associated with seeking refuge.
  • مَوْئِل: refuge, a place of escape, or a means of deliverance.
  • مَوْأَلَة: a refuge or sheltering place.
  • اِسْتَوْأَلَتْ: gathered together, of camels.

Arabic records these forms, but the Quran uses only mawʾilan.

Semantic core

Three features stand out in refuge-related uses.

First, there is a situation from which someone seeks release, such as danger, pursuit, or difficulty.

Second, the person turns toward someone, somewhere, or some means offering protection and possible safety.

Third, there is deliverance or hope of deliverance.

Someone escaping a flood may move toward higher ground. Withdrawal from the flood and approaching the elevation are two aspects of one action.

The root alone does not identify the danger, quality of shelter, or eventual success in every use.

Was gathering the original meaning?

Some early lexical analyses explain the family through gathering and seeking refuge.

Istawʾalat al-ibil describes camels gathering together.

Mawʾil is also recorded for a place where floodwater collects or settles.

A connection with shelter can be imagined, since creatures may gather in a place for safety.

That resemblance alone does not prove that gathering was historically first and refuge developed later.

Establishing such an order requires independent evidence from different periods.

The clear, attested senses of refuge and escape should therefore guide the Quranic definition.

The structure of mawʾil

Mawʾil follows the pattern mafʿil.

The pattern can form a place noun: a place where the root’s action occurs.

Mawʾil can accordingly be a sheltering place or somewhere sought for escape.

Grammatical accounts also allow a verbal-noun analysis, referring to seeking refuge or escape itself.

The mafʿil pattern alone therefore does not prove a bounded physical place in every sentence.

Quranic usage clearly concerns refuge or escape, but the word alone does not specify a particular place, person, protective means, or opportunity for deliverance.

3. Quranic usage: Al-Kahf 18:58

The sole Quranic occurrence is in Al-Kahf.

Al-Kahf 18:58

وَرَبُّكَ الْغَفُورُ ذُو الرَّحْمَةِ ۖ لَوْ يُؤَاخِذُهُمْ بِمَا كَسَبُوا لَعَجَّلَ لَهُمُ الْعَذَابَ ۚ بَلْ لَهُمْ مَوْعِدٌ لَنْ يَجِدُوا مِنْ دُونِهِ مَوْئِلًا

wa-rabbuka al-ghafūru dhū al-raḥmati law yuʾākhidhuhum bi-mā kasabū la-ʿajjala lahumu al-ʿadhāba bal lahum mawʿidun lan yajidū min dūnihi mawʾilā

Al-Kahf 18:58

“Your Lord is forgiving, possessed of mercy. Were He to take them to account for what they have earned, He would hasten punishment for them. Rather, they have an appointed time from which they will find no refuge.”

The verse follows discussion of people reminded of their Lord’s signs who turn away.

Al-Kahf 18:55 describes those reached by guidance who await their predecessors’ fate or confrontation with punishment.

Al-Kahf 18:56 speaks of arguing through falsehood to overthrow truth and mocking divine signs and warnings.

Al-Kahf 18:57 then describes those turning away after reminders of their Lord’s signs and forgetting their previous deeds.

Against this background, Al-Kahf 18:58 states that God does not punish them immediately despite their conduct.

Forgiveness and mercy at the opening

The verse begins by describing the Lord as al-ghafūru dhū al-raḥmah.

Forgiveness and mercy precede mention of the appointed punishment and inability to find refuge.

A conditional sentence follows:

“لَوْ يُؤَاخِذُهُمْ بِمَا كَسَبُوا لَعَجَّلَ لَهُمُ الْعَذَابَ”

If they were taken to account and punished immediately for their deeds, punishment would arrive sooner.

Instead, the Quran speaks of an appointment whose arrival has been deferred.

Two matters differ: punishment being delayed and punishment being cancelled.

The delay relates to divine mercy here, without implying that people can evade their deeds’ consequences indefinitely.

Bal lahum mawʿidun: an appointed time

Mawʿid belongs to wāw–ʿayn–dāl and concerns a promise or its appointed time or place.

Here it follows mention of punishment.

The Quran then denies their finding a refuge to escape that appointment.

Mawʾil thus stands in relation to an appointment that eventually arrives.

For illustration, someone required to answer for their actions at a set time might hope to find an escape before it comes.

The Quranic sentence states that, when the appointment arrives, they will find no way out of it.

The example clarifies appointment and escape; mawʾil does not itself mean a court or evading trial.

Lan yajidū: they will not find

Lan negates a future event here.

Yajidū belongs to wāw–jīm–dāl and means finding.

The verse does not report that they are now seeking refuge or previously reached it; it says they will not find refuge or escape.

This distinction matters.

Mawʾilan denotes possible refuge or deliverance, while lan yajidū denies access to that possibility in context.

The inability to find shelter comes from the construction, not from wāw–hamzah–lām itself meaning helplessness.

The pronoun in min dūnihi

The pronoun -hi is masculine singular.

Two possible antecedents can be considered: the Lord mentioned at the beginning, and mawʿid, the nearest preceding suitable noun.

This study favors mawʿid in view of the sentence’s continuity.

On that reading, they will find no escape or refuge from the appointed event.

The next verse supports this reading.

Al-Kahf 18:59 mentions communities destroyed because of wrongdoing, with an appointment set for their destruction.

The repeated mawʿid clarifies the connection between delayed punishment and its appointed arrival.

If -hi refers to God, the sentence would concern finding no refuge apart from Him. Other verses bear on that theological idea, but the nearby mawʿid and Al-Kahf 18:59 strengthen the first reading here.

Mawʾil thus denotes a refuge or escape effective against the appointment, and the Quran denies that they will find one.

Must mawʾil be a physical place here?

No. It can name a sheltering place, but the sentence extends beyond one particular location.

People may seek a cave against natural danger, a powerful person’s protection against an enemy, or another means of escape from difficulty.

All can exemplify refuge or deliverance.

Al-Kahf 18:58, however, concerns an appointment within God’s sovereignty and judgment.

Mawʾil should therefore not be restricted to a cave, house, fortress, or other specified physical place.

Here refuge means a way or support for escaping the appointed consequence, whether sought in a place or another’s power and protection.

4. Comparison with related roots

Wāw–hamzah–lām and lām–jīm–hamzah: mawʾil and maljaʾ

These roots are semantically very close.

Lajaʾa concerns turning to someone or somewhere for protection.

Maljaʾ names a place or means of shelter.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 describes people who would rush toward a refuge, caves, or an entrance if they found one.

Maljaʾ appears alongside maghārāt and muddakhal.

Mawʾil likewise denotes refuge or escape in Al-Kahf 18:58.

The words clearly overlap and can sometimes share a Persian equivalent.

Their roots and structures nevertheless differ.

Wāw–hamzah–lām also includes attaining safety and release. Lām–jīm–hamzah prominently concerns seeking shelter and relying on a place or party for protection.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule making maljaʾ only a place and mawʾil only successful deliverance.

Wāw–hamzah–lām and hamzah–wāw–yāʾ: mawʾil and maʾwā

Awā belongs to hamzah–wāw–yāʾ and describes turning to a place, sheltering, and settling in an abode in the Quran.

Al-Kahf 18:10 describes the young men taking refuge in the cave.

Awā expresses their sheltering there.

Maʾwā can name a resting place, abode, or refuge.

Mawʾil instead belongs to wāw–hamzah–lām, conveying refuge or escape in its Quranic use.

Both families meet in sheltering, while settling and remaining somewhere also matter in awā and maʾwā.

Arabic evidence additionally records deliverance and escape for waʾala.

This does not make every maʾwā permanent and every mawʾil temporary.

For a fuller discussion of the former family, see Forqan’s study of hamzah–wāw–yāʾ.

Wāw–hamzah–lām and fāʾ–rāʾ–rāʾ: refuge and flight

Farra concerns fleeing and withdrawing in the Quran.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:50 commands flight toward God.

Flight is directed toward a particular destination.

Waʾala can likewise concern movement toward escape, while waʾala ilayhi foregrounds turning toward shelter.

Imagine someone fleeing an enemy and then sheltering in a fortress.

Moving away from the enemy expresses flight.

Approaching the fortress for safety emphasizes refuge.

One act can contain both features; the roots are therefore not wholly separate or opposed.

Wāw–hamzah–lām and hā–rāʾ–bāʾ: an escape route and fleeing

Hā–rāʾ–bāʾ also concerns fleeing and attempting escape.

In Al-Jinn 72:12, jinn acknowledge that flight cannot render God powerless.

Haraban names the act of fleeing.

Mawʾilan in Al-Kahf 18:58 names something offering refuge or escape.

One Quranic use focuses on the act, the other on shelter or possible deliverance.

This does not establish that wāw–hamzah–lām never expresses escape itself, since Arabic evidence records that verbal sense too.

Wāw–hamzah–lām and lām–ḥāʾ–dāl: mawʾil and multaḥad

Al-Kahf 18:27 uses multaḥadan.

From lām–ḥāʾ–dāl, multaḥad can mean refuge or a place turned toward for escape in context.

The broader family concerns turning aside, inclining, and moving toward a side.

Multaḥad and mawʾil may both become “refuge” in some contexts without sharing identical roots or semantic development.

The resemblance also does not prove that mawʾil is necessarily direct shelter and multaḥad hidden or underground shelter.

Such fine distinctions require support from actual usage and context.

Wāw–hamzah–lām and hamzah–wāw–lām: mawʾil and maʾāl

Mawʾil and maʾāl may seem close in sound and in ideas of return or refuge.

Some lexical accounts also explain mawʾil as a place of return.

Maʾāl, however, is generally associated with return, outcome, and what something ultimately reaches.

It belongs to hamzah–wāw–lām.

Mawʾil belongs to wāw–hamzah–lām.

Early analyses differ over connections between these forms, but shared return imagery or similar sound alone does not prove one historical root.

Forqan’s indexing should examine the two independently.

For the second family, see Forqan’s study of hamzah–wāw–lām.

5. Interpretive and translation challenges

Does mawʾil guarantee deliverance?

No. It can denote a refuge or way of escape without itself guaranteeing that the seeker succeeds.

A person might shelter in a house that cannot withstand the danger.

Al-Kahf 18:58 does not report successful refuge at all: it says they will find none.

The negation therefore comes from lan yajidū, not mawʾil itself.

Is mawʾil always a place?

It can be a place noun, but its use is not confined to a physical location.

Refuge can be somewhere entered or a party whose protection is sought.

The word may also concern a route of escape or possibility of deliverance.

Because Al-Kahf 18:58 concerns appointed punishment, refuge must be understood in relation to escape from that appointment.

Does the absence of refuge mean no divine mercy?

No. The verse begins by recalling the Lord’s forgiveness and mercy.

Immediate reckoning would hasten punishment, but an appointment is set instead.

Mercy and respite appear first; inability to find refuge against the appointment comes at the end.

These parts should not be confused.

Delayed consequences do not make responsibility irrelevant. Nor does this appointment authorize judgments about every wrongdoer’s fate or repentance without considering other Quranic passages.

Is mawʾil used only for escaping punishment?

No. The Arabic family more broadly concerns refuge from varied dangers.

Its sole Quranic occurrence happens to concern appointed punishment.

That particular subject should not become the whole root’s lexical definition.

The word denotes refuge or escape; context establishes what someone seeks to escape.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents include:

وَأَلَ: sought refuge or escape; in some uses, escaped.

يَئِلُ: seeks refuge; attains safety.

وَأْل: seeking refuge; as a noun, refuge.

وُؤُول: seeking refuge; turning toward safety.

وَائِل: one seeking refuge or safety.

وَاءَلَ: sought deliverance; hurried toward shelter.

مُوَاءَلَة: seeking refuge or hastening toward safety.

مَوْئِل: refuge; a place or means of escape.

مَوْأَلَة: refuge; a sheltering place.

For Quranic mawʾilan, “a refuge” is a suitable base equivalent.

A proposed rendering of Al-Kahf 18:58 is:

“Your Lord is forgiving, possessed of mercy. Were He to take them to account for what they have earned, He would hasten punishment for them. Rather, they have an appointed time from which they will find no refuge.”

This preserves the word’s central sense while avoiding the suggestion that the appointment itself is a refuge.

“A way of escape” also fits the family’s Arabic range and can clarify refuge in reflection on the verse.

“A dwelling,” “a place of return,” or “a gathering place” are not precise equivalents here without sufficient contextual support.

Simply replacing mawʾil with “being saved” is also unsuitable in this construction: refuge or possible deliverance is its object, while lan yajidū denies access to it.

7. Conclusion

Early Arabic wāw–hamzah–lām concerns seeking refuge, turning toward safety, and finding escape. Some uses express deliverance itself.

Poetic evidence, especially yaʾilu in the line attributed to al-Aʿshā, illustrates escape. Dictionaries also record waʾl and mawʾil for refuge.

Other branches include camels gathering and a place where floodwater settles, but their historical relationship to one original meaning is not certain.

The Quran uses only mawʾilan.

In Al-Kahf 18:58, it relates to an appointment set for the consequences of a group’s turning away.

The verse begins with divine forgiveness and mercy, then makes clear that delayed punishment does not mean finding escape from the appointment.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its principal branch, wāw–hamzah–lām concerns seeking refuge and turning toward a place or means of escape from danger and difficulty. Some uses express attaining safety itself.

Mawʾil names refuge or a way of escape, something sought for protection and deliverance.

Quranic mawʾilan means “a refuge.” Lan yajidū min dūnihi mawʾilan denies finding shelter or escape from the appointed event.
