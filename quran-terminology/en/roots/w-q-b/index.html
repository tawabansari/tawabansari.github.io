Qur’anic Root W Q B

Wuqūb: entering, sinking in, and disappearing; from hollows in rock and sunset to night’s darkness arriving

Qur’anic forms

وَقَبَ
(waqaba)
Form I past tense; third-person masculine singular; once, in Al-Falaq 113:3

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root W Q B concerns entering, sinking in, disappearing into a place, and arriving. وَقَبَ describes something entering a hollow or location. In other uses, it also describes the sun setting, the moon becoming dark, and night’s darkness arriving.

The related noun وَقْب denotes a hollow, cavity, or depression in which something rests. Such a hollow may occur in rock or the ground, or in a part of the body, such as an eye socket.

The root W Q B occurs only once in the Qur’an:

وَقَبَ: a third-person masculine singular Form I past-tense verb, in Al-Falaq 113:3. It means “entered,” “arrived,” or “enveloped,” depending on the referent to which the verb is applied.

The following list presents this family’s lexical forms for comparison. Of these forms, only the past-tense وَقَبَ occurs in the Qur’an. The listed imperfect, verbal nouns, nouns, adjectives, and Form IV do not occur there:

  • وَقَبَ، يَقِبُ: entered, sank in, or settled within something.
  • وَقْب: a hollow, cavity, or depression; also a verbal noun of waqaba.
  • وُقُوب: entering, sinking in, and, in some uses, disappearing or setting.
  • وَقْبَة: a hollow or cavity, including a depression in rock or an opening in a wall.
  • وُقُوب and وِقَاب: plural forms denoting hollows and cavities.
  • وَاقِب: entering or sinking in; in a use concerning darkness, darkness arriving.
  • أَوْقَبَ: put something into a hollow or location.
  • إِيقَاب: putting something into a hollow or concealing it.
  • وَقْبَاء: in رَكِيَّةٌ وَقْبَاءُ, a description of a well whose water lies deep below.

Lexical sources also record other, more restricted uses, including وَقْب for a foolish or base person. Its relationship to the root’s central sense is disputed. Ibn Fāris treats it as ibdāl from وَغْب: substituting qāf for ghayn, rather than rearranging the letters. A report from Thaʿlab instead proposes a connection with “entering into baseness,” an etymological connection judged remote in Lisān al-ʿArab.

Consequently, not every form listed in a dictionary should be treated without historical investigation as a certain derivative of one original meaning.

In this root’s Qur’anic use, the central issue is entering or arriving. What enters or arrives is identified by ghāsiq in the same verse.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Waqb: a hollow or depression

One clear use of this family is وَقْب, meaning a hollow or depression.

Lexical sources use وَقْبُ الصَّخْرَةِ for a hollow in rock in which rainwater may collect.

This depression can be small or deeper, like a cavity in solid rock where water remains.

The related وَقْبَة is also attested for an opening or hollow containing shade.

These uses show that waqb did not concern only night and celestial phenomena. It also named hollows and recessed spaces observable in everyday life.

A hollow or depression, however, should not be equated with the act of entering.

وَقْب can name a location, whereas وَقَبَ expresses entering or sinking in.

The eye’s waqb and other bodily hollows

Early Arabic records وَقْبُ الْعَيْنِ for the eye socket.

وَقَبَتْ عَيْنَاهُ also describes someone whose eyes have sunk into their sockets.

Here the root concerns becoming recessed and retreating from the visible surface.

Similar uses concern certain depressions around the shoulder and elsewhere in the body.

These witnesses help explain the root’s semantic field: they concern neither night’s darkness nor sunset, but bodily form and the location of something within a hollow.

This does not mean that darkness in Al-Falaq must sink into a cavity like an eye. The verse has a different referent, and the verb’s meaning must be assessed in relation to its subject.

Hollows in everyday tools

وَقْب also denotes holes or recesses in certain tools.

For example, lexical explanations use it for the hole in a wheel into which the axle fits.

Related forms also describe the hollow of an oil vessel or a depression in food inside a dish.

Waqb is therefore not confined to natural cavities. A hollow may also result from making or using a tool.

Arabic shows a clear relationship between the names of these hollows and the verb expressing putting something into them.

That relationship does not mean that every kind of piercing, splitting, or making a cavity is expressed by waqaba. The name of a hollow is distinct from a verb denoting its creation.

Waqaba: entering and sinking in

Arabic records وَقَبَ الشَّيْءُ for something entering.

وَقَبَ فِي الْوَقْبِ likewise expresses entering a hollow or recessed location.

By contrast, أَوْقَبَ الشَّيْءَ can mean putting something into that location.

This morphological distinction matters.

With وَقَبَ, the subject itself enters or sinks in.

With أَوْقَبَ, the subject puts something in.

However, this root’s Form IV does not occur in the Qur’an. “Someone forcibly put something in” must therefore not replace the Form I verb in Al-Falaq 113:3. Form IV itself also does not inherently express force or compulsion.

Is the root’s original core a hollow or entering?

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris relates this family to something disappearing into a place of concealment. He describes waqbah as a depression into which something enters.

This explanation establishes an intelligible relationship between the name of a hollow and the verb expressing entering.

It does not establish with certainty whether the noun denoting a hollow arose first and generated the verb, or the reverse.

That judgment requires further historical and comparative evidence.

For Qur’anic study, the directly relevant evidence is the verb’s attested meaning: entering, sinking in, and, in suitable uses, arriving or disappearing.

The status of early evidence

Uses concerning hollows in rock, eye sockets, holes in tools, and entering are recorded in the early Arabic lexical tradition. Some of this material appears in Kitāb al-ʿAyn and subsequently in other sources.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn and later lexical transmissions quote a poetic fragment comparing a waqb to the hollow of an oil vessel:

فِي وَقْبِ خَوْصَاءَ كَوَقْبِ الْمُدْهُنِ

“In the hollow of a khawṣāʾ [something deep-set or deep], like the hollow of an oil vessel.”

The examined transmission does not clearly identify the poet or the fragment’s precise date, so it cannot be treated as certain pre-Islamic evidence. Arabic uses khawṣāʾ both of a sunken eye and of a deep well; without the full context, the description’s referent in this fragment should not be considered certain.

Hollow and depression are therefore meanings supported by reliable lexical accounts. An invented verse or a witness of uncertain attribution, however, must not be offered as certain pre-Qur’anic documentation.

3. Sunset and the moon disappearing

Applying waqaba to the sun and moon is another important use of the root.

وَقَبَتِ الشَّمْسُ: the sun setting

Arabic uses وَقَبَتِ الشَّمْسُ to mean that the sun set.

At sunset, the sun descends from the terrestrial observer’s field of view and disappears beyond the horizon.

This use shows a relationship between sinking and disappearing.

“The sun entered its place,” however, must not be read as an Arabic assertion that a literal cavity exists in the earth or sky into which the sun enters each night.

When Lisān al-ʿArab quotes an explanation describing sunset as the sun entering its place, Ibn Manẓūr explicitly calls the wording figurative. A specific physical location into which the sun enters should not be inferred from it.

This caution also matters for understanding Qur’anic language.

Words such as rising, setting, and wuqūb, when used of the sun, describe events visible to an observer. These words alone do not establish the physical structure of the universe.

Nor does W Q B always mean sunset. Setting is an instance of disappearing when the verb concerns the sun.

وَقَبَ الْقَمَرُ: the moon entering darkness

Lexical sources record وَقَبَ الْقَمَرُ for the moon entering shadow and undergoing an eclipse.

Here wuqūb concerns the moon being concealed or its light diminishing.

This resembles sunset in that both involve a celestial body passing from a visible, luminous condition to one in which its light or observable presence diminishes.

Setting and a lunar eclipse are nevertheless different events.

When a celestial body sets, it disappears relative to the observer’s horizon. During a lunar eclipse, the moon loses some of its illumination because it enters Earth’s shadow.

The root W Q B therefore should not be defined as lunar eclipse specifically.

Nor does the Arabic attestation of waqaba al-qamar alone establish that ghāsiq in Al-Falaq 113:3 must be an eclipsed moon. Identifying the verse’s referent requires examining ghāsiq itself and the surah’s context.

وَقَبَ الظَّلَامُ: darkness arriving

Lexical sources use وَقَبَ الظَّلَامُ for darkness arriving and spreading.

In this construction, darkness is presented as something coming upon people and their surroundings.

This use clearly relates to entering, but need not describe darkness entering a physical cavity.

As daylight ends, darkness spreads through the surroundings, making previously bright and visible things less apparent.

Waqaba al-ẓalām can therefore be translated as “darkness arrived” or “darkness enveloped everything.”

Enveloping here is a natural consequence of darkness arriving. Evil, fear, or harm, however, should not be extracted from waqaba itself.

4. وَمِنْ شَرِّ غَاسِقٍ إِذَا وَقَبَ: the root’s Qur’anic use

Al-Falaq 113:3

وَمِنْ شَرِّ غَاسِقٍ إِذَا وَقَبَ

“And from the harm of approaching darkness when it envelops.”

This is the only Qur’anic verse containing the verb وَقَبَ.

To understand it precisely, three parts of the sentence must be distinguished: sharr, ghāsiq, and waqaba.

Each word belongs to a different root and contributes its own role.

The syntax of وَقَبَ

وَقَبَ is a third-person masculine singular past-tense verb.

Its grammatical subject is an implicit pronoun referring back to ghāsiq.

إِذَا is a temporal adverb specifying the occurrence of wuqūb in this construction.

The sentence thus concerns harm associated with ghāsiq at the time of its wuqūb.

The past tense after idhā does not mean that the event must precede the moment of speaking. In Arabic, this construction can describe a condition or event to which the sentence applies whenever it occurs.

إِذَا وَقَبَ can therefore be understood as “when it arrives” or “whenever it envelops.”

What does ghāsiq identify?

غَاسِق belongs to the root GH S Q.

Arabic associates ghasaq with night becoming dark. In its familiar use, ghāsiq refers to dark night or something accompanied by darkness.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 also contains غَسَقِ اللَّيْلِ: night becoming dark, or its darkness arriving.

The interpretation of ghāsiq as dark night in Al-Falaq therefore has linguistic and internal Qur’anic support.

Darkness does not come from W Q B itself, however, but from ghāsiq and its context.

Waqaba expresses the condition in which ghāsiq is found, or the event that happens to it.

In this combination, ghāsiq therefore concerns darkness, while waqaba concerns arriving and enveloping.

Simply translating waqaba too as “became dark” obscures the distinction between these linguistic families.

The precise meaning of إِذَا وَقَبَ

Arabic usage supports several possible translations of this expression:

“When it arrived,” “when it entered,” and “when it enveloped.”

In a use concerning night’s darkness, “arriving” and “enveloping” produce more natural English.

The literal “when it entered” also shows the verb’s meaning, but without explanation may prompt the question of precisely what the darkness has entered.

Arabic وَقَبَ الظَّلَامُ can describe darkness coming upon people without naming a specific cavity or container.

“When it envelops” can therefore express the contextual meaning more suitably.

This does not mean that waqaba always means only enveloping. Entering is more prominent in uses concerning a hollow, and disappearing from view in uses concerning the sun.

Does waqaba mean attacking?

No. The root’s principal witnesses show entering, sinking in, disappearing, and arriving.

Waqaba itself does not mean attacking, causing harm, or destroying.

Al-Falaq 113:3 speaks of شَرِّ غَاسِقٍ. Evil or harm therefore comes from sharr and the context of seeking refuge.

Waqaba expresses the event through which the condition being described comes about.

Dangers may accompany night’s darkness, but those dangers should not be incorporated into the verb’s lexical meaning.

Reduced visibility, the possibility of harmful people remaining concealed, and difficulty detecting certain dangers are intelligible features of darkness. Waqaba itself, however, names none of these particular dangers.

Is darkness inherently evil?

The verse speaks of the harm of ghāsiq.

This is not equivalent to saying that all darkness is evil.

Night’s darkness is part of life’s natural order. Yūnus 10:67 presents night as a time for rest, and Al-Nabaʾ 78:10 describes it as a covering.

The verse therefore does not establish that night is inherently morally evil.

In Al-Falaq, a person seeks refuge in The God from harm that may be encountered when darkness arrives.

Nor should the expression be restricted to physical dangers at night. The verse does not specify every possible kind of injury.

The expression’s scope should be preserved as it stands in the Qur’an.

5. Must ghāsiq in this verse mean night?

Early lexical and exegetical explanations propose several referents for ghāsiq, most prominently dark night and the moon.

Since the present study concerns W Q B, the meaning of waqaba must be clarified for each interpretation.

The dark-night interpretation

On this interpretation, ghāsiq is night, and waqaba expresses its arrival and enveloping.

This meaning clearly fits Arabic waqaba al-ẓalām and ghasaq al-layl in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78.

Al-Falaq also begins by mentioning the Lord of daybreak, then seeks refuge from the harm of ghāsiq at the time of its wuqūb.

This connection between daybreak and night’s darkness supports the interpretation involving night.

It does not mean that falaq lexically means salvation or that waqaba means destruction. F L Q and W Q B are separate linguistic families.

The lunar interpretation

Some early explanations also identify ghāsiq with the moon.

This interpretation relates to Arabic waqaba al-qamar, a use that can concern the moon entering shadow and its light diminishing.

Idhā waqaba would then concern the moon disappearing or becoming dark.

The verse itself, however, does not name the moon.

Nor does waqaba alone establish that an eclipse must be intended. Setting, disappearing, and loss of light can arise in different uses of this verb.

The lunar interpretation is therefore a recognized possibility in explanations of the verse, but has no conclusive priority over the direct, familiar meaning of dark night.

More restricted interpretations

Some early accounts identify ghāsiq with a particular star, darkness accompanying an event, or other referents.

W Q B itself does not identify those referents.

This root concerns entering, sinking in, and disappearing. A star’s name, the kind of being involved, an exact time, or a mode of action cannot be derived from the verb’s independent meaning.

If an external account proposes a disease, magic, or a particular event, it should not enter the basic translation without Qur’anic evidence.

Taken together, the evidence favors “approaching darkness when it envelops” for translating the verse, while retaining the lunar interpretation as a possibility in the research discussion.

6. Waqaba in relation to the verses of Al-Falaq

Understanding waqaba’s role requires attention to the construction of refuge-seeking in Al-Falaq.

Al-Falaq 113:1

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ الْفَلَقِ

“Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of daybreak.”

Al-Falaq 113:2

مِنْ شَرِّ مَا خَلَقَ

“From the harm of what He has created.”

Al-Falaq 113:3

وَمِنْ شَرِّ غَاسِقٍ إِذَا وَقَبَ

“And from the harm of approaching darkness when it envelops.”

The first verse expresses seeking refuge in the Lord.

The second extends refuge-seeking to the harm of what has been created.

The third concerns the harm of ghāsiq at the time of its wuqūb.

This sequence shows that waqaba in the third verse contributes to describing the time or condition in which danger arises.

The verse does not merely describe darkness arriving: within the surah’s complete construction, this expression identifies something from whose harm a person seeks refuge in The God.

The following two verses concern the harm of those who blow into knots, Al-Falaq 113:4, and the harm of an envier when he envies, Al-Falaq 113:5.

This grouping shows an important pattern: in some verses, the Qur’an connects harm with a particular agent, and in some instances with the time or condition of that agent’s activity.

In ghāsiq idhā waqaba, the qualification idhā waqaba matters.

In ḥāsid idhā ḥasada, the verb ḥasada likewise clarifies the person’s condition or conduct.

This structural resemblance does not make waqaba and ḥasada identical in meaning.

Envy is conduct belonging to the family Ḥ S D, while waqaba is an event involving arriving or entering, from W Q B.

What they share is the role of the idhā construction in specifying the situation or time intended by the verse.

7. Comparison with related roots

W Q B (وُقُوب) and D KH L (دُخُول): entering and entry

These families are very close semantically.

In general usage, dakhala denotes something entering somewhere.

Waqaba is likewise attested in Arabic for entering and sinking in.

However, hollows and depressions are also established uses within W Q B. Waqaba also describes sunset and darkness arriving.

Consequently, not every instance of dukhūl should be called wuqūb, nor should all uses of waqaba be restricted to entering a building or room.

In Al-Falaq 113:3, waqaba combined with ghāsiq concerns darkness arriving and enveloping.

W Q B (وُقُوب) and W L J (وُلُوج): sinking in and entering

The Qur’an uses W L J for something entering or penetrating something else.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:27, it describes causing night to enter day and day to enter night through the verb tūliju.

In Al-Falaq 113:3, waqaba can likewise concern night’s darkness arriving.

There is nevertheless an important distinction.

In tūliju al-layla fī al-nahār, the verb is transitive and expresses causing one thing to enter another.

In idhā waqaba, the verb is Form I and intransitive, and ghāsiq itself is the subject of the event.

The constructions therefore should not be treated as identical in their subject and object roles.

Nor does the shared imagery of night entering make waqaba synonymous with wulūj in every use.

W Q B (وُقُوب) and GH S Q (غَسَق): arriving and becoming dark

These roots occur together in Al-Falaq 113:3.

Ghasaq concerns night becoming dark; ghāsiq in this use refers to darkness or dark night.

Waqaba concerns arriving and entering.

In ghāsiq idhā waqaba, ghāsiq therefore identifies the subject associated with darkness, while waqaba expresses its arriving or enveloping.

This distinction matters for translation.

If both words are rendered simply as becoming dark, each root’s separate contribution disappears.

Conversely, preserving the distinction should not produce a cumbersome, unintelligible translation.

“Approaching darkness when it envelops” expresses the phrase as a whole in natural English.

W Q B (وُقُوب) and GH R B (غُرُوب): the sun disappearing

Ghurūb concerns the sun descending beyond the horizon and disappearing from the observer’s view.

Arabic also records waqabat al-shams for the sun setting.

In this use, the two families are very close.

However, GH R B also concerns the direction of the west and other uses, whereas W Q B concerns hollows, depressions, entering, and darkness.

Not every instance of wuqūb can therefore be called ghurūb.

The verse in Al-Falaq does not directly name the sun, so waqaba’s meaning should not be restricted through its solar use alone.

W Q B (وُقُوب) and GH Y B (غَيْبَة): sinking in and disappearing

Maqāyīs al-Lughah emphasizes the connection between waqaba and something disappearing into a location.

Arabic GH Y B also concerns becoming unseen and being beyond sight or awareness.

Being concealed, however, does not necessarily result from entering a hollow.

Something may be unseen by a person from the outset, without previously having been visible and subsequently entering a place.

Conversely, something may enter a hollow while part of it remains visible.

These families therefore overlap, but their full ranges of use are not identical.

W Q B (وَقْب) and TH Q B (ثَقْب): hollows and holes

In physical uses, TH Q B concerns holes and piercing.

Waqb can likewise denote a hollow or depression.

Not every depression, however, is a hole opening from one side of an object through to the other.

For example, a hollow in rock where rainwater collects may simply be a closed depression.

An axle hole in a tool, by contrast, may allow another part to pass through it or fit within it.

Waqb and thaqb are consequently close in certain referents, but should not always receive identical translations.

Moreover, the hollow in W Q B names a structure, whereas waqaba in Al-Falaq is a verb expressing the event of arriving.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

In general usage, the basic equivalent of وَقَبَ is “entered” or “sank in.”

In the context of night’s darkness, however, “arrived” and “enveloped” are more natural.

The root’s explanation should preserve this distinction.

Al-Falaq 113:3

Arabic text:

وَمِنْ شَرِّ غَاسِقٍ إِذَا وَقَبَ

Suggested translation:

“And from the harm of approaching darkness when it envelops.”

This translation preserves the sentence’s three elements: sharr, ghāsiq, and waqaba.

Another translation is possible:

“And from the harm of dark night when it arrives.”

This rendering makes the familiar interpretation of ghāsiq as night more explicit.

“And from the harm of darkness when it becomes dark” is less suitable, because it shows the distinction between darkness and its arrival less clearly.

Nor should waqaba alone be translated as “brought evil,” “attacked,” “performed magic,” or “caused harm.”

A brief meaning for the word-by-word box

وَقَبَ: entered, arrived; in this verse, darkness enveloping.

If a shorter explanation is needed:

“وَقَبَ: arrived and enveloped.”

A detailed root study can add that Arabic also records this verb for entering a hollow, the sun setting, and the moon disappearing.

Caution in translation

Waqaba’s lexical meaning must not be equated with everything expressed by the verse’s context.

Darkness comes from ghāsiq.

Harm comes from sharr.

Idhā specifies the event’s time or condition.

Waqaba itself expresses arriving or entering.

These distinctions help keep translation precise and prevent one word’s meaning from being blended with the meanings of its companions.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root W Q B concerns entering, sinking in, disappearing, and arriving.

The related waqb also names hollows and depressions, such as a hollow in rock where water collects, an eye socket, and holes in certain tools.

As a verb, waqaba can express something entering a location. Arabic also uses this verb for sunset, the moon disappearing, and darkness arriving.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: Al-Falaq 113:3.

In that verse, waqaba refers back to ghāsiq and expresses its arriving or enveloping.

Interpreting ghāsiq as dark night clearly fits ghasaq al-layl in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 and the attested Arabic expression waqaba al-ẓalām.

Early sources also propose the moon, but waqaba itself does not conclusively identify that referent.

Nor should waqaba be equated with evil or harm. In Al-Falaq, harm comes from its own independent word and the context of seeking refuge in The God.

Lexically, entering or disappearing is not necessarily good or bad. The sun can set, the moon disappear from view, and night’s darkness arrive without waqaba itself expressing a particular moral value.

For Forqan, the basic definition of this family is: wuqūb is something entering, sinking into, or disappearing into a location. In uses concerning the sun, moon, and darkness, it can express setting, disappearing, arriving, or enveloping. Waqb also names a hollow or depression in which something rests.

This definition covers established Arabic meanings and the root’s sole Qur’anic use without turning entry and disappearance into moral evil, attack, magic, or a particular physical event.
