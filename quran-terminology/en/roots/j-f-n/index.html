1. Introducing the root

The Arabic triliteral root J F N has several familiar physical applications. Jafn denotes an eyelid or sword sheath; jafnah names a large vessel containing food. The family also appears in names for grapevines and other vegetation.

These applications suggest a shared feature: surrounding something, containing it, or providing a place to hold it. An eyelid protects an eye, a sheath holds a blade, and a jafnah collects food.

Ibn Fāris identifies this feature as the semantic core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْجِيمُ وَالْفَاءُ وَالنُّونُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ شَيْءٌ يُطِيفُ بِشَيْءٍ وَيَحْوِيهِ.

al-jīmu wa-al-fāʾu wa-al-nūnu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa shayʾun yuṭīfu bi-shayʾin wa-yaḥwīhi

Translation: “Jīm, fāʾ, and nūn have one basic meaning: something surrounding and containing something else.”

Maqāyīs: surrounding and containing

This account fits three major applications, but does not make every enclosing object a jafn. A proposed lexical core clarifies relationships within a family without replacing each word’s specific definition. Brief root meaning: Covering and containing; jafnah is a large food bowl.

The Qur’an uses this root only once, as jifānin in Sabaʾ 34:13.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole occurrence at 34:13

Jifān is the plural of jafnah, meaning large food bowls. The verse compares them with large basins to convey their remarkable size and capacity.

Besides everyday use, jafnah appears in early Arabic poetry of praise and pride. Large bowls filled with food portray a person’s or family’s generosity, hospitality, and capacity to receive guests.

A vessel’s size and the praise of generosity are nevertheless distinct meanings. Jafnah primarily names a vessel; generosity is inferred from the food, its use, and the context.

2. Forms and derivatives

The principal Arabic forms include:

Jafn: An eyelid, covering the eye from above and below. The form also occurs for a sword sheath and certain plants.

Ajfān, ajfun, and jufūn: Plurals of jafn in applications such as eyelids and sheaths.

Jafnah: A large, broad vessel for food. Some lexical applications also concern a grapevine.

Jifān: The familiar plural of jafnah, meaning large food bowls. This is the root’s Qur’anic form.

Jifan: Another plural of jafnah recorded in morphological and lexical sources.

Jafanāt: A further plural, particularly familiar from Arabic poetic witnesses.

Jafana: A Form I verb that, in one application, concerns preparing an animal’s meat and putting it in food bowls.

Jaffana and tajaffana: In grapevine applications, forms concerning the development or establishment of a stock or trunk. Dictionaries record other applications for some related verbal forms.

Lane: vine-related verbal forms

Mijfan: Its proposed meaning as a retaining instrument or container was not confirmed in the examined sources. Without an explicit witness, that meaning should not be assigned to it, nor should it be treated as a Qur’anic form.

Lisān: documented forms and meanings

Only jifānin occurs in the Qur’an.

Jafn and jafnah must be distinguished. Familiar uses of the former chiefly concern eyelids and sheaths; the latter names a large food bowl. Belonging to one family does not make them interchangeable in translation.

Ajfān and jifān also differ in formation and application. The former can pluralize eyelids or sheaths; Qur’anic jifān pluralizes food bowls.

3. Eyelids, sheaths, and containment

Jafn: The eye’s covering

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْجَفْنُ: جَفْنُ الْعَيْنِ.

al-jafnu jafnu al-ʿayn

That is: “Jafn is the eyelid.”

Lisān and Al-Muḥkam: the eyelid

Ibn Sīdah explains it as the eye’s covering from above and below.

An eyelid lies over the eye and can cover its surface through movement. Covering and protection are apparent in this use.

The eye is sensitive, and the lid protects it from external factors. Yet biological functions such as moistening the eye or distributing tears should not all become part of jafn’s lexical meaning.

The word means eyelid; its biological characteristics belong to knowledge of the organ itself.

Plurals such as ajfān and jufūn denote eyelids in Arabic.

Poetry can connect eyelids with eyes, sleep, tears, and beauty. Those literary uses do not restrict jafn’s primary meaning to sadness, sleep, or beauty.

A sword’s sheath

A second use of jafn concerns a sword sheath.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَجَفْنُ السَّيْفِ غِمْدُهُ.

wa-jafnu al-sayfi ghimduhu

Translation: “The jafn of a sword is its sheath.”

Lisān: the sword sheath

A sheath holds the sword’s blade, which can be drawn and returned to it.

Containment is clear: the sheath surrounds and holds the blade.

This fits Ibn Fāris’s analysis of surrounding and retaining.

An eyelid and sheath nevertheless differ: one is living bodily tissue, the other a manufactured object holding a tool.

Their shared covering and containing functions do not identify all their characteristics.

A poetic witness concerning a sword sheath

A verse attributed to Ḥudhayfah ibn Anas al-Hudhalī reads:

نَجَا سَالِمٌ وَالنَّفْسُ مِنْهُ بِشِدْقِهِ
وَلَمْ يَنْجُ إِلَّا جَفْنَ سَيْفٍ وَمِئْزَرَا

najā Sālimun wa-al-nafsu minhu bi-shidqihi / wa-lam yanju illā jafna sayfin wa-miʾzarā

Translation: “Sālim escaped with his life at his lips; nothing remained with him but a sword sheath and a waistcloth.”

Jafna sayfin denotes a sword sheath.

The poet depicts someone barely escaping with few possessions or implements left.

Lexical sources report disputed attribution. Ibn Barrī assigns it to Ḥudhayfah ibn Anas al-Hudhalī and rejects the attribution to Abū Khirāsh found in some sources.

Ibn Barrī in Lisān: disputed poetic attribution

Different syntactic explanations of jafna sayfin after illā are also recorded.

Neither the attribution dispute nor the grammatical analyses change jafn’s meaning: a sword sheath.

Until the composition’s date is established, it should not be presented as certainly pre-Islamic. Its principal value is as a recorded usage with a specific sheath meaning.

These applications within the root’s core

Both eyelid and sheath cover or contain something.

They differ from a jafnah, however: lids and sheaths chiefly surround something, whereas a jafnah is an open vessel holding food inside.

The shared feature can be containment or providing a place for something, but covering from above is not an invariant meaning of the entire family.

A jafnah need not cover food with a lid; it holds it.

This distinction matters for defining the Qur’anic application.

4. Jafnah: A large food bowl

Distinguishing a jafnah from an ordinary vessel

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَالْجَفْنَةُ مَعْرُوفَةٌ، أَعْظَمُ مَا يَكُونُ مِنَ الْقِصَاعِ.

wa-al-jafnatu maʿrūfatun aʿẓamu mā yakūnu mina al-qiṣāʿ

Translation: “Jafnah is well known: among the largest kinds of food bowls.”

Lisān: large jafnah and its plurals

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ also states:

وَالْجَفْنَةُ كَالْقَصْعَةِ.

wa-al-jafnatu ka-al-qaṣʿah

Translation: “Jafnah is like a qaṣʿah.”

Ṣiḥāḥ in Lisān: jafnah as a qaṣʿah

Qaṣʿah is a bowl or food vessel whose size can vary. The familiar jafnah is a large, broad vessel.

An everyday small bowl may hold one person’s food; a large vessel can hold food for several people.

This helps explain jafnah’s place in hospitality and communal eating.

It does not establish a fixed number of diners for every jafnah. The word itself supplies no exact dimensions.

Jafnah names a large food vessel; a separate comparison in the Qur’anic verse emphasizes its exceptional size.

A food vessel, not necessarily a cooking pot

Arabic distinguishes a vessel in which food is placed and served from one used over fire for cooking.

Jafnah is chiefly associated with holding and presenting food.

Qidr, by contrast, is a cooking pot.

Some vessels may combine those functions, but the words are not lexically identical.

Sabaʾ 34:13 names jifān and qudūr separately, indicating two recognized vessel types or functions.

Rendering jifān as cooking pots would erase that distinction.

“Large bowls” or “large food vessels” is more precise.

Does jafnah specify a material?

Lexical definitions focus on vessel type and function, rather than an invariant construction material.

A bowl might be made of wood, metal, pottery, stone, or another material.

Jafnah alone does not establish copper, gold, stone, or wood.

Sabaʾ 34:13 likewise does not specify the bowls’ material.

Nor does their proximity to qudūrin rāsiyāt establish that all the bowls were equally fixed and too heavy to move.

Rāsiyāt directly qualifies the cooking pots.

Jafnah and food preparation

Lisān al-ʿArab records jafana al-jazūra.

The expression concerns preparing camel meat and placing it in food bowls for guests.

Some explanations specify slaughtering the animal, cooking its meat, and placing it in the jifān.

Lisān: preparing meat and serving food

The vessel’s name can thus extend to a verb concerning food preparation and serving.

In that construction the verb describes a particular culinary action; its meaning should not be transferred to every form in the family.

Qur’anic jifān, for instance, names vessels rather than slaughtering, cooking, or feeding.

Jafnah and hospitality

A large food vessel could signify the capacity to receive guests in Arab social life.

Someone providing food for guests, travelers, and people in need required vessels holding substantial quantities.

Jafnah consequently became associated with generosity and hospitality in praise poetry.

Some figurative uses even call a generous person a jafnah, because he provides large bowls and feeds others from them.

A characteristic or familiar implement associated with someone is applied to the person himself.

This figurative application does not make generosity or a generous person jafnah’s primary meaning.

A large bowl may remain empty, or belong to someone who does not use it for others.

Generosity is known from its owner’s conduct, rather than the vessel alone.

5. Jafnah in pre-Islamic poetry and hospitality

A large jafnah in Al-Aʿshā’s poetry

One of the clearest pre-Islamic witnesses is a poem by Al-Aʿshā Maymūn ibn Qays praising Al-Muḥallaq al-Kilābī.

A familiar version reads:

تَرُوحُ عَلَى نَادِي الْمُحَلَّقِ جَفْنَةٌ
كَجَابِيَةِ الشَّيْخِ الْعِرَاقِيِّ تَفْهَقُ

tarūḥu ʿalā nādī al-Muḥallaqi jafnatun / ka-jābiyati al-shaykhi al-ʿirāqiyyi tafhaqu

Translation: “Each evening a great food bowl comes to Al-Muḥallaq’s gathering, like the Iraqi old man’s water basin, brimming full.”

Jafnah denotes a large food vessel.

Nādī is a gathering place or assembly. Bringing a bowl there creates an image of communal hospitality.

Jābiyah concerns a large water basin; tafhaqu expresses filling to the brim or overflowing.

The poet thus compares both the vessel’s size and fullness with a water basin, rather than depicting an ordinary bowl.

Some transmissions give the first hemistich as:

نَفَى الذَّمَّ عَنْ آلِ الْمُحَلَّقِ جَفْنَةٌ

nafā al-dhamma ʿan āli al-Muḥallaqi jafnatun

That is: “A great food bowl drove blame away from Al-Muḥallaq’s family.”

This version makes the connection with generosity more explicit: the family’s openhandedness appears in feeding others.

The second hemistich also varies. Both jābiyati al-shaykhi al-ʿirāqiyyi and jābiyati al-sayḥi al-ʿirāqiyyi occur in sources.

These variants should be preserved when evaluating the witness. In the principal versions, jafnah remains a large food bowl and jābiyah a large water basin.

Al-Aʿshā is a recognized pre-Islamic poet, and Arabic literary tradition assigns the praise of Al-Muḥallaq to that period.

The witness matters because it shows the comparison between a large food bowl and a water basin was familiar in Arabic before the Qur’anic application.

Al-Ṭabarī and editorial note: Al-Aʿshā and reading variants

Sabaʾ 34:13 employs the same field of imagery: large vessels like basins.

This proximity does not establish that the Qur’an borrowed its wording from Al-Aʿshā. The poetic witness merely shows that the image was recognizable in Arabic.

Jafanāt in Ḥassān ibn Thābit’s poetry

A poem attributed to Ḥassān ibn Thābit reads:

لَنَا الْجَفَنَاتُ الْغُرُّ يَلْمَعْنَ بِالضُّحَى
وَأَسْيَافُنَا يَقْطُرْنَ مِنْ نَجْدَةٍ دَمَا

lanā al-jafanātu al-ghurru yalmaʿna bi-al-ḍuḥā / wa-asyāfunā yaqṭurna min najdatin damā

Translation: “We have great, shining food bowls that gleam in daylight; our swords drip blood from valor.”

Al-jafanāt is a plural of jafnah.

The poet places generosity in hospitality beside courage in battle.

Food vessels signify the family’s openhandedness, while swords signify martial ability.

Al-ghurr concerns whiteness and brightness. Applied to bowls, it can concern their appearance or the food and fat within them.

It is a description added by the poet, rather than jafnah’s primary meaning.

Ḥassān was a mukhaḍram poet who experienced both pre-Islamic and Islamic times.

Some literary reports say he recited the verse to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī at ʿUkāẓ. Al-Nābighah reportedly criticized jafanāt rather than jifān, treating the former as a smaller-number plural and the latter as one of greater number.

The report’s historical reliability is disputed, so the attributed judgment should not be presented as an established event without examination.

Miṣbāḥ: uncertain criticism report and plural ranges

Linguistically, the verse clearly exemplifies jafanāt for large food bowls.

The two plural forms should not become an exceptionless rule that jafanāt always proves few and jifān necessarily proves a very large number.

Plural formation can suggest such distinctions in some uses, but exact number requires contextual evidence.

Jafnah and generosity’s social standing

In these two witnesses the bowl takes on a role in portraying a person’s or family’s social standing.

Large vessels suggest that food is prepared for others as well as the householder.

Where hospitality, receiving travelers, and helping people in need were recognized marks of status, a bowl full of food could signify generosity.

That social role should not replace the root’s lexical definition.

Qur’anic jifān names vessels. Ethical implications concerning blessing, resources, or their use must be drawn from the whole verse.

6. Grapevines and other applications

Jafn and jafnah for grapevines

Arabic records both jafn and jafnah in grapevine applications.

Some reports name the vine itself, others its stock or trunk, and others its shoots.

Ibn Fāris explains this through vine shoots surrounding or spreading over what supports them.

Ibn Fāris: vine and support image

This fits his proposed principle of enclosure.

It does not establish that every vine species at every growth stage necessarily winds around a support in precisely the same manner.

The neighboring applications also show that jafn can denote different plant parts in vine contexts.

Other plant names

Lisān transmits reports from Abū Ḥanīfah al-Dīnawarī about a plant called jafn.

It grows in some hard ground and is described through its form, leaves, and persistence.

Reports also concern an aromatic plant and another with short stems and gray-green leaves.

One Arabic name may thus be applied to several plants.

Identifying the precise botanical species in each report requires independent evidence.

The shared name alone cannot make them one plant or justify imposing a modern species identification on an early text.

These botanical applications are not Qur’anic and do not directly explain jifānin in Sabaʾ.

Grape skin and the surfaces of bread

Some sources use jafn for a grape’s skin or covering.

Its enclosing function is close to Ibn Fāris’s analysis.

Jafnā al-raghīfi is also recorded for a loaf’s two surfaces, upper and lower.

The two sides are conceived as its coverings.

These witnesses clarify semantic extension, but Qur’anic jifān denotes none of these things.

Lisān: plants, grape skin, and bread surfaces

Proper names

Jafnah also appears in personal, family, and tribal names, including the family historically associated with the Ghassanid rulers of Syria.

Jufaynah occurs as a proper name in some proverbs.

These names belong to linguistic history but do not independently establish the meanings bowl, eyelid, or vine.

A proper name should not be derived from a particular lexical image without historical evidence.

Lisān: Jafnah and Jufaynah as names

7. The Qur’anic occurrence: Sabaʾ 34:13

يَعْمَلُونَ لَهُ مَا يَشَاءُ مِن مَّحَارِيبَ وَتَمَاثِيلَ وَجِفَانٍ كَالْجَوَابِ وَقُدُورٍ رَّاسِيَاتٍ ۚ اعْمَلُوا آلَ دَاوُودَ شُكْرًا ۚ وَقَلِيلٌ مِّنْ عِبَادِيَ الشَّكُورُ

yaʿmalūna lahu mā yashāʾu min maḥārība wa-tamāthīla wa-jifānin ka-al-jawābi wa-qudūrin rāsiyātin iʿmalū āla Dāwūda shukran wa-qalīlun min ʿibādiya al-shakūr

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “They made for him whatever he wished of sanctuaries, statues, bowls like basins, and firmly set cooking pots. Family of David, work gratefully; few of My servants are grateful.”

The verse concerns work performed for Solomon.

The preceding Sabaʾ 34:12 mentions wind, a flowing spring of copper, and a group of jinn working by their Lord’s permission.

The following list names things made for Solomon: sanctuaries, statues, large food vessels, and firmly set cooking pots.

The list concerns a variety of resources and manufactured objects.

Some involve buildings and spaces, others figures and statues, and others food preparation or serving.

Wa-jifānin: A plural of large vessels

Jifānin is the plural of jafnah.

It denotes large food bowls here.

Wa connects them with the other things being made.

Jifānin is indefinite and genitive within a list introduced by min.

The expression consequently names a manufactured object type, rather than the act of eating or feeding.

The word alone does not establish the vessels’ number, material, or location within a particular building or place.

Jafnah’s lexical meaning nevertheless identifies them as large food vessels.

Ka-al-jawābi: Comparison with water basins

Immediately after jifānin comes:

“Ka-al-jawābi.”

Ka is a comparison particle.

Al-jawāb here denotes large basins and relates to jawābī, the plural of jābiyah. The verse’s written and recited al-jawābi omits jawābī’s final yāʾ. It is the basin plural under J B Y, rather than jawāb meaning an answer under J W B.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: al-jawāb under J B Y

Jābiyah denotes a basin collecting water, particularly a large one also used to water animals.

The verse thus compares food vessels with water basins.

Their size and capacity are highlighted.

A jafnah is a food vessel and a jābiyah a water basin. Their functions differ, although shape and scale may resemble each other.

The food vessels are not thereby literally identified as water basins.

The comparison explains their remarkable size.

Distinguishing jifān and jawāb

These words belong to separate roots.

Jifān belongs to J F N and denotes large food bowls.

Jawāb here relates to J B Y and jābiyah.

Translation and root analysis must preserve the distinction.

Jifānin ka-al-jawābi compares two objects.

Rendering both words simply as basins would erase the two sides of the comparison.

“Bowls like basins” preserves the distinction.

Although the Persian word for bowl can suggest a relatively small vessel, the basin comparison immediately clarifies the intended scale.

Did these bowls have a specified size?

Some exegetical reports give very large numbers concerning their size.

For example, many people are said to have been able to sit around one bowl and eat from it.

The verse supplies no numerical count of diners, dimensions, or capacity.

Comparison with a basin alone also fails to establish each vessel’s precise length, breadth, and depth.

No fixed capacity can therefore be calculated from ka-al-jawābi.

Their remarkable size and capacity are what is clearly conveyed.

Were the bowls made of stone or metal?

The preceding verse mentions a copper spring flowing for Solomon.

This might suggest that some vessels were also metal.

Sabaʾ 34:13 itself nevertheless does not specify jifān’s material.

Exegetical reports also mention carved vessels, but their material and manufacture are not detailed in the verse.

Assigning every bowl certainly to copper, gold, stone, or another material goes beyond the Qur’an’s direct information.

Al-Ṭabarī: exegetical reports and great basins

Lexical study identifies vessel type while leaving construction material open.

Bowls and firmly set cooking pots

Wa-qudūrin rāsiyāt follows jifānin ka-al-jawābi.

Qudūr is the plural of qidr, meaning cooking pots.

Rāsiyāt belongs to R S W and conveys being firmly established or fixed.

It qualifies the pots in Solomon’s list of manufactured objects.

Functionally, a jafnah chiefly holds and presents food, while a cooking pot prepares it.

A large establishment could naturally use both: cooking food in pots and preparing or serving it in large vessels.

The verse does not directly explain kitchen organization or hospitality arrangements.

This practical illustration clarifies two vessel types; it should not become a definite account of Solomon’s catering system.

Their place in Solomon’s list

The verse names four groups: sanctuaries, statues, food bowls, and cooking pots.

Solomon’s resources were consequently not confined to one field.

Building structures, shaping statues, and making large vessels all belonged to the work performed.

The list’s order does not establish that the bowls stood inside the sanctuaries or that statues decorated the vessels.

The Qur’an lists these objects without explaining all their precise spatial or technical relationships.

Nor can the word jifān independently settle the permissibility of making statues or details of religious rites.

Each topic requires its own lexical and contextual study.

8. Jafnah, resources, and gratitude in action

From a food bowl to the call for gratitude

The verse ends by addressing David’s family:

“Iʿmalū āla Dāwūda shukran.”

The command follows the reminder of resources and objects associated with David and Solomon.

Blessing here is not confined to a single possession: power, craft, construction capacity, and everyday implements are among their resources.

The large bowls are one example.

Jafnah itself does not lexically mean gratitude. The connection comes from its place among the blessings and the concluding command.

Does a bowl’s size prove generosity?

A large food-filled bowl could signify generosity in Arabic poetry.

Sabaʾ 34:13 does not directly say that the vessels were made to feed poor people, guests, or another specified group.

Those applications fit the vessel’s nature, but its precise use here is not stated.

Size alone cannot establish that Solomon fed a particular daily number or organized a specified public feeding system.

The clearly conveyed point is the availability of vessels with very large capacity.

The subsequent command to gratitude recalls that resources call for a practical response.

How that responsibility is fulfilled requires examining other verses about David, Solomon, and gratitude.

Gratitude involves more than speech

Iʿmalū āla Dāwūda shukran speaks of acting.

Gratitude here concerns a practical response to blessing.

Al-Ṭabarī: gratitude through action

Possessing large vessels, buildings, craft, and power does not by itself exhaust human responsibility.

Resources may be used rightly or wrongly; responsibility for their use is distinct from their availability.

Forqan’s independent study “Shukr in the Qur’an” examines this relationship in detail.

Study: Shukr in the Qur’an

A J F N root article need not repeat the full discussion of gratitude, power, and political responsibility.

For the root itself, jafnah is a large food bowl and here one of the objects associated with Solomon’s resources.

9. Comparison with neighboring roots

J F N (jafnah) and Q Ṣ ʿ (qaṣʿah)

Qaṣʿah is an Arabic bowl or food vessel.

Lexical sources explain jafnah through qaṣʿah, with some definitions emphasizing jafnah’s size.

The words are consequently close in food-vessel applications.

Jafnah in poetry and lexical definitions nevertheless concerns a large vessel suitable for communal food.

Qaṣʿah can be a more general food-vessel designation.

The difference should not become a numerical threshold below which every vessel is a qaṣʿah and above which every vessel is a jafnah.

“Large bowls” can convey jafnah in Qur’anic translation.

J F N (jifān) and J B Y (jābiyah)

Study of J B Y

Jābiyah denotes a large water basin.

Jifān is compared with those basins in Sabaʾ 34:13.

Their functions differ: jafnah is familiar for food, jābiyah for collected water.

Both nevertheless have capacity and interior space.

The verse compares their size and breadth.

Jifānin ka-al-jawābi should therefore not be reduced to a single object type.

J F N (jafnah) and Q D R (qidr)

Study of Q D R

Qidr is a cooking pot and can be placed over fire.

Jafnah is a large food vessel chiefly associated with holding and serving food.

Sabaʾ 34:13 mentions both together.

That juxtaposition shows the use of two different designations.

Although some vessels may serve several functions, jafnah and qidr should not be identified in translation.

J F N (jafn) and GH M D (ghimd)

Ghimd denotes a sword sheath.

One application of jafn has the same meaning.

They can be synonymous in the context of holding a blade.

The J F N family also has applications such as eyelids, grapevines, and food vessels.

A shared sheath meaning does not make the families identical throughout their derivatives and uses.

J F N (jafn) and Ṭ R F (ṭarf)

Study of Ṭ R F

Some Arabic applications of ṭarf concern the eye or gaze.

Jafn denotes the eyelid.

An eyelid and a gaze are different things.

An eyelid is a bodily organ covering the eye and participating in its movement; gaze concerns seeing or the direction of visual attention.

Poetry may place them in one image, but contextual proximity does not identify their lexical meanings.

J F N (jafn) and W ʿ Y (wiʿāʾ)

Study of W ʿ Y

Wiʿāʾ concerns a vessel or something that holds objects.

A jafnah is a container in that respect.

Wiʿāʾ is broader, covering diverse vessels and, figuratively, retention of information or subject matter.

Jafnah names a food vessel with a more specific function.

Although container correctly explains jafnah, it should not be identified with every application of wiʿāʾ.

J F N (jifān) and J F W (jafāʾ)

Study of J F W

The initial consonants resemble each other, but these families differ in root and meaning.

Qur’anic J F N concerns large food bowls.

J F W concerns distance, separation, and, in some uses, harsh behavior.

Al-Sajdah 32:16 uses tatajāfā from that second family.

Surface resemblance cannot make jafnah mean distance, violence, or unkindness.

J F N (jifān) and J F A (jufāʾ)

Study of J F A

Jufāʾ in Al-Raʿd 13:17 concerns foam and discarded matter.

Jifān in Sabaʾ 34:13 concerns food vessels.

The words differ in form and meaning.

Sharing jīm and fāʾ is insufficient to establish a root relationship.

Jafnah involves holding food, while jufāʾ highlights displacement and being driven aside.

10. Questions of understanding and translation

Is jafnah an ordinary small bowl?

The Persian word for bowl can suggest a small vessel for one person’s food.

In early Arabic, however, jafnah is a large vessel.

A bare translation as bowl might therefore fail to make its size clear.

Sabaʾ 34:13 addresses this through comparison with basins.

Forqan’s existing Persian phrase, “bowls like basins,” consequently preserves the sentence’s meaning well.

Does jafnah require a particular material?

No. The word itself does not identify the material.

A vessel might be made of wood, stone, metal, or something else.

The verse likewise leaves its material unspecified.

Jifān cannot therefore be translated as copper, golden, or stone bowls without evidence.

Does a large bowl necessarily contain abundant food?

Practically, a large vessel permits substantial amounts of food.

Capacity is not the same as actually being full at a given moment.

Tafhaqu establishes fullness or overflowing in Al-Aʿshā’s poem.

Sabaʾ 34:13 concerns making the vessels without directly describing their food content.

Fullness or food type should not become part of jifān’s meaning itself.

Does jafnah always signify generosity?

A large jafnah can signify generosity in praise poetry.

That social meaning comes from the vessel’s use and the manner of hospitality.

Jafnah itself simply names the food vessel.

Qur’anic jifān should therefore not be rendered as abundant gifts or feeding the needy.

Those may be topics for analyzing a vessel’s use, but are not its direct meaning.

Could the bowls serve thousands?

Some exegetical reports describe exceptionally large capacities.

The Qur’an specifies no number for vessel size or diners.

Comparison with a basin conveys size but cannot calculate exact capacity.

Any particular numerical capacity must therefore be examined as an external report, rather than the verse’s translation or certain implication.

Do all derivatives concern food vessels?

No.

Jafn can denote an eyelid or sword sheath; jafnah is a large food bowl and can also denote a grapevine.

Lexical sources record other words concerning plants and parts of food in the family.

Each derivative consequently requires its own identification.

The proposed containing core can clarify relationships between some applications, without replacing their specific meanings.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

The primary food-related equivalent of jafnah is “large bowl” or “large food vessel.”

For its plural jifān, use “large bowls” or “large food vessels.”

The expression in Sabaʾ 34:13:

وَجِفَانٍ كَالْجَوَابِ

wa-jifānin ka-al-jawāb

is rendered in Forqan’s existing Persian translation as:

“And bowls like basins.”

This is natural and faithful to the Arabic construction; no change is needed.

For the eye-related jafn, “eyelid” is appropriate.

“Sword sheath” naturally and clearly renders jafnu al-sayfi.

In vine contexts, “grapevine,” “vine stock,” or “vine shoot” may fit the evidence; these meanings should not be interchanged without support.

For jafana al-jazūra in food preparation, an explanation such as “he prepared camel meat and placed it in food bowls” is appropriate.

Figurative jafnah for a generous person can be rendered “hospitable, openhanded man,” provided the context actually supports that figurative application.

These non-Qur’anic senses should not be transferred to jifān in Sabaʾ.

12. Conclusion

Arabic J F N has several physical applications: jafn concerns an eyelid or sword sheath, and jafnah a large food bowl. Vine-related uses and the skin or covering of certain things also occur.

Ibn Fāris identifies surrounding and containing as their shared feature. This fits eyelid, sheath, and vessel, but should not become so broad that every covering or container is called jafn.

Early Arabic jafnah was a large vessel suitable for communal food, helping explain its association with generosity and hospitality in pre-Islamic poetry.

Al-Aʿshā Maymūn ibn Qays compares a great food bowl with a water basin, showing that the bowl’s size and the basin comparison were familiar in pre-Islamic Arabic.

In the verse attributed to Ḥassān ibn Thābit, jafanāt appears beside swords to signify family generosity and courage. The social value arises from context, rather than the vessel’s lexical meaning alone.

The Qur’an uses the root only once, as jifānin in Sabaʾ 34:13.

Large food vessels appear among the objects made for Solomon.

Ka-al-jawābi compares them with large basins, conveying remarkable capacity.

The verse nevertheless specifies no material, numerical size, number of users, or exact application.

It separately names jifān and qudūr: the former chiefly holds and presents food, while the latter concerns cooking.

The concluding call to gratitude in action places these resources within blessing and responsibility; it does not make jafnah itself mean generosity, gratitude, or feeding.

For Forqan, “large bowls” and “large food vessels” are therefore the principal equivalents in the Qur’anic application.

Jifān directly means large food bowls. Their exceptional scale comes from the basin comparison, and their relationship with blessing and gratitude from the full verse’s context.

Forqan’s existing Persian “bowls like basins” preserves these distinctions and suits the Qur’anic application.
