Root H M S — Faint sound and quiet movement

Qur’anic and lexical forms

هَمْسًا
(hamsan)
A faint sound; accusative verbal noun

From soft speech and gentle footsteps to the subsiding of voices on the Day of Resurrection

The Arabic triliteral root ه م س concerns faint sound, soft speech, and quiet movement. Hams is not loud or conspicuous, but can still be heard. It may arise from human speech, feet touching the ground, animal movement, or another activity.

The Qur’an uses the root once, as هَمْسًا. The verse depicts people following a caller on the Day of Resurrection, their voices subsiding before the Entirely Merciful until nothing but a very faint sound is heard.

Hams is not restricted to whispering. Arabic also uses it for soft footsteps. Understanding the verse therefore requires distinguishing quiet speech from the faint sound of people moving.

Their common feature is subdued, barely audible sound, not necessarily absolute silence, fear, secrecy, or speech addressed to a particular person.

1. The root in early Arabic

Classical Arabic records هَمَسَ يَهْمِسُ هَمْسًا for speaking softly, suppressing the voice, or producing a faint sound.

هَمَسَ فُلَانٌ إِلَى فُلَانٍ means that someone spoke softly and privately to another.

هَمْسُ الْأَقْدَامِ denotes the very soft sound of walking feet.

هَمِيس and هَمُوس also describe sounds or movements without a conspicuous, loud noise.

The source of hams varies, but these usages share the faintness of an audible sound.

A witness from early Arabic

A rajaz line transmitted in lexicons and reports about the early Islamic period reads:

«وَهُنَّ يَمْشِينَ بِنَا هَمِيسًا»

Wa-hunna yamshīna binā hamīsā.

“And those she-camels carry us along with quiet steps.”

هَمِيسًا describes the soft sound of the camels’ movement, produced as their footpads meet the ground.

Here hams is neither human speech nor a whispered conversation. An animal is moving, and this word family describes its faint footsteps.

Reports also attribute recitation of this line to Ibn ʿAbbās while driving camels. Its original composer and date are not independently established, however, so it is not conclusive evidence of pre-Qur’anic poetry.

It witnesses the use of hamīs for faint movement sounds in the linguistic tradition close to the revelation period.

Hams in private conversation

Another line transmitted in lexicons reads:

«فَتَهَامَسُوا سِرًّا وَقَالُوا عَرِّسُوا
فِي غَيْرِ تَمْئِنَةٍ بِغَيْرِ مُعَرَّسِ»

Fa-tahāmasū sirran wa-qālū ʿarrisū / fī ghayri tamʾinatin bi-ghayri muʿarrasi.

“They spoke secretly together and said: Stop for the night—without reassurance, in no suitable stopping place.”

تَهَامَسُوا describes several people speaking softly together.

Form VI here fits reciprocal conversation: people exchange quiet words with one another.

Reciprocity follows from the context as well as the morphological pattern; the passage itself depicts a group conversing.

Some lexical sources attribute the line to al-Marrār. They also transmit a variant, فَتَنَاءَمُوا, in place of فَتَهَامَسُوا. It therefore illustrates the usage recorded in the quoted recension, but does not independently establish either an exact date or a secure pre-Qur’anic witness for this root.

The transmitted evidence illustrates faint movement sounds and soft speech. Dating the emergence of each requires further independent evidence. The Qur’an itself clearly witnesses the family’s usage during revelation.

2. Semantic branches

Speaking softly

A principal use of hams is speech in a low voice.

The person speaks without a loud or conspicuous voice. Someone nearby may hear while people farther away do not.

Such speech can serve secrecy, respect, caution, or avoidance of disturbance.

The root itself does not identify the reason for speaking softly.

A quietly spoken statement can be true or false. A concealed voice alone does not establish the speech’s moral value.

Faint footsteps

In walking contexts, hams describes feet meeting the ground very softly.

Someone wearing heavy shoes may make an obvious noise in a quiet setting; gentle steps instead produce only a faint sound.

Arabic can call that faint sound hams.

It is thus not solely produced by the tongue and mouth. Bodily movement or contact between things can also produce it.

This matters in Ṭā Hā 20:108, which depicts people following a caller.

Animal movement

أَسَدٌ هَمُوسٌ can describe a lion that moves with gentle, quiet steps.

Such movement can let the animal approach without conspicuous noise.

Hamūs does not consequently always mean predatory, hunting, or dangerous. Those properties may describe a lion, but the relevant meaning here is quiet movement.

Some sources also record هَمَّاس for a lion’s forceful biting. That usage concerns pressure and the teeth’s effect, and should not be recast as faint sound without evidence.

Eating and chewing

Classical Arabic also records هَمَسَ الطَّعَامَ for chewing food with the mouth closed.

This concerns an activity performed without visibly opening the mouth or making a loud noise.

Lexicons also record branches involving pressing or squeezing. Evidence does not establish a single historical image behind all of them.

Faint sound is clear in speech and movement usages, but should not be imposed on every recorded form in every period.

3. Forms and derivatives

The Qur’an uses ه م س once, in the following form:

هَمْسًا — Ṭā Hā 20:108

هَمْس is the verbal noun of basic-form هَمَسَ. Here it is indefinite and accusative.

Recorded Arabic forms include:

  • هَمَسَ: spoke softly or produced a faint sound.
  • يَهْمِسُ: speaks softly or produces a faint sound.
  • هَمْس: faint sound, whispering, or soft movement sounds.
  • هَمِيس: faint sound or quiet movement.
  • هَمُوس: describing a person or animal that moves very quietly.
  • هَامِس: someone speaking softly.
  • مَهْمُوس: something expressed softly or with a faint sound.
  • تَهَامَسَ: spoke softly and privately with another.
  • تَهَامُس: soft, private conversation between people.

Among these recorded Arabic forms, the Qur’an uses only هَمْسًا; the other listed forms do not occur there.

Other Arabic usages help establish the semantic field, but their every feature should not be imposed on هَمْسًا in Ṭā Hā 20:108.

4. Qur’anic usage — Hams on the Day of Resurrection

Ṭā Hā 20:108

«يَوْمَئِذٍ يَتَّبِعُونَ الدَّاعِيَ لَا عِوَجَ لَهُ وَخَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا»

Yawmaʾidhin yattabiʿūna d-dāʿiya lā ʿiwaja lahu wa-khashaʿati l-aṣwātu li-r-raḥmāni fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsā.

“That Day they follow the caller without turning aside from him; and voices subside before the Entirely Merciful, so you hear nothing but a faint sound.”

The verse forms part of the depiction of resurrection and humanity’s gathering.

The preceding verses describe the blowing of the trumpet, the gathering of people, and the transformation of earth and mountains.

Now people follow the caller, their voices subsiding before the Entirely Merciful.

يَتَّبِعُونَ الدَّاعِيَ — Following the caller

At the verse’s opening, people follow the caller.

يَتَّبِعُونَ belongs to ت ب ع and denotes following or going after.

الدَّاعِيَ belongs to د ع و and identifies one who calls.

The scene thus involves more than standing silently: the people follow the caller.

This strengthens the possibility that هَمْسًا concerns their faint footsteps.

Following a caller does not by itself prove that the sound must be footsteps, however. The scene involves movement, but hams can have a broader meaning.

وَخَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ — Voices subsiding

خَشَعَتْ belongs to خ ش ع, here describing voices subsiding, becoming quiet, and submitting.

الْأَصْوَاتُ is the plural of ṣawt, sound or voice.

Rather than merely describing tongues falling silent, the verse describes sounds subsiding before the Entirely Merciful.

Saying that a crowd’s voices subsided can mean that its clamor ended and loud, conspicuous sounds ceased.

That need not imply the absence of every audible sound.

The continuation makes this distinction clear:

«فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا»

Fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsā.

That is, “So you hear nothing but a faint sound.”

If every sound had disappeared without exception, there would be no hams left to hear.

The scene therefore depicts prominent sounds subsiding while a very faint sound remains.

Is hams quiet speech?

Speaking softly is one recognized meaning of hams.

On this reading, people’s loud voices subside, leaving only quiet speech or very faint whispers.

This is linguistically possible.

Ṭā Hā 20:103 also describes people speaking quietly together about how long they remained.

Quiet speech thus occurs in the nearby passage.

In Ṭā Hā 20:108, however, هَمْسًا alone identifies neither a speaker, a listener, nor a subject of conversation.

Without independent evidence, it cannot be declared a particular private conversation or speech about a specific subject.

Is hams the sound of footsteps?

Another reading identifies people’s faint footsteps.

Two considerations support this possibility.

First, Arabic uses hams for soft movement sounds, as the camel expression illustrates.

Second, the verse begins with people following a caller. In the gathering scene, this is associated with movement toward a destination.

One can therefore envisage loud voices subsiding while the crowd’s soft footsteps remain audible.

This reading has both lexical and contextual support.

It should not be declared the sole certain meaning: the verse does not explicitly say “feet,” “walking,” or another expression identifying footsteps.

Are the readings incompatible?

Not necessarily.

Hams can name a faint sound arising either from speech or from movement.

The basic meaning can therefore accommodate both usages.

For direct translation, “a faint sound” preserves that range without substituting one possible instance for the entire meaning.

A lexical explanation can introduce both readings and note how footsteps suit the movement following the caller.

The function of إِلَّا هَمْسًا

In فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا, تَسْمَعُ denotes hearing.

إِلَّا introduces the exception; هَمْسًا identifies what is excepted from the negation of hearing.

The sentence directly says that no other sound is heard except hams.

Crucially, hams is something heard, however faint.

Some older explanations associate hams with lips moving without a conspicuous voice. Such movement is visible, but if it produces no sound at all, it cannot literally be something heard.

Preserving the sound component is therefore more precise in translating this verse.

تَسْمَعُ is singular, but can address any listener witnessing the scene. The addressee should not be restricted to a particular person without contextual evidence.

5. Human speech at the Resurrection

Qur’anic depictions of human speech at the Resurrection are not uniform.

Some scenes involve silence and subdued voices; elsewhere people converse, answer questions, or speak.

For example, Hūd 11:105 makes speech on that Day dependent on divine permission.

Al-Mursalāt 77:35 describes a Day when they do not speak.

Yā Sīn 36:65 describes mouths being sealed and hands and feet bearing witness.

Each passage describes a particular situation.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 should therefore not be taken to mean that no human speaks at any stage of the Resurrection.

It describes the audible condition of a scene in which voices have subsided.

Nor must hams be the sound of someone attempting to conceal a secret from The God. Ṭā Hā 20:7 states that He knows the secret and what is still more hidden.

A quiet voice is therefore not concealed from divine knowledge.

وَخَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ describes voices submitting before the Entirely Merciful. It does not independently establish identical intentions, feelings, or levels of faith among everyone present.

The collective soundscape should remain distinct from judgments about each person’s inner state.

6. Comparison with nearby roots

ه م س and خ ف ت — Faint sound and lowering the voice

Khaft concerns a voice becoming low and speech becoming quiet.

Ṭā Hā 20:103 uses يَتَخَافَتُونَ for quiet conversation between people.

Hams can also describe quiet speech but extends to soft footsteps.

In these Qur’anic usages, takhāfut specifically involves human conversation, while hams can cover faint sounds more broadly.

This is a contextual tendency, not an absolute separation between the roots.

ه م س and ن ج و — Quiet sound and confidential conversation

Najwā concerns private conversation, secrets, and speech exchanged within a limited circle.

Not every hams is therefore najwā.

Someone may speak softly through illness, fatigue, or respect, without intending to conceal a particular secret.

Faint footsteps are also hams but are not najwā.

Najwā may involve soft speech, but in conversational usages its central feature is the privacy of communication.

Hams alone therefore establishes neither secretive intent nor the exchange of secrets.

ه م س and س ر ر — Quiet sound and concealment

Sirr concerns what is hidden or not disclosed.

Hams in its principal auditory sense concerns low sound intensity.

Quiet speech can remain intelligible to nearby listeners. Conversely, someone can keep a secret without speaking at all.

Concealed information and faint sound are distinct concepts.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 concerns faint sound, not necessarily secrets people have concealed.

ه م س and خ ش ع — Faint sound and subsiding

These roots occur together in Ṭā Hā 20:108.

خَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ describes voices subsiding and submitting.

هَمْسًا identifies the faint sound still heard.

Khushūʿ here describes the general condition of sounds; hams identifies the remaining low sound level.

They complement the scene without being exact synonyms.

ه م س and ه م ز — Faint sound and pricking or pressure

The roots differ only in their final consonant but have distinct principal meanings.

Physical hamz concerns pressure, pricking, or striking; human behavioral usages can involve taunting, humiliation, and faultfinding.

The Qur’anic branch of ه م س concerns faint sound.

Someone might quietly utter a humiliating remark: the manner of delivery could be hams, while the behavior could be hamz.

But not every hams is a taunt, and not every hamz is performed quietly.

Forqan’s separate study of the root ه م ز examines this distinction further.

ه م س and ه م ه م — Faint and indistinct sound

هَمْهَمَة can denote a low, indistinct sound or speech whose words cannot clearly be distinguished.

It is related in usage to hams, but not identical.

Hams can be soft yet entirely intelligible speech, especially to a nearby listener.

Hamhamah may instead be audible without its words being recognizable.

Quietness and unintelligibility are thus distinct features that sometimes coincide.

7. Hams in Arabic phonetics

Traditional Arabic phonetics recognizes “whispered” consonants, الْحُرُوفُ الْمَهْمُوسَة.

Arabic linguists distinguish mahmūs from majhūr consonants when describing their production.

In this terminology, hams concerns airflow during articulation.

For example, س, ش, and ف belong to the traditional mahmūs category.

This technical use differs from ordinary hams.

A mahmūs consonant can be pronounced loudly. Its classification does not mean that a listener necessarily hears a very faint sound.

By contrast, فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا describes an audible situation in which the remaining sound is very faint.

Phonetic terminology helps trace the word’s later applications, but should not be imposed on the verse’s direct meaning.

8. Suggested translations for Forqan

For هَمْسًا in Ṭā Hā 20:108, suitable equivalents include “a faint sound,” “a very quiet sound,” and, where context supports it, “a quiet whisper.”

“Whisper” alone restricts the meaning to human speech and excludes soft footsteps.

“Footsteps” fits the movement scene but substitutes a possible instance for the general meaning in a direct translation.

For Forqan’s base text, the following is therefore preferable:

«فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا»

Fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsā.

“So you hear nothing but a faint sound.”

The entire verse can be rendered:

“That Day they follow the caller without turning aside from him; and voices subside before the Entirely Merciful, so you hear nothing but a faint sound.”

This fits the Arabic structure and preserves both supported possibilities.

A lexical explanation can add:

“Hamsan: a very faint sound, possibly quiet whispering or the soft footsteps of people following the caller.”

Other Arabic usages require equivalents suited to their subject.

هَمَسَ بِالْكَلَامِ can be translated “he spoke softly.”

هَمْسُ الْأَقْدَامِ means “the soft sound of footsteps.”

In conversation, تَهَامَسُوا can mean “they spoke softly together” or “they conversed privately.”

Movement-related أَسَدٌ هَمُوسٌ means “a lion quiet in its walking.”

Fear, secrecy, faultfinding, or a particular intention should not be added to these meanings without evidence.

9. Conclusion

ه م س concerns faint sound, soft speech, and quiet movement.

Its recorded usages include speech, human footsteps, animal movement, and even chewing with the mouth closed.

The transmitted evidence shows that hams extended beyond speech to soft movement sounds. Some poetic witnesses have uncertain dates and cannot all be confidently assigned to pre-Qur’anic times.

The Qur’an uses the root once, as هَمْسًا.

In Ṭā Hā 20:108, people follow a caller and voices subside before the Entirely Merciful, leaving only hams audible.

Very quiet speech and faint footsteps both have support. Footsteps fit the people’s following movement, but the verse does not exclusively identify the sound’s source.

Forqan definition: In its principal branch, ه م س concerns producing or hearing faint, inconspicuous sound. Speech, footsteps, or another movement can produce it. Qur’anic هَمْسًا identifies the very faint sound remaining after conspicuous voices subside, without the word itself restricting its source to speech or footsteps.

The distinctions from takhāfut, najwā, sirr, khushūʿ, and hamz should remain: quietness, concealed information, private communication, submission, and faultfinding can overlap in a situation without being identical meanings.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 depicts the clamor of voices subsiding before the Entirely Merciful, leaving a very faint sound. That contrast between subdued loud voices and audible hams clarifies the word’s contextual meaning.
