Covering, concealing, and keeping protected: from mountain shelters to coverings over hearts and things described as maknūn

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root K N N concerns covering something, placing it in shelter, and keeping it from others’ sight or reach. The covering may be physical, such as a cave or canopy beneath which someone takes shelter, or nonphysical, such as a thought or decision concealed inwardly.

The Qur’an uses the root in four principal groups: the verb aknana, concealing; aknān, shelters; akinnah, coverings; and maknūn, covered or protected.

The principal Qur’anic forms are:

  • أَكْنَنتُمْ (aknantum): you concealed; once, in Al-Baqarah 2:235.
  • تُكِنُّ (tukinnu): conceals; twice, in Al-Naml 27:74 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:69.
  • أَكْنَانًا (aknānan): shelters; once, in Al-Naḥl 16:81.
  • أَكِنَّة (akinnah): coverings; four times, in Al-Anʿām 6:25, Al-Isrāʾ 17:46, Al-Kahf 18:57, and Fuṣṣilat 41:5.
  • مَكْنُون (maknūn): covered, protected, or kept safe; four times, in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:49, Al-Ṭūr 52:24, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:23, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:78.

Early Arabic also knows kinn, kinān, kinānah, kanna, iktanna, and istakanna, but these particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

A covering that encloses something

Lexical sources use kinn for a covering or place that contains something and protects it from external conditions.

The use was clear in everyday Arab life: people escaped sun, wind, cold, or rain under a canopy or shelter, or inside a cave. Such shelter was called kinn.

Covering here means more than making something disappear from sight. Sometimes its principal purpose is protection.

Kinānah, for example, denotes a quiver for keeping arrows. Made of leather or other materials, it held them inside.

An arrow in a quiver is hidden from view, but concealment is not the quiver’s only function: storage and protection also matter.

In domestic architecture, kunnah is recorded for a canopy or covering beside a wall or above a doorway. It could intercept direct sunlight and rain.

The family thus closely connects covering with sheltering and protection.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr, and Asās al-Balāghah: kinn, quivers, and canopies

Distinguishing covering from concealing

Kanna and akanna both belong to this Arabic family.

Some lexicographers distinguish them: kanna more commonly covers and safeguards something, while akanna conceals it inwardly or from others.

Kanna can, for example, describe something placed in shelter, and akanna a thought kept hidden in the heart.

The boundary is not definitive across every Arabic witness.

Al-Farrāʾ, Abū Zayd, and Ibn al-Aʿrābī report overlapping uses. In some expressions, both verbs cover external objects and conceal inward matters.

An absolute rule restricting kanna to physical covering and akanna to hiding thoughts would therefore be unsound.

What is clear in the three Qur’anic occurrences of aknana is the concealment of something inward.

Early lexicons: overlapping kanna and akanna

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

In his Muʿallaqah, the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes a man keeping a decision hidden:

وَكَانَ طَوَى كَشْحًا عَلَى مُسْتَكِنَّةٍ
فَلَا هُوَ أَبْدَاهَا وَلَمْ يَتَقَدَّمِ

wa-kāna ṭawā kashḥan ʿalā mustakinnatin / fa-lā huwa abdāhā wa-lam yataqaddami

Translation:

“He had harbored a concealed matter within himself; he had neither revealed it nor yet stepped forward to carry it out.”

Mustakinnah belongs to this family and denotes a hidden matter.

The poem concerns Ḥuṣayn ibn Ḍamḍam, who kept a vengeful intention inwardly after peace had been established between two groups.

Concealment here is therefore more than a physical condition: it concerns an intention or decision not yet disclosed.

The witness shows that K N N was familiar for inward, hidden matters in Arabic poetry before the Qur’an.

The concealed intention in this story is harmful, but that follows from the narrative and context, rather than the root itself. Something maknūn may be good, bad, or morally neutral.

3. Aknān: mountain shelters

Al-Naḥl says:

وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ أَكْنَانًا

wa-jaʿala lakum mina al-jibāli aknānan

“And from the mountains He made shelters for you.”

Al-Naḥl 16:81

Aknān is a plural in the kinn family and refers here to natural mountain shelters.

Caves, large fissures, rock recesses, and covered parts of a mountain can exemplify such shelters.

Covering here means neither an obstacle to knowledge nor deliberate secrecy. It concerns somewhere people can escape difficult external conditions.

The context reinforces this: shade precedes aknānan, while clothing protecting people from heat or harm in warfare follows it.

Mountain shelters are thus presented among natural blessings and means of human protection.

Aknān here emphasizes shelter more than hiddenness. Someone may also disappear from view inside a cave, but that is not the word’s entire meaning.

Nor should every shelter be restricted to a cave. The verse identifies places in mountains that provide shelter.

Al-Ṭabarī: aknān as mountain shelters

4. Akinnah: coverings that obstruct understanding

Akinnah occurs four times in the Qur’an, each time in relation to hearts.

Coverings over hearts

Al-Anʿām says:

وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَسْتَمِعُ إِلَيْكَ وَجَعَلْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةً أَنْ يَفْقَهُوهُ وَفِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرًا

wa-minhum man yastamiʿu ilayka wa-jaʿalnā ʿalā qulūbihim akinnatan an yafqahūhu wa-fī ādhānihim waqran

“Some of them listen to you, and We have placed coverings over their hearts that prevent understanding it, and heaviness in their ears.”

Al-Anʿām 6:25

Akinnah occurs with the preposition ʿalā:

عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةً (ʿalā qulūbihim akinnatan)

That is, coverings over their hearts.

An yafqahūhu connects these coverings with not understanding the message in context.

Wa-fī ādhānihim waqran also refers to heaviness in the ears.

The verse thus describes two obstacles: coverings over hearts and heaviness in ears.

Without evidence, the image must not become a physical membrane over the bodily heart or a particular hearing disorder. Across Qur’anic verses, the heart relates to understanding, perception, inclination, and decision; the obstacle here likewise concerns receiving the message.

The same construction in Al-Isrāʾ and Al-Kahf

Al-Isrāʾ says:

وَجَعَلْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةً أَنْ يَفْقَهُوهُ وَفِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرًا

wa-jaʿalnā ʿalā qulūbihim akinnatan an yafqahūhu wa-fī ādhānihim waqran

“And We have placed coverings over their hearts that prevent understanding it, and heaviness in their ears.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:46

The principal construction matches Al-Anʿām 6:25.

The preceding verse speaks of reciting the Qur’an and a barrier between the Messenger and those who do not believe in the hereafter. Akinnah again concerns receiving or rejecting the message.

Al-Kahf likewise says:

إِنَّا جَعَلْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةً أَنْ يَفْقَهُوهُ وَفِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرًا

innā jaʿalnā ʿalā qulūbihim akinnatan an yafqahūhu wa-fī ādhānihim waqran

“Indeed, We have placed coverings over their hearts that prevent understanding it, and heaviness in their ears.”

Al-Kahf 18:57

The verse begins with someone reminded of the Lord’s signs who turns away and forgets what he has sent ahead.

The context matters: it places the heart’s covered condition alongside human behavior and response to the reminder.

In all three verses, however, the Qur’an itself attributes placing the coverings to God. That explicit attribution must not be removed or reduced entirely to human behavior without evidence.

Conversely, akinnah alone does not explain every detail of how this condition arises, its relation to human choice, or each person’s ultimate fate. Those questions require other verses on guidance, turning away, choice, and responsibility.

Qulūbunā fī akinnatin

Fuṣṣilat says:

وَقَالُوا قُلُوبُنَا فِي أَكِنَّةٍ مِمَّا تَدْعُونَا إِلَيْهِ وَفِي آذَانِنَا وَقْرٌ

wa-qālū qulūbunā fī akinnatin mimmā tadʿūnā ilayhi wa-fī ādhāninā waqrun

“They said: Our hearts are within coverings against what you call us to, and there is heaviness in our ears.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:5

The construction differs this time.

Earlier verses said that coverings had been placed over their hearts. Here the opponents themselves say:

قُلُوبُنَا فِي أَكِنَّةٍ (qulūbunā fī akinnatin)

“Our hearts are within coverings.”

The hearts are described inside coverings, rather than coverings being placed over the hearts.

This is a real difference in linguistic imagery, although both constructions describe an obstacle to receiving the message in context.

The sentence also quotes the opponents. Merely recording their speech must not be equated with independent endorsement of every claim they make.

The same verse continues with their claim of a barrier between themselves and the Messenger and their declaration of practical opposition. The condition is therefore more than missing information or a natural difficulty understanding language: their speech also takes a position against the invitation.

Covering is not always protection

Comparing aknān in Al-Naḥl 16:81 with akinnah in these passages reveals an important difference.

In the mountains, covering provides useful shelter.

Over hearts, it obstructs understanding the message in a context of censure or turning away.

K N N therefore has no fixed positive or negative moral value. A covering’s value and effect depend on its object, circumstances, and result.

Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: akinnah as the plural of kinān and the meaning of covering

5. Aknana: concealing inwardly

Aknana occurs three times in the Qur’an, each concerning something concealed within a person.

Keeping an intention concealed

Al-Baqarah says:

وَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِيمَا عَرَّضْتُمْ بِهِ مِنْ خِطْبَةِ النِّسَاءِ أَوْ أَكْنَنتُمْ فِي أَنْفُسِكُمْ

wa-lā junāḥa ʿalaykum fī-mā ʿarraḍtum bihi min khiṭbati al-nisāʾi aw aknantum fī anfusikum

“There is no blame upon you for what you indicate indirectly of a marriage proposal to women, or keep concealed within yourselves.”

Al-Baqarah 2:235

Aknantum is a Form IV perfect verb addressed to a plural audience.

Fī anfusikum specifies where the concealment occurs: within yourselves.

The verse distinguishes something indirectly expressed from something still hidden inwardly.

Aknantum therefore concerns an intention or thought not disclosed.

The verse continues with limits concerning secret promises and marriage contracts. Permission to keep an intention inwardly therefore does not permit every secret action.

Lexically, aknantum expresses concealment itself. The proposal context and fī anfusikum identify what is concealed.

What breasts conceal

Al-Naml says:

وَإِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَيَعْلَمُ مَا تُكِنُّ صُدُورُهُمْ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ

wa-inna rabbaka la-yaʿlamu mā tukinnu ṣudūruhum wa-mā yuʿlinūna

“Indeed, your Lord knows what their breasts conceal and what they disclose.”

Al-Naml 27:74

Al-Qaṣaṣ expresses the same theme:

وَرَبُّكَ يَعْلَمُ مَا تُكِنُّ صُدُورُهُمْ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ

wa-rabbuka yaʿlamu mā tukinnu ṣudūruhum wa-mā yuʿlinūna

“And your Lord knows what their breasts conceal and what they disclose.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:69

Both verses combine tukinnu with ṣudūruhum.

Breasts are presented as the location of inward matters: a thought, intention, feeling, or decision not disclosed to others.

Tukinnu also contrasts with yuʿlinūna: what they conceal and what they disclose.

Concealment, contrasted with disclosure, is thus the root’s prominent meaning here.

The verb alone does not establish that everything concealed is a lie, hypocrisy, or deliberate suppression of truth. The concealed matter’s nature follows from context.

6. Maknūn: something remaining covered and protected

Maknūn is a passive participle of this family. It describes something covered or placed under covering and protection.

Its Qur’anic uses particularly highlight being safeguarded.

Bayḍun maknūnun

Al-Ṣāffāt says:

كَأَنَّهُنَّ بَيْضٌ مَكْنُونٌ

ka-annahunna bayḍun maknūnun

“As though they were eggs, covered and protected.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:49

Bayḍun maknūnun occurs in a simile.

Eggs kept somewhere safe or under covering are protected from external conditions. Arabic also uses this image for delicacy and preservation.

The simile itself must nevertheless be distinguished from its possible explanations.

Maknūn alone identifies neither the bird to which the eggs belong nor a particular color. Such specifics require separate evidence.

The word’s direct meaning is being covered and protected.

Luʾluʾun maknūnun

Al-Ṭūr says:

وَيَطُوفُ عَلَيْهِمْ غِلْمَانٌ لَهُمْ كَأَنَّهُمْ لُؤْلُؤٌ مَكْنُونٌ

wa-yaṭūfu ʿalayhim ghilmānun lahum ka-annahum luʾluʾun maknūnun

“Serving youths circulate among them, as though they were pearls, covered and protected.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:24

Maknūn describes pearls here.

A safeguarded pearl is kept within a covering or storage place, away from harm and external conditions.

The simile concerns the beauty and delicacy of the beings described. Maknūn itself, however, specifies neither their sex, color, nor biological nature.

Al-Wāqiʿah also says:

كَأَمْثَالِ اللُّؤْلُؤِ الْمَكْنُونِ

ka-amthāli al-luʾluʾi al-maknūni

“Like pearls, protected and covered.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:23

Pearls are again described as protected.

The two verses show safeguarded pearls as a familiar image of beauty and preservation, but K N N does not supply every detail of that image.

Kitābin maknūnin

Al-Wāqiʿah says:

إِنَّهُ لَقُرْآنٌ كَرِيمٌ
فِي كِتَابٍ مَكْنُونٍ

innahu la-qurʾānun karīmun / fī kitābin maknūnin

“Indeed, it is a noble Qur’an, in a safeguarded book.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:77 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:78

Maknūn describes kitāb here.

This differs from eggs or pearls: the subject is a book in relation to which the Qur’an is introduced.

The semantic core remains being safeguarded and covered.

The next verse says:

لَا يَمَسُّهُ إِلَّا الْمُطَهَّرُونَ

lā yamassuhu illā al-muṭahharūna

“None touches it except those purified.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:79

This continuity places the book’s protection alongside access and purity.

Maknūn itself, however, determines neither the book’s entire nature, its location, nor how it is accessed.

Nor should the expression be restricted to a ruling about touching a physical copy of the Qur’an before examining the pronoun’s referent and the verses’ context.

What is clear for root study is the book’s protection, rather than protection’s precise mechanism.

Al-Ṭabarī: kitābin maknūnin and the meaning of protection

Being covered and being valuable

In the first three examples, the covered thing appears within imagery of beauty and delicacy. In the fourth, a safeguarded book relates to the noble Qur’an.

This association might suggest that maknūn itself means sacred, pure, precious, or extremely beautiful.

That conclusion, however, is lexically imprecise.

Maknūn means being safeguarded and covered. The protected object’s value, beauty, or special standing follows from its identity and context.

It should therefore not automatically be replaced with sacred, flawless, untouched, or pure.

7. Distinguishing related roots

K N N (kanna) and S T R (satara)

The families are close in the meaning of covering.

Satara generally concerns covering and hiding from sight. Kanna also expresses covering, but kinn, aknān, and maknūn more clearly connect with shelter and preservation.

This does not mean every S T R covering is mere secrecy and every K N N covering protection. Their ranges overlap.

K N N (kanna) and K T M (katama)

Katama more commonly concerns concealing something that could be disclosed or stated, such as speech, news, testimony, or truth.

K N N applies both to keeping thoughts hidden inwardly and to covering and storing physical objects.

Aknantum fī anfusikum in Al-Baqarah 2:235 concerns a concealed intention; aknānan in Al-Naḥl 16:81 concerns mountain shelters.

K N N therefore cannot be defined solely as suppressing news or truth.

Study of K T M

K N N (kanna) and KH F Y (khafiya)

KH F Y concerns hiddenness, concealment, and not being apparent.

K N N may express that same result, but many of its uses involve a covering, container, or something placed in shelter.

The tendency can be expressed as follows: something may be hidden without its covering being specified, while fī akinnatin and fī kitābin maknūnin draw attention to its covered or protected condition.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute rule separating every use of the roots.

Study of KH F Y

K N N (kanna) and J N N (janna)

J N N likewise concerns being covered, but its Qur’anic family includes different derivatives such as jinn, jannah, janīn, and majnūn.

Al-Anʿām 6:76, for example, says:

فَلَمَّا جَنَّ عَلَيْهِ اللَّيْلُ رَأَىٰ كَوْكَبًا

fa-lammā janna ʿalayhi al-laylu raʾā kawkaban

“When night covered him, he saw a star.”

Janna concerns enveloping and covering here.

K N N also concerns covering, but its Qur’anic family particularly includes shelters, coverings over hearts, inward concealment, and safeguarded things.

Similar meanings do not make the roots identical.

Study of J N N

K N N (kanna) and Ḥ J B (ḥajaba)

Ḥijāb concerns a barrier or curtain between two sides, which may restrict sight, access, or communication.

K N N more commonly expresses the covering or protected condition of something itself.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:46 place the images close together: a concealed barrier between the Messenger and some opponents, followed by coverings over their hearts.

The expressions can therefore describe one situation while emphasizing different aspects: a barrier between two sides and coverings over hearts.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Each derivative’s equivalent should follow its subject and context.

Aknantum: you kept concealed inwardly.

Tukinnu: conceals.

Aknānan: shelters; places of shelter.

Akinnah: coverings; in the heart passages, coverings obstructing understanding.

Maknūn: covered, safeguarded, kept under covering.

For example:

أَوْ أَكْنَنتُمْ فِي أَنْفُسِكُمْ (Aw aknantum fī anfusikum) — or keep concealed within yourselves.

وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ أَكْنَانًا (Wa-jaʿala lakum mina al-jibāli aknānan) — and from the mountains He made shelters for you.

وَجَعَلْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةً (Wa-jaʿalnā ʿalā qulūbihim akinnatan) — and We placed coverings over their hearts.

مَا تُكِنُّ صُدُورُهُمْ (Mā tukinnu ṣudūruhum) — what their breasts conceal.

كَأَنَّهُنَّ بَيْضٌ مَكْنُونٌ (Ka-annahunna bayḍun maknūnun) — as though they were eggs, covered and protected.

لُؤْلُؤٌ مَكْنُونٌ (Luʾluʾun maknūnun) — a safeguarded pearl.

فِي كِتَابٍ مَكْنُونٍ (Fī kitābin maknūnin) — in a safeguarded book.

Where maknūn describes an object, safeguarded is usually natural and suitable. If context emphasizes being hidden from sight or access, covered or concealed may also suit.

9. Conclusion

K N N concerns covering, concealing, and keeping protected. Early Arabic applies this to shelters, canopies, quivers, and matters concealed inwardly.

Four principal Qur’anic uses appear: aknān for mountain shelters; akinnah for coverings placed over hearts or enclosing them; aknana for concealing inward matters; and maknūn for something remaining covered or safeguarded.

The principal differences concern the covering’s subject and effect. Shelter may protect a person from harm; a heart’s covering may obstruct understanding; a concealed thought may remain unspoken; and a covered object may be kept secure.

Covering in this family is therefore inherently neither good nor bad. Its value or consequence follows from subject and context.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Covering, concealing, and keeping something protected within shelter or a covering, whether physical or inward and nonphysical.

Each derivative’s precise meaning follows from its form and verse context.
