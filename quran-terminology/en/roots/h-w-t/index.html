From fish and movement in water to circling, evasion, and Qur’anic uses

In Arabic, «ح و ت» concerns the name for a fish and, in another branch, movement, circling around something, and changing course. «حُوت» (ḥūt) means fish, although some lexicographers use it particularly for a large fish. The plurals «حِيتَان» (ḥītān) and «أَحْوَات» (aḥwāt) are also established in Arabic.

Within the same family, «حَاتَ الطَّائِرُ» describes a bird circling around something, while «حَاوَتَ» can concern evasion, changing course, and avoiding a direct encounter.

Ibn Fāris considers movement and changing course the family’s underlying semantic principle. The animal’s name must nevertheless be distinguished from the verbs of movement. A fish’s movement through water does not establish that ḥūt itself means motion or circling in every sentence.

The Qur’an contains five occurrences of «ح و ت»: one occurrence of ḥītānuhum in the story of the coastal town, two of ḥūt in the account of Moses and his companion, and two in Jonah’s story.

Qur’anic forms: «حِيتَانُهُمْ» in Al-Aʿrāf 7:163, «حُوتَهُمَا» in Al-Kahf 18:61, «الْحُوتَ» in Al-Kahf 18:63, «الْحُوتُ» in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142, and «الْحُوتِ» in Al-Qalam 68:48.

All these forms are nouns. No verb from «ح و ت» occurs in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five nominal occurrences in five verses

Other forms recorded in Arabic include «حَاتَ», «يَحُوتُ», «حَوْت», «حَوَتَان», «حَاوَتَ», «مُحَاوَتَة», «أَحْوَات», and «حَوْتَاء», but these do not occur in the Qur’anic text in those forms.

1. The root’s meaning in early Arabic

Ḥūt: fish

In Al-ʿAyn, Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad identifies ḥūt as the name of a familiar animal, gives its plural as ḥītān, and places it in the field of fish.

Al-Jawharī states in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ:

الْحُوتُ: السَّمَكَةُ، وَالْجَمْعُ الْحِيتَانُ

al-ḥūtu al-samakatu wa-l-jamʿu al-ḥītānu

That is, ḥūt means a fish, and its plural is ḥītān.

Ibn Manẓūr records two uses in Lisān al-ʿArab: in one, ḥūt is a general name for fish; in the other, it particularly denotes a large fish.

This distinction matters.

Restricting ḥūt to a very large fish would overlook its broader Arabic usage.

Conversely, when a context calls a creature large enough to swallow a human ḥūt, that size is compatible with the word’s established range.

The basic meaning of ḥūt is therefore fish, while its size must be determined from the particular use.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: fish and the large-fish use

Does ḥūt mean a whale?

In present-day Persian, nahang generally refers to large marine mammals called whales.

In zoological classification, however, whales differ from fish. Fish and marine mammals differ in bodily structure, breathing, and reproduction.

NOAA: whales as aquatic mammals

Early Arabic animal names were not based on this modern scientific classification.

Aquatic animals were named through observable features, size, appearance, and behavior.

Thus, ḥūt could refer generally to fish and in some uses also denote a very large aquatic creature.

In modern Arabic too, ḥūt can function as a general name for fish in some uses and as a name for large animals such as whales in others.

That modern usage does not make every ḥūt in the Qur’an necessarily a whale.

The species and size of the fish in Moses’s story are unspecified. In Jonah’s story, the creature swallows him and must therefore be capable of doing so; the verse does not state its zoological species.

For direct translation, fish is therefore the broader and more faithful equivalent. Jonah’s story can be explained with reference to a fish or large aquatic creature without attributing a particular species to the text.

Arabic lexica: the range of ḥūt, distinct from identifying a species

Ḥītān and aḥwāt: plurals of ḥūt

Ḥītān is an established Arabic plural of ḥūt.

Aḥwāt is also recorded in Lisān al-ʿArab and other dictionaries.

Only the plural ḥītān occurs in the Qur’an.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:163, it takes the pronoun hum, forming ḥītānuhum.

This pronoun concerns fish associated with the town and its people.

That relationship does not itself establish the people’s personal ownership of every fish in the sea.

Attaching a pronoun to an Arabic noun can express different relationships, including location, familiarity, use, or ownership.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:163, the fish are presented in connection with the coastal community’s life and trial.

Ḥāta: circling around something

Al-ʿAyn records «حَوْت» and «حَوَتَان» for a bird’s circling around water or something else.

«حَاتَ الطَّائِرُ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ يَحُوتُ» means that a bird circles around something.

This movement may involve circling, approaching, and changing direction again.

For example, a bird might circle several times before landing on the water.

What matters here is the bird’s movement around a point or area.

The verb does not itself mean landing, hunting, or eating.

After circling, the bird might land or move away. The outcome is not determined by the verb alone.

Some uses of «حات به» also describe a wild animal moving around something.

Al-ʿAyn: ḥūt, ḥītān, and a bird’s circling

Ḥāwata: evading and changing course

Arabic uses «حَاوَتَنِي فُلَانٌ» for someone who changes course or behavior to avoid a direct encounter or lead another person astray.

Al-Jawharī explains it through «رَاوَغَنِي»: he evaded me or dodged out of reach.

Muḥāwatah likewise denotes such conduct.

Here, movement involves more than a simple change of position: it includes changing course and avoiding a direct encounter.

In Asās al-Balāghah, Al-Zamakhsharī connects this use with a fish’s movement through water.

Movement must nevertheless be distinguished from morally deceptive conduct.

Changing course or dodging may be a natural way to avoid danger. In another situation, someone may use such conduct to evade accountability or deceive another person.

Muḥāwatah therefore does not always mean lying or betrayal. Any such judgment must come from the context.

Ibn Fāris on the root’s underlying principle

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the underlying principle of «ح و ت» through «الاضطراب والروغان»: movement and changing course or evasion.

He points to the fish’s movement and then gives the expression «حاوتني فلان».

This analysis connects the animal’s name with movement and evasive behavior.

It should not, however, become a categorical historical claim that the Arabic name ḥūt necessarily developed from a verb of circling or evading.

Both uses are lexically established, but the historical order of their emergence has not been independently demonstrated.

Fish also differ in their movement. Some change course rapidly, while others may at times remain calm or almost motionless.

Constant, uninterrupted movement should therefore not be treated as a biological definition of ḥūt itself.

In Qur’anic usage, ḥūt names the animal; it is not a verb expressing movement or evasion.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Ibn Fāris on movement and evasion

Other non-Qur’anic uses

Arabic also uses al-ḥūt for one of the twelve zodiacal signs.

That name relates to the familiar figure of a fish, but does not show that the Qur’an’s ḥūt passages concern zodiacal signs.

Some dictionaries also record ḥawtāʾ for a woman with large flanks and a fleshy body.

A connection between this description of bodily shape and the animal’s name is possible, but this form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Banū Ḥūt was also the name of a group in Arabic genealogical accounts.

That proper name does not change the lexical meaning of ḥūt in the Qur’an.

2. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

A circling bird in verse attributed to Ṭarafah ibn Al-ʿAbd

A rajaz passage attributed to Ṭarafah ibn Al-ʿAbd reads:

مَا كُنْتُ مَجْدُودًا إِذَا غَدَوْتُ
وَمَا لَقِيتُ مِثْلَ مَا لَقِيتُ
كَطَائِرٍ ظَلَّ بِنَا يَحُوتُ
يَنْصَبُّ فِي اللُّوحِ فَمَا يَفُوتُ
يَكَادُ مِنْ رَهْبَتِنَا يَمُوتُ

mā kuntu majdūdan idhā ghadawtu / wa-mā laqītu mithla mā laqītu / ka-ṭāʾirin ẓalla binā yaḥūtu / yanṣabbu fī al-lūḥi fa-mā yafūtu / yakādu min rahbatinā yamūtu

The general meaning is: “I was not fortunate when I went out in the morning, and I encountered something unlike anything I had encountered: a bird circling around us, swooping through the open space without escaping, seemingly close to dying from fear of us.”

Here, yaḥūtu denotes a bird’s movement around something.

The bird approaches a point, changes direction, and circles around it again.

This clearly differs from the meaning fish. Yaḥūtu is a verb used of a bird, while al-ḥūt names an aquatic animal.

The passage is cited in Al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab, and appears in the supplements to Ṭarafah’s collected poetry.

Its wording nevertheless varies across sources. Some give majdūdan and others maḥdūdan; other words also have transmitted variants.

The witness is useful for understanding an early use of the verb, while its textual variants and its placement among supplements to the collected poems must remain acknowledged.

Lisān al-ʿArab: rajaz attributed to Ṭarafah and the majdūdan reading

Muḥāwatah in the witness transmitted by Thaʿlab

Thaʿlab cites this verse when explaining ḥāwata:

ظَلَّتْ تُحَاوِتُنِي رَمْدَاءُ دَاهِيَةٌ
يَوْمَ الثَّوِيَّةِ عَنْ أَهْلِي وَعَنْ مَالِي

ẓallat tuḥāwitunī ramdāʾu dāhiyatun / yawma al-thawiyyati ʿan ahlī wa-ʿan mālī

An approximate rendering is: “On the day of Al-Thawiyyah, the one described as ramdāʾ dāhiyah kept evading me and drawing me away from my family and property.” The precise identity of this referent is not clear from the short quotation.

Tuḥāwitunī here concerns behavior involving changes of course, evasion, or leading another person astray.

This witness also appears in Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab.

The poet and precise date of composition, however, have not been securely identified in the sources examined.

It therefore cannot be presented as a securely pre-Islamic poetic witness.

Its importance is that early lexicographic sources record muḥāwatah in the sense of evasion.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: the witness transmitted by Thaʿlab

Together with lexicographers’ direct reports, these two witnesses show that «ح و ت» includes uses involving movement, circling, and changing course in addition to the name for fish.

Those verbal uses do not occur in the Qur’an itself.

3. Ḥītānuhum: the fish of the coastal town

وَاسْأَلْهُمْ عَنِ الْقَرْيَةِ الَّتِي كَانَتْ حَاضِرَةَ الْبَحْرِ إِذْ يَعْدُونَ فِي السَّبْتِ إِذْ تَأْتِيهِمْ حِيتَانُهُمْ يَوْمَ سَبْتِهِمْ شُرَّعًا وَيَوْمَ لَا يَسْبِتُونَ لَا تَأْتِيهِمْ كَذَٰلِكَ نَبْلُوهُمْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَفْسُقُونَ

wa-sʾalhum ʿani al-qaryati allatī kānat ḥāḍirata al-baḥri idh yaʿdūna fī al-sabti idh taʾtīhim ḥītānuhum yawma sabtihim shurraʿan wa-yawma lā yasbitūna lā taʾtīhim ka-dhālika nablūhum bi-mā kānū yafsuqūna

Forqan translation:

“Ask them about the town by the sea, when they transgressed on the Sabbath: their fish came openly to them on their Sabbath day, but on the day they did not keep Sabbath, they did not come. Thus We tested them because of their disobedience.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:163

Here, ḥītānuhum is the plural of ḥūt with the pronoun hum.

Grammatically, ḥītānuhum is the subject of taʾtīhim: the fish came to them.

The verse describes fish coming openly to the townspeople on the Sabbath, while not appearing in that way on other days.

Ḥītān: fish, not necessarily whales

The word here is plural and concerns fish associated with a coastal town.

No particular species is named.

Ḥītānuhum should therefore not be restricted without evidence to whales or very large fish.

The fish could have differed in species and size, but the verse supplies no such zoological details.

Its direct concern is the fish’s appearance in relation to the trial faced by the townspeople.

Their fish is therefore an appropriate translation.

What does shurraʿan mean?

«شُرَّعًا» is from «ش ر ع», rather than «ح و ت».

Early explanations describe fish appearing openly and within reach, including fish approaching the water’s surface.

Some reports mention the fish raising their heads or approaching the shore.

These details concern shurraʿan and the contextual explanation, rather than ḥītān itself.

Ḥūt here therefore does not mean an openly appearing fish. Their being visible is expressed by another word.

Shurraʿan alone also does not determine the fish’s species or their exact number on each occasion.

Al-Ṭabarī: shurraʿan and the fish’s visible appearance

The townspeople’s trial

The verse itself mentions transgressing on the Sabbath.

It then explains how the fish appeared on different days.

It ends with «كَذَٰلِكَ نَبْلُوهُمْ»: thus We tested them.

The fish’s appearance therefore provides the setting for the people’s trial.

The word ḥūt itself cannot establish a prohibition on fishing on a particular day for all people and all times.

The verse concerns a particular group and their Sabbath observance.

The following verses discuss different groups’ behavior in the town, admonition, disobedience, and its consequences.

These matters should be examined through Al-Aʿrāf 7:163, Al-Aʿrāf 7:164, Al-Aʿrāf 7:165, and Al-Aʿrāf 7:166 together.

The townspeople’s trial also does not make the fish inherently impure, forbidden, or ominous creatures.

What is condemned is the people’s response to the limits and responsibilities placed before them, rather than fish as animals.

4. Ḥūt in the story of Moses and his companion

Al-Kahf 18:61: the fish that took its way into the sea

فَلَمَّا بَلَغَا مَجْمَعَ بَيْنِهِمَا نَسِيَا حُوتَهُمَا فَاتَّخَذَ سَبِيلَهُ فِي الْبَحْرِ سَرَبًا

fa-lammā balaghā majmaʿa baynihimā nasiyā ḥūtahumā fa-ttakhadha sabīlahu fī al-baḥri saraban

Forqan translation:

“When they reached the junction between them, they forgot their fish, and it took its way into the sea like a tunnel.”

Al-Kahf 18:61

Ḥūtahumā here consists of the singular noun ḥūt and the dual pronoun humā.

The pronoun refers to Moses and his companion.

Ḥūtahumā therefore does not mean two fish: it denotes the fish belonging to the two of them.

This morphological distinction matters.

A translation such as their fish may leave the number unspecified, whereas the Arabic form makes clear that ḥūt is singular and its attached pronoun dual.

“Nasiyā ḥūtahumā”

The verse begins with nasiyā: the two of them forgot.

In Al-Kahf 18:63, however, Moses’s companion says that he himself forgot to mention the fish.

Early commentaries explain that the forgetting concerned Moses’s companion, but was narrated in the dual because the two were traveling together.

The text makes clear that the fish left their reach and that Moses’s companion did not recall the matter at the appropriate time.

Ḥūt itself, however, does not mean a sign, forgetfulness, or food.

Forgetting is expressed by nasiya, and the fish’s movement by ittakhadha.

Study of N S Y

Was the fish dead and then brought back to life?

Some exegetical reports say that the fish had been prepared as food, then miraculously came to life and entered the sea.

Some accounts mention a basket, a salted fish, and a spring with a particular property.

These details are not stated in the text of Al-Kahf 18:61.

The verse says only that they forgot their fish and that it took its way into the sea.

In Al-Kahf 18:62, Moses asks his companion to bring their meal. This may relate to carrying the fish for food, but the verse does not explicitly say that it was dead or salted when the journey began.

The Qur’anic text alone therefore does not establish with certainty that the fish had previously died and returned to life.

Conversely, its movement into the sea should not be denied or turned without evidence into a wholly symbolic concept.

The verse itself describes the fish’s movement.

What remains unspecified is the fish’s precise condition before the event and how its extraordinary movement occurred.

Al-Ṭabarī: nasiyā, the fish, and transmitted explanations

Al-Kahf 18:63: recalling the fish

قَالَ أَرَأَيْتَ إِذْ أَوَيْنَا إِلَى الصَّخْرَةِ فَإِنِّي نَسِيتُ الْحُوتَ وَمَا أَنْسَانِيهُ إِلَّا الشَّيْطَانُ أَنْ أَذْكُرَهُ وَاتَّخَذَ سَبِيلَهُ فِي الْبَحْرِ عَجَبًا

qāla a-raʾayta idh awaynā ilā al-ṣakhrati fa-innī nasītu al-ḥūta wa-mā ansānīhu illā al-shayṭānu an adhkurahu wa-ttakhadha sabīlahu fī al-baḥri ʿajaban

Forqan translation:

He said, “Did you see? When we took refuge by the rock, I forgot the fish. None but Satan made me forget to mention it. It took its way into the sea in a wondrous manner.”

Al-Kahf 18:63

Al-ḥūta here is the same fish introduced in Al-Kahf 18:61.

The definite article al- refers to the fish already known within the story.

Moses’s companion recalls the event by the rock and says he had neglected to mention it.

The verse ends with ʿajaban.

This word is from «ع ج ب» and expresses the event’s wondrous character.

The wonder of the movement is therefore not the meaning of ḥūt itself.

In Al-Kahf 18:64, Moses connects this event with what they were seeking, and the two retrace their steps.

This continuity shows that the fish’s behavior matters in the course of their journey.

The root «ح و ت» itself, however, cannot establish that fish are always symbols of guidance or a path to knowledge in Arabic.

In this story, the fish is part of a particular event. Its role comes from the narrative, rather than from the animal’s general name.

5. Ḥūt in Jonah’s story

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142: the fish that swallowed Jonah

فَالْتَقَمَهُ الْحُوتُ وَهُوَ مُلِيمٌ

fa-ltaqamahu al-ḥūtu wa-huwa mulīmun

Forqan translation:

“Then the fish swallowed him while he was blameworthy.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142

Al-ḥūtu here is the subject of fa-ltaqamahu.

Iltaqama is from «ل ق م» and means taking into the mouth or swallowing.

Swallowing therefore does not come from ḥūt itself; a separate verb expresses the action.

Mulīmun is from «ل و م» and concerns Jonah’s blameworthy state.

This description applies to Jonah, rather than the fish.

The preceding verses mention Jonah, his departure toward the laden ship, and the drawing of lots.

His being swallowed by the fish follows.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:143 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:144 discuss glorification and the possibility of his remaining in the fish’s belly.

The text thus makes clear that the fish swallowed him and that Jonah was inside its belly.

The verse itself does not identify the species, exact size, duration of Jonah’s stay, or biological mechanism of his survival.

Was Jonah’s fish necessarily a whale?

The swallowing of a human implies that the creature in question had the capacity to do so.

This contextual indication points to its large size.

Size, however, does not identify a precise species.

In early Arabic, ḥūt could mean a large fish, but that naming practice differs from modern scientific classification.

Al-ḥūt alone therefore does not establish that the creature was necessarily a blue whale, sperm whale, or any other particular marine mammal.

Selecting a specific animal and then attempting to explain the story’s details through that animal’s biology also goes beyond the Qur’anic text when done without independent evidence.

The verse’s direct meaning is clear: the fish or large aquatic creature in question swallowed Jonah.

Its precise species is unstated.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: ḥūt forms in Jonah’s account

Ṣāḥib al-ḥūt: Al-Qalam 68:48

فَاصْبِرْ لِحُكْمِ رَبِّكَ وَلَا تَكُنْ كَصَاحِبِ الْحُوتِ إِذْ نَادَىٰ وَهُوَ مَكْظُومٌ

fa-ṣbir li-ḥukmi rabbika wa-lā takun ka-ṣāḥibi al-ḥūti idh nādā wa-huwa makẓūmun

Forqan translation: “Be patient for your Lord’s judgment, and do not be like the companion of the fish, when he called out, filled with anguish.”

Al-Qalam 68:48

Here, al-ḥūti is the genitive complement of ṣāḥib.

Ṣāḥib al-ḥūt denotes a person known through the account of the fish.

Reading this verse alongside Al-Ṣāffāt 37:139, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:143, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:144, and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145 makes clear that Jonah is intended.

Ṣāḥib al-ḥūt does not, however, mean the fish’s owner or breeder.

Arabic ṣāḥib can express companionship, association, or being known through an event.

Here, Jonah receives this title because of his experience with the fish.

Connection with Dhā Al-Nūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87 identifies Jonah as «ذَا النُّونِ».

Nūn is an established Arabic name for a fish.

Dhā Al-Nūn and ṣāḥib al-ḥūt can therefore refer to the same person and event.

N W N and Ḥ W T are nevertheless different word families.

Nūn should therefore not be counted as a sixth occurrence of «ح و ت».

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87 also describes Jonah’s call in the darkness and his acknowledgment that he had wronged himself.

It helps explain ṣāḥib al-ḥūt in context, without changing the meaning of ḥūt into prayer, repentance, or darkness.

Patience and Jonah’s experience

In Al-Qalam 68:48, «فَاصْبِرْ لِحُكْمِ رَبِّكَ» precedes the reminder of Jonah’s story.

The direct focus of the instruction is therefore patience in awaiting the Lord’s judgment.

Jonah’s story is recalled to illuminate this point.

Al-Qalam 68:49 and Al-Qalam 68:50 also mention the Lord’s favor, His choosing Jonah again, and placing him among the righteous.

«وَلَا تَكُنْ كَصَاحِبِ الْحُوتِ» should therefore not be taken as an absolute, permanent condemnation of Jonah’s character.

It refers to a particular situation in his story, while the following verses describe his outcome.

For lexical study, ḥūt remains the animal’s name. Patience, haste, calling out, and corrected conduct are understood from the other clauses and related verses.

6. Distinctions from related roots and translation proposals

Ḥ W T (ḥūt) and S M K (samak)

Samak is an established Arabic name for fish.

Ḥūt likewise denotes fish, with large size more prominent in some uses.

Early dictionaries sometimes explain ḥūt through samak.

The two words therefore overlap in naming aquatic animals.

This proximity should not produce an absolute distinction whereby samak always means a small fish and ḥūt always a large whale.

The Qur’an contains forms of ḥūt and ḥītān; the noun samak meaning fish does not occur in its text.

Ḥ W T (ḥūt) and N W N (nūn)

Nūn can mean a fish in Arabic.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87, Dhā Al-Nūn is a title for Jonah.

In Al-Qalam 68:48, the same person is identified as ṣāḥib al-ḥūt.

The titles are related in their referent, but belong to different roots.

The use of nūn as a detached opening letter at the beginning of Al-Qalam also does not automatically mean fish and requires separate examination.

Ḥ W T (ḥūt) and Ṣ Y D (ṣayd)

Ṣayd concerns hunting and game.

Ḥūt names the animal.

Al-Māʾidah 5:96 mentions ṣaydu al-baḥri, sea game or the catch of the sea.

Fish may be an instance of sea game, but ṣayd is not simply a name for fish.

The ruling concerning sea game must therefore be examined within its own text and context. Its scope should not be changed by restricting ḥūt to one kind of animal.

The root «ح و ت» itself also establishes no independent ruling on whether hunting is lawful or forbidden.

Ḥ W T (ḥūt) and B Ḥ R (baḥr)

Baḥr concerns an expanse of water.

Ḥūt names an animal living in that environment.

Al-Kahf 18:61 and Al-Kahf 18:63 each contain both ḥūt and baḥr.

The environment’s name and the animal’s name are nevertheless distinct.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:163 too, the townspeople live beside the sea, and the fish are mentioned in relation to their lives.

Ḥūt in these verses does not mean the sea itself or the movement of waves.

Study of B Ḥ R

Ḥ W T (ḥāta) and Ḥ W M (ḥāma)

«حَامَ الطَّائِرُ حَوْلَ الشَّيْءِ» concerns a bird circling around something.

Ḥāta al-ṭāʾiru is also used in this field.

The verbs are close in meaning, but their roots differ.

Lexicographic reports sometimes explain one through the other.

Their shared sense of circling does not establish that every use of ḥūt means going around something.

Ḥ W T (ḥāwata) and R W GH (rāwagha)

Rāwagha concerns changing course and evasion.

Arabic also explains ḥāwata through this meaning.

That use concerns the behavior of a person or creature avoiding a direct encounter.

In the Qur’an, ḥūt is solely a noun naming the animal.

Evasion should therefore not be transferred without evidence into the fish’s name in the stories of Moses or Jonah.

Ḥ W T (ḥūt) and Ḥ W Ṭ (iḥāṭah)

Ḥ W Ṭ concerns surrounding, encompassing, and protecting.

The verbal branch of Ḥ W T can concern circling around something.

Their spatial images may seem close, but they are independent roots.

Circling around something does not necessarily mean completely encompassing or surrounding it.

Ḥūt in the Qur’an therefore cannot be translated as an encompassing or besieging creature by treating it as a word from Ḥ W Ṭ.

Study of Ḥ W Ṭ

Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:163, ḥītānuhum should be translated as their fish.

In Al-Kahf 18:61, their fish is appropriate for ḥūtahumā, with the clarification that the noun is singular and its pronoun dual.

In Al-Kahf 18:63, al-ḥūta can be rendered the fish or that fish.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142, al-ḥūtu should be rendered the fish. The size needed to swallow a human comes from the verb and context, rather than a definite zoological identification.

In Al-Qalam 68:48, ṣāḥib al-ḥūt can be explained as the companion of the fish, or someone associated with the fish’s story. In direct translation it is preferable to preserve the original expression and explain that Jonah is intended.

Outside the Qur’an, ḥāta al-ṭāʾiru can be rendered “the bird circled around something”; ḥawatān as circling around something; and ḥāwatanī as “he evaded me or led me astray.”

Conclusion

Brief root meaning: Fish; in non-Qur’anic uses of this family, circling and changing course.

In early dictionaries, ḥūt can be a general name for fish or be used particularly for a large fish.

Ibn Fāris identifies movement and changing course as the family’s underlying principle. This does not make the Qur’anic name for the fish itself mean evasion or circling.

All five Qur’anic occurrences are nouns concerning an aquatic animal.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:163, the fish associated with the coastal town appear in the setting of its people’s trial.

In Al-Kahf 18:61 and Al-Kahf 18:63, the fish of Moses and his companion takes its way into the sea, an event that matters in the continuation of their journey.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142, the fish swallows Jonah; Al-Qalam 68:48 identifies Jonah as ṣāḥib al-ḥūt.

The stories and the animal’s role differ, while the lexical meaning of ḥūt in each remains within the field of fish and aquatic animals.

The direct, defensible meaning in the Qur’anic uses is fish, without assigning a definite species, size, or zoological characteristic that the verse does not state.

The fish’s movement into the sea, swallowing Jonah, the townspeople’s Sabbath trial, and the reminder of Jonah’s experience all come from the verbs and complete constructions of the verses, rather than the name ḥūt itself.

Fish is therefore an appropriate English equivalent, singular or plural as required, in Forqan’s translation.

A lexical explanation may examine this name’s range, including large fish and large aquatic creatures described under it in early language. It should not, however, be restricted without evidence to a whale of a particular species.

This distinction preserves the clear Arabic meaning and prevents exegetical narratives, zoological hypotheses, or symbolic readings from replacing the Qur’anic word itself.
