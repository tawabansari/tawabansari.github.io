Qur’anic Root Q M L (ق م ل)

Lice and small animal pests; smallness, lowliness, and infestation

1. Basic meaning and linguistic background

ق م ل (Q M L) is the heading of a lexical family. Its words, such as قَمْل (qaml) and قُمَّل (qummal), name creatures; the root itself is not an animal name. This article examines the Qur’anic word qummal, distinguishing it from qaml, lice, and other derivatives of the family.

Poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā supports early usage of qummal, but does not by itself identify the precise creature or establish an exact date for the word’s emergence.

Classical Arabic records distinctions between قَمْل (qaml) and قُمَّل (qummal). Qaml is the familiar name for lice, whereas qummal has also been applied to small creatures such as grain pests, immature locusts, and tick-like animals. Some lexicographers allow both forms to share the meaning “lice.”

Ibn Fāris identifies smallness and lowliness as the family’s semantic core. This analysis accords with names for small creatures, descriptions of people regarded as insignificant, and usages concerning the appearance of small leaves.

Qur’anic word examined: الْقُمَّلَ (al-qummala), occurring once, in Al-Aʿrāf 7:133. “Small animal pests” is an explanatory translation intended to preserve uncertainty about its referent.

Other Arabic forms include قَمْل (qaml), lice; قَمْلَة (qamlah), one louse; قُمَّلَة (qummalah), one creature called qummal; قَمِلَ (qamila), became infested with lice; and قَمَلِيّ (qamalī), a person regarded as lowly or insignificant. These forms do not occur in the commonly transmitted Qur’anic text.

2. Evidence from early Arabic usage

Lice and infestation

قَمِلَ رَأْسُهُ (qamila raʾsuhu) means that his head became infested with lice; qaml names the creature collectively and qamlah a single louse. In غُلٌّ قَمِلٌ (ghullun qamilun), the adjective qamil describes an infested restraint or shackle. Lexicons explain the expression by reference to rawhide bonds with hair still attached.

A poetic witness attributed to al-Aʿshā

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, in the context of captivity and the suffering of a people’s children, states:

قَوْمًا تُعَالِجُ قُمَّلًا أَبْنَاؤُهُمْ وَسَلَاسِلًا أُجُدًا وَبَابًا مُؤْصَدَا

“A people whose children struggle with qummal, strong chains, and a closed door.”

The juxtaposition of qummal with chains and a closed door foregrounds the creatures’ torment in captivity. This witness attributed to al-Aʿshā supports early usage of the word but does not identify the precise creature.

Pests of plants and crops

Lexicons describe قُمَّلَةُ الزَّرْعِ (qummalatu z-zarʿ) as a small crop pest. Ibn al-Sikkīt is reported as describing qummal damaging a young ear of grain, so that the stalk grows but the ear fails to develop properly. Grain pests, immature locusts, and tick-like creatures also occur in explanations of the word.

These are differing lexical and exegetical identifications, not a definitive species identification based on modern zoology. They establish neither that all referents are one species nor that all are intended simultaneously in the verse.

Smallness and abundance

رَجُلٌ قَمَلِيٌّ (rajulun qamaliyyun) describes a person deemed insignificant. أَقْمَلَ الرِّمْثُ (aqmala r-rimthu) describes small leaves appearing on the desert plant rimth and is explained through resemblance to tiny creatures. Lexicons also record قَمِلَ الْقَوْمُ (qamila l-qawmu) for a people increasing in number. Smallness, lowliness, and abundance should not be imposed on every derivative without attention to form and context.

3. The Qur’anic use of qummal

The Qur’an uses this word in the story of Moses and Pharaoh, among the signs sent upon Pharaoh’s people:

فَأَرسَلنا عَلَيهِمُ الطّوفانَ وَالجَرادَ وَالقُمَّلَ وَالضَّفادِعَ وَالدَّمَ ءايٰتٍ مُفَصَّلٰتٍ فَاستَكبَروا وَكانوا قَومًا مُجرِمينَ

Fa-arsalnā ʿalayhimu ṭ-ṭūfāna wa-l-jarāda wa-l-qummala wa-ḍ-ḍafādiʿa wa-d-dama āyātin mufaṣṣalātin fa-stakbarū wa-kānū qawman mujrimīn.

“So We sent upon them the flood, locusts, small animal pests, frogs, and blood as distinct signs. Yet they acted arrogantly and were a wrongdoing people.”

Al-A'raf 7:133

الْقُمَّلَ (al-qummala) is an accusative noun coordinated with the other objects of أَرْسَلْنَا (arsalnā), We sent.

Qummal has collective reference here. قُمَّلَة (qummalah) is recorded as a unit noun. قَامِل (qāmil) has also been proposed by morphological analogy in some analyses, rather than being established as an actually attested singular.

Qummal: lice or a crop pest?

Lexical and exegetical explanations propose several principal identifications:

  • Lice infesting human bodies and clothing.
  • Small pests harming grain and crops.
  • Immature locusts whose wings have not yet developed.
  • Small tick-like creatures troubling humans or livestock.

Each identification has support in the Arabic lexical tradition, but the verse itself does not specify the creatures’ bodily features, habitat, or feeding behavior.

The separate mention of جَرَاد (jarād) and قُمَّل (qummal) distinguishes two designations in the verse. The distinction could concern two types of creature or two developmental stages. Their juxtaposition alone therefore does not rule out the interpretation “immature locusts.”

The reduction in crops in Al-Aʿrāf 7:130 is consistent with a crop pest, but does not establish that identification: the signs in this context are not confined to farmland.

Reports of variant readings attribute الْقَمْلَ (al-qamla), with fatḥah on the qāf and sukūn on the mīm, to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī. It refers to familiar lice. This is not among the ten accepted canonical readings, but it provides evidence for the history of this interpretation of the word.

Ḥafṣ’s transmission from ʿĀṣim reads الْقُمَّلَ (al-qummala). “Lice” is a defensible rendering, but restricting it to human head lice lacks sufficient support.

4. Qummal among the signs sent upon Pharaoh’s people

Before listing these signs, the Qur’an states:

وَلَقَد أَخَذنا ءالَ فِرعَونَ بِالسِّنينَ وَنَقصٍ مِنَ الثَّمَرٰتِ لَعَلَّهُم يَذَّكَّرونَ

Wa-laqad akhadhnā āla Firʿawna bi-s-sinīna wa-naqṣin mina th-thamarāti laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūn.

“We certainly afflicted Pharaoh’s people with years of hardship and a reduction in crops, so that they might take heed.”

Al-A'raf 7:130

Economic hardship and reduced crops are presented here as an occasion for Pharaoh’s people to awaken and reconsider.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:132, however, they declare that whatever sign Moses brings, they will not believe him.

The Qur’an also describes their response after the five signs were sent:

وَلَمّا وَقَعَ عَلَيهِمُ الرِّجزُ قالوا يٰموسَى ادعُ لَنا رَبَّكَ بِما عَهِدَ عِندَكَ لَئِن كَشَفتَ عَنَّا الرِّجزَ لَنُؤمِنَنَّ لَكَ وَلَنُرسِلَنَّ مَعَكَ بَنى إِسرٰءيلَ

Wa-lammā waqaʿa ʿalayhimu r-rijzu qālū yā Mūsā dʿu lanā rabbaka bi-mā ʿahida ʿindaka laʾin kashafta ʿannā r-rijza la-nuʾminanna laka wa-la-nursilanna maʿaka banī Isrāʾīl.

“When the affliction fell upon them, they said: Moses, call upon your Lord for us by what He has pledged to you. If you remove this affliction from us, we will certainly believe you and send the Children of Israel with you.”

Al-A'raf 7:134

The next verse, Al-Aʿrāf 7:135, describes their breaking the pledge after the affliction was removed.

In this passage, qummal is part of events revealing the powerlessness of Pharaoh’s order before phenomena of creation.

A creature considered insignificant because of its size can, when it multiplies widely, threaten human comfort, livestock well-being, or a community’s food resources.

The verse does not explain how qummal multiplied, how long the infestation lasted, or the extent of the damage. Nor does the conjunction وَ (wa), and, establish the precise chronological order or intervals between the five signs.

Qummal is not intrinsically a name for divine punishment. Its role as a sign and affliction comes from the context of Pharaoh’s story. Similarity to this event does not justify treating every infestation or insect outbreak as divine punishment.

5. Comparison with related roots and words

Q M L (qummal) and J R D (jarād): small pests and locusts

Jarād is the familiar Arabic name for locusts. They can move in great swarms and consume vegetation over extensive areas.

Lexical usages of qummal refer to smaller creatures that may harm human or animal bodies or different parts of crops.

Both words appear together in Al-Aʿrāf 7:133. Jarād has a clearer lexical referent; the precise identification of qummal remains disputed.

If qummal denotes immature locusts, the two designations distinguish developmental stages. If it means lice or grain pests, they refer to different groups of creatures.

Q M L (qummal) and D B B (dābbah): a creature’s name and a general designation

Dābbah is a broad designation for creatures that move, walk, or crawl upon the ground.

Unlike dābbah, qummal is not a general name for all small creatures; it has been applied to particular groups of small pests.

Translating qummal simply as “moving creatures” or “animals,” without further qualification, therefore makes its range too broad.

Qaml, burghūth, and qurād

Arabic قَمْل (qaml) names lice, بُرْغُوث (burghūth) a flea, and قُرَاد (qurād) ticks.

These creatures are not biologically identical, although small size, parasitic life, and causing discomfort are shared by many of them.

Some early explanations identify qummal with fleas or tick-like creatures; these senses must not be generalized to every occurrence of qaml.

Using a scientific name or naming a particular species in Qur’anic translation without decisive evidence can also narrow the word excessively.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

“Small animal pests” is proposed for الْقُمَّلَ (al-qummala) in Al-Aʿrāf 7:133. This is an explanatory, cautious equivalent preserving the disputed referent, not a scientific name or a claim to cover every historical usage exactly.

“Lice” is a common equivalent with lexical support, but remains one among several defensible interpretations.

“Grain pests” and “immature locusts” also follow particular lexical explanations, although conclusively selecting either as the verse’s referent is difficult.

For non-Qur’anic قَمْل (qaml), “lice” is appropriate; قَمْلَة (qamlah) means “a louse.”

7. Conclusion

Within Q M L, qaml names lice while the referent of qummal is disputed. Other derivatives express infestation, smallness, lowliness, or increase in number.

The Qur’an uses qummal among the signs sent upon Pharaoh’s people. Lice, grain pests, immature locusts, and tick-like creatures are recorded identifications in Arabic.

Since the Qur’an does not specify the creatures’ precise features, the translation need not be restricted to one species.

Proposed definition for Forqan: Qummal in Al-Aʿrāf 7:133 names pests whose precise identity is disputed; the proposed explanatory rendering is “small animal pests.” Within Q M L, qaml denotes lice, while other derivatives, according to form and context, also concern infestation, smallness, and lowliness.
