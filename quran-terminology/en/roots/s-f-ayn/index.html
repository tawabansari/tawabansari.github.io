The Arabic root S F ʿ has two recognized semantic branches: first, forcefully taking hold of something, seizing and pulling it; second, dusky coloration, particularly black tinged with red, and marks appearing on skin or other surfaces.

In the first branch, «سَفَعَ بِنَاصِيَتِهِ» means taking someone by the forelock and pulling it. In the second, sufʿah denotes darkness or a mark that may appear on the face or other surfaces through fire, sunlight, or other causes.

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as «لَنَسْفَعًا» in Al-ʿAlaq 96:15. The verse warns someone who prevents another from worshipping that, unless he desists, his forelock will be forcibly seized.

Forceful seizing and pulling have stronger lexical and contextual support in this verse. Blackening or marking is also recorded in Arabic, but preferring that interpretation here requires independent evidence.

1. The root in early Arabic

Safʿ: forceful seizing and pulling

Arabic lexical accounts explain «سَفَعَ بِنَاصِيَتِهِ» as taking and pulling a person’s forelock.

Three verbs explain this usage: «أَخَذَ», «قَبَضَ», and «جَذَبَ»—taking, grasping, and pulling.

For example, «سَفَعَ بِنَاصِيَةِ الْفَرَسِ» describes someone who takes a horse by its forelock to control it.

Taking the forelock here is more than touching the head or resting a hand on it: the action involves restraint, pulling, or force.

Grasping and pulling are not precisely the same action. Someone may grip a forelock firmly without yet pulling in any direction. Pulling it, in turn, may involve pain, coercion, or humiliation.

The semantic core of this branch may therefore be expressed as:

Taking a firm, forceful hold of something, particularly a body part or forelock, with the possibility of pulling and restraining it.

Arabic also records «سَفَعَ بِيَدِهِ» for taking someone by the hand.

The verb is thus not restricted to the forelock, although safʿ bi-al-nāṣiyah is a clear, established expression.

A poetic witness for taking the forelock

A verse attributed to Ḥumayd ibn Thawr, a poet who lived across the transition to Islam, reads:

قَوْمٌ إِذَا سَمِعُوا الصَّرِيخَ رَأَيْتَهُمْ
مِنْ بَيْنِ مُلْجِمِ مُهْرِهِ أَوْ سَافِعِ

qawmun idhā samiʿū al-ṣarīkha raʾaytahum / min bayni muljimi muhrihi aw sāfiʿi

“A people who, when they hear a cry for help, you see either bridling their horses or taking hold of their forelocks.”

The poet describes people preparing to move upon hearing an appeal for help.

Some are bridling their horses; others hold the forelocks to control them or keep them ready for bridling.

Sāfiʿ is an active participle here, identifying someone holding a horse’s forelock.

The witness matters because it illustrates the expression independently of the Qur’anic verse.

The attribution to Ḥumayd ibn Thawr is recorded in poetic transmissions and literary commentaries. He lived through the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods.

The date of this particular line is nevertheless uncertain, so it cannot be treated as securely pre-Islamic evidence. Its preservation in early poetic and lexical traditions supports the meaning of grasping and restraining by the forelock.

Safʿ here does not mean burning or blackening. The specific action is taking hold of the horse’s forelock.

Safʿ: dusky coloration and black tinged with red

The root’s other branch concerns color.

Sufʿah denotes darkness or blackness; some more specific definitions describe black tinged with red.

Asfaʿ consequently describes something of this coloration, and safʿāʾ is its feminine form.

The description is recorded for human skin, birds, stones, and traces left by fire.

«سَفَعَتْهُ النَّارُ» describes fire affecting someone’s skin and changing its color.

«سَفَعَتْهُ الشَّمْسُ» can likewise describe sunlight affecting the face and darkening the skin.

These usages concern a change of color through contact or an external influence, rather than grasping and pulling by hand.

Darkness need not result from burning. A bird’s natural plumage or a dark patch of skin can also be described through this family.

Sufʿah should therefore not always be translated as a burn.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness for dark coloration

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā contains:

أَثَافِيَّ سُفْعًا فِي مُعَرَّسِ مِرْجَلٍ
وَنُؤْيًا كَجِذْمِ الْحَوْضِ لَمْ يَتَثَلَّمِ

athāfiyya sufʿan fī muʿarrasi mirjalin / wa-nuʾyan ka-jidhmi al-ḥawḍi lam yatathallami

“Hearthstones darkened by smoke and fire where a cooking pot rested, and a trench around the tent, remaining like the unbroken edge of a water basin.”

Athāfī is the plural of uthfiyyah: stones placed to support a cooking pot over a fire.

Sufʿan describes those stones. Their darkness comes from smoke and fire at the cooking place.

The word here expresses dark coloration and the effect of fire, rather than seizing, pulling, or striking.

The witness belongs to the well-known Muʿallaqah of Zuhayr, a pre-Islamic poet, and survives in established transmissions of his poetry. It is important evidence for the early color sense, while the precise dating and transmission of an individual line remain distinct from the attribution of the poem as a whole.

This witness shows that sufʿ could describe the visible darkness of objects in early Arabic.

It does not establish that taking the forelock developed historically from the blackening of stones.

Do the two branches share one origin?

Some lexical explanations have sought a connection between taking the forelock and changing color: a forceful grip, for instance, may leave a bodily mark or place someone in a humiliating position.

Such a connection is insufficient to establish a historical derivation.

A lexical analysis of this family distinguishes two semantic bases: color, and taking something with the hand.

The distinction matters. Darkness and marks stand out in the color branch; grasping and pulling in the other.

Both effects may coincide in some actions, but the root’s uses need not all derive from one original image.

Qur’anic analysis should first establish which branch fits the verse’s construction and context.

2. Forms and Qur’anic usage

The root S F ʿ occurs only once in the Qur’an:

لَنَسْفَعًا — Al-ʿAlaq 96:15

The word derives from the basic triliteral verb «سَفَعَ يَسْفَعُ».

Its Qur’anic form is a first-person plural imperfect: “we seize” or “we will seize.”

The initial lām and light emphatic nūn reinforce the threat. In «لَنَسْفَعًا», the light nūn is represented in the written form by an alif and a sign resembling accusative tanwīn; grammatically it is an emphatic nūn attached to a verb, not nominal tanwīn. When stopping on this word, it is pronounced with a final long ā.

It should therefore not be translated as an ordinary verb lacking emphasis.

The subject is expressed in the first-person plural. This grammatical plural alone does not determine how many agents execute the threat or how it is carried out.

Other forms recorded in Arabic include:

سَفَعَ: seized forcefully, pulled, struck, or, in the other branch, darkened something’s color.

يَسْفَعُ: seizes or pulls; the precise sense depends on construction and context.

سَفْع: forceful grasping and pulling; also striking and an effect on color in some uses.

سَافِع: one who seizes or pulls forcefully.

سُفْعَة: dusky coloration or a dark mark.

أَسْفَع: dark-colored, particularly black tinged with red.

سَفْعَاء: the feminine color adjective.

مُسَافَعَة: mutual fighting or striking.

These forms illuminate the root’s semantic range, but none appears in the Qur’an in precisely the form listed here.

Qur’anic analysis must therefore concentrate on «لَنَسْفَعًا», using other senses to clarify the possibilities of Arabic usage.

3. La-nasfaʿan bi-al-nāṣiyah: forcefully taking the forelock

Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 states:

كَلَّا لَئِنْ لَمْ يَنْتَهِ لَنَسْفَعًا بِالنَّاصِيَةِ

kallā laʾin lam yantahi la-nasfaʿan bi-al-nāṣiyah

Al-ʿAlaq 96:15

“No indeed! If he does not desist, We will surely seize him firmly by the forelock.”

The context describes a defiant person who obstructs another.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:9 states:

أَرَأَيْتَ الَّذِي يَنْهَىٰ

a-raʾayta alladhī yanhā

Al-ʿAlaq 96:9

“Have you seen the one who forbids?”

And Al-ʿAlaq 96:10:

عَبْدًا إِذَا صَلَّىٰ

ʿabdan idhā ṣallā

Al-ʿAlaq 96:10

“A servant when he prays?”

The subject is someone preventing another from worshipping.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:13 mentions denial and turning away; Al-ʿAlaq 96:14 asks about his awareness that God sees him.

The warning then follows: unless he desists, his forelock will be forcefully seized.

Why the forelock?

Nāṣiyah denotes the front of the head, especially the front hairline and the hair growing there.

The Persian word for forehead is a natural equivalent, but it does not convey the full bodily precision of nāṣiyah: the forehead usually means the front surface below the hairline.

Taking someone by the forelock can restrict their movement and place them under the holder’s control.

In restraining humans or animals, gripping the hair at the front of the head can also serve to pull and forcibly direct them.

Forcefully taking the forelock and restraining its owner thus gives «لَنَسْفَعًا بِالنَّاصِيَةِ» a clear, natural sense.

The preposition bi- in bi-al-nāṣiyah specifies the point of grasping.

The verse does more than say the person will be held accountable or defeated: it presents the bodily image of taking the forelock.

Taking the forelock and humiliating the arrogant

Earlier in this passage, Al-ʿAlaq 96:6 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:7 describe a human being transgressing because he considers himself self-sufficient.

The conduct of someone seeking to prevent another from worshipping is then introduced.

That person assumes authority over another, attempting to control their behavior and prevent their action.

The warning «لَنَسْفَعًا بِالنَّاصِيَةِ» confronts him with the prospect of being restrained and losing his own freedom of movement.

In context, taking the forelock can therefore also convey humiliation and the collapse of his claim to power.

Humiliating is nevertheless not the root’s sole direct meaning: it is an implication of the forceful action and the person’s place in the account.

A lying, wrongdoing forelock

Al-ʿAlaq 96:16 states:

نَاصِيَةٍ كَاذِبَةٍ خَاطِئَةٍ

nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭiʾah

Al-ʿAlaq 96:16

“A lying, wrongdoing forelock.”

The forelock is given two descriptions: kādhibah and khāṭiʾah.

Grammatically, the descriptions belong to the forelock, while lying and wrongdoing are the conduct and responsibility of its owner.

This is intelligible figurative language: a body part stands for its owner and receives the person’s characteristics.

The image emphasizes the connection between the warning and the person’s conduct: the forelock seized belongs to someone described as lying and wrongdoing.

It does not imply that the hair itself speaks or is biologically responsible for lies and sin.

Nor should nāṣiyah be replaced in the basic translation by a scientific term for brain anatomy or a decision-making center. Any such discussion belongs to a separate inquiry into Qur’anic language and biological knowledge, rather than the lexical meaning of nāṣiyah or safʿ.

Challenging power and summoning companions

Al-ʿAlaq 96:17 states:

فَلْيَدْعُ نَادِيَهُ

fa-l-yadʿu nādiyah

Al-ʿAlaq 96:17

“Then let him call his assembly.”

And Al-ʿAlaq 96:18:

سَنَدْعُ الزَّبَانِيَةَ

sa-nadʿu al-zabāniyah

Al-ʿAlaq 96:18

“We will call the agents of punishment.”

These verses continue the earlier warning.

The person may summon his group and supporters; against this stands the summoning of divine agents.

Nādiyahu refers to his assembly and gathering, while al-zabāniyah identifies another group in a context of punishment.

The contrast shows that social power and supporters do not guarantee escape from accountability.

Safʿ itself, however, does not mean summoning agents of punishment. That information comes from the following verses.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:19 also commands:

كَلَّا لَا تُطِعْهُ وَاسْجُدْ وَاقْتَرِبْ

kallā lā tuṭiʿhu wa-sjud wa-qtarib

Al-ʿAlaq 96:19

“No indeed! Do not obey him; prostrate yourself and draw near.”

The passage ends with more than a warning to the obstructer: the person being obstructed is commanded not to obey him and to continue worshipping.

The sequence places the threat of taking the forelock against conduct intended to prevent another’s worship.

4. Seizing, pulling, or blackening: which meaning best fits?

Lexical and exegetical accounts offer several interpretations of «لَنَسْفَعًا» in Al-ʿAlaq 96:15.

First reading: forceful seizing and pulling

On this reading, safʿ means taking hold with force and pulling.

The construction bi-al-nāṣiyah fits this sense clearly: Arabic recognizes taking the forelock as a way to control a person or animal.

The poetic witness concerning a horse’s forelock supports the same usage.

Grasping the forelock in this sense can serve to drag someone and bring them under control.

The verse itself does not specify a destination. Whether the person is dragged into fire, or when and how the threat is fulfilled, is not established by «لَنَسْفَعًا» alone.

“We will drag him into the fire” should therefore not replace the verb in translation unless that addition is separately identified and supported by Qur’anic evidence.

Second reading: blackening or marking

In its color branch, safʿ can refer to changing or darkening a color.

Some early explanations consequently understand «لَنَسْفَعًا بِالنَّاصِيَةِ» as blackening or marking the forelock.

The Arabic color branch gives this possibility a lexical basis.

Zuhayr’s verse about fire-darkened stones likewise shows that darkness is a genuine sense within the family.

The verse, however, specifies neither a color nor an instrument of branding or marking.

The expression for taking the forelock, by comparison, has direct, established Arabic evidence.

The mere presence of blackening in a dictionary is therefore insufficient to prefer it in Al-ʿAlaq 96:15.

Third reading: striking

Arabic records «سَفَعَ وَجْهَهُ بِيَدِهِ» for striking a face with the hand, and «سَفَعَهُ بِالْعَصَا» for striking someone with a stick.

Striking is thus a linguistically possible sense to consider.

In the Qur’anic wording, however, the construction with the forelock points more readily to grasping and pulling the front hair than to slapping the face.

The direct evidence for taking the forelock supports preferring this reading to striking; the mere absence of a named instrument does not, by itself, rule out the latter.

Striking consequently has weaker contextual support here than forcefully taking the forelock.

Another verse about taking the forelock

Al-Raḥmān 55:41 states:

يُعْرَفُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ بِسِيمَاهُمْ فَيُؤْخَذُ بِالنَّوَاصِي وَالْأَقْدَامِ

yuʿrafu al-mujrimūna bi-sīmāhum fa-yuʾkhadhu bi-al-nawāṣī wa-al-aqdām

Al-Raḥmān 55:41

“The guilty are recognized by their marks; then they are seized by the forelocks and feet.”

The verb yuʾkhadhu, from A KH DH, expresses taking hold of the forelocks and feet.

Although safʿ and akhdh have different roots, this verse shows that taking the forelock is a concrete Qur’anic image of restraint and capture.

It supports the grasping interpretation in Al-ʿAlaq 96:15.

The similar imagery does not mean that both verses necessarily describe all the details of one event.

Al-Raḥmān 55:41 presents the guilty on the Day of Judgment; Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 warns someone who persists in obstructing another.

The semantic connection is clear, while the details of the threat’s fulfillment require a broader study of the verses.

5. Comparison with related roots

S F ʿ and A KH DH: general taking and forceful seizing

Akhadha concerns taking and has a broad semantic range.

A person may take something gently, receive a gift, pick up property, or bring someone under control.

Safʿ in its grasping sense, however, commonly involves force, pulling, and intensity.

Hūd 11:56 uses ākhidh for taking living creatures by the forelock, expressing God’s authority and control.

In Al-ʿAlaq 96:15, «لَنَسْفَعًا» foregrounds forceful taking of the forelock.

Both roots can concern taking, but akhdh by itself does not specify severity or violence.

This is a tendency dependent on usage; it does not mean akhdh can never involve force.

S F ʿ and N Z ʿ: grasping and pulling out

N Z ʿ concerns pulling out, separating, and removing something from its place.

Taking the forelock in safʿ bi-al-nāṣiyah may include pulling, but the expression does not necessarily mean tearing the hair from the head.

Nazʿ can emphasize extraction and separation, whereas safʿ also fits a firm grip and restraint.

«لَنَسْفَعًا» should therefore not be translated “We will tear out his forelock” without supporting evidence.

The hair may remain attached while the action still constitutes safʿ.

S F ʿ and Ṣ F ʿ: grasping and slapping

Ṣafaʿa concerns slapping or striking with the hand.

Safʿ too approaches hand-striking in certain usages.

The roots are nevertheless distinct.

Ṣafʿ foregrounds the hand’s blow; safʿ in safʿ bi-al-nāṣiyah concerns grasping and pulling.

Their phonetic similarity does not establish that slapping is intended in Al-ʿAlaq 96:15.

S F ʿ and S W D: dusky coloration and blackness

S W D concerns blackness and becoming black.

The color branch of S F ʿ also involves darkness, but lexical descriptions specify black tinged with red and particular color marks.

Not every meaning of sufʿah can therefore be equated with the general word sawād.

Nor does this color branch make blackening the necessary Qur’anic meaning of «لَنَسْفَعًا».

The construction with the forelock and the warning context favor forceful seizing in translation.

S F ʿ and SH F ʿ: two different roots

SH F ʿ begins with shīn and concerns pairing, adding one thing to another, and uses associated with intercession.

S F ʿ begins with sīn and is used for forceful seizing and dusky coloration.

Their written forms resemble one another, but their meanings and Qur’anic uses differ.

«لَنَسْفَعًا» thus has no direct connection with intercession; visual similarity must not merge the two families.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

A translation of «لَنَسْفَعًا» in Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 should preserve forceful taking, the Arabic emphasis, and the connection with the forelock.

A natural rendering is:

“No indeed! If he does not desist, We will surely seize him firmly by the forelock.”

This is a proposed rendering within the root study; installing it does not alter the translation on the verse page.

“Forehead” is fluent in Persian, but a more precise explanation should identify nāṣiyah as the front of the head, particularly its hairline and forelock.

To foreground the bodily image, an explanation may say:

“We firmly grasp and pull his forelock.”

This makes the relation between grasping and pulling explicit; for the basic rendering, the shorter “We seize him firmly by the forelock” is suitable.

“We drag him by force” conveys part of the possible meaning, but omits the point of grasping and is insufficient as a complete verse translation.

“We blacken his face,” “We brand his forehead,” and “We strike his head,” although related to some lexical uses, are not preferred for the basic rendering here.

“We drag him into the fire” likewise adds a destination not specified in Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 itself.

Suitable equivalents for non-Qur’anic forms include:

  • سَفَعَ بِنَاصِيَتِهِ: took him by the forelock and pulled.
  • سَفَعَ بِيَدِهِ: firmly took him by the hand.
  • سَافِع: one who grasps or pulls forcefully, according to context.
  • سَفَعَ وَجْهَهُ: struck his face.
  • سُفْعَة: dusky coloration or a dark mark.
  • أَسْفَع: dark-colored, particularly black tinged with red.
  • سَفْعَاء: the feminine of the same color adjective.
  • سَفَعَتْهُ النَّارُ: fire affected his skin and darkened it.

For the body part mentioned in the verse and its use in the language of authority and restraint, see Forqan’s separate study of N Ṣ Y and nāṣiyah.

Study of N Ṣ Y

Conclusion

S F ʿ has two principal recognized branches in Arabic: forceful seizing and pulling, and dusky coloration or dark marks on skin and objects.

Early poetry supplies examples of both. The line attributed to Ḥumayd ibn Thawr depicts taking a horse by its forelock; Zuhayr’s line describes darkened hearthstones.

These witnesses establish two actual Arabic usages, but do not sufficiently demonstrate a single historical meaning from which both developed.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-ʿAlaq 96:15.

«لَنَسْفَعًا بِالنَّاصِيَةِ» concerns forcefully taking the forelock. The action can restrain and drag someone; in the warning to a defiant person, it depicts the collapse of his claim to power and his capture.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:16 further identifies the person threatened as lying and wrongdoing.

Blackening or marking remains a possible lexical reading because of the color branch, but it has weaker direct support here than grasping and pulling the forelock.

The final definition should therefore preserve the distinction:

S F ʿ concerns firm grasping and forceful pulling in one branch, and dusky coloration and dark marks, particularly black tinged with red, in the other. Forceful taking of the forelock is preferred in the Qur’anic usage at Al-ʿAlaq 96:15.

In context, someone seeking to prevent another’s worship is himself threatened with restraint and accountability.

The verse’s message begins with the bodily image of taking the forelock and extends to a broader issue: human power and social influence neither confer a right to obstruct others’ path to guidance nor remove responsibility for one’s conduct.
