Lodging in the throat, difficulty swallowing, constriction, and distressing restriction

Arabic gh ṣ ṣ concerns food, drink, or something else lodging in the throat and becoming difficult to swallow. Ghaṣṣa describes this obstruction or difficulty. Ghuṣṣah can name what causes it or the state itself.

Ghaṣaṣ denotes the condition, ghāṣṣ a person experiencing it, and aghaṣṣa causing another to experience it.

Extended uses concern a place becoming crowded and constricted, distress, and sorrow.

These share an image of constriction and difficult passage without being identical: food lodged in the throat, difficulty drinking, a crowded place, and inward sorrow are distinct uses.

The root occurs once in the Quran, as ghuṣṣatin in Al-Muzzammil 73:13:

وَطَعَامًا ذَا غُصَّةٍ وَعَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

wa-ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin wa-ʿadhāban alīmā

Al-Muzzammil 73:13

“And food that catches in the throat, and painful punishment.”

Ghuṣṣah characterizes food difficult and distressing to swallow. The image contrasts with the worldly ease of people who enjoyed abundance yet denied the truth.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Ghaṣṣa bi-l-ṭaʿām describes food lodging in someone’s throat so that they cannot readily swallow it.

For example, a large or dry morsel insufficiently chewed may lodge in the throat. That difficulty can be called ghuṣṣah.

It differs from food merely tasting unpleasant.

Bitter, salty, or unpleasant food may be swallowed easily; pleasant-tasting food may lodge in the throat.

The salient problem is obstruction and difficult passage, not necessarily taste or nutritional quality.

The root is not restricted to solid food.

Ghaṣṣa bi-l-māʾ describes throat obstruction or difficulty swallowing while drinking water.

Lexicons record both ghaṣaṣtu and ghaṣiṣtu, and the verbal nouns ghaṣṣ and ghaṣaṣ.

Some lexical accounts distinguish food, water, saliva, and other causes, but those boundaries are not uniform across usage.

The family should therefore be defined through something lodging in the throat and difficulty swallowing, rather than one specific food or drink.

An early witness: ghaṣṣān in ʿAdī ibn Zayd’s poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd al-ʿIbādī says:

لَوْ بِغَيْرِ الْمَاءِ حَلْقِي شَرِقٌ

law bi-ghayri al-māʾi ḥalqī shariqun

كُنْتُ كَالْغَصَّانِ بِالْمَاءِ اعْتِصَارِي

kuntu ka-l-ghaṣṣāni bi-l-māʾi iʿtiṣārī

Interpretive rendering of the image:

“Had something other than water caught in my throat, water would have been my recourse for swallowing it.” The implied contrast is: if water itself causes the obstruction, where can I turn?

Al-ghaṣṣān denotes someone with something caught in the throat.

The literary image treats water as the expected means of relief, which itself becomes the source of distress. The point is the failure of an expected refuge, rather than instruction on treating a physical obstruction.

Literary accounts place the verse in ʿAdī ibn Zayd’s address to al-Nuʿmān ibn al-Mundhir: the person from whom he expected protection and release had become the source of harm.

The bodily image thus expresses distress and disappointed reliance on an expected protector.

Early grammatical and literary works, including Sībawayh’s Kitāb, cite the verse; later works also use it as evidence.

Although ʿAdī is a recognized pre-Islamic poet, his name alone does not establish this verse’s precise date. Its familiar attribution and early transmission make it an important witness to ghaṣṣān and throat obstruction in early Arabic.

The witness illustrates two points.

First, obstruction involving food or water was a familiar bodily meaning.

Second, that situation could illustrate human distress and the loss of a way out.

The analogy does not establish sorrow or despair as the root’s historical origin. Physical meaning and figurative use should remain distinct.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Recognized Arabic forms include:

  • Ghaṣṣa / yaghaṣṣu: something lodged in the throat, or someone had difficulty swallowing.
  • Ghaṣaṣtu / ghaṣiṣtu: recorded first-person forms with the doubled consonant separated.
  • Ghaṣṣ and ghaṣaṣ: verbal nouns for throat obstruction and difficulty swallowing.
  • Ghuṣṣah: something lodged in the throat; also the obstructed state or difficulty swallowing.
  • Ghuṣaṣ: plural of ghuṣṣah; distressing obstructions.
  • Ghāṣṣ: someone with food or drink caught in the throat.
  • Ghaṣṣān: a person experiencing throat obstruction.
  • Aghaṣṣa / yughiṣṣu: caused another to experience it.
  • Ighṣāṣ: causing obstruction or difficulty swallowing.
  • Ghaṣṣa al-makānu bi-ahlihi: the place became full and constricted with people.
  • Aghaṣṣa al-makāna: caused a place to become crowded or constricted.

Only ghuṣṣatin occurs in the Quran.

The verb ghaṣṣa, ghāṣṣ, ghaṣṣān, and other derivatives are recognized in Arabic but have no separate Quranic occurrence.

The form ghuṣṣah

Ghuṣṣah is a feminine noun from the doubled root gh ṣ ṣ.

A doubled root has identical second and third radicals; here the two ṣāds appear as a doubled consonant in many forms.

In ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin, ghuṣṣah follows dhā.

Dhā means possessing something or characterized by it.

The phrase thus denotes food associated with or causing throat obstruction.

Ghuṣṣah does not name the food itself, but describes its distressing quality or effect.

Dhā grammatically qualifies ṭaʿāman, and ghuṣṣatin is its genitive complement.

A natural rendering is “food that catches in the throat.”

3. Semantic core and extensions

A. Lodging in the throat

The clearest use concerns food or drink caught in the throat.

Normally people chew and swallow; an obstruction makes the process difficult.

Pressure, pain, or discomfort may accompany the difficulty.

This fits lexical explanations of ghuṣṣah.

The word should not be restricted to a particular medical condition or complete airway obstruction.

Its lexical meaning describes constriction and difficult passage, without diagnosing a cause or specifying severity.

B. Difficulty with drink

Arabic also records ghaṣṣa bi-l-māʾ.

A person drinking water may cough or experience difficulty swallowing it.

This shows the root is not limited to solid morsels.

Some lexical sources also use shariqa for difficulty with water.

The distinction is not absolute, and overlapping uses are recorded.

No exceptionless rule should restrict gh ṣ ṣ to food and sh r q to drink.

C. A place constricted by crowds

Arabic also records ghaṣṣa al-makānu bi-ahlihi.

It describes a place full of people, with little room remaining.

A small room crowded enough to impede movement illustrates it.

Throats and food are not the immediate subject.

Constriction and difficult passage connect the images.

In the bodily use, something obstructs swallowing.

In the spatial use, the crowd restricts room for people and movement.

The connection is understandable but does not establish when or from which use the spatial sense developed.

D. Sorrow and inward distress

Arabic and Persian also use ghuṣṣah for sorrow and distress.

Someone mourning a loved one might feel a constriction in the throat and find speech difficult.

No food or object need actually be lodged there.

Throat obstruction becomes an image of inward suffering.

This does not mean every sorrow involves that bodily sensation or establish one biological mechanism for all grief.

In Persian, ghoṣṣeh commonly means sorrow.

That familiar Persian sense should not automatically replace Quranic Arabic meaning.

In Al-Muzzammil 73:13 the word directly qualifies food, so physical difficulty swallowing is primary.

4. Ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin in the Quran

Al-Muzzammil 73:13

وَطَعَامًا ذَا غُصَّةٍ وَعَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

wa-ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin wa-ʿadhāban alīmā

Al-Muzzammil 73:13

“And food that catches in the throat, and painful punishment.”

This belongs to the surrounding description of punishment.

Al-Muzzammil 73:11 tells Muḥammad to leave affluent deniers to God and grant them a little time.

Al-Muzzammil 73:12 mentions fetters and blazing fire.

Al-Muzzammil 73:13 then describes obstructive food and painful punishment.

Al-Muzzammil 73:14 describes earth and mountains shaking and mountains becoming heaps of shifting sand.

The sequence places the food within the deniers’ punishment and final outcome.

Why food that catches in the throat?

Ordinary food meets bodily needs, relieves hunger, and provides enjoyment.

People generally eat expecting to swallow and benefit from it.

Here food is characterized by something that makes eating itself difficult and distressing.

Imagine a hungry person offered food that catches in the throat whenever they attempt to swallow.

What should meet a need becomes a source of suffering.

This helps explain the phrase.

The illustration does not establish that the Quran specifies food quantities, morsel sizes, or physical composition.

The verse states that the food has an obstructive quality.

The contrast with worldly ease

Al-Muzzammil 73:11 describes the deniers as ulī al-naʿmah, people enjoying ease and abundance.

The following verses describe obstructive food and painful punishment.

The contrast is significant.

Worldly comfort may allow people to eat, drink, and enjoy resources easily.

In the punishment scene, even food catches in the throat.

Worldly abundance therefore does not itself guarantee final well-being.

Ghuṣṣah itself does not mean losing wealth, social deprivation, or punishment for denial; those connections depend on context.

Nor does the verse classify every comfortable person as a denier. It addresses a particular group combining abundance with denial.

Ghuṣṣah and ʿadhāban alīman

The verse coordinates ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin with ʿadhāban alīman.

Ghuṣṣah characterizes the food.

Alīman describes the punishment as painful.

They should not be treated as exactly the same meaning.

The food presents a specific image of suffering while eating; painful punishment is a broader description.

Their coordination does not confine all punishment to throat obstruction.

Each phrase contributes separately to the scene.

5. Other Quranic descriptions of food and drink

Difficulty swallowing

Ibrāhīm 14:17 describes someone sipping a drink but scarcely able to swallow it.

Yusīghuhu belongs to s w gh and concerns getting something down or making it pass readily.

The image relates clearly to ghuṣṣah.

One situation involves difficulty swallowing food or drink.

The contrasting situation involves easy passage through the throat.

Gh ṣ ṣ and s w gh are independent roots, however, and their meanings should not be transferred indiscriminately.

The sipping and swallowing verbs in Ibrāhīm 14:17 together describe the person’s situation.

Ghuṣṣah in Al-Muzzammil 73:13 names the food’s obstructive quality.

The verses describe painful eating and drinking from different angles.

Food without the expected benefit

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:6 identifies food of ḍarīʿ.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7 says it neither fattens nor relieves hunger.

Its result relates to the obstructive-food image without being the same meaning.

Ghuṣṣah emphasizes difficult swallowing.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7 concerns unrelieved hunger and lack of the expected benefit.

Ghuṣṣah should therefore not be translated as food that fails to satisfy hunger.

Food may catch in the throat; other food may lack nutritional benefit without doing so.

These are different situations.

Ghislīn and zaqqūm

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:36 describes ghislīn as food of punishment.

Al-Dukhān 44:43 mentions zaqqūm.

Each term has a specific role in descriptions of punishment.

Some commentaries identify ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin with one of these foods.

Al-Muzzammil 73:13 itself names neither zaqqūm, ghislīn, nor ḍarīʿ.

No such referent should become ghuṣṣah’s definitive meaning without independent evidence.

Ghuṣṣah describes obstruction, not a particular plant or substance.

Different foods could share this quality, but that possibility does not prove all foods mentioned in the verses identical.

A drink easy to swallow

Al-Naḥl 16:66 describes milk as sāʾighan li-l-shāribīn, pleasant and easy for drinkers to swallow.

This clarifies the opposite of difficult swallowing.

People ordinarily benefit from agreeable food and drink that passes without obstruction or distress.

Ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin makes that process difficult.

Ease or difficulty of swallowing can therefore contribute to Quranic descriptions of blessing or punishment.

Sāʾigh and ghuṣṣah remain distinct lexical concepts; neither should be reduced to pleasant or unpleasant taste.

6. Comparison with related roots

Gh ṣ ṣ and sh r q: obstruction and difficulty with drink

Shariqa bi-l-māʾ describes difficulty in the throat when drinking water.

Gh ṣ ṣ can concern both food and drink.

Some lexical accounts tend to explain gh ṣ ṣ through food and sh r q through drink.

The distinction is not absolute.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd’s verse likewise places ghaṣṣān and shariq close together in describing a related condition.

The words overlap, but each use must be understood within its sentence.

Gh ṣ ṣ and s w gh: difficult and easy swallowing

Sāgha and asāgha concern something passing readily or being swallowed easily.

Gh ṣ ṣ concerns difficulty in that process.

Soft, suitable food may pass easily; a dry or unsuitable morsel may lodge in the throat.

The families share an imagery of passage and can describe opposing conditions.

Sāʾigh should not mean merely tasty, nor ghuṣṣah merely bad-tasting.

The relevant distinction is ease or difficulty of swallowing.

Gh ṣ ṣ and kh n q: obstruction and suffocation

Khanq concerns constricting the throat and preventing breathing.

Gh ṣ ṣ concerns something lodging in the throat and difficulty swallowing.

The conditions may overlap but are not identical.

A morsel may obstruct swallowing and cause discomfort; in severe circumstances breathing may also be affected.

Gh ṣ ṣ alone does not establish complete airway obstruction in every use.

“Food that catches in the throat” is therefore more precise in Al-Muzzammil 73:13 than adding “food that chokes one to death” without evidence.

Gh ṣ ṣ and ḍ y q: obstruction and restricted space

Ḍayq concerns narrowness and limited space or capacity.

Gh ṣ ṣ principally concerns the throat.

Its crowding use also describes a place becoming full and constricted.

The roots thus approach one another through an image of restriction.

Not every narrowness is ghuṣṣah.

A corridor may be narrow without a crowd or obstruction.

Ghaṣṣa al-makānu bi-ahlihi describes fullness with people and consequent restriction of room and movement.

Gh ṣ ṣ and k ẓ m: constriction and restraint

Kaẓm concerns holding back or containing; the Quran also uses it for restraining anger.

Gh ṣ ṣ principally concerns something caught in the throat.

Persian may speak of “swallowing sorrow” or “swallowing anger,” but similar expressions do not merge the roots.

Restraining anger controls feeling and response; physical obstruction makes food or drink difficult to pass.

Figurative uses may bring the images together while their principal meanings remain distinct.

Gh ṣ ṣ and gh ṣ b: independent roots

The written forms are similar but the roots differ.

Gh ṣ ṣ concerns throat obstruction and difficult swallowing.

Gh ṣ b concerns forcible, wrongful taking.

An imagined element of pressure in both does not establish historical kinship or identical meanings.

Ghāṣṣ, a person with something caught in the throat, should also not be confused with ghāʾiṣ or ghāṣa from gh w ṣ, concerning diving or immersion.

7. Difficulties in understanding and translation

Does Quranic ghuṣṣah mean sorrow?

Persian ghoṣṣeh commonly means sorrow and distress.

Al-Muzzammil 73:13 uses it in ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin.

Its direct meaning therefore concerns food and difficulty swallowing.

Sorrow is a recognized figurative branch, but should not replace the verse’s physical meaning.

Does obstructive food mean bitter food?

No.

Bitterness concerns taste; obstruction concerns swallowing.

Bitter food may pass easily, while pleasant food may lodge in the throat.

Al-Muzzammil 73:13 does not specify the food’s taste.

Does ghuṣṣah mean complete suffocation?

Not necessarily. It can denote something lodged in the throat and difficulty swallowing.

This may become severe, but complete interruption of breathing is not essential to every use.

A particular medical severity should not be imposed without evidence.

Is the phrase the name of a particular food?

No. It describes food through its obstructive quality.

The phrase alone does not specify composition, shape, or identification with a food named elsewhere.

Is it solely a metaphor for regret?

Because the word can denote sorrow, reflection on inward suffering is possible.

Here, however, it directly qualifies food.

The primary, clear reading is food difficult and distressing to swallow.

That direct meaning should not be reduced to regret, remorse, or psychological sorrow.

Nor should the food’s physical nature in the Hereafter be described with certainty beyond what the Quran states.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ghuṣṣatin in Al-Muzzammil 73:13, “throat obstruction” or “difficulty swallowing” conveys the lexical sense.

The complete phrase should be natural in translation.

Ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin — “food that catches in the throat.”

This is brief and preserves the direct meaning.

The complete verse:

“And food that catches in the throat, and painful punishment.”

For non-Quranic uses:

Ghaṣṣa bi-l-ṭaʿām: food caught in the person’s throat.

Ghaṣṣa bi-l-māʾ: experienced throat obstruction while drinking water.

Ghaṣṣ and ghaṣaṣ: throat obstruction or difficulty swallowing.

Ghuṣṣah: something caught in the throat, or difficulty swallowing; figuratively, sorrow and distress.

Ghuṣaṣ: obstructions; figuratively, sorrows and distressing troubles.

Ghāṣṣ: someone with something caught in the throat.

Ghaṣṣān: a person experiencing throat obstruction.

Aghaṣṣahu: caused that person to experience throat obstruction.

Ghaṣṣa al-makānu bi-ahlihi: the place became full and constricted with people.

Each use requires distinguishing bodily meaning, figurative use, and spatial crowding.

Conclusion

Arabic gh ṣ ṣ concerns food, drink, or something else lodging in the throat and difficulty swallowing.

The sense occurs in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic ʿAdī ibn Zayd. Water causing the difficulty becomes an image of a predicament whose usual refuge has failed.

Other Arabic uses concern a place crowded with people and inward sorrow or distress.

The bodily meaning and figurative extensions should remain distinct.

The Quran uses only ghuṣṣatin in Al-Muzzammil 73:13.

In ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin it describes food difficult and distressing to swallow.

The context is punishment of deniers, alongside fetters, blazing fire, and painful punishment. Their worldly comfort contrasts with their final food.

Ghuṣṣah is neither a particular food’s name nor bitterness, nor a complete physical description of punishment in the Hereafter.

The familiar Persian sorrow sense should not replace the direct Quranic meaning.

The core is something lodging in the throat and becoming difficult to swallow; extended uses concern constriction that impedes passage, movement, or ease.

Al-Muzzammil 73:13 uses that principal sense in food that catches in the throat: eating associated with distress rather than ease.
