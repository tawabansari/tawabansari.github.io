The Qur’anic Root B T K (ب ت ك)

Cutting, separating, and pulling something from its attachment: from physical severance to the ritual marking of livestock

Qur’anic and lexical forms

In Arabic, B T K concerns cutting and separating something from where it is attached. Bataka (بَتَكَ) describes cutting, and some earlier uses concern pulling hair or feathers so that part is torn away. Bitkah (بِتْكَة) names a piece separated by such cutting or plucking.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an, as fa-la-yubattikunna (فَلَيُبَتِّكُنَّ) in Al-Nisāʾ. Satan says he will command people to cut or slit livestock’s ears. This action occurs among his promises to mislead people and make them alter the creation of The God.

Lexically, batk names an act of cutting, rather than worship, a religious custom, or a ruling about permitted and forbidden things. In Qur’anic context, however, cutting livestock’s ears connects with obedience to a false authority and creating religious rules without The God’s authorization. This distinction prevents confining the root to one historical custom or deriving a universal ruling on all cutting and bodily alteration from one specific use.

1. Linguistic forms and earlier Arabic usage

The Qur’anic form is fa-la-yubattikunna, built from form II battaka (بَتَّكَ).

Arabic records the form I bataka with both yabtiku (يَبْتِكُ) and yabtuku (يَبْتُكُ), meaning to cut or separate. Its verbal noun is batk (بَتْك).

Battaka is form II of the same family. Inbataka (اِنْبَتَكَ) and tabattaka (تَبَتَّكَ) can concern becoming cut off or separated. Bitkah is a detached piece, with the plural bitak (بِتَك). Bātik (بَاتِك) and batūk (بَتُوك) also describe something, especially a sword, capable of cutting.

These forms have different grammatical roles. Some express cutting, others the resulting separation, and others describe a cutting instrument. Only the form II verb occurs in the Qur’an; the remaining forms provide Arabic linguistic evidence for the root’s semantic field.

The semantic core: cutting and separation

Ibn Fāris identifies cutting as the root’s basic meaning in Maqāyīs al-Lughah. The same meaning is recorded in Kitāb al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab.

In this family, cutting may be performed with a sharp instrument or result from pulling and tearing something away from its attachment.

For example, gripping a bird’s feathers and pulling until some come away can be described with batk. The detached pieces are called bitak.

What matters here is the separation of part of a whole. Cutting may occur near a root or attachment, but the family’s uses do not all require one physical method.

Some lexical explanations define batk as cutting something off at its root. Others record cutting in a broader sense.

“Uprooting” must therefore not be made an obligatory component of every derivative without examination. The separation of a part is a clearer shared meaning; the particular use determines how it happens.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

The collected poetry of Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, a pre-Islamic poet, includes:

حَتَّى إِذَا مَا هَوَتْ كَفُّ الْغُلَامِ لَهَا
طَارَتْ وَفِي كَفِّهِ مِنْ رِيشِهَا بِتَكُ

ḥattā idhā mā hawat kaffu al-ghulāmi lahā / ṭārat wa-fī kaffihi min rīshihā bitaku

“Until, when the boy’s hand swooped towards her, the bird flew away, with pieces of her feathers remaining in his hand.”

Here bitak denotes portions of feathers separated from the bird’s body.

The bird escapes the person trying to catch her, but some feathers are plucked out and remain in his hand.

This witness shows the root’s meaning in a tangible event. Bitak names neither the bird, the entire wing, nor hunting itself: it denotes detached portions of her feathers.

Plucking and separation are clear here, without reference to a sword, a religious ruling, or marking an animal.

The line is transmitted in the collection attributed to Zuhayr and in several earlier lexicons. The use of batk for detached feather portions therefore has linguistic evidence independent of the Qur’anic verse.

A cutting sword and a detached piece

Arabic uses sayfun bātikun (سَيْفٌ بَاتِكٌ) for a cutting sword. Batūk belongs to the same field.

Here the root describes the capacity to cut. The sword is bātik because it cuts, rather than because it must cut a particular kind of object.

Bitkah, by comparison, denotes a detached piece. This contrast between the cutting agent and the result of cutting illustrates the family’s morphological range.

Some lexicons also record the rarer use of bitkah for a portion of the night’s darkness. Its historical evidence and precise relationship to physical severance are insufficiently clear to support a theory of the root’s origin. This sense is not involved in the Qur’anic use.

2. Fa-la-yubattikunna: the root in the Qur’an

Al-Nisāʾ states:

وَلَأُضِلَّنَّهُمْ وَلَأُمَنِّيَنَّهُمْ وَلَآمُرَنَّهُمْ فَلَيُبَتِّكُنَّ آذَانَ الْأَنْعَامِ وَلَآمُرَنَّهُمْ فَلَيُغَيِّرُنَّ خَلْقَ اللَّهِ وَمَنْ يَتَّخِذِ الشَّيْطَانَ وَلِيًّا مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ فَقَدْ خَسِرَ خُسْرَانًا مُبِينًا

wa-la-uḍillannahum wa-la-umanniyannahum wa-la-āmurannahum fa-la-yubattikunna ādhāna al-anʿāmi wa-la-āmurannahum fa-la-yughayyirunna khalqa llāhi wa-man yattakhidhi al-shayṭāna waliyyan min dūni llāhi fa-qad khasira khusrānan mubīnan

“And I will surely mislead them, fill them with wishes, and command them to slit livestock’s ears; and I will command them to alter the creation of The God.” Whoever takes Satan as a guardian instead of The God has suffered a clear loss. (Al-Nisāʾ 4:119)

Morphological and syntactic construction

Fa-la-yubattikunna is a form II imperfect, third-person masculine plural, with the heavy emphatic nūn.

The initial fāʾ connects the clause to the preceding command. Lām and the emphatic nūn strongly assert, within Satan’s speech, the action he intends them to perform.

The subject consists of the people Satan says he will command. Structurally, this matters: Satan says that he commands them and they perform the act, rather than saying that he cuts the ears himself.

Ādhāna (آذَانَ) is the verb’s object, and al-anʿāmi (الْأَنْعَامِ) its genitive complement. The ears of livestock are thus what is cut or slit in this clause.

In Qur’anic Arabic, al-anʿām denotes livestock such as camels, cattle, sheep, and goats. Al-Anʿām 6:143 and Al-Anʿām 6:144 present these four groups as eight individual animals, a male and female of each group.

The object of the verb in Al-Nisāʾ 4:119 is therefore specific. It concerns a particular act involving livestock’s ears, rather than cutting parts of every kind of living being or cutting anything at all.

Does form II mean repeated cutting?

Battaka is form II, and lexical explanations connect its doubled consonant with multiplicity of cutting.

Such multiplicity may concern the number of acts, the number of things cut, or the action’s intensity or repetition.

The morphological pattern alone, however, does not establish that every ear must be cut several times here, or that the cut must reach the ear’s root.

The clause concerns a group of people and livestock’s ears in the plural. Multiplicity is also compatible with that breadth.

The basic meaning is cutting and separation. The precise manner must be established through context and evidence about the custom, rather than the morphological pattern alone.

Removing the whole ear or slitting it?

Lexical sources provide two related but different explanations of the Qur’anic use.

Al-Khalīl explains the root through cutting the ear; some transmissions specify cutting at its attachment.

Explanations of pre-Islamic customs, by comparison, more often concern slitting livestock’s ears and making a distinctive cut in them.

Linguistically, the images are not identical. Cutting an ear off at its root implies removing the whole ear, whereas slitting can leave most of it attached.

In this verse, however, the cutting damages the animal’s ear through severance or a slit. The clause itself specifies neither the cut’s size nor how much of the ear was removed.

“Cut livestock’s ears” is consequently close to the verb’s general meaning, while “slit livestock’s ears” suits the familiar historical practice and Forqan’s current rendering.

Preferring one should not require denying the other’s lexical scope.

3. Cutting livestock’s ears and the baḥīrah custom

Arabic historical and lexical sources describe a custom of slitting or cutting certain animals’ ears to mark a special status.

One familiar designation was baḥīrah (بَحِيرَة). Accounts describe an animal being marked after meeting particular breeding or other conditions, followed by restrictions on its use, riding, milking, or consumption.

The reports differ over the number of births and the precise conditions for these names. One account should therefore not be presented, without sufficient evidence, as the custom’s sole definitive form.

For understanding the root, the connection between cutting an ear and marking an animal is clear.

Baḥīrah belongs to another root

Baḥīrah does not share the root B T K. It belongs to B Ḥ R.

Earlier Arabic uses baḥara udhuna al-nāqati (بَحَرَ أُذُنَ النَّاقَةِ) for slitting a she-camel’s ear; baḥīrah is explained through this use.

The two linguistic families thus converge in describing one custom, but have different roles.

Batk denotes cutting and separation. Baḥīrah designates an animal recognized within a social and ritual system through particular conditions and a mark.

This distinction must remain. Batk itself means neither “making a baḥīrah” nor “declaring an animal forbidden.” In a particular historical situation, however, cutting an ear could be part of naming and assigning the animal a ritual status.

Rejecting invented religious rules about animals

Al-Māʾidah states:

مَا جَعَلَ اللَّهُ مِنْ بَحِيرَةٍ وَلَا سَائِبَةٍ وَلَا وَصِيلَةٍ وَلَا حَامٍ وَلَٰكِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا يَفْتَرُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ وَأَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

mā jaʿala llāhu min baḥīratin wa-lā sāʾibatin wa-lā waṣīlatin wa-lā ḥāmin wa-lākinna alladhīna kafarū yaftarūna ʿalā llāhi al-kadhiba wa-aktharuhum lā yaʿqilūna

“The God has prescribed no baḥīrah, sāʾibah, waṣīlah, or ḥām; but those who disbelieve invent lies against The God, and most of them do not reason.” (Al-Māʾidah 5:103)

This verse matters for understanding the background of the action mentioned in Al-Nisāʾ 4:119.

The Qur’an names several livestock designations and expressly denies that The God prescribed such a system.

The continuation speaks of inventing lies against The God. The issue therefore involves more than a bodily cut: it also concerns attributing religious rules and statuses to The God without His authorization.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:104, when people are called to what The God has revealed and to the Messenger, they appeal to their ancestors’ customs. This sequence shows that following an inherited custom and claiming religious authority for it is part of the Qur’anic criticism.

Al-Anʿām 6:138 describes people restricting consumption of certain livestock and crops and forbidding riding some animals. Al-Anʿām 6:139 mentions further restrictions based on the consumers’ sex.

Together, these verses give a broader picture of people making religious rules about using blessings without divine evidence.

In that setting, cutting an animal’s ear could serve as a visible mark confirming a status to which people had themselves attributed sacred authority.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:119 itself, however, does not name baḥīrah. The connection follows from linguistic evidence and verses about invented prohibitions, rather than the two words being lexically identical.

4. Bodily cutting and altering the creation of The God

After the command to cut livestock’s ears in Al-Nisāʾ 4:119, Satan’s speech contains another command by which people alter khalq Allāh (خلق الله), the creation of The God.

The two passages are closely related, but are not one Arabic expression.

Fa-la-yubattikunna belongs to B T K and concerns cutting. Fa-la-yughayyirunna (فَلَيُغَيِّرُنَّ) belongs to GH Y R and concerns changing or altering.

Study of GH Y R

Every cut changes the condition of what is cut, but not every change is cutting.

For example, someone can change an object’s colour, shape, or function without cutting off a part. The field of change is therefore broader than the particular act of batk.

Do the two commands concern the same action?

One possibility is that cutting livestock’s ears is a specific example of the alteration Satan intends, and the next clause describes the broader field of those actions.

Another is that Satan gives two related kinds of command: first, cutting livestock’s ears within a particular custom; then, altering divine creation more broadly.

Structurally, each passage begins wa-la-āmurannahum (وَلَآمُرَنَّهُمْ). Two commands are thus separately expressed within Satan’s speech, although their semantic relationship is clear.

This construction alone establishes neither that altering the creation of The God means only cutting livestock’s ears nor, conversely, that every physical alteration of nature or the body is what Satan means.

Establishing its scope requires examining together the verses concerning creation, fiṭrah, religion, legislation, and the actions being condemned.

Bodily alteration or changing religious direction?

The history of interpretation presents two major approaches to altering the creation of The God.

One emphasizes physical changes to living beings, including cutting or interfering with animals’ bodies.

The other concerns changing divine religion and fiṭrah, the created human disposition. Al-Rūm 30:30 places fiṭrah, human creation, and upright religion in one context and is important evidence for examining this reading.

These two interpretive approaches show that defining B T K alone cannot settle the issue of altering the creation of The God.

Conversely, a reading concerning religion and fiṭrah does not justify ignoring the physical example of cutting livestock’s ears in Al-Nisāʾ 4:119. The verse expressly mentions it.

What is clear in context is that these actions belong among Satan’s commands intended to mislead people. Obeying Satan and distorting what The God has ordained are central to the condemnation.

Does the verse condemn every cut or bodily alteration?

Lexically, batk means cutting and can apply to various objects. Nothing in the root itself establishes that every cut is inherently sinful or satanic.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:119 mentions a particular action in a setting of misleading people. Its moral and religious evaluation follows from the actor, commanding authority, aim, and context, rather than all severance being inherently improper.

The meaning of fa-la-yubattikunna alone therefore cannot yield one ruling covering all medical procedures, surgery, animal marking, hair cutting, or other bodily alterations.

Such actions may differ in aim, necessity, harm, consent, and consequences. Each requires the evidence relevant to its own subject.

This caution does not authorize all bodily alterations. Its point is that a lexical verb should not become a universal rule governing every change without examining context.

Nor should the condemnation of cutting livestock’s ears here be reduced to a merely external detail while ignoring its connection with obeying Satan.

Al-Naḥl 16:116 also prohibits attributing permitted and forbidden things to The God through false assertions. Together with Al-Māʾidah 5:103, it shows that inventing religious rules without The God’s authorization is a serious issue in its own right.

A physical action can acquire a particular religious significance when performed under a religious command or claim. That claim, however, is not part of batk’s lexical meaning.

5. Distinctions from nearby roots

B T K (bataka) compared with Q Ṭ ʿ (qaṭaʿa)

Study of Q Ṭ ʿ

Qaṭaʿa is widely used in Arabic for cutting, separating, and severing. The family applies to physical things as well as nonphysical relationships and connections.

Batk also concerns cutting, but familiar examples emphasize separating part of something, pulling it from its attachment, or tangible severance.

Zuhayr’s poem describes some feathers becoming detached. Al-Nisāʾ 4:119 concerns cutting livestock’s ears.

The distinction must not be absolute. Lexicons directly define batk through qaṭʿ, and the two are synonymous in some uses.

B T K (bataka) compared with B T R (batara)

Study of B T R

Batr concerns cutting off a tail or continuation. In al-abtar (الأَبْتَرُ), as in Al-Kawthar 108:3, severance can take on human and social meanings.

Batk more directly expresses the act of cutting or separating a part.

Physically, both families can concern severance. Some batr constructions, however, emphasize the resulting loss of continuation, while the Qur’anic batk occurrence concerns performing a cutting action.

Fa-la-yubattikunna therefore does not mean “they will deprive them of descendants.” Its object is livestock’s ears, and the meaning must be understood with that object.

B T K (bataka) compared with B T L (batala)

Study of B T L

Batl concerns separating something from something else or becoming separate.

In Al-Muzzammil 73:8, tabattal (تَبَتَّلْ) concerns turning and devoting oneself to The God.

Batk and batl are close in their image of separation, but their uses differ.

The Qur’anic batk occurrence concerns physical cutting, whereas tabattul in Al-Muzzammil concerns a person’s direction and devotion to The God.

A difference of just one root letter does not itself establish common derivation or a definite transfer of meaning between the families.

B T K (bataka) compared with SH Q Q (shaqqa)

Study of SH Q Q

Shaqq concerns splitting and making a slit.

Something may be split without the entire affected part becoming detached. Many lexical uses of batk, by comparison, involve cutting and detachment of the severed part.

The concepts converge in the livestock-ear custom, since making a slit can also involve cutting some ear tissue.

This explains why lexical accounts of Al-Nisāʾ 4:119 sometimes describe cutting and sometimes slitting ears.

Their proximity must not redefine batk everywhere as merely making a slit. In Zuhayr’s poem, feather portions become detached, rather than merely developing a slit.

B T K (bataka) compared with B Ḥ R (baḥara)

Study of B Ḥ R

Earlier Arabic uses baḥara udhuna al-nāqati for slitting a she-camel’s ear, and explains baḥīrah through this use.

Here B Ḥ R and B T K converge in one particular action.

B Ḥ R also has a broad Qur’anic branch concerning the sea and large bodies of water. That branch should not be derived from cutting an ear.

Baḥīrah in Al-Māʾidah 5:103 names a ritual livestock designation, whereas fa-la-yubattikunna in Al-Nisāʾ 4:119 expresses cutting.

This distinction prevents confusing the animal’s designation with the cutting action itself.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Form I bataka can be rendered “cut,” “cut off,” “separated from its attachment,” or, where appropriate, “plucked.”

For hair or feathers pulled out, “pluck” can be more natural. For cutting with a sharp instrument, “cut” or “cut off” is appropriate.

Depending on the sentence, inbataka and tabattaka can mean “became cut off,” “became detached,” or “was pulled out.”

Bitkah means “a detached piece.” Bātik describing a sword means “cutting” or “sharp and cutting.”

Two translation approaches should be distinguished for fa-la-yubattikunna in Al-Nisāʾ 4:119.

In terms of the lexical core, “so they will surely cut livestock’s ears” is close to the verb’s direct meaning.

In the livestock custom involving ritual cuts to ears, “so they will surely slit livestock’s ears” or, preserving the commanding construction, “command them to slit livestock’s ears” is also defensible.

Forqan’s current translation chooses the latter approach. It suits the historical use being discussed and need not be abandoned simply because batk has a broader lexical meaning.

The root explanation must keep clear that slitting ears is a contextual instance of cutting, rather than the sole meaning of B T K in every use.

The Qur’anic expression should also not be translated as “declaring livestock forbidden.” Prohibitions and claims to religious authority are part of the related background; fa-la-yubattikunna expresses cutting the ears themselves.

Conclusion

In Arabic, B T K concerns cutting and separating something from its attachment. This can occur through a cutting instrument or through pulling and plucking. Bitkah names a detached piece.

Zuhayr’s poetic witness illustrates the meaning through detached bird feathers. Uses such as a bātik sword and the verbs inbataka and tabattaka further explain the same family.

The Qur’an contains only fa-la-yubattikunna. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:119, it belongs to Satan’s speech: he says he commands people to cut or slit livestock’s ears.

Linguistically, cutting an ear is a physical act. In context, it appears alongside misleading people, obeying Satan, and altering the creation of The God.

Verses concerning baḥīrah and other invented livestock designations provide a suitable setting for understanding the action. Marking the animal could accompany ritual restrictions and attribution of rules to The God, while Al-Māʾidah 5:103 denies their divine authorization.

Cutting, the ritual custom’s designation, and a religious ruling are nevertheless three distinct concepts and should not be merged into one lexical definition.

The semantic core of B T K is cutting and separating part of something. Fa-la-yubattikunna in Al-Nisāʾ 4:119 denotes cutting or slitting livestock’s ears, an action presented in context as part of conduct arising from obedience to Satan.
