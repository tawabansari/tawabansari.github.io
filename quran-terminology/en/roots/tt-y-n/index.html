Core meaning: clay or mud; earth mixed with water, capable of adhesion and shaping, which may become hard and firm through drying or heating.

1. The root in early Arabic

The three-consonant root ṭ y n is known in early Arabic chiefly through the noun ṭīn. It denotes earth mixed with water: soil that has ceased to be dry and loose and has become clay or mud.

Arabic records various uses of this material: coating walls and roofs, making bricks, closing gaps, shaping objects, and sealing documents. Their common feature is that clay can adhere to a surface, take a shape, or fill a space. The pre-Islamic poetic evidence below specifically attests its use in building; it does not establish the date of every use listed here.

Ṭīn is closely related to waḥl. Both can denote mud or wet earth. Waḥl more readily evokes muddy, slippery ground, whereas ṭīn also names material used for shaping, coating, and construction.

Clay may still be called ṭīn after drying. Water matters in its formation, but the word does not necessarily mean that the material remains wet and soft at the moment being discussed. A modifier or verb may be needed to specify stickiness, dryness, or firing.

Evidence from pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī contains:

فَأَبْقَى بَاطِلِي وَالْجِدُّ مِنْهَا

fa-abqā bāṭilī wa-l-jiddu minhā

كَدُكَّانِ الدَّرَابِنَةِ الْمَطِينِ

ka-dukkāni al-darābinati al-maṭīni

The expression دُكَّانِ الدَّرَابِنَةِ الْمَطِينِ describes a clay-coated platform associated with gatekeepers or guards.

Al-maṭīn here means something coated with clay. This illustrates the use of ṭīn for covering and plastering surfaces.

The line appears in the well-known poem beginning أَفَاطِمُ قَبْلَ بَيْنِكِ مَتِّعِينِي, preserved in the collection attributed to the poet. Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet matters for early usage, although the date of this particular line is not independently established.

This example attests an early adjective describing something coated with clay. The verbal forms recorded in dictionaries belong to the same family; this line alone does not date every such form.

Another building-related example

A verse attributed to al-Mutalammis includes this description of construction:

يُطَانُ بِآجُرٍّ عَلَيْهِ وَيُكْلَسُ

yuṭānu bi-ājurrin ʿalayhi wa-yuklasu

The passage describes construction with bricks and coating with lime. The verb يُطَانُ denotes coating with clay; بِآجُرٍّ also names bricks among the building materials.

The wording has variant readings. One version has بِطَانٍ عَلَى صُمِّ الصَّفِي وَبِكِلْسِ. Despite the variation, the first version clearly contains the verb associated with coating a building with clay.

These examples show that ṭīn was an earthly material used in construction in early Arabic. The meaning of clay and its use as a coating are historically attested, but these examples do not yield a precise chemical definition or a uniform mineral composition for every kind of ṭīn.

2. Lexical forms and extensions of meaning

Important recorded forms include طِين, طِينَة, أَطْيَان, طَانَ, يَطِينُ, طَيَّنَ, تَطْيِين, مَطِين, طَيَّان, and طِيَانَة.

Ṭīn: clay and earth mixed with water

Ṭīn is a material noun denoting clay, mud, or earth mixed with water.

In classical Arabic it can denote naturally occurring mud as well as clay prepared for building or making objects.

Mud beside water and clay prepared by a builder for coating a wall may both be called ṭīn, although their water content, adhesion, and composition differ.

Thus ṭīn names a material, not a fixed degree of moisture or hardness.

Ṭīnah: a piece of clay and one's nature

In a material sense, ṭīnah denotes a piece or particular quantity of clay.

In older usage it could denote clay used to seal a document or close something. Soft clay received the impression of a seal, retaining it after drying.

Ṭīnah also occurs with the meaning of nature, disposition, or the substance of one's character.

Describing someone as having a particular ṭīnah refers to their qualities and disposition, not the kind of soil in their body.

This extension is understandable because clay can be shaped into a particular form. Nevertheless, human character is not the direct meaning of ṭīn in the Quranic passages.

Nor can the antiquity of every figurative use of ṭīnah be inferred from pre-Islamic building imagery. These meanings are recorded in Arabic sources, but each branch requires its own historical evidence.

Aṭyān: kinds of clay and clay lands

Aṭyān is a plural of ṭīn and may refer to kinds of clay or clay lands.

In land and agricultural usage it has also been applied to land with clay soil.

This form does not occur in the Quran.

Ṭāna and ṭayyana: coating with clay

Ṭāna and ṭayyana are recorded for covering something with clay, plastering walls, and using clay in building.

In طَيَّنَ الْحَائِطَ, a wall is coated with clay. The wall itself need not have been made of clay; it may be stone or another material with clay applied as a covering.

Verbs from this family were also used for sealing documents with clay.

Maṭīn describes something coated with clay; ṭayyān names a worker in clay, and ṭiyānah the work or occupation.

These derivatives do not occur in the Quran, but they help establish the root's semantic range by showing clay as material used for coating and shaping.

The central meaning of the family

The lexical evidence places earth mixed with water in a clay or muddy state at the center of ṭ y n.

Adhesion, softness, moldability, and the capacity to harden characterize some kinds and states of ṭīn. They are not equally prominent in every occurrence.

Shaping a bird emphasizes moldability; coating a wall emphasizes adhesion and coverage; making building materials emphasizes drying or hardening.

These differences follow from the material's use and the accompanying words, without requiring unrelated meanings for the root in each case.

3. Quranic occurrences

The root ṭ y n occurs 12 times in 12 verses across 11 surahs. Every occurrence is the noun ṭīn, varying only in definiteness and grammatical case.

No verb or other derivative of this family occurs in the Quran.

The occurrences have four main settings: human creation, shaping clay in a sign associated with Jesus, building with clay, and stones of clay in a punishment narrative.

A. Clay in human creation

Most Quranic occurrences of ṭīn concern human creation.

هُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ طِينٍ ثُمَّ قَضَىٰ أَجَلًا ۖ وَأَجَلٌ مُسَمًّى عِنْدَهُ ۖ ثُمَّ أَنْتُمْ تَمْتَرُونَ

huwa alladhī khalaqakum min ṭīnin thumma qaḍā ajalan wa-ajalun musamman ʿindahu thumma antum tamtarūn

Al-Anʿām 6:2

He is the One who created you from clay, then decreed a term; and a specified term is with Him. Yet you doubt.

Al-Anʿām 6:2

Here مِنْ طِينٍ indicates the material origin of human creation.

The preposition min relates to the material or starting point of creation. The phrase itself does not explain how that material becomes a human being.

The verse also connects human creation with an appointed term and outcome. It concerns human creation and life under divine power and governance, beyond the initial material alone.

وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ مِنْ سُلَالَةٍ مِنْ طِينٍ

wa-laqad khalaqnā al-insāna min sulālatin min ṭīn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:12

And We certainly created the human being from an extract of clay.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:12

Sulālah, from s l l, denotes something drawn out or extracted.

The Quran therefore speaks specifically of سُلَالَةٍ مِنْ طِينٍ: something drawn or extracted from that material.

Ṭīn still names the clay material; extraction is conveyed by sulālah. The meaning of extract must not be built into the lexical definition of ṭīn.

The following verses describe a drop placed in a secure resting place and subsequent stages of human formation: Al-Muʾminūn 23:13 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:14.

This sequence presents human creation in stages and through different expressions. Verse 12 alone does not identify a particular chemical composition or biological stage for sulālah min ṭīn.

الَّذِي أَحْسَنَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ خَلَقَهُ ۖ وَبَدَأَ خَلْقَ الْإِنْسَانِ مِنْ طِينٍ

alladhī aḥsana kulla shayʾin khalaqahu wa-badaʾa khalqa al-insāni min ṭīn

Al-Sajdah 32:7

The One who made good everything He created, and began the creation of the human being from clay.

Al-Sajdah 32:7

The word badaʾa, “began,” matters here: creation from clay is presented as the beginning of human creation.

The next verse, Al-Sajdah 32:8, speaks of making human offspring from an extract of a lowly fluid.

These verses distinguish the beginning of human creation from the continuation of human offspring. The word ṭīn itself does not explain the initial process or every stage in the emergence of human generations.

فَاسْتَفْتِهِمْ أَهُمْ أَشَدُّ خَلْقًا أَمْ مَنْ خَلَقْنَا ۚ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَاهُمْ مِنْ طِينٍ لَازِبٍ

fa-staftihim a-hum ashaddu khalqan am man khalaqnā innā khalaqnāhum min ṭīnin lāzib

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:11

Ask them, then: are they stronger in constitution, or those We have created? Indeed, We created them from sticky clay.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:11

Here ṭīn is modified by lāzib.

Lāzib, from l z b, describes something adhesive, cohesive, or firmly attached.

Ṭīn lāzib is consequently clay with adhering, cohesive parts.

This combination helps distinguish the material from its property: ṭīn names the material, while lāzib explicitly emphasizes adhesion. An adjective may reinforce a familiar characteristic; its presence alone does not prove that the noun excludes that characteristic.

“Sticky clay” therefore suits the whole phrase. In a brief root definition, adhesion should be described as a possible or usual property of the material, not an obligatory feature of every use.

The passage concerns human creation in the context of The God's power to create and raise again.

B. Clay and Iblīs's objection to Adam's creation

In Adam's story, ṭīn occurs both in divine speech and in Iblīs's response.

إِذْ قَالَ رَبُّكَ لِلْمَلَائِكَةِ إِنِّي خَالِقٌ بَشَرًا مِنْ طِينٍ

idh qāla rabbuka li-l-malāʾikati innī khāliqun basharan min ṭīn

Ṣād 38:71

When your Lord said to the angels: “Indeed, I am creating a human being from clay.”

Ṣād 38:71

Here ṭīn names the material from which the human being is to be created.

Later in the account, Iblīs uses the difference between his own material origin and Adam's to claim superiority.

قَالَ أَنَا خَيْرٌ مِنْهُ ۖ خَلَقْتَنِي مِنْ نَارٍ وَخَلَقْتَهُ مِنْ طِينٍ

qāla anā khayrun minhu khalaqtanī min nārin wa-khalaqtahu min ṭīn

Ṣād 38:76

He said: “I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay.”

Ṣād 38:76

The same argument appears in Al-Aʿrāf 7:12. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:61, Iblīs questions the command to prostrate by referring to Adam's creation from clay.

Nār and ṭīn name different materials, but these words themselves assign no moral rank to beings created from them.

Iblīs infers his superiority from the difference in material. This is his claim, not the lexical meaning of ṭīn or the Quran's judgment of human worth.

Elsewhere, the material Iblīs treats as a mark of inferiority is presented as the origin of a human being whom The God has entrusted with responsibility and standing.

Thus ṭīn in the Quran does not itself signify worthlessness, impurity, or baseness. Such judgments do not follow from the word alone.

C. Shaping clay into a bird in Jesus's story

In Āl ʿImrān 3:49, Jesus describes a sign: he makes a bird-like form from clay and breathes into it, and it becomes a bird by The God's permission.

This event is recalled in The God's address to Jesus in Al-Māʾidah 5:110.

In both verses مِنَ الطِّينِ names the material from which the form is made.

For this root study, ṭīn is a moldable material from which a definite form can be made.

Shaping clay and its becoming alive are distinct concepts.

Akhluqu or takhluqu concerns making a bird-like form; breathing into it and its becoming a bird are then described with emphasis on divine permission.

Life-giving is not a meaning of ṭīn. Clay is the moldable material; its coming to life is a separate sign in the narrative, dependent on The God's permission.

These verses likewise do not establish that every piece of clay can naturally turn directly into a living creature. They describe a particular sign in Jesus's story, not a general law of clay.

D. Clay in construction

An important Quranic use connects the root directly with building.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38, Pharaoh commands Hāmān to kindle a fire upon clay and build him a lofty structure.

فَأَوْقِدْ لِي يَا هَامَانُ عَلَى الطِّينِ refers to heating clay in preparing building materials.

This relates to the familiar production of sun-dried or fired bricks. Clay can be molded and used in construction after drying or heating.

The distinction from human creation and the shaping of a bird is clear.

In the creation passages, human creation is attributed to clay; in Jesus's story, clay is shaped into a bird-like form; in Pharaoh's story, it is prepared for construction.

In each case ṭīn names the material, while the verb and context establish what is done with it.

Awqid commands the kindling of fire, so hardening through heat is intelligible from this context. “Fired brick” cannot replace ṭīn throughout the Quran, because firing is not expressed by the noun itself.

E. Stones of clay

لِنُرْسِلَ عَلَيْهِمْ حِجَارَةً مِنْ طِينٍ

li-nursila ʿalayhim ḥijāratan min ṭīn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33

So that we may send upon them stones of clay.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33

The verse occurs in the divine envoys' speech about a guilty people.

Ḥijārah, from ḥ j r, means stones; min ṭīn specifies their material.

Clay is not presented here simply as soft, wet mud. The passage describes stones whose material is identified as clay.

Clay can harden through drying, compaction, or heating. The verse does not specify which process produced these stones.

Hūd 11:82 and Al-Ḥijr 15:74 use حِجَارَةً مِنْ سِجِّيلٍ for the stones of punishment.

These expressions are thematically related to حِجَارَةً مِنْ طِينٍ, but sijjīl is an independent word whose derivation and precise semantic range have been discussed by lexicographers.

A common subject does not make ṭīn and sijjīl identical in root or lexical meaning.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33 makes one point clear: ṭīn can be the original material of something described in its final state as hard and stone-like. Its definition should therefore not be restricted to material that is always soft and wet.

4. Distinctions from related roots

Ṭ y n (ṭīn) and t r b (turāb): clay and soil

Turāb, from t r b, denotes soil and the loose, fine material of the ground.

Ṭīn denotes earth mixed with water and made into clay or mud.

The difference concerns the material's condition. Soil may be dry and loose, whereas clay forms through mixing earth and water and can acquire adhesion and moldability.

For example, Āl ʿImrān 3:59 speaks of Adam's creation from turāb, while Ṣād 38:71 speaks of creating a human being from ṭīn.

These expressions need not conflict, because turāb and ṭīn may represent different states of earthly material.

Nevertheless, the verses should not be made to express a complete, precise chronological sequence from soil to clay to humanity without evidence. The lexical distinction is clear; determining every stage of creation exceeds the definition of either root.

Ṭ y n (ṭīn) and w ḥ l (waḥl): moldable clay and muddy ground

Waḥl denotes mud or muddy conditions, often associated with moisture and slipperiness.

Ṭīn names material found naturally or used in making things.

Rain-soaked ground may contain waḥl; material used to make a vessel or brick is also called ṭīn.

These words overlap and should not be separated absolutely. Still, the Quran's uses for shaping a bird and for construction emphasize clay as a moldable material.

Ṭ y n (ṭīn) and ṣ l ṣ l (ṣalṣāl): clay and dried clay

Ṣalṣāl, from ṣ l ṣ l, denotes dry clay that makes a pottery-like sound when struck or moved.

Al-Ḥijr 15:26 speaks of creating the human being from صَلْصَالٍ مِنْ حَمَإٍ مَسْنُونٍ.

Al-Raḥmān 55:14 compares ṣalṣāl to fakhkhār.

These uses emphasize a particular state of clay, especially dryness and the sound it produces.

Ṭīn is more general and does not necessarily express this dry condition.

“Clay” for ṭīn and “dry, sounding clay” for ṣalṣāl better preserve the distinction.

Ṭ y n (ṭīn) and ḥ m ʾ (ḥamaʾ): clay and dark mud

Ḥamaʾ denotes dark mud. In Al-Ḥijr 15:26, an interpretation involving alteration also relates to the accompanying adjective مَسْنُون, for which several explanations have been proposed.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:26, the word occurs with masnūn.

Although both ḥamaʾ and ṭīn concern muddy materials, ḥamaʾ more particularly emphasizes darkness and a muddy condition.

Ṭīn alone does not require these characteristics. Translating it everywhere as “sludge” would unnecessarily narrow its meaning.

Ṭ y n (ṭīn) and f kh r (fakhkhār): clay and pottery

Fakhkhār denotes pottery or clay material hardened by firing.

Al-Raḥmān 55:14 describes human creation from sounding clay like pottery.

Fakhkhār and ṭīn are materially related because pottery can be made from clay, but they are not identical words.

Ṭīn is the clay material; fakhkhār is a product made from suitable material through shaping and firing.

This explains the significance of kindling fire upon clay in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38: the passage concerns preparing building materials.

Ṭ y n (ṭīn) and l z b (lazib): material and adhesion

Lāzib, from l z b, describes adhesion and cohesion.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:11, it modifies ṭīn.

Ṭīn names the material, while lāzib specifies its adhesive quality.

The translation of طِينٍ لَازِبٍ should preserve both contributions: “sticky clay.”

Although adhesion is a familiar property of clay, this combination explicitly emphasizes it.

5. Grammatical and semantic observations

Min ṭīn: origin or material

In most Quranic occurrences, ṭīn follows min.

In خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ طِينٍ, the construction expresses the material or origin of creation.

In أَخْلُقُ لَكُمْ مِنَ الطِّينِ, it names the material from which the bird-like form is made.

In حِجَارَةً مِنْ طِينٍ, it concerns the stones' material or material origin.

These constructions connect something to its initial or constituent material without explaining every detail of its formation or manufacture.

Ṭīnan in Al-Isrāʾ 17:61

In this verse, Iblīs says:

وَإِذْ قُلْنَا لِلْمَلَائِكَةِ اسْجُدُوا لِآدَمَ فَسَجَدُوا إِلَّا إِبْلِيسَ قَالَ أَأَسْجُدُ لِمَنْ خَلَقْتَ طِينًا

wa-idh qulnā li-l-malāʾikati usjudū li-ādama fa-sajadū illā iblīsa qāla a-asjudu li-man khalaqta ṭīnā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:61

And when We said to the angels, “Prostrate to Adam,” they prostrated, except Iblīs. He said: “Shall I prostrate to one You created from clay?”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:61

Ṭīnan is accusative here. Unlike many other occurrences, it is not preceded by an explicit min.

One grammatical explanation treats it as a circumstantial accusative referring to the understood object of khalaqta: one created in a clay state. Another supplies an omitted preposition, giving the sense min ṭīn.

Both explanations connect Adam's creation with clay, but the grammatical construction differs from verses with an explicit min.

This difference should neither be overlooked nor made to imply a new meaning such as clay's inherent inferiority.

Definite and indefinite ṭīn

Al-ṭīn is definite in Āl ʿImrān 3:49, Al-Māʾidah 5:110, and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38.

It is indefinite elsewhere, including Al-Anʿām 6:2 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:11.

This grammatical difference does not by itself establish that the groups of verses concern entirely different materials.

The lexical core remains clay in all these cases; context establishes the material's role.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

The primary, natural Persian equivalent of ṭīn is gol, “clay” or “mud.” In English, “clay” suits the Quranic contexts discussed here.

“Earth mixed with water” gives a fuller lexical explanation, but most verses do not require replacing the short noun with that longer phrase.

“From clay” suits the creation passages. Where the text says سُلَالَةٍ مِنْ طِينٍ, the contribution of sulālah must also be retained.

In the passages about Jesus, “from clay” identifies the moldable material from which the bird-like form is made.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38, “clay” remains the direct equivalent. “Clay for making bricks” or “clay building material” may serve as explanation, but “brick” should not directly replace ṭīn without accounting for the command to kindle fire.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33, “stones of clay” retains both ḥijārah and ṭīn.

Suggested Quranic word-box explanations:

Brief root meaning: Clay; earth mixed with water, capable of adhesion and shaping.

Context in human creation passages: Clay as the material or material origin of human creation.

Context in Al-Muʾminūn 23:12: Clay from which an extract is presented as the origin of human creation.

Context in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:11: Clay; the accompanying adjective lāzib emphasizes its stickiness. The complete phrase means “sticky clay.”

Context in Āl ʿImrān 3:49 and Al-Māʾidah 5:110: Clay from which Jesus makes a bird-like form.

Context in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38: Clay used in preparing building materials through heating.

Context in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33: Clay as the material of the stones mentioned in the verse.

7. Conclusion

The main branch of ṭ y n denotes clay: earth mixed with water. This material can adhere, take shape, and be used for coating, making objects, sealing, and preparing building materials.

Pre-Islamic poetic evidence, including maṭīn in the verse attributed to al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī, attests the use of clay for coating in early Arabic language and life. Recorded nominal and verbal forms further illustrate this field of use.

All twelve Quranic occurrences are the noun ṭīn. It appears in human creation, Iblīs's story, Jesus's shaping of a bird-like form, Pharaoh's building command, and stones associated with punishment.

The basic material meaning persists, although its role and condition differ: an origin of creation, a material for shaping, a building material, or the substance of hard stones.

Ṭīn should therefore not always be restricted to soft, wet mud. Equally, strong adhesion, dryness, firing, or becoming stone should not be included in every occurrence. These features follow from particular modifiers and contexts.

The distinctions between ṭīn, turāb, ṣalṣāl, ḥamaʾ, fakhkhār, and lāzib must also remain clear. They may concern related materials or different states of one material, but each contributes its own meaning.

Final definition: In its main sense, ṭ y n denotes clay or mud: earth mixed with water, capable of adhesion and shaping. It may refer to material in various states, from wet mud to dried or hardened material. In the Quran, ṭīn consistently names the material; adhesion, shaping, heating, and becoming a hard object are conveyed by the accompanying words and context.
