Linguistic Note

The core meaning of أ ت ي (A T Y) is reaching from an origin to a destination. Sometimes this reaching is spatial: someone comes, a group arrives, or a person enters a place. Sometimes it is transfer: book, wealth, wisdom, sign, or reward is given and delivered to someone. Sometimes it is the occurrence of an event: the command of The God, punishment, the Hour, or a promise moves from expectation into realization.

For this reason, the root is not only “to come.” In many verses, آتى (ātā) means giving and delivering, as in آتُوا الزَّكَاةَ (ātū al-zakāh) or آتَيْنَا مُوسَى الْكِتَابَ (ātaynā Mūsā al-kitāb). In these cases, something reaches the recipient from the giver. The core is still reaching, but the direction changes: sometimes the subject comes, sometimes the subject brings something, and sometimes the subject delivers something to another.

If every form of this root is translated only as “come,” many verses break. If every form is translated as “give,” the field of coming, arriving, and occurring disappears. The key question in each verse is: what reaches what, from where, and to whom?

Central Meaning

A T Y in the Qur’an points to coming, arriving, bringing, delivering something to someone, and in some forms giving or fulfilling something so that it reaches its proper destination.

Material Image

The material image is the movement of someone or something toward a destination. A human comes from one place and reaches another. News arrives and surrounds the listener. A book is given to a prophet. Wealth is delivered to its rightful owner. Punishment comes and escape is no longer available.

In this image, A T Y always has some kind of arrival. Even when it means “giving,” something reaches the hand or possession of the recipient. And when it means “occurrence,” an event from the future or unseen enters the field of human experience.

The root can be pictured as a traveler moving along a path until reaching a destination. This image should not be made overly poetic, but it helps distinguish A T Y from some nearby words. A T Y emphasizes the path of arrival and reaching the destination.

Classical Language Note

In classical Arabic, A T Y is a broad and living root used for coming, arriving, bringing, and delivering. Because it is a basic root, it is not limited to one field such as travel, battle, trade, or worship. In ordinary life, a person comes, something is brought, news arrives, a command comes, and wealth is given to someone.

Some lexical discussions connect forms of this root with movement like flowing toward a destination. Without a direct witness, the safer method is to rely on clear Qur’anic usage. The Qur’an itself shows enough: this root extends from simple coming to delivering book, zakat, wisdom, and divine command.

The Coming of the Command of The God

One important use of A T Y is the coming of the command of The God or of a certain event. In 16:1, the Qur’an says: أَتَىٰ أَمْرُ اللَّهِ فَلَا تَسْتَعْجِلُوهُ (atā amru Allāhi fa-lā tastaʿjilūh).

Although atā is formally past tense, it is used for a matter in which the addressees are told not to be hasty. This structure can express certainty of occurrence: something is so certain that it is spoken of as already arrived. So atā amru Allāh should not be read only as a past event. The verse speaks in the language of certainty: the command of The God is arriving and certain, and human haste changes nothing.

A Time or Stage Coming Over the Human Being

In Sūrat al-Insān, the Qur’an says: هَلْ أَتَىٰ عَلَى الْإِنسَانِ حِينٌ مِّنَ الدَّهْرِ لَمْ يَكُن شَيْئًا مَّذْكُورًا (hal atā ʿalā al-insāni ḥīnun mina al-dahr lam yakun shay’an madhkūrā) in 76:1.

Atā ʿalā al-insān means that a time passed over the human being or came upon him. This use shows that A T Y is not always a person coming. Sometimes a time, period, or state comes over something and encompasses it. The phrase atā ʿalā is important: time comes over the human being, rather than the human being going toward time.

Coming to a Place or Reaching a Position

The root A T Y is clear in spatial arrival. In 27:18, the Qur’an says: حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَتَوْا عَلَىٰ وَادِ النَّمْلِ (ḥattā idhā ataw ʿalā wādi al-naml).

Ataw ʿalā wādi al-naml means that they reached the valley of the ants or came upon it. Here A T Y shows movement and arrival at a place. When it comes with ʿalā, it may mean reaching a territory or passing over it. Translation should follow the scene of the army reaching the valley.

The Coming of Signs

The Qur’an also speaks of the coming of signs. In 6:158, it says: هَلْ يَنظُرُونَ إِلَّا أَن تَأْتِيَهُمُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ أَوْ يَأْتِيَ رَبُّكَ أَوْ يَأْتِيَ بَعْضُ آيَاتِ رَبِّكَ (hal yanẓurūna illā an ta’tiyahumu al-malā’ikah aw ya’tiya rabbuka aw ya’tiya baʿḍu āyāti rabbik).

The coming of signs means the appearance of a decisive stage of judgment and truth. A T Y here is not merely spatial movement. The coming of an āyah means that a decisive sign reaches the human scene. This use shows that A T Y can refer to unseen matters, final signs, and events that move from expectation to occurrence.

Bringing in Challenge

One important use of A T Y is bringing something in a challenge or demand for proof. In 2:23, the Qur’an says: فَأْتُوا بِسُورَةٍ مِّن مِّثْلِهِ (fa’tū bi-sūratin min mithlih).

Fa’tū bi-sūrah means bring a surah or present one. This is not a person coming; it is bringing a text, presenting an example, and delivering it into the field of judgment. In 10:38, the same field appears: قُلْ فَأْتُوا بِسُورَةٍ مِّثْلِهِ (qul fa’tū bi-sūratin mithlih).

In this use, A T Y comes with bā: atū bi-sūrah. The structure means to bring something with you, present it, or make it available in the field of response. In English, “bring a surah” is more natural than “come with a surah.”

Ātā: Giving, Delivering, Fulfilling

One very important branch of this root is ātā: to give, deliver, or grant something to someone. In 2:43, the Qur’an says: وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ (wa-aqīmū al-ṣalāh wa-ātū al-zakāh).

Ātū al-zakāh means give zakat, fulfill it, or deliver it to its proper place and recipients. Here ātā is not merely bringing. It is delivering a financial and social right to its destination.

In 4:2, the Qur’an says: وَآتُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ أَمْوَالَهُمْ (wa-ātū al-yatāmā amwālahum). Ātā here carries a clear legal weight: wealth that belongs to orphans must be delivered to them. This is not merely voluntary generosity; it is delivering a right to its owner.

Ātaynā: Giving Book, Wisdom, and Signs

The Qur’an often uses ātaynā for giving book, judgment, wisdom, or signs. In 32:23, it says: وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَا مُوسَى الْكِتَابَ (wa-laqad ātaynā Mūsā al-kitāb). We gave Moses the Book: something reached a prophet from The God.

In 2:269, the Qur’an says: يُؤْتِي الْحِكْمَةَ مَن يَشَاءُ وَمَن يُؤْتَ الْحِكْمَةَ فَقَدْ أُوتِيَ خَيْرًا كَثِيرًا (yu’tī al-ḥikmata man yashā wa-man yu’ta al-ḥikmata faqad ūtiya khayran kathīrā). Wisdom is something delivered and given to a human being. Ūtiya is passive: someone has been given. “Was given” is natural; “was come” is not.

Mā Ātākum: Delivery in Social Distribution

In Sūrat al-Ḥashr, the Qur’an says: وَمَا آتَاكُمُ الرَّسُولُ فَخُذُوهُ وَمَا نَهَاكُمْ عَنْهُ فَانتَهُوا (wa-mā ātākumu al-rasūlu fa-khudhūhu wa-mā nahākum ʿanhu fa-intahū) in 59:7.

Mā ātākumu al-rasūl appears in the context of fay’, public distribution, and preventing wealth from circulating only among the rich. From the root angle, ātā means to give and deliver. In this context, the Messenger delivers something to people within the system of distribution and social order, and they are to take it.

This is important for Forqan translation. The phrase mā ātākumu al-rasūl should not be detached from the economic and social context of the verse and carelessly extended to every extra-textual report. In the verse itself, the matter is what is given and delivered in this system of distribution, and what is withheld in the same framework.

Giving Zakat: Delivering a Social Right

In the Qur’an, giving zakat frequently appears beside establishing salat. In 27:3, the Qur’an says: الَّذِينَ يُقِيمُونَ الصَّلَاةَ وَيُؤْتُونَ الزَّكَاةَ (alladhīna yuqīmūna al-ṣalāh wa-yu’tūna al-zakāh).

Yu’tūna al-zakāh means, from the root angle, they deliver zakat. This is not merely mechanical payment; it is delivering something that belongs within a structure of purification, growth, and social responsibility. “They give zakat” is smooth in translation, but the root explanation should preserve delivery and fulfillment of a right.

The Coming of Punishment or Promise

The Qur’an also speaks of punishment or promise coming with this root. In 11:32, the opponents of Noah say: فَأْتِنَا بِمَا تَعِدُنَا إِن كُنتَ مِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ (fa’tinā bimā taʿidunā in kunta mina al-ṣādiqīn).

Fa’tinā bimā taʿidunā means bring us what you promise us. Here A T Y is in the form of demanding that something be brought onto the scene. They request the occurrence of the promise, but in a tone of denial and challenge.

Ātiyah: The Hour as Coming

In 20:15, the Qur’an says: إِنَّ السَّاعَةَ آتِيَةٌ أَكَادُ أُخْفِيهَا (inna al-sāʿata ātiyatun akādu ukh'fīhā).

Ātiyah means coming, arriving, and certain to occur. This form shows that A T Y can express a certain future: something has not yet occurred within human experience, but its coming is certain.

I’tiyā: Coming in Response to Command

In a verse about heaven and earth, the Qur’an says: فَقَالَ لَهَا وَلِلْأَرْضِ ائْتِيَا طَوْعًا أَوْ كَرْهًا قَالَتَا أَتَيْنَا طَائِعِينَ (fa-qāla lahā wa-li-l-arḍi i’tiyā ṭawʿan aw karhan qālatā ataynā ṭā’iʿīn) in 41:11.

Here A T Y appears in the language of cosmic command. I’tiyā means come, and ataynā means we have come. This is not ordinary human movement; it expresses heaven and earth entering the order of divine command and decree. The verse speaks about the responsiveness of heaven and earth to command, not about external philosophical or scientific detail.

Distinguishing Qur’anic Uses

In 16:1, atā amru Allāh shows the coming and certainty of the command of The God. In 76:1, atā ʿalā al-insān is time coming over or passing over the human being. In 27:18, A T Y marks spatial arrival. In 6:158, it concerns the coming of signs and final events.

In 2:23 and 10:38, fa’tū bi-sūrah means bringing and presenting something in challenge. In ātū al-zakāh and ātū al-yatāmā amwālahum, ātā concerns giving, fulfilling, and delivering a right. In ātaynā Mūsā al-kitāb, it concerns divine giving and delivery of the Book. In 59:7, ātā appears in the context of delivery and public distribution. In 20:15, ātiyah shows coming and certainty of occurrence.

The core in all these uses is reaching, but the direction differs: sometimes a person comes, sometimes time or command comes, sometimes something is brought, and sometimes something is given and delivered to someone.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

In أَتَىٰ أَمْرُ اللَّهِ (atā amru Allāh), the perfect verb is used for a certain matter. This structure can express certainty of occurrence. In أَتَىٰ عَلَى الْإِنسَانِ (atā ʿalā al-insān), ʿalā shows that time came over the human being or passed over him.

In أَتَوْا عَلَىٰ وَادِ النَّمْلِ (ataw ʿalā wādi al-naml), the construction with ʿalā expresses reaching an area or passing over it. In فَأْتُوا بِسُورَةٍ (fa’tū bi-sūrah), the bā is important. The meaning is not merely that the people should come; they must bring and present something.

In آتُوا الزَّكَاةَ (ātū al-zakāh), the object is direct. Zakat must be given, fulfilled, and delivered. In آتَيْنَا مُوسَى الْكِتَابَ (ātaynā Mūsā al-kitāb), the sides of meaning are clear: The God is the giver, Moses is the receiver, and the Book is what is given.

In أُوتِيَ خَيْرًا كَثِيرًا (ūtiya khayran kathīrā), the verb is passive; focus falls on the receiver and the gift received. In ائْتِيَا (i’tiyā) and أَتَيْنَا (ataynā), the root appears in command and response; coming here means entering the order of cosmic command.

Difference from Nearby Roots

A T Y is close to جاء (J Y ʾ / jā’a), but they are not the same. Jā’a often emphasizes manifest arrival, appearance, and the weight of occurrence, as in the coming of truth or help. A T Y is broader and softer; it can include coming, bringing, giving, and delivering. In many places, jā’a highlights presence and manifestation, while A T Y highlights the path of reaching a destination.

A T Y differs from حضر (Ḥ Ḍ R). Ḥaḍara means to be present, be in a place, or attend. A T Y shows movement and arrival toward a destination. A T Y also differs from ورد (W R D), which often concerns arriving at water, entering a place, or entering a particular situation. A T Y is more general.

A T Y is close to أقبل (A Q B L), but not identical. Aqbal means to turn toward and approach from a direction. A T Y states coming or arrival without always highlighting face-direction or turning toward.

A T Y is close to أعطى (A ʿ Ṭ Y), especially in the ātā branch. Aʿṭā focuses more directly on the act of giving or granting. Ātā, in addition to giving, carries delivery and making something reach its destination. This is why in ātū al-zakāh and ātū al-yatāmā amwālahum, delivering a right to its proper place and owner matters.

A T Y differs from وهب (W H B). Wahaba means to grant or gift out of generosity. Ātā may be giving a right, delivering wealth, divine granting, or providing something to someone. Every wahb can be a kind of ātā, but not every ātā is wahb.

A T Y is also not the same as جلب (J L B). Jalb means bringing or drawing something toward oneself. A T Y is broader: the subject may come, bring something, or deliver something to someone else.

Forqan Translation Method

In Forqan translation, A T Y should not always be translated as “come.” In atā amru Allāh, “came” or “has arrived” is suitable, but the certainty of occurrence must be felt. In fa’tū bi-sūrah, “bring” or “present” is suitable. In ātū al-zakāh, “give,” “fulfill,” or “deliver” may be more accurate. In ātaynā Mūsā al-kitāb, “We gave” is natural. In ūtiya al-ḥikmah, “was given wisdom” is suitable.

The difference between atā and ātā should also be preserved. Atā is more about coming, arriving, or bringing. Ātā is more about giving and delivering something to someone. Both belong to the same root and share the core of reaching.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

  • For أَتَىٰ أَمْرُ اللَّهِ (atā amru Allāh): the command of The God has arrived; or in explanation: the command of The God is coming and certain.
  • For هَلْ أَتَىٰ عَلَى الْإِنسَانِ (hal atā ʿalā al-insān): has there come over the human being; or: has a time passed over the human being.
  • For أَتَوْا عَلَىٰ وَادِ النَّمْلِ (ataw ʿalā wādi al-naml): they reached the valley of the ants; or: they came upon the valley of the ants.
  • For فَأْتُوا بِسُورَةٍ (fa’tū bi-sūrah): bring a surah; or: present a surah in the field of judgment.
  • For آتُوا الزَّكَاةَ (ātū al-zakāh): give zakat; or: fulfill zakat / deliver it to its proper recipients.
  • For آتُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ أَمْوَالَهُمْ (ātū al-yatāmā amwālahum): give the orphans their wealth; or: deliver the orphans’ wealth to them.
  • For آتَيْنَا مُوسَى الْكِتَابَ (ātaynā Mūsā al-kitāb): We gave Moses the Book.
  • For يُؤْتِي الْحِكْمَةَ (yu’tī al-ḥikmah): He gives wisdom; or: He delivers wisdom to whom He wills.
  • For أُوتِيَ خَيْرًا كَثِيرًا (ūtiya khayran kathīrā): abundant good has been given.
  • For مَا آتَاكُمُ الرَّسُولُ (mā ātākumu al-rasūl): what the Messenger gave you; or with context: what the Messenger delivered to you.
  • For إِنَّ السَّاعَةَ آتِيَةٌ (inna al-sāʿata ātiyah): the Hour is coming; or: the Hour will surely arrive.
  • For ائْتِيَا طَوْعًا أَوْ كَرْهًا (i’tiyā ṭawʿan aw karhā): come, willingly or unwillingly.

Summary

A T Y in the Qur’an indicates coming, arriving, bringing, and being delivered. At the material level, someone or something reaches a destination from somewhere. In the field of revelation and guidance, book, wisdom, zakat, wealth, or sign is given and delivered to someone. In divine events, the command of The God, signs, punishment, or the Hour comes; that is, it moves from expectation or the unseen into realization.

The main difference between A T Y and jā’a is that jā’a more often emphasizes manifest arrival and presence, while A T Y is broader and includes bringing and giving. Its difference from aʿṭā is that aʿṭā expresses giving more directly, while ātā also carries delivery and reaching the destination. Its difference from ḥaḍara is that ḥaḍara is being present, while atā is arriving and coming toward a destination.

In summary: A T Y in the Qur’an points to something reaching a destination from a direction. Sometimes the person or command itself comes; sometimes something is brought; and sometimes book, wealth, wisdom, zakat, or reward is given and delivered to someone.
