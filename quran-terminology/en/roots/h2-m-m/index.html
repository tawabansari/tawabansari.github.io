Root H M M — Intention, resolve, and preoccupation

Qur’anic and lexical forms

هَمَّ / هَمَّتْ
(hamma / hammat)
Intending or contemplating an action

أَهَمَّتْهُمْ
(ahammathum)
Has preoccupied them

From forming an intention to concern, attentive effort, and a decision that may or may not become action

The Arabic triliteral root ه م م has several semantic branches. One concerns intending, contemplating an action, and directing one’s will toward it. Another concerns worry, sorrow, and matters occupying the mind. Physical usages include melting, wearing away, and losing strength or structural firmness.

The Qur’an uses the first two branches. هَمَّ concerns intention or inclination toward an action, while أَهَمَّ in one verse concerns preoccupation with oneself.

The essential distinction is between intending and doing. A person may decide, incline toward something, or prepare for it without ultimately acting. Conversely, hamm is not invariably a fleeting, insignificant thought: it can describe a serious intention approaching action.

Qur’anic usages concern believers close to faltering, people contemplating harm or expulsion of the Messenger, the woman and Joseph in a personal encounter, and a group overcome by concern for themselves.

The root should therefore not be predetermined to mean sinful intent, sexual desire, worry, or an irrevocable decision.

1. The root in early Arabic

Classical Arabic هَمَّ بِالشَّيْءِ means to intend, desire, or contemplate doing something.

هَمَمْتُ بِالأَمْرِ means “I intended to do that.”

The intention may not yet have become action. The person may subsequently abandon it, or an external obstacle may prevent its realization.

الْهَمّ can also name what occupies a person’s mind and will, including an intention inwardly entertained.

The same noun also denotes worry or sorrow; الْهُمُوم is its plural in that sense.

الْهِمَّة and الْهِمَّة الْعَالِيَة concern attention and will directed toward a significant goal. Persian likewise uses the borrowed term for determination and effort toward lofty aims.

These usages are related but distinct. Intention can accompany worry, but neither every intention is sorrow nor every concern a decision to act.

A pre-Qur’anic poetic witness

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd reads:

«إِنِّي وَجَدِّكَ مَا هَجَوْتُكَ وَالْأَنْصَابِ يُسْفَحُ بَيْنَهُنَّ دَمُ»

Innī wa-jaddika mā hajawtuka wa-al-anṣābi yusfaḥu baynahunna damu.

“By your good fortune and the sacrificial stones among which blood is poured, I have not lampooned you.”

He then says:

«وَلَقَدْ هَمَمْتُ بِذَاكَ إِذْ حُبِسَتْ وَأُمِرَّ دُونَ عُبَيْدَةَ الْوَذَمُ»

Wa-laqad hamamtu bi-dhāka idh ḥubisat wa-umirra dūna ʿUbaydata al-wadhamu.

“Yet I had intended that when the withholding occurred and the matter was settled without ʿUbaydah’s involvement.”

هَمَمْتُ بِذَاكَ refers to the lampooning whose performance he denied in the preceding line.

He distinguishes having intended to lampoon from actually having done so.

This makes the witness significant: hamamtu expresses contemplating or intending the act, while mā hajawtuka denies its realization.

Intention and action are distinguishable stages.

The poem appears in Ṭarafah’s attributed collection and is associated with his dispute with ʿAmr ibn Hind. The expression أُمِرَّ دُونَ عُبَيْدَةَ الْوَذَمُ also occurs in Arabic proverb collections for settling a matter without the person concerned.

Ṭarafah is a recognized pre-Islamic poet, but this poem’s exact date is not independently established. It is a witness from the transmitted pre-Islamic poetic tradition, not a precisely dated document.

An Islamic-period poetic witness

The Umayyad poet Kuthayyir ʿAzzah says:

«هَمَمْتُ وَهَمَّتْ ثُمَّ هَابَتْ وَهِبْتُهَا
حَيَاءً وَمِثْلِي بِالْحَيَاءِ حَقِيقُ»

Hamamtu wa-hammat thumma hābat wa-hibtuhā / ḥayāʾan wa-mithlī bi-al-ḥayāʾi ḥaqīqu.

“I intended, and she intended; then she hesitated, and I stood in awe of her, out of modesty—and one like me is rightly modest.”

هَمَمْتُ وَهَمَّتْ expresses the two people’s mutual inclination or intention.

The poet immediately mentions hesitation and modesty. Forming an intention therefore does not mean completing the act.

The construction resembles هَمَّتْ بِهِ وَهَمَّ بِهَا in Yūsuf 12:24.

Kuthayyir lived after the revelation period. His poem is not a pre-Islamic witness, but illustrates this reciprocal use of hamm in Umayyad Arabic.

Together, the witnesses show the mind and will turning toward an action without the word alone establishing completion or how far the intention developed.

2. Other Arabic branches

Worry and sorrow

الْهَمُّ also means worry or sorrow.

أَهَمَّهُ الْأَمْرُ means that a matter worried or preoccupied someone.

اهْتَمَّ بِالأَمْرِ can mean attending seriously to a matter and, in some contexts, becoming worried or distressed.

The connection with intention is intelligible: a matter that becomes important attracts attention and can engage thought and will.

Yet not all attentive effort is anxiety. Someone may seriously attend to children’s education, responsibilities, or research without sorrow or distress.

Himmah and determination

الْهِمَّة denotes will, resolve, or serious attention toward a goal.

عَالِي الْهِمَّة describes someone with lofty aims and determination to attain them.

ضَعِيفُ الْهِمَّة can describe weak motivation or determination.

Persian retains this usage in expressions for making an effort, lofty determination, and lack of initiative.

Himmah itself does not occur in the Qur’an. Its familiar Persian meaning should not automatically be assigned to every Qur’anic hamm.

Physical هَمَّ and انْهَمَّ

Lexicons record هَمَّ الشَّحْمَ for melting fat and اِنْهَمَّ الشَّحْمُ for fat melting.

هَمَّنِي الْمَرَضُ describes illness weakening or wearing a person down.

These concern lost strength or firmness rather than intention.

Some Arabic lexicographers relate this physical branch to sorrow: severe worry wears a person down as something melts away.

That image can clarify a semantic association, but does not prove that the psychological meaning historically developed from melting fat.

Arabic also records هُمَّام for a person of great determination and هِمّ for someone very old. Some lexicons discuss الْهَوَامّ, creeping creatures or insects, under this family and connect it with their quiet movement.

These branches require historical caution. Shared consonants or an imaginable common image do not establish a definitive origin for all meanings.

3. Qur’anic forms

The root occurs nine times in eight Qur’anic verses.

Two principal morphological forms appear:

  • هَمَّ: basic-form perfect verbs expressing intention or inclination toward action.
  • أَهَمَّتْ: Form IV, used for causing concern or preoccupation.

Forms found in the text include:

هَمَّتْ, لَهَمَّتْ, هَمَّ, هَمُّوا, وَهَمَّتْ, وَهَمَّ, and أَهَمَّتْهُمْ.

These differences principally concern subject, number, gender, prefixes, and attached pronouns; they are not all separate derivational types.

The occurrences are in Āl ʿImrān 3:122, Āl ʿImrān 3:154, al-Nisāʾ 4:113, al-Māʾidah 5:11, al-Tawbah 9:13, al-Tawbah 9:74, Yūsuf 12:24, and Ghāfir 40:5.

Arabic forms such as هَمّ, هِمَّة, هُمُوم, هُمَّام, هَامّ, اهْتَمَّ, and اهْتِمَام do not occur as such in the Qur’an.

The plural pronoun هُمْ, “they,” and وَهُمْ, “and they,” do not belong to this root.

Written resemblance to هَمَّ must not confuse identification of the root’s occurrences.

4. Intention that may not become action

Āl ʿImrān 3:122 — Approaching a loss of resolve

«إِذْ هَمَّتْ طَائِفَتَانِ مِنْكُمْ أَنْ تَفْشَلَا وَاللَّهُ وَلِيُّهُمَا وَعَلَى اللَّهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ»

Idh hammat ṭāʾifatāni minkum an tafshalā wa-Allāhu waliyyuhumā wa-ʿalā Allāhi fa-l-yatawakkali al-muʾminūn.

“When two groups among you were close to faltering, while The God was their protector; and let the believers place their trust in The God.”

هَمَّتْ here concerns two groups of believers.

طَائِفَتَانِ denotes two groups, while the preceding verb is feminine singular, in accordance with Arabic agreement when the verb precedes its subject.

أَنْ تَفْشَلَا identifies the object of their inclination.

Fashal here concerns weakness, lost firmness, and faltering in confrontation.

The groups need not have consciously decided to be defeated; people do not normally enter battle intending defeat.

The natural sense is that they approached faltering or withdrawal, or developed such an inclination.

Hamm can therefore describe an inclination or inward readiness for conduct not yet realized.

The continuation identifies The God as their protector and directs believers to trust Him.

This usage does not make every hamm a final, unchangeable decision.

Under difficulty, someone may incline toward faltering but turn away through changed circumstances, remembered responsibility, or regained trust.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:113 — Intending to mislead

Al-Nisāʾ 4:113 states that, were it not for The God’s favor and mercy upon Muhammad, a group would have intended to mislead him.

لَهَمَّتْ is connected with the conditional construction لَوْلَا.

The conditional presents divine favor and mercy as protection; the continuation denies the opponents’ desired result. The conditional intention should not simply be turned into an unqualified report of a successful action.

The passage distinguishes intention from attainment of its goal.

People may plan to mislead someone, but intention or planning does not establish success.

The continuation says that they mislead only themselves and do not harm Muhammad.

An intention to deceive and actual deception remain distinct: a person can plan deception without the other being deceived.

The negative value here comes from the intended action, misleading, rather than from hamm itself.

Al-Māʾidah 5:11 — Intending harm

«يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اذْكُرُوا نِعْمَتَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ إِذْ هَمَّ قَوْمٌ أَنْ يَبْسُطُوا إِلَيْكُمْ أَيْدِيَهُمْ فَكَفَّ أَيْدِيَهُمْ عَنْكُمْ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَعَلَى اللَّهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ»

Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū udhkurū niʿmata Allāhi ʿalaykum idh hamma qawmun an yabsuṭū ilaykum aydiyahum fa-kaffa aydiyahum ʿankum wa-ittaqū Allāha wa-ʿalā Allāhi fa-l-yatawakkali al-muʾminūn.

“You who have believed, remember The God’s favor upon you when a people intended to stretch their hands toward you, but He restrained their hands from you. Be mindful of The God, and let the believers place their trust in The God.”

هَمَّ identifies a group’s intention to act against believers.

أَنْ يَبْسُطُوا إِلَيْكُمْ أَيْدِيَهُمْ identifies what they intended.

In context, stretching their hands toward believers concerns aggression and harm.

فَكَفَّ أَيْدِيَهُمْ عَنْكُمْ then says their hands were restrained.

Intending harm and carrying it out are therefore separated.

Hamm alone does not establish that every person completed practical action. The verse describes their intention and the restraint of their hands.

The root itself does not mean attacking, striking, or killing; these arise from the intended action and confrontation context.

Al-Tawbah 9:13 — Intending to expel the Messenger

Al-Tawbah 9:13 describes people who broke their oaths and intended to expel the Messenger.

وَهَمُّوا بِإِخْرَاجِ الرَّسُولِ expresses that intention.

Hammū is followed by bāʾ and the verbal noun ikhrāj.

إِخْرَاج belongs to خ ر ج and expresses expelling.

Hammū expresses intention; إِخْرَاجِ الرَّسُولِ identifies its object.

The verse also says that they initiated the confrontation.

These are not additions to hamm’s lexical meaning. Breaking oaths and initiating war are expressed by independent clauses.

Al-Tawbah 9:74 — An intention that failed to attain its object

Al-Tawbah 9:74 says وَهَمُّوا بِمَا لَمْ يَنَالُوا.

They intended something they did not attain.

This is a particularly clear Qur’anic distinction between intention and realization.

هَمُّوا expresses their intention.

لَمْ يَنَالُوا expresses their failure to attain the goal.

The sentence itself shows that hamm can exist without attainment.

It does not alone determine how much practical action occurred. An intention may stop at planning, or actions may take place without the desired result.

Historical reports identify particular plots, but neither the root’s meaning nor the verse’s direct statement depends on accepting one specific report.

The text establishes intended action and failure to attain its object.

Ghāfir 40:5 — Intending to seize the Messengers

«كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ وَالْأَحْزَابُ مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ وَهَمَّتْ كُلُّ أُمَّةٍ بِرَسُولِهِمْ لِيَأْخُذُوهُ وَجَادَلُوا بِالْبَاطِلِ لِيُدْحِضُوا بِهِ الْحَقَّ فَأَخَذْتُهُمْ فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عِقَابِ»

Kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥin wa-al-aḥzābu min baʿdihim wa-hammat kullu ummatin bi-rasūlihim li-yaʾkhudhūhu wa-jādalū bi-al-bāṭili li-yudḥiḍū bihi al-ḥaqqa fa-akhadhtuhum fa-kayfa kāna ʿiqāb.

“Before them, Noah’s people and the factions after them denied; every community intended to seize its Messenger, and they disputed with falsehood to overthrow the truth by it. So I seized them; how, then, was My punishment?”

هَمَّتْ is followed by بِرَسُولِهِمْ; لِيَأْخُذُوهُ clarifies the aim.

They intended to seize or capture their Messenger.

هَمَّتْ بِرَسُولِهِمْ should therefore not be translated independently of لِيَأْخُذُوهُ.

Hamm itself does not mean capture: intention and taking are expressed by different roots.

These communities denied their Messengers and planned opposition. Their conduct does not give hamm an inherent negative moral value.

The same verb can elsewhere express intending a worthy deed.

5. Hamm and lawlā in Joseph’s story

Yūsuf 12:24

«وَلَقَدْ هَمَّتْ بِهِ وَهَمَّ بِهَا لَوْلَا أَنْ رَأَىٰ بُرْهَانَ رَبِّهِ كَذَٰلِكَ لِنَصْرِفَ عَنْهُ السُّوءَ وَالْفَحْشَاءَ إِنَّهُ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا الْمُخْلَصِينَ»

Wa-laqad hammat bihi wa-hamma bihā lawlā an raʾā burhāna rabbihi kadhālika li-naṣrifa ʿanhu al-sūʾa wa-al-faḥshāʾa innahu min ʿibādinā al-mukhlaṣīn.

“She certainly intended him; and he would have intended her had he not seen his Lord’s proof. Thus it was, so that We might turn evil and indecency away from him; he was indeed among Our chosen servants.”

The same verb occurs for two people, but with the qualification لَوْلَا أَنْ رَأَىٰ بُرْهَانَ رَبِّهِ.

Yūsuf 12:23 describes the woman soliciting Joseph, closing the doors, and calling him to her. He seeks refuge in The God and declares wrongdoing unsuccessful.

Yūsuf 12:24 then addresses her intention and his.

Intention and performance

هَمَّتْ بِهِ states her intention toward him.

Even here, the verb does not itself mean completion of the intended act. Details of her conduct, including closing doors and inviting him, come from the surrounding verses.

وَهَمَّ بِهَا requires the same care.

The verb concerns intention or inclination, but the following lawlā qualifies the construction.

The role of lawlā

Here lawlā introduces a circumstance whose presence prevents a result.

“Without his help, I would have fallen,” for example, describes an event prevented by the help.

In Yūsuf 12:24, seeing his Lord’s proof is connected with Joseph’s hamm.

Two familiar analyses occur in the grammatical and interpretive tradition.

One links وَهَمَّ بِهَا in meaning to lawlā: had Joseph not seen his Lord’s proof, he would have intended her. The proof prevents the intention itself in this reading.

Another allows an inward inclination while understanding the divine proof to prevent its continuation into an improper act.

The analyses differ over the stage at which the proof intervened.

Grammarians also disagree whether lawlā’s answer can precede it or whether the preceding words merely indicate an omitted answer.

Neither reading should be declared the root’s certain lexical meaning merely because of a theological theory about Prophets.

Intention, inward inclination, and practical resolve

Hamm can describe inclination or intention not yet realized in action.

That does not necessarily make it an involuntary, fleeting sensation.

Wanting, contemplating, and deciding are related states, but differ in intensity and practical responsibility.

Human attraction or the possibility of inclination cannot be ruled out solely by invoking Joseph’s prophetic status. The Qur’an presents Prophets as human beings.

Conversely, hamm alone does not establish that Joseph committed an improper act.

The verse mentions the Lord’s proof and evil and indecency being turned away. In Yūsuf 12:25 both race toward the door and his shirt is torn from behind, fitting his effort to leave the situation.

In Yūsuf 12:33 he prefers prison to what the women invite him to and asks The God for help.

The wider story emphasizes resisting the improper invitation and the importance of divine guidance.

Were both intentions identical?

Using one verb for two people does not establish identical objects, intensity, stages, or outcomes of intention.

She had already acted toward her aim. Joseph’s clause involves lawlā, and the stated outcome concerns evil and indecency being turned away from him.

Even if intention or inclination is accepted for both, these differences prevent treating their stages and outcomes as identical without further examination.

Interpreting Joseph’s intention as striking or repelling her also requires independent evidence. The verse does not explicitly identify that object; the context highlights sexual solicitation and avoidance.

A root study should retain intention or inclination toward action. The proof’s role, whether intention arose, and moral assessment require syntax and the wider story, not alteration of hamm’s lexical meaning.

6. أَهَمَّتْ — Concern and preoccupation

Āl ʿImrān 3:154

The verse contains أَهَمَّتْهُمْ أَنْفُسُهُمْ.

This construction differs from those above.

أَهَمَّتْ is a Form IV perfect verb.

هُمْ refers to the group and is the object.

أَنْفُسُهُمْ is the subject: their own selves or lives occupied their minds.

A natural rendering is:

“A group whom concern for themselves had engrossed.”

Or:

“A group concerned only with themselves.”

The second fits the context, but “only” reflects interpretation of their condition, not an independent meaning of Form IV.

Context

The preceding passage concerns believers’ difficult battlefield situation and the sorrow and confusion following the fighting.

Āl ʿImrān 3:154 distinguishes two groups.

One receives security and drowsiness after sorrow.

Another is preoccupied with itself and holds wrongful assumptions about The God.

The expression is therefore not merely a reference to natural fear; the continuation describes their outlook and speech.

They think wrongly about divine ordering, conceal things inwardly, and assess the battle in a particular way.

Personal concern dominates their outlook and judgment, rather than simply existing as concern for life.

Is concern for one’s life blameworthy?

The root itself gives no such verdict.

Illness, war, danger, or threats can make someone anxious about life and the future. That feeling alone establishes neither moral corruption nor weak faith.

The criticism in Āl ʿImrān 3:154 concerns the group’s wider conduct and assumptions.

It should not be extended automatically to everyone concerned for personal safety.

Nevertheless, personal anxiety can dominate someone so completely that responsibilities and circumstances are no longer judged fairly.

Lexically, ahamma here means occupying the mind or causing preoccupation. Moral evaluation comes from context.

Hamma and ahamma

In Qur’anic hamma, a person intends or inclines toward an action.

In أَهَمَّتْهُمْ, a matter weighs on people’s minds and preoccupies them.

The difference is not merely intensity: sentence structure and the direction of the relationship change.

In هَمَّ بِالأَمْرِ, the person turns toward doing something.

In أَهَمَّهُ الْأَمْرُ, the matter arouses the person’s attention, concern, or preoccupation.

This Qur’anic distinction should not become an absolute rule for every Arabic usage of the two forms.

7. Comparison with nearby roots

ه م م and ع ز م — Intention and firm resolve

ʿAzm concerns decision and firmness of will.

Hamm can also be serious, but sometimes describes an inclination or decision not yet fully settled.

The groups in Āl ʿImrān 3:122 approach faltering; this need not be a final decision to withdraw.

Decision-related ʿazm can emphasize established resolve to act.

Yet hamm is not invariably weak and ʿazm invariably final. Arabic lexicons sometimes explain hamm through ʿazm.

The distinction is a tendency requiring examination in each verse.

ه م م and ر و د — Particular intention and willing

Irādah broadly concerns wanting and directing one’s will toward something.

Qur’anic hamm more often concerns intending or inclining toward a particular action.

Someone may desire justice and a better life as a general direction of will.

When intending a particular step toward that aim, hamm may also be appropriate.

The distinction is not absolute; the terms overlap.

Irādah itself does not invariably establish realization. Wanting, deciding, and doing must remain distinct in the sentence.

ه م م and ق ص د — Turning toward a goal and intending an act

Qaṣd concerns directing oneself toward a goal and, in some usages, moderation.

Volitional hamm likewise concerns intending action.

Not every qaṣd involves worry or inner struggle, and not every hamm becomes physical movement toward a goal.

Someone may intend to travel without having made preparations.

Hamm can then concern inward decision, while qaṣd in other constructions may emphasize movement’s direction or goal.

Evidence for each usage must govern the distinction.

ه م م and ك ي د — Intention and planning

Qur’anic kayd concerns planning, devising, and in some contexts plotting against another.

Hamm can precede implementing a plan but does not itself mean a secret plot.

In Ghāfir 40:5 communities intend to seize their Messengers; the context concerns opposition to them.

The root itself expresses intention. Details of planning, secrecy, and deception require other evidence.

A plot may involve intention, but not every intention is a plot.

ه م م and غ م م — Preoccupation and sorrow

Qur’anic ghamm concerns sorrow and oppressive distress.

Āl ʿImrān 3:153 describes sorrow upon sorrow; Āl ʿImrān 3:154 then describes a group through أَهَمَّتْهُمْ أَنْفُسُهُمْ.

Their contextual proximity helps comparison.

Ghamm describes distress; ahammat-hum here identifies what occupies their thought and concern.

They can coexist without being identical.

The intention branch of hamm requires no sorrow at all.

Translating every form as grief would erase real differences.

ه م م and ح ز ن — Concern and sadness

Ḥuzn concerns sadness or sorrow.

Hamm can share that sense in Arabic, but also has a distinct intention branch.

Some lexical explanations associate hamm more with concern over what lies ahead and ḥuzn more with suffering already experienced.

That can illuminate particular usages but is not an absolute language-wide rule.

A person may worry about the future while grieving the past, or one matter may arouse both.

The sentence and context determine the appropriate sense.

ه م م and ه م ز — Intention and pricking or faultfinding

These independent roots differ only in their final consonant.

Some Qur’anic hamz usages concern taunting and faultfinding.

Qur’anic hamm concerns intention and preoccupation.

Someone may intend humiliation: the intention could be hamm, while the humiliating behavior could be expressed through hamz.

Sharing a situation does not make the roots identical.

ه م م and ه م س — Inward intention and faint sound

Hams concerns quiet sound and movement.

Hamm can be entirely inward, with no overt speech or behavior yet arising.

One can hold an unspoken intention or speak quietly without intending a particular act.

Hidden intention and faint sound are distinct concepts.

8. Interpretation and translation challenges

A difficulty arises when hamm is restricted either to an irrevocable decision or to a fleeting thought.

Both restrictions can mislead.

Rendering Āl ʿImrān 3:122 as a “final decision to be defeated” determines intention’s intensity and object beyond the verse.

Treating al-Māʾidah 5:11 as a merely passing thought can unjustifiably diminish the threat’s seriousness.

In al-Tawbah 9:74 the intention existed, but its goal was not attained.

In Yūsuf 12:24 lawlā is crucial to the relationship between intention and its prevention.

No single equivalent adequately explains every instance without syntax and context.

Suggested translations for Forqan

For هَمَّتْ in Āl ʿImrān 3:122, “were on the verge of” or “were close to” suits the two groups’ faltering.

Conditional لَهَمَّتْ in al-Nisāʾ 4:113 suits “would have intended.”

هَمَّ قَوْمٌ in al-Māʾidah 5:11 suits “a people intended.”

وَهَمُّوا بِإِخْرَاجِ الرَّسُولِ in al-Tawbah 9:13 means “and they intended to expel the Messenger.”

وَهَمُّوا بِمَا لَمْ يَنَالُوا in al-Tawbah 9:74 means “and they intended what they did not attain.”

In Ghāfir 40:5, لِيَأْخُذُوهُ identifies the aim as seizing the Messenger.

In Yūsuf 12:24, هَمَّتْ بِهِ describes her intention; وَهَمَّ بِهَا must be read with لَوْلَا أَنْ رَأَىٰ بُرْهَانَ رَبِّهِ. The base translation must preserve the conditional connection and the proof’s role.

أَهَمَّتْهُمْ أَنْفُسُهُمْ in Āl ʿImrān 3:154 suits “concern for themselves had engrossed them” or “they were preoccupied with themselves.”

This last expression should not be rendered as intending an action: its construction and direction differ.

9. Conclusion

The principal branches of ه م م include intending action, concern and preoccupation, and physical melting or wearing away.

Ṭarafah’s attributed poem distinguishes intention from performance. Kuthayyir’s poem presents mutual inclination checked by hesitation and modesty.

Qur’anic hamma concerns different actions: believers approaching faltering, opponents intending aggression or expulsion of the Messenger, and intentions failing to attain their goal.

In Joseph’s story, lawlā and the Lord’s proof are essential to understanding intention and prevention.

أَهَمَّتْ in Āl ʿImrān 3:154 illustrates preoccupation directing attention toward oneself.

Forqan definition: In its volitional branch, ه م م denotes forming an intention or inclination toward action. It can be weak or serious, but the word alone does not establish performance. Its psychological branch concerns worry or matters occupying the mind. Qur’anic أَهَمَّ expresses being engrossed in such concern.

The root has no inherent positive or negative moral value. One may intend a worthy or wrongful deed, or experience legitimate concern or concern that distorts judgment.

Intention, firm resolve, practical action, and achieved results must remain distinct. That distinction is central to these Qur’anic usages.
