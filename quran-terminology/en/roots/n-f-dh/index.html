The meaning of nafadh in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from an arrow passing through and a route reaching a destination to effective commands and passing beyond the bounds of the heavens and earth

The three-consonant root ن ف ذ in Arabic concerns passing through, crossing, finding a way and proceeding to a destination or beyond a boundary. Words from this root are used when an arrow passes through a target’s body, a road leads from one place to another or a letter reaches its destination.

Another branch concerns something being carried through or a command taking effect. Familiar forms include nufūdh, nāfidh, infādh and tanfīdh. Persian likewise uses nufūdh for influence and power, and tanfīdh for putting a command or ruling into effect.

Nevertheless, the entire family must not be confined to political power or influence over others. In its familiar core sense, nafadh can describe physically passing through an object or crossing a boundary; effective commands and power belong to extended uses of the same family.

The root occurs in only one Qur’anic verse, in three verbal forms: تَنْفُذُوا, فَانْفُذُوا and تَنْفُذُونَ in Al-Raḥmān 55:33. Jinn and humans are told that if they can pass beyond the bounds of the heavens and earth, they should do so; yet they cannot pass except with sulṭān.

This raises questions about crossing, created beings’ capacities and their relationship to the power and sovereignty of The God. Answering requires first understanding nafadh through Arabic usage, then examining aqṭār, istaṭaʿtum and sulṭān within the verse’s context.

1. Core Meaning and Forms

Important Arabic forms include:

  • نَفَذَ، يَنْفُذُ: Passed through, crossed, reached a destination or took effect.
  • نَفَاذ و نُفُوذ: Passing through, crossing and taking effect; in some uses, power or influence.
  • نَفَذ: A passage or opening; also carrying something into effect.
  • نَفْذ: A verbal noun concerning an arrow passing through; in one use, its tip emerging from the other side of a target.
  • نَافِذ: Passing through; in other uses, an effective person or a command with operative force.
  • نَفَّاذ و نَفُوذ: Someone or something that proceeds effectively in affairs and brings about an effect.
  • مَنْفَذ: A passage, crossing point or exit.
  • أَنْفَذَ: Made something pass through, conveyed it or carried it into effect.
  • نَفَّذَ و تَنْفِيذ: Executing or putting a command or decision into practice.
  • نَافَذَ و مُنَافَذَة: In some Arabic uses, engaging another in argument, contest or adjudication of a dispute.

The Qur’an uses only the basic verb, in imperfect and imperative forms. Derivatives such as nufūdh, nāfidh, manfadh, anfadha and tanfīdh do not occur in its text.

The three Qur’anic forms are not three independent derivatives. They are grammatical forms of one verb, used in one sentence for the condition, command and statement of limitation.

Semantic Core

In its principal uses, nafadh denotes passing through something, crossing a boundary or proceeding to the other side or a destination.

Several situations illustrate this meaning.

An arrow passes into a target or emerges from its other side. A road leads through one place to another. A letter travels from sender to recipient. A command goes beyond being spoken or issued and takes effect in practice.

Each involves passing or reaching an outcome, though in different ways. An arrow’s passage is physical movement; a letter’s arrival concerns its transmission; a command taking effect concerns its operative force and practical fulfilment.

The definition should therefore connect these branches without imposing the distinctive features of one on all the others.

An arrow passing through something does not make every occurrence mean perforating a body. Nor does an effective command make every use mean power, government or control over others.

2. Early Arabic Uses

An Arrow Passing Through and Opening a Passage

One clear use of nafadh concerns an arrow or spear passing through a target.

In the Arabic expression:

نَفَذَ السَّهْمُ مِنَ الرَّمِيَّةِ

nafadha al-sahmu mina al-ramiyyati

Translation: “The arrow passed through the target.”

Lexical explanations describe an arrow entering a body, with its tip emerging on the other side.

This is more than striking the surface: passage through the body and reaching the other side are involved.

Yet this feature must not be extended to every use. Saying that a letter reached someone does not require a particular physical obstacle to have been perforated.

Pre-Islamic Poetry: A Wound Passing Through the Body

Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm, a pre-Islamic poet of the Aws tribe, describes a blow against an enemy:

طَعَنْتُ ابْنَ عَبْدِ الْقَيْسِ طَعْنَةَ ثَائِرٍ
لَهَا نَفَذٌ لَوْلَا الشُّعَاعُ أَضَاءَهَا

ṭaʿantu ibna ʿabdi al-qaysi ṭaʿnata thāʾirin / lahā nafadhun lawlā al-shuʿāʿu aḍāʾahā

Translation: “I struck Ibn ʿAbd al-Qays with an avenger’s spear-thrust; it opened a passage through him that would have shone with light, had the scattered blood not obscured it.”

The poet continues the image in the next verse:

مَلَكْتُ بِهَا كَفِّي فَأَنْهَرْتُ فَتْقَهَا
يَرَى قَائِمًا مِنْ خَلْفِهَا مَا وَرَاءَهَا

malaktu bihā kaffī fa-anhartu fatqahā / yarā qāʾiman min khalfihā mā warāʾahā

Translation: “I held my hand firm in delivering it and opened the wound’s cleft, so that someone behind it could see what lay beyond.”

The poet exaggerates the blow’s severity. Nevertheless, nafadh has a clear meaning: the opening or passage created in the body by the thrust.

This witness matters for the material sense of the root, directly connecting it with passing through an object and reaching the other side.

These verses are transmitted in early Arabic poetry and lexical records to illustrate nafadh. Some word readings and explanations of the blood-and-light image differ, but those differences do not alter nafadh as a penetrating opening.

The witness shows that passing through had a clear use in Arabic before the Qur’an.

A Through Road and a Passage

Arabic uses طريق نافذ for a road along which one can pass and reach somewhere.

For example:

طَرِيقٌ نَافِذٌ

ṭarīqun nāfidhun

Translation: “A through road leading elsewhere.”

Lexical explanations also allow such a road to be open to the public rather than restricted to a particular group.

It is also said:

هَذَا الطَّرِيقُ يَنْفُذُ إِلَى مَكَانٍ

hādhā al-ṭarīqu yanfudhu ilā makānin

Translation: “This road leads to a place.”

Here, nafadh concerns more than movement: it expresses the possibility of passage and reaching a destination.

Manfadh belongs to the same field: a place through which one can pass or exit.

In modern Persian, manfadh sometimes denotes a small hole or aperture. This relates to passage, but smallness or narrowness is not essential to the root’s meaning.

A broad road can also be nāfidh if it permits passage toward another destination.

A Letter Arriving and a Command Taking Effect

Another Arabic expression is:

نَفَذَ الْكِتَابُ إِلَى فُلَانٍ

nafadha al-kitābu ilā fulānin

Translation: “The letter reached so-and-so.”

Nafadh here denotes reaching a destination. The letter may have travelled a long route, but the sentence focuses on its arrival at the recipient.

Also:

نَفَذَ الْأَمْرُ

nafadha al-amru

Translation: “The command took effect” or “The command was carried out.”

Here, no physical object passes through an obstacle. Instead, an issued command is fulfilled in practice.

Accordingly, أمر نافذ describes a command that has operative force and is obeyed.

أنفذ الأمر and نفّذ الأمر likewise mean putting a command into effect.

These uses do not establish “political power” as the original meaning. Effective commands form one branch, related to something proceeding to fulfilment and practical effect.

An Effective or Influential Person

Arabic uses رجل نافذ في أمره for someone who advances in affairs and whose decisions or actions take effect.

Nāfidh here describes someone whose work does not remain stalled but reaches an outcome.

In modern Persian, an “influential person” more often means someone able to affect decisions, people or institutions.

This relates to the Arabic meaning, but the whole modern concept of social and political influence must not be assigned to every early use.

Influence also need not mean force, coercion or unlawful control. A person may influence an affair through position, knowledge, reputation or lawful authority.

The root itself does not determine influence’s moral character. Power can serve justice or oppression; assessment depends on conduct and context.

3. Qur’anic Use: Al-Raḥmān 55:33

يَا مَعْشَرَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنسِ إِنِ اسْتَطَعْتُمْ أَن تَنفُذُوا مِنْ أَقْطَارِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ فَانفُذُوا لَا تَنفُذُونَ إِلَّا بِسُلْطَانٍ

yā maʿshara al-jinni wa-al-insi ini istaṭaʿtum an tanfudhū min aqṭāri al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi fa-unfudhū lā tanfudhūna illā bi-sulṭānin

Translation: “O assembly of jinn and humans, if you can pass beyond the bounds of the heavens and earth, then pass; you do not pass except with a power.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:33

This is the root’s only location in the Qur’an.

The verb occurs three times in one sentence, each with a different role.

أَن تَنفُذُوا occurs in the conditional construction and concerns the ability to pass through.

فَانفُذُوا is an imperative: “Then pass.”

لَا تَنفُذُونَ is a negative declarative clause expressing a limit on their ability.

The repetition highlights the relationship between a claim of ability, a challenge to act and the condition needed for fulfilment.

Nafadh in This Verse

Nafadh here means passing through or crossing.

مِنْ أَقْطَارِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ places the passage at the bounds of an expanse.

“Pass beyond” or “cross” is therefore natural and appropriate.

Although Arabic also uses the root for effective commands and fulfilled affairs, this verse does not concern a ruling taking effect or political influence.

The Persian equivalent “penetrate” may evoke infiltration, intelligence operations or influence over others for a modern reader. Such associations do not fit this construction.

“Pass beyond” conveys the meaning more clearly here.

أَقْطَارِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ

Aqṭār is the plural of quṭr, here concerning bounds, regions and surrounding areas.

It belongs to Q Ṭ R rather than N F DH, but matters for understanding the verb because it identifies the expanse whose limits are to be crossed.

Passing through a region can mean traversing its interior. Here, however, نفذ من أقطار more strongly suggests passing beyond its bounds and outside the limits of an expanse.

It should therefore not be restricted to travelling from one point on earth to another.

The verse does not explain the heavens’ geometry, the shape of their boundaries, their distances or a physical route across them.

Nafadh and aqṭār alone cannot yield a theory of the universe’s structure.

إِنِ اسْتَطَعْتُمْ: The Condition of Ability

إِنِ اسْتَطَعْتُمْ means “if you were able” or “if you can.”

The sentence makes performing the action dependent on the addressees’ ability.

A condition alone, however, does not establish that they possess that ability. Someone might say, “If you can cross this obstacle, cross it,” while intending to challenge the other person’s ability.

Conversely, a conditional construction alone does not prove the action eternally impossible in every situation and by every means.

The continuation and context must determine the intended point.

فَانفُذُوا: Command or Challenge?

Grammatically, فَانفُذُوا is an imperative.

Not every imperative, however, imposes an obligation. Imperatives can grant permission, invite, threaten or challenge an addressee.

Here, “If you can ... then pass” fits a challenge to their ability.

It is immediately followed by:

لَا تَنفُذُونَ إِلَّا بِسُلْطَانٍ

lā tanfudhūna illā bi-sulṭānin

“You do not pass except with a power.”

The verse is therefore more than a simple invitation to travel: it presents the capacity of jinn and humans in relation to divine power and sovereignty.

That conclusion follows from the complete statement and context, rather than the verb nafadh alone.

4. إِلَّا بِسُلْطَانٍ: Power, Authority or Proof?

The meaning of sulṭān is a significant question in this verse.

Sulṭān belongs to S L Ṭ and has Qur’anic uses concerning power, control, authority and proof.

For example, in Ibrāhīm 14:22, Satan says:

وَمَا كَانَ لِيَ عَلَيْكُم مِّن سُلْطَانٍ إِلَّا أَن دَعَوْتُكُمْ فَاسْتَجَبْتُمْ لِي

wa-mā kāna liya ʿalaykum min sulṭānin illā an daʿawtukum fa-istajabtum lī

Translation: “I had no authority over you, except that I called you and you answered me.”

Here, sulṭān concerns power or authority by which others could have been controlled.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:65 also says:

إِنَّ عِبَادِي لَيْسَ لَكَ عَلَيْهِمْ سُلْطَانٌ

inna ʿibādī laysa laka ʿalayhim sulṭānun

Translation: “Indeed, you have no authority over My servants.”

Elsewhere in the Qur’an, sulṭān is also connected with clear proof or evidence.

The word therefore has a broad semantic range and must not be confined to one equivalent in every verse.

Sulṭān in Al-Raḥmān 55:33

لَا تَنفُذُونَ إِلَّا بِسُلْطَانٍ identifies sulṭān as the condition for passage.

“Power,” “force” or “the necessary authority” are defensible senses in this context.

Capacity, authority and proof nevertheless differ. Someone may have the physical or technical capacity to act without lawful authority, while establishing a claim requires evidence.

Interpretations of this verse have variously explained sulṭān as power, proof, or divine command or permission.

This disagreement does not concern nafadh itself, which still means passing through. The issue is what kind of sulṭān the passage requires.

The construction clearly denies passage without sulṭān. The phrase alone does not determine precisely what kind of capacity or authority is meant.

The surrounding verses, concerning the power of The God and created beings’ inability before Him, provide an important clue to this limitation.

Does the Exception Establish That Passage Is Possible?

Arabic لا ... إلا denies an action except under specified conditions.

The verse denies passage without sulṭān. Its exception must therefore not disappear from translation.

An exception does not necessarily establish that the addressees actually possess the required sulṭān in the situation described.

For example, “You cannot pass here except with an authority you do not possess” states a necessary condition without establishing that the addressee has it.

The wording thus makes passage dependent on sulṭān; its actual fulfilment, the type of sulṭān and the ultimate point of the passage must be assessed in context.

5. Context: Crossing the Bounds or Escaping Accountability?

Understanding Al-Raḥmān 55:33 requires more than isolating nafadh from the surrounding verses.

Al-Raḥmān 55:26 and Al-Raḥmān 55:27 say:

كُلُّ مَنْ عَلَيْهَا فَانٍ ۝ وَيَبْقَىٰ وَجْهُ رَبِّكَ ذُو الْجَلَالِ وَالْإِكْرَامِ

kullu man ʿalayhā fānin / wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika dhū al-jalāli wa-al-ikrāmi

Translation: “Everyone upon it is passing away; and the face of your Lord, Possessor of majesty and honour, remains.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:31 then addresses the two groups:

سَنَفْرُغُ لَكُمْ أَيُّهَ الثَّقَلَانِ

sa-nafrughu lakum ayyuhā al-thaqalāni

Translation: “We shall soon attend to you, O two weighty groups.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:33 then challenges jinn and humans to pass beyond the bounds of the heavens and earth.

Al-Raḥmān 55:35 also says:

يُرْسَلُ عَلَيْكُمَا شُوَاظٌ مِّن نَّارٍ وَنُحَاسٌ فَلَا تَنتَصِرَانِ

yursalu ʿalaykumā shuwāẓun min nārin wa-nuḥāsun fa-lā tantaṣirāni

Translation: “A flame of fire and molten copper will be sent upon you two groups, and you will not be able to defend yourselves.”

Later verses describe the sky splitting and the condition of the guilty in the scene of judgment.

This continuity situates the passage amid divine power, created beings’ mortality and accountability.

Is the Point Escape from the Sovereignty of The God?

One familiar interpretation understands the verse as challenging jinn and humans before divine power: if you can leave the domain of the sovereignty of The God and escape His judgment, do so; yet you cannot.

This reading fits the context of warning and accountability.

Crossing the bounds of the heavens and earth would then represent a hypothetical escape route from the situation confronting jinn and humans.

Yet “escaping accountability” is not the lexical meaning of nafadh. The verb means passing through; escape from accountability is an explanation drawn from the context and the addressees’ situation.

Al-Raḥmān 55:33 also does not explicitly say “Leave My sovereignty.” That interpretive conclusion must remain distinct from the phrase’s direct meaning.

Is the Verse about Escaping Death?

Another interpretation in exegetical history connects crossing the bounds with an attempt to escape death.

On this reading, jinn and humans are reminded that wherever they go, they cannot escape death or the power of The God.

This meaning fits other Qur’anic passages, such as:

أَيْنَمَا تَكُونُوا يُدْرِككُّمُ الْمَوْتُ وَلَوْ كُنتُمْ فِي بُرُوجٍ مُّشَيَّدَةٍ

aynamā takūnū yudrikkumu al-mawtu wa-law kuntum fī burūjin mushayyadatin

Translation: “Wherever you are, death will overtake you, even if you are in fortified towers.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:78

Nevertheless, death is not mentioned directly in Al-Raḥmān 55:33. Connecting it with escape from death is a contextual and Qur’anic reading, rather than a meaning conveyed by nafadh alone.

Does the Qur’an Declare Space Travel Forbidden or Impossible?

Some contemporary readings connect this verse with space travel and humanity’s ability to go beyond the earth’s atmosphere.

Some take إِلَّا بِسُلْطَانٍ to mean that crossing the bounds becomes possible through scientific and technical power. Others take the verse as proof that human space travel is impossible.

Both go beyond the text if presented as its certain meaning.

First, the verse names no spacecraft, technology, propulsion or journey from earth to the moon or other bodies.

Second, أقطار السماوات والأرض concerns the bounds of an expanse far wider than the atmosphere. Leaving the atmosphere or reaching the moon cannot, without evidence, be equated with passing beyond all the bounds of the heavens and earth.

Third, the context concerns accountability, divine power and the position of jinn and humans before it. That setting cannot be discarded in favour of treating the verse solely as a prediction of technological progress.

Conversely, the verse does not say that no human can use natural means and laws to leave the earth or travel within part of its surrounding space.

Crossing a region within creation differs from leaving the domain of divine power and sovereignty.

This verse is therefore neither scientific proof of space travel nor conclusive evidence that such travel is forbidden or impossible.

Nafadh still literally means passing through; in Al-Raḥmān, that passage is set before a power on which jinn and humans necessarily depend.

6. Comparison with Related Roots

N F DH and N F D

These roots are close in writing and pronunciation but differ in meaning.

N F DH, with dhāl, concerns passing through, penetration and taking effect.

N F D, with dāl, concerns running out, ending or the exhaustion of an amount or supply.

Al-Naḥl 16:96 says:

مَا عِندَكُمْ يَنفَدُ وَمَا عِندَ اللَّهِ بَاقٍ

mā ʿindakum yanfadu wa-mā ʿinda Allāhi bāqin

Translation: “What you have runs out, while what is with The God remains.”

يَنْفَدُ here belongs to N F D rather than N F DH.

Al-Kahf 18:109 also says:

لَنَفِدَ الْبَحْرُ قَبْلَ أَن تَنفَدَ كَلِمَاتُ رَبِّي

la-nafida al-baḥru qabla an tanfada kalimātu rabbī

Translation: “The sea would run out before the words of my Lord ran out.”

Here too, the subject is exhaustion rather than passage.

Nufūdh and nafād must therefore remain distinct.

The distinction also matters in Persian: an effective command concerns influence or execution, whereas the exhaustion of a supply concerns its running out.

Similarity in sound alone does not establish a historical relationship between the roots.

N F DH and KH R J

Khurūj, in its familiar sense, concerns coming out.

Nafadh can also concern going out, but its principal uses highlight passing through something and reaching the other side.

Someone leaving a house can naturally be described with kharaja. Nafadha is more suitable when an arrow passes through a body or a road is open from one region to another.

In Al-Raḥmān 55:33, nafadh combined with aqṭār concerns passing beyond the bounds.

This is a tendency in usage, rather than an absolute claim that khurūj never involves crossing a limit or nafadh never means going out.

Study of KH R J

N F DH and J W Z

Jāza concerns crossing or passing, and overlaps with nafadha in some uses.

Nafadh can particularly highlight passing through an interior, reaching the other side or something taking effect.

Jawāz has a broader range of passing or crossing in certain constructions.

This closeness explains why lexical accounts gloss nafādh through jawāz.

The roots must therefore not be separated by an absolute boundary without overlap. Their differences depend on construction and context.

Study of J W Z

N F DH and N Q DH

Familiar uses of N Q DH concern rescuing someone from danger.

For example, Āl ʿImrān 3:103 uses أَنْقَذَكُم in the context of rescue from fire.

Nafadh, however, concerns passing through, crossing or reaching.

Rescue may involve bringing someone out of danger, but that shared result does not give the roots the same meaning.

Al-Raḥmān 55:33 concerns crossing the bounds. If escape from accountability enters its interpretation, that follows from context and must not become the lexical meaning of nafadh itself.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For nafadha in contexts of passage, appropriate Persian equivalents mean “passed,” “crossed” or, in some constructions, “emerged from the other side.”

For an arrow, “passed through the target” or “penetrated the target” may fit.

For طريق نافذ, “a through road” or “a road leading elsewhere” is more natural.

نَفَذَ الْكِتَابُ إِلَى فُلَانٍ can be rendered “The letter reached so-and-so.”

نَفَذَ الْأَمْرُ can clearly be rendered “The command took effect” or “The command was carried out.”

أَنْفَذَ الْأَمْرَ and نَفَّذَ الْأَمْرَ can be rendered “He put the command into effect.”

In Al-Raḥmān 55:33, however, “passing beyond” or “crossing” is the most suitable equivalent.

Forqan’s existing Persian translation preserves the verb’s core meaning and the construction well. Rendered into English:

يَا مَعْشَرَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنسِ إِنِ اسْتَطَعْتُمْ أَن تَنفُذُوا مِنْ أَقْطَارِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ فَانفُذُوا لَا تَنفُذُونَ إِلَّا بِسُلْطَانٍ

yā maʿshara al-jinni wa-al-insi ini istaṭaʿtum an tanfudhū min aqṭāri al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi fa-unfudhū lā tanfudhūna illā bi-sulṭānin

“O assembly of jinn and humans, if you can pass beyond the bounds of the heavens and earth, then pass; you do not pass except with a power.”

Here, “pass” renders nafadh, and “power” is the chosen equivalent of sulṭān.

The kind of sulṭān and the actual possibility of passage belong to the verse’s explanation; they do not replace the verb’s direct meaning.

For Forqan’s word boxes:

Brief root meaning: Passing through, crossing and proceeding to a destination or the other side; in other uses, taking effect and being carried through.

تَنْفُذُوا in Al-Raḥmān 55:33: Pass beyond; within the conditional construction, crossing the bounds of the heavens and earth.

فَانفُذُوا in Al-Raḥmān 55:33: Then pass beyond; an imperative challenging the addressees’ ability.

تَنْفُذُونَ in Al-Raḥmān 55:33: You pass beyond; in the negative clause, passage whose fulfilment is made dependent on sulṭān.

These explanations distinguish the verb’s meaning from power and from the interpretation concerning escape from accountability.

8. Conclusion

N F DH in Arabic concerns passing through, crossing and proceeding to the other side or a destination. This appears clearly in an arrow passing through a target, a road leading elsewhere and a letter reaching its recipient.

Other branches concern commands taking effect and decisions being effective. Nufūdh, nāfidh, infādh and tanfīdh belong to this semantic field.

Uses involving power and the execution of commands must not be extended to the whole family. Nafadh can describe actual passage without any political or legal meaning.

The Qur’an uses the root only in Al-Raḥmān 55:33, in three forms of the basic verb. Jinn and humans are challenged to pass beyond the bounds of the heavens and earth, then told they cannot do so except with sulṭān.

Lexically, nafadh means passing through here. Aqṭār specifies the bounds to be crossed, and sulṭān states the condition for passage.

The context of divine power, created beings’ mortality and accountability supports a reading that challenges jinn and humans and highlights their inability to escape divine sovereignty.

This contextual reading must nevertheless remain distinct from the root’s own meaning.

Nor can the verse, without evidence, be treated as a prediction of space travel or a declaration that every human movement in space is impossible. It speaks of crossing the bounds of the heavens and earth, dependent on sulṭān, without explaining technical instruments, cosmic geometry or the physical method of such passage.

Final definition: N F DH primarily denotes passing through something, reaching the other side or proceeding to a destination. Extended uses include commands taking effect and their implementation. In its Qur’anic use, the root concerns passing beyond the bounds of the heavens and earth, within a context of divine power and judgment that denies such passage without sulṭān.
