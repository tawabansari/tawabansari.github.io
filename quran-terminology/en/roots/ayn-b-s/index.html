Core meaning: contracting the face and furrowing the brow, so that displeasure, tension, or strain becomes visible; descriptively, grimness and severity associated with hardship.

1. The root in early Arabic

The triliteral root ع ب س concerns frowning and a contracted, displeased expression. عَبَسَ describes a face losing its open, relaxed appearance as the brow and surrounding features draw together.

عَبَسَ وَجْهُهُ means “his face contracted,” and رَجُلٌ عَابِسٌ describes a man with a frowning expression.

This may result from anger, displeasure, worry, pain, or strain. Frowning alone does not identify its cause or a person's intention: someone may frown at another's conduct, while someone else does so from physical pain without being displeased with anyone.

Early usage is not confined to people. Animals too can be described this way, particularly when hardship and strain appear in their expression.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

The Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād, the pre-Islamic poet and warrior, includes:

وَالْخَيْلُ تَقْتَحِمُ الْخَبَارَ عَوَابِسًا

wa-l-khaylu taqtaḥimu al-khabāra ʿawābisan

مَا بَيْنَ شَيْظَمَةٍ وَأَجْرَدَ شَيْظَمِ

mā bayna shayẓamatin wa-ajrāda shayẓami

Translation: The horses plunge into soft, difficult ground, grim-faced: tall mares and tall, short-haired stallions.

الْخَبَار denotes soft, loose ground in which movement is difficult and a horse's feet may sink.

عَوَابِسًا is a plural of عَابِسَة and describes the horses' state as they cross this ground. Its tanwīn is a poetic allowance for a plural normally declining without tanwīn.

The poet depicts the horses as grim-faced amid exertion and difficult terrain. This illustrates the scene's hardship; it is not a precise physiological description of equine facial movement.

The witness matters because grimness in early Arabic is not confined to human anger or arrogance. Physical strain can also be depicted through an animal's expression.

The wording used here follows a transmitted reading in early lexicographical sources. Variants occur in collections of the poem; the lexical witness is عَوَابِسًا in the first half-line.

Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet and transmission among the Muʿallaqah's verses provide historical evidence for early usage, although the precise date of this line is not independently known.

The witness attests the description of a tense or contracted expression, especially amid strain and adversity, in that literary setting.

Another use: dried, adhering dirt

عَبَس also names substances such as dung and urine that dry and adhere to an animal's tail hair.

For this sense, dictionaries cite Abū al-Najm al-ʿIjlī, a poet of the Umayyad period:

كَأَنَّ فِي أَذْنَابِهِنَّ الشُّوَّلِ

ka-anna fī adhnābihinna al-shuwwali

مِنْ عَبَسِ الصَّيْفِ قُرُونَ الْأُيَّلِ

min ʿabasi al-ṣayfi qurūna al-uyyali

Translation: It is as though, on their raised tails, the summer's dried dirt has formed deer antlers.

عَبَسِ الصَّيْفِ refers to substances dried onto the camels' tail hair during summer.

Here the word names an adhering material, rather than a facial expression.

Some lexicographers relate these branches through contraction, adhesion, and an unpleasant resulting condition.

This relationship can be examined as a physical image, but it does not establish that frowning historically developed from dried dirt.

Abū al-Najm's witness is post-Quranic and alone does not establish this material sense in pre-Islamic usage.

Two evidential levels should therefore remain distinct: the facial sense is attested in poetry attributed to ʿAntarah, while the dried-dirt sense is recorded in dictionaries and later poetry.

2. Lexical forms and the word family

Recorded forms include عَبَسَ, يَعْبِسُ, عَبْس, عُبُوس, عَابِس, عَابِسَة, عَوَابِس, عَبُوس, عَبَّسَ, تَعْبِيس, تَعَبَّسَ, عَبَّاس, أَعْبَسَ, and عَنْبَس.

عَبَسَ: frowning

عَبَسَ is the perfect verb; يَعْبِسُ is its imperfect.

In the facial sense, it means frowning or displaying a contracted, displeased expression.

عَبَسَ الرَّجُلُ means “the man frowned.”

عَبَسَ وَجْهُهُ means “his face contracted into a frown.”

The constructions differ grammatically, but both describe a change of expression.

The basic verb does not necessarily include speaking, turning away, insulting, or harming someone. Such conduct may accompany frowning but has its own meaning.

عُبُوس: frowning and a grim expression

عُبُوس is an established verbal noun for frowning or facial contraction.

It may be temporary, as when someone hears unwelcome news or experiences distress.

It may also describe someone who habitually wears a grim expression.

A single frown does not establish a permanent personality trait.

عَابِس: a frowning person or creature

عَابِس is the active participle of عَبَسَ.

It describes someone with a contracted expression.

Its feminine is عَابِسَة. عُبُوس is recorded as a plural of عَابِس, and عَوَابِس as a plural of عَابِسَة. The plural عُبُوس is distinguished from the identically vocalized verbal noun by its grammatical use.

As ʿAntarah's verse shows, the description can extend to animals.

Lexically it describes appearance. The cause of the expression or the object of displeasure must be established from context.

عَبُوس: grim or severe

عَبُوس follows the pattern فَعُول and can describe a markedly grim person or a harsh, disagreeable situation.

It is used for people and for a difficult day.

يَوْمٌ عَبُوسٌ is a grim, difficult day whose terrors may make people frown.

The day has no human face or feelings: a human description is applied to the time and events that produce that state.

Applied to a person it may describe the face; applied to a day it emphasizes severity.

عَبَّسَ and تَعَبَّسَ: displaying a frown

Form II عَبَّسَ and Form V تَعَبَّسَ are recorded in Arabic.

They concern contracting the face and displaying grimness.

عَبَّسَ وَجْهَهُ means contracting one's face into a frown.

تَعْبِيس is the Form II verbal noun, naming that act.

The doubled form may emphasize intensity or repetition in some uses, but Form II does not invariably establish deliberate intent or a fixed degree of intensity.

عَبَّاس: markedly grim

عَبَّاس is an intensive form describing someone very grim or stern-faced.

It is also established as a personal name.

عَبَّاس and عَنْبَس are also recorded names for a lion, related to its stern, imposing appearance. In the traditional analysis of عَنْبَس as فَنْعَل, the nūn is additional and does not make this a four-letter root.

As a personal name, ʿAbbās does not license conclusions about its bearer's character or expression.

Names may originate in descriptions without necessarily conveying that description on every subsequent use.

عَبَس: dried dirt

In the other material branch, عَبَس names dried dirt adhering to an animal's tail hair or body.

The verb عَبِسَ is also recorded for becoming dirty or encrusted.

This differs from frowning.

A resemblance involving contraction or unpleasantness does not by itself establish a single definitive meaning for both branches.

The dried-dirt branch belongs in the lexical account but must not displace the clear Quranic facial sense.

3. Quranic uses

The root occurs three times in three verses, in two morphological forms.

عَبَسَ occurs in Al-Muddaththir 74:22 and ʿAbasa 80:1; the adjective عَبُوسًا occurs in Al-Insān 76:10.

The first two concern a human expression; the third describes a harsh, frightening day.

A. عَبَسَ: frowning in response to truth

ثُمَّ عَبَسَ وَبَسَرَ

thumma ʿabasa wa-basara

Al-Muddaththir 74:22

Then he frowned and scowled.

Al-Muddaththir 74:22

This describes someone considering the divine message and adopting a hostile position.

Al-Muddaththir 74:18 mentions his thinking and calculating.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:21 he looks; in Al-Muddaththir 74:22 he frowns and scowls.

عَبَسَ and بَسَرَ occur together.

The former concerns facial contraction; the latter also describes a grim, sour expression.

They share a semantic field without necessarily being identical names for a single movement.

Together they depict marked facial tension and displeasure.

From expression to overt conduct

Al-Muddaththir 74:23 continues:

ثُمَّ أَدْبَرَ وَاسْتَكْبَرَ

thumma adbara wa-stakbara

Al-Muddaththir 74:23

Then he turned his back and acted arrogantly.

Al-Muddaththir 74:23

أَدْبَرَ, from د ب ر, means turning one's back or turning away.

اسْتَكْبَرَ, from ك ب ر, concerns arrogance.

Neither is identical to عَبَسَ.

Al-Muddaththir 74:22 describes the expression; Al-Muddaththir 74:23 describes turning away and arrogance.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:24 and Al-Muddaththir 74:25, the person calls the message magic and human speech.

Frowning is thus part of a sequence involving turning away, arrogance, and denial.

عَبَسَ itself does not mean acting arrogantly or denying.

The verb alone does not establish that someone is arrogant, disbelieving, or knowingly rejecting truth.

In this surah, the subsequent actions and statements establish those features.

The same outward expression can accompany different motives and actions in different settings.

B. عَبَسَ: frowning and turning away from a visitor

عَبَسَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ

ʿabasa wa-tawallā

ʿAbasa 80:1

He frowned and turned away.

ʿAbasa 80:1

This opens Surah ʿAbasa.

It contains two perfect verbs: عَبَسَ and تَوَلَّىٰ.

One describes expression; the other describes turning away.

It therefore names frowning and turning away, two related but distinct actions. وَ alone does not establish “then”; inattention is understood from the context.

Both verbs are third-person masculine singular. The opening verse does not explicitly name their subject.

The continuation clarifies the event's cause.

أَنْ جَاءَهُ الْأَعْمَىٰ

an jāʾahu al-aʿmā

ʿAbasa 80:2

Because the blind man came to him.

ʿAbasa 80:2

This connects the blind person's arrival with the preceding conduct.

الْأَعْمَىٰ identifies him as blind, without supplying his name.

The next verse mentions the possibility of his purification.

وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ لَعَلَّهُ يَزَّكَّىٰ

wa-mā yudrīka laʿallahu yazzakkā

ʿAbasa 80:3

And what would make you know? Perhaps he would purify himself.

ʿAbasa 80:3

The discourse shifts from third-person report to direct address.

This shift must remain visible in analysis. The addressee's identity and relationship with the opening subject require reading the passage together; a lexical translation need not insert a name into عَبَسَ.

The passage's teaching about conduct

Two situations are contrasted in the following verses.

One person considers himself self-sufficient and receives considerable attention; another arrives earnestly and in awe but does not receive appropriate attention.

ʿAbasa 80:5 and ʿAbasa 80:6 concern attention to the person who considers himself self-sufficient.

ʿAbasa 80:8 and ʿAbasa 80:9 describe someone arriving earnestly and in awe.

ʿAbasa 80:10 describes distraction from this visitor.

The issue thus extends beyond facial muscles to treatment of people and priorities in attending to visitors.

One may draw an ethical lesson here: a person seeking guidance should not be disregarded because of social standing, physical disability, or others' low estimation. This is a contextual conclusion, not a dictionary definition of عَبَسَ.

That conclusion comes from the passage as a whole, not the verb alone.

Does frowning always indicate contempt?

Here frowning accompanies turning away and inattention, becoming part of conduct open to criticism.

Someone frowning from pain, grief, or worry cannot be accused of contempt merely from that appearance.

Conversely, someone smiling may demean others or disregard their rights.

Frowning and showing contempt are distinct concepts.

The expression's connection with turning away and unequal attention gives the episode its ethical significance.

This also prevents turning the root's meaning into a fixed judgment about intention or character.

The two Quranic verb occurrences compared

Al-Muddaththir 74:22 places frowning amid deliberation, turning away, arrogance, and denial.

ʿAbasa 80:1 places it in an encounter with a blind visitor and contrasting treatment of people in different situations.

The lexical meaning is the same in both: frowning.

Its role in the conduct and message of each passage differs.

One is part of opposition to the divine message; the other concerns treatment of someone seeking reminder and purification.

Understanding the root therefore requires distinguishing the verb's meaning from accompanying actions and contextual conclusions.

C. عَبُوسًا: a grim and frightening day

إِنَّا نَخَافُ مِنْ رَبِّنَا يَوْمًا عَبُوسًا قَمْطَرِيرًا

innā nakhāfu min rabbinā yawman ʿabūsan qamṭarīrā

Al-Insān 76:10

We fear from our Lord a grim, intensely severe day.

Al-Insān 76:10

عَبُوسًا qualifies يَوْمًا, “a day.”

Unlike the previous cases, the subject is a day of the Hereafter rather than a person or animal contracting its face.

The adjective conveys the day's harshness and severity.

A human description is transferred to time: events causing such fear and distress that faces contract can make it a “grim day.”

The day does not literally have a face or frown; the expression describes its dreadful condition.

عَبُوسًا and قَمْطَرِيرًا

The verse places two adjectives together.

عَبُوسًا belongs to ع ب س and pictures the day's grim severity.

قَمْطَرِيرًا likewise expresses intense hardship.

Both emphasize the day's burden and dread but come from different roots.

عَبُوس evokes a contracted face and grim atmosphere; قَمْطَرِير emphasizes great severity.

“A harsh and frightening day” conveys the broad meaning, while “a grim, intensely severe day” retains the facial image more closely.

Connection with the following verse

فَوَقَاهُمُ اللَّهُ شَرَّ ذَٰلِكَ الْيَوْمِ وَلَقَّاهُمْ نَضْرَةً وَسُرُورًا

fa-waqāhumu allāhu sharra dhālika al-yawmi wa-laqqāhum naḍratan wa-surūrā

Al-Insān 76:11

So The God protected them from the evil of that day and bestowed upon them radiance and joy.

Al-Insān 76:11

After the grim day, the verse describes people protected from its evil and receiving نَضْرَة and سُرُور.

نَضْرَة concerns freshness and radiance of appearance; سُرُور means joy.

They contrast with the preceding grimness.

The passage thus describes both the day's dread and the possibility of protection, radiance, and joy.

The day's severity does not mean everyone shares the same outcome.

In Al-Insān 76:7, the group fulfils vows; in Al-Insān 76:8, they feed those in need. Al-Insān 76:10 describes their fear of that day, and Al-Insān 76:11 their protection and reward.

يَوْمًا عَبُوسًا describes the day's severity, not an identical fate for everyone.

4. Comparison with related roots

عَبَسَ and بَسَرَ: frowning and scowling

These verbs occur together in Al-Muddaththir 74:22.

عَبَسَ concerns contracting the face and furrowing the brow.

بَسَرَ likewise concerns a grim, sour expression.

Lexical uses can describe marked facial contraction and displeasure.

Their conjunction can emphasize the intensity and visibility of that expression.

It does not establish a precise, invariant muscular movement for each verb in every context.

The surah also mentions turning away and arrogance. The ethical meaning comes from that whole context, beyond facial expression alone.

عَبَسَ and قَطَبَ: frowning and drawing the brows together

قَطَبَ concerns gathering or drawing together.

قَطَّبَ وَجْهَهُ means contracting the face and furrowing the area between the brows.

This is very close to عَبَسَ.

Dictionaries also explain عَبَسَ as drawing together the area between the eyes. Thus no fixed anatomical distinction between the verbs should be imposed without contextual evidence.

Both may describe the same situation, so their boundary is not absolute.

The Quran uses ع ب س; ق ط ب in the frowning sense does not occur there.

عَبَسَ and كَلَحَ: frowning and exposed teeth

ك ل ح concerns an unpleasant facial state, particularly lips drawing back and teeth becoming visible.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:104, كَالِحُونَ describes people whose faces the fire scorches.

Their expression is connected with suffering and severe punishment.

عَبَسَ emphasizes a contracted face or frown; كَلَحَ can involve drawn-back lips and exposed teeth.

They share the field of distressed expressions without presenting exactly the same bodily image.

عَبَسَ and تَوَلَّىٰ: expression and turning away

They occur together in ʿAbasa 80:1.

عَبَسَ describes facial expression.

تَوَلَّىٰ here means turning away.

Someone can frown while continuing to listen, or turn away without frowning.

The verbs therefore describe distinct conduct.

Their conjunction relates expression to treatment of and attention to the visitor.

Rendering عَبَسَ merely as “disregarded” loses the facial meaning; disregard arises more clearly from تَوَلَّىٰ and the subsequent context.

عَبُوس and نَضْرَة: grimness and radiance

نَضْرَة, from ن ض ر, concerns freshness, radiance, and beauty of appearance.

Al-Insān 76:11 places it alongside joy.

عَبُوس in Al-Insān 76:10 describes a grim and frightening day.

The imagery contrasts tension and harshness with freshness and radiance.

Related imagery also appears in descriptions of faces in the Hereafter.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:22 describes radiant faces; Al-Qiyāmah 75:24 describes grim ones.

These words are not absolute opposites throughout Arabic usage: here one describes a day and the other human appearance.

The contextual contrast is severity and grimness versus radiance and joy.

عَبَسَ and غَضِبَ: expression and anger

غَضَب belongs to غ ض ب and means anger.

Anger is an inward state that may appear through frowning, raised voice, silence, or harsh action.

عَبَسَ primarily describes outward expression.

An angry person may frown, but not every frown establishes anger.

ʿAntarah depicts horses as grim amid difficult movement; the description alone does not establish anger toward someone.

“He became angry” can therefore add more than عَبَسَ itself says.

“He frowned” is more precise and leaves the cause to context.

ع ب س and ع ب ث: frowning and futile activity

The roots look and sound similar but differ in their final consonant.

ع ب س, with sīn, concerns frowning and related descriptions.

ع ب ث, with thāʾ, concerns futile activity and, in another branch, mixing.

عَبَثًا in Al-Muʾminūn 23:115 means “in vain,” without a direct connection to frowning.

Their similarity should not lead to merging their meanings.

5. Morphological and semantic points

Quranic عَبَسَ

عَبَسَ is the third-person masculine singular perfect of Form I.

It has this form in Al-Muddaththir 74:22 and ʿAbasa 80:1.

Both subjects frown, but the contexts differ.

In Al-Muddaththir it appears among successive actions culminating in denial of the message.

In ʿAbasa it opens the passage in connection with a blind person's arrival.

Morphologically it is neither passive nor an intensive form. Its basic meaning is frowning.

The form عَبُوسًا

عَبُوس is an adjective on the pattern فَعُول.

In Al-Insān 76:10 it is accusative and qualifies يَوْمًا.

It therefore describes the day, not directly a person.

The facial description extends to the severity of a day.

The pattern alone does not establish unlimited intensity. The accompanying قَمْطَرِيرًا further emphasizes severity.

Outward expression and inward intention

A crucial distinction separates expression from its cause.

A frown suggests tension or discomfort at that moment, but its exact cause may remain unknown.

A person may dislike a statement, experience pain, fear an event, or undergo bodily strain.

Where only عَبَسَ appears, arrogance, hatred, contempt, or concealment of truth should not be added without evidence.

The Quranic passages preserve this distinction.

Following Al-Muddaththir 74:22, arrogance is stated by اسْتَكْبَرَ in Al-Muddaththir 74:23.

In ʿAbasa 80:1, تَوَلَّىٰ expresses turning away; attention to visitors is explained by the continuation.

Expression, accompanying conduct, and intention are three distinct levels.

Is frowning always blameworthy?

Lexically, frowning names an expression without itself issuing a final moral judgment.

Frowning at injustice or news of a crime is not necessarily blameworthy.

A smiling person can also act wrongly or deceptively.

If a frown demeans someone seeking help or understanding, it becomes part of objectionable conduct.

Assessment therefore requires knowing the cause, situation, addressee, and accompanying conduct.

Appearance alone cannot conclusively establish someone's intention or character.

The material sense of عَبَس

In this branch, عَبَس names matter adhering to and drying on an animal's hair or body.

The image can be compared with facial contraction, but the uses remain distinct.

None of the three Quranic occurrences concerns dried dirt.

عَبَسَ in ʿAbasa or Al-Muddaththir therefore cannot mean something drying or sticking onto a face.

Nor does عَبُوسًا in Al-Insān mean a dirty day: it describes harshness and severity.

6. Translation suggestions for Forqan

For the facial sense, “he frowned” corresponds to the Persian “he contracted his face.”

It preserves the change of expression without adding anger, hatred, or arrogance without evidence.

“Frowning” is also a brief, natural equivalent for the activity.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:22, “frowned” for عَبَسَ and “scowled” for بَسَرَ preserve separate renderings for the two verbs, as do the suggested Persian equivalents.

عَبَسَ: he frowned; contracted his face.

يَعْبِسُ: he frowns.

عُبُوس: frowning; facial grimness.

عَابِس: frowning; grim-faced.

عَبُوس: grim or stern-faced; of a day, harsh and severe.

عَبَّسَ: he contracted his face; frowned.

عَبَّاس: markedly grim; also a personal name.

عَبَس, in the material branch: dried dirt adhering to an animal's hair or body.

Al-Muddaththir 74:22

“Then he frowned and scowled.”

This preserves separate equivalents for عَبَسَ and بَسَرَ.

ʿAbasa 80:1

“He frowned and turned away.”

It adds no personal name and retains the two distinct actions.

Al-Insān 76:10

“We fear from our Lord a grim, intensely severe day.”

“Grim” preserves the original facial image. “Harsh and frightening” is also possible but makes that image less explicit; the same choice arises between the proposed Persian renderings.

Brief meanings for word boxes

Brief root meaning: frowning and facial contraction; descriptively, grimness and severity.

عَبَسَ — Al-Muddaththir 74:22

Contextual meaning: he frowned; the outward reaction preceding turning away and acting arrogantly.

عَبَسَ — ʿAbasa 80:1

Contextual meaning: he frowned, in connection with the blind visitor's arrival and turning away.

عَبُوسًا — Al-Insān 76:10

Contextual meaning: grim and severe; describing a dreadful, difficult day.

7. Synthesis

The root's principal branch concerns a contracted face displaying tension or displeasure.

Early usage is not confined to people. The poem attributed to ʿAntarah describes horses struggling across soft ground as عَوَابِس. This literary depiction associates grimness with physical hardship rather than necessarily anger, arrogance, or hostility.

Dictionaries also record عَبَس for dried dirt adhering to tail hair, illustrated by the post-Quranic poet Abū al-Najm. A possible connection with facial contraction does not establish which sense historically developed from the other.

The Quran uses the root three times.

Al-Muddaththir 74:22 describes a person whose subsequent conduct includes turning away, arrogance, and denial. Frowning belongs to that sequence without itself meaning arrogance or denial.

ʿAbasa 80:1 joins the verb to تَوَلَّىٰ. The passage continues with a blind visitor and contrasting treatment of a self-sufficient person and one seeking reminder and purification. The expression matters in relation to attention and treatment.

Al-Insān 76:10 uses عَبُوسًا for a dreadful day, transferring the facial image to its severity without attributing a literal human face or feelings to it.

The comparisons with بَسَرَ, قَطَبَ, كَلَحَ, تَوَلَّىٰ, and غَضِبَ preserve distinctions: expression differs from turning away, arrogance, anger, and other accompanying conduct established by context and separate words.

A single frown, without knowledge of the situation and conduct, cannot conclusively establish intention or character. When joined to contempt, turning away, or disregard for rights, it becomes part of an ethically significant situation.

Final definition: ع ب س primarily denotes frowning and visible facial tension, displeasure, or strain. عَبَسَ names the change and عَابِس the person displaying it. عَبُوس may describe a person or, by extension, a harsh and dreadful day. In the Quran, the two verb occurrences describe human expression; the adjective pictures the severity of the Day of Resurrection.
