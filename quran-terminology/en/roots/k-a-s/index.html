The Qur’anic Root K ʾ S (ك أ س)

The cup and its contents: from drinking gatherings in early Arabic to the blessings of Paradise

1. Meaning in Arabic before the Qur’an

In pre-Islamic Arabic, كَأْس denoted a cup containing a drink, particularly wine. It could also denote the drink itself.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets indicates that both uses were familiar before the Qur’an.

The ode attributed to ʿAmr ibn Kulthum includes:

صَبَنْتِ الْكَأْسَ عَنَّا أُمَّ عَمْرٍو وَكَانَ الْكَأْسُ مَجْرَاهَا الْيَمِينَا

Umm ʿAmr, you turned the cup away from us, although the cup circulated to the right.

Another line of the same ode reads:

وَكَأْسٍ قَدْ شَرِبْتُ بِبَعْلَبَكٍّ وَأُخْرَى فِي دِمَشْقَ وَقَاصِرِينَا

Many a cup I drank in Baalbek, and another in Damascus and Qasirin.

In the first line, kaʾs is the cup circulating among drinkers; in the second, it is the contents that the poet drinks.

Although transmitted through later literary traditions, these examples illustrate the word’s established range in early Arabic.

Arabic lexicons also distinguish كَأْس from قَدَح. Qadaḥ names the drinking vessel, whereas the more specific use of kaʾs considers the vessel together with its contents. Some lexicographers also allow kaʾs for the vessel itself, so this distinction is not an exceptionless rule.

2. Forms and derivatives

Attested forms in Arabic include:

  • كَأْس: a cup containing a drink; also its contents.
  • كَاس: a lightened pronunciation of kaʾs, replacing the hamzah with an alif.
  • أَكْؤُس: cups.
  • كُؤُوس: cups.
  • كِئَاس: another plural of kaʾs.

كَأْس is grammatically feminine, although it has no overt feminine ending.

Forms examined in the Qur’an: بِكَأْسٍ، كَأْسٍ، وَكَأْسٍ، كَأْسًا، وَكَأْسًا.

All are forms of the singular noun كَأْس. Their differences reflect case endings or attached conjunctions and prepositions. Its plural forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Semantic core and physical image

The semantic focus is a cup containing a drink and, by reference to its contents, the drink itself.

The physical image is a vessel filled with a drink and offered for drinking. Accordingly, the word can be the object of taking, circulating, passing, or drinking.

Persian, too, can speak of “drinking a cup,” meaning its contents rather than the vessel.

Classical Arabic also uses figurative expressions such as كَأْسُ الْمَوْتِ, “the cup of death,” and كَأْسُ الذُّلِّ, “the cup of humiliation.” A bitter or unavoidable experience is compared to drinking a cup’s contents.

Kaʾs therefore does not inherently mean pleasure or suffering. The kind of drink, its properties, and the positive or negative value of drinking it come from context.

The Qur’an uses this word exclusively in descriptions of the blessings of Paradise.

4. Qur’anic uses

A cup from a flowing source

The first verse associates the cup with a drink supplied from a flowing source. The next two describe its whiteness, pleasantness, and freedom from harmful or consciousness-impairing effects.

بَيْضَاءَ agrees with the grammatical gender of kaʾs, but whiteness and pleasantness here describe the drink inside the cup.

غَوْل, from غ و ل, denotes harm that reaches a person and can impair strength or health.

This description separates the pleasure of drinking from harmful effects.

A cup passed among the inhabitants of Paradise

يَتَنَازَعُونَ belongs to Form VI of ن ز ع. Here it describes taking and exchanging the cup among drinkers, rather than hostility or conflict.

The physical image is clear: a cup passes from one hand to another.

Alongside this image, the Qur’an excludes idle speech and sinful conduct. Drinking in such a gathering is not accompanied by either.

This description can be read alongside Al-Ma’idah 5:91, which speaks of hostility and hatred arising through wine and gambling in worldly life.

A cup alongside other serving vessels

Three distinct words occur together:

  • أَكْوَاب: cups and drinking vessels.
  • أَبَارِيق: ewers or pitchers for pouring liquids.
  • كَأْس: a cup containing a drink.

Their juxtaposition clarifies kaʾs. While akwāb and abārīq identify serving vessels, kaʾs draws attention to the cup together with the drink it holds.

In the transmission of Hafs from ʿAsim, يُنْزَفُونَ in Al-Saffat 37:47 has a fatḥah on the zay and is passive, whereas يُنْزِفُونَ in Al-Waqiʿah 56:19 has a kasrah and belongs to Form IV.

Arabic أَنْزَفَ can refer both to losing awareness through intoxication and to exhausting a supply of drink. Given the context and the parallel description in Al-Saffat, retaining awareness is a suitable interpretation of Al-Waqiʿah 56:19.

A cup blended with camphor

مِزَاج, from م ز ج, denotes something mixed with something else.

Camphor is identified as the drink’s admixture. The feminine pronoun هَا in مِزَاجُهَا refers to kaʾs, but what is blended is the cup’s contents.

This context connects the cup and its blend with a spring and the flowing drink.

A cup blended with ginger

Here the same word kaʾs describes a drink blended with ginger.

In Al-Insan 76:5, يَشْرَبُونَ describes drinking from the drinkers’ perspective. In Al-Insan 76:17, the passive يُسْقَوْنَ emphasizes that they are served a drink.

The use of kaʾs for different blends shows that it is not the name of a single material composition. Nor does the Qur’an describe the precise physical composition of these paradisal drinks in detail.

An overflowing cup

دِهَاق, from د ه ق, here describes a full, overflowing cup. Lexical explanations also associate this description with abundance and succession.

Kaʾs identifies the cup containing a drink; dihāq emphasizes its fullness.

This agrees with the heavenly gathering in Al-Tur 52:23: enjoyment in surroundings free from idle and false speech.

5. Comparison with related roots

ك أ س (كَأْس) and ك و ب (كُوب)

كُوب names a drinking vessel, described in Arabic lexicons particularly as one without a handle or spout.

In its specific use, كَأْس is the cup together with the drink it contains.

In Al-Waqiʿah 56:18, the plural أَكْوَاب appears alongside kaʾs. This helps distinguish the name of a vessel from a cup considered with its contents.

ك أ س (كَأْس) and خ م ر (خَمْر)

Khamr names a drink that is intoxicating in its familiar worldly use; kaʾs names a cup containing a drink and sometimes the drink itself.

Muhammad 47:15 describes rivers of wine delightful to drinkers in Paradise. Al-Saffat and Al-Waqiʿah describe the heavenly drink as not impairing awareness.

Early Arabic sometimes uses kaʾs directly for wine. Nevertheless, translating every Qur’anic use as “wine” or “intoxicating liquor” would erase the lexical distinction and the properties specified by the verses.

ك أ س (كَأْس) and ش ر ب (شُرْب)

ش ر ب concerns drinking; شَرَاب is the general name for something drunk.

In the Qur’an, sharāb can be pure and pleasant or a drink of punishment. The word alone determines neither the vessel nor the manner of serving nor the drink’s positive or negative value.

Kaʾs, by contrast, presents the drink in relation to the cup in which it is offered.

ك أ س (كَأْس) and س ق ي (سَقْي)

س ق ي expresses giving water or a drink to another.

In Al-Insan 76:17, يُسْقَوْنَ identifies serving the drink, كَأْسًا its cup, and مِزَاجُهَا its admixture.

Each word expresses a different aspect of one event.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Cup” is a suitable general equivalent of kaʾs. Like Persian جام, it can refer to the drinking vessel or, by reference to its contents, the drink.

Arabic form or expressionSuggested rendering
كَأْسcup
بِكَأْسٍ مِنْ مَعِينٍwith a cup from a flowing source
يَتَنَازَعُونَ فِيهَا كَأْسًاthey pass a cup among themselves there
يَشْرَبُونَ مِنْ كَأْسٍthey drink from a cup
يُسْقَوْنَ فِيهَا كَأْسًاthey are served a cup there
كَأْسًا دِهَاقًاan overflowing cup

Rendering kaʾs as “cup” leaves the drink’s properties, blend, and effects to be conveyed by the other words of the verse.

Conclusion

ك أ س concerns a cup containing a drink and, by reference to its contents, the drink itself.

The Qur’an employs this established meaning for the blessings of Paradise: a circulating cup, supplied from a flowing source, with different blends, whose drinking entails neither harm, headache, impaired awareness, nor sinful conduct.

These properties come from the descriptions and context, not from the inherent meaning of kaʾs.

Proposed definition for Forqan:

كَأْس denotes a cup containing a drink and sometimes the drink itself. Its kind and properties are determined by context.
