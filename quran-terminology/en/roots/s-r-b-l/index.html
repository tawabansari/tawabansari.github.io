1. Root Meaning and Word Structure

In Arabic, sirbāl denotes a garment covering the human body. Early uses encompass ordinary shirts and clothing as well as armor and battle dress. Its plural is sarābīl, the form found in the Qur’an.

Al-ʿAyn, one of the earliest comprehensive Arabic dictionaries, defines sirbāl as al-qamīṣ, “shirt.” Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ gives the same meaning.

Lisān al-ʿArab gives a broader definition: al-sirbālu al-qamīṣu wa-l-dirʿu, “sirbāl is a shirt and armor.” It then records another view, according to which anything worn can be called sirbāl.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: shirt, garment, and armor

These differences matter. Defining sirbāl only as a shirt fails to account for armor; translating it only as armor loses its ordinary clothing uses.

A defensible general meaning is therefore a body garment, or clothing worn on the body. A shirt and armor are two different kinds of that covering.

With a shirt, covering the body is central, and clothing can also provide protection against environmental conditions. With armor, that covering is designed to reduce harm from blows and weapons.

Qur’anic morphological indexes classify S R B L as quadriliteral. Its four-consonant structure within Arabic must nevertheless be distinguished from proof of its historical origin.

Unlike Z L Z L and W S W S, S R B L does not repeat a two-consonant sequence. Nor should it be divided into S R B plus L without evidence.

Alongside the noun sirbāl, Arabic records sarbala, to clothe another person, and tasarbala, to put on such clothing or become covered with something. Morphological analysis can connect these verbs with the name of the garment, but their relationship alone does not establish which historically came first.

The existence of these verbs does not prove that the noun originally developed from a native Arabic verbal root. A noun might have entered Arabic from another language and then acquired new verbs and derivatives according to Arabic morphology.

2. Lexical Forms, Historical Origin, and Early Evidence

Principal Forms

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

سِرْبال — sirbāl: A singular noun: shirt, body garment, armor, or another covering worn on the body.

سَرَابِيل — sarābīl: The broken plural of sirbāl, following faʿālīl. Only this plural occurs in the Qur’an.

سَرْبَلَ — sarbala: A quadriliteral past-tense verb: he clothed someone in a sirbāl or put a garment on them.

يُسَرْبِلُ — yusarbilu: The imperfect of sarbala: he clothes someone or puts a garment on them.

سَرْبَلَة — sarbalah: The verbal noun of sarbala, clothing someone. Lexical sources also record the same form as the name of a very fatty dish. A definite historical and semantic connection between that use and clothing is unclear; the food sense should not enter the definition of the Qur’anic root.

تَسَرْبَلَ — tasarbala: He put on a sirbāl or became covered with something.

مُتَسَرْبِل — mutasarbil: Someone or something wearing a sirbāl or enveloped in a covering.

Of these forms, only the plural noun sarābīl occurs in the Qur’an. Sarbala and the other derivatives do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

Al-Muḥkam and Tāj al-ʿArūs: lexical forms and the separate food use

Historical Origin

Scholars of ancient languages have proposed different accounts of sirbāl’s origin.

Some regard it as an Iranian word borrowed into Arabic, suggesting a relationship with shalwār and Iranian clothing terms.

That connection presents a semantic difficulty: shalwār specifically concerns clothing for the lower body, whereas Arabic sirbāl chiefly denotes a shirt, body garment, or armor.

Others investigate transmission through Aramaic. Aramaic records forms close to sarbāl for clothing and garments, together with related verbs meaning to clothe.

These Aramaic forms, alongside early Arabic usage, support the possibility of transmission through language contact. Whether Arabic borrowed directly from an Iranian language or through Aramaic nevertheless requires caution. In his study of foreign Qur’anic vocabulary, Arthur Jeffery favors an early borrowing from Aramaic and even considers whether the Arabic noun developed from the verb. This too is a historical hypothesis, rather than a certain conclusion.

Arthur Jeffery, The Foreign Vocabulary of the Qur’ān, pp.168–169: Iranian and Aramaic hypotheses and noun/verb priority

The disagreement does not decisively affect the word’s established Qur’anic meaning. Whether originally native or borrowed, it had become established in Arabic before the Qur’an and was used for clothing the body.

Its original, certain meaning cannot therefore be established merely by dividing it into hypothetical Persian, Aramaic, or Arabic components. Evidence of actual usage takes precedence over such possible analyses.

Clothed in Blood: A Witness from ʿAntarah

An important pre-Islamic Arabic witness appears in the Muʿallaqah of ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād:

مَا زِلْتُ أَرْمِيهِمْ بِثُغْرَةِ نَحْرِهِ
وَلَبَانِهِ حَتَّى تَسَرْبَلَ بِالدَّمِ

mā ziltu armīhim bi-thughrati naḥrihi / wa-labānihi ḥattā tasarbala bi-l-dami

“I kept charging them with the upper neck and breast of my horse, until it was clothed in blood.”

Some transmissions have thughrati wajhihi in place of thughrati naḥrihi. Both preserve ḥattā tasarbala bi-l-dami and its meaning of becoming clothed in blood.

Al-Jalīs al-Ṣāliḥ: ʿAntarah’s verse and naḥrihi/wajhihi variants

This use matters because tasarbala does not describe putting on an actual shirt or armor. The blood on the horse’s body is likened to a covering lying upon it like clothing.

The central feature in this image is the body’s surface becoming covered by something placed upon it.

It establishes the family’s presence in pre-Islamic poetic Arabic and the verb’s capacity to extend beyond putting on sewn clothing.

It does not make sirbāl originally the name of any substance resting on anything. The verse extends bodily clothing to being covered with blood through comparison.

Sirbāl as Armor: A Witness from Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr

Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr, a poet of the early Islamic period, says in a famous verse:

شُمُّ الْعَرَانِينِ أَبْطَالٌ لَبُوسُهُمْ
مِنْ نَسْجِ دَاوُدَ فِي الْهَيْجَا سَرَابِيلُ

shummu al-ʿarānīni abṭālun labūsuhum / min nasji dāwuda fī al-hayjā sarābīlu

“Proud and valiant men, whose clothing in battle consists of garments of David’s weaving.”

Sarābīl here denotes battle clothing, and its association with nasji Dāwuda, “David’s weaving,” supports the meaning of armor.

Sirbāl could thus denote a firm covering for battle, rather than only a fabric garment.

The contrast between the two witnesses is clear. ʿAntarah uses tasarbala for a body covered with blood; Kaʿb uses sarābīl for battle clothing itself.

Kaʿb’s poem belongs to the Islamic period, while ʿAntarah provides an earlier witness to the verb in pre-Islamic language.

Siyar Aʿlām al-Nubalāʾ: Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr’s armor verse

Together, the witnesses establish both actual clothing and the capacity for figurative use in Arabic.

3. Qur’anic Sarābīl: Ordinary Clothing and Battle Dress

S R B L occurs three times in two Qur’anic verses: once in Ibrāhīm 14:50 and twice in Al-Naḥl 16:81.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three occurrences of sarābīl

All three occurrences use the noun sarābīl, while context distinguishes the kind of covering and its function.

Al-Naḥl 16:81: Clothing That Protects against Heat

Al-Naḥl 16:81

وَاللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِمَّا خَلَقَ ظِلَالًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ أَكْنَانًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ سَرَابِيلَ تَقِيكُمُ الْحَرَّ وَسَرَابِيلَ تَقِيكُمْ بَأْسَكُمْ كَذَٰلِكَ يُتِمُّ نِعْمَتَهُ عَلَيْكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُسْلِمُونَ

wa-Allāhu jaʿala lakum mimmā khalaqa ẓilālan wa-jaʿala lakum mina al-jibāli aknānan wa-jaʿala lakum sarābīla taqīkumu al-ḥarra wa-sarābīla taqīkum baʾsakum kadhālika yutimmu niʿmatahu ʿalaykum laʿallakum tuslimūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The God has provided you shade from what He created, and shelters in the mountains, and garments protecting you against heat and garments guarding you against the harm of your battles. Thus He completes His blessing upon you, so that you may submit.”

Sarābīl occurs twice in this verse.

In the first occurrence, taqīkumu al-ḥarra specifies the covering’s function: protection against heat.

In the second, taqīkum baʾsakum expresses another kind of protection: guarding people against the harm and severity of conflict and battle.

One noun thus describes two kinds of covering with different functions.

Covering the Body and Protection against Heat

Sarābīla taqīkumu al-ḥarra denotes garments covering the body and protecting it against heat.

Shirts or body garments fit this use. Nothing specifies that they must be made of a particular material such as cotton, wool, or linen.

The verse likewise specifies no shape, color, length, or shortness.

It describes clothing’s function: covering the body and protecting it against an environmental condition.

In hot climates, suitable clothing can help protect people by shading the body’s surface and reducing direct skin exposure to sunlight and environmental conditions.

Not every garment protects against heat under every condition. Material, thickness, color, shape, and suitability to the environment affect performance. The verse should therefore not become a claim that every garment lowers body temperature in all circumstances.

The function described is protection through suitable clothing.

Why Is Heat Mentioned, but Not Cold?

The verse says al-ḥarr, heat. Neither bard nor cold appears in this expression.

Some lexicographers and commentators explain that clothing can protect against both heat and cold, with the verse mentioning only one.

Others relate the prominence of heat to the first audience’s living conditions and the importance of protection against environmental heat.

These explanations are intelligible in terms of experience and clothing’s function, but the wording must be distinguished from inference.

The verse explicitly describes protection against heat. Protection against cold is also a real function of many garments, but cold should not replace or be added to al-ḥarr in direct translation.

At the same time, not mentioning cold does not deny that clothing can protect against it.

This matters for the root study: sirbāl is inherently neither specifically summer nor winter clothing. Context highlights one function.

Al-Ṭabarī: clothing and the mention of heat rather than cold

Battle Clothing and Protection against Injury

The second part states: wa-sarābīla taqīkum baʾsakum.

Heat is no longer what protection concerns; baʾsakum is named instead.

Baʾs in the Qur’an can concern intensity, power, hardship, and the harm of confrontation. With protective body clothing here, injury in war and battle clearly fits.

The second sarābīl therefore denotes battle dress or armor.

Armor is a body garment designed to reduce injury during battle. It may consist of metal rings or other protective structures.

Sirbāl alone does not specify that construction. Mail is one historical example, rather than the word’s exclusive meaning.

This use is not lexically unrelated to the first. Both concern something worn on the body; the danger and protective function differ.

Ordinary clothing protects against environmental conditions, while armor protects against bodily injury in conflict.

The repeated sarābīl therefore does not juxtapose wholly unrelated meanings. Both are body garments with different uses.

Al-Ṭabarī: the first garments and battle coverings

The Connection with David’s Battle Clothing

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80 describes teaching David to make labūs, a covering protecting people against the harm of battle.

Sabaʾ 34:11 likewise commands making sābighāt and observing proportion in sard.

Alongside Al-Naḥl 16:81, these verses show different Qur’anic expressions for protective battle clothing.

Labūs, sābighāt, sard, and sarābīl do not share one root and should not be reduced to one lexical meaning.

Sarābīl names the covering, which is armor in a battle context. Labūs can likewise concern protective clothing, whereas sard in Sabaʾ concerns constructing and linking the armor’s components.

Together, the verses show that the Qur’an recognizes battle clothing as a means of human protection.

Listing this covering among blessings is not, in itself, a command to fight. Using protective equipment and a war’s legitimacy are separate questions.

Al-Naḥl 16:81 chiefly concerns the blessing of available protection, rather than independently ruling on starting a war or its purpose.

4. Garments of Tar: Clothing in a Scene of Punishment

Ibrāhīm 14:50

سَرَابِيلُهُمْ مِنْ قَطِرَانٍ وَتَغْشَىٰ وُجُوهَهُمُ النَّارُ

sarābīluhum min qaṭirānin wa-taghshā wujūhahumu al-nāru

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Their garments are of tar, and fire envelops their faces.”

Sarābīluhum denotes the clothing or body garments of a group described in a scene of punishment.

Ibrāhīm 14:49 describes offenders bound together in fetters. Ibrāhīm 14:50 then describes their covering and fire enveloping their faces.

Unlike Al-Naḥl 16:81, the garment does not function as protection here.

Min qaṭirānin specifies the covering’s material. Qaṭirān is a dark, tar-like substance with adhesive and combustible properties in its known uses.

Some lexical and exegetical explanations emphasize its blackness, stickiness, and unpleasant smell. These can intensify the image of punishment, but the details of its manner must remain separate from the direct meaning of sarābīl.

Sirbāl still denotes a covering on the body. It has not acquired the meaning of fire or punishment; the material and sentence place it within a scene of punishment.

This distinction matters.

In Al-Naḥl 16:81 the garment protects against harm. In Ibrāhīm 14:50 it is made of tar and described alongside fire enveloping faces.

Protection therefore cannot be a fixed, inseparable component of sirbāl’s lexical meaning.

The core meaning is a body garment. Protection is one possible function explicitly stated in Al-Naḥl, but absent from the Ibrāhīm verse.

Al-Ṭabarī: tar garments within punishment

Is the Tar Covering Tangible or Figurative?

The verse uses tangible language for tar garments and fire enveloping faces.

Lexically, nothing requires translating sarābīluhum as a psychological state, sin, or the offenders’ inward qualities.

Philosophical and interpretive questions may arise about the nature and manner of punishment in the Hereafter. The word itself nevertheless retains its established meaning of garment and covering.

Nor should the expression be used to extract unestablished details about bodies, the sewing of garments, or the physical process of burning in the Hereafter.

The verse names the covering and material, then fire enveloping faces. This suffices for understanding sirbāl.

The Contrast between the Qur’anic Uses

Placing Al-Naḥl 16:81 alongside Ibrāhīm 14:50 reveals a clear contrast.

In one verse the garment protects people in worldly life; in the other it forms part of the image of suffering and punishment.

This contrast is semantically significant, but should not become a claim that S R B L originally denotes protection and punishment simultaneously.

Protection and punishment are different outcomes or conditions. What both uses share is a covering placed on the body.

Their common meaning should explain both verses without incorporating contextual features into the definition.

5. Sirbāl Compared with Libās, Thawb, Kiswah, and Other Coverings

Sirbāl and Libās

Libās is a broad word for covering.

In the Qur’an it can concern clothing the body, but is also attributed to night, God-consciousness, the marital relationship, and even an enveloping condition of hunger and fear.

Sirbāl in its Qur’anic uses is narrower and more specific, denoting a body garment.

Every sirbāl is thus clothing, but not everything called libās in the Qur’an is a sirbāl.

Al-Baqarah 2:187, for example, calls spouses each other’s libās. No Qur’anic use calls spouses each other’s sarābīl.

The semantic extensions of one word should therefore not be transferred to a related word without evidence.

Study of L B S

Sirbāl and Thawb

Thawb also means a garment or clothing.

Arabic thawb can denote clothing someone wears. Sirbāl is more specifically explained as a shirt or body garment, though some lexicographers extend it to anything worn.

The Qur’an uses thiyāb for ordinary clothing as well as garments of the Garden.

The distinction is not absolute, since both can denote body clothing. Yet sirbāl in the verses examined accompanies particular functions or settings: protection or garments within punishment.

Study of TH W B

Sirbāl and Kiswah

Kiswah concerns clothing someone and providing clothes.

In uses such as Al-Baqarah 2:233, it concerns clothing supplied as a necessity of life.

Sirbāl, by contrast, names the garment itself, rather than necessarily the act of clothing or supplying clothes.

The words may concern related referents without having identical semantic structures.

Sirbāl and Labūs

Labūs belongs to L B S and concerns protective battle clothing in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80.

In that context, labūs and sirbāl can refer to the same kind of thing: armor.

Sirbāl nevertheless names a body garment and is not confined to war. Labūs likewise belongs to a family with a broader range of wearing and covering.

Their shared reference to armor does not establish identity of root or all meanings.

Sirbāl and Dirʿ

Dirʿ in military usage names armor.

A sirbāl can be armor, but is not always armor. Al-Naḥl 16:81 itself makes this clear: the first garments protect against heat; the second against battle injury.

Translating both as armor without regard to context would distort the first use.

Body garment is therefore a better general equivalent for the family, with armor selected in a battle context.

Sirbāl and Sirwāl

Sirwāl or sarāwīl concerns lower-body clothing in Arabic.

Sirbāl and sirwāl are formally similar, and proposals exist about their historical connection.

They should nevertheless not simply be equated in established Arabic usage.

Sirbāl chiefly concerns shirts, body garments, and armor; sirwāl is specialized for the legs and lower body.

This matters for borrowing hypotheses: formal resemblance alone does not prove identical meanings at every historical stage.

Sirbāl and Sarāb

Sarāb denotes a visual phenomenon in which something like water appears in the distance, while what is actually there differs from the observer’s impression.

Sirbāl is not that word, despite sharing its first three consonants.

There is insufficient evidence to derive clothing from mirage or movement and flow associated with S R B.

Formal resemblance to S R B must not replace independent etymological evidence when investigating S R B L.

6. Conclusion and Translation Proposals

S R B L is a quadriliteral Arabic family whose central noun is sirbāl, plural sarābīl.

Its documented meanings are shirt, body garment, and, in military use, armor. Some lexicographers extend it to anything worn on the body.

Early poetry attests verbs such as sarbala and tasarbala alongside the noun, with uses extending from actual clothing to being covered in another substance, such as blood. This evidence alone does not establish the noun’s historical priority over the verb.

Proposed historical connections concern Iranian and Aramaic languages, but the precise route into Arabic is not conclusively established. The disagreement should not encourage an invented historical meaning for the four consonants.

The root occurs three times in two Qur’anic verses, always as the plural sarābīl.

Al-Naḥl 16:81 first mentions garments protecting against heat, then coverings protecting against battle injury.

Ibrāhīm 14:50 describes garments of tar in a scene of punishment.

Sirbāl should therefore not be defined solely by protection. It names a covering placed on the body; its material, function, and outcome emerge from context.

Brief root meaning: A body garment, shirt, or clothing covering the body; in military usage, armor and protective dress.

Meanings of the Qur’anic forms and translation proposals:

Sarābīluhum — Ibrāhīm 14:50: Their body garments, described here as made of tar. Proposed translation: “their garments.”

Sarābīla — Al-Naḥl 16:81, first occurrence: Clothing protecting the body against heat. Proposed translation: “garments.”

Wa-sarābīla — Al-Naḥl 16:81, second occurrence: Battle coverings used to protect against injury. Proposed translation: “and armor” or “and protective garments.”

The most suitable general Persian equivalent is tan-pūsh, body garment, encompassing both shirts and battle dress.

Each verse’s translation should nevertheless identify the kind of covering from context.

In Al-Naḥl, sarābīl forms part of life’s blessings and a means of protection. In Ibrāhīm, the same name for clothing appears within punishment.

Both verses share clothing the body with a garment, rather than protection in every case or punishment in every case.

This distinction preserves the lexical meaning while allowing each Qur’anic use to be understood in its own setting.
