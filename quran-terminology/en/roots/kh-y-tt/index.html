From thread and needle to the thread of dawn: slender extension, joining parts, and the Qur’anic image of impossibility

The Arabic root KH Y Ṭ concerns thin, threadlike extension. Its clearest instance is khayṭ, a long, slender thread used for sewing, binding, or joining things. The same family includes khāṭa, to sew, and khiyāṭ, a needle.

In early Arabic, the root’s uses extend beyond thread and sewing. Its words also describe the thin light at dawn, white strands among dark hair, slender sunlight, and even animals moving continuously in a line.

A shared feature is something slender appearing or extending along a particular course. This does not mean, however, that every thin thing is necessarily called khayṭ.

KH Y Ṭ occurs three times in two Qur’anic verses. In Al-Baqarah 2:187, khayṭ appears twice to describe the distinction between dawn’s light and night’s darkness. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:40, khiyāṭ names a needle whose eye figures in an image of impossibility.

These uses show two different aspects of the root family: one employs the slender shape and extension of a thread to describe a natural phenomenon; the other places an actual sewing implement within a comparison.

1. Forms and semantic core

KH Y Ṭ is a triliteral hollow root with medial yāʾ. Its second consonant is yāʾ, which becomes alif in certain inflected forms.

Its basic verb is «خَاطَ ـ يَخِيطُ».

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • خَاطَ ـ يَخِيطُ: to sew; join pieces of cloth or other material with thread.
  • خَيْط: thread or a slender strand used for sewing, binding, or connecting parts.
  • خُيُوط، أَخْيَاط، خُيُوطَة: plurals of khayṭ.
  • خَيْط، خِيَاطَة: verbal nouns concerning sewing.
  • خَيَّطَ ـ يُخَيِّطُ: to sew; in some uses, join something through multiple stitches.
  • تَخْيِيط: sewing or stitching.
  • خَائِط، خَيَّاط: someone who sews, especially a person whose trade is making garments.
  • خِيَاط: a needle or sewing implement.
  • مِخْيَط: a needle, used to pass thread through cloth or another material.
  • مَخِيط، مَخْيُوط: something that has been sewn.
  • اِخْتَاطَ: in some uses, to sew; in another, to go toward someone or pass along.
  • تَخَيَّطَ: in some uses, white strands of hair appeared among dark hair.
  • خَيْطَاء: an adjective recorded for an ostrich, particularly concerning its long neck or features of color and shape.
  • خِيط، خَيْط: in another use, a group or line of ostriches, cattle, or locusts moving continuously.
  • خَيْطَة: a thread or thin cord; in some dialects, a peg or means of attachment and support.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Only two nouns from this family occur there:

Khayṭ twice in Al-Baqarah 2:187, and khiyāṭ once in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40.

The difference between «الْخَيْطُ» and «الْخَيْطِ» in Al-Baqarah 2:187 concerns grammatical function and case, rather than a difference in lexical meaning.

Khiyāṭ, a needle, must also be distinguished from khayyāṭ, a tailor. The doubled yāʾ and different word pattern produce different meanings.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three occurrences in two verses

The semantic core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris writes of this root:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى امْتِدَادِ الشَّيْءِ فِي دِقَّةٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā imtidādi al-shayʾi fī diqqatin

That is:

“One underlying principle indicating the extension of something in slenderness.”

Two features matter in this definition.

First, extension: something stretches or continues in a direction.

Second, slenderness: its width is small relative to its length.

Thread clearly exemplifies both: a slender strand extending to a certain length.

Ibn Fāris also applies the field to certain long, slender objects even when standing upright.

Extension in this root therefore does not necessarily mean horizontal extension.

A thread may lie along the ground, hang from above, pass through a needle’s eye, or extend among parts of an object.

This also matters for dawn: khayṭ alone cannot definitively establish a horizontal or vertical direction. The direction and kind of light must be understood through the natural phenomenon and the verse’s context.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: extension in slenderness

2. Thread, sewing, and material uses in early Arabic

Khayṭ: a strand for joining

Lisān al-ʿArab explains khayṭ as silk: a strand or something used for passing through, binding, and connecting.

Thread can be made from different fibers. Twisting or gathering fibers yields a strand suitable for sewing and binding.

In early Arab life, thread and cord served in sewing garments, making coverings, constructing or repairing equipment, and securing certain tools.

Khayṭ is nevertheless not identical to every kind of rope or cord.

A rope can be thick and heavy, whereas khayṭ in its basic sense suggests a finer strand.

Lexical sources sometimes also use khayṭ for a slender cord thicker than ordinary sewing thread.

A fixed diameter should therefore not be assigned to khayṭ.

The main feature is relative slenderness alongside extension, rather than a requirement that every khayṭ be used with a garment-sewing needle.

Khāṭa: sewing and joining parts

«خَاطَ الثَّوْبَ» means that he sewed the garment.

In sewing, a needle or similar implement passes through cloth, and thread joins its parts.

Two separate cloth edges can thus be connected by stitching.

Thread here is more than a slender strand: it provides a connection between parts.

The root’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the result of an action.

Khayṭ names the thread, while khāṭa expresses sewing.

Joining therefore cannot replace the meaning “thread” in every use of khayṭ.

The thread of dawn’s light, for example, sews nothing. Its shape and extension are compared to thread.

Khiyāṭ and mikhyaṭ: needle and sewing implement

Arabic records khiyāṭ and mikhyaṭ for a needle.

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ states:

وَالْمِخْيَطُ: الْإِبْرَةُ، وَكَذَلِكَ الْخِيَاطُ

wa-al-mikhyaṭu al-ibratu wa-kadhālika al-khiyāṭu

That is, “Mikhyaṭ is the needle, and khiyāṭ likewise.”

The family of sewing thus also supplies a name for its implement.

A needle is a slender, usually pointed object used to pass through cloth or another material.

One end has a small opening through which thread passes.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:40, this opening is called samm al-khiyāṭ.

Khiyāṭ there is the needle itself, rather than thread or a person who sews.

Distinguishing the implement mikhyaṭ from the sewn makhīṭ

In Arabic sources, mikhyaṭ, with kasrah on the mīm, names the sewing implement.

Makhīṭ and makhyūṭ, however, are passive participial forms describing something that has been sewn.

These forms have very similar consonants, but different vowels and morphological structures.

Sewn cloth, for example, is makhīṭ; the needle used for sewing is mikhyaṭ.

Lisān al-ʿArab discusses the phonological development of makhyūṭ and the shorter makhīṭ at length.

Similar consonants therefore do not establish that all the forms have the same grammatical function or meaning.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: thread, needle, and passive participle

Coupling two animals

«خَاطَ الْبَعِيرَ بِالْبَعِيرِ» is recorded for coupling two camels.

This does not concern garment-making, but connecting the animals to one another.

Its relationship to the root’s main field is understandable through attachment with thread or a cord.

It does not imply that the camels are literally stitched together with a needle.

Joining can thus extend from clothing to binding and connecting other things.

3. Slender extension in light, hair, and nature

The thread of dawn in Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī’s poetry

An important witness for khayṭ describing the first light is a poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī:

فَلَمَّا أَضَاءَتْ لَنَا سُدْفَةٌ
وَلَاحَ مِنَ الصُّبْحِ خَيْطٌ أَنَارَا

fa-lammā aḍāʾat lanā sudfatun / wa-lāḥa mina al-ṣubḥi khayṭun anārā

Translation:

“When the darkness grew light for us, and a shining thread appeared from the dawn.”

Khayṭ here denotes the slender light visible at the beginning of morning.

The poet is not speaking of literal thread, but comparing emerging light to a long, slender strand.

This helps explain Al-Baqarah 2:187, since it shows that describing dawn’s light as khayṭ was also known in Arabic poetic language.

Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī is assigned to the pre-Islamic period, and early lexical sources, including Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab, transmit this line.

Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet alone does not establish the precise date of every line. The lexical evidence lies in this recorded use within attributed poetry and reputable early sources.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: Abū Duʾād’s witness and sudfah

White strands among dark hair

«خَيَّطَ الشَّيْبُ الرَّأْسَ» is recorded for white strands appearing among dark hair.

A poem by Badr ibn ʿĀmir al-Hudhalī says:

أَقْسَمْتُ لَا أَنْسَى مَنِيحَةَ وَاحِدٍ
حَتَّى تَخَيَّطَ بِالْبَيَاضِ قُرُونِي

aqsamtu lā ansā manīḥata wāḥidin / ḥattā takhayyaṭa bi-al-bayāḍi qurūnī

Translation:

“I swore not to forget that man’s gift, until my hair becomes interspersed with white strands.”

Whiteness is compared to lines or strands appearing among dark hair.

Ibn Barrī distinguishes two conditions in explaining the witness.

In the first, scattered white strands appear among black hair.

In the second, whiteness spreads until its parts become joined.

Early transmissions vary in the verb’s vocalization and certain words. In addition to takhayyaṭa, Ibn Barrī explains tukhayyaṭa and tukhayyiṭa through passive and active analyses; some transmissions also have tawakhkhaṭa.

Some versions also begin the first hemistich with «تَاللَّهِ لَا أَنْسَى».

The variants leave one central point unchanged: white hair is likened to slender, extended strands.

Alongside Abū Duʾād’s verse, this shows that khayṭ is not merely a sewing material; it can describe slender lines of color or light.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: Badr’s witness and Ibn Barrī’s analyses

Khayṭ bāṭil: an exceedingly fine strand

Lexical sources also record khayṭ bāṭil.

Several explanations of its referent exist.

One describes a very fine ray entering a house through an opening, making dust particles visible.

Another identifies it as a thin strand produced by a spider.

Some lexicographers also associate it with fine, wavering strands of sunlight.

The expression also supplies a proverb for something slender or insignificant:

أَدَقُّ مِنْ خَيْطِ بَاطِلٍ

adaqqu min khayṭi bāṭilin

That is, “Finer than khayṭ bāṭil.”

This supports slenderness and extension within the root’s semantic field.

Lexicographers nevertheless disagree over the precise referent. No single explanation should be presented unreservedly as its only fixed meaning.

Khayṭ bāṭil also appears as a person’s nickname in some Umayyad poetry. This nickname does not establish the root’s primary meaning or a definitely pre-Qur’anic use.

Khayṭ and animals moving in a continuous line

Arabic sources record khīṭ and khayṭ for a group of ostriches and sometimes cattle or locusts.

One explanation is that the animals move in a continuous line, appearing from a distance like an extended thread.

Naʿāmah khayṭāʾ also describes an ostrich with a long neck or particular features of bodily color and shape.

These uses concern slender extension and continuity of form, but do not establish an animal group as the Qur’anic meaning of khayṭ.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 concerns dawn; in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40, khiyāṭ concerns a needle.

Animal uses belong to Arabic’s broader range, rather than the preferred meaning in those verses.

Lexical sources: khayṭ bāṭil and animal uses

4. The white thread and black thread in Al-Baqarah 2:187

Al-Baqarah says:

وَكُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَكُمُ الْخَيْطُ الْأَبْيَضُ مِنَ الْخَيْطِ الْأَسْوَدِ مِنَ الْفَجْرِ ثُمَّ أَتِمُّوا الصِّيَامَ إِلَى اللَّيْلِ

wa-kulū wa-ishrabū ḥattā yatabayyana lakumu al-khayṭu al-abyaḍu mina al-khayṭi al-aswadi mina al-fajri thumma atimmū al-ṣiyāma ilā al-layli

Proposed translation:

“Eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct to you from the black thread. Then complete the fasting until night.”

Al-Baqarah 2:187

This passage belongs to a verse setting the limits of fasting.

It permits eating and drinking until the distinction between dawn’s light and night’s darkness becomes apparent.

It then commands completion of fasting until night.

Why compare light and darkness to two threads?

At dawn, light gradually appears along the horizon.

Darkness previously dominates the scene. Light then begins to appear, making the boundary between darkness and whiteness discernible.

The light can be pictured as a thin strand or band emerging amid darkness.

This fits khayṭ as a slender, extended strand.

The verse does not mean two actual black and white threads.

The decisive contextual indication is min al-fajr.

This connects the white thread with dawn, while the contrasting black thread concerns night’s darkness.

The verse presents an image of a natural phenomenon, rather than instructions to place two actual threads or cords side by side.

The role of yatabayyana

In the expression:

حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَكُمُ

ḥattā yatabayyana lakum

Yatabayyana belongs to B Y N and concerns becoming apparent and distinguishable.

The verse therefore does more than mention light and darkness: it describes their distinction becoming apparent to its addressees.

Al-khayṭ al-abyaḍ is grammatically the subject of yatabayyana and is consequently nominative.

Al-khayṭ al-aswad follows min and is genitive.

This difference in case reflects the phrases’ different grammatical functions, rather than khayṭ meaning thread in one and something else in the other.

Min in min al-khayṭ al-aswad concerns one becoming distinct from the other.

Min al-fajr then helps identify the kind of thread intended.

What does min al-fajr qualify?

In the familiar grammatical analysis, min al-fajr explains the white thread: the white strand consisting of dawn’s light.

Under this reading, the black thread refers to night’s darkness.

This fits the familiar contrast between night and dawn.

Lexical sources also transmit another interpretation identifying the black thread with fajr mustaṭīl, or false dawn. This transmission must be distinguished from the interpretation as night’s darkness.

That possibility must remain separate from the phrase’s preferred interpretation.

In the complete verse, the white and black threads form a contrast, and min al-fajr clarifies the time and phenomenon in which that distinction appears.

The clearer reading is therefore the white strand of morning light against night’s darkness.

Dawn versus sunrise

Dawn’s light naturally appears before the sun itself becomes visible.

The sky is still largely dark at the beginning of this stage, then gradually brightens.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 connects the beginning of daily abstention with the white thread becoming distinguishable from the black, min al-fajr.

This does not establish permission to eat and drink until the sun’s disk has fully risen.

The verse speaks of dawn, rather than sunrise.

Early explanations also distinguish a narrow, temporary upward light from dawn spreading along the horizon.

This helps identify the natural phenomenon. A root definition, however, should not become a catalogue of calendrical terminology and detailed disagreements over clock times.

The lexical meaning of khayṭ remains a slender thread. Al-fajr, yatabayyana, and the complete construction establish which natural sign it describes.

Al-Ṭabarī: the white and black threads and the sign of dawn

Does the verse specify a length of time?

No. Khayṭ is not a unit of time.

It means neither an hour nor a minute, nor a fixed numerical interval before sunrise.

The verse identifies a natural sign for the beginning of fasting.

Under ordinary conditions, it concerns the appearance of dawn’s light and its distinction from night’s darkness.

The lengths of day and night and the appearance of light differ considerably in some parts of the earth.

Understanding fasting in regions with very long days or nights, or where ordinary dawn and darkness are not clearly separated, in light of human capacity and divine ease requires a broader study of the fasting verses together.

Khayṭ alone cannot supply a substitute hour, Mecca time, or a fixed calculation system for such conditions.

Khayṭ and the end of fasting

After identifying the beginning, the same verse says:

ثُمَّ أَتِمُّوا الصِّيَامَ إِلَى اللَّيْلِ

thumma atimmū al-ṣiyāma ilā al-layli

The Qur’an thus presents two temporal signs for the beginning and end of daily abstention.

The beginning concerns dawn becoming apparent; the end concerns the arrival of night.

KH Y Ṭ appears only in describing the beginning.

Its meaning must therefore not be extended to the entire concept of fasting, night, or day.

Ṣiyām belongs to Ṣ W M; layl and fajr likewise have their own lexical families.

Khayṭ provides the verse’s linguistic image for the distinction between light and darkness.

5. Samm al-khiyāṭ: the needle’s eye in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40

Al-Aʿrāf says:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا عَنْهَا لَا تُفَتَّحُ لَهُمْ أَبْوَابُ السَّمَاءِ وَلَا يَدْخُلُونَ الْجَنَّةَ حَتَّىٰ يَلِجَ الْجَمَلُ فِي سَمِّ الْخِيَاطِ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي الْمُجْرِمِينَ

inna alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-istakbarū ʿanhā lā tufattaḥu lahum abwābu al-samāʾi wa-lā yadkhulūna al-jannata ḥattā yalija al-jamalu fī sammi al-khiyāṭi wa-kadhālika najzī al-mujrimīna

Proposed translation:

“Those who denied Our signs and treated them arrogantly will not have the gates of heaven opened for them, nor enter the Garden until the camel passes through the needle’s eye. Thus do We recompense the offenders.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:40

Al-khiyāṭ here means the needle.

It is the slender implement through whose eye thread passes.

Samm means the opening or aperture in the Qur’anic expression. Khiyāṭ itself does not mean the opening.

Samm belongs to S M M and here expresses the needle’s small opening.

Samm al-khiyāṭ therefore means the needle’s eye.

Translating khiyāṭ as thread would destroy the image, since the opening belongs to the needle rather than the thread.

Nor does khiyāṭ here mean a tailor or person who sews.

Why place a camel alongside a needle’s eye?

In the familiar reading, al-jamal means the camel.

A camel is a large animal; a needle’s eye is a very small opening.

Combining them evokes the impossibility of the animal passing through that opening.

Thus the clause:

حَتَّىٰ يَلِجَ الْجَمَلُ فِي سَمِّ الْخِيَاطِ

ḥattā yalija al-jamalu fī sammi al-khiyāṭi

presents a condition that cannot occur under ordinary circumstances.

The image does not prescribe an experiment in putting a camel through a needle. It expresses emphatic impossibility concerning the outcome described for the specified group.

Language sometimes emphatically denies an event by making it depend on something impossible.

The expression is clear even though its surface form uses “until.”

Does al-jamal mean camel in every reading?

Early explanations and sources on Qur’anic readings also report another reading of this word.

Some transmissions have al-jummal, with ḍammah on the jīm and a doubled mīm, referring to a thick ship’s rope.

Al-Ṭabarī reports this reading from certain readers and language authorities. He nevertheless chooses the widespread al-jamal, meaning camel. Reporting the rope reading does not assign it equal standing with the familiar reading.

Under that reading, the image concerns a very thick rope passing through a needle’s eye.

The familiar Qur’anic reading, however, is al-jamal, whose recognized meaning is camel.

Both images emphasize incompatible sizes: something very large or thick against a very small opening.

Their referents nevertheless differ, and they must not be substituted for one another without explanation.

The disagreement also concerns jamal, rather than khiyāṭ.

In either reading, khiyāṭ remains the needle, and samm al-khiyāṭ its eye.

Al-Ṭabarī: needle, its eye, and the standing of the jummal reading

Does the verse concern everyone who makes mistakes?

The verse identifies the people concerned through two specific descriptions:

كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا

kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā

and

وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا عَنْهَا

wa-istakbarū ʿanhā

Those who denied the signs and treated them arrogantly.

The image must not be detached from these qualifications and applied to everyone who has made a mistake or doubts their understanding of an issue.

The verse states the fate of a group with specified qualities.

Khiyāṭ itself does not mean denial, arrogance, punishment, or exclusion from the Garden.

It names an actual implement employed within the Qur’anic image.

The moral significance and outcome come from the complete verse.

6. Differences from related roots

KH Y Ṭ (khayṭ) compared with Ḥ B L (ḥabl)

Ḥabl denotes rope, cord, or a binding used to connect, pull, and secure things.

Khayṭ is also a strand usable for binding or joining.

They are close in some uses, but khayṭ in its familiar sense foregrounds a finer strand.

A thin cord may be called khayṭ in early Arabic, but not every thick rope can be called that without regard to attested usage.

“Thread” is therefore more suitable than “rope” in Al-Baqarah 2:187, since the image concerns the slender extension of dawn’s light.

Study of Ḥ B L

KH Y Ṭ (khiyāṭah) compared with N S J (nasj)

Nasj concerns weaving or interlacing strands to produce cloth or a similar structure.

Khiyāṭah commonly concerns sewing existing parts together with thread.

In weaving, strands can produce the cloth’s surface itself.

In sewing, thread can connect pre-existing pieces of cloth.

This helps explain khāṭa, even though weaving and sewing may occur together in some activities.

KH Y Ṭ (khayṭ) compared with F T L (fatl)

Fatl concerns twisting strands around one another.

Thread or cord can be made by twisting fibers.

Fatl may therefore contribute to producing khayṭ.

Khayṭ names the strand, while fatl expresses the twisting action.

Al-khayṭ al-abyaḍ in Al-Baqarah 2:187 must therefore not be translated as a twisted strand of light, since twisting is not a necessary part of the image.

KH Y Ṭ (khayṭ) compared with S L K (silk)

Silk in Arabic can concern a strand or something passed through parts or openings.

Lexical sources explain khayṭ through silk.

Verbal salaka also concerns inserting and passing along a course.

Sewing combines thread with passing it through cloth, but the strand’s name and the act of passing are not a single concept.

Salaka appears elsewhere in the Qur’an. Those uses must not be transferred into khayṭ without independent investigation.

KH Y Ṭ (khayṭ) compared with KH Ṭ Ṭ (khaṭṭ)

KH Ṭ Ṭ concerns making a line or extended mark on a surface.

KH Y Ṭ also involves slender extension, but its basic use refers to an actual strand.

A line on paper may be thin like thread; a strand lying on the ground may look like a line.

A drawn line and an actual thread are nevertheless different things.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, comparing dawn’s light to thread is understandable through slender extension. It must not be equated with drawing lines or writing.

Study of KH Ṭ Ṭ

KH Y Ṭ (khayṭ) compared with F J R (fajr)

Fajr in Arabic concerns splitting and the appearance of morning light.

Khayṭ in its basic sense is a thin strand.

Both occur in one expression in Al-Baqarah 2:187, but perform different semantic functions.

Khayṭ provides the image of light’s slender extension.

Fajr identifies the natural phenomenon concerned.

Dawn therefore cannot be derived from khayṭ alone, just as fajr is not limited to a strand of thread.

Study of F J R

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

In general Arabic use, khayṭ means thread or strand.

When sewing garments is involved, thread is more natural.

For a slender line of light, hair, or another extension, strand may be more suitable.

Khiyāṭ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40 means needle.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, the expression’s imagery should be retained.

Proposed translation:

حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَكُمُ الْخَيْطُ الْأَبْيَضُ مِنَ الْخَيْطِ الْأَسْوَدِ مِنَ الْفَجْرِ

ḥattā yatabayyana lakumu al-khayṭu al-abyaḍu mina al-khayṭi al-aswadi mina al-fajri

“Until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct to you from the black thread.”

This retains both the thread image and its connection with dawn.

The lexical explanation clarifies that the white thread denotes the first morning light and the black thread night’s darkness.

“Until a specified hour before sunrise” or “until the dawn call to prayer” is not the direct meaning of khayṭ. Such expressions replace the verse’s natural image with a different temporal sign or term.

Proposed translation in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40:

حَتَّىٰ يَلِجَ الْجَمَلُ فِي سَمِّ الْخِيَاطِ

ḥattā yalija al-jamalu fī sammi al-khiyāṭi

“Until the camel passes through the needle’s eye.”

Khiyāṭ means needle here, and samm is its eye or opening.

Brief root meaning

A slender, extended strand or form; in sewing, thread, stitching, and its implements.

Meanings in Qur’anic uses

الْخَيْطُ الْأَبْيَضُ — Al-Baqarah 2:187: the white thread; the slender extension of the first light of dawn.

الْخَيْطِ الْأَسْوَدِ — Al-Baqarah 2:187: the black thread; night’s darkness contrasted with dawn’s light.

الْخِيَاطِ — Al-Aʿrāf 7:40: the needle; the sewing implement whose small eye figures in the image.

These equivalents keep the brief root meaning distinct from the meaning in the verse.

Conclusion

KH Y Ṭ concerns slender, threadlike extension. Thread is its clearest instance, and the family also supplies the verb for sewing and the name of its implement.

Early Arabic extends the field to dawn’s light, white strands of hair, and certain elongated natural forms. Poetic witnesses show khayṭ describing light or color visible in a slender extension.

The two occurrences in Al-Baqarah 2:187 concern light and darkness. Khayṭ does not identify two actual threads; it pictures the slender extension of the emerging boundary between dawn and night.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:40, khiyāṭ names a needle, and samm al-khiyāṭ its eye. This implement figures in an image of a specified group’s impossible entry into the Garden.

The fundamental meaning can thus be expressed as slender, threadlike extension. In actual usage, khayṭ names thread, and the same family includes sewing and its implements.

This distinction helps explain both verses: Al-Baqarah uses the thread’s slender shape for a natural sign; Al-Aʿrāf uses a sewing implement’s actual eye within an image of an impossible event.

The root’s uses should therefore neither be restricted to literal thread nor detached from their specific lexical meanings in Qur’anic imagery. Brief root meaning: A slender, extended strand or form; in sewing, thread, stitching, and its implements.
