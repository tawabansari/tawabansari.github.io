Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root SH Y A

Willing and wanting; in the nominal branch, a thing or matter; distinguishing lexical meaning from interpretation of divine will.

Important Forms from This Root

شَاءَ / يَشَاءُ (shāʾa / yashāʾu)

Willed; wills; Qur’anic form I verb.

تَشَاءُونَ (tashāʾūn)

You will; second-person plural imperfect.

شِئْتَ / شَاءُوا (shiʾta / shāʾū)

You willed; they willed; past forms.

مَشِيئَة (mashīʾah)

Willing or volition; a lexical verbal noun, not a Qur’anic form.

شَيْء / أَشْيَاء

Thing or matter; things or matters; Qur’anic nouns.

Method note

The Qur’anic morphological index groups two branches under SH Y A: shāʾa, “to will,” and shayʾ, “thing.” It records 519 occurrences: 236 verbs and 283 nouns. This morphological classification does not by itself establish the historical development of the two meanings.

In studying will, lexical meaning must be distinguished from interpretation. “To will” is the verb’s direct meaning. The relation of divine will to human choice, events, guidance, and responsibility requires the sentence and wider Qur’anic context; these discussions should not all be inserted into the translation of one word.

Central meaning and the two branches

Shāʾa and yashāʾu denote willing or wanting. Their subject can be a human being or God. The object or continuation identifies what is willed. The verb does not invariably mean permitting, loving, or approving.

Shayʾ is a general noun for a thing or matter under discussion; its plural is ashyāʾ. Al-Kahf Al-Kahf 18:23 uses it for a future undertaking, while Al-Māʾidah Al-Ma'idah 5:101 uses the plural for matters asked about. The noun must not be translated as “willing.” A shared morphological family does not make noun and verb identical in meaning.

An illustration, not an established material origin

A person may want to act yet lack the ability, opportunity, or means. This illustrates the distinction between willing and realization, but is not evidence of a material origin or historical derivation of the root.

Expressions such as “opening the field of possibility” are interpretive explanations, not the fixed lexical meaning of shāʾa. Such illustrations should not become compulsory translation equivalents.

Classical Arabic and morphology

Arabic lexicons identify mashīʾah as a verbal noun of shāʾa, yashāʾu, meaning willing or volition. Mashīʾah itself does not occur in the Qur’an, although the verb’s forms are frequent.

Shiʾta is second-person masculine singular past; shāʾū is third-person masculine plural past; tashāʾūna is second-person masculine plural imperfect. These are form I verbs. Shayʾ and its plural ashyāʾ are nouns and must be distinguished from the verbal forms.

Mashīʾah and irādah overlap lexically. Without contextual evidence, no absolute rule makes one mere intention and the other guaranteed realization. Nor does grouping shāʾa and shayʾ together establish a philosophical conclusion about all existence.

Qur’anic uses

The future and divine will

وَلا تَقولَنَّ لِشَا۟ىءٍ إِنّى فاعِلٌ ذٰلِكَ غَدًا

And never say of anything, “I will do that tomorrow,”

Al-Kahf 18:23

إِلّا أَن يَشاءَ اللَّهُ وَاذكُر رَبَّكَ إِذا نَسيتَ وَقُل عَسىٰ أَن يَهدِيَنِ رَبّى لِأَقرَبَ مِن هٰذا رَشَدًا

unless The God wills. Remember your Lord when you forget, and say, “Perhaps my Lord will guide me to what is nearer than this to right conduct.”

Al-Kahf 18:24

The exception at the start of the second verse qualifies the preceding statement. These verses relate an assertion about tomorrow’s action to God’s will; the second also commands remembrance after forgetting and seeking guidance to a nearer course of right conduct.

Human willing and its dependence

لِمَن شاءَ مِنكُم أَن يَستَقيمَ

For whoever among you wills to be upright.

At-Takwir 81:28

وَما تَشاءونَ إِلّا أَن يَشاءَ اللَّهُ رَبُّ العٰلَمينَ

And you do not will unless The God, Lord of the worlds, wills.

At-Takwir 81:29

The first speaks of a person willing uprightness; the next denies that willing is independent of God. They should be read together, without treating either as a complete account of how freedom and divine will operate.

Different ways and testing

وَأَنزَلنا إِلَيكَ الكِتٰبَ بِالحَقِّ مُصَدِّقًا لِما بَينَ يَدَيهِ مِنَ الكِتٰبِ وَمُهَيمِنًا عَلَيهِ فَاحكُم بَينَهُم بِما أَنزَلَ اللَّهُ وَلا تَتَّبِع أَهواءَهُم عَمّا جاءَكَ مِنَ الحَقِّ لِكُلٍّ جَعَلنا مِنكُم شِرعَةً وَمِنهاجًا وَلَو شاءَ اللَّهُ لَجَعَلَكُم أُمَّةً وٰحِدَةً وَلٰكِن لِيَبلُوَكُم فى ما ءاتىٰكُم فَاستَبِقُوا الخَيرٰتِ إِلَى اللَّهِ مَرجِعُكُم جَميعًا فَيُنَبِّئُكُم بِما كُنتُم فيهِ تَختَلِفونَ

We sent down to you the Book in truth, confirming the scripture before it and guarding over it. So judge between them by what The God has sent down, and do not follow their desires away from the truth that has come to you. For each of you We appointed a law and a clear way. Had The God willed, He would have made you one community, but He tests you in what He has given you. So compete in good deeds. To The God you will all return, and He will inform you about that in which you differed.

Al-Ma'idah 5:48

This verse brings together will, differing appointed ways, testing, and competition in good deeds. Their relation comes from the whole verse, not from translating shāʾa as a philosophical definition.

Faith and rejection of coercion

وَلَو شاءَ رَبُّكَ لَـٔامَنَ مَن فِى الأَرضِ كُلُّهُم جَميعًا أَفَأَنتَ تُكرِهُ النّاسَ حَتّىٰ يَكونوا مُؤمِنينَ

Had your Lord willed, everyone on earth would have believed, all together. Will you then compel people to become believers?

Yunus 10:99

The closing question challenges compelling people to believe. This concerns the audience’s choice and responsibility, while shāʾa retains the meaning of willing.

Will and creation

قالَت رَبِّ أَنّىٰ يَكونُ لى وَلَدٌ وَلَم يَمسَسنى بَشَرٌ قالَ كَذٰلِكِ اللَّهُ يَخلُقُ ما يَشاءُ إِذا قَضىٰ أَمرًا فَإِنَّما يَقولُ لَهُ كُن فَيَكونُ

She said, “My Lord, how can I have a child when no human has touched me?” He said, “Thus The God creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He only says to it, ‘Be,’ and it is.”

Al-Imran 3:47

The answer to Mary relates creation to God’s will. Willing, creating, and decreeing are expressed by separate verbs and should remain distinct in translation.

Will and moral approval

إِن تَكفُروا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَنِىٌّ عَنكُم وَلا يَرضىٰ لِعِبادِهِ الكُفرَ وَإِن تَشكُروا يَرضَهُ لَكُم وَلا تَزِرُ وازِرَةٌ وِزرَ أُخرىٰ ثُمَّ إِلىٰ رَبِّكُم مَرجِعُكُم فَيُنَبِّئُكُم بِما كُنتُم تَعمَلونَ إِنَّهُ عَليمٌ بِذاتِ الصُّدورِ

If you disbelieve, The God is independent of you, and He does not approve disbelief for His servants. If you give thanks, He approves it for you. No bearer of burdens bears another’s burden. Then to your Lord is your return, and He will inform you of what you used to do. He knows what is within the breasts.

Az-Zumar 39:7

This verse connects disapproval of disbelief and approval of gratitude with individual responsibility and return to the Lord. It concerns approval, not an occurrence of shāʾa, and serves here to clarify the conceptual distinction.

Distinctions from related words

Irādah and mashīʾah both concern willing; any distinction must be established in a particular use. Idhn concerns permission, amr command, and qadar measure or determination. Their occurrence in a shared discussion does not make them synonyms.

Riḍā concerns pleasure or approval. Al-Zumar Az-Zumar 39:7 explicitly states that God does not approve disbelief for His servants. Discussion of an act’s occurrence in relation to divine will should therefore not be equated with moral endorsement.

Translation method

“Willed,” “wanted,” or “wills” normally translates shāʾa according to tense and syntax. Preserve subject, object, negation, condition, and exception. Discussion of wisdom, choice, or possible realization belongs in commentary, not as a fixed addition to the verb.

Al-Kahf Al-Kahf 18:23 and Al-Kahf Al-Kahf 18:24 are connected in meaning but have separate verse numbers. Quoting each completely and numbering it separately is compatible with explaining their connection.

Translation suggestions

Shāʾa: “willed” or “wanted”; yashāʾu: “wills”; shiʾta: “you willed”; shāʾū: “they willed”; tashāʾūna: “you will.” The final wording depends on tense, conditional structure, and context.

Shayʾ: “thing” or “matter”; ashyāʾ: “things” or “matters.” In negative or universal constructions, preserve the sentence’s scope, such as “nothing” or “everything,” without assigning negation or universality to the noun alone.

Complete translations of the examples appear in the Qur’anic-use section. Explaining a construction should not merge pieces of separate verses or present a fragment as a complete verse.

Summary

Within SH Y A, shāʾa denotes willing and shayʾ denotes a thing or matter. Their forms and meanings must be distinguished. Mashīʾah is a lexical verbal noun; that form itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

The verses speak both of human willing and its dependence on divine will. Their relationship requires interpretation across the relevant verses; lexical meaning alone does not settle the entire question of freedom. Will, permission, command, and moral approval should not receive one undifferentiated translation.
