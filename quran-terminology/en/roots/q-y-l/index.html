Qaylūlah: resting at midday; maqīl: a place of ease and rest

1. Meaning and background in early Arabic

In its principal Quranic use, Q Y L concerns resting at midday. In Arabic, qāla yaqīlu qaylūlatan means to rest or sleep at midday.

A qāʾil is someone resting at that time; maqīl is the place or time of such rest.

In Arabian life, especially during hot seasons, midday offered an occasion to reduce activity and seek shade and coolness. People and animals might stop moving, rest somewhere suitable, and resume their activities after the heat diminished.

Imagine, for example, a caravan travelling since morning. As the sun rises high and the heat intensifies, its travellers stop under trees or beside a rock. Some sleep, some eat, and others simply rest.

Arabic calls this time and its associated rest qāʾilah and qaylūlah.

Qaylūlah does not necessarily mean falling asleep.

Some early definitions identify it as midday sleep; others also include resting at that time without sleeping.

Three things must therefore be distinguished: midday itself, rest at midday, and sleep that may form part of that rest.

A witness from Labīd ibn Rabīʿah

A poem attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, whose life spanned the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

سَلَبْتُ بِهَا هَجْرًا بُيُوتَ نِعَاجِهِ

salabtu bihā hajran buyūta niʿājihi

وَرُعْتُ قَطَاهُ فِي الْمَبِيتِ وَقَائِلَا

wa-ruʿtu qaṭāhu fī al-mabīti wa-qāʾilan

“On that mount, in the midday heat I drove the wild animals from their shelters and startled the sandgrouse at their night resting place and at midday rest.”

The poet describes travelling through desert country and encountering its animals.

Here al-mabīt concerns resting overnight, whereas qāʾilan refers to midday rest or its time.

The contrast shows that midday rest was a familiar time and condition in the daily life described by early poetry.

Early explanations of the poem likewise relate qāʾil to qaylūlah.

Labīd lived before and after the rise of Islam. The precise date of this verse is unclear, so it cannot confidently be assigned to the pre-Islamic period.

It nevertheless provides evidence from the early poetic tradition for this family's association with midday rest.

A witness from Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

فَلَوْ أَنَّ أَهْلَ الدَّارِ فِيهَا كَعَهْدِنَا

fa-law anna ahla al-dāri fīhā ka-ʿahdinā

وَجَدْتُ مَقِيلًا عِنْدَهُمْ وَمُعَرَّسَا

wajadtu maqīlan ʿindahum wa-muʿarrasan

“If the people of the dwelling were there as we once knew them, I would find among them a place for midday rest and a place to halt at night.”

The poet speaks of a place formerly inhabited by people familiar to him.

Maqīl here is a daytime resting place, while muʿarras concerns a place to halt at night.

Together they express two different needs of a traveller: rest at midday and a nighttime stopping place.

Maqīl does not merely denote sleeping here; it refers to a place where one stops to rest.

The verse occurs in the collected poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays. Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet is familiar, but its exact wording and date of composition are not independently certain.

What the historical witnesses establish

These witnesses connect qāʾilah, qaylūlah, and maqīl in the early Arabic tradition with midday, rest at that time, and a suitable resting place.

This meaning also appears in the Quran.

A defensible historical definition of the Quranic branch is stopping to rest at midday, especially in the heat, whether or not accompanied by sleep.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • Qāla yaqīlu: to rest or sleep at midday.
  • Qaylūlah: midday rest; specifically, sleep at that time.
  • Qāʾilah: midday or its resting time; sometimes qaylūlah itself.
  • Qayl: midday rest in one use, and a drink or milk taken then in another.
  • Qāʾil: someone resting at midday.
  • Quyyal and qiyāl: recorded plurals for those resting at midday.
  • Maqīl: a midday resting place; also the rest itself or its time.
  • Maqāl: a midday resting place in this root's relevant use.
  • Qayyala: to provide a place for midday rest; also to give a midday drink.
  • Taqyīl: providing rest or a drink at midday.
  • Taqayyala: in some uses, to rest or drink at midday.
  • Aqāla al-ibila: to bring camels to drink at midday.
  • Qaylah: one occasion of drinking at midday.
  • Qaylūlāt: plural of qaylūlah, occasions of midday rest.

Arabic sources also record aqāla al-bayʿa, to cancel a transaction, and aqāla ʿathratahu, to pardon someone's lapse or remove its consequences.

These uses are formally close to Q Y L and appear under the same entry in some dictionaries. Their meanings should not, however, be derived directly from midday sleep without historical investigation.

Taqayyala is also recorded in the sense of resembling someone. Early sources associate this use with resemblance and following a previous example; its historical origin is not necessarily identical to that of qaylūlah.

The semantic core of the Quranic branch

Three main elements appear in uses concerning qaylūlah.

First is midday, when the heat may approach its greatest intensity.

Second is stopping or reducing activity in order to rest.

Third is a place where a person or animal can settle down.

These elements are connected but are not synonymous.

Qāʾilah can denote midday itself.

Qaylūlah concerns rest at that time.

Maqīl can denote the resting place.

Someone may, for example, be at home at noon but still working. The time of qāʾilah has arrived, but qaylūlah has not necessarily taken place.

If that person stops working and rests somewhere suitable, qaylūlah becomes applicable as well.

Does qaylūlah necessarily involve sleep?

Midday sleep is a clear example of qaylūlah in established Arabic usage.

Rest without sleep also belongs within its semantic range.

This distinction matters when translating Quranic verses.

Always translating qāʾilūn as “sleepers” would lose the possibility of resting without sleep.

Conversely, defining it only as “remaining awake at midday” would exclude its familiar use for sleep.

“Resting at midday” is therefore the broader meaning that covers both conditions.

3. Quranic use: Al-Aʿrāf 7:4

This root occurs twice in the Quran.

Its first occurrence is qāʾilūn in Al-Aʿrāf 7:4.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:4

وَكَمْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ أَهْلَكْنَاهَا فَجَاءَهَا بَأْسُنَا بَيَاتًا أَوْ هُمْ قَائِلُونَ

wa-kam min qaryatin ahlaknāhā fa-jāʾahā baʾsunā bayātan aw hum qāʾilūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:4

“How many towns We destroyed! Our severity came upon them by night or while they were resting at midday.”

Qāʾilūn here describes people resting at midday.

The structure of qāʾilūn

Qāʾilūn is the sound masculine plural of the active participle of qāla yaqīlu.

Its formation follows the active-participle pattern of the basic triliteral verb.

Its singular is qāʾil and its plural qāʾilūn.

In the verse, hum is a plural pronoun and qāʾilūn its predicate.

Hum qāʾilūn thus describes the people's condition when the event in question reached them.

Qāʾilūn in this verse does not mean “speakers.”

Arabic qāʾil can be formed from two different roots:

From Q W L, meaning someone who speaks.

From Q Y L, meaning someone resting at midday.

The outward form is identical, but the root and meaning differ.

The contrast between night and midday in Al-Aʿrāf 7:4 indicates the second meaning.

Bayātan aw hum qāʾilūn

The verse contrasts two times or conditions.

Bayātan belongs to B Y T and concerns night and staying overnight in this context.

Hum qāʾilūn refers to people resting at midday.

The Quran thus describes severity arriving at two familiar times of rest: night and midday.

At night, many people set aside their daytime activities and return home.

In the midday heat, people may likewise stop working or travelling to rest.

In either condition, they may lack their usual readiness for an unexpected event.

This feature emerges from the juxtaposition of bayātan and qāʾilūn.

Qāʾilūn itself, however, does not mean unaware, heedless, or unable to defend oneself.

Those ideas may belong to the overall scene, while the word directly denotes midday rest.

The people and subject of the verse

The verse begins with qaryah.

In Quranic usage, qaryah can refer to a settlement and its resident community.

The feminine singular pronouns in ahlaknāhā and fa-jāʾahā agree with qaryah.

In hum qāʾilūn, however, the plural pronoun refers to the settlement's people.

The people rest, not the settlement's buildings or land.

This distinction in pronoun reference must be maintained.

Does the verse identify every midday disaster as punishment?

No. It concerns settlements whose destruction is presented within divine judgment.

Immediately beforehand, Al-Aʿrāf 7:3 commands people to follow what has been sent down from their Lord.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:5 describes wrongdoers acknowledging their wrongdoing when the severity arrives.

The passage thus concerns responsibility and the consequences of communities' conduct.

It does not establish that every earthquake, flood, illness, or midday disaster is divine punishment of its victims.

A natural event occurring and the moral responsibility of individuals being established are separate matters.

Nor does Q Y L itself mean punishment or destruction. Those meanings come from other words and the context.

Connection with Al-Aʿrāf 7:5

The next verse describes the people's reaction when severity arrives: they acknowledge their wrongdoing.

This connection makes their time of rest in Al-Aʿrāf 7:4 part of the picture of consequences arriving unexpectedly.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:4 presents their time and condition when the severity reaches them.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:5 presents their reaction and acknowledgment.

Each verse contributes a distinct element to the scene.

4. Quranic use: Al-Furqān 25:24

The resting place of the Garden's people

Al-Furqān 25:24

أَصْحَابُ الْجَنَّةِ يَوْمَئِذٍ خَيْرٌ مُسْتَقَرًّا وَأَحْسَنُ مَقِيلًا

aṣḥābu al-jannati yawmaʾidhin khayrun mustaqarran wa-aḥsanu maqīlan

Al-Furqān 25:24

“The companions of the Garden that day have a better settlement and a finer resting place.”

Maqīlan here concerns a place of ease and rest.

The structure of maqīl

Maqīl is formed from Q Y L. In its place-denoting use, its underlying form is maqyil on the mafʿil pattern; vowel transfer and the resulting sound change yield maqīl.

In this familiar use it is a noun of place: somewhere qaylūlah takes place.

Early sources also record maqīl for the rest itself and sometimes its time.

Three aspects can therefore be considered in its structure and use:

The place of rest.

The time of rest.

The act of resting itself.

In Al-Furqān 25:24, its pairing with mustaqarran supports the sense of a resting place.

Mustaqarran belongs to Q R R and concerns a place of settlement or stability.

Maqīlan likewise denotes a place of ease and rest.

The sentence thus describes both a better settlement and a finer resting place for the Garden's people.

The distinction between mustaqarr and maqīl

Their meanings are close but not identical.

Mustaqarr concerns settling and establishment.

Maqīl concerns ease and rest, drawing on the familiar image of qaylūlah.

For example, a home may be someone's residence without being a suitable resting place because of heat, noise, or other conditions.

Conversely, someone may rest briefly under a tree without making it a permanent residence.

A residence and a resting place can coincide, but they express different characteristics.

Al-Furqān 25:24 brings these characteristics together in describing the Garden's people.

Does maqīl in the Garden mean midday sleep?

In Arabic, maqīl is related to qaylūlah: rest at midday.

Its use here does not by itself prove that the Garden's people necessarily sleep at a specific hour.

Early Arabic sources allow maqīl and qaylūlah to refer to resting without sleep.

Understanding this verse therefore does not require treating sleep as an established part of their condition.

Nor should the lexical association with midday rest be erased merely because the verse concerns the Hereafter.

The Quran uses a familiar image: a place where one withdraws from heat, fatigue, and strenuous activity to rest.

Applied to the Garden's people, this image expresses the goodness and ease of their abode.

The word alone does not fully specify the temporal or bodily nature of that rest.

Does the verse specify an hour on the Day of Resurrection?

No. Some early exegetical accounts say that judgment ends within part of the day and the Garden's people reach their resting place at midday.

Al-Furqān 25:24 itself does not provide such a precise timetable.

Yawmaʾidhin points to that day without specifying an hour or elapsed duration.

Maqīlan describes the resting place in this context.

The meaning of qaylūlah should therefore not be used to claim that the Quran necessarily sets the end of judgment at midday.

Connection with the preceding verses

Al-Furqān 25:22 speaks of the day when the guilty see the angels but receive no good news.

Al-Furqān 25:23 describes their deeds being reduced to scattered dust.

Al-Furqān 25:24 then presents the Garden's people as having a better settlement and a finer resting place.

This sequence presents two different conditions.

On one side are those facing the adverse outcome of their deeds.

On the other are the Garden's people, enjoying a good and restful abode.

Maqīl thus does more than name a place in this context: the contrast of outcomes highlights the ease and goodness of their abode.

Its value in the Hereafter nevertheless comes from the whole sentence and context; Q Y L itself does not mean reward, the Garden, or salvation.

Khayrun and aḥsanu

Khayrun and aḥsanu express the superiority and goodness of the Garden's abode.

In Arabic, a comparative construction does not necessarily imply that the other side possesses the same good quality.

When two outcomes are compared, one may be truly good while the other is fundamentally undesirable.

Khayrun mustaqarran therefore does not establish that the guilty also have a good abode, with the Garden only slightly better.

The verse emphasizes the goodness and superiority of the Garden's abode.

5. Distinguishing Q Y L from related roots and words

Q Y L (qāla yaqīlu) and Q W L (qāla yaqūlu): resting and speaking

This comparison is especially important for identifying the root.

Arabic has two verbs with the same past-tense form:

Qāla yaqīlu: to rest at midday.

Qāla yaqūlu: to speak.

Both can form the active participle qāʾil.

Their roots, however, differ.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:4, qāʾilūn belongs to Q Y L and denotes people resting at midday.

By contrast, qāʾilun in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:51 denotes someone who speaks.

Not every qāʾil can therefore be assigned to Q W L without considering context.

Qīla in expressions such as wa-qīla also belongs to the verb of speaking and Q W L, not to qaylūlah.

The verb, its formation, and the sentence's meaning clarify the distinction.

Q Y L (qaylūlah) and N W M (nawm): midday rest and sleep

Nawm is used in the Quran for sleep in general.

Al-Rūm 30:23 identifies human sleep at night and by day as one of the divine signs.

Nawm is therefore not confined to a particular time of day.

Qaylūlah, in its principal use, concerns resting at midday.

Sleep may form part of it, but not every qaylūlah necessarily involves sleep.

Nawm names sleep itself, whereas qaylūlah describes rest associated with a particular time.

Q Y L (qaylūlah) and H J ʿ (hujūʿ): midday rest and nighttime sleep

Hujūʿ belongs to H J ʿ and in familiar Arabic usage concerns sleep, especially at night.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17, yahjaʿūn describes people who slept little of the night.

Qāʾilūn in Al-Aʿrāf 7:4, however, concerns midday rest.

The temporal distinction is clear in these two Quranic uses.

One concerns nighttime sleep, the other rest at midday.

This should not become an absolute rule that all derivatives of hujūʿ concern only night or all derivatives of qaylūlah require actual sleep.

Qaylūlah also includes resting without sleep.

Q Y L (qaylūlah) and H J D (tahajjud): rest and nighttime wakefulness

Tahajjud occurs in Al-Isrāʾ 17:79 in the context of nighttime wakefulness for worship.

Qaylūlah, by contrast, concerns midday rest.

In ordinary life, someone who has stayed awake for part of the night may rest for a while at midday.

This practical relationship does not make qaylūlah and tahajjud lexically identical.

Nor does the Quran set a devotional schedule for dividing sleep between night and day in Al-Aʿrāf 7:4 or Al-Furqān 25:24.

Q Y L (maqīl) and Q R R (mustaqarr): resting place and settlement

These words appear together in Al-Furqān 25:24.

Mustaqarr concerns settling and establishment.

Maqīl concerns a place of ease and rest.

One place may serve both residence and rest.

That overlap does not make the two roots identical.

Together, the two terms describe the goodness of the Garden's abode from two perspectives.

Q Y L (qāʾilah) and H J R (hājirah): midday and midday heat

Hājirah concerns the time of intense daytime heat, especially around midday.

Qāʾilah names the time of midday rest.

The two can refer to the same period while differing in meaning.

Hājirah highlights the heat and the hot part of the day.

Qāʾilah identifies the time when people rest.

Midday heat may thus be a familiar reason for qaylūlah, but qaylūlah itself does not mean heat.

Qayl as a title of rulership

Arabic sources also record qayl as a title for certain Himyarite rulers and notables in Yemen, with plurals including aqyāl and aqwāl.

Its derivation is disputed.

Some associate it with Q W L and the authority of a ruler whose word carries force.

Others explain it through forms concerning following or resembling a previous ruler.

Its origins in ancient South Arabian languages are also a subject of separate investigation.

This historical title should therefore not be treated as a derivative of qaylūlah without evidence.

Neither qāʾilūn nor maqīlan in the Quran means ruler.

6. Challenges in interpretation and translation

Does qāʾilūn in Al-Aʿrāf 7:4 mean speakers?

No. Although its outward form can match the active participle of speaking, the context establishes midday rest.

Bayātan is contrasted here with hum qāʾilūn.

The passage concerns people's condition at night or midday, not their being engaged in speech.

Must qāʾilūn mean sleepers?

Midday sleep is a familiar use of the family, but Arabic also applies qaylūlah to rest without sleep.

“While they were resting at midday” is therefore appropriate.

It preserves the central meaning without unnecessarily restricting the word to sleeping.

Does maqīl in Al-Furqān 25:24 mean a sleeping place?

Maqīl can be a place for midday sleep, but its broader meaning includes a place of ease and rest.

The verse in Al-Furqān concerns the abode of the Garden's people.

“Resting place” is therefore suitable: it retains rest without fixing a particular kind of sleep or exact time.

Must the Garden have midday and night in their earthly form?

Maqīl alone does not establish that conclusion.

Language can use the name of a time or activity to describe a place without transferring all its natural conditions to the new setting.

Calling somewhere a resting place, for example, means it is suitable for rest, not that it can be used only at a particular hour.

Al-Furqān 25:24 clearly expresses the goodness of the Garden's resting place.

The nature of time, sleep, and rest there requires independent evidence.

Does the Quran command qaylūlah as worship?

No. Neither Quranic occurrence of this root is a command to take midday rest.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:4 describes people resting when severity reaches them.

Al-Furqān 25:24 describes the resting place of the Garden's people.

These two verses alone do not establish an obligation or a specific schedule for midday sleep.

Rest at midday may be useful in particular bodily or living conditions. That is distinct from the root's lexical meaning and from a separate religious ruling requiring its own evidence.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for familiar forms include:

Qāla yaqīlu: to rest or sleep at midday.

Qaylūlah: midday rest; more specifically, midday sleep.

Qāʾilah: midday; the time of midday rest.

Qāʾil: someone resting at midday.

Qayl: in this branch, midday rest or a midday drink.

Maqīl: a resting place; in another use, midday rest itself.

Maqāl: in this root's relevant use, a midday resting place.

Qayyala: to provide someone with a place for midday rest; alternatively, to give someone a midday drink.

Taqyīl: providing rest or a drink at midday.

Taqayyala: in the qaylūlah branch, to rest or drink at midday.

Uses concerning transactions and lapses are appropriately rendered “cancel a transaction” and “pardon a fault.” These branches should not be added to the definition of qaylūlah without derivational investigation.

Quranic translations

Al-Aʿrāf 7:4: qāʾilūn

Suggested equivalent: “while they were resting at midday.”

The existing Forqan translation:

“How many towns We destroyed! Our severity came upon them by night or while they were resting at midday.”

This rendering retains midday and does not restrict rest to sleeping.

Al-Furqān 25:24: maqīlan

Suggested equivalent: “resting place” or “place of ease.”

The existing Forqan translation:

“The companions of the Garden that day have a better settlement and a finer resting place.”

This is appropriate because it preserves the distinction between mustaqarr and maqīl.

Mustaqarr is a settlement; maqīl is a resting place.

Neither verse therefore requires a change to the current Forqan translation on this point.

8. Conclusion

In its Quranic branch, Q Y L concerns resting at midday.

Qaylūlah may involve sleep, but Arabic also applies it to rest without sleep.

Qāʾil names someone resting at that time; maqīl names a place used for such rest.

Early poetic witnesses, including qāʾil in Labīd and maqīl in poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, connect these words with daily life, travel, and rest in the midday heat.

Q Y L occurs twice in the Quran.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:4, qāʾilūn describes people resting at midday when severity reaches them.

In Al-Furqān 25:24, maqīlan describes the Garden's abode alongside mustaqarran, expressing its ease and goodness.

Together these uses show that midday rest can describe an earthly condition and also supply imagery for an abode of rest in the Hereafter.

The root itself, however, means neither punishment nor the Garden and reward.

It must also be distinguished from Q W L, speaking. Qāʾil can have the same outward form in both families while differing in root and meaning.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its principal Quranic branch, Q Y L denotes resting at midday, whether accompanied by sleep or simply by stopping and settling down during the day's heat.

Qāʾil is someone resting at that time; maqīl is the place or setting of that rest.

In the Quran, qāʾilūn means those resting at midday and maqīlan means a resting place. Its use for the Hereafter retains the image of rest without authorizing the transfer of earthly timetables and bodily conditions without evidence.
