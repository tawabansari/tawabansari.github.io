Nuʿās in early Arabic and the Qur’an: its distinction from full sleep and its connection with security and calm in difficult circumstances

The three-letter Arabic root ن ع س concerns drowsiness, approaching sleep or falling into light sleep. In familiar usage, nuʿās denotes the heaviness of sleepiness, diminished attentiveness and the beginning of sleep, usually distinguished from deep or full sleep.

Only nuʿās occurs from this root in the Qur’an. Both occurrences accompany amanah, security or a sense of safety: in Āl ʿImrān after distress and disruption in battle, and in Al-Anfāl amid difficult circumstances before battle.

This association matters. Nuʿās itself means neither security nor psychological calm, but drowsiness. In these two particular settings, the Qur’an connects it with security and calm granted by The God to a group of believers.

A precise study should distinguish natural drowsiness, full sleep and the role drowsiness plays in these two contexts.

1. Semantic Core and Forms

Established Arabic forms include:

  • نَعَسَ (naʿasa): became drowsy; sleepiness overtook him.
  • نُعَاس (nuʿās): drowsiness, dozing and the onset of sleep.
  • نَعْسَة (naʿsah): a single spell of drowsiness or a brief doze.
  • نَاعِس (nāʿis): drowsy; someone experiencing nuʿās.
  • نَعْسَان (naʿsān): drowsy; an attested form whose acceptability and usage have been disputed by lexicographers.
  • نَاعِسَة و نَعُوس (nāʿisah, naʿūs): feminine descriptions of drowsiness.
  • نَاقَةٌ نَعُوسٌ (nāqatun naʿūsun): a she-camel that becomes drowsy when milked.

Extended Arabic uses also concern bodily slackness, weakness and sluggish trade.

Only the noun nuʿās occurs in the Qur’an: nuʿāsan in Āl ʿImrān 3:154 and al-nuʿāsa in Al-Anfāl 8:11. The definite article and the distinction between definite and indefinite do not create separate derivatives; both are the same noun.

Semantic Core

The root’s clear core is becoming drowsy or approaching sleep.

A person may experience heavy eyes, reduced awareness of the surroundings and a desire to sleep. This may lead to a brief doze or precede a longer sleep.

Nuʿās should therefore not always be equated with deep sleep. Arabic lexical accounts do not, however, establish one rigid boundary between nuʿās and nawm. Some call nuʿās sleep itself; others describe approaching sleep, its heaviness or its beginning.

This variation does not erase the distinction, but shows that the words’ ranges can overlap.

“Drowsiness and the onset of sleep” is a more precise brief definition than “deep sleep.” Brief dozing and light sleep should not be excluded absolutely from the word’s range either.

2. N ʿ S in Early Arabic

Drowsiness and Dozing

Arabic lexical accounts use naʿasa when someone becomes sleepy and sometimes falls briefly asleep.

One established expression is:

نَعَسْنَا نَعْسَةً وَاحِدَةً

naʿasnā naʿsatan wāḥidatan

Translation: “We dozed once.”

Naʿsah expresses one spell of nuʿās. It can therefore describe a brief, bounded occurrence rather than necessarily several hours of sleep.

Nāʿim, “comfortable” or “at ease,” should not be confused with nāʿis, “drowsy.” Comfort may help someone become sleepy, but it is a possible cause or setting, not the meaning of nāʿis.

A Poetic Witness: Nuʿās and Nawm

ʿAdī ibn al-Riqāʿ, a poet of the Umayyad period, describes the eyes:

وَسْنَانُ أَقْصَدَهُ النُّعَاسُ فَرَنَّقَتْ
فِي عَيْنِهِ سِنَةٌ وَلَيْسَ بِنَائِمِ

wasnānu aqṣadahu al-nuʿāsu fa-rannaqat / fī ʿaynihi sinatun wa-laysa bi-nāʾimi

Translation: “A drowsy one whom sleepiness has overcome: a haze of drowsiness settles in his eye, yet he is not asleep.”

The poet distinguishes drowsiness from sleeping. Nuʿās has affected the person and appears in his eyes, yet he is not called nāʾim, “asleep.”

This helps explain nuʿās, sinah and nawm. The verse belongs to the Umayyad period, however, and should not be presented as pre-Islamic evidence.

Lexical accounts also dispute naʿsān. Some accept it on the model of wasnān; others prefer nāʿis. This concerns descriptive forms rather than changing the basic meaning of nuʿās.

A Drowsy Camel and Everyday Usage

Al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī, a poet of the early Islamic period, says:

نَعُوسٌ إِذَا دَرَّتْ جَرُوزٌ إِذَا غَدَتْ
بُوَيْزِلُ عَامٍ أَوْ سَدِيسٌ كَبَازِلِ

naʿūsun idhā darrat jarūzun idhā ghadat / buwayzilu ʿāmin aw sadīsun ka-bāzili

Translation: “Drowsy when giving milk, a hearty eater when going out to graze; newly at the bāzil stage, or a sadīs that looks like a bāzil.”

Naʿūs describes a she-camel that closes her eyes or grows drowsy during milking. Buwayzil and sadīs in the latter part identify age and dental stages: buwayzil here has newly reached the bāzil stage; sadīs is one stage younger but is described as looking like a bāzil.

This shows that the root was not confined to people: it could describe a similar condition in animals.

Naʿūs should not therefore be defined as “giving abundant milk.” The poet’s camel is described as generous in milk, but naʿūs concerns drowsiness during milking. Milk production and drowsiness are related features of this animal’s description, rather than identical meanings.

This witness is also Islamic-era poetry. Early lexical evidence for the root is clear, but dating a particular verse before the Qur’an requires independent support.

Extension to Slackness and Sluggish Trade

Lexical accounts also record:

نَعَسَتِ السُّوقُ

naʿasati al-sūqu

Translation: “The market became sluggish.”

The market does not literally sleep. Reduced activity and trade are likened to drowsiness.

Some accounts also use al-naʿs for bodily slackness or weakness of judgment.

These uses can be connected with diminished activity. They do not establish that “the slackness of anything” was historically the original meaning and that drowsiness was merely one instance.

Drowsiness is central in the root’s clear, familiar uses. Applications to trade or weakness are semantic extensions.

3. First Qur’anic Use: Āl ʿImrān 3:154

ثُمَّ أَنْزَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ الْغَمِّ أَمَنَةً نُّعَاسًا يَغْشَىٰ طَائِفَةً مِّنْكُمْ

thumma anzala ʿalaykum min baʿdi al-ghammi amanatan nuʿāsan yaghshā ṭāʾifatan minkum

Translation: “Then, after distress, He sent down security upon you: drowsiness overtaking a group of you.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:154

This follows the account of fighting and distress affecting the Muslims after disruption and harm on the battlefield.

Āl ʿImrān 3:152 and Āl ʿImrān 3:153 describe disobedience, disagreement and distress. The later part of Āl ʿImrān 3:154 also mentions killing. Its opening describes amanah and nuʿās being sent down.

Nuʿās and Amanah

Amanatan nuʿāsan places two words together without making their meanings identical.

Amanah concerns safety and a sense of security; nuʿās concerns drowsiness and the beginning of sleep.

One established grammatical analysis takes nuʿāsan as an appositional substitute or explanatory expression for amanah. Drowsiness is thus presented as the particular form taken by the granted security.

Another valid analysis takes drowsiness as what was sent down and amanah as expressing its associated condition or purpose.

Both analyses establish a connection without making “security” the lexical meaning of nuʿās.

Nuʿās is drowsiness accompanied, in this particular setting, by security and calm.

Study of A M N

Why Drowsiness after Distress Matters

Distress, worry and perceived danger can keep a person agitated and alert. In such circumstances, becoming calm and able to feel sleepy can indicate reduced pressure and worry.

The verse describes drowsiness after distress, making its contextual connection with relief clear.

This should not become an absolute rule that everyone safe becomes sleepy, or that everyone sleepy is actually safe.

Severe tiredness can make someone sleepy even in danger. Someone safe may also be unable to sleep for various reasons.

The reassuring role of nuʿās here follows from the Qur’anic account and its particular setting, rather than a universal rule about human sleep.

“Overtaking a Group of You”: Different Conditions

The continuation matters:

وَطَائِفَةٌ قَدْ أَهَمَّتْهُمْ أَنْفُسُهُمْ يَظُنُّونَ بِاللَّهِ غَيْرَ الْحَقِّ ظَنَّ الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ

wa-ṭāʾifatun qad ahammathum anfusuhum yaẓunnūna bi-allāhi ghayra al-ḥaqqi ẓanna al-jāhiliyyati

Translation: “Another group was preoccupied with themselves, entertaining an untrue thought about The God, the thought of ignorance.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:154

The Qur’an explicitly says drowsiness overtook a group. It then describes another group as concerned about themselves and entertaining false thoughts.

Not everyone present responded in the same way.

This does not make every person who failed to grow sleepy necessarily faithless, cowardly or distrustful of The God. The second group is described through other features, rather than simply the absence of nuʿās.

The moral judgment follows from that full description, not from sleeping or remaining awake alone.

Drowsiness Being Sent Down

Anzala attributes this security and drowsiness to divine giving.

This does not make drowsiness a physical substance descending like rain. Qur’anic “sending down” can express divine granting or bestowal; how it occurs must be assessed in context.

The passage describes a real condition reaching a group of people without explaining its precise bodily mechanism.

4. Second Qur’anic Use: Al-Anfāl 8:11

إِذْ يُغَشِّيكُمُ النُّعَاسَ أَمَنَةً مِّنْهُ وَيُنَزِّلُ عَلَيْكُم مِّنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً لِّيُطَهِّرَكُم بِهِ وَيُذْهِبَ عَنكُمْ رِجْزَ الشَّيْطَانِ وَلِيَرْبِطَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِكُمْ وَيُثَبِّتَ بِهِ الْأَقْدَامَ

idh yughasshīkumu al-nuʿāsa amanatan minhu wa-yunazzilu ʿalaykum mina al-samāʾi māʾan li-yuṭahhirakum bihi wa-yudhhiba ʿankum rijza al-shayṭāni wa-li-yarbiṭa ʿalā qulūbikum wa-yuthabbita bihi al-aqdāma

Translation: “When He spread drowsiness over you as security from Him, and sent water down upon you from heaven to purify you with it, remove Satan’s defilement from you, strengthen your hearts and make your feet firm.”

Al-Anfāl 8:11

This occurs amid the Muslims’ encounter with the enemy, the promise of divine help and the difficulties of that event.

Al-Anfāl 8:10 recalls that help comes only from The God. Al-Anfāl 8:11 then describes several gifts and sources of strength: drowsiness, security, rain, purification, strengthened hearts and firm feet.

The Meaning of Yughasshīkumu al-Nuʿāsa

In the familiar Ḥafṣ reading, yughasshīkumu is a Form II verb belonging to GH SH Y and concerns covering or causing something to envelop someone.

Nuʿās is what is spread over the addressees in this construction.

The expression pictures drowsiness overtaking people, a condition they experience as affecting their attention and wakefulness.

The covering image comes from yughasshīkum, not N ʿ S itself. Nuʿās should therefore not be redefined as “a covering” or “something that covers a person.”

Study of GH SH W

Variant Readings and the Stable Meaning of Nuʿās

Three established reading constructions occur here:

يُغَشِّيكُمُ النُّعَاسَ

yughasshīkumu al-nuʿāsa

In this reading, The God spreads drowsiness over the addressees.

يُغْشِيكُمُ النُّعَاسَ

yughshīkumu al-nuʿāsa

In this reading, the verb is Form IV and is again attributed to The God; its general meaning is close to the first construction.

يَغْشَاكُمُ النُّعَاسُ

yaghshākumu al-nuʿāsu

Here nuʿās is the grammatical subject: drowsiness overtakes them.

The differences concern the form of the verb ghashiya and its grammatical relation to nuʿās. None changes nuʿās into fear, security or deep sleep.

Despite the grammatical variation, all describe drowsiness coming upon the addressees in that setting, connected with divine security.

Drowsiness, Rain and Strengthened Hearts

In Al-Anfāl, drowsiness appears alongside rain and its effects.

Not all effects attributed to the rain should be assigned to drowsiness. Purification, removal of Satan’s defilement and firm feet are connected grammatically with the descending water and divine provision.

Nuʿās belongs within the same series of gifts, but its role in the opening is expressed through amanatan minhu, “security from Him.”

This distinction matters. Nuʿās is presented as reassuring here, but its lexical meaning does not include purification, strengthening hearts or military victory.

The verse specifies neither the exact timing, duration nor depth of individuals’ sleepiness. Nuʿās cannot establish that everyone fell deeply asleep or slept for a particular period.

5. Related Roots

N ʿ S and N W M

Nawm is a general term for sleep, whether light or deep.

Nuʿās more characteristically denotes sleepiness, the heaviness of approaching sleep and dozing.

This is a difference of usual emphasis. Nawm can describe actual sleep, whereas nuʿās often describes approaching it or not yet being fully asleep.

There is no absolute boundary making nuʿās always full wakefulness and nawm always complete unawareness. Arabic lexical accounts also record overlap.

Nawm occurs in several Qur’anic settings, for example:

وَجَعَلْنَا نَوْمَكُمْ سُبَاتًا

wa-jaʿalnā nawmakum subātan

Translation: “And We made your sleep a means of rest and cessation.”

Al-Nabaʾ 78:9

Sleep is part of the ordering of human life here. Nuʿās in Āl ʿImrān and Al-Anfāl occurs in particular circumstances of distress and danger.

This contextual difference does not establish a categorical difference in every bodily feature of the states. It shows each word being used in its appropriate setting.

N ʿ S and W S N

Wasan and sinah are very close to nuʿās.

Al-Baqarah 2:255 says:

لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ

lā taʾkhudhuhu sinatun wa-lā nawmun

Translation: “Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him.”

Sinah and nawm stand together, both denied of The God.

Sinah ordinarily concerns the beginning of sleep and the heaviness of drowsiness. Nuʿās occupies the same semantic field.

Some lexical accounts propose degrees: sinah as the onset, nuʿās as heavy eyes and nawm as deep sleep. Other accounts differ in their order and precise definitions.

These explanations should not be turned into a fixed hierarchy governing all Arabic and every verse.

The point of Al-Baqarah 2:255 is clear: no drowsiness or sleep overtakes The God. The wording denies sleep and diminished wakefulness of Him.

This should not become a theory about a divine body or nervous system. Drowsiness and sleep are familiar features of created beings that the Qur’an denies of The God.

N ʿ S and R Q D

Ruqūd concerns sleeping or being in a state of sleep and rest.

The Qur’an says of the people of the cave:

وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ

wa-taḥsabuhum ayqāẓan wa-hum ruqūdun

Translation: “You would think them awake, while they are asleep.”

Al-Kahf 18:18

Ruqūd is contrasted with wakefulness here.

Nuʿās more directly emphasizes growing drowsy or approaching sleep. Someone may experience nuʿās while still counted awake; ruqūd in this verse describes being asleep.

This does not make ruqūd necessarily a very long or motionless sleep. The duration in the cave’s account comes from other verses, not from R Q D alone.

N ʿ S and GH F L

Ghaflah concerns inattention, unawareness or overlooking something.

Drowsiness may reduce attention, but that effect does not make nuʿās identical to ghaflah.

Someone fully awake may overlook an important truth. Someone may also grow naturally sleepy after prolonged effort and alertness without committing a moral failure.

Qur’anic nuʿās should therefore not be translated as heedlessness, weak faith or unawareness of truth.

Both passages under discussion connect nuʿās with divine security. Negative implications sometimes belonging to heedlessness should not be transferred into this root.

Study of GH F L

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For نَعَسَ (naʿasa), “became drowsy” or, in suitable settings, “dozed” is natural.

For نُعَاس (nuʿās), “drowsiness” is the best basic rendering. “Dozing” can fit where brief, light sleep is clear, but should not replace it in every setting.

For Āl ʿImrān 3:154:

أَمَنَةً نُّعَاسًا يَغْشَىٰ طَائِفَةً مِّنْكُمْ

amanatan nuʿāsan yaghshā ṭāʾifatan minkum

“Security: drowsiness overtaking a group of you.”

The full sentence can be made fluent while preserving the connection between drowsiness and security without treating them as lexically identical.

For Al-Anfāl 8:11:

إِذْ يُغَشِّيكُمُ النُّعَاسَ أَمَنَةً مِّنْهُ

idh yughasshīkumu al-nuʿāsa amanatan minhu

“When He spread drowsiness over you as security from Him.”

“Spread over you” renders yughasshīkum, not nuʿās itself.

For Forqan’s word boxes, two levels are appropriate:

Brief root meaning: drowsiness, dozing and the beginning of sleep.

Meaning in Āl ʿImrān 3:154: drowsiness overtaking a group of believers after distress, accompanied in context by divine security.

Meaning in Al-Anfāl 8:11: drowsiness that The God caused to overtake the believers during their encounter with the enemy, accompanied by security from Him.

“Calm,” “security” and “reassurance of the heart” should not replace nuʿās in the direct translation. They follow from amanah and the surrounding context.

“Deep sleep” is also unsuitable: neither the root nor the constructions in these verses specifies that degree of sleep.

7. Conclusion

N ʿ S concerns drowsiness and the onset of sleep. Forms such as nuʿās, naʿsah, nāʿis and naʿūs describe this condition in humans and sometimes animals. Extended uses apply the image of slackness or reduced activity to sluggish trade and weakness.

Nuʿās shares a semantic field with nawm and wasan without being perfectly identical to them. Its usual emphasis is drowsiness and light sleep, although Arabic accounts do not always draw the same boundaries.

Only nuʿās occurs in the Qur’an, in two verses. Both connect it with amanah, security. Āl ʿImrān 3:154 describes a group’s drowsiness after distress; Al-Anfāl 8:11 includes it among gifts reaching believers in a difficult encounter with the enemy.

In these settings, drowsiness is associated with calm and safety, rather than carrying an assumed implication of slackness or heedlessness. This follows from these particular uses, rather than making “security” or “reassurance of the heart” the independent meaning of nuʿās.

Final definition: N ʿ S denotes becoming drowsy and the beginning or light condition of sleep, which may involve heavy eyes, reduced attention and dozing. Qur’anic nuʿās describes drowsiness accompanied by divine security and calm in two difficult situations. “Drowsiness” is therefore Forqan’s appropriate basic rendering; its connection with security belongs in the explanation of each verse’s context.
