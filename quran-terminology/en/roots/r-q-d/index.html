Sleeping, being asleep, and a resting place

Brief root meaning: Sleeping and being asleep; in some linguistic uses, settling or subsiding. مَرْقَد also denotes a place for sleeping or resting.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic triliteral root ر ق د is chiefly associated with sleep and sleeping. رَقَدَ means “he slept,” رُقاد means sleep, راقد means asleep, and مَرْقَد denotes a sleeping or resting place.

This root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, but both occurrences belong to scenes beyond ordinary sleep: the companions of the cave who remained there for many years, and people raised from their graves at the resurrection.

The first occurrence distinguishes an appearance of wakefulness from actually being asleep. In the second, the language of sleep and awakening describes being raised after death.

Examined forms and Qur’anic derivatives

Two forms of this root occur in the Qur’an:

  • رُقُودٌ: The plural of راقد, meaning sleepers; Al-Kahf 18:18.
  • مَرْقَدِنَا: مَرْقَد with the pronoun نا, meaning our sleeping place or our resting place; Yā Sīn 36:52.

Other Arabic forms include رَقَدَ, يَرْقُدُ, رُقاد, رَقْدَة, راقد, مَراقِد, أَرْقَدَ, and تَراقَدَ, but these particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

مَراقِد is the plural of مَرْقَد and lexically means sleeping or resting places.

An important morphological distinction is that رُقُود can be either a verbal noun of رَقَدَ or the plural of راقد. In Al-Kahf 18:18, the sentence structure and reference to a group of people indicate its descriptive plural use.

2. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Sleep: the principal meaning

The clear central meaning of ر ق د in Arabic is sleeping.

The verb and its verbal nouns are expressed as follows:

رَقَدَ يَرْقُدُ رَقْدًا وَرُقُودًا وَرُقادًا

raqada yarqudu raqdan wa-ruqūdan wa-ruqādan

That is, “he slept”; رَقْد, رُقُود, and رُقاد are its verbal nouns.

Within this family, الرَّقْدَة denotes one occasion of sleeping. There is therefore a structural distinction between رُقاد, the state of sleep, and رَقْدَة, a single episode of sleep, although both may naturally be translated as sleep.

راقد is someone asleep, and its plural may be رُقُود or رُقَّد.

A poetic witness whose pre-Qur’anic dating is uncertain reads:

وَلَقَدْ رَقَيْتَ كِلَابَ أَهْلِكَ بِالرُّقَى
حَتَّى تَرَكْتَ عَقُورَهُنَّ رَقُودًا

wa-laqad raqayta kilāba ahlika bi-al-ruqā / ḥattā tarakta ʿaqūrahunna raqūdan

Translation: “You recited charms over your household’s dogs, until you left even their biting one fast asleep.”

Here رَقُودًا, with fatḥah on the rāʾ, describes the biting dog as given to sleep. This differs from Qur’anic رُقُودٌ, with ḍammah, the plural of راقد. The dog’s sleeping conveys a subsiding of activity, but the word itself does not mean being tamed, becoming harmless, or losing the ability to bite.

This distinction matters: the consequence of sleeping must not replace the meaning of the root itself.

Does ruqūd mean sleep at night and ruqād sleep by day?

Some early lexical accounts propose a distinction between the two verbal nouns: رُقُود for sleep at night and رُقاد for sleep during the day.

This restriction was also disputed in early Arabic linguistic discussion: both words can describe sleep by night or day.

Thus, the form رُقُود alone does not establish that sleeping occurred at night. Nor does the word itself impose this restriction in Al-Kahf 18:18.

A distinction recorded in lexical discussion is insufficient to establish a general rule, especially where other linguistic evidence does not support that restriction.

Extension to stillness and reduced activity

Arabic also records expressions such as رَقَدَ الحَرُّ, meaning that the heat subsided, and رَقَدَتِ السُّوقُ, meaning that the market became sluggish.

In these uses, human sleep supplies an image for reduced activity or a subsiding condition.

The market does not literally sleep; its trade and vitality decline. Heat is not a sleeping creature either; its intensity or effect diminishes.

These are intelligible semantic extensions, but they do not establish that the original meaning of ر ق د was simply “cessation of movement.”

Replacing sleep with such a broad definition would erase distinctions between this root and words for stagnation or stillness.

Staying, rapid movement, and distinct uses

أَرْقَدَ بِالْمَكَانِ is also recorded for staying in a place. This can be considered alongside remaining and settling, but does not make every stay a kind of sleep.

By contrast, ارْقَدَّ and its imperfect يَرْقَدُّ are used in some witnesses for moving rapidly.

A poem attributed to al-ʿAjjāj describes a wild bull’s rapid movement:

فَظَلَّ يَرْقَدُّ مِنَ النَّشَاطِ
كَالْبَرْبَرِيِّ لَجَّ فِي انْخِرَاطِ

fa-ẓalla yarqaddu mina al-nashāṭi / ka-al-barbariyyi lajja fī inkhirāṭi

Translation: “It went on bounding with energy, like a Barbary horse persisting in its headlong run.”

This poetry belongs to the period after the Qur’an’s revelation, and the first half-line is transmitted in differing forms.

يَرْقَدُّ, with a doubled dāl, must not be confused with يَرْقُدُ, “he sleeps.” Its inclusion in the same lexical entry does not by itself establish a shared historical derivation; in any case, it offers no basis for translating Qur’anic رُقُود as rapid movement.

راقود is also recorded as the name of a large vessel, considered by some lexicographers an Arabicized loanword. Its shared letters with ر ق د do not establish that the vessel’s name derives from sleeping.

Sleeping thus remains the clear central meaning relevant to understanding the Qur’an, although dictionary entries record other uses as well.

3. First Qur’anic occurrence: ruqūd in the cave narrative

Al-Kahf 18:18

Verse text:

وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ ۚ وَنُقَلِّبُهُمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ ۖ وَكَلْبُهُمْ بَاسِطٌ ذِرَاعَيْهِ بِالْوَصِيدِ ۚ لَوِ اطَّلَعْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ لَوَلَّيْتَ مِنْهُمْ فِرَارًا وَلَمُلِئْتَ مِنْهُمْ رُعْبًا

wa-taḥsabuhum ayqāẓan wa-hum ruqūdun wa-nuqallibuhum dhāta al-yamīni wa-dhāta al-shimāli wa-kalbuhum bāsiṭun dhirāʿayhi bi-al-waṣīdi lawi iṭṭalaʿta ʿalayhim la-wallayta minhum firāran wa-la-muliʾta minhum ruʿban

Al-Kahf 18:18

Translation:

“You would think them awake, while they are asleep; We turn them to the right and to the left, and their dog stretches its forelegs at the entrance. Had you looked upon them, you would have fled from them and been filled with fear of them.”

Al-Kahf 18:18

The contrast between ayqāẓ and ruqūd

The verse opens by contrasting two states:

أَيْقَاظًا — awake people

رُقُودٌ — sleepers

وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ describes the observer’s impression: someone seeing them might think them awake, although their actual state is different.

This contrast shows that رقود here describes sleep, rather than death or absolute immobility.

Their appearance may explain that impression. Some interpretations mention open eyes, but the verse itself specifies neither open eyes nor how their bodily organs were functioning.

What it directly states is the difference between an observer’s impression and their actual condition.

“They are asleep” is therefore more precise than renderings that extract a particular condition, such as sleeping with open eyes or medical unconsciousness, from the word itself.

Sleep and bodily movement

The verse continues:

وَنُقَلِّبُهُمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ

wa-nuqallibuhum dhāta al-yamīni wa-dhāta al-shimāli

Al-Kahf 18:18

That is, they are turned to the right and to the left.

This matters for understanding رقود: sleep in this passage does not require the body to remain entirely motionless throughout.

The verse attributes their turning to The God; it should not be reduced to ordinary movements during sleep without evidence. Nor does the phrase specify how often they were turned, the intervals, or the physical mechanism involved.

رقود thus describes their state, rather than reporting the complete cessation of bodily movement.

Prolonged sleep: a feature of the narrative, not the root

Al-Kahf 18:11 states that The God cast a covering over their ears in the cave for a number of years. Al-Kahf 18:25 also discusses the duration of their stay.

These verses concern the cave companions’ extraordinary, prolonged condition.

Length of time, however, is not contained in رقود itself. In Arabic, someone sleeping briefly can also be called راقد.

In Al-Kahf 18:18, رقود conveys being asleep; the long duration and extraordinary character emerge from the surrounding narrative.

Being raised from sleep

Al-Kahf 18:12 and Al-Kahf 18:19 use بَعَثَ of the cave companions.

In Al-Kahf 18:19, after being raised, they question one another about how long they stayed. This context connects بعث with coming out of sleep and resuming conscious activity.

Yet بعث in the Qur’an is not limited to awakening sleepers; it also describes raising people after death.

This distinction connects the narrative with the second occurrence of ر ق د, in Yā Sīn.

4. Second Qur’anic occurrence: marqad at the resurrection

Yā Sīn 36:52

Verse text:

قَالُوا يَا وَيْلَنَا مَنْ بَعَثَنَا مِنْ مَرْقَدِنَا ۜ ۗ هَٰذَا مَا وَعَدَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَصَدَقَ الْمُرْسَلُونَ

qālū yā-waylanā man baʿathanā min marqadinā hādhā mā waʿada al-raḥmānu wa-ṣadaqa al-mursalūna

Yā Sīn 36:52

Translation:

“They say: Woe to us! Who has raised us from our sleeping place? This is what the Entirely Merciful promised, and the messengers spoke the truth.”

Yā Sīn 36:52

The meaning of marqad

مَرْقَد follows the pattern مَفْعَل and is ordinarily a place noun: a place where someone sleeps or rests.

A verbal-noun interpretation is also recorded, whereby مَرْقَد refers to sleeping itself or the state of sleep.

The lexical possibilities of مِنْ مَرْقَدِنَا therefore include “from our sleeping place” and “from our sleep.” Here, however, the phrase is linked to بَعَثَنَا and expresses emergence from a previous state.

The preceding verse must also be considered to identify the intended reference.

Marqad and the graves

Yā Sīn 36:51 reads:

وَنُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ فَإِذَا هُمْ مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَنْسِلُونَ

wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-idhā hum mina al-ajdāthi ilā rabbihim yansilūna

Yā Sīn 36:51

“And the trumpet is blown; suddenly they hasten from the graves toward their Lord.”

Yā Sīn 36:51

الأجداث denotes graves. When people speak of مرقدنا in the next verse, the context therefore makes clear that they are being raised after death.

Yet أجداث and مرقد are not lexically identical.

أجداث names graves, whereas مرقد names a sleeping or resting place. Yā Sīn 36:51 identifies the place of emergence as graves; Yā Sīn 36:52 presents the same scene through the raised people’s expression “sleeping place.”

In this context, مرقد can thus refer to where the dead have lain, without restricting its lexical meaning to a grave in every use.

This distinction is worth preserving in translation. Rendering مرقدنا directly as “our grave” conveys the contextual referent but loses the word’s imagery of sleep and awakening.

“Our sleeping place” preserves that image, while the previous verse establishes the resurrection setting.

Sleep and death: resemblance without identity

The Qur’an connects sleep and death without presenting them as identical.

Al-Zumar 39:42 speaks of taking souls at death and during sleep. The same verse distinguishes those for whom death has been decreed from those released until an appointed time.

The Qur’an thus establishes a relationship between sleep and death for examination, without simply calling death ordinary sleep.

In Al-Kahf, the cave companions are asleep and then raised. In Yā Sīn, people are raised from graves after death and use the expression مرقدنا.

Both scenes involve emergence from a previous state and the beginning of a new stage. The shared verb بعث and imagery of sleep do not erase the actual distinction between sleep and death.

مرقدنا in Yā Sīn 36:52 can convey lying at rest and subsequently being raised, but does not by itself determine human life or awareness throughout the interval between death and resurrection.

Other verses, including Āl ʿImrān 3:169, Ghāfir 40:46, and Al-Muʾminūn 23:100, also discuss the state after death. Understanding that subject requires considering these passages together, rather than drawing a conclusion from one word.

مرقدنا alone therefore cannot establish that all the dead remain in a state exactly like natural sleep, wholly without awareness, throughout the interval before resurrection.

Conversely, the sleeping-place image should not be removed from the verse and replaced solely with a theological term for the world after death.

Who is speaking?

The first part begins with قَالُوا and reports the words of those raised.

The second part, هَٰذَا مَا وَعَدَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَصَدَقَ الْمُرْسَلُونَ, does not explicitly identify a new speaker.

Grammatically and contextually, this can be understood either as their continued speech or as another group’s reply. Neither possibility alters the lexical meaning of مرقدنا.

For this root study, the main point is that مرقدنا occurs in speech expressing astonishment and fear at being raised, rather than in an independent explanation of the structure of the afterlife.

5. Comparison with related roots

R Q D (ruqād) and N W M (nawm)

Both families refer to sleep and can overlap in many uses.

نوم occurs in the Qur’an for ordinary human sleep, sleep as part of life, and the relationship between sleep and death.

For example, Al-Nabaʾ 78:9 associates human sleep with سُباتًا, while Al-Baqarah 2:255 denies both سِنَة and نَوْم of The God.

رقود likewise describes being asleep; in the root’s two Qur’anic occurrences, one form describes sleeping people and the other names a resting place.

This evidence does not establish that رقود always means deeper, longer, or heavier sleep than نوم. The prolonged sleep of the cave companions comes from context, not a categorical distinction between these roots.

R Q D (ruqād) and S B T (sabt)

Al-Nabaʾ 78:9 reads:

وَجَعَلْنَا نَوْمَكُمْ سُبَاتًا

wa-jaʿalnā nawmakum subātan

Al-Nabaʾ 78:9

“We made your sleep a rest.”

سباتا in this verse relates to rest and cessation of activity.

رقود, however, primarily means sleeping. A cessation or reduction of activity may accompany sleep, but does not exhaust its meaning.

Translating رقود simply as stillness or cessation would obscure its difference from other states that occur without sleep.

R Q D (ruqād) and Y Q Ẓ (yaqaẓ)

ي ق ظ belongs to the field of wakefulness.

In Al-Kahf 18:18, أيقاظا stands directly opposite رقود.

This contrast is among the clearest indications of the Qur’anic meaning of رقود. An observer can think people awake from their appearance while they are actually asleep.

Wakefulness and sleep are distinct states here, but bodily appearance is not presented as always sufficient to distinguish them.

The observer’s impression that they are awake does not establish that they were actually seeing, hearing, or thinking.

R Q D (ruqād) and R K D (rukūd)

Despite similarities in spelling and sound, ر ق د and ر ك د are different roots.

ر ق د primarily means sleeping, while ر ك د concerns stillness and cessation of movement.

In Al-Shūrā 42:33, رَوَاكِدَ describes ships that stop moving when the wind becomes still.

A stationary ship is not asleep. A sleeping person is راقد, but this description alone does not establish complete bodily immobility.

Sleep and stillness may therefore coincide, but they are not identical concepts.

R Q D (ruqād) and B ʿ TH (baʿth)

بعث does not share the root of رقد and has its own meaning, yet it is importantly related to both Qur’anic occurrences of ر ق د.

The cave companions are raised after sleeping; in Yā Sīn 36:52, people speak of being raised from their مرقد.

This relationship shows that the Qur’an can use بعث for raising people from sleep as well as after death.

Nevertheless, بعث itself does not mean sleeping. Its presence in both scenes does not make the cave companions’ sleep and the state of the dead identical in every respect.

6. Interpretive challenges and translation proposals for Forqan

The main challenge is turning a linguistic image into a definitive theory of sleep, death, or awareness.

In Al-Kahf 18:18, رُقُود clearly denotes being asleep. The verse also discusses their appearance and the turning of their bodies, but this word alone cannot specify brain activity, a sleep stage, or a medical condition.

In Yā Sīn 36:52, مَرْقَد lexically denotes a sleeping or resting place. The resurrection context connects it with graves, but the word alone does not define the entire human condition after death.

Verbal nouns and place nouns must also remain distinct. رُقُود can be a verbal noun for sleeping, but in Al-Kahf 18:18 it describes a plural group of sleepers. مَرْقَد ordinarily functions as a place noun, although a verbal-noun interpretation is also recorded.

Proposed translations of the Qur’anic forms

رُقُودٌ — Al-Kahf 18:18

Suitable renderings: “they are asleep,” “sleepers,” or “sleeping.”

In the complete sentence, “while they are asleep” is natural and precise.

“Unconscious,” “dead,” “completely motionless,” or “in deep sleep” add information beyond the word itself unless independently supported.

مَرْقَدِنَا — Yā Sīn 36:52

Suitable renderings: “our sleeping place,” “our resting place,” or “our place of sleep.”

Within the verse, “Who has raised us from our sleeping place?” is fluent and preserves the word’s central image.

“Our grave” can express the contextual referent, but “our sleeping place” has the advantage of retaining the linguistic image in the base translation.

An explanatory note can clarify that the preceding context describes emergence from graves.

Proposed Forqan root box

Root: R Q D

Brief root meaning: Sleeping; being asleep; in its place noun, a sleeping or resting place.

For رُقُودٌ in Al-Kahf 18:18, an appropriate contextual meaning is “asleep, despite being thought awake.”

For مَرْقَدِنَا in Yā Sīn 36:52, an appropriate contextual meaning is “our sleeping or resting place, in the context of being raised from graves.”

7. Conclusion and final definition

In its clear primary sense, ر ق د denotes sleeping and the state of sleep. Within this family, راقد describes a sleeper, رُقُود can denote sleep or a group of sleepers, and مَرْقَد is a sleeping or resting place.

Other recorded uses include subsiding heat, a sluggish market, or staying somewhere, but these should not displace sleeping from the root’s central definition. Special forms such as ارقدّ, associated with rapid movement, must also be distinguished from ordinary رقد.

The two Qur’anic occurrences offer related images:

In Al-Kahf 18:18, people are actually asleep although they may appear awake. In Yā Sīn 36:52, people raised after death refer to their former place as مرقدنا.

Their relationship illustrates how sleep and awakening can describe being raised, without equating sleep and death in nature or condition.

Final definition of ر ق د:

In its primary sense, ر ق د means sleeping and being asleep. Its derivatives can express a sleeping person, sleep itself, or a resting place. Extended uses sometimes describe subsiding activity or settling, but should not replace the principal meaning of sleep.

In the Qur’an, رُقُود describes sleepers and مَرْقَد denotes a resting place. The first is clarified by the contrast between appearance and actual sleep in the cave narrative; the second refers contextually to the place from which the dead arise at resurrection.

Understanding and translating this root requires preserving the clear sense of sleep and resting, without extracting from it a scientific account of sleep, a full account of life after death, or the state of awareness of all the dead.
