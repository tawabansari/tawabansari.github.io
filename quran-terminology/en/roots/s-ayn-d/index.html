The Arabic root S ʿ D concerns blessedness, well-being, success, and a favorable condition. Saʿd denotes good fortune or an auspicious outcome; saʿādah contrasts with shaqāwah. Saʿīd describes someone enjoying blessedness and a favorable outcome.

In contemporary Persian, saʿādat can refer to happiness, satisfaction with life, prosperity, or well-being. These senses belong to its semantic field but do not exhaust it. Someone may be joyful for a moment yet face a poor outcome, or endure hardship while a good outcome awaits them.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both in Hūd. Saʿīd first contrasts with shaqiyy; suʿidū then describes those whose place in the hereafter is the Garden.

Understanding the Qur’anic family therefore requires distinguishing passing joy, worldly good fortune and success, enjoyment of good, and a person’s ultimate blessedness.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Saʿd: good fortune and a favorable outcome

Classical Arabic explains al-saʿd through al-yumn: good fortune, auspiciousness, and a favorable outcome. Its opposite, al-naḥs, denotes misfortune or an unfavorable outcome.

Yawmu saʿdin describes a day considered auspicious, just as yawmu naḥsin describes an ill-omened or unfortunate day.

Saʿd here need not describe an inward feeling of joy. Its subject can be a time, situation, or event considered favorable and accompanied by good.

Saʿida al-rajulu means that a man became fortunate or enjoyed well-being. The adjective saʿīd derives from this verb and contrasts with shaqiyy.

Two related fields appear: favorable circumstances or outcomes, and the well-being of someone enjoying good and a desirable outcome.

They are related but not identical. Human blessedness cannot always be reduced to random luck.

Evidence attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah al-Yashkurī includes:

لَا يَرْتَجِي لِلْمَالِ يُنْفِقُهُ
سَعْدُ النُّجُومِ إِلَيْهِ كَالنَّحْسِ

lā yartajī li-al-māli yunfiquhu / saʿdu al-nujūmi ilayhi ka-al-naḥsi

“He does not fear spending his wealth; auspicious and inauspicious stars are alike to him.”

The verse praises someone whose generosity does not depend on supposedly favorable or unfavorable times.

Saʿdu al-nujūm here refers to a time or celestial configuration considered favorable under beliefs about the stars. Al-naḥs stands opposite it.

The phrase treats the contrast between good and bad fortune as familiar, while praising the man for giving equally under either condition.

It supports the early sense of saʿd as good, auspiciousness, and favorable fortune, opposed to naḥs.

The poem is attributed to Al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah and transmitted in collections of early Arabic poetry. The poet’s pre-Islamic classification does not by itself date every individual verse precisely. This is evidence of early usage, not proof of when the word first arose.

Saʿd and beliefs about the stars

Arabic records names such as Saʿd al-Dhābiḥ, Saʿd Bulaʿ, Saʿd al-Suʿūd, and Saʿd al-Akhbiyah for star groups or recognized celestial stations.

Saʿd in these names is associated with the language of auspiciousness and good fortune; the names alone do not establish the precise historical reason for each naming.

These names do not prove that stars determine human happiness or misery. Language can preserve expressions rooted in social beliefs without establishing those beliefs as facts.

Nor do they establish that Qur’anic blessedness derives from belief in stellar control of destiny.

The Qur’anic occurrences concern final judgment and human outcomes, not particular configurations of stars.

Saʿādah: blessedness opposed to wretchedness

Saʿādah is a recognized verbal noun in this family, opposed to shaqāwah.

It can describe well-being and a favorable condition beyond momentary pleasure or advantage. The word alone does not make every instance a statement about a person’s final outcome.

Someone may rejoice over acquiring money that later brings corruption or harm. The immediate joy is real, but it does not prove ultimate blessedness.

Conversely, someone may endure difficulty to do what is right, with that conduct eventually bringing good and success.

These examples distinguish joy from well-being. They should not be projected backward as a claim that every pre-Islamic use expressed precisely this philosophical distinction.

The attested meaning concerns well-being and enjoyment of good; context determines its scope.

2. Other branches of S ʿ D

Sāʿada: helping

Sāʿada is also an established Arabic verb for helping or assisting.

Musāʿadah means assistance or cooperation; a musāʿid is a helper.

Lexical explanations associate helping with sāʿid, the forearm between elbow and wrist: people work with their arms and, in cooperation, add their strength to another’s.

This illustrates a connection between help and practical ability.

Without historical evidence, however, it should not be presented as the certain origin of every word in the family.

Helping is not identical to being blessed. It may enable good and success, but sāʿada directly denotes assistance.

Isʿād and saʿdayka

Isʿād can denote helping and providing assistance.

Some early uses specifically describe joining a bereaved person in weeping or lamentation. A woman joining another in mourning could be said to perform isʿād for her.

Isʿād therefore does not always mean making someone happy. In mourning, it means accompanying or assisting them in grief.

The expression labbayka wa-saʿdayka is also established. Saʿdayka is explained through repeated assistance and response: sustained readiness to support and comply.

Shared root letters do not justify translating every use of isʿād, musāʿadah, and saʿādah as making someone happy.

Sāʿid and saʿdān

Sāʿid names the forearm between elbow and wrist, involved in grasping, lifting, and practical work.

Saʿdān names a thorny plant described as particularly good camel forage.

An Arabic proverb states:

مَرْعًى وَلَا كَالسَّعْدَانِ

marʿan wa-lā ka-al-saʿdāni

“Pasture, yes—but not like saʿdān.”

It compares something good with something better.

The plant’s placement in this lexical family does not establish that every S ʿ D word was named after happiness or assistance.

The plant, the body part, helping, and blessedness have distinct established uses. Shared letters alone do not settle all their historical relationships.

For Qur’anic study, blessedness and a favorable outcome are central, since both occurrences belong to that field.

3. Qur’anic forms and occurrences

S ʿ D occurs twice, in two verses of Hūd.

سَعِيدٌ — Hūd 11:105

An adjective on the faʿīl pattern, from saʿida: blessed or enjoying a favorable outcome.

سُعِدُوا — Hūd 11:108

A masculine plural past verb, passive in the reading of Ḥafṣ: they were made blessed or granted blessedness.

Other recognized readings have the active saʿidū, with fatḥah on the sīn: they became blessed.

Forms such as saʿādah, saʿd, suʿadāʾ, masʿūd, asʿada, musāʿadah, and sāʿid are established in Arabic but do not occur in those forms in the Qur’an.

Both Qur’anic occurrences share one context. People on the final day are first described as shaqiyy and saʿīd, after which the outcome of each group is explained.

General Arabic can apply saʿd to days, stars, fortune, or worldly success. These two Qur’anic occurrences directly concern human outcomes in the hereafter.

4. Saʿīd: blessed, opposed to shaqiyy

Hūd 11:105 states:

يَوْمَ يَأْتِ لَا تَكَلَّمُ نَفْسٌ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ فَمِنْهُمْ شَقِيٌّ وَسَعِيدٌ

yawma yaʾti lā takallamu nafsun illā bi-idhnihi fa-minhum shaqiyyun wa-saʿīdun

Hūd 11:105

“On the day it comes, no soul speaks except by His permission; among them are the wretched and the blessed.”

The verse sets shaqiyy and saʿīd opposite each other.

That contrast helps explain both words.

Shaqiyy belongs to SH Q W and concerns wretchedness, suffering, and an unfavorable outcome.

Saʿīd, by contrast, describes someone enjoying blessedness and a favorable outcome.

The subject is the final day. Neither condition should therefore be reduced to worldly finances, health, comfort, or passing feelings.

Does saʿīd mean a joyful person?

Persian sometimes renders saʿīd as happy. That may fit some general uses, but it is not the best rendering in Hūd 11:105.

The verse concerns a person’s condition and outcome at judgment, not momentary joy.

Someone may be very cheerful in this life without that proving ultimate blessedness.

Conversely, illness, poverty, or oppression do not by themselves establish a person’s wretchedness at final judgment.

“Blessed” therefore fits this context more closely than “joyful.”

Are these descriptions fixed and unchangeable from the outset?

Fa-minhum shaqiyyun wa-saʿīdun means that among them are the wretched and the blessed.

The sentence alone does not establish that people were divided from creation into unchangeable groups independently of their deeds and choices.

It describes their condition on the day in question without fully explaining how they reached it.

Other verses must also be considered when relating human outcomes to conduct.

Hūd 11:111, following this discussion, states that the Lord fully repays their deeds and knows what they do.

Hūd 11:112 commands steadfastness and avoidance of transgression.

The broader context thus does not separate conduct and responsibility from judgment and outcome.

This does not mean that S ʿ D alone explains divine will, human choice, and accountability. Its lexical meaning is blessedness; those wider questions require a broader range of verses.

5. Suʿidū: being granted blessedness and divine bounty

Hūd 11:108 states:

وَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ سُعِدُوا فَفِي الْجَنَّةِ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا مَا دَامَتِ السَّمَاوَاتُ وَالْأَرْضُ إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ رَبُّكَ عَطَاءً غَيْرَ مَجْذُوذٍ

wa-ammā alladhīna suʿidū fa-fī al-jannati khālidīna fīhā mā dāmati al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍu illā mā shāʾa rabbuka ʿaṭāʾan ghayra majdhūdhin

Hūd 11:108

“As for those made blessed, they are in the Garden, abiding there as long as the heavens and earth endure, except as your Lord wills—a gift without interruption.”

The verse describes the blessed group’s outcome.

In the earlier verse, Hūd 11:105, saʿīd was an adjective; here suʿidū is a verb.

That grammatical difference matters.

The difference between saʿīd and suʿidū

Saʿīd describes someone who possesses blessedness and a favorable outcome.

Suʿidū in the reading of Ḥafṣ is passive and describes their being granted blessedness.

Arabic saʿida means to become blessed. Asʿadahu, and the less common transitive saʿadahu, mean to make someone blessed or bring them well-being. The passive suʿidū has been explained through the latter, although grammatical accounts of this usage differ.

Suʿidū can therefore be understood as “they were made blessed.”

The passive does not explain every stage or cause of that blessedness, and it does not name the agent within the verb itself.

The context describes divine bounty and their place in the Garden, connecting their blessedness with divine giving.

The readings suʿidū and saʿidū

Ḥafṣ reads suʿidū, with ḍammah on the sīn, as passive.

Some other recognized readings have saʿidū, with fatḥah on the sīn, as active.

The latter naturally means:

“As for those who became blessed, they are in the Garden.”

The former more explicitly presents blessedness as something granted to them.

This grammatical difference alone does not establish two opposing doctrines of human choice.

A passive verb does not prove that conduct has no role in the outcome. An active verb likewise does not mean independence from divine mercy and bounty.

Both readings occur in a context that includes divine judgment, human conduct, and the Lord’s gift.

For a base translation following Ḥafṣ, “were made blessed” more closely preserves the passive structure. A natural rendering such as “became blessed” can convey the resulting condition, but its simplification of the passive should be explained; an alternate reading does not change the grammar of Ḥafṣ’s reading.

Does blessedness mean the Garden?

Hūd 11:108 places the blessed in the Garden.

That association does not make the Garden the lexical meaning of saʿādah.

Suʿidū describes being granted blessedness, while fa-fī al-jannati identifies their location.

The two expressions have distinct roles: one describes the people’s condition, and the other specifies their place.

The Garden here is the setting and manifestation of their ultimate blessedness, not a lexical equivalent of S ʿ D.

Uninterrupted bounty

The verse ends:

عَطَاءً غَيْرَ مَجْذُوذٍ

ʿaṭāʾan ghayra majdhūdhin

Hūd 11:108

“A gift without interruption.”

ʿAṭāʾ denotes a gift; majdhūdh belongs to J DH DH and concerns being cut off.

Continuity of divine bounty follows from this phrase, not from suʿidū itself.

The same verse contains illā mā shāʾa rabbuka, which requires separate consideration in studying the Garden’s duration and divine will.

It cannot simply be omitted from translation, nor can blessedness alone settle the duration and character of every condition in the hereafter.

For root study, the conclusion is clear: suʿidū describes receiving blessedness, while the rest of the verse explains its setting and accompanying bounty.

6. Comparison with related roots

S ʿ D and SH Q W: blessedness and wretchedness

The clearest Qur’anic contrast for S ʿ D is SH Q W.

Saʿīd stands opposite shaqiyy in Hūd 11:105; the following verses explain each group’s outcome.

In this context, saʿādah concerns a favorable outcome, while shaqāwah concerns an unfavorable one.

This does not make every suffering person in this world shaqiyy or every comfortable, prosperous person saʿīd.

Worldly suffering and comfort can arise from many circumstances. These two descriptions concern final judgment.

S ʿ D and F L Ḥ: blessedness and success

Qur’anic falāḥ concerns success and flourishing.

It clearly overlaps with saʿādah: genuine success entails good and a favorable outcome.

Falāḥ often emphasizes attaining the desired result, whereas saʿīd can describe a person’s blessed condition or outcome.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary. The roots do not establish that falāḥ always precedes saʿādah or that saʿādah is a later stage.

S ʿ D and F W Z: blessedness and attaining success

Qur’anic fawz concerns attaining what is desired, succeeding, and escaping harm.

Āl ʿImrān 3:185 describes being removed from the Fire and admitted to the Garden with fa-qad fāza: one has succeeded.

It is related in meaning to Hūd 11:108, since both concern a favorable outcome in the hereafter.

Fawz highlights achieving success, while saʿādah can describe the condition of blessedness.

They may receive similar translations in some contexts without losing all distinctions of usage.

S ʿ D and F R Ḥ: blessedness and joy

Faraḥ concerns joy and rejoicing.

Qur’anic joy does not always carry the same evaluation. Rejoicing in divine mercy and bounty differs from the condemned joy of arrogance and conceit.

The Qur’anic occurrences of S ʿ D concern blessedness and a favorable outcome.

Not every joy is blessedness, and not every pain or sorrow is wretchedness.

Rejoicing over successful oppression does not make someone ethically blessed. Grief while resisting injustice does not establish ultimate wretchedness.

This distinction matters in Hūd 11:105: “blessed” is more appropriate than “joyful.”

S ʿ D and N ʿ M: blessedness and enjoyment of bounty

Qur’anic N ʿ M concerns bounty, comfort, and favorable living conditions.

Bounty may be material or immaterial: provision, security, ability, guidance, and other gifts.

Possessing such gifts does not by itself establish ultimate blessedness.

A gift may become an occasion for gratitude, improvement, and good, or it may be misused to harm oneself and others.

Bounty and blessedness are related, but not fully synonymous.

S ʿ D and Y M N: auspiciousness and good fortune

Alongside other Y M N uses, yumn denotes auspiciousness and good fortune.

Lexical explanations frequently use it to explain saʿd.

That closeness shows how saʿd can describe a favorable outcome or an auspicious circumstance.

Yumn in this sense should not be equated with every use of yamīn, such as a right hand, an oath, or the right side.

Nor does their connection make Qur’anic blessedness depend on the right direction, auspicious stars, or good-luck rituals.

The Qur’anic occurrences place human outcomes within judgment, conduct, and divine bounty.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the Qur’anic derivatives, the primary Persian equivalent is nīk-bakhtī, or blessedness.

Khushbakhtī, happiness or good fortune, is also familiar Persian, but can suggest prosperity, personal satisfaction, or favorable worldly conditions.

In discussion of the final outcome, nīk-bakhtī conveys a broader condition and avoids reducing the word to an emotion.

سَعِيدٌ — Hūd 11:105

Suggested rendering: “blessed.”

Within the complete phrase:

“Among them are the wretched and the blessed.”

سُعِدُوا — Hūd 11:108

Suggested rendering reflecting the passive of Ḥafṣ: “were made blessed.”

Natural Persian may also express the resulting condition as “became blessed.”

Explain the grammatical difference without forcing the verse into a predetermined theological theory.

Other forms in the family, together with a reminder of the Qur’anic adjective saʿīd, can be rendered as follows:

Saʿd: auspiciousness, good fortune, or a favorable outcome, according to context.

Saʿādah: blessedness and enjoyment of good.

Saʿīd: blessed or fortunate; this adjective does occur in the Qur’an.

Masʿūd: fortunate or enjoying blessedness.

Asʿada: made someone blessed or brought them success; in some uses, helped.

Sāʿada: helped or assisted.

Musāʿadah: help and cooperation.

Sāʿid: the forearm between elbow and wrist.

In Qur’anic translation, saʿīd should not simply become wealthy, cheerful, free of suffering, lucky, or an inhabitant of Paradise. Some of these may be contextually associated with blessedness, but none is its complete lexical equivalent.

In particular, the Garden as their destination follows from the rest of Hūd 11:108 rather than from the root alone.

Conclusion

S ʿ D concerns good fortune, enjoyment of good, success, and blessedness.

Early Arabic contrasts saʿd with naḥs and saʿādah with shaqāwah. Auspicious days and favorable stars belong to its linguistic and cultural field, but do not prove stellar control over human destiny.

Other branches include helping, the forearm, and plant names. They should not be reduced to one meaning without historical evidence.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an, both in Hūd.

Saʿīd in Hūd 11:105 contrasts blessedness with shaqiyy, wretchedness.

Suʿidū in Hūd 11:108 describes those placed in the Garden and receiving uninterrupted divine bounty.

These uses are not limited to passing joy, wealth, or random luck. They concern a favorable condition and outcome at judgment.

The core Qur’anic meaning is blessedness and a favorable outcome, opposed to wretchedness and an unfavorable outcome.

Context connects this blessedness with divine bounty and human destiny. The root alone does not settle the process of judgment, the role of deeds, divine will, or the duration of conditions in the hereafter.

That distinguishes it from joy: joy can be momentary, whereas these verses concern the human outcome. Worldly cheerfulness does not prove blessedness, nor does suffering prove wretchedness.

For Forqan, “blessed” and “being granted blessedness” are suitable base renderings, preserving the lexical meaning and the context of judgment in Hūd.
