Qur’anic Root أ ز ر

Strength, firmness and support: from strengthening Moses’ back to reinforcement within a growing plant

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَزْرِي
(azrī)
Qur’anic noun with “my”: my strength and backing, Ṭā Hā 20:31

فَآزَرَهُ
(fa-āzarahu)
Qur’anic perfect verb with an object pronoun: then it strengthened it, Al-Fatḥ 48:29

أَزَرَهُ
(azarahu)
Lexical form: he strengthened him or it; this form is absent from the Ḥafṣ reading

مُؤَازَرَة
(muʾāzarah)
Lexical verbal noun: support and cooperation; not Qur’anic

تَأَزَّرَ
(taʾazzara)
Lexical verb: of vegetation, it grew strong and dense; not Qur’anic

إِزَار
(izār)
Lexical noun: a garment or covering, especially around the waist; not Qur’anic

مِئْزَر
(miʾzar)
Lexical noun: a waist covering; not Qur’anic

مُؤَزَّر
(muʾazzar)
Lexical description: strong and reinforced; not Qur’anic

فَأَزَرَهُ
(fa-azarahu)
Short form in Ibn Dhakwān’s transmission; distinct from the Ḥafṣ form

آزَرَ
(āzar)
Qur’anic proper name in Al-Anʿām 6:74; Arabic derivation is unproven

The Arabic root أ ز ر concerns strength, firmness and strengthening. أَزْر denotes strength or backing, while آزَرَهُ means to help and give strength to another. Within this word family, something may become firm through its own strength or through the support of something else.

The Qur’an uses this root in two different settings. In Ṭā Hā 20:31, Moses asks The God to strengthen his capacity and backing through his brother Aaron. In Al-Fatḥ 48:29, a plant is described whose fresh shoots grow alongside its main stem and contribute to the firmness and strength of the whole plant.

Strengthening and firmness are central in both uses, but one concerns human cooperation and the other plant growth. This shared meaning shows that أ ز ر is restricted neither to military assistance, family ties nor religious support. Its basic meaning is broader; context identifies the kind of support involved.

The Qur’an also contains آزَرَ as a proper name in Al-Anʿām 6:74. In writing, this name resembles the verb آزَرَ, but it should not be treated as a derivative of the same Arabic root without independent evidence.

1. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

The Arabic root أ ز ر has two derivative occurrences in the Qur’an:

أَزْرِي — Ṭā Hā 20:31

This is the noun أَزْر with the first-person possessive pronoun. In Moses’ prayer, it refers to his capacity, strength or backing.

فَآزَرَهُ — Al-Fatḥ 48:29

This is a third-person masculine singular perfect verb with an object pronoun, referring to strengthening and giving force during plant growth. Morphologically, it has been analyzed as Form IV; some lexicons also give a Form III analysis. The long-vowel form alone does not establish that the assistance is reciprocal.

These two forms have different structures. أَزْر is a noun denoting strength or backing; آزَرَ is a verb expressing the giving of strength or assistance to another.

Other forms known in Arabic include أَزَرَهُ, مُؤَازَرَة, تَأَزَّرَ, إِزَار, مِئْزَر and مُؤَزَّر, but none occurs in the Qur’an in these particular forms.

The shared semantic core of the two Qur’anic uses can be expressed as follows:

Strength and firmness, and strengthening something or someone so that its capacity, endurance or backing increases.

This definition does not mean that every occurrence of the root necessarily denotes mutual help. One person may strengthen another, or several parts of a whole may contribute to one another’s firmness. The sentence and its context establish the precise relationship.

2. The root in early Arabic: back, strength and binding the waist

أَزْر: strength and backing

In early Arabic, أَزْر is recorded with the meaning of strength or force. “Back” is another recorded sense: the back and waist help the body bear loads and remain upright.

The expression شَدَّ أَزْرَهُ belongs to this semantic field: strengthening someone’s back or capacity and making that person more capable of undertaking a task.

A verse attributed to Al-Baʿīth Al-Mujāshiʿī, a poet of the Umayyad period, reads:

شَدَدْتُ لَهُ أَزْرِي بِمِرَّةِ حَازِمٍ
عَلَى مَوْقِعٍ مِنْ أَمْرِهِ مُتَفَاقِمِ

shadadtu lahu azrī bi-mirrati ḥāzimin / ʿalā mawqiʿin min amrihi mutafāqimi

“To help him, I brought my strength to bear with the firmness and judgment of a resolute man, facing a difficult turn in his affairs.”

This attestation shows that shadda azra was used in Arabic for providing support and applying one’s strength to help another.

The meaning here is not physically binding a back. The poet is speaking of strength and support.

This verse is an attestation from Umayyad Arabic, rather than a securely pre-Islamic witness. Its preservation in early lexicons nevertheless shows that shadda azra was known in Arabic beyond its Qur’anic use.

إِزَار and مِئْزَر: a garment tied around the waist

إِزَار names a garment or piece of cloth tied or wrapped around the body, especially the waist and lower body. مِئْزَر likewise denotes a covering within this word family.

Tying such a covering involves securing it firmly around the waist. There is thus an intelligible connection between إِزَار and the idea of making firm or binding securely.

Some lexical explanations illustrate strength and firmness through the image of tying the izār tightly. Another possible explanation derives the garment’s name from the idea of strength and firmness itself.

The historical order of this development is not certain. We cannot confidently say that the garment’s name came first and the sense of strength developed later, or the reverse.

What is clear is the semantic association of these uses in Arabic: binding, securing and firmness.

The expression shadda miʾzara is also known for preparing oneself and applying oneself earnestly to a task. Securing the waist covering can accompany practical readiness, but this image should not become a compulsory definition for every derivative of the root.

Accordingly, أَزْرِي in Ṭā Hā 20:31 cannot simply mean “my izār” or “the garment tied around my waist.” The verse concerns strengthening Moses through Aaron, rather than his clothing.

آزَرَ and مُؤَازَرَة: strengthening and supporting

Arabic records both أَزَرَهُ and آزَرَهُ for strengthening and helping.

A distinction is sometimes drawn between these forms: أَزَرَهُ emphasizes making another strong, while آزَرَهُ emphasizes helping him. This should not become an absolute rule, since strengthening and helping overlap in actual usage.

مُؤَازَرَة denotes support and cooperation in undertaking a task. In such cooperation, a person is more than merely present: that person’s strength contributes to accomplishing the task and supporting another.

مُؤَزَّر, from the same family, has also been used to mean strong and firm.

These forms show that the root’s meaning is not confined to possessing strength. Giving strength, increasing capacity and providing practical support also belong to its field.

تَأَزَّرَ النَّبْتُ: vegetation becoming strong and dense

The Arabic expression تَأَزَّرَ النَّبْتُ has been used of vegetation becoming strong and dense.

A poetic attestation transmitted in early lexicons reads:

تَأَزَّرَ فِيهِ النَّبْتُ حَتَّىٰ تَخَايَلَتْ
رُبَاهُ وَحَتَّىٰ مَا تُرَى الشَّاءُ نُوَّمَا

taʾazzara fīhi al-nabtu ḥattā takhāyalat / rubāhu wa-ḥattā mā turā al-shāʾu nuwwamā

“The vegetation there grew so strong and dense that its hills became luxuriant and flowering, and sleeping sheep among it could no longer be seen.”

This attestation describes dense, vigorous plant growth with taʾazzara. It shows that strength and firmness in this root were not restricted to the human body or social support.

Another recorded use, آزَرَ الشيءُ الشيءَ, describes one thing matching or standing level with another. In descriptions of plants, this sense relates to fresh growth reaching the size and firmness of other stems.

A verse attributed to Imruʾ Al-Qays, a pre-Islamic poet, is preserved in lexical quotations and his collected poetry, though its precise date of composition is unknown:

بِمَحْنِيَةٍ قَدْ آزَرَ الضَّالَ نَبْتُهَا
مَجَرِّ جُيُوشٍ غَانِمِينَ وَخُيَّبِ

bi-maḥniyatin qad āzara al-ḍāla nabtuhā / majarri juyūshin ghānimīna wa-khuyyabi

“At a bend in a valley whose vegetation had grown level with the wild lote trees, a passage for armies, some returning with gains and others empty-handed.”

Here آزَرَ describes vegetation reaching the height of the trees: نَبْتُهَا is the subject and الضَّالَ the object. The witness illustrates the sense of matching height in Arabic attributed to the pre-Islamic period; the precise meaning of the Qur’anic verb must still be established from its own context.

This use does not establish that آزَرَ always means to become equal in size. In the Qur’an, strengthening is its clearer and broader sense; stems reaching a similar height may be a result of growth and reinforcement.

3. اشْدُدْ بِهِ أَزْرِي: Moses’ request for stronger backing

Moses’ prayer includes:

اشْدُدْ بِهِ أَزْرِي

ushdud bihi azrī

“Through him, strengthen my capacity and backing.”

Ṭā Hā 20:31

This short verse is one of the clearest Qur’anic uses of أ ز ر.

Understanding it requires attention to the requests before and after it.

After being commissioned to go to Pharaoh, Moses asks The God to open his breast, ease his task and loosen a knot in his tongue so that his speech may be understood. He then asks that Aaron, his brother, be appointed as his minister and companion (Ṭā Hā 20:29 and Ṭā Hā 20:30).

He continues: اشْدُدْ بِهِ أَزْرِي.

The pronoun in بِهِ refers to Aaron. Moses is not merely asking The God to increase his physical strength; he wants his brother to be a means of supporting and strengthening him in carrying out his mission.

Lexically, أَزْرِي can be understood as “my back” or “my strength”; both meanings are recorded in Arabic. In this verse, however, the physical back is not the ultimate point. The expression concerns the backing and strength Moses needs to fulfill his responsibility.

The verb اشْدُدْ comes from the separate root ش د د and denotes making firm or strong. Alongside أَزْرِي, it makes the sense of strengthening more explicit.

Study of SH D D

Two elements therefore meet in this sentence: أَزْر as capacity and backing, and شَدّ as the act of making it firm.

Is Moses speaking of his own weakness?

Some lexical reports also record “weakness” as a meaning of أَزْر. On that basis, ushdud bihi azrī can be explained as removing weakness and remedying a lack of strength.

The better-known meaning of أَزْر in this construction, however, is strength and backing. The context itself points to increasing Moses’ strength through Aaron’s companionship.

Asking for help does not mean that Moses has no independent ability or competence. He has undertaken a difficult mission and seeks the companionship of someone who can make a real contribution to it.

The next verse matters for this reason as well: Moses wants Aaron to share in his task (Ṭā Hā 20:32).

Strengthening someone’s backing and sharing in the task are closely related but distinct expressions. The first emphasizes giving Moses strength; the second emphasizes Aaron’s participation in carrying out the mission.

Thus, ushdud bihi azrī is more than a wish for emotional companionship or a brother’s ceremonial presence. It requests greater practical capacity for a particular responsibility.

A related passage in Al-Qaṣaṣ

The same idea is expressed differently in Al-Qaṣaṣ:

قَالَ سَنَشُدُّ عَضُدَكَ بِأَخِيكَ وَنَجْعَلُ لَكُمَا سُلْطَانًا فَلَا يَصِلُونَ إِلَيْكُمَا بِآيَاتِنَا أَنْتُمَا وَمَنِ اتَّبَعَكُمَا الْغَالِبُونَ

qāla sa-nashuddu ʿaḍudaka bi-akhīka wa-najʿalu lakumā sulṭānan fa-lā yaṣilūna ilaykumā bi-āyātinā antumā wa-mani ittabaʿakumā al-ghālibūn

“He said: Through your brother We shall strengthen your arm, and We shall give you both authority and proof; then, with Our signs, they will not be able to reach you. You and those who follow you will prevail.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:35

Here عَضُد, referring to the upper arm, appears in place of أَزْر.

The two constructions are closely related. In Ṭā Hā, Moses asks The God to strengthen his backing through Aaron; in Al-Qaṣaṣ, The God promises to strengthen his arm through his brother.

This connection clarifies the meaning of support and strengthening, but does not make أ ز ر and ع ض د the same root.

Study of ʿ Ḍ D

عَضُد names a part of the body used here to convey power and support. أَزْر can likewise concern the back and strength. These different bodily images operate within a shared semantic setting.

Taken together, these verses show that strengthening Moses through Aaron means increasing his ability to undertake the task through cooperation and companionship.

4. فَآزَرَهُ: strengthening in the image of plant growth

The root’s second derivative occurrence is in Al-Fatḥ 48:29:

مُّحَمَّدٌ رَّسُولُ اللَّهِ وَالَّذِينَ مَعَهُ أَشِدَّاءُ عَلَى الْكُفَّارِ رُحَمَاءُ بَيْنَهُمْ تَرَاهُمْ رُكَّعًا سُجَّدًا يَبْتَغُونَ فَضْلًا مِّنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانًا سِيمَاهُمْ فِي وُجُوهِهِم مِّنْ أَثَرِ السُّجُودِ ذَٰلِكَ مَثَلُهُمْ فِي التَّوْرَاةِ وَمَثَلُهُمْ فِي الْإِنجِيلِ كَزَرْعٍ أَخْرَجَ شَطْأَهُ فَآزَرَهُ فَاسْتَغْلَظَ فَاسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِ يُعْجِبُ الزُّرَّاعَ لِيَغِيظَ بِهِمُ الْكُفَّارَ وَعَدَ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ مِنْهُم مَّغْفِرَةً وَأَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

muḥammadun rasūlu allāhi wa-alladhīna maʿahu ashiddāʾu ʿalā al-kuffāri ruḥamāʾu baynahum tarāhum rukkaʿan sujjadan yabtaghūna faḍlan mina allāhi wa-riḍwānan sīmāhum fī wujūhihim min athari al-sujūdi dhālika mathaluhum fī al-tawrāti wa-mathaluhum fī al-injīli ka-zarʿin akhraja shaṭʾahu fa-āzarahu fa-istaghlaẓa fa-istawā ʿalā sūqihi yuʿjibu al-zurrāʿa li-yaghīẓa bihimu al-kuffāra waʿada allāhu alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti minhum maghfiratan wa-ajran ʿaẓīmā

“Muhammad is the messenger of The God. Those with him are stern toward the disbelievers and merciful among themselves. You see them bowing and prostrating, seeking favor and approval from The God. Their mark is on their faces from the effect of prostration. That is their description in the Torah; their likeness in the Gospel is a crop that puts forth its fresh shoots, which then strengthen and support it; it then grows thick and stands upright on its stems, delighting the cultivators, so that through them He may enrage the disbelievers. The God has promised those among them who believe and do righteous deeds forgiveness and a great reward.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

Using an image of plant growth, this verse describes a community alongside Muhammad.

First, the plant puts forth its fresh shoots and growth. These and the main stem then gain strength alongside one another; the plant becomes thick and stands firmly on its stems.

The verb فَآزَرَهُ appears midway through this description.

شَطْأ and its relationship to the main plant

In this verse, شَطْأ refers to fresh growth or shoots emerging beside the plant’s base.

Study of SH Ṭ A

This growth is not separate from, or unrelated to, the plant as a whole. The shoots grow alongside its main stem and help form a stronger cluster of vegetation.

فَآزَرَهُ therefore has a clear meaning here: one part of the plant’s growth helps strengthen another.

In a familiar grammatical reading, the subject of آزَرَ refers to شَطْأ and the object pronoun to the main plant: the fresh shoots strengthen the main plant.

Another reading is also worth considering: the main plant strengthens its own branches or shoots. This possibility has been discussed in terms of the pronoun’s construction and the relationship between the two parts.

In either reading, the central meaning remains the same: as it grows, the plant as a whole gains strength through the connection and reinforcement of its parts.

This image should not be restricted without evidence to one specific botanical process. The verse speaks of putting forth shoots, strengthening, growing thick and standing on stems; its purpose is not to describe every biological detail of plant growth.

Does فَآزَرَهُ mean the same as فَاسْتَغْلَظَ?

Two further verbs follow فَآزَرَهُ: فَاسْتَغْلَظَ and فَاسْتَوَىٰ.

These verbs form a sequence, but they are not synonyms.

فَآزَرَهُ emphasizes strengthening and giving force.

فَاسْتَغْلَظَ comes from غ ل ظ and denotes becoming stout or thick.

Study of GH L Ẓ

فَاسْتَوَىٰ comes from س و ي and here concerns the plant standing upright and firmly upon its stems.

Study of S W Y

The verse thus speaks of strengthening, thickening and standing firmly. These stages are related, but each verb makes its own semantic contribution.

Translating فَآزَرَهُ simply as “it became thick” erases its distinction from فَاسْتَغْلَظَ.

Likewise, translating it only as “it grew tall” leaves its distinction from the subsequent growth and firmness unclear.

“It strengthened it” or “it reinforced it” better preserves the relationship between these verbs.

An alternative reading of the verb

Ibn Dhakwān’s transmission from Ibn ʿĀmir also records فَأَزَرَهُ, while Ḥafṣ reads فَآزَرَهُ. Both forms have been transmitted from Hishām, also a transmitter from Ibn ʿĀmir; the short form should therefore not be attributed without qualification to every transmission route from Ibn ʿĀmir.

The shorter form corresponds to the verb أَزَرَ and belongs to the same field of strengthening. The difference in reading therefore does not remove the basic meaning of giving strength in the verse.

Nor should the different structures be taken, without evidence, to mean that one necessarily expresses unilateral strengthening while the other always expresses mutual strengthening. Morphology alone does not establish such an absolute distinction.

The image of collective growth

The plant analogy describes Muhammad and those with him. In this image, growth and firmness emerge from a whole whose parts gain strength alongside one another.

Its connection with muʾāzarah is clear. Collective strength is not exhausted by one individual or one stem; the connection and cooperation of parts can make the whole firmer.

The root’s meaning does not establish that every collective union is inherently right or commendable. Collective strength may serve different goals. In this verse, the community’s worth and direction are established by the believers’ qualities, conduct and the stated purpose, rather than by the verb آزَرَ alone.

The concluding promise is also accompanied by faith and righteous deeds. The image of growth and solidarity should not be used to extract an unconditional guarantee for every person associated with a group.

فَآزَرَهُ therefore has a clear lexical meaning while participating in a broader analogy. Its lexical meaning is strengthening; the verse’s collective and ethical message emerges from the description as a whole.

5. آزَر in Abraham’s story: a proper name or a root derivative?

Al-Anʿām 6:74 states:

وَإِذْ قَالَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ لِأَبِيهِ آزَرَ أَتَتَّخِذُ أَصْنَامًا آلِهَةً إِنِّي أَرَاكَ وَقَوْمَكَ فِي ضَلَالٍ مُّبِينٍ

wa-idh qāla ibrāhīmu li-abīhi āzara a-tattakhidhu aṣnāman ālihatan innī arāka wa-qawmaka fī ḍalālin mubīn

“And when Abraham said to his father, Āzar: Do you take idols as deities? I indeed see you and your people in clear error.”

Al-Anʿām 6:74

In writing, آزَرَ resembles the verb آزَرَ in the أ ز ر family, but its role here is different.

The word follows لِأَبِيهِ and, in the usual reading, is the proper name of the person Abraham addresses. Grammatically, too, it is recognized as a proper noun, rather than the verb “to strengthen.”

Historical and lexical sources report different views about this person’s identity and name. Some regard Āzar as his name, others connect it with a different name, and others offer different explanations of its place in the account.

These disagreements do not determine the meaning of the Arabic root أ ز ر.

Even if a meaning connected with help or strength is proposed for Āzar in another language, similarity in sound and spelling is insufficient to establish Arabic derivation. Proper names may enter Arabic from other languages and be written in Arabic script without preserving their original structure.

Qur’anic occurrence counts must therefore distinguish two matters: أَزْرِي and فَآزَرَهُ belong to the semantic family of strength and strengthening; آزَرَ in Al-Anʿām 6:74 is a proper name and cannot be counted as a third semantic derivative of this root without independent evidence.

The name has its own historical and interpretive significance in the Qur’an. Research into Abraham’s genealogy and his father’s identity is nevertheless separate from the lexical meaning of أ ز ر.

6. Distinguishing أ ز ر from و ز ر, ع ز ر, ن ص ر and ش د د

أ ز ر (أَزْر) and و ز ر (وِزْر)

These roots resemble one another in form and, in some uses, in semantic field, but they are distinct roots.

Study of W Z R

أَزْر concerns strength and backing, whereas an important branch of وِزْر concerns a load and its weight. Another use within و ز ر is وَزَر, a refuge or place of shelter.

وَزِير also names someone who helps another administer affairs and takes on part of the responsibility. Different historical derivations have been proposed, including connections with burden and responsibility or with refuge and backing. Some early explanations also propose a connection with أَزْر.

Semantic proximity must not replace examination of the actual root of each form.

Notably, Moses requests وَزِيرًا in Ṭā Hā 20:29 and says أَزْرِي in Ṭā Hā 20:31. These expressions illuminate each other: he seeks a companion who can help bear the responsibility and strengthen his capacity.

Their juxtaposition does not establish that وَزِير and أَزْر necessarily share one historical root.

أ ز ر (مُؤَازَرَة) and ع ز ر (تَعْزِير)

The root ع ز ر appears in Qur’anic forms such as عَزَّرُوهُ and can concern support, assistance and honoring.

Study of ʿ Z R

Its sense of support is close to أ ز ر, but the different first consonant is real and must be respected.

مُؤَازَرَة concerns strengthening and backing. In Qur’anic usage, تَعْزِير can also encompass support and honoring.

In later jurisprudential language, taʿzīr acquired a specific technical meaning concerning punishment. This later meaning should neither be transferred to أ ز ر nor replace the Qur’anic meaning of ع ز ر.

Despite their overlap in assistance, these word families should therefore be studied separately.

أ ز ر (آزَرَ) and ن ص ر (نَصَرَ)

In the Qur’an, نَصَرَ concerns giving help and, in many settings, relates to overcoming an enemy, removing a threat or providing support in a confrontation.

Study of N Ṣ R

آزَرَ can also mean to help, but strengthening and giving force are more prominent in its Qur’anic use.

The image of the plant and its fresh shoots in Al-Fatḥ 48:29 describes the reinforcement of the plant’s parts, rather than warfare or military victory itself. Other parts of the same verse do mention the disbelievers and their anger. This explanation therefore concerns the plant image and the verb within it, rather than denying a setting of confrontation in the verse as a whole.

Naṣr, by comparison, can be used when one side is helped to overcome an obstacle or enemy.

This is a tendency, not an absolute distinction. We cannot say that āzara never appears in battle contexts, or that naṣr means only military aid. Both belong to the field of support, while the direction and kind of help vary with context.

أ ز ر (أَزْر) and ش د د (شَدّ)

Shadd concerns making firm, binding and securing.

In Ṭā Hā 20:31, this very verb accompanies أَزْر. The collocation clarifies their relationship: a capacity or backing exists, and the request is for it to be made firm and strong.

In that sentence, أَزْر names the strength or backing, while شَدّ expresses the act of strengthening and securing it.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:29, فَآزَرَهُ likewise occurs alongside فَاسْتَغْلَظَ and فَاسْتَوَىٰ, a group of verbs presenting plant growth and firmness from different angles.

These collocations are no reason to merge the roots. Rather, they help us understand each word’s particular semantic contribution.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation of أ ز ر should distinguish the noun for strength from the verb for strengthening.

For أَزْرِي in Ṭā Hā 20:31, the proposed rendering is:

“Through him, strengthen my capacity and backing.”

This preserves the sense of strength while clarifying Aaron’s relationship to Moses’ mission.

“Through him, strengthen my back” stays closer to the lexical image, but can be understood too literally. “With his help, make me strong” is also natural, though it makes the noun أَزْر, referring to capacity and backing, less visible.

For فَآزَرَهُ in Al-Fatḥ 48:29, “it strengthened it” or “it reinforced it” is an appropriate basic equivalent.

Within the sentence, one may say:

“Like a crop that put forth its fresh shoots, which then strengthened it; it then grew thick and stood firmly upon its stems.”

This preserves the distinction between strengthening, thickening and standing firmly.

If the relationship between the fresh growth and the main plant needs clarification in a note, it can be explained that the new shoots and stems help strengthen the whole plant. Contextual explanation should not, however, be inserted into the basic translation in a way that presents a pronoun reference or relationship still requiring examination as incautiously settled.

For the family’s non-Qur’anic derivatives, مُؤَازَرَة usually means “support,” “mutual assistance” or “strengthening.” In a suitable context, مُؤَزَّر can be rendered “strong,” “firm” or “reinforced.”

إِزَار names a covering or garment and should not simply be translated as “strength,” though its lexical relationship with binding and securing can be examined.

For آزَرَ in Al-Anʿām 6:74, the proper name should be retained. Translating it as “helper,” “strong” or “supporter” without reliable historical evidence imposes the Arabic root’s meaning on a name whose derivation has not been established.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root أ ز ر concerns strength, firmness and support. أَزْر can express strength or the back, while آزَرَ denotes strengthening and helping.

In Arabic, this semantic field has connections with tying and securing the waist covering, supporting another and vegetation becoming strong. The image of the izār or binding the waist should nevertheless not be treated as the certain historical origin of every derivative.

In Ṭā Hā 20:31, Moses seeks stronger capacity and backing through his brother Aaron. In Al-Fatḥ 48:29, the same semantic family describes a growing plant whose fresh shoots help make the whole firm.

These uses show that strengthening can be human or natural. Support by itself makes no judgment about the rightness of an aim, an agent’s ethical worth or a group’s legitimacy; context establishes these matters.

The proper name Āzar in Al-Anʿām 6:74 must remain separate from these two derivatives. Its outward resemblance to the Arabic verb is insufficient evidence for derivation or transfer of meaning.

Forqan’s final definition of the root أ ز ر:

“Strength and firmness; and strengthening or supporting someone or something so that its capacity and endurance increase. This reinforcement may arise through another’s companionship and help, or through the connection of parts within a whole. In the Qur’an, أَزْر denotes capacity and backing, and آزَرَ denotes giving strength, whereas Āzar in Abraham’s story is a proper name whose derivational relationship with this root is unproven.”
