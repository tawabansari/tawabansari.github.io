Hiddenness and disclosure: from remaining hidden and concealing to bringing something out of concealment

The Arabic root خ ف ي belongs to an important word family concerning remaining concealed, concealing something, and emerging from concealment. A notable feature of this root is that authoritative early Arabic sources record not only the familiar sense of hiddenness but also opposite senses: becoming apparent and making something apparent.

Consequently, this root cannot always be translated as “to hide something” without examining the verbal construction and context. In some uses, something is hidden from sight or knowledge; in others, someone conceals it; and elsewhere, something previously hidden is brought out into view.

In the Qur’an, the root describes divine knowledge, concealed deeds and intentions, quiet supplication, human speech and conduct, blessings not yet disclosed, and the disclosure of truth.

These uses show that secrecy is neither inherently virtuous nor inherently sinful. Concealing assistance to those in need can be commendable; concealing truth or wrongdoing can be blameworthy; and some things remain unknown to others without anyone deliberately hiding them.

1. Forms and derivatives

خ ف ي is a triliteral root with a weak final yāʾ: its third radical is yāʾ, which undergoes phonetic changes in many inflections. This classification of the Qur’anic forms must be distinguished from related forms with final wāw that some dictionaries discuss beside this entry.

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • خَفِيَ ـ يَخْفَى: to be or remain hidden, concealed from someone’s sight or knowledge.
  • خَفَاء: hiddenness or concealment.
  • خَفِيّ: hidden, concealed, or unobtrusive.
  • خَافٍ: something hidden or not apparent.
  • خَافِيَة: a concealed matter or thing.
  • خُفْيَة: a state of secrecy or concealment; in speech, an unobtrusive utterance or supplication.
  • أَخْفَى ـ يُخْفِي: to conceal something or prevent its disclosure.
  • إِخْفَاء: concealing or keeping something hidden.
  • اِسْتَخْفَى ـ يَسْتَخْفِي: to conceal oneself or seek to avoid being seen or recognized.
  • مُسْتَخْفٍ: someone concealing himself or remaining hidden.
  • اِخْتَفَى: to disappear or become hidden.
  • خَفَا ـ يَخْفُو: a related form with final wāw in the arrangement of some dictionaries: lightning shining faintly. It should not be presented without qualification as an inflection of a final-yāʾ root or as a Qur’anic form.
  • خَفَى الشَّيْءَ: in certain early uses, to reveal something or bring it out of concealment.

The Qur’an uses this family in eight morphological and lexical categories:

Form I verbs such as يَخْفَى; Form IV verbs such as تُخْفُونَ; Form X verbs such as يَسْتَخْفُونَ; the adjective خَفِيّ; the noun خُفْيَة; the elative أَخْفَى, “more hidden”; the feminine active participle خَافِيَة; and the active participle مُسْتَخْفٍ.

The Qur’anic morphological index records 34 occurrences in 31 verses. The number of words differs from the number of verses because certain verses contain more than one member of the family. The distribution is: six Form I verbs, eighteen Form IV verbs, three Form X verbs, one elative أَخْفَى, two adjectives خَفِيّ, two instances of خُفْيَة, one خَافِيَة, and one مُسْتَخْفٍ.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 34 occurrences in eight derived categories

Forms such as تُخْفُوهُ, تُخْفُوهَا, تُخْفُونَ, and أَخْفَيْتُمْ differ in pronoun, tense, and person, but all belong to the verb أَخْفَى.

2. Two semantic fields in early Arabic: concealing and revealing

Remaining hidden from sight and knowledge

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two opposite foundations for this root:

الْخَاءُ وَالْفَاءُ وَالْيَاءُ أَصْلَانِ مُتَبَايِنَانِ مُتَضَادَّانِ، فَالْأَوَّلُ السَّتْرُ، وَالثَّانِي الْإِظْهَارُ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-fāʾu wa-al-yāʾu aṣlāni mutabāyināni mutaḍāddāni fa-al-awwalu al-satru wa-al-thānī al-iẓhāru

That is:

“Khāʾ, fāʾ, and yāʾ have two distinct and opposite foundations: the first is covering, and the second is making apparent.”

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the opposite foundations of covering and disclosure

In the first branch, خَفِيَ الشَّيْءُ means that something remained hidden or was not apparent.

Such hiddenness may result from a physical covering, as when an object stands behind an obstacle and cannot be seen. It can also concern not knowing a matter or being unaware of a truth.

For example, Arabic uses the expression:

خَفِيَ عَلَيْهِ الْأَمْرُ

khafiya ʿalayhi al-amru

Meaning: “The matter remained hidden from him.”

Here, something need not lie beneath a physical covering. A person does not know the truth or circumstances of a matter, and it is not clear to him.

This distinction matters because khafāʾ applies both to sensory perception and to knowledge and awareness.

An object may be hidden behind a wall; news may not have reached someone; an intention may not have been disclosed; or the outcome of a matter may remain unknown. All these situations can involve hiddenness, but their kinds of hiddenness differ.

Vocalization, prepositions, and sentence structure

Lexical sources transmit the following constructions with different meanings, but the vocalization of the verb and the particular source also matter:

خَفِيَ عَلَيْهِ الْأَمْرُ means that the matter remained hidden from him.

In Al-Muṭarrizī’s Al-Mughrib, خُفِيَ لَهُ, with ḍammah on khāʾ and kasrah on fāʾ, is transmitted as “it became apparent to him,” in contrast to خَفِيَ عَلَيْهِ, which expresses remaining hidden.

Thus, in this transmission, the preposition is not the only change: the verb’s vocalization differs too. This should not become a rule that every active خَفِيَ لَهُ in every source necessarily means becoming apparent. In contrast, Al-Fayyūmī’s Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr transmits the ʿalayhi/lahu distinction with active vocalization and attributes it to some lexicographers. The differences between sources and vocalizations must therefore be retained.

The example shows that the root alone does not supply all the information needed for translation. Syntactic structure and the verb’s relationship to other sentence elements also contribute to the actual meaning.

Nor is this distinction an absolute rule across all periods and uses of Arabic. Attested usages must be examined within their particular constructions and settings.

Al-Mughrib and Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr: the vocalizations khafiya ʿalayhi and khufiya lahu

Covering and bringing out of concealment

In the second branch, خَفَى can mean revealing something or bringing it out of its hiding place.

For example, early Arabic records this expression:

خَفَى الْمَطَرُ الْفِئْرَانَ

khafā al-maṭaru al-fiʾrāna

Meaning: “The rain brought the mice out of their holes.”

Here the mice were previously hidden in their burrows, and rain caused them to emerge.

Accordingly, making apparent in this branch concerns bringing something out of a state of concealment.

This relationship does not establish that either meaning necessarily developed from the other. Ibn Fāris presents two opposite foundations, and the available evidence does not determine one certain historical trajectory.

Beside this entry, sources such as Asās al-Balāghah also record the final-wāw form خَفَا الْبَرْقُ يَخْفُو خَفْوًا for lightning shining faintly. Its third radical must be distinguished from final yāʾ.

In this use, lightning appears or becomes visible among the clouds. Its appearance may be brief or faint, but the verb concerns appearing rather than becoming hidden.

Asās al-Balāghah: the final-wāw form for lightning shining

Physical images of concealment

One important expression in this family is الْخَوَافِي: wing feathers situated beneath or behind the outer feathers, making them less visible.

Lexical sources also record khawāfī for leaves or branches near the inner, concealed part of a date palm.

These uses provide a clear physical image of concealment: something exists but remains out of direct sight because of its position behind or beneath something else.

The name الْخَفِيَّة is also recorded for a deep well, or one that was covered and filled in for a time and subsequently reopened.

In these uses, concealment is a physical condition, but the root’s meaning is not limited to such coverings.

This image helps explain its wider application to news, thought, intentions, and unknown matters, without requiring all these uses to refer back to physically covering an object.

Al-Muḥkam and Tāj al-ʿArūs: feathers, palms, and wells

3. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry and usage

Bringing out of concealment: a verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

خَفَاهُنَّ مِنْ أَنْفَاقِهِنَّ كَأَنَّمَا
خَفَاهُنَّ وَدْقٌ مِنْ سَحَابٍ مُرَكَّبِ

khafāhunna min anfāqihinna ka-annamā / khafāhunna wadqun min saḥābin murakkabi

Translation:

“He brought them out of their burrows, as though rain from a piled-up cloud had brought them out of their shelters.”

Lexical transmissions cite this verse as evidence that خَفَى can mean bringing out or making apparent.

The wording of its second hemistich varies. Ibn Barrī notes that مِنْ عَشِيٍّ مُجَلِّبِ also occurs in the poetry of Imruʾ al-Qays.

We therefore should not assume that every manuscript has identical wording.

In this witness, خَفَاهُنَّ does not mean hiding them: the passage concerns creatures emerging from burrows in which they had been concealed.

This is one of the clearest early witnesses for the revealing branch of خ ف ي.

Al-Muḥkam: the witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and textual variants

Concealing or revealing a secret: another early Arabic witness

Another verse is attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays ibn ʿĀbis al-Kindī. He is distinct from the famous pre-Islamic Imruʾ al-Qays and is counted among the mukhaḍram poets who lived across the advent of Islam. The date of this particular verse is unknown, so it is not presented as certain evidence predating Qur’anic revelation:

فَإِنْ تَكْتُمُوا السِّرَّ لَا نَخْفِهِ
وَإِنْ تَبْعَثُوا الْحَرْبَ لَا نَقْعُدِ

fa-in taktumū al-sirra lā nakhfihi / wa-in tabʿathū al-ḥarba lā naqʿudi

Translation:

“If you conceal the secret, we will not reveal it; and if you stir up war, we will not sit it out.”

Lexical explanations gloss لَا نَخْفِهِ here as لَا نُظْهِرْهُ, “we will not reveal it.” In the transmitted vocalization, the nūn of نَخْفِهِ has fatḥah, and the verb is Form I; it should not be confused with Form IV نُخْفِيهِ.

The witness matters because the verb belongs to the same family but has a meaning opposite to the common modern Arabic use of ikhfāʾ.

Some sources transmit the opening differently, including فَإِنْ تَدْفِنُوا الدَّاءَ or فَإِنْ تَكْتُمُوا الشَّرَّ.

These variants must be considered when examining attribution and meaning, but lexical sources clearly attest a use of خَفَى meaning to reveal.

Nevertheless, neither witness establishes that every Qur’anic form of أَخْفَى means revealing. The verb, morphological pattern, reading, and context of each occurrence require separate examination.

Al-Ṭabarī: Ibn ʿĀbis’s witness, fatḥah on nūn, and variant transmissions

4. Qur’anic uses: being hidden, concealing, and hiding oneself

What does not remain hidden from knowledge

Āl ʿImrān 3:5 states:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَخْفَىٰ عَلَيْهِ شَيْءٌ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا فِي السَّمَاءِ

inna allāha lā yakhfā ʿalayhi shayʾun fī al-arḍi wa-lā fī al-samāʾi

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Indeed, nothing on earth or in heaven remains hidden from The God.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:5

يَخْفَى is a Form I verb. In this construction, the subject is something remaining hidden from knowledge, rather than an act of concealment performed by a person.

The construction لَا يَخْفَىٰ عَلَيْهِ recurs in the Qur’an.

This excerpt also occurs in Ibrāhīm 14:38:

وَمَا يَخْفَىٰ عَلَى اللَّهِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا فِي السَّمَاءِ

wa-mā yakhfā ʿalā allāhi min shayʾin fī al-arḍi wa-lā fī al-samāʾi

Proposed translation of this excerpt from Ibrāhīm 14:38: “Nothing on earth or in heaven remains hidden from The God.”

Likewise, in Ghāfir 40:16, describing the day when people emerge into view, this excerpt occurs:

لَا يَخْفَىٰ عَلَى اللَّهِ مِنْهُمْ شَيْءٌ

lā yakhfā ʿalā allāhi minhum shayʾun

Proposed translation of this excerpt from Ghāfir 40:16: “Nothing about them remains hidden from The God.”

These uses establish a fundamental distinction: something may be hidden from human sight or awareness without being hidden from divine knowledge.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:40, لَا يَخْفَوْنَ عَلَيْنَا concerns those who deviate regarding the divine signs. Here, the people themselves and their conduct are not hidden from divine knowledge.

In Al-Aʿlā 87:7, divine knowledge is described by يَعْلَمُ الْجَهْرَ وَمَا يَخْفَىٰ: He knows both what is openly expressed and what remains hidden.

In these verses, khafiya concerns a lack of accessibility or visibility for an observer. Such hiddenness, however, is denied in relation to The God.

Concealing: an action someone performs

In Form IV, أَخْفَى ـ يُخْفِي usually means making something hidden.

The distinction can be explained as follows:

خَفِيَ الشَّيْءُ means that something was or remained hidden.

أَخْفَى الشَّيْءَ means that someone concealed that thing.

This excerpt occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:284:

وَإِنْ تُبْدُوا مَا فِي أَنْفُسِكُمْ أَوْ تُخْفُوهُ يُحَاسِبْكُمْ بِهِ اللَّهُ

wa-in tubdū mā fī anfusikum aw tukhfūhu yuḥāsibkum bihi allāhu

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Whether you disclose what is within yourselves or conceal it, The God will call you to account for it.”

Al-Baqarah 2:284

Here تُخْفُوهُ stands opposite تُبْدُوا.

Ibdāʾ means making apparent, whereas ikhfāʾ means keeping concealed.

The same opposition appears in Āl ʿImrān 3:29, Al-Aḥzāb 33:54, and other verses.

In these uses, a matter’s being hidden does not make it unimportant. The Qur’an explicitly states that whether something is apparent or concealed to people does not remove it from divine knowledge.

Accountability for deeds and intentions, however, comes from the verse’s context. The lexical meaning of ikhfāʾ does not itself mean sin or moral responsibility.

Concealing intentions, enmity, and what is within the breast

This excerpt occurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:118:

قَدْ بَدَتِ الْبَغْضَاءُ مِنْ أَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَمَا تُخْفِي صُدُورُهُمْ أَكْبَرُ

qad badati al-baghḍāʾu min afwāhihim wa-mā tukhfī ṣudūruhum akbaru

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Hatred has already appeared from their mouths, and what their breasts conceal is greater.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:118

Two conditions are opposed here: what speech has disclosed, and what still remains hidden within.

Literally, تُخْفِي صُدُورُهُمْ describes breasts concealing something. The breast here is the figurative location of what a person keeps within.

This concealment is unlike covering an object with cloth: it concerns an intention, feeling, or inward reality.

Ghāfir 40:19 also states:

يَعْلَمُ خَائِنَةَ الْأَعْيُنِ وَمَا تُخْفِي الصُّدُورُ

yaʿlamu khāʾinata al-aʿyuni wa-mā tukhfī al-ṣudūru

This sentence expresses divine knowledge of the eyes’ conduct and what breasts conceal. Proposed translation for this article: “He knows the treachery of the eyes and what the breasts conceal.” This proposal does not change the installed verse translation.

Āl ʿImrān 3:154 likewise describes people hiding within themselves something they do not disclose to the Prophet.

Addressing the Prophet, this excerpt occurs in Al-Aḥzāb 33:37:

وَتُخْفِي فِي نَفْسِكَ مَا اللَّهُ مُبْدِيهِ

wa-tukhfī fī nafsika mā allāhu mubdīhi

Proposed translation of this excerpt: “And you conceal within yourself what The God will disclose.” This proposal does not change the installed translation.

The use shows that ikhfāʾ can concern something that will later be revealed. The verb itself does not determine what that thing was; identifying it depends on the complete verse’s context.

Concealing truth and information

This excerpt occurs in Al-Māʾidah 5:15:

قَدْ جَاءَكُمْ رَسُولُنَا يُبَيِّنُ لَكُمْ كَثِيرًا مِمَّا كُنْتُمْ تُخْفُونَ مِنَ الْكِتَابِ وَيَعْفُو عَنْ كَثِيرٍ

qad jāʾakum rasūlunā yubayyinu lakum kathīran mimmā kuntum tukhfūna mina al-kitābi wa-yaʿfū ʿan kathīrin

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Our messenger has come to you, clarifying for you much of what you used to conceal of the Book, and passing over much.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:15

تُخْفُونَ here concerns concealing some of the Book’s contents.

Al-Anʿām 6:91 also describes people placing writings on sheets, disclosing some and concealing much.

In both uses, ikhfāʾ concerns information and material in people’s possession that they do not disclose fully to others.

This point must remain distinct from two other questions.

First, concealing a text is not identical to altering it. Someone may hide material from others without changing its words.

Second, concealing something does not itself establish that someone knowingly intends to deceive others. Intention, awareness, and responsibility require independent indications.

In the contexts of Al-Māʾidah 5:15 and Al-Anʿām 6:91, the conduct of particular groups is criticized. The lexical meaning of ikhfāʾ should not be equated with every moral characteristic of those groups.

Concealing a good deed

This excerpt occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:271:

إِنْ تُبْدُوا الصَّدَقَاتِ فَنِعِمَّا هِيَ وَإِنْ تُخْفُوهَا وَتُؤْتُوهَا الْفُقَرَاءَ فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ

in tubdū al-ṣadaqāti fa-niʿimmā hiya wa-in tukhfūhā wa-tuʾtūhā al-fuqarāʾa fa-huwa khayrun lakum

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“If you disclose charitable gifts, how good that is; but if you conceal them and give them to the poor, that is better for you.”

Al-Baqarah 2:271

تُخْفُوهَا here refers to charitable gifts.

Unlike concealing truth or an objectionable intention, concealing an action here can be commendable.

The verse distinguishes public from secret giving without condemning public charity outright. It accords a particular advantage to giving to the poor in secret.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:149 also mentions disclosing or concealing a good deed alongside pardoning an injury.

These uses show that ikhfāʾ is a way of performing or keeping something; its moral value does not follow from the root itself.

Consequently, a root definition should not invariably associate concealing with deception, hypocrisy, or wrongdoing.

Concealed adornment and the disclosure of indications

This excerpt occurs in Al-Nūr 24:31:

وَلَا يَضْرِبْنَ بِأَرْجُلِهِنَّ لِيُعْلَمَ مَا يُخْفِينَ مِنْ زِينَتِهِنَّ

wa-lā yaḍribna bi-arjulihinna li-yuʿlama mā yukhfīna min zīnatihinna

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And let them not strike their feet so that what they conceal of their adornment becomes known.”

Al-Nūr 24:31

يُخْفِينَ refers to adornment kept hidden. It is a third-person feminine plural Form IV imperfect; the final nūn is the feminine plural nūn.

Notably, something may be unseen directly yet become known through another indication or effect.

Here the relationship is between concealed adornment and a movement that makes people aware of it.

The use shows that khafāʾ is not restricted to being hidden from sight: it can concern something whose existence or characteristics are kept from others’ awareness.

The kind of adornment and the scope of the instruction must be examined from the whole verse. يُخْفِينَ alone does not determine the ruling concerning every kind of adornment.

A hidden blessing not yet known

Al-Sajdah 32:17 states:

فَلَا تَعْلَمُ نَفْسٌ مَا أُخْفِيَ لَهُمْ مِنْ قُرَّةِ أَعْيُنٍ جَزَاءً بِمَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

fa-lā taʿlamu nafsun mā ukhfiya lahum min qurrati aʿyunin jazāʾan bi-mā kānū yaʿmalūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“No soul knows what comfort of the eyes has been kept hidden for them, as a reward for what they used to do.”

Al-Sajdah 32:17

أُخْفِيَ is a Form IV passive perfect: something has been kept hidden.

This differs from concealing a human intention or action. The subject here is a reward whose reality or measure has not been disclosed to people.

The root can therefore describe something still unknown that will later become apparent or be received.

The blessing’s hiddenness does not mean that it does not exist. Rather, it emphasizes the addressee’s lack of knowledge of what has been prepared.

5. Istikhfāʾ: hiding oneself and seeking to remain unknown to others

Form X occurs in the Qur’an as يَسْتَخْفُونَ, لِيَسْتَخْفُوا, and مُسْتَخْفٍ.

We read this excerpt in Al-Nisāʾ 4:108:

يَسْتَخْفُونَ مِنَ النَّاسِ وَلَا يَسْتَخْفُونَ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَهُوَ مَعَهُمْ إِذْ يُبَيِّتُونَ مَا لَا يَرْضَىٰ مِنَ الْقَوْلِ

yastakhfūna mina al-nāsi wa-lā yastakhfūna mina allāhi wa-huwa maʿahum idh yubayyitūna mā lā yarḍā mina al-qawli

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“They hide themselves from people, but do not hide from The God, although He is with them when they devise by night speech that He does not approve.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:108

Istikhfāʾ here concerns seeking to remain hidden from others.

People may remain beyond human sight or awareness, but that action does not make them hidden from The God.

This excerpt also occurs in Hūd 11:5:

أَلَا إِنَّهُمْ يَثْنُونَ صُدُورَهُمْ لِيَسْتَخْفُوا مِنْهُ

alā innahum yathnūna ṣudūrahum li-yastakhfū minhu

Proposed translation of this excerpt from Hūd 11:5: “Indeed, they fold their breasts to hide themselves from Him.”

لِيَسْتَخْفُوا expresses the purpose of remaining hidden. The continuation emphasizes that The God knows what they conceal and what they disclose.

In Al-Raʿd 13:10, مُسْتَخْفٍ بِاللَّيْلِ stands opposite سَارِبٌ بِالنَّهَارِ.

This contrast sets remaining hidden by night against moving or being openly present by day.

Here istikhfāʾ is not inherently objectionable conduct. It is one of two different human conditions, both equally within divine knowledge.

Comparing these uses shows that Form X can mean hiding oneself or trying to remain hidden without itself determining anyone’s moral intention.

6. Khafiyy, khufyah, and khāfiyah: hiddenness in voice, gaze, and human circumstances

Zechariah’s private supplication

Maryam 19:3 states:

إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ نِدَاءً خَفِيًّا

idh nādā rabbahu nidāʾan khafiyyan

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“When he called upon his Lord with a private call.”

Maryam 19:3

خَفِيًّا qualifies نِدَاءً.

A call still exists here, but it is hidden and unobtrusive.

Lexically, a nidāʾ khafiyy can be a call not apparent to others or one uttered in a low voice.

Its hiddenness must not be confused with a divine inability to hear or know it. The verse describes Zechariah’s manner of calling, rather than a limitation on divine hearing.

Nor does khafiyy here directly mean sincerity of intention. Sincerity may accompany such a supplication, but the adjective alone does not establish it.

Supplication in khufyah

This excerpt occurs in Al-Anʿām 6:63:

تَدْعُونَهُ تَضَرُّعًا وَخُفْيَةً

tadʿūnahu taḍarruʿan wa-khufyatan

Proposed translation of this excerpt from Al-Anʿām 6:63: “You call upon Him humbly and in secret.”

We also read in Al-Aʿrāf 7:55:

ادْعُوا رَبَّكُمْ تَضَرُّعًا وَخُفْيَةً إِنَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُعْتَدِينَ

idʿū rabbakum taḍarruʿan wa-khufyatan innahu lā yuḥibbu al-muʿtadīna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Call upon your Lord humbly and in secret; indeed, He does not love those who overstep.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:55

خُفْيَةً concerns the manner of supplication in both verses.

Taḍarruʿ and khufyah stand together in each, but they are not synonyms.

Taḍarruʿ concerns humility and need in making a request, whereas khufyah describes its concealed, unobtrusive character.

Khufyah should therefore not be translated merely as humility, nor taḍarruʿ treated as equivalent to speaking quietly.

A covert, subdued gaze

This excerpt occurs in Al-Shūrā 42:45:

وَتَرَاهُمْ يُعْرَضُونَ عَلَيْهَا خَاشِعِينَ مِنَ الذُّلِّ يَنْظُرُونَ مِنْ طَرْفٍ خَفِيٍّ

wa-tarāhum yuʿraḍūna ʿalayhā khāshiʿīna mina al-dhulli yanẓurūna min ṭarfin khafiyyin

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And you see them being presented to it, subdued by humiliation, looking with a covert, restrained glance.”

Al-Shūrā 42:45

خَفِيٍّ qualifies طَرْفٍ here.

Unlike Maryam 19:3, where a private call was described, hiddenness here concerns looking.

Humiliation and dread are prominent in the context. Their gaze is not open and unrestrained; it is subdued and unobtrusive.

Khafiyy does not itself mean being afraid. Fear and humiliation arise from the whole scene and accompanying expressions.

What will not remain hidden on the day of account

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:18 states:

يَوْمَئِذٍ تُعْرَضُونَ لَا تَخْفَىٰ مِنْكُمْ خَافِيَةٌ

yawmaʾidhin tuʿraḍūna lā takhfā minkum khāfiyatun

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“On that day you will be presented; no hidden thing about you will remain concealed.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:18

This verse contains two forms of the same root: تَخْفَىٰ and خَافِيَةٌ.

Khāfiyah is a feminine active participle used here for a hidden matter.

Together, the two words express the complete negation of matters concerning people remaining concealed on that day.

This does not mean that khāfiyah itself means sin. Any concealed matter can be khāfiyah; in the context of accountability, however, the disclosure of what was previously hidden has particular importance.

7. Al-sirra wa-akhfā and the question of disclosure in akādu ukhfīhā

Two passages in Ṭā Hā are particularly important for understanding this root’s breadth.

Al-sirra wa-akhfā — Ṭā Hā 20:7

وَإِنْ تَجْهَرْ بِالْقَوْلِ فَإِنَّهُ يَعْلَمُ السِّرَّ وَأَخْفَى

wa-in tajhar bi-al-qawli fa-innahu yaʿlamu al-sirra wa-akhfā

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And if you speak aloud, He knows the secret and what is more hidden.”

Ṭā Hā 20:7

أَخْفَى has the afʿal pattern and an elative meaning here.

It stands alongside al-sirr: The God knows the secret, and what is even more concealed than the secret is not unknown to Him.

Various identifications of akhfā have been proposed, including what has not yet formed within a person and what the person himself does not know.

The verse itself, however, does not exclusively identify one of these possibilities.

Its clear meaning is the breadth of divine knowledge beyond audible speech and known secrets.

Akādu ukhfīhā — Ṭā Hā 20:15

إِنَّ السَّاعَةَ آتِيَةٌ أَكَادُ أُخْفِيهَا لِتُجْزَىٰ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ بِمَا تَسْعَىٰ

inna al-sāʿata ātiyatun akādu ukhfīhā li-tujzā kullu nafsin bi-mā tasʿā

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Indeed, the Hour is coming; I am near to concealing it, so that every soul may be recompensed for what it strives for.”

Ṭā Hā 20:15

This is an important case in which examining the root’s opposite semantic branches contributes to understanding the verse.

In the common reading, أُخْفِيهَا has ḍammah on its initial hamzah and belongs to Form IV.

The verb’s familiar meaning is concealing. Many early explanations accordingly relate the expression to the time of the Resurrection remaining concealed.

A linguistic question nevertheless arises: if the Hour is already hidden, why say أَكَادُ أُخْفِيهَا?

One early explanation treats this as emphasizing how thoroughly it is kept hidden, as though even the announcement of its coming might not have been disclosed.

This explanation fits the context, but it remains an interpretation of the expression rather than a word-for-word translation of every component.

Other analyses: making apparent

Some early transmissions report أَخْفِيهَا with fatḥah on the initial hamzah, explained through خَفَى meaning to reveal. Al-Ṭabarī transmits this reading from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, but does not accept it for recitation and himself prefers concealment.

On this reading, the expression can refer to the Hour being close to disclosure.

Disclosure, however, is not confined to the fatḥah reading. Al-Muḥkam transmits Ibn Jinnī’s analysis that أُخْفِيهَا, with the same ḍammah, can be privative: “I remove its hiddenness.” This Form IV analysis and the transmitted fatḥah reading are two distinct routes to explaining a sense of disclosure.

Al-Muḥkam: Ibn Jinnī’s privative analysis of ukh fīhā as removing hiddenness

The witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays about creatures emerging from their holes is among the evidence lexicographers cite for this early meaning.

Nevertheless, two issues must be distinguished:

First, early Arabic genuinely attests خَفَى meaning to reveal.

Second, which meaning is preferable in the Qur’anic expression أَكَادُ أُخْفِيهَا, given its common reading and context?

The mere existence of revealing in Form I does not settle its selection in the verse. But Form IV morphology alone does not rule out the privative analysis either. Preferring one analysis requires considering lexical transmissions, the reading, and context.

This article proposes concealing as its preferred interpretation, consistent with Al-Ṭabarī’s choice, rather than the only morphologically possible meaning. Revealing has early support both in the transmitted fatḥah reading and in the analysis of removing hiddenness for the form with ḍammah.

Al-Ṭabarī: the common and transmitted readings and his preference for concealment

In either interpretation, the beginning explicitly states that the Hour is coming. The disagreement over ukhfīhā concerns how it remains concealed or becomes disclosed.

Elsewhere, as in Al-Aʿrāf 7:187, knowledge of the Hour’s time is described as belonging to The God.

We must therefore distinguish the coming of the Resurrection, the time of its occurrence, and the disclosure of the event itself.

8. Semantic boundaries with related roots

KH F Y (khafāʾ) compared with S R R (sirr)

Sirr concerns something secret, a confidential matter, or something kept inwardly or within a private sphere.

Khafāʾ has a broader field: anything not apparent, whether an object, news, intention, sound, or action.

In Ṭā Hā 20:7, the two concepts stand together: السِّرَّ وَأَخْفَى.

This use shows that secrecy and hiddenness are close without being wholly synonymous.

Study of S R R

KH F Y (khafāʾ) compared with K T M (katm)

Kitmān emphasizes keeping something concealed and not expressing it.

Khafiya can simply describe something’s hidden condition, even when no one has deliberately concealed it.

A matter remaining outside someone’s awareness does not necessarily mean another person is withholding it.

Conversely, hiding part of the Book, as in Al-Māʾidah 5:15, can approach withholding truth in a particular context.

Ikhfāʾ and kitmān therefore overlap in certain situations but should not be equated in every use.

Study of K T M

KH F Y (khafāʾ) compared with S T R (satr)

Satr concerns covering something or its position behind a covering.

Khafāʾ can result from such a covering, but not everything hidden necessarily lies under a physical cover.

An undisclosed intention or unknown matter may be khafiyy without having a physical covering over it.

Satr and khafāʾ are consequently close, but equating them in every context is not defensible.

KH F Y (khafāʾ) compared with GH Y B (ghayb)

Ghayb concerns what lies outside presence, observation, or accessible knowledge.

Khafāʾ can likewise mean being unseen or unknown.

Ghayb, however, has a wider conceptual field in the Qur’an, and stands opposite shahādah in expressions such as عَالِمُ الْغَيْبِ وَالشَّهَادَةِ.

Many uses of خ ف ي concern a particular act of concealing or the condition of a particular matter remaining hidden.

Consequently, something khafiyy need not carry the full conceptual weight of Qur’anic ghayb.

Study of GH Y B

KH F Y (khafāʾ) compared with KH F T (khufūt)

In speech, خ ف ت concerns a voice becoming lower and speaking quietly.

خ ف ي concerns being hidden or keeping something concealed, and is not restricted to sound.

For example, نِدَاءً خَفِيًّا in Maryam 19:3 can describe an unobtrusive call. This does not make the two roots lexically identical.

Quiet speech may keep an utterance hidden from others, but hiddenness and low volume remain two distinct characteristics.

Study of KH F T

KH F Y (khafāʾ) compared with B D W (buduww)

Badā and its derivatives repeatedly stand opposite ikhfāʾ in the Qur’an, as in Al-Baqarah 2:284 and Āl ʿImrān 3:29.

Ibdāʾ means making apparent; ikhfāʾ in these contexts means keeping concealed.

Yet خ ف ي itself has an early branch meaning to reveal. The Qur’anic opposition should not be taken to erase that branch from the root’s lexical history.

Each word’s meaning is determined by its actual construction and context, rather than simply by all the meanings recorded for its root letters.

Study of B D W

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

A single fixed equivalent should not be imposed on every form of خ ف ي.

With Form I, as in لَا يَخْفَىٰ عَلَيْهِ, “does not remain hidden from Him” is appropriate.

With Form IV, as in تُخْفُونَ and تُخْفُوهُ, “you conceal” or “you keep hidden” conveys the direct meaning.

With Form X, as in يَسْتَخْفُونَ, “they hide themselves,” or where appropriate “they seek to remain hidden,” is more accurate.

Depending on what it qualifies, khafiyy can mean hidden, quiet, or unobtrusive. It concerns a call in Maryam 19:3 and a gaze in Al-Shūrā 42:45, so its contextual meaning is not identical in both verses.

Khufyatan in the supplications of Al-Anʿām 6:63 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:55 approaches “in secret” or “unobtrusively.”

Akhfā in Ṭā Hā 20:7 means “more hidden,” whereas ukhfīhā in Ṭā Hā 20:15 is Form IV and commonly means “I conceal it.” Their visual resemblance does not make them identical. Early sources also report the alternative privative Form IV analysis, removing hiddenness; the preference just stated is this article’s interpretive choice.

Brief root meaning

Being or remaining hidden; concealing; and, in a branch of early Arabic, becoming apparent or revealing something previously concealed.

This definition preserves both semantic fields recorded in early sources, while the meaning of each Qur’anic derivative must still be determined from its construction and context.

Conclusion

خ ف ي encompasses an Arabic word family concerning hiddenness and appearance. In its familiar branch, something remains concealed from sight or knowledge, or someone conceals it. In another branch recorded by early lexicographers, words in the family concern appearing and bringing something out of concealment.

Qur’anic uses primarily concern hiddenness and concealing. The Qur’an describes things hidden from people but not from The God; deeds and intentions people conceal; unobtrusive speech and supplication; and blessings whose reality is not yet known to human beings.

The difference between being hidden and concealing is fundamental. Something may be hidden without anyone’s deliberate intervention, whereas ikhfāʾ also involves the act of concealing.

Concealing has no fixed moral value in itself. The same root used for hiding truth or wrongdoing also occurs concerning secret charity, a good deed, and a reward prepared for people.

In Ṭā Hā 20:15, the opposite early Arabic branch of revealing makes examining أَكَادُ أُخْفِيهَا necessary. The preferred interpretation of the common reading, however, must be assessed from morphology, transmitted readings, and context.

A final definition must therefore preserve the field of hiddenness and concealment while recognizing the early branch of becoming apparent and revealing, without extending either branch’s meaning to every Qur’anic occurrence. Brief root meaning: Being or remaining hidden; concealing; and, in a branch of early Arabic, becoming apparent or revealing something concealed.
