The sun: light, motion, time, and its place in creation

In early Arabic, SH M S is chiefly known through shams, the sun: the luminous, warming celestial body whose rising and setting on the horizon, daylight, and changing shadows shape much of human timekeeping.

The name should be distinguished from the sun’s properties and effects. Shams names the sun itself; light, radiation, warmth, rising, setting, and motion are expressed through other words and constructions. The sun gives light, but shams does not mean light in general.

The Arabic family also has a branch concerning skittishness, unruliness, and resistance to restraint. Shamūs describes a horse or camel that is difficult to settle or control, and is also applied to unruly people or those who resist another’s approach.

Shared consonants do not prove that the sun’s name arose from animal unruliness, or that skittishness arose from solar motion. Such historical claims require evidence.

The Quran uses only the noun shams from this family. The sun appears as a sign of creation and order, in relation to light and time, in historical narratives and dreams, and in resurrection scenes. The word means the sun throughout; differences arise from the surrounding wording and context.

1. Shams in early Arabic

An ancient name for a celestial body

Before Islam, shams was the familiar Arabic name for the sun, visible by day and illuminating and warming the earth.

This meaning need not be extracted from metaphor. As qamar names the moon and najm a star, shams independently names the sun.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar reads:

أَلَمْ تَكْسِفِ الشَّمْسُ وَالْبَدْرُ وَالْكَوَاكِبُ لِلْجَبَلِ الْوَاجِبِ

a-lam taksifi al-shamsu wa-l-badru wa-l-kawākibu li-l-jabali al-wājibi

Translation:

“Have the sun, the full moon, and the stars not darkened for the fallen mountain?” The “mountain” represents the great man who has died.

The poet mourns a great man through an image of celestial darkening. Shams is the familiar sun, named alongside the moon and stars. Al-jabal al-wājib evokes a fallen mountain, not merely a steadfast man.

The darkening is an image of mourning. The sun’s familiar brightness makes it available to express loss and sorrow.

Another passage attributed to the same poet reads:

كَأَنَّ قُرُونَ الشَّمْسِ عِنْدَ ارْتِفَاعِهَا

ka-anna qurūna al-shamsi ʿinda irtifāʿihā

وَقَدْ صَادَفَتْ طَلْقًا مِنَ النَّجْمِ أَعْزَلَا

wa-qad ṣādafat ṭalqan mina al-najmi aʿzalā

“As though the sun’s rays at its rising, in the clear weather of the rising of the star al-Aʿzal…” This explanatory rendering follows the lexical commentary; the following line completes the image with light reflected on the armor.

The image describes shining armor. A lexical commentary explains the sun’s rays in the clear weather associated with the rising of al-Simāk al-Aʿzal. The second hemistich should therefore not simply be rendered as an open, unobstructed space. Its transmitted wording varies; the clear image is sunlight reflected on armor.

Qurūn al-shams refers here to the sun’s projecting rays. The expression relates the celestial body to its light without making their names identical.

These transmitted pre-Islamic witnesses show the sun as a natural phenomenon. This study has not independently dated each line, and they do not establish when the name shams first arose.

The name’s antiquity and earlier naming practices

Related names for the sun occur in other Semitic languages, including Hebrew shemesh and Akkadian shamshu. Such parallels are relevant to the name’s antiquity.

They place it in a very old linguistic family; its origin should not be explained solely through an Arabic verb without historical evidence.

Shams is also familiar in naming traditions, including the Arabic personal name ʿAbd Shams. Particular inscriptional claims need identified texts and dates; no precise inscriptional dating is established here.

Such naming and religious traditions reflect the sun’s importance. A solar divine name or personal name does not mean the word originally designated only an idol.

Three uses must be distinguished: the sun’s ordinary name, a proper divine name in particular cultures, and a component of personal names.

In the Quranic passages considered, al-shams names the sun. In accounts of its worship, that celestial body becomes an object of worship; “deity” is not automatically its lexical meaning.

Sunshine, sunny days, and warmth

Classical Arabic uses shamasa yawmunā and ashmasa yawmunā for a day on which the sun is visible and shining.

Yawmun shāmisun means a sunny day, with visible sunlight unobscured by cloud. Some uses also emphasize its heat.

Tashammasa describes sitting in the sun or exposing oneself to sunlight.

Mushammas can describe something exposed to the sun or dried and prepared by it.

These uses directly concern the sun. Its radiation can warm, illuminate, or dry something, but these distinct effects should not each become an indispensable part of the name’s definition.

Shamūs: skittishness and resistance to restraint

Another branch appears in shamasati al-dābbah and the adjective shamūs.

Shamūs describes a skittish or unruly animal that resists settling, evades restraint, or is difficult to ride.

Shimās likewise concerns unruliness, resistance, and refusal of restraint.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī reads:

شُمُسٌ مَوَانِعُ كُلِّ لَيْلَةٍ حُرَّةٍ

shumusun mawāniʿu kulli laylatin ḥurratin

يُخْلِفْنَ ظَنَّ الْفَاحِشِ الْمِغْيَارِ

yukhlifna ẓanna al-fāḥishi al-mighyāri

Translation:

“Reserved and withholding, they guard themselves each night and confound the suspicions of the foul-minded, excessively jealous man.” This is an explanatory rendering; laylah ḥurrah also has a specific marital usage in the dictionaries.

Shumus is a plural adjective of reserve and refusal of unwelcome intimacy. The poetic praise concerns chastity. Dictionaries explain laylah ḥurrah as a wedding night without consummation; it should not simply be glossed as any ordinary night.

This branch is not invariably negative. An animal’s skittishness may trouble its handler, human defiance may be blamed, and resisting improper intimacy may be praised in another setting.

One early lexical explanation connects the sun’s continual movement with an unruly animal’s restlessness. That analogy can make the relationship imaginable without establishing historical derivation.

The family should therefore not be forced into one historical image. The solar name and the adjective of resistance each require their own evidence and uses.

2. Quranic forms and core meaning

All Quranic occurrences belong to the noun shams: 33 occurrences in 32 verses.

It usually appears as definite al-shams, sometimes with a conjunction or preposition, as in wa-l-shamsi, bi-l-shamsi, and li-l-shamsi.

Al-Insān 76:13 also has indefinite shamsan.

These are not separate derivatives. Conjunctions, prepositions, and case endings do not create fundamentally different meanings of the sun’s name.

Arabic forms such as shamasa, ashmasa, tashammasa, shāmis, shamūs, and shimās do not occur in the Quran.

Shams is grammatically feminine. Associated verbs and pronouns therefore often take feminine forms, including ṭalaʿat, gharabat, tajrī, and kuwwirat.

Grammatical gender makes no biological claim about the sun.

The noun’s Quranic core is clear:

The sun: the familiar celestial body whose light and warmth affect the earth and whose observed rising, setting, and regular motion are associated with human cycles of time.

The celestial body is central to this definition. Light, warmth, and motion are its properties and effects, described with distinct words.

3. Light, motion, and created order

Shams alongside ḍiyāʾ, nūr, and sirāj

Yūnus 10:5 states:

هُوَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ الشَّمْسَ ضِيَاءً وَالْقَمَرَ نُورًا

huwa alladhī jaʿala al-shamsa ḍiyāʾan wa-l-qamara nūrā

Yūnus 10:5

Translation:

“He is the One who made the sun radiance and the moon light.”

Shams names the sun; ḍiyāʾ characterizes its radiance.

Qamar names the moon, while nūr expresses its light.

The bodies are thus distinguished from their luminous properties.

Shams does not mean ḍiyāʾ, just as qamar does not mean nūr.

Nūḥ 71:16 also states:

وَجَعَلَ الْقَمَرَ فِيهِنَّ نُورًا وَجَعَلَ الشَّمْسَ سِرَاجًا

wa-jaʿala al-qamara fīhinna nūran wa-jaʿala al-shamsa sirājā

Nūḥ 71:16

Translation:

“And made the moon a light among them, and made the sun a lamp.”

Sirāj is a lamp or means of illumination. The sun is described as a lamp: a body whose radiation supplies light.

This connects the sun with illumination, but the entire physical mechanism of solar energy cannot be extracted from sirāj.

Modern knowledge distinguishes the sun’s emitted radiation from the moon’s visible reflected sunlight. This is compatible with the descriptions, but does not prove that ḍiyāʾ and nūr are exclusively reserved throughout Arabic for emitted and reflected light respectively.

For independent discussion, see Forqan’s studies of Ḍ W ʾ and N W R.

Study of Ḍ W ʾ; Study of N W R

The sun and timekeeping

Al-Anʿām 6:96 describes the sun and moon with ḥusbānan.

Al-Raḥmān 55:5 states:

الشَّمْسُ وَالْقَمَرُ بِحُسْبَانٍ

al-shamsu wa-l-qamaru bi-ḥusbān

Al-Raḥmān 55:5

Translation:

“The sun and moon follow a reckoning.”

Ḥusbān comes from Ḥ S B and concerns counting, reckoning, and calculable order.

These verses associate the sun and moon with an order through which time and recurring periods can be recognized.

Sunrise and sunset are familiar markers of the day. Together with the moon’s apparent motion, they help people recognize and organize recurring periods.

Reckoning and order are expressed by ḥusbān, not by SH M S itself.

The sun’s name alone yields neither the number of days in a year, orbital dimensions, nor a particular calendar.

Solar motion and an appointed order

Al-Raʿd 13:2 states:

وَسَخَّرَ الشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ كُلٌّ يَجْرِي لِأَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى

wa-sakhkhara al-shamsa wa-l-qamara kullun yajrī li-ajalin musammā

Al-Raʿd 13:2

Translation:

“And subjected the sun and moon; each runs to an appointed term.”

Similar wording occurs in Luqmān 31:29, Fāṭir 35:13, and Al-Zumar 39:5.

The bodies belong to an order in which their movement and term are related to divine determination and command.

Ibrāhīm 14:33 calls them dāʾibayn: continuously pursuing their appointed course.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:54 and Al-Naḥl 16:12 likewise associate celestial bodies with subjection to divine command. Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:61 recalls this order in addressing people who acknowledge The God as creator of the heavens and earth.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:33 states:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ اللَّيْلَ وَالنَّهَارَ وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ كُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ

wa-huwa alladhī khalaqa al-layla wa-l-nahāra wa-l-shamsa wa-l-qamara kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:33

Translation:

“He is the One who created night and day, the sun and moon; each swims in an orbit.”

Yā-Sīn 36:38 also states:

وَالشَّمْسُ تَجْرِي لِمُسْتَقَرٍّ لَهَا

wa-l-shamsu tajrī li-mustaqarrin lahā

Yā Sīn 36:38

Translation:

“And the sun runs to an appointed resting place or term of its own.”

Tajrī expresses motion; mustaqarr concerns a resting place, settled location, or appointed destination or term.

The expression may be read spatially or temporally in context, but alone it does not identify coordinates in space or a particular astronomical mechanism.

Solar movement should neither be confined to apparent sunrise and sunset nor treated as a lexical statement of every detail of galactic motion.

Scientific knowledge of solar movement is independently useful for understanding nature; shams still simply names the sun.

The sun and moon’s distinct order

Yā-Sīn 36:40 states:

لَا الشَّمْسُ يَنْبَغِي لَهَا أَنْ تُدْرِكَ الْقَمَرَ وَلَا اللَّيْلُ سَابِقُ النَّهَارِ

lā al-shamsu yanbaghī lahā an tudrika al-qamara wa-lā al-laylu sābiqu al-nahār

Yā Sīn 36:40

Translation:

“It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does night precede day.”

The verse then describes each swimming in an orbit.

It presents the sun, moon, night, and day within a differentiated order.

Tudrika, from D R K, means to reach or overtake. The precise relationship intended between their movements and cycles requires consideration of the whole verse.

The expression does not alone establish that the sun and moon can never align visually or that eclipses cannot occur.

Neither the word shams nor the construction alone supports such a conclusion.

The sun and shadow

Al-Furqān 25:45 states:

ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَا الشَّمْسَ عَلَيْهِ دَلِيلًا

thumma jaʿalnā al-shamsa ʿalayhi dalīlā

Al-Furqān 25:45

Translation:

“Then We made the sun a guide to it.”

The pronoun in ʿalayhi refers to the shadow mentioned earlier in the verse.

The sun is related to shadow: sunlight and the sun’s apparent position help reveal shadows, their extension, and their change.

Being a “guide” is the sun’s role in relation to shadow here, not the noun’s lexical meaning.

The Quran attends not only to the sun as a luminous body but also to its effects in life and observation.

4. Timekeeping and Quranic narratives

Rising and setting

Ṭulūʿ al-shams means sunrise; ghurūb al-shams means its setting on the visible horizon.

Ṭā-Hā 20:130 states:

قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ الشَّمْسِ وَقَبْلَ غُرُوبِهَا

qabla ṭulūʿi al-shamsi wa-qabla ghurūbihā

Ṭā Hā 20:130

Translation:

“Before sunrise and before its setting.”

Qāf 50:39 likewise uses sunrise and sunset to mark times of glorification.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 uses li-dulūki al-shams in the command to establish prayer.

Dulūk, from D L K, concerns the sun’s movement away from a daily position. Interpretations differ between its decline after midday and sunset.

That question belongs to the study of dulūk and the verse. Shams itself determines neither that interpretation nor every detail of prayer times.

The sun here is a natural time marker. Rising, setting, and dulūk are events or states involving it, not separate meanings of its name.

The sun in Abraham’s story

Al-Anʿām 6:78 states:

فَلَمَّا رَأَى الشَّمْسَ بَازِغَةً قَالَ هَٰذَا رَبِّي هَٰذَا أَكْبَرُ

fa-lammā raʾā al-shamsa bāzighatan qāla hādhā rabbī hādhā akbar

Al-Anʿām 6:78

Translation:

“When he saw the sun rising, he said: This is my Lord; this is greater.”

When it sets, the verse continues, Abraham disowns what his people associate with The God.

Bāzighatan describes its rising, and afalat its setting or disappearance.

These belong to independent roots and should not be absorbed into the definition of shams.

The masculine demonstrative hādhā in the quoted speech, despite shams being feminine, does not establish a physical sex for the sun. It can refer to the observed body or object; the construction deserves separate grammatical analysis.

In Al-Baqarah 2:258, Abraham tells the person disputing with him about his Lord:

فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ يَأْتِي بِالشَّمْسِ مِنَ الْمَشْرِقِ فَأْتِ بِهَا مِنَ الْمَغْرِبِ

fa-inna allāha yaʾtī bi-l-shamsi mina al-mashriqi fa-ʾti bihā mina al-maghrib

Al-Baqarah 2:258

Translation:

“The God brings the sun from the east; then bring it from the west.”

The familiar order of sunrise serves Abraham’s argument.

Shams remains the sun; mashriq and maghrib identify directions or places of rising and setting.

Ordinary speech about the sun rising in the east does not constitute a complete theory of geocentrism or the structure of all celestial bodies.

People today still speak of sunrise and sunset while knowing that the earth moves: these are observable events from an earthly viewpoint.

For the distinction between rising, shining, and the east, see Forqan’s study of SH R Q.

Study of SH R Q

The cave companions and Dhū al-Qarnayn

Al-Kahf 18:17 describes sunrise and sunset relative to the companions’ cave.

At sunrise the sun inclines to one side of the cave; at sunset it passes on the other side.

This concerns the cave, illumination, and the apparent solar path. Shams alone does not identify the cave’s geographical location or precise orientation.

Al-Kahf 18:86 says of Dhū al-Qarnayn:

وَجَدَهَا تَغْرُبُ فِي عَيْنٍ حَمِئَةٍ

wajadahā taghrubu fī ʿaynin ḥamiʾah

Al-Kahf 18:86

Translation:

“He found it setting in a muddy spring.”

The pronoun refers to the sun, within wajadahā: what Dhū al-Qarnayn encountered from his position as observer.

Reading this as an observed scene does not entail the sun’s physical body entering an earthly spring. This is an interpretation of the narrative setting, not part of the lexical meaning of shams.

In Al-Kahf 18:90 he reaches the sun’s rising place and encounters people for whom no covering against it has been provided.

This concerns their exposure to the sun. Shams itself does not mean burning, exposure, or a particular climate.

The sun in Joseph’s dream

Yūsuf 12:4 states:

إِنِّي رَأَيْتُ أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ رَأَيْتُهُمْ لِي سَاجِدِينَ

innī raʾaytu aḥada ʿashara kawkaban wa-l-shamsa wa-l-qamara raʾaytuhum lī sājidīn

Yūsuf 12:4

Translation:

“I saw eleven stars, and the sun and moon; I saw them prostrating to me.”

The sun is part of Joseph’s dream.

Shams still names the sun within the dream, although its place in the dream image may be symbolic.

The image seen must be distinguished from its interpretation. Which person or family relationship the sun represents is not the word’s lexical meaning.

Its brightness and prominence also make the sun available for literary comparison and imagery without altering its ordinary meaning.

5. The sun as a sign of creation and the rejection of its worship

Many Quranic passages name the sun with the moon, stars, night, and day as signs of creation.

Al-Ḥajj 22:18 includes the sun and moon among those that prostrate to The God.

Their prostration should not be confined without evidence to the human bodily movement in prayer. Prostration and the obedience of different creatures require their own lexical and Quranic study.

In Al-Naml 27:24, the hoopoe says of the people of Sheba:

وَجَدْتُهَا وَقَوْمَهَا يَسْجُدُونَ لِلشَّمْسِ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ

wajadtuhā wa-qawmahā yasjudūna li-l-shamsi min dūni allāh

Al-Naml 27:24

Translation:

“I found her and her people prostrating to the sun instead of The God.”

The sun has become an object of human worship.

The Quran distinguishes the created sun from the sun adopted by people as a deity.

Fuṣṣilat 41:37 makes the distinction explicit:

لَا تَسْجُدُوا لِلشَّمْسِ وَلَا لِلْقَمَرِ وَاسْجُدُوا لِلَّهِ الَّذِي خَلَقَهُنَّ

lā tasjudū li-l-shamsi wa-lā li-l-qamari wa-sjudū li-llāhi alladhī khalaqahunna

Fuṣṣilat 41:37

Translation:

“Do not prostrate to the sun or moon; prostrate to The God who created them.”

The same verse first identifies the sun and moon as His signs.

The sun’s greatness, light, order, and influence on human life do not make it worthy of worship. The prohibition is not restricted to worship that claims it is independent of its creator.

A sign of the creator’s power and governance does not replace the creator.

This distinction also matters lexically. SH M S does not mean worship, divinity, or sovereignty; these concern a relationship people establish with the sun.

6. Resurrection and the world to come

The sun transformed

Al-Takwīr 81:1 states:

إِذَا الشَّمْسُ كُوِّرَتْ

idhā al-shamsu kuwwirat

Al-Takwīr 81:1

Translation:

“When the sun is wound up.”

Kuwwirat comes from K W R, concerning winding and gathering something into a wrapped form.

The sun is described in a condition unlike its familiar present order.

Neither the verb nor modern stellar science should be made to specify every physical detail of this transformation.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:9 also states:

وَجُمِعَ الشَّمْسُ وَالْقَمَرُ

wa-jumiʿa al-shamsu wa-l-qamar

Al-Qiyāmah 75:9

Translation:

“And the sun and moon are gathered together.”

Gathering is conveyed by jumiʿa, not by shams.

Both verses describe a transformation of the familiar celestial order, but the sun’s name does not thereby designate another kind of being.

Without independent evidence, gathering the sun and moon should not be asserted to specify the exact form or mechanism of a physical collision.

Not seeing the sun in the description of the garden

Al-Insān 76:13 states:

لَا يَرَوْنَ فِيهَا شَمْسًا وَلَا زَمْهَرِيرًا

lā yarawna fīhā shamsan wa-lā zamharīrā

Al-Insān 76:13

Translation:

“There they see neither sun nor bitter cold.”

Shamsan appears without the definite article.

It stands beside zamharīr, severe cold. The juxtaposition contributes to the description of heavenly comfort.

In this setting, the sun may evoke the felt radiation and heat of earthly experience, while the word remains the sun’s name.

For a direct translation, “sun” preserves the wording. Rendering it as “scorching heat” is an explanatory interpretation replacing the body with one of its effects; that interpretive step should be clear.

Not seeing the sun here does not alone establish an absence of all light, or precisely how light and time compare with earthly conditions.

The oath by the sun and its brightness

Al-Shams 91:1 opens:

وَالشَّمْسِ وَضُحَاهَا

wa-l-shamsi wa-ḍuḥāhā

Al-Shams 91:1

Translation:

“By the sun and its morning brightness.”

Shams names the sun; ḍuḥāhā concerns its brightness and the time of its manifest light in the earlier part of the day.

Al-Shams 91:2 mentions the moon following it.

The oaths extend to day, night, heaven, earth, and the human self, before addressing the self’s purification or corruption.

The sun belongs to the natural signs that draw attention within this structure.

Shams itself does not mean purity, guidance, or goodness. Such connections arise from the surah as a whole and its sequence of oaths.

7. Comparison with related roots

SH M S and Q M R

Shams names the sun; qamar names the moon.

Both are celestial bodies repeatedly named together, but their names are independent.

Their difference is not merely stronger and weaker light. They are different bodies, each with its place in observation, apparent movement, and timekeeping.

Yūnus 10:5 characterizes their light with ḍiyāʾ and nūr. Other passages discuss their motion and order.

The sun’s name should therefore not be equated with daylight, nor the moon’s with night light.

SH M S and Ḍ W ʾ

Shams names the sun; ḍawʾ and ḍiyāʾ concern light and radiance.

The sun has radiance, but not all radiance is the sun. Lamps and fire also give light.

Ḍiyāʾ should therefore not be defined exclusively as sunlight.

Yūnus 10:5 makes the distinction clear: the sun is what is described, and radiance its characterization.

SH M S and N W R

Nūr has a broad range: perceptible light and, elsewhere, guidance or spiritual illumination.

Shams instead names a particular body.

The sun provides light, but not all light belongs to the sun.

The general meaning of nūr can describe illumination, while Quranic uses also concern divine light and guidance. These meanings cannot all be reduced to the sun; the Quran explicitly calls the sun ḍiyāʾ and sirāj in the passages cited.

SH M S and S R J

Sirāj means a lamp or means of illumination.

Nūḥ 71:16 calls the sun a sirāj, relating it to a lamp through its illumination.

One thing can be characterized with another noun without those nouns becoming complete lexical synonyms.

The sun is described as a lamp in creation, but SH M S does not mean a manufactured lamp or implement.

SH M S and SH R Q

Shams is the sun’s name.

SH R Q in solar uses concerns rising, shining, and becoming bright. Mashriq names the direction or place of sunrise.

Al-shams names the body; al-mashriq names its familiar direction or place of rising.

Their co-occurrence, as in Al-Baqarah 2:258, does not make them synonymous.

SH M S and Ṭ L ʿ

Ṭulūʿ concerns appearing and rising. It can apply to the sun, moon, and other things.

Ṭā-Hā 20:130 uses ṭulūʿ al-shams for sunrise. The body’s name and the act of rising are distinct concepts.

Rising is an observable event involving the sun, not the lexical meaning of its name.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The natural Persian equivalent of shams is khurshīd, “sun.”

It can be retained throughout the Quran: creation, Abraham’s story, Joseph’s dream, celestial movement, and resurrection.

Important expressions may be rendered as follows:

  • Al-shams: the sun
  • Ṭulūʿ al-shams: sunrise or the rising of the sun
  • Ghurūb al-shams: sunset or the setting of the sun
  • Al-shamsu tajrī: the sun runs or moves
  • Al-shamsu wa-l-qamaru bi-ḥusbān: the sun and moon follow a reckoning
  • Wa-l-shamsi wa-ḍuḥāhā: by the sun and its morning brightness
  • Lā yarawna fīhā shamsan: there they see no sun

Without contextual grounds, a direct translation should not replace “sun” with “star,” “center of the solar system,” “energy source,” “solar deity,” or “daylight.” These may belong in scientific, historical, or interpretive explanation; shams itself names the sun. The proposals here do not change installed verse translations.

Dreams and figurative passages should likewise retain the sun’s name, with possible symbolism explained separately.

Conclusion

The Arabic family has two important branches: the sun’s name and uses concerning skittishness, unruliness, and resistance to control.

Shams names the celestial body whose radiation gives light and warmth, and whose observed movement and position relate to time. This pre-Islamic meaning remains in the Quranic uses.

Shamūs describes a skittish animal or a person resisting approach or control. Earlier poetry attests this branch, but its precise historical relationship with the solar name is not established.

The Quran uses only the noun shams. It appears with radiance, light, and lamp imagery; in movement and reckoning; in time and direction; in the stories of Abraham, Joseph, and Dhū al-Qarnayn; and in resurrection scenes.

Varied settings do not create varied lexical definitions. The word consistently names the familiar body. Motion, radiation, prostration, subjection, reckoning, rising, setting, and transformation come from associated verbs, descriptions, and context.

The Quran also distinguishes the sun as a sign of creation from its adoption as an object of worship. Its immense effects do not confer divine standing or entitlement to worship.

The final Quranic definition is:

Shams is the sun: the luminous, warming celestial body described within an appointed order of creation, whose radiation and observed position affect light, timekeeping, and earthly life.

This definition should remain distinct from solar effects, metaphorical descriptions, and the family’s branch concerning skittishness. “Sun” is its basic, sufficient equivalent.
