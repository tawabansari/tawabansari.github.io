From curved sand dunes to the Qur’anic region of Al-Aḥqāf

In Arabic, Ḥ Q F concerns bending, curvature, and something extending in a shape that is not straight. Ḥiqf denotes an elongated, curved sand ridge; its plural is aḥqāf. Within this family, iḥqawqafa describes something becoming curved or bow-shaped, such as the crescent moon or an animal’s back bent while resting.

The word matters particularly in describing sandy terrain. Desert sand can accumulate in tall, elongated ridges with curved sides. Arabic uses ḥiqf for such forms. The word therefore concerns both sand and its particular shape and extension.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: bi-l-aḥqāfi in Al-Aḥqāf 46:21. It refers to a region inhabited by ʿĀd, where their messenger warned them. The Qur’an’s forty-sixth surah takes its name, Al-Aḥqāf, from this word.

Qur’anic form: «بِالْأَحْقَافِ», the plural of ḥiqf with the preposition bi-, in Al-Aḥqāf 46:21.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence of Ḥ Q F in Al-Aḥqāf 46:21

Other forms recorded in Arabic include «حِقْف», «حِقَاف», «حُقُوف», «حَاقِف», «أَحْقَف», «احْقَوْقَفَ», and «مُحْقَوْقِف», but these forms do not occur as such in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic principle and early Arabic usage

Curvature and extension

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for Ḥ Q F: «مَيْلُ الشَّيْءِ وَعِوَجُهُ», a thing’s inclination and bending.

Bending here does not mean a defect, corruption, or moral deviation. It concerns the physical shape of something whose extension is curved rather than straight.

Ibn Fāris then uses iḥqawqafa for something that has bent, relating ḥiqf to a sand ridge with that shape.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah transmits Al-Aṣmaʿī’s explanation of ḥiqf as curved sand. In explaining the Qur’anic use, Al-Farrāʾ describes an elongated, elevated sand ridge.

These accounts identify two important features of the familiar meaning of ḥiqf: the ridge’s extension and its curvature.

The root’s semantic principle can therefore be understood through a thing’s curved shape. Ḥiqf itself, however, does not name every curved object: its principal use denotes a kind of sand ridge.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: curvature, ḥāqif, and muḥqawqif

Ḥiqf: a curved sand ridge

Ḥiqf denotes a sand ridge with an elongated, curved shape.

Lexical sources describe it through height, extension, and curvature.

It may rise above the surrounding ground without being a mountain. Some early explanations expressly describe it as mountain-like sand that falls short of a mountain.

Not every sandy rise should therefore automatically be called a ḥiqf: the particular use also takes account of a familiar shape.

Nor does ḥiqf simply mean desert terrain or any surface covered in sand. A sandy plain can be almost flat, whereas ḥiqf concerns a particular raised, extended form.

The Persian equivalents “sand hill” or “sandy ridge” are suitable, with extension and curvature explained more precisely in the accompanying discussion.

Aḥqāf: the plural of sand ridges

Aḥqāf is the plural of ḥiqf. Other recorded plurals include ḥiqāf and ḥuqūf.

These plurals may describe a collection of sandy ridges or rises.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:21, however, al-aḥqāf is also used for a particular region in the account of ʿĀd, rather than merely a general collection of sand hills.

This distinction matters. The lexical meaning concerns the terrain’s shape; its use as a place name identifies a particular land.

As regions can take their names from natural features, Al-Aḥqāf may likewise be a name associated with the area’s sand ridges.

The lexical meaning alone does not establish that region’s precise geographical coordinates.

Iḥqawqafa: becoming curved

Arabic uses iḥqawqafa for something becoming curved or bow-shaped.

Lisān al-ʿArab records it for sand, the crescent moon, and a camel’s back.

Iḥqawqafa al-raml describes a sand ridge curving along its length.

Iḥqawqafa al-hilāl concerns a crescent whose curvature is apparent.

For an animal’s back, the verb can describe bending and arching.

These uses show that curvature in the family extends beyond the terrain. Different things may share a feature of shape and be described through derivatives of the same root.

Iḥqawqafa nevertheless does not necessarily mean becoming a sand dune. In describing the moon or an animal’s back, the relevant feature is curvature.

Ḥāqif: an animal bent or resting at a sand ridge

Arabic uses ẓabyun ḥāqifun of a gazelle with its body bent while resting.

Early explanations offer two possibilities: the gazelle has settled at a ḥiqf, a sand ridge, or its back and body are bent during sleep or rest.

These explanations do not involve the same semantic relationship.

The first concerns the animal’s location; the second concerns its bent shape.

Neither should therefore be generalised to every use of ḥāqif without examining construction and context.

Lexical sources also record jamalun aḥqafu for a camel with a particular bodily condition. Some explain it through leanness and a hollowed appearance.

The family thus also describes animal bodies, but aḥqaf in such uses does not directly mean a sand hill.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: sand, the crescent, and animal bodies

2. Early poetic witnesses and the image of sand ridges

Ḥiqāf in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry

The Muʿallaqah of the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays is transmitted with the following reading:

فَلَمَّا أَجَزْنَا سَاحَةَ الْحَيِّ وَانْتَحَى
بِنَا بَطْنُ خَبْتٍ ذِي حِقَافٍ عَقَنْقَلِ

fa-lammā ajaznā sāḥata al-ḥayyi wa-ntaḥā / bi-nā baṭnu khabtin dhī ḥiqāfin ʿaqanqali

Meaning: “When we had crossed the tribe’s open space and reached a hollow tract of desert surrounded by tall, interwoven sand ridges.”

Khabt denotes level or low-lying ground here, while ḥiqāf concerns sand ridges.

The poem depicts ground among or beside such ridges.

Lexical explanations likewise relate ʿaqanqal to heaped, interwoven sand.

This witness matters because one transmitted version of a pre-Islamic poet’s work uses ḥiqāf to describe terrain. The date of recording a version is not the date of composition, however, and variant readings must remain part of the assessment.

The verse’s wording is not uniform. Some versions have dhī qifāfin instead of dhī ḥiqāfin; another gives a different expression for the heaped sand.

Qifāf belongs to a different family and concerns elevated ground.

The version with ḥiqāf therefore witnesses that use, but qifāf and other readings should not be concealed or one version presented as undisputed.

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary: ḥiqāf and qifāf variants in Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah

Ḥiqf in a poetic simile

Another verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

كَحِقْفِ النَّقَا يَمْشِي الْوَلِيدَانِ فَوْقَهُ
بِمَا احْتَسَبَا مِنْ لِينِ مَسٍّ وَتَسْهَالِ

ka-ḥiqfi al-naqā yamshī al-walīdāni fawqahu / bi-mā iḥtasabā min līni massin wa-tas-hāli

Translation of the image: “Like a ridge of soft sand on which two children walk, content with its soft touch and easy footing.” The poet likens the softness and shape of part of his beloved’s body to that ridge.

Ḥiqfi al-naqā denotes a sand ridge whose shape and softness are suited to the simile.

This shows that ḥiqf was a tangible, familiar literary image which could help describe the curved shape of other things.

The sand’s softness here should not become an invariant meaning of every use of the root. It comes from the sand and the purpose of the simile, while ḥiqf particularly concerns the ridge’s shape. Khizānat al-Adab also transmits this verse with ka-diʿṣi al-naqā; its editor identifies ka-ḥiqfi al-naqā as the collected poetry’s version. This witness also has variants.

Khizānat al-Adab: ka-diʿṣi al-naqā and the editor’s ka-ḥiqfi al-naqā variant

Iḥqawqafa describing the crescent

Al-ʿAjjāj’s poetry includes:

طَيَّ اللَّيَالِي زُلَفًا فَزُلَفَا
سَمَاوَةَ الْهِلَالِ حَتَّى احْقَوْقَفَا

ṭayya al-layālī zulafan fa-zulafā / samāwata al-hilāli ḥattā iḥqawqafā

Translation of this passage: “As the passing nights, in successive intervals, diminish the moon’s form until it bends.” In the full rajaz, a camel worn by travel is compared with the moon’s waning to a crescent. Under the reading applying iḥqawqafa to the moon, the image concerns its diminution and curvature, rather than a fixed crescent suddenly acquiring a bend.

This reading of the moon image shows curvature apart from sand. Grammatical commentaries differ over samāwata al-hilāl and the passage’s construction, so one analysis should not be presented as the only possibility.

Al-ʿAjjāj was a poet of the Islamic period. This witness should therefore not be called pre-Islamic poetry.

It matters as evidence of the verb’s broader Arabic use, rather than proof that the use must predate the Qur’an.

Sharḥ Kitāb Sībawayh: the camel and moon in Al-ʿAjjāj’s rajaz and differing grammatical analyses

Together, these poetic witnesses show ḥiqf denoting a curved sand ridge, with the feature of curvature also applicable to other things.

3. «بِالْأَحْقَافِ»: the Qur’anic use

وَاذْكُرْ أَخَا عَادٍ إِذْ أَنْذَرَ قَوْمَهُ بِالْأَحْقَافِ وَقَدْ خَلَتِ النُّذُرُ مِنْ بَيْنِ يَدَيْهِ وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِ أَلَّا تَعْبُدُوا إِلَّا اللَّهَ إِنِّي أَخَافُ عَلَيْكُمْ عَذَابَ يَوْمٍ عَظِيمٍ

wa-dhkur akhā ʿādin idh andhara qawmahu bi-l-aḥqāfi wa-qad khalati al-nudhuru min bayni yadayhi wa-min khalfihi allā taʿbudū illā allāha innī akhāfu ʿalaykum ʿadhāba yawmin ʿaẓīmin

Translation: And remember the brother of ʿĀd, when he warned his people among the sand dunes—warners had already passed before him and after him—saying: “Worship none but The God. I fear for you the punishment of a tremendous day.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:21

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Bi-l-aḥqāfi consists of the preposition bi- and the definite noun al-aḥqāf.

Al-Aḥqāf is the plural of ḥiqf, while also identifying the region occupied by ʿĀd here. The noun is genitive after bi- in this construction.

Bi- identifies where the warning occurred: the brother of ʿĀd warned his people in that region.

It does not indicate the instrument of warning here; the verse does not say that the messenger used the sand ridges to warn them.

Al-Aḥqāf: place name or description of terrain?

Two aspects should be distinguished.

Lexically, al-aḥqāf denotes a collection of elongated, curved sand ridges.

In the sentence, it names the region where ʿĀd received the warning.

Such uses are natural in geographical names: a familiar feature of the land may become its name.

This does not make every place with curved sand ridges the Qur’an’s Al-Aḥqāf.

Nor does the name’s lexical meaning locate it precisely in history.

The verse gives no geographical coordinates, distance from other towns, or political boundary for Al-Aḥqāf.

Lexical witnesses can therefore illuminate the natural terrain, while identifying the historical location requires further evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Aḥqāf 46:21: sand ridges and the place of warning

The brother of ʿĀd and the place of warning

Al-Aḥqāf 46:21 introduces the messenger as akhā ʿādin, the brother of ʿĀd.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:65 and Hūd 11:50 name Hūd in connection with ʿĀd.

Reading these verses together identifies Hūd as the messenger concerned.

Ḥ Q F itself, however, means neither Hūd, ʿĀd, nor warning. The word identifies where the event occurred.

The warning’s central message follows in the verse: worshipping none besides The God, and warning of a tremendous day’s punishment.

The geographical meaning of Al-Aḥqāf should therefore not be changed into disbelief, associating partners, punishment, or destruction.

Those concepts concern ʿĀd’s story and the warning they received, rather than the lexical meaning of sand ridges.

The following verses

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:22, ʿĀd responds to its messenger by demanding the punishment with which they have been threatened.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:23, Hūd states that knowledge of it belongs to The God and that his task is to convey the message.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:24 describes something the people suppose is a rain-bearing cloud, but identifies a wind bringing painful punishment.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:25 then describes their destruction.

This sequence makes Al-Aḥqāf the story’s spatial setting; the place name itself does not determine the kind of punishment.

Wind, cloud, rain, and destruction each appear in separate expressions.

Ḥ Q F therefore does not establish that the dunes caused their destruction or that a particular sandstorm must have occurred.

The manner of punishment must be studied through the wind-related expressions and other verses recounting ʿĀd’s story.

4. Al-Aḥqāf’s geographical location: what is and is not established

Early geographical and commentarial accounts give differing locations for Al-Aḥqāf.

Some relate it to southern Arabian sands and land near Ḥaḍramawt.

One report identifies the region between ʿUmān and Mahrah; another places ʿĀd’s settlements in sands between ʿUmān and Ḥaḍramawt.

Some early explanations also mention Al-Shiḥr on the southern Arabian coast.

Not all reports concern southern Arabia, however: some mention locations in Al-Shām or the region of Ḥismā.

These differences show that the precise historical location was not an entirely settled question within historical and commentarial tradition.

Al-Qurṭubī: reports concerning ʿUmān, Mahrah, Al-Shiḥr, Ḥaḍramawt, and Ḥismā

Lexical evidence and geographical reports

Two kinds of evidence should be distinguished.

The first concerns Arabic meaning: lexical witnesses relate aḥqāf to elongated, curved sand ridges.

The second identifies which historical region bore that name.

Meaning alone cannot answer the second question. Historical and geographical accounts, and any other independent evidence available, must be considered.

Several regions may have similar sand ridges. Their presence in a region alone does not prove that ʿĀd lived there.

After reporting several locations, Al-Ṭabarī stresses the familiar Arabic meaning of aḥqāf without considering it necessary to determine one proposed site conclusively.

This matters methodologically: confidence in a lexical meaning should not be converted into certainty about a historical location.

Al-Ṭabarī: distinguishing lexical meaning from historical location

Did Al-Aḥqāf mean high mountains?

Some reports explain Al-Aḥqāf as a mountain or mountain-like elevations.

Al-Farrāʾ and other lexicographers, however, describe ḥiqf as an elongated, elevated sand ridge.

Some explanations say these ridges can resemble mountains while falling short of mountains in the ordinary sense.

Translating them simply as “mountains,” without their sandy material and particular shape, therefore loses an important feature.

Nor does Al-Aḥqāf itself mean rocky mountain ranges.

“Sand hills” or “sand ridges” fits the lexical evidence more clearly.

Why the exact location does not determine the root’s meaning

A root study begins with the word, rather than an assumed map.

If independent historical and archaeological evidence eventually clarifies Al-Aḥqāf’s location, that will matter for understanding ʿĀd’s history.

Such a discovery would not by itself change the meaning of ḥiqf.

Just as disagreement over an ancient city’s location does not necessarily alter the meaning of the word from which its name comes, disagreement about Al-Aḥqāf should not displace the clear meaning of sand ridges.

Translation and lexical study should therefore preserve the name and the natural image, while presenting geographical identification with its actual degree of confidence.

5. Distinctions from related roots and words

Ḥ Q F (ḥiqf) and K Th B (kathīb)

Kathīb denotes a heap or mound of sand in Arabic.

Ḥiqf is also a sand ridge, but familiar explanations place more emphasis on its elongated, curved shape.

They overlap in the field of sandy terrain without necessarily being exact synonyms.

Al-Muzzammil 73:14 uses kathīban mahīlan for mountains in a scene of tremendous transformation.

It has a semantic relationship with Al-Aḥqāf through sand, but comes from a different root and occurs in a different context.

Ḥ Q F (ḥiqf) and R M L (raml)

Raml concerns sand and sandy terrain in Arabic.

Ḥiqf concerns a particular form of heaped, raised sand.

Thus a ḥiqf is a kind of sand ridge in familiar usage, but not every sandy surface or desert is necessarily a ḥiqf.

Rendering Al-Aḥqāf only as “desert” omits some of its particular meaning.

“Sand hills” or “sand ridges” gives a more precise image.

Ḥ Q F (ḥiqf) and J B L (jabal)

Jabal denotes a mountain and may describe large, firm elevations.

The principal use of ḥiqf concerns sand with an elongated, curved shape.

Large sand ridges may look mountain-like, but that resemblance does not make the words identical.

Lexical accounts likewise distinguish a ḥiqf from a mountain.

Translating Al-Aḥqāf as rocky mountains or mountainous country is therefore unsuitable without independent evidence.

Study of J B L

Ḥ Q F (ḥiqf) and Q F F (qifāf)

One reading of Imruʾ al-Qays’s verse has qifāf instead of ḥiqāf.

Qifāf concerns elevated ground and is close to ḥiqāf in subject matter.

Their roots nevertheless differ: Q F F and Ḥ Q F.

Two variants in one verse do not establish identical roots or meanings; they show that both words could suit that landscape image.

Lexical study should preserve the variant rather than replacing one form with the other without investigation.

Ḥ Q F (ḥiqf) and Ḥ Q B (ḥuqub)

Qur’anic Ḥ Q B concerns a period of time.

In Al-Kahf 18:60, ḥuquban denotes a long period; in Al-Nabaʾ 78:23, aḥqāban denotes multiple temporal periods.

Ḥ Q F in Al-Aḥqāf 46:21 instead concerns the terrain and the name of a sandy region.

The roots differ only in their final letter, but have distinct fields in their Qur’anic uses.

Aḥqāb in Al-Nabaʾ should therefore not be confused with aḥqāf in Al-Aḥqāf: the former concerns periods, the latter sand ridges.

Study of Ḥ Q B

Ḥ Q F (ḥiqf) and Ḥ Q Q (ḥaqq)

Ḥ Q Q concerns right, truth, stability, and realisation.

Ḥ Q F in its familiar meaning concerns curvature and the shape of sand ridges.

Two shared initial letters do not establish a direct semantic relationship.

Al-Aḥqāf therefore does not lexically mean the land of truth, right, or the fulfilment of a divine promise.

ʿĀd’s Qur’anic story may concern truth and divine judgment, but those ideas come from its context rather than from Ḥ Q F.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Bi-l-aḥqāfi in Al-Aḥqāf 46:21 identifies a region associated with sand ridges.

“Among the sand dunes” is a natural, clear, defensible base rendering.

The Persian equivalent preserves the word’s tangible general meaning and is accessible to a Persian reader.

“Among the curved sand ridges” is lexically more explicit, but may make the Persian verse cumbersome.

The base translation need not state every detail of the terrain. The root article can explain that the ridges have particular extension and curvature.

“In the land of Al-Aḥqāf” is also possible, particularly when preserving the historical place name is intended.

If chosen, an explanation should identify aḥqāf as the plural of ḥiqf and the name of a region with sand ridges.

Forqan’s existing Persian “among the sand dunes” is appropriate; there is no need to replace it with a geographical name whose precise site is uncertain.

Outside the Qur’an, ḥiqf can be rendered “curved sand ridge”; ḥiqāf and ḥuqūf, “sand ridges”; iḥqawqafa, “became curved” or “arched”; and ḥāqif, according to context, an animal with its body bent or settled at a sandy place.

Conclusion

Ḥ Q F concerns curvature and a thing’s bent shape in Arabic. Ibn Fāris identifies one semantic principle. Lexical sources record sand, the crescent moon, and an animal’s bent body within this field.

Ḥiqf principally denotes an elongated, curved sand ridge; its plural is aḥqāf. Early poetic witnesses likewise show its use for terrain and sand ridges.

Only bi-l-aḥqāfi occurs in the Qur’an, in Al-Aḥqāf 46:21. It identifies the region where ʿĀd was warned.

Its precise historical location has differing accounts. Some place it in southern Arabia near ʿUmān, Mahrah, Al-Shiḥr, and Ḥaḍramawt; others suggest different regions. The Qur’an itself provides no geographical coordinates.

The lexical meaning should therefore be distinguished from identifying the historical location.

Brief root meaning: Bending and curvature; ḥiqf is an elongated, curved sand ridge; aḥqāf is its plural and the name of a region in ʿĀd’s story.

The word itself does not mean desert in general, mountainous country, punishment, destruction, or ʿĀd. Such matters are known through the story and other evidence.

For Forqan, “sand dunes” is suitable and fluent. The root article retains a fuller explanation of the ridges’ elongated, curved form, clarifying the word without adding unstated lexical or geographical details to the verse.
