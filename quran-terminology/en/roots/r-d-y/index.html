Falling, perishing, and bringing to ruin

In Arabic, the root R D Y is associated with being thrown, falling, collapse, and destruction. Early uses concern throwing stones, falling into wells or precipices, rapid movement, and a ruinous outcome. In the Qur’an, this family expresses perishing, bringing someone else to ruin, and a fall that can lead to death.

The family distinguishes رَدِيَ (radiya), to perish; أَرْدَى (ardā), to cause to perish; and تَرَدَّى (taraddā), to fall or tumble down. These forms belong to one semantic field but are not interchangeable in every sentence.

An animal injured or killed by a fall, for example, is called مُتَرَدِّيَة (mutaraddiyah). Elsewhere, a person can be brought to ruin through a mistaken belief, following misleading desires, or another person’s influence. Those passages do not necessarily concern falling physically into a well or precipice.

The root occurs six times in six Qur’anic verses. Its forms comprise the Form I verb فَتَرْدَى (fa-tardā); Form IV verbs in لِيُرْدُوهُمْ (li-yurdūhum), لَتُرْدِينِ (la-turdīni), and أَرْدَاكُمْ (ardākum); the Form V verb تَرَدَّى (taraddā); and the active participle of that form, الْمُتَرَدِّيَة (al-mutaraddiyah).

Qur’anic forms: الْمُتَرَدِّيَةُ in Al-Māʾidah 5:3; لِيُرْدُوهُمْ in Al-Anʿām 6:137; فَتَرْدَىٰ in Ṭā Hā 20:16; لَتُرْدِينِ in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:56; أَرْدَاكُمْ in Fuṣṣilat 41:23; and تَرَدَّىٰ in Al-Layl 92:11.

Arabic also records رَدًى (radan), رَدِيَ (radiya), رَدٍ (radin), الرَّدِي (al-radī), رَدْي (rady), أَرْدَى (ardā), إِرْدَاء (irdāʾ), تَرَدٍّ (taraddin), مُتَرَدٍّ (mutaraddin), مِرْدَى (mirdā), مُرْدِيّ (murdiyy), مِرْدَاة (mirdāh), and رَدَيَان (radayān). These forms matter for the root’s semantic range but do not occur in the Qur’an in precisely these forms.

1. Semantic cores and early Arabic usage

Throwing and dislodging

One lexical explanation proposes throwing and projectile-like movement as a connection between uses of this family.

رَدَيْتُهُ بِالْحِجَارَةِ (radaytuhu bi-al-ḥijārati) means targeting someone with stones.

رَدَيْتُ الْحَجَرَ (radaytu al-ḥajara) is recorded both for throwing a stone and for striking a stone to break it.

In this branch, the word can thus concern propelling an object or its collision and impact.

مِرْدَاة (mirdāh) also names a large, heavy stone used to strike other stones or break and crush hard objects.

مُرْدِيّ (murdiyy) names a pole with which a boatman propels a boat. This vocalization should be distinguished from مِرْدَى (mirdā), a stone used as a projectile.

These examples establish the field of throwing and forceful movement in early Arabic.

They do not make every form of this root mean throwing stones. Qur’anic fa-tardā and ardākum occur where perishing and ruin are prominent, rather than stone-throwing.

A witness from the Muʿallaqah of al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah includes:

وَكَأَنَّ الْمَنُونَ تَرْدِي بِنَا أَرْعَنَ جَوْنًا يَنْجَابُ عَنْهُ الْعَمَاءُ

wa-ka-anna al-manūna tardī binā arʿana jawnan yanjābu ʿanhu al-ʿamāʾu

“It is as though fate, striking us with hardships, targets a lofty, dark mountain around which the clouds part.”

تَرْدِي (tardī) here concerns throwing and targeting.

الْمَنُون (al-manūn) refers to death or the destructive events of time. أَرْعَن (arʿan) describes a mountain with projecting parts; جَوْن (jawn) here concerns its dark color, and الْعَمَاء (al-ʿamāʾ) means cloud.

The poet likens his people to a lofty, steadfast mountain struck by the events of time as though by projectiles, yet retaining its firmness.

An early explanation of the Muʿallaqah glosses al-rady here as al-ramy: throwing.

The witness matters because the use concerning throwing and impact is not merely a later lexical reconstruction; it also appears in poetry attributed to the period before the Qur’an.

The mountain and the impact of events do not make R D Y inherently mean life’s hardships or resistance to adversity. Those concepts arise from the comparison and the verse as a whole.

The verb itself here concerns throwing.

Mirdāh: a large, heavy stone

A poem by the pre-Islamic poet Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī includes:

وَسَلْهَبَةٍ تَنْضُو الْجِيَادَ كَأَنَّهَا
رَدَاةٌ تَدَلَّتْ مِنْ فُرُوعِ يَلَمْلَمِ

wa-salhabatin tanḍū al-jiyāda ka-annahā / radātun tadallat min furūʿi Yalamlam

“And a tall mare that outstrips swift horses, as though she were a great boulder hanging down from the heights of Yalamlam.”

رَدَاة (radāh) here names a large, heavy stone.

Yalamlam is a known mountain name. To portray the horse’s size and power, the poet likens it to a massive boulder.

The verse is attributed to Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī and is also transmitted as lexical evidence for radāh. Another transmission has صُخُورِ يَلَمْلَمِ (ṣukhūri Yalamlam, the rocks of Yalamlam) instead of فُرُوعِ يَلَمْلَمِ (furūʿi Yalamlam, the heights of Yalamlam). The variation does not change the stone’s meaning.

The use of radāh shows that a name for a large, heavy stone was established within this lexical family.

It does not make every radā or taraddā mean a stone striking another object. In Qur’anic verses, the verb form and context identify the intended sense.

Falling into wells and precipices or from a height

Arabic رَدِيَ فِي الْهُوَّةِ (radiya fī al-huwwati) and تَرَدَّى فِي الْهُوَّةِ (taraddā fī al-huwwati) concern falling into a pit or precipice.

الْهُوَّة (al-huwwah) names a deep cavity or chasm into which something can fall.

Lexical usage records رَدِيَ فِي الْقَلِيبِ (radiya fī al-qalībi), falling into a well, and تَرَدَّى مِنَ الْجَبَلِ (taraddā mina al-jabali), falling from a mountain.

The material uses are clear: someone or something falls from its position, potentially to a lower place.

Falling itself, however, does not always mean dying.

A person or animal can fall into a well and survive, suffer injury, or perish from the fall.

Some uses of taraddā have an established association with a fatal fall. Careful analysis nevertheless distinguishes the act of falling from its result.

This matters in Al-Māʾidah 5:3, where the Qur’an mentions the fallen animal and an exception concerning slaughter in the same verse.

Radā: perishing and ruin

Arabic رَدِيَ يَرْدَى رَدًى (radiya yardā radan) means to perish.

الرَّدَى (al-radā) denotes destruction or perishing, while رَدٍ (radin) describes one who has perished. The definite form of the adjective is الرَّدِي (al-radī), with no doubling of the final yāʾ.

أَرْدَاهُ (ardāhu) means that someone or some cause brought another to destruction or ruin.

These uses relate to falling and being dislodged. Someone falling into a precipice may perish, which helps explain the semantic proximity of falling and destruction.

That proximity does not mean every use concerning destruction must first have referred to a physical fall and subsequently become figurative.

One lexical classification places throwing, projectile-like movement, and perishing within one family. Such a classification alone does not establish the precise historical development of the meaning.

The evidence clearly establishes both uses in Arabic: falling and perishing.

Rapid movement and secondary uses

رَدَى الْفَرَسُ (radā al-farasu) is recorded for a horse moving rapidly or running in a particular manner.

رَدَيَان (radayān) is also used for the movement and running of certain quadrupeds.

Lexical explanations connect these uses with projectile-like movement.

Other reports use forms of this family for raising one foot and hopping on the other.

The root’s Arabic field therefore extends beyond destruction to rapid movement and leaping.

None of its Qur’anic occurrences, however, simply means a horse running or a person hopping.

A secondary sense of رَدَى (radā) and أَرْدَى (ardā) concerns increasing, such as exceeding a specified number. Poetic witnesses are transmitted for it, but some vary in wording: ardā is sometimes reported alongside readings such as أَرْبَى (arbā) or أَرْمَى (armā).

This should be recognized as a recorded branch requiring care, rather than made the basis for defining every form of the root.

The Qur’anic uses under examination do not express an increase in quantity.

2. Al-mutaraddiyah: the fallen animal in Al-Māʾidah

One of the root’s clearest material uses occurs in Al-Māʾidah 5:3; an excerpt:

حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةُ وَالدَّمُ وَلَحْمُ الْخِنْزِيرِ وَمَا أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ بِهِ وَالْمُنْخَنِقَةُ وَالْمَوْقُوذَةُ وَالْمُتَرَدِّيَةُ وَالنَّطِيحَةُ وَمَا أَكَلَ السَّبُعُ إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ

ḥurrimat ʿalaykumu al-maytatu wa-al-damu wa-laḥmu al-khinzīri wa-mā uhilla li-ghayri allāhi bihi wa-al-munkhaniqatu wa-al-mawqūdhatu wa-al-mutaraddiyatu wa-al-naṭīḥatu wa-mā akala al-sabuʿu illā mā dhakkaytum

“Carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, that over which a name other than The God’s has been proclaimed, the strangled, the beaten to death, the fallen from a height, the gored, and that eaten by a predator—except what you slaughter—have been forbidden to you.”

The form al-mutaraddiyah

الْمُتَرَدِّيَة (al-mutaraddiyah) is a feminine active participle of Form V, تَرَدَّى (taraddā).

The feminine form fits the verse’s context, which concerns an animal in one of the conditions listed.

Feminine forms also occur in الْمُنْخَنِقَة (al-munkhaniqah), الْمَوْقُوذَة (al-mawqūdhah), and النَّطِيحَة (al-naṭīḥah).

Lexically, mutaraddiyah refers to something that has fallen.

In the established use of this verse, it is an animal that has fallen from a height, into a well or pit, or suffered an injury through falling that can lead to death.

The sense of fallen is therefore not restricted to a mountain or a very high precipice.

Falling into a well is also recorded in early usage of the family.

However, every animal that has ever stumbled should not be treated as an instance of the verse’s ruling without regard to its condition. The phrase concerns food and the animal’s death, rather than listing every ordinary movement or fall.

Falling and death caused by falling

Al-mutaraddiyah appears alongside several other conditions in this verse.

Al-munkhaniqah concerns strangulation.

Al-mawqūdhah concerns an animal injured or killed by a blow.

Al-naṭīḥah names a gored animal.

مَا أَكَلَ السَّبُعُ (mā akala al-sabuʿu) concerns an animal from which a predator has eaten.

Each designation thus identifies a condition or cause of injury.

In al-mutaraddiyah, the particular cause is falling.

Death, the food ruling, and the possibility of slaughter must be understood from the complete verse.

Lexically, falling and dying are different. A fall can cause death, but not every fall is fatal.

The importance of illā mā dhakkaytum

After these designations, Al-Māʾidah 5:3 states; an excerpt:

إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ

illā mā dhakkaytum

“Except what you slaughter.”

This exception shows why conditions such as falling and goring should not immediately be equated with being carrion at every stage.

An animal may be injured by a fall but still alive and capable of being slaughtered.

An animal that has died from a fall differs from a living animal slaughtered before its death.

Al-mutaraddiyah therefore requires distinguishing the cause of injury, the occurrence of death, and the possibility of slaughter.

That distinction comes from the complete verse. Taraddā itself does not contain every ruling on slaughter and food.

For lexical research, al-mutaraddiyah names the fallen animal in this context. The ruling’s precise scope is a separate question requiring the whole passage and the related food verses.

Does al-mutaraddiyah include every kind of destruction?

No.

Elsewhere this root concerns moral ruin and the hereafter. In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, however, the subject is an animal and its physical condition.

Al-mutaraddiyah cannot therefore be translated here as a morally corrupted animal or a creature with an unfortunate destiny.

Conversely, this material use does not make ardā in every other verse mean only throwing a body from a height.

Each derivative must be interpreted in its own context.

3. Fa-tardā and taraddā: from falling to human destruction

Two further Qur’anic verses use this root for a human being’s outcome.

One warns against being turned away from remembrance of resurrection and following misleading desires; the other concerns a person whose wealth is of no use when he falls or perishes.

Fa-tardā: a warning to Moses

Ṭā Hā 20:16 states:

فَلَا يَصُدَّنَّكَ عَنْهَا مَنْ لَا يُؤْمِنُ بِهَا وَاتَّبَعَ هَوَاهُ فَتَرْدَىٰ

fa-lā yaṣuddannaka ʿanhā man lā yuʾminu bihā wa-ittabaʿa hawāhu fa-tardā

“So let no one who does not believe in it and follows his desire turn you away from it, lest you perish.”

To understand this verse, consider Ṭā Hā 20:15:

إِنَّ السَّاعَةَ آتِيَةٌ أَكَادُ أُخْفِيهَا لِتُجْزَىٰ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ بِمَا تَسْعَىٰ

inna al-sāʿata ātiyatun akādu ukhfīhā li-tujzā kullu nafsin bi-mā tasʿā

“The Hour is coming; I am close to concealing it, so that every soul may be recompensed for what it strives for.”

In this passage, The God speaks of the coming Hour and recompense for human deeds, then warns Moses not to let those who disbelieve in it and follow their desires turn him away from attention to it.

Who is addressed by fa-tardā?

Fa-tardā here is a second-person masculine singular imperfect verb. It addresses Moses.

This grammatical point is significant.

The verse does not simply say that whoever follows his desire perishes, although that conclusion may appear elsewhere.

Its construction is: do not let someone who disbelieves in the Hour and follows his desire turn you away from it, with the result that you perish.

Fa-tardā follows a prohibition and expresses the possible consequence of being turned away.

In the established grammatical analysis, the verb after the causal fāʾ in response to the prohibition is subjunctive. Its final vowel is not visible because it is a defective verb.

The warning therefore concerns Moses himself.

The danger is not confined to the misguided person’s belief: he may also turn someone else away from their path.

Turning away should not be inserted into the lexical meaning of R D Y. It comes from يَصُدَّنَّكَ (yaṣuddannaka), while perishing comes from fa-tardā.

Physical death or ruin of one’s path and outcome?

This verse does not speak of Moses falling into a well or precipice.

Its concerns are belief in the Hour, attention to accountability, and the danger of following someone who follows his desires.

Fa-tardā therefore concerns a ruinous outcome here, rather than a fall necessarily involving bodily movement.

The root can extend beyond physical falling and death to losing the right path and meeting a destructive end.

This should not be reduced to something vague such as diminished spiritual growth or weakness of character.

Radā retains the serious warning of destruction in this context.

Idhā taraddā: wealth when one falls and perishes

Al-Layl 92:11 states:

وَمَا يُغْنِي عَنْهُ مَالُهُ إِذَا تَرَدَّىٰ

wa-mā yughnī ʿanhu māluhu idhā taraddā

“And what use will his wealth be to him when he falls to ruin?”

Al-Layl 92:8, Al-Layl 92:9, and Al-Layl 92:10 describe this person’s situation:

وَأَمَّا مَنْ بَخِلَ وَاسْتَغْنَىٰ ۝ وَكَذَّبَ بِالْحُسْنَىٰ ۝ فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُ لِلْعُسْرَىٰ

wa-ammā man bakhila wa-istaghnā / wa-kadhdhaba bi-al-ḥusnā / fa-sanuyassiruhu li-al-ʿusrā

“But as for one who was miserly, considered himself self-sufficient, and denied the greater good, We shall ease him toward hardship.”

Taraddā here concerns the outcome of a person characterized by miserliness, self-sufficiency, and denial in conduct and belief.

The verse asks what benefit his wealth will provide when he meets that outcome.

Taraddā is Form V and expresses falling. In this context, the fall is associated with destruction.

Death or falling into the Fire?

Early explanations of this verse present two principal interpretations.

One understands idhā taraddā as the time of the person’s death or destruction.

The other understands it as falling into the Fire of the hereafter.

Assessing these possibilities also requires considering Al-Layl 92:14:

فَأَنْذَرْتُكُمْ نَارًا تَلَظَّىٰ

fa-andhartukum nāran talaẓẓā

“So I have warned you of a blazing Fire.”

This verse strengthens the association between destruction, the hereafter, and the Fire.

The phrase idhā taraddā itself, however, explicitly mentions neither fire, grave, nor death.

A basic translation should therefore preserve falling and destruction, leaving the precise referent to contextual explanation.

“When he falls to ruin” can preserve that range.

Writing only “when he dies” partly loses the image of falling present in this use of Form V.

Conversely, translating it without explanation as “when he falls into the depths of Hell” inserts a referent drawn from the wider verses and interpretation into the phrase’s direct meaning.

Fa-tardā and taraddā compared

Ṭā Hā 20:16 uses Form I: fa-tardā.

Al-Layl 92:11 uses Form V: taraddā.

The morphological difference can accompany a difference in usage.

Radiya is established in Arabic for perishing.

In its clear falling uses, taraddā emphasizes tumbling down and can also concern destruction caused by a fall.

This does not make Form I invariably refer to destruction in the hereafter and Form V invariably to a physical fall.

Al-mutaraddiyah in Al-Māʾidah has a physical application, whereas taraddā in Al-Layl concerns a human being’s outcome.

Morphology therefore indicates semantic possibilities; context clarifies the particular kind of falling and destruction.

4. Ardā: bringing another to destruction

The root’s three other Qur’anic occurrences are Form IV.

They emphasize causing another to perish or bringing them to ruin.

The cause differs between the verses: supposed partners and the beautification of a destructive act; a misleading companion; and a false assumption about The God.

Li-yurdūhum: making the killing of children appear attractive

Al-Anʿām 6:137 states; an excerpt:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ زَيَّنَ لِكَثِيرٍ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ قَتْلَ أَوْلَادِهِمْ شُرَكَاؤُهُمْ لِيُرْدُوهُمْ وَلِيَلْبِسُوا عَلَيْهِمْ دِينَهُمْ

wa-kadhālika zayyana li-kathīrin mina al-mushrikīna qatla awlādihim shurakāʾuhum li-yurdūhum wa-li-yalbisū ʿalayhim dīnahum

“And thus their partners made the killing of their children appear attractive to many of the associators, to bring them to destruction and confuse their religion for them.”

Li-yurdūhum is Form IV and means bringing them to destruction.

The subject is understood in connection with شُرَكَاؤُهُمْ (shurakāʾuhum): the persons or things the associators assigned as partners to The God, to whom the sentence attributes making the killing of children appear attractive.

The act described is profoundly destructive: killing children.

The killing itself must nevertheless be distinguished from إِرْدَاء (irdāʾ).

قَتْلَ أَوْلَادِهِمْ (qatla awlādihim) names killing their children.

Li-yurdūhum concerns bringing to destruction those to whom that act was made attractive.

The object pronoun hum in li-yurdūhum refers to the associators, rather than simply the children killed.

The verse therefore does not describe only the children’s destruction; those accepting the destructive conduct are also brought to ruin.

Wa-li-yalbisū ʿalayhim dīnahum

Al-Anʿām 6:137 continues; an excerpt:

وَلِيَلْبِسُوا عَلَيْهِمْ دِينَهُمْ

wa-li-yalbisū ʿalayhim dīnahum

“And to confuse their religion for them.”

يَلْبِسُوا (yalbisū) belongs to L B S and concerns mixing and creating confusion here.

Two results or purposes appear together: bringing to destruction and confusing religion.

They are connected, but their lexical meanings differ.

Irdāʾ concerns bringing to destruction; labs concerns mixture and confusion.

Their association suggests that making destructive conduct appear attractive under a religious guise can lead both to real harm and to corruption of understanding and action.

That conclusion comes from the whole verse, rather than from an original meaning of religious deception in R D Y.

In kidta la-turdīni: a companion who almost caused destruction

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:56 states:

قَالَ تَاللَّهِ إِنْ كِدْتَ لَتُرْدِينِ

qāla ta-allāhi in kidta la-turdīni

“He said: By The God, you nearly brought me to destruction.”

This occurs in a conversation between someone enjoying blessings and his former companion.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:52 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:53, the person recalls a companion who questioned his belief in resurrection and accountability:

يَقُولُ أَئِنَّكَ لَمِنَ الْمُصَدِّقِينَ ۝ أَئِذَا مِتْنَا وَكُنَّا تُرَابًا وَعِظَامًا أَئِنَّا لَمَدِينُونَ

yaqūlu a-innaka la-mina al-muṣaddiqīna / a-idhā mitnā wa-kunnā turāban wa-ʿiẓāman a-innā la-madīnūna

“Who would say: Are you really one of those who affirm it? When we have died and become dust and bones, shall we truly be recompensed?”

The speaker then sees his companion amid the Fire and says that he had nearly caused his destruction.

La-turdīni here means “bring me to destruction”: the addressee is the former companion, while the speaker is the person who almost perished. The verb is second-person masculine singular. In this written form, the first-person object yāʾ following the protective nūn is omitted.

This destruction concerns the companion’s misleading influence. The verse does not describe his physically throwing the speaker.

In kidta: destruction that did not occur

Kidta expresses proximity to occurrence. In this analysis, in is the lightened form of inna, and the lām of la-turdīni distinguishes it from negative in. The sentence therefore does not deny that destruction was near.

The speaker does not say that his companion actually destroyed him, but that he nearly did so.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:57 also states:

وَلَوْلَا نِعْمَةُ رَبِّي لَكُنْتُ مِنَ الْمُحْضَرِينَ

wa-lawlā niʿmatu rabbī la-kuntu mina al-muḥḍarīna

“And had it not been for my Lord’s blessing, I would have been among those brought forth.”

The continuation shows that the speaker escaped that outcome.

La-turdīni thus concerns destruction that threatened but did not occur.

Translation must preserve this. Saying “you destroyed me” changes the verse’s meaning.

The root itself does not establish that every companionship with someone holding a mistaken belief necessarily causes destruction.

This verse concerns an effort or misleading influence that approached the point of destruction in a particular situation.

Having a companion, being influenced by him, and being brought to destruction must therefore be distinguished.

Ardākum: an assumption that caused destruction

Fuṣṣilat 41:23 states:

وَذَٰلِكُمْ ظَنُّكُمُ الَّذِي ظَنَنْتُمْ بِرَبِّكُمْ أَرْدَاكُمْ فَأَصْبَحْتُمْ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

wa-dhālikum ẓannukumu alladhī ẓanantum bi-rabbikum ardākum fa-aṣbaḥtum mina al-khāsirīna

“And that assumption you held about your Lord brought you to destruction, so you became among the losers.”

Ardākum is a Form IV perfect verb. The pronoun kum is its object.

The subject of ardākum is an implicit pronoun referring back to ظَنُّكُمُ (ẓannukumu), the assumption they held about their Lord. In a common analysis, ẓannukumu is an appositional substitute or explanation of ذَٰلِكُمْ (dhālikum); it should not be treated as the verb’s overt subject.

The verse therefore attributes the cause of destruction to an assumption.

Identifying that assumption requires Fuṣṣilat 41:22; an excerpt:

وَلَٰكِنْ ظَنَنْتُمْ أَنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَعْلَمُ كَثِيرًا مِمَّا تَعْمَلُونَ

wa-lākin ẓanantum anna allāha lā yaʿlamu kathīran mimmā taʿmalūna

“Rather, you assumed that The God did not know much of what you were doing.”

The assumption is clear: they thought many of their deeds remained hidden from The God’s knowledge.

This belief occurs in the context of deeds and the testimony of bodily members.

Ardākum concerns destruction connected with that mistaken outlook and the conduct accompanying it.

Destruction through belief or conduct?

The verse identifies their mistaken assumption as a cause of destruction.

It should not, however, be separated from the context of their actions.

Fuṣṣilat 41:20 and Fuṣṣilat 41:21 describe their hearing, sight, and skins testifying about what they had done.

Their mistaken assumption is therefore not discussed in a vacuum; it relates to their actual behavior.

A person’s view of The God and responsibility for their actions can have serious consequences.

R D Y itself does not mean holding a false belief here. The particular belief is identified by ẓannukumu and the preceding verse; ardākum expresses its destructive result.

Ardākum and al-khāsirīn

The end of Fuṣṣilat 41:23 states; an excerpt:

فَأَصْبَحْتُمْ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

fa-aṣbaḥtum mina al-khāsirīna

“So you became among the losers.”

خَاسِرِينَ (khāsirīn) belongs to KH S R and concerns loss and forfeiture.

Destruction and loss occur in one sentence but belong to different roots.

Ardākum expresses bringing to destruction, while khāsirīn describes their state of loss.

R D Y should therefore not always be translated simply as financial loss or damage.

Loss is one consequence expressed in this verse, but the root’s semantic core is broader and more severe.

5. R D Y and related roots

R D Y (radā) and S Q Ṭ (suqūṭ)

Arabic سُقُوط (suqūṭ) concerns falling and descending.

Sabaʾ 34:9 states; an excerpt:

أَوْ نُسْقِطْ عَلَيْهِمْ كِسَفًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ

aw nusqiṭ ʿalayhim kisafan mina al-samāʾi

“Or cause pieces of the sky to fall upon them.”

نُسْقِطْ (nusqiṭ) expresses causing to fall.

Taraddā can also express falling. In established uses concerning precipices, wells, and human outcomes, however, it is also associated with dangerous falling and destruction.

There is no absolute rule that every suqūṭ is harmless and every taraddī fatal.

Al-mutaraddiyah in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 concerns an injured animal and food rulings. Taraddā in Al-Layl 92:11 concerns a person’s ruinous outcome.

Suqūṭ therefore has a broader application to falling, whereas R D Y in many established uses is associated with dangerous falling and destruction.

This is a tendency, rather than an exceptionless boundary.

R D Y (radā) and KH R R (khurūr)

The Qur’an uses خَرَّ (kharra) for falling down or falling to the ground.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:143 states; an excerpt:

وَخَرَّ مُوسَىٰ صَعِقًا

wa-kharra mūsā ṣaʿiqan

“And Moses fell down unconscious.”

Kharra names Moses’ falling, while صَعِقًا (ṣaʿiqan) describes his condition at the time.

The verse does not say Moses perished from that fall.

Elsewhere, khurūr can describe falling in prostration or as an intense response.

Kharra itself should therefore not invariably be taken as death or destruction.

Some uses of taraddā approach this sense of falling, but the association of R D Y with perishing is also clear in lexical and Qur’anic evidence.

Study of KH R R

R D Y (radā) and H L K (halāk)

هَلَكَ (halaka) in Arabic denotes destruction or ceasing to exist.

This can concern a person’s death, a people’s destruction, or the loss of something.

For example, Moses says in Al-Aʿrāf 7:155; an excerpt:

أَتُهْلِكُنَا بِمَا فَعَلَ السُّفَهَاءُ مِنَّا

a-tuhlikunā bi-mā faʿala al-sufahāʾu minnā

“Will You destroy us for what the foolish among us have done?”

تُهْلِكُنَا (tuhlikunā) concerns destruction here.

Radiya and ardā can be very close to halaka and أَهْلَكَ (ahlaka).

Ardākum in Fuṣṣilat 41:23, for instance, is aptly rendered “brought you to destruction.”

In general usage, however, halāk does not necessarily evoke falling or being dislodged.

Early evidence connects R D Y with both falling and perishing.

The families overlap in destruction, but their history and range of application are not identical.

Study of H L K

R D Y (radā) and R D ʾ (ridʾ)

These roots are close in writing but differ in their third letter.

R D Y has yāʾ as its third radical.

R D ʾ has hamzah as its third radical.

رِدْء (ridʾ) occurs in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:34 as a supporter or helper; an excerpt:

فَأَرْسِلْهُ مَعِيَ رِدْءًا يُصَدِّقُنِي

fa-arsilhu maʿiya ridʾan yuṣaddiqunī

“So send him with me as a helper to confirm me.”

Ardākum in Fuṣṣilat 41:23, however, means bringing to destruction.

The two should not be confused.

رَدِيء (radīʾ), meaning inferior or undesirable, belongs to the family with hamzah, R D ʾ.

Its similar spelling does not make it identical with radiya, to perish.

Some lexical sources transmit forms with and without hamzah alongside one another. Their phonetic proximity alone does not establish a single original historical meaning for them all.

Study of R D ʾ

R D Y (radā) and R D D (radd)

Radd belongs to the doubled root R D D and in its established use concerns returning something.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:13 states; an excerpt:

فَرَدَدْنَاهُ إِلَىٰ أُمِّهِ

fa-radadnāhu ilā ummihi

“So We returned him to his mother.”

رَدَدْنَاهُ (radadnāhu) concerns returning Moses to his mother.

Radā in the sense of perishing concerns destruction or falling.

The meaning of returning should not be transferred to taraddā, nor falling and destruction inserted into radd.

رِدَاء (ridāʾ), an outer covering, and اِرْتَدَى (irtadā), putting it on, are also examined within this family. Even تَرَدَّى بِالثَّوْبِ (taraddā bi-al-thawbi) is recorded for putting on a garment. Taraddā alone therefore does not always mean falling: its accompanying words and context distinguish wearing from falling.

Word formation and actual usage matter in determining meaning.

Study of R D D

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translating this family requires distinguishing throwing, falling, perishing, and causing to perish.

رَدِيَ (radiya): perished; ceased to exist.

رَدًى (radan): destruction; perishing; a ruinous outcome.

رَدٍ / الرَّدِي (radin / al-radī): perished; the indefinite and definite adjective describing one who has perished. The final yāʾ in the definite form is not doubled.

رَدَى (radā): in throwing usage, threw or targeted with stones. Other uses of this form also relate to perishing.

أَرْدَى (ardā): brought to destruction; caused ruin. Other non-Qur’anic uses of related forms include throwing or increasing.

إِرْدَاء (irdāʾ): bringing to destruction; causing to perish.

تَرَدَّى (taraddā): fell; tumbled down; in contexts of a final outcome, fell to ruin.

تَرَدٍّ (taraddin): falling; tumbling down; in some uses, a fatal fall.

مُتَرَدٍّ (mutaraddin): falling; someone or something that has fallen.

مُتَرَدِّيَة (mutaraddiyah): fallen; in Al-Māʾidah, an animal injured or killed through falling.

مِرْدَاة (mirdāh): a large, heavy stone used for throwing or striking.

رَدَيَان (radayān): a kind of rapid movement or running in quadruped usage.

The following renderings suit the six Qur’anic occurrences:

VerseQur’anic formProposed meaning
Al-Māʾidah 5:3الْمُتَرَدِّيَةُAn animal that has fallen; fallen from a height
Al-Anʿām 6:137لِيُرْدُوهُمْTo bring them to destruction
Ṭā Hā 20:16فَتَرْدَىٰLest you perish
Al-Ṣāffāt 37:56لَتُرْدِينِBring me to destruction
Fuṣṣilat 41:23أَرْدَاكُمْBrought you to destruction
Al-Layl 92:11تَرَدَّىٰFell to ruin; fell

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 should retain physical falling. In Ṭā Hā 20:16 and Fuṣṣilat 41:23, destruction is the more natural rendering. In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:56, the full translation must retain the near occurrence conveyed by kidta.

In Al-Layl 92:11, “falls to ruin” suitably conveys both the destructive result and, to an extent, the image of falling.

Ardā should not invariably be translated directly as killing.

Killing can be one way of causing destruction, but Al-Anʿām 6:137 and Fuṣṣilat 41:23 concern more than physically killing a person.

“Bring to destruction” or “bring to ruin” better preserves the meaning in these cases.

7. Conclusion

Arabic R D Y has uses concerning throwing, falling, projectile-like movement, and perishing.

Lexical evidence and early poetry show that throwing stones, forceful impact, and falling into pits or precipices were established uses of this family.

Radiya, to perish, and ardā, to cause to perish, are also established in the language.

These uses are semantically related. Without firm historical evidence, however, it should not be asserted that every sense of destruction necessarily developed from stone-throwing or a physical fall.

The Qur’an’s material use appears in al-mutaraddiyah, concerning an animal injured or killed by a fall.

Fa-tardā in Ṭā Hā 20:16 warns Moses of destruction associated with being turned away from attention to the Hour and accountability.

Taraddā in Al-Layl 92:11 concerns a person whose wealth does not benefit him when he falls or perishes.

The three Form IV occurrences show the cause of destruction in different situations.

Al-Anʿām 6:137 connects making the killing of children attractive and confusing religion with bringing the associators to ruin.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:56, a person speaks of a companion who almost brought him to destruction, although that destruction did not occur.

Fuṣṣilat 41:23 identifies a false assumption about The God’s knowledge, connected with human deeds, as a cause of destruction.

These uses show that destruction does not always mean physical death at that moment. Mistaken conduct and belief can lead someone toward a ruinous outcome.

R D Y should nevertheless not be reduced to broad, vague ideas such as any mistake, failure, loss, or reduction in ability. Its Qur’anic uses retain the serious force of falling and destruction.

Nor should features particular to each verse—killing children, denying resurrection, miserliness, misleading companionship, or assumptions about The God—be made part of the root’s independent meaning.

Final definition: Established Arabic uses of R D Y concern throwing and falling, as well as perishing and causing to perish. In the Qur’an, the family expresses both dangerous physical falling and a ruinous human outcome. Radiya denotes perishing; ardā denotes causing to perish; and taraddā denotes falling or tumbling, which context can associate with destruction. The precise cause, kind, and result of that destruction must be learned from the verb’s construction and the verse’s context, rather than from one fixed material image or moral meaning imposed upon every derivative.
