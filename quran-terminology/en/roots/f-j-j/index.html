The Root ف ج ج — Broad Passages, Openings between Heights, and Passable Routes

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَجٍّ
(fajjin)
A passage; singular

فِجَاجًا
(fijājan)
Broad passages; plural

The three-consonant Arabic root ف ج ج concerns separation and openness between two parts, especially open passages between mountains and elevated terrain. In geographical use, فَجّ is a route or passage through heights, or an open, traversable space across the land.

Its familiar plural is فِجاج.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an: al-Anbiya 21:31, al-Hajj 22:27, and Nuh 71:20. All three concern routes and passages people can traverse. A crucial translation point remains: fajj should not always be restricted to a narrow passage between two mountains, since breadth, openness, and the extension of a route are also recognized features in Arabic.

1. The Root’s Meaning in Pre-Islamic Arabic

In early Arabic, fajj designated a route through mountains, hills, or elevated ground. It was generally open and suitable for travel.

Early lexical accounts offer several descriptions: a broad route between two mountains, a mountain passage, a route facing a mountainside, or an open, extensive path running between heights.

These differences show that fajj was not confined to one fixed geometrical shape.

For example, an open route between two facing mountains can be called a fajj. Some uses also encompass a broad route passing beside elevated ground or extending across the land.

First Witness: ‘Urwah ibn al-Ward

The pre-Islamic poet ‘Urwah ibn al-Ward al-‘Absi says, concerning a poor man’s life and relationship with his relatives:

وَسَائِلَةٍ أَيْنَ الرَّحِيلُ وَسَائِلٍ
وَمَنْ يَسْأَلُ الصُّعْلُوكَ أَيْنَ مَذَاهِبُهْ

Wa-sāʾilatin ayna al-raḥīlu wa-sāʾilin / wa-man yasʾalu al-ṣuʿlūka ayna madhāhibuh.

مَذَاهِبُهُ أَنَّ الْفِجَاجَ عَرِيضَةٌ
إِذَا ضَنَّ عَنْهُ بِالْفَعَالِ أَقَارِبُهْ

Madhāhibuhu anna al-fijāja ʿarīḍatun / idhā ḍanna ʿanhu bi-al-faʿāli aqāribuh.

Translation: “A woman and a man ask, ‘Where is the departure to?’ But who asks the destitute man where his paths lead? His course is that the passages are broad when his relatives withhold their help.”

الفجاج here means broad routes by which someone can travel to other lands and opportunities.

Against his relatives’ restrictions and lack of kindness, the poet sets the breadth of the world’s roads.

This shows that pre-Islamic fijāj could, beyond its geographical application, evoke open routes and the possibility of reaching other lands.

Hope, freedom, and escape from poverty are not inherent in the root, however. They arise from the poet’s situation and the poem’s context.

Second Witness: Abu Kabir al-Hudhali

A poem attributed to Abu Kabir al-Hudhali, describing Ta’abbata Sharran’s agility and strength, contains:

وَإِذَا رَمَيْتَ بِهِ الْفِجَاجَ رَأَيْتَهُ
يَهْوِي مَخَارِمَهَا هُوِيَّ الْأَجْدَلِ

Wa-idhā ramayta bihi al-fijāja raʾaytahu / yahwī makhārimahā huwiyya al-ajdali.

Translation: “When you send him into the passes, you see him speeding toward their mountain openings like a falcon swooping.”

الفجاج here describes open, difficult routes between heights. مخارم likewise refers to mountain passages and breaks.

This clarifies the word’s geographical use in mountainous terrain.

The verse survives in early literary transmission, but its wording and attribution reach us through later compilations, not a direct record from the time of composition. Alongside يَهْوِي مَخَارِمَهَا, another transmitted reading is يَنْضُو مَخَارِمَهَا, meaning that he traverses or passes through them.

Together these witnesses indicate that before the Qur’an, Arabic poetry associated fajj with passages, open routes, and movement across extensive or mountainous terrain.

2. Linguistic Forms and the Semantic Core

The principal recorded forms include:

  • فَجّ: a broad passage, open route, or pass between heights.
  • فِجاج: the plural of fajj; passages and open routes.
  • أَفِجَّة: another, less common plural recorded in lexicons.
  • إِفْجِيج: in some Arabic uses, a broad valley or passage.
  • فَجَّ: in certain verbal constructions, to open or move two parts apart.
  • تَفَاجَّ: to spread the legs apart.
  • أَفَجّ: describing a person or animal with unusually wide spacing between the legs or certain joints.

In bodily usage, فَجَّ رِجْلَيْهِ means opening or spreading the legs apart.

Forms of this family also describe a bow whose string stands away from its body.

These uses concern spacing and openness. Their shared meaning does not prove that the name for a mountain passage necessarily arose from spreading the legs or from the shape of a bow.

Lexicons also record فِجّ, with a kasrah on the first letter, for unripe fruit or food not yet ready. It should not be confused with فَجّ meaning a passage. Although both are recorded under the same consonants, their historical connection is uncertain, and unripeness is absent from this root’s Qur’anic uses.

The semantic core relevant to Qur’anic usage is openness and separation between parts of the terrain, providing a passable route or passage.

Breadth and traversability are therefore important features of fajj. Mountains are one familiar setting, but not an exclusive condition in every use.

3. Qur’anic Uses

A. Al-Anbiya 21:31 — Passages across the Earth for Travel and Finding One’s Way

وَجَعَلْنَا فِي الْأَرْضِ رَوَاسِيَ أَنْ تَمِيدَ بِهِمْ وَجَعَلْنَا فِيهَا فِجَاجًا سُبُلًا لَعَلَّهُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ

Wa-jaʿalnā fī al-arḍi rawāsiya an tamīda bihim wa-jaʿalnā fīhā fijājan subulan laʿallahum yahtadūna.

Translation: “And We placed firm mountains in the earth so that it would not sway with them, and made passages in it for travel, so that they might find their way.”

فِجَاجًا and سُبُلًا occur together here.

Fijāj indicates openings and traversable passages, while subul indicates routes along which people can reach a destination.

The words are close in meaning but differ in emphasis.

Fajj highlights a route’s geographical character and openness; sabīl emphasizes its being a way that can be followed.

For example, an opening between mountains can be a fajj. When people use that passage to reach a destination, it is also a sabīl.

The verse combines the concepts: The God has placed passages in the earth through which people can find their way.

What Does فِيهَا Refer To?

The verse begins with الأرض and رواسي. Grammatically, the pronoun in fīhā can refer to the earth or, in some readings, the firm mountains.

If it refers to the earth, the statement broadly concerns its passages, both mountainous and elsewhere.

If it refers to the mountains, the focus falls more on routes through elevated terrain.

This study considers the first reading more consistent with the explicit فِي الْأَرْضِ and the broad range of traversable routes, while the second remains worth considering in connection with rawāsi.

Either way, the central subject remains: despite its elevations and uneven terrain, the earth offers routes for human movement.

لَعَلَّهُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ

The verse concludes:

«لَعَلَّهُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ»

Laʿallahum yahtadūna.

Here guidance can concern finding one’s route and reaching a destination.

Finding one’s way across the land is a real human need. Passages enable travel, connections between regions, and the movement of people and goods.

Since the Qur’an presents these natural provisions among creation’s signs, broader reflection may consider recognizing blessings and the wisdom of creation. But fijāj directly means actual earthly passages, not paths of spiritual guidance.

B. Al-Hajj 22:27 — People Coming from Distant Routes

وَأَذِّنْ فِي النَّاسِ بِالْحَجِّ يَأْتُوكَ رِجَالًا وَعَلَىٰ كُلِّ ضَامِرٍ يَأْتِينَ مِنْ كُلِّ فَجٍّ عَمِيقٍ

Wa-adhdhin fī al-nāsi bi-al-ḥajji yaʾtūka rijālan wa-ʿalā kulli ḍāmirin yaʾtīna min kulli fajjin ʿamīqin.

Translation: “And proclaim hajj among the people: they will come to you on foot and on every lean mount, the mounts coming from every distant passage.”

Here fajj is singular:

«مِنْ كُلِّ فَجٍّ عَمِيقٍ»

Min kulli fajjin ʿamīqin.

That is, from every distant, extended passage.

Fajj itself identifies the passage; ʿamīq describes it.

In its basic use, ʿamīq can concern depth and extension into something. In the context of travel and people arriving from different lands, distance and the length of the route are prominent.

Fajj ʿamīq should therefore not necessarily be translated as “a very deep valley.”

A reference solely to a valley’s physical depth would emphasize its geological character; here the subject is people coming for hajj.

مِنْ كُلِّ also conveys the many directions of arrival. People travel by different routes and from distant lands.

“Distant” should not become the obligatory meaning of every fajj. Although lexicons also record the word for a far-off course or destination, in this verse ʿamīq and the travel context particularly emphasize distance.

Fajj is the route or passage; ʿamīq expresses its distance and extension here.

Unlike al-Anbiya 21:31, this verse does not concern the existence of passages itself, but people’s use of them to reach a shared destination.

Passages from different directions lead to one destination. This image can inform reflection on people gathering from different lands; fajj itself does not mean religious unity or a human assembly. Those meanings come from the hajj context.

C. Nuh 71:20 — Broad, Traversable Routes

لِتَسْلُكُوا مِنْهَا سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا

Li-taslukū minhā subulan fijājan.

Translation: “So that you may travel along broad, open routes in it.”

This continues Nuh 71:19, which describes The God spreading out the earth for people and making it available for their use.

The word order differs from al-Anbiya 21:31.

Al-Anbiya has:

«فِجَاجًا سُبُلًا»

Fijājan subulan.

Nuh instead has:

«سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا»

Subulan fijājan.

In Nuh, fijājan follows subulan and can function descriptively, indicating the routes’ breadth and openness.

Subulan denotes the paths traveled; fijājan highlights their open, traversable character.

The verb تَسْلُكُوا belongs to س ل ك and expresses following or passing along a route.

Three components therefore occur together:

Sulūk — the act of traveling a route.

Subul — the paths traveled.

Fijāj — these routes’ openness and breadth.

The verse makes no direct reference to mountains. Its subject is the earth and people’s ability to travel across it.

Restricting fijāj to clefts between two mountains would therefore narrow its natural scope without justification.

بِسَاطًا in Nuh 71:19 also establishes the earth’s availability for life and travel. A theory of the entire earth’s geometrical shape should not be extracted from it without independent examination.

4. Morphology and Syntax

Singular and Plural

فَجّ is a singular noun from the geminate root ف ج ج: its second and third consonants are identical.

In the Qur’anic singular, the two jīms appear as one letter with a shaddah:

فَجٍّ — al-Hajj 22:27.

Its broken plural, fijāj, appears in the other two verses:

فِجَاجًا — al-Anbiya 21:31.

فِجَاجًا — Nuh 71:20.

The Qur’anic forms are thus singular fajj and plural fijāj. No verb of this root occurs in the Qur’an.

The Relationship between Fijāj and Subul

In al-Anbiya 21:31, fijājan precedes subulan. Subulan can grammatically be read as an appositional substitute or explanation of fijājan: passages that constitute traversable routes.

Another analysis treats fijājan descriptively, as a circumstantial expression describing the routes.

In Nuh 71:20 the order is reversed, with fijājan after subulan, making the descriptive reading “broad routes” more natural.

This difference should not produce two wholly separate meanings of fijāj. Both verses concern routes and passages.

Nor does fijāj merely mean “many roads.” Its breadth and openness should not be reduced to the number of routes.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

ف ج ج (فَجّ) and س ب ل (سَبِيل)

Sabīl is a broad Qur’anic term for a way, route, or means of reaching a destination.

The way may be physical, as with earthly roads. Elsewhere it can describe a course of conduct, choice, or guidance.

Fajj, however, occurs in the context of traversable earthly routes in all three Qur’anic uses.

In al-Anbiya 21:31 and Nuh 71:20 the words stand together. This shows their closeness, while fajj emphasizes openness and the character of a passage.

ف ج ج (فَجّ) and ف ج و (فَجْوَة)

ف ج و is a separate root associated with breadth and open space.

In al-Kahf 18:17, فَجْوَةٍ describes an open space or spacious part of the cave.

Fajwah can be an open area within a place without necessarily being a route to another destination.

Fajj in Qur’anic usage, by contrast, concerns a passage or route of movement.

Semantic proximity does not prove historical identity. Each root requires examination through its own forms and evidence.

ف ج ج (فَجّ) and ف ر ج (فَرْج)

ف ر ج concerns openings and spaces between two parts; its uses range from gaps and intervals to the private parts and relief.

Some lexical uses of ف ج ج also concern spacing between two parts.

Similar meaning does not make the roots identical. In the Qur’an, fajj names a passage, while farj occurs in other contexts.

Release from distress or relief of difficulties should not be inserted into fijāj’s translation merely through resemblance to faraj.

ف ج ج (فَجّ) and ش ع ب (شِعْب)

In Arabic, شِعْب can designate a cleft or path between mountains. Other forms of the family concern branches and separated portions.

Some lexical explanations describe a fajj as broader than a shiʿb.

This distinction helps explain Arabic geographical terminology, but should not become an absolute rule for every location.

The Qur’anic shuʿūb in al-Hujurat 49:13 concerns human communities and should not be equated with a mountain passage.

6. Challenges in Understanding and Translation

The first challenge is confining fajj to a narrow valley.

Breadth and openness are recognized features of the word. A valley may lie between mountains, but if it offers a broad passage, fajj can describe it.

The second challenge is equating every road with a fajj.

Sabīl and ṭarīq have broader semantic ranges. A route can be a road without foregrounding an opening between heights or a broad passage.

The third challenge is transferring the adjective ʿamīq into the root’s fixed definition. In al-Hajj 22:27, distance is understood from the whole expression. Fajj does not invariably mean a distant road, although Arabic also records it for far-off courses or destinations.

The fourth challenge is treating fijāj as spiritual paths throughout the Qur’an.

Al-Anbiya 21:31, Nuh 71:20, and al-Hajj 22:27 primarily concern real earthly passages. Reflection on guidance, travel, and human connections may develop broader implications, but should not replace the direct lexical meaning.

Finally, openness does not mean fijāj necessarily describes the earth splitting in a particular event. The verses describe existing, traversable passages; their geological formation is a separate question.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Qur’anic fajj can be rendered “passage,” “open route,” or, where appropriate, “mountain pass.”

For fijāj, suitable equivalents include “broad passages,” “open routes,” and “open passes.”

In al-Anbiya 21:31, وَجَعَلْنَا فِيهَا فِجَاجًا سُبُلًا can be rendered:

“And We made passages in it for travel.”

Or, retaining the root’s image more explicitly:

“And We placed open passages in it as routes.”

The first rendering is more natural and conveys the two close concepts fluently in Persian.

In al-Hajj 22:27, مِنْ كُلِّ فَجٍّ عَمِيقٍ is best rendered:

“From every distant passage.”

This preserves “passage” from fajj and “distant” from ʿamīq.

In Nuh 71:20, a suitable rendering of سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا is:

“Broad, open routes.”

Or in the complete verse:

“So that you may travel along broad, open routes in it.”

This preserves the relationship between subul and fijāj and the role of taslukū in natural Persian.

8. Conclusion

Early Arabic ف ج ج concerns spacing and openness between parts of the terrain, especially broad passages between mountains and heights.

Pre-Islamic poetic evidence shows fijāj used for both extensive travel routes and mountain passages.

The Qur’an uses only فَجّ and فِجاج; all three occurrences concern actual routes of movement.

Al-Anbiya 21:31 describes the earth’s passages for finding one’s way; al-Hajj 22:27 describes arrival from distant routes; and Nuh 71:20 emphasizes broad, traversable earthly paths.

The defensible semantic core is a passage or open space between parts of the terrain that allows movement through it.

Breadth and openness matter in this family, while mountainous location, distance, and spiritual destination must be determined from each verse’s context and accompanying words, not imposed as a fixed definition.
