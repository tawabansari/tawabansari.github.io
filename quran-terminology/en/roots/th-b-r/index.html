Qur’anic Root TH B R

Destruction, ruin, and helplessness: cries of doom in the Hereafter, and restraint, level ground, and perseverance in early Arabic

The Arabic root TH B R has several semantic branches. Thubūr denotes destruction, ruin, and a devastating outcome. In another use, thabara concerns restraining, preventing, or turning someone away from an action. Muthābarah denotes persistence in pursuing something. In a further branch, thabrah refers to a type of ground or soil layer.

These meanings cannot be reduced to a single definition without independent evidence. A connection can be imagined between being kept from good and falling into ruin, just as attachment to an activity can be associated with persistence. Such similarities alone, however, do not establish how the words historically developed.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies three bases for this family: soft, level ground; destruction; and persistence in an activity. Other sources also clearly record restraint and deprivation.

The root occurs five times in four Qur’anic verses: four instances of thubūran and one of mathbūran.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five occurrences in four verses

Mathbūran occurs in Moses’ address to Pharaoh, where, after recalling the divine signs, Moses describes Pharaoh as facing an outcome of ruin.

Thubūran occurs in Al-Furqān and Al-Inshiqāq, concerning the cries of people confronted with the consequences of their conduct.

All the Qur’anic occurrences thus belong to the field of destruction, loss, and helplessness. Yet an important distinction remains: mathbūr describes a person, whereas thubūr in the other verses names what people cry out in intense distress and helplessness.

1. Principal branches in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«الثَّاءُ وَالْبَاءُ وَالرَّاءُ أُصُولٌ ثَلَاثَةٌ: الْأَوَّلُ السُّهُولَةُ، وَالثَّانِي الْهَلَاكُ، وَالثَّالِثُ الْمُوَاظَبَةُ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ»

al-thāʾu wa-al-bāʾu wa-al-rāʾu uṣūlun thalāthatun: al-awwalu al-suhūlatu, wa-al-thānī al-halāku, wa-al-thālithu al-muwāẓabatu ʿalā al-shayʾi

This family has three bases: first, softness and ease of terrain; second, destruction; and third, persistence in something.

This classification matters because Ibn Fāris identifies three separate branches, unlike approaches that attempt to derive every meaning from one initial image.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: three distinct bases

Lisān al-ʿArab also highlights another branch: restraining someone or preventing an action.

This branch is particularly relevant to understanding the differing early explanations of mathbūran in Al-Isrāʾ 17:102.

Thubūr: destruction and ruin

Lexicons explain thubūr through destruction, loss, and annihilation.

Lisān al-ʿArab places three explanatory words together:

«الْهَلَاكُ وَالْخُسْرَانُ وَالْوَيْلُ»

al-halāku wa-al-khusrānu wa-al-waylu

That is, destruction, loss, and woe.

These belong to a closely related semantic field, but they are not identical.

Halāk concerns perishing and destruction.

Khusrān concerns loss and the disappearance of something valuable.

Wayl in Arabic can express a cry of grief, a threat of an evil outcome, or helpless distress.

Thubūr can therefore denote destruction itself in one setting and, when someone cries it out, become an expression of helplessness before destruction in another.

This distinction is clearly visible in the Qur’an.

In Al-Furqān, people trapped in a confined place cry out thubūr.

In Al-Inshiqāq, a person likewise calls out thubūr after receiving the record of deeds in a manner associated with condemnation.

Here the word is not an ordinary report that someone has died; it forms part of a person’s verbal response to a dreadful outcome.

Does thubūr mean only physical death?

In some uses, thubūr denotes actual destruction.

When accompanied by cries or requests, however, its meaning can extend beyond the occurrence of a physical death.

Someone saying “Woe to me—destruction!” may be expressing helplessness in an appalling situation.

The hearer is not necessarily receiving a report that the speaker has died at that moment.

A person may also wish for destruction out of extreme suffering, or wish for the situation to end.

These two understandings must nevertheless be considered separately.

A cry of doom does not necessarily constitute an explicit request to The God for death.

Such an interpretation requires attention to the accompanying verb, sentence construction, and context.

In the Qur’an, the verb daʿā accompanies thubūr, and this combination is decisive.

2. Thabara: restraining and preventing

One early Arabic use of thabara concerns keeping someone from an action.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

«ثَبَرَهُ يَثْبُرُهُ ثَبْرًا وَثَبْرَةً، كِلَاهُمَا: حَبَسَهُ»

thabarahu yathburuhu thabran wa-thabratan, kilāhumā: ḥabasahu

That is, he restrained a person, preventing movement or action.

Abū Zayd also records thabartu fulānan ʿani al-shayʾ for turning someone back or dissuading someone from something.

Lisān al-ʿArab: restraint and deprivation

In mā thabaraka ʿan hādhā?, the question asks what prevented or diverted the person from the action.

Here the root does not denote destruction itself.

Someone may be restrained from a dangerous action, and that restraint may benefit them.

Conversely, a person may be deprived of the opportunity to act rightly, with harmful consequences.

Restraint in itself is therefore neither positive nor negative.

Its value is understood from what the person is restrained from and why.

The relation of restraint to mathbūr

Morphologically, mathbūr is a passive participle.

If derived from thabarahu in the sense of restraining, it denotes someone restrained or deprived.

In explaining Al-Isrāʾ 17:102, Al-Farrāʾ relates it to someone overcome and deprived of good.

Al-Ṭabarī also considers this possibility alongside destruction.

This use does not establish that mathbūr in the verse means only a physical prisoner or someone unable to move.

In Moses’ statement, Pharaoh remains a ruler and an addressee of argument.

If this semantic branch is adopted, his restraint is associated with deprivation of good and an evil outcome, rather than a description of physical bonds or imprisonment.

The basic meaning of a verb must thus be distinguished from a consequence attributed to it in context.

3. Muthābarah: persistence and pursuit

Another branch comprises thābara and muthābarah.

Thābara ʿalā al-shayʾ means that someone continued pursuing something without giving it up.

Muthābarah concerns persistence, continuity, and sustained pursuit.

Persian also knows this use through muthābarat.

Someone working regularly at education, farming, research, or a profession may be described as persevering.

Muthābarah must nevertheless be distinguished from thabāt.

Thabāt chiefly concerns remaining established and firm.

Muthābarah here highlights continuing an action or pursuing it repeatedly.

Someone may remain firm in a decision while doing little to carry it out.

Conversely, someone may regularly repeat an action even when it is morally or intellectually mistaken.

Persistence alone therefore does not establish the rightness of the activity pursued.

Does thubūr mean persistence in destruction?

Some later explanations seek a connection between persistence and thubūr, as though destruction were called thubūr because of its continuity and endlessness.

This is conceivable as a semantic image, but it does not by itself establish the history of derivation.

Ibn Fāris identifies destruction and persistence as two separate bases.

Thubūr in the Qur’an should therefore not be translated as “steadfastness,” “constancy,” or “persistence.”

Clearer and more direct evidence supports destruction and ruin in the Qur’anic occurrences.

4. Thabrah: ground, soil, and other physical uses

Lexical sources apply thabrah to soft or level ground.

Ibn Fāris places it under the root’s first base.

In Ibn Durayd’s explanation, however, thabrah also denotes a soil layer resembling lime or plaster that halts a date palm’s roots when they reach it.

These explanations should not carelessly be treated as identical.

Ground may be level or soft at the surface while containing a deeper layer that roots find difficult to penetrate.

Surface evenness and the properties of an underlying layer need not therefore be the same meaning.

Thabrah in relation to ground thus requires attention to soil and environmental conditions.

It should not be turned into a categorical theory about all soil types or date-palm root growth.

Thabara al-baḥru

Lisān al-ʿArab records thabara al-baḥru for the sea’s withdrawal at low tide.

Ibn Fāris connects this with soft, level ground becoming exposed as the water withdraws.

Here the verb does not describe the destruction of the sea.

Water retreats from certain areas, revealing previously covered ground.

This use should therefore not be translated as “the sea died.”

Nor is this branch the direct meaning of thubūr in the resurrection passages.

Mathbir: a place of giving birth

Mathbir, with kasrah on the bāʾ, occurs in early sources for the place where a woman or she-camel gives birth.

Some reports also apply it to the place where a camel is slaughtered.

The lexical explanation of this place noun records no verb with this same childbirth meaning; Ibn Sīdah regards it as belonging to the category exemplified by makhdaʿ.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: mathbir and Ibn Sīdah’s explanation

This matters because mathbir, with kasrah on the bāʾ, and Qur’anic mathbūr are different forms.

The first is the name of a place.

The second describes someone presented in the Qur’anic context as facing ruin or deprivation of good.

Similarity in writing alone does not justify importing the meaning of a birthing place into mathbūran.

Thabīr: a place name

In Arabic geographical sources, Thabīr names a well-known mountain near Mecca.

Some sources discuss several locations bearing the name.

The presence of a geographical name in a lexical family does not by itself establish that it shares the meaning of all the root’s derivatives.

Proper names may have their own naming history and motivation.

Thabīr can therefore be recorded among the linguistic uses, but the Qur’anic meaning of destruction should not be inferred from a mountain’s name.

5. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

Ḥudhayfah ibn Anas al-Hudhalī: muthabbar in a setting of weakness and restraint

A poem attributed to Ḥudhayfah ibn Anas al-Hudhalī says:

أَلَا يَا فَتَى مَا نَازَلَ الْقَوْمَ وَاحِدًا
بِنُعْمَانَ لَمْ يُخْلَقْ ضَعِيفًا مُثَبَّرًا

a-lā yā fatā mā nāzala al-qawma wāḥidan / bi-Nuʿmāna lam yukhlaq ḍaʿīfan muthabbaran

Sense translation: “O young man, the one who confronted the people alone at Nuʿmān was not created weak and helpless.”

Here muthabbaran stands beside ḍaʿīfan, “weak.”

This pairing connects the word with weakness, restraint, or restricted ability.

The context describes someone who, in contrast to weakness and helplessness, could confront the opposing group.

The verse is quoted in lexical sources and collections of Hudhalī poetry. Some lexicon versions give only its second hemistich; in one source, a textual corruption has produced a different beginning.

Its attribution to Ḥudhayfah ibn Anas is discussed in textual scholarship, but its precise date is uncertain.

It is useful evidence for the early use of muthabbar, but should not be assigned to a definite period before Qur’anic revelation without evidence.

Kitāb al-Afʿāl: editorial note on the Hudhalī witness and corruption

ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zibaʿrā: mathbūr and the outcome of error

ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zibaʿrā says:

إِذْ أُجَارِي الشَّيْطَانَ فِي سَنَنِ الْغَيِّ
وَمَنْ مَالَ مَيْلَهُ مَثْبُورُ

idh ujārī al-shayṭāna fī sanani al-ghayyi / wa-man māla maylahu mathbūru

Translation: “When I followed and vied with Satan along the path of error; whoever inclines his way is doomed.”

Here mathbūr describes someone turning towards the path of error.

Destruction and ruin are clear in this setting.

Mathbūr itself does not, however, mean Satan, error, or inclining.

Separate words in the verse express those ideas.

Mathbūr describes the condition and outcome of the person choosing the path of error.

Al-Ṭabarī quotes this witness when explaining Al-Isrāʾ 17:102 and Al-Furqān 25:14.

ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zibaʿrā was a poet of early Islam, and this verse belongs to a poem transmitted in connection with his change of religious allegiance.

It should therefore not be called pre-Islamic poetry, although it is useful for Arabic usage close to the period of revelation.

Al-Ṭabarī: Ibn al-Zibaʿrā’s witness and interpretations of mathbūr

Al-Kumayt ibn Zayd: loss and mistaken judgment

Al-Kumayt ibn Zayd al-Asadī says:

وَرَأَتْ قُضَاعَةُ فِي الْأَيَامِنِ
رَأْيَ مَثْبُورٍ وَثَابِرْ

wa-raʾat Quḍāʿatu fī al-ayāmini / raʾya mathbūrin wa-thābir

Sense translation: “Quḍāʿah, in claiming affiliation with the Yemenis, adopted the judgment of one suffering loss and one incurring loss.”

Mathbūr and thābir here describe the holders of the judgment: Quḍāʿah’s decision is likened to that of someone suffering and incurring loss.

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī explains mathbūr and thābir respectively as makhsūr and khāsir: one suffering loss and one incurring loss.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: Al-Kumayt’s witness, makhsūr and khāsir

Thābir here does not necessarily have the familiar sense of thābara ʿalā al-shayʾ, sustained effort in something.

The poem’s construction and context require independent examination.

In some poetic uses, this lexical family can thus extend beyond physical death to the loss and ruin associated with a decision or social position.

Al-Kumayt was an Umayyad poet. This witness therefore postdates Qur’anic revelation and illuminates continuing usage, rather than proving pre-Islamic usage.

What the poetic witnesses establish

The three witnesses indicate three related but distinct directions.

In the Hudhalī poem, muthabbar is associated with weakness and restraint.

In Ibn al-Zibaʿrā’s poem, mathbūr describes someone whose pursuit of error leads to ruin.

In Al-Kumayt’s poem, the family concerns loss and harmful judgment.

These witnesses support the breadth of the root’s usage; none permits destruction, restraint, and persistence to be inserted together into every verse’s translation without regard to context.

6. Mathbūran in the exchange between Moses and Pharaoh

Al-Isrāʾ 17:102

Verse text:

قَالَ لَقَدْ عَلِمْتَ مَا أَنْزَلَ هَٰؤُلَاءِ إِلَّا رَبُّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ بَصَائِرَ وَإِنِّي لَأَظُنُّكَ يَا فِرْعَوْنُ مَثْبُورًا

qāla la-qad ʿalimta mā anzala hāʾulāʾi illā rabbu al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi baṣāʾira wa-innī la-aẓunnuka yā Firʿawnu mathbūran

Translation:

“He said: You have certainly known that none but the Lord of the heavens and the earth sent these down as illuminating signs; and I do indeed think, Pharaoh, that you face destruction and ruin.”

In this verse, Moses replies to Pharaoh.

The preceding verse, Al-Isrāʾ 17:101, mentions the clear signs given to Moses.

In that verse Pharaoh calls Moses masḥūran.

Moses then emphasises Pharaoh’s knowledge of the signs’ source and concludes by calling him mathbūran.

Mathbūr thus occurs within an argumentative exchange and confrontation between Moses and Pharaoh.

The meaning of mathbūr

Early sources record several explanations of the word.

Some explain it as hālik, someone facing destruction and a ruinous outcome.

Others understand it as “accursed.”

Al-Farrāʾ explains it through being overcome and deprived of good.

Another report proposes deficient understanding or impaired reason.

These differences require evaluation.

They do not all convey the word’s direct meaning to the same degree.

Hālik connects directly with the clear branch of thubūr meaning destruction.

“Kept from good” also accords with thabarahu ʿani al-shayʾ, restraining someone from something.

“Accursed,” however, can explain the person’s outcome or deprivation of mercy.

“Deficient in understanding” can likewise function as contextual condemnation of faulty judgment, but cannot define every occurrence of mathbūr.

For a basic translation, “facing ruin and destruction” therefore conveys a more widely recognised shared meaning.

Does Moses describe Pharaoh as already dead?

At the time of this exchange, Pharaoh is alive and still speaking with Moses.

Mathbūr does not therefore mean that he has already physically died.

It can describe someone on a path towards destruction or with ruin as their outcome.

This distinction matters in Persian translation.

“You have been destroyed” may suggest that the destruction has already occurred.

“You face destruction and ruin” expresses his condemned condition and outcome more clearly.

The relation to Pharaoh’s fate

After the exchange, Al-Isrāʾ 17:103 describes Pharaoh’s attempt to drive Moses and his companions from the land, followed by the drowning of Pharaoh and those with him.

This continuity supports reading mathbūr as facing a ruinous outcome.

The word itself should nevertheless not be restricted to “one who will drown.”

Drowning is the manner of Pharaoh’s end in the narrative.

Mathbūr more broadly describes ruin and destruction.

Nor does the verse establish that everyone defeated in a debate or unable to answer an argument necessarily faces Pharaoh’s fate.

It concerns a Pharaoh whose conduct and position are made clear within the wider narrative.

La-aẓunnuka and the certainty of the statement

La-aẓunnuka derives from Ẓ N N.

The verb can convey supposition and, in some contexts, firm conviction.

Mathbūr itself, however, means neither certainty nor supposition.

The translation should preferably preserve Moses’ construction: “I think that you face ruin.”

The contextual strength of Moses’ conviction can be discussed in explanation, but the verb ẓanna should not be removed from the translation without justification.

Moses’ response to masḥūran

In the preceding verse, Pharaoh calls Moses masḥūr.

Moses responds by calling Pharaoh mathbūr.

This conversational contrast deserves attention.

The words are not synonyms, however.

Masḥūr derives from S Ḥ R and concerns the effect of magic or an allegation that someone is under its influence.

Mathbūr derives from TH B R and concerns ruin or deprivation of good.

Moses’ response is therefore more than the same accusation repeated with another word.

He first identifies the signs’ source, then describes Pharaoh’s condition and outcome.

7. Daʿaw hunālika thubūran: a cry amid confined punishment

Al-Furqān 25:13

Verse text:

وَإِذَا أُلْقُوا مِنْهَا مَكَانًا ضَيِّقًا مُقَرَّنِينَ دَعَوْا هُنَالِكَ ثُبُورًا

wa-idhā ulqū minhā makānan ḍayyiqan muqarranīna daʿaw hunālika thubūran

Translation:

“When they are cast, bound together, into a confined place within it, they cry out there for destruction.”

Thubūran here occurs in a setting of punishment.

Al-Furqān 25:11 describes people who denied the Hour, for whom a blazing fire has been prepared.

Al-Furqān 25:12 describes the intensity of that fire.

Al-Furqān 25:13 then describes people cast into a confined place within it, bound together.

In this condition they cry out thubūr.

Confinement and the cry of doom

Makānan ḍayyiqan denotes a confined place.

Muqarranīn derives from the independent root Q R N and here concerns people bound or joined together.

Early explanations mention hands being bound, chains, or people being linked together.

Thubūr itself does not mean chains, spatial confinement, or prison.

These are the circumstances in which the cry of doom arises.

They indicate the severity of the people’s helplessness.

Daʿaw: prayer or a cry?

Daʿaw derives from D ʿ W.

This root can concern calling, crying out, prayer, or requesting.

Here, however, its object is thubūr.

In explaining Al-Inshiqāq 84:11, Al-Ṭabarī, like several lexicographers explaining this usage, describes a cry such as wā-thubūrāh: a cry of doom and woe for oneself.

Al-Ṭabarī: the cry of doom in Al-Inshiqāq

It can be compared with the Arabic exclamations wā-waylāh and wā-ḥasratāh.

Someone saying “Woe to me!” is not necessarily offering a formal prayer to The God.

They may simply express intense grief, helplessness, or terror.

Daʿaw hunālika thubūran can therefore describe cries and lamentation over destruction and ruin.

Another reading understands the people, in extreme torment, as wishing for their own destruction, seeing it as an end to suffering.

This possibility also fits the scene’s intensity.

The wording itself does not, however, conclusively establish that they address The God in prayer to bring about their death.

A more explicit contextual indication would be needed for that conclusion.

The meaning of thubūran

Thubūr in this sentence denotes destruction.

Its combination with daʿaw gives the sentence the character of a cry and an expression of helplessness.

“They cried out for destruction” therefore conveys it more naturally than the bare “they requested destruction.”

Conversely, thubūr alone should not be defined as “screaming.”

The cry is understood from the verb’s construction and the situation.

Thubūr remains destruction and ruin.

8. Thubūran wāḥidan and thubūran kathīran: repeated cries or multiple destructions?

Al-Furqān 25:14

Verse text:

لَا تَدْعُوا الْيَوْمَ ثُبُورًا وَاحِدًا وَادْعُوا ثُبُورًا كَثِيرًا

lā tadʿū al-yawma thubūran wāḥidan wa-udʿū thubūran kathīran

Translation:

“Do not cry out for destruction just once today; cry out for destruction many times.”

This verse immediately follows the account of the cries of those undergoing punishment.

In Al-Furqān 25:13, they cry out destruction.

In Al-Furqān 25:14, they are told not to settle for one thubūr, but to call for much thubūr.

Understanding this requires examining the verbal noun thubūr and the functions of wāḥidan and kathīran.

Thubūr is a verbal noun

Al-Farrāʾ and Al-Zajjāj explain thubūr as a verbal noun.

An Arabic verbal noun can denote an event or condition without requiring a plural form to express repetition or intensity.

For example, one may speak of “lengthy sitting” or “much striking” without pluralising the verbal noun.

Here too thubūr retains its singular form while being qualified by wāḥidan and kathīran.

Thubūran kathīran does not therefore necessarily mean several independent kinds of physical death.

It can emphasise abundant cries, intense wishes for destruction, or continuing helplessness.

Al-Ṭabarī: verbal noun thubūr and repeated cries

Does the verse say they die several times?

The wording alone does not establish that these people experience several separate physical deaths.

Thubūr concerns destruction, but the immediate subject here is their cries and response.

Kathīran can express the intensity and abundance of that response.

“Today you will not die once, but many times” therefore changes the sentence’s meaning unless supported by an independent explanation.

It removes daʿaw and turns a cry or request into a report of repeated deaths occurring.

The verse’s direct construction does not support that change.

Is the address consoling?

The apparent sense of the verse is not consolation.

Those undergoing punishment cry out for destruction and are answered that one cry is insufficient.

The address can convey condemnation, reproach, and the severity of their helplessness.

The root TH B R itself does not mean reproach or mockery, however.

That quality is understood from the connection between the two verses and the form of address.

Much thubūr and the ending of punishment

Some exegetical explanations connect abundant thubūr with intense and continuing punishment.

This connection fits the scene.

Two matters must nevertheless be distinguished.

One is the people’s many successive cries.

The other is the duration and nature of the punishment they face.

Al-Furqān 25:14 directly concerns calling and crying out for abundant thubūr.

Determining punishment’s duration and people’s final fate requires consideration of the relevant Hereafter passages together, rather than deriving every detail from kathīran.

9. Fa-sawfa yadʿū thubūran: a cry after receiving the record

Al-Inshiqāq 84:11

Verse text:

فَسَوْفَ يَدْعُو ثُبُورًا

fa-sawfa yadʿū thubūran

Translation:

“Then he will soon cry out for destruction.”

The verse is short, but its context is clear.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:7, Al-Inshiqāq 84:8, and Al-Inshiqāq 84:9 describe someone receiving the record in the right hand, undergoing an easy reckoning, and returning joyfully.

By contrast, Al-Inshiqāq 84:10 describes someone receiving the record from behind the back.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:11 says that this person will soon cry out thubūr.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:12 then describes entry into a blazing fire.

This sequence connects the cry of thubūr with awareness of an appalling outcome.

The difference from Al-Furqān

In Al-Furqān 25:13, the cry follows the people’s being cast into a confined place within the fire.

In Al-Inshiqāq 84:11, it follows the receipt of the record from behind the back.

Both concern a dreadful human outcome, but the cry occupies a different point in each scene’s sequence.

Al-Furqān places punishment and the cry of thubūr together.

In Al-Inshiqāq, receiving the record and discovering the outcome provide the cry’s setting.

The lexical meaning of thubūr in both remains destruction and ruin.

Does this person ask for death?

Early explanations understand yadʿū thubūran through cries such as wā-thubūrāh and wā-waylāh.

Such a cry can express a wish for destruction or intense regret and helplessness.

The verse itself does not state the exact words spoken.

Nor does it specify whom the cry addresses.

“He will cry out for destruction” is therefore preferable as a basic translation.

Explanation can also consider a possible wish for destruction and an end to suffering.

Fa-sawfa and the future

Here sawfa accompanies the imperfect verb yadʿū.

This construction refers to a future event.

The Qur’an is not saying that the person has already cried out at the moment the verse is spoken; it reports a future response.

This grammatical distinction must be preserved in translation.

Past joy and a future cry

Al-Inshiqāq 84:13 mentions the person’s joy among the family.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:14 also presents the belief that there would be no return.

The cry of thubūr thus involves more than physical pain: it is also associated with discovering that one’s conception of the final outcome was mistaken.

This does not establish that everyone joyful in earthly life faces such a fate.

The subject is not joy in general, but someone with a mistaken understanding of return and reckoning.

In the same surah, another person returns joyfully after an easy reckoning.

Joy is not therefore inherently condemned, just as grief alone does not establish virtue.

Conduct is evaluated through its object, orientation, and deeds.

10. Thubūr and related words

TH B R and H L K

Study of H L K

Halāk is among the closest words to thubūr.

Lexical sources repeatedly explain thubūr through halāk.

In Qur’anic usage, particularly when combined with daʿā, thubūr can form part of a cry and lament over destruction.

Halāk does not necessarily have that exclamatory function.

The roots are close in the meaning of destruction itself, while sentence construction can reveal a difference in use.

TH B R and B W R

Study of B W R

Bawār concerns ruin, destruction, stagnation, and fruitlessness.

Thubūr likewise concerns destruction and loss.

Bawār, however, has clear economic and agricultural uses in Arabic, such as a stagnant market or fruitless work.

Qur’anic thubūr concerns human destruction and an appalling outcome.

This does not mean that the words can never overlap.

The entire sense of market stagnation or uncultivated land should nevertheless not be transferred to thubūr.

The Al-Furqān and Al-Inshiqāq passages concern cries of doom, rather than commerce or uncultivated land.

TH B R and T B R

Study of T B R

T B R is an independent Qur’anic root associated with destruction and breaking apart.

Tabār and thubūr sometimes occupy a related semantic field.

Their root letters and word structures nevertheless differ.

Tabār occurs in passages concerning the destruction of groups and their conduct.

Thubūr in the verses examined chiefly appears in cries of doom or descriptions of someone facing ruin.

This distinction should not become an absolute rule, but each root requires independent treatment in translation.

TH B R and W Y L

Study of W Y L

Wayl concerns cries of grief, threats of an evil outcome, or expressions of misery.

Early explanations also gloss thubūr through wayl.

This proximity is clear in Al-Furqān and Al-Inshiqāq, where people cry out.

Wayl and thubūr are not complete synonyms, however.

Thubūr has a particular connection with destruction and ruin.

Wayl can occupy a broader field of threats, regret, and grief.

Translating thubūr as “woe” may convey the sentence’s emotion without clearly preserving destruction on its own.

“A cry of doom” is more precise in such a context.

TH B R and KH S R

Study of KH S R

Khusrān concerns loss and losing something.

Some uses of thubūr also connect with loss and a damaging outcome.

Not every loss, however, amounts to destruction.

Someone may lose in trade or miss an opportunity without perishing.

Qur’anic thubūr expresses a far graver outcome.

The relationship lies in their shared field of loss and ruin, rather than identical ranges of meaning.

TH B R and Ṣ B R

Study of Ṣ B R

The roots share their two final letters in the same positions, but differ in the initial letter and their principal meanings.

Ṣ B R concerns endurance and resisting collapse under pressure.

The Qur’anic branch of TH B R concerns destruction and ruin.

Persian muthābarat can nevertheless approach the sense of endurance and effort.

This proximity must not lead to Qur’anic thubūr being translated as patience, endurance, or steadfastness.

Such a meaning does not fit the Hereafter passages.

TH B R and TH B T

Study of TH B T

TH B T concerns firmness and remaining established.

One branch of TH B R concerns persistence, while another concerns destruction.

They are independent roots.

Muthābarah and thabāt can overlap in human activity, since continuing an action sometimes requires endurance.

Thabāt highlights a condition, whereas muthābarah highlights continued action.

None of these connections makes thubūr synonymous with thabāt.

TH B R and TH B Ṭ

Study of TH B Ṭ

Qur’anic TH B Ṭ concerns hindering and slowing movement.

Early Arabic TH B R also has a branch meaning restraint.

In this respect some uses of the roots are close.

Thabaṭa and thabara are nevertheless distinct.

Qur’anic TH B R foregrounds destruction and ruin; TH B Ṭ requires study through its own evidence.

Even different roots sharing some meanings should not be treated as one family merely because they sound similar.

11. Proposed equivalents for Forqan

For thubūr relating to destruction, “destruction,” “ruin,” or “annihilation” is appropriate.

In cries and lamentation, “a cry of doom” or “crying out for destruction” can convey the construction more naturally.

For mathbūr relating to destruction, “facing ruin,” “facing destruction,” or “doomed” is appropriate.

When restraint is meant, “restrained,” “deprived,” or “prevented from moving or acting” may fit, depending on the sentence.

For thabarahu ʿani al-shayʾ, “he restrained him from it” or “dissuaded him” is appropriate.

For muthābarah, “persistence” and “sustained pursuit” fit.

For thabrah relating to ground, “level ground” or, depending on the precise soil description, “a particular soil layer” fits.

For thabara al-baḥru, “the seawater receded” or “the tide ebbed” is appropriate.

For mathbir in childbirth usage, “a place of giving birth” fits.

These Arabic branches are attested, but their meanings should not be inserted into Qur’anic translations without contextual evidence.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:102

«وَإِنِّي لَأَظُنُّكَ يَا فِرْعَوْنُ مَثْبُورًا»

wa-innī la-aẓunnuka yā Firʿawnu mathbūran

Proposed translation:

“And I do indeed think, Pharaoh, that you face destruction and ruin.”

Explanation can examine the relationship with deprivation of good, being overcome, and Pharaoh’s fate.

Mathbūr should nevertheless not be translated directly as “mad,” “bewitched,” or “drowned.”

Al-Furqān 25:13

«دَعَوْا هُنَالِكَ ثُبُورًا»

daʿaw hunālika thubūran

Proposed translation:

“They cry out there for destruction.”

Here thubūr denotes destruction; the cry is understood from the verb and context.

Al-Furqān 25:14

«لَا تَدْعُوا الْيَوْمَ ثُبُورًا وَاحِدًا وَادْعُوا ثُبُورًا كَثِيرًا»

lā tadʿū al-yawma thubūran wāḥidan wa-udʿū thubūran kathīran

Proposed translation:

“Do not cry out for destruction just once today; cry out for destruction many times.”

This rendering accords with the established explanation of thubūr as a verbal noun and the setting of successive cries.

“Once” and “many times” naturally explain how wāḥidan and kathīran relate to the cry; thubūr itself denotes destruction in both occurrences.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:11

«فَسَوْفَ يَدْعُو ثُبُورًا»

fa-sawfa yadʿū thubūran

Proposed translation:

“Then he will soon cry out for destruction.”

Explanation can discuss intense regret and a possible wish for destruction, but should not identify the cry’s addressee or exact wording without evidence.

12. Conclusion

TH B R has several semantic branches in Arabic.

Ibn Fāris records three bases: soft, level ground; destruction; and persistence in something.

Other sources also clearly attest restraining and turning a person away from an action.

Without reliable historical evidence, these branches cannot be reduced to one shared initial image.

In its familiar use, thubūr denotes destruction, loss, and ruin.

Mathbūr can describe someone facing destruction or deprived of good.

Muthābarah concerns persistence and continued pursuit.

Thabrah, in its ground-related use, concerns soil properties and levelness.

Only the two forms thubūr and mathbūr occur in the Qur’an.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:102, after identifying the divine signs’ source, Moses calls Pharaoh mathbūr. The lexical explanations and narrative continuation support understanding this as facing ruin and a destructive outcome.

In Al-Furqān 25:13, those undergoing punishment cry out thubūr in a confined place while bound together.

In Al-Furqān 25:14, they are told not to settle for one thubūr but to call for much thubūr. This should not be turned into several physical deaths occurring without evidence, since the sentence directly concerns their cries and requests.

In Al-Inshiqāq 84:11, someone receiving the record from behind the back will likewise soon cry out thubūr.

In all three verses where thubūr forms part of a cry, destruction and helplessness are connected.

The word itself nevertheless does not mean crying out. Daʿā expresses the calling, and thubūr names its object.

These verses alone also do not establish that every cry of thubūr is a formal prayer for death. It can express regret and helplessness or a wish for destruction amid suffering; a more precise determination requires contextual study.

Final definition in Qur’anic usage: TH B R in its Qur’anic branch denotes destruction, ruin, and a disastrous outcome. Mathbūr describes someone facing that condition; thubūr names the destruction and ruin that become the object of people’s cries and lamentation in the Hereafter passages.

The distinctive feature is destruction and loss before which a person becomes helpless: neither persistence in an activity, nor steadfastness, nor mere physical restraint.

Accordingly, “facing destruction and ruin” fits mathbūran in Al-Isrāʾ 17:102, while “crying out for destruction” fits the thubūran constructions in Al-Furqān and Al-Inshiqāq.

These equivalents preserve the root’s shared meaning, while each verse’s construction distinguishes a description of a person from a cry of doom.
