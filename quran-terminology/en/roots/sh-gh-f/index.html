Attachment reaching the depths of the heart: from shaghāf in early Arabic to the devotion of the dignitary’s wife in Joseph’s story

Early Arabic connects SH GH F with shaghāf, the heart’s covering or membrane. Lexical explanations use this tangible image to explain attachment reaching deep within a person; this connection alone does not establish the historical sequence of every meaning.

Shaghafahu al-ḥubb describes love affecting someone as though it had passed through the heart’s covering and entered within. It expresses a powerful attachment affecting feelings and attention, beyond a passing, superficial liking.

Shaghaf is not restricted to affection between a woman and a man. Deep attachment may concern a person, idea, belief, or another object; the precise sense depends on the construction and what is loved.

The root appears once in the Quran, in qad shaghafahā ḥubban in Yūsuf 12:30. Women in the city say that attachment to Joseph has reached deep into the dignitary’s wife’s heart.

The wording combines the general term ḥubb with a verb from SH GH F. Within the women’s speech, it portrays love as a deep and powerful attachment affecting her.

The word alone establishes neither sin nor virtue. Strong affection, wrongdoing arising from it, and responsibility for one’s response must be distinguished.

1. Early Arabic usage and poetic evidence

Shaghāf: the heart’s covering

Shaghāf denotes a covering, membrane, or layer associated with the heart.

Lexical explanations variously identify a surrounding covering, an attached membrane, or the heart’s inner part or depth.

These descriptions should not be converted into precise modern anatomical definitions without further examination.

For understanding the root, the important point is its association with the heart’s covering and sensitive interior in Arabic imagery.

This helps explain shaghafahu al-ḥubb: love has affected the heart’s covering or passed through it, penetrating a person’s inward life.

The image depicts love reaching deeply and exerting a powerful effect, beyond a simple feeling.

It does not assert that love physically punctures a cardiac membrane or travels through an organ. Bodily imagery expresses the intensity and depth of an inward state.

A poetic witness from Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm

A line attributed to Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, reads:

إِنِّي لَأَهْوَاكِ غَيْرَ ذِي كَذِبٍ

innī la-ahwāki ghayra dhī kadhibin

قَدْ شَفَّ مِنِّي الْأَحْشَاءُ وَالشَّغَفُ

qad shaffa minnī al-aḥshāʾu wa-l-shaghafu

Translation:

“I love you without any falsehood; my inward parts and the covering of my heart have been worn thin.”

The poet describes his love and its effects on his inward parts and the heart’s covering.

Al-aḥshāʾ denotes the body’s interior and organs; al-shaghaf here concerns the covering or part associated with the heart.

The verb shaffa belongs to another root, SH F F, and concerns love’s wasting effect. The line therefore illustrates the noun al-shaghaf and its association with the heart, rather than the Quranic verb shaghafa.

Love is depicted as exerting a bodily effect on the internal organs and heart’s covering.

The witness shows that poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period used this image in describing affection.

This line’s precise date has not been independently established here. It remains an important early poetic witness without establishing when the usage first arose.

A witness attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī reads:

وَقَدْ حَالَ هَمٌّ دُونَ ذَلِكَ وَالِجٌ

wa-qad ḥāla hammun dūna dhālika wālijun

مَكَانَ الشُّغَافِ تَبْتَغِيهِ الْأَصَابِعُ

makāna al-shughāfi tabtaghīhi al-aṣābiʿu

Translation:

“An intrusive sorrow has intervened before that, entering the place of shughāf that fingers seek.”

The poet portrays a heavy sorrow within him that prevents ease.

In the version quoted here, shughāf is explained as a pain or illness, and the fingers as physicians’ fingers seeking its location. The image of sorrow therefore involves inward bodily pain and should not be reduced to the heart’s covering alone.

Versions differ: some have shāghil, others wālij, and the phrase concerning shughāf also varies. The quotation here follows wālij and makāna al-shughāf; other explanations connect it with the heart’s covering.

The versions and explanations share an inward image of sorrow, while pain and the heart’s covering must remain distinct possibilities.

The witness matters because the family appears in the imagery of sorrow and pain as well as romantic affection.

Shaghāf should therefore not simply be defined as “love.” The covering of the heart and the recorded name of an illness must be distinguished from their use in describing love or grief.

Shughāf and illness

Shughāf is also recorded as a name for pain or illness in the chest region.

Some early explanations place it beneath the ribs; others associate it with illness or injury near the heart.

The terminology is related to the heart’s shaghāf, but the early accounts differ about the condition and its location.

This does not establish that Quranic shaghaf means a bodily illness or a specific cardiac disorder.

Another recorded use of shaghaf concerns the bark or covering of the ghāf tree. This alone does not show that the heart covering was named after bark, or vice versa.

The evidence establishes a semantic field concerning the heart’s covering and profound effects upon it. The historical origins of every branch require further independent evidence.

From the heart’s covering to deep attachment

A familiar explanation of shaghafahu al-ḥubb is love reaching the heart’s shaghāf.

Some descriptions have love reaching the covering; others have it passing through that covering into the heart.

Despite the different images, both describe love reaching deeply and powerfully affecting the person.

Someone may enjoy seeing another person but experience only a short-lived liking.

Alternatively, attachment may occupy much of a person’s attention, thought, and feeling. This better illustrates shaghaf.

It does not follow that every such attachment destroys reason or necessarily results in uncontrollable conduct.

Depth of attachment and the way someone acts remain different questions.

2. Linguistic forms and semantic core

The Quran uses SH GH F only in the verbal form shaghafahā.

Shaghafa is a basic Form I past verb; -hā is an attached feminine singular object pronoun.

Other Arabic forms include shaghifa, yashghafu, shaghf, shaghaf, shaghāf, shaghif, mashghūf, and tashaghghafa. These particular forms do not occur in the Quran.

Shaghāf names the heart’s covering; Quranic shaghafa describes love’s profound effect.

Mashghūf describes someone powerfully affected by attachment or affection.

The passive shughifa bi-l-shayʾ is recorded for being deeply attached to something. Shaghifa bi-l-shayʾ, with kasrah on ghayn, is also explained as becoming restless. Vocalization and construction should be considered separately.

Such uses are not restricted to relations between women and men. Strong attachment can also concern an idea, possessions, or another desired object.

In the Quran, however, the object of attachment is identified in Joseph’s story.

The Quranic semantic core is:

Love or attachment reaching deep into the heart and powerfully affecting a person emotionally.

It relates to love without being identical to every ordinary liking.

The word itself establishes neither the relationship’s nature, mutuality, permissibility, nor conduct toward the beloved.

3. Shaghaf in Joseph’s story

The root’s sole Quranic occurrence is Yūsuf 12:30:

وَقَالَ نِسْوَةٌ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ امْرَأَتُ الْعَزِيزِ تُرَاوِدُ فَتَاهَا عَنْ نَفْسِهِ قَدْ شَغَفَهَا حُبًّا إِنَّا لَنَرَاهَا فِي ضَلَالٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-qāla niswatun fī al-madīnati imraʾatu al-ʿazīzi turāwidu fatāhā ʿan nafsihi qad shaghafahā ḥubban innā la-narāhā fī ḍalālin mubīn

Yūsuf 12:30

Translation:

“Women in the city said: The dignitary’s wife is trying to seduce her young servant; love has penetrated deep into her heart. We certainly see her in manifest error.”

The speakers are women in the city discussing the dignitary’s wife.

They mention her pursuit of Joseph and her powerful attachment to him.

They then judge her conduct, saying that they see her in manifest error.

These three elements must be distinguished when interpreting shaghafahā ḥubban.

The meaning of qad shaghafahā ḥubban

Qad accompanies a past verb and emphasizes the realization of the state described.

Shaghafahā portrays her as powerfully affected, with love reaching deep into her heart.

Ḥubban belongs to the separate root Ḥ B B and specifies the kind of effect.

Grammatically, ḥubban is accusative and commonly analyzed as a specifying complement, tamyīz. The sense is explained as qad shaghafahā ḥubbuhu: her love for him has profoundly affected her. This does not imply his love for her.

The verb’s implicit masculine subject may refer to the young man mentioned earlier. That grammatical role does not establish a deliberate effort by Joseph to make her attached to him.

This distinction matters.

Saying that someone has captured another person’s heart or become their beloved does not necessarily imply an action intended to create that affection.

Shaghafahā therefore does not establish that Joseph deceived her, sought to attract her, or wanted a relationship with her.

The surrounding verses resist that simplistic inference.

Shaghaf and murāwadah

In Yūsuf 12:23, the dignitary’s wife pursues Joseph and shuts the doors. He seeks refuge in God from her invitation.

In Yūsuf 12:30, the women say turāwidu fatāhā ʿan nafsihi.

Turāwidu is from R W D and here concerns her effort to draw Joseph into accepting her wish.

Shaghafahā ḥubban describes the intensity of her attachment.

One concerns her actions; the other her emotional condition.

They may be connected without being synonymous.

Someone may feel deeply attached while respecting the other person’s choices.

Another person may respond with insistence, pressure, or wrongdoing.

Wrongdoing therefore cannot be derived from shaghaf alone; it must be assessed in conduct.

Does her attachment establish mutual love?

The verse describes the dignitary’s wife’s condition.

Love reaching her heart does not establish that Joseph shared her feelings.

Whether affection is mutual cannot be determined from shaghaf itself.

In Yūsuf 12:32, she acknowledges before the women that she sought to seduce him but that he restrained himself.

She then threatens imprisonment and humiliation if he refuses her demand.

In Yūsuf 12:33, Joseph prefers prison to what he is being invited to do.

The story thus distinguishes her attachment from Joseph’s wishes and decisions.

Even powerful affection on one side establishes neither consent, reciprocal love, nor a right to control the other person’s life.

Attachment and abuse of position

In Yūsuf 12:32, the dignitary’s wife goes beyond expressing affection and threatens Joseph with imprisonment and humiliation.

The issue is then more than love or infatuation alone.

Using position and power to compel an unwanted relationship differs from affection itself.

Strong feelings confer no right to violate another person’s freedom or choice.

This emerges from considering her conduct, Joseph’s refusal, and her subsequent threat together.

Shaghaf should nevertheless not be lexically defined as coercion, threat, or abuse of power. These are actions in the story requiring separate analysis.

The women’s speech and judgment

The identity of the speaker also matters.

The Quran says wa-qāla niswatun fī al-madīnah: women in the city spoke.

Qad shaghafahā ḥubban belongs to their speech.

Every detail of their judgment should not automatically be equated with a direct narrative statement about her intentions or the depth of her emotions.

They spoke about her conduct and attachment; other verses also narrate her pursuit of Joseph.

This sentence alone does not establish the full extent of their knowledge of her motives and inward feelings.

Yūsuf 12:31 describes her response and invitation to the women, who are then astonished on seeing Joseph.

Their reaction shows a powerful impression, but astonishment alone should not be equated with the shaghaf they had attributed to her.

The passage thus illustrates the root while requiring a distinction between characters’ speech and direct narrative judgment.

4. Related roots and distinctions

SH GH F (shaghaf) and Ḥ B B (ḥubb)

Ḥubb concerns love and liking.

Its object may be God, people, family, wealth, blessings, or other things.

In the field of affection, shaghaf emphasizes love’s profound effect on the heart.

Yūsuf 12:30 combines the two: shaghafahā ḥubban.

Ḥubb identifies the feeling; shaghaf emphasizes how deeply it has affected the person.

This does not establish a fixed classification in which every affection begins as ḥubb and necessarily becomes shaghaf at a measurable threshold.

The words are related, but language does not always assign feelings to fixed, quantifiable stages.

SH GH F (shaghaf) and SH ʿ F (shaʿaf)

These are different roots, sometimes compared because of their similar sounds and uses concerning love.

Shaʿaf also has uses concerning overpowering love and infatuation.

Shaʿafatu al-jabal, meanwhile, means a mountain’s summit.

Some lexical explanations connect elevation with intense attachment, but that image alone does not establish the historical origin of the emotional sense.

Reading reports for Yūsuf 12:30 attribute shaʿafahā, with ʿayn, to some readers.

The Ḥafṣ reading used on this site has shaghafahā, with ghayn.

Features of SH ʿ F should not be transferred to SH GH F without evidence.

Nor can either be said invariably to describe stronger or deeper love; their overlapping uses do not support that absolute distinction.

SH GH F (shaghaf) and H W Y (hawā)

In its branch concerning desire, hawā denotes inward inclination.

It may concern a person, wish, idea, or preferred course.

Quranic shaghaf emphasizes deep attachment and love’s effect upon the heart.

A person may incline toward something without becoming deeply attached.

Strong love may also arise without being allowed to determine every decision.

Quranic hawā, particularly when followed, can involve disregard for truth and justice.

Shaghaf in Yūsuf 12:30 does not itself name following inclination or moral deviation. Those issues emerge from conduct and context.

SH GH F (shaghaf) and SH H W (shahwah)

Shahwah concerns the soul’s inclination toward something desirable and pleasant.

It may involve food, comfort, relationships, or sexual pleasure.

Quranic shaghaf describes powerful attachment reaching deeply into the heart.

Shahwah and shaghaf may coexist without being identical.

Someone may have an appetite for food without deep emotional attachment to it.

Someone may also feel profound affection for a person that cannot be reduced entirely to sexual desire.

The wife’s attachment occurs within a narrative of sexual pursuit, but that setting comes from the story rather than an exclusively sexual definition of SH GH F.

Study of SH H W

SH GH F (shaghaf) and W D D (wudd)

Wudd concerns affection and the desire for an affectionate bond.

The Quran uses this family for love and, in other constructions, wishing something to happen.

Shaghaf emphasizes depth of attachment and its effect on the heart.

Two people may share lasting, respectful affection without it being described as shaghaf.

Conversely, intense attachment does not guarantee a mutual, healthy, or lasting relationship.

Shaghaf should therefore not automatically mean enduring affection, reciprocal love, or an ethically sound relationship.

SH GH F (shaghaf) and SH GH L (shughl)

These roots resemble one another in sound and writing but remain distinct.

Shughl concerns occupation or engagement with something.

Shaghaf concerns the heart’s covering and, emotionally, attachment’s profound effect upon it.

Intense attachment may continually occupy the mind, but occupation itself is not shaghaf.

Work, worry, illness, or family responsibilities may occupy someone without deep attachment to those matters.

Shaghaf should therefore not be reduced to mental preoccupation.

5. Attachment, love, and human responsibility

A central distinction is between emotional intensity and the ethical value of conduct.

Deep affection may encourage goodwill, loyalty, and respect for the beloved’s rights.

In another situation, strong attachment may accompany selfishness, jealousy, insistence, or attempts to impose a wish.

Both may involve intense feeling, while the conduct differs.

Someone may love a person who does not reciprocate, yet accept their decision and respect their choice despite the difficulty.

Another may invoke intense affection to justify threats or the use of power to obtain the person they desire.

The presence of strong love does not make these behaviors equivalent.

Joseph’s story illustrates the distinction: the wife’s attachment accompanies pursuit and then threats of imprisonment and humiliation.

Criticism of her conduct should not become a claim that all deep attachment is inherently wrong.

Nor can intense love justify violating another person’s rights.

Love may dominate someone’s heart without extinguishing the other person’s right to decide and choose.

This conclusion follows from the story’s actions and conversations; it is not all contained in shaghaf’s lexical meaning.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In contexts of love, shaghafa may be rendered “penetrate deeply into the heart,” “deeply affect the heart,” “overwhelm the heart,” or, in some contexts, “enrapture.”

The role of ḥubban should also be retained in Yūsuf 12:30.

“Has enraptured her” conveys part of the sense, but leaves the image of love reaching deep into the heart less explicit.

“She has fallen in love with him” is natural, but may obscure the distinction between shaghaf and ḥubb.

“Joseph made her fall in love with him” may suggest a deliberate effort by Joseph to arouse her feelings. The phrase itself does not establish that.

A preferable rendering for Forqan is:

“Love has penetrated deep into her heart.”

Or, identifying its object more explicitly:

“Her love for Joseph has reached the depths of her heart.”

This retains the deep attachment without implying a deliberate act by Joseph.

Proposed rendering of Yūsuf 12:30, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Women in the city said: The dignitary’s wife is trying to seduce her young servant; love has penetrated deep into her heart. We certainly see her in manifest error.”

For non-Quranic forms, shaghāf may be explained as “the heart’s covering,” and mashghūf as “attached,” “enraptured,” or “deeply devoted,” according to context.

The image of penetration into the heart should not be converted into a physical or medical event.

Conclusion

SH GH F is associated with shaghāf, the covering or a part of the heart. Poetry attributed to Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm and al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī uses the family in images of love and grief; the latter witness also requires attention to shughāf as a name for pain or illness.

In emotional usage, shaghafa describes profound attachment affecting the heart, as if love had reached its covering or depths.

The Quran uses the root only in Yūsuf 12:30, in qad shaghafahā ḥubban.

The women describe the wife’s powerful attachment to Joseph. The expression establishes neither reciprocal love, deliberate encouragement by Joseph, nor the permissibility of her conduct.

Other verses show her pursuit, his refusal, and her threats. These actions must be examined separately from the emotional attachment itself.

Shaghaf overlaps with ḥubb, hawā, shahwah, and affection, without being fully synonymous with any. Its distinctive Quranic emphasis is love’s depth and powerful effect upon the heart.

The resulting definition is:

SH GH F is associated with shaghāf, the heart’s covering. Emotionally, it describes love and strong attachment reaching deep into the heart and powerfully affecting the person. The word alone determines neither moral value nor reciprocal affection, and does not necessitate any particular conduct.

For Forqan’s Persian translation, the equivalent of “Love has penetrated deep into her heart” naturally conveys qad shaghafahā ḥubban.
