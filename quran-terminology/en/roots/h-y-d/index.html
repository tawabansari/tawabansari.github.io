Moving aside, departing from a course, and avoiding what one does not wish to face

The Arabic root ح ي د concerns moving aside from a course, turning in another direction, and avoiding something. حَادَ يَحِيدُ (ḥāda yaḥīdu) can describe someone turning from a straight path or changing course to avoid an encounter.

Moving aside does not always mean fleeing in fear. A person may turn from a previous course to avoid danger or conflict, change direction, or for another reason. Whether the action is right or wrong comes from its subject and circumstances, rather than the root itself.

Early Arabic also uses the family for projections extending to one side from a mountain, horn, or bone. This tangible use illustrates departure from an alignment or course.

The Qur’an uses the root only once, in مَا كُنْتَ مِنْهُ تَحِيدُ (mā kunta minhu taḥīdu), concerning the arrival of death. Here moving aside takes on the sense of fleeing and avoiding an encounter with death.

1. Forms and semantic core

The root ح ي د occurs in the Qur’an only as تَحِيدُ (taḥīdu), in Qāf 50:19. It is an imperfect verb from حَادَ ـ يَحِيدُ (ḥāda–yaḥīdu).

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence of Ḥ Y D in Qāf 50:19

Arabic records other forms in the same family.

Ḥāda yaḥīdu means moving aside, inclining, or turning away from something. حَيْد (ḥayd), حَيْدَة (ḥaydah), and حُيُود (ḥuyūd) are verbal nouns describing turning aside and changing course. حَائِد (ḥāʾid) describes someone or something moving away from a course or position.

مَحِيد (maḥīd) concerns turning aside and a route or possibility of escape. مَا لَكَ مَحِيدٌ عَنْ ذَلِكَ (mā laka maḥīdun ʿan dhālika) means that you have no way of avoiding or escaping it.

حَايَدَ (ḥāyada) and مُحَايَدَة (muḥāyadah) concern standing apart from another, avoiding confrontation, or not entering a conflict.

حَيُود (ḥayūd) describes someone who frequently turns aside or repeatedly changes course. حَيَدَى (ḥayadā) is also recorded for an animal that repeatedly darts sideways or leaves its course.

In tangible uses, حَيْد (ḥayd) also denotes a projection on the side of a mountain, horn, or bone. Its recorded plurals include حُيُود (ḥuyūd) and أَحْيَاد (aḥyād).

These forms are known in Arabic lexica, but none occurs in the Qur’an apart from taḥīdu.

Semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris describes the central meaning as:

الْمَيْلُ وَالْعُدُولُ عَنْ طَرِيقِ الِاسْتِوَاءِ

al-maylu wa-l-ʿudūlu ʿan ṭarīqi al-istiwāʾi

Inclining and departing from a straight course or usual alignment.

This definition has two important features: a course or position associated with a person or thing, and a departure or change of direction relative to it.

Someone walking along a road who suddenly turns right or left has ḥāda from the previous course. Avoiding an obstacle may motivate the change, but an obstacle or danger is not essential to the verb’s meaning.

In a non-spatial use, someone can withdraw from a subject or avoid confronting something without making a particular bodily movement.

The semantic core can therefore be understood as moving aside or changing direction relative to a course, position, or something one faces.

Fleeing, avoiding, and keeping away arise within this field, but the reason and moral value of the avoidance must be established separately.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: inclination and departure from a straight course

2. Tangible imagery and early Arabic witnesses

Projections on mountains and horns

Lisān al-ʿArab records ḥayd for a projection extending from the side of something.

حَيْدُ الْجَبَلِ (ḥaydu al-jabal), for example, is a projection from a mountainside, extending like a wing.

In Ibn Sīdah’s explanation, it projects from the side, rather than necessarily from the highest point.

حُيُودُ الْقَرْنِ (ḥuyūdu al-qarn) likewise describes bends or projections on animals’ horns. The natural curves and knobs of an ibex’s horn and those of some other animals can be described this way.

Something projects to one side from an even alignment or usual course in these uses.

The image is clearly connected with moving aside. It does not establish that the verb ḥāda necessarily developed from the name of a mountain projection or horn formation. The evidence shows semantic proximity, rather than a certain historical sequence.

Nor does curvature mean a defect here. An animal’s horn may curve naturally, and a mountain may have projections, without any negative moral implication.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Muḥkam, and Lisān al-ʿArab: mountain and horn projections

An animal darting sideways

A verse attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh al-Hudhalī reads:

أَوْ أَصْحَمَ حَامٍ جَرَامِيزَهُ
حَزَابِيَةٍ حَيَدَى بِالدِّحَالِ

aw aṣḥama ḥāmin jarāmīzahu / ḥazābiyatin ḥayadā bi-l-diḥāli

Approximate translation:

“Or like a dark, sturdy wild ass protecting itself, repeatedly darting aside in flight and resistance.”

Ḥayadā describes an animal whose movement involves turning aside, twisting, and changing direction.

Lexicographers explain that the animal moves this way to protect itself from archers. Some also describe ḥayadā through a wild ass’s liveliness and agility. In the explanation transmitted from Al-Aṣmaʿī, al-diḥāl in this very verse means refusal and resistance, rather than a pit or underground passage. “Among the pits” is therefore unsuitable for that reading.

Tāj al-ʿArūs, D Ḥ L: Al-Aṣmaʿī explains al-diḥāl here as resistance, not an underground passage

The witness shows that turning aside can be a movement away from danger. Yet ḥayadā does not name fear or an archer: those elements come from the complete poetic image and its lexical explanation.

The form has variant readings. Some sources transmit ḥayadā, others حَيِّد (ḥayyid). This matters morphologically, but does not erase the general sense of turning aside and changing direction in the witness.

Some sources describe Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh as having lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, others as an Umayyad poet. This verse is consequently not securely dated before the Qur’an, although it remains a useful witness to the family’s early Arabic use.

Khizānat al-Adab: Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh and the account of his spanning both periods

Moving aside to avoid confrontation

Lisān al-ʿArab, reporting Al-Azharī, explains that someone may turn aside from something out of fear or unwillingness to face it.

Ḥāda ʿan shayʾin is therefore more than bodily movement to the right or left. It can concern avoiding an encounter.

Someone may change course to avoid an enemy; someone else may withdraw from an issue to avoid a dispute.

Both can involve moving aside. The verb alone does not tell us that the person was cowardly, wise, unjust, or cautious.

The old expression حِيدِي حَيَادِ (ḥīdī ḥayādi) also conveys turning aside and making way. Some uses accompany retreat from a confrontation.

A clear linguistic conclusion follows: ح ي د describes movement aside or avoiding an encounter, while the motive varies between situations.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: fear and aversion, and the animal protecting itself from archers

3. Qur’anic use: avoiding an encounter with death

وَجَاءَتْ سَكْرَةُ الْمَوْتِ بِالْحَقِّ ذَٰلِكَ مَا كُنْتَ مِنْهُ تَحِيدُ

wa-jāʾat sakratu al-mawti bi-l-ḥaqqi dhālika mā kunta minhu taḥīdu

Proposed translation:

“The overwhelming stupor of death has arrived with the truth: this is what you used to flee from.”

Qāf 50:19

This is the only Qur’anic verse containing the root ح ي د.

Understanding it requires considering the surah’s context.

Qāf 50:16, Qāf 50:17, and Qāf 50:18 concern humanity’s creation, divine knowledge of the self’s whispering, and the recording of speech. Qāf 50:19 then describes death arriving.

The following passage concerns the trumpet being blown, each self arriving with a driver and witness, and the veil of heedlessness being removed (Qāf 50:20, Qāf 50:21, and Qāf 50:22).

Qāf 50:19 is thus part of a transition from human life to an encounter with a reality that can no longer be ignored or kept at a distance.

تَحِيدُ (taḥīdu): moving away from what?

The key expression is:

ذَٰلِكَ مَا كُنْتَ مِنْهُ تَحِيدُ (dhālika mā kunta minhu taḥīdu)

Taḥīdu comes from ḥāda yaḥīdu: you were turning aside, avoiding, or seeking to escape an encounter with it.

Min in minhu specifies the relationship of distancing to what someone sought to avoid.

In this context, the nearest referent is death and the arrival of its reality.

“This is what you used to flee from” therefore fits both the root and the construction.

Three matters should nevertheless be distinguished:

First, ḥayd itself, meaning turning aside and avoiding.

Second, its object here, connected with death and the reality of its arrival.

Third, the inability to escape ultimately, understood from the arrival of sakrat al-mawt and the verse’s context.

Inability to escape must therefore not become ḥayd’s lexical definition. Someone may turn aside successfully and distance themselves from something. This verse, however, concerns what eventually arrives and cannot be avoided forever.

Al-Ṭabarī: death, its reality, and the person’s previous avoidance

Does taḥīdu mean fearing death?

Fear of death is one natural motive for avoiding it, but taḥīdu itself does not mean being afraid.

Someone may fear death, try to delay it, avoid thinking about it, or live as though they will never have to encounter it.

These may illustrate different kinds of avoidance, but the verse does not detail every person’s psychology or behavior.

“You were afraid of it” consequently fails to convey the whole sense, confining turning aside or flight to a feeling of fear.

“You used to flee from it,” however, preserves movement or avoidance and is natural in the context of death arriving.

Does fleeing death mean denying the hereafter?

That conclusion does not follow from taḥīdu itself.

Someone may believe in death and the hereafter while avoiding pain, separation, or the end of worldly life. Another person may accept death while doubting a life after it. Another may knowingly deny the hereafter.

Those situations differ in knowledge, intention, and moral responsibility.

Qāf 50:19 concerns what someone sought to avoid but has now arrived. Taḥīdu alone cannot establish that the addressee necessarily denied resurrection knowingly.

Other verses of Sūrat Qāf certainly confront denial of resurrection and doubts about renewed creation. That matters to the surah’s wider message, but should not redefine taḥīdu lexically as doctrinal denial.

Avoiding death, denying death, and denying resurrection are different concepts. They may coincide in someone’s behavior, but are not synonymous.

كُنْتَ تَحِيدُ (kunta taḥīdu): the person’s previous state

Kunta appears with the imperfect taḥīdu.

Arabic can use this construction for a past behavior or state involving duration or repetition.

The sentence need not concern one momentary movement: it can describe how someone used to avoid facing this reality during their earlier life.

The construction does not establish that the person was fleeing every instant without exception. Context specifies the precise duration or repetition.

The address is also masculine singular: kunta and taḥīdu.

The preceding verses speak of al-insān, the human being. The address can therefore concern humanity generally, without the masculine verb alone restricting it to men.

The wider context also discusses denial and heedlessness. Exactly which groups the address encompasses in all its implications is consequently a contextual question, rather than something verb morphology alone settles.

What does ذَٰلِكَ (dhālika) refer to?

The nearest referent is death and the arrival of its condition.

The verse begins with sakrat al-mawt bi-l-ḥaqq arriving. Its explanation can therefore connect death itself with the reality disclosed by its arrival.

These are contextually close, but the whole meaning of ḥaqq and every detail of the hereafter should not be placed inside taḥīdu without evidence.

The verb simply shows that someone used to avoid what they now face.

A contrast with the reality that eventually arrives

The verse begins wa-jāʾat: death has arrived.

It then speaks of what the addressee had previously been avoiding.

There is a clear contrast: someone seeks distance from something, yet that very thing eventually reaches them.

The contrast does not make every effort to preserve life worthless or wrong. Treating illness, preventing danger, and protecting life are separate matters; taḥīdu does not itself condemn them.

The verse’s point is that, despite every effort, a person cannot keep death out of their life forever.

4. Comparison with related words

ح ي د (ḥayd) and ح ي ص (ḥayṣ): moving aside and finding escape

ح ي ص also concerns fleeing, moving aside, and finding a way out.

The proximity matters within Sūrat Qāf itself: Qāf 50:36 uses مَحِيص (maḥīṣ).

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُمْ مِنْ قَرْنٍ هُمْ أَشَدُّ مِنْهُمْ بَطْشًا فَنَقَّبُوا فِي الْبِلَادِ هَلْ مِنْ مَحِيصٍ

wa-kam ahlaknā qablahum min qarnin hum ashaddu minhum baṭshan fa-naqqabū fī al-bilādi hal min maḥīṣin

Proposed translation:

“How many generations We destroyed before them, stronger than these in force! They traveled and searched throughout the lands. Was there any escape?”

Qāf 50:36

In Qāf 50:19, taḥīdu is a verb describing avoidance or turning aside.

In Qāf 50:36, maḥīṣ is a noun in the question hal min maḥīṣin, concerning the possibility or means of escape.

The roots overlap in escape but have different radicals: taḥīdu comes from ح ي د; maḥīṣ from ح ي ص.

The difference between fleeing and having an escape comes from the verb, noun, and their contexts. One example cannot establish that ḥayd always means unsuccessful effort while ḥayṣ always means successful escape.

Both verses concern the limits of human power to escape death and what arrives through divine judgment.

ح ي د (ḥayd) and م ي ل (mayl): changing direction and inclination

Arabic mayl has a broad field, including the inclination of a body, direction of movement, desire, or human preference.

Ḥayd likewise concerns inclination and changing course, but its established uses foreground moving away from something or leaving a previous course.

An object inclining to one side, for example, need not be fleeing. Ḥāda ʿanhu, however, means turning aside or avoiding something.

The distinction is not absolute: māla ʿanhu can also mean turning away from something.

Mayl is thus a valid lexical explanation of ḥayd, without making all uses of the two families identical.

ح ي د (ḥayd) and ف ر ر (firār): turning aside and fleeing

Firār more directly emphasizes fleeing and distancing oneself from a situation or opposing party.

Ḥayd has a broader lexical range and can describe turning or changing direction without danger being present.

Given the subject of death, “you used to flee” is appropriate for taḥīdu in Qāf 50:19.

Yet “fleeing” would make no sense for the family’s uses describing mountain projections or the curves of a horn.

Firār is therefore an appropriate contextual equivalent in this Qur’anic use, rather than a definition of the whole family ح ي د.

ح ي د (ḥayd) and ح ر ف (ḥarf): moving aside and changing position

ح ر ف concerns a side or edge and, in some derivatives, changes of direction and position.

Al-Anfāl 8:16 uses mutaḥarrifan li-qitālin for maneuvering to continue a battle.

Ḥayd can likewise concern changing course, but is not lexically confined to a particular military situation.

Changing direction also need not mean evading responsibility: someone may change course to face danger more effectively.

The comparison should therefore consider the verb, its complement, and the purpose expressed by the sentence. Every movement aside should not be called flight, nor every repositioning a blameworthy retreat.

5. حِياد (ḥiyād) and contemporary neutrality

Ḥiyād and muḥāyadah are familiar words in this family.

Classical Arabic records ḥāyadahu muḥāyadatan for standing apart from someone, avoiding conflict, or not entering a confrontation with them.

In contemporary Arabic and Persian, ḥiyād and neutrality can describe a person or state not taking either side in a dispute.

This connects with standing aside and not entering a conflict. Legal and political concepts of neutrality, including neutral states’ obligations or impartiality in adjudication, are later technical meanings.

Those meanings must not be transferred to taḥīdu in Qāf 50:19.

The verse concerns avoiding an encounter with death, rather than remaining neutral between two parties.

Nor is neutrality invariably a virtue or invariably wrong. Staying out of a dispute may be right in some circumstances; withholding support from someone oppressed may help injustice continue in others.

That judgment concerns circumstances and responsibility, rather than the root itself.

Social muḥāyadah must therefore be distinguished from ḥayd as changing course or fleeing, although both belong to the same family.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Tāj al-ʿArūs: ḥāyadahu, muḥāyadah, and ḥiyād

6. Translation proposal for Forqan

Suitable general equivalents include moving aside, leaving a course, changing direction, and avoiding.

Fleeing is also appropriate where someone avoids danger, confrontation, or something unwelcome.

In Qāf 50:19, mā kunta minhu taḥīdu is best rendered:

“What you used to flee from.”

In the complete verse, “This is what you used to flee from” is natural and clear.

“What you used to avoid” is also faithful to the lexical sense, but less strongly conveys the encounter with death.

“You were afraid of it” is insufficient as a direct translation, substituting a feeling of fear for the action of turning aside.

“You denied it” is likewise unsuitable: denial is not the verb’s meaning and requires separate contextual evidence.

“You deviated from it” is close to changing direction at root level, but its Persian equivalent can suggest moral or doctrinal deviation and is less precise here than “you used to flee from it.”

The main proposal for Qāf 50:19 is therefore “you used to flee from it,” while the article retains the lexical explanation of turning aside from a course.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root ح ي د concerns moving aside, inclining away from a course, and avoiding something. Tangible witnesses include mountain projections and horn curves; behavioral examples include changing direction and animals darting sideways.

It does not inherently mean fear, sin, cowardly flight, or denial of truth. Those notions can accompany turning aside only in particular circumstances.

The Qur’anic taḥīdu occurs only in Qāf 50:19. A person faces the reality of death, the very thing they had previously avoided.

Lexically, the verse emphasizes flight and avoidance. Contextually, death arriving shows that such flight cannot continue forever.

This must not become a condemnation of every effort to preserve life, or an accusation that everyone avoiding death knowingly denies the hereafter.

Final definition: ح ي د denotes moving aside or changing direction relative to a course, position, or something one faces. This may be spatial or behavioral; in an appropriate context, it means avoiding or fleeing. In Qāf 50:19, taḥīdu concerns avoiding the encounter with death. Brief root meaning: Moving aside and changing course; avoiding an encounter and, in an appropriate context, fleeing.
