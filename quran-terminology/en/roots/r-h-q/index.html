Choice drink, clarity, and purity

The Arabic root R Ḥ Q comprises a small family of words, the best known of which is raḥīq. In early Arabic it was a name for wine, used particularly for a clear, pure, fine, and choice drink.

Lexical explanations variously describe raḥīq as wine itself, its clear and choice portion, or a pure drink free of adulteration. These differences show why no single quality, such as transparency, age, pleasant taste, or purity, should be made its complete definition.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as raḥīqin. In Al-Muṭaffifīn it describes a drink served to the virtuous amid the blessings of the hereafter.

Qur’anic form: raḥīqin (Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25).

Arabic also records ruḥāq with a meaning close to raḥīq, but it does not occur in the Qur’an. Unlike many other roots, this family has no well-established set of frequently used inflected verbs in early sources. The three root letters alone do not justify inventing verbs or derivatives and assigning meanings to them.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Raḥīq: a fine and choice drink

In early Arabic, raḥīq was one of the names of wine.

Raḥīq is listed among the names of khamr and also explained by the short expression ṣafwat al-khamr: the clear and choice portion of wine.

Another explanation describes raḥīq as a name for wine and one of its finest kinds.

Early explanations offer several descriptions together: raḥīq is a name for wine, among its finest and most mature kinds, its clear and choice portion, or a drink free of adulteration and undesirable admixture.

These explanations are close, but not identical.

A clear drink is not necessarily an aged drink. Fine quality does not always mean transparency or lack of color. Describing a drink as one of the finest kinds of wine does not mean that every example had an identical quality.

Established Arabic usage thus associates raḥīq with a drink in the category of wine, emphasizing clarity, quality, and choiceness. Every such quality need not be an essential and certain part of its meaning in each occurrence.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

كَأَنَّ مَكَاكِيَّ الْجِوَاءِ غُدَيَّةً
صُبِحْنَ سُلَافًا مِنْ رَحِيقٍ مُفَلْفَلِ

ka-anna makākiyya al-jiwāʾi ghudayyatan / ṣubiḥna sulāfan min raḥīqin mufalfali

“As though the mukāʾ birds in that valley had been given, at morning, a choice wine mixed with pepper.”

The poet compares the birds’ song and liveliness to the animation of people who have drunk a potent drink.

Makākī is the plural of mukāʾ, a kind of songbird. Sulāf denotes the choice and fine portion of a drink. Mufalfal describes a drink mixed with pepper or an associated seasoning.

Early explanations of the Muʿallaqah understand raḥīq here as wine and sulāf as its choice portion.

Some lexical transmissions reverse the order of sulāf and raḥīq, while preserving the use of raḥīq for a familiar Arabic drink.

The verse matters in several respects.

First, it shows that raḥīq was known in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period; the word did not originate solely as a Qur’anic description of a drink in the hereafter.

Second, raḥīq accompanies sulāf here, indicating a choice and fine drink.

Third, the adjective mufalfal shows that the drink in question could be mixed with another substance.

This matters for understanding purity. If raḥīq necessarily meant an absolutely unmixed drink in every use, describing it as mufalfal would require a different explanation.

Clarity or purity should therefore not become an absolute rule excluding every possible admixture.

This poetic witness alone does not prove that every drink called raḥīq was mixed. It only shows that the word could describe a fine drink accompanied by another seasoning.

Ruḥāq and the limited set of derivatives

Ruḥāq is also recorded with a meaning close to raḥīq.

One explanation of the word’s structure presents al-raḥq as the underlying construction of raḥīq and reports the meaning of clarity for it.

Ruḥāq is also reported in eloquent Arabic poetry with the meaning of raḥīq, alongside the observation that the reporter had heard no inflected verb for this family.

This report has methodological significance.

For many Arabic roots, verbs, verbal nouns, and active and passive participles can be observed in living usage. Here, however, the known evidence centers on the noun raḥīq and the closely related ruḥāq.

The existence of raḥīq therefore does not establish a hypothetical raḥaqa meaning “became clear” or “clarified the drink” as a documented Arabic usage.

Such a report does not prove that no verb ever existed in any period of Arabic. It means that no inflected verb was known in the evidence examined by the reporter.

Raḥīq follows the faʿīl pattern, but the pattern alone does not establish a degree of purity, a stage of clarification, the drink’s age, or its preparation time.

Purity beyond drinks

Arabic lexicons also record expressions such as ḥasabun raḥīqun and miskun raḥīqun.

The first denotes pure, unadulterated honor or standing; ḥasab must not be equated with nasab, meaning lineage. The second denotes pure musk without adulteration.

These uses show that raḥīq can extend beyond drinks to describe the clarity or purity of other things.

These expressions are recorded in lexicons, but independently datable pre-Islamic evidence is not available for all of them. They cannot all confidently be called pre-Qur’anic usages.

Raḥīq is also recorded for a kind of fragrant substance. This secondary usage does not justify translating its Qur’anic occurrence merely as perfume or fragrance, since the verse speaks of giving it to drink.

In modern Arabic, the nectar of flowers is also called raḥīq. Contemporary dictionaries mark this specific sense as a newer usage; it should also be distinguished from the older use of raḥīq for honey.

The modern sense of floral nectar should therefore not be presented as the original early meaning, or read back into the Qur’anic verse, without historical evidence.

2. Raḥīqin makhtūmin in Al-Muṭaffifīn

The root’s only Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25:

يُسْقَوْنَ مِنْ رَحِيقٍ مَخْتُومٍ

yusqawna min raḥīqin makhtūmin

“They are given to drink from a pure, sealed drink.”

Understanding this occurrence requires examining both raḥīq and the surrounding verses.

The recipients and setting

A few verses earlier, Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:22 states:

إِنَّ الْأَبْرَارَ لَفِي نَعِيمٍ

inna al-abrāra la-fī naʿīmin

“Indeed, the virtuous are amid bliss.”

Their place, their looking on, and the freshness visible in their faces are then described.

Raḥīq is introduced next.

Here raḥīq is part of the description of blessings for the virtuous in the hereafter. That setting comes from the surah’s context; raḥīq did not originally mean “the reward of the virtuous” in Arabic.

In early Arabic it named a familiar drink, which the Qur’an employs in describing a blessing of the hereafter.

This distinction should be preserved.

Yusqawna: they are given to drink

Yusqawna is a passive imperfect verb from S Q Y, denoting being given to drink.

The verb shows that raḥīq here is something drinkable.

Min raḥīqin identifies what is served. In this construction, min expresses being given to drink from that drink; it does not by itself determine the vessel, quantity, or preparation method.

The sentence thus makes its drinkable character clear, without requiring the word to be turned into a wholly abstract concept.

Raḥīqin: a pure and choice drink

Raḥīqin is an indefinite noun in the genitive after min.

As the lexical evidence shows, early Arabic knew the noun for fine, clear, and choice wine.

Renderings such as “pure drink” or “pure wine” therefore have lexical support.

Several considerations matter for the Persian translation.

Rendering it merely as “clear water” loses the kind of drink known in early Arabic.

If it is translated simply with the Persian word sharāb, the reader may equate it with every characteristic of earthly intoxicating wine.

Conversely, avoiding that impression by denying its established early meaning of wine would sacrifice lexical accuracy.

A natural rendering such as “pure drink” can preserve the basic meaning, while the root explanation makes clear that raḥīq was an early Arabic name for fine wine.

This respects Arabic usage without assuming that a drink in the hereafter has the same properties as earthly drinks.

Makhtūmin: a separate description

In the same verse raḥīq is described as makhtūmin.

Makhtūm comes from KH T M and describes something sealed or brought to a close.

Being sealed is therefore not part of the meaning of R Ḥ Q itself.

This matters because some explanations define raḥīq makhtūm as though raḥīq itself lexically meant a sealed or packaged drink.

The verse instead contains two distinct words: raḥīqin names the drink, and makhtūmin describes it.

Together they portray a pure, sealed drink.

Sealing may suggest preservation and protection from tampering or prior use. It does not establish the vessel, sealing material, or storage method in the hereafter.

3. Khitāmuhu miskun: distinguishing raḥīq from its accompanying descriptions

The next verse, Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26, states:

خِتَامُهُ مِسْكٌ وَفِي ذَٰلِكَ فَلْيَتَنَافَسِ الْمُتَنَافِسُونَ

khitāmuhu miskun wa-fī dhālika fa-l-yatanāfasi al-mutanāfisūna

“Its seal is musk; for that let those who compete compete.”

The pronoun -hu in khitāmuhu refers to the preceding drink.

Misk is another independent word, denoting a fragrant substance.

Two recognized readings, among others, have been proposed for khitāmuhu miskun.

In one, khitām concerns the seal and closure of the drink, portraying a drink whose seal is musk.

In another, khitām concerns the end of drinking or the drink’s final effect or taste: its ending is accompanied by the scent or fragrant effect of musk.

Determining the precise meaning requires a separate examination of KH T M, the uses of khitām, and the sentence structure.

For the present study, however, one point is clear: the fragrance of musk comes from misk and the description khitāmuhu, rather than from raḥīq itself.

Raḥīq should therefore not be defined lexically as “a musk-scented drink.”

Raḥīq names the drink; the quality concerning musk is expressed in the next verse.

Nor does this description establish the vessel’s precise shape, the sealing method, the drink’s material composition, or how its fragrance will be experienced in the hereafter.

The Qur’an portrays the blessing; physical details that it does not state should not be supplied by conjecture.

4. Wa-mizājuhu min tasnīmin: is raḥīq always unmixed?

The next verse, Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27, offers particularly important evidence for the limits of raḥīq’s meaning:

وَمِزَاجُهُ مِنْ تَسْنِيمٍ

wa-mizājuhu min tasnīmin

“And its admixture is from Tasnīm.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28 then states:

عَيْنًا يَشْرَبُ بِهَا الْمُقَرَّبُونَ

ʿaynan yashrabu bihā al-muqarrabūna

“A spring from which those brought near drink.”

Mizāj comes from M Z J and concerns mixing and what is mixed with something else.

Tasnīm in this context concerns a spring associated with the drink under discussion.

This raises a question: if raḥīq is a pure drink, why does the Qur’an speak of its admixture?

The answer lies in distinguishing purity from the absolute absence of mixing.

Purity does not exclude every admixture

Lexical explanations associate raḥīq with clarity, choiceness, and freedom from impurity or adulteration.

Freedom from adulteration does not necessarily mean never being mixed with anything else.

A drink may be fine and wholesome while being mixed with another substance for consumption.

This distinction also appears in the witness from Imruʾ al-Qays, where raḥīq is described as mufalfal.

In Al-Muṭaffifīn, raḥīq is described as makhtūm, followed by a statement about its mizāj.

Defining raḥīq as an absolutely unmixed drink therefore fits the complete passage poorly.

“A pure and choice drink,” by contrast, can accommodate its being mixed with Tasnīm.

This does not mean the Qur’an gives information about chemical properties, proportions, or preparation methods.

The text clearly supplies two descriptions: raḥīq is a pure, sealed drink, and its admixture is described as coming from Tasnīm.

Is Tasnīm the name of a kind of raḥīq?

No such conclusion follows from the root’s meaning.

Raḥīq names the drink, while Tasnīm concerns a spring in the following verses.

The two words should not be equated.

Nor should raḥīq originally be defined as “water from the spring of Tasnīm.” The spring’s name and its relationship to the drink are given in another verse.

Each word contributes its own meaning here as well.

Raḥīq identifies the familiar kind or quality of drink; mizāj conveys admixture; and Tasnīm identifies what participates in that admixture.

The complete meaning emerges from the verses together, rather than from artificially expanding the definition of R Ḥ Q.

5. Raḥīq and other Qur’anic drinks

R Ḥ Q (raḥīq) and KH M R (khamr)

The two words are related in early Arabic usage.

Lexicons identify raḥīq as a name for khamr, while khamr is a broader and better-known word.

The Qur’an uses khamr both in passages about earthly life and in descriptions of blessings in the hereafter.

For example, part of Muḥammad 47:15 states:

وَأَنْهَارٌ مِنْ خَمْرٍ لَذَّةٍ لِلشَّارِبِينَ

wa-anhārun min khamrin ladhdhatin li-al-shāribīna

“And rivers of wine delightful to those who drink.”

Here the Qur’an directly uses khamr to describe a blessing of the hereafter.

This shows why raḥīq’s association with Paradise does not justify erasing its established connection with wine from the history of Arabic.

Conversely, it does not follow that every feature of earthly wine, including intoxication and its harms, also characterizes the drink of the hereafter.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 describes a drink of the hereafter:

لَا فِيهَا غَوْلٌ وَلَا هُمْ عَنْهَا يُنْزَفُونَ

lā fīhā ghawlun wa-lā hum ʿanhā yunzafūna

“There is no harm or corruption in it, nor are they deprived of their senses by it.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19 also states:

لَا يُصَدَّعُونَ عَنْهَا وَلَا يُنْزِفُونَ

lā yuṣaddaʿūna ʿanhā wa-lā yunzifūna

“They suffer no headache from it, nor do they lose their reason and awareness.”

These verses describe drinks of the hereafter and contribute to their broader portrayal, although they alone do not prove that all the drinks mentioned in different surahs are precisely identical.

The familiar name of a drink in Arabic must therefore be distinguished from its qualities in the context of the hereafter.

This distinction neither denies raḥīq’s lexical meaning nor equates the conditions of the hereafter with earthly drinks.

Study of KH M R

R Ḥ Q (raḥīq) and Ṣ F W (ṣafw)

Ṣafw concerns clarity, purity, and choiceness, and is also used to explain raḥīq lexically.

Ṣafw has a broader semantic range, applicable to various objects, relationships, and conditions.

In its main established usage, raḥīq names a kind of drink.

Not everything clear or pure can therefore be called raḥīq.

Clear water, pure metal, or unadulterated speech may be described with words concerning purity. That shared quality does not make them lexically raḥīq.

In recorded adjectival uses such as miskun raḥīqun, purity extends to another substance. These uses should be understood through their own evidence, without displacing the principal meaning of a drink.

Study of Ṣ F W

R Ḥ Q (raḥīq) and SH R B (sharāb)

Sharāb is a general name for something that is drunk.

It can apply to water, other drinks, and what is described among blessings or punishments.

Raḥīq in early Arabic was a more specific name for a familiar, fine drink.

Translating raḥīq simply as “drink” therefore preserves the general meaning while losing some of its qualities and lexical history.

“Pure drink” can convey at least its choiceness and clarity.

Even “pure drink” does not convey its entire history as a name for wine, so the root explanation remains necessary to clarify that background.

Study of SH R B

R Ḥ Q (raḥīq) and M Z J (mazj)

Mazj concerns mixing one thing with another.

The Qur’an also uses this root to describe drinks of the hereafter, for example in Al-Insān 76:5:

إِنَّ الْأَبْرَارَ يَشْرَبُونَ مِنْ كَأْسٍ كَانَ مِزَاجُهَا كَافُورًا

inna al-abrāra yashrabūna min kaʾsin kāna mizājuhā kāfūran

“Indeed, the virtuous drink from a cup whose admixture is camphor.”

And in Al-Insān 76:17:

وَيُسْقَوْنَ فِيهَا كَأْسًا كَانَ مِزَاجُهَا زَنْجَبِيلًا

wa-yusqawna fīhā kaʾsan kāna mizājuhā zanjabīlan

“And there they are given a cup whose admixture is ginger.”

These verses show that admixture in descriptions of drinks among the blessings of the hereafter is not limited to raḥīq and Tasnīm.

They do not establish that raḥīq, camphor, ginger, and Tasnīm all name one substance or drink.

Each expression should be understood in its own context.

For this root, the main point is that mixing a drink with something else does not in itself conflict with fine quality and choiceness.

6. Lexical meaning and descriptions of blessings in the hereafter

Al-Muṭaffifīn describes a drink for the virtuous without stating every detail of its nature.

It is called raḥīq, described as makhtūm, its khitām is associated with musk, and its mizāj is identified as coming from Tasnīm.

These descriptions do not establish that the drink is made precisely from grapes or another particular fruit, contains a set percentage of a substance, has a fixed color or transparency, or follows familiar earthly production methods.

The modern sense of floral nectar likewise does not establish that this drink is made from flowers.

The verse does not state such details.

Conversely, unfamiliarity with the nature of these blessings does not justify abandoning the drink’s meaning and presenting raḥīq solely as a symbol of awareness, wisdom, divine love, or inward knowledge.

The Qur’an says yusqawna and then uses words concerning a drink, admixture, and a spring.

Its direct, natural meaning as a drink should therefore be retained.

Any spiritual or figurative reflection requires its own evidence and reasoning, and should not replace the basic translation.

Raḥīq in the Qur’an does not name a legal ruling or a command to drink. Its use describes a blessing of the hereafter; this single verse does not independently establish a ruling on the permissibility or prohibition of earthly drinks.

That subject requires a separate examination of the verses about khamr, drinks, and rulings.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Raḥīq: a pure drink; a clear and choice drink; in early Arabic, fine and pure wine.

Ruḥāq: a lexical form close to raḥīq, meaning a clear or choice drink. It does not occur in the Qur’an.

Raḥīqin makhtūmin: a pure, sealed drink; a clear, sealed drink.

The current Persian Forqan page renders Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25 as follows:

يُسْقَوْنَ مِنْ رَحِيقٍ مَخْتُومٍ

yusqawna min raḥīqin makhtūmin

“They are given to drink from a clear, sealed drink.”

This rendering preserves its drinkable character and clarity, while conveying makhtūm separately.

The root explanation should clarify that “clear” here concerns purity and fineness; the Qur’an does not specify the drink’s color, degree of optical transparency, or chemical composition.

“Pure drink” is also a natural and defensible equivalent, emphasizing quality and purity without attributing every earthly characteristic of wine to the drink of the hereafter.

Its established early Arabic use as a name for khamr should nevertheless remain in the lexical explanation.

In translating the following verses, khitāmuhu miskun and wa-mizājuhu min tasnīmin must retain their independent meanings rather than becoming part of raḥīq’s definition.

8. Conclusion

R Ḥ Q has a small Arabic word family, whose principal known form is raḥīq.

Early sources identify raḥīq as a name for wine, particularly associated with clarity, quality, choiceness, and purity.

The witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays in the Muʿallaqah shows its use for a familiar Arabic drink. Mufalfal in the same verse also cautions against interpreting purity as the absolute exclusion of any admixture.

The root occurs in the Qur’an only in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25, naming a drink given to the virtuous among the blessings of the hereafter.

Makhtūm is a separate description of this drink. Khitāmuhu miskun and wa-mizājuhu min tasnīmin supply further qualities not derived from R Ḥ Q itself.

The verse about raḥīq’s admixture shows why purity and fine quality should not be equated with the absence of every kind of mixing.

The modern floral-nectar sense likewise should not become the word’s original Qur’anic Arabic meaning without historical evidence.

“Pure drink” or “clear drink” can convey the Qur’anic meaning naturally, provided its lexical history as a name for fine wine is understood and the qualities of the hereafter are not equated with those of earthly drinks.

Final definition: In established early Arabic, raḥīq named a drink in the category of wine whose clarity, purity, and quality were prominent. The Qur’an uses it for a pure drink among the blessings of the hereafter. Sealing, the fragrance of musk, and admixture with Tasnīm are qualities separately stated in the accompanying verses; they should not be added to the meaning of the root itself.
