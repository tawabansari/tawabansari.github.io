Root w-ḍ-n — Firm weaving, interlacing, and joining components

The Arabic root w-ḍ-n concerns weaving, interlacing, and firmly joining the components of something. This connection may involve weaving strands, linking rings, or fitting structural elements together.

In early Arabic, this family was used for weaving broad straps, making linked armor, and interlacing the components of couches and other objects.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as mawḍūnah. In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15, it describes the couches of the foremost.

Mawḍūnah does not inherently mean made of gold, silver, or jewels. Its central meaning concerns the manner of weaving and firmly joining components. Materials, adornment, and the precise form of the couches require separate evidence.

1. The root in early Arabic

Weaving and interlacing strands

In early Arabic, waḍana al-shayʾa was used for interlacing and bringing together the components of something so that they became connected.

This meaning appears in making everyday equipment, particularly camel gear and armor.

One recognized form is waḍīn.

A waḍīn was a broad woven girth used to secure a load or riding seat on a camel’s back.

It could be made from leather strips or other weaving materials. The strands were interwoven to produce a broad, strong strap.

A waḍīn was therefore more than a thin cord: its weave and connected components mattered to its strength and function.

A poetic witness from Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī

A poem by the pre-Islamic poet Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī says:

تَقُولُ إِذَا دَرَأْتُ لَهَا وَضِينِي

taqūlu idhā daraʾtu lahā waḍīnī

أَهَذَا دِينُهُ أَبَدًا وَدِينِي

a-hādhā dīnuhu abadan wa-dīnī

“As though, when I fasten my broad saddle girth around her, she says: Is this forever his way and mine?”

The poet describes a she-camel repeatedly prepared for travel.

In the following lines, the camel seems to complain about constant journeys and their hardship.

Waḍīnī here refers to the woven girth fastened around the camel to secure its load or saddle.

This witness is recorded in Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī’s collected poetry and his well-known ode. Lexical sources also transmit daʾbuhu, “his habitual practice,” in place of dīnuhu, “his way.”

The usage associates this family with woven and interlaced equipment in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The material application clarifies the meaning: several strands or strips are joined to make an object that can hold a load securely.

This witness does not make every derivative refer to securing a camel’s load. Waḍīn names one particular object within this family.

Woven armor

Another use concerns the manufacture of armor.

Mail armor consisted of interlinked metal rings. Their connection produced a flexible, resistant covering.

Such armor was called dirʿun mawḍūnah in Arabic.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Aʿshā says:

وَمِنْ نَسْجِ دَاوُدَ مَوْضُونَةً

wa-min nasji Dāwūda mawḍūnatan

تُسَاقُ مَعَ الْحَيِّ عِيرًا فَعِيرًا

tusāqu maʿa al-ḥayyi ʿīran fa-ʿīrā

“And woven armor of David’s weaving, carried with the people, caravan after caravan.”

This part of the poem describes military equipment and preparedness.

Mawḍūnah here denotes armor whose rings are firmly interwoven. A variant transmission reads yusāqu bihā al-ḥayyu ʿīran fa-ʿīrā in the second half-line.

The witness uses the same form, mawḍūnah, as the Qur’an, in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Attribution alone, however, does not independently establish the date and transmission of the wording.

Here mawḍūnah does not merely mean decorated or beautiful. Armor may have an orderly, attractive appearance, but its defining feature is the firm connection of its interwoven rings.

“Woven” or “firmly interwoven” is therefore more precise than “decorated” alone.

Fitting stones and bricks together

Arabic lexicons also record waḍana al-ḥajara wa-al-ājurra: placing stones or bricks together so that their parts form a connected structure.

This application does not involve weaving strands or rings.

It concerns fitting solid components together and making the whole firm.

The root’s semantic range thus extends beyond weaving strands to arranging and firmly joining components.

Recording this expression in later lexicons does not by itself establish that this exact construction-related usage was current before Islam.

The poetic witnesses attributed to Al-Muthaqqib and Al-Aʿshā offer clearer evidence for the old weaving and interlacing sense.

What the historical witnesses show

The early witnesses associate w-ḍ-n with making objects whose components were interwoven or firmly joined.

The camel girth and linked armor are two clear examples.

In both, the connection of the components defines the structure.

A historically defensible meaning is weaving and firmly interconnecting components, rather than mere adornment or placement without connection.

2. Forms and semantic core

Important forms in Arabic include:

Waḍana: he wove, interlaced the components of something, or joined them firmly.

Waḍn: the act of weaving and interconnecting components.

Waḍīn: a broad woven girth used to secure a camel’s load, saddle, or riding seat.

Wuḍun: an attested plural of waḍīn.

Mawḍūn: something whose components have been interwoven or firmly joined.

Mawḍūnah: the feminine form of mawḍūn; woven, with interlaced components.

Waḍnah: in lexical usage, a chair or seat with a woven structure.

Mīḍanah: a type of container or basket woven from leaves and plant fibers, recorded in Arabic sources.

Some sources also record tawaḍḍana in senses of drawing near, showing humility, or affection. The historical connection of these limited uses with weaving and interlacing is uncertain; they do not occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic form

Only one derivative of this family occurs in the Qur’an:

Mawḍūnatin — Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15.

It is the feminine passive participle of the Form I verb waḍana.

The passive participle describes something that has undergone weaving or interconnection.

In the verse, mawḍūnah qualifies surur.

Surur is the plural of sarīr, “couch.” Arabic nonhuman plurals can take feminine singular adjectives.

Mawḍūnah is also genitive, agreeing with surur in this phrase.

The feminine form does not imply that the couches are exclusively for women. It follows Arabic grammatical agreement.

The semantic core

The principal semantic core is:

Weaving or firmly joining components so that their strands, rings, or parts interlace and form an integrated whole.

Three features matter: connectable components, their interlacing, and the resulting connected structure.

Material, color, and monetary value are not fixed elements of the root’s meaning.

An object woven from leather, fibers, metal, or other materials can appropriately be called mawḍūn.

3. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15 — Woven, interlaced couches

عَلَىٰ سُرُرٍ مَّوْضُونَةٍ

ʿalā sururin mawḍūnah

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15

“Upon firmly interwoven couches.”

Surur here denotes couches or seats.

Mawḍūnah describes their construction or interconnected arrangement.

The verse presents couches with interwoven components or woven sections. Skillfulness is an inference from the scene, not a separate lexical meaning of the word.

The word does not specify whether the entire couch is woven or only parts, such as its sitting surface or covering.

What the root conveys is weaving and firm interconnection.

The surrounding verses

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:10 mentions the foremost.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:11 identifies them as those brought near.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:12 places them in gardens of bliss.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:13 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:14 then mention many from the earlier generations and few from the later ones.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15 describes their couches.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:16 describes their posture: reclining upon them, facing one another.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15 therefore contributes to the depiction of the foremost and their blessings.

The couches are described as mawḍūnah, while Al-Wāqiʿah 56:16 describes their use.

These verses should be read together, without merging their meanings.

Mawḍūnah describes the construction or weave of the couches.

Muttakiʾīn describes the people’s reclining posture.

Mutaqābilīn specifies that they face one another.

Neither facing one another nor reclining can therefore be extracted from w-ḍ-n itself.

Are the couches made of gold and jewels?

Early explanations describe couches adorned with gold, pearls, rubies, or precious stones.

These descriptions may portray the splendor and beauty of heavenly couches, but interpretive explanation should be distinguished from direct lexical meaning.

Mawḍūnah concerns weaving and interconnecting components.

The word alone does not specify their material.

The same root also describes leather camel girths and linked armor.

It is therefore unwarranted to claim that mawḍūnah originally means “woven from gold” or “jewel-studded.”

Equally, its interlacing and firm weave should not be reduced to “decorated.”

An object can be beautiful and adorned without having woven components.

“Woven” or “firmly interwoven” is consequently more precise in translation than “golden” or “jewel-studded.”

Are the physical properties of the heavenly couches specified?

The verse offers an intelligible image of couches with an interwoven structure. Beauty belongs to the scene of blessing and should not be treated as an independent lexical meaning of mawḍūnah.

Their exact material, dimensions, tools of manufacture, and physical weaving technique are unspecified.

The use of this root for armor does not entail that heavenly couches are made from iron rings like mail.

Its use for camel girths likewise does not establish that the couches are woven from leather.

Material examples in Arabic help explain the meaning, without transferring every property of those objects to the couches in the verse.

The Qur’anic image should be preserved without adding unstated details about the construction of the hereafter.

4. Comparison with related roots

W-ḍ-n compared with n-s-j

N-s-j concerns weaving and passing strands through one another.

The principal branch of w-ḍ-n likewise concerns weaving and joining components.

Recorded uses of waḍn emphasize a firm weave, close components, and their interlacing.

Linked armor and broad camel girths are clear examples.

Nasj can also describe ordinary weaving of cloth and other things.

The roots overlap; no absolute boundary should be drawn whereby every nasj is loose and every waḍn firm.

The witnesses clearly establish waḍn as a term for connected, firm weaves.

W-ḍ-n compared with n-ḍ-d

Naḍd concerns stacking and arranging things in order.

Study of n-ḍ-d

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29 contains wa-ṭalḥin manḍūd, where manḍūd concerns layered arrangement and the ordering of components.

The principal use of waḍn concerns weaving and interconnection.

Several things may be stacked without being interwoven.

Stones, for example, can be arranged in a stack, whereas a leather strap is made by interlacing strips.

Arranging and weaving are thus related but distinct actions.

Arabic sources nevertheless also record waḍn for firmly fitting stones and bricks together. The distinction should not become an absolute separation.

W-ḍ-n compared with ṣ-f-f

Al-Ṭūr 52:20 describes couches as maṣfūfah.

This derives from ṣ-f-f and concerns arranging things in rows or an orderly formation.

Study of ṣ-f-f

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15 uses mawḍūnah.

The words are not synonymous.

Maṣfūfah emphasizes the arrangement of the couches.

Mawḍūnah emphasizes the weave and interconnection of their components.

A couch can be woven and also stand in an orderly arrangement alongside other couches.

The descriptions can coexist, but neither independently expresses the other’s meaning.

W-ḍ-n compared with ḍ-f-r

Ḍ-f-r concerns braiding and intertwining strands, especially hair and cord.

Waḍn is close to it in interlacing components.

Their specific uses nevertheless differ.

Ḍafr prominently involves twisting and braiding strands.

Waḍn is known for firmly interlacing woven components, such as a broad girth or armor rings.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute rule covering every historical use of either root.

Mawḍūnah compared with mawḍūʿah

These forms resemble one another in spelling and sound but derive from different roots.

Mawḍūnah derives from w-ḍ-n and means woven or interconnected.

Mawḍūʿah derives from w-ḍ-ʿ and means placed or set down.

Study of w-ḍ-ʿ

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:14 describes cups as mawḍūʿah: cups set in place.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15, however, the couches are mawḍūnah: woven, with interconnected components.

Mawḍūnah should therefore not be translated as “placed.”

Changing one root consonant can fundamentally change a word’s meaning.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

A brief, direct rendering of Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15 follows. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

“Upon woven couches.”

The corresponding short Persian rendering is natural and preserves the principal meaning.

To make the interlacing and firmness more explicit, one can write:

“Upon firmly interwoven couches.”

The second rendering makes the attested quality of the weave more explicit; it does not imply that ordinary weaving is necessarily loose.

The shorter form suffices for the base translation, while detailed explanation can remain in the root study.

“Jewel-studded couches” is not proposed because the word itself does not establish jeweled adornment.

“Golden couches” likewise adds a material to the verse.

“Arranged couches” emphasizes another meaning, closer to maṣfūfah or manḍūdah.

“Couches set in place” concerns w-ḍ-ʿ and loses the weaving sense.

Non-Qur’anic forms should be translated according to context.

Waḍana al-shayʾa means “he wove something firmly” or “interconnected its components.”

Waḍīn means “a broad woven girth for securing a camel’s load or saddle.”

Dirʿun mawḍūnah means “armor with firmly interwoven rings.”

Waḍnah, when used for a seat, means “a woven chair.”

Mīḍanah, when used for a container, means “a woven basket or vessel.”

The semantic core of weaving and joining components can thus apply to different objects without requiring identical materials or functions.

6. Conclusion

W-ḍ-n concerns weaving and firmly interconnecting components.

In poetry attributed to Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī, waḍīn is the broad woven girth securing a camel’s load or saddle.

In poetry attributed to Al-Aʿshā, mawḍūnah describes armor whose rings are firmly interlinked.

These witnesses associate the root in early Arabic with a woven, connected structure.

Only one form occurs in the Qur’an: mawḍūnah in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15.

It describes the couches of the foremost, conveying their firm weave or interconnected components.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:16 describes reclining and facing one another. These features relate to the couches but are not part of the lexical meaning of mawḍūnah.

The root also leaves the material unspecified. Weaving with gold, jewels, or other materials requires separate evidence.

The final definition is: weaving and firmly interconnecting components so that strands, rings, or parts interlace and produce a connected, stable structure.

This should be distinguished from nasj, weaving in a broader sense; naḍd, stacking; ṣaff, arranging in rows; and waḍʿ, placing or setting down.

For Forqan’s translation, “woven” is a suitable brief equivalent; “firmly interwoven” gives a more explicit account of the same meaning.

The principal feature conveyed by mawḍūnah in the Qur’an is therefore how the components of the couches are joined, rather than the material or monetary value of their adornment.
